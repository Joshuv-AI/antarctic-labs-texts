Dynamical Programming for off-the-grid dynamic Inverse Problems Thanks: This work was funded by the ANR CIPRESSI project, grant ANR-19-CE48-0017-01 of the French Agence Nationale de la Recherche.
Vincent Duval, Robert Tovey

Abstract
. In this work we consider off-the-grid algorithms for the reconstruction of sparse measures from time-varying data. In particular, the reconstruction is a finite collection of Dirac measures whose locations and masses vary continuously in time. Recent work showed that this decomposition was possible by minimising a convex variational model which combined a quadratic data fidelity with dynamical Optimal Transport. We generalise this framework and propose new numerical methods which leverage efficient classical algorithms for computing shortest paths on directed acyclic graphs. Our theoretical analysis confirms that these methods converge to globally optimal reconstructions. Numerically, we show new examples for unbalanced Optimal Transport penalties, and for balanced examples we are 100 times faster in comparison to the previously known method.

{resume}

Dans cet article, nous considérons des algorithmes de reconstruction sans grille pour des mesures parcimonieuses à partir de données dynamiques. En particulier, le signal reconstruit est une somme finie de mesures de Dirac dont les positions et les amplitudes varient continûment.
Des travaux récents ont montré qu’une telle décomposition était possible dans un cadre variationnel convexe qui combine une attache aux données quadratique avec la formulation dynamique du transport optimal. Nous généralisons ce cadre et proposons une nouvelle méthode numérique qui exploite des algorithmes efficaces classiques de plus court chemin sur des graphes acycliques orientés. Notre analyse théorique confirme que ces méthodes convergent vers des minimiseurs globaux. Nous illustrons ces méthodes sur des exemples nouveaux impliquant le transport optimal non-équilibré, et, pour le transport classique, notre méthode est près de 100 fois plus rapide que l’état de l’art.

1.
Introduction

The signal-processing task of dynamical super-resolution involves retrieving fine-scale features, in space and time, from a signal which evolves over time. A convex variational model was recently proposed for such tasks using Optimal Transport (OT) to regularise the associated inverse problem
[
11
,
12
]
. This new approach allows the decomposition of a signal into a finite sum of smooth curves, for example to track the centers of multiple particles in time with smooth trajectories. Similar ideas were explored for a specific example in
[
1
]
where the shape of curves is built into the model, and without appealing to OT.

In this work we focus on dynamical super-resolution problems regularised by OT. We can consider potential models to be partitioned into two classes, depending on whether the particles have constant mass/brightness in time, or if mass is allowed to vary. These classes are referred to as balanced or unbalanced problems respectively, mathematically encoded in the choice of OT cost. Current literature provides analysis for the balanced Benamou-Brenier (BB)
[
11
]
and the unbalanced Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao (WFR)
[
9
]
energies, both are shown to reconstruct data into a finite number of smooth curves with constant or smoothly varying mass. Initial numerical experiments for the Benamou-Brenier model have also been carried out showing great promise
[
10
]
, however current methods are too slow for large-scale applications.

Let
\Omega
be an open bounded spatial domain. At the heart of the analysis of Bredies
et al.
is the interplay between measures
\rho(t,x)
defined on the time-space cylinder
\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}
, and measures
\sigma(\gamma)
defined on the space of curves
\gamma=(h,\xi)
with mass
h\in{\rm C}(\left[0,1\right])
and trajectory
\xi\in{\rm C}(\left[0,1\right];\overline{\Omega})
. We can think of
\rho
as representing the evolving physical volume that can be observed with a microscope, whereas
\sigma
more efficiently represents a collection of (trajectories of) particles, that we wish to reconstruct.
The structure of the problem as seen from this second viewpoint closely resembles the Beurling-LASSO which is now well-understood
[
13
,
4
,
22
,
26
]
. Its main advantage is that it paves the way for “off-the-grid” numerical methods when solving such dynamical inverse problems. Recent works in the field of sparse spike recovery
[
13
,
6
,
19
]
have demonstrated that it is possible to design efficient numerical solvers without reconstructing the unknown on a grid, by exploiting a conditional gradient descent / Frank-Wolfe approach together with a good knowledge of the regularising term. Indeed, the Frank-Wolfe minimisation algorithm and its variants (see the review
[
25
]
) build iterates that are convex combinations of the extreme points of level sets of the regulariser; being able to easily encode and handle such extreme points makes it possible to solve variational problems in a continuous (or up to floating point) setting.
Moreover, having iterates that are convex combinations of a few extreme points of the level sets of the regulariser is particularly relevant, as it is known in inverse problems that some solutions have precisely that structure when the observation consists of a finite number of linear measurements
[
30
,
8
,
7
]
.

1.1.
Motivating example

To make these observations and the contribution of this work more concrete we will make reference to the motivating example described in
[
11
]
with numerical examples in
[
10
]
. We will describe this problem briefly here and postpone precise assumptions and details of function spaces to Section
\Rref
sec:WFR example where it is the
\delta=+\infty
case.
For
\alpha,\beta>0
the Benamou-Brenier penalty
\mathcal{W}
is a map of non-negative space-time measures
\rho
defined by

\mathcal{W}(\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\inf_{v\in\xLtwo_{\rho}(\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega};\xR^{d})}\Bigg\{\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\left[\alpha+\frac{\beta}{2}|v|^{2}\right]\xdif\rho\ \st\ \partial_{t}\rho+\operatorname{div}(v\rho)=0\Bigg\},

(1.1)

where the continuity equation is satisfied in the weak sense which will be clarified in (
1.14
). The function with
\alpha=0
,
\beta=1
is referred to as the Benamou-Brenier energy whose main properties can be found in
[
28
, Sec. 5.3.1]
. It was shown (
\cf
[
28
,
11
]
) that whenever
\mathcal{W}(\rho)<+\infty
, there exists a “disintegration” into spatial measures
\{\rho_{t}\}_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}
such that the curve
t\mapsto\rho_{t}
is continuous in the narrow topology and such that

\forall\psi\in\xLone(\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega};\rho),\quad\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif\rho(t,x)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif\rho_{t}(x)\right)\xdif t.

(1.2)

Thanks to this property, given times
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{T}=1
, it is justified to consider “slices”
\rho_{t_{j}}
and to assume that we are given data
b_{j}\in\xR^{m}
(for simplicity assume
m
does not change with
j
) at time
t_{j}
, corresponding to linear observations of
\rho_{t_{j}}
, given by narrowly continuous linear operators
A_{j}
. The more involved case of continuous-time observations is handled in
[
12
]
although we do not discuss it further in this work. To solve the corresponding inverse problem,
[
11
, (43)]
proposes the minimisation of

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{T}{\left\lVert A_{j}\rho_{t_{j}}-b_{j}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\mathcal{W}(\rho).

(1.3)

It is then shown in
[
11
, Theorem 10]
that there is a minimiser
\rho^{*}
which is a finite sum of extreme points of the Benamou-Brenier unit ball,
\ie

\text{for some }a_{i}\geq 0,\ \xi^{i}\in{\rm AC}^{2}(\left[0,1\right];\overline{\Omega}),\qquad\forall t\in\left[0,1\right],\qquad\rho^{*}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{m(T+1)}a_{i}\delta_{\xi^{i}(t)},

(1.4)

where
{\rm AC}^{2}
is the set of absolutely continuous function such that their (a.e. defined) pointwise derivative is square-integrable (see also
[
3
, Sec. 1.1]
).
The sparse structure of
\rho^{*}
motivates us to look at problems like (
1.3
) from the viewpoint of measures on paths,
\ie
to have a minimiser
\sigma^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{m(T+1)}a_{i}\delta_{\xi^{i}}
. This will enable us to greatly generalise the types of regulariser
\mathcal{W}
for which minimisers are sparsely supported on curves, and develop a new specialised tool for “off-the-grid” numerical methods in this setting.

1.2.
Outline of the paper

In Section
\Rref
sec: preliminary we gather and present several results on the measure representation of the solutions of the continuity equation that are useful to understand the perspective of measures on paths.
Our first main theoretical contribution is found in Section
\Rref
sec: generalisation where we introduce our main variational inverse problem and provide several theoretical results on the existence of sparse minimisers and their “geodesic” structure. Algorithmic details are discussed in Section
\Rref
sec: FW convergence where we analyse a new stochastic Frank-Wolfe variant which is practically computable and proven to converge almost surely. This analysis is our other main theoretical contribution.

Numerical work on the Benamou-Brenier example was recently presented in
[
10
]
. The most time consuming step is to compute new extreme points,
\ie
curves
\gamma
, to add to the reconstruction. Even though only a small subset of
\xLinfty(\left[0,1\right];\xR\times\overline{\Omega})
is involved, optimising over such a set is a challenging task, the computation times are hardly compatible with practical applications.
Our main numerical contribution is to reformulate this step into a shortest-path problem on an ordered, weighted, directed, acyclic graph in Section
\Rref
sec: dynamical programming, which can be solved very efficiently using dynamical programming. Numerical experiments demonstrate the efficiency of this new technique in Section
\Rref
sec: results. In particular, we compare with the algorithm of
[
10
]
for the Benamou-Brenier penalty outlined in Section
\Rref
sec:BB example intro. Finally we show new results for the unbalanced Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao penalty which is described in detail in Section
\Rref
sec:WFR example.

1.3.
Notation

Convex sets and extreme points.

Let
V
be a linear space. For all
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\in V
, we define the closed line segment between
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma_{1}
as
\left[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left\{\,\lambda\sigma_{0}+(1-\lambda)\sigma_{1}\mid 0\leq\lambda\leq 1\,\right\}
. Similarly, we define the open line segment
\left]\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\right[\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\right]\setminus\{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\}
.
A set
D\subseteq V
is called
convex
if
\left]\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\right[\subset D
for all
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\in D
. We say that
\sigma\in D
is an
extreme point
(or
atom
) of
D
, and write
\sigma\in\operatorname{Ext}\left(D\right)
, if there are no points
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\in D
such that
\sigma\in\left]\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\right[
.
In other words,

\forall\lambda\in\left]0,1\right[,\forall\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\in D,\quad\left(\sigma=\lambda\sigma_{0}+(1-\lambda)\sigma_{1}\right)\quad\implies\quad\left(\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{1}=\sigma\right).

(1.5)

Furthermore, it is possible to define the notion of face (and elementary face) of a convex set, which extends the notion of extreme point to higher-dimensional sets. We refer to
[
21
]
for more detail.

Measure spaces.

For a separable metric space
\Gamma
and Banach space
X
, we define
{\rm C_{b}}(\Gamma;X)
to be the set of continuous bounded functions from
\Gamma
to
X
. When
X=\xR
, we simply write
{\rm C_{b}}(\Gamma)
. Recall that for any Borel measure
\sigma
on
\Gamma
, we can define the non-negative Borel measure
{\left|\sigma\right|}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)
by

{\left|\sigma\right|}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sup\left\{\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left|\sigma(A_{i})\right|}\mid n\in\xN,\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\}\ \mbox{Borel partition of $A$}\,\right\}

(1.6)

for all Borel measurable sets
A\subset\Gamma
. We denote by
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
the space of signed Borel measures
\sigma
with finite total variation,
\ie

{\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\int_{\Gamma}\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}<+\infty
.
The total variation
{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}
defines a norm on
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
, but it is sometimes more convenient to use the
narrow topology
,
\ie
the weakest topology on
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
which makes the integration against continuous bounded functions a continuous linear form. The narrow topology is equivalent to the weak-* topology on
({\rm C_{b}}(\Gamma))^{\prime}
, in particular, a sequence
\{\sigma^{n}\}_{n\in\xN}\subset\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
converges to
\sigma^{*}\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
in the narrow topology (denoted
\sigma^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\sigma
) if

\forall\phi\in{\rm C_{b}}(\Gamma),\quad\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Gamma}\phi\xdif\sigma^{n}=\int_{\Gamma}\phi\xdif\sigma^{*}.

(1.7)

The support of
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)
is defined as

\operatorname{supp}(\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left(\bigcup\left\{\,U\mid\sigma(U)=0,\ U\text{ is open}\,\right\}\right)^{c}.

(1.8)

This is a closed set satisfying
\sigma(\operatorname{supp}(\sigma))=\sigma(\Gamma)
.

Function domains.

In this work we consider two measure domains, the time-space cylinder

{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\quad\text{for an open, bounded, convex domain }\quad\Omega\subseteq\xR^{d},\ d\geq 1

(1.9)

and a closed set
\Gamma
of continuous (weighted) curves which are viewed as pairs
\gamma=(h,\xi)
where
h(t)
is the mass at time
t
and
\xi(t)
is the location. A formal definition will be given in Lemma
\Rref
lmma: metric properties. In the time-space cylinder we will denote measures
\rho\in\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
with test functions
\psi\in{\rm C}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
, and similarly on the space of curves
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
and
\phi\in{\rm C_{b}}(\Gamma)
.

Narrowly continuous measures.

An important subspace of
\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
is the space of narrowly continuous measures. With a slight abuse of the standard notation, we will say
\rho\in{\rm C_{w}}(\left[0,1\right];\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega}))\subset\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
if there exists a map
t\mapsto\rho_{t}\in\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega})
(informally,
\rho_{t}=\rho(t,\cdot)
is a “time slice” of
\rho
) such that

\forall\psi\in{\rm C}(\overline{\Omega}),\qquad\left[t\mapsto\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\psi(x)\xdif\rho_{t}(x)\right]\in{\rm C}(\left[0,1\right])

(1.10)

and

\forall\psi\in\xLone_{\rho}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}),\qquad\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif\rho(t,x)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif\rho_{t}(x)\right)\xdif t.

(1.11)

Given a measure on paths
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
such that
\int_{\Gamma}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{\infty}\xdif\sigma(h,\xi)<+\infty
, one may define the family of measures
(e_{t})_{\sharp}{\sigma}\in\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega})
, for
t\in\left[0,1\right]
, by

\forall\psi\in{\rm C}(\overline{\Omega}),\qquad\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\psi(x)\xdif[(e_{t})_{\sharp}{\sigma}](x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\int_{\Gamma}h(t)\psi(\xi(t))\xdif\sigma(h,\xi).

(1.12)

Formally,
(e_{t})_{\sharp}{\sigma}
is the image measure of
\sigma
by the evaluation at time
t
. That family is narrowly continuous, and as we explain in Theorem
\Rref
thm: Theta properties below, it is the evaluation (disintegration) of some measure
\Theta(\sigma)\in{\rm C_{w}}(\left[0,1\right];\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega}))
.

The continuity equation.

In the rest of the paper, we use the following distributional definition of the continuity equation, formally

\partial_{t}\rho+\operatorname{div}(\rho v)=g\rho,

(1.13)

which expresses mass variation (or mass conservation if
g=0
).

{dfntn}

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
be a measure. We say that
\rho
satisfies the
continuity equation
if there exists
v\in\xLone_{{\left|\rho\right|}}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}};\xR^{d})
,
g\in\xLone_{{\left|\rho\right|}}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
such that

\forall\psi\in{\rm C}_{c}^{1}(\left]0,1\right[\times\overline{\Omega}),\qquad\int[\partial_{t}\psi+\nabla\psi\cdot v+\psi g]\xdif\rho=0.

(1.14)

2.
Preliminary results

As previously mentioned, the main function space of this work is the space of measures on paths. In particular,
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
where
\Gamma\subset{\Gamma_{0}}
is the set of continuous weighted paths in
\overline{\Omega}
, modelling particles with (varying) mass
h(t)
at location
\xi(t)
defined by

{\Gamma_{0}}=\left\{\,\gamma=(h,\xi)\mid h\in{\rm C}(\left[0,1\right]),\ \xi\colon\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\overline{\Omega},\ \xi|_{\{h\neq 0\}}\text{ is continuous}\,\right\}.

(2.1)

For technical reasons we permit curves
\xi
which may not be continuous at points
t
where
h(t)=0
. Intuitively, the location
\xi(t)
is not necessarily meaningful if the particle has no mass and cannot be observed.

In this section we review the necessary assumptions for
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
to be a sufficiently well-behaved space. Firstly we require
\Gamma
to be a complete separable metric space. We follow the suggestion of
[
9
, Prop. 3.6]
who used the flat metric on the space of measures
\left\{\,h\delta_{\xi}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})\mid(h,\xi)\in\Gamma,\ h\geq 0\,\right\}
for a particular
\Gamma\subset{\Gamma_{0}}
. We use the isometric space
({\Gamma_{0}},\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma})
, the properties of which are given by the following lemma.

{lmm}

Define
\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}\colon{\Gamma_{0}}\times{\Gamma_{0}}\to\left[0,+\infty\right[
by

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}((h_{1},\xi_{1}),(h_{2},\xi_{2}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sup_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}\operatorname{d}_{F}((h_{1}(t),\xi_{1}(t)),(h_{2}(t),\xi_{2}(t)))\qquad\text{where}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}_{F}((r_{1},x_{1}),(r_{2},x_{2}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|&r_{1}r_{2}\leq 0\text{ or }|x_{1}-x_{2}|\geq 2\\
|r_{1}-r_{2}|+\min(|r_{1}|,|r_{2}|)|x_{1}-x_{2}|&\qquad\text{else,}\end{cases}

(2.3)

then
(\nicefrac{{{\Gamma_{0}}}}{{\sim}},\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma})
is a complete separable metric space where

(h_{1},\xi_{1})\sim(h_{2},\xi_{2})\iff h_{1}=h_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\forall t\in\{h_{1}\neq 0\},\quad\xi_{1}(t)=\xi_{2}(t).

(2.4)

Convergence of a sequence
\gamma_{n}=(h_{n},\xi_{n})\in{\Gamma_{0}}
in the metric
\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}
can equivalently be stated as:

\left[\gamma_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}}}{{\to}}(h,\xi)\right]\iff\Big[h_{n}\to h\text{ in }{\rm C}(\left[0,1\right])\text{ and for all $\varepsilon>0$, }\xi_{n}\to\xi\text{ in }{\rm C}(\{|h|\geq\varepsilon\})\Big]

(2.5)

Furthermore, for any
\psi\in{\rm C}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
, we have
\Psi\in{\rm C}(\left[0,1\right]\times{\Gamma_{0}})
where

\forall t\in\left[0,1\right],\ (h,\xi)\in{\Gamma_{0}},\qquad\Psi(t,h,\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}h(t)\psi(t,\xi(t)).

(2.6)

The proof is elementary but given in Appendix
\Rref
app: prelims for completeness as no specific reference could be found.

A key analytical tool in related prior works (
\cf
[
11
,
12
,
9
,
10
]
) is a mapping between measures
\rho\in\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
which satisfy the continuity equation, and measures on (weighted) paths
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}({\Gamma_{0}})
, making some structures become more apparent. We will now recap and expand on those previous results. The first theorem collects results from
[
3
,
9
]
and provides minor extensions for the scope of the current work. In particular, we remove the necessity for
h\geq 0
or elements
(h,\xi)
to satisfy further smoothness conditions.

{thrm}

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}({\Gamma_{0}})
. If
\int_{\Gamma_{0}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{1}\xdif|\sigma|(h,\xi)<+\infty
, then there is a unique finite Borel measure
\Theta(\sigma)\in\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
such that

\forall\psi\in{\rm C}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}),\qquad\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif\Theta(\sigma)(t,x)=\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\psi(t,\xi(t))\xdif t\right)\xdif\sigma(h,\xi).

(2.7)

Moreover,

(1)

The mapping
\Theta\colon\left\{\,\sigma\in\mathcal{M}({\Gamma_{0}})\mid\int_{\Gamma_{0}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{1}\xdif|\sigma|<+\infty\,\right\}\to\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
is linear.

(2)

Equality (
2.7
) holds for all
\psi\in\xLone_{|\Theta(\sigma)|}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
.

(3)

If
\int_{\Gamma_{0}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{\infty}\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}<+\infty
, then
\Theta(\sigma)\in{\rm C_{w}}(\left[0,1\right];\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega}))
.

(4)

Suppose
h,\xi\in{\rm AC}^{2}(\left[0,1\right])
for
\sigma
-a.e.
(h,\xi)\in{\Gamma_{0}}
. If there exist Borel measurable functions
v\colon{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\rightarrow\xR^{d}
and

such that

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(t)=g(t,\xi(t))h(t)\text{ for $\sigma$-a.e. $(h,\xi)$ and a.e. $t\in\left]0,1\right[$},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}(t)=v(t,\xi(t))\text{ for $\sigma$-a.e. $(h,\xi)$ and a.e. $t$ such that $h(t)\neq 0$},

(2.9)

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}(1+|v(t,\xi(t))|+|g(t,\xi(t))|)\,|h(t)|\,\xdif t\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}(h,\xi)<+\infty,

(2.10)

then
\int_{\Gamma_{0}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{\infty}\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}<+\infty
and
\Theta(\sigma)
satisfies the continuity equation (
1.14
).

Conversely, given
\rho\in\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
, if
\rho\geq 0
satisfies the continuity equation (
1.14
) and

\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}(1+|v(t,x)|^{2}+|g(t,x)|^{2})\xdif\rho(t,x)<+\infty,

(2.11)

then
\rho=\Theta(\sigma)
for some
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{+}({\Gamma_{0}})
such that (
2.8
)-(
2.10
) hold and
\int_{\Gamma_{0}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{\infty}\xdif\sigma<+\infty
.

These results are mainly proved in
[
3
,
9
]
, we extend to signed measures
\sigma
in the appendix (Theorem
\Rref

\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
to
\mathcal{M}({\Gamma_{0}})
is well-defined, and when
\Theta(\sigma)\in{\rm C_{w}}(\left[0,1\right];\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega}))
. Weak continuity is of practical importance in applications. Without it, for example in the application of microscopy, we could not consider one frame of video to correspond to a single instance in time. Unfortunately we have seen that not all
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}({\Gamma_{0}})
satisfy this smoothness requirement. However, in the next lemma we will confirm that, if
\Gamma\subset{\Gamma_{0}}
is sufficiently “small”, then
\Theta(\sigma)\in{\rm C_{w}}(\left[0,1\right];\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega}))
for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
due to the implicit assumption that
{\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert}<+\infty
. A related property is the continuity of the operator
\Theta
which we also confirm.

{lmm}

For each
p\in\left[1,+\infty\right]
define the set

\Gamma_{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left\{\,\gamma=(h,\xi)\in{\Gamma_{0}}\mid{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{p}\leq 1\,\right\},

(2.12)

then

\left\{\,\Theta(\sigma)\mid\sigma\in\mathcal{M}({\Gamma_{0}}),\ \int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{p}\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}<+\infty\,\right\}=\left\{\,\Theta(\widehat{\sigma})\mid\widehat{\sigma}\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{p})\,\right\}

Furthermore, if

\forall t\in\left[0,1\right]
,
(e_{t})_{\sharp}{\colon}\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{\infty})\to\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega})
is also narrowly continuous.

In particular, sequentially we have that, for any sequence
\sigma^{n}\xrightharpoonup{*}\sigma
narrowly in
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{p})
:

\displaystyle\text{for all }p\in\left[1,+\infty\right],

\displaystyle\qquad\Theta(\sigma^{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\Theta(\sigma)\text{ narrowly in }\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}),

(2.14)

\displaystyle\text{if }p=+\infty,\ \forall t\in\left[0,1\right],

\displaystyle\qquad(e_{t})_{\sharp}{\sigma^{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}(e_{t})_{\sharp}{\sigma}\text{ narrowly in }\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega}).

(2.15)

The proof of this lemma is found in Lemma

]
.

{rmrk}

Note that the definition of
\Gamma_{p}
is consistent with the relation
\sim
, so

is also a metric space. In the rest of the paper, we require that
\Gamma
is a closed subset of
\nicefrac{{\Gamma_{\infty}}}{{\sim}}
in order for it to be a complete separable metric space with

.

Summarising the results of this section, in the remainder of this work we want to use a domain

such that
\Theta|_{D}
has nice properties with respect to the space
{\rm C_{w}}(\left[0,1\right];\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega}))
. The combination of Theorem
\Rref
thm: Theta properties and Lemma
\Rref
lmma: Gamma_infty vs Gamma_1 show that it is sufficient to consider either
D\subset\left\{\,\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{+}({\Gamma_{0}})\mid\int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{\infty}\xdif\sigma<\infty\,\right\}
or simply
D\subset\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma_{\infty})
. Analytically we will always consider
D\subset\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma_{\infty})
as it is more concise, although numerically either convention is equivalent. The generality of allowing any closed subset
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{\infty}
allows us to treat different applications with the same analysis, for example:

\bf{\Gamma\subset\left\{\,(h,\xi)\in\Gamma_{\infty}\mid h\equiv 1\,\right\}}
:

This enforces balanced transport (
e.g.
the Benamou-Brenier example
[
11
]
). If
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)
, then
\Theta(\sigma)\geq 0
and mass is preserved on paths (
e.g.

t\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\xdif\Theta(\sigma)_{t}
is constant).

\bf{\Gamma\subset\left\{\,(h,\xi)\in\Gamma_{\infty}\mid h\in{\rm C}(\left[0,1\right];\left[0,1\right])\,\right\}}
:

This allows unbalanced transport of non-negative mass (
e.g.
the

Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao example
[
9
]
). We still have
\Theta(\sigma)\geq 0
, but
t\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\xdif\Theta(\sigma)_{t}
is not (necessarily) constant. In particular, mass can be created or destroyed (continuously) at any time.

\bf{\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{\infty}}
:

In the general case
\Theta(\sigma)
is a general signed measure, even when
\sigma\geq 0
. In words,
\sigma
can give positive weight to curves with negative mass. The only constraint is that
\Theta(\sigma)\in{\rm C_{w}}(\left[0,1\right];\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega}))
is continuous in time.

3.
Core variational problem

In this work we focus on inverse problems with dynamical but discrete-time structure. In particular, there exist observation times
t_{j}\in\left[0,1\right]
,
j=0,\ldots,T
and narrowly continuous linear operators
A_{j}\colon\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega})\to\xR^{m}
. The operators
A_{j}
are described by
a_{i}^{j}\in{\rm C}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

\forall\rho\in\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega}),\ i=1,\ldots,m,\ j=0,\ldots,T,\qquad(A_{j}\rho)_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\int_{\overline{\Omega}}a_{i}^{j}(x)\xdif\rho(x).

(3.1)

As stated at the end of Section
\Rref
sec: preliminary, we work with a closed set of curves

\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left\{\,\gamma=(h,\xi)\mid h\in{\rm C}(\left[0,1\right];\left[-1,1\right]),\ \xi\colon\left[0,1\right]\to\overline{\Omega},\ \xi|_{\{h\neq 0\}}\text{ is continuous}\,\right\},

(3.2)

so that
\forall t\in\left[0,1\right]
, the map
(e_{t})_{\sharp}{}\colon\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)\to\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega})
is narrowly continuous. We therefore choose a data fidelity
\operatorname{F}\colon\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)\to\left[0,+\infty\right[
of the form

\text{for some convex }\operatorname{F}_{j}\in\xCtwo(\xR^{m};\left[0,+\infty\right[),\qquad\operatorname{F}(\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=0}^{T}\operatorname{F}_{j}(A_{j}\left[(e_{t_{j}})_{\sharp}{\sigma}\right]).

(3.3)

For lower semi-continuous
w,\varphi\colon\Gamma\to\left[0,+\infty\right]
define
\operatorname{W}\colon\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)\to\left]-\infty,+\infty\right]
,
D\subset\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)
by

\forall\sigma\in D,\quad\operatorname{W}(\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\int_{\Gamma}w(\gamma)\xdif\sigma(\gamma)\quad\text{where}\quad D\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left\{\,\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)\quad\mid\quad\sigma\geq 0,\quad\int_{\Gamma}\varphi(\gamma)\xdif\sigma\leq 1\,\right\}.

(3.4)

We consider minimising the energy
\operatorname{E}\colon D\to\left]-\infty,+\infty\right]
defined by

\forall\sigma\in D,\qquad\operatorname{E}(\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\operatorname{F}(\sigma)+\operatorname{W}(\sigma).

(3.5)

The motivation behind this in an Inverse Problems setting is that
\operatorname{F}
represents a smooth data fidelity with linear observations recorded at times
t_{j}
, and the combination of
\operatorname{W}
and
D
represent a regularisation of the problem. The choice of
\varphi
(hence of
D
) is often made to ensure the well-posedness of the model, but we are mostly interested in cases where the constraint
\int_{\Gamma}\varphi(\gamma)\xdif\sigma\leq 1
is not active, so that the choice of
\varphi
has no impact on the set of minimisers.

3.1.
Existence of sparse minimisers

Any choice of energy in this framework leads to a sparse reconstruction in the space of curves.

{thrm}

If
A_{j}
are given by (
3.1
) and
\varphi,w\colon\Gamma\to\left[0,+\infty\right]
are lower semi-continuous, then
\operatorname{F}
and
\operatorname{E}
are lower semi-continuous. Furthermore, recall
\operatorname{F}
is bounded below. If
w
or
\varphi
have compact sub-levelsets, and
\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\varphi(\gamma)>0
, then
\operatorname{E}|_{D}
has compact sub-levelsets. There exists a choice of minimiser
\sigma^{*}\in\argmin_{\sigma\in D}\operatorname{E}(\sigma)
such that

\text{for some }a_{i}\geq 0,\ \gamma^{i}\in\Gamma,\qquad\sigma^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}\delta_{\gamma^{i}}

(3.6)

for some
s\leq m(T+1)+1
. If in addition
\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma^{*}<1
, then
s\leq m(T+1)
.

The proof is given in Appendix
\Rref
app: structure of E. The smaller value of
s
will often be valid in practice, for example any choice
\varphi(\gamma)\leq\frac{w(\gamma)}{\operatorname{E}(0)+1}
is always sufficient.

{rmrk}

The Benamou-Brenier example from Section
\Rref
sec:BB example intro can be formulated in this setting with

\Gamma=\left\{\,(h,\xi)\in{\Gamma_{0}}\mid h\equiv 1,\xi\in{\rm AC}^{2}(\left[0,1\right];\overline{\Omega})\,\right\},\quad\operatorname{F}_{j}(A_{j}\rho)=\frac{1}{2}{\left\lVert A_{j}\rho-b_{j}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad w(h,\xi)=\int_{0}^{1}\alpha+\frac{\beta}{2}|\xi^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\xdif t.

(3.7)

More details are given in Section
\Rref
sec:WFR example where the Benamou-Brenier example is the limiting case
\delta\to+\infty
. The choice of
\varphi
in
[
10
]
was equivalent to
\varphi(\gamma)=\frac{w(\gamma)}{\operatorname{E}(0)}
, whereas we suggest the default of
\varphi(\gamma)=\frac{\alpha}{\operatorname{E}(0)}
which is easier to analyse. Both functions are strictly positive but sufficiently small to ensure
\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma^{*}<1
.

3.2.
Discrete-time formulation

Recall that
\Gamma\subset L^{\infty}(\left[0,1\right];\left[-1,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega})
is a space of continuous-time curves, denote the discrete-time space

\widetilde{\Gamma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left\{\,(\gamma(t_{0}),\ldots,\gamma(t_{T}))\mid\gamma\in\Gamma\,\right\}\subset(\left[-1,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega})^{T+1}.

(3.8)

Until now we have formulated
\operatorname{E}
as a function of measures

of

For the remainder of this section, without loss of generality, we assume there is a minimiser

which is also a minimiser of the energy considered in (

must be supported on “geodesics” of

{lmm}

Suppose

of (

The first part of the proof uses a measurable choice theorem from

{lmm}

There exists a measurable function

Proof of Lemma
\Rref

Let the set

The proof will show the contradiction that

\operatorname{F}(g_{\sharp}\sigma^{*})=\operatorname{F}(\sigma^{*})

\displaystyle(A_{j}(e_{t_{j}})_{\sharp}{(g_{\sharp})\sigma^{*}})=\int_{\overline{\Omega}}a_{i}^{j}(x)\xdif(e_{t_{j}})_{\sharp}{(g_{\sharp})\sigma^{*}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}h(t_{j})a_{i}^{j}(\xi(t_{j}))\xdif\sigma^{*}(\gamma)\quad\text{where}\quad(h,\xi)=\gamma

(3.13)

(3.14)

Restricting to this domain of curves is much more computationally convenient without losing analytical accuracy.

4.
Frank-Wolfe convergence

While Theorem
\Rref
thm: structure of E guarantees an analytical structure of minimisers, we must now choose a reconstruction algorithm which is capable of taking advantage of this structure. As previously stated, we will use a variant of the Frank-Wolfe algorithm to take advantage of the sparse structure of reconstructions.

4.1.
The Frank-Wolfe algorithm

In order to derive a variant of the Frank-Wolfe algorithm with inexact or stochastic steps, we first need to highlight a few properties which, to the best of our knowledge, have not been stated in the literature. We refer the reader to
[
25
]
for a thorough introduction to the Frank-Wolfe algorithm.
For the sake of generality and future reference, we work in an abstract setting, assuming that we want to minimize a convex function
f\colon D\rightarrow\xR
, where
D
is a nonempty convex set of a Hausdorff locally convex vector space
\mathcal{X}
.

Standard results in convex analysis
[
2
, Chap. 7]
ensure that for all
x\in D
and all
h\in\left(D-x\right)
, the directional derivative

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x;h)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(x+th)-f(x)}{t}

exists in
\xR\cup\{-\infty\}
and is a convex function of
h
. The main steps of the Frank-Wolfe algorithm are given in Algorithm
\Rref
alg: abstract FW. The standard choice
[
24
,
18
]
uses the
Linear Minimisation Oracle
defined as

\displaystyle s^{n+1}=\scalebox{.9}{\rm LMO}(x^{n})\in\argmin_{s\in D}\left(f(x^{n})+f^{\prime}(x^{n};s-x^{n})\right)=\argmin_{s\in D}f^{\prime}(x^{n};s-x^{n}).

(4.1)

In the large majority of cases the existence of the

LMO

is implied by
f
being Gâteaux-differentiable and
D
compact.
The analysis in
[
25
]
stresses that Algorithm
\Rref
alg: abstract FW with the linear minimisation oracle (
4.1
) yields a
minimising sequence
,
\ie

\lim_{n\to+\infty}f(x^{n})=\inf_{D}f
, provided the curvature of
f
is finite,

C_{f}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in D,\widetilde{x}\in D\\
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right[\end{subarray}}\frac{f(x+\lambda(\widetilde{x}-x))-f(x)-\lambda f^{\prime}(x;\widetilde{x}-x)}{\lambda^{2}}<+\infty.

(4.2)

In situations where the

LMO

is not easy to compute, we can instead choose any
s^{n+1}\in D
and measure its suitability using the primal-dual gap

\forall x,s\in D,\qquad\gap(x;s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}f^{\prime}(x;s-x)-\inf_{\widetilde{s}\in D}f^{\prime}(x;\widetilde{s}-x).

(4.3)

In general
\gap\geq 0
and
\gap(x;\scalebox{.9}{\rm LMO}(x))=0
, suppose
\gap(x^{n};s^{n+1})\leq\varepsilon_{n}
for some controlled error
\varepsilon_{n}\geq 0
. It has previously been shown in
[
25
, Thm. 1]
that
x^{n}
is a minimising sequence whenever
\varepsilon_{n}=O(1/n)
.

In our context, the guarantee
\varepsilon_{n}=O(1/n)
would still be prohibitive for large
n
. The linear minimisation oracle consists of finding a curve
\gamma\in\Gamma
which minimises a certain energy, see Section
\Rref
sec: fw back ip below. Instead, we propose to use random discretisations of the domain so that implicitly
\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=0
, without a guaranteed rate. In comparison to the result of
[
25
]
, the relaxed assumption on
\varepsilon_{n}
is accounted for by the linesearch in Line
\Rref
alg: abstract linesearch which implicitly selects large/small steps when
\varepsilon_{n}
is correspondingly large/small,
\ie
when we have a lucky/unlucky draw. Our only assumptions on
f
are that it is Gâteaux differentiable with bounded curvature (
C_{f}<+\infty
) and
\gap(x;s)<+\infty
for all
x,s\in D
. Then, in the case that
\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=0
(possibly almost surely), we show that
x^{n}
is a minimising sequence (almost surely).

One final aspect of our algorithm is the freedom which is granted in Line
\Rref
alg: abstract improve: one may choose any point which has lower energy than the one provided by the linesearch. This is now a standard addition to the Frank-Wolfe algorithm to enable much faster practical convergence, see for instance
[
13
,
6
,
19
]
. We exploit this in Section
\Rref
sec: results.

1:

Choose
x^{0}\in D
,
n\leftarrow 0
,

2:

repeat

3:

Choose
s^{n+1}\in D

\triangleright
oracle

4:

\lambda_{n}\leftarrow\argmin_{\lambda\in\left[0,1\right]}f((1-\lambda)x^{n}+\lambda s^{n+1})

\triangleright
exact linesearch

5:

Choose
x^{n+1}
such that
f(x^{n+1})\leq f((1-\lambda_{n})x^{n}+\lambda_{n}s^{n+1})

\triangleright

6:

n\leftarrow n+1

7:

until
converged

Algorithm 1

Abstract Frank-Wolfe algorithm

To ease the analysis of the randomised algorithm, we first analyse the deterministic version.

Proposition 1
.

Let
f
,
D
be as above, and assume that
C_{f}<+\infty
(see (
4.2
)). If

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\gap(x^{n};s^{n+1})=0,\quad\text{\ie}\quad\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\left(f^{\prime}(x^{n};s^{n+1}-x^{n})-\min_{D}f^{\prime}(x^{n};\cdot-x^{n})\right)=0,

(4.4)

then Algorithm
\Rref
alg: abstract FW yields a minimizing sequence.

Proof.

First observe from Lines
\Rref
alg: abstract linesearch and
\Rref
alg: abstract improve, and by definition of
C_{f}
that

\displaystyle\forall\lambda\in\left[0,1\right],\quad

\displaystyle f(x^{n+1})\leq f((1-\lambda)x^{n}+\lambda s^{n+1})\leq f(x^{n})+\lambda f^{\prime}(x^{n};s^{n+1}-x^{n})+C_{f}\lambda^{2}

(4.5)

hence

\displaystyle f(x^{n+1})-f(x^{n})-\lambda\min_{D}\left(f^{\prime}(x^{n};\cdot-x^{n})\right)\leq\lambda\gap(x^{n};s^{n+1})+C_{f}\lambda^{2}.

(4.6)

Note from the
\lambda=0
case we have
f(x^{n})\geq f(x^{n+1})
for each
n
, therefore it is clear that
(f(x^{n}))_{n\in\xN}
converges to some limit
\ell\geq\inf_{x\in D}f(x)\geq-\infty
.
We are required to show that
\ell=\inf_{x\in D}f(x)
.

The case
\ell=-\infty
is trivial, otherwise we also have
\lim_{n\to+\infty}f(x^{n+1})-f(x^{n})=0
. Now, taking the
\liminf
on both sides of (
4.6
) gives

\liminf_{n\to+\infty}-\lambda\min_{D}\left(f^{\prime}(x^{n};\cdot-x^{n})\right)\leq C_{f}\lambda^{2}.

(4.7)

Dividing by
\lambda\to 0^{+}
, we obtain
\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\min_{D}\left(f^{\prime}(x^{n};\cdot-x^{n})\right)\geq 0
. On the other hand, by convexity,

\displaystyle\forall x\in D,\quad f(x)\geq\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\left[f(x^{n})+f^{\prime}(x^{n};x-x^{n})\right]\geq\ell+\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\min_{D}\left(f^{\prime}(x^{n};\cdot-x^{n})\right)\geq\ell.

(4.8)

Since
x\in D
is arbitrary, we deduce that
\ell=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x^{n})=\inf_{D}f
, as required.
∎

Proposition 2

Let

If for all

p_{n}(\varepsilon)>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\gap(X^{n};s^{n+1})<\varepsilon|\mathcal{S}^{n})\geq p_{n}(\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{almost surely,}

and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}(\varepsilon)=+\infty
,
then
(f(X^{n}))_{n\in\xN}
is a minimizing sequence almost surely.

The above proposition is a consequence of the following lemma, setting
G^{n+1}=\gap(X^{n};s^{n+1})
.

{lmm}

Let
(\mathcal{S}^{n})_{n\in\xN}
be a filtration, and
(G^{n})_{n\in\xN}
a family of random variables such that for each
n,m\in\xN
,
n\leq m
,
G^{n}
is
\mathcal{S}^{m}
-measurable. If for all
\varepsilon>0
,
n\in\xN
, there is some deterministic
p_{n}(\varepsilon)\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(G^{n+1}<\varepsilon|\mathcal{S}^{n})\geq p_{n}(\varepsilon)\quad\mbox{almost surely}

(4.9)

and
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}(\varepsilon)=+\infty
, then
\liminf_{n\to\infty}G^{n}\leq 0
almost surely.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. For all
N,M\in\xN
with
M\geq N
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n\geq N}\{G^{n}\geq\varepsilon\}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n=N}^{M+1}\{G^{n}\geq\varepsilon\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{G^{M+1}>\varepsilon\}}\left(\prod_{n=N}^{M}\mathds{1}_{\{G^{n}\geq\varepsilon\}}\right)\right]

(4.10)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left(\mathds{1}_{\{G^{M+1}\geq\varepsilon\}}|\mathcal{S}^{M}\right)\prod_{n=N}^{M}\mathds{1}_{\{G^{n}\geq\varepsilon\}}\right]

(4.11)

\displaystyle\leq(1-p_{M+1}(\varepsilon))\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n\geq N}\{G^{n}\geq\varepsilon\}\right)

(4.12)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{n=N}^{M+1}(1-p_{n}(\varepsilon))

(4.13)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{n=N}^{M+1}p_{n}(\varepsilon)\right).

(4.14)

Letting
M\to+\infty
, we get
\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n\geq N}\{G^{n}\geq\varepsilon\}\right)=0
, that is
\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{n\geq N}\{G^{n}<\varepsilon\}\right)=1
. As a result,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}G^{n}\leq 0\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{k\in\xN^{*}}\bigcap_{N\in\xN}\bigcup_{n\geq N}\left\{G^{n}<\frac{1}{k}\right\}\right)=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\lim_{N\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{n\geq N}\left\{G^{n}<\frac{1}{k}\right\}\right)=1.

∎

To summarise, the main convergence requirement of Proposition
\Rref
prop: inexact FW is to guarantee
\liminf_{n\to+\infty}G^{n}\leq 0
. Our solution to this is to use random discretisations so that the stochastic variant of Algorithm
\Rref
alg: abstract FW still converges asymptotically (almost surely), but the complexity of each individual iteration remains low. A similar idea in the setting of stochastic Frank-Wolfe was pursued in
[
29
]
where their proof relies on what is called Assumption P.8, in our notation this requires the sum
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}G^{n}<+\infty
to be finite almost surely, where
\lambda_{n}
is chosen deterministically. To apply this algorithm in our setting we would therefore need to bound the magnitude of
G^{n}
relative to the
\apriori
choice of
\lambda_{n}
. With Proposition
\Rref
prop: liminf, we overcome this limitation with the linesearch for
\lambda
, so the only remaining requirement is to guarantee a uniform probability of achieving a good search direction.

4.3.
Back to the dynamic inverse problem

We consider again the setting of Section
\Rref
sec: generalisation.
The function
\operatorname{E}
is convex on
D
, and we note that, for each

, its directional derivative is given by

\displaystyle\forall\nu\in D,\quad\operatorname{E}^{\prime}(\sigma;\nu-\sigma)=\int_{\Gamma}\left(\operatorname{F}^{\prime}(\sigma)(\gamma)+\operatorname{W}^{\prime}(\sigma)(\gamma)\right)\xdif(\nu-\sigma)(\gamma),

with
\operatorname{W}^{\prime}(\sigma)=\left[\gamma\mapsto w(\gamma)\right]
and

\displaystyle\operatorname{F}^{\prime}(\sigma)=\left[(h,\xi)\mapsto\sum_{j=0}^{T}h(t_{j})\eta_{j}(\xi(t_{j}))\right]\in{\rm C_{b}}(\Gamma),\quad\text{where}\quad\eta_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}A_{j}^{*}\nabla\operatorname{F}_{j}(A_{j}(e_{t_{j}})_{\sharp}{\sigma}).

In particular, note that we can write
\operatorname{E}^{\prime}(\sigma;\mu-\sigma)=\int_{\Gamma}\operatorname{E}^{\prime}(\sigma)\xdif[\mu-\sigma]
with
\operatorname{E}^{\prime}(\sigma)\colon\Gamma\to\xR
. This structure enables us to prove that
\operatorname{E}
satisfies the major requirement of Section
\Rref
sec: FW convergence, that
\inf_{\mu\in D}\operatorname{E}^{\prime}(\sigma;\mu-\sigma)>-\infty
for all
\sigma\in D
. To do this, we show that the infimum is always achieved by an extreme point of

.

{lmm}

Suppose
\varphi,w\colon\Gamma\to\left[0,+\infty\right]
are lower semi-continuous. If
\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\varphi(\gamma)>0
and either
\varphi
or
w
have compact sub-levelsets, then

is lower semi-continuous and coercive on
D
. Moreover it has minimiser of the form

(4.15)

where
\eta(h,\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=0}^{T}h(t_{j})\eta_{j}(\xi(t_{j}))\in{\rm C_{b}}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Recall the definition of

LMO

,

\scalebox{.9}{\rm LMO}(\sigma)\in\argmin_{\widetilde{\sigma}\in D}\widetilde{\operatorname{E}}(\widetilde{\sigma})\quad\text{where}\quad\widetilde{\operatorname{E}}(\widetilde{\sigma})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\eta+w\right)\xdif\widetilde{\sigma}.

(4.16)

The properties of
D
come from Lemma
\Rref
lmma: extreme point computation, in particular
D
is convex, closed and bounded, so

and

is lower semi-continuous (Lemma

.

Case
D
is compact:

If
\varphi
has compact sub-levelsets, then
D
is compact by Lemma
\Rref
lmma: extreme point computation. Bauer’s principle therefore states that there exists a point

(4.17)

Else:

Otherwise,
w
has compact sub-levelsets, so the sub-levelset

is compact by Theorem

.

Suppose for contradiction that there exists
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\in D\setminus\{\sigma^{*}\}
with
\sigma^{*}\in\left]\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\right[
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\sigma^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{1}
and we define

Consider the function

In both cases we see there is a choice

\operatorname{Ext}\left(D\right)=\{0\}\cup\left\{\,\varphi(\gamma)^{-1}\delta_{\gamma}\mid\varphi(\gamma)<+\infty\,\right\},

(4.20)

so we deduce (

) as required.
∎

This lemma is also useful for applying the stochastic Frank-Wolfe algorithm, Lemma

(4.21)

5.
Dynamical programming method

Throughout Section
\Rref
sec: FW convergence we have shown that we can find a minimising sequence to
\operatorname{E}
from Section
\Rref
sec: generalisation by repeatedly evaluating a simplified linear oracle. In particular, we compute the minimiser from Lemma
\Rref
lmma: well defined linear oracle, but over a discretised domain. In Section
\Rref
sec: discrete time formulation we motivated discretising in time to
\widetilde{\Gamma}=(\left[-1,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega})^{T+1}
without loss of precision, now we also discretise in space using the domain
\Lambda\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\prod_{j=0}^{T}\Lambda_{j}
for some discrete sets
\Lambda_{j}\subset\left[-1,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}
(“grids” in the mass-location space). Whilst
\Lambda
is much smaller than
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, naive computation time for
\gamma^{*}
still scales exponentially in
T
. We propose to compute this discrete minimiser efficiently using dynamical programming, for which we require two final simplifications:

\displaystyle\varphi(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}

\displaystyle\text{ for some constant }\varphi_{0}>0,\text{ and}

(A1)

\displaystyle w(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\step_{j}(\gamma(t_{j-1}),\gamma(t_{j}))

\displaystyle\text{ for some }\step_{j}\colon(\left[-1,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega})^{2}\to\xR.

(A2)

{rmrk}

For the Benamou-Brenier example, these assumptions are satisfied by the choice

\varphi_{0}=\frac{\alpha}{\operatorname{E}(0)},\qquad\step_{j}(\gamma(t_{j-1}),\gamma(t_{j}))=\alpha(t_{j}-t_{j-1})+\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{|\xi(t_{j})-\xi(t_{j-1})|^{2}}{t_{j}-t_{j-1}}

(5.1)

for all
\gamma=(h,\xi)\in\Gamma
.
We will see later in Section
\Rref
sec:WFR discrete-time energy that these assumptions are also satisfied in the Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao example. In particular, the step function of the Benamou-Brenier penalty is Wasserstein optimal transport, and the step function of the dynamic Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao penalty is the static Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao penalty.

Under assumptions (
A1
) and (
A2
), the discretised optimisation problem (
4.15
) can be greatly simplified to

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{Y}\left\{\,\sum_{j=0}^{T}\eta_{j}(Y_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{T}\step_{j}(Y_{j-1},Y_{j})\mid Y\in\prod_{j=0}^{T}\Lambda_{j}\,\right\}

(5.2)

\displaystyle\quad\text{where}\quad\forall j=0,\ldots,T,\qquad\eta_{j}(h_{j},\xi_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}h_{j}[A_{j}^{*}\nabla\operatorname{F}_{j}(A_{j}(e_{t_{j}})_{\sharp}{\sigma})](\xi_{j}).

(5.3)

This minimisation problem can now be formulated as computing the minimal path on a weighted, directed, acyclic graph. The vertices are the points
(t_{j},y)
for
y\in\Lambda_{j}
, the edge weights are given by
\eta_{j}
and
\step_{j}
, and the time index provides an ordering and prevents cycles in the graph. Algorithms for computing minimal paths on directed acyclic graphs are well studied, the complexity bound is given below.

{thrm}
[
[
17
, Sec. 24.2]
]
Suppose assumptions (
A1
) and (
A2
) hold and
|\Lambda_{j}|\leq N
for each
j
, then
\gamma^{*}
can be computed with complexity

\text{total time}=O\left(N\sum_{j=0}^{T}\operatorname{cost}(\eta_{j})+N^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\operatorname{cost}(\step_{j})\right)

(5.4)

where
\operatorname{cost}(\eta_{j})
is the cost of evaluating
\eta_{j}
once
\etc
.

{rmrk}

In graph terminology, the two terms of (
5.4
) represent the number of vertices plus the number of edges respectively, asymptotically
O(NT)
and
O(N^{2}T)
respectively. If, for example, the paths have a maximum velocity
V
, then the number of edges per vertex is reduced from
N
to
O(N(VT^{-1})^{d})
. This results in a reduced total complexity of
O(NT+N^{2}T^{1-d}V^{d})
.

Finally we outline how the minimal path can be computed efficiently in our specific example by using dynamic programming. To do so, define the truncated energies and minimal paths:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\operatorname{E}}_{J}(Y)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\eta_{j}(Y_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\step_{j}(Y_{j-1},Y_{j}),

(5.5)

\displaystyle Y^{*}[y,J]

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{Y}\left\{\,\widetilde{\operatorname{E}}_{J}(Y)\mid Y\in\prod_{j=0}^{J}\Lambda_{j},\ Y_{J}=y\,\right\}

(5.6)

for each
J=0,\ldots,T
and
y\in\Lambda_{J}
. This generates
\gamma^{*}
through the computation

\gamma^{*}=Y^{*}[y^{*},T],\qquad y^{*}\in\argmin_{y\in\Lambda_{T}}\widetilde{\operatorname{E}}_{T}(Y^{*}[y,T]).

(5.7)

Observe that for all
J=1,\ldots,T
and
y\in\Lambda_{J}
,

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in\prod_{j=0}^{J}\Lambda_{j}\\
Y_{J}=y\end{subarray}}\widetilde{\operatorname{E}}_{J}(Y)

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in\prod_{j=0}^{J}\Lambda_{j}\end{subarray}}\left\{\widetilde{\operatorname{E}}_{J-1}(Y)+\eta_{J}(y)+\step_{J}(Y_{J-1},y)\right\}

(5.8)

\displaystyle=\eta_{J}(y)+\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{J-1}\end{subarray}}\Bigg\{\step_{J}(y^{\prime},y)+\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\in\prod_{j=0}^{J-1}\Lambda_{j}\\
Y_{J-1}=y^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\widetilde{\operatorname{E}}_{J-1}(Y)\Bigg\}.

(5.9)

In particular, we can choose
Y^{*}[y,J]
inductively to be

Y^{*}[y,J]=\begin{pmatrix}Y^{*}[y^{\prime},J-1]&y\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for}\quad y^{\prime}\in\argmin_{y^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{J-1}}\left[\step_{J}(y^{\prime},y)+\widetilde{\operatorname{E}}_{J-1}(Y^{*}[y^{\prime},J-1])\right].

(5.10)

For each
y
and
J
each of these steps requires
O(N)
computation, confirming the global complexity of
O(N^{2}T)
.

The unbalanced Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao example

The two numerical examples considered in this work use the Benamou-Brenier and Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao (WFR) penalties. The former has already been discussed in previous remarks and is a limiting case of the WFR penalty so we will not discuss it in further detail here.

The penalty considered in

such that for all

6.2.
Discrete-time formulation

Problems of the form (
6.8
) also have a discrete-time structure inherited from discrete-time data. The same argument from Section
3.2
is valid for the WFR penalty, although it is very hard to find the explicit form. Analytically the WFR penalty can be expressed in the form required for Assumption
\Rref
eq: step penalty. In particular, for

\step_{j}(\gamma_{j-1},\gamma_{j})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\left\{\,\int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_{j}}\left[\alpha+\frac{\beta}{2}|\xi^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+\frac{\beta\delta^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{h^{\prime}(t)}{h(t)}\right)^{2}\right]h(t)\xdif t\mid\gamma=(h,\xi),\begin{array}[]{c}\gamma(t_{j-1})=\gamma_{j-1},\\
\gamma(t_{j})=\gamma_{j}\end{array}\,\right\},

(6.21)

geodesics of the WFR penalty satisfy

w(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\left[\alpha+\frac{\beta}{2}|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{\beta\delta^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{h^{\prime}}{h}\right)^{2}\right]h\xdif t=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\step_{j}(\gamma(t_{j-1}),\gamma(t_{j})).

(6.22)

This formula can be verified with the Euler-Lagrange equation. The key point is that the left-hand side only involves up to first order derivatives so only the zeroth order constraints are needed to interpolate on intervals
\left]t_{j-1},t_{j}\right[
. The exact shape or formulae for the WFR-geodesics is not so convenient as for the Benamou-Brenier penalty (
\delta\to+\infty
), although for
\alpha=0
it is known from (
[
16
, Thm. 5.6]
)

\step_{j}((h_{j-1},\xi_{j-1}),(h_{j},\xi_{j}))=\frac{4\beta\delta^{2}}{t_{j}-t_{j-1}}\left[\tfrac{h_{j}+h_{j-1}}{2}-\sqrt{h_{j}h_{j-1}}\cos\left(\min\left(\tfrac{|\xi_{j}-\xi_{j-1}|}{2\delta},\pi\right)\right)\right].

(6.23)

More details can be found in Corollary 4.1(i) of the preprint (
https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.06430v2.pdf
) of
[
15
]
. In summary, the map
\gamma\mapsto\argmin_{\widetilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma}\left\{\,w(\widetilde{\gamma})\mid\widetilde{\gamma}(t_{j})=\gamma(t_{j})\,\right\}
is continuous,
\widetilde{h}(t)
is a simple quadratic on each
\left]t_{j},t_{j+1}\right[
, and
\widetilde{\xi}(t)
follows a straight line between
\xi(t_{j})
and
\xi(t_{j+1})
with speed varying like arctan.

7.3.
Observations on parameter choices

Both the random and uniform mesh algorithms have three parameters to choose: the multi-start parameter
k
, spatial resolution
N
, and mass resolution
M
. The first phantom (balanced or unbalanced) nicely highlights characteristics of the reconstructions but is too trivial numerically to compare different algorithm choices, all methods converge within a few iterations. For the second phantom we prioritised the trade-off of between energy and computation time.

The classical choice of
k=1
was always a competitive choice. Large
k
potentially enables the algorithm to find multiple atoms in one iteration, but slows down the sliding steps. We found that
k\in[1,5]
was a reasonable range depending on the sparsity of the signal to be recovered.

Choice of spatial resolution made the largest impact on performance. If the resolution is too small then the algorithm will not find new atoms, but computation time of the linear oracle scales with
N^{4}
. For the random mesh, we found that
N=25
was a good balance. For the uniform mesh the resolution is also a stopping criterion so we used the more conservative
N=256
and
512
for the balanced and unbalanced examples respectively.

In the unbalanced experiments the value of
M
had little effect. Although we don’t include this figure, we observed that the choice
M=0
was also competitive for our unbalanced phantom 2, achieving energies within
10^{-3}\%
of the best observed energy. It’s unclear whether this will generalise to more complicated examples, we chose
M=10
as a default. Computational complexity of the linear oracle also scales with
M^{2}
, so
M
should not be too large.

Both the random and uniform mesh algorithms have advantages over each other. The main advantages of a uniform mesh are computational: one can use a finer resolution (
\ie
larger
N
), and there is a clear stopping criterion. In practice this was a very reliable method without parameter tuning and was as fast as the random algorithm. The random algorithms have analytical guarantees of converging asymptotically to the true minimiser, and indeed it achieved the best observed energy in all but one of our experiments, performing noticeably better in the noisy case. The challenge is setting a stopping criterion, in phantom 2 of Figure
\Rref
fig: unbalanced convergence one sees several plateaux where the algorithm fails to find a descent direction for a number of iterations before continuing to descend.

Figure
\Rref
fig: spread convergence shows the random variation of the random mesh algorithm with different values of
N\in\{5,10,15\}
. Increasing
N
both decreases the spread and improves the performance of the algorithm. In the balanced example, the three algorithms complete 1000 iterations in the same time, showing that the linear oracle step is a small part of the total time. On the other hand, with larger
M
the
N=25
algorithm is nearly 10 times slower than
N=10
, indicating that the
O(N^{4}M^{2})
cost is starting to dominate the computation time. It’s possible that
N=5
is slower than
N=10
for the unbalanced example because the sliding step has to work much harder to find local minima.

8.
Conclusion

The main contribution of this work was to extend a variational model proposed by Bredies
et al.
in order to accelerate the reconstruction of sparse measures in dynamical inverse problems. Using algorithms developed for computing shortest paths on graphs, we can improve the speed by a factor of 100 while still finding lower energy solutions. This allows us to process new unbalanced examples where the mass of curves is not constant in time, our proposed algorithms still recover good reconstructions in a reasonable amount of time.

We also presented new analysis of a stochastic variant of Frank-Wolfe which guarantees the convergence of our algorithm (in energy) to a globally optimal solution. This is supported by our experiments where we see that the random-mesh algorithm achieves the lowest energy in almost every example.

One feature of the algorithm in
[
10
]
which we did not take advantage of was the idea of importance sampling. We chose points
y\in\Lambda_{j}
uniformly randomly, whereas it is likely to be beneficial to choose
y
such that
\eta_{j}(y)
is small. As an example, if the step functions
\step_{j}
satisfy the triangle inequality, then this bias could be implemented by choosing points
y
such that
y
is a local minima of

\widetilde{y}\mapsto\eta_{j}(\widetilde{y})+\step_{j-1}(y,\widetilde{y})+\step_{j}(\widetilde{y},y).

(8.1)

In Algorithm
\Rref
alg: final algorithm, implementing such a sliding step on
\Lambda_{j}
between lines 4 and 5 would preserve the analytical properties of the current algorithm, whilst possibly improving practical performance. However, it is also possible that the sliding step already present (
e.g.
line 7) is powerful enough to find these improved mesh points after computing
\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}
, without the extra help beforehand. It is likely that the benefits depend on the application, particularly on the smoothness of the operators
A_{j}
.

{lmm}

[Lemma

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\sup_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}\operatorname{d}_{F}((h_{1}(t),\xi_{1}(t)),(h_{2}(t),\xi_{2}(t)))\qquad\text{where}

(A.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}_{F}((r_{1},x_{1}),(r_{2},x_{2}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|&r_{1}r_{2}\leq 0\text{ or }|x_{1}-x_{2}|\geq 2\\

(A.2)

then

(A.3)

Convergence of a sequence
\gamma_{n}=(h_{n},\xi_{n})\in{\Gamma_{0}}
in the metric
\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}

\left[\gamma_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}}}{{\to}}(h,\xi)\right]\iff\Big[h_{n}\to h\text{ in }{\rm C}(\left[0,1\right])\text{ and for all $\varepsilon>0$, }\xi_{n}\to\xi\text{ in }{\rm C}(\{|h|\geq\varepsilon\})\Big]

Proof.

Complete metric space.

In

9

{\Gamma_{0}}

9

(A.6)

It follows that

Convergence.

First, note from the definition that for all

\left|\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\psi(t,\xi(t))\xdif t\xdif\sigma(h,\xi)\right|\leq{\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert}_{\infty}\int_{\Gamma_{0}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{1}\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}.

(A.31)

By the Riesz representation theorem, that linear form is represented by a unique Radon measure
\Theta(\sigma)\in\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
. This confirms that
\Theta
satisfies the required properties for point (1).

Points (2)-(4) have been proved in
[
9
]
under the additional assumptions
\sigma\geq 0
and
\inf_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}h(t)\geq 0
. To apply these results, we need the following modified Hahn-Jordan decomposition.

{clm}

For any
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}({\Gamma_{0}})
with
\int_{\Gamma_{0}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{1}\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}<+\infty
there exists
\sigma^{+},\sigma^{-}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}({\Gamma_{0}})
such that

\sigma^{\pm}\left(\left\{\,(h,\xi)\in{\Gamma_{0}}\mid\inf_{t\in\left[0,1\right]}h(t)<0\,\right\}\right)=0

(A.32)

with
\int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{1}\xdif\sigma^{\pm}<+\infty
and
\Theta(\sigma)=\Theta(\sigma^{+})-\Theta(\sigma^{-})
.

Proof of claim.

The Hahn-Jordan decomposition gives
\sigma=\max(0,\sigma)-\max(0,-\sigma)
, therefore it is sufficient to consider the case
\sigma\geq 0
. Define the maps
T^{\pm}\colon{\Gamma_{0}}\rightarrow{\Gamma_{0}}
by

\forall(h,\xi)\in{\Gamma_{0}},\qquad T^{\pm}(h,\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}(\max(0,\pm h),\xi).

(A.33)

Because
\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}(T^{\pm}(\gamma_{1}),T^{\pm}(\gamma_{2}))\leq\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
,
T^{\pm}
are continuous (therefore Borel), and we can define the image measures
\sigma^{\pm}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}(T^{\pm})_{\sharp}\sigma
. Since the push-forward operation does not increase the total variation, we have
\sigma^{+},\sigma^{-}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}({\Gamma_{0}})
. Moreover, (
A.32
) holds, and by construction of the image measure

\int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}\phi(h,\xi)\xdif\sigma^{\pm}=\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\phi(\max(0,\pm h),\xi)\xdif\sigma

(A.34)

for all
\phi\in{\rm C_{b}}({\Gamma_{0}})
, and by monotone convergence

\int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{1}\xdif\sigma^{\pm}=\int_{\Gamma_{0}}{\left\lVert\max(0,\pm h)\right\rVert}_{1}\xdif\sigma\leq\int_{\Gamma_{0}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{1}\xdif\sigma.

(A.35)

Finally, we confirm that for all
\psi\in{\rm C}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})
,

\displaystyle\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif\Theta(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\psi(t,\xi(t))\xdif t\xdif\sigma

(A.36)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}[\max(0,h(t))-\max(0,-h(t))]\psi(t,\xi(t))\xdif t\xdif\sigma

(A.37)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\psi(t,\xi(t))\xdif t\xdif(\sigma^{+}-\sigma^{-})

(A.38)

\displaystyle=\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif(\Theta(\sigma^{+})-\Theta(\sigma^{-}))

(A.39)

as required.
∎

With this decomposition, we can apply the results of
[

]
to each
\sigma^{\pm}
. Point (2) becomes a direct consequence of
[
9
, Lemma 4.4]
. Under the assumptions of point (3), the same lemma also guarantees the existence of a disintegration

of

\forall\psi\in{\rm C}(\overline{\Omega}),\ t\in\left[0,1\right],\qquad\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\psi(x)\xdif\rho_{t}^{\pm}(x)=\int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}h(t)\psi(\xi(t))\xdif\sigma^{\pm}(h,\xi).

(A.40)

The same property holds for
\Theta(\sigma)
by linearity. Point (3) requires

to be continuous for all

\Rref

and

\left|\int_{\overline{\Omega}}\psi\xdif(\rho_{\tau}-\rho_{t})\right|\leq\int_{\Gamma_{0}}|h(\tau)\psi(\xi(\tau))-h(t)\psi(\xi(t))|\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}(h,\xi)=\int_{\Gamma_{0}}|\Psi(\tau,\gamma)-\Psi(t,\gamma)|\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}(\gamma).

(A.41)

The integrand is pointwise bounded by
2{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{\infty}{\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert}_{\infty}
, therefore we conclude that the limit of the integral as

is 0 by dominated convergence.

Point (4) and its converse are addressed by

∎

{lmm}

[Lemma

For each

then

\left\{\,\Theta(\sigma)\mid\sigma\in\mathcal{M}({\Gamma_{0}}),\ \int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{p}\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}<+\infty\,\right\}=\left\{\,\Theta(\widehat{\sigma})\mid\widehat{\sigma}\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{p})\,\right\}

Furthermore, if

In particular, sequentially we have that, for any sequence

\displaystyle\text{for all }p\in\left[1,+\infty\right],

\displaystyle\qquad\Theta(\sigma^{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\Theta(\sigma)\text{ narrowly in }\mathcal{M}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}),

\displaystyle\text{if }p=+\infty,\ \forall t\in\left[0,1\right],

Proof.

The “
\supset
” inclusion is clear: if
\widehat{\sigma}\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{p})
, extend
\widehat{\sigma}
by 0 such that

as required. Conversely, suppose

Note as

and

and
\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\geq{\left\lVert h_{1}-h_{2}\right\rVert}_{\infty}

and
(h,\xi)\mapsto(\frac{h}{\max(1,{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{p})},\xi)
are continuous w.r.t.
\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}
. Define
\widehat{\sigma}
through the (rescaled) push-forward
\widehat{\sigma}(h,\xi)=\max(1,{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{p})\cdot T_{\sharp}\sigma(h,\xi)
where

.

is Borel measurable, therefore
\widehat{\sigma}
is a Borel measure which satisfies

\forall\phi\in{\rm C_{b}}(\Gamma_{p}),\qquad\int_{\Gamma_{p}}\phi\xdif\widehat{\sigma}=\int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}\max(1,{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{p})\phi\left(\frac{h}{\max(1,{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{p})},\xi\right)\xdif\sigma(h,\xi).

(A.47)

Furthermore,
\widehat{\sigma}\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{p})

\left|\int_{\Gamma_{p}}\phi\xdif\widehat{\sigma}\right|\leq{\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert}_{\infty}\int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}(1+{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{p})\xdif{\left|\sigma\right|}(h,\xi),

so
{\left\lVert\widehat{\sigma}\right\rVert}<+\infty
. The last step is to confirm

. For any

observe

\displaystyle\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif\Theta(\widehat{\sigma})(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{p}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\psi(t,\xi(t))\xdif t\right)\xdif\widehat{\sigma}(h,\xi)

(A.49)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\Gamma_{0}}}\max(1,{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{p})\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{h(t)}{\max(1,{\left\lVert h\right\rVert}_{p})}\psi(t,\xi(t))\xdif t\right)\xdif\sigma(h,\xi)

(A.50)

\displaystyle=\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif\Theta(\sigma)(t,x).

(A.51)

This shows the “
\subset
” direction, therefore we conclude the equality in (
A.43
). For continuity of
\Theta
,
[
31
, Thm. 1, Sec. 1.6]
states that
\Theta
is continuous in
({\rm C}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}))^{\prime}

\psi\in{\rm C}({\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}})

such that

\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{p}),\qquad\left|\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif\Theta(\sigma)\right|\leq\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\left|\int_{\Gamma_{p}}\phi(h,\xi)\xdif\sigma(h,\xi)\right|.

(A.52)

From Lemma
\Rref
lmma: metric properties, define
\Psi(t,h,\xi)=h(t)\psi(t,\xi(t))

for each

\int_{\left[0,1\right]\times\overline{\Omega}}\psi(t,x)\xdif\Theta(\sigma)=\int_{\Gamma_{p}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\psi(t,\xi(t))\xdif t\right)\xdif\sigma(h,\xi)=\int_{\Gamma_{p}}\phi(h,\xi)\xdif\sigma(h,\xi)

for each

Appendix B
Results for the structure of
\operatorname{E}

Here we prove Theorem
\Rref
thm: structure of E as a result of a sequence of simple lemmas. We start with results for the linear term
\operatorname{W}
. Throughout this section we fix lower semi-continuous
w\colon\Gamma\to\left[0,+\infty\right]
for a complete separable metric space
\Gamma
and define
\operatorname{W}\colon\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)\to\left[0,+\infty\right]
by

\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma),\qquad\operatorname{W}(\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\int_{\Gamma}w(\gamma)\xdif\sigma(\gamma).

(B.1)

B.1.
Properties of
\operatorname{W}

The following lower semi-continuity result is well known (see for instance
[
28
, Prop. 7.1]
, or
[
3
, Prop. 5.1.7]
).

{lmm}
[Fatou’s lemma for measures]
If
w\colon\Gamma\rightarrow\left[0,+\infty\right]
is lower semi-continuous, then the functional
\operatorname{W}
is lower semi-continuous on
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)
with respect to the narrow topology.

Compactness in spaces of measure can be characterised by the following lemmas.

{lmm}
[Prokhorov’s theorem on measure spaces, e.g.
[
5
, Thms. 7.1.7, 8.6.7-8]
]

If
\Gamma
is a complete separable metric space and
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
is a family of Borel measures, then

\mathcal{A}\text{ is relatively compact in the narrow topology \quad if and only if }\quad\mathcal{A}\text{ is tight and norm-bounded.}

(B.2)

Because of this lemma, we can give sufficient conditions for the compactness of sub-levelsets of
\operatorname{W}
.

{lmm}

Let
D
be a closed subset of
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)
and denote
U_{t}=\{\sigma\in D\st\operatorname{W}(\sigma)\leq t\}
for
t\in\xR
. If
w\colon\Gamma\rightarrow\left[0,+\infty\right]
is lower semi-continuous,
w
has compact sub-levelsets, and
U_{t}
is bounded in norm for each
t
, then
\operatorname{W}|_{D}
has compact sub-levelsets in the narrow topology of
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

As
w\geq 0
, Lemma
\Rref
thm: Fatou’s lemma shows that
\operatorname{W}
is lower semi-continuous, therefore
U_{t}
is closed. As
U_{t}
is bounded, Lemma
\Rref
thm: Prokhorov equates compactness with tightness. Finally,
[
9
, Prop. A.2]
(adapted from
[
3
, Rem. 5.1.5]
) states:

If
\Gamma
is a complete separable metric space and
w
has compact sub-levelsets, then
U_{t}
is tight for each
t\in\xR
.

We conclude that
U_{t}
is narrowly compact for each
t\in\xR
.
∎

We now confirm that the results of this section are applicable to the examples of Sections
\Rref
sec:BB example intro and
\Rref
sec:WFR example.

{lmm}
[Lower semi-continuity and coercivity of optimal transport regularisations]
Choose
\alpha,\beta,\delta>0
,
\Gamma
a closed subset of
\Gamma_{\infty}
, and let
w:\Gamma\rightarrow\left]0,+\infty\right]
be defined by

\displaystyle\forall(h,\xi)\in\Gamma,\qquad w(\gamma)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}\int_{h>0}\left[\alpha+\frac{\beta}{2}|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{\beta\delta^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{h^{\prime}}{h}\right)^{2}\right]h\xdif t&\mbox{$\sqrt{h},\sqrt{h}\xi\in{\rm AC}^{2}$}\\
+\infty&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(B.3)

Then
w
is lower semi-continuous and has compact sub-levelsets (in the metric
\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}
).

Proof.

It has already been shown that the map
h\delta_{\xi}\mapsto w(h,\xi)
is lower semi-continuous with compact sub-levelsets in
\left\{\,t\mapsto h(t)\delta_{\xi(t)}\mid w(h,\xi)<+\infty\,\right\}
with the flat metric
[
9
, Prop. 3.10]
. In the proof of Lemma
\Rref
app: metric properties, we showed that this metric is isometrically equivalent to
\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}
, so
w
is also lower semi-continuous and coercive with respect to
\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}
.

∎

Note that the lower semi-continuity and coercivity of the Benamou-Brenier penalty follows immediately if we consider it to be the function

(h,\xi)\mapsto w(h,\xi)+\begin{cases}0&h=1\\
+\infty&\text{else.}\end{cases}

(B.4)

The constraint-set is closed, therefore the function is still coercive and lower semi-continuous.

B.2.
Properties of
D

We consider
D
of the form in (
3.4
), that is for some lower semi-continuous
\varphi\colon\Gamma\to\left]0,+\infty\right]
,

D\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\left\{\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)\quad\st\quad\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma\leq 1\right\}.

(B.5)

The only difference between
w
and
\varphi
is that
\varphi>0
, so we can re-use many of the results for
\operatorname{W}
.

{lmm}

If
\varphi\colon\Gamma\to\left]0,+\infty\right]
is lower semi-continuous, then
D
is closed and

\operatorname{Ext}\left(D\right)=\{0\}\cup\left\{\,\varphi(\gamma)^{-1}\delta_{\gamma}\mid\varphi(\gamma)<+\infty\,\right\}.

(B.6)

Furthermore, if
\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\varphi(\gamma)\geq\varepsilon>0
, then
D
is bounded.
Finally, if
\varphi
has compact sub-levelsets, then
D
is also compact.

Proof.

Note by Lemma
\Rref
thm: Fatou’s lemma that
D
is a sub-levelset of the lower semi-continuous function
\sigma\mapsto\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma
, therefore it is closed. Also, for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)
with
{\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert}>\varepsilon^{-1}
, we have
\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma>1
. In particular,
\sigma\notin D
, so
D
is bounded. If
\varphi
has compact sub-levelsets, taking
w=\varphi
in Lemma
\Rref
thm: compact W sublevel confirms that
D
is compact.

It remains to prove (
B.6
), we begin with the “
\supset
” inclusion. By non-negativity, note that for all
\sigma,\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\in D
and
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right[
,

\sigma=\lambda\sigma_{0}+(1-\lambda)\sigma_{1}\qquad\implies\qquad\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\ll\sigma,\text{ \ie\ }\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_{i})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\sigma).

(B.7)

In particular, taking
\sigma=0
, we obtain
\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{1}=0
, hence
0\in\operatorname{Ext}\left(D\right)
. Now, setting
\sigma=\varphi(\gamma)^{-1}\delta_{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
with
\varphi(\gamma)<+\infty
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}=\frac{\alpha}{\varphi(\gamma)}\delta_{\gamma},\ \sigma_{1}=\frac{\beta}{\varphi(\gamma)}\delta_{\gamma},\ \mbox{for some $\alpha,\beta\in\left[0,1\right]$}.

(B.8)

Since
\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma=1
, we must have
\lambda\alpha+(1-\lambda)\beta=1
hence
\alpha=\beta=1
, so that
\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{1}=\sigma
. As a result,
\varphi(\gamma)^{-1}\delta_{\gamma}
is an extreme point of
D
.

To show that this inclusion is sharp, we make the following claim.

{clm}

If
\sigma\in D
and there exists
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\in\operatorname{supp}(\sigma)
distinct, then
\sigma\in\left]\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\right[
for some
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\in D
such that
\gamma_{0}\in\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_{0})\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_{1})
and
\gamma_{1}\in\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_{1})\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_{0})
.

Proof of claim.

Let
r=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
and set
\Gamma_{1}=\{\operatorname{d}_{\Gamma}(\gamma,\gamma_{1})<r\}
. Define
\sigma_{1}=\mathds{1}_{\Gamma_{1}}\sigma
and
\sigma_{0}=\sigma-\sigma_{1}
. By the definition of support, for both
i=0,1
we have
\sigma_{i}\neq 0
,
\gamma_{i}\in\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_{i})\setminus\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_{1-i})
. Also,
\alpha\sigma_{0}+\beta\sigma_{1}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)
for all
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
, the only challenge is the constraint with
\varphi
.

Case
\bf{\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma_{i}=0}
:

In this case, by non-negativity,
\sigma_{i}=0
so
\operatorname{supp}(\sigma_{i})=\emptyset
contradicts the assumption. We conclude that
\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma_{i}\in\left]0,1\right[
for both
i=0,1
.

Else:

Set
\lambda\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma_{0}\in\left]0,1\right[
and
\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma_{1}=\int_{\Gamma}\varphi(\xdif\sigma-\xdif\sigma_{0})\leq 1-\lambda\in\left]0,1\right[
, therefore

\sigma=\lambda\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\lambda}+(1-\lambda)\frac{\sigma_{1}}{1-\lambda},

(B.9)

which confirms
\sigma\in\left]\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\right[
as required.

∎

This claim immediately confirms that all extreme points must have at most one point in their support. Combined with the constraint
\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma\leq 1
, we must have

\operatorname{Ext}\left(D\right)\subset\{0\}\cup\left\{\,\lambda\delta_{\gamma}\mid\gamma\in\Gamma,\ \varphi(\gamma)<+\infty,\ 0<\lambda\leq\varphi(\gamma)^{-1}\,\right\}.

(B.10)

Finally, if
0<\lambda<\varphi(\gamma)^{-1}
, then
\lambda\delta_{\gamma}\in\left]0,\varphi(\gamma)^{-1}\delta_{\gamma}\right[
, so
\lambda\delta_{\gamma}\notin\operatorname{Ext}\left(D\right)
. This confirms that the only extreme points are those found in the first half of the proof.

∎

Recall that

\Rref

B.4.

We now prove that minimisers of

has compact sub-levelsets. Note in the case

{thrm}

Suppose

\operatorname{F}
is convex lower semi-continuous.
If

is bounded from below,

also has compact sub-levelsets.

Proof.

Lemma
\Rref
thm: compact W sublevel shows that
\operatorname{W}|_{D}
has compact sub-levelsets. Therefore for any
t\in\xR

\displaystyle\left\{\,\sigma\in D\mid\operatorname{E}(\sigma)\leq t\,\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\,\sigma\in D\mid\operatorname{F}(\sigma)+\operatorname{W}(\sigma)\leq t\,\right\}

(B.11)

\displaystyle\subset\left\{\,\sigma\in D\mid\operatorname{W}(\sigma)\leq t-\inf_{\widetilde{\sigma}\in D}\operatorname{F}(\widetilde{\sigma})\,\right\}.

(B.12)

The function
\operatorname{E}
is lower semi-continuous, so the left-hand side is closed and the right-hand side is compact by assumption. We conclude that the left-hand side is compact for each
t
.
∎

B.5.
\operatorname{E}
has sparse minimisers

Since the function
\operatorname{F}
is of the form

\operatorname{F}(\sigma)=\sum_{j=0}^{T}\operatorname{F}_{j}(A_{j}(e_{t_{j}})_{\sharp}{\sigma})\quad\text{for some}\quad A_{j}\colon\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)\to\xR^{m},\text{ convex }\operatorname{F}_{j},

(B.13)

with
A_{j}
as in (
3.1
),
\operatorname{F}
is convex lower semi-continuous. We can therefore use a representer theorem (
e.g.

[
7
, Cor. 3.8]
) to demonstrate the sparsity of minimisers.

{lmm}

Suppose
\argmin_{\sigma\in D}\operatorname{E}(\sigma)\neq\emptyset
for some
D\subset\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Gamma)
closed and bounded of the form in (
B.5
). Then there exists a minimiser
\sigma^{*}\in D
such that

\displaystyle\sigma^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}\delta_{\gamma_{i}}\quad\text{for some }\quad a_{i}\geq 0,\ \gamma_{i}\in\Gamma\ \mbox{with}\ \varphi(\gamma_{i})<+\infty,

(B.14)

where
s\leq m(T+1)+1
. If in addition
\int\varphi\xdif\sigma^{*}<1
, then
s\leq m(T+1)
.

Proof.

We reformulate the problem
\min_{\sigma\in D}E(\sigma)
as

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma\in V}H(\widetilde{A}\sigma)+R(\sigma)

(B.15)

where
V
is the vector space of all
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
such that
w\in\xLone_{{\left|\sigma\right|}}(\Gamma)
,
R
is a convex regulariser

\displaystyle R(\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}W(\sigma)+\chi_{D}(\sigma)\quad\text{where}\quad\chi_{D}(\sigma)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}0&\sigma\in D\\
+\infty&\text{else,}\end{cases}

(B.16)

with linearly closed level sets. The observation operator
\widetilde{A}\colon V\rightarrow(\xR^{m})^{(T+1)}
, is linear, defined as
\sigma\mapsto(A_{j}(e_{t_{j}})_{\sharp}{\sigma})_{0\leq j\leq T}
, and
H\colon(a_{0},\ldots,a_{T})\mapsto\sum_{j=0}^{T}F_{j}(a_{j})
is a convex “fidelity term”.

Set
k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}m(T+1)
. We claim that there exists a minimiser
\sigma^{*}
such that

\sigma^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\theta_{i}\mu^{i},\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\theta_{i}=1,\text{ and }\forall i,\ \theta_{i}\geq 0,\ \mu^{i}\in\operatorname{Ext}\left(D\right).

(B.17)

To prove this, fix
t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}R(\sigma)
for some arbitrary
\sigma\in\argmin_{\sigma\in D}\operatorname{E}(\sigma)
and define

\widetilde{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def.}}}{{=}}D\cap\left\{\,\sigma\in V\mid\left\langle w,\sigma\right\rangle=t\,\right\}\subset\{R\leq t\}.

(B.18)

We now split the analysis into two cases depending on the value of
t
.

Case
t=\inf_{V}R
:

In the case
t=\inf_{V}R=0
,
[
7
, Cor. 3.8]
tells us that there exists a minimiser
\sigma^{*}
which belongs to an elementary face of
\{R\leq t\}
with dimension at most
k
, therefore also a face of
\widetilde{D}
with dimension at most
k
. In particular, by Carathéodory’s theorem, we can express
\sigma^{*}
in the form in (
B.17
) but with
\mu^{i}\in\operatorname{Ext}\left(\widetilde{D}\right)
.

Observe that for
t=0
, we can equivalently write
\widetilde{D}
as

\widetilde{D}=\left\{\,\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\{w=0\})\mid\int_{\Gamma}\varphi\xdif\sigma\leq 1\,\right\}\subset D

(B.19)

therefore the extreme points of
\widetilde{D}
can be computed explicitly. By Lemma
\Rref
lmma: extreme point computation,

\operatorname{Ext}\left(\widetilde{D}\right)=\{0\}\cup\left\{\,\varphi(\gamma)^{-1}\delta_{\gamma}\mid\varphi(\gamma)<+\infty,\ w(\gamma)=0\,\right\}\subset\operatorname{Ext}\left(D\right),

(B.20)

so (
B.17
) is satisfied.

Case
t>\inf_{V}R
:

In this case the minimiser
\sigma^{*}
from
[
7
, Cor. 3.8]
belongs to an elementary face of
\{R\leq t\}
with dimension at most
(k-1)
(hence also for
\widetilde{D}
).

Since
\left\{\,\sigma\in V\mid\left\langle w,\sigma\right\rangle=t\,\right\}
is a hyperplane, it is possible to prove (see for instance
[
21
]
) that
\sigma^{*}
belongs to a face of
D
with dimension at most
k
. The formulation of (
B.17
) is again given by Carathéodory’s theorem.

We may now deduce (
B.14
) from (
B.17
). From Lemma
\Rref
lmma: extreme point computation, we know that the extreme points of
D
are either
0
or of the form
\varphi(\gamma)^{-1}\delta_{\gamma}
where
\varphi(\gamma)<+\infty
, hence the general form of (
B.14
) where
s\leq k+1
.

In the special case of
\int\varphi\xdif\sigma^{*}<1
, one of the atoms
\mu^{i}
must be
0
. As a result, we may remove it from the sum, so that
s\leq k
.

∎
