Abstract
In Bayesian inference, the maximum a posteriori (MAP) problem combines the most probable explanation (MPE) and marginalization (MAR) problems. The counterpart in propositional logic is the exist-random stochastic satisfiability (ER-SSAT) problem, which combines the satisfiability (SAT) and weighted model counting (WMC) problems. Both MAP and ER-SSAT have the form argmax X ∑ Y f ( X , Y ) \argmax_{X}\sum_{Y}f\left(X,Y\right) , where f f is a real-valued function over disjoint sets X X and Y Y of variables. These two optimization problems request a value assignment for the X X variables that maximizes the weighted sum of f ⁡ ( X , Y ) f\left(X,Y\right) over all value assignments for the Y Y variables. ER-SSAT has been shown to be a promising approach to formally verify fairness in supervised learning. Recently, dynamic programming on graded project-join trees has been proposed to solve weighted projected model counting (WPMC) , a related problem that has the form ∑ X max Y ⁡ f ⁡ ( X , Y ) \sum_{X}\max_{Y}f\left(X,Y\right) . We extend this WPMC framework to exactly solve ER-SSAT and implement a dynamic-programming solver named DPER . Our empirical evaluation indicates that DPER contributes to the portfolio of state-of-the-art ER-SSAT solvers ( DC-SSAT and erSSAT ) through competitive performance on low-width problem instances.



1
Introduction

Bayesian networks
[
Pearl, 1985
]
are used in many application domains, such as medicine
[
Shwe et al., 1991
]
and industrial systems
[
Cai et al., 2017
]
.
In Bayesian inference, the well-known
most probable explanation (MPE)
problem requests a variable instantiation with the highest probability
[
Park, 2002
]
.
MPE has the form
\argmax_{X}f\left(X\right)
, where
f
is a real-valued function over a set
X
of variables.
Also having that form is the
satisfiability (SAT)
problem, which requests a
satisfying truth assignment (model)
of a Boolean formula
[
Cook, 1971
]
.
SAT can be viewed as a counterpart of MPE where
f
is a Boolean function
[
Littman et al., 2001
]
.

Another fundamental problem in Bayesian inference is
marginalization (MAR)
, which requests the marginal probability of observing a given piece of evidence
[
Chavira et al., 2005
]
.
MAR has the form
\sum_{X}f\left(X\right)
, where the summation is over variable instantiations that are consistent with the evidence.
Also having that form is the
weighted model counting (WMC)
problem, which requests the sum of weights of models of a Boolean formula
[
Valiant, 1979
]
.
Indeed, MAR can be solved via reduction to WMC
[
Sang et al., 2005
]
.

A generalization of MPE and MAR is the
maximum a posteriori (MAP)
problem, which requests a value assignment for some variables that results in the highest marginal probability (over the remaining variables) in a Bayesian network
[
Park and Darwiche, 2004
]
.
MAP has the form
\argmax_{X}\sum_{Y}f\left(X,Y\right)
, where
X
and
Y
are disjoint sets of variables.
Also having that form is the
exist-random stochastic satisfiability (ER-SSAT)
problem.
Given a Boolean formula
\varphi
over
X\cup Y
, ER-SSAT requests a truth assignment
\tau_{X}
for
X
that maximizes the sum of weights of models
\tau_{Y}
of the residual formula
\varphi\restriction_{\tau_{X}}
over
Y

[
Lee et al., 2018
]
.
ER-SSAT can be viewed as a generalization of SAT and WMC.

Another generalization of SAT and WMC is the
weighted projected model counting (WPMC)
problem, which has the form
\sum_{X}\max_{Y}f\left(X,Y\right)
.
Given a Boolean formula
\varphi
over
X\cup Y
, WPMC requests the sum of weights of truth assignments
\tau_{X}
for
X
such that there exists a truth assignment
\tau_{Y}
to
Y
where
\tau_{X}\cup\tau_{Y}
is a model of
\varphi

[
Zawadzki et al., 2013
]
.
A recent WPMC framework,
ProCount

[
Dudek et al., 2021
]
, employs
dynamic programming

[
Bellman, 1966
]
, a well-known strategy to solve a large instance by decomposing it into parts then combining partial solutions into the final answer.
Dynamic programming has also been applied to other problems, such as SAT
[
Pan and Vardi, 2005
]
, WMC
[
Dudek et al., 2020
,
Fichte et al., 2020
]
, and
quantified Boolean formula (QBF)
evaluation
[
Charwat and Woltran, 2016
]
.

To guide dynamic programming for WPMC,
ProCount
uses
project-join trees
as execution plans.
Given a Boolean formula in
conjunctive normal form (CNF)
, i.e., as a set of clauses, a project-join tree specifies how to conjoin clauses and project out variables.
The
width
of a project-join tree is an indicator of the hardness of the instance
[
Dudek et al., 2020
]
.
As WPMC handles two disjoint sets
X
and
Y
of variables differently, a project-join tree needs to be
graded
, i.e.,
X
nodes appear above
Y
nodes.
Gradedness guarantees correctness because summation (over
X
) and maximization (over
Y
) do not commute in general
[
Dudek et al., 2021
]
.

ProCount
operates in two phases.
First, the
planning phase
builds a graded project-join tree with graph-decomposition techniques
[
Robertson and Seymour, 1991
,
Strasser, 2017
]
.
Second, the
execution phase
uses the built tree to compute the final answer, where intermediate results are represented by
algebraic decision diagrams (ADDs)

[
Bahar et al., 1997
,
Somenzi, 2015
]
.

Here, we build on the approach of
ProCount
to develop an exact solver for ER-SSAT.
To find a
maximizing truth assignment (maximizer)
for ER-SSAT, we leverage an iterative technique that was recently proposed to solve
maximum satisfiability (MaxSAT)

[
Kyrillidis et al., 2022
]
and
Boolean MPE

[
Phan and Vardi, 2022
]
.
MaxSAT is an optimization version of SAT and requests a truth assignment that maximizes the number of satisfied clauses of an unsatisfiable CNF formula
[
Krentel, 1988
]
.
By building on techniques from WPMC
[
Dudek et al., 2021
]
, Boolean MPE
[
Phan and Vardi, 2022
]
, and MaxSAT
[
Kyrillidis et al., 2022
]
, we construct
DPER
, a dynamic-programming ER-SSAT framework.
We then compare
DPER
to state-of-the-art solvers (
DC-SSAT

[
Majercik and Boots, 2005
]
and
erSSAT

[
Lee et al., 2018
]
).
We observe that
DPER
contributes to the portfolio of exact ER-SSAT solvers, through very competitive performance on instances with low-width project-join trees.

2
Related Work

More general than ER-SSAT is the
stochastic satisfiability (SSAT)
problem, which allows arbitrary alternations of the form
\argmax_{X}\sum_{Y}\argmax_{X^{\prime}}\sum_{Y^{\prime}}\ldots f\left(X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},\ldots\right)
.
A complete SSAT solver is
DC-SSAT

[
Majercik and Boots, 2005
]
, which employs a
divide-and-conquer
strategy.

DC-SSAT
splits a CNF formula into subformulas, caches
viable partial assignments (VPAs)
derived from solving subformulas, then merges VPAs into a solution to the original formula.
To reduce memory usage,
DC-SSAT
only caches VPAs that are necessary to construct the final answer.

The ER-SSAT solver
erSSAT

[
Lee et al., 2018
]
uses
clause-containment learning
, a technique inspired by
clause selection

[
Janota and Marques-Silva, 2015
]
in QBF evaluation.
Clause-containment learning prunes the search space with blocking clauses that are deduced after trying some truth assignments.
These learned clauses are further strengthened by
minimal clause selection
,
clause subsumption
, and
partial assignment pruning
.
The
erSSAT
solver extends a WPMC tool
[
Lee et al., 2017
]
that combines SAT and WMC techniques.

While
DC-SSAT
and
erSSAT
are exact solvers for ER-SSAT,
MaxCount

[
Fremont et al., 2017
]
is a probabilistically approximately correct tool for a related problem,
maximum model counting
, which has the form
\max_{X}\sum_{Y}\max_{Z}f\left(X,Y,Z\right)
.
The approximation tolerance and confidence level are provided as part of the input.
To provide these guarantees,
MaxCount
relies on techniques in WPMC and sampling
[
Chakraborty et al., 2016
]
.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Graphs

In a
graph

G
, let
\mathcal{V}\left(G\right)
denote the set of vertices.
A
tree
is an undirected graph that is connected and acyclic.
We refer to a vertex of a tree as a
node
.
A
rooted tree
is a tree
T
together with a distinguished node
r\in\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)
called the
root
.
In a rooted tree
\langle T,r\rangle
, each node
v\in\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)
has a (possibly empty) set of
children
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}\left(T,r,v\right)
, which contains every node
v^{\prime}
adjacent to
v
such that the path from
v^{\prime}
to
r
passes through
v
.
A
leaf
of a rooted tree
\langle T,r\rangle
is a non-root node of degree one.
Let
\mathcal{L}\left(T,r\right)
denote the set of leaves of
\langle T,r\rangle
.
An
internal node
is a member of
\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)\setminus\mathcal{L}\left(T,r\right)
, including the root.

3.2
Pseudo-Boolean Functions

Let
B^{A}
denote the set of all functions
f
with domain
\operatorname{dom}\left(f\right):=A
and codomain
B
.
The
restriction
of
f
to a set
S
is a function defined by
f\restriction_{S}:=\left\{\langle a,b\rangle\in f\mid a\in S\right\}
.

From now on, every variable is binary unless noted otherwise.
A
truth assignment
for a set
X
of variables is a function
\tau:X\to\mathbb{B}
.

A
pseudo-Boolean (PB) function
over a set
X
of variables is a function
f:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Define
\operatorname{vars}\left(f\right):=X
.
We say that
f
is
constant
if
X=\varnothing
.
Given some
S\subseteq X
, a truth assignment
\tau:S\to\mathbb{B}
is
total
w.r.t.
f
if
S=X
and is
partial
otherwise.
A
Boolean function
is a special PB function
f:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{B}
.

Definition 1

(Join)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{B}^{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be PB functions.
The
(multiplicative) join
of
f
and
g
is a PB function, denoted by
f\cdot g:\mathbb{B}^{X\cup Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined for each
\tau\in\mathbb{B}^{X\cup Y}
by
(f\cdot g)(\tau):=f(\tau\restriction_{X})\cdot g(\tau\restriction_{Y})
.

Join is commutative and associative: we have
f\cdot g=g\cdot f
as well as
(f\cdot g)\cdot h=f\cdot(g\cdot h)
for all PB functions
f
,
g
, and
h
.
Then define
\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}:=f_{1}\cdot f_{2}\cdot\ldots\cdot f_{n}
.

Definition 2

(Existential Projection)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a PB function and
x
be a variable.
The
existential projection
of
f
w.r.t.
x
is a PB function, denoted by
\exists_{x}f:\mathbb{B}^{X\setminus\left\{x\right\}}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined for each
\tau\in\mathbb{B}^{X\setminus\left\{x\right\}}
by
(\exists_{x}f)(\tau):=\max\left(f(\tau\cup\left\{\langle x,0\rangle\right\}),f(\tau\cup\left\{\langle x,1\rangle\right\})\right)
.

Existential projection is commutative:
\exists_{x}(\exists_{y}f)=\exists_{y}(\exists_{x}f)
for all variables
x
and
y
.
Then define
\exists_{S}f:=\exists_{x}\exists_{y}\ldots f
, where
S=\left\{x,y,\ldots\right\}
is a set of variables.
By convention,
\exists_{\varnothing}f:=f
.

Definition 3

(Maximum)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a PB function.
The
maximum
of
f
is the real number
(\exists_{X}f)(\varnothing)
.

Definition 4

(Maximizer)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a PB function.
A
maximizer
of
f
is a truth assignment
\tau\in\mathbb{B}^{X}
such that
f(\tau)=(\exists_{X}f)(\varnothing)
.

Definition 5

(Random Projection)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a PB function,
x
be a variable, and
0\leq p\leq 1
be a probability.
The
random projection
of
f
w.r.t.
x
and
p
is a PB function, denoted by
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p}_{x}f:\mathbb{B}^{X\setminus\left\{x\right\}}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined for each
\tau\in\mathbb{B}^{X\setminus\left\{x\right\}}
by
(\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p}_{x}f)(\tau):=p\cdot f(\tau\cup\left\{\langle x,1\rangle\right\})+(1-p)\cdot f(\tau\cup\left\{\langle x,0\rangle\right\})
.

We sometimes write
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}_{x}
instead of
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p}_{x}
, leaving
p
implicit.

We define a
probability mapping
for a set
X
of variables as a function
\rho:X\to\left[0,1\right]
.
Note that random projection is commutative:
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p_{1}}_{x_{1}}(\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p_{2}}_{x_{2}}f)=\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p_{2}}_{x_{2}}(\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p_{1}}_{x_{1}}f)
for all PB functions
f
, variables
x_{1},x_{2}
, and probabilities
p_{1},p_{2}
.
Then given a probability mapping
\rho
for
X
and a set
S=\left\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\right\}\subseteq X
, define
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}_{S}f:=\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho(x_{1})}_{x_{1}}\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho(x_{2})}_{x_{2}}\ldots f
.
By convention,
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}_{\varnothing}f:=f
.
We sometimes write
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}_{S}
instead of
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}_{S}
, leaving
\rho
implicit.

Given a Boolean formula
\varphi
, define
\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)

\varphi
.
Then
\varphi
represents a Boolean function, denoted by
\left[\varphi\right]:\mathbb{B}^{\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)}\to\mathbb{B}

Boolean formulas can be generalized for probabilistic domains as follows

Papadimitriou, 1985
]
.

Definition 6

(Stochastic Formula)
.

A
stochastic formula
is one of the following:

1.

a Boolean formula,

2.

the
existential quantification
of a stochastic formula
\varphi
w.r.t. a variable
x
, denoted by
\exists x\varphi
, or

3.

the
random quantification
of a stochastic formula
\varphi
w.r.t. a variable
x
and a probability
p
, denoted by
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p}x\varphi
.

We sometimes write
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}x
instead of
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p}x
, leaving
p
implicit.

A stochastic formula
Q_{1}x_{1}Q_{2}x_{2}\ldots\varphi
(where each
Q_{i}\in\left\{\exists,\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}\right\}
and

is a Boolean formula) represents a PB function, denoted by
\left[Q_{1}x_{1}Q_{2}x_{2}\ldots\varphi\right]:\mathbb{B}^{\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)\setminus\left\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\right\}}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 7

(Stochastic Semantics)
.

A stochastic formula is interpreted as follows:

1.

A Boolean formula
\varphi
represents the Boolean function
\left[\varphi\right]
according to Boolean semantics.

2.

The existential quantification
\exists x\varphi
represents a PB function, denoted by
\left[\exists x\varphi\right]:\mathbb{B}^{X\setminus\left\{x\right\}}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined by
\left[\exists x\varphi\right]:=\exists_{x}\left[\varphi\right]
, where
\left[\varphi\right]:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is the PB function represented by the stochastic formula
\varphi
.

3.

The random quantification
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p}x\varphi
represents a PB function, denoted by
\left[\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p}x\varphi\right]:\mathbb{B}^{X\setminus\left\{x\right\}}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined by
\left[\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p}x\varphi\right]:=\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{p}_{x}\left[\varphi\right]
, where
\left[\varphi\right]:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is the PB function represented by the stochastic formula
\varphi
.

For brevity, define
\exists S:=\exists x\exists y\ldots
if
S=\left\{x,y,\ldots\right\}
is a non-empty set of variables.
Also define
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}S:=\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho(x)}x\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho(y)}y\ldots
if
\rho
is a probability mapping where
S\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\left(\rho\right)
.
We sometimes write
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}S
instead of
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}S
, leaving
\rho
implicit.
Then a stochastic formula has the form
Q_{1}X_{1}Q_{2}X_{2}\ldots\varphi
, where each
Q_{i}\in\left\{\exists,\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}\right\}
with
Q_{i}\neq Q_{i+1}
, each
X_{i}
is a non-empty set of variables, and
\varphi
is a Boolean formula.
Several fundamental problems can be formulated with stochastic formulas.

Definition 8

(Weighted Model Counting)
.

Let
\varphi
be a Boolean formula,
X
be
\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)
, and
\rho
be a probability mapping for
X
.
The
weighted model counting (WMC)
problem on
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}X\varphi
requests the real number
\left[\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}X\varphi\right](\varnothing)
.

We focus on stochastic formulas with one quantifier alternation.
An
exist-random (ER) formula
has the form
\exists X\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\varphi
, where
\varphi
is a Boolean formula.
In a similar fashion, an
RE formula
has the form
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\exists X\varphi
.
The two following problems concern both kinds of quantifiers.

Definition 9

(Weighted Projected Model Counting)
.

Let
\varphi
be a Boolean formula,
\left\{X,Y\right\}
be a partition of
\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)
, and
\rho
be a probability mapping for
Y
.
The
weighted projected model counting (WPMC)
problem on
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}Y\exists X\varphi
requests the real number
\left[\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}Y\exists X\varphi\right](\varnothing)
.

Definition 10

(Exist-Random Stochastic Satisfiability)
.

Let
\varphi
be a Boolean formula,
\left\{X,Y\right\}
be a partition of
\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)
, and
\rho
be a probability mapping for
Y
.
The
exist-random stochastic satisfiability (ER-SSAT)
problem on
\exists X\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}Y\varphi
requests the maximum and a maximizer of
\left[\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}Y\varphi\right]
.

Note that the maximum of
\left[\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}Y\varphi\right]
is the real number
\left[\exists X\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}Y\varphi\right](\varnothing)
.

4
Solving ER-SSAT

4.1
Monolithic Approach

A Boolean formula is usually given in
conjunctive normal form (CNF)
, i.e., as a set of
clauses
.
A clause is a disjunction of literals, which are variables or negated variables.

Given a CNF formula
\varphi
, we have the factorization
\left[\varphi\right]=\prod_{c\in\varphi}\left[c\right]
.
In this section, we present an inefficient algorithm to solve ER-SSAT that treats
\varphi
as a monolithic structure and ignores the CNF factored representation.
The next section describes a more efficient algorithm.

To find maximizers for ER-SSAT, we leverage the following idea, which originated from the
basic algorithm
for PB programming
[
Crama et al., 1990
]
then was adapted for MaxSAT
[
Kyrillidis et al., 2022
]
and Boolean MPE
[
Phan and Vardi, 2022
]
.

Definition 11

(Derivative Sign)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a PB function and
x
be a variable.
The
derivative sign
of
f
w.r.t.
x
is a function, denoted by
\dsgn_{x}f:\mathbb{B}^{X\setminus\left\{x\right\}}\to\mathbb{B}^{\left\{x\right\}}
, defined for each
\tau\in\mathbb{B}^{X\setminus\left\{x\right\}}
by
\left(\dsgn_{x}f\right)(\tau):=\left\{\langle x,1\rangle\right\}
if
f(\tau\cup\left\{\langle x,1\rangle\right\})\geq f(\tau\cup\left\{\langle x,0\rangle\right\})
, and
\left(\dsgn_{x}f\right)(\tau):=\left\{\langle x,0\rangle\right\}
otherwise.

Note that the truth assignment
\tau:X\setminus\left\{x\right\}\to\mathbb{B}
is partial w.r.t.
f
(since
x
is unassigned).
The derivative sign indicates how to extend
\tau
into a total truth assignment
\tau^{\prime}:X\to\mathbb{B}
(by mapping
x
to
0
or
1
) in order to maximize
f(\tau^{\prime})
.

The following result leads to an iterative process to find maximizers of PB functions
[
Kyrillidis et al., 2022
,
Phan and Vardi, 2022
]
.

Proposition 1

(Iterative Maximization)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a PB function and
x
be a variable.
Assume that a truth assignment
\tau
is a maximizer of
\exists_{x}f:\mathbb{B}^{X\setminus\left\{x\right\}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then the truth assignment
\tau\cup\left(\dsgn_{x}f\right)(\tau)
is a maximizer of

.

given the input
f_{n}=\left[\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}Y\varphi\right]
, which is a PB function over a set
X_{n}=X
of variables.

ER-SSAT on
\exists X\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\varphi
can be solved by calling
Algorithm

1
with
\left[\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\varphi\right]
as an input.
But the PB function
\left[\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\varphi\right]
may be too large to fit in main memory, making the computation slow or even impossible.
In the next section, we exploit the CNF factorization of
\varphi
and propose a more efficient solution.

4.2
Dynamic Programming

ER-SSAT on
\exists X\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\varphi
involves the PB function
\left[\exists X\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\varphi\right]=\exists_{X}\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}_{Y}\left[\varphi\right]=\exists_{X}\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}_{Y}\prod_{c\in\varphi}\left[c\right]
, where the CNF formula
\varphi
is a set of clauses
c
.
Instead of projecting all variables in
X
and
Y
after joining all clauses, we can be more efficient and project some variables early as follows
[
Dudek et al., 2021
]
.

Proposition 3

(Early Projection)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{B}^{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{B}^{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be PB functions.
Assume that
\rho
is a probability mapping for
X\cup Y
.
Then for all
S\subseteq X\setminus Y
, we have
\exists_{S}(f\cdot g)=(\exists_{S}f)\cdot g
and
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}_{S}(f\cdot g)=(\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}_{S}f)\cdot g
.

Early projection can lead to smaller intermediate PB functions.
For example, the bottleneck in computing
\exists_{S}(f\cdot g)
is
f\cdot g
with size
s=\left|\operatorname{vars}\left(f\cdot g\right)\right|=\left|X\cup Y\right|
.
The bottleneck in computing
(\exists_{S}f)\cdot g
is
f
with size
s_{1}=\left|\operatorname{vars}\left(f\right)\right|=\left|X\right|
or is
(\exists_{S}f)\cdot g
with size
s_{2}=\left|\operatorname{vars}\left((\exists_{S}f)\cdot g\right)\right|=\left|X\cup Y\setminus S\right|
.
Notice that
s\geq\max(s_{1},s_{2})
.
The difference is consequential since an operation on a PB function
h
may take
\operatorname{O}\left(2^{\left|\operatorname{vars}\left(h\right)\right|}\right)
time and space.

When there is only one kind of projection (e.g., just

\mathsf{R}

and no
\exists
), we can apply early projection systematically as in the following framework,
DPMC
, which uses dynamic programming for WMC
[
Dudek et al., 2020
]
.

Definition 12

(Project-Join Tree)
.

Let
\varphi
be a CNF formula (i.e., a set of clauses).
A
project-join tree
for
\varphi
is a tuple
\mathcal{T}=\langle T,r,\gamma,\pi\rangle
, where:

•

\langle T,r\rangle
is a rooted tree,

•

\gamma:\mathcal{L}\left(T,r\right)\to\varphi
is a bijection, and

•

\pi:\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)\setminus\mathcal{L}\left(T,r\right)\to\mathbb{B}^{\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)}
is a function.

A project-join tree must satisfy the following criteria:

1.

The set
\left\{\pi(v)\mid v\in\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)\setminus\mathcal{L}\left(T,r\right)\right\}
is a partition of
\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)
, where some
\pi(v)
sets may be empty.

2.

For each internal node
v
, variable
x\in\pi(v)
, and clause
c\in\varphi
, if
x\in\operatorname{vars}\left(c\right)
, then the leaf
\gamma^{-1}\left(c\right)
is a descendant of
v
in
\langle T,r\rangle
.

For a leaf
v\in\mathcal{L}\left(T,r\right)
, define
\operatorname{vars}\left(v\right):=\operatorname{vars}\left(\gamma(v)\right)
, i.e., the set of variables that appear in the clause
\gamma(v)\in\varphi
.
For an internal node
v\in\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)\setminus\mathcal{L}\left(T,r\right)
, define
\operatorname{vars}\left(v\right):=\left(\bigcup_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\left(T,r,v\right)}\operatorname{vars}\left(v^{\prime}\right)\right)\setminus\pi(v)
.

ER-SSAT has both kinds of projection in a particular order (
\exists X\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\varphi
).
Since
\exists_{x}\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}_{y}f\neq\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}_{y}\exists_{x}f
in general, early projection must be applied carefully.
We use the following idea from
ProCount

[
Dudek et al., 2021
]
, an extension of
DPMC
for WPMC (
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}X\exists Y\varphi
).

Definition 13

(Graded Project-Join Tree)
.

Let
\varphi
be a CNF formula,
\mathcal{T}=\langle T,r,\gamma,\pi\rangle
be a project-join tree for
\varphi
, and
\left\{X,Y\right\}
be a partition of
\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)
.
We say that
\mathcal{T}
is
\langle X,Y\rangle
-graded
if there are sets
\mathcal{I}_{X}
and
\mathcal{I}_{Y}
, called
grades
, that satisfy the following properties:

1.

The set
\left\{\mathcal{I}_{X},\mathcal{I}_{Y}\right\}
is a partition of
\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)\setminus\mathcal{L}\left(T,r\right)
.

2.

If
v\in\mathcal{I}_{X}
, then
\pi(v)\subseteq X
.

3.

If
v\in\mathcal{I}_{Y}
, then
\pi(v)\subseteq Y
.

4.

If
v_{X}\in\mathcal{I}_{X}
and
v_{Y}\in\mathcal{I}_{Y}
, then
v_{X}
is not a descendant of
v_{Y}
in the rooted tree
\langle T,r\rangle
.

Definition 14

(Valuation of Project-Join Tree Node)
.

Let
\varphi
be a CNF formula,
\left\{X,Y\right\}
be a partition of
\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)
, and
\langle T,r,\gamma,\pi\rangle
be an
\langle X,Y\rangle
-graded project-join tree for
\varphi
with grades
\mathcal{I}_{X},\mathcal{I}_{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)
.
Also, let
\rho
be a probability mapping for
Y
and
v\in\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)
be a node.
The
\rho
-valuation
of
v
is a PB function, denoted by
{\llbracket v\rrbracket}_{\rho}:\mathbb{B}^{\operatorname{vars}\left(v\right)}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined by the following:

\displaystyle{\llbracket v\rrbracket}_{\rho}:=\begin{cases}\left[\gamma(v)\right]&\text{if }v\in\mathcal{L}\left(T,r\right)\\
\displaystyle\bigexists_{\pi(v)}\left(\prod_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\left(T,r,v\right)}{\llbracket v^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\rho}\right)&\text{if }v\in\mathcal{I}_{X}\\
\displaystyle\bigrand^{\rho}_{\pi(v)}\left(\prod_{v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\left(T,r,v\right)}{\llbracket v^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\rho}\right)&\text{if }v\in\mathcal{I}_{Y}\end{cases}

Recall that
\left[\gamma(v)\right]
is the Boolean function represented by the clause
\gamma(v)\in\varphi
.
Also,
{\llbracket v^{\prime}\rrbracket}_{\rho}
is the
\rho
-valuation of a child
v^{\prime}
of the node
v
in the rooted tree
\langle T,r\rangle
.
Note that the
\rho
-valuation of the root
r
is a constant PB function.

Definition 15

(Width of Project-Join Tree)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=\langle T,r,\gamma,\pi\rangle
be a project-join tree.
For a leaf
v
of
\mathcal{T}
, define
0pt{v}:=\left|\operatorname{vars}\left(v\right)\right|
.
For an internal node
v
of
\mathcal{T}
, define
0pt{v}:=\left|\operatorname{vars}\left(v\right)\cup\pi(v)\right|
.
The
width
of
\mathcal{T}
is
0pt{\mathcal{T}}:=\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)}0pt{v}
.

Note that
0pt{\mathcal{T}}
is the maximum number of variables needed to valuate a node
v\in\mathcal{V}\left(T\right)
.
Valuating
\mathcal{T}
may take
\operatorname{O}\left(2^{0pt{\mathcal{T}}}\right)
time and space.

[
Dudek et al., 2021

\exists X\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y

\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}X\exists Y

5
Evaluation

We conducted computational experiments to answer the following
empirical questions
.

1.

Does
DPER
contribute to the portfolio of state-of-the-art exact solvers for ER-SSAT (
DC-SSAT

[
Majercik and Boots, 2005
]
and
erSSAT

[
Lee et al., 2018
]
)?

2.

Is there a class of ER formulas on which
DPER
outperforms
DC-SSAT
and
erSSAT
?

We used a high-performance computing cluster.
Each solver-benchmark pair was exclusively run on a single core of an Intel Xeon CPU (E5-2650 v2 at 2.60GHz) with a RAM cap of 100 GB and a time cap of 1000 seconds.

Code, benchmarks, and experimental data are available in a public repository:

https://github.com/vuphan314/DPER

5.1
Benchmarks

Initially, we considered 941 stochastic formulas that were used to verify fairness in supervised learning
[
Ghosh et al., 2021
]
.
But 729 of these benchmarks are
ERE formulas
(of the form
\exists X\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\exists Z\varphi
), which are not the focus of this paper.
The remaining 212 benchmarks are ER formulas, but each of them was easily solved in a second.

Therefore, we considered a more challenging benchmark suite, which was used to compare WPMC solvers
[
Dudek et al., 2021
]
.
Each benchmark in the suite is an RE formula of the form
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}^{\rho}Y\exists X\varphi
, where
\varphi
is a CNF formula,
\left\{X,Y\right\}
is a partition of
\operatorname{vars}\left(\varphi\right)
, and
\rho
be a probability mapping for
Y
.
This 849-benchmark suite comprises two families.
The first family was originally used for weighted projected sampling
[
Gupta et al., 2019
]
and contains 90 RE formulas, each with a non-constant
\rho
.
The second family was originally used for (unweighted) projected sampling
[
Soos and Meel, 2019
]
and contains 759 RE formulas, where every probability was changed from constantly
0.5
to randomly
0.4
or
0.6
.
We converted these 849 harder RE formulas (of the form
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\exists X\varphi
) to ER formulas (of the form
\exists X\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\varphi
).
Each ER-SSAT solver (

,

, and
erSSAT
) was required to compute the maximum and a maximizer of

.

5.2
Correctness

On each of the 849 ER formulas selected, if both
DC-SSAT
and
DPER
finished, then these solvers agreed on the maximum of
\left[\reflectbox{$\mathsf{R}$}Y\varphi\right]
.
But
erSSAT
returned a different number on some benchmarks.
Thus, we have high confidence that
DC-SSAT
and
DPER
were implemented correctly, less so about
erSSAT
.
We notified the authors of
erSSAT
about this correctness issue, which they are investigating.

To count only correct solutions while allowing some tolerance for the finite precision of floating-point arithmetic, we used a solution
m
by
DC-SSAT
as the ground truth and disqualified a solution
n
by another solver if
\left|m-n\right|>10^{-6}
.
(Note that every correct solution is a real number between
0
and
1
.)
If
m
was unavailable (e.g., when
DC-SSAT
timed out), then we simply accepted
n
.

and that
y
is the mean PAR-2 score of the solver on benchmarks of widths

.

DPER
appears to be the fastest solver on benchmarks with low widths (under

To answer the second empirical question, we identify a class of ER formulas on which

.
This class comprises formulas for which there exist graded project-join trees of low widths (under

).

6
Conclusion

We introduce
DPER
, an exact ER-SSAT solver that employs dynamic programming on graded project-join trees, based on a WPMC solver
[
Dudek et al., 2021
]
.

DPER
also adapts an iterative procedure to find maximizers from MaxSAT
[
Kyrillidis et al., 2022
]
and Boolean MPE
[
Phan and Vardi, 2022
]
.
Our experiments show that
DPER
contributes to the portfolio of state-of-the-art ER-SSAT solvers (
DC-SSAT

[
Majercik and Boots, 2005
]
and
erSSAT

[
Lee et al., 2018
]
) through competitiveness on low-width instances.

For future work, we plan to add support for hybrid inputs, such as XOR, PB, and cardinality constraints.
Also,
DPER
can be extended to solve more general problems, e.g., maximum model counting (on ERE formulas)
[
Fremont et al., 2017
]
and
functional aggregate queries

[
Abo Khamis et al., 2016
]
.
Another research direction is to improve

with parallelism, as a portfolio solver (e.g.,
[
Xu et al., 2008
]
) or with a multi-core ADD package (e.g.,
[
van Dijk and van de Pol, 2015
]
).
