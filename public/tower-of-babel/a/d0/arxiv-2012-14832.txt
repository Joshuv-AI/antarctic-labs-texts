Abstract

This work presents a self-contained continuum formulation for coupled chemical, mechanical and thermal contact interactions.
The formulation is very general and hence admits arbitrary geometry, deformation and material behavior.
All model equations are derived rigorously from the balance laws of mass, momentum, energy and entropy in the framework of irreversible thermodynamics,
thus exposing all the coupling present in the field equations and constitutive relations.
In the process, the conjugated kinematic and kinetic variables for mechanical, thermal and chemical contact are identified, and the analogies between mechanical, thermal and chemical contact are highlighted.
Particular focus is placed on the thermodynamics of chemical bonding distinguishing between exothermic and endothermic contact reactions.
Distinction is also made between long-range, non-touching surface interactions and short-range, touching contact.
For all constitutive relations examples are proposed and discussed comprehensively with particular focus on their coupling.
Finally, three analytical test cases are presented that illustrate the thermo-chemo-mechanical contact coupling and are useful for verifying computational models.
While the main novelty is the extension of existing contact formulations to chemical contact, the presented formulation also sheds new light on thermo-mechanical contact, since it is consistently derived from basic principles using only few assumptions.

Keywords:
chemical reactions, continuum contact mechanics, constitutive modeling, coupled problems, irreversible thermodynamics, nonlinear field theories

Introduction

Many applications in science and technology involve coupled interactions at interfaces.
An example is the mechanically sensitive chemical bonding appearing in adhesive joining, implant osseointegration and cell adhesion.
Another example is the thermal heating arising in frictional contact, adhesive joining and electrical contacts.
Further examples are the temperature-dependent mechanical contact conditions in melt-based production technologies, such as welding, soldering, casting and additive manufacturing.
A general understanding and description of these examples requires a general theory that couples chemical, mechanical, thermal and electrical contact.
Such a theory is developed here for the first three fields in the general framework of nonlinear continuum mechanics and irreversible thermodynamics.
Two cases are considered in the theory: touching contact and non-touching interactions.
While the former is dominating at large length scales, the latter provides a link to atomistic contact models.

There is a large literature body on coupled contact models.
General approaches deal, however, with two-field and not with three-field contact coupling, such as is considered here.

General thermo-mechanical contact models have appeared in the early nineties, starting with the work of
Zavarise et al., 1992
that combined a frictionless contact model with heat conduction.
Following that,
Johansson and Klarbring, 1993
presented the full coupling for linear thermo-elasticity.
This was extended by
Wriggers and Miehe, 1994
to large deformations using an operator split technique for the coupling - a staggering scheme based on successively solving mechanical and thermal subproblems.

Oancea and Laursen, 1997
extended this to a monolithical coupling formulation and proposed general constitutive models for thermo-mechanical contact.
Following these initial works, many thermo-mechanical contact studies have appeared, studying the extension to wear
(
Strömberg et al., 1996
;
Stupkiewicz and Mróz, 1999
;
Molinari et al., 2001
)
, rough-surface contact
(
Willner, 1999
)
multiscale contact
(
Temizer and Wriggers, 2010
;
Temizer, 2014
;
Temizer, 2016
)
and adhesion
(
Dittmann et al., 2019
)
.
Beyond that, there have also been many advancements in the computational description of thermo-mechancial contact
(
Agelet de Saracibar, 1998
;
Laursen, 1999
;
Strömberg, 1999
;
Pantuso et al., 2000
;
Adam and Ponthot, 2002
;
Xing and Makinouchi, 2002
;
Bergman and Oldenburg, 2004
;
Rieger and Wriggers, 2004
;
Hüeber and Wohlmuth, 2009
;
Dittmann et al., 2014
;
Khoei et al., 2018
;
Seitz et al., 2019
)
.

General chemo-mechanical contact models go back even further – to the work of
Derjaguin et al., 1975
that describes molecular, e.g. van der Waals, adhesion between an elastic sphere and a half-space.

Argento et al., 1997
then extended this approach to a general surface formulation for interacting continua.
The work was then generalized to a nonlinear continuum mechanical contact formulation by
Sauer, 2006
and
Sauer and Li, 2007a
;
Sauer and Li, 2007b
.
Subsequently, the framework was applied to the study of cell adhesion
(
Zeng and Li, 2011
)
, generalized to various surface interaction models
(
Sauer and De Lorenzis, 2013
)
, and combined with sliding friction models
(
Mergel et al., 2019
)
.
In a strict sense, the chemo-mechanical contact models mentioned so far are not coupled models.
Rather, the chemical surface interaction is described by distance-dependent potentials.
Hence, the resulting problem is a single-field problem that only depends on deformation.
A second field variable representing the chemical contact state is not used.

This is different to the debonding model of
Frémond, 1988
.
There a state variable is introduced in order to describe irreversible damage during debonding.
It is essentially a phenomenological debonding model, where the bond degrades over time following a first order ordinary differential equation (ODE).

Raous et al., 1999
extended the model to sliding contact and implemented it within a finite element formulation.
Its finite element implementation is also discussed in
Wriggers, 2006
in the framework of large deformations.
Subsequently the model has been extended to thermal effects
(
Bonetti et al., 2009
)
, applied to multiscale contact
(
Wriggers and Reinelt, 2009
)
and generalized to various constitutive models
(
Del Piero and Raous, 2010
)
, among others.
Even though the Frémond model is a coupled two-field model, it only describes debonding and not bonding.
It therefore does not provide a general link to chemical contact reactions.

The adhesion and debonding models mentioned above are similar to
cohesive zone models
(CZMs).
Those propose phenomenological traction-separation laws for debonding, that are often derived from a potential, like the seminal model of
Xu and Needleman, 1993
, thus ensuring thermodynamic consistency.
CZMs have been extended to thermo-mechanical
debonding through the works of
Hattiangadi and Siegmund, 2004
;
Willam et al., 2004
;
Fagerström and Larsson, 2008
;
Özdemir et al., 2010
;
Fleischhauer et al., 2013
and
Esmaeili et al., 2016
, among others.
Recently, CZMs have also been coupled to a hydrogen diffusion model in order to study fatigue
(
Busto et al., 2017
)
.
CZMs usually have a damage/degradation part that sometimes follows from an evolution law, e.g. see
Willam et al., 2004
.
This makes them very similar to the
Frémond, 1988
model.
Like the Frémond model, CZMs have not yet been combined with chemical contact reactions, and so this aspect is still absent in general contact models.

Adhesion models based on chemical bonding and debonding reactions have been developed by Bell and coworkers in the late seventies and early eighties in the context of cell adhesion
(
Bell, 1978
;
Bell et al., 1984
)
.
Many subsequent works have appeared based on these models, for example to study substrate adhesion
(
Hammer and Lauffenburger, 1987
)
, strip peeling
(
Dembo et al., 1988
)
, nanoparticle endocytosis
(
Decuzzi and Ferrari, 2007
)
, sliding contact
(
Deshpande et al., 2008
)
, cell nanoindentation
(
Zhang and Zhang, 2008
)
, cell migration
(
Sarvestani and Jabbari, 2009
)
, cell spreading
(
Sun et al., 2009
)
, focal adhesion dynamics
(
Olberding et al., 2010
)
and substrate compliance
(
Huang et al., 2011
)
, among others.
Similar chemical bonding models have also been used to describe sticking and sliding friction, see
Srinivasan and Walcott, 2009
.
The Bell model is an ODE for the chemical reaction.
Assuming separation of chemical and mechanical time scales, this can be simplified into an algebraic equation that describes chemical equilibrium
(
Evans, 1985
)
.
Even though Bell-like models have been combined with contact-induced deformations in several of the works mentioned above,
the contact formulations that have been considered in those works are not general continuum mechanical contact formulations as will be considered here.

Another related topic is the field of
tribochemistry
that is concerned with the growth of so-called
tribofilms
during sliding contact.
Chemical evolution models are used to describe the tribofilms in the framework of elementary contact models,
e.g. see
Andersson et al., 2012
and
Ghanbarzadeh et al., 2016
.
There is also a review article on how mechanical stresses can affect chemical reactions at the molecular scale
(
Kochhar et al., 2015
)
.
But none of these works use general continuum mechanical contact formulations.

Chemical contact reactions can be described by a state
\phi(\bm{x},t)
that follows from an evolution law of the kind
\dot{\phi}=f(\phi)
.
Mathematically, they are thus similar to the description of contact ageing
(
Dieterich, 1978
;
Rice and Ruina, 1983
;
Ruina, 1983
)
, contact wear
(
Strömberg et al., 1996
;
Strömberg, 1999
;
Stupkiewicz and Mróz, 1999
)
and contact debonding
(
Frémond, 1988
;
Raous et al., 1999
)
.
The thermodynamics is, however, very different.

None of the present chemo-mechanical models is a general contact model accounting for the general contact kinematics, balance laws and constitutive relations.
This motivates the development of such a formulation here.
To the best of our knowledge, it is the first general thermo-chemo-mechanical contact model that accounts for large-deformation contact and sliding, chemical bonding and debonding reactions, thermal contact and the full coupling of these fields.
It is derived consistently from the general contact kinematics, balance laws and thermodynamics, introducing constitutive examples only at the end.
Its generality serves as a basis for computational formulations and later extensions such as membrane contact or thermo-electro-chemo-mechanical contact.

The novelties of the proposed formulation can be summarized as follows. The formulation

•

derives a self-contained, fully coupled chemical, mechanical and thermal contact model,

•

accounts for general non-linear deformations and material behavior,

•

consistently captures all the coupling present in the interfacial balance laws,

•

highlights the similarities between chemical, mechanical and thermal contact

•

obtains the general material-independent constitutive contact relations,

•

provides several interface material models derived from the 2. law of thermodynamics,

•

is illustrated by three elementary contact solutions.

Chemical reactions here are restricted to reactions across the contact interface.
In this, two assumptions are made: (1) that the reaction rate is much higher than any sliding rate, and (2) that there is no tangential diffusion of bonds on the contact surface.
Reactions inside the bodies and on their free surfaces, that can also occur without contact, are not considered in this study.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows:
Sec.
2
presents the generalized continuum kinematics characterizing the mechanical, chemical and thermal contact behavior.
The kinematics are required in order to formulate the general balance laws that govern the coupled contact system in Sec.
3
.
Based on these laws the general constitutive relations are derived in Sec.
4
.
Sec.
5
then gives several coupled constitutive examples satisfying these relations.
In order to illustrate these, Sec.
6
provides three analytical test cases for coupled contact.
The paper concludes with Sec.
7
.

2
Continuum contact kinematics

This section introduces the kinematic variables characterizing the mechanical, thermal and chemical interaction between two deforming bodies
\mathcal{B}_{k}
,
k=1,2
, following the established developments of continuum mechanics
(
Holzapfel, 2000
)
and contact mechanics
(
Laursen, 2002
;
Wriggers, 2006
)
.
The novelty here consists in their contrasting juxtaposition.

The primary field variables are the current mass density
\rho_{k}=\rho_{k}(\bm{X}_{\!k},t)
, current surface bonding site density
n_{k}=n_{k}(\bm{X}_{\!k},t)
, current position
\bm{x}_{k}=\bm{x}_{k}(\bm{X}_{\!k},t)
, current velocity
\dot{\bm{x}}_{k}=:\bm{v}_{k}=\bm{v}_{k}(\bm{X}_{\!k},t)
and current temperature
T_{k}=T_{k}(\bm{X}_{\!k},t)
that are all functions of space and time.
Here, the spatial dependency is expressed through the initial position
\bm{X}_{k}=\bm{x}_{k}\big|_{t\,=\,0}
, and the dot denotes the material time derivative

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{...}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial{...}}{\partial{t}}\Big|_{\bm{X}_{k}\,=\,\mathrm{fixed}}\,.\end{array}

(1)

The two bodies can come into contact and interact mechanically, thermally and chemically on their common contact surface
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}:=\partial\mathcal{B}_{1}\cap\partial\mathcal{B}_{2}
, see Fig.
1
.
In case of long-range interaction, like van der Waals adhesion, they may also interact when not in direct contact.
In this case, interaction is considered to take place on the (non-touching) surfaces regions
\mathcal{S}_{k}\subset\partial\mathcal{B}_{k}
.
2
2

2

In principle, long-range interaction can also take place in the volume. The present theory can be extended straightforwardly to account for such interactions. Alternatively, one may also project them onto the surface following
Sauer and Wriggers, 2009
.

The deformation within each body is characterized by the deformation gradient

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{F}_{k}:=\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bm{x}_{k}}}{\partial{\bm{X}_{k}}}\,,\end{array}

(2)

from which various strain measures can be derived, such as the Green-Lagrange strain tensor

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{E}_{k}:=\big(\bm{F}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{F}_{k}-\mathbf{1}\big)/2\,.\end{array}

(3)

The deformation generally contains elastic and inelastic contributions that lead to the additive strain decomposition
3
3

3

Even though the additive decomposition of the strain tensor is motivated from small deformations, it also applies to large deformations, where a multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient in the form
\bm{F}=\bm{F}_{\mathrm{e}}\bm{F}_{\mathrm{i}}
is usually considered. In this case
\bm{E}^{\mathrm{e}}=\big(\bm{F}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{F}-\bm{F}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{F}_{\mathrm{i}}\big)/2
and
\bm{E}^{\mathrm{i}}=\big(\bm{F}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{F}_{\mathrm{i}}-\mathbf{1}\big)/2
.

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{E}_{k}=\bm{E}_{k}^{\mathrm{e}}+\bm{E}_{k}^{\mathrm{i}}\,.\end{array}

(4)

An example for an inelastic strain is thermal expansion.
From
\bm{F}_{k}
also follows the quantity

\begin{array}[]{l}J_{k}:=\det\bm{F}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(5)

which governs the local volume change

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{d}v_{k}=J_{k}\,\mathrm{d}V_{k}\end{array}

(6)

at
\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
between undeformed and deformed configuration.
Similarly, the local surface area change

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{d}a_{k}=J_{\mathrm{s}k}\,\mathrm{d}A_{k}\end{array}

(7)

at
\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
is governed by the quantity

\begin{array}[]{l}J_{\mathrm{s}k}:=\det_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{F}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(8)

where
\det_{\mathrm{s}}(...)
is the surface determinant on
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, e.g. see
Sauer et al., 2019
.

Time dependent deformation is characterized by the symmetric velocity gradient

\begin{array}[]{l}\ \bm{D}_{k}:=\big(\nabla\bm{v}_{k}+\nabla\bm{v}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\big)/2\,,\end{array}

(9)

also known as the rate of deformation tensor.
The velocity also gives rise to the identities

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\dot{J}_{k}}{J_{k}}=\mathrm{div}\,\bm{v}_{k}\end{array}

(10)

and

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\dot{J}_{\mathrm{s}k}}{J_{\mathrm{s}k}}=\mathrm{div}\,_{\!\mathrm{s}}\,\bm{v}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(11)

where
\mathrm{div}\,_{\!\mathrm{s}}(...)
is the surface divergence on
\mathcal{S}_{k}
.

Mechanical interaction between the two bodies is characterized by the gap vector

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{g}:=\bm{x}_{2}-\bm{x}_{1}\,,\end{array}

(12)

that is defined for all pairs
\bm{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\bm{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
.
Two cases have to be distinguished:
i. Both
\bm{x}_{1}
and
\bm{x}_{2}
are material points – a setting that is suitable for describing long-range interactions; or more generally:
ii. One of the points is not necessarily a material point – a setting that arises when using a closest point projection suitable for short-range interactions.
In the latter case, one surface, say
\mathcal{S}_{1}
, is designated the so-called
master surface
, while the other,
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, is designated the so-called
slave surface

(
Hallquist et al., 1985
)
.
The master surface is then used to define normal and tangential contact contributions.
Therefore the master point
\bm{x}_{1}
in (
12
) is determined as the closest point projection of slave point
\bm{x}_{2}
onto master surface
\mathcal{S}_{1}
.
Parameterizing
\mathcal{S}_{1}
by the curvilinear surface coordinates
\xi^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\{1,\,2\}
, the closest projection point is given by

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{x}_{\mathrm{p}}:=\bm{x}_{1}(\xi^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}},t)\,,\end{array}

(13)

where
\xi^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}
is the value of
\xi^{\alpha}
that solves the minimum distance problem

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{g}\cdot\bm{a}_{\alpha}=0\,.\end{array}

(14)

Here
\bm{a}_{\alpha}:=\partial\bm{x}_{1}/\partial\xi^{\alpha}
(
\alpha=1,2
) are tangent vectors of
\mathcal{S}_{1}
, which at
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{p}}
are denoted
\bm{a}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{p}}
, i.e.
\bm{a}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{p}}:=\bm{a}_{\alpha}(\xi^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}},t)
.
Likewise
\bm{n}_{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{n}_{1}(\xi^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}},t)
is the surface normal at
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{p}}
.
The closest point projection is illustrated in Fig.
2
.

The gap can be decomposed into its normal and tangential parts

\begin{array}[]{l}g_{\mathrm{n}}:=\bm{g}\cdot\bm{n}\,,\quad\bm{g}_{\mathrm{t}}:=\bm{g}-g_{\mathrm{n}}\,\bm{n}\,,\end{array}

(15)

where the latter is zero at
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{p}}
.
Hence, for contact
\bm{g}=g_{\mathrm{n}}\,\bm{n}_{\mathrm{p}}
and
g_{\mathrm{n}}=0
, so that

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\bm{g}}=\dot{g}_{\mathrm{n}}\,\bm{n}_{\mathrm{p}}\,.\end{array}

(16)

Based on (
12
) and (
13
), the material time derivative of
\bm{g}
can also be written as

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\bm{g}}=\dot{\bm{x}}_{2}-\dot{\bm{x}}_{1}-\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\bm{a}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{p}}\,,\end{array}

(17)

where, in agreement with definition (
1
),
\dot{\bm{x}}_{1}=\partial\bm{x}_{1}/\partial t|_{\xi^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}\,=\,\mathrm{fixed}}
and
\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}=\partial\xi^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}/\partial t|_{\bm{X}_{2}\,=\,\mathrm{fixed}}
.
The latter part

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{t}}:=\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}\,\bm{a}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{p}}\,,\end{array}

(18)

(with summation implied over
\alpha
and often denoted
\mathcal{L}_{v}\bm{g}_{\mathrm{t}}
) is equal to the
Lie derivative
of the tangential gap vector

(
Wriggers, 2006
)
.
Likewise (
16
) is equal to the Lie derivative of the normal gap vector.

\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{t}}
is zero when
\xi^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}
is fixed (s.t.
\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}=0
), i.e. when
\bm{x}_{1}
is a material point as in case i above.
In case ii,
\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{t}}
is equal to the relative tangential velocity between the two surfaces.
Thus,
\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}=0
also denotes the case of tangential sticking, while
\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}\neq 0
characterizes tangential sliding.
The sliding motion is irreversible and accumulates over time.
Combining (
16
) and (
17
), one can identify the velocity jump

\begin{array}[]{l}[\![\bm{v}]\!]:=\bm{v}_{2}-\bm{v}_{1}=\dot{g}_{\mathrm{n}}\,\bm{n}_{\mathrm{p}}+\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{t}}=\mathring{\bm{g}}\,,\end{array}

(19)

and see that it is the Lie derivative of the total gap vector in case ii.
Even during sticking, it can be advantageous (see remark
2.2
) to allow for (small) motion that is reversible upon unloading.
This leads to the tangential velocity decomposition

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}=\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{e}}+\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{i}}\,,\end{array}

(20)

where
\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{e}}
captures the reversible (elastic) motion during sticking, while
\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{i}}
captures the irreversible (inelastic) motion during sliding.
Plugging (
20
) into (
19
) yields

\begin{array}[]{l}[\![\bm{v}]\!]=\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{e}}+\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}\,,\end{array}

(21)

where
\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{e}}:=\dot{g}_{\mathrm{n}}\,\bm{n}_{\mathrm{p}}+\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\bm{a}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\alpha}
characterizes normal contact and tangential sticking, while
\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}:=\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{i}}\,\bm{a}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\alpha}
characterizes tangential sliding.
Decomposition (
21
) is analogous to decomposition (
4
), making the contact kinematics analogous to the kinematics of the bodies.
It contains Eq. (
19
) for the case
\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{e}}=0
.
Expressions for
\dot{g}_{\mathrm{n}}
and
\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}
can be found e.g. in
Wriggers, 2006
.

Remark 2.1
:

Apart from the tangential sticking constraint
\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}=0
, there is also the normal contact constraint
g_{\mathrm{n}}\geq 0
.
Normal and tangential contact are usually treated separately in
most friction algorithms, but there are also unified approaches directly based on the gap vector
\bm{g}
, e.g. see
Wriggers and Haraldsson, 2003
and
Duong and Sauer, 2019
.
Such an approach is also taken in the following sections.

Remark 2.2
:

There are reasons for relaxing the contact constraints
g_{\mathrm{n}}\geq 0
and
\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{p}}=0
.
One is to use a penalty regularization, which is typically simpler to implement than the exact constraint enforcement.
Another is to use the elastic gap
\bm{g}_{\mathrm{e}}
to capture the elastic deformation of (microscale) surface asperities during contact.
In both cases
\bm{g}_{\mathrm{e}}
is nonzero (but typically small) during contact.

Remark 2.3
:

The designation into
master
and
slave
surfaces introduces a bias in the contact formulation that can affect the accuracy and robustness of computational methods.
The bias can be removed if alternating
master/slave
designations are used, as is done for 3D friction in
Jones and Papadopoulos, 2006
and
Sauer and De Lorenzis, 2015
.

Remark 2.4
:

As seen above, the Lie derivatives
\mathring{\bm{g}}
and
\mathring{\bm{g}}_{e}
only contain the relative changes of the normal and tangential gap.
They do not contain the basis changes
\dot{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{p}}
and
\dot{\bm{a}}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\alpha}
.
This makes the Lie derivative objective and a suitable quantity for the constitutive modeling
(
Laursen, 2002
)
, see Sec.
4.1
.

3
Balance laws

This section derives the chemical, mechanical and thermal balance laws for a generally coupled two-body system.
The resulting equations turn out to be in the same form as the known relations for chemical reactions
(
Prigogine, 1961
)
and thermo-mechanical contact
(
Laursen, 2002
)
.
The novelty here lies in establishing their coupling and highlighting the similarities between the chemical, mechanical and thermal contact equations.
The derivation is based on the following three mathematical ingredients.
The first is Reynold’s transport theorem for volume integrals,

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}...\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}=\int_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\Big(\dot{(...)}+\mathrm{div}\,\bm{v}_{k}\,(...)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}\,.\end{array}

(23)

It follows from substituting (
6
) on the left, using the product rule, and then applying (
10
).
Applied to surfaces, Reynold’s transport theorem simply adapts to

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}...\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}\Big(\dot{(...)}+\mathrm{div}\,_{\!\mathrm{s}}\,\bm{v}_{k}\,(...)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}\,.\end{array}

(24)

It follows from substituting (
7
) on the left, using the product rule, and then applying (
11
).

The second ingredient is the divergence theorem,

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{B}_{k}}...\,\bm{n}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}=\int_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\mathrm{div}\,(...)\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}\,,\end{array}

(25)

where
\bm{n}_{k}
is the outward normal vector of boundary
\partial\mathcal{B}_{k}
.
The third ingredient is the localization theorem,

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}}...\,\mathrm{d}v=0\quad\forall\,\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{B}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad...=0\quad\forall\,\bm{x}\in\mathcal{B}\,,\end{array}

(26)

that can be equally applied to surface integrals.

3.1
Conservation of mass and bonding sites

Assuming no mass sources, the mass balance of each body is given by the statement

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}=0\quad\forall\,\mathcal{P}_{k}\subset\mathcal{B}_{k}\,.\end{array}

(27)

Applying (
23
) and (
26
), this leads to the local balance law

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\rho}_{k}+\rho_{k}\,\mathrm{div}\,\bm{v}_{k}=0\quad\forall\,\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}\,.\end{array}

(28)

Due to (
10
), this ODE is solved by
\rho_{k}=\rho_{0k}/J_{k}
, where
\rho_{0k}:=\rho_{k}\big|_{t=0}
is the initial mass density.

In order to model chemical bonding, the interacting surfaces are considered to have a certain bonding site density
n_{k}
(the number of bonding sites per current area) composed of bonded sites and unbonded sites, i.e.

\begin{array}[]{l}n_{k}=n_{k}^{\mathrm{b}}+n_{k}^{\mathrm{u}\mathrm{b}}\,.\end{array}

(29)

The number of bonding sites is considered to be conserved, i.e.

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}n_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}=0\quad\forall\,\mathcal{P}_{k}\subset\mathcal{S}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(30)

implying

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{n}_{k}+n_{k}\,\mathrm{div}\,_{\!\mathrm{s}}\,\bm{v}_{k}=0\quad\forall\,\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(31)

due to (
24
) and (
26
).
Due to (
11
), this ODE is solved by
n_{k}=N_{k}/J_{\mathrm{s}k}
, where
N_{k}:=n_{k}\big|_{t=0}
is the initial bonding site density.
Two cases will be considered in the following, see Fig.
4
:

(a) Long-range interaction

(between non-touching surfaces), where each bonding site on one surface can interact with all other bonding sites on the other surface.

Since, in this case, the total number of bonded sites is the same on both surfaces, we must have

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}n_{1}^{\mathrm{b}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}n_{2}^{\mathrm{b}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}}\,.\end{array}

(32)

If the two surfaces are very close,
\mathcal{S}_{1}\approx\mathcal{S}_{2}
can be replaced by a common reference surface
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}
.
Eq. (
32
) then implies that
n^{\mathrm{b}}_{1}=n^{\mathrm{b}}_{2}=:n_{\mathrm{b}}
due to localization (since (
32
) is still true for any subregion
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{c}}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}
).
This case is considered in the following.

The evolution of
n_{\mathrm{b}}
is described by the chemical reaction

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{A+B\rightleftharpoons AB}\,,\end{array}

(33)

where
\mathsf{AB}
denotes the bond, while
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
are its components on the two surfaces prior to bonding, see Fig.
4
b.
The bonding reaction is characterized by the reaction rate

\begin{array}[]{l}R_{\mathrm{c}}=\overrightarrow{R_{\mathrm{c}}}-\overleftarrow{R}_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(34)

composed of the bonding reaction rate
\overrightarrow{R_{\mathrm{c}}}
(forward reaction) and the debonding reaction rate
\overleftarrow{R}_{\mathrm{c}}
(backward reaction).
An example for these is discussed in Sec.
5.7
.
Given
R_{\mathrm{c}}
, the balance law for the bonded sites (considering no surface diffusion) then follows as
(
Sahu et al., 2017
)

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{c}}}n_{\mathrm{b}}\,\mathrm{d}a=\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{c}}}R_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a\quad\forall\,\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{c}}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(35)

which gives

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{n}_{\mathrm{b}}+n_{\mathrm{b}}\,\mathrm{div}\,_{\!\mathrm{s}}\,\bm{v}_{\mathrm{c}}=R_{\mathrm{c}}\quad\forall\,\bm{x}_{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(36)

where
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{c}}:=\bm{v}_{1}=\bm{v}_{2}
is the common velocity required to enable chemical bonding.
For the unbonded sites on the two surfaces, we have the two balance laws

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}n_{k}^{\mathrm{u}\mathrm{b}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}=-\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}R_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}\quad\forall\,\mathcal{P}_{k}\subset\mathcal{S}_{k}\,,\quad k=1,2\,,\end{array}

(37)

leading to

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{n}_{k}^{\mathrm{u}\mathrm{b}}+n_{k}^{\mathrm{u}\mathrm{b}}\,\mathrm{div}\,_{\!\mathrm{s}}\,\bm{v}_{k}=-R_{\mathrm{c}}\quad\forall\,\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(38)

where still
\bm{v}_{1}=\bm{v}_{2}
(as long as
n_{\mathrm{b}}>0
).
Summing (
36
) and (
38
) leads to (
31
), and so one equation (for each
k
) is redundant.

For convenience, the non dimensional phase field

\begin{array}[]{l}\phi:=\displaystyle\frac{n_{\mathrm{b}}}{n_{\mathrm{c}}}\,,\quad 0\leq\phi\leq 1\,,\end{array}

(39)

is introduced, where
n_{\mathrm{c}}
is the reference value for the bonding site density, either picked as
n_{\mathrm{c}}=n_{1}
, in case surface 1 is taken as reference, or
n_{\mathrm{c}}=n_{2}
, in case surface 2 is taken as reference.
5
5

5

Both choices are useful: The first implies using the
master surface

\mathcal{S}_{1}
, already used as a reference for mechanical contact, as reference surface for chemical contact.
On the other hand, the second choice is advantageous in computations, where usually the
slave surface

\mathcal{S}_{2}
is used for the integration of the weak form.

Then
(
36
) can be rewritten into

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{n_{\mathrm{c}}\,\dot{\phi}=R_{\mathrm{c}}}\quad\forall\,\bm{x}_{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(40)

using Eq. (
31
).
Eq. (
40
) is the evolution law for the chemical contact state.

Remark 3.1
:

As we are focusing on solids, the derivation of (
40
) assumes no surface mobility (or diffusion) of the bonds. In the context of fluidic membranes, the surface mobility of bonds is usually accounted for, e.g. see
Brochard-Wyart and de Gennes, 2002
and
Freund and Lin, 2004
.

Remark 3.2
:

In the derivation leading up to Eq. (
31
),
\bm{v}_{1}=\bm{v}_{2}
has been used.
But it suffices to assume that the time scale of chemical reactions is much smaller than the timescale of sliding, such that the two reacting surfaces can be assumed stationary w.r.t. one another.

3.2
Momentum balance

The linear momentum balance for the entire two-body system is given by

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,\bm{v}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\bigg[\int_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,\bar{\bm{b}}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}+\int_{\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}_{k}}\bar{\bm{t}}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}\bigg]\,,\end{array}

(41)

where
\bar{\bm{b}}_{k}
and
\bar{\bm{t}}_{k}
are prescribed body forces and surface tractions.
The latter are prescribed on the Neumann boundary
\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}_{k}\subset\partial\mathcal{B}_{k}
that is disjoint from contact surface
\mathcal{S}_{k}
.
The bonding events, described by Eq. (
40
), are not considered to affect the momentum of the system.

If the two bodies are cut apart, the additional contact interaction traction
\bm{t}_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}
needs to be taken into account on the interface (see Fig.
1
).
The individual momentum balance for every part
\mathcal{P}_{k}\subset\mathcal{B}_{k}
(
k=1,2
) then reads

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,\bm{v}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}=\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,\bar{\bm{b}}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}+\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}_{k}}\bm{t}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}\quad\forall\,\mathcal{P}_{k}\subset\mathcal{B}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(42)

where
\bm{t}_{k}
is the traction on surface
\partial\mathcal{P}_{k}
that contains the cases

\begin{array}[]{llll}\bm{t}_{k}&=&\!\!\!\bar{\bm{t}}_{k}&$on $\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}_{k}\,,\\[2.84526pt]
\bm{t}_{k}&=&\!\!\!\bm{t}^{\mathrm{c}}_{k}&$on $\mathcal{S}_{k}\,.\end{array}

(43)

Applying (
23
), (
25
) and (
26
) leads to the corresponding local form

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\rho_{k}\,\dot{\bm{v}}_{k}=\mathrm{div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}+\rho_{k}\,\bar{\bm{b}}_{k}}\quad\forall\,\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(44)

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}
is the Cauchy stress at
\bm{x}_{k}
that is defined by the formula

\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}\,\bm{n}_{k}=\bm{t}_{k}\,.\end{array}

(45)

Using (
42
), Eq. (
41
) implies

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}\bm{t}_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}\bm{t}_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}=\mathbf{0}}\,.\end{array}

(46)

This simply states that the net contact forces are in equilibrium.
It is the corresponding statement to Eq. (
32
) for mechanical contact.
If the two surfaces touch, i.e.
\mathcal{S}_{1}\approx\mathcal{S}_{2}=:\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\mathrm{d}a_{1}\approx\mathrm{d}a_{2}
, (
46
) implies that

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\bm{t}^{\mathrm{c}}_{1}=-\bm{t}^{\mathrm{c}}_{2}=:\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}}\quad\forall\bm{x}_{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}\,.\end{array}

(47)

Hence there is no jump in the contact traction.
Such a jump only arises if an interface stress (e.g. surface tension) is considered.

The angular momentum balances for the individual bodies and the entire two-body system can be written down analogously to (
42
) and
(
41
), respectively.
As long as no distributed body and surface moments are considered, the angular momentum balance of each body has the well-known consequence
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}^{\mathrm{T}}=\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}

(
Chadwick, 1976
)
, while global angular momentum balance implies

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}\bm{x}_{1}\times\bm{t}_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}\bm{x}_{2}\times\bm{t}_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}=\mathbf{0}}\,,\end{array}

(48)

analogously to (
46
).
This statement is relevant for separated surfaces (
\mathcal{S}_{1}\neq\mathcal{S}_{2}
), but in the case of touching surfaces (
\mathcal{S}_{1}\approx\mathcal{S}_{2}
), it leads to the already known traction equivalence (
47
) at common contact points
\bm{x}_{1}=\bm{x}_{2}
.

Remark 3.3
:

In (
47
) the
master surface

\mathcal{S}_{1}
is taken as reference surface for defining the reference traction
\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}
, which is commonly done in mechanical contact formulations
(
Wriggers, 2006
)
.
In case of chemical and thermal contact, however, the two possible choices
n_{\mathrm{c}}=n_{1}
or
n_{\mathrm{c}}=n_{2}
for the reference bonding site density introduced in (
39
) are maintained in this treatment.

3.3
Energy balance

The energy balance for the entire two-body system is

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{E}}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\bigg[\int_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,\bar{r}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}+\int_{\partial_{q}\mathcal{B}_{k}}\bar{q}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}+\int_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,\bm{v}_{k}\cdot\bar{\bm{b}}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}+\int_{\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}_{k}}\bm{v}_{k}\cdot\bar{\bm{t}}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}\bigg]\,,\end{array}

(49)

where the total energy in the system,

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{K}+\mathbb{U}\,,\end{array}

(50)

is given by the kinetic energy

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{K}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\rho_{1}\,\bm{v}_{1}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}v_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}\rho_{2}\,\bm{v}_{2}\cdot\bm{v}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}v_{2}\end{array}

(51)

and the internal energy

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{U}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\rho_{1}\,u_{1}\,\mathrm{d}v_{1}+\int_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}\rho_{2}\,u_{2}\,\mathrm{d}v_{2}+\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(52)

which accounts for the individual energies
u_{k}=u_{k}(\bm{x}_{k})
in
\mathcal{B}_{k}
and the contact energy
\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{c}}
.

\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{c}}
can describe long range surface interactions, as discussed in Remark
3.6
, or it can correspond to the energy of a third medium residing in the contact interface, e.g. a thin film of lubricants or wear particles.
In the latter case, assuming a sufficiently thin film,
\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{c}}
can be expressed as the surface integral

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{c}}:=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}}n_{\mathrm{c}}\,u_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a\,,\end{array}

(53)

where
u_{\mathrm{c}}
is the contact energy per bonding site on contact surface
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}
.
6
6

6

where – as in Eq. (
39
) – either surface 1 or surface 2 can be used as reference surface for defining
n_{\mathrm{c}}
, see also footnote 5.

Further,
\bar{r}_{k}
and
\bar{q}_{k}
in (
49
) denote external heat sources in
\mathcal{B}_{k}
and external heat influxes on
\partial_{q}\mathcal{B}_{k}\subset\partial\mathcal{B}_{k}
, respectively.
The Neumann boundary
\partial_{q}\mathcal{B}_{k}
is considered disjoint from contact surface
\mathcal{S}_{k}
.
An external heat source on the interface is not required here.
As will be seen later, the present setup already accounts for the heat from interfacial friction and interfacial reactions.

If the two bodies are cut apart, the additional contact heat influx
q_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}
needs to be taken into account on the interface (see Fig.
1
), leading to the individual energy balance for each body

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,e_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}&=&\!\!\!\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,\bar{r}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}+\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}_{k}}q_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}\\[11.38109pt]
&+&\!\!\!\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\bm{v}_{k}\cdot\rho_{k}\,\bar{\bm{b}}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}+\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}_{k}}\bm{v}_{k}\cdot\bm{t}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}\quad\forall\,\mathcal{P}_{k}\subset\mathcal{B}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(54)

where
e_{k}:=u_{k}+\bm{v}_{k}\cdot\bm{v}_{k}/2
and
\bm{t}_{k}
satisfies (
43
).
Further,
q_{k}
is the heat influx on surface
\partial\mathcal{P}_{k}
that contains the cases

\begin{array}[]{llll}q_{k}&=&\!\!\!\bar{q}_{k}&$on $\partial_{q}\mathcal{B}_{k}\,,\\[2.84526pt]
q_{k}&=&\!\!\!q^{\mathrm{c}}_{k}&$on $\mathcal{S}_{k}\,.\end{array}

(55)

Introducing the heat flux vector
\bm{q}_{k}
at
\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
that is defined by Stokes formula

\begin{array}[]{l}-\bm{q}_{k}\cdot\bm{n}_{k}=q_{k}\,,\end{array}

(56)

and using (
45
), the divergence theorem (
25
) can be applied to obtain

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}_{k}}q_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}=-\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\mathrm{div}\,\bm{q}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}\end{array}

(57)

and

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}_{k}}\bm{v}_{k}\cdot\bm{t}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}=\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\big(\bm{v}_{k}\cdot\mathrm{div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}+\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}:\bm{D}_{k}\big)\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}\,,\end{array}

(58)

where
\bm{D}_{k}
is the symmetric velocity gradient from (
9
).
Using (
23
), (
26
) and (
44
), the local form of (
54
) thus becomes

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\rho_{k}\,\dot{u}_{k}=\rho_{k}\,\bar{r}_{k}-\mathrm{div}\,\bm{q}_{k}+\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}:\bm{D}_{k}}\quad\forall\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}\,.\end{array}

(59)

With this and Eq. (
44
), the combined balance statement (
49
) implies

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\displaystyle\dot{\mathbb{U}}_{\mathrm{c}}+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}\big(q_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}+\bm{t}_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}\big)\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}\big(q_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}+\bm{t}_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}\cdot\bm{v}_{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}=0}\,,\end{array}

(60)

which is the corresponding statement to Eqs. (
32
) and (
46
) for thermal contact.
Here

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\mathbb{U}}_{\mathrm{c}}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}}n_{\mathrm{c}}\,\dot{u}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a\,,\end{array}

(61)

follows from (
53
) for bonding site conservation (
30
).
If the two surfaces are very close or even touch, i.e.
\mathcal{S}_{1}\approx\mathcal{S}_{2}=:\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\mathrm{d}a_{1}\approx\mathrm{d}a_{2}
(i.e. for very thin interfacial media), this implies that (since
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}
can be considered an arbitrary subregion of the contact surface)

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{n_{\mathrm{c}}\,\dot{u}_{\mathrm{c}}+q_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}+q_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}+\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot(\bm{v}_{1}-\bm{v}_{2})=0}\quad\forall\bm{x}_{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(62)

due to (
47
).
Eq. (
62
) states that the energy rate
\dot{u}_{\mathrm{c}}
and the mechanical contact power
\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot(\bm{v}_{1}-\bm{v}_{2})
cause the heat influxes
q_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}
on
\mathcal{S}_{k}
.
Eq. (
62
) is equivalent to Eq. (6.63) of
Laursen, 2002
.
7
7

7

In
Laursen, 2002
the contact traction
\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}
and heat fluxes
q_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}
are defined with opposite sign, and (
62
) is written per unit reference area.
Further,
u_{\mathrm{c}}
does not contain chemical energy in
Laursen, 2002
.

Remark 3.4
:

Using (
21
), the contact power becomes

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot(\bm{v}_{1}-\bm{v}_{2})=\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{e}}+\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}\,,\end{array}

(63)

showing that it contains elastic and inelastic contributions associated with sticking and sliding, respectively.
The first one is associated with energy stored in the contact interface (defined by (
82
), later).
This energy vanishes for an exact enforcement of sticking (
\bm{g}_{\mathrm{e}}=\mathbf{0}
).
The second contribution causes dissipation, which is also zero in the case of sticking (since
\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathbf{0}
) and in case of frictionless sliding (since
\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}\perp\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}
).
In principle, the second contribution could also contain stored energy.
In the context of plasticity such a stored energy occurs for hardening
(
Rosakis et al., 2000
)
.
But this is not considered here.

Remark 3.5
:

Note, that contrary to the contact tractions governed by (
47
), the contact heat flux according to (
62
) can have a jump across the interface.
This has also been recently explored by
Javili et al., 2014
.

Remark 3.6
:

For long-range interactions between two surfaces,
\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{c}}
can be written as
8
8

8

e.g. by coarse-graining the molecular interactions across the two surfaces
(
Sauer and Li, 2007b
)

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{U}_{\mathrm{c}}:=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}n_{1}\,n_{2}\,u_{12}\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}\,,\end{array}

(64)

where
u_{12}=u_{12}(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})
is an interaction energy defined between points on the two surfaces.
For bonding site conservation (
30
) this leads to

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\mathbb{U}}_{\mathrm{c}}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}n_{1}\,n_{2}\,\dot{u}_{12}\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}\,,\end{array}

(65)

in (
60
).
Localization in the form of (
62
) is not possible for long-range interaction so that Eq. (
60
) then remains the only governing equation.

3.4
Entropy balance and the second law of thermodynamics

The entropy balance for the entire two-body system can be written as
6

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{S}}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\bigg[\int_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,\big(\eta^{\mathrm{e}}_{k}+\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{k}\big)\,\mathrm{d}v_{k}+\int_{\partial_{q}\mathcal{B}_{k}}\bar{\tilde{q}}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a_{k}\bigg]+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}}n_{\mathrm{c}}\,\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a\,,\end{array}

(66)

where the total entropy in the system,

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{S}:=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}\rho_{1}\,s_{1}\,\mathrm{d}v_{1}+\int_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}\rho_{2}\,s_{2}\,\mathrm{d}v_{2}+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}}n_{\mathrm{c}}\,s_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a\,,\end{array}

(67)

accounts for the individual entropies
s_{k}
in
\mathcal{B}_{k}
and the contact entropy
s_{\mathrm{c}}
that is associated with an interfacial medium.
Further,
\eta_{k}^{\mathrm{e}}
and
\eta_{k}^{\mathrm{i}}
are the external and internal entropy production rates in
\mathcal{B}_{k}
,
\bar{\tilde{q}}_{k}
is the entropy influx on the heat flux boundary
\partial_{q}\mathcal{B}_{k}
, and
\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{\mathrm{c}}
is the internal entropy production rate of the interface.
An external entropy production rate is not needed for the interface, as long as no heat source is considered in the interface.
Like
u_{\mathrm{c}}
in (
53
),
s_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{\mathrm{c}}
are bond-specific.

If the two bodies are cut apart, the additional contact entropy influx
\tilde{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}
needs to be taken into account on the interface, leading to the individual entropy balances (
k=1,2
)

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,s_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a=\int_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\rho_{k}\,\big(\eta^{\mathrm{e}}_{k}+\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{k}\big)\,\mathrm{d}v+\int_{\partial\mathcal{P}_{k}}\tilde{q}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}a\quad\forall\,\mathcal{P}_{k}\subset\mathcal{B}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(68)

where
\tilde{q}_{k}
is the entropy influx on surface
\partial\mathcal{P}_{k}
that contains the cases

\begin{array}[]{llll}\tilde{q}_{k}&=&\!\!\!\bar{\tilde{q}}_{k}&$on $\partial_{q}\mathcal{B}_{k}\,,\\[2.84526pt]
\tilde{q}_{k}&=&\!\!\!\tilde{q}^{\mathrm{c}}_{k}&$on $\mathcal{S}_{k}\,.\end{array}

(69)

Introducing the entropy flux
\tilde{\bm{q}}_{k}
in body
\mathcal{B}_{k}
defined by

\begin{array}[]{l}-\tilde{\bm{q}}_{k}\cdot\bm{n}_{k}=\tilde{q}_{k}\,,\end{array}

(70)

theorems (
23
), (
25
) and (
26
) can be applied to (
68
) to give the local form

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\rho_{k}\,\dot{s}_{k}=\rho_{k}\,\eta^{\mathrm{e}}_{k}+\rho_{k}\,\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{k}-\mathrm{div}\,\tilde{\bm{q}}_{k}}\quad\forall\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}\,.\end{array}

(71)

Plugging this equation into the total entropy balance (
66
) implies

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}}n_{\mathrm{c}}\,\big(\dot{s}_{\mathrm{c}}-\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{\mathrm{c}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}a+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}\tilde{q}_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}\tilde{q}_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}=0}\,.\end{array}

(72)

This is the corresponding entropy statement to Eqs. (
32
), (
46
) and (
60
).
If the two surfaces touch, i.e.
\mathcal{S}_{1}\,\approx\mathcal{S}_{2}=:\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}
, it implies that

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{n_{\mathrm{c}}\,\dot{s}_{\mathrm{c}}=n_{1}\,\eta_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{i}}-\tilde{q}_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}-\tilde{q}_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}}\quad\forall\bm{x}_{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{c}}\,.\end{array}

(73)

The second law of thermodynamics states that

\begin{array}[]{l}\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{k}\geq 0\,,\quad\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{\mathrm{c}}\geq 0\,.\end{array}

(74)

In the absence of chemical contact, (
73
) then becomes equivalent to Eq. (6.66) of
Laursen, 2002
.

4
General constitution

This section derives the general constitutive equations for the two bodies and their contact interface – as they follow from the internal energy and the second law of thermodynamics.
In general, the internal energy is a function of the mechanical, chemical and thermal state of the system that is characterized by the deformation, the bonding state and the entropy.
Introducing the Helmholtz free energy, the thermal state can be characterized by the temperature instead of the entropy.
The derivation uses the framework of general irreversible thermodynamics established in chemistry
(
Onsager, 1931a
;
Onsager, 1931b
;
Prigogine, 1961
;
de Groot and Mazur, 1984
)
and mechanics
(
Coleman and Noll, 1964
)
following the coupled thermo-mechanical treatment of
Laursen, 2002
and the coupled chemo-mechanical treatment of
Sahu et al., 2017
.
The novelty here is the extension to coupled thermo-chemo-mechanical contact leading to the establishment of the interfacial thermo-chemo-mechanical energy balance in Eq. (
108
), which is then discussed in detail.

4.1
Thermodynamic potentials

For each
\mathcal{B}_{k}
, the Helmholtz free energy (per mass),

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi_{k}=u_{k}-T_{k}\,s_{k}\,,\end{array}

(75)

is introduced as the chosen thermodynamic potential.
Thus,

\begin{array}[]{l}T_{k}\,\dot{s}_{k}=\dot{u}_{k}-\dot{\psi}_{k}-\dot{T}_{k}\,s_{k}\,.\end{array}

(76)

Inserting (
59
), then leads to

\begin{array}[]{l}T_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,\dot{s}_{k}=\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}:\bm{D}_{k}+\rho_{k}\,\bar{r}_{k}-\mathrm{div}\,\bm{q}_{k}-\rho_{k}\,\big(\dot{\psi}_{k}+\dot{T}_{k}\,s_{k}\big)\,.\end{array}

(77)

For the interface, the Helmholtz free energy (per bonding site) is introduced as

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi_{\mathrm{c}}=u_{\mathrm{c}}-T_{\mathrm{c}}\,s_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(78)

where
T_{\mathrm{c}}
is the interface temperature introduced in Sec.
2
(see Fig.
1
).
Thus,

\begin{array}[]{l}T_{\mathrm{c}}\,\dot{s}_{\mathrm{c}}=\dot{u}_{\mathrm{c}}-\dot{\psi}_{\mathrm{c}}-\dot{T}_{\mathrm{c}}\,s_{\mathrm{c}}\,.\end{array}

(79)

Inserting (
62
), then leads to

\begin{array}[]{l}n_{\mathrm{c}}\,T_{\mathrm{c}}\,\dot{s}_{\mathrm{c}}=-\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot(\bm{v}_{1}-\bm{v}_{2})-q_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}-q_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}-n_{\mathrm{c}}\,\big(\dot{\psi}_{\mathrm{c}}+\dot{T}_{\mathrm{c}}\,s_{\mathrm{c}}\big)\,.\end{array}

(80)

In this study, the Helmholtz free energy within
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is considered to take the functional form

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi_{k}=\psi_{k}\big(\bm{E}^{\mathrm{e}}_{k},T_{k}\big)\,,\end{array}

(81)

where
\bm{E}^{\mathrm{e}}_{k}
is the elastic Green-Lagrange strain tensor introduced by Eq. (
4
).
For the interface, the Helmholtz free energy is considered to take the form

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi_{\mathrm{c}}=\psi_{\mathrm{c}}\big(\bm{g}_{\mathrm{e}},T_{\mathrm{c}},\phi\big)\,,\end{array}

(82)

where
\bm{g}_{\mathrm{e}}=g_{\mathrm{n}}\,\bm{n}_{\mathrm{p}}+\xi^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\bm{a}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\alpha}
is the elastic part of the gap as introduced by Eq. (
21
).
From Eqs. (
81
) and (
82
) follow

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\psi}_{k}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\psi_{k}}}{\partial{\bm{E}^{\mathrm{e}}_{k}}}:\dot{\bm{E}}^{\mathrm{e}}_{k}+\frac{\partial{\psi_{k}}}{\partial{T_{k}}}\,\dot{T}_{k}\end{array}

(83)

and

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\psi}_{\mathrm{c}}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\psi_{\mathrm{c}}}}{\partial{\bm{g}_{\mathrm{e}}}}\cdot\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{e}}+\frac{\partial{\psi_{\mathrm{c}}}}{\partial{T_{\mathrm{c}}}}\,\dot{T}_{\mathrm{c}}+\frac{\partial{\psi_{\mathrm{c}}}}{\partial{\phi}}\,\dot{\phi}\,.\end{array}

(84)

In (
84
)
\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{e}}=\dot{g}_{\mathrm{n}}\,\bm{n}_{\mathrm{p}}+\dot{\xi}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{e}}\,\bm{a}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\alpha}
appears instead of
\dot{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{e}}
(cf.
Laursen, 2002
, Eq. (6.72)), since
\psi_{\mathrm{c}}
should be objective and hence not depend on the surface basis, i.e.
\partial\psi_{\mathrm{c}}/\partial\bm{n}_{\mathrm{p}}
and
\partial\psi_{\mathrm{c}}/\partial\bm{a}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{p}}
vanish.
The following subsections proceed to derive the general constitutive equations for the two bodies and their interaction based on
\psi_{k}
and
\psi_{\mathrm{c}}
.

4.2
Constitutive equations for the two individual bodies

Inserting (
71
) and (
77
) into (
74
.1), gives

\begin{array}[]{l}\rho_{k}\,\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{k}=\rho_{k}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{\bar{r}_{k}}{T_{k}}-\eta_{k}^{\mathrm{e}}\bigg)-\mathrm{div}\,\bigg(\frac{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{k}}}{T_{k}}-\tilde{\bm{q}}_{\mathrm{k}}\bigg)-\frac{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\mathrm{grad}\,T_{k}}{T_{k}^{2}}+\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}:\bm{D}_{k}}{T_{k}}-\frac{\rho_{k}}{T_{k}}\big(\dot{\psi}_{k}+\dot{T}_{k}\,s_{k}\big)\geq 0\,.\end{array}

(85)

Since this is true for any
\bar{r}_{k}
,
\bm{q}_{k}
,
T_{k}
,
\bm{v}_{k}
and
\dot{\psi}_{k}
, we can identify the relations

\begin{array}[]{l}\eta_{k}^{e}=\displaystyle\frac{\bar{r}_{k}}{T_{k}}~~$and$~~\tilde{\bm{q}}_{k}=\displaystyle\frac{\bm{q}_{k}}{T_{k}}\end{array}

(86)

for the external entropy source and the entropy flux.
We are then left with the well know dissipation inequality

\begin{array}[]{l}\rho_{k}\,\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{k}=\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}:\bm{D}_{k}}{T_{k}}-\frac{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\mathrm{grad}\,T_{k}}{T_{k}^{2}}-\frac{\rho_{k}}{T_{k}}\big(\dot{\psi}_{k}+\dot{T}_{k}\,s_{k}\big)\geq 0\,,\end{array}

(87)

which, in view of (
4
), (
83
) and

\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}:\bm{D}_{k}=\bm{S}_{\!k}:\dot{\bm{E}}_{k}/J_{k}\,,\end{array}

(88)

where
\bm{S}_{k}:=J_{k}\,\bm{F}_{\!k}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}\,\bm{F}_{\!k}^{-\mathrm{T}}
is the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress, becomes

\begin{array}[]{l}T_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,\eta^{\mathrm{i}}_{k}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{J_{k}}\bigg(\bm{S}_{\!k}-\frac{\partial{\mathsf{\Psi}_{k}}}{\partial{\bm{E}^{\mathrm{e}}_{k}}}\bigg):\dot{\bm{E}}^{\mathrm{e}}_{k}-\rho_{k}\,\bigg(s_{k}+\frac{\partial{\psi_{k}}}{\partial{T_{k}}}\bigg)\dot{T}_{k}+\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{k}:\bm{D}_{k}^{\mathrm{i}}-\frac{\bm{q}_{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\mathrm{grad}\,T_{k}}{T_{k}}\geq 0\,.\end{array}

(89)

Here
\mathsf{\Psi}_{k}:=J_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,\psi_{k}=\rho_{k0}\,\psi_{k}
is the Helmholtz free energy per reference volume, and

\bm{D}_{k}^{\mathrm{i}}
is the inelastic part of
\bm{D}_{k}
that is related to
\bm{E}_{k}^{\mathrm{i}}
via (
88
).
Since (
89
) is true for any
\bm{E}_{k}
and
T_{k}
, we find the constitutive relations

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\bm{S}_{k}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathsf{\Psi}_{k}}}{\partial{\bm{E}^{\mathrm{e}}_{k}}}}\,,\quad\boxed{s_{k}=-\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\psi_{k}}}{\partial{T_{k}}}}\,,\quad\boxed{\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}\cdot\bm{D}^{\mathrm{i}}_{k}\geq 0}\,,\quad\boxed{\bm{q}_{k}\cdot\mathrm{grad}\,T_{k}\leq 0}\,.\end{array}

(90)

Inserting (
83
) and (
90
) back into (
77
) then leads to the entropy evolution equation

\begin{array}[]{l}T_{k}\,\rho_{k}\,\dot{s}_{k}=\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}:\bm{D}^{\mathrm{i}}_{k}+\rho_{k}\,\bar{r}_{k}-\mathrm{div}\,\bm{q}_{k}\,.\end{array}

(91)

Here, the term
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}:\bm{D}^{\mathrm{i}}_{k}+\rho_{k}\,\bar{r}_{k}
can be understood as an effective heat source.

The equations in (
90
) are the classical constitutive equations for thermo-mechanical bodies.
They happen to be very similar to the contact equations obtained in the following section.

Remark 4.1
:

The above derivation considers elastic and inelastic strain rates following from (
4
), which are analogous counterparts to the elastic and inelastic velocity jump, see (
21
), required for a general contact description.
On the other hand, a decomposition of stress
\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{\!k}
is not considered, which corresponds to a Maxwell-like model for the elastic and inelastic behavior.
For other constitutive models, that are not considered here, elastic and inelastic stress contributions can appear and need to be separated.

Remark 4.2
:

Apart from constitutive equations (
90
) and the corresponding field equations (
44
) and (
91
), one also needs to determine the strain decomposition of (
4
).
Hence another equation is needed, e.g. an evolution law for the inelastic strain
\bm{E}_{k}^{\mathrm{i}}
.
A simple example is thermal expansion, where
\bm{E}_{k}^{\mathrm{i}}
follows from temperature
T_{k}
.
Likewise, an evolution law for the inelastic gap
\bm{g}_{\mathrm{i}}
will be needed, e.g. see
Wriggers, 2006
.

Constitutive equations for the contact interface

Since inequality (

T_{k}

\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{e}}

\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\bm{\mathsf{t}}_{\mathsf{c}}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathsf{\Psi_{c}}}}{\partial{\bm{g}_{\mathrm{e}}}}}\,,\quad\boxed{\bm{t}_{\mathrm{c}}\cdot\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}\geq 0}\end{array}

(95)

for the contact tractions,

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{s_{\mathrm{c}}=-\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\psi_{\mathrm{c}}}}{\partial{T_{\mathrm{c}}}}}\end{array}

(96)

for the interfacial entropy,

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\mu_{\mathrm{c}}\,R_{\mathrm{c}}\leq 0}\end{array}

(97)

for the reaction rate
R_{\mathrm{c}}
(from (

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{1}{T_{1}}-\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{c}}}\bigg)q_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}+\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{1}{T_{2}}-\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{c}}}\bigg)q_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}\geq 0\,,\end{array}

Multiplying by

\begin{array}[]{lll}T_{2}\big(T_{\mathrm{c}}-T_{1}\big)\,q_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}+T_{1}\big(T_{\mathrm{c}}-T_{2}\big)\,q_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}\geq 0\,.\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{l}q_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}=h_{k}\,\big(T_{\mathrm{c}}-T_{k}\big)\,,\end{array}

(

\begin{array}[]{l}q^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{m}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}>0&$exothermic contact reaction,$\\

4.4
Constitutive equations for non-touching (non-dissipative) interactions

n case of non-touching (long-range) interactions, the two surfaces remain distinct, i.e.
\mathcal{S}_{1}\neq\mathcal{S}_{2}
.
For simplification we consider that the surfaces have the uniform temperatures
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, and that the space between between
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
has no mass, temperature or entropy, i.e.
s_{\mathrm{c}}\equiv 0
.
Further, since the surfaces don’t touch, no bonding can take place, i.e.
\phi\equiv 0
.
The Helmholtz free energy of the interface then is

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi_{12}=u_{12}\,,\end{array}

(117)

so that
\dot{u}_{12}=\dot{\psi}_{12}
.
The Helmholtz free energy is now considered to take the form

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi_{12}=\psi_{12}\big(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})\,,\end{array}

(118)

such that

\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\psi}_{12}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\psi_{12}}}{\partial{\bm{x}_{1}}}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}+\frac{\partial{\psi_{12}}}{\partial{\bm{x}_{2}}}\cdot\bm{v}_{2}\,.\end{array}

(119)

Inserting this into (
60
) yields

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}\bigg(\bm{t}_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}+n_{1}\frac{\partial{\psi^{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}}{\partial{\bm{x}_{1}}}\bigg)\cdot\bm{v}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}+\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}\bigg(\bm{t}_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}+n_{2}\frac{\partial{\psi^{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}}{\partial{\bm{x}_{2}}}\bigg)\cdot\bm{v}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}q^{\mathrm{c}}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}+\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}q^{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}=0\end{array}

(120)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi^{\mathrm{c}}_{1}:=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}n_{1}\,\psi_{12}\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}~,\quad\psi^{\mathrm{c}}_{2}:=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}n_{2}\,\psi_{12}\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}\,.\end{array}

(121)

Noting that
\bm{v}_{k}
and
q_{k}^{\mathrm{c}}
are arbitrary and can be independently taken as zero, we find

\begin{array}[]{l}\boxed{\bm{t}_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}=-n_{1}\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\psi^{\mathrm{c}}_{2}}}{\partial{\bm{x}_{1}}}}~,\quad\boxed{\bm{t}_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}=-n_{2}\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\psi^{\mathrm{c}}_{1}}}{\partial{\bm{x}_{2}}}}~,\end{array}

(122)

for the interaction tractions,
and

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}q_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{1}+\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}_{2}}q_{2}^{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{d}a_{2}=0\,,\end{array}

(123)

for the interaction heat fluxes.
At the same time (
72
) and (

A simple example for the contact potential (per unit reference area) is the quadratic function

Remark 5.1
:

As noted in Remark

Remark 5.2

The last part in (

Remark 5.3

Choice (

Remark 5.4

Remark 5.5

The contact traction

Remark 5.6

5.2
Adhesion / debonding limit

An adhesion or debonding limit implies a lower bound on the contact pressure
p_{\mathrm{c}}
, i.e.

\begin{array}[]{l}-p_{\mathrm{c}}\leq t_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{max}}\,,\end{array}

(131)

\begin{array}[]{l}t_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{max}}=\phi\,\displaystyle\frac{T_{0}}{T_{\mathrm{c}}}\,t_{0}^{\mathrm{max}}\,.\end{array}

(132)

According to this, the bond strength is increasing with
\phi
and decreasing with
T_{\mathrm{c}}
.

Once the limit is reached, sudden debonding occurs, resulting in
\phi=0
.

5.3
Non-touching adhesion

An example for non-touching contact interactions according to Sec.

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi_{12}(\bar{g}_{k})=\displaystyle\psi_{0}\,\bigg[\frac{1}{45}\bigg(\frac{g_{0}}{\bar{g}_{k}}\bigg)^{\!\!10}\!-\frac{1}{3}\bigg(\frac{g_{0}}{\bar{g}_{k}}\bigg)^{\!\!4}\,\bigg]\,,\quad\bar{g}_{k}:=\|\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{x}_{\ell}\|>0\,,\end{array}

5.4
Sticking limit

Similar to the debonding limit (
131
), a sticking limit implies a bound on the tangential traction
\bm{t}_{\mathrm{t}}

\begin{array}[]{l}\|\bm{t}_{\mathrm{t}}\|\leq t_{\mathrm{t}}^{\mathrm{max}}\,,\end{array}

(139)

where the limit value

is generally a function of contact pressure, temperature and bonding state, i.e.

t_{\mathrm{t}}^{\mathrm{max}}=t_{\mathrm{t}}^{\mathrm{max}}(p_{\mathrm{c}},T_{\mathrm{c}},\phi)

It is reasonable to assume that it is monotonically increasing with
p_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\phi
and decreasing with
T_{\mathrm{c}}

\begin{array}[]{l}t_{\mathrm{t}}^{\mathrm{max}}=\mu_{0}\,p_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(140)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}\mu_{0}=\big(\phi\,\mu_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}+(1-\phi)\,\mu_{0}^{\mathrm{ub}}\big)\displaystyle\frac{T_{0}}{T_{\mathrm{c}}}\end{array}

(141)

is a temperature- and bonding state-dependent coefficient of sticking friction based on the constants
\mu_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}
and
\mu_{0}^{\mathrm{ub}}
describing the limits for full bonding and full unbonding, respectively.
Once the limit is reached, the bonds break (
\phi=0
) and tangential sliding occurs, as is discussed in the following section.

5.5
Sliding models

The simplest viscous friction model satisfying (
95
.2) is the tangential traction model

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{t}_{\mathrm{t}}=\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\,\displaystyle\frac{\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}}{g_{\mathrm{n}}}\,,\end{array}

(142)

where
\eta
is the positive definite dynamic viscosity tensor and
g_{\mathrm{n}}
is the normal gap.
In the isotropic case,
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}=\eta\mathbf{1}
.

One of the simplest dry friction models satisfying (
95
.2) for
p_{\mathrm{c}}\geq 0
is the Amontons-Coulomb law
12
12

12

Often just referred to as Amontons’ law or Coulomb’s law.

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{t}_{\mathrm{t}}=\bm{\mu}\,p_{\mathrm{c}}\displaystyle\frac{\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}}{\|\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}\|}\,,\end{array}

(143)

where
\bm{\mu}
is the positive definite coefficient tensor for sliding friction.
In the isotropic case,
\bm{\mu}=\mu\mathbf{1}
.
For adhesion, where
p_{\mathrm{c}}\geq-t^{\mathrm{max}}_{\mathrm{n}}
, this extends to

\begin{array}[]{l}\bm{t}_{\mathrm{t}}=\bm{\mu}\,\big(p_{\mathrm{c}}+t_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{max}}\big)\displaystyle\frac{\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}}{\|\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}\|}\,.\end{array}

(144)

In the adhesion literature this extension is often attributed to
Derjaguin, 1934
, whereas in soil mechanics it is usually referred to as Mohr-Coulomb’s law.
Note that
\bm{\mu}\,t_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{max}}
can be taken as a new constant.
The application of (
144
) to coupled adhesion and friction in the context of nonlinear 3D elasticity has been recently considered by
Mergel et al., 2019
;
Mergel et al., 2021
.

Remark 5.12
:

A transition model between dry (rate-independent) and viscous (rate-dependent) sliding friction is Stribeck’s curve, e.g. see
Gelinck and Schipper, 2000
.

Remark 5.13
:

The above sliding models can be temperature dependent.
An example for a temperature dependent friction coefficient
\mu=\mu(T_{\mathrm{c}})
is given in
Laursen, 2002
.
Temperature dependent viscosity models are e.g. discussed in
Roelands, 1966
and
Seeton, 2006
.

Remark 5.14
:

The present formulation assumes fixed bonding sites that break during sliding friction.
Sliding friction is thus independent from
\phi
.
Bonding sites can, however, also be mobile and hence stay intact during sliding.
Sliding friction may thus depend on
\phi
.
This requires a mobility model for the bonds, e.g. following the work of
Freund and Lin, 2004
.

Remark 5.15
:

The friction coefficient can be considered dependent on the surface deformation, as has been done by
Stupkiewicz, 2001
.

Remark 5.16
:

Friction coefficients that are dependent on the sliding velocity and (wear) state have been considered in the framework of
rate and state friction models
(
Dieterich, 1978
;
Ruina, 1983
)
.
Those models are usually based on an additive decomposition of the shear traction
\bm{t}_{\mathrm{t}}
instead of an additive slip decomposition as is used here.

Remark 5.17
:

Note that
\bm{t}_{\mathrm{t}}
causes the mechanical dissipation
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mech}}=\bm{t}_{\mathrm{t}}\cdot\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}
that leads to heating of the contact bodies
due to Eq. (
108
).
The mechanical dissipation is
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mech}}=\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\,\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}/g_{\mathrm{n}}
for model (
142
) and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{mech}}=p_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}\cdot\bm{\mu}\,\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}/\|\mathring{\bm{g}}_{\mathrm{i}}\|
for model (
143
); see Sec.
6.1
for an example.

5.6
Heat transfer

The simplest heat transfer model satisfying condition (
105
) is the linear relationship already given in (
106
), where
h>0
is the heat transfer coefficient, also referred to as the thermal contact conductance.
Eq. (
106
) is analogous to the mechanical flux model in (
129
.1).
Similar to the mechanical case,
h\rightarrow\infty
implies
T_{2}-T_{1}\rightarrow 0
.
In general,
h
can be a function of the contact pressure, gap or bonding state, i.e.
h=h(p_{\mathrm{c}},g_{\mathrm{n}},\phi)
.
Various models have been considered in the past.
Those usually consider
h
to be additively split into a contribution coming from actual contact and contributions coming from radiation and convection across a small contact gap.

During actual contact (
g_{\mathrm{n}}=0
,
p_{\mathrm{c}}>0
) a simple model is the power law dependency on the contact pressure,

\begin{array}[]{l}h=h_{0}+h_{\mathrm{c}}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{p_{\mathrm{c}}}{H}\bigg)^{\!q}\,,\end{array}

(145)

where
h_{0}
,
h_{\mathrm{c}}
,
H
and
q
are positive constants
(
Wriggers and Miehe, 1994
;
Laschet et al., 2004
)
.

This model is a simplification of the more detailed model of
Song and Yovanovich, 1988
that accounts for microscopic contact roughness.
Another model for
h
is the model by
Mikić, 1974
.
A new model has also been proposed recently by
Martins et al., 2016
.
The dependency of (
145
) on the bonding state can, for example, be taken as

\begin{array}[]{lllll}h_{0}&=&\!\!\!\phi\,h_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}&+&\!\!\!(1-\phi)\,h_{0}^{\mathrm{ub}}\,,\\[4.2679pt]
h_{\mathrm{c}}&=&\!\!\!\phi\,h_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}&+&\!\!\!(1-\phi)\,h_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{ub}}\,,\end{array}

(146)

i.e. assuming that
h_{0}
and
h_{\mathrm{c}}
increase monotonically with
\phi
.
Here
h_{0}^{\mathrm{b}}
,
h_{0}^{\mathrm{ub}}
,
h_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{b}}
and
h_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{ub}}
are model constants.

Out of contact (
g_{\mathrm{n}}>0
,
p_{\mathrm{c}}=0
,
\phi=0
), the heat tranfer depends on the contact gap.
Considering small
g_{\mathrm{n}}
,
Laschet et al., 2004
propose an exponential decay of
h
with
g_{\mathrm{n}}
according to

\begin{array}[]{l}h=h_{\mathrm{rad}}+h_{\mathrm{gas}}+(h_{0}-h_{\mathrm{rad}}-h_{\mathrm{gas}})\exp(-C_{\mathrm{trans}}\,g_{\mathrm{n}})\,,\end{array}

(147)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}h_{\mathrm{rad}}=\displaystyle\frac{c_{\mathrm{rad}}\,(T_{1}^{2}+T_{2}^{2})\,(T_{1}+T_{2})}{\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}+\varepsilon_{2}^{-1}-1}\end{array}

(148)

and

\begin{array}[]{l}h_{\mathrm{gas}}=\displaystyle\frac{k_{\mathrm{gas}}(T_{\mathrm{c}})}{g_{\mathrm{n}}+g_{0}(T_{\mathrm{c}})}\end{array}

(149)

correspond to the heat transfer across the gap due to radiation and gas convection, respectively.
Here,
C_{\mathrm{trans}}
,
c_{\mathrm{rad}}
,
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
are constants, while
k_{\mathrm{gas}}
and
g_{0}
depend on the contact temperature
T_{\mathrm{c}}
.

We note that all the above models are consistent with the 2. law as long as
h>0
.

5.7
Bonding reactions

The simplest reaction rate model satisfying condition (
97
) is the linear relationship,

\begin{array}[]{l}R_{\mathrm{c}}=-C_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mu_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(150)

where the constant
C_{\mathrm{r}}\geq 0
can for example be a function of the contact temperature and pressure, i.e.
C_{\mathrm{r}}=C_{\mathrm{r}}(p_{\mathrm{c}},T_{\mathrm{c}})
.
Writing
C_{\mathrm{r}}=n_{\mathrm{c}}\,c_{\mathrm{r}}
, the reaction equation in (
40
) thus becomes
\dot{\phi}=-c_{\mathrm{r}}\,\mu_{\mathrm{c}}
.
Alternatively (and in consistency with (
97
)),
c_{\mathrm{r}}\geq 0
can be taken as a constant.

Another, more sophisticated example that satisfies (
97
) is the exponential relationship
(
Sahu et al., 2017
)
,

\begin{array}[]{l}R_{\mathrm{c}}=C_{\mathrm{r}}\,R\,T_{\mathrm{c}}\,\bigg(1-\displaystyle\exp\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{c}}}{R\,T_{\mathrm{c}}}\bigg)\,,\end{array}

(151)

where
R
is the universal gas constant.

Remark 5.18
:

Plugging example (
129
.3) and (
39
) into (
150
) yields
R_{\mathrm{c}}=c_{\mathrm{r}}\,k_{\mathrm{c}}\,(n_{\mathrm{c}}-n_{\mathrm{b}})
with
k_{\mathrm{c}}=\mathsf{K_{c}}/N_{\mathrm{c}}
, which is a pure forward reaction (see also Remark
5.3
).

\overrightarrow{k}:=c_{\mathrm{r}}\,k_{\mathrm{c}}
is then the forward reaction rate coefficient.
An example on how this could depend on the contact gap is given in
Sun et al., 2009
.

Remark 5.19
:

On the other hand, modification (
130
) leads to
\mathsf{M_{c}}=\mathsf{K_{c}}\,(\phi-\phi_{\mathrm{eq}})
instead of (
129
.3).
Substituting
\mathsf{K_{c}}=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{K_{c}}}+\overleftarrow{\mathsf{K_{c}}}
and
\phi_{\mathrm{eq}}=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{K_{c}}}/\mathsf{K_{c}}
then leads to

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{M_{c}}=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{K_{c}}}\,(\phi-1)+\overleftarrow{\mathsf{K_{c}}}\,\phi\,,\end{array}

(152)

such that (
150
) leads to (
34
) with the reaction rates

\begin{array}[]{llllll}\overrightarrow{R_{\mathrm{c}}}&=&\!\!\!\overrightarrow{k}\,(n_{\mathrm{c}}-n_{\mathrm{b}})\,,&\overrightarrow{k}&:=&\!\!\!c_{\mathrm{r}}\,\overrightarrow{\mathsf{K_{c}}}/N_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\\[2.84526pt]
\overleftarrow{R_{\mathrm{c}}}&=&\!\!\!\overleftarrow{k}\,n_{\mathrm{b}}\,,&\overleftarrow{k}&:=&\!\!\!c_{\mathrm{r}}\,\overleftarrow{\mathsf{K_{c}}}/N_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(153)

for chemical bonding (forward reaction) and debonding (backward reaction), respectively.
The case in Remark
5.18

\overleftarrow{k}=0

6
Elementary contact test cases

This section presents the analytical solution of three new elementary contact test cases:
thermo-mechanical sliding, thermo-chemical bonding and thermo-chemo-mechanical debonding.
Such test cases are for example useful for the verification of a computational implementation.
Another test case – for thermo-mechanical normal contact – can be found in
Wriggers and Miehe, 1994
.
All test cases consider two blocks with initial height
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
brought into contact.
The energy and temperature change in the contacting bodies is then computed
assuming instantaneous heat transfer through the bodies (
\bm{q}_{k}=\mathbf{0}
),
no heat source in the bulk (
\bar{r}_{k}=0
),
no boundary heat flux apart from
q^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{m}}
(
\bar{q}_{k}=0
&
q^{\mathrm{c}}_{\mathrm{t}}=0
),
equal temperature within the bodies (
T_{1}=T_{2}=T_{\mathrm{c}}
),
quasi-static deformation (
\bm{D}_{k}=\mathbf{0}
),
homogeneous deformation (
\bm{E}_{k}\neq\bm{E}_{k}(\bm{x}_{k})
),
uniform contact conditions (
\bm{g}\neq\bm{g}(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{c}})
),
and fixed contact area (
J_{sk}=1
),
such that one can work with the Helmholtz free contact energy
\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}=n_{\mathrm{c}}\,\psi_{\mathrm{c}}
.
13
13

13

J_{\mathrm{c}}=1
leads to
\mathsf{U_{c}}=U_{\mathrm{c}}
,
\mathsf{S_{c}}=S_{\mathrm{c}}
,
\mathsf{\Psi_{c}}=\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}
, etc.

Under these conditions the temperature change within each body is governed by the energy balance

\begin{array}[]{l}\rho_{k0}\,H_{k}\,\dot{u}_{k}=q_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(154)

that follows directly from integrating (
59
) over the reference volume, writing
\mathrm{d}V_{k}=H_{k}\,\mathrm{d}A_{k}
on the left hand side and applying the divergence theorem on the right. Considering the bulk energy

\begin{array}[]{l}\psi_{k}=\psi_{\mathrm{mech}}-\displaystyle\frac{c_{k}}{2T_{0}}\big(T_{k}-T_{0}\big)^{2}\,,\end{array}

(155)

where
c_{k}
is the heat capacity per unit mass, leads to

\begin{array}[]{l}u_{k}=u_{\mathrm{mech}}+\displaystyle\frac{c_{k}}{2T_{0}}\big(T_{k}^{2}-T_{0}^{2}\big)\,,\end{array}

(156)

due to (
75
) and (
90
).
For quasi-static deformations then follows
\dot{u}_{k}=c_{k}\,T_{k}\,\dot{T}_{k}/T_{0}
.
Inserting this into (
154
) then gives

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{C_{k}}{T_{0}}\,T_{\mathrm{c}}\,\dot{T}_{\mathrm{c}}=q_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{array}

(157)

where
C_{k}:=H_{k}\,\rho_{k0}\,c_{k}
is the heat capacity per unit contact area satisfying
C_{1}=C_{2}
.

6.3
Debonding thermodynamics

The last test case illustrates thermo-chemo-mechanical coupling by calculating the temperature change due to mechanical debonding.
The test case is illustrated in Fig.
11
a: Two blocks initially bonded and at temperature
T_{0}
are pulled apart leading to debonding and rising temperature.
Now the contact energy density

\begin{array}[]{l}\Psi_{\mathrm{c}}=-\displaystyle\frac{C_{\mathrm{c}}}{2T_{0}}(T_{\mathrm{c}}-T_{0})^{2}+\frac{K_{0}}{2}(\phi-1)^{2}\end{array}

(174)

is used.
When the bond breaks, elastic strain energy is converted into surface energy and kinetic energy.
The former corresponds to the stored bond energy (and is given by the second term in (
174
)).
If viscosity is present, the latter then transforms into thermal energy.
Waiting long enough, the kinetic energy
\mathbb{K}
completely dissipates into heat.
The temperature rise can then be calculated from the energy balance.
Setting the energy (per contact area) before and after debonding equal, gives

\begin{array}[]{l}U_{\mathrm{mech}}+\displaystyle\frac{C_{\mathrm{tot}}}{2T_{0}}\,T_{0}^{2}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{tot}}}{2T_{0}}\,T_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}+\frac{K_{0}}{2}\,,\end{array}

(175)

where
U_{\mathrm{mech}}
is the mechanical energy at debonding and
C_{\mathrm{tot}}:=C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{\mathrm{c}}
is the total heat capacity of the system.
Considering linear elasticity gives
U_{\mathrm{mech}}=\frac{1}{2}(t_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{max}})^{2}/k_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
where
k_{\mathrm{eff}}=(H_{1}/E_{1}+H_{2}/E_{2})^{-1}
is the effective stiffness of the two-body system based on height
H_{k}
and Young’s modulus
E_{k}
. This then leads to the temperature change

\begin{array}[]{l}T_{\mathrm{c}}=T_{0}\,\sqrt{1-\kappa+(t_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{max}}/t_{0})^{2}}\,,\end{array}

(176)

with the positive constants
\kappa:=K_{0}/(T_{0}\,C_{\mathrm{tot}})
and
t_{0}^{2}:=T_{0}\,C_{\mathrm{tot}}\,k_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
It is shown in Fig.
11
b for various values of
\kappa
and
t_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{max}}/t_{0}
.

Figure 11:
Debonding thermodynamics: a. model problem; b. temperature change during debonding.

Increasing the bond energy (represented by
\kappa
) leads to lower final temperatures, while higher bond strengths
t_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{max}}
lead to higher final temperatures.
As a consequence, the final temperature can either be higher or lower than the initial temperature.
But note that
t_{\mathrm{n}}^{\mathrm{max}}/t_{0}>\kappa-1
to ensure
T_{\mathrm{c}}>0
.
This implies that for large
\kappa>1
higher stresses are needed to break the bond.

7
Conclusion

This work has presented a unified continuum theory for coupled nonlinear thermo-chemo-mechanical contact as it follows from the fundamental balance laws and principles of irreversible thermodynamics.
It highlights the analogies between the different physical field equations, and it discusses the coupling present in the balance laws and constitutive relations.
Of particular importance is (
108
), the equation for the mean contact heat influx.
It identifies how mechanical dissipation, chemical dissipation and interfacial entropy changes lead to interfacial heat generation.
This is illustrated by analytically solved contact test cases for steady sliding, exothermic bonding, endothermic bonding and forced bond-breaking.

The proposed theory applies to all contact problems characterized by single-field or coupled thermal, chemical and mechanical contact.
There are several applications of particular interest to the present authors that are planned to be studied in future work.
One is the pressure-dependent curing thermodynamics of adhesives
(
Sain et al., 2018
)
.
A second is the study of the bonding thermodynamics of insect and lizard adhesion based on the viscolelastic multiscale adhesion model of
Sauer, 2010
. A third is the local modeling and study of bond strength and failure of osseointegrated implants
(
Immel et al., 2020
)
.
There are also interesting applications that require an extension of the present theory.
An example is electro-chemo-mechanical contact interactions in batteries.
Therefore the extension to electrical contact is required, e.g. following the framework of
Weißenfels and Wriggers, 2010
.
Another example is contact and adhesion of droplets and cells.
Therefore the extension to surface stresses, surface mobility of bonding sites, contact angles and appropriate bond reactions is needed, e.g. following the framework of
Sauer, 2016a
and
Sahu et al., 2017
.
