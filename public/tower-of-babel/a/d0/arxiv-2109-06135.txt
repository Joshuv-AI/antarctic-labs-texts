Counterexample to the Laptev–Safronov conjecture
Sabine Bögli, Jean-Claude Cuenin

Abstract
. We prove that the Laptev–Safronov conjecture (Comm. Math. Phys. 2009) is false in the range that is not covered by Frank’s positive result (Bull. Lond. Math. Soc. 2011). The simple counterexample is adaptable to a large class of Schrödinger type operators, for which we also prove new sharp upper bounds.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
35P15, 31Q12.

1.
Introduction

Consider a Schrödinger operator
H_{V}=-\Delta+V
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with a complex-valued potential
V
. The Laptev–Safronov conjecture
[
27
]
stipulates that in
d\geq 2
dimensions any
non-positive
eigenvalue
z
of
H_{V}
satisfies the bound

\displaystyle|z|^{\gamma}\leq D_{\gamma,d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V(x)|^{\gamma+\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}x

(1)

for
0<\gamma\leq d/2
, and with
D_{\gamma,d}
independent of
V
and
z
. It is known that the condition
\gamma\leq d/2
is necessary, see
[
4
]
. The inequality (
1
) is known to be true if
d=1
and
\gamma=1/2
or if
d\geq 2
and
\gamma\leq 1/2
. The one-dimensional bound (with the sharp constant
D_{1/2,1}=1/2
) is due to Abramov-Aslanyan-Davies
[
1
]
, and the higher dimensional bound is due to Frank
[
12
]
. Originally, these bounds were stated for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{+}
, but it was later realized by Frank and Simon
[
14
]
that embedded eigenvalues
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
can also be accommodated. In fact, Frank and Simon “almost disproved” the Laptev–Safronov conjecture by constructing a counterexample (based on an earlier example of Ionescu and Jerison
[
23
]
) that prohibits (
1
) for
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
whenever
d\geq 2
and
\gamma>1/2
. Here we prove that the Laptev–Safronov conjecture is
false
in the form originally stated. In the following we write
q=\gamma+d/2
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, let
\chi_{\varepsilon}
be the indicator function of

\{x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}:\,|x_{1}|<\varepsilon^{-1},|x^{\prime}|<\varepsilon^{-1/2}\}.

We construct potentials
V_{\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon>0
, with
|V_{\varepsilon}|\leq\varepsilon\chi_{\varepsilon}
and such that
z_{\varepsilon}=1+i\varepsilon
is an eigenvalue of
H_{V_{\varepsilon}}
. This allows us to disprove the conjecture.

Theorem 1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
q>(d+1)/2
. Then

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\,\frac{|z_{\varepsilon}|^{q-\frac{d}{2}}}{\|V_{\varepsilon}\|_{q}^{q}}=+\infty.

Actually, our counterexample shows more, namely that the following substitute of (
1
) for “long-range” potentials (i.e.
q>(d+1)/2
), due to Frank
[
13
]
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(z,\mathbb{R}_{+})^{q-\frac{d+1}{2}}|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C_{q,d}\|V\|_{q}^{q},

(2)

is
sharp
(in the sense that the exponent of
\operatorname{dist}(z,\mathbb{R}_{+})
cannot be made smaller while preserving scale-invariance).

Theorem 2
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
q\geq(d+1)/2
. Then

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\,\frac{\operatorname{dist}(z_{\varepsilon},\mathbb{R}_{+})^{q-\frac{d+1}{2}}|z_{\varepsilon}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\|V_{\varepsilon}\|_{q}^{q}}>0.

In addition, the same example saturates the recent bound of the second author
[
8
, Th. 1.1]
, which states that

\displaystyle|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C_{d}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V(x)|^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\exp(-E|x-y|)\mathrm{d}x,

(3)

with
E=\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{z}>0
, and generalizes the one-dimensional analog of Davies and Nath
[
9
]
to higher dimensions. Note that estimating the right hand side of (
3
) from above by the same expression with
E=0
results in the endpoint estimate (
1
) with
\gamma=1/2
(or equivalently
q=(d+1)/2
). Hence the endpoint case in Theorem 2 already implies that (
3
) is optimal in some sense.
The exponential factor in (
3
) effectively localizes the integration to a ball
B(y,C/E)
.
Moreover, the right hand side of (
3
) may be much smaller than that of (
2
).
There are estimates similar to (
3
) for any
q\in(d/2,(d+1)/2]
, see
[
8
]
. We do not state these here but remark that our counterexample also shows that an analog of
[
8
, Th. 1]
cannot hold for
q>(d+1)/2
. For brevity, we denote the right hand side of (
3
) by
F_{V}(E)
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
d\geq 2
. There exists
C_{d}^{\prime}>0
such that for all
L\geq 1

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\,\frac{|z_{\varepsilon}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{F_{V}(L\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{z_{\varepsilon}})}\geq C_{d}^{\prime}\,L.

These three theorems show that the bounds (
1
),(
2
),(
3
) provide a rather complete picture of sharp eigenvalue inequalities for Schrödinger operators with complex potentials. Some refinements for singular potentials are known, see e.g.
[
12
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
29
]
,
[
5
]
, but we focus here on the long-range aspects of the potential.
This is reflected by the fact that the construction of our counterexample is local in Fourier space, similar to the examples for embedded eigenvalues in
[
7
]
, where a connection between the aforementioned Ionescu–Jerison example and the “Knapp example” in Fourier restriction theory (see e.g.
[
36
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
30
]
,
[
11
]
for textbook presentations) was made. The examples in
[
7
]
are based on superpositions of infinitely many Knapp wavepackets, while our example here is based on a single such wavepacket.

As in
[
7
]
, the locality in Fourier space affords the flexibility to adapt the counterexample to a large class of Schrödinger type operators of the form

\displaystyle H_{V}=h_{0}(D)+V(x),

(4)

where
h_{0}
is a tempered distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, smooth in a neighborhood of some point
\xi^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and such that
\lambda:=h_{0}(\xi^{0})
is a regular value of
h_{0}
. This means that the isoenergy surface

\displaystyle S_{\lambda}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:h_{0}(\xi)=\lambda\}

(5)

is a smooth nonempty hyersurface near
\xi^{0}
.
Here
h_{0}(D)f=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(h_{0}\hat{f})
is the Fourier multiplier corresponding to
h_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
is the inverse Fourier transform. It is well known that upper bounds for the resolvent
(H_{0}-z)^{-1}
, for
z
close to
\lambda
, crucially depend on curvature properties of
S_{\lambda}
, see e.g.
[
17
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
38
]
. For the Laplacian
H_{0}=-\Delta
, i.e.
h_{0}(\xi)=\xi^{2}
, the surface
S_{\lambda}=\sqrt{\lambda}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
has everywhere nonvanishing Gauss curvature if
\lambda>0
. This fact lies at the heart of the Stein–Tomas theorem as well as the uniform resolvent estimates of Kenig–Ruiz–Sogge
[
24
]
that are behind the upper bound (
1
) for
\gamma\leq 1/2
. We will prove generalizations of (
2
), (
3
) in Section
5
for operators of the form (
4
)
(we actually allow
V
to be a pseudodifferential operator). Our counterexamples show that these upper bounds are sharp. To simplify the exposition we state the result for the fractional Laplacian
H_{0}=(-\Delta)^{s}
. We remark that part (i) of the following theorem was already proved in
[
6
, Th. 6.1]
(see also
[
22
]
for related resolvent estimates).

Theorem 4
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
s>0
and
q\geq q_{s}
, where

\displaystyle q_{s}:=\begin{cases}d/s\quad&\mbox{if }s<d,\\
1+\quad&\mbox{if }s=d,\\
1\quad&\mbox{if }s>d.\end{cases}

(6)

Let
H_{V}=(-\Delta)^{s/2}+V
. Then then any eigenvalue
z\in\mathbb{C}
of
H_{V}
satisfies the following.

(i)

If
q\leq(d+1)/2
, then

\displaystyle|z|^{q-\frac{d}{s}}\leq D_{d,s,q}\|V\|_{q}^{q}.

(7)

(ii)

If
q>(d+1)/2
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(z,\mathbb{R}_{+})^{q-\frac{d+1}{2}}|z|^{\frac{d+1}{2}-\frac{d}{s}}\leq D_{d,s,q}\|V\|_{q}^{q}.

(8)

(iii)

For any natural number
N
,

\displaystyle|z|^{\frac{d+1}{2}-\frac{d}{s}}\leq C_{d,s,N}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\operatorname{Im}z(x-y)|)^{-N}|V(x)|^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\mathrm{d}x.

The estimate in (iii) corresponds to (
3
). Using explicit formulas for the resolvent kernel of the fractional Laplacian in terms of special functions one could probably replace the rapid decay by an exponential one. However, our proof only uses stationary phase estimates and works for more general constant coefficient operators
H_{0}
. In practice, the difference between (
3
) and (iii) is not significant; only the decay scale
|\operatorname{Im}z|^{-1}
is. Observe that if
s<2d/(d+1)
(this condition appears in
[
6
,
22
]
), then one is always in the long-range case (ii) since
(d+1)/2<q_{s}
. The proof of (ii), (iii) could be obtained by closely following the arguments in
[
13
]
and
[
8
]
, respectively.
However, our main point here is to show that all the statements of Theorem 4 follow from the general results of Propositions
20
and
24
below in the special case
h_{0}(\xi)=|\xi|^{s}
.

As a further consequence of our counterexample to the Laptev–Safronov conjecture, one can modify the construction of
[
4
, Th. 1]
, valid for
q>d
, to
q>(d+1)/2
.
Here,
\sigma_{\rm p}(H_{V})
denotes the set of eigenvalues.

Theorem 5
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
q>(d+1)/2
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Then there exists
V\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\max\{\|V\|_{\infty},\|V\|_{q}\}\leq\varepsilon
such that
\sigma_{p}(H_{V})\backslash\mathbb{R}_{+}
accumulates at every point in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

To conclude the introduction we give some comments on the idea behind the counterexample to the Laptev–Safronov conjecture.
A key difference to the constructions in
[
14
,
23
,
7
]
(for embedded eigenvalues) is that the potential here is not explicit, but depends on an (unknown) eigenfunction of a compact operator
K
(see the proof of Lemma
9
for details).
In
[
14
,
23
,
7
]
one starts with a putative eigenfunction of
H_{V}
and determines
V
from the eigenvalue equation. The strategy we adopt here more closely follows the standard approach to prove upper bounds, the so-called Birman–Schwinger principle
[
3
]
,
[
34
]
.
In its simplest form, this principle says that
z
is an eigenvalue of
H_{V}
if and only if
-1
is an eigenvalue of the compact operator
\sqrt{|V|}(H_{0}-z)^{-1}\sqrt{V}
. A simplified sketch of the proof of (
1
) for
d\geq 2
and
\gamma\leq 1/2
then goes as follows,

\displaystyle 1\leq\|\sqrt{|V|}(H_{0}-z)^{-1}\sqrt{V}\|\leq\|V\|_{q}\|(H_{0}-z)^{-1}\|_{p\to p^{\prime}}\leq(D_{\gamma,d}|z|^{-\gamma})^{1/q}\|V\|_{q},

(9)

where
p^{-1}+(p^{\prime})^{-1}=q^{-1}
and we recall that
q=\gamma+d/2
. The second inequality above is simply Hölder’s inequality, while the last inequality follows from the work of Kenig–Ruiz–Sogge
[
24
]
(and is due to Frank
[
12
]
in its rescaled version). This inequality is closely related to the Stein–Tomas theorem for the Fourier restriction operator
F_{S}f:=\hat{f}\upharpoonright S
, where
S=\sqrt{\lambda}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
if
z=\lambda+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon
. The Knapp example shows that
p=2(d+1)/(d+3)
(corresponding to
q=(d+1)/2
) is the best (largest) possible exponent in the inequality
\|\hat{f}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\leq C_{p}\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. The same is true for the
p\to p^{\prime}
resolvent estimate since

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(H_{0}-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon))^{-1}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{(H_{0}-\lambda)^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}

(10)

and the right hand side converges to a constant times
F_{S}^{*}F_{S}
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Similarly to the previous argument, the proof of (
2
) for
q>(d+1)/2
(
\gamma>1/2
) in
[
13
]
uses the non-uniform bound

\displaystyle\|(H_{0}-z)^{-1}\|_{p\to p^{\prime}}\lesssim\operatorname{dist}(z,\mathbb{R}_{+})^{\frac{d+1}{2q}-1}\quad(|z|=1),

which is also sharp
[
26
, Prop. 1.3]
. In our construction the potential
V
is adapted to the Knapp example, making the second (Hölder) and third inequality in (
9
) optimal simultanously. The only possible loss of optimality thus comes from the first inequality, and this may happen if the spectral radius of
\sqrt{|V|}(H_{0}-z)^{-1}\sqrt{V}
is much smaller than its norm. We avoid this problem by working with (
10
) instead of the full resolvent. It turns out that one can redefine
V
(without making it larger in
L^{q}
norm) in such a way that
z
becomes an eigenvalue of
H_{V}
.

Organization of the paper.

In Section 2 we construct the counterexample to the Laptev–Safronov conjecture and prove Theorems 1–3 and 5. In Section 3 we give an alternative (non compactly supported) counterexample that is a perturbation of the Frank–Simon example for embedded eigenvalues. In Section 4 we prove an almost sharp quantitative lower bound on the norm of the compact operator
K
(which implies an upper bound on the potential) and generalize the counterexample to generalized Schrödinger operators of the form (
4
). Corresponding upper bounds for such operators (in particular, a proof of Theorem 4) are collected in Section 5.

Notation.
For
a,b\geq 0

the statement
a\lesssim b
means that
a\leq Cb
for some universal
constant
C
. The expression
a\asymp b
means
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
.
If the estimate depends on a parameter
\tau
, we indicate
this by writing
a\lesssim_{\tau}b
. In particular, if
\tau=N
, we always mean that the estimate is true for any natural number
N
, with a constant depending on
N
. The expression
a\lesssim b^{\kappa+}
(
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
) means
a\lesssim_{\delta}b^{\kappa+\delta}
for any
\delta>0
, and similarly for
\kappa-
.
The dependence on the dimension and on other fixed quantities is always suppressed. An assumption
a\ll b
means that there is a small constant
c
such that if
a\leq cb
, then the ensuing conclusion holds. We also use
c
as a generic positive constant in estimates involving exponentials, as in (
3
). The big oh notation
a=\mathcal{O}(b)
means
|a|\lesssim b
(here we are not assuming
a\geq 0
). For an integral operator
K
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote by
\|K\|_{r\to s}
its
L^{r}\to L^{s}
operator norm. If
r,s=2
, then we just write
\|K\|
. Similarly, we denote the
L^{r}
norm of a function
f
by
\|f\|_{r}
and write
\|f\|
if
r=2
.
We denote by
\sigma(T)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,T-z\text{ not boundedly invertible}\}
the spectrum of a linear operator
T
. We write
\langle x\rangle=(1+x^{2})^{1/2}
, where
x^{2}=x\cdot x
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. If nothing else is indicated, integrals are always understood to be over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The indicator function of a set
A
is denoted by
\mathbf{1}_{A}
.
If we speak of a bump function we mean a smooth, compactly supported, real-valued function with values in
[0,1]
.

2.
Counterexample to the conjecture

The counterexample to the Laptev–Safronov conjecture is based on Lemmas
9
and
11
below.
In the following we use the notation

\delta_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(H_{0}):=\frac{\varepsilon}{(H_{0}-\lambda)^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}},

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varepsilon>0
. We abbreviate this by
\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})
if
\lambda=1
.

Let
H_{0}=h_{0}(x,D)
be a self-adjoint, elliptic pseudodifferential operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with domain

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}{(H_{0})}:=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):H_{0}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}.

Here
h(x,D)
is the Kohn–Nirenberg quantization of a symbol
h\in S_{\varrho,\delta}^{m}
(the standard Hörmander classes, see e.g.
[
39
]
) where
0\leq\delta<\varrho\leq 1
and
m>0
. Hence,

\displaystyle h_{0}(x,D)u(x)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}h(x,\xi)\hat{u}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi,

where
\hat{u}
is the Fourier transform of a Schwartz function
u
. We will write
H_{0}\in OPS_{\varrho,\delta}^{m}
.
We assume that
H_{0}
is elliptic, i.e.
|h_{0}(x,\xi)|\gtrsim|\xi|^{m}
for
|\xi|\geq C
. By
[
39
, Prop. 5.5]
, we have
\mathcal{D}{(H_{0})}=H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We also assume that
H_{0}
is
real
, i.e. commutes with complex conjugation. On the symbol level, this means that
\overline{h_{0}(x,\xi)}=h_{0}(x,-\xi)
. Note that this is the case for the Laplacian, for which
h_{0}(\xi)=\xi^{2}
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a nonempty open, precompact set,
and let
\chi=\mathbf{1}_{U}
be its indicator function. In the following we will consider the operator
K:=\chi\delta_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(H_{0})\chi
.

Lemma 6
.

K
is compact.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda=(1-\Delta)^{1/2}
and write

\displaystyle K=\chi\Lambda^{-m}(\Lambda^{m}\delta_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(H_{0})\Lambda^{m})\Lambda^{-m}\chi.

By the Kato–Seiler–Simon inequality
[
35
, Theorem 4.1]
,
\chi\Lambda^{-m}
is in the Schatten class
\mathfrak{S}^{p}
for any
p>d/m
(and
p\geq 2
). In particular, it is compact, and so is its adjoint
\Lambda^{-m}\chi
. It remains to show that
\Lambda^{m}\delta_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(H_{0})\Lambda^{m}
is
L^{2}
bounded. By Beal’s theorem
[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
it follows that
\delta_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(H_{0})\in OPS_{\varrho,\delta}^{-2m}
, and by the
L^{2}
boundedness of zero order pseudodifferential operators (see e.g.
[
39
, Theorem 5.3]
for the symbol classes considered here),
\Lambda^{m}\delta_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(H_{0})\Lambda^{m}
is bounded.
∎

We next state an analog of the Birman–Schwinger principle for the operator
K
. The proof is a straightforward verification. Here we do not need to assume
\chi=\mathbf{1}_{U}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\mu
is an eigenvalue of
K
if and only if the operator
(H_{0}-\lambda)^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}-(\varepsilon/\mu)\chi^{2}
has nontrivial kernel. Moreover,

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(H_{0})\chi:\ker(K-\mu)\to\ker((H_{0}-\lambda)^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}-(\varepsilon/\mu)\chi^{2})

is a linear isomorphism with inverse
\mu^{-1}\chi
.

The following lemma is a standard elliptic regularity result, but we provide a proof for completeness.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\ker(K-\mu)\subset C^{\infty}(U)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
7
it suffices to prove that if
u\in\mathcal{D}{(H_{0}^{2})}
,

\displaystyle(H_{0}-\lambda)^{2}u+\varepsilon^{2}u-(\varepsilon/\mu)\chi^{2}u=0,

then
u\in C^{\infty}(U)
. It is clear that the above equation takes the form
Pu=f
with
P\in OPS^{2m}_{\varrho,\delta}
elliptic and
u,f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
Q\in OPS^{2m}_{\varrho,\delta}
be a parametrix for
P
(see e.g.
[
39
, Ch. 1, Sect. 4]
). Then, modulo smooth functions, we have
QPu=u
and hence
u=Qf\in H^{2m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
[
39
, Prop. 5.5]
. To bootstrap this, we localize near a point
x_{0}\in U
and let
\chi_{j}
be a sequence of bump functions in
U
such that
\chi_{j}=1
near
x_{0}
and
\chi_{j}\chi_{j-1}=\chi_{j}
. Then, again modulo smooth functions,
u_{j}=\chi_{j}u
satisfies
Pu_{j}=f_{j}
with
f_{j}=[P,\chi_{j}]u=[P,\chi_{j}]u_{j-1}\in H^{j(\varrho-\delta)}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(again by
[
39
, Prop. 5.5]
and since the commutator reduces the order by
\varrho-\delta
, see
[
39
, (3.24)]
). Applying the previous elliptic regularity estimate successively yields
u_{j}\in H^{2m+j(\varrho-\delta)}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. By Sobolev embedding,
u_{j}\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
2m+j(\varrho-\delta)>d/2+k
. This shows that
u
is smooth at
x_{0}
.
∎

Lemma 9
.

There exists
V\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
z=\lambda+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon
is an eigenvalue of
H_{V}
and
|V|\leq\|K\|^{-1}\chi
.

Proof.

Since
K
is a nonnegative compact operator, its largest eigenvalue equals
\|K\|
. Hence, there is a nontrivial
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
K\phi=\|K\|\phi
. Since
K
is real we may and will assume that
\phi
is real-valued. Using this together with the identity

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(H_{0})=\operatorname{Im}(H_{0}-z)^{-1}=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}((H_{0}-z)^{-1}-(H_{0}-\overline{z})^{-1}),

the eigenvalue equation takes the form

\displaystyle(H_{0}-z)\psi=\|K\|^{-1}\chi\operatorname{Im}\psi,

where
\psi:=(H_{0}-z)^{-1}\phi
and where we used that
\chi\phi=\phi
since
\chi^{2}=\chi
. Here,
\operatorname{Im}\psi
denotes the imaginary part of a function, in distinction to the meaning of
\operatorname{Im}
above for the imaginary part of an operator. Let
\mathcal{N}:=\{x\in U:\psi(x)=0\}
be the nodal set of
\psi
, and set
V:=-\|K\|^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{U\setminus\mathcal{N}}\frac{\operatorname{Im}\psi}{\psi}
. Note that the nodal set is well defined since
\phi
is smooth in
U
, by Lemma
8
, and hence
\psi
is smooth in
U
, by the pseudolocal property
[
39
, page 6]
. Here we are again using Beal’s theorem to assert that
(H_{0}-z)^{-1}
is a pseudodifferential operator. Then

\displaystyle(H_{0}-z)\psi+V\psi=\|K\|^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{N}}\operatorname{Im}\psi=0

and
V
satisfies the claimed bound.
∎

Remark 10
.

In the case of the Laplacian,
H_{0}=-\Delta
, the set
U\setminus\mathcal{N}
has positive Lebesgue measure. This can be most easily seen a posteriori: Since
\phi
is nontrivial,

\psi
is nontrivial as well. If
U\setminus\mathcal{N}
had zero measure, then we would have
-\Delta\psi=z\psi
, but this has no
H^{2}
solution, as a consequence of Rellich’s theorem
[
32
]
.

The
L^{q}
-norm of
V
is estimated by

\displaystyle\|V\|_{q}\leq\|K\|^{-1}\|\chi\|_{q},

(11)

so it remains to estimate
\|K\|
from below and
\|\chi\|_{q}
from above.

To avoid technicalities at this stage we restrict attention to the Laplacian
H_{0}=-\Delta
, i.e.
h_{0}(\xi)=\xi^{2}
. Without loss of generality (scaling) we restrict ourselves to
\lambda=1
. For
\varepsilon>0
let
T_{\varepsilon}
be an
\varepsilon^{-1}\times\varepsilon^{-1/2}
tube centred at the origin, with long side pointing in the
x_{1}
direction, i.e.

\displaystyle T_{\varepsilon}=\{|x_{1}|<\varepsilon^{-1},\,|x^{\prime}|<\varepsilon^{-1/2}\}.

(12)

Let
\chi_{\varepsilon}
be the indicator function of the tube
T_{\varepsilon}
.
We then have the following (qualitative) lower bound for
K_{\varepsilon}=\chi_{\varepsilon}\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})\chi_{\varepsilon}
. In Lemma
17
below we will prove a quantitative (almost optimal) lower bound.

Lemma 11
.

Let
H_{0}=-\Delta
,
\lambda=1
and
\chi_{\varepsilon}
as above. For
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
the operator norm
\varepsilon\|K_{\varepsilon}\|
is bounded below by a positive constant, independent of
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We conjugate
K_{\varepsilon}
by
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x_{1}}
and rescale
(y_{1},y^{\prime})=(\varepsilon x_{1},\varepsilon^{1/2}x^{\prime})
. The resulting operator is isospectral to
\varepsilon K_{\varepsilon}
and given by

\displaystyle K^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=\chi_{1}\delta_{1}(H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})\chi_{1}

(13)

where
\chi_{1}
is the indicator function of
T_{1}
and
H_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=-2\mathrm{i}\partial_{y_{1}}-\Delta_{y^{\prime}}-\varepsilon\partial_{y_{1}}^{2}
. In the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
the operator
K^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
converges strongly to
K^{\prime}_{0}
. Since this is not the zero operator, there exists an
L^{2}
-normalized function
f
such that
\|K_{0}^{\prime}f\|>0
. Now the strong convergence implies that for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
we have
\|K_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}f\|\geq\|K_{0}^{\prime}f\|/2
. This implies that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\|K_{\varepsilon}\|=\|K_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\|\geq\|K_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}f\|\geq\|K_{0}^{\prime}f\|/2

and thus proves the claim.
∎

Remark 12
.

Laptev and Safronov based their conjecture on the famous Wigner–von Neumann example

]
(see also
[

]
), which is a potential decaying like

Remark 13

3.
Perturbation of embedded eigenvalues

In this section we provide an alternative counterexample to the Laptev–Safronov conjecture that is closer to that suggested by Frank and Simon
[
14
]
. We continue to make the same assumptions on
H_{0}
and
U
as in Lemma
9
.

Proposition 15
.

Let
\lambda\in\sigma(H_{0})
and
f\in\mathcal{D}(H_{0})
with
\|f\|=1
.
Assume that

\|(1-\chi)f\|\leq 1/4.

Then, for any
\varepsilon\geq 2\|(H_{0}-\lambda)f\|
, there exists
V\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
z=\lambda+i\varepsilon
is an eigenvalue of
H_{0}+V
and
|V|\leq 4\varepsilon\chi
.
In particular, if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue and
(H_{0}-\lambda)f=0
, then the conclusion holds for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
.

Proof.

We set
X=(H_{0}-\lambda)
and write
\varepsilon\delta_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(H_{0})=1-X^{2}/(X^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})
. By the Peter Paul inequality,
\varepsilon\|X/(X^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})\|\leq 1/2
. Since
\|Xf\|\leq\varepsilon/2
, this yields
\|X^{2}/(X^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})f\|\leq 1/4
, and hence (using
\|X^{2}/(X^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})\|\leq 1
and
\|\chi\|\leq 1
)

\displaystyle\|\chi X^{2}/(X^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})\chi f\|\leq\|X^{2}/(X^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})f\|+\|X^{2}/(X^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})\|\|(1-\chi)f\|\leq\frac{1}{2}.

We have also used
\|(1-\chi)f\|\leq 1/4
in the last inequality. Using this once more, we obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon\|\chi\delta_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(H_{0})\chi f\|\geq\|\chi f\|-\frac{1}{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}.

Thus
\varepsilon\|K\|\geq 1/4
and Lemma
9
implies the claim.
∎

Remark 16
.

Recall that the self-adjointness of
H_{0}
implies that for every
\lambda\in\sigma(H_{0})
there exists a normalized sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{D}(H_{0})
with
\|(H_{0}-\lambda)f_{n}\|\to 0
. Thus we can always find a normalized function for which
\|(H_{0}-\lambda)f\|
is as small as we want. However, we need to make sure that
U
is so large that the assumption
\|(1-\chi)f\|\leq 1/4
is satisfied.

3.1.
Alternative counterexample to Laptev–Safronov conjecture

Let
q>(d+1)/2
.
Consider the sequence of potentials
V_{n}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, in
[
14
, Theorem 2.1]
where
\lambda=1
is an embedded eigenvalue of
-\Delta+V_{n}
for each
n
. The potentials satisfy
|V_{n}(x)|\lesssim(n+|x_{1}|+|x^{\prime}|^{2})^{-1}
.
In particular,
\|V_{n}\|_{q}\to 0
and
\|V_{n}\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Now fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote by
f_{n}
a normalized eigenfunction corresponding to the embedded eigenvalue.
Let
U_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact subset that is so large that
\|(1-\mathbf{1}_{U_{n}})f_{n}\|\leq 1/4
.
Then, by Proposition
15
, for all
\varepsilon>0
there exist potentials
W_{n,\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
z_{\varepsilon}=1+i\varepsilon\in\sigma_{p}(-\Delta+V_{n}+W_{n,\varepsilon})
with

|W_{n,\varepsilon}|\leq 4\varepsilon\mathbf{1}_{U_{n}}
.
Let
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}
be such that
\varepsilon_{n}=o(|U_{n}|^{-1/q})
as
n\to\infty
.
Then

\|W_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}\|_{q}\leq 4\varepsilon_{n}|U_{n}|^{1/q}=o(1).

This disproves the Laptev-Safronov conjecture
since

z_{\varepsilon_{n}}\to 1
,
\|V_{n}+W_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}\|_{q}\to 0
in the limit
n\to\infty
.
Note that this construction is similar to the one in the previous section, as one can take
\varepsilon_{n}=1/n
and
U_{n}=T_{c_{0}/n}
for a small positive constant
c_{0}
. However, due to the additional
V_{n}
, the potentials here don’t have compact supports.

4.
Quantitative lower bounds

The aim of this section is to optimize the lower bound on
\varepsilon\|K_{\varepsilon}\|
in Lemma
11
. Since the proof relied on soft arguments it did not provide a quantitative lower bound. The trivial upper bound is
\varepsilon\|K_{\varepsilon}\|\leq 1
. By stretching the tube
T_{\varepsilon}
in (
12
), we are able to prove an almost sharp lower bound. More precisely, let
\chi_{\varepsilon}
be the indicator function of
T_{\varepsilon/M}
, where
M\gg 1
.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\chi_{\varepsilon}
be as above. Then

\displaystyle\varepsilon\|K_{\varepsilon}\|\geq 1-\mathcal{O}(M^{-2+}).

(15)

Proof.

To prove the lower bound in (
15
) we first write

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon}=\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})-(1-\chi_{\varepsilon})\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})-\chi_{\varepsilon}\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})(1-\chi_{\varepsilon}).

We will treat
\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})
as a main term and the other terms as errors. Since
\|\chi_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\|K_{\varepsilon}f\|\geq\|\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})f\|-\|(1-\chi_{\varepsilon})\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})f\|-\|\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})(1-\chi_{\varepsilon})f\|

for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We take

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}(\xi):=\eta_{0}((c_{0}\varepsilon)^{-1}(\xi_{1}-1),(c_{0}\varepsilon)^{-1/2}\xi^{\prime}),

where
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
,
\eta_{0}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B(0,2))
is a nonnegative bump function equal to
1
on
B(0,1)
, and
c_{0}
is a small positive constant, to be chosen later. We mention that
f
is known as a ’Knapp example’ in harmonic analysis, see e.g.
[
11
, Example 1.8]
. For
\xi
in the support of
\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}
, we have
|\xi^{2}-1|=\mathcal{O}(c_{0}\varepsilon)
, which means that
\varepsilon\delta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)\geq 1-\mathcal{O}(c_{0}^{2})
there. Here and in the following
\delta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)=\frac{\varepsilon}{(\xi^{2}-1)^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}
. By Plancherel, we conclude that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\|\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})f_{\varepsilon}\|\geq(1-\mathcal{O}(c_{0}^{2}))\|f_{\varepsilon}\|.

(16)

Note that
\varepsilon\delta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)\leq 1
and is smooth on the scale of
f_{\varepsilon}
; in other words, we can write

\displaystyle(\delta_{\varepsilon}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon})(\xi)=\varepsilon^{-1}\eta((c_{0}\varepsilon)^{-1}(\xi_{1}-1),(c_{0}\varepsilon)^{-1/2}\xi^{\prime})

for some bump function
\eta
, similar to
\eta_{0}
. More precisely, by
\eta
we really mean a family of such bump functions, with smooth norms bounded uniformly in
\varepsilon
. Thus, both
f_{\varepsilon}
and
\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})f_{\varepsilon}
are Schwartz functions decaying rapidly away from
T_{c_{0}\varepsilon}
; in particular,

\displaystyle\|(1-\chi_{\varepsilon})f_{\varepsilon}\|\lesssim_{N}(c_{0}M)^{-N}\|f_{\varepsilon}\|,\quad\|(1-\chi_{\varepsilon})\delta_{\varepsilon}(H_{0})f_{\varepsilon}\|\lesssim_{N}\varepsilon^{-1}(c_{0}M)^{-N}\|f_{\varepsilon}\|,

where we used that
\|f_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\asymp(c_{0}\varepsilon)^{\frac{d+1}{2}}
and
\chi_{\varepsilon}=1
on
T_{\varepsilon/M}
. Together with (
16
) and the fact that
\varepsilon\delta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)\leq 1
, this yields

\displaystyle\varepsilon\|K_{\varepsilon}f_{\varepsilon}\|\geq(1-\mathcal{O}(c_{0}^{2})-\mathcal{O}_{N}((c_{0}M)^{-N}))\|f_{\varepsilon}\|.

Choosing
c_{0}=M^{-1+}
and taking
N
sufficiently large yields the lower bound in (
15
).
∎

Remark 18
.

In view of
\varepsilon\|K_{\varepsilon}\|\leq 1
the bound (
15
) is optimal in the limit
M\to\infty
. If we choose e.g.
M=\log(1/\varepsilon)
, then we obtain from (
15
) that for all
q>(d+1)/2
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\|K_{\varepsilon}\|=1,\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|V_{\varepsilon}\|_{q}=0.

4.1.
Lower bounds for constant-coefficient operators

Here we supplement Lemma
11
with quantitative bounds. We consider more general constant-coefficient operators than the Laplacian, that is we allow
H_{0}=h_{0}(D)
. For instance, for the fractional Laplacian (Theorem 4) we have
h_{0}(\xi)=|\xi|^{s}
. For the lower bound we only assume that
h_{0}
is a tempered distribution, smooth in a neighborhood of some
\xi^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and such that
\lambda:=h_{0}(\xi^{0})
is a regular value of
h_{0}
. If the hypersurface
\{h_{0}(\xi)=\lambda\}
has everywhere nonvanishing Gauss curvature, then local versions of (
1
), (
2
) hold (see Section
5
). If the Gauss curvature vanishes at some point, then the upper bound will be worse, but the following example still provides a lower bound. One could improve the example if more is known about the local geometry of the isoenergy surface.

The following example is very close to that of Lemma
17
and is based on the factorization

\displaystyle h_{0}(\xi)-\lambda=e(\xi)(\xi_{1}-a(\xi^{\prime})),

which holds locally near
\xi^{0}
, with
e
nonvanishing there. We suppress the dependence of
e,a
on
\lambda
.
By a linear change of coordinates we assume, as we may, that
a(0)=0
and
\partial_{\xi^{\prime}}a(0)=0
. Then
a(\xi^{\prime})=\mathcal{O}(|\xi^{\prime}|^{2})
. We take a Knapp example
f
whose Fourier support is contained in the cap

\displaystyle\kappa_{\varepsilon}:=\{|\xi_{1}|<c_{0}\varepsilon,|\xi^{\prime}|<(c_{0}\varepsilon)^{1/2}\}.

Clearly,
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
is contained in an
\varepsilon
neighborhood of the isoenergy surface
\{\xi_{1}=a(\xi^{\prime})\}
, hence

\displaystyle\varepsilon\delta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{(h_{0}(\xi)-\lambda)^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\geq 1-\mathcal{O}(c_{0}^{2}).

Now the same argument as for the Laplacian shows that (
15
) holds, for exactly the same function
\chi_{\varepsilon}
.

5.
Upper bounds

In this section we prove a generalization of the bounds (
1
), (
2
) for Schrödinger type operators of the form

\displaystyle H_{V}=h_{0}(D)+V(x,D).

(17)

We first consider the classical Schrödinger operator
H_{V}=-\Delta+V(x)
to explain what types of estimates we will prove. By homogeneity, the estimates (
1
), (
2
) may by reduced to
|z|=1
and by elliptic regularity to a small neighborhood of

. Hence these bounds can collectively be expressed as

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(z,\sigma(H_{0}))^{(q-(d+1)/2)_{+}}\lesssim\|V\|_{q}^{q}

for

(or

if

\displaystyle 1\lesssim\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V(x)|^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\exp(-c|\operatorname{Im}z||x-y|)\mathrm{d}x

(19)

for some constant

(we used that
\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{z}\asymp|\operatorname{Im}z|
for

small). Here,
\sigma(H_{0})
denotes the spectrum of
H_{0}
. The bounds (
18
)–(
19
) are

in the sense that they are essentially independent of the specific form of
h_{0}
, in a sense that we will make precise now.

In the following, we always assume that the spectral parameter is
z=\lambda+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon
, with
\lambda\in\sigma(H_{0})
and
|\varepsilon|\leq 1
. Then
\operatorname{dist}(z,\sigma(H_{0}))=|\varepsilon|
. One could use the Phragmén-Lindelöf maximum principle to extend the results to the region
|\varepsilon|\geq 1
(see e.g.
[
6
, Appendix A]
,
[
19
]
,
[
33
]
), but we will not pursue this.

We assume that
h_{0}
is a tempered distribution that is smooth near the foliation (see (
5
) for the definition of
S_{\lambda}
)

\displaystyle S=\bigcup_{\lambda\in I}S_{\lambda}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:h_{0}(\xi)\in I\}.

(20)

Here
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is a fixed compact subset of the set

\displaystyle\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\nabla h_{0}(\xi)\neq 0\mbox{ for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ such that }h_{0}(\xi)=\lambda\}

of regular values of
h_{0}
. We assume that
S_{\lambda}
is compact and has everywhere non-vanishing curvature for each
\lambda\in I
. The following lemma was proved e.g. in
[
6
, Lemma 3.3]
. It is closely related to the Stein–Tomas theorem for the Fourier restriction operator.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\eta
be a bump function. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda\in I}}{{|\varepsilon|\leq 1}}}\|\eta(D)[h_{0}(D)-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)]^{-1}\|_{p\to p^{\prime}}\lesssim 1.

The setup (
17
)
generalizes (
4
)
in that we allow
V(x,D)
to be a pseudodifferential operator. We assume that its Kohn–Nirenberg symbol
V(x,\xi)
is smooth in the fibre variable
\xi
, but we don’t assume smoothness in
x
. More precisely, assume that

\displaystyle C_{q,\Omega,N}(V):=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq N}\sup_{\xi\in\Omega}\|\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}V(\cdot,\xi)\|_{q}<\infty

(21)

for some sufficiently large
N
(
N>d
would suffice) and some pre-compact subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
S\Subset\Omega
.
The condition (
21
) is the natural generalization of
V\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and reduces to the latter if
V
is a potential. The order of
V(x,\xi)
will not play a role here since we will always localize in Fourier space. In fact, we place ourselves in the following abstract setting:
Let
\eta,\overline{\eta}
be bump functions supported on
\Omega
such that
\eta^{2}+\overline{\eta}^{2}=1
in a neighborhood of
S
. Define

\displaystyle H_{V}^{\eta}

\displaystyle:=h_{0}(D)+\eta(D)V\eta(D):\mathop{\rm Ran}P\to\mathop{\rm Ran}P,

\displaystyle H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}

\displaystyle:=h_{0}(D)+\overline{\eta}(D)V\overline{\eta}(D):\mathop{\rm Ran}\overline{P}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\overline{P},

where
P=1_{\Omega}(D)
,
\overline{P}=1-P
.
In order to avoid imposing global conditions on
h_{0}
and
V
in
\xi
we make the following assumption:

\displaystyle I\subset\varrho(H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}),\quad c_{q,I}:=\sup_{\lambda\in I,\,|\varepsilon|\leq 1}\|[H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)]^{-1}\|_{p\to p^{\prime}}<\infty.

(22)

Here
p
is uniquely determined by
q^{-1}=p^{-1}-(p^{\prime})^{-1}
,
\varrho(\cdot)
denotes the resolvent set and
\|\cdot\|_{p\to p^{\prime}}
denotes the
L^{p}\to L^{p^{\prime}}
norm. In most applications, (

) can easily be proved by standard elliptic estimates (see Subsection
5.1
for an example). This usually requires that

for some
q_{0}\geq 1
depending on
h_{0}
. We will ignore this and simply use (
22
) as a black box assumption. Hence, we only assume that
q\geq 1
in the following. In the proofs of Theorems
20
–
24
below we will make use of the
smooth Feshbach-Schur map

[
18
]
. The latter is defined by
(H_{V}-z)\mapsto F_{\eta}(z)
, where

\displaystyle F_{\eta}(z):=H_{V}^{\eta}-z-\eta V\overline{\eta}[H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}-z]^{-1}\overline{\eta}V\eta:\mathop{\rm Ran}P\to\mathop{\rm Ran}P.

Here and in the following we abbreviate
\eta=\eta(D)
and
\overline{\eta}=\overline{\eta}(D)
.
Theorem 1 in
[
18
]
(with

there) asserts that
H_{V}-z
is invertible if and only if
F_{\eta}(z)
is invertible, and that

\displaystyle[H_{V}-z]^{-1}=Q[F_{\eta}(z)]^{-1}Q^{\#}+\overline{\eta}[H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}-z]^{-1}\overline{\eta},

where

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=\eta-\overline{\eta}[H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}-z]^{-1}\overline{\eta}V\eta:\mathop{\rm Ran}P\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle\quad Q^{\#}

\displaystyle:=\eta-\eta V\overline{\eta}[H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}-z]^{-1}\overline{\eta}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathop{\rm Ran}P.

Proposition 20
.

Assume
H_{V}
is as above and that (
21
), (
22
) hold. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(z,\sigma(H_{0}))^{(q-(d+1)/2)_{+}}\lesssim C_{q,\Omega,N}(V)^{q}

(23)

holds for every eigenvalue
z=\lambda+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon
of
H_{V}
,
\lambda\in I
,
|\varepsilon|\leq 1
, with implicit constant depending on
h_{0},d,q,I,|\Omega|
, but not on
z
,
V
.

Proof.

By compactness of

, it suffices to prove (
23
) at
\operatorname{Re}z=\lambda
for a single

.
We first consider the case

.
Then (
23
) is equivalent to the statement that if
C_{q,\Omega,N}(V)
is sufficiently small, then

is not an eigenvalue of
H_{V}
. We will show that
H_{V}-z
is invertible using the smooth Feshbach-Schur map.
We write

\displaystyle F_{\eta}(z)=h_{0}(D)-z+\eta\widetilde{V}_{z}\eta,\quad\widetilde{V}_{z}:=V-V\overline{\eta}[H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}-z]^{-1}\overline{\eta}V.

(24)

By Lemma
21
below and (

\displaystyle\|\eta\widetilde{V}_{z}\|_{p^{\prime}\to p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\eta V\|_{p^{\prime}\to p}+\|[H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}-z]^{-1}\|_{p\to p^{\prime}}\|\eta V\|_{p^{\prime}\to p}\|\overline{\eta}V\|_{p^{\prime}\to p}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{|\Omega|}C_{q,\Omega,N}(V)+c_{q,I}C_{q,\Omega,N}(V)^{2}=:D_{I,q,\Omega,N},

where the

boundedness of
\eta,\overline{\eta}
is a consequence of the Mikhlin multiplier theorem
[
16
, Th. 5.2.7]
. By Lemma

we conclude that

\displaystyle\|\eta\widetilde{V}_{z}\eta[h_{0}(D)-z]^{-1}\|_{p\to p}\leq\|\eta\widetilde{V}_{z}\|_{p^{\prime}\to p}\|\eta[h_{0}(D)-z]^{-1}\|_{p\to p^{\prime}}\lesssim D_{I,q,\Omega,N},

and hence, by a geometric series argument, that
F_{\eta}(z)
is boundedly invertible in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if
C_{q,\Omega,N}(V)\ll 1
. Since the spectrum is independent of
p
(see e.g.
[
10
, Th. 14.3.10]
1
1

1

The statement there is given in one dimension. However, the proof only uses general facts about
L^{p}
spaces, valid in any dimension.
) it follows that
0
is not in the
L^{2}
spectrum of

, or equivalently, that
z
is not in the spectrum of
H_{V}
.

To prove the claim for
q>(d+1)/2
we follow
[

]
and interpolate the bound of Lemma

(for

) with the trivial estimate

\displaystyle\|[h_{0}(D)-z]^{-1}\|_{2\to 2}\leq\operatorname{dist}(z,\sigma(H_{0}))^{-1},

which yields

\displaystyle\|\eta[h_{0}(D)-z]^{-1}\|_{p\to p^{\prime}}\lesssim|\varepsilon|^{\frac{d+1}{2q}-1}.

Repeating the previous argument, we find that

is too small; in other words, if

. If we set

, then this means that we must have

|\varepsilon|^{1-\frac{d+1}{2q}}\lesssim a+c_{q,I}a^{2}

Since we are assuming

In the above proof we used the following generalization of Hölder’s inequality.

Lemma 21
.

Assume that
V
satisfies (
21
) and
\eta
is a bump function supported on
\Omega
. Then, for
N
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\|V(x,D)\eta(D)\|_{p^{\prime}\to p}+\|\eta(D)V(x,D)\|_{p^{\prime}\to p}\lesssim|\Omega|C_{q,\Omega,N}(V)

whenever
q^{-1}=p^{-1}-(p^{\prime})^{-1}
.

Proof.

By duality it suffices to estimate the first summand on the left. The kernel of
V(x,D)\eta(D)
(recall that we use the Kohn–Nirenberg quantization) is given by

\displaystyle k(x,x-y)=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(x-y)\cdot\xi}V(x,\xi)\eta(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi.

Due to the cutoff
\eta
the integral is restricted to
\xi\in\Omega
. Integration by parts shows that

\displaystyle|k(x,u)|\lesssim_{N}\langle u\rangle^{-N}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq N}|\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}V(x,\xi)|\mathrm{d}\xi,

and (
21
), together with Minkowski’s inequality, then provides the estimate

\displaystyle\|k(x,u)\|_{L^{q}_{x}}\lesssim_{N}\langle u\rangle^{-N}|\Omega|C_{q,\Omega,N}(V).

(25)

Changing variables from
y
to
u=x-y
and using Minkowski’s and Hölder’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\|\int k(x,x-y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y\|_{L^{p}_{x}}\leq\int\|k(x,u)f(x-u)\|_{L^{p}_{x}}\mathrm{d}u\leq\|f\|_{p^{\prime}}\int\|k(x,u)\|_{L^{q}_{x}}\mathrm{d}u

and (
25
) yields the claimed inequality.

Remark 22
.

If
V
is a potential, then of course
\|V\eta(D)\|_{p^{\prime}\to p}\lesssim\|V\|_{q}
, i.e. the factor
|\Omega|
can be dispensed with. From this point of view, a more natural norm than (
21
) would e.g. be

\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\Omega}\|V(\cdot,\xi)\|_{q}+\sum_{1\leq|\alpha|\leq N}\int_{\Omega}\|\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}V(\cdot,\xi)\|_{q}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

However, we view
\Omega
as fixed, which justifies our use of the simpler norm (
21
).

We now establish a more precise version of Lemma
19
. In the following we denote
R^{\eta,\zeta}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}=\eta(D)[h_{0}(D)-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)]^{-\zeta}
and also write
R^{\eta,\zeta}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x-y)
for its kernel.

Lemma 23
.

Let
\eta
be a bump function and
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
,
0\leq\operatorname{Re}\zeta\leq(d+1)/2
,
\varepsilon\in[-1,1]
. Then we have the kernel bound

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in I}|R^{\eta,\zeta}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x)|\lesssim_{N}\mathrm{e}^{C|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|^{2}}\langle x\rangle^{-\frac{d-1}{2}+\operatorname{Re}\zeta}\langle\varepsilon x\rangle^{-N}

(26)

for some
C>0
.

Proof.

Again, it suffices to prove this for a fixed
\lambda
.
We absorb
\lambda
into the symbol, i.e. we consider
p(\xi)=h_{0}(\xi)-\lambda
. By a partition of unity and a linear change of coordinates we may assume that, locally near an arbitrary point of
\Omega
, either
p\neq 0
or
\partial p/\partial\xi_{1}>0
. In the case
p\neq 0
we get the stronger bound

\displaystyle|R^{\eta,\zeta}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x)|\lesssim_{N}\langle x-y\rangle^{-N}.

We turn to the case
\partial p/\partial\xi_{1}>0
and consider
\zeta=1
first (i.e. the resolvent).
By the implicit function theorem,
\{p(\xi)=0\}
is then the graph of a smooth function
\xi_{1}=a(\xi^{\prime})
, and we have the factorization

\displaystyle(p(\xi)-i\varepsilon)^{-1}=(\xi_{1}-a(\xi^{\prime})-i\varepsilon q(\xi))^{-1}q(\xi)

(27)

where
q(\xi)=(\xi_{1}-a(\xi^{\prime}))/p(\xi)>0
, see e.g.
[
20
, Section 14.2]
,
[
6
, Lemma 3.3]
and
[
37
, Section 3.1]
. There it is sufficient to work with the limiting distributions corresponding to
\varepsilon=0\pm
, which would yield (
26
) in this case. Here we need to keep
\varepsilon
fixed to get the desired decay for nonzero
\varepsilon
. In the following we assume that
\varepsilon>0
; the case
\varepsilon<0
is similar.
The factorization (
27
) does not work well for this since
q
depends on
\xi_{1}
. To remedy this problem, we follow the approach of Koch–Tataru
[
25
]
, albeit in the much simpler setting of constant coefficients. Lemma 3.8 in
[
25
]
provides the alternative factorization

\displaystyle e(\xi)(p(\xi)-i\varepsilon)=\xi_{1}+a(\xi^{\prime})+i\varepsilon b(\xi^{\prime}),

(28)

where
e
is elliptic (
e\neq 0
) and
a,b
are real-valued. This is a version of the Malgrange preparation theorem
[
21
, Th. 7.5.5]
or the classical Weierstrass preparation theorem
[
21
, Th. 7.5.1]
in the analytic case. We appeal to
[
25
]
because it makes the dependence on
\varepsilon
explicit. Note that the imaginary part
b
is now independent of
\xi_{1}
. The symbols
e,a,b
can be found by iteratively solving a system of algebraic equations and using Borel resummation of the resulting formal series (see
[
25
, Lemma 3.9 and 3.10]
). Moreover,
a,b
have asymptotic expansions in powers of
\varepsilon
, while
e
has an asymptotic expansions in powers of
\varepsilon
and
\xi_{1}
. We will only need the first term
b_{1}
in the expansion of
b
. Changing variables
\xi\to\xi_{1}+a(\xi^{\prime})
we are reduced to
p(\xi)=\xi_{1}+i\varepsilon q(\xi)
for some real-valued function
q
. By the proof of
[
25
, Lemma 3.9]
we have
b_{1}=1/(1+q_{1}^{2})
, where
q_{1}=\partial_{\xi_{1}}q|_{\xi_{1}=0}
. Therefore,
b\geq c
on the closure of
\Omega
for some constant
c>0
(we used compactness and the smallness of
\varepsilon
). Since we have constant coefficients, the simple parametrix (5.5) in
[
25
]
, with (operator-valued) kernel
K(x_{1}-y_{1})
, given by

\displaystyle K(x_{1})=\mathbf{1}_{x_{1}<0}\,\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon x_{1}b(D^{\prime})}e^{-ix_{1}a(D^{\prime})},

(29)

is exact, i.e.
(D_{1}+a(D^{\prime})+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon b(D^{\prime}))K
is the identity (we denote both the operator and the kernel by
K
here). By the stationary phase estimate (for complex-valued phase functions)
[
21
, Th. 7.7.5]
,

\displaystyle|K(x)|\lesssim\langle x\rangle^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-c|\varepsilon x|}+\mathcal{O}_{N}(\langle x\rangle^{-N}),

Using the factorization (
28
) and extending
1/e
globally as a Schwartz function, we obtain (
26
) in the case
\zeta=1
. The case
\zeta\neq 1
requires only minor modifications. The kernel in (
29
)
is replaced by

\displaystyle K_{\zeta}(x_{1})=\chi_{-}^{\zeta-1}(x_{1})\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon x_{1}b(D^{\prime})}e^{-ix_{1}a(D^{\prime})},

where
\chi_{-}^{w}(\tau):=1_{\tau<0}|\tau|^{w}/\Gamma(w+1)
,

, where
\Gamma
is the usual Gamma function.
Then
(D_{1}+a(D^{\prime})+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon b(D^{\prime}))^{-\zeta}K_{\zeta}
is the identity. This follows immediately by applying the inverse Fourier transformation to the following identity (see
[
21
]
, specifically the explanation after Example 7.1.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(\tau\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\tau}\chi_{+}^{\zeta}(\tau\right)(\xi)=\mathrm{e}^{-i\pi(\zeta+1)/2}(\xi-\mathrm{i}\delta)^{-\zeta-1},\quad\delta>0,\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{C}.

Again, by stationary phase,

\displaystyle|K_{\zeta}(x)|\lesssim\mathrm{e}^{C|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|^{2}}(\langle x\rangle^{-\frac{d-1}{2}+\operatorname{Re}\zeta}\mathrm{e}^{-c|\varepsilon x|}+\mathcal{O}_{N}(\langle x\rangle^{-N}))

for

. The growth estimate in

comes from a standard estimate on the Gamma function (see e.g.
[
16
, Appendix A.7]
).

Proposition 24

Assume that (

\displaystyle 1\lesssim C_{\varepsilon,q,\Omega,N}(V)

Proof.

We again use the smooth Feshbach–Schur map. Thus the claim (

) is equivalent to the statement

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon,q,\Omega,N}(V)\ll_{\lambda}1\implies F_{\eta}(z)\mbox{ boundedly invertible},

where

is given by (

Again, by

\displaystyle\|\eta\widetilde{V}_{z}\eta[h_{0}(D)-z]^{-1}\|_{p\to p}<1,

\displaystyle\|V\eta[h_{0}(D)-z]^{-1}\|_{p\to p}\lesssim C_{\varepsilon,q,\Omega,N}(V)

\displaystyle\|\eta\widetilde{V}_{z}\eta[h_{0}(D)-z]^{-1}\|_{p\to p}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\|\eta V\overline{\eta}[H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}-z]^{-1}\overline{\eta}\|_{p\to p})C_{\varepsilon,q,\Omega,N}(V)

(where

is a bump function like

\displaystyle K(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(x,x-u)R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{\eta}(u-y)\mathrm{d}u.

\displaystyle\|K\|_{1\to 1}\leq\sup_{y}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|K(x,y)|\mathrm{d}x\lesssim_{N}\sup_{y}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V(x)|\langle\varepsilon(x-y)\rangle^{-N}\mathrm{d}x.

\displaystyle\|K_{\zeta}\|_{2\to 2}\lesssim\mathrm{e}^{c|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|}\quad(\operatorname{Re}\zeta=0).

\displaystyle\|K_{\zeta}\|_{1\to 1}\lesssim\mathrm{e}^{c|\operatorname{Im}\zeta|}C_{\varepsilon,q,\Omega,N}(V)^{q}\quad(\operatorname{Re}\zeta=q).

5.1.
Proof of Theorem 4

By scaling, it suffices to prove the bounds for
|z|=1
only. We only give a proof in the case
\operatorname{Re}z\gtrsim 1
, which is the most difficult one. Note that the factor
\operatorname{Im}z(x-y)
is dimensionless as is should be. Hence we can take
I=[1-\delta,1+\delta]
in (
20
) and find that
S=\{1-\delta^{\prime}\leq|\xi|\leq 1+\delta^{\prime}\}
for some small positive constants
\delta,\delta^{\prime}
. Recall that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
was chosen such that
S\Subset\Omega
(see the paragraph after (

|h_{0}(\xi)-z|\geq C_{\Omega}+\langle\xi\rangle^{s}
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega
and hence, by Sobolev embedding,

\|[H_{0}^{\overline{\eta}}-z]^{-1}\|_{p\to p^{\prime}}\lesssim\|[H_{0}^{\overline{\eta}}-z]^{-1}\|_{H^{-\frac{\sigma}{2}}\to H^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}}\lesssim C_{\Omega}^{\frac{\sigma}{s}-1}

for
\sigma/d\geq p^{-1}-(p^{\prime})^{-1}=q^{-1}
, where we denoted the
L^{2}
based Sobolev space of order
\sigma
by
H^{\sigma}
and used Plancherel in the last inequality.
We choose
\sigma=d/q_{s}
.
If
C_{\Omega}^{\frac{\sigma}{s}-1}\|V\|_{q}\ll 1
, then (
22
) holds by Hölder’s inequality and a geometric series argument, and hence Proposition
20
yields (i), (ii).

Moving on to the proof of (iii), the claim would follow from Proposition
24
if we could show (
31
). For brevity, we restrict our attention to the case
s<d
. Precisely, we will show that
c_{q,I,V}<\infty
if

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x-y\rangle^{-N}|V(x)|^{q}\mathrm{d}x\ll 1,

(36)

where
N\gg 1
and
q\geq d/s
.
Let us abbreviate the constant
c_{q,I,V}
by
c_{V}
. We also set
c_{0}:=\|V\overline{\eta}[H_{V}^{\overline{\eta}}-z]^{-1}\|_{p\to p}
(by compactness we can fix

). Without loss of generality assume that the inverse Fourier transform of
\eta
is normalized in

, so that
\eta(D)
is an isometry in

(and similarly for
\overline{\eta}
). If we could prove

, then a geometric series argument would yield

and we would be done. The next lemma establishes

Lemma 25
.

Let
s<d
. If (
36

q\geq d/s
, then
c_{0}<1
.

Proof.

By the Mikhlin multiplier theorem
[
16
, Th. 5.2.7]
it suffices to prove this for
\|V\Lambda^{-s}\|_{p\to p}
in place of
c_{0}
, where we recall that
\Lambda=(1-\Delta)
. Standard estimates for Bessel potentials (see e.g.
[
15
, Prop. 6.1.5]
) yield
\Lambda^{-s}(x-y)\lesssim|x-y|^{s-d}\mathrm{e}^{-|x-y|/2}
. Clearly, we may bound the exponential from above by
\langle x-y\rangle^{-N}
for any
N
, which we will do. In view of the elementary estimate

\displaystyle\langle x-y\rangle^{-N}\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-Nj}\mathbf{1}_{|x-y|\leq 2^{j}}

(37)

it would suffice to prove the following bound on

c_{j}:=\|V\Lambda_{s,j}\|_{p\to p}
, where
\Lambda_{s,j}
has kernel
|x-y|^{s-d}\mathbf{1}_{|x-y|\leq 2^{j}}
:

\displaystyle c_{j}\lesssim 2^{j(d+s-d/q)}\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|V\|_{L^{q}(B(u,2^{j+1}))}.

By homogeneity it suffices to prove this for
j=0
. Using
|V(x)|\leq\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|V_{u}(x)|
, with
V_{u}(x)=V(x)\mathbf{1}_{|x-u|\leq 2}
, we estimate

\displaystyle|\langle V\Lambda_{s,0}f,g\rangle|\leq\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int\int|V_{u}(x)||g_{u}(x)||f(y)||x-y|^{s-d}\mathbf{1}_{|x-y|\leq 1}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Note that, by the triangle inequality, we can insert
\mathbf{1}_{|y-u|\leq 3}
for free into the integral. Then, by Young’s inequality (or by the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev inequality if
q=d/s
) and Hölder (once for integrals and once for sums),

\displaystyle|\langle V\Lambda_{s,0}f,g\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\|V\|_{L^{q}(B(u,2))}\|f\|_{L^{p}(B(u,3))}\|g\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(B(u,2))}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\|V\|_{L^{q}(B(u,2))}\big(\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\|f\|_{L^{p}(B(u,3))}^{p}\big)^{1/p}\big(\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\|g\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(B(u,2))}^{p^{\prime}}\big)^{1/p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|V\|_{L^{q}(B(u,2))}\|f\|_{p}\|g\|_{p^{\prime}}.

This completes the proof.
