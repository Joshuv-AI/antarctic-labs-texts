A universal formula for counting cubic surfaces
Anand Deopurkar, Anand Patel & Dennis Tseng

Abstract
. Using equivariant geometry, we find a universal formula that computes the number of times a general cubic surface arises in a family. As applications, we show that the PGL 4 \operatorname{PGL}_{4} orbit closure of a generic cubic surface has degree 96120, and that a general cubic surface arises 42120 times as a hyperplane section of a general cubic 3-fold.



1.
Introduction

Cubic surfaces have fascinated us for centuries. Ever since Cayley and
Salmon discovered the 27 lines, it has become somewhat of a tradition
to revisit their geometry, using increasingly sophisticated tools to
uncover more and more. Our contribution to this story provides a
universal answer to a collection of natural enumerative questions
about families of cubic surfaces.

The isomorphism classes of cubic surfaces form a 4-dimensional moduli
space. So, we expect that in a 4-dimensional family, a general
isomorphism class would appear finitely many times. How can we
determine this number? For example, how many times does a general
cubic surface appear, up to isomorphism, as

(1)

a member of a 4-dimensional general linear system of cubic surfaces in
{\bf P}^{3}
?

(2)

a hyperplane section of a general cubic threefold in
{\bf P}^{4}
?

(3)

a blow up of 6 points in
{\bf P}^{2}
varying in a general
4-dimensional linear system on a cubic curve?

Our formula answers all questions of this kind. The answers to these
specific questions are
96120
,
42120
, and
25920
, respectively.

To state our formula, we must fix a precise moduli space of cubics.
Let us denote by
\mathcal{M}
the GIT quotient of the set of
semistable cubic surfaces in
{\bf P}^{3}
by the action of
\operatorname{PGL}_{4}
. By a

family of cubic surfaces
, we mean a flat, proper morphism whose
geometric fibers are isomorphic to cubic surfaces in
{\bf P}^{3}
.

1.0.1
.

Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}{\longrightarrow}B
be a good family of cubic surfaces over a
proper base
B
.
Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the rank 4 vector bundle

\pi_{*}\left(\omega_{\pi}^{-1}\right)
, and let

v_{i}=c_{i}\left(\mathcal{V}\right)
be its Chern classes. Assume that a
general fiber of
\pi
is semistable, and let

\mu\colon B\dashrightarrow\mathcal{M}
be the induced moduli map. Then,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\deg\mu=1080\int_{B}\left(v_{1}^{2}v_{2}-v_{1}v_{3}+9v_{4}\right).

2.4.1
gives the precise meaning of a good family. This notion includes any family whose fibers are
GIT-semistable or have a finite automorphism group.

1.1.
Degree of the orbit closure

Question
1
in
§ 1
fits in a broader story
that has been an ongoing topic of study. Consider a hypersurface

X
in a projective space
{\bf P}V
cut out by a homogeneous polynomial

F\in\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee}
. In
{\bf P}\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee}
, consider the orbit of

[F]
under the action of
\operatorname{GL}(V)
, and let
\Orb([F])
be its
closure. What is the degree of
\Orb([F])
? To our knowledge,
Enriques and Fano in 1897 were the first to address this question.
They computed the degree for simple binary forms of all degrees

[
16
]
. In a series of seminal papers
[
5
,
3
,
4
]
, Aluffi and Faber computed the degree of the
orbit closure for
all
binary and ternary forms, finishing off
the cases of points in
{\bf P}^{1}
and curves in
{\bf P}^{2}
. The next case is
that of cubic surfaces in
{\bf P}^{3}
.
Theorem 1.0.1
applied to
Question
1
settles it for general cubic surfaces—the
degree of the orbit closure of a general cubic surface is
96120
.

Question
1
appears as one of the “twenty-seven questions
about the cubic surface” by Ranestad and Sturmfels
[
24
]
.
In 2019, Brustenga i Moncusí, Timme, and Weinstein gave a
“numerical proof” for the number 96120. In 2020, Cazzador and
Skauli outlined an approach towards a proof by following the
techniques of Aluffi and Faber
[
10
,
12
]
.
Our approach is different.

1.2.
The equivariant class of the orbit closure

Our key idea is to consider not just the degree of the orbit closure,
but the class of the orbit closure in the equivariant Chow ring

A^{\bullet}_{\operatorname{GL}V}(\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee})
.
The equivariant class subsumes the
degree, but, counter-intuitively, it is easier to find.

Let us explain how the equivariant class yields the degree.
We have natural maps

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{\bullet}_{\operatorname{GL}V}(\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{\bullet}_{\operatorname{GL}V}(\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee}\setminus\{0\})\otimes{\bf Q}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{\bullet}_{\operatorname{PGL}V}({\bf P}\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee})\otimes{\bf Q}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A^{\bullet}({\bf P}\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee})\otimes{\bf Q}.}

Under the composite map, the class of
\Orb(F)\in A^{\bullet}_{\operatorname{GL}V}(\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee})
is mapped to the class of
\Orb([F])\in A^{\bullet}({\bf P}\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee})\otimes{\bf Q}
, which is simply the degree of
\Orb([F])
.

Let us explain what makes it possible to find the equivariant
class. The ring
A^{\bullet}_{\operatorname{GL}V}(\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee})
is the polynomial ring
in variables
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
, where
r=\dim V
, and the variable

c_{i}
has degree
i
. The equivariant class of
\Orb(F)
lies in the
graded component
A^{m}(\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee})
, where

m=\dim\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee}-\dim\operatorname{GL}V
. As a result, it can be expressed as
a
{\bf Z}
-linear combination of monomials in
c_{i}
of total degree

m
. We can now use the method of undetermined coefficients. We
construct maps
B{\longrightarrow}[\operatorname{Sym}^{d}V^{\vee}/\operatorname{GL}V]
where the pull-back of

\Orb(F)
as well as the classes
c_{i}
can be explicitly
computed. Every such family gives a linear relationship between the
coefficients in the expression of
\Orb(F)
, which are to be determined.
If we have enough families, we get enough information to find all the coefficients.
For cubic surfaces, we construct more than enough families—there are 5
coefficients to be determined and we construct 8 families. The redundancy
serves as an extra check on the result.

1.2.1
.

Let
V
be a 4-dimensional vector space and let
F
be a general
element of
\operatorname{Sym}^{3}V^{\vee}
.
The equivariant class of the
\operatorname{GL}(V)
-orbit closure
\Orb(F)
of
F

in
A^{\bullet}_{\operatorname{GL}V}(\operatorname{Sym}^{3}V^{\vee})
is given by

[\Orb F]=24c_{1}^{4}+12c_{1}^{2}c_{2}-6c_{1}c_{3}+9c_{4},

where the
c_{i}=c_{i}(V)
are the Chern classes of the tautological vector
bundle
V
.

The formula in
Theorem 1.2.1
appears to be different from the one
in
Theorem 1.0.1
, but they are related by a simple change of
variables; see
§ 4.2
.

Most of the paper is devoted to constructing
provably
good
families of cubic surfaces on which we can compute
\deg\mu
and the
Chern classes
c_{i}
. Proofs of goodness are sometimes challenging—
they require solving instances of the problem of determining which
homogeneous forms lie in the orbit closure of which other forms.
To give a teaser for our test families, we list their
bases
B
here:
{\bf P}^{4}
,
\overline{M}_{0,7}
, a
Hassett-weighted variant of
\overline{M}_{0,7}
, a blow up of the
Hilbert scheme of two points on a quintic del Pezzo surface, and last
but not least, the stack
B\mathbf{G}_{m}
.

The method of undetermined coefficients applies to hypersurfaces of
any degree and dimension. The obstacle, in general, is in
constructing enough families. Our constructions crucially use the
beautiful but specific geometry cubic surfaces.

1.3.
The orbit closure problem

To apply the method of undetermined coefficients, we have to ensure that no fibers of our family lie in the orbit closure of generic cubic surfaces (otherwise, we get excess intersection).
Thus, we have to prove statements of the form “a given cubic surface
S
is not in the closure of a suitably generic cubic surface.”
This is, in general, a difficult problem.
We develop a range of tools to address problems of this kind.
To preserve the flow of the paper, and because these techniques may be of independent interest, we assemble these tools in a separate section (
§ 5
).

1.4.
Further remarks

The formula in
Theorem 1.0.1
has some curious consequences.

1.4.1
.

If
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}{\longrightarrow}B
is a good family of cubic
surfaces parametrized by a
4
dimensional proper variety
B
,
then

\int_{B}v_{1}^{2}v_{2}-v_{1}v_{3}+9v_{4}\geq 0

with
equality holding if and only if a general cubic surface does not
arise as a fiber.

1.4.2
.

Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}{\longrightarrow}B
is a good family of cubic
surfaces parametrized by a
4
dimensional proper variety
B
.
Assume that a general fiber of
\pi
is smooth and the induced map

\mu\colon B\dashrightarrow\mathcal{M}
is dominant.
Then the degree of
\mu
is divisible by 1080.

In particular, a parameter space for cubic surfaces that carries a
good universal family must cover
\mathcal{M}
by a map of degree
divisible by 1080. Interestingly, the number 1080 is also the least
common multiple of the orders of the finite groups that arise as
automorphism groups of cubic surfaces.
We wonder if
1080
is the minimal positive integer realizable as
\deg\mu
as we vary
across all good families of cubic surfaces.

1.5.
Future directions

Theorem 1.2.1
is only the beginning. Each
particular

cubic surface
F=0
registers its own equivariant orbit class

[\Orb(F)]
. A refined study of the methods developed in this paper
may afford us a complete understanding of how this class changes
according to special changes in the geometry of
F=0
, e.g. acquiring
singularities or Eckardt points. This is something we hope to pursue
in the future.

In a different direction, there should be analogous formulas counting
Del Pezzo surfaces of degrees
1,2
and
4
. We fully expect our
methods to establish formulas in these settings, though we have not
even begun the analysis.

Finally, we repeat a comment of Aluffi and Faber
[
3
]
: this paper computes the equivariant orbit class of a general vector in
one
representation of
one
group. A whole lot of work needs to be done if we want to solve the problem in general.

1.6.
Organization

In
§ 2
, we recall standard facts about cubic surfaces and their moduli.
We also review the basic notions of equivariant Chow theory.
The main new ingredient in this section is the definition of a good family (
2.4.1
).
In
§ 3
, we construct a number of test families of cubic surfaces and evaluate the linear relation on the coefficients of the class of the orbit closure given by each family.
For each family, we first give an informal description.
The first four families, taken up in the first four subsections, use variations on the theme of blowing up 6 points in
{\bf P}^{2}
.
The next four families, taken up in the fifth subsection, are isotrivial; their base space is the stack
B\mathbf{G}_{m}
.
Altogether, we produce 8 families for 5 undetermined coefficients.
In
§ 4
, we tie together the enumerative results and obtain the main theorems stated in the introduction.
The last section,
§ 5
is devoted to developing methods to solve cases of the orbit closure problem.

2.2.
Moduli of cubic surfaces

Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}{\longrightarrow}B
be a family of cubic surfaces. That is,
let
\pi
be a flat, proper morphism whose fibers are isomorphic to
cubic hypersurfaces in
{\bf P}^{3}
. Since
\pi
is flat and its fibers are
Gorenstein, it is a Gorenstein morphism, and hence admits a dualizing
line bundle
\omega_{\pi}
. By the adjunction formula, it follows that
the restriction of
\omega_{\pi}^{-1}
to every fiber is isomorphic to
the restriction of
\mathcal{O}(1)
with respect to an embedding of the fiber

{\bf P}^{3}
. By standard theorems about

R^{i}\pi_{*}\left(\omega_{\pi}^{-1}\right)

a vector bundle for

\mathcal{V}=\pi_{*}\left(\omega_{\pi}^{-1}\right).

The natural map

\pi^{*}\mathcal{V}{\longrightarrow}\omega_{\pi}^{-1}

yields a closed embedding

(2.1)

\mathcal{X}\subset{\bf P}\mathcal{V}^{\vee}.

We know that

\omega_{{\bf P}\mathcal{V}^{\vee}/B}=\mathcal{O}(-4)\otimes\det\mathcal{V}^{\vee},

and hence, by adjunction,

\omega_{\mathcal{X}/B}=\mathcal{O}(-4)\otimes\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{X})\otimes\det\mathcal{V}^{\vee}|_{\mathcal{X}}.

By construction, we also have

\omega_{\mathcal{X}/B}=\mathcal{O}(-1)|_{\mathcal{X}},

and hence we obtain that

(2.2)

\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{O}(3)\otimes\det\mathcal{V}.

In other words,
\mathcal{X}\subset{\bf P}\mathcal{V}^{\vee}
is the zero locus of
a section of the line bundle
\mathcal{O}(3)\otimes\det\mathcal{V}^{\vee}
.

Consider the category
\mathscr{C}
fibered in groupoids over the
category of schemes whose objects over a scheme

cubic surfaces

\mathscr{M}
fibered in groupoids
over the category of schemes whose objects over a scheme

vector bundles
\mathcal{V}
over

\xi

are pull-back diagrams. Let

be a

is simply the quotient stack

\mathscr{M}=\left[\operatorname{Sym}^{3}V^{\vee}\otimes\det V\setminus\{0\}/\operatorname{GL}V\right].

We recall perhaps the most famous fact about cubic surfaces, discovered in 1849.

2.2.2

[
11
]

[

Every smooth cubic surface contains exactly

As we know, the 27 lines are key to understanding geometry of the

at 6 points.

Following Schläfli
[

–

and

\ell_{i}
and
\ell^{\prime}_{j}

.

2.2.3

[
28
]
.

Each six on a smooth cubic surface has a unique extension to a double-six, and every smooth cubic surface contains

Thus, each smooth cubic surface admits 72 distinct maps to

, up to automorphisms of

, each of which

2.3.
Equivariant Chow groups and equivariant orbits

The standard reference for equivariant Chow theory is
[
15
]
.
We recall a few relevant notions, mostly to set notation.

Given an algebraic space
X
with an action of an algebraic group
G
,
denote by
A^{i}_{G}(X)
the
G
-equivariant Chow group of codimension
i

cycles. Given a
G
-invariant subspace
Y\subset X
of pure
codimension
i
, denote by
[Y]\in A^{i}_{G}(X)
the fundamental class of

Y
. If
Y\subset X
has codimension
c
and
c>i
, then the restriction map

A^{i}_{G}(X){\longrightarrow}A^{i}_{G}(X\setminus Y)

is an isomorphism; in general, it is surjective with kernel is
A^{i-c}_{G}(Y)
.
A
G
-linearized vector bundle

V
on
X
admits equivariant Chern classes
c_{i}(V)\in A^{i}_{G}(X)
.
The equivariant Chow groups are homotopy invariant: if
Y{\longrightarrow}X
is a

G
-equivariant vector bundle, then the pull-back map

A^{i}_{G}(X){\longrightarrow}A^{i}_{G}(Y)
is an isomorphism. If
X
is smooth, then

\bigoplus_{i}A^{i}_{G}(X)=A^{*}_{G}(X)
is a graded ring, where the multiplication is given by the
intersection product.

The
G
-equivariant Chow groups of
X
are the same as the Chow groups
of the quotient stack
[X/G]
:

A^{i}\left([X/G]\right)=A^{i}_{G}(X).

The equivariant Chow ring of a point with the action of
G=\operatorname{GL}_{n}
is
the polynomial ring

A_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(\bullet)={\bf Z}[c_{1},\dots,c_{n}],

where
c_{i}
has
degree
i
and can be identified with the
i
-th Chern class of the
standard
n
-dimensional representation of
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
. By homotopy
invariance, for any
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
representation
W
, we have

A_{\operatorname{GL}_{n}}(W)={\bf Z}[c_{1},\dots,c_{n}].

If the group under consideration is
\operatorname{GL}V
, where
V
is an
n
-dimensional vector space,
then we write
v_{i}
for the
i
-th Chern class of the tautological
representation
V
. Then we have

A_{\operatorname{GL}V}(W)={\bf Z}[v_{1},\dots,v_{n}].

Let
V
be a
4
-dimensional vector space.
Consider the
\operatorname{GL}V
representation
W=\operatorname{Sym}^{3}V^{\vee}\otimes\det V
.
By
2.2.1
, (non-zero)
\operatorname{GL}V
orbits in
W
correspond canonically to cubic surfaces.
Note that the action of
\operatorname{GL}V
on
W
is generically free, and hence the codimension of a generic orbit is
20-16=4
.
Given a point
w\in W
, let
\Orb(w)
denote the closure of its
\operatorname{GL}V

[\Orb(w)]\in A^{4}_{\operatorname{GL}V}(W)=A^{4}(\mathscr{M})

denote the fundamental class.
By a slight abuse of notation, if
X\subset{\bf P}^{3}
is a cubic surface or
F\in k[X_{0},\dots,X_{3}]
is a non-zero cubic form, we let
\Orb(X)
or

denote the closure of the orbit in

the fundamental class.
Since

2.4.
Good families

Our strategy is to compute
[\Orb(X)]
for a generic
X
by pulling it back to various families
B{\longrightarrow}\mathscr{M}
.
If there is a point of
B
that lies in every
\Orb(X)
, then we have the issue of excess intersection.
A good family is one that does not suffer from this defect.
Recall that
V
is a
4
-dimensional
k
-vector space.

2.4.1
Good family.

A family of cubic surfaces
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}{\longrightarrow}B
is
good
if
the following holds: there exists a non-empty Zariski open subset

U\subset\operatorname{Sym}^{3}V^{\vee}
such that for every
F\in U
and
b\in B
,
the fiber
\mathcal{X}_{b}
does not lie in
\partial\Orb(F)
.

2.4.2
Fibers with finite automorphism groups.

Suppose every
\mathcal{X}_{b}
has a finite automorphism group. Then

\pi\colon\mathcal{X}{\longrightarrow}B
is good. Indeed, let
U
be the subset
consisting of elements with finite automorphism group. For every

F\in U
, the boundary
\partial\Orb(F)
consists of elements with
a positive dimensional automorphism group, and hence it cannot
contain
\mathcal{X}_{b}
.

2.4.3
GIT semistable fibers.

Suppose every fiber
\mathcal{X}_{b}
is GIT-semistable.
Then the family
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}{\longrightarrow}B
is good.
Indeed, let
U
be the subset consisting of GIT-stable elements.
For every
F\in U
, the orbit of
F
is closed in the semistable locus, and hence
\partial\Orb(F)
cannot contain
\mathcal{X}_{b}
.

2.4.4
A combination.

Suppose every fiber
\mathcal{X}_{b}
has a finite automorphism group or is GIT semistable.
Let
U
be the subset corresponding to smooth cubic surfaces.
Since smooth cubic surfaces have a finite automorphism group and are GIT stable, we see that
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}{\longrightarrow}B
is good.

2.4.5
.

Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}{\longrightarrow}B
be a good family of cubic surfaces.
Assume that
B
is proper of dimension 4 and a general fiber
\mathcal{X}_{b}
is semistable.
Let
\mu\colon B{\longrightarrow}\mathscr{M}
be the map induced by
\pi
and
m\colon B\dashrightarrow\mathcal{M}
the rational map to the coarse moduli space.
Then

\deg m=\deg\mu^{*}\left(\Orb(F)\right)

for a general cubic form
F
.

Proof.

There exists an open
W\subset\mathcal{M}
such that
m^{-1}(W){\longrightarrow}W
is a finite étale morphism.
Then the degree of
m
is simply the number of points in
m^{-1}(u)
for
u\in W
.
By shrinking
W
if necessary, we may assume that the map

\mathscr{M}^{ss}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{M}

is an isomorphism over
W
.

Let
U
be as in the definition a good family.
Then, for every
u\in U
, we have the equality

\mathcal{X}\times_{\mathscr{M}}u=\mathcal{X}\times_{\mathscr{M}}\overline{u}.

By shrinking
U
if necessary, we may assume that
U
consists of semistable points and maps to
W\subset\mathcal{M}
in the coarse space.
Then, for every
u\in U
, we have

\displaystyle\deg\mu^{*}\Orb(u)

\displaystyle=\#\left(\mathcal{X}\times_{\mathscr{M}}u\right)

\displaystyle=\#\left(\mathcal{X}\times_{\mathcal{M}}u\right)

\displaystyle=\deg m.

∎

2.5.
Cubic surfaces as blow-ups

Cubic surfaces arise as blow ups of
{\bf P}^{2}
at six points, or more generally, as blow ups of del Pezzo surfaces of degree
d
in
(d-3)
points.
Varying the center of blow up is a convenient source of families of cubic surfaces.
The following proposition identifies certain nice sub-schemes to blow up.

2.5.1
Admissible subscheme.

Let
S
be a smooth del Pezzo surface of degree
d\geq 3
and let
Z\subset S
be a subscheme of length
(d-3)
.
We say that
Z
is
admissible
if

(1)

Z
is curvilinear (contained in a smooth curve),

(2)

h^{0}\left(\mathcal{I}_{Z}\otimes\omega_{S}^{-1}\right)=4
, and

(3)

\mathcal{I}_{Z}\otimes\omega_{S}^{-1}
is generated by its global sections.

The last condition means that
Z
is scheme-theoretically cut out by the sections of
\omega_{S}^{-1}
that vanish on it.

2.5.2
.

Let
Z\subset S
be an admissible length
(d-3)
subscheme.
Set
X=\Bl_{Z}S
.
Then the following hold.

(1)

X
has at worst
A_{n}
singularities.

(2)

H^{0}\left(X,\omega^{-1}_{X}\right)
is
4
-dimensional and base-point free.

(3)

H^{i}(X,\omega_{X}^{-n})=0
for all
n\geq 0
and
i>0
.

(4)

The image of the map
X{\longrightarrow}{\bf P}^{3}
induced by
\omega_{X}^{-1}
is a cubic surface
Y
; the map
X{\longrightarrow}Y
is birational.

(5)

Let
S=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}S_{n}
be the homogeneous coordinate ring of
Y
.
The pull-back map
S_{n}{\longrightarrow}H^{0}\left(X,\omega_{X}^{-n}\right)
is an isomorphism.

(6)

Y
is normal and has rational double point (ADE) singularities.

Proof.

Let
\pi\colon X{\longrightarrow}S
be the blow-up map.
Since
Z
is curvilinear,
X
has at worst
A_{n}
singularities.
Indeed, if
Z
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Spec}k[t]/t^{n+1}
at a point
z
in its support, then
X
is singular at a unique point of
\pi^{-1}(z)
; this singularity is of type
A_{n}
.
Note that
E_{z}:=\pi^{-1}(z)
(taken with the reduced structure) is isomorphic to
{\bf P}^{1}
.

Let
E\subset X
be the exceptional divisor, namely, the Cartier divisor defined by the ideal
\pi^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{Z}
.
Over the point
z
as above,
E
has multiplicity
n
along the component
E_{z}
.
Furthermore, we have
(nE_{z})^{2}=-n
, and hence
E^{2}=-(d-3)
.
Also, see that we have

(2.4)

\omega_{X}=\pi^{*}\omega_{S}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(E),

and therefore,

\omega_{X}^{-1}=\pi^{*}\omega^{-1}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(-E).

By applying
\pi_{*}
, we get

\pi_{*}\left(\omega_{X}^{-1}\right)=\mathcal{I}_{Z}\otimes\omega_{S}^{-1}.

By our assumption,
H^{0}\left(\mathcal{I}_{Z}\otimes\omega_{S}^{-1}\right)
is
4
-dimensional and its base-locus is precisely
Z
.
Hence
H^{0}\left(X,\omega_{X}^{-1}\right)
is
4
-dimensional and base-point free.

For the vanishing of higher cohomology, we induct on
n
.
Since
X
is a rational surface with rational singularities, we have
h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X})=h^{1}(\omega_{X})=0
.
Let
C\subset X
be the zero locus of a general section of
\omega_{X}^{-1}
.
By Bertini’s theorem,
C
is smooth and by the adjunction formula, it has arithmetic genus
1
.
Taking global sections in the sequence

0{\longrightarrow}\omega_{X}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{O}_{X}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{O}_{C}{\longrightarrow}0

shows that
h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{C})=1
, and hence
C
is connected.
Twisting the above sequence by
\omega_{X}^{-n}
gives

0{\longrightarrow}\omega_{X}^{-n+1}{\longrightarrow}\omega_{X}^{-n}{\longrightarrow}\omega_{X}^{-n}|_{C}{\longrightarrow}0.

The restriction
\omega_{X}^{-n}|_{C}
has degree
3n
, which is positive if
n
is positive, and hence
h^{i}\left(\omega_{X}^{-n}|_{C}\right)=0
for
i>0
.
By applying the long exact sequence in cohomology, we see that if
i>0
, then
h^{i}(\omega_{X}^{-n+1})=0
implies
h^{i}(\omega_{X}^{-n})=0
.
By induction on
n
, we conclude
h^{i}(\omega_{X}^{-n})=0
for all
n\geq 0
and
i>0
.

To get the degree of the image of
X{\longrightarrow}{\bf P}^{3}
, we compute the self-intersection
c_{1}(\omega_{X})^{2}
.
Using (
2.4
) and
E^{2}=-(d-3)
, we compute

c_{1}(\omega_{X})^{2}=c_{1}(\omega_{S})^{2}-d+3=3.

Thus, either the image is a hyperplane
\Pi
and the map
X{\longrightarrow}\Pi
has degree 3, or the image is a cubic surface
Y
and the map
X{\longrightarrow}Y
is birational.
We rule out the first possibility since the map
X{\longrightarrow}{\bf P}^{3}
is defined by linearly independent sections, and hence does not factor through a hyperplane.

By Riemann–Roch, we get that
\chi(X,\omega_{X}^{-n})=\chi(Y,\mathcal{O}(n))
.
We also know that for
i>0
and
n\geq 0
, both
h^{i}(X,\omega_{X}^{-n})
and
h^{i}(Y,\mathcal{O}(n))
vanish.
Hence, we get
h^{0}(X,\omega_{X}^{-n})=h^{0}(Y,\mathcal{O}(n))
.
Recall that, the
n
-th graded component
S_{n}
of the homogeneous coordinate ring of
Y
is precisely
H^{0}(Y,\mathcal{O}(n))
.
Since
X{\longrightarrow}Y
is surjective, the natural map
S_{n}{\longrightarrow}H^{0}(X,\omega_{X}^{-n})
is injective.
Since the dimensions of the source and the target are equal, it must be an isomorphism.

From the isomorphism
S\cong\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}H^{0}(X,\omega_{X}^{-n})
, we get

Y={\text{\rm Proj}\,}\left(\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}H^{0}\left(X,\omega_{X}^{-1}\right)\right).

Since
X
is normal, so is
Y
.
It is known that an irreducible, normal, rational cubic surface, such as
Y
, only has rational double point (ADE) singularities
[
9
]
.
∎

2.5.3
Associated cubic surface.

Let
S
be a smooth del Pezzo surface of degree
d
and let
Z\subset S
be an admissible subscheme of length
d-3
.
The
cubic surface associated to
Z\subset S
, denoted by

X_{Z}\subset{\bf P}^{3}
, is the image of the map

\Bl_{Z}S{\longrightarrow}{\bf P}^{3}
induced by the anti-canonical linear system
H^{0}\left(\Bl_{Z}S,\omega_{\Bl_{Z}S}^{-1}\right)
.

By
2.5.2
, we may equivalently define
X_{Z}
as

X_{Z}={\text{\rm Proj}\,}\left(\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}H^{0}\left(\Bl_{Z}S,\omega_{\Bl_{Z}S}^{-n}\right)\right).

Let us make the construction
Z\leadsto X_{Z}
in families.
Let
B
be a scheme,
P{\longrightarrow}B
a smooth proper family of del-Pezzo surfaces and
Z\subset P
a closed subscheme, flat over
B
, whose fibers are admissible subschemes.
Consider the blowup
\Bl_{Z}P
.
Note that
Z\subset P
is a local complete intersection, and hence the blow-up is
B
-flat and commutes with arbitrary base change.
In particular, the fiber
(\Bl_{Z}P)_{b}
is the blow up of
P_{b}
along
Z_{b}
.
(The statement about blow-ups and base-change must be well-known, but we could not find a reference so we include a proof; see
2.5.4
.)
The family of cubic surfaces
X_{Z}{\longrightarrow}B
associated to
Z\subset P{\longrightarrow}B
is defined by

X_{Z}={\text{\rm Proj}\,}_{B}\left(\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\pi_{*}\left(\Bl_{Z}P,\omega_{\Bl_{Z}P/B}^{-n}\right)\right).

By
2.5.2
, we see that
X_{Z}
is flat over
B

and its fiber over
b\in B
is the cubic surface associated to

Z_{b}\subset P_{b}
, as in
2.5.3
.

2.5.4
.

Let
\pi\colon P{\longrightarrow}B
be a smooth morphism and
Z\subset P
a closed subscheme flat and lci over
B
.
Then
\Bl_{Z}P{\longrightarrow}B
is flat and for any
C{\longrightarrow}B
, the canonical map

\Bl_{Z_{C}}P_{C}{\longrightarrow}\left(\Bl_{Z}P\right)_{C}.

is an isomorphism.

Proof.

The statement is local on
P
.
After passing to a suitable affine open, we are reduced to the following situation in algebra.
We have a Noetherian ring
B
and a finitely generated smooth
B
-algebra
A
.
We have an ideal
I\subset A
such that
A/I
is
B
-flat and
I
is generated by a regular sequence.
The blow-up is the
Proj
of the Rees algebra

R_{I}(A)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}I^{n}.

Since
I\subset A
is generated by a regular sequence, the associated graded ring
\operatorname{gr}R_{I}(A)
is a polynomial algebra over
A/I
, and hence flat over
B
.
It follows that
R_{I}(A)
is flat over
B
, and hence its
Proj
is flat over
\operatorname{Spec}B
.

For the statement about base-change, we show that the formation of the Rees algebra commutes with base-change.
For a
B
-algebra
C
, let
J\subset A\otimes_{B}C
be the image of
I\otimes_{B}C
.
Consider the map

i\colon R_{I}(A)\otimes_{B}C{\longrightarrow}R_{J}(C).

We use again that the associated graded rings are polynomial algebras, on which the map induced by
i
is clearly an isomorphism.
Hence
i
is an isomorphism.
∎

Let
Z\subset{\bf P}^{2}
be an admissible subscheme of length 6.
An automorphism of the pair
({\bf P}^{2},Z)
induces an automorphism of
\Bl_{Z}{\bf P}^{2}
and hence an automorphism of the associated cubic surface
X_{Z}
.

2.5.5
.

Let
Z\subset{\bf P}^{2}
be an admissible, length
6

subscheme and
X_{Z}\subset{\bf P}^{3}
its associated cubic surface.
The map

\operatorname{Aut}({\bf P}^{2},Z){\longrightarrow}\operatorname{Aut}(X_{Z})

is injective and its image has finite index.
In particular,
\operatorname{Aut}({\bf P}^{2},Z)
is finite if and only if
\operatorname{Aut}(X_{Z})
is.

Proof.

Since we have a birational isomorphism
{\bf P}^{2}\dashrightarrow X_{Z}
, a non-trivial automorphism of
{\bf P}^{2}
cannot induce a trivial automorphism of
X_{Z}
.
Hence the map of automorphism groups is injective.

To show that the image has finite index, let
\tau\colon\widetilde{Y}{\longrightarrow}\Bl_{Z}{\bf P}^{2}
be the minimal desingularization and set
Y=X_{Z}
.
Since
\Bl_{Z}{\bf P}^{2}
has only
A_{n}
singularities and such singularities are canonical, we conclude that

\tau^{*}\omega_{\Bl_{Z}{\bf P}^{2}}=\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}.

Consider the composite
\mu
of

\widetilde{Y}\xrightarrow{\tau}\Bl_{Z}{\bf P}^{2}\xrightarrow{\kappa}Y.

Note that

\mu^{*}\omega_{Y}=\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}.

We claim that
\mu\colon\widetilde{Y}{\longrightarrow}Y
is the minimal desingularization of
Y
.
To see this, let
Y^{\prime}{\longrightarrow}Y
be the minimal desingularization.
Since
\widetilde{Y}
is non-singular, the birational morphism
\mu\colon\widetilde{Y}{\longrightarrow}Y
factors as a composite

\widetilde{Y}\xrightarrow{\alpha}Y^{\prime}{\longrightarrow}Y.

Since
\alpha
is a birational morphism between non-singular surfaces, it is a composite of blow-ups at points.
In particular, if it is not an isomorphism, then there exists a
(-1)
-curve
E\subset\widetilde{Y}
contracted by
\alpha
.
But then we have

E\cdot\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}=E\cdot\mu^{*}\omega_{Y}=0,

which contradicts the adjunction formula

\left(\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}+E\right)\cdot E=-2.

Thus, we deduce that
\alpha
is an isomorphism, and hence
\widetilde{Y}{\longrightarrow}Y
is the minimal desingularization of
Y
.

Since every automorphism of a surface lifts uniquely to its minimal
desingularization, we have an injective map

\operatorname{Aut}({\bf P}^{3},X_{Z}){\longrightarrow}\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y}).

It suffices to show that the image of
\operatorname{Aut}({\bf P}^{2},Z)
has finite index in
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y})
.

Let
H\in\operatorname{Pic}(\widetilde{Y})
be the pullback of
\mathcal{O}(1)
under the map
\widetilde{Y}{\longrightarrow}{\bf P}^{2}
.
We claim that the orbit of
H
under
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y})
is finite.
To see this, note that we have

\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}^{2}=3\text{ and }\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}\cdot H=-3,

and hence
(\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}+H)
is orthogonal to
\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}
under the intersection pairing.
Since
\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}
has positive self-intersection, its orthogonal complement
\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}^{\perp}\subset\operatorname{Pic}(\widetilde{Y})={\rm NS}(\widetilde{Y})
is a negative definite lattice by the Hodge index theorem.
Thus, it has only finitely many elements of a given self-intersection.
Any automorphism of
\widetilde{Y}
preserves
\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}
and the self-intersection number.
Therefore, under
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y})
, the orbit of
\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}+H
and hence the orbit of
H
, must be finite.

Since the orbit of
H
is finite, its stabilizer is a finite index subgroup.
We claim that this subgroup is precisely the image of
\operatorname{Aut}({\bf P}^{2},Z)
.
Let
\tau\colon\widetilde{Y}{\longrightarrow}\widetilde{Y}
be an automorphism fixing
H
.
The map
\beta\colon\widetilde{Y}{\longrightarrow}{\bf P}^{2}
is given by the complete linear system
|H|
.
Hence
\tau
induces an automorphism
\tau^{\prime}\colon{\bf P}^{2}{\longrightarrow}{\bf P}^{2}
.
It remains to show that
\tau^{\prime}
preserves
Z
.
Note that we have

\beta_{*}\left(\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}^{-1}\right)=\mathcal{O}(3)\otimes\mathcal{I}_{Z},

and hence

\beta_{*}\left(\omega_{\widetilde{Y}}^{-1}(-3H)\right)=\mathcal{I}_{Z}.

Therefore, the induced map
\tau^{\prime}
preserves
\mathcal{I}_{Z}
, and hence
Z
.
∎

The paper contains a staggering number of enumerative results about cuspidal cubics.
Our answer is in the first column in the row labelled

We briefly explain how to read off the result from the table in

and hence a map
\mathscr{N}{\longrightarrow}\mathscr{M}
.
Under this map, the pull-back of an orbit closure is an orbit closure.
Note that
\mathscr{X}\subset{\bf P}\mathcal{V}
is the zero locus of
\mathcal{O}(3)
.
By adjunction, we have

\omega_{\pi}=\mathcal{O}(1)\otimes\det V|_{\mathscr{X}},

and hence

\pi_{*}\omega_{\pi}=V^{\vee}\otimes\det V.

Theorem 4.0.1
gives the class of the pull-back of a generic orbit closure in terms of the Chern classes
v_{i}=c_{i}(V^{\vee}\otimes\det V)
.
Writing them in terms of
c_{i}=c_{i}(V)
yields the result.

5.
Addressing the orbit closure problem

We have repeatedly faced the problem of showing that a certain cubic surface is not in the orbit closure of a certain (generic) cubic surface.
In this section, we develop two sets of techniques to address this question.

5.1.
Using GIT

The first technique uses GIT and applies broadly to any orbit closure problem.
Let
W
be a quasi-projective variety with a linearized action of a reductive group
G
.
The basic observation is that if
x\in W
is semi-stable and
s\in W
is stable, then
x
does not lie in the orbit closure of
s
.
We prove an extension of this idea that turns out to be highly effective.

5.1.1
.

Let
G
be a reductive group.
Let
W
and
H
be smooth quasi-projective varieties along with linearized actions of
G
.
Let

be points satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

has a reductive stabilizer
T\subset G
.

(2)

for some
h\in H
fixed by the identity component of
T
, the point
(x,h)\in W\times H
is semi-stable.

(3)

for all
h\in H
, the point
(s,h)\in W\times H
is stable.

Then

does not lie in the closure of the
G
-orbit of

The proof needs the following lemma.
It is well-known to experts—it appears as a remark in

]
(see § 1.3, Immediate consequences: 5)—but we include a proof for completeness.
Recall that

is an algebraically closed field of characteristic 0, all schemes considered are of finite type over

5.1.2

Let

Let

If

-orbit of a point

\lambda\colon\mathbf{G}_{m}{\longrightarrow}T
and a point

in the

-orbit of

x=\lim_{t{\longrightarrow}0}\lambda(t)\cdot s^{\prime}.

Proof.

By

, there exists a

(Note that this result is substantially easier than the main theorem of

Define

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

5.2.
Using 6 points in
{\bf P}^{2}

The second technique is specific to cubic surfaces.

Let

T_{r}W
does not contain
p
or
q
.
Then
W
does not lie in the
\operatorname{PGL}_{3}
orbit closure of a star-free configuration
Z
.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive.
Let
\phi\in\operatorname{Aut}({\bf P}^{2}_{K})
be an automorphism such that
W
is the flat limit of
\phi(Z_{K})
.
We show that
T_{r}W
contains
p
or
q
.
The key idea is to consider flat limits of lines in
{\bf P}^{2}
under the action of
\phi
.
To do so, we use the same analysis as before but for the map between dual projective spaces

\phi^{\vee}\colon({\bf P}^{2})_{R}^{\vee}\dashrightarrow({\bf P}^{2})_{R}^{\vee}.

If
A
is the
3\times 3
matrix defining
\phi
, then the matrix defining
\phi^{\vee}
is
(A^{T})^{-1}
.

If
\phi_{0}^{\vee}
is defined for a line
\ell\in({\bf P}^{2})^{\vee}
, then the flat limit of the family of lines
\phi(\ell_{K})\subset{\bf P}^{2}_{K}
is the line corresponding to
\phi_{0}^{\vee}(\ell)
.
The statements (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) for the dual projective space say the following.
If
\phi^{\vee}
has rank 1, then the indeterminacy locus of
\phi^{\vee}_{0}
consists of a particular
\Lambda\in({\bf P}^{2})^{\vee}
.
For
\ell\neq\Lambda
, the map
\phi_{0}^{\vee}
is the composite of
\ell\mapsto\ell\cap\Lambda
and a linear inclusion
\Lambda{\longrightarrow}({\bf P}^{2})^{\vee}
.
In this case, a fiber of
\phi_{0}^{\vee}
consists of a collection of concurrent lines, with the point of concurrency on
\Lambda
.
If
\phi^{\vee}
has rank 1, then the indeterminacy locus of
\phi^{\vee}_{0}
consists of lines through a particular point
p\in{\bf P}^{2}
.
For
\ell\in({\bf P}^{2})^{\vee}
not containing
p
, the map
\phi_{0}^{\vee}
is constant.

Consider a line
\ell
joining pairs of points of
Z
.
By semi-continuity, the flat limit of
\phi(\ell_{K})
must intersect
W
in a scheme of length at least 2.
There are at most four lines in
{\bf P}^{2}
that intersect
W
in a scheme of length at least 2: the lines
\overline{pq}
,
\overline{pr}
,
\overline{qr}
, and
T_{r}W
.
In particular, if
\phi_{0}^{\vee}
is defined at
\ell
, then
\phi_{0}^{\vee}(\ell)
must be one of these 4.
This immediately implies that
\phi^{\vee}
cannot have rank 1.
To see this, suppose it has rank 1.
Consider the
{6\choose 2}=15
lines joining pairs of points of
Z
.
If
\phi_{0}^{\vee}
takes only 4 values on these lines (or may be undefined on one of them), we would be able to partition them into 4 collections of concurrent lines, such that the points of concurrency are collinear.
But it is easy to check that this is impossible for a star-free configuration
Z
.

We have now concluded that
\phi^{\vee}
has rank 2, and hence
\phi_{0}^{\vee}
is constant on its domain.
Suppose the constant value of
\phi_{0}^{\vee}
corresponds to the line
L\subset{\bf P}^{2}
.
Suppose
z_{1}\in Z
(resp.
z_{2}\in Z
) is such that the flat limit of
\phi({z_{1}}_{K})
is
p
(resp.
q
).
Consider the 8 lines
\overline{z_{i}z}
for
i=1,2
and
z\in Z\setminus\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
.
Since these lines are not all concurrent,
\phi_{0}^{\vee}
is defined for at least one of them, say
\ell=\overline{z_{1}z}
.
Then
\phi^{\vee}_{0}(\ell)
corresponds to the line
L
.
Since
\ell
contains
z_{1}
, the flat limit
L
of
\phi(\ell_{K})
contains the flat limit
p
of
\phi({z_{1}}_{K})
.

Now consider the
{4\choose 2}=6
lines joining pairs of points of
Z\setminus\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
.
Since the 6 lines are not all concurrent,
\phi_{0}^{\vee}
is defined on at least one of them, say
\ell
.
Then the flat limit of
\phi(\ell_{K})
is also
L
.
Since the flat limit of a point of
Z\setminus\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
is
r
, by semi-continuity, we have

\mult_{r}(L\cap W)\geq 2.

In other words,
L=T_{r}W
.
But then
T_{r}W
contains
p
.
∎

5.2.4
.

The same conclusion holds for a curvilinear
W
with cycle structure
2p+4r
such that
T_{r}W
does not contain
p
.
The same proof works with
p=q
.

5.2.3.
Applications

Let
X\subset{\bf P}^{3}
denote the cubic surface defined in homogeneous coordinates
\left(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)
by the equation

x_{3}f_{2}-f_{3}=0,

where
f_{2}=x_{0}x_{1}
and
f_{3}=x_{0}^{3}+x_{1}^{3}-x_{1}x_{2}^{2}
.
This is the unique cubic surface possessing a single
A_{5}
singularity, up to projective equivalence.
For the reader’s amusement, we include a real picture of
X
in
Figure 8
.

.

W\subset{\bf P}^{2}

and

.

W

p+q+4r
, where

,

,

T_{r}W

.

5.2.5
.

Let
S
be a smooth cubic surface without an Eckardt point.
Then
X
is not in the
\operatorname{PGL}_{4}
orbit closure of
S
.

Proof.

We know that
S
is isomorphic to
\Bl_{Z}{\bf P}^{2}
, where
Z\subset{\bf P}^{2}
is a star-free configuration.
The result now follows from
5.2.1
and
5.2.3
.
∎
