Fusion Reactivities with Drift bi-Maxwellian Ion Velocity Distributions Journal: Plasma Phys. Control. Fusion
Huasheng Xie, Muzhi Tan, Di Luo, Zhi Li, Bing Liu

Abstract
The calculation of fusion reactivity involves a complex six-dimensional integral that takes into account the fusion cross section and velocity distributions of two reactants. However, a more simplified one-dimensional integral form can be useful in certain cases, such as for studying fusion yield or diagnosing ion energy spectra. This simpler form has been derived in a few special cases, such as for a combination of two Maxwellian distributions, a beam-Maxwellian combination, and a beam-target combination, and can greatly reduce computational costs. In this study, it is shown that the reactivity for two drift bi-Maxwellian reactants with different drift velocities, temperatures, and anisotropies can also be reduced to a one-dimensional form, unifying existing derivations into a single expression. This result is used to investigate the potential enhancement of fusion reactivity due to the combination of beam and temperature anisotropies. For relevant parameters in fusion energy, the enhancement factor can be larger than 20%, which is particularly significant for proton-boron (p-B11) fusion, as this factor can have a significant impact on the Lawson fusion gain criteria.

1
Introduction

The counting of fusion reactions per unit volume and per unit time is
[
1
,
2
]
:

R_{12}=\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{1+\delta_{12}}\langle\sigma v\rangle,

(1)

where
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are the number densities of the two reactants, respectively. The term
\delta_{12}
is equal to 0 for different reactants and equal to 1 for the same reactants, in order to prevent double counting of the reaction.

Here,
\sigma=\sigma(E)
or
\sigma=\sigma(v)
represents the fusion cross section, with
E
being the energy in the center-of-mass frame, defined as

E=\frac{1}{2}m_{r}v^{2},~~v=|{\bm{v}}|=|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{2}|,~~m_{r}=\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},

(2)

where
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
denote the mass of the two reactants, and
m_{r}
represents the reduced mass of the system.
The fusion reactivity
\langle\sigma v\rangle
is calculated as the integral of the fusion cross section and the velocity distribution functions of the reactants

\langle\sigma v\rangle=\int\int d{\bm{v}}_{1}d{\bm{v}}_{2}\sigma(|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{2}|)|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{2}|f_{1}({\bm{v}}_{1})f_{2}({\bm{v}}_{2}),

(3)

where
f_{1},f_{2}
are normalized velocity distribution functions of the two ions, i.e.,
\int f_{j}({\bm{v}}_{j})d{\bm{v}}_{j}=1
with
j=1,2
, and
d{\bm{v}}_{j}=dv_{xj}dv_{yj}dv_{zj}
. Eq.(
3
) is crucial not only for calculating the fusion yield in laboratory or stellar plasmas
[
1
,
2
]
, but also for diagnostics
[
3
,
4
]
by providing information about the spectrum of the distributions
f_{1,2}
. However, calculating
\langle\sigma v\rangle
is challenging, as it requires a six-dimensional (6D) integral of the velocity, which is usually calculated numerically using high-dimensional integral methods such as Monte-Carlo methods
[
5
]
or orthogonal polynomials expansion methods
[
6
]
. If the integral can be reduced to a one-dimensional (1D) or two-dimensional (2D) form, the computation cost would be significantly reduced, and it would also provide analytical insights into the effect of the distributions on reactivity.

Fortunately, in Maxwellian-Maxwellian
[
1
,
2
,
7
]
or beam-target
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
distributions of
f_{1,2}
, the one-dimensional form of
\langle\sigma v\rangle
can be easily obtained. However, for more general distributions, a simple form of
\langle\sigma v\rangle
is often not available
[
11
]
. Non-Maxwellian fusion reactions are common
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Recently, there have been efforts to address this issue, such as Nath et al
[
15
]
reducing the integral to three dimensions for drift tri-Maxwellian reactants, Ou et al
[
16
]
studying some beam and target cases, and Kolmes et al
[
17
]
(as well as Li et al
[
18
]
) providing a one-dimensional form for bi-Maxwellian reactants
[
19
]
with the use of the error function. For plasma physics studies, one of the most widely used distributions is the drift bi-Maxwellian distribution, which is commonly used in studies of plasma waves and instabilities
[
20
,
21
]
and can also be reduced to the isotropic and thermal Maxwellian case. This distribution is also widely used to model the ion distribution functions in plasma and fusion experiments
[
22
,
23
]
, for fast ions generated through wave heating in the ion cyclotron range of frequencies or neutral beam injection.

In this work, we derive two- and one-dimensional integral forms of
\langle\sigma v\rangle
, which combine the existing results for Maxwellian (thermal), beam (drift), and bi-Maxwellian (anisotropic) distributions, using drift bi-Maxwellian distributions. This will provide valuable input for the modeling of fast ion fusion yields in numerical codes, such as NUBEAM
[
24
]
.

In Section
2
, we present the derivations. In Section
3
, we explore the potential enhancement of reactivity due to beam and temperature anisotropies and examine the modification to the Lawson fusion gain criteria. Finally, in Section
4
, we summarize our results.

2
Fusion Reactivity for Drift bi-Maxwellian Ion Velocity Distributions

Our goal is to derive the one-dimensional integral form of
\langle\sigma v\rangle
for the most general reactant distribution functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. In this section, only the main results will be summarized, with all the details of the derivations and proofs provided in the supplemental document.

2.1
Several existing results

In this subsection, we summarize several existing results for reference.

When considering two Maxwellian ions/reactants with distribution functions given by

f_{j}({\bm{v}})=\Big(\frac{m_{j}}{2\pi k_{B}T_{j}}\Big)^{3/2}\exp\Big(-\frac{m_{j}v^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{j}}\Big),

(4)

where
j=1,2
, and
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant, it is well-known
[
7
]
that the integral form of
\langle\sigma v\rangle
is

\langle\sigma v\rangle_{M}=\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi m_{r}}}\frac{1}{(k_{B}T_{r})^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(E)E\exp\Big(-\frac{E}{k_{B}T_{r}}\Big)dE,

(5)

where
T_{r}
is an effective temperature defined as

T_{r}=\frac{m_{1}T_{2}+m_{2}T_{1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}.

(6)

It is important to note that the temperatures of the two reactants
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are not necessarily equal, and increasing the temperature of the lighter reactant has a more significant impact on reactivity compared to increasing the temperature of the heavier reactant, which is due to the larger increase of the relative velocity between two reactants.

For two drift Maxwellian ions, the velocity distribution function is given by

f_{j}({\bm{v}})=\Big(\frac{m_{j}}{2\pi k_{B}T_{j}}\Big)^{3/2}\exp\Big[-\frac{m_{j}({\bm{v}}-{\bm{v}}_{dj})^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{j}}\Big].

(7)

After some derivations (similar to those in Sec.
2.2
), we find that

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle_{DM}

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}v_{tr}v_{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(v)v^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{v^{2}+v_{d}^{2}}{v_{tr}^{2}}\Big)\cdot

\displaystyle\sinh\Big(2\frac{vv_{d}}{v_{tr}^{2}}\Big)dv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi m_{r}k_{B}^{2}T_{r}T_{d}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(E)\sqrt{E}\cdot

\displaystyle\exp\Big(-\frac{E+E_{d}}{k_{B}T_{r}}\Big)\sinh\Big(\frac{2\sqrt{EE_{d}}}{k_{B}T_{r}}\Big)dE,

where
\sinh(x)=(e^{x}-e^{-x})/2\simeq x+x^{3}/6+\cdots
, the effective temperature
T_{r}
, thermal velocity
v_{tr}
, drift velocity
v_{d}
, and drift energy
E_{d}
are defined as follows

\displaystyle T_{r}=\frac{m_{1}T_{2}+m_{2}T_{1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},~~v_{tr}=\sqrt{\frac{2k_{B}T_{r}}{m_{r}}},

\displaystyle v_{d}=|{\bm{v}}_{d2}-{\bm{v}}_{d1}|,~~E_{d}\equiv k_{B}T_{d}=\frac{m_{r}v_{d}^{2}}{2}.

(9)

Note that the drift velocity
{\bm{v}}_{dj}
can be in arbitrary directions, i.e.,
{\bm{v}}_{d1}
and
{\bm{v}}_{d2}
are not required to be in the same direction. The result described by Eq.(
8
) is more general than those in other literature, such as Refs.
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
16
,
25
,
18
]
. For example, for
T_{1}\to 0
and
v_{d2}=0
, the result reduces to a beam-target case
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. If considering only the beam in the same direction, Eq.(
8
) can be reduced to the results in
[
3
,
16
,
18
]
. If
E_{d}=0
, Eq.(
8
) reduces to Eq.(
5
).

For two bi-Maxwellian ions, the velocity distribution function is given by

\displaystyle f_{j}({\bm{v}})=\frac{1}{T_{\parallel j}^{1/2}T_{\perp j}}\Big(\frac{m_{j}}{2\pi k_{B}}\Big)^{3/2}\exp\Big(-\frac{m_{j}v_{\perp}^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{\perp j}}-\frac{m_{j}v_{\parallel}^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{\parallel j}}\Big).

(10)

It has been shown in Refs.
[
17
,
18
]
that the reactivity can be reduced to

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle_{BM}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{2}{\pi m_{r}k_{B}^{3}}\Big)^{1/2}\frac{1}{{T_{\parallel r}^{1/2}T_{\perp r}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dE_{\perp}\int_{0}^{\infty}dE_{\parallel}\cdot

(11)

\displaystyle\sigma(E)\sqrt{\frac{E}{E_{\parallel}}}\exp\Big[-\frac{E_{\parallel}}{{k_{B}}T_{\parallel r}}-\frac{E_{\perp}}{{k_{B}}T_{\perp r}}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{m_{r}k_{B}^{2}T_{\perp r}(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dE\sigma(E){\sqrt{E}}\cdot

\displaystyle\exp\Big(-\frac{E}{k_{B}T_{\perp r}}\Big){\rm erf}\Big[\sqrt{\frac{E(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}{k_{B}T_{\perp r}T_{\parallel r}}}\Big],

where
{\rm erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}dt\simeq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}(x-\frac{x^{3}}{3}+\cdots)
is the error function, and the effective parallel and perpendicular temperatures and energies are defined as

\displaystyle T_{\parallel r}=\frac{m_{1}T_{\parallel 2}+m_{2}T_{\parallel 1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},~~T_{\perp r}=\frac{m_{1}T_{\perp 2}+m_{2}T_{\perp 1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},

\displaystyle E=E_{\parallel}+E_{\perp},~~E_{\parallel}=\frac{1}{2}m_{r}v_{\parallel}^{2},~~E_{\perp}=\frac{1}{2}m_{r}v_{\perp}^{2}.

(12)

To calculate
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{BM}
for
T_{\perp r}<T_{\parallel r}
, we can use the relation
{\rm erf}(ix)=i\cdot{\rm erfi}(x)
to keep the argument of the error function a real number, with
{\rm erfi}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{t^{2}}dt
. For
T_{\perp r}=T_{\parallel r}
, Eq.(
11
) reduces to Eq.(
5
). It should be noted that as
x\to+\infty
,
{\rm erf}(x)\simeq 1-\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{2x^{3}}+\frac{3}{4x^{5}}+\cdots)
and
{\rm erf}(-x)=-{\rm erf}(x)
.

In equations (
5
), (
8
), and (
11
), the subscripts ’M’, ’DM’, and ’BM’ respectively denote Maxwellian, drift Maxwellian, and bi-Maxwellian distributions.

2.2
Drift bi-Maxwellian ions

In principle, if we were to use drift tri-Maxwellian distribution functions, the results for
\langle\sigma v\rangle
would contain all of the results from Subsection
2.1
. However, only a three-dimensional integral form has been obtained
[
15
]
, and it is not straightforward to obtain a one-dimensional form.

As a result, in this analysis, we will limit ourselves to two drift bi-Maxwellian ions. The distribution functions are given by

\displaystyle f_{j}({\bm{v}_{j}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{\parallel j}^{1/2}T_{\perp j}}\Big(\frac{m_{j}}{2\pi k_{B}}\Big)^{3/2}\cdot

(13)

\displaystyle\exp\Big[-\frac{m_{j}v_{\perp j}^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{\perp j}}-\frac{m_{j}(v_{\parallel j}-v_{dj})^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{\parallel j}}\Big],

with
\int f_{j}({\bm{v}_{j}})d{\bm{v}}_{j}=1
,
v_{\perp j}^{2}=v_{xj}^{2}+v_{yj}^{2}
, and
v_{\parallel j}=v_{zj}
. Here, unlike in Eq.(
7
), we limit the drift velocities
{\bm{v}}_{dj}=v_{djz}{\hat{\bm{z}}}
to be in only the parallel direction, which is typically parallel to the magnetic field
{\bm{B}}=B{\hat{\bm{z}}}
.

Using the following transformation

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}={\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{2},~~m_{r}\equiv\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},~~E=\frac{1}{2}m_{r}v^{2},

\displaystyle T_{\parallel r}=\frac{m_{1}T_{\parallel 2}+m_{2}T_{\parallel 1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},~~T_{\perp r}=\frac{m_{1}T_{\perp 2}+m_{2}T_{\perp 1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}},

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}_{c}=\frac{m_{2}T_{\perp 1}{\bm{v}}_{\perp 2}+m_{1}T_{\perp 2}{\bm{v}}_{\perp 1}}{{m_{1}T_{\perp 2}}+m_{2}T_{\perp 1}}+

\displaystyle\frac{m_{2}T_{\parallel 1}({\bm{v}}_{\parallel 2}-{\bm{v}}_{d2})+m_{1}T_{\parallel 2}({\bm{v}}_{\parallel 1}-{\bm{v}}_{d1})}{{m_{1}T_{\parallel 2}}+m_{2}T_{\parallel 1}},

(14)

we have the Jacobian
J=|d{\bm{v}}_{1}d{\bm{v}}_{2}/d{\bm{v}}_{c}d{\bm{v}}|=1
, and

\displaystyle\frac{m_{1}(v_{\parallel 1}-v_{d1})^{2}}{T_{\parallel 1}}+\frac{m_{2}(v_{\parallel 2}-v_{d2})^{2}}{T_{\parallel 2}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{m_{1}}{T_{\parallel 1}}+\frac{m_{2}}{T_{\parallel 2}}\Big){\bm{v}}_{c\parallel}^{2}+\frac{m_{r}}{T_{\parallel r}}v_{d}^{2}+\frac{m_{r}}{T_{\parallel r}}{\bm{v}}_{\parallel}^{2}+2\frac{m_{r}}{T_{\parallel r}}{\bm{v}}_{\parallel}v_{d}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle_{DBM}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int d{\bm{v}}_{1}d{\bm{v}}_{2}\sigma(|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{2}|)|{\bm{v}}_{1}-{\bm{v}}_{2}|f_{1}({\bm{v}}_{1})f_{2}({\bm{v}}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{m_{r}}{2\pi k_{B}}\Big)^{3/2}\frac{1}{T_{\parallel r}^{1/2}T_{\perp r}}\int d{\bm{v}}\sigma(v)v\cdot

\displaystyle\exp\Big[-\frac{m_{r}v_{\perp}^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{\perp r}}-\frac{m_{r}(v_{\parallel}+v_{d})^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{\parallel r}}\Big].

Therefore, the six-dimensional integral is reduced to a three-dimensional one. A similar treatment can be found in reference
[
15
]
.

We then use the following definitions

\displaystyle E=E_{\parallel}+E_{\perp},~~E_{\parallel}=\frac{1}{2}m_{r}v_{\parallel}^{2},~~E_{\perp}=\frac{1}{2}m_{r}v_{\perp}^{2},

\displaystyle v_{d}=v_{d2}-v_{d1},~~E_{d}=k_{B}T_{d}=\frac{m_{r}v_{d}^{2}}{2},

\displaystyle v_{x}=\sqrt{2E_{\perp}/m_{r}}\cos\phi,~~v_{y}=\sqrt{2E_{\perp}/m_{r}}\sin\phi,

\displaystyle v_{z}=\pm\sqrt{2E_{\parallel}/m_{r}},

where
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
,
E_{\perp}\in[0,\infty)
and
E_{\parallel}\in[0,\infty)
. This results in

\displaystyle d{\bm{v}}=\frac{1}{m_{r}^{3/2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2E_{\parallel}}}dE_{\parallel}dE_{\perp}d\phi.

Thus, equation (
15
) can be rewritten as a two-dimensional integral

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle_{DBM}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi m_{r}k_{B}^{3}}\Big)^{1/2}}\frac{1}{T_{\parallel r}^{1/2}T_{\perp r}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}dE_{\parallel}dE_{\perp}\sigma(E)\cdot

(16)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{E}{E_{\parallel}}}\exp\Big[-\frac{E_{\perp}}{k_{B}T_{\perp r}}-\frac{(E_{\parallel}+E_{d})}{k_{B}T_{\parallel r}}\Big]\cdot

\displaystyle\Big[\exp\Big(-2\frac{\sqrt{E_{\parallel}E_{d}}}{k_{B}T_{\parallel r}}\Big)+\exp\Big(2\frac{\sqrt{E_{\parallel}E_{d}}}{k_{B}T_{\parallel r}}\Big)\Big].

We can proceed further with another transformation, given by

\displaystyle E=E_{\parallel}+E_{\perp},~~t^{2}=\frac{E_{\parallel}(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}{k_{B}T_{\perp r}T_{\parallel r}},

or,

\displaystyle E_{\parallel}=t^{2}\frac{k_{B}T_{\perp r}T_{\parallel r}}{{(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}},~~E_{\perp}=E-t^{2}\frac{k_{B}T_{\perp r}T_{\parallel r}}{{(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}},

\displaystyle dE_{\parallel}dE_{\perp}=2t\frac{k_{B}T_{\perp r}T_{\parallel r}}{{(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}}dEdt.

With this, we can finally arrive at a one-dimensional integral form of fusion reactivity for drift bi-Maxwellian reactants, as follows

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle_{DBM}=

\displaystyle\exp\Big[\frac{E_{d}}{k_{B}(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}\Big]\sqrt{\frac{1}{2m_{r}k_{B}^{2}T_{\perp r}(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}}\cdot

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dE\sigma(E)\sqrt{E}\exp\Big(-\frac{E}{k_{B}T_{\perp r}}\Big)\cdot

\displaystyle\Big[{\rm erf}\Big(\sqrt{E\frac{(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}{k_{B}T_{\parallel r}T_{\perp r}}}+\sqrt{\frac{T_{\perp r}E_{d}}{k_{B}T_{\parallel r}(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}}\Big)+

\displaystyle{\rm erf}\Big(\sqrt{E\frac{(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}{k_{B}T_{\parallel r}T_{\perp r}}}-\sqrt{\frac{T_{\perp r}E_{d}}{k_{B}T_{\parallel r}(T_{\perp r}-T_{\parallel r})}}\Big)\Big].

(17)

The above result holds for both
T_{\perp r}>T_{\parallel r}
and
T_{\perp r}<T_{\parallel r}
, if the imaginary error function
{\rm erf}(ix)=i\cdot{\rm erfi}(x)
is used. The subscript ‘DBM’ in the result stands for drift bi-Maxwellian.

Both equations (
2.2
) and (
16
) can be used for practical numerical calculations, as the computation costs for the 1D and 2D integrals are not excessive. Equation (
2.2
) can be easily reduced to equation (
11
) when
E_{d}=0
, and can be reduced to equation (
8
) when
T_{\parallel r}=T_{\perp r}
(as detailed in the supplemental document). Fast approximate formulations can be used to calculate the error functions
{\rm erf}(x)
and
{\rm erfi}(x)
. Care should be taken when the two reactants are the same, for example, in D-D (Deuterium-Deuterium) fusion, the drift
E_{d}
should be treated as two groups of reactants (representing the two groups of D ions colliding with each other) to avoid double-counting. In practical situations, the velocity distribution of one species can be constructed using multiple drift bi-Maxwellian distributions, and the calculation of the fusion reactivity would still be straightforward using the above formulations.

2.3
Analytical insights

The one-dimensional integral form of the fusion reactivity,
\langle\sigma v\rangle
, provides analytical insight into the effect of the reactants velocity distribution functions,
f_{1,2}
, on the fusion reactivity. To this end, we can define a kernel distribution function,
K(E)
, as follows

\displaystyle\langle\sigma v\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{r}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(E)K(E)dE.

(18)

K(E)
can be easily obtained from Eqs. (
5
), (
8
), (
11
) and (
2.2
) for Maxwellian, drift Maxwellian, bi-Maxwellian, and drift bi-Maxwellian distributions, respectively. For example, for a Maxwellian distribution

\displaystyle K_{M}(E)=\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}}\frac{1}{(k_{B}T_{r})^{3/2}}E\exp\Big(-\frac{E}{k_{B}T_{r}}\Big).

(19)

The fusion reactivity,
\langle\sigma v\rangle
, is determined by the overlap between the kernel function,
K(E)
, and the peak regions of the cross section,
\sigma(E)
. In this sense,
K(E)
can be viewed as a weighted function of the cross section,
\sigma(E)
. Typical kernel functions,
K(E)
, will be displayed in Sec.
3
(Fig.
8
).

) for different temperatures. The shading in the figure indicates regions where

. For large beam drift, the center of mass energy
E
is larger than the peak cross section energy, which decreases the fusion reactivity.

3.2
Potential for Increased Reactivity

Although the physics of a possible reactivity enhancement due to the synergy of beam effect and temperature anisotropies can be studied via the 3D integral formulation in Ref.
[
15
]
, the 2D formulation given by Eq. (
16
) and the 1D formulation given by Eq. (
2.2
) are much simpler and more efficient for calculations, and can also be used to evaluate the impact of each term.

To investigate the possibility of a reactivity enhancement, we fix some parameters. For
E_{d}=0
, we keep the total kinetic energy constant, resulting in the same
T_{r}
. However, for
E_{d}\neq 0
, the situation is different. The average kinetic energy is calculated from the distribution function as
E_{k}=\frac{1}{2}m\int v^{2}f({\bm{v}})d{\bm{v}}
. For the drift bi-Maxwellian as described in Eq.(
10
), the average kinetic energy is given by

\displaystyle E_{kj}=\frac{k_{B}T_{\parallel j}+2k_{B}T_{\perp j}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}m_{j}v_{dj}^{2},

(20)

where
E_{kj}
is the sum of the thermal energy,
E_{th,j}=\frac{k_{B}T_{\parallel j}+2k_{B}T_{\perp j}}{2}
, and the drift energy,
E_{dj}=\frac{1}{2}m_{j}v_{dj}^{2}
. Equation (
20
) was derived by evaluating the velocity space integrals using the formulas
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v^{2}\exp[-(v-v_{d})^{2}/a^{2}]dv=\sqrt{\pi}a(2v_{d}^{2}+a^{2})/2
and
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp[-(v-v_{d})^{2}/a^{2}]dv=\sqrt{\pi}a
.

It is assumed that the masses satisfy
m_{1}\leq m_{2}
, meaning the first reactant is lighter than the second. The fusion reactivity
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{DBM}
depends only on
m_{r}
,
T_{\perp r}
,
T_{\parallel r}
, and
E_{d}
. To minimize the total kinetic energy
E_{k}
, it is optimal to choose the first reactant as the one that is both drift and thermal, i.e.,
v_{d2}\ll v_{d1}
and
T_{2}\ll T_{1}
.

To study the reactivity enhancement, it is important to keep the total kinetic energy,
n_{1}E_{k1}+n_{2}E_{k2}
, constant. In this study, to simplify the discussion, we keep the average effective kinetic energy constant, defined as
E_{kr}=(k_{B}T_{\parallel r}+2k_{B}T_{\perp r})/2+E_{d}
. To facilitate the analysis, we introduce new variables

\displaystyle T_{r}=\frac{(2T_{\perp r}+T_{\parallel r})}{3},~R_{t}=\frac{T_{\perp r}}{T_{\parallel r}},~R_{d}=\frac{E_{d}}{k_{B}T_{r}}.

(21)

The thermal energy is kept constant for a fixed
T_{r}
, and the average effective kinetic energy is kept constant for a fixed
E_{kr}
. Hence, we study the relation between the fusion reactivity
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{DBM}
and
E_{kr}
,
R_{t}
, and
R_{d}
. We also define the fusion reactivity enhancement factor as

\displaystyle f_{\langle\sigma v\rangle}\equiv\frac{{\langle\sigma v\rangle}}{{\langle\sigma v\rangle}_{R_{t}=1,R_{d}=0}},

(22)

where
E_{kr}
is kept constant. Finally, we introduce a new temperature
T_{r}^{*}\equiv 2E_{kr}/(3k_{B})
, which reduces to
T_{r}^{*}=T_{r}
for
E_{d}=0
.

Figure
4
shows the fusion reactivity enhancement factors for different temperatures with only temperature anisotropy considered. It can be seen that for all four of these fusion reactions, the enhancements only occur at low temperatures, such as the critical temperatures for D-T, D-D, D-He3, and p-B11, which are around 15 keV, 50 keV, 60 keV, and 140 keV, respectively. Similar conclusions have been drawn in previous studies, as reported in Refs.
[
19
,
15
,
17
,
18
]
. The reason for this can be found by examining the cross section plot (Fig.
1
) and the kernel function
K(E)
. The enhancements occur when the effects of the cross section peak are maximized. For the D-D fusion reactivity, the anisotropic effects are weak and contribute less than 5% in most ranges. The effects are slightly larger for D-He3 compared to D-D. For D-T and p-B11, changes greater than 5% can easily be achieved. Since the enhancements are small in most cases, the results reported in Ref.
[
29
]
are understandable, which concluded that the fusion reactivity in TFTR affected by the distortion of bulk ions from Maxwellians is small.

Figures
5
,
6
, and
7
show the fusion reactivity enhancement factors with both temperature anisotropy and parallel drift, for
R_{d}=0.5,~1,~3
, respectively. It can be observed that the effects on the D-D and D-He3 fusion reactions are small. The p-B11 result is calculated using the cross-section data from Ref.
[
7
]
. In comparison to the case without drift, the case with drift can also increase the reactivity for
T_{\perp r}\gtrsim T_{\parallel r}
with a large
T_{r}^{*}
. Among all cases, in the range of
R_{t}\lesssim 1
and low
T_{r}^{*}
, the fusion reactivity can be enhanced, even by over 100%. These situations commonly occur in modern tokamak experiments, such as TFTR, by using neutral beam injection (NBI) or the ion cyclotron range of frequencies (ICRF)
[
30
]
.

We are particularly interested in the p-B11 fusion, which is typically considered difficult as a fusion energy source
[
7
]
. In Fig.
7
, at
T_{r}^{*}\simeq 250
keV and
T_{\perp r}>0.75T_{\parallel r}
, the enhancement can reach around
20\%
. Although this enhancement factor is not large, its effects on the fusion energy gain factor could be significant, as demonstrated in subsection
3.3
. Figure
8
shows the kernel functions
K(E)
and the D-T fusion cross-section
\sigma(E)
for typical different values of
R_{t}
and
R_{d}
. It is readily apparent that the overlap of the kernel function
K(E)
with the peak of the cross-section
\sigma(E)
is largest for the case
(R_{t}=3,R_{d}=3)
, indicating that the fusion reactivity for this case should be the largest among the six cases in the figure. By comparing the results in Figs.
4
and
7
, we see that this is indeed the case.

3.3
Impact on the Lawson criteria

The fusion reactivity enhancement factors are variable for different parameters. In order to show the impact on the Lawson fusion condition
[
31
,
32
]
, we choose a fixed
f_{\langle\sigma v\rangle}
. The fusion gain factor
Q
is defined as
Q\equiv(P_{out}-P_{in})/P_{in}
, where
P_{in}
is the input power and
P_{out}
is the output power. A scientific breakeven occurs when
Q=1
and ignition occurs when
Q=\infty
. For a steady fusion reactor, we can calculate
Q
using the following equation

\displaystyle Q=\frac{P_{fus}}{E_{th}/\tau_{E}-f_{ion}P_{fus}+P_{rad}},

(23)

where
\tau_{E}
is the energy confinement time, and the thermal energy and fusion power are given by

\displaystyle E_{th}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}k_{B}\sum_{j}n_{j}T_{j}=\frac{3}{2}k_{B}(n_{e}T_{e}+n_{i}T_{i}),

(24)

\displaystyle P_{fus}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\delta_{12}}n_{1}n_{2}\langle\sigma v\rangle Y.

(25)

Here,
Y
is the energy release of each fusion reaction, and
Y_{+}
is the energy to charged products, with
f_{ion}=Y_{+}/Y
. The densities of the two ions,
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
, are given as
n_{1}=x_{1}n_{i}
and
n_{2}=x_{2}n_{i}
, respectively, where
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are the ion concentrations and
n_{i}=n_{1}+n_{2}
. If the two ions are not the same, then
x_{1}=x
and
x_{2}=1-x
. The quasi-neutrality condition requires the electron density,
n_{e}
, to be equal to
n_{e}=Z_{1}n_{1}+Z_{2}n_{2}=Z_{i}n_{i}
, where
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are the charge numbers of the two ions. The effective charge,
Z_{eff}
, is given by
Z_{eff}=[x_{1}Z_{1}^{2}+x_{2}Z_{2}^{2}]/[x_{1}Z_{1}+x_{2}Z_{2}]
.

We consider only the weak relativistic bremsstrahlung radiation losses
P_{rad}=P_{brem}
, with

\displaystyle P_{brem}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{B}n_{e}^{2}\sqrt{k_{B}T_{e}}Z_{eff}g_{eff}~{\rm[MW\cdot m^{-3}]},

(26)

\displaystyle g_{eff}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[1+0.7936\frac{k_{B}T_{e}}{m_{e}c^{2}}+1.874\Big(\frac{k_{B}T_{e}}{m_{e}c^{2}}\Big)^{2}\Big]

(27)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{Z_{eff}}\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{k_{B}T_{e}}{m_{e}c^{2}},

in which
C_{B}=5.34\times 10^{-37}
. The unit for
k_{B}T_{e}
is keV, for density
n_{e}
is
{\rm m}^{-3}
.

We can finally obtain the Lawson criteria

\displaystyle n_{e}\tau_{E}=\frac{\frac{3}{2}k_{B}(T_{e}+T_{i}/Z_{i})}{\frac{(1/Q+f_{ion})}{1+\delta_{12}}\frac{x_{1}x_{2}}{Z_{i}^{2}}\langle\sigma v\rangle Y-C_{B}\sqrt{k_{B}T_{e}}Z_{eff}g_{eff}}.

(28)

Here, we consider
\langle\sigma v\rangle=f_{\langle\sigma v\rangle}\cdot\langle\sigma v\rangle_{M}
, and we would like to see how much the enhancement factor
f_{\langle\sigma v\rangle}
can affect the Lawson criteria. Figure

f_{\langle\sigma v\rangle}=0.8,~1.0,~1.2,~2.0
on the Lawson criteria of D-T, D-D, D-He3, p-B11, and catalyzed D-D fusions, for both
Q=1
and
Q=\infty

f_{\langle\sigma v\rangle}
affects the Lawson criteria for all these fusion reactions, with the most significant effect seen for the p-B11 fusion. For the catalyzed D-D fusion, we use the same fusion reactivity as D-D fusion, but with a larger yield energy of
Y=21.6
MeV instead of
Y=3.66

Q\geq 1

4
Summary and Conclusion

The formulation of the fusion reactivity for two drift bi-Maxwellian reactants has been derived into both two- and one-dimensional integral forms. This formulation combines previous results in the literature, including Maxwellian-Maxwellian, beam-target, and bi-Maxwellian reactants. The results show that enhancement factors in fusion reactivity due to temperature anisotropies and parallel drift can be greater than 20% at temperatures relevant to fusion energy. This enhancement is particularly important for the p-B11 fusion reaction. To maximize reactivity enhancement, the distribution function should remain close to the peak region of the cross section, which can be understood from the kernel function
K(E)
in the one-dimensional fusion reactivity integral formulation. This means that the reactivity can only be enhanced within specific ranges. For all four fusion reactions (D-T, D-D, D-He3, and p-B11), the ranges that benefit the most from reactivity enhancement without considering drift are
T<T_{c}
, where
T_{c}
represents critical temperatures of
T_{c}^{DT}\simeq 15
keV,
T_{c}^{DD}\simeq 50
keV,
T_{c}^{DHe3}\simeq 60
keV, and
T_{c}^{pB11}\simeq 140
keV. When drift is taken into account, enhancement also occurs when
T>T_{c}
if
T_{\perp r}\gtrsim T_{\parallel r}
. Results show that even a modest increase in fusion reactivity can lower the Lawson criteria for p-B11 fusion dramatically. Thus, the impact of the distribution function on the fusion reactivity is crucial in the study of p-B11 fusion. Previous studies
[
36
,
37
]
of p-B11 fusion with beam fusion should be carefully examined, as they treated drift energy oversimplified. A more practical study would keep the total energy (
n_{1}E_{k1}+n_{2}E_{k2}
) constant, which could provide more insight into the best choice for
T_{\parallel,\perp j}
and
v_{dj}
, and lead to further results based on those shown in section
3
. It should be noted that the beam and anisotropy can also affect the confinement time
\tau_{E}
, which has not been discussed here.

The present work could also be useful in improving the ion energy spectrum diagnostic method, as discussed in Refs.
[
3
,
4
]
. Furthermore, it is interesting to consider if there are other integral forms of the fusion reactivity that can be obtained in two- or one-dimensional, such as suprathermal distributions
[
38
,
39
]
, fast ion tails
[
40
,
41
]
, and drift bi-Maxwellian distributions with perpendicular drift and ring drift
[
21
]
. These could be topics for future research.
