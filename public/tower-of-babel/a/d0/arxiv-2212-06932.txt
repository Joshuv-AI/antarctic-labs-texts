Multiplication Kernels for the Analytic Langlands Program in Genus Zero
Daniil Klyuev, Sanjay Raman

Abstract
We provide an explicit proof of a recent result of Gaiotto [ 5 ] which gives an explicit formula for a so-called ‘‘multiplication kernel’’ K 3 ​ ( x , y , z , t ) K_{3}(x,y,z;t) intertwining the action of Hecke operators and Gaudin operators in three sets of variables. This function K 3 K_{3} arises naturally in the context of the analytic formulation of the geometric Langlands program in the genus-zero case [ 2 , 3 , 4 ] . We also discuss how the kernel K 3 K_{3} relates to other objects typically considered in the analytic Langlands program.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Quantum Hitchin Hamiltonians and the Gaudin System

We will now review the relevant ingredients from the analytic Langlands program. Our discussion follows the foundational work in
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

The starting point consists of a reductive algebraic group
G
and an smooth irreducible projective curve
X
over a local field
F
with a specified zero-dimensional subvariety
S\subset X
. Let
\mathrm{Bun}_{G}(X,S)
be the moduli stack of
G
-bundles over
X
with a specified reduction to a Borel subgroup
B\subset G
at the subvariety
S\subset X
, and let
\operatorname{Bun}_{G}^{\circ}(X,S)
be the substack of such
stable

G
-bundles (defined precisely in Definition 2.1 of
[
3
]
).

If
\left\lvert S\right\rvert
is sufficiently large, then
\operatorname{Bun}_{G}^{\circ}(X,S)
is open and dense in
\mathrm{Bun}_{G}(X,S)
. If we take
G
to be simple and simply-connected, and if
X
is a curve of genus
g
, then
\operatorname{Bun}_{G}^{\circ}(X,S)
can be viewed as a smooth quasiprojective variety of dimension
3g-3+\left\lvert S\right\rvert

[
3
]
. We then define
V_{G}(X,S)
to be the space of smooth, compactly supported sections of the line bundle of half-densities
\Omega^{1/2}_{\mathrm{Bun}}
over
\mathrm{Bun}^{\circ}_{G}(X,S)
.
V_{G}(X,S)
admits an inner product:

\left\langle v,w\right\rangle:=\int_{\mathrm{Bun}_{G}^{\circ}(X,S)}v\cdot\overline{w}.

We then define
\mathcal{H}_{G}(X,S)
to be the Hilbert space completion of
V_{G}(X,S)
with respect to
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
.

Now, take
F=\mathbb{C}
. To make contact with the geometric Langlands program, we wish to analyze the joint spectrum of a commutative algebra
\mathcal{A}
of
global
differential operators acting on
\Omega^{1/2}_{\mathrm{Bun}}
. Specifically, take
K_{\mathrm{Bun}}
to be the canonical line bundle on
\mathrm{Bun}^{\circ}_{G}(X,S)
. Now, it is known that
K_{\mathrm{Bun}}
has a unique square root
K^{1/2}_{\mathrm{Bun}}
up to isomorphism. The commutative algebra
D_{G}
of
global
holomorphic differential operators on
K^{1/2}_{\mathrm{Bun}}
is generated by the
quantum Hitchin Hamiltonians
, and their properties were first described in
[
10
]
. We may just as well consider the antiholomorphic operators
\overline{D_{G}}
acting on the conjugate
\overline{K}^{1/2}_{\mathrm{Bun}}
of
K^{1/2}_{\mathrm{Bun}}
. Following
[
2
]
, we define the commutative algebra
\mathcal{A}
as follows:

\mathcal{A}=D_{G}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\overline{D_{G}}.

To understand the joint spectrum of
\mathcal{A}
, we wish to study the action of holomorphic differential operators on half-densities in
\Omega^{1/2}_{\mathrm{Bun}}=K^{1/2}_{\mathrm{Bun}}\otimes\overline{K}^{1/2}_{\mathrm{Bun}}
.
To establish (over
F=\mathbb{C}
) the analytic Langlands correspondence, it is thus essential to understand the joint spectra of the quantum Hitchin Hamiltonians. For general
G
and
X
, this is quite a difficult problem; however, some progress has been made for the simplest non-abelian
G
. In
[
3
,
5
]
, the specific case of
G=PGL_{2}
over
X=\mathbb{P}^{1}
is considered, and this is the case we shall focus on as well. In genus zero, we require
\left\lvert S\right\rvert\geq 3
parabolic points for the moduli stack
\mathrm{Bun}_{G}^{\circ}(X,S)
of stable
G
-bundles to be open and dense in
\mathrm{Bun}_{G}(X,S)
, and for
\left\lvert S\right\rvert=3
, this moduli stack consists of a single point. Thus, we henceforth take

S=\{t_{0},\cdots,t_{m+1}\}

where
m\geq 2
. Specifying a Borel reduction of
G
at
S
then amounts to giving each point
t_{i}
a
parabolic structure
, which is simply a choice of direction
y_{i}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. In fact,
\mathrm{Bun}_{G}^{\circ}(X,S)
is parametrized precisely by the
y_{i}
. We have the following birational equivalence
[
3
]
:

\mathrm{Bun}_{G}^{\circ}(X,S)\ \mathop{\longrightarrow}^{\sim}\ \mathbb{P}^{m-1}.

The fact that
\mathrm{Bun}_{G}^{\circ}(X,S)
has dimension
(m-1)
can intuitively be seen by the fact that we may translate by
PGL_{2}
to fix three of the parabolic structures at the marked points.

Given a choice of
y_{i}
, the (holomorphic) quantum Hitchin Hamiltonians reduce to the so-called
Gaudin operators

[
10
]
:

G_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{t_{j}-t_{i}}\left[-(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}+(y_{i}-y_{j})(\partial_{i}-\partial_{j})+\frac{1}{2}\right],

where
\partial_{i}\equiv\partial/\partial y_{i}
. It is easy to verify that the algebra of Gaudin operators is indeed commutative:
[G_{i},G_{j}]=0
. Our problem is then to understand the joint spectrum of the Gaudin system.

2.2
Hecke Operators

The original number-theoretic Langlands program for curves
X
over finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{q}
studies the joint spectrum of commuting Hecke operators. The notion of Hecke operators have a corresponding analog in the analytic Langlands program, where
X
is now a curve over some local field
F
. When
F=\mathbb{C}
or
F=\mathbb{R}
, the Hecke operators strongly commute with the quantum Hitchin Hamiltonians, so the joint spectrum of the Hecke operators can also be seen via Langlands duality to correspond to the
\mathchoice{\hphantom{{}^{{{L}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.33472pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.38194pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}G^{{\kern-9.67181pt{L}\kern 6.22598pt}}_{{\kern-17.0082pt\mathchoice{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.33472pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.38194pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 6.22598pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{L}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.33472pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.38194pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}G^{{\kern-9.67181pt{L}\kern 6.22598pt}}_{{\kern-17.0082pt\mathchoice{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.33472pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.38194pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 6.22598pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{L}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.33472pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.38194pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}G^{{\kern-6.34567pt{L}\kern 3.85262pt}}_{{\kern-11.58594pt\mathchoice{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.33472pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.38194pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 3.85262pt}}}{\hphantom{{}^{{{L}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.33472pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.38194pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}}G^{{\kern-5.24492pt{L}\kern 2.75186pt}}_{{\kern-10.48518pt\mathchoice{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[5.48615pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[3.33472pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.38194pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\kern 2.75186pt}}}
-opers with real monodromy. Understanding the spectra of the Hecke operators therefore gives us a grip on the geometric Langlands program for non-archimedean local fields
F
, where we no longer have access to the quantum Hitchin Hamiltonians.

A full definition of Hecke operators is given in
[
4
]
, but we are only concerned with the case where
X=\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
G=PGL_{2}
, so we will restrict our attention to the case of rank-two vector bundles over
X
with marked points
t_{0},\cdots,t_{m+1}
with parabolic structures
y_{0},\cdots,y_{m+1}
. The Hecke operators form a commuting set of compact operators
H_{t}
for each point
t\in X
. Taking as usual
t_{m+1}=\infty
and further setting
y_{m+1}=\infty
, there is an explicit formula for the action of the Hecke operators (Proposition 3.9 of
[
3
]
):

H_{t}\psi(y_{0},\cdots,y_{m})=\left\|\prod_{i=0}^{m}(t-t_{i})\right\|^{1/2}\int_{F}\frac{\left\|ds\right\|}{\prod_{i=0}^{m}\left\|s-y_{i}\right\|}\psi\left(\frac{t_{0}-t}{s-y_{0}},\cdots,\frac{t_{m}-t}{s-y_{m}}\right).

Over
F=\mathbb{C}
, the Hecke operators strongly commute with the Gaudin system, and the joint spectrum of this commutative algebra of operators is simple
[
3
]
.

2.3
The Gaiotto Kernel

It was Gaiotto and Witten
[
6
]
who first considered the symmetric
multiplication kernel

K_{3}(x,y,z;t)
in three sets of variables intertwining the action of the Gaudin and Hecke operators over
\mathbb{C}
. More precisely, if we let
x_{0},\cdots,x_{m},y_{0},\cdots,y_{m},z_{0},\cdots,z_{m}
be three copies of variables, they were interested in a function
K_{3}(x,y,z;t)
symmetric in
x,y,z
satisfying

G_{i}^{x}K_{3}(x,y,z;t)=G_{i}^{y}K_{3}(x,y,z;t)=G_{i}^{z}K_{3}(x,y,z;t);

H_{t^{\prime}}^{x}K_{3}(x,y,z;t)=H_{t^{\prime}}^{y}K_{3}(x,y,z;t)=H_{t^{\prime}}^{z}K_{3}(x,y,z;t).

Here
G_{i}^{u}
and
H_{t^{\prime}}^{u}
denote the Gaudin and Hecke operators, respectively, with respect to the variables
u_{0},\cdots,u_{m}
for
u=x,y,z
.

The object
K_{3}
is of interest to us for several reasons. First of all, it furnishes a ‘‘multiplication kernel’’ in the language of
[
9
]
. That is, it defines a commutative associative product
*
on
\mathcal{H}
. Specifically, if we define

(f*g)(x)=\int_{\mathrm{Bun}_{G}^{\circ}(X,S)\times\mathrm{Bun}_{G}^{\circ}(X,S)}K_{3}(x,y,z)f(y)g(z)\,\left\|dy\right\|\left\|dz\right\|,

then the product
*
is commutative (by virtue of the symmetry of
K_{3}
in
x,y,z
) and associative (as shown in Section 4 of
[
5
]
).

Further, since
K_{3}
intertwines the action of the Gaudin and Hecke operators, it may be shown that integral
Gaiotto operators

K_{x}
defined by integrating over the kernel
K_{3}
(for arbitrary
x
) commute with the Gaudin and Hecke operators. Explicitly, for
f\in\mathcal{H}
, we find

(K_{x}\circ G_{i})f=(G_{i}\circ K_{x})f;

(K_{x}\circ H_{t})f=(H_{t}\circ K_{x})f;

Thus, the Gaiotto operators
K_{x}
diagonalize in the joint eigenbasis of the Gaudin operators. An explicit computation of
K_{3}
would thus yield information about the joint spectrum of the Gaudin and Hecke operators.

In his recent work
[
5
]
, Gaiotto produced a simple explicit expression for the kernel
K_{3}
over
F=\mathbb{C}
. He defined the matrix
A_{ij}
with entries as follows:

A_{ij}=\frac{(x_{i}-x_{j})(y_{i}-y_{j})(z_{i}-z_{j})}{t_{i}-t_{j}},\ 0\leq i\neq j\leq m

and
A_{ii}=0
. Using arguments from conformal field theory, he gave the following formula, stated above in Theorem
1.1
:

K_{3}(x,y,z;t)=\frac{1}{\left\lvert\det A\right\rvert}.

That the Gaiotto operators commute with the Hecke operators follows from the fact that they commute with the Gaudin operators, since the joint spectrum is simple
[
3
]
. Gaiotto provided an explicit proof of his formula for four marked points
t_{i}
and a numerical verification for five points. In Section
3
, we will provide a complete proof of Theorem
1.1
. We will also provide a direct proof of the analogous intertwining formula for Hecke operators in a following paper
[
8
]
.

3
Proof of Gaiotto’s Formula

3.1
Outline of Proof

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.1
. Inspired by the physical argument, we write
K_{3}
as a formal Gaussian integral and consider the action of the holomorphic Gaudin operators by differentiating inside the integral. Care must be taken to ensure that the differentiation under the integral sign is allowed and that the representation of
K_{3}
as a Gaussian integral is valid. The resulting integral can be evaluated using Wick’s theorem. When the dust clears, the resulting expression is symmetric in
x,y,z
.

3.2
Main Proof

To prove Gaiotto’s formula, we note that the determinant formula for
K_{3}
arises in Gaiotto’s chiral algebra construction as the result of a Gaussian integral over formal parameters
\lambda_{i}

[
5
]
. To this end, we will perform some formal calculations involving Gaussian integrals and relate these to what we wish to prove. To begin, notice that we may write

K_{3}(x,y,z;t)=\frac{1}{\left\lvert\det A\right\rvert}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det A}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\overline{A}}}.

The holomorphic Gaudin operators care only about the determinant of the
holomorphic
matrix
A
, so it suffices to represent
(\det A)^{-1/2}
as a Gaussian integral and consider the action of the Gaudin operator on this piece. To begin, we quote two well-known elementary results for real matrices:

Lemma 3.1

(Gaussian Integration)
.

Let
A
be a real, symmetric positive-definite matrix. We have (for suitable normalization of the Lebesgue measure)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{u},A\mathbb{u})}d^{n}\mathbb{u}=(\det A)^{-1/2}.

Lemma 3.2

(Wick’s Theorem)
.

Let
A
be a real, symmetric positive-definite matrix as above, and let
B,C
be symmetric matrices. Then the following relations hold:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(B\mathbb{u},A\mathbb{u})e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{u},A\mathbb{u})}d^{n}\mathbb{u}={\rm Tr}(B)(\det A)^{-1/2};

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(B\mathbb{u},A\mathbb{u})(C\mathbb{u},A\mathbb{u})e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{u},A\mathbb{u})}d^{n}\mathbb{u}=({\rm Tr}(B){\rm Tr}(C)+2{\rm Tr}(BC))(\det A)^{-1/2}.

Proof.

These results are classical and well-known.
∎

Now,
x_{i},y_{i},z_{i},t_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
be real variables, and define a symmetric matrix

A_{ij}=\frac{(x_{i}-x_{j})(y_{i}-y_{j})(z_{i}-z_{j})}{t_{i}-t_{j}},\ i\neq js

and
A_{ii}=0
, as in Gaiotto’s formula. The diagonal entries of
a_{ij}
are defined to be zero. This is obviously a real, symmetric matrix, but it is not necessarily positive-definite. Nevertheless, we may still use the Gaussian integral representation and Lemma

to compute the action of the Gaudin operators

on
(\det A)^{-1/2}

Lemma 3.3
.

Let the matrix
A
and the Gaudin operators

be defined as above. The following formula holds:

4G_{r}^{y}(\det A)^{-1/2}=\sum_{s\neq r}\tfrac{y_{r}-y_{s}}{t_{r}-t_{s}}\sum_{m\neq r}z_{m}\bigg[-\sum_{p\neq s}\Big((y_{r}-y_{s})z_{p}({\rm Tr}(D_{rm}A^{-1}){\rm Tr}(D_{sp}A^{-1})+\\
\left.2{\rm Tr}(D_{rm}A^{-1}D_{sp}A^{-1})\right.)\Big)-2{\rm Tr}((D_{rm}-D_{sm})A^{-1})\bigg].

(1)

Proof.

Let

Lemma 3.4
.

The following formula holds:

(\det A)^{1/2}G_{r}^{y}(\det A)^{-1/2}:=\Omega_{r}:=\sum_{s\neq r}\frac{\Omega_{rs}}{t_{r}-t_{s}},

where
\Omega_{rs}
is defined by

\Omega_{rs}=\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\tfrac{(y_{r}-y_{s})^{2}(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{p}-z_{s})(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}(b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr})

-\sum_{m\neq r}\tfrac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})}{t_{r}-t_{m}}b_{rm}+\sum_{s\neq p}\tfrac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{t_{s}-t_{p}}b_{sp}.

Proof.

We have

D_{sp}A^{-1}=-\frac{x_{s}-x_{p}}{t_{s}-t_{p}}(E_{sp}+E_{ps})A^{-1},

so the

Since

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm Tr}(D_{sp}A^{-1})&=-2\frac{x_{i}-x_{p}}{t_{i}-t_{p}}b_{sp},\ \ s\neq p;\\
{\rm Tr}(D_{ss}A^{-1})&=2\sum_{p\neq s}\frac{x_{i}-x_{p}}{t_{i}-t_{p}}b_{sp}.\end{split}

(5)

Note now that we may also write

D_{sp}A^{-1}=-\frac{x_{s}-x_{p}}{t_{s}-t_{p}}\sum_{j}(E_{sj}b_{pj}+E_{pj}b_{sj}).

When

D_{rm}A^{-1}D_{sp}A^{-1}=\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}\sum_{j,k}(E_{sj}b_{pj}+E_{pj}b_{sj})(E_{rk}b_{mk}+E_{mk}b_{rk})=\\
\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}\sum_{j,k}\delta_{jr}(E_{sk}b_{pj}b_{mk}+E_{pk}b_{sj}b_{mk})+\delta_{jm}(E_{sk}b_{pj}b_{rk}+E_{pk}b_{sj}b_{rk})=\\
\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}\sum_{k}(E_{sk}(b_{pr}b_{mk}+b_{pm}b_{rk})+E_{pk}(b_{sr}b_{mk}+b_{sm}b_{rk}))

We conclude that

{\rm Tr}(D_{rm}A^{-1}D_{sp}A^{-1})=2F_{rmsp},\ \ r\neq m,\ \ s\neq p

In the case when

Similarly, if

F_{rmss}=-\sum_{p\neq s}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}(b_{ms}b_{pr}+b_{mp}b_{sr}).

Finally,

F_{rrss}=\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}(b_{ms}b_{pr}+b_{mp}b_{sr}).

This allows us to write the following:

{\rm Tr}(D_{rm}A^{-1}){\rm Tr}(D_{sp}A^{-1})+2{\rm Tr}(D_{rm}A^{-1}D_{sp}A^{-1})=\\
4\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}(b_{rm}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{pr}+b_{mp}b_{sr})

(6)

when

.

Let

,

\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}(b_{rm}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{pr}+b_{mp}b_{sr})

term when we combine (

-z_{m}z_{p}

-z_{r}z_{s}

(y_{r}-y_{s})^{2}(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{p}-z_{s})
.

We similarly compute degree

-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{m}z_{m}\operatorname{Tr}(D_{rm}-D_{sm})A^{-1}=\sum_{m\neq r}z_{m}\frac{x_{r}-x_{m}}{t_{r}-t_{m}}b_{rm}-\sum_{m\neq r}z_{r}\frac{x_{r}-x_{m}}{t_{r}-t_{m}}b_{rm}-\\
\sum_{m\neq s}z_{m}\frac{x_{s}-x_{m}}{t_{s}-t_{m}}b_{sm}+\sum_{m\neq s}z_{s}\frac{x_{s}-x_{m}}{t_{s}-t_{m}}b_{sm}=\\

Combining the above equations with (

(\det A)^{1/2}G_{r}^{y}(\det A)^{-1/2}:=\Omega_{r}:=\sum_{s\neq r}\frac{\Omega_{rs}}{t_{r}-t_{s}},

where
\Omega_{rs}

\Omega_{rs}=\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\tfrac{(y_{r}-y_{s})^{2}(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{p}-z_{s})(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}(b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr})

Now, decompose
\Omega_{r}=\Omega_{r}^{(1)}+\Omega_{r}^{(2)}
, where

in the symbols

. In particular,

\Omega_{r}^{(2)}=\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\tfrac{(y_{r}-y_{s})^{2}(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{p}-z_{s})(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}(b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr});

\Omega_{r}^{(1)}=-\sum_{m\neq r}\tfrac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})}{t_{r}-t_{m}}b_{rm}+\sum_{s\neq p}\tfrac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{t_{s}-t_{p}}b_{sp}.

Our strategy will be to rearrange
\Omega_{r}^{(2)}
until we obtain an expression which can be shown to be symmetric in
y
and
z
. Along the way, we will obtain additional degree-1 terms, which we will denote by
\Sigma_{r}^{(1)}
. Finally, we will demonstrate that
\Omega_{r}^{(1)}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1)}

.

We begin by noticing that

(y_{r}-y_{s})^{2}=(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{r}-y_{p})+(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{p}-y_{s}).

Moreover, we find that

\sum_{p\neq s}\frac{(y_{p}-y_{s})(z_{p}-z_{s})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{t_{s}-t_{p}}(b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr})=b_{rm}+\delta_{sr}b_{ms}+\delta_{ms}b_{rs}.

We then see that

\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\frac{(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{p}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{p}-z_{s})(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}(b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr})=

\sum_{m,s\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{r}-t_{s})}b_{rm}+\cdots:=\Sigma_{r}^{(1),1},

where the terms
\cdots
are manifestly symmetric in
y,z
.

The remaining degree-two piece looks as follows:

{\Omega^{\prime}}_{r}^{(2)}=\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq r,s}(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{r}-y_{p})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{p}-z_{s})(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})\\
\times\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{s})}(b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr}),

so that

\Omega_{r}^{(2)}={\Omega^{\prime}}_{r}^{(2)}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1),1}.

Note that the
y_{r}-y_{p}
term allows us to add a condition
p\neq r
without changing the sum. Hence the summation is symmetric with respect to the exchange of summation indices
s\leftrightarrow p
. Thus, we may symmetrize with respect to change
s\leftrightarrow p
. Note that the numerator is symmetric with respect to
s\leftrightarrow p
. The denominator transforms as follows:

\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{s})}\implies

\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{s})}+\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{p}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{p})}\right]

=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}\left[\frac{1}{t_{r}-t_{s}}-\frac{1}{t_{r}-t_{p}}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{p})}.

Now, we write

y_{r}-y_{p}=(y_{r}-y_{m})+(y_{m}-y_{p}).

and we notice that

\sum_{m\neq r}\frac{(y_{r}-y_{m})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{r}-x_{m})}{t_{r}-t_{m}}(b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr})=b_{sp}+\delta_{rs}b_{rp}+\delta_{rp}b_{rs}.

We see then that

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq r,s}(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{r}-y_{m})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{p}-z_{s})(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})\\
\times\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{r}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{s})}(b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr})

=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p,s\neq r}\frac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{s})}b_{sp}+\cdots:=\Sigma_{r}^{(1),2},

where the
\cdots
are terms manifestly symmetric in
y,z
.

The degree-two term that we are left with is then

{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}_{r}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,s,p\neq r}(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{m}-y_{p})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{p}-z_{s})(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})\\
\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{p})}(b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr})

:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,s,p\neq r}A_{rmsp}(b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr}).

so that

\Omega_{r}^{(2)}={\Omega^{\prime}}_{r}^{(2)}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1),1}={\Omega^{\prime\prime}}_{r}^{(2)}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1),1}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1),2}.

We note that the presence of terms
z_{p}-z_{s}
in the numerator allows us to safely drop the condition
p\neq s
in the sums.

At this point, we are in a position to explicitly show the symmetry of the degree-two piece in
y,z
.

Lemma 3.5
.

The quantity
{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}_{r}^{(2)}
is symmetric in
y,z
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that the terms
b_{mr}b_{sp}+b_{ms}b_{rp}+b_{mp}b_{sr}
are invariant under any cyclic permutation of the indices
m,s,p
. By a sum over cyclic permutations of
m,s,p
, we see that for fixed
m,s,p
the coefficient on
b_{mr}b_{sp}
is
\frac{1}{2}(A_{rmsp}+A_{rspm}+A_{rpms})
. We will prove that
A_{rmsp}+A_{rspm}+A_{rpms}
is symmetric in
y
and
z
.

Define
H_{rmsp}(x)
as follows:

H_{rmsp}(x)=(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p}).

We have

A_{rmsp}=\frac{H_{rspm}(y)H_{rmsp}(x)H_{rmsp}(z)}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{p})}.

Since the denominator is symmetric in
m,p,s
we should prove that the sum of cyclic shifts of
H_{rspm}(y)H_{rmsp}(x)H_{rmsp}(z)
in these three indices is symmetric with respect to
y,z
. Explicitly, we wish to show that

H_{rspm}(y)H_{rmsp}(x)H_{rmsp}(z)+H_{rmsp}(y)H_{rpms}(x)H_{rpms}(z)+H_{rpms}(y)H_{rspm}(x)H_{rspm}(z)

is symmetric in
y,z
.

We first note by direct computation that

H_{rmsp}(x)+H_{rspm}(x)+H_{rpms}(x)=0,

so we may replace
H_{rpms}(x)
with
-H_{rmsp}(x)-H_{rspm}(x)
. We find that

H_{rspm}(y)H_{rmsp}(x)H_{rmsp}(z)+H_{rmsp}(y)H_{rpms}(x)H_{rpms}(z)+H_{rpms}(y)H_{rspm}(x)H_{rspm}(z)

=C^{(1)}_{rmsp}+C^{(2)}_{rmsp},

where

C^{(1)}_{rmsp}=H_{rmsp}(x)(H_{rspm}(y)H_{rmsp}(z)-H_{rmsp}(y)H_{rpms}(z));

C^{(2)}_{rmsp}=H_{rspm}(x)(H_{rpms}(y)H_{rspm}(z)-H_{rmsp}(y)H_{rpms}(z)).

We will prove that the first cyclic shift
C_{rmsp}^{(1)}
is symmetric in
y,z
; the proof that
C_{rmsp}^{(2)}
is symmetric in
y,z
is entirely analogous. We wish to show that

H_{rspm}(y)H_{rmsp}(z)-H_{rmsp}(y)H_{rpms}(z)=H_{rspm}(z)H_{rmsp}(y)-H_{rmsp}(z)H_{rpms}(y).

Indeed, the leftmost and the rightmost term have sum

H_{rmsp}(z)(H_{rspm}(y)+H_{rpms}(y))=-H_{rmsp}(z)H_{rmsp}(y).

The middle two terms have sum

H_{rmsp}(y)(H_{rpms}(z)+H_{rspm}(z))=-H_{rmsp}(y)H_{rmsp}(z),

hence the left-hand side is equal to the right-hand side. Thus, the first cyclic shift is symmetric. The second cyclic shift is done similarly. We are therefore done.
∎

Now, we focus on the degree-one pieces.

Lemma 3.6
.

In particular, define by
\Sigma_{r}^{(1)}
the remaining terms of degree 1 after we have transformed the degree-two piece as above:

\Sigma^{(1)}_{r}=\Sigma_{r}^{(1),1}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1),2}

Then
\Omega_{r}^{(1)}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1)}
is symmetric in
y,z
.

Proof.

In particular, let us denote by
\Sigma_{r}^{(1)}
the remaining terms of degree 1 after we have transformed the degree-two piece as above. We have computed the terms of
\Sigma_{r}^{(1)}
already:

\Sigma^{(1)}_{r}=\Sigma_{r}^{(1),1}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1),2}

=\sum_{m,s\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{r}-t_{s})}b_{rm}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p,s\neq r}\frac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{s})}b_{sp}.

On the other hand, the degree-one terms present in the original
\Omega_{r}^{(1)}
are as follows:

\Omega_{r}^{(1)}=-\sum_{m,s\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{r}-t_{s})}b_{rm}+\sum_{p,s\neq r}\frac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{s}-t_{p})}b_{sp}.

The combined degree-one term we require is
\Omega_{rs}^{(1)}+\Sigma_{rs}^{(1)}
. The first terms of
\Omega_{rs}^{(1)}
and
\Sigma_{rs}^{(1)}
cancel, so we are left with

\Omega_{r}^{(1)}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1)}=\sum_{p,s\neq r}\frac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})}\left[\frac{1}{t_{s}-t_{p}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{t_{r}-t_{p}}\right]b_{sp}=

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p,s\neq r}\frac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{s}-z_{p})(2t_{r}-t_{s}-t_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{s}-t_{p})}b_{sp}.

Since
b_{sp}
is symmetric under
s\leftrightarrow p
for the summed indices
s,p
, we may symmetrize its coefficient. We find that

\frac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{s}-z_{p})(2t_{r}-t_{s}-t_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{s}-t_{p})}\ \longmapsto

-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{s}-y_{p})(z_{s}-z_{p})(2t_{r}-t_{s}-t_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{s}-t_{p})}.

But we now see that this summand is symmetric in
y,z
. Thus, the entirety of
\Omega_{r}
is indeed symmetric in
y,z
, so
G_{r}^{y}
and
G_{r}^{z}
act identically. We are done.
∎

Now, we can put all of the pieces together. Recall that

\Omega_{r}=\Omega_{r}^{(2)}+\Omega_{r}^{(1)}={\Omega^{\prime\prime}}_{r}^{(2)}+(\Omega_{r}^{(1)}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1)}).

By Lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
,
{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}_{r}^{(2)}
and
\Omega_{r}^{(1)}+\Sigma_{r}^{(1)}
are each symmetric in

, so
\Omega_{r}
must also be. By Lemma

,

is therefore symmetric in

, so

G_{r}^{y}(\det A)^{-1/2}=G_{r}^{z}(\det A)^{-1/2},

exactly as we wanted to show. We are done.

3.3
\lambda
-Twisted Generalization

A natural generalization of our formula incorporates a so-called
\lambda
-

. In
[
2
]
,
\lambda
-twisted versions of the Gaudin operators are defined:

G_{i}^{\lambda}=\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{j}}\left[-(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}+(y_{i}-y_{j})(\lambda_{i}\partial_{i}-\lambda_{j}\partial_{j})+\frac{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}{2}\right].

In particular, we modify the Gaussian integral representation of
K_{3}
as follows to obtain a kernel
K_{3}^{\lambda}
:

K_{3}^{\lambda}(x,y,z;t)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}d^{m}ud^{m}\overline{u}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{m}\left\lvert u_{i}\right\rvert^{-2(\lambda_{i}+1)}\right]e^{(\mathbf{u},A\mathbf{u})-(\mathbf{\overline{u}},\overline{A}\mathbf{\overline{u}})},

This integral can be sensibly defined in the sense of tempered distributions as per the techniques in Appendices
A
and
B
. In particular, we show in
B.9
that we may take the action of the holomorphic Gaudin operator inside the integral sign. Now, let
n_{i}=-\lambda_{i}-1
; the action of the (nonconstant part of the) holomorphic Gaudin operator
G_{i}^{\lambda}
on the holomorphic part of the above integral yields the following:

G_{r}^{\lambda}\left(\int d^{n}u\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{n_{i}}\right]e^{(\mathbf{u},A\mathbf{u})}\right)=

\int d^{n}u\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{n_{i}}\right]\sum_{s\neq r}\frac{1}{t_{r}-t_{s}}\left[-(y_{r}-y_{s})^{2}\partial_{r}\partial_{s}+(y_{r}-y_{s})((n_{s}+1)\partial_{r}-(n_{r}+1)\partial_{s})\right]e^{(\mathbf{u},A\mathbf{u})}

\int d^{n}u\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{n_{i}}\right]e^{(\mathbf{u},A\mathbf{u})}\times

=\Bigg[-\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\frac{(y_{r}-y_{s})^{2}(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}

+\sum_{s\neq r}(n_{s}+1)\sum_{m\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})}u_{r}u_{m}

-(n_{r}+1)\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{p\neq s}\frac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{s}-t_{p})}u_{s}u_{p}\Bigg].

(7)

At this point, we wish to integrate the second and third sums by parts, which is legitimate by Proposition
B.11
. In particular, we recognize that

(n_{s}+1)u_{s}^{n_{s}}=\frac{d}{du_{s}}u_{s}^{n_{s}+1}.

In integrating by parts, we integrate this term and differentiate the coefficient it multiplies (including the exponential) as follows:

\int u\,dv\implies-\int v\,du.

Taking

u=e^{(\mathbf{u},A\mathbf{u})}\sum_{m\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})}u_{r}u_{m},\quad dv=(n_{s}+1)u_{s}^{n_{s}}\cdot\prod_{i\neq s}u_{i}^{n_{i}}du_{s},

we find that

u\,dv=-\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{n_{i}}du_{s}\cdot(n_{s}+1)\sum_{m\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})}u_{r}u_{m};

-v\,du=\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{n_{i}}du_{s}\cdot\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{s}-y_{p})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}.

Thus, dropping boundary terms as per Proposition
B.11
, we may effectively replace the second sum in brackets in (
7
) with

\sum_{s\neq r}(n_{s}+1)\sum_{m\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})}u_{r}u_{m}\implies

-\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{s}-y_{p})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}.

(8)

By analogous reasoning,

-(n_{r}+1)\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{p\neq s}\frac{(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{s}-t_{p})}u_{s}u_{p}\implies

\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{r}-y_{m})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}

(9)

The term in (
8
) in now combines nicely with the first sum in brackets in (
7
):

-\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\frac{(y_{r}-y_{s})^{2}(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}

+\sum_{s\neq r}(n_{s}+1)\sum_{m\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(y_{r}-y_{s})(z_{r}-z_{m})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})}u_{r}u_{m}=

-\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq s}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{r}-y_{p})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}.

Note that the term with
r=p
vanishes due to the factor of
y_{r}-y_{p}
in the numerator. Thus, the summation indices
s,p
run over the same range. We may thus symmetrize the coefficient of
u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}
in the indices
s,p
. Note that everything within the sum is symmetric in
s,p
except for
\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{s}-t_{p})}
. We find

\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{s}-t_{p})}+\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{p})(t_{p}-t_{s})}=\frac{1}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{p})},

and we obtain

-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{r}-y_{p})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{r}-t_{p})}u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}.

Now, write

y_{r}-y_{p}=y_{r}-y_{m}+y_{m}-y_{p}.

Focus on the piece corresponding to
y_{m}-y_{p}
:

-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,s,p\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{m}-y_{p})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{s}-t_{p})}u_{r}u_{m}u_{s}u_{p}.

Using the notation of Sec.
3.2
, the coefficient of
u_{r}u_{m}u_{s}u_{p}
is given by

A_{rmsp}=\frac{H_{rspm}(y)H_{rmsp}(x)H_{rmsp}(z)}{(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{p})}.

We already showed that
H_{rspm}(y)H_{rmsp}(x)H_{rmsp}(z)
is symmetric in
y,z
when summed over cyclic permutations of the indices
s,m,p
. The relevant computations are performed in Sec.
3.2
. The same computation follows through in this case, as the term
u_{r}u_{m}u_{s}u_{p}
is invariant under these cyclic permutations.

This leaves the following term:

-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq r}\frac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{r}-y_{m})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})(t_{r}-t_{p})}u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}.

Adding this to the term in (
9
) gives

\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq r}\tfrac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{r}-y_{m})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{m})}\left[\tfrac{1}{t_{s}-t_{p}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{1}{t_{r}-t_{p}}\right]u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}=

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq r}\tfrac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{r}-y_{s})(y_{r}-y_{m})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})(2t_{r}-t_{s}-t_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{p})(t_{s}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{m})}u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}.

Symmetrizing the coefficient of
u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p}
in
s
and
p
, we obtain

-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{s\neq r}\sum_{m\neq r,p\neq r}\tfrac{(x_{r}-x_{m})(x_{s}-x_{p})(y_{s}-y_{p})(y_{r}-y_{m})(z_{r}-z_{m})(z_{s}-z_{p})(2t_{r}-t_{s}-t_{p})}{(t_{r}-t_{s})(t_{r}-t_{p})(t_{s}-t_{p})(t_{r}-t_{m})}u_{r}u_{s}u_{m}u_{p},

which is symmetric in
y,z
. We are done.

4
Discussion

In summary, we have proved a conjectured formula for an intertwining kernel for the quantum Hitchin Hamiltonians in genus zero (Gaudin operators) over
\mathbb{C}
with
\left\lvert S\right\rvert\geq 4
marked points. We have additionally demonstrated a natural generalization of our formula to account for a possible ‘‘
\lambda
-twisting.’’ With all of this in mind, we now assess the various implications of our result.

First of all, it is of natural interest to consider the analytic geometric Langlands program over fields other than
\mathbb{C}
. The case of
\mathbb{R}
is handled very similarly to
\mathbb{C}
. Gaiotto
[
5
]
produces a very similar formula for an intertwining kernel for the
real
Gaudin system in three sets of variables:

K_{3}^{\mathbb{R}}(x,y,z;t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\lvert\det A\right\rvert}}.

The argument from the previous section goes through essentially unchanged to demonstrate this formula as well. We represent
(\det A)^{-1/2}
by a Gaussian integral, act on the integral by the Gaudin operator, and apply Wick’s theorem. The resulting expression can be massaged into a form which is manifestly symmetric in the
x,y,z
variables.

Over local fields, the story is more complicated. We no longer have at our disposal the differential Gaudin operators, and to understand the spectrum of the Hecke operators, it would thus be an important stop to construct a Gaiotto kernel over local fields
F
. One might naturally guess something of the following form:

K_{3}^{F}(x,y,z;t)=\frac{\theta(\det A)}{\sqrt{\left\|\det A\right\|}},

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|
denotes a (non-Archimedean) absolute value over
F
. Here we define
\theta(x)=1
if
x
is a square in
F
and
\theta(x)=0
otherwise.

To show that
K_{3}^{F}
intertwines the Hecke operators over
all
fields
F
is therefore an important next step. We will show this with a calculation of the action of the Hecke operators on
K_{3}^{F}
in the upcoming work
[
8
]
.

Appendix A
Differentiating under the Integral Sign

In this section, we want to prove that

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\int f(x,y)dy=\int\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}f(x,y)dy

for sufficiently ‘‘good’’ functions
f
. This will allow us to make the analytic manipulations we use in Section
3.2
. We will also use the result of this section in the next Section
B
.

Since we are differentiating in one variable at a time, we will assume that
x
is just one variable. Let
n
be a positive integer, and suppose
f(x,y)
is a smooth function defined on
U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
U
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Let

g(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x,y)dy,

where we assume that the right-hand side is absolutely convergent for all
x\in U
.
Let
x^{*}
be a point in
U
. Also denote
\partial_{x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
.

Lemma A.1
.

1.

Suppose that there exists a neighborhood
V
of
x^{*}
and a function
M(y)
with the following property: for all
x\in V
we have
\left\lvert\partial_{x}f(x,y)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\partial_{x}^{2}f(x,y)\right\rvert\leq M(y)
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}M(y)\leq\infty
. Then
g(x)
has a derivative at point
x^{*}
that can be computed as

g^{\prime}(x^{*})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{x}f(x^{*},y)dy.

2.

Suppose that
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
U
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
f(x,y)
is a smooth function defined on
U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
g(x)
is defined as above. Let
x^{*}
be a point in
U
. Assume that for any partial differential operator
D
in
x
with constant coefficients there exists a neighborhood
V_{D}
of
x^{*}
and a function
M_{D}(y)
with the following property: for all
x\in V_{D}
we have
\left\lvert Df(x,y)\right\rvert\leq M_{D}(y)
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}M_{D}(y)\leq\infty
. Then
g(x)
is smooth at
x^{*}
and
Dg(x)
can be computed as
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}Df(x^{*},y)dy
.

Proof.

The second statement immediately follows from the first using induction on the order of
D
.

By definition,

g^{\prime}(x^{*})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(g(x^{*}+\varepsilon)-g(x^{*})).

We have

g(x^{*}+\varepsilon)-g(x^{*})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[f(x^{*}+x_{i},y)-f(x^{*},y)\right]dy=\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{x}f(x^{*}+a(y),y)dy.

Here, we have used mean value theorem, and we have let
a
be a map from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
[0,\varepsilon]
. Thus,

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(g(x^{*}+\varepsilon x_{i})-g(x^{*}))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{x}f(x^{*}+x_{i}a(y),y)dy.

Using the mean value theorem again, we find that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{x}f(x^{*}+a(y),y)dy-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{x}f(x^{*},y)dy=\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial^{2}_{x}f(x^{*}+b(y),y)dy,

where
b
is another map from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
[0,\varepsilon]
. Using the condition in the statement of the lemma we see that the expression on the right hand side is at most
C\varepsilon
, where
C
is a constant positive number. Hence it tends to zero when
\varepsilon
tends to zero, and indeed

g^{\prime}(x^{*})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{x}f(x^{*},y)dy.

∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
D_{km}
be fixed matrices,
A=\sum y_{k}z_{m}D_{km}
. Let
U
be the set of all
x
such that the corresponding matrix
A
is positive definite,
x^{*}
be an element of
U
.

Then function
f(x,\mathbf{u})=e^{-\frac{1}{2}(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})}
satisfies conditions of Lemma
A.1
with
y=\mathbf{u}
. In particular, we can compute a partial differential operator
D(\sqrt{\det A})
as
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}D(e^{-\frac{1}{2}(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})})d\mathbf{u}
times a constant.

Proof.

For any partial differential operator
D
with constant coefficients we have
Df(x,\mathbf{u})=p(x,\mathbf{u})e^{-\frac{1}{2}(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})}
, where
p
is a polynomial in
x,\mathbf{u}
. In a neighborhood
V
of
x^{*}
we can say that
p(x,\mathbf{u})\leq C_{1}+C_{2}\left\lvert\mathbf{u}\right\rvert^{N}
, where
C_{1},C_{2},N
are constant positive numbers. Since any two norms on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
are equivalent, we have that

(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})\geq\varepsilon(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}).

for any
\mathbf{u}
. In fact, we can choose
\varepsilon>0
such that the above equation holds true for all
A
in a neighborhood
V\ni x^{*}
.

Consider
M(\mathbf{u})=(C_{1}+C_{2}\left\lvert\mathbf{u}\right\rvert^{N})e^{-\varepsilon(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})}
. We just showed that
\left\lvert Df(x,\mathbf{u})\right\rvert\leq M(\mathbf{u})
for
x\in V
.
We also see that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}M(\mathbf{u})d\mathbf{u}<\infty
. It follows that
f(x,\mathbf{u})
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
A.1
.
∎

With the preceding two lemmas, we have established the validity of differentiating sufficiently convergent real integrals. This finishes the details of the proof in Section
3.2
. In the next section, we will explain how to deal with the integrals arising in the proof of the twisted Gaiotto formula in Section
3.3
.

Appendix B
Integrals in the Sense of Tempered Distributions

In this section, our goal will be to explain what to do with the integral containing
e^{(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})-\overline{(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})}}
in the definition of twisted kernel in Section
3.3
. We wish to verify that the manipulations applied in that section (e.g. differentiation under the integral sign, integration by parts) are legitimate. In effect, we will formalize the details of the proof in Sec.
3.3
.

Our starting point is the following integral:

\int d^{m}\mathbf{x}d^{m}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{s}\right]e^{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})-\overline{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})}},

where
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}y_{i}
is a bilinear form, not sesquilinear.
This integral is the primary object of interest in Sec.
3.3
, but a rigorous definition thereof is not immediately clear. Moreover, our manipulations of differentiating this integral and integrating by parts are not
a priori
justified. In order to rectify this situation, we will need to define this integral using the notion of a Fourier transform of a tempered distribution.

Note that the results of this section are not new: distrubutions and operations on them are known since the middle of twentieth century. We include proofs of these results for reader’s convenience. It is possible that the properties of the integral used for Gaiotto kernel are described somewhere in the mathematical analysis literature, but the authors could not locate a source formalizing precisely the manipulations used in Sec.
3.3
. For this reason we (re)prove the results we need below.

We say that a smooth function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is
rapidly decreasing
if for all
n\geq 0
function
|x|^{n}f
is bounded. Let
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the Schwartz space. It consists of smooth functions
f
such that
f
and all its derivatives are rapidly decreasing. The Schwartz space is a topological space with a family of seminorms
\|f\|_{\alpha,\beta}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x^{\alpha}D^{\beta}f|
.
We further define
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
via
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
. Define the
space of tempered distributions
to be continuous dual
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{S}
.

The
Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}
is an automorphism
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
defined in the usual way. We can then define Fourier transform on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
as a transpose operator. We now introduce some convenient notation. For functions
f,g
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
define a bilinear pairing
(f,g)
via

(f,g):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d^{n}\mathbf{x}f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x}).

Again, this notation is slightly nonstandard, but it will be more convenient for us. We embed
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
into
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
via
f(g)=(f,g)
. In this way Fourier transform on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
extends Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}\colon L^{1}\to L^{\infty}
.

If Fourier transform of
f
can be computed as a well-behaved conditionally convergent integral, the result will coincide with
\mathcal{F}f
:

Proposition B.1
.

Let
f
be a locally integrable function that belongs to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
and let
K_{1}\subset K_{2}\subset\cdots
be a sequence of compact sets with union
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Suppose that

I_{n}(\mathbf{y}):=\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,f(\mathbf{x})e^{i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}

is uniformly bounded in
n,\mathbf{y}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{n}(\mathbf{y})
exists for all
\mathbf{y}
and is uniform on compact sets. Then the function

g(\mathbf{y})=\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{n}(\mathbf{y})

defined by this limit satisfies
g\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
; moreover,
g=\mathcal{F}f
.

Proof.

We have

(\mathcal{F}f,h)=(f,\mathcal{F}h)=\int d^{m}\mathbf{y}\,f(\mathbf{y})\mathcal{F}h(\mathbf{y})=\\
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{y}\,f(\mathbf{y})\mathcal{F}h(\mathbf{y})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{y}\,f(\mathbf{y})\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,e^{i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}h(\mathbf{x})\right]=\\
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,h(\mathbf{x})\left[\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{y}\,e^{i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}f(\mathbf{y})\right]

(10)

In the last step, we used that
f
is locally integrable to apply Fubini’s theorem. Since
I_{n}(\mathbf{y}):=\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{y}\,e^{i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}f(\mathbf{y})
is uniformly bounded, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact set
K_{\varepsilon}
such that the difference between

\int_{\mathbb{R}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,h(\mathbf{x})\left[\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{y}\,e^{i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}f(\mathbf{y})\right]

and

\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,h(\mathbf{x})\left[\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{y}\,e^{i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}f(\mathbf{y})\right]

is at most
\varepsilon/2
for any
n
. We may take
K_{\varepsilon}
so that it contains the ball with the radius
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
centered at zero.

Since the limit of
\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,f(\mathbf{x})e^{i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}
is uniform on compact sets we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,h(\mathbf{x})\left[\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{y}\,e^{i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}f(\mathbf{y})\right]=\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,h(\mathbf{x})\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{y}\,e^{i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}f(\mathbf{y})\right]

=\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,h(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x}).

It follows that for large enough
n
the difference between

\int_{\mathbb{R}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,h(\mathbf{x})\left[\int_{K_{n}}d^{m}\mathbf{y}\,e^{i(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}f(\mathbf{y})\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,h(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x})

is at most
\varepsilon
. Using (
10
), we deduce that the difference between
(\mathcal{F}f,h)
and
\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,h(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x})
is at most
\varepsilon
.

Note that
g(\mathbf{x})
is a bounded continuous function, hence it gives an element of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}\,h(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x})
can be computed as the limit
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}d^{m}\mathbf{x}h(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x})=(\mathcal{F}f,h)
, hence
g=\mathcal{F}f
.
∎

Equipped with a definition of the Fourier transform on tempered distributions, we may now define the integral over a tempered distribution:

Definition B.2
.

Let
f
be a tempered distribution such that
\mathcal{F}f
is represented by a function continuous at zero. Then we define the integral of
f
as

\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,f(x):=\mathcal{F}f(0).

Thus, in order to define an integral

\int d^{m}\mathbf{x}d^{m}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{s_{i}}\right]e^{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})-\overline{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})}}

in this sense, we should compute

\mathcal{F}\left(\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{s_{i}}\right]e^{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})-\overline{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})}}\right).

We will break up this computation into several steps. First we will compute
\mathcal{F}e^{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})-\overline{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})}}
in Lemma
B.3
. The function
\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{s_{i}}
gives an element of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
only for
\Re s_{i}>-2
, but the corresponding distribution can be meromorphically continued to
\mathbb{C}\setminus-2\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, and its Fourier transform is known. A usual Fourier transform of a product of
functions
is a convolution of Fourier transforms under some conditions. In our case, for certain values of
s_{i}
we can also make sense of the integral in the definition of convolution and the result is a smooth function. We prove that it gives a Fourier transform of the product and then use meromorphic continuation to prove that Fourier transform is always given by a smooth function, hence the integral is well-defined. The right hand side tends to zero as

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{-n}^{n}dx\,e^{ix^{2}+ixy}=e^{-\frac{iy^{2}}{4}}

Lemma B.4
.

Let
\Re s>-2
,
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{C})
,
f\in\mathcal{S}
. Then
(\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s},f)
is well-defined and gives meromorphic function in
s
, holomorphic over
s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus 2\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Since
\Re s>-2
, the function
\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s}
is locally integrable and has at most polynomial growth at infinity. Thus, the function
\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s}
belongs in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. It remains to show that
(\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s},f)
is meromorphic with poles at
2\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
. Since
f
decays rapidly, we note that for any
V\subset\mathbb{C}
,

\int_{\mathbb{C}}dud\overline{u}\,\max_{s\in V}\left\lvert\partial_{s}^{j}\left(\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s}f\right)\right\rvert=\int_{\mathbb{C}}dud\overline{u}\,\max_{s\in V}\left\lvert\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s}\log^{j}\left\lvert u\right\rvert f\right\rvert.

The integral on the right hand side is always absolutely convergent for
\Re s>-2
. We conclude by lemma
A.1
that
(\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s},f)
is infinitely differentiable in
s
(and thus holomorphic) when
\Re s>-2
.

Now, notice that

\Delta\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s}=\partial_{u}\partial_{\overline{u}}(u\overline{u})^{\frac{s}{2}}=\frac{s^{2}}{4}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s-2}.

Suppose that
\Re s>0
. Let
\varepsilon>0
,
K=\{u\mid\varepsilon<\left\lvert u\right\rvert<\varepsilon^{-1}\}
. Let us transform the following integral using Green’s theorem:

\int_{K}\Delta(\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s})fdxdy.

The boundary term on the circle
\left\lvert u\right\rvert=\varepsilon^{-1}
clearly tends to zero, so we look at boundary term on the circle
\left\lvert u\right\rvert=\varepsilon
. For any two smooth functions
g,h
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0,0\}
with compact support for large enough
\varepsilon
we have

\int_{K}(\Delta g)h=\int_{K}(g^{\prime\prime}_{xx}+g^{\prime\prime}_{yy})hdxdy=\int_{\left\lvert u\right\rvert=\varepsilon}(hg^{\prime}_{x}dy-hg^{\prime}_{y}dx)-\int_{K}(g^{\prime}_{x}h^{\prime}_{x}+g^{\prime}_{y}h^{\prime}_{y})dxdy.

When
g=f
,
h=\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s}
, both
hg^{\prime}_{x}
and
hg^{\prime}_{y}
are continuous at zero, hence the boundary term goes to zero.

When
g=\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s}
and
h=f
the boundary term tends to zero when
\varepsilon
tends to zero. More explicitly, writing
g=\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s}=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{\frac{s}{2}}
, we have

g^{\prime}_{x}=sx\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s-2},\quad f^{\prime}_{y}=sy\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s-2}.

Hence,

hg^{\prime}_{x}dy-hg^{\prime}_{y}dx=sh\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s-2}(xdy-ydx).

If we parametrize the boundary circle using
x=r\cos\phi
,
y=r\sin\phi
we get
xdy-ydx=r^{2}d\phi
. Note that
r^{2}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s-2}=\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s}
. The function
h\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s-2}
tends to zero when
\left\lvert u\right\rvert
tends to zero, hence this boundary term tends to zero when
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Thus, using Green’s theorem twice allows us to write (for
\Re s>0
)

(\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s-2},f)=\frac{4}{s^{2}}(\Delta\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s},f)=\frac{4}{s^{2}}(\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s},\Delta f).

This allows us to define
(\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s},f)
for
s
such that
\Re s>-4
,
s\neq-2
. It also proves that
(\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s},f)
is holomorphic for
\Re s>-4
except at
s=-2
, where it has a pole of order
2
. Continuing in this way, we see that
(\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{s},f)
continues to a meromorphic function with poles at
-2,-4,\ldots
and so on.
∎

(M(\mathbf{x}),E(A,x)\mathcal{F}h)=\frac{C_{2}}{\left\lvert\det A\right\rvert}\left(M(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{F}(E(A^{-1},x)*h)\right)=\frac{C_{2}}{\left\lvert\det A\right\rvert}(M(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{F}(f*h)).

Finally, by Lemma
B.5
, we find that

\frac{C_{2}}{\left\lvert\det A\right\rvert}(M(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{F}(f*h))=\frac{C_{2}}{\left\lvert\det A\right\rvert}\left(\mathcal{F}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{s_{i}}\right],f*h\right)\\
=\frac{C_{2}}{\left\lvert\det A\right\rvert}\prod_{i=1}^{n}C(s_{i})\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{-2-s_{i}},f*h\right).

Comparing this with (
14
), we get

\mathcal{F}\left[E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x})\right]=\frac{C_{2}}{\left\lvert\det A\right\rvert}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}C(s_{i})\right)g(\mathbf{y}).

∎

Proposition B.8
.

Let
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus 2\mathbb{Z}
, and recall that
E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x})
defines an element of
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
as above. Then
g(\mathbf{y}):=\mathcal{F}(E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x}))
is given by a function smooth in
\mathbf{y}
and
A
and holomorphic over
s_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus 2\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

We have
(\mathcal{F}(E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x}),h)=(M(\mathbf{x}),E(A,x)\mathcal{F}h)
. Thus,

(M(\mathbf{x}),E(A,\mathbf{x})\mathcal{F}h)=\prod c(s_{i},m)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{s_{i}+2m},\prod\Delta_{i}^{m}(E(A,\mathbf{x})\mathcal{F}h)\right).

Note that for any partial differential operator
D
with constant coefficients we have
DE(\mathbf{x})=P_{D}(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{x}})E(\mathbf{x})
, where
P_{D}
is a polynomial in
x,\overline{x}
. Hence for any partial differential operator
D
with constant coefficients we have

D(E(\mathbf{x})\mathcal{F}h)=\sum_{D_{1},D_{2}}D_{1}E(\mathbf{x})D_{2}\mathcal{F}h=E(\mathbf{x})\sum_{P_{1},D_{2}}P_{1}(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{x}})D_{2}\mathcal{F}h.

Here sum comes from the formula for the derivative of a product,
P_{1}=P_{D_{1}}
is a polynomial such that
D_{1}E(\mathbf{x})=P_{1}E(\mathbf{x})
. Hence
D(E(x)\mathcal{F}h)=E(x)\tilde{D}\mathcal{F}h
, where
\tilde{D}
is a partial differential operator with polynomial coefficients. Since Fourier transform interchanges
x
with
\partial_{x}
(up to a constant factor), we get
\tilde{D}\mathcal{F}h=\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{D}h)
, where
\mathbb{D}
is also a partial differential operator with polynomial coefficients. When
D=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}^{m}
, denote the corresponding
\mathbb{D}
by
\mathbb{D}_{m}
.

Hence, defining

M_{m}(\mathbf{x}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{s_{i}+2m},

we find that

\prod_{i=1}^{n}c(s_{i},m)\left(M_{m}(\mathbf{x}),\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}^{m}(E(A,x)\mathcal{F}h)\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}c(s_{i},m)(M_{m}(\mathbf{x}),E(A,x)\mathcal{F}\mathbb{D}_{m}h).

We already know that

(M_{m}(\mathbf{x}),E(A,\mathbf{x})\mathcal{F}h)=(g_{m}(\mathbf{x}),h(\mathbf{x})),

where for
m
sufficiently large we may write

g_{m}(\mathbf{y})=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}d^{n}\mathbf{x}d^{n}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\,E(-A^{-1},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{-2-s_{i}-2m}

as per Proposition
B.6
. Note that
g_{m}
is smooth in
\mathbf{y}
, holomorophic on
s_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus 2\mathbb{Z}
and has at least
m-c
partial derivatives in
a_{ij}
for some constant
c>0
that does not depend on
m
. We thus find

\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}c(s_{i},m)\right](M_{m}(\mathbf{x}),E(A,x)\mathcal{F}\mathbb{D}_{m}h)\\
=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}c(s_{i},m)\right](g_{m}(\mathbf{y}),\mathbb{D}_{m}h(\mathbf{y}))=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}c(s_{i},m)\right](\mathbb{D}_{m}^{*}g_{m}(\mathbf{y}),h(\mathbf{y})),

hence

\mathcal{F}(E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x}))=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}c(s_{i},m)\right]\mathbb{D}_{m}^{*}g_{m}(\mathbf{y})

for
m
large enough. The right-hand side is holomorphic for
s_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus 2\mathbb{Z}
and smooth in
\mathbf{y}
and also has
m-c
partial derivaties in
a_{ij}
. Since the left-hand side does not depend on
m
we deduce that is smooth in
a_{ij}
.
∎

Proposition B.9
.

Let
D_{A}
be a differential operator in
a_{ij}
. Then

D_{A}(\mathcal{F}(E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x})))=\mathcal{F}(D_{A}(E(A,\mathbf{x}))M(\mathbf{x})).

Proof.

It is enough to check that both sides give the same element of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. It is not hard to see that for any
A^{*}
there exists a neighborhood
U
such that

\max_{A\in U}D_{A}\mathcal{F}(E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x}))

is still an element of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
. Indeed,
g(\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{F}(E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x}))
is smooth in
A
and
\mathbf{y}
, so we may safely take
D_{A}g(\mathbf{y})
. We find

D_{A}g(\mathbf{y})=P(\mathbf{y},\overline{\mathbf{y}})g(\mathbf{y})

for some polynomial
P
; integrating
D_{A}g(\mathbf{y})
against any test function
h\in\mathcal{S}
thus yields an absolutely convergent integral.

Thus, we use Lemma
A.1
to get

\left(D_{A}(\mathcal{F}(E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x}))),h\right)=D_{A}(\mathcal{F}(E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x})),h)=D_{A}(E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{F}h).

We then find

D_{A}(E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{F}h)=(D_{A}(E(A,\mathbf{x}))M(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{F}h).

The proposition follows.
∎

Corollary B.10
.

Let
K=g(0)
be the twisted Gaiotto kernel,
D_{A}
be as above. Then
D_{A}K
can be computed as an integral in the sense of tempered distributions

D_{A}K=\int d^{n}\mathbf{x}d^{n}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\,(D_{A}(E(A,\mathbf{x}))M(\mathbf{x}).

Proof.

Note that

(D_{A}(E(A,\mathbf{x}))M(\mathbf{x})=P(\mathbf{x})E(A,\mathbf{x})M(\mathbf{x})

for some polynomial
P(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{x}})
in
\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{x}}
. This expression has a good Fourier transform in the sense of tempered distributions. Thus, it is enough to set
\mathbf{y}=0
in Proposition
B.9
.
∎

Proposition B.11
.

Let
P(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{x}})
be a polynomial in
\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{x}}
. Then, for any
i
we have

\int d^{n}\mathbf{x}d^{n}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\,\partial_{i}\left[P(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{x}})e^{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})-\overline{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})}}M(\mathbf{x})\right]=0.

In particular, we can evaluate integrals of the form

\int d^{n}\mathbf{x}d^{n}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\,P(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{x}})e^{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})-\overline{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})}}M(\mathbf{x})

by parts without boundary terms.

Proof.

We wish to compute these integrals in the sense of tempered distributions. Note that
g(\mathbf{y})
is smooth in
\mathbf{y}
, so we find that

\int d^{n}\mathbf{x}d^{n}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\,P(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{x}})e^{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})-\overline{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})}}M(\mathbf{x})=P(\partial_{\mathbf{y}},\partial_{\overline{\mathbf{y}}})g(\mathbf{y}),

and the right hand side is a smooth function of
\mathbf{y}
. We thus find that

\int d^{n}\mathbf{x}d^{n}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\,\partial_{i}\left[P(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{x}})e^{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})-\overline{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})}}M(\mathbf{x})\right]=y_{i}P(\partial_{\mathbf{y}},\partial_{\overline{\mathbf{y}}})g(\mathbf{y})\bigg|_{\mathbf{y}=0}=0.

We are done.
∎

In summary, we have shown that we may sensibly define the following integral in the sense of tempered distributions:

\int d^{m}\mathbf{x}d^{m}\overline{\mathbf{x}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert^{s}\right]e^{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})-\overline{(\mathbf{x},A\mathbf{x})}}.

We have then shown that we may safely differentiate under the integral sign and integrate by parts as we have done in Sec.
3.3
, justifying all of the algebraic manipulations in our proof of the twisted Gaiotto formula.
