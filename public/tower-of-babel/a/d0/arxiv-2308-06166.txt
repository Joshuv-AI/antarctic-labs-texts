Introduction

Let
\mu
be a positive finite Borel measure with finite moments, whose support
\Delta\subset\mathds{R}
contains infinitely many points. We will denote by
\mathbf{Co}\!\left(A\right)
the convex hull of a set
A
and by
A^{\mathrm{o}}\!
its interior.

Let
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the monic orthogonal polynomial sequence with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}f(x)g(x)d\mu(x).

An inner product is called standard if the multiplication operator is symmetric with respect to the inner product. Obviously,
\langle xf,g\rangle_{\mu}=\langle f,xg\rangle_{\mu}
, i.e.,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}
is standard. Significant parts of the applications of orthogonal polynomials in mathematics and particular sciences are based on the following three consequences of this fact.

1.

The polynomial
P_{n}
has exactly
n
real simple zeros in
\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\Delta\right)^{\mathrm{o}}\!
. Moreover, there is a zero of
P_{n-1}
between any two consecutive zeros of
P_{n}
.

2.

The three-term recurrence relation

xP_{n}(x)=P_{n+1}(x)+\beta_{n}P_{n}(x)+\gamma^{2}_{n}P_{n-1}(x);\quad P_{0}(x)=1,\;P_{-1}(x)=0,

where
\gamma_{n}={\|P_{n}\|_{\mu}}/{\|P_{n-1}\|_{\mu}}
for
n\geq 1
,
{\beta}_{n}={\langle P_{n},xP_{n}\rangle_{\mu}}/{\|P_{n}\|_{\mu}^{2}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mu}=\sqrt{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}}
denotes the norm induced by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}
.

3.

For the kernel polynomials

K_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{P_{k}(x)P_{k}(y)}{\left\|P_{k}\right\|_{\mu}^{2}},

(1)

we have the Christoffel–Darboux identities

\displaystyle K_{n}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{P_{n+1}(x)P_{n}(y)-P_{n+1}(y)P_{n}(x)}{\|P_{n}\|_{\mu}^{2}\,(x-y)},&\hbox{if }\;x\neq y;\\
\frac{P_{n+1}^{\prime}(x)P_{n}(x)-P_{n+1}(x)P_{n}^{\prime}(x)}{\|P_{n}\|_{\mu}^{2}},&\hbox{if }\;x=y.\end{cases}

(2)

These identities play a fundamental role in the treatment of Fourier expansions with respect to a system of orthogonal polynomials (see
[
1
]
, Section 2.2). For a review of the use of (
1
) and (
2
) in the spectral theory of orthogonal polynomials, we refer the reader to
[
2
]
. In addition, see the usual references
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, for a basic background on these and other properties of
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
.

Let
(a,b)=\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\mathop{\rm supp}{\mu}\right)^{\mathrm{o}}\!
,
N,d_{j}\in\mathds{Z}_{+}
,
\lambda_{j,k}\geq 0
, for
j=1,2,\dots,N
,
k=0,1,\dots,d_{j}
,
\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{N}\}\subset\mathds{R}\!\setminus\!(a,b)
, where
c_{i}\neq c_{j}
if
i\neq j
and
I_{+}=\{(j,k):\lambda_{j,k}>0\}
. We consider the following
Sobolev-type (or discrete Sobolev) inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{{\mathsf{s}}}

\displaystyle=\!\int\!f(x)g(x)d\mu(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=0}^{d_{j}}\lambda_{j,k}f^{(k)}(c_{j})g^{(k)}(c_{j})

\displaystyle=\!\int\!f(x)g(x)d\mu(x)+\sum_{(j,k)\in I_{+}}\!\!\!\!\lambda_{j,k}f^{(k)}(c_{j})g^{(k)}(c_{j}),

(3)

where
f^{(k)}
denotes the
k
-th derivative of the function
f
. Without loss of generality, we also assume
\{(j,d_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset I_{+}
and
d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq\cdots\leq d_{N}
. For
n\in\mathds{Z}_{+}
, we shall denote by
S_{n}
the monic polynomial of the lowest degree satisfying

\langle x^{k},S_{n}\rangle_{{\mathsf{s}}}=0,\quad\text{for }\;k=0,1,\dots,n-1.

(4)

It is easy to see that for all
n\geq 0
, there exists such a unique polynomial
S_{n}
of degree
n
. This is deduced by solving a homogeneous linear system with
n
equations and
n+1
unknowns. Uniqueness follows from the minimality of the degree for the polynomial solution. We refer the reader to
[
6
,
7
]
for a review of this type of non-standard orthogonality.

Clearly, (
3
) is not standard, i.e.,
\langle xp,q\rangle_{{\mathsf{s}}}\neq\langle p,xq\rangle_{{\mathsf{s}}}
, for some
p,q\in\mathds{P}
.
It is well known that the properties of orthogonal polynomials with respect to standard inner products differ from those of the Sobolev-type polynomials. In particular, the zeros of the Sobolev-type polynomials can be complex, or if real, they can be located outside the convex hull of the support of the measure
\mu
, as can be seen in the following example.

Example 1

(Zeros outside the convex hull of the measures supports)
.

Let

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{{\mathsf{s}}}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)g(x)e^{-x}dx+2f^{\prime}(-1)g^{\prime}(-1),

then the corresponding second-degree monic Sobolev-type orthogonal polynomial is
S_{2}(z)=z^{2}-2
, whose zeros are
z_{1,2}=\pm\sqrt{2}
. Note that
-\sqrt{2}\not\in[-1,\infty)
.

Let
\{Q_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of monic orthogonal polynomials with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu_{\rho}}=

\displaystyle\int f(x)\;g(x)\;d\mu_{\rho}(x),\;\text{ where }\;\rho(x)=\prod_{c_{j}\leq a}\left(x-c_{j}\right)^{d_{j}+1}\!\!\prod_{c_{j}\geq b}\left(c_{j}-x\right)^{d_{j}+1}

\displaystyle\text{ and }\;d\mu_{\rho}(x)=\rho(x)d\mu(x).

Note that
\rho
is a polynomial of degree
d=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(d_{j}+1)
, which is positive on
(a,b)
. If
n>d
, from (
4
),
\{S_{n}\}
satisfies the following quasi-orthogonality relations with respect to
\mu_{\rho}

\langle S_{n},f\rangle_{\mu_{\rho}}=\langle S_{n},\rho f\rangle_{\mu}=\int S_{n}(x)f(x)\rho(x)d\mu(x)=\langle S_{n},\rho f\rangle_{{\mathsf{s}}}=0,

for
f\in\mathds{P}_{n-d-1}
, where
\mathds{P}_{n}
is the linear space of polynomials with real coefficients and the degree at most
n\in\mathds{Z}_{+}
. Hence,
the polynomial
S_{n}
is quasi-orthogonal of order
d
with respect to
\mu_{\rho}
and by this argument, we obtain that
S_{n}
has at least
(n-d)
changes of sign in
(a,b)
.

The results obtained for measures
\mu
with bounded support (see
[
8
]
, (1.10)) suggest that the number of zeros located in the interior of the support of the measure is closely related to
d^{*}=|I_{+}|
, the number of terms in the discrete part of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathsf{s}}}
(i.e.,
\lambda_{j,k}>0
), instead of this greater quantity
d
.

Our first result, Theorem
1
, goes in this direction for the case when the inner product is sequentially ordered. This kind of inner product is introduced in Section
2
(see
Definition
2.1
).

Theorem 1
.

If the discrete Sobolev inner product (
3
) is sequentially ordered, then
S_{n}
has at least
n-d^{*}
changes of sign on
(a,b)
, where
d^{*}
is the number of positive coefficients
\lambda_{j,k}
in (
3
).

Previously, this result was obtained for more restricted cases in (
[
9
]
, Th. 2.2) and (
[
10
]
, Th. 1). In (
[
9
]
, Th. 2.2), the authors proved this result for the case
N=1
. In (
[
10
]
, Th. 1), the notion of a sequentially ordered inner product is more restrictive than here, because it did not include the case when the Sobolev inner product has more than one derivative order at the same mass point.

In the second part of this paper, we focus our attention on the Laguerre–Sobolev-type polynomials (i.e.,
d\mu=x^{\alpha}e^{-x}dx
, with
\alpha>-1
). In the case of the inner product, (
3
) takes the form

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{{\mathsf{s}}}=\!\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)g(x)x^{\alpha}e^{-x}dx+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}f^{(d_{j})}(c_{j})g^{(d_{j})}(c_{j}),

(5)

where
\lambda_{j}:=\lambda_{j,d_{j}}>0
,
c_{j}<0
, for
j=1,2,\dots,N
, we obtain the outer relative asymptotic of the Laguerre–Sobolev-type polynomials.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\{L^{\alpha}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of monic Laguerre polynomials and let
\{S_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the monic orthogonal polynomials with respect to the inner product (
5
). Then,

\displaystyle\frac{S_{n}(x)}{\left.L_{n}^{\alpha}\right.(x)}\rightrightarrows\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\sqrt{-x}-\sqrt{|c_{j}|}}{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{|c_{j}|}}\right),\quad K\subset\overline{\mathds{C}}\setminus\mathds{R}_{+}.

(6)

Throughout this paper, we use the notation
\displaystyle f_{n}\rightrightarrows f,\;K\subset\mathds{U}
when the sequence of functions
f_{n}
converges to
f
uniformly on every compact subset
K
of the region
\mathds{U}
.

Combining this result with Theorem
1
, we obtain that the Sobolev polynomials
S_{n}
, orthogonal with respect to a sequentially ordered inner product in the form (
5
), have at least
n\text{--}N
zeros in
(0,\infty)
and, for sufficiently large
n
, each one of the other
N
zeros are contained in a neighborhood of each mass point
c_{j}
(
j=1,\dots,N
). Then, we have located all zeros of
S_{n}
and we obtain that for a sufficiently large
n
, they are simple and real, as in the Krall case (see
[
11
]
) or the Krall–Laguerre-type orthogonal polynomial (see
[
12
]
). This is summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
\mu=\mu_{\alpha}
be the classical Laguerre measure (
d\mu_{\alpha}(x)=x^{\alpha}e^{-x}dx
) and (
5
) a sequentially ordered discrete Sobolev inner product. Then, the following statements hold:

1.

Every point
c_{j}
attracts exactly one zero of
S_{n}
for sufficiently large
n
, while the remaining
n\text{--}N
zeros are contained in
(0,\infty)
.
This means:

For every
r>0
, there exists a natural value
\mathcal{N}
such that if
n\geq\mathcal{N}
, then the
n
zeros of
S_{n}

\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
satisfy

\displaystyle\xi_{j}\in B(c_{j},r)\ \text{ for }j=1,\dots,N

\displaystyle{and}

\displaystyle\xi_{i}\in(0,\infty)\text{ for }i=N+1,N+2,\dots,n.

2.

The zeros of
S_{n}
are real and simple for large-enough values of
n
.

3.

The zeros of
\{S_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
are at a finite distance from
(0,\infty)
. This means that there exists a positive constant
M
such that if
\xi
is a zero of
S_{n}
, then

\displaystyle d(\xi,(0,\infty)):=\inf_{x>0}\{|x-\xi|\}<M.

Section
2
is devoted to introducing the notion of a sequentially ordered Sobolev inner product and to prove Theorem
1
. In Section
3
, we summarize some auxiliary properties of Laguerre polynomials to be used in the proof of Theorem
2
. Some results about the asymptotic behavior of the reproducing kernels are given. The aim of the last section is to prove Theorem
2
and some of its consequences stated in Corollary
2.2
.

2
Sequentially Ordered Inner Product

Definition 2.1

(Sequentially ordered Sobolev inner product)
.

Consider a discrete Sobolev inner product in the general form (
3
) and assume
d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq\dots\leq d_{N}
without loss of generality. We say that a discrete Sobolev inner product is
sequentially ordered
if the conditions

\Delta_{k}\cap\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\cup_{i=0}^{k-1}\Delta_{i}\right)^{\mathrm{o}}\!=\emptyset,\quad\quad k=1,2,\dots,d_{N},

hold, where

\Delta_{k}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\mathop{\rm supp}{\mu}\cup\{c_{j}:\lambda_{j,0}>0\}\right),&\hbox{if }\;k=0;\\
\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\{c_{j}:\lambda_{j,k}>0\}\right),&\hbox{if }\;1\leq k\leq d_{N}.\end{cases}

(7)

Note that
\Delta_{k}
is the convex hull of the support of the measure associated with the
k
-th order derivative in the Sobolev inner product (
3
). Let us see two examples.

Example 2

(Sequentially ordered inner product)
.

Let

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{{\mathsf{s}}}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)g(x)e^{-x}dx+10f(-1)g(-1)+5f^{\prime}(-3)g^{\prime}(-3)

\displaystyle+5f^{\prime}(-9)g^{\prime}(-9)+20f^{\prime\prime\prime}(-10)g^{\prime\prime\prime}(-10),

then the corresponding fifth-degree Sobolev orthogonal polynomial has the following exact expression

\displaystyle S_{5}(x)=

\displaystyle x^{5}+\frac{380961336355365}{16894750106161}x^{4}+\frac{1836311881214045}{16894750106161}x^{3}-\frac{7830454972601355}{16894750106161}x^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{36972053870326650}{16894750106161}x-\frac{22386262325875230}{16894750106161},

whose zeros are approximately
\xi_{1}\approx 4.46
,
\xi_{2}\approx-0.74
,
\xi_{3}\approx-2.8
,
\xi_{4}\approx-11.74+2.51i
and
\xi_{5}\approx-11.74-2.51i
. Note that four of them are outside of
(0,\infty)
and two are even complex.

Example 3

(Non-sequentially ordered inner product)
.

Let

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)g(x)\,e^{-x}dx+f^{\prime}(-15)g^{\prime}(-15)+f^{\prime\prime}(-9)g^{\prime\prime}(-9),

then the corresponding fifth-degree Sobolev orthogonal polynomial has the following exact expression

\displaystyle S_{5}(x)=

\displaystyle x^{5}+\frac{55079160}{21682477}x^{4}-\frac{5053767275}{21682477}x^{3}+\frac{40953207555}{21682477}x^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{98030649090}{21682477}x+\frac{42523040550}{21682477},

whose zeros are approximately
\xi_{1}\approx 0.55
,
\xi_{2}\approx 3.36
,
\xi_{3}\approx 6.66+3.02i
,
\xi_{4}\approx 6.66-3.02i
and
\xi_{5}\approx-19.77
. Note that, in spite of Theorem
1
,
d^{*}=2
and three of the zeros of
S_{5}
are outside of
(0,\infty)
, with two of them as not even real.

In the sequentially ordered example (Example
2
),
S_{5}
has exactly
1=5-4=n-d^{*}
simple zeros on the interior of the convex hull of the support of the Laguerre measure
(0,\infty)
, and thus, the bound of Theorem
1
is sharp. In addition, this example shows that the remaining
d^{*}
zeros might even be complex, although Corollary
2.1
shows that this does not happen when
n
is sufficiently large.

On the other hand, in the non-sequentially ordered example (Example
3
), this condition is not satisfied, since
S_{5}
has only
2<3=5-2=n-d^{*}
zeros on
(0,\infty)
, showing that the sequential order plays a main role in the localization of the zeros of
S_{n}
, at least to obtain this property for every value of
n
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\{I_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m}
be a set of
m+1
intervals on the real line and let
P
be a polynomial with real coefficients of degree
\geq m
. If

\displaystyle I_{k}\cap\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\cup_{i=0}^{k-1}I_{i}\right)^{\mathrm{o}}\!=\emptyset,\quad\quad k=1,2,\dots,m,

(8)

then

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{z}\!\left(P;J\right)+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P;I_{0}\setminus J\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{(i)};I_{i}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{N}_{z}\!\left(P^{(m)};J\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{(m)};\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\cup_{i=0}^{m}I_{i}\right)\setminus J\right)+m,

(9)

for every closed subinterval
J
of
I_{0}^{\mathrm{o}}\!
(both empty set and unitary sets are assumed to be intervals). Here, given a real set
A
and a polynomial
P
,
\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P;A\right)
denotes the number of values where the polynomial
P
vanishes on
A
(i.e., zeros of
P
on
A
without counting multiplicities), and
\mathbf{N}_{z}\!\left(P;A\right)
denotes the total number of zeros (counting multiplicities) of
P
on
A
.

Proof.

First, we point out the following consequence of Rolle’s Theorem. If
I
is a real interval and
J
is a closed subinterval of
I^{\mathrm{o}}\!
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{z}\!\left(P;J\right)+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P;I\setminus J\right)\leq\mathbf{N}_{z}\!\left(P^{\prime};J\right)+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{\prime};I^{\mathrm{o}}\!\setminus J\right)+1.

(10)

It is easy to see that (
9
) holds for
m=0
. We now proceed by induction on
m
. Suppose that we have
m+1
intervals
\{I_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m}
satisfying (
8
); thus, the first
m
intervals
\{I_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m-1}
also satisfy (
8
), and we obtain (
9
) by induction hypothesis (taking
m-1
instead of
m
). Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{z}\!\left(P;J\right)+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P;I_{0}\setminus J\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{(i)};I_{i}\right),

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{N}_{z}\!\left(P^{(m-1)};J\right)+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{(m-1)};\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\cup_{i=0}^{m-1}I_{i}\right)\setminus J\right)+m-1+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{(m)};I_{m}\right),

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{N}_{z}\!\left(P^{(m)};J\right)+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{(m)};\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\cup_{i=0}^{m-1}I_{i}\right)^{\mathrm{o}}\!\setminus J\right)+m+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{(m)};I_{m}\right),

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{N}_{z}\!\left(P^{(m)};J\right)+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{(m)};\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\cup_{i=0}^{m}I_{i}\right)\setminus J\right)+m,

where in the second inequality, we have used (
10
).
∎

As an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.1
, the following result is obtained.

Lemma 2.2
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}_{z}\!\left(P;J\right)+\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P;I_{0}\setminus J\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{(i)};I_{i}\right)\leq\deg{P}

(11)

for every
J
closed subinterval of
I_{0}^{\mathrm{o}}\!
. In particular, for
J=\emptyset
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathbf{N}_{o}\!\left(P^{(i)};I_{i}\right)\leq\deg{P}.

(12)

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\{(r_{i},\nu_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}\subset\mathds{R}\times\mathds{Z}_{+}
be a set of
M
ordered pairs. Then, there exists a unique monic polynomial
U_{M}
of minimal degree (with
0\leq\deg{U_{M}}\leq M
), such that

\displaystyle U_{M}^{(\nu_{i})}(r_{i})=0,\quad i=1,2,\dots,M.

(13)

Furthermore, if the intervals
I_{k}=\mathbf{Co}\!\left(\{r_{i}:\nu_{i}=k\}\right)
,
k=0,1,2,\dots,\nu_{M}
, satisfy (
8
), then

U_{M}
has degree
\mathfrak{u}_{M}=\min\mathfrak{I}_{M}-1
, where

\mathfrak{I}_{M}=\{i:1\leq i\leq M\text{ and }\nu_{i}\geq i\}\cup\{M+1\}.

Proof.

The existence of a nonidentical zero polynomial with degree
\leq M
satisfying (
13
) reduces to solving a homogeneous linear system with
M
equations and
M+1
unknowns (its coefficients). Thus, a non-trivial solution always exists. In addition, if we suppose that there exist two different minimal monic polynomials
U_{M}
and
\widetilde{U}_{M}
, then the polynomial
\widehat{U}_{M}=U_{M}-\widetilde{U}_{M}
is not identically zero, it satisfies (
13

\deg{\widehat{U}_{M}}<\deg{U_{M}}
. Thus, if we divide
\widehat{U}_{M}
by its leading coefficient, we reach a contradiction.

The rest of the proof runs by induction on the number of points
M
. For
M=1
, the result follows taking

U_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}x-r_{1},&\hbox{if }\;\nu_{1}=0;\\
1,&\hbox{if }\;\nu_{1}\geq 1.\end{cases}

Suppose that, for each sequentially ordered sequence of
M
ordered pairs, the corresponding minimal polynomial
U_{M}
has degree
\mathfrak{u}_{M}
.

Let
\{(r_{i},\nu_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}
be a set of
M

8
). Obviously,
\{(r_{i},\nu_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M-1}
also satisfies (
8
) and

satisfies (

) for

; thus, we obtain
\deg{U_{M-1}}=\mathfrak{u}_{M-1}
and
\deg{U_{M}}\geq\deg{U_{M-1}}
. Now, we divide the proof into two cases:

1.

If
\mathfrak{u}_{M}=M
, then for all

, which yields

\deg{U_{M}}\geq\deg{U_{M-1}}=\mathfrak{u}_{M-1}=M-1\geq\nu_{M}.

Since

satisfies (
8
), from (

Some of the structural properties of this family are listed in the following proposition in order to be used later.

Let

1.

For every

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(\alpha)}\left(x\right)=\frac{\left(-1\right)^{n}}{n!}L_{n}^{\alpha}\left(x\right).

(16)

2.

Three-term recurrence relation. For every

\displaystyle xL^{\alpha}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=L^{\alpha}_{n+1}(x)+(2n+\alpha+1)L^{\alpha}_{n}(x)+n(n+\alpha)L^{\alpha}_{n-1}(x)

3.

Structure relation. For every

4.

For every

||L_{n}^{(\alpha)}||_{\mu}^{2}=\Gamma(\alpha+1)\binom{n+\alpha}{n}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n+1)}{n!}.

(18)

Now, we will find the behavior of the coefficients

when
n
approaches infinity. If
k=j

\displaystyle a_{k,k}(n,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}+K_{n-1}^{(d_{k},d_{k})}\!(c_{k},c_{k})\right)}{\left(L^{(\alpha)}_{n}\right)^{(d_{k})}\!\!(c_{k})K_{n-1}^{(0,d_{k})}\!(x,c_{k})}

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}+\frac{L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n}(c_{k})L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n}(c_{k})}{n^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{-c_{k}}+\sqrt{-c_{k}}}\left((-1)^{d_{k}+d_{k}}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})\right)\right)}{(-1)^{d_{k}}L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n-d_{k}}(c_{k})\frac{L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n}(c_{k})L^{(\alpha)}_{n}(x)}{n^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{-c_{k}})}\left((-1)^{d_{k}}+\mathcal{O}_{x}(n^{-1/2})\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{-c_{k}}}{2\sqrt{-c_{k}}}\frac{\left(\frac{n^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}}{\lambda_{k}L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n}(c_{k})}+L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n}(c_{k})\left(1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})\right)\right)}{L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n-d_{k}}(c_{k})\left(1+\mathcal{O}_{x}(n^{-1/2})\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{-c_{k}}}{2\sqrt{-c_{k}}}\frac{\left(\frac{n^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}}{\lambda_{k}\left(L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n}(c_{k})\right)^{2}}+1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})\right)}{1+\mathcal{O}_{x}(n^{-1/2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{-c_{k}}}{2\sqrt{-c_{k}}}\frac{1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})}{1+\mathcal{O}_{x}(n^{-1/2})},

where in the last equality we use Perron’s Asymptotic Formula (

) to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{n^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}}{(L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n}(c_{k}))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi n^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}}{e^{c_{k}+4\sqrt{-c_{k}}\sqrt{n}}}\frac{(-c_{k})^{\alpha+d_{k}+\frac{1}{2}}}{n^{\alpha+d_{k}-\frac{1}{2}}}\mathcal{O}(1)=\frac{1}{n^{d_{k}}e^{4\sqrt{-c_{k}}\sqrt{n}}}\mathcal{O}(1),

which has exponential decay (
c_{k}<0
). On the other hand, if
k\neq j
, we obtain

\displaystyle a_{k,j}(n,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)K_{n-1}^{(d_{k},d_{j})}(c_{k},c_{j})}{\left(L^{(\alpha)}_{n}\right)^{(d_{k})}\!\!(c_{k})K_{n-1}^{(0,d_{j})}(x,c_{j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n}(c_{k})}{\sqrt{-c_{k}}+\sqrt{-c_{j}}}\left((-1)^{d_{k}+d_{j}}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})\right)}{(-1)^{d_{k}}\frac{L^{(\alpha+d_{k})}_{n-d_{k}}(c_{k})}{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{-c_{j}}}\left((-1)^{d_{j}}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{-c_{j}}}{\sqrt{-c_{k}}+\sqrt{-c_{j}}}\frac{\left(1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})\right)}{\left(1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})\right)}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{k,j}(n,x)=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{-c_{j}}}{\sqrt{-c_{k}}+\sqrt{-c_{j}}},\quad&\text{if }j\neq\penalty\ k\\
\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{-c_{k}}}{2\sqrt{-c_{k}}},\quad&\text{if }j=k\end{cases}=\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{|c_{j}|}}{\sqrt{|c_{k}|}+\sqrt{|c_{j}|}}.

Next, taking limits on both sides of (
4

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{|c_{1}|}}{\sqrt{|c_{1}|}+\sqrt{|c_{1}|}}&\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{|c_{2}|}}{\sqrt{|c_{1}|}+\sqrt{|c_{2}|}}&\cdots&\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{|c_{N}|}}{\sqrt{|c_{1}|}+\sqrt{|c_{N}|}}\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{|c_{1}|}}{\sqrt{|c_{N}|}+\sqrt{|c_{1}|}}&\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{|c_{2}|}}{\sqrt{|c_{N}|}+\sqrt{|c_{2}|}}&\cdots&\frac{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{|c_{N}|}}{\sqrt{|c_{N}|}+\sqrt{|c_{N}|}}\\

Using Cauchy determinants, it is not difficult to prove that the

\displaystyle P^{\alpha}_{j}(x)=\frac{-2\sqrt{|c_{j}|}}{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{|c_{j}|}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1\\

Now, from (

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\widehat{S}_{n}(x)}{L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)}=1+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2\sqrt{|c_{j}|}}{\sqrt{-x}+\sqrt{|c_{j}|}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}\left(\frac{\sqrt{|c_{j}|}+\sqrt{|c_{l}|}}{\sqrt{|c_{j}|}-\sqrt{|c_{l}|}}\right).

If we consider the change of variable

and for simplicity we also consider the notation

, then we obtain the following partial fraction decomposition

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{2t_{j}}{z+t_{j}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}\left(\frac{t_{j}+t_{l}}{t_{j}-t_{l}}\right).

Thus, we only have to prove that this is the partial fraction decomposition of

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{z-t_{j}}{z+t_{j}}\right).

Let
P_{N}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(z-t_{j})
and
Q_{N}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(z+t_{j})
, then

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{z-t_{j}}{z+t_{j}}\right)=\frac{P_{N}(z)}{Q_{N}(z)}=1+\frac{P_{N}(z)-Q_{N}(z)}{Q_{N}(z)}=1+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{A_{j}}{z+t_{j}},

where

\displaystyle A_{j}

\displaystyle=\lim_{z\to-t_{j}}(z+t_{j})\frac{P_{N}(z)-Q_{N}(z)}{Q_{N}(z)}=\frac{P_{N}(-t_{j})-Q_{N}(-t_{j})}{Q^{\prime}_{N}(-t_{j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{l=1}^{N}(-t_{j}-t_{l})-\prod_{l=1}^{N}(-t_{j}+t_{l})}{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}(-t_{j}+t_{l})}=\frac{(-1)^{N}2t_{j}}{(-1)^{N}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}\left(\frac{t_{j}+t_{l}}{t_{j}-t_{l}}\right)=2t_{j}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}\left(\frac{t_{j}+t_{l}}{t_{j}-t_{l}}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎
