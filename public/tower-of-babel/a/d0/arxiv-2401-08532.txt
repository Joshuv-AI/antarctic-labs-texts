Abstract
. We give a formula for the essential dimension of a cohomology class α \alpha in H d ​ ( K , ℚ p / ℤ p ​ ( d ) ) H^{d}(K,\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(d)) when K K is a strictly Henselian field. This formula is particularly explicit in the case, where α \alpha is a Brauer class (for d = 2 d=2 ). As an application of our bound with d = 3 d=3 , we study the essential dimension of exceptional groups by examining the image of the Rost invariant.

1.
Introduction

Let
k
be a base field and
k\subset K
be a field extension. Consider an algebraic object

\alpha
defined over
K
. That is,
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}(K)
is an object of a covariant functor
\mathcal{F}:\operatorname{Fields}_{k}\to\operatorname{Sets}
,
where
\operatorname{Fields}_{k}
is the category of field extensions
K/k
and
\operatorname{Sets}
is the category of sets.
We think of the functor
\mathcal{F}
as specifying the type of object under consideration
(a quadratic form, an associative algebra, a Lie algebra, etc.) and

\mathcal{F}(K)
as the set of isomorphism classes of objects this type defined over
K
.
The essential dimension
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)
of our object
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}(K)
is the minimal transcendence degree

\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}(K_{0})
of an intermediate field
k\subset K_{0}\subset K
to which
\alpha
descends.
Informally speaking,
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)
is the minimal number of parameters required to define
\alpha
.
The essential dimension
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\mathcal{F})
of the functor
\mathcal{F}
is then defined as the maximal value
of
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)
taken over all
L\in\operatorname{Fields}_{k}
and all

\alpha\in\mathcal{F}(L)
. These definitions are recalled in a more formal way in Section
2
.
For surveys of this research area, we refer the reader to
[
1
,
29
,
37
]
.

Much of the work on essential dimension has centered on the functor

(1.1)

H^{1}(\ast,G)\colon K\mapsto H^{1}(K,G).

Here
H^{1}(K,G)
denotes the non-abelian cohomology set, whose elements are isomorphism classes of (fppf)
G
-torsors
over
\operatorname{Spec}(K)
. The essential dimension of this functor is called the essential dimension of
G
and is denoted
by
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(G)
.

The main focus of this paper will be on a different functor,

H_{p}^{d}\colon K\mapsto H^{d}(K,\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(d)),

where
p\neq\operatorname{char}k
is a prime. We will study the essential dimension of objects of this functor (i.e., cohomology classes)
by valuation-theoretic methods, in the spirit of
[
26
, Section 3f]
or
[
30
]
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a valued field with value group
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. Assume
k\subset F
and
\nu
is trivial on
k
.
There is a canonical homomorphism

\wedge\nu:H^{d}_{p}(F)\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\otimes\bigwedge\mathbb{Z}^{r},

where
\bigwedge\mathbb{Z}^{r}
is the exterior algebra on
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. It is given, on symbols, by

\wedge\nu(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{p^{n}}=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes\nu(a_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge\nu(a_{d});

for details see Section
3
.

In Definition
6.1
, we associate to any
\omega\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\otimes\bigwedge\mathbb{Z}^{r}
,
a finite abelian subgroup
A_{\omega}\subset(\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{r}
. Given
\omega
, the group
A_{\omega}
is usually
easy to compute. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a valued field with value group
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. Assume
\nu
is trivial on a subfield
k\subset F
with
\operatorname{char}k\neq p
. Let
\alpha\in H^{d}_{p}(F)
and
\omega=\wedge\nu(\alpha)
. Then

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\geqslant\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(A_{\omega}/pA_{\omega}).

Moreover, if
(F,\nu)
is strictly Henselian, then equality holds:

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(A_{\omega}/pA_{\omega}).

Here
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)
is
the essential dimension of
\alpha
at
p
; see Section
2.1
.
Note that first inequality in the statement of Theorem
1.1
,
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)
,
is immediate from the definition of
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)
; see (
2.1
).
As a consequence of Theorem
1.1
we obtain the following:

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
p
be a prime and
k
be a field of characteristic
\neq p
. Then

(1.2)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(H^{d}_{p};p)=\infty

for any
d\geqslant 2
.

For a proof, see Section
7
.

Central simple algebras and Brauer classes

Let us now consider the functor (
1.1
) in the special case, where
G
is the projective linear group
\operatorname{PGL}_{n}
.
It is well known that the functor
H^{1}(*,\operatorname{PGL}_{n})
is isomorphic to

\operatorname{CSA}_{n}\colon K\mapsto\{\text{isomorphism classes of central simple algebras of degree $n$ over $K$}\}.

Computing the essential dimension of
\operatorname{CSA}_{n}
is a long-standing problem, going back to Procesi
[
35
, Section 2]
.
The deepest results to date have been about
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{CSA}_{n};p)
. Using primary decomposition
it is easy to see that
\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{CSA}_{n};p)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{CSA}_{p^{r}};p)
, where
p^{r}
is the highest power of
p
dividing
n
.
Thus we may assume without loss of generality that
n=p^{r}
.
When
r=1
, it is known that
\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{PGL}_{p};2)=2
; see,
e.g.,
[
39
, Lemma 8.5.7]
.
For
r\geqslant 2
, we have

(r-1)p^{r}+1\leqslant\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{PGL}_{p^{r}};p)\leqslant p^{2r-2}+1\,,

where the lower bound is due to Merkurjev
[
28
]
and the upper bound
is due to Ruozzi
[
41
]
. In particular,

(1.3)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{PGL}_{p^{2}})=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{PGL}_{p^{2}};p)=p^{2}+1;

see
[
27
]
.

Now consider the morphism of functors
\operatorname{CSA}_{n}\to\operatorname{Br}
taking a central simple algebra
A
to its Brauer class
[A]
.
If
A
is not split and
k
is algebraically closed, then Tsen’s Theorem tells us that

(1.4)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}([A];p)\geqslant 2.

We also have the obvious inequalities

(1.5)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}([A])\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(A)\quad\text{and}\operatorname{ed}_{k}([A];p)\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(A;p)\,.

The inequalities (
1.5
) may be sharp, because
the essential dimension of
\operatorname{M}_{s}(A)
may be strictly smaller than the essential dimension of
A
for some
s>1
.
In fact, this is exactly what happens when
A
is a universal division algebra of degree
4
. In this case (
1.3
)
tells us that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(A)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(A;2)=5
; on the other hand, by
[
22
, Corollary 1.4]
,
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{M}_{2}(A))=4
.

Using Theorem
1.1
, we can give lower bounds on the essential dimension of some Brauer classes that go beyond (
1.4
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
p
be a prime,
k
be a field containing a primitive root of unity of degree
p^{d}
for every
d\geqslant 1
.
Let
(F,\nu)
be a valued field with value group
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. Assume that
k\subset F
and
\nu_{|k}
is trivial. Let
\alpha\in\operatorname{Br}(F)
is a sum of Brauer classes of symbol algebras:

\alpha=(a_{1},b_{1})_{p^{n}}+\dots+(a_{r},b_{r})_{p^{n}},

for some integer
n\geqslant 1
. Consider the skew-symmetric matrix

M=\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\nu(a_{i})\nu(b_{i})^{t}-\nu(b_{i})\nu(a_{i})^{t}\in\operatorname{M}_{r}(\mathbb{Z}),

where we view
\nu(a_{i})
and
\nu(b_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
as
r\times 1
matrices with integer entries
and their transposes,
\nu(a_{i})^{t}
and
\nu(b_{i})^{t}
as
1\times r
matrices.
Let
d_{1}\mid d_{2}\mid\dots\mid d_{r}
be the elementary divisors of
M
and assume
i_{0}
is the largest subscript
such that
p^{n}
does not divide
d_{i_{0}}
. Then

(a)
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\geqslant i_{0}
, where
\alpha
is viewed as an object of the functor
\operatorname{Br}
.
In particular, if
p^{r}
does not divide
\det(M)
, then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)\geqslant r
.

(b) If
(F,\nu)
is strictly Henselian, then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)=i_{0}
.

The assumption that
\operatorname{char}k\neq p
is crucial here.
If
\operatorname{char}(k)=p
, then (
1.4
) is tight; see Proposition
9.2
.

As far as we know, Theorem
1.3
is the first known bound on
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)
that is stronger than (
1.4
).
There are some lower bounds in the literature on the essential dimension of Brauer classes considered as objects of
\operatorname{Br}_{p}
, where
\operatorname{Br}_{p}(K)
denotes the
p
-torsion subgroup of
\operatorname{Br}(K)
(see
[
23
]
and the last paragraph of Section 2 in
[
37
]
).
When a Brauer class
\alpha
of exponent
p
is considered as an object of
\operatorname{Br}_{p}
, it is only allowed to descend to Brauer classes of exponent
p
.
When we view
\alpha
as an object of
\operatorname{Br}
(as we do in the setting of Theorem
1.3
),
it is allowed to descend to a Brauer class of higher exponent, so a priori the essential dimension may drop.

Here

is any discrete
\operatorname{Gal}(k)
-module. We refer the reader to

for a detailed discussion of cohomological invariants.
We will assume that

[

As an easy consequence of the definition of essential dimension, we see that

(1.6)

\operatorname{ed}(G;p)\geqslant\operatorname{ed}(\gamma;p)\geqslant\operatorname{ed}(\eta(\gamma);p);

cf.
[

. Of particular interest to us will be the Rost invariant

Recall that the Rost invariant is defined for every semisimple simply connected group

;

]

\text{(i) \;}\operatorname{ed}_{k}(E_{7}^{\rm sc};2)\geqslant 7,\quad\quad\text{(ii) \;}\operatorname{ed}_{k}(E_{8};2)\geqslant 9,\quad\quad\text{(iii) \;}\operatorname{ed}_{k}(E_{8};3)\geqslant 5.

2.
Notation and preliminaries

2.1.
Essential dimension

Let
k
be a base field,
\operatorname{Fields}_{k}
be the category of field extensions
K/k
,
\operatorname{Sets}
be the category of sets, and
\mathcal{F}\colon\operatorname{Fields}_{k}\to\operatorname{Sets}
be a covariant functor.
Given a field extension
K/k
, we will say that
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}(K)

descends
to an intermediate field
k\subseteq K_{0}\subseteq K
if
\alpha
is in the image of the induced map
\mathcal{F}(K_{0})\to\mathcal{F}(K)
.
The essential dimension
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)
is the smallest integer
d
such that
\alpha
descends to a field
k\subset K_{0}\subset K
with
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}(K_{0})=d
.
The essential dimension
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)
of
\alpha
at a prime
p
is defined as the minimal value of
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha_{L})
,
where
L/K
ranges over field extensions whose degree
[L:K]
is finite and prime to
p
.

The essential dimension
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\mathcal{F})
(respectively, the essential
p
-dimension
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\mathcal{F};p)
)
of the functor
\mathcal{F}
is the supremum of
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)
(respectively,
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)
) taken over all
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}(K)
with
K
in
\operatorname{Fields}_{k}
. Clearly

(2.1)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\mathcal{F})\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\mathcal{F};p)

for every object
\alpha
of
\mathcal{F}
and every prime
p
.

We also remark that if
f\colon\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{G}
is a morphism of functors from
\operatorname{Fields}_{k}
to
\operatorname{Sets}
, then

(2.2)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(f(\alpha))\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(f(\alpha);p)

for every object
\alpha
of
\mathcal{F}
; cf.
[
37
, Lemma 2.2]
.
If
k\subset k^{\prime}\subset L\subset L^{\prime}
are fields with
k^{\prime}/k
algebraic and
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}(L)
, then

(2.3)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha_{L^{\prime}};p)\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)=\operatorname{ed}_{k^{\prime}}(\alpha;p).

Here the inequality on the left follows from the fact that any prime-to-
p
extension of
L
embeds into a prime-to-
p
extension of
L^{\prime}
, see
[
26
, Lemma 6.1]
. The equality on the right follows from the fact that

\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}(L_{0})=\operatorname{trdeg}_{k^{\prime}}(L_{0})
for any intermediate field
k\subset k^{\prime}\subset L_{0}\subset L^{\prime}
.

2.2.
The norm residue isomorphism

Let
F
be a field over
k
and let
p
be a prime different from
\operatorname{char}k
. For any integer
d\in\mathbb{N}
we set

\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(d):=\underset{r\in\mathbb{N}}{\operatorname{colim}}\ \mathbb{Z}/p^{r}(d);

see
[
32
, Definition 7.3.6]
.
Here the colimit is taken relative to the maps
\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}
given by multiplication by
{p^{s-r}}
, where
s\geqslant r\geqslant 0
. Note that
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(1)
is isomorphic to the
p
-primary part of the group of roots of unity in
k^{\operatorname{sep}}
.
Set

H_{p}(F):=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb{N}}H^{d}(F,\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(d)).

This abelian group is naturally graded and functorial in
F
. For any
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in F^{*}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the Kummer map gives cohomology classes
(a_{i})_{p^{n}}\in H^{1}(F,\mu_{p^{n}})\cong F^{*}/F^{*p^{n}}
. The cup product of these classes defines a class
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{p^{n}}\in H^{d}_{p}(F)
which is called a symbol of degree
d
. The norm residue isomorphism theorem gives a simple presentation for
H_{p}(F)
in terms of symbols.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
K(F)
denote the Milnor K-theory of a field
F
. For any prime
p
different from
\operatorname{char}F
, there is an isomorphism of abelian graded groups
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\otimes K(F)\overset{h}{\to}H_{p}(F)
given on generators by:

h(\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes\{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\})=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})_{p^{n}}

Theorem
2.1
is equivalent to the standard formulation of the norm residue isomorphism theorem. We include an explanation of the equivalence in the appendix.

Remark 2.2
.

The norm residue isomorphism theorem is notoriously difficult to prove for general fields. For strictly Henselian fields it is much simpler. In this setting it is an easy consequence of
[
8
, Theorem 2.6]
; see
[
48
, Corollary 3.13]
. To simplify the exposition, we will appeal to the norm residue theorem over general fields. Some readers may prefer to pass to the strict Henselization of a field before applying the norm residue isomorphism theorem. Doing this repeatedly will show that the proofs of our main results only require the norm residue isomorphism theorem over strictly Henselian fields.

2.3.
Notational conventions

Throughout this paper
k
will denote a base field and
k^{\rm sep}
will denote the separable closure of
k
.

Let
A
be an abelian group. For any positive integer
m
, we set
A/m:=A/mA
.
We will write elements of
A/m
as
a\mod m
, where
a\in A
.
If
p
is a prime, we will write

A/p^{\infty}:=\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}A=\operatorname{colim}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A/p^{n}.

We denote the
n
-th exterior product of
A
by
\bigwedge^{n}A
. When
A
is a finitely-generated free
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}/m
-module and
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
is a basis of
A
,
\bigwedge^{n}A
is also a free module generated by the “pure wedges”
e_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{i_{n}}
for some

1<i_{1}<\ldots<i_{n}\leqslant d
.

Note that there is a canonical isomorphism between
(\bigwedge^{n}A)/m
and
\bigwedge^{n}(A/m)
. We will identify these groups and simply write
\bigwedge^{n}A/m
.
The group
\bigwedge A/p^{\infty}
is
(\bigwedge A)/p^{\infty}
and not
\bigwedge(A/p^{\infty})
(the latter is the trivial group).

We fix a compatible system of roots of unity
\zeta_{m}\in k^{\operatorname{sep}}
for all
m
different from
\operatorname{char}(k)
.
That is,
\zeta_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{m_{1}}=\zeta_{m_{2}}
.
This is equivalent to fixing compatible
isomorphisms
\mu_{m}\to\mathbb{Z}/m(1)
, where
\mathbb{Z}/m(1)
is the Tate twist of
\mathbb{Z}/m
; see

[
32
, Definition 7.3.6]
.

A
valued field over

k
is a field
F
equipped with a valuation

\nu\colon F^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{r}
such that
k\subset F
,
\nu(k^{*})=0
. We will call
(F,\nu)

strictly Henselian
if it satisfies Hensel’s Lemma and its residue field is separably closed. We will need to consider the
Henselization
and the
strict Henselization
of a valued field. We refer the reader to
[
46
, Appendix A.3]
for the definition and properties of the Henselization. The strict Henselization of a valued field is the
inertial closure
of its Henselization (also called its maximal unramified extension)
[
46
, Definition A.20]
. We will frequently use the fact that passing to the (strict) Henselization of a valued field does not change the value group
[
46
, Corollary A.28]
.

An important example of a Henselian valuation is the
(t_{0},\dots,t_{n-1})
-adic valuation

\nu:F_{n}^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
on the field of iterated Laurent series
F_{n}=k((t_{0}))\dots((t_{n-1}))
, where

n
is a positive integer; see
[
46
, Example A.16]
.
Here
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is given the reverse lexicographic ordering with respect to the standard basis
e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1}
and

\nu(t_{i})=e_{i}
for any
0\leq i\leq n-1
. The residue field of
(F_{n},\nu)
is naturally identified with
k
. In particular,
F_{n}
is strictly Henselian if
k=k^{\operatorname{sep}}
.

We will denote the
d
-th Galois cohomology group of a
\operatorname{Gal}(F)
-module
M
by
H^{d}(F,M)
.

3.
The homomorphism
\wedge\nu

Let
(F,\nu)
be a valued field over
k
. That is,
F
contains the base field
k
and
\nu_{|k}=0
(remember that
\operatorname{char}k\neq p
).
Denote the value group
\nu F
by
\Gamma
.
Our first goal is to construct the homomorphism
H_{p}(F)\to\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}
.
Our starting point is the following homomorphism.

Proposition 3.1
.

There is a well-defined homomorphism of graded algebras:

\wedge^{\prime}\nu:K(F)\to\bigwedge\Gamma,\ \ \{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\}\mapsto\nu(a_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge\nu(a_{d}).

Our proof will make use of the fact that

(3.1)

If
\nu(a)>\nu(b)
, then
\nu(a+b)=\nu(b)
;

see, e.g.,
[
21
, p. 481]
.

Proof.

Since the function
(F^{*})^{d}\to\bigwedge^{d}\Gamma,\ (a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\mapsto\nu(a_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge\nu(a_{d})
is
\mathbb{Z}
-multilinear, it factors through the tensor product to give homomorphisms:

(F^{*})^{\otimes d}\to\bigwedge^{d}\Gamma,

for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
. To check that these homomorphisms through
K(F)
we need to verify
the Steinberg relation,

\nu(a)\wedge\nu(1-a)=0,

for every
a\in F^{*}
. Consider three cases.

Case 1. If
\nu(a)=0
, then
\nu(a)\wedge\nu(1-a)=0
by bilinearity.

Case 2: If
\nu(a)>0
, then
\nu(1-a)=\nu(1)=0
; see (
3.1
). Hence,
\nu(a)\wedge\nu(1-a)=0
.

Case 3. If
\nu(a)<0
then
\nu(1-a)=\nu(a)
by (
3.1
). In this case,
\nu(a)\wedge\nu(1-a)=0
by anti-symmetry.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

It is clear from the definition of
\wedge^{\prime}\nu
that it is functorial. For any extension of valued fields
(F,\nu)\subset(\tilde{F},\tilde{\nu})
and any element
\alpha\in K(F)
we have

\wedge^{\prime}(\tilde{\nu})(\alpha_{\tilde{F}})=\wedge^{\prime}\nu(\alpha),

under the natural identification of
\bigwedge\nu F
with a subring of
\bigwedge\tilde{\nu}\tilde{F}
.

Combining Proposition
3.1
with Theorem
2.1
, we obtain:

Corollary 3.3
.

There exists a well-defined homomorphism of graded abelian groups
\wedge\nu:H_{p}(F)\to\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}
given on symbols by:

\wedge\nu(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{p^{n}}=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes\nu(a_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge\nu(a_{d}).

4.
First properties of
\wedge\nu

Let
(F,\nu)
be a valued field over
k
as in the previous section.
In this section we will explore some basic properties of
\wedge\nu
. To this end, it is convenient to choose a uniformizing parameter for
\nu
.

Definition 4.1
.

A left inverse
\pi\colon\Gamma\to F^{*}
to
\nu\colon F^{*}\to\Gamma
will be called a
uniformizing parameter
.
Since the group operation in
\Gamma
is written additively while
F^{*}
is written multiplicatively, it will be convenient for us to use the exponential notation
\pi^{\gamma}
in place of
\pi(\gamma)
, for any

\gamma\in\Gamma
.

Note that a uniformizing parameter always exists because
\Gamma
is a free abelian group.
From now on we will fix a uniformizing parameter
\pi
for
\nu
. Since elements in
H^{1}(F,\mathbb{Z}/{p^{n}})
anti-commute for any
n
,
\pi
induces a left inverse
s_{\pi}:\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}\to H_{p}(F)
given on generators by:

s_{\pi}(\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes\gamma_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\gamma_{d})\mapsto(\pi^{\gamma_{1}},\dots,\pi^{\gamma_{d}})_{p^{n}}.

We call the image of
s_{\pi}
the group of
\pi
-monomial classes.

Definition 4.2
.

An element
\alpha\in H_{p}(F)
will be called
monomial
if it is of the form
\alpha=s_{\pi}(\omega)
for some uniformizing parameter
\pi
and class
\omega\in\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}
.

It is important to keep in mind that any information captured by
\wedge\nu
is contained in the subgroup of
\pi
-monomial classes, which is a small part of
H_{p}(F)
. Moreover,
\wedge\nu
allows us to split
H_{p}(F)
as a direct product of a divisible subgroup and the kernel of
\wedge\nu
. The following lemma gives a convenient generating set for this kernel.

Lemma 4.3
.

The kernel of
\wedge\nu:H_{p}^{d}(F)\to\bigwedge^{d}\Gamma/p^{\infty}
is generated by symbols
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{p^{n}}
with
\nu(a_{1})=0
.

Proof.

Let
U\subset H_{p}^{d}(F)
be the subgroup generated by symbols
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{p^{n}}
where
\nu(a_{1})=0
. It is clear that
\wedge\nu(U)=0
. Note that any symbol
s=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{p^{n}}\in H_{p}^{d}(F)
is equivalent to a
\pi
-monomial class modulo
U
. Indeed, if we denote:

u_{1}=(a_{1}\cdot\pi^{-\nu(a_{1})},a_{2},\dots,a_{d})_{p^{n}}\in U,

then by multi-multiplicativity:

s-u_{1}=(\pi^{\nu(a_{1})},a_{2},\dots,a_{d})_{p^{n}}\in U

Proceeding iteratively and using anti-symmetry we find elements
u_{2},\dots,u_{d}\in U
such that:

s-u_{1}-\dots-u_{d}=(\pi^{\nu(a_{1})},\dots,\pi^{\nu(a_{d})})_{p^{n}}.

Thus for any element
\alpha\in H_{p}^{d}(F)
mapped to
0
under
\wedge\nu
we can find an element
u\in U
such that
\alpha-u
is
\pi
-monomial. Since
\wedge\nu(\alpha)=\wedge\nu(u)=0
and
s_{\pi}
is a section of
\wedge\nu
this implies:

\alpha-u=s_{\pi}(\wedge\nu(\alpha-u))=0.

Therefore
\alpha\in U
, as we wanted to show.
∎

Let
(\hat{F},\nu)
be the strict Henselization of
(F,\nu)
. The passage to the Henselization may be viewed as a process of localization with respect to
\nu
. The next corollary shows
\wedge\nu
gives a local description of
H_{p}(F)
. It can be easily deduced from a theorem of Wadsworth together with the norm-residue isomorphism theorem for strictly Henselian fields
[
48
, Proposition 2.1]
. In
[
5
]
, Brussel used a similar description to study the Brauer group of a strictly Henselian field; see also
[
46
, Chapter 6]
.

Corollary 4.4
.

Denote by
H_{p}(\hat{F}/F)\subset H_{p}(F)
the subgroup of elements split by
\hat{F}
. There is an exact sequence:

0\to H_{p}(\hat{F}/F)\to H_{p}(F)\overset{\wedge\nu}{\to}\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}\to 0.

In particular, if
(F,\nu)
is strictly Henselian, then
\wedge\nu
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Since
(\hat{F},\nu)
has the same value group as
(F,\nu)
, one has for all
\alpha\in H(F)
:

\wedge\nu(\alpha)=\wedge\nu(\alpha_{\hat{F}}).

Therefore
H_{p}(\hat{F}/F)\subset\ker\wedge\nu
. For the reverse inclusion, it suffices to check that symbols of the form
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{p^{n}}
with
\nu(a_{1})=0
are split by
\hat{F}
by Lemma
4.3
. This follows from the fact that if
a\in\hat{F}
and
\nu(a)=0
, then
a
has an
{p^{n}}
-th root in
\hat{F}
. The residue class of
a
has a
{p^{n}}
-th root in the residue field of
\hat{F}
because it is separably closed. The residue field has the same characteristic as
k
because
\nu(k^{*})=0
and so this
{p^{n}}
-th root may be lifted to
\hat{F}
using Hensel’s lemma.
∎

5.
A lower bound on essential dimension

Now that we have constructed the invariant
\wedge\nu
, our goal is to show that if
\wedge\nu(\alpha)
is ”complicated enough” for
\alpha\in H_{p}(F)
, then
\alpha
cannot descend to a subfield
F_{0}\subset F
of small transcendence degree over
k
. The first step is to define a measure of complexity for elements of
\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}
.

Definition 5.1
.

The width
\rho(\omega)
of an element
\omega\in\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}
is defined as follows:

\rho(\omega)=\min\bigg\{\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}W\mid\ \begin{array}[]{cc}\text{Subgroups}\ W\subset\Gamma,\\
\text{such that }\omega\in\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}\end{array}\bigg\}.

This definition is clearly analogous to the definition of essential dimension. Our next proposition makes the analogy precise. In the next section we will explain how to compute
\rho(\omega)
in general.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a valued field with residue field
k
. Assume
\operatorname{char}k\neq p
. Then

(a)
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\beta;p)\geqslant\rho(\wedge\nu(\beta))
for any
\beta\in H_{p}(F)

(b) Moreover, if
\beta
is monomial (see Definition
4.2
), then

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\beta)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\beta;p)=\rho(\wedge(\beta))
.

Proof.

(a) Let
F\subset L
be a prime to
p
extension such that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\beta_{L})=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\beta;p)
. By Lemma
13.3
we can choose an extension
\tilde{\nu}
of
\nu
to
L
such that
[\tilde{\nu}L:\nu E]
is prime to
p
. By Lemma
13.4
for any subgroup
W\subset\tilde{\nu}L
we have:

(5.1)

\bigwedge W\cap\nu E/p^{\infty}=\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}.

This implies:

(5.2)

\rho(\wedge\nu(\beta))=\rho(\wedge\tilde{\nu}(\beta_{L})).

Assume that
\beta_{L}
descends to
k\subset L_{0}\subset L
. Then

\wedge\tilde{\nu}(\beta_{L})\in\bigwedge\tilde{\nu}L_{0}/p^{\infty}
, and (
5.2
) gives

(5.3)

\operatorname{rank}(\nu L_{0})\geqslant\rho(\wedge\tilde{\nu}(\beta_{L}))=\rho(\wedge\nu(\beta)).

Now recall that by
[
17
, Chapter XVII, Section 4, Theorem II]
,
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}(L_{0})\geqslant\operatorname{rank}(\nu L_{0})
;
see also
[
49
, Chapter VI, Theorem 3, Corollary 1]
,
[
30
, Theorem 3.1]
. Combining this with (
5.3
), we
obtain

\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}(L_{0})\geqslant\rho(\wedge\nu(\beta))
.
Therefore,
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\beta;p)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\beta_{L})\geqslant\rho(\wedge\nu(\beta))
.

(b) Now assume that
\beta
is monomial. Then there exists a uniformizer
\pi
of
\nu
and a class
\omega\in\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}
such that
s_{\pi}(\omega)=\beta
. By definition we can find a subgroup
W\subset\Gamma
such that
\omega\in\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}
and

\operatorname{rank}(W)=\rho(\wedge\nu(\beta)).

Choose a basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
of
W
and set
F_{0}=k(\pi^{e_{1}},\dots,\pi^{e_{r}})
. Since
\omega\in\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}
and
W=\tilde{\nu}F_{0}
, we have:

\beta=s_{\pi}(\omega)\in\operatorname{im}(H_{p}(F_{0})\to H_{p}(F)).

We conclude that if
\beta
is monomial, then

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\beta)\leqslant\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}(F_{0})\leqslant r=\rho(\wedge\nu(\beta)).

Combining this inequality with part (a) and remembering (
2.1
), we deduce part (b).
∎

6.
Computing
\rho

In this section we show
\rho
is relatively easy to compute using linear algebra. Our main tools are the contraction maps of
\bigwedge\Gamma
. Contraction is a way of “applying” an element of
\bigwedge\Gamma
to an element of
the dual group
\Gamma^{*}=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
that generalizes multiplying a vector by a matrix (this special case will play a key role in Section
8
).

Definition 6.1
.

For any
\eta\in\bigwedge\Gamma^{*}
, we will write
\iota_{\eta}:\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}\to\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}
for the tensor product
\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\otimes\iota^{\prime}_{\eta}
where
\iota^{\prime}_{\eta}:\bigwedge\Gamma\to\bigwedge\Gamma
is the usual contraction map from linear algebra, see
[
3
, Chapter III, p. 602]
. We recall the explicit formula for
\iota_{\eta}
because it will be used often. For any
f\in\Gamma^{*}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d}\in\Gamma
we have:

\iota_{f}(\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes\gamma_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\gamma_{d})=\sum_{i=1,\dots,d}\frac{f(\gamma_{i})(-1)^{i-1}}{p^{n}}\otimes(\gamma_{1}\wedge\dots\hat{\wedge\gamma_{i}\wedge}\dots\wedge\gamma_{d}).

Here
\hat{\gamma_{i}}
means ”omit
\gamma_{i}
”. This formula determines
\iota_{f}
uniquely by linearity together with the condition
\iota_{f}(\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes 1_{\bigwedge\Gamma})=0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For general
\eta\in\bigwedge\Gamma^{*}
,
\iota_{\eta}
is defined by linearity in
\eta
and by the formula:

\iota_{f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{d}}=\iota_{f_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\iota_{f_{d}}.

We use the convention
\iota_{1}(\cdot)=\operatorname{id}_{\bigwedge\Gamma}
, where
1=1_{\bigwedge\Gamma^{*}}
is the unit of
\bigwedge\Gamma^{*}
.
Note that for
\eta\in\bigwedge^{d}\Gamma^{*}
and
\omega\in\bigwedge^{t}\Gamma/p^{\infty}
we have
\iota_{\eta}(\omega)\in\bigwedge^{t-d}\Gamma/p^{\infty}
. In particular, if
d>t
, then
\iota_{\eta}(\omega)=0
.
For any
\omega\in\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}
, we denote by
A_{\omega}\subset\Gamma/p^{\infty}
the finite subgroup generated by degree
1
parts
[\iota_{\eta}(\omega)]_{1}
as we vary over
\eta\in\bigwedge\Gamma^{*}
.

The next proposition shows that we can compute
\rho(\omega)
using contractions.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\omega\in\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}
. The following hold:

(1)

If
W\subset\Gamma
is a subgroup such that
\omega\in\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}
, then
A_{\omega}\subset W/p^{\infty}
.

(2)

The minimal number of generators of
A_{\omega}
is
\rho(\omega)
.

(3)

\rho(\omega)=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}A_{\omega}/p
.

Proof.

(1) It is clear from the definition that for any
\eta\in\bigwedge\Gamma^{*}

and subgroup
W\subset\Gamma
:

\iota_{\eta}\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}\subset\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}.

Therefore if
\omega\in\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}
, then
\iota_{\eta}(\omega)\in\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}
. Letting

\eta
vary over
\bigwedge\Gamma^{*}
, we see that
A_{\omega}\subset W/p^{\infty}
.

(2) Assume that
\omega\in\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}
for some subgroup
W\subset\Gamma
.
By part (1),
A_{\omega}\subset W/p^{\infty}
. For some
n
, we have
p^{n}\omega=0
and so:

A_{\omega}\subset\frac{1}{p^{n}}\mathbb{Z}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\otimes W\cong W/p^{n}W.

Therefore
A_{\omega}
is generated by
\operatorname{rank}W
elements
[
46
, Proposition A.36]
. Taking
\operatorname{rank}W
to be minimal we get that
A_{\omega}
is generated by
\rho(\omega)
elements.

It remains to show that
A_{\omega}

cannot be generated by fewer than
\rho(\omega)
elements.
Suppose that
A_{\omega}
is generated by
m
elements. Our goal is to prove
\rho(\omega)\leqslant m
. Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\in\Gamma
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
be elements such that:

(6.1)

A_{\omega}=\langle\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes u_{i}\mid i=1,\dots,m\rangle.

Let
U
be the saturation of the subgroup generated by
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
in
\Gamma
. Then

(6.2)

\operatorname{rank}U\leqslant m.

Now, we use the fact that any saturated subgroup of
\Gamma
is a direct summand. Therefore we may assume that
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{r}
with basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
and
U=\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{s}\rangle
, where
s=\operatorname{rank}U
. There are integers
a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}
indexed by increasing sequences such that:

\omega=\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\dots<i_{d}\leqslant r}\frac{a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}}{p^{n}}\otimes e_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_{d}}.

Let
j_{1}<\dots<j_{d}
be a sequence such
j_{d}>s
. It suffices to show
a_{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}}
is divisible by
p^{n}
for any such sequence. Let
e^{1},\dots,e^{r}\in\Gamma^{*}
be the dual basis of
\Gamma^{*}
. We denote:

\eta=e^{j_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\hat{e^{j_{t}}}\wedge\dots\wedge e^{j_{d}},

where
\hat{e^{j_{t}}}
means “omit
e^{j_{t}}
”, and compute the degree
1
homogeneous part of
\iota_{\eta}(\omega)
:

\displaystyle[\iota_{\eta}(\omega)]_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\dots<i_{d}\leqslant r}\frac{a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}}{p^{n}}\otimes[\iota_{\eta}(e_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_{d}})]_{1}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{a_{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}}}{p^{n}}\otimes e_{j_{d}}+\sum_{i\neq j_{d}}\frac{b_{i}}{p^{n}}\otimes e_{i}.

for some
b_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
[\iota_{\eta}(\omega)]_{1}\in A_{\omega}
, (
6.1
) implies there exists
u\in U=\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{s}\rangle
such that:

[\iota_{\eta}(\omega)]_{1}=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes u.

We conclude that
a_{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}}
is divisible by
p^{n}
because
j_{d}>s
. This shows that
\omega\in\bigwedge U/p^{\infty}
. Together with (
6.2
) this gives
\rho(\omega)\leqslant s=\operatorname{rank}U\leqslant m
, as desired.

(3) Since
A_{\omega}
is an abelian
p
-group, the minimal number of generators of
A_{\omega}
equals

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(A_{\omega}/p)
.
∎

7.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2

Putting together the results of the previous two sections we are now able to prove our main result, Theorem
1.1
.
We restate it here in slightly greater generality.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a valued field over
k
with value group
\Gamma
. Assume
\operatorname{char}k\neq p
.
Let
\alpha\in H_{p}(F)
be a cohomology class,
\omega=\wedge\nu(\alpha)\in\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}
, and
A_{\omega}\subset\Gamma/p^{\infty}
be the subgroup associated to
\omega
; see Definition
6.1
. Then

(7.1)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\geqslant\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(A_{\omega}/pA_{\omega}).

Furthermore, if
\alpha
is monomial (see Definition
4.2
), then

(7.2)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(A_{\omega}/pA_{\omega}).

In particular, equality (
7.2
) holds whenever
(F,\nu)
is strictly Henselian.

Proof.

Both (
7.1
) and (
7.2
) follow immediately from Proposition
5.2
and Proposition
6.2
. If
(F,\nu)
is strictly Henselian, then every class is monomial (see Corollary
4.4
) and so (
7.2
) holds in that case.
∎

We now turn to the proof of Corollary
1.2
. We need to show that for any
d\geqslant 2
and any prime number

p
there exist objects of
H^{d}_{p}(*)
of arbitrarily large essential
p
-dimension. This follows from Proposition
7.2

below: just let
r\longrightarrow\infty
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
r\geqslant 1
and
d\geqslant 2
be integers.
Let

F_{rd}=k((a_{11}))\ldots((a_{1d}))((a_{21}))\ldots((a_{2d}))\ldots((a_{rd})),

be the field of iterated Laurent series in
rd
variables
a_{ij}
over
k
.
Consider the class
\alpha\in H_{p}^{d}(K)
given by

\alpha=(a_{11},\ldots,a_{1d})_{p^{n}}+\ldots+(a_{r1},\ldots,a_{rd})_{p^{n}}.

Then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)=rd
.

Proof.

Let
\nu:F_{rd}\to\mathbb{Z}^{rd}
be the
(a_{11},\dots,a_{rd})
-adic valuation on
F_{rd}
(see Section
2.3
) and recall that

\nu(a_{ij})=e_{ij}
for all
i,j
, where
e_{ij}
is the natural basis of
\mathbb{Z}^{rd}
.
We now evaluate
\omega=\wedge\nu(\alpha)
:

\omega=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes(e_{11}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{1d}+e_{21}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{2d}+\ldots+e_{r1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{rd}).

Denote by
e^{ij}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{rd})^{*}
the dual basis. That is, for all
i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}
:

e^{ij}(e_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=i^{\prime}\ \text{and }j=j^{\prime}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For any
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant r
let
\eta=e^{1j}\wedge\dots\wedge\hat{e^{ij}}\wedge\dots\wedge e^{dj}\in\bigwedge^{d-1}\Gamma^{*}
be the wedge product where we omit
e^{ij}
. We have:

\iota_{\eta}(\omega)=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes e_{ij}.

Therefore
\mathbb{Z}^{rd}/p^{n}\subset A_{\omega}
. The inclusion
A_{\omega}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{rd}/p^{n}
is checked easily using the definition of the contraction map. Therefore
A_{\omega}=\mathbb{Z}^{rd}/p^{n}
and
A_{\omega}/p\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}^{rd}
. Since
\alpha
is monomial, Theorem
7.1
gives
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)=rd
.
∎

8.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section we will deduce Theorem
1.3
from Theorem
7.1
. Let
(F,\nu)
be a valued field over
k
with value group
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. There is a natural identification of
\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{Z}^{r}/p^{\infty}
with the skew-symmetric matrices in
M_{r}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p})
by the homomorphism:

\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes u\wedge v\mapsto\frac{1}{p^{n}}(uv^{t}-vu^{t}).

Here we view
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
as
r\times 1
matrices (i.e. column vectors)
and their transposes,
u^{t}
and
v^{t}
as
1\times r
matrices (i.e., row vectors)
with integer entries. The products
uv^{t}
and
vu^{t}
are
r\times r
matrices.

Under the above identification, the contraction map is given by matrix multiplication. That is, if
\omega\in\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{Z}^{r}/p^{\infty}
is identified with the matrix
M\in M_{r}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p})
and we identify
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}^{r},\mathbb{Z})
with
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
using the dot product, then:

\iota_{(\cdot)}(\omega):\mathbb{Z}^{r}\to\bigwedge^{1}\Gamma/p^{\infty}=\mathbb{Z}^{r}/p^{\infty}=(\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{r}.

is given by
\iota_{v}(\omega)=Mv
. To see this, assume that
\omega=\frac{1}{p^{n}}u\wedge w
for some
u,w\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and calculate using Definition
6.1
:

Mv=\frac{1}{p^{n}}(uw^{t}-wu^{t})v=\frac{1}{p^{n}}(w^{t}v)u-\frac{1}{p^{n}}(u^{t}v)w=\iota_{v}(\omega).

We now proceed with the proof of Theorem
1.3
. In fact, we will prove a slightly stronger
assertion, Theorem
8.1
below. If
k
contains a primitive root of unity of degree
p^{d}
for every

d\geqslant 1
, then
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(2)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(1)
. For any field
F
containing
k
,

H_{p}^{2}(F)=H^{2}(F,\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(2))
is then isomorphic to the
p
-primary part of
\operatorname{Br}(F)
. Moreover, under this isomorphism
the symbols
(a,b)_{p^{n}}
correspond to Brauer classes of cyclic algebras; see
[
15
, Proposition 4.7.1]
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a valued field over
k
with value group
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and
\operatorname{char}k\neq p
. Assume
\alpha\in H_{p}^{2}(F)
is a sum of symbols,

\alpha=(a_{1},b_{1})_{p^{n}}+\dots+(a_{k},b_{k})_{p^{n}}.

Let
M\in M_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
be the following skew-symmetric matrix:

M=\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\nu(a_{i})\nu(b_{i})^{t}-\nu(b_{i})\nu(a_{i})^{t}.

Assume
d_{1}\mid d_{2}\mid\dots\mid d_{r}
are the elementary divisors of
M
and
i_{0}
is the largest subscript
such that
{p^{n}}
does not divide
d_{i_{0}}
. The following hold

(a)
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\geqslant i_{0}
. In particular, if
p^{r}
does not divide
\det(M)
, then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)\geqslant r
.

(b) If
(F,\nu)
is strictly Henselian, then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)=i_{0}
.

Proof.

Denote
\omega=\wedge\nu(\alpha)
. By Corollary
3.3
:

\omega=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes(\nu(a_{1})\wedge\nu(b_{1})+\dots+\nu(a_{k})\wedge\nu(b_{k})).

Under the identification of
\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{Z}^{r}/p^{\infty}
with skew-symmetric matrices in
M_{r}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p})
we have:

\omega=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\sum_{i=1,\dots,k}\nu(a_{i})\nu(b_{i})^{t}-\nu(b_{i})\nu(a_{i})^{t}=\frac{1}{p^{n}}M.

As explained above, we can identify
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}^{r},\mathbb{Z})
with
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
so that
\iota_{v}(\omega)
is given by matrix multiplication:

(8.1)

\iota_{v}(\omega)=\frac{1}{p^{n}}Mv\in(\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{r}.

Set

A_{\omega}=\langle\iota_{v}(\omega)\mid v\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\rangle\subset(\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{r}
,
as in Definition
6.1
.
Let
d_{1}\mid d_{2}\mid\dots\mid d_{r}
be the elementary divisors of
M\in M_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
and let
\displaystyle k_{i}:=\frac{p^{n}}{\gcd({p^{n}},d_{i})}
for each
i=1,\dots,r
. Using (
8.1
) and elementary group theory one sees that

A_{\omega}\simeq\mathbb{Z}/k_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/k_{r}.

Therefore
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(A_{\omega}/p)=i_{0}
, where
i_{0}
is the number of
k_{i}
’s that are different from
1
. Equivalently,
i_{0}
is the largest integer such that
{p^{n}}
does not divide
d_{i_{0}}
.

Now parts
(a)
and
(b)
follow from Theorem
7.1
.
∎

Example 8.2
.

Let
F_{2r}=k((a_{11}))((a_{12})),((a_{21})),(a_{22}))\ldots((a_{r1}))((a_{r2}))
, be the field of iterated Laurent series in
2r
variables
a_{ij}
. Assume that
k
contains a primitive root of unity of degree
p^{n}
.
By Proposition
7.2
, the Brauer class
[D]\in\operatorname{Br}(F)

of the division algebra
D=(a_{11},a_{12})_{p^{n}}\otimes\dots\otimes(a_{r1},a_{r2})_{p^{n}}

has essential dimension
2r
. This can also be seen from Theorem
1.3
. Indeed,
carrying out the algorithm outlined there, we see that in this case

M=\begin{pmatrix}B&0&\dots&0\\
0&B&\dots&0\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
0&0&\dots&B\end{pmatrix}.

Here each entry represents a
2\times 2
block:
0
stands for the
2\times 2
zero matrix, and

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}
. The diagonal
2\times 2
block
B
appears
r
times.
Clearly,
\det(M)=\det(B)^{r}=1
and Theorem
1.3
tells us that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}\,[D]=2r
.

Corollary 8.3
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a strictly Henselian valued field over a field
k
with
\operatorname{char}k\neq p
. Then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha,p)
is even for any a Brauer class
\alpha\in\operatorname{Br}(F)
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem

because non-zero elementary divisors of an anti-symmetric matrix have even multiplicity; see

9.
Some limitations of Theorem
1.3

The following proposition shows that Theorem
1.3

cannot be used to prove a lower bound greater than
2n
on the essential dimension of a Brauer class
of index
p^{n}
. This is because
\wedge\nu
is unaffected by passing to the strict Henselization; see Remark
3.2
.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a strictly Henselian valued field over
k
with value group
\Gamma\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. If
\alpha\in H^{2}_{p}(F)

is split by a field extension
L/F
of degree
p^{n}
, then

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)\leqslant 2n.

Proof.

By Chevalley’s extension theorem
[
9
, Theorem 3.1.1]
, we can extend
\nu
to a valuation
\tilde{\nu}\colon L^{*}\to\tilde{\Gamma}
. By Ostrowski’s theorem
[
46
, Theorem A.12]
,

[\tilde{\Gamma}:\Gamma]\mid[L:F]=p^{n}.

By the Elementary Divisors Theorem
[
21
, Theorem III.7.8]
, we there exists a basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
of
\tilde{\Gamma}
and integers
d_{1},\dots,d_{r}
such that
d_{1}e_{1},\dots,d_{r}e_{r}
is a basis for
\Gamma
and
\prod_{i}d_{i}=[\tilde{\Gamma}:\Gamma]\mid p^{n}
.
Thus each
d_{i}
is
\pm
a power of
p
, and we assume without loss of generality that
d_{i}=\pm 1
for all
i>n
. For some
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
we have:

\wedge\nu(\alpha)=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant r}a_{ij}\otimes(d_{i}e_{i}\wedge d_{j}e_{j}).

Since
\alpha_{L}=0
,
\wedge\nu(\alpha)
is in the kernel of the homomorphism:

\phi:\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}\to\bigwedge\tilde{\Gamma}/p^{\infty}.

Since
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
is a basis of
\tilde{\Gamma}
,
\phi(\wedge\nu(\alpha))=0
implies for all
i<j
:

\phi(a_{ij}\otimes d_{i}e_{i}\wedge d_{j}e_{j})=a_{ij}d_{i}d_{j}\otimes(e_{i}\wedge e_{j})=0

We conclude that
a_{ij}=0
for all
n<i<j\leqslant r
because
d_{i},d_{j}=\pm 1
. Assume that
\displaystyle a_{ij}=\frac{b_{ij}}{p^{N}}
for some integers
b_{ij}
. Then

\wedge\nu(\alpha)=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\frac{1}{p^{N}}\otimes d_{i}e_{i}\wedge v_{i},

where
v_{i}=\sum_{i<j\leqslant r}b_{ij}d_{j}e_{j}
. Let
W\subset\Gamma
be the subgroup generated by
d_{1}e_{1},\dots,d_{n}e_{n}
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
. Since
\wedge\nu(\alpha)\in\bigwedge W/p^{\infty}
, Theorem
7.1
tells us that

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)=\rho(\wedge\nu(\alpha))\leqslant 2n
.
∎

We will now show that the assumption that
\operatorname{char}(k)\neq p
in the statement of Theorem
8.1
cannot be dropped.

Proposition 9.2
.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic
p
. Then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{Br};p)=2
.

Proof.

First we will show that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{Br};p)\leqslant 2
. In other words,
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\leqslant 2
for
every field
K
containing
k
and every
\alpha\in\operatorname{Br}(K)
. By the Primary Decomposition Theorem,
we can write
\alpha=\alpha_{p}+\beta
, where the index of
\alpha_{p}
is a power of
p
,
and the index of
\beta
is prime to
p
. Let
K^{\prime}/K
be a finite prime-to-
p
extension which splits
\beta
.
Then
\alpha_{K^{\prime}}=(\alpha_{p})_{K^{\prime}}
, and
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha_{K^{\prime}};p)
.
Here the last equality is an easy consequence of the definition
of
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)
; see, e.g., the proof of
[
26
, Proposition 1.5(2)]
.
After replacing
K
by
K^{\prime}
and
\alpha
by
\alpha_{K^{\prime}}
, we may assume without loss of generality
that the index of
\alpha
is a power of
p
. By a Theorem of Albert,
\alpha
is represented by a cyclic algebra over
K
;
see
[
15
, Theorem 9.1.8]
. In other words, it lies in the image of the natural (functorial) morphism

j_{r}\colon H^{1}(K,\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z})\times H^{0}(K,\mathbb{G}_{m})\to\operatorname{Br}(K)

for some integer
r\geqslant 1
; see
[
15
, p. 260]
. Recall that
H^{1}(K,\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z})
is in a functorial
bijective correspondence with isomorphism classes of cyclic étale algebras
L/K
of dimension
p^{r}
and
H^{0}(K,\mathbb{G}_{m})=K^{*}
.
If
\tau
is a generator of the (cyclic) Galois group of
L/K
, then
j_{r}([L/K],[b])
is the cyclic algebra generated by
L
and
u
,
subject to relations
ua=\tau(a)u
and
u^{p^{r}}=b
. Here
[L/K]
is the class of
L/K
in
H^{1}(K,\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z})
and

[b]
is the class of
b
in
H^{0}(K,\mathbb{G}_{m})
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{ed}(\alpha;p)

\displaystyle\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(H^{1}(\ast,\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z})\times H^{0}(K,\mathbb{G}_{m});\,p)

\displaystyle\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(H^{1}(\ast,\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z});\,p)+\operatorname{ed}_{k}(H^{0}(\ast,\mathbb{G}_{m});\,p)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z};\,p)+\operatorname{ed}_{k}(H^{0}(\ast,\mathbb{G}_{m});\,p).

Here the first inequality follows from the fact that
\alpha
lies in the image of
j_{r}
and the second
from
[
29
, Proposition 2.2]
. The equality on the third line follows from the definition of
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z};\,p)
.
Now observe that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z};\,p)=1
by
[
38
, Theorem 1]
. On the other hand,

\operatorname{ed}(H^{0}(\ast,\mathbb{G}_{m});\,p)\leqslant\operatorname{ed}(H^{0}(\ast,\mathbb{G}_{m}))\leq 1.

Here the first inequality is a special case of (
2.1
),
and the second follows from the fact that an object
b\in H^{0}(K,\mathbb{G}_{m})
descends to
k(b)
.
We conclude that
\operatorname{ed}(\alpha;p)\leqslant 1+1=2
, as desired.

It remains to show that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{Br};p)\geqslant 2
. Assume the contrary. Then

\operatorname{ed}_{\overline{k}}(\operatorname{Br};p)\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{Br};p)\leqslant 1,

where
\overline{k}
is the algebraic closure of
k
;
see, e.g.,
[
26
, Proposition 1.5(2)]
.
After replacing
k
by
\overline{k}
, we may assume that
k
is algebraically closed.
Choose a class
\alpha\in\operatorname{Br}(K)
of index
p
for some field
K
containing
k

(for example, the class of a universal division algebra of degree
p
).
By our assumption
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\leqslant 1
, i.e., there exists a prime-to-
p

field extension
L/K
such that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha_{L})\leqslant 1
. In other words,

\alpha
descends to
\alpha_{0}\in\operatorname{Br}(L_{0})
for some intermediate field

k\subset L_{0}\subset L
such that
\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}(L_{0})\leqslant 1
.
By Tsen’s Theorem,
\operatorname{Br}(L_{0})=0
; see
[
15
, Lemma 6.2.3 and Proposition 6.2.8]
.
We conclude that
\alpha_{L_{0}}=0
and thus
\alpha_{L}=0
in
\operatorname{Br}(L)
, i.e.,
L/K

splits
\alpha
. This is a contradiction, because
\alpha
has index
p
over
K
, and

[L:K]
is prime to
p
. Thus
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{Br};p)\geqslant 2
.
∎

A further consequence of Theorem
1.1

Proposition 10.1
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a strictly Henselian valued field over
k
. Let
\alpha\in H^{d}_{p}(F)
for some
p\neq\operatorname{char}k
. The following holds:

(1)

\alpha\neq 0
if and only if
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\geqslant d
.

(2)

\alpha
is not a symbol if and only
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)\geqslant d+2
.

In particular,
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)
can never be equal to
d+1
.

Proof.

Set
\omega:=\wedge\nu(\alpha)\in\bigwedge^{d}\nu F/p^{\infty}
. By Theorem
7.1
,

(10.1)

\rho(\omega)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p).

Choose a subgroup
W\subset\nu F
of rank
\rho(\omega)
such that
\omega\in\bigwedge^{d}W/p^{\infty}
.

(1)
If
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)<d
then
\operatorname{rank}W=\rho(\omega)<d
and thus

\omega\in\bigwedge^{d}W/p^{\infty}=0
.
Now Corollary
4.4
tells us that
\alpha=0
.

(2)
If
\alpha=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{p}
is a symbol, then
\alpha
descends to
k(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
and so
d\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)
.

Now assume that
d+1\geqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)
. Our goal is to show that
\alpha
is a symbol. By Corollary
4.4
, it suffices to show
\omega
is a pure tensor. By (
10.1
), we may enlarge
W
to assume
\operatorname{rank}W=d+1
and
\omega\in\bigwedge^{d}W/p^{\infty}
. Since
\bigwedge^{d}W/p^{\infty}
is generated by pure tensors it suffices to show the sum of two pure tensors is a pure tensor. A pure tensor in
\bigwedge^{d}W/p^{\infty}
is of the form:

\eta=\frac{a}{p^{n}}\otimes u_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{d},

for some
u_{1},\dots,u_{d}\in W
. Let
U=\langle u_{1},\dots,u_{d}\rangle
and denote by
U^{\operatorname{sat}}
its saturation in
W
. If
u^{\prime}_{1},\dots,u^{\prime}_{d}
are a basis of
U^{\operatorname{sat}}
, then
\eta=\frac{a^{\prime}}{p^{n}}\otimes u^{\prime}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge u^{\prime}_{d}
for some
a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
(one can check this using the Smith normal form). Therefore we may assume
U
is saturated. Let
\theta
be another pure tensor:

\theta=\frac{b}{p^{n}}\otimes v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{d},

for some
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\in W
such that
V=\langle v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\rangle
is saturated in
W
. We will show
\eta+\theta
is a pure tensor. Since
U
and
V
are saturated we have:

(U\cap V)_{\mathbb{Q}}=U_{\mathbb{Q}}\cap V_{\mathbb{Q}},

where
U_{\mathbb{Q}}=U\otimes\mathbb{Q}
and similarly for
V_{\mathbb{Q}}
and
(U\cap V)_{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Therefore,

\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{Z}}(U\cap V)=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(U_{\mathbb{Q}}\cap V_{\mathbb{Q}})\geqslant\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(U_{\mathbb{Q}})+\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(V_{\mathbb{Q}})-\dim(W_{\mathbb{Q}})=2d-\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(W_{\mathbb{Q}})=d-1.

Since
U\cap V
is clearly saturated in both
U
and
V
we can complete a basis of
U\cap V
to a basis of
U
and to a basis of
V
. In other words, we may assume
u_{i}=v_{i}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant d-1
. Then

\displaystyle\eta+\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{p^{n}}\otimes u_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{d}+\frac{b}{p^{n}}\otimes u_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{d-1}\wedge v_{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes u_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge(au_{d}+bv_{d}).

The right hand side is a pure tensor. This shows that any element in
\bigwedge^{d}W/p^{\infty}
is a pure tensor and thus completes the proof of part (b).
∎

In light of Corollary
8.3
, one might guess that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)
is divisible by
d
for any
\alpha\in H^{d}_{p}(F)
. The following proposition shows that this is false for any
d\geqslant 3
.

Proposition 10.2
.

Let
F_{n}=k((t_{0}))\dots((t_{n-1}))
. Assume that
d\geqslant 3
. Then for any
n\geqslant d+2
and
p\neq\operatorname{char}k
,
there exists
\alpha\in H^{d}_{p}(F_{n})
such that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\beta;p)=n
.

Proof.

We claim that

\alpha=\sum_{i_{1}+\ldots+i_{d}\equiv 0\pmod{n}}(t_{i_{1}})_{p}\cup\ldots\cup(t_{i_{d}})_{p}

has the desired property. Here the sum is taken over ordered
d
-tuples
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})
such that

0\leqslant i_{1}<\ldots<i_{d}\leqslant n-1
and
i_{1}+\ldots+i_{d}\equiv 0\pmod{n}
. Let
\nu
be the
(t_{0},\dots,t_{n-1})
-adic valuation on
F_{n}
and set

\omega:=\wedge\nu(\alpha)=\frac{1}{p}\otimes\sum_{i_{1}+\ldots+i_{d}\equiv 0\pmod{n}}e_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{i_{d}},

where
e_{0},e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
is the standard basis in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
In view of Theorem
7.1
it suffices to show that

A_{\omega}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}/p\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}/p^{\infty}
.
Let
e^{1},\dots,e^{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{n})^{*}
be the dual basis. From the definition of
\omega
, we see that when we contract
\omega
with a tensor of
the form
e^{j_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e^{j_{d-1}}
, where
0\leqslant j_{1}<\ldots<j_{d-1}
,
we obtain
\pm e_{j}
, where
0\leqslant j\leqslant n-1
is uniquely determined by the congruence

j\equiv-j_{1}-\ldots-j_{d-1}\pmod{n}
. By definition, every

e_{j}
of this form lies in
A_{\omega}
. To complete the proof, we need to show that every basis vector
e_{0},\ldots,e_{n-1}

can be obtained in this way. Equivalently, it remains to prove the following.

Claim:
Assume
d\geqslant 3
and
n\geqslant d+2
are integers. Then
for every
j\in\mathbb{Z}/n
, there exists a subset
S\subset\mathbb{Z}/n
consisting of
d
distinct
elements,
i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}
, such that
j\in S
and
i_{1}+\ldots+i_{d}=0
in
\mathbb{Z}/n
.

To prove the Claim, let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of pairs
\{i,-i\}
, where
2i\neq 0
in
\mathbb{Z}/n
. If
n
is odd,
then
2i=0
is only possible in
\mathbb{Z}/n
if
i=0
, and thus
|\mathcal{P}|=(n-1)/2
. If
n=2m
is even, then
the equation
2i=0
has two solutions in
\mathbb{Z}/n
,
i=0
or
i=m
. In this case

|\mathcal{P}|=(n-2)/2=m-1
.

Case 1.

2j\neq 0
in
\mathbb{Z}/n
, and
d
is even. Take
S
to be the union of
d/2
pairs from
\mathcal{P}
, one of these pairs being
\{\pm j\}
.
This is possible because we are assuming that
n\geqslant d+2
, and thus

|\mathcal{P}|\geqslant(n-2)/2\geqslant d/2
.

Case 2.

2j\neq 0
in
\mathbb{Z}/n
, and
d
is odd. Take
S
to be the union of
0
and
(d-1)/2

pairs from
\mathcal{P}
, one of these pairs being
\{\pm j\}
. Again, this is possible because

|\mathcal{P}|\geqslant d/2
.

Case 3.

2j=0
and
d
is odd. Take
S
to be the union of
\{1,j,j-1\}
and
(d-3)/2
pairs from
\mathcal{P}
,
other than
\{\pm 1\}
and
\{\pm(j-1)\}
. Note that
1
,
j
and
j-1
are distinct in
\mathbb{Z}/n
; this readily follows from our assumption that
n\geqslant d+2\geqslant 5
. Note also that
(d-3)/2
pairs, other than

\{\pm 1\}
and
\{\pm(j-1)\}
, exist in
\mathcal{P}
, because when

is odd,
the inequality
|\mathcal{P}|\geqslant d/2
can be strengthened to
|\mathcal{P}|\geqslant(d+1)/2=(d-3)/2+2
.

Case 4a.

2j=0
,
d
is even, and
n
is odd. In this case
j=0
in
\mathbb{Z}/n
.
Moreover, for even
d
, the inequality
d\geqslant 3
can be strengthened to
d\geqslant 4
, and for odd

,
the inequality
n\geqslant d+2\geqslant 6
can be strengthened to
n\geqslant 7
. Consequently,
the four elements

,
1
,

and

are
distinct in
\mathbb{Z}/n
, and we define

to be the union of
\{0,1,2,-3\}
and
(d-4)/2
pairs from
\mathcal{P}
, other than

\{\pm 1\}
,
\{\pm 2\}
and
\{\pm 3\}
. Note that

. Since

is even, this translates to

|\mathcal{P}|\geqslant(d+2)/2=(d-4)/2+3
.

Case 4b.

2j=0
,
d
is even, and
n=2m
is even. Here

or
m
, and we define
S
as the union of
\{0,1,m-1,m\}

and
(d-4)/2
pairs from
\mathcal{P}
, other than
\{\pm 1\}
and

. The elements

,

,

are distinct in
\mathbb{Z}/n
, because

, and
\mathcal{P}
has
(d-4)/2

\{\pm 1\}

Remark 10.3
.

Similarly, in light of Proposition

, one might guess that if
\alpha\in H^{d}_{p}(F)
is split by a field extension
E/F
of degree
n
, then
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)\leqslant dn
. This is also false. For example, let
d=3
,
F=k((t_{0}))\dots((t_{2r}))
, and

\alpha=(t_{0})_{p}\cup\big((t_{1},t_{2})_{p}+\ldots+(t_{2r-1},t_{2r})_{p}\big).

Then
\alpha
is split by
E=F(\sqrt[p]{t_{0}})
, where
[E:F]=p
. On the other hand,
contracting

\omega:=\wedge\nu(\alpha)=\frac{1}{p}\otimes e_{0}\wedge[e_{1}\wedge e_{2}+\ldots+e_{2r-1}\wedge e_{2r}]

with tensors of the form
e^{j_{1}}\wedge e^{j_{2}}
, as we did in the proof of Proposition
10.2
,
we see that
A_{\omega}=\mathbb{Z}^{2r+1}/p\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2r+1}/p^{\infty}
.
By Theorem
7.1
,
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)=\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha;p)=2r+1
. Since
r
can be taken to be an
arbitrary positive integer, the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\alpha)}{[E:F]}=\frac{2r+1}{p}
can be arbitrarily large.

11.
Anisotropic torsors

In this section take a slight detour to prove a structural result about anisotropic
E_{8}
-torsors, Proposition
11.3
below. This proposition will be used
in the proof of Theorem
1.4
in the next section.

Let
G
be a connected quasi-split reductive group and
T\in H^{1}(F,G)
a
G
-torsor over a field
F\in\operatorname{Fields}_{k}
. For any subgroup
H\subset G
, we will say that
T

admits reduction of structure to

H
, if it is in the image of
H^{1}(F,H)\to H^{1}(F,G)
. The torsor
T
is called
isotropic
if it admits reduction of structure to a proper parabolic subgroup of
G
(or equivalently, if the twisted group
{}_{T}G
is isotropic as an algebraic group
[
34
, Lemma 2.2]
). Otherwise,
T
is said to be
anisotropic
. Recall that if
H
is a finite constant group,
H^{1}(F,H)
classifies Galois etale algebras of
F
whose Galois group is a homomorphic image of
H
. The next lemma is inspired by
[
14
, Proposition 7.2]
.

Lemma 11.1
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a strictly Henselian field with residue field
k
and valuation ring
\mathcal{O}
. Let
G
be a connected quasi-split reductive group over a field
F_{0}\subset\mathcal{O}
. Assume that
G_{\mathcal{O}}
contains a constant finite subgroup
\iota:H\hookrightarrow G_{\mathcal{O}}
such that
H_{\overline{k}}
is not contained in any proper parabolic subgroup of
G_{\overline{k}}
. Then for any torsor
T\in H^{1}(F,H)
corresponding to an
H
-Galois field extension
L/F
, the push-forward
\iota_{*}(T)\in H^{1}(F,G)
is anisotropic.

Proof.

By assumption, there exists an isomorphism
c:\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)\to H
and
\iota_{*}(T)
is represented by the cocycle
\iota\circ c
.
Assume that
\iota_{*}(T)
admits reduction of structure to a parabolic subgroup
P\subset G
. Our goal is
to show
P=G
. Since
\iota_{*}(T)
admits reduction of structure to
P
, there exists
g\in G(L)
such that for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)
:

(11.1)

g^{-1}c_{\sigma}{}^{\sigma}g\in P(L).

Setting
x\in(G/P)(L)
to be the coset represented by
g
, we see that for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)
:

(11.2)

c(\sigma){}^{\sigma}x=x.

Since
\nu
is Henselian, it has a unique extension to
L
. By abuse of notation, we will continue to denote this extended valuation by
\nu
. We will write
\mathcal{O}_{L}
for the valuation ring of
\nu
in
L
and
l
be the residue field. Since
k
is separably closed,
l
is a purely inseparable extension of
k
. Since
G/P
is projective,
x\in(G/P)(L)

can be lifted to a point in
(G/P)(\mathcal{O}_{L})
. By abuse of notation, we will simply say that
x\in(G/P)(\mathcal{O}_{L})
. Note that
\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)
preserves
\nu
because
L
is Henselian. Therefore
\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)
acts on
\mathcal{O}_{L}
. The reduction homomorphism
\mathcal{O}_{L}\to l
is
\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)
-equivariant with respect to the trivial
\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)
-action on
l
because
l/k
is purely inseparable. Let
\pi:G(\mathcal{O}_{L})\to G(l)
be the induced homomorphism. Applying
\pi
to (
11.2
) gives:

\pi(x)=\pi(c(\sigma){}^{\sigma}x)=\pi(c(\sigma))\pi(x).

Since
H\subset G_{\mathcal{O}}
is constant,
H(\mathcal{O}_{L})=H(l)
and the restriction of
\pi
to
H
is the identity. Therefore we get:

\pi(x)=\pi(c(\sigma){}^{\sigma}x)=c(\sigma)\pi(x).

Since
l
is separably closed, we can find
g\in G(l)
such that
\pi(x)=gP(l)
. Since
c
is an isomorphism we get
HgP(l)=gP(l)

and therefore:

H_{l}\subset gP_{l}g^{-1}.

Since we are assuming that
H_{\overline{k}}
is not contained in any proper parabolic of
G_{\overline{k}}
, we conclude that
gP_{l}g^{-1}=G_{l}
and thus
P=G
. This finishes the proof.
∎

The next lemma gives a convenient sufficient condition for a subgroup
H\subset G
to not be contained in any proper parabolic subgroup
P\subset G
.

Lemma 11.2
.

Let
G
be a connected semisimple group over a field
k
and let
H\subset G
be a finite subgroup. If the centralizer
C_{G}(H)
is finite (i.e., 0-dimensional) and
|H|
is coprime to
\operatorname{char}k
, then
H
is not contained in any proper parabolic subgroup of
G
.

Proof.

Assume that
H
is contained in a parabolic subgroup
P\subset G
.
Since
H
is of order prime to
p
it is linearly reductive over
k
. Therefore
H
is contained in a Levi subgroup
L\subset P
(see
[
18
, Lemma 11.24]
). If
P\neq G
, then the center
Z_{L}
of
L
contains a split torus
[
2
, Proposition 20.6]
. This contradicts our assumption that
C_{G}(H)
is finite because
clearly
Z_{L}\subset C_{G}(H)
. Therefore we must have
P=G
.
∎

We are now ready to proceed with the main result of this section.

Proposition 11.3
.

Let
G
be a split group of type
E_{8}
over a field
k
and assume
\operatorname{char}k\neq 2,3
. Let

be a torsor over a strictly Henselian field
F\in\operatorname{Fields}_{k}
over
k
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(T;3)\geqslant 5
.

(2)

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{G}(T);3)\geqslant 5
.

(3)

T_{L}
is anisotropic for any prime-to-
3
extension
F\subset L
.

Proof.

Passing from

to its prime-to-
3
extension

does not affect the validity of
assertions (1), (2) and (3). Thus we may assume without loss of generality that

is a
3
-special field.
In particular, since
\operatorname{char}F\neq 3
, this implies that

is perfect. By assumption,

is equipped with a Henselian valuation

that is trivial on

. Therefore the residue field of
\nu
has the same characteristic as

. Hensel’s lemma implies that

contains all primitive roots of unity of order

for

. Let

be the field generated by all roots of unity in

. We replace
k
by

without effecting assertions
(1)
and
(2)
by (
2.3
). This allows us to view the Rost invariant as a natural homomorphism
H^{1}(\ast,G)\to H^{3}_{3}(\ast)
because
\mathbb{Q}_{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{3}(2)\cong\mathbb{Q}_{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{3}(3)
as Galois modules over
k
.

(3)
\iff
(2) By Proposition
10.1
,
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{G}(T);3)\geqslant 5
if and only if
R_{G}(T)
is not a symbol. Since
F
is
3
-special,
[
11
, Theorem 10.24]
implies that
R_{G}(T)
is a symbol if and only if

is isotropic. Therefore

is anisotropic if and only if
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{G}(T);3)\geqslant 5

(2)
\implies
(1) follows directly from (
1.6
).

(1)
\implies
(3) We assume
T
is isotropic and show
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(T;3)\leqslant 4
. Since
T
is isotropic, it admits reduction of structure to some proper parabolic subgroup
P\subset G
. Assume without loss of generality that
P
is maximal amongst the proper parabolic subgroups. Let

be a Levi subgroup of

and let

be the corresponding derived subgroup (it exists by
[
31
, Theorem 25.6]
). Since

is perfect,

is bijective
[
42
, Lemme 1.13]
. Note that
Z(L)^{\circ}
is a split torus because it is contained in a maximal torus of

. Therefore,

is a split torus as a homomorphic image of

[

, Chapter V, Theorem 15.4]
, and

is surjective by Hilbert

. Therefore the composition

is surjective and

admits reduction of structure to

. Therefore
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(T;3)\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(L^{\prime};3)
and it suffices to prove:

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(L^{\prime};3)\leqslant 4.

Since

is a maximal proper parabolic of

, the Dynkin diagram of

is obtained from the Dynkin diagram of

by removing one vertex
[

]
. By

,

is simply connected. Hence,

is a product of simple simply connected groups.
Removing each of the eight vertices in the Dynkin diagram of

, we obtain the following possibilities for

(i)

, (ii)

, (iii)

, (iv)

, (v)

,

12.
Proof of Theorem
1.4
and a remark on spin groups

For the reader’s convenience, we begin by reproducing the statement of Theorem
1.4
.

Theorem
1.4
.
Let
k
be a base field of characteristic
\neq 2
and

F_{n}
be the iterated Laurent series field
F_{n}=k((t_{0}))((t_{2}))\ldots((t_{n-1}))
. Then there exist
(i) an
E_{7}^{\rm sc}
-torsor
T_{1}\to\operatorname{Spec}(F_{7})
, (ii) an
E_{8}
-torsor
T_{2}\to\operatorname{Spec}(F_{9})
, and (iii) an
E_{8}
-torsor
T_{3}\to\operatorname{Spec}(F_{5})

such that (i)
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{E_{7}^{\rm sc}}(T_{1});2)=7
, (ii)
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{E_{8}}(T_{2});2)=9
, and (iii)
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{E_{8}}(T_{3});3)=5
, respectively.

Here in (iii) we are assuming that
k
contains a primitive
3
rd root of unity.

Proof.

We assume at first that
k
is separably closed. In this case, The functors
H^{3}(\ast,\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(2))
and
H^{3}_{p}(\ast)
are naturally isomorphic because
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(2)
and
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}(3)
are isomorphic as Galois modules for all

. Therefore we can and shall see
R_{G}
as taking values in
H^{3}_{p}
where
p=2
in parts (i),(ii) and
p=3
in part (iii).

Part (i):
Let
G
be a simply connected split group of type
E_{7}
over
k
. Let
L=k(s_{0},\dots,s_{6})
be a field of rational functions in
7
independent variables and
H
a quasi-split simply connected group of type
E_{6}
over
L
that is split by the extension
L(\sqrt{s_{0}})
.
In
[
6
, Section 6.2.3]
, Chernousov constructed a torsor

such that:

R_{H}(S)=(s_{0})_{2}\cup[(s_{1},s_{3})_{2}+(s_{2}s_{3}s_{5},s_{4})_{2}+(s_{5},s_{6})_{2}].

Moreover, there is an embedding
\iota:H\to G
such that
R_{G}(\iota_{*}(S))=R_{H}(S)

[
10
, Proposition 3.6]
(see also
[
13
, Example A.3]
).
We make a rational change of variables
t_{0}=s_{0}
,
t_{1}=s_{1},t_{2}=s_{3},t_{3}=s_{2}s_{3}s_{4},t_{4}=s_{4},t_{5}=s_{5},t_{6}=s_{6}
.
Clearly
L=k(t_{0},\dots,t_{6})
and we have:

R_{G}(\iota_{*}(S))=(t_{0})_{2}\cup[(t_{1},t_{2})_{2}+(t_{3},t_{4})_{2}+(t_{5},t_{6})_{2}].

We set
T_{1}=\iota_{*}(T_{0})_{F_{7}}
and observe that
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{G}(T_{1});2)=7
by Remark
10.3
.

Part (ii):
Let
G
be a split group of type
E_{8}
over
k
. In
[
12
, Corollary 11.3]
Garibaldi proves there exists a torsor
T_{2}\in H^{1}(F_{9},G)
such that:

R_{G}(T_{2})=(t_{0})_{2}\cup[(t_{1},t_{2})_{2}+\dots+(t_{7},t_{8})_{2}].

We have
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{G}(T_{2});2)=9
by Remark
10.3
again.

Part (iii):
Let
G=E_{8}
as in the previous part. Denote
H:=\mathbb{Z}^{5}/3
. By
[
16
, Lemma 11.5]
, there is an embedding
\iota:H\hookrightarrow G
such that
\iota(H)
is self-centralizing. Let
K_{5}=k(t_{0},\dots,t_{4})
be a rational field in
5
variables. We denote by

the torsor corresponding to the
H
-Galois extension
L_{5}=K_{5}(t_{0}^{1/3},\dots,t_{4}^{1/3})
of
K_{5}
. Let
T_{3}=\iota_{*}(S)_{F_{5}}
. Inequality (
2.2
) implies:

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{G}(T_{3});3)\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{G}(T_{3}))\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(T_{3})\leqslant\operatorname{trdeg}_{k}(K_{5})=5.

Let
\nu
be the
(t_{0},\dots,t_{4})
-adic valuation on
F_{5}
. Since
k
is separably closed,
(F_{5},\nu)
is strictly Henselian. Let
E/F_{5}
be an arbitrary prime-to-
3
extension. There is a unique extension of
\nu
to

which we will again denote

by abuse of notation. Note that

. The torsor

Remark 12.1
.

One may wonder if similar techniques can be used to recover the exponential lower bounds

(12.1)

\operatorname{ed}(\operatorname{Spin}_{n};2)\geqslant\begin{cases}2^{(n-1)/2}-\frac{(n-1)n}{2}&\text{if $n$ is odd, and}\\
2^{(n-2)/2}-\frac{(n-1)n}{2}&\text{if $n$ is even}\end{cases}

from
[
4
, Theorem 3.3]
. The answer is “no”.
(Here we assume that
k
is of characteristic
0
and
n\geqslant 15
. In the case, where
n
is divisible by
4
, the lower bound in (
12.1
) is not optimal, but that shall not concern us here.)

Indeed, recall that the Rost invariant for
R_{G}\colon H^{1}(F,\operatorname{Spin}_{n})\to H^{3}(F,\mu_{2})\subset H^{3}(F,\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}(2))
for
G=\operatorname{Spin}_{n}
may be computed as follows.
Let
T\in H^{1}(F,\operatorname{Spin}_{n})
be a torsor. The image of
T
under the natural map
H^{1}(F,\operatorname{Spin}_{n})\to H^{1}(F,\operatorname{SO}_{n})
is represented by a quadratic form
q
of rank
n
, with trivial discriminant and trivial Hasse-Witt invariant.
Then
R_{G}(T)={\rm ar}(q)
is the Arason invariant of
q
; see
[
20
, p. 437]
.
In other words, if we represent
q
as a sum of 3-fold Pfister forms

(12.2)

q=\langle\langle a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\rangle\rangle+\ldots+\langle\langle a_{3r-2},a_{3r-1},a_{3r}\rangle\rangle

in the Witt ring
W(F)
, then

{\rm ar}(q)=(a_{1})_{2}\cup(a_{2})_{2}\cup(a_{3})_{2}+\ldots+(a_{3r-2})_{2}\cup(a_{3r-1})_{2}\cup(a_{3r})_{2}\in H^{3}(K,\mu_{2}).

Note that
q
can be represented in the form (
12.2
) by a theorem of Merkurjev
[
24
]
.

Now assume
F
is equipped with a strictly Henselian valuation.
By a theorem of Raczek
[
36
, Theorem 1.13]
, we can assume
r\leqslant n^{2}/8
in (
12.2
).
Since
R_{G}(T)={\rm ar}(q)
descends to
k(a_{1},\dots,a_{3r})
we conclude:

\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{G}(T);2)\leqslant\operatorname{ed}_{k}(R_{G}(T))\leqslant 3r\leqslant 3n^{2}/8.

For large
n
this is smaller than the lower bound on
\operatorname{ed}_{k}(\operatorname{Spin}_{n};2)
in (
12.1
).

The norm residue isomorphism

Let
F

by
F^{\operatorname{sep}}
.
Choose compatible a compatible system of roots of primitive
m
th unity
\zeta_{m}\in F^{\operatorname{sep}}

Now fix one particular integer

prime to

Recall that the Kummer map is the connecting homomorphism :

\partial_{m}:F^{*}\to H^{1}(F,\mu_{m})

K_{d}(F)/m\to H^{d}(F,\mu^{\otimes d}_{m}),\ \ \{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\}\mapsto\partial_{m}(a_{1})\cup\dots\cup\partial_{m}(a_{d}).

Here

is Milnor

-theory. We will denote by
h^{d}_{m}:K_{d}(F)\to H^{d}(F,\mathbb{Z}/{m}(d))
the Galois symbol,
given by the composition:

K_{d}(F)\to H^{d}(F,\mu^{\otimes d}_{m})\to H^{d}(F,(\mathbb{Z}/m(1))^{\otimes d})\to H^{d}(F,\mathbb{Z}/m(d)).

For
d=0
, we set
h^{0}_{m}:K_{0}(F)=\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/m
to be the reduction modulo
m
homomorphism. Note that
h^{d}_{m}
depends on our choice of the root of unity
\zeta_{m}
, which induces an isomorphism
\mu_{m}\to\mathbb{Z}/m(1)
.

We will use the following notation for the symbol defined by
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in F^{*}
:

(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{m}:=h^{d}_{m}(\{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\}).

Putting these together for all
d\geqslant 0
yields a homomorphism of graded groups

h_{m}:=\oplus h^{d}_{m}:K(F)\overset{}{\to}\bigoplus_{d}H^{d}(F,\mathbb{Z}/m(d)).

As we vary
m
, the homomorphisms
h_{m}
transform like our chosen roots of unity
\zeta_{m}
.

Lemma 13.1
.

For all
m\mid n
coprime to
\operatorname{char}F
we have:

i_{m,n}\circ h_{m}=\frac{n}{m}h_{n}

where
i_{m,n}:\bigoplus_{d}H^{d}(F,\mathbb{Z}/m(d))\to\bigoplus_{d}H^{d}(F,\mathbb{Z}/n(d))
is the natural inclusion.

Proof.

Let
\iota:\mathbb{Z}/m(d)\to\mathbb{Z}/n(d)
be the inclusion given by multiplication by
{\frac{n}{m}}
and let
\iota_{*}:H^{d}(F,\mathbb{Z}/m(d))\to H^{d}(F,\mathbb{Z}/n(d))
be the push-forward on Galois cohomology. It suffices to prove for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in F^{*}
:

(13.2)

\iota_{*}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{m}=\frac{n}{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{n}.

We do this on the level of cocycles. Let
b_{i}\in{F^{\operatorname{sep}}}
be an
n
-th root of
a_{i}
. By definition of the Kummer map, the function

c^{\prime}_{i}(\sigma):=b_{i}\sigma(b_{i})^{-1}\in\mu_{n}

is a cocycle representing the class of
\partial_{n}(a_{i})\in H^{1}(F,\mu_{n})
; see (
13.1
). Similarly, the cocycle

c_{i}(\sigma)=b_{i}^{\frac{n}{m}}\sigma(b_{i}^{-\frac{n}{m}})=(b_{i}\sigma(b_{i}^{-1}))^{\frac{n}{m}}=c^{\prime}_{i}(\sigma)^{\frac{n}{m}}

represents the class of
\partial_{m}(a_{i})\in H^{1}(F,\mu_{m})
. Using our choice of isomorphisms
\mu_{n}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n(1)
, we can find a cocycle
\chi_{i}:\operatorname{Gal}(F)\to\mathbb{Z}/n(1)
such that

(13.3)

\text{for all }\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(F):c^{\prime}_{i}(\sigma)=\zeta_{n}^{\chi_{i}(\sigma)}

We calculate:

c_{i}(\sigma)=c^{\prime}_{i}(\sigma)^{\frac{n}{m}}=(\zeta^{\chi_{i}(\sigma)}_{n})^{\frac{n}{m}}=\zeta^{\chi_{i}(\sigma)}_{m}.

This shows that under the isomorphism
H^{1}(F,\mu_{m})\tilde{\to}H^{1}(F,\mathbb{Z}/m(1))

the cohomology class
\partial_{m}(a_{i})
is sent to the class represented by the cocycle:

\operatorname{Gal}(F)\to\mathbb{Z}/m(1),\ \ \sigma\mapsto\chi_{i}(\sigma)\mod m.

Therefore,
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{m}
is represented by the
n
-cocycle:

f:\operatorname{Gal}(F)^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}/m(n),\ \ f(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d})=\prod_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\chi_{i}(\sigma_{i})\mod m.

Pushing
f
forward using
\iota
, we get a cocycle representing
\iota_{*}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{m}
:

\iota_{*}(f)(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d})=\frac{n}{m}\prod_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\chi_{i}(\sigma_{i})\mod n.

This cocycle represents
\displaystyle\frac{n}{m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})_{n}
because the cocycle
\chi_{i}
represents the image of
\partial_{n}(a_{i})
under the isomorphism
H^{1}(F,\mu_{n})\tilde{\to}H^{1}(F,\mathbb{Z}/n(1))
by (
13.3
). This implies (
13.2
) and finishes the proof.
∎

We can now deduce the ”global” form of the norm residue isomorphism stated in Theorem
2.1
from the norm residue isomorphism theorem and Lemma
13.1
. Recall that
K(F)/p^{\infty}=\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}K(F)
by definition.

Theorem 13.2
.

For any prime
p
different from
\operatorname{char}k
, there is an isomorphism of abelian graded groups
K(F)/p^{\infty}\overset{h}{\to}H_{p}(F)
given on generators by:

h(\frac{1}{p^{n}}\otimes\{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\})=h^{d}_{p^{n}}(\{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\})=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})_{p^{n}}

Proof.

The homomorphism
h
is well-defined by Lemma
13.1
and because
h_{1}=0
by definition. Moreover
h
is surjective by the norm residue isomorphism theorem. Assume
h(\alpha)=0
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}K(F)
. We can write
\alpha\cong\frac{1}{p^{n}}x
for some
x\in K(F)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
h(\alpha)=h_{p^{n}}(x)=0
, the norm residue isomorphism theorem implies
x=p^{n}x^{\prime}
for some
x^{\prime}\in K(F)
. Therefore
\alpha=0
. This shows
h
is indeed an isomorphism.
∎

13.2.
Extensions of valuations

The following lemmas will be used to handle extensions of degree prime to
p
.

Lemma 13.3
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a valued field over
k
and let
F\subset L
be a finite extension of degree prime to
p
. There exists an extension
w
of
\nu
to
L
such that the ramification degree
[wL:\nu F]
is prime to
p
.

Proof.

Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{r}
be all the extensions of
\nu
to
L
. Denote by
F^{h}
the Henselization of
(F,\nu)
, and choose Henselizations
F^{h}\subset L^{h}_{i}
of
(L,w_{i})
(these exist by
[
46
, Theorem A.30]
). Using
[
46
, Theorem A.32]
, we get an
F^{h}
-linear isomorphism:

L\otimes_{F}F^{h}\cong L^{h}_{1}\times\dots\times L^{h}_{r}.

Comparing dimensions over
F^{h}
we find:

(13.4)

[L:F]=[L^{h}_{1}:F^{h}]+\dots+[L^{h}_{r}:F^{h}].

Let
e_{i}=[w_{i}L:\nu F]
be the ramification degree of
(L,w_{i})
over
(F,\nu)
and note that it is also the ramification degree of
(L^{h}_{i},w_{i})
over
(F^{h},\nu)
. Ostrowski’s theorem implies
e_{i}
divides
[L^{h}_{i}:F^{h}]
for all
i

[
46
, Theorem A.12]
. Since the left hand side of (
13.4
) is prime to
p
, we conclude that
e_{i}
is prime to
p
for some
i
.
∎

Lemma 13.4
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\Gamma^{\prime}
be finitely generated free abelian groups such that
[\Gamma^{\prime}:\Gamma]
is prime to
p
. The canonical inclusion
\bigwedge\Gamma\subset\bigwedge\Gamma^{\prime}
induces an isomorphism:

(13.5)

\bigwedge\Gamma/p^{\infty}\cong\bigwedge\Gamma^{\prime}/p^{\infty}.

Proof.

By the Elementary Divisors Theorem (as in the proof of Proposition
9.1
),

\Gamma^{\prime}
has a basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
such that
d_{1}e_{1},\dots,d_{r}e_{r}
is a basis of
\Gamma
for some positive integers
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
.
Then
[\Gamma^{\prime}:\Gamma]=\prod d_{i}
; in particular,
d_{i}
is prime to
p
for every
i
. The group
\bigwedge\Gamma^{\prime}
has a basis
\{e_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_{k}}\}
indexed by increasing sequences
1\leqslant i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leqslant r
. Similarly, the pure tensors
\{d_{i_{1}}e_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge d_{i_{k}}e_{i_{k}}\}
form a basis for
\bigwedge\Gamma
. Therefore the index
[\bigwedge\Gamma^{\prime}:\bigwedge\Gamma]
is prime to
p
.
Consequently, the inclusion
\bigwedge\Gamma\hookrightarrow\bigwedge\Gamma^{\prime}
induces an isomorphism

(\bigwedge\Gamma)/p^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}(\bigwedge\Gamma^{\prime})/p^{n}
for every
n\geqslant 1
.
Taking the colimit as
n\to\infty
,
we obtain the isomorphism (
13.5
).
∎
