Bijections on strictly convex sets and closed convex projective surfaces that preserve complete geodesics
Drimik Roy Chowdhury

Abstract
. In this paper, we study bijections on strictly convex sets of 𝐑𝐏 n {\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{n} for n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 and closed convex projective surfaces equipped with the Hilbert metric that map complete geodesics to complete geodesics as sets. Hyperbolic n n -space with its standard metric is a special example of the spaces we consider, and it is known that these bijections in this context are precisely the isometries [ Jef00 ] . We first prove that this result generalizes to an arbitrary strictly convex set. For the surfaces setting, we prove the equivalence of mapping simple closed geodesics to simple closed geodesics and mapping closed geodesics to closed geodesics. We also outline some future directions and questions to further explore these topics.



1.
Introduction

We want to study the behavior of bijections on certain metric spaces that preserve complete geodesics as sets. The general motivation for this investigation began with the results described in
[
Jef00
]
, which asked if more could be said about bijective maps on each of
{\mathbf{R}}^{n},{\mathbf{H}}^{n}
and
{\mathbf{R}}^{n}\cup\{\infty\}
if they preserved complete geodesics with respect to their standard metrics. On its own merit, the question to what extent complete geodesics characterize your space (or perhaps, more specifically, what attributes of your space may be determined) stands as an intriguing premise. For example,
[
Jef00
]
proves that such a map on
{\mathbf{R}}^{n}
must be affine and on
{\mathbf{H}}^{n}
must be an isometry. In this sense, we see that preserving geodesics as sets imposes very rigid constraints on the function in study.

The notion of a convex set is ubiquitous in mathematics; they are used in areas such as number theory, geometry, dynamical systems and optimization. Hilbert identified a metric one could construct on a properly convex set in
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
based on the cross ratio where the resulting spaces were metrically and geodesically complete. If the convex set is strictly convex, then geodesics are projective lines of
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
restricted to our set. In the special case for when the convex set is a ball, or more generally an ellipsoid, the Hilbert metric coincides with the Klein-Beltrami model of hyperbolic space
[
PT14b
, Introduction]
. Hence, we see that Hilbert geometry includes hyperbolic geometry. Moreover, since the cross ratio is invariant under projective transformations, we see that the Hilbert metric is a canonical attribute of a properly convex set of
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
i.e., independent of the affine chart in study. Hilbert geometry provides a rich mechanism to study questions from convexity theory and has deep connections to differential geometry via Finsler geometry.

Our goal is to characterize properties of bijections on strictly convex sets in
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
and closed convex projective surfaces with the Hilbert metric that preserve complete geodesics as sets.

We now state our main result in the strictly convex set case, which is that a bijection preserving complete geodesics must be an isometry. Thus, our paper directly generalizes the known result in the hyperbolic scenario mentioned above. The following fact is Theorem
3.1
in the paper.

Theorem A
.

For
n\geq 2
, let
\Omega\subset{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
be a strictly convex set endowed with the Hilbert metric and let
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega
be a bijection, mapping complete geodesics to complete geodesics as sets. Then
f
is an isometry of
\Omega
and can be extended to a projective transformation of
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
via an isometric embedding of
\Omega
into
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
.

We prove this by showing any
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega
as in the theorem must preserve the cyclic order of points along any complete geodesic and hence, must preserve geodesic segments. Theorem 2.17 of
[
AAFM12
]
then tells us that
f
must be a fractional linear map, and this essentially concludes the argument. See Section
2
for the precise definitions of the aforementioned terms and relevant background. Section
3
states and proves this main result.

Following this, we visit closed (real) convex projective surfaces. Let
S
be such a surface and
f\colon S\to S
be a bijection mapping complete geodesics to complete geodesics as sets. Our conjecture was that
f
must be an isometry. To explore this, we first asked what would be the consequences of this result. In particular,
f
would be continuous and so it would map closed geodesics to closed geodesics. Our main result in the surfaces setting is that preserving closed geodesics is equivalent to preserving simple closed geodesics. The exact statement, Theorem
5.1
, is quite technical, and so we have chosen to omit it here. Section
4
discusses the necessary background for our exploration on convex projective surfaces and Section
5
states the main result. Whether or not
f\colon S\to S
is an isometry is still an open question.

Section
6
discusses geodesic laminations and a context in which the main result of our exploration on surfaces holds. Lastly, Section
7
outlines notable questions to follow from the work conducted in this paper.

3.2.
Proving order preserving along any geodesic

Before we proceed, we note quickly that for any bijection preserving complete geodesics on
\Omega

Lemma 3.2
.

For
n\geq 2
, let
\Omega\subset{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{n}
be a strictly convex set endowed with the Hilbert metric and
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega
be a bijection, mapping complete geodesics to complete geodesics as sets. Then

sends complete geodesics to complete geodesics as sets.

Proof.

Let
\ell^{\prime}\subset\Omega
be a line and
y,y^{\prime}\in\ell^{\prime}
be distinct points. There exists distinct unique
x,x^{\prime}\in\Omega
such that
f(x)=y,f(x^{\prime})=y^{\prime}
. Since any two distinct points in
\Omega
determine a unique line, let
\ell
be the line passing through
x,x^{\prime}
. Hence,
f(\ell)
by assumption is a line through
y,y^{\prime}
and
\Omega
being uniquely geodesic tells us that it must be the case that

so

.
∎

Let

4.
Preliminaries on convex projective surfaces

Definition 4.1

(Convex projective surface)
.

Let
\Omega
be a strictly convex set of
{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{2}
and

\operatorname{PGL}(3,{\mathbf{R}})

\Omega

Definition 4.2

(Hyperbolic isometries)
.

Let
\Omega

{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{2}

The covering map

is that

tells us non-identity elements of

We shall only consider closed convex projective surfaces in this paper.

This makes the language to follow in the paper a little simpler.

To tackle our motivating question, we tried to understand the possible implications of this statement and perhaps properties of our map that might be simpler to recognize. .

Let

Let
\alpha

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
S
be a closed convex projective surface. Suppose

be a bijection, mapping geodesic laminations to geodesic laminations that induces a bijection on the set of laminations of

. Then

sends simple closed geodesics to simple closed geodesics.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be a simple closed geodesic. Since

The main statement concerning geodesic laminations is Proposition
6.4
below. We note that Lemma
6.3
with the assumptions of the below proposition already guarantee Theorem
5.1
to apply; we now provide a quicker, more direct proof of this fact.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
S
be a closed convex projective surface. Suppose
f\colon S\to S
is a bijection, mapping complete geodesics to complete geodesics as sets, which induces a bijection on the set of these geodesics. Suppose in addition that
f
sends geodesic laminations to geodesic laminations and induces a bijection on the set of laminations of
S
. Then
f
maps closed geodesics to closed geodesics and simple closed geodesics to simple closed geodesics (i.e., Theorem
5.1
holds for
f
).

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be a simple closed geodesic. Then
f(\gamma)
is a minimal lamination; thus,
f(\gamma)
must be a simple closed geodesic or a disjoint collection of geodesics. The assumption that
f
maps lines to lines tells us the former must hold.
∎

7.
Future directions

Let
S
be a closed convex projective surface. Suppose
f\colon S\to S
is a bijection, mapping complete geodesics to complete geodesics as sets, which induces a bijection on the set of geodesics of
S
. A satisfying picture to have would be that our map
f
is induced by a bijection
\Omega\to\Omega
that also sends lines to lines. If
f
is continuous, then we argue now that this is necessarily true:
f
must be a homeomorphism as
f^{-1}
is a map with the same properties as
f
so
f^{-1}
is continuous as well. Hence, the lifting criterion tells us that we can lift
f
to a homeomorphism
\tilde{f}:\Omega\to\Omega
where
\tilde{f}^{-1}=\widetilde{f^{-1}}
. Let
\pi:\Omega\to S
denote the quotient map. To see that
\tilde{f}
maps lines to lines, we note that
\pi\circ\tilde{f}=f\circ\pi
so
\pi=f\circ\pi\circ\widetilde{f^{-1}}
. Since
\pi(\ell)
is a line in
S
iff
\ell
is a line in
\Omega
and the same holds for
f
(i.e., for
\ell^{\prime}\subset S
,
f(\ell^{\prime})
is a line iff
\ell^{\prime}
is a line), it follows that
\widetilde{f^{-1}}
preserves lines and hence so does
\tilde{f}
.

What would of course be more interesting is whether the map
f\colon S\to S
is always continuous; this still remains to be answered. We outline and include a few questions (some of which may have been mentioned above) that might help understand properties of our bijection (if at all).

•

Does Theorem
5.1
always hold i.e., does
f
always map closed geodesics to closed geodesics?

•

Is
f
continuous on geodesics? Is
f
continuous on all of
S
?

•

Is
f
an isometry of the Hilbert metric on
S
?

•

Is
f
a descent of an isometry
\Omega\to\Omega
where
\Omega\subset{\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{P}}^{2}
is a strictly convex set as the universal cover of
S
?

If the answer to the last question is in the affirmative, then this result would parallel the fundamental theorem of projective geometry.
