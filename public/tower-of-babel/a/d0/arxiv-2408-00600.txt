Ground-to-UAV and RIS-assisted UAV-to-Ground Communication Under Channel Aging: Statistical Characterization and Outage Performance
Thanh Luan Nguyen, Georges Kaddoum, Tri Nhu Do, Zygmunt J. Haas

Abstract
This paper studies the statistical characterization of ground-to-air (G2A) and reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS)-assisted air-to-ground (A2G) communications in RIS-assisted UAV networks under the impact of channel aging. A comprehensive channel model is presented, which incorporates the time-varying fading, three-dimensional (3D) mobility, Doppler shifts, and the effects of channel aging on array antenna structures. We provide analytical expressions for the G2A signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) probability density function (PDF) and cumulative distribution function (CDF), demonstrating that the G2A SNR follows a mixture of noncentral χ 2 \chi^{2} distributions. The A2G communication is characterized under RIS arbitrary phase-shift configurations, showing that the A2G SNR can be represented as the product of two correlated noncentral χ 2 \chi^{2} random variables (RVs). Additionally, we present the PDF and the CDF of the product of two independently distributed noncentral χ 2 \chi^{2} RVs, which accurately characterize the A2G SNR’s distribution. Our paper confirms the effectiveness of RISs in mitigating channel aging effects within the coherence time. Finally, we propose an adaptive spectral efficiency method that ensures consistent system performance and satisfactory outage levels when the UAV and the ground user equipments are in motion.

This paper aims to fill the aforementioned gaps by studying the statistical characterization of G2A and RIS-assisted A2G communications under the impact of channel aging.
Specifically, we derive the exact distribution, including the probability density function (PDF) and cumulative distribution function (CDF), of the G2A SNR using Laplace transform and inverse Laplace transform.
For the RIS-assisted A2G communication, we first derive the characteristic function (CF) of the A2G communication under arbitrary phase-shift configurations (PSCs).
We then propose characterizing the A2G channel as a complex Gaussian distribution conditioned on the estimated (i.e., delayed) CSI and RIS phase shifts.

Continuous-Time Mobility Model

The GUE’s motion with respect to the origin can be modeled by the change of its positions over time as
{\mathbf{p}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t,\tau)=\mathbf{p}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t+\tau)-\mathbf{p}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t,\tau)=\mathbf{p}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)+\tau\mathbf{v}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t,\tau).

and
k
, and
\bar{\rho}_{\textnormal{AB}}[t_{k}]=\sqrt{1-\rho_{\textnormal{AB}}^{2}[t_{k}]}
.
As in
[

,
34
]
, we consider that the channels evolve according to Jakes’ model, thus

\displaystyle\rho_{\textnormal{AB}}[t_{k}]=J_{0}\Big(2\pi t_{k}T_{s}\Delta\textrm{f}^{\textrm{max}}_{\textnormal{AB}}(t_{k}T_{s})\Big),

(12)

where
J_{0}(x)
is the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind and
T_{s}
[sec] is the channel sampling duration.
Since
\rho_{\textnormal{AB}}[t_{k}]
is a Bessel function, either change in the UAV velocity, GUE velocity, or a higher sample index
t_{k}
will yield a non-monotonic decrease in the correlation coefficient which oscillates around zero with decreasing magnitude.

Considering that the channels’ estimates at time instant
\tau
are perfect and that these estimates are used as the initial states to get estimates of the channels at all other time instants, then the channels’ estimates at later time instants will be inaccurate
[
34
,
16
]
.
Thus, the current channel at time instant
t_{k}
can be expressed in terms of the estimated channel at the
\tau^{th}
time instant as
[

]

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{\textnormal{AB}}[t_{k}]=\rho_{\textnormal{AB}}[\tau-t_{k}]{\mathbf{h}}_{\textnormal{AB}}[\tau]+\bar{\rho}_{\textnormal{AB}}[\tau-t_{k}]\mathbf{z}_{\textnormal{AB}}[t_{k}],

(13)

where
\mathbf{z}_{\textnormal{AB}}[t_{k}]\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
denotes the independent innovation component that correlates
\mathbf{h}_{\textnormal{AB}}[t_{k}]
and the (outdated) estimated CSI
{\mathbf{h}}_{\textnormal{AB}}[\tau]
.
Hereafter, we drop the time indices and denote the delayed CSI as
{\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{\textnormal{AB}}={\mathbf{h}}_{\textnormal{AB}}[\tau]}
for convenience.

II-E

Ground-to-Air Signal-to-Noise Ratio

In the first time slot, the BS applies the beamforming vector
\mathbf{w}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}
to steer the desired signal
x_{\textnormal{S}}
to the UAV, where
\textnormal{E}[|x_{{\textnormal{S}}}|^{2}]=1
. Hence, the received signal at the UAV is obtained as

\displaystyle y_{{\textnormal{U}}}=\sqrt{P_{\textnormal{S}}\ell_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}}\left[(\rho_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}+\bar{\rho}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\mathbf{z}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}})^{\sf H}\mathbf{w}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\right]x_{{\textnormal{S}}}+n_{\textnormal{U}},

(14)

where
P_{\textnormal{S}}
[W] denotes the BS’s transmit power and
n_{{\textnormal{U}}}
is the additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) with zero mean and variance
\sigma^{2}_{{\textnormal{U}}}
[W] at the UAV.
We assume that
\ell_{{\textnormal{S}}_{1}{\textnormal{U}}}\approx\ell_{{\textnormal{S}}_{2}{\textnormal{U}}}\approx\dots\approx\ell_{{\textnormal{S}}_{M}{\textnormal{U}}}=\ell_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}
since the maximum dimension of the BS’s antenna array is significantly smaller than its distance to the UAV.
Moreover, we consider that the BS adopts maximal ratio transmission (MRT) using the estimated CSI, where
{\mathbf{w}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}}{\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\right\|}}
.
Hence, the G2A SNR under delayed CSI-based MRT is obtained as

\displaystyle{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{g2a}}}=\frac{P_{\textnormal{S}}\ell_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}}{\sigma_{\textnormal{U}}^{2}}\left|(\rho_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}+\bar{\rho}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}{\mathbf{z}}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}})^{\sf H}\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\right|^{2}\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\right\|^{-2}.

(15)

II-F

Air-to-Ground Signal-to-Noise Ratio

In the second time slot, the UAV decodes
x_{\textnormal{S}}
and forwards the re-encoded version
{\widehat{x}_{{\textnormal{U}}}}
to the RIS.
Next, the RIS reflects the signal
\widehat{x}_{{\textnormal{U}}}
from the UAV to the GUE by intelligently adjusting its phase-shift matrix.
Hence, the received signal at the GUE is obtained as

\displaystyle y_{{\textnormal{D}}}=\sqrt{P_{{\textnormal{U}}}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sqrt{{\ell}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}}h_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}\beta_{n}\sqrt{{\ell}_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}h_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}e^{j\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}\right)\widehat{x}_{\textnormal{U}}+n_{\textnormal{D}},

(16)

where
P_{{\textnormal{U}}}
[W] is the UAV’s transmit power,
n_{\textnormal{D}}
is the AWGN with zero mean and variance
\sigma_{\textnormal{D}}^{2}
[W] at the GUE,
\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
and
{\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}\!=\![0,2\pi)}
are the amplitude reflection coefficient and the phase-shift value of the reflecting element
{n\in{\cal N}}
, respectively
[
33
,
24
]
.
In practice, the number of phase-shifts is limited and constrained by the phase-shift resolution, denoted by
{Q\triangleq 2^{b}}
, where
b
is the number of quantization bits and the value of a phase shift belongs to the set
{\cal Q}=\left\{0,\frac{2\pi}{Q},\frac{4\pi}{Q},...,\frac{2\pi(Q-1)}{Q}\right\}

[
35
]
.
Hence, the SNR received at the GUE is obtained as

\displaystyle{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{\textnormal{U}}}{\sigma_{\textnormal{D}}^{2}}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sqrt{{\ell}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}}h_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}\beta_{n}\sqrt{{\ell}_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}h_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}e^{j\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}\right|^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle\mathop{\approxeq}\limits^{(a)}\bar{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}\left|\mathbf{h}_{{\textnormal{R}}{\textnormal{D}}}^{\sf H}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{{\textnormal{R}}}\mathbf{h}_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}}\right|^{2},\forall n\in{\cal N},

(18)

where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{{\textnormal{R}}}\triangleq\textnormal{diag}\left(\beta_{1}e^{j\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{1}}},\dots,\beta_{N}e^{j\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{N}}}\right)
represents the phase-shift matrix,

\bar{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}\triangleq\frac{P_{\textnormal{U}}}{\sigma_{\textnormal{D}}^{2}}\ell_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}\ell_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}
for any
n\in{\cal N}
when the RIS’s maximum dimension being considerably smaller than the distances
d_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}
and
d_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}

[
35
,
23
]
. It is noted that our analysis is applicable when the RIS dimensions are non-negligible.
In this case, we define
\ell_{\mathsf{a2g}}\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sqrt{\ell_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}}\beta_{n}\sqrt{\ell_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}\right)^{2}
as the average effective A2G path loss, and the terms
\beta_{n}
and
\bar{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}
must be modified to
\frac{\sqrt{\ell_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}}\beta_{n}\sqrt{\ell_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}}{\sqrt{\ell_{\textnormal{R}}}}
and
\frac{P_{\textnormal{U}}\ell_{\mathsf{a2g}}}{\sigma_{\textnormal{D}}^{2}}
, respectively.

For the A2G communication, we propose to characterize the A2G channel distribution conditioned on the estimated CSI. Then, the PDF and CDF of the A2G SNR are derived.
Moreover, we focus on the distributions of the LoS communication scenario since the NLoS communication scenario can be analogously obtained by setting the Rician-

The PDF and CDF of this distribution can be represented by an infinite series of modified Bessel functions

In Theorem

Theorem 2

In this case, the PDF of the RIS-assisted A2G SNR is obtained as

\displaystyle f_{{\gamma}_{\mathsf{a2g}}}(z)

\displaystyle=

and

\displaystyle f_{K}(a,\alpha;x)=x^{\frac{a+\alpha}{2}-1}\frac{2K_{a-\alpha}(2\sqrt{x})}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(\alpha)}=f_{K}(\alpha,a;x),

(58)

for
x>0
specifies the PDF of the product of two non-IID
\chi^{2}

In Theorem

.
Some special instances include

For large arguments, i.e., large

Corollary 3

Under arbitrary PSCs, the CDF of the RIS-assisted A2G SNR is obtained by replacing

III-C

Phase-Shift Configurations

From (
49
) and (
51
) in Proposition
3
, we assert that the average A2G SNR, derived as

\displaystyle\textnormal{E}\left[{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}\right]=\bar{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}\left(\textnormal{E}\left[\sigma_{Z}^{2}\right]+\textnormal{E}\left[\left|\mu_{Z}\right|^{2}\right]\right),

(59)

is a increasing function of
\real\left(\textnormal{E}\left[\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{m}}^{\ast}\right]\right)
, upper bounded by
\textnormal{E}\left[\left|\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}\right|\right]\textnormal{E}\left[\left|\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{m}}\right|\right]
. Thus, configuring phase shifts to increase this value correspondingly improves the A2G SNR.
Considering PSCs where
\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}
and
\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{m}}
are
statistically independent
for
m\neq n
, we have
{\textnormal{E}\left[\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{m}}^{\ast}\right]=\textnormal{E}\left[\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}\right]\textnormal{E}^{\ast}\left[\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{m}}\right]}
.
This is the case for the delayed CSI-based PSC (DPSC) where we have
{\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}=-\angle\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}
for
n\in{\cal N}
, which does not exploit phase shift values from other reflecting elements.
It is noted that the DPSC
yields the maximum average A2G SNR since

\displaystyle\real\left(\textnormal{E}\left[\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{m}}^{\ast}\right]\right)=\textnormal{E}\left[\left|\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}\right|\right]\textnormal{E}\left[\left|\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{m}}\right|\right].

(60)

However, the DPSC comes with the trade-off of increasing computational complexity within the RIS, which becomes particularly significant as the number of RIS elements scales into the hundreds.
In practice, the RIS can utilizes other less CSI-demanding PSCs while minimizing performance degradation.
For PSCs that disregard the information from NLoS components in the estimated CSI, we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{E}\left[\hat{\mu}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}\right]=\sqrt{\frac{K_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}}}{K_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}}+1}\frac{K_{{\textnormal{R}}{\textnormal{D}}}}{K_{{\textnormal{R}}{\textnormal{D}}}+1}}\bar{h}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}^{\ast}\bar{h}_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}\Phi_{\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}(j\omega|_{\omega=1}),

(61)

where
\Phi_{\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}(j\omega)
is the CF of the phase shift
\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}
for
{n\in{\cal N}}
and
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.
Three common PSCs that fall into this category are:

+

Random PSCs:
\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}
follows a specified distribution. The most common adopted distribution is the uniform phase-shift within the interval
[a,b]\subseteq[-\pi,\pi]
where we obtain
\Phi_{\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}(j\omega|_{\omega=1})=\frac{\sin b-\sin a}{b-a}+j\frac{\cos a-\cos b}{b-a}
.

+

Fixed-value (static) PSCs:
\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}
is fixed and
\Phi_{\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}(j)=e^{j\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}
for
n\in{\cal N}
. It is noted that
\vartheta_{1}=\dots=\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{N}}=0
depicts the communication scenario without PSCs.

+

LoS-based PSCs:
\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}
is a function of the LoS component. Here,
\vartheta_{{\textnormal{R}}_{l}}=-\angle\bar{h}_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}+\angle\bar{h}_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}
yields the highest expected value.

III-D

Performance Analysis

For performance analysis, we consider the end-to-end outage probability (eOP), defined as the probability that end-to-end SNR drops below a fixed target average SNR threshold
{\bar{\gamma}_{\textrm{th}}=2^{2R}-1}
, where
R
[bps/Hz] denotes the target spectral efficiency (SE). The eOP is formulated as
[
10
]

\displaystyle P_{\mathtt{e2e}}^{\textrm{out}}(\bar{\gamma}_{\textrm{th}})

\displaystyle=\Pr\left\{\min\left[{\gamma}_{\mathsf{g2a}},{\gamma}_{\mathsf{a2g}}\right]<\bar{\gamma}_{\textrm{th}}\right\}

(62)

\displaystyle\mathop{=}^{(a)}1-\left[1-F_{{\gamma}_{\mathsf{g2a}}}(\bar{\gamma}_{\textrm{th}})\right]\left[1-F_{{\gamma}_{\mathsf{a2g}}}(\bar{\gamma}_{\textrm{th}})\right],

(63)

where
(a)
is the result of
{\gamma}_{\mathsf{g2a}}
and
{\gamma}_{\mathsf{a2g}}
being non-IID, with
F_{{\gamma}_{\mathsf{g2a}}}(x)
given in (
23
), and
F_{{\gamma}_{\mathsf{a2g}}}(x)
presented in Corollary
3
.

Additionally, when the BS transmits with sufficiently large power, we simplify the G2A SNR CDF as

\displaystyle F_{{\gamma}_{\mathsf{g2a}}}(x)\to e^{-\frac{MK_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\rho_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}^{2}}{K_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\bar{\rho}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}^{2}+1}}\frac{(K_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}+1)^{M}(\bar{\rho}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}^{2})^{M-1}}{(K_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\bar{\rho}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}^{2}+1)^{M}}\frac{x}{\bar{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{g2a}}}},

(64)

for
x>0
, and where
(x)_{n}\triangleq\frac{\Gamma(x+n)}{\Gamma(x)}
is the Pochhammer symbol.
Moreover, when
N
is asymptotically large, e.g., hundreds of reflecting elements, the CLT yields
{\sigma_{Z}^{2}\!\to\!\textnormal{E}\left[\sigma_{Z}^{2}\right]}
. Denoting

\Omega_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}\triangleq\bar{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}\textnormal{E}\left[\sigma_{Z}^{2}\right]\!=\!\frac{P_{\textnormal{U}}}{\sigma_{\textnormal{D}}^{2}}(1-\rho_{{\textnormal{R}}{\textnormal{D}}}^{2}\rho_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}}^{2})\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\ell_{{\textnormal{R}}_{n}{\textnormal{D}}}\beta_{n}^{2}\ell_{{\textnormal{U}}{\textnormal{R}}_{n}}}
,
and since the normal (Gaussian) and the noncentral
\chi^{2}
distributions are related as

\widetilde{\chi}_{k_{\cal R}}^{2}\left(\lambda_{\cal R}\right)\to^{d}{\cal N}\left(k_{\cal R}+\lambda_{\cal R},k_{\cal R}+2\lambda_{\cal R}\right)
when
\lambda_{\cal R}
is sufficiently large, the A2G SNR CDF can be simplified as

\displaystyle F_{{\gamma}_{\mathsf{a2g}}}(x)\to 1-\textrm{Q}\left(\frac{\frac{x}{\bar{\gamma}_{\cal R}\Omega_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}}-\left(k_{\cal R}+\lambda_{\cal R}\right)}{\sqrt{k_{\cal R}+2\lambda_{\cal R}}}\right),~x>0,

(65)

where
\textrm{Q}(x)
is the Q-function. It is noted that (
65
) is applicable even when the maximum dimension of the RIS is non-negligible, e.g., with hundreds of reflecting elements.

IV
Mobility Model

In this section, we consider a case study where the GUE follows the Random Waypoint Mobility (RWM) model. The RWP model is adopted to capture the random and unpredictable nature of the GUE’s motion
[
42
]
.
Moreover, the mobility of the UAV is characterized by the Reference Point Group Mobility (RPGM) model to introduce a degree of deviation in the UAV’s motion relative to the GUE’s motion
[
43
]
.

IV-A

Random Waypoint Mobility (RWM) Model

The RWM model assumes that the GUE is initially moving from a source waypoint
\mathbf{w}^{{\mathtt{src}}}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)
[m] to a destination waypoint
\mathbf{w}^{{\mathtt{des}}}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)
[m].
Here, the waypoints are statistically IID and uniformly distributed within the same active area
{\mathcal{R}}_{\textnormal{D}}

[
42
]
.
During the movement from
\mathbf{w}^{{\mathtt{src}}}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)
to
\mathbf{w}^{{\mathtt{des}}}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)
, the GUE’s velocity
{\mathbf{v}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}
[m/sec] remains constant and is determined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)=v_{\textnormal{D}}(t)\frac{\mathbf{w}^{{\mathtt{des}}}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)-\mathbf{w}_{{\textnormal{D}}}^{{\mathtt{src}}}(t)}{\left\|\mathbf{w}^{{\mathtt{des}}}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)-\mathbf{w}_{{\textnormal{D}}}^{{\mathtt{src}}}(t)\right\|}\triangleq v_{\textnormal{D}}(t)\mathbf{r}^{\mathtt{rwp}}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t),

(66)

where
v_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)
[m/sec] is the GUE’s speed, which is uniformly distributed within the interval
[v_{\min},v_{\max}]
, where
v_{\min}
[m/sec] and
v_{\max}
[m/sec] are the minimum and the maximum GUE speeds, respectively, and

\mathbf{r}_{{\textnormal{D}}}^{\mathtt{rwp}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
represents the GUE’s Cartesian direction of movement.
Upon arring at
\mathbf{w}^{{\mathtt{des}}}_{\textnormal{D}}(t)
, the GUE pauses (stays still) with a probability
p_{\textrm{pause}}
for a duration of
\tau_{\textrm{pause}}(t)
, uniformly distributed within the interval
[\tau_{\min},\tau_{\max}]
[sec], where
\tau_{\min}
and
\tau_{\max}
are the minimum and maximum pause durations, respectively. For a probability of
1-p_{\textrm{pause}}
, the GUE moves to a new source waypoint
\mathbf{w}^{{\mathtt{src}}}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t+\Delta\tau(t))=\mathbf{w}^{{\mathtt{des}}}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)
and destination waypoint
\mathbf{w}^{{\mathtt{des}}}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t+\Delta\tau(t))
following the previously presented procedure.

IV-B

Reference Point Group Mobility (RPGM) Model

In the RPGM model
[
43
]
, the UAV is initially positioned uniformly within a circular area
{\mathcal{R}}_{{\textnormal{U}}}
centered at a predefined reference point
\mathbf{p}^{{\mathtt{ref}}}_{{\textnormal{U}}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
This reference point has the same motion as the GUE, i.e.,
{\mathbf{p}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t,\tau)=\mathbf{p}^{{\mathtt{ref}}}_{{\textnormal{U}}}(t,\tau)}

[
44
]
.
However, due to imperfect alignment, the UAV’s true motion deviates from the reference point by the motion vector
\Delta\mathbf{p}_{{\textnormal{U}}}(t,\tau)
following a uniform distribution.
Therefore, the time-varying velocity of the UAV is

\mathbf{v}_{{\textnormal{U}}}(t)=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{p}_{{\textnormal{U}}}(t,\tau)}{\tau}=\mathbf{v}_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)+\Delta\mathbf{v}_{{\textnormal{U}}}(t)
,
where
\Delta\mathbf{v}_{{\textnormal{U}}}(t)
[m/sec] is the random deviation in the UAV’s velocity compared to the GUE’s velocity,
\Delta v_{{\textnormal{D}}}(t)\triangleq\left\|\Delta\mathbf{v}_{{\textnormal{U}}}(t)\right\|
denotes the UAV’s speed deviation, which is uniformly distributed within the interval
[\Delta v_{\min},\Delta v_{\max}]
.

IV-C

Adaptive Target Spectral Efficiency

When both the GUE and the UAV are in motion, the eOP widely fluctuate over time under a fixed target SE policy.
This fluctuation poses a challenge to maintaining a consistent and reliable communication system.
To combat this, we introduce an adaptive target approach designed to stabilize the eOP.
This method ensures that the eOP consistently stays below a predetermined threshold
L
, increasing robustness against the unpredictable mobility of the UAV and GUE.

The adaptive target SE, denoted as
\widehat{R}
[bps/Hz], is obtained by solving the eOP constraint

P^{\textrm{out}}_{\mathsf{e2e}}\left(\hat{{\gamma}}_{\textrm{th}}\right)\leq L
for
\hat{{\gamma}}_{\textrm{th}}
, thus
\widehat{R}=\frac{\log_{2}(1+\hat{{\gamma}}_{\textrm{th}})}{2}
.
Here, we adopt the following steps
[
28
]

i) Solve
F_{{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{g2a}}}}\left(\hat{{\gamma}}_{{\mathsf{g2a}}}\right)=L
for
\hat{{\gamma}}_{{\mathsf{g2a}}}
with
F_{{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{g2a}}}}(x)
given in (
64
).

ii) Solve
F_{{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}}\left(\hat{{\gamma}}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}\right)=L
for
\hat{{\gamma}}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}
with
F_{{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}}(x)
given in (
65
).

iii) Compute
\hat{{\gamma}}_{\textrm{th}}=\min\left[\hat{{\gamma}}_{{\mathsf{g2a}}},\hat{{\gamma}}_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}\right]
, yielding

\displaystyle\hat{{\gamma}}_{\textrm{th}}

\displaystyle=\min\bigg[e^{\frac{K_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\bar{\rho}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}^{2}+1}{MK_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\rho_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}^{2}}}\left(\frac{K_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}+1}{K_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}\bar{\rho}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}^{2}+1}\right)^{M}\left(\bar{\rho}_{{\textnormal{S}}{\textnormal{U}}}^{2}\right)^{M-1}\bar{\gamma}_{{\mathsf{g2a}}}L,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\Omega_{{\mathsf{a2g}}}\left(\textrm{Q}^{-1}(1\!-\!L)\sqrt{\textnormal{Var}\left[{\gamma}_{\cal R}\right]}+\textnormal{E}\left[{\gamma}_{\cal R}\right]\right)\bigg],

(67)

where
\textrm{Q}^{-1}(x)
is the inverse Q-function, i.e.,
\textrm{Q}^{-1}(\textrm{Q}(x))=x
.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed a comprehensive channel model that captures the time-varying fading, 3D mobility, Doppler shifts, and array antenna structures under the impact of channel aging.
Our analytical expressions for the G2A SNR PDF and CDF, based on the noncentral
\chi^{2}
distribution, demonstrated that the G2A SNR follows a mixture of noncentral
\chi^{2}
distributions.
The statistical characteristics of the A2G communication is thoroughly studied, revealing that the A2G SNR can be modeled as the product of two independent noncentral
\chi^{2}
RVs.
Furthermore, we have obtained precise PDF and CDF expressions for the product of two non-IID noncentral
\chi^{2}
RVs to derive the A2G SNR distribution.
Our results revealed that the RIS is effective against channel aging as long as the communication is carried out within a coherence time.
Lastly, our adaptive SE method ensures stable system performance at satisfactory outage levels.
