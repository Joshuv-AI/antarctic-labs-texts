Abstract
. We study the light-cone Siegel transform, transforming functions on the light cone of a rational indefinite quadratic form Q Q to a function on the homogenous space SO Q + ⁡ ( ℤ ) \ SO Q + ⁡ ( ℝ ) \operatorname{SO}^{+}_{Q}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\operatorname{SO}^{+}_{Q}(\mathbb{R}) . In particular, we prove a second moment formula for this transform for forms of signature ( n + 1 , 1 ) (n+1,1) , and show how it can be used for various applications involving counting integer points on the light cone. In particular, we prove some new results on intrinsic Diophantine approximations on ellipsoids as well as on the distribution of values of random linear and quadratic forms on the light cone.



1.
Introduction

The Siegel transform, introduced in
[
Sie45
]
, transforms a function,
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of sufficient decay, to a function
Sf
on
X_{n}
, the space of unimodular lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by averaging over the lattice.
The space of lattices
X_{n}
can be identified with the homogenous space
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and is equipped with a natural
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
-invariant probability measure; the Siegel mean value theorem expresses the average of
Sf
in terms of the average of
f
.
Later, generalizing Siegel’s result, Rogers
[
Rog55
]
proved a general
k
-th moment formula for
Sf
for
k
up to
n-1
.
While in general these formulas are quite involved, the second moment formula for
Sf
is relatively simple, and can be expressed in terms of the first and (twisted) second moment of
f
.
A masterful application of the second moment formula, was given by Schmidt
[
Sch60b
]
, who proved very strong asymptotic formulas counting lattice points in an increasing family of sets.

In recent years there have been some renewed interests in these moment formulas, which were used in a number of new applications for problems in geometry of numbers and Diophantine approximations.
To list just a few examples, in
[
AM09
]
Athreya and Margulis used the second moment formula to prove a random version of Minkowski’s theorem;
while in
[
AGT15
]
, by using Siegel’s mean value theorem, together with Birkhoff ergodic
theorem, Athreya, Ghosh and Tseng proved a quantitative variant of Dirichlet’s theorem with approximants restricted in certain fixed directions.
This was extended in
[
AGY21
]
by Alam, Ghosh and Yu, who proved a quantitative variant of Khintchine’s theorem with a congruence condition. The strategy used in
[
AGY21
]
follows mostly that of
[
AGT15
]
, but with the ergodic theorem replaced by Schmidt’s lattice point counting arguments which rely on moment formulas of Siegel transforms.
In another direction, Athreya and Margulis
[
AM18
]
found another novel use of the second moment formula to study value distribution of random quadratic forms at integers points improving on previous results of

[
GK18
,
GGN20
]
using homogenous dynamics. The method of Athreya and Margulis was further refined by the authors in
[
KY20
]
, giving an asymptotic counting formula for the number of integer solution of a generic form in a shrinking interval, as well as treating more general homogenous polynomials of higher degrees. Similar results were later obtained for more general functions in
[
BGH21
,
KS21
,
GKY22
]
using the second moment formula as a main ingredient. Finally we mention the results of
[
BG23a
,
BG23b
]
in which these moment formulas were a crucial ingredient for proving various limiting
laws for counting lattice points and some related problems in geometry of numbers.

Following this renewed interest in moment formulas for the Siegel transform, in
[
KY21
]
it was observed that the Siegel transform can be seen as an incomplete Eisenstein series, and one can use the spectral theory of Eisenstein series to give an alternative proof of the second moment formula. The advantage of this viewpoint is that it opens the way to prove similar moment formulas when the homogenous space
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is replaced by other homogenous spaces. To showcase this approach in
[
KY21
]
the authors proved a new second moment formula for Siegel transforms restricted to the space of symplectic lattices
\operatorname{Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\operatorname{Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{R})
. More recently, using this spectral approach, together with Ghosh
[
GKY22
]
we proved a new second moment formula for Siegel transforms defined on a certain congruence cover of the space of lattices.
We mention that in all these new moment formulas, the Siegel transforms under consideration are all defined such that they take a function on the Euclidean space to a function on the corresponding homogeneous space. In this paper we apply this spectral approach to study a new type of Siegel transform where the Euclidean space is replaced by the light cone of a quadratic form.
Below we prove first and second moment formulas for this Siegel transform and use these moment formulas to prove some new results on intrinsic Diophantine approximation on ellipsoids as well on the value distribution of random linear and quadratic forms on the light cone.

1.1.
Light-cone Siegel transform and its moment formulas

Fix an integer
n\geq 1
, and let
Q:\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a rational
\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic quadratic form of signature
(n+1,1)
. Recall that
Q
is
\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic
means that there exists a nonzero
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n+2}
such that
Q(\bm{v})=0
; note that
in view of Meyer’s theorem (see e.g.
[
Ser73
, p. 43]
) this assumption is satisfied as soon as
n\geq 3
.
The light cone of
Q
is defined as

\mathcal{V}_{Q}:=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}:Q(\bm{v})=0\right\}.

It has two connected components and we let
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
be one of these components; see section
2.1.1
for more details. Since
Q
is assumed to be
\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic, the set of integer points
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}):=\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n+2}
is infinite. We also let
\mathcal{L}_{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})
be the subset of primitive integer points.
Let
G=\operatorname{SO}_{Q}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
be the identity component of the special orthogonal group preserving
Q
and let
\Gamma=\operatorname{SO}_{Q}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}):=G\cap\operatorname{SL}_{n+2}(\mathbb{Z})
be the subgroup of integer points (which is a non-uniform lattice of
G
since
Q
is
\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic).
Note that
G
acts transitively on
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
via right multiplication and that the induced action of
\Gamma
on
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
preserves
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
. We can thus parameterize the space of
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
-translates by the homogeneous space
Y_{Q}=\Gamma\backslash G
via the map
\Gamma g\in\Gamma\backslash G\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{Q}g
. Generalizing the classical Siegel transform, given a function
f:\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
of sufficient decay, its
light-cone Siegel transform
, is a function on
Y_{Q}
, defined for any
g\in G
by

\displaystyle S_{Q}f(g):=\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{L}_{Q}}f(\bm{v}g).

Our main goal of this paper is to prove moment formulas, relating moments of
S_{Q}f
to integrals of the original function
f
defined on
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
.

When taking the light cone Siegel transform of the function
f(\bm{v})=\|\bm{v}\|_{Q}^{-s}
for
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
an appropriate norm (see (
2.33
) below) and
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)>n
, we get the
light-cone Eisenstein series
,
E_{Q}(s,g)
, that was studied in
[
KY22
]
. In particular, it was shown there that
E_{Q}(s,g)
has an analytic continuation with a simple pole at
s=n
with constant residue

\omega_{Q}:=\operatorname{Res}_{s=n}E_{Q}(s,g)

and at most one more exceptional simple pole in the half plane
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)\geq\frac{n}{2}
located at
s_{n}:=\left\lfloor{\tfrac{n+2}{2}}\right\rfloor
with residue
\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{n}}E_{Q}(s,g)\in L^{2}(Y_{Q})
(when
n=1,2
we have
s_{n}=n
and there is no exceptional pole); see section
2.4
below for more details. Moreover, also for
n\geq 3
it is shown that, in many examples, there is no exceptional pole; see
Remark
1.3
below.

Before we state the moment formulas, we need to fix some measures. Let
\mu_{Q}
be the unique
G
-invariant probability measure on
Y_{Q}
. Similarly, there is also a unique (up to scaling)
G
-invariant measure on
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
, which we fix and denote by
m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q}}
(see (
2.7
) below). One should think of this measure as the counterpart of the Lebesgue measure in the classical Siegel transform setting. For simplicity of notation, for any function
f
on
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
we will abbreviate the space average
\int_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}f(\bm{v})\ \text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(\bm{v})
simply by
m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f)
. With these measures and notation we have the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f:\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
be measurable, bounded and compactly supported. Then we have

(1.1)

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{Q}}S_{Q}f(g)\,\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g)=\omega_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f).

Further assume
f
is smooth, then

(1.2)

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{Q}}\left|S_{Q}f(g)\right|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g)=|\omega_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f)|^{2}+c_{Q}M_{f,f}\left(s_{n}\right)+O\left(m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(|f|^{2})\right),

where
s_{n}=\left\lfloor{\tfrac{n+2}{2}}\right\rfloor
, the term
M_{f,f}(s)
is a quadratic form on
f
given in (
2.26
) and
c_{Q}\geq 0
with
c_{Q}=0
if and only if
E_{Q}(s,g)
has no exceptional pole.

Remark 1.3
.

Since some of our applications are nicer when there is no secondary term, i.e. when
c_{Q}=0
, it is worthwhile to give some explicit examples for which we know this is the case. First, when
n=1,2
there is no exceptional pole for any form. Next, for the standard forms

(1.4)

Q_{n}(\bm{v})=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}v_{j}^{2}-v_{n+2}^{2}

it follows from
[
KY22
, Theorem 4.4]
that
c_{Q_{n}}>0
if and only if
n>1
with
n\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8)
. In fact, the same holds for the forms
Q_{n,d}(\bm{v})=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}v_{j}^{2}-d^{2}v_{n+2}^{2}
with
d
odd and square-free.
For a general rational
\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic form,
Q(\bm{v})=\bm{v}^{t}J\bm{v}
, of signature
(n+1,1)
it follows from
[
KY22
, Theorem 1.8]
that
c_{Q}=0
when
n\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
and
\sqrt{-\det(J)}
is irrational or when
n\equiv 2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)
.

For our applications to counting problems, we take the function
f
to be an indicator function of a measurable set
B\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
of finite measure and, after approximating
f
by smooth functions, we get mean square bound for the discrepancy

(1.5)

D(\mathcal{L}_{Q}g,B)=|\#(\mathcal{L}_{Q}g\cap B)-\omega_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)|.

When
c_{Q}=0
, there is no secondary term in our second moment formula and our variance bound is sharp and holds for any finite-measure set. When
c_{Q}>0
some work is needed in order to control the secondary term. While this secondary term is given by some explicit formula, it is in general difficult to bound without imposing additional restrictions on the set
B
(e.g., assuming that it is given by dilation of some fixed set or a generalized sector (see
Definition
2.37
)). This restriction is responsible for some constraints in our later applications.
In the following, let

\displaystyle\beta_{Q}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&c_{Q}=0,\\
\frac{2s_{n}}{n}&c_{Q}>0.\end{array}\right.

We prove the following variance bound.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
B\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
be a Borel set of finite measure. If
c_{Q}>0
we further assume that
B
is a generalized sector. We then have

(1.8)

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{Q}}\left|D(\mathcal{L}_{Q}g,B)\right|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g)\ll_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)^{\beta_{Q}}+m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B).

Remark 1.9
.

We note that for the case when
c_{Q}>0
, the exponent
\beta_{Q}
is optimal. In fact, given any family of dilations
\{tB\}_{t>1}
, their measure grows like
m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(tB)=t^{n}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)
and the secondary terms
M_{\chi_{tB},\chi_{tB}}(s)=t^{\frac{2s}{n}}M_{\chi_{B},\chi_{B}}(s)
for any
s\in(\frac{n}{2},n)
(see
Remark
2.31
). From this and the moment formulas in
Theorem
1.1
we get that when
c_{Q}>0
, for any
t>1
,

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{Q}}D(\mathcal{L}_{Q}g,tB)^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g)\asymp_{B}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(tB)^{\beta_{Q}}.

1.2.
Applications to counting points on the light cone

We now discuss several applications of our moment formulas to counting primitive integer points on the light cone. Fix
\beta_{Q}
as in (
1.1
).
First, we can apply Schmidt’s arguments with the variance bounds in
Corollary
1.2
to get the following result on counting points of generic
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
-translates in increasing sets. Here a family of Borel sets
\{B_{t}\}_{t>0}
in
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
is called
increasing
if
B_{t_{1}}\subseteq B_{t_{2}}
whenever
t_{1}<t_{2}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\{B_{t}\}_{t>0}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
be an increasing family of Borel sets of finite measure. If
c_{Q}>0
we further assume either
B_{t}
are all generalized sectors and that the function
t\mapsto m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B_{t})
is continuous, or that
B_{t}=tB
are dilations of some fixed star-shaped set
B
. Then we have for
\mu_{Q}
-a.e.
\Gamma g\in Y_{Q}
and for all sufficiently large
t
,

\displaystyle\#(\mathcal{L}_{Q}g\cap B_{t})=\omega_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B_{t})+O_{g,\epsilon}\left(m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B_{t})^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\beta_{Q}}{2}}+\epsilon}\right).

Next, following the strategy outlined in
[
KY20
]
we can also apply the moment formulas to study the value distribution of random functions at the discrete set
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
.
As explained in
[
KY20
]
, such results follow from a second moment formula together with effective volume estimates for the functions under consideration.
We will prove such volume estimates for both linear and quadratic forms (see
Proposition
4.5
and
Proposition
4.7
), and thus proving the desired quantitative results concerning the value distribution of these functions.
We note that although both results follow from the same strategy, the volume estimates for quadratic forms are much more involved and require extra assumptions; we thus state these results separately.
We first state our result for a linear form, or rather several linear forms simultaneously.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume
n\geq 2
and
c_{Q}=0
. Let
m<n
be a positive integer and let
\{\Omega_{T}\}_{T>1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a decreasing family of bounded measurable sets with
\operatorname{vol}(\Omega_{T})=T^{-a}
for some
0<a<n-m
. Then there is some
\nu>0
such that for almost every linear map
L:\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
Q|_{\ker{L}}
indefinite, for all sufficiently large
T

\#\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{L}_{Q}:\|\bm{v}\|_{Q}\leq T,\;\bm{v}L\in\Omega_{T}\}=c(Q,L)T^{n-m-a}(1+O(T^{-\nu})),

where
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
is a certain norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
depending on
Q
(see (
2.33
) below) and
c(Q,L)
is some positive constant depending only on
Q
and
L
.

Remark 1.10
.

Using a classification result of Sargent
[
Sar14
, Lemma 2.2]
(see also
Lemma
4.1
and
Lemma
4.2
below), we will make the notion of “almost every” in
Theorem
1.4
(as well as in
Theorem
1.6
below) more precise; see
Remark
4.3
.

By taking the sets
\Omega_{T}
to be shrinking norm balls in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
we get the following corollary extending the result of
[
GGN20
, Theorem 1.3]
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Assume
n\geq 2
and
c_{Q}=0
. Let
m<n
be a positive integer and let
\kappa>{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{m}{n-m}}
. Given any
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for almost every linear map
L:\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
Q|_{\ker{L}}
indefinite, for all
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small the system of inequalities

(1.11)

\|\bm{v}L-\bm{\xi}\|<\epsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\|\bm{v}\|<\epsilon^{-\kappa}

has a solution
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{L}_{Q}
.

Remark 1.12
.

As explained in
[
GGN20
]
the threshold of
\kappa>{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{m}{n-m}}
is optimal as can be seen from the pigeonhole principle.
When
n=m+1

Corollary
1.5
follows from
[
GGN20
, Theorem 1.3]
, but for larger
n
their method gives a worse threshold for
\kappa
. We should note however that the method of
[
GGN20
]
deals with more general quadratic surfaces of the form
\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}:Q(\bm{v})=k\}
for any rational
k\in\mathbb{Q}
and not just
k=0
and also does not have the restriction
c_{Q}=0
on the quadratic form.

For quadratic forms on the light cone we need to introduce one more definition. Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real quadratic form of rank
m<n
, that is, it has a null subspace
V_{\rm{null}}(F)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
(i.e.
F|_{V_{\rm{null}}(F)}=0
) of dimension
n+2-m
. We say that
F
is
indefinite on
\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q}
if both
F
and
Q|_{V_{\rm{null}}(F)}
are indefinite.

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume
n\geq 3
and
c_{Q}=0
. Let
m<n
be a positive integer and let
\{I_{T}\}_{T>1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a decreasing family of bounded measurable sets with
|I_{T}|=T^{-a}
for some
0<a<n-2
. Then there is some
\nu>0
such that for almost every rank
m
quadratic form
F
that is indefinite on
\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q}
and for all sufficiently large
T

\displaystyle\#\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{L}_{Q}:\|\bm{v}\|_{Q}\leq T,\ F(\bm{v})\in I_{T}\right\}=c(Q,F)T^{n-2-a}(1+O(T^{-\nu})),

where
c(Q,F)
is some positive constant depending only on
Q
and
F
.

We note that the assumption
c_{Q}=0
in both
Theorem
1.4
and
Theorem
1.6
comes from the fact that the underlying sets within which we count are not generalized sectors.
Finally we mention that we will prove a similar counting result for a certain more general family of homogeneous functions and
Theorem
1.6
will be a special case of this result when the degree is two; see
Theorem
4.4
.

1.3.
Application to intrinsic Diophantine approximations on ellipsoids

In
[
KM15
]
, Kleinbock and Merril used dynamical methods to study problems in intrinsic Diophantine approximations on the sphere, that is, the question of how well can one approximate a point on the sphere by rational points on the sphere of a bounded height (where the height of a rational point

\frac{\bm{p}}{q}
is the denominator
q
when written in lowest terms). Their methods and results were then generalized by Fishman, Kleinbock, Merrill, and Simmons
[
FKMS22
]
to deal with more general nonsingular rational quadratic hypersurfaces. In what follows we give a refined and quantitative version of many of their results.

Before stating our results, let us first fix some notation. For the remaining of this section we consider rational
\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic quadratic forms of the form

(1.13)

\displaystyle Q(\bm{x},y)=\mathcal{Q}(\bm{x})-y^{2}

with
\mathcal{Q}
a positive definite rational quadratic form in
n+1
variables. Let

(1.14)

\displaystyle S_{\mathcal{Q}}:=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\mathcal{Q}(\bm{x})=1\right\}

be the ellipsoid associated to
\mathcal{Q}
. For instance when
Q=Q_{n}
is the standard form as in (
1.4
),

S_{\mathcal{Q}}=S^{n}:=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\|\bm{x}\|=1\right\}

is the unit
n
-sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. On
S^{n}
there is a unique rotation-invariant probability measure which we denote by
\sigma_{n}
. In general,
S_{\mathcal{Q}}=S^{n}\tilde{\tau}^{-1}
with
\tilde{\tau}\in\operatorname{GL}_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{x})=\|\bm{x}\tilde{\tau}\|^{2}
. We denote by
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
the probability measure on
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
obtained as the pushforward of
\sigma_{n}
under the right
\tilde{\tau}^{-1}
-multiplication map. We also let
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Q}}
be the norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
given by
\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}:=\sqrt{\mathcal{Q}(\bm{x})}
. For any
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
and
r>0
let

(1.15)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(\bm{x}):=\left\{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}:\|\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}<r\right\}

be the open ball in
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
of radius
r
with center
\bm{x}
, and for any
T>1
consider the following counting function:

(1.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r,T):=\#\left\{\tfrac{\bm{p}}{q}\in\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(\bm{x}):\gcd(\bm{p},q)=1,\ 1\leq q<T\right\}.

Here
\gcd(\bm{p},q)
is the greatest common divisor of the entries of the vector
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
together with the integer
q\in\mathbb{N}
.
Note that for
r\geq 2
this counting function just counts the number of rational points in
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
with denominator bounded by
T
; denote this counting function by
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(T)
. It was shown by Duke in
[
Duk03
]
that

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(T)\sim\kappa_{\mathcal{Q}}T^{n}
for some
\kappa_{\mathcal{Q}}>0
and moreover that the rational points are equidistributed on
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
in the sense that

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r,T)
is asymptotic to
\kappa_{\mathcal{Q}}T^{n}\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(\bm{x}))
. While his results were stated for the special case of
S_{\mathcal{Q}}=S^{2}
, his methods extend to higher dimensions and general ellipsoids.

Going beyond equidistribution, an interesting question (related to intrinsic Diophantine approximation) is how small can one take
r
in terms of
T
and still have this asymptotic formula. A natural threshold is suggested by the results of Fishman, Kleinbock, Merrill, and Simmons
[
FKMS22
, Theorem 5.1]
(and earlier by Kleinbock and Merrill
[
KM15
, Theorem 4.1]
for the special case of spheres), who proved an analogue of Dirichlet’s theorem, showing that there is a constant
C>0
such that for any
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
, there always exists a rational point
\frac{\bm{p}}{q}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
satisfying

\left\|\bm{x}-\frac{\bm{p}}{q}\right\|_{\mathcal{Q}}<\frac{C}{T^{\frac{1}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}}}\quad\text{and}\quad 1\leq q\leq T,

implying in particular that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r,T)>0
for
r\geq CT^{-1/2}
. We now give the following quantitative result for this counting function.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
Q(\bm{x},y)=\mathcal{Q}(\bm{x})-y^{2}
be as in (
1.13
). Let
\kappa_{\mathcal{Q}}=\frac{\omega_{Q}}{n}
and
\beta=\beta_{Q}
. Then there exists a constant
C_{\mathcal{Q}}>0
such that
for any
r,T>0
satisfying

C_{\mathcal{Q}}T^{-\frac{2-\beta}{3-\beta}}\leq r<1
and for any
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
,

(1.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r,T)=\kappa_{\mathcal{Q}}T^{n}\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(\bm{x}))\left(1+O_{\mathcal{Q}}\left(r^{-\frac{(3-\beta)n}{2n+3}}T^{-\frac{(2-\beta)n}{2n+3}}\right)\right).

Remark 1.18
.

Fixing
r
and letting
T\to\infty
the above counting formula shows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r,T)=\kappa_{\mathcal{Q}}T^{n}\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(\bm{x}))\left(1+O_{\mathcal{Q},r}\left(T^{-\frac{(2-\beta)n}{2n+3}}\right)\right),

giving an effective equidistribution result with power saving estimates for the remainder. Moreover, as part of the theorem we have
\kappa_{\mathcal{Q}}=\frac{\omega_{Q}}{n}
. When
Q=Q_{n}
as in (
1.4
), i.e. when
S_{\mathcal{Q}}=S^{n}
,
\omega_{Q_{n}}
was explicitly computed in terms of special values of zeta and
L
-functions in
[
KY22
, Equation (1.7)]
, we thus get an explicit expression for
\kappa_{\mathcal{Q}}
in this case, generalizing a formula of Duke
[
Duk03
, Theorem 1]
for
\kappa_{\mathcal{Q}}
when
n=2
.

Remark 1.19
.

When
c_{Q}=0
(i.e. when
\beta_{Q}=1
) we can get the asymptotic estimate in the full range of
C_{\mathcal{Q}}T^{-1/2}\leq r<1
(where the results of

[
FKMS22
]
imply that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r,T)>0
). We expect the same estimate should hold in general, however, when
c_{Q}>0
we can only establish this asymptotic estimate in a slightly smaller range of
C_{\mathcal{Q}}T^{-\frac{n-2}{2n-2}}\leq r<1
when
n
is even and
C_{\mathcal{Q}}T^{-\frac{n-1}{2n-1}}\leq r<1
when
n
is odd, which is an artifact of the secondary term in the moment formula.

Since the asymptotic results of
Theorem
1.7
apply also to rational
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
the exponent of
T^{-1/2}
can not be improved, (e.g. for
S_{\mathcal{Q}}=S^{n}
and
\bm{x}=(\bm{0},1)
we have
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r,T)=1
for all
r\leq T^{-1/2}
).
For generic
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
, on the other hand, it is expected that one could do even better. Indeed, for
S_{\mathcal{Q}}=S^{n}
, it was shown by Ghosh, Gorodnik and Nevo
[
GGN13
]
for odd
n\geq 5
for almost all
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},T^{-\lambda},T)>0
for
\lambda\in(0,\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{2n})
(and similarly for
\lambda\in(0,\frac{2}{3})
when
n=3
).
For this problem we can improve the above range to the full range of
\lambda\in(0,1)
while still get an asymptotic counting formula with an explicit power saving error term.
When
r=\frac{1}{T}
the main term in the asymptotic formula no longer grows with
T
so
\lambda<1
is a natural limit for such asymptotic estimates.
Nevertheless, our method also works when taking
r=\frac{(\log T)^{\lambda}}{T}
as long as the exponent of
\log(T)
is sufficiently large. Explicitly we show the following.

Theorem 1.8
.

Keep the notation and assumptions as in
Theorem
1.7

and let
\varkappa_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{Q}}}=\tfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+3}{2}\right)\omega_{Q}}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}n(n+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2}{2}\right)}
.

(1)

Let
r_{T}=T^{-\lambda}
for some
0<\lambda<1
. Then for
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
-a.e.
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
and for all
T
sufficiently large

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r_{T},T)=\varkappa_{\mathcal{Q}}T^{n-n\lambda}+O_{Q}\left(T^{(n-n\lambda)(1-\frac{2-\beta}{n+4})}\log(T)+T^{n-n\lambda-2\lambda}\right).

(2)

Let
r_{T}=\tfrac{(\log T)^{\lambda}}{T}
for some
\lambda>\frac{1}{n(2-\beta)}
. Then for
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
-a.e.
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
and for all
T
sufficiently large

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}\left(\bm{x},r_{T},T\right)=\varkappa_{\mathcal{Q}}\left(\log T\right)^{n\lambda}+O_{Q,\lambda}\left(\left(\log T\right)^{n\lambda-\frac{n\lambda(2-\beta)-1}{n+4}}\log\log T\right).

The results of
Theorem
1.8
imply, in particular, that for any
0<\lambda<1
for
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
-a.e.
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
the inequality
\|\bm{x}-\frac{\bm{p}}{q}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}\leq q^{-\lambda}
has infinitely many solutions in
\frac{\bm{p}}{q}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
. One can ask the same question for more general approximating functions
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
. Borrowing the terminology of
[
KM15
]
, we say that a point
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
is
\psi
-approximable
in
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
, if there are infinitely many rational points
\frac{\bm{p}}{q}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
satisfying
\|\bm{x}-\frac{\bm{p}}{q}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}<\psi(q)
.
For any decreasing function
\psi
, if the series
\sum_{q\in\mathbb{N}}q^{n-1}\psi(q)^{n}
is convergent, it follows from the easy half of the Borel-Cantelli lemma that
\bm{x}
is not
\psi
-approximable in
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
for
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
-a.e.
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
(see
[
KM15
, p. 297]
).
Using a dynamical argument it was proved in
[
FKMS22
, Theorem 6.3]
(and
[
KM15
, Theorem 1.3]
for the special case of the sphere) that the converse is also true, under the additional assumption that the function
q\mapsto q\psi(q)
is decreasing.

Their result is analogous to Khintchine’s theorem on Diophantine approximation in Euclidean spaces which has a quantitative counterpart proved by Schmidt
[
Sch60a
]
. We now give a corresponding quantitative refinement of the above result, providing an asymptotic estimate, analogous to the quantitative result of Schmidt. To this end we define the following counting function that for
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
and
T>1
,

(1.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q},\psi}(\bm{x},T):=\#\left\{\tfrac{\bm{p}}{q}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}:\gcd(\bm{p},q)=1,\ \|\bm{x}-\tfrac{\bm{p}}{q}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}<\psi(q),\ 1\leq q<T\right\}.

Note that the function
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q},\psi}(\bm{x},T)
is increasing in
T
and that it is unbounded if and only if
\bm{x}
is
\psi
-approximable in
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
. We also denote by

(1.21)

\displaystyle J_{\psi}(T):=\sum_{1\leq q<T}q^{n-1}\psi(q)^{n}\quad\mbox{and }\quad I_{\psi}(T):=\sum_{1\leq q<T}q^{n-1}\psi(q)^{n+2}.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
Q(\bm{x},y)=\mathcal{Q}(\bm{x})-y^{2}
be as in
Theorem
1.7
and assume
c_{Q}=0
. For any decreasing
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
satisfying that
\lim\limits_{q\to\infty}\psi(q)=0
and
\sum_{q\in\mathbb{N}}q^{n-1}\psi(q)^{n}=\infty
, for
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
-a.e.
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
and for all sufficiently large
T
,

(1.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q},\psi}(\bm{x},T)=n\varkappa_{\mathcal{Q}}J_{\psi}(T)+O_{\mathcal{Q},\psi}\left(J_{\psi}(T)^{\frac{n+3}{n+4}}\log(J_{\psi}(T))+I_{\psi}(T)\right),

where
\varkappa_{\mathcal{Q}}
is as in
Theorem
1.8
.

If the series
\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{n-1}\psi(q)^{n+2}
converges (and in particular if
q\psi(q)
is decreasing), then
I_{\psi}(T)
is bounded and we get a power saving estimate for the remainder.
For a general decreasing function
\psi
, it is not hard to see that the term
I_{\psi}(T)
is dominated by the main term so that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q},\psi}(\bm{x},T)\sim n\varkappa_{Q}J_{\psi}(T)
as
T\to\infty
. In particular, we get the following corollary recovering the result of
[
FKMS22
, Theorem 6.3]
without the additional assumption that
q\psi(q)
is decreasing.

Corollary 1.10
.

Keep the assumptions as in
Theorem
1.9
. For any decreasing function
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
we have that
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
-almost every (respectively almost no)
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
is
\psi
-approximable if and only if the series
\sum_{q\in\mathbb{N}}q^{n-1}\psi(q)^{n}
diverges (respectively converges).

It is reasonable to expect that the same result holds also when
c_{Q}>0
.
We note that for the special case
\psi(q)=c/q
with
c>0
, one can get around this restriction using dynamics as illustrated by Alam and Ghosh in
[
AG22
]
, who proved a similar counting formula for the sphere in all dimensions for this explicit function, and more recent work of Ouaggag
[
Oua23
]
improving on
[
AG22
]
by providing a power-saving estimate on the error term.

1.4.
Structure of the paper

For the readers convenience we give a short outline of the paper describing the general ideas going into the proofs. In section
2
we define the light cone Siegel transform, establish the moment formula, and then use it to prove the mean square bound for the discrepancy.
In section
3
we prove several new results showing how one can obtain counting estimates from a mean square bound on the discrepancy.
We expect these ideas to be useful in a larger context so we state and prove them in greater generality. While for a family of increasing sets, the classical argument of Schmidt gives very good estimates that hold for almost all translates, we give new arguments that can also handle families of sets that are not increasing, as well as estimates that hold for almost all translates from a subgroup or even for all translates; see
Theorem
3.5
,
Theorem
3.7
and
Theorem
3.8
. For the latter we need to assume our sets satisfy suitable well roundedness properties. With these general arguments at hand, our main results are then proved by translating the problem to a counting problem in families of sets and then establishing the corresponding well roundedness properties in each case. Explicitly, in section
4
, we use the classification of linear and quadratic forms on the light cone to translate the problem to a suitable counting problem in a family of sets and establish the corresponding volume estimates for these sets thus proving
Theorem
1.4
and
Theorem
1.6
. In section
5
we translate the problem of counting rational points on the sphere to counting primitive points on certain families of growing sets on the light cone, we then establish corresponding well-roundedness properties and volume estimates for these sets from which we can deduce Theorems
1.7
–
1.9
.

2.
Light-cone Siegel transform and its moment formulas

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a positive integer and let
Q:\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a rational
\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic quadratic form of signature
(n+1,1)
. Here recall that
Q
is
\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic means that there exists a nonzero
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n+2}
such that
Q(\bm{v})=0
. Let
G=\operatorname{SO}_{Q}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
be the identity component of the special orthogonal group preserving
Q
and let
\Gamma=\operatorname{SO}_{Q}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}):=G\cap\operatorname{SL}_{n+2}(\mathbb{Z})
be the lattice of integer points. Let

\mathcal{V}_{Q}:=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}:Q(\bm{v})=0\right\}

be the light cone of
Q
and let
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}\subset\mathcal{V}_{Q}
be a connected component of
\mathcal{V}_{Q}
(which will be chosen more explicitly in section
2.1.1
below). Let

\mathbb{Z}_{\rm pr}^{n+2}:=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+2}:\gcd(\bm{v})=1\right\}

be the set of primitive integer points in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}

and denote by
\mathcal{L}_{Q}:=\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}\cap\mathbb{Z}_{\rm pr}^{n+2}
the subset of primitive integer points in
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
. Since
Q
is
\mathbb{Q}
-isotropic,
\Gamma
is a non-uniform lattice of
G
and
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
is an infinite discrete subset of
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
. Note that the right action of
G
on
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
is transitive, and since
\Gamma<\operatorname{SL}_{n+2}(\mathbb{Z})
, the induced action of
\Gamma
on
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
preserves
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{Q}\gamma=\mathcal{L}_{Q}
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. As described in the introduction the homogeneous space
Y_{Q}=\Gamma\backslash G
parameterizes the space of
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
-translates via
\Gamma g\leftrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{Q}g
.
Generalizing the classical Siegel transform on the space of lattices, for any bounded and compactly supported function
f:\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q}\to\mathbb{C}
we define its
light-cone Siegel transform
by

(2.1)

\displaystyle S_{Q}f(g):=\sum_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{L}_{Q}}f(\bm{v}g),\quad(g\in G).

Since
f
is bounded and compactly supported, for any
g\in G
the series defining
S_{Q}f
is a finite sum and hence absolutely converges. Moreover,
S_{Q}f
is left
\Gamma
-invariant, and can be viewed as a function on
Y_{Q}
. The main goal of this section is to prove the moment formulas of the light-cone Siegel transform stated in
Theorem
1.1
.

such that
P_{i}=k_{i}Pk_{i}^{-1}
, and thus it has a Langlands decomposition
P_{i}=U_{i}A_{i}M_{i}
with
U_{i}=k_{i}Uk_{i}^{-1}
,
A_{i}=k_{i}Ak_{i}^{-1}
and
M_{i}=k_{i}Mk_{i}^{-1}
. We denote by
\Gamma_{U_{i}}:=\Gamma\cap U_{i}
and
\Gamma_{P_{i}}:=\Gamma\cap P_{i}
. By definition,
\Gamma_{U_{i}}
is non-trivial, and indeed it is a finite-index subgroup of
\Gamma_{P_{i}}
; see
[
KY22
, Lemma 2.1]
.

For each
P_{i}
, we fix the
scaling matrix

\tau_{i}=k_{i}a_{y_{i}}
with
k_{i}\in K
as above and
y_{i}>0
the unique number such that
\mu_{L}(\tau_{i}^{-1}\Gamma_{P_{i}}\tau_{i}\backslash L)=1
. The
(spherical) Eisenstein series
corresponding to the
i
-th cusp is then defined
for
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)>n
and
g\in G
by the convergent series

(2.11)

E_{i}(s,g)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\!P_{i}}\backslash\Gamma}y(\tau_{i}^{-1}\gamma g)^{s},

where
y(g)
is the
y
-parameter in the Iwasawa decomposition of
g
, that is,
g=u_{\bm{x}}a_{y(g)}k
. For each
1\leq j\leq\kappa
the Eisenstein series
E_{i}(s,\tau_{j}u_{\bm{x}}g)
(as a function in
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
) is invariant under translation by
\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{U_{j}}\tau_{j}
; it thus has a Fourier expansion with respect to
\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{U_{j}}\tau_{j}\backslash U
and the
constant term
of this Fourier expansion is given by

(2.12)

c_{ij}(s,g):=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{U_{j}}\tau_{j}\backslash U)}\int_{\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{U_{j}}\tau_{j}\backslash U}E_{i}(s,\tau_{j}u_{\bm{x}}g)\,\text{d}\bm{x},

which is known to be of the form

(2.13)

c_{ij}(s,g)=\delta_{ij}y(g)^{s}+\varphi_{ij}(s)y(g)^{n-s}

for some holomorphic function
\varphi_{ij}
defined for
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)>n
; see e.g.
[
CS80
, Chapter 6, (1.11)]
.

The series
E_{i}(s,g)
(and hence also
\varphi_{ij}
) has a meromorphic continuation to the whole
s
-plane, which on the half plane
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)\geq\frac{n}{2}
is holomorphic except for a simple pole at
s=n
(called the
trivial pole
) and possibly finitely many simple poles on the interval
(\frac{n}{2},n)
(called
exceptional poles
). We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}\subseteq(\frac{n}{2},n)
the finite set of exceptional poles of all Eisenstein series of
\Gamma
.

The residue of
E_{i}(s,g)
at
s=n
is a constant which is the same for Eisenstein series at all cusps, given by the reciprocal of the measure of the homogeneous space
Y_{Q}
, that is, for each
1\leq i\leq\kappa
and
g\in G
,

(2.14)

\displaystyle\omega_{\Gamma}:=\operatorname{Res}_{s=n}E_{i}(s,g)=\mu_{G}(Y_{Q})^{-1},

(see e.g.
[
KY22
, Proposition 2.4]
). In view of this relation, the unique
G
-invariant probability measure on
Y_{Q}
, is given by

(2.15)

\displaystyle\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g)=\omega_{\Gamma}\,y^{-(n+1)}\,\text{d}\bm{x}\text{d}y\text{d}\mu_{M}(m)\text{d}\mu_{M\backslash K}(k),

where
g=u_{\bm{x}}a_{y}mk
with
u_{\bm{x}}\in U
,
a_{y}\in A
,
m\in M
and
k\in M\backslash K
.

These Eisenstein series satisfy a functional equation relating
s
and
n-s
, and a consequence of this functional equation is that on the line
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)=\frac{n}{2}
the functions
\varphi_{ij}
(
1\leq i,j\leq\kappa
) are holomorphic and satisfy the bound that

(2.16)

\displaystyle|\varphi_{ij}(s)|\leq 1,\quad\forall\ {\mathfrak{Re}}(s)=\tfrac{n}{2}.

Moreover, all poles of the Eisenstein series come from the poles of the scattering matrix
\varphi_{ij}(s)
and for any
1\leq i,j\leq\kappa
,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Res}_{s=n}\varphi_{ij}(s)=\operatorname{Res}_{s=n}E_{i}(s,g)=\omega_{\Gamma},

and for any of the exceptional poles
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}
, it follows from the Maass-Selberg relations (given in
[
Sel89
, Equation (7.44)]
and

[
CS80
, Chapter 6, (1.62)]
) that

(2.18)

\operatorname{Res}_{s=\sigma}\varphi_{ij}(s)=\left\langle\operatorname{Res}_{s=\sigma}E_{i}(s,\cdot),\operatorname{Res}_{s=\sigma}E_{j}(s,\cdot)\right\rangle,

where the inner product is with respect to
\mu_{Q}
.

2.2.
Relations to incomplete Eisenstein series

Recall that
\Gamma
acts on the discrete set
\mathcal{L}_{Q}
. This action is in general not transitive and the orbits of this action are in one-to-one correspondence with cusps of
\Gamma
(see
[
KY22
, Lemma 2.2]
). This correspondence implies that the light-cone Siegel transform is a sum of incomplete Eisenstein series as follows.

Let
P_{1},\ldots,P_{\kappa}
be a full set of cusp representatives as before. For any bounded and compactly supported function
f:\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q}\to\mathbb{C}
, the
incomplete Eisenstein series attached to
f
at
P_{i}
is defined by

\displaystyle E_{i}(g\,|\,f):=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{P_{i}}\backslash\Gamma}f(\bm{e}_{0}\tau_{i}^{-1}\gamma g),\quad(g\in G)

where
\Gamma_{P_{i}}=\Gamma\cap P_{i}
and
\tau_{i}
is the
i
-th scaling matrix as before.
Since
f
is bounded and compactly supported, the defining series
for
E_{i}(g\,|\,f)
is absolutely convergent and since
E_{i}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}g}\,|\,f)
is left

\Gamma
-invariant, it can be viewed as a function on the homogeneous space
Y_{Q}
. We have the following relation.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
KY22
, Lemma 3.1]
)
.

There exist constants
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\kappa}>0
such that for any bounded and compactly supported function
f:\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
and for any
g\in G
,

\displaystyle S_{Q}f{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(g)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}E_{i}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}g}\,|\,f_{\lambda_{i}}),

where for any
\lambda>0
,
f_{\lambda}(\bm{v}):=f(\lambda^{-1}\bm{v})
.

Remark 2.19
.

The constants
\lambda_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq\kappa
) measure the size of cusps of the lattice
\Gamma
, and are intrinsic to
\Gamma
(hence also to
Q
); see
[
KY22
, Lemmas 2.3 and 3.1]
.

In view of
Lemma
2.1
, we have

(2.20)

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{Q}}S_{Q}f(g)\,\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g)=\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}\int_{Y_{Q}}E_{i}(g\,|\,f_{\lambda_{i}})\,\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g),

and

(2.21)

\displaystyle\langle S_{Q}f,S_{Q}f\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\kappa}\langle E_{i}(\cdot\,|\,f_{\lambda_{i}}),E_{j}(\cdot\,|\,f_{\lambda_{j}})\rangle.

Thus in order to compute the moments of
S_{Q}f
, it suffices to prove moment formulas of incomplete Eisenstein series which can be computed using the spectral approach in
[
KM12
,
Yu17
]
. For reader’s convenience, we outline this spectral computation in next subsection.

2.3.
Moment formulas of incomplete Eisenstein series

Using a standard unfolding argument we can deduce the following first moment formula of
E_{i}(g\,|\,f)
whose standard proof we omit.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any bounded and compactly supported
f:\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
and for each
1\leq i\leq\kappa
,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{Q}}E_{i}(g\,|\,f)\,d\mu_{Q}(g)=\omega_{\Gamma}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f),

where
\omega_{\Gamma}
is the constant residue as in (
2.14
) and
m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}
is the
G
-invariant measure on
\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
as in (
2.7
).

Next, we prove an integration formula for products of two incomplete Eisenstein series.
We first introduce some relevant notation.

Let
c_{\tau}
be the
\tau
-conjugation isomorphism from
K=K_{Q}
to
K_{Q_{n}}
and let
\iota:K_{Q_{n}}\to\operatorname{SO}_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})
be the natural isomorphism sending
k=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\tilde{k}&\\
&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in K_{Q_{n}}
to
\tilde{k}\in\operatorname{SO}_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then the isomorphism
\phi=\iota\circ c_{\tau}
induces an isomorphism of representations between
L^{2}(M\backslash K)
(as a
K
-representation) and
L^{2}(S^{n})
(as a
\operatorname{SO}_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})
-representation). Here the
L^{2}
-norms are with respect to
\mu_{M\backslash K}
and
\sigma_{n}
respectively.
By the classical spherical harmonic analysis, the function space
L^{2}(S^{n})
decomposes into irreducible
\operatorname{SO}_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})
-representations as following:

\displaystyle L^{2}(S^{n})=\bigoplus_{d\geq 0}L^{2}(S^{n},d),

where
L^{2}(S^{n},d)
is the space of degree
d

n+1
variables restricted to
S^{n}
. This in turn induces the following decomposition of
L^{2}(M\backslash K)
into irreducible
K
-representations

\displaystyle L^{2}(M\backslash K)=\bigoplus_{d\geq 0}L^{2}(M\backslash K,d),

where
L^{2}(M\backslash K,d)
is the pre-image of
L^{2}(S^{n},d)
under the above isomorphism between
L^{2}(M\backslash K)
and
L^{2}(S^{n})
.
For each
d\geq 0
, let us fix an orthonormal basis

\{\psi_{d,l}:1\leq l\leq\dim_{\mathbb{C}}L^{2}(M\backslash K,d)\}

for
L^{2}(M\backslash K,d)
.
For any
f:\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
bounded and of compact support, let

(2.23)

\displaystyle f_{d,l}(y):=\int_{M\backslash K}f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)\overline{\psi_{d,l}(k)}\,\text{d}\mu_{M\backslash K}(k).

so that
f
has a
spherical expansion

(2.24)

\displaystyle f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\sum_{d,l}f_{d,l}(y)\psi_{d,l}(k)

in
L^{2}
and also pointwise if
f
is smooth.
For any function
\rho
on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we denote by
\widehat{\rho}(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(y)y^{-(s+1)}\,\text{d}y
(
s\in\mathbb{C}
) its
Mellin transform
whenever this defining integral is absolutely convergent.
Since
\psi_{0,1}=1
is constant, we have

(2.25)

\displaystyle m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{M\backslash K}f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)\,\text{d}\mu_{M\backslash K}(k)y^{-(n+1)}\,\text{d}y=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{0,1}(y)y^{-(n+1)}\,\text{d}y=\widehat{f}_{0,1}(n).

Using the spherical expansion we define the following bilinear form on the space
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
: For any
f,f^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
and any
s\in(\tfrac{n}{2},n)
define

(2.26)

\displaystyle M_{f,f^{\prime}}(s):=\sum_{d,l}P_{d}(s)\widehat{f_{d,l}}(s)\overline{\widehat{f^{\prime}_{d,l}}(s)},

with
P_{1}(s):=1
and
P_{d}(s):=\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{n-s+i}{s+i}
if
d\geq 1
. We note that for each
d\geq 0
,
P_{d}(s)
satisfies the functional equation
P_{d}(s)P_{d}(n-s)=1
. In particular, when
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)=\frac{n}{2}
we have

(2.27)

\displaystyle|P_{d}(s)|^{2}=P_{d}(s)P_{d}(\bar{s})=P_{d}(s)P_{d}(n-s)=1.

We now state our integration formula.

Theorem 2.3
.

For any
1\leq i,j\leq\kappa
, there exists a bounded linear operator
\mathcal{T}_{ij}:L^{2}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
with operator norm
\|\mathcal{T}_{ij}\|_{\rm op}\leq 1
such that for any
f,f^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
,

\displaystyle\langle E_{i}(\cdot\,|\,f),E_{j}(\cdot\,|\,f^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\omega_{\Gamma}^{2}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f)m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(\overline{f^{\prime}})+\omega_{\Gamma}\left\langle\delta_{ij}f+\mathcal{T}_{ij}(f),f^{\prime}\right\rangle+\omega_{\Gamma}\sum_{s_{l}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}}M_{f,f^{\prime}}(s_{l})\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}\varphi_{ij}(s),

Proof.

For each
d\geq 0
, let
\{\psi_{d,l}:1\leq l\leq\dim_{\mathbb{C}}L^{2}(M\backslash K,d)\}
be the orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(M\backslash K,d)
fixed as above. Let
f,f^{\prime}:\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
be two smooth and compactly supported functions. Doing an unfolding argument, using the left
\Gamma
-invariance of
E_{i}(g\,|\,f)
, and using the Haar measure description (
2.15
) we get

(2.28)

\displaystyle\langle E_{i}(\cdot\,|\,f),E_{j}(\cdot\,|\,f^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\omega_{\Gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{M\backslash K}\overline{f^{\prime}(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)}\mathcal{I}_{ij}(f)(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)\,y^{-(n+1)}\,\text{d}y\text{d}\mu_{M\backslash K}(k),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{ij}(f)(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k):=\int_{\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{P_{j}}\tau_{j}\backslash UM}E_{i}(\tau_{j}u_{\bm{x}}ma_{y}k\,|\,f)\,\text{d}\bm{x}\text{d}\mu_{M}(m).

Now write
f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\sum_{d,l}f_{d,l}(y)\psi_{d,l}(k)
and define
\mathcal{T}_{ij}(f)
by

(2.29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{ij}(f)(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{I}_{ij}(f)(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)-\delta_{ij}f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)-\omega_{\Gamma}\widehat{f}_{0,1}(n)

\displaystyle-\sum_{s_{l}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}}\sum_{d,l}P_{d}(s)\widehat{f}_{d,l}(s)y^{n-s}\psi_{d,l}(k)\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}\varphi_{ij}(s).

This relation equivalently gives a formula for
\mathcal{I}_{ij}(f)
. We note that the desired second moment formula can be checked to follow by plugging this formula into (
2.28
) and noting that
\widehat{f}_{0,1}(n)=m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f)
(see (
2.25
)).
Since
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
, any bounded linear operator
\mathcal{T}
defined on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
uniquely extends to a bounded linear operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
with the same operator norm. Clearly
\mathcal{T}_{ij}
is linear; it thus suffices to show
\|\mathcal{T}_{ij}\|_{\rm op}\leq 1
.

To show this we will show that for any
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})

(2.30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{ij}(f)(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\sum_{d,l}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\tfrac{n}{2})}P_{d}(s)\varphi_{ij}(s)\widehat{f}_{d,l}(s)y^{n-s}\psi_{d,l}(k)\,\text{d}s.

Here for any
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and any function
F
on
\mathbb{C}
,
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}F(s)\,\text{d}s:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\sigma+it)\,\text{d}t
. We note that assuming this claim, then applying the Plancherel’s theorem and using the estimate
|P_{d}(s)\varphi_{ij}(s)|\leq 1
for
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)=\frac{n}{2}
(cf. (
2.16
) and (
2.27
)), we see that indeed
\|\mathcal{T}_{ij}(f)\|_{2}\leq\|f\|_{2}
, implying the desired operator norm bound. It thus remains to prove (
2.30
).

Now using the fact that
\Gamma_{U_{j}}
is a finite-index subgroup of
\Gamma_{P_{j}}
(see
[
KY22
, Lemma 2.1]
) we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{ij}(f)(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\frac{1}{[\Gamma_{P_{j}}:\Gamma_{U_{j}}]}\int_{\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{U_{j}}\tau_{j}\backslash U}\int_{M}E_{i}(\tau_{j}u_{\bm{x}}ma_{y}k\,|\,f)\,\text{d}\bm{x}\text{d}\mu_{M}(m).

Next, note that by our normalization,

1=\mu_{L}(\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{P_{j}}\tau_{j}\backslash UM)=\frac{\mu_{L}(\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{U_{j}}\tau_{j}\backslash UM)}{[\Gamma_{P_{j}}:\Gamma_{U_{j}}]}=\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{U_{j}}\tau_{j}\backslash U)}{[\Gamma_{P_{j}}:\Gamma_{U_{j}}]}.

This implies that
[\Gamma_{P_{j}}:\Gamma_{U_{j}}]=\operatorname{vol}(\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{U_{j}}\tau_{j}\backslash U)
. We can then use this identity and change the order of integration to get

\mathcal{I}_{ij}(f)(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\int_{M}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{P}_{ij}(f)}(ma_{y}k)\,\text{d}\mu_{M}(m),

where

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{P}_{ij}(f)}(ma_{y}k):=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(\tau_{j}^{-1}\Gamma_{U_{j}}\tau_{j}\backslash U)}\int_{\tau_{j}^{-1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Gamma_{U_{j}}}\tau_{j}\backslash U}E_{i}(\tau_{j}u_{\bm{x}}ma_{y}k\,|\,f)\,\text{d}\bm{x}.

First if
f
is spherical, i.e.
f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\rho(y)
for some
\rho\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, applying Mellin inversion formula
\rho(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}\widehat{\rho}(s)y^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s}}\,\text{d}s
(
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
) (see e.g.
[
Bum97
, p. 55-56]
) and the constant term formula (
2.13
) we get that for any
\sigma>n

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{P}_{ij}(f)}(ma_{y}k)

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\rho(y)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}\varphi_{ij}(s)\widehat{\rho}(s)y^{n-s}\,\text{d}s.

Next, for functions of the form
f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\rho(y)\psi_{d,l}(k)
by applying suitable raising operators to the above identity (see
[
Yu17
, p. 460-463]
, especially
[
Yu17
, (3.12)]
) we get that
for any
\sigma>n
,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{P}_{ij}(f)}(ma_{y}k)

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}P_{d}(s)\varphi_{ij}(s)\widehat{\rho}(s)y^{n-s}\psi_{d,l}(k)\,\text{d}s.

Finally, for a general function, we can expand
f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\sum_{d,l}f_{d,l}(y)\psi_{d,l}(k)
to get that

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{P}_{ij}(f)}(ma_{y}k)

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)+\sum_{d,l}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}P_{d}(s)\varphi_{ij}(s)\widehat{f}_{d,l}(s)y^{n-s}\psi_{d,l}(k)\,\text{d}s.

In particular, we see that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{P}_{ij}(f)}(ma_{y}k)
is independent of the variable
m\in M
. Thus
\mathcal{I}_{ij}(f)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{P}_{ij}(f)}
, or equivalently,

\left(\mathcal{I}_{ij}(f)-\delta_{ij}f\right)(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\sum_{d,l}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}P_{d}(s)\varphi_{ij}(s)\widehat{f}_{d,l}(s)y^{n-s}\psi_{d,l}(k)\,\text{d}s.

We can now further simplify the right hand side by shifting the contour of integration to the critical line
{\mathfrak{Re}}(s)=\frac{n}{2}
and picking up the contribution of the exceptional poles
s_{l}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}
and the trivial pole
s=n
(and noting that
P_{d}(n)=\delta_{d0}
and
\operatorname{Res}_{s=n}\varphi_{ij}(s)=\omega_{\Gamma}
(see (
2.17
))) to get

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{I}_{ij}(f)-\delta_{ij}f\right)(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\Gamma}\widehat{f}_{0,1}(n)+\sum_{s_{l}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}}\sum_{d,l}P_{d}(s)\widehat{f}_{d,l}(s)y^{n-s}\psi_{d,l}(k)\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}\varphi_{ij}(s)

\displaystyle+\sum_{d,l}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(\tfrac{n}{2})}P_{d}(s)\varphi_{ij}(s)\widehat{f}_{d,l}(s)y^{n-s}\psi_{d,l}(k)\,\text{d}s.

The formula (
2.30
) then follows immediately by comparing (
2.29
) and the above equation. This finishes the proof.

Proof of Theorem

[
KY22
, Theorem 1.8]
that in fact there is at most one such pole located at
s_{n}=\left\lfloor{\tfrac{n+2}{2}}\right\rfloor
if
n\geq 3
and no poles for
n<3
.
The residue
\omega_{Q}
then satisfies the relation

(2.36)

\displaystyle\omega_{Q}=\omega_{\Gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}\lambda_{i}^{n}.

Proof of
Theorem
1.1
.

The first moment formula follows immediately by combining
Lemma
2.2
and the relations (
2.20
) and (
2.36
).

For the second moment formula, further assume
f
is smooth. For each
1\leq i,j\leq\kappa
we use

Theorem
2.3
together with the observation that for any
\lambda>0
we have
m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f_{\lambda})=\lambda^{n}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f)
and the relation (
2.32
) to get that

\displaystyle\langle E_{i}(\cdot\,|\,f_{\lambda_{i}}),E_{j}(\cdot\,|\,f_{\lambda_{j}})\rangle

\displaystyle=\omega_{\Gamma}^{2}\lambda_{i}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{n}|m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f)|^{2}+\omega_{\Gamma}\sum_{s_{l}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}}\lambda_{i}^{s_{l}}\lambda_{j}^{s_{l}}M_{f,f}(s_{l})\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}\varphi_{ij}(s)+O_{Q}\left(m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(|f|^{2})\right).

Next, for each pole
s_{l}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}
, expanding
E_{Q}(s,g)=\sum_{j=1}^{\kappa}\lambda_{j}^{s}E_{j}(s,g)
and taking residues we get that

\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}E_{Q}(s,g)=\sum_{j=1}^{\kappa}\lambda_{j}^{s_{l}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}E_{j}(s,g).

Taking an inner product and using (
2.18
) we get that

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}E_{Q}(s,g)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\kappa}\lambda_{i}^{s_{l}}\lambda_{j}^{s_{l}}\langle\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}E_{i}(s,\cdot),\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}E_{j}(s,\cdot)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\kappa}\lambda_{i}^{s_{l}}\lambda_{j}^{s_{l}}\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}\varphi_{ij}(s).

Using the relations (
2.21
), (
2.36
) we get that

\displaystyle\langle S_{Q}f,S_{Q}f\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\omega_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(f)\right|^{2}+\omega_{\Gamma}\sum_{s_{l}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Gamma}}\|\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}E_{Q}(s,g)\|^{2}M_{f,f}(s_{l})+O_{Q}\left(m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(|f|^{2})\right).

Since
\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{l}}E_{Q}(s,g)=0
unless
s_{l}
is a pole of
E_{Q}(s,g)
we get that the only secondary term comes from the possible pole at
s=s_{n}
and setting

c_{Q}=\omega_{\Gamma}\|\operatorname{Res}_{s=s_{n}}E_{Q}(s,g)\|^{2}
concludes the proof.

∎

2.5.
Applications to discrepancies

In this subsection we prove the discrepancy bound in
Corollary
1.2

using the moment formulas proved above together with some approximating arguments.
As mentioned in the introduction, the case when
c_{Q}>0
is more tricky due to the existence of an exceptional pole. In this case, we need to bound the secondary term which we can only prove for certain Borel sets defined below.

Definition 2.37
.

We say a subset
B\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}
is a
generalized sector
if
B
is of finite measure and its indicator function is of the form
\chi_{B}(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\rho(y)\phi(k)
, where
\rho
and
\phi
are indicator functions of some Borel sets in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
M\backslash K
respectively.

Remark 2.38
.

The main benefit of working with generalized sectors is that their indicator functions naturally
separate the variables
. In practice, we can take
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
of the form
f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\rho(y)\phi(k)
to approximate the indicator function of a generalized sector. For such
f
, the secondary term
M_{f,f}(s_{n})
has a simpler expression (see (
2.39
) below) from which one can invoke some bounds from spherical harmonic analysis and use the fact that
\rho
is close to some indicator function to bound
M_{f,f}(s_{n})
in terms of the
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
norms of
f
. For
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
approximating a general finite-measure Borel set, such a simpler expression is not available and it is not clear to us how to bound
M_{f,f}(s_{n})
.

Proof of
Corollary
1.2
.

First assume that
c_{Q}=0
. In this case, using the second moment formula for smooth compactly supported functions, we can deduce the same formula for measurable, bounded and compactly supported functions. Indeed, for any measurable, bounded and compactly supported
f:\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}:\to\mathbb{C}
, we can find a sequence
\{f_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
with
f_{j}
converging to
f
in
L^{1}
and in
L^{2}
(and hence, after taking a subsequence also pointwise almost everywhere). We then have that
S_{Q}f_{j}
converges pointwise almost everywhere to
S_{Q}f
. To show that this convergence is also in
L^{2}(Y_{Q})
note that the sequence
\{f_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{1}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})\cap L^{2}(\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+})
and we can use the second moment formula to bound for any
i\neq j
,

\|S_{Q}f_{i}-S_{Q}f_{j}\|^{2}=\langle S_{Q}(f_{i}-f_{j}),S_{Q}(f_{i}-f_{j})\rangle\ll_{Q}\|f_{i}-f_{j}\|^{2}_{1}+\|f_{i}-f_{j}\|^{2}_{2}.

Hence the sequence
\{S_{Q}f_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(Y_{Q})
and thus converges to
S_{Q}f
in
L^{2}(Y_{Q})
. We can now write

\langle S_{Q}f,S_{Q}f\rangle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\langle S_{Q}f_{j},S_{Q}f_{j}\rangle
and deduce the second moment formula for
S_{Q}f
from the second moment formula for
S_{Q}f_{j}
. Moreover, if
f=\chi_{B}
is the indicator function of some measurable set
B
of finite measure, then we can approximate
f
pointwise from below by a monotone sequence
\{\chi_{B_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\chi_{B_{j}}
measurable and of compact support. Hence
S_{Q}\chi_{B_{j}}\to S_{Q}f
pointwise monotonously and by the monotone convergence theorem we conclude that the same first and second moment formula holds for
S_{Q}f
.

Now let
f=\chi_{B}
so that
S_{Q}f(g)=\#(\mathcal{L}_{Q}g\cap B)
. Expanding the square

D(\mathcal{L}_{Q}g,B)^{2}=\left|S_{Q}f(g)-\omega_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)\right|^{2}=|S_{Q}f(g)|^{2}+\omega^{2}_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)^{2}-2S_{Q}f(g)\omega_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B),

and applying (
1.1
) we get

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{Q}}\left|D(\mathcal{L}_{Q}g,B)\right|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g)

\displaystyle=\int_{Y_{Q}}\left(S_{Q}f(g)\right)^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g)-\omega_{Q}^{2}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)^{2}.

Recall we assume
c_{Q}=0
and hence (
1.8
) immediately follows from (
1.2
).

Next when
c_{Q}>0
(in particular, we can assume
n\geq 3
), we need to further assume that
B
is a generalized sector. In this case we can write
f=\chi_{B}
as
f(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\rho(y)\phi(k)
for some indicator functions
\rho
and
\phi
and note that
m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)=\widehat{\rho}(n)\mu_{M\backslash K}(\phi)
and
\mu_{M\backslash K}(\phi)=\|\phi\|_{1}=\|\phi\|_{2}^{2}
.

We first assume
f
is of compact support. While we cannot apply the second moment formula directly to
f
, we can find a sequence of smooth non-negative compactly supported functions
f_{j}(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\rho_{j}(y)\phi_{j}(k)
with
\rho_{j}\to\rho
and
\phi_{j}\to\phi
pointwise almost everywhere. Then also
S_{Q}f_{j}\to S_{Q}f
pointwise almost everywhere. Since
f
is of compact support we can find some non-negative, smooth compactly supported function dominating
f
and all
f_{j}
and then by dominated convergence we have
\langle S_{Q}f,S_{Q}f\rangle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\langle S_{Q}f_{j},S_{Q}f_{j}\rangle
. We can now use the second moment formula for
S_{Q}f_{j}
and take a limit. However, to get our result we need an estimate on the secondary term
M_{f_{j},f_{j}}(s_{n})
. Using the fact that
f_{j}(\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k)=\rho_{j}(y)\phi_{j}(k)
factors it is not hard to see that for any
s\in(\frac{n}{2},n)
,

(2.39)

\displaystyle M_{f_{j},f_{j}}(s)=\left|\widehat{\rho}_{j}(s)\right|^{2}\sum_{d\geq 0}P_{d}(s)\|\phi_{j,d}\|_{2}^{2},

where for any
d\geq 0
,
\phi_{j,d}=\text{pr}_{d}(\phi_{j})
with
\text{pr}_{d}:L^{2}(M\backslash K)\to L^{2}(M\backslash K,d)
the projection operator from
L^{2}(M\backslash K)
to
L^{2}(M\backslash K,d)
.
It was shown in
[
Yu17
, p. 468-470]
that for any function
\phi\in L^{2}(M\backslash K)

\sum_{d\geq 0}P_{d}(s)\|\phi_{d}\|_{2}^{2}\ll_{s,n}\|\phi\|^{2(\frac{2s}{n}-1)}_{1}\|\phi\|^{4(1-\frac{s}{n})}_{2}.

Hence

M_{f_{j},f_{j}}(s)\ll_{s,n}\left|\widehat{\rho_{j}}(s)\right|^{2}\|\phi_{j}\|^{2(\frac{2s}{n}-1)}_{1}\|\phi_{j}\|^{4(1-\frac{s}{n})}_{2},

and we can take the limit and apply the dominated convergence theorem to the right side of the above inequality to get

\mathop{\overline{\lim}}_{j\to\infty}M_{f_{j},f_{j}}(s)\ll_{s,n}\left|\widehat{\rho}(s)\right|^{2}\|\phi\|^{2(\frac{2s}{n}-1)}_{1}\|\phi\|^{4(1-\frac{s}{n})}_{2}=\left|\widehat{\rho}(s)\right|^{2}\mu_{M\backslash K}(\phi)^{\frac{2s}{n}}.

Moreover, using the fact that
\rho
is an indicator function and applying Hölder’s inequality one can show,

\widehat{\rho}(s)\leq 2\widehat{\rho}(n)^{\frac{s}{n}}
; see
[
Yu18
, Lemma 4.3.1]
and recall that we assume here
n\geq 3
. We can thus bound

\mathop{\overline{\lim}}_{j\to\infty}M_{f_{j},f_{j}}(s)\ll_{s,n}\widehat{\rho}(n)^{\frac{2s}{n}}\mu_{M\backslash K}(\phi)^{\frac{2s}{n}}=m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)^{\frac{2s}{n}}.

Taking the limit in the second moment formula for
S_{Q}f_{j}
and applying the above bound we can conclude the proof for a precompact generalized sector. Finally for a general generalized sector
B
, we can similarly approximate
\chi_{B}
from below by a monotone sequence
\{\chi_{B_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of indicator functions of precompact generalized sectors
B_{j}
and use the monotone convergence theorem to deduce the second moment formula

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{Q}}\left|S_{Q}\chi_{B}(g)\right|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g)=\omega_{Q}^{2}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)^{2}+O_{Q}\left(m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)^{\beta_{Q}}+m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)\right),

and the desired discrepancy bound. ∎

Remark 2.40
.

For applications we will need to apply the discrepancy bound (
1.8
) for difference sets of two nested generalized sectors which may no longer be a generalized sector. Nevertheless, these difference sets can be expressed as a disjoint union of at most two generalized sectors.
To handle these sets, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
using the definition (
2.26
), the fact that the map
f\mapsto\widehat{f}_{d,l}
is linear in
f
for any
d\geq 0,1\leq l\leq\dim_{\mathbb{C}}L^{2}(S^{n},d)
and the estimate
\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}\right|^{2}\leq k\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|z_{i}\right|^{2}
(
z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
) we have for any
s\in(\frac{n}{2},n)
and for any
k
smooth compactly supported functions
f_{i}:\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}\to\mathbb{C}
, and
f:=\sum_{i}f_{i}

\displaystyle M_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f,f}}(s)\leq k\left(M_{f_{1},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{1}}}(s)+\cdots{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+}M_{f_{k},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{k}}}(s)\right).

In particular, if
B=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{k}B_{i}
is a disjoint union of
k
generalized sectors
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}
, then using the above estimate together with an approximating argument we get

\displaystyle\int_{Y_{Q}}\left|D(\mathcal{L}_{Q}g,B)\right|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{Q}(g)

\displaystyle\ll_{Q,k}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B_{i})^{\beta_{Q}}\leq m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)+m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}(B)^{\beta_{Q}}.

Conjugation

Let
\tau=ka\in\operatorname{GL}_{n+2}(\mathbb{R})
be as in

2.4
.
Later for our applications, we will need to work with explicit coordinates, hence, instead of working on

for which we can use the explicit coordinates introduced in section

Recall that

: For any finite-measure Borel subset

(which is further assumed to be a generalized sector if

(2.41)

3.
From mean square discrepancy to counting

In this section we show how one can use a mean square bound on the discrepancy in order to obtain effective counting estimates. These arguments are quite general and can be applicable in other settings so we present them in greater generality. For this we assume that
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is some algebraic variety with a transitive action of a real unimodular algebraic group
G
. Let
\Gamma\leq G
be a non-uniform lattice and
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
an infinite discrete set that is stable under the right action of
\Gamma
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}\gamma=\mathcal{L}
for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Let
\mu_{\Gamma}
be the probability
G
-invariant measure on
\Gamma\backslash G
. With slight abuse of notation, we also denote by
\mu_{\Gamma}
the Haar measure of
G
that locally agrees with
\mu_{\Gamma}
. We further assume that there is an appropriately normalized
G
-invariant measure
m_{\mathcal{V}}
on
\mathcal{V}
such that for any Borel set
B\subseteq\mathcal{V}
of finite measure

\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap B)\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(g)=m_{\mathcal{V}}(B).

For any
g\in G
and
B\subseteq\mathcal{V}
of finite measure, define the discrepancy
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}D(\mathcal{L}g,B)}=|\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap B)-m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)|
. Assuming a mean square bound for the discrepancy that holds for all sets is sometimes too strong, but in many cases we can have a bound on a large family of sets. To make this precise,
for
\beta\in[1,2)
and
c>0
let
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
be the family of all finite-measure Borel subsets
B\subseteq\mathcal{V}
satisfying that

(3.1)

\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}|D(\mathcal{L}g,B)|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(g)\leq c\left(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\beta}+m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\right).

In the rest of this section we show how one can use this assumption to get various estimates on the counting function
\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap B_{T})
for families of sets in the family
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
under various regularity assumptions.

Remark 3.2
.

Let
Q(\bm{v})=Q_{n}(\bm{v}\tau)
be as before and let
\beta_{Q}
be as in (
1.1
). As discussed in section
2.6
, for the case of
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
and
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{Q}\tau
, we can take
G=\operatorname{SO}_{Q_{n}}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
,
\Gamma=\tau^{-1}\operatorname{SO}_{Q}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})\tau
,
\mu_{\Gamma}=\tilde{\mu}_{Q}
and
m_{\mathcal{V}}=\omega_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q}^{+}}
. In this case, by (
2.41
),
there exists some
c>0
depending only on
Q
such that when
c_{Q}=0
(so that
\beta_{Q}=1
),
\mathcal{A}_{1,c}
contains all the finite-measure Borel subsets in
\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
, and when
c_{Q}>0
,
\mathcal{A}_{\beta_{Q},c}
contains any Borel set which is a disjoint union of at most two generalized sectors; see also
Remark
2.40
. Then clearly
\mathcal{A}_{\beta_{Q},c}
contains all the generalized sectors. In addition, if
B_{1},B_{2}
are two generalized sectors with
B_{1}\subseteq B_{2}
, it is then not difficult to see, using the polar coordinates on
\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
, that the difference set
B_{2}\setminus B_{1}
is a disjoint union of at most two generalized sectors, thus also belongs to
\mathcal{A}_{\beta_{Q},c}
.

3.1.
Counting results for almost all translates

Using a classical counting argument by Schmidt
[
Sch60b
]
one can use the mean square bound on the discrepancy to get very good estimates on
\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap B_{T})
that hold for almost all
\Gamma g\in\Gamma\backslash G
. Following his argument one can show the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any increasing family of sets
\{B_{T}\}_{T>0}
in
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
such that
T\mapsto m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{T})
is continuous and the differences
B_{T}\setminus B_{T^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
for any
T^{\prime}<T
we have for
\mu_{\Gamma}
-a.e.
\Gamma g\in\Gamma\backslash G

\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap B_{T})=m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{T})+O_{g,\epsilon}(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{T})^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\beta}{2}}+\epsilon}),\quad\text{as $T\to\infty$}.

The proof of this result is essentially identical to the proof of Schmidt’s result
[
Sch60b
]
, and we omit the details; see also
[
KY21
, Theorem 6.1]
.

Remark 3.3
.

We note that when
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
contains all finite-measure Borel sets in
\mathcal{V}
the condition on the continuity of the function
T\mapsto m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{T})
is not needed, as one can artificially add sets to the collection to make it so (and the second condition that differences are in
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
holds automatically). In particular in this case the result holds for any increasing family of sets in
\mathcal{V}
.

For some applications, one is interested in a family of sets that is not necessarily increasing. For example, for applications to Diophantine approximation, one is naturally interested in sets that increase in one direction and decrease in another. In order to handle such cases we give here the following general argument that is more flexible, but as a tradeoff we get a weaker bound for the remainder.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq G
be a subset endowed with some finite measure
\mu_{\Omega}
(which could be singular with respect to
\mu_{\Gamma}
).
Let
\mathcal{B}
be a family of Borel sets in
\mathcal{V}
satisfying that
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)>1
for any
B\in\mathcal{B}
and there exists
0<C<1
such that for any
B\in\mathcal{B}
and for any
0<X\leq Cm_{\mathcal{V}}(B)

(3.4)

\mu_{\Omega}\left(\left\{g\in\Omega:D(\mathcal{L}g,B)\geq X\right\}\right)\ll_{\Omega}\left(\tfrac{m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)}{X}\right)^{a}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{-b}

for some
a>1
and
0<b\leq 1
.

(1)

Then for any sequence
\{B_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of sets from
\mathcal{B}
with

(3.5)

\sum_{k}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})^{a\nu-b}\log\left(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})\right)^{-a}<\infty

for some
0<\nu<\frac{b}{a}
, we have for
\mu_{\Omega}
-a.e.
g\in\Omega
and for all sufficiently large
k

D(\mathcal{L}g,B_{k})\leq m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})^{1-\nu}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})).

(2)

Assume we can find two sequences
\{B_{k}^{\pm}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{B}
each satisfying (
3.5
)
such that any
B\in\mathcal{B}
with
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)
sufficiently large satisfies
B_{k}^{-}\subseteq B\subseteq B_{k}^{+}
for some
k
, and that
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{+}\setminus B_{k}^{-})\ll m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{-})^{1-\nu}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{-}))

for all sufficiently large
k
. Then for
\mu_{\Omega}
-a.e.
g\in\Omega
, for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
with
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)
sufficiently large

D(\mathcal{L}g,B)\ll m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{1-\nu}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)).

Proof.

For the first part let
\mathcal{M}_{k}=\{g\in\Omega:D(\mathcal{L}g,B_{k})>m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})^{1-\nu}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}))\}
. We then need to show that the set

\mathcal{M}_{\infty}=\bigcap_{m\geq 1}\bigcup_{k\geq m}\mathcal{M}_{k}

is a null set (with respect to
\mu_{\Omega}
).
Note that the summability assumption (
3.5
) implies
\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})=\infty
. Thus we can apply (
3.4
) to get for all
k
sufficiently large

\mu_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}_{k})\ll_{\Omega}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})^{a\nu-b}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}))^{-a}.

Then by the Borel-Cantelli lemma we get from this estimate and the summability assumption (
3.5
) that
\mu_{\Omega}(\mathcal{M}_{\infty})=0
.
The second part follows from the first together with the observation that

D(\mathcal{L}g,B)\leq\max\left\{D(\mathcal{L}g,B^{-}_{k}),D(\mathcal{L}g,B_{k}^{+})\right\}+m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{+}\setminus B_{k}^{-}),

whenever
B_{k}^{+}\subseteq B\subseteq B_{k}^{+}
.
∎

In particular for an increasing family we get the following.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be an increasing family of sets satisfying (
3.4
) for some
a>1
and
0<b\leq 1
and that
\{m_{\mathcal{V}}(B):B\in\mathcal{B}\}\supseteq[V,\infty)
for some
V>0
. Then for
\mu_{\Omega}
-a.e.
g\in\Omega

and for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
with
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)
sufficiently large

\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap B)=m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)+O\left(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{1-\frac{b}{a+1}}\log\left(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\right)\right).

Proof.

Let
\nu=\frac{b}{a+1}
and
\alpha=\frac{1}{b-a\nu}=\frac{1}{\nu}
. Let
\{B_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
be a sequence with
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})=\max\{V,k^{\alpha}\log(k)\}
.
Since our family is increasing then any
B\in\mathcal{B}
with
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)>V
satisfies
B_{k}\subseteq B\subseteq B_{k+1}
for some
k
and we can estimate for all
k
sufficiently large

\displaystyle m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k+1}\setminus B_{k})\ll k^{\alpha-1}\log(k)\ll k^{\alpha-1}\log(k)^{2-\nu}\asymp_{a,b}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})^{1-\nu}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})).

We can also estimate

\sum_{k}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})^{a\nu-b}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}))^{-a}\ll\sum_{k}k^{-1}\log(k)^{-a}<\infty,

and the result now follows from
Lemma
3.2
.
∎

In order to compare this to
Theorem
3.1
, note that a standard application of the Chebyshev inequality implies that any set
B\in\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
satisfies (
3.4
) with
a=2
and
b=2-\beta
.
Hence we can take
\Omega=\Gamma\backslash G
(or more precisely, a fundamental domain for
\Gamma\backslash G
) and
\mu_{\Omega}=\mu_{\Gamma}
, and note that
\frac{a+1-b}{a+1}=\frac{1+\beta}{3}
to get
D(\mathcal{L}g,B)\leq m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\frac{1+\beta}{3}}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B))
for
\mu_{\Gamma}
-a.e.
\Gamma g\in\Gamma\backslash G
. While this exponent is not as good as the one in
Theorem
3.1
, this argument also works for families that are not increasing as shown in the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a family of sets satisfying (
3.4
) for some
a>1
and
0<b\leq 1
. Let
\{B_{t,r}\}_{t>1,0<r<1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
be increasing in both parameters and assume we have the measure estimates
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{t,r})\asymp tr
and

(3.6)

m_{\mathcal{V}}\left(B_{t_{1},r_{1}}\setminus B_{t_{2},r_{2}}\right)\ll t_{1}(r_{1}-r_{2})+(t_{1}-t_{2})r_{1},\;\forall\ 1<t_{2}<t_{1},\ 0<r_{2}<r_{1}<1.

We then have the following.

(1)

For any
0<\lambda<1
let
r_{t}=t^{-\lambda}
. Then for
\mu_{\Omega}
-a.e.
g\in\Omega
and all sufficiently large
t

\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap B_{t,r_{t}})=m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{t,r_{t}})+O\left(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{t,r_{t}})^{1-\frac{b}{a+1}}\log\left(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{t,r_{t}})\right)\right).

(2)

For any
\lambda>\frac{1}{b}
, let
r_{t}=\tfrac{(\log t)^{\lambda}}{t}
. Then for
\mu_{\Omega}
-a.e.
g\in\Omega
and all sufficiently large
t

\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap B_{t,r_{t}})=m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{t,r_{t}})+O\left(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{t,r_{t}})^{1-\frac{\lambda b-1}{\lambda(a+1)}}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{t,r_{t}}))\right).

Proof.

For the first part, let
\nu=\frac{b}{a+1},\;\alpha=\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)\nu}
,
t_{k}=\max\{k^{\alpha}\log(k),2\}
and consider the sequences
\{B_{k}^{\pm}\}
with

B_{k}^{+}=B_{t_{k+1},r_{t_{k}}}
and
B_{k}^{-}=B_{t_{k},r_{t_{k+1}}}
. Then for any
t>t_{1}
we have
B_{t,r_{t}}
satisfies
B^{-}_{k}\subseteq B_{t,r_{t}}\subseteq B_{k}^{+}
(with
k
such that
t_{k}\leq t<t_{k+1}
) and we can estimate

m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{\pm})\gg t_{k}t_{k+1}^{-\lambda}\gg k^{\alpha(1-\lambda)}\log(k)^{1-\lambda}\gg k^{\alpha(1-\lambda)},

so that

\sum_{k}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{\pm})^{a\nu-b}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{\pm}))^{-a}\ll\sum_{k}k^{-1}\log(k)^{-a}<\infty.

Moreover we can apply (
3.6
) to get

m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{+}\setminus B_{k}^{-})\ll m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k})^{\frac{a+1-b}{a+1}}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}))
,
so the result follows from
Lemma
3.2
.

For the second part let
\nu=\frac{\lambda b-1}{\lambda(1+a)}
and
\alpha=\frac{1+a}{\lambda b+a}
. First note that since
\nu=\frac{\lambda b-1}{\lambda(1+a)}
and
\lambda>1/b
we have
\alpha=\frac{1+a}{\lambda b+a}\in(0,1)
. Now we again take
B_{k}^{+}=B_{t_{k+1},r_{t_{k}}}
and
B_{k}^{-}=B_{t_{k},r_{t_{k+1}}}
but with the sequence
t_{k}=e^{k^{\alpha}}
. Since
\{B_{t,r}\}
is increasing in both parameters and
r_{t}=\frac{(\log t)^{\lambda}}{t}
is eventually strictly decreasing in
t
, we have for all
t
sufficiently large,
B_{k}^{-}\subseteq B_{t,r_{t}}\subseteq B_{k}^{+}
with
k
such that
t_{k}\leq t<t_{k+1}
. With this choice we have

m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{-})\asymp e^{k^{\alpha}-(k+1)^{\alpha}}(k+1)^{\lambda\alpha}\asymp k^{\lambda\alpha}.

Here we used that
0<(x+1)^{\alpha}-x^{\alpha}<1
for any
x>0
and
0<\alpha<1
. Similarly, we also have
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{+})\asymp k^{\lambda\alpha}
. Thus

\sum_{k}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{\pm})^{a\nu-b}\log(m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{\pm}))^{-a}\ll\sum_{k}k^{-1}\log(k)^{-a}<\infty.

For the difference by (
3.6
) we have for all
k
sufficiently large

\displaystyle m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{+}\setminus B_{k}^{-})

\displaystyle\ll e^{(k+1)^{\alpha}}\left(\tfrac{k^{\lambda\alpha}}{e^{k^{\alpha}}}-\tfrac{(k+1)^{\lambda\alpha}}{e^{(k+1)^{\alpha}}}\right)+\left(e^{(k+1)^{\alpha}}-e^{k^{\alpha}}\right)\tfrac{k^{\lambda\alpha}}{e^{k^{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=2e^{(k+1)^{\alpha}-k^{\alpha}}k^{\lambda\alpha}-(k+1)^{\lambda\alpha}-k^{\lambda\alpha}

\displaystyle<2\left(1+O_{\alpha}(k^{\alpha-1})\right)k^{\lambda\alpha}-2k^{\lambda\alpha}

\displaystyle\ll k^{\alpha-1+\lambda\alpha}\ll m_{\mathcal{V}}(B_{k}^{-})^{1-\nu}.

Here for the last estimate we used the relation
\alpha-1+\lambda\alpha=\lambda\alpha(1-\nu)
which is equivalent to
\alpha(1+\lambda\nu)=1
. The latter can be checked using
\nu=\frac{\lambda b-1}{\lambda(1+a)}
and
\alpha=\frac{1+a}{\lambda b+a}
.
The result then follows from
Lemma
3.2
.
∎

3.2.
Counting result for all translates

In this subsection we give a different (and more direct) approach to get a counting result which holds for
all

\mathcal{L}
-translates.
Such results will then be used in the proof of
Theorem
1.7
.
In order to deduce such a result we need to impose some regularity conditions on our sets.
Let
\mathcal{O}=\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\}_{0<\epsilon\leq 1}\subseteq G
denote some fixed increasing family of compact identity neighborhoods. We will assume
\mathcal{O}
is invariant under inversion, that is,
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}^{-1}=\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
for all
0<\epsilon\leq 1
. We say that a family of sets
\mathcal{B}
is
strongly
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
-well rounded with respect to
\mathcal{O}
if there exist some
C_{0}\geq 0
and
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
such that for any
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
there are sets
\underline{B}_{\epsilon},\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
satisfying

(3.7)

\displaystyle\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\subseteq\bigcap_{h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}}Bh\subseteq\bigcup_{h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}}Bh\subseteq\overline{B}_{\epsilon},\quad m_{\mathcal{V}}(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon})\leq{C_{0}(\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)+1)}\quad\mbox{and }\quad\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{O}=\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\}_{0<\epsilon\leq 1}
be an increasing family of compact inversion-invariant identity neighborhoods of
G
satisfying

(3.8)

\mu_{\Gamma}(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon})\geq\delta\epsilon^{d}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon_{2}}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\eta(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})}

for some
\delta,d,\eta>0
. Let
\mathcal{B}
be a family that is strongly
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
-well rounded with respect to
\mathcal{O}
. Then for all
g\in G
and all
B\in\mathcal{B}
with
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\geq\max\{\delta^{-\frac{1}{2-\beta}},\delta^{\frac{1}{d+\beta}}\}

\displaystyle\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap B)=m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)+O_{d,\eta,g}\left(\delta^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{1-\frac{2-\beta}{d+2}}\right).

Remark 3.9
.

Note that the condition
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\geq\max\{\delta^{-\frac{1}{2-\beta}},\delta^{\frac{1}{d+\beta}}\}
implies that
\delta^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{1-\frac{2-\beta}{d+2}}\leq m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)
, and without this condition our bound on the remainder may be larger than the main term.

Proof of
Theorem
3.5
.

Fix
g\in G
. First note that since
\mathcal{O}_{1}
is compact,
g\mathcal{O}_{1}
(hence also
g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
for any
0<\epsilon<1
) is contained in some finite number (which may depend on
g
) of fundamental domains for
\Gamma\backslash G
. For any
B\in\mathcal{B}
let
\epsilon=\epsilon(B,\delta)
to be chosen later and
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\epsilon}{{\max\{1,2\eta\}}}
with
\eta
as in (
3.8
). We then bound

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}}|D(\mathcal{L}g,(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon})h)|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}}|D(\mathcal{L}gh,(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}))|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{g\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}}|D(\mathcal{L}h,(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}))|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(h)

\displaystyle\ll_{g}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}|D(\mathcal{L}h,(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}))|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(h)

\displaystyle\ll m_{\mathcal{V}}(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon})^{\beta}+m_{\mathcal{V}}(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon})\ll_{C_{0}}(\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B))^{\beta},

where in the first line we used that
D(\mathcal{L}g,Bh)=D(\mathcal{L}gh^{-1},B)
and made a change of variable
h\mapsto h^{-1}
(using also that both the Haar measure and
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
are inversion-invariant), in the second we made a change of variables
h\mapsto g^{-1}h
, and in the last estimate we applied the second condition in (
3.7
) and restricted our choice of
\epsilon
to satisfy
\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\geq 1
.

From this inequality and the lower bound on
\mu_{\Gamma}(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}})
in (
3.8
) we can deduce that there is some
h_{0}\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
with

|D(\mathcal{L}g,(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon})h_{0})|\ll_{\eta,g}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{\frac{\beta-d}{2}}
, and hence

(3.10)

\displaystyle\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon})h_{0})\ll_{\eta,g}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{\frac{\beta-d}{2}}+\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\ll\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B).

For the last inequality we restrict our choice of
\epsilon
to satisfy

(3.11)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\beta-d-{2}}\leq\delta m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{2-\beta}.

Next note that, since any
h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
can be written as
h=h_{1}h_{0}
with
h_{1}\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
(cf. (
3.8
)), we have

\underline{B}_{\epsilon}h_{0}\subseteq Bh\subseteq\overline{B}_{\epsilon}h_{0}
(cf. (
3.7
)). On the other hand, since
\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\epsilon
(so that
h_{0}^{-1}\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}
) we also have
\underline{B}_{\epsilon}h_{0}\subseteq B\subseteq\overline{B}_{\epsilon}h_{0}
. Noting also that
m_{\mathcal{V}}(Bh)=m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)
we can bound

\displaystyle\left|D(\mathcal{L}g,Bh)-D(\mathcal{L}g,B)\right|

\displaystyle\leq|(\#\mathcal{L}g\cap Bh)-(\#\mathcal{L}g\cap B)|

\displaystyle{\leq\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap Bh\Delta B)}\leq\#(\mathcal{L}g\cap(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon})h_{0}).

This, together with (
3.10
) implies that for any
h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
,

D(\mathcal{L}g,Bh)=D(\mathcal{L}g,B)+O_{d,\eta,g}\left(\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\right).

Hence there is some constant
C_{1}>0
(which may depend on
d,\eta
and
g
) such that

D(\mathcal{L}g,Bh)\geq D(\mathcal{L}g,B)-C_{1}\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)

for all
h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
. Now we take
\epsilon=m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{-\frac{2-\beta}{d+2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}
so that
\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)=m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{2}}=m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\frac{d+\beta}{d+2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}
and one easily checks that the conditions
\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\geq 1
and (
3.11
) are satisfied whenever
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\geq\max\{\delta^{-\frac{1}{2-\beta}},\delta^{\frac{1}{d+\beta}}\}
. If
D(\mathcal{L}g,B)-C_{1}\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\leq 0
, then we have

D(\mathcal{L}g,B)\leq C_{1}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\frac{d+\beta}{d+2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}.

Otherwise,
D(\mathcal{L}g,B)-C_{1}\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)>0
and thus we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}}|D(\mathcal{L}g,Bh)|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(h)\geq|D(\mathcal{L}g,B)-C_{1}\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)|^{2}\mu_{\Gamma}(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}})\gg_{\eta,d}\left|D(\mathcal{L}g,B)-C_{1}\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\right|^{2}\delta\epsilon^{d}.

Since we also have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}}|D(\mathcal{L}g,Bh)|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(h)\ll_{g}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}|D(\mathcal{L}h,B)|^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(h)\ll m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\beta},

we get that there is some
C_{2}>0
(which only depends on
d,\eta
and
g
) with

\left|D(\mathcal{L}g,B)-C_{1}\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\right|^{2}\leq C_{2}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\beta}\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-d}.

Since
D(\mathcal{L}g,B)-C_{1}\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)>0
, this implies

D(\mathcal{L}g,B)\leq C_{1}\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)+\sqrt{C_{2}}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{2}}=(C_{1}+\sqrt{C_{2}})m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\frac{d+\beta}{d+2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}.

To conclude, in both cases
D(\mathcal{L}g,B)\ll_{d,\eta,g}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\frac{d+\beta}{d+2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}
whenever
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\geq\max\{\delta^{-\frac{1}{2-\beta}},\delta^{\frac{1}{d+\beta}}\}
. ∎

3.3.
Subgroup translates

In this subsection, we apply the general counting results obtained in section
3.1
to the special case concerning counting for generic subgroup translates.
Let
H
be a closed subgroup of
G
and fix a continuous section,
\iota_{H}:H\backslash G\to G
, of the natural projection from
G
to
H\backslash G
.
We can then write any
g\in G
of the form
g=h\iota(x)
for a unique
h\in H
and
x\in H\backslash G
. For some applications one is interested in counting results in translates
\mathcal{L}h\iota_{H}(x)
that hold for
all

h\in H
and almost all
x\in H\backslash G
. To be more precise we need to specify what measure we are using on the quotient
H\backslash G
. When
H
is unimodular the natural choice is the unique (up to scaling)
G
-invariant measure on the quotient
H\backslash G
.
When
H
is not unimodular, there is no such measure and instead we will use a
quasi-invariant measure
on
H\backslash G
as introduced in
[
RS00
]
. Explicitly, let
\Delta_{H}
be the modular function of
H
and it is shown in

[
RS00
, Proposition 8.1.3 and Theorem 8.1.13]
that there exists a strictly positive continuous function
\lambda_{H}:G\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
satisfying the functional equation

(3.12)

\displaystyle\lambda_{H}(hg)=\Delta_{H}(h)\lambda_{H}(g),\quad\forall\ g\in G,\ h\in H,

and a measure
\mu_{H\backslash G}
on
H\backslash G
satisfying

(3.13)

\displaystyle\int_{G}f(g)\lambda_{H}(g)\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(g)=\int_{H\backslash G}\left(\int_{H}f(hg)\,\text{d}\mu_{H}(h)\right)\,\text{d}\mu_{H\backslash G}(Hg),\quad\forall\ f\in C_{c}(G),

where
\mu_{\Gamma}
is the Haar measure of
G
which locally agrees with the probability
G
-invariant measure on
\Gamma\backslash G
and
\mu_{H}
is a right Haar measure of
H
.

Remark 3.14
.

When
H\leq G
is unimodular, then
\lambda_{H}
can be taken to be a constant function and
\mu_{H\backslash G}
is a
G
-invariant measure mentioned above.
For our later applications, we will consider the case when
G=\operatorname{SO}_{Q_{n}}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and
H=P
the parabolic subgroup fixing the line spanned by
\bm{e}_{0}=(-1,\bm{0},1)\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
. Note that in this case
P\backslash G\cong S^{n}
and we can take
\iota_{P}=\mathfrak{s}
(so that
\iota_{P}(\bm{\alpha})=k_{\bm{\alpha}}
for all
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
). Moreover, it is not difficult to see that under the coordinates
g=pk_{\bm{\alpha}}\in G
with
p=u_{\bm{x}}a_{y}m\in UAM=P
and
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
, the modular function is given by
\Delta_{P}(p)=y^{n}
and we can take
\text{d}\mu_{P}(p)=y^{-1}\,\text{d}\bm{x}\text{d}y\text{d}\mu_{M}(m)
,
\lambda_{H}(g)={\omega_{\Gamma}}y^{n}
and
\mu_{P\backslash G}=\sigma_{n}
(cf. (
2.15
)).

Let
\mathcal{O}=\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\}_{0<\epsilon<1}\subseteq H
be an increasing family of inversion-invariant identity neighborhoods of
H
. We say a family of finite-measure Borel subsets
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
is
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
-well rounded
with respect to
\mathcal{O}
if there exist
T_{0}>1
,
C_{0}>0
and
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
such that for any
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
with
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\geq T_{0}
, there exist sets
\overline{B}_{\epsilon},\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
satisfying that

(3.15)

\displaystyle\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\subseteq\bigcap_{h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}}Bh\subseteq\bigcup_{h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}}Bh\subseteq\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\quad\text{and}\quad m_{\mathcal{V}}(\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon})\leq{C_{0}(\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)+1)}.

Remark 3.16
.

The main difference between the well roundedness property defined here and the strong well roundedness property defined in (
3.7
) is that here we do not require the differences
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}
to also belong to
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
.

We will apply
Lemma
3.2
to get counting results for subgroup translates. The key ingredient to our arguments is the following proposition which verifies (
3.4
) for well bounded families.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
H\leq G
be a closed subgroup and let
\mathcal{O}=\{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\}_{0<\epsilon\leq 1}
be an increasing family of compact inversion-invariant identity neighborhoods of
H
satisfying that

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mu_{H}(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon})\gg\epsilon^{d},\quad\forall\ 0<\epsilon\leq 1

for some
d>0
.
Fix
h_{0}\in H
and
\Omega
a compact subset of
H\backslash G
. Let
\mathcal{B}
be a family of Borel subsets of
\mathcal{V}
which is
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
-well rounded with respect to
\mathcal{O}
(with parameters
T_{0},C_{0},\epsilon_{0}
). Then for any
B\in\mathcal{B}
with
m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)>\max\left\{\frac{2}{\epsilon_{0}},T_{0}\right\}
and for any
4C_{0}<X<2C_{0}\epsilon_{0}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)
we have

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mu_{H\backslash G}\left(\left\{x\in\Omega:D(\mathcal{L}\iota_{H}(x)^{-1}h_{0}^{-1},B)>X\right\}\right)\ll_{h_{0},\Omega}\frac{m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\beta+d}}{X^{2+d}},

where the implied constant may also depend on
\mathcal{O}
and the parameters
c,C_{0}
and
d
.

Proof.

Let
B\in\mathcal{B}
be as in this proposition and for any
4C_{0}<X<2C_{0}\epsilon_{0}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)
let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{B,X}=\mathcal{M}_{B,X}^{h_{0},\Omega}:=\left\{x\in\Omega:D(\mathcal{L}\iota_{H}(x)^{-1}h_{0}^{-1},B)>X\right\}.

Let us also denote by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{B,X}^{\pm}:=\left\{x\in\Omega:\pm\left(\#(\mathcal{L}\iota_{H}(x)^{-1}h_{0}^{-1}\cap B)-m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)\right)>X\right\}

so
\mathcal{M}_{B,X}=\mathcal{M}_{B,X}^{+}\bigcup\mathcal{M}_{B,X}^{-}
. It thus suffices to show
\mu_{H\backslash G}(\mathcal{M}_{B,X}^{\pm})\ll_{h_{0},\Omega}\frac{m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\beta+d}}{X^{2+d}}
.

Now take
\epsilon=\frac{X-2C_{0}}{2C_{0}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)}
(so
X-{C_{0}(\epsilon m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)+1)}=\frac{X}{2}
). Note that since
4C_{0}<X<2C_{0}\epsilon_{0}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)
we have
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
. Observe that if
x\in\mathcal{M}_{B,X}^{+}
, i.e.
\#(\mathcal{L}\iota_{H}(x)^{-1}h_{0}^{-1}\cap B)-m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)>X
, then, by the assumption (
3.15
) for any
h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\#(\mathcal{L}\iota_{H}(x)^{-1}h_{0}^{-1}\cap\overline{B}_{\epsilon}h)

\displaystyle\geq\#(\mathcal{L}\iota_{H}(x)^{-1}h_{0}^{-1}\cap B)>m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)+X

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{X^{2}}{4}\mu_{H\backslash G}(\mathcal{M}_{B,X}^{+})\mu_{H}(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon})<\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}}D(\mathcal{L}\iota_{H}(x)^{-1}h_{0}^{-1}h^{-1},\overline{B}_{\epsilon})^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{H}(h)\,\text{d}\mu_{H\backslash G}(x)=:I.

On the other hand, using (

we get

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}}D(\mathcal{L}\iota_{H}(x)^{-1}h^{-1},\overline{B}_{\epsilon})^{2}\lambda_{H}(h\iota_{H}(x))\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(h\iota_{H}(x))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}}D(\mathcal{L}g^{-1},\overline{B}_{\epsilon})^{2}\lambda_{H}(g)\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(g)=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}^{-1}}D(\mathcal{L}g,\overline{B}_{\epsilon})^{2}\lambda_{H}(g^{-1})\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(g).

Here

\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon,h_{0},\Omega}:=\left\{h\iota_{H}(x):h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}h_{0},\ x\in\Omega\right\}\subseteq G
.

are compact,

Thus

\displaystyle I\ll_{h_{0},\mathcal{O}_{1},\Omega}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon}^{-1}}D(\mathcal{L}g,\overline{B}_{\epsilon})^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(g)\ll_{\mathcal{O}_{1}h_{0},\Omega}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}D(\mathcal{L}g,\overline{B}_{\epsilon})^{2}\,\text{d}\mu_{\Gamma}(g)\ll_{c,C_{0}}m_{\mathcal{V}}(\overline{B}_{\epsilon})^{\beta}\ll m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\beta}.

Here for the last estimate we used that

\displaystyle\mu_{H\backslash G}(\mathcal{M}_{B,X}^{+})\ll_{c,C_{0},\mathcal{O}_{1},h_{0},\Omega}\tfrac{m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\beta}}{X^{2}}\mu_{H}(\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon})^{-1}\ll\tfrac{m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\beta}}{X^{2}}\left(\tfrac{X-2C_{0}}{2C_{0}m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)}\right)^{-d}\asymp_{C_{0},d}\tfrac{m_{\mathcal{V}}(B)^{\beta+d}}{X^{2+d}}.

Similarly we also have

∎

3.4.
Distribution of values of generic functions

For any fixed function

and

Theorem 3.9
.

4.2.
Volume calculation for linear maps

We first consider the case of a linear map and prove the following volume estimate that is needed in order to apply
Theorem
3.9
. In the following all balls are assumed to be centered at the origin and all implied constants may depend on the fixed parameters
n,m
and
d
. Let
L:\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a linear map of rank
m<n
with
Q_{n,L}:=Q_{n}|_{\ker(L)}
indefinite. We need to introduce a relevant measure on
\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n,L}}^{+}:=\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}\cap\ker(L)
. When
L=L_{0}
,
\ker(L_{0})
naturally identifies with
\mathbb{R}^{n+2-m}
and with this identification
Q_{n,L_{0}}=Q_{n-m}
and
\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}^{+}=\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n-m}}^{+}
. We denote by
m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}^{+}}
the
\operatorname{SO}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
-invariant measure on
\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}^{+}
given as in (
2.7
) with
n-m
in place of
n
. More generally, by
Lemma
4.1
, there exist
g_{L}\in G
and
h_{L}\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
such that
L=g_{L}L_{0}h_{L}
. Then
\ker(L)=\ker(L_{0})g_{L}^{-1}
and
\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n,L}}^{+}=\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}^{+}g_{L}^{-1}
. We define
m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n,L}}^{+}}
to be the pushforward of
m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}^{+}}
under the right
g_{L}^{-1}
-multiplication map.

Proposition 4.5
.

For any linear map
L=g_{L}L_{0}h_{L}:\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
of rank
m<n
with
Q_{n,L}
indefinite, for any measurable
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
contained in a ball of radius
c_{\Omega}
and for any
T>2c_{\Omega}\|h_{L}\|_{\rm op}
we have

\displaystyle m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}\left(\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}:\|\bm{v}\|\leq T,\;\bm{v}L\in\Omega\right\}\right)

\displaystyle={\frac{c_{n,m}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)T^{n-m}}{|\det(h_{L})|}}\left(V_{L}+O\left(c_{\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|h_{L}\|_{\rm op}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|g_{L}\|_{\rm op}^{n-m+\frac{1}{2}}T^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right).

Here
c_{n,m}:=\frac{(n-m+1)\Gamma(\frac{n+3}{2})}{(n+1)\pi^{\frac{m}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{n-m+3}{2})}
with
\Gamma(s)
the Gamma function and

V_{L}:=m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L}}}\left(\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L}}:\|\bm{v}\|\leq 1\}\right)
.

Remark 4.4
.

The assumption that
Q_{n,L}
is indefinite is necessary here. For example, for
\bm{v}L=(v_{n-m+3},\ldots,v_{n+2})
for which
Q_{n,L}
is definite, one can see

\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}:\|\bm{v}\|\leq T,\;\bm{v}L\in\Omega\right\}\subseteq\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}:\|\bm{v}\|\leq\sqrt{2}c_{\Omega}\right\}

is uniformly bounded and independent on
T
.

We first prove a smoothed version of this volume estimate.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
f\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})
supported in a ball of radius
c_{f}\geq 1
and for any
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
contained in the unit ball we have
for all
T\geq 2

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}f\left(\tfrac{\bm{v}}{T}\right)\chi_{\Omega}(\bm{v}L_{0})\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=c_{n,m}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)\left(T^{n-m}\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}f\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}+O\left(\mathcal{S}_{1}(f)c_{f}^{n-m}T^{n-m-1}\right)\right),

where
c_{n,m}
is as in
Proposition
4.5
and

\mathcal{S}_{1}(f):=\max\left\{\|f\|_{\infty},\ \|\frac{\partial f}{\partial v_{j}}\|_{\infty},\ 1\leq j\leq n+2\right\}
.

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{I}:=\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}f\left(\tfrac{\bm{v}}{T}\right)\chi_{\Omega}(\bm{v}L_{0})\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}(\bm{v})
.
We can write any
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}
as
\bm{v}=r(\bm{\alpha},1)
with
r>0
and
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
and in these coordinates, by (
2.7
) we have
\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}(\bm{v})=r^{n-1}\,\text{d}r\text{d}\sigma_{n}(\bm{\alpha})
, where
\sigma_{n}
is the rotation invariant probability measure on
S^{n}
as before. We thus get

\mathcal{I}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{S^{n}}f\left(r\tfrac{(\bm{\alpha},1)}{T}\right)\chi_{\Omega}(r(\bm{\alpha},1)L_{0})r^{n-1}\,\text{d}\sigma_{n}(\bm{\alpha})\text{d}r.

Writing Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
in polar coordinates
\text{d}\bm{x}={(n+1)\nu_{n+1}}t^{n}\,\text{d}t\text{d}\sigma_{n}(\bm{\alpha})
with
\nu_{n+1}=\frac{\pi^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{n+3}{2})}
the volume of the unit ball
B^{n+1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
we can replace the integral over
S^{n}
by an integral over
B^{n+1}
, and make a change of variables
r\mapsto rT
to get

\mathcal{I}={\frac{T^{n}}{\nu_{n+1}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{B^{n+1}}f\left(r(\tfrac{\bm{x}}{\|\bm{x}\|},1)\right)\chi_{\Omega}\left(rT(\tfrac{\bm{x}}{\|\bm{x}\|},1)L_{0}\right)r^{n-1}\,\text{d}\bm{x}\text{d}r.

Now write
\bm{x}=(\bm{y},\bm{z})
with
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1-m}
and make a change of variables
\bm{y}\mapsto\|\bm{z}\|\bm{y}
to get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{T^{n}}{\nu_{n+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\|\bm{y}\|^{2}+\|\bm{z}\|^{2}\leq 1}f\left(r(\tfrac{\bm{y}}{\sqrt{\|\bm{y}\|^{2}+\|\bm{z}\|^{2}}},\tfrac{\bm{z}}{\sqrt{\|\bm{y}\|^{2}+\|\bm{z}\|^{2}}},1)\right)\chi_{\Omega}\left(\tfrac{rT\bm{y}}{\sqrt{\|\bm{y}\|^{2}+\|\bm{z}\|^{2}}}\right)r^{n-1}\,\text{d}\bm{y}\text{d}\bm{z}dr

\displaystyle=\frac{T^{n}}{\nu_{n+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\|\bm{z}\|^{2}(\|\bm{y}\|^{2}+1)\leq 1}f\left(r(\tfrac{\bm{y}}{\sqrt{\|\bm{y}\|^{2}+1}},\tfrac{\bm{z}}{\|\bm{z}\|\sqrt{\|\bm{y}\|^{2}+1}},1)\right)\chi_{\Omega}\left(\tfrac{rT\bm{y}}{\sqrt{\|\bm{y}\|^{2}+1}}\right)r^{n-1}\|\bm{z}\|^{m}\,\text{d}\bm{y}\text{d}\bm{z}\text{d}r.

Now make another change of variables
\bm{u}=\frac{\bm{y}}{\sqrt{1+\|\bm{y}\|^{2}}}
so that
\bm{y}=\frac{\bm{u}}{\sqrt{1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}}}
and note that the Jacobian of this transformation is given by
J(\bm{u})=(1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2})^{-\frac{2+m}{2}}
.
With this change of variables the region
\|\bm{z}\|^{2}(\|\bm{y}\|^{2}+1)\leq 1
transforms into
\|\bm{z}\|^{2}+\|\bm{u}\|^{2}\leq 1
so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{T^{n}}{\nu_{n+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\|\bm{u}\|<1}\int_{\|\bm{z}\|^{2}\leq 1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}}f\left(r(\bm{u},\tfrac{\bm{z}\sqrt{1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}}}{\|\bm{z}\|},1)\right)\chi_{\Omega}(rT\bm{u})r^{n-1}\|\bm{z}\|^{m}J(\bm{u})\,\text{d}\bm{z}\text{d}\bm{u}\text{d}r.

Writing
\bm{z}=\zeta\bm{\omega}
with
\zeta=\|\bm{z}\|
and
\bm{\omega}\in S^{n-m}
in polar coordinates and noting that
c_{n,m}=\frac{(n-m+1)\nu_{n-m+1}}{(n+1)\nu_{n+1}}

we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle={c_{n,m}}T^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\|\bm{u}\|<1}\int_{S^{n-m}}(1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2})^{\frac{n-m-1}{2}}f\left(r(\bm{u},\bm{\omega}\sqrt{1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}},1)\right)\chi_{\Omega}(rT\bm{u})r^{n-1}\,\text{d}\sigma_{n-m}(\bm{\omega})\text{d}\bm{u}\text{d}r.

Let
\mathcal{I}^{-}
(respectively
\mathcal{I}^{+}
) be the above triple integral except with the integration range of
r
given by
0\leq r\leq\frac{1}{T}
(respectively
r>\frac{1}{T}
) so that
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}^{-}+\mathcal{I}^{+}
. We first estimate
\mathcal{I}^{-}
trivially by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{-}

\displaystyle\ll\|f\|_{\infty}T^{n}\int_{0}^{T^{-1}}(rT)^{-m}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)r^{n-1}\text{d}r\ll\|f\|_{\infty}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega).

Next to estimate
\mathcal{I}^{+}
, i.e. for
r
in the range
r>\tfrac{1}{T}
, we may assume
\|\bm{u}\|\leq\frac{1}{rT}<1
(since
rT\bm{u}\in\Omega
) and bound

\left\|r(\bm{u},\bm{\omega}\sqrt{1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}},1)-r(\bm{0},\bm{\omega},1)\right\|^{2}\leq 2r^{2}\|\bm{u}\|^{2}\leq 2T^{-2},

so that

f\left(r(\bm{u},\bm{\omega}\sqrt{1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}},1)\right)=f\left(r(\bm{0},\bm{\omega},1)\right)+O\left(\tfrac{\mathcal{S}_{1}(f)}{T}\right).

Using this approximation we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{+}

\displaystyle=c_{n,m}T^{n}\int_{1/T}^{\infty}\int_{(rT)^{-1}\Omega}\int_{S^{n-m}}(1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2})^{\frac{n-m-1}{2}}f\left(r(\bm{0},\bm{\omega},1)\right)r^{n-1}\,\text{d}\sigma_{n-m}(\bm{\omega})\text{d}\bm{u}\text{d}r

\displaystyle+O\left(\mathcal{S}_{1}(f)\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)c_{f}^{n-m}T^{n-m-1}\right).

Next approximating
(1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2})^{\frac{n-m-1}{2}}=1+O((\frac{1}{rT})^{2})
uniformly for
\|\bm{u}\|\leq\frac{1}{rT}
, we can replace
(1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2})^{\frac{n-m-1}{2}}
by
1
after adding an error of

O\left(\|f\|_{\infty}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)T^{n-m-2}\int_{1/T}^{c_{f}}r^{n-m-3}\,\text{d}r\right)
,
which can be absorbed into the previous error term, to get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{+}

\displaystyle=c_{n,m}T^{n-m}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)\int_{1/T}^{\infty}\int_{S^{n-m}}f\left(r(\bm{0},\bm{\omega},1)\right)r^{n-m-1}\,\text{d}\sigma_{n-m}(\bm{\omega})\text{d}r

\displaystyle+O\left(\mathcal{S}_{1}(f)\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)c_{f}^{n-m}T^{n-m-1}\right).

We now add back the contribution of
0\leq r\leq\frac{1}{T}
and note that it is bounded by
O(\|f\|_{\infty}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega))

to get

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=c_{n,m}T^{n-m}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{S^{n-m}}f\left(r(\bm{0},\bm{\omega},1)\right)r^{n-m-1}\,\text{d}\sigma_{n-m}(\bm{\omega})\text{d}r

\displaystyle+O\left(\mathcal{S}_{1}(f)\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)c_{f}^{n-m}T^{n-m-1}\right).

Finally, we conclude the proof by noting that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{S^{n-m}}f\left(r(\bm{0},\bm{\omega},1)\right)r^{n-m-1}\,\text{d}\sigma_{n-m}(\bm{\omega})\text{d}r=\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}f\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}.\qed

Proof of
Proposition
4.5
.

Abbreviate
g=g_{L}\in G
and
h=h_{L}\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
.
Note that

\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}:\|\bm{v}\|\leq T,\;\bm{v}L\in\Omega\right\}=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}:\|\bm{v}g^{-1}\|\leq T,\;\bm{v}L_{0}\in\Omega h^{-1}\right\}g^{-1}.

Let
\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega h^{-1}
, and since

m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}
is
G
-invariant, it is enough to estimate

R:=m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}\left(\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}:\|\bm{v}g^{-1}\|\leq T,\;\bm{v}L_{0}\in\Omega^{\prime}\right\}\right).

By a simple rescaling argument and the estimate
c_{\Omega^{\prime}}\leq c_{\Omega}\|h\|_{\rm op}
, it is enough to consider the case when
\Omega^{\prime}
is contained in the unit ball and
T\geq 2
.
For
0<\delta<1
let
\psi^{\pm}_{\delta}
approximate the indicator function of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
in the sense that

\psi_{\delta}^{+}(\bm{v})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\|\bm{v}\|\leq 1,\\
0&\|\bm{v}\|\geq 1+\delta,\end{array}\right.

and similarly

\psi_{\delta}^{-}(\bm{v})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\|\bm{v}\|\leq 1-\delta,\\
0&\|\bm{v}\|\geq 1,\end{array}\right.

with
\mathcal{S}_{1}(\psi_{\delta}^{\pm})\asymp\delta^{-1}
. For any
g\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}G}
we define

\psi_{\delta,g}^{\pm}(\bm{v})=\psi_{\delta}^{\pm}(\bm{v}g^{-1})
and note that

\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}\psi^{-}_{\delta,g}\left(\tfrac{\bm{v}}{T}\right)\chi_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\bm{v}L_{0})\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}(\bm{v})\leq R\leq\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}\psi^{+}_{\delta,g}\left(\tfrac{\bm{v}}{T}\right)\chi_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\bm{v}L_{0})\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}(\bm{v}).

Applying
Lemma
4.6
with
f=\psi^{\pm}_{\delta,g}
, noting that
\mathcal{S}_{1}(f)\ll\|g\|_{\rm op}\delta^{-1}
and
c_{f}\ll\|g\|_{\rm op}
we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}\psi^{\pm}_{\delta,g}\left(\tfrac{\bm{v}}{T}\right)\chi_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\bm{v}L)\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}(\bm{v})

\displaystyle={c_{n,m}}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega^{\prime})T^{n-m}\left(\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}\psi^{\pm}_{\delta,g}\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}+O\left(\|g\|_{\rm op}^{n-m+1}\delta^{-1}T^{-1}\right)\right).

Further estimate (see
[
KY20
, p. 8686-8687]
)

\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}\psi^{\pm}_{\delta,g}\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}=m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}\left(\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}:\|\bm{v}g^{-1}\|\leq 1\right\}\right)+O\left(\|g\|_{\rm op}^{n-m}\delta\right),

to get

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=c_{n,m}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega^{\prime})T^{n-m}\left(m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}\left(\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}:\|\bm{v}g^{-1}\|\leq 1\}\right)+O\left(\|g\|_{\rm op}^{n-m}\left(\delta+\|g\|_{\rm op}\delta^{-1}T^{-1}\right)\right)\right).

Now setting
\delta=T^{-1/2}
we get

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=c_{n,m}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega^{\prime})T^{n-m}\left(m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}\left(\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}:\|\bm{v}g^{-1}\|\leq 1\right\}\right)+O\left(\|g\|_{\rm op}^{n-m+1}T^{-1/2}\right)\right).

Finally, recall that
\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}g^{-1}=\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L}}
and
m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L}}}
is defined as the pushforward of
m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n,L_{0}}}}
by
g^{-1}
. Using also the relation

\operatorname{vol}(\Omega^{\prime})=\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)}{|\det(h)|}
we get our result.
∎

4.3.
Volume calculation for homogenous functions

We now state the volume estimate needed for
Theorem
4.4
. Before doing so, we first introduce some more notation.

Fix
1<d<m
and given
F\in\mathscr{F}_{d,Q_{n}}
with
F(\bm{v})=F_{p,q}^{(d)}(\bm{v}gL_{0}h)
for some
p\geq q\geq 1
with
p+q=m
,
g\in\operatorname{SO}_{Q_{n}}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and
h\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
, we denote by

\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n},F}=\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}:F(\bm{v})=0\}.

Note that
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n},F}
is of the form

\bm{v}g=r(\bm{u},\bm{\omega}\sqrt{1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}},1)
for some
r>0
,
\bm{\omega}\in S^{n-m}
and
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\|\bm{u}\|\leq 1
and
F^{(d)}_{p,q}(\bm{u}h)=0
. Further decomposing
\bm{u}h=(\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2})
with
\bm{u}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\bm{u}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
then the condition
F^{(d)}_{p,q}(\bm{u}h)=0
implies that
\|\bm{u}_{1}\|_{d}=\|\bm{u}_{2}\|_{d}
. Letting
S_{d}^{p,q}=S_{d}^{p-1}\times S_{d}^{q-1}
denote the product of two unit spheres with respect to the
L^{d}
-norm, we can write
\bm{u}h=\tfrac{\|\bm{u}h\|}{\|\tilde{\bm{\omega}}\|}\tilde{\bm{\omega}}
with
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}\in S_{d}^{p,q}
. Let
t=\|\bm{u}\|\in[0,1]
so that
\bm{u}=t\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\omega}}h^{-1}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\omega}}h^{-1}\|}
. Thus we can write
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n},F}
of the form

\bm{v}=r\left(t\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\omega}}h^{-1}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\omega}}h^{-1}\|},\bm{\omega}\sqrt{1-t^{2}},1\right)g^{-1}

for some
r>0
,
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}\in S_{d}^{p,q}
,
\bm{w}\in S^{n-m}
and
0\leq t\leq 1
. Under these coordinates we define the measure
m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n},F}}
on
\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n},F}
by

(4.5)

\displaystyle\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n},F}}(\bm{v}):=\|\tilde{\bm{\omega}}h^{-1}\|^{d-m}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{n-m-1}{2}}t^{m-d-1}r^{n-d-1}\,\text{d}t\text{d}r\text{d}\sigma_{n-m}(\bm{\omega})\text{d}\sigma_{d}^{p,q}(\tilde{\bm{\omega}}),

where
\sigma_{d}^{p,q}:=\sigma_{d}^{p}\times\sigma_{d}^{q}
is the surface area on
S_{d}^{p,q}
such that
\text{d}\bm{x}=t_{1}^{p-1}t_{2}^{q-1}\text{d}\sigma_{d}^{p,q}(\tilde{\bm{\omega}})
under the polar coordinates
\bm{x}=(t_{1}\bm{\omega}_{1},t_{2}\bm{\omega}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
with
t_{1},t_{2}>0
and
\tilde{\bm{\omega}}=(\bm{\omega}_{1},\bm{\omega}_{2})\in S_{d}^{p,q}
.

We now state our volume estimate for these homogeneous functions.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
F\in\mathscr{F}_{d,Q_{n}}
be such that
F(\bm{v})=F_{p,q}^{(d)}(\bm{v}gL_{0}h)
for some
p\geq q\geq 1
with
p+q=m
,
g\in G
and
h\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
and let
N\geq 1
. For any measurable
I\subseteq[-N,N]
and for any
T>2N^{1/d}
,

m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}\left(\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}:\|\bm{v}\|\leq T,\ F(\bm{v})\in I\right\}\right)=\frac{{c_{n,m}}|I|T^{n-d}}{|\det(h)|d}\left(V_{F}+O_{h,g}\left(T^{-\nu}\right)\right),

where
c_{n,m}
is as in
Proposition
4.5
,
V_{F}:=m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n},F}}\left(\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n},F}:\|\bm{v}\|\leq 1\}\right)
,
\nu=\min\{{\frac{d}{4}},\frac{m-d}{2}\}
and the implied constant can be bounded by some powers of
\max\{\|h\|_{\rm op},\|h^{-1}\|_{\rm op}\}\|g\|_{\rm op}
.

As before, we first prove a smoothed version.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
F\in\mathscr{F}_{d,Q_{n}}
with
F(\bm{v})=F_{p,q}^{(d)}(\bm{v}L_{0}h)
for some pair
(p,q)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
with
p+q=m
and
h\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
f\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})
be supported in a ball of radius
c_{f}\geq 1
. For any measurable
I\subseteq[-1,1]
and for any
T\geq 2
,

\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}f\left(\tfrac{\bm{v}}{T}\right)\chi_{I}\left(F(\bm{v})\right)\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}(\bm{v})=\frac{c_{n,m}|I|T^{n-d}}{|\det(h)|d}\left(\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n},F}}f\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n},F}}+O_{h}\left(\mathcal{S}_{1}(f)c_{f}^{n+1}\left(T^{d-m}+T^{-\frac{d}{2}}\log(T)\right)\right)\right),

where the implied constant can be bounded by some powers of
\max\{\|h\|_{\rm op},\|h^{-1}\|_{\rm op}\}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{I}:=\int_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}f\left(\tfrac{\bm{v}}{T}\right)\chi_{I}\left(F_{p,q}^{(d)}(\bm{v}L_{0}h)\right)\,\text{d}m_{\mathcal{V}^{+}_{Q_{n}}}(\bm{v}).

Using the same change of variables as in the proof of
Lemma
4.6
we get that
\mathcal{I}
equals

\displaystyle{c_{n,m}}T^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\|\bm{u}\|<1}\int_{S^{n-m}}f(r(\bm{u},\bm{\omega}\sqrt{1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2}},1))\chi_{I}(r^{d}T^{d}F_{p,q}^{(d)}(\bm{u}h))(1-\|\bm{u}\|^{2})^{\frac{n-m-1}{2}}r^{n-1}\,\text{d}\sigma_{n-m}(\bm{\omega})\text{d}\bm{u}\text{d}r.

After further making a change of variables
\bm{u}\mapsto\bm{u}h^{-1}
we write the new variable
\bm{u}=\bm{u}(t_{1},t_{2},\tilde{\bm{\omega}})=(t_{1}\bm{\omega}_{1},t_{2}\bm{\omega}_{2})
with
t_{1},t_{2}>0
and

\tilde{\bm{\omega}}=(\bm{\omega}_{1},\bm{\omega}_{2})\in S_{d}^{p,q}
in polar coordinates with respect to the
L^{d}
-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathbb{R}^{q}
respectively. In these coordinates

|B|
. Let
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
be the ellipsoid defined in (
1.14
) and recall the norm
\|\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}:=\sqrt{\mathcal{Q}(\bm{x})}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. For any
\bm{x}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
and
r>0
let
\mathfrak{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}_{r}(\bm{x})
be the open
r
-ball on
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
be defined in (
1.15
). When
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{x})=\|\bm{x}\|^{2}
(so that

) we abbreviate

by
\mathfrak{D}_{r}(\bm{x})
.

5.1.
Reduction to counting points on the light cone

In this section we reduce counting rational solutions to Diophantine inequalities to counting
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}
-points on the light cone

\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
.
We first introduce the sets that are relevant for our reduction. Recall from
Remark
2.8
that for any
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
we have fixed a rotation
k_{\bm{\alpha}}\in K
with
K
as in (
2.3
)
satisfying
\bm{e}_{0}k_{\bm{\alpha}}=\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}
with
\bm{e}_{0}=(-1,\bm{0},1)\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
and
\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}:=(\bm{\alpha},1)\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
. For any
T>0
,
r\in(0,1)
and
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
, let

\displaystyle S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}:=\left\{\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k_{\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}:y>T^{-1},\ \bm{\alpha}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{r}(\bm{\alpha})\right\}

be the sector with height
T
and spherical part given by
\mathfrak{D}_{r}(\bm{\alpha})
. We also consider another family of sets: For any continuous, decreasing function
\psi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and for any
T>0
define

(5.1)

\displaystyle E_{\psi,T}:=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}:\left\|\bm{e}_{0}-\tfrac{\bm{v}}{v_{n+2}}\right\|<\psi(v_{n+2}),\ 0<v_{n+2}<T\right\}.

The starting point of this reduction is the simple observation that, for
Q(\bm{x},y)=\mathcal{Q}(\bm{x})-y^{2}
,
(\bm{p},q)\in\mathcal{L}_{Q}
if and only if
\frac{\bm{p}}{q}
is a rational point on
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
written in lowest terms. We have the following simple lemma relating the counting of rational points on
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
to counting points in corresponding
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}
-translates.

Lemma 5.1
.

Fix
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
and let
\bm{x}=\bm{\alpha}\tilde{\tau}^{-1}\in S_{\mathcal{Q}}
. Then for any
0<r<1
and
T>1
,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r,T)=\#\left(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}\cap S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}\right),

and for any continuous decreasing function
\psi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and any
T>1
,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q},\psi}(\bm{x},T)=\#\left(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}k_{\bm{\alpha}}^{-1}\cap E_{\psi,T}\right).

Here
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r,T)
and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q},\psi}(\bm{x},T)
are the two counting functions defined in (
1.16
) and (
1.20
) respectively.

Proof.

Let
(\bm{p},q)\in\mathcal{L}_{Q}
. For (
5.2
), note that
(\bm{p},q)\tau=(\bm{p}\tilde{\tau},q)\in\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}\cap S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}
if and only if
\|\tfrac{\bm{p}\tilde{\tau}}{q}-\bm{\alpha}\|<r
and
1\leq q<T
. The condition
\|\tfrac{\bm{p}\tilde{\tau}}{q}-\bm{\alpha}\|<r
is equivalent to
\|\tfrac{\bm{p}}{q}-\bm{x}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}<r
. This finishes the proof of (
5.2
).

Next, for (
5.3
), it suffices to show
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q},\psi}(\bm{x},T)=\#(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}\cap E_{\psi,T}k_{\bm{\alpha}})
. First note that

\displaystyle E_{\psi,T}k_{\bm{\alpha}}=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}:\left\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}-\tfrac{\bm{v}}{v_{n+2}}\right\|<\psi(v_{n+2}),\ 0<v_{n+2}<T\right\}.

This follows from the relation
\bm{e}_{0}k_{\bm{\alpha}}=\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}
and by noting that the
(n+2)
-th coordinate of
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
remains unchanged under the right multiplication of
k_{\bm{\alpha}}
. Thus
(\bm{p},q)\tau=(\bm{p}\tilde{\tau},q)\in\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}\cap E_{\psi,T}k_{\bm{\alpha}}
if and only if
\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}-(\tfrac{\bm{p}\tilde{\tau}}{q},1)\|<\psi(q)
and
1\leq q<T
. The first condition is further equivalent to
\|\bm{\alpha}-\tfrac{\bm{p}\tilde{\tau}}{q}\|<\psi(q)\ \Leftrightarrow\ \|\bm{x}-\tfrac{\bm{p}}{q}\|_{\mathcal{Q}}<\psi(q)
.
This finishes the proof of (
5.3
).
∎

5.2.
Measure estimates

In this section we prove various measure estimates regrading the sets
S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}
and
E_{\psi,T}
, which in view of
Lemma
5.1
and the counting results in section
3
, will contribute the main terms in our counting formulas for
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bm{x},r,T)
and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q},\psi}(\bm{x},T)
.

We first prove a measure estimate for balls and differences of balls on spheres.
Recall that the spherical measure
\sigma_{n}
has the following characterization that for any Borel set
B\subseteq S^{n}
,

(5.4)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(B)=\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\widetilde{B})}{\operatorname{vol}(\widetilde{S}^{n})},

where
\widetilde{B}:=\left\{t\bm{x}:0\leq t\leq 1,\bm{x}\in B\right\}
. In particular,
\widetilde{S}^{n}
is the closed unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and recall that
\operatorname{vol}(\widetilde{S}^{n})=\frac{\pi^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{n+3}{2})}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
and for any
r>0
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(\mathfrak{D}_{r}(\bm{\alpha}))=\tfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+3}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}(n+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2}{2}\right)}r^{n}+O_{n}(r^{n+2}).

Moreover, for any
0<r_{2}<r_{1}<1.1
,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{r_{1}}\left(\bm{\alpha}\right)\setminus\mathfrak{D}_{r_{2}}\left(\bm{\alpha}\right)\right)\ll_{n}r_{1}^{n}-r_{2}^{n}.

Proof.

Up to enlarging the bounding constant, (
5.5
) holds trivially for
r\geq 1
; thus we may assume
0<r<1
. Since
\sigma_{n}
and the Euclidean norm (on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
) are both rotation-invariant, without loss of generality, we may assume
\bm{\alpha}=(1,\bm{0})
. Then by direct computation we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{r}(\bm{\alpha})=\left\{\bm{v}\in S^{n}:v_{1}>1-\tfrac{r^{2}}{2}\right\}

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{r_{1},r_{2}}:=\mathfrak{D}_{r_{1}}(\bm{\alpha})\setminus\mathfrak{D}_{r_{2}}(\bm{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{v}\in S^{n}:1-\tfrac{r_{1}^{2}}{2}<v_{1}\leq 1-\tfrac{r_{2}^{2}}{2}\right\}.

Writing
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
as
\bm{v}=(v_{1},\bm{w})
with
v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one can verify that

\displaystyle\left\{(v_{1},\bm{w}):\|\bm{w}\|^{2}<a^{2}_{r}v_{1}^{2},\ 0<v_{1}<1-r^{2}/2\right\}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathfrak{D}}_{r}(\bm{\alpha})\subseteq\left\{(v_{1},\bm{w}):\|\bm{w}\|^{2}\leq a_{r}^{2}v_{1}^{2},\ 0\leq v_{1}\leq 1\right\},

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathfrak{C}}_{r_{1},r_{2}}\subseteq\left\{(v_{1},\bm{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:a_{r_{2}}^{2}v_{1}^{2}\leq\|\bm{w}\|^{2}\leq a_{r_{1}}^{2}v_{1}^{2},\ 0\leq v_{1}\leq 1-\tfrac{r_{2}^{2}}{2}\right\},

where for any
t\in(0,1)
,
a_{t}:=\frac{t\sqrt{1-t^{2}/4}}{1-t^{2}/2}
.
This, together with (
5.4
) implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\widetilde{S}^{n-1})}{\operatorname{vol}(\widetilde{S}^{n})}\int_{0}^{1-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}a_{r}^{n}v_{1}^{n}\,\text{d}v_{1}\leq\sigma_{n}(\mathfrak{D}_{r}(\bm{\alpha}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\widetilde{S}^{n-1})}{\operatorname{vol}(\widetilde{S}^{n})}\int_{0}^{1}a_{r}^{n}v_{1}^{n}\,\text{d}v_{1},

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(\mathfrak{C}_{r_{1},r_{2}})

\displaystyle\ll_{n}\int_{0}^{1-r_{2}^{2}/2}\left(a_{r_{1}}^{n}-a_{r_{2}}^{n}\right)v_{1}^{n}\,\text{d}v_{1}\asymp_{n}a_{r_{1}}^{n}-a_{r_{2}}^{n}.

Now (
5.5
) follows from the estimate that
a_{t}=t+O(t^{3})
for all
t\in(0,1)
together with the relation
\frac{\operatorname{vol}(\widetilde{S}^{n-1})}{(n+1)\operatorname{vol}(\widetilde{S}^{n})}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+3}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}(n+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2}{2}\right)}
.

For (
5.6
), it suffices to show
a_{r_{1}}^{n}-a_{r_{2}}^{n}\ll_{n}r_{1}^{2}-r_{2}^{n}
. If
r_{1}>2r_{2}
then

\displaystyle a_{r_{1}}^{n}-a_{r_{2}}^{n}<a_{r_{1}}^{n}\asymp r_{1}^{n}\asymp r_{1}^{n}-r_{2}^{n}.

If
r_{1}\leq 2r_{2}
, let
h(t):=a_{t}^{n}
so that
h^{\prime}(t)=\frac{8na_{t}^{n-1}}{\sqrt{4-t^{2}}(2-t^{2})^{2}}
. Then by the mean value theorem we can estimate

\displaystyle a_{r_{1}}^{n}-a_{r_{2}}^{n}

\displaystyle=(r_{1}-r_{2})h^{\prime}(t)

for some
t\in[r_{2},r_{1}]
. Now again using the estimate
a_{t}=t+O(t^{3})
for all
t\in(0,1)
, we see that
|h^{\prime}(t)|\ll_{n}r_{2}^{n-1}
for all
t\in[r_{2},r_{1}]\subseteq[r_{2},2r_{2}]
, implying that in this case we also have

\displaystyle a_{r_{1}}^{n}-a_{r_{2}}^{n}

\displaystyle\ll_{n}(r_{1}-r_{2})r_{2}^{n-1}<r_{1}^{n}-r_{2}^{n},

as needed.
∎

This lemma implies the following measure estimate for the sectors considered above: For any
T>0
,
r\in(0,1)
and
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
,

(5.7)

\displaystyle m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}}(S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}})=\tfrac{1}{n}T^{n}\sigma_{n}(\mathfrak{D}_{r}(\bm{\alpha}))=\tfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+3}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}n(n+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2}{2}\right)}T^{n}(r^{n}+O_{n}(r^{n+2})).

Next, fix
\psi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
a continuous decreasing function, we compute the measure of the set
E_{\psi,T}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
T>0
we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}}(E_{\psi,T})=\tfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+3}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}(n+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2}{2}\right)}\mathcal{J}_{\psi}(T)+O_{n}(\mathcal{I}_{\psi}(T)),

where
\mathcal{J}_{\psi}(T):=\int_{0}^{T}t^{n-1}\psi(t)^{n}\,\text{d}t
and
\mathcal{I}_{\psi}(T):=\int_{0}^{T}t^{n-1}\psi(t)^{n+2}\,\text{d}t
.

Proof.

Fix
T>0
and let
\bm{\alpha}_{0}=(-1,\bm{0})\in S^{n}
so that
(\bm{\alpha}_{0},1)=\bm{e}_{0}
. It is then easy to see that under the polar coordinates
(y,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times S^{n}
the set
E_{\psi,T}
can be described as following

\displaystyle E_{\psi,T}

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k_{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}:\|\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0}\|<\psi(y^{-1}),\ y>T^{-1}\right\},

which then together with (
5.5
) implies that

\displaystyle m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}}(E_{\psi,T})

\displaystyle=\int_{T^{-1}}^{\infty}\sigma_{n}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{\psi(y^{-1})}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})\right)y^{-(n+1)}\,\text{d}y

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+3}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}(n+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2}{2}\right)}\int_{T^{-1}}^{\infty}y^{-(n+1)}\left(\psi(y^{-1})^{n}+O_{n}\left(\psi(y^{-1})^{n+2}\right)\right)\,\text{d}y

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+3}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}(n+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2}{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{T}t^{n-1}\psi(t)^{n}\,\text{d}t+O_{n}\left(\int_{0}^{T}t^{n-1}\psi(t)^{n+2}\,\text{d}t\right).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Recall that in
Theorem
1.9
the approximating function
\psi
is only defined on the set of positive integers, and the main term is given in term of the discrete sum
J_{\psi}(T)=\sum_{1\leq q<T}q^{n-1}\psi(q)^{n}
. On the other hand here for our measure estimate
\psi
is assumed to be continuous and the main term is given in terms of the integral
\mathcal{J}_{\psi}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}t^{n-1}{\psi}(t)^{n}\,\text{d}t
.
The following lemma shows that these two terms are the same up to a smaller error term.

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume further
\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n-1}\psi(t)^{n}\,\text{d}t=\infty
. Then for any
T>1
we have

(5.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\psi}(T)=J_{\psi}(T)+O_{\psi}\left(J_{\psi}(T)^{\frac{n+3}{n+4}}\right).

Proof.

Fix
T>1
. For any integer
1\leq q<T
, since
\psi
is decreasing, we can estimate

\displaystyle\int_{q}^{q+1}t^{n-1}\psi(t)^{n}\,dt\leq(q+1)^{n-1}\psi(q)^{n}=q^{n-1}\psi(q)^{n}+O_{n}(q^{n-2}\psi(q)^{n}).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{q}^{q+1}t^{n-1}\psi(t)^{n}\,dt\geq q^{n-1}\psi(q+1)^{n}=(q+1)^{n-1}\psi(q+1)^{n}+O_{n}\left((q+1)^{n-2}\psi(q+1)^{n}\right).

From these two estimates one easily sees that

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\psi}(T)=J_{\psi}(T)+O_{\psi}\left(R_{\psi}(T)+1\right),

where
R_{\psi}(T):=\sum_{1\leq q\leq T}q^{n-2}\psi(q)^{n}
.
Since
\psi
is continuous, decreasing and
\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n-1}\psi(t)^{n}\,\text{d}t=\infty
, we have
J_{\psi}(T)\to\infty
as
T\to\infty
. Thus the error
O_{\psi}(1)
can be absorbed into the desired error
O_{\psi}\left(J_{\psi}(T)^{\frac{n+3}{n+4}}\right)
, and it remains to show
R_{\psi}(T)\ll_{n,\psi}J_{\psi}(T)^{\frac{n+3}{n+4}}
for all
T>1
. For this by taking

\displaystyle(p,p^{\prime})=(\tfrac{n+4}{n+3},n+4)\quad\text{and}\quad(a_{q},b_{q})=(q^{\frac{(n-1)(n+3)}{n+4}}\psi(q)^{\frac{n(n+3)}{n+4}},q^{-\frac{5}{n+4}}\psi(q)^{\frac{n}{n+4}})

(so that
a_{q}b_{q}=q^{n-2}\psi(q)^{n}
and
a_{q}^{p}=q^{n-1}\psi(q)^{n}
) and applying Hölder’s inequality we get

\displaystyle R_{\psi}(T)=\sum_{1\leq q\leq T}a_{q}b_{q}\leq\left(\sum_{1\leq q\leq T}a_{q}^{p}\right)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{1\leq q\leq T}b_{q}^{p^{\prime}}\right)^{1/p^{\prime}}\ll_{n,\psi}J_{\psi}(T)^{\frac{n+3}{n+4}},

where for the second estimate we used the estimate that

\sum_{1\leq q\leq T}b_{q}^{p^{\prime}}=\sum_{1\leq q\leq T}q^{-5}\psi(q)^{n}\leq\psi(1)^{n}\sum_{q\in\mathbb{N}}q^{-5}\ll_{\psi}1.

This finishes the proof.
∎

5.3.
Choosing identity neighborhoods

In this subsection we choose appropriate families of identity neighborhoods with respect to which we verify well-roundedness. First, to prove
Theorem
5.5
we choose the following family of identity neighborhoods in
G
.

Lemma 5.8
.

For any
\epsilon,r\in(0,1)
and
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
, define

(5.11)

\displaystyle G_{\epsilon,r}(\bm{\alpha}):=\left\{g\in G:\|g\|_{\rm op}<1+\epsilon,\ \max\left\{\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|,\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g^{-1}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g^{-1}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|\right\}<r\epsilon\right\},

where
\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}:=(\bm{\alpha},1)\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
is as before. Then
G_{\epsilon,r}(\bm{\alpha})
is an inversion invariant identity neighborhood with

\displaystyle\mu_{G}(G_{\epsilon,r}(\bm{\alpha}))\asymp_{n}r^{n}\epsilon^{2n+1}\quad\text{and}\quad G_{\epsilon_{1},r}(\bm{\alpha})G_{\epsilon_{2},r}(\bm{\alpha})\subseteq G_{3(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}),r}(\bm{\alpha}),\quad\forall\ \epsilon,r\in(0,1),\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3}).

Proof.

To show invariance under inversion it suffices to show
\|g\|_{\rm op}=\|g^{-1}\|_{\rm op}
for all
g\in G
.
Indeed using the
KA^{+}K
-decomposition we can write
g=k_{1}a_{y}k_{2}
with
k_{1},k_{2}\in K
and
a_{y}\in A
with
y\geq 1
. Since
\|\cdot\|_{\rm op}
is bi-
K
-invariant, we get
\|g\|_{\rm op}=\|a_{y}\|_{\rm op}
and similarly
\|g^{-1}\|_{\rm op}=\|a_{y}^{-1}\|_{\rm op}
. A simple calculation then shows that
\|a_{y}\|_{\rm op}=\|a_{y}^{-1}\|_{\rm op}=\max\{y,y^{-1}\}
, implying that
\|g\|_{\rm op}=\|g^{-1}\|_{\rm op}
.

Next for the volume estimate, by the
K
-invariance of the Haar measure
\mu_{G}
and of the Euclidean norm, we may assume
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\alpha}_{0}\in S^{n}
.
We claim that

(5.12)

G_{\epsilon/4,r}^{\prime}\subseteq G_{\epsilon,r}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})\subseteq G^{\prime}_{4\epsilon,r},\quad\forall\ \epsilon,r\in(0,1),

where

\displaystyle G^{\prime}_{\epsilon,r}:=\left\{u_{\bm{x}}a_{y}mk_{\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}}\in G:\|\bm{x}\|<\epsilon,\ |y-1|<\epsilon,\ m\in M,\ \|\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}-\bm{\alpha}_{0}\|<r\epsilon\right\}.

To show the first inclusion, fix
r,\epsilon\in(0,1)
and take
g=u_{\bm{x}}a_{y}mk_{\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}}\in G^{\prime}_{\epsilon/4,r}
.
Then

\|g\|_{\rm op}=\|u_{\bm{x}}a_{y}\|_{\rm op}\leq\|u_{\bm{x}}\|_{\rm op}\|a_{y}\|_{\rm op}.

We have
\|a_{y}\|_{\rm op}=\max\{y,y^{-1}\}<(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})
and a simple calculation shows that

\|u_{\bm{x}}\|_{\rm op}=1+\frac{\|\bm{x}\|^{2}+\|\bm{x}\|\sqrt{\|\bm{x}\|^{2}+4}}{2}<1+\frac{\epsilon}{2},

so that
\|g\|_{\rm op}<1+\epsilon
. Next note that

\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g=\bm{e}_{0}u_{\bm{x}}a_{y}mk_{\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}}=y^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}
and hence
\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g\|}=\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}
and

\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}-\bm{\alpha}_{0}\|\leq\tfrac{r\epsilon}{4\sqrt{2}}<r\epsilon.

Next, we can use again that
\|g\|_{\rm op}<1+\epsilon
to get that

\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g^{-1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|=\left\|\left(\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)g^{-1}\right\|<(1+\epsilon)\tfrac{r\epsilon}{4\sqrt{2}}.

We can use the following simple geometric observation,

(5.13)

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{u}_{1}-\bm{u}_{2}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{\|\bm{u}_{1}-\lambda\bm{u}_{2}\|^{2}}{\lambda},\quad\text{$\forall\ \bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2}\in S^{n}$ and $\lambda>0$},

with
\bm{u}_{1}=\frac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}
,
\bm{u}_{2}=\frac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g^{-1}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g^{-1}\|}
and
\lambda=\frac{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g^{-1}\|}{2}
to get that

\displaystyle\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g^{-1}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g^{-1}\|}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\tfrac{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g\|}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g^{-1}\|}\right)^{1/2}\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}g^{-1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|<(1+\epsilon)^{2}\tfrac{r\epsilon}{4\sqrt{2}}<r\epsilon.

This shows that
G_{\epsilon/4,r}^{\prime}\subseteq G_{\epsilon,r}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})
. The arguments for the other inclusion are similar (and simpler) and we omit the details.

Now using the Haar measure description (
2.5
) and the measure decomposition
\text{d}\mu_{K}(mk_{\bm{\alpha}})=\text{d}\mu_{M}(m)\text{d}\sigma_{n}(\bm{\alpha})
and the volume estimate (
5.5
), we can compute for any
\epsilon,r\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mu_{G}\left(G^{\prime}_{\epsilon,r}\right)=\int_{\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\>:\>\|\bm{x}\|<\epsilon\}}\,\text{d}\bm{x}\int_{1-\epsilon}^{1+\epsilon}\,y^{n}\,\text{d}y\sigma_{n}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{r\epsilon}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})\right)\asymp_{n}r^{n}\epsilon^{2n+1}.

This estimate together with (
5.12
) implies that

\displaystyle r^{n}\epsilon^{2n+1}\ll_{n}\mu_{G}\left(G^{\prime}_{\epsilon/4,r}\right)\leq\mu_{G}\left(G_{\epsilon,r}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})\right)\leq\mu_{G}\left(G^{\prime}_{4\epsilon,r}\right)\ll_{n}r^{n}\epsilon^{2n+1},

thus proving this volume estimate.

Finally, we prove the translation relation. For any
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3})
, take
g_{1}\in G_{\epsilon_{1},r}(\bm{\alpha})
and
g_{2}\in G_{\epsilon_{2},r}(\bm{\alpha})
we need to show
g_{1}g_{2}\in G_{3(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}),r}(\bm{\alpha})
. That is,
\|g_{1}g_{2}\|_{\rm op}\leq 1+3(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})
and

\max\left\{\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|,\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|\right\}<3r(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}).

The first relation is easy since

\|g_{1}g_{2}\|_{\rm op}\leq\|g_{1}\|_{\rm op}\|g_{2}\|_{\rm op}<(1+\epsilon_{1})(1+\epsilon_{2})<1+3(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}).

For the second relation, we only prove
\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|<3r(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})
and the other inequality follows similarly. For this, first observe

\displaystyle\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|\leq\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|\|g_{2}\|_{\rm op}<r\epsilon_{1}(1+\epsilon_{2}).

Now applying (
5.13
) with
(\bm{u}_{1}=\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}\|},\bm{u}_{2}=\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}\|}
and
\lambda=\tfrac{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}\|\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}\|}{\sqrt{2}\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}\|}
we get

\displaystyle\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}\|}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{2}\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}\|}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}\|\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}\|}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|<{r\epsilon_{1}(1+\epsilon_{2})^{2}}<3r\epsilon_{1}.

Hence by the triangle inequality we get

\displaystyle\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|\leq\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{1}g_{2}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}\|}\right\|+\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}g_{2}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|<3r\epsilon_{1}+r\epsilon_{2}<3r(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}).

This finishes the proof.
∎

Next, to prove
Theorem
5.6
we choose the following family of identity neighborhoods of the parabolic subgroup
P
.

Lemma 5.9
.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
let
P_{\epsilon}:=G_{\epsilon,r}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})\cap P
for some
r\in(0,1)
. Then
P_{\epsilon}
is independent of the choice of
r
and it satisfies

\displaystyle\mu_{P}(P_{\epsilon})\asymp\epsilon^{n+1},\ \forall\ \epsilon\in(0,1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{\epsilon_{1}}P_{\epsilon_{2}}\subseteq P_{3(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})},\ \forall\ \epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3}),

where for
p=u_{\bm{x}}a_{y}m\in P=UAM
,
\text{d}\mu_{P}(p)=y^{-1}\,\text{d}\bm{x}\text{d}y\text{d}\mu_{M}(m)
is the right Haar measure of
P
as in
Remark
3.14
.

Proof.

To show that
P_{\epsilon}
is independent of the choice of
r
, we claim that

\displaystyle P_{\epsilon}=\left\{p\in P:\|p\|_{\rm op}<1+\epsilon\right\}.

For this, note that
\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}=\bm{e}_{0}
and for
p=u_{\bm{x}}a_{y}m\in P
,
\bm{e}_{0}p=y^{-1}\bm{e}_{0}
, implying that
\frac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}p}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}p\|}=\frac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}
. This implies that the second defining condition of
G_{\epsilon,r}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})
is always satisfied for any
r\in(0,1)
. Hence the claim holds. From the analysis in the previous proof, we see that
P_{\epsilon/4}^{\prime}\subseteq P_{\epsilon}\subseteq P_{4\epsilon}^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle P_{\epsilon}^{\prime}:=G_{\epsilon,r}^{\prime}\cap P=\left\{u_{\bm{x}}a_{y}m\in P:\|\bm{x}\|<\epsilon,\ |y-1|<\epsilon,\ m\in M\right\}.

The volume estimate then follows easily from this inclusion relation. The translation relation also follows easily from the translation relation in
Lemma
5.8
and the definition
P_{\epsilon}=G_{\epsilon,r}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})\cap P
.
∎

Finally, for
Theorem
5.7
we choose another family of identity neighborhoods of
P
. It turns out it will be more convenient to write elements
p\in P
in the form
p=ma_{y}u_{\bm{x}}
with
m\in M
,
a_{y}\in A
and
u_{\bm{x}}\in U
. (This can be done by expressing
p^{-1}
using the Langlands decomposition
P=UAM
.) We note that under these coordinates

(5.14)

\displaystyle\text{d}\mu_{P}(ma_{y}u_{\bm{x}})=y^{n-1}\,\text{d}\mu_{M}(m)\text{d}y\text{d}\bm{x}.

We now introduce the family of identity neighborhoods we will be working with.

Lemma 5.10
.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
define
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}:=P^{\prime}_{\epsilon}\cap(P^{\prime})_{\epsilon}^{-1}
, where

\displaystyle P_{\epsilon}^{\prime}:=\left\{ma_{y}u_{\bm{x}}\in P:m\in M,\ \|a_{y}\|_{\rm op}<1+\tfrac{\epsilon}{4},\ \|\bm{x}\|<\tfrac{\epsilon}{4}\right\}.

Then we have
\mu_{P}(\tilde{P}_{\epsilon})\asymp_{n}\epsilon^{n+1}
for any
0<\epsilon<1
.

Proof.

Using (
5.14
) and recalling that
\|a_{y}\|_{\rm op}=\max\{y,y^{-1}\}
it is easy to see that

\mu_{P}(P^{\prime}_{\epsilon})\asymp\epsilon^{n+1},\quad\forall\ 0<\epsilon<1.

It thus suffices to show
P^{\prime}_{\epsilon/2}\subseteq\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Since
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}=P^{\prime}_{\epsilon}\cap(P^{\prime})_{\epsilon}^{-1}
and
P^{\prime}_{\epsilon/2}\subseteq P^{\prime}_{\epsilon}
, we only need to show
P^{\prime}_{\epsilon/2}\subseteq(P^{\prime})_{\epsilon}^{-1}
. Take
p=ma_{y}u_{\bm{x}}\in P^{\prime}_{\epsilon/2}
, i.e.
m\in M
,
\|a_{y}\|_{\rm op}<1+\frac{\epsilon}{8}
and
\|\bm{x}\|<\frac{\epsilon}{8}
, we would like to show
p^{-1}\in P^{\prime}_{\epsilon}
. Recall also that
m\in M
is of the form
m=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&&\\
&\tilde{m}&\\
&&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
for some
\tilde{m}\in\operatorname{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. By direct computation we have

\displaystyle p^{-1}=u_{-\bm{x}}a_{1/y}m^{-1}=m^{-1}a_{1/y}(ma_{y}u_{-\bm{x}}a_{1/y}m^{-1})=m^{\prime}a_{y^{\prime}}u_{\bm{x}^{\prime}},

with
m^{\prime}=m^{-1}
,
y^{\prime}=y^{-1}
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}=-y(\bm{x}\tilde{m}^{-1})
. Thus
\|a_{y^{\prime}}\|_{\rm op}=\|a_{y}\|_{\rm op}<1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}
and

\|\bm{x}^{\prime}\|=\|-y(\bm{x}\tilde{m}^{-1})\|=y\|\bm{x}\|<\left(1+\tfrac{\epsilon}{8}\right)\times\tfrac{\epsilon}{8}<\tfrac{\epsilon}{4}.

This implies
p^{-1}\in P^{\prime}_{\epsilon}
, finishing the proof.
∎

5.4.
Verifying well-roundedness

In this subsection, we verify well-roundedness for the family
\{S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}\}_{T>1}
(with
r\in(0,1)
and
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
fixed), the two-parameter family
\{S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}_{0}}\}_{T>1,0<r<1}
and the family
\{E_{\psi,T}\}_{T>1}
with respect to the three corresponding families of identity neighborhoods introduced above.
Recall from section
3
, for any
\beta\in[1,2),c>0
,
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
is defined to be the family of Borel sets
B\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
which satisfy (
3.1
) (with
\Gamma\backslash G=\tau^{-1}\operatorname{SO}_{Q}^{+}(\mathbb{Z})\tau\backslash\operatorname{SO}_{Q_{n}}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathcal{L}=\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{Q}
,
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
,
\mu_{\Gamma}=\tilde{\mu}_{Q}
and
m_{\mathcal{V}}=\omega_{Q}m_{\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}}
).
As discussed in
Remark
3.2
, there exists some constant
c>0
depending only on
Q
such that
\mathcal{A}_{\beta_{Q},c}
contains all the generalized sectors, and all the differences of two nested generalized sectors. Moreover, if
c_{Q}=0
(so that
\beta_{Q}=1
),
\mathcal{A}_{\beta_{Q},c}
contains all the finite-measure Borel sets in
\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
. We fix this constant
c
and let
\beta=\beta_{Q}
for the remaining of this section.

We first verify well-roundedness for the first two families.

Lemma 5.11
.

For any
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
, for any
T>0
and for any
\epsilon,r\in(0,1)
we have

(5.15)

\displaystyle S_{(1-\epsilon)T,(1-\epsilon)r,\bm{\alpha}}\subseteq S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}h\subseteq S_{(1+\epsilon)T,(1+\epsilon)r,\bm{\alpha}},\quad\forall\ h\in G_{\epsilon/6,r}(\bm{\alpha}).

Remark 5.16
.

When
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\alpha}_{0}=(-1,\bm{0})\in S^{n}
, since
P_{\epsilon}\subseteq G_{\epsilon,r}(\bm{\alpha}_{0})
, the relations in (
5.15
) also holds for any
h\in P_{\epsilon}
.

Proof.

First note that since for any
\bm{v}=\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k_{\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}}=y^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
,
\frac{\bm{v}}{\|\bm{v}\|}=\frac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}\|}=\frac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}
we have

\displaystyle S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}:0<v_{n+2}<T,\ \left\|\tfrac{\bm{v}}{\|\bm{v}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|<\tfrac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\right\}.

Here
\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}=(\bm{\alpha}^{\prime},1)\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
. We now prove (
5.15
). We first prove the second inclusion relation in (
5.15
). Take any
\bm{v}\in S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}
(i.e.
0<v_{n+2}<T
and
\left\|\tfrac{\bm{v}}{\|\bm{v}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|<\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}
) and
h\in G_{\epsilon/6,r}(\bm{\alpha})
(i.e.
\|h\|_{\rm op}<1+\tfrac{\epsilon}{6}
and
\max\left\{\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|,\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h^{-1}}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h^{-1}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|\right\}<\frac{r\epsilon}{6}
), we need to show
\bm{v}h\in S_{(1+\epsilon)T,r(1+\epsilon),\bm{\alpha}}
.
Let
(\bm{v}h)_{n+2}
be the
(n+2)
-th coordinate of
\bm{v}h
. Since
\|h\|_{\rm op}<1+\frac{\epsilon}{6}
we get

0<(\bm{v}h)_{n+2}=\tfrac{\|\bm{v}h\|}{\sqrt{2}}=\left(1+\tfrac{\epsilon}{6}\right)\tfrac{\|\bm{v}\|}{\sqrt{2}}<(1+\epsilon)v_{n+2}<(1+\epsilon)T.

Moreover, we have
\left\|\tfrac{\bm{v}h}{\|\bm{v}\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|<\frac{(1+\tfrac{\epsilon}{6})r}{\sqrt{2}}
. Now apply the geometric inequality (
5.13
) with
\bm{u}_{1}=\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h\|}
,
\bm{u}_{2}=\tfrac{\bm{v}h}{\|\bm{v}h\|}
and

\lambda=\tfrac{\sqrt{2}\|\bm{v}h\|}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h\|\|\bm{v}\|}
to bound

\displaystyle\left\|\tfrac{\bm{v}h}{\|\bm{v}h\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h\|}\right\|\leq

\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{\|\bm{v}\|\sqrt{2}}{\|\bm{v}h\|\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h\|}\right)^{1/2}\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h}{\sqrt{2}}-\tfrac{\bm{v}h}{\|\bm{v}\|}\right\|\leq\tfrac{(1+\tfrac{\epsilon}{6})^{2}r}{\sqrt{2}}.

Next, using that
\left\|\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h}{\|\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}h\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|<\frac{r\epsilon}{6}
we can bound

\left\|\tfrac{\bm{v}h}{\|\bm{v}h\|}-\tfrac{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right\|\leq\left((1+\tfrac{\epsilon}{6})^{2}+\tfrac{\sqrt{2}\epsilon}{6}\right)\tfrac{r}{\sqrt{2}}<\tfrac{(1+\epsilon)r}{\sqrt{2}}.

This shows that
\bm{v}h\in S_{(1+\epsilon)T,(1+\epsilon)r,\bm{\alpha}}
, thus proving the second inclusion relation in (
5.15
).
The first inclusion relation in (
5.15
) follows from the above analysis since for any
h\in G_{\epsilon/6}(\bm{\alpha})
(thus also
h^{-1}\in G_{\epsilon/6}(\bm{\alpha})
)

\displaystyle S_{(1-\epsilon)T,(1-\epsilon)r,\bm{\alpha}}h^{-1}\subseteq S_{(1-\epsilon^{2})T,(1-\epsilon^{2})r,\bm{\alpha}}\subseteq S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}},

which is equivalent to the first inclusion relation in (
5.15
). ∎

Proposition 5.12
.

For any
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
and for any
r\in(0,1)
, the family
\{S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}\}_{T>1}
is strongly
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
-well rounded with respect to the family
\left\{G_{\epsilon/6,r}(\bm{\alpha})\right\}_{0<\epsilon<1}\subseteq G
. Similarly, when
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\alpha}_{0}
, the family
\left\{S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}_{0}}\right\}_{T>1,0<r<1}
is
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
-well rounded with respect to the family
\left\{P_{\epsilon/6}\right\}_{0<\epsilon<1}
.

Proof.

For the first half of this proposition, fix
\bm{\alpha}\in S^{n}
and
r\in(0,1)
and denote by
\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}=G_{\epsilon/6,r}(\bm{\alpha})
. First note that clearly for any
T>1
,
S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}
is a generalized sector. Thus
\{S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}\}_{T>1}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
. Next, by definition, we need to show that for any
B=S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}}
with
T>1
and for any
\epsilon\in(0,0.1)
, there exists
\overline{B}_{\epsilon},\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
satisfying that

\displaystyle\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\subseteq\bigcap_{h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}}Bh\subseteq\bigcup_{h\in\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}}Bh\subseteq\overline{B}_{\epsilon},\quad|\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}|\ll_{n}\epsilon|B|\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}.

For each
\epsilon\in(0,0.1)
, we take
\underline{B}_{\epsilon}=S_{(1-\epsilon)T,(1-\epsilon)r,\bm{\alpha}}
and
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}=S_{(1+\epsilon)T,(1+\epsilon)r,\bm{\alpha}}
. Then the above first relation follows from
Lemma
5.11
. Note also that both
\underline{B}_{\epsilon}
and
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}
are generalized sectors and
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\subseteq\underline{B}_{\epsilon}
, thus
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
. It thus remains to show the above measure bound. For this note that

\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\subseteq R_{1}\bigcup R_{2}

with

\displaystyle R_{1}:=\left\{\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k_{\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}:0<y^{-1}\leq(1-\epsilon)T,\ \bm{\alpha}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{r(1+\epsilon)}(\bm{\alpha})\setminus\mathfrak{D}_{r(1-\epsilon)}(\bm{\alpha})\right\},

and

\displaystyle R_{2}:=\left\{\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k_{\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}:(1-\epsilon)T\leq y^{-1}\leq(1+\epsilon)T,\ \bm{\alpha}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{r(1+\epsilon)}(\bm{\alpha})\right\}.

We can then apply (
5.6
) and (
5.5
) respectively to estimate that

\displaystyle|R_{1}|=\frac{\omega_{Q}}{n}(1-\epsilon)^{n}T^{n}\sigma_{n}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{r(1+\epsilon)}(\bm{\alpha})\setminus\mathfrak{D}_{r(1-\epsilon)}(\bm{\alpha})\right)\ll_{Q,n}\epsilon T^{n}r^{n}\asymp_{Q,n}\epsilon|B|,

and

\displaystyle|R_{2}|=\frac{\omega_{Q}}{n}\left((1+\epsilon)^{n}-(1-\epsilon)^{n}\right)T^{n}\sigma_{n}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{r(1+\epsilon)}(\bm{\alpha})\right)\ll_{Q,n}\epsilon T^{n}r^{n}\asymp_{Q,n}\epsilon|B|.

This proves the above bound, and hence also the first half of this proposition.

For the second half of this proposition, note that using similar arguments with
Remark
5.16
in place of
Lemma
5.11
we see that for any fixed
r\in(0,1)
, the family
\left\{S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}_{0}}\right\}_{T>1}
is
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
-well rounded with respect to
\left\{P_{\epsilon/6}\right\}_{0<\epsilon<1}
(with the parameter
\epsilon_{0}=\frac{1}{3}
). Since the family
\{P_{\epsilon/6}\}_{0<\epsilon<1}
is independent of
r
, we see that that larger family
\left\{S_{T,r,\bm{\alpha}_{0}}\right\}_{T>1,0<r<1}
is
\mathcal{A}_{\beta,c}
-well rounded with respect to
\{P_{\epsilon/6}\}_{0<\epsilon<1}
.
∎

Finally, we verify well-roundedness for the family
\{E_{\psi,T}\}_{T>1}
.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
\{E_{\psi,T}\}_{T>1}
be as in (
5.1
) with
\psi:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
decreasing and continuous and let
\{\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}\}_{0<\epsilon<1}
be as in
Lemma
5.10
.
Then we have

(5.17)

\displaystyle E_{\psi^{-}_{\epsilon},(1+\epsilon)^{-1}T}\subseteq E_{\psi,T}h\subseteq E_{\psi^{+}_{\epsilon},(1+\epsilon)T},\quad\forall\ T>0,\ h\in\tilde{P}_{\epsilon},\ \epsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}),

where

\psi^{\pm}_{\epsilon}(t):=(1\pm\epsilon)\psi((1+\epsilon)^{\mp 1}t)
for any
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, for any
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
, we write it in the form
\bm{v}=(v_{1},\bm{w},v_{n+2})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
For any
g\in G
, we denote by
(v_{1}(g),\bm{w}(g),v_{n+2}(g)):=\bm{v}g
.
We first note that
E_{\psi,T}
has the following alternative description that for any
T>1

\displaystyle E_{\psi,T}

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}:2(v_{1}+v_{n+2})<v_{n+2}\psi^{2}(v_{n+2}),\ 0<v_{n+2}<T\right\}.

We now prove the second inclusion relation in (
5.17
). Fix
T>0
and
\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
. Take
\bm{v}\in E_{\psi,T}
and
h\in\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
, we would like to show
\bm{v}h\in E_{\psi^{+}_{\epsilon},(1+\epsilon)T}
, that is

(5.18)

\displaystyle 0<v_{n+2}(h)<(1+\epsilon)T,

and

(5.19)

\displaystyle 2(v_{n+2}(h)+v_{n+1}(h))<(1+\epsilon)^{2}v_{n+2}(h)\psi^{2}\left((1+\epsilon)^{-1}v_{n+2}(h)\right).

By direct computation we have

\displaystyle v_{1}(h)=-\bm{w}\cdot\bm{x}+\tfrac{y}{2}(1-\|\bm{x}\|^{2})(v_{n+2}+v_{1})-\tfrac{1}{2y}(v_{n+2}-v_{1}),

and

\displaystyle v_{n+2}(h)=\bm{w}\cdot\bm{x}+\tfrac{y}{2}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{2})(v_{n+2}+v_{1})+\tfrac{1}{2y}(v_{n+2}-v_{1}).

Since
h\in\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}\subseteq P^{\prime}_{\epsilon}
, we can write
h=ma_{y}u_{\bm{x}}
with
m\in M
,
(1+\epsilon/4)^{-1}<y<1+\epsilon/4
and
\|\bm{x}\|<\epsilon/4
.
For (
5.18
) note that
v_{n+2}(h)>0
is automatically satisfied since
\bm{v}h\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}
; we thus only need to prove
v_{n+2}(h)<(1+\epsilon)T
. For this, by rewriting
v_{n+2}-v_{1}=2v_{n+2}-(v_{n+2}+v_{1})
we get

\displaystyle v_{n+2}(h)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{v_{n+2}}{y}+\bm{w}\cdot\bm{x}+\left(\tfrac{y}{2}(1+\|\bm{x}\|^{2})-\tfrac{1}{2y}\right)(v_{n+2}+v_{1}),

implying that (using the estimates
(1+\epsilon/4)^{-1}<y<1+\epsilon/4
,
\|\bm{x}\|<\epsilon/4
and
0<\epsilon<\tfrac{1}{2}<1
)

\displaystyle\left|v_{n+2}(h)-\tfrac{v_{n+2}}{y}\right|<\tfrac{\|\bm{w}\|\epsilon}{4}+\tfrac{5\epsilon}{16}(v_{n+2}+v_{1})=\tfrac{\epsilon}{4}(\|\bm{w}\|+v_{1})+\tfrac{\epsilon}{16}v_{1}+\tfrac{5\epsilon}{16}v_{n+2}.

Now using the estimates
(\|\bm{w}\|+v_{1})^{2}\leq 2(\|\bm{w}\|^{2}+v_{1}^{2})=2v_{n+2}^{2}
and
v_{1}\leq v_{n+2}
we can estimate

(5.20)

\displaystyle\left|v_{n+2}(h)-\tfrac{v_{n+2}}{y}\right|\leq\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{4}+\tfrac{1}{16}+\tfrac{5}{16}\right)\epsilon v_{n+2}<\tfrac{3\epsilon}{4}v_{n+2},

which then further implies (using also
1-\frac{\epsilon}{4}<(1+\frac{\epsilon}{4})^{-1}<y<1+\frac{\epsilon}{4}
)

(5.21)

\displaystyle\left(1-\epsilon\right)v_{n+2}<v_{n+2}(h)<\left(1+\epsilon\right)v_{n+2}.

This then immediately implies
v_{n+2}(h)<(1+\epsilon)v_{n+2}<(1+\epsilon)T
as desired.

For (
5.19
) note

\displaystyle v_{n+2}(h)+v_{1}(h)=y(v_{n+2}+v_{1}).

Thus using the assumptions that
\bm{v}\in E_{\psi,T}
(so that
2(v_{n+2}+v_{1})<v_{n+2}\psi^{2}(v_{n+2})
) and that
\psi(\cdot)
is decreasing and the estimates (
5.21
) and
(1+\epsilon/4)(1-\epsilon)^{-1}<(1+\epsilon)^{2}
(this is true since
\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
) we have

\displaystyle 2(v_{n+2}(h)+v_{1}(h))

\displaystyle<\left(1+\tfrac{\epsilon}{4}\right)v_{n+2}\psi^{2}(v_{n+2})<(1+\epsilon)^{2}v_{n+2}(h)\psi^{2}\left((1+\epsilon)^{-1}v_{n+2}(h)\right),

as desired. This finishes the proof of the second inclusion relation in (
5.17
).

For the other inclusion relation, take any
h\in\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}
, we need to show
E_{\psi^{-}_{\epsilon},(1+\epsilon)^{-1}T}\subseteq E_{\psi,T}h
, or equivalently,
E_{\psi^{-}_{\epsilon},(1+\epsilon)^{-1}T}h^{-1}\subseteq E_{\psi,T}
. This then follows from similar analysis and noting that
h^{-1}\in P^{\prime}_{\epsilon}
(since
\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}=P^{\prime}_{\epsilon}\cap(P^{\prime})_{\epsilon}^{-1}
).
∎

Proposition 5.14
.

Let
Q
and
\psi:[0,\infty)\to(0,\frac{1}{2})
be as in
Theorem
5.7
. Then the family
\{E_{\psi,T}\}_{T>1}
is
\mathcal{A}_{1,c}
-well rounded with respect to
\{\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}\}_{0<\epsilon<1}
.

Proof.

We show that for any
B=E_{\psi,T}
with
T>1
and for any
\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
there exist
\overline{B}_{\epsilon},\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{A}_{1,c}
satisfying

\displaystyle\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\subseteq\bigcap_{h\in\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}}Bh\subseteq\bigcup_{h\in\tilde{P}_{\epsilon}}Bh\subseteq\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\quad\text{and}\quad|\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}|\ll_{Q,n}\epsilon|B|.

For this, we take
\underline{B}_{\epsilon}=E_{\psi^{-}_{\epsilon},(1+\epsilon)^{-1}T}
and
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}=E_{\psi^{+}_{\epsilon},(1+\epsilon)T}
, where
\psi^{\pm}_{\epsilon}
are as in
Lemma
5.13
.
Clearly, both
\underline{B}_{\epsilon}
and
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}
are Borel sets with finite measure, and thus are contained in
\mathcal{A}_{1,c}
. The above inclusion relations follow from
Lemma
5.13
. For the measure bound, we note that
\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}\subseteq R_{1}\bigcup R_{2}
with

\displaystyle R_{1}:=\left\{\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k_{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}:\psi^{-}_{\epsilon}(y^{-1})\leq\|\bm{\alpha}_{0}-\bm{\alpha}\|\leq\psi^{+}_{\epsilon}(y^{-1}),\ 0<y^{-1}\leq(1+\epsilon)^{-1}T\right\},

and

\displaystyle R_{2}:=\left\{\bm{e}_{0}a_{y}k_{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{V}_{Q_{n}}^{+}:\|\bm{\alpha}_{0}-\bm{\alpha}\|\leq\psi^{+}_{\epsilon}(y^{-1}),\ (1+\epsilon)^{-1}T\leq y^{-1}\leq(1+\epsilon)T\right\}.

By
Lemma
5.2
we have (noting also that by our assumptions
\psi_{\epsilon}^{+}(t)<1
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
)

\displaystyle|R_{1}|\ll_{Q,n}\int_{0}^{(1+\epsilon)^{-1}T}t^{n-1}\left(\psi^{+}_{\epsilon}(t)^{n}-\psi^{-}_{\epsilon}(t)^{n}\right)\,\text{d}t\quad\text{and}\quad|R_{2}|\ll_{Q,n}\int_{(1+\epsilon)^{-1}T}^{(1+\epsilon)T}t^{n-1}\psi^{+}_{\epsilon}(t)^{n}\,\text{d}t.

This, together with the measure estimate
|B|\asymp_{Q,n}\mathcal{J}_{\psi}(T)
(cf.
Lemma
5.3
) implies that

\displaystyle|\overline{B}_{\epsilon}\setminus\underline{B}_{\epsilon}|

\displaystyle\ll_{Q,n}\int_{0}^{(1+\epsilon)T}t^{n-1}\psi^{+}_{\epsilon}(t)\,\text{d}t-\int_{0}^{(1+\epsilon)^{-1}T}t^{n-1}\psi^{-}_{\epsilon}(t)\,\text{d}t

\displaystyle=\left((1+\epsilon)^{2n}-(1-\epsilon)^{2n}\right)\int_{0}^{T}t^{n-1}\psi(t)^{n}\,\text{d}t\ll_{Q,n}\epsilon|B|.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}{\psi}(t)=0

. We can thus apply

5.7
(with

), the relation (
5.3
) and the fact that the right
\tilde{\tau}^{-1}
-multiplication map from
S^{n}
to
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
sends a full
\sigma_{n}
-measure set in
S^{n}
to a full
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
-measure set in
S_{\mathcal{Q}}
to get for
\sigma_{\mathcal{Q}}
-a.e.

and for all sufficiently large

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q},\psi}(\bm{x},T)=|E_{\psi,T}|+O_{Q}\left(|E_{\psi,T}|^{\frac{n+3}{n+4}}\log(|E_{\psi,T}|)\right).
