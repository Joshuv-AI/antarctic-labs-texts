Quantum simulations of quantum electrodynamics in Coulomb gauge
Tianyin Li

Abstract
In recent years, the quantum computing method has been used to address the sign problem in traditional Monte Carlo lattice gauge theory (LGT) simulations. We propose that the Coulomb gauge (CG) should be used in quantum simulations of LGT. Since the redundant degrees of freedom of gauge fields can be eliminated in CG, the Hamiltonian in CG does not need to be gauge invariance, allowing the gauge field to be discretized naively. Then the discretized gauge fields and fermion fields should be placed on momentum and position lattices, respectively. Under this scheme, the CG condition and Gauss’s law can be conveniently preserved by solving for the polarization vectors from algebraic equations. Furthermore, we discuss the mapping of gauge fields to qubits and evaluate the associated qubit and gate cost of this framework. We point out that this formalism is efficient for simulating hadron scattering processes on future fault-tolerant quantum computers. Finally, we calculate the vacuum expectation value of the U(1) plaquette operator and the Wilson loop on a classical device to test the performance of our discretization scheme.

H

G\ket{\rm Phys}=0

[G,H]=0

\Lambda

[G,H]=0

The quantum link model preserves Gauss’s law while truncating the electric basis, but this method sacrifices the unitarity of the gauge link. [
73

However, this method has a challenge when considering the theory with dynamical quarks, which is necessary to define the hadron.

In this work, we propose a formalism for simulating LGT on a quantum computer that aims to satisfy all four requirements, at least in the case of Quantum Electrodynamics (QED). At a cost, we need to introduce non-local interaction terms in the Hamiltonian, so more quantum gates are needed when simulating time evolution. Different from K-S formalism, our formalism is based on fixing the Coulomb Gauge (CG). The choice of CG is motivated by its preservation of rotational symmetries, unlike the axial and light-cone gauges. Compared with the maximum tree gauge, CG can handle dynamical fermions naturally. Once the CG is fixed, there are no nontrivial gauge transformations that can simultaneously preserve both the Hamiltonian and the constraints. From this perspective, we can utilize the discrete version of

II
Discretizations of Coulomb gauge QED

Both fermion fields and gauge fields need to be discretized. Here, we give important results of CG QED on the lattice, while the details can be found in the supplementary materials. As shown in Fig.
1
, we put the fermion fields
\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{n})
and gauge fields
a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}
in the lattice of position and momentum space, respectively, where
a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}
is the annihilation operator of photons with given momentum
\mathbf{p}
and porlization
r=\pm
. On the lattice, the gauge potential
\hat{A}_{0}(\mathbf{n})
,
\hat{A}_{i}(\mathbf{n})
and its conjugate momentum
\hat{\Pi}_{\perp,i}
can be represented by:

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{0}(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{J^{0}(\mathbf{m})}{M^{3}\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{i}(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=\mathbf{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}M^{3}}}\sum_{r}\left[\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}+{\rm H.c.}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{\perp i}(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=\mathbf{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}{2M^{3}}}\sum_{r}\left[-i\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}+{\rm H.c.}\right]\,,

(1)

where
J^{\mu}(\mathbf{n})=g\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\gamma^{\mu}\psi(\mathbf{n})
is the electric current,
\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{4}{a^{2}}\sum_{i}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{p^{i}a}{2}\right)}
is the dispersion relation of photon on the lattice with lattice spacing
a
, and
M
is the number of lattice site in each special dimension. On the lattice of momentum space, the momentum
\mathbf{p}
can be evaluated as
\mathbf{p}=(2\pi k_{x}/Ma,2\pi k_{y}/Ma,2\pi k_{z}/Ma)
, with
k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}=0,\pm 1,...,\pm\lfloor M/2\rfloor
. The unitary gauge link, given by
U_{i}(\mathbf{n})=\exp(-igA_i(\mathbf{n}))
, fulfills the requirement 2) in the introduction.
\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})
in the Eq. (
1
), are polarization vectors of photon, which need to be determined by solving:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(e^{ip^{i}a}-1)\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})=0\,,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\hat{\epsilon}^{s}_{i}(\mathbf{p})=\delta_{rs}\,,

\displaystyle\sum_{r}\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{j}(\mathbf{p})=\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}}(e^{-ip^{i}a}-1)(e^{ip^{j}a}-1)\,.

(2)

Once the solution of
\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})
is obtained, Gauss’s law and CG condition will be satisfied automatically. It’s worth noting that both
\hat{A}_{0}
and
\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})
do not depend on the truncation of photon Fock states. This means that all constraints, including Gauss’s law and the CG condition, will be preserved accurately after the digitization of gauge fields, which is important for simulating gauge field theories on quantum computers, and fulfill requirement 4) in our introduction.

After discretizations of fields, the Hamiltonian of CG QED is given by
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{H}_{I}+\hat{H}_{V}+\hat{H}_{M}+\hat{H}_{W}
(we set
a=1
here):

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B}=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=\mathbf{0}}\sum_{r}\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}=\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\sum_{r}\frac{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}}\left[\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}+{\rm H.c.}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{V}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{J^{0}(\mathbf{n})J^{0}(\mathbf{m})}{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{M}=\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{x})\left[-i\gamma^{i}\frac{\psi(\mathbf{n}+\hat{i})-\psi(\mathbf{n}-\hat{i})}{2}+m\bar{\psi}\psi\right]\,,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{W}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}-\frac{w}{2}\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\hat{\Delta}\psi(\mathbf{n})\,,

(3)

where
\hat{H}_{W}
comes from discretizing the fermion field by using the Wilson fermion method, and the parameter
w
needs to satisfy
0<w<1
.

III
Mapping fields to qubits

Both fermion fields
\hat{\psi}(\mathbf{n})
and photon annihilation operators
a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}
need to be mapped to qubits. In Fig. (
1
), we assume
K
qubits are used to represent Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\rm ph}_{\mathbf{p},r}
of photons with momentum
\mathbf{p}
and helicity
r
. Then, the entire Hilbert space of photons can be written as
\mathcal{H}^{\rm ph}=\otimes_{\mathbf{p},r}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{p},r}
, where
\otimes
denotes the direct product. The computational basis of
\mathcal{H}^{\rm ph}_{\mathbf{p},r}
can be written as
\ket{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}\equiv\ket{i_{0}i_{1}...i_{K-1}}_{\mathbf{p},r}
, where
\mathcal{N}
is the decimal numeral corresponding to the binary number
i_{0}i_{1},...,i_{K-1}
, which also can be denoted as
\mathcal{N}(i_{K-1},...,i_{0})
. As shown in Fig.
1
, three lower indices
(J,\mathbf{p},r)\,,J=0,1,...,K-1
are used to label the qubits in the state
\ket{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}
.
a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}
and
a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}
can raise and low the state
\ket{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}

\displaystyle a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}\ket{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}=\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}\ket{\mathcal{N}-1}_{\mathbf{p},r}\,,

\displaystyle a^{\dagger r}_{\mathbf{p}}\ket{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}=\sqrt{\mathcal{N}+1}\ket{\mathcal{N}+1}_{\mathbf{p},r}\,.

(4)

To satisfy the Eq. (
4
) the qubit representations of
a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}
and
a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}
need to be written as:

\displaystyle a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}=\left\{\sum_{J=0}^{K-1}\left[\sigma^{+}_{J,\mathbf{p},r}\left(\prod_{L=0}^{J-1}\sigma^{-}_{L,\mathbf{p},r}\right)\right]\right\}\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}}\,,

\displaystyle a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}=\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}}\sum_{J=0}^{K-1}\left[\sigma^{-}_{J,\mathbf{p},r}\left(\prod_{L=0}^{J-1}\sigma^{+}_{L,\mathbf{p},r}\right)\right]\,.

(5)

where
\sigma^{+}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{1}+i\sigma^{2})
and
\mathcal{\hat{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}
is the particle number operator of the photon with quantum numbers
(\mathbf{p},r)
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}=\sum_{J=0}^{K-1}2^{J}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(I-\sigma^{3}_{J,\mathbf{p},r}\right)\right]\,.

(6)

Here, the Pauli operators
I
,
\sigma^{x}
,
\sigma^{y}
, and
\sigma^{z}
are denoted as
\sigma^{0}
,
\sigma^{1}
,
\sigma^{2}
and
\sigma^{3}
. In Eq. (
5
), the Fock states with photon occupation numbers greater than
\Lambda=2^{K}-1
need to be truncated, as
a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}\ket{\Lambda}=0
due to the finite-dimensional Hilbert space of a quantum computer. The square root of
\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}
is given by:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}}=\sum_{\mu_{0},...,\mu_{K-1}=0,3}f_{\mu_{K-1},...,\mu_{0}}\sigma^{\mu_{K-1}}_{{K-1},\mathbf{p},r}\otimes...\otimes\sigma^{\mu_{0}}_{0,\mathbf{p},r},

(7)

with

\displaystyle f_{\mu_{K-1},...,\mu_{0}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{K}}\sum_{i_{0},...,i_{K-1}=0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathcal{N}(i_{K-1},...,i_{0})}

\displaystyle\times(-1)^{i_{K-1}\mu_{K-1}}\times...\times(-1)^{i_{0}\mu_{0}}\,.

(8)

We point out that Eq. (
7
) and Eq. (
8
) can be generalized to other functions
F(\mathcal{\hat{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r})
of particle-number operator. To achieve this, we only need to replace
\sqrt{\mathcal{N}(i_{K-1},...,i_{0})}
in the Eq. (
8
) by
F(\mathcal{N}(i_{K-1},...,i_{0}))
. The successful mapping of
a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}
to qubits implies that we can effectively handle photons with specific polarization and momentum on quantum computers, thereby satisfying requirement 3) outlined in the introduction.

The fermion fields
\hat{\psi}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{n})
can be mapped to qubits by Jordan-Winger (J-W) transformation
[
74
]
,

\displaystyle\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{n})=

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{l^{\prime}<l}\left(\prod_{\beta=1}^{4}\sigma^{z}_{\mathbf{m}(l^{\prime}),\beta}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\left(\prod_{\beta=1}^{\alpha-1}\sigma^{z}_{\mathbf{n}(l),\beta}\right)\times\sigma^{+}_{\mathbf{n}(l),\alpha}\,,

(9)

where
l
is the path parameter of J-W transformation (see Fig.
1
). We use the lower indices
(\mathbf{n},\alpha)
to label the qubits representing the fermion degrees of freedom. We can see that the J-W transformation is highly non-local. However, the fermion fields always appear as
\bar{\psi}\psi
in the position space. Therefore, put the fermion field on the lattice of position space instead of momentum space can significantly reduce the non-locality of the qubit Hamiltonian. Now, both fermion and gauge fields are mapped to the qubits and the whole Hilbert of QED can be written as
\mathcal{H}=H^{\rm ph}\otimes H^{\rm f}
, where
H^{\rm f}
is the Hilbert of fermions.

IV
Complexity

Let’s begin with the estimation of qubit scaling. Consider a
d
-dimensional spatial lattice system with
M^{d}
lattice sites on
(d+1)
-dimensional space-time. Fermion fields with
N_{s}
spinor components and
N_{f}
flavors are discretized on the lattice system. Then,
N_{f}N_{s}M^{d}
qubits need to be used to encode the fermion fields. For example, the blue lattice in Fig.
1
shows the case with
N_{f}=1
,
N_{s}=4
and
d=3
. Photon fields on this lattice have
d-1
different transverse directions. Therefore,
(d-1)\times\log_{2}\Lambda\times M^{d}
are needed to encode the photon fields. The case of
d-1=2
and
\Lambda=2^{K}-1
can be found in the green lattice of Fig.
1
. The total qubit cost should sum over both the qubit cost of encoding fermion fields and photon fields:

\displaystyle N^{\rm q,F}_{\rm CG}+N^{\rm q,G}_{\rm CG}=[N_{f}N_{s}+(d-1)\log_{2}\Lambda]M^{d}\,,

(10)

where
N^{\rm q,F}_{\rm CG}
and
N^{\rm q,G}_{\rm CG}
are the qubit cost of encoding the fermion fields and gauge fields in our formalism.

In the Eq. (
10
), the cutoff
\Lambda
should be further estimated. Suppose the physics we are concerned about has an energy scale

. If we want to control the Fock state truncation error of the arbitrary state
\ket{\Psi}
under
\varepsilon_{s}

should be no more than

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(d-1)M^{d}}{2\varepsilon_{s}}\left\{\left[\frac{8dg}{(2\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}}M^{\frac{d+3}{2}}+\left(\frac{8d^{2}g^{2}}{\pi^{3}}M^{d+3}\right.\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.\quad+\frac{[3+4d^{2}(d-1)]g^{2}}{8\pi^{3}}M^{2d+3}\right.\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.\quad+\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{3+m^{2}+12mw+36w^{2}}M^{d+1}\right.\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.\quad+\frac{E}{\pi}M\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{2}-1\right\}\equiv\Lambda_{\max}\,.

(11)

The proof of this inequality can be found in the supplementary materials, and the procedure of proof is similar to
[
78
,
79
]
. From Eq. (
11
), we find that
\Lambda
depends on
M
,
g
,
E
,
m
,

, and
\varepsilon_{s}
polynomially, which shows our formalism can reach the continuum limit of QED efficiently as well as satisfy requirement 1). The Eq. (

) shows that the worst scaling behavior depends on

because we consider an arbitrary state

in the Hilbert space. If we consider a specific case, such as the ground state of the Hamiltonian, the truncated state will converge to the untruncated state exponentially (see supplementary materials). To make Eq. (

\displaystyle\Lambda\sim O\left(\frac{g^{2}M^{3d+3}+EM^{d+1}}{\varepsilon_{s}}\right)

(12)

N^{\rm q,G}_{\rm CG}
scales as

\displaystyle N^{\rm q,G}_{\rm CG}

\displaystyle\sim(d-1)M^{d}\left\{\log_{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{s}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\log_{2}\left[O\left(g^{2}M^{3d+3}+EM^{d+1}\right)\right]\right\}\,.

(13)

\displaystyle N^{G,EB}_{\rm CNOT}=0\,.

\displaystyle N^{G,I}_{\rm OQ}=

\displaystyle 4(d-1)M^{2d}\left[d\Lambda(\log_{2}\Lambda)(\log_{2}\Lambda+5)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-4\Lambda\log_{2}\Lambda\right]\,,

\displaystyle N^{G,I}_{\rm CNOT}=

\displaystyle 2(d-1)M^{2d}\left[d\Lambda(\log_{2}\Lambda)(3\log_{2}\Lambda+5)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-4\Lambda\log_{2}\Lambda\right]\,.

\displaystyle N^{G,V}_{\rm OQ}=N_{s}M^{2d}\,,

\displaystyle N^{G,M}_{\rm OQ}(d=2)=72M^{2}\,,

\displaystyle N^{G,M}_{\rm OQ}(d=3)=104M^{3}\,,

\displaystyle N^{G}_{\rm CG,OQ}=

\displaystyle\frac{t^{3/2}}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}\left(N^{G,EB}_{\rm OQ}+N^{G,I}_{\rm OQ}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+N^{G,V}_{\rm OQ}+N^{G,M}_{\rm OQ}+N^{G,W}_{\rm OQ}\right)\,,

\displaystyle N^{G}_{\rm CG,CNOT}=

\displaystyle\frac{t^{3/2}}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}\left(N^{G,EB}_{\rm CNOT}+N^{G,I}_{\rm CNOT}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+N^{G,V}_{\rm CNOT}+N^{G,M}_{\rm CNOT}+N^{G,W}_{\rm CNOT}\right)\,.

(19)

The leading term of

(20)

In the K-S formalism, the leading term of the gate cost for simulating

VI
Summary and outlook

In this study, based on the CG, we propose a new formalism for simulating LGT on a quantum computer, encompassing the discretization of fields and the mapping of these fields to qubits. As a preliminary exploration, we discuss the quantum simulations of QED. We discretize the gauge fields directly in our formalism. This formalism preserves Gauss’s law and the CG condition by solving the lattice photon polarization vectors either numerically or analytically. One significant advantage of this approach is its efficiency in simulating hadron scattering processes on future fault-tolerant quantum computers. We also discuss the complexity of our formalism, including the qubit cost of encoding fields and the gate cost of simulating time evolution. We also investigate the convergence behavior in the limit
\Lambda\to\infty
of our formalism. Compared with K-S formalism, our formalism costs more gates to simulate time evolution. Finally, the results for
\braket{U_{p}}
, the long-time behavior of the Wilson loop, and the static potential
V(R)
validate the rationality of our formalism.

Extending this formalism to non-Abelian cases presents another intriguing area of investigation. The Hamiltonian for lattice SU(N) gauge theory in the CG has been discussed in
[
83
]
. However, unlike the Abelian case, the CG in non-Abelian theories cannot eliminate all residual degrees of freedom due to the presence of Gribov ambiguity
[
84
]
. Consequently, addressing Gribov ambiguity on quantum computers constitutes a primary challenge for extending our formalism to non-Abelian gauge theories. Another challenge involves solving Gauss’s law for non-Abelian gauge theories in the CG framework. Strategies for tackling these two challenges will be explored in our future work.

S1
QED Hamiltonian in Coulomb gauge

In the CG, the QED Hamiltonian is given by
[
3
]

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}x\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Pi}_{\perp}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\times\mathbf{A})^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{A}+\frac{1}{2}J^{0}A^{0}\right]+H_{\mathrm{M}}

\displaystyle\equiv H_{E}+H_{B}+H_{I}+H_{V}+H_{M}\,,

(S1)

with four constraints

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\Pi^{0}=0\,,

\displaystyle\chi_{2}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\partial_{i}\Pi^{i}-J^{0}=0\,,

\displaystyle\chi_{3}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\partial_{i}A^{i}=0\,,

\displaystyle\chi_{4}(\mathbf{x})\equiv\partial_{i}\Pi^{i}-\partial_{i}\partial^{i}A^{0}=0\,,

(S2)

where
\Pi^{i}_{\perp}={\Pi}^{i}(x)-\partial^{i}A^{0}
is the conjugate momentum to the gauge field
A_{i}
,
J^{\mu}=g\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\psi
is the electric current and
H_{M}
is the Hamiltonian of matter field

\displaystyle H_{M}=\int d^{3}x\bar{\psi}(-i\gamma^{i}\partial_{i}+m)\psi\,.

(S3)

Note that all of the residual gauge freedoms are eliminated in the CG. This can be explained as follows: suppose
\partial_{i}A^{i}(x)=0
and there is another potential
A^{\prime\mu}
that also satisfies the CG. Then We have
\partial_{i}A^{\prime i}(x)=\partial_{i}\partial^{i}\eta(x)=0
for all times, where
\eta
is a scalar function. By choosing boundary conditions such that
\eta(x)
vanishes at infinity, it follows that
\eta(x)=0
. Therefore, there are no nontrivial gauge transformations that can simultaneously preserve both the Hamiltonian and the CG condition. Hence, selecting the CG can satisfy requirement 5) in the introduction. This is not the case for the temporal gauge, where it is possible to maintain
A^{0}(x)=0
by transforming
A^{\mu}
with an arbitrary time-independent scalar function
\eta(\mathbf{x})
. In the CG, Gauss’s law is a constraint for
A^{0}
. After solving Gauss’s law,
A^{0}
can be written as

\displaystyle A^{0}(t,\mathbf{x})=\int d^{3}y\frac{J^{0}(t,\mathbf{y})}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}\,.

(S4)

To preserve Gauss’s law and CG condition for all the time,
A^{i}
and their conjugate momenta should satisfy the Dirac bracket quantization relations

\displaystyle{\left[A_{i}(\mathbf{x}),\Pi^{j}_{\perp}(\mathbf{y})\right]=i\delta_{i}^{j}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})+i\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{j}\partial x^{i}}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}\right),}

\displaystyle{\left[A_{i}(\mathbf{x}),A_{j}(\mathbf{y})\right]=\left[\Pi^{i}_{\perp}(\mathbf{x}),\Pi^{j}_{\perp}(\mathbf{y})\right]=0}\,.

(S5)

The fermion fields satisfy the anti-commutation relations

\displaystyle\{\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\psi^{\dagger}_{\beta}(\mathbf{y}))\}=\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\,,

\displaystyle\{\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}),\psi_{\beta}(\mathbf{y})\}=\{\psi^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}),\psi^{\dagger}_{\beta}(\mathbf{y})\}=0

(S6)

In the Schrodinger picture, the
A_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})
and
\Pi_{\perp\mu}(\mathbf{x})
can be expanded as

\displaystyle A_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}\sqrt{2\absolutevalue{\mathbf{p}}}}\sum_{r}\left[\epsilon^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}}+{\rm H.c.}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\perp i}(\mathbf{x})=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sqrt{\frac{\absolutevalue{\mathbf{p}}}{2}}\sum_{r}\left[-i\epsilon^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}}+{\rm H.c.}\right]\,,

(S7)

where
\epsilon^{r}_{\mu}(\mathbf{p}),\,r=\pm 1
are the polarization vectors, which can be written as
\epsilon^{r}(\mathbf{p})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{p})\epsilon^{r}
.
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{p})
is the standard rotation connect the direction of
z
-axis and
\mathbf{p}
.
\epsilon^{\pm}_{\mu}=(0,1/\sqrt{2},\pm i/\sqrt{2},0)
are the standard polarization vectors of the photon. To be consistent with the commutation relations in Eq. (
S5
), the commutation relations between
a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}
and
a^{r\prime}_{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}
should be

\displaystyle[a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}},a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}]=\delta_{rs}\delta_{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}\mathbf{p}}\,,

\displaystyle[a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}},a^{s}_{\mathbf{p}}]=[a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}},a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}]=0\,.

(S8)

It is not hard to check that the mode expansion in Eq. (
S7
) satisfies the quantization condition Eq. (
S5
) and the CG condition.

S2
Discretizations of Coulomb gauge QED

Both gauge fields and fermion fields need to be discretized. The discretization of the gauge field will be discussed first. Because there are no residual gauge degrees of freedom in the CG, we can discrete the gauge fields
A_{i}(\mathbf{x})
and their conjugate momentum
\Pi^{i}(\mathbf{x})
directly

\displaystyle\hat{\partial}^{R}_{i}\hat{A}^{j}(\mathbf{n})\equiv\frac{1}{a}[\hat{A}^{j}(\mathbf{n}+a\hat{i})-\hat{A}^{j}(\mathbf{n})]\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\partial}^{R}_{i}\hat{\Pi}^{j}(\mathbf{n})=\frac{1}{a}[\hat{\Pi}^{j}(\mathbf{n}+a\hat{i})-\hat{\Pi}^{j}(\mathbf{n})]\,,

(S9)

where
\hat{i}
is the unit vector along the
i
direction,
a
is the lattice spacing,
\hat{A}
is the discrete version of gauge field
A
, and the space point can be written as
\mathbf{n}=(n_{x},n_{y},n_{z}),\,n_{x},n_{y},n_{z}=0,1...,M-1
, so there are
M^{d}
lattice sites in
d
-dimensional space. The Laplace operator
\Delta=-\partial_{i}\partial^{i}
appears in the Hamiltonian of CG QED. The discretized version of Laplace operator
\hat{\Delta}
is

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}\hat{A}_{j}(\mathbf{n})\equiv\frac{1}{a^{2}}\sum_{i}[\hat{A}_{j}(\mathbf{n}+a\hat{i})-2\hat{A}_{j}(\mathbf{n})+\hat{A}_{j}(\mathbf{n}-a\hat{i})]\,.

(S10)

For a constrained system, the constraints in Eq. (
S2
) should be modified to the lattice version

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{1}(\mathbf{n})\equiv\hat{\Pi}^{0}=0\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{2}(\mathbf{n})\equiv\hat{\partial}^{R}_{i}\hat{\Pi}^{i}-J^{0}=0\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{3}(\mathbf{n})\equiv\hat{\partial}^{R}_{i}\hat{A}^{i}=0\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{4}(\mathbf{n})\equiv\hat{\partial}^{R}_{i}\hat{\Pi}^{i}+\hat{\Delta}\hat{A}^{0}=0\,.

(S11)

The
\hat{A}^{0}
can be obtained by solving
\hat{\Delta}\hat{A}^{0}=-J^{0}

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{0}(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{J^{0}(\mathbf{m})}{M^{3}\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\,.

(S12)

We sum over the non-vanishing momentum mode because the photon with
\mathbf{p}=0
is non-physical. The
\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}
is the lattice dispersion relation of photons, which can be obtained by plugging the plane wave solutions into the equation of motion
[(\partial_{0})^{2}-\hat{\Delta}]\hat{A}_{j}=0
,

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{4}{a^{2}}\sum_{i}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{p^{i}a}{2}\right)}\,.

(S13)

Eq. (
S12
) demonstrates that Gauss’s law with dynamical fermions in QED can be naturally resolved on the lattice, thereby meeting requirement 2) outlined in the introduction. According to the Dirac bracket quantization, the commutation relations in Eq. (
S5
) should also be modified because the constraints change to its lattice version in Eq. (
S11
)

\displaystyle\left[\hat{A}_{i}(\mathbf{n}),\hat{\Pi}^{j}(\mathbf{m})\right]=i\delta_{j}^{i}\delta_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}+\frac{i\sum_{\mathbf{p}}(e^{-ip^{i}a}-1)(e^{ip^{j}a}-1)}{M^{3}a^{2}\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\left[\hat{A}_{i}(\mathbf{n}),\hat{A}_{j}(\mathbf{m})\right]=\left[\hat{\Pi}^{i}(\mathbf{n}),\hat{\Pi}^{j}(\mathbf{m})\right]=0\,.

(S14)

Now, the discrete version of Eq. (
S7
) can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{i}(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=\mathbf{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}M^{3}}}\sum_{r}\left[\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}+{\rm H.c.}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}_{\perp i}(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=\mathbf{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}{2M^{3}}}\sum_{r}\left[-i\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}+{\rm H.c.}\right]\,,

(S15)

where
\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}
are the lattice polarization vectors, which need to be determined later. Eq. (
S15
) shows that
\hat{A}_{i}(\mathbf{n})
are Hermitian operators, enabling us to express unitary Wilsons line as
U_{i}(\mathbf{n})=\exp(-iga\hat{A}_i(\mathbf{n}))
, thereby fulfilling requirement 3) outlined in the introduction. On the lattice of momentum space, the momentum
\mathbf{p}
can be evaluated as
\mathbf{p}=(2\pi k_{x}/Ma,2\pi k_{y}/Ma,2\pi k_{z}/Ma)
, with
k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}=0,\pm 1,...,\pm\lfloor M/2\rfloor
. To preserve the lattice CG condition, Gauss’s law and the lattice Dirac bracket quantization conditions in Eq. (
S14
), the lattice polarization vectors
\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}
should satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(e^{ip^{i}a}-1)\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})=0\,,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\hat{\epsilon}^{s}_{i}(\mathbf{p})=\delta_{rs}\,,

\displaystyle\sum_{r}\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{j}(\mathbf{p})=\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}}(e^{-ip^{i}a}-1)(e^{ip^{j}a}-1)\,.

(S16)

So,
\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}
can be obtained by solving Eq. (
S16
) numerically or analytically.

It’s worth noting that both the solution of
\hat{A}_{0}
in Eq. (
S12
) and the solution of
\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})
in Eq. (
S16
) do not depend on the truncation of photon Fock states. This means four constraints in Eq. (
S11
) will be preserved accurately after the digitization of gauge fields, which is important for simulating gauge field theories on quantum computers.

For the fermion fields, they can be discretized by various methods, here, we use Wilson fermion. Fermion fields can be discredited naively in this discretization scheme, but one needs to add a Wilson term
\hat{H}_{W}
into the Hamiltonian. After doing so, the
\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B}
,
\hat{H}_{I}
,
\hat{H}_{V}
,
\hat{H}_{M}
and
\hat{H}_{W}
can be written as (we set
a=1
here)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B}=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=\mathbf{0}}\sum_{r}\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}=\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\sum_{r}\frac{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}}\left[\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}+{\rm H.c.}\right]\,,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{V}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{J^{0}(\mathbf{n})J^{0}(\mathbf{m})}{M^{3}\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{M}=\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\left[-i\gamma^{i}\frac{\psi(\mathbf{n}+\hat{i})-\psi(\mathbf{n}-\hat{i})}{2}+m\bar{\psi}\psi\right]\,,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{W}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}-\frac{w}{2}\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\hat{\Delta}\psi(\mathbf{n})\,,

(S17)

where
0<w<1
is the coefficient of the Wilson term. Finally, the discretized CG QED Hamiltonian is
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{H}_{I}+\hat{H}_{V}+\hat{H}_{M}+\hat{H}_{W}
.

S3
Estimation of the truncation error

In this section, we will estimate the error associated with truncating the photon Fock state.

S3.1
A toy model

We first consider a toy model

\displaystyle H_{\rm toy}=\omega(a^{\dagger}a)+g(a+a^{\dagger})\,,

(S18)

with the commutation relation
[a,a^{\dagger}]=1
. The eigenstate of occupation operator
\hat{\mathcal{N}}=a^{\dagger}a
needs to be truncated:
a^{\dagger}\ket{\mathcal{N}=\Lambda-1}=0
. Now, the problem is: if there is a state
\ket{\psi}
and the vacuum state
\ket{\Omega}
, which has
E=\bra{\psi}H\ket{\psi}-\braket{\Omega|H|\Omega}
, and we want the error of the truncated state
\ket{\psi(\Lambda)}
below
\varepsilon
. Then, how can we choose the truncation
\Lambda
? This is equivalent to letting the probability
P\left({\hat{\mathcal{N}}}>{\Lambda-1}\right)
smaller than
\varepsilon
when measure the operator
\hat{\mathcal{N}}
in the quantum state
\psi
. To solve this problem, we first define a new creation and annihilation operator
\bar{a}
and
\bar{a}^{\dagger}
, then calculate the
\braket{\Omega|H|\Omega}
:

\displaystyle\bar{a}=a+\frac{g}{\omega}\,,\ \bar{a}^{\dagger}=a^{\dagger}+\frac{g}{\omega}\,.

(S19)

The new creation and annihilation operators also satisfy the commutation relation
[\bar{a},\bar{a}^{\dagger}]=1
. Using those operators, the Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

\displaystyle H_{\rm toy}=\omega\bar{a}^{\dagger}\bar{a}-\frac{g^{2}}{\omega}.

(S20)

From the above equation, we can solve the vacuum energy of
H_{\rm toy}
:

\displaystyle\braket{\Omega|H|\Omega}=E_{0}=-\frac{g^{2}}{\omega}\,.

(S21)

We then have
\braket{\psi|H_{\rm toy}|\psi}=E-g^{2}/\omega
.

Next, we need to estimate the upper bound of the expectation value
|\braket{\psi|a^{\dagger}+a|\psi}|
. To achieve this, we need to define the operators
X
and
P
:

\displaystyle X=\frac{a+a^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,\ P=\frac{a-a^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}i}\,.

(S22)

From the above equations, we can find that

\displaystyle X^{2}=2\hat{\mathcal{N}}-P^{2}+1\,.

(S23)

Then we have the following inequality:

\displaystyle|\braket{a+a^{\dagger}}|\leq\sqrt{\braket{(a+a^{\dagger})^{2}}}=\sqrt{\braket{2X^{2}}}=\sqrt{2\braket{2\hat{\mathcal{N}}-P^{2}+1}}\leq\sqrt{\braket{4\hat{\mathcal{N}}+2}}\,,

(S24)

where
\braket{...}
represents the expectation value
\braket{\psi|...|\psi}
. From the
\braket{H_{\rm toy}}=E-g^{2}/\omega
and the Eq. (
S24
), we can obtain:

\displaystyle E-g^{2}/\omega\geq\omega\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}}-|g\braket{a+a^{\dagger}}|\geq\omega\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}}-|g|\sqrt{4\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}}+2}.

(S25)

Then, we can solve the upper bound of the
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}}
by solving the above inequation:

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}}\leq\left(\frac{|g|}{\omega}+\sqrt{\frac{E}{\omega}+\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\,.

(S26)

The above equation gives the correct limit in
E=0
and
g=0
. Once known the
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}}
, we can obtain the probability
P(\hat{\mathcal{N}}>\Lambda)=\sum_{\mathcal{N}=\Lambda}^{\infty}|\braket{\psi|\mathcal{N}}|^{2}
by using the Markov’s inequality:

\displaystyle P(Y\geq\Lambda)\leq\frac{\braket{Y}}{\Lambda}\,,

(S27)

where
Y
is an arbitrary not-negative random variable. In our case, we have
Y=\hat{\mathcal{N}}
, so:

\displaystyle P(\hat{\mathcal{N}}\geq\Lambda)\leq\frac{\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}}}{\Lambda}\leq\frac{1}{\Lambda}\left[\left(\frac{|g|}{\omega}+\sqrt{\frac{E}{\omega}+\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right]=\varepsilon_{s}\,.

(S28)

It can be seen that when
\varepsilon_{s}\to 0
, the amplitude square
\sum_{\mathcal{N}=\Lambda}^{\infty}|\braket{\psi|\mathcal{N}}|^{2}
will tend to zero, then the truncated tends to the untruncated state. The final equal sign in the above equation gives us the bound of truncation
\Lambda
with given precision
\varepsilon_{s}
:

\displaystyle\Lambda=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{s}}\left[\left(\frac{|g|}{\omega}+\sqrt{\frac{E}{\omega}+\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right]\,.

(S29)

We find that as
\varepsilon_{s}\propto 1/\Lambda
in Eq. (
S29
). However, this is the worst case because we consider the most general state
\ket{\psi}
when we deduce Eq. (
S29
). As shown in Fig.
S1
, if we consider the ground and lower excited state of
H_{\rm toy}
,
\varepsilon_{s}
converges exponentially to
0
as
\Lambda
increases.

\log(\varepsilon_s)
dependence of

s=0,1,2

S3.2
QED in CG

Although the toy model discussed in the last subsection is simple, most of the derivation in the toy model can be generalized to CG QED. Suppose the physics we are interested in has energy scale
E=\braket{\Psi|\hat{H}|\Psi}\equiv\braket{\hat{H}}
, then we have:

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{q},s)+\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}=E-\braket{\hat{H}_{V}}-\braket{\hat{H}_{M}+\hat{H}_{W}}-\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=\mathbf{q}\,{\rm and}\,r\not=s}\braket{\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{p},r)+\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{p},r)}\,,

(S30)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{p},r)\equiv\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{p},r)\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\frac{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{d}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}}\left[\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}+{\rm H.c.}}\right]\,.

(S31)

According to the discussions of the toy model, we need to estimate the upper bound of
|\braket{\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}|
to obtain the upper bound of the
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}}
. To achieve this, we need to calculate the lower bound of
\braket{\hat{H}_{V}}
,
\braket{\hat{H}_{M}+\hat{H}_{W}}
, and every
\braket{\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{p},r)+\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{p},r)}
.

Firstly, we estimate the lower bound of
\hat{H}_{V}
.
J^{0}(\mathbf{n})
is the only operator appearing in
\hat{H}_{V}
. At each space point, the charge density must be equal to or larger than
g
, so the operator
J^{0}(\mathbf{n})+g
must be positive definite:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{\braket{[J^{0}(\mathbf{n})+g][J^{0}(\mathbf{m})+g]}}{M^{d}\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}=\braket{\hat{H}_{V}}+\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{g\braket{J^{0}(\mathbf{n})}}{M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}+\frac{g^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}}{M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}\geq 0\,.

(S32)

Then, we have

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{H}_{V}}

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{g\left|\braket{J^{0}(\mathbf{n})}\right|}{M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}\left|e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\right|-\frac{g^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{\left|e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\right|}{M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{g\left|\braket{J^{0}(\mathbf{n})}\right|}{M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}-\frac{g^{2}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{1}{M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{g^{2}M^{d}}{\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}-\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{g^{2}M^{d}}{2\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{3g^{2}M^{d+2}}{8\pi^{2}}\approx-\frac{3g^{2}M^{2d+2}}{8\pi^{2}}\,.

(S33)

In the second line of the above inequality, we use
|e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}|\leq 1
. The third line is because
\braket{J^{0}(\mathbf{n})}\leq g
. The forth line is because
\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}=2\sqrt{\sum_{i}\sin^{2}(2\pi k_{i}/2M)}\geq 2\sqrt{\sin^{2}(\pi/M)}\approx 2\pi/M
.

The upper bound of
\braket{\hat{H}_{M}+\hat{H}_{W}}
is given in
[
79
]
, which is

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{H}_{M}+\hat{H}_{W}}\geq-2M^{d}\sqrt{3+m^{2}+12mw+36w^{2}}\,.

(S34)

The upper bound of every
\braket{\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{p},r)+\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{p},r)}
also needs to be estimated. As we have done in the toy model case, we need to define a new annihilation operator
\bar{a}^{r,i}_{\mathbf{p}}
:

\displaystyle\bar{a}^{r,i}_{\mathbf{p}}=a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}+\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\frac{dJ^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{\sqrt{2}M^{\frac{d}{2}}\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}^{\frac{3}{2}}}\hat{\epsilon}^{r*}_{i}(\mathbf{p})e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{m}}\,.

(S35)

It is worth noting that the index
i
has not been contracted in the definition of
\bar{a}^{r,i}_{\mathbf{p}}
. It is not so hard to check the operator
\bar{a}^{r,i}_{\mathbf{p}}
and
\bar{a}^{r,i\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}
satisfy:

\displaystyle[\bar{a}^{r,i}_{\mathbf{p}},\bar{a}^{s,i\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}}]=[a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}},a^{s}_{\mathbf{q}}]=\delta^{rs}\delta_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\,.

(S36)

Using the operator
\bar{a}^{r,i}_{\mathbf{p}}
, the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{p},r)+\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{p},r)
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{p},r)+\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{p},r)=\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}{d}\bar{a}^{r,i\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}\bar{a}^{r,i}_{\mathbf{p}}-\sum_{i}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\frac{dJ^{i}(\mathbf{n})J^{i}(\mathbf{m})}{2M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\hat{\epsilon}^{r*}_{i}(\mathbf{p})e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\,.

(S37)

From Eq. (
S36
), we know that the first term on the right hand side of Eq. (
S37
) is positive definite, so that we can find the following lower bound:

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{p},r)+\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{p},r)}

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{i}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\frac{d\left|\braket{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})J^{i}(\mathbf{m})}\right|}{2M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\hat{\epsilon}^{r*}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\left|e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\right|

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{i}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\frac{d\left|\braket{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})J^{i}(\mathbf{m})}\right|}{2M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}\left|e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\right|

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{i}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\frac{d\left|\braket{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})J^{i}(\mathbf{m})}\right|}{2M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{i}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\frac{2dg^{2}}{M^{d}\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}=-\frac{2d^{2}M^{d}g^{2}}{\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}\,.

(S38)

The second line of this inequality is due to
\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\hat{\epsilon}^{r*}_{i}(\mathbf{p})\leq 1
, which comes from the normalization of polarization vectors. In the third line, we use the fact that
|e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}|\leq 1
. From Eq. (
S61
), we can find that the upper bound of
|\braket{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})J^{i}(\mathbf{m})}|
is
4g^{2}
, and then we can obtain the fourth line of the inequality.

Now, the upper bound of
\braket{\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{q},s)+\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}
can be obtained by combining the Eq. (
S30
), Eq. (
S33
), Eq. (
S34
), and Eq. (
S38
):

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{q},s)+\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}

\displaystyle\leq E+\frac{3g^{2}M^{2d+2}}{8\pi^{2}}+2M^{d}\sqrt{3+m^{2}+12mw+36w^{2}}+\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=\mathbf{q}\ {\rm and}\ r\not=s}\frac{2d^{2}g^{2}M^{d}}{\hat{E}^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}}

\displaystyle\leq E+\frac{3g^{2}M^{2d+2}}{8\pi^{2}}+2M^{d}\sqrt{3+m^{2}+12mw+36w^{2}}+\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=\mathbf{q}\ {\rm and}\ r\not=s}\frac{d^{2}g^{2}M^{d+2}}{2\pi^{2}}

\displaystyle<E+\frac{[3+4d^{2}(d-1)]g^{2}M^{2d+2}}{8\pi^{2}}+2M^{d}\sqrt{3+m^{2}+12mw+36w^{2}}\,.

(S39)

On the other hand, we have:

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{H}_{EB}(\mathbf{q},s)+\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}=\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}}+\braket{\hat{H}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}\geq\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}}-|\braket{\hat{H}^{S}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}|-|\braket{\hat{H}^{A}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}|\,,

(S40)

where
\hat{H_{I}}(\mathbf{q},s)=\hat{H}^{S}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)+\hat{H}^{A}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)
and the definition of
\hat{H}^{S}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)
and
\hat{H}^{A}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)
are:

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{S}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)=\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\frac{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{d}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}}\left[{\rm Re}(\hat{\epsilon}^{s}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\cos(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})-{\rm Im}(\hat{\epsilon}^{s}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\sin(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})\right](a^{s}_{\mathbf{q}}+a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}})\,,

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{A}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)=i\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\frac{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{d}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}}\left[{\rm Re}(\hat{\epsilon}^{s}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\sin(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})+{\rm Im}(\hat{\epsilon}^{s}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\cos(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})\right](a^{s}_{\mathbf{q}}-a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}})\,.

(S41)

Then, we need to represent the upper bound of
|\braket{\hat{H}^{S(A)}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}|
as the function of
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}}
. The key point of this step is using the following inequality:

\displaystyle|\braket{\psi|O_{A}O_{B}|\psi}|\leq\sqrt{\braket{\psi|O^{2}_{A}|\psi}\braket{\psi|O^{2}_{B}|\psi}}\,,

(S42)

where
O_{A}
and
O_{B}
are Hermitian operators and
\ket{\psi}
is an arbitrary state. This inequality can be proven by using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle|\braket{\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}}|\leq\sqrt{\braket{\psi_{1}|\psi_{1}}\braket{\psi_{2}|\psi_{2}}}\,.

(S43)

Replacing
\ket{\psi_{1}}
by
\ket{O_{A}\psi}
and
\ket{\psi_{2}}
by
\ket{O_{B}\psi}
in the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we can obtain Eq. (
S42
) immediately. Let
O_{A}=\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\frac{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{d}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}}\left[{\rm Re}(\hat{\epsilon}^{s}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\cos(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})-{\rm Im}(\hat{\epsilon}^{s}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\sin(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})\right]
and
O_{B}=a^{s}_{\mathbf{p}}+a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}
, the upper bound of
|\braket{\hat{H}^{S}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}|
can be written as

\displaystyle|\braket{\hat{H}^{S}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\left\langle\left\{\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\frac{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{d}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}}}\left[{\rm Re}(\hat{\epsilon}^{s}_{i}(\mathbf{q}))\cos(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{n})-{\rm Im}(\hat{\epsilon}^{s}_{i}(\mathbf{q}))\sin(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{n})\right]\right\}^{2}\right\rangle\braket{(a^{s}_{\mathbf{q}}+a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}})^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4dgM^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}}}\sqrt{\braket{(a^{s}_{\mathbf{q}}+a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}})^{2}}}\leq\frac{4dgM^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\sqrt{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}}}\sqrt{2\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}}+1}\,.

(S44)

Similarly, we can compute the upper bound of
\braket{\hat{H}^{A}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}
, and the result is the same:

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{H}^{A}_{I}(\mathbf{q},s)}\leq\frac{4dgM^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\sqrt{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}}}\sqrt{2\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}}+1}\,.

(S45)

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},s}}-\frac{8dgM^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\sqrt{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}}}\sqrt{2\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}}+1}<E+\frac{[3+4d^{2}(d-1)]g^{2}M^{2d+2}}{8\pi^{2}}+2M^{d}\sqrt{3+m^{2}+12mw+36w^{2}}\,.

(S46)

From the above inequality, we can solve the upper bound of
\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}}
:

\displaystyle\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\{\left[\frac{8dgM^{d/2}}{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}^{3/2}}+\left(\frac{64d^{2}g^{2}M^{d}}{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}^{3}}+\frac{[3+4d^{2}(d-1)]g^{2}M^{2d+2}}{4\pi^{2}\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}}\right.\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.\quad+\frac{4M^{d}}{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}}\sqrt{3+m^{2}+12mw+36w^{2}}+\frac{2E}{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{q}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{2}-1\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\{\left[\frac{8dg}{(2\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}}M^{\frac{d+3}{2}}+\left(\frac{8d^{2}g^{2}}{\pi^{3}}M^{d+3}+\frac{[3+4d^{2}(d-1)]g^{2}}{8\pi^{3}}M^{2d+3}\right.\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.\quad+\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{3+m^{2}+12mw+36w^{2}}M^{d+1}+\frac{E}{\pi}M\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{2}-1\right\}\equiv\max(\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}})\,.

(S47)

Using Markov’s inequality in Eq. (
S27
), we find that:

\displaystyle P(\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}\geq\Lambda)\leq\frac{\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}}}{\Lambda}\leq\frac{\max(\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}})}{\Lambda}\,.

(S48)

We have
(d-1)M^{d}
different operators
\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}
, so the
\max(\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}})/\Lambda
should satisfy
\max(\braket{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{q},s}})/\Lambda=\varepsilon_{s}/[(d-1)M^{3}]
to make the total error bounded by
\varepsilon_{s}
. This gives us the final upper bound of the cutoff
\Lambda
:

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(d-1)M^{d}}{2\varepsilon_{s}}\left\{\left[\frac{8dg}{(2\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}}M^{\frac{d+3}{2}}+\left(\frac{8d^{2}g^{2}}{\pi^{3}}M^{d+3}+\frac{[3+4d^{2}(d-1)]g^{2}}{8\pi^{3}}M^{2d+3}\right.\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.\quad+\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{3+m^{2}+12mw+36w^{2}}M^{d+1}+\frac{E}{\pi}M\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{2}-1\right\}\equiv\Lambda_{\max}\,.

(S49)

Therefore, encoding the photon with given momentum
\mathbf{p}
and polarization
r
needs no more than
\log_{2}\Lambda_{\max}
qubits. The total cost of qubits for encoding the gauge field is given by:

\displaystyle N^{\rm q,G}_{\rm CG}=(d-1)M^{d}\log_{2}\Lambda_{\rm max}\,.

(S50)

S4
Gate cost estimation of time evolution of CG QED

The Hamiltonian
H
of an
N_{q}
qubit system can be represented by the linear combination of Pauli strings
\sigma^{\mu_{N_{q}-1}}_{N_{q}-1}\otimes...\otimes\sigma^{\mu_{0}}_{0}
:

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\mu_{0},...,\mu_{N_{q}-1}=0}^{3}C_{\mu_{N_{q}-1},...,\mu_{0}}\sigma^{\mu_{N_{q}-1}}_{N_{q}-1}\otimes...\otimes\sigma^{\mu_{0}}_{0}\equiv\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\mathcal{M}_{G}}h_{\alpha}\,,

(S51)

where
\mathcal{M}_{G}
is the number of non-vanishing terms inside the summation
\sum_{\mu_{0},...,\mu_{N_{q}-1}}
. According to the second-order Suzuki formula, the time evolution operator
e^{-iHt}
can be approximated by:

\displaystyle e^{-iHt}

\displaystyle\approx\left[\prod_{\alpha=\mathcal{M}_{G}}^{1}\exp\left(-ih_{\alpha}\frac{t}{2N_{T}}\right)\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\mathcal{M}_{G}}\exp\left(-ih_{\alpha}\frac{t}{2N_{T}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t^{3}}{N_{T}^{3}}\right)\right]^{N_{T}}

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{\alpha=\mathcal{M}_{G}}^{1}\exp\left(-ih_{\alpha}\frac{\delta t}{2}\right)\prod_{\alpha=1}^{\mathcal{M}_{G}}\exp\left(-ih_{\alpha}\frac{\delta t}{2}\right)\right]^{N_{T}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t^{3}}{N_{T}^{2}}\right).

(S52)

We have
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}(t^{3}/N_{T}^{2})
for a given precision
\varepsilon
, so we can obtain
N_{T}=t^{3/2}/\varepsilon^{1/2}
. The gate cost of simulating
\exp(-i h_\alpha\delta t/2)
is well known. For example, the quantum circuit in Fig.
S2
can be used to simulate the evolution operator
e^{-ih_{\alpha}\delta t/2}
, with
h_{\alpha}=C_{\alpha}\sigma^{3}_{{N_{q}}-1}...\sigma^{3}_{2}\sigma^{2}_{1}\sigma^{1}_{0}
. This means that as long as we decompose the Hamiltonian into the form of Eq. (
S51
), we can estimate the number of quantum gates required to simulate the time evolution operator. In the main text, we already know that the Hamiltonian of CG QED is given by
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{H}_{I}+\hat{H}_{V}+\hat{H}_{M}+\hat{H}_{W}
. Next, we will give the specific expressions of the Hamiltonian after each term is decomposed into Eq. (
S51
) form.

Figure S2:
Quantum circuit of simulating
e^{-ih_{\alpha}\delta t/2}
, with
h_{\alpha}=C_{\alpha}\sigma^{3}_{{N_{q}}-1}...\sigma^{3}_{2}\sigma^{2}_{1}\sigma^{1}_{0}
. Here, we set the parameter
\theta=\delta tC_{\alpha}
. Analogous circuits can represent other evolution operators generated by
h_{\alpha}
.

In the main text, the
\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B}=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\sum_{r}\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}a^{r\dagger}_{\mathbf{p}}a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\sum_{r}\sum_{J=0}^{K-1}2^{J}\left[\frac{1}{2}(I-\sigma^{3}_{J,\mathbf{p},r})\right]\,.

(S53)

After dropping the identity operator, the time evolution operator
e^{-i(\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B})\delta t/2}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle e^{-i(\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B})\delta t/2}=\prod_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\prod_{r}\prod_{J=0}^{K-1}\exp\left(i2^{J-1}\sigma^{3}_{J,\mathbf{p},r}\delta t\right)\,,

(S54)

which is nothing but the
R_{z}
gate on a quantum computer. For
(d+1)
-dimensional space-time,
\prod_{\mathbf{p}}
and
\prod_{r}
have
M^{d}
and
d-1
different terms. Therefore, we need
N^{G,EB}_{\rm OQ}=(d-1)M^{d}K\approx(d-1)M^{d}\log_{2}\Lambda
one-qubit gates to simulate
e^{-i(\hat{H}_{E}+\hat{H}_{B})\delta t/2}
. We use
\approx
here because
\Lambda=2^{K}-1\approx 2^{K}
when
2^{K}\gg 1
.

e^{-i\hat{H}_{I}\delta t/2}
is the most complex part in the time evolution operator. In the main text, the
\hat{H}_{I}
is given by:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}=\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\sum_{r}\frac{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}}\left[\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p})a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n}}+{\rm H.c.}\right]\,,

(S55)

where the qubit form of
a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}
and
\sqrt{\hat{N}_{\mathbf{p},r}}
are given by

\displaystyle a^{r}_{\mathbf{p}}=\left\{\sum_{J=0}^{K-1}\left[\sigma^{+}_{J,\mathbf{p},r}\left(\prod_{L=0}^{J-1}\sigma^{-}_{L,\mathbf{p},r}\right)\right]\right\}\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}}\,,

(S56)

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{p},r}}=\sum_{\mu_{0},...,\mu_{K-1}=0,3}f_{\mu_{K-1},...,\mu_{0}}\sigma^{\mu_{K-1}}_{{K-1},\mathbf{p},r}\otimes...\otimes\sigma^{\mu_{0}}_{0,\mathbf{p},r}\,.

(S57)

The expression of coefficients
f_{\mu_{K-1},...,\mu_{0}}
can be found in the main text. Putting Eq. (
S56
) and Eq. (
S57
) into Eq. (
S55
), we have

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\sum_{r}\sum_{L=0}^{K-1}\sum_{\mu_{L+1},...,\mu_{K-1}=0,3}\frac{J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}}

\displaystyle\times\left\{\left[\real(\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\cos(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})-\imaginary(\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\sin(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})\right]\mathcal{F}_{\mu_{K-1},...,\mu_{L+1}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\sigma^{\mu_{K-1}}_{K-1,\mathbf{p},r}...\sigma^{\mu_{L+1}}_{L+1,\mathbf{p},r}(\sigma^{+}_{L,\mathbf{p},r}\sigma^{-}_{L-1,,\mathbf{p},r}...\sigma^{-}_{0,\mathbf{p},r}+{\rm H.c.})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+{\rm i}\left[\real(\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\sin(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})+\imaginary(\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\cos(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})\right]\mathcal{F}_{\mu_{K-1},...,\mu_{L+1}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\times\sigma^{\mu_{K-1}}_{K-1,\mathbf{p},r}...\sigma^{\mu_{L+1}}_{L+1,\mathbf{p},r}(\sigma^{+}_{L,\mathbf{p},r}\sigma^{-}_{L-1,\mathbf{p},r}...\sigma^{-}_{0,\mathbf{p},r}-{\rm H.c.})\right\}

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mu_{K-1},...,\mu_{L+1}}=\sum_{\mu_{0},...,\mu_{L}=0,3}(-1)^{\mu_{L}}f_{\mu_{K-1},...,\mu_{0}}
. From the above equation, we find that the
\hat{H}_{I}
needs to divide into two parts,
\hat{H}_{I}=\hat{H}^{S}_{I}+\hat{H}^{A}_{I}\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\sum_{r}[\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{S}_{I}(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p},i,r)+\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{A}_{I}(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p},i,r)]
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{S}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\sum_{r}\sum_{L=0}^{K-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu_{0},...,\mu_{L}=1,2\\
\mu_{0}+...+\mu_{L}-L-1={\rm even}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mu_{L+1},...,\mu_{K-1}=0,3}\frac{2^{-L}J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\real(\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\cos(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})-\imaginary(\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\sin(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})\right]\mathcal{F}_{\mu_{K-1},...,\mu_{L+1}}\mathcal{G}^{S}_{\mu_{L},...,\mu_{0}}\sigma^{\mu_{K-1}}_{K-1,\mathbf{p},r}...\sigma^{\mu_{L+1}}_{L+1,\mathbf{p},r}\sigma^{\mu_{L}}_{L,\mathbf{p},r}...\sigma^{\mu_{0}}_{0,\mathbf{p},r}\,,

(S59)

where
\mathcal{G}^{S}_{\mu_{L},...,\mu_{0}}=(-1)^{\mu_{L}-1}(-1)^{\left(-L-1+\sum_{J=0}^{L}\mu_{J}\right)/2}
, and the
\hat{H}^{A}_{I}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{A}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{n},i}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\sum_{r}\sum_{L=0}^{K-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu_{0},...,\mu_{L}=1,2\\
\mu_{0}+...+\mu_{L}-L-1={\rm odd}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mu_{L+1},...,\mu_{K-1}=0,3}\frac{2^{-L}J^{i}(\mathbf{n})}{M^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\real(\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\sin(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})+\imaginary(\hat{\epsilon}^{r}_{i}(\mathbf{p}))\cos(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{n})\right]\mathcal{F}_{\mu_{K-1},...,\mu_{L+1}}\mathcal{G}^{A}_{\mu_{L},...,\mu_{0}}\sigma^{\mu_{K-1}}_{K-1,\mathbf{p},r}...\sigma^{\mu_{L+1}}_{L+1,\mathbf{p},r}\sigma^{\mu_{L}}_{L,\mathbf{p},r}...\sigma^{\mu_{0}}_{0,\mathbf{p},r}\,,

(S60)

where
\mathcal{G}^{A}_{\mu_{L},...,\mu_{0}}=(-1)^{\mu_{L}-1}(-1)^{\left(-L-2+\sum_{J=0}^{L}\mu_{J}\right)/2}
. The electric current operator
J^{i}(\mathbf{n})=g\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\gamma^{i}\psi(\mathbf{n})
in Eq. (
S59
) and Eq. (
S60
) can be mapped to qubits by Jordan-Wigner transformation:

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\gamma^{1}\psi(\mathbf{n})=\frac{g}{2}\sum_{h=0,2}(-1)^{\frac{h}{2}}\left(\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},h+1}\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},h+2}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},h+1}\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},h+2}\right)\,

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\gamma^{2}\psi(\mathbf{n})=-\frac{g}{2}\sum_{h=0,2}(-1)^{\frac{h}{2}}\left(\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},h+1}\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},h+2}-\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},h+1}\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},h+2}\right)\,

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\gamma^{3}\psi(\mathbf{n})=\frac{g}{2}\sum_{h=0,2}(-1)^{\frac{h}{2}}\left(\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},h+1}-\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},h+2}\right)\,,

(S61)

where
h=0
and
h=2
come from the contribution of left and right-handed fermion fields, respectively. In the case of
d=2,3
, the Dirac gamma matrices are given by:

\displaystyle\gamma^{0}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&I\\
I&0\end{array}\right]\,,\ \gamma^{i}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\sigma^{i}\\
-\sigma^{i}&0\end{array}\right]\,.

Now, we are ready to estimate the gate cost to simulate the evolution operator
e^{-i(\hat{H}^{S}_{I}+\hat{H}^{A}_{I})\delta t/2}\approx e^{-i\hat{H}^{S}_{I}\delta t/2}e^{-i\hat{H}^{A}_{I}\delta t/2}
. To achieve this, we need to calculate the length of the qubit string in
\hat{H}^{S}_{I}
and
\hat{H}^{A}_{I}
. The evolution operator generated by length
L
Pauli string needs to be represented by
2(L-1)
CNOT gates in the ideal case. The lengths of Pauli string
J^{i}(\mathbf{n})\sigma^{\mu_{L}}_{L}...\sigma^{\mu_{0}}_{0}
are
L+3
for
i=1,2
and
L+2
for
i=3
. However, the length of
\sigma^{\mu_{K-1}}_{K-1}...\sigma^{\mu_{L+1}}_{L+1}
needs to be treated more carefully because the identity operator will not increase the length of the Pauli string. There are
C^{I}_{K-L-1}
terms that have total Pauli string length
L+3+I
in the summation
\sum_{\mu_{L+1},...,\mu_{K-1}}
, while
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu_{0},...,\mu_{L}=1,2\\
\mu_{0}+...+\mu_{L}-L-1={\rm even(odd)}\end{subarray}}
contribute
2^{L}
terms. Therefore, the CNOT gate cost of simulating the evolution
\exp\left[-i\left(\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{S}_{I}(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p},i,r)+\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{A}_{I}(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{p},i,r)\right)\delta t/2\right]
is:

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{L=0}^{K-1}\sum_{I=0}^{K-L-1}2(L+2+I)C^{I}_{K-L-1}2^{L}\right]\times 2^{3}=K(3K+5)2^{K+1}\approx 2\Lambda(\log_{2}\Lambda)(3\log_{2}\Lambda+5)\ {\rm if}\ i=1,2\,,

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{L=0}^{K-1}\sum_{I=0}^{K-L-1}2(L+1+I)C^{I}_{K-L-1}2^{L}\right]\times 2^{3}\approx 2\Lambda(\log_{2}\Lambda)(3\log_{2}\Lambda+1)\ {\rm if}\ i=3\,.

(S66)

The Eq. (
S66
) dose not depend on
\mathbf{n}
,
\mathbf{p}
, and
r
, so the total CNOT gate cost of simulating
e^{-i\hat{H}_{I}\delta t/2}
is

\displaystyle N^{G,I}_{\rm CNOT}=2(d-1)M^{2d}\left[d\Lambda(\log_{2}\Lambda)(3\log_{2}\Lambda+5)-4\Lambda\log_{2}\Lambda\right]\,.

(S67)

Similarly, we can estimate the single qubit gate cost of simulating
e^{-i\hat{H}_{I}\delta t/2}
:

\displaystyle N^{G,I}_{\rm OQ}=4(d-1)M^{2d}\left[d\Lambda(\log_{2}\Lambda)(\log_{2}\Lambda+5)-4\Lambda\log_{2}\Lambda\right]\,.

(S68)

Then we need to consider the complexity of simulating
e^{-iH_{V}\delta t/2}
. In the main text,
H_{V}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{V}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{J^{0}(\mathbf{n})J^{0}(\mathbf{m})}{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}^{2}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}

(S69)

After the Jordan-Wigner transformation, we can translate
\hat{H}_{V}
to the linear combination of Pauli strings:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{V}=-\frac{g^{2}N_{s}}{8}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}\sum_{\mathbf{m}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{1}{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}+\frac{g^{2}}{8}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{n}\not=\mathbf{m}\\
\alpha\not=\beta\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathbf{p}\not=0}\frac{1}{\hat{E}_{\mathbf{p}}}e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{m})}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{m},\beta}\,.

(S70)

In the above equation, we dropped the constant term in the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{V}
. There are
N_{s}M^{2d}
and
N_{s}(N_{s}-1)M^{d}(M^{d}-1)
of one-Pauli and “
zz
” two-Pauli terms inside the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{V}
. Therefore, the one and two-qubit gate cost of simulating
e^{-iH_{V}\delta t/2}
are

\displaystyle N^{G,V}_{\rm OQ}=N_{s}M^{2d}\,,

\displaystyle N^{G,V}_{\rm CNOT}=2N_{s}M^{d}(N_{s}M^{d}-1)\,.

(S71)

Kinetic and mass terms of the fermion field are given by:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{M}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\left[-{\rm i}\sum_{i}\gamma^{i}\frac{\psi(\mathbf{n}+\hat{i})-\psi(\mathbf{n}-\hat{i})}{2}+m\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\psi(\mathbf{n})\right]\,.

(S72)

After the Jordan-Wigner transformation, the
\hat{H}_{M}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{M}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\sum_{h=0,2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{s_{1},s_{2}=1}^{2}-\frac{(-1)^{\frac{h}{2}}\sigma^{i}_{s_{1}s_{2}}}{4}\left(\prod_{\gamma=s_{1}+1+h}^{N_{s}}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},\gamma}\right)\left[\prod_{l=1}^{M^{i-1}-1}\left(\prod_{\gamma=1}^{N_{s}}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{m}(l),\gamma}\right)\right]\left(\prod_{\gamma=1}^{s_{2}-1+h}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n}+\hat{i},\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},s_{1}+h}\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n}+\hat{i},s_{2}+h}-\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},s_{1}+h}\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n}+\hat{i},s_{2}+h}\right)-\frac{m}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\sum_{s=1}^{2}\left(\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},s}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},s+1}\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},s+2}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},s}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},s+1}\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},s+2}\right)\,,

(S73)

In the Eq. (
S73
),
\mathbf{m}(l)
is defined as

\displaystyle m_{x}(l)=(n_{x}+l)\mod M

\displaystyle m_{y}(l)=\left[n_{y}+\frac{n_{x}+l-m_{x}(l)}{M}\right]\mod M

\displaystyle m_{z}(l)=n_{z}+\left[n_{y}+\frac{n_{x}+l-m_{x}(l)}{M}-m_{y}(l)\right]\mod M\,.

(S74)

Then, we can obtain the one-qubit and CNOT gate cost of simulating

\displaystyle N^{G,M}_{\rm OQ}(d=2)=72M^{2}\,,

\displaystyle N^{G,M}_{\rm OQ}(d=3)=104M^{3}\,,

\displaystyle N^{G,M}_{\rm CNOT}(d=2)=16N_{s}M^{2}(1+M)+8M^{2}\,

\displaystyle N^{G,M}_{\rm CNOT}(d=3)=16N_{s}M^{3}(1+M+M^{2})+8M^{3}\,.

(S75)

At last, we discuss the complexity of simulating
e^{-i\hat{H}_{W}\delta t/2}

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{W}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}-\frac{w}{2}\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\hat{\Delta}\psi(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}-\frac{w}{2a^{2}}\bar{\psi}(\mathbf{n})\left[\psi(\mathbf{n}+\hat{i})+\psi(\mathbf{n}-\hat{i})-2\psi(\mathbf{n})\right]\,.

(S76)

After the Jordan-Wigner transformation, the Wilson term

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{W}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{s=1}^{2}\frac{w}{4a^{2}}\left(\prod_{\gamma=s+1}^{N_{s}}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},\gamma}\right)\left[\prod_{l=1}^{M^{i-1}-1}\left(\prod_{\gamma=1}^{N_{s}}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{m}(l),\gamma}\right)\right]\left(\prod_{\gamma=1}^{s+1}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},\gamma}\right)\left(\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},s}\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n}+\hat{i},s+2}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},s}\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n}+\hat{i},s+2}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{s=1}^{2}\frac{w}{4a^{2}}\left(\prod_{\gamma=s+3}^{N_{s}}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},\gamma}\right)\left[\prod_{l=1}^{M^{i-1}-1}\left(\prod_{\gamma=1}^{N_{s}}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{m}(l),\gamma}\right)\right]\left(\prod_{\gamma=1}^{s-1}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},\gamma}\right)\left(\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},s+2}\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n}+\hat{i},s}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},s+2}\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n}+\hat{i},s}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{w}{2a^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\sum_{s=1}^{2}\left(\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},s}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},s+1}\sigma^{1}_{\mathbf{n},s+2}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},s}\sigma^{3}_{\mathbf{n},s+1}\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n},s+2}\right)\,.

(S77)

Then, the one-qubit and CNOT gate cost of simulating

\displaystyle N^{G,W}_{\rm OQ}(d=2)=72M^{2}\,,

\displaystyle N^{G,W}_{\rm OQ}(d=3)=104M^{3}\,,

\displaystyle N^{G,W}_{\rm CNOT}(d=2)=16N_{s}M^{2}(1+M)+8M^{2}\,
