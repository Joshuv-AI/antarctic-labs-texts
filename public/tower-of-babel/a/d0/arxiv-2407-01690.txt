A central degeneracy induced outer core in fermionic dark matter halos
Weikang Lin

Abstract
Several galaxy-scale observations challenge the predictions of the cold dark matter paradigm based on cosmological simulations. The cusp-core problem is one of the most outstanding. However, current simulations are unable to consider quantum statistical effects. For a fermionic dark matter halo, a degenerate inner core induces an extended outer core that dominates in gravity in the region relevant for rotation curve observation. We study the properties of this outer core and show that its density and radius are correlated. The prediction remarkably agrees with the measured scaling relation in both the slope and the magnitude. Such consistency suggests that the observed cored profiles do not contradict massive dark matter but indicate its fermionic nature.



I
Introduction

Accumulated observations suggest that the dark matter (DM) halos are cored for dwarf galaxies and low-surface-brightness (LSB) galaxies
Moore 1994
;
Flores and Primack 1994
;
de Blok et al. 2008
;
Oh et al. 2015
;
Rodrigues et al. 2017
. This differs from the results of DM-only N-body simulations that predict cuspy profiles at the center
Navarro et al. 1996
. Such a discrepancy is called the “cusp-core” problem and is difficult to explain with baryon effects for those DM-dominated systems
de Blok 2010
;
Salucci 2019
;
Boldrini 2021
.

New DM physics are motivated to resolve the problem
Del Popolo and Le Delliou 2021
, such as fuzzy DM
Hu et al. 2000
and DM self-interactions
Spergel and Steinhardt 2000
. But those proposals remain controversial. For example, it is argued that light boson DM cannot correctly account for the core properties
Deng et al. 2018
;
Burkert 2020
;
Bañares Hernández et al. 2023
and the required self-interaction strength is constrained by other considerations
Randall et al. 2008
;
Hayashi et al. 2021
. Nonetheless, the cusp-core problem is thought to disfavor collisionless massive DM candidates, if complicated baryon effects are not involved. This work shall show the opposite.

One fundamental limitation of current N-body simulations is their incapability to capture the quantum statistical effects. This is rooted in the fact that particles in simulations are distinguishable, not to mention the unrealistically huge test particle mass.
Recently, several works investigated the role played by quantum statistics on DM halos at galaxy scales
Destri et al. 2013
;
de Vega et al. 2014
;
Destri et al. 2013
;
Ruffini et al. 2015
;
Chavanis et al. 2015
;
Randall et al. 2017
.
In a regime where DM is degenerate at the center, the DM density profiles are cored. Most efforts were devoted to this core, which is caused by the Fermi degenerate pressure preventing the system from gravitational collapse
Oppenheimer and Volkoff 1939
.
Unfortunately, to account for the observed core size ranging from hundreds of pc to a few tens of kpc
de Blok et al. 2008
;
Rodrigues et al. 2017
, the required DM mass is about one to two orders of magnitude smaller than the mass lower bound obtained from the cosmic large-scale structure
Bar et al. 2021
;
Carena et al. 2022
;
Zhang and Lin 2024
.

There is more to the degenerate regime and the DM profile has a low-density but extended plateau enclosing the high-density core
Ruffini et al. 2015
. Due to its dominant mass over the inner compact core, this plateau should be more relevant for the rotational curve observation for dwarf-size galaxies
Siutsou et al. 2015
. We call this plateau an “outer core”. The properties of this outer core have been completely overlooked in the literature, which we study and show that there is significant implication regarding the cusp-core problem.

II
Method

We highlight here the key steps in obtaining the fermionic isothermal profiles, adopting the natural units where
c=\hbar=k_{\textsc{b}}=1
. We consider a spherical halo of fermionic DM in dynamical equilibrium,

\frac{dP}{dr}=-\frac{G\rho}{r^{2}}M(r)\,,

(1)

and DM follows a nonrelativistic Fermi-Dirac distribution presumably due to violent relaxation
Lynden-Bell 1967
,

f(p)=\frac{g}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{p^{2}}{\exp\big[(\frac{p^{2}}{2m_{\rm d}}-\mu_{\rm eff})/T_{\rm d}\big]+1}\,,

(2)

where
g
is taken to be
2
but can be generalized. The effective chemical potential is

\mu_{\rm eff}=\mu-m\phi\,,

(3)

where
\phi
is the gravitational potential and
\mu
is the real chemical potential. Assuming there is no net particle flow at any radius, we take
\mu
to be spatially independent. This treatment corresponds to the Thomas-Fermi approach applied in
Destri et al. 2013
. The above settings are expected to break down at a large radius
r
but should be a good approximation for the region we are interested in.

The mass density and pressure are then calculated in a standard statistical mechanics way and it can be derived that
Zhang and Lin 2024
,

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}Q_{*}\left(\frac{T_{\rm d}}{m_{\rm d}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}J_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}^{\textsc{f}}(\Delta)\,,

(4)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{3/2}Q_{*}}{3}\left(\frac{T_{\rm d}}{m_{\rm d}}\right)^{\frac{5}{2}}J_{\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}}^{\textsc{f}}(\Delta)\,,

(5)

where
\Delta\equiv\mu_{\rm eff}/T_{\rm d}
is the degree of degeneracy,
Q_{*}=\frac{gm_{\rm d}^{4}}{2\pi^{2}}
and
J_{s}^{\textsc{f}}(\Delta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{s-1}{\rm d}z}{\exp(z-\Delta)+1}
. Eq. (
2
) reduces to the classical Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution when
\Delta<0
and describes a degenerate fermionic system when
\Delta>0
.

The last equation is Poisson’s equation for the gravitational potential,

\frac{d\phi}{dr}=\frac{GM(r)}{r^{2}}\,.

(6)

We have ignored the general relativistic correction (see the RAR model
Ruffini et al. 2015
). The above conditions require the resultant halo to be isothermal, i.e.,
T_{\rm d}
is spatial independent. So, the solutions are fermionic isothermal spheres.

With the above settings, the DM density profile is then fully determined by the following 2nd-order differential equation about
\Delta
,

\Delta^{\prime\prime}+\frac{2}{z}\Delta^{\prime}=-J_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}^{\textsc{f}}(\Delta)\,,

(7)

where
′
denotes the derivative with respect to
z\equiv r/r_{*}
. The characteristic radius
r_{*}
is,

\displaystyle r_{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{g^{1/2}}\left(\frac{m_{\rm d}}{2T_{\rm d}}\right)^{1/4}\left(\frac{m_{\rm p}}{m_{\rm d}}\right)^{2}\ell_{\rm p}\,,

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{g}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{30\,\text{km/s}}{\sigma_{v}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{5\,\text{keV}}{m_{\rm d}}\right)^{2}\times 0.23\,{\rm pc}\,,

(8)

where
\sigma_{v}=\sqrt{T_{\rm d}/m_{\rm d}}
is the (1-D) DM velocity dispersion,
m_{\rm p}=2.4\times 10^{24}
keV the reduced Planck mass and
\ell_{\rm p}=\frac{1}{m_{\rm p}}=8.2\times 10^{-35}
m. Note that
r_{*}
is macroscopic for a wide range of massive DM candidates, although still small compared to kpc scales.

The solutions to Eq. (
7
) are characterized by the central degeneracy, denoted as
\Delta_{0}
. Resultant DM profiles are shown in Figure
1
presented as the normalized density versus
r/r_{*}
with their values of
\Delta_{0}
labeled in curves. The profiles obtained are consistent with previous studies
Destri et al. 2013
;
de Vega et al. 2014
;
Destri et al. 2013
;
Ruffini et al. 2015
;
Chavanis et al. 2015
;
Randall et al. 2017
. Two classes of profile are readily identified, one with
\Delta_{0}<0
and the other with
\Delta_{0}>0
. Profiles with
\Delta_{0}<0
are nothing but the classical cored isothermal sphere obtained by imposing a finite central density
Weinberg 2008
and suffer unstable gravitational collapse
Padmanabhan 1990
.
Profiles with
\Delta_{0}>0
have a high-density but compact inner core followed by a low-density but extended outer core. It has been shown that the outer core can explain the individual rotation curve of LSB galaxies
Siutsou et al. 2015
. As more cored profiles of dwarf-size galaxies are determined, a universal scaling relation between the core density and radius is reported and supported by increasing observations
Donato et al. 2009
;
Burkert 2015
;
Salucci 2019
. We therefore focus on the properties of the outer core, in particular its relation between density and radius.

III
Analysis and results

Intuitively, the outer core appears because of the sudden drops of the density right outside the inner core, making the right-hand side of Eq. (
1
) very small and allowing the pressure to vary slowly for a large range. The density profile of the outer core and beyond is in a region where DM has a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution and
J_{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}^{\textsc{f}}(\Delta)\simeq\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}e^{\Delta}
. It implies that the outer core radius should behave like a King’s radius such that it is correlated with the outer core density. This can be clearly seen with the solution to Eq. (
7
) in the outer core region, which reads,

\Delta\simeq\Delta_{\rm oc}+\frac{B}{z}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{12}e^{\Delta_{\rm oc}}z^{2}\,,

(9)

where the constants
\Delta_{\rm oc}
and
B
are determined by patching the outer solution with the inner solution. The second term decreases with
z
quickly and can be ignored.
The outer core radius
r_{\rm oc}
can be estimated by the location at which the third term becomes unity, that is,
z_{\rm oc}\propto\exp(-\Delta_{\rm oc}/2)
and thus
\frac{r_{\rm oc}}{r_{*}}\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho_{\rm oc}}}
.

\sigma_{v}

Numerically, we define
r_{\rm oc}
to be the location where the logarithmic slope of the density profile is
-1.5
to match the core radius in the Burkert profile
Burkert 1995
, that is
\frac{d\ln\rho}{d\ln r}\big\rvert_{r_{\rm{c}}}=-1.5
. Whereas, the outer core density
\rho_{\rm oc}
is defined to be the density at the peak of the logarithmic slope of the density profile in the outer core region. From these definitions along with Eq. (
II
), we find that
\rho_{\rm oc}
and
r_{\rm oc}
are correlated by,

\rho_{\rm oc}=1.4\times 10^{8}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}}{30\,{\text{km/s}}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1\,{\text{kpc}}}{r_{\rm oc}}\right)^{2}\,\frac{M_{\odot}}{{\rm kpc}^{3}}\,.

(10)

This relation contains no other free parameter, such as the DM mass, as long as the outer core is induced. We compare the above prediction with the best-fit core densities and radii of a Burkert profile fitted to the rotation curves of LSB galaxies
Rodrigues et al. 2017
and dwarf galaxies
Oh et al. 2015
.

\sigma_{v}

\sigma_{v}
and
M_{\rm b}
motivated by the empirical Faber-Jackson relation for dispersion-supported galaxies,

\sigma_{v}=\left(\frac{M_{\rm b}}{10^{9}\,M_{\odot}}\right)^{1/4}\sigma_{v}^{\rm ref}\,,

(11)

where
M_{\rm b}
is the baryon mass of the galaxy and
\sigma_{v}^{\rm ref}
is some reference DM velocity dispersion at
M_{\rm b}=10^{9}M_{\odot}
. We take
\sigma_{v}^{\rm ref}=40
km/s motivated by the stellar velocity dispersion for a dwarf galaxy of similar size
Binney and Tremaine 2011
. Note that the Faber-Jackson relation is between the luminosity (or the baryon amount) and the stellar velocity dispersion for elliptical galaxies. However, it is reasonable to assume that the invisible DM velocity dispersion is correlated with the baryon amount similarly.

For each galaxy, we use the galactic baryon mass (the sum of the masses of disk, bulge, and gas) given in
Rodrigues et al. 2014
;
Oh et al. 2015
to infer the DM velocity dispersion according to Eq. (
11
). Then, we take the measured core radius and the inferred
\sigma_{v}
as the inputs of Eq. (
10
) to predict the core density. The results are shown by the red symbols in Figure
2
. The predictions exhibit an excellent consistency with the measurements in both the slope and the magnitude.

The outer core radius is determined by the DM mass and the central degeneracy with,

r_{\rm oc}\simeq 4.5\exp(0.37\Delta_{0})\,r_{*}\,.

(12)

Interestingly, for
r_{\rm oc}\sim 1
kpc, the DM mass that satisfies cosmological constraints
\gtrsim 5
keV
Iršič et al. 2024
requires
\Delta_{0}\gtrsim 19
so that a clear outer core is induced. Thus, the central degeneracy-induced outer core resolving the cusp-core problem for massive fermionic DM is fully consistent in cosmology.

IV
Discussion

The agreement between the predictions and measurements shown in Figure
2
suggests that the observed core radii for dwarf galaxies and LSB galaxies are a sort of King radius. But unlike the classical King radius, the outer core radius here is induced by the central inner core that is stabilized by the fermi degenerate pressure. Thus, the central degeneracy-induced outer core constitutes a viable and promising resolution to the cusp-core problem.

There appeared to be a dilemma in the nature of DM. On one hand, cosmological probes are consistent with the cold dark matter paradigm
Ade and others (2020) Planck Collaboration
. On the other hand, the cusp-core problem seemed to suggest otherwise.
Our result suggests that the observed cored DM profiles are not contradictory to the standard DM scenario, but are pointing to the fermionic nature of DM whose mass is consistent with cosmological constraints.

Our result also suggests that the observed
\rho_{\rm c}
-
r_{\rm c}
relation should fundamentally be a
\rho_{\rm c}
-
r_{\rm c}
-
\sigma_{v}
relation. Or, in terms of observables, it is a
\rho_{\rm c}
-
r_{\rm c}
-
M_{\rm b}
relation assuming an empirically motivated scaling relation between the DM velocity dispersion and the galactic baryon mass. Only viewed in the projected
\rho_{\rm c}
-
r_{\rm c}
plane, the product
\rho_{\rm c}r_{\rm c}
is nearly constant as reported in the literature
Donato et al. 2009
;
Burkert 2015
;
Salucci 2019
.

For a given DM particle mass, a larger outer core radius corresponds to a higher central degeneracy and a higher central density. This hints at the connection between the central degeneracy and the halo collapse history.
A caveat is that we are considering DM-only systems, which are suitable for DM-dominated systems such as dwarf galaxies and LSB galaxies. The interplay between DM and baryon for larger systems may exhibit a wider diversity.
Numerical simulations are motivated to consider or mimic the quantum statistical effects. To do so, the resolved scales are required to be smaller than not only the observed core sizes but also the characteristic scale
r_{*}
that depends on DM particle mass.
