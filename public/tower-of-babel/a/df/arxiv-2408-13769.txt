Suszko’s Thesis and Many-valued Logical Structures Thanks: Corresponding author: sayantanr@iiitd.ac.in
Sayantan Roy, Sankha S. Basu, Mihir K. Chakraborty

Abstract
. In this article, we try to formulate a definition of many-valued logical structures . For this, we embark on a deeper study of Suszko’s Thesis ( ST ) and show that the truth or falsity of ST depends, at least, on the precise notion of semantics. We propose two different notions of semantics and three different notions of entailment. The first helps us formulate a precise definition of inferentially many-valued logical structures. The second and third help us to generalise Suszko Reduction and provide adequate bivalent semantics for every logical structure. All these lead us to a closer examination of the role played by the language/metalanguage hierarchy vis-à-vis ST . We conclude that many-valued logical structures can be obtained if the bivalence of all the higher-order metalogics of the logic under consideration is discarded, building formal bridges between the theory of graded consequence and the theory of many-valued logical structures, culminating in generalisations of ST .

What is Logical Many-valuedness?

Undoubtedly, a fundamental problem concerning

many-valuedness is to know what it really is.

[
19
, p. 281]

A
logical structure
(see
[
6
, p. 84]
) is a pair
(\mathscr{L},\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-})
, where
\mathscr{L}
is a set,
\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
denotes the power set of
\mathscr{L}

and
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}\,\subseteq\,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\times\mathscr{L}
. Alternatively, a logical structure may be defined as a pair
(\mathscr{L},W)
, where
\mathscr{L}
is a set and
W:\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
is called a
consequence operator
. These two definitions are equivalent in the following sense.

\bullet

Every logical structure of the form
(\mathscr{L},\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-})
induces a logical structure of the form
(\mathscr{L},W^{\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}})
where
W^{\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}}:\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
and for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}

\alpha\in W^{\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}}(\Gamma)~\text{iff}~\Gamma\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}\alpha

\bullet

Every logical structure of the form
(\mathscr{L},W)
induces a logical structure of the form
(\mathscr{L},\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{W})
where
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{W}\,\subseteq\,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\times\mathscr{L}
and for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}

\Gamma\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{W}\alpha~\text{iff}~\alpha\in W(\Gamma)

Henceforth, we will use either of these descriptions interchangeably.

Although the terms ‘logic’ and ‘logical structure’ have been considered synonymous in
[
6
, p. 84]
, we distinguish between these here. The term
logic
is reserved for referring to those pairs
(\mathscr{L},W)
where
\mathscr{L}
is an algebra.

Given a set
\mathscr{L}
, a
logical matrix for
\mathscr{L}
is a triple
\langle\mathcal{V},D,S\rangle
where
D\subseteq\mathcal{V}
,
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathcal{V}
are algebras of similar type and
S
is the set of all homomorphisms from
\mathscr{L}
to
\mathcal{V}
. The elements of
S
are often referred to as
valuations
. To any such matrix
\mathfrak{M}=\langle\mathcal{V},D,S\rangle
for
\mathscr{L}
, one can associate a relation
\models_{\mathfrak{M}}\,\subseteq\,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\times\mathscr{L}
as follows:

\Gamma\models_{\mathfrak{M}}\alpha~\text{iff for all}~v\in S,v(\Gamma)\subseteq D~\text{implies that}~v(\alpha)\in D

for all
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
. This relation is often referred to as the
entailment relation induced by the matrix
\mathfrak{M}
. It satisfies the following properties.

(1)

For all
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\alpha\in\Gamma
implies that
\Gamma\models_{\mathfrak{M}}\alpha
. (Reflexivity)

(2)

For all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
,
\Gamma\models_{\mathfrak{M}}\alpha
implies that
\Sigma\models_{\mathfrak{M}}\alpha
. (Monotonicity)

(3)

For all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
, if
\Gamma\models_{\mathfrak{M}}\beta
for all
\beta\in\Sigma
and
\Sigma\models_{\mathfrak{M}}\alpha
then
\Gamma\models_{\mathfrak{M}}\alpha
as well. (Transitivity)

In terms of
W^{\models_{\mathfrak{M}}}
, the above properties can be formulated as follows.

(1)

For all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Gamma\subseteq W^{\models_{\mathfrak{M}}}(\Gamma)
. (Reflexivity)

(2)

For all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
,
W^{\models_{\mathfrak{M}}}(\Gamma)\subseteq W^{\models_{\mathfrak{M}}}(\Sigma)
. (Monotonicity)

(3)

For all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Gamma\subseteq W^{\models_{\mathfrak{M}}}(\Sigma)
implies that
W^{\models_{\mathfrak{M}}}(\Gamma)\subseteq W^{\models_{\mathfrak{M}}}(\Sigma)
. (Transitivity)

A logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
for which
W
satisfies the above three conditions is said to be a
Tarski-type logical structure
. A logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
is said to be
reflexive
(respectively,
monotonic, transitive
) if
W
satisfies reflexivity (respectively, monotonicity, transitivity).

2.
Fundamental Notions

In the discussions on
\mathbf{ST}
, an important thing that comes out regularly but is seldom emphasised may be stated as follows:
the justification (or refutation) of
\mathbf{ST}
depends on the notion of semantics
. Usually, the term ‘semantics’ is accompanied by an adjective, e.g.,
truth-functional semantics, matrix semantics, referential semantics
, etc. However, these terms are rather ambiguous because they make sense only if one has a definition of semantics. In
[
21
, p. 384]
, e.g., Font says the following (emphasis ours).

Suszko avoids speaking of
truth values
. Instead, one of his main points is to distinguish between
algebraic
values (the members of the algebra of truth values, introduced by Łukasiewicz and others and usually interpreted as such in the literature) and
logical
values (of which his intuition admits only two,
true
and
false
).
The mathematical result
\ldots
can be paraphrased
\ldots
only after the adoption of a peculiar conception of what is a “two-valued semantics”, and in particular of what is, or can be, a “semantics”
\ldots

So, we propose the following definition of semantics.

Definition 2.1

(
Semantics
)
.

Given a set
\mathscr{L}
, a
semantics for
\mathscr{L}
is a tuple
(\mathbf{M},\{\models_{i}\}_{i\in I},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
, where

(i)

\mathbf{M}
is a set,

(ii)

I\neq\emptyset
and
\models_{i}\,\subseteq\,\mathbf{M}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
for each
i\in I
,

(iii)

\emptyset\subsetneq S\subseteq\{(\models_{i},\models_{j}):(i,j)\in I\times I\}
.

If, in particular,
I=\{1,\ldots,n\}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, we write
(\mathbf{M},\models_{1},\ldots,\models_{n},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
instead of
(\mathbf{M},\{\models_{i}\}_{i\in I},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
. Finally, if
(m,\Gamma)\in\,\models_{i}
, we write
m\models_{i}\Gamma
.

Definition 2.2

(
Entailment Relation/Logical Structure Induced by a Semantics
)
.

Given a set
\mathscr{L}
and a semantics
\mathfrak{S}=(\mathbf{M},\{\models_{i}\}_{i\in I},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
for
\mathscr{L}
, the
entailment relation induced by
\mathfrak{S}
,
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{\mathfrak{S}}\,\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\times\mathscr{L}
is defined as follows.

\Gamma\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{\mathfrak{S}}\alpha\iff\text{for all}~(\models_{i},\models_{j})\in S~\text{and}~m\in\mathbf{M},~m\models_{i}\Gamma~\text{implies that}~m\models_{j}\{\alpha\}

The corresponding consequence operator is denoted by
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
. The corresponding logical structure, denoted by
(\mathscr{L},\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{\mathfrak{S}})
(respectively,
(\mathscr{L},W_{\mathfrak{S}})
), is said to be the
logical structure induced by
\mathfrak{S}
.

Definition 2.3

(
Adequate Semantics
)
.

A logical structure
(\mathscr{L},\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-})
(respectively,
(\mathscr{L},W)
) is said to be
adequate with respect to a semantics
\mathfrak{S}
if
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}\,=\,\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{\mathfrak{S}}
(respectively,
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
). It is said to have an
adequate semantics
if there exists a semantics
\mathfrak{S}
for
\mathscr{L}
such that
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}\,=\,\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{\mathfrak{S}}
(respectively,
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
).

Remark 2.4
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W_{\mathfrak{S}})
be a logical structure induced by the entailment relation corresponding to the semantics
\mathfrak{S}=(\mathbf{M},\{\models_{i}\}_{i\in I},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
for

such that

.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a set and

Remark 2.7
.

Given a set
\mathscr{L}
and a semantics
\mathfrak{S}
for
\mathscr{L}
, the entailment relation
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{\mathfrak{S}}
as we have defined above, is not the only possible one. Below we mention two other possibilities.

\Gamma\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{\mathfrak{S}}\alpha\iff\text{for all}~(\models_{i},\models_{j})\in S~\text{and}~(m,n)\in\mathbf{M}\times\mathbf{M},~m\models_{i}\Gamma~\text{implies that}~n\models_{j}\{\alpha\}

or,

\Gamma\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{\mathfrak{S}}\alpha\iff\text{there exists}~(\models_{i},\models_{j})\in S~\text{such that for some}~m\in\mathbf{M},~m\models_{i}\Gamma~\text{implies that}~m\models_{j}\{\alpha\}

However, every logical structure is adequate with respect to some semantics, as the following theorem shows.

Theorem 2.8

(
Canonical Semantics of a Logical Structure
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a logical structure. Consider the following semantics for
\mathscr{L}
,
\mathfrak{S}=(\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}),\models_{1},\models_{2},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
where
S=\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\}
and for all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\models_{1}
and
\models_{2}
are defined respectively as follows:

\Sigma\models_{1}\Gamma~\text{iff}~\Sigma=\Gamma

and

\Sigma\models_{2}\Gamma~\text{iff}~\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma).

Then,
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
.

Proof.

Suppose the contrary, i.e., suppose that
W\neq W_{\mathfrak{S}}
. Then, for some
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
W(\Gamma)\neq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
—i.e., either
W(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
or
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W(\Gamma)
.

If possible, let
W(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Then there exists
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. So, there exists
\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Sigma\models_{1}\Gamma
but
\Sigma\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. Now,
\Sigma\models_{1}\Gamma
implies that
\Sigma=\Gamma
. But then, since
\Sigma\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
, we must have
\{\alpha\}\not\subseteq W(\Gamma)
, i.e.,
\alpha\notin W(\Gamma)
—a contradiction. Thus,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
.

So,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W(\Gamma)
. Then, there exists
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
but
\alpha\notin W(\Gamma)
. Choose this
\Gamma
and note that
\Gamma\models_{1}\Gamma
. Therefore, since
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
, we must have
\Gamma\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
, i.e.,
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
—a contradiction. Consequently,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
as well. Hence the theorem.∎

Definition 2.9

(

)

A

\mathfrak{S}
for

(\mathbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\},\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))

3.
Monotonic Logical Structures: A Case Study

In this section, we consider monotonic logical structures. We show that the class of such logical structures can be split into four subclasses, each inferentially 3-valued at least. Later, in

Interestingly enough, the entailment relations induced by strongly granular semantics give rise to monotonic logical structures. This is the content of the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(
Representation Theorem for Monotonic Logical Structures (Part I)
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\{\models_{i}\}_{i\in I},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
be a strongly granular semantics for

. Then
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is monotonic.

Proof.

If possible, let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
be such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
but

. Hence, there exists

such that

. Since

and

. Hence,

is monotonic.
∎

\underline{(5)\implies(1):}
Let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
be such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
. Then,
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
which, by (5), implies that
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Thus,
W
is monotonic.

Now, for any
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma
and hence by the monotonicity of
W
,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W\left(W(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma\right)
. The reverse inclusion is immediate since, by (5),
W(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma\subseteq W(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma
implies that
W\left(W(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma\right)\subseteq W(\Gamma)
. Hence,
W
is a
q
-consequence operator. This proves (1).
∎

Theorem 3.8

(
Representation Theorem for
q
-type (Part I)
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S_{q},\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
be a strongly granular semantics for
\mathscr{L}
such that
\models_{2}\,\subseteq\,\models_{1}
and
S_{q}=\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\}
. Then
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is a
q
-consequence operator.

Proof.

Monotonicity of
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
follows from
Theorem
3.1
. Hence, it suffices to show that
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
satisfies the quasi-closure. Suppose the contrary, i.e., suppose that for some
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma)\neq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Now, observe that, since
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is monotonic,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma)
; hence, we must have
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma)\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. So, there exists
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Consequently, there exists
m\in\mathbf{M}
such that
m\models_{1}\Gamma
but
m\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
.

Let
\beta\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Then, since
m\models_{1}\Gamma
, we have
m\models_{2}\{\beta\}
. Furthermore, since
\models_{2}\,\subseteq\,\models_{1}
and
m\models_{2}\{\beta\}
, it follows that
m\models_{1}\{\beta\}
as well. Thus,
m\models_{1}\{\beta\}
. Now, since
m\models_{1}\Gamma
and
\mathfrak{S}
is strongly granular,
m\models_{1}\{\gamma\}
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Hence
m\models_{1}\{\sigma\}
for all
\sigma\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma
. Again, since
\mathfrak{S}
is strongly granular,
m\models_{1}W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma
. But then, since
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma)
, we must have
m\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
—a contradiction. Thus,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma)\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Hence, the theorem.∎

Let
(\mathbscr{L},W)
be a logic, where
\mathbscr{L}
is an algebra with universe
\mathscr{L}
. It is said to satisfy
structurality
if for all homomorphisms
\sigma:\mathbscr{L}\to\mathbscr{L}
and all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\sigma(W(\Gamma))\subseteq W(\sigma(\Gamma))
. If a logic satisfies structurality, we call it
structural
(see
[
22
,
20
]
for more details and context).

The proof that every structural
q
-type logic has a 3-valued description has already been given, e.g., in
[
35
, pp. 204
-
206]
. However, a similar argument works for arbitrary
q
-type logical structures as well. Below we give a proof of the same, to keep the presentation self-contained.

Theorem 3.9

(
Representation Theorem for
q
-type (Part II)
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a
q
-type logical structure. Let
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S_{q},\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
be a semantics for
\mathscr{L}
satisfying the following properties.

\bullet

\mathbf{M}=\{\mu_{\Sigma}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathscr{L}}:\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}
, where

\mu_{\Sigma}(\beta)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}~\beta\notin W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma\\
1&\text{if}~\beta\in W(\Sigma)\\
2&\text{if}~\beta\in(W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma)\setminus W(\Sigma)\end{cases}

\bullet

For all
m\in\mathbf{M},\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,

m\models_{1}\Gamma~\text{iff}~m(\Gamma)\subseteq\left\{1,2\right\}

m\models_{2}\Gamma~\text{iff}~m(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}

\bullet

S_{q}
is as defined in the previous theorem.

Then,
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
is such that
W(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Then, there exists
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Hence, there exists
m\in\textbf{M}
such that
m\models_{1}\Gamma
but
m\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. Let
m=\mu_{\Sigma}
for some
\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
. Then,
\mu_{\Sigma}\models_{1}\Gamma
implies that
\mu_{\Sigma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1,2\}
, or equivalently
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
. Since
W
is a
q
-consequence operator, it is monotonic. So,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma)
. Moreover, since
W
satisfies quasi-closure, it follows that
W(W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma)=W(\Sigma)
. Consequently,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Then, as
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
,
\alpha\in W(\Sigma)
. But then
\mu_{\Sigma}(\alpha)=1
, i.e.,
\mu_{\Sigma}\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
, i.e.,
m\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
—a contradiction. Hence,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
.

Next, let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
and
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Now,
\mu_{\Gamma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1,2\}
, i.e.,
\mu_{\Gamma}\models_{1}\Gamma
. Therefore, as
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
, it follows that
\mu_{\Gamma}\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
, which implies that
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
. Thus,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
. Hence
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
.
∎

Theorem 3.10

(
Representation Theorem for
q
-type (Part III)
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a logical structure where
W
is a
q
-consequence operator. Let
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
where
S=\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\}
is a strongly granular semantics for
\mathscr{L}
. If
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
, then
\models_{1}
and
\models_{2}
satisfy the following property:

For all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
,
\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma\}))\not\subseteq\,\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\alpha\}\}))
implies that
\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{\Gamma\}))\not\subseteq\,\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\alpha\}\}))
.

Here
\pi_{1}:\mathbf{M}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\to\mathbf{M}
is defined as follows:
\pi_{1}(m,\Delta)=m
for all
(m,\Delta)\in\mathbf{M}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
be such that
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
. Therefore, since
W
is a
q
-consequence operator, by
Theorem
3.7
(5)
we have
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Since
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
, this implies
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}
be such that
\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma\}))\not\subseteq\,\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\alpha\}\}))
. Then, for some
m\in\mathbf{M}
,
m\in\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma\}))
but
m\notin\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\alpha\}\}))
. Now,
m\in\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma\}))
implies that
m\models_{1}W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
. Since
\mathfrak{S}
is strongly granular (and hence granular at the first component),
m\models_{1}W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
implies that
m\models_{1}\Gamma
. Consequently,
m\in\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{\Gamma\}))
but
m\notin\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\alpha\}\}))
, i.e.,
\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{\Gamma\}))\,\not\subseteq\,\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap~(\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\alpha\}\}))
.
∎

By
Theorem
3.9
, every
q
-consequence operator has an adequate functionally
3
-valued canonical semantics. But, not every
q
-consequence operator is inferentially
3
-valued. For example, every logical structure induced by a reflexive
q
-consequence operator is of Tarski-type and it is inferentially 2-valued. However, as shown below, every
non-reflexive

q
-consequence operator is inferentially 3-valued.

Theorem 3.11

(
Inferential Irreducibility Theorem for
q
-type
)
.

Every logical structure induced by a non-reflexive
q
-consequence operator is inferentially 3-valued.

Proof.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a logical structure induced by a non-reflexive
q
-consequence operator. Note that, by
Theorem
3.9
, it is functionally
3
-valued. Suppose that it is not inferentially
3
-valued. So, there exists some functionally
2
-valued canonical semantics for
\mathscr{L}
, say
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S_{q},\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
such that
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
. Since
\mathfrak{S}
is functionally
2
-valued, without loss of generality, we may assume that
\mathbf{M}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathscr{L}}
.

So, for each
i\in\{1,2\}
, there exists
\emptyset\subsetneq D_{i}\subsetneq\{0,1\}
such that
D_{1}\cup D_{2}\subsetneq\{0,1\}
. Since
\emptyset\subsetneq D_{1}\cup D_{2}\subsetneq\{0,1\}
, either
D_{1}\cup D_{2}=\{1\}
or
D_{1}\cup D_{2}=\{0\}
. Without loss of generality, let us assume that
D_{1}\cup D_{2}=\{1\}
. Consequently, since
\emptyset\subsetneq D_{i}\subsetneq\{0,1\}
for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, it follows that
D_{1}=\{1\}=D_{2}
.

Thus, for all
m\in\mathbf{M}
and
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
m\models_{1}\Gamma
implies that
m(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
. Similarly, for all
m\in\mathbf{M}
and
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
m\models_{2}\Gamma
implies that
m(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
. Therefore,
\models_{1}\,=\,\models_{2}
.

We now claim that
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is reflexive.
To prove this, we first show that
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\{\alpha\})
for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}
. Suppose the contrary. Then there exists
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}
such that
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\{\alpha\})
. But this would mean that there exists
m\in\mathbf{M}
such that
m\models_{1}\{\alpha\}
but
m\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
—a contradiction since
\models_{1}\,=\,\models_{2}
. Thus
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\{\alpha\})
for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}
.

Now, let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
and
\beta\in\Gamma
, i.e.,
\{\beta\}\subseteq\Gamma
. Since
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
and
W
is monotonic,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is monotonic as well. This implies that
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\{\beta\})\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Since
\beta\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\{\beta\})
, we must have
\beta\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. So
\beta\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
for all
\beta\in\Gamma
. Consequently,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is reflexive, which implies that
W
is reflexive as well—a contradiction. This establishes the theorem.
∎

3.2.
p
-consequence Operators

p
-consequence operators were introduced by Frankowski to formalise the notion of ‘plausible inferences’. In
[
23
, p. 41]
, he writes,

The key intuition behind plausibility in the sense of Ajdukiewicz
[
1
]
is that it admits reasonings wherein the degree of strength of the conclusion (i.e. the conviction it is true) is smaller than that of the premises. In consequence in the formal description of plausible inference, the standard notions of consequence and rule of inference cannot be countenanced as entirely suitable.

Following is the formal definition of a
p
-consequence operator.

Definition 3.12

(
p
-consequence Operator
)
.

A
p
-consequence operator on
\mathscr{L}
is a function
W:\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
satisfying the following properties.

(i)

For all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Gamma)
. (Reflexivity)

(ii)

For all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
, if
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
then
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. (Monotonicity)

A logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
is said to be of
p-type
if
W
is a
p
-consequence operator on
\mathscr{L}
.

The following theorem provides another criterion for characterising
p
-consequence operators, which we will need in a later theorem.

Theorem 3.13

(
Characterisation of
p
-type
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a logical structure. Then,
W
is a
p
-consequence operator iff for all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
implies that
\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

Suppose
W
is a
p
-consequence operator. Let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
. Since
W
is reflexive,
\Sigma\subseteq W(\Sigma)
, which implies that
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Moreover, as
W
is monotonic,
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
implies that
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
as well. Consequently,
\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
for all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
.

Conversely, suppose that for all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
implies
\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Let
\Sigma=\Gamma
. Then, since
\Gamma\subseteq\Gamma
,
\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Gamma)
, which implies that
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Gamma)
. Thus,
W
is reflexive.

Let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
. Then,
\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
which implies that
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. So
W
is monotonic as well.

Hence,
W
is a
p
-consequence operator.
∎

Theorem 3.14

(
Representation Theorem for
p
-type (Part I)
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S_{p},\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
be a strongly granular semantics for
\mathscr{L}
such that
S_{p}=\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\}
and
\models_{1}\,\subseteq\,\models_{2}
. Then,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is a
p
-consequence operator.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
but
\Gamma\cup W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)
. This implies that there exists
\alpha\in\Gamma\cup W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)
.

Since
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)
, there exists
m\in\mathbf{M}
such that
m\models_{1}\Sigma
but
m\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. Since
\mathfrak{S}
is strongly granular (and hence granular at the first component),
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
and
m\models_{1}\Sigma
, it follows that
m\models_{1}\Gamma
. Since
\alpha\in\Gamma\cup W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
, either
\alpha\in\Gamma
or
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
.

If
\alpha\in\Gamma
, then as
m\models_{1}\Gamma
and
\mathfrak{S}
is strongly granular,
m\models_{1}\{\alpha\}
as well. Since
\models_{1}\,\subseteq\,\models_{2}
, this implies
m\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
—a contradiction. If, on the other hand,
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
, then since
m\models_{1}\Gamma
, we have
m\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
—a contradiction again. Thus
\Gamma\cup W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)
. Therefore, by the previous theorem,
W
is a
p
-consequence operator.
∎

It has been proved in
[
24
, Theorem 5]
that every
p
-consequence operator can be given a 3-valued description. In our language this is equivalent to stating that every
p
-type logical structure has an adequate functionally
3
-valued canonical semantics. Below we give a proof of the same result mainly for completeness.

Theorem 3.15

(
Representation Theorem for
p
-type (Part II)
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a
p
-type logical structure and
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S_{p},\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
a semantics for
\mathscr{L}
satisfying the following properties.

\bullet

\mathbf{M}=\{\mu_{\Sigma}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\mathscr{L}}:\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}
where

\mu_{\Sigma}(\beta)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}~\beta\notin W(\Sigma)\\
1&\text{if}~\beta\in\Sigma\\
2&\text{if}~\beta\in W(\Sigma)\setminus\Sigma\end{cases}

\bullet

For all
m\in\mathbf{M},\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,

m\models_{1}\Gamma~\text{iff}~m(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}

m\models_{2}\Gamma~\text{iff}~m(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1,2\}

\bullet

S_{p}
is as defined in the previous theorem.

Then,
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
.

Proof.

Let for some
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
W(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Then, there exists
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. So, there exists
m\in\textbf{M}
such that
m\models_{1}\Gamma
but
m\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. Since
m\in\textbf{M}
, there exists
\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
m=\mu_{\Sigma}
. Now,
\mu_{\Sigma}\models_{1}\Gamma
implies that
\mu_{\Sigma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
, i.e.,
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
. Since
W
is monotonic,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. So,
\alpha\in W(\Sigma)
and hence
\mu_{\Sigma}\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
, i.e.,
m\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
—a contradiction. Consequently,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
.

Next, let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
and
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Now,
\mu_{\Gamma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
and so
\mu_{\Gamma}\models_{1}\Gamma
. As
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
,
\mu_{\Gamma}\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. Thus,
\mu_{\Gamma}(\{\alpha\})\subseteq\{1,2\}
. If
\mu_{\Gamma}(\alpha)=1
then
\alpha\in\Gamma
and since
W
is reflexive,
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
as well. If, on the other hand,
\mu_{\Gamma}(\alpha)=2
then also
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
. Thus, in either case,
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
. Hence,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.16

(
Representation Theorem for
p
-type (Part III)
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a
p
-type logical structure and
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
a strongly granular semantics for
\mathscr{L}
such that
S=\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\}
and

W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
. Then
\models_{1}\,\subseteq\,\models_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}
and
m\in\mathbf{M}
be such that
m\models_{1}\{\alpha\}
. If possible, let
m\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. But then
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\{\alpha\})
which implies that
\alpha\notin W(\{\alpha\})
as
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
. This, however, is a contradiction since
W
is reflexive. Thus, for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{L},m\in\mathbf{M}
,
m\models_{1}\{\alpha\}
implies that
m\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
.

Now, let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
be such that for some
n\in\mathbf{M}
,
n\models_{1}\Gamma
. Then, as
\mathfrak{S}
is strongly granular (and hence granular at the first component)
n\models_{1}\{\gamma\}
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. So, by the above argument
n\models_{2}\{\gamma\}
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Then again, as
\mathfrak{S}
is strongly granular (and hence granular at the second component)
n\models_{2}\Gamma
. Hence,
\models_{1}\,\subseteq\,\models_{2}
.
∎

By
Theorem
3.15
every
p
-consequence operator has an adequate functionally
3
-valued canonical semantics. However, not every
p
-type logical structure is inferentially
3
-valued.

Given a logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
,
W
is said to be
idempotent
if
W=W\circ W
. We now show that every logical structure induced by a non-idempotent
p
-consequence operator is inferentially
3
-valued.

Theorem 3.17

(
Inferential Irreducibility Theorem for
p
-type
)
.

Every logical structure induced by a non-idempotent
p
-consequence operator is inferentially 3-valued.

Proof.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a logical structure induced by a non-idempotent
p
-consequence operator. Suppose that it is not inferentially
3
-valued. Then, there exists some functionally
2
-valued canonical semantics for
\mathscr{L}
, say
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S_{p},\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
where
S_{p}=\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\}
and
\models_{1}\,\subseteq\,\models_{2}
, such that
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
.

Using an argument similar to that in
Theorem
3.11
, we can show that for all
m\in\mathbf{M},\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,

m\models_{1}\Gamma\iff m(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}\iff m\models_{2}\Gamma

In other words,
\models_{1}\,=\,\models_{2}
. Let
\models_{1}\,=\,\models_{2}\,=\,\models
.

Now, let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(W(\Sigma))\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)
. Then, there exists
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(W(\Sigma))
such that
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)
. So, there exists
m\in\mathbf{M}
such that
m\models\Sigma
but
m\not\models\{\alpha\}
. Observe that for all
\beta\in W(\Sigma)
,
m\models\{\beta\}
. Consequently, as
\mathfrak{S}
is strongly granular,
m\models W(\Sigma)
. Now, as
m\not\models\{\alpha\}
,
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(W(\Sigma))
—a contradiction. Hence
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(W(\Sigma))\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)
.

Since
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
, this shows that
W(W(\Sigma))\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Now, by the reflexivity of
W
, we have
W(\Sigma)\subseteq W(W(\Sigma))
. This shows that
W(W(\Sigma))=W(\Sigma)
for all
\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
. Thus,
W
is idempotent—a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
s_{\kappa}
-consequence Operators

We now discuss a family of non-reflexive monotonic consequence operators. Given a logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
, a nonempty set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
is said to satisfy
anti-reflexivity
if
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)
, i.e.,
\Gamma\cap W(\Gamma)=\emptyset
. We will say that
(\mathscr{L},W)
is
anti-reflexive
if for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Gamma
satisfies anti-reflexivity. Note that there exists only one monotonic logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
such that
\Gamma
is anti-reflexive for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
. More precisely, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.18
.

A monotonic logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
with
\mathscr{L}\neq\emptyset
is anti-reflexive iff for all nonempty
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
W(\Gamma)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a monotonic logical structure such that for all nonempty
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
W(\Gamma)=\emptyset
. Therefore, for any nonempty
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)=\mathscr{L}\setminus\emptyset=\mathscr{L}
. Consequently,
W
is anti-reflexive.

Conversely, suppose that
(\mathscr{L},W)
is anti-reflexive. Now, as
\mathscr{L}\neq\emptyset
, by anti-reflexivity,
\mathscr{L}\cap W(\mathscr{L})=\emptyset
, which implies
W(\mathscr{L})=\emptyset
. Then, for any nonempty
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\emptyset\subseteq W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\mathscr{L})=\emptyset
, i.e.,
W(\Gamma)=\emptyset
by monotonicity and we are done.
∎

The above theorem shows that the concept of ‘global’ anti-reflexivity in the presence of monotonicity is too strong. This, however, is not the case for non-monotonic logical structures (see, e.g.,
[
4

). This is also not the case if one considers certain ‘relativised’ versions of anti-reflexivity.

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be an
s^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
-type logical structure where
\mathscr{L}\neq\emptyset
. Then, for all
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
, the following statements hold.

(1)

For all
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma))
. The reverse inclusion holds if
\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)\in\mathsf{K}
as well.

(2)

If
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
is such that both
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
and
\mathscr{L}\setminus\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
, then
W(\Gamma)\cap W(\mathscr{L}\setminus\Gamma)=\emptyset
.

(3)

For all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
and
\Sigma\in\mathsf{K}
such that
\Gamma\cap\Sigma\neq\emptyset
,
\Gamma\not\subseteq W(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

(1) Let
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
. Then
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)
by the
\text{anti-reflexivity}^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
of
W
. So, by monotonicity,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma))
.

Now, suppose that
\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)\in\mathsf{K}
as well. Then, for any
\alpha\notin W(\Gamma)
,
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)
, which implies that
\alpha\notin W(\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma))
as
W
is
\text{anti-reflexive}^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
. Thus,
W(\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma))\subseteq W(\Gamma)
.

(2) Let
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
such that
\mathscr{L}\setminus\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
. Then, for any
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
,

\displaystyle\alpha\notin\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\implies\alpha\notin\Gamma

\displaystyle(\text{since}~W~\text{is}~\text{anti-reflexive}^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa})

\displaystyle\implies\alpha\in\mathscr{L}\setminus\Gamma

\displaystyle\implies\alpha\in\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\mathscr{L}\setminus\Gamma)

\displaystyle(\text{since}~\mathscr{L}\setminus\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}~\text{and}~W~\text{is}~\text{anti-reflexive}^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa})

\displaystyle\implies\alpha\notin W(\mathscr{L}\setminus\Gamma)

Thus,
W(\Gamma)\cap W(\mathscr{L}\setminus\Gamma)=\emptyset
.

(3) Suppose there exist
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
and
\Sigma\in\mathsf{K}
with
\Gamma\cap\Sigma\neq\emptyset
such that
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Now, since

is
\text{anti-reflexive}^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
, we have
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus\Sigma
, which implies that
\Gamma\cap\Sigma=\emptyset
—a contradiction. Hence
\Gamma\not\subseteq W(\Sigma)
.
∎

Example 3.23
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a logical structure, where
\mathscr{L}=\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
and
W:\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
is defined as follows.

W(\Gamma)=\begin{cases}\{(n,m):(m,n)\in\Gamma\}&\text{if}~\Gamma\neq\emptyset\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for any
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
.

Let
\Phi=\{(2n+1,2n+2):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
\mathsf{K}=\mathcal{P}(\Phi)\setminus\{\emptyset\}
. It is easy to see that
\mathsf{K}
is internally 1. We now claim that for all
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
,
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)
.

Suppose the contrary, i.e., there exists
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
such that
\Gamma\not\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)
. Let
(a,b)\in\Gamma\cap W(\Gamma)
. Now,
(a,b)\in W(\Gamma)
implies that
(b,a)\in\Gamma\subseteq\Phi
—an impossibility! Thus,
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
.

Hence
(\mathscr{L},W)
is
\text{anti-reflexive}^{\mathsf{K}}_{1}
.

Next, let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
. If
\Gamma=\emptyset
, then
W(\Gamma)=\emptyset
and hence
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
.

Suppose that
\Gamma\neq\emptyset
. So
W(\Gamma)\neq\emptyset
. Then,

\displaystyle(n,m)\in W(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\implies(m,n)\in\Gamma

\displaystyle\text{(by the definition of}~W)

\displaystyle\implies(m,n)\in\Sigma

\displaystyle\text{(since}~\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma)

\displaystyle\implies(n,m)\in W(\Sigma)

\displaystyle\text{(by the definition of}~W)

So
W
is monotonic as well. Thus,
W
is an
s^{\mathsf{K}}_{1}
-consequence operator.

We now show that given any infinite set
\mathscr{L}
and
0<\lambda<|\mathscr{L}|
, there exist a logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
and
\emptyset\subsetneq\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
such that
W
is an
s^{\mathsf{K}}_{\lambda}
-consequence operator. But for this, we need the following result about cardinal numbers.

Theorem 3.24
.

Given a set
X
and cardinal
\kappa
such that
|X|=\kappa
, the following statement holds for all
\lambda\leq\kappa
: there exists
Y\subseteq X
such that
|Y|=\lambda
.

Example 3.25
.

Let
\kappa
be an infinite cardinal and
\mathscr{L}
a set such that
|\mathscr{L}|=\kappa
.

Then,
|\mathscr{L}\times\{0\}|=|\mathscr{L}\times\{1\}|=\kappa
. So, there exists a bijection
\sigma:\mathscr{L}\to(\mathscr{L}\times\{0\})\cup(\mathscr{L}\times\{1\})
. Let
X=\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{L}\times\{0\})
and
Y=\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{L}\times\{1\})
. Now,

X\cap Y=\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{L}\times\{0\})\cap\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{L}\times\{1\})=\sigma^{-1}((\mathscr{L}\times\{0\})\cap(\mathscr{L}\times\{1\}))=\sigma^{-1}(\emptyset)=\emptyset

and

X\cup Y=\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{L}\times\{0\})\cup\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{L}\times\{1\})=\sigma^{-1}((\mathscr{L}\times\{0\})\cup(\mathscr{L}\times\{1\}))=\mathscr{L}.

Moreover,
|X|=|Y|=\kappa
.

We now define
f:\mathscr{L}\to\mathscr{L}
as follows.

f(z)=\begin{cases}\sigma^{-1}(a,1)&\text{if}~\sigma(z)=(a,0)\\
\sigma^{-1}(a,0)&\text{if}~\sigma(z)=(a,1)\end{cases}

Well-definedness of
f
follows from the fact that
\sigma
is a bijection. Moreover, note that
f(X)=f(\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{L}\times\{0\}))=\sigma^{-1}(\mathscr{L}\times\{1\})=Y
.

Let us now choose any
0<\lambda<\kappa
and define
W_{\lambda}:\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
as follows.

W_{\lambda}(\Gamma)=\begin{cases}f(\Gamma)&\text{if}~|\Gamma|\geq\lambda\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Finally, let
\mathsf{K}=\{\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}:|\Gamma|\geq\lambda~\text{and}~(\Gamma\subseteq X~\text{or}~\Gamma\subseteq Y)\}
and consider the logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W_{\lambda})
.

We note that since
\lambda<\kappa
and
|X|=\kappa
, by
Theorem
3.24
, there exists
\Delta\subseteq X
such that
|\Delta|=\lambda
. Thus,
\Delta\in\mathsf{K}
. Moreover, for every
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
,
|\Gamma|\geq\lambda
. Therefore,
\mathsf{K}
is internally
\lambda
.

We now claim that for all
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
,
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W_{\lambda}(\Gamma)
. Let
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
. Then,
|\Gamma|\geq\lambda
and
\Gamma\subseteq X
or
\Gamma\subseteq Y
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
\Gamma\subseteq X
. Then,
W_{\lambda}(\Gamma)=f(\Gamma)\subseteq Y
. Now, as
X\cap Y=\emptyset
,
\Gamma\cap W_{\lambda}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
, i.e.,
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W_{\lambda}(\Gamma)
. Thus
W_{\lambda}
is
\text{anti-reflexive}^{\mathsf{K}}_{\lambda}
.

Next, let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
. If
|\Gamma|<\lambda
then
W_{\lambda}(\Gamma)=\emptyset
which implies
W_{\lambda}(\Gamma)\subseteq W_{\lambda}(\Sigma)
. If, on the other hand,
|\Gamma|\geq\lambda
, then as
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
,
|\Sigma|\geq\lambda
as well. So,

W_{\lambda}(\Gamma)=f(\Gamma)\subseteq f(\Sigma)=W_{\lambda}(\Sigma)

Therefore
W_{\lambda}
is monotonic. Consequently,
W_{\lambda}
is an
s^{\mathsf{K}}_{\lambda}
-consequence operator.

.

Let

D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\emptyset
. Let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma
is nontrivial and
\Gamma\cap W(\Gamma)\neq\emptyset
(such a
\Gamma
exists since
W
is nontrivially non-anti-reflexive). Let
\alpha\in\Gamma\cap W(\Gamma)
. Since
\Gamma
is nontrivial,
W(\Gamma)\neq\mathscr{L}
; so there exists
\beta\in\mathscr{L}
such that
\beta\notin W(\Gamma)
. As
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
, this in particular implies that for some
m\in\mathbf{M}
,
m\models_{1}\Gamma
, i.e.,
m(\Gamma)\subseteq D_{1}
. Then, as
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\emptyset
,
m(\alpha)\notin D_{2}
, i.e.,
m\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. In other words,
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)=W(\Gamma)
—a contradiction. So,
D_{1}\cap D_{2}\neq\emptyset
.

If
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\{1,2\}=D_{1}\cup D_{2}
, then
D_{1}=D_{2}
, contrary to our earlier conclusions that
D_{1}\not\subseteq D_{2}
and
D_{2}\not\subseteq D_{1}
. Therefore,
D_{1}\cap D_{2}\subsetneq\{1,2\}
. Without loss of generality, let us assume that
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\{1\}
. Now, as
D_{1}\not\subseteq D_{2}
, there exists
a\in D_{1}
such that
a\notin D_{2}
. Consequently,
a=2
. So,
D_{1}=\{1,2\}
. Then, as
D_{1}\cup D_{2}\subsetneq\{0,1,2\}
,
D_{2}=\{1\}
—a contradiction, since
D_{2}\not\subseteq D_{1}
. Hence,
W
is inferentially 4-valued.
∎

3.4.
r_{\kappa}
-consequence Operators

In this section, we identify another class of inferentially 4-valued monotonic logical structures different from those induced by
s_{\kappa}
-consequence operators for a given cardinal
\kappa>0
. More specifically, for each cardinal
\kappa>0
, we now discuss another class of logical structures that is disjoint from the one induced by
s_{\kappa}
-consequence operators.

Suppose
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
where
S=\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\}
is a strongly granular semantics for
\mathscr{L}
such that
(\mathscr{L},W_{\mathfrak{S}})
is an inferentially
4
-valued monotonic logical structure and
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is not an
s_{\kappa}
-consequence operator. We know from
Theorem
3.9
,
Theorem
3.15
that every
q
-,
p
-type logical structure is functionally
3
-valued and hence cannot be inferentially
4
-valued. To sum up,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
cannot be a
q
-,
p
- or
s_{\kappa}
-consequence operator.

Now, by
Theorem
3.8
, if
\models_{2}\,\subseteq\,\models_{1}
, then
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is a
q
-consequence operator. Similarly, if
\models_{1}\,\subseteq\,\models_{2}
, then by
Theorem
3.14

W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is a
p
-consequence operator. Therefore, since
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is not a
q
- or
p
-consequence operator,
\models_{1}
and
\models_{2}
must be incomparable.

Moreover, if there exists
\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
that is internally
\kappa
for some cardinal
\kappa
such that for all
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
with
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
and
\alpha\in\Gamma
,
\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap\,(\mathbf{M}\times\{\Gamma\}))\not\subseteq\,\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap\,(\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\alpha\}\}))
, then by
Theorem
3.29
,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
would be an
s_{\kappa}
-consequence operator. Therefore, since
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is not an
s_{\kappa}
-consequence operator, for all
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
with
|\Gamma|\geq\kappa
there exists
\alpha\in\Gamma
such that
\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap\,(\mathbf{M}\times\{\Gamma\}))\subseteq\,\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap\,(\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\alpha\}\}))
.

Now, let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a monotonic logical structure which is inferentially
4
-valued. Then, by
Theorem
3.3
there exists a strongly granular semantics
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
for
\mathscr{L}
such that
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
. So, by the above discussion,
W
is not a
q
-,
p
- or
s_{\kappa}
-consequence operator.

(a)

Since
W
is not a
q
-consequence operator, there exists
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
W(\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma))\neq W(\Gamma)
. However, since
W
is monotonic, this inequality holds only if
W(\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma))\subsetneq W(\Gamma)
. This, however, would imply that
W
satisfies strong non-quasi-closure.

(b)

Since
W
is monotonic, but not a
p
-consequence operator, it is not reflexive. So, there exists
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\not\subseteq W(\Gamma)
.

(c)

Since
W
is monotonic, but not an
s_{\kappa}
-consequence operator, it is not
\text{anti-reflexive}^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
for any
\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
that is internally
\kappa
. This is the same as saying that for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
with
|\Gamma|\geq\kappa
,
\Gamma\not\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)
.

The above discussion motivates the following definition of
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operators.

Definition 3.32

(
r_{\kappa}
-consequence Operator
)
.

Given a cardinal
\kappa
, an
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operator on
\mathscr{L}
is a function
W:\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
satisfying the following properties.

(i)

There exists
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\not\subseteq W(\Gamma)
. (Non-reflexivity)

(ii)

There exists
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
W(\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma))\not\subseteq W(\Gamma)
. (Strong non-quasi-closure)

(iii)

For all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
, with
|\Gamma|\geq\kappa
,
\Gamma\not\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)
. (
\text{Non-anti-reflexivity}_{\kappa}
)

(iv)

For all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
, if
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
then
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. (Monotonicity)

A logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
is said to be of
r_{\kappa}\emph{-type}
if
W
is an
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operator on
\mathscr{L}
.

Theorem 3.33
.

There exists no logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
such that
W
is an
r_{1}
-consequence operator.

Proof.

Suppose the contrary. Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a logical structure such that
W
is an
r_{1}
-consequence operator. Since
W
is non-reflexive, there exists
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\not\subseteq W(\Gamma)
. Therefore, there exists
\alpha\in\Gamma
such that
\alpha\notin W(\Gamma)
. Since
W
is monotonic, this implies that
\alpha\notin W(\{\alpha\})
as well. However,
\alpha\notin W(\{\alpha\})
implies that
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\{\alpha\})
, i.e.,
\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\{\alpha\})
, contradicting the fact that
W
satisfies
\text{non-anti-reflexivity}_{1}
. Thus
(\mathscr{L},W)
cannot be of
r_{1}
-type.
∎

However, there exists an
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operator for any finite
\kappa\geq 2
, as shown in the following example.

Example 3.34
.

Suppose
\mathscr{L}
is an infinite set and
\kappa\geq 2
is a finite cardinal. Let
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}
and
W:\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
be defined as follows.

W(\Gamma)=\begin{cases}\{\alpha\}&\text{if}~|\Gamma|<\kappa\\
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

We note that, since
\mathscr{L}
is infinite, there exists
\beta\in\mathscr{L}
such that
\alpha\neq\beta
. Now, since
|\{\beta\}|=1<\kappa
,
W(\{\beta\})=\{\alpha\}
. Then, as
\{\beta\}\not\subseteq W(\{\beta\})=\{\alpha\}
,
W
is non-reflexive. Now, choose any
|\Sigma|=\kappa-1<\kappa
such that
\alpha\notin\Sigma
. Then,
W(W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma)=W(\Sigma\cup\{\alpha\})=\Sigma\cup\{\alpha\}\not\subseteq\{\alpha\}=W(\Sigma)
. Therefore,
W
satisfies strong non-quasi-closure as well.

Next, let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
|\Gamma|\geq\kappa
. Then,
W(\Gamma)=\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}
and hence
\Gamma\not\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)
. Thus
W
satisfies
\text{non-anti-reflexivity}_{\kappa}
.

Finally, let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
. If
|\Gamma|\geq\kappa
, then as
\Sigma\supseteq\Gamma
,
|\Sigma|\geq\kappa
as well. So,
W(\Gamma)=\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\Sigma\cup\{\alpha\}=W(\Sigma)
. If, on the other hand,
|\Gamma|<\kappa
, then
W(\Gamma)=\{\alpha\}
. Now, irrespective of
\Sigma
,
\{\alpha\}\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Thus, in all cases
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
and so
W
is an
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operator.

Remark 3.35
.

It is worth pointing out that the
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operator
W
in the above example is
not
an
r_{\lambda}
-consequence operator for any finite
\lambda
such that
2\leq\lambda<\kappa
. To see this, let
\lambda
be a cardinal such that
2\leq\lambda<\kappa
and
\Gamma_{\lambda}
a set of cardinality
\lambda
such that
\alpha\notin\Gamma_{\lambda}
. The existence of such a
\Gamma_{\lambda}
can be justified by considering any set
\Gamma
of cardinality
\lambda
,
\beta\notin\Gamma
(such a
\beta
exists since
\mathscr{L}
is infinite but
\Gamma
is finite) and defining

\Gamma_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}\Gamma&\text{if}~\alpha\notin\Gamma\\
(\Gamma\setminus\{\alpha\})\cup\{\beta\}&\text{else}\end{cases}

Then, as
|\Gamma_{\lambda}|=\lambda<\kappa
,
W(\Gamma_{\lambda})=\{\alpha\}
, which implies that
\Gamma_{\lambda}\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma_{\lambda})=\mathscr{L}\setminus\{\alpha\}
. This shows that
W
does not satisfy
\text{non-anti-reflexivity}_{\lambda}
. Thus,
W
is not an
r_{\lambda}
-consequence operator on
\mathscr{L}
for any cardinal
\lambda
such that
2\leq\lambda<\kappa
. One might compare this with
Example
3.25
.

Theorem 3.36

(
Representation Theorem for
r_{\kappa}
-type
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S_{r_{\kappa}},\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
be a strongly granular semantics for
\mathscr{L}
. Let
S_{r_{\kappa}}=\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\}
be such that,

(1)

\models_{1}\,\not\subseteq\,\models_{2}
,

(2)

there exists
\Sigma\cup\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\Sigma\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta)\cup\Delta
but
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta)
,

(3)

for all
\Lambda\subseteq\mathscr{L}
with
|\Lambda|\geq\kappa
there exists
\lambda\in\Lambda
such that
\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap\,\mathbf{M}\times\{\Lambda\})\subseteq\,\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap\,\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\lambda\}\})
.

Then,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is an
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operator.

Proof.

Since
\mathfrak{S}
is strongly granular, by
Theorem
3.1
,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is monotonic.

Since
\models_{1}\,\not\subseteq\,\models_{2}
, there exist
m_{\Gamma}\in\mathbf{M}
and
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
m_{\Gamma}\models_{1}\Gamma
but
m_{\Gamma}\not\models_{2}\Gamma
. Since
\mathfrak{S}
is strongly granular, hence granular at the second component, there exists
\beta\in\Gamma
such that
m_{\Gamma}\not\models_{2}\{\beta\}
. So, as
m_{\Gamma}\models_{1}\Gamma
,
\beta\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
, i.e.,
\Gamma\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Therefore,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is non-reflexive.

Next, let us choose any
\Sigma\cup\Delta\subseteq\mathscr{L}
satisfying (2). Then,
\Sigma\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta)\cup\Delta
but
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta)
. So, there exists
\alpha\in\mathscr{L}
such that
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)
but
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta)
. Thus, there exists
n\in\mathbf{M}
such that
n\models_{1}\Delta
and
n\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
, but for all
m\in\mathbf{M}
either
m\not\models_{1}\Sigma
or
m\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. We note that
\Sigma\not\subseteq\Delta
because otherwise, since
n\models_{1}\Delta
, we would have
n\models_{1}\Sigma
. This, coupled with the fact that
n\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
, would give us
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)
—a contradiction. Now, as
\Sigma\not\subseteq\Delta
, there exists
\sigma\in\Sigma
such that
\sigma\notin\Delta
. However, since
\Sigma\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta)\cup\Delta
,
\sigma\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta)
. By monotonicity and (2),
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta\cup W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta))
but
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Sigma)\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta)
; hence
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta\cup W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta))\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Delta)
. Therefore,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
satisfies strong non-quasi-closure as well.

Finally, let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
|\Lambda|\geq\kappa
. Then, by (3), there exists
\lambda\in\Lambda
such that
\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap\,(\mathbf{M}\times\{\Lambda\}))\,\subseteq\,\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap\,(\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\lambda\}\}))
. We choose any such
\lambda
.

If there exists no
m\in\mathbf{M}
such that
m\models_{1}\Lambda
, then
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Lambda)=\mathscr{L}
. Therefore, clearly,
\Lambda\not\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Lambda)
. If, on the other hand, there exists
m\in\mathbf{M}
such that
m\models_{1}\Lambda
, then since
\pi_{1}(\models_{1}\cap\,(\mathbf{M}\times\{\Lambda\}))\,\subseteq\,\pi_{1}(\models_{2}\cap\,(\mathbf{M}\times\{\{\lambda\}\}))
, we have
m\models_{2}\{\lambda\}
. Thus,
\lambda\in\Lambda\cap W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Lambda)
and hence
\Lambda\not\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Lambda)
. Therefore, in either case,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
satisfies
\text{non-anti-reflexivity}_{\kappa}
as well.

Consequently,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}
is an
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operator.∎

Given a logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
,
W
is said to be
\emph{non-trivial}_{\kappa}
if for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
with
|\Gamma|\geq\kappa
,
W(\Gamma)\neq\mathscr{L}
. We now show that every logical structure induced by a
\text{non-trivial}_{\kappa}

r_{\kappa}
consequence operator is inferentially 4-valued.

Theorem 3.37

(
Inferential Irreducibility Theorem for
r_{\kappa}
-type
)
.

Every logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
induced by a
\text{non-trivial}_{\kappa}

r_{\kappa}
consequence operator, where with
|\mathscr{L}|\geq k>0
, is inferentially 4-valued.

Proof.

We first note that every
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operator has a functionally
4
-valued characterisation by
Theorem
3.3
. Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a logical structure induced by an
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operator. Suppose that it is not inferentially
4
-valued. So, there exists some functionally
2
-valued or
3
-valued canonical semantics for
\mathscr{L}
, say
\mathfrak{S}=(\textbf{M},\models_{1},\models_{2},S_{r_{\kappa}},\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
such that
W=W_{\mathfrak{S}}
and
S_{r_{\kappa}}=\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\}
.

If
\mathfrak{S}
is functionally
2
-valued, then along the same lines as in the proof of
Theorem
3.11
, we are done. We skip this and move on to the case where
\mathfrak{S}
is functionally
3
-valued. In this case, without loss of generality, we may assume that
\mathbf{M}\subseteq\{0,1,2\}^{\mathscr{L}}
.

Then, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, there exists
\emptyset\subsetneq D_{i}\subsetneq\{0,1,2\}
such that
D_{1}\cup D_{2}\subsetneq\{0,1,2\}
and for all
m\in\mathbf{M}
,
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
m\models_{i}\Gamma
iff
m(\Gamma)\subseteq D_{i}
. If
D_{1}\cup D_{2}
is a singleton set, then using arguments similar to the one in the proof of
Theorem
3.11
, we are done. Without loss of generality, let
D_{1}\cup D_{2}=\{1,2\}
. Now,
D_{1}\subseteq D_{2}
implies that
\models_{1}\subseteq\models_{2}
, which by
Theorem
3.14
implies that
W
is a
p
-consequence operator. Similarly,
D_{2}\subseteq D_{1}
implies that
\models_{2}\subseteq\models_{1}
, which by
Theorem
3.8
implies that
W
is a
q
-consequence operator. Therefore,
D_{1}\not\subseteq D_{2}
and
D_{2}\not\subseteq D_{1}
as
W
is neither a
p
- nor
q
-consequence operator.

Either
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\emptyset
or
D_{1}\cap D_{2}\neq\emptyset
. Suppose
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\emptyset
. Let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
|\Gamma|\geq\kappa
and
\alpha\in\Gamma
. Now, let
m\in\mathbf{M}
such that
m\models_{1}\Gamma
, i.e.,
m(\Gamma)\subseteq D_{1}
(such an
m
exists since
W
is
\text{non-trivial}_{\kappa}
). Then, as
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\emptyset
,
m(\alpha)\notin D_{2}
, i.e.,
m\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. In other words,
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Thus
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
for all
|\Gamma|\geq\kappa
, which implies that
W
is
\text{anti-reflexive}^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
where
\mathsf{K}=\{\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}:|\Gamma|\geq\kappa\}
. This is a contradiction to the assumption that
W
is an
r_{\kappa}
-consequence operator. So,
D_{1}\cap D_{2}\neq\emptyset
.

If
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\{1,2\}=D_{1}\cup D_{2}
, then
D_{1}=D_{2}
, contrary to our earlier conclusions that
D_{1}\not\subseteq D_{2}
and
D_{2}\not\subseteq D_{1}
. Therefore,
D_{1}\cap D_{2}\subsetneq\{1,2\}
. Without loss of generality, let us assume that
D_{1}\cap D_{2}=\{1\}
. Now, as
D_{1}\not\subseteq D_{2}
, there exists
a\in D_{1}
such that
a\notin D_{2}
. Again, without loss of generality, let
a=2
. So,
D_{1}=\{1,2\}
. Then, as
D_{1}\cup D_{2}\subsetneq\{0,1,2\}
,
D_{2}=\{1\}
—a contradiction, since
D_{2}\not\subseteq D_{1}
. Thus
W\neq W_{\mathfrak{S}}
. Hence
W
is inferentially 4-valued.
∎

Corollary 3.38

(
Inferential Characterisation of the Class of Monotonic Logical Structures
)
.

The class of monotonic logical structures is inferentially 4-valued.

4.
Suszko Reduction: A Generalised Perspective

In this section, we propose a different notion of semantics than the one in
Definition
2.1
and show that every logical structure has an adequate 2-valued semantics of this kind.

Definition 4.1

(
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
)
.

Given a set
\mathscr{L}
, a
Suszko semantics for
\mathscr{L}
or
\mathtt{S}
-semantics for
\mathscr{L}
, say
\mathsf{S}
, is a tuple
(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
satisfying the following properties.

(i)

\mathbf{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}\times\{0,1\})
.

(ii)

R\subseteq\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{B}
.

(iii)

\models\,\subseteq\mathbf{B}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
.

A
witness function on
\mathsf{S}
is a function
f:\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\to R
.

Definition 4.2

(
\mathtt{S}
-entailment Relations
)
.

Given an
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
, the
type-I
\mathtt{S}
-entailment relation on
\mathscr{L}
, denoted by
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is defined as follows:

\Gamma\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}\alpha\iff~\text{for all}~(v,w)\in R,~v\models\Gamma~\text{implies that}~w\models\{\alpha\}

The corresponding consequence operator is denoted by
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
. The
type-II
\mathtt{S}
-entailment relation on
\mathscr{L}
relative to the witness function
f
on
\mathsf{S}
, denoted by
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}^{\mathsf{S}}_{II_{f}}
, is defined as follows.

\displaystyle\Gamma\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}^{\mathsf{S}}_{II_{f}}\alpha\iff

\displaystyle~\text{there exists}~U\subseteq R~\text{with}~|U|>1~\text{such that}~f(\Gamma)\in U

\displaystyle\text{and}~\text{for all}~(v,w)\in U,v\models\Gamma~\text{implies that}~w\models\{\alpha\}

The corresponding consequence operator is denoted by
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{II_{f}}
.

Definition 4.3

(
Adequate
\mathtt{S}
-semantics, Bivalent Logical Structure
)
.

Given a logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
and an
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
\mathsf{S}
for
\mathscr{L}
,
(\mathscr{L},W)
is said to have an
adequate type-I
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
(respectively,
adequate type-II
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
) if
W=W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
(respectively, if
W=W^{\mathsf{S}}_{II_{f}}
, for some witness function
f
on
\mathsf{S}
). We call a logical structure
bivalent
if it is adequate with respect to type-I (or type-II)
\mathtt{S}
-semantics.

Remark 4.4
.

The name ‘Suszko semantics’ has already been used in
[
21
, p. 384]
. We quote the relevant passage below. Our definition, however, is more general than the one given below.

Suszko and his followers admit as such an
arbitrary
family of
arbitrary
functions from the set of propositional formulas to the set of logical values. This last set can be identified with the set
2=\{0,1\}
, where
0
represents the value
\mathsf{false}
and
1
represents the value
\mathsf{true}
. Such a semantics can be called a
Suszko semantics
\ldots

Remark 4.5
.

Let
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
be an
\mathtt{S}
-semantics for a set
\mathscr{L}
such that
\mathbf{B}=\emptyset
. If there exists
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
, then there exists
(v,w)\in R
such that
v\models\Gamma
but
w\not\models\{\alpha\}
. This, however, is a contradiction since
R=\emptyset
as
\emptyset\subseteq R\subseteq\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{B}=\emptyset\times\emptyset=\emptyset
. Hence,
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)=\mathscr{L}
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
.

Theorem 4.6

(
Suszko Reduction for Logical Structures
)
.

Every logical structure has an adequate type-I
\mathtt{S}
-semantics.

Proof.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a logical structure. By
Theorem
2.8
, it has an adequate semantics
\mathfrak{S}=(\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}),\models_{1},\models_{2},S,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
where
S=\{(\models_{1},\models_{2})\}
and for all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Sigma\models_{1}\Gamma
iff
\Sigma=\Gamma
as well as
\Sigma\models_{2}\Gamma
iff
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma)
.

If
\mathscr{L}=\emptyset
, then we consider the following
\mathtt{S}
-semantics:
(\{\emptyset\},\emptyset,\models,\mathcal{P}(\emptyset))
. So, it is enough to deal with the case when
\mathscr{L}\neq\emptyset
.

We begin by observing that
|\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\times\{1,2\}|\leq|\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}\times\{0,1\})|
. So, there exists
f:\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\times\{1,2\}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}\times\{0,1\})
such that
f
is an injection. For each
(\Sigma,i)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\times\{1,2\}
we abbreviate
f(\Sigma,i)
as
v^{\Sigma}_{i}
.

We now define an
\mathtt{S}
-semantics for
\mathscr{L}
,
\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{S}}=(\textbf{B}^{\mathfrak{S}},R^{\mathfrak{S}},\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
satisfying the following properties.

\bullet

\mathbf{B}^{\mathfrak{S}}=\{v^{\Sigma}_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}\times\{0,1\}):(\Sigma,i)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\times\{1,2\}\}
.

\bullet

R^{\mathfrak{S}}=\{(v^{\Sigma}_{1},v^{\Sigma}_{2}):\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}
.

\bullet

For all
(\Sigma,i)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\times\{1,2\}
,
v^{\Sigma}_{i}\models\Gamma
iff
\Sigma\models_{i}\Gamma
.

We claim that
W_{\mathfrak{S}}=W^{\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{S}}}_{I}
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
be such that
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{S}}}_{I}(\Gamma)
. Then, there exists
\alpha\in W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{S}}}_{I}(\Gamma)
. So, there exists
(v^{\Sigma}_{1},v^{\Sigma}_{2})\in R^{\mathfrak{S}}
such that
v^{\Sigma}_{1}\models\Gamma
, but
v^{\Sigma}_{2}\not\models\{\alpha\}
. Now,
v^{\Sigma}_{1}\models\Gamma
implies that
\Sigma\models_{1}\Gamma
and
v^{\Sigma}_{2}\not\models\{\alpha\}
implies that
\Sigma\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. In other words,
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
—a contradiction. Thus
W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{S}}}_{I}(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
.

Next, let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
be such that
W^{\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{S}}}_{I}(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. Then there exists
\alpha\in W^{\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{S}}}_{I}(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
. So, there exists
\Sigma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
such that
\Sigma\models_{1}\Gamma
, but
\Sigma\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
. Now,
\Sigma\models_{1}\Gamma
implies that
v^{\Sigma}_{1}\models\Gamma
and
\Sigma\not\models_{2}\{\alpha\}
implies that
v^{\Sigma}_{2}\not\models\{\alpha\}
; in other words, there exists
(v^{\Sigma}_{1},v^{\Sigma}_{2})\in R^{\mathfrak{S}}
such that
v^{\Sigma}_{1}\models\Gamma
but
v^{\Sigma}_{2}\not\models\{\alpha\}
. Consequently,
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{S}}}_{I}(\Gamma)
—a contradiction. Thus
W^{\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{S}}}_{I}(\Gamma)\subseteq W_{\mathfrak{S}}(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
. Therefore,
W_{\mathfrak{S}}=W^{\mathsf{S}^{\mathfrak{S}}}_{I}
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

If
\mathscr{L}
is infinite, then in the above argument
\mathbf{B}^{\mathfrak{S}}
can be taken to be a subset of
\{0,1\}^{\mathscr{L}}
since
|\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}\times\{0,1\})|=|\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})\times\{1,2\}|
.

Remark 4.8
.

Since, by
Theorem
4.6
, every logical structure has an adequate type-I
\mathtt{S}
-semantics, according to
Definition
4.3
, every logical structure is bivalent. We will come back to this, probably surprising, observation later in
Section
5
.

4.1.
Suszko Reduction for Monotonic Logical Structures

While
Theorem
4.6
proves that every logical structure is bivalent, the resulting
\mathtt{S}
-semantics depends
only
on the canonical semantics of the corresponding logical structure. For example, in the case of monotonic logical structures, induced by a strongly granular semantics, the resulting
\mathtt{S}
-semantics does not reflect any property similar to that of ‘(strong) granularity’. This results in a loss of important semantic information, which might be desirable sometimes. In view of this, we now introduce a particular type of
\mathtt{S}
-semantics that fulfils this requirement.

Definition 4.9

(
Atomic
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
)
.

Given a set
\mathscr{L}
and an
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
for
\mathscr{L}
, it is said to be
atomic
if for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
and
v\in\mathbf{B}
,
v\models\Gamma
iff
v\models\{\beta\}
for all
\beta\in\Gamma
.

Remark 4.10
.

Given a set
\mathscr{L}
and an atomic
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
for
\mathscr{L}
, one can construct the following
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
\mathsf{S}_{2}=(\mathbf{B}_{2},R_{2},\models_{2},\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
for
\mathscr{L}
such that the following conditions hold.

\bullet

\mathbf{B}_{2}=\{\nu_{v}:v\in\mathbf{B}\}
where
\nu_{v}:\mathscr{L}\to\{0,1\}
is defined as follows.

\nu_{v}(\beta)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}~v\models\{\beta\}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\bullet

R_{2}=\{(\nu_{v},\nu_{w}):(v,w)\in R\}
.

\bullet

For all
\nu_{v}\in\mathbf{B}_{2}
,
\nu_{v}\models_{2}\Gamma
iff
\nu_{v}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
.

Note that,

\displaystyle\nu_{v}\models_{2}\Gamma

\displaystyle\iff\nu_{v}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}

\displaystyle\iff\nu_{v}(\beta)=1

\displaystyle\text{for all}~\beta\in\Gamma

\displaystyle\iff v\models\{\beta\}

\displaystyle\text{for all}~\beta\in\Gamma

\displaystyle\iff v\models\Gamma

\displaystyle\text{since}~\mathsf{S}~\text{is atomic}

One can then easily show that
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}=W^{\mathsf{S}_{2}}_{I}
.

Due to this, we will henceforth consider only those (atomic)
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
for which
\mathbf{B}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathscr{L}}
and
v\models\Gamma
iff
v(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
. This gives rise to the following definition.

Definition 4.11

(
Normal

S
-semantics
)
.

An
\mathtt{S}
-semantics
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
for a set
\mathscr{L}
is said to be
normal
if for all
v\in\mathbf{B}
and
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
v\models\Gamma
iff
v(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
.

In the following two theorems, we show that every monotonic logical structure has an adequate normal type-I
\mathtt{S}
-semantics.

Theorem 4.12

(
Suszko Reduction for Monotonic Logical Structures (Part I)
)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a set and
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
a normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics for
\mathscr{L}
. Then
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is monotonic.

Proof.

Suppose the contrary. Let
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)
. So, there exists
\alpha\in W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)
. Then, there exists
(v,w)\in R
such that
v\models\Sigma
but
w\not\models\{\alpha\}
, i.e.,
v(\Sigma)\subseteq\{1\}
but
w(\alpha)\neq 1
. Now, as
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
,
v(\Sigma)\subseteq\{1\}
implies that
v(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
. Then, since
w(\alpha)\neq 1
,
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
—a contradiction. Hence,
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is monotonic.
∎

Theorem 4.13

(
Suszko Reduction for Monotonic Logical Structures (Part II)
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a monotonic logical structure and
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R_{m},\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
be a normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics for
\mathscr{L}
where

\mathbf{B}=\{\chi_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}

and

R_{m}=\{(\chi_{\Gamma},\chi_{W(\Gamma)}):\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}

Then,
W=W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
. Here
\chi_{\Gamma}:\mathscr{L}\to\{0,1\}
is defined as follows.

\chi_{\Gamma}(\beta)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}~\beta\in\Gamma\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
be such that
W(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
. Then, there exists
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
. So, there exists
(\chi_{\Sigma},\chi_{W(\Sigma)})\in R_{m}
such that
\chi_{\Sigma}\models\Gamma
but
\chi_{W(\Sigma)}\not\models\{\alpha\}
, i.e.,
\chi_{\Sigma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
but
\chi_{W(\Sigma)}(\alpha)\neq 1
. Now,
\chi_{\Sigma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
implies that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
and
\chi_{W(\Sigma)}(\alpha)\neq 1
implies that
\alpha\notin W(\Sigma)
. But, since
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
and
W
is monotonic,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Then, as
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
,
\alpha\in W(\Sigma)
—a contradiction. Thus
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
.

Next, let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W(\Gamma)
. Then, there exists
\alpha\in W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W(\Gamma)
. Now
(\chi_{\Gamma},\chi_{W(\Gamma)})\in R_{m}
,
\chi_{\Gamma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
but
\chi_{W(\Gamma)}(\alpha)\neq 1
. So,
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
—a contradiction. Consequently,
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
. Hence
W=W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
.
∎

Since every monotonic logical structure has an adequate type-I normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics, any
q
-,
p
-,
s_{\kappa}
- or
r_{\kappa}
-type logical structure has an adequate type-I normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics. For
q
-,
p
- and
s_{\kappa}
-type logical structures, however, the elements of
\mathbf{B}
and
R
satisfy some additional properties, as demonstrated in the following theorems.

Theorem 4.14

(
Suszko Reduction for
q
-type (Part I)
)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a set and
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
be a normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics such that,

(v,w)\in R~\text{iff for all}~\alpha\in\mathscr{L},w(\alpha)=1~\text{implies that}~v(\alpha)=1

Then,
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is a
q
-consequence operator.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
be such that
\Gamma\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
but
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)
. So, there exists
\alpha\in W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)
. Then, there exists
(v,w)\in R
such that
v(\Sigma)\subseteq\{1\}
but
w(\alpha)\neq 1
.

If
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)=\emptyset
then
\Gamma\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
implies that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
. Consequently, as
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is monotonic (by
Theorem
4.12
), we have
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)
.

Suppose now that
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)\neq\emptyset
and let
\beta\in W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)
. Then, as
v(\Sigma)\subseteq\{1\}
and
(v,w)\in R
,
w(\beta)=1
. Now, by the definition of
R
, since
(v,w)\in R
and
w(\beta)=1
, it follows that
v(\beta)=1
as well. Consequently,
v(W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma)\subseteq\{1\}
. Since
\Gamma\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
,
v(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
as well. Therefore, as
w(\alpha)\neq 1
,
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
—a contradiction. Thus
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)
. Hence for all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Gamma\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
implies that
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)
. So, by
Theorem
3.7
(5)
,
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is a
q
-consequence operator.
∎

Theorem 4.15

(
Suszko Reduction for
q
-type (Part II)
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a
q
-type logical structure and
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R_{q},\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
be a normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics for
\mathscr{L}
, where

\mathbf{B}=\{\chi_{W(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma}:\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}\cup\{\chi_{W(\Gamma)}:\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}

and

R_{q}=\{(\chi_{W(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma},\chi_{W(\Gamma)}):\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}

Then,
W=W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
.
\chi_{\Gamma}
’s are defined as in
Theorem
4.13
.

Proof.

Suppose
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
W(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
. So, there exists
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
. Now, as
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
, there exists
(\chi_{W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma},\chi_{W(\Sigma)})\in R_{q}
such that
\chi_{W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
but
\chi_{W(\Sigma)}(\alpha)\neq 1
. Now,
\chi_{W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
implies that
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
, while
\chi_{W(\Sigma)}(\alpha)\neq 1
implies that
\alpha\notin W(\Sigma)
. Since
\Gamma\subseteq W(\Sigma)\cup\Sigma
and
W
is a
q
-consequence operator, by
Theorem
3.7
(5)
,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Then, as
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
,
\alpha\in W(\Sigma)
—a contradiction. Thus,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
.

Next, let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
and
\alpha\in W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
. Then,
(\chi_{W(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma},\chi_{W(\Gamma)})\in R_{q}
and
\chi_{W(\Gamma)\cup\Gamma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
. Since
\alpha\in W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
,
\chi_{W(\Gamma)}(\alpha)=1
, i.e.,
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
. Thus
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
. Hence
W=W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
.
∎

Theorem 4.16

(
Suszko Reduction for
p
-type (Part I)
)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a set and
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
a normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics such that,

(v,w)\in R~\text{iff for all}~\alpha\in\mathscr{L},v(\alpha)=1~\text{implies that}~w(\alpha)=1

Then,
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is a
p
-consequence operator.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
be such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
but
\Gamma\cup W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)
. So, there exists
\alpha\in\Gamma\cup W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Sigma)
. Then, there exists
(v,w)\in R
such that
v(\Sigma)\subseteq\{1\}
but
w(\alpha)\neq 1
.

Since
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
and
v(\Sigma)\subseteq\{1\}
,
v(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
as well. Also, since
w(\alpha)\neq 1
,
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
. So,
\alpha\in\Gamma
.
Then
v(\alpha)=1
, since
v(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
. Now, by the definition of
R
, since
(v,w)\in R
,
v(\alpha)=1
implies that
w(\alpha)=1
—a contradiction. Hence for all
\Gamma\cup\Sigma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
implies that
\Gamma\cup W_{I}^{\mathsf{S}}(\Gamma)\subseteq W_{I}^{\mathsf{S}}(\Sigma)
. So, by
Theorem
3.13
,
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is a
p
-consequence operator.
∎

Theorem 4.17

(
Suszko Reduction for
p
-type (Part II)
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be a
p
-type logical structure and
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R_{p},\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
a normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics for
\mathscr{L}
, where

\mathbf{B}=\{\chi_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}\cup\{\chi_{\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)}:\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}

and

R_{p}=\{(\chi_{\Gamma},\chi_{\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)}):\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\}

Then,
W=W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
be such that
W(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
. Then, there exists
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
. So, there exists
(\chi_{\Sigma},\chi_{\Sigma\cup W(\Sigma)})\in R_{p}
such that
\chi_{\Sigma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
but
\chi_{\Sigma\cup W(\Sigma)}(\alpha)\neq 1
. But,
\chi_{\Sigma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
implies that
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
, while
\chi_{\Sigma\cup W(\Sigma)}(\alpha)\neq 1
implies that
\alpha\notin\Sigma\cup W(\Sigma)
and so
\alpha\notin W(\Sigma)
. Since
\Gamma\subseteq\Sigma
and
W
is a
p
-consequence operator, by
Theorem
3.13
,
\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Sigma)
. Then, as
\alpha\notin W(\Sigma)
,
\alpha\notin\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)
, which implies that
\alpha\notin W(\Gamma)
—a contradiction. Thus,
W(\Gamma)\subseteq W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
.

Next, let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
such that
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\not\subseteq W(\Gamma)
. Then, there exists
\alpha\in W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
such that
\alpha\notin W(\Gamma)
. Since
W
is a
p
-consequence operator and hence reflexive,
\alpha\notin W(\Gamma)
implies that
\alpha\notin\Gamma
as well. Thus,
\alpha\notin\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)
.

Now,
(\chi_{\Gamma},\chi_{\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)})\in R_{p}
,
\chi_{\Gamma}(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
but
\chi_{\Gamma\cup W(\Gamma)}(\alpha)\neq 1
. Hence,
\alpha\notin W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
—a contradiction. Thus,
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)\subseteq W(\Gamma)
for all
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}
. Hence
W=W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
.
∎

Theorem 4.18

(
Suszko Reduction for
s^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
-type (Part I)
)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a set and
\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L})
be internally
\kappa
for some cardinal
\kappa
. Let
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R,\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
be a normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics such that for each
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
with
\alpha\in\Gamma
and
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
, there exists
(v,w)\in R
such that
v(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
but
w(\alpha)\notin\{1\}
. Then
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is an
s^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
-consequence operator.

Proof.

We first note that
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is monotonic, by
Theorem
4.12
.

Now, let
\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}
,
\alpha\in\Gamma
and
(v,w)\in R
such that
v(\Gamma)\subseteq\{1\}
and
w(\alpha)\neq 1
(such a

, which implies that

. Hence,
\Gamma\subseteq\mathscr{L}\setminus W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}(\Gamma)
for all

. Thus,
W^{\mathsf{S}}_{I}
is
\text{anti-reflexive}^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
and hence an

-consequence operator.
∎

Theorem 4.19

(
Suszko Reduction for
s^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
-type (Part II)
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{L},W)
be an
s^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}
-type logical structure and
\mathsf{S}=(\mathbf{B},R_{s^{\mathsf{K}}_{\kappa}},\models,\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}))
a normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics for
\mathscr{L}
satisfying the following conditions.

\bullet

\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}_{1}\cup\mathbf{B}_{2}\cup\mathbf{B}_{3}\cup\mathbf{B}_{4}
, where

\mathbf{B}_{1}=\{\chi_{\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)}:\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}~\text{and}~\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)\in\mathsf{K}\}

\mathbf{B}_{2}=\{\chi_{W(\Gamma)}:\Gamma\in\mathsf{K}~\text{and}~\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)\in\mathsf{K}\}

\mathbf{B}_{3}=\{\chi_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\notin\mathsf{K}~\text{or}~\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)\notin\mathsf{K}\}

\mathbf{B}_{4}=\{\chi_{W(\Gamma)}:\Gamma\notin\mathsf{K}~\text{or}~\mathscr{L}\setminus W(\Gamma)\notin\mathsf{K}\}

\bullet

What is a Many-valued Logical Structure?

A
many-valued logic
is usually thought to be a logic which does not have an adequate bivalent semantics. The term ‘bivalent semantics’ is generally understood via the notion of logical matrix (see, e.g.,
[
31
, p. 30]
). However, as we have pointed out in
Section
2
, there is no
formal
definition of bivalent semantics. The definition based on logical matrices is problematic, as pointed out in
[
19
, p. 283]
.

[T]his characterization is purely matrical and there are several reasons to wish not to be blocked within matrix theory. Firstly, because the intuitive idea of many-valuedness does not necessarily depend on matrix theory and secondly, because the rise of two-valued non-matrical semantics has shed a new light on the problem
\ldots
.

This problem is perhaps of even greater significance while attempting to define many-valued
logical structures
. In fact, one might argue that there is no
a priori
reason to be ‘confined’ by
any
particular definition of many-valued logical structures based on a specific notion of semantics, including those proposed in
Definition
2.1
or
Definition
4.1
.

However, if we are willing to maintain such a relativistic perspective, we have to accept that different definitions of semantics will lead to different conclusions regarding
\mathbf{ST}
(cf. e.g.,
Theorem
3.11
and
Theorem
4.15
) and hence, potentially different, mutually incomparable definitions of many-valued logical structures.

Although this might be mathematically enriching, it is perhaps unsatisfactory from a philosophical perspective because such an approach fails to elucidate the intuition behind ‘many-valuedness’. Consequently, one cannot help but wonder whether something more fundamental is hidden underneath this potential menagerie of notions. The issue of many-valuedness is perhaps intimately connected with the
principle of bivalence
. We expound on this connection in the rest of this section.

The following statements are two very general formulations of the principle of bivalence in the context of logical structures.

\mathbf{PoB}^{P}_{1}:
There is a property
P
such that for any logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
exactly one of the following holds:

(a)

(\mathscr{L},W)
satisfies
P
.

(b)

(\mathscr{L},W)
does not satisfy
P
.

\mathbf{PoB}^{P}_{2}:
For any logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
, there is a property
P
such that exactly one of the following statements holds:

(a)

(\mathscr{L},W)
satisfies
P
.

(b)

(\mathscr{L},W)
does not satisfy
P
.

One needs to be a bit careful regarding the precise notion of ‘property’ here. One way to interpret
ST
is to view it as a statement about properties preserved in a valid logical inference. This can be phrased as follows:
there is a property
Q
that is preserved from the premise set(s) to the conclusion(s) in a valid logical inference
. A more precise version of this statement in the context of logical structures would be the following:
there is a property
Q
such that for any logical structure
(\mathscr{L},W)
and
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
, the following statement holds: if
\alpha\in W(\Gamma)
and
\Gamma
satisfies
Q
then so does
\alpha
. In other words, every logically valid inference is
Q
-preserving; the importance of
ST
lies in the fact that it declares
Q
to be
truth
.
9
9

9

This is more or less the analysis of
[
46

. However, whereas Wansing and Shramko argue for the

, we argue for the

Notice that any such property

(\mathscr{L},W)

Henceforth, whenever we refer to
\mathbf{PoB}^{P}_{1}

\overline{Q}

Now, if one subscribes to the

—the view that in logical inferences, ‘truth’ is necessarily preserved from the premise(s) to the conclusion(s)—and maintains that there is a generic notion of ‘truth’, then

can be seen as a principle of bivalence of the form

has an adequate bivalent semantics, then every element of

is either designated or anti-designated (but not both). Now, if we identify the (metalogical) property of ‘designation’ with ‘truth’ (as Suszko also does), we obtain

. This is explained in more detail in the following passage from
[
42

-

[T]o identify a property with truth, at the bare minimum, it must be possible to treat such a property as a semantic value of sentences. This is a reasonable assumption, since truth and falsehood are not just semantic properties, but the semantic properties
par excellence
of sentences.
If a property could not be treated as a value of sentences in a suitable semantics, then an identification with truth would be prevented. Suszko’s theorem fills this possible gap between designation and truth by showing that designation can actually be turned into a semantic (namely algebraic) value.
Clearly, the mere possibility of treating a property as a semantic referent of a suitable semantic is not sufficient to conclude that it represents truth. After all, a semantic value could also stand for other semantic properties, like, for instance, falsehood. Suszko’s theorem alone only shows that designation can be identified with a semantic value, but it does not show that it is a semantic value for truth. This, however, is a little step easily covered once it is accepted that validity consists in necessary
truth
preservation. If valid inferences are those necessarily preserving truth, and designation is the value preserved in valid inferences, then designation and truth must be identical.
That validity consists in truth preservation is a reasonable philosophical assumption whose legitimacy Suszko accepts without further inquiry, and that it is also assumed in the truth pluralist debate. The crucial and critical step, thus, is exactly the one handled by Suszko’s theorem, which shows that designation can be turned into a semantic (algebraic) value of an adequate semantics.

This identification, however, is not maintained by every
truth pluralist

A

is a person who believes ‘that sentences belonging to different areas of discourse can be and in some cases are, true in different ways’
[

even if they believe in the truth-theoretic account of logical validity. The reason behind this is explained succinctly in the following passage
[

The reason is that the notion of designation, unproblematic as it might look, is troublesome in the context of truth pluralism. It is not hard to see why. The definition of validity is made possible by having at disposal a set of designated values that allows to speak of a sentence having an unspecified designated value.
\ldots
It thus follows that having a designated value is equivalent to
[\ldots]
being true in some way. Since this seems to amount to a generic notion of truth, designation arguably carries with it a commitment to a generic property of truth. Consequently, if truth pluralists reject generic truth, the notion of designation is anathema to them.

However, not committing to a truth-theoretic account of validity is not going to help if one is a pluralist relative to some other property
P
as well. Moreover, not being a pluralist relative to

Nevertheless, one can maintain

without any qualms since in this case the property

depends on the logical structure under consideration. More precisely, given two properties,
P

We note the following passage from

-

To make the point more vivid, and clearly see why the theorem is crucial to defend the philosophical thesis, suppose that Suszko’s theorem did not go through, and designation could not be turned into an algebraic value of a suitable semantics. In this case, two ingredients would be disentangled in the formulation of a many-valued logic: a level of semantic properties (like truth, falsehood, and possibly others) represented by algebraic values and a level of logical notions employed to define validity (like designation and non-designation). Algebraic values would correspond to properties of sentences that characterise their semantic status. Logical notions, by contrast, would not characterise any semantic feature of sentences, but just display their logical relations. While certainly connected, logic and semantics would be taken apart. In particular, logical notions like designation would not be, even implicitly, semantic values.
Designation, far from being a value standing for a semantic property on its own, would just be a metalogical notion employed to characterise what relations among semantic values take place in valid inferences.
In such a case, the philosophical claim that every logic is logically two-
valued
would vanish. First of all, with the reduction neutralised, the semantic values transmitted in valid inferences could only be those in the many-valued semantics, because there would not be other values at all. Strictly speaking, designation could not be a preserved value, because it could not even be a value. Secondly, given that designation could not be a value, let alone the preserved value, it could not be identified with truth. Thirdly, given that designation would not stand for a value,
logical bivalence
(reflected in the bivalent distinction between designated and non-designated) would not collapse into semantic
two-valuedness
. Many-valued logic would be authentically semantically many-valued and truth one of the semantic values—and indeed the preserved value—despite logical validity being defined in bivalent terms by means of bivalent metatheoretical notions. In this way, the threat to many-valued logic would be thwarted.

Suszko’s Theorem (Suszko Reduction) thus shows us that the metalogical property of designation can be ‘expressed’ in the object language. The fact that there can only be two possibilities, i.e., designation and non-designation, is thus crucial to Suszko Reduction. However, this dichotomy is nothing but a metalinguistic assumption about designation. This point has been made, e.g., in
[
21

.

One of the ways to understand Suszko’s distinction between “algebraic values” and “logical values” is to interpret the latter as
the truth values of the metatheory
, while the former would be
the truth values of the theory
.

In other words,

can be seen as a pointer to the bivalence of the metatheory. Now, what happens if the metatheory is

). We, therefore, propose a notion of many-valued logical structures via many-valued
\models_{i}
’s, similar to what has been done in GCT. More specifically, we define the following.

Definition 5.1

(
\kappa
-valued Semantics of Order
\lambda
)
.

Given a set
\mathscr{L}
, a cardinal
\kappa>0
and an ordinal
\lambda>0
, a
\kappa
-valued semantics of order
\lambda
for a family of sets
\{\mathscr{L}_{i}\}_{i\in\lambda}
is a tuple of the following form

\mathfrak{S}=(\{\mathbf{M}_{i}\}_{i\in\lambda},\{\models_{i}\}_{i\in\lambda},\{A_{i}\}_{i\in\lambda},S,\{\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}_{i})\}_{i\in\lambda},\{f_{[i,j]}\}_{(i,j)\in\lambda\times\lambda})

satisfying the following conditions.

(i)

\mathscr{L}_{0}=\mathscr{L}
.

(ii)

For each
(i,j)\in\lambda\times\lambda,f_{[i,j]}:\mathscr{L}_{i}\to\mathscr{L}_{j}
is an injective function.

(iii)

For each
i\in\lambda
,
\mathbf{M}_{i},A_{i}
are sets,
|A_{i}|\geq\kappa
and
\models_{i}:\mathbf{M}_{i}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}_{i})\to A_{i}
.

(iv)

There exists
i\in\lambda
such that
|A_{i}|=\kappa
.

(v)

\emptyset\subsetneq S\subseteq\{((\models_{i},a_{i}),(\models_{j},a_{j})):(a_{i},a_{j})\in A_{i}\times A_{j}\}
.

Given a
\kappa
-valued semantics of order
\lambda
for a family of sets
\{\mathscr{L}_{i}\}_{i\in\lambda}
,
the entailment relation induced by
\mathfrak{S}
, denoted by
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}^{(\kappa,\lambda)}_{\mathfrak{S}}
is defined as follows. For all
\Gamma\cup\{\alpha\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}^{(\kappa,\lambda)}_{\mathfrak{S}}\alpha\iff

\displaystyle~\text{for all}~((\models_{i},a_{i}),(\models_{j},a_{j}))\in S~\text{and}~(m_{i},m_{j})\in\mathbf{M}_{i}\times\mathbf{M}_{j},

\displaystyle\models_{i}(m_{i},f_{[0,i]}\Gamma)=a_{i}~\text{implies that}~\models_{j}(m_{j},\{f_{[0,j]}(\alpha)\})=a_{j}

We now define a many-valued logical structure as follows.

Definition 5.2

(
\kappa
-valued Logical Structure of Order
\lambda
)
.

Suppose
\kappa>0
is a cardinal,
\lambda>0
is an ordinal,
\{(\mathscr{L}_{i},\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{i})\}_{i\in\lambda}
is a family of logical structures and

\mathfrak{S}=(\{\mathbf{M}_{i}\}_{i\in\lambda},\{\models_{i}\}_{i\in\lambda},\{A_{i}\}_{i\in\lambda},S,\{\mathcal{P}(\mathscr{L}_{i})\}_{i\in\lambda},\{f_{[i,j]}\}_{(i,j)\in\lambda\times\lambda})

is a
\kappa
-valued semantics of order
\lambda
for
\{\mathscr{L}_{i}\}_{i\in\lambda}
relative to
\{f_{[i,j]}\}_{(i,j)\in\lambda\times\lambda}
. A logical structure
(\mathscr{L},\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-})
is said to be
\kappa
-valued of order
\lambda
for
\{(\mathscr{L}_{i},\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{i})\}_{i\in\lambda}
relative to

\mathfrak{S}
if the following conditions hold.

(i)

\mathscr{L}_{0}=\mathscr{L}
.

(ii)

\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}\,=\,\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}^{(\kappa,\lambda)}_{\mathfrak{S}}
.

Remark 5.3
.

It is imperative to emphasise that even though we defined
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}^{(\kappa,\lambda)}_{\mathfrak{S}}
in a specific manner, other definitions are possible. Thus, a definition of
\kappa
-valued logical structure depends on how
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}^{(\kappa,\lambda)}_{\mathfrak{S}}
is defined.

Remark 5.4
.

(a)

The key idea behind the above definition is the observation of the distinction between object and metalanguage and the resulting hierarchy of languages (see, e.g.,
[
27
]
). This is the main reason for considering a family of logical structures
\{(\mathscr{L}_{i},\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-}_{i})\}_{i\in\lambda}
. This family is indexed via ordinals so as to introduce an ordering among the
\mathscr{L}_{i}
’s in such a way that for all
i\in\lambda
,
\mathscr{L}_{i+1}
can be thought of as a metalanguage of
\mathscr{L}_{i}
.

(b)

To distinguish between the
use
and
mention
of a word or symbol, we sometimes put quotation marks around that symbol whenever we mention it. In the context of (formal) object language and (formal) metalanguage, this means that for every formula
\alpha
of the object language, ‘
\alpha
’ is a constant symbol of the metalanguage (see,
[
16
, Footnote 136]
and
[
15
, Section 6.2]
). This gives rise to a natural injective function from the object language to the metalanguage under consideration.
Definition
5.1
(ii)
has been formulated with this issue in mind.

(c)

Each
\mathbf{M}_{i}
can be thought of as a ‘class of models’ of the language
\mathscr{L}_{i}
. Similarly,
\models_{i}
’s can be thought of as ‘satisfaction relations’ between the models and the corresponding languages.

(d)

The motivations for
Definition
5.2
(ii)
and
Definition
5.2
(iii)
are similar to those in
Remark
2.13
.

Since this hierarchy of languages is not unique for a given (object) language, the crux of all these discussions is that
many-valuedness of a logical structure is not dependent on the logical structure alone but also on the language/metalanguage hierarchy.
Hence, interpreting ‘logical value’ as truth-values of the metatheory/metalogic makes us wonder about the reason for ignoring the truth-values of the higher order metatheories/metalogics. A justification for this, however, can be provided by observing that in most cases, it suffices to consider the metametalogic to be a fragment of classical propositional logic, as e.g., in
[
15
]
. Nevertheless, there is no
a priori
reason for not considering a many-valued metametalogic and continuing in this line of argument, we can conclude that there is no reason to consider
any one
of the higher metalogics to be two-valued.

This motivates us to propose the following generalisations of
\mathbf{ST}
.

\mathbf{ST}_{1}:

Let
(\mathscr{L},\mathrel{|}\joinrel\mkern-0.5mu\mathrel{-})
be a logical structure which is

\mathbf{ST}_{2}:

Let

be a logical structure which is

6.
Looking into the Future

The main contributions of this article to the study of
\mathbf{ST}
, in particular and to the study of many-valued logics, in general, are as follows.

\bullet

A rather generalised notion of semantics has been proposed. We have also shown that every logical structure has a canonical adequate semantics (
Theorem
2.8
).

\bullet

A precise definition of inferentially
\kappa
-valued logical structures has been given. We have identified the subclasses of
q
- and
p
-type which are inferentially
3
-valued (see
Theorem
3.11
and
Theorem
3.17
for details) and proved that the class of logical structures induced by monotonic consequence operators is inferentially
4
-valued (
Corollary
3.38
).

\bullet

We have generalised Suszko Reduction, introduced the notion of
\mathtt{S}
-semantics and using this notion, shown that every logical structure has an adequate bivalent semantics (
Theorem
4.6
). We then focused on a particular type of
\mathtt{S}
-semantics—normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics—and showed that all monotonic logical structures, as well as all non-monotonic logical structures satisfying cautious monotonicity or weak cumulative transitivity, have adequate normal
\mathtt{S}
-semantics (see
Theorem
4.13
,
Theorem
4.22
and
Theorem
4.23
respectively). The adequacy theorems for non-monotonic logical structures are, according to us, one of the most important contributions of this section.

\bullet

An explicit definition of ‘
\kappa
-valued logical structure’ (
Definition
5.2
) and generalised versions of
\mathbf{ST}
are discussed.

There are, however, several questions which need to be investigated further. These are left for future work. We list some of these below.

1.

In
Theorem
4.22
(respectively,
Theorem
4.23
) we have shown that every logical structure satisfying cautious monotonicity (respectively, weak cumulative transitivity) has an adequate type-II normal
\mathtt{S}

\mathtt{S}
-entailment)?

2.

Given an ordinal
\lambda

3.

Is it possible to give examples of

5.

Even though the existence of an adequate bivalent semantics for a given logical structure is philosophically interesting, it has been pointed out in
[

to

Therefore, for a given logical structure, there are reasons for
not
wanting an adequate bivalent semantics. But then, what kind of many-valued semantics are we looking for? Although the answer to this question is subjective, applications of

investigations of this problem would help us in coming up with different notions of many-valuedness that are also practically relevant.

6.

As we have noted above, every monotonic logical structure is inferentially 4-valued. However, if one considers logical structures which are of
p
-type (i.e. reflexive and monotonic), it is inferentially 3-valued; while a Tarski-type logical structure is inferentially 2-valued. It would be interesting to have a more detailed idea of the relationships between the properties of logical structures and their inferential values. For example, we know that both
q
-type and
p
-type logical structures are inferentially 3-valued. Even though reflexivity and quasi-closure are two very different properties, why, when combined with monotonicity, do they give rise to the same inferentially valued logics? Does this imply that the nature of these inferential values differs, even though they are the same in number?

In conclusion, it seems to us that the fundamental problem in the theory of many-valued logic(al structure)s is not a justification (or falsification) of
ST
because there are several, often mutually incomparable, interpretations of ‘logical value’. Each of them is philosophically interesting and important. The more important problem, perhaps, is to accommodate these different viewpoints in a coherent whole. Mathematically, this means providing a framework within which each such conception has a natural habitat; while, philosophically, this means having an understanding of the reasons for which they qualify as justifiable interpretations of ‘logical value’. If we think of Logic as ‘the science of logical values’ (see
[
40
, Section 1.5]
for details), then this would also help us in having a clearer understanding of what Logic is all about.
