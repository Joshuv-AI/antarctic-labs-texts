Symmetry-adapted single crystal yield criterion for non-Schmid materials
Roman Gröger

Abstract
All yield criteria that determine the onset of plastic deformation in crystalline materials must be invariant under the inversion symmetry associated with a simultaneous change of sign of the slip direction and the slip plane normal. We demonstrate the consequences of this symmetry on the functional form of the effective stress, where only the lowest order terms that obey this symmetry are retained. A particular form of yield criterion is obtained for materials that do not obey the Schmid law, hereafter called non-Schmid materials. Application of this model to body-centered cubic and hexagonal close-packed metals shows under which conditions the non-Schmid stress terms become significant in predicting the onset of yielding. In the special case, where the contributions of all non-Schmid stresses vanish, this model reduces to the maximum shear stress theory of Tresca.

Introduction

Yield criteria play indispensable roles in identifying the stress states that cause the initiation of elastic-plastic deformation
(
Hill 1998
)
. At the single-crystal level, the onset of plastic deformation is associated with the stress state that activates dislocation glide on the most highly stressed slip system compatible with the underlying crystallographic space group.
Schmid and Boas 1950
postulated that slip on a particular crystallographic system commences when the shear stress parallel to the slip direction acting in the slip plane reaches its critical value. This argument constitutes a central point in the maximum shear stress theory developed already by
Tresca 1864
. Almost a century ago, Taylor and Elam
(
Taylor and Elam 1926
;
Taylor 1928
)
pointed out that single crystals of
\alpha
-Fe and
\beta
-CuZn do not behave according to the rules established by the Schmid law. Since then, the pool of materials for which the Schmid law is not capable of correctly predicting the initiation of plastic flow grew steadily to include body-centered cubic (bcc) metals
(
Christian 1983
;
Duesbery 1984
;
Ito and Vitek 2001
)
, B2 and L1
2
intermetallics
(
Paidar et al. 1984
)
,
CuZnAl

(
Alkan et al. 2018
)
, single crystal Ni-base superalloys
(
Tinga et al. 2010
)
, NiTi shape memory alloys
(
Alkan et al. 2017
)
and Inconel 718 steel
(
Ghorbanpour et al. 2017
)
. It is now widely accepted that the main distinguishing factor between the yielding of close-packed and non-close-packed metals are non-planar cores of screw dislocations in the latter structures
(
Ito and Vitek 2001
;
Bassani et al. 2001
;
Vitek and Paidar 2008
)
. Moreover, the widespread observation of the breakdown of the Schmid law implies that Tresca’s model of yielding is a special case of a more general yield criterion.

The importance of non-Schmid stresses was first recognized by
Asaro and Rice 1977
on the basis of their finite deformation studies of strain localization in ductile crystals. They observed the formation of shear bands that resulted from stress bifurcations, which were attributed to micromechanical processes such as cross-slip of screw dislocations. If the crystal deforms by multiple slip, lattice rotation plays an important role in the localization process
(
Dao and Asaro 1993
)
. In order to quantify the effect of non-Schmid stresses on the initiation of elastic-plastic deformation,
Qin and Bassani 1992
proposed a simple yield criterion

\tau^{*\alpha}(\bm{m}^{\alpha},\bm{n}^{\alpha}|\bm{\sigma})\leq\tau^{*}_{cr}\ ,

(1)

where the effective stress
\tau^{*\alpha}
, corresponding to the slip system
\alpha
, was written as a linear combination of several components of the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
with coefficients representing the relative importance of each stress. The work of
Duesbery 1984
,
Ito and Vitek 2001
, as well as our more recent contribution
(
Gröger 2014
;
Gröger and Vitek 2019
)
, have demonstrated that the relevant stress components are primarily shear stresses acting both parallel and perpendicular to the slip direction. To incorporate these effects, the effective stress assigned to the slip system
\alpha
was expressed using stress components resolved in two orthogonal systems mutually rotated by
60^{\circ}
in the zone of the slip direction
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
(see Fig.
1

\bm{n}^{\alpha}
, whereas the so-called non-glide plane in the second (auxiliary) system has normal

\tau^{*\alpha}=\sigma_{nm}^{\alpha}+a_{1}\sigma_{n^{\prime}m}^{\alpha}+a_{2}\sigma_{tn}^{\alpha}+a_{3}\sigma_{t^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\ ,

(2)

where

\sigma_{tn}=\bm{\sigma}:[(\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha}\times\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha})\otimes\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha}]
,
\sigma_{t^{\prime}n^{\prime}}=\bm{\sigma}:[(\hat{\bm{n}}^{\prime\alpha}\times\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha})\otimes\hat{\bm{n}}^{\prime\alpha}]
shear stresses perpendicular to the slip direction, all resolved in the black and blue coordinate frames in Fig.
1
(a). Hereafter, overhat represents a unit vector, colon the double-dot product (which yields a scalar), and the symbol

The effective yield criterion should not only capture the results of atomistic simulations and experiments, but the symmetry of the effective stress must be compatible with crystallography. To be more specific, consider an arbitrary slip system
\alpha
in bcc crystals that is characterized by two vectors:
\bm{n}^{\alpha}
representing the normal of the slip plane, and
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
that is parallel to the slip direction. The two possible choices for the orientation of the normal of the slip plane imply that the Burgers circuit can be made around the
same
dislocation in two
opposite
directions. For example, the
(\bar{1}01)[111]
system can be described by two pairs of vectors
(\bm{m}^{\alpha},\bm{n}^{\alpha})
: (i)
\bm{m}^{\alpha}=[111]
and
\bm{n}^{\alpha}=[\bar{1}01]
, or (ii)
\bm{m}^{\alpha}=[\bar{1}\bar{1}\bar{1}]
and
\bm{n}^{\alpha}=[10\bar{1}]
. These two choices lead to opposite slip directions
\pm\bm{m}^{\alpha}
and thus to opposite directions of the Burgers vector of the dislocation,
\pm\bm{b}=\pm\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha}b
, where
b
is the magnitude of the Burgers vector, and
\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha}
is a unit vector parallel to the slip direction. However,
the Burgers vector is a topological quantity and does not depend on the choice of the coordinate frame
. In the spirit of Neumann’s principle
(
Neumann 1885
)
, we thus demand that any physical quantity involving the slip system
\alpha
must be invariant under a
simultaneous
change of sign of the vectors
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
and
\bm{n}^{\alpha}
. Therefore, the effective stresses involved in all single crystal yield criteria must obey the inversion symmetry

\tau^{*\alpha}(\bm{m}^{\alpha},\bm{n}^{\alpha}|\bm{\sigma})=\tau^{*\alpha}(-\bm{m}^{\alpha},-\bm{n}^{\alpha}|\bm{\sigma})\ .

(3)

It can be easily shown that Eq. (
3
) is a statement of invariance of
\tau^{*\alpha}
under rigid-body rotation. In Fig.
1
, we define the orientation of an arbitrary slip system
\alpha
by three lattice vectors
\bm{t}^{\alpha}
,
\bm{n}^{\alpha}
,
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
, all written relative to the fixed frame. Assume that the effective stress for this system is
\tau^{*\alpha}(\bm{m}^{\alpha},\bm{n}^{\alpha}|\bm{\sigma})
. Now, carry out a rigid-body rotation of the crystal by 180
∘
around the axis
\bm{t}^{\alpha}
, which brings
\bm{m}^{\alpha}\rightarrow-\bm{m}^{\alpha}
and
\bm{n}^{\alpha}\rightarrow-\bm{n}^{\alpha}
. For this new orientation, the effective stress is
\tau^{*\alpha}(-\bm{m}^{\alpha},-\bm{n}^{\alpha}|\bm{\sigma})
. Obviously, in non-polar crystal structures, rigid-body rotations by
180^{\circ}
are symmetry operations and all physical quantities must be invariant under this transformation, which proves the validity of Eq. (
3
). It is evident from (
2
) that only the Schmid stress
\sigma_{nm}
in the first term obeys the symmetry (
3
). However, this symmetry is broken by the shear stress perpendicular to the slip direction,
\sigma_{tn}
, which changes sign under the transformation
(\bm{m}^{\alpha},\bm{n}^{\alpha})\rightarrow(-\bm{m}^{\alpha},-\bm{n}^{\alpha})
. The symmetry (
3
) is broken also for the remaining two stress terms, but this matter requires a deeper analysis that will be made in the next Section.

Despite the fact that the yield criterion (
1
) with the effective stress defined by (
2
) incorporates correctly the effect of non-Schmid stresses, it does not obey the fundamental inversion symmetry (
3
) that is dictated by crystallography. When using these yield criteria, only certain combinations of (
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
,
\bm{n}^{\alpha}
) are allowed for which the coefficients of the yield criterion were determined from atomistic simulations or from experiments. However, the equivalent pairs
(-\bm{m}^{\alpha},-\bm{n}^{\alpha})
must be avoided, because they would result in different
\tau^{*\alpha}
for crystallographically identical systems. To remove this drawback and to avoid ambiguity when predicting the onset of slip activity, we first derive a general form of the effective stress as a power series expansion in terms of all components of the stress tensor and investigate which stress terms are allowed by the symmetry (
3
). The obtained yield criterion is then applied to materials of cubic and hexagonal symmetry to predict the primary and secondary slip systems for a few combinations of their parameters.

2
Symmetry-adapted effective stress

The stress state at a point of an elastic body is characterized by six components of a symmetric stress tensor. An infinite number of possibilities exist to combine these stress components to arrive at a scalar quantity (hereafter called as an effective stress), which can be used in the yield criteria to determine whether the material is locally in the state of purely elastic or elastic-plastic deformation. The simplest representation of this effective stress, due to Tresca, is based on the validity of the Schmid law. In the following, our objective
is to generalize this formulation to all crystalline materials in which the onset of plastic deformation depends also on other (non-Schmid) stresses.

2.1
Nonlinear effective stress

We begin by writing the effective stress (
\tau^{*}
) as a weighted linear combination of six stress functions
f_{ij}(\sigma_{ij})
,

\tau^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=i}^{3}c_{ij}f_{ij}(\sigma_{ij})\ .

(4)

Each function
f_{ij}(\sigma_{ij})
will be defined by its Maclaurin series as
f_{ij}(\sigma_{ij})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(1/n!)\sigma_{ij}^{n}f_{ij}^{(n)}(0)
, where the superscript
(n)
represents the
n
-th derivative with respect to
\sigma_{ij}
. To simplify the notation, we introduce a new set of coefficients
c_{ijn}=c_{ij}(1/n!)f_{ij}^{(n)}(0)
. Incorporating the last two expressions into (
4
) then yields an equivalent representation of the effective stress,

\tau^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=i}^{3}(c_{ij0}+c_{ij1}\sigma_{ij}+c_{ij2}\sigma_{ij}^{2}+\dots)\ ,

(5)

where
ij=\{tt,tn,tm,nn,nm,mm\}
refer to the three axes in Fig.
1
. We first require that
\tau^{*}=0
for zero applied stress, i.e. when all
\sigma_{ij}=0
. This can be satisfied easily by setting all
c_{ij0}
to zero.

In order to write (
5
) for a particular slip system
\alpha
, we first resolve all stress components
\sigma_{ij}^{\alpha}
using the unit vectors of the slip plane normal
\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha}
and the slip direction
\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha}
corresponding to the system
\alpha
. The vector orthogonal to both is then
\hat{\bm{t}}^{\alpha}=\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha}\times\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha}
. Only the components of
\sigma_{ij}^{\alpha}
that obey the inversion symmetry defined by Eq. (
3
) will be retained. All three normal stresses, i.e.
\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha}=\bm{\sigma}:(\hat{\bm{t}}^{\alpha}\otimes\hat{\bm{t}}^{\alpha})
,
\sigma_{nn}^{\alpha}=\bm{\sigma}:(\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha}\otimes\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha})
, and
\sigma_{mm}^{\alpha}=\bm{\sigma}:(\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha}\otimes\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha})
, and also the shear stress
\sigma_{nm}^{\alpha}=\bm{\sigma}:(\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha}\otimes\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha})
are clearly invariant under this symmetry. To obtain the simplest possible form of the effective stress for the system
\alpha
, we retain in (
5
) only the lowest-order (i.e., linear) terms containing these stresses. The remaining two shear stresses perpendicular to the slip direction are defined as
\sigma_{tm}^{\alpha}=\bm{\sigma}:(\hat{\bm{t}}^{\alpha}\otimes\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha})
and
\sigma_{tn}^{\alpha}=\bm{\sigma}:(\hat{\bm{t}}^{\alpha}\otimes\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha})
. Both these stresses violate the aforementioned inversion symmetry in that they change sign as
(\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha},\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha})\rightarrow(-\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha},-\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha})
. Therefore, in order to comply with (
3
), only even powers of these stresses are allowed in the yield criterion with the lowest order being quadratic. Owing to these arguments, it follows from Eq. (
5
) that the effective stress exerted on any slip system
\alpha
that satisfies the condition (
3
) has the following form:

\tau^{*\alpha}=c_{tt1}\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha}+c_{tn2}(\sigma_{tn}^{\alpha})^{2}+c_{tm2}(\sigma_{tm}^{\alpha})^{2}+c_{nn1}\sigma_{nn}^{\alpha}+c_{nm1}\sigma_{nm}^{\alpha}+c_{mm1}\sigma_{mm}^{\alpha}\ .

(6)

If all non-Schmid contributions to
\tau^{*\alpha}
vanish (i.e., when
c_{tt1}=c_{tn2}=c_{tm2}=c_{nn1}=c_{mm1}=0
),
\tau^{*\alpha}
must equal to the Schmid stress
\sigma_{nm}^{\alpha}
. One possibility

1

Another possibility is to divide both sides of (
6
) by
c_{nm1}
. This would renormalize all remaining coefficients in the right-hand side of (
6
) as well as the effective yield stress
\tau^{*}_{cr}
. However, the form of the effective stress would remain the same.
to satisfy this condition is to take
c_{nm1}=1
. In the other extreme, i.e. when all non-Schmid stresses play role in the initiation of plastic deformation, it is customary to express
\tau^{*\alpha}
in terms of the hydrostatic stress
\sigma_{h}^{\alpha}
. The presence of
\sigma_{h}^{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{mm}^{\alpha}
causes that the two remaining normal stresses (
\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{nn}^{\alpha}
) are not independent. Starting with (
6
), it is possible to rewrite the first, the fourth, and the sixth term using
\sigma_{nn}^{\alpha}-\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha}
,
\sigma_{h}^{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{mm}^{\alpha}
. This results in an equivalent but physically more transparent representation of the effective stress

\tau^{*\alpha}=\sigma_{nm}^{\alpha}+c_{tm2}(\sigma_{tm}^{\alpha})^{2}+a_{2}(\sigma_{nn}^{\alpha}-\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha})+c_{tn2}(\sigma_{tn}^{\alpha})^{2}+a_{4}\sigma_{mm}^{\alpha}+a_{5}\sigma_{h}^{\alpha}\ ,

(7)

where we have introduced new coefficients to replace the linear combinations of
c_{ijn}
. The first term in (
7
) is the resolved Schmid stress for the system
\alpha
. The second term is the shear stress parallel to the slip direction acting in the plane with the normal
\bm{t}^{\alpha}
(this is perpendicular to the slip direction and lies in the slip plane). The third term is a combination of two normal stresses, which generates a
shear
stress parallel to the slip direction in another coordinate system, where the axes
\bm{t}^{\alpha}
and
\bm{n}^{\alpha}
are rotated by
45^{\circ}
in the zone of
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
. The fourth term is directly the shear stress perpendicular to the slip direction. The fifth and the sixth terms incorporate the effects of the normal stress parallel to the dislocation line and of the hydrostatic stress, respectively. A notable feature of (
7
) is that all stress components entering
\tau^{*\alpha}
are resolved in a
single
coordinate system spanned by the vectors
\bm{t}^{\alpha}
,
\bm{n}^{\alpha}
and
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
.

2.2
Linear effective stress using an auxiliary system

Although the form of the effective stress (
7
) obeys the inversion symmetry (
3
), the presence of quadratic terms may lead to a number of complications in homogenizations and developments of plastic flow rules for random and textured polycrystals
(
Dao and Asaro 1993
;
Bassani 1994
)
. In the following, we demonstrate that a form equivalent to (
7
) can be obtained if the power series in (
5
) is terminated
before
the quadratic terms
(\sigma_{tm}^{\alpha})^{2}
and
(\sigma_{tn}^{\alpha})^{2}
. Since the linear terms of these stresses are absent by symmetry, the effective stress reduces to a simple form

\tau^{*\alpha}\approx\sigma_{nm}^{\alpha}+a_{2}(\sigma_{nn}^{\alpha}-\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha})+a_{4}\sigma_{mm}^{\alpha}+a_{5}\sigma_{h}^{\alpha}\ .

(8)

All stresses in this expression are resolved in a single orientation defined by the vectors
\bm{t}^{\alpha}
,
\bm{n}^{\alpha}
and
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
. Therefore, this form cannot capture the variation of the CRSS with the orientation of the maximum resolved shear stress plane (MRSSP) and on the orientation of the shear stress perpendicular to the slip direction, as explained in the Introduction. The simplest way to re-introduce these effects is to write
\tau^{*\alpha}
as a linear superposition of the stresses
\sigma_{nm}^{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{nn}^{\alpha}-\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha}
resolved in
two different
orientations in the zone of the slip direction, which is the same step as taken previously by
Qin and Bassani 1992
to define their effective stress (
2
). This superposition extends (
8
) to

\tau^{*\alpha}=\sigma_{nm}^{\alpha}+a_{1}\sigma_{n^{\prime}m}^{\alpha}+a_{2}(\sigma_{nn}^{\alpha}-\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha})+a_{3}(\sigma_{n^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{\alpha}-\sigma_{t^{\prime}t^{\prime}}^{\alpha})+a_{4}\sigma_{mm}^{\alpha}+a_{5}\sigma_{h}^{\alpha}\ ,

(9)

where we have added the terms with coefficients
a_{1}
and
a_{3}
, in which
\bm{n}^{\prime\alpha}
is the normal of an auxiliary plane in the zone of
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
. The explicit forms of the individual stress components in (
9
) are given in Tab.
3
(column “bcc crystals”). The presence of
\bm{n}^{\prime\alpha}
and
\bm{t}^{\prime\alpha}
requires generalization of the symmetry condition (
3
). We can see from Fig.
1
that a rigid-body rotation of the crystal by
180^{\circ}
about the axis
\bm{t}^{\alpha}
results in the transformations
\bm{m}^{\alpha}\rightarrow-\bm{m}^{\alpha}
,
\bm{n}^{\alpha}\rightarrow-\bm{n}^{\alpha}
and
\bm{n}^{\prime\alpha}\rightarrow-\bm{n}^{\prime\prime\alpha}
. The last transformation involves normals of two different planes, which is a natural consequence of introducing the auxiliary plane. In order for the effective stress (
9
) to be invariant under this transformation, we thus require that it satisfies the symmetry condition

\tau^{*\alpha}(\bm{m}^{\alpha},\bm{n}^{\alpha},\bm{n}^{\prime\alpha}|\bm{\sigma})=\tau^{*\alpha}(-\bm{m}^{\alpha},-\bm{n}^{\alpha},-\bm{n}^{\prime\prime\alpha}|\bm{\sigma})\ .

(10)

Similarly as Eq. (
3
) defines the symmetry of the quadratic form of
\tau^{*\alpha}
in (
7
), Eq. (
10
) defines the symmetry of the physically more appealing linear form of
\tau^{*\alpha}
in (
9
). It is demonstrated in Fig.
1
that both these relations are statements of physical invariance under rigid-body rotations about the axis
\bm{t}^{\alpha}
.

In the previously used effective stress (
2
), the effects of shear stresses perpendicular to the slip direction are incorporated by directly including the
shear stresses

\sigma_{tn}^{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{t^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{\alpha}
. We have shown that these stress terms violate the symmetry represented by (
3
) and (
10
). On the contrary, in the effective stress (
9
), the effects of these shear stresses are incorporated via
differences of normal stresses

\sigma_{nn}-\sigma_{tt}
and
\sigma_{n^{\prime}n^{\prime}}-\sigma_{t^{\prime}t^{\prime}}
. These stresses do not violate the aforementioned symmetries and thus the form (
9
) is consistent with crystallography.

The effective stress (
9
) together with the yield criterion (
1
) constitute the primary result of this paper. All stress components in (
9
) are invariant under the generalized inversion symmetry defined by (
10
). The presence of different auxiliary systems for
(\bm{m}^{\alpha},\bm{n}^{\alpha})
(with the normal
\bm{n}^{\prime\alpha}
), and
(-\bm{m}^{\alpha},-\bm{n}^{\alpha})
(with the normal
-\bm{n}^{\prime\prime\alpha}
) is the consequence of choosing the angle of the auxiliary plane different from
90^{\circ}
. This projection cannot be used, because it would change
\sigma_{n^{\prime}m}^{\alpha}
to
-\sigma_{tm}^{\alpha}
, which is the stress that is not allowed by the symmetry conditions (
3
) and (
10
). The chosen angle of the auxiliary plane (
60^{\circ}
) is only one possible choice and other orientations of the auxiliary plane would result in different forms of the effective stress. Nevertheless, all these effective stresses would be equally applicable in defining the yield criteria and predicting the onset of yielding. Since no details have been given so far about the Miller indices representing the vectors
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
and
\bm{n}^{\alpha}
, the effective stress (
9
) is applicable to all non-polar crystal structures. In the following, we will demonstrate its applicability to bcc and hcp crystals.

3
Application to cubic and hexagonal crystals

3.1
Body-centered cubic crystals

The plastic deformation of bcc metals is governed by the glide of
1/2\langle 111\rangle
screw dislocations moving by elementary steps on
\{110\}
planes. We will be concerned with the usual (engineering) case, where the initiation of plastic deformation is not affected by the hydrostatic stress and by the tension/compression acting parallel to the dislocation line. Under these assumptions, the effective stress (
9
) reduces to

\tau^{*\alpha}=\sigma_{nm}^{\alpha}+a_{1}\sigma_{n^{\prime}m}^{\alpha}+a_{2}(\sigma_{nn}^{\alpha}-\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha})+a_{3}(\sigma_{n^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{\alpha}-\sigma_{t^{\prime}t^{\prime}}^{\alpha})\ .

(11)

The individual stress components are expressed as explained in Section
2
with the (non-normalized) vectors
\bm{m}^{\alpha}
,
\bm{n}^{\alpha}
, and

3.2
Hexagonal crystals

In hexagonal crystallography, it is customary to characterize directions and normals of planes using four-index notations. The first three indices constitute projections onto the axes
\bm{a}_{1}
,
\bm{a}_{2}
,
\bm{a}_{3}
in the basal plane, whereas the fourth is the projection onto the
\bm{c}
axis, as shown in Fig.
3
. The addition of the third axis, i.e.
\bm{a}_{3}=-(\bm{a}_{1}+\bm{a}_{2})
, ensures that crystallographically equivalent directions have similar Miller indices in the four-index notation. In order to simplify mathematical operations in the hexagonal system,
Otte and Crocker 1965
proposed to convert the four-index representations of slip directions and slip plane normals in the hexagonal system into an ortho-hexagonal system spanned by mutually orthogonal lattice vectors
\bm{a}
,
\bm{b}
,
\bm{c}
shown in Fig.
3
. We will use these projection rules in the following to derive expressions for the six stress components
\sigma_{ij}
in (
9
) directly in terms of the slip directions and slip plane normals defined in the four-index hexagonal notation.

3.2.1
Slip directions in the ortho-hexagonal basis

Consider an arbitrary direction
\bm{m}
that is represented by the Miller indices
[uvtw]
in the four-index hexagonal system. The vector
\bm{m}
can be recovered from these Miller indices using the basis vectors of the hexagonal system, i.e.
\bm{m}=u\bm{a}_{1}+v\bm{a}_{2}+(u+v)(\bm{a}_{1}+\bm{a}_{2})+w\bm{c}
, where we have used well-known identities
t=-(u+v)
and
\bm{a}_{3}=-(\bm{a}_{1}+\bm{a}_{2})
. In the ortho-hexagonal system, the same direction
\bm{m}
is represented by the Miller indices
[pqr]
, i.e.
\bm{m}=p\bm{a}+q\bm{b}+r\bm{c}
. According to
Otte and Crocker 1965
the relations between the lattice vectors in the two systems are
\bm{a}=\bm{a}_{1}
,
\bm{b}=\bm{a}_{1}+2\bm{a}_{2}
, whereas the third axis remains the same in both systems. Equating the right-hand sides of the two representations of the vector
\bm{m}
above and implementing these transformation rules provides the indices of directions in the ortho-hexagonal system in terms of those in the hexagonal system:
p=3u/2
,
q=u/2+v
,
r=w
. It is clear that the third index in
[uvtw]
is redundant and can be omitted (it is replaced in the following by a dot). Therefore, any direction represented by the vector
\bm{M}=[uv.w]
defined in the hexagonal system can be expressed by the vector
\bm{m}=[pqr]
in the ortho-hexagonal system as

\bm{m}=\bm{M}\bm{Q}\ ,

(12)

where both
\bm{m}
and
\bm{M}
are row vectors with three values. The transformation matrix
\bm{Q}
has the form

\bm{Q}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}3/2&1/2&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right]\ .

(13)

It is important to emphasize that the indices
[pqr]
representing
\bm{m}
in the ortho-hexagonal basis refer to the basis vectors
\bm{a}=a\hat{\bm{a}}
,
\bm{b}=a\sqrt{3}\hat{\bm{b}}
and
\bm{c}=c\hat{\bm{c}}
,
where
\hat{\bm{a}}
,
\hat{\bm{b}}
,
\hat{\bm{c}}
are unit vectors along the three directions. In terms of these basis vectors,
\bm{m}=[pqr]
can be expressed as

\bm{m}=pa\hat{\bm{a}}+qa\sqrt{3}\hat{\bm{b}}+rc\hat{\bm{c}}\ .

(14)

We want to find the unit vector
\hat{\bm{m}}=[\hat{p}\hat{q}\hat{r}]
parallel to
\bm{m}
, which we define as

\hat{\bm{m}}=\hat{p}\hat{\bm{a}}+\hat{q}\hat{\bm{b}}+\hat{r}\hat{\bm{c}}\ .

(15)

The two vectors are related by
\hat{\bm{m}}=\bm{m}/\lambda_{m}
, where
\lambda_{m}
is a normalization factor. This condition gives three equations that define the components of the unit vector
\hat{\bm{m}}
:
\hat{p}=pa/\lambda_{m}
,
\hat{q}=qa\sqrt{3}/\lambda_{m}
,
\hat{r}=rc/\lambda_{m}
. Substituting these equations into the normalization condition
\hat{p}^{2}+\hat{q}^{2}+\hat{r}^{2}=1
provides the normalization factor
\lambda_{m}=a\sqrt{p^{2}+3q^{2}+(c/a)^{2}r^{2}}
. It is evident that substituting
\lambda_{m}
back into the expressions for
\hat{p}
,
\hat{q}
,
\hat{r}
eliminates the lattice parameter
a
. For the sake of simplicity, we thus redefine the components of the unit vector
\hat{\bm{m}}=[\hat{p}\hat{q}\hat{r}]
and the normalization factor as

\displaystyle\hat{p}=p/\lambda_{m}\ ,\quad\hat{q}=q\sqrt{3}/\lambda_{m}\ ,\quad\hat{r}=r(c/a)/\lambda_{m}

(16)

\displaystyle\lambda_{m}=\sqrt{p^{2}+3q^{2}+(c/a)^{2}r^{2}}

\displaystyle\ .

Employing (
12
) in the definition of
\hat{\bm{m}}
allows to express the unit vector parallel to the slip direction in the (orthonormal) ortho-hexagonal basis simply as a division by the factor
\lambda_{m}
:

\hat{\bm{m}}=\frac{\bm{M}\bm{Q}}{\lambda_{m}}\ .

(17)

3.2.2
Slip plane normals in the ortho-hexagonal basis

Different transformation rules apply for directions of plane normals
(
Otte and Crocker 1965
)
. To demonstrate this, consider an arbitrary plane defined by the indices
(hkil)
in the four-index hexagonal system, where
i=-(h+k)
as explained above. The vector normal to this plane is then
\bm{n}=h\bm{a}_{1}^{*}+k\bm{a}_{2}^{*}+l\bm{c}^{*}
, where
\bm{a}_{1}^{*},\bm{a}_{2}^{*},\bm{c}^{*}
are reciprocal lattice vectors. In the ortho-hexagonal system, this plane is represented by the Miller index
(efg)
, which defines the same vector
\bm{n}=e\bm{a}^{*}+f\bm{b}^{*}+g\bm{c}^{*}
. The transformation rules between the two reciprocal bases are
\bm{a}^{*}=\bm{a}_{1}^{*}-\bm{a}_{2}^{*}/2
,
\bm{b}^{*}=\bm{a}_{2}^{*}/2
with
\bm{c}^{*}
being parallel in both bases. Equating the factors in front of
\bm{a}_{1}^{*}
,
\bm{a}_{2}^{*}
and
\bm{c}^{*}
provides the relations between the indices in the three-index ortho-hexagonal system in terms of the indices in the four-index hexagonal system:
e=h
,
f=h+2k
,
g=l
. Similarly as above, the third index in
(hkil)
is redundant and does not enter the transformation (it is again replaced by a dot). Therefore, the vector normal to the plane
(hk.l)
in the hexagonal system, represented by the vector
\bm{N}=[hk.l]
, can be expressed as the vector
\bm{n}=[efg]
in the ortho-hexagonal system as

\bm{n}=\bm{N}\bm{Q}^{*}\ ,

(18)

where both
\bm{n}
and
\bm{N}
are again row vectors with three values. The transformation matrix
\bm{Q}^{*}
has the form

\bm{Q}^{*}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&0\\
0&2&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right]\ .

(19)

Similar procedure as above can be applied to derive the normalization factors for plane normals. In the ortho-hexagonal basis, the basis vectors for plane normals are
\bm{a}^{*}=\bm{a}/a^{2}
,
\bm{b}^{*}=\bm{b}/3a^{2}
,
\bm{c}^{*}=\bm{c}/c^{2}
. Using these basis vectors, the plane normals
\bm{n}=[efg]
can be expressed as

\bm{n}=\frac{e}{a}\hat{\bm{a}}+\frac{f}{a\sqrt{3}}\hat{\bm{b}}+\frac{g}{c}\hat{\bm{c}}\ .

(20)

We want to find the unit vector
\hat{\bm{n}}=[\hat{e}\hat{f}\hat{g}]
parallel to
\bm{n}
, which we define as

\hat{\bm{n}}=\hat{e}\hat{\bm{a}}+\hat{f}\hat{\bm{b}}+\hat{g}\hat{\bm{c}}\ .

(21)

The two vectors are related by
\hat{\bm{n}}=\bm{n}/\lambda_{n}
, where
\lambda_{n}
is a normalization factor. This condition gives three equations that define the components of the unit vector
\hat{\bm{n}}
:
\hat{e}=e/a\lambda_{n}
,
\hat{f}=f/a\sqrt{3}\lambda_{n}
,
\hat{g}=g/c\lambda_{n}
. Substituting these equations into the normalization condition
\hat{e}^{2}+\hat{f}^{2}+\hat{g}^{2}=1
provides the normalization factor
\lambda_{n}=(1/a)\sqrt{e^{2}+f^{2}/3+g^{2}/(c/a)^{2}}
. It is again evident that substituting
\lambda_{n}
back into the expressions for
\hat{e}
,
\hat{f}
,
\hat{g}
eliminates the lattice parameter
a
. We thus redefine the components of the unit vector
\hat{\bm{n}}=[\hat{e}\hat{f}\hat{g}]
and the normalization factor as

\displaystyle\hat{e}=e/\lambda_{n}\ ,\quad\hat{f}=f/\sqrt{3}\lambda_{n}\ ,\quad\hat{g}=g/(c/a)\lambda_{n}

(22)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=\sqrt{e^{2}+f^{2}/3+g^{2}/(c/a)^{2}}

\displaystyle\ .

Employing (
18
) in the definition of
\hat{\bm{n}}
allows to express the unit normal of the slip plane in the (orthonormal) ortho-hexagonal basis simply as a division by the factor
\lambda_{n}
:

\hat{\bm{n}}=\frac{\bm{N}\bm{Q}^{*}}{\lambda_{n}}\ .

(23)

, where the slip direction
\hat{\bm{m}}^{\alpha}

\hat{\bm{n}}^{\alpha}

\bm{M}^{\alpha}
and
\bm{N}^{\alpha}

\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha}=\frac{[(\bm{N}^{\alpha}\bm{Q}^{*})\times(\bm{M}^{\alpha}\bm{Q})]\bm{\sigma}[(\bm{N}^{\alpha}\bm{Q}^{*})\times(\bm{M}^{\alpha}\bm{Q})]^{T}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}\lambda_{m}^{2}}\ .

We want to derive an equivalent form of this expression in which the vectors
\bm{N}^{\alpha}
and

4
Conclusions

We have applied Neumann’s principle to demonstrate that the single-crystal yield criteria for materials with non-polar structures must be invariant under a simultaneous change of sign of the slip direction (
\bm{m}^{\alpha}\rightarrow-\bm{m}^{\alpha}
) and the slip plane normal (
\bm{n}^{\alpha}\rightarrow-\bm{n}^{\alpha}
) for all slip systems
\alpha
determined by the underlying space group. This symmetry is satisfied by the criterion proposed by
Tresca 1864
but it is violated in all recent generalizations of this model that aim to describe the onset of yielding in materials that do not obey the Schmid law. In the latter models, only certain combinations of the pairs
(\bm{m}^{\alpha},\bm{n}^{\alpha})
may thus enter the yield criteria, whereas the conjugates
(-\bm{m}^{\alpha},-\bm{n}^{\alpha})
are not allowed despite the fact that they are equivalent by symmetry.

To remove this drawback and avoid ambiguity in interpreting the activity of individual slip systems, we have explored the possibility to derive a general form of the effective stress that satisfies this symmetry. The effective stress is written as a sum of power series for each independent component of the stress tensor. For simplicity, we retain only the lowest order stress terms that are invariant under the symmetry above. This results in a homogeneous effective stress of order two in which the quadratic stresses are associated with shear stresses parallel and perpendicular to the slip direction. We show that the presence of these quadratic terms can be avoided by introducing an auxiliary system in the zone of the slip direction, which leads to generalization of the symmetry condition that involves also the normal of the auxiliary plane. This transformation provides a linear yield criterion that is conceptually similar to that proposed by
Qin and Bassani 1992
but, additionally, satisfies Neumann’s principle.

The obtained yield criterion can be used to describe the slip activity in bcc and hcp crystals as well as in other non-polar crystal structures. In the case of hexagonal symmetry, we use the transformation rules between ortho-hexagonal and hexagonal systems to express all stress terms using four-index representations of slip directions and slip plane normals. These expressions are very similar to those used for bcc lattices provided the applied stress tensor is also generalized into the four-index notation. Somewhat different forms are obtained for the stresses
\sigma_{tt}^{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{t^{\prime}t^{\prime}}^{\alpha}
, which follows from different transformation rules for slip directions and slip plane normals.

If non-Schmid stresses play no role in the initiation of plastic deformation, as it is the case in fcc metals and for basal slip in hcp metals, the effective stress derived here reduces to the Schmid stress. The theoretical framework established in this paper then reduces to the well-established maximum shear stress theory developed by
Tresca 1864
.
