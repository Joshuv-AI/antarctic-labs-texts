1
Introduction

For every two positive integers
s
and
d
, let
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(s,d)
denote the smallest integer
n
such that in every red-blue coloring of all the one-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
there must exist either a
d
-dimensional subspace for which all of its one-dimensional subspaces get colored red or an
s
-dimensional subspace for which all of one-dimensional subspaces get colored blue. This quantity was first introduced in 1972 (in much greater generality) by Graham, Leeb and Rothschild
[
14
]
, who showed that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(s,d)
is finite for every choice of
s
and
d
. The result was subsequently simplified (and generalized) by Spencer in
[
24
]
, however the quantitative bounds implied by either of these arguments are all rather poor, due to the repeated uses of the Hales-Jewett Theorem. Despite the rich activity on their combinatorial counterparts over the years, there hasn’t been much progress on these Ramsey numbers since.

In
[
19
]
, Nelson and Nomoto established a new connection between
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
and the
\chi
-boundedness of certain classes of binary matroids, which in some sense reignited interest for this problem. Recall that a class
\mathcal{M}
of matroids is
\chi
-bounded by a function
f
if
\chi(M)\leqslant f(\omega(M))
for all
M\in\mathcal{M}
, and that
\mathcal{M}
is
\chi
-bounded if it is
\chi
-bounded by some
f
. Here
\omega(M)
denotes the
clique number
of the matroid
M
and
\chi(M)
denotes its
chromatic number
. In particular, for a simple binary
n
-dimensional matroid
M=(E,G)
defined on a finite projective geometry
G:=\operatorname{PG}(n-1,2)
and having ground set
E\subset G
,
\omega(M)
is defined to be the dimension of the largest subgeometry of
M
contained in
E
, and
\chi(M)
is defined as
\chi(M)=n-\omega(M^{c})
, where
M^{c}(G\setminus E,G)
is the matroid complement of
M
. These parameters represent natural analogues of the usual clique number
\omega(G)
and the chromatic number
\chi(G)
of a graph
G
, respectively. See for example
[
21
]
for a discussion. A simple binary matroid is called
claw-free
if none of its rank-
3
flats are independent sets. Unlike claw-free graphs, however, claw-free (simple binary) matroids are not necessarily
\chi
-bounded
[
6
]
: for all
k\geqslant 0
, there is a so-called even-plane matroid
M
with
\omega(M)=2
and
\chi(M)\geqslant k
. A simple binary matroid
M
is called an
even plane matroid
M
if all of its 3-dimensional induced restrictions have even-sized ground sets. It is not difficult to see that such matroids are claw-free, and so this implies that the class of claw-free matroids is not
\chi
-bounded. One of the main results in
[
19
]
is that even-plane matroids present the only obstruction to
\chi
-boundedness.

Theorem 1.1

(Nelson-Nomoto)
.

If
N
is an even-plane matroid, then the class of
N
-free, claw-free matroids is
\chi
-bounded by the function
f(d)=R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d+1)+d(\dim(N)+4)
.

Moreover, in
[
19
]
Nelson and Nomoto also showed that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)\leqslant(d+1)2^{d}
, thus improving upon the bounds from
[
14
]
and
[
24
]
, and establishing that the class of
N
-free, claw-free matroids above is
\chi
-bounded by an exponential function. In a very recent paper
[
13
]
, Frederickson and Yepremyan further improved this estimate to
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)\leqslant 1.566^{d}
(by using some new ideas which also allow one to improve upon a few other particular cases of the Graham-Leeb-Rothschild theorem).

On the other hand, it is well-known that the class of claw-free graphs is
\chi
-bounded by a quadratic function: in a claw-free graph
G
, each vertex has the property that its neighborhood has no independent set of size
3
, so the maximum degree of
G
is upper bounded by the usual off-diagonal Ramsey number
R(3,\omega(G)+1)
; see for example
[
9
]
and the references therein for more details. In light of this analogy, it thus seems natural to ask whether or not it could also be the case that for any even-plane matroid
N
, the class of
N
-free, claw-free matroids is
\chi
-bounded by a function
f(d)
that grows only polynomially in
d
(or in other words, is polynomially
\chi
-bounded).

In this paper, we settle this question in the affirmative by showing that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
is polynomial in
d
. Specifically, our main result is the following estimate:

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
, such that for every integer
d\geqslant 1
:

R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)\leqslant Cd^{7}.

This result settles a recent question of Nelson
[
20
]
, alongside the original problem of Nelson-Nomoto from
[
19
]
(also reiterated in
[
13
]
), which originally asked to determine whether or not
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
is subexponential.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
will build upon the original argument from
[
19
]
, and provide an upper bound for
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
via a reduction to a well-studied additive combinatorics problem, originally posed by Green in
[
15
]
: given a set
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with density
\alpha\in[0,1]
, what is the largest subspace that we can find in
A+A
? To derive a polynomial bound for
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
, we will establish the following new result for this problem, by using ideas from the breakthrough paper of Kelley and Meka on sets of integers without three-term arithmetic progressions
[
17
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n\geqslant 1
. There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds: for any subset
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with
|A|=\alpha 2^{n}
, the set
A+A
must always contain a subspace
V
of dimension

\operatorname{dim}(V)\geqslant c\left(\frac{n}{\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{5}}\right)^{1/2}-1.

In the regime where
1/\alpha
is large with respect to
n
, Theorem
1.3
improves upon a well-known result of Sanders
[
22
]
. We will discuss this additive combinatorics problem in more detail in Section
2
, after first revisiting its connection with the Ramsey number
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
.

Our argument will also allow us to get strong bounds for the following multi-color generalization of the function
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
. To formulate this more general statement, it will be convenient to use the following notation: given a vector space
V
, let
{V\brack k}
denote as usual the collection of all
k
-dimensional linear subspaces of
V
. Given an
r
-tuple of positive integers
(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})
, let
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
denote the smallest
n
, such that for every
r
-coloring
C:{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\brack 1}\to\{1,\dots,r\}
, there exists some
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
and a subspace
V\leqslant\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of dimension
d_{i}
such that
C(W)=i
for all
1
-dimensional subspaces
W\subset V
. From
[
14
]
and
[
24
]
, we know that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
is finite for every choice of
(d_{1},\dots,d_{r})
. In what follows, we establish the following quantitative improvement when
d_{1}=\ldots=d_{r-1}=2
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
r\geqslant 2
and let
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r)}(2;d)
denote the
r
-color Ramsey number
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,\ldots,2,d)
. There exists an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds: for every integer
d\geqslant 1
,

R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r)}(2;d)\leqslant Cr(\log r+d)^{7}.

This not only recovers Theorem
1.2
in the case when
r=2
, but it also provides an upper bound of the same quality for all
r\geqslant 2
(up to constant factors depending on
r
). We will present the proof of Theorem
1.4
in Section
3
, after sketching the argument for two colors in Section
2
.

Notational conventions
. We will use standard asymptotic notation, as follows. For functions
f=f(n)
and
g=g(n)
, we write
f=O(g)
to mean that there is a constant
C
such that

|f(n)|\leqslant C|g(n)|
for sufficiently large
n
. Similarly, we write
f=\Omega(g)
to mean that there is a constant
c>0
such that
f(n)\geqslant c|g(n)|
for sufficiently large n. Finally, we write
f=\Theta(g)
to mean that
f=O(g)
and
g=O(f)
, and we write
f=o(g)
or
g=\omega(f)
to mean that

f(n)/g(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. We write
O_{H}(1)
for some unspecified constant that can be chosen as
some bounded value depending only on
H
. Whenever convenient, we will also use the Vinogradov notation
f\ll g
to denote that
f=O(g)
, that is, there exists some constant
C>0
such that
f<Cg
. Similarly, we will write
f\gg g
whenever
g\ll f
.

Given a finite additive abelian group
G
and a set
A\subset G
, we write
\mu(A):=|A|/|G|
for the density of
A
in
G
. Given two sets
A,X\subset G
, we write
\mu_{X}(A):=|A\cap X|/|X|
for the relative density of
A
inside
X
. We will also use
1_{A}
to denote the characteristic function of the set
A
, and use
\mu_{A}
for the normalized characteristic function
\frac{1}{\mu(A)}1_{A}
.

For any two functions
f,g:G\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
f\circ g
the difference convolution of
f
and
g
, namely

f\circ g(x)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{y\in G}f(x+y)g(x).

Note that
\mu_{A}\circ\mu_{B}(x):=\mathbb{P}(a-b=x)
where
a\sim A,b\sim B
are sampled uniformly and independently from their respective sets.

Last but not least, given an integer
k\geqslant 1
, we sometimes use the shorthand
[k]:=\{1,\dots,k\}
. Given a positive real number
a
, we also write
\mathcal{L}(a)
to denote the least integer
m\geqslant 1
such that
2^{m}a\geqslant 1
.

2
Proof overview for Theorem
1.2

In the same spirit as the arguments from
[
19
]
and
[
13
]
, our starting point will be the observation that in order to obtain an upper bound for
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
, it will suffice to study a certain additive combinatorics problem.

Observation 2.1
.

If
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)>n
, then there must exist some
S\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{(0,\ldots,0)\}
such that
S+S
and the complement
S^{c}:=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus S
contain no
d
-dimensional subspaces.

Indeed, let us denote the additive group
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
by
G
and the identity vector
(0,\ldots,0)
by
0_{G}
for convenience, and then note that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)>n
implies the existence of a partition of
G\setminus\{0_{G}\}
into sets
R
and
S
such that:

•

R\cup\{0_{G}\}=G\setminus S
does not contain a
d
-dimensional subspace;

•

S\cup\{0_{G}\}
does not contain a
2
-dimensional subspace.

The second bullet says that
S
does not contain any pattern of the form
x,y,x+y
with
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and such that
x\neq y
; in other words,
S
is sum-free. This implies that
S+S\subset G\setminus S
, which by the first bullet means that
S+S
cannot contain a
d
-subspace.

We next note that the complement of
S
lacking
d
-dimensional subspaces tells us something about the density of
S
in
G
. To this end, we recall the following classical result going back to Bose and Burton
[
7
]
:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a vector space, and
S\subset G\setminus\{0_{G}\}
have density
\sigma:=\mu(S)
. Consider some
d\in[n]
, and assume
\sigma<1/2^{d}
so that
|S|<\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{d}-1}
. Then
S^{c}
contains a vector space
V\leqslant G
with
\dim(V)\geqslant d
.

The above result is sharp up to a factor of
\approx 2
. Indeed, if
V^{\prime}\subset G
has dimension
n+1-d
, then
\dim(V^{\prime}\cap V)\geqslant 1
for each
V\leqslant G
with
\dim(V)=d
. Thus, taking
S=V^{\prime}\setminus\{0_{G}\}
, we have that
S^{c}
contains no subspace with dimension
d
. In fact,
[
7
, Theorem 2]
shows that this construction of
S
is the worst-case scenario; however, for the sake of self-containment, we decided to instead state the slightly weaker version above in order to include a short probabilistic proof.

Proof.

Sample
\mathbf{V}
uniformly at random among all the
d
-dimensional subspaces of
G
. For every
s\in G\setminus\{0_{G}\}
, we shall prove
P_{s}:=\mathbb{P}(s\in\mathbf{V})
is equal to
(2^{d}-1)/(2^{n}-1)
. Assuming this, the result follows by a union bound, since

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{V}\cap S\neq\emptyset)\leqslant|S|\frac{2^{d}-1}{2^{n}-1}<1.

To start, note that for
s,s^{\prime}\in G\setminus\{0_{G}\}
, there is an automorphism
\phi:G\to G
sending
s
to
s^{\prime}
(by changing bases appropriately). Meanwhile, for any automorphism
\phi
, the distribution of
\phi(\mathbf{V})
should remain uniform over vector spaces of dimension
d
. Thus, there is some constant
P
such that
P_{s}=P
for all
s\in G\setminus\{0_{G}\}
. By double-counting, we get

2^{d}-1=\mathbb{E}[|\mathbf{V}\setminus\{0_{G}\}|]=\sum_{s\in G\setminus\{0_{G}\}}\mathbb{P}(s\in\mathbf{V})=(2^{n}-1)P,

which rearranges to give the result.
\Box

Putting together Observation
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
, we arrive at the following reduction, first noted essentially in
[
19
]
.

Observation 2.3
.

If
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)>n
, then there must exist a subset
S\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
where
\mu(S)\geqslant 2^{-d}
and such that
S+S
contains no
d
-dimensional subspaces.

This naturally leads us to the following additive combinatorics problem, originally posed by Green in
[
15
]
: given a set
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with density
\alpha\in[0,1]
, what is the largest subspace that one can find in
A+A
?

In the case when
\alpha
is fixed, the best known quantitative result for this problem comes from
[
22
]
(see also
[
10
]
).

Theorem 2.1

(Sanders)
.

The exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that the following holds: for any subset
A\subset G:=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, we have that
A+A
contains a subset
V\leqslant G
with

\dim(V)\geqslant c\mu(A)n.

Note that Theorem
2.1
combined with Observation
2.3
immediately tells us that

R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)\leqslant\frac{d}{c}2^{d}<2^{(1+o(1))d}.

In general, given
n\geqslant 1
along with
\alpha>0
, define
f(n,\alpha)
to be the maximal
d
such that: for every
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with
\mu(A)\geqslant\alpha
, there exists
d
-subspace
V\leqslant\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with
V\subset A+A
. We briefly recall what is known here. Thus far in the literature, everyone has primarily focused on the regime where
\alpha>0
is fixed and then
n
tends to
\infty
. Using this notation, Theorem
2.1
states that

f(n,\alpha)\gg\alpha n.

(2.1)

The best known upper bound for fixed
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
is

f(n,\alpha)\leqslant n-(C_{\alpha}-o(1))\sqrt{n},

where
C_{\alpha}>0
is chosen such that if
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a standard Gaussian, then
\mathbb{P}(X<-C_{\alpha})=\alpha
; this is due to a beautiful “niveau-set construction” where
A
is taken to be set of vectors with Hamming weight at most
n/2-(C_{\alpha}/2-o(1))\sqrt{n}
. We refer to
[
22
, Section 3]
for more details. Despite the general belief that this upper bound is closer to the truth (i.e., that
f(n,\alpha)\geqslant(1-o(1))n
), Theorem
2.1
remains the ‘state-of-the-art’ in the fixed density regime.

Nevertheless, for the purposes of estimating
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
it turns out that the density
\alpha
goes to
0
very fast as
n
grows, and in this case our Theorem
1.3
improves substantially upon the result of Sanders. Using the new notation, Theorem
1.3
states the following: given
n\geqslant 1
and
\alpha>0
,

f(n,\alpha)+1\gg\left(\frac{n}{\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{5}}\right)^{1/2}.

(2.2)

Indeed, note that (
2.2
) instantly implies that there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that
f(Cd^{7},2^{-d})\geqslant d
, and by Observation
2.3
this subsequently yields that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)=O(d^{7})
.

2.1
Proof of Theorem
1.3

The new lower bound for
f(n,\alpha)
from (
2.2
) can be proven using recent “approximate Bogolyubov-Ruzsa results”, which have been made possible by the recent breakthrough paper by Kelley and Meka
[
17
]
.

Roughly speaking, an “
exact
Bogolyubov-Ruzsa result” takes a dense subset
A
, and then finds a large subspace
V
contained in some type of sumset of
A
(say
A-A
); meanwhile, an “approximate Bogolyubov-Ruzsa result” instead finds a large subspace
V
, such that the intersection of
V
with
A-A
is at least a
(1-\epsilon)
-fraction of
V
.

The specific result we need is the following lemma. Results like the one below are known to those familiar with the work of Kelley and Meka
[
17
]
, but for completeness we note it follows from a more general result that we prove in the next section (see Remark
3.4
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
A\subset G
with
\alpha:=\mu(A)>0
. Given
\gamma>0
, we can find
V\leqslant G
with
\cdim(V)\ll\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{5}\mathcal{L}(\gamma)^{2}
, such that writing

D:=\{v\in V:\mu_{A}\circ\mu_{A}(v)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mu(V)^{2}\}

to denote the set of “locally popular diffences”, we have

\mu_{V}(D)\geqslant 1-\gamma.

Remark 2.5
.

To prove Theorem
1.3
, it would be satisfactory to replace ‘
D
’ by ‘
A-A
’. However, the proof easily gives this stronger form, so we saw no reason not to mention it.

The important property here is that the dependence on the codimension is polylogarithmatically-efficient with respect to both
1/\alpha
and
1/\gamma
(which should be optimal, up to the powers of logs).

Given Lemma
2.4
, along with Proposition
2.2
from before, we may quickly obtain Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
assuming Lemma
2.4
.

Let
C_{0}
be the implicit absolute constant from Lemma
2.4
.

Then suppose we are given a non-empty subset
A\subset G:=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and write
\alpha:=\mu(A)>0
. Now, let
C
be some sufficiently large absolute constant. If
n<C\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{5}
, then the conclusion of Theorem
1.3
is trivially satisfied by taking
V=\{0_{G}\}
which has dimension
0\geqslant\frac{1}{C}\left(\frac{n}{\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{5}}\right)^{1/2}-1
.

Otherwise, by the largeness of
C
, we will have that
\frac{n}{2C_{0}\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{5}}>\frac{C}{2C_{0}}>1
. In which case
we may take
\gamma\in(0,1)
so that
C_{0}\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{5}\mathcal{L}(\gamma)^{2}=n/2
, meaning

\log_{2}(1/\gamma)=\left(\frac{n}{2C_{0}\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{5}}\right)^{1/2}.

Write
d:=\lfloor\left(\frac{n}{2C_{0}\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{5}}\right)^{1/2}\rfloor
, so that
\gamma\leqslant 2^{-d}
.

Applying Lemma
2.4
(which holds with the constant
C_{0}
) to the set
A
and the constant
\gamma
, we can find some subspace
V\leqslant G
with
\dim(V)\geqslant n/2
, such that defining

D:=\{v\in V:\mu_{A}\circ\mu_{A}(v)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mu(V)^{2}\}

to be the set of locally popular differences, we have
\mu_{V}(D)\geqslant 1-\gamma
. Noting that
D\subset A-A=A+A
(recall that
A=-A
, as
G=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
), it will suffice to show that
D
contains a
d
-space (recalling
d\gg(n/\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{5})^{1/2}
).

We shall prove this using Lemma
2.2
. Let
S:=V\setminus D
, so that
D=S^{c}
(where the complement is taken with respect to
V
). Clearly,
\mu_{V}(S)\leqslant\gamma\leqslant 2^{-d}
; so we will be done if we can confirm that the trivial non-degeneracy assumptions in Lemma
2.2
are also satisfied.

First, note that
\mu_{A}\circ\mu_{A}(0_{V})=1/\alpha\geqslant 1
, thus
0_{V}\not\in S
, as desired. Also, without loss of generality, we may suppose
n\geqslant 4
. Whence,
d\leqslant n/2\leqslant\dim(V)
(recalling
d\leqslant n^{1/2}
). This establishes the two non-degeneracy assumptions, and thus the result.

\Box

3
Proof of Theorem
1.4

A key ingredient of this section is the following density-increment result.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
5
, Proposition 9]
, in characteristic two)
.

Let
V\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a vector space, and consider
A,C\subset V
with
\mu(A)=\alpha,\mu(C)=\gamma
. Suppose that
|\langle\mu_{A}\circ\mu_{A},\mu_{C}\rangle-1|>1/2
.

Then there exists a coset
U=t+V^{\prime}
where
\cdim(V^{\prime})\ll\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{4}\mathcal{L}(\gamma)^{2}
and
\mu_{U}(A)\geqslant(1+1/128)\alpha
.

Remark 3.2
.

In the original work of Kelley and Meka
[
17
]
, a slightly weaker version of this result is proven where
\cdim(V)\ll\mathcal{L}(\alpha)^{4}\mathcal{L}(\gamma)^{4}
. The improved
\gamma
-dependence in the above lemma is based off a combinatorial trick which improves the quantitative performance of an intermediate step of the proof. In the following applications, the same proofs will continue to work with the previous increment bounds, only with slightly worse numbers. For example, we would instead get an upper bound of
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)\leqslant O(d^{9})
.

Another application

In this section, we would like to note that our methods can be extended further to provide an even stronger form of Theorem
1.4
(namely, Theorem
4.1
), which gives similar results for arbitrary finite-fields.

Given a prime
p\geqslant 2
, let us say that a set
S\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
is
solution-free
, if there exists some choice
a,b\in\{1,\dots,p-1\}
such that there is no
x,y,z\in S
satisfying
a\cdot(x-y)=b\cdot z
. Note that every sum-free set
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
is a solution-free set (by taking
a=b=1
, and rearranging
(x-y)=z
as
x=y+z
).

This definition is motivated by “homogeneous partition-regular equations” (discussed below). Obtaining quantitative bounds for coloring problems related to these equations is an important topic in additive combinatorics, as such questions represent generalizations of the celebrated theorem of Schur (which states that for any finite coloring of
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, there must be a color class which is not sum-free). We will establish nearly sharp bounds in the finite-field setting for such problems.

In the integer setting, it is known that for any fixed
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and any
r\geqslant 1
, there exists some finite
N
so that for any
r
-coloring
C:[N]\to[r]
, we can find some monochromatic triple
x,y,z
such that
a(x-y)=bz
. Specializing to the case of
a=b=1
, this is the content of Schur’s Theorem (which alternatively says that
[N]
cannot be covered by
r
sum-free sets (assuming
N
is large enough with respect to
r
)). In
[
11
]
, Cwalina and Schoen studied the growth of
N=N_{a,b}(r)
for any fixed
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
as
r\to\infty
. They demonstrated that

\exp(\Omega_{a,b}(r))\leqslant N_{a,b}(r)\leqslant\exp(O_{a,b}(r^{4}\log(r)^{4})).

We defer the interested reader to
[
11
]
for further motivation on the study of such quantities; here, we will provide some nearly sharp bounds to a finite-field analogue of this problem.

Theorem 4.1
.

For each prime
p
, there exists a constant
C_{p}
such that the following holds:

Consider solution-free subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}\subset G:=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
. Furthermore, suppose that

for some

.

Then there exists

with
\dim(V)\geqslant d
so that
V
is disjoint from
\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}

\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}

5
Concluding remarks and open problems

In this paper, we proved that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)=O(d^{7})
, and more generally that

R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r)}(2;d)=O_{r}(d^{7})

holds for all
r\geqslant 2
. We would like to begin this section by contrasting our multi-color result with the following result of Alon and Rödl from
[
2
]
, generalizing the works of Ajtai-Komloś-Szemerédi
[
1
]
and Kim
[
18
]
about the usual off-diagonal Ramsey number
R(3,m)
.

Theorem 5.1

(Alon-Rödl)
.

Let
R^{(r)}(3;m)
denote the
r
-color Ramsey number
R(3,\ldots,3;m)
. For every
r\geqslant 2
, we have that

R^{(r)}(3;m)=\Theta(m^{r}/\operatorname{polylog}m).

For convenience,
\operatorname{polylog}m
here stands for an unspecified polynomial factor in
\log m
. For
r=2
,
R^{(2)}(3;m)=R(3,m)
, in which case Theorem
5.1
recovers (up to this polylogarithmic factor) the fact that
R(3,m)=\Theta(m^{2}/\log m)
. For every
r\geqslant 3
, Theorem
5.1
implies for instance that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{R^{(r)}(3;m)}{R^{(r-1)}(3;m)}=\infty,

formerly a conjecture of Erdős and Sós
[
12
]
already in the case
r=3
. It would be tempting to make the analogous conjecture for
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r)}(2;d)
, namely that for every
r\geqslant 3
,

\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r)}(2;d)}{R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r-1)}(2;d)}=\infty

(5.1)

must always hold. However, despite the successful analogies between
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
and
R(3,m)
thus far, we suspect (
5.1
) should not hold. Instead, we would like to make the following competing conjecture regarding the growth of
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
.

Conjecture 1
.

R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)=\Theta(d).

A standard probabilistic construction can be used to see that
3
3

3

Technically speaking, the “all-red coloring” of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d-1}
already proves that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)\geqslant d-1
. However, using a random coloring and a straightforward application of the Lovász Local Lemma, one can show that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)\geqslant(2-o(1))d
(thus we have
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)-d\gg d
). We note that we are not aware of any better bounds than this basic probabilistic argument. In fact, we do not even know how to prove a better bound using (finitely many) additional colors (for example, it would already be interesting to show that
R^{(1000)}(2;d)\geqslant(2+c-o(1))d
for some absolute constant
c>0
).

R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)=\Omega(d)
, so in order to prove Conjecture
1
it would suffice to show that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)=O(d)
. Furthermore, note that

R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r)}(2;d)\leqslant R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r-1)}(2;R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d))

holds for every
r\geqslant 3
. Therefore, if
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)=O(d)
, then
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r)}(2;d)=O_{r}(d)
follows for every
r\geqslant 3
as well. Moreover, it is clear that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r)}(2,d)\geqslant R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r-1)}(2,d)
holds for every
r\geqslant 3
, thus Conjecture
1
implies that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r)}(2;d)=\Theta_{r}(d)
holds for every
r\geqslant 2
.

That being said, we still believe that it would be interesting to distinguish quantitatively between, say,
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,d)
and
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(2,2,d)
. For example, if (
5.1
) is too much to ask for, can one at least show that

\lim_{d\to\infty}\left(R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r)}(2;d)-R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{(r-1)}(2;d)\right)=\infty

for every
r\geqslant 3
?

In light of the additive combinatorics approach developed in this paper, an important problem that is directly relevant to the faith of Conjecture
1
seems to be the question of determining whether or not there exists some absolute constant
C>0
such that
f(Cd,2^{-d})\geqslant d
for all
d\geqslant 1
.

There are also several natural open problems that one may pose about the general behavior of
R_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}(s,d)
for other choices of
p,s\geqslant 2
(as
d
goes to
\infty
). We would like to share a few of our favorite ones.

Conjecture 2
.

For every
s\geqslant 2
, there exists some
C=C(s)>0
such that

R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(s,d)=O(d^{C}).

In other words, we believe that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(s,d)
is polynomial in
d
for every
s\geqslant 2
. We suggest a possible approach, which could potentially extend the ideas of this paper. Given
n,k\geqslant 1
and
\alpha>0
, define
f_{k}(n,\alpha)
to be the largest
d
, such that given any
A\subset G:=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with
\mu(A)\geqslant\alpha
, we have that there exist some
V\leqslant G
with
\dim(V)\geqslant d
, such that for each
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in V
, there exists
a\in A\setminus V
such that the parallelepiped

\{a+\sum_{i\in I}v_{i}:I\subset[k]\}

is contained entirely inside
A
. Note that
f_{1}(n,\alpha)
is equal to the function
f(n,\alpha)
we introduced previously.

Using arguments like before, one can show that if

f_{s-1}(n,2^{-d})\geqslant R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(s-1,d),

then we have that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(s,d)\leqslant n
. Indeed, either the first color class will be “sparse” (density
<2^{-d}
), which implies a dimension
d
subspace in the complement, or one should be able to pass to some subspace
V
where every
(s-1)
-dimensional
V^{\prime}
subspace has some disjoint ‘coset’/parallelepiped which can extend
V^{\prime}
to a
s
-subspace (thus
V
should be colored to not have a red
(s-1)
-subspace nor blue
d
-subspace, allowing us to iterate). Thus, if for each
k\geqslant 1
, one could show that
f_{k}(Cd^{C},2^{-d})\geqslant d
for some constant
C=C_{k}
, then
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(s,d)\ll d^{O_{s}(1)}
for every
s\geqslant 2
. Furthermore, if
f_{k}(C_{k}d,2^{-d})\geqslant d
holds for some constant
C_{k}
, the sketch above would readily imply that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(s,d)=O_{s}(d)
for every
s\geqslant 2
.

Last but not least, it would also be interesting to get reasonable bounds for the off-diagonal numbers in different finite fields. For example, it is already not clear how to extend the arguments of this paper to show that
R_{\mathbb{F}_{3}}(2,d)
is polynomial in
d
. This presents new challenges, as now there is a difference between the set of
1
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{n}
, and the set of non-zero points in this space (indeed, one can define a 2-coloring
C:\mathbb{F}_{3}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\{1,2\}
so that
C(v)\neq C(2\cdot v)
for all non-zero
v
, in which case every non-trivial subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{n}
receives multiple colors). We currently do not have ideas for any additive combinatorial result that would readily give polynomial bounds in this setting.
