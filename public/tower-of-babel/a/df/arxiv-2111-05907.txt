The concentration inequality for a discrete height function model perturbed by random potential
Andrew Krieger

Abstract
. We present a proof of the concentration inequality for a discrete random surface model, where the underlying potential is perturbed by an additive random potential. The proof is based on annealing the random potential, and follows the method of [ CEP96 ] and other works. Our result demonstrates the robustness of this method.

The main result of this note is the concentration inequality,
presented as Theorem

Our result implies that the probability mass of a
perturbed random distribution
\mu(\cdot,\omega)

concentrates on functions that are “close,”
relative to the size of the domain,
to the
\mu(\cdot,\omega)
-expected height at each point in the domain.
To properly ground this result in the surrounding literature,
let us consider analogues of the concentration inequality in other settings.
Generally, concentration inequalities control
the magnitude and likelihood of fluctuations of a random variable,
especially a random statistic that quantifies some large-scale property
of an underlying random system.
The concentration inequality is a bound on the probability that the random
variable differs from its expected value (or sometimes, another typical value)
by more than
t>0
(in some metric).
The probability bound vanishes as
t\to\infty

or as the size of the underlying random system goes to infinity.
The simplest example is the weak law of large numbers
(cf.
[
Dur10
, Theorem 2.2.3]
):
Let
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}
be uncorrelated random variables
(meaning that
E(X_{i}X_{j})=E(X_{i})E(X_{j})
for
i\neq j
),
all with common mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
common variance bound
E[(X_{i}-E(X_{i}))^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
for some
\sigma^{2}>0
.
Define
M_{n}=(X_{1}+\dotsb+X_{n})/n
.
Then
E(M_{n})=\mu
and for any
t>0
,

P\biggl(|M_{n}-\mu|>\frac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\biggr)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{t^{2}}.

Focusing on random surface models, concentration inequalities are closely
related to the limit shape phenomenon.
A limit shape occurs when samples of the random
microscopic model converge with high probability to a deterministic shape, i.e. the limit shape, under a suitable scaling limit.
The concentration inequality provides a route towards proving
the existence of a limit shape.
Indeed, the concentration inequality implies that when the system size is large,
a sample of the model is close to the expected value for that system size,
with high probability; see for example Figure
1
.
Then it remains to show that these expected values converge
as the system size tends to infinity,
and the limit is necessarily the limit shape for the model.
This convergence can be established by means of a variational principle,
which characterizes the limit shape via a variational problem.
See e.g.
[
MT20
, Theorem 2.12]
, which is the variational principle for a
similar model, and whose proof is based on a concentration inequality

[
MT20
, Theorem 3.10]

whose proof is the inspiration for the work that follows.

]

for a wide class of random surface models,
and
[

from that large deviations principle under certain conditions

Now, let us describe our strategy for proving the homogenized concentration
inequality.
For the simpler case of random surface models without random perturbations,
there are two approaches in the literature
to proving the concentration inequality directly:
an approach that leverages monotonicity
and stochastic dominance (e.g.
[
CEP96
,
LT20
]
)
and a dynamic approach using a natural Markov chain
on height functions (e.g.
[
MT20
]
).
The second approach does not easily carry over to the case
of random perturbations.
It relies on monotonicity on the quenched level
(i.e. with fixed random potential
\omega
),
but monotonicity only occurs in the annealed setting
(i.e. after averaging over
\omega
).
This complicates the dynamic structure of the second approach.
Therefore we use the first approach.
See the statement and proof of Lemma
10
for details.

Definitions

The notation and definitions below are similar to those in
[
KMT21
]
.
For further explanation and further examples,
we kindly refer the reader to
[
KMT21
, Section 2]
.

Throughout the sequel, whenever
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
is viewed as a graph,
the edge set is always taken to be the collection of all nearest neighbor edges;
in other words, two points
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
are adjacent if and only if
the
\ell^{1}
distance
|x-y|_{1}
is exactly
1
.
Likewise, subsets of
R\subset\mathbb{Z}^{m}
are taken to be graphs
with the induced subgraph structure,
so any two vertices
x,y\in\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{m}
are adjacent
if and only if
|x-y|_{1}=1
.
We assume that the subgraph
R\subset\mathbb{Z}^{m}
is connected.
The edges of these graphs are written as
e_{x,y}
for the edge connecting

x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
These are undirected edges, i.e.
e_{y,x}=e_{x,y}
.

Definition 1

(Height function)
.

A
height function
on a connected subgraph
R\subset\mathbb{Z}^{m}

is a parity-preserving graph homomorphism
h_{R}:R\to\mathbb{Z}
.
In other words, if
x,y\in R
and
x\sim y
,
then
\lvert h_{R}(x)-h_{R}(y)\rvert=1
,
and for any
x=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m})\in R
,

(1)

h_{R}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}\pmod{2}.

The set of all height functions on
R
is denoted as

(2)

M(R):=\bigl\{h_{R}:R\to\mathbb{Z}\,\big|\,\text{$h_{R}$ is a height function}\bigr\}

and given a subgraph
R^{\prime}\subset R
and a height function

h_{R^{\prime}}\in M(R^{\prime})
,
the set of height functions extending
h_{R^{\prime}}
to
R
is denoted as

(3)

M(R;h_{R^{\prime}}):=\bigl\{h_{R}\in M(R)\,\big|\,h_{R}|_{R^{\prime}}=h_{R^{\prime}}\bigr\}.

The set
M(R)
is never empty; indeed, the function
h_{R}
that maps each point
in
R
to either
0
or to
1
according to the parity of the source point
is a height function.
A natural question to ask is under which conditions the set of extensions

M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})
is nonempty. Necessary and sufficient conditions are given by
the Kirszbraun theorem for functions on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
which is a discrete analogue of the
classical Kirszbraun theorem for Lipschitz functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This is a well-known result (see e.g.
[
She05
, Lemma 4.3.1]
),
and we omit the proof from this article.

Theorem 2

(Kirszbraun theorem for
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
)
.

Let
R
be a connected region of
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, let
R^{\prime}
be a subset of
R
,
and let
h^{\prime}:R^{\prime}\to\mathbb{Z}
be a graph homomorphism that preserves parity.
There exists a graph homomorphism
h:R\to\mathbb{Z}

such that
h=h^{\prime}
on
R^{\prime}
if and only if for all
x,y\in R^{\prime}
,

(4)

\bigl|h^{\prime}(x)-h^{\prime}(y)\bigr|\leq d_{R}(x,y),

where
d_{R}
denotes the graph distance on
R
.

Remark 3
.

One might hope to derive sufficient conditions that are easier to verify,
such as the following: suppose that
R^{\prime}
is a line segment,
i.e.
R^{\prime}=\{v,v+e,v+2e,\dotsc,v+(\ell-1)e\}

for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
v,e\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
|e|_{1}=1
.
Then
R^{\prime}
is an isometric subgraph of
R
,
meaning that for any points
x,y\in R^{\prime}
,
d_{R^{\prime}}(x,y)=d_{R}(x,y)
.
Indeed, clearly
d_{R^{\prime}}(x,y)\geq d_{R}(x,y)
,
and for any
i,j
, by observation

d_{R^{\prime}}(v+ie,v+je)=|i-j|=d_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(v+ie,v+je)\leq d_{R}(x,y)
.
Since
R^{\prime}
is an isometric subgraph of every
R\supseteq R^{\prime}
,
it follows that for any
h_{R^{\prime}}\in M(R^{\prime})
and any
x,y\in R^{\prime}
,
we have
|h_{R^{\prime}}(x)-h_{R^{\prime}}(y)|\leq d_{R^{\prime}}(x,y)=d_{R}(x,y)
.
Thus by the Kirszbraun theorem
h_{R^{\prime}}
admits at least one extension to
R
.
This idea can be pushed a little bit farther.
For example, the exact same argument works whenever
R^{\prime}
is a geodesic,
i.e. a shortest path between two points in
R
;
a similar argument via the isometric subgraph property applies
when ever
R^{\prime}
is a box,
i.e. when
R^{\prime}=\{(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\colon a_{i}\leq z_{i}\leq b_{i}\}

for some
a_{1}\leq b_{1},\dotsc,a_{d}\leq b_{d}
.
However, this approach ultimately is not fruitful for our current purposes.
The motivating example we use is the case where
R
is a box
and
R^{\prime}
is its boundary, and one can check easily that this subgraph
R^{\prime}

is not isometric. For example, in two dimensions, if

R=\{0,1,2\}\times\{0,1,2\}
,
then the opposite midpoints
(0,1)
and
(2,1)
have

d_{R^{\prime}}((0,1),(2,1))=4>2=d_{R}((0,1),(2,1))
.
Indeed, one can check that there is a unique height function on
R^{\prime}

with
h_{R^{\prime}}((0,1))=1
and
h_{R^{\prime}}((2,1))=5
,
which violates the Kirszbraun hypothesis (
4
).
As such, in the sequel we must account for the possibility that

M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})
may be empty for some or even for all
h_{R^{\prime}}\in M(R^{\prime})
.

Assumption 4

(Random potential

.

Let
\omega=(\omega_{e})_{e\in E(\mathbb{Z})}\in\mathbb{R}^{E(\mathbb{Z})}

denote a random potentials, defined on the set of edges

of

that satisfies the following assumptions:

\omega
is shift invariant, i.e. for any

(6)

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\xi(\omega_{e_{x_{1},y_{1}}},\dotsc,\omega_{e_{x_{k},y_{k}}})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\xi(\omega_{e_{x_{1}+z,y_{1}+z}},\dotsc,\omega_{e_{x_{k}+z,y_{k}+z}})\bigr].

•

Moreover, the random potential
\omega
is ergodic
with respect to the set of shifts

\{\tau_{z}\,|\,z\in\mathbb{Z},\,z=0\pmod{2}\}
,
where
\tau_{z}(e_{x,y})=e_{x+z,y+z}
.
This means that if
E=\tau_{2}^{-1}(E)
,
then
\mathbb{P}(E)\in\{0,1\}
.

•

As a matter of normalization, assume that

\mathbb{E}[\omega_{(0,1)}]=0
.

Definition 5

(The Hamiltonian)
.

Given a finite connected graph
R\subset\mathbb{Z}^{m}
,
the interior Hamiltonian
H^{\circ}_{R}:M(R)\to\mathbb{R}

and the exterior Hamiltonian
H^{+}_{R}:M(R^{+})\to\mathbb{R}

are defined as

(7)

H^{\circ}_{R}(h_{R},\omega)=\sum_{e\in E(R)}\omega_{h_{R}(e)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H^{+}_{R}(h_{R^{+}},\omega)=\sum_{e\in E(R^{+})}\omega_{h_{R^{+}}(e)},

where
R^{+}:=R\cup\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\colon\exists y\in R,x\sim y\}

is the extension of the subgraph
R
by all neighboring vertices in the ambient
graph
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.

Definition 6

(Quenched measure)
.

Given a realization
\omega
of the random potential
and a set
A\subset M(R)
of height functions,
the partition function
Z_{\omega}(A)
is given by

(8)

Z_{\omega}(A)=\sum_{h_{R}\in A}\exp\bigl(H^{\circ}_{R}(h_{R},\omega)\bigr)\,.

Given a subgraph
R^{\prime}\subset R
,
a height function
h_{R^{\prime}}\in M(R^{\prime})
,
and a realization
\omega
of the random potential,
the perturbed measure
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(\cdot,\omega)

is the probability measure on the finite space
M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})
,
defined on atoms
h_{R}\in M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})
by

(9)

\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(h_{R},\omega):=\frac{\exp\bigl(H^{\circ}_{R}(h_{R},\omega)\bigr)}{Z_{\omega}\bigl(M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})\bigr)}.

Definition 7

(Annealed measure)
.

Below we prove that, for any
R^{\prime}\subset R
and any
h_{R^{\prime}}\in M(R^{\prime})
,
the function
(\omega,A)\mapsto\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(A,\omega)

from
\Omega\times\mathcal{P}(M(R;h_{R^{\prime}}))
to
[0,1]

is a probability kernel.
In other words, for all
\omega\in\Omega
,

A\mapsto\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(A,\omega)
is a probability measure,
and for all
A\subset M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})
,

\omega\mapsto\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(A,\omega)
is measurable.
Thus, the formula

(\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\circ\mathbb{P})(A):=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(A,\omega)],\quad A\subset M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})

defines a probability measure
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\circ\mathbb{P}
on
M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})
.
Moreover, for any function
f:M(R;R^{\prime})\to\mathbb{R}
,
if
h_{R}
is a random variable with law
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\circ\mathbb{P}

then

(10)

E[f(X)]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[E_{\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(\cdot,\omega)}(f)\bigr].

Proof (of claims in Definition
7
).

By definition
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(\cdot,\omega)
is a probability measure for fixed
\omega
.
The second property follows from how
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(h_{R},\omega)
is defined:
the numerator is a sum of finitely many random potential values
\omega_{e}

inside of the (continuous, hence measurable) exponential function,
and the denominator is a finite sum over copies of the numerator,
only using different height functions to select the random potential
values
\omega_{e}
.
The equation (

) is a standard identity for regular conditional
distributions and its proof is a straightforward exercise;
see e.g.
[
Dur10
, Exercise 5.1.14]
. Note that since
M(R;R^{\prime})
is finite,
each function
f:M(R;R^{\prime})\to\mathbb{R}
is measurable.

3.
The concentration inequality

Having stated the definitions above,
we are prepared to state the main result of this note,
then move on to proofs.

Theorem 8
.

Let
R_{n}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{m}
be a sequence of finite, connected subgraphs
such that
\diam(R_{n}):=\max_{x,y\in R_{n}}|x-y|_{1}\leq An
for some
A>0
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
and
h_{\partial R_{n}}\in M({\partial R_{n}})
be given,
and let
\mu_{n}=\mu_{M(R;h_{\partial R_{n}})}(\cdot,\omega)

denote the (perturbed) distribution on
M(R;h_{\partial R_{n}})
.
Then for any
c>0
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(11)

\mu_{n}\circ\mathbb{P}\biggl(\max_{v\in R_{n}}\,\Big\lvert h_{R_{n}}(v)-E_{\mu_{n}}(h_{R_{n}}(v))\Big\rvert\geq c\sqrt{n}\biggr)\leq 2\,|R_{n}|\,e^{-nc^{2}/A}.

Remark 9
.

Theorem
8
gives quantitative bounds for the probability that
a height function
h_{R}
differs from expected value on the scale of
\sqrt{n}
.
The probability bounds are exponential in a constant times
n
,
which comes about because of the one-dimensional
nature of the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality.
In comparison, results such as the large deviations principle
of
[
She05
,
LT20
]
achieve a volume-order term in the exponential.
The cost of those results is that control over the size of fluctuations
is not quantitative.
For example, ignoring the random potential
\omega

and using the uniform measure on
M(R;h_{\partial R})
instead,
the large deviations principle implies that

\mu_{n}(\max_{v}|h_{R_{n}}(v)-E_{\mu_{n}}(h_{R_{n}}(v))|\geq\varepsilon)\leq\exp(-n^{m}I(\varepsilon))
.
Here
I(\varepsilon)>0
is (related to) the rate function of the LDP,
which does not admit an obvious closed form expression in terms of
\varepsilon
.
It would be interesting if we could obtain a quantitative concentration result
with volume-order term in the exponential of the probability bound.

We will build up to the proof of the concentration inequality
via a few intermediate results.
Lemma
10
below establishes the monotonicity property,
which is the main ingredient of the proof.
We derive from it Corollary
11
,
which is used in the proof of an auxiliary concentration inequality
in Lemma
13

The difference between Lemma
13
and the main theorem
is that the former addresses only a single point

,
whereas the latter concerns the maximum deviation from the mean
over the entire domain.
The statements and proofs of these results are based on
the method presented in
[
CEP96
]
;
we cite the analogous steps where appropriate below.
Differences arise starting with Corollary
11

where the shift-invariant and ergodic properties of the law of
\omega

must be used to account for the fact that height functions
with different base heights “see” different random potential values
\omega_{e}
.
However, the essential steps of the proof still goes through,
since even under the influence of
\omega
the relevant measures
are Gibbs measures, based upon a finite-range potential
(indeed, a nearest-neighbor potential, which enforces the Lipschitz property).
It seems like the proof should extend to other finite-range models
and perhaps beyond, but for brevity we will not explore that idea further here.

, then this case does indeed reduce
to the trivial assertion that
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}
is stochastically dominated by itself.)
However, it does hold that
h_{R^{\prime}}
and
\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}
have the same extensions
to
R\setminus R^{\prime}
; to be very precise,

(12)

\bigl\{h_{R}|_{R\setminus R^{\prime}}\colon h_{R}\in M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})\bigr\}=\bigl\{\widetilde{h}_{R}|_{R\setminus R^{\prime}}\colon\widetilde{h}_{R}\in M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})\bigr\}.

For clarity, let us repeat the above paragraph
in the context of Figure
2
.
Case 1 of the proof concerns data
h_{R^{\prime}}
and
\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}
that agree
on the solid black circle region (i.e.
\partial_{R}(R^{\prime})
),
though they may differ on the solid black square points
(i.e.
R^{\prime}\setminus\partial_{R}(R^{\prime})
).
By definition of
\partial_{R}(R^{\prime})
, the solid black circle points surround
the white circle region (i.e.
R\setminus R^{\prime}
),
at least relative to the domain
R
.
(Often we will assume that
\partial R\subset R^{\prime}
,
but that assumption isn’t necessary here, and it does not hold in the figure.)
Since
h_{R^{\prime}}
and
\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}
agree on
\partial_{R}(R^{\prime})
,
they have the same extensions to the white circle region,
in the sense of (
12
).

An easy calculation shows that for
h_{R}\in M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})
,

(13)

\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(h_{R},\omega)=\frac{\exp\bigl(H^{+}_{R\setminus R^{\prime}}(h_{R},\omega)\bigr)}{\sum_{f_{R}\in M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\exp\bigl(H^{+}_{R\setminus R^{\prime}}(f_{R},\omega)\bigr)},

where we recall that
H^{+}_{R\setminus R^{\prime}}
denotes the Hamiltonian on domain

R\setminus R^{\prime}
, including the edges that cross between
R\setminus R^{\prime}

and
\partial_{R}(R^{\prime})
.
Therefore in particular that the right-hand expression (
13
)
depends only on the values of the extension
h_{R}

restricted
(R\setminus R^{\prime})\cup\partial_{R}(R^{\prime})
.
The same is true of extensions
\widetilde{h}_{R}\in M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})
.
As such, the obvious bijection between the two sets in (
12
)
is measure-preserving in both directions.
The existence of a coupling
\pi
satisfying the claims of the lemma
follows immediately.

Case 2: Assume instead that
h_{R^{\prime}}(v)<\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}(v)
for some
v\in R^{\prime}

adjacent to a vertex
v^{\prime\prime}\in R\setminus R^{\prime}
.
In Figure
2
,
v^{\prime\prime}
is the white circle marked with an “X,”
and
v
might be either of the adjacent solid black circles.
Let
R^{\prime\prime}=R^{\prime}\cup\{v^{\prime\prime}\}
.
We proceed by induction on the cardinality of
R\setminus R^{\prime}
.
The induction hypothesis states that given any height functions

h_{R^{\prime\prime}},\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime\prime}}\in M(R^{\prime\prime})
such that
h_{R^{\prime\prime}}\leq\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime\prime}}

and such that both
M(R;h_{R^{\prime\prime}})
and
M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime\prime}})
are nonempty,
the measure
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime\prime}})}
is stochastically dominated
by
\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime\prime}})}
.
Note that the base case of the induction occurs when
R\setminus R^{\prime}=\{v^{\prime\prime}\}

has cardinality
1
, and so
R^{\prime\prime}=R
; the lemma is trivial in this case.

So let us extend induction hypothesis from
R^{\prime\prime}
to
R^{\prime}
.
From the hypotheses of the lemma, each of
h_{R^{\prime}}
and
\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}

admits at least one extension to
R
.
Therefore each admits at least one extension to
R^{\prime\prime}

that in turn admits an extension to
R
.
On the other hand since
R^{\prime\prime}\setminus R^{\prime}=\{v^{\prime\prime}\}

is a set of cardinality
1
,
each of
h_{R^{\prime}}
and
\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}
admits at most two extensions to
R^{\prime}
.
Formally, let
h_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{+}
and
h_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{-}
denote
the two possible extensions of
h_{R^{\prime}}
to
R^{\prime\prime}
,
where
h_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{\pm}(v^{\prime\prime})=h_{R^{\prime}}(v)\pm 1
.
Below we will address the possibility that one or the other of these
putative extensions does not exist.
Likewise, let
\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{\pm}\in M(R^{\prime\prime};\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})
denote the two extensions
of
\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}
to
R^{\prime\prime}
, subject to the possibility that one or the other of the
two extensions may not exist.

By conditioning on the height value at
v^{\prime\prime}
,
we see that

(14)

\displaystyle\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=p^{+}\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{+})}+p^{-}\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{-})}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{p}^{+}\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{+})}+\widetilde{p}^{-}\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{-})},

where

(15)

\displaystyle p^{\pm}

\displaystyle:=\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(\{h_{R}(v^{\prime\prime})=h_{R^{\prime}}(v)\pm 1\})\in[0,1]\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}^{\pm}

\displaystyle:=\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})}(\{\widetilde{h}_{R}(v^{\prime\prime})=\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}(v)\pm 1\})\in[0,1].

This addresses the issue noted above, about the possibility that one (but not
both) of
h_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{\pm}
may not exist; if so, the corresponding
p^{\pm}
term
is
0
, and the other
p^{\mp}
term is
1
.
By parity considerations, it must hold (assuming that the various extensions
exist), that

h_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{-}(v^{\prime\prime})<h_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{+}(v^{\prime\prime})=h_{R^{\prime}}(v)+1\leq\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}(v)-1=\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{-}(v^{\prime\prime})<\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{+}(v^{\prime\prime}).

By (up to) four applications of the induction hypothesis,
we conclude that each of the measures
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{\pm})}

is stochastically dominated by each of the measures

\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime\prime}}^{\pm})}
.
Since all the measures are probability measures
and since all four of
p^{\pm},\widetilde{p}^{\pm}
are nonnegative,
the identities (
14
) implies that

\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}
is stochastically dominated by
\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})}
.
∎

We will make use of stochastic dominance via expectations,
as captured in the following corollary.

Corollary 11

(cf.
[
CEP96
, Corollary 19]
)
.

Let
R^{\prime}\subset R
, let
v\in R\setminus R^{\prime}
,
and let
h_{R^{\prime}},\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}\in M(R^{\prime})
with
h_{R^{\prime}}\leq\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}+2
.
Let
h_{R}(v)
and
\widetilde{h}_{R}(v)
denote the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variables obtained
by sampling
h_{R}
from
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\circ\mathbb{P}
and
\widetilde{h}_{R}
from
\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})}\circ\mathbb{P}

and evaluating the respective height functions at
v
.
Then

E_{\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\circ\mathbb{P}}[h_{R}(v)]\leq E_{\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})}\circ\mathbb{P}}[\widetilde{h}_{R}(v)]+2.

Remark 12
.

Notice that unlike the stochastic monotonicity result of Lemma
10
,
which is almost sure in
\omega
,
the corollary above requires an expectation over the law
\mathbb{P}
of
\omega
.
This is a substantial difference from
[
CEP96
]

caused by the random potential.
Indeed, the requirement arises from the fact that
h_{R^{\prime}}
and
\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}+2

“see” a different part of the random potential
\omega
.
Since
\mu
is a shift-invariant Gibbs measure,
we can average out this height shift by annealing over the random potential.

Proof.

By Lemma
10
, for each fixed
\omega

we have
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(\cdot,\omega)\overset{\textnormal{law}}{\leq}\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}+2)}(\cdot,\omega)
,
so

E_{\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(\cdot,\omega)}[h_{R}(v)]\leq E_{\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}+2)}(\cdot,\omega)}[\widetilde{h}_{R}(v)],\quad\text{for a.e.~$\omega$}.

We will transfer the height shift from the “
\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}+2
”
into the random potential
\omega
and into the height function
inside the expectation.
Indeed, from the definition of the Hamiltonian, we have for
R\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}

and
f_{R}\in M(R)
that

(16)

H^{\circ}_{R}(f_{R}+2,\omega)=H^{\circ}_{R}(f_{R},\tau_{2}\omega),

where
\tau_{2}:\Omega\to\Omega
is defined by

(\tau_{2}(\omega))_{x,x+1}=\omega_{x+2,x+3}
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
.
A straightforward calculation (see Appendix
A
)
establishes that, for any
h_{R}\in M(R)
,

(17)

\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}+2)}(h_{R},\omega)=\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})}(h_{R}-2,\tau_{2}\omega).

By change of variables,

E_{\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}(\cdot,\omega)}[h_{R}(v)]\leq E_{\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})}(\cdot,\tau_{2}\omega)}[\widetilde{h}_{R}(v)+2],\quad\text{for a.e.~$\omega$}.

Take expectations with respect to
\mathbb{P}
.
Under the expectation the shift
\tau_{2}
vanishes, by ergodicity.
The result follows by construction of the measures

\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\circ\mathbb{P}
and
\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})}\circ\mathbb{P}
;
cf. equation (
10
).
∎

Now we are prepared to prove a limited version of the concentration inequality,
where we are concerned with only a single point
v\in R
.
The key to the proof is the monotonicity of Corollary
11
.
We translate this into an inductive bound on martingale differences:
each time we take a “step” starting at the boundary
\partial R
and “walking”
towards
v
, the two possible extensions at that step differ by at most
2
.
Then we use the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality to establish the probability bound.
From this point on the proof is standard,
following closely to the methods used in
[
CEP96
]
and other works.

Lemma 13

(Auxiliary concentration inequality
cf.
[
CEP96
, Theorem 21]
)
.

Let
h_{\partial R}\in M({\partial R})
and let
v\in R
be such that there is a path

x_{0}\in{\partial R},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{l-1}=v
of length
l

with
x_{i}\sim x_{i-1}
for
i=1,\dotsc,l-1
.
Then for any
c>0
,

\mu_{M(R;h_{\partial R})}\circ\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\Bigl\{h_{R}\in M(R;h_{\partial R})\colon\big\lvert h_{R}(v)-E_{\mu_{M(R;h_{\partial R})}}[h_{R}(v)]\big\rvert>lc\Bigr\}\Bigr)<2e^{-lc^{2}/2}.

Proof.

For
k=1,\dotsc,l
, define
\sigma
-algebras

\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(h_{R}\mapsto h_{R}(x_{i}),0\leq i<k)\subset\mathcal{P}(M(R;h_{\partial R}))

and define a martingale
M_{k}:=E[h_{R}(v)|\mathcal{F}_{k}]
,
where
E[\,\cdot\,]
denotes the expectation
with respect to the measure
\mu_{M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\circ\mathbb{P}
.
Note that
M_{1}=E[h_{R}(v)]

and that
M_{l}=h_{R}(v)
.

We claim that for each
k=1,\dotsc,l-1
,
the martingale difference
|M_{k+1}-M_{k}|

is less than or equal to
2
almost surely.
To this end, fix
k

and condition on
h(x_{i})=z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
i=0,\dotsc,k-1
.
To avoid events of probability zero,
assume that
z_{0},\dotsc,z_{k-1}
are such that
there exists at least one extension in
M(h_{R};h_{\partial R})

with
h_{R}(x_{i})=z_{i}
for each
i
;
by hypothesis
M(h_{R};h_{\partial R})
is nonempty,
so at least one such assignment of heights
z_{i}
exists.

Having fixed these height values,
there are at most two assignments
of the height value
z_{k}:=h_{R}(x_{k})

which admit further extensions in
M(R;h_{\partial R})
:
namely,
z_{k}=z_{k-1}\pm 1
.
Therefore the martingale
M_{k+1}=E[h_{R}(v)|\mathcal{F}_{k+1}]

takes at most two distinct values
conditioned on
\{h_{R}(x_{i})=z_{i},\,i=0,\dotsc,k-1\}
.
Because the (at most) two possible values of
h_{R}(x_{k})
differ by at most
2
,
and because the height values at
x_{0},\dotsc,x_{k-1}
have been fixed,
Corollary
11
applied with
R^{\prime}=\{x_{0},\dotsc,x_{k}\}
implies that
the (at most) two distinct values of
M_{k+1}
differ by at most
2
.
Since
M_{k}=E[M_{k+1}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]
is the weighted average of these (at most)
two values of
M_{k+1}
, it follows that
\lvert M_{k+1}-M_{k}\rvert\leq 2
.
The conclusion follows immediately from the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality.
∎

Now we are prepared to prove the main result, i.e. the concentration
inequality.

Proof of Theorem
8
.

By the union bound and Lemma
13
,

(18)

\mu_{n}\circ\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\max_{v\in R_{n}}\>\big\lvert h_{R_{n}}(v)-E_{\mu_{n}}(h_{R_{n}}(v))\big\rvert\geq c\sqrt{n}\Bigr)\leq\sum_{v\in R_{n}}\mu_{n}\circ\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\,\big\lvert h_{R_{n}}(v)-E_{\mu_{n}}(h_{R_{n}}(v))\big\rvert\geq c\sqrt{n}\Bigr).

For each
v\in R_{n}
, apply Lemma
13

with the path length parameter
l_{v}
chosen as small as possible
and with parameter
c_{v}
chosen
such that
l_{v}c_{v}=c\sqrt{n}
.
Recall that by hypothesis the diameter of
R_{n}
is at most
An
,
so
l\leq An/2
.
It follows that

l_{v}c_{v}^{2}=\frac{(l_{v}c_{v})^{2}}{l_{v}}=\frac{c^{2}n}{l_{v}}\geq\frac{2c^{2}n}{A}.

Using also the hypotheses that
|R_{n}|\leq Bn^{m}
, we have

(19)

\displaystyle\mu_{n}\circ\mathbb{P}\biggl(\max_{v\in R_{n}}\>\big\lvert h_{R_{n}}(v)-E_{\mu}(h_{R_{n}}(v))\big\rvert\geq c\sqrt{n}\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in R_{n}}\mu_{n}\circ\mathbb{P}\biggl(\big\lvert h_{R_{n}}(v)-E_{\mu_{n}}\bigl(h_{R_{n}}(v)\bigr)\big\rvert\geq l_{v}c_{v}\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in R_{n}}2e^{-l_{v}c_{v}^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2|R_{n}|e^{-c^{2}n/A}.

∎

Appendix A
Proof of Eq. (
17
) from Corollary
11

The following equation was used in the proof of Corollary
11
above.
The proof that this equation holds has been moved here in an appendix,
since it requires several lines, and it is not enlightening or crucial enough to
justify taking space in the body of the proof.
The equation in question is (cf. (
17
) above):

\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})+2}(h_{R},\omega)=\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})}(h_{R}-2,\tau_{2}\omega).

Indeed:

\displaystyle\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}}+2)}(h_{R},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\bigl(H^{\circ}_{R}(h_{R},\omega)\bigr)}{\sum_{f_{R}\in M(R;h_{R^{\prime}}+2)}\exp\bigl(H^{\circ}_{R}(f_{R},\omega)\bigr)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\bigl(H^{\circ}_{R}(h_{R},\omega)\bigr)}{\sum_{f_{R}\in M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\exp\bigl(H^{\circ}_{R}(f_{R}+2,\omega)\bigr)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\bigl(H^{\circ}_{R}(h_{R},\omega)\bigr)}{\sum_{f_{R}\in M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\exp\bigl(H^{\circ}_{R}(f_{R},\tau_{2}\omega)\bigr)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\bigl(H^{\circ}_{R}(h_{R}-2,\tau_{2}\omega)\bigr)}{\sum_{f_{R}\in M(R;h_{R^{\prime}})}\exp\bigl(H^{\circ}_{R}(f_{R},\tau_{2}\omega)\bigr)}

\displaystyle=\mu_{M(R;\widetilde{h}_{R^{\prime}})}(h_{R}-2,\tau_{2}\omega).

∎
