𝒒 \boldsymbol{q} -Middle Convolution and 𝒒 \boldsymbol{q} -Painlevé Equation
Shoko SASAKI a, Shun TAKAGI a and Kouichi TAKEMURA b

Abstract
A q q -deformation of the middle convolution was introduced by Sakai and Yamaguchi. We apply it to a linear q q -difference equation associated with the q q -Painlevé VI equation. Then we obtain integral transformations. We investigate the q q -middle convolution in terms of the affine Weyl group symmetry of the q q -Painlevé VI equation. We deduce an integral transformation on the q q -Heun equation.

Introduction

The middle convolution was introduced by Katz
[
8
]
for local systems on a punctured Riemann sphere, and Dettweiler and Reiter
[
3
,
4
]
reformulated it for the Fuchsian system of differential equations.
Here the Fuchsian system of differential equations is the system of linear differential equations written as

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}Y}{{\rm d}x}=\bigg(\frac{A_{1}}{x-t_{1}}+\frac{A_{2}}{x-t_{2}}+\dots+\frac{A_{r}}{x-t_{r}}\bigg)Y,

(1.1)

where
Y
is a column vector with
n
entries and
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{r}
are constant matrices of size
n\times n
.
We review briefly the definition of the middle convolution for equation (
1.1
) (or the tuple of the matrices
(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})
).
Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
F_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,r
, be the matrix of size
nr\times nr
of the form

\displaystyle F_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}O&\cdots&O&\cdots&O\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i}+\lambda I_{n}&\cdots&A_{r}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
O&\cdots&O&\cdots&O\end{pmatrix}{\scriptstyle(i)},

(1.2)

where
I_{n}
is the identity matrix of size
n
.
Then the correspondence of the tuple of matrices
(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})\mapsto(F_{1},\dots,F_{r})
(or the correspondence of the associated Fuchsian system) is called the convolution.
The convolution does not preserve the irreducibility in general.
It is shown that the following subspaces
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{L}
of
\mathbb{C}^{nr}
are preserved by the action of
F_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,r
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\begin{pmatrix}\ker A_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\ker A_{r}\end{pmatrix}\!,\qquad\mathcal{L}=\ker(F_{1}+F_{2}+\cdots+F_{r}).

We denote the linear transformation induced from the action of
F_{i}
on the quotient space
\mathbb{C}^{nr}/(\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{L})
by
\overline{F}_{i}
.
The correspondence of the tuple of matrices
(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})\mapsto\big(\overline{F}_{1},\dots,\overline{F}_{r}\big)
(or the correspondence of the associated Fuchsian system) is called the middle convolution.
It was shown in
[
3
]
that the convolution is related with Euler’s integral transformation.
Let
Y(x)
be a solution of equation (
1.1
).
Set

\displaystyle W_{j}(x)=\frac{Y(x)}{x-t_{j}},\qquad W(x)=\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
W_{r}(x)\end{pmatrix}\!.

Then
W(x)
is a column vector with
nr
entries.
We apply Euler’s integral transformation for each entry of
W(x)
, i.e., we set

\displaystyle G(x)=\int_{\Delta}W(s)(x-s)^{\lambda}\,{\rm d}s,

where
\Delta
is an appropriate cycle in
\mathbb{C}
with the variable
s
.
Then the function
G(x)
satisfies the following Fuchsian system of differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}Y}{{\rm d}x}=\bigg(\frac{F_{1}}{x-t_{1}}+\frac{F_{2}}{x-t_{2}}+\dots+\frac{F_{r}}{x-t_{r}}\bigg)Y,

where
F_{1},\dots,F_{r}
were defined in equation (
1.2
).

Sakai and Yamaguchi
[
10
]
constructed a theory of a
q
-deformation of the middle convolution for systems of
q
-difference equations.
Here the system is described as

\displaystyle Y(qx)=\bigg(B_{\infty}+\frac{B_{1}}{1-x/t_{1}}+\dots+\frac{B_{r}}{1-x/t_{r}}\bigg)Y(x),

where
Y(x)
is a column vector with
n
entries and
B_{\infty},B_{1},\dots,B_{r}
are constant matrices of size
n\times n
.
The construction of the
q
-middle convolution is similar to the case of the Fuchsian system of differential equations.
For details, see Section
2
.

In this paper we apply the
q
-middle convolution to linear
q
-difference equations which are related to the
q
-Painlevé VI equation

\displaystyle\frac{y\overline{y}}{a_{3}a_{4}}=\frac{(\overline{z}-tb_{1})(\overline{z}-tb_{2})}{(\overline{z}-b_{3})(\overline{z}-b_{4})},\qquad\frac{z\overline{z}}{b_{3}b_{4}}=\frac{(y-ta_{1})(y-ta_{2})}{(y-a_{3})(y-a_{4})},

(1.3)

with the constraint
b_{1}b_{2}a_{3}a_{4}=qa_{1}a_{2}b_{3}b_{4}
.
Here
\overline{y}
and
\overline{z}
denotes the time evolution
t\mapsto qt
of
y
and
z
, and the parameters
a_{1},\dots,a_{4},b_{1},\dots,b_{4}
are time-independent.
The
q
-Painlevé VI equation was introduced by Jimbo and Sakai
[
6
]
as a
q
-deformation of the Painlevé VI equation.
They obtained equation (
1.3
) by introducing a
q
-analogue of the monodromy preserving deformation, and it is related to the linear
q
-difference equations

\displaystyle Y(qx)=A(x)Y(x),

(1.4)

where
A(x)
is a
2\times 2
matrix with polynomial entries (see equation (
3.2
) for details).
The linear
q
-difference equation which we apply the
q
-middle convolution is not equation (
1.4
) but the transformed equation

\displaystyle Y(qx)=B(x)Y(x),

\displaystyle B(x)=\frac{A(x)}{c_{0}(x-ta_{1})(x-ta_{2})}=B_{\infty}+\frac{B_{1}}{1-x/(ta_{1})}+\frac{B_{2}}{1-x/(ta_{2})}

(1.5)

for some constant
c_{0}
.
By applying the
q
-middle convolution with the parameter
\lambda
, we obtain
2\times 2
matrices, if we choose the constants
c_{0}
and
\lambda
suitably.
Then we obtain a correspondence of the parameters, and we may regard it as a correspondence of the
q
-Painlevé VI equations.
On the other hand, it is known that the
q
-Painlevé VI equation has a symmetry of the affine Weyl group of type
D^{(1)}_{5}
, and a realization of the symmetry was described in the review
[
7
]
of Kajiwara, Noumi and Yamada.
In this paper, we express the symmetry by the
q
-middle convolution in terms of the generators of the affine Weyl group.

In
[
11
]
, a relationship between the
q
-Painlevé VI equation and the
q
-Heun equation

\displaystyle\big(x-h_{1}q^{1/2}\big)\big(x-h_{2}q^{1/2}\big)g(x/q)+l_{3}l_{4}\big(x-l_{1}q^{-1/2}\big)\big(x-l_{2}q^{-1/2}\big)g(qx)

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\big\{(l_{3}+l_{4})x^{2}+Ex+(l_{1}l_{2}l_{3}l_{4}h_{1}h_{2})^{1/2}\big(h_{3}^{1/2}+h_{3}^{-1/2}\big)\big\}g(x)=0

was studied from a viewpoint of the initial value space.
In particular, the
q
-Heun equation was obtained from the linear
q
-difference equation associated to the
q
-Painlevé VI equation by specializing the parameters.
On the other hand, the
q
-middle convolution induces an integral transformation of the linear
q
-difference equation.
By considering a particular specialization, the linear
q
-difference equation turns out to be the
q
-Heun equation and we obtain an integral transformation of
q
-Heun equation.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we review a part of the theory of the
q
-middle convolution established by Sakai and Yamaguchi
[
10
]
.
In Section
3
, we recall the linear
q
-difference equation associated to the
q
-Painlevé VI equation and calculate the
q
-middle convolution for it.
In Section
4
, we investigate the symmetry by the
q
-middle convolution in terms of the Weyl group symmetry of the
q
-Painlevé VI equation.
For this purpose, we clarify a relationship between equation (
1.5
) and the Lax pair in
[
7
]
.
In Section
5
, we obtain an integral transformation on the
q
-Heun equation.
In Section
6
, we give concluding remarks.

2
\boldsymbol{q}
-middle convolution

We recall the
q
-convolution and the
q
-middle convolution introduced by Sakai and Yamaguchi
[
10
]
.

Let
\mathbf{B}=(B_{\infty};B_{1},\dots,B_{N})
be the tuple of the square matrices of the same size and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{N})
be the tuple of the non-zero complex numbers which are different from one another.
We denote by
E_{\mathbf{B},\mathbf{b}}
the linear
q
-difference equations

\displaystyle Y(qx)=B(x)Y(x),\qquad B(x)=B_{\infty}+\sum^{N}_{i=1}\frac{B_{i}}{1-x/b_{i}}.

Definition 2.1

(
q
-convolution,
[
10
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{B}=(B_{\infty};B_{1},\dots,B_{N})
be the tuple of
m\times m
matrices and
(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{N})
be the tuple of the non-zero complex numbers which are different one another.
Set
B_{0}=I_{m}-B_{\infty}-B_{1}-\dots-B_{N}
,
We define the
q
-convolution
c_{\lambda}\colon(B_{\infty};B_{1},\dots,B_{N})\mapsto(F_{\infty};F_{1},\dots,F_{N})
as follows:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$F$}=(F_{\infty};F_{1},\dots,F_{N})\mbox{ \rm is a tuple of $(N+1)m\times(N+1)m$ matrices,}

\displaystyle F_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}{}&{}&O&{}&{}\\[5.0pt]
B_{0}&\cdots&B_{i}-\big(1-q^{\lambda}\big)I_{m}&\cdots&B_{N}\\[5.0pt]
{}&{}&O&{}&{}\end{pmatrix}{\scriptstyle(i+1)},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N,

\displaystyle F_{\infty}=I_{(N+1)m}-\widehat{F},\qquad\widehat{F}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{0}&\cdots&B_{N}\\[5.0pt]
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\[5.0pt]
B_{0}&\cdots&B_{N}\end{pmatrix}\!.

Let
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
.
The
q
-convolution induces the
q
-analogue of the Euler’s integral transformation in terms of the Jackson integral

\displaystyle\int^{\xi\infty}_{0}f(x)\,{\rm d}_{q}x=(1-q)\sum^{\infty}_{n=-\infty}q^{n}\xi f(q^{n}\xi)

for the solutions of the
q
-difference equations.
Note that the value of the Jackson integral may depend on the value
\xi
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Let
Y(x)
be a solution of
E_{\mathbf{B},\mathbf{b}}
.
Set
b_{0}=0
and

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(x,s)=\frac{\big(q^{\lambda+1}s/x;q\big)_{\infty}}{(qs/x;q)_{\infty}}=\prod^{\infty}_{i=0}\frac{x-q^{i+\lambda+1}s}{x-q^{i+1}s}.

Define the function
\widehat{Y}(x)
by

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{i}(x)=\int^{\xi\infty}_{0}\frac{P_{\lambda}(x,s)}{s-b_{i}}Y(s)\,{\rm d}_{q}s,\quad i=0,\dots,N,\qquad\widehat{Y}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{Y}_{0}(x)\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{Y}_{N}(x)\end{pmatrix}\!.

Then the function
\widehat{Y}(x)
satisfies the equation
E_{\mathbf{F},\mathbf{b}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}(qx)=\bigg(F_{\infty}+\sum^{N}_{i=1}\frac{F_{i}}{1-x/b_{i}}\bigg)\widehat{Y}(x).

Although the original theorem by Sakai and Yamaguchi was restricted to the case
\xi=1
in the Jackson integral, we may just extend it to the case
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
, which was motivated by the theory of the Jackson integral due to Aomoto
[
1
]
.
The convergence of the Jackson integrals
\widehat{Y}_{i}(x)
,
i=0,\dots,N
, would not be considered in
[
10
]
.
In this paper, we discuss the Jackson integrals formally and we do not consider the convergence in details.
Namely, we discuss the Jackson integrals under the assumption that the integrals converge absolutely and Theorem
2.2
holds true with the convergence.
Thus we use the phrasing “formally” in theorems which are related to the Jackson integral.
An aspect of convergence on Theorem
2.2
will be discussed in
[
2
]
.

The
q
-middle convolution is defined by considering an appropriate quotient space.

Definition 2.3

(
q
-middle convolution,
[
10
]
)
.

We define the
\boldsymbol{F}
-invariant subspaces
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{L}
of
(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{N+1}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{V}}=\begin{pmatrix}\ker B_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\ker B_{N}\end{pmatrix}\!,\qquad\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)=\ker\big(\widehat{F}-\big(1-q^{\lambda}\big)I_{(N+1)m}\big).

We denote the action of
F_{k}
on the quotient space
(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{N+1}/(\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{L})
by
\overline{F}_{k}
,
k=\infty,1,\dots,N
.
Then the
q
-middle convolution
mc_{\lambda}
is defined by the correspondence
E_{\mathbf{B},\mathbf{b}}\mapsto E_{\overline{\mathbf{F}},\mathbf{b}}
, where
\overline{\mathbf{F}}=\big(\overline{F}_{\infty};\overline{F}_{1},\dots,\overline{F}_{N}\big)
.

The
q
-middle convolution
mc_{\lambda}
would induce the integral transformation of the solutions by applying the integral transformation on the
q
-convolution, although it would be necessary to consider the subspace
\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{L}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{N+1}
.

Linear
\boldsymbol{q}
-difference equation associated

to
\boldsymbol{q}

We recall the linear
q
-difference equation

\displaystyle Y(qx)=A(x)Y(x),

(3.1)

which was discussed by Jimbo and Sakai
[
6
]
to obtain the
q
-Painlevé VI equation by the connection preserving deformation.

We take the
2\times 2
matrix
A(x)
in equation (
3.1
) to be of the form

\displaystyle A(x)=A_{0}(t)+A_{1}(t)x+A_{2}x^{2},\qquad A_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{1}&0\\
0&\chi_{2}\end{pmatrix}\!,

\displaystyle A_{0}(t)\mbox{ has eigenvalues }t\theta_{1},\ t\theta_{2},

\displaystyle\det A(x)=\chi_{1}\chi_{2}(x-ta_{1})(x-ta_{2})(x-a_{3})(x-a_{4}).

(3.2)

Then we have the following relation

\displaystyle\chi_{1}\chi_{2}a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}=\theta_{1}\theta_{2}.

(3.3)

Note that the relations to the parameter of the
q
-Painlevé VI equation in equation (
1.3
) are given by
b_{1}=a_{1}a_{2}/\theta_{1}
,
b_{2}=a_{1}a_{2}/\theta_{2}
,
b_{3}=1/(q\chi_{1})
,
b_{4}=1/\chi_{2}
.

We need accessory parameters to determine uniquely the elements of the matrix
A(x)
.
Write

\displaystyle A(x)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}(x)&a_{12}(x)\\
a_{21}(x)&a_{22}(x)\end{pmatrix}\!.

Then
a_{12}(x)
is a linear polynomial.
We introduce the parameters
w
,
y
,
z
and impose the condition

\displaystyle a_{12}(x)=\chi_{2}w(x-y),\qquad a_{11}(x)|_{x=y}=(y-ta_{1})(y-ta_{2})/(qz).

(3.4)

Then the elements of
A(x)
are determined as

\displaystyle A(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{1}((x-y)(x-\alpha)+z_{1})&\chi_{2}w(x-y)\\
\chi_{1}w^{-1}(\gamma x+\delta)&\chi_{2}((x-y)(x-\beta)+z_{2})\end{pmatrix}\!,

(3.5)

where

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{1}{\chi_{1}-\chi_{2}}\big[y^{-1}((\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})t-\chi_{1}z_{1}-\chi_{2}z_{2})-\chi_{2}((a_{1}+a_{2})t+a_{3}+a_{4}-2y)\big],

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{1}{\chi_{1}-\chi_{2}}\big[{-}y^{-1}((\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})t-\chi_{1}z_{1}-\chi_{2}z_{2})+\chi_{1}((a_{1}+a_{2})t+a_{3}+a_{4}-2y)\big],

\displaystyle\gamma=z_{1}+z_{2}+(y+\alpha)(y+\beta)+(\alpha+\beta)y-a_{1}a_{2}t^{2}-(a_{1}+a_{2})(a_{3}+a_{4})t-a_{3}a_{4},

\displaystyle\delta=y^{-1}\big(a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}t^{2}-(\alpha y+z_{1})(\beta y+z_{2})\big)

and

\displaystyle z_{1}=\frac{(y-ta_{1})(y-ta_{2})}{q\chi_{1}z},\qquad z_{2}=q\chi_{1}(y-a_{3})(y-a_{4})z.

We consider the
q
-middle convolution for the
q
-difference equation

\displaystyle Y(qx)=B(x)Y(x),\qquad B(x)=\frac{A(x)}{c_{0}(x-ta_{1})(x-ta_{2})},

(3.6)

where
c_{0}
is a constant which will be fixed later.
Note that, if
\tilde{Y}(x)
is a solution of equation (
3.1
) and the parameter
\mu
satisfies
q^{\mu}=1/\big(c_{0}a_{1}a_{2}t^{2}\big)
, then the function

\displaystyle Y(x)=x^{\mu}(x/(ta_{1});q)_{\infty}(x/(ta_{2});q)_{\infty}\tilde{Y}(x)

satisfies equation (
3.6
).
Write

\displaystyle B(x)=B_{\infty}+\frac{B_{1}}{1-x/(ta_{1})}+\frac{B_{2}}{1-x/(ta_{2})}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}(x)&b_{12}(x)\\
b_{21}(x)&b_{22}(x)\end{pmatrix}\!.

(3.7)

Then

\displaystyle B_{\infty}=\frac{1}{c_{0}}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{1}&0\\
0&\chi_{2}\end{pmatrix}\!,\qquad B_{2}=B_{1}|_{a_{1}\leftrightarrow a_{2}},

(3.8)

\displaystyle B_{1}=\frac{a_{2}}{t\theta_{1}(a_{1}-a_{2})}\begin{pmatrix}(y-ta_{1})b_{1}^{[1]}/(qyz(\chi_{1}-\chi_{2}))&w\chi_{2}(y-ta_{1})\\[4.30554pt]
-b_{1}^{[1]}b_{2}^{[1]}/\big(q^{2}wy^{2}z^{2}(\chi_{1}-\chi_{2})^{2}\big)&-\chi_{2}b_{2}^{[1]}/(qyz(\chi_{1}-\chi_{2}))\end{pmatrix}\!,

where

\displaystyle b_{1}^{[1]}

\displaystyle=q^{2}\chi_{1}^{2}\chi_{2}(y-a_{3})(y-a_{4})z^{2}+(y-ta_{2})(\chi_{2}y-\chi_{1}ta_{1})

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-q\chi_{1}\big\{2\chi_{2}y^{2}-(\chi_{1}ta_{1}+\chi_{2}ta_{2}+\chi_{2}a_{3}+\chi_{2}a_{4})y+t(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})\big\}z,

\displaystyle b_{2}^{[1]}

\displaystyle=q^{2}\chi_{1}(y-a_{3})(y-a_{4})(\chi_{1}y-\chi_{2}ta_{1})z^{2}+(y-ta_{1})^{2}(y-ta_{2})

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-q(y-ta_{1})\big\{2\chi_{1}y^{2}-(\chi_{2}ta_{1}+\chi_{1}ta_{2}+\chi_{1}a_{3}+\chi_{1}a_{4})y+t(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})\big\}z.

It is shown directly that
\det B_{1}=0
and
\det B_{2}=0
.
Set
B_{0}=I_{2}-B_{\infty}-B_{1}-B_{2}(=I_{2}-B(0))
.
Then the condition

is equivalent to
c_{0}=\theta_{1}/(ta_{1}a_{2})
or
c_{0}=\theta_{2}/(ta_{1}a_{2})
.
We now impose the condition
\det B_{0}=0
.
For this purpose, we restrict to the case
c_{0}=\theta_{1}/(ta_{1}a_{2})
.
Note that the case
c_{0}=\theta_{2}/(ta_{1}a_{2})
can be discussed by replacing the parameters as
\theta_{1}\leftrightarrow\theta_{2}
.
We eliminate the parameter
\theta_{2}
by equation (
3.3
).
It follows from
\det B_{0}=0
,
\det B_{1}=0
and
\det B_{2}=0
that there exists non-zero vectors

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}v_{01}\\
v_{02}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}v_{11}\\
v_{12}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}v_{21}\\

such that

\displaystyle B_{0}\begin{pmatrix}v_{01}\\

0\end{pmatrix}\!,\qquad B_{1}\begin{pmatrix}v_{11}\\
v_{12}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}\!,\qquad B_{2}\begin{pmatrix}v_{21}\\
v_{22}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}\!.

(3.9)

We normalize the vectors by setting

\displaystyle v_{01}=qwyz\theta_{1}(\chi_{1}-\chi_{2}),\quad\ v_{11}=qwyz\theta_{1}\chi_{2}(\chi_{1}-\chi_{2}),\quad\ v_{21}=qwyz\theta_{1}\chi_{2}(\chi_{1}-\chi_{2}).

(3.10)

We now apply Definition

.

\displaystyle F_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}{O}&O&{O}\\
B_{0}&B_{1}-\big(1-q^{\lambda}\big)I_{2}&B_{2}\\
{O}&O&{O}\end{pmatrix}\!,\qquad F_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}{O}&O&{O}\\
{O}&O&{O}\\
B_{0}&B_{1}&B_{2}-\big(1-q^{\lambda}\big)I_{2}\end{pmatrix}\!,

\displaystyle\widehat{F}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{0}&B_{1}&B_{2}\\
B_{0}&B_{1}&B_{2}\\
B_{0}&B_{1}&B_{2}\end{pmatrix}\!,\qquad F_{\infty}=I_{6}-\widehat{F}.

(3.11)

The invariant subspaces
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{L}
are described as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{V}}=\begin{pmatrix}\ker B_{0}\\
\ker B_{1}\\
\ker B_{2}\end{pmatrix}\!,\qquad\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{V}}(\lambda)=\ker\big(\widehat{F}-\big(1-q^{\lambda}\big)I_{6}\big).

(3.12)

Hence a basis of the space
\mathcal{K}
is

\displaystyle\left\{\!\begin{pmatrix}v_{01}\\
v_{02}\\
0\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}\!,\begin{pmatrix}0\\

\end{pmatrix}\!\right\}\!.

If

(resp.

) then
\dim(\mathcal{L})=1
and the vector
{}^{t}(0,1,0,1,0,1)
(resp.

) is a basis of the space
\mathcal{L}
.
Here we continue the discussion by setting
q^{\lambda}=\chi_{2}ta_{1}a_{2}/\theta_{1}
.

We introduce the matrix
P
by

\displaystyle P=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&v_{01}&0&0\\
g_{1}&g_{3}&1&v_{02}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&v_{11}&0\\
g_{2}&g_{4}&1&0&v_{12}&0\\
