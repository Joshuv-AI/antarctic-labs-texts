Pushed, pulled and pushmi-pullyu fronts of the Burgers-FKPP equation
Jing An, Christopher Henderson, Lenya Ryzhik

Abstract
We consider the long time behavior of the solutions to the Burgers-FKPP equation with advection of a strength β ∈ ℝ \beta\in{\mathbb{R}} . This equation exhibits a transition from pulled to pushed front behavior at β c = 2 \beta_{c}=2 . We prove convergence of the solutions to a traveling wave in a reference frame centered at a position m β ​ ( t ) m_{\beta}(t) and study the asymptotics of the front location m β ​ ( t ) m_{\beta}(t) . When β < 2 \beta<2 , it has the same form as for the standard Fisher-KPP equation established by Bramson [ 11 , 12 ] : m β ​ ( t ) = 2 ​ t − ( 3 / 2 ) ​ log ⁡ ( t ) + x ∞ + o ⁡ ( 1 ) m_{\beta}(t)=2t-(3/2)\log(t)+x_{\infty}+o(1) as t → + ∞ t\to+\infty . This form is typical of pulled fronts. When β > 2 \beta>2 , the front is located at the position m β ​ ( t ) = c ∗ ​ ( β ) ​ t + x ∞ + o ⁡ ( 1 ) m_{\beta}(t)=c_{*}(\beta)t+x_{\infty}+o(1) with c ∗ ​ ( β ) = β / 2 + 2 / β c_{*}(\beta)=\beta/2+2/\beta , which is the typical form of pushed fronts. However, at the critical value β c = 2 \beta_{c}=2 , the expansion changes to m β ​ ( t ) = 2 ​ t − ( 1 / 2 ) ​ log ⁡ ( t ) + x ∞ + o ⁡ ( 1 ) m_{\beta}(t)=2t-(1/2)\log(t)+x_{\infty}+o(1) , reflecting the “pushmi-pullyu” nature of the front. The arguments for β < 2 \beta<2 rely on a new weighted Hopf-Cole transform that allows to control the advection term, when combined with additional steepness comparison arguments. The case β > 2 \beta>2 relies on standard pushed front techniques. The proof in the case β = β c \beta=\beta_{c} is much more intricate and involves arguments not usually encountered in the study of the Bramson correction. It relies on a somewhat hidden viscous conservation law structure of the Burgers-FKPP equation at β c = 2 \beta_{c}=2 and utilizes a dissipation inequality, which comes from a relative entropy type computation, together with a weighted Nash inequality involving dynamically changing weights.



1
Introduction

We consider the long time behavior of the solutions to the Burgers-FKPP equation

\displaystyle u_{t}+\beta uu_{x}=u_{xx}+u-u^{2},~~t>0,~x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(1.1)

Here,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
is a parameter that measures the strength of the advection effect.
The relevance of this type of nonlinear advection-reaction-diffusion model in biological and chemical applications is discussed in
Chapter 13.4 of
[
42
]
. We refer the interested reader to this book, as well as the references therein, rather than attempting to replicate the discussion here.

Our main interest is in the study of the transition from the “pulled” to “pushed” nature of the Burgers-FKPP equation that happens
at
\beta_{c}=2
and its effect on the long time behavior of the solutions. In order to motivate the
basic questions addressed in the present paper, and before introducing this transition phenomenon, we recall the
well-known results for the classical Fisher-KPP equation as well as the notions of “pulled” and “pushed” fronts.

The classical results for the Fisher-KPP equation

When
\beta=0
, the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
) reduces to the classical Fisher-KPP equation

u_{t}=u_{xx}+u-u^{2},

(1.2)

that dates back to the seminal work of Fisher
[
21
]

and Kolmogorov, Petrovskii and Piskunov
[
33
]
, and has been studied extensively since. This equation arises in numerous applications
in the physical and biological sciences, also discussed in
[
42
]
.
Beyond this, it has been used to study the fine properties of branching Brownian motion after McKean
[
40
]
discovered its connection to (
1.2
); see, for instance,
[
2
,
3
,
13
,
14
,
43
]
and references therein.

Both of the original papers
[
21
]
and
[
33
]

showed that (
1.2
) admits traveling wave solutions
of the form
u(t,x)=U_{c}(x-ct)
for all
c\geq c_{*}=2
, which necessarily satisfy the ODE

-cU_{c}^{\prime}=U_{c}^{\prime\prime}+U_{c}-U_{c}^{2},~~~U_{c}(-\infty)=1,~~~\text{and}~~~U_{c}(+\infty)=0.

(1.3)

We denote by
U_{*}(x)
the traveling wave moving with the minimal speed
c_{*}=2
.
Given
c\geq c_{*}
, solutions to (
1.3
) are unique up to translation in
x
.
One may fix a normalization for
U_{*}(x)
, for example, by requiring that
U_{*}(0)=1/2
.

It was observed already in
[
21
,
33
]
(albeit at a different level of
mathematical rigor) that the
solution to (
1.2
) with an initial condition
that is a step function

\displaystyle u(0,x)=\mathds{1}(x\leq 0):=\begin{cases}1,~~~&\text{if }x\leq 0,\\
\displaystyle 0,&\text{if }x>0,\end{cases}

(1.4)

converges in shape
to a traveling wave: there exists a reference frame
m(t)
so that

u(t,x+m(t))\to U_{*}(x),~~\hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$, uniformly in $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$.}

(1.5)

It was also argued informally in
[
21
]
and proved in

[
33
]
that

m(t)=2t+o(t)\hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$.}

(1.6)

We refer to
m(t)
as the location of the front at time
t>0
since, roughly, it separates the regions
\{u(t,x)\approx 1\}
for
x\ll m(t)
and

\{u(t,x)\approx 0\}
for
x\gg m(t)
.

This result was refined in the pioneering works by Bramson
[
11
,
12
]
, who
used probabilistic techniques and the
connection of (
1.2
) to branching Brownian motion to analyze the front position
m(t)
.
In particular, Bramson showed
that there exists a constant
x_{\infty}
that depends
on the initial condition
u_{\rm in}(x)
for (
1.2
), so that

m(t)=2t-\frac{3}{2}\log t+x_{\infty}+o(1)\hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$.}

(1.7)

This asymptotic expansion holds as long as the initial conditions
u_{\rm in}(x)
decay sufficiently fast to zero as
x\to+\infty
.
We also mention the related work by Uchiyama
[
52
]
and Lau
[
34
]
, which are based on PDE techniques, and more recent refinements and alternative
proofs of Bramson’s result in
[
8
,
27
,
29
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
,
that use both probabilistic and PDE methods. Very recently, spectral techniques have been applied
to study the “Bramson shift” in
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
, including some problems that do not obey the comparison principle.

In particular, it was shown in
[
27
,
45
]
that
convergence in (
1.5
) is only algebraic in time:

|u(t,x+m(t))-U_{*}(x)|\leq\frac{C(1+|x|)e^{-x}}{\sqrt{t}},~~~~x>0.

(1.8)

The more precise results in
[
27
]
show that the convergence rate can not be improved to an exponential in time rate: even
convergence in shape to a traveling
wave does not hold beyond the order
O(t^{-1})
. The algebraic rate of convergence of the solution to (
1.2
) to a shift of a traveling wave is closely related to the fact that FKPP fronts are “pulled”– that is, the long time behavior of the solutions is governed by the behavior far ahead of the front, where
u
is small.
Hence, the problem is not compact in a certain sense, making the algebraic-in-time (rather than exponential) rate of convergence
natural.

The “pulled” behavior should be contrasted with the class of equations of the
form similar to (
1.2
):

v_{t}=v_{xx}+f(v)

(1.9)

but with solutions that behave as “pushed fronts” – that is, the long time behavior of the solutions is governed by the behavior at the front, where
u
is neither small nor approximately
1
. An example of such
f
is a bistable nonlinearity of the form
f(v)=v(1-v)(\theta-v)
,
with some
\theta\in(0,1)
. Unlike the Fisher-KPP equation, in the pushed cases
solutions to the initial value problem for (
1.9
) with a rapidly decaying initial condition
v(0,x)=v_{\rm{in}}(x)

converge to a shift of the traveling wave
U_{f}(x)
for (
1.9
) exponentially fast in time: there exists
\omega>0
such that

|v(t,x+m_{f}(t))-U_{f}(x)|\leq Ce^{-\omega t}.

(1.10)

Moreover,
the front location has the asymptotics

m_{f}(t)=c_{f}t+x_{\infty},~~\hbox{as $t\to+\infty$},

(1.11)

without any logarithmic in time correction.
The rate of convergence in (
1.10
) is exponential in time precisely because the fronts are “pushed,” so that the long time behavior
is determined by what happens in a compact region around the front, and not by the tail behavior as
x\to+\infty
.
We refer to the monograph
[
38
]
and extensive presentations
in
[
19
,
51
]

for, respectively, an applied mathematics and a physics perspective on pushed
and pulled fronts, and to
[
24
]
for a recent mathematical analysis, among
many other references.

The pulled to pushed fronts transition in the Burgers-FKPP equation

As we have mentioned, the long time behavior of the reaction-diffusion equations that admit
either pulled or pushed fronts is reasonably well-understood, at least on an intuitive level.
An interesting aspect of the
Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
) is that it exhibits a transition from the pulled
to pushed behavior at
\beta_{c}=2
.

The behavior of traveling waves for (
1.1
) already illustrates the change in behavior at
\beta_{c}=2
. For a given
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
) admits
traveling wave solutions for all
c\geq c_{*}(\beta)
, with the minimal speed

\displaystyle c_{*}=\begin{cases}2,~~~&\text{if }\beta\leq 2,\\
\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2}+\frac{2}{\beta},&\text{if }\beta\geq 2.\end{cases}

(1.12)

The minimal speed traveling wave
\phi_{\beta}
satisfies

-c_{*}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}+\beta\phi_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}=\phi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}+\phi_{\beta}-\phi_{\beta}^{2},~~~~\phi_{\beta}(-\infty)=1,~~~~\text{and}~~~~\phi_{\beta}(+\infty)=0.

(1.13)

Once again, the solution to (
1.13
) is unique only up to a translation in
x
. We fix
the normalization by requiring that
\phi_{\beta}(0)=1/2
. It happens that the traveling wave profile for
\beta\geq 2
is explicit. Indeed, one can check by direct computation that

\phi_{\beta}(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta x/2}},~~\hbox{ for $\beta\geq 2$.}

(1.14)

On the other hand, when
\beta<2
, the profile of the minimal speed traveling wave is, to the best of our knowledge, not explicit, and the asymptotics of
\phi_{\beta}
as
x\to+\infty
are no longer purely exponential, being given by

\phi_{\beta}(x)\sim(Ax+B)e^{-x},~~\hbox{ as $x\to+\infty$, for $\beta<2$,}

(1.15)

with some
A>0
and
B\in{\mathbb{R}}
that depend on
\beta
. This was shown, for instance, in
[
42
]
by a phase plane analysis. It is discussed further in Appendix
A
.

As explained in Remark 1 of
[
24
]
,
a quantitative mathematical criterion for a traveling wave profile
U_{c}(x)
, that moves with a
speed
c\geq 0
, to be pushed is that

U_{c}(x)e^{cx/2}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}).

(1.16)

Otherwise, a traveling wave is pulled. We will see the motivation behind the criterion
(
1.16
) in the discussion of the long time behavior of the
solutions to (
1.1
) for
\beta>2
, which is contained in
Section

7
.
According to this classification, the Burgers-FKPP
traveling waves are pushed for
\beta>2
, and pulled for
\beta\leq 2
, as can be seen
from (
1.14
) and (
1.15
).

While the case
\beta=2
obviously fails the pushed front criterion (
1.16
), it has an additional property distinguishing it from the case
\beta<2
.
The traveling wave, still given by

\phi_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{x}}

(1.17)

due to (
1.14
), satisfies the weaker condition (cf. (
1.16
), recalling that
c_{*}=2
when
\beta=2
) that

\phi_{2}(x)e^{x}=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

(1.18)

As we will see, this reflects a very different long time
behavior of the solutions to (
1.1
) at
\beta=2
compared both to
\beta<2
and

\beta>2
. Borrowing the terminology of
[
36
]
, we will refer to such “dual nature”
fronts at
\beta=\beta_{c}
as “pushmi-pullyu” fronts.

The large time behavior of the solutions

We now describe the main results of this paper on the large time behavior of the solutions to
the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
) with
\beta\neq 0
and rapidly decaying initial
conditions, generalizing the results on the standard Fisher-KPP equation (
1.2
) discussed above.
The main new feature is the aforementioned transition from the pulled to pushed behavior at
\beta_{c}=2
. The analysis at
\beta=\beta_{c}
turns out to be surprisingly delicate.

The study of the large time behavior in the present paper relies, in particular, on the notion of steepness of the solution. While such arguments
date back to the original KPP paper
[
33
]
, we were to a large extent motivated by
the definition in the recent paper of Giletti and Matano
[
26
]
. As we will only need it for smooth functions, we can formulate
their notion as follows. Let us denote by
{\cal W}
the class of
C^{1}
, monotonically decreasing function
u(x)
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, such that

\lim_{x\to-\infty}u(x)=1,~~\lim_{x\to+\infty}u(x)=0.

(1.19)

Let
\overline{\mathcal{W}}
be its closure in
L^{1}
.
Given two functions
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\cal W}
, we say that
u_{1}
is steeper than
u_{2}
if

|u_{1}^{\prime}(u_{1}^{-1}(z))|>|u_{2}^{\prime}(u_{2}^{-1}(z))|,~~\hbox{for all $z\in(0,1)$.}

(1.20)

In other words, the graph of
u_{1}(x)
is steeper than the graph of
u_{2}(x)
when compared
at each fixed level
z\in(0,1)
, rather than at a fixed point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. This notion is translation
invariant; if
u_{1}
is steeper than
u_{2}
, it is also steeper than any translate
u_{2}(\cdot+h)
,
with a fixed
h\in{\mathbb{R}}
. For
u_{1},u_{2}\in\overline{\mathcal{W}}
, we say that
u_{1}
is steeper than
u_{2}
, if
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
can be approximated by
C^{1}
-functions
u_{1,\varepsilon}
and
u_{2,\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
such that
u_{1,\varepsilon}
is steeper than
u_{2,\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon
.

Equation (
1.1
) has the following important property.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
u_{1}(t,x)
and
u_{2}(t,x)
be the solutions to (
1.1
) with the corresponding initial conditions
u_{10},u_{20}\in\overline{\mathcal{W}}
.
If
u_{10}
is steeper than
u_{20}
, then

u_{1}(t,\cdot)
is steeper than
u_{2}(t,\cdot)
for all
t>0
.

This result was essentially proved for the classical Fisher-KPP equation (
1.2
) in the original KPP paper
[
33
]
.
For the convenience of the reader, we present the proof for the Burgers-FKPP case in Section
2
.
An interesting aspect here is that while the steepness comparison is used throughout the paper, most of its applications are not in the spirit of the elegant intersection number type of arguments but to produce
estimates.
A novel element of the use of these ideas in the present paper
is their quantitative application. In contrast to the qualitative arguments used in previous works, we use “steepness” to obtain estimates on
u
and integral quantities involving it.
An exception is the proof of Proposition
1
itself and its consequences discussed in Section
2
.

The main result of the present paper is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u(t,x)
be the solution to (
1.1
) with the initial condition
u_{\rm in}\in\overline{\mathcal{W}}
such that
u_{\rm in}
is steeper than the minimal speed traveling wave
\phi_{\eta}
.
Then, for each
\beta\leq 2
, there exists a constant
x_{\infty}
that depends on
\beta
and the initial
condition
u_{\rm in}
so that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}u(t,x+m_{\beta}(t))=\phi_{\beta}(x),

(1.21)

with the function
m_{\beta}(t)
given by

\displaystyle m_{\beta}(t)=2t-\frac{3}{2}\log(t+1)-x_{\infty}+o(1),~\text{as}~t\to+\infty,

(1.22)

if
\beta<2
, and, otherwise, by

\displaystyle m_{\beta=2}(t)=2t-\frac{1}{2}\log(t+1)-x_{\infty}+o(1),~\text{as}~t\to+\infty.

(1.23)

For
\beta>2
, there exists
\omega>0
, which depends on
\beta
but not on
u_{\rm in}
, and
K>0
, which depends both on
\beta
and
u_{\rm in}
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|u(t,x)-\phi_{\beta}(x-c_{*}t-x_{\infty})|<Ke^{-\omega t}.

(1.24)

We note that the class of initial data considered in
1.1
includes the Heaviside initial data:
u_{\rm in}=\mathds{1}\left(x<0\right)
. Indeed, it is easy to see that
u_{\rm in}
is the limit in
L^{1}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
of

u_{{\rm in},\varepsilon}=\begin{cases}1\qquad&\text{ if }x\leq-\varepsilon\\
\phi_{\beta}\big(\frac{x}{\varepsilon^{2}-x^{2}}\big)\qquad&\text{ if }x\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\\
0\qquad&\text{ if }x\geq\varepsilon,\end{cases}

and
u_{{\rm in},\varepsilon}
is clearly steeper than
\phi_{\beta}
for
\varepsilon<1
.

1.1
reflects the different nature of the Burgers-FKPP fronts we have discussed above for various values of
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
For
\beta<2
, the solution is pulled and the front location has the same asymptotics (
1.22
)
as (
1.7
) for the standard Fisher-KPP equation (
1.2
). For
\beta>2
, the solution
is pushed and the exponential-in-time convergence to the traveling wave (
1.24
) agrees with what we have seen
in (
1.10
) for pushed fronts. The new asymptotics (
1.23
) for the “pushmi-pullyu” solutions at

\beta=\beta_{c}
is different from both of these cases.

One may ask if the asymptotics (
1.22
) and (
1.23
) can be
refined, as was done in
[
7
,
8
,
19
,
27
,
45
]

for the standard Fisher-KPP equation (
1.2
).
It seems that this is possible using the fascinating, if formal, technique of
[
8
]
. Arguing in the manner of
[
8
]
, it turns out that the
o(1)
terms in the expansion of
m_{\beta}(t)
are exactly the same as in the Fisher-KPP case when
\beta<2
; however, they are different when
\beta=2
. In this “pushmi-pullyu” case, one finds

\displaystyle m_{2}(t)=2t-\frac{1}{2}\log(t)-x_{\infty}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{t}}+\frac{(1-\log 2)}{4}\frac{\log t}{t}+o\Big(\frac{\log t}{t}\Big).

(1.25)

The first terms in this
expansion above agree with the formal computation in
[
19
]
,
performed by a completely different method,
where it was computed up to the order

O(t^{-1/2})
. While the error in
[
19
]
is given as
O(1/t)
, and
the
O(\log t/t)
correction seems to be
missing,
it is tempting to conjecture that (
1.25
) is universal for the “pushmi-pullyu” situations,
such as, for instance,
the reaction-diffusion equation (
1.28
) below. The application
of the formal technique of
[
8
]
to derive (
1.25
)
is contained in
Section

8
.

We should mention that the convergence of the solution to the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
)
to a traveling wave was studied using the matched asymptotic expansions in
[
37
]
. Their formal
results agree with Theorem
1.1
in the cases
\beta<2
and
\beta>2
but unfortunately overlook some of the details in the case
\beta=2
,
leading to an incorrect prediction. On the other hand, as we have mentioned, the
matched asymptotics analysis in
[
19
,
38
]

predicts the shift
(1/2)\log t
for the “pushmi-pullyu” transition
situations, such as (
1.28
) below, mimicking, in a certain sense,
the Burgers-FKPP equation at
\beta=2
.

Connection to other pulled to pushed transition problems

The Burgers-FKPP equation at
\beta=2
is not the only one example of a “pushmi-pullyu”
front.
A well known instance is a particular generalized Fisher nonlinearity considered in
[
28
]
:

u_{t}=u_{xx}+u(1-u)(1+au).

(1.26)

Here, the situation is qualitatively and even quantitatively
extremely similar to the Burgers-FKPP picture: traveling waves exist for all
c\geq c_{*}(a)
, with
c_{*}(a)=2
for all
0\leq a\leq 2
, and

c_{*}(a)=\sqrt{\frac{a}{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}},~~\hbox{for $a>2$,}

(1.27)

as in (
1.12
).
Even the traveling wave profile for
a=2
is given exactly by
\phi_{\beta=2}
in (
1.17
).

As explored in
[
19
]
, (
1.26
) is a special case of the more general class of equations

u_{t}=u_{xx}+u(1-u^{n})(1+au^{n}),

(1.28)

in which the pulled to pushed transition occurs at
a=n+1
: when
a\leq n+1
, the minimal speed is
c_{*}=2
and when
a>n+1
,
c_{*}=\sqrt{\frac{a}{n+1}}+\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{a}}>2
. In the critical case
a=n+1
, the corresponding traveling wave is explicit,

\phi(x)=\big(1+e^{nx}\big)^{-1/n},

(1.29)

with the same purely exponential decay as in (
1.17
).

Numerous other examples, including the pushed-pulled transition for systems of reaction-diffusion
equations arising in chemistry and biology are discussed in Section 3.13 of
[
51
]
.
We also mention the repulsive Keller-Segel-FKPP equation that was recently used to model the spread
of a population in which individuals reproduce and diffuse, influenced by a preference
for low population density regions with strength
|\chi|
. This is seen, for instance, in slime
molds
[
39
]
and bacteria
[
54
]
. Origins of some other similar chemotaxis models
have been discussed in
[
22
,
23
]
.
The pushed-pulled transition
at the level of traveling waves has been shown in
[
31
]
:
there exists a threshold
\chi_{0}>0
such that, when
|\chi|<\chi_{0}
, the fronts are pulled,
while, as
\chi\to-\infty
, fronts are pushed. In the same vein is a system of equations considered in
[
10
]
: one equation is a Fisher-KPP equation for
T
with drift
u
and the other, which governs
u
, satisfies a Burgers type equation with a Boussinesq-type forcing depending on
T
. Actually, this specializes to (
1.1
) for a certain choice of parameters. As with the Keller-Segel-FKPP, a pushed-pulled transition occurs. In both models, the pushed-pulled analysis in
[
10
]

is performed only at the level of the traveling wave, which is a much simpler setting. The Cauchy problem, on the other hand, appears to require new ideas.

A matched asymptotic analysis in
[
19
,
38
]

predicts the results as in Theorem
1.1
to hold for the long time behavior
of the solutions of equations at the pushed-pulled transition,
such as (
1.28
). However, to the best of our knowledge, there
are no rigorous results with this precision in any of such critical cases.
The best result in this direction
seems to be the very recent paper
[
25
]
, which
shows that when
a=2
, the level sets of the solutions to (
1.26
) are located at a position

m(t)=2t-\frac{1}{2}\log t+o(\log t),~~\hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$.}

As discussed in detail in
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
,
the threshold cases that separate the pulled and pushed fronts
seem to be also outside the scope of the currently available spectral methods.

Comments on the proofs

Let us comment on the strategy of the proof of the three cases in
Theorem
1.1
, in the order of increasing difficulty and intricacy. The case
\beta>2
falls into the category of pushed fronts, and the
proof follows the classical strategy of
[
47
,
48
,
49
]
,
with appropriate modifications.

For
\beta<2
in Theorem
1.1
, we use an extension of the arguments in
[
29
,
44
]
for the Fisher-KPP equation,
approximating the dynamics by the Dirichlet problem for the
linear heat equation on a half line. The Burgers drift term causes a difficulty, since the linearization strategy
used, for instance, in
[
27
,
30
,
29
,
44
,
45
]
taking out the spatial exponential decay in the solution
seems insufficient for
0<\beta<2
. To overcome this, we pass to the moving frame
x\to x-2t
, setting

\widehat{u}(t,x)=u(t,x+2t),

(1.30)

and introduce a weighted Hopf-Cole transform

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\exp\Big(x+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{x}^{\infty}\widehat{u}(t,y)dy\Big)\widehat{u}(t,x),

(1.31)

combining the standard Hopf-Cole transform for the heat equation and the exponential weight used in the standard Fisher-KPP arguments. It turns out that
if the initial condition
u_{\rm in}
for (
1.1
) is steeper than the traveling wave
\phi_{\beta}
, then
v(t,x)
satisfies a differential inequality

v_{t}\leq v_{xx},

(1.32)

as in Proposition
3
below. We stress that the steepness comparison of the initial condition to a traveling wave plays a crucial role in
showing that
v(t,x)
satisfies the differential inequality (
1.32
).

With (
1.32
) in hand, we are able to construct upper and lower barriers in the self-similar variables for the linearized equation for
v(t,x)
on the half line,
and then the convergence in the tail implies the convergence in the bulk due to the pulled-front nature of the dynamics, as in
[
44
]
.
Interestingly, this last step also utilizes the assumption that the initial condition, and hence the solution,
is steeper than the minimal speed traveling wave, in an explicit quantitative way. Qualitatively, the case
\beta<2
is similar to the standard Fisher-KPP equation,
and the weighted Hopf-Cole transform gives a tool to see that. However, the repeated use of the steepness comparison is something new in this argument
for Burgers-FKPP equation.

The weighted Hopf-Cole transform also indicates the technical reason for the transition at
\beta=2
: while it is easy to see from (
1.31
)
that
v(t,x)\to 0
as
x\to-\infty
for
\beta<2
(recall that
\widehat{u}\leq 1
), in the case

\beta=2
the function
v(t,x)
approaches a positive constant as
x\to-\infty
. This modifies the boundary condition for the linearized problem
for the upper and lower barriers in the
self-similar variables, and ultimately leads to the change in the logarithmic shift
from
(3/2)\log t
to
(1/2)\log t
at
\beta=2
.

Let us now discuss the ingredients of the the proof of Theorem
1.1
in the critical case
\beta=2
, which is remarkably different from
the approach for the standard Fisher-KPP equation. This analysis is probably the most novel part of the present paper.
The first key observation is that when
\beta=2
, the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
) has a special structure: the function

p(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x),

(1.33)

satisfies a spatially inhomogeneous conservation law:

\displaystyle p_{t}+(e^{-x}p^{2})_{x}=p_{xx}.

(1.34)

Here,
\widehat{u}(t,x)
is defined in (
1.30
). An immediate consequence of (
1.34
) is a conservation law for the exponential moment of
\widehat{u}(t,x)
:

\int e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x)dx=\int e^{x}u_{\rm in}(x)dx,~~\hbox{ for all $t>0$.}

(1.35)

This conservation law eventually leads to a lower bound for
m_{2}(t)
of the form

m_{2}(t)\geq 2t-\frac{1}{2}\log t+O(1),~~\hbox{as $t\to+\infty$,}

(1.36)

see the proof of Lemma
5.1.2
in Section
5.3
below.

A matching upper bound for
m_{2}(t)
is related to the behavior of
p(t,x)
.
Note that, together with the explicit expression (
1.17
) for the profile
\phi_{2}(x)
, the convergence to a traveling wave in shape in (
1.21
) yields, roughly,

p(t,0)\approx\exp\big(-(2t-m_{2}(t))\big).

(1.37)

Thus, an upper bound of the form

m_{2}(t)\leq 2t-\frac{1}{2}\log t+O(1),~~\hbox{as $t\to+\infty$,}

(1.38)

would follow from an
L^{\infty}
-bound on
p(t,x)
of the form

p(t,x)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}.

(1.39)

Such decay, while natural to expect in view of (
1.34
), is not automatic for solutions of mass-conserving
advection-diffusion equations, even if the advection is bounded: see the end of Section
5.1
for simple
examples of such equations with solutions
that do not decay in time.

The proof of (
1.39
), presented in Section
5
, turns out to be rather
intricate. While (
1.34
) looks like a degenerate viscous conservation law,
we were unable to adapt the methods of
[
16
]
or
[
32
]
to (
1.34
) and instead
take a different approach. The first step is a relative entropy computation inspired by
[
17
,
41
]
where it was used for linear advection-diffusion
equations. An unusual twist is that
we compute the relative entropy not with respect to another solution but to a super-solution to (
1.34
). This leads to a weighted dissipation inequality for the function

\varphi(t,x)=\frac{p(t,x)}{\rho(t,x)},\quad\text{ where }\rho(t,x)={1-u(t,x+2t)},

(1.40)

of the form

\frac{d}{dt}\int\ \varphi^{2}(t,x)\rho(t,x)dx\leq-2\int\varphi_{x}^{2}(t,x)\rho(t,x)dx.

(1.41)

The dissipation identity (
1.41
) is similar to that for the standard heat equation, where it takes the form

\frac{d}{dt}\int\ \varphi^{2}(t,x)dx\leq-2\int\varphi_{x}^{2}(t,x)dx,

(1.42)

that is, as in (
1.41
) but without the weight
\rho(t,x)
.
In the latter case, (
1.42
) combined with the Nash inequality and a standard duality argument directly
leads to the temporal decay rate
t^{-1/2}
in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Here, the time-dependent weight
\rho(t,x)
that appears
in (
1.41
) is degenerate as
x\to-\infty
, so the standard Nash inequality can not be used.
Instead, we obtain a Nash-type inequality for weighted spaces for a certain class of degenerate weights: see Proposition
5.4
below.
The weights need to satisfy certain quantitative assumptions,
and we need to verify that the dynamics do not take the weight
\rho(t,x)
, defined in (
1.40
),
out of the class of the admissible weights or make the constants in the
weighted Nash inequality in
Section

5.4
degenerate as
t\to+\infty
. Applying the weighted Nash inequality in (
1.41
), leads to the appropriate decay of
\varphi(t,x)
in a weighted
L^{2}
-space.
However, the nature of the Nash inequality leads to an extra delay
in time, before the decay sets in, that depends on the initial
condition. This, among other issues, prevents us from using the duality argument to establish the weighted
L^{\infty}
-decay of
\varphi(t,x)

from the
L^{2}
-estimate.
Instead, it comes from additional ad hoc arguments, first establishing the bound at a large time
t^{\prime}
less than, but comparable to,
t
and then extending it to the final time

t
. The decay of
p(t,x)
follows from that of
\varphi(t,x)
and bounds on the weights.

An extra technical complication is that the
function
\rho(t,x)
appears in the denominator in the definition (
1.40
) of the function
\varphi(t,x)
but vanishes as
x\to-\infty
.
As a result, the
L^{2}
-norm of
\varphi(t,x)
is actually infinite for a large class of interesting initial conditions and extra approximations have to be used
to deal with this issue.
At this stage, the assumption that
u_{\rm in}(x)=1

for
x\leq L_{1}
is actually not a simplification but a complication that can not be avoided if one wants to include the Heaviside function into the class
of admissible initial conditions. The need to control the behavior in the back of the front is another reflection of the “pushmi-pullyu” nature of the
solution.

Convergence to a traveling wave in shape

In this section, as a preliminary step to the proof of Theorem
1.1
,
we use a strategy inspired by
the original KPP paper
[
33
]
to show convergence in shape
of a solution to the Burgers-FKPP equation to a traveling wave.
As the first step, we prove Proposition
1
.

The proof of Proposition
1

It is enough to assume that
u_{10},u_{20}\in\mathcal{W}
by standard density arguments.
Let
u_{1}(t,x)
and
u_{2}(t,x)
be the solutions to (
1.1
) with the respective
initial conditions
u_{10},u_{20}
such that
u_{10}
is steeper than
u_{20}
.
First, we note that since the initial conditions are decreasing, both
u_{1}(t,x)
and

u_{2}(t,x)
are decreasing and have the left and right limits as in (
1.19
),
so that both
u_{1}(t,\cdot)
and
u_{2}(t,\cdot)
lie in
\cal W
.

Recall the definition of “steeper” (
1.20
). To show that
u_{1}(t,\cdot)
is steeper than
u_{2}(t,\cdot)
for any
t>0
, consider the functions

w(t,x;k_{0})=u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(t,x+k_{0}),~~q(t,x)=u_{1}(t,x)+u_{2}(t,x+k_{0}),

for a fixed
k_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
The function
w(t,x;k_{0})
satisfies

w_{t}+\frac{\beta}{2}(qw)_{x}=w_{xx}+w-qw,

(2.1)

with the initial condition

w(0,x;k_{0})=u_{10}(x)-u_{20}(x+k_{0}).

(2.2)

Since
u_{10}
is steeper than
u_{20}
, it is also steeper than
u_{20}(\cdot+k_{0})
.
Therefore, there exists
x_{0}
so that

\hbox{$w(0,x;k_{0})>0$ for all $x<x_{0}$},

and

w(0,x;k_{0})<0
for all
x>x_{0}
.

As
w(t,x;k_{0})
is a solution to the parabolic equation (
2.1
), a consequence of
[
1
, Theorems A and B]
is
that the function
w(t,x;k_{0})
has exactly one zero
y(t;k_{0})
for all
t>0
. Indeed,
[
1
]
shows that the number of zeros is nonincreasing and that a zero may only disappear at a time
t_{0}>0
when two zeros “collide.” Hence,

w(t,x;k_{0})>0
for all
x<y(t;k_{0})
and
w(t,x;k_{0})<0
for all
x>y(t;k_{0})
, with
y(0;k_{0})=x_{0}
. In addition, we have

\partial_{x}u_{1}(t,y(t;k_{0}))<\partial_{x}u_{2}(t,y(t;k_{0})).

(2.3)

Since this is true for all
k_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, it follows that
u_{1}(t,\cdot)
is steeper than

u_{2}(t,\cdot)
.
\Box

A standard approximation argument shows the following.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
v(t,x)
and
u(t,x)
be the solutions to (
1.1
) with the
respective initial conditions
v_{\rm in}\in{\cal W}
and
u_{\rm in}(x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
.
Assume that
v_{\rm in}(x)
is steeper than the minimal speed traveling wave
\phi_{\beta}(x)
.
Then, for any
t>0
the solution
v(t,\cdot)
is steeper than
\phi_{\beta}
, and
is less steep than
u(t,x)
.

Convergence in shape

We now establish convergence of the solution in shape to a traveling wave.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
u(t,x)
be the solution to (
1.1
)
with the initial condition
u_{\rm in}\in{\cal W}
that is steeper
than the minimal speed traveling wave
\phi_{\beta}(x)
, or with
u_{\rm in}(x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
.
Then, there exists a function
m_{\beta}(t)
such that
\dot{m}_{\beta}(t)\to c_{*}(\beta)

as
t\to+\infty
and

u(t,x+m_{\beta}(t))\to\phi_{\beta}(x)~~\hbox{as $t\to+\infty$, uniformly on ${\mathbb{R}}$.}

(2.4)

Here,
\phi_{\beta}(x)
is a solution to (
1.13
) with the minimal speed
c_{*}=c_{*}(\beta)
.

Corollary
2
shows that it suffices to consider the solution
u(t,x)

to (
1.1
) with the initial condition

u(0,x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
. Note that for any
\tau>0
, the function
u^{(\tau)}(t,x)=u(t+\tau,x)

is the solution to (
1.1
) with the initial condition
u^{(\tau)}(0,x)=u(\tau,x)

that is less steep than
u(0,x)
. It follows that for any
t>0
and
\tau>0
the function

u(t,\cdot)
is steeper than
u(t+\tau,\cdot)
. In addition,
u(t,\cdot)
is steeper
than the minimal speed traveling wave
\phi_{\beta}(x)
for all
t>0
.
Hence, if for each
v\in(0,1)
and
t>0
,
we let
x(t,v)
be the unique point such that
u(t,x(t,v))=v
,
then, the function

E(t,v)=u_{x}(t,x(t,v))<0,

(2.5)

is increasing in
t
for all
v\in(0,1)
,
and

E(t,v)\leq\bar{E}(v):=\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}(\phi_{\beta}^{-1}(v)).

(2.6)

Let now
m_{\beta}(t)
be the position such that
u(t,m_{\beta}(t))=1/2
for all
t>0
, and consider the translate

\tilde{u}(t,x)=u(t,x+m_{\beta}(t)),

as well as the corresponding inverse
\xi(t,v)
defined by

\tilde{u}(t,\xi(t,v))=v
, for
0<v<1
. Observe that
\xi(t,1/2)=0
for all
t>0
.
We see from (
2.5
)-(
2.6
)
that the function
E(t,v)
is negative and increasing in time. Thus, it has a limit

E(t,v)\to E_{\infty}(v)\leq\bar{E}(v)=\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}(\phi_{\beta}^{-1}(v))<0,~~\hbox{as $t\to+\infty$.}

(2.7)

Hence

\frac{\partial{\xi(t,v)}}{\partial{v}}=\frac{1}{E(t,v)}\to\frac{1}{E_{\infty}(v)},~~\hbox{as $t\to+\infty$,}

and

\xi(t,v)=\int_{1/2}^{v}\frac{\partial{\xi(t,v^{\prime})}}{\partial{v^{\prime}}}dv^{\prime}\to\int_{1/2}^{v}\frac{dv^{\prime}}{E_{\infty}(v^{\prime})}:=\xi_{\infty}(v).

(2.8)

As a consequence, the function
\tilde{u}(t,x)
also converges uniformly on compact sets
to a limit
\tilde{u}_{\infty}(x)
:

\tilde{u}(t,x)\to\tilde{u}_{\infty}(x)\hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$,}

(2.9)

with
\tilde{u}_{\infty}(x)
determined by

\xi_{\infty}(\tilde{u}_{\infty}(x))=x.

(2.10)

Moreover, due to (
2.7
), we have

|\xi_{\infty}(v)|=\int_{1/2}^{v}\frac{dv^{\prime}}{|E_{\infty}(v^{\prime})|}\leq\int_{1/2}^{v}\frac{dv^{\prime}}{|\bar{E}(v^{\prime})|}:=\bar{\xi}(v).

(2.11)

This yields the correct behavior of the limits
x\to\pm\infty
:

\tilde{u}_{\infty}(-\infty)=1,~~\tilde{u}_{\infty}(+\infty)=0.

(2.12)

Indeed, considering, for example the behavior
x\to+\infty
, we have

+\infty=\lim_{x\to+\infty}x=\lim_{x\to+\infty}\xi_{\infty}(\tilde{u}_{\infty}(x))\leq\bar{\xi}(\tilde{u}_{\infty}(x)).

and
\bar{\xi}^{-1}(\infty)=0
. The argument for
x\to-\infty
is similar.

Furthermore, as
u(t,x)
is strictly decreasing in
x
and
u_{x}(t,m_{\beta}(t))<0
, the function
m_{\beta}(t)
is differentiable in
t
. Hence,
\tilde{u}(t,x)
satisfies

\tilde{u}_{t}-\dot{m}_{\beta}(t)\tilde{u}_{x}+\beta\tilde{u}\tilde{u}_{x}=\tilde{u}_{xx}+\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u}).

(2.13)

Notice that

\dot{m}_{\beta}(t)=-\frac{u_{t}(t,m_{\beta}(t))}{u_{x}(t,m_{\beta}(t))}=-\frac{u_{t}(t,m_{\beta}(t))}{\tilde{u}_{x}(t,0)}.

By parabolic regularity theory, the numerator is bounded and,
by (
2.6
) with
v=1/2
, the denominator is bounded away from zero. It follows that
\dot{m}_{\beta}
is bounded uniformly in
t
. Hence, for any sequence
t_{n}\to\infty
, there is a subsequence
t_{n_{k}}\to\infty
and a real number
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\dot{m}_{\beta}(t_{n_{k}})\to c
.
Using then the convergence (
2.9
), we deduce
that

-c\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{\infty}+\beta\tilde{u}_{\infty}\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{\infty}=\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{u}_{\infty}+\tilde{u}_{\infty}(1-\tilde{u}_{\infty}),

(2.14)

where we have switched to
\partial
notation to avoid the awkward double subscript.

From (
2.14
), we see that
\tilde{u}_{\infty}(x)
is a traveling wave solution to (
1.1
) moving
with the speed
c
. It remains to show that
c=c_{*}(\beta)
. The key point is that the
steepness comparison argument above
applies to any traveling wave solution to

-c\phi_{x}+\beta\phi\phi_{x}=\phi_{xx}+\phi-\phi^{2}.

(2.15)

In other words, if we set

E_{\phi}(v)=\phi^{\prime}(\phi^{-1}(v)),~~\hbox{ for $0<v<1$},

then we know that

E_{\infty}(v)\leq E_{\phi}(v),

for any
\phi
that satisfies (
2.15
) with some
c\geq c_{*}(\beta)
. Therefore,
the limit
\tilde{u}_{\infty}(x)
is the
traveling wave that is the steepest among all traveling wave solutions. Lemma
A
implies
that
\tilde{u}_{\infty}(x)=\phi_{\beta}(x)
is the minimal speed traveling wave and, thus,
c=c_{*}(\beta)
. By the arbitrariness of the sequence
t_{n}
, it follows that
\dot{m}_{\beta}(t)\to c_{*}(\beta)
as
t\to\infty
. This finishes the proof of Proposition
2
.
\Box

3
The weighted Hopf-Cole transform

In this section, we discuss a weighted Hopf-Cole transform that will play a key role in
the analysis of the Burgers-FKPP equation for
\beta\leq 2
.
Let us recall that the standard Burgers equation

u_{t}+\beta uu_{x}=u_{xx}

(3.1)

can be linearized by means of the Hopf-Cole transform. Namely, if
u
is
a solution to (
3.1
) then the function

v(t,x)=\exp\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{x}^{+\infty}u(t,y)dy\Big)

(3.2)

satisfies the heat equation

v_{t}=v_{xx}.

(3.3)

The second simple observation is that if
\widehat{u}(t,x)
is the solution to the standard
Fisher-KPP equation in a frame moving with the speed
c_{*}=2
:

\widehat{u}_{t}-2\widehat{u}_{x}=\widehat{u}_{xx}+\widehat{u}-\widehat{u}^{2},

(3.4)

then the function

v(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x)

(3.5)

satisfies

v_{t}=v_{xx}-e^{-x}v^{2}.

(3.6)

The nonlinear term in (
3.6
) is negligible for
x
very large and positive but plays the role of a large absorption
for
x
very negative. Therefore, the solution to (
3.6
) should be well approximated by the solution of the heat equation
on a half-line
x>0
with the Dirichlet boundary condition:

\displaystyle v_{t}=v_{xx},~~x>0,

(3.7)

\displaystyle v(t,0)=0.

This simple idea is what is driving the convergence to a traveling wave in
[
27
,
29
,
44
,
45
]
.

The weighted Hopf-Cole transform that we discuss below allows us to adapt this intuition to the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
)
with
\beta\leq 2
, and also shows why the transition from pulled to pushed fronts happens at
\beta=2
.

We will consider the solution to (
1.1
) in the reference frame

\tilde{u}(t,x)=u(t,x+m_{\beta}(t)),

(3.8)

centered at

m_{\beta}(t)=2t-\frac{r(\beta)}{2}\log(t+1).

(3.9)

Here, we take

r(\beta)=\left\{\begin{matrix}3,~~\hbox{ if $\beta<2$},\cr 1,~~\hbox{ if $\beta=2$},\cr\end{matrix}\right.

(3.10)

in accordance with the different behavior in Theorem
1.1
in these two cases.
In the above reference frame, (
1.1
)
takes the form

\tilde{u}_{t}-\Big(2-\frac{r(\beta)}{2(t+1)}\Big)\tilde{u}_{x}+\beta\tilde{u}\tilde{u}_{x}=\tilde{u}_{xx}+\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}^{2}.

(3.11)

Motivated by (
3.2
) and (
3.5
), we introduce the weighted Hopf-Cole transform

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\exp(\Gamma(t,x))\tilde{u}(t,x),~~\Gamma(t,x)=x+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{x}^{+\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)dy,

(3.12)

that is a combination of (
3.2
) and (
3.5
).

Let us point out one way to see why
\beta=2
is the critical value by using
v
. Since
\tilde{u}
is steeper than
\phi
and
\phi
converges exponentially to
1
as
x\to-\infty
, we find

-C_{t}-\frac{\beta}{2}x\leq\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)dy\leq C_{t}-\frac{\beta}{2}x.

The constant
C_{t}
may depend on
t
, but not on
x
. From this, we see that, as
x\to-\infty
,

v(t,x)=e^{O(1)+x\left(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\right)}\to\begin{cases}0\qquad&\text{ if }\beta<2,\\
e^{O(1)}\qquad&\text{ if }\beta=2,\\
\infty\qquad&\text{ if }\beta>2.\end{cases}

These differences reflect the three different behaviors in
1.1
: when
\beta<2
, the nonlinear term (integral of
\tilde{u}
) does not dominate, and when
\beta>2
, the nonlinear term dominates.

The main result of this section is
the following analogue of (
3.6
) in the standard Fisher-KPP case. It will allow us to adapt an
approximation similar to the linear Dirichlet boundary problem (
3.7
) for
\beta<2
in Section
4

and with a different boundary condition for
\beta=2
in Section
6
. This will be extremely
important for the proof of Theorem
1.1
for
\beta\leq 2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
u(t,x)
be the solution to (
1.1
) with
\beta\leq 2
and the initial
condition
u(0,x)
as in Theorem
1.1
. Then, the function
v(t,x)
defined
in (
3.12
) satisfies the differential inequality

v_{t}-v_{xx}+\frac{r(\beta)}{2(t+1)}(v_{x}-v)\leq 0.

(3.13)

As we will see in the proof, it is here, among other places, that the steepness assumption on the initial condition
u(0,x)

plays a crucial role, together with propagation of steepness in Proposition

.

Proof of Proposition
3

We claim that the function
v(t,x)

satisfies an equation of the form

v_{t}-v_{xx}+\frac{r(\beta)}{2(t+1)}(v_{x}-v)=-G(t,x;\tilde{u})v,

(3.14)

where

G(t,x;\tilde{u})=\tilde{u}(t,x)-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{x}^{+\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)(1-\tilde{u}(t,y))dy.

(3.15)

Let us verify that (
3.14
) holds. We compute

v_{t}=e^{\Gamma}\tilde{u}_{t}+\frac{\beta v}{2}\int_{x}^{+\infty}\tilde{u}_{t}(t,y)dy\quad\text{ and }\quad v_{x}=e^{\Gamma}\tilde{u}_{x}+\Big(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}\Big)v,

(3.16)

so that

\displaystyle v_{xx}

\displaystyle=e^{\Gamma}\tilde{u}_{xx}+\Big(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}\Big)e^{\Gamma}\tilde{u}_{x}-\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}_{x}v+\Big(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}\Big)v_{x}

(3.17)

\displaystyle=e^{\Gamma}\tilde{u}_{xx}+2\Big(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}\Big)v_{x}-\Big(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}\Big)^{2}v-\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}_{x}v.

Using these identities in (
3.11
) gives

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta v}{2}\int_{x}^{+\infty}\tilde{u}_{t}(t,y)dy-\Big(2-\frac{r(\beta)}{2(t+1)}\Big)\Big(v_{x}-\Big(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}\Big)v\Big)+\beta v\tilde{u}_{x}

(3.18)

\displaystyle=v_{xx}-2\Big(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}\Big)v_{x}+\Big(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}\Big)^{2}v+\frac{\beta}{2}v\tilde{u}_{x}+v-v\tilde{u}.

To simplify this equation, we integrate (
3.11
) from
x
to
\infty
to get

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{+\infty}\tilde{u}_{t}(t,y)dy=-\Big(2-\frac{r(\beta)}{2(t+1)}\Big)\tilde{u}+\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}^{2}-\tilde{u}_{x}+\int_{x}^{+\infty}\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u})dy.

(3.19)

Substituting this back into (
3.18
) gives, after some algebra

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle-v_{xx}+\frac{r(\beta)}{2(t+1)}\big(v_{x}-v\big)+\beta v\tilde{u}_{x}

(3.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta v}{2}\int_{x}^{+\infty}\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u})dy+{\beta}\tilde{u}v_{x}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\tilde{u}^{2}v-\beta\tilde{u}v-v\tilde{u}.

Using the second identity in (
3.16
) in the right side gives

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle-v_{xx}+\frac{r(\beta)}{2(t+1)}\big(v_{x}-v\big)=\frac{\beta v}{2}\int_{x}^{+\infty}\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u})dy-v\tilde{u},

(3.21)

which is exactly (
3.14
)-(
3.15
).

Here is the key observation.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\beta\leq 2
and the initial condition
u(0,x)
is as in Theorem
1.1
,
then
G(t,x;\tilde{u})\geq 0
for all
t>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.
The claim is trivially true for

\beta\leq 0
, so we only consider the case
0<\beta\leq 2
. Let us first show that a traveling wave
\phi_{\beta}(x)
satisfies
the inequality

\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{x}^{+\infty}\phi_{\beta}(y)(1-\phi_{\beta}(y))dy\leq\phi_{\beta}(x),~~\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$.}

(3.22)

We will prove (
3.22
) for
\beta<2
, and deduce the conclusion for
\beta=2
by continuity.
Recall that for
\beta<2
the traveling wave has the asymptotics (
A.24
):

\phi_{\beta}(x)\sim(Ax+B)e^{-x},~~\hbox{ as $x\to+\infty$,}

with some constants
A
and
B
. As
\beta<2
, it follows that there exists
L>0
so that (
3.22
) holds for all
x\geq L
,
and we only need to prove this inequality for
x<L
.

Next, we integrate the traveling wave equation, with
c_{*}(\beta)=2
:

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\phi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}-2\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}+\beta\phi_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}=\phi_{\beta}-\phi_{\beta}^{2},

(3.23)

\displaystyle\phi_{\beta}(-\infty)=1,~~\phi_{\beta}(+\infty)=0,

from
x
to
+\infty
to get

\displaystyle\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}(x)+2\phi_{\beta}(x)-\frac{\beta}{2}\phi_{\beta}^{2}(x)=\int_{x}^{+\infty}\phi_{\beta}(y)(1-\phi_{\beta}(y))dy.

(3.24)

Passing to the limit
x\to-\infty
in (
3.24
),
and keeping in mind that
\phi_{\beta}(-\infty)=1
, gives

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\phi_{\beta}(y)(1-\phi_{\beta}(y))dy-\phi_{\beta}(-\infty)=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}+\beta-1=-(\frac{\beta}{2}-1)^{2}<0.

Therefore, there exists
L_{1}<0
so that (
3.22
) holds for all
x<L_{1}
. In order to show that this inequality also holds for

L_{1}<x<L
, consider the function

G(x,\phi_{\beta})=\phi_{\beta}(x)-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{x}^{+\infty}\phi_{\beta}(y)(1-\phi_{\beta}(y))dy.

(3.25)

Note that

G^{\prime}(x,\phi_{\beta})=\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}(x)+\frac{\beta}{2}\phi_{\beta}(1-\phi_{\beta}).

Using (
3.23
), we obtain

G^{\prime\prime}(x,\phi_{\beta})=\phi_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{\beta}{2}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}-\beta\phi_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}=\phi_{\beta}^{2}-\phi_{\beta}-2\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}+\frac{\beta}{2}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}.

Therefore, if
x_{0}
is a critical point of

, then

\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}(x_{0})=-\frac{\beta}{2}\phi_{\beta}(x_{0})(1-\phi_{\beta}(x_{0})),

and

G^{\prime\prime}(x_{0},\phi_{\beta})=\frac{2}{\beta}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}-2\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}+\frac{\beta}{2}\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}=\Big(\frac{2}{\beta}+\frac{\beta}{2}-2\Big)\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}<0.

Hence, the only possible critical points of
G(x,\phi_{\beta})
are local maxima.
As
G(x)>0
for
x>L
and
x<L_{1}
we deduce that
G(x,\phi_{\beta})>0
for all

x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and (

) holds.

To finish the proof of Lemma

, consider the integral

I(t,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)(1-\tilde{u}(t,y))dy.

(3.26)

To write this integral differently, define
\tilde{x}
by
\tilde{u}(t,\tilde{x}(t,v))=v
and note that

\tilde{u}_{x}(t,\tilde{x}(t,v))=E(t,v),~~\hbox{ for $0<v<1$},

(3.27)

with
E(t,v)
as in (
2.5
).
Then, making the change of variables
y\mapsto v
via
y=\tilde{x}(t,v)
, we find

\displaystyle I(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)(1-\tilde{u}(t,y))dy=\int_{0}^{\tilde{u}(t,x)}v(1-v)\frac{dv}{|E(t,v)|}\leq\int_{0}^{\tilde{u}(t,x)}v(1-v)\frac{dv}{|\bar{E}(v)|}

(3.28)

\displaystyle=\int_{\phi_{\beta}^{-1}(\tilde{u}(t,x))}^{\infty}\phi_{\beta}(y)(1-\phi_{\beta}(y))dy,

with
\bar{E}(v)
defined in (
2.6
).
We used (
2.6
) in the inequality in the first line in (
3.28
),
employing the assumption that the initial condition is steeper than the minimal
speed traveling wave.
Using (
3.22
) in (
3.28
) we see that

\displaystyle I(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\phi_{\beta}^{-1}(\tilde{u}(t,x))}^{\infty}\phi_{\beta}(y)(1-\phi_{\beta}(y))dy\leq\frac{2}{\beta}\phi_{\beta}(\phi_{\beta}^{-1}(\tilde{u}(t,x)))=\frac{2}{\beta}\tilde{u}(t,x).

(3.29)

We conclude that

G(t,x;\tilde{u})=\tilde{u}(t,x)-\frac{\beta}{2}I(t,x)\geq 0,

finishing the proof of Lemma
3
, and hence that of Proposition
3
as well.

4
The proof of Theorem
1.1
for
\beta<2

Outline of the proof

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
for
\beta<2
. The overall
strategy of the proof is similar to
[
44
]
which considers the classical
Fisher-KPP equation with
\beta=0
but there are also non-trivial differences worth
mentioning.

The first step is to get control of the solution on the spatial scales
x\sim O(\sqrt{t})
.
This is done using the self-similar variables.
The estimates are precise enough to include the tail behavior
of
\tilde{u}(t,x)
on the intermediate
scales
x\sim O(t^{\gamma})
, with
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
that are
between the traveling wave scale
x\sim O(1)
and the diffusive scale
x\sim O(\sqrt{t})
.
Unlike in
[
44
]
, in this step we rely crucially on the weighted Hopf-Cole transform and
Proposition
3
to construct upper and lower barriers for the
solution. The main estimate is the following:

Lemma 4.1
.

For
\beta<2
, let
\tilde{u}(t,x)
be the solution to (
3.11
)
with the initial condition as in Theorem
1.1
.
There exist
\alpha_{\infty}>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
so that, for any
0<\gamma<1/2

and
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})

there exists

such that

\displaystyle|\tilde{u}(t,x_{\gamma})-\alpha_{\infty}x_{\gamma}e^{-x_{\gamma}}|\leq{\varepsilon}x_{\gamma}e^{-x_{\gamma}}\qquad\text{ for all }t>T_{\varepsilon,\gamma},

(4.1)

where
x_{\gamma}=(t+1)^{\gamma}
.

The second step is to use the pulled nature of the problem to show that the control of
\tilde{u}
given in
Section

4
at
x=x_{\gamma}(t)

induces convergence to a traveling wave
on the scales
x\sim O(1)
. Before discussing the modifications required for
\beta\neq 0
, we recall the argument in
[
44
]
. It proceeds by constructing
solutions
\tilde{u}_{\alpha}
to the same equation
as satisfied by
\tilde{u}
(which, in
[
44
]
is (
3.11
) with
\beta=0
), considered
on the half-line
(-\infty,(t+1)^{\gamma})
, with the boundary condition

\tilde{u}_{\alpha}(t,(t+1)^{\gamma})=\alpha x_{\gamma}e^{-x_{\gamma}},

and with
\alpha=\alpha_{\infty}\pm\varepsilon
(cf.
[
44
, Section 4]
). Then, the analogue of (
4.1
) for
\beta=0
, which is Lemma 5.1 in
[
44
]
,
and the comparison principle imply that

\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{\infty}-\varepsilon}\leq\tilde{u}\leq\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{\infty}+\varepsilon}.

One concludes after proving the convergence of
\tilde{u}_{\alpha}(t,x)
, as
t\to+\infty
, to a suitable shift
\varphi_{\alpha}(x)
of the traveling wave, showing that
the shifts for the waves
\varphi_{\alpha_{\infty}\pm\varepsilon}

are
O(\varepsilon)
apart, and, finally, letting
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Unfortunately, a direct attempt to do this in our setting fails for several reasons.
The convergence of
\tilde{u}_{\alpha}
to
\varphi_{\alpha}
in
[
44
]
is achieved by the construction of an explicit super-solution for the equation
for the difference
e^{x}(\tilde{u}_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\alpha})
. However, the Burgers term in our context leads to a growth term in this equation when
\beta>0

(see, in contrast, (
4.63
) when
\beta\leq 0
). We bypass this issue by working with the weighted Hopf-Cole transforms of
\tilde{u}
and the traveling wave.
One might be tempted to define
\tilde{u}_{\alpha}
as above and then take its weighted Hopf-Cole transform. However, there is no apparent reason for

\tilde{u}_{\alpha}
to be steeper
than the traveling wave, meaning that
Section

3
does not apply, and we cannot deduce
the key differential inequality (
3.13
). To bypass this difficulty, we work at the level of the weighted Hopf-Cole transform, defining
v_{\alpha}
solving (
3.14
)
treated as a linear equation with the
\tilde{u}
terms serving as given coefficients (see (
4.29
)–(
4.31
) below), with a suitable boundary condition at
x=(t+1)^{\gamma}
.
Proceeding as in
[
44
]
, we obtain an upper bound on
v_{\alpha}
given by a shift of the weighted Hopf-Cole transform of the traveling wave and a decaying term.
The lower bound is obtained using yet another steepness comparison. Afterwards, an additional argument is needed to upgrade this to the convergence of
\tilde{u}
due to the fact
that
\tilde{u}
and
v
are connected in a nonlocal fashion.

Below, we first prove Lemma
4
, then apply it to show closeness of the weighted Hopf-Cole transforms of
\tilde{u}

and the traveling wave
\phi_{\beta}
in
Section

4
, and, finally, deduce the closeness of
\tilde{u}
and
\phi_{\beta}
from this.
For the sake of concreteness, we take
\tilde{u}(0,x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
. The argument for general initial conditions as in Theorem
1.1
is nearly verbatim.

Analysis in the self-similar variables

We start with equations (
3.14
)-(
3.15
)
and pass to self-similar variables: let

\tau=\log(t+1),~~\eta=\frac{x}{\sqrt{t+1}}~~\text{and}~~\omega(\tau,\eta)=v\big(e^{\tau}-1,\eta e^{\tau/2}\big)e^{-\tau/2}.

(4.2)

In these variables, (
3.14
)-(
3.15
) becomes

\displaystyle\omega_{\tau}+\mathcal{L}\omega+\frac{3}{2}e^{-\tau/2}\omega_{\eta}=e^{\tau}\omega\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\eta e^{\tau/2}}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u})dy-\tilde{u}(e^{\tau}-1,\eta e^{\tau/2})\Big),~~\tau>0,~\eta\in{\mathbb{R}},

(4.3)

with the operator
\mathcal{L}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\omega:=-\omega_{\eta\eta}-\frac{\eta}{2}\omega_{\eta}-\omega.

(4.4)

Note that when
\beta=0

\displaystyle\omega_{\tau}+\mathcal{L}\omega+\frac{3}{2}e^{-\tau/2}\omega_{\eta}=-e^{3\tau/2-\eta\exp(\tau/2)}\omega^{2},~~\tau>0,~\eta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

In that case, the nonlinear term in the right side is very small for
\eta>0
but plays the role
of a large absorption for
\eta<0
. This was used in
[
44
]
to show that (

)
is well-approximated by the linear problem

\bar{\omega}_{\tau}+\mathcal{L}\bar{\omega}+\frac{3}{2}e^{-\tau/2}\bar{\omega}_{\eta}=0,~~\eta>0,

augmented with the Dirichlet boundary condition
\bar{\omega}(\tau,0)=0
. This is the main intuition behind the proof of the long-time asymptotics of
\omega
in
[
44
]
.

We now collect the ingredients that would allow us to use the arguments of
[
44
]
.
First, Lemma
3
implies that, as long as the initial condition
u(0,x)
is steeper than
the minimal speed traveling wave, the right hand side of (
4.3
) is non-positive.
Hence, the solution to (
4.6
) is still a super-solution to (
4.3
).

Second, the coefficient in the parenthesis in the right side of (
4.3
) can be bounded below as follows: by
Section

3
, we have that

\int_{\eta e^{\tau/2}}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u})dy\leq\frac{2}{|\beta|}\tilde{u},

leading to

e^{\tau}\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\eta e^{\tau/2}}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u})dy-\tilde{u}\Big)\geq-2e^{\tau}\tilde{u}.

(4.7)

It follows that the solution to

\underline{\omega}_{\tau}+\mathcal{L}\underline{\omega}+\frac{3}{2}e^{-\tau/2}\underline{\omega}_{\eta}=-2e^{\tau}\tilde{u}\underline{\omega}

(4.8)

is a sub-solution to (
4.3
).

The third observation is that
the right side of (
4.3
) is very small for
\eta\gg e^{-\tau/2}
. To see this, we first show that, if
\beta<2
, then there exist
A>1
and
L>0
so that

u(t,x)\leq\bar{u}(t,x):=\frac{A}{1+e^{x-2t-L}},~~\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$}.

(4.9)

Indeed, the function
\bar{u}(t,x)
satisfies (we set

momentarily to simplify the notation)

\bar{u}_{t}=\frac{2Ae^{x-2t}}{(1+e^{x-2t})^{2}},~~~\bar{u}_{x}=-\frac{Ae^{x-2t}}{(1+e^{x-2t})^{2}},~~~\bar{u}_{xx}=-\frac{Ae^{x-2t}(1-e^{x-2t})}{(1+e^{x-2t})^{3}}.

Hence, as long as
\beta<2
, we may choose
A\in(1,2/\beta)
so that

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{t}+\beta\bar{u}\bar{u}_{x}-\bar{u}_{xx}-\bar{u}(1-\bar{u})=A\frac{(2-\beta A)e^{x-2t}}{(1+e^{x-2t})^{3}}\geq 0,

With this choice of

.

\begin{split}\omega(\tau,-e^{-(1/2-\gamma)\tau})&=e^{-\tau/2}\tilde{u}(e^{\tau}-1,-e^{\gamma\tau})\exp\left\{-e^{\gamma\tau}+\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{-e^{\gamma\tau}}^{\infty}\tilde{u}dy\right\}\\

Hence, the dominant behavior for

require exactly the four ingredients listed above: equations for the super- and sub-solutions (given in

)
(see (

) and (

\omega
using the spectral properties of

and omit the details.

), and increasing
t
suitably, we obtain

\sup_{x\in[-L,L]}\left(\tilde{u}(t,x)-\varphi_{\alpha_{\infty}+\varepsilon}\right)\leq C_{L}\varepsilon.

(4.62)

The claim (
4.52
) then follows from (
4.53
), (
4.55
), and (
4.62
). This concludes the proof for
\beta\in(0,2)
.

The case
\beta\leq 0

This case is essentially the same, except for some simplifications,
so we only highlight the changes that need to be made. The key estimate is to obtain an upper bound on

s_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=e^{x}(\tilde{u}(t,x)-\varphi_{\alpha_{\infty}+\varepsilon}).

Now,
\varphi_{\alpha}
is defined such that

\varphi_{\alpha}(t,(t+1)^{\gamma})=\alpha(t+1)^{\gamma}e^{-(t+1)^{\gamma}-t^{2\gamma-1}/4}.

In this case
\zeta_{\alpha}
has the same asymptotics as in (
4.50
). Then
s_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

\displaystyle s_{t}-s_{xx}

\displaystyle+\frac{3}{2(t+1)}(s_{x}-s)+\beta\tilde{u}(s_{x}-s)+s(\tilde{u}+\varphi_{\alpha_{\infty}+\varepsilon})

(4.63)

\displaystyle=-\beta s\partial_{x}\varphi_{\alpha_{\infty}+\varepsilon}-\partial_{x}\varphi_{\alpha_{\infty}+\varepsilon}\left(\dot{\zeta}_{\alpha_{\infty}+\varepsilon}+\frac{3}{2(t+1)}\right)\leq\frac{C}{(t+1)^{1-\gamma}}.

The last inequality holds when
|x|\leq(t+1)^{\gamma}
for the same reasons as in (
4.27
) and due to the fact
that
\beta\leq 0
and
\partial_{x}\varphi_{\alpha_{\infty}+\varepsilon}=\phi_{\beta}^{\prime}\leq 0
. Thus, the same upper bound as in
Section

4
holds.

On the other hand, it is easy to check that
\tilde{u}>\varphi_{\alpha_{\infty}-\varepsilon}
on
x<(t+1)^{\gamma}
for
t
sufficiently large. This is
due to
Section

4
, which yields the correct ordering at
x=(t+1)^{\gamma}
, and the fact that
\tilde{u}
is steeper than
\varphi_{\alpha_{\infty}-\varepsilon}
.

The combination of the above with a simpler version of the argument for the case
\beta\in(0,2)
yields the desired convergence. This finishes the proof of
1.1
when
\beta<2
.
\Box

5
The case
\beta=2
: bounds on the front location

We now turn to the case
\beta=2
, where the analysis is particularly delicate.
Let us define the shift
\mu(t)
by

u(t,2t-\mu(t))=\frac{1}{2}.

(5.1)

Note that, due to the sign convention in (
5.1
), an upper bound on
\mu(t)
is a lower bound on the front location
and vice versa.
Our goal in this section is to prove the following upper and lower bounds on

\mu(t)
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
u(t,x)
be the solution to the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
),
with the initial condition as in Theorem
1.1
, and let
\mu(t)
be defined by
(
5.1
).
There exists
m_{0}>0
that depends on
u(0,x)
so that

\frac{1}{2}\log t-m_{0}\leq\mu(t)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log t+m_{0},~~\hbox{ for all $t\geq 1$.}

(5.2)

Later, in Section
6
,
we will improve these bounds to the precise asymptotics of
1.1
:

\mu(t)=\frac{1}{2}\log t+x_{0}+o(1)\hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$.}

(5.3)

Proposition
5
is, however, a crucial step in the proof of (
5.3
).
Its proof occupies almost all of the rest of this section and is the heart of this paper. In order to explain
the outline of the proof, we will need to do some preliminary transformations, leading
to Lemmas
5.1.2
and
5.1.2
below that imply the conclusion
of Proposition
5
. We will discuss their
proofs when we come to their respective statements.
As a technical comment, we mention that without loss of generality we will, once again, take
u(0,x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
.

At the very end of this section, we use Proposition
5
to obtain a helpful bound in an intermediate
region in a short Section
5.6
.

5.1
Outline of the proof of Proposition
5

5.1.1
An exponential moment

We first give a heuristic argument to explain the delay in the case
\beta=2
and the role of
\beta
.
Consider the Burgers-FKPP equation, in the moving frame
x\to x-2t+\mu(t)
, with
an unknown shift
\mu(t)
:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{t}-2\tilde{u}_{x}+\mu^{\prime}(t)\tilde{u}_{x}+\beta\tilde{u}\tilde{u}_{x}=\tilde{u}_{xx}+\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}^{2}.

(5.4)

To highlight the role of
\beta
, we have not yet specified it to the value
\beta=2
.
A simple computation, using only (
5.4
) and several integration by parts, shows that the exponential moment

I(t)=\int\tilde{u}(t,x)e^{x}dx

(5.5)

satisfies an ODE

\begin{split}\frac{dI(t)}{dt}&=\int(2\tilde{u}_{x}-\mu^{\prime}(t)\tilde{u}_{x}-\beta\tilde{u}\tilde{u}_{x}+\tilde{u}_{xx}+\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}^{2})e^{x}dx\\
&=\int(-2\tilde{u}+\mu^{\prime}(t)\tilde{u}-\frac{\beta}{2}(\tilde{u}^{2})_{x}-\tilde{u}_{x}+\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}^{2})e^{x}dx\\
&=\int(-2\tilde{u}+\mu^{\prime}(t)\tilde{u}+\frac{\beta}{2}\tilde{u}^{2}+\tilde{u}+\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}^{2})e^{x}dx\\
&=\int\Big(\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}-1\Big)\tilde{u}^{2}+\mu^{\prime}(t)\tilde{u}\Big)e^{x}dx=\mu^{\prime}(t)\int\tilde{u}e^{x}dx=\mu^{\prime}(t)I(t).\end{split}

(5.6)

In the first equality, we simply used (
5.4
). In the second-to-last inequality, we used that
\beta=2
. In every inequality between, we only integrated by parts and cancelled terms.
Therefore,

\displaystyle I(t)=I(0)e^{\mu(t)},

(5.7)

as long as we choose
\mu(0)=0
. As is clear from (
5.6
), this algebraic property is specific to
\beta=2
.

If
\mu(t)
is the “correct frame,”
in the sense that
\tilde{u}(t,x)
converges to a traveling wave, we expect from the philosophy of the self-similar variables that
\tilde{u}(t,x)

has the asymptotics

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(t,x)\sim\frac{1}{1+e^{x-x_{0}}}e^{-{x^{2}}/{(4t)}},~~\hbox{ for $t\gg 1$ and $x\geq 0$.}

(5.8)

This indicates that if
I(t)
is computed in the correct reference frame, then

I(t)=\int e^{x}\tilde{u}(t,x)dx\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x-x_{0}}}e^{-{x^{2}}/{(4t)}}dx\sim C_{0}\sqrt{t},~~\hbox{ for $t\gg 1$,}

(5.9)

with an explicit constant
C_{0}
that depends on
x_{0}
.
For this to be consistent with (
5.7
), we should have

\mu(t)\sim\frac{1}{2}\log t+C_{1},~~~~\hbox{ for $t\gg 1$,}

(5.10)

explaining the
(1/2)\log t
shift for the front position.
The rest of this section and the following one is
a justification of (
5.10
).

5.1.2
An inhomogeneous conservation law

In order to explain the outline of the proof of Proposition
5
, we need
to introduce a change of variable related to the evolution of mass for the exponential moment
in (
5.6
).
Let
u(t,x)
be the solution to the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
)
with the specific value
\beta=2
, and set

\widehat{u}(t,x)=u(t,x+2t),

(5.11)

which
satisfies

\displaystyle\widehat{u}_{t}-2\widehat{u}_{x}+2\widehat{u}\widehat{u}_{x}=\widehat{u}_{xx}+\widehat{u}(1-\widehat{u}),

(5.12)

Note that we use a slightly different notation here for the shifted function
\widehat{u}
rather than
\tilde{u}
.
This is to denote the difference in shift:
\widehat{u}
is shifted into the moving frame
2t
, while
\tilde{u}
is shifted to the moving frame
2t-\mu(t)
matching the front, as,
for instance, in the proof of
Section

2
.

The first key observation is that the function

p(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x),

(5.13)

satisfies a viscous spatially inhomogeneous conservation law:

p_{t}+\big(p^{2}e^{-x}\big)_{x}=p_{xx},

(5.14)

with the initial condition

p(0,x)=e^{x}\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0).

(5.15)

Recall that we assume, without loss of generality, that
u(0,x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
.
Notice that (
5.14
) conserves mass for
p(t,x)
:

\int p(t,x)dx=\int p(0,x)dx=1.

(5.16)

The normalization (
5.1
)
in terms of
\widehat{u}(t,x)
becomes

\widehat{u}(t,-\mu(t))=\frac{1}{2},

(5.17)

which translates into

p(t,-\mu(t))=\frac{1}{2}e^{-\mu(t)}.

(5.18)

A simple preliminary observation is that
the solution to (
5.14
)-(
5.15
) satisfies

p(t,x)\leq e^{x},~~\hbox{ for all $t\geq 0$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$,}

(5.19)

simply because
\widehat{u}(t,x)\leq 1
. Note also that
p(x)=e^{x}
and

p(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{(x-\xi)}}

(5.20)

are exact solutions to (
5.14
), for any shift
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

The proof of Proposition
5
relies on the analysis of
the solution to (
5.14
)-(
5.15
) and
proceeds in the following steps. First, we prove an upper bound on
\mu(t)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

There exists
K_{1}>0
so that
\mu(t)
be defined by (
5.17
) satisfies

\mu(t)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log t+K_{1},~~\hbox{ for all $t\geq 1$.}

(5.21)

Lemma
5.1.2
is proved in Section
5.3
. Let us give a brief outline of the proof.
It is based on the conservation (
5.16
)
of the total mass of
p(t,x)
, together with estimating the mass of
p(t,x)
separately
in the regions
\{x<-\mu(t)\}
,
\{-\mu(t)<x<N\sqrt{t}\}
and
\{x>N\sqrt{t}\}
.

First, we will show that the mass of
p(t,x)
in the region
\{x>N\sqrt{t}\}
is exponentially small in
N

by an argument that bounds
exponential moments of
p
. Next, using the simple exponential bound (
5.19
), we can check that
the mass in the region
\{x<-\mu(t)\}
is bounded above by
\exp\{-\mu(t)\}
.

To bound the mass in the middle region, recall that when
\beta=2
, the traveling wave moving with the minimal speed
c_{*}=2
is explicit
and is given by (
1.14
):

\phi(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{x}}.

(5.22)

From this and the steepness comparison in Proposition
1
, we immediately deduce the following useful property.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
u(0,\cdot)
is steeper than
\phi
given by (
5.22
), then, for each
v\in(0,1)
and
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle u(t,x)\geq\ \Big(1+\frac{1-v}{v}e^{x-x(t,v)}\Big)^{-1},~~\text{if }x<x(t,v),

(5.23)

\displaystyle u(t,x)\leq\ \Big(1+\frac{1-v}{v}e^{x-x(t,v)}\Big)^{-1},~~\text{if }x>x(t,v).

Here,
x(t,v)
is defined implicitly by
v=u(t,x(t,v))
.

By
Section

5.1.2
,
and (
5.18
), we know that

p(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x)\leq\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x+\mu(t)}}\leq e^{-\mu(t)},~~~\hbox{ for $x>-\mu(t)$}.

(5.24)

It follows that the mass in the region
-\mu(t)\leq x\leq N\sqrt{t}
is bounded by
2N\sqrt{t}e^{-\mu(t)}

(it is easy to show the weak bound
\mu(t)<N\sqrt{t}
,
see (
5.28
) below). Combining all three bounds and recalling mass conservation (
5.16
) of
p
, we deduce that
\mu(t)

must satisfy the upper bound in
Section

5.1.2
. The details are given in
Section

5.3
.

To prove a lower bound for
\mu(t)
we use the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

There exist
C>0
so that, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t>0
,

p(t,x)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}.

(5.25)

The constant
C
depends on the initial data nontrivially.

We prove
Section

5.1.2
in
Section

5.4
, and its surprisingly delicate
proof is outlined there.
When combined with (
5.18
), Lemma
5.1.2
implies the lower bound
in Proposition
5

\mu(t)\geq\frac{1}{2}\log t-C,\hbox{ for all $t\geq 1$.}

(5.26)

Let us make a brief comment that the
t^{-1/2}
decay rate in (
5.25
)
is standard for parabolic equations in one dimension, and would be expected
for a solution to (
5.13
). Nevertheless,
the proof of Lemma
5.1.2
is much less straightforward than one would naively expect.
To illustrate the potential obstacles, notice that (
5.14
) can be written as

p_{t}+(\widehat{u}p)_{x}=p_{xx}.

(5.27)

One might hope to “forget” the connection between
\widehat{u}
and
p
,
and prove
t^{-1/2}
decay for linear divergence form advection-diffusion equations
of the form (
5.27
) in general,
with an advection term
\widehat{u}(t,x)
that, say,
connects two constants on the left and on the right. This seems to
be a good cartoon for
\widehat{u}(t,x)
.
However, such decay cannot hold in general. Indeed, consider the following explicit example. Let

q(x)=\frac{1}{e^{x}+e^{-x}},\quad\text{ and }\quad v(x)=-\frac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{e^{x}+e^{-x}},

so that
q(x)
is a steady solution to

q_{t}+(vq)_{x}=q_{xx}.

The function
q(x)
is rapidly decaying at infinity but, of course, it
does not decay in time. This shows that the boundedness or existence of the limits
at infinity of
\widehat{u}
are not sufficient to determine the decay.
It is crucial that
\widehat{u}
is negligible on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and its profile does not move
much – these are, however, exactly the properties that we are trying to prove. Circumventing
these difficulties requires interesting a priori estimates for our specific problem,
which will be discussed in Section
5.4
.

To summarize,
Section

5
reduces to Lemmas
5.1.2
and
5.1.2
and we prove these lemmas in the rest of this section.

5.2
Preliminary weak bounds on
\mu

Let us first give some very poor bounds on
\mu(t)
that, at least, ensure that it does not behave too wildly. These are useful in the sequel.

For a very simple bound, a comparison to the standard KPP equation
with
\beta=0
that uses monotonicity of
u(t,x)
in
x
, implies that

\mu(t)\leq\frac{3}{2}\log t+C,

(5.28)

with a universal constant
C
.

We now use the conservation of mass for
p
(
5.16
) to obtain a lower bound on
\mu
.
Recalling
Section

5.1.2
and the definition (
5.1
) of
\mu
, we find

p(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x)\geq\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x+\mu(t)}},~~\hbox{ for $x<-\mu(t)$.}

We emphasize that the proof of
Section

5.1.2
is independent of all other lemmas in this section – it is simply a consequence of the steepness comparison. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(0,x)dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(t,x)dx\geq\int_{-\infty}^{-\mu(t)}\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x+\mu(t)}}dx=e^{-\mu(t)}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}dx\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-\mu(t)}.

We conclude that

\mu(t)\geq-\log 2.

(5.29)

The bounds (
5.28
) and (
5.29
) will be greatly improved below.

5.3
An upper bound on the shift: the proof of Lemma
5.1.2

As we have mentioned, the strategy of the proof is to show that if
\mu(t)
is too large then

p(t,x)
can not have a mass larger than
1/10
in any of the three regions

L=\{x<-\mu(t)\},~~M=\{-\mu(t)\leq x\leq N\sqrt{t}\},~~R=\{x>N\sqrt{t}\},

(5.30)

provided that
N
is also chosen sufficiently large. This would contradict (
5.16
).
Note that (
5.29
) implies
N\sqrt{t}\geq-\mu(t)
for
t\geq 1
and

N
sufficiently large, so that the regions above are well-defined.

For the left region
L
, we simply apply (
5.19
) and write

\int_{L}p(t,x)dx=\int_{-\infty}^{-\mu(t)}p(t,x)dx\leq\int_{-\infty}^{-\mu(t)}e^{x}dx=e^{-\mu(t)}.

(5.31)

For the middle region
M
, we also have a simple estimate that uses
Section

5.1.2
:

\begin{split}\int_{M}p(t,x)dx&=\int_{-\mu(t)}^{N\sqrt{t}}e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x)dx\leq\int_{-\mu(t)}^{N\sqrt{t}}\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x+\mu(t)}}dx\\
&=e^{-\mu(t)}\int_{0}^{N\sqrt{t}+\mu(t)}\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}dx\leq CNe^{-\mu(t)}\sqrt{t},\end{split}

(5.32)

for all
t\geq 1
. We used (
5.28
) in the last step above.

Now, we deal with the right region
R
.
First we state a bound on an exponential moment of
p
: fix any
m\in(0,1/2)
and let

I_{m}(t)=\int\big(e^{mx}+e^{-mx}\big)p(t,x)dx.

(5.33)

We claim that

I_{m}(t)\leq Ce^{Cm^{2}t}.

(5.34)

We postpone the proof of (
5.34
) momentarily and show how to conclude the bound for
the integral over
R
. For
N>1
large, we estimate this mass by using (
5.33
)
with
m=1/\sqrt{t}
. This gives

\displaystyle\int_{R}p(t,x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{N\sqrt{t}}^{\infty}p(t,x)dx\leq\Big(\int_{N\sqrt{t}}^{\infty}e^{x/\sqrt{t}}p(t,x)dx\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_{N\sqrt{t}}^{\infty}e^{-x/\sqrt{t}}p(t,x)dx\Big)^{1/2}

(5.35)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\int_{N\sqrt{t}}^{\infty}e^{-x/\sqrt{t}}p(t,x)dx\Big)\leq C\Big(\int_{N\sqrt{t}}^{\infty}e^{-N}p(t,x)dx\Big)^{1/2}\leq Ce^{-N/2}.

We used the conservation of mass (
5.16
) in the last step.

We may now put the estimates (
5.31
), (
5.32
) and (
5.35
) together to obtain

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(0,x)dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(t,x)dx=\int_{L}p(t,x)dx+\int_{M}p(t,x)dx+\int_{R}p(t,x)dx

(5.36)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\mu(t)}+CN\sqrt{t}e^{-\mu(t)}+Ce^{-N/2}.

Choosing
N=2\log(2C)
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\mu(t)}+C\sqrt{t}e^{-\mu(t)},

(5.37)

so that

e^{\mu(t)}\leq C(1+\sqrt{t}),

(5.38)

and (
5.21
) follows, as desired.

All that remains to finish the proof of Lemma
5.1.2
is to establish (
5.34
), which we do now. Recalling
I_{m}
from (
5.33
), multiplying (
5.14
) by
e^{mx}+e^{-mx}
, and integrating yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}I_{m}=m^{2}\int(e^{mx}+e^{-mx})p(t,x)dx+m\int e^{-x}[e^{mx}-e^{-mx}]p^{2}(t,x)dx.

(5.39)

We decompose the last integral:

\displaystyle J_{m}=m\int e^{-x}[e^{mx}-e^{-mx}]p^{2}(t,x)dx=m\int e^{-x}\tanh(mx)[e^{mx}+e^{-mx}]p^{2}(t,x)dx

(5.40)

\displaystyle\leq m\int_{-\infty}^{\log(2)}e^{-x}|\tanh(mx)|[e^{mx}+e^{-mx}]p^{2}(t,x)dx

\displaystyle+m\int^{\infty}_{\log(2)}e^{-x}|\tanh(mx)|[e^{mx}+e^{-mx}]p^{2}(t,x)dx=J_{m}^{-}+J_{m}^{+}.

For
J_{m}^{-}
, we use (
5.19
), the bound

|\tanh(mx)|\leq m|x|,

(5.41)

and the assumption
0<m<1/2
to obtain

\displaystyle J_{m}^{-}

\displaystyle\leq m\int_{-\infty}^{\log(2)}|\tanh(mx)|[e^{mx}+e^{-mx}]e^{x}dx\leq m^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\log(2)}|x|e^{(1-m)x}dx\leq Cm^{2}.

(5.42)

On the other hand, as
x>\log(2)>-\mu(t)
, by (
5.29
), we may apply
Section

5.1.2
to obtain

\displaystyle J_{m}^{+}

\displaystyle=m\int_{\log(2)}^{+\infty}|\tanh(mx)|[e^{mx}+e^{-mx}]\widehat{u}(t,x)p(t,x)dx

\displaystyle\leq m\int_{\log(2)}^{+\infty}|\tanh(mx)|[e^{mx}+e^{-mx}]\frac{p(t,x)}{1+e^{x+\mu(t)}}dx

\displaystyle\leq m^{2}\int_{\log(2)}^{+\infty}\frac{|x|}{1+e^{x+\mu(t)}}[e^{mx}+e^{-mx}]p(t,x)dx\leq m^{2}e^{-\mu(t)}\int_{\log(2)}^{+\infty}[e^{mx}+e^{-mx}]p(t,x)dx.

In the second inequality, we used (
5.41
),
and in the last inequality, we used that

|x|\leq e^{x}\leq e^{-\mu(t)}(1+e^{x+\mu(t)}).

Recalling that
-\mu(t)\leq\log(2)
from (
5.29
), we find

J_{m}^{+}\leq Cm^{2}I_{m}.

(5.43)

The combination of (
5.42
) and (
5.43
) yields

\frac{dI_{m}}{dt}\leq C_{0}m^{2}(I_{m}+1),

for some universal constant
C_{0}
. This implies that

I_{m}(t)\leq e^{C_{0}m^{2}t}[I_{m}(0)+1]\leq 5e^{C_{0}m^{2}t},

since, recalling that
m<1/2
,

I_{m}(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{x}(e^{mx}+e^{-mx})dx\leq 4.

This concludes the proof of (
5.34
) and, thus, that of Lemma
5.1.2
.
\Box

5.4
A lower bound on the shift: the proof of Lemma
5.1.2

The proof of Lemma
5.1.2
is quite a bit more involved than that for Lemma
5.1.2
. Let us first explain the main steps of the proof. Recall that the standard
L^{\infty}
-decay for diffusion equations
of the self-adjoint form

z_{t}=\nabla\cdot(a(x)\nabla z),~~x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

(5.44)

with a uniformly positive and bounded diffusivity
a(x)
is obtained as follows: first, one gets the dissipation inequality

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int|z(t,x)|^{2}dx\leq-C\int|\nabla z(t,x)|^{2}dz.

(5.45)

An application of the Nash inequality leads, after solving an elementary differential inequality,
to the
L^{1}-L^{2}
decay estimate

\|z(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{t^{n/4}}\|z(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}.

(5.46)

The self-adjoint form of (
5.44
) and the estimate (
5.46
) give the dual bound

\|z(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{C}{t^{n/4}}\|z(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}.

(5.47)

The last step is to apply the semi-group property and the above estimates to deduce that

\|z(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{C}{t^{n/4}}\|z(t/2,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{t^{n/2}}\|z(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}.

(5.48)

See, e.g.,
[
18
, Section 2.4]
for a full treatment of this.

It seems not possible to directly obtain a dissipation inequality for the
L^{2}
-norm of the function
p(t,x)
,
starting with (
5.14
), due to the spatial inhomogeneity of the nonlinear term.
Instead, to get an analogue of (
5.45
), we will use a
suitably chosen weight
\rho(t,x)
that weighs
\mathbb{R}_{+}
more than
\mathbb{R}_{-}
, and establish
an
L_{w}^{2}
-dissipation inequality for the function

\varphi=\frac{p}{\rho}.

(5.49)

Here we use
w
as a subscript to emphasize that we are working in weighted Lebesgue spaces. Such weight
allows to focus on where advection is negligible and diffusion dominates the evolution
of (
5.27
).

It turns out that the weight
\rho(t,x)=1-\widehat{u}(t,x)

can actually be used to produce a dissipation inequality because, as we will see,
the function
1-\widehat{u}
is a super-solution for (
5.14
) satisfied by
p(t,x)
.
This property is not purely algebraic: it will, once again, use the steepness comparison of
\widehat{u}(t,x)
to the traveling wave.
The dissipation computation is directly inspired by the relative entropy
arguments for linear advection-diffusion equations in
[
17
,
41
]
. Here, however,
we compute the entropy relative not to a solution but a super-solution, and the nonlinear
nature of the present situation requires specific cancellations. This is the subject of
Section

5.4
below.

We also establish a weighted Nash inequality stated in Proposition
5.4
below.
When adapted to our setting, it yields the appropriate long time decay of the
L_{w}^{2}
-norm of
\varphi
,
up to a (potentially large) boundary layer in time. An interesting complication is that the weighted Nash inequality
holds for a nontrivial class of weights satisfying certain assumptions, and we need to control the fact that our weight,
coming from the solution to a nonlinear evolution equation, satisfies these assumptions
for all
t>0
in a uniform way. This is done in the course of the proof of Lemma
5.4

below.

In contrast to the standard proof for diffusion equations, we cannot directly pass from
L^{2}_{w}
-decay to
L^{\infty}
-decay. Indeed, due to the weight, the aforementioned
L_{w}^{2}
-decay estimate
is
not
an
L_{w}^{1}\to L_{w}^{2}
estimate as the boundary layer depends
on the initial
L^{2}_{w}
-norm of
\varphi
and, hence, the usual adjointness trick in (
5.47
),
used to establish
the
L^{\infty}
decay is not available. This complication is present even for the linear equation (
5.27
) when the relationship between
p
and
\widehat{u}
is “forgotten.”
In fact, decay in
L^{\infty}_{w}
of
\varphi

is not even expected in the setting in which we find ourselves.
To pass from the
L_{w}^{2}
-bounds for the function
\varphi(t,x)
to the
L^{\infty}
decay for

p(t,x)
, we use time averages to find a particular intermediate
time
T_{g}<T
, at which
\varphi
satisfies “good” pointwise bounds in a region of interest.
Those bounds, stated in Lemma
5.4
, can be transferred
to show that
p
is bounded both by
e^{x}
and
C/\sqrt{T}
. We can then “trap” that estimate
going forward in time, from the time
T_{g}
until the time
T
,
by breaking
p
up into a small mass part, which necessarily stays small
due to conservation of mass and parabolic regularity theory, and a part that sits under an explicit,
small super-solution. This will conclude the proof.

As the reader will surely have noticed, there is a subtle, but serious, issue in the above outline.
The
L^{2}_{w}
decay requires that the initial
L^{2}_{w}
norm be bounded,
which, as can be immediately seen from (
5.49
),
is not true when
\widehat{u}(0,\cdot)
is the Heaviside function,
the function
p(0,\cdot)
is given by (
5.15
), and
\rho=1-\widehat{u}
.
This requires an extra step where we choose an approximate initial condition

\widehat{u}_{a}(0,x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\gamma(x-a)}}\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0),

(5.50)

with
\gamma\in(1,2)
, to obtain new solutions
\widehat{u}_{a}
and
p_{a}
. Here,
a>0

is a parameter depending on the final time
T
at which we wish to establish the upper bound.
A careful analysis shows that, with an appropriate choice of
a
,
the two solutions,
p
and
p_{a}
, stay
O(1/\sqrt{T})
away from each other,
due to the error estimate in Lemma
5.4
.

Below, we state the upper bound on the modification
p_{a}
and show how to bootstrap that
bound to the decay of
p
itself. This is
done in
Section

5.4.1
. Then we give the proof of the upper bound on
p_{a}
following the outline above, in
Section

5.4.2
.

5.4.1
The proof of
Section

5.1.2

A modified initial condition

We first construct the modified solution
p_{a}(t,x)
.
Recall that
\widehat{u}(t,x)
is the solution to the Burgers-FKPP equation

\widehat{u}_{t}-2\widehat{u}_{x}+2\widehat{u}\widehat{u}_{x}=\widehat{u}_{xx}+\widehat{u}-\widehat{u}^{2},

(5.51)

with the initial condition
\widehat{u}_{\rm in}(x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
. For any
a>0
and
\gamma\in(1,2)
, let

\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x)
be the solution to (
5.51
) with the initial condition

\widehat{u}_{a}(0,x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\gamma(x-a)}}\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0),

(5.52)

and set

p(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x),~~p_{a}(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x).

(5.53)

As in (
5.14
) and (
5.27
), the function
p_{a}
satisfies

\partial_{t}p_{a}+\partial_{x}(p_{a}^{2}e^{-x})=\partial_{x}^{2}p_{a}

(5.54)

and

\partial_{t}p_{a}+\partial_{x}(\widehat{u}_{a}p_{a})=\partial_{x}^{2}p_{a}.

(5.55)

We have made the switch to
\partial_{t,x}
notation to avoid the awkward double sub-script.
It is easy to observe that
\widehat{u}_{a}\leq 1
, and we have, by the comparison principle (notice that
e^{x}
solves (
5.54
)):

p_{a}(t,x)\leq e^{x}\qquad\text{ for all }t\geq 0,~~x\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.56)

Two important quantities of interest for us are

\begin{split}&M_{a}(t)=\int\left(\frac{p_{a}(t,x)}{1-\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x)}\right)(1-\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x))\,dx=\int p_{a}(t,x)\,dx=M_{a}(0),\\
&I_{a}(t)=\int\left(\frac{p_{a}(t,x)}{1-\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x)}\right)^{2}(1-\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x))\,dx=\int\frac{p_{a}(t,x)^{2}}{1-\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x)}\,dx.\end{split}

(5.57)

The last equality in the first line above follows by conservation of mass. Note that
M_{a}

and
I_{a}
are, respectively, the weighted
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
norms of the function

\varphi=p_{a}/(1-\widehat{u}_{a})
with weight
1-\widehat{u}_{a}
.
Two easy computations show that
M_{a}(0)
is uniformly bounded in
a>0
:

\log(2)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}\,dx\leq M_{a}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{x}\,dx=1

(5.58)

and
I_{a}(0)
is finite for all
a>0
:

\displaystyle I_{a}(0)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{e^{2x}\widehat{u}_{a}^{2}(0,x)}{1-\widehat{u}_{a}(0,x)}\,dx\leq\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{e^{2x}}{1-\widehat{u}_{a}(0,x)}\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{2x}\frac{1+e^{\gamma(x-a)}}{e^{\gamma(x-a)}}\,dx

(5.59)

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{\gamma a}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{(2-\gamma)x}\,dx=\frac{2}{2-\gamma}e^{\gamma a}

but is not uniformly bounded as
a\to+\infty
. Note that an analogous estimate for
I(0)

with
u_{a}
and
p_{a}
replaced by
u
and
p
, respectively, does not hold, as

\frac{p^{2}(0,x)}{1-u(0,x)}=+\infty\cdot\mathbbm{1}(x<0).

(5.60)

This is what prevents us from establishing the decay of
p
directly.

As we see in the sequel, another crucial feature of this altered initial condition is that

\widehat{u}_{a}(0,\cdot)
is steeper than the traveling wave
1/(1+e^{x})
.
This is why we take
\gamma>1
in (
5.52
).
The restriction
\gamma<2

comes from the upper bound on
I_{a}(0)
in (
5.59
).

The main estimate we establish on
p_{a}
is:

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x)
be the solution to (
5.51
) with the initial condition (
5.52
) for some
a>0
and
1<\gamma<2
,
and
p_{a}(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x)
. There exists a universal constant
C_{\ref{l:altered u_0 decay}}>0
such that the following holds. Set

t_{1}(a)=C_{\ref{l:altered u_0 decay}}I_{a}(0),

(5.61)

with
I_{a}
defined in (
5.57
).
There exists
K>0
that does not depend on
a>1
so that

p_{a}(t,x)\leq\frac{K}{\sqrt{t}},~~\hbox{ for all }t\geq 4t_{1}(a).

(5.62)

We postpone the proof of
Section

5.4
until Section
5.4.2
.
First, we obtain a closeness estimate on
p
and
p_{a}
. Its proof is succinct enough to give it immediately.

Lemma 5.6
.

Fix
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
\gamma>0
. Let
\widehat{u}(t,x)
and
\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x)
be the solutions
to (
5.51
) with the respective initial conditions
\widehat{u}(0,x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)

and (
5.52
), respectively.
There is a constant
C>0
that does not depend on
a
or
\gamma
such that
p(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x)

and
p_{a}(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x)
satisfy

0\leq p(t,x)-p_{a}(t,x)\leq Ce^{-\gamma a}\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$ and $t\geq 1$.}

(5.63)

Proof.

Let
h(t,x)=p(t,x)-p_{a}(t,x)
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that
h(0,\cdot)\geq 0

and, due to (
5.14
) and (
5.54
), that
h
satisfies the parabolic equation

h_{t}-h_{xx}=-(p^{2}e^{-x})_{x}+(p_{a}^{2}e^{-x})_{x}=-(hw)_{x},

(5.64)

with

w(t,x)=e^{-x}(p_{a}(t,x)+p(t,x))=\widehat{u}(t,x)+\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x).

Hence, the comparison principle implies that
h\geq 0
. This finishes the lower bound in (
5.63
).

To conclude the upper bound, we use
mass conservation for
p(t,x)
and
p_{a}(t,x)
. Indeed,

\begin{split}\int h(t,x)\,dx&=\int(p(t,x)-p_{a}(t,x))\,dx=\int(p(0,x)-p_{a}(0,x))\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{x}\frac{e^{\gamma(x-a)}}{1+e^{\gamma(x-a)}}\,dx\\
&=e^{a}\int_{-\infty}^{-a}\frac{e^{(1+\gamma)x}}{1+e^{\gamma x}}\,dx\leq\frac{1}{1+\gamma}e^{a}e^{-(1+\gamma)a}=\frac{1}{1+\gamma}e^{-\gamma a}.\end{split}

(5.65)

Using parabolic regularity theory together with (
5.65
) and positivity of
h
,
we get immediately that

\sup_{x}h(t,x)\leq Ce^{-\gamma a}\qquad\hbox{for all $t\geq 1$ and $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$,}

(5.66)

finishing the proof. The constant
C
does not depend on
a
or
\gamma
.
\Box

Proof of Lemma
5.1.2
.

We are now in a position to combine
Section

5.4
with
Section

5.4
,
in order to prove
Section

5.1.2
. One delicate point is that Lemma
5.4

requires us to take
a
large. On the other hand,

I_{a}(0)
in (
5.59
) blows up as
a\to+\infty
. This will require a careful balancing act.

We note that we need only prove
Section

5.1.2
for
T
sufficiently large, as the claim follows for “smaller”
T
by simply increasing the constant
C
.

Let
\gamma=3/2
, and let
C_{\ref{l:altered u_0 decay}}
be the universal constant from
Section

5.4
. Given any

T>(16C_{\ref{l:altered u_0 decay}})^{2}+1,

(5.67)

choose

a=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log(T)>0.

(5.68)

Recall from (
5.59
) and the choice of
\gamma
that

\displaystyle I_{a}(0)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{2-\gamma}e^{\gamma a}=4e^{\gamma a}.

(5.69)

By our choice of
a
and (
5.61
), it follows that

4t_{1}(a)\,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}=4C_{\ref{l:altered u_0 decay}}I_{a}(0)\leq 16C_{\ref{l:altered u_0 decay}}e^{\gamma a}=16C_{\ref{l:altered u_0 decay}}e^{\gamma\left(\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log T\right)}=16C_{\ref{l:altered u_0 decay}}\sqrt{T}}\leq T,

(5.70)

where the last inequality follows due to (
5.67
).
Due to (
5.70
), we may apply
Section

5.4
to find

\sup_{x}\,p_{a}(T,x)\leq\frac{K}{\sqrt{T}}.

(5.71)

We may also use Lemma
5.4
to see that

0\leq p(t,x)-p_{a}(t,x)\leq Ce^{-\gamma a},~~~~\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$ and $t>1$.}

(5.72)

Recalling the choice (
5.68
) of
\gamma
and
a
in (
5.72
) and using (
5.71
) to

, we deduce that

\sup_{x}\,p(T,x)\leq\sup_{x}\,p_{a}(T,x)+Ce^{-\gamma a}\leq\frac{K}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}},

finishing the proof of Lemma
5.1.2
.
\Box

The proof of
Section

5.4

We now prove Lemma
5.4
,
following the outline from the beginning of
Section

5.4
.

Step one: decay in a weighted
L^{2}
space

Our first goal is to obtain an
L^{2}
-decay estimate, an analogue of (
5.46
) in the
present situation.
We will work with norms weighted by

\rho_{a}=1-\widehat{u}_{a}.

(5.73)

and make a change of function

\varphi_{a}=\frac{p_{a}}{1-\widehat{u}_{a}}=\frac{p_{a}}{\rho_{a}}.

(5.74)

The main goal of this step is the decay rate of
I_{a}
, which we state here.
Recall that
\widehat{u}_{a}
and
p_{a}
are defined in
Section

5.4
.

Lemma 5.7
.

For
a>0
and
\gamma\in(1,2)
, let
t_{1}(a)
,
M_{a}
, and
I_{a}(0)
be as in (
5.61
) and (
5.59
). Then

I_{a}(t_{1})=\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t_{1},x)\rho_{a}(t_{1},x)\,dx\leq 1,

(5.75)

and

I_{a}(t)=\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)dx\leq\frac{C}{1+\sqrt{t-t_{1}(a)}},~~\hbox{ for $t\geq t_{1}(a)$.}

(5.76)

Note that
I_{a}
may be thought of as
\|\varphi_{a}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{a})}
. To prove
Section

5.4
, we
obtain a more general result for weighted
L^{2}
decay, identifying assumptions on the weight under which
decay holds. Afterwards, we show that
\rho_{a}
satisfies these assumptions and apply the general result
to our setting.

The first step is a dissipation inequality, inspired by the relative entropy arguments
for linear equations in
[
17
,
41
]
. As in
[
17
]
, given a weight

\rho(t,x)
and an advection
v(t,x)
, we consider an
operator
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}
defined by

\mathcal{D}_{\rho}q=\rho^{-2}\partial_{x}(\rho^{2}\partial_{x}q)-v\partial_{x}q.

(5.77)

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
v(t,x)
be a smooth bounded function,
q(t,x)
be a solution to

q_{t}+(vq)_{x}=q_{xx},

(5.78)

and
\rho(t,x)
be a super-solution to (
5.78
):

\rho_{t}+(v\rho)_{x}\geq\rho_{xx},

(5.79)

with initial conditions
p(0,x)\geq 0
and
\rho(0,x)>0
such that

\int\varphi(0,x)^{2}\rho(0,x)dx<+\infty,

(5.80)

where
\varphi(t,x)=q(t,x)/\rho(t,x)
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\varphi
satisfies

\varphi_{t}-\mathcal{D}_{\rho}\varphi=-\varphi^{2}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}-\rho_{xx}+(v\rho)_{x}}{\rho}\right),

(5.81)

and

\frac{d}{dt}\int\ \varphi^{2}(t,x)\rho(t,x)dx\leq-2\int\varphi_{x}^{2}(t,x)\rho(t,x)dx.

(5.82)

The main difference with
[
17
,
41
]
is that the function
\rho(t,x)
is
not a solution to the advection-diffusion equation (
5.78
) but a super-solution.

The second general result is an adaptation of the Nash inequality for weighted spaces. This allows us to make use of the dissipation inequality in
Section

5.4
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let an increasing function
r\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{-})\cap C(\mathbb{R})
satisfy the following assumptions:

\displaystyle(i)~~~\lim_{x\to-\infty}r(x)=0,~~0<\lim_{x\to+\infty}r(x)=r_{+}<+\infty,

(5.83)

\displaystyle(ii)~~~\overline{\overline{r}}(x)\leq C_{1}\max\{1,\overline{r}(x)^{2}\}r(x),~~\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$},

with

\bar{r}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}r(y)dy,~~\overline{\overline{r}}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\bar{r}(y)dy.

(5.84)

Then, for any
\theta>0
and any smooth non-negative function
\varphi(x)
that is sufficiently rapidly decaying as
x\to+\infty
and bounded as
x\to-\infty
we have

\int\varphi^{2}(x)r(x)\,dx\leq\frac{2}{\theta}\Big(\int\varphi(x)r(x)dx\Big)^{2}+8C_{1}\max\{1,\theta^{2}\}\int|\varphi_{x}(x)|^{2}r(x)dx.

(5.85)

Let us point out that the weight

r(x)=1-\phi_{2}(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}

satisfies assumptions of Proposition
5.4
, and this is an example the reader may want to keep in mind.

Let us also briefly note the connection with the standard Nash inequality. A key step in the proof
of the latter is to establish (
5.85
) with
\max\{1,\theta^{2}\}
replaced by
\theta^{2}
.
The change here reflects the fact that the measure induced by
r
is finite on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
.

The proofs of
Section

5.4
and
Section

5.4
are postponed until
Section

5.5
.
We first apply these results to establish the
L^{2}
decay of
p_{a}
, that is,
Section

5.4
.

Proof of
Section

5.4

We first assume that the assumptions of the dissipation and Nash inequalities,
that is,
Section

5.4
and
Section

5.4
, hold in a uniform way for
\rho_{a}(t,x)
,
and show how to conclude. Afterwards, we show how to verify those assumptions.

Assuming that we can apply
Section

5.4
and
Section

5.4
, we proceed as follows. By
Section

5.4
,
we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)dx\leq-2\int(\partial_{x}\varphi_{a})^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)dx.

(5.86)

Moreover, recalling the definition of
M_{a}
from (
5.57
) and that it is uniformly
bounded, as in (
5.58
), and applying
Section

5.4
, we find, for any
\theta>0
,

\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(x)\,dx\leq\frac{C}{\theta}+C\max\{1,\theta^{2}\}\int|\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}(t,x)|^{2}\rho_{a}(t,x)dx,

(5.87)

with a constant
C
that does not depend on
t
,
\gamma
, or
a
.

There are two cases to consider: first, assume that at some
t>0
we have

\int|\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}(t,x)|^{2}\rho_{a}(t,x)\,dx\geq 1.

(5.88)

Then, we get from (
5.86
):

\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)\,dx\leq-2.

(5.89)

On the other hand, if (
5.88
) fails, so that

\int|\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}(t,x)|^{2}\rho_{a}(t,x)\,dx<1,

we can use (
5.87
) with the constant

\theta=\Big(\int|\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}|^{2}\rho_{a}\,dx\Big)^{-1/3}\geq 1.

Keeping in mind the upper bound (
5.58
) on

, this leads to

\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(x)\,dx\leq C\Big(\int|\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}(t,x)|^{2}\rho_{a}(t,x)dx\Big)^{1/3}.

Therefore, in the second case we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)\,dx\leq-\frac{1}{C}\Big(\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)\,dx\Big)^{3}.

(5.90)

Putting (
5.89
) and (
5.90
) together, we find

\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)\,dx\leq-\frac{1}{C}\min\Big\{1,\Big(\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)\,dx\Big)^{3}\Big\}.

(5.91)

Setting

t_{1}(a)=CI_{a}(0)\geq C(I_{a}(0)-1)_{+},

(5.92)

we see from (

) that

\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t_{1},x)\rho_{a}(t_{1},x)\,dx\leq 1.

(5.93)

Therefore, for

, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)\,dx\leq-C\Big(\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)\,dx\Big)^{3},

(5.94)

and

\int\varphi_{a}(t,x)^{2}\rho_{a}(t,x)dx\leq\frac{C}{1+\sqrt{t-t_{1}}}.

(5.95)

Hence, all that remains in the proof of Lemma
5.4
is to verify
the assumptions of
Section

5.4
and
Section

5.4
.

We consider first the assumptions of
Section

5.4
. In the notation of
Section

5.4
,
we have
q=p_{a}
,
v=\widehat{u}
, and
\rho=\rho_{a}
. In view of (
5.55
) and (
5.59
),
the assumptions (
5.78
) and (
5.80
) are satisfied. Hence, the only assumption to
check is (
5.79
), a somewhat miraculous property that

\partial_{t}\rho_{a}+\partial_{x}(\widehat{u}_{a}\rho_{a})\geq\partial_{x}^{2}\rho_{a}.

(5.96)

First, using that
\widehat{u}_{a}
satisfies (
5.51
), we find

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho_{a}+\partial_{x}(\widehat{u}_{a}\rho_{a})-\partial_{x}^{2}\rho_{a}&=-\partial_{t}\widehat{u}_{a}+\partial_{x}\widehat{u}_{a}-2\widehat{u}_{a}\partial_{x}\widehat{u}_{a}+\partial_{x}^{2}\widehat{u}_{a}=-\partial_{x}\widehat{u}_{a}-\widehat{u}_{a}(1-\widehat{u}_{a}).\end{split}

(5.97)

The initial condition for
\widehat{u}_{a}
in (

) was chosen to be steeper than

given by (

) – this is

Using Proposition

\widehat{u}_{a}(t,x)

.
Applying this property to (

Using the explicit expression (

) for

,
it is straightforward to check that the right side of (

) is non-negative everywhere:

\begin{split}-\phi_{x}-\phi(1-\phi)&=-\left(-\frac{e^{x}}{(1+e^{x})^{2}}\right)-\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}\frac{1}{1+e^{x}}=0.\end{split}

(5.99)

This can, of course, be also obtained from (
3.24
).
Hence, (

) is established.

Next, we verify that

satisfies assumptions (i) and (ii) of

(5.100)

It is useful to (implicitly) define the analogue

Consider first the case when

,
by Proposition

This gives

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{a}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\rho_{a}(t,y)\,dy\leq\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{\rho_{a}(t,x)e^{y-x}}{1-\rho_{a}(t,x)+\rho_{a}(t,x)e^{y-x}}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho_{a}(t,x)}{1-\rho_{a}(t,x)}\int_{-\infty}^{x}{e^{y-x}}\,dy=\frac{\rho_{a}(t,x)}{1-\rho_{a}(t,x)}\leq 2\rho_{a}(t,x).

In the last line we used that
x<-\mu_{a}(t)
, so that

This implies (
5.100
) for

:

\overline{\overline{\rho}}_{a}(t,x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,y)\,dy\leq 2\int_{-\infty}^{x}\rho_{a}(t,y)\,dy=2\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,x)\leq 4\rho_{a}(t,x).

(5.104)

On the other hand, when
x\geq-\mu_{a}(t)
, we have
\rho_{a}(t,x)\geq 1/2
, so that

\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,x)\leq 2\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x),~~\hbox{ for $x\geq-\mu_{a}(t)$.}

(5.105)

Since
\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,x)
and
\rho_{a}
are increasing, we find,
using (
5.103
) for
y<-\mu_{a}(t)
and (
5.105
) for

:

\begin{split}&\overline{\overline{\rho}}_{a}(t,x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,y)\,dy=\int_{-\infty}^{-\mu_{a}(t)}\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,y)\,dy+\int_{-\mu_{a}(t)}^{x}\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,y)\,dy\\
&\leq 2\int_{-\infty}^{-\mu_{a}(t)}\rho_{a}(t,y)dy+2\int_{-\infty}^{x}\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,y)\rho_{a}(t,y)\,dy\leq 2\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,-\mu_{a}(t))+2\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,x)\int_{-\infty}^{x}\rho_{a}(t,y)\,dy\\
&\leq 4\rho_{a}(t,-\mu_{a}(t))+2\overline{\rho}_{a}^{2}(t,x)\leq 4\rho_{a}(t,x)+4\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,x)^{2}\rho_{a}(t,x)\leq 8\max\{1,\overline{\rho}_{a}(t,x)^{2}\}\rho_{a}(t,x).\end{split}

(5.106)

Taking the maximum of (
5.104
) and (
5.106
),
we arrive at (
5.100
). We deduce that the function
\rho_{a}(t,x)

satisfies the assumptions of the weighted Nash inequality in
Section

5.4
, for all

.
This concludes the proof of Lemma

.
\Box

Step two: pointwise bounds at a particular time

We now find a “good time”
T_{g}<T
when
\varphi_{a}
satisfies the desired bounds via a time averaging.
The catch is that we do not have control over
T_{g}
and, thus, a third step is required afterwards,
to control the solution on the time interval

.

Lemma 5.10
.

For
a>0
and
\gamma\in(1,2)
, let
t_{1}(a)
be as in (
5.61
).
Given any
T>4(t_{1}+1)
, there exists
T_{g}\in[T/2,3T/4]
such that

\begin{split}(i)\quad\sup_{x\geq-\mu_{a}(T_{g})}\varphi_{a}(t_{T},x)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad(ii)\quad\mu_{a}(T_{g})\geq\frac{1}{2}\log(T)-C,\end{split}

(5.107)

where
C
does not depend on
a
or
\gamma
.

Proof.
We first note that (
5.107
)(ii) follows immediately from (
5.107
)(i).
Indeed, (
5.101
) yields

\varphi_{a}(t,-\mu_{a}(t))=e^{-\mu_{a}(t)},

(5.108)

which, in turn, implies, using (
5.107
)(i):

\mu_{a}(T_{g})=-\log\varphi_{a}(t,-\mu_{a}(t))\geq\frac{1}{2}\log T-C,

which is (
5.107
)(ii). Thus, we focus on proving (
5.107
)(i).

To this end, we use time averages in order to find the “good time”
T_{g}

when the (weighted)
L^{2}
bound of
\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}
is small. Since

T/2\geq 2t_{1}(a)
,
Section

5.4
yields

\int\varphi_{a}^{2}(t,x)\rho_{a}(t,x)dx\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}},

(5.109)

for all
t\geq T/2
. Integrating the dissipation inequality (
5.82
) and using (
5.109
), we obtain

\frac{4}{T}\int_{T/2}^{3T/4}\int|\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}(s,x)|^{2}\rho_{a}(s,x)dxds\leq\frac{C}{T^{3/2}}.

As a result, there exists
T_{g}\in[T/2,3T/4]
such that

\int|\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}(T_{g},x)|^{2}\rho_{a}(T_{g},x)dx\leq\frac{C}{T^{3/2}}.

(5.110)

Heuristically, we conclude by arguing that, due to (
5.110
), if
\varphi_{a}
is “too big” somewhere to the right of
-\mu_{a}(t)
,
then it must be “too big” on a large set. However, in this region,
\rho_{a}
is bounded above and below and
p_{a}
and
\varphi_{a}
are comparable. As a result,
p_{a}
will be “too big” on a large set, violating mass conservation. The key point here that makes the above reasoning work is that, by the definitions (
5.73
), (
5.74
), and (
5.101
) of
\rho_{a}
,
\varphi_{a}
, and
\mu_{a}
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\leq\rho_{a}(t,x)\leq 1,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{2}\varphi_{a}(t,x)\leq p_{a}(t,x)\leq\varphi_{a}(t,x),\qquad\text{ for $x>-\mu_{a}(t)$, }

(5.111)

In particular, working on
x>-\mu_{a}(t)
is crucial here as
\rho_{a}
is potentially small to the left of
-\mu_{a}(t)
.

To make this reasoning rigorous, we take any
x_{0}\geq-\mu_{a}(T_{g})
, and
use the Newton-Leibniz formula for any

along with (
5.110
)-(
5.111
), to obtain a lower bound for

:

\begin{split}\varphi_{a}(T_{g},x_{0})&\leq\varphi_{a}(T_{g},y)+\int_{x_{0}}^{y}|\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}|\,dx\leq\varphi_{a}(T_{g},y)+\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\sqrt{T}}|\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}|\,dx\\
&\leq\varphi_{a}(T_{g},y)+\sqrt{2}T^{1/4}\Big(\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\sqrt{T}}|\partial_{x}\varphi_{a}|^{2}\rho_{a}\,dx\Big)^{1/2}\leq\varphi_{a}(T_{g},y)+\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}}.\end{split}

Using again (

), this yields

\inf_{x\in[x_{0},x_{0}+\sqrt{T}]}p_{a}(T_{g},x)\geq\frac{1}{2}\Big(p_{a}(T_{g},x_{0})-\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}}\Big).

(5.112)

Recalling mass conservation (
5.16
) and using (
5.112
) we arrive at

\begin{split}1&=\int p_{a}(T_{g},x)\,dx\geq\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\sqrt{T}}p_{a}(T_{g},x)\,dx\\
&\geq\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\sqrt{T}}\frac{1}{2}\Big(p_{a}(T_{g},x_{0})-\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}}\Big)\,dx=\frac{\sqrt{T}}{2}p_{a}(T_{g},x_{0})-\frac{C}{2}.\end{split}

Rearranging the above and using the arbitrariness of
x_{0}>-\mu_{a}(t)
, as well
as (
5.111
), to translate this into a bound for
\varphi_{a}(t,x_{0})
, finishes the proof
of Lemma
5.4
.
\Box
.

Step three: preserving the
L^{\infty}
-smallness over
[T_{g},T]

We are now in a position to combine the results in the first two steps
to finish the proof Lemma
5.4
,
establishing the upper bound (
5.62
) on

.

Proof of
Section

5.4
.

Combining (
5.111
),
Section

5.4
, and (
5.56
), we find
C>1
such that

p_{a}(T_{g},x)\leq\min\Big(e^{x},\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}}\Big).

(5.113)

The function

P(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{1+\frac{\sqrt{T}}{2C}e^{x}}

(5.114)

is a steady solution to the equation (
5.54
) that
p_{a}
satisfies as

\partial_{x}(P^{2}e^{-x})=\partial_{x}^{2}P.

(5.115)

Moreover, it obeys a uniform bound

\sup_{x}\,P(x)\leq\frac{2C}{\sqrt{T}}.

(5.116)

The next step is to split
p_{a}
into a portion bounded above by
P
and a small error part.
Let us write

p_{a}(t,x)=\psi_{P}(t,x)+\psi_{E}(t,x)\qquad\text{ for $T_{g}\leq t\leq T$.}

(5.117)

Here, the “
P
-portion”
\psi_{P}\geq 0
solves

\partial_{t}\psi_{P}+\partial_{x}(\psi_{P}^{2}e^{-x})=\partial_{x}^{2}\psi_{P},~~T_{g}\leq t\leq T,

(5.118)

with the initial condition

\psi_{P}(T_{g},x)=\min\{p_{a}(T_{g},x),P(x)\},~~x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(5.119)

The “error part”
\psi_{E}\geq 0
solves

\partial_{t}\psi_{E}+\partial_{x}((2\psi_{P}+\psi_{E})\psi_{E}e^{-x})=\partial_{x}^{2}\psi_{E},~~T_{g}\leq t\leq T,

(5.120)

with the initial condition

\psi_{E}(T_{g},x)=p_{a}(T_{g},x)-\min\{p_{a}(T_{g},x),P(x)\}.

(5.121)

We now estimate
\psi_{P}
and
\psi_{E}
at time
T
.
From (
5.115
) and (
5.119
), it is clear that

\psi_{P}(T_{g},x)\leq P(x),~~\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$},

and both functions solve (
5.118
). Hence, the comparison principle implies
that
\psi_{P}(T,x)\leq P(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Using this and (
5.116
), we find

\sup_{x}\,\psi_{P}(T,x)\leq\sup_{x}\,P(x)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}},

(5.122)

as desired.

On the other hand, (
5.120
) implies that the total mass of

is conserved:

\int\psi_{E}(T,x)dx=\int\psi_{E}(T_{g},x)dx.

(5.123)

We now bound the right hand side. First, by (
5.113
) and (
5.114
),

p_{a}(T_{g},x)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}}\leq P(x),\quad\text{for all }x\geq-\frac{1}{2}\log(T)+\log(2C).

From this and (
5.120
), it follows that

\psi_{E}(T_{g},x)=0,\quad\text{for all }x\geq-\frac{1}{2}\log(T)+\log(2C).

Hence, using (
5.121
) and (
5.56
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int\psi_{E}(T_{g},x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{1}{2}\log(T)+\log(2C)}\psi_{E}(T_{g},x)dx\leq\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{1}{2}\log(T)+\log(2C)}p_{a}(T_{g},x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{-\frac{1}{2}\log(T)+\log(2C)}e^{x}dx=\frac{2C}{\sqrt{T}}.

Invoking (
5.123
), we obtain

\int\psi_{E}(T,x)dx\leq\frac{2C}{\sqrt{T}}.

Recall that
T_{g}\leq 3T/4
and
T
is sufficiently large.
We may, thus, apply parabolic regularity theory (recall that
\psi_{E}
solves (
5.120
)) to conclude that, up to increasing

, we have

\sup\psi_{E}(T,x)\leq C\int\psi_{E}(T,x)dx\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}}.

(5.125)

Combining (
5.117
), (
5.122
), and (
5.125
), finishes the proof
of
Section

5.4
.

First, we establish the dissipation inequality in

Proof of
Section

5.4
.

Following
[
17

, let us write an equation for
h=H(\varphi)
, with a given function
H
, and
not just for the cases
H(\varphi)=\varphi
and

we use here.
Setting

\mathcal{D}_{\rho}h=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\partial_{x}(\rho^{2}\partial_{x}h)-v\partial_{x}h,

(5.126)

we find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h-\mathcal{D}_{\rho}h

\displaystyle=H^{\prime}(\varphi)\varphi_{t}-\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}(\rho^{2}H^{\prime}(\varphi)\varphi_{x})_{x}+vH^{\prime}(\varphi)\varphi_{x}

(5.127)

\displaystyle=H^{\prime}(\varphi)\Big(\frac{q_{t}}{\rho}-\frac{q}{\rho}\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho}\Big)-2\frac{\rho_{x}}{\rho}H^{\prime}(\varphi)\left(\frac{q_{x}}{\rho}-\frac{q}{\rho}\frac{\rho_{x}}{\rho}\right)-H^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)\varphi_{x}^{2}

\displaystyle-H^{\prime}(\varphi)\left(\frac{q_{xx}}{\rho}-2\frac{q_{x}\rho_{x}}{\rho^{2}}-\frac{q}{\rho}\frac{\rho_{xx}}{\rho}+2q\frac{\rho_{x}^{2}}{\rho^{3}}\right)+vH^{\prime}(\varphi)\left(\frac{q_{x}}{\rho}-\frac{q\rho_{x}}{\rho^{2}}\right).

We now use (
5.78
) to obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h-\mathcal{D}_{\rho}h

\displaystyle=-H^{\prime}(\varphi)\frac{q\rho_{t}}{\rho^{2}}-2\frac{\rho_{x}}{\rho}H^{\prime}(\varphi)\Big(\frac{q_{x}}{\rho}-\frac{q}{\rho}\frac{\rho_{x}}{\rho}\Big)-H^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)\varphi_{x}^{2}

(5.128)

\displaystyle+H^{\prime}(\varphi)\Big(2\frac{q_{x}\rho_{x}}{\rho^{2}}+\frac{q}{\rho}\frac{\rho_{xx}}{\rho}-2q\frac{\rho_{x}^{2}}{\rho^{3}}\Big)+vH^{\prime}(\varphi)\Big(-\frac{q\rho_{x}}{\rho^{2}}\Big)-v_{x}H^{\prime}(\varphi)\frac{q}{\rho}

\displaystyle=-H^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)\varphi_{x}^{2}-H^{\prime}(\varphi)\frac{q}{\rho^{2}}(\rho_{t}-\rho_{xx}+(v\rho)_{x}).

When

, this establishes (
5.81
).

Let us now specialize to the case

to prove (
5.82
). Multiplying (

) by

and integrating by parts, we find

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\int\rho\varphi^{2}&=\partial_{t}\int\rho h=\int\rho_{t}h+\int\rho\Big(\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}(\rho^{2}h_{x})_{x}-vh_{x}-2\varphi_{x}^{2}\Big)-2\int h(\rho_{t}-\rho_{xx}+(u\rho)_{x})\\

finishing the proof.
\Box

Next, we prove the analogue of Nash’s inequality. Due to the inhomogeneity, we are not able to use the
standard Fourier-based proof. The proof is more similar to that of Carlen and Loss
[
15
]
, which is
based on the Poincaré inequality. A complication in our setting is that the decay of
r
on the left
makes our space more akin to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
than
\mathbb{R}
; however, we only have a mild “boundary condition” on the
left in that we only know that
\varphi^{2}r
is integrable.

Proof of
Section

5.4
.
Let us first assume that
\varphi
is not only non-negative, bounded, and decays to zero sufficiently fast as
x\to+\infty
but also monotonically decreasing. Fix
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and notice that, due to the assumptions on
r
, we may find
L\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\bar{r}(L)=\theta
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
to be chosen, and write

\begin{split}0&\leq\int_{-\infty}^{L}\Big|\varphi_{x}\sqrt{\overline{\overline{r}}}+\varepsilon\varphi\frac{\overline{r}}{\sqrt{\overline{\overline{r}}}}\Big|^{2}dx=\int_{-\infty}^{L}\Big[|\varphi_{x}|^{2}\overline{\overline{r}}+\varepsilon(\varphi^{2})_{x}\overline{r}+\varepsilon^{2}\varphi^{2}\frac{\overline{r}^{2}}{\overline{\overline{r}}}\Big]dx\\
&=\int_{-\infty}^{L}\Big[|\varphi_{x}|^{2}\overline{\overline{r}}-\varepsilon\varphi^{2}r+\varepsilon^{2}\varphi^{2}\frac{\overline{r}^{2}}{\overline{\overline{r}}}\Big]dx+\varepsilon\overline{r}(L)\varphi^{2}(L).\end{split}

(5.130)

Note that, since
\varphi(x)
is decreasing,

\varphi(L)\overline{r}(L)\leq\int_{-\infty}^{L}\varphi r\,dx\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi r\,dx.

(5.131)

Rearranging (
5.130
) and using (
5.131
), we find

\varepsilon\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi^{2}r\Big(1-\varepsilon\frac{\overline{r}^{2}}{r\overline{\overline{r}}}\Big)\,dx\leq\int_{-\infty}^{L}|\varphi_{x}|^{2}\overline{\overline{r}}\,dx+\frac{\varepsilon}{\overline{r}(L)}\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi r\,dx\Big)^{2}.

(5.132)

Since
r
is increasing, we have

\overline{r}^{2}(x)=2\int_{-\infty}^{x}\bar{r}(y)\bar{r}_{y}(y)\,dy=2\int_{-\infty}^{x}\bar{r}(y)r(y)\,dy\leq 2r(x)\int_{-\infty}^{x}\bar{r}(y)dy=2r(x)\overline{\overline{r}}(x).

(5.133)

Hence, using (
5.133
) and choosing
\varepsilon=1/4
, (
5.132
) becomes

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi^{2}r\,dx

\displaystyle\leq 8\int_{-\infty}^{L}|\varphi_{x}|^{2}\overline{\overline{r}}\,dx+\frac{2}{\overline{r}(L)}\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi r\,dx\Big)^{2}.

(5.134)

Next, using assumption (ii) in (
5.83
), together with monotonicity of
\bar{r}(x)
gives

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi^{2}r\,dx

\displaystyle\leq 8C_{1}\max(1,\bar{r}(L)^{2})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\varphi_{x}|^{2}r\,dx+\frac{2}{\overline{r}(L)}\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi r\,dx\Big)^{2},

(5.135)

proving (
5.85
) for monotonically decreasing functions
\varphi(x)
as
\bar{r}(L)=\theta
.

In order to complete the proof for non-monotonically decreasing non-negative
functions
\varphi(x)
,
we define an analogue of the decreasing rearrangement as follows. Let
m_{r}
be the measure

m_{r}(A)=\int_{A}r\,dx,

(5.136)

and for any measurable set
A
define

A^{*}=\{x:\bar{r}(x)<m_{r}(A)\}.

Note that
A^{*}
is a half line of the form
A^{*}=(-\infty,x^{*}(A))
such that

m_{r}(A)=\bar{r}(x^{*}(A))=m_{r}(A^{*}).

(5.137)

We may then define rearrangements of functions through the “layer cake decomposition”:

\varphi^{*}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\{x:\varphi(x)>t\}^{*}}(t)dt.

As with the usual symmetric decreasing rearrangement, we immediately find that
\varphi^{*}
is
decreasing, the functions
\varphi
and
\varphi^{*}
have super-level sets of equal measure, and, for any
p>0
,

\int|\varphi^{*}(x)|^{p}r(x)dx=\int|\varphi(x)|^{p}r(x)dx.

(5.138)

We claim that an analogue of the Pólya-Szegö inequality holds:

\int|\varphi^{*}_{x}(x)|^{2}r(x)dx\leq\int|\varphi_{x}(x)|^{2}r(x)dx.

(5.139)

Since we have already established (
5.85
) for decreasing functions, the fact that (
5.85
) holds for a general function
\varphi
is an immediate consequence of (
5.138
) and (
5.139
).

We now prove (
5.139
). In principle,
it is an immediate consequence of a more general result in
[
50
]
. We present a simpler proof
in our present one-dimensional setting for the convenience of the reader. We may assume without loss of generality that the function
\varphi(x)
is
positive everywhere, smooth and
takes each value finitely many times.
A useful consequence is that any level set of
\varphi^{*}
has at most one point.

First, we use the coarea formula to rewrite

\int\varphi_{x}(x)^{2}r(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(t)}|\varphi_{x}(x)|r(x)\,dt.

(5.140)

Then, we note that

\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(t)}r(x)\leq\Big(\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(t)}r(x)|\varphi_{x}(x)|\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(t)}\frac{r(x)}{|\varphi_{x}(x)|}\Big)^{1/2},

(5.141)

so that (
5.140
) becomes

\int\varphi_{x}(x)^{2}r(x)\,dx\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\big(\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(t)}r(x)\big)^{2}}{\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(t)}\frac{r(x)}{|\varphi_{x}(x)|}}dt.

(5.142)

Next, we show that both the numerator and denominator in the right hand side of (
5.142
)
may be replaced by their analogues with
\varphi^{*}
in place of
\varphi
, up to an inequality.

First, since

\overline{r}((\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t))=m_{r}(\{\varphi^{*}>t\})=m_{r}(\{\varphi>t\})\leq\overline{r}(\sup\varphi^{-1}(t)),

we conclude that
(\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t)\leq\sup\varphi^{-1}(t)
. Using that
r
is increasing, we conclude that

\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(t)}r(x)\geq r(\sup\varphi^{-1}(t))\geq r((\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t))

(5.143)

On the other hand, by the coarea formula, we have that, for any
t>0

m_{r}(\{x:\varphi(x)>t\})=\int_{\{x:\varphi(x)>t\}}r(x)dx=\int_{t}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(s)}\frac{r(x)}{|\varphi_{x}(x)|}\Big)ds.

An immediate consequence is that

\frac{d}{dt}m_{r}(\{x:\varphi(x)>t\})=-\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(t)}\frac{r(x)}{|\varphi_{x}(x)|}.

The analogous formula for
\varphi^{*}
holds. Since
m_{r}(\{x:\varphi(x)>t\})=m_{r}(\{x:\varphi^{*}(x)>t\})
for all
t
, it follows that

\sum_{x\in\varphi^{-1}(t)}\frac{r(x)}{|\varphi_{x}(x)|}=\sum_{x\in(\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t)}\frac{r(x)}{|\varphi^{*}_{x}(x)|}=\frac{r((\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t))}{\varphi^{*}_{x}((\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t))}.

(5.144)

The last equality above is due to the fact that
(\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t)
is a one point set.

Including (
5.143
) and (
5.144
) in (
5.142
), we conclude that

\int\varphi_{x}(x)^{2}r(x)\,dx\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r((\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t))^{2}}{\frac{r((\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t))}{\varphi^{*}_{x}((\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t))}}dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi^{*}_{x}((\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t))r((\varphi^{*})^{-1}(t))dt.

Reapplying the coarea formula to the rightmost quantity above yields

\int\varphi_{x}(x)^{2}r(x)\,dx\geq\int\varphi^{*}_{x}(x)^{2}r(x)\,dx.

which concludes the proof of (
5.139
) and, thus, of
Section

5.4
.
\Box

5.6
A lower bound on
p(t,x)
in the middle region

We finish this section with a lower bound on
p(t,x)
in an intermediate region that will be useful to us later on.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
u(t,x)
be the solution to the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
) with the initial condition
u(0,x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
, and
p(t,x)
be given by
(
5.11
) and (
5.13
).
There exist
k>0
,
T_{0}>0
and
c_{0}>0
so that we have

p(t,x)\geq\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{t}},~~\hbox{ for all $t\geq T_{0}$ and $\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log t\leq x\leq k\sqrt{t}$.}

(5.145)

Proof.
Let us take
t>0
sufficiently large. We argue by contradiction. For
\varepsilon\in(0,1/10)
and
k>0
to be chosen, suppose there exists

x_{0}\in\big(-\frac{1}{2}\log t,k\sqrt{t}\big)

(5.146)

such that

p(t,x_{0})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{t}},

(5.147)

whence

\widehat{u}(t,x_{0})=e^{-x_{0}}p(t,x_{0})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{t}}e^{-x_{0}}.

(5.148)

We will show that this violates mass conservation (
5.16
):

\int p(t,x)dx=\int p(0,x)dx,

(5.149)

if
\varepsilon
and
k
are chosen sufficiently small.

The first step is to notice that the mass to the left of
(-1/2)\log t
is small.
Indeed, due to the upper bound (
5.19
):

\int_{-\infty}^{-(1/2)\log t}p(t,x)dx\leq\int_{-\infty}^{-(1/2)\log t}e^{x}dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}.

(5.150)

We also know that the mass to the far right is small. Indeed, recall from (
5.35
) that
there exist
T_{0}
and
N_{0}
so that for all
t>T_{0}
and

N>N_{0}
, we have

\int_{N\sqrt{t}}^{\infty}p(t,x)dx\leq Ce^{-N/2}.

(5.151)

To estimate the mass of
p
in the middle region, we split it into two parts.
When
x\in[k\sqrt{t},N\sqrt{t}]
, we use (
5.148
) together with
the steepness estimate in Lemma
5.1.2
to find

\displaystyle p(t,x)

\displaystyle=e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x)\leq e^{x}\Big(1+\Big(\frac{1}{\widehat{u}(t,x_{0})}-1\Big)e^{x-x_{0}}\Big)^{-1}\leq e^{x}\Big(1+\Big(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\varepsilon}e^{x_{0}}-1\Big)e^{x-x_{0}}\Big)^{-1}

(5.152)

\displaystyle\leq e^{x}\Big(1+\frac{\sqrt{t}}{2\varepsilon}e^{x_{0}}e^{x-x_{0}}\Big)^{-1}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{t}},~~\text{for}~~x>x_{0}.

We used (
5.146
) in the second inequality above. It follows that

\int_{k\sqrt{t}}^{N\sqrt{t}}p(t,x)dx\leq N\sqrt{t}\frac{2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{t}}=2\varepsilon N.

(5.153)

On the other hand, when
x\in[-(1/2)\log t,k\sqrt{t}]
, we use
Section

5.1.2
to find

\int_{-(1/2)\log t}^{k\sqrt{t}}p(t,x)dx\leq 2k\sqrt{t}\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}=2kC.

(5.154)

Putting together (
5.150
), (
5.151
), (
5.153
), and (
5.154
) yields

\int p(t,x)dx\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}+Ce^{-N/2}+2\varepsilon N+2kC.

Taking
\varepsilon
and
k
sufficiently small, and
N
sufficiently large
we obtain a contradiction to (
5.149
). The conclusion of Lemma
5.6
follows.
\Box

6
The proof of Theorem
1.1
for
\beta=2

We now prove Theorem
1.1
in the critical case
\beta=2
. As in the case
\beta<2

considered in Section
4
, the proof is based
on the analysis in the self-similar variables, and using the pulled nature of the problem
to show that convergence on the diffusive scales implies convergence to a traveling wave on scales
x\sim O(1)
.
The key difference with the situation for
\beta<2
is that, as we have mentioned previously, the Dirichlet boundary condition at
\eta=0

for the function
v
, introduced by the weighted Hopf-Cole transform (
3.12
), no longer approximately holds in the self-similar
variables. Instead, the function
v
has a positive but a priori unknown limit on the left: see Lemma
6
below.
The bounds in Proposition
5

will be a crucial ingredient in establishing the correct boundary condition in the
self-similar variables.
After we pass to the self-similar variables, the non-zero boundary condition changes the
long time behavior in the self-similar variables. This is an algebraic reason for adjusting the logarithmic shift
(3/2)\log t

in the front position to
(1/2)\log t
. With these bounds in hand, the argument has many similarities
with the case
\beta<2
, so we will omit many of the details, only highlighting the differences.
As before, we will assume without loss of generality
that the initial condition is
u(0,x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
.

The weighted Hopf-Cole transform

Motivated by Proposition
5
, we consider the moving frame:
x\mapsto x-2t+(1/2)\log(t+1)
, and set

\tilde{u}(t,x)=u(t,x+2t-\frac{1}{2}\log(t+1)).

(6.1)

Here,
u(t,x)
is the solution to the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
),
in the non-shifted reference frame. We stress the difference between the function
\widehat{u}(t,x)
defined in (
5.11
) and
used throughout Section
5
, and the function
\tilde{u}(t,x)
used in the present section. The former is

u(t,x)
in the reference frame
x\to x-2t
, while the latter refers to the solution in the
reference frame used in (
6.1
). In particular, the function
p(t,x)
used throughout Section
5
and
\tilde{u}(t,x)
are related by

p(t,x)=e^{x}\widehat{u}(t,x)=e^{x}u(t,x+2t)=e^{x}\tilde{u}\Big(t,x+\frac{1}{2}\log(t+1)\Big).

(6.2)

The function

\tilde{u}(t,x)
satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{t}-2\tilde{u}_{x}+\frac{1}{2(t+1)}\tilde{u}_{x}+2\tilde{u}\tilde{u}_{x}=\tilde{u}_{xx}+\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u}).

(6.3)

We will use the weighted Hopf-Cole transform, as in (
3.12
) with
\beta=2
:

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\exp\Big(x+\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)dy\Big)\tilde{u}(t,x).

(6.4)

This gives

\displaystyle v_{t}-v_{xx}+\frac{1}{2(t+1)}(v_{x}-v)=v\Big(\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u})dy-\tilde{u}\Big).

(6.5)

The key step in the proof will again be to analyze the long time behavior of
v
at scales
O((t+1)^{\gamma})
for
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
, and this will be done through the use of self-similar variables. However, the key difference with the case
\beta<2
is that
v
does not decay to zero as
x\to-\infty
(cf. (
4.13
)). This is quantified in the following:

Lemma 6.1
.

There exist
T_{0}
,
k_{0}
,
b_{0}
,
b_{1}>0
so that

v(t,x)\leq b_{0}<+\infty,~~\hbox{ for all $t\geq T_{0}$ and any $x$},

(6.6)

and

0<b_{1}\leq v(t,x),~~\hbox{ for all $x\leq k_{0}\sqrt{t}$ and $t\geq T_{0}$.}

(6.7)

Proof.
Let us define
\tilde{\mu}(t)
by

\tilde{u}(t,\tilde{\mu}(t))=\frac{1}{2}.

(6.8)

Proposition
5
in terms of
\tilde{\mu}(t)
says that
there exists a constant
K>0
and
T_{0}>0
so that

|\tilde{\mu}(t)|\leq K\qquad\text{ for all }t\geq T_{0}.

(6.9)

Lemma
5.1.2
and (
6.9
) imply that

\tilde{u}(t,x)\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{x-K}},~~\hbox{ for $t\geq T_{0}$ and $x>K$,}

(6.10)

and

\tilde{u}(t,x)\geq\frac{1}{1+e^{x+K}},~~x<-K,~~t\geq T_{0}.

(6.11)

Hence, for
x\leq 0
we can estimate
v(t,x)
from above by

\displaystyle v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(t,x)\exp\Big(x+\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)dy\Big)\leq\exp\Big(\int_{x}^{0}(\tilde{u}(t,y)-1)dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)dy\Big)

(6.12)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)dy\Big)\leq\exp\Big(K+\int_{K}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{1+e^{x-K}}\Big)\leq C,

which is (
6.6
). The proof for
x\geq 0
is simpler and follows directly from (
6.10
).

To obtain (
6.7
), we first use (
6.11
) to write for
x\leq-K
:

\displaystyle v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(t,x)\exp\Big(x+\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)dy\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\Big(\int_{x}^{0}(\tilde{u}(t,y)-1)dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)dy\Big)

(6.13)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\Big(-K-\int_{-\infty}^{-K}\Big(1-\frac{1}{1+e^{x+K}}\Big)dy\Big)\geq C.

In order to extend this lower bound on the right, we recall the lower bound in Lemma
5.6
. Combined with the change of variables
(
6.2
), it
shows that we can find
c_{0}>0
and
k_{0}>0
so that for
0\leq x\leq k_{0}\sqrt{t}
and
t\geq T_{0}
we have

\tilde{u}(t,x)=\sqrt{t+1}e^{-x}p(t,x-\frac{1}{2}\log(t+1))\geq c_{0}e^{-x}.

(6.14)

We deduce that

\displaystyle v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(t,x)\exp\Big(x+\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)dy\Big)\geq c_{0},~~\hbox{ for all $t\geq T_{0}$ and $0\leq x\leq k_{0}\sqrt{t}$.}

(6.15)

Finally, for
x\in(-K,0)
, we see from the monotonicity of
\tilde{u}(t,x)
and (
6.14
) that

\tilde{u}(t,x)\geq c_{0}
, so that

v(t,x)=\tilde{u}(t,x)\exp\Big(x+\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(t,y)dy\Big)\geq c_{0}e^{-K},~~\hbox{ for all $t\geq T_{0}$.}

This proves (
6.7
).
\Box

Analysis in the self-similar variables

We now outline the main ingredients required to establish the long-time behavior of
v
. The thrust of the
argument is similar to the case
\beta<2
, so the outline will be made in reference to the ideas used in Section
4
.

First, apply the familiar self-similar change of variables:

v(t,x)=w\Big(\log(t+1),\frac{x}{\sqrt{t+1}}\Big).

(6.16)

Then, (
6.5
) leads to the evolution equation

\displaystyle w_{\tau}+\mathcal{L}w+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\tau/2}w_{\eta}=e^{\tau}w(\tau,\eta)\Big(\int_{\eta e^{\tau/2}}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u})dy-\tilde{u}\Big),

(6.17)

with the operator
{\cal L}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}w:=-w_{\eta\eta}-\frac{\eta}{2}w_{\eta}-\frac{1}{2}w.

(6.18)

This is different from the operator
L
in (
4.4
) by a multiple of the identity.
Considering
\mathcal{L}
as an operator on
H^{1}(e^{\eta^{2}/4}d\eta;\mathbb{R}_{+})
augmented with Neumann boundary conditions, its spectrum
consists of the eigenvalues
0,1,2,\dots
with (unnormalized) principal eigenfunction

\psi_{0}(\eta)=e^{-\eta^{2}/4}.

Importantly, Lemma
3
shows that the right side of (
6.17
) is negative:

\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u})dy-\tilde{u}\leq 0~~\hbox{ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$.}

(6.19)

Recall the major ingredients in establishing the long-time dynamics in self-similar variables when
\beta<2
:
(1) a super-solution solving a tractable equation; (2) a sub-solution solving a (potentially different) tractable equation;
(3) smallness of the right hand side when
\eta\gg e^{-\tau/2}
; (4) an approximate boundary condition when
\eta\ll-e^{-\tau/2}
.
We now check that analogous ingredients are available in this case.

First, we find a super-solution. This follows directly from
Section

3
. Indeed, as a result, we have that any solution to

\displaystyle\bar{w}_{\tau}+\mathcal{L}\bar{w}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\tau/2}\bar{w}_{\eta}=0,~~\tau>0,~~\eta>-e^{-(1/2-\gamma)\tau},

(6.20)

augmented with appropriate boundary conditions on
\bar{w}_{\eta}(\tau,-e^{-(1/2-\gamma)\tau})
(see the fourth point, below), is a super-solution to (
6.17
).

Second, we see that the solution
\underline{w}
to

\underline{w}_{\tau}+\mathcal{L}\underline{w}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\tau/2}\underline{w}_{\eta}=-e^{\tau}\underline{w}\tilde{u},~~\tau>0,~~\eta>e^{-(1/2-\gamma)\tau},

(6.21)

is a sub-solution to (
6.17
), up to stating an appropriate boundary condition that we do now. Due to the fact that
\tilde{u}
is steeper than
\phi_{2}
, (
3.24
), (
3.15
), and
Section

3
, notice that

w_{\eta}(\tau,\eta)=(\tilde{u}_{x}+\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u}))\exp\Big\{\tau/2+x+\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}dy\Big\}\leq 0.

(6.22)

Hence, we may take Neumann boundary conditions for
\underline{w}_{\eta}
:

\underline{w}_{\eta}(\tau,e^{-(1/2-\gamma)\tau})=0

(6.23)

and we are guaranteed that, with ordered initial data,
\underline{w}\leq w
.

Third, we establish the smallness of the right hand side of (
6.17
) whenever
\eta\geq e^{-(1/2-\gamma)\tau}
. This follows directly from (
6.19
),
Section

6
, and (
6.10
):

\begin{split}0&\geq e^{\tau}\Big(\int_{\eta e^{\tau/2}}^{\infty}\tilde{u}(1-\tilde{u})dy-\tilde{u}(e^{\tau},\eta e^{\tau/2})\Big)w(\tau,\eta)\\
&\geq-e^{\tau}\tilde{u}(e^{\tau},\eta e^{\tau/2})w(\tau,\eta)\geq-Ce^{\tau-\eta e^{\tau/2}}\geq-Ce^{\tau-e^{\gamma\tau}}.\end{split}

(6.24)

Hence, the right hand side of (
6.17
) is double exponentially small.

Finally, we address the approximate boundary conditions of
\omega
at
\eta=-e^{-(1/2-\gamma)\tau}
. Keeping
Section

6
in mind, an approximate Dirichlet boundary condition is not possible. This is, of course, part of the reason for the different shift when
\beta=2
. Instead, we have an approximate Neumann boundary condition. First notice that, due to (
6.11
), we have that

|1-\tilde{u}(e^{\tau},-e^{\gamma\tau})|\leq Ce^{-e^{\gamma\tau}}.

(6.25)

By parabolic regularity theory, we find

|\tilde{u}_{x}(e^{\tau},-e^{\gamma\tau})|\leq Ce^{-e^{\gamma\tau}}.

(6.26)

Using (
6.25
) and (
6.26
) in (
6.22
), we find

|w_{\eta}(\tau,-e^{-(1/2-\gamma)\tau})|\leq Ce^{\tau-e^{\gamma\tau}}.

(6.27)

Hence,
w
satisfies an approximate Neumann boundary condition with double exponentially small error.

Thus, the main ingredients to an analogous argument as in the case
\beta<2
are in place, with the only major difference being the change from
approximate Dirichlet boundary conditions to approximate Neumann boundary conditions. The change in boundary conditions changes the principal
eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}
and suggests that the long-time behavior of
w
should look like
\alpha_{\infty}e^{-\eta^{2}/4}
for
\eta\geq 0
.
This is confirmed by the following key estimate, which is the analogue of
Section

4
. As the proof follows from similar arguments as in
Section

4
,
using the four ingredients above and our knowledge of the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
, we omit the details.

Lemma 6.2
.

Given
w
solving (
6.17
) with initial conditions
w(0,\eta)=\mathds{1}(\eta\leq 0)
, there exists a constant
\alpha_{\infty}>0
and a function
R_{\gamma}
such that

w(\tau,\eta)=\alpha_{\infty}e^{-\eta^{2}/4}+R_{\gamma}(\tau,\eta)e^{-\eta^{2}/6}\qquad\text{ for }\eta\geq-e^{-(1/2-\gamma)\tau},

(6.28)

for any
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
and

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}|R_{\gamma}(\tau,\eta)|=0\qquad\text{ uniformly for $\eta\geq-e^{-(1/2-\gamma)\tau}$}.

(6.29)

Before proceeding, we note that the role of
Section

6
in the proof of
Section

6
is to guarantee the positivity of
\alpha_{\infty}
and the boundedness of
w
. From
Section

6
, we immediately obtain the long-time behavior of
\tilde{u}
at scales between
O(1)
and
O(\sqrt{t})
.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
, fix
\varepsilon>0
, and set
x_{\gamma}=(t+1)^{\gamma}
. There exist
\alpha_{\infty}>0
and
T_{\varepsilon,\gamma}
, so that

\displaystyle|\tilde{u}(t,x_{\gamma})

(6.30)

Convergence to a single wave: the proof of Theorem
1.1
for
\beta=2

With
Section

6
and
Section

6

\beta<2
in

4
. The main reason being that we cannot verify that an analogously defined

(cf. (

As in (

\begin{split}\tilde{u}(t,x)&=v(t,x)\exp\Big\{-x-\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}dy\Big\}\leq(\alpha_{\infty}+\varepsilon)\exp\Big\{-x-\int_{x}^{\infty}\tilde{u}dy\Big\}\\

]
for the
convergence to a traveling wave in the pushed front regime.
Let us recall that for all
\beta>0
there is a traveling wave solution to the Burgers-FKPP equation
(
1.1
) of the form

\phi_{\beta}(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{{\beta x}/{2}}}

(7.1)

that moves with the speed

c_{*}(\beta)=\frac{\beta}{2}+\frac{2}{\beta}\geq 2.

(7.2)

A key point is that for
\beta\geq 2
, the speed
c_{*}(\beta)
given by (
7.2
)
also happens to be the minimal front
speed. In other words, for
\beta\geq 2
the minimal speed wave profile is explicit and
given by (
7.1
). Another property that will be crucial for the analysis is that
for
\beta>2
we have

\phi_{\beta}(x)e^{c_{*}x/2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

(7.3)

According to the criterion of
[
24
]
, this puts the traveling front (
7.1
) into the category of pushed fronts, unlike the
fronts for
\beta\leq 2
, for which (
7.3
) does not hold.
Accordingly, the proof of the large time
convergence of the solutions to the initial value problem for (
1.1
)
with
\beta>2
consists of three steps that are common in such results for pushed fronts:
compactness, local stability and quasi-convergence.

Compactness

As the first step, we show that the solution
u(t,x)
to (
1.1
) with

\beta>2
can be trapped between an explicitly constructed
super-solution
\bar{u}
and a sub-solution
\underline{u}
.
Each of them will converge to a separate shift of the traveling front, exponentially fast in time.
Here, we follow the construction in
[
47
]
. We denote by
c_{*}=c_{*}(\beta)
, as given by (
7.2
),
and drop the subscript
\beta
in the notation
for the traveling wave profile
\phi_{\beta}(x)
given by (
7.1
).
Consider the
moving frame
z=x-c_{*}t
, setting

\widehat{u}(t,z)=u(t,z+c_{*}t).

(7.4)

This function satisfies

\displaystyle{\cal M}[\widehat{u}]:=\widehat{u}_{t}-c_{*}\widehat{u}_{x}+\beta\widehat{u}\widehat{u}_{x}-\widehat{u}_{xx}-\widehat{u}(1-\widehat{u})=0,

(7.5)

with the initial condition
\widehat{u}(0,z)=u_{\rm in}(z)
. The sub- and super-solutions
in the moving frame are described by the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

Fix
\lambda\in(2/\beta,\beta/2)
and
\underline{z}_{0},\overline{z}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. For
\underline{q}_{0},\overline{q}_{0},\mu>0
, let

\begin{split}&\underline{v}(t,z):=\phi(z+\underline{\xi}(t))-\underline{q}(t,z+\underline{z}_{0})\qquad\text{and}\\
&\overline{v}(t,z):=\phi(z-\overline{\xi}(t))+\overline{q}(t,z-\overline{z}_{0})\qquad\text{ for all }t>0,z\in\mathbb{R}\end{split}

(7.6)

with

\underline{q}(t,z)=\underline{q}_{0}e^{-\mu t}\min\{\exp\big(-\lambda z\big),1\},\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{q}(t,z)=\overline{q}_{0}e^{-\mu t}\min\{\exp\big(-\lambda z\big),1\},

and, for
K>0
,

\underline{\xi}^{\prime}(t)=K\underline{q}_{0}e^{-\mu t}\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\xi}^{\prime}(t)=K\overline{q}_{0}e^{-\mu t}.

(7.7)

Suppose, for some
D_{0}>0
,

\frac{K\underline{q}_{0}}{\mu},~~\frac{K\overline{q}_{0}}{\mu},~~|\underline{\xi}(0)+\underline{z}_{0}|~~|\overline{\xi}(0)+\overline{z}_{0}|\leq D_{0}.

(7.8)

Then, if
\underline{q}_{0},\overline{q}_{0}
and
\mu
are sufficiently small and
K
is sufficiently large, all depending only on
\beta
,
\lambda
, and
D_{0}
, then
\underline{v}
and
\overline{v}

are sub- and super-solutions of (
7.5
), respectively.

Proof.
We only prove the claim for
\underline{v}
. We drop the subscripts of
q_{1,2}
and
\xi_{1,2}
and superscripts of
q_{0}^{(1,2)}
, and
z_{0}^{(1,2)}
in order
to simplify
the notation.
Let us insert the ansatz for
\underline{v}(t,z)
given by the left side of (
7.6
) into the desired inequality

{\cal M}[\underline{v}]\leq 0,

(7.9)

that needs to hold for
\underline{v}(t,x)
to be a sub-solution to (
1.1
).
This gives

\displaystyle{\cal M}[\underline{v}]

\displaystyle=\xi^{\prime}(t)\phi^{\prime}-q_{t}+\beta(\phi-q)(\phi^{\prime}-q_{z})-c_{*}(\phi^{\prime}-q_{z})-\phi^{\prime\prime}+q_{zz}-F(\phi-q)

(7.10)

\displaystyle=\xi^{\prime}(t)\phi^{\prime}-q_{t}+c_{*}q_{z}-\beta\phi q_{z}-\beta q\phi^{\prime}+\beta qq_{z}+q_{zz}+F(\phi)-F(\phi-q).

Here, we have set

F(u)=u(1-u).

We fix a sufficiently small
\delta>0
, and consider (
7.10
) in three regions of
z\in{\mathbb{R}}

separately.

The far right region:

R=\{z:~\phi(z-\xi(t))\leq\delta\}.

(7.11)

In other words
z\geq\xi(t)+\phi^{-1}(\delta)
. Thus,

z+z_{0}\geq\phi^{-1}(\delta)+\xi(t)+z_{0}.

(7.12)

By (
7.7
)-(
7.8
),

|\xi(t)+z_{0}|\leq 2D_{0}.

(7.13)

Hence, by choosing
\delta>0
sufficiently small depending only on
D_{0}
, we have

z+z_{0}\geq 0,

(7.14)

so that

q(t,z+z_{0})=q_{0}\exp\{-\mu t-\lambda(z-z_{0})\},~~\hbox{ for all $z\in R$}.

(7.15)

It follows that

q_{t}=-\mu q,~~q_{z}=-\lambda q,~~q_{zz}=\lambda^{2}q,~~\hbox{ for all $z\in R$}.

As
\xi^{\prime}(t)>0
,
\phi^{\prime}<0
, and
\phi(t,x)<\delta
for
all
x\in R
, we deduce that

\displaystyle{\cal M}[\underline{v}]

\displaystyle=\xi^{\prime}(t)\phi^{\prime}+(\mu-c_{*}\lambda+\lambda^{2}+\beta\lambda\phi-\beta\phi^{\prime})q+F(\phi)-F(\phi-q)-\lambda\beta q^{2}

(7.16)

\displaystyle\leq(\mu-c_{*}\lambda+\lambda^{2}+\beta\delta\lambda-\beta\phi^{\prime})q+F(\phi)-F(\phi-q).

Notice that the traveling wave satisfies

\displaystyle c_{*}\phi-\frac{\beta}{2}\phi^{2}=-\phi^{\prime}+\int_{x}^{\infty}\phi(1-\phi)dy,

(7.17)

we see that

\phi^{\prime}=-c_{*}\phi+\frac{\beta}{2}\phi^{2}+\int_{x}^{\infty}\phi(1-\phi)dy\geq-c_{*}\phi~~~\hbox{ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$}.

(7.18)

In addition, we have

F(\phi)-F(\phi-q)=\phi-\phi^{2}-\phi+q+(\phi-q)^{2}=q-2\phi q+q^{2}\leq(1+q_{0})q.

(7.19)

Using these inequalities, together with (
7.11
), in (
7.16
), gives

\displaystyle{\cal M}[\underline{v}]

\displaystyle\leq(\lambda^{2}-c_{*}\lambda+\beta\delta\lambda+\mu+1+q_{0}+c_{*}\beta\delta)q.

(7.20)

Due to the assumption on
\lambda
, we have
\lambda^{2}-c_{*}\lambda+1<0
.
Then take
\delta>0
,
\mu>0
and
q_{0}>0
all sufficiently small, depending on
\lambda
, so that

{\cal M}[\underline{v}]\leq 0.

(7.21)

We stress that here we use the assumption that
\beta>2
, which makes
c_{*}>2
.

The middle region:

M=\{z:~\delta\leq\phi(z-\xi(t))\leq 1-\delta\}.

(7.22)

There exists
\alpha_{\delta}>0
, which depends on
\delta>0
, so that for all
z
in the middle region we have

\phi^{\prime}(z-\xi(t))\leq-\alpha_{\delta}.

(7.23)

We need to consider separately the points where
z\geq z_{0}
, or
z<z_{0}
, as this changes the definition of
q(t,z)
.
If
z>z_{0}
, then
q
satisfies (
7.15
), and we use (
7.23
) to obtain, using positivity
of
\xi^{\prime}(t)
once again,

\displaystyle{\cal M}[\underline{v}]

\displaystyle=\xi^{\prime}(t)\phi^{\prime}-q_{t}+c_{*}q_{z}-\beta\phi q_{z}-\beta q\phi^{\prime}+\beta qq_{z}+q_{zz}+F(\phi)-F(\phi-q)

(7.24)

\displaystyle\leq-\alpha_{\delta}\xi^{\prime}(t)+(\mu+\lambda^{2}-c_{*}\lambda+\beta\phi\lambda+c_{*}\beta\phi)q+(1+q)q-\lambda\beta q^{2}\leq-\alpha\xi^{\prime}(t)+Cq_{0}e^{-\mu t}.

Above we used that
F(\phi)-F(\phi-q)\leq q(1+q)
. Due to the assumption on
\lambda
,
\lambda\beta>1
, which implies that
q^{2}-\lambda\beta q^{2}\leq 0
. It follows that
C
does not depend on
q_{0}
.

On the other hand, if
z\leq z_{0}
, then

q_{t}=-\mu q,~~q_{z}=0,

(7.25)

and, as long as we choose
q_{0}<\delta
, we get, after another appplication of (
7.18
):

\begin{split}{\cal M}[\underline{v}]\leq\xi^{\prime}(t)\phi^{\prime}+(\mu+c_{*}\beta\phi)q+F(\phi)-F(\phi-q)&\leq-\alpha_{\delta}\xi^{\prime}(t)+(\mu+c_{*}\beta+1)q\\
&\leq-\alpha_{\delta}\xi^{\prime}(t)+Cq_{0}e^{-\mu t}.\end{split}

(7.26)

It follows that
M[\underline{v}]\leq 0
both in (
7.24
) and (
7.26
) since, by (
7.7
)

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}(t)=Kq_{0}e^{-\mu t}\geq\frac{C}{\alpha_{\delta}}q_{0}e^{-\mu t},

(7.27)

after possibly increasing
K
depending only on
\delta
, which, in turn, depends on
D_{0}
and
\lambda
.

The far left region:

L=\{z:~\phi(z-\xi(t))\geq 1-\delta\}.

(7.28)

Arguing as in the far right region setting, we see that, up to further decreasing
\delta
depending on
D_{0}
, we have
z+z_{0}\leq 0
.
Therefore,
q(t)
satisfies (
7.25
), and we have

\displaystyle{\cal M}[\underline{v}]

\displaystyle=\xi^{\prime}(t)\phi^{\prime}-q_{t}+c_{*}q_{z}-\beta\phi q_{z}-\beta q\phi^{\prime}+\beta qq_{z}+q_{zz}+F(\phi)-F(\phi-q)

(7.29)

\displaystyle=\xi^{\prime}(t)\phi^{\prime}+(\mu-\beta\phi^{\prime})q+F(\phi)-F(\phi-q).

The explicit form (
7.1
) of the profile
\phi(x)
as
x\to-\infty
implies that in the far left region we have

-\frac{\beta}{2}\delta\leq\phi^{\prime}<0.

(7.30)

As we also have
F^{\prime}(1)=-1
, we can assure that

\displaystyle M[\underline{v}]\leq\Big(\mu+\frac{\beta^{2}\delta}{2}\Big)q+F(\phi)-F(\phi-q)\leq 0,

(7.31)

as long as we choose
\mu>0
and
\delta>0
sufficiently small.

Thus, in all cases, we have show that
M[\underline{v}]\leq 0
, which finishes the proof of Lemma
7
.
\Box

Local stability

Lemma
7
implies a certain compactness for the solution
v(t,z)
to (
7.5
)
that eventually will lead to the nonlinear stability result. To formulate it, we need to introduce the
weighted Banach space

B_{\lambda}=\{u\in C(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}):\|u\|_{\lambda}<+\infty\},

with the norm

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\lambda}=\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{|v(z)|}{\eta(z)}\qquad\text{ where }\eta(z)=\min\{1,\exp(-\lambda z)\}.

(7.32)

We also introduce the norm

\|v\|_{\lambda,1}=\|v\|_{\lambda}+\|v_{z}\|_{\lambda}

(7.33)

for a later usage.
Let us define the
\omega
-limit set of
u_{\rm in}\in B_{\lambda}
with respect to the evolution (
7.5
) as

\displaystyle\omega(u_{\rm in}):=\{\psi\in B_{\lambda}\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R}):~~\exists~t_{n}\to+\infty~\text{ s.t }~v(t_{n},\cdot)\to\psi(\cdot)\text{ in }C_{loc}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\}.

By the standard parabolic regularity theory (see, e.g.,
[
35
, Chapter IV]
), we know that the “orbit”
\{v(t,\cdot):t\geq 1\}
is relatively compact in
C^{2}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}})
.
Thus, the set
\omega(u_{\rm in})
is nonempty for a given initial condition
u_{\rm in}\in B_{\lambda}
.
Proposition
2
implies that each element of
\omega(u_{\rm in})
is either a constant

0
or
1
, or a shift of the
traveling wave
\phi(x)
. Indeed,
v
is simply
u
in the moving frame
c_{*}t
. Hence, if
m_{\beta}(t_{n})-c_{*}t_{n}\to\infty
, then
Section

2
implies that
v(t_{n},\cdot)\to 1
as the front
m_{\beta}(t_{n})
is “far ahead.” Similarly, if
m_{\beta}(t_{n})-c_{*}t_{n}\to-\infty
, then
v(t_{n},\cdot)\to 0
. Finally, if
m_{\beta}(t_{n})-c_{*}t_{n}
converges to a constant, then
Section

2
implies that
v(t_{n},\cdot)
converges to (a shift of) the traveling wave.

Lemma
7
rules out the possibility of convergence to a constant.
Our goal is to prove that it consists of exactly one element, and that the solution
converges exponentially fast to that particular shift of a traveling wave.

Lemma
7
implies the following local stability result.

Lemma 7.2
.

Fix
\lambda\in(2/\beta,\beta/2)
. For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\gamma>0
such that whenever

\|v(0,\cdot)-\phi(\cdot-s_{0})\|_{\lambda}<\gamma,

(7.34)

we have

\|v(t,\cdot)-\phi(\cdot-s_{0})\|_{\lambda}<\varepsilon~~\hbox{ for all $t\geq 0$.}

(7.35)

Proof.
Let us assume that
s_{0}=0
. We see from (
7.34
) that

\phi(z)-\gamma\eta(z)<v(0,z)<\phi(z)+\gamma\eta(z)~~\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{R}.

(7.36)

This allows us to apply Lemma
7
with
\xi(t)
satisfying (
7.27
) and
\xi(0)=0
:

\xi(t)=\frac{K\gamma}{\mu}\Big(1-e^{-\mu t}\Big),

leading to

\displaystyle\phi(z-\xi(t))-\gamma\eta(z)<v(t,z)<\phi(z+\xi(t))+\gamma\eta(z),~~t\geq 0,~z\in\mathbb{R}.

Because

\phi(z)\sim e^{-{\beta z}/{2}}~~~\hbox{ as $x\to+\infty$,}

and
\lambda<{\beta}/{2}
, there exists a constant
K_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\|\phi(\cdot)-\phi(\cdot\pm\xi(t))\|_{\lambda}\leq K_{1}\xi(t)\leq K_{1}\gamma,~~t\geq 0,

finishing the proof.
\Box

Quasi-convergence and exponential stability

Now, convergence in shape in Proposition
2
and the steepness comparison in
Section

1
, together with Lemma
7

imply the following quasi-convergence property.

Corollary 7.3
.

Fix
\lambda\in(2/\beta,\beta/2)
. Let
v(t,x)
be the solution to (
7.5
) with the initial condition
v_{0}(x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
.
Then there exists a sequence
t_{n}\to+\infty
and
s_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
so that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\|v(t_{n},\cdot)-\phi(\cdot-s_{0})\|_{\lambda}=0.

(7.37)

To improve the result of Corollary
7
to the exponential convergence, we will use the
method of
[
48
,
49
]
,
which consider convergence to traveling waves for equations of the form

\displaystyle u_{t}=u_{xx}+f(u,u_{x}).

In the Burgers-KPP case, we have

f(u,u_{x})=-\beta uu_{x}+u(1-u).

If one looks for solutions that are perturbations of a traveling wave, that is

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\phi(x-c_{*}t)+w(t,x),

then in the moving coordinate
z=x-c_{*}t
, the equation of
w
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}={\cal L}w+R(w).

Here, the linearized operator is

\displaystyle{\cal L}w=w_{zz}+c_{*}w_{z}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi^{\prime}}w_{z}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}w=w_{zz}+(c_{*}-\beta\phi)w_{z}+(-\beta\phi^{\prime}+1-2\phi)w.

(7.38)

The remainder
R
is a nonlinear operator whose Fréchet derivative vanishes at
w=0
.

The main result of
[
49
]
is that a local stability result, as we have
in Corollary
7
, implies exponential convergence to a traveling wave,
as long as the operator
{\cal L}
satisfies certain spectral assumptions
that we will now recall and specify to the Burgers-FKPP equation.
Let us put the linearized operator in the from

\displaystyle{\cal L}w=w^{\prime\prime}-2bw^{\prime}+qw,~~b=\frac{1}{2}(\beta\phi-c_{*}),~~q=-\beta\phi^{\prime}+1-2\phi,

(7.39)

and set

B(z)=\int_{0}^{z}b(s)ds.

To remove the drift term in the operator
{\cal L}
in (
7.39
),
Sattinger introduces the operator

\widetilde{\cal M}=e^{-B}{\cal L}e^{B},

so that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\cal M}w=w^{\prime\prime}+pw,~~p=b^{\prime}-b^{2}+q.

(7.40)

In our case, with
\phi(x)
given explicitly by (
7.1
), and
c_{*}
by (
7.2
), we have the left and right limits

\displaystyle b_{+}=\lim_{x\to+\infty}b(x)=-\frac{c_{*}}{2}=-\frac{\beta}{4}-\frac{1}{\beta},\qquad b_{-}=\lim_{x\to-\infty}b(x)=\frac{1}{2}(\beta-c_{*})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{2}{\beta}\Big),

\displaystyle q_{\pm}=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}q(x)=\pm 1.

We see that

p_{\pm}=\lim_{z\to\pm\infty}p(z)

are given by

p_{+}=-b_{+}^{2}+q_{+}=-\Big(\frac{\beta}{4}+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)^{2}+1=-\Big(\frac{\beta}{4}-\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)^{2}<0,

as
\beta>2
, and

p_{-}=-b_{-}^{2}+q_{-}=-\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{\beta}{4}\Big)^{2}-1<0.

As both
p_{+}<0
and
p_{-}<0
, the operator
\widetilde{\cal M}
is stable both as
x\to+\infty
and
x\to-\infty
,
and Theorems 1 and 2 in
[
49
]
imply that convergence
in Corollary
7
is actually exponential in time.

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
v(t,x)
be the solution to (
7.5
) with the initial condition
v_{0}(x)=\mathbbm{1}(x\leq 0)
.
There exist
C>0
and
\omega_{0}>0
, as well as
s_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
so that

\|v(t,\cdot)-\phi(\cdot-s_{0})\|_{\lambda}\leq Ce^{-\omega_{0}t}.

(7.41)

Using that
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\lambda}
, this completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
for
\beta>2
.

8
An informal derivation of the higher corrections

We explain in this section how the non-rigorous but extremely interesting
methodology of
[
8
]
can be used to predict the higher
order corrections to the logarithmic shift in the front position. This strategy was applied in
[
8
]

for the classical Fisher-KPP equation, and the first two extra
terms in the expansion were rigorously confirmed in
[
27
,
45
]
,
leading to the long-time front position asymptotics

\displaystyle m(t)=2t-\frac{3}{2}\log(t+1)+x_{\infty}-\frac{3\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{t+1}}+\frac{9}{8}(5-6\log 2)\frac{\log(t+1)}{t+1}+o\Big(\frac{\log t}{t}\Big),

(8.1)

a significant refinement of Theorem
1.1
.
The terms that appear in (
8.1
), except for
x_{\infty}
, do not depend on the initial conditions.
Moreover, they
are expected to be universal for a large class of Fisher-KPP type problems. That is, this expansion
has been shown to hold, with exactly the same coefficients,
for all equations of the form

u_{t}=u_{xx}+f(u),

(8.2)

with a nonlinearity
f(u)
of the Fisher-KPP type, normalized so that
f^{\prime}(0)=1
as long as
f\in C^{1,\delta}
near
0
(see
[
9
]
for the treatment of the less regular case).

An analysis similar to what we do in this section for
\beta=2
would show that (
8.1
) holds
with
exactly
the same coefficients for the Burgers-FKPP equation (
1.1
)
for all
\beta<2
, confirming the universality prediction of
[
8
]
. We omit the details.
The goal of this section is to show that when
\beta=2
this expansion changes the coefficients
but not the form of the individual terms to

\displaystyle m(t)=2t-\frac{1}{2}\log(t+1)-x_{\infty}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{t+1}}+\frac{(1-\log 2)}{4}\frac{\log(t+1)}{t+1}+o\Big(\frac{\log t}{t}\Big).

(8.3)

It is tempting to conjecture
that the expansion (
8.3
) is also universal for problems that combine the pulled nature with traveling waves that decay as
e^{-x}
rather
than the Fisher-KPP asymptotics
xe^{-x}
. This regime is referred to as Case (II) in Chapter 2 of

[
38
]
for reaction-diffusion equations of the form (
8.2
).

We begin with the following observation, inspired by
[
8
]
, that we state for all
\beta\leq 2
. Let
u(t,x)
be the solution
to (
1.1
) with an initial condition
u_{\rm in}(x)
and set

\displaystyle\varphi(t,r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}(t,z)e^{rz}dz,~~\Phi_{1}(r)=(1-r\beta/2)\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(t,r)e^{-(r^{2}+1)t}dt,

(8.4)

and

\displaystyle\Phi(r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{\rm in}(x)e^{rx}dx.

(8.5)

Note that the function
\Phi(r)
is smooth for all
r>0
, as long as the initial condition
u_{\rm in}(x)
is compactly supported on the right.
On the other hand, as we will see below, the function
\Phi_{1}(r)
may potentially blow up as
r\to 1^{-}
. This possibility is removed by the
following identity.

Proposition 8.1
.

Assume that the initial condition
u_{\rm in}(x)
for (
1.1
) satisfies

u_{\rm in}(x)\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{x-L_{0}}},~~\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$},

(8.6)

with some
L_{0}>0
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(r)=\Phi_{1}(r),~~\hbox{for all~$\beta\leq 2$ and $r\in(0,1)$}.

(8.7)

Proof.
The proof is a modification of the argument in
[
8
]
. Without loss of generality we assume that
L_{0}=0
.
First, we recall that, as we have shown in (
4.9
)-(
4.10
), for all
\beta\leq 2
, we have the upper bound

u(t,x)\leq\bar{u}(t,x):=\frac{1}{1+e^{x-2t}},~~\hbox{ for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$}.

(8.8)

It follows that
\varphi(t,r)
is defined and differentiable in
r
for all
r<2
.
This also shows that for all
r\in(0,1)
we have

g(t,r):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,x)e^{rx}dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{rx}}{1+e^{x-2t}}dx=I_{0}(r)e^{2rt},

(8.9)

with

I_{0}(r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{rx}}{1+e^{x}}dx<\infty,

since
r\in(0,1)
. Next,
differentiating
g(t,r)
in
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle g_{t}(t,r)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{t}(t,x)e^{rx}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(-\frac{\beta}{2}(u^{2})_{x}+u_{xx}+u-u^{2}\Big)e^{rx}dx

(8.10)

\displaystyle=(1+r^{2})g(t,r)-(1-r\beta/2)\varphi(t,r).

Integrating this identity from
0
to
t
and using the definition of
\Phi(r)
, we see that

\displaystyle g(t,r)e^{-(1+r^{2})t}=\Phi(r)-(1-r\beta/2)\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(s,r)e^{-(1+r^{2})s}ds.

As
2r<1+r^{2}
, we may use (
8.9
) to pass to the limit
t\to+\infty
and obtain (
8.7
).
\Box

An immediate consequence of Proposition
8
is that the function
\Phi_{1}(r)
remains regular
and even infinitely differentiable, with bounded derivatives as
r\to 1^{-}
. A surprising discovery of
[
8
]

is that for the classical Fisher-KPP equation one can perform a careful analysis of that limit in terms of the
front location
m(t)
and this regularity alone
can be used to obtain the asymptotics (
8.1
) of
m(t)
as
t\to+\infty
.
As noted above, this approach can be applied nearly verbatim for
\beta<2
, so we only consider
\beta=2
below.

Assumptions on the rate of convergence

Let us now formalize the assumptions that go into the derivation of (
8.1
) and
(
8.3
).
We know from Proposition
2
that there is a reference frame
m(t)
such that

u(t,x+m(t))\to\phi_{2}(x),

(8.11)

and from Theorem
1.1
that, when
\beta=2
:

m(t)=2t-\gamma(t),~~\gamma(t)=a\log(t+1)-\alpha(t),~~\alpha(t)=x_{\infty}+o(1)~~\hbox{as $t\to+\infty$}.

(8.12)

Of course, we already know that
a=1/2
when
\beta=2
but we leave this coefficient undetermined for now, to show how this value can be discovered
by the arguments below.
One assumption of
[
8
]
and
later proved in
[
27
]
, is that the analogue of (
8.11
) with
\beta=0

holds at the rate
O(1/t)
.
For the Burgers-FKPP equation this translates into the assumption that

u(t,x+m(t))=\phi_{2}(x)+\frac{1}{t}\eta(t,x),~~\hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$,}

(8.13)

with a rapidly decaying in space (but not necessarily in time) function
\eta(t,x)
. A result of
[
27
]
for
\beta=0
is that
\eta(t,x)

has a positive limit as
t\to+\infty
, and the rate
O(t^{-1})
cannot be improved. We stress that (
8.13
) is an assumption and
not a rigorous claim, even though we believe that it holds, as it does for the classical Fisher-KPP equation.

Let us now re-write the function
\Phi_{1}(r)
in terms of
m(t)
. We introduce

\displaystyle\varphi_{m}(t,r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,z+m(t))^{2}e^{rz}dz=e^{-rm(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,z)^{2}e^{rz}dz=e^{-rm(t)}\varphi(t,r),

(8.14)

and write

\Phi_{1}(r)=(1-r)\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{m}(t,r)e^{rm(t)-(r^{2}+1)t}dt.

(8.15)

Given the exponential decay of
u(t,x)
and
\phi_{2}(x)
, and assumption (
8.13
), we deduce that there exists a function
\tilde{\eta}(t,r)
, so that

\varphi_{m}(t,r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t,z+m(t))^{2}e^{rz}dz=\tilde{\varphi}(r)+\frac{1}{t+1}\tilde{\eta}(t,r),

(8.16)

with

\tilde{\varphi}(r):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{2}(z)^{2}e^{rz}dz,

(8.17)

and

|\tilde{\eta}(t,r)|\leq K,~~\hbox{ for all $t>0$ and $r\in(1/2,3/2)$. }

(8.18)

Inserting (
8.16
) into (
8.15
) gives

\Phi_{1}(r)=(1-r)\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\tilde{\varphi}(r)+\frac{1}{t+1}\tilde{\eta}(t,r)\Big)e^{rm(t)-(r^{2}+1)t}dt.

(8.19)

Higher order corrections from the limit
r\to 1^{-}

We now pass to the limit
r\to 1^{-}
in (
8.19
) and use the regularity of
\Phi_{1}(r)
in this limit, implied by Proposition
8
,
to get extra terms in the asymptotic expansion for
m(t)
as
t\to+\infty
.
We take
r=1-\varepsilon
in (
8.19
), with
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and use expression
(
8.12
) for
m(t)
:

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(1-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\tilde{\varphi}(1-\varepsilon)+\frac{1}{t+1}\tilde{\eta}(t,1-\varepsilon)\Big)e^{(1-\varepsilon)(2t-\gamma(t))-((1-\varepsilon)^{2}+1)t}dt

(8.20)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\varphi}(1-\varepsilon)I(\varepsilon)+E(\varepsilon).

Here, the main term is

I(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(1-\varepsilon)\gamma(t)-\varepsilon^{2}t}dt,

(8.21)

and

E(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t+1}\tilde{\eta}(t,1-\varepsilon)e^{-(1-\varepsilon)\gamma(t)-\varepsilon^{2}t}dt

(8.22)

is the error term.

In order to avoid additional technicalities in an argument that is not rigorous (because assumption (
8.13
)
has not been justified), we use (
8.13
) to replace the rigorous claim that
\Phi_{1}(1-\varepsilon)
remains regular as
\varepsilon\to 0
by the
assumption that

I(\varepsilon)\hbox{ remains strictly positive and regular as $\varepsilon\to 0$,}

(8.23)

neglecting the error term
E(\varepsilon)
. Note that the function
\tilde{\varphi}(r)
is regular near
r=1
, as can be seen immediately from its definition (
8.17
).
The positivity of
I(\varepsilon)
holds because we know from (
8.7
) that
\Phi_{1}(1-\varepsilon)
is finite and not small for any
\varepsilon>0
, and so is
\tilde{\varphi}(1)
.

The surprising fact is that (
8.23
) by itself leads to the asymptotic
expansion (
8.3
) for
m(t)
.
We first explain how we can find from (
8.23
) that the coefficient
a
that appears in (
8.12
) equals to
1/2
, as expected.
Using the expression for
\gamma(t)
in (
8.12
), we write

\displaystyle I(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\varepsilon^{2}t+(1-\varepsilon)\alpha(t)}}{(t+1)^{a(1-\varepsilon)}}dt=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-s+(1-\varepsilon)\alpha(s/\varepsilon^{2})}}{(s/\varepsilon^{2}+1)^{a(1-\varepsilon)}}\frac{ds}{\varepsilon^{2}}

(8.24)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\big(1+o(1)\big)e^{(1-\varepsilon)x_{\infty}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon^{2a(1-\varepsilon)-2}}{(s+\varepsilon^{2})^{a(1-\varepsilon)}}e^{-s}ds

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2a(1-\varepsilon)-1}\big(1+o(1)\big)e^{(1-\varepsilon)x_{\infty}}\int_{\varepsilon^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-r+\varepsilon^{2}}}{r^{a(1-\varepsilon)}}dr,

where the
o(1)
is according to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. We replaced
\alpha(s/\varepsilon^{2})
by its limit
x_{\infty}
in second-to-the last step above.
If
a>1
, then the non-integrability of
r^{-a}
near the origin will cause the integral to grow like
\varepsilon^{-2(a-1)}
. In this case, we deduce that
I(\varepsilon)\sim\varepsilon
, which cannot happen as
I(\varepsilon)
is uniformly positive (
8.23
). A similar argument applies to the case
a=1
. Hence,
a<1
. In this case, the integral is finite, so that
I(\varepsilon)\sim\varepsilon^{2a-1}
. Since
I
is positive and bounded (again, by (
8.23
)), the only choice is
a=1/2
as in
1.1
.

Next, we show how the remaining terms in (
8.3
) come about.
Taking
a=1/2
in (
8.24
) and writing

\alpha(t)=x_{\infty}+p(t),~~p(t)\to 0\hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$},

(8.25)

leads to

\displaystyle I(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon e^{(1-\varepsilon)x_{\infty}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}e^{(\varepsilon/2)\log(t+1)+(1-\varepsilon)p(t)-\varepsilon^{2}t}dt.

(8.26)

Let us assume without loss of generality that
x_{\infty}=0
, and note that

I(\varepsilon)
has the limit

\displaystyle I(\varepsilon)\to\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{\sqrt{t}}dt=\sqrt{\pi},~~\hbox{ as $\varepsilon\to 0$},

(8.27)

so the positivity requirement in (
8.23
) holds automatically. Expanding further gives

\displaystyle I(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log(t+1)+p(t)\Big)e^{-\varepsilon^{2}t}dt+o(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon).

(8.28)

Note that the first term in the integral above has the asymptotics

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log(t+1)}{\sqrt{t+1}}e^{-\varepsilon^{2}t}dt=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log t}{\sqrt{t}}e^{-\varepsilon^{2}t}dt+o(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon)

(8.29)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\varepsilon^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{\log(t/\varepsilon^{2})}{\sqrt{t}}e^{-t}dt+o(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon)=-\sqrt{\pi}\varepsilon\log\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon).

We now make an ansatz

p(t)=\frac{b}{\sqrt{t}}+o\big(t^{-1/2}\big),~~\hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$},

(8.30)

with the constant
b
to be determined, and insert it into the second integral in (
8.28
):

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{p(t)}{\sqrt{t+1}}e^{-\varepsilon^{2}t}dt=\varepsilon b\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\varepsilon^{2}t}}{t}dt+o(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon)=\varepsilon b\log(\varepsilon^{-2})+o(\varepsilon\log\varepsilon).

(8.31)

Taking into account the regularity of
I(\varepsilon)
together with (
8.28
)-(
8.29
)
and (
8.31
), we conclude that, since the overall coefficient in front of
\varepsilon\log\varepsilon

has to cancel, we must have

b=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}.

(8.32)

We deduce that when
\beta=2
the front location has the asymptotics

m(t)=2t-\frac{1}{2}\log t-x_{\infty}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{t}}+o(t^{-1/2}),~~\hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$,}

(8.33)

recovering all but the last term in the right side of (
8.3
).

A similar computation, expanding
I(\varepsilon)
further in
\varepsilon
, and
observing that the terms of the order
\varepsilon^{2}\log\varepsilon
must cancel, improves (
8.33
) to

\displaystyle m(t)=2t-\frac{1}{2}\log t-x_{\infty}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{t}}+\frac{(1-\log 2)}{4}\frac{\log t}{t}+....

(8.34)

which is (
8.3
).
We leave the computational details to an interested reader.

Appendix A
Traveling waves for the Burgers-FKPP equation

In this appendix, we recall some basic facts on the Burgers-FKPP traveling waves.
Most of them can be found in Section 13.4 of
[
42
]
, at least on a formal level,
and also in
[
53
]
for
\beta<0
.

We will use the notation

f(u)=u(1-u)

(A.1)

for the reaction term, and write the traveling wave Burgers-FKPP equation as

-cU^{\prime}+\beta UU^{\prime}=U^{\prime\prime}+f(U),~~~U(-\infty)=1,~U(+\infty)=0.

(A.2)

Existence of the traveling waves

We first state the result on the existence of the traveling waves.

Proposition A.1
.

A traveling wave solution to (
A.2
) exists for all
c\geq c_{*}(\beta)
, with

c_{*}(\beta)=\left\{\begin{matrix}2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}=2,~~~&\text{if }\beta<2,\cr\dfrac{\beta}{2}+\dfrac{2}{\beta},&\text{if }\beta\geq 2.\cr\end{matrix}\right.

(A.3)

Proof.
First, we note that the boundary conditions in (
A.2
) allow us to use the
sliding method to deduce that any traveling wave solution
U(x)
to (
A.2
) is monotonically
decreasing.
Introducing
V=-U^{\prime}>0
, we write (
A.2
) as a system

\frac{dU}{dx}=-V,~~\frac{dV}{dx}=(\beta U-c)V+f(U).

(A.4)

This leads to

\displaystyle\frac{dV}{dU}=c-\beta U-\frac{f(U)}{V}.

(A.5)

Consider the plane formed by the horizontal
U
-axis and the vertical
V
-axis.
The explicit form of
f(u)
in (
A.1
) shows that the system (
A.4
) has two equilibrium points

E_{1}=(0,0)
and
E_{2}=(1,0)
.

A traveling wave is a heteroclinic orbit of (
A.4
) that goes from
E_{2}
to
E_{1}
.

The linearization of (
A.4
) around
E_{1}
is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{U}\\
\tilde{V}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
f^{\prime}(0)&-c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{U}\\
\tilde{V}\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of this matrix are

\lambda_{1,2}=\frac{-c\pm\sqrt{c^{2}-4}}{2},

(A.6)

and are both real and negative if
c\geq 2
. Thus, the point
E_{1}
is a stable equilibrium of
c\geq 2
.

On the other hand, the linearization of (
A.2
) around
E_{2}
is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{U}\\
\tilde{V}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
f^{\prime}(1)&\beta-c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{U}\\
\tilde{V}\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of this matrix are

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\beta-c\pm\sqrt{(\beta-c)^{2}+4}}{2}.

(A.7)

They are real and have opposing signs, so
E_{2}
is a saddle point.

Existence of traveling waves for
c\geq c_{*}(\beta)

We prove existence of a heteroclinic orbit by constructing an invariant region in the
(U,V)
-plane
that contains
the unstable manifold of the point
(1,0)
. The regions are different for
\beta\geq 2
and
\beta<2
,
so we consider them separately.

When
\beta\geq 2
, we consider the region
D_{1}
formed by the interval

\ell_{1}=[0,1]\times\{0\}
along the
U
-axis, and the curve

\ell_{2}=\{V=(\beta/{2})f(U)\}
that connects the equilibrium points
E_{1}
and
E_{2}

and lies in the upper half-plane
\{V>0\}
. Note that the slope of
\ell_{2}
at the point
E_{2}

is
(-\beta/2)
, while the slope
e_{u}
of the unstable orbit at
E_{2}
is
(-\lambda_{+})
. Hence, the
unstable orbit starting at
E_{2}
enters the region
D_{1}
at the point
E_{2}
if
\lambda_{+}<\beta/2
.
This condition is satisfied if

\lambda_{+}=\frac{\beta-c+\sqrt{(\beta-c)^{2}+4}}{2}<\frac{\beta}{2},

(A.8)

or

c>c_{*}(\beta)=\frac{\beta}{2}+\frac{2}{\beta}.

(A.9)

Now, we check that the region
D_{1}
is invariant.
Along the interval
\ell_{1}
, we have, from (
A.4
):

\frac{dU}{dx}=0,~~\frac{dV}{dx}=f(U)>0,

(A.10)

so the trajectories point upward, into
D_{1}
. Along the curve
\ell_{2}
, we have, from (
A.5
):

\frac{dV}{dU}=c-\beta U-\frac{2}{\beta}.

(A.11)

The slope of the curve
\ell_{2}
itself is

\frac{\beta}{2}f^{\prime}(U)=\beta\Big(\frac{1}{2}-U\Big).

(A.12)

Thus, the trajectories along
\ell_{2}
point into
D_{1}
if

\displaystyle c-\beta U-\frac{2}{\beta}>\beta\Big(\frac{1}{2}-U\Big),~~\hbox{ for all $U\in[0,1]$,}

(A.13)

or, equivalently,

c>c_{*}(\beta)=\frac{\beta}{2}+\frac{2}{\beta}.

(A.14)

This shows that traveling waves exist for all
c>c_{*}(\beta)
when
\beta\geq 2
.
It is easy to see that the curve
\ell_{2}
itself forms a heteroclinic connection between
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
when
c=c_{*}(\beta)
.

When
0<\beta<2
, we consider a different region
D_{2}
formed by the same interval
\ell_{1}=[0,1]\times\{0\}

and the curve
\ell_{3}=\{V=f(U)\}
, also connecting the points
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
through the upper half-plane.
The slope of
\ell_{3}
at
E_{2}
is
(-1)
, hence the condition that the unstable direction
e_{u}
points into the region
D_{1}
at
the point
E_{2}
is
\lambda_{+}<1
, which is

\lambda_{+}=\frac{\beta-c+\sqrt{(\beta-c)^{2}+4}}{2}<1.

(A.15)

This condition holds as soon as
c>\beta
.

To check that the region
D_{2}
is invariant, we first note that,
as for
\beta\geq 2
, the trajectories point upward and inside
D_{2}
along the interval
\ell_{1}
. The slope of the curve
\ell_{3}
is
1-2U
, and we have,
from (
A.5
):

\displaystyle\frac{dU}{dx}<0,~~\frac{dV}{dU}=c-\beta U-1\geq 1-2U,~~\hbox{for all $U\in[0,1]$},

(A.16)

as long as
c\geq c_{*}=2
. We use here the fact that
\beta<2
. Thus, the region
D_{2}
is invariant. As the unstable direction
e_{u}
enters
D_{2}
,
existence of the heteroclinic orbit connecting
E_{2}
and
E_{1}
follows.

Non-existence of traveling waves for
c<c_{*}(\beta)

Consider first the case
\beta\leq 2
. Suppose that
c<c_{*}(\beta)=2
. Given a wave
U(x)
that satisfies (
A.2
), define the normalized translates

U_{n}(x)=\frac{U(x+n)}{U(n)},

that satisfy

-cU_{n}^{\prime}+\beta U(n)U_{n}U_{n}^{\prime}=U_{n}^{\prime\prime}+U_{n}-U(n)U_{n}^{2},~~U_{n}(0)=1.

(A.17)

As
U(n)\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
, the Harnack inequality and normalization
U_{n}(0)=1
imply that the sequence
U_{n}(x)
converges locally uniformly to a limit

\bar{U}(x)>0
that satisfies

-c\bar{U}^{\prime}=\bar{U}^{\prime\prime}+\bar{U},~~\bar{U}(0)=1.

(A.18)

Since
c<2
, solutions to (
A.18
) are exponentials of the form

\bar{U}(x)=A_{1}e^{\lambda_{1}x}+A_{2}e^{\lambda_{2}x},

with
\lambda_{1,2}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
given by (
A.6
). On the other hand,
\bar{U}
is real and positive. This is only possible if
A_{1}=A_{2}=0
, which violates the normalization
\bar{U}(0)=1
. This is a contradiction, and, thus,
c<2
is not possible when
\beta\leq 2
.

In the case
\beta\geq 2
we argue as follows to show that no wave can exist for
c<c_{*}(\beta)
. First, the same argument as for
\beta<2
implies that
there exist no waves for
c<2
. Let us assume that there there exists a heteroclinic orbit connecting
E_{2}

to
E_{1}
for some
c<c_{*}(\beta)
.
Then, (
A.6
) implies that its slope at the point
E_{1}=(0,0)
is

\frac{dV}{dU}\Big|_{(0,0)}=\frac{c-\sqrt{c^{2}-4}}{2}.

(A.19)

On the other hand, its slope at the point
E_{2}
is still given by
(-\lambda_{+})
, and we see from (
A.8
) that it goes out of the region

D_{1}
as it leaves
E_{2}
. Moreover, looking at (
A.11
) and (
A.12
) we see that if
c<c_{*}(\beta)
,
then the heteroclinic orbit can not re-enter the domain
D_{1}
along the curve
\ell_{2}
at a point with
0<U<1
.
Therefore, if it arrives to
E_{1}
, it has to do that above the curve
\ell_{2}
, and its slope at this point has to satisfy

\frac{dV}{dU}\Big|_{(0,0)}>\frac{\beta}{2}.

(A.20)

Combining (
A.19
) and (
A.20
) gives

\frac{c-\sqrt{c^{2}-4}}{2}>\frac{\beta}{2}.

(A.21)

It follows that

\beta<\frac{4}{c+\sqrt{c^{2}-4}}\leq\frac{4}{c}\leq 2,

(A.22)

as we already know that
c\geq 2
. This is a contradiction to the assumption that
\beta\geq 2
, finishing the proof of Proposition
A
.
\Box

Remark.

When
c=2
and
\beta\in(0,2)
, the above proof shows that for
\beta\in(0,2)
, all trajectories are trapped in the region bounded by the curves
\ell_{2}=\{V=({\beta}/{2})f(U)\}

and
\ell_{3}=\{V=f(U)\}
, that is
D_{2}\setminus D_{1}
. The reasons for this are that the inequality in (
A.13
) is reversed and (
A.16
) holds, so this region is invariant, and
\beta/2<\lambda_{+}<1
, so trajectories enter
D_{2}\setminus D_{1}
from
E_{2}
. This implies that for any
0<z<1
we have

\frac{z(1-z)}{|\bar{E}(z)|}\leq\frac{2}{\beta},

(A.23)

with
\bar{E}(z)
defined in (
2.6
).

The asymptotic profile of the traveling waves

We briefly summarize asymptotic profiles of traveling wave solutions. The details are essentially identical to the Fisher-KPP equation,
see, for instance,
[
38
]
for a detailed analysis.

When
\beta<2
, the minimal speed traveling wave has the following asymptotics on the right:

\displaystyle U(z)=(Az+B)e^{-z}+O(e^{-(1+\delta)z}),~~\text{as }z\to+\infty.

(A.24)

with
A>0
and
\delta>0
.
When
\beta\geq 2
, the critical front has an explicit form

\displaystyle U(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta z/2}}.

(A.25)

Steepness comparison of the waves

Finally, we present a steepness comparison for the traveling waves that is used to show convergence of the solution in shape to the minimal speed traveling wave.

Lemma A.2
.

Fix any
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
be two traveling waves with speeds
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, respectively.
If
c_{1}\leq c_{2}
, then
U_{1}
is steeper than
U_{2}
.

Proof.
The proof follows an approach from
[
20
]
. If we define

\bar{E}_{i}(z)=-U_{i}^{\prime}(U_{i}^{-1}(z)),

(A.26)

it is enough to show that

\bar{E}_{1}(z)\leq\bar{E}_{2}(z),~~\hbox{ for all $z\in(0,1)$.}

(A.27)

Using (
A.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{i}^{\prime}(z)+\beta z+\frac{f(z)}{\bar{E}_{i}(z)}=c_{i},

(A.28)

so that

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{1}^{\prime}(z)-\bar{E}_{2}^{\prime}(z)-\frac{f(z)}{\bar{E}_{1}(z)\bar{E}_{2}(z)}(\bar{E}_{1}(z)-\bar{E}_{2}(z))=c_{1}-c_{2}.

(A.29)

It follows that the function

\displaystyle F(z)=(\bar{E}_{1}(z)-\bar{E}_{2}(z))\exp\Big(-\int_{1/2}^{z}\frac{f(z^{\prime})}{\bar{E}_{1}(z^{\prime})\bar{E}_{2}(z^{\prime})}dz^{\prime}\Big),

satisfies

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(z)=(c_{1}-c_{2})\exp\Big(-\int_{1/2}^{z}\frac{f(z^{\prime})}{\bar{E}_{1}(z^{\prime})\bar{E}_{2}(z^{\prime})}dz^{\prime}\Big).

(A.30)

Observe that
\bar{E}_{1}-\bar{E}_{2}\to 0
as
z\to 1
. It follows that
F(z)\to 0
, as well.
On the other hand, since
c_{1}\leq c_{2}
, we see from (
A.30
) that

F(z)
is decreasing. It follows that
F(z)\leq 0
for all
z
, which implies (
A.27
) and concludes the proof.
\Box
