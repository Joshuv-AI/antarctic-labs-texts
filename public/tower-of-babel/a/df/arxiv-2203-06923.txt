The Fractional Malmheden Theorem
Serena Dipierro, Giovanni Giacomin and Enrico Valdinoci

Abstract
We provide a fractional counterpart of the classical results by Schwarz and Malmheden on harmonic functions. From that we obtain a representation formula for s s -harmonic functions as a linear superposition of weighted classical harmonic functions which also entails a new proof of the fractional Harnack inequality. This proof also leads to optimal constants for the fractional Harnack inequality in the ball.

Introduction

In 1934, Harry William Malmheden
[
13
]
proved a simple algorithm to compute the value of a harmonic function
at a point of
B_{1}
, knowing its value on the boundary.

The Malmheden Theorem makes use of two fundamental geometric ingredients:

1.

the notion of affine interpolation between the values of a given function at two different points of the space,

2.

the projections of a point inside a ball to the boundary in a given direction.

Hence, to state the Malmheden Theorem explicitly, we now formalize these two notions into a precise mathematical setting.
We start by introducing a notation
for the affine interpolation between the values of some given function. That is, given a set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
a function
f:K\to\mathbb{R}
, two distinct points
a
,
b\in K
, and a point
x
on the segment
L
joining
a
and
b
, we define
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{a,b}(x)
as the affine function on
L
such that
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{a,b}(a)=f(a)
and
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{a,b}(b)=f(b)
.

Of course, one can write this affine function explicitly by using the analytic expression

\mathcal{L}_{f}^{a,b}(x)=\frac{(x-a)\cdot e}{|b-a|}\,f(b)+\frac{(b-x)\cdot e}{|b-a|}\,f(a),\qquad{\mbox{where }}e:=\frac{b-a}{|b-a|}.

(1.1)

One can call
\mathcal{L}_{f}^{a,b}(x)
the “affine function of
f
with extrema
a
and
b

evaluated at the point
x
”.

Figure 1:
The projections
Q_{\pm}^{x}(e)
used in the Malmheden Theorem.

Now we discuss the notation regarding the projections of a point
inside a ball to the boundary of the ball in a given direction.
For this, given a point
x\in B_{1}

and a direction
e\in\partial B_{1}
we consider the intersections
Q_{+}^{x}(e)
and
Q_{-}^{x}(e)
of
\partial B_{1}
with the straight line passing through
x\in B_{1}
with direction
e
, with the convention that
Q_{+}^{x}(e)-Q_{-}^{x}(e)

has the same orientation of
e
, see Figure
1
.

Clearly, from the analytic point of view, one can write explicitly these projections in the form

\begin{split}&Q_{+}^{x}(e):=x+r_{+}^{x}(e)e\\
{\mbox{and }}\qquad&Q_{-}^{x}(e):=x+r_{-}^{x}(e)e,\end{split}

(1.2)

where

\begin{split}&r_{+}^{x}(e)=-x\cdot e+\sqrt{(x\cdot e)^{2}-|x|^{2}+1}\\
{\mbox{and }}\qquad&r_{-}^{x}(e)=-x\cdot e-\sqrt{(x\cdot e)^{2}-|x|^{2}+1}.\end{split}

(1.3)

We note from equations (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) that
Q_{\pm}^{x}(e)
are
continuous functions in
(x,e)\in B_{1}\times\partial B_{1}
. Moreover,

\lim_{x\to 0}Q_{\pm}^{x}(e)=\pm e

(1.4)

for each
e\in\partial B_{1}
. This tells us that the maps
Q_{\pm}^{x}
simply reduce to
\pm id_{\partial B_{1}}
when
x=0
.

Given a boundary datum
f:\partial B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
,
the core of the Malmheden Theorem is thus to consider, for every point
x\in B_{1}
and every direction
e\in\partial B_{1}
,
the affine function of
f
with extrema
Q_{-}^{x}(e)
and
Q_{+}^{x}(e)
, namely the function

\mathcal{L}_{f}^{Q_{-}^{x}(e),Q_{+}^{x}(e)}(x)

(1.5)

and then to average in all directions
e
.

The remarkable result by Malmheden is that this averaging procedure of linear interpolations
produces precisely the solution of the classical Dirichlet problem in
B_{1}
with boundary datum
f
, according to the
following classical statement (see
[
13
]
):

Theorem 1.1

(Malmheden Theorem)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
and
f:\partial B_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuous. Then

u_{f}(x):=\fint_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{f}^{Q_{-}^{x}(e),Q_{+}^{x}(e)}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}

(1.6)

is the harmonic function in
B_{1}
with boundary datum
f
.

As usual, here above and in the rest of this paper, we denoted by
H^{n-1}
the
(n-1)
-Hausdorff measure
(hence, the integral on the right hand side of (
1.6
) is simply the spherical integral along
\partial B_{1}
;
we kept the explicit notation with the Hausdorff measure to have a typographical evidence
of the surface integrals, to be distinguished by the classical volume ones).

We remark that Theorem
1.1
contains the Mean Value Theorem for harmonic functions as a particular case:
indeed, in light of (
1.1
) and (
1.4
),
if we take
x:=0
then (
1.6
) reduces to

u_{f}(0)=\fint_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{f}^{-e,e}(0)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}=\fint_{\partial B_{1}}\left(\frac{f(e)}{2}+\frac{f(-e)}{2}\right)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}=\fint_{\partial B_{1}}f(e)\,dH_{e}^{n-1},

(1.7)

which is the content of the Mean Value Theorem.

We also stress that an elegant result such as Theorem
1.1
is specific for balls and
cannot be extended in general to other domains, as pointed out in
[
1
]
.

Interestingly, Theorem
1.1
contains as a particular case a classical result due to Hermann Amandus Schwarz
[
18
]

about the Dirichlet problem in the plane and related to conformal mappings in the complex framework.

Figure 2:
The reflection
Q^{x}(\omega)
used in the Schwarz Theorem.

To state Schwarz result it is convenient to introduce the reflection of a point
\omega\in\partial B_{1}
through a point
x\in B_{1}
, see Figure
2
.
More precisely, given
x\in B_{1}
and
\omega\in\partial B_{1}
we define

Q^{x}(\omega):=\omega-2\frac{(x-\omega)\cdot\omega}{|x-\omega|^{2}}(x-\omega).

(1.8)

Comparing with (
1.2
), one sees that if
e:=\frac{\omega-x}{|\omega-x|}
then
Q_{+}^{x}(e)=\omega

and
Q_{-}^{x}(e)=Q^{x}(\omega)
.

In this setting, the result by Schwarz is that the average of the boundary datum composed with the above reflection returns
the solution of the Dirichlet problem in the ball. More explicitly:

Theorem 1.2

(Schwarz Theorem)
.

Let
n=2
and
f:\partial B_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuous. Then

u_{f}(x):=\fint_{\partial B_{1}}f(Q^{x}(e))\,dH_{e}^{n-1}

(1.9)

is the harmonic function in
B_{1}
with external datum
f
.

Theorem
1.2
can be proved in several ways using
either complex or real analysis (see e.g.
[
17
,
9
,
15
]
), but it is
also a direct consequence of Theorem
1.1
,
see e.g.
[
8
]
for a detailed presentation of this classical
argument.

Example 1.3
.

A very neat application of Theorem
1.2
(see e.g.
[
16
]
)
consists in the determination of the stationary temperature
u
at a point
x
in a plate (say
B_{1}
)
when the temperature along the boundary of the plate is
1
along some arc
\Sigma

and
0
outside. In this case, the reflection in (
1.8
) sends
\Sigma
into an arc
\Sigma^{\prime}

(the symmetric of
\Sigma
through
x
, see Figure
3
) and it therefore follows from
Theorem
1.2
that

u(x)=\frac{|\Sigma^{\prime}|}{2\pi},

where
|\Sigma^{\prime}|
is the length of the arc
\Sigma^{\prime}
, thus providing an elementary geometric construction
to solve a problem of physical relevance.

The geometric construction to detect the temperature of a plate at the point
x
.

The objective of this paper
is to obtain a fractional counterpart for the Malmheden and
Schwarz Theorems.

We will thus replace the notion of harmonic functions in
B_{1}
with that of
s
-harmonic functions,
namely functions whose fractional Laplacian vanishes in
B_{1}
, that is, for all
x\in B_{1}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy=0,

(1.10)

where the integral above is intended in the principal value sense. Here above and throughout the paper
the fractional parameter
s\in(0,1)
.

Rather than a boundary value along
\partial B_{1}
, as usual in the nonlocal setting,
we complement (
1.10
)
with an external condition of the type
u=f
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
.

We recall that in general
s
-harmonic functions behave way more wildly that their classical counterparts,
see e.g.
[
7
]
. Therefore, in principle one cannot easily expect that a “simple formulation”
such as the one in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
accounts for all the complex situations arising in the fractional
setting.

However, we will prove that a counterpart of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
carries over to the case of the fractional Laplacian,
considering the following structural modifications:

1.

the classical spherical averages are replaced by suitable weighted averages on spheres of radius larger than
1
,

2.

the geometric transformations in (
1.2
) and (
1.8
) are scaled in dependence of the radius of each of these spheres.

To clarify these points, and thus reconsider (
1.5
) in a nonlocal setting, given
\rho>1
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
,
for all
x\in\partial B_{1}
we define

f_{\rho}(x):=f(\rho x).

(1.11)

Hence, in the notation of (
1.1
), we define

\mathcal{L}_{f,e,\rho}(x):=\mathcal{L}_{f_{\rho}}^{Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e),Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right).

(1.12)

Notice that when
\rho=1
the above setting reduces to (
1.5
),
otherwise one is considering here a similar framework but for a rescaled version of the function
f
and rescaled points.

To detect the long-range effect of the fractional Laplacian, it is also useful to consider the kernel

B_{1}\times(1,+\infty)\ni(x,\rho)\longmapsto{\mathcal{E}}(x,\rho):=c(n,s)\,\frac{\rho\,(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}{(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}\,(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})},

(1.13)

where

c(n,s):=\frac{\displaystyle\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)\,\sin(\pi s)}{\pi^{\frac{n}{2}+1}}.

(1.14)

For our purposes, the kernel
{\mathcal{E}}
will play the role of a suitable spherical average
1
1

1

More precisely, the intuition behind
{\mathcal{E}}

is that it satisfies, for each
x\in B_{1}
and
\rho\in(1,+\infty)
,

{\mathcal{E}}(x,\rho)=\rho^{n-1}\fint_{\partial B_{\rho}}P(x,y)\,dH^{n-1}_{y},

where
P
is the Fractional Poisson Kernel.
This relation can be obtained as a consequence of
our Fractional Malmheden Theorem.
Though our approach does not pass explicitly through this
identity, for the sake of completeness we provide an independent proof in Appendix
A.1
.

of a Fractional Poisson Kernel
and the constant
c(n,s)
is merely needed for normalization purposes.

We also define the space

L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}):=\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}{\mbox{ measurable }}\,:\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{|f(x)|}{|x|^{n+2s}}\,dx<\infty\right\}.

(1.15)

With this, we can state the main result of this paper as follows:

Theorem 1.4

(Fractional Malmheden Theorem)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,
s\in(0,1)
,
R>1

and
f\in L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
.

Then, the unique solution (up to a zero measure subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
) to the problem

\begin{cases}\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{s}u&=0\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;B_{1},\\
u&=f\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}\end{split}\end{cases}

(1.16)

can be written, for each
x\in B_{1}
, as

u_{f}^{(s)}(x):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}{\mathcal{E}}(x,\rho)\,\mathcal{L}_{f,e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho.

(1.17)

As a fractional counterpart of the observation in (
1.7
),
we point out that Theorem
1.4
entails as a straightforward
consequence the Mean Value Formula
for
s
-harmonis functions. Indeed, by the changes of
variable
e:=\omega/|\omega|
and
y:=\rho\omega/|\omega|
,

\displaystyle u_{f}^{(s)}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}{\mathcal{E}}(0,\rho)\,\mathcal{L}_{f,e,\rho}(0)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(n,s)\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{1}{\rho(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\,\mathcal{L}_{f_{\rho}}^{-e,e}(0)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(n,s)\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{1}{\rho(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\,\left(\frac{f(\rho e)}{2}+\frac{f(-\rho e)}{2}\right)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(n,s)\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\frac{1}{\rho^{n}(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\,\left(\frac{f(\omega)}{2}+\frac{f(-\omega)}{2}\right)\,dH_{\omega}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(n,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{1}{|y|^{n}(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}}\,\left(\frac{f(y)}{2}+\frac{f(-y)}{2}\right)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(n,s)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{f(y)}{|y|^{n}(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}}\,dy,

which is the Mean Value Formula
for
s
-harmonis functions, see e.g. formula (1.3) in
[
3
]
.

We consider Theorem
1.4
as the natural fractional counterpart of
Theorem
1.1
and we mention that indeed one can “recover” Theorem
1.1
in the limit as
s\nearrow 1
, according to
the following result:

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,
s_{0}\in(0,1)
,
R>1

and
f\in C(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})

for each
s\in(s_{0},1]
. Then, for each
x\in B_{1}
, it holds that

\lim_{s\nearrow 1}u_{f}^{(s)}(x)=u_{f}(x),

where
u_{f}^{(s)}
and
u_{f}
are defined in (
1.17
) and (
1.6
), respectively.

As a straightforward consequence of the classical Malmheden Theorem (Theorem
1.1
) and its fractional formulation Theorem (Theorem
1.4
), we deduce the following result.

Theorem 1.6

(An
s
-harmonic function is the superposition of classical harmonic functions)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,
s\in(0,1)
and
f\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
. For each
\rho>1
we define
{u}_{f_{\rho}}
as the unique solution to the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}\begin{split}\Delta u&=0\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;B_{1},\\
u|_{\partial B_{1}}&=f_{\rho}|_{\partial B_{1}},\end{split}\end{cases}

(1.18)

where
f_{\rho}
is defined in (
1.11
).

Then the unique solution
u_{f}^{(s)}
to (
1.16
) can be written as

u_{f}^{(s)}(x)=|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\,{u}_{f_{\rho}}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho.

(1.19)

The interest of Theorem
1.6
is that it allows us to write an
s
-harmonic function in
B_{1}
as a weighted integral of classical harmonic functions, where the weight coincide with
\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)
.
Besides being interesting in itself, this result is very useful
to deduce properties of
s
-harmonic functions, as the Harnack inequality (see Section
4
),
starting from their local counterpart.

As a matter of fact, as a consequence of Theorem
1.6
one obtains a new proof of the Harnack inequality for
s
-harmonic functions in
B_{1}
. The result goes as follows:

Theorem 1.7

(Harnack inequality)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,
s\in(0,1)
,
R>1
and
u
be non negative,
s
-harmonic in
B_{1}
and such that
u\in L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
.

Then, for each
r\in(0,1)
and
x\in B_{r}
,

\frac{(1-r^{2})^{s}}{(1+r)^{n}}u(0)\leqslant u(x)\leqslant\frac{(1-r^{2})^{s}}{(1-r)^{n}}u(0).

(1.20)

The constants in (
1.20
) are optimal, and for
s\nearrow 1
they converge to the optimal constants of the classical Harnack inequality in
B_{r}
for harmonic functions in
B_{1}
.

For different proofs of the fractional Harnack inequality see
[
12
,
6
,
11
]
and the references therein.

1.4
is the fractional version
of Schwarz result:

Theorem 1.8

(Fractional Schwarz Theorem)
.

Let

,

,

Remark 1.10
.

It is worth pointing out that from Theorem

we can evince the identity

for each

As an application of Theorem
1.8
, we have:

Example 1.11
.

Let
n=2
and take an arc
\Sigma\subset\partial B_{1}
. Consider the function defined
on

as

\tilde{\chi}_{\Sigma}(y):=\begin{cases}\begin{split}1\qquad\textrm{if}\;\;&\displaystyle\frac{y}{|y|}\in\Sigma,\\

It is clear that

we get that for each

u_{\tilde{\chi}_{\Sigma}}^{(s)}(x)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)|\Sigma_{x/\rho}^{\prime}|\,d\rho

where
\Sigma_{x/\rho}^{\prime}
is the projected arc of
\Sigma
on

through the focal point

, as constructed in Example

. We denoted with
|\Sigma_{x/\rho}^{\prime}|
its length.

This gives a simple geometrical procedure to compute the
solution of

\begin{cases}\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{s}u&=0\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;B_{1},\\
u&=\tilde{\chi}_{\Sigma}\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{1}\end{split}\end{cases}

at a point

of the two dimensional disc when the non local boundary condition is given by (

If

2
Preliminary results on the Fractional Poisson Kernel

In this section, we revisit the well-established result according to which fractional harmonic functions
can be represented as an integral of the datum outside the domain against a suitable Poisson Kernel.
For completeness, we extend this result to the case in which the datum is not necessarily continuous,
so to be able to present the results of this paper in a suitable generality.
Notice that the extension to functions that are not necessarily continuous is also useful for us to
comprise situations as in Example
1.11
.

The framework that we consider is the following.
For
n\geqslant 2
and
s\in(0,1)
, we consider the space
L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})

as defined in (
1.15
). Given
f\in L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
, we denote
the norm on
L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
by

\|f\|_{L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{|f(x)|}{|x|^{n+2s}}\,dx.

Furthermore, we define the following
Fractional Poisson Kernel
in the unit ball

P(x,y):=c(n,s)\left(\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{|y|^{2}-1}\right)^{s}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n}}

(2.1)

for
x\in B_{1}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
, and
c(n,s)
is the normalizing constant in (
1.14
).

As customary, the role of the constant
c(n,s)
is to normalize the Poisson Kernel, namely we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}P(x,y)\,dy=1,

(2.2)

see e.g. formula (1.14) and Lemma A.5 in
[
5
]
.

We also remark that

P(\cdot,y)\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})

and, for every
\rho\in(0,1)
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
,

\sup_{x\in B_{\rho}}|D_{x}^{\alpha}P(x,y)|\leqslant\frac{C_{\rho}}{(|y|-1)^{s}\,|y|^{n+s+|\alpha|}},

(2.3)

where
C_{\rho}>0
depends only on
\rho
,
n
and
s
and, as usual, we have denoted the length
of the multi-index
\alpha
as
|\alpha|:=\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}
.

Then, we define

u_{f}^{(s)}(x):=\begin{cases}\begin{split}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}P(x,y)f(y)\,dy\;\;&\textrm{if}\;x\in B_{1},\\
f(x)\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;&\textrm{if}\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1},\end{split}\end{cases}

(2.4)

and we have the following result on the representation of
s
-harmonic functions:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,
s\in(0,1)
and
f\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
. Then the function in (
2.4
) is the unique pointwise continuous solution to the problem

\begin{cases}\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}^{(s)}&=0\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;B_{1},\\
u_{f}^{(s)}&=f\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}.\end{split}\end{cases}

(2.5)

For a proof of Theorem
2.1
see e.g. Theorem 2.10 in
[
5
]
.

We now generalize
Theorem
2.1
by allowing external data that are not necessarily continuous:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,
s\in(0,1)
,
R>1
and
f\in L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
. Then the function defined in (
2.4
) is the unique solution (up to a zero measure subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
) to the problem in (
2.5
).

Proof.

We argue by approximation, owing to Theorem
2.1
.
The gist is indeed to take a sequence of continuous functions
f_{k}
approaching
f
as
k\to+\infty
,
use Theorem
2.1
and then pass to the limit. To implement this idea, one needs to take care
of some regularity issues.

The details of this technical argument go as follows. By (
2.3
),
for each
x\in B_{1}
and multi-index
\alpha
we have that

D_{x}^{\alpha}P(x,\cdot)f(\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B}_{1}).

As a consequence, we obtain that
u_{f}^{(s)}(x)
in (
2.4
) is well defined and smooth inside
B_{1}
.

To complete the proof of Proposition
2.2
,
we need to show that
u_{f}^{(s)}
, as defined in (
2.4
), is the unique solution
of (
2.5
).
To do so, we start by checking that
u_{f}^{(s)}
is
s
-harmonic in
B_{1}
. We consider a sequence
\{f_{k}\}_{k}\subset C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
, such that

f_{k}\rightarrow f
in
L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
as
k\to+\infty
.

(2.6)

More specifically, we take
f_{k}:=(\chi_{B_{k}}\tilde{f})*\eta_{\frac{1}{k}}

with
k\geqslant 2
, where
\tilde{f}
is defined as

\tilde{f}(x):=\begin{cases}\begin{split}f(x)\;\;\textrm{if}\;\;&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1},\\
0\;\;\textrm{if}\;\;&x\in B_{1},\end{split}\end{cases}

and
\eta_{\frac{1}{k}}
is a mollifier of radius
\frac{1}{k}
, while
\chi_{B_{k}}
is the characteristic function of
B_{k}
. We also let
u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}
be the unique
pointwise continuous solution to the problem (
2.5
),
according to Theorem
2.1
.

Then we have that for each multi-index
\alpha

\|D^{\alpha}u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}-D^{\alpha}u_{f}^{(s)}\|_{L_{\textrm{loc}}^{\infty}(B_{1})}\to 0\quad{\mbox{ as~$k\to+\infty$}}.

(2.7)

Indeed for each multi-index
\alpha
and
g\in L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
one finds that

D^{\alpha}u_{g}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}D_{x}^{\alpha}P(x,y)g(y)\,dy

for each
x\in B_{1}
, and therefore, choosing
R_{0}\in(1,R)
, we see that, for every
x\in B^{\prime}
with
B^{\prime}\Subset B_{1}
,

\begin{split}&|D^{\alpha}u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)-D^{\alpha}u_{f}^{(s)}(x)|\\
&\qquad\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}|D_{x}^{\alpha}P(x,y)||f_{k}(y)-f(y)|\,dy+\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}}|D_{x}^{\alpha}P(x,y)||f_{k}(y)-f(y)|\,dy\\
&\qquad\leqslant c\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{|f_{k}(y)-f(y)|}{|y|^{n+2s}}\,dy+\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}}|D_{x}^{\alpha}P(x,y)||f_{k}(y)-f(y)|\,dy,\end{split}

(2.8)

where
c
is a positive constant depending on
\alpha
,
R_{0}
,
n
,
s
and
B^{\prime}
.
The first term in the third
line in (
2.8
) converges to zero as
k\to+\infty
,
thanks to (
2.6
).
We also
observe that, if
y\in B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}
, then
|f_{k}(y)-f(y)|\leqslant 2\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})}
, and therefore, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem,
we have that also the second term in the third
line in (
2.8
) converges to zero as
k\to+\infty
. These
considerations prove (
2.7
).

Furthermore note that if
\alpha=0
, taking
R_{0}\in(1,R)
and
using also (
2.2
), we have that, for all
x\in B_{1}
,

\begin{split}|u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)|\leqslant\;&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}|P(x,y)||f_{k}(y)|\,dy+\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}}|P(x,y)||f_{k}(y)|\,dy\\
\leqslant\;&C\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{|f_{k}(y)|}{|y|^{n+2s}}\,dy+\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}}|P(x,y)||f_{k}(y)|\,dy\\
\leqslant\;&C\|f_{k}\|_{L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})},\end{split}

(2.9)

where
C
is a positive constant depending on
R_{0}
,
n
and
s
. Now,
we observe that the sequence
\|f_{k}\|_{L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})}
is uniformly bounded,
thanks to (
2.6
). Accordingly, from (
2.9
)
we see that

u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}
is uniformly bounded in
B_{1}
.

(2.10)

Now, if
x\in B_{1}
, taking
\delta\in\left(0,1-|x|\right)
,
we have that

\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)-(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}^{(s)}(x)=\;&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)-u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(y)-u_{f}^{(s)}(x)+u_{f}^{(s)}(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy\\
=\;&A+B+C+D+E+F,\end{split}

(2.11)

where

\displaystyle A:=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)-u_{f}^{(s)}(x)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy,\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle B:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(y)-u_{f}^{(s)}(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy,

\displaystyle C:=\displaystyle\int_{B_{\delta}(x)}\frac{u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)-u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy,\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle D:=\int_{B_{\delta}(x)}\frac{u_{f}^{(s)}(y)-u_{f}^{(s)}(x)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy,

\displaystyle E:=\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{\delta}(x)}\frac{u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)-u_{f}^{(s)}(x)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy\qquad{\mbox{ and }}\qquad

\displaystyle F:=\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{\delta}(x)}\frac{u_{f}^{(s)}(y)-u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy.

Notice that

|A+E|\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\delta}(x)}\frac{|u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)-u_{f}^{(s)}(x)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy\leqslant|u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)-u_{f}^{(s)}(x)|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\delta}}\frac{dz}{|z|^{n+2s}}\leqslant\frac{C}{\delta^{2s}}|u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)-u_{f}^{(s)}(x)|,

which converges to zero as
k\to+\infty
, thanks to (
2.7
).

Furthermore, we observe that if
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
then

|x-y|\geqslant|y|-|x|=\delta|y|+(1-\delta)|y|-|x|\geqslant\delta|y|+1-\delta-|x|\geqslant\delta|y|,

and thus

|B|\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{|f_{k}(y)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta^{n+2s}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{|f_{k}(y)-f(y)|}{|y|^{n+2s}}\,dy

which, in light of (
2.6
), converges to zero as
k\to+\infty
.

Moreover, from (
2.7
) and the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we see that
the quantity
C+D
converges to zero as
k\to+\infty
.

Finally, recalling (
2.10
)
and making again use of the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we have that
F
converges to zero
as
k\to+\infty
.

These considerations and (
2.11
) give that

(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)
converges to
(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}^{(s)}(x)
as
k\to+\infty

for every
x\in B_{1}
.
Since, by Theorem
2.1
, we know that
(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}(x)=0
for each
x\in B_{1}
,
we conclude that
(-\Delta)^{s}u_{f}^{(s)}(x)=0
. This proves that
u_{f}^{(s)}
solves (
2.5
).

It is only left to show the uniqueness statement. Suppose that there exists
u_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\begin{cases}\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{s}u_{1}&=0\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;B_{1},\\
u_{1}&=f\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}.\end{split}\end{cases}

Then both
v:=u_{f}^{(s)}-u_{1}
and
-v=u_{1}-u_{f}^{(s)}
are solutions to

\begin{cases}\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{s}u&=0\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;B_{1},\\
u&=0\;\;\textrm{in}\;\;\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1},\end{split}\end{cases}

and therefore by the maximum principle for the fractional Laplacian (see e.g. Theorem 2.3.2. in
[
4
]
) we have that
v=0
in
B_{1}
, leading to uniqueness.
∎

3
Proof of the Fractional Malmheden
and Schwarz Theorems

In this section we provide the proofs of the Fractional Malmheden
and Schwarz results, as stated in Theorems
1.4
and
1.8
, and of the
convergence result in Proposition
1.5
.

We start with the main argument to prove Theorem
1.4
. For this, we
employ the following change of variable result (see Lemma 2.13.3 in
[
8
]
for the proof of it):

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,
x\in B_{1}
and
Q_{\pm}^{x}
and
r_{\pm}^{x}

be defined as in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), respectively. Then for each
e\in\partial B_{1}
it holds
that

|\det DQ_{\pm}^{x}(e)|=\frac{(\pm r_{\pm}^{x}(e))^{n}}{1-|x|^{2}-r_{\pm}^{x}(e)x\cdot e},

and for each continuous
f:\partial B_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we have that

\int_{\partial B_{1}}f(e)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}=\int_{\partial B_{1}}f(Q_{\pm}^{x}(e))\frac{(\pm r_{\pm}^{x}(e))^{n}}{1-|x|^{2}-r_{\pm}^{x}(e)x\cdot e}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}.

With this notation, Theorem
1.4
will be a consequence of the following statement.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,

s\in(0,1)
,
R>1

and
f\in L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
.
Let
u_{f}^{(s)}
be as in (
2.4
).

Then, for each
x\in B_{1}
,

u_{f}^{(s)}(x)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}{\mathcal{E}}(x,\rho)\,\mathcal{L}_{f,e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho,

(3.1)

where the notation in (
1.12
) and (
1.13
) has been used.

Furthermore, if
f
is positively homogeneous of degree
\gamma
for some
\gamma\geqslant 0
, then we have that

u_{f}^{(s)}(x)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\rho^{\gamma}{\mathcal{E}}(x,\rho)\,\mathcal{L}_{f,e,1}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho.

(3.2)

Proof.

We first suppose
that
f\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
.
Let
x\in B_{1}
, then, using polar coordinates, from (
2.1
) and (
2.4
)
we get that

\begin{split}u_{f}^{(s)}(x)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}P(x,y)f(y)\,dy\\
&=c(n,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{1}{(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|^{n}}\,dy\\
&=c(n,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\frac{f(\omega)}{|x-\omega|^{n}}\,dH_{\omega}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
&=c(n,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{n-1}}{(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{f(\rho e)}{|x-\rho e|^{n}}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
&=c(n,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\rho(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{f(\rho e)}{\big|\frac{x}{\rho}-e\big|^{n}}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
&=c(n,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho}{(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{f(\rho e)}{\big|\frac{x}{\rho}-e\big|^{n}}\left(1-\frac{|x|^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
&=:{\mathcal{I}}.\end{split}

(3.3)

Hence, defining

g(e):=\frac{f(\rho e)}{\big|\frac{x}{\rho}-e\big|^{n}}\left(1-\frac{|x|^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)

and applying Lemma
3.1
we obtain that

\begin{split}&\frac{{\mathcal{I}}}{c(n,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}\\
&\quad=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho}{(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}g(e)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
&\quad=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho}{(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}g(Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))\frac{(-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))^{n}}{1-|x/\rho|^{2}-(x/\rho\cdot e)r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
&\quad=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho}{(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{f(\rho Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))}{\big|\frac{x}{\rho}-Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)\big|^{n}}\frac{\left(1-|x/\rho|^{2}\right)(-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))^{n}}{1-|x/\rho|^{2}-(x/\rho\cdot e)r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho.\end{split}

(3.4)

From equations (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) we deduce that

\left|\frac{x}{\rho}-Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)\right|=|r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)|=-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e),

and also
(see formula (2.13.25) in
[
8
]
)

\frac{1-|x/\rho|^{2}}{1-|x/\rho|^{2}-(x/\rho\cdot e)r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}=\frac{2r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)}{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}

which, together with (
3.4
), gives that

{\mathcal{I}}=c(n,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho}{(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{2r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)f(\rho Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))}{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho.

(3.5)

We now observe that

\begin{split}&\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{2r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)f(\rho Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))}{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\\
=&\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)f(\rho Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))}{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}-\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)f(\rho Q_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)))}{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\\
=&\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)f(\rho Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)f(\rho Q_{+}^{x/\rho}(e))}{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}.\end{split}

(3.6)

From (
1.2
), (
1.3
), (
1.11
) and (
1.12
) we also deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)f(\rho Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)f(\rho Q_{+}^{x/\rho}(e))}{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)f_{\rho}(Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)f_{\rho}(Q_{+}^{x/\rho}(e))}{|Q_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(Q_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\cdot e\,f_{\rho}(Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))+\left(\frac{x}{\rho}-Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)\right)\cdot e\,f_{\rho}(Q_{+}^{x/\rho}(e))}{|Q_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{f_{\rho}}^{Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e),Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{f,e,\rho}(x).

Plugging this information into (
3.6
) we find that

\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{2r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)f(\rho Q_{-}^{x/\rho}(e))}{r_{+}^{x/\rho}(e)-r_{-}^{x/\rho}(e)}\,dH_{e}^{n-1}=\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{f,e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1},

which in turn, together with (
3.5
), gives that

{\mathcal{I}}=c(n,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho}{(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{f,e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho.

Thus, recalling (
1.13
), this and (
3.3
) establish the desired result in (
3.1
)
under the additional assumption that
f
is continuous in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
.

Now we remove the continuity assumption on
f
by an approximation argument. Given
f\in L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
, we consider a sequence of functions
\{f_{k}\}_{k}\subset C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
as in (
2.6
)
and we let
u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}
be the unique
pointwise continuous solution to the problem (
2.5
),
according to Theorem
2.1
.

By (
2.7
),

\|u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}-u_{f}^{(s)}\|_{L_{\textrm{loc}}^{\infty}(B_{1})}\to 0.

(3.7)

Therefore we have that, for each
x\in B_{1}
,

u_{f}^{(s)}(x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho.

From this, we claim that
there exists a subsequence
\{f_{k_{j}}\}_{j}
, such that

\begin{split}u_{f}^{(s)}(x)&=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\mathcal{L}_{f_{k_{j}},e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
&=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\mathcal{L}_{f,e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho.\end{split}

(3.8)

For the convenience of the reader,
the technical proof of (
3.8
) can be found in Appendix
A.2
.

The claim in (
3.8
) establishes the desired result in (
3.1
)
also for non continuous functions.

To prove the claim in (
3.2
), we use (
1.1
) and (
1.12
) to see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{f,e,\rho}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{f_{\rho}}^{Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e),Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\frac{x}{\rho}-Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)\right)\cdot e}{|Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)-Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)|}\,f_{\rho}\big(Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)\big)+\frac{\left(Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)-\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\cdot e}{|Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)-Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)|}\,f_{\rho}\big(Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\frac{x}{\rho}-Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)\right)\cdot e}{|Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)-Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)|}\,f\big(\rho Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)\big)+\frac{\left(Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)-\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\cdot e}{|Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)-Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)|}\,f\big(\rho Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{\gamma}\left[\frac{\left(\frac{x}{\rho}-Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)\right)\cdot e}{|Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)-Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)|}\,f\big(Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)\big)+\frac{\left(Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)-\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\cdot e}{|Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)-Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)|}\,f\big(Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e)\big)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{\gamma}{\mathcal{L}}_{f}^{Q^{x/\rho}_{-}(e),Q^{x/\rho}_{+}(e)}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{\gamma}{\mathcal{L}}_{f,e,1}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right).

The claim in (
3.2
) then follows from this and (
3.1
).
This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

From Proposition
2.2
, we know that, under the hypotheses of Theorem
1.4
,
the function defined in (
2.4
) is the unique solution (up to a zero measure subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
) to the problem (
1.16
).
Then, the desired result in Theorem
1.4
follows from this and
Theorem
3.2
.
∎

We now give the proof of the Schwarz result in Theorem
1.8
.

4
Harnack inequality

In this section we provide a new proof of the Harnack inequality for
s
-harmonic functions as stated in Theorem
1.7
.
Our strategy is to use the Fractional Malmheden Theorem to show that this result can be directly inferred from the classical Harnack inequality for harmonic functions.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

For convenience we call
u|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}=f
. Let us first assume that
f\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
. Under this assumption, we can apply Theorem
1.6
and obtain that

u(x)=|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho){u}_{f_{\rho}}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho,

for each
x\in B_{1}
, where
{u}_{f_{\rho}}
has been defined in the statement of Theorem
1.6
. Therefore, we have that

u(0)=c(n,s)|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{{u}_{f_{\rho}}(0)}{\rho(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\,d\rho.

(4.1)

Now we fix
r\in(0,1)
and we consider
x\in B_{r}
. Applying the Harnack inequality for classical harmonic functions to
{u}_{f_{\rho}}
, we have that

{u}_{f_{\rho}}(0)\leqslant\frac{(1+|x|/\rho)^{n-1}}{1-|x|/\rho}{u}_{f\rho}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right).

From this, (
1.13
) and (
4.1
) we obtain that

\begin{split}u(0)\leqslant\;&c(n,s)|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\rho(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\frac{(1+|x|/\rho)^{n-1}}{1-|x|/\rho}\,{u}_{f\rho}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\frac{(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})}{\rho^{2}\,(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}\frac{(\rho+|x|)^{n-1}}{\rho^{n-2}(\rho-|x|)}\,{u}_{f\rho}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\frac{(\rho+|x|)^{n}}{\rho^{n}(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}\,{u}_{f\rho}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\,g(\rho,t)\,{u}_{f_{\rho}}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho,\end{split}

(4.2)

where for convenience we have called
t:=|x|
in the last line and defined

g(\rho,t):=\frac{(\rho+t)^{n}}{\rho^{n}(1-t^{2})^{s}},

with
(\rho,t)\in[1,\infty)\times[0,r]
.

Since
g(\rho,t)
is decreasing in
\rho
and increasing in
t
, we have that

\frac{(1+r)^{n}}{(1-r^{2})^{s}}=\sup_{(\rho,t)\in[1,\infty)\times[0,r]}g(\rho,t).

Therefore, from this, (
1.19
)
and (
4.2
) we obtain that

\displaystyle u(0)\leqslant|\partial B_{1}|\frac{(1+r)^{n}}{(1-r^{2})^{s}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\,{u}_{f_{\rho}}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho=\frac{(1+r)^{n}}{(1-r^{2})^{s}}u(x),

which establishes the first inequality in (
1.20
).

To prove the second inequality in (
1.20
),
we make use of the Harnack inequality for harmonic functions, thus obtaining that

{u}_{f_{\rho}}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\leqslant\frac{1+|x|/\rho}{(1-|x|/\rho)^{n-1}}{u}_{f_{\rho}}(0).

Using this and (
1.13
) into (
4.1
), we find that

\displaystyle u(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(n,s)|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{{u}_{f_{\rho}}(0)}{\rho(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\,d\rho

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle c(n,s)|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\rho(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\frac{(1-|x|/\rho)^{n-1}}{1+|x|/\rho}\,{u}_{f_{\rho}}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(n,s)|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{(\rho-|x|)^{n-1}}{\rho^{n-1}(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}(\rho+|x|)}\,{u}_{f_{\rho}}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\frac{(\rho-|x|)^{n}}{\rho^{n}(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}\,{u}_{f_{\rho}}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho.

Using again
the notation
t:=|x|
, we define the following function

g_{1}(\rho,t):=\frac{(\rho-t)^{n}}{\rho^{n}(1-t^{2})^{s}},

(4.3)

with
(\rho,t)\in[1,\infty)\times[0,r]
, and we see that

u(0)\geqslant|\partial B_{1}|\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)g_{1}(\rho,t)\,{u}_{f_{\rho}}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho.

(4.4)

Since
g_{1}
is increasing in
\rho
, we have that, for all
(\rho,t)\in[1,\infty)\times[0,r]
,

g_{1}(\rho,t)\geqslant g_{1}(1,t)=\frac{(1-t)^{n}}{(1-t^{2})^{s}}=\frac{(1-t)^{n-s}}{(1+t)^{s}}=:g_{2}(t).

Notice also that
g_{2}
is decreasing, and therefore, for all
(\rho,t)\in[1,\infty)\times[0,r]
,

g_{1}(\rho,t)\geqslant g_{2}(r)=\frac{(1-r)^{n-s}}{(1+r)^{s}}=\frac{(1-r)^{n}}{(1-r^{2})^{s}}.

Plugging this information into (
4.4
)
and recalling (
1.19
), we get

u(0)\geqslant|\partial B_{1}|\frac{(1-r)^{n}}{(1-r^{2})^{s}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\,{u}_{f_{\rho}}\left(\frac{x}{\rho}\right)\,d\rho\geqslant\frac{(1-r)^{n}}{(1-r^{2})^{s}}u(x),

which completes the proof
of (
1.20
) under the additional continuity assumption
on
f
.

To deal with the general case, we perform an approximation argument,
whose details go as follows.
If
f\in L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
, we take a sequence
\{f_{k}\}_{k}\subset C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})\cap L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
as in (
2.6
).
Then for
u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}
the two-sided inequality in (
1.20
) holds true, thanks to the previous step.
Also, by (
3.7
), we have the local uniform convergence

\|u_{f_{k}}^{(s)}-u\|_{L_{\textrm{loc}}^{\infty}(B_{1})}\to 0\quad{\mbox{ as }}k\to+\infty,

and therefore we deduce the two sided inequality (
1.20
) also in this case.

It is only left to prove that the constants provided in equation (
1.20
) are optimal. To show this let us fix some direction
e\in\partial B_{1}
, a constant
\epsilon>0
and the function

f_{\epsilon}(y):=\begin{cases}\begin{split}0\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;&\textrm{if}\;\;y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\epsilon}((1+\epsilon)e),\\
(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}\;\;&\textrm{if}\;\;y\in B_{\epsilon}((1+\epsilon)e).\end{split}\end{cases}

Then the function

u_{f_{\epsilon}}^{(s)}(x):=c(n,s)\int_{B_{\epsilon}((\epsilon+1)e)}\frac{(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}{|y-x|^{n}}\,dy

is
s
-harmonic in
B_{1}
, as a consequence of Proposition
2.2
. Therefore, if we fix
x=-re
for
r\in(0,1)
, we have that

\frac{u_{f_{\epsilon}}^{(s)}(0)}{u_{f_{\epsilon}}^{(s)}(-re)}=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{B_{\epsilon}((\epsilon+1)e)}\frac{dy}{|y|^{n}}}{\displaystyle\int_{B_{\epsilon}((\epsilon+1)e)}\frac{(1-r^{2})^{s}}{|y+re|^{n}}\,dy},

and thus, by Lebesgue Differentiation Theorem, we conclude that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{u_{f_{\epsilon}}^{(s)}(0)}{u_{f_{\epsilon}}^{(s)}(-re)}=\frac{(1+r)^{n}}{(1-r^{2})^{s}}.

This proves that the constant on the left hand side inequality in (
1.20
) is optimal.

Similarly,
taking
x=re
, one sees that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{u_{f_{\epsilon}}^{(s)}(re)}{u_{f_{\epsilon}}^{(s)}(0)}=\frac{(1-r^{2})^{s}}{(1-r)^{n}},

which shows that the constant on the right hand side inequality in (
1.20
) is also optimal.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.7
.
∎

only based on elementary computations and standard integral formulas.

More specifically, we aim at showing that

\fint_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{dH^{n-1}_{\omega}}{|x-\rho\omega|^{n}}=\frac{\rho^{2-n}}{\rho^{2}-|x|^{2}}.

(A.2)

Indeed, once (
A.2
) is established, we deduce from it, (
1.13
) and (
2.1
) that

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{\rho}}P(x,y)\,dH^{n-1}_{y}=c(n,s)\;(1-|x|^{2})^{s}\fint_{\partial B_{\rho}}\frac{dH^{n-1}_{y}}{(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}\;|x-y|^{n}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{c(n,s)\;(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}{|\partial B_{1}|\,\rho^{n-1}}\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{\rho^{n-1}\,dH^{n-1}_{\omega}}{(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}\;|x-\rho\omega|^{n}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{c(n,s)\;(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}{(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\fint_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{dH^{n-1}_{\omega}}{|x-\rho\omega|^{n}}=c(n,s)\,\frac{\rho^{2-n}\,(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}{(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}\,(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})}

\displaystyle\qquad=\rho^{1-n}{\mathcal{E}}(x,\rho)

and this would complete the proof of (
A.1
).

Hence, we focus now on proving (
A.2
). To this end,
we use spherical coordinates on
\partial B_{1}
, with
\theta
,
\theta_{1}
,
\dots
,
\theta_{n-3}\in[0,\pi]

and
\theta_{n-2}\in[0,2\pi)
, see e.g. equation (A.23) in
[
5
]

or pages 60-61 in
[
14
]
, which correspond to
\omega=\omega(\theta)
of the form

\begin{cases}\omega_{1}=\sin\theta\,\sin\theta_{1}\dots\sin\theta_{n-3}\sin\theta_{n-2},\\
\omega_{2}=\sin\theta\,\sin\theta_{1}\dots\sin\theta_{n-3}\cos\theta_{n-2},\\
\omega_{3}=\sin\theta\,\sin\theta_{1}\dots\cos\theta_{n-3},\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{n}=\cos\theta\end{cases}

and produce a surface element of the form

\sin^{n-2}\theta\;\sin^{n-3}\theta_{1}\;\dots\;\sin^{2}\theta_{n-4}\;\sin\theta_{n-3}\,d\theta\,d\theta_{1}\,\dots\,d\theta_{n-2}.

Also, up to a rotation, to prove (
A.2
) we can assume that
x=(0,\dots,0,|x|)
.
In this way, we find that

|x-\rho\omega|^{2}=|x|^{2}+\rho^{2}-2\rho x\cdot\omega=|x|^{2}+\rho^{2}-2\rho|x|\,\omega_{n}=|x|^{2}+\rho^{2}-2\rho|x|\,\cos\theta,

whence
it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{dH^{n-1}_{\omega}}{|x-\rho\omega|^{n}}=\int_{{\theta,\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-3}\in[0,\pi]}\atop{\theta_{n-2}\in[0,2\pi)}}\frac{\sin^{n-2}\theta\;\sin^{n-3}\theta_{1}\;\dots\;\sin^{2}\theta_{n-4}\;\sin\theta_{n-3}\,d\theta\,d\theta_{1}\,\dots\,d\theta_{n-2}}{\big(|x|^{2}+\rho^{2}-2\rho|x|\,\cos\theta\big)^{\frac{n}{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad=2\pi\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n-3}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n-j-2}\theta_{j}\;d\theta_{j}\right)\int_{\theta\in[0,\pi]}\frac{\sin^{n-2}\theta\,d\theta}{\big(|x|^{2}+\rho^{2}-2\rho|x|\,\cos\theta\big)^{\frac{n}{2}}}.

Thus, we use the notation
\tau:=\frac{\rho}{|x|}\in(1,+\infty)
and, by Proposition A.9
in
[
5
]
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{dH^{n-1}_{\omega}}{|x-\rho\omega|^{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{|x|^{n}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n-3}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{k}\vartheta\,d\vartheta\right)\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sin^{n-2}\theta\,d\theta}{\big(\tau^{2}+1-2\tau\,\cos\theta\big)^{\frac{n}{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{|x|^{n}\,\tau^{n-2}(\tau^{2}-1)}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n-3}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{k}\vartheta\,d\vartheta\right)\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n-2}\alpha\,d\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{|x|^{n}\,\tau^{n-2}(\tau^{2}-1)}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n-2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{k}\vartheta\,d\vartheta\right).

This and Proposition A.10
in
[
5
]
yield that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{dH^{n-1}_{\omega}}{|x-\rho\omega|^{n}}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}}{|x|^{n}\,\tau^{n-2}(\tau^{2}-1)\;\Gamma\displaystyle\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}=\frac{|\partial B_{1}|}{|x|^{n}\,\tau^{n-2}(\tau^{2}-1)}=\frac{|\partial B_{1}|\;\rho^{2-n}}{\rho^{2}-|x|^{2}}.

The proof of (
A.2
) is thereby complete.

A.2
Proof of (
3.8
)

We recall (
1.13
) and we employ the change of variable
e:=\omega/|\omega|
to see that

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{c(n,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho}{(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{2}}{\rho^{n}(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})}\left(\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},\omega/|\omega|,|\omega|}(x)\,dH_{\omega}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{|y|^{2}}{|y|^{n}(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}(|y|^{2}-|x|^{2})}\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},y/|y|,|y|}(x)\,dy.\end{split}

(A.3)

It also follows from (
2.6
) that, for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
,

\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},y/|y|,|y|}(x)\to\mathcal{L}_{f,y/|y|,|y|}(x)\quad{\mbox{ as }}k\to+\infty.

(A.4)

Now we take
R_{0}\in(1,R)
and we deduce from (
A.3
) that

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{c(n,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{|y|^{2}}{|y|^{n}(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}(|y|^{2}-|x|^{2})}\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},y/|y|,|y|}(x)\,dy\\
&\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{|y|^{2}}{|y|^{n}(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}(|y|^{2}-|x|^{2})}\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},y/|y|,|y|}(x)\,dy.\end{split}

(A.5)

Recalling the computation in (
3.16
),
for
k
large enough we have that

\|\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},y/|y|,|y|}(x)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1})}\leqslant\frac{2R_{0}}{\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})}.

Consequently, using this, (
A.4
) and the Dominated Convergence
Theorem,

\begin{split}&\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{|y|^{2}}{|y|^{n}(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}(|y|^{2}-|x|^{2})}\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},y/|y|,|y|}(x)\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad=\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{|y|^{2}}{|y|^{n}(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}(|y|^{2}-|x|^{2})}\mathcal{L}_{f,y/|y|,|y|}(x)\,dy.\end{split}

(A.6)

Also, we claim that there exists a subsequence
\{f_{k_{j}}\}_{j}
such that

\|\mathcal{L}_{f_{k_{j}},y/|y|,|y|}(x)-\mathcal{L}_{f,y/|y|,|y|}(x)\|_{L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}})}\to 0\quad{\mbox{ as }}j\to+\infty.

(A.7)

To show (
A.7
), we recall (
3.15
)
and we observe that, for every
x\in B_{1}

and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}
,

\begin{split}&\left|\mathcal{L}_{f_{k},y/|y|,|y|}(x)\right|\\
=\;&\left|\frac{\left(\frac{x}{|y|}-Q^{x/|y|}_{-}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)\right)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}}{|Q^{x/|y|}_{+}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)-Q^{x/|y|}_{-}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)|}\,f_{k}\left(|y|Q^{x/|y|}_{+}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)\right)\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{\left(Q^{x/|y|}_{+}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)-\frac{x}{|y|}\right)\cdot\frac{y}{|y|}}{|Q^{x/|y|}_{+}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)-Q^{x/|y|}_{-}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)|}\,f_{k}\left(Q^{x/|y|}_{-}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)\right)\right|\\
\\
\leqslant\;&\frac{R_{0}}{\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}}\bigg[\left|f_{k}\left(|y|Q_{-}^{x/|y|}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)\right)\right|+\left|f_{k}\left(|y|Q_{-}^{x/|y|}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)\right)\right|\bigg].\end{split}

(A.8)

Moreover, by (
2.6
) there exists a subsequence

\{f_{k_{j}}\}_{j}
and a
function
h\in L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})

such that
|f_{k_{j}}(y)|\leqslant h(y)
for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
(see for instance Theorem 4.9 in
[
2
]
).
Therefore, using this information into (
A.8
), we have

\left|\mathcal{L}_{f_{k_{j}},y/|y|,|y|}(x)\right|\leqslant\frac{R_{0}}{\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}}\bigg[h\left(|y|Q_{-}^{x/|y|}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)\right)+h\left(|y|Q_{+}^{x/|y|}\left(\frac{y}{|y|}\right)\right)\bigg]

(A.9)

for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}
.

Now we recall the map
F_{\pm}
defined in (
3.21
), which
is
C^{1}
and invertible, and therefore,
by Theorem 2 in Section 3.3.3 of
[
10
]
and (
3.22
),
we get that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{h\left(|y|Q_{\pm}^{x/|y|}(y/|y|)\right)}{\left|y\right|^{n+2s}}\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{h\left(|y|Q_{\pm}^{x/|y|}(y/|y|)\right)}{\left||y|Q_{\pm}^{x/|y|}(y/|y|)\right|^{n+2s}}\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{h(z)}{|z|^{n+2s}}|\det(DF_{\pm}^{-1}(z))|\,dz\leqslant C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{h(z)}{|z|^{n+2s}}\,dz.

Accordingly, we deduce that

h\left(|y|Q_{\pm}^{x/|y|}(y/|y|)\right)\in L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}).

This, the bound in (
A.9
), the
pointwise convergence in (
A.4
)
and the Dominated Convergence Theorem lead to (
A.7
), as desired.

Hence, putting together (
A.5
), (
A.6
) and (
A.7
),
we obtain that

u_{f}(x)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)\mathcal{L}_{f,e,\rho}(x)\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\,d\rho

for each
x\in B_{1}
, which completes the proof of (
3.8
).

\Box

A.3
Proof of (
3.9
)

The proof of (
3.9
) is similar to the one of (
3.8
). We provide
here the details for the convenience of the reader.

From (
1.13
) with
n=2
and the
change of variable
e:=\omega/|\omega|
,

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{c(2,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)f_{k}(\rho Q^{x/\rho}(e))\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\rho}{(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}f_{k}(\rho Q^{x/\rho}(e))\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\rho^{2}-|x|^{2})(\rho^{2}-1)^{s}}\left(\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}f_{k}(|\omega|Q^{x/|\omega|}(\omega/|\omega|))\,dH_{\omega}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{1}{(|y|^{2}-|x|^{2})(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}}f_{k}(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))\,dy\end{split}

(A.10)

By (
2.6
), we have that, for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
,

f_{k}(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))\to f(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))\quad{\mbox{ as }}k\to+\infty.

(A.11)

Now we take
R_{0}\in(1,R)
and we get
from (
A.10
) that

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{c(2,s)(1-|x|^{2})^{s}}\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)f_{k}(\rho Q^{x/\rho}(e))\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho\\
=\;&\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{1}{(|y|^{2}-|x|^{2})(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}}f_{k}(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{1}{(|y|^{2}-|x|^{2})(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}}f_{k}(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))\,dy\end{split}

(A.12)

Notice that, for
k
large enough,

\|f_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1})}\leqslant\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R}\setminus B_{1})}.

As a consequence,

\begin{split}&\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{1}{(|y|^{2}-|x|^{2})(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}}f_{k}(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad=\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{1}{(|y|^{2}-|x|^{2})(|y|^{2}-1)^{s}}f(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))\,dy\end{split}

(A.13)

Furthemore, recalling (
2.6
)
(see also Theorem 4.9 in
[
2
]
)
we deduce the existence of a subsequence
\{f_{k_{j}}\}_{j}

and of a function
h\in L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
such that

\left|f_{k_{j}}(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y|y|))\right|\leqslant h(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))

(A.14)

for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}
.

Furthermore, we claim that

{\mbox{$h(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))$ belongs
to $L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}})$}}.

(A.15)

Indeed, the function

\begin{split}F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus&B_{R_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}\\
&y\mapsto|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|)\end{split}

is
C^{1}
and invertible. Moreover,
since

\lim_{|y|\to\infty}Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|)=id_{\partial B_{1}},

we find that

\|\det(DF^{-1}(z))\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}})}\leqslant\tilde{C},

for some positive constant
\tilde{C}>0
.

From this and Theorem 2 in Section 3.3.3 of
[
10
]
we have that

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{h(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))}{|y|^{2+2s}}\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{h(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|))}{||y|Q^{x/|y|}(y/|y|)|^{2+2s}}\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{h(z)}{|z|^{{2+2s}}}|\det(DF^{-1}(z))|\,dz\leqslant\tilde{C}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}}\frac{h(z)}{|z|^{{2+2s}}}\,dz,\end{split}

which establishes (
A.15
).

From (
A.11
),
(
A.14
)
and (
A.15
) and the Dominated Convergence
Theorem, we deduce that

\|f_{k_{j}}(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y|y|))-f(|y|Q^{x/|y|}(y|y|))\|_{L_{s}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}})}\to 0\quad{\mbox{ as }}j\to+\infty.

Gathering together this,
(
A.12
)
and (
A.13
),
we conclude that

u_{f}^{(s)}(x)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathcal{E}(x,\rho)f(\rho Q^{x/\rho}(e))\,dH_{e}^{n-1}\right)\,d\rho.

This finishes the proof
of (
3.9
).
\Box
