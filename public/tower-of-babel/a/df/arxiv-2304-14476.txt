Causal State Estimation and the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle
Junxin Chen, Benjamin B. Lane, Su Direkci, Dhruva Ganapathy, Xinghui Yin, Nergis Mavalvala, Yanbei Chen, Vivishek Sudhir

Abstract
The observables of a noisy quantum system can be estimated by appropriately filtering the records of their continuous measurement. Such filtering is relevant for state estimation, and if the filter is causal, also relevant for measurement-based feedback control. It is therefore imperative that a pair of conjugate observables estimated causally satisfy the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. In this letter, we prove this fact — without assuming Markovian dynamics or Gaussian noises, in the presence or absence of feedback control of the system, and where in the feedback loop (inside or outside) the measurement record is accessed. Indeed, causal estimators using the in-loop measurement record can be as precise as those using the out-of-loop record. These results clarify the role of causal estimators to non-Markovian quantum systems, restores the equanimity of in-loop and out-of-loop measurements in their estimation and control, and simplifies future experiments on measurement-based quantum feedback control.



Introduction.

Estimating the state of a system from a noisy measurement record is ubiquitous in engineering and
science. For a classical linear system driven by stationary Gaussian noise, the optimal estimator —
in the sense of minimizing the mean-square error — is given by the Wiener filter

Wiener 1942
;
Kailath 1981
. For prediction problems where only the past measurement record is
available, the Wiener filter is causal and is given in terms of the spectrum of the measurement
record and a model of its cross-spectrum with the state.
Additionally, if the dynamics are Markovian, a state-space model of the system
and its observation is available and the Kalman filter uses that description to produce an equivalent,
more tractable estimate of the state
Kalman 1960
.
State estimation is also a crucial element in optimal control: the separation principle

Wonham 1968
;
Lindquist 1973
asserts that the feedback controller that minimizes a quadratic cost
function of a linear system driven by Gaussian noise can be split into a causal state estimator
and a linear regulator, with any error in the controlled state set by that of the estimator.

These ideas have been fully transposed to quantum systems: the theory of quantum state estimation

Belavkin 1989
;
Barchielli and Belavkin 1991
;
Belavkin 1992
;
Wiseman 1996
;
Doherty et al. 1999
;
Bouten et al. 2004
, feedback control

Wiseman 1994
;
Wiseman and Doherty 2005
;
Genoni et al. 2013
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2010
;
Jacobs 2014
, and the separation principle

Bouten and Handel 2008
have been developed. A central distinction between classical and quantum
systems is that the latter has to obey Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle:
a pair of conjugate observables cannot be determined with arbitrary precision simultaneously, even in principle.
Consequently, it is believed that causal estimation of conjugate observables cannot be simultaneously
more precise than that allowed for the observables themselves.
Violation of this expectation would result in an unphysical conditional state
(i.e. a state estimated through the measurement record)
Doherty et al. 1999
, and,
by the separation principle, the possibility of
transmuting the unphysical conditional state to an unphysical steady state by feedback control.

The purpose of this paper is to prove, in general and explicitly, that the
product of the variance of the errors in the causal estimate of observables
is lower bounded by the minimum variance product of corresponding physical observables allowed by
the Heisenberg uncertainty principle.
In another words, the uncertainties of causal estimation errors respect the uncertainty principle of the corresponding physical observables, and thus the causal conditional state has to be physical.
Prior work guarantees this in Markovian and Gaussian settings through

;
Barchielli and Belavkin 1991
;
Belavkin 1992
;
Doherty et al. 1999
;
Tsang et al. 2009

Statement of problem and main result.

Consider a quantum system, and any pair of observables
\hat{A}(t),\hat{B}(t)
, in the Heisenberg
(or interaction) picture,
which will be the object of estimation via continuous linear measurement.
The uncertainities in them are lower-bounded by
the uncertainty principle

\begin{split}\sigma_{A(t)}^{2}\sigma_{B(t)}^{2}\geq&\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{2}\avg{\comm{\hat{A}(t)}
{\hat{B}(t)}}}^{2}\\
&+\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{2} \avg{\acomm{\hat{A}(t)}{\hat{B}(t)}}}^{2}\\
\geq&\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{2}\avg{\comm{\hat{A}(t)}{\hat{B}(t)]}}}^{2},\end{split}

(1)

where
\sigma_{O}^{2}\equiv\expectationvalue*{\hat{O}^2}
is the variance of the operator
\hat{O}
,

\commutator*{\cdot}{\cdot}
is the commutator, and
\anticommutator*{\cdot}{\cdot}
the anti-commutator.
Here we assume, without loss of generality, that observables are zero-mean.
In passing to the last line, we have also dropped an overall positive term on the right-hand side,
which makes the resulting bound weaker, but has the advantage that for bosonic
(and linearized fermionic
Klein and Marshalek 1991
;
Auerbach 1994
;
Yuan et al. 2022
) systems the lower bound can be state-independent.

The premise of the uncertainty principle is the positivity of the quantum state
\hat{\rho}
:
clearly,
\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{M}(t)^{\dagger}\hat{M}(t)\hat{\rho}]\geq 0
for any operator
\hat{M}(t)
;
in particular, also for
\hat{M}=\hat{A}+\lambda\hat{B}
for any constant
\lambda

Further, the inequality must still hold for the minimum value of its left-hand side as a function of
\lambda
;
this gives
eq.

1
.
So as long as the quantum state is guaranteed to be positive, the uncertainty
relation holds. The problem is that in non-Markovian settings — i.e. where the system may be driven
by non-Markovian noises, its measurements may be contaminated by non-Markovian noises, and/or feedback
maybe non-Markovian — positivity of the conditional state is difficult to extract from models of its evolution.

We therefore analyse the
problem in the interaction picture for the observables of interest, assuming only linearity of measurement
and feedback. In particular, we will show that for causal linear measurement, the estimation errors

\Delta\hat{A},\Delta\hat{B}
(to be defined below) of the observables
\hat{A},\hat{B}

satisfy

\sigma_{\Delta A(t)}^{2}\sigma_{\Delta B(t)}^{2}\geq\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{2} \avg{\comm{\hat{A}(t)}{\hat{B}(t)}}}^{2};

(2)

i.e. causal estimates of conjugate observables are no more precise than the observables themselves.

Proof for case without feedback.

We now consider a setup for quantum state estimation where the system is monitored continuously,
the result of which is described by the measurement record
\hat{Y}

\displaystyle=\hat{A}(t)-\hat{A}_{e}(t)=\hat{A}(t)-(W_{A}*\hat{Y})(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{B}(t)-\hat{B}_{e}(t)=\hat{B}(t)-(W_{B}*\hat{Y})(t),

(7)

which characterize how close the estimators follow the physical operators.
To see this, note that in the Heisenberg picture, the positivity of the
initial

\begin{split}\sigma_{\Delta A(t)}^{2}\sigma_{\Delta B(t)}^{2}\geq\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{2}\avg{\comm{\Delta\hat{A}(t)}{\Delta\hat{B}(t)}}}^{2}.\end{split}

(8)

That is, the estimation errors cannot be determined simultaneously with arbitrary precision.

We will now show that causality of the estimation filters

[

so that the lower bound in

.

Using

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{[\Delta\hat{A}(t),\Delta\hat{B}(t)]}=

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{[\hat{A}(t),\hat{B}(t)]}-\expectationvalue{[\hat{A}(t), \hat{B}_e(t)]}

\displaystyle-\expectationvalue{[\hat{A}_e(t),\hat{B}(t)]}.

(9)

The key result of the theory is Kubo’s formula, which gives the linear response of the average of a system observable

Proof for case with feedback.

We now consider the case where the system is feedback-controlled, and using the in-loop
measurement record
\hat{Y}_{\mathrm{c}}

1

However the open-loop record

In order to use this fact, note that the in-loop and open-loop measurement records,
are related to each other as

(17)

where

is the inverse Fourier transform of

1

Importantly, if

Imagine now estimation based on the in-loop measurement record

\hat{A}_{\mathrm{ec}}(t)=(W_{A}^{\mathrm{c}}*\hat{Y}_{\mathrm{c}})(t)
and
\hat{B}_{\mathrm{ec}}(t)=(W_{B}^{\mathrm{c}}*\hat{Y}_{\mathrm{c}})(t)
, with
W_{A}^{\mathrm{c}}
and
W_{B}^{\mathrm{c}}
the estimation filters in the in-loop case.
Following the same line of reasoning as in the open-loop case, the question of whether the estimated
observables respect the Heisenberg uncertainty principle of the original observables boils down to whether

\expectationvalue*{[\hat{A}(t), \hat{Y}_c(t')]}=0
for
t\geq t^{\prime}
. Clearly,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{[\hat{A}(t), \hat{Y}_c(t')]}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K_{c}(t^{\prime}-\tau)\expectationvalue{[\hat{A}(t), \hat{Y}_\t{o} (\tau)]}\differential{\tau}.

We consider the integrand in two complementary intervals,

and

. In the former region,

is causal.
In the latter region, since

, we have that

\expectationvalue*{[\hat{A}(t), \hat{Y}_\t{o} (\tau)]}=0

In sum, the integral is zero in both intervals due to causality.
Thus
\expectationvalue*{[\hat{A}(t), \hat{Y}_c(t')]}
vanishes as long as

.
A similar argument holds for
\expectationvalue*{[\hat{B}(t), \hat{Y}_c(t')]}
. Thus

\expectationvalue*{[\hat{A}(t),\hat{Y}_c(t')]}=0=\expectationvalue*{[\hat{B}(t), \hat{Y}_c(t')]}

just as in the open-loop case.
Thus the in-loop causal estimation errors respect the uncertainty principle of the corresponding physical observables just as in the open-loop case.

Effect of feedback on estimation error.

The in-loop measurement record
\hat{Y}_{\mathrm{c}}
is often deemed untrustworthy for state
estimation. Historically, this view originated from the apparent violation of the Heisenberg uncertainty
principle by the in-loop field in linear measurement-based feedback control of
optical fields
Yamamoto et al. 1986
. This behavior,
called “noise squashing”
Wiseman 1999
, arises because fields inside a feedback
loop are not freely propagating and so need not satisfy the canonical commutation relations

Shapiro et al. 1987
;
Taubman et al. 1995
.

In fact, if a model of the feedback loop is available, then state estimation using the
in-loop measurement record is as accurate as an estimate based on the out-of-loop record.
We can always write the system observable in the presence of feedback,

\hat{A}_{\mathrm{c}}
, as the sum of the open-loop one
\hat{A}_{\mathrm{o}}
plus a feedback
term:

\hat{A}_{\mathrm{c}}(t)=\hat{A}_{\mathrm{o}}(t)+\hat{A}_{\mathrm{fb}}(t)=\hat{A}_{\mathrm{o}}(t)+(K*\hat{Y}_{\mathrm{c}})(t),

(19)

where
K
is a causal feedback control filter.
Now consider two filters
W_{A}^{i}[\omega]

(i=\mathrm{c,o})
, one estimating the observable
\hat{A}
from
the in-loop record and the other from the out-of-loop record.
The respective errors are
\Delta\hat{A}_{i}(t)=\hat{A}_{i}(t)-(W_{A}^{i}*\hat{Y}_{i})(t)
.
It is straightforward to show that if the in-loop filter is chosen to be

W_{A}^{\mathrm{c}}[\omega]=\frac{W_{A}^{\mathrm{o}}[\omega]}{K_{\mathrm{c}}[\omega]}+K[\omega]=(1-W_{A}^{\mathrm{o}}[\omega]M[\omega])K[\omega]+W_{A}^{\mathrm{o}}[\omega],

then
\Delta\hat{A}_{\mathrm{c}}=\Delta\hat{A}_{\mathrm{o}}
.
Note that if the feedback loop and
W_{A}^{\mathrm{o}}
are stable and causal, so is
W_{A}^{\mathrm{c}}
.
Therefore by proper filter design, state estimation can be performed using the
in-loop measurement record without loss of fidelity.

Example of a structurally damped mechanical oscillator.

A canonical example of non-Markovian behavior of a subject of contemporary interest to quantum state estimation is a
structurally damped mechanical oscillator

;

Such a system is described by the linear response of its displacement

The thermal displacement fluctuations of the oscillator, as given by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem

The structural damping model is unphysical and inconsistent if the loss angle

is
frequency independent. Unphysical, because the

\real\chi^{-1}[\Omega]=\real\chi^{-1}[-\Omega]

The simplest example of such a loss angle is velocity-propotional damping, which however

Conclusion.

We have proved — with or without feedback, and without invoking any Markovian or Gaussian character of the system dynamics or measurement —
that the errors in causal estimation of quantum observables from a linear continuous measurement
respects the Heisenberg uncertainty principle for the corresponding physical observables.
We utilize linear response theory, widely applicable to current experimental measurement-based quantum control.
Furthermore, in the scenario with feedback control, we clarify that despite “noise squashing”,
the in-loop measurement record can provide as faithful an estimate of an observable as the
out-of-loop record. As a matter of practice, this vastly simplifies experiments and extends the reach of
quantum state estimation to non-Markovian scenarios
Groeblacher et al. 2015
;
Meng et al. 2022
;
Whittle et al. 2021
.
Importantly, this insight eliminates the compromise in measurement efficiency
that is required in having two simultaneous measurements on the same system.
