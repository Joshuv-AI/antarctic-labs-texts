Mott’s law for the Miller–Abrahams random resistor network and for Mott’s random walk
Alessandra Faggionato

Abstract
. Mott’s variable range hopping (v.r.h.) is the phonon-induced hopping of electrons in disordered solids (as doped semiconductors) within the regime of strong Anderson localization. It was introduced by N. Mott to explain the anomalous low temperature conductivity decay in dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , corresponding now to the so called Mott’s law. We provide a rigorous derivation of this Physics law for two effective models of Mott v.r.h.: the Miller-Abrahams random resistor network and Mott’s random walk. We also determine the constant multiplying the power of the inverse temperature in the exponent in Mott’s law, which was an open problem also on a heuristic level. Our proof uses also our previous results of stochastic homogenization and percolation for the above models. The percolation results, and therefore also our derivation of Mott’s law, are exhaustive when the energy marks have a fixed sign. For energy marks with different signs we have derived the low temperature behavior of the critical conductance, while the proof of Mott’s law still works if the expected percolation property of left-right crossings in the supercritical phase is assumed as an Ansatz. Keywords : marked simple point process, Poisson point process, Mott’s random walk, Miller–Abrahams random resistor network, Mott’s law, percolation, stochastic homogenization. AMS 2010 Subject Classification : 60G55, 82B43, 82D30



1.
Introduction

Mott’s variable range hopping is a mechanism of phonon–assisted electron transport taking place in amorphous solids (as doped semiconductors) in the regime of strong Anderson localization
[
1
,
29
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
. It has been introduced by N. Mott in order to explain the anomalous
decay of the conductivity at low temperature
[
27
,
28
]
.

Calling
x
the impurity positions in the doped semiconductor, the electron Hamiltonian has exponentially localized quantum eigenstates with localization centers
x
and corresponding energy
E_{x}
close to the Fermi level, set equal to zero in what follows. At low temperature phonons induce transitions between the localized eigenstates, the rate of which can be calculated by the Fermi golden rule
[
26
,
34
]
. In the simplification of spinless electrons, the resulting rate for an electron to hop from
x
to the unoccupied site
y
is then given by (cf.
[
1
, Eq. (3.6)]
)

\exp\Big\{-\frac{2}{\gamma}|x-y|-\beta\{E_{x}-E_{y}\}_{+}\Big\}\,.

(1)

In (
1
)
\gamma
is the localization length,
\beta=1/kT
is the inverse temperature and
\{a\}_{+}:=\max\{0,a\}
.

The above set
of impurity locations
can be modeled by a random simple point process (SPP), marked by random variables
E_{x}
(called energy marks) which are usually taken i.i.d. with some common distribution
\nu
. The physically relevant distributions for inorganic media are of the form
\nu(dE)=c\,|E|^{\alpha}dE
with finite support
[-A,A]
for some exponent
\alpha\geq 0

[
34
]
. Mott’s law
[
27
,
28
,
29
,
34
]
then predicts that, for
d\geq 2
, the DC conductivity matrix
\sigma(\beta)
of the medium decays to zero when
\beta\to+\infty
as

\sigma(\beta)\approx A(\beta)\exp\bigl\{-\kappa\,\beta^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha+1+d}}\bigr\}\,,

(2)

where the prefactor matrix
A(\beta)
exhibits a negligible
\beta
–dependence (to simplify here the exposition, we restrict just now to isotropic media).
Strictly speaking, Mott derived the above asymptotics for
\alpha=0
, while Efros and Shklovskii derived its extension.
For
d=1
the DC conductivity presents an Arrenhius–type decay as
\beta\to+\infty
for all
\alpha\geq 0

[
23
]
, i.e.

\sigma(\beta)\approx A(\beta)\exp\bigl\{-\kappa\beta\}\,.

(3)

Due to localization one can treat the above electron conduction by a hopping process of classical particles (see also
[
2
,
3
]
), thus leading anyway to a complicate simple exclusion process due to the Pauli blocking. The reversible measure of the exclusion process is the Fermi-Dirac distribution (i.e. the Bernoulli product probability measure such that the probability of having a particle at
x
is proportional to
e^{-\beta(E_{x}-c)}
,
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
being the chemical potential).
Effective simplified models are given by the Miller–Abrahams (MA) random resistor network
[
1
,
26
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
and by Mott’s random walk
[
18
]
.
The MA random resistor network has nodes
x
and, between any pair of nodes
x\not=y
, it has an electrical filament of conductivity

c_{x,y}:=\exp\Big\{-\frac{2}{\gamma}|x-y|-\frac{\beta}{2}(|E_{x}|+|E_{y}|+|E_{x}-E_{y}|)\Big\}\,.

(4)

Mott’s random walk is the continuous–time random walk with
states given by the impurity locations
and probability rate for a jump from
x
to
y
given by (
4
). We point out that the r.h.s. of (
4
) corresponds to the leading term for
\beta
large of (
1
) multiplied by the probability in the Fermi-Dirac distribution that
x
and
y
are, respectively, occupied and unoccupied by an electron
(see
[
1
, Eq. (3.7)]
). A more detailed discussion on the MA resistor network and Mott’s random walk is provided in Sections
4
and
5
.

The original derivation of the laws (
2
) and (
3
) is rather heuristic. More robust arguments have been proposed in the Physics literature (see
[
1
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
), in particular for the case
d\geq 2
. Rigorous bounds in agreement with (
2
) and (
3
) have beed derived in
[
6
,
14
,
15
,
18
]
(see Section
5
). The characterization of the constant
\kappa
was an open problem, even at heuristic level (see
[
32
, Chapter 5]
).

Let us describe our main contributions. First of all we point out that, by means of homogenization theory, under very general assumptions in
[
14
]

we proved that the conductivity matrix
\sigma(\beta)
of the MA random resistor network equals the asymptotic diffusion matrix
D(\beta)
of Mott’s random walk times the intensity
\rho
of the SSP (i.e. the mean impurity density). Hence, all results presented below for
\sigma(\beta)
hold also for
D(\beta)
.

In Section
7
we take
d\geq 2
and consider as marked SPP a homogeneous Poisson point process (shortly, PPP) with intensity
\rho
and i.i.d. energy marks with common distribution
\nu
which, in some neighborhood of the origin,

has density proportional to
|E|^{\alpha}
or
E^{\alpha}\mathds{1}(E\geq 0)
, where
\alpha\geq 0
. In particular, for some
C_{0}>0
, near the origin
\nu

has the form
(\alpha+1)C_{0}^{-\alpha-1}2^{-1}|E|^{\alpha}dE
(case
\nu\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}}
) or
(\alpha+1)C_{0}^{-\alpha-1}E^{\alpha}\mathds{1}(E\geq 0)dE
(case
\nu\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{V}^{+}_{\alpha}}
).
We first consider
the critical conductance
c_{c}(\beta,\rho,\nu)
. According to
[
1
]

c_{c}(\beta,\rho,\nu)
is such that the graph obtained from the MA resistor network by keeping only the filaments
\{x,y\}

with conductivity
c_{x,y}>C
a.s. does not percolate if
C>c_{c}(\beta,\rho,\nu)
, while it a.s. percolates if
c_{c}(\beta,\rho,\nu)>C
. In Theorem
7.5
and its Corollary
7.6
we show that the limit

\chi(\rho,\nu):=\lim_{\beta\to+\infty}\beta^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha+1+d}}\ln c_{c}(\beta,\rho,\nu)\,

exists and compute it.
We then prove Mott’s law (
2
) for
\nu\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{V}^{+}_{\alpha}}
(cf. Theorem
7.5
and Corollary
7.12
), showing the the constant
\kappa
in (

) indeed equals
-\chi(\rho,\nu)
. It turns out that

-\kappa=\chi(\rho,\nu)=\bigl({\lambda_{c}^{+}(\alpha)C_{0}^{\alpha+1}/\rho}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1+d}}\,,

(5)

where
\lambda_{c}^{+}(\alpha)\in(0,+\infty)
is a constant of percolation type depending only from
\alpha
and
d
(although the dependence on
d
does not appear in the notation). It has the following characterization. Consider the MA resistor network on a PPP with density
\lambda
and i.i.d. energy marks in
[0,1]
having distribution
\nu_{1,\alpha}^{+}
with density proportional to
E^{\alpha}
on
[0,1]
. Afterwards keep only filaments with conductivity at least
e^{-1}
at inverse temperature
\beta=1
. Then the resulting network a.s. percolates for
\lambda>\lambda_{c}^{+}(\alpha)
and a.s. does not percolate for
\lambda<\lambda_{c}^{+}(\alpha)
.

Our proof of the above Mott’s law relies also on our previous results in percolation
[
16
,
17
]
(obtained in collaboration with H.A. Mimun) and stochastic homogenization
[
14
]
. Having expressed
\sigma(\beta)
in terms of the effective homogenized matrix in
[
14
]
, we can use the variational characterization of the latter to obtain upper bounds on
\sigma(\beta)
via suitable test functions. On the other hand, being
\sigma(\beta)
the limit of the suitably rescaled conductivity of finite volume resistor networks as described in Section
4
, we can use Rayleigh’s monotonicity law to lower bound

\sigma(\beta)
by thinning the finite volume resistor networks when having a good knowledge about the statistics of their vertex-disjoint left-right (LR) crossings. The procedure is anyway different (and much refined) from the one in
[
15
,
18
]
and now our upper and lower bounds on
\sigma(\beta)
asymptotically match as
\beta\to+\infty
(thanks also to suitable rescaling arguments). The test functions and thinned resistor networks used in
[
15
,
18
]
, respectively, are of different nature, not optimal, while the ones presented here are inspired by the paradigm of Critical Path Analysis
[
1
]
and require a detailed knowledge of a suitable percolation model investigated separately in
[
16
,
17
]
.
The overall derivation of Mott’s law has required (and motivated) the results derived in
[
14
,
16
,
17
]
(although we treated there larger classes of models).

The analysis of LR crossings in the supercritical phase provided in
[
17
]
uses the FKG inequality which is satisfied when the energy marks have a fixed sign (case
\nu\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{V}^{+}_{\alpha}}
) but is violated otherwise (case
\nu\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}}
), as discussed in Appendix
C
. Once the results of
[
17
]
are extended to the MA resistor network with energy marks of arbitrary signs, then our analysis here provides Mott’s law (
2
) also for the case
\nu\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}}
. Hence, in Corollary
7.12
we obtain Mott’s law for
\nu\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}}
under a suitable Ansatz on the LR crossings. In this case
(
5
) is valid with the constant
\lambda_{c}^{+}(\alpha)
replaced by
\lambda_{c}(\alpha)
. The definition of
\lambda_{c}(\alpha)
is the same of
\lambda_{c}^{+}(\alpha)
with exception that the energy marks have value in
[-1,1]
with distribution
\nu_{1,\alpha}
having density proportional to
|E|^{\alpha}
on
[-1,1]
.
The above Ansatz is used to derive a lower bound in (
2
) for
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
, while the upper bound is proved without any Ansatz (see Theorems
7.7
and
7.11
).
We point out that when the intensity of the PPP is large enough the above Ansatz can be proved by stochastic domination with the Poisson Boolean model (see Appendix
A
for this domination).We stress that also the spinless modeling common in the Physics literature is a simplification of the realistic model with spins.

Another main contribution is presented in Section
8
and comes from the investigation of the universality in Mott’s law as Physics law. The derivation of the results
commented above
holds in good part for generic marked SPPs, and not only for the ones obtained by marking a PPP by i.i.d. random variables. As the reader will see, a central role is played by a mechanism of thinning and rescaling which ultimately captures the backbone of filaments in the MA resistor network mainly contributing to the electron transport
(see the discussion at the end of Section
8
where an overview of the main strategy is provided)
. In particular, at inverse temperature
\beta
, just points
x
with marks
E_{x}
in a suitable window
[-E(\beta),E(\beta)]
give the main contribution to transport.
In Theorem
8.2
we show the following. Let us start with a generic
ergodic stationary SPP with i.i.d. energy marks having distribution
\nu
which asymptotically behaves as
\nu^{+}_{1,\alpha}
or
\nu_{1,\alpha}
while approaching the origin. Then
the rescaled set of marked points
\left(x/(\beta E(\beta)),E_{x}/E(\beta)\right)
with
|E_{x}|\leq E(\beta)

converges to a PPP with some intensity
\lambda
and with i.i.d. energy marks
having distribution respectively
\nu^{+}_{1,\alpha}
and
\nu_{1,\alpha}
(the same random geometric structure entering in the definition of
\lambda_{c}^{+}(\alpha)
and
\lambda_{c}(\alpha)
).
Hence, also when starting from a non Poissonian distribution of impurities and from a more general energy distribution
\nu
, at low temperature the above marked PPP emerges and the contribution to transport of the details of the original distribution progressively vanishes.

As next steps we aim to derive the scaling limit (

and prove the above Ansatz on LR crossings for energy marks of different signs. These problems are the object of our present ongoing work.

Some preliminaries on marked simple point processes on

In what follows, given a topological space

10

Due to the above interpretation of

\tau_{x}\hat{\omega}:=\{x_{i}-x\}\;\text{ if }\;\hat{\omega}=\{x_{i}\}\,.

Each translation

A set

For a stationary SPP with intensity

In order to introduce the marked simple point processes,

{\rm PPP}[\rho](A)
is the probability of the event
A\in\mathcal{B}(\hat{\Omega})
for the homogeneous Poisson point process with intensity
\rho
, and similarly for
{\rm PPP}[\rho,\nu](A)
with
A\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
.

We recall that a SPP
\hat{\xi}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a PPP
[\rho]
whenever
(i) for mutually disjoint Borel subsets
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the random variables
\hat{\xi}(A_{1}),\hat{\xi}(A_{2}),\dots,\hat{\xi}(A_{k})
are independent;
(ii) for any bounded set
A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
\hat{\xi}(A)
is a Poisson random variable with parameter
\rho\,\ell(A)
.
In particular, it holds
E[\xi(A)]=\hat{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{\omega}(A)]=\rho\ell(A)
for any
A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.
We recall that PPP
[\rho]
and PPP
[\rho,\nu]
are stationary and ergodic
(see e.g.
[
24
, Prop. 8.13]
, whose proof can be generalized to the marked case)
.

The families of PPP’s and marked PPP’s are closed w.r.t. some fundamental operations, which play a central role in Mott’s law:

Proposition 2.7
.

The following holds:

(i)

The
p
–thinning of

{\rm PPP}[\rho]
is given by

{\rm PPP}[p\rho]
.

(ii)

By sampling
\hat{\omega}\in\hat{\Omega}
according to
{\rm PPP}[\rho]
, the SPP obtained by rescaling each
x\in\hat{\omega}
into
x/\ell
for some fixed
\ell>0
has law
{\rm PPP}[\ell^{d}\rho]
.

(iii)

Fixed
\gamma>0
, by sampling
\omega\in\Omega
according to
{\rm PPP}[\rho,\nu]
, the marked SPP
\omega_{\gamma}:=\{(x,E_{x})\,:\,|E_{x}|\leq\gamma\}
has law
{\rm PPP}[p\rho,\nu_{\gamma}]
, where
p:=\nu([-\gamma,\gamma])
and
\nu_{\gamma}
is the probability measure
\nu
conditioned to the event
[-\gamma,\gamma]
.

Proof.

Properties (i) and (ii) are well known and follow easily from the above characterization of
{\rm PPP}[\rho]
.
Property (iii) follows from Lemma
9.3
in Section
9
and from Item (i).
∎

3.
Palm distribution and effective homogenized matrix
D(\beta)

In this section we introduce the effective homogenized matrix
D(\beta)
. As discussed later, it is intimately related to the conductivity properties of the MA random resistor network and to the diffusion properties of Mott’s random walk. To define
D(\beta)
we will first need to introduce the Palm distribution.

In what follows,
\beta
is a positive number which, in the applications, will correspond to the inverse temperature.

Definition 3.1

(Conductance field)
.

Given
\omega\in\Omega
we associate to each
unordered
pair
\{x,y\}
with
x,y\in\widehat{\omega}
and
x\not=y
the conducivity

c_{x,y}(\omega,\beta):=\exp\bigl\{-|x-y|-\beta(|E_{x}|+|E_{y}|+|E_{x}-E_{y}|)\}\,.

(9)

Note that (
9
) corresponds to (
4
) by taking
\gamma:=2
and by replacing
\beta/2
in (
4
) with
\beta
. Trivially, by suitably rescaling distance and energy, the results obtained for (
9
) can be extended to (
4
).

Trivially, we have

|E_{x}|+|E_{y}|+|E_{x}-E_{y}|=\begin{cases}2\max\big\{|E_{x}|,|E_{y}|\big\}&\text{ if }E_{x}\cdot E_{y}\geq 0\,,\\
2|E_{x}-E_{y}|&\text{ if }E_{x}\cdot E_{y}\leq 0\,.\end{cases}

(10)

To simplify the notation below, it is convenient to set

c_{x,x}(\omega,\beta):=0\qquad\forall\omega\in\Omega\,,\;x\in\widehat{\omega}\,.

Definition 3.2

(Palm distribution)
.

Let
{\mathbb{P}}
be the law of a stationary marked SPP on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with finite and positive intensity
\rho
. The Palm distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}
associated to
{\mathbb{P}}
is defined as the unique probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B}(\Omega))
concentrated on the Borel set
\Omega_{0}:=\{\omega\in\Omega\,:\,0\in\widehat{\omega}\}
and given by

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}(A)=\frac{1}{m}\int_{\Omega}d{\mathbb{P}}(\omega)\int_{[0,1]^{d}}d\widehat{\omega}(x)\mathds{1}_{A}(\tau_{x}\omega)\,,\qquad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\,.

(11)

Above, and in the rest, we denote by
\mathds{1}_{A}
or
\mathds{1}(A)
(according to better readability) the characteristic function of the event
A
.

Equation (
11
) is a special case of Campbell’s formula. A more general Campbell’s formula is the following (cf.
[
10
, Eq. (12.2.4)]
,
[
20
, Thm. 1.2.8]
): for any nonnegative Borel function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\Omega\to[0,\infty)
and for any function
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\Omega,dx\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}})
it holds

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}dx\int_{\Omega_{0}}d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}(\omega)f(x,\omega)=\frac{1}{m}\int_{\Omega}d{\mathbb{P}}(\omega)\sum_{x\in\widehat{\omega}}f(x,\tau_{x}\omega)\,.

(12)

As discussed in
[
10
, Chapter 12]
, roughly
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}={\mathbb{P}}(\cdot\,|\,0\in\widehat{\omega})
.

When
{\mathbb{P}}=
PPP
[\rho,\nu]
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}
can be realized as follows. Sample
\omega\in\Omega
with law
{\mathbb{P}}
and sample, independently from
\omega
, the number
E\in{\mathbb{R}}
with law
\nu
. Then
the configuration
\omega\cup\{(0,E)\}
has law
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}
(see
[
10
, Chapter 12]
, in addition note that
{\mathbb{P}}(0\in\widehat{\omega})=0
, hence the probability that in this construction the origin appears with two different marks is zero).

If
{\mathbb{P}}
is the
\nu
–randomization of
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
(i.e.
{\mathbb{P}}=\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
) and
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
is the law of a stationary SPP with finite and positive intensity
\rho
, then it is simple to check
that the Palm distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}
is the
\nu
–randomization of the Palm distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}
associated to
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
(i.e.
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}=({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}})_{\nu}
).
Indeed we recall that,
similarly to (
11
),
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}
is defined as the
unique probability measure on
(\hat{\Omega},\mathcal{B}(\hat{\Omega}))
concentrated on the Borel set
\hat{\Omega}_{0}:=\{\hat{\omega}\in\hat{\Omega}\,:\,0\in\hat{\omega}\}
and given by

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\hat{\mathbb{P}}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}(A)=\frac{1}{m}\int_{\hat{\Omega}}d\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{\omega})\int_{[0,1]^{d}}d\hat{\omega}(x)\mathds{1}_{A}(\tau_{x}\hat{\omega})\,,\qquad\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\hat{\Omega})\,.

(13)

Definition 3.3

(Effective homogenized matrix)
.

Let
{\mathbb{P}}
be the law of a stationary marked SPP on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with finite and positive intensity
\rho
and let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}
be the associated Palm distribution. Suppose that

\int_{\Omega_{0}}d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}(\omega)\sum_{x\in\widehat{\omega}}c_{0,x}(\omega,\beta)|x|^{2}<+\infty\,.

(14)

Then
the
effective homogenized matrix

D(\beta)
is
the unique
d\times d
nonnegative symmetric matrix such that

a\cdot D(\beta)a=\inf_{f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{P}}_{0})}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{0}}d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{P}}^{\hskip 0.36989pt0}}(\omega)\sum_{x\in\widehat{\omega}}c_{0,x}(\omega,\beta)\left(a\cdot x-\nabla_{x}f(\omega)\right)^{2}\,,

(15)

where
\nabla_{x}f(\omega):=f(\tau_{x}\omega)-f(\omega)
.

Note that condition (
14
) guarantees that the r.h.s. of (
15
) is finite (take
f\equiv 0
there).

5.
Mott’s random walk

Given
\omega\in\Omega
, Mott’s random walk (introduced in
[
18
]
) is the continuous-time
Markov chain with state space
\widehat{\omega}
and with jump rates
c_{x,y}(\omega,\beta)
: once arrived at the site
x\in\widehat{\omega}
the random walk waits an exponential time of rate
c_{x}(\omega,\beta):=\sum_{y\in\widehat{\omega}}c_{x,y}(\omega)
, afterwards it jumps to a different site
y\in\widehat{\omega}
with probability
c_{x,y}(\omega,\beta)/c_{x}(\omega,\beta)
(recall that
c_{x,x}(\omega,\beta):=0
). As random walk in a random environment, Mott’s random walk belongs to the class of random conductance models
[
5
]
.

To have a well defined Markov chain we assume that
c_{x}(\omega,\beta)<+\infty
for all
x\in\widehat{\omega}
and that no explosion occurs. Due to
[
13
, Lemma 3.5]
and the discussion
in
[
13
, Section 5.4]
devoted to Mott’s random walk, the above conditions are satisfied for
{\mathbb{P}}
–a.a.
\omega
if
{\mathbb{P}}
is a stationary ergodic marked SPP with finite and positive intensity
\rho
satisfying (
23
) and
{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\omega}([0,1]^{d})^{2}]<+\infty
.

The effective homogenized matrix
D(\beta)
corresponds to the asymptotic diffusion matrix of Mott’s random walk as formalized
Fact
5.1
below. We first introduce some notation.
Given
\varepsilon>0
we write
(P^{\varepsilon}_{\omega,t})_{t\geq 0}
for the
L^{2}(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{\omega})
–Markov semigroup associated to the random walk
(\varepsilon X^{\omega}_{\varepsilon^{-2}t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\varepsilon\widehat{\omega}
, where
\mu^{\varepsilon}_{\omega}:=\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\widehat{\omega}}\delta_{\varepsilon x}
. Simply
P^{\varepsilon}_{\omega,t}f(x)
is the expectation of
f(\varepsilon X^{\omega}_{\varepsilon^{-2}t})
when the random walk starts at
\varepsilon x
.
Similarly we write
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for the Markov semigroup on
L^{2}(\rho dx)
associated to the (possibly degenerate) Brownian motion on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with diffusion matrix
2D(\beta)
.
The following fact is a very special application of
a much more general result given by
[
13
, Theorem 4.4]
:

Fact 5.1

(A. Faggionato
[
13
]
)
.

Let
{\mathbb{P}}
be a stationary ergodic marked SPP with finite and positive intensity
\rho
. Suppose in addition that
{\mathbb{P}}
satisfies (
23
)
and that

{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\omega}([0,1]^{d})^{2}]<+\infty
. Then for
{\mathbb{P}}
–a.a.
\omega
, given
t\geq 0
and
f\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
it holds

L^{2}(\mu^{\varepsilon}_{\omega})\ni P^{\varepsilon}_{\omega,t}f\to P_{t}f\in L^{2}(\rho dx)
.

The precise meaning of the convergence
P^{\varepsilon}_{\omega,t}f\to P_{t}f
is recalled in
[
13
, Section 3.5]
.
The above assumptions on the law
{\mathbb{P}}
are the same of the ones in Fact
4.6
(with exception that here we do not require that
e_{1}
is an eigenvector of
D(\beta)
). Hence the comments following Fact
4.6
can be applied to the present setting
as well
.

Stronger form of CLTs or even invariance principles (annealed or quenched) have been proved under stronger conditions (we stress that the basin of application of Fact
5.1
is
very large
).
We recall that the annealed invariance principle of Mott’s random walk has been obtained in
[
18
]
. The quenched invariance principle has been obtained in
[
6
]
for
d=1
and in
[
7
]
for

. In the above papers, the hypotheses also
imply
the non-degeneracy of the diffusion matrix.

Bounds on the diffusion matrix in agreement with Mott’s law have been obtained under suitable assumptions in
[
15
, Thm. 1]
and
[
18
, Thm. 1]
for
d\geq 2
, while bounds
in agreement with an Arrhenius-type behavior have been obtained in

[
6
, Thm. 1.2]
for

. In particular
these
bounds are of the form

\displaystyle c_{1}\exp\bigl\{-c_{1}^{\prime}\,\beta^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha+d+1}}\bigr\}\mathbb{I}\leq D(\beta)\leq c_{2}\exp\bigl\{-c_{2}^{\prime}\,\beta^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha+d+1}}\bigr\}\mathbb{I}\qquad

\displaystyle(d\geq 2)\,,

\displaystyle c_{1}\exp\bigl\{-c_{1}^{\prime}\,\beta\bigr\}\leq D(\beta)\leq c_{2}\exp\bigl\{-c_{2}^{\prime}\,\beta\}\qquad

\displaystyle(d=1)\,,

for suitable
\beta
–independent positive constants
c_{1},c_{1}^{\prime},c_{2},c_{2}^{\prime}
.

Under Fact
4.6
, as pointed out in
[

, Corollary 3.1]
, the above bounds imply similar bounds for
\sigma(\beta)/\rho

8.
Main results concerning Universality

The results presented in this section hold for all dimensions
d\geq 1
.

The derivation of the results presented in the previous section holds in good part for generic marked SPPs. As the reader will see, in the derivation of the upper and lower bounds in agreement with Mott’s law (cf. Sections
12
and
13

We show here that, also when starting with a marked SPP not of the form

{\rm PPP}[\rho,\nu^{+}_{1,\alpha}]
or
{\rm PPP}[\rho,\nu_{1,\alpha}]

8.1.
Universality and strategy of the proofs: an overview

We now explain more in detail how Theorem
8.2
is related with universality of Mott’s law, giving also an overview of the strategy in the derivation of Theorems
7.7
and
7.11
.

Let us first observe that, given
E(\beta)
and
\zeta(\beta)
, by Rayleigh monotonicity theorem we decrease the infinite volume conductivity
\sigma(\beta)
if we erase filaments in the MA resistor network, in particular if we keep nodes
x
with
E_{x}\in[-E(\beta),E(\beta)]
and put a filament of conductance
c_{x,y}(\omega,\beta)
for any survived nodes
x\not=y
with
c_{x,y}(\omega,\beta)\geq e^{-\zeta(\beta)}
. Note that
this thinned resistor network is simply the weighted graph
\mathcal{G}[\zeta(\beta),\beta](\omega_{E(\beta)})
with weights
c_{x,y}(\omega,\beta)
. We further decrease the infinite volume conductivity if we replace the weights
c_{x,y}(\omega,\beta)
by
e^{-\zeta(\beta)}
. We now restrict to the case
E(\beta)=\zeta(\beta)/\beta
. Then, due to
Lemma

10.1
below, the map
x\mapsto x/\zeta(\beta)
gives an isomorphism of the graph
\mathcal{G}[\zeta(\beta),\beta](\omega_{E(\beta)})
with the graph
\mathcal{G}[1,1](\omega_{\zeta(\beta),\beta})
, where
\omega_{\zeta(\beta),\beta}
is defined as (
43
) with
\zeta(\beta)
instead of
\ell(\beta)
. Hence, by the above rescaling, we get a lower bound of
\sigma(\beta)
as
e^{-\zeta(\beta)}
times a suitable functional of the graph
\mathcal{G}[1,1](\omega_{\zeta(\beta),\beta})
(this is formalized in Lemma
13.2
below). This arguments hold in full generality, and do not restrict to
\nu
-randomized PPPs.

On the other hand, by using the variational formula (
15
) and Fact
4.6
, we can upper bound
D(\beta)_{1,1}
and therefore
\sigma(\beta)
in terms of suitable expectations of functions of the graph
\mathcal{G}[\zeta(\beta),\beta](\omega)
. See in particular Lemma
12.2
, where
C[\zeta,\beta]
is the cluster containing the origin in
\mathcal{G}[\zeta(\beta),\beta)(\omega)
. On the other hand, in Lemma
12.4
we show that the above expectations can be written in terms of expectations of functions of the graph
\mathcal{G}[\zeta(\beta),\beta](\omega_{E(\beta)})
. Again these functions can be thought in terms of
\mathcal{G}[1,1](\omega_{\zeta(\beta),\beta})
by the above isomorphism and
again this derivation does not restrict to
\nu
-randomized PPPs.

The idea is then to apply the above machinery by taking
\zeta(\beta)
as
\ell(\beta)
of Theorem
8.2
with
\lambda<\lambda_{c}^{*}(\alpha)
for the lower bound of
\sigma(\beta)
and with
\lambda>\lambda^{*}_{c}(\alpha)
for the upper bound of
\sigma(\beta)
, when the marked SPP
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
is as in Theorem
8.2
and
\lambda^{*}_{c}(\alpha)
equals
\lambda^{+}_{c}(\alpha)
or
\lambda_{c}(\alpha)

according to the limiting measure in Item (i) of the above theorem.

In both cases, Theorem
8.2
implies that the marked SPP
\omega_{\zeta(\beta),\beta}
(with
\omega
sampled by
{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu}
)
converges to
{\rm PPP}[\lambda,\nu^{+}_{1,\alpha}]
or

{\rm PPP}[\lambda,\nu_{1,\alpha}]
. Hence somehow the graph
\mathcal{G}[1,1](\omega_{\zeta(\beta),\beta})

suitably converges to the graph
\mathcal{G}[1,1]
read on the above marked PPP. This convergence and the above observations are in agreement with the experimented universality of Mott’s law.
From a rigorous viewpoint the conclusion of the proof of Mott law in this general case would need some quantitate estimate on the speed of convergence in Theorem
8.2
(at least for some observables), which is trivial when one starts from

Proof of Theorem

(lower bound in Mott’s law)

Before giving the proof of Theorem
7.11
we collect some preliminary results, keeping the discussion completely general.

We can extend the definitions introduced in Section
4.1
to general graphs. More precisely,
given a weighted non–oriented graph
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
with weight function
C:{\mathbb{E}}\to[0,+\infty)
and vertex set
{\mathbb{V}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and given
\ell>0
, we consider the resistor network on
S_{\ell}
with nodes
x\in{\mathbb{V}}\cap S_{\ell}
, edges
\{x,y\}
with
x,y\in{\mathbb{V}}\cap S_{\ell}
and
\{x,y\}\cap\Lambda_{\ell}\not=\emptyset
and electrical conductivity of the edge
\{x,y\}
given by
C(x,y)
.
We call
\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}},C)
the conductivity along the first direction when the electrical potential
V
has values
1
and
0
on
S_{\ell}^{-}
and
S_{\ell}^{+}
, respectively. We point out that
V
is not univocally defined on the connected components of the resistor network not intersecting
S_{\ell}^{-}\cup S_{\ell}^{+}
. Indeed, there we can only say that
V
is constant. Since the physics (and in particular
\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}},C)
) depends only on the increments of
V
(which are univocally determined), without loss we can impose that
V\equiv 0
on the above connected components.

As in the derivation of (
22
), one can easily proves Dirichlet’s principle (cf.
[
12
, Exercise 1.3.11]
):

\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}},C)=\inf\left\{\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(u)\,\big|\,u:{\mathbb{V}}\cap S_{\ell}\to[0,1]\,,u_{|{\mathbb{V}}\cap S_{\ell}^{-}}\equiv 1\,,\;u_{|{\mathbb{V}}\cap S_{\ell}^{+}}\equiv 0\right\}\,,

(79)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\ell}(u):=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in{\mathbb{E}}_{\ell}}C(x,y)\left[u(y)-u(x)\right]^{2}\,,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\ell}:=\bigl\{\,\{x,y\}\in{\mathbb{E}}\,:\,x\in{\mathbb{V}}\cap\Lambda_{\ell}\,,\;y\in{\mathbb{V}}\cap S_{\ell},\;x\not=y\bigr\}\,.

As a consequence,
\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}},C)
satisfies Rayleigh’s monotonicity law (cf.
[
12
, Section 1.4]
):

{\mathbb{G}}\supset{\mathbb{G}}^{\prime}\text{ and }C\geq C^{\prime}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}},C)\geq\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}}^{\prime},C^{\prime})\,.

(80)

Recall that, given a graph
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
with vertex set
{\mathbb{V}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}})
denotes the maximal number of vertex-disjoint LR crossings of
\Lambda_{\ell}
in
{\mathbb{G}}
(cf. Definition
7.8
).

Lemma 13.1
.

Given a graph
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
with vertex set
{\mathbb{V}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}},1)\geq\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}})^{2}}{2\mathcal{N}_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}})+|{\mathbb{V}}\cap\Lambda_{\ell}|}\geq\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}})^{2}}{3|{\mathbb{V}}\cap\Lambda_{\ell}|}\,.

(81)

Proof.

Let
n:=\mathcal{N}_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}})
.
Let
u:{\mathbb{V}}\cap S_{\ell}\to[0,1]
be a function with
u_{|{\mathbb{V}}\cap S_{\ell}^{-}}\equiv 1
and
u_{|{\mathbb{V}}\cap S_{\ell}^{+}}\equiv 0
. We set
\nabla_{x,y}u:=u(y)-u(x)
. We fix a family
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n}
of vertex–disjoint LR crossings of
\Lambda_{\ell}
in
{\mathbb{G}}
. Recall that
\pi_{i}
is a sequence
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k})
of
k\geq 3
distinct points in
{\mathbb{V}}
such that

\{x_{r},x_{r+1}\}\in{\mathbb{E}}
for all
r\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}
,

x_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}^{-}
,
x_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{\ell}^{+}
and
x_{r}\in\Lambda_{\ell}
for
1<r<k
. Below, we write
\{x,y\}\in\pi_{i}
if
\{x,y\}
is an edge of
\pi_{i}
, i.e.
\{x,y\}=\{x_{r},x_{r+1}\}
for some some
r
. We also set
|\pi_{i}|:=k
.

As the
\pi_{i}
’s are vertex-disjoint, they are also edge-disjoint. Hence, by Rayleigh’s monotonicity law (cf. (
80
)), we can bound

\sigma_{\ell}({\mathbb{G}},1)\geq\sum_{\{x,y\}\in{\mathbb{E}}_{\ell}}(\nabla_{x,y}u)^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in\pi_{i}}(\nabla_{x,y}u)^{2}\,.

(82)

By Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality we obtain
1=\Big(\sum_{\{x,y\}\in\pi_{i}}\nabla_{x,y}u\Big)^{2}
is upper bounded by
|\pi_{i}|\sum_{\{x,y\}\in\pi_{i}}(\nabla_{x,y}u)^{2}
.
Hence the r.h.s. in (
82
) can be bounded from below as

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in\pi_{i}}(\nabla_{x,y}u)^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\pi_{i}|}.\end{split}

(83)

By Jensen’s inequality applied to the convex function
x\mapsto 1/x
for
x>0
, we can bound from below the r.h.s. of (
83
) as

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\pi_{i}|}&=n\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\pi_{i}|}\geq n\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\pi_{i}|\right)^{-1}=\frac{n^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\pi_{i}|}\end{split}

(84)

(the above use of Jensen’s inequality is not new, see e.g. the proof of
[
9
, Prop. 3.2]
).
Recall that the
\pi_{i}
’s are vertex-disjoint and all vertexes of each
\pi_{i}
are in
{\mathbb{V}}\cap\Lambda_{\ell}
apart the extreme ones.
As a consequence, we can bound
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\pi_{i}|\leq 2n+|{\mathbb{V}}\cap\Lambda_{\ell}|
.
Combining this bound with (
82
), (
83
) and (
84
) we get the first bound in (
81
). For the second one it is enough to observe that the
\pi_{i}
’s are vertex-disjoint and that each
\pi_{i}
has at least one point in
{\mathbb{V}}\cap\Lambda_{\ell}
.
∎

\gamma\in(0,1)

\omega

Appendix C
Failure of the FKG inequality in the MA random resistor network

The results on LR crossings of
[
17
]
are derived under the assumption that the FKG inequality holds for the random graphs treated there. In the specific case of the MA random resistor network,
the most general form of the FKG inequality would be the following one.
First we call an event
A\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)

increasing
if, given
\omega\in\Omega
, it holds
\omega\in A
whenever
\mathcal{G}[\zeta,\beta](\omega^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{G}[\zeta,\beta](\omega)
for some
\omega^{\prime}\in A
. The above inclusion means that the vertex set and edge set of
\mathcal{G}[\zeta,\beta](\omega^{\prime})
are included rispectively in the
vertex set and edge set of
\mathcal{G}[\zeta,\beta](\omega)
.

We claim that the above general FKG does not always hold when the energy marks in the MA resistor network appear with different sign. To explain the reason let us consider first the following toy model. Consider the points
0=(0,0)
,
e_{1}=(1,0)
,
e_{2}=(0,1)
in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. We take independent random variables
E_{0},E_{e_{1}},E_{e_{2}}

uniformly distributed on
[-1,1]
. We consider the random graph
G
with vertex set
V=\{0,e_{1},e_{2}\}
and edges given by the pairs
\{x,y\}
with
x\not=y
in
V

such that
|x-y|+|E_{x}|+|E_{y}|+|E_{x}-E_{y}|\leq 4
.
Let us consider the decreasing (with respect to the partial order given by inclusion of graphs) events
A,B
defined as
A:=\big\{\text{neither }\{0,e_{1}\}\text{ nor }\{e_{1},e_{2}\}\text{ is an edge}\big\}
and
B:=\big\{\{0,e_{2}\}\text{ is not an edge}\big\}
. We claim that both
A
and
B
have positive probability to take place but
A\cap B
is impossible, hence
A
and
B
cannot be positively correlated (thus violating the FKG inequality). Let us prove our claim. We observe that
w(a,b):=|a|+|b|+|a-b|
equals
2\max\{|a|,|b|\}
if
ab\geq 0
and it equals
2|a-b|
if
ab\leq 0
. Hence if
w(a,b)>2

with
a,b\in[-1,1]
, then
a,b
must have different signs.
The event
A
corresponds to the requirement that
w(E_{0},E_{e_{1}})>3
and
w(E_{e_{1}},E_{e_{2}})>4-\sqrt{2}>2
. In particular, the event
A
has positive probability and implies that
E_{0},E_{e_{1}}
have different signs and that
E_{e_{1}},E_{e_{2}}
have different signs.
Similarly, the event
B
has positive probability and implies that
E_{0}
,
E_{e_{2}}
have different signs. The constraint on the signs makes the event
A\cap B
impossible.

Considering the MA random resistor network
\mathcal{G}[\zeta,\beta]=\mathcal{G}[4,1]
and
by playing with the above toy example, one can easily show that the FKG inequality fails when the energy marks
uniformly distributed on

[-1,1]
.
