A categorical foundation for structured reversible flowchart languages: Soundness and adequacy Thanks: The authors acknowledge the support given by COST Action IC1405 Reversible computation: Extending horizons of computing. We also thank the anonymous reviewers of MFPS XXXIII for their thoughtful and detailed comments on a previous version of this paper.
Robert Glück, Robin Kaarsgaard

Abstract
. Structured reversible flowchart languages is a class of imperative reversible programming languages allowing for a simple diagrammatic representation of control flow built from a limited set of control flow structures. This class includes the reversible programming language Janus (without recursion), as well as more recently developed reversible programming languages such as R-CORE and R-WHILE . In the present paper, we develop a categorical foundation for this class of languages based on inverse categories with joins. We generalize the notion of extensivity of restriction categories to one that may be accommodated by inverse categories, and use the resulting decisions to give a reversible representation of predicates and assertions. This leads to a categorical semantics for structured reversible flowcharts, which we show to be computationally sound and adequate, as well as equationally fully abstract with respect to the operational semantics under certain conditions.

reversible structured flowcharts.
It provides a framework for

object language are naturally ensured by the metalanguage.

The semantic framework we are going to present in this paper covers the
reversible structured languages regardless of their concrete formation, such as

The main contribution of this paper is to provide a metalanguage formalism based on join inverse categories that is geared to formalize the reversible flowchart languages. The languages formalized in this framework are ensured to have the defining reversible language properties, including backward determinism and local invertibility.
Another main property of the formalism is that every reversible structured language that is syntactically closed under inversion of its elementary operations is also closed under inversion of reversible control-flow operators. {defi}

A

3.1.
Partial order enrichment and joins

A consequence of how restriction (and inverse) categories are defined is that
hom sets
\mathscr{C}(A,B)
may be equipped with a partial order given by
f\leq g
iff

g\overline{f}=f
(this extends to an enrichment in the category of
partial orders and monotone functions). Intuitively, this states that
f
is
below
g
iff
g
behaves exactly like
f
when restricted to the points where

f
is defined. Notice that any morphism
e
below an identity
\text{id}_{X}
is a
restriction idempotent under this definition, since
e\leq\text{id}_{X}
iff

\text{id}_{X}\overline{e}=e
,
i.e.
, iff
\overline{e}=e
.

A sufficient condition for each
\mathscr{C}(A,B)
to have a least
element is that
\mathscr{C}
has a
restriction zero
; a zero object
0

in the usual sense which additionally satisfies
A\xrightarrow{0_{A,A}}A=A\xrightarrow{\overline{0_{A,A}}}A
for each endo-zero map
0_{A,A}
.
One may now wonder when
\mathscr{C}(A,B)
has joins as a partial order. Unfortunately,

\mathscr{C}(A,B)
has joins of all morphisms only in very degenerate cases. However, if
instead of considering arbitrary joins we consider joins of maps that are
somehow compatible, this becomes much more viable.

{defi}

In a restriction category, say that morphisms
X\xrightarrow{f}Y
and
X\xrightarrow{g}Y

are

disjoint
iff
f\overline{g}=0
; and
compatible
iff
f\overline{g}=g\overline{f}
.

It can be shown that disjointness implies compatibility, as disjointness is
expectedly symmetric. Further, we may extend this to say that a set of parallel
morphisms is disjoint iff each pair of morphisms is disjoint, and likewise for
compatibility. This gives suitable notions of
join restriction
categories
.

{defi}

A restriction category
\mathscr{C}
has compatible (disjoint) joins if it has a restriction zero, and satisfies
that for each compatible (disjoint) subset
S
of any hom
set
\mathscr{C}(A,B)
, there exists a morphism
\bigvee_{s\in S}s
such that

(i)

s\leq\bigvee_{s\in S}s
for all
s\in S
, and
s\leq t

for all
s\in S
implies
\bigvee_{s\in S}s\leq t
;

(ii)

\overline{\bigvee_{s\in S}s}=\bigvee_{s\in S}\overline{s}
;

(iii)

f\left(\bigvee_{s\in S}s\right)=\bigvee_{s\in S}(fs)
for all
f:B\to X
; and

(iv)

\left(\bigvee_{s\in S}s\right)g=\bigvee_{s\in S}(sg)
for all
g:Y\to A
.

For inverse categories, the situation is a bit more tricky, as the join of two compatible partial isomorphisms may not be a partial isomorphism. To ensure this, we need stronger relations:

{defi}

In an inverse category, say that parallel maps
f
and
g
are

disjoint
iff
f\overline{g}=0
and
f^{\dagger}\overline{g^{\dagger}}=0
; and
compatible
iff
f\overline{g}=g\overline{f}
and

f^{\dagger}\overline{g^{\dagger}}=g^{\dagger}\overline{f^{\dagger}}
.

We may now extend this to notions of disjoint sets and compatible sets of
morphisms in inverse categories as before. This finally gives notions of
join inverse categories
:

{defi}

An inverse category
\mathscr{C}
has compatible (disjoint) joins if it
has a restriction zero and satisfies that for all compatible
(disjoint) subsets
S
of all hom sets
\mathscr{C}(A,B)
, there exists a morphism

\vee_{s\in S}s
satisfying (
i
) –
(
iv
) of Definition
3.1
.

An example of a join inverse category is
\mathbf{PInj}
of sets and partial injective functions. Here,
\bigvee_{f\in S}f
for a set of compatible partial injective functions
S
is constructed as the function with graph the union of the graphs of all
f\in S
; or equivalently as the function

\left(\bigvee_{f\in S}f\right)(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}g(x)&\text{if there exists $g\in S$ such that $g(x)$ is defined}\\
\text{undefined}&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Similarly, the category
\mathbf{PHom}
of topological spaces and partial
homeomorphisms (
i.e.
, partial injective functions which are open and continuous
in their domain of definition) is an inverse category with joins constructed in
the same way. Further, for any join restriction category
\mathscr{C}
, the cofree inverse
category
\Inv(\mathscr{C})
of
\mathscr{C}
is a join inverse category as well
[
21

3.1.27]
, and joins on both restriction and inverse categories can be
freely adjoined
[
21
, Sec. 3.1]
.
A functor
F
between restriction (or inverse) categories with joins is said to
be join-preserving when
F(\bigvee_{s\in S}s)=\bigvee_{s\in S}F(s)
.

3.2.
Restriction coproducts, extensivity, and related concepts

While a restriction category may very well have coproducts, these are
ultimately only well-behaved when the coproduct injections
A\xrightarrow{\kappa_{1}}A+B
and
B\xrightarrow{\kappa_{2}}A+B
are total; if this is the case, we say that the
restriction category has
restriction coproducts
. If a restriction
category has all finite restriction coproducts, it also has a restriction zero
serving as unit. Note that in restriction categories with restriction
coproducts, the coproduct injections

4.
Extensivity of inverse categories

As discussed earlier, extensivity of restriction categories hinges on the
existence of certain partial isomorphisms – decisions – yet their
axiomatization relies on the presence of a map that is not a partial
isomorphism, the codiagonal.

In this section, we tweak the axiomatization of extensivity of restriction
categories to one that is equivalent, but additionally transports more easily
to inverse categories. We then give a definition of extensitivity for inverse
categories, from which it follows that
\Inv(\mathscr{C})
is an extensive inverse
category when
\mathscr{C}
is an extensive restriction category.

Recall that decisions satisfy the following two axioms:

(D.1):

\nabla\langle f\rangle=\overline{f}
and

(D.2):

(f+f)\langle f\rangle=(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})f

As mentioned previously, an immediate problem with this is the reliance on the
codiagonal. However, intuitively, what
(D.1)
states is simply that the
decision
\langle f\rangle
cannot do anything besides to tag its inputs appropriately. Using a disjoint join, we reformulate this axiom to the following:

(D’.1):

(\kappa_{1}^{\dagger}\langle f\rangle)\vee(\kappa_{2}^{\dagger}\langle f\rangle)=\overline{f}

Note that this axiom also subtly states that disjoint joins of the given form
always exist.

Say that a restriction category is
pre-extensive
if it has restriction
coproducts, a restriction zero, and a combinator mapping each map
A\xrightarrow{f}B+C
to a
pre-decision

A\xrightarrow{\langle f\rangle}A+A
(with no additional requirements). We can then show the following:

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a pre-extensive restriction category. The following are
equivalent:

(i)

\mathscr{C}
is an extensive restriction category.

(ii)

\mathscr{C}
satisfies
(D.1)
and

(D.2)
.

(iii)

Every pre-decision of
\mathscr{C}
satisfies
(D’.1)
and

(D.2)
.

To show this theorem, we will need the following lemma:

Lemma 4
.

In an extensive restriction category, joins of the form
(f+0)\vee(0+g)

and
C\xrightarrow{g}D
and are equal to

We can now continue with the proof.

.

Another subtle consequence of our amended first rule is that

Our next snag in transporting this definition to inverse categories has to do
with the restriction coproducts themselves, as it is observed in

that any inverse category with restriction coproducts is a
preorder. Intuitively, the problem is not that unicity of coproduct maps cannot
be guaranteed in non-preorder inverse categories, but rather that the coproduct

f
and

For this reason, we will consider the more general disjointness tensor for
sum-like constructions rather than full-on restriction coproducts, as inverse
categories may very well have a disjointness tensor without it leading to
immediate degeneracy. Notably,
\mathbf{PInj}
has a disjointness tensor, constructed

{defi}

An inverse category with a disjointness tensor is said to be
extensive

(\amalg_{1}^{\dagger}\langle f\rangle)\vee(\amalg_{2}^{\dagger}\langle f\rangle)=\overline{f}

(D’.2):

(f\oplus f)\langle f\rangle=(\amalg_{1}\oplus\amalg_{2})f

As an example,
\mathbf{PInj}

\text{undefined}&\text{if $f(x)$ is undefined}\end{array}\right.\kern 5.0pt.

must be its own
restriction idempotent. Since restriction idempotents are self-adjoint (
i.e.

), they are disjoint iff their inverses are disjoint.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be an inverse category with a disjointness tensor, a restriction

5.
Modelling structured reversible flowcharts

In the following, let
\mathscr{C}
be an inverse category with (at least countable)
compatible joins and a join-preserving disjointness tensor. As disjoint joins
are compatible, it follows that
\mathscr{C}
is an extensive inverse category with a
(uniform)
\dagger
-trace operator.

In this section, we will show how this framework can be used model reversible
structured flowchart languages. First, we will show how decisions in extensive
inverse categories can be used to model predicates, and then how this
representation extends to give very natural semantics to reversible flowcharts
corresponding to conditionals and loops.

5.1.
Predicates as decisions

In suitably equipped categories, one naturally considers predicates on an
object
A
as given by maps
A\to 1+1
. In inverse categories, however, the
mere idea of a predicate as a map of the form
A\to 1\oplus 1
is
problematic, as only very degenerate maps of this form are partial
isomorphisms. In the following, we show how decisions give rise to an
unconventional yet ultimately useful representation of predicates. To our
knowledge this representation is novel, motivated here by the necessity to
model predicates in a reversible fashion, as decisions are always partial
isomorphisms.

The simplest useful predicates are the predicates that are always true
(respectively always false). By convention, we represent these by the left
(respectively right) injection (which are both their own decisions),

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\mathit{tt}\right\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\amalg_{1}

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\mathit{ff}\right\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\amalg_{2}.

Semantically, we may think of decisions as a separation of an object
A
into

witnesses
and
counterexamples
of the predicate it represents.
In a certain sense, the axioms of decisions say that there is nothing more to a
decision than how it behaves when postcomposed with
\amalg_{1}^{\dagger}
or

\amalg_{2}^{\dagger}
. As such, given the convention above, we think of

\amalg_{1}^{\dagger}\langle p\rangle
as the witnesses of the predicate represented by
the decision
\langle p\rangle
, and
\amalg_{2}^{\dagger}\langle p\rangle
as its
counterexamples.

With this in mind, we turn to boolean combinators. The negation of a
predicate-as-a-decision must simply swap witnesses for counterexamples (and
vice versa). In other words, we obtain the negation of a decision by
postcomposing with the commutator
\gamma
of the disjointness tensor,

\left\llbracket\mathbf{not}~p\right\rrbracket=\gamma\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket.

With this, it is straightforward to verify that,
e.g.
,
\left\llbracket\mathbf{not}~\mathit{tt}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\mathit{ff}\right\rrbracket
, as

\left\llbracket\mathbf{not}~\mathit{tt}\right\rrbracket=\gamma\amalg_{1}=\gamma(\text{id}\oplus\text{0})\rho^{-1}=(\text{0}\oplus\text{id})\gamma\rho^{-1}=(\text{0}\oplus\text{id})\lambda^{-1}=\amalg_{2}=\left\llbracket\mathit{ff}\right\rrbracket.

For conjunction, we exploit that our category has (specifically) finite
disjoint joins, and define the conjunction of predicates-as-decisions
\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket
and
\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket
by

\left\llbracket p~\mathbf{and}~q\right\rrbracket=\left((\amalg_{1}\overline{\amalg_{1}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}~\overline{\amalg_{1}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket})\vee(\amalg_{2}(\overline{\amalg_{2}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}\vee\overline{\amalg_{2}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket}))\right)\overline{\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}~\overline{\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket}.

The intuition behind this definition is that the witnesses of a conjunction of
predicates is given by the meet of the witnesses of the each predicate, while
the counterexamples of a conjunction of predicates is the join of the
counterexamples of each predicate. Note that this is then precomposed with
\overline{\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}~\overline{\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket}
to ensure that the result is only defined where both
p
and
q
are; this gives

Noting that the meet of two restriction
idempotents is given by their composition, this is precisely what this
definition states. Similarly we define the disjunction of
\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket
and
\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket
by

\left\llbracket p~\mathbf{or}~q\right\rrbracket=\left((\amalg_{1}(\overline{\amalg_{1}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}\vee\overline{\amalg_{1}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket}))\vee(\amalg_{2}\overline{\amalg_{2}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}~\overline{\amalg_{2}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket})\right)\overline{\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}~\overline{\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket},

as
\left\llbracket p~\mathbf{or}~q\right\rrbracket
then has as witnesses the join of the witnesses of

\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket
and
\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket
, and as counterexamples the meet of the counterexamples
of
\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket
and
\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket
. With these definitions, it can be shown that,
e.g.
,
the De Morgan laws are satisfied. However, since we can thus construct this from conjunctions and negations, we will leave disjunctions as syntactic sugar.

That all of these are indeed decisions can be shown straightforwardly, as summarized in the following closure theorem.

Theorem 9
.

Decisions in
\mathscr{C}
are closed under Boolean negation, conjunction, and
disjunction.

5.2.
Reversible structured flowcharts, categorically

To give a categorical account of structured reversible flowchart languages, we
assume the existence of a suitable distinguished object
\Sigma
of stores,
which we think of as the
domain of computation
, such that we may give

Since atomic steps (corresponding to elementary operations,

\dagger

\Tr_{X,Y}^{U}(f)=f_{11}\vee\bigvee_{n\in\omega}f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}\kern 5.0pt.

Recall that
f_{ij}=\amalg_{j}^{\dagger}f\amalg_{i}
. As such, for our
loop construct defined above, the
f_{11}
-cases correpond to cases where a
given state bypasses the loop entirely;
f_{21}f_{12}
(that is, for

) to cases where exactly one iteration is performed by a given state
before exiting the loop;
f_{21}f_{22}f_{12}
to cases where two
iterations are performed before exiting; and so on. In this way, the given
trace semantics contain all successive loop unrollings, as desired. We will
make this more formal in the following section, where we show computational
soundness and adequacy for these with respect to the operational semantics.

In order to be able to provide a correspondence between categorical and operational semantics, we also need an interpretation of the meta-command
\underline{\mathit{loop}}
. While it may not be so clear at the present, it turns out that the appropriate one is

\left\llbracket\mathit{\underline{loop}}[p,c,q]\right\rrbracket=\bigvee_{n\in\omega}\beta[p,c,q]_{21}\beta[p,c,q]_{22}^{n}\kern 5.0pt.

While it may seem like a small point, the mere existence of a categorical
semantics in inverse categories for a reversible programming language has some
immediate benefits. In particular, that a programming language is reversible
can be rather complicated to show by means of operational semantics (see,
e.g.
,

[
40
, Sec. 2.3]
), yet it follows directly in our categorical
semantics, as it is compositional and all morphisms in inverse categories have a unique partial
inverse.

6.
Computational soundness and adequacy

Computational soundness and adequacy (see,
e.g.
,
[
13
]
)
are the two fundamental properties of operational semantics with respect to
their denotational counterparts, as soundness and completeness are for proof
systems with respect to their semantics. In brief, computational soundness and
adequacy state that the respective notions of
convergence
of the
operational and denotational semantics are in agreement.

In the operational semantics, the notion of convergence seems straightforward:
a program
c
converges in a state
\sigma
if there exists another state

\sigma^{\prime}
such that
\sigma\vdash c\downarrow\sigma^{\prime}
. On the denotational side,
it seems less obvious what a proper notion of convergence is.

An idea (used by,
e.g.
, Fiore
[
13
]
) is to let values (in this case,
states) be interpreted as
total
morphisms from some sufficiently simple
object
I
into an appropriate object
V
(here, we will use our object

\Sigma
of states). In this context, the notion of convergence for a program

p
in a state
\sigma
is then that the resulting morphism
\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket
is, again, (the denotation of) a state –
i.e.
, it is total.
Naturally, this approach requires machinery to separate total maps from partial
ones. As luck would have it inverse categories fit the bill perfectly, as they
can be regarded as special instances of restriction categories.

To make this idea more clear in the current context, and to allow us to use the
established formulations of computational soundness and adequacy,
we define a model of a structured reversible flowchart language to be the
following:

{defi}

A model of a structured reversible flowchart language
\mathcal{L}
consists
of a join inverse category
\mathscr{C}
with a disjointness tensor, further equipped
with distinguished objects
I
and
\Sigma
satisfying

(i)

the identity and zero maps on
I
are distinct,
i.e.
,
\text{id}_{I}\neq 0_{I,I}
,

(ii)

if
I\xrightarrow{e}I
is a restriction idempotent then
e=\text{id}_{I}
or
e=0_{I,I}
, and

(iii)

each
\mathcal{L}
-state
\sigma
is interpreted as a

total
morphism
I\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}\Sigma
.

Here, we think of
I
as the
indexing object
, and
\Sigma
as the

object of states
. In irreversible programming languages, the first two
conditions in the definition above are often left out, as the indexing object
is typically chosen to be the terminal object
1
. However, terminal
objects are degenerate in inverse categories, as they always coincide with the
initial object when they exist – that is, they are zero objects. For this
reason, we require instead the existence of a sufficiently simple indexing
object, as described by these two properties. For example, in
\mathbf{PInj}
, any
one-element set will satisfy these conditions.

Even further, the third condition is typically proven rather than assumed. We
include it here as an assumption since structured reversible flowchart
languages may take many different forms, and we have no way of knowing how the
concrete states are formed. As such, rather than limiting ourselves to
languages where states take a certain form in order to show totality of
interpretation, we instead assume it to be able to show properties about more programming languages.

This also leads us to another important point: We are only able to show
computational soundness and adequacy for the operational
semantics as they are stated,
i.e.
, we are not able to take into account the
specific atomic steps (besides
\mathbf{skip}
) or elementary predicates of the
language.

As such, computational soundness and adequacy (and what may
follow from that) should be understood
conditionally
: If a structured
reversible flowchart language has a model of the form above
and
it is
computationally sound and adequate with respect to its atomic steps and
elementary predicates, then the entire interpretation is sound and adequate as
well.

We begin by recalling the definition of the denotation of predicates and commands in a model of a structured reversible flowchart language from Section
5
.

{defi}

Recall the interpretation of predicates in
\mathcal{L}
as decisions in
\mathscr{C}
:

(i)

\left\llbracket\mathit{tt}\right\rrbracket=\amalg_{1}
,

(ii)

\left\llbracket\mathit{ff}\right\rrbracket=\amalg_{2}
,

(iii)

\left\llbracket\mathbf{not}~p\right\rrbracket=\gamma\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket
,

(iv)

\left\llbracket p~\mathbf{and}~q\right\rrbracket=\left((\amalg_{1}\overline{\amalg_{1}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}~\overline{\amalg_{1}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket})\vee(\amalg_{2}(\overline{\amalg_{2}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}\vee\overline{\amalg_{2}^{\dagger}\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket}))\right)\overline{\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}~\overline{\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket}
.

{defi}

Recall the interpretation of commands in
\mathcal{L}
(and the meta-command

\underline{\mathit{loop}}
) as morphisms
\Sigma\to\Sigma
in
\mathscr{C}
:

(i)

\left\llbracket\mathbf{skip}\right\rrbracket=\text{id}_{\Sigma}
,

(ii)

\left\llbracket c_{1}~\mathtt{;}~c_{2}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket c_{2}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket c_{1}\right\rrbracket
,

(iii)

\left\llbracket\mathbf{if}~p~\mathbf{then}~c_{1}~\mathbf{else}~c_{2}~\mathbf{fi}~q\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}(\left\llbracket c_{1}\right\rrbracket\oplus\left\llbracket c_{2}\right\rrbracket)\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket
,

(iv)

\left\llbracket\mathbf{from}~p~\mathbf{loop}~c~\mathbf{until}~q\right\rrbracket=\Tr_{\Sigma,\Sigma}^{\Sigma}(\beta[p,c,q])
,
and

(v)

\left\llbracket\mathit{\underline{loop}}[p,c,q]\right\rrbracket=\bigvee_{n\in\omega}\beta[p,c,q]_{21}\beta[p,c,q]_{22}^{n}

where
\beta[p,c,q]=(\text{id}_{\Sigma}\oplus\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket)\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}:\Sigma\oplus\Sigma\to\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
. Note also
that
f_{ij}=\amalg_{j}^{\dagger}f\amalg_{i}
.

The overall strategy we will use to show computational soundness and
computational adequacy for programs is to start by showing it for predicates.
To begin to tackle this, we first need a lemma regarding the totality of
predicates.

Lemma 10
.

Let
p
and
q
be
\mathcal{L}
-predicates. It is the case that

(i)

I\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\mathit{tt}\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
and
I\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\mathit{ff}\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
are total,

(ii)

I\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\mathbf{not}~p\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
is total iff
I\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
is, and

(iii)

I\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket p~\mathbf{and}~q\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
is total iff
I\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
and
I\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
both are.

.

For (

\overline{\left\llbracket\mathit{tt}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\amalg_{1}\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\overline{\amalg_{1}}\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\text{id}_{\Sigma}\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\text{id}_{I},

where the final equality follows by the definition of a model. The
case for
\mathit{ff}
is entirely analogous.

For (
ii
), we have that

\overline{\left\llbracket\mathbf{not}~p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\gamma\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\overline{\gamma}\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\text{id}_{\Sigma\oplus\Sigma}\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}

which implies directly that
I\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\mathbf{not}~p\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
is total iff
I\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\xrightarrow{\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket}\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
is.

For (

\overline{\left\llbracket p~\mathbf{and}~q\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}~\overline{\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}

\overline{\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\text{id}_{I}
then yields

\overline{\left\llbracket p~\mathbf{and}~q\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\text{id}_{I}\text{id}_{I}=\text{id}_{I}

directly; the other direction follows by the fact that if
\overline{g}\overline{f}=\text{id}
then
\text{id}=\overline{g}\overline{f}=\overline{g}\overline{f}~\overline{f}=\text{id}\overline{f}=\overline{f}
(and analogously for

Theorem 16

(Computational soundness)
.

If there exists
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
\sigma\vdash c\downarrow\sigma^{\prime}
then

\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket
is total.

Proof 6.7
.

Suppose there exists
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
\sigma\vdash c\downarrow\sigma^{\prime}
.
By Lemma
15
,
\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\sigma^{\prime}\right\rrbracket
, and since the interpretation of
any
state is assumed
to be total, it follows that
\overline{\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket}=\overline{\left\llbracket\sigma^{\prime}\right\rrbracket}=\text{id}_{I}
, which was what we wanted.

With computational soundness done, we only have computational adequacy left to
prove. Adequacy is much simpler than usual in our case, as we have no higher
order data to deal with, and as such, it can be shown by plain structural
induction rather than by the assistance of logical relations. Nevertheless, we
require two technical lemmas in order to succeed.

Lemma 17
.

Let
f:A\oplus U\to B\oplus U
and
s:I\to A
be morphisms. If

\Tr^{U}_{A,B}(f)s
is total, either

\Tr^{U}_{A,B}(f)s=f_{11}s
, or there exists
n\in\omega

such that
\Tr^{U}_{A,B}(f)s=f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}s
.

Proof 6.8
.

Since the trace is canonically constructed, it takes the form

\Tr^{U}_{A,B}(f)=f_{11}\vee\bigvee_{n\in\omega}f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}.

Further, in the proof of Theorem 20 in
[
26
]
, it is shown
that this join not only exists but is a
disjoint
join,
i.e.
, for any
choice of
n\in\omega
,

f_{11}\overline{f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}}=\left(f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}\right)\overline{f_{11}}=0_{A,B}

and for all
n,m\in\omega
with
n\neq m
,

\overline{f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}}f_{21}f_{22}^{m}f_{12}=\overline{f_{21}f_{22}^{m}f_{12}}f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}=0_{A,B}.

But then,

\displaystyle\overline{\Tr^{U}_{A,B}(f)s}

\displaystyle=\overline{\left(f_{11}\vee\bigvee_{n\in\omega}f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}\right)s}

\displaystyle=\overline{\left(f_{11}s\right)\vee\bigvee_{n\in\omega}\left(f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}s\right)}

\displaystyle=\overline{f_{11}s}\vee\bigvee_{n\in\omega}\overline{f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}s}.

Since all of the morphisms
\overline{f_{11}s}
and
\overline{f_{21}f_{22}^{n}f_{12}s}
for any
n\in\omega
are restriction
idempotents
I\to I
, it follows for each of them that they are either equal
to
\text{id}_{I}
or to
0_{I,I}
. Suppose that none of these are equal to the
identity
\text{id}_{I}
. Then they must all be
0_{I,I}
, and so

\overline{\Tr^{U}_{A,B}(f)s}=0_{I,I}\neq\text{id}_{I}
, contradicting totality.
On the other hand, suppose that there exists an identity among these. Then,
it follows by the disjointness property above that the rest must be
0_{I,I}
.

With these done, we are finally ready to tackle the adequacy theorem.

7.
Full abstraction

Where computational soundness and adequacy state an agreement in the notions of
convergence between the operational and denotational semantics, full
abstraction deals with their respective notions of equivalence (see,
e.g.
,

[
32
,
29
]
). Unlike the case for computational soundness
and computational adequacy, where defining a proper notion of convergence
required more work on the categorical side, the tables have turned when it
comes to program equivalence. In the categorical semantics, program equivalence
is clear – equality of interpretations. Operationally, however, there is
nothing immediately corresponding to equality of behaviour at runtime.

To produce this, we consider how two programs may behave when executed from the
same start state. If they always produce the same result, we say that they are operationally equivalent. Formally, we define this as follows:

{defi}

Say that programs
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are
operationally equivalent
,
denoted
p_{1}\approx p_{2}
, if for all states
\sigma
,

\sigma\vdash p_{1}\downarrow\sigma^{\prime}
if and only if

\sigma\vdash p_{2}\downarrow\sigma^{\prime}
.

A model is said to be
equationally fully abstract
(see,
e.g.
,

[
32
]
) if these two notions of program equivalence are in
agreement. In the present section, we will show a sufficient condition for
equational full abstraction of models of structured reversible flowchart
languages. This condition will be that the given model additionally has the
properties of being
I
-
well pointed
and
bijective on states
.

{defi}

Say that a model of a structured reversible flowchart
language is
I
-
well pointed
if, for all parallel morphisms
f,g:A\to B
,
f=g
precisely when
fp=gp
for all
p:I\to A
.

{defi}

Say that a model
\mathscr{C}
of a structured reversible flowchart
language
\mathcal{L}
is
bijective on states
if there is a
bijective correspondence between states of
\mathcal{L}
and
total

morphisms
I\to\Sigma
of
\mathscr{C}
.

If a computationally sound and adequate model of a structured reversible
flowchart language is bijective on states, we can show a stronger version of
Lemma
15
.

Lemma 19
.

If
\mathscr{C}
be a sound and adequate model of a structured reversible flowchart
language which is bijective on states. Then
\sigma\vdash c\downarrow\sigma^{\prime}

iff
\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\sigma^{\prime}\right\rrbracket
and this is total.

Proof 7.1
.

By Lemma
15
and Theorem
16
, we only
need to show that
\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\sigma^{\prime}\right\rrbracket
implies

\sigma\vdash c\downarrow\sigma^{\prime}
. Assume that
\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\sigma^{\prime}\right\rrbracket
and that this is total. By Theorem
18
, there
exists
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
such that
\sigma\vdash c\downarrow\sigma^{\prime\prime}
, so by
Lemma
15
,
\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\sigma^{\prime\prime}\right\rrbracket
. Thus
\left\llbracket\sigma^{\prime}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\sigma^{\prime\prime}\right\rrbracket
, so
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime\prime}
by bijectivity on states.

With this, we can show equational full abstraction.

Theorem 20

(Equational full abstraction)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a sound and adequate model of a structured reversible flowchart
language that is furthermore
I
-well pointed and bijective on states. Then

\mathscr{C}
is equationally fully abstract,
i.e.
,
p_{1}\approx p_{2}
if and only if

\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p_{2}\right\rrbracket
.

Proof 7.2
.

Suppose
p_{1}\approx p_{2}
,
i.e.
, for all
\sigma
,

\sigma\vdash p_{1}\downarrow\sigma^{\prime}
if and only if

\sigma\vdash p_{2}\downarrow\sigma^{\prime}
. Let

be a morphism. Since

\mathscr{C}
is a model of a structured reversible flowchart language, it follows
that either
\overline{s}=\text{id}_{I}
or
\overline{s}=0_{I,I}
where
\text{id}_{I}\neq 0_{I,I}
.

If
\overline{s}=0_{I,I}
, we have
\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket s=\left\llbracket p_{2}\right\rrbracket s=0_{I,\Sigma}
by unicity of the zero map.

On the other hand, if
\overline{s}=\text{id}_{I}
, by bijectivity on states there
exists
\sigma_{0}
such that
s=\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket
. Consider now
\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket s=\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket
. If this is total, by
Theorem
18
there exists
\sigma_{0}^{\prime}
such that

\sigma_{0}\vdash p_{1}\downarrow\sigma_{0}^{\prime}
, and by
p_{1}\approx p_{2}
,

\sigma_{0}\vdash p_{2}\downarrow\sigma_{0}^{\prime}
as well. But then, applying
Lemma
15
on both yields that

\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket s=\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}^{\prime}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p_{2}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p_{2}\right\rrbracket s\kern 5.0pt.

If, on the other hand,
\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket s
is not total, by the
contrapositive to Theorem
16
, there exists
no

\sigma_{0}^{\prime}
such that
\sigma_{0}\vdash p_{1}\downarrow\sigma_{0}^{\prime}
, so by
p_{1}\approx p_{2}
there exists no
\sigma_{0}^{\prime\prime}
such that

\sigma_{0}\vdash p_{1}\downarrow\sigma_{0}^{\prime\prime}
. But then, by the contrapositive
of Theorem
18

\text{id}_{I}
or
0_{I,I}
, it follows that

\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket s=\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket=0_{I,\Sigma}=\left\llbracket p_{2}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p_{2}\right\rrbracket s.

Since

\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p_{2}\right\rrbracket
.

In the other direction, suppose
\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p_{2}\right\rrbracket
, let
\sigma_{0}

be a state, and suppose that there exists
\sigma_{0}^{\prime}
such that

\sigma_{0}\vdash p_{1}\downarrow\sigma_{0}^{\prime}
. By Lemma
15
,

\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}^{\prime}\right\rrbracket

Theorem
16
this is total. But then, by
\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p_{2}\right\rrbracket
,
\left\llbracket p_{2}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p_{1}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\sigma_{0}^{\prime}\right\rrbracket

Applications

In this section, we briefly cover some applications of the developed theory: We show how the usual program invertion rules can be verified using the semantics;
introduce a small reversible flowchart language, and use the results from the
previous sections to give it semantics; and discuss how decisions may be used
as a programming technique to naturally represent predicates in a reversible
functional language.

8.1.
Verifying program inversion

A desirable syntactic property for reversible programming languages is to be
closed under program inversion, in the sense that for each program
p
, there
is another program
\mathcal{I}[p]
such that
\left\llbracket\mathcal{I}[p]\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}
. Janus,

R-WHILE
, and
R-CORE

[
40
,
19
,
20
]
are all
examples of reversible programming languages with this property. This is
typically witnessed by a
program inverter

[
1
,
2
]
, that is, a procedure
mapping the program text of a program to the program text of its inverse
program
3
3

3

While semantic inverses
are
unique, their program texts
generally are not. As such, a programming language may have many different
sound and complete program inverters, though they will all be equivalent up to program semantics.
.

Program inversion of irreversible languages is inherently difficult because of their backwards nondeterminism and the elimination of nondeterminism requires advanced machinery (
e.g.
, LR-based parsing methods
[
18
]
). Constructing program inversion for reversible languages is typically straightforward because of their backward determinism, but exploting this object language property,
e.g.
, for proving program inverters correct, is severly hindered by the fact that conventional metalanguages do not naturally capture these object-language properties. On the other hand, the categorical foundation considered here leads to a very concise theorem regarding program inversion for all reversible flowchart languages.

Suppose that we are given a language where elementary operations are closed
under program inversion (
i.e.
, where each elementary operation
b
has an
inverse
\mathcal{I}[b]
such that
\left\llbracket\mathcal{I}[b]\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket b\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}
). We can use the
semantics to verify the usual program inversion rules for
\mathbf{skip}
, sequencing,
reversible conditionals and loops as follows, by structural induction on
c

with the hypothesis that
\left\llbracket\mathcal{I}[c]\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}
. For
\mathbf{skip}
, we have

\left\llbracket\mathbf{skip}\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}=\text{id}_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}=\text{id}_{\Sigma}=\left\llbracket\mathbf{skip}\right\rrbracket

thus justifying the inversion rule
\mathcal{I}[\mathbf{skip}]=\mathbf{skip}
. Likewise for sequences,

\left\llbracket c_{1}~\mathtt{;}~c_{2}\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}=(\left\llbracket c_{2}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket c_{1}\right\rrbracket)^{\dagger}=\left\llbracket c_{1}\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}\left\llbracket c_{2}\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}=\left\llbracket\mathcal{I}[c_{1}]\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket\mathcal{I}[c_{2}]\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket\mathcal{I}[c_{2}]~\mathtt{;}~\mathcal{I}[c_{1}]\right\rrbracket

which verifies the inversion rule

\mathcal{I}[c_{1}~\mathtt{;}~c_{2}]=\mathcal{I}[c_{2}]~\mathtt{;}~\mathcal{I}[c_{1}]\kern 5.0pt.

Our approach becomes more interesting when we come to conditionals. Given some
conditional statement
\mathbf{if}~p~\mathbf{then}~c_{1}~\mathbf{else}~c_{2}~\mathbf{fi}~q
, we notice that

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\mathbf{if}~p~\mathbf{then}~c_{1}~\mathbf{else}~c_{2}~\mathbf{fi}~q\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=(\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}(\left\llbracket c_{1}\right\rrbracket\oplus\left\llbracket c_{2}\right\rrbracket)\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket)^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}(\left\llbracket c_{1}\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}\oplus\left\llbracket c_{2}\right\rrbracket^{\dagger})\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket^{\dagger\dagger}

\displaystyle=\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}(\left\llbracket c_{1}\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}\oplus\left\llbracket c_{2}\right\rrbracket^{\dagger})\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}(\left\llbracket\mathcal{I}[c_{1}]\right\rrbracket\oplus\left\llbracket\mathcal{I}[c_{2}]\right\rrbracket)\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\left\llbracket\mathbf{if}~q~\mathbf{then}~\mathcal{I}[c_{1}]~\mathbf{else}~\mathcal{I}[c_{2}]~\mathbf{fi}~p\right\rrbracket

which verifies the correctness of usual the inversion rule (see,
e.g.
,
[
19
,
20
]
)

\mathcal{I}[\mathbf{if}~p~\mathbf{then}~c_{1}~\mathbf{else}~c_{2}~\mathbf{fi}~q]=\mathbf{if}~q~\mathbf{then}~\mathcal{I}[c_{1}]~\mathbf{else}~\mathcal{I}[c_{2}]~\mathbf{fi}~p.

Finally, for reversible loops, we have

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\mathbf{from}~p~\mathbf{loop}~c~\mathbf{until}~q\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\Sigma,\Sigma}^{\Sigma}((\text{id}_{\Sigma}\oplus\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket)\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket^{\dagger})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\Sigma,\Sigma}^{\Sigma}(((\text{id}_{\Sigma}\oplus\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket)\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket^{\dagger})^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\Sigma,\Sigma}^{\Sigma}(\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}(\text{id}_{\Sigma}\oplus\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}))

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\Sigma,\Sigma}^{\Sigma}((\text{id}_{\Sigma}\oplus\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket^{\dagger})\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\Sigma,\Sigma}^{\Sigma}((\text{id}_{\Sigma}\oplus\left\llbracket\mathcal{I}[c]\right\rrbracket)\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket q\right\rrbracket^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\left\llbracket\mathbf{from}~q~\mathbf{loop}~\mathcal{I}[c]~\mathbf{until}~p\right\rrbracket

where the fact that it is a
\dagger
-trace allows us to move the dagger inside the
trace, and dinaturality of the trace in the second component allows us to move

\text{id}_{\Sigma}\oplus\left\llbracket c\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}
from the very right to the very left. This
brief argument verifies the correctness of the inversion rule (see

[
20
]
)

\mathcal{I}[\mathbf{from}~p~\mathbf{loop}~c~\mathbf{until}~q]=\mathbf{from}~q~\mathbf{loop}~\mathcal{I}[c]~\mathbf{until}~p

We summarize this in the following theorem:

Theorem 21
.

If a reversible structured flowchart language is syntactically closed under
inversion of elementary operations, it is also closed under inversion of
reversible sequencing, conditionals, and loops.

8.2.
Example: A reversible flowchart language

Consider the following family of (neither particularly useful nor particularly
useless) reversible flowchart languages for reversible computing with integer
data,
\mathtt{RINT}_{k}
.
\mathtt{RINT}_{k}
has precisely
k
variables
available for storage, denoted
\mathtt{x_{1}}
through
\mathtt{x_{k}}
(of which

\mathtt{x_{1}}
is designated by convention as the input/output variable), and
its only atomic operations are addition and subtraction of variables, as well
as addition with a constant. Variables are used as elementary predicates,
with zero designating truth and non-zero values all designating falsehood.
For control structures we have reversible conditionals and loops,
and sequencing as usual. This gives the syntax:

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle::=\mathit{tt}\mid\mathit{ff}\mid\mathtt{x_{i}}\mid p~\mathbf{and}~p\mid\mathbf{not}~p

(Tests)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle::=c~\mathtt{;}~c\mid\mathtt{x_{i}}\mathrel{+\!\!=}\mathtt{x_{j}}\mid\mathtt{x_{i}}\mathrel{-\!\!=}\mathtt{x_{j}}\mid\mathtt{x_{i}}\mathrel{+\!\!=}\overline{n}

\displaystyle\mid\mathbf{if}~p~\mathbf{then}~c~\mathbf{else}~c~\mathbf{fi}~p

\displaystyle\mid\mathbf{from}~p~\mathbf{loop}~c~\mathbf{until}~p

(Commands)

Here,
\overline{n}
is the syntactic representation of an integer
n
. In the
cases for addition and subtraction, we impose the additional syntactic
constraints that
1\leq i\leq k
,
1\leq j\leq k
, and
i\neq j
, the latter
to guarantee reversibility. Subtraction by a constant is not included as it may
be derived straightforwardly from addition with a constant. A program in

\mathtt{RINT}_{k}
is then simply a command.

We may now give semantics to this language in our framework. For a concrete
model, we select the category
\mathbf{PInj}
of sets and partial injections, which is
a join inverse category with a join-preserving disjointness tensor (given on
objects by the disjoint union of sets), so it is extensive in the sense of
Definition
4
by Theorem
6
. By our
developments previously in this section, to give a full semantics to

\mathtt{RINT}_{k}
in
\mathbf{PInj}
, it suffices to provide an object (
i.e.
, a set) of
stores
\Sigma
, denotations of our three classes of elementary operations
(addition by a variable, addition by a constant, and subtraction by a variable)
as morphisms (
i.e.
, partial injective functions)
\Sigma\to\Sigma
, and
denotations of our class of elementary predicates (here, testing whether a
variable is zero or not) as decisions
\Sigma\to\Sigma\oplus\Sigma
. These
are all shown in Figure
4
.

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Z}^{k}

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\mathtt{x_{i}}\right\rrbracket(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\amalg_{1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})&\hskip 9.24994pt\text{if }a_{i}=0\\
\amalg_{2}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})&\hskip 9.24994pt\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\mathtt{x_{i}}\mathrel{+\!\!=}\mathtt{x_{j}}\right\rrbracket(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})

\displaystyle=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1},a_{i}+a_{j},\dots,a_{k})

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\mathtt{x_{i}}\mathrel{+\!\!=}\overline{n}\right\rrbracket(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})

\displaystyle=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1},a_{i}+n,\dots,a_{k})

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\mathtt{x_{i}}\mathrel{-\!\!=}\mathtt{x_{j}}\right\rrbracket(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})

\displaystyle=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1},a_{i}-a_{j},\dots,a_{k})

The object of stores and semantics of elementary operations and
predicates of
\mathtt{RINT}_{k}
in
\mathbf{PInj}
.

It is uncomplicated to show
that all of these are partial injective functions, and that the denotation of
each predicate
\left\llbracket\mathtt{x_{i}}\right\rrbracket
is a decision, so that this is, in fact, a
model of
\mathtt{RINT}_{k}
in
\mathbf{PInj}
.

We can now reap the benefits in the form of a reversibility theorem for free.
Operationally, semantic reversibility of a program is taken to mean that it is

locally
forward and backward deterministic; roughly, that any execution
of any subpart of that program is performed in a way that is both forward and
backward deterministic (see,
e.g.
,
[
6
]
). Denotationally (see
[
25
, Sec. 1.1]
), the
semantic reversibility of a program becomes the property that all of its
meaningful subprograms (including itself) have denotations as partial
isomorphisms. Since every meaningful
\mathtt{RINT}_{k}
program fragment takes its semantics in an inverse category, reversibility follows directly.

Theorem 22

(Reversibility)
.

Every
\mathtt{RINT}_{k}
program is semantically reversible.

Further, since we can straightforwardly show that
\left\llbracket\mathtt{x_{i}}\mathrel{+\!\!=}\mathtt{x_{j}}\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}=\left\llbracket\mathtt{x_{i}}\mathrel{-\!\!=}\mathtt{x_{j}}\right\rrbracket
and
\left\llbracket\mathtt{x_{i}}\mathrel{+\!\!=}\overline{n}\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}=\left\llbracket\mathtt{x_{i}}\mathrel{+\!\!=}\overline{-n}~\!\right\rrbracket
, we can use the technique
from Sec.
8.1
to obtain a sound and
complete program inverter.

Theorem 23

(Program inversion)
.

\mathtt{RINT}_{k}
has a (sound and complete) program inverter. In
particular, for every
\mathtt{RINT}_{k}
program
p
there exists a program

\mathcal{I}[p]
such that
\left\llbracket\mathcal{I}[p]\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket^{\dagger}
.

8.3.
Decisions as a programming technique

Decisions offer a solution to the awkwardness in representing predicates
reversibly, as the usual representations of predicates on
X
as maps
\mathit{{}X\mskip 4.0mu plus 1.0mu\to Bool}
fail to be reversible in all but the most trivial cases. On the
programming side, the reversible
duplication/equality
operator

\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
(see
[
17
,
38
]
), defined on lists as

\displaystyle\lfloor\langle x\rangle\rfloor

\displaystyle=\langle x,x\rangle

\displaystyle\lfloor\langle x,y\rangle\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\langle x\rangle&\text{if $x=y$}\\
\langle x,y\rangle&\text{if $x\neq y$}\end{array}\right.\kern 5.0pt,

can be seen as a distant ancestor to this idea of predicates as decisions, in
that it provides an ad-hoc solution to the problem of checking whether two
values are equal in a reversible manner.

Decisions offer a more systematic approach: They suggest that one ought to
define Boolean values in reversible functional programming not in the usual
way, but rather by means of the polymorphic datatype

result

what
was tested to begin

9.
Concluding remarks

In the present paper, we have built on the work on extensive restriction
categories
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
to derive a related concept of extensivity for inverse categories.
We have used this concept to give a novel reversible representation of
predicates and their corresponding assertions in (specifically extensive) join
inverse categories with a disjointness tensor, and in turn used these to model
the fundamental control structures of reversible loops and conditionals in
structured reversible flowchart languages. We have shown that these categorical
semantics are computationally sound and adequate with respect to the
operational semantics, and given a sufficient condition for equational full
abstraction.

Further, this approach also allowed us to derive a program inversion theorem for
structured reversible flowchart languages, and we illustrated our approach by
developing a family of structured reversible flowchart languages and using our
framework to give it denotational semantics, with theorems regarding
reversibility and program inversion for free.

The idea to represent predicates by decisions was inspired by the

instruments
associated with predicates in Effectus
theory
[
23
]
. Given that
side
effect free
instruments
\iota
in Effectus theory satisfy a similar rule as decisions in extensive restriction categories, namely
\nabla\iota=\text{id}
,
and that Boolean effecti are extensive, it
could be interesting to explore the connections between extensive restriction
categories and Boolean effecti, especially as regards their internal logic.

Finally, on the programming language side, it could be interesting to further explore how decisions can be used in reversible programming,
e.g.
, to do the heavy lifting involved in pattern matching and branch joining.
As our focus has been
on the representation of predicates, our approach may be easily adapted to other reversible flowchart structures,

e.g.
, Janus-style loops
[
40
]
.
