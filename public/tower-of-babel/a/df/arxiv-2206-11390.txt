Surjective polymorphisms of directed reflexive cycles
Isabelle Larivière, Benoît Larose, David Emmanuel Pazmiño Pullas

Abstract
. A reflexive cycle is any reflexive digraph whose underlying undirected graph is a cycle. Call a relational structure Słupecki if its surjective polymorphisms are all essentially unary. We prove that all reflexive cycles of girth at least 4 have this property.



1.
Introduction

Surjective polymorphisms of finite relational structures play a central role in the study of the algorithmic complexity of quantified contraint satisfaction problems (see for instance
[
1
,
13
,
8
]
). In this paper we focus on the surjective polymorphisms of reflexive digraphs.
These relational structures are general enough to encode a wide range of computational problems (see for instance
[
5
]
), and on the other hand support a rich categorical structure: to each reflexive digraph one may naturally associate a simplicial complex which gives some information on the nature of the polymorphisms of the digraph
[
10
]
. For instance, it is known that if the
k
-th homotopy group of the complex is non-trivial for some
k\geq 1
but all of the proper retracts of the digraph have trivial
k
-th homotopy group, then the digraph has only trivial idempotent polymorphisms
[
6
]
.
In particular, any digraph whose simplicial complex triangulates a sphere has this property, among them truncated Boolean lattices, the directed 3-cycle and all cycles of girth at least 4.

Let us call a relational structure
Słupecki
if all its surjective polymorphisms are essentially unary. Such structures are known to have Pspace-complete QCSP
[
1
]
; since in particular their idempotent polymorphisms are projections, if one considers the analogous problem with added unary relations, the CSP is NP-complete.
The following question follows naturally from the previous discussion:
Which reflexive digraphs are Słupecki ?

In a companion paper
[
9
]
we investigate this and related questions; the present paper focuses on cycles, which triangulate 1-spheres. By reflexive cycle, we mean any reflexive digraph whose underlying undirected graph is a cycle; this allows for any orientation of edges and symmetric edges as well. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 4
, and let
\mathbb{G}
be a reflexive
n
-cycle. Then
\mathbb{G}
is Słupecki.

Some very special cases were known previously: directed cycles (a special case of a result of Fearnley
[
4
]
), and alternating cycles of even length (i.e. posets called
crowns
) (Demetrovics and Ronyai
[
3
]
).

The strategy is as follows. Let
\mathbb{G}
be an
n
-cycle with
n\geq 4
. Suppose there exists an onto polymorphism of
\mathbb{G}
which is not essentially unary. Then it follows easily from the relational description of the Słupecki clone (Lemma
3.2
) that there exists an onto homomorphism from
\prod_{i}\mathbb{Q}_{i}
onto
\mathbb{G}
where the
\mathbb{Q}_{i}
are induced subpaths of
\mathbb{G}
of length at most
n-2
. This property motivates the following definition: we say that the
n
-cycle
\mathbb{G}
satisfies the
path condition
if there exists no homomorphism from
a product of subpaths of
\mathbb{G}
of length at most
n-2
onto
\mathbb{G}
(Definition
3.1
); obviously a cycle with the path condition is Słupecki (Lemma
3.3
). So the rest of the paper consists of (1) characterising which cycles fail the path condition, and then (2) showing these are also Słupecki. To achieve (1), which is the most technically involved result of this paper, we first prove that if
\mathbb{G}
fails the path condition, then in fact there exists a homomorphism from a product of subpaths of
\mathbb{G}
of length at most
n-2
to
\mathbb{G}
whose image misses an arc of
\mathbb{G}
, i.e. is onto a subpath
\mathbb{P}
of
\mathbb{G}
of length
n-1
(Theorem
3.8
). This turns the problem into a purely combinatorial question on words since existence of a homomorphism to a path is purely metric (Theorem
3.9
). We thus obtain a description of cycles failing the path condition (Theorem
4.2
). It follows from this characterisation that these cycles contain a self-dual path
\mathbb{P}
of length
n-1
that contains at least two symmetric edges. We then invoke a criterion from our companion paper
[
9
]
to prove that any such cycle is Słupecki (Theorem
5.4
.)

Preliminaries: notation, definitions, etc.

2.1.
Reflexive digraphs

A

on

is an arc of a digraph. Consider the simple graph obtained from the digraph

as follows: it has the same set of vertices

In this paper, all digraphs are assumed to be reflexive.

Let

. The

is the usual product of relational structures, i.e. the digraph with set of vertices

and arcs

and
\mathbb{H}
respectively; notice that

is reflexive if both

and
\mathbb{H}
are reflexive. For every positive integer

, the product of

is the product of

with itself

times. A

is a homomorphism from

to

; it is
idempotent
if
f(x,\dots,x)=x

. It is

.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that the digraph
\mathbb{G}
is Słupecki if all its surjective polymorphisms are essentially unary.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
k\geq 0
. A path of length

is a digraph with vertex set
\{0,1,\dots,k\}
where for each
0\leq i\leq k-1
, one or both of the arcs
\{(i,i+1),(i+1,i)\}
is present, and there are no other arcs.

Definition 2.3
.

Let

. An
n
-cycle is a digraph with vertex set
\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
where for each
0\leq i<n-1
, one or both of the arcs
\{(i,i+1),(i+1,i)\}
is present, one or both of the arcs

We now define an ordering and an involution on the set of words on

Definition 2.5

Let

Definition 2.6

Definition 2.7

3.
The path condition

In this section, we introduce a property that guarantees a cycle is Słupecki, and prove a combinatorial characterisation that will allow us, in the next section, to describe explicitly those cycles that fail this property.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that the
n
-cycle
\mathbb{G}
satisfies the path condition if one cannot find
paths
\mathbb{Q}_{i}
of length
n-2
that embed in
\mathbb{G}
and a surjective homomorphism
f:\prod_{i}\mathbb{Q}_{i}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{G}
.

Let
\theta\subseteq G^{n}
be an
n
-ary relation on the non-empty set
A
, and let
f:A^{k}\rightarrow A
be a
k
-ary operation on
A
. We say that
f

preserves

\theta
if, whenever
(x^{1}_{j},\dots,x^{n}_{j})\in\theta
for
j=1,\dots,k
, we have that
(f(x^{1}_{1},\dots,x^{1}_{k}),\dots,f(x^{n}_{1},\dots,x^{n}_{k}))\in\theta
. The following fact can be found in
[
11
]
.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
11
]
, Lemma 4.1)
.

Let
A
be an
n
-element set,
n\geq 2
. Then the operations on
A
preserving the relation

\theta=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}):|\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}|<n\}

are precisely the essentially unary operations and the non-surjective operations on
A
.

Theorem 3.3
.

If a cycle satisfies the path condition then it is Słupecki.

Proof.

Suppose that the cycle
\mathbb{G}
is not Słupecki; by the previous lemma, there exists a polymorphism
f:\mathbb{G}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
that does not preserve the Słupecki relation
\theta
. Then there exist
k
tuples in
\theta
that
f
maps to some tuple outside
\theta
.
For each tuple in
\theta
, there exists a proper subset of
G
containing its coordinates; taking a largest one, it is a path of length
n-2
(obtained from
\mathbb{G}
by removing a single vertex.) Since
f
maps the
k
columns to a tuple whose coordinates are all vertices of
\mathbb{G}
, in particular, the restriction of

to the product of the

paths is onto
\mathbb{G}
.
∎

Our next goal is to show that, if an
n
-cycle

does not satisfy the path condition, then there exists a homomorphism of a product of subpaths of

of length

1

1

Our strategy to prove Lemma

if and only if there is one from

\mathbb{F}
such that
(f(t),f(t+1))=(a-1,a)
minus the number of pairs
(t,t+1)
in
\mathbb{F}
such that
(f(t),f(t+1))=(a,a-1)
. Intuitively, this corresponds to the “winding number” of the path
p
.

Our first goal is to show that
\alpha
is invariant under homotopy.
To prove this, we will use the notion of non-refinable edge from
[
12
]
. We simply rephrase Maróti and Zádori’s Lemma 2.10 for our purposes (the proof they give is still valid in the more general context of
Hom(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{G})
even though they formulate it only in the case of
Hom(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{G})
; see section
5
for a definition of these digraphs.)

Let
(p,q)
be an edge of
\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{G},a)
. Let
Neq(p,q)
denote the (finite) set of all
t\in F
such that
p(t)\neq q(t)
. The edge
(p,q)
is
non-refinable
if there is no non-empty proper subset
A
of
Neq(p,q)
such that changing the value of
q
to that of
p
on
A
yields an element of
\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{G},a)
.
Call an edge
(u,v)
of
\mathbb{F}

critical for
(p,q)
if
(q(u),p(v))
is not an edge of
\mathbb{G}
.

Lemma 3.4

(Based on Lemma 2.10 of
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a digraph and let
a\in G
.

(1)

If
p,q\in\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{G},a)
are homotopic then there exists a path of non-refinable edges from
p
to
q
;

(2)

An edge
(p,q)
is non-refinable if and only if
|Neq(p,q)|\leq 1
.

Proof.

(1) It clearly suffices to prove the result when
p\rightarrow q
. If
(p,q)
is non-refinable, we are done. Otherwise, there exists
r\in\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{G},a)
obtained by changing the value of
q
on certain, but not all, values in
Neq(p,q)
to that of
p
. Notice in particular that
r(t)\in\{p(t),q(t)\}
for all
t
and hence
p\rightarrow r\rightarrow q
; secondly, it is clear that
Neq(r,p)
and
Neq(r,q)
are proper non-empty subsets of
Neq(p,q)
. By repeating the argument, this process must terminate and we obtain the desired path.

(2) Necessity is immediate. Now suppose that
|Neq(p,q)|>1
. Consider the digraph
\mathbb{D}
whose set of vertices is
V=Neq(p,q)
and whose edges are the edges critical for
(p,q)
. Since this digraph is not strongly connected (it is a subgraph of
\mathbb{F}
which is acyclic), we can find a non-empty, non-trivial strong component
A
of
\mathbb{D}
such that no critical edge goes from
V\setminus A
to
A
; we can thus change the value of
q
on
A
to that of
p
to obtain an
r\in\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{G},a)
;
r
is a homomorphism by definition of critical edge and choice of
A
, and
r
satisfies the “boundary” requirements of
\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{G},a)
because
0\not\in Neq(p,q)
so
r(0)=a
; and
p(t)=q(t)=a
for all
t\geq N
for some
N
, so the same holds for
r
. ∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{G},a)
. If
p
and
q
are homotopic then
\alpha(p)=\alpha(q)
.

Proof.

By the last lemma, it suffices to prove that, if
p\rightarrow q
in
\mathbb{F}(\mathbb{G},a)
and
p
and
q
differ in exactly one place, then
\alpha(p)=\alpha(q)
. Let
\{c,d\}=\{a-1,a\}
. The result is clear if the edges of
\mathbb{F}
mapped by
p
and
q
onto
\{c,d\}
are the same.
Hence we may assume there exists a unique edge
e
of
\mathbb{F}
that one of
p,q
maps onto
\{c,d\}
but not the other. Let
e=(u,v)
and suppose that
(p(u),p(v))=(c,d)
(all the other cases are identical.) Then
p(u)\rightarrow q(v)
and
p(v)\rightarrow q(v)
implies that
q(v)\in\{c,d\}
(keeping in mind that
\mathbb{G}
is a cycle).
Suppose first that
q(v)=c
. Then
q(u)=p(u)=c
. By definition of
\mathbb{F}
, there exists
w
a neighbour of
v
distinct from
u
; by the same transitivity argument as above we get that
p(w)\in\{c,d\}
, and of course
q(w)=p(w)
. If
p(w)=d
then both
p
and
q
traverse
(c,d)
exactly once on
\{u,v,w\}
, and if
p(w)=c
then
p
traverses both
(c,d)
and
(d,c)
once while
q
traverses neither; in both cases we get that
\alpha(p)=\alpha(q)
. Suppose now that
q(v)=d
. Then
c=p(u)\rightarrow q(u)\rightarrow q(v)=d
implies that
q(u)\in\{c,d\}
, hence by hypothesis
q(u)=d
. Repeating the argument above using the neighbour
w^{\prime}
of
u
distinct from
v
(notice that
u\neq 0
since one of
p(u)
or
q(u)
is different from
a
) will complete the proof. ∎

Definition 3.6
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an
n
-cycle, and let
a\in G
. The universal covering space
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}
of
\mathbb{G}
is the (infinite) reflexive digraph with vertex set
G\times\mathbb{Z}
, and whose arcs are all pairs
((x,i),(y,i))
such that
(x,y)\in E(\mathbb{G})
and
\{x,y\}\neq\{a-1,a\}
, together with the arcs
((a-1,i),(a,i+1))
if
(a-1,a)\in E(\mathbb{G})
and
((a,i+1),(a-1,i))
if
(a,a-1)\in E(\mathbb{G})
, and this for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Notice that, in the previous definition, the choice of
a
is immaterial, i.e. the resulting digraphs will all be isomorphic. We chose this way of defining the universal covering space to make the following proof easier.
It is clear by construction that the map
\pi:\widehat{\mathbb{G}}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
defined by
\pi(x,i)=x
is a homomorphism onto
\mathbb{G}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a connected digraph with
\sigma_{1}(\mathbb{K})=0
, and let
f:\mathbb{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
. Then there exists a map
\widehat{f}:\mathbb{K}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{G}}
such that
\pi\circ\widehat{f}=f
.

Proof.

Choose a vertex
u\in K
and let
f(u)=a
. Let
v\in K
.

Claim.

If
p
and
q
are any paths from
u
to
v
in
\mathbb{K}
, then
\alpha(f(p))=\alpha(f(q))
.

Proof of Claim.
Consider the discrete homotopy groups
\sigma_{1}(\mathbb{K},u)
and
\sigma_{1}(\mathbb{G},a)
;
f
induces a group homomorphism from the first to the second.
Since
\sigma_{1}(\mathbb{K},u)
is trivial, for any two paths
p,q
from
u
to
v
we have that
pq^{-1}
is homotopic to the constant path at
u
. Applying
f
, we obtain that
f(pq^{-1})
is homotopic to the constant path at
a
; in particular, since
\alpha
is preserved under homotopy by Lemma
3.5
,
0=\alpha(f(pq^{-1}))=\alpha(f(p)f(q^{-1}))=\alpha(f(p)f(q)^{-1})
.
Since clearly
\alpha(f(p)f(q)^{-1})=\alpha(f(p))-\alpha(f(q))
the claim follows.

By the claim, if we set
\widehat{f}(v)=(f(v),\alpha(f(p)))
for any path
p
from
u
to
v
, this function is well-defined; clearly
\pi\circ\widehat{f}=f
, so it remains to prove
\widehat{f}
is a homomorphism. This is in fact straightforward: let
v\rightarrow w
be an arc in
\mathbb{K}
. Let
p
be any path from
u
to
v
, and let
p^{\prime}
be the path from
u
to
w
which is
p
followed by the arc
(v,w)
. Let
\alpha=\alpha(f(p))
and let
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha(f(p^{\prime}))
. Then
\widehat{f}(v)=(f(v),\alpha)
and
\widehat{f}(w)=(f(w),\alpha^{\prime})
. Notice that
\alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime}
precisely when
\{f(v),f(w)\}=\{a-1,a\}
. Thus if
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
we are done. Otherwise, we have that either
(f(v),f(w))=(a-1,a)
and then
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha+1
or
(f(v),f(w))=(a,a-1)
and then
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha-1
; in either case
(\widehat{f}(v),\widehat{f}(w))
is an arc.

∎

The next result shows that the path condition can be reformulated as a problem of determining if there exists a homomorphism from products of subpaths of
\mathbb{G}
onto a subpath of
\mathbb{G}
rather than
\mathbb{G}
itself; we then describe a simple combinatorial criterion to determine if such a homomorphism exists (Theorem
3.9
).

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an
n
-cycle. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathbb{G}
fails the path condition;

(2)

there exist paths
\mathbb{Q}_{i}
of length
n-2
that embed in
\mathbb{G}
, a path
\mathbb{P}
of length
n-1
that embeds in
\mathbb{G}
and a surjective homomorphism
f:\prod_{i}\mathbb{Q}_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
.

Proof.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
is immediate.
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
: suppose there exists a surjective homomorphism
f:\mathbb{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
where
\mathbb{K}
is a product of subpaths of
\mathbb{G}
of length
n-2
. By Lemma
3.7
, we have a homomorphism
\widehat{f}:\mathbb{K}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{G}}
such that
\pi\circ\widehat{f}=f
. In particular, the image of
\widehat{f}
is connected, and hence is a path, and has length at least
n-1
. It now suffices to observe that any path can be retracted to a subpath sending the endpoints of the longer path to the endpoints of the shorter one; the path of length
n-1
is a subpath of
\mathbb{G}
by construction of
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}
.
∎

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an
n
-cycle, and let
\mathbb{Q}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{Q}_{l}
denote its induced subpaths of length
n-2
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathbb{G}
satisfies the path condition;

(2)

for every induced subpath
\mathbb{P}
of
\mathbb{G}
of length
n-1
, there exists a word
W
such that
W\geq\mathbb{Q}_{i}
for all
i
but
W\not\geq\mathbb{P}
.

Before we launch into the proof, we shall require some facts about extending partial maps to paths. The next result is a consequence of Theorem 42 in
[
2
]
, see also Theorems 19 and 20 in
[
7
]
as well as the comment that follows them.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a path. Let
\mathbb{K}
be a digraph, let

and let

be any map. There exists a homomorphism from
\mathbb{K}
to
\mathbb{P}
whose restriction to

is

if and only if, for every

, and any word

, if

in
\mathbb{K}
then
g(x)Wg(y)
in
\mathbb{P}
.

Proof.

Necessity is immediate by Lemma
2.9
(6). Now suppose there exists no homomorphism from
\mathbb{K}
to
\mathbb{P}
whose restriction to

is

.
Consider the relational structure
\mathbb{P}^{c}
consisting of the path
\mathbb{P}
together with all singleton unary relations
\{p\}
,

; let
\tau
denote the signature of this structure. Consider the
\tau
-structure
\mathbb{K}^{c}
consisting of the digraph

where each element

is constrained by the unary relation

. Then
\mathbb{K}^{c}
does not admit a homomorphism to
\mathbb{P}^{c}
. It follows from the results mentioned above that

has path duality, hence there exists some
\tau

that admits a homomorphism

to

but no homomorphism to

. Because

is reflexive, it is easy to see that such a

\mathbb{Q}
, its endpoints by

and

which are constrained by the unary relations

and

respectively; let

be the homomorphism. Since

is a

and

belong to

and

,

. If

denotes the word

where

is the underlying path of

, then we have that

; but

does not hold, as desired. ∎

Proof.

4.
Cycles that fail the path condition: a characterisation

Definition 4.1
.

We say that the cycle
\mathbb{G}
is almost symmetric if
\mathbb{G}
contains exactly one non-symmetric edge.

The main theorem of this section is the following:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
n\geq 3
. The
n
-cycle
\mathbb{G}
fails the path condition if and only if
\mathbb{G}
is almost symmetric or
there exists a self-dual path
\mathbb{S}
and a positive integer
k
such that
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{S}**)^{k}\,\mathbb{S}+
.

We first make an easy but important observation.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
W,A,B
be words such that
W\geq A+B
and
W\geq A-B
. Then
W\geq A+-B
or
W\geq A-+B
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.9
(1) it easy to see that
W\geq A+B
implies we can write
W=U_{1}+U_{2}
with
U_{1}\geq A
and
U_{2}\geq B
; similarly we can write
W=V_{1}-V_{2}
with
V_{1}\geq A
and
V_{2}\geq B
. If
|V_{1}|<|U_{1}|
then
W=V_{1}-Z+U_{2}
for some word
Z
, and thus
W\geq A-+B
; otherwise
|U_{1}|<|V_{1}|
and a similar argument shows that
W\geq A+-B
.

∎

The next two lemmas prove the easy direction.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an almost symmetric cycle of girth at least 3. Then
\mathbb{G}
fails the path condition.

Proof.

Let
n
denote the girth of
\mathbb{G}
; let
\mathbb{P}=(*)^{n-1}
, let
\mathbb{Q}=(*)^{n-3}\,-
and let
\mathbb{R}=(*)^{n-3}\,+
. Clearly
\mathbb{P}
is an induced subpath of
\mathbb{G}
of length
n-1
and
\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{R}
are induced subpaths of
\mathbb{G}
of length
n-2
. Let
W\geq\mathbb{Q}
and
W\geq\mathbb{R}
. By Lemma
4.3
we have that
W\geq(*)^{n-3}-+
or
W\geq(*)^{n-3}+-
; in any case
W\geq(*)^{n-1}=\mathbb{P}
. It follows by Theorem
3.9
that
\mathbb{G}
fails the path condition. ∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathbb{S}
be a self-dual path, let
k
be a positive integer. Then the cycle
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{S}**)^{k}\,\mathbb{S}+
fails the path condition.

Proof.

Let
n
denote the girth of
\mathbb{G}
. Clearly
\mathbb{P}=(\mathbb{S}**)^{k}\,\mathbb{S}
is an induced subpath of
\mathbb{G}
of length
n-1
. It is easy to see that the paths
\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{S}+(\mathbb{S}**)^{k-1}\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{S}-(\mathbb{S}**)^{k-1}\mathbb{S}
are induced subpaths of
\mathbb{G}
of length
n-2
(for
\mathbb{R}
, read the word for
\mathbb{G}
“backwards”). Let
W\geq\mathbb{Q}
and
W\geq\mathbb{R}
. By Lemma
4.3
, we have that
W\geq\mathbb{S}+-(\mathbb{S}**)^{k-1}\mathbb{S}
or
W\geq\mathbb{S}-+(\mathbb{S}**)^{k-1}\mathbb{S}
; in any case we have that
W\geq\mathbb{S}**(\mathbb{S}**)^{k-1}\mathbb{S}
, i.e.
W\geq\mathbb{P}
. It follows by Theorem
3.9
that
\mathbb{G}
fails the path condition.

∎

4.1.
A necessary condition to fail the path condition

Our goal in this subsection is to prove the following technical result:

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a cycle that fails the path condition. Then there exists words
A
and
B
such that

\mathbb{G}=C(A**B+)=C(A+B**)=C(A-B**)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an
n
-cycle and let
\mathbb{P}_{i}
be its subpaths of length
n-2
. According to Theorem
3.9
,
\mathbb{G}
satisfies the path condition if we can find, for each of its induced subpaths
\mathbb{P}
of length
n-1
, a word
W\ngeq\mathbb{P}
such that
W\geq\mathbb{P}_{i}
for all
i
. It turns out that there is a uniform way of constructing, for each path
\mathbb{P}
, a word
W(\mathbb{P})
that will in most cases satisfy the desired condition; furthermore, this construction will help us finding a characterisation for the cycles that fail the path condition. The next definition describes the construction.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
, and let
N
be a positive integer.

(1)

Let

[x,y]=\begin{cases}max\{x,y\}&\mbox{ if $x$ and $y$ are comparable},\\
yx&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2)

Let
p=(+)^{N}
and
m=(-)^{N}
.

(3)

Let

\widehat{x}=\begin{cases}m&\mbox{ if $x$ is $+$},\\
p&\mbox{ if $x$ is $-$},\\
\epsilon&\mbox{ if $x$ is $*$}.\end{cases}

(4)

For any non-empty word
X=x_{1}\cdots x_{r}
, let

W_{N}(X)=\widehat{x_{1}}[x_{1},{x_{2}}]\widehat{x_{2}}\dots\widehat{x_{r-1}}[x_{r-1},{x_{r}}]\widehat{x_{r}}.

Remark.
In the following, we will assume
N
is a fixed integer, much larger than
n
where
n
is the girth of the cycle
\mathbb{G}
in question, and we shall omit the index
N
as much as possible.

The following lemma follows directly from the definitions.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
, and let
A,B,X
be words. Then

(1)

\widehat{x}\ngeq x
,

(2)

[x,y]\geq x
and
[x,y]\geq y
but
[x,y]\ngeq xy
.

∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
with
x\neq*
, and let
a,b
be integers such that
a+b\leq n
.

(1)

*^{a}\,\overline{x}\,*^{b}\leq\widehat{x}
;

(2)

*^{a}\,z\leq\widehat{x}\,[x,y]
;

(3)

z\,*^{a}\ \leq[y,x]\,\widehat{x}
;

(4)

yz\not\leq[y,z]\,\widehat{z}
.

Proof.

(1) is immediate by the definitions. (2) follows from (1) if

is
*
or
\overline{x}
; otherwise

, and then notice that

and
x\leq[x,y]
. (3) is identical to (2). An easy case-by-case analysis yields (4).
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
X=x_{1}\dots x_{r}
be a non-empty word on

, and write
X=x_{1}A=Bx_{r}
.

(1)

W(X)\geq A
,
W(X)\geq B
, but
W(X)\not\geq X
;

(2)

W_{N+1}(X)\geq W_{N}(X)
;

(3)

If
x_{1}\in\{+,-\}
then
W_{N+1}(X)\geq\overline{x_{1}}\,W_{N}(X)
; if
x_{r}\in\{+,-\}
then
W_{N+1}(X)\geq W_{N}(X)\,\overline{x_{r}}
.

Proof.

(1) The first two inequalities are immediate by Lemma
4.8
(2). Now we prove that

by induction on

. If
r=1
this is Lemma
4.8
(1). Let
Y=X\,x_{r+1}
; then
W(Y)=W(X)\,[x_{r},x_{r+1}]\,\widehat{x_{r+1}}
. Suppose for a contradiction that
W(Y)\geq Y
; by induction hypothesis, and using Lemma

(1), if
W(X)\not\geq X
then we must have
[x_{r},x_{r+1}]\,\widehat{x_{r+1}}\geq x_{r}\,x_{r+1}
, which contradicts Lemma

(4).

(2) is immediate.

(3) Simply observe that
W_{N}(X)
is of the form
\widehat{x_{1}}\,Y\,\widehat{x_{r}}
and that
\overline{x}\widehat{x}=\overline{x}^{N+1}
if

.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
X=x_{1}\dots x_{r}
be a non-empty word on
\mathcal{A}
, let
A,B
be any words on
\mathcal{A}
and let
z\in\mathcal{A}
.

(1)

W(X*)=W(X)\,x_{r}
and
W(*X)=x_{1}\,W(X)
;

(2)

W(A\,*^{k}\,B)=W(A*)\,*^{k-1}\,W(*B)
for all
k>0
;

(3)

W_{N+1}(A*B)\geq W_{N}(AB)
;

(4)

W(A\,z\,B)\geq W(A*B)
;

(5)

If
A
and
B
are non-empty and
B\geq A
then there exists
M\geq N
such that

W_{M}(B)\geq W_{N}(A)
.

Proof.

(1) is immediate. We prove (2) by induction on
k
. Suppose first that
k=1
: if
A
or
B
is empty the result is trivial. Otherwise, write
A=Ca
and
B=bD
and then

W(A*B)=W(A)[a,*]\,\widehat{*}\,[*,b]W(B)=W(A)\,ab\,W(B)=W(A*)W(*B)

the last equality following from (1). Now suppose the result holds for
k
. Then using the result for
k=1
we get

W(A*^{k+1}B)=W(A*\,(*^{k}B))=W(A*)W(*^{k+1}B)

and using the induction hypothesis we conclude that this is

W(A*)W(*\,*^{k}B)=W(A*)W(**)*^{k-1}W(*B)=W(A*)*^{k-1}W(*B).

(3) By (2) we have that
W_{N+1}(A*B)=W_{N+1}(A*)W_{N+1}(*B)
. If
A
or
B
is empty the inequality is easy, so assume that
A=Ca
and
B=bD
for some
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
.
Then by (1) we get that
W_{N+1}(A*B)=W_{N+1}(A)\,ab\,W_{N+1}(B)
; and directly from the definition we have that
W_{N}(AB)=W_{N}(A)[a,b]W_{N}(B)
. If
a
and
b
are comparable, then the inequality follows from Lemma
4.10
(2). Otherwise,
W_{N}(AB)=W_{N}(A)[a,b]W_{N}(B)=W_{N}(A)\,ba\,W_{N}(B)=W_{N}(A)\,\overline{a}\overline{b}\,W_{N}(B)
and the inequality now follows from Lemma
4.10
(3).

(4) If
A
or
B
is empty the result is easy, so assume that
A=Ca
and
B=bD
for some
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
. Then
W(AzB)=W(A)[a,z]\,\widehat{z}\,[z,b]W(B)\geq W(A)\,ab\,W(B)=W(A*B)
by (1), (2) and Lemma
4.8
(2).

(5) It is not hard to see, using Lemma
2.9
, that
B\geq A
precisely when
B
contains a subword from which

can be obtained by replacing certain

and
-
by
*
. From this, it follows easily that there exists a word
D
with the following properties: (i)
D
is obtained from
B
by replacing some

and
-
by
*
; (ii)
D
contains
A
as a subword; (iii) no symbol other than

appears in

outside the subword

. It follows from (4) that
W(B)\geq W(D)
. Now starting from
D
, repeatedly use (3), removing a

at each step, until the subword

is reached.

∎

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
X,Y
be words on
\mathcal{A}
such that

. Then
Y\leq W(X^{\prime})
and in particular
Y\leq W(X)
.

Proof.

Since
W(X^{\prime})\leq W(X)
by Lemma
4.11
(5) it suffices to prove that
Y\leq W(X^{\prime})
.
Write
X^{\prime}=x_{1}\dots x_{r}
and
Y^{\prime}=y_{1}\dots y_{k}
. Suppose first that
k<r
. By Lemma
4.9
(2) we have for any
a\leq n
that
*^{a}y_{i}\leq\widehat{x_{i}}[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
for all
i
and
*^{a}\leq\widehat{x_{k+1}}
; since
Y
is obtained from
Y^{\prime}
by inserting
*

.

Now we prove the result for
k\geq r-1
by induction on
k-(r-1)
. If
k-(r-1)=0
the previous case holds, so now assume that
k\geq r
and the result holds for smaller values. Since
X^{\prime}
is not a prefix of
Y^{\prime}
, there exists words
G
,
D
,
E
and
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
distinct such that
X^{\prime}=GxD
and
Y^{\prime}=GyE
; notice that
\overline{x}=y
and
\overline{y}=x
.

(i) Suppose first that
G=\epsilon
: then
*^{a}\,\overline{x}\,*^{b}\leq\widehat{x}
for any
a+b\leq n
by Lemma
4.9
(1), and by induction hypothesis any word
Z
with

will satisfy

.
Hence
Y=*^{a}\,\overline{x}\,*^{b}\,Z\leq\widehat{x}W(X^{\prime})\leq W(X^{\prime})
.

(ii) Now suppose that

is non-empty. Since

,

is of the form

with

and

.
First we have that

by Lemma
4.14
; secondly

for all

(1), and thirdly, by induction hypothesis, we have that

for any word

such that

. Since
\widehat{x}W(xD)\leq W(xD)

Y\leq W(G*)\widehat{x}W(xD)\leq W(G*)W(xD).
By Lemma

(1), if

is the last symbol in G then

W(G*)W(xD)=W(G)zW(xD)\leq W(G)[z,x]W(xD)=W(GxD)=W(X^{\prime})
and we are done.

∎

The next result states that words

and

such that

are comparable precisely when their stars align.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
X,Y
be words on
\mathcal{A}
such that

. Write
X=*^{x_{0}}c_{1}\dots*^{x_{r-1}}c_{r}*^{x_{r}}

Clearly if

. For the other direction suppose that

: by Lemma

∎

Proposition 4.17

Cycles without the path condition are Słupecki

In a companion paper

Theorem 5.1

[

Let

be an

We now apply Theorem

in

Lemma 5.2

Proof.

The automorphisms of

is non-trivial; if it contains some
