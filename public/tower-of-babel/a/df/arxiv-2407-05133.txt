Control Density Function for Robust Safety and Convergence
Joseph Moyalan, Sriram S. K. S Narayanan, Umesh Vaidya

Abstract
We introduce a novel approach for safe control design based on the density function. A control density function (CDF) is introduced to synthesize a safe controller for a nonlinear dynamic system. The CDF can be viewed as a dual to the control barrier function (CBF), a popular approach used for safe control design. While the safety certificate using the barrier function is based on the notion of invariance, the dual certificate involving the density function has a physical interpretation of occupancy. This occupancy-based physical interpretation is instrumental in providing an analytical construction of density function used for safe control synthesis. The safe control design problem is formulated using the density function as a quadratic programming (QP) problem. In contrast to the QP proposed for control synthesis using CBF, the proposed CDF-based QP can combine both the safety and convergence conditions to target state into single constraints. Further, we consider robustness against uncertainty in system dynamics and the initial condition and provide theoretical results for robust navigation using the CDF. Finally, we present simulation results for safe navigation with single integrator and double-gyre fluid flow-field examples, followed by robust navigation using the bicycle model and autonomous lane-keeping examples.

Introduction

Navigation is one of the crucial tasks in robotics applications, enabling robots to move efficiently and effectively in obstacle-riddled environments. Applications include vehicle autonomy on structured or unstructured terrain, space exploration, robotics manipulator, agriculture robotics, and underwater exploration

In the real-world use of navigation algorithms, one has to deal with various uncertainties, including uncertainty in system dynamics, sensing, and estimation of the robot’s location and the environment. This makes it imperative to develop navigation algorithms that are robust to these sources of uncertainties. Given the significance of this problem, there is a vast body of literature on this topic addressing various aspects of navigation problems. Some of them include avoiding static
[

]
and dynamic
[
10
]
obstacle sets, incorporating uncertainty because of unmodelled dynamics
[

, incorporating uncertainty in sensing and location of the obstacle sets
[

, and accounting for external disturbances which act as unknown input to the system dynamics
[

.

The potential and navigation functions-based methods dominate the various navigation algorithms
[
17
,
18
]
. These functions create a repelling and attractive field near the unsafe and target set, respectively, to guide the system trajectories to the target set while avoiding collision with the unsafe sets
[
19
]
. However, even with relatively simple system dynamics, these methods suffer from the existence of local minima and lack of systematic techniques for constructing such functions
[
20
,
21
]
. More recently, control barrier functions have been proposed for safe navigation. The barrier function at its heart relies on the weaker notion of set invariance proposed by Mitio Nagumo to ensure safety
[
22
,
23
]
. The barrier function is used in control dynamical system setting as control barrier function (CBF). The use of CBF in a control setting is similar to the use of the control Lyapunov function (CLF) for stabilization. For a given barrier function, the safe controller synthesis is formulated as a quadratic program (QP)
[
24
,
25
]
. The QP formulation is attractive due to its convex nature and is especially advantageous for solving safe navigation problems for systems with non-trivial dynamics. However, the control synthesis using the barrier function is inherently nonconvex if formulated as a joint search problem over the barrier function and the feedback controller.

The safe navigation problem can alternatively formulated in the space of density. The density-based approach can be viewed as dual to barrier function-based approach. The dual formulation has the advantage that the co-design problem for jointly finding the controller and the density function for safety and convergence can be written as a convex optimization problem. This convexity in the co-design problem has been exploited for safe controller synthesis in
[
26
,
27
]
. Although convex, the infinite-dimensional nature of the optimization problem presents challenges for its finite-dimensional approximation. The finite-dimensional approximation involves polynomial-based parameterization of the optimization variables and the Sum of Squares (SoS)-based computational method for finding the solution
[
28
,
29
]
.

In contrast to the pure optimization-based approach to the safe control synthesis using the density function, the occupancy-based physical interpretation of the density function can also be exploited for safe navigation problems. The measure associated with the density function has a physical interpretation of occupancy. The occupancy-based interpretation has a natural connection with the linear operator theoretic framework involving Perron-Frobenius and Koopman operators
[
30
,
31
]
. This link to the linear operator theory has provided alternate computational methods, not restricted to polynomial basis function, and based on the finite-dimensional data-driven approximation of these linear operators to solve the safe navigation problem
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
. Still, the co-design problem is computationally challenging for a system with large dimensional state space. The work in
[
37
]
circumvents the deficiency of constructing density functions for solving navigation problems in large dimensional state space. In particular, based on the occupancy-based interpretation of the density function, an analytical construction of the density function is proposed to solve the safe navigation problem for systems with simple integrator dynamics. This is a tremendous breakthrough as it allows us to overcome the challenge associated with the construction of the navigation and potential functions. This paper extends the use of analytically constructed density function as proposed in
[
37
]
for safe navigation to systems with non-trivial dynamics.

We propose the use of a control density function (CDF) to solve the safe navigation problem. The main contributions of this paper are as follows. The controller design problem for safe navigation is formulated as a QP problem using the CDF. Unlike QP formulated for the safe controller synthesis using CBF, the QP for CDF can combine both safety and convergence to target conditions using a single constraint. We also provide results on robust safety, where the safe controller is designed to be robust against modeling uncertainty and uncertainty in the initial condition.
Finally, we present simulation results involving the bicycle model and lane-keeping application to verify the main results of this paper. This paper serves as the extended version of
[
38
]
.
The robust safety and convergence results are new to this paper. The results proving the continuity property and feedback nature of the safe control input are also new to this paper.

II-B

Analytical Construction of Density Function for Safe Navigation

This paper is about safe control design for control affine dynamical system of the form

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}}

(1)

where
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. The vector field
{\mathbf{f}}
and
{\mathbf{g}}=({\mathbf{g}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{g}}_{m})
are assumed to be atleast
{\cal C}^{1}({\mathbf{X}})
. Following is the definition of a weaker almost everywhere (a.e.) navigation problem.

Definition 1

(Almost everywhere (a.e.) safe navigation) The a.e. safe navigation problem consists of steering the trajectories of system (
1
) starting from almost all (w.r.t. Lebesgue measure) initial conditions from the initial set
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}
to the target set
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}
while avoiding the unsafe set
{\mathbf{X}}_{u}
.

In
[
37
]
, analytical construction of density function,
\rho({\mathbf{x}})
for solving the a.e. safe navigation problem for a single integrator is provided. In particular, with integrator system dynamics of the form
\dot{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{u}}
, a feedback control input as the positive gradient of the density function, i.e.,
{\mathbf{u}}=\nabla\rho({\mathbf{x}})^{\top}
is proposed for solving a.e. safe navigation problem as defined in Definition
1
. In this paper, one of the main contributions is extending the use of the density function for solving a.e. navigation problem for nonlinear systems with nonzero drift as given in (
1
). In the following discussion, we provide the details of the analytically constructed density function as proposed in
[
37
]
.

Let there be
K
number of obstacles. The obstacle sets are characterized using a continuous function,
c_{k}({\mathbf{x}})
, whose level sets form connected components. In particular, we define

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}_{u_{k}}:=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}:c_{k}({\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}

(2)

Hence, the unsafe region is given by
{\mathbf{X}}_{u}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\;{\mathbf{X}}_{u_{k}}
. Next, we define a sensing region,
{\mathbf{X}}_{b_{k}}
, around the unsafe set
{\mathbf{X}}_{u_{k}}
using a scalar-valued function
b_{k}({\mathbf{x}})
, which is again assumed to be continuous with level sets forming connected components.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}_{b_{k}}:=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}:b_{k}({\mathbf{x}})\leq 0\}\setminus{\mathbf{X}}_{u_{k}}

(3)

Now, we will define a smooth inverse bump function by making use of
c_{k}({\mathbf{x}})
and
b_{k}({\mathbf{x}})
. We first start by constructing the following functions,

\displaystyle m_{k}({\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{k}({\mathbf{x}})}{c_{k}({\mathbf{x}})-b_{k}({\mathbf{x}})},

\displaystyle\psi_{k}({\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(\frac{-1}{m_{k}({\mathbf{x}})})}{\exp(\frac{-1}{m_{k}({\mathbf{x}})})+\exp(\frac{-1}{1-m_{k}({\mathbf{x}})})}.

Using the functions
m_{k}({\mathbf{x}})
and
\psi_{k}({\mathbf{x}})
, we define a piece-wise smooth inverse bump function
\Psi_{k}({\mathbf{x}})
as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}({\mathbf{x}})=\begin{cases}0,&{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}_{u_{k}}\\
\psi_{k}({\mathbf{x}}),&{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}_{b_{k}}\\
1,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(4)

Note that
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\mathbf{x}})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Psi_{k}({\mathbf{x}})
encodes the information of the unsafe set
{\mathbf{X}}_{u}
. Next, we will use a distance function
D({\mathbf{x}})
, utilizing the current state and the target state, to incorporate the information of the target set
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}
in the density function. The
D({\mathbf{x}})
can be modified to adjust to the geometry of the underlying configuration space. In the Euclidean space with
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
, we use
D({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{x}}^{\top}P{\mathbf{x}}
, for some
P>0
, where the target set is assumed to be at the origin. Therefore, the density function
\rho({\mathbf{x}})
for the safe control is given as follows:

\displaystyle\rho({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\mathbf{x}})}{D({\mathbf{x}})^{\alpha}}

(5)

for some
\alpha>0
. The following theorem from
[
37
]
provides the solution for a.e. safe navigation for
\dot{{\mathbf{x}}}={\mathbf{u}}
.

Theorem 1
(
[
37
]
)

Consider
\rho({\mathbf{x}})
given in (
5
). Then the safe control given by
{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}})=\nabla\rho({\mathbf{x}})
is the solution to the a.e. safe navigation as stated in Definition
1
for
\dot{{\mathbf{x}}}={\mathbf{u}}
.

{\mathbf{X}}_{u}
and target
{\mathbf{X}}_{T}
. Fig.

takes minimum value for
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}_{u}

.

III
Control Density Function (CDF) for Safety

This section introduces the CDF for solving motion planning problems with safety constraints. This will be along the lines of using CLF
[
39
,
40
]
and CBF
[
41
,
25
]
for stabilization and safe navigation, respectively. However, unlike the Lyapunov and barrier functions, the safety and convergence requirements can be combined using a single density function.

The safe navigation problem using the density function approach is, in fact, a co-design problem of jointly finding a feedback controller
k({\mathbf{x}})
and a density function
\rho({\mathbf{x}})
. One of the main advantages of using the density function over the barrier function as a safety certificate is that the co-design problem of jointly finding the density function for safety and the feedback controller is convex. This convexity property is exploited in the work of
[
35
,
42
]
to design the safety controller. Although the co-design problem is convex, solving this convex problem involves parameterization of the density function and controller using some basis functions. Determining appropriate parameterization is challenging, especially for systems with large dimensional state space. In this paper, we take a different approach where we make the use of analytically constructed density function as proposed in
[
37
]
(Eq.
5
) to determine the safety controller for the system with non-trivial drift terms. The main contribution is to show that the a.e. safe navigation problem as defined in Definition
1
can be formulated as a quadratic programming problem using the density function given in (
5
).
Following is the first main result of this paper.

Theorem 2

Let the density function
\rho({\mathbf{x}})
be as given in (
5
).
The a.e. navigation problem as stated in Definition
1
is solvable for the control system (
1
) if there exists a controller
{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{k}}({\mathbf{x}})
and
\lambda>0
satisfying the following conditions

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(({\mathbf{f}}+{\mathbf{g}}{\mathbf{k}})\rho)\geq 0,\;\;a.e.\;{\mathbf{x}}\in\bar{\mathbf{X}}

(6a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(({\mathbf{f}}+{\mathbf{g}}{\mathbf{k}})\rho)\geq\lambda>0,\;\;\;{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}_{o}

(6b)

We postpone the proof of this theorem to the Appendix.

The inequalities in (
6a
)-(
6b
) are linear in
{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{k}}({\mathbf{x}})
for a given density function
\rho({\mathbf{x}})
. As stated above, solving for

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}\rho+{\mathbf{g}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\geq 0,\;\;a.e.\;{\mathbf{x}}\in\bar{\mathbf{X}}

(7a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}\rho+{\mathbf{g}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\geq\lambda>0,\;\;\;{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}_{o}

(7b)

Although convex, the inequalities are infinite-dimensional and, hence, computationally challenging to solve for global safety controllers. Instead, if we solve for
{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}})
along a trajectory for a given initial condition, then the problem of finding the control input
{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}})
can be written as a finite-dimensional convex optimization problem. In fact, similar to the quadratic programming (QP) based formulation of safety using the CBF, safe navigation using the CDF can also be formulated as a QP problem. For any given fixed
{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{u}}}\;\|{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}

(8a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\rho+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}}\rho)\geq 0,\;\;a.e.\;{\mathbf{x}}\in\bar{{\mathbf{X}}}

(8b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\rho+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}}\rho)\geq\lambda>0,\;\;\forall\;{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}_{0}

(8c)

One of the advantages of the QP formulation given in (
8

{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
, for the system given by (
1

{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
in the absence of unsafe sets by introducing a cost function which minimizes
\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|^{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{u}}}\;\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|^{2}

(9a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\rho+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}}\rho)\geq 0,\;\;a.e.\;{\mathbf{x}}\in\bar{{\mathbf{X}}}

(9b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\rho+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}}\rho)\geq\lambda>0,\;\;\forall\;{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}_{0}

(9c)

We write the QP in the more general form for our next results which are as follows.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}^{\star}({\mathbf{x}})=\argmin_{{\mathbf{u}}}\;\;\;\;{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}}

(10a)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\;\;\;\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\rho+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})\rho{\mathbf{u}})\geq 0,\;\;a.e.\;{\mathbf{x}}\in\bar{{\mathbf{X}}}

(10b)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\rho+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{u}}\rho)\geq\lambda>0,\;\;\forall\;{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}_{0}

(10c)

Here,
{\mathbf{u}}=[u_{1},\dots,u_{m}]^{\top},{\mathbf{H}}({\mathbf{x}})\in\mathds{R}^{m\times m}

{\mathbf{x}}
, and
{\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{x}})\in\mathds{R}^{m}

Theorem 3

Assume that the vector field
{\mathbf{f}}
,
{\mathbf{g}}
, and the matrix valued function

Then the solution

IV
Robust safety and convergence

In this section, we propose using the control density function for robust safety. We consider robustness against uncertainty in system dynamics and the initial condition.

IV-B

Uncertainty in System States

In this section, we consider uncertainty in the state of the system. The uncertainty in the state estimate could be due to sensor noise or the error in the state estimate. For example, in robotic applications, uncertainty can arise due to uncertainty in the localization algorithm used to localize the robot. One could also consider uncertainty in the location of the unsafe set. However, this can typically be handled by enlarging the unsafe set to include the uncertainty bounds. We will assume that the uncertainty in the state estimate is bounded. Let
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}(t)
be the nominal or estimated state of the system at time
t
, and we assume that the true state,
{\mathbf{x}}(t)
of the system can be anywhere within
\beta
neighborhood of
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}(t)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{x}}(t)-{\mathbf{x}}_{0}(t)\|\leq\beta

(21)

Let
{\cal N}_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})
denotes the
\beta
neighborhood of
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
.
The safe navigation problem with the uncertainty in the system state can then be written as

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{u}}}\;\;

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}

(22a)

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\cal N}_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})}\;\;

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(({\mathbf{f}}+{\mathbf{g}}{\mathbf{u}})\rho)\leq 0,\;\;a.e.\;{\mathbf{x}}\in\bar{\mathbf{X}}

(22b)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(({\mathbf{f}}+{\mathbf{g}}{\mathbf{u}})\rho)+\lambda\leq 0,\;\;{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}_{0}

(22c)

The above problem is an infinite-dimensional robust optimization problem. The goal is to minimize the control effort for each
{\mathbf{x}}
, but the robustness involves maximizing the constraints for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\cal N}_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})
.
Unlike non-robust safety problems, which can be written as finite-dimensional QP, if we solve for
{\mathbf{u}}
along the trajectory, the above problem is
infinite-dimensional and nonconvex even if we solve for
{\mathbf{u}}
along a trajectory. This is due to the robustness
\max
constraints. The above problem can be converted to a finite-dimensional convex optimization problem by sampling over the set
{\cal N}_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})
. Let
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{p}\}_{p=1}^{N}
be the data points sampled randomly from the set
{\cal N}_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}}_{0})
, we can then approximate the infinite-dimensional constraints with finitely many constraints as

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{u}}}\;\;\|{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}

(23a)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}\;\;\nabla\cdot(({\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p})+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p}){\mathbf{u}})\rho({\mathbf{x}}_{p}))\geq 0,\;\;p=0,1,\ldots,N

(23b)

Since the data points
{\mathbf{x}}_{p}
are drawn randomly, the solution to the finite-dimensional optimization problem (
23
) will be random in nature. In the following discussion, we will assume that the sampling probability is uniform w.r.t. Lebesgue measure.
Now we can use results from scenario optimization from
[
44
]
to connect the results obtained using the finite-dimensional optimization problem (
23
) to the original infinite dimensional nonconvex robust optimization problem (
22
). Toward this goal, we define

{\cal F}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{u}}):=-\nabla\cdot(({\mathbf{f}}+{\mathbf{g}}{\mathbf{u}})\rho)

and the probability of violation as

G({\mathbf{u}}):={\rm Prob}\{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\cal N}_{\beta}({\mathbf{x}}_{0}):{\cal F}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{u}})>0\}

Hence,
G({\mathbf{u}})
measures the volume of bad parameter
{\mathbf{x}}
for which the constraints
{\cal F}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{u}})\leq 0
are violated. One can define
\epsilon
-level feasible solution as any solution for which
G({\mathbf{u}})\leq\epsilon
.

Definition 2

(
\epsilon
-Level Solution): Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. We say that
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbf{U}}
is
is an
\epsilon
-level robustly feasible (or, more simply, an
\epsilon
-level)
solution, if
G({\mathbf{u}})\leq\epsilon
.

Since the samples
{\mathbf{x}}_{p}
are drawn randomly, the optimal solution,
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{N}
of the optimization problem (
23
) will be
\epsilon
-level solution for the given random extraction and not the other. The
\sigma
is used to bound the probability that
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{N}
is not
a
\epsilon
-level solution for other random extraction. Hence, the parameter
\sigma
measures the risk of failure, or confidence,
associated with the randomized solution algorithm. In particular, the following theorem follows from
[
44
]
connecting the optimal solution of the optimization problem (
22
) to that of (
23
).

Theorem 5

Assume that all possible realization of samples
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{p}\}_{p=1}^{N}
either lead to an infeasible solution for the optimization problem (
23
) or, if feasible, admits a unique optimal solution. Fix two real numbers
\sigma\in(0,1)
(confidence parameter) and
\epsilon
(level parameter). If

N\geq\Big\lceil\frac{2}{\epsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\sigma}+2m+\frac{2m}{\epsilon}\ln\frac{2}{\epsilon}\Big\rceil

where
m
is the number of control inputs and
\lceil\cdot\rceil
denotes the smallest integer greater than or equal to the argument. Then with probability no smaller than
(1-\beta)
, either (
23
) is unfeasible or (
23
) is feasible, and then its optimal solution,
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{N}
, is
\epsilon
-level robust feasible.

Algorithm for QP-CDF

This section presents the algorithm for solving the QP presented in Eq. (
9
). In particular, the main part of the algorithm is the approximation of the spatial derivative of the control input
{\mathbf{u}}
.
The constraints in the QP can be rewritten for the multi-input case as

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\rho)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{g}}_{j}({\mathbf{x}})\rho)u_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla u_{j}{\mathbf{g}}_{j}\rho\geq 0

(24)

where
{\mathbf{g}}=({\mathbf{g}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{g}}_{m})
and
\nabla u_{j}
is a row vector.
We split the inequality in (
24
) as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}\rho)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{g}}_{j}\rho)u_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\zeta

(25a)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla u_{j}{\mathbf{g}}_{j}\rho

\displaystyle\geq-\zeta

(25b)

for some
\zeta>0
.
The following explains the procedure for approximating the spatial gradient involving the control input in the optimization problem (
9
). The left-hand side of the (
25b
) is equal to the Lie derivative of the
u_{j}
along the direction of the vector field
\rho{\mathbf{g}}_{j}
denoted by
{\cal L}_{\rho{\mathbf{g}}_{j}}u

\phi_{\rho{\mathbf{g}}_{j}}^{\Delta t}({\mathbf{x}})
be the flow of vector field
\rho{\mathbf{g}}_{j}
for time step
\Delta t

\Delta t
sufficiently small, we have

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\rho{\mathbf{g}}_{j}}u_{j}\approx\frac{u_{j}(\phi_{\rho{\mathbf{g}}_{j}}^{\Delta t}({\mathbf{x}}))-u_{j}({\mathbf{x}})}{\Delta t}

(26)

Furthermore, for sufficiently small
\Delta t
, we can approximate

\phi_{\rho{\mathbf{g}}_{j}}^{\Delta t}({\mathbf{x}})\approx{\mathbf{x}}+\Delta t\rho({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{g}}_{j}({\mathbf{x}}).

Letting
{\mathbf{z}}_{j}={\mathbf{x}}+\Delta t\rho({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{g}}_{j}({\mathbf{x}})

25b
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}({\mathbf{z}}_{j})-u_{j}({\mathbf{x}})\geq-\zeta\Delta t

(27)

Note that while we are interested in computing the control value at point
{\mathbf{x}}
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}})

{\mathbf{u}}
in the optimization problem, we are required to know the value of
u_{j}
at
{\mathbf{z}}_{j}

{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}})
, we have to introduce an additional variable
u_{j}({\mathbf{z}}_{j})=:\bar{u}_{j}
. Now, let
\bar{\mathbf{u}}:=(\bar{u}_{1},\ldots,\bar{u}_{m})^{\top}
,
\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0}=(u_{10}({\mathbf{z}}_{1}),\ldots,u_{m0}({\mathbf{z}}_{m}))^{\top}
, and
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}=(u_{10},\ldots,u_{m0})^{\top}
. Here,
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
and
\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
are the value of nominal input at
{\mathbf{x}}
and
\{{\mathbf{z}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{z}}_{m}\}
respectively. Therefore, we can write the optimization problem as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{u}},\bar{\mathbf{u}},\zeta}\|{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}({\mathbf{x}})\|+\|\bar{\mathbf{u}}-\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|+\zeta^{2}

(28a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\rho({\mathbf{x}}))+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{g}}_{j}({\mathbf{x}})\rho({\mathbf{x}}))u_{i}({\mathbf{x}})\geq\zeta

(28b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{z}}_{j})\rho({\mathbf{z}}_{j}))+\sum_{i\neq j}^{m}\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{g}}_{i}({\mathbf{z}}_{j})\rho({\mathbf{z}}_{j}))u_{i0}({\mathbf{z}}_{j})+

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\mathbf{g}}_{j}({\mathbf{z}}_{j})\rho({\mathbf{z}}_{j}))\bar{u}_{j}\geq\zeta,

(28c)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{u}_{j}-u_{j}({\mathbf{x}})\geq-\zeta\Delta t

(28d)

where
j=1,\ldots,m
and
{\mathbf{z}}_{j}={\mathbf{x}}+\Delta t\rho({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{g}}_{j}({\mathbf{x}})
.
The above equation has
2m+1
decision variables:
{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}}),\bar{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{1}({\mathbf{z}}_{1}),\ldots,u_{m}({\mathbf{z}}_{m}))
and
\zeta
.

In this section, we provide some examples to showcase the power of CDF for safe navigation. VII
Conclusion

A novel approach for safe control design based on density function is presented in this paper. We introduce the control density function (CDF) to synthesize a safe controller for nonlinear systems. The occupancy-based physical interpretation of the density function allows us to provide an analytical construction of the CDF, which is used as a constraint in the quadratic program (QP) for the safe control design. We also consider uncertainty in the system dynamics and initial conditions for robust safe navigation. Finally, we have validated our framework by providing simulation results for the bicycle model, double-gyre fluid flow field, and lane-keeping example.
