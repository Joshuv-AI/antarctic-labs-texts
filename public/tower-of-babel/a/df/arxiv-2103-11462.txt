From Binary Hermitian Forms to parabolic cocycles of Euclidean Bianchi groups Thanks: The author was partly supported by Assigned Release Time (ART) program for research from William Paterson University
Cihan Karabulut

Abstract
. We study a family of functions defined in a very simple way as sums of powers of binary Hermitian forms with coefficients in the ring of integers of an Euclidean imaginary quadratic field K K with discriminant d K d_{K} . Using these functions we construct a nontrivial cocycle belonging to the space of parabolic cocycles on Euclidean Bianchi groups. We also show that the average value of these functions is related to the special values of L ⁡ ( χ d K , s ) L(\chi_{d_{K}},s) . Using the properties of these functions we give new and computationally efficient formulas for computing some special values of L ⁡ ( χ d K , s ) L(\chi_{d_{K}},s) .

1.
Introduction

In
[
28
]
, Zagier studies the following family of functions: Let
D>0
be a non-square integer satisfying
D\equiv 0,1
(mod 4) and
k>0
be an even integer. Then, define
F_{k,D}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows,

F_{k,D}(x):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{disc}(Q)=D\\
a<0<Q(x)\end{subarray}}Q(x)^{k-1}

(1.1)

where
Q(X)=aX^{2}+bX+c
with
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
and
\text{disc}(Q)=b^{2}-4ac
. When
D
is a square discriminant, one has to add a simple correction term consisting of the
k
-th Bernoulli polynomial. But for the sake of brevity and simplicity, let us assume that
D
is a non-square discriminant.

Zagier shows that these functions have many surprising properties and are intimately related to modular forms of integral weight and half integral weight, certain values of Dirichlet
L
-functions, Diophantine approximation, continued fractions, and Dedekind sums.

For example, Zagier finds that
F_{2,5}
is constant with value
F_{2,5}(x)=F_{2,D}(0)=2
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
despite the fact that there are infinitely many quadratic polynomials contributing to the sum when
x
is irrational. More generally, he proves the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1

(Zagier 1999)
.

Let
D
be a positive non-square discriminant and
k
a positive even integer. Then

(1)

F_{2,D}(x)
has a constant value
\alpha_{D}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2)

F_{4,D}(x)
has a constant value
\beta_{D}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(3)

For
k\geq 6
,
F_{k,D}
is no longer constant but is a linear combination, with coefficients depending on
D
and
k
, of a finite collection of functions depending only on
k
.

To get an explicit formula for
\alpha_{D}
and
\beta_{D}
, Zagier uses the above theorem to write
\alpha_{D}
as
F_{2,D}(0)
and
\beta_{D}
as
F_{4,D}(0)
which gives the following formulas:

\alpha_{D}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{2}-4ac=D\\
a<0<c\end{subarray}}c=\sum_{|b|<\sqrt{D}}\sigma_{1}\left(\frac{D-b^{2}}{4}\right),

(1.2)

\beta_{D}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b^{2}-4ac=D\\
a<0<c\end{subarray}}c^{3}=\sum_{|b|<\sqrt{D}}\sigma_{3}\left(\frac{D-b^{2}}{4}\right)

(1.3)

where
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
. The sums involving the divisor functions
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{3}
have appeared in the literature before as the values of Dirichlet
L
-function
L(\chi_{D},s)
associated to the Kronecker symbol
\chi_{D}:=\left(\frac{D}{\cdot}\right)
at
s=-1
and
s=-3
. In fact, Cohen
[
3
,
4
]
and Zagier
[
26
]
using the results of Siegel
[
22
]
showed that

\displaystyle\alpha_{D}=

\displaystyle-5L(\chi_{D},-1),

(1.4)

\displaystyle\beta_{D}=

\displaystyle L(\chi_{D},-3).

(1.5)

The formulas in (
1.4
) and (
1.5
), which we refer to as Cohen-Zagier type formulas, are computationally very efficient and give a fast
O(D^{1/2+\epsilon})
method for computing the special values
s=-1
and
s=-3
. The usual formulas for computing special values in terms of finite character sums are
O(D)
and are not very practical for actual computations, except for small conductors (see Remarks on page 194 in
[
5
]
).

In recent years, there has been a renewed interest in Zagier’s paper, with
[
1
,
17
,
18
,
25
]
all further exploring the functions defined in (
1.1
). In this paper, generalizing in a different direction, we introduce an analogue of Zagier’s functions
F_{k,D}
by using binary Hermitian forms and prove a result similar to Theorem
1.1
for these functions. As an application, we get new formulas, similar to the ones given in (
1.4
) and (
1.5
).

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
with
d=1,2,3,7,11
be an Euclidean imaginary quadratic field with discriminant
d_{K}
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
be the ring of integers of
K
. For a positive integer
\Delta>0
which is not a norm of an algebraic integer in
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
and an odd integer
k\geq 1
, we define the function
H_{k,\Delta}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}
as

H_{k,\Delta}(z):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N(b)-ac=\Delta\\
a<0<h(z)\end{subarray}}h(z)^{k}\\

(1.6)

where
\displaystyle h(z)=a|z|^{2}+bz+\bar{b}\bar{z}+c
with
a,c\in\mathbb{Z}
and
b\in{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
is a binary Hermitian with discriminant
-\Delta
. Our first result is the following theorem which is analog of Theorem
1.1
:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a positive integer which is not a norm of an algebraic integer in
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
and
k\geq 1
be an odd integer. Then

(1)

H_{1,\Delta}(z)
has a constant value
\alpha_{1,\Delta}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

(2)

H_{3,\Delta}(z)
has a constant value
\alpha_{3,\Delta}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
when
d=1,3,7,

(3)

H_{5,\Delta}(z)
has a constant value
\alpha_{5,\Delta}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
when
d=3

where
\alpha_{k,\Delta}=H_{k,\Delta}(0)
.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is presented in Section
2
. The main idea in proving Theorem
1.2
is the observation that any
z\in K
, since
K
is Euclidean, can be reduced to zero by a finite number of iterations of the transformations
z\mapsto z+\lambda
and
z\mapsto\frac{1}{z}
. The
H_{k,\Delta}
is invariant under the transformation
z\mapsto z+\lambda
(see Proposition
2.2
) and transforms up to a certain polynomial
P_{k,\Delta}
under the transformation
z\mapsto\frac{1}{z}
(Propositions
2.3
). These polynomials satisfy various functional equations, and we use them to deduce that they are of a particular form when
k=1,3,5
. This allows us to conclude that for suitable choices of
d
,
H_{k,\Delta}(z)=H_{k,\Delta}(0)
for any
z\in K
when
k=1,3,5
. The fact that
H_{1,\Delta}(z)=\alpha_{1,\Delta}
,
H_{3,\Delta}(z)=\alpha_{3,\Delta}
and
H_{5,\Delta}(z)=\alpha_{5,\Delta}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
is deduced using the continuity of
H_{k,\Delta}
which is our next result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Delta
be a positive integer which is not a norm of an algebraic integer in
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
and
k\geq 1
be an odd integer. Then
H_{k,\Delta}
is continuous for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

We discuss the convergence and the continuity of
H_{k,\Delta}
and present the proof of Theorem
1.3
in Section
3
. When
k\geq 3
, the proof of Theorem
1.3
(see Theorem
3.1
) is straightforward since
h(z)=O(1/a_{h})
for all
h
occurring in Definition (
1.6
) and there are only
O(1)
functions of the form
h(z)=a|z|^{2}+bz+\bar{b}\bar{z}+c
for each value of
a
. So, the sum defining
H_{k,\Delta}
converges at most like
\sum_{a>0}a^{-k}
. However, this argument fails when
k=1
. In that case, we deduce the continuity of
H_{k,\Delta}
by showing that the binary Hermitian forms appearing in (
1.6
) for a fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}
decrease to zero exponentially. This is done by describing the binary Hermitian forms appearing in (
1.6
) for a fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}
using the the nearest integer continued fraction algorithm of
z
over the Euclidean imaginary quadratic fields.

Since
H_{k,\Delta}
is
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
-invariant, it has a well defined average value. In Section
4
, we prove the following theorem which gives the average value of
H_{k,\Delta}
in terms of
L(\chi_{d_{K}},k)
and
\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}}(k)
:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Delta
be a positive integer which is not a norm of an algebraic integer in
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
and
k\geq 1
be an odd integer. Then, the average value of
H_{k,\Delta}
is given by

\langle H_{k,\Delta}(z)\rangle_{av}=\frac{2\pi\Delta^{k+1}}{(k+1)\sqrt{|d_{K}|}}\theta(\Delta,k+1)\zeta_{\mathbb{Q}}(k+1)L(\chi_{d_{K}},k+2)^{-1}

(1.7)

where
\theta(\Delta,k)
is a finite Euler product whose factors depend on the prime factors of
\Delta
and
d_{K}
.

The proof (see Theorem
4.1
) consists of showing that the integral of
H_{k,\Delta}
over the fundamental parallelogram
\mathcal{P}
for the lattice
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
is, up to a constant, equal to the zeta function of binary Hermitian forms with coefficients in
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
and discriminant
-\Delta
. These zeta functions were studied by Elstrodt, Grunewald, and Mennicke
[
10
,
11
]
while developing the theory of representation numbers of binary Hermitian forms over
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
which parallels the classical theory of representation of integers by binary quadratic forms over
\mathbb{Z}
.

1.2
and Theorem

we immediately get the following formulas (see Corollary

(1)

Let

and

The above formulas for special values can be seen as Cohen-Zagier type formulas and as such they are computationally very efficient. In fact, using our formulas to compute the special values at

\displaystyle L(\chi_{-4},-2)=

\displaystyle L(\chi_{-4},-4)=

\displaystyle\frac{32\cdot 3^{4}}{15\cdot 68}\cdot\frac{425}{432}=\frac{5}{2}.

In the last section, we discuss the construction of a nontrivial parabolic cocycle on the Euclidean Bianchi group
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{{\mathcal{O}}}_{d})
using
H_{k,\Delta}
. We carry this out for each
d\in\{1,2,3,7,11\}
by showing that the space of parabolic cocycles on
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{{\mathcal{O}}}_{d})
is isomorphic to a certain space of polynomials
W_{k,k}
which is defined using the presentation of
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{{\mathcal{O}}}_{d})
and the cocycle condition. We then show that
P_{k,\Delta}
, which is the polynomial that
H_{k,\Delta}
transforms by under the map
z\mapsto\frac{1}{z}
, belongs to
W_{k,k}
. Consequently,
P_{k,\Delta}
, which is defined using
H_{k,\Delta}
, corresponds to a parabolic cocycle on
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{{\mathcal{O}}}_{d})
. In view of the fact that
P_{k,\Delta}
is invariant under the transformation
z\mapsto uz
where
u\in{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
is a unit, we study decomposition of
W_{k,k}
as a direct sum of the eigenspaces corresponding to the eigenvalues of the linear operator associated to the linear map
z\mapsto uz
. This allows us to precisely describe the subspace of
W_{k,k}
containing
P_{k,\Delta}
. We also compute the dimensions of
W_{k,k}
and its subspace containing
P_{k,\Delta}
for some odd values of
k
using a computer program that we wrote in Sagemath
[
21
]
. In each case, based on the numerical evidence, we conjecture dimension formulas for these spaces.

Finally, it is fair to say the cocycle property of
H_{k,\Delta}
is perhaps the most interesting and also the deepest property of
H_{k,\Delta}
as it connects
H_{k,\Delta}
to cohomology of Euclidean Bianchi groups which are central to the study of Bianchi modular forms. This connection to cohomology is a promising one and is worth studying more. Accordingly, we end the section with saying a few more words on this, and also describing some collaborative work
[
13
]
and other related questions.

2.1.
Preliminaries

In this section we establish some notation and conventions which are used throughout the rest of this paper. We fix
d=1,2,3,7,11
and let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
be the corresponding Euclidean imaginary quadratic number field with discriminant
d_{K}
. We let
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}={\mathcal{O}}_{K}
be the ring of integers of
K
and choose
\{1,\omega\}
with

\displaystyle\omega:=\frac{d_{K}+\sqrt{d_{K}}}{2}

as a fixed
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
.

Let
V_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be the space of polynomials in
z

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
. For a polynomial
P(z)
in
V_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and a matrix
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&e\end{pmatrix}\in\textbf{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
we have the right action

(P|\gamma):=(cz+e)^{n}P\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+e}\right).

(2.1)

Because
\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
acts trivially,
V_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is also
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
-module. Now, consider the
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
-module
V_{n,n}(\mathbb{C}):=V_{n}(\mathbb{C})\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muV_{n}(\mathbb{C})\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
where the overline indicates that the action of
\textbf{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
on the second factor is twisted with complex conjugation; that is for
P(z,\bar{z})
in
V_{n,n}(\mathbb{C})
and a matrix
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&e\end{pmatrix}\in\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
we have the right action

(P|\gamma):=(cz+e)^{n}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu(cz+e)\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{n}P\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+e},\frac{\bar{a}\bar{z}+\bar{b}}{\bar{c}\bar{z}+\bar{e}}\right).

(2.2)

We also extend the action of
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
on

2.2.
Sums of Binary Hermitian Forms with coefficients in
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}

Before we give the definition of
H_{k,\Delta}
we recall some relevant facts from the theory of binary Hermitian forms as discussed in
[
10
,
11
]
. Let
A
be a
2\times 2
matrix with entries in
\mathbb{C}
.
A
is said to be a Hermitian matrix if

\displaystyle A=\bar{A}^{t}

where
\bar{A}^{t}
is obtained from
A
by applying complex conjugation to each of the entries and then taking the transpose or vice versa. Let
R
be a subring of
\mathbb{C}
which is closed under conjugation, i.e.
R=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muR\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. We denote by
\mathcal{H}(R)
the set of all
2\times 2
Hermitian matrices with entries in
R
. Trivially,
h\in\mathcal{H}(R)
if and only if
h=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
\bar{b}&c\end{pmatrix}
with
a,c\in R\cap\mathbb{R}
and
b\in R
. Every
h\in\mathcal{H}(R)
defines a binary Hermitian form with coefficients in
R
. If
h=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
\bar{b}&c\end{pmatrix}
then the associated binary Hermitian form is the map
h:\mathbb{C\times C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by,

h(z,w)=\left(\begin{matrix}z&w\\
\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
\bar{b}&c\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\bar{z}\\
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\\
\end{matrix}\right)=a|z|^{2}+bz\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\bar{b}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muz\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5muw+c|w|^{2}.

We shall often call an element
h\in\mathcal{H}(R)
a binary Hermitian form with coefficients in
R
, and we shall use the notation
a_{h},b_{h},\bar{b}_{h},c_{h}
to refer to the individual matrix entries of
h
when the need arises. The discriminant
\Delta(h)
of

is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta(h)=\text{det}(h),

and
\mathcal{H}(R,\Delta)
denotes the set of all

with
\Delta(h)=\Delta
.

Similar to the action of the
\textbf{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
on binary quadratic forms, we have the action of
\textbf{GL}(2,R)
on
\mathcal{H}(R)
given by the formula

\sigma(h)=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\sigma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{t}h\sigma

(2.3)

where
\sigma\in\textbf{GL}(2,R)
and

.

]

3.
Continuity of
H_{k,\Delta}

Proving that
H_{k,\Delta}
is a continuous function when
k\geq 3
is straightforward since in that case the sum defining
H_{k,\Delta}
converges absolutely and uniformly as we show below. The argument we use to show the continuity for
k\geq 3
does not apply for
k=1
, since the bound we use to show the convergence and uniform continuity is not sharp enough. Since the summands that appear in the sum defining
H_{1,\Delta}
depend on
z
, to show the continuity of
H_{1,\Delta}
a more explicit description of these summands in terms of
z
is needed. The same issue also arises when trying to prove that
F_{2,D}
is continuous. Zagier gives an argument for the convergence of
F_{2,D}
and mentions that the continuity of
F_{2,D}
can be proved in an elementary way, but he does not provide an argument. However, based on computer experiments he observes that for a given
x
there is in fact a strong relation between the summands that appear in the sum for
F_{2,D}(x)
and the continued fraction expansion of
x
. More specifically, he observes that the summands which appear in the sum defining
F_{2,D}(x)
for a given
x
belong to a union of two lists whose elements tend to zero exponentially quickly, and each summand in each list is obtained from the previous one by applying an element of
\textbf{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
coming from the continued fraction expansion of
x
. This observation was shown to be true by Bengoechea in
[
1
]
, and as a result of this she obtains a direct proof of the continuity of
F_{2,D}
by showing that
F_{2,D}
converges exponentially quickly. To prove the continuity of
H_{1,\Delta}
we use Bengoechea’s methods but the difficulty with that approach is finding a continued fraction algorithm for complex numbers that is similar to the classical continued fraction (i.e., nearest integer continued fraction) expansion of real numbers which Bengoechea uses in her paper. Such an algorithm was developed by Hurwitz
[
16
]
and has been studied recently by a number of different people (see
[
2
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
15
]
, and other references cited therein).

3.1.
Continuity of
H_{k,\Delta}
for
k\geq 3

Theorem 3.1
.

For
k\geq 3
,
H_{k,\Delta}
is a continuous function.

Proof.

We can write
H_{k,\Delta}
as:

H_{k,\Delta}(z)=\sum_{a<0}\left(\sum_{N(b)-ac=\Delta}\max\left(0,(a|z|^{2}+bz+\bar{b}\bar{z}+c)^{k}\right)\right).

The identity
\Delta=N(\bar{b}+az)+|a|(a|z|^{2}+bz+\bar{b}\bar{z}+c),
and the inequalities
a<0<a|z|^{2}+bz+\bar{b}\bar{z}+c
together imply that
N(\bar{b}+az)<\sqrt{\Delta}
. So for each
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
\bar{b}
is an algebraic integer belonging to a disk of radius
\sqrt{\Delta}
centered at
-2az
. On the other hand the number of algebraic integers in a disk of fixed radius is finite. Thus, for each
a
the number of
b
’s satisfying the conditions of the sum is finite and independent of
z
which shows that for each
a
the number functions appearing in the inner sum is finite and independent of
z
. Also, for each
a
the functions appearing in the inner sum are bounded by
\frac{\Delta}{|a|}
. So, for each
a
and all
z
the inner sum is the sum of finitely many continuous functions
h(z,1)^{k}=(a|z|^{2}+bz+\bar{b}\bar{z}+c)^{k}
that are bounded by
\left(\frac{\Delta}{|a|}\right)^{k}
. Hence by the Weierstrass M-test, the sum in the definition of
H_{k,\Delta}
converges absolutely and uniformly and is a continuous function for
k\geq 3
.

∎

3.2.
Nearest Integer Continued Fraction Algorithm for the Euclidean Imaginary Quadratic Fields.

Let
z
be a complex number. We denote by
\lfloor z\rceil
the integer in
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
that is nearest to
z
with respect to Euclidean distance in complex plane, rounding down, in both the real and the imaginary components to break ties. The nearest integer continued fraction algorithm for the Euclidean imaginary quadratic fields proceeds by steps of the form

z_{0}=z,\quad\alpha_{n}=\lfloor z_{n}\rceil,\quad z_{n+1}=\frac{1}{z_{n}-\alpha_{n}}\quad

(3.1)

where
\alpha_{n}=a_{n}+b_{n}\omega\in{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
and
n\geq 0
. If
z\in K
the algorithm terminates when, as must eventually occur,
z_{n}=0
. If initially
z\notin K
then the algorithm continues indefinitely. Once the partial quotient
\alpha_{n}
’s have been computed, the partial convergents are computed using the usual formula from the real case:

p_{-2}=0,\quad p_{-1}=1,\quad p_{n}=\alpha_{n}p_{n-1}+p_{n-2}

(3.2)

q_{-2}=1,\quad q_{-1}=0,\quad q_{n}=\alpha_{n}q_{n-1}+q_{n-2}.

(3.3)

These numbers satisfy the equation

p_{n+1}q_{n}-p_{n}q_{n+1}=(-1)^{n},

and
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}=z
.

Similar to the real case, let us also define the numbers
\delta_{n}
associated to continued fraction expansion of
z
. The numbers
\delta_{n}
(
n\geq-1
) are defined inductively by

\delta_{-1}=z,\quad\delta_{0}=1,\quad\delta_{n+1}=\delta_{n-1}-\alpha_{n}\delta_{n},

(3.4)

and satisfy

\frac{\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n-1}}=\frac{1}{z_{n}}=z_{n-1}-\lfloor z_{n-1}\rceil.

(3.5)

If
z\in K
, then
z=\displaystyle\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}
for some
n
and the recurrence stops with
\delta_{n+1}=0
. If
z\notin K
, then
\delta_{n}
’s decay to zero exponentially quickly in
n
.

Finally, let us define the set of matrices associated to the continued fraction of
z

\displaystyle\hat{\Gamma}_{d}(z):=\left\{\gamma_{n}\in\hat{\Gamma}_{d}\,:\,\gamma_{n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}q_{n-2}&-p_{n-2}\\
-q_{n-1}&p_{n-1}\end{array}\right)\right\}

where
\hat{\Gamma}_{d}=\textbf{PGL}(2,{\mathcal{O}}_{d})
.

3.3.
Continuity of
H_{1,\Delta}

For a fixed

denote the set of functions appearing in the sum for

\Omega_{\Delta}(z)=\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{O}_{d},-\Delta)\ \big|\ a_{h}<0<h(z,1)\right\}.

Proposition 3.2

Proof.

First we check that the map is well defined. Let

\bar{b}&c\end{pmatrix}\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}{\ \ \text{and}\ \ }\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}r&s\\
t&v\end{pmatrix}\in\hat{\Gamma}_{d}(z).

\frac{1-D_{1}}{2}&\text{if }D_{1}\equiv 3\pmod{4}.\end{cases}

Theorem 4.1
.

k

\Delta

Proof.

We have

a_{h}<0\end{subarray}}\max(0,h(z,1)^{k})\,dx\,dy

Since

\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle\langle H_{k,\Delta}(z)\rangle_{av}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{O}_{d},-\Delta)\\

5.
Cocycle property of
H_{k,\Delta}

Another important property of
H_{k,\Delta}
is that it can be used to construct a nontrivial cocycle belonging to cohomology group
H^{1}(\Gamma_{d},V_{k,k}(\mathbb{C}))
. The groups
\Gamma=\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{{\mathcal{O}}})
where
{\mathcal{O}}
is the ring of integers of any imaginary quadratic field are called Bianchi groups and their cohomology groups are fundamental to the study of Bianchi modular forms (see
[
24
]
).

Let us now define
H^{1}(\Gamma_{d},V_{n,n})
. A map
f:\Gamma_{d}\to V_{n,n}
is called a
1
-cocycle if it satisfies

f(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2})=f(\gamma_{1})|\gamma_{2}+f(\gamma_{2})

(5.1)

for all
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma_{d}
and where
|\gamma_{2}
denotes the action of
\gamma_{2}
on
f(\gamma_{1})
as defined in equation (
2.2
). For a fixed
P\in V_{n,n}
the map

\gamma\mapsto P|\gamma-P

(5.2)

is a 1-cocycle and is called a 1-coboundary corresponding to
P
. The set of 1-cocycles denoted by
C(\Gamma_{d},V_{n,n})
and the set of 1-coboundaries denoted by
B(\Gamma_{d},V_{n,n})
are
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces. We have that
B(\Gamma_{d},V_{n,n})\subset C(\Gamma_{d},V_{n,n})
, and the (first) cohomology group
H^{1}(\Gamma_{d},V_{n,n})
of
\Gamma_{d}
with coefficients in
V_{n,n}
is defined to be

H^{1}(\Gamma_{d},V_{n,n})=\frac{C(\Gamma_{d},V_{n,n})}{B(\Gamma_{d},V_{n,n})}.

(5.3)

In general, if
G
is a finitely presented group and
M
is an
RG
-module for a commutative ring
R
then
H^{1}(G,M)
can be computed using the presentation of the group. An illustration of how this is carried out is given in
[
23
]
through an example (see also
[
14
]
). Since Bianchi groups are finitely presented this method is used in
[
23
,
14
]
to study the structure of
H^{1}(\Gamma,V_{n,n})
. Using the same method we will define a certain space of cocycles in
H^{1}(\Gamma_{d},V_{k,k})
that contains the cocycle constructed using
H_{k,\Delta}
.

Recall that our function
H_{k,\Delta}
is defined when
{\mathcal{O}}_{d}
is the ring of integers of an Euclidean imaginary quadratic field, i.e when
d=1,2,3,7,11
. So, we fix
\omega=i
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{1}
,
\omega=i\sqrt{2}
in
{\mathcal{O}}_{2}
,

in
{\mathcal{O}}_{3}

\displaystyle S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\

Proposition 5.1
.

The map sending

to
P_{S}\in V_{k,k}
is a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space isomorphism from

to

.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of the classical case which can be found in
[
6
, Lemma 11.8.9]
). Let
f\in C_{p}(\Gamma_{1},V_{k,k})
such that
f(\gamma)=P_{\gamma}\in V_{k,k}
and assume that
f\mapsto f(S)=P_{S}
. Since
S^{2}={\bf 1}
, the cocycle property of
f
gives

\displaystyle 0=f(S^{2})=f(S)|S+f(S)\implies P_{S}|S+P_{S}=P_{S}|({\bf 1}+S)=0.

Again using the cocycle property of
f
and the fact that
f(L)=0
leads to

\displaystyle f(SL)

\displaystyle=f(S)|L+f(L)=P_{S}|L

\displaystyle f(LS)

\displaystyle=f(L)|S+f(S)=P_{S}

But
SL=LS
and hence
P_{S}|({\bf 1}-L)=0
.

The relation
(ST)^{3}={\bf 1}
gives

\displaystyle f(ST)|(ST)^{2}+f(ST)|(ST)+f(ST)=0,

and since
f(ST)=f(S)|T+f(T)=P_{S}|T
we obtain

\displaystyle(P_{S}|T)|(ST)^{2}+(P_{S}|T)|ST+P_{S}|T

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle P_{S}|TSTST+P_{S}|TST+P_{S}|T

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(P_{S}|U^{2}+P_{S}|U+P_{S})|T

\displaystyle=0.

This shows that
P_{S}|({\bf 1}+U+U^{2})\in ker(T)
implying that
P_{S}|({\bf 1}+U+U^{2})
is the zero polynomial.

Similarly,
P_{S}|({\bf 1}+E+E^{2})=0
follows from the fact that

and

which proves that
P_{S}\in W_{k,k}
.

If

and the cocycle property of

for all

. Thus the map
f\mapsto f(S)=P_{S}
is injective.

Finally, we need to show that the map
f\mapsto f(S)=P_{S}
is surjective. Let
P\in W_{k,k}
and set

for

. Then, it is easily seen that
f
agrees with the defining relations of
\Gamma_{1}
, i.e.,
f(S^{2})=0
and the same holds for the other defining relations. The surjectivity then follows from the fact that

\displaystyle S^{2}=L^{2}=(SL)^{2}=(TL)^{2}=(T_{\omega}L)^{2}=(ST)^{3}=(T_{\omega}SL)^{3}=[T,T_{\omega}]={\bf 1}\

generates all relations for
\Gamma_{1}
.

\displaystyle P|(ST_{\omega}^{-1}+T^{-1}+STT_{\omega}^{-1}ST_{\omega}ST_{\omega}^{-1}+T_{\omega}T^{-1}ST_{\omega}^{-1}ST^{-1}+ST_{\omega}ST_{\omega}^{-1}+T_{\omega}^{-1}ST^{-1}).

But,

\displaystyle STT_{\omega}^{-1}ST_{\omega}ST_{\omega}^{-1}

\displaystyle=ST_{\omega}^{-1}TST_{\omega}ST_{\omega}^{-1}

\displaystyle T_{\omega}T^{-1}ST_{\omega}^{-1}ST^{-1}

\displaystyle=T^{-1}T_{\omega}ST_{\omega}^{-1}ST^{-1}

which shows that

\displaystyle P|(T^{-1}+ST_{\omega}^{-1}+T^{-1}T_{\omega}ST_{\omega}^{-1}ST^{-1}+ST_{\omega}^{-1}TST_{\omega}ST_{\omega}^{-1}+T_{\omega}^{-1}ST^{-1}+ST_{\omega}ST_{\omega}^{-1})=0.

It follows that
P|\varepsilon\in ker(T+ST_{\omega}+TE+ST_{\omega}E^{-1}+T_{\omega}ST+ST_{\omega}^{-1}ST_{\omega}).

∎

Proposition 5.19
.

The polynomial
P_{k,\Delta}
belongs to the subspace
W_{k,k}^{1}
and therefore the map
f:\Gamma_{11}\to V_{k,k}
defined by

\displaystyle\gamma\mapsto(H_{k,\Delta}|({\bf 1}-S))|\gamma

is a cocycle belonging to
C_{p}(\Gamma_{11},V_{k,k})
.

Proof.

The proof that
P_{k,\Delta}\in ker({\bf 1}+S)
and
P_{k,\Delta}\in ker({\bf 1}+U+U^{2})
is same as before. To show that
P_{k,\Delta}\in ker(T+ST_{\omega}+TE+ST_{\omega}E^{-1}+T_{\omega}ST+ST_{\omega}^{-1}ST_{\omega})
we calculate

\displaystyle(H_{k,\Delta}|({\bf 1}-S))|(T+ST_{\omega}+TE+ST_{\omega}E^{-1}+T_{\omega}ST+ST_{\omega}^{-1}ST_{\omega}).

Again, using the identities
H_{k,\Delta}|T_{\omega}=H_{k,\Delta}
and
H_{k,\Delta}|T_{\omega}^{-1}=H_{k,\Delta}
together with
E=T_{\omega}^{-1}ST_{\omega}ST
and
E^{-1}=T^{-1}ST_{\omega}^{-1}ST_{\omega}
we end up with

\displaystyle(H_{k,\Delta}|({\bf 1}-S))|(T+ST_{\omega}+TE+ST_{\omega}E^{-1}+T_{\omega}ST+ST_{\omega}^{-1}ST_{\omega})=H_{k,\Delta}|(ST_{\omega}E^{-1}-STE).

But

\displaystyle ST_{\omega}E^{-1}=T_{\omega}STE,

and so

\displaystyle H_{k,\Delta}|(ST_{\omega}E^{-1}-STE)=H_{k,\Delta}|(T_{\omega}STE-STE)=0

which shows that
P_{k,\Delta}\in ker(T+ST_{\omega}+TE+ST_{\omega}E^{-1}+T_{\omega}ST+ST_{\omega}^{-1}ST_{\omega})
. Finally, by part (2) of Proposition

\displaystyle P_{k,\Delta}|\varepsilon=P_{k,\Delta}

which concludes the proof that

.

5.6.
Some concluding remarks and future work

We close this section by elaborating on the importance of the conjectural formulas we have stated and how they relate to Bianchi cusp forms which are the analogs of cusp forms for imaginary quadratic fields. We also list some questions (some of which are being considered in
[
13
]
) that may have occurred to the reader but are left unanswered.

We will not define most of the objects we refer to in this subsection and be intentionally vague about them due to the fact giving precise definitions require a lot of sophisticated mathematical machinery. We also do not want to stray too much from the main theme of this paper which is to study the function
H_{k,\Delta}
. However, some good references that discuss some of the things we refer to are
[

We begin with the generalized Eichler-Shimura Isomorphism which states that

\displaystyle H^{1}_{\text{cusp}}(\Gamma,V_{n,n})\simeq S_{n+2}(\Gamma)

where
\Gamma
is a Bianchi group,
S_{n}(\Gamma)
is the vector space of Bianchi cusp forms of weight
n
on
\Gamma
, and
H^{1}_{\text{cusp}}
refers to the cuspidal cohomology of
\Gamma
. By translating the sheaf cohomology into group cohomology one can show that

\displaystyle H^{1}_{\text{cusp}}(\Gamma,V_{n,n})\simeq H^{1}_{\text{par}}(\Gamma,V_{n,n})

where
H^{1}_{\text{par}}
is the parabolic cohomology defined as the quotient of parabolic cocycles on
\Gamma
by parabolic coboundaries on
\Gamma
. We already saw that when
\Gamma=\Gamma_{d}
the space of parabolic cocycles can be identified with
W_{k,k}
. Also, one can easily show that the space of parabolic coboundaries can be identified with constant polynomials implying that it is of dimension 1. Consequently,

\displaystyle\textbf{dim}(S_{k}(\Gamma_{d}))=\textbf{dim}(W_{k-2,k-2})-1.

So, if the conjectural formulas we have stated are true then, at least in the case when
k
is odd, one immediately obtains explicit formulas, depending only on
k
, for the dimension of
S_{k}(\Gamma_{d})
. As far as we can tell, no explicit dimension formulas for
S_{k}(\Gamma)
are known. However, Finis et al.
[
14
]
have carried out extensive computations on the dimension of various cohomology groups associated to Bianchi groups which provides a way of checking the validity of our data. We are encouraged by the fact that our data does agree with theirs in the overlapping cases. Indeed, we checked our data for the

Finally, we discuss some future work and questions:

1)

The foremost question is obviously whether the formulas we have stated are true or not. Currently, we are working to answer this question in
[
13
]
. We are also trying to find and prove dimension formulas when
k
is even by looking at the experimental data. Another aspect of our work in
[
13
]
is to define the analog of the
period map
from the classical case of
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
for the Bianchi cusp forms.

2)

There is an action of Hecke operators on
H^{1}_{\text{cusp}}(\Gamma,V_{n,n})
and the isomorphism

\displaystyle H^{1}_{\text{cusp}}(\Gamma,V_{n,n})\simeq S_{n+2}(\Gamma)

is a Hecke module isomorphism. It will be interesting to transfer the action of Hecke operators to the space
W_{k,k}
and explore its consequences. Indeed, Zagier
[
27
]
has done this for the space of period polynomials which is defined similarly as
W_{k,k}
and has obtained interesting results for the traces of Hecke operators. We plan to carry this out in
[
13
]
or in a subsequent paper.

3)

Reader may be wondering if there are any other values of
k
, other than the ones we have found, for which
H_{k,\Delta}
is constant. The anonymous referee also asked about this. The answer is most likely no since it is shown in
[
14
]
that the dimension of
H^{1}_{\text{cusp}}(\Gamma,V_{n,n})
grows at least linearly in
n
which means that the dimension of the space of parabolic cocycles containing
P_{k,\Delta}
grows at least linearly in
n
as well, and
H_{k,\Delta}
is constant precisely when the subspace containing
P_{k,\Delta}
in the direct sum decomposition of the space of parabolic cocycles is one dimensional. This, along with computational evidence we have gathered, strongly suggest that there are no other
k
values, other than the ones we have found, for which
H_{k,\Delta}
is constant.

4)

Finally, the reader may also be wondering whether the kernels of the linear maps defining
W_{k,k}
can be combined into a single or fewer kernels. We have not pursued this, but it is likely to be true. Indeed, this is true for the space of parabolic cocycles on
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
defined as the intersection of
ker({\bf 1}+S)
and
ker({\bf 1}+U+U^{2})
where
S
and
U
are the generators of
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. It can be easily checked, which is left as an exercise in
[
28
]
, that

\displaystyle f\in ker({\bf 1}+S)\cap ker({\bf 1}+U+U^{2})\iff f\in ker({\bf 1}-US-U^{2}S)

where
f
is a parabolic cocycle on
\textbf{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
