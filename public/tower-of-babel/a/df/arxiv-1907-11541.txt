Phase Transition Unbiased Estimation in High Dimensional Settings
Stéphane Guerrier, Mucyo Karemera

Abstract
An important challenge in statistical analysis concerns the control of the finite sample bias of estimators. This problem is magnified in high dimensional settings where the number of variables p p diverge with the sample size n n . However, it is difficult to establish whether an estimator 𝜽 ^ \hat{\bm{\theta}} of 𝜽 0 \bm{\theta}_{0} is unbiased and the asymptotic order of 𝔼 ⁡ [ 𝜽 ^ ] − 𝜽 0 \mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]-\bm{\theta}_{0} is commonly used instead. We introduce a new property to assess the bias, called phase transition unbiasedness, which is weaker than unbiasedness but stronger than asymptotic results. An estimator satisfying this property is such that ∥ 𝔼 ⁡ [ 𝜽 ^ ] − 𝜽 0 ∥ 2 = 0 \big\lVert\mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]-\bm{\theta}_{0}\big\rVert_{2}=0 , for all n n greater than a finite sample size n ∗ n^{\ast} . We propose a phase transition unbiased estimator by matching an initial estimator computed on the sample and on simulated data. It is computed using an algorithm which is shown to converge exponentially fast. The initial estimator is not required to be consistent and thus may be conveniently chosen for computational efficiency or for other properties. We demonstrate the consistency and the limiting distribution of the estimator in high dimension. Finally, we develop new estimators for logistic regression models, with and without random effects, that enjoy additional properties such as robustness to data contamination and to the problem of separability.



Keywords:
Finite sample bias, Iterative bootstrap, Two-step estimators, Indirect inference, Robust estimation, Logistic regression.

1. Introduction

An important challenge in statistical analysis concerns the control of the (finite sample) bias of estimators. For example, the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) has a bias that can result in a significant inferential loss. Indeed, under usual regularity conditions, the MLE is consistent and has a bias of asymptotic order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
, where
n
denotes the sample size. More generally, we qualify an estimator as
asymptotically unbiased of order
\alpha
if it has a bias of asymptotic order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\alpha})
, elementwise, where
\alpha>0
. Thus, the MLE is typically asymptotically unbiased of order 1 and its bias vanishes as
n
diverges. However, when
n
is finite, the bias of the MLE may be large, and this bias is transferred to quantities such as test statistics where the MLE is used as a plug-in value (see e.g.
Kosmidis 2014a
for a review). This problem is typically magnified in situations where the number of variables
p
is large and possibly allowed to increase with
n
. For example,
Sur and Candès 2019
show that the MLE for the logistic regression model can be severely biased in the case where
n
and
p
become increasingly large (given a fixed ratio).

The bias of an estimator can often be reduced by using bias correction methods that have recently received substantive attention. Bias correction is often achieved by simulation methods such as the jackknife
(
Efron 1982
)
or the bootstrap
(
Efron 1979
)
, or can be approximated using asymptotic expansions (see e.g.
Cordeiro and Vasconcellos 1997
,
Cordeiro and Toyama Udo 2008
and the references therein
). For example, the bootstrap bias correction
(
Efron and Tibshirani 1994
)
allows to obtain an asymptotically unbiased estimator of order 2, provided that the initial estimator is consistent and asymptotically unbiased of order 1
(
Hall and Martin 1988
, see)
. An alternative approach is to correct the bias of an estimator by modifying its associated estimating equations. For example,
Firth 1993
provides an adjustment of the MLE score function and this approach has been successively adapted and extended to Generalized Linear Models (GLM), among others, by
Mehrabi and Matthews 1995
;
Bull et al. 2002
;
Kosmidis and Firth 2009
;
Kosmidis and Firth 2011
;
Kosmidis 2014b

. Several other bias correction methods have been proposed
and a review on existing methods can, for example, be found in
Kosmidis 2014a
.

It is in general difficult to establish whether an estimator (possibly resulting from a bias correction technique) is
unbiased
for all
n
(assuming that
n
is large enough so that the estimator can be computed), and therefore the
asymptotic
order of the bias is used to “quantify” its magnitude. However, the asymptotic order of the bias has mainly been studied in low-dimensional settings and its extension in asymptotic regimes where both
n
and
p
can tend to infinity is often unclear. To address this, we introduce a new property to quantify the bias of an estimator in possibly high dimensional settings called
Phase Transition (PT) unbiasedness
. This property, presented in Definition
1
(below), can serve as a middle-ground between unbiasedness and the asymptotic order of the bias.

Definition 1

(Phase Transition unbiasedness)
:

An estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is said to be PT-unbiased if there exists a
n^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\equiv\mathbb{N}\backslash\left\{0\right\}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
with
n\geq n^{*}
, we have
\big\lVert\mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]-\bm{\theta}_{0}\big\rVert_{2}=0
.

In short, this property implies that if an estimator is PT-unbiased, we have
\big\lVert\mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]-\bm{\theta}_{0}\big\rVert_{2}=0
for all
n
greater than a finite sample size
n^{\ast}
. Among others, this interpretation also explains the name of this property since, starting from a certain
n^{\ast}
, the estimator transitions from a biased phase to an unbiased phase. Therefore, PT-unbiasedness is weaker than the classical notion of unbiasedness but stronger than the asymptotic unbiasedness of order
\alpha
. Indeed, suppose that
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}
is an asymptotically unbiased estimator of order
\alpha
, then we can write
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{\theta}}]=\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\alpha}\right)
, elementwise, for a
specific

\alpha>0
. If
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
is a PT-unbiased estimator, we have
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{\theta}}]=\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\beta}\right)
, elementwise, for
all

\beta>0
and thus for all
\beta\geq\alpha
.

In this article, we propose a class of PT-unbiased estimators, called
IB-estimators
, building on the ideas of the Iterative Bootstrap (IB)

(
Kuk 1995
)
and of indirect inference
(
Smith 1993
;
Gourieroux et al. 1993
)
. In short, these simulation-based methods allow to obtain consistent estimators from inconsistent
initial
ones. Moreover,
Guerrier et al. 2019
study the order of the asymptotic bias of these estimators when the initial estimator is consistent. In particular, in low-dimensional settings, the latter work shows that these techniques present some strong similarities and can lead to asymptotically unbiased estimators of order
\delta
where
\delta\in(2,3]
. In Section
3
, we further study these simulation-based techniques in high dimensional settings and establish a connection between IB-estimators and PT-unbiasedness. In particular, we demonstrate, under suitable (and reasonable) conditions given in Section
2
that when the initial estimator is consistent or “slightly” asymptotically biased, the IB-estimator is a PT-unbiased estimator. In addition, we propose a two-step approach to attain PT-unbiasedness from inconsistent initial estimators. These new results have noticeable advantages in practical applications. As an illustration, in Section
4
we present a simulation study in a logistic regression setting (with and without random effects). We consider several slightly inconsistent initial estimators with different properties (e.g. “robustness” to separation and/or data contamination) and illustrate the effectiveness, in terms of finite sample bias and mean squared error, of the resulting IB-estimators. These simulation results are in line with the PT-unbiasedness property of IB-estimators. Moreover, they illustrate that IB-estimators can have additional properties (such as robustness) that are transferred from their initial estimator.

2. Mathematical Setup

Let
\mathbf{X}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
denote a random sample generated under model
F_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}
(possibly conditional on a set of fixed covariates), where
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\bm{\Theta}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{p}
is the parameter vector we wish to estimate. Moreover, we consider
simulated
samples that we denote as
\mathbf{X}_{h}^{*}(\bm{\theta},n)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
for
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
and where the subscript
h=1,\ldots,H
identifies the distinct samples. Next, we define
\hat{\bm{\pi}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n\right)
and
\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{*}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
as estimators of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and
\bm{\theta}
based on the samples
\mathbf{X}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)
and
\mathbf{X}^{*}_{h}(\bm{\theta},n)
, respectively. In general, we assume that
\hat{\bm{\pi}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n\right)
is a biased estimator of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
where this bias may be asymptotic and/or finite sample in nature.

In order to correct the bias of
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)
, similarly to
Guerrier et al. 2019
, we propose to use the IB to produce the sequence
\Big\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}\Big\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
defined as

\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}\equiv\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k-1)}+\left[\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{h}^{*}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k-1)},n\right)\right],

(1)

where
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}\in\bm{\Theta}
and
H
is an integer chosen
a priori
. For example, when
\hat{{\bm{\pi}}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\in\bm{\Theta}
, then this estimator can be used as the initial value of the above sequence. When this iterative procedure converges, we define the IB-estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
as the limit in
k
of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}
. It is possible that the sequence is stationary in that there exists a
k^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq k^{*}
we have
\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}
. Moreover, when
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)
in (
1
) is a consistent estimator of
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
, the first step of the IB sequence (i.e.
k=1
) is equivalent to the standard bootstrap bias correction proposed by
Efron and Tibshirani 1994
which can be used to reduce the bias of a consistent estimator (under some appropriate conditions).

Remark A
:

Before proceeding to the presentation of the main results of this article, the authors would like to make the reader aware of the fact that the notation used in the article is slightly different from the one used in its appendices where most of the proofs are presented. Indeed, a simplified notation is used in the article to enhance readability while a more detailed and precise notation is used in the appendices to prove the results and avoid misleading conclusions. The notation used in the appendices is presented and explained in Appendix
A
.

In order to present the approach, it is useful to define the set
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}
as

\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}\equiv\argzero_{\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}}\;\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}^{*}_{h}(\bm{\theta},n),

(2)

which will allow us to study the properties of the sequence
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}
. As one may expect, under some suitable conditions, we have that
\hat{\bm{\theta}}\in\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}
thereby explaining why some of our assumptions concern
\bm{\Theta}
.

Remark B
:

The elements of
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}
are in fact special cases of an indirect inference estimator. Indeed, using the previous notation, an indirect inference estimator can be defined as

\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{\ast}\in\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}^{\ast}\equiv\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}}\;\Big\|\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}^{\ast}_{h}(\bm{\theta},n)\Big\|^{2}_{\bm{\Phi}}\;,

(3)

where
\bm{\Phi}
is a positive-definite matrix. In the case where
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}\neq\emptyset
, we have that
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}=\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}^{\ast}
, which clearly shows the equivalence between (
2
) and (
3
), for any positive-definite matrix
\bm{\Phi}
. Note that
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)
is referred to as the auxiliary estimator in the indirect inference framework.

Before presenting the properties of the IB sequence, we first describe the assumptions we consider.

Assumption A
:

Let
\bm{\Theta}
be a convex and compact subset of
{\rm I\!R}^{p}
such that
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}\in\operatorname{Int}(\bm{\Theta})
and

\argzero_{\bm{\theta}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}\setminus\bm{\Theta}}\;\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}^{*}_{h}(\bm{\theta},n)=\emptyset\,.

Assumption
A
is quite mild although stronger than necessary. Indeed, the convexity condition and the fact that
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is required to be in the interior of
\bm{\Theta}
are convenient to ensure that expansions can be made between
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and an arbitrary point in
\bm{\Theta}
. Similarly, the compactness is particularly convenient as it allows to bound certain quantities. Finally, the last part of the assumption essentially ensures that no solution outside of
\bm{\Theta}
exists for the estimator defined in (
2
). Therefore, the solution set
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}
may equivalently be written as

\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}=\argzero_{\bm{\theta}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}}\;\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}^{\ast}_{h}(\bm{\theta},n).

Next, we impose some conditions on the initial estimator
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)
. Denoting
\mathbf{a}_{j}
as being the
j
-th entry of a generic vector
\mathbf{a}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
, the assumption is as follows.

Assumption B
:

For all
\left(\bm{\theta},\,n\right)\in\bm{\Theta}\times\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
, the expectation

\bm{\pi}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)\right]

exists and is finite, i.e.
\lvert\bm{\pi}_{j}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)\rvert<\infty
for all
j=1,\ldots,p
.
Moreover, for all
j=1,\ldots,p

\bm{\pi}_{j}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\equiv\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}}\bm{\pi}_{j}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
exists.

Assumption
B
is likely to be satisfied in the majority of practical situations
and is particularly useful as it allows to decompose the estimator
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)
into a non-stochastic component
\bm{\pi}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
and a random term
\mathbf{v}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
. Indeed, using Assumption
B
, we can write:

\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)=\bm{\pi}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)+\mathbf{v}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right),

(4)

where
\mathbf{v}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)\equiv\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)-\bm{\pi}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
is a zero-mean random vector. It must be noticed that this assumption does not imply that the functions
\bm{\pi}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
and
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)
are continuous in
{\bm{\theta}}
. Consequently, when using the IB (see Theorem
1
below), it is possible to make use of initial estimators that are not continuous in
{\bm{\theta}}
. In
Guerrier et al. 2019
, the continuity of these functions was (implicitly) assumed while the weaker assumptions presented in this article extend the applicability of this framework also to models for discrete data such as logistic regression, which is discussed in Section
4
. We now move onto Assumption
C
(below) that imposes some restrictions on the random vector
\mathbf{v}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
.

Assumption C
:

The second moment of
\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n)
exists and there is a real
\alpha>0
such that for every
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
and for all
j=1,\,\ldots,\,p
, we have

\mathbf{v}_{j}(\bm{\theta},n)=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(n^{-\alpha})\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\lim_{n\to\infty}\;\frac{p^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{n^{\alpha}}=0.

Assumption
C
is frequently employed and typically very mild as it simply requires that the variance of
\mathbf{v}_{j}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
exists and goes to zero as
n
increases. For example, if
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)
is
\sqrt{n}
-consistent
(towards
\bm{\pi}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
) then we would have
\alpha=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and Assumption
C
would simply require that
p=o(n)
. Next, we consider the bias of
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)
for
\bm{\theta}
and we let
\mathbf{d}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)\equiv\bm{\pi}(\bm{\theta},n)-\bm{\theta}
. Using this definition we can rewrite (
4
) as follows

\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)=\bm{\theta}+\mathbf{d}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)+\mathbf{v}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right).

Moreover, the bias function
\mathbf{d}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
can always be expressed as follows

\mathbf{d}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)=\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta})+\mathbf{c}(n)+\mathbf{b}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right),

(5)

where
\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})
is defined as the asymptotic bias in the sense that
\mathbf{a}_{j}(\bm{\theta})\equiv\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}}\;\mathbf{d}_{j}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
for
j=1,\ldots,p
, while

\mathbf{c}(n)
and
\mathbf{b}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
are used to represent the finite sample bias.
More precisely,
\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})
contains all the terms that are strictly functions of
{\bm{\theta}}
,
\mathbf{c}(n)
the terms that are strictly functions of
n
and
\mathbf{b}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
the rest. This definition implies that if
\mathbf{d}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
contains a constant term, it is included in
\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})
. Moreover, the function
\mathbf{b}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
can always be decomposed into a linear and a non-linear term in
{\bm{\theta}}
, i.e.

\mathbf{b}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)=\mathbf{L}(n)\bm{\theta}+\mathbf{r}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right),

(6)

where
\mathbf{L}(n)\in{\rm I\!R}^{p\times p}
and
\mathbf{r}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
does not contain any linear term in
\bm{\theta}
. Denoting
\mathbf{A}_{j,l}
as the entry in the
j
-th row and
l
-th column of a generic matrix
\mathbf{A}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p\times p}
, we consider Assumption
D
which imposes some restrictions on the different terms of the bias function.

Assumption D
:

The bias function
\mathbf{d}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
is as follows:

1.

The function
\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta})
is a contraction map in that for any
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\in\bm{\Theta}
such that
\bm{\theta}_{1}\neq\bm{\theta}_{2}
we have

\big\lVert\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta}_{2})-\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\big\rVert_{2}<\big\rVert\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\big\lVert_{2}\,.

2.

There exist real
\beta,\gamma>0
such that for all
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
and any
j,l=1,\dots,p
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{j,l}(n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\beta}),\;\;\;\mathbf{r}_{j}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma}),\;\;\;\lim_{n\to\infty}\;\frac{p^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}{n^{\beta}}=0\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\lim_{n\to\infty}\;\frac{p^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{n^{\gamma}}=0.

3.

Defining
c_{n}\equiv\displaystyle{\max_{j=1,\dots,p}}\mathbf{c}_{j}(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we require that the sequence
\left\{c_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\;p^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}c_{n}=0.

The first part of Assumption
D
is reasonable provided that the asymptotic bias is relatively “small” compared to
{\bm{\theta}}
(up to a constant term). For example, if
\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})
is sublinear in
{\bm{\theta}}
, i.e.
\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbf{M}{\bm{\theta}}+\mathbf{s}
, then the first part of Assumption
D
would be satisfied if the Frobenius norm is such that
||\mathbf{M}||_{F}<1
since

\Big\lVert\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta}_{2})-\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\Big\rVert_{2}=\Big\lVert\mathbf{M}(\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1})\Big\rVert_{2}\leq\big\lVert\mathbf{M}\big\rVert_{F}\big\lVert\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\big\rVert_{2}<\big\lVert\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\big\rVert_{2}.

(7)

Moreover, the function
{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta})
(as defined in Assumption
B
) is often called the asymptotic binding function in the indirect inference literature (see e.g.
Gourieroux et al. 1993
). To ensure the consistency of such estimators, it is typically required for
{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta})
to be continuous and injective. In our setting, this function is given by
{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta})={\bm{\theta}}+\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})
and its continuity and injectivity are directly implied by the first part of Assumption
D
. Indeed, since
\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta})
is a contraction map, it is continuous. Moreover, taking
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\in\bm{\Theta}
with

{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{1})={\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{2})
, then
\big\lVert{\mathbf{a}}(\bm{\theta}_{1})-{\mathbf{a}}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\big\rVert=\big\lVert\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}\big\rVert
,
which is only possible if
{\bm{\theta}}_{1}={\bm{\theta}}_{2}
. Thus,
\bm{\pi}(\bm{\theta})
is injective.

Remark C
:

In situations where
\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta})
is not a contraction map, a possible solution is to modify the sequence considered in the IB as follows:

\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k)}\equiv\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k-1)}+\varepsilon_{k}\left[\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}^{*}_{h}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(k-1)},n)\right],

with
\varepsilon_{k}\in(0,1]
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\varepsilon_{k}=\varepsilon
(i.e. a constant),
\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta})
does not need to be a contraction map. Indeed, if
\bm{\Theta}
is bounded and
\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta})
is differentiable, it is always possible to find an
\varepsilon
such that
\varepsilon\,\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta})
is a contraction map. A formal study on the influence of
\varepsilon_{k}
on the IB algorithm is, however, left for further research.

While more general, the second part of Assumption
D
would be satisfied, for example, if
\mathbf{b}(\bm{\theta},n)
is a sufficiently smooth function in
\bm{\theta}
and/or
n
, thereby allowing a Taylor expansion, as considered, for example, in
Guerrier et al. 2019
. Moreover, this assumption is typically less restrictive than the approximations that are commonly used to describe the bias of estimators. Indeed, a common assumption is that the bias of a consistent estimator (including the MLE), can be expanded in a power series in
n^{-1}
(see e.g.
Kosmidis 2014a
and
Hall and Martin 1988
in the context of the iterated bootstrap), i.e.

\mathbf{d}(\bm{\theta},n)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(j)}(\bm{\theta})}{n^{j}}+\mathbf{g}({\bm{\theta}},n),

(8)

where
\mathbf{h}^{(j)}(\bm{\theta})
is
\mathcal{O}(1)
elementwise, for
j=1,\ldots,m
, and
\mathbf{g}({\bm{\theta}},n)
is
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-(m+1)}\right)
elementwise, for some
m\geq 1
. The bias function
\mathbf{d}(\bm{\theta},n)
given in (
8
) clearly satisfies the requirements of Assumption
D
.
Moreover, under the form of the bias postulated in (
8
), we have that
\beta,\gamma\geq 1
. If the initial estimator is
\sqrt{n}
-consistent, we have
\alpha=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, and therefore the requirements of Assumptions
C
and
D
, i.e.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\;\max\left(\frac{p^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{n^{\min(\alpha,\gamma)}},\frac{p^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}{n^{\beta}}\right)=0,

are satisfied if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\;\frac{p^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}{n}=0.

(9)

The last part of Assumption
D
is particularly mild. Indeed, it simply requires that
\mathbf{c}(n)
, i.e. the part of bias that only depends on the sample size
n
, goes to
\mathbf{0}
faster than
\sqrt{p}
. For the vast majority of estimators the function
\mathbf{c}(n)
is either very small or equal to
\mathbf{0}
as the bias generally depends on
\bm{\theta}
.

The assumptions presented in this section are mild and likely satisfied in most practical situations. Moreover, compared to conditions considered in many instances (see e.g.
Guerrier et al. 2019
and the references therein), our assumption framework allows to relax various requirements:
(i)
the initial estimator may have an asymptotic bias that depends on
\bm{\theta}
;
(ii)
the initial estimator may also be discontinuous in
\bm{\theta}
(as is commonly the case when considering discrete data models);
(iii)
the finite sample bias of the initial estimator is allowed to have a more general expression with respect to previously defined bias functions;
(iv)
some of the technical requirements (for example on the topology of
\bm{\Theta}
) are relaxed
(v)

p
is allowed to increase with the sample size
n
. Nonetheless, our assumption framework is not necessarily the weakest possible in theory and may be further relaxed (as discussed in Remark
E
of Appendix
H
). However, we do not attempt to pursue the weakest possible conditions to avoid overly technical treatments in establishing the theoretical results of the following section.

3. Main results

In this section, we study the convergence of the IB sequence along with the properties of the IB-estimator. In particular, in Section
3.1
we discuss the convergence of the IB sequence together with the consistency of the IB-estimator. In Section
3.2
, under the conditions set in Section
2
, we show that the IB-estimator is PT-unbiased while the asymptotic distribution of the estimator is discussed in Section
3.3
. The asymptotic results presented in this section are somewhat unusual as we always consider arbitrarily large but finite

3.3.   Asymptotic Normality

In this section we study the asymptotic distribution of the IB-estimator. In the case where
p\to c<\infty
as
n\to\infty
, the results of
Gourieroux et al. 1993
can be directly applied to obtain

\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Xi}_{H}\right),

(14)

where

\bm{\Xi}_{H}\equiv\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)\left(\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}\right)^{T}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1},

and where

\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\equiv\mathbf{I}+\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\theta}^{T}}\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta})\right|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}},\;\;\;\;\;\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\equiv\lim_{n\to\infty}\;\sqrt{n}\var\left(\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\right).

Apart from technical requirements (such as the continuity of
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta})
in
\bm{\theta}_{0}
), the main condition ensuring the validity of (

\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)
(or
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)
), i.e.

\sqrt{n}\,\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\right).

However, in the case where
p\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, such a condition requires to be considerably modified by assuming instead that
\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)
satisfies a specific Gaussian approximation. Moreover, several other additional technical requirements are needed to adapt (

diverges. To avoid an overly technical discussion in the main text, these conditions are stated and discussed in Appendix

together with Theorem
3
in Appendix

which proposes a possible high dimensional counterpart of (

). Unfortunately, many of the conditions needed to derive these results can be strong and difficult to verify for a specific model and initial estimator. In the case where

as

, Theorem
3
becomes equivalent to (

. Informally, Theorem

such that

\sqrt{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)^{-1/2}\mathbf{u}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)^{-1/2}\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left({0},1\right),

(15)

In practice, the estimation of the variance of the IB-estimator can be obtained through different methods. A simple approach takes advantage of the results of the last iteration of the IB sequence to construct a parametric bootstrap estimator of the variance of the initial estimator
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)
. Indeed, in the last iteration of the IB sequence,
H

\hat{\bm{\theta}}
(which is a consistent estimator), which allows to compute the following quantity (assuming

to be sufficiently large)

\widehat{\var}(\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n))\equiv\frac{1}{H-1}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left[\hat{\bm{\pi}}^{*}_{h}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},n)-\bar{\bm{\pi}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},n)\right]\left[\hat{\bm{\pi}}^{*}_{h}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},n)-\bar{\bm{\pi}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},n)\right]^{T},

where
\bar{\bm{\pi}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},n)\equiv\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}^{*}_{h}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},n)
. Then, an estimator of the covariance matrix of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
can be obtained as follows

\widehat{\var}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})\equiv\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)\left(\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}\right)^{T}\widehat{\var}(\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n))\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{-1},

where
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}
can be obtained by numerical derivation of
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n)
evaluated at
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
.

4. Application: Logistic Regression Model

In this section, we apply the methodology developed in Sections
2
and
3
to investigate the performance of IB-estimators in three different settings. As previously mentioned, our conditions allow the initial estimator to be discontinuous in
\bm{\theta}
and it is therefore of interest to consider the logistic regression model, which may be the most commonly used model for binary (response) data. To illustrate the flexibility of the IB estimation approach, as initial estimators we select slightly modified versions of the MLE and of a robust estimator. As explained further on, these modifications were introduced to allow the estimators to be “robust” to the problem of data separation. To compare these IB-estimators, we consider the MLE, the robust estimator as well as the bias corrected estimator proposed by
Kosmidis and Firth 2009
. Their respective performance is studied when the data is generated at the model but also under slight model misspecification where “outliers” are randomly created. Then, we extend the logistic regression model to include a random intercept, a special case of Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) for which there is no closed form expression for the likelihood function. In this case, the initial estimator is selected to be a numerically simple approximation to the MLE, and its finite sample behaviour is compared to the ones of the MLE computed using several (more precise) approximation methods.

4.1.   Bias Corrected Estimators for the Logistic Regression Model

4.1.1. Introduction

The Logistic Regression Model (LRM)
(
Nelder and Wedderburn 1972
;
McCullagh and Nelder 1989
)
is one of the most frequently used models for binary response variables conditioned on a set of covariates. However, it is well known that in some quite frequent practical situations, the MLE is biased or its computation can become very unstable, especially when performing some type of resampling scheme for inference. The underlying reasons are diverse, but the main ones are the possibly large
p/n
ratio, separability (leading to regression slope estimates of infinite value) and data contamination (robustness).

The first two sources are often confounded and practical solutions are continuously sought to overcome the difficulty in performing “reasonable” inference. For example, in medical studies, the bias of the MLE together with the problem of separability has led to a rule of thumb called the number of Events Per Variable (EPV), that is the number of occurrences of the least frequent event over the number of predictors, which is used in practice to choose the maximal number of predictors one is “allowed” to use in a LRM (see e.g.
Austin and Steyerberg 2017
and the references therein
).

The problem of separation or near separation in logistic regression is linked to the existence of the MLE which is not always guaranteed
(
Silvapulle 1981
;
Albert and Anderson 1984
;
Santner and Duffy 1986
;
Zeng 2017
, see)
. Alternative conditions for the existence of the MLE have been recently developed in
Candès and Sur 2019

(
Sur and Candès 2019
, see also)
. In order to detect separation, several approaches have been proposed (see for instance,
Lesaffre and Albert 1989
;
Kolassa 1997
;
Christmann and Rousseeuw 2001
). The adjustment to the score function proposed by
Firth 1993
has been implemented in
Kosmidis and Firth 2009

(
Kosmidis and Firth 2010
, see also)
to propose a bias corrected MLE for GLM which has the additional natural property that it is not subject to the problem of separability
. Moreover, the MLE is known to be sensitive to slight model deviations that take the form of outliers in the data leading to the proposal of several robust estimators for the LRM and more generally for GLM (see e.g.
Cantoni and Ronchetti 2001
;
Cíźek 2008
;
Heritier et al. 2009
and the references therein
.

Despite all the proposals for finite sample bias correction, separation and data contamination problems, no estimator has so far been able to handle the three potential sources of bias jointly. In this section, we make use of the IB-estimator which is built through a simple adaptation of available estimators. Although the latter estimators might possibly not be the best ones for this problem, they at least guarantee, at a reasonable computational cost, a reduced finite sample bias which is comparable, for example, to that of the bias reduced MLE of
Kosmidis and Firth 2009
in uncontaminated data settings as well as reducing the bias in contaminated data settings. Moreover, in both cases, we adapt the initial estimator so that it is not affected by the problem of separability.

4.1.2. Bias Corrected Estimators

Consider the LRM with response
\mathbf{Y}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)
and linear predictor
\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}
, where
\mathbf{X}
is an
n\times p
matrix of fixed covariates with rows
\mathbf{x}_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
, and with logit link
\mu_{i}(\bm{\beta})\equiv\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta},n)]=\exp(\mathbf{x}_{i}\bm{\beta})/(1+\exp(\mathbf{x}_{i}\bm{\beta}))
. The MLE for
{\bm{\beta}}
is given by

\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)\equiv\argzero_{{\bm{\beta}}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\left[\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)-\mu_{i}(\bm{\beta})\right],

(16)

and can be used as an initial estimator in order to obtain the IB-estimator in (
1
).

To avoid the (potential) problem of separation, we follow the suggestion of
Rousseeuw and Christmann 2003
to transform the observed responses
\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)
to get pseudo-values

\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)=(1-\delta)\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)+\delta\left(1-\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)\right),

(17)

for all
i=1,\dots,n
, where
\delta\in\left[0,0.5\right)
is a (fixed) “small” (i.e. close to 0) scalar. For a discussion on the choice of
\delta
and also possible asymmetric transformations, see
Rousseeuw and Christmann 2003
. Jointly using these two approaches (i.e. MLE in (
16
) computed on the pseudo-values) as an initial estimator, we denote the estimator resulting from the IB procedure as the IB-MLE.

As a robust initial estimator, we consider the robust
M
-estimator proposed by
Cantoni and Ronchetti 2001
, with general estimating function (for GLMs) given by

\bm{\psi}_{\hat{{\bm{\pi}}}}\left({\bm{\beta}},\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)\right)\equiv\psi_{c}\left(r\left({\bm{\beta}},\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)\right)\right)w\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)V^{-1/2}\left(\mu_{i}({\bm{\beta}})\right)(\partial/\partial{\bm{\beta}})\mu_{i}({\bm{\beta}})-\mathbf{a}\left({\bm{\beta}}\right),

(18)

with
r\left({\bm{\beta}},\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)\right)\equiv\left(\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)-\mu_{i}({\bm{\beta}})\right)V^{-1/2}\left(\mu_{i}({\bm{\beta}})\right)
being the Pearson residuals and with consistency correction factor

\mathbf{a}\left({\bm{\beta}}\right)\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{c}\left(r\left({\bm{\beta}},\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)\right)\right)w\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)V^{-1/2}\left(\mu_{i}({\bm{\beta}})\right)(\partial/\partial{\bm{\beta}})\mu_{i}({\bm{\beta}})\right],

(19)

where the expectation is taken over the (conditional) distribution of the responses
\mathbf{Y}_{i}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)
(given
\mathbf{x}_{i}
). For the LRM, we have
V\left(\mu_{i}({\bm{\beta}})\right)\equiv\mu_{i}({\bm{\beta}})(1-\mu_{i}({\bm{\beta}}))
. We
compute the robust initial estimator
\hat{{\bm{\pi}}}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)
on the pseudo-values (
17
), using the implementation in the
glmrob
function of the
robustbase
package in R
(
Maechler et al. 2019
)
, with
\psi_{c}
in (
18
) being the Huber loss function (with default parameter
c
) (see
Huber 1964
) and
w\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)=\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}
,
h_{ii}
being the
i
th diagonal element of
\mathbf{X}\left(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}
. The resulting robust estimator is taken as the initial estimator in (
1
) to get a robust IB-estimator that we call IB-ROB. The resulting IB-estimator is in fact robust in the sense that it has a bounded influence function
(
Hampel et al. 1986
;
Hampel 1974
, see)
.

Both initial estimators are not consistent estimators, even if we expect the asymptotic bias to be very small (for small values of
\delta
in (
17
)), but both IB-estimators should have a reduced finite sample bias where, in addition, the IB-ROB is also robust to data contamination.

4.2.   Bias Corrected Estimator for the Random Intercept Logistic Regression Model

An interesting way of extending the LRM to account for the dependence structure between the observed responses is to use the GLMM family
(
Nelder and Wedderburn 1972
;
Breslow and Clayton 1993
;
Lee and Nelder 2001
;
McCulloch and Searle 2001
;
Jiang 2007
, see e.g.)
. We consider here the case of the random intercept model, which is a suitable model in many applied settings. The binary response is denoted by
\mathbf{Y}_{ij}(\bm{\beta}_{0},n)
, where
i=1,\ldots,m
and
j=1,\ldots,n_{i}
. The expected value of the response is expressed as

\bm{\mu}_{ij}(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta}|U_{i})\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{Y}_{ij}(\beta_{0},\bm{\beta},n)|U_{i}\right]=\frac{\exp{\left(\beta_{0}+\mathbf{x}_{ij}^{T}\bm{\beta}+U_{i}\right)}}{1+\exp{\left(\beta_{0}+\mathbf{x}_{ij}^{T}\bm{\beta}+U_{i}\right)}},

(20)

where
\beta_{0}
is the intercept,

\mathbf{x}_{ij}
is a
q
-vector of covariates,

\bm{\beta}
is a
q
-vector of regression coefficients and the random effect
U_{i},\;i=1,...,m
is a normal random variable with zero mean and (unknown) variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Because the random effects are not observed, the MLE is derived on the marginal likelihood function
where the random effects are integrated out. These integrals have no known closed-form solutions, so approximations to the (marginal) likelihood function have been proposed, including the pseudo- and Penalized Quasi- Likelihood (PQL)
(
Schall 1991
;
Wolfinger and O’connell 1993
;
Breslow and Clayton 1993
, see)
, LAplace approximations (LA)
(
Raudenbush et al. 2000
;
Huber et al. 2004
)
and adaptive Gauss-Hermite Quadrature (GHQ)
(
Pinheiro and Chao 2006
)
. It is well known that PQL methods lead to biased estimators while LA and GHQ are more accurate
(
Bolker et al. 2009
;
Kim et al. 2013
, for extensive accounts of methods across software and packages, see e.g.)
. The
lme4
R package
(
Bates et al. 2015
)
uses both the LA and GHQ to compute the likelihood function, while the
glmmPQL
R function of the
MASS
library
(
Venables and Ripley 2002
, that supports)
uses the PQL.

Appendix B
Mathematical Setup

In this appendix, we redefine the mathematical setup presented in the main text in order to make it more precise, by using the notations presented in Table
3
. Let us define
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}\right)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
as being a random sample generated under model
F_{\bm{\theta}}
(possibly conditional on a set of fixed covariates), where
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{p}
is the parameter vector of interest and
\bm{\omega}\in{\rm I\!R}^{m},\,m\geq n>0
, represents a random variable explaining the source of randomness of the sample. More specifically,
\bm{\omega}
can be considered as a random
seed
that produces, conditionally on its value and given a value of
\bm{\theta}
, the sample
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}\right)
of size
n
in a deterministic manner. Indeed,
\bm{\omega}
can be conceived as a random variable issued from a model
G
, thereby justifying the definition of the random sample as
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}\right)
. With this in mind, denoting “
\overset{d}{=}
” as “equality in distribution”, there is no requirement for
\bm{\omega}
to be unique since it is possible to have
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}\right)\overset{d}{=}\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}^{\ast}\right)
even though
\bm{\omega}\overset{d}{\neq}\bm{\omega}^{\ast}
. For simplicity, throughout this article we will consider
\bm{\omega}
as only belonging to a fixed set
{\bm{\Omega}}\equiv\left\{\bm{\omega}_{h}\right\}_{h=1,\dots,H}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{m}
. In this setting, defining
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\bm{\Theta}
, we let
\bm{\omega}_{0}
and
\bm{\theta}_{0}
denote respectively the unknown
random
vector and
fixed
parameter vector used to generate the random sample
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)
that will be used to estimate
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. Knowing that the sample
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)
is generated from a random seed
\bm{\omega}_{0}
, we can also consider other samples that we denote as
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}\right)
, where
(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega})\in\bm{\Theta}\times\mathbb{N}^{\ast}\times\bm{\Omega}
, which can therefore be simulated based on different values of
\bm{\omega}
(and
\bm{\theta})
. Notice that, based on this premise, we have that
\bm{\omega}_{0}\not\in{\bm{\Omega}}
which therefore implies that, in the context of this article, it is not possible to generate samples using
\bm{\omega}_{0}
. Hence, the use of
\bm{\omega}
allows to explicitly display the randomness of a sample. In addition, with this notation it is not only possible to clearly distinguish the observed sample and the simulated ones but also to define the difference (or equivalence) between two simulated samples, say
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}_{j}\right)
and
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}_{l}\right)
.

Now, we focus on the estimation procedure aimed at targeting the value of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
from
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)
. For this purpose, let us define
\hat{\bm{\pi}}\left(\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)\right)
as a biased estimator of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
which, despite being possibly inconsistent, is either readily available or can easily be computed. For simplicity of notation, from this point onward we will refer to the estimator
\hat{\bm{\pi}}\left(\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)\right)
as
\hat{\bm{\pi}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)
. Similarly, to the definitions of Section
2
we define, for all
(n,H)\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
, the following set

\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{(n,H)}\equiv\argzero_{\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}}\;\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0})-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}),

(B.1)

where
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}_{h})
is obtained on
\mathbf{X}\left(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right)
. This definition assumes that the solution set
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{(n,H)}
is not empty for all
(n,H)\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
, which is reasonable when
\dim(\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}))=\dim(\bm{\theta})
.
In similar fashion, the IB sequence
\left\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
can defined using the notation of this appendix as

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}\equiv\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k-1)}+\left[\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0})-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k-1)},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right)\right],

(B.2)

where
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(0)}\in\bm{\Theta}
. When this iterative procedure converges, we define the IB-estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
as the limit in
k
of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}
.

Appendix C
Assumptions and Asymptotic Framework

Various assumptions are considered and, in order to provide a general reference for the type of assumption, we employ the following conventions to name them:

•

Assumptions indexed by “A” refer to the topology of
\bm{\Theta}
;

•

Assumptions indexed by “B” refer to the existence of the expected value of the initial estimator;

•

Assumptions indexed by “C” refer to the random component of the initial estimator;

•

Assumptions indexed by “D” refer to the bias of the initial estimator.

When needed, sub-indices or upper-indices will be used to distinguish assumptions of the same type (for example Assumptions
A1
and
A
are different but both related to the topology of
\bm{\Theta}
).

As it was mentioned in the main text, the asymptotic framework we use here is somewhat unusual as we always consider arbitrarily large but finite
n
and
p
. Indeed, if
p
is such that
p\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, our asymptotic results may not be valid when taking the limit in
n
(and therefore in
p
) but are valid for all finite
n
and
p
. The difference between the considered framework and others where limits are studied is rather subtle as, in practice, asymptotic results are typically used to derive approximations in finite samples for which the infinite (dimensional) case is not necessarily informative. Indeed, the topology of
{\rm I\!R}^{p}
for any finite
p
and
{\rm I\!R}^{\infty}
are profoundly different. For example, the consistency of an estimator is generally dependent on the assumption that
\bm{\Theta}
is compact. In the infinite dimensional setting closed and bounded sets are not necessarily compact. Therefore, this assumption becomes rather implausible for many statistical models and would imply, among other things, a detailed topological discussion of requirements imposed on
\bm{\Theta}
. Similarly, many of the mathematical arguments presented in this article may not apply in the infinite dimensional case. Naturally, when
p\to c<\infty
as
n\to\infty
, limits in
n
are allowed. Although not necessary, we abstain from using statements or definitions such as “
\,\xrightarrow{p}
” (convergence in probability) or “
\,\xrightarrow{d}\,
” (convergence in distribution) which may lead one to believe that the limit in
n
exists. This choice is simply made to avoid confusion.

Appendix D

In this appendix, we define new assumptions that are weaker than the ones presented in the main text. These assumptions are used to study the properties of the IB sequence
\left\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
defined in (

). Our first assumption concerns the topology of the parameter space
\bm{\Theta}
.

Assumption A

:

Assumption
A1
is clearly weaker than Assumption

. It is very mild and essentially ensures that no solution outside of
\bm{\Theta}
exists for the IB-estimator defined in (

).
In addition, Assumption

doesn’t require

to be a compact set since, for example, it can represent an open set. Next, we simply restate Assumption

Assumption B

Lemma 2
:

Under Assumptions
A1
,
B
,
C
and
D1
, for all
H\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
sufficiently large, the solution space
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{(n,H)}
is a singleton.

Proof:

We define the function

\displaystyle T_{(n,H)}\;\;:

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle I\!R}^{p}

(D.2)

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta}),

where

T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta})\equiv\bm{\theta}+\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0})-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}).

We define the set of fixed points of
T_{(n,H)}
as follows

\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{(n,H)}=\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\;\;\Big|\;\;T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\theta}\right\}.

Clearly, we have that

\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{(n,H)}=\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{(n,H)},

(D.3)

as defined in (
B.1
). In the remainder of the proof we will show that an extension of the function
T_{(n,H)}
admits a unique fixed point in
{\rm I\!R}^{p}
by applying Kirszbraun theorem (see
Federer 2014
) and Banach fixed-point theorem. Then, using (
D.3
) and Assumption
A1
we will be able to conclude that the set
\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{(n,H)}
only contains this fixed point.
Let us start by using (
D.1
) and Assumptions
B
,
C
and
D1
, we have that

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0})

\displaystyle=\bm{\pi}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n\right)+\mathbf{v}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)=\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}}_{0})+\mathbf{c}(n)+\mathbf{L}(n)\bm{\theta}_{0}+\bm{\delta}^{(1)},

where
\bm{\delta}_{j}^{(1)}=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\gamma}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(n^{-\alpha}\right)
for
j=1,\,\ldots,\,p
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}_{h})

\displaystyle=\bm{\pi}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)+\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}_{h})

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}+\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})+\mathbf{c}(n)+\mathbf{L}(n)\bm{\theta}+\bm{\delta}^{(2)},

where
\bm{\delta}^{(2)}_{j}=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\gamma}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(H^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}n^{-\alpha}\right)
for
j=1,\,\ldots,\,p
. Therefore, we have

T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}}_{0})-\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})+\mathbf{L}(n)\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}\right)+\bm{\delta}^{(1)}-\bm{\delta}^{(2)}.

Next, we consider the following quantity for
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\in\bm{\Theta}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta}_{1})-T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}}_{2})-\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}}_{1})+\mathbf{L}(n)\left(\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\right)+\bm{\delta}^{(3)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}}_{2})-\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}}_{1})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{L}(n)\left(\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{\delta}^{(3)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

where
\bm{\delta}_{j}^{(3)}=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\gamma}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(n^{-\alpha}\right)
for
j=1,\,\ldots,\,p
. Then, using the fact that

\left\lVert\mathbf{L}(n)\left(\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{L}(n)^{T}\mathbf{L}(n)\right\rVert_{F}\,\left\lVert\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{F}
is the Frobenius norm, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta}_{1})-T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}}_{2})-\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}}_{1})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{L}(n)^{T}\mathbf{L}(n)\right\rVert_{F}\,\left\lVert\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\bm{\delta}^{(3)}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

We now consider each term of the above equation separately. First, since
\bm{a}({\bm{\theta}})
is a contraction map, there exists
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that

\left\lVert\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}}_{2})-\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}}_{1})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\varepsilon\left\lVert{\bm{\theta}}_{2}-{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\right\rVert_{2}.

Secondly, we have

\lVert\mathbf{L}(n)^{T}\mathbf{L}(n)\rVert_{F}\,\left\lVert\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\Delta\;\left\lVert\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

where
\Delta=\mathcal{O}\left(p^{2}n^{-2\beta}\right)
. Indeed, by writing
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{L}(n)^{T}\mathbf{L}(n)
we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathbf{B}\rVert_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{l=1}^{p}B_{j,l}^{2}}\leq p\max_{j,l=1,\,\ldots,\,p}|B_{j,l}|=p\max_{j,l=1,\,\ldots,\,p}|\sum_{m=1}^{p}\mathbf{L}_{m,j}(n)\mathbf{L}_{m,l}(n)|

\displaystyle\leq p^{2}\max_{j,l,m=1,\,\ldots,\,p}|\mathbf{L}_{m,j}(n)\mathbf{L}_{m,l}(n)|=\mathcal{O}\left(p^{2}n^{-2\beta}\right).

Finally, we have

\lVert\bm{\delta}^{(3)}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\bm{\delta}_{j}^{(3)}\right)^{2}\leq p\max_{j=1,\,\ldots,\,p}\left(\bm{\delta}_{j}^{(3)}\right)^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(pn^{-2\gamma}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(pn^{-2\alpha}\right).

By combining these results, we have

\left\|T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta}_{1})-T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq(\varepsilon^{2}+\Delta)\left\|\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(pn^{-2\gamma}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(pn^{-2\alpha}\right).

Since
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
by Assumptions
C
and
D1

, for sufficiently large
n
we have that for all
\bm{\theta}_{1},\,\bm{\theta}_{2}\in\bm{\Theta}

\left\|T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta}_{1})-T_{(n,H)}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\right\|_{2}<\left\|\bm{\theta}_{2}-\bm{\theta}_{1}\right\|_{2}.

(D.4)

Using Kirszbraun theorem, we can extend
T_{(n,H)}
to a contraction map from
{\rm I\!R}^{p}
to itself. Therefore, applying Banach fixed-point theorem, there exists a unique fixed point
\bm{\theta}^{\ast}_{(n,H)}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
such that

T_{(n,H)}\left(\bm{\theta}^{\ast}_{(j,n,H)}\right)=\bm{\theta}^{\ast}_{(j,n,H)}\,.

By Assumption
A1
we have that
\bm{\theta}^{\ast}_{(j,n,H)}\in\bm{\Theta}
, which implies by (
D.3
) that

\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{(n,H)}=\left\{\bm{\theta}^{\ast}_{(j,n,H)}\right\},

which concludes the proof.

∎

Building on the identification result given in the above lemma, the following proposition states that the IB-estimator is the limit of the IB sequence.

Proposition 1
:

Under Assumptions
A1
,
B
,
C
and
D1
, for all
H\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
sufficiently large, the IB sequence
\left\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
has a limit which is the IB-estimator, i.e.

\lim_{k\to\infty}\;\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}\in\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}_{(n,H)}.

Moreover, there exists a real
\epsilon\in(0,\,1)
such that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}

\left\lVert\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}\right\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}({p}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,\epsilon^{k}).

Proof:

We start by recalling that
\left\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined as

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}=T_{(n,H)}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k-1)}\right)

where
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(0)}\in\bm{\Theta}
and the function
T_{(n,H)}
is defined in (
D.2
).
Using the same arguments used in the proof of Lemma
2
we have that
T_{(n,H)}
allows to apply Banach fixed-point theorem implying that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\;\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},

which concludes the first part of the proof.

For the second part, consider a
k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
. By the inequality (
D.4
) of the proof of Lemma
2
, there exists an
\epsilon\in(0,\,1)
such that

\left\|T_{(n,H)}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}\right)-T_{(n,H)}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\left\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}\right\|_{2}.

By construction of the sequence
\left\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and since
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
is a fixed point of
T_{(n,H)}
, we can derive that

\left\|T_{(n,H)}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}\right)-T_{(n,H)}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon^{k}}{1-\epsilon}\left\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(0)}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(1)}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}({p}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\epsilon^{k}),

which concludes the second part of the proof.
∎

\argzero_{\bm{\theta}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}\setminus\bm{\Theta}}\;\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0})-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}_{h})=\emptyset\,.

D3
.

Assumption D
3
:

Consider the functions
\mathbf{d}(\bm{\theta},n)
,
\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})
,
\mathbf{b}(\bm{\theta},n)
and
\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta},n)
defined in (
5
) and (
6
) and let
\mathbf{R}(\bm{\theta},n)\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\,{\bm{\theta}}^{T}}\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta},n)\in{\rm I\!R}^{p\times p}
be the Jacobian matrix of
\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta},n)
in
{\bm{\theta}}
. These functions are such that for all
{\bm{\theta}}\in\bm{\Theta}
:

1.

There exists a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p\times p}
and a vector
\mathbf{s}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
such that
\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbf{M}{\bm{\theta}}+\mathbf{s}
.

2.

There exists a sample size
n^{*}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
satisfying
n\geq n^{*}
the matrix
(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{L}(n)+\mathbf{I})^{-1}
exists.

3.

There exists a real
\gamma>0
such that for all
j=1,\dots,p
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{j}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma})\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{p^{2}}{n^{\gamma}}=0.

4.

\mathbf{R}(\bm{\theta},n)
exists and is continuous in
{\bm{\theta}}\in\bm{\Theta}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
with
n\geq n^{\ast}
.

Compared to Assumption
D∗
, Assumption
D3
is weaker. First, the matrix
\mathbf{M}
may be such that
\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert_{F}\geq 1
, thereby relaxing the contraction mapping hypothesis on
\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})
given in Assumptions
D
,

D∗
and
D1
. Second, conditions 3 and 5 of Assumption
D∗
are removed.

Clearly, neither Assumption
D2
nor Assumption
D3
imply each other. The first part of Assumption
D3
imposes a restrictive form for the bias function
\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})
. If
\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega})
is a consistent estimator of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, then this restriction is automatically satisfied.
Under these new conditions, the PT-unbiasedness of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
is established in Proposition
3
below.

Proposition 3
:

Under Assumptions
A
,
B
and
D3
,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
is a PT-unbiased estimator, i.e. there exists a sample size
n^{*}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
satisfying
n\geq n^{*}
and for all
H\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
, we have
\Big\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}\right]-\bm{\theta}_{0}\Big\rVert_{2}=0
.

Before giving the proof we introduce the following useful notation. Let
\mathbf{f}\,:\bm{\Theta}\to{\rm I\!R}^{p}
and
\mathbf{F}({\bm{\theta}})~\equiv~\frac{\partial}{\partial\,{\bm{\theta}}^{T}}\mathbf{f}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\in{\rm I\!R}^{p\times p}
be its Jacobian matrix. Then, we define
\mathbf{F}\big({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{f})}\big)
such that when using the multivariate mean value theorem between
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
and
{{\bm{\theta}}}_{0}
we obtain

\mathbf{f}\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}\right)=\mathbf{f}\left({{\bm{\theta}}}_{0}\right)+\mathbf{F}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{f})}\right)\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-{{\bm{\theta}}}_{0}\right).

(F.1)

Therefore,
{\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{f})}
corresponds to a set of
p
vectors lying in the segment
(1-\lambda)\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}+\lambda{{\bm{\theta}}}_{0}
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
(with respect to the function
\mathbf{f}
). Keeping the latter notation in mind (for a generic function
\mathbf{f}
), we provide the proof of Proposition
3
.

Proof:

From (
B.1
) we have that
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
is such that for
(n,H)\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{N}^{\ast}

\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0})=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}).

Using Assumptions
B
and
D3
, we expand each side of the above equation as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0})

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta}_{0})+\mathbf{c}(n)+\mathbf{L}(n)\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)+\mathbf{v}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)

(F.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n,\bm{\omega}_{h})

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}+\mathbf{a}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)})+\mathbf{c}(n)+\mathbf{L}(n)\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}+\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbf{v}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}).

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n,\bm{\omega}_{h})-\hat{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{L}(n)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)})-\mathbf{a}(\bm{\theta}_{0})\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)-\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{L}(n)+\mathbf{M}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)-\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}.

By hypothesis,
n
is such that the inverse of
\mathbf{B}\equiv\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{L}(n)+\mathbf{M}
exists and we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right]=-\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)-\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\right].

(F.3)

By Assumptions
A
and
D3
,
\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)
is a bounded random variable on a compact set. Moreover, since
\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta},n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma})
elementwise by Assumption
D3
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)-\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\right]=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma})

(F.4)

elementwise. Consequently, we deduce from (
F.3
) that

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right]=\mathcal{O}(pn^{-\gamma})

(F.5)

elementwise.

The idea now is to re-evaluate
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)-\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\right]
using the mean value theorem. This will allow us to make an induction that will show that for all
\delta\in\mathbb{N}

\Big\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right]\Big\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left((p^{2}n^{-\gamma})^{\delta}\right).

We will then use Lemma
1
to conclude the proof.

Applying the mean value theorem for vector-valued functions to
\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)-\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)
we have

\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)-\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)=\mathbf{R}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(\mathbf{r})},n\right)\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right).

where the matrix
\mathbf{R}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(\mathbf{r})},n\right)
is defined in (
F.1
).
By Assumptions
A
and
D3
,
\mathbf{R}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(\mathbf{r})},n\right)
is also a bounded random variable. Moreover, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{R}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(\mathbf{r})},n\right)\right]=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma})
elementwise since by Assumption
D3

\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta},n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma})
elementwise, and

\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta},n)=\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)+\mathbf{R}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(\mathbf{r})},n\right)\left(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right).

for all
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
.
For simplicity, we denote

\mathbf{R}\equiv\mathbf{R}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(\mathbf{r})},n\right),\ \ \ \mathbf{\Delta}^{(\mathbf{r})}\equiv\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)-\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \mathbf{\Delta}\equiv\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0},

which implies that
\mathbf{\Delta}^{(\mathbf{r})}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Delta}
. Moreover, a consequence of (
F.5
) is that

\big|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}_{j}\right]\big|=\mathcal{O}(pn^{-\gamma}),

for any
j=1,\dots,p
and hence

\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}\right]\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(p^{2}n^{-\gamma}\right).

Now, for all
l=1,\dots,p
we have

\mathbf{\Delta}^{(\mathbf{r})}_{l}=\sum^{p}_{m=1}\mathbf{R}_{l,m}\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}\leq p\max_{m}\mathbf{R}_{l,m}\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}.

(F.6)

Using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathbf{R}_{l,m}\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{R}_{l,m}^{2}\right]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}^{2}\right]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

Since
\mathbf{R}_{l,m}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}
are bounded random variables on a compact set,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{R}_{l,m}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\gamma}\right)
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(pn^{-\gamma}\right)
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{R}_{l,m}^{2}\right]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}^{2}\right]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\gamma}\right)\mathcal{O}\left(pn^{-\gamma}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(pn^{-2\gamma}\right).

(F.7)

Therefore, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathbf{R}_{l,m}\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{R}_{l,m}^{2}\right]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}^{2}\right]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(pn^{-2\gamma}\right),

and hence, using (
F.6
) we deduce that

\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}^{(\mathbf{r})}_{l}\right]\Big|\leq p\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{m}|\mathbf{R}_{l,m}\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}|\right]=\mathcal{O}\left((pn^{-\gamma})^{2}\right).

Considering this, and since
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}\right]=-\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}^{(\mathbf{r})}\right]
, we have, for all
j=1,\dots,p

\big|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}_{j}\right]\big|=\mathcal{O}\left(p^{3}n^{-2\gamma}\right),

and consequently,

\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}\right]\rVert_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left((p^{2}n^{-\gamma})^{2}\right).

Since
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}\right]=-\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}^{(\mathbf{r})}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}^{(\mathbf{r})}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Delta}\right]
, one can repeat the same computations and deduce by induction that for all
\delta\in\mathbb{N}

\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}\right]\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left((p^{2}n^{-\gamma})^{\delta}\right).

Using equation (
F.7
) we see that

\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{\Delta}\right]\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}_{\delta\in\mathbb{N}}\left((p^{2}n^{-\gamma})^{\delta}\right),

which ends the proof by Lemma
1
. ∎

Assumption D

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{p^{\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}}}{n^{\gamma}}=0.

Clearly, Assumption

. Neither Assumption

under the conditions of Proposition

Corollary 1
:

Under Assumptions
A
,
B
,
C1
, and
D4
,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
is a consistent estimator of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
for all

in the sense of Proposition

.

Proof:

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
. We need to show that there exists a sample size
n^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
satisfying

\Pr\left(||\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}||_{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\delta.

From Chebyshev’s inequality we have that

\Pr\left(||\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}||_{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[||\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}||_{2}^{2}\right]}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Therefore, we only need to show that there exists a sample size
n^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
such that for all

\mathbb{E}\left[||\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}||_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{2}\delta.

Using the same notation as in the proof of Proposition
3
, we have from (
F.2
) and for
n
sufficiently large that

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}

(F.8)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{r}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)-\mathbf{r}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n)+\mathbf{v}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0}\right)-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbf{v}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n,\bm{\omega}_{h})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Delta}+\mathbf{v}\right),

where
\mathbf{v}
is zero mean random vector of order
\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(n^{-\alpha}\right)
elementewise by Assumption
C
. Thus, by Assumption
D4
we have that for all
j=1,\dots,p

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum^{p}_{l=1}\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\right)_{j,l}\left(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Delta}+\mathbf{v}\right)_{l}=\sum^{p}_{l=1}\sum^{p}_{m=1}\left(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\right)_{j,l}\left(\mathbf{R}_{l,m}\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}+\mathbf{v}_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum^{p}_{l=1}\sum^{p}_{m=1}\mathcal{O_{\rm p}}(n^{-\gamma})+\mathcal{O_{\rm p}}(n^{-\alpha})=\sum^{p}_{l=1}\sum^{p}_{m=1}\mathcal{O_{\rm p}}\left(\max(n^{-\alpha},n^{-\gamma})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O_{\rm p}}\left(p^{2}\max(n^{-\alpha},n^{-\gamma})\right).

Therefore we have,

\left\lVert\mathbf{\Delta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k}^{2}=p\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(p^{4}\max(n^{-2\alpha},n^{-2\gamma})\right)=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(p^{5}n^{-2\min(\alpha,\gamma)}\right),

and thus since
\bm{\Theta}
is compact by Assumption

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\mathbf{\Delta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(p^{5}n^{-2\min(\alpha,\gamma)}\right).

Using Assumptions
C1
and
D4
the last equality implies that there exists a sample size
n^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
satisfying
n\geq n^{\ast}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\mathbf{\Delta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{2}\delta.

Appendix G
Asymptotic Distribution of the IB-Estimator

In order to study the asymptotic normality of the IB-estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
we modify some of the assumptions in order to incorporate additional requirements on the auxiliary estimator.

Before introducing the next assumption we define various quantities. First, we let

\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}},n)\equiv\var\left(\sqrt{n}\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega})\right),

where
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}},n)
is nonsingular.
Then, using this definition, we introduce the following quantity

Y({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega},\mathbf{u})\equiv\sqrt{n}\mathbf{u}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}},n)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}),

where
\mathbf{u}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
is such that
||\mathbf{u}||_{2}=1
. Clearly, from the definition of
\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega})
we have that
\mathbb{E}[Y({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega},\mathbf{u})]=0
. Therefore, without loss of generality we can always decompose
Y({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega},\mathbf{u})
as follows:

Y({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega},\mathbf{u})=Z({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega},\mathbf{u})+\delta_{n}W({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega},\mathbf{u}),

(G.1)

where
W({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega},\mathbf{u})
and
Z({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega},\mathbf{u})
are zero mean random variables and
\big(\delta_{n}\big)\in\mathfrak{D}
where

\mathfrak{D}\equiv\left\{\left(\delta_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}}\,\big|\;\delta_{n}\in{\rm I\!R}\setminus(-\infty,0)\;\forall n,\;\delta_{l}\geq\delta_{m}\;\;\text{if}\;\;l<m\;\;\text{and}\;\;\lim_{n\to\infty}\delta_{n}=0\right\}.

In Assumption
C∗
below, we restrict the behaviour of
\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega})
and in particular we require that it satisfies a specific Gaussian approximation based on the decomposition considered in (
G.1
).

Assumption C
∗
:

Preserving the requirements of Assumption
C
we add the following conditions on
\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega})
. There exists a sample size
n^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that:

1.

For all
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
with
n\geq n^{\ast}
, the matrix
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}},n)
exists, is nonsingular and is such that
\bm{\Sigma}_{j,l}({\bm{\theta}},n)=\mathcal{O}(1)
for all
j,l=1,\dots,p
.

2.

For all
\bm{\omega}\in\bm{\Omega}
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
with
n\geq n^{\ast}
, the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{V}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega})\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\,{\bm{\theta}}^{T}}\mathbf{v}\left(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega}\right)
exists and is continuous in
{\bm{\theta}}\in\bm{\Theta}
.

3.

Considering the decomposition in (
G.1
), then for all
\mathbf{u}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
such that
||\mathbf{u}||_{2}=1
, there exist sequences
\big(\delta_{n}\big)\in\mathfrak{D}
, as well as a random variable
W({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega},\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(1)
, such that

Z({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega},\mathbf{u})
is a standard normal random variable.

The first requirement of Assumption
C∗
is quite mild and commonly assumed as it simply requires that
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}},n)
is a suitable covariance matrix. In addition, it implies that
\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega})=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)
, elementwise. Similarly to the discussion following Assumption
D∗
, our second requirement ensures that
\mathbf{V}_{j,l}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n,\bm{\omega})
is a bounded random variable for all
j,l=1,\dots,p
and all
(n,\bm{\omega})\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\times\bm{\Omega}
.
The third condition of Assumption
C∗
describes how “close” the distribution of
\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega})
is to a multivariate normal distribution. Such an assumption is quite strong and may not always be satisfied. In the case where
p\to c<\infty
as
n\to\infty
, our requirement on the distribution of
\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega})
can simply be expressed as

\sqrt{n}\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},n,\bm{\omega})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})),

where
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}})\equiv\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}},n)
. Finally, Assumption
C∗
clearly implies Assumption
C
but not necessarily Assumption
C1
.

In Assumption
D5
below, we impose an additional requirement on the bias of the auxiliary estimator.

Assumption D
5
:

There exists a sample size
n^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
satisfying
n\geq n^{\ast}
the Jacobian matrices
\mathbf{A}(\bm{\theta})\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\,{\bm{\theta}}^{T}}\mathbf{a}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
and
\mathbf{R}(\bm{\theta},n)\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\,{\bm{\theta}}^{T}}\mathbf{r}\left(\bm{\theta},n\right)
exist and are continuous in
{\bm{\theta}}\in\bm{\Theta}
. Moreover, we require that for all
\mathbf{u}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
with
||\mathbf{u}||_{2}=1
, there exists a sequence
(\delta_{n})\in\mathfrak{D}
such that

\scalebox{0.98}{$\sqrt{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{u}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Big[\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)-\mathbf{B}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{r})},n\right)\Big]\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n})$},

(G.2)

where

\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta},n)\equiv\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{A}(\bm{\theta})+\mathbf{L}(n)+\mathbf{R}(\bm{\theta},n),

\mathbf{B}\big({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{r})},n\big)\equiv\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{A}({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{a})})+\mathbf{L}(n)+\mathbf{R}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{r})},n\right).

while
\mathbf{A}({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{a})})
and
\mathbf{R}({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{r})})
are defined using notation in (
F.1
).

Assumption
D5
allows us to quantify how “far” the matrices
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)
and
\mathbf{B}\big(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)},n\big)
are from each other. In the case where
p\to c<\infty
as
n\to\infty
, the consistency of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
and the continuity of
\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta})\equiv\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta},n)
in
\bm{\theta}_{0}
would be sufficient so that Assumption
D5
would not be needed to establish the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
. However, when
p\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
the requirement in (
G.2
) can be strong and difficult to verify for a specific model and auxiliary estimator. Having stated this, the following proposition defines the distribution of the IB-estimator.

Proposition 4
:

Under Assumptions
A
,
B
,
C∗
and
D5
, for all
\mathbf{u}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
such that
||\mathbf{u}||_{2}=1
, there exist a sample size
n^{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
and a sequence
\left(\delta_{n}\right)\in\mathfrak{D}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
satisfying
n\geq n^{\ast}
we have

\scalebox{0.99}{$\sqrt{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{u}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\overset{d}{=}Z+\delta_{n}\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(\max\left(1,\frac{p^{2}}{\sqrt{H}}\right)\right)$},

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Proof:

Without loss of generality we can assume that the sample sizes
n^{*}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
of Assumption
C∗
and
D5
are the same. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
be such that
n\geq n^{*}
.
By the definition of the IB-estimator
we have

\hat{\bm{\pi}}\left({\bm{\theta}_{0}},n,{\bm{\omega}_{0}}\right)=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\hat{\bm{\pi}}\left({\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right).

(G.3)

Since the auxiliary estimator may be expressed, for any
({\bm{\theta}},n,\bm{\omega})\in\bm{\Theta}\times\mathbb{N}^{*}\times\bm{\Omega}
, as

\hat{\bm{\pi}}\left({\bm{\theta}},n,{\bm{\omega}}\right)=\bm{\theta}+\mathbf{a}\left({\bm{\theta}}\right)+\mathbf{c}(n)+\mathbf{L}_{n}\bm{\theta}+\mathbf{r}\left({\bm{\theta}},n\right)+\mathbf{v}\left({\bm{\theta}},n,{\bm{\omega}}\right).

(G.4)

we deduce from (
G.3
), by using the mean value theorem on
\mathbf{a}({\bm{\theta}})
and
\mathbf{r}({\bm{\theta}},n)
, that

\mathbf{B}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{r})},n\right)\bm{\Delta}=\mathbf{v}\left({\bm{\theta}_{0}},n,{\bm{\omega}_{0}}\right)-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbf{v}\left({\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right),

(G.5)

where
\mathbf{B}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{r})},n\right)\equiv\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{A}({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{a})})+\mathbf{L}(n)+\mathbf{R}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{r})},n\right)
and
\bm{\Delta}\equiv\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}
. Applying the mean value theorem on
\mathbf{v}\left({\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}},n,{\bm{\omega}_{h}}\right)
and using the notation defined in (
F.1
), we obtain for all
h=1,\dots,H

\mathbf{v}\left({\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}},n,{\bm{\omega}_{h}}\right)=\mathbf{v}\left({\bm{\theta}_{0}},n,{\bm{\omega}_{h}}\right)+\mathbf{V}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{v})},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right)\bm{\Delta}.

Hence, let
\mathbf{u}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
be such that
||\mathbf{u}||_{2}=1
, then by Assumption
C∗
we have,

\sqrt{n}\mathbf{u}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{v}\left({\bm{\theta}_{0}},n,{\bm{\omega}_{0}}\right)=Z(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0},\mathbf{u})+\delta_{n}^{(0)}W(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0},\mathbf{u})

and for all
h=1,\dots,H

\sqrt{n}\mathbf{u}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{v}\left({\bm{\theta}_{0}},n,{\bm{\omega}_{h}}\right)=Z(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{h},\mathbf{u})+\delta_{n}^{(h)}W(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{h},\mathbf{u})

where
(\delta_{n}^{(0)}),(\delta_{n}^{(1)}),\dots,(\delta_{n}^{(H)})\in\mathfrak{D}
and

\displaystyle Z(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0},\mathbf{u})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\;\;\;\;\;\;Z(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{h},\mathbf{u})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

\displaystyle W(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0},\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(1),\;\;\;\;\;\;W(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{h},\mathbf{u})=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(1).

Without loss of generality, we can suppose that
(\delta_{n})\equiv(\delta_{n}^{(0)})=(\delta_{n}^{(1)})=\dots=(\delta_{n}^{(H)})
as one may simply modify
W(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0},\mathbf{u})
and
W(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{h},\mathbf{u})
for all
h=1,\dots,H
. For simplicity, we write
\mathfrak{X}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\mathbf{u}}\equiv\sqrt{n}\mathbf{u}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
,
Z_{0}\equiv Z(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{0},\mathbf{u})
and
Z_{h}\equiv Z(\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\bm{\omega}_{h},\mathbf{u})
.
Multiplying the right hand side of (
G.5
) by
\mathfrak{X}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\mathbf{u}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\mathbf{u}}\left(\mathbf{v}\left({\bm{\theta}},n,{\bm{\omega}_{0}}\right)-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbf{v}\left({\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right)\right)

(G.6)

\displaystyle=Z_{0}+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n})-\dfrac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\big(Z_{h}+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n})\big)-\dfrac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathfrak{X}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{V}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{v})},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right)\bm{\Delta}

The first three terms of (
G.6
) are considered separately in the following computation

\displaystyle Z_{0}+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n})-\dfrac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\big(Z_{h}+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n})\big)

\displaystyle=Z_{0}-\dfrac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}Z_{h}+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n})-\dfrac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n})

(G.7)

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}Z+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n})-\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(\frac{\delta_{n}}{\sqrt{H}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}Z+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n}),

where
Z\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
. We now consider the last term of (
G.6
). Since
\bm{\Sigma}({\bm{\theta}},n)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\mathbf{V}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{v})},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right)=\mathcal{O}_{p}\left(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)
elementwise by Assumption
C∗
and
||\mathbf{u}||_{2}=1
we have for all
h=1,\dots,H

\mathfrak{X}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{V}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{v})},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right)\bm{\Delta}=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(p)\mathbf{1}_{p}^{T}\bm{\Delta},

where
\mathbf{1}_{p}\in{\rm I\!R}^{p}
is a vector of ones. Since Assumptions
A
,
B
,
C∗
and
D5
imply that
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
is consistent estimator of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
(see Figure
4
), there exists a sequence
(\delta^{\prime}_{n})\in\mathfrak{D}
such that
\bm{\Delta}=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta^{\prime}_{n})
elementwise for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
(\delta^{\prime}_{n})=(\delta_{n})
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{X}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{V}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{v})},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right)\bm{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(p)\mathbf{1}_{p}^{T}\bm{\Delta}=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(p)\mathbf{1}_{p}^{T}\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n})\mathbf{1}_{p}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(p\delta_{n})\mathbf{1}_{p}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{p}=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(p^{2}\delta_{n}),

which leads to the computation for the last term of (
G.6
),

\dfrac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathfrak{X}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{V}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{v})},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right)\bm{\Delta}=\dfrac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(p^{2}\delta_{n})=\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(\delta_{n}\frac{p^{2}}{\sqrt{H}}\right).

(G.8)

Combining (
G.5
), (
G.6
) and (
G.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathfrak{X}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{B}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{r})},n\right)\bm{\Delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathfrak{X}_{\bm{\theta}_{0},n,\mathbf{u}}\left(\mathbf{v}\left({\bm{\theta}},n,{\bm{\omega}_{0}}\right)-\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbf{v}\left({\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}},n,\bm{\omega}_{h}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}Z+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}(\delta_{n})+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(\delta_{n}\frac{p^{2}}{\sqrt{H}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}Z+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(\delta_{n}\sqrt{\frac{H}{H+1}}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(\delta_{n}\frac{p^{2}}{\sqrt{H+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=Z+\delta_{n}\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(1\right)+\delta_{n}\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(\frac{p^{2}}{\sqrt{H}}\right)=Z+\delta_{n}\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(\max\left(1,\frac{p^{2}}{\sqrt{H}}\right)\right).

The proof is completed using (
G.2
) of Assumption
D5
.
∎

Remark D
:

From the proof of Proposition
4
, we have that

\scalebox{0.99}{$\sqrt{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{H}\right)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{u}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}_{0},n)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{B}(\bm{\theta}^{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{r})},n)\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\overset{d}{=}Z+\delta_{n}\mathcal{O}_{\rm p}\left(\max\left(1,\frac{p^{2}}{\sqrt{H}}\right)\right)$},

) in Assumption

. As a result, Assumption

Remark E
:

Other results can be derived from Figure
4
. For example, one can state a theorem ensuring that the IB-estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
is unbiased and asymptotically normal (and therefore consistent) without the guarantee that the IB sequence
\left\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is convergent. This can be of interest if another method is used to compute the IB-estimator. Another case is when one is only interested in the convergence of the IB sequence
\left\{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and the consistency of the IB-estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{(n,H)}
. Assumption
A
is stronger than necessary for such a result. The reason we use this assumption to prove Theorem
1
is guided by the rationale that we want a restricted number of general assumptions for the main Theorems
1
,
2
and
3
.
