Self-similar solutions for the Muskat equation
Eduardo García-Juárez †, Javier Gómez-Serrano ‡, Huy Q. Nguyen ∗, Benoît Pausader §

Abstract
. We show the existence of self-similar solutions for the Muskat equation. These solutions are parameterized by 0 < s ≪ 1 0<s\ll 1 ; they are exact corners of slope s s at t = 0 t=0 and become smooth in x x for t > 0 t>0 .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Muskat problem

The Muskat problem describes the evolution of the free boundary between immiscible and incompressible fluids permeating a porous medium in a gravity field. Each fluid is assumed to have constant physical properties and their velocities are governed by Darcy’s law,

\frac{\mu}{\kappa}u(z,t)=-\nabla p(z,t)-\rho g(0,1),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

where
p
,
u
denote the pressure and velocity of the fluids,
\rho
,
\mu
are their density and viscosity,
\kappa
is the permeability constant of the medium, and
g
the gravitational constant.
With or without surface tension effects, it has long been known that the problem can be reduced to an evolution equation for the free interface
[
23
,
32
,
45
]
.
The case of a two-fluid graph interface

\Gamma(t)=\{(x,f(x,t)):x\in\mathbb{R}\}

with only gravity effects admits a particularly compact form
[
23
]
, now called the
Muskat equation
:

(1.1)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}f=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial_{x}\Delta_{\alpha}f}{1+(\Delta_{\alpha}f)^{2}}d\alpha,\qquad\Delta_{\alpha}f(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(x-\alpha)}{\alpha},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{split}

Above, all physical constants have been normalized for notational simplicity.
The Muskat equation is well-posed locally in time for sufficiently smooth initial data, and globally in time if the initial interface is sufficiently flat
[
8
,
20
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
45
,
46
,
47
]
.
Most notably, an initially smooth interface can turn
[
13
]
and later lose regularity in finite time
[
14
]
. Furthermore, many other behaviors are possible, with interfaces that turn and then go back to the graph scenario
[
25
,
26
]
. Thus, finding criteria for global existence became one of the main questions for the Muskat equation.
Since equation (
1.1
) has a natural scaling given by

f(x,t)\rightarrow\lambda^{-1}f(\lambda x,\lambda t),\qquad\lambda>0,

these criteria are stated in terms of critical regularity, i.e., spaces that scale like
\dot{W}^{1,\infty}
. In this sense, having the product of the maximal and minimal slopes strictly less than
1
is sufficient for global existence
[
11
]
. See also
[
28
]
. Medium-size initial data in critical spaces but with uniformly continuous slope guarantees global wellposedness
[
20
]
. If the initial data is sufficiently small in
\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}
, then the slope can be arbitrarily large
[
27
]
and even unbounded
[
5
,
7
]
. The result
[
5
]
also shows local existence and uniqueness in
H^{\frac{3}{2}}
. This is currently the best (lowest) regularity result in terms of the space of the initial data, which is a problem that has garnered a lot of attention recently (e.g.
[
3
,
6
,
17
,
18
,
21
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
).

1.2.
Main result

In this paper, we show the existence of self-similar solutions for the Muskat problem. These solutions correspond to the global-in-time evolution of initially exact corners, and thus they do not fit into the aforementioned results
1
1

1

The results in
[
12
]
allow for merely medium size bounded slopes but require sublinear growth of the profile, while our solutions grow linearly in space.
.

We can rewrite (
1.1
) in terms of a closed system for the slope
h=\partial_{x}f
:

(1.2)

\begin{split}0=\partial_{t}h-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\Delta_{\alpha}\partial_{x}h}{1+(\fint_{\alpha}h)^{2}}d\alpha+\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\Delta_{\alpha}h)^{2}\cdot\frac{\fint_{\alpha}h}{(1+(\fint_{\alpha}h)^{2})^{2}}d\alpha,\qquad\fint_{\alpha}h(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{x-\alpha}^{x}h(z)dz.\end{split}

Plugging the ansatz
h(x,t)=k(x/t)
in (
1.2
), we arrive at the equation

(1.3)

\begin{split}0=Sk+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\Delta_{\alpha}\partial_{y}k}{1+(\fint_{\alpha}k)^{2}}d\alpha-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\Delta_{\alpha}k)^{2}\cdot\frac{\fint_{\alpha}k}{(1+(\fint_{\alpha}k)^{2})^{2}}d\alpha,\qquad S:=y\partial_{y}.\end{split}

for which we construct a local curve of solutions:

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists
s_{\ast}>0
such that for all
|s|<s_{\ast}
, there exists a self-similar solution of (
1.2
)
h_{s}(x,t)=k_{s}(x/t)
satisfying
\lim_{y\to+\infty}k_{s}(y)=s
. In addition, we have that

\begin{split}\|k_{s}(y)-s\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan(y)\|_{H^{1}}+|s|\|\partial_{s}k_{s}(y)-\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan(y)\|_{H^{1}}&\lesssim|s|^{3}.\end{split}

Remark 1.2
.

(i)
In particular, we see that
k_{s}\in L^{\infty}\setminus\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
.
(ii)
In fact, we will compare
k_{s}
and
(2s/\pi)\arctan((1+s^{2}/3)y)
, but one can readily see that the difference between the two ansätze is
O(s^{3})
.
(iii)
Due to the symmetry we can find solutions with negative
s
by setting
k_{-s}(y)=-k_{s}(y)
. From now on, we assume
s\geq 0
.

[

From our numerical results, it might look surprising that, no matter how big the slope is, the initial corner instantly smooths out, as opposed to the known “waiting time” phenomenon in the Hele-Shaw problem

Moreover, some of these works are in a one-phase setting, which even for Muskat significantly changes the possible behaviors

1.3.
Outline of the paper

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we summarize the notation that will be used along the paper. Next, in Section
3
, we first extract the quasilinear structure of (
1.3
) and rewrite it as a fixed point equation. This section contains the proof of the main Theorem
1.1

2.
Notations

2.1.
General notations

In the following, we fix
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(-4/3,4/3)
, a nonnegative even function such that
\varphi\equiv 1
on
[-3/4,3/4]
. For simplicity of notation, we let
\fint_{\pm\alpha,0}f
be an arbitrary function among

\fint_{\pm\alpha,0}f\in\{f,\fint_{\alpha}f,\fint_{-\alpha}f\}.

We will work mostly on the Fourier side. We define the Fourier transform as

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)e^{-iy\xi}dy.\end{split}

We note that the Fourier transform of a real odd function is an odd function taking purely imaginary values.
We define the Fourier multiplier
|\nabla|
by

\mathcal{F}\left\{|\nabla|f\right\}(\xi)=|\xi|\widehat{f}(\xi).

Given an operator
T
, we let

(2.1)

\widehat{T}[f]=\mathcal{F}^{-1}T[\widehat{f}]

be its conjugation by the Fourier transform. To avoid functional analytic considerations, we say that a functional
g\mapsto\mathcal{N}(g)
is analytic
around
a function
L_{s}
if for any choice of
g_{1},g_{2}
in the appropriate space (here
H^{1}
) the restricted function

\begin{split}N_{g_{1},g_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2}):=\mathcal{N}(L_{s}+t_{1}g_{1}+t_{2}g_{2})\end{split}

is analytic. In this case, we denote

(2.2)

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{1}[g]:=\frac{d}{dt_{1}}N_{g,0}(0,0),\qquad\mathcal{N}_{\geq 2}[g]:=\mathcal{N}(L_{s}+g)-\mathcal{N}(L_{s})-\mathcal{N}_{1}[g],\qquad\mathcal{N}_{\geq 1}[g]:=\mathcal{N}_{1}[g]+\mathcal{N}_{\geq 2}[g],\end{split}

and we observe that

(2.3)

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{\geq 2}(L_{s}+g_{2})-\mathcal{N}_{\geq 2}(L_{s}+g_{1})&=\int_{t_{1}=0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt_{1}}\frac{d}{dt_{2}}N_{g_{1},g_{2}-g_{1}}(t_{1},0)dt_{1}+\int_{\theta=0}^{1}(1-\theta)\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}N_{g_{1},g_{2}-g_{1}}(1,\theta)d\theta.\end{split}

In these notations, the “center”
L_{s}
is implicit, but since we will always consider functionals around
L_{s}
defined in (
3.11
), there should be no ambiguity.

2.2.
Algebra of operators

We will use operators of the form

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}(f)(y)&:=\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}m(f;\alpha,y)\cdot G(f,\fint_{\alpha}f,\fint_{-\alpha}f)\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha}\end{split}

associated to some multilinear function
f\mapsto m(f;\alpha,y)
(i.e. multilinear in
f
for each fixed
\alpha
,
y
) and some numerical analytic function
G
. For such operators, we compute that

(2.4)

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{1}[g]&=\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}m_{1}(g,L_{s},\dots,L_{s};\alpha,y)\cdot G(L_{s},\fint_{\alpha}L_{s},\fint_{-\alpha}L_{s})\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha}\\
&\quad+\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}m(L_{s};\alpha,y)\cdot{\bf v}_{g}\cdot\nabla G(L_{s},\fint_{\alpha}L_{s},\fint_{-\alpha}L_{s})\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha},\qquad{\bf v}_{g}:=(g,\fint_{\alpha}g,\fint_{-\alpha}g),\end{split}

and

(2.5)

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt_{1}}\frac{d}{dt_{2}}N_{g_{1},g_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2})&=\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}m_{2}(g_{1},g_{2},L_{t_{1},t_{2}};\alpha,y)\cdot G(L_{t_{1},t_{2}},\fint_{\alpha}L_{t_{1},t_{2}},\fint_{-\alpha}L_{t_{1},t_{2}})\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha}\\
&\quad+\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}m_{1}(g_{1},L_{t_{1},t_{2}};\alpha,y)\cdot{\bf v}_{g_{2}}\cdot\nabla G(L_{t_{1},t_{2}},\fint_{\alpha}L_{t_{1},t_{2}},\fint_{-\alpha}L_{t_{1},t_{2}})\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha}\\
&\quad+\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}m_{1}(g_{2},L_{t_{1},t_{2}};\alpha,y)\cdot{\bf v}_{g_{1}}\cdot\nabla G(L_{t_{1},t_{2}},\fint_{\alpha}L_{t_{1},t_{2}},\fint_{-\alpha}L_{t_{1},t_{2}})\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha}\\
&\quad+\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}m(L_{t_{1},t_{2}};\alpha,y)\cdot\nabla^{2}G(L_{t_{1},t_{2}},\fint_{\alpha}L_{t_{1},t_{2}},\fint_{-\alpha}L_{t_{1},t_{2}})[{\bf v}_{g_{1}},{\bf v}_{g_{2}}]\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha},\end{split}

where
m_{1}(f,g,\dots g)=d_{g}m_{1}\cdot f
and similarly for
m_{j}
, and
L_{t_{1},t_{2}}:=L_{s}+t_{1}g_{1}+t_{2}g_{2}
. Similarly

(2.6)

\begin{split}\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}N_{g_{1},g_{2}-g_{1}}(1,\theta)&=\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}m_{2}(g_{2}-g_{1},g_{2}-g_{1},L_{\theta};\alpha,y)\cdot G(L_{\theta},\fint_{\alpha}L_{\theta},\fint_{-\alpha}L_{\theta})\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha}\\
&\quad+2\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}m_{1}(g_{1}-g_{1},L_{\theta};\alpha,y)\cdot{\bf v}_{g_{2}-g_{1}}\cdot\nabla G(L_{\theta},\fint_{\alpha}L_{\theta},\fint_{-\alpha}L_{\theta})\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha}\\
&\quad+\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}m(L_{\theta};\alpha,y)\cdot\nabla^{2}G(L_{\theta},\fint_{\alpha}L_{\theta},\fint_{-\alpha}L_{\theta})[{\bf v}_{g_{2}-g_{1}},{\bf v}_{g_{2}-g_{1}}]\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha}.\end{split}

3.
Reduction to a fixed point estimate

3.1.
Analysis of the quasilinear structure

We can extract the quasilinear part from (
1.3
). This will be defined in terms of two main terms. We define the function
F
and the operator
W
as follows

(3.1)

\begin{split}F(t):=\left[1+t^{2}\right]^{-1},\qquad W[g](y):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(\fint_{\alpha}g(y))^{2}-g^{2}(y)}{1+(\fint_{\alpha}g(y))^{2}}\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha},\end{split}

and we obtain the following expression:

Lemma 3.1
.

The self-similar profile
k
satisfies

(3.2)

\begin{split}|\nabla|k-S\left[k+\frac{k^{3}}{3}\right]+W[k]\partial_{y}k&=R[k]+\mathcal{T}[k],\end{split}

where the semilinear terms are defined as

(3.3)

\begin{split}R[h]&:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{\alpha}\left\{h(y-\alpha)\right\}\cdot\frac{(\fint_{\alpha}h)^{2}-h^{2}}{1+(\fint_{\alpha}h)^{2}}\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha},\\
\mathcal{T}[h]&:=(1+h^{2})T[h];\qquad T[h]:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\Delta_{\alpha}h)^{2}\cdot F^{\prime}(\fint_{\alpha}h)d\alpha.\end{split}

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Dividing by
1+k^{2}
, it suffices to show that (
1.3
) can be rewritten as

(3.4)

\begin{split}\left[F(k)|\nabla|-S\right]k+V[k]\partial_{x}k&=F(k)R[k]+T[k],\\
V[g](y)&:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{F(g(y))-F(\fint_{\alpha}g(y))\right\}\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha}.\end{split}

The only nontrivial part is the decomposition of the second term in (
1.3
). We can expand

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{y}(\Delta_{\alpha}g)\cdot F(\fint_{\alpha}g)d\alpha&=-F(g)\cdot|\nabla|g+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{y}(\Delta_{\alpha}g)\cdot\left\{F(\fint_{\alpha}g)-F(g)\right\}d\alpha\\
&=-F(g)\cdot|\nabla|g+\partial_{y}g(y)\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{F(\fint_{\alpha}g)-F(g)\right\}\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha}\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{y}g(y-\alpha)\cdot\left\{F(\fint_{\alpha}g)-F(g)\right\}\frac{d\alpha}{\alpha},\end{split}

and rearranging the terms, we arrive at (
3.4
).

∎

3.2.
Study of the linear equation: Duhamel formula

Given a constant
\kappa>0
, we now consider the linear adjusted equation from (
3.4
)

(3.5)

\begin{split}\left(\kappa|\nabla|-S\right)k&=p_{1}+\partial_{y}p_{2}\end{split}

for an odd function
k
. Taking the Fourier transform and using Duhamel’s formula, we obtain the ODE

\begin{split}\partial_{\xi}\left\{\xi e^{\kappa|\xi|}\widehat{k}\right\}&=e^{\kappa|\xi|}\widehat{p}_{1}+i\xi e^{\kappa|\xi|}\widehat{p}_{2}.\end{split}

If we assume that
\xi\widehat{k}(\xi)
is continuous at the origin, and we integrate from
0
, we find that

(3.6)

\begin{split}\widehat{k}(\xi)&=C\frac{e^{-\kappa|\xi|}}{\xi}+{\bf T}_{-1}[\widehat{p}_{1}]+{\bf T}_{0}[\widehat{p}_{2}],\end{split}

for some constant
C
, where the linear operators are given by

(3.7)

\begin{split}{\bf T}_{-1}:g&\mapsto\frac{1}{\xi}\int_{\eta=0}^{\xi}e^{\kappa(|\eta|-|\xi|)}g(\eta)d\eta,\qquad{\bf T}_{0}:g\mapsto\frac{i}{\xi}\int_{\eta=0}^{\xi}\eta e^{\kappa(|\eta|-|\xi|)}g(\eta)d\eta.\end{split}

In particular, under our assumptions, the odd solutions to the free equation
\left(\kappa|\nabla|-S\right)k=0
with the condition
\lim_{y\to+\infty}k(y)=s
are given by

(3.8)

\begin{split}L_{s,\kappa}(y)&:=\frac{2s}{\pi}\arctan(y/\kappa).\end{split}

From now on, we shall restrict ourselves to the study of odd solutions.

3.2.1.
Operator estimates

It remains to estimate the solution operators from (
3.7
).

Lemma 3.2
.

The operators

\begin{split}{\bf T}_{-1}:g&\mapsto\frac{1}{\xi}\int_{\eta=0}^{\xi}e^{\kappa(\eta-\xi)}g(\eta)d\eta,\qquad{\bf T}_{0}:g\mapsto\frac{i}{\xi}\int_{\eta=0}^{\xi}\eta e^{\kappa(\eta-\xi)}g(\eta)d\eta,\\
\end{split}

defined for functions on
L^{2}_{loc}(0,\infty)
satisfy the boundedness properties

\begin{split}\|\xi{\bf T}_{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}+\|{\bf T}_{-1}\|_{L^{3}\to L^{2}}&\lesssim 1,\\
\|{\bf T}_{0}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}+\|{\bf T}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\xi^{2}d\xi)\to L^{2}(\xi^{2}d\xi)}&\lesssim 1.\\
\end{split}

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

To control
{\bf T}_{-1}
, we first use Hölder’s inequality to bound

\begin{split}|{\bf T}_{-1}[g](\xi)|&\leq\xi^{-\frac{1}{p}}\left(\frac{1-e^{-\kappa\xi}}{\kappa\xi}\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\left(\int_{\eta=0}^{\xi}e^{\kappa(\eta-\xi)}|g(\eta)|^{p}d\eta\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{p}}\cdot\min\{\xi^{-\frac{1}{p}},\xi^{-1}\},\end{split}

which shows the second bound. For the first, we compute by duality that

\begin{split}\langle\xi{\bf T}_{-1}[g],h\rangle&=\iint K(\xi,\eta)\overline{g}(\eta)h(\xi)d\xi d\eta,\qquad K(\xi,\eta):=\mathfrak{1}_{\{0\leq\eta\leq\xi\}}e^{\kappa(\eta-\xi)},\end{split}

and Schur’s test allows to conclude. This also gives the second estimate on
{\bf T}_{0}
, and the first one follows a similar proof with kernel
K^{\prime}(\xi,\eta)=(\eta/\xi)K(\xi,\eta)
.

∎

3.3.
Fixed point formulation

3.3.1.
Linearization at self-similar profile

The key observation we will use is that for the solutions of the linearized problem (
3.8
), the quasilinear part simplifies significantly since
L_{s,\kappa}^{2}
is almost constant away from a neighborhood of the origin:

\begin{split}L_{s,\kappa}^{2}(y)=s^{2}+p(y),\qquad p(y)=O(\langle\kappa/y\rangle^{-1}),\qquad L_{s,\kappa}^{3}=s^{2}L_{s,\kappa}+(L_{s,\kappa}^{2}-s^{2})L_{s,\kappa}.\end{split}

We can now linearize (
3.2
) at the constant
s
to get an equation of the form (
3.5
) with

(3.9)

\kappa:=\left[1+s^{2}/3\right]^{-1}.

More precisely, we obtain

\begin{split}|\nabla|k-\left[1+s^{2}/3\right]Sk=\frac{1}{3}S\left[(k^{2}-s^{2})k\right]-W[k]\partial_{y}k+R[k]+\mathcal{T}[k].\end{split}

which we prefer to rewrite via a normal form as

(3.10)

\begin{split}\left[\kappa|\nabla|-S\right]h&=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{3}|\nabla|\left[(k^{2}-s^{2})k\right]-\kappa W[k]\partial_{y}k+\kappa R[k]+\kappa\mathcal{T}[k],\\
h&:=k+\frac{\kappa}{3}(k^{2}-s^{2})k.\end{split}

3.3.2.
Fixed point formulation

We can now seek solutions of (
3.10
) as perturbations of (
3.8
) when
s,\kappa
are related as in (
3.9
). We seek solutions of the form

(3.11)

\begin{split}k(y)=L_{s}(y)+g(y),\qquad L_{s}(y):=\frac{2s}{\pi}\arctan(y/\kappa)=s\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan((1+s^{2}/3)y).\end{split}

We note for later use that
L_{s}
is a smooth function of
s
and

(3.12)

\begin{split}\partial_{s}L_{s}&=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan(y/\kappa)+\frac{4}{3\pi}\frac{s^{2}y}{1+(y/\kappa)^{2}},\\
\partial_{s}(L_{s}^{2}-s^{2})&=2s\left(s^{-2}(L_{s}^{2}-s^{2})+\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan(y/\kappa)\frac{4}{3\pi}\frac{s^{2}y}{1+(y/\kappa)^{2}}\right).\end{split}

We define
\pi_{s}
by

\pi_{s}:=(L_{s}^{2}-s^{2})L_{s}.

Then, plugging (
3.11
) into (
3.10
), we obtain

\begin{split}h&=L_{s}+(\kappa/3)\pi_{s}+(1+\kappa(L_{s}^{2}-s^{2}/3))g+\kappa L_{s}g^{2}+(\kappa/3)g^{3},\\
\left[\kappa|\nabla|-S\right]h&=\kappa^{2}|\nabla|\left[(1/3)\pi_{s}+(L_{s}^{2}-s^{2}/3)g+L_{s}g^{2}+g^{3}/3\right]\\
&\quad-\kappa W[L_{s}]\partial_{y}L_{s}-\kappa\left(W[L_{s}+g]-W[L_{s}]\right)\partial_{y}\left(L_{s}+g\right)-\kappa\partial_{y}\left(W[L_{s}]g\right)+\kappa g\partial_{y}W[L_{s}]\\
&\quad+\kappa(R[L_{s}]+R_{1}[g]+R_{\geq 2}[g]+\mathcal{T}[L_{s}]+\mathcal{T}_{1}[g]+\mathcal{T}_{\geq 2}[g]).\end{split}

Thus, using (
3.6
) and the notation (
2.1
), we obtain two equations for
h
(after identifying the element in the kernel for the second equation from the limit at
\infty
):

\begin{split}h&=L_{s}+(\kappa/3)\pi_{s}+\left\{3(1+s^{2})/(3+s^{2})+\kappa(L_{s}^{2}-s^{2})\right\}g+\kappa L_{s}g^{2}+(\kappa/3)g^{3}\\
&=L_{s}+\kappa\widehat{{\bf T}}_{-1}\left\{(\kappa/3)|\nabla|\pi_{s}-W[L_{s}]\partial_{y}L_{s}+R[L_{s}]+\mathcal{T}[L_{s}]\right\}\\
&\quad+\kappa\widehat{{\bf T}}_{-1}\{\kappa|\nabla|(L_{s}^{2}-s^{2}/3)g-\partial_{y}\left(W[L_{s}]g\right)+g\partial_{y}W[L_{s}]-W_{1}[g]\partial_{y}L_{s}+R_{1}[g]+\mathcal{T}_{1}[g]\\
&\quad+\kappa\widehat{{\bf T}}_{-1}\{\kappa|\nabla|(L_{s}g^{2}+g^{3}/3)-W_{\geq 2}[g]\partial_{y}L_{s}-W^{hi}_{\geq 1}[g]\partial_{y}g-\partial_{y}(gW^{lo}_{\geq 1}[g])+g\partial_{y}W^{lo}[g]\\
&\quad\,+R_{\geq 2}[g]+\mathcal{T}_{\geq 2}[g]\},\end{split}

where
R_{1},\mathcal{T}_{1}
,
R_{\geq 2}
,
\mathcal{T}_{\geq 2}
follow the convention in (
2.2
) and
W=W^{lo}+W^{hi}
is a decomposition later introduced in (
4.18
). Combining the two equations for
h
, we arrive at the fixed-point formulation:

(3.13)

\begin{split}\Pi&=Ag+\mathcal{N}(g),\\
\end{split}

with forcing term

(3.14)

\begin{split}\Pi&:=(\kappa/3)\pi_{s}-(\kappa^{2}/3)\widehat{{\bf T}}_{-1}[|\nabla|\pi_{s}]+\kappa\widehat{{\bf T}}_{-1}\left[W[L_{s}]\partial_{y}L_{s}-R[L_{s}]-\mathcal{T}[L_{s}]\right],\\
\end{split}

linear operator

(3.15)

\begin{split}Ag&:=-(1+\frac{2s^{2}}{3+s^{2}}+\kappa(L_{s}^{2}-s^{2}))g+\kappa^{2}\widehat{{\bf T}}_{-1}\left[|\nabla|(L_{s}^{2}-s^{2}/3)g\right]\\
&\quad+\kappa\widehat{{\bf T}}_{-1}\left[g\partial_{y}W[L_{s}]-W_{1}[g]\partial_{y}L_{s}+R_{1}[g]\right]-\kappa\widehat{{\bf T}}_{0}[W[L_{s}]g]+\kappa\widehat{{\bf T}}_{-1}[\mathcal{T}_{1}[g]],\end{split}

5.
Numerical Results

In this section, we describe how to numerically compute the branch of solutions
k_{s}
starting from the zero solution. Their existence for small
s
was proved in Theorem
1.1
. See Figures
1
and
2
below for different depictions of the solutions.

The main advantage of working with the formulation of equation (
1.3
), as opposed to a self-similar equation for the function
f
, is that all the quantities involved are bounded. Nonetheless there are a few technicalities which we outline below.

The first step consists in changing variables and transforming the infinite domain into a finite one. We do so by setting
y=\tan(z)
and
\tilde{k}(z):=k(\tan(z))=k(y)
so that the domain of definition is mapped into
\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
. This change of variables has been used successfully for other problems in fluid mechanics (see for example
[
40
]
and references therein).

Moreover, we exploit the symmetry to gain an extra cancellation at
\frac{\pi}{2}
. After performing the change of variables and a lengthy calculation, (
1.3
) is transformed into

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sin(z)\cos(z)\tilde{k}^{\prime}(z)+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\tilde{k}^{\prime}(z)\cos(z)^{2}-\tilde{k}^{\prime}(y)\cos(y)^{2}}{\tan(z)-\tan(y)}\frac{\sec(y)^{2}}{1+(\tilde{\Delta}_{y}\tilde{k}(z))^{2}}dy

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left(\frac{\tilde{k}(z)-\tilde{k}(y)}{\tan(z)-\tan(y)}\right)^{2}\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{y}\tilde{k}(z)}{(1+(\tilde{\Delta}_{y}\tilde{k}(z))^{2})^{2}}\sec(y)^{2}dy

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\tilde{k}^{\prime}(z)\cos(z)^{2}-\tilde{k}^{\prime}(y)\cos(y)^{2}}{\tan(z)+\tan(y)}\frac{1}{1+(\tilde{\Delta}_{-y}\tilde{k}(z))^{2}}\sec(y)^{2}dy

(5.1)

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left(\frac{\tilde{k}(z)+\tilde{k}(y)}{\tan(z)+\tan(y)}\right)^{2}\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_{-y}\tilde{k}(z)}{(1+(\tilde{\Delta}_{-y}\tilde{k}(z))^{2})^{2}}\sec(y)^{2}dy,

where

We performed continuation in
s
in increments of
\Delta s=0.1
and did
70
iterations, using as initial guess

(the linear approximation) at the first iteration, and for the subsequent ones the result of the previous iteration plus

to ensure that the updated boundary condition at

, other than computation time, although the errors become bigger as

To compute a solution for a fixed

]
.
Our discretization variables consist on the values of

at gridpoints

, where we are using that

is odd to solve for positive

\tilde{k}^{\prime}(0)

5.1

z_{i}

i=1,\ldots,N-1

\tilde{k}(0)=0,\tilde{k}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=s

\tilde{k}
by the derivatives of the spline. To perform the integration, we integrated in (
5.1
) in the variable
y
using trapezoidal integration and a grid of
10N=1290
points. We also tried finer grids and saw virtually no difference with respect to the results. In order to get stable results, we took care of splitting the domain into 3 regions
(z\sim\frac{\pi}{2},z\sim y
and the rest
)
and computing separately the integrand in each of them, carefully computing the limit. For example, note that despite being bounded at
z\sim\frac{\pi}{2}

\infty-\infty
if not dealt with properly. The inner integrals in (
5.1
) (i.e. the
\tilde{\Delta}
integrals) were computed using an adaptive Gauss-Kronrod quadrature of 15 points, also taking care of the limits at
\frac{\pi}{2}
and at
y
