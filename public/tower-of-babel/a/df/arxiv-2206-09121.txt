Abstract
. We prove that for any cubic polynomial of slice rank r r , the intersection of all linear subspaces of minimal codimension contained in the corresponding hypersurface has codimension ≤ r 2 + ( r + 1 ) 2 4 + r \leq r^{2}+\frac{(r+1)^{2}}{4}+r in the affine space. This is deduced from the following result of independent interest. Consider the intersection I I of linear ideals ( P i ) (P_{i}) in 𝐤 ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x n ] \mathbf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}] , with dim P i ≤ r \dim P_{i}\leq r . Then the number of quadratic generators of I I is ≤ r 2 \leq r^{2} .



1.
Introduction

Let
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be a homogeneous cubic polynomial over a field
\mathbf{k}
.
Let
{\operatorname{rk}}(f)
denote the slice rank of
f
, i.e., the minimal number
r
such that
f
belongs to the ideal
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r})

generated by linear forms. Equivalently, this is the minimal codimension in
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}
of a linear subspace contained in the hypersurface

f=0
. Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{f}
denote the set of all subspaces of linear forms
P
of dimension
{\operatorname{rk}}(f)
such that
f\in P
, and let

L_{f}:=\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{f}}P.

Geometrically,
L_{f}
is the orthogonal to the intersection of all linear subspaces of minimal codimension in the hypersurface
f=0
.
Thus,
\dim L_{f}
is the codimension of this intersection in
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}
.

In
[
1
]
it was proved that
\dim L_{f}
is bounded by a universal polynomial of degree
4
in
{\operatorname{rk}}(f)
(which does not depend on
n
).
Our main result is the following improvement of this bound.

Theorem A
.
Let
f
be a cubic of slice rank
r
. Then
\dim L_{f}\leq r^{2}+\frac{(r+1)^{2}}{4}+r
.

We deduce this from another result, possibly of independent interest.
For a homogeneous ideal
I\subset S=\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, let us define the number of generators of degree
d
as
\dim I_{d}/(S_{1}\cdot I_{d-1})
.

Theorem B
.
Let
(P_{s})_{s\in S}
be any collection of subspaces of linear forms, such that
\dim P_{s}\leq r
. Consider the intersection of
the corresponding linear ideals
I=\cap_{s\in S}(P_{s})
. Then
I
has
\leq r^{2}
generators of degree
2
.

Note that the bound in Theorem A is not far from optimal: we show that the minimal
\dim L_{f}
grows quadratically with
r
.
More precisely, let us set

c(r):=\max_{\deg f=3}\dim L_{f}.

Then Theorem A gives a quadratic upper bound on
c(r)
. We find examples of cubics (see Proposition
3.3
) that give the following lower bound:

c(r)\geq{r+1\choose 2}+r.

It would be interesting to determine the exact values of
c(r)
for small
r
. It is easy to see that
c(1)=3
, and in
[
1
]
it was shown that
c(2)=6
. Here we prove (see Theorem
4.5
) that

10\leq c(3)\leq 12.

2.
Intersections of linear ideals

2.1.
Bound on quadratic generators

We set
S=\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
P_{1},...,P_{s}
be a collection of subspaces of linear forms such that
\dim P_{i}\leq r
and

P_{1}\cap\cdots\cap P_{s}=0
.
Consider the ideal
I=(P_{1})\cap\cdots\cap(P_{s})
. Then
\dim I_{2}\leq r^{2}
.

Proof.

We show this by induction on the number of spaces
s\geq 2
. In the base case
s=2
we have
(P_{1})\cap(P_{2})=(P_{1})\cdot(P_{2})
, which is generated by
\leq r^{2}
products
of linear generators of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
.

Now assume the assertion holds for some
s
. Suppose
we have subspaces of linear forms
P_{1},...,P_{s},P_{s+1}
with
P_{1}\cap\cdots\cap P_{s+1}=0
and
\dim P_{i}\leq r
.
Set
P_{1\ldots s}:=P_{1}\cap\cdots\cap P_{s}
, which is not necessarily trivial, say
P_{1\ldots s}=\langle\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{p}\rangle
with
p=\dim P_{1\ldots s}
. Also,
set
Q:=P_{1}+\ldots+P_{s}
, and pick a subspace
C\subset Q
to be the complement of
P_{1,...,s}
in
Q
such that
Q\cap P_{s+1}\subset C
(we can do this since
P_{1,\ldots,s}\cap P_{s+1}=0
.

Let
I:=(P_{1})\cap\cdots\cap(P_{s})
. By
[
1
, Lemma 3.2]
, we have
I=SI^{\prime}
, where
I^{\prime}\subset S(Q)
is the intersection of the ideals in
S(Q)
generated by
P_{1},\ldots,P_{s}
.
Consider the following diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S(C)}

\subset

where
\pi:S(Q)\to S(Q/(P_{1\ldots s}))
is the projection, and
\overline{I}=(P/P_{1\ldots s})\cap\ldots\cap(P/P_{1\ldots s})\subset S(Q/P_{1\ldots s})
.
Since
I^{\prime}=\pi^{-1}(\overline{I})
, it is generated by
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{p}
, together with lifts of generators of
\overline{I}
, which we can choose to be in
S(C)
.
Hence,
I

\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{p}
and quadratic generators
q_{1},\ldots,q_{d}\in S(C)

\dim P_{i}/P_{1\ldots s}\leq r-p

\sum_{i=1}^{p}m_{i}\ell_{i}\in((C)\cdot(C)+(P_{s+1}))\cap(P_{1\ldots s}).

(C)\cdot(C)+(P_{s+1})=(C^{\prime})\cdot(C^{\prime})+(P_{s+1}).

Since the subspaces
P_{1\ldots s}

Hence,

\sum_{i}m_{i}\ell_{i}\in(P_{1\ldots s})\cdot(P_{s+1}).

It follows that

\dim I_{2}\cap(P_{s+1})\leq\dim((P_{1\ldots s})\cdot(P_{s+1}))_{2}+d\leq p\cdot r+(r-p)^{2}\leq r^{2}.

Proof of Theorem B.

Set
Q:=\cap_{s\in S}P_{s}
,
\overline{P}_{s}:=P_{s}/Q
. Let us consider the quotient
\overline{S}:=S/(Q)

2.2.
Example

Here we consider a simple example of intersection of three linear ideals.

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider the ideal
I=(P_{1})\cap(P_{2})\cap(P_{3})
, where
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
and
P_{3}
are subspaces of linear forms, such that

\dim P_{i}=r
and
P_{i}\cap P_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
.
Let
k=\dim P_{3}\cap(P_{1}+P_{2})
. Then

\dim I_{2}={k\choose 2}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can write

P_{1}=\langle x_{1},...,x_{r}\rangle,\ \ P_{2}=\langle y_{1},...,y_{r}\rangle,\ \ P_{3}=\langle x_{1}+y_{1},...,x_{k}+y_{k},z_{1},...,z_{r-k}\rangle,

for some linearly independent elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r},y_{1},\ldots,y_{r}.z_{1},\ldots,z_{r-k}
.
The quadratic homogeneous part of
I
is

I_{2}=\left((x_{1},...,x_{k})\cap(y_{1},...,y_{k})\cap(x_{1}+y_{1},...,x_{k}+y_{k})\right)_{2}.

It is enough to prove that

Claim 2.3
.

I_{2}=\langle x_{i}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{i}|1\leq i<j\leq k\rangle
.

Proof:
Let
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2k-1}
be the projective variety defined by the vanishing locus of all (2,2)-minors of

\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}&...&x_{k}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&...&y_{k}\end{pmatrix}

As is well known,
X
is the image of the Segre embedding

\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{k-1}\xhookrightarrow{i}\mathbb{P}^{2k-1}
, and all the quadratic equations of
X
are linear combinations of
(x_{i}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{i})
.
The projectivizations of our three spaces
(P_{1}),(P_{2}),(P_{3})
are the images of
[0:1]\times\mathbb{P}^{k-1}
,
[1:0]\times\mathbb{P}^{k-1}
and
[1:-1]\times\mathbb{P}^{k-1}
, respectively.
It now suffices to show that any quadric
q
vanishing on these three subspaces, vanishes identically on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{k-1}
.

Consider the restriction

i^{*}q\in H^{0}(i^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2k-1}}(2))=H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{k-1}}(2,2))=H^{0}(\mathcal{O_{\mathbb{P}}}(2))\otimes H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{k-1}}(2)).

By assumption,
i^{*}q
must vanish on
p_{i}\times\mathbb{P}^{k-1}
,
i=1,2,3
, where
p_{1}=[0:1]
,
p_{2}=[1:0]
,
p_{3}=[1:-1]
. This implies that

i^{*}q\in\ker\left((eval_{p_{1}}\oplus eval_{p_{2}}\oplus eval_{p_{3}})\otimes id_{H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{k-1},\mathcal{O}(2))}\right),

where
eval_{p_{i}}:H^{0}({\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}}(2))\to\mathbf{k}
is the evaluation at the point
p_{i}
. Since
\cap_{i=1}^{3}\ker(eval_{p_{i}})=0
, we get that
i^{*}q=0
.

\blacksquare

∎

3.
Bounds for
c(r)

3.1.
Proof of Theorem A

Definition 3.1
.

Let us say that a polynomial
f\in S=\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
depends on
\leq m
variables if there exists an
m
-dimensional subspace
V
of linear forms such that

f\in S(V)\subset S
.

Note that if
f
is a homogeneous polynomial, and
f\in S(V)
for some subspace of linear forms
V
, then any subspace
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{f}
is contained in
V

(since
(P)\cap S(V)\subset(P\cap V)
), hence
L_{f}\subset V
. Thus, if
f
depends on
\leq m
variables then
\dim L_{f}\leq m
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f
be a cubic contained in the ideal
I=(P_{1})\cap\ldots\cap(P_{s})
. Assume that
\cap_{i=1}^{s}P_{i}=0
, and set
W=P_{1}+\ldots+P_{s}
.
Then
f
depends on
\leq\dim W+\dim I_{2}
variables.

Proof.

We can write
f
as

f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{i}q_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}c_{j}f_{j},

where
N=\dim I_{2}
,
\ell_{i}
are linear forms,
c_{j}
are constants, and
(q_{i})
(resp.,
(f_{j})
) are quadratic (resp., cubic) generators of the ideal
I
.
By
[
1
, Lemma 3.2]
, all these generators can be chosen in
S(W)\subset S
.
Hence,
f
belongs to
S(W+\langle\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{N}\rangle)
, so it depends on
\leq\dim W+N
variables.
∎

Proof of Theorem A.

We use induction on
n
. Set
n(r)=r^{2}+\frac{(r+1)^{2}}{4}+r
.

Assume first that
\cap_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{f}}P=0
. Then we can choose an irredundant collection
P_{1},..,P_{s}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{f}
with trivial common intersection.
By
[
1
, Lemma 3.6]
, for
W=P_{1}+\cdots P_{s}
we have

\dim W\leq\frac{(r+1)^{2}}{4}+r.

By Theorem
2.1
, we have

\dim((P_{1})\cap\cdots\cap(P_{s}))_{2}\leq r^{2}
. Hence, by Lemma
3.2
,

f
depends on at most
r^{2}+\dim W\leq r^{2}+\frac{(r+1)^{2}}{4}+r=n(r)
variables, and so
\dim L_{f}\leq n(r)
.

Now assume that
\cap_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{f}}P\neq 0
.
Then there is a nonzero
l\in\cap_{i}P_{i}
. So we get

\dim L_{f}=1+\dim L_{\overline{f}},

where
\overline{f}=f\mod(l)
is the reduced cubic in the ring
S/(l)\simeq\mathbf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}]
.
Thus, using the induction assumption, we get

\displaystyle\dim L_{f}

\displaystyle\leq 1+n(r-1)

\displaystyle=1+(r-1)^{2}+r-1+\frac{r^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle=r+(r-1)^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\leq r^{2}+r+\frac{(r+1)^{2}}{4}=n(r)

as required.
∎

3.2.
Test polynomials
f_{n}

For
n\geq 2
, consider the cubics

f_{n}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}x_{i}x_{j}y_{ij}

in the polynomial rink
\mathbf{k}[x_{i},y_{jk}\ |1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j<k\leq n]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

One has
{\operatorname{rk}}(f_{n})=n-1
,
\dim L_{f}=n+{n\choose 2}
.

Proof.

Since for any pair
i<j
one has
f_{n}\in(y_{ij},(x_{k}\ |\ k\neq i,j))
, it suffices to prove
{\operatorname{rk}}(f_{n})\geq n-1
.
We proceed by induction on
n
.
We have
f_{2}=x_{1}x_{2}y_{12}
, which has rank
1
.

Suppose now
n\geq 3
and
{\operatorname{rk}}(f_{n-1})=n-2
, and assume
{\operatorname{rk}}(f_{n})\leq n-2
, i.e. there is an
(n-2)
-dimensional space of linear forms
P=\langle l_{1},\ldots,l_{n-2}\rangle
such that
f_{n}\in(P)
.

First, are going to prove that
P\cap\langle x_{1},...,x_{n}\rangle\not=0
.
By assumption,
f_{n}=l_{1}q_{1}+...+l_{n-2}q_{n-2}
for some quadratic forms
q_{i}
.
Hence, the closed subscheme
V(l_{1},\ldots,l_{n-2},q_{1},\ldots,q_{n-2})
is contained in
the singular locus
\operatorname{Sing}(f_{n})
of the hypersurface
f_{n}=0
. This singular locus is given by the following equations:

\operatorname{Sing}(f_{n})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial f_{n}}{\partial x_{i}}=\sum_{j\not=i}x_{j}y_{ij}=0\\
&\frac{\partial f_{n}}{\partial y_{ij}}=x_{i}x_{j}=0\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

where we use the convention
y_{ji}=y_{ij}
.
Clearly, we have
(x_{1},...,x_{n})\subset\operatorname{Sing}(f_{n})
. We claim that this is the irreducible component of the lowest codimension and that all other components have codimension
\geq 2n-2
.
Indeed, let
Z\subset\operatorname{Sing}(f_{n})
be an irreducible component such that
x_{i}
is not identically zero
Z
, for some
i
.
Then by the second condition of (
1
), we must have
x_{j}|_{Z}=0
for all
j\neq i
, and by the first condition we get
y_{ij}|_{Z}=0
for all
j\neq i
.
Thus,
Z
is contained in a linear subspace of codimension
2n-2
, which proves our claim. Thus, we get

\begin{split}\left(\begin{aligned} &l_{1}=l_{2}=\cdots=l_{n-2}=0\\
&q_{1}=q_{2}=\cdots=q_{n-2}=0\end{aligned}\right)\end{split}\subset Sing(f_{n})=\mbox{ }\begin{split}(x_{1},...,x_{n})\mbox{ }\bigcup\mbox{ }\begin{split}\mbox{Some components }\\
\mbox{ of codim}\geq 2n-2\end{split}\end{split}

Since the locus
V(l_{1},\ldots,l_{n-2},q_{1},\ldots,q_{n-2})
has codimension
\leq 2n-4
, it follows that it must be contained in the component
(x_{1}=\ldots=x_{n}=0)
.
If
P\cap\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle=0
, then we would get that
V(l_{1},\ldots,l_{n-2},q_{1},\ldots,q_{n-2})
is contained in the linear subspace
V(l_{1},\ldots,l_{n-2},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

of codimensions
2n-2
, which is impossible. Hence we deduce that
P\cap\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle\neq 0
.

Reordering the variables, we can assume that
l_{1}=x_{n}-c_{1}x_{1}-...-c_{n-1}x_{n-1}\in P
. This implies that the restriction of
f_{n}
to any linear subspace contained in
l_{1}=0
has
slice rank
\leq n-3
. But we have

f_{n}|_{x_{n}=c_{1}x_{1}+...+c_{n-1}x_{n-1},y_{1n}=...=y_{n-1,n}=0}=f_{n-1}

so we get a contradiction. Hence,
{\operatorname{rk}}(f_{n})\geq n-1
, as required.
∎

4.
Cubics of rank
3

4.1.
Some lemmas

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose there exist

P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\in\mathcal{P}_{f}
with pairwise trivial intersections. Then
f
depends on
\leq\frac{r(r+3)}{2}
variables.

Proof.

Let
k=\dim P\cap(P_{1}+P_{2})
, then by Lemma
2.2
, for the ideal
I=(P_{1})\cap(P_{2})\cap(P_{3})
we have
\dim I_{2}={k\choose 2}
.
By Lemma
3.2
,
f
depends on
\leq\dim W+\dim I_{2}
variables, where
W=P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3}
has dimension
3r-k
.
It remains to observe that

\dim W+\dim I_{2}=3r-k+{{k}\choose{2}}=3r+\frac{k(k-3)}{2}\leq 3r+\frac{r(r-3)}{2}=\frac{r(r+3)}{2}.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f
be a cubic contained in the ideal
I=(P_{1})\cap\ldots\cap(P_{s})
, where
\cap_{i}P_{i}=0
. Set
W=\sum_{i}P_{i}
.
Assume we have
I_{2}\subset(P)
for some
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{f}
. Then
P\subset W
.

Proof.

Let us write as in the proof of Lemma
3.2
,

f=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{i}q_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}c_{j}f_{j},

where
(q_{i})
(resp.,
(f_{j})
) are quadratic (resp., cubic) generators of the ideal
I
.
Note that we have
q_{i},f_{j}\in S(W)\subset S
. Also, by assumption
q_{i}\in(P)
. It follows that

q_{i}\in(P)\cap S(W)\subset(P\cap W)
and

\sum_{j}c_{j}f_{j}\in(P)\cap S(W)\subset(P\cap W).

Hence,
f\in(P\cap W)
. Since
\dim P\cap W\leq\dim P
, the condition
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{f}
implies that
P\cap W=P
, i.e.,
P\subset W
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose we have a collection of
3
-dimensional subspaces
(P_{i})_{i\in I}
such that any two intersect in a
2
-dimensional space.
Then either
\dim\cap_{i\in I}P_{i}\geq 2
, or
\dim\sum_{i\in I}P_{i}\leq 4
.

Proof.

Suppose we could find
P,Q
in this set such that for a pair
P_{1},P_{2}
,
P_{1}\cap P_{2}\not\subset P\subset P_{1}+P_{2}
and
P_{1}\cap P_{2}\subset Q\not\subset P_{1}+P_{2}
. Then
P\cap Q\subset P_{1}\cap P_{2}
and both of them as dimension 2, so they are equal. But this will imply
P\supset P_{1}\cap P_{2}
, a contradiction.
So either all spaces intersect in the same 2-dimensional subspace, so any space is contained in
P_{1}+P_{2}
, which is 4-dimensional.
∎

4.2.
Bounds for
c(3)

Proposition 4.4
.

One has
c(3)\leq 12
. In other words, for any cubic
f
of slice rank
3
, we have
\dim L_{f}\leq 12
.

Proof.

Clearly we can assume that
{\mathcal{P}}_{f}
contains at least
4
elements.
We distinguish several cases.

Case 1
. Assume there exist
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{f}
such that
P_{1}\cap P_{2}=0
, say,
P_{1}=\langle x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\rangle,P_{2}=\langle x_{4},x_{5},x_{6}\rangle
.
We can also assume that there exists an element
P\in\mathcal{P}_{f}
such that

P\not\subset P_{1}+P_{2}
(otherwise we have
L_{f}=P_{1}+P_{2}
).
There are several possibilities:

1.a. If
P\cap P_{1}=P\cap P_{2}=0
then by Lemma
4.1
,
f
depends on
\leq 9
variables, so we are done.

1.b. If
\dim P\cap P_{1}=1
and
\dim P\cap(P_{1}+P_{2})=1
, then we can assume that
P=\langle x_{1},x_{7},x_{8}\rangle
. In this case the ideal

I:=(P_{1})\cap(P_{2})\cap(P)
has 3 quadratic generators, namely,
x_{1}x_{4},x_{1}x_{5},x_{1}x_{6}
. Hence, by Lemma
3.2
,
f
depends on
\leq 8+3=11
variables.

1.c. If
\dim P\cap P_{1}=1
,
\dim P\cap(P_{1}+P_{2})=2
, and
P\cap P_{2}=0
, then we can assume that
P=\langle x_{1},x_{2}+x_{4},x_{7}\rangle
. In this case

I
has 3 quadratic generators, hence by Lemma
3.2
,
f
depends on
\leq 7+3=10
variables.

1.d. If
\dim P\cap P_{1}=1
,
\dim P\cap P_{2}=1
, then we can assume that
P=\langle x_{1},x_{4},x_{7}\rangle
. In this case
I
has
5
