Abstract
Humans interact and cooperate in structured societies, which are often represented by complex networks. Previous explorations mainly focus on pairwise and static network structures, where each link connects two nodes permanently and never changes. However, empirical human collective interactions go beyond static time-invariant one-to-one relationships. Recently, researchers have made vital progress in modelling higher-order interactions using hypernetworks, where a link may connect more than two individuals. Here, we study collective cooperation through temporal hypernetworks, capturing the time-varying higher-order interactions in human societies. We find that temporal hypernetworks may promote cooperation compared with their static counterparts, and the traditional static pairwise network may underestimate the positive effect of local interactions on fostering cooperators. Moreover, temporal hypernetworks with sparse components and higher-order interactions can facilitate cooperation. Surprisingly, we report that when the scale of interactions is of the same order as population size, moderately small hyperlink sizes best facilitate cooperation. Our findings underscore the significant impact of real-world temporal higher-order interactions on the evolution of cooperation.

Introduction

Cooperation among unrelated individuals stands as the cornerstone of the Tower of Babel of intricate and diverse human society

The prevalence of cooperation in human and non-human societies reflects a remarkable, albeit not universal, inclination toward altruism

and Darwin’s notion of ‘survival of the fittest’

.
This viewpoint’s perturbation partly stems from the introduction of network-structured interactions, a notable inherent characteristic of human societies.
Network reciprocity is one of the fundamental explanations for the evolution of cooperation

, where networks impose local interactions and enable cooperators to form clusters to avoid exploitations by defectors

;

.
Different types of interactions on networks may have fundamental implications for the evolution of cooperation

;
Santos and Pacheco 2005
;
Nowak et al. 2010
.

Higher-order interactions, which occur among multiple individuals, are prevalent in human and non-human systems, such as co-authorship in academia
Patania et al. 2017
, mesoscopic interactions among neurons in the nervous system
Ganmor et al. 2011
, and competitions among species in ecosystems

.
Most previous studies about higher-order interactions on networks focus on pairwise network models, where nodes represent individuals and links interactions

Following this framework, a widely adopted setting for capturing higher-order interactions is that each individual and all its neighbours form a group and interact together

;
Zhou et al. 2015
;
Perc et al. 2013
.
This means individuals interact with not only their neighbours but also their neighbours’ neighbours.
However, while changing behaviours, individuals can only imitate their neighbours rather than all interaction partners

;
Li et al. 2017
, suggesting an incompatible definition of groups and the incapability of pairwise networks to accurately describe higher-order interactions
Battiston et al. 2020
;
Majhi et al. 2022
.
Recent works point out that hypernetworks that use hyperlinks (i.e., sets of nodes, or generalized versions of ‘links’ that can connect more than two individuals) to describe groups are better and more natural network models to capture higher-order interactions than pairwise counterparts
Alvarez-Rodriguez et al. 2021
;
Iacopini et al. 2019
(Fig.

And it is shown that the presence of higher-order interactions, compared with pairwise modelling on pairwise networks, has a fundamental impact on the evolution of cooperation

;

;
Pan et al. 2023
;
Malizia et al. 2023
;
Alvarez-Rodriguez et al. 2022
.

Although hypernetworks provide a valid tool for studying the effects of higher-order interactions on the evolution of cooperation, current studies often assume that these interactions exist permanently and never change.
In reality, interactions among individuals build and dissolve over time, indicating that links only exist intermittently.
For example, human interactions through digital or face-to-face communications usually last finitely in short durations
Toroczkai and Guclu 2007
and ecological networks alter with the seasons
Saavedra et al. 2016
.
More importantly, it has been shown that such network temporality can significantly alter various dynamical processes on networks, such as the spreading of information
Masuda et al. 2013
, the evolution of cooperation
Li et al. 2020
, and controllability of systems
Li et al. 2017
.
Simultaneously, research on other dynamics within the temporal higher-order interactions has already demonstrated profound impacts
Neuhäuser et al. 2021
;
Iacopini et al. 2022
;
Majhi et al. 2022
;
Chowdhary et al. 2021
;
Cencetti et al. 2021
;
Burgio et al. 2020
;
Kumar et al. 2021
, prompting us to investigate whether this novel insight will bring about fundamental changes to the study of the evolution of cooperation.

Here, we systematically investigate the evolution of cooperation on temporal hypernetworks, where hypernetwork structures change over time.
By combining numerical simulations and theoretical analysis on both synthetic and empirical temporal networks of human interactions, we find that temporal hypernetworks with higher-order interactions and sparse components can strongly promote cooperation, and even outperform their static counterparts.
Simultaneously, by comparing different modelling of the same datasets, we validate that pairwise and static network models may underestimate the potential emergence of cooperation.
Intriguingly, when we consider the limitations of order in human offline interactions and extend our focus to larger-scale higher-order interactions, we find that, in fact, interactions of moderately small order foster cooperation.
Larger hyperlinks can be detrimental to cooperation.
Our results unveil intriguing facets of how temporal higher-order interactions impact the evolution of cooperation.

II
Model

Here, we model a temporal hypernetwork as a series of subhypernetworks.
On each subhypernetwork, a hyperlink connects multiple individuals.
As subhypernetworks change over time, hyperlinks can build or dissolve.
We first construct empirical temporal hypernetworks using empirical datasets.
The dataset can be modelled as a sequence of
\mathcal{G}
snapshots, where each contains all interactions during a non-overlapping time window
\Delta t
on a fixed population of
N
individuals (Fig. 1a).
In each snapshot, every clique is represented by a hyperlink, in which individuals interact with each other (Fig. 1b).
The snapshots within each time interval
\tau
are then subsequently aggregated, generating a sequence of
\lceil\mathcal{G}/(\tau/\Delta t)\rceil
subhypernetworks that form a temporal hypernetwork (Fig. 1c).
When
\tau=\mathcal{G}\Delta t
, we obtain the corresponding static hypernetwork (Fig. 1d).

On temporal hypernetworks, individuals within the same hyperlink engage in higher-order interactions.
We model the interactions as the public goods game, where each cooperator (
C
) pays a cost
c
, and each defector (
D
) pays nothing.
The total costs are then collected and multiplied by a synergy factor
r
to generate payoffs that are evenly distributed to all individuals within the hyperlink.
The payoff of each defector and cooperator is

\pi_{D}(n_{C})=rcn_{C},

and

\pi_{C}(n_{C})=rcn_{C}-c,

where
n_{C}
is the number of cooperators within the hyperlink.
In each round, each individual
i
accumulates the payoff
P_{i}
from all games she joins, namely, all hyperlinks she belongs to on the subhypernetwork.

When it turns to strategy updating, all individuals decide whether to change their strategies or not synchronously.
Individual
i
selects a random hyperlink and compares her payoff with a random neighbour
j
in the group.
Here individual
i
imitates
j
’s strategy with probability given by the Fermi function
1/\{1+\exp[-s({P}_{j}-{P}_{i})]\}
,
and
s
is the intensity of selection
Szabó and Tőke 1998
;
Traulsen et al. 2006
.
Individuals play games with their neighbours
g
rounds on every subhypernetwork before it changes to the next one.
Note that isolated nodes neither play games with others nor update their strategies, as they are not involved in any interactions at this point.

To tell whether the evolution of cooperation is promoted, we focus on the fraction of cooperators
f_{C}
, and the critical value
r_{C}
where
f_{C}
is starting to be above zero.
As the size of the system is finite, the dynamics will inevitably arrive at an absorbing state (either full cooperation or defection), the fraction of cooperators
f_{C}
stands for the probability of the system reaching the full cooperation state.
A smaller
r_{C}
means that cooperation is easier to emerge and the corresponding model settings (e.g., parameters, network structures) are said to better promote the evolution of cooperation.

III
Results

III.1
Higher-order interactions and sparse components promote cooperation

We first focus on the impact of structure, such as density and group size.
Figure
2
a shows that when the time interval
\tau
of the subhypernetworks is constant, an increase in the time window
\Delta t
significantly promotes the frequency of the cooperators
f_{C}
and decreases the critical values
r_{C}
.
For a fixed
\Delta t
, as shown in Fig.
2
b, cooperation is facilitated when a subhypernetwork contains interactions within a moderate interval (i.e., moderate
\tau
).
Intuitively, the window
\Delta t
and interval
\tau
shape the structual characteristics of temporal hypernetwork series.
Enlarging the window
\Delta t
will allow more interactions to be encapsulated in the snapshots, making the sizes of hyperlinks larger and the degree (i.e., the number of hyperlinks each individual belongs to) higher.
Large
\tau
can increase the degree and size of components on subhypernetworks, which will affect the aggregation of cooperators.

To intuitively explain the impact of temporal hypernetworks on the evolution of cooperation, we start by a degree-homogeneous uniform temporal hypernetwork, in which each individual has the same degree and all hyperlinks have the same size
n
in every subhypernetwork.
The frequency of defectors
x_{D}
satisfies the replicator equation,

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{D}=

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{i}\left(1-x_{D}\right){}^{i}x_{D}^{n-i}\frac{i(n-i)}{n(n-1)}F(\pi_{D}-\pi_{C})

\displaystyle+\delta\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{i}\zeta^{i}(1-\zeta)^{n-i}\frac{i(n-i)}{n(n-1)}F(\pi_{D}-\pi_{C}),

(1)

, and

F(\pi_{D}-\pi_{C})={1}/\{1+\exp[-s(\pi_{D}-\pi_{C})]\}-{1}/\{1+\exp[-s(\pi_{C}-\pi_{D})]\},

capturing the net growth rate of cooperators.
Parameter
\delta
is the similarity between consecutive subhypernetworks.
Namely, in this temporal hypernetwork, when transitioning to the next subhypernetwork, the hyperlinks of the previous hypernetwork with a probability of
\delta
remain unchanged, while the edges with a probability of
(1-\delta)
are randomly reshuffled.

\zeta
is the probability that an individual is a defector in the unchanged hyperlinks (see Methos and Supplementary information for details).
Under such circumstances, the critical value
r_{C}
for cooperators to emerge can be theoretically obtained by solving
\dot{x}_{D}=0
, we have

r_{C}=\frac{1}{1+(n-1)(1-2q)\delta},

(2)

where
q<1/2
is a parameter related to the probability that neighbours are using different strategies during the evolution in the unchanged hyperlinks (see Methos and Supplementary information for details).
As shown in Eq. (
2
), we demonstrate that a larger group (hyperlink) size
n
reduces the critical value
r_{C}
, and thus promotes cooperation.

Previous studies indicate that a sparser network leads to a more pronounced aggregation phenomenon, representing a smaller
q
in connected static networks
Ohtsuki and Nowak 2006
;
Tarnita et al. 2009
;
Gómez-Gardenes et al. 2007a
;
Gómez-Gardenes et al. 2007b
.
However, in a temporal hypernetwork, the subhypernetworks may not be connected, making the dynamics solely occur in each component.
To better represent the connectivity between nodes, we use the concept of network density.
First, we map the hypernetwork to an unweighted network (i.e., projected network).
The traditional definition of network density is the ratio between the number of existing links and the number of possible links (i.e.
N(N-1)/2
).
As we stated before, this metric is no longer applicable to non-connected temporal networks.
Therefore, we introduce the concept of connected density of networks to better capture the internal structure.
For
i
th component with
N_{i}
nodes and
L_{i}
links, the density of the component is

\rho_{i}=\frac{2L_{i}}{N_{i}(N_{i}-1)},

(3)

which spans from 0 to 1.
Note that we ignore the isolated nodes, as neither interaction nor dynamics occurs.
Then, the connected density of this subnetwork is defined as the corresponding average over each component, namely,

\rho=\frac{\sum_{i}N_{i}\rho_{i}}{\sum_{i}N_{i}}.

(4)

For the temporal hypernetwork or traditional temporal network, the average density is
\langle\rho\rangle
defined over each subhypernetwork.

The purpose of the above definition is to focus only on the connectivity within components, which is the extent to which the dynamics can ripple.
And networks that are extremely sparse under the traditional definition can still be dense from the perspective of their connected density (Fig.
2
c).
For example, if a network is composed of just pairwise components, the traditional density will be low (sparse), but the connected density is equal to 1 (dense).
Intuitively, in such networks, the payoffs of cooperators are consistently lower than those of defectors, making the evolution of cooperation seemingly impossible.

Based on our framework, we next explain our findings by the connected density and hyperlink size induced by
\tau
and
\Delta t
.
As the time interval
\tau
of the subhypernetwork gets longer, the average density of the temporal hypernetwork decreases rapidly and then gradually increases.
The connected density reaches the minimum at a moderate
\tau
, and the results are displayed in Fig.
2
d.
That is, for a moderate
\tau
, the hypernetwork is sparser, which can help cooperators to aggregate together.
Such a phenomenon is actually a percolation-like process.
If
\tau
is extremely small, an increase in
\tau
significantly increases the size of the components compared to the degree of connectivity between nodes within the components.
However, once the entire network is close to fully connected, increasing
\tau
will only enlarge the number of links, thus making the connected density larger.
Meanwhile, we find that increasing
\Delta t
leads to an increase in hyperlink size (Fig.
2
e).
And Eq. (
2
) shows that increasing the size of hyperlinks will reduce the critical value
r_{C}
, which is consistent with previous study
Alvarez-Rodriguez et al. 2021
.

To verify the generality of our conclusions, we further construct synthetic temporal hypernetworks (see Methods).
We perform additional simulations on synthetic temporal hypernetworks with
p=0.05
(Disconnected) and
0.6
(Connected).
The temporal hypernetwork with
p=0.05
is disconnected for almost all individuals and has similar properties and performance to the empirical hypernetwork.
The cooperation is promoted at a moderate
\tau
(i.e., sparser hypernetworks), as the connected density decreases rapidly and then turns to rise (Figs.
2
f, g).
The temporal hypernetwork of
p=0.6
verifies our conclusions from another side, in which snapshots are all fully connected.
As
\tau
goes up, the subhypernetworks become denser (Fig.
2
j) and inhibit cooperation (Fig.
2
i).
However, the size of hyperlinks is larger for
p=0.6
(Figs.
2
f, j), making it easier for cooperation to emerge on temporal hypernetworks (Figs.
2
i, k).
This validates that the change in connected density induced by the temporal characteristics of hypernetworks is a crucial factor influencing the evolution of cooperation.

III.2
Temporal hypernetworks v.s. static hypernetworks

Having reported that the higher-order interactions and low connected density can promote cooperation, we next study what impact will temporality have on the evolution of cooperation.
Indeed, compared to static hypernetwork, temporal hypernetwork better models empirical interactions by involving the interaction time
Li et al. 2020
.
The existence of such temporal scale implies that the speed of interactions and the rate of structural changes can be different.

We find that enlarging the interaction time facilitates cooperation, even transcending static counterparts.
According to the above results, for proper time interval
\tau
the connected density is lower than the static hypernetwork, which provides an opportunity for the temporal hypernetwork to promote cooperation.
The round of interactions
g
over each subhypernetwork stands for the similarity
\delta
of the temporal hypernetworks.
If
g
gets larger, the similarity is higher for not changing subhypernetworks for a long time steps.
Thus, from Eq. (
2
), we know that increasing
g
will enhance cooperation.
Numerical results confirm this analysis (Fig.
3
a),
and we find that, for a series of temporal hypernetworks generated by proper
\tau
(corresponding to sparser subhypernetworks), if
g
is large, temporal hypernetworks facilitate cooperation more than their static counterparts.
As Fig.
3
a shows, for
\Delta t=0.1
h,
\tau=1
h, such a transition will occur between
g=100
and
g=5000
.

We further show that the magnitude of the promotion decreases with the increase of the round of interactions
g
(Fig.
3
b).
As
g
increases, cooperation is greatly facilitated, and the critical value
r_{C}
drops.
But when
g
continues to increase, the decrease in the critical values slows down.
This can be explained by the fact that as
g
goes up, the rise of similarity
\delta
of the temporal hypernetworks gradually slows down (proportional to
1/g
).
The same situation is observed in the synthetic temporal hypernetworks (Fig. 3c, d).
When
g=100
, all temporal hypernetworks outperform their static counterparts.
But by further increasing
g
from
100
to
1000
, the promotion is negligible.

The round of interactions
g
stands for the time scale between evolutionary procees and structure changing.
Intuitively, once the structure is defined, a fast evolutionary process, or, in other words (i.e. large
g
), having a long and fixed period of interaction helps the spread of cooperation.
Such a phenomenon is directly linked to the promotion of aggregations.
When the interaction time
g
is longer, it allows for the aggregation of cooperators to be fully established.
On the other hand, rapid changes in subhypernetworks (i.e. small
g
) can disrupt newly formed aggregations, thus discouraging cooperation.
However, our findings also indicate that
g
does not need to be particularly large.
A moderate
g
can already allow for the advantageous aggregation of cooperators to be fully realized.

III.3
Advantage of higher-order networks

To delve deeper into the impact of higher-order interactions on the evolution of cooperation, we contrast the effects of higher-order interactions with traditional pairwise interactions.
In classical pairwise interactions, a link connects two individuals, neglecting higher-order interactions among multiple individuals.
Specifically, we compare higher-order interactions on hypernetworks with classical pairwise interactions on traditional networks. Following previous framework
Santos and Pacheco 2005
;
Santos et al. 2006
;
Li et al. 2020
, we construct the traditional temporal networks under the same window
\Delta t=0.1
h and time interval
\tau=1
h through empirical data sets, where the cliques are no longer considered as hyperlinks
Li et al. 2020
.
The static network is undirected and unweighted, in which individuals are linked once they interact with each other.
The game is the same as that on hypernetworks, but the participants in each game are two individuals on each link.

Our findings indicate that representing group interactions as a set of pairwise interactions may lead to an underestimation of the potential for local interactions to promote cooperation.
We find that hypernetworks may significantly facilitate cooperation compared to traditional networks based on pairwise interactions for different settings of
g
(Fig. 4).
Cooperation emerges with a smaller synergy factor
r_{C}
in both temporal and static hypernetworks.
Based on Eq. (
2
), pairwise links on traditional networks are smaller in size while at the same time, each individual has a larger number of neighbors (namely, a larger degree) compared with hypernetworks.
Both factors may inhibit the evolution of cooperation as we have analyzed.
Through a comparison of four kinds of networks, we confirm the facilitating role of higher-order interactions in the evolution of cooperation.

III.4
Evolution of cooperation with macro-interactions

Our previous findings indicate that a large hyperlink size tends to promote cooperation.
Then, an intriguing question arises: should we continuously increase the scale of group interactions to further promote cooperation?
If so, the evolution of cooperation is maximally facilitated when all individuals are within a single hyperlink.
However, we notice that the previous scenarios encompass an assumption, due to the limitations of human interaction datasets, that the population size is significantly larger than the hyperlink size, i.e.,
N\gg n
(Fig.
2
e, h, k).
Hence, we next consider macro-interactions, where the hyperlink size is relatively large compared to the population size.
This scenario is common in empirical observations.
For example, members with diverse research directions, interests or grades within the same research group, gather together for collective discussions.
In such cases, the size of higher-order interactions relative to the population size may no longer be negligible.

For further investigations, we design a synthetic hypernetwork.
From a pool of
N
individuals, we randomly select
n
individuals to form a hyperlink and repeat this process for a total of
m
times to create one subhypernetwork.
We then follow the same procedure to construct a total of
\mathcal{G}
subhypernetworks.
This temporal hypernetwork maintains a constant number of hyperlinks with different hyperlink sizes.
We conduct simulations on above temporal hypernetworks constructed under different values of
n
(Fig.
5
a).
The results show that as the size of hyperlinks increases, cooperation is initially enhanced and subsequently suppressed.
While a larger hyperlink size can indeed reduce the critical
r_{C}
for cooperation (Fig.
5
b), in this scenario, the timing of cooperation emergence is not the sole metric.
The slope, i.e., the rate at which the fraction of cooperation
f_{C}
increases with the increase in the synergy factor
r
, is equally important.
As we observe a long gap as the entire population transitioned from near-full to full cooperation,
we introduce another critical point
r_{99}
, where
f_{C}
is beyond 0.99.
We find that increasing
n
only slightly reduces
r_{C}
(Fig.
5
b), but significantly increases
r_{99}
(Fig.
5
d), and thus decreace the slopes (Fig.
5
c), making it more challenging for the population to achieve a state of full cooperation.
Therefore, a moderately small hyperlink size
n
is most effective in promoting cooperation.

Based on our framework, this is attributed to the fact that when
N\gg n
, the density of the hypernetwork, hyperdegree and the size of hyperlinks are independent.
However, when dealing with macro-interactions, an increase in the size of hyperlinks inevitably leads to an increase in density, as it implies that all
n
individuals connect with each other.
Simultaneously, since each individual may participate in more hyperlinks, the average hyperdegree also increases.
All these reasons can impede the evolution of cooperation.

Furthermore, we validate these conclusions on empirical temporal hypernetworks.
We utilized an intergovernmental organizations (IGO) dataset
Pevehouse et al. 2020
, comprising international organizations involving 193 countries and regions.
We transform this dataset into a static hypernetwork (countries and regions for nodes, organizations for hyperlinks, shown in Fig.
6
a) and, following the same methodology used for constructing the synthetic temporal hypernetwork, converted it into a temporal hypernetwork (See Methods for details).
Specifically, we categorize hyperlinks into two types: small hyperlinks (
n<40
) and large ones (
n\geq 40
).
In each subhypernetwork, the activation probability for large hyperlinks is denoted by
p_{\text{L}}
, while for small hyperlinks, it is represented by
p_{\text{S}}
.
This can be viewed as the probability of interaction (i.e., organisations convening meetings) between hyperlinks of different sizes.

We perform simulations on temporal hypernetworks with different
p_{\text{L}}
and
p_{\text{S}}
.
If the probability for small hyperlinks to be activated is settled, the more active large hyperlinks significantly inhibit cooperation (Fig.
6
b).
This is because the presence of large hyperlinks notably increases the hypernetworks’ connected densities, making it easier for free-riders to take advantages.
Conversely, if the activation probability of large hyperlinks remains constant, a higher number of active small hyperlinks will enhance cooperation (Fig.
6
c).
It is worth noting that when small hyperlinks are not activated, cooperation remains at a very low level.
In this scenario, small hyperlinks reinforce the connections between certain nodes, representing an increase in the weight of connections between these points.
The influence of weights is overlooked in the definition of connected density due to its complexity.
High-weighted connections can facilitate reciprocity among nodes and, again, foster the aggregation of cooperators, which aligns with previous results
Allen et al. 2017
.

IV
Discussion

Group (higher-order) interactions are more complicated than pairwise ones
Friedman et al. 2017
.
In microbial populations, the outcomes of 3-species cannot be fully predicted by pairwise outcomes
Gokhale and Traulsen 2010
.
Our research indicates that the effects of high-order interactions exhibit complexity and nonlinearity.
Note that the proposed framework of evolutionary dynamics on temporal hypernetworks not only applies to public goods games but also to multi-strategy
Tarnita et al. 2011
;
Gokhale and Traulsen 2014
;
Sinervo and Lively 1996
, multi-player games with nonlinear and general payoff functions
Li et al. 2014
;
Zhou et al. 2018a
;
Li and Wang 2015
;
Zhou et al. 2015
;
Zhou et al. 2018b
;
Li et al. 2016
, which are important research topics in the future.

For human empirical interactions, the temporality of the temporal higher-order interactions is encoded in three time scales, namely,
\Delta t
,
\tau
, and
g
.
In this context,
\Delta t
represents the temporal scale of higher-order interactions, indicating how long pairwise interactions within a given period can be considered as higher-order interactions.
Considering the characteristics of human interactions and the empirical data environment in use, this time scale is normally short.
An increase of
\Delta t
can enlarge the size of higher-order interactions and thus promote cooperation.
The parameter
\tau
denotes the time scale of subhypernetworks, signifying how long interactions within a timeframe can be regarded as occurring simultaneously.
A higher ratio of
\tau
to
\Delta t
implies a large time span for the subhypernetwork, altering the density of it.
An intermediate
\tau
signifies a moderate level of simultaneous interactions, which can facilitate cooperation.
The time scale parameter
g
represents the ratio between interaction frequency and subhypernetwork switching frequency.
A larger
g
can be interpreted as a slower structural change (or more frequent interactions), while a smaller value implies rapid structural changes.
Our findings indicate that a slow structural change can promote the formation of cooperator aggregation, thereby fostering cooperation.

Our results indicate that, in fact, a moderate hyperlink size can promote cooperation.
We confirm such an effect on static uniform ring hypernetwork (Section 6 in SI), which ensures that every node has a hyperdegree of 2.
For real-world human interactions, it may often hold that
N\gg n
as the space and time are limited, so increasing the interaction size encourages cooperation.
However, if macro-interactions exist, larger sizes can strongly impede cooperation, as this allows a defector to exploit more cooperators.
With the development of the internet, human online interactions can indeed involve vast hyperlinks
Ellison et al. 2007
;
Shirky 2008
.
Our findings may also generate a possible explanation for why human interactions are often more amicable offline, while online interactions can more easily manifest hostile behaviours
Shachaf and Hara 2010
;
Tufekci 2017
.
Another example is international cooperation.
Our results indicate that smaller multilateral organizations may be more effective in promoting cooperation compared to larger ones.
Nevertheless, in certain games, such as climate change and preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons, all countries are compelled to participate.
Our findings suggest that if there exists a macro-interaction (organization), establishing more and smaller organizations can promote cooperation because the national interests within them are more closely aligned.
A direction worth exploring is asymmetric interactions, which are particularly pronounced in contexts like international organizations, where the costs individuals can incur
Hauser et al. 2019
and the distribution of benefits may vary.
This may have profound implications for the evolution of cooperation.

Previous studies have demonstrated that both temporal nature and high-order interactions can promote cooperation
Li et al. 2020
;
Alvarez-Rodriguez et al. 2021
. However, the synergistic effect between the two, as depicted in Fig. 4, is not linear.
In the scenario presented in Fig. 4a, both high-order interactions and temporal nature enhance cooperation compared to static pairwise interactions.
However, introducing temporal dynamics into high-order interactions inhibits cooperation, with the temporal hypernetwork promoting cooperation only when
g
is sufficiently large.
Our work reveals the interplay between high-order interactions and temporal nature and their impact on the evolution of cooperation.

V
Methods

V.1
Degree-homogeneous uniform temporal hypernetworks

To provide an intuitive theoretical framework, we assume a degree-homogeneous uniform temporal hypernetwork.
In this kind of temporal hypernetwork, each node has the same degree, and the size of each hyperedge is also uniform.
Let
\delta
represent the average similarity between consecutive subhypernetworks.
In this sequential process, a fraction
(1-\delta)
of the hyperlinks in the last subhypernetwork undergoes random reorganization to create a new subhypernetwork. Within the newly generated hyperlinks, the probability of an individual’s neighbour being a cooperator is denoted as
1-x_{D}
, while the probability of them being a defector is represented as
x_{D}
. The remaining hyperlinks remain unchanged, facilitating the aggregation of individuals with the same strategies, and increasing the likelihood of individuals and their neighbours sharing the same strategy.

V.2
Payoffs on degree-homogeneous uniform temporal hypernetworks

We introduce
q_{Y|X}
as the average probability of a given individual of type
X
being linked with an individual of type
Y
during the evolution process.
It follows that
q_{D|C}<1/2<q_{C|C}
and
q_{C|D}<1/2<q_{D|D}

Gómez-Gardenes et al. 2007a
;
Gómez-Gardenes et al. 2007b
;
Tarnita et al. 2009
.
For simplification purposes, we assume that
q_{C|D}=q_{D|C}=q<1/2
. Consequently, in such temporal hypernetworks, the probability of a defector having a cooperative neighbour in the unchanged hyperlinks is
q
, and vice versa.

By examining all conceivable strategy configurations among the remaining
n-1
nodes within a hyperlink, we compute the payoffs for both defectors and cooperators. In the reorganized hyperlinks, we determine the expected payoffs for a defector and a cooperator:

\displaystyle\pi^{1}_{D}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{i}\left(1-x_{D}\right){}^{i}x_{D}^{n-i-1}(ir),

(5a)

\displaystyle\pi^{1}_{C}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{i}\left(1-x_{D}\right){}^{i}x_{D}^{n-i-1}[(i+1)r-1].

(5b)

In the unchanged hyperlinks, the expected payoffs are

\displaystyle\pi^{2}_{D}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{i}q^{i}(1-q)^{n-i-1}(ir)

(6a)

\displaystyle\pi^{2}_{C}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{i}(1-q)^{i}q^{n-i-1}[(i+1)r-1].

(6b)

Summing up the payoffs from all hyperlinks, we can get the expected payoff of cooperators and defectors,

\displaystyle\pi_{C}=l(1-\delta)\pi^{1}_{C}+l\delta\pi^{2}_{C},

(7a)

\displaystyle\pi_{D}=l(1-\delta)\pi^{1}_{D}+l\delta\pi^{2}_{D},

(7b)

where
l(1-\delta)
is the expected number of reorganized hyperlinks, and
l\delta
is the expected number of unchanged hyperlinks.

V.3
Derivation of the replicator dynamics

The diffusion equation can be derived as the rate at which nodes change their strategies, a process triggered by interactions between nodes with different strategies within the same hyperlink. Within each hyperlink, the probability of encountering a C-D pair is given by
\frac{\omega_{C}\omega_{D}}{n(n-1)}
, where
\omega_{X}
represents the number of nodes of type
X
. The probability of a defector changing its strategy is expressed as
{1}/{1+\exp[-s(\pi_{C}-\pi_{D})]}
, with
s
denoting the selection intensity. Conversely, a cooperator adopts the defector’s strategy with a probability of
{1}/{1+\exp[-s(\pi_{D}-\pi_{C})]}
. We define the probability difference as

F(\pi_{D}-\pi_{C})=\frac{1}{1+\exp[-s(\pi_{D}-\pi_{C})]}-\frac{1}{1+\exp[-s(\pi_{C}-\pi_{D})]}

(8)

Considering all channels, the replicator equation can be formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{D}=

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{i}\left(1-x_{D}\right){}^{i}x_{D}^{n-i}\frac{i(n-i)}{n(n-1)}f(\pi_{D}-\pi_{C})

(9)

\displaystyle+\delta\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{i}(1-\zeta)^{i}\zeta^{n-i}\frac{i(n-i)}{n(n-1)}f(\pi_{D}-\pi_{C}),

Here,
\zeta(x_{D},q)
represents the average probability that an individual is a defector in the unchanged hyperlinks and is a function of
x_{D}
and
q
. This probability should intuitively satisfy the conditions
x_{D}=0,\zeta=0
, and
x_{D}=1,\zeta=1
.

V.4
Constructing the hypernetworks based on the empirical datasets

For human interactions, we refer to 3 datasets
Fournet and Barrat 2014
;
Mastrandrea et al. 2015
;
Génois et al. 2015
, the details are listed in SI.
Each dataset represents active contacts among a fixed population of
N
individuals in 20-second intervals.
These interactions are grouped into snapshots of a time window
\Delta t
, which is a multiple of 20 seconds, resulting in a sequence of
\mathcal{G}
snapshots.
Snapshots without interactions are excluded.
Hyperlinks are created based on these snapshots, where each clique represents a hyperlink representing interactions within
\Delta t
.
These snapshots are further aggregated, with
\tau/\Delta t
snapshots forming one subhypernetwork.
The total number of subhypernetworks is
\lceil\mathcal{G}/(\tau/\Delta t)\rceil
, and it’s worth noting that if two snapshots within
\tau
share the same group of individuals in their hyperlinks, they are considered as two separate hyperlinks with a weight of 2.
Thus, they interact two times, and the probability of them choosing this hyperlink doubles when they update strategies.
This structure models the temporal hypernetwork as a sequence of subhypernetworks, which shift one by one.

For datasets pertaining to intergovernmental organizations (IGOs), the original dataset comprises a compilation of members associated with various IGOs
Pevehouse et al. 2020
.
In our analysis, we have specifically chosen to focus on a subset of this data, encompassing 193 distinct countries and regions, as well as 309 different IGOs.
We have depicted the distribution of sizes of IGOs in Supplementary Fig. S11.
We treat each country as a distinct node, while each IGO is considered as a hyperlink connecting these nodes.
In this context, if a country is a member of a particular IGO, we establish that the corresponding node is part of the hyperlink.
In order to model the temporal dimension of our analysis, we denote the number of subhypernetworks as
\mathcal{G}
, which we set to 12 to emulate the 12 months in a year.
Within each subhypernetwork corresponding to a month, a hyperlink is subject to activation with a certain probability.
We categorize the hyperlinks into two distinct types: ”large” hyperlinks, defined as those with a membership size (
n
) greater than or equal to 40, and ”small” hyperlinks for
n<40
.
Each category of hyperlinks is associated with its unique activation probability, denoted as
p_{\text{L}}
for large hyperlinks and
p_{\text{S}}
for small hyperlinks.

V.5
Constructing the synthetic hypernetworks

In order to construct networks exhibiting some typical characteristics of empirical networks, such as high clustering coefficients
Watts and Strogatz 1998
and scale-free distributions
Barabási and Albert 1999
, we apply the Holme-Kim model
Holme and Kim 2002
, which generates a static network.
To construct each snapshot for the synthetic temporal hypernetwork, the links of the static network are activated with a probability
p
,
and all the resulting cliques are represented by hyperlinks.
This process is repeated
\mathcal{G}
times to construct each snapshot.
After that, a series of synthetic subhypernetwoks are constructed and form the synthetic temporal hypernetwork via the above procedure introduced for the empirical temporal hypernetworks.
