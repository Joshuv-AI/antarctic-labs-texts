Abstract
. Let X 1 = ( W 1 , Y 1 ) , … , X n = ( W n , Y n ) X_{1}=(W_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,X_{n}=(W_{n},Y_{n}) be n n pairs of independent random variables. We assume that, for each i ∈ { 1 , … , n } i\in\{1,\ldots,n\} , the conditional distribution of Y i Y_{i} given W i W_{i} belongs to a one-parameter exponential family with parameter 𝜸 ⋆ ​ ( W i ) ∈ ℝ {\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}(W_{i})\in{\mathbb{R}} , or at least, is close enough to a distribution of this form. The objective of the present paper is to estimate these conditional distributions on the basis of the observation 𝑿 = ( X 1 , … , X n ) {\bm{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}) and to do so, we propose a model selection procedure together with a non-asymptotic risk bound for the resulted estimator with respect to a Hellinger-type distance. When 𝜸 ⋆ {\bm{\gamma}}^{\star} does exist, the procedure allows to obtain an estimator 𝜸 ^ \widehat{\bm{\gamma}} of 𝜸 ⋆ {\bm{\gamma}}^{\star} adapted to a wide range of the anisotropic Besov spaces. When 𝜸 ⋆ {\bm{\gamma}}^{\star} has a general additive or multiple index structure, we construct suitable models and show the resulted estimators by our procedure based on such models can circumvent the curse of dimensionality. Moreover, we consider model selection problems for ReLU neural networks and provide an example where estimation based on neural networks enjoys a much faster converge rate than the classical models. Finally, we apply this procedure to solve variable selection problem in exponential families. The proofs in the paper rely on bounding the VC dimensions of several collections of functions, which can be of independent interest.

1.
Introduction

We observe
n
pairs of independent random variables
X_{i}=(W_{i},Y_{i})
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with values in a measurable product space
({\mathscr{W}}\times{\mathscr{Y}},{\mathcal{W}}\otimes{\mathcal{Y}})
and assume (even if this may not be true) that there exists an unknown function
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
on
{\mathscr{W}}
such that for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the conditional distribution of
Y_{i}
given
W_{i}
belongs to a one parameter exponential family with parameter
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}(W_{i})\in{\mathbb{R}}
. From the observation
{\bm{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, our aim is to estimate the conditional distributions of
Y_{i}
given
W_{i}
. Our approach is based on an estimation of the potential
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
(may not exist). When such a
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
does exist, the above statistical setting includes as particular cases those of binary, Gaussian and Poisson regressions as well as exponential multiplicative regression.

We are not aware of many results in the literature that tackle these regression problems and establish a risk bound for the proposed estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
of
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
. When the exponential family is given under its canonical form and
{\mathscr{W}}=\left[{0,1}\right]
,
Kolaczyk and Nowak, 2005
proposed a piecewise polynomial estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
of
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
under the assumption that the natural parameter is a smooth function of the mean. They showed that their estimator achieves the usual rate
n^{-2\alpha/(1+2\alpha)}
over the Besov spaces with regularity
\alpha>0
up to a logarithmic factor with respect to the squared Hellinger loss. When the family is a univariate natural exponential family where the variances of the distributions are quadratic functions of the means,
Antoniadis et al., 2001
proposed a wavelet shrinkage method to estimate
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
while
Brown et al., 2010
parametrized the exponential family by its mean and transformed the problem of estimating
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
into homoscedastic Gaussian regression estimation by stabilizing the variance. Under the assumption that
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
belongs to the Besov spaces and is bounded from both below and above,
Brown et al., 2010
showed their estimator achieves the converge rate
n^{-2\alpha/(1+2\alpha)}
with respect to the squared
{\mathbb{L}}_{2}
-loss. All of the literatures mentioned above make strong assumptions on the distributions of the covariates
W_{i}
where they require them to be known (for example deterministic) or partly known. Moreover, none of them consider the problem of estimation under a possible misspecification framework.

Recently,
Baraud and Chen, 2020
proposed a robust procedure based on the
\rho
-estimation to estimate
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
. Their approach is restricted to the case of a single model. Up to a numerical constant, the risk of their estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
on the model is bounded by the sum of an approximation term and a complexity term. Such an estimation procedure is satisfactory if we know in advance a suitable model for
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
, i.e. a model which is not too complex and provides a good enough approximation of
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
. However, such a model may not be easy to design without any prior information and a safer approach is to consider a family of candidate models instead and let the data decide which is the most appropriate one for estimating
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
.

1.1.
Our contributions

In this paper, we consider estimating the conditional distributions
R_{i}^{\star}(W_{i})
of
Y_{i}
given
W_{i}
by model selection. Our main contributions are as follows.

(i)

We propose a model selection procedure to estimate the conditional distributions and establish a non-asymptotic risk bound for the resulted estimator. Our approach is based on the presumption that there exists an unknown
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
on
{\mathscr{W}}
belonging to some of our models such that
R_{i}^{\star}(W_{i})
is of the form
R_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}(W_{i})}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. However, our approach is not restricted to this assumption. This is to say, we allow our statistical models to be slightly misspecified:
R_{i}^{\star}(W_{i})
may not be exactly of the form
R_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}(W_{i})}
and even if they were,
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
may not belong to any of our models. What we really assume is the form
R_{{\bm{\gamma}}(W_{i})}
with some
{\bm{\gamma}}
belonging to our models provides a suitable approximation of the conditional distributions.

(ii)

When
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d., this model selection procedure solves adaptation and variable selection problems in exponential families.

(iii)

In i.i.d. case, when the dimensionality
d
of covariate
W
is large, the converge rate of estimating
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
can be extremely slow which is, as a well-known phenomenon, called the curse of dimensionality. When
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
has some particular structures or at least close to some function with such features, we consider model selection problems based on the composite piecewise polynomials and ReLU neural networks and show that the resulted estimators by our procedure based on such models can circumvent the curse of dimensionality. The structures discussed in the paper includes genaralized additive structure, multiple index structure and multiple composition structure.

(iv)

In i.i.d. case, when
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
belongs to the Takagi class we provide an example where estimation based on ReLU neural networks results in an estimator converging to
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
with parametric rate although
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
has very little smoothness. At least for such an example, neural networks outperform all the other traditional approximation methods, e.g. piecewise polynomials and wavelets.

(v)

We construct models to approximate general additive and multiple index functions and derive VC dimension bounds for them. Besides, we adapt the VC dimension result of ReLU neural networks to the sparse setting. These results can be of independent interest for readers.

The paper is organized as follows. We introduce the specific statistical framework and set notations in Section
2
. An estimation procedure based on model selection is proposed in Section
3
together with the non-asymptotic exponential deviation inequalities. We then discuss the adaptive estimation problem in exponential families when the regression function belongs to anisotropic Besov spaces as an application in Section
4
. We show that under a suitable parametrization of exponential families, our estimator is adaptive over a wide range of the anisotropic Besov spaces with the risk bound independent of choice of the exponential family. In Section
5
, we consider the applications of our procedure to two examples of the structural assumptions, general additive functions and multiple index functions, to circumvent the curse of dimensionality. Estimation by model selection based on ReLU neural networks is discussed in Section
6
and variable selection problem in generalized linear models is considered in Section
7
. Finally, all the proofs of this paper can be found in the appendix.

2.
The statistical setting

As already mentioned, we observe
n
independent pairs of random variables
X_{1}=(W_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,X_{n}=(W_{n},Y_{n})
with values in a measurable product space
({\mathscr{X}},{\mathcal{X}})=({\mathscr{W}}\times{\mathscr{Y}},{\mathcal{W}}\otimes{\mathcal{Y}})
. We assume that for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the conditional distribution of
Y_{i}
given
W_{i}
is given by
R_{i}^{\star}(W_{i})
, where
R_{i}^{\star}
is a measurable function from
({\mathscr{W}},{\mathcal{W}})
to the set of all probabilities on
({\mathscr{Y}},{\mathcal{Y}})
equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathscr{T}}
associated to the Hellinger distance. We denote
{\mathbf{R}}^{\star}
by
n
-tuple
(R_{1}^{\star},\ldots,R_{n}^{\star})
.

Let
I
be a non-trivial interval of
{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e. the interior of
I
is not empty and
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}=\{R_{\gamma}=\overline{r}_{\gamma}\cdot\mu,\;\gamma\in I\}
be an exponential family under its general form on the measured space
({\mathscr{Y}},{\mathcal{Y}},\mu)
. More precisely,
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}
is a family of probabilities on
({\mathscr{Y}},{\mathcal{Y}})
admitting densities
\overline{r}_{\gamma}
with respect to
\mu
of the form, for all
y\in{\mathscr{Y}}
and
\gamma\in I

(1)

\overline{r}_{\gamma}(y)=e^{u(\gamma)T(y)-B(\gamma)}a(y)\text{\ where\ }B(\gamma)=\log\left[{\int_{{\mathscr{Y}}}e^{u(\gamma)T(y)}a(y)d\mu(y)}\right],

T
is a real-valued measurable function on
({\mathscr{Y}},{\mathcal{Y}})
which does not coincide with a constant
\nu=a\cdot\mu
-a.e.,
u
is a continuous, strictly monotone function on
I
and
a
is a nonnegative function on
{\mathscr{Y}}
. For convenience, we denote

(2)

r_{\gamma}(y)=e^{u(\gamma)T(y)-B(\gamma)},\text{\quad for all\ }y\in{\mathscr{Y}}\text{\ and\ }\gamma\in I,

and rewrite
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}=\{R_{\gamma}=r_{\gamma}\cdot\nu,\;\gamma\in I\}
.

Our estimator takes the form of a mapping
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}:{\mathbf{w}}=({\bm{w}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{w}}_{n})\in{\mathscr{W}}^{n}\mapsto(R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{w}}_{1})},\ldots,R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{w}}_{n})})
with values in
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}^{n}
, where
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
is a (random) function from
{\mathscr{W}}
into
I
. When the mapping

{\mathbf{R}}^{\star}
is also of this form, i.e.
{\mathbf{R}}^{\star}={\mathbf{R}}_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}}
for some (deterministic) function
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}:{\mathscr{W}}\to I
,
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
provides an estimator of the so called
regression function
.

In order to evaluate the performance of our estimator
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}
of
{\mathbf{R}}^{\star}
, we introduce a loss function based on the Hellinger distance. Given
P
and
Q
two probabilities dominated by some reference measure
\mu
on a measurable space
(A,{\mathcal{A}})
, the Hellinger distance between
P=p\cdot\mu
and
Q=q\cdot\mu
is given by

(3)

h(P,Q)=\left[{\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}\left({\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{q}}\right)^{2}d\mu}\right]^{1/2}.

We remark that (
3
) does not depend on the choice of
\mu
. We denote
{\mathscr{Q}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}
as the set of all measurable mappings from
({\mathscr{W}},{\mathcal{W}})
to the space of probabilities on
({\mathscr{Y}},{\mathcal{Y}})
equipped with the topology
{\mathscr{T}}
and define
{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}={\mathscr{Q}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}^{n}
. Therefore, both
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}
and
{\mathbf{R}}^{\star}=(R_{1}^{\star},\ldots,R_{n}^{\star})
belong to
{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}
. We endow the space
{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}
with the pseudo Hellinger distance
{\mathbf{h}}
defined for
{\mathbf{R}}=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})
and
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}=(R^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,R^{\prime}_{n})
in
{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}
by

(4)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}},{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{n}h^{2}\left({R_{i}(W_{i}),R^{\prime}_{i}(W_{i})}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathscr{W}}}h^{2}\left({R_{i}(w),R^{\prime}_{i}(w)}\right)dP_{W_{i}}(w),

where
h
is Hellinger distance defined in (
3
). We evaluate the performance of the estimator
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}
of
{\mathbf{R}}^{\star}
by the quantity
{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})
. In the favourable situation where
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
does exist, we automatically deduce a performance of
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
with respect to
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
by the distance
d({\bm{\gamma}}^{\star},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})={\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}},{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})
.

When
W_{i}
are i.i.d. with the common distribution
P_{W}
and
R_{i}^{\star}=R^{\star}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we slightly abuse the notation
h^{2}(R^{\star},R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})
to measure the distance between
R^{\star}
and
R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}
which is defined as

h^{2}(R^{\star},R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})=\frac{1}{n}{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})=\int_{{\mathscr{W}}}h^{2}(R^{\star}(w),R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}(w)})dP_{W}(w).

We end this section by introducing some notations for later use. We denote
{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
the set of all positive natural numbers,
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
the set of all non-negative real numbers and
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}
the set of all positive real numbers. For a set
m
, we use
|m|
to denote its cardinality. By
(x)_{+}
, we mean the function
\max\{0,x\}
. We denote
x\vee y
the largest value among
\left\{x,y\right\}
while
x\wedge y
is the smallest. We use the notation
\lfloor x\rfloor
for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
to denote the largest integer strictly smaller than
x
. For a
{\mathbf{R}}\in{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}
and a set
{\mathbf{A}}\subset{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{{\mathscr{W}}}
, we define
{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}},{\mathbf{A}})=\inf_{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{A}}}{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}},{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime})
. Unless otherwise specified,
\log
denotes the logarithm function with base
e
. Let
(A,{\mathcal{A}})
be a measurable space and
\mu
be a
\sigma
-finite measure on
(A,{\mathcal{A}})
. For
k\in\left[{1,+\infty}\right]
, we define
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(A,\mu)
the collection of all the measurable functions
f
on
(A,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
such that
\|f\|_{k,\mu}<+\infty
, where

\|f\|_{k,\mu}=\left(\int_{A}|f|^{k}d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{k}},\mbox{\quad for\ }k\in[1,+\infty),

\|f\|_{\infty,\mu}=\inf\{K>0,\;|f|\leq K\;\mu-\mbox{a.e.}\},\mbox{\quad for\ }k=\infty.

We denote the associated equivalent classes as
{\mathbb{L}}_{k}(A,\mu)
where any two functions coincide for
\mu
-a.e. can not be distinguished. In particular, we write the norm
\|\cdot\|_{k}
with
k\in\left[{1,+\infty}\right]
when
\mu=\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure. Throughout the paper,
C
denotes positive numerical constant which may vary from line to line.

3.
Estimation based on model selection

Our approach is based on
\rho
-estimation. For basic ideas that underline the construction of the
\rho
-estimator, we refer
Baraud and Birgé, 2018
and
Baraud et al., 2017
.

3.1.
Main assumption

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a finite or countable set. For each
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
stands for a class of measurable functions from
{\mathscr{W}}
into
I
, which we call it
a model
.
We begin with an at most countable family
\{{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m},\;m\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
of classes and assume the following.

Assumption 1
.

For any
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
is VC-subgraph on
{\mathscr{W}}
with dimension not larger than
V_{m}\geq 1
.

For completeness, we recall the definition of VC-subgraph. An (open) subgraph of a function
{\bm{\gamma}}
in
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
is the subset of
{\mathscr{W}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
given by

{\mathscr{S}}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}=\left\{(w,t)\in{\mathscr{W}}\times{\mathbb{R}},\;t<{\bm{\gamma}}(w)\right\}.

A collection
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
of measurable functions on
{\mathscr{W}}
is VC-subgraph with dimension not larger than
V_{m}
if, for any finite subset
{\mathcal{S}}\subset{\mathscr{W}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
with
|{\mathcal{S}}|=V_{m}+1
, there exists at least one subset
S
of
{\mathcal{S}}
such that for any
{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
,
S
is not the intersection of
{\mathcal{S}}
with
{\mathscr{S}}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
, i.e.

S\neq{\mathcal{S}}\cap{\mathscr{S}}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}\quad\text{whatever ${\bm{\gamma}}\in{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}$.}

In particular, when
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
is contained in a linear space with finite dimension
d_{m}\geq 1
Assumption
1
is fulfilled with
V_{m}=d_{m}+1
. Another property which can be derived from Lemma 2.6.18 of
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
is that if
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}
is VC-subgraph on a set
{\mathscr{W}}
with dimension
V
and
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
are fixed numbers, then the classes of functions
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{a}=\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}\vee a,\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}\right\}
and
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{b}=\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}\wedge b,\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}\right\}
are also VC-subgraphs on
{\mathscr{W}}
with dimension not larger than
V
. For more properties of the VC-subgraph class of functions, we refer the reader to Section 2.6.2 of
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
and Section 8 of
Baraud et al., 2017
.

3.2.
Model selection procedure

We consider
\left\{{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m},\;m\in{\mathcal{M}}\right\}
an at most countable family of models satisfying Assumption
1
. To avoid measurability issues, for any
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we take
\bm{\Gamma}_{m}
a finite or countable subset of
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
and denote
\bm{\Gamma}=\cup_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}\bm{\Gamma}_{m}
. Let
\psi
be the map defined on
[0,+\infty]
as

(5)

\psi(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{x-1}{x+1}&,&\quad\mbox{$x\in[0,+\infty)$,}\\
&1&,&\quad\mbox{$x=+\infty$.}\end{aligned}\right.

For any
{\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Gamma}
, we define the
{\mathbf{T}}
-statistic as

(6)

{\mathbf{T}}({\bm{X}},{\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi\left({\sqrt{{r_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}(W_{i})}(Y_{i})}\over{r_{{\bm{\gamma}}(W_{i})}(Y_{i})}}}\right)

with the conventions
0/0=1
and
c/0=+\infty
for all
c>0
.

Let
\Delta
be a map from
{\mathcal{M}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. For each
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we associate it with a nonnegative weight
\Delta(m)
which satisfies

(7)

\Sigma=\sum_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}e^{-\Delta(m)}<+\infty.

In particular, when
\Sigma=1
, this gives a Bayesian flavour to our procedure by regarding
\Delta(m)
as a prior distribution on the family
\{\bm{\Gamma}_{m},\;m\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
.

Let
D_{n}
be a map from
{\mathcal{M}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
defined as, for any
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
,

D_{n}(m)=10^{3}V_{m}\left[{9.11+\log_{+}\left(\frac{n}{V_{m}}\right)}\right],

where
V_{m}
stands for the VC dimension of the class
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
. We define the penalty function from
\bm{\Gamma}
to
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
as

(8)

\mathop{\rm\bf pen}\nolimits({\bm{\gamma}})=10^{2}\inf_{\left\{{m\in{\mathcal{M}}|{\bm{\gamma}}\in\bm{\Gamma}_{m}}\right\}}\left[{D_{n}(m)+4.7\Delta(m)}\right],\mbox{\quad for all\ }{\bm{\gamma}}\in\bm{\Gamma}.

For all
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\bm{\Gamma}
, we set

(9)

\bm{\upsilon}({\bm{X}},{\bm{\gamma}})=\sup_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Gamma}}\left[{{\mathbf{T}}({\bm{X}},{\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime})-\mathop{\rm\bf pen}\nolimits({\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime})}\right]+\mathop{\rm\bf pen}\nolimits({\bm{\gamma}}).

We define
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}=\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{X}})
as any measurable element of the random (and non-void) set

(10)

{\mathcal{E}}({\bm{X}})=\left\{{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\bm{\Gamma}\;\mbox{ such that }\;\bm{\upsilon}({\bm{X}},{\bm{\gamma}})\leq\inf_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Gamma}}\bm{\upsilon}({\bm{X}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime})+1}\right\}.

Finally,
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}=(R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}},\ldots,R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})
is our estimator for
{\mathbf{R}}^{\star}
.

We comment that the number 1 in (
10
) does not play any role. Any small number
\delta>0
can work for defining our estimator. We hereby choose
\delta=1
just for presenting our results in a simple way.

As one can observe from the construction procedure, our estimator depends on the choice of the exponential family in (
6
), the countable subsets
\bm{\Gamma}_{m}
of
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
and the weights
\Delta(m)
we choose. However, we do not require any information for the distributions of covariates
W_{i}
which, therefore, could be unknown. This is one of the feature distinguishing our procedure with the ones
Antoniadis et al., 2001
,
Brown et al., 2010
and
Kolaczyk and Nowak, 2005
in the literature.

3.3.
The performance of the estimator

Theorem 1
.

Let
{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{m}=\{{\mathbf{R}}_{{\bm{\gamma}}},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in\bm{\Gamma}_{m}\}
and
\Xi(m)=D_{n}(m)/4.7+\Delta(m)
, for all
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
. Under Assumption
1
, whatever the conditional probabilities
{\mathbf{R}}^{\star}=(R_{1}^{\star},\ldots,R_{n}^{\star})
of
Y_{i}
given
W_{i}
and the distributions of
W_{i}
, the estimator
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}
obtained by our model selection procedure in Section
3.2
satisfies for any
\xi>0
, with a probability at least
1-\Sigma e^{-\xi}

(11)

{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})\leq\inf_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{c_{1}{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{m})+c_{2}\left(\Xi(m)+1.49+\xi\right)}\right],

where
c_{1}=149.8
and
c_{2}=5013.2
.

The proof of Theorem
1
is postponed to Appendix A. We shall use (
11
) in the forthcoming sections to solve many model selection problems simultaneously. We need to point out that the result we present here is different with the work in Section 8 of
Baraud and Birgé, 2018
, where they assumed the pairs of random variables
X_{i}=(W_{i},Y_{i})
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
are i.i.d.. Besides, we study the regression problem in exponential families so that it is more natural to put assumption on the models
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
of the potential regression function
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
. In this statistical setting, additional work has to be done to understand the performance of the
\rho
-estimator.

We give some comments on our result here. The numerical constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are independent of the choice of the exponential family. For all
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
, let us set
\overline{{\mathscrbf{Q}}}_{m}=\{{\mathbf{R}}_{{\bm{\gamma}}},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}\}
. If for all
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{m}
is dense in
\overline{{\mathscrbf{Q}}}_{m}
with respect to the pseudo Hellinger distance
{\mathbf{h}}
, i.e.
{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},\overline{{\mathscrbf{Q}}}_{m})={\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{m})
, (
11
) is equivalent to

{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})\leq\inf_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{c_{1}{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},\overline{{\mathscrbf{Q}}}_{m})+c_{2}\left(\Xi(m)+1.49+\xi\right)}\right],

where we involve the models
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
into the risk bound of our estimator but not its countable subset
\bm{\Gamma}_{m}
as we derived in (
11
). As it was discussed in Section 4.2 of
Baraud and Birgé, 2018
, this is exact the case when
\bm{\Gamma}_{m}
is a dense subset of
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
for the topology of pointwise convergence for all
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
.

An integration of (
11
) with respect to
\xi
leads to

(12)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})}\right]\leq\inf_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{c_{1}{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{m})+c_{2}\left(\Xi(m)+\Sigma+1.49\right)}\right].

We note from (
12
) that the risk of the estimator
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}
is bounded, up to a constant depending on
\Sigma
, by the infimum over the whole family
{\mathcal{M}}
of the quantity summing up the distance from each
{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{m}
to
{\mathbf{R}}^{\star}
, the complexity of each
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}
(up to a logarithmic factor) and the associated weight
\Delta(m)
. The magnitude of the bias term and the complexity term is of the optimal order so that if for all
m\in{\mathcal{M}}
, the weight function
\Delta(m)
is chosen to be not larger than
V_{m}
(up to a logarithmic factor), we are able to select the model achieving the best trade-off between approximation and model’s complexity among the collection
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Moreover, the bias term
{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{m})
in (
12
) accounts for the robustness property of our estimator with respect to the possible model misspecification and data contamination. To illustrate it simply, let us focus on each single
\bm{\Gamma}_{m}
and assume the weight
\Delta(m)
has been assigned such that
\Delta(m)\lesssim D_{n}(m)
. If
\bm{\Gamma}_{m}
is exact, i.e.
{\mathbf{R}}^{\star}={\mathbf{R}}_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}}
with
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in\bm{\Gamma}_{m}
, up to a constant, the risk of the estimator
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}
will be smaller than
V_{m}\left[{1+\log_{+}\left(n/V_{m}\right)}\right]
. If it is not the case, the risk involves an additional bias term
{\mathbf{h}}^{2}({\mathbf{R}}^{\star},{\mathscrbf{Q}}_{m})
due to a potential model misspecification or data contamination. However, as long as this bias term remains small compared to
V_{m}\left[{1+\log_{+}\left(n/V_{m}\right)}\right]
, the performance of our estimator will not deteriorate much as the case when
\bm{\Gamma}_{m}
is exact.

In the situation where the covariates
W_{i}
are truly i.i.d. with a common distribution
P_{W}
and
R^{\star}_{i}=R^{\star}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we deduce from (
12
) that for any
R^{\star}
and
P_{W}
, our estimator
R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}
satisfies

(13)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{h^{2}(R^{\star},R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})}\right]\leq c_{2}\left(c_{3}+\Sigma\right)\inf_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}\left[{h^{2}(R^{\star},{\mathscr{Q}}_{m})+\frac{\Delta(m)}{n}+\frac{V_{m}}{n}L_{n}(m)}\right],

where
c_{3}=1939.8
,
{\mathscr{Q}}_{m}=\{R_{{\bm{\gamma}}},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in\bm{\Gamma}_{m}\}
and
L_{n}(m)=1+\log_{+}\left(n/V_{m}\right)
.

4.
Adaptation to anisotropic Besov spaces

In this section, we assume the covariates
W_{i}
are truly i.i.d. on
{\mathscr{W}}=\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
,
d\geq 1
with a common distribution
P_{W}
and
R_{i}^{\star}=R^{\star}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and consider adaptive estimation in exponential families. The problem is stated as follows.

Let
0<p,q\leq\infty
,
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{d}
and
R\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}
. We denote
B_{p,q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\left[{0,1}\right]^{d},R)
as the anisotropic Besov ball which gathers all the functions
f
in the anisotropic Besov space
B_{p,q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\left[{0,1}\right]^{d})
with (quasi-) semi-norm
|f|_{{\bm{\alpha}},p,q}<R
. Including Hölder and Sobolev spaces, Besov space is a considerable general function space. It can also capture the spatial inhomogeneity of the smoothness property as discussed by
Suzuki and Nitanda, 2021
. For readers who concern the definitions, we refer Chapter 5 of
Triebel, 2006
and
Hochmuch, 2002
which gives a detailed introduction restricted to
d=2
but can be generalized easily. Similarly to the isotropic case, the
d
-dimensional parameter
{\bm{\alpha}}
indicates the smooth property in each direction
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. More precisely, for all functions
f\in B_{p,q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\left[{0,1}\right]^{d})
, if
\alpha_{j}
is large, then
f
is smooth to the
j
-th direction.

For a given interval
\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]\subset I
with
v_{-}<v_{+}
, the notation
B_{p,q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}(R,v_{-},v_{+})
stands for the collection of functions
f\in B_{p,q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\left[{0,1}\right]^{d},R)
with
f({\bm{w}})\in\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]
for all
{\bm{w}}\in\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
. We assume that the regression function
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in B_{p,q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}(R,v_{-},v_{+})
. Our aim, in this section, is to design a specific procedure for estimating this
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
without assuming the parameters
{\bm{\alpha}}
,
p
and
R
to be known.

4.1.
Models construction

We begin with introducing the conception of hyperrectangle. Given
s_{j}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
1\leq j\leq d
, for any
k_{j}\in\Psi(s_{j})=\{0,\ldots,2^{s_{j}}-1\}
, we set

(14)

I_{j}(k_{j})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left[{0,2^{-s_{j}}}\right]&,&\quad k_{j}=0,\\
&\ (k_{j}2^{-s_{j}},(k_{j}+1)2^{-s_{j}}]&,&\quad k_{j}=1,\ldots,2^{s_{j}}-1.\end{aligned}\right.

We call a hyperrectangle by any subset of
\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
of the form
\prod_{j=1}^{d}I_{j}(k_{j})
. Given a vector
{\bm{s}}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
, we denote
M^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{\bm{s}}
the resulted partition of
\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
into the union of hyperrectangles
\cup_{(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\Psi(s_{1})\times\cdots\times\Psi(s_{d})}\prod_{j=1}^{d}I_{j}(k_{j})
.

We take
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathbb{N}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{N}}
. Given
({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we define
\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)}
as the space of piecewise polynomial functions on
\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
, where on each hyperrectangle
\prod_{j=1}^{d}I_{j}(k_{j})
,
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)}
is a polynomial in
d
variables of degree at most
r
for each variable. This is to say given
({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}
, for any
(\overline{k}_{1},\ldots,\overline{k}_{d})\in\Psi(s_{1})\times\cdots\times\Psi(s_{d})
, any
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)}
is of the form for all
{\bm{w}}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})\in\prod_{j=1}^{d}I_{j}(\overline{k}_{j})

(15)

{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{w}})=\sum_{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})\in\{0,\ldots,r\}^{d}}\gamma_{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})}\prod_{j=1}^{d}{w_{j}}^{r_{j}},

where
\gamma_{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, for all
0\leq r_{j}\leq r
,
1\leq j\leq d
.

Recall that in our setting
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
takes values in some non-trivial interval
I
which may vary from the choice of the exponential family and the choice of parametrization. A few examples are given in Section 2.1 of
Baraud and Chen, 2020
when the exponential family is parametrized in its natural form. To estimate
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
, we assume that we have a prior information of
v_{-},v_{+}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that the regression function
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
with values in
\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]\subset I
. For each
({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we define
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)}=\left\{({\bm{\gamma}}\vee v_{-})\wedge v_{+},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)}\right\}
and the family of models is given by
\left\{{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)},\;({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}\right\}
. For each
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{({\bm{s}},r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}
, we take its countable subset
{\bm{S}}_{({\bm{s}},r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}
as the collection of functions of the same form in (
15
) apart from restricting
\gamma_{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, for all
0\leq r_{j}\leq r
,
1\leq j\leq d
and define
\bm{\Gamma}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)}=\left\{({\bm{\gamma}}\vee v_{-})\wedge v_{+},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\bm{S}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)}\right\}
.

Lemma 1
.

For any
d\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\bm{s}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
,
{\bm{S}}_{({\bm{s}},r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}
is dense in
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{({\bm{s}},r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}
and
\bm{\Gamma}_{({\bm{s}},r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}
is dense in
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{({\bm{s}},r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}
with respect to the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.

For any
({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}
, since
M^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{\bm{s}}
is a partition of
\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
with
\prod_{j=1}^{d}2^{s_{j}}
hyperrectangles and on each hyperrectangle the space of functions is spanned by
(r+1)^{d}
basis,
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{({\bm{s}},r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}
is a
(r+1)^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}2^{s_{j}}
dimensional vector space. By the properties of VC-subgraph we introduced in Section
3
, for any
({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)}
is a VC-subgraph on
{\mathscr{W}}
with dimension not lager than
(r+1)^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}2^{s_{j}}+1
which fulfills Assumption
1
with

(16)

V_{({\bm{s}},r)}=(r+1)^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}2^{s_{j}}+1.

For each
({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we associate it with the weight

(17)

\Delta({\bm{s}},r)=\log(8d)\prod_{j=1}^{d}2^{s_{j}}+r.

We have the following result which shows inequality (
7
) is satisfied with the weights defined by (
17
).

Lemma 2
.

For each
({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}
, let the weight be assigned by (
17
). Then

\sum_{({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}}e^{-\Delta({\bm{s}},r)}\leq\frac{e}{e-1}.

We denote
M^{{\mathcal{B}},d}=\cup_{{\bm{s}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}}M^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{\bm{s}}
. Given a partition
\pi\in M^{{\mathcal{B}},d}
without knowing the specific values of
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})
, sometimes it is useful to introduce an alternative notation
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{(\pi,r)}=\left\{({\bm{\gamma}}\vee v_{-})\wedge v_{+},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{(\pi,r)}\right\}
for
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)}
, where
\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{(\pi,r)}
characterises the space of piecewise polynomial functions on
\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
such that on each hyperrectangle of
\pi
, any
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{(\pi,r)}
is a polynomial in
d
variables of degree not larger than
r
for each variable. Similarly, the VC dimension bound for the class of functions
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{(\pi,r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}
on
{\mathscr{W}}
is given by

(18)

V_{(\pi,r)}=(r+1)^{d}|\pi|+1,

where
|\pi|
denotes the cardinality of hyperrectangles given by the partition
\pi
of
\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
.
Under this new notation, the weight associated to each
(\pi,r)\in M^{{\mathcal{B}},d}\times{\mathbb{N}}
can be deduced from (
17
) as

(19)

\Delta(\pi,r)=\log(8d)|\pi|+r.

4.2.
Adaptivity result

Before deriving the risk bound for our estimator based on the constructed family
\left\{\bm{\Gamma}_{({\bm{s}},r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},d},\;({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}\right\}
, we first discuss the parametrization issue of the exponential family. As it has been explained in Section 4.1 and 4.2 of
Baraud and Chen, 2020
, the parametrization of the exponential family influences the converge rate of
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}
to
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
. For example, when
d=1
one can see from Section 4.1 of
Baraud and Chen, 2020
that if we parametrize exponential families by their mean, Poisson regression achieves much slower rate than the Gaussian case under the same
\alpha
-Hölder smoothness assumption on
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
with
\alpha\in(0,1]
. However, there do exist ways of parametrization such that the same rate of convergence can be achieved uniformly regardless the choice of the exponential family. We assume the following holds.

Assumption 2
.

The exponential family
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}=\{R_{\gamma},\;\gamma\in I\}
has been parametrized in the way that there exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that

h(R_{\gamma},R_{\gamma^{\prime}})\leq\kappa|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}|\text{\hskip 10.22217ptfor all\hskip 10.22217pt}\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in I.

Let us remark that, by Proposition 2 of
Baraud and Chen, 2020
, Assumption
2
is fulfilled with
\kappa=1
when the exponential family is parametrized by
\gamma=\upsilon(\theta)
, where
\theta
is the natural parameter and
\upsilon
satisfies
\upsilon^{\prime}(\theta)=\sqrt{A^{\prime\prime}(\theta)/8}
with the function
A
defined as

A(\theta)=\log\left[{\int_{{\mathscr{Y}}}e^{\theta T(y)}d\nu(y)}\right].

For any
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{d}
, we denote
\alpha_{\min}=\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\alpha_{j}
and
\overline{\alpha}
the harmonic mean of
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
, i.e.

\overline{\alpha}=\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}\right)^{-1}.

With the family of models
\left\{{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)},\;({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}\right\}
defined in Section
4.1
, the associated countable subsets
\left\{\bm{\Gamma}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)},\;({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}\right\}
and the weights defined by (
17
), we are now able to apply the model selection procedure introduced in Section
3.2
to estimate
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
. The following result shows that under Assumption
2
, the resulted estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{X}})
based on
\left\{\bm{\Gamma}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)},\;({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}\right\}
is adapted to the possible anisotropy over a wide range of the anisotropic Besov spaces with a risk bound of order
n^{-2\overline{\alpha}/(2\overline{\alpha}+d)}
up to a logarithmic factor with respect to the distance
d({\bm{\gamma}}^{\star},\widehat{\bm{\gamma}})=h^{2}\left({R_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}},R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}}\right)
. One nice feature is that this risk bound is independent of the choice of the exponential family.

Corollary 1
.

Under Assumption
2
, whatever the distribution of
W
, the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{X}})
given by the model selection
procedure in Section
3.2
over the family
\left\{\bm{\Gamma}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)},\;({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{N}}\right\}
with the weights defined by (
17
) satisfies for all
R>0
,
p>0
and
{\bm{\alpha}}\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{d}
such that
\overline{\alpha}/d>1/p
,

\sup_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in B_{p,q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(R,v_{-},v_{+}\right)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{h^{2}\left({R_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}},R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}}\right)}\right]\leq C_{\kappa,d,{\bm{\alpha}},p}\left(R^{\frac{2d}{d+2\overline{\alpha}}}n^{-\frac{2\overline{\alpha}}{d+2\overline{\alpha}}}+\frac{1}{n}\right)\left(1+\log n\right),

where
q=\infty
if
0<p\leq 1
or
p\geq 2
and
q=p
if
1<p<2
,
C_{\kappa,d,{\bm{\alpha}},p}
is a constant depending on
\kappa,d,{\bm{\alpha}},p
only.

The proof of Corollary
1
is postponed to Appendix A. We hereby give some comments on this result. First, Corollary
1
in fact holds for any
0<q\leq\infty
, if
0<p\leq 1
or
p\geq 2
and
0<q\leq p
, if
1<p<2
as a consequence of embedding the anisotropic Besov spaces to some bigger spaces. We will not discuss too much on this direction but refer the reader to Section 2.3 of
Akakpo, 2012
. Second, as it has been discussed by Section 2.1 of
Suzuki and Nitanda, 2021
, the parameter
p
plays a role of controlling the spatial inhomogeneity of the smoothness. In particular, when
p=\infty
, the smoothness is ensured uniformly. Our result, therefore, is also adapted to
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
with potentially inhomogeneous smoothness. Third, the rate is optimal up to a logarithmic factor in the minimax sense at least when
d=1
as it has been shown in Proposition 4 of
Baraud and Chen, 2020
. Finally, the condition
\overline{\alpha}/d>1/p
appearing in the result is more strict than the usual one which only requires
\overline{\alpha}/d>\left(1/p-1/2\right)_{+}
. This is because we do not make any assumption on the distribution of the covariate
W
. Therefore, we bound the approximation bias with respect to the sup-norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. As one can see from the proof of Corollary
1
, this bias bound can be reconsidered if the specific distribution of the covariate
W
is given. In the particular case when the probability measure
P_{W}
admits a density
P_{W}=p_{W}\cdot\lambda
with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
and
\|p_{W}\|_{\infty}\leq K
(i.e. the probability measure
P_{W}
is equivalent to the Lebesgue probability on
{\mathscr{W}}=\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
), we only need to require the usual condition
\overline{\alpha}/d>\left(1/p-1/2\right)_{+}
to obtain the same rate in Corollary
1
, where the numerical constant depends on
K
,
\kappa
,
d
,
{\bm{\alpha}}
and
p
.

5.
Model selection under structural assumptions

In the last section, we have seen that when the covariates
W_{i}
are truly i.i.d. on
\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
and
R_{i}^{\star}=R_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in B_{p,q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(R,v_{-},v_{+}\right)
, the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{X}})
obtained from our model selection procedure based on
\left\{\bm{\Gamma}^{{\mathcal{B}},d}_{({\bm{s}},r)},\;({\bm{s}},r)\in{\mathcal{M}}\right\}
achieves the converge rate
n^{-2\overline{\alpha}/(d+2\overline{\alpha})}
adaptively. When the value of
d
is large, this rate becomes slow, which is, as a well-known phenomenon, called the curse of dimensionality. To circumvent it, we impose structural assumptions on
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
in this section and consider additional models to implement our procedure. We mainly discuss two examples of the structural assumptions: generalized additive structure and multiple index structure.

We begin with setting some notations. Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
{\bm{w}}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})\in\left[{0,1}\right]^{k}
. For a vector
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{k}
with
\alpha_{j}=r_{j}+\alpha_{j}^{\prime}
,

and
\alpha^{\prime}_{j}\in(0,1]
for

, Hölder space
{\mathcal{H}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\left[{0,1}\right]^{k})
denotes the collection of functions
f
on
\left[{0,1}\right]^{k}

satisfying for any
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{j-1},w_{j+1},\ldots,w_{k})\in\left[{0,1}\right]^{k-1}
and all

\left|\partial_{j}^{r_{j}}f(w_{1},\ldots,x,\ldots,w_{k})-\partial_{j}^{r_{j}}f(w_{1},\ldots,y,\ldots,w_{k})\right|\leq L(f)|x-y|^{\alpha^{\prime}_{j}},

where
\partial_{j}^{r_{j}}f
denotes the
r_{j}
-th order partial derivative of the function
f
on the
j
-th component. We define the anisotropic Hölder class
{\mathcal{H}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\left[{0,1}\right]^{k},L)
as the collection of all the functions
f\in{\mathcal{H}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\left[{0,1}\right]^{k})
with

\overline{L}
such that

|f({\bm{w}})-f({\bm{w}}^{\prime})|\leq\overline{L}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|w_{j}-w^{\prime}_{j}|^{\alpha_{j}\wedge 1},\text{\ for all\ }{\bm{w}},{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\in\left[{0,1}\right]^{k}

and define
{\mathcal{H}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}(L,v_{-},v_{+})
as the collection of all functions

taking values in

with

.

Given
t_{j}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,

, for any
h_{j}\in\Phi(t_{j})=\{0,\ldots,t_{j}-1\}
, we define

(20)

I^{\prime}_{j}(h_{j})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left[{0,1/t_{j}}\right]&,&\quad h_{j}=0,\\
&\ (h_{j}/t_{j},(h_{j}+1)/t_{j}]&,&\quad h_{j}=1,\ldots,t_{j}-1.\end{aligned}\right.

For a given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
{\bm{t}}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{k}
, we denote
M_{\bm{t}}^{{\mathcal{H}},k}
the resulted partition of
\left[{0,1}\right]^{k}
into the union of
\prod_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}
hyperrectangles

\cup_{(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k})\in\Phi(t_{1})\times\cdots\times\Phi(t_{k})}\prod_{j=1}^{k}I^{\prime}_{j}(h_{j}),

where on
j
-th direction the interval
\left[{0,1}\right]
is divided into
t_{j}
regular subintervals, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
{\bm{t}}\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{k}
and

, we denote
\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{H}},k}_{({\bm{t}},r)}
the space of piecewise polynomial functions
f
on
\left[{0,1}\right]^{k}
such that the restriction of
f
to each hyperrectangle is a polynomial in
k
variables of degree not larger than
r
for each variable and
{\bm{S}}^{{\mathcal{H}},k}_{({\bm{t}},r)}
the collection of functions with the same form as the ones belonging to
\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{H}},k}_{({\bm{t}},r)}
apart from restricting the coefficients in front of the polynomial basis to be rational numbers. With a similar argument as the proof of Lemma
1
, the following result is easy to obtain.

Lemma 3
.

For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
{\bm{t}}\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{k}
and

,
{\bm{S}}_{({\bm{t}},r)}^{{\mathcal{H}},k}

.

5.1.
Generalized additive structure

Generalized additive functions, as a classical structural assumption, have been considered in many statistical literatures. Let
\alpha,L\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}
,
{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{d}
,
{\bf{p}}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{d}
and
{\bf{R}}=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{d})\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{d}
. We denote
{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(\alpha,{\bm{\beta}},{\bf{p}},L,{\bf{R}})
the collection of functions
{\bm{\gamma}}:\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}\rightarrow\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]\subset I
of the following form

{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{w}})=f\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}g_{j}(w_{j})\right),\mbox{\quad for all\ }{\bm{w}}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})\in\left[{0,1}\right]^{d},

where
f\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\alpha}(L,v_{-},v_{+})
and
g_{j}\in B^{\beta_{j}}_{p_{j},p_{j}}(\left[{0,1}\right],R_{j})
taking values in
\left[{0,1/d}\right]
, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

We assume the regression function
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(\alpha,{\bm{\beta}},{\bf{p}},L,{\bf{R}})
but without the knowledge of
\alpha
,
{\bm{\beta}}
,
{\bf{p}}
,
L
and
{\bf{R}}
. To estimate
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
by our model selection procedure, we need to first build suitable approximation models for the class of functions
{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(\alpha,{\bm{\beta}},{\bf{p}},L,{\bf{R}})
.

To approximate the Besov class of functions
B^{\beta_{j}}_{p_{j},p_{j}}(\left[{0,1}\right],R_{j})
, we consider the family
\left\{\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(s,r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},1},\;(s,r)\in{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}\right\}
introduced in Section
4.1
taking
d=1
. We recall that the functions belonging to the above family are built based on the collection of particular partitions
M^{{\mathcal{B}},1}=\cup_{s\in{\mathbb{N}}}M_{s}^{{\mathcal{B}},1}
. Therefore, we can rewrite the family in an alternative way
\left\{\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(\pi,r)}^{{\mathcal{B}},1},\;(\pi,r)\in M^{{\mathcal{B}},1}\times{\mathbb{N}}\right\}
. To approximate the Hölder class of functions
{\mathcal{H}}^{\alpha}(\left[{0,1}\right],L)
with values in
\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]
, we consider the family
\left\{{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{{\mathcal{H}},1}_{(t,r)},\;(t,r)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}\right\}
, where
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{{\mathcal{H}},1}_{(t,r)}=\left\{({\bm{\gamma}}\vee v_{-})\wedge v_{+},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{H}},1}_{(t,r)}\right\}
.

For any
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
t\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
{\bm{\pi}}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d})\in(M^{{\mathcal{B}},1})^{d}
, we define
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{A}_{({\bm{\pi}},t,r)}
the collection of all the functions
{\bm{\gamma}}
on
{\mathscr{W}}=\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
of the form

(21)

{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{w}})=f\left[{(g({\bm{w}})\vee 0)\wedge 1}\right],\mbox{\quad for all\ }{\bm{w}}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})\in\left[{0,1}\right]^{d},

where
g({\bm{w}})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}g_{j}(w_{j})
with
g_{j}\in\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},1}_{(\pi_{j},r)}
, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
f\in{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{{\mathcal{H}},1}_{(t,r)}
.
The following result reveals the upper bound of the VC dimension for the class of functions
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{A}_{({\bm{\pi}},t,r)}
.

Proposition 1
.

Given
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
t\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
{\bm{\pi}}\in(M^{{\mathcal{B}},1})^{d}
, the class of functions
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{A}_{({\bm{\pi}},t,r)}
is a VC-subgraph on
\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
with dimension

V^{A}_{({\bm{\pi}},t,r)}\leq 2+\left[{t(r+1)+2\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\pi_{j}|(r+1)}\right]\log_{2}\left[{4eU\log_{2}\left(2eU\right)}\right],

where
U=t+r+2
.

The proof is postponed to Appendix C. For each
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
t\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
{\bm{\pi}}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d})\in(M^{{\mathcal{B}},1})^{d}
, we take the countable subset
\bm{\Gamma}^{A}_{({\bm{\pi}},t,r)}
of
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{A}_{({\bm{\pi}},t,r)}
defined as

\bm{\Gamma}^{A}_{({\bm{\pi}},t,r)}=\left\{f\left[{(g\vee 0)\wedge 1}\right],\;f\in\bm{\Gamma}^{{\mathcal{H}},1}_{(t,r)},\;g_{j}\in{\bm{S}}^{{\mathcal{B}},1}_{(\pi_{j},r)},\;j=1,\ldots,d\right\},

where
{\bm{S}}^{{\mathcal{B}},1}_{(\pi_{j},r)}
is the rational version of
\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{B}},1}_{(\pi_{j},r)}
which has been introduced in Section
4.1
,
\bm{\Gamma}^{{\mathcal{H}},1}_{(t,r)}=\left\{({\bm{\gamma}}\vee v_{-})\wedge v_{+},\;{\bm{S}}^{{\mathcal{H}},1}_{(t,r)}\right\}
and
g({\bm{w}})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}g_{j}(w_{j})
, for all
{\bm{w}}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})\in\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(M^{{\mathcal{B}},1})^{d}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}
. For any
({\bm{\pi}},t,r)\in(M^{{\mathcal{B}},1})^{d}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}
, we associate it with the weight

(22)

\Delta({\bm{\pi}},t,r)=3\log 2\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\pi_{j}|\right)+r+t.

The following result shows inequality (
7
) is satisfied with the weights defined by (
22
).

Lemma 4
.

With the weights defined by (
22
), we have

\sum_{({\bm{\pi}},t,r)\in(M^{{\mathcal{B}},1})^{d}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}}e^{-\Delta({\bm{\pi}},t,r)}\leq\frac{e}{e-1}.

With Proposition
1
and Lemma
4
, we can apply the model selection procedure introduced in Section
3.2
and obtain the following.

Corollary 2
.

Under Assumption
2
, no matter what the distribution of
W
is, the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{X}})
given by the model selection procedure in Section
3.2
over
\left\{\bm{\Gamma}^{A}_{({\bm{\pi}},t,r)},\;({\bm{\pi}},t,r)\in(M^{{\mathcal{B}},1})^{d}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}\right\}
with the weights defined by (
22
) satisfies for all
\alpha,L\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}
and
{\bm{\beta}},{\bf{p}},{\bf{R}}\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{d}
such that
\beta_{j}>1/p_{j}

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(\alpha,{\bm{\beta}},{\bf{p}},L,{\bf{R}})}C^{\prime}_{\kappa,d,\alpha,{\bm{\beta}},{\bf{p}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{h^{2}(R_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}},R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})}\right]

(23)

\displaystyle\leq\left\{\left[{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(LR_{j}^{\alpha\wedge 1}\right)^{\frac{2}{2(\alpha\wedge 1)\beta_{j}+1}}n^{-\frac{2(\alpha\wedge 1)\beta_{j}}{2(\alpha\wedge 1)\beta_{j}+1}}}\right]+L^{\frac{2}{2\alpha+1}}n^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}+\frac{1}{n}\right\}{\mathcal{L}}_{n}^{2},

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{n}=\log n\vee\log L^{2}\vee 1
and
C^{\prime}_{\kappa,d,\alpha,{\bm{\beta}},{\bf{p}}}
is a constant depending on
\kappa
,
d
,
\alpha
,
{\bm{\beta}}
and
{\bf{p}}
.

Corollary
2
tells that in the ideal situation
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(\alpha,{\bm{\beta}},{\bf{p}},L,{\bf{R}})
for some
\alpha
,
{\bm{\beta}}
,
{\bf{p}}
,
L
and
{\mathbf{R}}
, the converge rate of the estimator is independent of
d
which entails the procedure does not suffer from the curse of dimensionality. When
R^{\star}\not=R_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}}
or
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
exists but does not belong to any
{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(\alpha,{\bm{\beta}},{\bf{p}},L,{\bf{R}})
, a bias term will be added into the risk bound in Corollary
2
. However, as long as the bias term is not too large compared to the quantity on the right hand side of (
23
), the accuracy of the resulted estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{X}})
remains the same magnitude as the ideal case which confirms the robustness of our estimator.

5.2.
Multiple index structure

Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
be the unit ball for the
\ell_{1}
-norm, i.e.

{\mathcal{C}}_{d}=\left\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\;\sum_{j=1}^{d}|c_{j}|\leq 1\right\}.

For some known
l\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
(typically
l\leq d
), we denote
{\mathcal{G}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}({\bm{\alpha}},L)
the collection of all the functions
{\bm{\gamma}}
of the following form

(24)

{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{w}})=f\circ g({\bm{w}}),\mbox{\quad for all\ }{\bm{w}}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})\in\left[{0,1}\right]^{d},

where
g:\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}\rightarrow\left[{0,1}\right]^{l}
defined as
g({\bm{w}})=(g_{1}({\bm{w}}),\ldots,g_{l}({\bm{w}}))
with

g_{j}({\bm{w}})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\langle a_{j},{\bm{w}}\rangle+1\right],\;a_{j}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{d}\mbox{\quad for all \ }j\in\{1,\ldots,l\}

and
f\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\bm{\alpha}}\left(L,v_{-},v_{+}\right)
mapping
\left[{0,1}\right]^{l}
to
\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]\subset I
with
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{*}_{+}
,
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{l})\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{l}
and
v_{-}<v_{+}
. We assume
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}({\bm{\alpha}},L)
but without knowing the values of
{\bm{\alpha}}
and
L
.

To approximate the Hölder classes on
\left[{0,1}\right]^{l}
with values in
\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]
, we adopt the same strategy by considering the family
\left\{{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{({\bm{t}},r)}^{{\mathcal{H}},l},\;({\bm{t}},r)\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{l}\times{\mathbb{N}}\right\}
, where
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{({\bm{t}},r)}^{{\mathcal{H}},l}=\left\{({\bm{\gamma}}\vee v_{-})\wedge v_{+},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{({\bm{t}},r)}^{{\mathcal{H}},l}\right\}
. Let
\left[{l}\right]=\{1,\ldots,l\}
. For any
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\bm{t}}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{l})\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{l}
, we define the class of functions
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{M}_{({\bm{t}},r)}
on
{\mathscr{W}}=\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
as

{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{M}_{({\bm{t}},r)}=\left\{f\left(g_{1}(\cdot),\ldots,g_{l}(\cdot)\right),\;f\in{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{{\mathcal{H}},l}_{({\bm{t}},r)},\;g_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\langle a_{j},\cdot\rangle+1\right],\;a_{j}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{d},\;j\in\left[{l}\right]\right\}.

The following result entails that
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{M}_{({\bm{t}},r)}
is VC-subgraph on
{\mathscr{W}}
.

Proposition 2
.

For any
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\bm{t}}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{l})\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{l}
, the class of functions
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{M}_{({\bm{t}},r)}
is a VC-subgraph on
{\mathscr{W}}=\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
with dimension

(25)

V^{M}_{({\bm{t}},r)}\leq 2+\left[{2ld+\left(\prod_{j=1}^{l}t_{j}\right)(r+1)^{l}}\right]\log_{2}\left[{4eU\log_{2}\left(2eU\right)}\right],

where
U=\sum_{j=1}^{l}t_{j}+lr+l+1
.

The proof is postponed to Appendix C. For any
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\bm{t}}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{l})\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{l}
, we take the countable subset
\bm{\Gamma}^{M}_{({\bm{t}},r)}
of
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}^{M}_{({\bm{t}},r)}
defined as

\bm{\Gamma}^{M}_{({\bm{t}},r)}=\left\{f\left(g_{1}(\cdot),\ldots,g_{l}(\cdot)\right),\;f\in\bm{\Gamma}^{{\mathcal{H}},l}_{({\bm{t}},r)},\;g_{j}=\frac{\left[\langle a_{j},\cdot\rangle+1\right]}{2},\;a_{j}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{d}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}^{d},\;j\in\left[{l}\right]\right\},

where
\bm{\Gamma}^{{\mathcal{H}},l}_{({\bm{t}},r)}=\left\{({\bm{\gamma}}\vee v_{-})\wedge v_{+},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\bm{S}}^{{\mathcal{H}},l}_{({\bm{t}},r)}\right\}
with
{\bm{S}}^{{\mathcal{H}},l}_{({\bm{t}},r)}
the countable subset of
\overline{{\bm{S}}}^{{\mathcal{H}},l}_{({\bm{t}},r)}
as we introduced in the beginning of this section.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{l}\times{\mathbb{N}}
. For any
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\bm{t}}\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{l}
, we associate it with the weight

(26)

\Delta({\bm{t}},r)=\sum_{j=1}^{l}t_{j}+r.

The following result shows inequality (
7
) is satisfied with the weights defined by (
26
).

Lemma 5
.

With the weights defined by (
26
), we have

\sum_{({\bm{t}},r)\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{l}\times{\mathbb{N}}}e^{-\Delta({\bm{t}},r)}\leq\frac{e}{e-1}.

The proof is postponed to Appendix B. With Proposition
2
and Lemma
5
, we are able to apply the model selection procedure introduced in Section
3.2
and obtain the following.

Corollary 3
.

Under Assumption
2
, no matter what the distribution of
W
is, the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{X}})
given by the model selection procedure in Section
3.2
over
\left\{\bm{\Gamma}^{M}_{({\bm{t}},r)},\;({\bm{t}},r)\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{l}\times{\mathbb{N}}\right\}
with the weights defined by (
26
) satisfies for all
{\bm{\alpha}}\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{l}
and
L>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}({\bm{\alpha}},L)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{h^{2}(R_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}},R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\kappa,l,{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(L^{\frac{2l}{2\overline{\alpha}+l}}n^{-\frac{2\overline{\alpha}}{2\overline{\alpha}+l}}+\frac{d}{n}\right){\mathcal{L}}_{n}^{2},

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{n}=\log n\vee\log L^{2}\vee 1
and
C_{\kappa,l,{\bm{\alpha}}}
is a constant depending only on
\kappa
,
l
and
{\bm{\alpha}}
.

The result tells that if for some
{\bm{\alpha}}\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{l}
and
L>0
,
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}({\bm{\alpha}},L)
where the value of
l
is smaller than
d
, we mitigate the curse of dimensionality by taking the information that
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
is a multiple index function. If it is not the case, a bias term will be added into the risk bound in Corollary
3
. But as long as the conditional distribution
R^{\star}
is not far away from some set of conditional distributions
\left\{R_{{\bm{\gamma}}},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}({\bm{\alpha}},L)\right\}
, the performance of our estimator will not deteriorate too much.

When the value of
l
is large (
l>d
), the multiple index model (
24
) does not help to circumvent the curse of dimensionality. In this situation, we could assume
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
has an additive structure, i.e.

{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}({\bm{w}})=\sum_{j=1}^{l}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}\left(\frac{\langle a_{j},{\bm{w}}\rangle+1}{2}\right),\mbox{\quad for all\ }{\bm{w}}\in\left[{0,1}\right]^{d},

where
a_{j}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{d}
. Imposing some smoothness on
{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}
, we can construct models and perform our model selection procedure to mitigate the curse of dimensionality. The construction is similar to a combination of what we have done in Section
5.1
and
5.2
.

6.
Model selection for neural networks

Throughout this section, we assume the covariates
W_{i}
are i.i.d. on
\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
with the common distribution
P_{W}
and
R_{i}^{\star}=R_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The idea in this section is to estimate the regression function
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
by our model selection procedure based on ReLU neural networks.

We start with setting some notations. We recall the Rectifier Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
defined as

\sigma(x)=\max(0,x).

For any vector
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
with some
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, by writing
\sigma({\bm{x}})
we mean the activation function operating component-wise, i.e.

\sigma({\bm{x}})=(\max\{0,x_{1}\},\ldots,\max\{0,x_{d}\})^{\top}.

We formulate
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(L,p)}
the Multi-Layer Perception (MLP) with width
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and depth
L\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, which is a collection of functions of the form

(27)

f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},\quad{\bm{w}}\mapsto f({\bm{w}})=M_{L}\circ\sigma\circ M_{L-1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma\circ M_{0}({\bm{w}}),

where

M_{l}({\bm{y}})=A_{l}({\bm{y}})+b_{l},\mbox{\quad for\ }l=0,\ldots,L,

A_{l}
is a
p\times p
weight matrix for
l\in\{1,\ldots,L-1\}
,
A_{0}
has size
p\times d
,
A_{L}
has size
1\times p
and the shift vectors
b_{l}
is of size
p
if
l\in\{0,\ldots,L-1\}
, a scalar if
l=L
. All the parameters in weight matrices and shift vectors vary in
{\mathbb{R}}
. We denote the MLP as
{\bm{S}}_{(L,p)}
when it has the same architecture as
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(L,p)}
but all the parameters in weight matrices and shift vectors vary in
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Besides learning all the parameters in weight matrices and shift vectors, people also enforce their algorithm on some sparse neural networks depending on the problem they want to solve. Some examples can be found in Section 7.10 of
Goodfellow et al., 2016
. Another more tuitive example for the sparse setting is the convolutional neural network (CNN) which has been widely used in computer vision, sequence analysis in bioinformatics and natural language processing.

We formulate the sparse ReLU neural networks as follows. For
l\in\{0,\ldots,L\}
, we define
{\bm{s}}_{l}
the indicator vector in which the component is either 0 or 1. The size of the vector
{\bm{s}}_{l}
equals to the total number of parameters in weight matrix
A_{l}
and shift vector
b_{l}
. For
l=0
,
{\bm{s}}_{0}
is of size
p(d+1)
, for
l\in\{1,\ldots,L-1\}
,
{\bm{s}}_{l}
is of size
p(p+1)
and for
l=L
,
{\bm{s}}_{L}
is of size
p+1
. Essentially, indicator vectors
{\bm{s}}_{l}
,
l\in\{0,\ldots,L\}
represent collections of functions based on the structure of neural networks. The last
p
components in
{\bm{s}}_{l}
,
l\in\{0,\ldots,L-1\}
and the last one in
{\bm{s}}_{L}
address to the collection of shift vectors
b_{l}
. More precisely, for any component in
b_{l}
if the corresponding position in
{\bm{s}}_{l}
is 1, we allow this component in
b_{l}
varies in
{\mathbb{R}}
otherwise the value of it is fixed at 0. The other components in
{\bm{s}}_{l}
address to the collection of weight matrices
A_{l}
with the same way as we have introduced to
b_{l}
after reshaping the matrices one row after another into vectors. To illustrate, we take
p=2
,
L=3
and
l=1
as an example. Let
{\bm{s}}_{1}=(1,0,0,1,1,0)^{\top}
which is a vector of size
6
. As mentioned before,
A_{1}
is a
2\times 2
matrix which we write as

A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{3}\\
a_{4}&a_{2}\end{pmatrix}

and
b_{1}
is of size 2. The last 2 components in
{\bm{s}}_{1}
is
(1,0)^{\top}
which entails that the first component in
b_{1}
varies in
{\mathbb{R}}
and the second is fixed at 0. We then reshape
A_{1}
one row after another into a vector, namely
(a_{1},a_{3},a_{4},a_{2})^{\top}
. The remaining components of
{\bm{s}}_{l}
is
(1,0,0,1)^{\top}
which entails
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are allowed varying in
{\mathbb{R}}
while
a_{3},a_{4}=0
. To conclude, such an indicator vector
{\bm{s}}_{1}
corresponds to the collection of weight matrices

A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&0\\
0&a_{2}\end{pmatrix},\mbox{\quad with\ }a_{1},a_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}

and shift vectors
b_{1}=(b,0)^{\top}
with
b\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Given
p,L\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and a joint indicator vector
{\bm{s}}=({\bm{s}}_{0}^{\top},\ldots,{\bm{s}}_{L}^{\top})^{\top}
, we denote
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}
as the corresponding collection of functions on
\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
. Similarly,
{\bm{S}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}
denotes the class of functions with the same architecture as
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}
, where the non-zero parameters vary in
{\mathbb{Q}}
but not
{\mathbb{R}}
. Let us remark that given
L\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
{\bm{s}}
is of size

\overline{p}=p^{2}(L-1)+p(L+d+1)+1.

The following result gives an upper bound of the VC dimension for the class of the functions
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}
on
{\mathscr{W}}=\left[{0,1}\right]^{d}
.

Proposition 3
.

For any
L\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
{\bm{s}}\in\{0,1\}^{\overline{p}}
, a fixed designed neural network
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}
is a VC-subgraph on
{\mathscr{W}}
with dimension

V_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}\leq(L+1)(\|{\bm{s}}\|_{0}+1)\log_{2}\left[{2\left(2e(L+1)\left(\frac{pL}{2}+1\right)\right)^{2}}\right],

where
\|{\bm{s}}\|_{0}
denotes the number of non-zero components in
{\bm{s}}
.

The proof is postponed to Appendix C. In particular, when all the components in
{\bm{s}}
are 1,
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}
is the Multi-Layer Perception
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(L,p)}
and Proposition
3
entails the VC dimension of
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(L,p)}
is, up to a constant, bounded by
\overline{p}L\log\left[{(L+1)\left(pL/2+1\right)}\right]
.

6.1.
The Takagi class of functions

We provide an example in this subsection where estimation based on ReLU neural networks enjoys a significant advantage.

Let
v_{-},v_{+}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
v_{-}<v_{+}
and
\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]\subset I
. For any
t\in(-1,1)
,
l\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
{\bf{p}}=(p_{1},p_{2})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
K\geq 0
, we denote
{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(t,l,{\bf{p}},K)
the collection of functions where for all
f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(t,l,{\bf{p}},K)
, it takes values in
\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]\subset I
and is of the form

(28)

f(w)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}}t^{k}g\left(h^{\circ k}(w)\right),\;\mbox{\quad for all\ }w\in\left[{0,1}\right],

where
g\in{\bm{S}}_{(l,p_{1})}
defined on
\left[{0,1}\right]
,
\|g\|_{\infty}\leq K
,
h\in{\bm{S}}_{(l,p_{2})}
maps
\left[{0,1}\right]
to
\left[{0,1}\right]
and
h^{\circ k}=h\circ\cdots\circ h
denotes the resulted function when
h
is composed with itself
k
times. We assume the regression function

{\bf{p}}

&2(1-w)&,&\quad\frac{1}{2}<w\leq 1.\end{aligned}\right.

Let

. The family of models we consider here is given by

\left\{\bm{\Gamma}_{(L,p)},\;(L,p)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\right\},

and satisfies Assumption

with

, up to a constant, bounded by
\overline{p}L\log\left[{(L+1)\left(pL/2+1\right)}\right]
. For any
(L,p)\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we associate it with the weight

(30)

\Delta(L,p)=L+p.

As an immediate consequence, we have
\Sigma=\sum_{(L,p)\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{2}}e^{-\Delta(L,p)}\leq 1
which satisfies the inequality (

3.2
and obtain the following result.

Corollary 4
.

Under Assumption
2
, whatever the distribution of
W
, the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{X}})
given by the model selection procedure in Section
3.2
over the family

with the weights defined by (

,

,

\kappa,t,l,{\bf{p}},K

The risk bound is optimal up to the logarithmic factors since any two probabilities with a Hellinger distance smaller than
{\mathcal{O}}(1/\sqrt{n})
are indistinguishable. To comment upon this result further, we consider a specific example of
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
in Gaussian regression problem with a known variance
\sigma>0

\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}=\left\{R_{\gamma},\;\gamma\in I\right\}

is the mean so that by Proposition 2 of
Baraud and Chen, 2020

2
is satisfied with

and

. We therefore can take
v_{-}
the smallest integer in computer and
v_{+}
the largest so that
\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]\subset I

{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}}2^{-k}h^{\circ k}
with

defined by (
29

is the identity function on
\left[{0,1}\right]
so that

and

in the general formalization (

and

by rewriting them into the following forms

g(x)=\sigma\left(x+0\right),\mbox{\quad for all\ }x\in\left[{0,1}\right]

and

h(w)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2&-4\\
\end{array}\right)\sigma\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\end{array}\right)w+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\

Therefore, we have

based on the fully connected ReLU neural networks converges to

is nowhere differentiable hence it has very little smoothness in the classical sense. Estimation based on the traditional models will result a miserably slow rate considering the minimax converge rate for an

6.2.
Composite Hölder class of functions

We have seen in the last subsection that the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{X}})
based on MLPs converges to the truth with an optimal rate for some class of functions. In this subsection, we continue to consider the problem of circumventing the curse of dimensionality based on deep ReLU neural networks. A natural structure of the regression function
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
for neural networks to exhibit advantages could be a composition of several functions which has been considered by
Schmidt-Hieber, 2020
for Gaussian regression. We shall reconsider it from another point of view where we perform our model selection procedure based on the result of controlling the VC dimension of sparse ReLU neural networks.

Let us introduce notations first. Given
t\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}
, we define
{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}_{t}(D,K)
an
\alpha
-Hölder ball with radius
K
as the collection of functions
f:D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{t}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{t})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{t}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{t}\beta_{j}<\alpha\end{subarray}}\|\partial^{\bm{\beta}}f\|_{\infty}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\beta}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{t}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{t}\beta_{j}=\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in D\\
{\bm{x}}\not={\bm{y}}\end{subarray}}\frac{\left|\partial^{\bm{\beta}}f({\bm{x}})-\partial^{\bm{\beta}}f({\bm{y}})\right|}{|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}|_{\infty}^{\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}}\leq K,

where for any
{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{t})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{t}
,
\partial^{\bm{\beta}}=\partial^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\partial^{\beta_{t}}
and for any
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{t}
,
|{\bm{x}}|_{\infty}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,t}|x_{i}|
.

For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
{\bf{d}}=(d_{0},\ldots,d_{k})\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{k+1}
,
{\bf{t}}=(t_{0},\ldots,t_{k})\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{k+1}
,
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\in({\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*})^{k+1}
and
K\geq 0
, we denote
{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(k,{\bf{d}},{\bf{t}},{\bm{\alpha}},K)
the class of functions with values in
\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]\subset I
as,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(k,{\bf{d}},{\bf{t}},{\bm{\alpha}},K)=

\displaystyle\left\{f_{k}\circ\cdots\circ f_{0},\;f_{i}=(f_{ij})_{j}:\left[{a_{i},b_{i}}\right]^{d_{i}}\rightarrow\left[{a_{i+1},b_{i+1}}\right]^{d_{i+1}},\right.

\displaystyle\left.f_{ij}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha_{i}}_{t_{i}}(\left[{a_{i},b_{i}}\right]^{t_{i}},K),\;\mbox{for some }|a_{i}|,|b_{i}|\leq K\right\},

where
d_{k+1}=1
. We assume the regression function
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}={\bm{\gamma}}_{k}\circ\cdots\circ{\bm{\gamma}}_{0}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(k,{\bf{d}},{\bf{t}},{\bm{\alpha}},K)
but without the knowledge of
k
,
{\bf{d}}
,
{\bf{t}}
,
{\bm{\alpha}}
and
K
.

To approximate these classes of functions
{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(k,{\bf{d}},{\bf{t}},{\bm{\alpha}},K)
, we consider the sparse ReLU neural networks. Recall that for any
(L,p)\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{2}
,
{\bm{s}}=({\bm{s}}_{0}^{\top},\ldots,{\bm{s}}_{L}^{\top})^{\top}\in\{0,1\}^{\overline{p}}
with
\overline{p}=p^{2}(L-1)+p(L+d+1)+1
indicating the sparsity design of a MLP with architecture
(L,p)
. More precisely, setting
{\mathcal{M}}=({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{2}\times\{0,1\}^{\overline{p}}
, we consider the family of models based on sparse ReLU neural networks
\left\{{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})},\;(L,p,{\bm{s}})\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{2}\times\{0,1\}^{\overline{p}}\right\}
, where
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}=\left\{({\bm{\gamma}}\vee v_{-})\wedge v_{+},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}\right\}
. The VC dimension
V_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}
of each
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}
is bounded by Proposition
3
. For each
(L,p,{\bm{s}})\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we take the countable subset
\bm{\Gamma}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}
of
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}
as
\bm{\Gamma}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}=\left\{({\bm{\gamma}}\vee v_{-})\wedge v_{+},\;{\bm{\gamma}}\in{\bm{S}}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})}\right\}
.

For each
(L,p,{\bm{s}})\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{2}\times\{0,1\}^{\overline{p}}
, we associate it with the weight

(31)

\Delta(L,p,{\bm{s}})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\|{\bm{s}}\|_{0}\log\left(\frac{2e\overline{p}}{\|{\bm{s}}\|_{0}}\right)+p+L&,&\mbox{\quad$\|\bm{s}\|_{0}\not=0$,}\\
&p+L&,&\mbox{\quad$\|\bm{s}\|_{0}=0$.}\end{aligned}\right.

The following result shows (
7

Lemma 6

For any

,

and

by (

\sum_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{2}\times\{0,1\}^{\overline{p}}}e^{-\Delta(L,p,{\bm{s}})}\leq 2.

For any

Under Assumption
2

W

\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{X}})

over the family
\left\{\bm{\Gamma}_{(L,p,{\bm{s}})},\;(L,p,{\bm{s}})\in({\mathbb{N}}^{*})^{2}\times\{0,1\}^{\overline{p}}\right\}

\sup_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\left[{v_{-},v_{+}}\right]}(k,{\bf{d}},{\bf{t}},{\bm{\alpha}},K)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{h^{2}\left({R_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}},R_{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}}}\right)}\right]\leq C_{\kappa,k,{\bf{d}},{\bf{t}},{\bm{\alpha}},K}\phi_{n}\log^{4}n,

where

By Corollary

, we provide a theoretical guarantee for an alternative estimation procedure based on sparse ReLU neural networks besides maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) discussed in
Schmidt-Hieber, 2020
for the Gaussian regression. Our procedure is, however, designed to handle the regression problems in exponential families and not only restricted to the Gaussian case. It also endows the estimator an additional robust property compared to MLE. When there is a misspecification or data contamination, as long as the bias remains small compared to the right hand side of (

7.
Variable selection in exponential families

In this section, we propose to handle variable selection problem in exponential families by model selection. The statistical setting is stated as follows. Assuming that
W_{i}
are i.i.d. on
{\mathscr{W}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
and for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we observe

where

represents the observation of the explanatory variable
W^{(j)}
in the
i
-th experiment. The value

stands for the number of the explanatory variables. This number may be large, possibly larger than
n
. The exponential family

is parametrized in its natural form, i.e. for all

,
\gamma\in I
,

r_{\gamma}(y)=e^{\gamma T(y)-B(\gamma)},

which is the particular situation when taking

as the identity function in (
2
). We assume that there exists an unknown function
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\star}
on
{\mathscr{W}}
taking values in

with
v_{-}<v_{+}

p
explanatory variables, namely

\gamma_{j}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}

given

belongs to a natural exponential family with natural parameter

together with selecting the most significant explanatory variables among the

We set

and

, we define
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{m}
as the collection of functions
{\bm{\gamma}}
on

of the form

(33)

. We define
{\bm{S}}_{m}

{\mathbb{Q}}
is dense in
{\mathbb{R}}

{\bm{S}}_{m}
is dense in
\overline{{\bm{S}}}_{m}
for the topology of pointwise convergence. One can observe that such dense property also holds for each
\bm{\Gamma}_{m}
in
{\overline{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{m}

m\in{\mathcal{M}}
.

We define
{\mathcal{M}}_{o}=\left\{m_{d}=\{1,\ldots,d\},\;1\leq d\leq p\right\}\cup\varnothing
. For each

, we associate it with the weight

(34)

\Delta(m)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &2\log(1+|m|)&,&\mbox{\quad$m\in{\mathcal{M}}_{o}$,}\\
&|m|\log\left(\frac{2ep}{|m|}\right)&,&\mbox{\quad$m\in{\mathcal{M}}\backslash{\mathcal{M}}_{o}$.}\end{aligned}\right.

The following result shows with the weights defined by (

Lemma 7
