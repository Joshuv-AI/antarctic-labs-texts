When does Metropolized Hamiltonian Monte Carlo provably outperform Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm?
Yuansi Chen, Khashayar Gatmiry

Abstract
We analyze the mixing time of Metropolized Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) with the leapfrog integrator to sample from a distribution on d whose log-density is smooth, has Lipschitz Hessian in Frobenius norm and satisfies isoperimetry. We bound the gradient complexity to reach ϵ \epsilon error in total variation distance from a warm start by O ~ ​ ( d 1 / 4 ​ polylog ​ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) \tilde{O}(d^{1/4}\text{polylog}(1/\epsilon)) and demonstrate the benefit of choosing the number of leapfrog steps to be larger than 1. To surpass previous analysis on Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) that has O ~ ​ ( d 1 / 2 ​ polylog ​ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) \tilde{O}(d^{1/2}\text{polylog}(1/\epsilon)) dimension dependency [ WSC22 ] , we reveal a key feature in our proof that the joint distribution of the location and velocity variables of the discretization of the continuous HMC dynamics stays approximately invariant. This key feature, when shown via induction over the number of leapfrog steps, enables us to obtain estimates on moments of various quantities that appear in the acceptance rate control of Metropolized HMC. Moreover, to deal with another bottleneck on the HMC proposal distribution overlap control in the literature, we provide a new approach to upper bound the Kullback-Leibler divergence between push-forwards of the Gaussian distribution through HMC dynamics initialized at two different points. Notably, our analysis does not require log-concavity or independence of the marginals, and only relies on an isoperimetric inequality. To illustrate the applicability of our result, several examples of natural functions that fall into our framework are discussed.



1
Introduction

The Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm is a popular Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm for sampling from a smooth distribution on
d
. The algorithm originates from the physics literature
[
AW59
,
DKPR87
]
and has important applications in statistical
physics, computational chemistry, etc. Since its introduction to the statistics by Neal
[
Nea94
]
, HMC has since become very popular as a generic tool for sampling smooth distributions in Bayesian statistical inference. Notably, HMC is implemented the default sampling algorithm for sampling complex smooth distribution in many state-of-the-art software packages, including Stan
[
CGH+17
]
, PyMC3
[
SWF16
]
, Mamba and Tensorflow
[
AAB+16
]
.

HMC’s popularity can be attributed to its superior empirical performance when compared to traditional MCMC sampling algorithms like Metropolized Random Walk (MRW) and Metropolis-adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA). There are several heuristic arguments that suggest why HMC might perform better. For example, based on intuition from the continuous-time limit of the Hamiltonian dynamics, it was suggested that HMC can suppress random walk behavior using momentum
[
Nea11
]
. Others highlighted HMC’s ability to maintain a
constant asymptotic accept-reject rate with large step-size
[
Cre88
]
. Although these intuitive arguments may be compelling, their quantitative understanding is limited, and they cannot be relied on to determine the optimal free parameters of HMC.

There are two fundamental quantitative questions that are crucial to understanding the fast convergence of HMC. The first question concerns the conditions under which HMC will converge faster than MALA, as HMC is expected to perform better, but the specific conditions of this advantage are not well-understood. This lack of clarity poses difficulties when one has to choose between MALA and HMC. The second question is related to the sensitivity of HMC to its free parameters, such as the step-size and number of integration steps. If too few integration steps are taken, HMC can behave similarly to MALA, whereas too many steps can cause HMC to wander back to the initial state’s neighborhood, resulting in slower mixing and wasted computation
[
BBG14
]
. Although analysis under simplified scenarios exists
[
BPR+13
]
, it remains unclear how to achieve the optimal free parameter choices under general settings.

In the paper, we investigate the mixing time of Metropolized HMC for sampling a smooth target density satisfying an isoperimetric inequality in
d
. With the help of the theoretical mixing time analysis, we aim to offer guidance over the optimal free parameter specification of HMC.

1.1
Related work

We start by reviewing the existing literature on the Metropolized Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA). MALA is essentially a special case of Metropolized HMC, where only one leapfrog integrator step is taken. The unadjusted version of MALA, the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA), has been extensively studied in the literature, with numerous papers discussing its properties
[
Par81
,
GM94
,
Dal17
,
DM17
,
CB18
,
DM19
,
VW19
,
AT22
]
. However, unadjusted sampling algorithms suffer from a significant limitation: their mixing time depends polynomially on the error tolerance. As a result, they are not suitable to obtain high-quality samples, hindering their widespread adoption in practical applications.

MALA was introduced by Besag
[
Bes94
]
as the adjusted Langevin algorithm that uses the Metropolis-Hastings step to ensure its convergence to the correct stationary distribution. Unlike ULA, MALA has a logarithmic dependence on the error tolerance
[
DCWY19
]
. Extensive work has been done on establishing the mixing time of MALA for various distributions
[
RT96
,
RR98
,
BRH13
,
DCWY19
,
CDWY20
,
LST21b
,
CLA+21
,
WSC22
]
. Specifically, the mixing time of MALA for sampling a
m
-strongly log-concave distribution with second-order smoothness parameter
L
is settled at
\tilde{O}(d^{\frac{1}{2}}L/m)
from a warm start. This bound is shown to be tight up to logarithmic factors
[
WSC22
]
. However, for general distributions with higher-order smoothness, it remains an open question how the mixing time scales with the dimension
d
. Roberts and Rosenthal
[
RR98
]
suggested a
d^{\frac{1}{3}}
scaling limit for sufficiently regular product distributions, but more research is needed to extend these results beyond product distributions.

While the mixing time of the Metropolized Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA) has been well studied in recent years, much less is known about MCMC methods that rely on Hamiltonian Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs). To transform HMC’s continuous ODE into an algorithm, numerical integrators are often used to discretize the ODE and produce a Markov chain based on a finite number of gradient evaluations. The leapfrog or Störmer-Verlet integrator is a commonly used choice that performs well in practice, and it discretizes the integral of the force using a trapezoidal rule
[
Nea11
]
. In the effort to theoretically characterize its convergence properties, Monmarché
[
Mon22
]
demonstrates that the unadjusted Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (uHMC) with the leapfrog integrator has a gradient complexity of
\tilde{O}(d^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1/2})
under the Lipschitz property of the Hessian. The result is obtained by examining a more general family of algorithms that are discretizations of underdamped Langevin diffusions (ULD). Shen et al.
[
SL19
]
incorporate the idea of Randomized Midpoint Method (RMM) to ULD, obtained a gradient complexity of
\tilde{O}(d^{1/3}\epsilon^{-2/3})
. This dimension dependency is shown to tight in Cao et al.
[
CLW21
]
. Recently, Nawaf and Milo
[
BRM22
]
use this idea of RMM to propose a random-time integrator, which they call “Stratified Monte Carlo” for uHMC. This algorithm achieves a gradient complexity of
\tilde{O}(d^{1/3}\epsilon^{-2/3})
without high-order derivative assumptions. All results for uHMC are stated for convergence in the Wasserstein distance.

Similar to the situation in Langevin algorithms, adding a Metropolis-Hastings step to uHMC can lead to a mixing time with logarithmic dependence on the error tolerance. In particular, Chen et al.
[
CDWY20
]
demonstrate a gradient complexity of
\tilde{O}(d^{11/12}\text{polylog}(1/\epsilon))
for sampling from a strongly log-concave and third-order smooth target distribution in total variation (TV) distance under a warm start. It is worth noting that there is a gap between the above result and what is known for sampling a regular product distribution via Metropolized HMC, where the gradient complexity only has dimension dependency
d^{\frac{1}{4}}

[
BPR+13
]
.

Our contribution

d

\epsilon

\tilde{O}\left(d^{\frac{1}{4}}\log(1/\epsilon)\right)

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the Metropolized HMC algorithm and provide necessary background knowledge to establish mixing time of Markov chains.

2.1
Markov chain basics

We consider the problem of sampling from a target measure
\mu
with density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
d
. Given a
Markov chain with transition kernel

P:\real^{d}\times\mathcal{B}(\real^{n})\to\real_{\geq 0}
where
\mathcal{B}(\real^{d})
denotes the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
d
, the
k
-step transition kernel
P^{k}
is defined recursively by
P^{k}(x,dy):=\int_{z\in\real^{n}}P^{k-1}(x,dz)P(z,dy)
.
The Markov chain is called
reversible
with respect to the target measure
\mu
if

\displaystyle\mu(dx)P(x,dy)=\mu(dy)P(y,dx).

One can associate the Markov chain with a transition operator
\mathcal{T}_{P}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{P}(\nu)(S):=\int_{y\in\real^{n}}d\nu(y)P(y,S),\quad\forall S\in\mathcal{B}(\real^{n}).

In words, when
\nu
is the distribution of the current state,
\mathcal{T}_{P}(\nu)
gives the distribution of the next state. And
\mathcal{T}_{P}^{n}(\nu):=\mathcal{T}_{P^{n}}(\nu)
is the distribution of the state after
n
steps.

s
-conductance.

For
s\in(0,1)
, we define the
s
-conductance
\Phi_{s}
of the Markov chain
P
with its stationary measure
\mu
as follows

\displaystyle\Phi_{s}(P):=\inf_{S:s<\mu(S)<1-s}\frac{\int_{S}P(x,S^{c})\mu(dx)}{\min\left\{\mu(S),\mu(S^{c})\right\}-s}.

(1)

When compared to conductance (the case
s=0
),
s
-conductance allows us to ignore small parts of the distribution where the conductance is difficult to bound.

Lazy chain.

Given a Markov chain with transition kernel
P
. We define its lazy variant
P^{\text{lazy}}
, which stays in the same state with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
, as

\displaystyle P^{\text{lazy}}(x,S):=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{x\to S}+\frac{1}{2}P(x,S).

Here
\delta_{x\to\cdot}
is the Dirac measure at
x
.
Since the lazy variant only slows down the convergence rate by a constant factor, we study lazy Markov chains in this paper to simplify our
theoretical analysis.

Total-variance distance.

Let the total variation (TV) distance between two probability distributions
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}
be

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}(\real^{n})}\left|\mathcal{P}_{1}(A)-\mathcal{P}_{2}(A)\right|,

where
\mathcal{B}(\real^{n})
is the Borel sigma-algebra on
n
. If
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
admit densities
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
respectively, we may write
{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\int\left|p_{1}(x)-p_{2}(x)\right|dx
.

Warm start.

We say an initial measure
\mu_{0}
is
M
-warm
if it satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{S\in\mathcal{B}(\real^{n})}\frac{\mu_{0}(S)}{\mu(S)}\leq M.

Mixing time.

For an error tolerance
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the total variance distance
\epsilon
-mixing time of the Markov chain
P
with initial distribution
\mu_{0}
and target distribution
\mu
is defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{mix}}^{P}(\epsilon,\mu_{0},\mu):=\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{T}^{n}_{P}(\mu_{0}),\mu\right)\leq\epsilon\right\}.

The
gradient complexity
of the Markov chain
P
is defined as the product of the number of gradient evaluations per-iteration and the mixing time.

2.2
HMC basics

In accordance with
[
Nea11
]
, we introduce a few basic facts about the HMC algorithm.

Continuous HMC dynamics.

The continuous HMC dynamics describes the evolution of a state vector
\mathfrak{q}_{t}\in\real^{d}
and its momentum
\mathfrak{p}_{t}\in\real^{d}
over time
t\geq 0
based on a Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to\real
via Hamilton’s equations:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathfrak{q}_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial p}(\mathfrak{q}_{t},\mathfrak{p}_{t})

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathfrak{p}_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial q}(\mathfrak{q}_{t},\mathfrak{p}_{t}).

Using our choice of the Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}(q,p):=f(q)+\frac{1}{2}\left\|p\right\|_{2}^{2}
, it becomes

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathfrak{q}_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{p}_{t}

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathfrak{p}_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\nabla f(\mathfrak{q}_{t}).

(2)

We can write Eq. (
2.2
) in its integral form as follows

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{q}_{0}+\int_{0}^{\eta}\mathfrak{p}_{s}ds

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{p}_{0}-\int_{0}^{\eta}\nabla f(\mathfrak{q}_{s})ds.

(3)

We denote the forward mapping from
(\mathfrak{q}_{0},\mathfrak{p}_{0})
to
(\mathfrak{q}_{t},\mathfrak{p}_{t})
as
\bar{F}_{t}
. That is,
\bar{F}_{t}(\mathfrak{q}_{0},\mathfrak{p}_{0})=(\mathfrak{q}_{t},\mathfrak{p}_{t})
.

Metropolized HMC with leapfrog integrator.

To implement continuous HMC practically, it needs to be approximated through time discretization. We use the leapfrog or Störmer-Verlet integrator to discretize the continuous HMC dynamics. For integer
K\geq 1
and step-size
\eta
, HMC with leapfrog integrator is an iterative algorithm that works as follows. At each iteration, given
q_{0}\in\real^{d}
and an independent
p_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{d})
, it runs the following updates for
k=0,1,\ldots,K-1
:

\displaystyle p_{k+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=p_{k}-\frac{\eta}{2}\nabla f(q_{k})

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=q_{k}+\eta p_{k+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle p_{k+1}

\displaystyle=p_{k+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{\eta}{2}\nabla f(q_{k+1}).

That is,

\displaystyle q_{k+1}

\displaystyle=q_{k}+\eta p_{k}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\nabla f(q_{k})

\displaystyle p_{k+1}

\displaystyle=p_{k}-\frac{\eta}{2}\nabla f(q_{k})-\frac{\eta}{2}\nabla f(q_{k+1}).

(4)

The forward mapping from
(q_{0},p_{0})
to
(q_{k},p_{k})
is denoted
F_{k}
. That is,
F_{k}(q_{0},p_{0})=(q_{k},p_{k})
.
Since discretizing the dynamics generates discretization errors at each iteration, a Metropolis-Hastings adjustment is used to ensure the correct stationary measure. The Metropolized HMC accepts the proposal
(q_{K},p_{K})
with acceptance probability

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{K,\eta}(q_{0},p_{0})

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{1,\frac{\exp(-\mathcal{H}(q_{K},p_{K}))}{\exp(-\mathcal{H}(q_{0},p_{0}))}\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{1,\exp\left(-f(q_{K})-\frac{1}{2}\left\|p_{K}\right\|_{2}^{2}+f(q_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\left\|p_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right\}.

(5)

The resulting algorithm is what we call the Metropolized HMC with step-size
\eta
and
K
leapfrog steps. Throughout the paper, this algorithm is what we refer to when we simply say the HMC algorithm. We denote its Markov transition kernel by
\mathcal{T}_{\text{HMC}(K,\eta)}
. The dependence on
K
and
\eta
is omitted if it is clear from the context. Note that MALA can be seen as a special case of Metropolized HMC with a single leapfrog step (K=1), as described in
[
Nea11
]
.

2.3
Notation

For a vector
q\in\real^{d}
, we use
\left\|q\right\|_{2}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
to denote its Euclidean norm. Given an integer
\ell\geq 1
, we introduce the
\ell
-th norm of a random variable
\mathbf{q}
on
d
as

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\!\left|\mathbf{q}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{{\ell}}:=\left({\mathbb{E}}\left\|\mathbf{q}\right\|_{2}^{\ell}\right)^{\frac{1}{\ell}}.

This

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\!\left|\mathbf{q}_{1}+\mathbf{q}_{2}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{{\ell}}\leq\left|\!\left|\!\left|\mathbf{q}_{1}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{{\ell}}+\left|\!\left|\!\left|\mathbf{q}_{2}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{{\ell}}.

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|\!\left|\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{q}_{s}ds\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{{\ell}}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq t^{\ell-1}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\!\left|\!\left|\mathbf{q}_{s}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{{\ell}}^{\ell}ds\leq\left(t\sup_{s\in[0,t]}{\left|\!\left|\!\left|\mathbf{q}_{s}\right|\!\right|\!\right|_{{\ell}}}\right)^{\ell}

(6)

.

x
respectively by
\nabla f(x)\in\real^{d}
,
\nabla^{2}f_{x}\in\real^{d\times d}
and
\nabla^{3}f_{x}\in\real^{d\times d\times d}
. Here

\displaystyle\left[\nabla f(x)\right]_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}f(x),\left[\nabla^{2}f_{x}\right]_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}f(x),\left[\nabla^{3}f_{x}\right]_{ijk}=\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}\partial x_{k}}f(x).

We use
[d]
to denote the set
\left\{1,2,\ldots,d\right\}
.
For
A\in\real^{d\times d\times d}
a 3-tensor, we use the notation
A[x,y,z]=\sum_{(i,j,k)\in[d]^{3}}A_{ijk}x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}
. For integer
k\geq 1

I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}

I_{1}\ldots I_{k}

\displaystyle\left\|A\right\|_{I_{1}\ldots I_{k}}:=\sup\left\{\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[d]^{3}}A_{\mathbf{i}}x^{(1)}_{\mathbf{i}_{I_{1}}}\cdots x^{(k)}_{\mathbf{i}_{I_{k}}}\middle|x^{(j)}_{\mathbf{i}_{I_{j}}}\in\real^{d\times\left|I_{j}\right|},\sum_{\mathbf{i}_{I_{j}}\in[d]^{\left|I_{j}\right|}}\left(x^{(j)}_{\mathbf{i}_{I_{j}}}\right)^{2}\leq 1,\forall j\in[k]\right\}.

(7)

Here

\mathbf{i}_{\ell}

. In particular, for

\displaystyle\left\|A\right\|_{\left\{1,2,3\right\}}=\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in[d]^{3}}A_{\mathbf{i}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|A\right\|_{\left\{1\right\}\left\{2\right\}\left\{3\right\}}=\sup\left\{\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}A_{ijk}x_{i}y_{j}z_{k}\middle|\left\|x\right\|_{2},\left\|y\right\|_{2},\left\|z\right\|_{2}\leq 1\right\}.

For any partition
\mathcal{P}
of
[3]

\left\|A\right\|_{\left\{1\right\}\left\{2\right\}\left\{3\right\}}\leq\left\|A\right\|_{\mathcal{P}}\leq\left\|A\right\|_{[3]}
.

I_{1}\cdots I_{k}

bounded for any

\left\|\nabla^{3}f_{\cdot}\right\|_{I_{1}\ldots I_{k}}:=\sup_{x\in\real^{d}}\left\|\nabla^{3}f_{x}\right\|_{I_{1}\ldots I_{k}}
. The norms for higher order tensors can be defined similarly (see e.g.

]
).

\mathbf{D}_{1}F
, for

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{1}F(x,y)=\left[\frac{\partial F_{i}(x,y)}{\partial x_{j}}\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq d}.

O(\cdot)

\log(x)
.

2.4
Regularity properties of a target density

Below we list the regularity properties that one could impose on a target density.

•

A twice differentiable function
f:\real^{d}\to\real
is

Main proof strategy.

We follow the framework of lower bounding the conductance of Markov chains to analyze mixing times

Lemma 1

(Lovasz and Simonovits
[

]
)
.

Consider a reversible lazy Markov chain with kernel

\mu
. Let
\mu_{0}
be an
M
-warm initial measure. Let

. Then

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{T}_{P}^{n}(\mu_{0}),\mu\right)\leq Ms+M\left(1-\frac{\Phi_{s}^{2}}{2}\right)^{n}.

In order to lower bound the conductance in the high probability region, we follow the proof of Theorem 3 in
[
WSC22
]
, which is an extension of a result by Lovász
[
Lov99
]
(see also Lemma 2 in
[

]

whenever

\Phi_{s}\geq\frac{\rho\Delta}{\psi_{\mu}}
. Consequently, it remains to bound the transition overlap, namely the TV-distance between
\mathcal{T}_{x}
and

for two close points

We bound the TV-distance via the following triangle inequality,

\displaystyle{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{T}_{x},\mathcal{T}_{y}\right)\leq{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{T}_{x},\mathcal{P}_{x}\right)+{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{P}_{x},\mathcal{P}_{y}\right)+{\rm{d}}_{{\rm{TV}}}\left(\mathcal{P}_{y},\mathcal{T}_{y}\right),

where
\mathcal{P}_{x}
is the proposal probability measure of HMC at
x

The remaining part of the proof is naturally divided into two parts: in Section
4
, we bound the proposal overlap, which is the TV-distance between

and
\mathcal{P}_{y}
for two close points
x,y
; in Section
5
, we bound the acceptance rate in Eq. (

).

6
Examples of functions with small strongly Hessian Lipschitz coefficient

In this section, we discuss several target density examples which have small strongly Hessian Lipschitz coefficient. We start by introducing ridge separable functions.

Ridge separable functions:

we say a function
f:\real^{d}\to\real
is
ridge separable
, if there exist an integer
n\geq 1
and univariate functions
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, vectors
\{a_{i}\}
in
d
such that

\displaystyle f(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}(a_{i}^{\top}\theta).

(27)

As discussed in
[
MMW+21
]
, ridge separability naturally arises in posterior sampling in Bayesian statistical models such as Bayesian ridge regression, generalized linear models, etc. Lemma
17
bounds various norms of the third order derivatives tensor of
f
. Its proof is provided in Appendix
C
.

Lemma 17

(Third derivative tensor bound for ridge separable
f
)
.

Given a ridge separable function
f
defined in Equation (
27
), assume that
a_{i}
’s are of unit norm. Suppose we have second and third order derivative bounds for
u_{i}
, i.e.
\sup_{\theta\in\real^{d}}|u^{\prime\prime}_{i}(z)|\leq\rho_{2}
and
\sup_{\theta\in\real^{d}}|u_{i}^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)|\leq\rho_{3}
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{2}f(\theta)\right\|_{2}\leq n\rho_{2},

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{3}f(\theta)\right\|_{\left\{12\right\}\left\{3\right\}}\leq\left\|\nabla^{3}f(\theta)\right\|_{\left\{123\right\}}\leq n\rho_{3}.

Note that
n
can be much smaller than
d
in practice. When the second and third order derivatives of the activation are bounded as above, then
f
is
\gamma L^{3/2}
-strongly Hessian Lipschitz for small
\gamma
, which means that using
K>1
in HMC is beneficial in reducing gradient complexity, as we discussed around Corollary
1
.
To see this argument quantitatively, we introduce a corollary that bounds the gradient complexity of HMC when
f
is ridge separable and the corresponding density satisfies an isoperimetric inequality.

Corollary 3
.

Let
f
be a ridge separable function defined in Equation (
27
). Given that the target measure
\mu
with density
\propto e^{-f}
satisfies an isoperimetric inequality with constant
\psi_{\mu}
and satisfies the derivative bounds in Lemma
17
, then the gradient complexity of HMC with the optimal choices of free parameters as in Corollary
1
is
\tilde{O}(d^{\frac{1}{4}}(n^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho_{3}\rho_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}}+n\rho_{2})\psi_{\mu}^{-2})
.

Proof.

Note that from Lemma
17
,
f
is
L
smooth and
\gamma L^{\frac{3}{2}}
-strongly Hessian Lipschitz with
L=n\rho_{2}
and
\gamma=(n\rho_{3})/(n\rho_{2})^{3/2}=\rho_{3}/(n^{1/2}\rho_{2}^{3/2})
. The mixing time then follows directly from Corollary
1
.
∎

Next, we present an example of a posterior distribution that arises in Bayesian logistic regression where its log density is ridge separable.

Lemma 18

(Logistic regression)
.

Consider the logistic regression problem.
We observe a set of data points
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
where
x_{i}\in\real^{d}
and
y_{i}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
. For simplicity, assume
\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}=1,\ \forall i\in[n]
. The logistic loss is defined as

\displaystyle\ell(z,1):=\log{\big(1+\exp{\left(-z\right)}\big)},

\displaystyle\ell(z,0):=z+\log{\big(1+\exp{\left(-z\right)}\big)}.

The total loss on the dataset is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\theta^{\top}x_{i},y_{i}).

Given
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we would like to sample from the posterior distribution
\mu
on
\theta
with likelihood
\propto e^{-\mathcal{L}(\theta)}
and prior
\mathcal{N}(0,\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{I}_{d})
. That is,

\displaystyle\mu(\theta)\propto e^{-\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\|\theta\right\|_{2}^{2}}.

Then from a warm start, HMC has gradient complexity
\tilde{O}(d^{\frac{1}{4}}(n+\alpha^{2})/\alpha^{2})
.

Finally, we consider the problem of posterior sampling for two layer neural networks with fixed second-layer weights. This is another example where the log-density is ridge separable.

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u^{\prime\prime\prime}_{i}(a_{i}^{\top}\theta)a_{i}\otimes a_{i}\otimes a_{i}.

(37)

First, for the operator norm of the Hessian, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{2}f_{\theta}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|u^{\prime\prime}_{i}(a_{i}^{\top}\theta)\right|\big\|a_{i}^{2\otimes}\big\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|u^{\prime\prime}_{i}(a_{i}^{\top}\theta)\right|\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n\rho_{2}.

Similarly, for the
\left\{1\right\}\left\{2\right\}\left\{3\right\}
-norm of the third derivative, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{3}f_{\theta}\right\|_{\left\{1\right\}\left\{2\right\}\left\{3\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|u^{\prime\prime\prime}_{i}(a_{i}^{\top}\theta)\big|\left\|{a_{i}}^{3\otimes}\right\|_{\left\{1\right\}\left\{2\right\}\left\{3\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u^{\prime\prime\prime}_{i}(a_{i}^{\top}\theta)|\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{2}^{3}

\displaystyle\leq n\rho_{3}.

To bound the
\|.\|_{\left\{123\right\}}
norm:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{3}f_{\theta}\right\|_{\left\{123\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|u^{\prime\prime\prime}_{i}(a_{i}^{\top}\theta)\right|\left\|a_{i}^{3\otimes}\right\|_{\left\{123\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|u^{\prime\prime\prime}_{i}(a_{i}^{\top}\theta)\right|\left\|a_{i}\right\|_{2}^{3}

\displaystyle\leq n\rho_{3}.

Finally note that the
\|.\|_{\left\{12\right\}\left\{3\right\}}
norm is upper bounded by the
\|.\|_{\left\{123\right\}}
norm.

∎

Proof of Lemma
18
.

Calculating the second derivative of the logistic loss function:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}\ell(y,-1)=\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}\ell(y,+1)=\frac{e^{-y}}{(1+e^{-y})^{2}}>0.

Therefore,
\mathcal{L}
is convex in
\theta
. Additionally, for
f(\theta):=\mathcal{L}(\theta)+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left\|\theta\right\|_{2}^{2}
,
e^{-f}
satisfies isoperimetric inequality with coefficient
\Omega(\alpha)
.
Moreover, for smoothness of
\ell
, note that

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}\ell(y,-1)\Big|\leq 1.

Moreover,
the third derivative of
\ell
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}}{dy^{3}}\ell(y,-1)=\frac{-e^{-y}(1+e^{-y})^{2}-2e^{-y}(1+e^{-y})(-e^{-y})}{(1+e^{-y})^{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-3y}-e^{-y}}{(1+e^{-y})^{4}},

which is bounded as

\displaystyle\big|\frac{d^{3}}{dy^{3}}\ell(y,-1)\big|\leq 1.

Therefore, using Lemma
17
we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|_{2}\leq n,

which implies that
f
is
n+\alpha
smooth. Moreover, using Lemma
17
for the third derivative

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{3}f(\theta)\|_{\left\{12\right\}\left\{3\right\}}\leq n.

Plugging this into the mixing bound in Corollary
3
for Ridge separable functions completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
19
.

To bound the Hessian, note that for any
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
,

\displaystyle v^{\top}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-f_{NN}(x_{i}))\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})(x_{i}^{\top}v_{j})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})(x_{i}^{\top}v_{j})\right)^{2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|y_{i}-f_{NN}(x_{i})\big|\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})^{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})^{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq nmc^{2}.

Here, again we are able to bound the norm of the third derivative tensor of
f
efficiently. We take three times derivatives of
f
in direction
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})
in the space of neurons, where for all
j
,
v_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
.

\displaystyle\nabla^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}[v,v,v]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-f_{NN}(x_{i})\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}({\theta_{j}}^{\top}x_{i})({x_{i}}^{\top}v_{j})^{3}\right)

\displaystyle+6\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{j}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})(x_{i}^{\top}v_{j})^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{j}\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})(x_{i}^{\top}v_{j})\right).

(38)

Hence, the
\|.\|_{\left\{123\right\}}
norm can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}\right\|_{\left\{123\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\big|y_{i}-f_{NN}(x_{i})\big|^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})}^{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{6}\right)}

\displaystyle+6\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})}^{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{4}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})}^{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)}.

But from the boundedness of the activations
|\sigma|\leq c
and
y_{i}=O(mc)
, we have
\big|y_{i}-f_{NN}(x_{i})\big|=O(mc)
. Combining this with the fact that
|\sigma^{\prime}|,|\sigma^{\prime\prime}|,|\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}|\leq c
and the fact that the data is norm one, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}\right\|_{\left\{123\right\}}\lesssim c^{2}m\sqrt{m}n.

Next, we bound the
\|.\|_{\left\{12\right\}\left\{3\right\}}
norm of the third order tensor
more efficiently.
To see this, note that similar to the bound for
\|.\|_{\left\{123\right\}}
norm, we consider the decomposition
\nabla^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=A_{1}+A_{2}
as the sum of two tensors, where
A_{1}[v,v,v]
is the first term in Equation (
38
) and
A_{2}[v,v,v]
is the second term in Equation (
38
). We can bound the norm of
A_{1}
as

\displaystyle\left\|A_{1}\right\|_{\left\{12\right\}\left\{3\right\}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{v=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{m},\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(v_{j}^{\top}x_{i})\left(y_{i}-f_{NN}(x_{i})\right)\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{m},\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-f_{NN}(x_{i})\right)^{2}(v_{j}^{\top}x_{i})^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})^{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{4}\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{v=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{m},\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1}c^{2}m\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\right)v_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{m},\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1}c^{2}m\sqrt{n}\sqrt{\beta\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\|v_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}}=c^{2}m\sqrt{\beta n}.

(39)

where recall that
\beta
is the largest eigenvalue of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}
. On the other hand, for the second term in
\nabla^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|A_{2}\right\|_{\left\{12\right\}\left\{3\right\}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{v=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{m},\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})(v_{j}^{\top}x_{i})\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle+\sup_{v=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{m},\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})(x_{i}^{\top}v_{j})x_{i}\right)_{j=1}^{m}{\left(\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})x_{i}\right)_{j=1}^{m}}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{m},\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})x_{i}x_{i}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{j^{\prime}}^{\top}x_{i})(v_{j^{\prime}}^{\top}x_{i})\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\sup_{v=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{m},\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1}n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})^{2}(x_{i}^{\top}v_{j})^{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})^{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim mnc^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})^{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle+\sup_{v=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{m},\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1}nmc^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{j}^{\top}x_{i})^{2}\left\|v_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim m^{2}nc^{4}+mnc^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim nm^{2}c^{4}+n^{2}mc^{4},

(40)

where we used the fact that
\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1
, which implies
\big|v_{j}^{\top}x_{i}\big|\leq\left\|v_{j}\right\|_{2}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}=\left\|v_{j}\right\|_{2}
. Hence

\displaystyle\left\|A_{2}\right\|_{\left\{12\right\}\left\{3\right\}}\leq m\sqrt{n}c^{2}+n\sqrt{m}c^{2}.

Combining Equations (
39
) and (
40
), we conclude

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}\right\|_{\left\{12\right\}\left\{3\right\}}\leq\left(m\sqrt{n}(1+\sqrt{\beta})+n\sqrt{m}\right)c^{2}.

Comparing
\left\|\nabla^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}\right\|_{\left\{12\right\}\left\{3\right\}}
to
\left\|\nabla^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}\right\|_{\left\{123\right\}}
, we see that we gained a factor of
\sqrt{m}\min\{(1+\sqrt{\beta})\sqrt{n},\sqrt{m}\}
over
\left\|\nabla^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}\right\|_{\left\{123\right\}}
(Note that
\beta\leq n
). Also note that
\left(m\sqrt{n}(1+\sqrt{\beta})+n\sqrt{m}\right)c^{2}
can be much smaller than the overall dimension of the weight space, which is
md^{\prime}
.

∎
