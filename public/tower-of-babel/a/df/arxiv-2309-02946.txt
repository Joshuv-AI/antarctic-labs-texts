Numerical Construction of initial data sets for inhomogeneous cosmological spacetimes with spatial topology of 𝕋 3 \mathbb{T}^{3}
Alejandro Estrada, Leon Escobar-Diaz

Abstract
In this work, we numerically construct initial data sets for cosmological spacetimes with a spatial topology of 𝕋 3 \mathbb{T}^{3} , which are generally inhomogeneous. To do so, we implement a pseudo-spectral approach based on the discrete Fourier transform for numerically solving Einstein’s constraint equations in an algebraic-hyperbolic form. We explore the advantages and disadvantages of this method by comparing the numerical solutions with known analytical initial data sets. Furthermore, we obtain new families of initial data sets numerically for different configurations of the boundary values of certain geometric variables.



1
Introduction

The evolution of cosmological space-time is modelled mathematically as an initial value problem in the framework of general relativity. This implies that two sets of equations must be solved; namely, evolution and constraint equations.
In particular, this last set of equations determines the
initial data
of the evolution problem. In general, using the constraint equations to find initial data analytically or numerically is not a simple task, since, in general, they constitute a system of coupled nonlinear
partial differential equations.

One of the standard numerical approaches for solving this system is based on the Lichnerowicz-York conformal method, which involves utilizing a conformal transformation to reduce the equations to a set of coupled non-linear elliptic partial differential equations (for a detailed presentation of the subject, see
[
1
,
4
]
). This method has proven to be highly effective in constructing initial data sets for various scenarios involving asymptotically flat space-times, such as binary black hole systems. Its success stems from the simplification of the elliptic system achieved by assuming that the initial data is conformally flat.
However, applying this method in cosmological cases is not as straightforward due to the non-asymptotically flat nature of space-time, making it challenging to choose an appropriate conformal metric for the transformation of the equations. For an application of this method in a cosmological context, refer to
[
14
]
.

In this work, we explore an alternative method for numerically solving the constraint equations
introduced by Rácz in
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. Instead of reducing the constraints to a system of elliptic equations, this approach transforms the constraints into either a parabolic-hyperbolic system or an algebraic-hyperbolic system, requiring data only on a two-dimensional spatial surface. There are several works in the literature where this approach has been applied to obtain initial data sets for various asymptotically flat scenarios involving black holes configurations. See for instance
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
]
and more recently
[
10
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, this method has not yet been employed in cosmological scenarios.

In the present contribution, we investigate the Algebraic-Hyperbolic Formulation (AHF) of the constraint equations in cosmological scenarios. Specifically, we focus on the
\mathbb{T}^{3}
-Gowdy and Perturbed FRLW metrics, assuming a spatial topology of
\mathbb{T}^{3}
.
To achieve this, we employ a pseudo-spectral method based on the Fourier transform to solve the system of partial differential equations (PDEs) obtained through the AHF. We successfully reproduce the analytical solutions for these two cases. Additionally, we generate new sets of initial data by proposing modifications to the original AHF system. Specifically, we explore three different approaches to reduce the constraints of the Algebraic-Hyperbolic Formulation, enabling the attainment of stable numerical results.

This work is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we present the algebraic-hyperbolic formulation of the Einstein constraint equations for a Riemannian manifold with
\mathbb{T}^{3}
-topology. Later, in Section
3
, we provide a brief summary of the Fourier pseudo-spectral method for numerically solving these equations. In Section
4
, we explore the advantages and disadvantages of this method by comparing the numerical solutions with known analytical initial data sets, such as Minkowski,
T^{3}
-Gowdy, and perturbed FRW metrics. We conduct convergence tests and explore spectral filters to enhance the accuracy and stability of the method. In Section
5
, we present new sets of initial data for different configurations of the boundary data of the geometric variables. Finally, in Section
6
, we discuss the main results of this work.

3.1
The angular discretization

3.1.1
The angular derivatives

Let us start by defining the angular grid. We will assume that the domain for the coordinates
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
is some closed region given by
[-L,L]\times[-L,L]

x_{1i}=-L+ih_{1}
and

, where
h_{1}=2L/N_{x_{1}}
and
h_{2}=2L/N_{x_{2}}
are the step sizes in the directions of the variables

and

We will denote the samples of the fields

on the angular grid as
u(r)_{ij}:=u(r,x_{1(i)},x_{2(j)})
, assuming

\displaystyle u(r)_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{(2L)^{2}}\sum_{k_{1}=-N_{x_{1}}/2+1}^{N_{x_{1}}/2}\left(\sum_{k_{2}=-N_{x_{2}}/2+1}^{N_{x_{2}}/2}\tilde{u}(r)_{k_{1}k_{2}}\ b_{k1,k2}(x_{1(i)},x_{2(j)})\right),

The

3.1.2
The aliasing error and filtering strategy

The spectral convergence of the Fourier differentiation method (see
[
15
,
8
]
) is not enough for achieving good numerical approximations of the system (
6
)-(
8
) because of a common source of error proper to spectral methods, known in the literature as aliasing. This kind of error emerges from the non-linearities of the PDE and is due to the finiteness of the grid. Since our equations (eqs. (
6
) to (
8
)) are non-linear, dealing with this error is a must in order to obtain accurate numerical results.

To illustrate this phenomena let us consider a
1-
dimensional region
[-L,L]
and a
N
nodes regular grid
x_{i}=-L+ih
with
h=2L/N
. Now, assume that we want to compute the product of two periodic fields
u
and
v
in
[-L,L]
. Since they are periodic functions, the samples of the fields
u_{i}
and
v_{j}
at the node
x_{i}
can be decomposed in a truncate Fourier series as

\displaystyle u_{i}=\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{k=-N/2+1}^{N/2}\tilde{u}_{k}b_{k}(x_{i}),\quad v_{i}=\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{l=-N/2+1}^{N/2}\tilde{v}_{l}b_{l}(x_{i}),

where
b_{k}(x_{i})
is the Fourier basis in
1
-dimension. The numbers
k
and
l
are known as the
wave numbers
or
frequencies
. Clearly the product between these two fields at the node
x_{i}
is given by

\begin{split}u_{i}v_{i}&=\frac{1}{(2L)^{2}}\sum_{k,l=-N/2+1}^{N/2}b_{k+l}(x_{i})\tilde{u}_{k}\tilde{v}_{l},\end{split}

which contains terms with frequencies
k+l
that exceed the range
[-N/2+1,N/2]
, leading to the so-called
aliased frequencies
. Since these frequencies cannot be represented in the frequency grid, due to the
k
periodicity of the basis functions
b_{k}(x_{i})
, the spectral coefficients associated to them are absorbed by their periodic equivalents inside the grid. As a result, the computed product
u_{i}v_{i}
may deviate significantly from the true product at the node
x_{i}
. This is what is known as the aliasing error, and it can be quickly accumulated during the evolution of the system (
6
)-(
8
) due to non-linear terms in the equations.

To control this error, we can modify the Fourier series of the fields by truncating them up to some frequency
k_{M}
. The idea is that for any
k,l\in[-k_{M},k_{M}]
we have that the frequencies
k+l\in[-N/2+1,N/2]
, avoiding the aliased frequencies. For instance, it can be easily proved (see for instance
[
15
]
) that by demanding

k_{M}\leq\frac{2}{3}(N/2),

the aliasing due to products of pairs of functions is removed. This is known as the
(2/3)-
filter
and is the easier way of imposing some kind of control on the aliasing error. Equivalently, it is easy to see that with

k_{M}\leq\frac{1}{2}(N/2),

we can remove the aliasing due to products of triplets of functions, we call this the
(1/2)-
filter
.

In general, a filter can be defined as a modification of the truncated Fourier series as (see
[
8
]
)

\displaystyle u_{i}=\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{k=-N/2+1}^{N/2}\sigma_{k}\tilde{u}_{k}\ b_{k}(x_{i}),

where
\sigma_{k}
is called the filter function. For the
(2/3)-
and
(1/2)-
filters

\sigma_{k}
takes the form of the step function

\sigma_{k}=\begin{cases}1\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ if }\penalty\ |k|\leq k_{M}\\
0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ otherwise }\end{cases}.

This is why we refer to these filters as
step filters
. There are many other filtering strategies in the literature. See for instance
[
8
]
for a deep discussion about this topic. In particular, in this work we will be interested on the exponential filter where the filter function is given by

\sigma_{k}:=\sigma(\theta_{k}=\frac{2\pi k}{N})=e^{-\alpha\theta_{k}^{p}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \alpha>0\text{ and }p\in 2\mathbb{N}.

(13)

It is possible to choose combinations of the parameters

and
p
that produce approximations of the step filters. To establish these combinations, notice that the first derivative of
\sigma(k)
, denoted by
\sigma^{\prime}
, is zero in

and

is big enough. This means that
\sigma^{\prime}
reaches its maximum at some

(0,N/2]
. In the case of the step filters, the derivative is zero everywhere except at

(with

we recover the
(1/2)-
filter
and the

respectively) where we can think of it as infinity.
By identifying the exponential filter derivative’s maximum with

and

The radial discretization

The derivatives along the

\boldsymbol{U}(r_{n},x_{1},x_{2})=\left(X(r_{n},x_{1},x_{2}),Y_{1}(r_{n},x_{1},x_{2}),Y_{2}(r_{n},x_{1},x_{2})\right)

\boldsymbol{U}^{(n)}_{ij}:=\left(X(r_{n},x_{1(i)},x_{2(j)}),Y_{1}(r_{n},x_{1(i)},x_{2(j)}),Y_{2}(r_{n},x_{1(i)},x_{2(j)})\right),

\boldsymbol{U}^{(n+1)}_{ij}=\boldsymbol{f}\left(r_{n},\boldsymbol{U}^{(n)}_{ij},\partial\boldsymbol{U}^{(n)}_{ij}\right),

\boldsymbol{U}^{(n)}_{ij}

\displaystyle\partial_{r}X

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}}}X+\hat{\alpha}\left((Z-\frac{1}{2}X)H_{j}^{\penalty\ j}-H_{ji}\mathring{k}^{ji}-8\pi J^{(\perp)}\right),

5.3
Initial data sets of type 3

We conclude this section by exploring a different way to solve
eq.

30
without losing generality in the degrees of freedom of the sources, that is, keeping
J_{i}^{(||)}
as a free choice. The idea for this approach arises from the following observation: if we assume the field
\beta_{i}=0
, we eliminate the spatial derivatives in the equation
eq.

31
for the field
X
. Thus, by assuming
H^{i}_{\penalty\ i}=0
, indicating that we are foliating
\Sigma
with minimal surfaces, the equation
eq.

31
becomes independent of
X
. Therefore, it can be solved through integration along the radial coordinate by

X(r)=\int_{r_{0}}^{r_{f}}\hat{\alpha}(H_{ji}\mathring{k}^{ji}-8\pi J^{(\perp)})dr+F(x_{1},x_{2}),

(37)

With
F(x_{1},x_{2})
being an arbitrary function that depends solely on the angular variables, the integral term in eq. (
37
) can be computed analytically or numerically using conventional methods, as
H_{ji}
,
\kappa^{ji}
, and
J^{(\perp)}
are all known functions. Therefore, to determine
X
, we just have to compute the function
F(x_{1},x_{2})
on the initial surface
S_{r_{0}}
. To achieve this, we solve eq. (
30
) for
\nabla_{i}X
, leading to

\mathbf{D}_{i}X=\frac{-1}{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{Z_{0}}{2X}}\left(-\frac{\mathbf{D}_{i}Z_{0}}{2X}-Z\dot{\hat{n}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{\hat{n}}_{i}X+\dot{\hat{n}}^{j}\mathring{k}_{ij}-\mathbf{D}^{j}\mathring{k}_{ij}+8\pi J^{(||)}_{i}\right).

Then, substituting
X=I+F
, where
I
is the integral term of
eq.

37
, we obtain

\mathbf{D}_{i}F=\hat{G}_{i}(X)-\mathbf{D}_{i}I=G_{i}(F;r,x_{1},x_{2}),

(38)

with

\hat{G}_{i}(X):=\frac{-1}{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{Z_{0}}{2X}}\left(-\frac{\mathbf{D}_{i}Z_{0}}{2X}-Z\dot{\hat{n}}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{\hat{n}}_{i}X+\dot{\hat{n}}^{j}\mathring{k}_{ij}-\mathbf{D}^{j}\mathring{k}_{ij}+8\pi J^{(||)}_{i}\right).

Finally, applying covariant derivatives on both sides of the equation and contracting with the angular metric
h_{ab}
, we obtain the following second order PDE for
F

\Delta F=\mathbf{D}^{i}\mathbf{D}_{i}F=\mathbf{D}^{i}G_{i}=g\left(F,\partial F;r,x_{1},x_{2}\right).

(39)

Because the principal operator of this equation is the Laplacian on
S_{r}
for some fixed value of
r
, it can be solved by standard numerical methods for treating elliptic PDEs, such as the fixed point or Newton algorithms, for instance. However, these methods require a further analytical analysis in order to guarantee convergences of the solution. Furthermore, since the discrete Laplacian obtained through Fourier differentiation does not lead to an invertible matrix in the angular grid (see for instance
[
26
]
or
[
15
]
), the application of these methods is not a trivial task. Therefore, to avoid such complications, we simply transform the elliptic problem
eq.

39
into the following parabolic initial value problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F

\displaystyle=\Delta F-g\left(F,\partial F;r,x_{1},x_{2}\right),

(40)

\displaystyle F|_{t_{0}}

\displaystyle=F_{0}\,

(41)

where
t
is a free parameter and
F_{0}
is a known function on
S_{r}
. Local existence and uniqueness of solutions for this kind of parabolic equations are well established. See, for instance,
[
25
]
, ch. 15. In particular, it is known that given initial condition of
F
belonging to
C^{1}(M)
, the solution of
eq.

40
should exists and is unique on
(t,\vec{x})\in[0,T]\times M
for some
T>0
, where
M
is a Riemannian manifold.

At this point, it is worth discussing the type of initial data that we can obtain from this approach. We recall that in addition to imposing the condition
Y_{i}=0
, we also assume that
\beta_{i}=0
and
H^{i}_{\penalty\ i}=0
, which are restrictions on the foliations
S_{r}
along the coordinate
r
. The first condition simply means that the normal vector to the surfaces
S_{r}
is aligned with the coordinate
r
, and the second condition requires that the surfaces
S_{r}
are minimal for any fixed value of
r
. One way to easily achieve this condition is by demanding that the components of the spatial metric
\gamma_{ab}
are independent of
r
, which implies that the metric is
\mathbb{S}^{1}
symmetric. However, this does not necessarily mean that the resulting initial data set
(\gamma_{ab},K_{ab},\rho,J_{a})

\mathbb{S}^{1}
symmetric, as the components of the extrinsic curvature can still depend on the
r

37

It is important to note that eq. (
38

Y_{i}
equation eq. (
7

R=||\mathbf{D}{i}F+\mathbf{D}{i}I-\hat{G}_{i}(X)||,

(42)

can be used as a measure of the
fulfillment
of the hyperbolic constraint equations obtained through the solution of eq. (
40
). Therefore, we can state two stopping criteria:
(1)
Since the solutions of eq. (
40
) must reach a steady state, if
X_{n}
and
X_{n-1}
are solutions obtained with successive
t
steps
t_{n}
and
t_{n-1}
, we expect
||X_{n}-X_{n+1}||
to converge to zero. Then, when
||X_{n}-X_{n+1}||
gets under a certain tolerance, we can stop the evolution.
(2)
Since eq. (
42
) tells us if the equation for
Y_{i}
is being fulfilled, when
R
gets under a certain tolerance, we can stop the evolution. The difference between these two stopping criteria is displayed below.

With the above in mind, as in the previous sections, we need to close the system by setting the free fields. To do so we will consider the following

g_{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}-f(t,x_{1})&0&0&0\\

By a straightforward

decomposition we can compute

and the analytical

. Then through the

), by evaluating the constraint equations, eqs. (

) and (

Therefore, with the conditions

To illustrate the behavior of this approach, we perform two kinds of experiments: initial condition modifications, as in sec.

6
Discussion

In this work, we have presented a numerical construction of initial data sets for cosmological space-times with a spatial topology of
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. To tackle this problem, we have employed a pseudo-spectral approach based on the discrete Fourier transform, taking advantage of the periodic boundary conditions induced by the geometry of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. This approach allowed us to numerically solve Einstein’s constraint equations, which are formulated in an algebraic-hyperbolic form. By implementing this method, we have explored the advantages and disadvantages of this approach by comparing our numerical solutions to known analytical initial data sets. Through this comparison process, we assessed the accuracy of this method and identify its limitations.

Regarding its limitations, we have observed that even in cases where the field
Y_{i}
is non-zero, the hyperbolic constraints become numerically unstable, significantly affecting the accuracy of the solutions and therefore the initial data
(\gamma_{ab},K_{ab})
. This instability also arises when searching for new initial data sets. Although spectral filters can partially control this numerical instability in some cases, further study is needed to fully understand and address this issue. It is possible that rewriting the system in different variables or implementing a different numerical integrator, such as an implicit method or a combination of implicit-explicit methods (see for instance
[
3
]
), could help resolve this issue. This topic presents an interesting avenue for future research.

Despite these restrictions, we have successfully obtained new families of initial data sets by modifying the original system of equations. These modifications of the AHF have allowed us to investigate various geometric scenarios and expand our understanding of the behavior and properties of in-homogeneous cosmological space-times. Although more research is needed, these results show the potential and flexibility of this formulation to address the initial data problem in cosmology.

In summary, this work contributes to the numerical construction of initial data sets for cosmological space-times with a spatial topology of
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. It provides insights into the in-homogeneous nature of these space-times and expands the range of available initial data configurations for future investigations in the field.
