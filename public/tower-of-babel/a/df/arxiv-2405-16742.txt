Free energy formulas for confined nematic liquid crystals based on analogies with Kirchhoff-Routh theory in vortex dynamics
Hiroyuki Miyoshi, Hiroki Miyazako, Takaaki Nara

Abstract
Active nematics are influenced by alignment angle singularities called topological defects. The localization of these defects is of major interest for biological applications. The total distortion of alignment angles due to defects is evaluated using Frank free energy, which is one of the criteria used to determine the location and stability of these defects. Previous work used the line integrals of a complex potential associated with the alignments for the energy calculation (Miyazako and Nara, R. Soc. Open Sci., 2022), which has a high computational cost. We propose analytical formulas for the free energy in the presence of multiple topological defects in confined geometries. The formulas derived here are an analogue of Kirchhoff-Routh functions in vortex dynamics. The proposed formulas are explicit with respect to the defect locations and conformal maps, which enables the explicit calculation of the energy extrema. The formulas are applied to calculate the locations of defects in so-called doublets and triplets by solving simple polynomial formulas. A stability analysis is also conducted to detect whether defect pairs with charges ± 1 / 2 \pm 1/2 are stable or unstable in triplet regions. Our numerical results are shown to match the experimental results (Ienaga et al., Soft Matter, 2023).

Introduction

A spindle-shaped cell is characterized by its elongated shape.
Such cells play crucial roles in various physiological processes, including muscle contraction, tissue organization, and cell migration.
Investigations of the dynamics of biological cells are important for elucidating fundamental processes in development, disease progression, and tissue homeostasis.
In such investigations, spindle- and rod-shaped cells are modeled as nematic liquid crystals, which are also known as “active nematics”
[
1
,
2
]
.
Active nematics are essential not only for understanding the collective dynamics of biological systems such as cells and tissues
[
3
]
but also for artificial biological systems.
The various phenomena observed in active nematics under diverse scenarios have been reviewed in
[
4
]
.

An important property in the field of active nematics
is the existence of topological defects
[
5
,
2
]
.
Topological defects are regions where the alignment angles of nematic liquid crystals are not defined.
Such singularities influence the orientation of surrounding nematics
[
6
]
and determine the physical behaviours of active nematics.
The information of the rotations in the surrounding alignments
is referred to as the topological charge of defects, which takes discrete values of integers or half-integers.

Topological defects play a significant role in biological systems.
Maroudas-Sacks
et al
. presented experimental data on topological defects in
Hydra
and showed
that topological defects with topological charges
-1/2
,
+1/2
, and
+1
regulate the process of morphogenesis
[
7
]
.
Kawaguchi
et al.
found that topological defects drive the aggregation of cultured murine neural progenitor cells
[
8
]
.
They demonstrated that cells concentrate around defects with a charge of
+1/2
, and escape from defects with a charge of
-1/2
.

The behaviour of topological defects is influenced by the presence of boundaries.
Saw
et al.
observed topological defects with charges of
+1/2
and
-1/2
in a star-shaped region to study cell death and extrusion
[
9
]
.
Ienaga
et al
. modified the closed domains of single circular shapes
to regions consisting of multiple discs overlapping each other (doublets and triplets) to study the relationship between existing defects and geometries
[
10
]
.
They conjectured that the positions of defects in both doublets and triplets linearly correlate with the distances between the centres of circles.
Furthermore, they experimentally examined the transition from a formation where two defects with a charge of
+1/2
exist
to a state where a defect with a charge of
-1/2
and three defects with a charge of
+1/2
exist
as the three discs of triplets move apart.

Based on liquid crystal theory and one-constant approximation
[
5
]
,
mathematical analysis using the theory of two-dimensional (2D) harmonic functions has been conducted to determine the location and stability of topological defects in confined geometries.
Duclos
et al.
investigated the positions of topological defects in circular closed domains
and confirmed both experimentally and mathematically
that two defects with a charge of
+1/2
are located at a distance of
5^{-1/4}R

from the centre of the disc with radius
R

[
11
]
.
They modelled the Frank free energy associated with cell alignment angles based on one-constant approximation
and mathematically proved that defects exist at the minimum of this free energy.
They expressed the energy as the surface integral of the square of the spatial gradients of orientation excluding the vanishingly small defect core regions.

Because the alignment angles are harmonic functions in 2D, it is natural to use a complex analysis formulation.
Tarnavskyy
et al.
showed that topological defects exist on the edge of a half of a lens-shaped region using conformal mappings
[
12
]
.
Chandler and Spagnolie used Schwarz-Christoffel mappings to find topological defects on the boundary of a polygonal-shaped immersed body
[
13
]
.
The theory developed by Chandler and Spagnolie was also applied to study two-body interactions such as body forces and torques
[
14
]
.
Tang
et al.
showed that Frank free energy can be classified into torque-free energy and orientation energy and
that torque determines the dynamics of topological defects
[
15
]
.

Miyazako and Nara proposed a complex potential for cell alignment angles
based on complex analysis to represent the orientational order of cells
[
16
]
.
They derived an analytical formula that describes
the orientation of cells with topological defects at arbitrary positions
strictly inside arbitrary closed domains
[
16
]
.
They employed the theory of conformal mapping
to compute cell orientations in arbitrary shapes.
Additionally, by utilizing the complex Green’s theorem,
they evaluated the energy of the domain as line integrals to avoid the divergence of energy at singularities of defects.
They updated defect positions using steepest descent methods,
and discussed whether the initial configuration of defects would annihilate or stabilize in star-shaped domains.
Recently, this approach was extended to doubly connected domains
[
17
]
using the prime functions developed by Crowdy
[
18
]
.

Following Duclos
et al.

[
11
]
, we assume that topological defects become stable when the Frank free energy is minimized.
Energy calculations are necessary for localizing topological defects at arbitrary positions in confined domains.
However, there are no analytical formulas for the Frank free energy in these geometries except for the line integrals proposed by Miyazako and Nara
[
16
]
,
which have a high numerical cost and require finding a suitable branch cut of the logarithmic singularities associated with defects.
This significantly complicates the theoretical analysis of the positions of topological defects.

In this paper, we develop analytical formulas for the Frank free energy when the radius of the defect core is small as shown in Figure
1
.
The explicit formulas are derived from an expression for the
complex potential, representing the sum of each defect, excluding the contribution from the defect itself.
The proposed formulas are new in the field of liquid crystal theory.
This paper provides an analogy between the concept of Hamiltonian or Kirchhoff-Routh functions for vortex dynamics
[
19
,
18
]
and the Frank free energy of nematic liquid crystals.
The proposed formulas enable to derive the explicit expression for the energy up to the order

\epsilon\log\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
is the size of defect cores.
It is mathematically demonstrated that
two defects are located symmetrically at
\pm 5^{-1/4}
in the unit disc.
We also validate the conjecture regarding the positions of topological defects in doublets and triplets given by Ienaga
et al.

[
10
]
.
It is found that our analytical results closely match the experimental results for doublets reported by Ienaga
et al.
,
and detect the transition of the formation of topological defects in triplets.

Section

2
Formulation of topological defects and Frank-free energy

2.1
Problem setting

Following the theory developed by Miyazako and Nara
[
11
]
,
this section introduces a mathematical formulation of alignment angles in the presence of multiple topological defects based on complex analysis.
The nematic liquid crystals are assumed to be spindle-shaped and small relative to the outer boundary, thus allowing them to be treated as rod-like particles.
They are assumed to be aligned in the direction of a unit vector
\bm{n}_{\rm d}
as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{n}_{\rm d}=(\cos\phi(u,v),\sin\phi(u,v))^{\top},

(1)

where
\phi(u,v)
is the angle of the field at position
(u,v)
.
The direction
\bm{n}_{\rm d}
is called a “director”.
Since
\bm{n}_{\rm d}
and
-\bm{n}_{\rm d}
are indistinguishable, an arbitrary angle
\phi
is equivalent to
\hat{\phi}\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2)

[
16
]
; that is,
the two angles
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are equivalent if there exists an integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}+n\pi
.

The governing equation for the alignment angle
\phi
is derived using the one-constant approximation of elastic energy called Frank free energy and its variational formula.
The Frank free energy with
\phi(u,v)
at position
(u,v)

is defined as the integral of a surface
\Omega
as follows:

\displaystyle F\equiv\frac{K}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi(u,v)|^{2}{\rm d}u{\rm d}v,

(2)

where
K
is a Frank elastic constant and
K/2=1
in this paper for simplicity.
The variation of the energy defined in equation (
2
) leads to
the energy taking a minimum when
the alignment angle
\phi(u,v)
satisfies the following 2D Laplace’s equation:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi(u,v)=0,\quad(u,v)\in\Omega.

(3)

Four branch cuts exist due to the logarithmic singularities in the unit disc.
(b) Angle

Note that
\theta+\chi(\theta)

A complex analysis formulation of the alignment for simply connected domains is now introduced.
First, an analytical formula for the unit disc is introduced.
Let
z=x+{\rm i}y
define a complex plane and
\mathbb{D}
define the unit disc.
There exist topological defects at
z_{k}=x_{k}+{\rm i}y_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,N
, strictly inside
\mathbb{D}
, whose topological charges are
q_{k}
.
The total number of topological charges is

and the charges are integers or half-integers except

; that is:

\displaystyle q_{k}\in\{n/2|\ n\in\mathbb{Z},\ n\neq 0\}.

(4)

We denote the set of positions of defects as
\mathcal{Z}\equiv\{z_{k}|k=1,2,\ldots,N\}
.

The alignment angle
\phi(x,y)
created by these topological defects satisfies the following conditions
[
11
,
16
]
:

1.

Alignment angle
\phi
satisfies 2D Laplace’s equation except at
\mathcal{Z}
:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi(x,y)=0,\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{D}\backslash\mathcal{Z}.

(5)

2.

The sum of topological charges
q_{k}
is equal to
1

[
16
]
; that is:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}=1.

(6)

3.

For simply connected domains
D_{k}\subset\mathbb{D}

(k=1,\ldots,N)
that contain only the
k
-th defect, the circulation around
D_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle\oint_{\partial D_{k}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial s}{\rm d}s=2\pi q_{k}.

(7)

4.

Because the directors align along the boundary of
\mathbb{D}
, alignment angle
\phi(x,y)
satisfies

\displaystyle\phi(x,y)=\nu(x,y),\quad(x,y)\in\partial\mathbb{D},

(8)

where
\nu(x,y)
is a tangential angle on
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

Miyazako and Nara derived an analytical formula that describes the alignment angle that satisfies conditions (i)-(iv) in
\mathbb{D}

[
16
]
.
The alignment angle
\phi(x,y)
for the unit disc is given by

\phi(x,y)={\rm Re}[f_{\mathbb{D}}(z|\mathcal{Z})]
, where

is an analytic function in domain
\mathbb{D}\backslash\mathcal{Z}
which is explicitly given by

\displaystyle f_{\mathbb{D}}(z|\mathcal{Z})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}\mathcal{G}(z,z_{k})-\frac{\pi}{2},

(9)

where a complex function for the cell alignment angles is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(z,z_{k})\equiv-{\rm i}(\log(z-z_{k})+\log(1-\overline{z_{k}}z)),

(10)

where
\overline{a}
represents the complex conjugate of complex number

. Topological charge
q_{k}
satisfies condition (
6
).
Figure
2
(a) shows an example of
\phi
in the presence of three topological defects with a charge of

and a defect with a charge of

.

Miyazako and Nara extended the analytical formula to simply connected arbitrarily shaped domains using conformal mappings.
Riemann’s mapping theorem states that there exists a conformal mapping
w=g(z)

from the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
in the complex

-plane to the region
\Omega
in a complex

-plane.
The positions of topological defects inside
\Omega
are denoted as

,

, and the set of these positions is

The preimage of

is denoted as

; that is,
z_{k}=g^{-1}(w_{k})\in\mathbb{D}
,

Using the map

\displaystyle\nu(\theta)={\rm Re}\left[-{\rm i}\log\left(\frac{du}{d\theta}+{\rm i}\frac{dv}{d\theta}\right)\right]={\rm Re}\left[-{\rm i}\log g^{\prime}(z)+\theta+\frac{\pi}{2}\right],\quad z=e^{{\rm i}\theta},

which gives the following complex potential of cell alignment for

17

\displaystyle f_{\Omega}(z|\mathcal{Z})=f_{\mathbb{D}}(z|\mathcal{Z})-{\rm i}\log g^{\prime}(z),\quad w=g(z).

(12)

2.2
Line integral for energy calculations

The positions of topological defects
w_{k}
tend to be located where the energy defined by equation (
2
) satisfies the local minimum
[
11
,
16
]
.
Due to the presence of topological defects at
w_{k}
, the surface integral of
|\nabla\phi|^{2}
is divergent and thus
special care must be taken.
Duclos
et al
.
[
11
]
used the Fourier expansions and
calculated the energy in the disc with radius
R
except for small regions around the defects.
They showed that the free energy of the disc is minimized when there exist two defects located in the disc at a distance
\pm 5^{-1/4}R
from the origin.
Miyazako and Nara modified the calculations while preventing the energy divergence of
|\nabla\phi|^{2}
at the defects and calculated the energy in star-shaped domains
[
16
]
.
With the use of the complex Green’s theorem, the energy (
2
) is written as the following line integral:

\displaystyle F=\int_{\Omega(\epsilon)}|\nabla\phi(u,v)|^{2}{\rm d}u{\rm d}v=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\oint_{\partial\Omega(\epsilon)}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial w}\overline{f_{\Omega}}dw,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial u}-{\rm i}\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\right),

(13)

where a small disc around the
k
-th defect with radius
\epsilon
is defined by

\displaystyle D_{k}(\epsilon)\equiv\{w_{k}+re^{{\rm i}\theta}|\ 0\leq r\leq\epsilon,0\leq\theta<2\pi\},

(14)

and
\Omega(\epsilon)\equiv\Omega\backslash\cup_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}
.
The path of the line integral for the case
N=2
is illustrated in Figure
3
.

Line integral along
\partial(\mathbb{D}\backslash D(\epsilon))

Using the line integral formula (
13
),
Miyazako and Nara confirmed that for the unit disc,
the energy is minimized when defects exist at positions
z=\pm 5^{-1/4}
.
They verified that when there are multiple
+1/2
defects and
-1/2
defects within the unit disc,
the pair of positive and negative defects annihilate through energy minimization.
They also showed that this annihilation happens on symmetrical domains that contain multiple convex regions such as triplets.

Although the line integral (
13
) is evaluated in a simple manner,
the logarithmic branch cuts of
f_{\Omega}
exist between
\partial D_{k}(\epsilon)
and
\partial\Omega
.
When there exist multiple defects,
special care must be taken when selecting the paths to prevent these branch cuts from crossing.

3
Analytical formulas for the Frank free energy

This section proposes an analytical formula for the energy defined by (
2
) in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
and in arbitrary domain
\Omega
.
This can be done by splitting equation (
9
) into
a singular part that includes a logarithmic pole at
z=z_{k}
and
a regular part around
z=z_{k}
.
The concept of this function is identical to that of the Kirchhoff-Routh functions in the field of vortex dynamics
[
19
,
21
]
.
This enables the explicit evaluation of the line integral (
13
),
which gives an analytical formula for the energy up to the order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\log\epsilon)
.

3.1
Analytical formula for unit disc

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that there exist
N
topological defects with an integer or half-integer charge located at
z_{k}\in\mathbb{D}
,
k=1,\ldots,N
,
z_{k}\notin\partial\mathbb{D}
.
The sum of the topological charges satisfies (
6
).
Then the total energy
F
of the region
\mathbb{D}\backslash D(\epsilon)
,
D(\epsilon)\equiv\cup_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}(\epsilon)
is written as follows:

\displaystyle F=-2\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}^{2}\log\epsilon+2\pi F_{d}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\log\epsilon),

(15)

where the first term is independent of
z_{k}
and

F_{d}
only depends on the position of the defects
z_{k}
, and is given by

\displaystyle F_{d}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}^{2}{\rm Im}[\mathcal{R}(z_{k})]+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l\neq k}^{N}q_{k}q_{l}{\rm Im}[\mathcal{G}(z_{k},z_{l})],

(16)

where
\mathcal{G}(.,.)
is given in equation (
10
) and

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(z_{k})=\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{k}}\left(\mathcal{G}(z,z_{k})+{\rm i}\log(z-z_{k})\right)=-{\rm i}\log(1-z_{k}\overline{z_{k}})-\frac{\pi}{2}.

(17)

The function
\mathcal{G}(z)+{\rm i}\log(z-z_{k})
is a function analytic at
z=z_{k}
, which is obtained by
removing the contribution of the logarithmic pole at
z=z_{k}
.

Remark 1:

The first term in (
15
) is independent of locations
z_{k}
, which means that
z_{k}
only contributes the second term in (
15
).
The minimum of the energy is obtained by minimizing
F_{d}
.

Remark 2:

When we assume that two topological defects with a charge of
q_{1}=q_{2}=+1/2
are located at
z=\pm r
,
0<r<1
,
the energy
F_{d}
for this case is given by

\displaystyle F_{d}=-\frac{1}{2}[\log(1-r^{2})+\log(1+r^{2})+\log(2r)].

(18)

Taking derivative with respect to

gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{d}}{\partial r}=\frac{5r^{4}-1}{2r(1-r^{4})}.

(19)

Solving the equation
\partial F_{d}/\partial r=0
, we have

, which is consistent with the results given by Duclos
et al.

[
11
]
.

Remark 3:

Our results show that the concept of this expression is identical to that of Hamiltonians for multiple vortices.
In classical fluid dynamics, Hamiltonians are referred to as Kirchhoff-Routh path functions, which give the vortex trajectories (see p. 121 in
[
22
]
).
The theory of Kirchhoff-Routh path functions was extended to multiply connected domains by Lin
[
19
]

and a mathematical formulation for multiply connected domains with prime functions was given in a pioneering work by Crowdy
[

]
.
The concept presented here has been used to derive a variational formulation for vortex dynamics
based on the principle of least action and asymptotic formulas for the kinematic energy have been obtained
[
24
]
.
A Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
in the presence of multiple point vortices with circulation
\Gamma_{k}
,

is given by
[
22
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Gamma_{k}^{2}R(z_{k})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{k\neq l}^{N}\Gamma_{k}\Gamma_{l}G(z_{k},z_{l}),

(20)

where
G(.,.)
is a hydrodynamic Green’s function for a point vortex and
R(.)
is a Robin function
[
25
]
for the Green’s function
G(.,.)
and is defined by

\displaystyle R(z_{k})=\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{k}}\left(G(z,z_{k})-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|z-z_{k}|\right).

(21)

Note that the Green’s function for a point vortex in the unit disc is given by

\displaystyle G(z,z_{k})\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\left|\frac{z-z_{k}}{|z_{k}|(z-1/\overline{z_{k}})}\right|.

(22)

The imaginary part of

is similar to

except for the opposite sign of the second term in (

Our result indicates that the classical approach used in vortex dynamics can be extended to the theory of nematic liquid crystals.

\partial D_{k}(\epsilon)

The arguments of the function
\log(z-z_{k})
at
z=b_{k}^{\pm}
are set to
{\rm arg}(b_{k}^{\pm}-z_{k})=\beta_{k}^{\pm}
.
Using the change of variables along
\partial D_{k}(\epsilon)
such that
z=z_{k}+\epsilon e^{{\rm i}\theta}
,
\theta\in[\beta^{-}_{k},\beta_{k}^{+}]
and equation (
23
), the line integral of
\partial D_{k}(\epsilon)
is evaluated as follows:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\oint_{\partial D_{k}(\epsilon)}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{\beta_{k}^{-}}^{\beta_{k}^{+}}\left[-\frac{{\rm i}q_{k}}{\epsilon e^{{\rm i}\theta}}+\frac{\partial r_{k}}{\partial z}(z_{k}+\epsilon e^{{\rm i}\theta})\right]\left[{\rm i}q_{k}\log(\epsilon e^{-{\rm i}\theta})+\overline{r_{k}(z_{k}+\epsilon e^{{\rm i}\theta})}\right]{\rm i}\epsilon e^{{\rm i}\theta}{\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\beta_{k}^{-}}^{\beta_{k}^{+}}\left[-{\rm i}q_{k}\overline{r_{k}(z_{k})}+q_{k}^{2}\log\epsilon-{\rm i}q_{k}^{2}\theta\right]{\rm d}\theta+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\log\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\pi q_{k}^{2}\log\epsilon+{\rm i}\pi q_{k}\overline{r_{k}(z_{k})}+{\rm i}\pi^{2}q_{k}^{2}+{\rm i}\pi q_{k}^{2}\beta_{k}^{-}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\log\epsilon).

(29)

where we used that
r_{k}(z_{k}+\epsilon e^{{\rm i}\theta})=r_{k}(z_{k})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
and
\beta_{k}^{+}-\beta_{k}^{-}=2\pi
from the second to third line.

(ii) Integrals along
l_{k}^{+}
and
l_{k}^{-}

The line integrals along
l_{k}^{\pm}
are given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{l_{k}^{\pm}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\rm d}z=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{a_{k}^{\pm}}^{b_{k}^{\pm}}\left(-\frac{{\rm i}q_{k}}{z-z_{k}}+\frac{\partial r_{k}}{\partial z}\right)\left({\rm i}q_{k}\log\overline{(z^{\pm}-z_{k})}+\overline{r_{k}(z)}\right){\rm d}z,

(30)

where
z^{\pm}
are points on
l_{k}^{\pm}
.
The line integral along
l_{k}^{+}
minus the integral along
l_{k}^{-}
becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{l_{k}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{l_{k}^{-}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\rm d}z=2\pi q_{k}\cdot\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{b_{k}^{-}}\left[-\frac{{\rm i}q_{k}}{z-z_{k}}+\frac{\partial r_{k}}{\partial z}\right]{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}\pi q_{k}\left[-{\rm i}q_{k}(\log(b_{k}^{-}-z_{k})-\log(a_{k}^{-}-z_{k}))+r_{k}(b_{k}^{-})-r_{k}(a_{k}^{-})\right].

(31)

Due to the fact that
\log(b_{k}^{-}-z_{k})=\log\epsilon+{\rm i}\beta_{k}^{-}
and
r_{k}(b_{k}^{-})=r_{k}(z_{k})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, equation (
31
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{l_{k}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{l_{k}^{-}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\rm d}z=-\pi q_{k}^{2}\log\epsilon-{\rm i}\pi q_{k}^{2}\beta_{k}^{-}-{\rm i}\pi q_{k}r_{k}(z_{k})+{\rm i}\pi q_{k}f_{\mathbb{D}}(a_{k}^{-}|\mathcal{Z})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(32)

(iii) Line integral along the unit circle

First we note that
\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}/\partial z
is analytic everywhere in
\mathbb{D}\backslash\mathcal{Z}
.
Using equation (
26
), the line integral of the unit circle becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\rm d}z=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}(2\theta+2\chi(\theta)-\pi-f_{\mathbb{D}}){\rm d}z.

(33)

Let the argument of
a_{k}^{\pm}
define
\alpha_{k}^{\pm}
.
Note that
\chi(\alpha_{k}^{+})-\chi(\alpha_{k}^{-})=-2\pi q_{k}
.
The first two terms in (
33
) are

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}(\theta+\chi(\theta))\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}{\rm d}z=-{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{N}[(\theta+\chi(\theta))f_{\mathbb{D}}(e^{{\rm i}\theta}|\mathcal{Z})]_{\theta=\alpha_{k}^{-}}^{\theta=\alpha_{k+1}^{+}}+{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{\alpha_{k}^{-}}^{\alpha_{k+1}^{+}}f_{\mathbb{D}}(e^{{\rm i}\theta}|\mathcal{Z}){\rm d}\theta

(34)

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(2\pi q_{k}f_{\mathbb{D}}(a_{k}^{-}|\mathcal{Z})+4\pi^{2}q_{k}^{2}+2\pi q_{k}(\chi(\alpha_{k}^{-})+\alpha_{k}^{+}))+{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(2\pi^{2}q_{k}^{2}+2\pi q_{k}(\chi(\alpha_{k}^{-})+\alpha_{k}^{+}))-{\rm i}\pi^{2}

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(2\pi q_{k}f_{\mathbb{D}}(a_{k}^{-}|\mathcal{Z})+2\pi^{2}q_{k}^{2})-{\rm i}\pi^{2},

(35)

where the second term in (
34
) is calculated as

\displaystyle{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{\alpha_{k}^{-}}^{\alpha_{k+1}^{+}}f_{\mathbb{D}}(e^{{\rm i}\theta}|\mathcal{Z}){\rm d}\theta

\displaystyle={\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{\alpha_{k}^{-}}^{\alpha_{k+1}^{+}}{\rm Re}[f_{\mathbb{D}}(e^{{\rm i}\theta}|\mathcal{Z})]{\rm d}\theta=-{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{\alpha_{k}^{-}}^{\alpha_{k+1}^{+}}\left(\theta+\chi(\theta)-\frac{\pi}{2}\right){\rm d}\theta.

(36)

For the first equality in (
36
), we used the fact that when
|t|<1
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|e^{{\rm i}\theta}-t|d\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|1-\overline{t}e^{{\rm i}\theta}|d\theta=0,

(37)

which can be proven by the residue theorem for
|t|<1
:

\displaystyle\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{\log(1-tz)}{z}{\rm d}z=0.

(38)

The third term in (
33
) is

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}{\rm d}z=-\frac{\pi}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[f_{\mathbb{D}}(z|\mathcal{Z})\right]_{z=a_{k}^{-}}^{z=a_{k+1}^{+}}={\rm i}\pi^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}={\rm i}\pi^{2}.

(39)

where we used the fact that
f_{\mathbb{D}}
shifts
2\pi q_{k}
at
z=a_{k}^{\pm}
.
The fourth term in (
33
) is

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}f_{\mathbb{D}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[f^{2}_{\mathbb{D}}(z|\mathcal{Z})\right]_{z=a_{k}^{-}}^{z=a_{k+1}^{+}}=-\frac{1}{4{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}2\pi q_{k}(2f_{\mathbb{D}}(a_{k}^{-}|\mathcal{Z})+2\pi q_{k}).

Hence, the line integral around
\partial\mathbb{D}
in (
33
) is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}(q_{k}f_{\mathbb{D}}(a_{k}^{-}|\mathcal{Z})+\pi q_{k}^{2}).

(40)

Combining (
29
), (
32
), and (
40
), we have

\displaystyle F=-2\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}^{2}\log\epsilon+2\pi F_{d}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\log\epsilon),\quad F_{d}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}{\rm Im}[r_{k}(z_{k})].

(41)

Using the formula (
24
), the final expression (
15
) is derived.
∎

3.2
Analytical formula for arbitrarily shaped domain

The theory of energy calculation on the unit disc shown in the previous section is utilized to derive an explicit formula for energy in arbitrarily shaped regions.

Theorem 3.2
.

We consider an arbitrarily shaped simply connected domain
\Omega
in the
w
-plane mapped from the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
by a conformal mapping represented as
w=g(z)
,
where the boundary of the domain is sufficiently smooth and
g^{\prime}(z)\neq 0
for
z\in\mathbb{D}
.
Assume that there exist
N
topological defects with an integer or half-integer charge located at
w_{k}\in\Omega
,
k=1,\ldots,N
,
w_{k}\notin\partial\Omega
, and
define the set of topological defects as
\mathcal{W}\equiv\{w_{k}|k=1,\ldots,N\}
.
We also define the set of topological defects
\mathcal{Z}\equiv\{z_{k}|k=1,\ldots,N\}
,
z_{k}=g^{-1}(w_{k})
, mapped from
\mathcal{W}
and
the small disc with radius
\epsilon
which encloses only
w_{k}
as
D_{k}(\epsilon)\equiv\{w_{k}+re^{{\rm i}\theta}|\ 0\leq r\leq\epsilon,0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\}
.
The sum of topological charges satisfies (
6
).
The total energy
F
of the region
\Omega\backslash D(\epsilon)
,
D(\epsilon)\equiv\cup_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}(\epsilon)
, is written as follows:

\displaystyle F=-2\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}^{2}\log\epsilon+2\pi F_{d}+F_{g}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\log\epsilon),

(42)

where

\displaystyle F_{d}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(q_{k}^{2}-2q_{k}\right)\log|g^{\prime}(z_{k})|+\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}^{2}{\rm Im}[\mathcal{R}(z_{k})]+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l\neq k}^{N}q_{k}q_{l}{\rm Im}[\mathcal{G}(z_{k},z_{l})],

(43)

\displaystyle F_{g}\equiv\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left|\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(z)}{g^{\prime}(z)}\right|^{2}{\rm d}S+4\pi\log|g^{\prime}(0)|.

(44)

The energy
F_{d}
depends on
z_{k}
and the map
g(z)
, whereas
F_{g}
is independent of
z_{k}
. The energy
F_{g}
can be seen as the total energy of the map
g(z)
in
\mathbb{D}
.

Figure 4:
Line integral along
\partial(\Omega\backslash D(\epsilon))
for case
N=2
. Branch cuts of logarithmic singularities
z_{k}
are chosen as
l_{k}
and argument of
\log(z-z_{k})
jumps on
l_{k}
by
2\pi
.
The path of integrals in the
w
-plane is transformed into the line integral in the
z
-plane under a conformal map
w=g(z)
.

Proof.

Since the energy defined by equation (
2
) is conformally invariant,
the Frank free energy in the domain
\Omega
is expressed by line integrals:

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\oint_{\partial(\Omega\backslash D(\epsilon))}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial w}(g^{-1}(w)|\mathcal{Z})\overline{f_{\Omega}(g^{-1}(w)|\mathcal{Z})}{\rm d}w=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\oint_{\partial(\mathbb{D}\backslash\tilde{D}(\epsilon))}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial z}(z|\mathcal{Z})\overline{f_{\Omega}(z|\mathcal{Z})}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\Omega}}{\rm d}z+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\int_{l_{k}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\Omega}}{\rm d}z-\int_{l_{k}^{-}}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\Omega}}{\rm d}z-\oint_{\partial\tilde{D}_{k}(\epsilon)}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\Omega}}{\rm d}z\right)\right],

(45)

where the points
a_{k}^{\pm}
,
b_{k}^{\pm}
, and the paths
l_{k}^{\pm}
are chosen in the same manner as that in the unit disc case
and
\tilde{D}_{k}(\epsilon)\equiv g^{-1}(D_{k}(\epsilon))
.
Figure
4
shows the paths of the line integrals.
The point on
\partial D_{k}(\epsilon)
is parameterized as
w=w_{k}+\epsilon e^{{\rm i}\theta}
, and
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, which is transformed into

\displaystyle g^{-1}(w_{k}+\epsilon e^{{\rm i}\theta})=z_{k}+\frac{1}{g^{\prime}(z_{k})}\epsilon e^{{\rm i}\theta}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})

(46)

under the inverse of the conformal map of
g(z)
.
This implies that the disc at
w=w_{k}=g(z_{k})
with radius
\epsilon
is mapped to the disc at
z=z_{k}
with radius
\tilde{\epsilon}\equiv\epsilon/|g^{\prime}(z_{k})|
up to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
.

Now we use the same technique as that used for the unit disc.
Before proving this, it is convenient to define
h(z)\equiv-{\rm i}\log g^{\prime}(z)
and a function that excludes only the singularity at
z=z_{k}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{k}(z)\equiv f_{\Omega}(z|\mathcal{Z})+{\rm i}\log(z-z_{k})=r_{k}(z)+h(z).

(47)

(i) Line integral along
\partial\tilde{D}_{k}(\epsilon)

The integral along
\partial\tilde{D}_{k}(\epsilon)
follows the calculations for the unit disc except for two simple modifications, namely,
the inner radius
\epsilon
on the
w
-plane is
\tilde{\epsilon}
on the
z
-plane and the function
r_{k}(z)
analytic at
z=z_{k}
is
\tilde{r}_{k}(z)
in this case.
Following equation (
29
), the line integral around
\tilde{D}_{k}(\epsilon)
is

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\oint_{\partial\tilde{D}_{k}(\epsilon)}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\Omega}}dz=-\pi q_{k}^{2}\log\tilde{\epsilon}+{\rm i}\pi q_{k}\overline{\tilde{r}_{k}(z_{k})}+{\rm i}\pi^{2}q_{k}^{2}+{\rm i}\pi q_{k}^{2}\beta_{k}^{-}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\log\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\pi q_{k}^{2}\log\epsilon+\pi q_{k}^{2}\log|g^{\prime}(z_{k})|+{\rm i}\pi q_{k}\overline{r_{k}(z_{k})}-\pi q_{k}\log\overline{g^{\prime}(z_{k})}+{\rm i}\pi^{2}q_{k}^{2}+{\rm i}\pi q_{k}^{2}\beta_{k}^{-}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\log\epsilon).

(ii) Integrals along branch cuts
l_{k}^{+}
and
l_{k}^{-}

This integral is calculated using the same technique as that used for the unit disc:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{l_{k}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\Omega}}{\rm d}z-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{l_{k}^{-}}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\Omega}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=-\pi q_{k}^{2}\log\epsilon+\pi q_{k}^{2}\log|g^{\prime}(z_{k})|-{\rm i}\pi q_{k}^{2}\beta_{k}^{-}-{\rm i}\pi q_{k}r_{k}(z_{k})-\pi q_{k}\log g^{\prime}(z_{k})+{\rm i}\pi q_{k}f_{\Omega}(a_{k}^{-}|\mathcal{Z})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(iii) Line integral along the unit circle

The line integral along the unit circle is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\Omega}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial h}{\partial z}\right)\overline{(f_{\mathbb{D}}+h)}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}+\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{h}+\frac{\partial h}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}+\frac{\partial h}{\partial z}\overline{h}\right){\rm d}z.

(48)

Using the same argument as that for the unit disc, the first term in (
48
) is identical to (
40
); that is,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\rm d}z=-{\rm i}\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}(q_{k}f_{\mathbb{D}}(a_{k}^{-}|\mathcal{Z})+\pi q_{k}^{2}).

(49)

The last term in (
48
) is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial h}{\partial z}\overline{h}{\rm d}z=\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left|\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime}}\right|^{2}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y,

(50)

where the complex Green’s theorem and the fact that
h(z)
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}
are used.
The second term in (
48
) is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}\overline{h}{\rm d}z=-\frac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}\left(\frac{1}{z-z_{k}}-\frac{\overline{z_{k}}}{1-\overline{z_{k}}z}\right)\overline{h}{\rm d}z={\rm i}\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}\overline{h(z_{k})}-2{\rm i}\pi\overline{h(0)},

(51)

where we used
\overline{z}=1/z
,
{\rm d}\overline{z}=-1/z^{2}{\rm d}z
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
, and the following residue theorem:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\overline{h(z)}}{z-z_{k}}{\rm d}z=\frac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{\overline{h(z)}}{z-z_{k}}{\rm d}z=\overline{\frac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{h(z)}{1/z-\overline{z_{k}}}\left(-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right){\rm d}z}={\rm i}\pi\overline{h(0)},

(52)

\displaystyle-\frac{\overline{z_{k}}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\overline{h(z)}}{1-\overline{z_{k}}z}{\rm d}z=-\frac{\overline{z_{k}}}{2}\overline{\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{h(z)}{1-z_{k}/z}\left(-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right){\rm d}z}=-{\rm i}\pi\overline{h(z_{k})}+{\rm i}\pi\overline{h(0)}.

(53)

The third term is calculated based on the property that
\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}=2\theta+2\chi(\theta)-\pi-f_{\mathbb{D}}
on the unit circle:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial h}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\rm d}z=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial h}{\partial z}(2\theta+2\chi(\theta)-\pi-f_{\mathbb{D}}){\rm d}z.

(54)

The first two terms in (
54
) are explicitly calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial h}{\partial z}(\theta+\chi(\theta)){\rm d}z=-2{\rm i}\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}h(a_{k}^{-})+{\rm i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(e^{{\rm i}\theta}){\rm d}\theta=-2{\rm i}\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}h(a_{k}^{-})+2{\rm i}\pi h(0).

The third term in (
54
) is zero because
h(z)
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}
. The fourth term in (
54
) is

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial h}{\partial z}f_{\mathbb{D}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}[h(z)f_{\mathbb{D}}]_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}+\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\mathbb{D}}}{\partial z}h{\rm d}z

\displaystyle={\rm i}\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}h(a_{k}^{-})-{\rm i}\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}h(z_{k}),

(55)

where we used the residue theorem. Hence, the line integral along the unit circle is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{a_{k}^{-}}^{a_{k+1}^{+}}\frac{\partial f_{\Omega}}{\partial z}\overline{f_{\Omega}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=-{\rm i}\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}h(a_{k}^{-})-2{\rm i}\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}{\rm Im}[h(z_{k})]+4\pi\log|g^{\prime}(0)|

\displaystyle-{\rm i}\pi\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}f_{\mathbb{D}}(a_{k}^{-}|\mathcal{Z})-{\rm i}\pi^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}q_{k}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left|\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime}}\right|^{2}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

(56)

Combining (i), (ii), and (iii), the energy for
f_{\Omega}(z|\mathcal{Z})
given by (
42
) is derived.

∎

4
Topological defects localization on quadrature domains

Section
3
presented the explicit formula for the Frank free energy of arbitrarily shaped domains.
The complex potential
f_{\Omega}
was split into analytic and logarithmic parts and the line integrals along boundaries were evaluated up to
\epsilon\log\epsilon
.
The formulas derived here are explicit, written as logarithmic singularities plus the contribution from conformal maps.
This section applies the formulas (
42
) to validate a conjecture given by Ienaga
et al.

[
10
]
, namely,
that the positions of defects in both doublets and triplets linearly correlate with the distances between the centres of the overlapping discs.

The simplest extension of localizing topological defects with boundaries, as done by Duclos
et al.

[
11
]
,
is to estimate defect positions in regions with multiple discs that intersect each other.
When two or three discs with the same radius overlap at a distance
\Delta
, they are called “doublets” and “triplets”, respectively.
These geometries exhibit ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic order in bacterial vortex lattices,
depending on the distance between the centres of the discs relative to the radius of the discs
[
26
]
.
Ienaga
et al
. created regions with two or three circular areas and
conducted cell alignment experiments within these regions to investigate the number and positioning of defects
[
10
]
.
They hypothesized a proportional relationship between the distance
\Delta
between the centres of two overlapping discs and
the distance
D_{++}
between two
+1/2
defects within those doublets. They conjectured the following equation for doublets through fitting:

\displaystyle D_{++}=2\left(D_{++,0}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\right),\quad D_{++,0}=0.58.

(57)

For triplets, it was experimentally confirmed that
when
\Delta
is large, a topological defect with a charge of
-1/2
exists at the centre, and three topological defects with a charge of
+1/2
are located on each disc.
They also hypothesized that the distance
D_{+-}
between the
-1/2
defect and the
+1/2
defect,
and the distance between disc centres
\Delta
, have a proportional relationship with a slope of
1/\sqrt{3}
.

In this paper,
we analytically verify these hypotheses using the proposed formula and the mapping of quadrature domains
[
20
]
.
The conformal mapping for a region with
M
-overlapping unit discs can be approximated by the following rational function:

\displaystyle w=g(z)=\frac{a^{2M}-1}{a^{M}-a^{M-1}+1}\cdot\frac{z}{a^{M}-z^{M}},

(58)

where the real parameter
a
should be greater than
1
and
M
is a positive integer.
This region is known as the quadrature domains.
Such domains have been extensively studied in fields such as Hele-Shaw flows
[
18
,
20
]
.

and

w=g(\omega_{M}^{m}/a)
,
\omega_{M}\equiv\exp(2\pi{\rm i}/M)
,

, which are considered as the centres of the discs.

a=1.05
,

, (ii)

,

, and (iii)

,

.

M=3
and two stationary topological defects.
Two topological defects with a charge of
+1/2
exist inside the unit disc.
Because of the potential

, topological defects with a charge of
+1/2
are also located at

,

For

,

has three simple zeros and three double poles outside the unit disc, which indicates that the correction term
h(z)=-{\rm i}\log g^{\prime}(z)
produces topological defects with a charge of
+1
and
-2
.
The problem of finding the stationary positions of defects is equivalent to finding an equilibrium of ten topological defects in the infinite plane.

Quadrature domains are applied to calculate topological defects for the first time in the field of active nematics.
The advantage of using the rational function (
58
) is
that it enables the boundary of doublets or triplets to be represented by changing a single parameter
a
.
Figure

shows the results of mappings for various values of
M
and

.
Through this mapping, the origin of the unit disc in the
z
-plane is mapped to the origin of the
w
-plane, corresponding to the centre of the region in the
w
-plane.
The first and second derivatives of this function are given as follows:

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(z)=\frac{a^{2M}-1}{a^{M}-a^{M-1}+1}\cdot\frac{(a^{M}+(M-1)z^{M})}{(a^{M}-z^{M})^{2}},

(59)

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{a^{2M}-1}{a^{M}-a^{M-1}+1}\cdot\frac{Mz^{M-1}[(M-1)z^{M}+(M+1)a^{M}]}{(a^{M}-z^{M})^{3}}.

(60)

The mapping

may exhibit self-intersections for certain value of

The condition for no self-intersections is that the turning number of
g(z)
around the unit disc is
1
, that is,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\theta}{\rm Im}\left[\log g^{\prime}(e^{{\rm i}\theta})\right]{\rm d}\theta=1,

(61)

which results in no zeros of
g^{\prime}(z)
being in the unit disc, i.e.,
a>(M-1)^{1/M}
.

The centres of the discs are well approximated by
g(\omega^{m}_{M}/a)
,
m=0,1,\ldots,M-1
,
\omega_{M}=\exp(2\pi{\rm i}/M)
.
The coefficients of the mapping in (

equals

The advantage of the map is that

is rational, allowing the Frank free energy in the presence of topological defects to be expressed as logarithm of certain rational functions.
This enables the calculation of stationary defect locations and stability using standard calculus.
We note that

\displaystyle-{\rm i}\log g^{\prime}(z)=-{\rm i}\log(M-1)-{\rm i}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\log(z+a\omega_{M}^{m}/(M-1)^{1/M})+2{\rm i}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\log(z-a\omega_{M}^{m}),

(62)

where

This indicates that the problem of finding two stationary defects considered here is
to find the stability of four topological defects with a charge of
+1/2

m=0,\ldots,M-1

,
m=0,\ldots,M-1
in the infinite plane.

4.1
M=2

We first investigate the defect positions and
their alignment angles in doublet regions.
The condition for no self-intersections is
a>1

\displaystyle g(z)=\frac{a^{4}-1}{a^{2}-a+1}\cdot\frac{z}{a^{2}-z^{2}}.

(63)

Since the centres of the discs of doublet regions are represented as
g(\pm 1/a)
, the distance between the centres of the two discs is given by

\displaystyle\Delta=2g(1/a)=\frac{2a}{a^{2}-a+1}.

(64)

Using the symmetry in the region where the two discs intersect,
we assume that there exist topological defects with charges
q_{1}=q_{2}=+1/2
at two symmetric points
\pm g(s)
along the

0<s<1

In other words, the positions of topological defects in the predomain are

Using equation (

\displaystyle F_{d}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log[2s(1-s^{4})]-\frac{3}{4}\log|g^{\prime}(s)|-\frac{3}{4}\log|g^{\prime}(-s)|.

(65)

To find the extrema of the free energy, consider the derivative with respect to
s
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{d}}{\partial s}=-\frac{3g^{\prime\prime}(s)}{4g^{\prime}(s)}+\frac{3g^{\prime\prime}(-s)}{4g^{\prime}(-s)}+\frac{5s^{4}-1}{2s(1-s^{4})}=\frac{3s(s^{2}+3a^{2})}{s^{4}-a^{4}}+\frac{5s^{4}-1}{2s(1-s^{4})}=0.

(66)

This gives an
8

\displaystyle p_{2}(t)=t^{4}+18a^{2}t^{3}+5(a^{4}-1)t^{2}-18a^{2}t-a^{4}=0,\quad t=s^{2}.

(67)

Using the fact that
p_{2}(0)=-a^{4}<0
and
p_{2}(1)=4(a^{4}-1)>0
,

has at least one real solution in the interval
0<t<1

\displaystyle p_{2}^{\prime}(t)=4t^{3}+54a^{2}t^{2}+10(a^{4}-1)t-18a^{2},

(68)

\displaystyle p_{2}^{\prime\prime}(t)=12t^{2}+108a^{2}t+10(a^{4}-1)>0,\quad 0<t<1,

(69)

which indicates that
p_{2}(t)
has only one real solution in the interval
0<t<1
.
We denote this solution as
t_{0}
and define
s_{0}^{2}=t_{0}
,
s_{0}>0
.
We solve the polynomial equation (
67

.
We refer to this method as the polynomial method.

For

\displaystyle g(z)=\frac{a^{6}-1}{a^{3}-a^{2}+1}\cdot\frac{z}{a^{3}-z^{3}},\quad g^{\prime}(z)=\frac{a^{6}-1}{a^{3}-a^{2}+1}\cdot\frac{a^{3}+2z^{3}}{(a^{3}-z^{3})^{2}},

(73)

where
a>2^{1/3}
due to the condition for no self-intersections.
Figure

Ienaga
et al

They observed that when the distances between the discs are sufficiently large, a defect with a charge of

To investigate whether the resulting defects are stable, the Hessian of the energy at these positions is examined and their stability is evaluated based on whether the Hessian is positive definite. For the calculation of the Hessian, the following quantities are precomputed:

\displaystyle\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime}}=\frac{6z^{2}}{a^{3}+2z^{3}}+\frac{6z^{2}}{a^{3}-z^{3}},\quad\left(\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime}}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{12z(a^{3}-z^{3})}{(a^{3}+2z^{3})^{2}}+\frac{6z(2a^{3}+z^{3})}{(a^{3}-z^{3})^{2}}.

The distance between discs is given by

\displaystyle\Delta=\sqrt{3}g(1/a)=\frac{\sqrt{3}a^{2}}{a^{3}-a^{2}+1}.

(75)

Two defects (i): two defects located on real axis of predomain

Assume that there exist two defects on the centre line of the quadrature domain.
The coordinates of these defects in the predomain are defined by
z_{1}=r_{1},\ z_{2}=r_{2}
,
-1<r_{1}<r_{2}<1

F_{d}
is explicitly given by

\displaystyle F_{d}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\left[\log(1-r_{1}^{2})+\log(1-r_{2}^{2})+2\log(r_{2}-r_{1})+2\log(1-r_{1}r_{2})\right]-\frac{3}{4}[\log g^{\prime}(r_{1})+\log g^{\prime}(r_{2})].

The derivative of

with respect to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial F_{d}}{\partial r_{1}}&=\frac{r_{1}}{2(1-r_{1}^{2})}-\frac{1}{2(r_{2}-r_{1})}+\frac{r_{2}}{2(1-r_{1}r_{2})}-\frac{3}{4}\left[\frac{6r_{1}^{2}}{a^{3}+2r_{1}^{3}}-\frac{6r_{1}^{2}}{r_{1}^{3}-a^{3}}\right]=0,\\
\frac{\partial F_{d}}{\partial r_{2}}&=\frac{r_{2}}{2(1-r_{2}^{2})}-\frac{1}{2(r_{1}-r_{2})}+\frac{r_{1}}{2(1-r_{1}r_{2})}-\frac{3}{4}\left[\frac{6r_{2}^{2}}{a^{3}+2r_{2}^{3}}-\frac{6r_{2}^{2}}{r_{2}^{3}-a^{3}}\right]=0.\end{aligned}\right.

The solution (

and

\displaystyle H=\begin{pmatrix}J&\mathcal{O}\\

\mathcal{O}&J\end{pmatrix}^{\top},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\

5
Discussion and conclusion

This paper provides analytical formulas for Frank free energy in the presence of topological defects based on analogies to vortex dynamics.
The energy representation was obtained using the approach of Hamiltonians for vortex dynamics.
The analytical formulas derived in this paper were used to obtain the positions of defects in doublet and triplet regions.
The proposed formulas are explicit and give a more accurate condition for
\Delta
when the transition of formation of topological defects occurs.

Our analytical results closely match the experimental results reported by Ienaga
et al.
, particularly in the case of doublets
[
10
]
.
However, the defect distances
D_{++}
obtained using the actual experiments conducted by Ienaga
et al.
were found to be shorter than those obtained from our analytical methods.
This suggests that the influence of cells must be considered, as we assumed cells to behave as nematic liquid crystals.

Although the present paper focuses on simply connected domains,
it is important to note that Miyazako and Sakajo derived an analytical formula for cell alignments in doubly connected domains
[
17
]
.
The derivation of the explicit formulas for energy in doubly connected domains is an ongoing work.
Using the analytical energy formulas and the theory of quadrature domains in doubly connected domains described in
[
18
]
,
it might become possible to study ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic order in bacterial vortex lattices in more complicated geometries, such as three merging circles with an inner holes.

Appendix A
Hessian

We derive the Hessian with complex coordinates
z_{k}
and
\overline{z_{k}}
.
Because the derivatives with respect to
z_{k}
and
\overline{z_{k}}
are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}-{\rm i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{k}}\right),\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}+{\rm i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{k}}\right),

(82)

we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{k}\partial x_{l}}=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{k}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{l}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z_{l}}}\right)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{l}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}\partial z_{l}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{k}\partial\overline{z_{l}}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}\partial\overline{z_{l}}},

(83)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{k}\partial y_{l}}={\rm i}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{k}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{l}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z_{l}}}\right)={\rm i}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{l}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}\partial z_{l}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{k}\partial\overline{z_{l}}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}\partial\overline{z_{l}}}\right),

(84)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y_{k}\partial y_{l}}=-\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{k}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{l}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z_{l}}}\right)=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{l}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}\partial z_{l}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{k}\partial\overline{z_{l}}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}\partial\overline{z_{l}}}.

(85)

Using this transformation, the Hessian with respect to
x_{k}
and
y_{l}
,
k,l=1,\ldots,N
, is given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{k}\partial x_{l}}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{k}\partial y_{l}}\\
\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y_{k}\partial x_{l}}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y_{k}\partial y_{l}}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{J}\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{k}\partial z_{l}}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{k}\partial\overline{z_{l}}}\\
\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}\partial z_{l}}&\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{z_{k}}\partial\overline{z_{l}}}\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{J}^{\top},\quad\mathcal{J}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
{\rm i}&-{\rm i}\end{pmatrix}.

(86)
