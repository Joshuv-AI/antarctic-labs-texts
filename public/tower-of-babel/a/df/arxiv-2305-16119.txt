1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
be a finite classical polar space of rank
r\geq 2
. A tight set is a subset
{\mathcal{T}}
of points of
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
such that for all points
P
of
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
, the intersection sizes
|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{T}}|
take exactly two values involving a parameter
i\geq 1
, according as
P\in{\mathcal{T}}
or not, where
P^{\perp}
is the set of points in
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
that are collinear with
P
. Tight sets in finite generalized quadrangles were first introduced by Payne
[
13
]
, and this notion has been generalized to finite classical polar spaces by Drudge
[
6
]
. Parallel to the notion of a tight set is the concept of an
m
-ovoid. The notions of tight sets and
m
-ovoids were unified under the umbrella of
intriguing sets
in
[
2
]
. During the past two decades, intriguing sets have been extensively investigated because of their close connections with many other combinatorial/geometric objects such as strongly regular graphs, partial difference sets, Boolean degree one functions, and Cameron-Liebler line classes, cf.
[
2
,
7
,
8
]
.

In general, most known examples of tight sets are found in finite polar spaces of low ranks. However, 1-tight sets exist in all finite classical polar space
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
: every generator
\mathcal{G}
of
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
is a 1-tight set since
|P^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{G}|=|\mathcal{G}|
if
P\in\mathcal{G}
, and
|P^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{G}|=
the size of a hyperplane of
\mathcal{G}
if
P\not\in\mathcal{G}
. In fact, Drudge
[
6
]
proved that any 1-tight set of
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
must be a generator. Tight sets with large parameters are much more complicated. One of the main problems concerning tight sets is to determine for which values of
i
there exist
i
-tight sets in
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
. A second problem is to characterize
i
-tight sets in
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
for specific parameter
i
.

As we saw before, one can deduce fairly easily that a generator of
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
is a 1-tight set. Suppose that
A
and
B
are an
i
-tight set and a
j
-tight set in
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
, respectively. If
A,B
are disjoint, then
A\cup B
is an
(i+j)
-tight set; if
A\subseteq B
, then
B\setminus A
is a
(j-i)
-tight set. Thus, the union of any
i
pairwise disjoint generators (i.e., the elements of a partial spread) of
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
forms an
i
-tight set. For this reason, the first question has a simple and complete answer if the classical polar space
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
admits a spread of generators. Thus, we will only consider the problem of constructing tight sets for polar spaces which do not admit a spread; and for such a polar space, it is interesting to construct tight sets with large parameters
i
, where
i
is greater than the maximum size of a partial spread. Some results on the existence of spreads of classical polar spaces can be found in
[
10
, Table 7.4]
.

In this paper, we construct a new family of
(q^{4}+1)
-tight sets in
Q(24,q)
or
Q^{-}(25,q)
according as
q=3^{f}
or
q\equiv 2\pmod{3}
. Our main theorem in this paper is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
q=p^{f}
, where
p
is prime and
f\geq 1
. If
q\equiv 2\pmod{3}
, then the classical polar space
Q^{-}(25,q)
admits a
(q^{4}+1)
-tight set with an automorphism group isomorphic to the exceptional group
F_{4}(q)
; if
q=3^{f}
, then the classical polar space
Q(24,q)
admits a
(q^{4}+1)
-tight set with an automorphism group isomorphic to the exceptional group
F_{4}(q)
.

The novelty of the construction is the use of the action of the exceptional simple group
F_{4}(q)
on its minimal module over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. We remark that the characteristic
3
case of Theorem
1.1
is essentially due to Cohen and Cooperstein. They showed in
[
5
, Table 2]
that the group
F_{4}(q)
has two orbits on the singular points of the polar space
Q(24,q)
associated with its
25
-dimensional minimal module, and from this fact we deduce that each orbit is a tight set.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce some preliminary results on intriguing sets, octonions and the minimal module of
F_{4}(q)
. In particular, we give a more effective way to decide whether a subset
{\mathcal{M}}
of points of a finite classical polar space
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
is an intriguing set, see Lemma
2.1
. In Section 3, we present the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

To prove that a candidate subset
{\mathcal{M}}
of points of

is an intriguing set, one needs to show that
{\mathcal{M}}
is a two-intersection set with respect to the perp of singular points, cf.
[

. However, there is one shortcut method which seems to have gone unnoticed (see Leema 2.1 below). We observe that
\theta_{r}-1=q(\theta_{r-1}-1)
by Table

.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a subset of size
i\frac{q^{r}-1}{q-1}
or
m\theta_{r}
in
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
for some
i
or
m
, and let
h_{1},h_{2}
be the corresponding parameters determined by
|{\mathcal{M}}|
(see above). Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

{\mathcal{M}}
is an intriguing set in
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
;

(2)

|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|=h_{1}
for all
P\in{\mathcal{M}}
;

(3)

|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|=h_{2}
for all
P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}\setminus{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proof.

We will prove the equivalence of (1) and (2). It is clear that (1) implies (2). So we only need to prove that (2) implies (1). We will use
{\mathcal{P}}_{j}
to denote the polar space of rank
j
and of the same type as
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
. We compute
\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}}|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|
and
\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}}|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|^{2}
, and will show that
\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}\setminus{\mathcal{M}}}\left(|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|-h_{2}\right)^{2}=0
. First we have

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}}|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|

\displaystyle=|\{(P,z):P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r},z\in{\mathcal{M}},P\sim z\}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in{\mathcal{M}}}|z^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{r}|=q|{\mathcal{M}}|\cdot|{\mathcal{P}}_{r-1}|+|{\mathcal{M}}|.

For the last equality, we observe that for a point
z\in{\mathcal{M}}
the intersection
z^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
is a cone with vertex
z
and base
{\mathcal{P}}_{r-1}
.
Furthermore we have

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}}|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}}|\{(P,z_{1},z_{2}):\,z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathcal{M}},\,z_{1}\sim P,\,z_{2}\sim P\}|

(2.1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in{\mathcal{M}}}|z^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{r}|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathcal{M}},\\
z_{1}\neq z_{2}\end{subarray}}|\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{r}|.

For the last summation in (
2.1
), each summand takes value either
q^{2}\cdot|{\mathcal{P}}_{r-2}|+q+1
or
|{\mathcal{P}}_{r-1}|
depending on whether
z_{1},z_{2}
are perpendicular or not:
\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
is a cone with vertex
\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle
and base
{\mathcal{P}}_{r-2}
if
\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle
is totally singular or isotropic, and it is
{\mathcal{P}}_{r-1}
otherwise. Next we compute directly

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}}\left(|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|-h_{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}}\Big(|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|^{2}-2h_{2}|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|+h_{2}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=|{\mathcal{M}}|\cdot\Big(|{\mathcal{P}}_{r-1}|(|{\mathcal{M}}|+q-h_{1}-2h_{2}q)+(qh_{1}-q+h_{1}-2h_{2})

(2.2)

\displaystyle\ +q^{2}(h_{1}-1)|{\mathcal{P}}_{r-2}|+\frac{h_{2}^{2}|{\mathcal{P}}_{r}|}{|{\mathcal{M}}|}\Big).

First consider the case where
{\mathcal{M}}
is an
i
-tight set, so that
|{\mathcal{M}}|=i\frac{q^{r}-1}{q-1},h_{1}=i\frac{q^{r-1}-1}{q-1}+q^{r-1},h_{2}=i\frac{q^{r-1}-1}{q-1}
. Observe that the right hand side of
(
2.2
)
divided by
|{\mathcal{M}}|
can be viewed as a degree-one polynomial in variable
i
. Then some tedious but routine computations show that the coefficient of
i
equals
0
. Hence we are only concerned with the constant term of the aforementioned polynomial, which equals

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{M}}|\big(|{\mathcal{P}}_{r-1}|(q-q^{r-1})+(q+1)q^{r-1}-q+q^{2}(q^{r-1}-1)|{\mathcal{P}}_{r-2}|\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q^{2r-2}=|{\mathcal{M}}|\cdot(h_{1}-h_{2})^{2}.

From
\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}}\left(|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|-h_{2}\right)^{2}=|{\mathcal{M}}|(h_{1}-h_{2})^{2}
we deduce that
\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}\setminus{\mathcal{M}}}\left(|P^{\perp}\cap{\mathcal{M}}|-h_{2}\right)^{2}=0
. This proves that (2) implies (1) when
{\mathcal{M}}
is an
i
-tight set. When
{\mathcal{M}}
is an
m
-ovoid, we can use the same method to obtain the result. The proof of (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(3) is similar and we omit the details. This completes the proof.

Lemma 2.4
.

For a natural number

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
the the algebra of
3\times 3
Hermitian matrices over the octonions
\mathbb{O}
. To be specific, a
3\times 3
matrix over
\mathbb{O}
is an Hermitian matrix if
x^{\top}=\overline{x}
and the diagonal entries of
x
lie in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Here,
\overline{x}
is the matrix obtained by applying the octonion conjugate to the entries of
x
. For
d,e,f\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and
D,E,F\in\mathbb{O}
, we define

(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}^{\prime},\lambda_{0}^{\prime\prime}\mid D,E,F):=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{0}&F&\overline{E}\\
\overline{F}&\lambda_{0}^{\prime}&D\\
E&\overline{D}&\lambda_{0}^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}

which is in
{\mathcal{A}}
. For
u,v\in{\mathcal{A}}
, their product in the algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is
u\circ v:=uv+vu
, where
uv
and
vu
are matrix multiplications. We set
I:=(1,1,1\mid 0,0,0)
, so that
I\circ a=2a
for any element
a
in
{\mathcal{A}}
. The algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
has close connections with Albert algebras and Jordan algebras, cf.
[
12
,
14
]
. We choose a basis
\{w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime},w_{i}^{\prime\prime}:\,0\leq i\leq 8\}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
as follows:

\displaystyle w_{0}=(1,0,0\mid 0,0,0)\textup{ and }w_{i}=(0,0,0\mid x_{i},0,0)\textup{ for }i>0,

\displaystyle w_{0}^{\prime}=(0,1,0\mid 0,0,0)\textup{ and }w_{i}^{\prime}=(0,0,0\mid 0,x_{i},0)\textup{ for }i>0,

\displaystyle w_{0}^{\prime\prime}=(0,0,1\mid 0,0,0)\textup{ and }w_{i}^{\prime\prime}=(0,0,0\mid 0,0,x_{i})\textup{ for }i>0.

The multiplication table of
{\mathcal{A}}
with respect to the basis
\{w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime},w_{i}^{\prime\prime}:\,0\leq i\leq 8\}
can be written down explicitly as in
[
17
, (4.90)-(4.92)]
. The similarity group of the Dickson-Freudenthal determinant of
{\mathcal{A}}
, which we denote by
\tilde{E}_{6}(q)
, is the universal covering group of the simple group
E_{6}(q)
, cf.
[
16
]
. It has three orbits on the nonzero vectors of
{\mathcal{A}}
, which are called the white, gray and black vectors respectively, cf.
[
1
,
5
]
. They correspond to vectors of rank
1
, rank
2
and rank
3
in
[
11
]
. The
1
-dimensional subspace spanned by a white, gray, black vector is called a white, gray and black point respectively. The stabilizer of
I
in
\tilde{E}_{6}(q)
is

.

For an element
v=\sum_{t=0}^{8}(\lambda_{t}w_{t}+\lambda_{t}^{\prime}w_{t}^{\prime}+\lambda_{t}^{\prime\prime}w_{t}^{\prime\prime})\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we define the trace of

as
\textup{Tr}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(v)=\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{0}^{\prime}+\lambda_{0}^{\prime\prime}
. We define the
F_{4}(q)
-invariant subspaces

,
U^{\prime}=\langle I\rangle_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
, and set

. For

Q_{0}(v)=\lambda_{0}^{2}+\lambda_{0}\lambda_{0}^{\prime}+\lambda_{0}^{\prime 2}+\sum_{t=1}^{4}(\lambda_{t}\lambda_{9-t}+\lambda_{t}^{\prime}\lambda_{9-t}^{\prime}+\lambda_{t}^{\prime\prime}\lambda_{9-t}^{\prime\prime}).

In particular, if

, then
Q_{0}(v)=\lambda_{0}^{2}+\lambda_{0}\lambda_{0}^{\prime}+\lambda_{0}^{\prime 2}+D\overline{D}+E\overline{E}+F\overline{F}
.
The quadratic form
Q_{0}
on
U
is
F_{4}(q)
-invariant and has

as its radical, so it induces a nondegenerate quadratic form

on

. The space

is the minimal module of

, and
\dim(W)=25
or

according as the characteristic

of

is

3.
The proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section we give the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Take the same notation as in the previous section, and we consider the polar space defined by the quadratic space
(W,Q)
. If the characteristic
p
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
is
3
, then the associated polar space is
Q(24,q)
and the group
F_{4}(q)
has two orbits on the singular points by
[
5
, Table 2]
. One orbit
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
has size
(q^{4}+1)\frac{q^{12}-1}{q-1}
which is not divisible by the ovoid number
q^{12}+1
, so it is a
(q^{4}+1)
-tight set of
Q(24,q)
as desired. We suppose that
q\equiv 2\pmod{3}
in the sequel, so that
W=U
and the associated polar space is
Q^{-}(25,q)
. Let
M_{1}
be the set of white vectors in
U
, and write
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
for the corresponding set of projective points. The set

has size
(q^{4}+1)(q^{12}-1)
and forms a single
F_{4}(q)
-orbit by
[
5
, (W.3)]
. By
[
16
, Lemma 5]
, we enumerate the white vectors in
U
as follows:

(I)

v=f\cdot(A\overline{A},B\overline{B},1\mid\overline{B},A,\overline{A}B)
for some

and
A,B\in\mathbb{O}
such that
A\overline{A}+B\overline{B}+1=0
,

(II)

v=e\cdot(C\overline{C},1,0\mid A,\overline{CA},C)
for some
e\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
and octonions
A,C
such that
A\overline{A}=0
,
C\overline{C}+1=0
,

(III)

v=(0,0,0\mid D,E,F)
, where
D,E,F
are octonions such that

(3.1)

D\overline{D}=E\overline{E}=F\overline{F}=0,\quad DE=EF=FD=0.

In particular,
M_{1}
is
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
-invariant and it contains the singular vector
(0,0,0\mid x_{1},0,0)
. It follows that
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
has size
(q^{4}+1)\frac{q^{12}-1}{q-1}
and consists of singular points of

.

We claim that
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
is a
(q^{4}+1)
-tight set of

. By Lemma
2.1
and the fact

is single

-orbit, it suffices to show that there are

points in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
that is perpendicular to
\langle(0,0,0\mid x_{1},0,0)\rangle
. Suppose that
\langle v\rangle
is such a point in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
, and we examine the three types (I)-(III) one by one. If

, then the condition that
\langle v\rangle
is perpendicular to
\langle(0,0,0\mid x_{1},0,0)\rangle
translates to
\textup{Tr}(x_{1}\overline{D})=0
.

First consider the case where
\langle v\rangle
is of type (I). The number of such points in
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
that are perpendicular to
\langle(0,0,0\mid x_{1},0,0)\rangle
equals the number of
(A,B)
pairs such that

,
\textup{Tr}(x_{1}B)=0
. If we write

, then
\textup{Tr}(x_{1}B)=-b_{8}=0
and

. By Lemma

, we deduce that the number of such

. Here,

.

Next consider the case where
\langle v\rangle
is of type (II). The number of such points in

(1)
