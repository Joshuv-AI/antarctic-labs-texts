,

further increased the capabilities of this method. Within the framework of this approach, a number of studies have obtained interesting results for correlation functions in the

In this paper, we apply the ABA to the study of correlation functions of a one-dimensional Bose gas (the Lieb–Liniger model) with attractive interaction.
The Hamiltonian of this model is given by

H=\int_{0}^{L}\Big(\partial_{x}\Psi^{\dagger}(x)\partial_{x}\Psi(x)+c\Psi^{\dagger}(x)\Psi^{\dagger}(x)\Psi(x)\Psi(x)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(1.1)

Here
\Psi^{\dagger}(x)
and
\Psi(x)
are canonical Bose fields

[\Psi(x),\Psi^{\dagger}(y)]=\delta(x-y),

(1.2)

acting in a Fock space

\Psi(x)|0\rangle=0,\qquad\langle 0|\Psi^{\dagger}(x)=0.

(1.3)

The parameter
c
in (
1.1
) is a coupling constant. The case of attraction corresponds to
c<0
. Therefore, in what follows we set
c=-\varkappa
and consider
\varkappa>0
.

The spectral problem for this model was solved in
[
37
,
38
]
.
The ground state energy and the wave function in the attractive case were studied in
[
39
,
40
]
.
A formulation of the model in the ABA language was given in
[
41
,
42
]
.
A series of multiple integrals for correlation functions of this model was derived in
[
43
,
44
]
. In
[
29

, a representation for the correlation function was obtained in the form of a Fredholm determinant, depending on auxiliary quantum fields.
Numerical analysis of correlation functions in the Lieb–Liniger model with repulsion was carried out in
[
36
]
using explicit formulas for form factors obtained within the framework of the ABA.

In
[
45
,
46
]
, the dynamical correlation functions of one-dimensional bosons with attraction at zero temperature were studied. The calculation method was based on the expansion of correlation functions over form factors, which in turn were previously calculated using the ABA. It was shown that taking into account only two types of excited states already provides good accuracy for correlations.

In this paper, we consider equal-time correlation functions of the one-dimensional bosons with attraction at zero temperature. Unlike dynamical correlations, there is no need to use the form factor expansion in this case. In particular, in
[
25
]
a closed representation was obtained for the generating function of the density correlations in any quantum integrable model with a 6-vertex
R
-matrix
2
2

2

A similar representation for the field correlation functions is obtained in this work.
. This result is quite applicable to the Lieb–Liniger model. It should be said, however, that the above-mentioned representation is very cumbersome and contains summation over all possible partitions of the Bethe parameters. In
[
25
]
, this sum over partitions was reduced to a multiple contour integral of Cauchy type (master-equation
[
23
]
), which in turn generates numerous integral representations for correlation functions in the thermodynamic limit
[
25
,
27
]
.

However, in the case of one-dimensional bosons with attraction, there is a significant simplification of all formulas in the
\varkappa L\gg 1
regime. It is based on a very simple ground state structure. The Bethe equations, which characterize the spectrum of the Hamiltonian, can be solved explicitly up to exponentially small corrections at
\varkappa L\gg 1
. At the same time, just a few numbers of terms survive in the representations for correlation functions. Moreover, in the case of an arbitrary solution of the Bethe equations, these terms contain rather complex determinants. However, for the ground state of one-dimensional bosons with attraction, these determinants are also simplified and can be calculated explicitly. It was this feature that the authors of
[
45
,
46
]
took advantage of, obtaining explicit and very simple formulas for form factors. In our case, all the simplifications listed above lead to the fact that we obtain explicit results for the correlation functions of densities and fields up to exponentially small corrections at
\varkappa L\gg 1
.

We present now the main results of the paper. The ground state correlation function of fields is given by

\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(x)\Psi(0)\rangle=\frac{1}{L}e^{-\varkappa x(N-1)/2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}e^{-\varkappa x(\ell-1)(N-\ell)}\left(1+\frac{\varkappa x}{2}(N-2\ell+1)^{2}\right)+O\big((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\big),

(1.4)

where
x>0
, and
N
is the number of particles in the ground state. The density correlation function has the following form:

\langle j(x)j(0)\rangle=\frac{1}{\varkappa L}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}e^{-\varkappa x\ell(N-\ell)}\left(1+\frac{x\varkappa}{2}(N-2\ell)^{2}\right)+O\big((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\big),

(1.5)

where the density operator
j(x)
is

j(x)=\Psi^{\dagger}(x)\Psi(x).

(1.6)

Section
4

5

3
. Finally, in section
6

A

2
Algebraic Bethe ansatz

In this section, we briefly recall the basic information about the ABA, which is necessary for further understanding of the paper. The reader can find a more detailed presentation of this method in the original works
[
10
,
11
]
or in numerous monographs (see, for example,
[
13
,
14
]
). We mainly adhere to the terminology and notation of
[
14
]
.

One of the main objects of the ABA is an
R
-matrix
R(u,v)
. The one-dimensional bosons model is described by
the
GL(2)
-invariant
R
-matrix acting in the tensor product of two auxiliary spaces
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
, where

V_{k}\sim\mathbb{C}^{2}
,
k=1,2
:

R(u,v)=\mathbf{I}+g(u,v)\mathbf{P},\qquad g(u,v)=\frac{\eta}{u-v}.

(2.1)

In the above definition,
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix in
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
,
\mathbf{P}
is the permutation matrix
that exchanges
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, and
\eta
is a constant. For the model of one-dimensional bosons, it is related to the coupling constant by
\eta=i\varkappa
.

Another important object of the ABA is a monodromy matrix
T(u)
:

T(u)=\begin{pmatrix}A(u)&B(u)\\
C(u)&D(u)\end{pmatrix}.

(2.2)

The entries of the monodromy matrix are operators acting in the quantum space
\mathcal{H}
of the Hamiltonian (
1.1
). They depend on a complex parameter
u

and satisfy the following algebra:

R_{12}(u_{1},u_{2})T_{1}(u_{1})T_{2}(u_{2})=T_{2}(u_{2})T_{1}(u_{1})R_{12}(u_{1},u_{2}).

(2.3)

Equation (
2.3
) holds in the tensor product
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\otimes\mathcal{H}
,
where
V_{k}\sim\mathbb{C}^{2}
,
k=1,2
, are the auxiliary linear spaces. The
R
-matrix acts non-trivially in
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
, the matrices
T_{k}(u)
act non-trivially in

V_{k}\otimes\mathcal{H}
.

The trace of the monodromy matrix in the auxiliary space
\mathop{\rm tr}T(u)=A(u)+D(u)
is called a transfer matrix. It is a generating
functional of integrals of motion of the model. The eigenvectors of the transfer matrix are
called on-shell Bethe vectors (or simply on-shell vectors). They can be parameterized by a set of complex parameters
satisfying Bethe equations (see below).

A necessary condition for the applicability of the ABA is the existence in the space
\mathcal{H}
of a special vector called pseudovacuum. In the case of the Lieb–Liniger model, this vector coincides with the Fock vacuum
|0\rangle
(
1.3
). It is an eigenvector to the diagonal matrix elements of the monodromy matrix and is annihilated by the operator
C(u)
:

\displaystyle A(u)|0\rangle=a(u)|0\rangle,

(2.4)

\displaystyle D(u)|0\rangle=d(u)|0\rangle,

\displaystyle C(u)|0\rangle=0.

In the one-dimensional bosons model

a(u)=e^{-iLu/2},\qquad d(u)=e^{iLu/2}.

(2.5)

Below we will often use the ratio of the functions
a(u)
and
d(u)

r(u)=\frac{a(u)}{d(u)}=e^{-iLu}.

(2.6)

We will also need a dual pseudovacuum vector
\langle 0|
belonging to the dual space
\mathcal{H}^{*}
. It possesses similar properties

\displaystyle\langle 0|A(u)=a(u)\langle 0|,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\langle 0|D(u)=d(u)\langle 0|,

\displaystyle\langle 0|B(u)=0,

with the same functions
a(u)
and
d(u)
(
2.5
).

2.1
Notation

Before moving on we say some words about the notation used in the paper. Besides the function
g(u,v)
(
2.1
) we also introduce rational functions

f(u,v)=1+g(u,v)=\frac{u-v+\eta}{u-v}

(2.8)

and

h(u,v)=\frac{f(u,v)}{g(u,v)}=\frac{u-v+c}{\eta},\qquad t(u,v)=\frac{g(u,v)}{h(u,v)}=\frac{\eta^{2}}{(u-v)(u-v+c)}.

(2.9)

We will permanently deal with sets of parameters. These sets are always denoted by bars:
\bar{u}
,
\bar{\lambda}
etc.
Individual elements of the sets are denoted by subscripts:
u_{j}
,
\lambda_{k}
etc. As a rule, the number of elements in the
sets is not shown explicitly in the equations; however, we give these cardinalities in
special comments after the formulas.
We also use a special notation for subsets with one element omitted
\bar{u}_{k}=\bar{u}\setminus u_{k}
,
\bar{\lambda}_{j}=\bar{\lambda}\setminus\lambda_{j}
,
and so on.

To avoid formulas being too cumbersome we use shorthand notation for products of scalar
functions or commuting operators. Namely, if functions
a
,
d
,
r
, or the monodromy matrix entries depend
on sets of parameters, this means that one should take the product over the corresponding set.
For example,

d(\bar{u})=\prod_{u_{j}\in\bar{u}}d(u_{j});\quad r(\bar{v}_{j})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{k}\in\bar{v}\\
v_{k}\neq v_{j}\end{subarray}}r(v_{k});\quad B(\bar{\lambda})=\prod_{\lambda_{j}\in\bar{\lambda}}B(\lambda_{j}).

(2.10)

The last equation is well defined since
[B(u),B(v)]=0
due to (
2.3
). We will extend this convention on the shorthand notation for the products to some other functions and operators that will appear as we proceed.

We use the same convention for the rational functions introduced above

g(u_{k},\bar{v})=\prod_{v_{j}\in\bar{v}}g(u_{k},v_{j});\quad h(u,\bar{v}_{j})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{k}\in\bar{v}\\
v_{k}\neq v_{j}\end{subarray}}h(u,v_{k});\quad f(\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu})=\prod_{\lambda_{j}\in\bar{\lambda}}\prod_{\mu_{k}\in\bar{\mu}}f(\lambda_{j},\mu_{k}).

(2.11)

By definition, any product over the empty set is equal to
1
. A double product is equal to
1
if at least one of the sets
is empty.

Finally, let us introduce a new notation for special products of the functions
g(u,v)

\Delta(\bar{u})=\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}g(u_{k},u_{j}),\qquad\Delta^{\prime}(\bar{u})=\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}g(u_{j},u_{k}),

(2.12)

where
N=\#\bar{u}\geq 2
. It is clear that
\Delta^{\prime}(\bar{u})=(-1)^{N(N-1)/2}\Delta(\bar{u})
. For
N=0,1
, we set
\Delta(\bar{u})=\Delta^{\prime}(\bar{u})=1
by definition.

2.2
Bethe vectors

Eigenvectors of the transfer matrix
A(z)+D(z)
can be obtained by the successive action of the
B
-operators on the pseudovacuum. Let
\bar{u}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{N}\}
, where

N=0,1,\dots
. Then a vector
B(\bar{u})|0\rangle
is an eigenvector of the transfer matrix

\big(A(z)+D(z)\big)B(\bar{u})|0\rangle=\tau(z;\bar{u})B(\bar{u})|0\rangle,

(2.13)

provided the parameters
\bar{u}
satisfy a system of Bethe equations

r(u_{j})=\frac{f(u_{j},\bar{u}_{j})}{f(\bar{u}_{j},u_{j})},\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

(2.14)

Then

\tau(z;\bar{u})=a(z)f(\bar{u},z)+d(z)f(z,\bar{u}).

(2.15)

We call vectors
B(\bar{u})|0\rangle
on-shell Bethe vectors if the set
\bar{u}
satisfies equations (
2.14
). Otherwise, if
\bar{u}
consists of arbitrary complex numbers,
we call the corresponding vectors
B(\bar{u})|0\rangle
off-shell Bethe vectors.

One can also define dual Bethe vectors belonging to the dual space
\mathcal{H}^{*}
. They are constructed by the right successive action of the
C
-operators
on the dual pseudovacuum
\langle 0|
. Then

\langle 0|C(\bar{u})\big(A(z)+D(z)\big)=\tau(z;\bar{u})\langle 0|C(\bar{u}),

(2.16)

provided
\bar{u}
satisfy Bethe equations (
2.14
). In this case, we call
\langle 0|C(\bar{u})
a dual on-shell Bethe vector. If
\bar{u}
consists of arbitrary complex numbers,
then
\langle 0|C(\bar{u})
is called a dual off-shell Bethe vector.

We also will consider twisted Bethe equations

r(v_{j})=e^{\beta}\frac{f(v_{j},\bar{v}_{j})}{f(\bar{v}_{j},v_{j})},\qquad j=1,\dots,N,

(2.17)

where
\beta
is a complex number. The corresponding vectors
\langle 0|C(\bar{v})
and
B(\bar{v})|0\rangle
then are eigenvectors of the twisted transfer matrix

A(z)+e^{\beta}D(z)
. We call them twisted (dual) on-shell Bethe vectors.

Below, it will be convenient for us to normalize the elements of the monodromy matrix to the function
d(u)
(
2.4
), (
2.5
)

\boldsymbol{B}(u)=\frac{B(u)}{d(u)},\qquad\boldsymbol{C}(u)=\frac{C(u)}{d(u)},

(2.18)

and construct Bethe vectors using
the
\boldsymbol{B}
- and
\boldsymbol{C}
-operators

\boldsymbol{B}(\bar{u})|0\rangle,\qquad\qquad\langle 0|\boldsymbol{C}(\bar{u}).

(2.19)

Here we have extended the convention on the shorthand notation (
2.10
) to the products of operators
\boldsymbol{B}(u)
and
\boldsymbol{C}(u)
.
With this normalization, scalar products and correlation functions automatically depend on the function
r(u)
(
2.6
).

2.3
Composite model

Actions of the Bose fields
\Psi(0)
and
\Psi^{\dagger}(0)
on off-shell Bethe vectors were calculated in
[
44
,
29
]

\displaystyle\Psi(0)\boldsymbol{B}(\bar{u})|0\rangle=\sqrt{\varkappa}\sum_{j=1}^{N}r(u_{j})f(\bar{u}_{j},u_{j})\boldsymbol{B}(\bar{u}_{j})|0\rangle,

(2.20)

\displaystyle\langle 0|\boldsymbol{C}(\bar{u})\Psi^{\dagger}(0)=-\sqrt{\varkappa}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(u_{j},\bar{u}_{j})\langle 0|\boldsymbol{C}(\bar{u}_{j}).

Here
\bar{u}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{N}\}
are arbitrary complex numbers.

Remark 2.1
.

The action of the fields
\Psi(0)
and
\Psi^{\dagger}(0)
on Bethe vectors can be obtained using the model of one-dimensional bosons on a lattice
[
47
]
. In this model, there is some ambiguity in the definition of the
B
and
C
-operators. This ambiguity is partially preserved in the continuous limit. In particular, we can replace
B\to\omega B
and
C\to\omega^{-1}C
, where
\omega
is an arbitrary non-zero number. The transformed monodromy matrix still satisfies the
RTT
-relation (
2.3
) and has the same pseudovacuum vector as the original one. Obviously, additional factors appear in formulas (
2.20
) under this transformation. However, they compensate each other in quadratic expressions. We choose a normalization of the
B
and
C
-operators such that
C(u)=-B^{\dagger}(u^{*})
.

To find the actions of
\Psi(x)
and
\Psi^{\dagger}(x)
on off-shell Bethe vectors, we
introduce a composite model
[
15
]
. In this model, we present the total monodromy matrix
T(u)
as a product of two partial monodromy matrices

T(u)=T^{(2)}(u)T^{(1)}(u).

(2.21)

Here the total monodromy matrix
T(u)
is related to the interval
[0,L]
, while the partial monodromy matrices
T^{(1)}(u)
and
T^{(2)}(u)
respectively are associated with the intervals
[0,x[
and
[x,L]
. Both
T^{(1)}(u)
and
T^{(2)}(u)
have the form (
2.2
)

T^{(j)}(u)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A^{(j)}(u)&B^{(j)}(u)\\
C^{(j)}(u)&D^{(j)}(u)\end{array}\right),\qquad j=1,2.

(2.22)

Each of the partial monodromy matrices satisfies the
RTT
-relation (
2.3
)
with the
R
-matrix (
2.1
). The matrix elements of the different partial matrices
commute with each other. Each of the matrices

T^{(j)}(u)
possesses a vector
|0\rangle^{(j)}
and a dual vector

\langle 0|^{(j)}
, where
|0\rangle=|0\rangle^{(2)}\otimes|0\rangle^{(1)}
.
The action formulas of the operators on these vectors are similar to the action formulas for the total monodromy matrix

\displaystyle A^{(j)}(u)|0\rangle^{(j)}=a^{(j)}(u)|0\rangle^{(j)},

\displaystyle\langle 0|^{(j)}A^{(j)}(u)=a^{(j)}(u)\langle 0|^{(j)},

(2.23)

\displaystyle D^{(j)}(u)|0\rangle^{(j)}=d^{(j)}(u)|0\rangle^{(j)},

\displaystyle\langle 0|^{(j)}D^{(j)}(u)=d^{(j)}(u)\langle 0|^{(j)},

\displaystyle C^{(j)}(u)|0\rangle^{(j)}=0,

\displaystyle\langle 0|^{(j)}B^{(j)}(u)=0,

where functions
a^{(j)}(u)
and
d^{(j)}(u)
in the Lieb–Liniger model are

\displaystyle a^{(1)}(u)=e^{-ixu/2},

\displaystyle d^{(1)}(u)=e^{ixu/2},

(2.24)

\displaystyle a^{(2)}(u)=e^{-i(L-x)u/2},

\displaystyle d^{(2)}(u)=e^{i(L-x)u/2}.

We will also often use the ratio of the functions
a^{(j)}(u)
and
d^{(j)}(u)

r^{(1)}(u)=e^{-iux},\qquad r^{(2)}(u)=e^{-iu(L-x)}=\frac{r(u)}{r^{(1)}(u)}.

(2.25)

In complete analogy with (
2.18
), we introduce

\boldsymbol{B}^{(j)}(u)=\frac{B^{(j)}(u)}{d^{(j)}(u)},\qquad\boldsymbol{C}^{(j)}(u)=\frac{C^{(j)}(u)}{d^{(j)}(u)},\qquad j=1,2,

(2.26)

and extend the convention on the shorthand notation to the products of partial operators and partial vacuum eigenvalues. For example,

\boldsymbol{B}^{(j)}(\bar{v})=\prod_{v_{k}\in\bar{v}}\boldsymbol{B}^{(j)}(v_{k}),\qquad r^{(j)}(\bar{u})=\prod_{u_{l}\in\bar{u}}r^{(j)}(u_{l}),

(2.27)

and so on.

The key formulas of the composite model are expressions of the total off-shell Bethe vectors
\boldsymbol{B}(\bar{u})|0\rangle
and
\langle 0|\boldsymbol{C}(\bar{u})
in
terms of partial ones
\boldsymbol{B}^{(j)}(\bar{u})|0\rangle
and
\langle 0|\boldsymbol{C}^{(j)}(\bar{u})
. These formulas were obtained in
[
15
]

(see also
[
13
,
14
]
):

\boldsymbol{B}(\bar{u})|0\rangle=\sum_{\bar{u}\mapsto\{\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}\}}r^{(2)}(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})\boldsymbol{B}^{(1)}(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})|0\rangle^{(1)}\boldsymbol{B}^{(2)}(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})|0\rangle^{(2)}f(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI},\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}),

(2.28)

and

\langle 0|\boldsymbol{C}(\bar{u})=\sum_{\bar{u}\mapsto\{\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}\}}r^{(1)}(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})\langle 0|^{(1)}\boldsymbol{C}^{(1)}(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})\langle 0|^{(2)}\boldsymbol{C}^{(2)}(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})\;f(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}).

(2.29)

Here the set
\bar{u}
consists of arbitrary complex numbers. The sums in (
2.28
) and (
2.29
) are taken over all possible partitions of the set
\bar{u}
into two disjoint subsets
\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}
and
\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}
(including the partition into the empty set and the full set).

It is easy to see that the intermediate point
x
plays the same role for the matrix
T^{(2)}(u)
as point
0
does for the matrix
T(u)
. Therefore, the action
of the fields
\Psi(x)
and
\Psi^{\dagger}(x)
on vectors
\boldsymbol{B}^{(2)}(\bar{u})|0\rangle
and
\langle 0|\boldsymbol{C}^{(2)}(\bar{u})
is similar to actions (
2.20
)

\displaystyle\Psi(x)\boldsymbol{B}^{(2)}(\bar{u})|0\rangle=\sqrt{\varkappa}\sum_{j=1}^{N}r^{(2)}(u_{j})f(\bar{u}_{j},u_{j})\boldsymbol{B}^{(2)}(\bar{u}_{j})|0\rangle,

(2.30)

\displaystyle\langle 0|\boldsymbol{C}^{(2)}(\bar{u})\Psi^{\dagger}(x)=-\sqrt{\varkappa}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(u_{j},\bar{u}_{j})\langle 0|\boldsymbol{C}^{(2)}(\bar{u}_{j}).

Equations (
2.30
) together with formulas (
2.28
) and (
2.29
) provide the actions of
\Psi(x)
and
\Psi^{\dagger}(x)
on off-shell Bethe vectors.

2.4
Ground state

In the model of one-dimensional bosons with repulsion, all solutions of the Bethe equations are real. In the case of attraction, in addition to real roots, complex conjugate pairs can also arise. They form so-called string solutions.

The Bethe equations have the following form:

e^{iLu_{j}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}\frac{u_{j}-u_{k}-i\varkappa}{u_{j}-u_{k}+i\varkappa},\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

(2.31)

Twisted Bethe equations have the form

e^{iLv_{j}}=e^{-\beta}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}\frac{v_{j}-v_{k}-i\varkappa}{v_{j}-v_{k}+i\varkappa},\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

(2.32)

It is easy to see that if a set
\bar{u}
is a solution to the ordinary Bethe equations (
2.31
), then the set
\bar{v}
, where
v_{j}=u_{j}+i\beta/L
, solves the twisted Bethe equations (
2.32
).

If
\bar{u}
is a solution to the Bethe equations, then the eigenvalue of the Hamiltonian on the on-shell vector
\boldsymbol{B}(\bar{u})|0\rangle
has the form

E[\bar{u}]=\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{2}.

(2.33)

In this paper, we consider correlation functions in the ground state. We will denote the corresponding roots of the Bethe equations by
\bar{\lambda}=\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}\}
,
where
N=0,1,\dots
. This solution forms one string centered at zero. In other words, all
\lambda_{j}
are purely imaginary so that
\lambda_{j}=-\lambda_{N-j+1}
.

Let
\lambda_{j}=i\varkappa\tilde{\lambda}_{j}

\tilde{\lambda}_{j}

We see that in the

3

Except for the root

where

We will also deal with a twisted ground state corresponding to the solution of the twisted Bethe equations. We denote the corresponding roots by

To conclude this section, we recall that

(2.36)

In the ABA method, it is customary to call the value

3

In this section, we present some formulas for scalar products of Bethe vectors. We also give formulas for form factors that are a direct consequence of the determinant representations for the scalar products.

3.1
General scalar products

We call the scalar product the pseudovacuum average

\boldsymbol{S}_{N}(\bar{v},\bar{u})=\langle 0|\boldsymbol{C}(\bar{v})\boldsymbol{B}(\bar{u})|0\rangle.

(3.1)

We do not impose any constraint
on the sets
\bar{v}
and
\bar{u}

To describe the scalar product (

(3.2)

Then the scalar product (

(3.3)

The sum in (

\bar{u}
into two disjoint subsets
\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}
and
\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}
and the set
\bar{v}
into two disjoint subsets
\bar{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}
and
\bar{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}

5.2
Expansion over

In the representations for the correlation functions, we need either the values of the functions

and

and then restrict ourselves to only the first few terms of the expansions. Then formulas (

Indeed, it is easy to see that under the conditions of proposition

g(u_{k+1},\bar{u}_{k+1})=\frac{(-1)^{n-1-k}}{(n-1)!}\binom{n-1}{k}.

Thus, the sums over

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\binom{n-1}{k}e^{k\beta}(-1)^{n-1-k}}{g(u_{k+1},\bar{v})}=(n-1)!\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{g(u_{k},\bar{u}_{k})}{g(u_{k},\bar{v})}e^{\beta/g(u_{k},u_{1})}.

(5.14)

The sums in the rhs of (
5.14

\beta
. For example, in the case of the function
S^{\mathbf{J}}_{n}(\bar{v},\bar{u}|\beta)
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{g(u_{k},\bar{u}_{k})}{g(u_{k},\bar{v})}e^{\beta/g(u_{k},u_{1})}=\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(u_{k}-v_{j})}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n}(u_{k}-u_{j})}+O(\beta)=\frac{1}{2\pi i\eta}\oint\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{z-v_{j}}{z-u_{j}}\,\mathrm{d}z+O(\beta).

(5.15)

Here the integration is carried out along an anticlockwise oriented contour surrounding the parameters
\bar{u}
. Evaluating this integral by the residue at infinity we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{g(u_{k},\bar{u}_{k})}{g(u_{k},\bar{v})}e^{\beta/g(u_{k},u_{1})}=\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{j}-v_{j})+O(\beta).

(5.16)

Similarly, in the case of the function
S^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}(\bar{v},\bar{u}|\beta)
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{g(u_{k},\bar{u}_{k})}{g(u_{k},\bar{v})}e^{\beta/g(u_{k},u_{1})}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(z-v_{j})}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z-u_{j})}\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\eta}(z-u_{1})\right)\,\mathrm{d}z+O(\beta^{2})\\
=1+\frac{\beta}{\eta}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{n}u_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}v_{j}\right)+O(\beta^{2}).

(5.17)

Thus,

\displaystyle S^{\mathbf{J}}_{n}(\bar{v},\bar{u}|\beta)=\frac{\beta}{\eta}n!(n-1)!g(u_{1},\bar{v})g(u_{n},\bar{v})\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{j}-v_{j})+O(\beta^{2}),

(5.18)

\displaystyle S^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}(\bar{v},\bar{u}|\beta)=n!(n-1)!g(u_{1},\bar{v})g(u_{n},\bar{v})\left(1+\beta(n-1)+\frac{\beta}{\eta}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(u_{j}-v_{j})\right)+O(\beta^{2}).

Using this method, one can find higher orders in the
\beta

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{N}(x;\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})=\frac{(-1)^{N-1}\varkappa\pi(N!)^{2}e^{-ix\lambda_{1}}}{2\sin\pi\gamma\Gamma(N-\gamma)\Gamma(N+\gamma)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}e^{i\eta x(\ell-1)(N-\ell)}M^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{\ell}(\beta)+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right),

(6.14)

where

M^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{\ell}(\beta)=\Lambda^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{\ell}+\Lambda^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{N-\ell+1}=(2\ell-N-1-\gamma)e^{-i\eta\gamma x(N-\ell+1)}\;{}_{2}\hskip-1.0ptF_{1}(\ell-N-\gamma,1-\ell;1;1-e^{\beta})\\
-(2\ell-N-1+\gamma)e^{-i\eta\gamma x\ell}\;{}_{2}\hskip-1.0ptF_{1}(1-\ell-\gamma,\ell-N;1;1-e^{\beta}).

(6.15)

We see that
M^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{\ell}(0)=0
. Hence, taking the
\beta\to 0
limit (that is, sending
\bar{\mu}\to\bar{\lambda}
) we arrive at

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{N}(x;\bar{\lambda},\bar{\lambda})=(-1)^{N-1}\varkappa^{2}LN^{2}e^{-\varkappa x(N-1)/2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}e^{-\varkappa x(\ell-1)(N-\ell)}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}M^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{\ell}(\beta)\Bigr|_{\beta=0}+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right).

(6.16)

Using (
5.25
) we find

\displaystyle{}_{2}\hskip-1.0ptF_{1}(\ell-N-\gamma,1-\ell;1;1-e^{\beta})=1-\beta(\ell-1)(N-\ell)+O(\beta^{2}),

(6.17)

\displaystyle{}_{2}\hskip-1.0ptF_{1}(1-\ell-\gamma,\ell-N;1;1-e^{\beta})=1-\beta(\ell-1)(N-\ell)+O(\beta^{2}).

Hence,

M^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{\ell}(\beta)=i\eta\gamma x(N-\ell+1)^{2}-2\gamma+O(\beta^{2}),

(6.18)

and

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}M^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{\ell}(\beta)\Bigr|_{\beta=0}=-\frac{2}{\varkappa L}-\frac{x}{L}(N-2\ell+1)^{2}.

(6.19)

Then equation (
6.16
) yields

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{N}(x;\bar{\lambda},\bar{\lambda})=(-1)^{N}\varkappa N^{2}e^{-\varkappa x(N-1)/2}\\
\times\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}e^{-\varkappa x(\ell-1)(N-\ell)}\left(1+\frac{\varkappa x}{2}(N-2\ell+1)^{2}\right)+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right).

(6.20)

It remains to divide this result by (
6.5
), and we arrive at representation (
1.4
).

6.3
Correlation function of densities

The correlation function of densities is calculated similarly. There are a few minor differences that we will note below.

First of all, we rewrite representation (
4.7
), putting
\bar{u}=\bar{\lambda}
and
\bar{v}=\bar{\mu}
in it

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(x;\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})=e^{\alpha N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{\mu}\mapsto\{\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}\}\\
\bar{\lambda}\mapsto\{\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}\}\end{subarray}}\frac{r^{(1)}(\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})}{r^{(1)}(\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})}f(\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI},\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})f(\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})S^{\mathbf{J}}_{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}}(\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}|\beta-\alpha)S^{\mathbf{J}}_{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}}(\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI},\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}|-\alpha).

(6.21)

An analysis completely similar to that carried out in the previous section shows that finite contributions to the generating function
\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(x;\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})
are given only by partitions of the following form:

\begin{aligned} &\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}=\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell}\},&\qquad&\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}=\{\lambda_{\ell+1},\dots,\lambda_{N}\},\\
&\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}=\{\mu_{N-\ell+1},\dots,\mu_{N}\},&\qquad&\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}=\{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N-\ell}\},\end{aligned}\qquad\ell=0,\dots,N.

(6.22)

The slight difference with (
6.22
) is explained by the fact that now
\#\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}=\#\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}
and

\#\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}=\#\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}
.

Then we obtain

\displaystyle f(\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI},\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})=\binom{N}{\ell}+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right),

(6.23)

\displaystyle f(\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})=\binom{N}{\ell}+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right),

and

\frac{r^{(1)}(\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})}{r^{(1)}(\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})}=\exp\Big\{i\eta x(\ell-\gamma)(N-\ell)\Big\}+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right).

(6.24)

We also have

\displaystyle S^{\mathbf{J}}_{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}}(\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}|\beta-\alpha)=S^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}(N-\ell+\gamma|\beta-\alpha)+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right),

\displaystyle\ell>0,

(6.25)

\displaystyle S^{\mathbf{J}}_{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}}(\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI},\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}|-\alpha)=S^{\mathbf{J}}_{N-\ell}(\ell-\gamma|-\alpha)+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right),

\displaystyle\ell<N.

Recall also the
S^{\mathbf{J}}_{0}(\emptyset,\emptyset|\beta)=1
by definition.

Let us consider contributions to (
6.21
) provided by the partitions with
\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}=\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}=\emptyset

\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{0}=e^{\alpha N}\frac{r^{(1)}(\bar{\mu})}{r^{(1)}(\bar{\lambda})}S^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(\bar{\lambda},\bar{\mu}|-\alpha)=e^{\alpha N+x\gamma\varkappa N}S^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(-\gamma|-\alpha)+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right),

(6.26)

and the partitions with
\bar{\lambda}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}=\bar{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}=\emptyset

\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}=e^{\alpha N}S^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda}|\beta-\alpha)=e^{\alpha N}S^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(\gamma|\beta-\alpha)+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right).

(6.27)

We have due to (
5.29
)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{0}=(-1)^{N-1}\frac{\pi(N!)^{2}\left(e^{-\alpha}-1\right)e^{\alpha N+x\gamma\varkappa N}}{\sin\pi\gamma\Gamma(N+\gamma)\Gamma(N-\gamma)}\;{}_{2}\hskip-1.0ptF_{1}(1+\gamma,1-N;2;1-e^{-\alpha}),

(6.28)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}=(-1)^{N}\frac{\pi(N!)^{2}\left(e^{\beta-\alpha}-1\right)e^{\alpha N}}{\sin\pi\gamma\Gamma(N+\gamma)\Gamma(N-\gamma)}\;{}_{2}\hskip-1.0ptF_{1}(1-\gamma,1-N;2;1-e^{\beta-\alpha}).

Obviously, the sum
\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{0}+\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}
has a well defined
\beta\to 0
limit. Moreover, it is easy to see that

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\Big(\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{0}+\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}\Big)=ax+b,

(6.29)

where
a
and
b
are
x
-independent. Thus, this contribution will be killed by the second
x
-derivative (see (
4.5
)).

The remaining partitions give the following contribution to equation (
6.21
):

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(x;\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})=\frac{(-1)^{N}\pi(N!)^{2}\left(e^{\beta-\alpha}-1\right)\left(e^{-\alpha}-1\right)e^{\alpha N}}{\sin\pi\gamma\Gamma(N-\gamma)\Gamma(N+\gamma)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}e^{i\eta x\ell(N-\ell)}\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right),

(6.30)

where

\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}=(N-2\ell+\gamma)e^{-i\eta\gamma x(N-\ell)}\;{}_{2}\hskip-1.0ptF_{1}(\ell+1-N-\gamma,1-\ell;2;1-e^{\beta-\alpha})\;{}_{2}\hskip-1.0ptF_{1}(1-\ell+\gamma,\ell+1-N;2;1-e^{-\alpha}).

(6.31)

We see that again
\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}+\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{N-\ell}
is non-singular at
\beta\to 0

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(x;\bar{\mu},\bar{\lambda})=\frac{(-1)^{N}\pi(N!)^{2}\left(e^{\beta-\alpha}-1\right)\left(e^{-\alpha}-1\right)e^{\alpha N}}{2\sin\pi\gamma\Gamma(N-\gamma)\Gamma(N+\gamma)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-1}e^{i\eta x\ell(N-\ell)}M^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}(\beta,\alpha)+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right),

(6.32)

where
M^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}(\beta,\alpha)=\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}+\Lambda^{\mathbf{J}}_{N-\ell}
.
It is easy to see that
M^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}(0,\alpha)=0
. Hence, taking the
\beta\to 0

\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(x;\bar{\lambda},\bar{\lambda})=(-1)^{N}\varkappa LN^{2}\left(e^{-\alpha}-1\right)^{2}e^{\alpha N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}e^{-\varkappa x\ell(N-\ell)}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}M^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}(\beta,\alpha)\Bigr|_{\beta=0}+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right).

\alpha

\alpha=0
(see (
4.5
)). It is clear that this derivative acts only on the factor
\left(e^{-\alpha}-1\right)^{2}
. In the rest of equation (

\alpha=0

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(x;\bar{\lambda},\bar{\lambda})\Bigr|_{\alpha=0}=(-1)^{N}\varkappa LN^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}e^{-\varkappa x\ell(N-\ell)}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}M^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}(\beta,0)\Bigr|_{\beta=0}+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right),

(6.34)

where

M^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}(\beta,0)=(N-2\ell+\gamma)e^{x\varkappa\gamma(N-\ell)}\;{}_{2}\hskip-1.0ptF_{1}(\ell+1-N-\gamma,1-\ell;2;1-e^{\beta})\\
-(N-2\ell-\gamma)e^{x\varkappa\gamma\ell}\;{}_{2}\hskip-1.0ptF_{1}(1-\ell-\gamma,\ell+1-N;2;1-e^{\beta}).

(6.35)

Using (
5.25
) we obtain

M^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}(\beta,0)=2\gamma+\varkappa x\gamma(N-2\ell)^{2}+O(\beta^{2}),

(6.36)

and hence,

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}M^{\mathbf{J}}_{\ell}(\beta,0)\Bigr|_{\beta=0}=\frac{1}{\varkappa L}\left(1+\frac{\varkappa x}{2}(N-2\ell)^{2}\right).

(6.37)

Then equation (
6.33

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{J}}_{N}(x;\bar{\lambda},\bar{\lambda})\Bigr|_{\alpha=0}=(-1)^{N}N^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}e^{-\varkappa x\ell(N-\ell)}\left(1+\frac{\varkappa x}{2}(N-2\ell)^{2}\right)+O\left((\varkappa L)^{-\infty}\right).

(6.38)

Normalizing this result by (
6.5
) and acting with the second
x
-derivative we arrive at representation (
1.5
).

Conclusion

We studied the ground state correlation functions of densities and fields in the model of one-dimensional bosons with attraction in the
\varkappa L\gg 1
regime. The key formulas in our approach are representations (
4.7
) and (
4.8
). They define generating functions for correlations of densities and fields. These representations are obtained using the ABA method. They are valid for any coupling constant, as well as for any eigenstate of the Hamiltonian.

In the case of the Lieb–Liniger model with repulsion, equations (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) ultimately lead to representations for the correlation functions in the form of multiple integrals
[
25
]
. The analysis of these integrals is a very difficult task. To date, based on these integral representations, it has been possible to obtain only the asymptotic behavior of correlation functions at a large distance
x

[
27
]
.

However, for the attractive Lieb–Liniger model in the
\varkappa L\gg 1
regime, equations (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) are dramatically simplified. Of the entire sum by partitioning of
2N
roots of the Bethe equations, only
N
terms make a finite contribution to the correlation. Moreover, these terms are also simplified, since all the determinants included in them are explicitly calculated. As a result, we obtain very simple and explicit results for the correlation functions.

The reason for the simplifications noted above is the very special form of solution of the Bethe equations corresponding to the ground state. The roots of this solution form a so-called string, and the interval between the roots is equal to
i\varkappa
up to exponentially small corrections. It is thanks to this structure of the ground state that it is possible to explicitly take into account all contributions to the correlation functions.

Since representations (
4.7
), (
4.8
) are valid for any eigenstate of the Hamiltonian, we can also use them to calculate temperature correlation functions. However, we have to deal with states that contain a mixture of strings of different lengths in this case. Therefore, we no longer have such significant simplifications, despite the fact that string solutions of the Bethe equations are still determined up to exponentially small corrections in the
\varkappa L\gg 1
regime. Many more terms survive in the sums over partitions, and these terms themselves have a more complex form. This observation further highlights the fact that it is the remarkable ground state structure that is responsible for the dramatic simplifications of the correlation functions at zero temperature in the attractive Lieb–Lineger model. Apparently, this is the main difference between the model under consideration and
1+1
-dimensional models of quantum field theory, in which significant simplifications are also observed in the large system size limit.

Unfortunately, the method used in this paper is not suitable for computing dynamical correlation functions. Either it should be significantly modified, or resort to the form factor decomposition, as was done in
[
45
,
46
]
. In the latter case, the question arises about summation over the complete set of intermediate states. Recall that in
[
45
,
46
]
, contributions from only two types of excitations were taken into account. It is quite possible that taking into account all types of excitations when calculating dynamic correlations will lead to simpler results.

(A.10)

Here the notation is similar to the one in (

Now we express the functions

(A.11)

and

(A.12)

where
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}}=\bar{u}_{0}
and
\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}}=\bar{v}_{0}

\boldsymbol{S}_{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}}^{(2)}(\bar{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}|\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})

\boldsymbol{S}_{N_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}}^{(2)}(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}|\bar{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}\mapsto\{\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}}\}\\

(A.13)

We also have

\frac{r^{(1)}(\bar{v}_{0})r^{(1)}(\bar{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})}{r^{(1)}(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})}f(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})f(\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{u}_{0})f(\bar{u}_{0},\bar{u}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})\;f(\bar{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})f(\bar{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{v}_{0})f(\bar{v}_{0},\bar{v}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})=\frac{r^{(1)}(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}})r^{(1)}(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}})r^{(1)}(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}})}{r^{(1)}(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}})r^{(1)}(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})}\\

\times f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{3}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{3}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{3}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}}).

(A.14)

Now we need to substitute formulas (
A.9
), (
A.13

We obtain

\times\mathbb{K}_{N_{1}}(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}}|\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}})\mathbb{K}_{N_{3}}(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}|\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{3}})\mathbb{K}_{N_{2}}(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}}|\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}})\mathbb{K}_{N_{4}}(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{4}}|\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}}),

(A.15)

where

(A.16)

and

F_{v}=f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{3}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{3}})\\
\times f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{3}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{3}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}})f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}}).

(A.17)

The sum in (
A.15

\bar{v}
and
\bar{u}
into five subsets each:

\bar{u}\mapsto\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{4}}\},\qquad\bar{v}\mapsto\{\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{3}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}}\}.

\bar{v}\mapsto\{\bar{v}^{\rm i},\bar{v}^{\rm ii}\}\end{subarray}}\;\frac{r^{(1)}(\bar{v}^{\rm ii})}{r^{(1)}(\bar{u}^{\rm ii})}\;f(\bar{u}^{\rm ii},\bar{u}^{\rm i})f(\bar{v}^{\rm i},\bar{v}^{\rm ii})\\

N_{\rm ii}=N_{\boldsymbol{2}}+N_{\boldsymbol{3}}+1
. The functions

and

S^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{N_{\rm i}}(\bar{v}^{\rm i},\bar{u}^{\rm i}|\beta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{u}^{\rm i}\mapsto\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{4}}\}\\
\bar{v}^{\rm i}\mapsto\{\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}}\}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{K}_{N_{\boldsymbol{1}}}(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}}|\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}})\mathbb{K}_{N_{\boldsymbol{4}}}(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{4}}|\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}})e^{N_{\boldsymbol{4}}\beta}f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{4}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{4}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{4}}),

(A.22)

and

S^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{N_{\rm ii}}(\bar{u}^{\rm ii},\bar{v}^{\rm ii}|0)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{u}^{\rm ii}\mapsto\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}\}\\

A.3
Determinant representation for the field form factor

Summation over partitions in (
A.22
) and (
A.23
) is based on two auxiliary lemmas. These lemmas were proved in
[
52
]
(see also
[
14
]
) and represent identities for the highest coefficient
\mathbb{K}_{N}(\bar{v}|\bar{u})
(
3.2
).

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\bar{\xi}
,
\bar{y}
and
\bar{z}
be sets of complex parameters with
\#\bar{y}=m_{1}
,

\#\bar{z}=m_{2}
, and
\#\bar{\xi}=m_{1}+m_{2}
. Then

\sum_{\bar{\xi}\mapsto\{\bar{\xi}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{\xi}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}\}}\mathbb{K}_{m_{1}}(\bar{\xi}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}|\bar{y})\mathbb{K}_{m_{2}}(\bar{z}|\bar{\xi}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})f(\bar{\xi}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI},\bar{\xi}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})=(-1)^{m_{1}}f(\bar{\xi},\bar{y})\mathbb{K}_{m_{1}+m_{2}}(\bar{y}-\eta,\bar{z}|\bar{\xi}).

(A.24)

The sum is taken with respect to all partitions of the set
\bar{\xi}
into disjoint
subsets
\bar{\xi}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}
and
\bar{\xi}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}
with
\#\bar{\xi}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}=m_{1}
and
\#\bar{\xi}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}=m_{2}
.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\bar{w}
and
\bar{\xi}
be two sets of generic complex numbers with
\#\bar{w}=\#\bar{\xi}=m
. Let
also
C_{1}(w)
and
C_{2}(w)
be two arbitrary functions of a complex variable
w
. Then

\sum_{\bar{w}\mapsto\{\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}\}}\mathbb{K}_{m}(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}-\eta,\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}|\bar{\xi})f(\bar{\xi},\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})f(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI},\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})C_{1}(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})C_{2}(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})\\
=\Delta^{\prime}(\bar{\xi})\Delta(\bar{w})\det_{m}\Bigl(C_{2}(w_{k})t(w_{k},\xi_{j})h(w_{k},\bar{\xi})+(-1)^{m}C_{1}(w_{k})t(\xi_{j},w_{k})h(\bar{\xi},w_{k})\Bigr).

(A.25)

Here the sum is taken over all possible partitions of the set
\bar{w}
into disjoint subsets
\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}

and
\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}
. We have also extended the convention on the shorthand notation (
2.10
) to the products of functions
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.

The proofs of lemma
A.1
and lemma
A.2
are given in
[
52
]
(see also
[
14
]
).

Corollary A.1
.

Under the conditions and with the notation of lemma
A.2

\sum_{\bar{w}\mapsto\{\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}\}}\mathbb{K}_{m}(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}-\eta,\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI}|\bar{\xi})f(\bar{\xi},\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})f(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})C_{1}(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})C_{2}(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})\\
=\Delta^{\prime}(\bar{\xi})\Delta(\bar{w})\det_{m}\Bigl(C_{2}(w_{k})t(w_{k},\xi_{j})h(w_{k},\bar{\xi})+(-1)^{m}C_{1}(w_{k})\frac{f(w_{k},\bar{w}_{k})}{f(\bar{w}_{k},w_{k})}t(\xi_{j},w_{k})h(\bar{\xi},w_{k})\Bigr).

(A.26)

Proof.
Let us introduce a function
\tilde{C}_{1}(w_{k})
by

\tilde{C}_{1}(w_{k})=C_{1}(w_{k})\frac{f(w_{k},\bar{w}_{k})}{f(\bar{w}_{k},w_{k})}.

(A.27)

Then obviously,

C_{1}(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})f(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I},\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI})=\tilde{C}_{1}(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I})f(\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I\hskip-1.0ptI},\bar{w}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm I}),

(A.28)

and we can use lemma
A.2
, replacing there
C_{1}
with
\tilde{C}_{1}
.
\square

Now summation over partitions in (
A.22
) and (
A.23
) becomes straightforward. First of all, we note that equations (
A.22
) and (
A.23
) differ from each other only in the replacement
\bar{v}\leftrightarrow\bar{u}
and the subset indices. Therefore, it is enough to calculate one of these sums.

Let
\bar{u}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\}
and
\bar{v}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1}\}
. Define a function
S^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}(\bar{v},\bar{u}|\beta)
by

S^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}(\bar{v},\bar{u}|\beta)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{u}\mapsto\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}\\
\bar{v}\mapsto\{\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{K}_{n_{\boldsymbol{1}}}(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}}|\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}})\mathbb{K}_{n_{\boldsymbol{2}}}(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}|\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}})e^{n_{\boldsymbol{2}}\beta}f(\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}).

(A.29)

Here the sum is taken over partitions of the set
\bar{u}
into three subsets
\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}
and the set
\bar{v}
into two
subsets
\{\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}
. These partitions are independent except that
\#\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}}=1
,
\#\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{1}}=\#\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}}=n_{\boldsymbol{1}}
,
\#\bar{v}^{\boldsymbol{2}}=\#\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}=n_{\boldsymbol{2}}=n-n_{\boldsymbol{1}}-1
, and
n_{\boldsymbol{1}}=0,1,\dots,n-1
.

Obviously, sum (
A.29
) differs from sum (
A.22
) only in notation.
Applying lemma
A.1
to the sum over partitions of
\bar{v}
in (
A.29
) we obtain

S^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}(\bar{v},\bar{u}|\beta)=\sum_{\bar{u}\mapsto\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}}\mathbb{K}_{n-1}(\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}}-\eta,\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}|\bar{v})(-1)^{n_{\boldsymbol{1}}}e^{n_{\boldsymbol{2}}\beta}f(\bar{v},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}).

(A.30)

Let us introduce a union
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}=\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}=\bar{u}\setminus\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
. Then we can think that the summation in (
A.30
) occurs in two stages:

\sum_{\bar{u}\mapsto\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}}=\sum_{\bar{u}\mapsto\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}\}}\sum_{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}\mapsto\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}}\;.

(A.31)

In the first stage, we split the set
\bar{u}
into subsets
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}}
and
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}
, so that
\#\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}}=1
. In the second stage, the summation occurs over all partitions of the set
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}
into
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}}
and
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}
. It is to this sum that we can apply corollary
A.1
. We set

C_{1}(u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k})=-f(u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}}),\qquad C_{2}(u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k})=e^{\beta}f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k}),

(A.32)

where
u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k}
means the
k
th element of the subset
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}
. Then after simple algebra, we obtain

\sum_{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}\mapsto\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}}\mathbb{K}_{n-1}(\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}}-\eta,\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}}\}|\bar{v})(-1)^{n_{\boldsymbol{1}}}e^{n_{\boldsymbol{2}}\beta}f(\bar{v},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{1}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{2}})\\
=\Delta^{\prime}(\bar{v})\Delta(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})h(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}},\bar{v})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})\det_{n-1}\mathcal{M}_{jk}(\bar{v},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}),

(A.33)

where
\mathcal{M}_{jk}(\bar{v},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})
is an
(n-1)\times(n-1)
matrix

\mathcal{M}_{jk}(\bar{v},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})=e^{\beta}t(u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k},v_{j})-t(v_{j},u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k})\frac{h(u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k},\bar{u})h(\bar{v},u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k})}{h(\bar{u},u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k})h(u^{\boldsymbol{3}}_{k},\bar{v})}.

(A.34)

Thus, the sum over partitions (
A.30
) turns into

S^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}(\bar{v},\bar{u}|\beta)=\sum_{\bar{u}\mapsto\{\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}\}}\Delta^{\prime}(\bar{v})\Delta(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})h(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}},\bar{v})f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})\det_{n-1}\mathcal{M}_{jk}(\bar{v},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}),

(A.35)

where
\#\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}}=1
and
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}=\bar{u}\setminus\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}}
.

It is easy to see that the remaining sum (
A.35
) is nothing but an expansion of
\det_{n}\mathcal{M}^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{jk}
(
3.14
) with respect to the last row.
Indeed, let
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}}=u_{m}
, where
m=1,\dots,n
. Then
\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}}=\bar{u}_{m}
. In its turn, the determinant of the matrix
\mathcal{M}_{jk}
is a minor of the matrix
\mathcal{M}^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{jk}
, which is obtained by removing the last row and the
m
th column:

\det_{n-1}\mathcal{M}_{jk}(\bar{v},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})=\det_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq n\\
k\neq m\end{subarray}}\mathcal{M}^{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{jk}(\bar{v},\bar{u}).

(A.36)

Using
f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})=g(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})h(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u})
we also have

f(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{0}},\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})\Delta(\bar{u}^{\boldsymbol{3}})=g(u_{m},\bar{u}_{m})h(u_{m},\bar{u})\Delta(\bar{u}_{m})=(-1)^{m+n}h(u_{m},\bar{u})\Delta(\bar{u}).

(A.37)

Substituting this into (
A.35
