Reduced Power Graphs of P ​ G ​ L n ​ ( 𝔽 q ) PGL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
Yilong Yang

Abstract
Given a group G G , let us connect two non-identity elements by an edge if and only if one is a power of another. This gives a graph structure on G G minus identity, called the reduced power graph. It is conjectured by Akbari and Ashrafi that if a non-abelian finite simple group has connected reeuced power graph, then it must be an alternating group. In this paper, we shall give a complete descprition about when the reduced power graphs of P ​ G ​ L n ​ ( 𝔽 q ) PGL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}) is connected for all q q and all n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . In particular, the conjectured by Akbari and Ashrafi is false. We shall also provide an upper bound in their diameters, and in case of disconnection, provide a description of all connected components.

Introduction

1.1
Background and Main Results

Graphical representations of an algebraic structure is always a fascinating research topic. The most well-known class of graphs is the Cayley graphs of finitely generated groups, which has been studied for a long time. Recently, some other graphical representations of groups have also been gathering more attention from researchers. Around 2000, the concept of a directed power graph was introduced by Kelarev and Quinn
[
5
]
as a new kinds of graphical representations, and later on Chakrabarty et al.
[
2
]
introduced the undirected power graph of a group.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a group
G
, the
directed power graph
of
G
has all elements of
G
as vertices, and two distinct vertices
g,h
are connected by an edge from
g
to
h
if and only if
h
is a power of
g
. The
(undirected) power graph
of
G
is the same graph but we drop all directions on all edges. The
reduced power graph
of
G
is obtained from the undirected power graph by deleting the identity element.

In this paper, by power graph we shall always mean the undirected power graph, unless specifically said otherwise.

Power graphs have been shown to capture many algebraic properties of a group. For example, the power graph of a finite group is a tree if and only if every element of the group is its own inverse
[
11
]
. If a finite group’s power graph lack particular kinds of subgraphs, then its isomorphism types can be classified
[
3
]
. A finite group is a non-cyclic group of prime exponent if and only if its power graph is non-complete and minimally edge-connected
[
8
]
. For more results of this type, see
[
6
]
.

One interesting aspect of the power graph is its connectivity. For finite groups, obviously power graphs are always connected,because every element is connected to the identity element. For this purpose, it is more interesting to consider the connectivity of the reduced power graph, i.e., power graph with the identity element removed. In particular, Akbari and Ashrafi proposed the following conjecture in 2015.

Conjectures 1.2

(Akbari and Ashrafi,
[
1
]
)
.

The reduced power graph of a non-abelian simple group
G
is connected only if
G
is isomorphic to some alternating group
A_{n}
.

In this paper, we shall show that this conjecture is false.

1.2
Notations and Outline of Proof

We use
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to denote the field with
q
elements, and
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
to denote the group of invertible
n\times n
matrices on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We use
Z(\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
to denote the center of the group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, i.e., the subgroup made of scalar multiples of identity. We use
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
to denote the quotient group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})/Z(\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
. Throughout this paper, we use
I_{k}
to denote the
k\times k
identity matrix.

In order to study the reduced power graphs of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, we shall study a special graph on
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, which has the reduced power graph of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
as a quotient graph.

Definition 1.5
.

Given a group
G
, let
Z
be its center. Then the projectively reduced power graph of
G
is obtained from the undirected power graph of
G
by deleting vertices in
Z
.

Proposition 1.6
.

Given a group
G
, let
Z
be its center. Let
\Gamma
be projective reduced power graph of
G
, and let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be the reduced power graph of
G/Z
. If
\Gamma
is connected, then
\Gamma^{\prime}
is connected with the same or less diameter.

Proof.

\Gamma^{\prime}
is a quotient graph of
\Gamma
.
∎

Therefore, to establish connectivity, diameters and distances on the reduced power graph of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, it is enough to establish the same statements in Theorem
1.3
on the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

In order to achieve connection, we first need to identify elements of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
that are connected to as much other elements as possible. Note that if
A
connected to
B
in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, then
B
must be a power or a root of
A
. Therefore
B
must be in the centralizer of
A
. Consequently, to search for elements with many connections, we can try to find elements with large centralizers. We shall call these the
pivot matrices
.

Definition 1.7
.

For a prime power
q
and an integer
n\geq 2
, an
n\times n
matrix
A
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is called a
pivot matrix
if
A
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and
A
has exactly two eigenvalues not counting multiplicity. An
n\times n
matrix
A
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is called a
Jordan pivot matrix
if all eigenvalues of
A
are
1
, and
\dim\mathrm{Ker}(A-I)=n-1
.

In particular, pivot matrices are those similar to
\begin{bmatrix}x&&&&&\\
&\ddots&&&&\\
&&x&&&\\
&&&y&&\\
&&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&&y\end{bmatrix}
for some distinct
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. Jordan pivot matrices are those similar to
\begin{bmatrix}1&&1\\
&\ddots&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
.

Our focus is on the pivot matrices when
q\neq 2
. But for
q=2
, there are no pivot matrices, so we have to resort to Jordan pivot matrices. (And sometimes, even for
q\neq 2
, connections via Jordan pivot matrices might also give a shorter distance.) All these matrices are important, because they all have big centralizers.

In particular, in Section
3
, we shall show that all (Jordan) pivot matrices in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
are connected to each other by some short path if
n
is large enough. (We need

when
q\neq 2
, and
n\geq 4
when
q=2
.)

In Section
5
, we shall show that almost all matrices in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
have paths to (Jordan) pivot matrices, with exceptions outlined in Section
4
. These results will establish Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Combine Proposition
3.1
and Proposition
5.4
.

Prime powers

Lemma 2.1
.

If

Proof.

This is a easy and special case of Catalan’s conjecture, proven by Mihăilescu

]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
q
and
q-1
are both prime powers, and
q\neq 2
. For any positive integer

,

has a prime factor coprime to

, unless

.

Note that
q-1
is a prime power. So if

is also a prime power, hence

and

Since

and

must have a proper factor

, the only possibility is

.

2.2
Matrices over finite fields

In this subsection, we focus on
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
whose minimal polynomial is irreducible of degree

. Note that such a matrix must be similar to the campanion matrix of an irreducible polynomial.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose the characteristic polynomial of a matrix
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is irreducible. Then polynomials of
C
of degree at most
n-1
form a field
\mathbb{F}_{q}[C]
isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
under the usual matrix addition and matrix multiplication. In particular,

is invertible, with multiplicative order dividing

Proof.

It is easy to very that this is a field with
q^{n}
elements.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose the characteristic polynomial of a matrix
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is irreducible, and the multiplicative order of
C
is
k
. For any positive integer
t
, the minimal polynomial of
C^{t}
is irreducible. For any factor
t
of
k
, if
\frac{k}{t}
is a factor of
q^{m}-1
, then the minimal polynomial of
C^{t}
is irreducible with degree at most
m
.

Proof.

Let the characteristic polynomial of
C
be
f(x)
. Since this is irreducible,
f(x)
is also the minimal polynomial for
C
.

Suppose the minimal polynomial of
C^{t}
is reducible, say it is
g(x)h(x)
for polynomials
g(x),h(x)
of degree at least one. Then we must have
g(C^{t})h(C^{t})=0
, and thus the minimal polynomial
f(x)
of
C
must divide
g(x^{t})h(x^{t})
. Since
f(x)
is irreducible,
f(x)
must divide
g(x^{t})
or
h(x^{t})
. But this means we have
g(C^{t})=0
or
h(C^{t})=0
, contradicting the fact that
g(x)h(x)
is supposed to be the minimal polynomial for
C^{t}
. Hence the minimal polynomial of
C^{t}
is irreducible.

Now suppose
t
is a factor of
k
, then
C^{t}
has order
\frac{k}{t}
, which is a factor of
q^{m}-1
, therefore in the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}[C]
,
C^{t}
is contained in the field with
q^{m}-1
elements. Hence its minimal polynomial has degree at most
m
.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose the characteristic polynomial of a matrix
C\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is irreducible, and the multiplicative order of
C
is
k
. For any factor
t
of
k
,
\frac{k}{t}
is a factor of
q-1
if and only if
C^{t}
is a scalar multiple of identity.

Corollary 2.6
.

Suppose
C
is an
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,

, and its characteristic polynomial is irreducible. If

is a scalar multiple of identity for a prime number

, then

is the smallest positive integer such that

divides

Proof.

Let

be the smallest positive integer such that

divides

. We shall show that

.

Since

and the characteristic polynomial of

is irreducible, by Corollary

, the multiplicative order of

cannot divide

, but must divide

. On the other hand, since

is a scalar multiple of identity,

. So the multiplicative order of

. Together, this means the least common multiple of

and the multiplicative order of

.

Suppose

is a scalar multiple of identity,

. So

Suppose the characteristic polynomial of a matrix

2.3
Generalized Jordan canonical form over finite fields

We have the following well-known results for matrices over any field.

Theorem 2.10
.

For any matrix
A
over a field
K
,
A
is similar to a block diagonal matrix
\begin{bmatrix}J_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&J_{t}\end{bmatrix}
over
K
, such that each diagonal block is
J_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{i}&N&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&N\\
&&&C_{i}\end{bmatrix}
for a companion matrix
C_{i}
of some irreducible polynomial over
K
, and
N=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\iddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&0\\
1&0&\dots&0\end{bmatrix}
.

This block diagonal matrix is called the generalized Jordan canonical form of
A
over the field
K
, and it is unique up to permutations of the diagonal blocks. For more detail, see for example
[
9
]
.

Since the focus of this paper is on matrices over finite fields, we can actually obtain a better result, by combining the following two statements.

Proposition 2.11
.

Every irreducible polynomial over a finite field is separable.

Proof.

Commonly found in abstract algebra textbooks, say
[
4
]
.
∎

Proposition 2.12

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
C
be a companion matrix of an irreducible polynomial

over a field
K
. Again let

\vdots&\vdots&\dots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&0\\
1&0&\dots&0\end{bmatrix}

\begin{bmatrix}C&N&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&N\\
&&&C\end{bmatrix}
and
\begin{bmatrix}C&I&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&I\\
&&&C\end{bmatrix}
are similar if and only if

is separable.

Combining these two statements, we have the follower prettier version of generalized Jordan canonical form over finite fields.

for a companion matrix
C_{i}
of some irreducible polynomial over
K
.

This version of canonical form is much easier to calculate.
We establish some easy results here for later use.

Lemma 2.14
.

If
A
is an
n\times n
matrix whose minimal polynomial is an irreducible polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of degree
n
, and
B=A^{k}
for some positive integer
k
. Then the generalized Jordan canonical form of
B
is block diagonal with identical diagonal blocks, each block is a companion matrix to the same irreducible polynomial.

Proof.

This is simply Lemma
2.4
in terms of generalized Jordan canonical form.
∎

Lemma 2.15
.

If
A
is an
n\times n
matrix whose characteristic polynomial is an irreducible or has an irreducible factor of degree
n-1
, and
B^{k}=A
for some positive integer
k
. Then the characteristic polynomial of
B
is irreducible or has an irreducible factor of degree
n-1
, respectively.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositives. If
B
has reducible characteristic polynomial, then the generalized Jordan canonical form of
B
will be
\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&*&*\\
&\ddots&*\\
&&C_{t}\end{bmatrix}
for some companion matrices
C_{1},\dots,C_{t}
and
t\geq 2
. Therefore
A
will be similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}^{k}&*&*\\
&\ddots&*\\
&&C_{t}^{k}\end{bmatrix}
, and thus its characteristic polynomial is also reducible.

The other statement about
n-1
can be proven similarly.
∎

3
Connections between Pivot Matrices

In this section, we shall show that all (Jordan) pivot matrices are connected by a short path in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, when
n
is not too small. In particular, we should establish the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
, in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, all pivot matrices has distance at most
8
to each other.

For
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
or for
q=2
and
n\geq 4
, in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, all Jordan pivot matrices has distance at most
8
to each other.

Proof.

Combine Proposition
3.6
, Proposition
3.10
and Proposition
3.13
.
∎

As a special remark, the proofs here rely fundamentally on the fact that these (Jordan) piviot matrices have big centralizers. In fact, every matrix in the group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
can be written as the product of at most five matrices in the centralizers of some Jordan pivot matrices and some pivot matrices.

3.1
From Jordan to Jordan

We only consider the cases for
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
, or for
q=2
and
n\geq 4
.

We consider the group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. In this group, pick
J=\begin{bmatrix}1&&1\\
&\ddots&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
, which is a Jordan pivot matrix. We also pick
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&&&&\\
&x&&&\\
&&1&&\\
&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&1\end{bmatrix}
, where
x\neq 1\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
for
q\neq 2
, and
x
is a block
\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
when
q=2
. Note that for
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
, or for
q=2
and
n\geq 4
, we always have
AJ=JA
. We also always have
A^{q^{2}-1}=I
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
or for
q=2
and
n\geq 4
,
A,J
are both powers of
AJ
. In particular,
A
and
J
have distance
2
in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

Proof.

Let
p
be the characteristic of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Since
AJ=JA
,
J^{p}=I
and
A^{q^{2}-1}=I
, we have
(AJ)^{q^{2}}=A
.

On the other hand,
J^{q^{2}-1}=J^{-1}
. So we have
(AJ)^{(q^{2}-1)^{2}}=J
.
∎

Let
\mathrm{Comm}(A)
and
\mathrm{Comm}(J)
be the centralizer of the two elements in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
respectively. We adopt the notation that for any two subsets
H,K
of a group
G
, then
HK
stands for the the subset
\{hk\in G:h]inH,k\in K\}
. Our goal here is to show that these two centralizers can generate
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
quickly, and specifically
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)
. Our first step is to show that, most of the time,
\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)
is enough.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
or for
q=2
and
n\geq 4
, suppose a matrix
X\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
has non-zero lower left entry and invertible lower left
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block, then
X\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)
.

Proof.

This is essentially the LU decomposition.

Pick
T=\begin{bmatrix}&&1\\
&I_{n-2}&\\
1&&\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
. Then
TX
has non-zero upper left entry, and invertible upper left
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block. Consequently, it has a unique block LDU decomposition
TX=LDU
where
L=\begin{bmatrix}1&&\\
*&I_{n-2}&\\
*&*&1\end{bmatrix}
and
U=\begin{bmatrix}1&*&*\\
&U_{n-2}&*\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
and
D=\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}&&\\
&I_{n-2}&\\
&&d_{n}\end{bmatrix}
for some
(n-2)\times(n-2)
invertible matrix
U_{n-2}
and some
d_{1},d_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. Note that
TLT,U\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)
and
TD\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
.

So
X=TLDU=(TLT)(TD)U\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)
.
∎

Our second step is to show that every matrix can be adjusted to the case as described in Lemma
3.3
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
or for
q=2
and
n\geq 4
, and any
X\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, we can find a unit upper triangular matrix
U
and a matrix
T=\begin{bmatrix}&&1\\
&I_{n-2}&\\
1&&\end{bmatrix}
, such that
XUT
has non-zero lower left entry, and invertible lower left
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block.

Proof.

Consider
X=\begin{bmatrix}*&*&*\\
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}&\dots&\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\end{bmatrix}
where
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-1}
. Since
X
is invertible, these vectors cannot all have zero last coordinate. So
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}_{n}+u_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\dots+u_{n-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{n-1}
will have non-zero last coordinate for some constants
u_{1},\dots,u_{n-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Set
U_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&&&u_{1}\\
&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&1&u_{n-1}\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}
. Then
XU_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}*&*&*&*\\
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}&\dots&\boldsymbol{x}_{n-1}&\boldsymbol{y}\end{bmatrix}
will have non-zero lower right entry.

Let
V
be the space spanned by
\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{y}
. If
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-1}
, then
XU_{1}
will also have invertible lower right
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block. So
XU_{1}T
is as desired.

Suppose that is not the case. Since
X
is invertible, we must have
\dim V=n-2
, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\notin V
. Now
\boldsymbol{y}
has non-zero last coordinate, so
\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
. Since the collection
\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{y}
spans
V
and since
\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, therefore we can find a subcollection of
n-2
vectors including
\boldsymbol{y}
that already spans
V
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
be the excluded vector for some
2\leq i\leq n-1
.

Then since
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\notin V
, we must have
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\notin V
. So if we set
U_{2}
be the unit upper triangular matrix that corresponds to the elementary column operation of adding the first column to the
i
-th column, then
XU_{1}U_{2}
will now have non-zero lower right entry and invertible lower right
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block. So
XU_{1}U_{2}T
is as desired.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
or for
q=2
and
n\geq 4
,

\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J).

Proof.

Pick any
X\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. By Lemma
3.4
, we can find a unit upper triangular matrix
U
and
T=\begin{bmatrix}&&1\\
&I_{n-2}&\\
1&&\end{bmatrix}
, such that
XUT
has non-zero lower left entry, and invertible lower left
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block. Then by Lemma
3.3
, we see that
XUT\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)
. However, we also see that
U\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)
and
T\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
. So our result follows.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
or for
q=2
and
n\geq 4
, any two Jordan pivot matrices in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
will have distance at most
8
.

Proof.

Suppose
J_{1},J_{2}
are two Jordan pivot matrices. Say
J_{1}=X_{1}JX_{1}^{-1}
and
J_{2}=X_{2}JX_{2}^{-1}
for invertible matrices
X_{1},X_{2}
. Set
X=X_{1}^{-1}X_{2}
.

By Lemma
3.5
, we have
X=M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}M_{5}
where
M_{1},M_{3},M_{5}\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)
and
M_{2},M_{4}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
.

Now we have the following path.

\displaystyle J_{1}=

\displaystyle X_{1}JX_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}JM_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}AM_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}AM_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}JM_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}JM_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}AM_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}AM_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}JM_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}M_{5}JM_{5}^{-1}M_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{1}XJX^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{2}JX_{2}^{-1}=J_{2}.

∎

3.2
From Pivot to Pivot when
n\geq 4

When
q\neq 2
, it is more important to have a path between pivot matrices. For now, we only consider the cases for
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 4
.

As before, we consider the group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. In this group, pick
J=\begin{bmatrix}1&&1\\
&\ddots&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
, which is a Jordan pivot matrix. We also pick
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&&&&\\
&x&&&\\
&&1&&\\
&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&1\end{bmatrix}
, where
x\neq 1\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
.

We first establish a version of Lemma
3.3
with a weaker requirement. However, this only works for
n\geq 4
.

Lemma 3.7
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 4
, suppose a matrix
X\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
has non-zero lower left entry, then
X\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)
.

Proof.

Since the lower left entry of
X
is non-zero, using elementary row and column operations, we can find unit upper triangular matrices
U_{1},U_{2}
such that
U_{1}XU_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\begin{matrix}*&\dots\end{matrix}&*\\
\begin{matrix}\vdots\\
0\end{matrix}&X^{\prime}&\begin{matrix}\vdots\\
*\end{matrix}\\
x_{n1}&\begin{matrix}0&\dots\end{matrix}&0\end{bmatrix}
for some
(n-2)\times(n-2)
matrix
X^{\prime}
and some
x_{n1}\neq 0
.

Since
U_{1}XU_{2}
is invertible, its upper right
(n-1)\times(n-1)
block will have rank
n-1
. Hence
X^{\prime}
has rank at least
n-3
. When
n\geq 4
, this implies that
X^{\prime}
is not zero. So
R^{\prime}_{1}X^{\prime}R^{\prime}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\\
&X^{\prime\prime}\end{bmatrix}
for some invertible
(n-2)\times(n-2)
matrices
R^{\prime}_{1},R^{\prime}_{2}
and some
(n-3)\times(n-3)
matrix
X^{\prime\prime}
. Set
R_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&&\\
&R^{\prime}_{1}&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
and
R_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&&\\
&R^{\prime}_{2}&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
, then
R_{1}U_{1}XU_{2}R_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&*&\begin{matrix}*&\dots&*\end{matrix}&*\\
0&1&\begin{matrix}0&\dots&0\end{matrix}&*\\
\begin{matrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{matrix}&X^{\prime\prime}&\begin{matrix}*\\
\vdots\\
*\end{matrix}\\
x_{n1}&0&\begin{matrix}0&\dots&0\end{matrix}&0\end{bmatrix}
.

Then using elementary row and column operations, we can find unit upper triangular matrices
U_{3},U_{4}
such that
U_{3}R_{1}U_{1}XU_{2}R_{2}U_{4}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\begin{matrix}*&\dots&*\end{matrix}&*\\
0&1&\begin{matrix}0&\dots&0\end{matrix}&0\\
\begin{matrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{matrix}&X^{\prime\prime}&\begin{matrix}*\\
\vdots\\
*\end{matrix}\\
x_{n1}&0&\begin{matrix}0&\dots&0\end{matrix}&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
.

Since
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3},U_{4},R_{1},R_{2}\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)
, we are done.
∎

Now a pivot matrix is diagonalizable, but its diagonal form does not necessarily looks like
A
. So the analogue to Lemma
3.5
are the followings.

Lemma 3.8
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
, in the group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, we pick an arbitrary
A_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}I_{n-m_{1}-1}&&\\
&y_{1}I_{m_{1}}&\\
&&x_{1}\end{bmatrix}
and
A_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{2}I_{n-m_{2}-1}&&\\
&y_{2}I_{m_{2}}&\\
&&x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
for some positive integers
m_{1},m_{2}\leq n-2
, and some
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
such that
x_{1}\neq y_{1}
and
x_{2}\neq y_{2}
.

Suppose
m_{1}+m_{2}<n
, then
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\mathrm{Comm}(A_{1})\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A_{2})
.

Suppose
m_{1}=\frac{n}{2}
, then
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is the union of
\mathrm{Comm}(A_{1})\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A_{2})
and
P\mathrm{Comm}(A_{1})\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A_{2})
for a permutation matrix
P
such that
PA_{1}P^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}I_{n-m_{1}-1}&&\\
&x_{1}I_{m_{1}}&\\
&&y_{1}\end{bmatrix}
.

Proof.

For any
X\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, we break it down into block form
X=\begin{bmatrix}X_{11}&X_{12}&X_{13}\\
X_{21}&X_{22}&X_{23}\\
X_{31}&X_{32}&X_{33}\end{bmatrix}
where the rows are grouped into the first
n-m_{1}-1
rows, the next
m_{1}
rows, and the last row, and the columns are grouped into the first
n-m_{2}-1
columns, the next
m_{2}
columns, and the last column.

The submatrix
\begin{bmatrix}X_{11}&X_{13}\\
X_{31}&X_{33}\end{bmatrix}
has rank at least
n-m_{1}-m_{2}
. If
m_{1}+m_{2}<n
, then this submatrix is non-zero. So we can find invertible matrices
\begin{bmatrix}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
R_{21}&R_{22}\end{bmatrix}
and
\begin{bmatrix}R^{\prime}_{11}&R^{\prime}_{12}\\
R^{\prime}_{21}&R^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
such that
\begin{bmatrix}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
R_{21}&R_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X_{11}&X_{13}\\
X_{31}&X_{33}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R^{\prime}_{11}&R^{\prime}_{12}\\
R^{\prime}_{21}&R^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
has a non-zero lower left entry.

Let
R=\begin{bmatrix}R^{\prime}_{11}&&R^{\prime}_{12}\\
&I_{m_{1}}&\\
R^{\prime}_{21}&&R^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
and
R^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}R^{\prime}_{11}&&R^{\prime}_{12}\\
&I_{m_{2}}&\\
R^{\prime}_{21}&&R^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
. Then
RXR^{\prime}
will have non-zero lower left entry. So by Lemma
3.7
,
RXR^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)
. Since
R\in\mathrm{Comm}(A_{1})
and
R^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A_{2})
, we are done.

Now suppose
m_{1}=\frac{n}{2}
. Then
\begin{bmatrix}X_{13}\\
X_{23}\\
X_{33}\end{bmatrix}
is in fact the last column of
X
. Since
X
is invertible, entries in this column cannot all be zero. Let
P
be any permutation matrix such that
PA_{1}P^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}I_{n-m_{1}-1}&&\\
&x_{1}I_{m_{1}}&\\
&&y_{1}\end{bmatrix}
. Then
P
swaps the
\frac{n}{2}
rows involved in
X_{23}
with the
\frac{n}{2}
rows involved in
X_{13}
and
X_{33}
. Then
X
or
PX
will have a block structure
\begin{bmatrix}X^{\prime}_{11}&X^{\prime}_{12}&X^{\prime}_{13}\\
X^{\prime}_{21}&X^{\prime}_{22}&X^{\prime}_{23}\\
X^{\prime}_{31}&X^{\prime}_{32}&X^{\prime}_{33}\end{bmatrix}
where the submatrix
\begin{bmatrix}X^{\prime}_{11}&X^{\prime}_{13}\\
X^{\prime}_{31}&X^{\prime}_{33}\end{bmatrix}
is non-zero.

So we can find invertible matrices
\begin{bmatrix}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
R_{21}&R_{22}\end{bmatrix}
and
\begin{bmatrix}R^{\prime}_{11}&R^{\prime}_{12}\\
R^{\prime}_{21}&R^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
such that
\begin{bmatrix}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
R_{21}&R_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X^{\prime}_{11}&X^{\prime}_{13}\\
X^{\prime}_{31}&X^{\prime}_{33}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R^{\prime}_{11}&R^{\prime}_{12}\\
R^{\prime}_{21}&R^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
has a non-zero lower left entry.

Let
R=\begin{bmatrix}R^{\prime}_{11}&&R^{\prime}_{12}\\
&I_{n/2}&\\
R^{\prime}_{21}&&R^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
and
R^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}R^{\prime}_{11}&&R^{\prime}_{12}\\
&I_{n/2}&\\
R^{\prime}_{21}&&R^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
. Then
RXR^{\prime}
or
RPXR^{\prime}
will have non-zero lower left entry. So by Lemma
3.7
,
RXR^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)
or
RPXR^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)
.

Since
R\in\mathrm{Comm}(A_{1})
and
R^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A_{2})
, we see that
X
or
PX
is in
\mathrm{Comm}(A_{1})\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A_{2})
. So we are done.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
. Let
A^{\prime}
be a diagonal matrix whose upper left entry and lower right entry are identical. Then
A^{\prime},J
are both powers of
A^{\prime}J
. In particular,
A^{\prime}
and
J
have distance
2
in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

Proof.

Let
p
be the characteristic of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Since
A_{i}J=JA_{i}
,
J^{p}=I
and
A_{i}^{q-1}=I
, we have
(A_{i}J)^{q}=A_{i}
.

On the other hand,
J^{q-1}=J^{-1}
. So we have
(A_{i}J)^{(q-1)^{2}}=J
.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 4
, any two pivot matrices in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
will have distance at most
8
.

Proof.

Suppose we have two Jordan pivot matrices. Say they are
X_{1}A_{1}X_{1}^{-1}
and
X_{2}A_{2}X_{2}^{-1}
for invertible matrices
X_{1},X_{2}
, and
A_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}I_{n-m_{1}-1}&&\\
&y_{1}I_{m_{1}}&\\
&&x_{1}\end{bmatrix}
and
A_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{2}I_{n-m_{2}-1}&&\\
&y_{2}I_{m_{2}}&\\
&&x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
for some integers
m_{1},m_{2}\leq n-2
, and some
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
such that
x_{1}\neq y_{1}
and
x_{2}\neq y_{2}
. By choosing
X_{1},X_{2}
carefully, we may assume that
m_{1},m_{2}\leq\frac{n}{2}
. Then we must either have
m_{1}+m_{2}<n
, or we must have
m_{1}=m_{2}=\frac{n}{2}
.

Set
X=X_{1}^{-1}X_{2}
. By Lemma
3.8
, we must either have
X=M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}M_{5}
or have
PX=M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}M_{5}
, where
M_{1}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A_{1}),M_{3}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A),M_{5}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A_{2})
and
M_{2},M_{4}\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)
, and
P
is a permutation matrix such that
PA_{1}P^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}I_{n-m_{1}-1}&&\\
&x_{1}I_{m_{1}}&\\
&&y_{1}\end{bmatrix}
.

Suppose we have
X=M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}M_{5}
. Then we have the following path.

\displaystyle X_{1}A_{1}X_{1}^{-1}=

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}A_{1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}JM_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}JM_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}AM_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}AM_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}JM_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}JM_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}A_{2}M_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}M_{5}A_{2}M_{5}^{-1}M_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{1}XA_{2}X^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{2}A_{2}X_{2}^{-1}.

Now suppose we have
PX=M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}M_{5}
. Let
A^{\prime}_{1}=PA_{1}P^{-1}
. Then we have the following path.

\displaystyle X_{1}A_{1}X_{1}^{-1}=

\displaystyle X_{1}P^{-1}A^{\prime}_{1}PX_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}P^{-1}M_{1}A^{\prime}_{1}M_{1}^{-1}PX_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}P^{-1}M_{1}JM_{1}^{-1}PX_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}P^{-1}M_{1}M_{2}JM_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}PX_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}P^{-1}M_{1}M_{2}AM_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}PX_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}P^{-1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}AM_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}PX_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}P^{-1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}JM_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}PX_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}P^{-1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}JM_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}PX_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}P^{-1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}A_{2}M_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}PX_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{1}P^{-1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}M_{5}A_{2}M_{5}^{-1}M_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}PX_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{1}P^{-1}PXA_{2}X^{-1}P^{-1}PX_{1}^{-1}=X_{2}A_{2}X_{2}^{-1}.

∎

3.3
From Pivot to Pivot when
n=3

For
q\neq 2,n=3
, we do not have Lemma
3.7
. So we are forced to use Lemma
3.3
which requires a stronger assumption. For this end, we need a stronger version of Lemma
3.8
.

As before, we consider the group
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. In this group, pick
J=\begin{bmatrix}1&&1\\
&1&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
, which is a Jordan pivot matrix. We also pick
A=\begin{bmatrix}x&&\\
&y&\\
&&x\end{bmatrix}
for any
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
such that
x\neq y
. note that the specific values of
x,y
does not matter, because the centralizer
\mathrm{Comm}(A)
will always be the same.

Lemma 3.11
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n=3
, for any
X\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, we can find
R_{1},R_{2}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
such that
R_{1}XR_{2}
has non-zero lower left entry, and invertible lower left
2\times 2
block.

Proof.

Suppose
X=\begin{bmatrix}x_{11}&x_{12}&x_{13}\\
x_{21}&x_{22}&x_{23}\\
x_{31}&x_{32}&x_{33}\end{bmatrix}
. Then since
X
is invertible, the submatrix
\begin{bmatrix}x_{11}&x_{13}\\
x_{31}&x_{33}\end{bmatrix}
cannot be zero. So we can find
\begin{bmatrix}r_{11}&r_{12}\\
r_{21}&r_{22}\end{bmatrix}
and
\begin{bmatrix}r^{\prime}_{11}&r^{\prime}_{12}\\
r^{\prime}_{21}&r^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
such that
\begin{bmatrix}r_{11}&r_{12}\\
r_{21}&r_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{11}&x_{13}\\
x_{31}&x_{33}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}r^{\prime}_{11}&r^{\prime}_{12}\\
r^{\prime}_{21}&r^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
is either
\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
.

Let
R=\begin{bmatrix}r_{11}&&r_{12}\\
&1&\\
r_{21}&&r_{22}\end{bmatrix}
and
R^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}r^{\prime}_{11}&&r^{\prime}_{12}\\
&1&\\
r^{\prime}_{21}&&r^{\prime}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
, then
R,R^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
and
RXR^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}0&*&*\\
*&*&*\\
1&*&0\end{bmatrix}
where the upper right entry is
0
or
1
.

First, let us deal with the case
RXR^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}0&a&1\\
b&c&d\\
1&e&0\end{bmatrix}
.

If the lower left
2\times 2
block is already invertible, then we are done. If the upper right
2\times 2
block is invertible, then set
T=\begin{bmatrix}&&1\\
&1&\\
1&&\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
. We would then have
TRXR^{\prime}T
with non-zero lower left entry and invertible lower left
2\times 2
block as well. Since
T\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
as well, we are also done.

Now suppose that the lower left
2\times 2
block and the upper right
2\times 2
block of
RXR^{\prime}
are both not invertible. Since the lower left block is not invertible, we must have
a,d\neq 0
. Since the upper right block is not invertible, we must have
b,e\neq 0
. Finally, since both the lower left block and the upper right block must have determinant
0
, we must have
c=ad=be
. Set
L=\begin{bmatrix}1&&\\
&1&\\
1&&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
, then
RXR^{\prime}L=\begin{bmatrix}1&a&1\\
b+d&c&d\\
1&e&0\end{bmatrix}
, and its lower left
2\times 2
block has determinant
(b+d)e-c=de\neq 0
. So it is invertible, and we are done.

Second, let us deal with the case
RXR^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}0&a&0\\
b&c&d\\
1&e&0\end{bmatrix}
. Again if the lower left
2\times 2
block is already invertible, then we are done. Suppose that this is not the case, then
be-c=0

Since
RXR^{\prime}
is invertible, we must have
a,d\neq 0
. Set
L=\begin{bmatrix}1&&\\
&1&\\
1&&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
. If
b\neq 0
, then
LRXR^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}0&a&0\\
b&c&d\\
1&e+a&0\end{bmatrix}
, and the lower left block has determinant
b(e+a)-c=ab\neq 0
. So we are done. Similarly, if
e\neq 0
, then
RXR^{\prime}L=\begin{bmatrix}0&a&0\\
b+d&c&d\\
1&e&0\end{bmatrix}
, and the lower left block has determinant
(b+d)e-c=de\neq 0
. So we are done.

Suppose
b=e=0
. Since the lower left block should not be invertible, we must have
c=0
as well. So we have
RXR^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}0&a&0\\
0&0&d\\
1&0&0\end{bmatrix}
. Then
LRXR^{\prime}L=\begin{bmatrix}0&a&0\\
d&0&d\\
1&a&0\end{bmatrix}
, and the lower left block has determinant
ad\neq 0
. So we are done.

In all cases, we can find
R_{1},R_{2}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
such that
R_{1}XR_{2}
has non-zero lower left entry, and invertible lower left
2\times 2
block.
∎

Corollary 3.12
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n=3
,
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)
.

Proof.

For any
X\in\mathrm{GL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, by lemma
3.11
, we can find
R_{1},R_{2}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
such that
R_{1}XR_{2}
has non-zero lower left entry, and invertible lower left
2\times 2
block. The by Lemma
3.3
,
R_{1}XR_{2}\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)
. Therefore
X\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)\mathrm{Comm}(J)\mathrm{Comm}(A)
.
∎

Proposition 3.13
.

For
q\neq 2
and
n=3
, any two pivot matrices in the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
will have distance at most
8
.

Proof.

Suppose we have two pivot matrices. Note that a
3\times 3
pivot matrix must have exactly one eigenvalue with multiplicity
2
, and another eigenvalue with multiplicity
1
. Say they are
X_{1}A_{1}X_{1}^{-1}
and
X_{2}A_{2}X_{2}^{-1}
for invertible matrices
X_{1},X_{2}
, and
A_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&&\\
&y_{1}&\\
&&x_{1}\end{bmatrix}
and
A_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{2}&&\\
&y_{2}&\\
&&x_{2}\end{bmatrix}
for some
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
such that
x_{1}\neq y_{1}
and
x_{2}\neq y_{2}
. Note that we must have
\mathrm{Comm}(A_{1})=\mathrm{Comm}(A_{2})=\mathrm{Comm}(A)
.

Set
X=X_{1}^{-1}X_{2}
.

By Corollary
3.12
, we have
X=M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}M_{5}
where
M_{1},M_{3},M_{5}\in\mathrm{Comm}(A)
and
M_{2},M_{4}\in\mathrm{Comm}(J)
.

Now we have the following path.

\displaystyle X_{1}A_{1}X_{1}^{-1}=

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}A_{1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}JM_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}JM_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}AM_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}AM_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}JM_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}JM_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{distance 2}}

\displaystyle X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}A_{2}M_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}M_{4}M_{5}A_{2}M_{5}^{-1}M_{4}^{-1}M_{3}^{-1}M_{2}^{-1}M_{1}^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{1}XA_{2}X^{-1}X_{1}^{-1}=X_{2}A_{2}X_{2}^{-1}.

So we are done.
∎

4
Obstructions to connectivity

In this section, we show several types of obstructions to the connectivity of the reduced power graphs of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, and the projectively reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. As we shall see later, Proposition
4.1
, Proposition
4.2
and Proposition
4.4
are in fact the only obstructions when
q\neq 2
and
n\geq 3
.

For
q=2
, there are two extra types of obstructions by Proposition
4.5
and Proposition
4.6
. Again as we shall see later, they are the only extra obstructions for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
and
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
when
n\geq 6
.

Proposition 4.1

(Jordan-type obstruction)
.

Consider a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with characteristic
p
. If
2\leq n\leq p
, let
A
be any matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
similar to the
n\times n
matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&1\\
&&&\lambda\end{bmatrix}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. Then the image of
A
in the reduced power graph of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(q)
is trapped in a connected component with
p-1
vertices and diameter
1
, made of images of powers of
A
that are not scalar multiples of identity.

Proof.

We want to show that, in the reduced power graph of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, images of powers of
A
that are not scalar multiples of identity form a connected component. Then it is enough to show that, if
B
is a root of
A^{\prime}
, a scalar multiple of a power of
A
that is not a scalar multiple of identity, then
B
is a multiple of a power of
A
.

Note that since
n\leq p
,
A^{p}
is a scalar multiple of identity. Suppose
B^{k}=zA^{t}
for some
z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
and positive integers
k,t
such that
A^{t}
is not a scalar multiple of identity. Then
t
and
p
must be coprime. Hence
A
is a multiple of a power of
A^{t}
as well. So we may WLOG assume that
t=1
.

Suppose
B^{k}=zA
for some
z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
and a positive integer
k
. Then we have
AB=BA
. Hence
B
must be a polynomial of
A
. This implies that
B^{p}
is a scalar multiple of identity, say
B^{p}=bI
for some
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. In particular,
k
cannot be a multiple of
p
. So we can find a positive integer
k^{\prime}
such that
kk^{\prime}=mp+1
for some positive integer
m
. Then
z^{k^{\prime}}A^{k^{\prime}}=B^{kk^{\prime}}=b^{m}B
. So
B
is a scalar multiple of a power of
A
.

Hence images of powers of
A
that are not scalar multiples of identity form a connected component. They are the images of
A,A^{2},\dots,A^{p-1}
. They all corresponds to distinct vertices, and they are all powers of each other. Hence this connected component has
p-1
vertices and diameter
1
.
∎

Proposition 4.2

(Irredicible obstruction)
.

Let
n
be prime and
A
be a matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
whose characteristic polynomial is irreducible. Then the image of
A
in the reduced power graph of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(q)
is trapped in a connected component with
(q^{n}-q)/(q-1)
vertices, made of images of polynomials of
A
that are not scalar multiples of identity. The diameter of this component is at most
2
.

Proof.

We know polynomials of
A
form a field
\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]
with
q^{n}
elements. Pick
C
that generate
\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]^{*}
multiplicatively. Then all non-zero polynomials of
A
are powers of
C
. Clearly
C
is also similar to a companion matrix of an irreducible polynomial of degree
n
.

It is enough to show that, if
B
is a root of
C^{\prime}
, a scalar multiple of a power of
C
that is not a scalar multiple of identity, then
B
is a scalar multiple of a power of
C
. Then this would imply that images of polynomials of
A
(or equivalently, powers of
C
) that are not scalar multiples of identity form a connected component, where everyone is connected to
C
. Hence it will have
(q^{n}-q)/(q-1)
vertices and diameter at most
2
.

Suppose
B^{k}=zC^{t}
for some
z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
and positive integer
k,t
such that
C^{t}
is not a scalar multiple of identity. Note that by Lemma
2.14
, the generalized Jordan canonical form of
C^{t}
is made of identical diagonal blocks that are the same companion matrix of some irreducible polynomial. Since
n
is prime, either these blocks are
1\times 1
which implies that
C^{t}
is a scalar multiple of identity, or the block is
n\times n
. Only the latter case is possible. So
C^{t}
is similar to a companion matrix of an irreducible polynomial.

Now we have
BC^{t}=C^{t}B
. By Lemma
4.3
,
B
must be a polynomial of
C^{t}
. In particular,
B\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]
. Since we know
B^{k}=zC
, we know that
B
is invertible and hence non-zero. Therefore
B\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[C]^{*}
, and it must be a power of
C
. So we are done.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For
n\geq 2
, consider an
n\times n
companion matrix
A=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{e}_{2}&\boldsymbol{e}_{3}&\dots&\boldsymbol{e}_{n}&\boldsymbol{v}\end{bmatrix}
where
\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}_{n}
are the standard basis for
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
is an arbitrary vector in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. If
AB=BA
for some
n\times n
matrix
B
, then
B
is a polynomial of
A
.

Proof.

Let
B=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}&\dots&\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\end{bmatrix}
. Then we have

BA=\begin{bmatrix}B\boldsymbol{e}_{2}&B\boldsymbol{e}_{3}&\dots&B\boldsymbol{e}_{n}&B\boldsymbol{v}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{b}_{2}&\dots&\boldsymbol{b}_{n}&B\boldsymbol{v}\end{bmatrix},

AB=\begin{bmatrix}A\boldsymbol{b}_{1}&\dots&A\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence
AB=BA
implies that
\boldsymbol{b}_{i+1}=A\boldsymbol{b}_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. So we have

B=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}&A\boldsymbol{b}_{1}&\dots&A^{n-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

Now we set

B_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}&A\boldsymbol{e}_{i}&\dots&A^{n-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=A^{i-1}.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{b}_{1}=\sum x_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
for coefficients
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then
B=\sum x_{i}B_{i}=\sum x_{i}A^{i-1}
. Hence
B
is a polynomial of
A
.
∎

Proposition 4.4

(Diagonalizable obstruction)
.

Suppose
q>2
is a power of
2
with
q-1
prime, and
2\leq n<q
. Let
A
be an
n\times n
diagonalizable matrix with
n
distinct non-zero eigenvalues over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then the image of
A
in the reduced power graph of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(q)
is trapped in a connected component with
q-2
vertices and diameter
1
, made of images of non-identity powers of
A
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that, if
B
is a root of
A^{\prime}
, a scalar multiple of a power of
A
that is not a scalar multiple of identity, then
B
is a scalar multiple of a power of
A
. Then powers of
A
that are not scalar multiples of identity would form a connected component in the reduced power graph of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(q)
with vertices represented by
A,\dots,A^{q-2}
. None of these matrices can be scalar multiples of each other. They all have order
q-1
, which is prime, so they are all powers of each other. So this is a connected component with
q-2
vertices and diameter
1
.

Suppose
B^{k}=zA^{t}
for some
z\in\mathbb{F}^{*}
and positive integer
k,t
such that
A^{t}
is not a scalar multiple of identity. Note that if
A^{t}
is not a scalar multiple of identity, then
t
must be coprime to the prime number
q-1
. Hence
A^{t}
and
A
are powers of each other, and
A^{t}
also has
n
distinct non-zero eigenvalues over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then
BA^{t}=A^{t}B
implies that
A^{t},B
are simultaneously diagonalizable over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Suppose
A^{t}
has eigenvalues
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
and
B
has corresponding eigenvalues
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
such that
b_{i}^{k}=za_{i}
. Note that
q-1
is prime, so the multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
is cyclic of prime order. Hence if all
b_{i}^{k}
are distinct, we must have
k
coprime to
q-1
. Let
k^{\prime}
be a positive integer such that
kk^{\prime}
is
1
modulus
q-1
. Then
b_{i}=z^{k^{\prime}}a_{i}^{k^{\prime}}
. Hence we have
B=z^{k^{\prime}}A^{tk^{\prime}}
. So we are done.
∎

Finally, we discuss two special type of obstructions to connectivity when
q=2
. Note that in this case,
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(2)=\mathrm{GL}_{n}(2)
.

The following obstruction is essentially a variant of the obstruction above.

Proposition 4.5

(Quasi-diagonalizable obstruction when
q=2
)
.

Suppose we have a prime
p_{0}=2^{p_{1}}-1
(i.e., Mersenne prime) for some positive integer
p_{1}
.
Suppose
n\leq p_{0}
.
Let
A
be an
n\times n
non-identity matrix such that
A^{p_{0}}=I
, and its minimal polynomial equal to its characteristic polynomial. Then the
A
in the reduced power graph of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(2)
is trapped in a connected component with
p_{0}-1
vertices and diameter
1
, made of non-identity powers of
A
.

Note that in this case,
p_{1}
must be a prime factor of
n
or
n-1
.

Proof.

Set
q=p_{0}+1
. Then
q
is a power of
2
, so we can pick an extension
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Let us treat
A
as a matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
instead.

Then
A^{q-1}=I
implies that
A
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. And since its minimal polynomial is the same as its characteristic polynomials, it has
n
distinct non-zero eigenvalues over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and
2\leq n<q
, and
q-1
is a prime. So we are in the case of Proposition
4.4
. So
A
is trapped in a connected component even in the reduced power graph of
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(q)
, made of non-identity powers of
A
. Therefore in the subgraph made of matrices in
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(2)
,
A
is also trapped in the same connected component.

For the last statement, if
p_{0}=2^{p_{1}}-1
is a prime, then it is a Mersenne prime. Thus
p_{1}
is also prime.

Now if
A
has multiplicative order
p_{0}
, then by Lemma
5.2
, the generalized Jordan canonical form of
A
is block diagonal with diagonal blocks
C_{1},\dots,C_{t}
of companion matrices for irreducible polynomials. Since
A
has multiplicative order
p_{0}
, each
C_{i}
must have multiplicative order dividing
p_{0}
. So either
C_{i}
is
1\times 1
, or
C_{i}
is

The second one is a variant of Proposition

Proposition 4.6

(Extra irredicible obstruction when

)
.

Let

be prime and

vertices, made of non-identity powers of a matrix

. The diameter of this component is at most

.

Proof.

Consider the generalized Jordan canonical form of

, we must have

&1\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
for invertible

, where

is a companion matrix of an irreducible polynomial of degree

.

We know polynomials of

form a field
\mathbb{F}_{q}[A^{\prime}]
with

elements. Pick

that generate

multiplicatively. Then

is a power of
C^{\prime}
. Then all non-zero polynomials of

are powers of
C^{\prime}
.
Let

&1\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
, then

is similar to a power of

.

It is enough to show that, if

is a root of

, a scalar multiple of a power of

is a scalar multiple of a power of

. Then this would imply that non-identity powers of

form a connected component, where everyone is connected to

. Since

has multiplicative order

, this connected component will have

.

Suppose
B^{k}=C^{t}
for some positive integers

such that

&1\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
is not identity. In particular,

is not identity. By Lemma

, the generalized Jordan canonical form of

is made of identical diagonal blocks that are the same companion matrix of some irreducible polynomial. Since

Now we have

&1\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}

Proposition 4.7
.

In Proposition
4.2
, the diameter of each connected component is

if and only if

In either case, the connected component is made of powers of the same element. So this follows by the property of cyclic groups below in Lemma

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
q
be a power of a prime
p
, and pick any positive integer
n\geq 3
. In a cyclic group
G
with
q^{n}-1
elements, if we can find two subgroups
H,K
such that neither contains the other, and neither is the subgroup whose order divides
q-1
, then
q-1
is a prime power and
\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}
is a prime, and this condition is necessary and sufficient.

Proof.

Let
x
be a generator of
G
. Let us write the group operation in
G
multiplicatively.

Suppose
q-1
is not a prime power. Then we can find distinct prime factors
p_{1},p_{2}
of
q-1
, and the elements
x^{p_{1}},x^{p_{2}}
would respectively generate subgroups
H,K
that does not contain each other. Since
p_{i}<q-1\leq\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}
for

, neither
H
nor
K
can have order dividing
q-1
.

Now suppose
q-1
is a power of a prime
p_{1}
, but
\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}
is not a prime. Pick any prime factor
p_{2}
of
\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}
. If
p_{1}=p_{2}
, then
q^{n}-1
is a prime power. By Lemma
2.2
, this means
q=3
and
n=2
. So this cannot happen when
n\geq 3
. Now suppose
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
. Then again

would respectively generate subgroups
H,K
that does not contain each other. Now obviously

, but since
\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}
is not prime, we have

. So again neither

can have

Finally, suppose

is a power of a prime

, and

of

∎

\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

Definition 5.1
.

For a matrix

in

This concept is useful to understand the generalized Jordan canonical form of

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

or some companion matrix of an irreducible polynomial.

Proof.

Suppose

for some
k
coprime to
p
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. Then the minimal polynomial of
A
is a factor of

. Since

is coprime to
p
, the polynomial

is separable. Hence the minimal polynomial of

is separable, and our statement follows.
∎

Given any matrix, by raising it to some power, we can obtian a matrix whose projective order is a prime number. In fact, we can also require that this matrix also has prime-power multiplicative order.

Lemma 5.3
.

If

is a prime number dividing the projective order of a matrix

for a positive integer

p_{0}
, and a positive integer

p_{0}
, and a positive integer

must divide
m^{\prime}
and we must have

.

Now set

has multiplicative order

q^{k}-1
that does not divide
q-1
.

By Lemma
2.3
, since
A
has irreducible characteristic polynomial,
\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]
is a field. So
A
has a root
A^{\prime}
which is a multiplicative generator of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]
. Since
p_{1}
divides
q^{k}-1
, it also divides
q^{n}-1
. So
p_{1}
divides the multiplicative order of
A^{\prime}
. So
A^{\prime}
has a power
B
with multiplicative order
p_{1}
. But since
p_{1}
does not divide
q-1
,
B
is not a scalar multiple of identity. Hence
B
has projective order exactly
p_{1}
.

Agains since
p_{1}
divides
q^{k}-1
, by Lemma
2.6
, the characteristic polynomial of
B
cannot be irreducible. Hence by Lemma
5.7
,
B
has distance at most
2
to some pivot matrix. Hence
A
has distance at most
4
to some pivot matrix.

Now let us consider the case when
q=3
and
n=4
. Again by Lemma
2.3
, since
A
has irreducible characteristic polynomial,
\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]
is a field. So any multiplicative generator of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]
is a root of
A
. We can pick a multiplicative generator
A^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]
which has multiplicative order
80
. Since
A^{\prime}
is a root of
A
, it is also similar to the companion matrix of some polynomials. Now
(A^{\prime})^{2}0
has multiplicative order
4
, which is not a factor of
q-1
, but a factor of
q^{2}-1
. So by Lemma
2.4
, the minimal polynomial of
(A^{\prime})^{2}0
is irreducible with degree
2
. In particular,
(A^{\prime})^{20}
must be similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
for some companion matrix
C
to some irreducible polynomial. Over
\mathbb{F}_{3}
, it is easy to see that there is only one such polynomial, so we have
C=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
.

Now
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
has a
9
-th root
\begin{bmatrix}C&I\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
, whose fourth power is
\begin{bmatrix}I&-C\\
&I\end{bmatrix}
, which has a
4
-th root
\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&1\\
&-1&1&0\\
&&-1&0\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}
, whose third power is
\begin{bmatrix}1&&&\\
&-1&&\\
&&-1&\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}
, a pivot matrix. So
(A^{\prime})^{2}0
has distance at most
4
to a pivot matrix.

All in all,
A
has distance at most
2
to
(A^{\prime})^{20}
, which has distance at most
4
to a pivot matrix. So we are done.
∎

For the rest of this section, we can assume that the projective order of
A
and
q-1
are both powers of the same prime. However, if
q-1
is a prime power, then by Lemma
2.1
, either
q=9
, or
q
is a Fermat prime, or
q
is a power of
2
with
q-1
prime.

First we deal with the diagonalizable case.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
q
be Fermat prime or
9
, and let
n\geq 2
. Suppose a matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})-Z(\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, then it has distance at most
1
to a pivot matrix.

Proof.

Suppose
A
is diagonalizable with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and suppose
A
has projective order
m\neq 1
. Then
m
divides
q-1
and thus it is a power of
2
. So
A^{\frac{m}{2}}
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}^{\frac{m}{2}},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\frac{m}{2}}
, and all these eigenvalues have the same square. However, in the cyclic group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
, each element can have at most two square roots. Hence
\lambda_{1}^{\frac{m}{2}},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\frac{m}{2}}
can take only two possible values. Since
A^{\frac{m}{2}}
is not a scalar multiple of identity, it must be a pivot matrix.
∎

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
q
be a power of
2
such that
q-1
is prime, and let
n\geq 2
. Suppose a matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})-Z(\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
but not all eigenvalues are distinct, then it has distance at most
4
to a pivot matrix.

Proof.

Suppose
\lambda
is a repeating eigenvalue for
A
. Then we have
A=X\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&\\
&C_{2}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
for some invertible
X
, where
C_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&\\
&\lambda\end{bmatrix}
and
C_{2}
is diagonal.

By Lemma
2.2
, we can find a prime factor
p_{1}
of
q^{2}-1
coprime to
q-1
. Since
C_{1}
has multiplicative order dividing
q-1
, therefore
p_{1}(q-1)
divides
q^{2}-1
. Hence by Lemma
2.9
, we can find a matrix
C^{\prime}_{1}
which is a
p_{1}
-th root of
C_{1}
, and has
p_{1}
dividing its multiplicative order. Note that since
p_{1}
and
q-1
are coprime,
p_{1}
must also divide the projective order of
C^{\prime}_{1}
.

Since
q-1
and
p_{1}
are coprime, each diagonal entry of
C_{2}
has a unique
p_{1}
-th root. So
C_{2}
has a
p_{1}
-th root
C^{\prime}_{2}
. Therefore
A
has a
p_{1}
-th root
A^{\prime}=X\begin{bmatrix}C^{\prime}_{1}&\\
&C^{\prime}_{2}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
, and since
p_{1}
divides the projective order of
C^{\prime}_{1}
, it must also divide the projective order of
A^{\prime}
. Furthermore, since
A^{\prime}
is similar to a block diagonal matrix, its characteristic polynomial is reducible. So by Proposition
5.8
,
A^{\prime}
has distance at most
3
to a pivot matrix. Hence
A
has distance at most
4
to a pivot matrix.
∎

Note that if
q
be a power of
2
such that
q-1
is prime, and a matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})-Z(\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
n
distinct eigenvalues. Then we must have
n<q
, and this is exactly the situation described by Proposition
4.4
. So we have completed all discussions on diagonalizable matrices.

We now sum up the remaining cases.

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
q
be a power of a prime
p
, and
q-1
is a power of a prime
p_{0}
, and let
n\geq 2
. Suppose a matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})-Z(\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}))
is not diagonalizable, has reducible characteristic polynomial, and its projective order is also a power of
p_{0}
. Then either
A
has distance at most
4
to a pivot matrix, or
q=3
and
n=4
and
A
has distance at most
5
to a pivot matrix.

Proof.

Since the projective order of
A
is coprime to
p
, by Lemma
5.2
, the generalized Jordan canonical form of
A
is block diagonal, with companion matrices for irreducible polynomials on its diagonal. Since
A
is not diagonalizable, one of these diagonal blocks
C_{1}
is not
1\times 1
.

Since
A
has reducible characteristic polynomial, we must have
A=X\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&\\
&C_{2}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
for some invertible
X
, where
C_{2}
is block diagonal, with companion matrices for irreducible polynomials on its diagonal.

Suppose
C_{1}
is
k\times k
. If
q\neq 3
, then by Lemma
2.2
, we can find a prime factor
p_{1}
of
q^{k}-1
coprime to
q-1
. Since the projective order of
C_{1}
and all diagonal blocks of
C_{2}
are powers of
p_{0}
, by Lemma
2.8
,
C_{1},C_{2}
have
p_{1}
-th root
C^{\prime}_{1},C^{\prime}_{2}
. Furthremore, since
p_{1},p_{0}
are distinct primes dividing
q^{k}-1
, by Lemma
2.8
again, we can choose
C^{\prime}_{1}
such that
p_{1}
divides its multiplicative order. Note that since
p_{1}
and
q-1
are coprime,
p_{1}
must also divide the projective order of
C^{\prime}_{1}
.

So
A
has a
p_{1}
-th root
A^{\prime}=X\begin{bmatrix}C^{\prime}_{1}&\\
&C^{\prime}_{2}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
with reducible characteristic polynomial, and
p_{1}
divides the projective order of
A^{\prime}
. Then by Proposition
5.8
,
A^{\prime}
has distance at most
3
to a pivot matrix. Hence
A
has distance at most
4
to a pivot matrix.

Now suppose
q=3
. Suppose in the diagonal blocks of the generalized Jordan canonical form of
A
, at least one block
C_{1}
is
k\times k
for some
k\geq 3
. Then again by Lemma
2.2
, we can find a prime factor
p_{1}
of
q^{k}-1
coprime to
q-1
. The rest of the argument is identical to above.

Now suppose
q=3
, but all diagonal blocks of the generalized Jordan canonical form of
A
are either
1\times 1
or
2\times 2
. Suppose
A
has multiplicative order
m
, which must be a power of
2
. If
A^{\frac{m}{2}}\neq-I
, then it must be a diagonalizable matrix with eigenvalues
1
and
-1
, hence it is a pivot matrix. If
A^{\frac{m}{2}}=-I
, then for the matrix
A^{\prime}=A^{\frac{m}{4}}
, its generalized Jordan canonical form must have all diagonal blocks identical to
\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
.

If
n>4
, then again
A=X\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&\\
&C_{2}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
where
C_{1},C_{2}
are both block diagonal with all diagonal blocks identical to
\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
, but
C_{1}
only have two diagonal blocks while
C_{2}
have
\frac{n-4}{2}
diagonal blocks. Then by Lemma
2.9
,
C_{1}
has a
5
-th root
C^{\prime}_{1}
, and
C^{\prime}_{1}
have projective order a multiple of
5
. We also have
C_{2}
as its own
5
-th root. Hence
A
has a
5
-th root
A^{\prime}=X\begin{bmatrix}C^{\prime}_{1}&\\
&C_{2}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
with reducible characteristic polynomial, and
5
divides the projective order of
A^{\prime}
. So again by Proposition
5.8
,
A^{\prime}
has distance at most
3
to a pivot matrix. Hence
A
has distance at most
4
to a pivot matrix.

Finally, the last case requires
n=4
. Then
A^{\prime}=A^{\frac{m}{4}}
is similar to a matrix which is block diagonal, and the two diagonal blocks are both
\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
. We have already encountered this matrix in Proposition
5.9
, and it has distance at most
4
to a pivot matrix. Hence
A
has distance at most
5
to a pivot matrix.
∎

5.3
When
q=2

Now we turn to the case of
q=2
. Since there is no pivot matrix in this case, we would connect matrices in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})-Z(\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2}))
to Jordan pivot matrices.

Proposition 5.13
.

For
n\geq 6
, if
2
divides the multiplicative order of a matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
, then
A
has distance at most
5
to a Jordan pivot matrix.

Proof.

First, by Lemma
5.5
,
A
has a power
A^{\prime}
whose generalized Jordan canonical form is made of diagonal blocks of the form
\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
&1\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}1\end{bmatrix}
. If
n\geq 6
(in fact
n\geq 4
is enough here), then either this Jordan canonical form has at least two
1\times 1
blocks, or it has at least two
2\times 2
blocks.

Note that
\begin{bmatrix}1&1&&\\
&1&&\\
&&1&1\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix}
is similar to
\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&I_{2}\\
&I_{2}\end{bmatrix}
. Therefore, consider the generalized Jordan canonical form of
A^{\prime}
, we have
A^{\prime}=X\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&\\
&A^{\prime\prime}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
or
X\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&I_{2}&\\
&I_{2}&\\
&&A^{\prime\prime}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
for some invertible
X
, where
A^{\prime\prime}
has multiplicative order exactly
2
.

Set
P=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}
which has multiplicative order
3
. Set
B=X\begin{bmatrix}P&\\
&A^{\prime\prime}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
or
X\begin{bmatrix}P&P&\\
&P&\\
&&A^{\prime\prime}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
respectively. Either way, we have
B^{3}=A^{\prime}
,
B^{6}=I_{n}
and
B^{4}=X\begin{bmatrix}P&\\
&I_{n-2}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
or
X\begin{bmatrix}P&&\\
&P&\\
&&I_{n-4}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
. Let
C=X\begin{bmatrix}I_{n-2}&\\
&J\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
where
J=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
&1\end{bmatrix}
. Then as
n\geq 6
,
B^{4}
must commute with
C
, and hence
(B^{4}C)^{4}=B^{4}
and
(B^{4}C)^{3}=C
.

Then we have the following path

A\xrightarrow{\text{power}}A^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\text{$3$-rd root}}B\xrightarrow{\text{$4$-th power}}B^{4}\xrightarrow{\text{$4$-th root}}B^{4}C\xrightarrow{\text{$3$-rd power}}C.

So we have reached a Jordan pivot matrix in
5
steps.
∎

Lemma 5.14
.

For
n\geq 4
, suppose a non-identity matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
has odd multiplicative order, and
(x-1)^{2}
is a factor of the characteristic polynomial
f(x)
of
A
. Then
A
has distance at most
2
to a Jordan pivot matrix.

Proof.

Since the projective order of
A
is coprime to
2
, by Lemma
5.2
, the generalized Jordan canonical form of
A
is block diagonal, with companion matrices for irreducible polynomials on its diagonal. Since
(x-1)^{2}
is a factor of the characteristic polynomial
f(x)
of
A
, therefore
A=X\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
for some invertible
X
, where
I_{2}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix and
C
has the same multiplicative order as
A
.

Let
k
be the multiplicative order of
A
. Since
k
is an odd number, therefore
A
has a
(k+1)
-th root
A^{\prime}=X\begin{bmatrix}J&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
where
J=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
&1\end{bmatrix}
, and
(A^{\prime})^{k}
is a Jordan pivot matrix. So
A
has distance at most
2
to a Jordan pivot matrix.
∎

Proposition 5.15
.

For
n\geq 6
, suppose the multiplicative order of a non-identity matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
is an odd prime
p
,
p+1
is not a power of
2
, and the characteristic polynomial of
A
has no irreducible factor of degree
n
or
n-1
. Then
A
has distance at most
4
to a Jordan pivot matrix.

Proof.

Let
m
be the smallest positive integer such that
p
divides
2^{m}-1
. Since
p+1
is not a power of
2
, this means that
2^{m}-1
is a composite number. In particular, by Lemma
2.1
,
2^{m}-1
cannot be a prime power. So we can find a prime factor
p_{0}\neq p
of
2^{m}-1
.

Since we must have
p\neq 2
, by Lemma
5.2
,
A=X\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&C_{t}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
for some invertible
X
, where
C_{1},\dots,C_{t}
are companion matrices to various irreducible polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. By Corollary
2.6
, all
C_{i}
are either
1\times 1
or
m\times m
. Since
A
is not identity, one of these
C_{i}
is
m\times m
. Say
C_{1}
is
m\times m
.

Now
\mathbb{F}_{2}[C_{1}]
is a field with
2^{m}
elements. So
C_{1}
has a
p_{0}
-th root
C^{\prime}_{1}
with multiplicative order
pp_{0}
. For other
C_{i}
, since each has multiplicative order
1
or
p
, each has a
p_{0}
-th root
C^{\prime}_{i}
with multiplicative order
1
or
p
. Hence
A
has a
p_{0}
-th root
A^{\prime}=X\begin{bmatrix}C^{\prime}_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&C^{\prime}_{t}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
, which has multiplicative order
pp_{0}
.

Now,
(A^{\prime})^{p}=X\begin{bmatrix}(C^{\prime}_{1})^{p}&\\
&I_{n-m}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
has multiplicative order
p_{0}
. Since the characteristic polynomial of
A
has no irreducible factor of degree
n
or
n-1
, therefore
m\leq n-2
. So
(x-1)^{2}
is a factor of the characteristic polynomial
f(x)
of
(A^{\prime})^{p}
. Hence by Lemma
5.14
,
(A^{\prime})^{p}
has distance at most
2
to a Jordan pivot matrix. Therefore,
A
has distance at most
4
to a Jordan pivot matrix.
∎

Lemma 5.16
.

For
n\geq 6
, suppose the multiplicative order of a non-identity matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
is an odd prime
p
, and
p+1=2^{m}
for some positive integer
m
. If the minimal polynomial and the characteristic polynomial of
A
are not the same, then either
A
has distance at most
4
to a Jordan pivot matrix, or
A
is similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}C&&\\
&C&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
for some companion matrix
C
to an irreducible polynomial.

Proof.

Since we must have
p\neq 2
, by Lemma
5.2
,
A=X\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&C_{t}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
for some invertible
X
, where
C_{1},\dots,C_{t}
are companion matrices to various irreducible polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. By Corollary
2.6
, all
C_{i}
are either
1\times 1
or
m\times m
. Since the minimal polynomial and the characteristic polynomial of
A
are not the same, two of these
C_{i}
are identical. Say
C_{1}=C_{2}
.

If
C_{1},C_{2}
are both
1\times 1
, then
(x-1)^{2}
divides the characteristic polynomial of
A
. Hence
A
has distance at most
2
to a Jordan pivot matrix.

Now suppose
C_{1},C_{2}
are both
m\times m
. Note that
2^{2m}-1=(2^{m}-1)(2^{m}+1)
, and
2^{m}-1,2^{m}+1
must be coprime. Hence we can find a prime factor
p_{0}\neq p
of
2^{m}+1
. By Lemma
2.9
, we can find a matrix
C^{\prime}
such that
(C^{\prime})^{p_{0}}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&\\
&C_{2}\end{bmatrix}
and
p_{0}
divides the multiplicative order of
C^{\prime}
.

For all
i>2
, since
p_{0}\neq p
,
C_{i}
has a
p_{0}
-th root
C^{\prime}_{i}
by Lemma
2.8
. So all in all,
A
has a
p_{0}
-th root
A^{\prime}=X\begin{bmatrix}C^{\prime}&&&\\
&C^{\prime}_{3}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&C^{\prime}_{t}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
, and
(A^{\prime})^{p}=X\begin{bmatrix}(C^{\prime})^{p}&\\
&I_{n-2m}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
. Since
p_{0}\neq p
divides the multiplicative order of
C^{\prime}
, we see that
(A^{\prime})^{p}
is not an identity matrix.

Suppose that
A
is not similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}C&&\\
&C&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
for some companion matrix
C
to an irreducible polynomial. Then the matrix
\begin{bmatrix}C_{3}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&C_{t}\end{bmatrix}
is at least
2\times 2
, and hence
n-2m\geq 2
. Therefore
(A^{\prime})^{p}
satisfies the requirement of Lemma
5.14
. Hence
(A^{\prime})^{p}
has distance at most
2
to a Jordan pivot matrix, and
A
has distance at most
4
to a Jordan pivot matrix.
∎

Proposition 5.17
.

For
n\geq 6
, suppose the multiplicative order of a non-identity matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
is an odd prime
p
, and
p+1=2^{m}
for some positive integer
m
. If the minimal polynomial and the characteristic polynomial of
A
are not the same, then
A
has distance at most
6
to a Jordan pivot matrix.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.16
, we only need to consider the case when
A
is similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}C&&\\
&C&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
for some
m\times m
companion matrix
C
to an irreducible polynomial. Since
n\geq 6
, this implies that
m\geq 3
. So
3
divides
2^{2m}-1
but
3\neq p
.

By Lemma
2.9
, we can find a matrix
C^{\prime}
such that
(C^{\prime})^{3}=\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
and the multiplicative order of
C^{\prime}
is exactly
3p
. Therefore, if
A
is similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}C&&\\
&C&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
, then
A
has a third root
A^{\prime}
similar to
C^{\prime}
or
\begin{bmatrix}C^{\prime}&\\
&1\end{bmatrix}
. In particular,
A^{\prime}
has multiplicative order
3p
, and
(A^{\prime})^{p}
has multiplicative order
3
. Its minimal polynomial has degree at most
3
, so it cannot equal to its characteristic polynomial.

Furthermore, since
3=2^{2}-1
, by Corollary
2.6
, the generalized Jordan canonical form of
(A^{\prime})^{p}
must be block diagonal by
1\times 1
or
2\times 2
blocks. Since
n\geq 6
, it cannot be similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C^{\prime\prime}&\\
&C^{\prime\prime}\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}C^{\prime\prime}&&\\
&C^{\prime\prime}&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
for some
2\times 2
companion matrix
C^{\prime\prime}
to an irreducible polynomial. So by Lemma
5.16
,
(A^{\prime})^{p}
has distance at most
4
to a Jordan pivot matrix. Hence
A
has distance at most
6
to a Jordan pivot matrix.
∎

Note that in the case above, if the minimal polynomial and the characteristic polynomial of
A
are the same, then this is exactly the case described by Proposition
4.5
.

Proposition 5.18
.

For
n\geq 6
, suppose the multiplicative order of a non-identity matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
is an odd composite number, and the characteristic polynomial of
A
has no irreducible factor of degree
n
or
n-1
. Then
A
has distance at most
5
to a Jordan pivot matrix.

Proof.

Suppose the multiplicative order of
A
has a prime factor
p
such that
p+1
is not a power of
2
, then
A
has a power
A^{\prime}
of multiplicative order
p
. By Lemma
2.15
, the characteristic polynomial of
A^{\prime}
has no irreducible factor of degree
n
or
n-1
. Therefore, by Proposition
5.15
,
A^{\prime}
has distance at most
4
to a Jordan pivot matrix. So
A
has distance at most
5
to a Jordan pivot matrix.

Now suppose all prime factors of the multiplicative order of
A
are one less than some powers of two. Say the multiplicative order of
A
has a prime factor
p=2^{m}-1
for some positive integer
m
. Then
A
has a power
A^{\prime}
of multiplicative order
p
.

By Lemma
5.2
,
A=X\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&C_{t}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
for some invertible
X
, where
C_{1},\dots,C_{t}
are companion matrices to various irreducible polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Let
k
be the multiplicative order of
A
, then
A^{\prime}=A^{\frac{k}{p}}=X\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}^{\frac{k}{p}}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&C_{t}^{\frac{k}{p}}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
.

Note that each
C_{i}^{\frac{k}{p}}
has multiplicative order
1
or
p=2^{m}-1
. If all
C_{i}^{\frac{k}{p}}
have irreducible characteristic polynomials, then by Corollary
2.6
, they all have size
1\times 1
or
m\times m
. But then
C_{i}
must have multiplicative order dividing
1
or
p=2^{m}-1
. So
A
has prime multiplicative order, contradiction. So for some
C_{i}
,
C_{i}^{\frac{k}{p}}
no longer has irreducible characteristic polynomial. In particular,
A^{\prime}
must have distinct minimal polynomial and characteristic polynomial.

Suppose for contradiction that
A^{\prime}
is similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}C&&\\
&C&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
for some companion matrix
C
of some irreducible polynomial. Since
C
must have multiplicative order
p
, it must be
m\times m
. Since the characteristic polynomial of
A
has no irreducible factor of degree
n
or
n-1
, compare with the generalized Jordan canonical forms of
A
and of
A^{\prime}
, we can only have
A=\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&\\
&C_{2}\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&&\\
&C_{2}&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
, where
C_{1},C_{2}
are both
m\times m
. But since
p=2^{m}-1
, the multiplicative orders of
C_{1},C_{2}
must divide
p
. So again
A
has prime multiplicative order, contradiction. So
A^{\prime}
is not similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}C&&\\
&C&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
for some companion matrix
C
of some irreducible polynomial.

In conclusion, Lemma
5.16
must apply to
A^{\prime}
. So
A^{\prime}
has distance at most
4
to a Jordan pivot matrix. Thus
A
has distance at most
5
to a Jordan pivot matrix.
∎

Lemma 5.19
.

For a compositite number
n\geq 6
, suppose the multiplicative order of a non-identity matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
is an odd number. If the characteristic polynomial of
A
is irreducible, then
A
has distance at most
6
to a Jordan pivot matrix.

Proof.

Since the characteristic polynomial of
A
is irreducible,
\mathbb{F}_{q}[A]
is a field with
q^{n}
elements. Let
A^{\prime}
be a multiplicative generator of this field (and hence a root of
A
).

Since
n\geq 6
is a composite number, let
m\geq 2
be its smallest prime factor, then
n\geq 3m\geq 2m+2
. Pick any prime factor
p
dividing
2^{m}-1
. Then
p
also divides
2^{n}-1
, and therefore
A^{\prime}
has a power
A^{\prime\prime}
whose multiplicative order is
p
. By Lemma
5.2
,
A^{\prime\prime}=X\begin{bmatrix}C_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&C_{t}\end{bmatrix}X^{-1}
for some invertible
X
, where
C_{1},\dots,C_{t}
are companion matrices to various irreducible polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Furthermore, since each
C_{i}
has multiplicative order dividing
p
, by Corollary
2.6
, therefore it is either
1\times 1
or
m\times m
.

Since
n\geq 3m
, we must have
t\geq 3
, so the characteristic polynomial of
A^{\prime\prime}
cannot have irreducible factor of degree
n
or
n-1
. If
p\neq 2^{m}-1
, then by Proposition
5.15
,
A^{\prime\prime}
has distance at most
4
to a Jordan pivot matrix. Therefore
A
has distance at most
6
to a Jordan pivot matrix.

If
p=2^{m}-1
, note that
n\geq 2m+2
, so
A^{\prime\prime}
cannot be similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&C\end{bmatrix}
or
\begin{bmatrix}C&&\\
&C&\\
&&1\end{bmatrix}
for some companion matrix
C
of some irreducible polynomial. Furthermore,
A^{\prime\prime}
is a power of
A^{\prime}
, so by Lemma
2.4
, its minimal polynomial must be irreducible. So its minimal polynomial and characteristic polynomial are different. Hence by Lemma
5.16
,
A^{\prime\prime}
has distance at most
4
to a Jordan pivot matrix. Therefore
A
has distance at most
6
to a Jordan pivot matrix.
∎

Proposition 5.20
.

For
n\geq 6
, suppose the multiplicative order of a non-identity matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
is an odd number. If the characteristic polynomial of
A
has an irreducible of degree
n
or
n-1
, and this degree is a composite number, then
A
has distance at most
6
to a Jordan pivot matrix.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.19
, we only need to prove the case when the characteristic polynomial of
A
has an irreducible of degree
n-1
, and
n-1
is a composite number. Then the generalized Jordan canonical form of
A
must be
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&1\end{bmatrix}
where
C
is a companion matrix to an irreducible polynomial of degree
n-1
.

Since
n-1
is a composite number, we cannot have
n=6
. So
n\geq 7
. Then
C
has irreducible characteristic polynomial, and its dimension is
n-1\geq 6
. Therefore by Lemma
5.19
,
C
has distance at most
6
to a Jordan pivot matrix. Since
A
is similar to
\begin{bmatrix}C&\\
&1\end{bmatrix}
and
1
is its own power and root,
A
has distance at most
6
to a Jordan pivot matrix.
∎
