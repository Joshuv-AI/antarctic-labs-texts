Topological Properties of Single-Particle States Decaying into a Continuum due to Interaction
B. Hawashin, J. Sirker, G. S. Uhrig

Abstract
We investigate how topological Chern numbers can be defined when single-particle states hybridize with continua. We do so exemplarily in a bosonic Haldane model at zero temperature modified by an additional on-site decay of one boson into two and the conjugate fusion of two bosons into one. Restricting the Hilbert space to two bosons at maximum, the exact self-energy is accessible. We use the bilinear Hamiltonian H 0 H_{0} corrected by the self-energy Σ \Sigma to compute Chern numbers by two different approaches. The results are gauged against a full many-body calculation in the restricted Hilbert space. We find evidence that the effective Hamiltonian H 0 ​ ( k → ) + Σ ⁡ ( ω , k → ) H_{0}(\vec{k})+\Sigma(\omega,\vec{k}) reproduces the correct many-body topology even if the considered band overlaps with the continuum. However, in the latter case the bulk-boundary correspondence appears to be no longer valid and the edge modes delocalize.



Introduction

The first striking topological effect discovered in condensed matter physics
is the integer quantum Hall effect
v. Klitzing et al. 1980
. Here, the number of contributing
edge modes defines the Hall conductivity
Thouless et al. 1982
;
Kohmoto 1985
to an incredible precision
revolutionizing metrology
Weis and von
Klitzing 2011
.
No non-linearities occur
Klein and Seiler 1990
;
Uhrig 1991
. Also, fractional charges are possible
enabling the fractional quantum Hall effect
Tsui et al. 1982
. Both topological effects
gained their discoverers Nobel Prizes
nob 1985
;
nob 1998
and topology in
condensed matter has continued to gain great interest in the last decades
nob 2016
.
The possible types of topological order have been classified for Gaussian fermionic systems,
both in the case of non-spatial and spatial symmetries

Schnyder et al. 2008
;
Ryu et al. 2010
;
Chiu et al. 2016
;
Hughes et al. 2011
;
Fang et al. 2013
;
Alexandradinata et al. 2014
.
A non-trivial topological ground state of a fermionic insulator cannot be connected adiabatically
to the atomic limit without closing the gap or, if present, breaking the symmetry protecting the
topological order. These topological states thus have non-trivial entanglement and typically
also robust edge states
Ryu and Hatsugai 2006
;
Fidkowski 2010
;
Monkman and Sirker 2020
;
Monkman and Sirker 2022
;
Monkman and Sirker 2023
.
For quantum Hall systems, in particular, topologically non-trivial ground states were
proposed even for models without external magnetic field
Haldane 1988
and realized
in experiment
König et al. 2007
;
Ando 2013
;
Jotzu et al. 2014
;
Yue et al. 2019
. Numerous technological applications
are expected not only in fermionic systems
Kong et al. 2010
;
Kong and Cui 2011
;
Tokura et al. 2019
;
Frolov et al. 2020
but also
in insulating quantum magnets, see e.g. Ref.
Malki and Uhrig 2020
.

A crucial aspect of quantum Hall systems breaking time reversal symmetry is the occurrence
of robust, chiral edge modes which generically allow for motion
only in one direction. These systems promise a high degree of tunability
Malki and Uhrig 2017
;
Wang et al. 2018
.
The number of these chiral edge modes is linked
to the Chern number of a dispersive band, i.e., a topological invariant given by the Berry phase
of a quantum state tracked around the Brillouin zone (BZ). This is commonly referred to as the bulk-boundary correspondence
Hasan and Kane 2010
;
Bernevig and Hughes 2013
. However, already for non-interacting
systems, this correspondence must be used
with caution because it only holds if the dispersive band is protected by
indirect

energy gaps
Malki et al. 2019
;
Malki and Uhrig 2019
. Otherwise, the potential boundary mode is not
localized, but extends into the continuum. Such a breakdown of the bulk-boundary
correspondence can also occur in manifestly non-Hermitian systems
Shen et al. 2018
;
Bergholtz et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Okuma and Sato 2023
.

Thus, the hybridization of modes
with continua can destroy the desired topological properties. Clearly, if a single-particle
excitation is no longer defined in the whole BZ, its Berry connection
Berry 1984

cannot be defined in the standard way. Such a hybridization of single-particle states with
continua is generic in interacting systems. Hence, a generalization of topological invariants
is required. For ground states of fermionic topological insulators,
topological invariants such as the Chern number can be expressed in terms of
Green’s functions at zero frequency
Niu et al. 1985
;
Wang et al. 2010
;
Gurarie 2011
.
Clearly, this allows for a straightforward extension to systems with interactions although
zeroes in the Green’s function need to be considered carefully. The appearance of
edge modes at zero energy at the boundary between two topologically distinct phases
is then the generic scenario, supporting the bulk-boundary correspondence. Similarly, a classification of symmetry-protected topological phases in interacting bosonic systems based on group cohomology theory exists
Chen et al. 2012
.

Here, we do not focus on the ground state and its topological properties but rather on
the bands of elementary, single-particle excitations. Without interactions, the Berry curvature in this case
is unambiguously defined. Including interactions, two situations need to be
distinguished: (I) if the single-particle states are adiabatically connected
to the ones in the absence of interactions, i.e, the hybridization only renormalizes
the single-particle states, then the Chern number can be defined in a mathematically rigorous way; (II)
if the single-particle states hybridize strongly with the continua so that they merge
with them, the situation is far from clear. This is the topic of this Letter.

We define a bosonic model reduced to the essentials which allows for various approaches
to treat it. Our study will provide evidence that the self-consistent solution
of the effective single-particle problem defined by the non-interacting
Hamiltonian plus the proper self-energy is the appropriate generalization to calculate Chern numbers in the interacting case.
This approach provides a connection between many-particle Hermitian models and
single-particle non-Hermitian models.
We emphasize, however, that usually non-Hermiticity is invoked on the basis of
gains and losses. Here, in contrast, we investigate a Hermitian many-body
problem which can be reduced to an effective single-particle problem if the
complex self-energy with its full frequency dependence is known. This effective problem
can be non-Hermitian, but its non-Hermiticity depends on frequency.

Chern numbers

For non-interacting systems, the Chern number of the
n
-th band is defined as

C=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\text{BZ}}F_{12}d^{2}k
, where

F_{12}=\partial_{1}A_{2}-\partial_{2}A_{1}
denotes the Berry curvature and

A_{i}(\vec{k})=\langle u_{n}(\vec{k})|\partial_{i}|u_{n}(\vec{k})\rangle
is the Berry connection.
The states
|u_{n}(\vec{k})\rangle
are the single-particle states at wave vector
\vec{k}
.
In order to calculate these states, the coefficient matrix
H_{0}(\vec{k})
of the bilinear Hamiltonian
at this wave vector is sufficient. For the model under study, this amounts to the solution
of a
2\times 2
problem at each given point of a mesh in the BZ.
In our calculations, we use meshes ranging from
20\times 20
to
30\times 30
.
These sizes are sufficient to compute the Chern numbers
Fukui et al. 2005
.

We proceed in three distinct ways:

a) Topological approach

We use the eigenstates of

H_{\text{top}}(\vec{k})=H_{0}(\vec{k})+\Sigma(\omega=0,\vec{k})

(2a)

fulfilling

H_{\text{top}}(\vec{k})|u_{n}(\vec{k})\rangle=E_{n}(\vec{k})|u_{n}(\vec{k})\rangle.

(2b)

Obviously, this is still a
2\times 2
problem. At zero frequency, the
self-energy is Hermitian so that no issues from non-Hermiticity arise;
E_{n}(\vec{k})

is real. Wang et al. showed that for fermionic models at
T=0

the sum
\sum_{\alpha\text{ occ.}}C_{\alpha}

of the Chern numbers
C_{\alpha}
obtained from
H_{\text{top}}(\vec{k})
for the occupied bands
provides the Chern number of the ground state which defines the Hall conductivity
Wang et al. 2010
;
Wang and Zhang 2012
.

b) Effective approach

We use the eigenstates of

H_{\text{eff}}(\omega,\vec{k})=H_{0}(\vec{k})+\Sigma(\omega,\vec{k})

(3a)

fulfilling the self-consistent eigenvalue equation

H_{\text{eff}}(E,\vec{k})|u(E,\vec{k})\rangle=E(\vec{k})|u(E,\vec{k})\rangle.

(3b)

This is still a
2\times 2
problem, but with the calculation of the self-consistent
E(\vec{k})

as additional challenge. Note that
E(\vec{k})
is complex, in general, because the
self-energy is non-Hermitian
if the single-boson energies overlap with the two-boson continuum, see below.
For non-Hermitian diagonalizations, the right and left eigenstates differ,
but it has been shown that they yield the same Chern numbers
Shen et al. 2018
. In Ref.
Wang and Yan 2012
, the suitability of the
effective Hamiltonian
H_{\text{eff}}(\omega,\vec{k})
was critically discussed
because it did not provide the correct Chern number of fermionic ground states.
Since, however, our focus is a different one it is worthwhile considering
H_{\text{eff}}(E,\vec{k})
.

c) Two-body approach

We use the eigenstates at a given total wave vector
\vec{k}

in the total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

\hat{H}(\vec{k})|u_{n}(\vec{k})\rangle=E_{n}(\vec{k})|u_{n}(\vec{k})\rangle.

(4)

This is a standard Hermitian diagonalization problem, but in a large Hilbert space
of which the dimension at fixed wave vector
\vec{k}
is given by the number of sites in the model. Numerically, we
deal with sizes of 100 to 1000 sites. The calculation of Berry
connections does not pose a conceptual difficulty in the total Hilbert space. But
the correct identification of the relevant eigenstates is an issue if the energies
of the one-boson states lie within the continuum of the two-boson states, see below.

Phase transition at gap closure

In a two-band system, the Chern number of a band does not change if it is only
adiabatically modified while staying separated from the other band by a finite gap. Thus,
a phase transition between regions of different Chern numbers must be accompanied by the vanishing of the direct energy gap between the two bands, i.e., at some
\vec{k}

\Delta E(\vec{k})=|E_{1}(\vec{k})-E_{2}(\vec{k})|=0.

(5)

In the Haldane model, the gap closes for the chosen parameters
t_{1}=t_{2}=1
,
\phi=\pi/2
at the

K
point if
M=3\sqrt{3}
. We reproduce this analytical result numerically
by an optimization algorithm. In the sequel, we fix all parameters and vary
M
to
determine
M_{C}
where the gap closes. Then, we determine the Chern number
in the regions
M<M_{C}
and
M>M_{C}
.

Conclusions

We have studied the topological properties of
an interacting bosonic model on the honeycomb lattice
allowing for the decay of a boson to two
bosons and the inverse fusion. While the ground state (vacuum)
is topologically trivial, the excited states are not. We investigated
how Chern numbers
C
can be defined for these states and if edge states exist for non-vanishing
C
.

If the single-boson states
are only renormalized by the continuum without overlap in energy,
Chern numbers can be computed either
in the many-body Hilbert space with the full Hamiltonian or in the
single-boson space with the bilinear Hamiltonian plus the
self-consistent self-energy (effective Hamiltonian).
It is the first key finding that
these two approaches agree while taking the self-energy at zero energy does not agree. Furthermore, our results indicate that a non-vanishing Chern number in this case does imply the existence of edge states.

In the case that both single-boson bands lie energetically

within
the two-boson continuum, we extended the computation of the Chern
number based on the many-body spectrum by identifying the dressed
single-particle states using their large one-particle spectral weight. The second key result
is the observation that the Chern numbers determined in this way coincide with the Chern numbers
computed self-consistently from the effective single-particle Hamiltonian.
The latter is a computation for a non-Hermitian
and frequency dependent Hamiltonian which is much simpler
than the many-body computation. The self-energy at zero energy does not
yield the same result. Thus, we find strong evidence in favor of the
effective approach.

The third key result is the numerical evidence that
the hybridization with a continuum with energetic overlap
delocalizes edges states. This is supported by the vanishing
of the inverse participation ratio IPR in the limit of infinitely wide strips. This phenomenon is similar to the one
for bilinear, interaction-free Hamiltonians where an overlap with
a continuum can occur in confined geometries
Malki et al. 2019
;
Malki and Uhrig 2019
.
This puts the bulk-boundary correspondence
into perspective but leads to the intriguing question which other physical properties signal the non-trivial topology if the edge modes are delocalized.

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}\big((U(\vec{k}+\vec{q})_{1,\alpha})^{*}(U(-\vec{q})_{1,\beta})^{*}U(\vec{k})_{1,\gamma}

\displaystyle+(U(\vec{k}+\vec{q})_{2,\alpha})^{*}(U(-\vec{q})_{2,\beta})^{*}U(\vec{k})_{2,\gamma}\big).

(9)

At
T=0
, the spectral representation of the retarded Green’s function reads

G_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{k},\omega)=\langle 0|b_{\vec{k},\alpha}\frac{1}{\omega+i0^{+}-\hat{H}+\epsilon_{0}}b_{\vec{k},\beta}^{\dagger}|0\rangle,

(10)

where
\epsilon_{0}
is the energy of the ground state
|0\rangle
; it is zero in our case. In the diagonal
basis of

\hat{H}_{0}
for fixed
\vec{k}
the full Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
on
\mathcal{H}(1)\oplus\mathcal{H}(2)
reads

H(\vec{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}D(\vec{k})\end{matrix}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&V^{\dagger}(\vec{k})\\
\hline\cr V(\vec{k})&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\begin{matrix}A(\vec{k})\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

Then, the self-energy can be exactly determined to be

\Sigma(\vec{k},\omega)=V(\vec{k})^{\dagger}\left(\omega-D(\vec{k})\right)^{-1}V(\vec{k}).

(12)

In components,

\displaystyle\Sigma(\vec{k},\omega)_{\gamma,\delta}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{q}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}C(\vec{k},\vec{q})\frac{\tilde{g}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}(\vec{k},\vec{q})^{*}\tilde{g}_{\alpha\beta\delta}(\vec{k},\vec{q})}{\omega+i0^{+}-E_{\alpha}(\vec{k}+\vec{q})-E_{\beta}(-\vec{q})},

(13a)

\displaystyle\text{with}\qquad C(\vec{k},\vec{q})=\begin{cases}2&if $\vec{k}+2\vec{q}\in\bar{\mathcal{B}}$,\\
1&else.\end{cases},

(13b)

where
\bar{\mathcal{B}}
denotes the reciprocal lattice.

This result can also be understood in perturbation theory at zero temperature. The only contributing diagram is shown in Fig.
4
. Higher-order diagrams do not contribute since they would involve three or more propagators
between two times which vanish due to the restriction of the Hilbert space to
at maximum two bosons. Also, no vertex corrections occur.

Figure 4:
The only diagrammatic contribution for the case
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}(0)\oplus\mathcal{H}(1)\oplus\mathcal{H}(2)
,
which is of second order in
g
. Neither higher-order diagrams nor vertex corrections
are contributing for the considered restricted Hilbert space.

II
Generalized Lanczos Tridiagonalization and Continued Fraction Representation

The numerical evaluation of the sum over the free momentum in Eq. (
13b
) depends on the discretization
of the Brillouin zone. Generic results stay very spiky and do not allow for an interpretation based on a smooth
imaginary part unless the
\delta
-peaks are artificially broadened by a finite imaginary part of the frequency
argument. To circumvent these issues, we generalize the continued fraction representation of scalar
self-energies and propagators
Pettifor and Weaire 1985
;
Viswanath and Müller 1994
to matrices. As in the scalar case we find that
an appropriate terminator leads to smooth multi-dimensional self-energies, essentially independent
from the discretization, allowing for further evaluations as done in the main text.

We consider

\Sigma=V^{\dagger}\left(\omega-H\right)^{-1}V

(14)

where
V\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times d}
and
H\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
. Hence, the self-energy
is a
d\times d
matrix. The well-established scalar case
is recovered for
d=1
. For the calculations in the main text we need
d=2
, but the following
derivation is valid for general
d
.
The generalized Lanczos algorithm yields a block tridiagonal matrix with
d\times d
blocks such that

H_{T}:=U^{\dagger}HU=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}A_{0}&B_{1}^{\dagger}&0&\cdots\\
B_{1}&A_{1}&B_{2}^{\dagger}&\ldots\\
0&B_{2}&A_{2}&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right),

(15)

with
A_{i},B_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
and after
L
iterations,
U
is of the form

U=(V_{0},V_{1},...,V_{L})\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times dL}.

(16)

The very starting point is defined by
V_{0}=V
if
V^{\dagger}V=\mathds{1}_{d}
. Otherwise, we express

V=V_{0}B_{0}
by means of a standard QR-decomposition
Stoer and Bulirsch 1978
where
V_{0}
takes the role of
Q
and
B_{0}
the role
of
R
. Then,
V_{0}^{\dagger}V_{0}=\mathds{1}
holds by construction.

We aim at a proof by induction over
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Hence, we assume that we have shown the following two relations up to and including
j,m\leq n

\displaystyle V^{\dagger}_{j}V_{m}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{d}\delta_{j,m}

\displaystyle\forall j,m\geq 0

(17a)

\displaystyle V^{\dagger}_{j}HV_{m}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall|j-m|>1.

(17b)

Base case

For
n=0
,
V_{0}
has the desired property by construction and
the structure of the Hamiltonian (
15
) implies

A_{0}=V_{0}^{\dagger}HV_{0}.

(18)

Induction step

Assuming that (
17
) holds up to
n
,

\displaystyle A_{j}

\displaystyle=V_{j}^{\dagger}HV_{j}

(19a)

\displaystyle B_{j}

\displaystyle=V^{\dagger}_{j}HV_{j-1}

(19b)

holds by construction. To pass to
n+1
we consider

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=HV_{n}-V_{n}A_{n}-V_{n-1}B_{n}^{\dagger}=V_{n+1}B_{n+1},

(20)

where the second equality refers to the
QR
-decomposition of
S_{n}
;
the diagonal elements of
B_{n+1}
are chosen to be positive.
We exclude the possibility that one of these elements vanishes for the time being.
Note that we set
V_{-1}:=0
to maintain the general validity of this step.

We show (
17a
) in three steps. First, we multiply (
20
)
from the left with
V_{n}^{\dagger}
yielding

\displaystyle V_{n}^{\dagger}V_{n+1}B_{n+1}

\displaystyle=V_{n}^{\dagger}HV_{n}-V_{n}^{\dagger}V_{n}A_{n}-V_{n}^{\dagger}V_{n-1}B_{n}^{\dagger}

(21a)

\displaystyle=0,

(21b)

where the first term on the right hand side of (
21a
)
cancels with the second using (
17a
) and (
19a
)
while the last one vanishes due to (
17a
). Thus, we know

V_{n}^{\dagger}V_{n+1}=0=V_{n+1}^{\dagger}V_{n}.

(22)

Second, we multiply (
20
)
from the left with
V_{n-1}^{\dagger}
yielding

\displaystyle V_{n-1}^{\dagger}V_{n+1}B_{n+1}

\displaystyle=V_{n-1}^{\dagger}HV_{n}-V_{n-1}^{\dagger}V_{n}A_{n}-V_{n-1}^{\dagger}V_{n-1}B_{n}^{\dagger}

(23a)

\displaystyle=0,

(23b)

where the first term on the right hand side of (
23
) cancels with the last term using (
17a
) and (
19b
)
while the second term vanishes due to (
17a
). Thus, we know

V_{n-1}^{\dagger}V_{n+1}=0=V_{n+1}^{\dagger}V_{n-1}.

(24)

Third, we multiply (
20
)
from the left with
V_{m}^{\dagger}
with
0\leq m<n-1
which results in

\displaystyle V_{m}^{\dagger}V_{n+1}B_{n+1}

\displaystyle=V_{m}^{\dagger}HV_{n}-V_{m}^{\dagger}V_{n}A_{n}-V_{m}^{\dagger}V_{n-1}B_{n}^{\dagger}

(25a)

\displaystyle=0,

(25b)

where the first term on the right hand side of (
25
) vanishes due to (
17b
) and the
second and third term vanish due to (
17a
). Thus, we know

V_{m}^{\dagger}V_{n+1}=0=V_{n+1}^{\dagger}V_{m}.

(26)

Hence, we know that (
17a
) holds also up to
n+1
.

What is left to complete the induction step is to prove
V_{n+1}^{\dagger}HV_{m}=0
for

0\leq m<n
. To this end, we multiply (
20
) for
n=m
from the left with
V_{n+1}^{\dagger}

yielding

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=V_{n+1}^{\dagger}V_{m+1}B_{m+1}

(27a)

\displaystyle=V_{n+1}^{\dagger}HV_{m}-V_{n+1}^{\dagger}V_{m}A_{m}-V_{n+1}^{\dagger}V_{m-1}B_{m}^{\dagger}

(27b)

\displaystyle=V_{n+1}^{\dagger}HV_{m}.

(27c)

The first identity ensues from (
17a
) which we just have shown to hold. The second and
third terms in (
27b
) vanish also due to (
17a
) so that the
vanishing of the blocks beyond the minor diagonals in the Hamiltonian matrix is also
extended to
n+1
. Recall that
V_{m}^{\dagger}HV_{n+1}=0
follows from hermiticity.
This concludes the proof by induction.

Above, we assumed that the
B_{n}
always display the full rank of
d
.
We point out that it can happen that for some
n=n_{0}
, one column of
V_{n}

vanishes. Then, the above algorithm reduces to a
(d-1)-
dimensional Lanczos
algorithm for
n>n_{0}
.

Multidimensional continued fraction expansion

Given a basis in which
H
is block tridiagonal, we derive the continued fraction representation
of the self-energy. We start for block matrices from the general identity

\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}{A}&{B}\\
{C}&{D}\end{array}\right)^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left({A}-{BD}^{-1}{C}\right)^{-1}&*\\
&*\end{array}\right),

(28)

where we assume the existence of the inverse on the left hand side and of
D^{-1}
; the blocks
*
do not matter. Iterating this relation for the blocks of
H_{T}
we obtain

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=V^{\dagger}\frac{1}{\omega-H}V

(29a)

\displaystyle=B_{0}^{\dagger}P^{\dagger}\left(\frac{1}{\omega-H_{T}}\right)PB_{0}

(29b)

\displaystyle=B_{0}^{\dagger}\left(\omega-A_{0}-B_{1}^{\dagger}\left(\omega-A_{1}-\ldots\right)^{-1}B_{1}\right)^{-1}B_{0},

(29c)

where we have used
V=V_{0}B_{0}
and
P=(\mathds{1}_{d},0)^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times d}
.
This relation can be expressed best as recursion

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n}

\displaystyle=B_{n}^{\dagger}\frac{1}{\omega-A_{n}-\Sigma_{n+1}}B_{n}

(30)

with
\Sigma=\Sigma_{0}
. This provides the continued fraction expansion
generalized to matrices.

Termination

Finally, we discuss the issue of how to terminate the continued fraction expansion.
Stopping at a finite depth of the fraction
n_{0}
, i.e., setting
\Sigma_{n_{0}}=0

yields an imaginary part of the self-energy made up from a finite set of
\delta
-distributions. For

d=1
there are
n_{0}
poles and generally we expect
dn_{0}
poles. But this is still
unsatisfactory because no smooth function is generated. In the scalar case of
d=1

the square-root terminator, see Refs.
Pettifor and Weaire 1985
;
Viswanath and Müller 1994
as well as below in Eq. (
37
),
allows one to terminate the continued fraction at finite depth while still
yielding a smooth function. Here we briefly discuss whether an equivalent
procedure is also possible in the multidimensional case. The answer is a
yes, but less generally.

We assume that the following limits exist

\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{n}=:A_{\infty},\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{n}=:B_{\infty}

(31)

in the sense that each matrix element converges to a complex number. In order to derive a terminator,
the following equation has to be solved

\Sigma_{\infty}=B_{\infty}^{\dagger}\frac{1}{\omega-A_{\infty}-\Sigma_{\infty}}B_{\infty},

(32)

which is equivalent to

\Sigma_{\infty}{B_{\infty}^{-1}}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{\infty}-\left(\omega-A_{\infty}\right){B_{\infty}^{-1}}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{\infty}+B_{\infty}=0.

(33)

We multiply
{B_{\infty}^{-1}}^{\dagger}
from the left and define

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle:={B_{\infty}^{-1}}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{\infty},

(34a)

\displaystyle O

\displaystyle=-{B_{\infty}^{-1}}^{\dagger}\left(\omega-A_{\infty}\right){B_{\infty}^{-1}}^{\dagger},

(34b)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle:={B_{\infty}^{-1}}^{\dagger}B_{\infty},

(34c)

to obtain the quadratic matrix equation

X^{2}+OX+P=0.

(35)

As discussed in Ref.
Higham and Kim 2000
, the solution of such an equation is not straightforward.
For this reason, no general terminating procedure can be provided.

In our calculations, we observed, however, that often the block matrices
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
become
more and more diagonal as
n
increases so that

(A_{\infty})_{ij}=(B_{\infty})_{ij}\approx 0\quad\text{if}\quad i\neq j.

(36)

Then, a simple solution suggests itself. The approximate diagonality of the Lanczos coefficients imply that

(\Sigma_{\infty})_{ij}\approx 0
so that Eq.
35
reduces to
d
independent equations for the diagonals, each solved by the standard square-root terminator

\Sigma_{\infty}\!=\!\begin{cases}\frac{\omega-a_{\infty}}{2}-\sqrt{\left(\frac{\omega-a_{\infty}}{2}\right)^{2}-b_{\infty}^{2}},&\omega>E^{(2)}_{\text{max}},\\
\frac{\omega-a_{\infty}}{2}-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{b_{\infty}^{2}-\left(\frac{\omega-a_{\infty}}{2}\right)^{2}},&\omega\in\left[E^{(2)}_{\text{min}},E^{(2)}_{\text{max}}\right],\\
\frac{\omega-a_{\infty}}{2}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\omega-a_{\infty}}{2}\right)^{2}-b_{\infty}^{2}},&\omega<E^{(2)}_{\text{min}},\end{cases}

(37)

for real values of
\omega

where
E^{(2)}_{\text{min/max}}
corresponds to the minimum/maximum of the spectrum of
H

and
a_{\infty}
and
b_{\infty}
are the diagonal element of
A_{\infty}
and
B_{\infty}
, respectively.
They are given by
Pettifor and Weaire 1985
;
Viswanath and Müller 1994

a_{\infty}=(E^{(2)}_{\text{max}}+E^{(2)}_{\text{min}})/2\qquad b_{\infty}=(E^{(2)}_{\text{max}}-E^{(2)}_{\text{min}})/4.

(38)

For complex frequencies
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, i.e.,
\Im z>0
,
\Sigma_{\infty}

is given by the retarded solution of the quadratic equation

\Sigma_{\infty}^{2}-(z-a_{\infty})\Sigma_{\infty}+b_{\infty}^{2}=0,

(39)

i.e., the one with negative imaginary part. This is the scalar version, i.e., for
d=1
,
of Eq. (
33
).
In this work, we used this termination procedure as an approximation for the general matrix case
yielding robust and smooth results.

III
Edge States on a Honeycomb Ribbon

To answer the question whether non-trivial Chern numbers imply edge states in the interacting case
similar to the non-interacting case, we consider the model defined in Eq. (
1
)
on the honeycomb ribbon with
N_{x}N_{y}
sites as sketched in Fig.
5
.
One signature of localization is a finite inverse participation ratio as defined in
Eq. (
6
). From this definition, it is easy to see that a totally local
state where only one site has the finite amplitude 1 yields
I_{n}=1
while a state completely
delocalized over
N
states implies
I_{n}=1/N
. The corresponding evaluations
and extrapolations are given in the main text.

N_{x}N_{y}

(i,j)

Identifying each site

Since we are interested in the localization at the edges

N_{y}
is the finite width
of the ribbon in
y

x_{i}
coordinates, yielding

(41)

which is the quantity of interest in the following.

For completeness and comparison, we first discuss the well-known
non-interacting case of Eq.
1
. We choose

As mentioned in the main text, the Chern number changes in this case
