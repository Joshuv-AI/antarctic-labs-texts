Some remarks related to the density of { ( b n ( mod n ) ) / n : n ∈ ℕ } \{(b^{n}\pmod{n})/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
Martin Lind

Abstract
For b ∈ ℕ , b ≥ 2 b\in\mathbb{N},b\geq 2 we determine the limit points of certain subsets of { b n ( mod n ) n : n ∈ ℕ } . \left\{\frac{b^{n}\pmod{n}}{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}. As a consequence, we obtain the density of the latter set in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , a result first established in 2013 by Cilleruelo, Kumchev, Luca, Rué and Shparlinski.

Introduction

Let
b\in\mathbb{N},b\geq 2
. In 2013, Cilleruelo et al.
[
2
]
proved that

\left\{\frac{b^{n}\pmod{n}}{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

(1)

is dense in
[0,1]
. In fact, they proved a stronger statement. Let
\mathbb{P}=\{2,3,\ldots\}
denote the prime numbers and set

\left\{\frac{b^{qp}\pmod{qp}}{qp}:p,q\in\mathbb{P},q\leq\log(p)/\log(b)\right\}.

(2)

The main result of
[
2
]
is an explicit rate of decay for the discrepancy of (
2
). In particular, (
2
) is equidistributed modulo 1 (in the sense of Weyl); this implies the density of (
2
). In this direction, we also mention the works of Dubickas
[
3
,
4
]
where, among other results, the density is established for much more general sets than (
1
).

In this note, we consider (
2
) from a different point of view to
[
2
]
. Namely, for a
fixed

q\in\mathbb{P}
, we determine all possible limits of
b^{pq}\pmod{pq}/pq
as
p\rightarrow\infty,p\in\mathbb{P}
. (Theorem
1
below). This result is sufficient to establish the density of (
1
).
For any set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, let
A^{\prime}
be the set of all limit points of
A
. (
A^{\prime}
is usually referred to as the
derived set
of
A
.)

Theorem 1
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{N},b\geq 2
and let
q\in\mathbb{P}
be a fixed but arbitrary prime number with
q>b^{2}
. Denote

\mathcal{S}_{b,q}=\left\{\frac{b^{qp}\pmod{qp}}{qp}:p\in\mathbb{P}\right\}.

Then

\mathcal{S}_{b,q}^{\prime}=\left\{\frac{k}{q}:k=0,1,2,\ldots,q-1\right\}.

(3)

Figure
1
below illustrate the rather striking structure of
\mathcal{S}_{2,13}
. Note in particular the 13 ”bands” corresponding to the 13 limit points.

Figure 1:
2^{13p}\pmod{13p}/13p
for the first 5000 prime numbers
p
.

We have the following immediate consequence.

Corollary 2
.

The set
\mathcal{S}_{b}
defined by (
1
) is dense in
[0,1]
.

Proof.

We must show that for any interval
J\subset[0,1]
there is
s\in\mathcal{S}_{b}
such that
s\in J
. Take a prime
q
such that
1/q<\text{length}(J)
. Then there is
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,q-1\}
such that
k/q\in J
. By Theorem
1
, there is a prime
p
such that
(b^{qp}\pmod{qp})/qp\in J
(actually, there’s an infinite number of such primes). Since
(b^{qp}\pmod{qp})/qp\in\mathcal{S}_{b}
, we may take
s=(b^{qp}\pmod{qp})/qp
.
∎

To calculate the order of the discrepancy of the sequence (
2
), the authors of
[
2
]
use careful estimates of exponential sums. Our proof of Theorem
1
is on the other hand completely elementary, except for invoking Dirichlet’s theorem on the infinitude of primes in arithmetic progression at one point.
We discuss in more detail our proof. Denote by
\mathbb{Z}[n]=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{*}[n]=(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{*}
the additive group and multiplicative group of integers modulo
n
, respectively.
A crucial step of the proof of Theorem
1
consists of showing that certain modular equations always have solutions. More specifically, fix a prime number
q>b^{2}
. For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}[q]
we consider the equation

b^{x}\equiv kx+b\pmod{q}.

(4)

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}[q]
denote by
Z_{k}
the set of solutions in

\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)]

to the equation (
4
), i.e.,

Z_{k}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)]:b^{x}\equiv kx+b\pmod{q}\}.

(5)

In Section 2, we show (Lemma
8
) that
Z_{k}\neq\emptyset
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}[q]
. Using this fact together with the above mentioned theorem of Dirichlet on primes in arithmetic progressions, Theorem
1
can be proved.

In Section 3, we elaborate on Lemma
8
and determine the exact structure of the sets (
5
) (see Theorem
9
below). Since this further analysis is not needed for the proof of Theorem
1
, we place it in a separate section. Furthermore, Theorem
9
is rather complicated to formulate so we omit its statement here. However, we point out a few of its consequences below.

For Theorem
1
we require
q>b^{2}
. In fact, (
3
) may fail if there are no restrictions on the relationship between

and
q
. For instance, if
b=6

, then a simulation shows

\lim_{p\rightarrow\infty,p\in\mathbb{P}}\frac{6^{7p}\pmod{7p}}{7p}=0,

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{6,7}=\{0\}
. As a consequence of Theorem

, we have the following dichotomy.

Proposition 3

Let
b\in\mathbb{N},b\geq 2
and and

.

(i)

If

, then (

(ii)

If

, then
\mathcal{S}_{b,q}^{\prime}=\{0\}
.

Note that if

, then automatically

. Therefore, Proposition

(i)
agrees with Theorem

.

Theorem

also provides an immediate way to calculate the number of elements in
Z_{k}~~(k=0,1,\ldots,q-1)
. In contrast, Lemma

.

denotes the cardinality of the finite set

.

Proposition 4

Let
b\in\mathbb{N},b\geq 2
and

. Then

|Z_{0}|=(q-1)m_{b}(q),

(6)

|Z_{k}|=\varphi(q-1)-m_{b}(q)\quad(k=1,2,\ldots,q-1),

We mention that if

Proof of Theorem
1

We shall need the following simple lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}~~(N\geq 3)
.

(i)

If
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N-1]
and
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N]
, and

x=\alpha N+\beta(N-1)\pmod{N(N-1)},

(8)

then
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N(N-1)]
.

(ii)

Conversely, for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N(N-1)]
there exist unique
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N-1]
and
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N]
such that (
8
) holds.

Remark 7
.

To avoid confusion, we make the following remark on notation. As usual,
x\equiv y\pmod{n}
simply means
n|(x-y)
. When we write
x=y\pmod{n}
, we mean that
x\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
is the residue of
y
modulo
n
.

Proof of Lemma
6
.

We first prove statement
(i)
.
Let
d
be an arbitrary divisor of
N(N-1)
. Since
\gcd(N,N-1)=1
either
d|N
or
d|(N-1)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
d
is a divisor of
N
. Then
d|(\alpha N+nN(N-1))
for any
a\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N-1]
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. On the other hand,
d
does not divide
N-1
, and since
\gcd(\beta,N)=1
it follows that
d
does not divide
\beta(N-1)
for any
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N]
. Hence,
d
does not divide
\alpha N+\beta(N-1)+nN(N-1)
. To summarize, any divisor of
N(N-1)
does not divide
x=\alpha N+\beta(N-1)\pmod{N(N-1)}
, this proves statement
(i)
.

We proceed to prove statement
(ii)

Take
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N(N-1)]
and define
\beta=N-x\pmod{N}
. Since
\gcd(x,N)=1
it is easy to see that
\gcd(\beta,N)=1
, i.e.,
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N]
. Furthermore,
N|(x+\beta)
so
\alpha=(x+\beta)/N-\beta\pmod{N-1}
is well-defined.
Thus, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
N\alpha=x-\beta(N-1)+nN(N-1)
. Since
\gcd(x,N-1)=1
, it follows that
\gcd(N\alpha,N-1)=1
. Therefore,
\gcd(\alpha,N-1)=1
. Further, by definition
x=\alpha N+\beta(N-1)\pmod{N(N-1)}
, proving the existence of the representation (
8
).
To prove uniqueness of
\alpha,\beta
, assume that

x=\alpha^{\prime}N+\beta^{\prime}(N-1)=\alpha^{\prime\prime}N+\beta^{\prime\prime}(N-1)\pmod{N(N-1)}

for
\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N-1]
and
\beta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[N]
.
It follows that there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime\prime})N+(\beta^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime\prime})(N-1)+nN(N-1)=0

Rearranging the above equality, we get that
(N-1)|(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime\prime})N
.
Since
|\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime\prime}|<N-1
and
\gcd(N,N-1)=1
, the only possibility is
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime\prime}
. A similar argument shows that
\beta^{\prime}=\beta^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{N},b\geq 2
and
q\in\mathbb{P}
satisfies
q>b^{2}
. Let the sets
Z_{k}
be defined by (
5
), then

Z_{k}\neq\emptyset\quad\text{for each}\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}[q],

(9)

and

\bigsqcup_{k=0}^{q-1}Z_{k}=\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)].

(10)

(Here,
\sqcup
denotes disjoint union.)

Proof.

We prove (
9
) first.
Note that if
k=0
, then we may take
x=2q-1\in\mathbb{Z}[q(q-1)]
(by Lemma
6

(i)
). Further, by Fermat’s little theorem
b^{x}\equiv b\pmod{q}
while
b-0\times x=b
, i.e.,
x\in Z_{0}
.

Fix now
k\in\mathbb{Z}[q]\setminus\{0\}
. Since
q-2\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]
, it follows from Lemma
6

(i)
that

x=q(q-2)+\beta(q-1)\pmod{q(q-1)}

(11)

belongs to
\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)]
for any
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]
. We shall determine a specific
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]
such that
x\in Z_{k}
.
Note that

b^{x}\equiv b^{q-2}\pmod{q}\quad\text{and}\quad b+kx\equiv b-k\beta\pmod{q}.

(12)

For the first relation of (
12
), we used Fermat’s little theorem to obtain that

b^{q(q-2)+\beta(q-1)+nq(q-1)}\equiv b^{q-2}\pmod{q}.

(Note that this also holds when
n<0
, since
b^{-1}\equiv b^{q-2}\pmod{q}
.)

Denote by

S=\{b-k\beta\pmod{q}:\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]\}.

We shall prove that
S=\mathbb{Z}[q]\setminus\{b\}
. Clearly,
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[q]
. Furthermore,
|S|=q-1
. Indeed, let
\beta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]
and assume
b-k\beta^{\prime}\equiv b-k\beta^{\prime\prime}\pmod{q}
. The latter congruence implies
k(\beta^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime\prime})\equiv 0\pmod{q}
which is only true if
\beta^{\prime}=\beta^{\prime\prime}
, since
q
is prime. By the same token,
b\notin S
, since
b\in S
would imply
k\beta\equiv 0\pmod{q}
, which is impossible since
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]
and
k\neq 0
. Consequently,
S=\mathbb{Z}[q]\setminus\{b\}
.

Consider now
\langle b\rangle
, the cyclic subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]
generated by
b
. Denote
\delta=|\langle b\rangle|
. Since
S=\mathbb{Z}[q]\setminus\{b\}
, we have

S\cap\langle b\rangle=\langle b\rangle\setminus\{b\}=\{b^{l}\pmod{q}:l=0,2,3\ldots,\delta-1\}.

(13)

By Lagrange’s theorem,
q-1=N\delta
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore

b^{q-2}=b^{\delta-1+(N-1)\delta}\equiv b^{\delta-1}\pmod{q}.

Since
\delta>2
we have
\delta-1>1
.
Therefore, by (
13
), there is a unique
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]
defined by

b-k\beta\equiv b^{\delta-1}\pmod{q}

(14)

Clearly
x
defined by (
11
) with
\beta
given by (
14
) satisfies
x\in Z_{k}
. This concludes the proof of (
9
).

We continue to prove (
10
). Assume that
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)]
satisfies both
b^{x}\equiv kx+b\pmod{q}
and
b^{x}\equiv lx+b\pmod{q}
. Then
(k-l)x\equiv 0\pmod{q}
. Since
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)]
the residue
x\pmod{q}\neq 0
and the only possibility is
k-l\equiv 0\pmod{q}
, whence
k=l
. This shows
Z_{k}\cap Z_{l}=\emptyset
for
k\neq l
. Take now
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)]
. We shall prove that there is
k\in\mathbb{Z}[q]
such that
x\in Z_{k}
. Since
\gcd(x,q)=1
there are integers
A,B
such that
Ax+Bq=1
. Therefore,
b^{x}-b=(b^{x}-b)Ax+(b^{x}-b)Bq
and we set
k=k(x)=(b^{x}-b)A\pmod{q}
. Clearly
b^{x}-b\equiv kx\pmod{q}
, or equivalently,
x\in Z_{k}

∎

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Recall that
\mathbb{P}
denotes the set of prime numbers.
Fix
k\in\mathbb{Z}[q]
, we shall show that there exists a sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{P}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{b^{qp_{n}}\pmod{qp_{n}}}{qp_{n}}=\frac{k}{q}.

(15)

For any
p\in\mathbb{P}
we consider the quantity

f_{q,k}(p)=b^{qp}-b^{q}-kp.

By Fermat’s little theorem, we have
f_{q,k}(p)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
for all
p\in\mathbb{P}
.
By Lemma
8
, there exists
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)]
such that

b^{x}-kx-b\equiv 0\pmod{q}

Assume that
p\in\mathbb{P}
satisfies
p\equiv x\pmod{q(q-1)}
, i.e.,
p=Nq(q-1)+x
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
. By a repeated application of Fermat’s little theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle f_{q,k}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b^{Nq^{2}(q-1)+qx}-b^{q}-k(Nq(q-1)+x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b^{q-1})^{q^{2}N}\times(b^{q})^{x}-b^{q}-q(kN(q-1))-kx

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle b^{x}-b-kx\pmod{q}

Hence, for any
p\in\mathbb{P}
that satisfies
p\equiv x\pmod{q(q-1)}
we have

f_{q,k}(p)\equiv 0\pmod{p}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{q,k}(p)\equiv 0\pmod{q}.

Since
p,q\in\mathbb{P}
, it follows that

f_{q,k}(p)\equiv 0\pmod{qp}
. In other words, for any
q\in\mathbb{P}
and
p\in\mathbb{P}
with
p\equiv x\pmod{q(q-1)}
, we have

b^{qp}=Mqp+b^{q}+kp

for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
By Dirichlet’s theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions
[
1
, Chapter 7]
, for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)]
there are infinitely many
p\in\mathbb{P}
with
p\equiv x\pmod{q(q-1)}
. Take a sequence
\{p_{n}\}
of such primes sufficiently big to have
b^{q}+kp_{n}<qp_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

b^{qp_{n}}\pmod{qp_{n}}=b^{q}+kp_{n}

Thus,

\frac{b^{qp_{n}}\pmod{qp_{n}}}{qp_{n}}=\frac{b^{q}}{p_{n}}+\frac{k}{q},

Whence, (
15
) follows.

We proceed to show that no other limit points exist. Taken any sequence
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{P}
and assume that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{b^{qp_{n}}\pmod{qp_{n}}}{qp_{n}}=l.

Clearly, there is
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)]
such that an infinite number of elements from
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfy

p_{n}\equiv x\pmod{q(q-1)}.

(16)

Let
\{p_{n_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be the sub-sequence consisting of all
p_{n}
that satisfies (
16
). Then

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{b^{qp_{n_{j}}}\pmod{qp_{n_{j}}}}{qp_{n_{j}}}=\frac{k}{q}

where
k
is the unique index such that
x\in Z_{k}
(such
k
exists due to (
10
)). By uniqueness of limit, we have
l=k/q
.
∎

Theorem 9
.

The following holds.

1.

For
k=0
, we have

Z_{0}=\{\alpha q+(q-1)\beta\pmod{q(q-1)}:\alpha\in\mathcal{N},\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]\}

(17)

2.

For each
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]
and
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,q-1\}
let
\beta_{k}(\alpha)
be the unique solution in
\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]
to the equation

b-k\beta\equiv b^{\alpha\pmod{\delta}}\pmod{q}.

(18)

Then

Z_{k}=\{x(\alpha):\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]\setminus\mathcal{N}\}.

(19)

where

x(\alpha)=\alpha q+\beta_{k}(\alpha)(q-1)\pmod{q(q-1)}

(20)

Proof.

Take
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q(q-1)]
. By Lemma
6

(ii)
, we have
x=\alpha q+\beta(q-1)\pmod{q(q-1)}
.
Assume first that
\alpha\in\mathcal{N}
, i.e.,
\alpha=1+n\delta
. It follows that for any
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]
there holds
b^{x}\equiv b^{q}\equiv b\pmod{q}\equiv b+0\times x\pmod{q}
, so clearly,
x\in Z_{0}
. On the other hand, if
\alpha\notin\mathcal{N}
, then
\alpha=r+n\delta
for
r\in\{0,2,\ldots,\delta-1\}
and in every case
b^{x}\equiv b^{rq}\equiv b^{r}\not\equiv b\pmod{q}
so for every
x=\alpha q+\beta(q-1)
with
\alpha\notin\mathcal{N}

we have
x\notin Z_{0}
. This proves (
17
).

Take now
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]\setminus\mathcal{N}
and
k\neq 0
. Write again
\alpha=r+n\delta
with
r\in\{0,2,\ldots,\delta-1\}
. As in the proof of Lemma
8
, the equation (
13
) holds. Thus, for fixed
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]\setminus\mathcal{N}
, there is a unique solution
\beta_{k}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q]
to (
18
). Furthermore,
b^{x}\equiv b^{r}\pmod{q}
so clearly
x(\alpha)
defined by (
20
) belongs to
Z_{k}
. Thus,

\{x(\alpha):\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]\setminus\mathcal{N}\}\subset Z_{k}.

On the other hand, take any
x=\alpha q+\beta(q-1)\in Z_{k}
. (By Lemma
6

(ii)
we may assume every
x\in Z_{k}
has this form.) It is clear that
\alpha\notin\mathcal{N}
, since otherwise
b^{x}\equiv b^{q}\equiv b\pmod{q}
and then

b^{x}\equiv kx+b\pmod{q}\Leftrightarrow b\equiv kx+b\pmod{q}\Leftrightarrow kx\equiv 0\pmod{q}

and this forces the contradiction
k=0
since
\gcd(x,q)=1
. Thus,
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]\setminus\mathcal{N}
. A very similar argument shows that for fixed
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]\setminus\mathcal{N}
the only possible value for
\beta
solves (
18
). Since the solution to (
18
) is unique, this concludes the proof of (
19
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
3
.

It is instructive to prove property
(ii)
first.
Assume that
b^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{q}
. This means that
\delta=|\langle b\rangle|=2
, so
\mathcal{N}
consists of the odd elements of
\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]
. On the other hand,
q-1
is even so
\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]
only contains odd elements. Consequently,
\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]=\mathcal{N}
. Since we cannot select
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}[q-1]\setminus\mathcal{N}
the sets
Z_{k}~~(k=1,2,\ldots,q-1)
are all empty. Hence,
