Large-scale dispersive estimates for acoustic operators: homogenization meets localization
Mitia Duerinckx, Antoine Gloria

Abstract
. This work relates quantitatively homogenization to Anderson localization for acoustic operators in disordered media. By blending dispersive estimates for homogenized operators and quantitative homogenization of the wave equation, we derive large-scale dispersive estimates for waves in disordered media that we apply to the spreading of low-energy eigenstates. This gives a short and direct proof that the lower spectrum of the acoustic operator is purely absolutely continuous in case of periodic media, and it further provides new lower bounds on the localization length of possible eigenstates in case of quasiperiodic or random media. AMS classification: 82B44, 35B27, 35L05, 47B80, 60H25



1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
General overview

Given a coefficient field
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
that is symmetric and uniformly elliptic in the sense of

(1.1)

e\cdot A(x)e\geq\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}|e|^{2},\qquad|A(x)e|\leq|e|,\qquad\text{for all $x,e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

for some
C_{0}\geq 1
, we consider the associated acoustic operator

H_{a}:=-\nabla\cdot A\nabla,\qquad\text{on $\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$}.

Depending on properties of
A
, the spectrum of
H_{a}
can be of different types, but not much is known in general: there has indeed been surprisingly few studies on the spectrum of this operator despite its role in the description of classical wave propagation in disordered media. Only two particular settings are well understood:

—

If the coefficient field
A
is periodic, then the lower spectrum of
H_{a}
is absolutely continuous in any dimension
d\geq 1
(see e.g.
[
31
, Section 6.3]
).
In fact, the whole spectrum of
H_{a}
is conjectured to be continuous in that case provided that
A
is smooth enough (see
[
31
, Conjecture 6.13]
), while large eigenvalues are known to exist in general in the non-smooth setting
[
23
]
.

—

If the coefficient field
A
is random (at least, in case of the so-called random displacement model defined in
[
37
, eqn (1.3)]
), and if the space dimension is
d=1
, then the operator
H_{a}
displays Anderson localization in the sense that it has dense pure point spectrum with exponentially decaying eigenstates
[
37
]
— just like
1
-dimensional random Schrödinger operators.

In the higher-dimensional random setting,
the comparison with random Schrödinger operators is less clear: for
d>2
, while Schrödinger operators are expected to display a metal–insulator transition, there is still no definite consensus on what to expect for the type of the lower spectrum of
H_{a}
.
The results in
[
22
]
show that Anderson localization holds for
H_{a}
near all spectral edges except near the bottom of the spectrum, where the situation remains unclear.
The particularity of the lower spectrum of
H_{a}
originates in its degeneracy:
the constant function
1
is always an extended state at energy
0=\inf\sigma(H_{a})
, in link with the expected degeneracy of Lyapunov exponents, cf.
[
37
]
.

In the present contribution, we focus on the spectrum of
H_{a}
close to the lowest energy
0
and we show that specific techniques such as homogenization theory
can be pushed forward to bring valuable new information in that spectral region, cf. Theorem
1.1
below.
This is nontrivial as homogenization theory is a priori far from giving any information on the resolvent of
H_{a}
close to its spectrum. Instead of elliptic homogenization, the crux of our approach is to build upon recent results on the long-time homogenization for the wave equation associated with
H_{a}
. More precisely, these hyperbolic homogenization results are used to first derive what we call large-scale dispersive estimates, cf. Theorem
1.4
below, from which we deduce strong estimates on the spatial spreading of eigenstates if any.

1.2.
Main result: spreading of eigenstates

Our main result below provides a strong control on the spatial spreading of the mass density of eigenstates in the lower spectrum (as well as on the spreading of their energy density, cf. Remark
1.2
). This is expressed as a lower bound on the spatial ‘width’, or localization length, of an eigenstate
\psi
if it exists, which we define as

(1.2)

\ell_{\theta}(\psi)\,:=\,\inf\big\{r\geq 0:\|\psi\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{r})}\geq{(1-\theta)\|\psi\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\big\},\qquad\text{given $0<\theta<\tfrac{1}{2}$}.

Our approach is quite robust and applies to essentially any kind of disorder for the coefficient field
A
under suitable statistical spatial homogeneity assumptions, which we illustrate by considering at the same time periodic, quasiperiodic, and random coefficient fields in any dimension. In the random setting, we only cover the strongest mixing conditions for conciseness.
1
1

1

Under weaker mixing conditions, we would be led to weaker lower bounds on the localization length, and we refer to
[
26
,
17
]
for the corresponding homogenization tools.

We emphasize that the spreading of low-energy eigenstates is particularly striking when compared to generic low-energy functions obtained by rescaling: indeed,
for
\phi_{\lambda}:=\lambda^{d/4}\phi(\lambda^{1/2}\cdot)
,
we find

(1.3)

\|(H_{a})^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\simeq\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\phi\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},\qquad\ell_{\theta}(\phi_{\lambda})=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ell_{\theta}(\phi),

while, in the result below, any possible eigenstate
\psi_{\lambda}
at energy
\lambda
is shown to have a width
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})
always substantially much larger than
O(\lambda^{-1/2})
.
The proof is given in Section
4
and is based on long-time hyperbolic homogenization in form of the large-scale dispersion estimates that we introduce in Section
1.4
below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
and let the coefficient field
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be symmetric and uniformly elliptic in the sense of (
1.1
).

(i)

Periodic setting:

Assume that
A
is periodic. Then there exists
\lambda_{0}>0
(only depending on
d,C_{0}
) such that the operator
H_{a}
admits no eigenvalue in
[0,\lambda_{0}]
.

(ii)

Quasiperiodic setting:

Assume that
A
takes the form

A(x)=A_{0}(Fx)

for some frequency matrix
F\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
, with
M>d
, and for some lifted map
A_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
that is periodic on
\mathbb{R}^{M}
and Gevrey regular. Further assume that the frequency matrix
F
satisfies a Diophantine condition, that is, for some
C,\gamma>0
,

\qquad|Fz|\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{C}|z|^{-\gamma},\qquad\text{for all $z\in\mathbb{Z}^{M}\setminus\{0\}$}.

Then, given
0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}
, there exists
\lambda_{\theta}>0
with the following property:
if
H_{a}
has an eigenvalue
\lambda\leq\lambda_{\theta}
, any associated eigenstate
\psi_{\lambda}
satisfies

\qquad\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\,\geq\,\exp(\lambda^{-\sigma}),

where we recall the notation (
1.2
) for the spatial spreading of
\psi_{\lambda}
, and where
\sigma>0
only depends on the Diophantine exponent
\gamma
and on the Gevrey regularity of
A_{0}
.

(iii)

Random setting:

Assume that
A=\tilde{A}(\cdot,\omega_{0})
is a realization of a random field
\tilde{A}
: more precisely, consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
and a statistically spatially homogeneous, uniformly elliptic, random field
\tilde{A}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, in the following sense,

—

Random field:

\tilde{A}
is a jointly measurable map
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the function
\tilde{A}(x,\cdot):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is measurable.

—

Symmetry and uniform ellipticity:
for
\mathbb{P}
-almost all
\omega
, the realization
\tilde{A}(\cdot,\omega)
is a symmetric matrix field and satisfies the uniform ellipticity condition (
1.1
).

—

Statistical spatial homogeneity:
the finite-dimensional law of
\tilde{A}
is shift-invariant, that is, for any finite set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
the law of
\{\tilde{A}(x+y,\cdot)\}_{x\in E}
does not depend on the shift
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Further assume that
\tilde{A}
satisfies strong enough mixing assumptions, for instance:

2

The same result holds if, instead of the finite range of dependence, we rather assume for instance that
\tilde{A}
has a Gaussian structure with integrable covariance — more precisely, this means to assume that
\tilde{A}
can be written as
\tilde{A}(x,\omega)=A_{0}(G(x,\omega))
, where
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a statistically spatially homogeneous Gaussian field, for some
p\geq 1
, where
A_{0}\in C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{p};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
, and where we require the covariance function of
G
to be written as
\mathbb{E}\left[G(x,\cdot)\otimes G(y,\cdot)\right]=(C_{0}\ast C_{0})(x-y)
for some even convolution kernel
C_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
satisfying the integrability condition
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\sup_{B(x)}|C_{0}|)\,dx<\infty
.

—

Finite range of dependence:
for all finite subsets
E,F\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\operatorname{dist}(E,F)\geq 1
, the families of random variables
(\tilde{A}(x,\cdot))_{x\in E}
and
(\tilde{A}(x,\cdot))_{x\in F}
are independent.

Then, given
0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a positive random variable
\lambda_{\theta,\varepsilon}
with finite (inverse) stretched exponential moments,

\qquad\mathbb{E}\Big[{\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{C}(\lambda_{\theta,\varepsilon})^{-\frac{1}{C}}\big)}\Big]<\infty,\qquad\text{for some $C<\infty$ (depending on $d,C_{0},\theta,\varepsilon$)},

such that the following property holds: for
\mathbb{P}
-almost all
\omega_{0}
, if for the coefficient field
A=\tilde{A}(\cdot,\omega_{0})
the operator
H_{a}
has an eigenvalue
\lambda\leq\lambda_{\theta,\varepsilon}(\omega_{0})
, any associated eigenstate
\psi_{\lambda}
satisfies

\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\,\geq\,\lambda^{\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{d}{2}+1)}.

Remark 1.2

(Energy distribution)
.

In the above result, in the definition (
1.2
) of the width of eigenstates, the local mass
\|\psi\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{r})}
can be replaced by the local (rescaled) energy: more precisely, for an eigenstate
\psi_{\lambda}
at energy
\lambda
, the same estimates hold as above if
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})
is replaced by

(1.4)

\ell_{\theta}^{\prime}(\psi_{\lambda})\,:=\,\inf\Big\{r\geq 0:\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\|\sqrt{A}\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{r})}\geq(1-\theta)\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big\}.

Note that the rescaling of the local energy is to ensure

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\|\sqrt{A}\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{r})}\,\xrightarrow{r\uparrow\infty}\,\big(\tfrac{1}{{\lambda}}\textstyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,=\,\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

In other words, we show that the spatial spreading of the mass density and of the energy density of eigenstates are comparable. A proof is included in Step 7 of Section
4
.

We briefly comment on the above result and compare it to the literature, separately discussing the periodic, quasi-periodic, and random settings:

—

Periodic setting:

In the periodic setting, item (i) is well known to the expert and is part of the folklore of Floquet theory, see e.g.
[
31
, Section 6.3]
.
In
[
4
]
, Armstrong, Kuusi, and Smart gave a different proof of (i), based on periodic elliptic homogenization theory in form of a large-scale analyticity result for
A
-harmonic functions,
which prevents the existence of
exponentially-localized
eigenstates in the lower spectrum.
To extend this to the non-existence of general
\operatorname{L}^{2}
eigenstates, the authors relied on a deep result by Kuchment based on Floquet theory, which states that the existence of an
\operatorname{L}^{2}
eigenstate would imply the existence of an exponentially-localized eigenstate (see
[
31
, Theorem 6.15]
).
In the present work, our proof of item (i) also relies on homogenization theory, but now applied to the wave equation rather than to the elliptic equation associated with the operator
H_{a}
. As opposed to
[
4
]
, the non-existence of general
\operatorname{L}^{2}
eigenstates follows directly from our arguments without relying on Floquet theory.
3
3

3

Our proof would in fact be substantially simpler if we restricted ourselves to the study of exponentially-localized eigenstates.

This latter point is crucial as it precisely allows us to extend our argument to more general disordered media, such as the quasiperiodic and random settings, which could not be covered so far by other available techniques.
In the same survey article
[
31
]
, Kuchment conjectures that there is no
\operatorname{L}^{2}
eigenstate even higher in the spectrum provided
A
is periodic and smooth enough — such a result is beyond the reach of arguments based on homogenization (which, by definition, focuses on low frequencies).

—

Quasiperiodic and random settings:

The results of items (ii) and (iii) in the quasiperiodic and random settings are new and provide lower bounds on the spatial spreading of the mass density of potential eigenstates in the lower spectrum. This is new even in the 1D random setting of
[
37
]
, where only the qualitative result
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\uparrow\infty
was known so far as
\lambda\downarrow 0
.
As stated above, our result further provides a lower bound
which reads
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\geq\lambda^{\varepsilon-3/4}
in the 1D random setting as
\lambda\downarrow 0
for any
0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}
and
\varepsilon>0
(see also Corollary
1.3
for an improved estimate in the specific setting of
[
37
]
).

Yet, we do not expect these convergence rates to be sharp. Indeed, in the quasiperiodic setting, we do not expect eigenvalues to exist at all in the lower spectrum: the strong spatial spreading stated in item (ii) should be seen as a partial indication in that direction — while the continuity of the lower spectrum remains an open problem. Note that in the quasiperiodic setting the story is still incomplete even for Schrödinger operators beyond the 1D case of
[
21
]
:
the existence of continuous spectrum has been established in
[
29
]
in dimension
d=2
only for very specific quasiperiodic potentials, without even ruling out the existence of localized eigenstates. In
[
18
]
, Shirley and the authors construct formal series for extended states, which somehow correspond to the corrector expansions that we consider in the sequel, but these are not absolutely convergent; the exact same difficulty arises for the long-time homogenization of acoustic waves in
[
17
]
.

As already mentioned, the problem is richer and even more poorly understood in the random setting, to the point that no definite conjecture has been formulated for the type of the lower spectrum of the operator
H_{a}
beyond the 1D case of
[
37
]
. Item (iii) does provide some new, nontrivial information, although it is not expected to be optimal.

—

Some improvements in the random setting:

The result of item (iii)
holds for any random coefficient field that satisfies some form of independence,
and in general it is the best result that can be obtained from our method.
Yet, this does not mean that additional assumptions cannot yield an improved scaling, and the 1D random displacement model of
[
37
]
turns out to be such an example. Indeed, the coefficient field
A
in that case is such that
A^{-1}
is hyperuniform in the sense of
[
39
]
, which means that the variance of large-scale spatial averages of
A^{-1}
decays faster than the CLT scaling.
In 1D, this is obviously transferred to averages of the first corrector gradient in homogenization theory (which is indeed proportional to
A^{-1}-\mathbb{E}[A^{-1}]
),
thus implying that the corrector for this specific hyperuniform 1D model has the same growth as a generic 2D corrector; see discussion in
[
15
, Section 2.7]
.
Hence, the proof of Theorem
1.1
yields the following improvement of Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\tilde{A}
be given by the 1D random displacement model of
[
37
, eqn (1.3)]
.
Given
0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a positive random variable
\lambda_{\theta,\varepsilon}
with finite (inverse) stretched exponential moments with the following property: for
\mathbb{P}
-almost all
\omega_{0}
, if for the coefficient field
A=\tilde{A}(\cdot,\omega_{0})
the operator
H_{a}
has an eigenvalue
\lambda\leq\lambda_{\theta,\varepsilon}(\omega_{0})
, any associated eigenstate
\psi_{\lambda}
satisfies

\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\,\geq\,\lambda^{\varepsilon-1}.

Hyperuniformity is not the only way to improve scalings: this issue is well-known to the expert in fluctuations in stochastic homogenization and is related in 1D to the Hermite rank, see
[
38
,
8
,
33
,
14
]
.
In any case, this observation suggests
that the localization length might depend on further specific features of the coefficient field, and that it could be for instance significantly larger for the random displacement model than for amorphous models such as Poisson models in 1D.

1.3.
General strategy: homogenization meets localization

We briefly describe the general idea
to use homogenization to obtain information on localized eigenstates.
As opposed to previous works based on spectral-theoretical methods, or on the study of the elliptic eigenvalue problem
H_{a}\psi_{\lambda}=\lambda\psi_{\lambda}
, we adopt a dynamical point of view and capitalize on recent advances on the long-time homogenization of heterogeneous wave equations
[
11
,
10
,
2
,
17
]
.
Let us first present the heuristic of our strategy (which, unfortunately, we cannot implement so straightforwardly).
Our argument is the following: if
H_{a}
admits an eigenvalue, we can consider the associated standing wave, which consists of time oscillations around the eigenstate and solves the heterogeneous wave equation. If the eigenvalue is close to
0
, homogenization theory can be applied and ensures that the low-energy standing wave must behave on some long time window like the solution of a corresponding ‘homogenized’ constant-coefficient wave equation: in particular, it must display ballistic transport as the latter on this long time window.
This should imply that the eigenstate cannot be too localized: indeed, if the mass
of the eigenstate were supported in a ball that was too small, then the ballistic transport inherited by homogenization would end up pushing the standing wave away from its support — a contradiction.
With this in mind, the statement of Theorem
1.1
is not surprising and can be expected to follow from long-time homogenization for waves.
For periodic coefficients, as homogenization holds up to arbitrarily long times at low energy, the argument should imply that no eigenvalue can exist in the lower spectrum, thus proving item (i) in Theorem
1.1
. For quasiperiodic and random coefficients, homogenization ultimately breaks down on very long times: this leaves the possibility of existence of eigenstates in the lower spectrum, but we should still be able to deduce nontrivial lower bounds on the spatial spreading of low-energy eigenstates as stated in items (ii) and (iii).

Although fairly natural, the above general strategy of using long-time homogenization in form of transport properties fails to prove Theorem
1.1
.
Indeed, this approach requires moment bounds on the eigenstate, which we do not assume and which cannot be fixed by an approximation argument.
Instead, we shall rely on another important property of homogenized wave equations, that is, dispersion.
More precisely, rather than describing how far the wave gets transported, we shall use dispersion to show that the wave vanishes away in the supremum norm: if the mass of the standing wave ends up getting too small on the support of the considered eigenstate, we still get a contradiction.
The question is thus how to use homogenization to show that low-energy waves in disordered media indeed have similar dispersive properties as waves in homogeneous media on some long time window.
This leads us to establishing what we call “large-scale dispersive estimates” as described in Section
1.4
below,
which should be put on the same footing as the large-scale elliptic regularity theory recently developed for heterogeneous elliptic operators
[
6
,
3
,
25
]
(see also the above-mentioned large-scale analyticity in
[
4
]
).

Another difficulty
is that potential eigenstates taken as initial data for the wave equation in our argument need not have a definite scaling in general:
they are oscillatory and do certainly not take the simple form of smooth rescaled bumps as considered in previous long-time homogenization results
[
10
,
2
,
17
]
(such a form would indeed yield suboptimal results, cf. (
1.3
)). Their oscillations thus need to be further monitored and controlled, preventing us from blindly appealing to the long-time homogenization results of
[
10
,
2
,
17
]
, see Section
2
. This is taken care of by a suitable approximation argument, see Step 1 in Section
3
.

As lower bounds on the localization length of eigenstates are deduced from the competition between dispersion and localization,
we are led to a natural comment regarding the expected suboptimality of the results obtained with this general strategy:
beyond the time window for ballistic transport and dispersion, one actually expects the existence of another time window with only diffusive transport. Improved lower bounds on the spatial width of eigenstates might then follow from the competition between this diffusive transport and localization, provided one can derive dispersive estimates from diffusive transport. However, diffusive transport has not been established yet in any form for low-energy waves (neither its relation to dispersive estimates), and remains an open problem.

1.4.
Large-scale dispersive estimates

We start by recalling the standard form of dispersive, i.e.
\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, estimates for classical waves in an homogeneous medium: in any dimension
d\geq 1
, given an initial condition
u^{\circ}\in\operatorname{L}^{1}\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that has Fourier transform supported in the unit ball
B
(say), we have for all
t\geq 0
,

(1.5)

\|\!\cos(t\sqrt{-\triangle})u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,(1+t)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

In case of localized data, a better time decay can actually be obtained for the supremum norm on the localized initial support when time exceeds the size of the support: more precisely, for any
\eta>0
, we further have for all
R,L\geq 1
and
t\gg R+L
,

(1.6)

\|\!\cos(t\sqrt{-\triangle})(u^{\circ}\mathds{1}_{B_{L}})\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(B_{R})}\,\lesssim_{\eta}\,(1+t)^{\eta-d}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(B_{L})}.

This second estimate easily follows from the standard proof of (
1.5
), based on a careful analysis of oscillatory Fourier integrals, see e.g.
[
7
, Section 8.1.3]
.

In the spirit of the large-scale elliptic regularity inherited by heterogeneous elliptic equations by homogenization
[
3
,
25
]
,
we may naturally investigate what kind of dispersion properties can be proved for waves in heterogeneous media.
As localized eigenvalues may appear in general for heterogeneous operators even in the lower spectrum, such as for
H_{a}
in the 1D random setting
[
37
]
, dispersive estimates (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) cannot hold in the same form: eigenvalues lead to time-periodic solutions that do not satisfy any time decay.

To motivate the form of the large-scale dispersive estimates that we shall state below,
we first comment on the periodic setting.
If
A
is a periodic uniformly elliptic coefficient field, we may expect to prove dispersion by analyzing the integral representation of the wave by Floquet–Bloch instead of Fourier theory. Yet, the operator
H_{a}
on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
may then have eigenvalues in the upper spectrum in general, cf.
[
23
]
, which would destroy dispersion: a projection
P_{c}
on the continuous part of the spectrum is necessary and we only expect for all
t\geq 0
, provided that
u^{\circ}
has again Fourier transform supported in
B
(say),

(1.7)

\|\!\cos(t\sqrt{H_{a}})P_{c}u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,(1+t)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Such an estimate has two main drawbacks in our context of general disordered media: it is an empty statement if the lower spectrum is pure point (which is already the case in the 1D random setting
[
37
]
), and moreover the projection
P_{c}
is not explicit and depends on the disorder.
We thus need to further reformulate this estimate.
In the periodic setting, as at least the lower spectrum of
H_{a}
is known to be purely absolutely continuous (see e.g.
[
31
, Section 6.3]
, or also Theorem
1.1
(i) above), the projection can be neglected in
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
up to an error estimated by the energy norm: if the spectrum is continuous below
\lambda_{0}>0
, we find

\|(1-P_{c})u\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\leq\,\lambda_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\sqrt{H_{a}}u\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,\lambda_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla u\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Averaging the above dispersive estimate (
1.7
) on a ball of size
R>0
, and noting that the assumption that the Fourier transform of
u^{\circ}
is supported in
B
can also be removed up to an additional error term involving the energy norm,
we are led to the following estimate without projection: for all
t\geq 0
,
R>0
, and
u^{\circ}\in\operatorname{L}^{1}\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(1.8)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{R^{-\frac{d}{2}}}\|\!\cos(t\sqrt{H_{a}})u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{R}}(x))}\,\lesssim\,(1+t)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\[-2.84526pt]
+(1\wedge\lambda_{0})^{-\frac{1}{2}}R^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Whereas (
1.7
) is a neat
\operatorname{L}^{\infty}-\operatorname{L}^{1}
estimate, this estimate (
1.8
) takes the form of a large-scale averaged
\operatorname{L}^{\infty}-\operatorname{L}^{1}
estimate with a correction term involving the energy norm.
There is obviously a trade-off in
R
in this estimate: the smaller
R
, the stronger the left-hand side;
the larger
R
, the smaller the correction term in the right-hand side. Note that this estimate (
1.8
) still encodes dispersive effects in the regime of times
t
and averaging
scales
R
for which the correction term can be absorbed into the left-hand side
(this is the way we shall proceed in the proof of Theorem
1.1
).
In view of the conservation of the
\operatorname{L}^{2}
norm, this estimate therefore bears information if the energy norm is much smaller than the
\operatorname{L}^{2}
norm, that is, at low Fourier modes.
This contrasts with the standard way how dispersive estimates such as (
1.5
) are exploited, that is, typically in form of Strichartz estimates to control high frequencies.

In order to take better advantage of (
1.8
) at low frequencies, we note that it can be naturally post-processed by scaling.
More precisely, the above periodic argument shows that this estimate is unchanged upon rescaling the underlying periodic coefficient field.
Applying (
1.8
) with
u^{\circ}
,
t
,
R
, and the coefficient field
a
replaced by
u^{\circ}(\frac{\cdot}{\kappa})
,
\kappa t
,
\kappa R
, and
a(\frac{\cdot}{\kappa})
, respectively, and changing variables, we obtain
for all
t\geq 0
,
\kappa,R>0
, and
u^{\circ}\in\operatorname{L}^{1}\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(1.9)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{R^{-\frac{d}{2}}}\|\!\cos(t\sqrt{H_{a}})u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{R}}(x))}\,\lesssim\,\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
+(1\wedge\lambda_{0})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\kappa^{-1}R^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

The additional frequency parameter
\kappa
in this estimate further increases the possible nontrivial trade-off between the terms.

We are now in position to state our main result on large-scale dispersive estimates. More precisely, we show that the estimate (
1.9
) indeed holds in the periodic setting (yet, in a slightly weaker version, with an additional error term
\kappa R^{-d/2}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
in the right-hand side), and we derive a corresponding improved estimate for localized data in the spirit of (
1.6
). Although this could be proved by means of Floquet–Bloch theory as suggested above starting from (
1.7
), we provide a different approach based on homogenization.
As it does not rely on Floquet–Bloch theory, our method can also be used to obtain corresponding results in the quasiperiodic and random settings.

Theorem 1.4

(Large-scale dispersive estimates)
.

Let
d\geq 1
, consider a coefficient field
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
that is symmetric and uniformly elliptic in the sense of (
1.1
),
denote by
U(t):=\cos(t\sqrt{H_{a}})
the associated heterogeneous wave flow on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
consider an initial condition
u^{\circ}\in\operatorname{L}^{1}\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Let also
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a smooth cut-off with
\chi(x)=1
for
|x|<\frac{1}{2}
and
\chi(x)=0
for
|x|>1
, and set
\chi_{L}:=\chi(\frac{\cdot}{L})
for
L\geq 1
.

(i)

Periodic setting:

Assume that
A
is periodic.
Then there exists a constant
0<\kappa_{*}\leq 1
such that we have
for all
0<\kappa\leq\kappa_{*}
and
t,R\geq 1
,

\qquad\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}R^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|U(t)u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{R}}(x))}\lesssim\,\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
+{R^{-\frac{d}{2}}}\Big(\kappa\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big).

In addition, given
\eta>0
, for all
0<\kappa\leq\kappa_{*}
,
R,L\geq 1
, and
t\gg R+L

\qquad R^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|U(t)(\chi_{L}u^{\circ})\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{R}})}\,\lesssim_{\eta}\,\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{\eta-d}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(B_{L})}\\
+R^{-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(\big(\kappa+(\kappa L)^{-1}\big)\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{L})}+\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{L})}\Big).

(ii)

Quasiperiodic setting:

Assume that
A
is quasiperiodic with Diophantine and smoothness conditions as in the statement of Theorem

(ii).
Then there exist an exponent

(only depending on the Diophantine and smoothness conditions on

for all

,

, and

,

\qquad\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{R^{-\frac{d}{2}}}\|U(t)u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{R}}(x))}\,\lesssim\,\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
+R^{-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(\kappa\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big).

In addition, given
\eta>0
, for all
0<\kappa\leq\kappa_{*}
,
R,L\geq 1
, and
R+L\ll t\leq\exp(\tfrac{1}{C}\kappa^{-\frac{1}{\theta}})
,

\qquad R^{-\frac{d}{2}}\|U(t)(\chi_{L}u^{\circ})\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\lesssim_{\eta}\,\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{\eta-d}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(B_{L})}\\
+R^{-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(\big(\kappa+(\kappa L)^{-1}\big)\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{L})}+\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{L})}\Big).

(iii)

Assume that
A=\tilde{A}(\cdot,\omega_{\circ})
is a realization of a random field
\tilde{A}
as in the statement of Theorem

(iii),
and set

{\alpha(d)\,:=\,\frac{d}{2}+1.}

For all

with finite (inverse) stretched exponential moments,

\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(C^{-1}\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}^{-1/C})\big]\,<\,\infty,\qquad\text{for some $C<\infty$ (depending on $d,C_{0},\varepsilon$)},

+R^{-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}(x)^{-2}\kappa^{r-\varepsilon}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big).

\displaystyle\hskip 65.44142pt+R^{-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(\big(\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}(0)^{-2}\kappa^{r-\varepsilon}+(\kappa L)^{-1}\big)\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{L})}+\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{L})}\Big).

Note that quite some attention has been devoted in the literature to the validity of dispersive estimates for waves in heterogeneous media. We refer to
[
36
]
and references therein for dispersive estimates for Schrödinger operators with a decaying potential field. Closer to the statistically spatially homogeneous setting considered in the present work, we also refer to
[
9
]
, where dispersive estimates for 1D quasiperiodic Schrödinger operators are deduced from the very precise known description of the spectrum of such operators.
In these references, dispersive estimates are exclusively sought in the standard form (
1.5
), possibly up to a projection on the continuous part of the spectrum as in (

). To our knowledge, the above result is the first investigation of weak dispersive estimates
with error terms involving the energy.

2.
A primer on long-time homogenization for waves

Homogenization theory aims at describing the behavior of solutions of PDEs with heterogeneous coefficients in the regime when the typical spatial scale of the coefficients is much smaller than the spatial scale of external forces and initial data: in such a regime, one expects the coefficients to average out in some sense, so that solutions of the PDE would be described to leading order by solutions of a corresponding ‘homogenized’ constant-coefficient PDE (possibly of a different type). We refer
to
[
11
]
for a systematic treatment of homogenization of various PDEs with periodic coefficients.

For the purpose of the present work, we shall consider the wave equation associated with the heterogeneous acoustic operator
H_{a}=-\nabla\cdot A\nabla
,

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}u_{\kappa}=\nabla\cdot A\nabla u_{\kappa},\\
u_{\kappa}|_{t=0}=u^{\circ}_{\kappa},\\
\partial_{t}u_{\kappa}|_{t=0}=0,\end{array}\right.

with some (smooth) initial condition
u^{\circ}_{\kappa}\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
If the initial condition is localized on low frequencies
0<\kappa\ll 1
(say for instance
u_{\kappa}^{\circ}=\kappa^{d/2}u^{\circ}(\kappa\cdot)
), then there is a scale separation with respect to variations of
A
on the unit scale,
so that homogenization is expected to hold — under suitable statistical spatial homogeneity assumptions on the coefficient field
A
(for instance, if
A
is periodic, quasiperiodic, or if
A
is a typical realization of a random field with statistical spatial homogeneity as in Theorem
1.1
(iii)).
More precisely, as shown e.g. in
[
12
]
in the periodic setting, it is a standard result of homogenization theory that
u_{\kappa}
is then close to the solution
\bar{u}_{\kappa}
of the homogenized wave equation

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}\bar{u}_{\kappa}=\nabla\cdot\bar{A}\nabla\bar{u}_{\kappa},\\
\bar{u}_{\kappa}|_{t=0}=u^{\circ}_{\kappa},\\
\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{\kappa}|_{t=0}=0,\end{array}\right.

on any compact time interval
[0,\kappa^{-1}T]
, where the homogenized coefficient
\bar{A}
is some constant matrix.
Recall that
\bar{A}
only depends on
A
and is in particular independent of initial data. What is less standard and was only established recently, is the quantification of the maximal timescale up to which
u_{\kappa}
indeed remains close to
\bar{u}_{\kappa}
. In the periodic setting, as first understood in
[
35
]
, this holds only up to times
t\ll\kappa^{-3}
, while on longer timescales the homogenized equation (
2.2
) is no longer accurate and dispersive corrections need to be added to the homogenized operator
-\nabla\cdot\bar{A}\nabla
.
More precisely, as shown in
[
10
,
17
]
, there exist higher-order constant tensors
\{\bar{A}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
with
\bar{A}^{1}=\bar{A}
such that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
the heterogeneous solution
u_{\kappa}
is well-approximated up to times
t\ll\kappa^{-N-1}
by the solution
\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}
of the corrected homogenized wave equation

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}=\nabla\cdot\big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{A}^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\nabla^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\big)\nabla\bar{u}_{\kappa,N},\\
\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}|_{t=0}=u^{\circ}_{\kappa},\\
\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}|_{t=0}=0.\end{array}\right.

Of course, the maximal timescale up to which the result holds strongly depends on assumptions on
A
: whereas in the periodic setting one can take any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the result can only hold up to some maximal exponent
N_{0}
in the random setting (which in 1D can be related to Anderson localization
[
37
]
).

In order to motivate the upcoming results, let us quickly describe informally why
u_{\kappa}
might indeed be described by the solution
\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}
of a homogenized equation. To this aim, we follow the approach introduced in
[
10
]
and further developed in
[
17
]
, based on so-called “approximate spectral theory” (that builds upon the first results
[
32
,
13
]
in the field).
Starting point is to try diagonalizing the acoustic operator
H_{a}=-\nabla\cdot A\nabla
at the bottom of its spectrum by means of extended states in form of Bloch waves
x\mapsto e^{ix\cdot\xi}\chi_{\xi}(x)
, where
\chi_{\xi}
would have the same type of statistical spatial homogeneity as
A
.
In the periodic case, this is precisely realized by Floquet theory, see e.g.
[
31
]
, whereas in the random case statistically homogeneous Bloch waves are not expected to exist
[
20
]
. With this failure of Floquet theory, we are led to rather look for an approximate diagonalization of
H_{a}
.
More precisely, we start with the following observation: in the periodic setting, as shown e.g. in
[
1
]
, the gradient of the Bloch wave
\chi_{\xi}
with respect to the wavenumber
\xi
at
0
coincides with the so-called corrector
\phi
from elliptic homogenization theory, that is, the periodic solution (with vanishing average) of

-\nabla\cdot A(\nabla\phi+\operatorname{Id})=0.

In other words, one has a Taylor expansion
\chi_{\xi}=1+i\xi_{j}\phi_{j}+O(|\xi|^{2})
for the Bloch wave.
As shown in
[
10
]
, this expansion can be pursued to higher orders and we find

\chi_{\xi}\,=\,\sum_{n=0}^{N}(i\xi)^{\otimes n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+O(|\xi|^{N+1})

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
in the periodic setting, where the coefficients
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n}
are so-called higher-order (spectral) correctors and satisfy some hierarchy of PDEs.
The main idea in
[
10
]
was then to replace the use of the Bloch wave
\chi_{\xi}
by its higher-order expansions for all practical purposes: the advantage is that
correctors
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n}
can be defined in settings when Bloch waves do not exist (or are not known to exist) — for instance, all of them can easily be defined in the quasiperiodic setting (under some Diophantine condition), and at least a finite number of them in the random setting (under suitable mixing assumptions).
The drawback is of course that these expansions do not exactly diagonalize the acoustic operator
H_{a}=-\nabla\cdot A\nabla
: there is a remainder in the generalized eigenvalue equation (called eigendefect in
[
10
]
). Still, this yields an approximate diagonalization of
H_{a}
and we are led to an approximation of
u_{\kappa}
by means of a so-called two-scale expansion of the form

(2.4)

\sum_{n=0}^{N}\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,N},

for some (smooth) effective profile
\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}
. Inserting this ansatz into (
2.1
) easily shows that this profile
\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}
must solve an homogenized wave equation of the form (
2.3
). For initial data
u_{\kappa}^{\circ}
localized on low frequencies
0<\kappa\ll 1
,
we find
\nabla^{n}\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}=O(\kappa^{n})
in
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so the above expansion (
2.4
) coincides with
\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}
to leading order. This formally justifies why
\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}
indeed provides a good description of
u_{\kappa}
in
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Yet, the whole approach only works provided that we can control the error made by using an approximate diagonalization of
H_{a}
, and this depends on the size of the remainder in the eigenvalue equation: the smaller this error, the longer the time the approximate solution
\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}
remains close to
u_{\kappa}
.
If the corrector
\phi^{N}
is a bounded function, the remainder in the eigenvalue equation is essentially
O(\kappa^{N+1})
, and then using energy estimates on the wave equation leads us to an approximation error
u_{\kappa}-\bar{u}_{\kappa,N}=O(\kappa+t\kappa^{N+1})
in
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
This is the origin for the timescale condition
t\ll\kappa^{-N-1}
mentioned above.

The quantitative analysis of corrector equations has been central in modern homogenization, and we have by now very accurate bounds on correctors
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n}
in various settings, building up on
[
30
]
in the quasiperiodic setting and on
[
27
,
28
,
5
]
in the random setting.
Before we state these bounds, let us give the precise form of the hierarchy of corrector equations.
To cover all settings at once, we formulate these equations in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, while implicitly understanding that we look for periodic solutions in the periodic setting, for quasiperiodic solutions in the quasiperiodic setting, and for statistically spatially homogeneous solutions (when possible) in the random setting. We also denote by
\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\right]
the ‘mean’ or infinite-volume average, which is understood as average over the periodicity cell in the periodic setting, average over the underlying high-dimensional periodicity cell in the quasiperiodic setting, and expectation in the random setting.
With this notation, correctors
\phi^{n}=(\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}})_{1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\leq d}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n})
are defined inductively by
\phi^{0}=1
and by the following equations, for all
n\geq 1
,

(2.5)

\qquad-\nabla\cdot A\nabla\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\,=\,\nabla\cdot\big(A\phi^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\big)+e_{j_{n}}\cdot A\big(\nabla\phi^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}+\phi^{n-2}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-2}}e_{j_{n-1}}\big)\\
-\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}e_{j_{n}}\cdot\bar{A}^{m}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{m-1}}\phi^{n-m-1}_{j_{m}\ldots j_{n-2}}e_{j_{n-1}},

where we choose
\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}
with zero mean
\mathbb{E}[\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}]=0
(when possible),
and where the constant tensors
\{\bar{A}^{m}\}_{m}
are iteratively chosen to make sure that the right-hand side of (
2.5
) also has zero mean. More precisely, this amounts to defining for all
m\geq 1
,

\bar{A}^{m}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{m-1}}e_{j_{m}}\,:=\,\mathbb{E}\big[A\big(\nabla\phi^{m}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{m}}+\phi_{j_{1}\ldots j_{m-1}}^{m-1}e_{j_{m}}\big)\big].

We implicitly set
\phi^{-1}=0
for notational convenience.
As usual in homogenization theory, it is convenient to further introduce flux correctors
\sigma^{n}:=(\sigma^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}})_{1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\leq d}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n})
:
for all
n\geq 1
, we define

\sigma^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}=\nabla\Phi_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n},

where
\Phi_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}\in H^{2}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies

\triangle\Phi_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}=A\big(\nabla\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\phi^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\big)-\sum_{m=1}^{n}\bar{A}^{m}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{m-1}}\phi^{n-m}_{j_{m}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}.

In particular, if well-defined, we note that
this construction ensures for all
n\geq 1
,

(2.6)

\nabla\cdot\sigma_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}=A\big(\nabla\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\phi^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\big)-\sum_{m=1}^{n}\bar{A}^{m}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{m-1}}\phi^{n-m}_{j_{m}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}.

We also recall that
\bar{A}^{n}=0
for
n
even, if well-defined, cf.
[
10
,
17
]
, which ensures the symmetry of the differential operator in the homogenized wave equation (
2.3
).
Quantitative homogenization theory provides the following optimal corrector estimates; note how the result strongly varies between the periodic, quasiperiodic, and random settings.

Lemma 2.1

(Correctors in periodic setting; e.g.
[
11
,
16
]
)
.

Assume that the coefficient field
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is symmetric and uniformly elliptic in the sense of (
1.1
), and is periodic on
Q=[0,1)^{d}
. Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the correctors
(\phi^{n},\sigma^{n})
are uniquely defined as mean-zero
Q
-periodic functions and satisfy

\|(\phi^{n},\sigma^{n})\|_{H^{1}(Q)}\,\leq\,C^{n},\qquad|\bar{A}^{n}|\,\leq\,C^{n}.

Lemma 2.2

(Correctors in quasiperiodic setting; e.g.
[
30
,
24
,
18
]
)
.

Assume that the coefficient field
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is symmetric and uniformly elliptic in the sense of (
1.1
), and takes the form

A(x)=A_{0}(Fx)
for some frequency matrix
F\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
with
M>d
, and for some lifted map
A_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
that is periodic on
\mathbb{R}^{M}
and Gevrey-regular. Further assume that the frequency matrix
F
satisfies a Diophantine condition, that is, for some
C,\gamma>0
,

|Fz|\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{C}|z|^{-\gamma},\qquad\text{for all $z\in\mathbb{Z}^{M}\setminus\{0\}$}.

Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the correctors
(\phi^{n},\sigma^{n})
are uniquely defined as mean-zero quasiperiodic functions and satisfy

\|(\phi^{n},\psi^{n})\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\leq\,(Cn^{\theta})^{n},\qquad|\bar{A}^{n}|\,\leq\,(Cn^{\theta})^{n},

for some exponent
\theta>0
only depending on
\gamma
and on the Gevrey regularity of
A_{0}
.

Lemma 2.3

(Correctors in random setting; e.g.
[
26
,
3
,
19
,
17
]
)
.

Assume that the coefficient field is given by a symmetric, uniformly elliptic, statistically spatially homogeneous random field
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, constructed on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
, and further assume that it satisfies strong enough mixing assumptions, as for instance having finite range of dependence, in the sense of Theorem
1.1
(iii).
Then, the corrector gradients
(\nabla\phi^{n},\nabla\sigma^{n})
are uniquely defined as mean-zero statistically spatially homogeneous random fields for all
1\leq n\leq\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
, such that the correctors
(\phi^{n},\sigma^{n})
are themselves mean-zero statistically homogeneous fields for
1\leq n<\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
, and such that corrector equations with coefficients
A(\cdot,\omega)
are satisfied by
\{\phi^{n}(\cdot,\omega),\sigma^{n}(\cdot,\omega)\}_{n}
for
\mathbb{P}
-almost all
\omega
.
The homogenized tensors
\bar{A}^{m}
are then well-defined for all
1\leq m\leq\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
.
Moreover, for all
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>\frac{1}{2}
, there exists a random variable
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
with finite stretched-exponential moments,

\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(C^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}^{1/C})\big]<\infty,\qquad\text{for some $C<\infty$ (depending on $d,C_{0},\varepsilon,\delta$)},

such that,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely,

—

for all
n\leq\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor
,

\quad|(\phi^{n},\sigma^{n})(x)|+|(\nabla\phi^{n},\nabla\sigma^{n})(x)|\,\leq\,\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\langle x\rangle^{\varepsilon};

—

for
d
odd and
n=\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
,

(2.7)

\quad|(\phi^{n},\sigma^{n})(x)|\,\leq\,\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\langle x\rangle^{\delta}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|(\nabla\phi^{n},\nabla\sigma^{n})(x)|\,\leq\,\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\langle x\rangle^{\varepsilon}.

For the proof of long-time homogenization, and in particular in our application to large-scale dispersive estimates in the random setting, the algebraic growth of the corrector of order
n=\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
(when
d
is odd) is problematic and only leads to pessimistic error bounds. Instead, it is more advantageous to introduce an artificial cut-off at low frequencies (e.g. in the spirit of the approximation of homogenized coefficients in
[
24
]
).
Such a cut-off was not used in previous works on long-time homogenization
[
10
,
17
]
and was inspired to us by a recent related work on the long-wave approximation of random FPUT lattices
[
34
]
; a detailed account of this improvement is postponed to a separate note, which we briefly summarize here.
Next to the above-defined corrector
\phi^{n}
, for
n\leq\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
, in the random setting of Lemma
2.3
, we define its massive version
\phi^{n}_{\kappa}
as the unique statistically spatially homogeneous solution of

(2.8)

\kappa^{2}\phi^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}-\nabla\cdot A\nabla\phi^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\,=\,\nabla\cdot\big(A\phi^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\big)+e_{j_{n}}\cdot A\big(\nabla\phi^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}+\phi^{n-2}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-2}}e_{j_{n-1}}\big)\\
-\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}e_{j_{n}}\cdot\bar{A}^{m}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{m-1}}\phi^{n-m-1}_{j_{m}\ldots j_{n-2}}e_{j_{n-1}},

which corresponds to the corrector equation (
2.5
) with a massive term
\kappa^{2}
.
Note that the well-posedness of this equation is ensured by the massive term.
This gives rise to the massive approximation
\bar{A}^{n}_{\kappa}
of
\bar{A}^{n}
that we define by

\bar{A}^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\,:=\,\mathbb{E}\big[A\big(\nabla\phi^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\phi_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}^{n-1}e_{j_{n}}\big)\big].

Because of the massive term in (
2.8
), the flux
A\big(\nabla\phi^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\phi_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}^{n-1}e_{j_{n}}\big)
is not divergence-free, and one cannot put

A\big(\nabla\phi^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\phi_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}^{n-1}e_{j_{n}}\big)-\bar{A}^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}-\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\bar{A}^{m}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{m-1}}\phi^{n-m}_{j_{m}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}

exactly in divergence form as we do in (
2.6
). Similarly as in
[
25
]
, there is a defect and we are led to the decomposition

(2.9)

A\big(\nabla\phi^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\phi_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}^{n-1}e_{j_{n}}\big)-\bar{A}^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}-\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\bar{A}^{m}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{m-1}}\phi^{n-m}_{j_{m}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\\
=\nabla\cdot\sigma^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\kappa g^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}},

where we define

\sigma^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}:=\nabla\Phi^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}=-\kappa\Phi^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}},

in terms of the unique statistically spatially homogeneous solution
\Phi_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}
of

\kappa^{2}\Phi_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}-\triangle\Phi_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}\\
=\,-A\big(\nabla\phi^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\phi_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}^{n-1}e_{j_{n}}\big)+\bar{A}^{n}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}+\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\bar{A}^{m}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{m-1}}\phi^{n-m}_{j_{m}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}.

In the setting of Lemma
2.3
, for
n=\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
, we have, instead of (
2.7
),

(2.10)

\displaystyle|(\phi^{n}_{\kappa},\sigma^{n}_{\kappa},g^{n}_{\kappa})(x)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\langle x\rangle^{\varepsilon}\kappa^{-\delta},

\displaystyle|(\nabla\phi^{n}_{\kappa},\nabla\sigma^{n}_{\kappa})(x)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\langle x\rangle^{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle|\bar{A}^{n}_{\kappa}-\bar{A}^{n}|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\kappa,

where now
\delta=0
if
d
is even and
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
if
d
is odd.
In the periodic and quasiperiodic settings, the massive correctors remain uniformly bounded.

3.
Proof of the large-scale dispersive estimates

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
Given an initial condition
u^{\circ}\in\operatorname{L}^{1}\cap H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with normalization
\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
, consider the solution
u^{t}=U(t)u^{\circ}
of the heterogeneous wave equation

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}u=\nabla\cdot A\nabla u,\\
u|_{t=0}=u^{\circ},\\
\partial_{t}u|_{t=0}=0.\end{array}\right.

We split the proof into six main steps.

Step
1. Regularization of initial data.

We wish to appeal to homogenization theory for (
3.1
) while only assuming that the initial data
u^{\circ}
has low energy
\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\ll 1
. Long-time homogenization results
[
10
,
2
,
17
]
however require a finer control on the low-frequency nature of initial data, typically in form of improved smallness for higher derivatives
\nabla^{k}u^{\circ}
,
k\geq 1
.
To deal with this issue, we start by defining a suitable regularization of initial data
u^{\circ}
:
given a nonnegative cut-off function
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B)
with
\chi=1
on
\frac{1}{2}B
, and given a nonnegative convolution kernel
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho=1
, whose Fourier transform
\hat{\rho}
is supported in
B
,
we consider the rescalings

\chi_{L}\,:=\,\chi(\tfrac{\cdot}{L}),\qquad\rho_{\kappa}\,:=\,\kappa^{d}\rho(\kappa\cdot),

where

L\geq 1
and

0<\kappa\leq 1
will be properly chosen later on in the proof. We then define the regularized initial condition

(3.2)

u_{\kappa,L}^{\circ}\,:=\,\rho_{\kappa}\ast(\chi_{L}u^{\circ}),

and we consider the corresponding wave equation,

(3.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}u_{\kappa,L}=\nabla\cdot A\nabla u_{\kappa,L},\\
u_{\kappa,L}|_{t=0}=u_{\kappa,L}^{\circ},\\
\partial_{t}u_{\kappa,L}|_{t=0}=0.\end{array}\right.

Comparing with (
3.1
) and appealing to the finite speed of propagation
c\simeq 1
, which implies that at time
t
we have
(U(t)u^{\circ})|_{B_{R}}=(U(t)(u^{\circ}\mathds{1}_{B_{R+ct}})|_{B_{R}}
, we find
for all
R,t\geq 0
and
L\geq 4(R+ct)
,

\displaystyle\|u^{t}-u_{\kappa,L}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|u^{\circ}-u_{\kappa,L}^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R+ct})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\rho_{\kappa}*(u^{\circ}(1-\chi_{L}))\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{L/4})}+\|u^{\circ}-\rho_{\kappa}\ast u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Using that
1-\chi_{L}
vanishes on
B_{L/2}
, using Jensen’s inequality with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\kappa}=1
, and using that
\rho
has superalgebraic decay at infinity, we find for the first right-hand side term, for any
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\|\rho_{\kappa}*(u^{\circ}(1-\chi_{L}))\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{L/4})}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{L/4}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{\kappa}(x-y)u^{\circ}(y)(1-\chi_{L}(y))\,dy\Big)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B_{L/4}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{L/2}}\rho_{\kappa}(x-y)|u^{\circ}(y)|\,dy\Big)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u^{\circ}|^{2}\Big)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{L/4}}\rho_{\kappa}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\lesssim_{n}

\displaystyle(\kappa L)^{-2n}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

Further noting that

\|u^{\circ}-\rho_{\kappa}\ast u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\leq\,\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|y|^{2}\rho_{\kappa}(y)\,dy\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

the above becomes, for all
R,t\geq 0
,
L\geq 4(R+ct)
, and
n\geq 1
,

(3.4)

\|u^{t}-u_{\kappa,L}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\leq\,C_{n}(\kappa L)^{-n}+C\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

If the initial condition
u^{\circ}
is supported in
B_{L/2}
,
we do not need the finite speed of propagation and we simply find for all
t,R\geq 0
,

(3.5)

\|u^{t}-u_{\kappa,L}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\leq\,C\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Step
2. Higher-order correctors and homogenized equations.

We assume that we can iteratively construct
N\geq 1
controlled correctors
\{\phi^{n},\sigma^{n}\}_{1\leq n<N}

and
\{\phi^{N}_{\kappa},\sigma^{N}_{\kappa},g^{N}_{\kappa}\}
, and homogenized tensors
\{\bar{A}^{n}\}_{1\leq n<N}
and
\bar{A}^{N}_{\kappa}
in the sense of Section
2
with the following pointwise bounds,

(3.6)

\displaystyle|\bar{A}^{n}|\leq K_{n}\quad\text{for all\penalty\ \mbox{$1\leq n<N$}},\quad{|\bar{A}^{N}_{\kappa}|\leq K_{N},}

\displaystyle|(\phi^{n},\sigma^{n})(x)|+|(\nabla\phi^{n},\nabla\sigma^{n})(x)|\leq K_{n}\langle x\rangle^{\varepsilon},\quad\text{for all\penalty\ \mbox{$1\leq n<N$}},

\displaystyle{|(\phi^{N}_{\kappa},\sigma^{N}_{\kappa},g_{\kappa}^{N})(x)|\leq K_{N}\langle x\rangle^{\varepsilon}\kappa^{-\delta},\quad|(\nabla\phi^{N}_{\kappa},\nabla\sigma^{N}_{\kappa})(x)|\leq K_{n}\langle x\rangle^{\varepsilon}}

for some constants
K_{0},\ldots,K_{N}\geq 1
and some exponents
0\leq\varepsilon\leq\delta<1
.
In the sequel of the proof, we argue by assuming that those properties are satisfied in this general form, before particularizing them to the specific case of periodic, quasiperiodic, and random coefficient fields, as described in Section
2
. In a nutshell:

—

in the periodic case, the above bounds hold with
N\uparrow\infty
,
\varepsilon=\delta=0
, and
K_{n}=C^{n}
;

—

in the quasiperiodic case, under a Diophantine condition and Gevrey regularity, the above bounds hold with
N\uparrow\infty
,
\varepsilon=\delta=0
, and
K_{n}=(Cn^{\theta})^{n}
for some
\theta>0
;

—

in the random case, under suitable mixing assumptions, the above bounds hold with only
N=\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
, with any
\varepsilon>0
, and with
\delta=0
if the spatial dimension
d
is even and
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
if
d
is odd.

For notational convenience, when there is no confusion, we drop the subscript
\kappa
in correctors and homogenized tensors of order
N
.

In terms of the above homogenized tensors, we consider the associated (constant-coefficient) homogenized wave equation

(3.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}=\nabla\cdot\big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{A}^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\nabla^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\big)\nabla\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N},\\
\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}|_{t=0}=u_{\kappa,L}^{\circ},\\
\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}|_{t=0}=0.\end{array}\right.

This equation is however ill-posed for general initial conditions due to the lack of definiteness of the symbol

(3.8)

\mu_{N}(i\xi):=\xi\cdot\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}(i\xi)^{\otimes(n-1)}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\bar{A}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}^{n}\Big)\xi.

To circumvent this issue, first recall that the uniform ellipticity condition (
1.1
) entails that
\bar{A}=\bar{A}^{1}
also satisfies

(3.9)

\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}|e|^{2}\,\leq\,e\cdot\bar{A}e\,\leq\,C_{0}|e|^{2},\qquad\text{for all $e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

see e.g.
[
11
]
.
Therefore, using the above bounds (
3.6
),
we find for the symbol
\mu_{N}
, for all
\xi\in\kappa B
,

(3.10)

\mu_{N}(i\xi)\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\geq\,\xi\cdot\bar{A}\xi\big(1-C_{0}\sum_{n=2}^{N}K_{n}\kappa^{n-1}\big)\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{2}\xi\cdot\bar{A}\xi\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{2C_{0}}|\xi|^{2},\\
\\
\leq\,\xi\cdot\bar{A}\xi\big(1+C_{0}\sum_{n=2}^{N}K_{n}\kappa^{n-1}\big)\,\leq\,\tfrac{3}{2}\xi\cdot\bar{A}\xi\,\leq\,\tfrac{3C_{0}}{2}|\xi|^{2},\end{array}\right.

provided that
\kappa
is small enough in the sense of

(3.11)

\sum_{n=2}^{N}K_{n}\kappa^{n-1}\,\leq\,\tfrac{1}{2C_{0}}.

(The symbol
\mu_{N}
is obviously real-valued as
\bar{A}^{n}=0
for
n
even, cf.
[
10
,
17
]
.)
Noting that by definition the initial Fourier transform
\hat{u}_{\kappa,L}^{\circ}
is supported in
\kappa B
, just as
\hat{\rho}_{\kappa}
, and using that by (
3.10
) the symbol is definite and bounded in
\kappa B
, equation (
3.7
) admits a unique solution given by the Fourier formula

(3.12)

\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}(x)\,:=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\cos\big(t\sqrt{\mu_{N}(i\xi)}\big)\,\hat{u}^{\circ}_{\kappa,L}(\xi)\,d^{*}\xi.

Step
3. Two-scale analysis for
u_{\kappa,L}
.

In terms of the above correctors, in the spirit of (
2.4
), we claim that the two-scale expansion

\sum_{n=0}^{N}\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}

provides a good approximation for
u_{\kappa,L}
.
To prove that, we proceed by examining the wave equation satisfied by the error.
Applying the wave operator, we obtain

\big(\partial_{t}^{2}-\nabla\cdot A\nabla\big)\Big(u_{\kappa,L}-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\Big)\,=\,-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\partial_{t}^{2}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\\
+\sum_{n=0}^{N}\nabla\cdot(A\nabla\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}})\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}+\sum_{n=0}^{N}e_{j_{n+1}}\cdot A\nabla\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\nabla^{n+1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n+1}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\\
+\sum_{n=0}^{N}\nabla\cdot(A\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}e_{j_{n+1}})\nabla^{n+1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n+1}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}+\sum_{n=0}^{N}e_{j_{n+2}}\cdot A\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}e_{j_{n+1}}\nabla^{n+2}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n+2}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}.

Inserting the homogenized wave equation (
3.7
) for
\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}
in the first right-hand side term for
n<N
, relabeling the different sums, and recognizing the corrector equations (
2.5
) and (
2.8
), this becomes

\big(\partial_{t}^{2}-\nabla\cdot A\nabla\big)\Big(u_{\kappa,L}-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\Big)\\
\,=\,\phi^{N}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}(\kappa^{2}-\partial_{t}^{2})\nabla^{N}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}+e_{j_{N+1}}\cdot A\big(\nabla\phi^{N}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}+\phi^{N-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{N-1}}e_{j_{N}}\big)\nabla^{N+1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{N+1}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\\
+\nabla\cdot(A\phi^{N}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}e_{j_{N+1}})\nabla^{N+1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{N+1}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}+e_{j_{N+2}}\cdot A\phi^{N}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}e_{j_{N+1}}\nabla^{N+2}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{N+2}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\\
-\sum_{n=N+1}^{2N}\sum_{m=n-N}^{N}e_{j_{n}}\cdot\phi^{n-m-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-m-1}}\bar{A}^{m}_{j_{n-m}\ldots j_{n-2}}e_{j_{n-1}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N},

or equivalently, after recombining the terms and using the definition (
2.9
) of the flux correctors
\sigma^{N}_{\kappa}
and
g^{N}_{\kappa}
,

(3.13)

\big(\partial_{t}^{2}-\nabla\cdot A\nabla\big)\Big(u_{\kappa,L}-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\Big)\\
\,=\,\nabla\cdot\Big(\big(A\phi^{N}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}+(\sigma^{N}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{N}})^{T}\big)\nabla\nabla^{N}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\Big)-\partial_{t}\Big(\phi^{N}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}\nabla^{N}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\Big)\\
+\Big(\kappa^{2}\phi^{N}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\kappa g^{N}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}\cdot\nabla-\sigma^{N}_{\kappa,j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}:\nabla^{2}\Big)\nabla^{N}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{N}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\\
-\sum_{n=N+2}^{2N}\sum_{m=n-N}^{N}e_{j_{n}}\cdot\phi^{n-m-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-m-1}}\bar{A}^{m}_{j_{n-m}\ldots j_{n-2}}e_{j_{n-1}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}.

We now use the following standard estimate for the wave equation, see e.g.
[
17
, Lemma B.1]
: if
v
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\nabla\cdot A\nabla)v=\nabla\cdot f+\partial_{t}g+h,\\
v|_{t=0}=v^{\circ},\\
\partial_{t}v|_{t=0}=0,\end{array}\right.

for some
f,g,h,v^{\circ}
with
g|_{t=0}=0
, then we have

(3.14)

\|v^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,\|v^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+t\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\|(f^{s},g^{s})\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+t\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\Big\|\int_{0}^{s}h\Big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Applying this to the above wave equation (
3.13
),
we get

\Big\|u_{\kappa,L}^{t}-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\Big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,A_{\kappa,L,N}^{\circ}+t\big(B_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}+C_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}+D_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\big),

and thus by the triangle inequality,

(3.15)

\|u_{\kappa,L}^{t}-\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,A_{\kappa,L,N}^{\circ}+A_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}+t\big(B_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}+C_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}+D_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\big),

where we have set for abbreviation

(3.16)

\displaystyle A_{\kappa,L,N}^{\circ}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Big\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}u^{\circ}_{\kappa,L}\Big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

\displaystyle A_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Big\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\phi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\Big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

\displaystyle B_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\Big\|(\phi^{N}_{\kappa},\sigma^{N}_{\kappa})(\partial_{s},\nabla)\nabla^{N}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{s}\Big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle C_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\Big\|\big(\kappa^{2}\phi^{N}_{\kappa}+\kappa g^{N}_{\kappa}\cdot\nabla-\sigma^{N}_{\kappa}:\nabla^{2}\big)\nabla^{N}\int_{0}^{s}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\Big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

\displaystyle D_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=N+2}^{2N}\sum_{m=n-N}^{N}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\Big\|\phi^{n-m-1}\bar{A}^{m}\nabla^{n}\int_{0}^{s}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\Big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Step
4. Proof that, under assumption (
3.11
), we have for all
t\geq 0
,

(3.17)

\displaystyle A_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(\kappa+K_{N}\kappa^{N-\delta})(L+\kappa^{-1}+t)^{\varepsilon},

(3.18)

\displaystyle B_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle K_{N}\kappa^{N+1-\delta}(L+\kappa^{-1}+t)^{\varepsilon},

(3.19)

\displaystyle C_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle K_{N}\kappa^{N+1-\delta}(L+\kappa^{-1}+t)^{\varepsilon},

(3.20)

\displaystyle D_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle M_{N,\kappa}\kappa^{N+1}(L+\kappa^{-1}+t)^{\varepsilon},

where we have set for abbreviation

(3.21)

M_{N,\kappa}\,:=\,\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\kappa^{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{N-n}K_{N-m}K_{n+m},

so that (
3.15
) becomes

(3.22)

\|u_{\kappa,L}^{t}-\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,\Big(\kappa+K_{N}\kappa^{N-\delta}+(K_{N}+M_{N,\kappa})\kappa^{N+1-\delta}t\Big)(L+\kappa^{-1}+t)^{\varepsilon}.

We start with the proof of (
3.17
). By definition (
3.16
) of
A_{\kappa,L,N}
, the corrector bounds (
3.6
) allow to estimate

(3.23)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{n}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{n}u^{\circ}_{\kappa,L}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

\displaystyle\||\!\cdot\!|\nabla^{n}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\|(|\!\cdot\!|+t)\nabla^{n}u^{\circ}_{\kappa,L}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

\|\langle\cdot\rangle^{\varepsilon}\nabla^{n}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,\|(\langle\cdot\rangle+t)\nabla^{n}u^{\circ}_{\kappa,L}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{\varepsilon}\|\nabla^{n}u^{\circ}_{\kappa,L}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{1-\varepsilon}.

Inserting this into (

A similar argument leads to (

We turn to the proof of (

C_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\,\lesssim\,K_{N}\kappa^{-\delta}\Big\|\langle\cdot\rangle^{\varepsilon}(\kappa^{2},\kappa\nabla,\nabla^{2})\nabla^{N}\int_{0}^{s}\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\Big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Noting that the Fourier formula (

Step

(i)

\|\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(B_{R})}\,\lesssim\,\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{\eta-d}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(B_{L})}.

(ii)

Provided that (

As

solves the homogenized wave equation (

\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}=G_{\kappa,N}\ast(\chi_{L}u^{\circ}),

where

is the smoothened Green’s function, solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}G_{\kappa,N}=\nabla\cdot\big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bar{A}^{n}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-1}}\nabla^{n-1}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-1}}\big)\nabla G_{\kappa,N},\\

\partial_{t}G_{\kappa,N}|_{t=0}=0,\end{array}\right.

which is understood as in (

G_{\kappa,N}^{t}(x)\,:=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\cos\big(t\sqrt{\mu_{N}(i\xi)}\big)\,\hat{\rho}_{\kappa}(\xi)\,d^{*}\xi.

This representation of

|\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}(x)|\,\leq\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|G_{\kappa,N}(y)||\chi_{L}(x-y)u^{\circ}(x-y)|\,dy\,\leq\,\int_{B_{L+R}}|G_{\kappa,N}(y)||u^{\circ}(x-y)|\,dy,

\|\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(B_{R})}\,\leq\,\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(B_{L})}\|G_{\kappa,N}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(B_{R+L})}.

Likewise,

\|\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|G_{\kappa,N}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

We start by using a

G_{\kappa,N}^{t}(x)\,=\,\kappa^{d}\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N}^{\kappa t}(\kappa x),\qquad\,\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N}^{t}(x):=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\cos\big(t\mu_{\kappa,N}(i\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)\,\hat{\rho}(\xi)\,d^{*}\xi,

in terms of

(3.32)

\mu_{\kappa,N}(i\xi)\,:=\,\kappa^{-2}\mu_{N}(i\kappa\xi)\,=\,\xi\cdot\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\kappa^{n-1}(i\xi)^{\otimes(n-1)}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\bar{A}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}^{n}\Big)\xi.

Next, we decompose

\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N}\,=\,\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N,+}+\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N,-}\big),

where
\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N,+}
and
\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N,-}

\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N,\pm}^{t}(x)\,:=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\Big({i\big(x\cdot\xi\pm t\mu_{\kappa,N}(i\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)}\Big)\hat{\rho}(\xi)\,d^{*}\xi.

Given

\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N,\pm}^{t}(x)\,=\,\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N,\pm;\zeta,0}^{t}(x)+\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N,\pm;\zeta,1}^{t}(x),

in terms of

(3.34)

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N,\pm;\zeta,\sigma}^{t}(x)\,:=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\Big({i\big(x\cdot\xi\pm t\mu_{\kappa,N}(i\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)}\Big)\hat{\rho}_{\zeta,\sigma}(\xi)\,d^{*}\xi,

\exp\Big({i\big(x\cdot\xi\pm t\mu_{\kappa,N}(i\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)}\Big)\\

with the short-hand notation

Inserting this into (
3.34
) and performing

\tilde{G}_{\kappa,N,\pm;\zeta,\sigma}^{t}(x)\,=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\Big({i\big(x\cdot\xi\pm t\mu_{\kappa,N}(i\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)}\Big)\big(D_{\xi;x,t,\pm}^{k}\hat{\rho}_{\zeta,\sigma}\big)(\xi)\,d^{*}\xi,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1\pm i(\nabla\cdot\nu_{\kappa,N})(\xi)}{1+t|\frac{x}{t}\pm\nu_{\kappa,N}(\xi)|^{2}}\mp 2t\tfrac{i(\frac{x}{t}\pm\nu_{\kappa,N}(\xi))^{\otimes 2}:\nabla\nu_{\kappa,N}(\xi)}{(1+t|\frac{x}{t}\pm\nu_{\kappa,N}(\xi)|^{2})^{2}}+\tfrac{i(\frac{x}{t}\pm\nu_{\kappa,N}(\xi))}{1+t|\frac{x}{t}\pm\nu_{\kappa,N}(\xi)|^{2}}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}.

\|u^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\lesssim\,\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\kappa+\textstyle\liminf_{N\uparrow\infty}\|\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}.

Now inserting the dispersive estimate (
3.26
), we get the following: given
\eta>0
, provided that
\kappa\ll_{\eta}1
is small enough,
we have for all
R\geq 1
and
t\gg R+L
,

\|u^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\lesssim_{\eta}\,\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\kappa+R^{\frac{d}{2}}\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{\eta-d}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(B_{L})}.

To get the desired estimate for non-necessarily compactly supported initial conditions, it suffices to apply this result to
\chi_{L}u^{\circ}
and to notice that

\displaystyle\|\nabla(\chi_{L}u^{\circ})\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{L})}+L^{-1}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{L})},

\displaystyle\|\chi_{L}u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}(B_{L})}.

Substep
6.2. Quasiperiodic setting: proof of (ii).

In the quasiperiodic setting, by Lemma
2.2
, under a Diophantine condition and Gevrey regularity,
we can choose
N
arbitrarily large,
\varepsilon=\delta=0
, and
K_{n}=(Cn^{\theta})^{n}
for some
\theta>0
.
The above result (
3.45
) then reads as follows:
for all
R,N\geq 1
,
\kappa>0
,
t\geq 0
,
L\geq 4(R+ct)
, and
n\geq 1
, provided that
\kappa N^{\theta}\ll 1
, we have

(3.48)

\|u^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\leq\,C\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\bar{u}_{\kappa,L,N}^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\\
+C_{n}(\kappa L)^{-n}+C\kappa+(C\kappa N^{\theta})^{N}(1+\kappa t).

We now appeal to the dispersive estimates of Step 5 to bound the second right-hand side term.
By (
3.27
), we get for all
R,N\geq 1
,
\kappa>0
,
t\geq 0
,
L\geq 4(R+ct)
, and
n\geq 1
, provided that
\kappa N^{\theta}\ll 1
,

\|u^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\leq\,C\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+R^{\frac{d}{2}}\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
+C_{n}(\kappa L)^{-n}+C\kappa+(C\kappa N^{\theta})^{N}(1+\kappa t).

Optimizing with respect to
N
with
\kappa N^{\theta}\ll 1
,
this becomes

\|u^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\leq\,C\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+R^{\frac{d}{2}}\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
+C_{n}(\kappa L)^{-n}+C\kappa\Big(1+t\exp(-\tfrac{1}{C}\kappa^{-\frac{1}{\theta}})\Big).

This yields the first part of item (ii) by taking
t\leq\exp(\tfrac{1}{C}\kappa^{-\frac{1}{\theta}})
and letting
L\uparrow\infty
.

Next,
if
u^{\circ}
is supported in
B_{L/2}
, then by (
3.46
) we may upgrade (
3.48
) as follows: for all
R,L,N\geq 1
,
\kappa>0
, and
t\geq 0
, provided that
\kappa N^{\theta}\ll 1
is small enough,

\|u^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\leq\,C\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\bar{u}^{t}_{\kappa,L,N}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}+C\kappa+(C\kappa N^{\theta})^{N}(1+\kappa t).

Now inserting the dispersive estimate (
3.26
), we get the following: given
\eta>0
, we obtain for all
R,L,N\geq 1
,
\kappa>0
, and
t\gg R+L
, provided that
\kappa N^{\theta}\ll_{\eta}1
,

\|u^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\lesssim_{\eta}\,\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+R^{\frac{d}{2}}\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{\eta-d}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(B_{L})}+\kappa+(C\kappa N^{\theta})^{N}(1+\kappa t).

Optimizing with respect to
N
as above, this entails

\|u^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\lesssim_{\eta}\,\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+R^{\frac{d}{2}}\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{\eta-d}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(B_{L})}\\
+\kappa\Big(1+t\exp(-\tfrac{1}{C}\kappa^{-\frac{1}{\theta}})\Big).

Applying this result to
\chi_{L}u^{\circ}
for non-necessarily compactly supported initial conditions
u^{\circ}
,
this yields the second part of item (ii).

Substep
6.3. Random setting.

In the random setting, by Lemma
2.3
and the subsequent discussion in Section
2
, under suitable mixing assumptions,
we can choose
N=\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
, any
\varepsilon>0
,
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
if

is odd,
\delta=0
if

is even,
and
K_{n}\leq\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}
for some random variable
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}
with finite stretched exponential moments.
Note that this yields

whether

is odd or even.

Set
\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}:=(\frac{1}{C}\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})\wedge 1
with a constant

large enough so that (
3.11
) and (

) with

hold for

.
The above result (

) then reads as follows: for all

,

,

,

, and

+C_{n}(\kappa L)^{-n}+\Big(\kappa+\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}^{-1}\kappa^{\frac{d}{2}}+\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}^{-2}\kappa^{\frac{d}{2}+1}t\Big)(L+\kappa^{-1}+t)^{\varepsilon},

and thus, using the dispersive estimate (

(3.49)

\|u^{t}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\lesssim\,\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+R^{\frac{d}{2}}\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\|u^{\circ}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
+C_{n}(\kappa L)^{-n}+\Big(\kappa+\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}^{-1}\kappa^{\frac{d}{2}}+\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}^{-2}\kappa^{\frac{d}{2}+1}t\Big)(L+\kappa^{-1}+t)^{\varepsilon},

Restricting to times

4.
Proof of the spreading of eigenstates in the lower spectrum

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
and of Remark
1.2
.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Assume that the heterogeneous acoustic operator
H_{a}=-\nabla\cdot A\nabla
admits an eigenvalue
\lambda>0
with normalized eigenstate
\psi_{\lambda}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, that is,

(4.1)

-\nabla\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}=\lambda\psi_{\lambda},\qquad\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1.

Given
0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}
, recall our definition (
1.2
) of the localization length of
\psi_{\lambda}
, that is, the minimal radius

(4.2)

\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\,:=\,\inf\big\{r\geq 0:\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{r})}\geq 1-\theta\big\}\,<\,\infty.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we take a dynamical approach and consider the large-scale behavior of the standing wave
(t,x)\mapsto\cos(t\sqrt{\lambda})\psi_{\lambda}(x)
associated with the eigenstate
\psi_{\lambda}
. As this eigenstate solves the heterogeneous wave equation, we can apply the large-scale dispersive estimates of Theorem
1.4
.

Step
1. Periodic setting.

Let
0<\eta<1
be fixed. Write
U(t)\psi_{\lambda}=U(t)(\chi_{2L}\psi_{\lambda})+U(t)((1-{\chi_{2L}})\psi_{\lambda})
.
On the one hand, by unitarity,

\|U(t)((1-{\chi_{2L}})\psi_{\lambda})\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{L})}.

On the other hand, by Theorem
1.4
(i), we have for all
0<\kappa\leq\kappa_{*}
,
R,L\geq 1
, and
t\gg R+L
,

\|U(t)({\chi_{2L}}\psi_{\lambda})\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\lesssim\,R^{\frac{d}{2}}\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{\eta-d}\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}({B_{2L}})}\\
+\big(\kappa+(\kappa L)^{-1}\big)\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{2L}})}+\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{2L}})}.

Hence, since
U(t)\psi_{\lambda}=\cos(t\sqrt{\lambda})\psi_{\lambda}
, this entails for

t\gg R+L
with
t\in 2\pi\sqrt{\lambda}{}^{-1}\mathbb{N}
(which ensures
\cos(t\sqrt{\lambda})=1
),

\displaystyle\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|U(t)({\chi_{2L}}\psi_{\lambda})\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}+\|U(t)((1-{\chi_{2L}})\psi_{\lambda})\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CR^{\frac{d}{2}}\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{\eta-d}\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}({B_{2L}})}

\displaystyle+C\big(\kappa+(\kappa L)^{-1}\big)\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{2L}})}+C\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{2L}})}+\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{L})}.

Letting
t\uparrow\infty
to get rid of the first right-hand side term, and
then
L\uparrow\infty
, using the property
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}=\lambda
of the eigenfunction, and choosing
R=\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})
,
this entails for all
0<\kappa\leq\kappa_{*}
,

0\,<\,{1-\theta}\,\leq\,C\kappa+C\kappa^{-1}\sqrt{\lambda}.

Taking
\kappa:=\lambda^{\frac{1}{4}}\leq\kappa_{*}
, this yields a contradiction for
\lambda
small enough. Hence
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})=\infty
and such an eigenfunction cannot exist.

Step
2. Quasiperiodic setting.

Let
0<\eta<1
be fixed.
Proceeding as in the periodic setting, we obtain using Theorem
1.4
(ii)
for all
0<\kappa\leq\kappa_{*}
,
R,L\geq 1
, and
R+L\ll t\leq\exp(\tfrac{1}{C}\kappa^{-\frac{1}{\theta}})
with
t\in 2\pi\sqrt{\lambda}{}^{-1}\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CR^{\frac{d}{2}}\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{\eta-d}\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}({B_{2L}})}

\displaystyle+C\big(\kappa+(\kappa L)^{-1}\big)\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{2L}})}+C\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{2L}})}+\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{B_{L}})}.

Choose
\kappa=\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}\leq\kappa_{*}
.
If
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\gtrsim\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{C}\lambda^{-(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{\theta}}\big)
, there is nothing to prove. We may then assume that
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\ll\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{C}\lambda^{-(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{\theta}}\big)
.
For the choice
R=\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})
,
L=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\vee\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})
, and
t=\exp(\tfrac{1}{C}\lambda^{-(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{\theta}})
, the above yields

{1-2\theta}\,\leq\,C\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})^{\frac{d}{2}}\lambda^{d(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)}(\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\vee\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda}))^{\frac{d}{2}}\lambda^{-(d-\eta)(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)}\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{C}(d-\eta)\lambda^{-(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{\theta}}\big)\\
+C(\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}+\lambda^{\varepsilon}).

Absorbing the last right-hand side term into the left-hand side for
\lambda\ll_{\theta}1
yields the claimed estimate.

Step
3. Random setting.

Let
0<r,\eta,\varepsilon\leq 1

be fixed.
Using now Theorem
1.4
(iii), we obtain for all
0<\kappa\leq\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}(0)
,
R,L\geq 1
, and
R+L\ll t\leq\kappa^{r-1-\frac{d}{2}}
,

\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(B_{R})}\,\leq\,CR^{\frac{d}{2}}\kappa^{d}(1+\kappa t)^{\eta-d}\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}({B_{2L}})}\\
+C\big(\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}(0)^{-2}\kappa^{r-\varepsilon}+(\kappa L)^{-1}\big)\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{2L}})}+C\kappa^{-1}\|\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}({B_{2L}})}+\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{B_{L}})}.

Choose
\kappa=\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}\leq\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}(0)
.
If
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\gtrsim\kappa^{r-1-\frac{d}{2}}=\lambda^{(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)(r-1-\frac{d}{2})}
, there is nothing to prove (upon redefining
\varepsilon
in the statement). We may then assume that
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\ll\lambda^{(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)(r-1-\frac{d}{2})}
.
For the choice
R=\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})
,
L=\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\vee\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})
, and
t=\lambda^{(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)(r-1-\frac{d}{2})}
, the above yields

{1-2\theta}\,\leq\,C\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})^{\frac{d}{2}}\lambda^{d(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)}(\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\vee\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda}))^{\frac{d}{2}}\lambda^{-(d-\eta)(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)}\lambda^{(d-\eta)(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)(\frac{d}{2}+1-r)}\\
+C\big(\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}(0)^{-2}\lambda^{(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon)(r-\varepsilon)}+\lambda^{\varepsilon}\big).

Absorbing the last right-hand side term into the left-hand side for
\lambda\ll_{\theta}\kappa_{*,\varepsilon}(0)^{\frac{4}{(1-2\varepsilon)(r-\varepsilon)}}
,
the conclusion follows by choosing
r
,
\varepsilon
, and
\eta
small enough.
∎

4.2.
Proof of Remark
1.2

The result is obtained as a post-processing of the result for the mass density using an exponentially-weighted energy estimate.
Given
\alpha\geq 1
, consider the exponential weight

\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(x)\,:=\,K_{\lambda,\alpha}^{-1}\exp(-\tfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{\alpha}|x|),\qquad K_{\lambda,\alpha}\,:=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(-\tfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{\alpha}|x|)\,dx.

For any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, testing the eigenvalue relation (
4.1
) with
\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(\cdot-z)\psi_{\lambda}
, we find

\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(\cdot-z)|\psi_{\lambda}|^{2}\,=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(\cdot-z)\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\\
-\tfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(z-x)\psi_{\lambda}(x)\tfrac{z-x}{|z-x|}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}(x)\,dx.

By Young’s inequality, with
\alpha\geq 1
, we deduce

(4.3)

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(\cdot-z)|\psi_{\lambda}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tfrac{1+\frac{1}{2\alpha}}{1-\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(\cdot-z)\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\tfrac{2}{\alpha})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(\cdot-z)\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}.

Next, we upgrade this estimate to get a comparison between the local norms
\lambda\int_{B_{R}}|\psi_{\lambda}|^{2}
and
\int_{B_{R}}\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}
(with non-smooth cut-off).
For that purpose, using local averaging with the weight
\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}
, we start by writing

(4.4)

\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R}}|\psi_{\lambda}|^{2}\,=\,\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(x-z)|\psi_{\lambda}(x)|^{2}\,dx\Big)\,dz,

and we separately consider the near-field and far-field contributions in this integral.
On the one hand, for the integral over
|z|\leq R/2
, using that the conditions
|z|\leq R/2
and
|x|\geq R
imply
|x-z|\geq R/2
, and using the normalization of
\psi_{\lambda}
, we find

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{B_{R/2}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(x-z)|\psi_{\lambda}(x)|^{2}\,dx\Big)\,dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\lambda R^{d}\|\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/2})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\lambda\exp(-\tfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{4\alpha}R).

On the other hand, for the integral over
|z|\geq R/2
, we appeal to (
4.3
) and obtain

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/2}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(x-z)|\psi_{\lambda}(x)|^{2}\,dx\Big)\,dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/2}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(\cdot-z)|\psi_{\lambda}|^{2}\Big)\,dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\tfrac{2}{\alpha})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/2}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(\cdot-z)\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\Big)\,dz.

We further split the last integral in bracket over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/4}
and
B_{R/4}
, and we note respectively that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/2}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/4}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(\cdot-z)\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\Big)\,dz\,\leq\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/4}}\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda},

and that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/2}}\Big(\int_{B_{R/4}}\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}(\cdot-z)\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\Big)\,dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/2}}\|\zeta_{\lambda,\alpha}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(B_{R/4}(z))}\,dz\Big)\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\lambda\exp(-\tfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{8\alpha}R).

Combining these different estimates back into (
4.4
), we obtain

(4.5)

\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R}}|\psi_{\lambda}(x)|^{2}\,dx\,\leq\,(1+\tfrac{2}{\alpha})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/4}}\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\cdot A\nabla\psi_{\lambda}+C\lambda\exp(-\tfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{8\alpha}R).

We can now use this bound to compare the minimal radii
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})
and
\ell^{\prime}_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})
defined in (
1.2
) and (
1.4
): for the choice
R:=\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})
, the left-hand side in (
4.5
) is by definition equal to
\lambda\theta^{2}
, so we can deduce

\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\|\sqrt{A}\nabla\psi_{\lambda}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R/4})}\,\geq\,\big(1+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(\theta^{2}-C\exp(-\tfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{8\alpha}R)\big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

which means

\ell^{\prime}_{\hat{\theta}}(\psi_{\lambda})\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{4}\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda}),\qquad\text{provided $\hat{\theta}\leq\big(1+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(\theta^{2}-C\exp(-\tfrac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{8\alpha}\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda}))\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}$}.

In all the cases considered, as stated in Theorem
1.1
, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}
, we have shown that we have at least
\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda})\geq\lambda^{\varepsilon-3/4}
for
\lambda
small enough, so the above becomes

\ell^{\prime}_{\hat{\theta}}(\psi_{\lambda})\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{4}\ell_{\theta}(\psi_{\lambda}),\qquad\text{provided $\hat{\theta}\leq\big(1+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(\theta^{2}-C\exp(-\tfrac{\lambda^{-1/8}}{8\alpha})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}$}.

Choosing
\alpha
arbitrarily large to reach any
0<\hat{\theta}<\frac{1}{2}
up to taking
\lambda
small enough, the statement of Remark
1.2
follows.
∎
