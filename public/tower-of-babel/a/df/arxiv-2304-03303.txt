Enhanced Superconducting Diode Effect due to coexisting Phases
Sayan Banerjee, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
The superconducting diode effect refers to an asymmetry in the critical supercurrent J c ​ ( n ^ ) J_{c}(\hat{n}) along opposite directions, J c ​ ( n ^ ) ≠ J c ​ ( − n ^ ) J_{c}(\hat{n})\neq J_{c}(-\hat{n}) . While the basic symmetry requirements for this effect are known, it is, for junction-free systems, difficult to capture within current theoretical models the large current asymmetries J c ​ ( n ^ ) / J c ​ ( − n ^ ) J_{c}(\hat{n})/J_{c}(-\hat{n}) recently observed in experiment. We here propose and develop a theory for an enhancement mechanism of the diode effect arising from spontaneous symmetry breaking. We show—both within a phenomenological and a microscopic theory—that there is a coupling of the supercurrent and the underlying symmetry-breaking order parameter. This coupling can enhance the current asymmetry significantly. Our work might not only provide a possible explanation for recent experiments on trilayer graphene but also pave the way for future realizations of the superconducting diode effect with large current asymmetries.

Model for SDE.—
As motivated above, we study an electronic Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{c}=&\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}\xi_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{k}},\eta}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}+\Phi_{V}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}\eta c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}\\
&+\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}}\left[\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2,+}c^{\dagger}_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2,-}+\text{H.c.}\right],\end{split}

(1)

where
c_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}^{\dagger}
creates an electron in valley
\eta=\pm
and at momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
. The three terms in
\mathcal{H}_{c}
capture the non-interacting bandstructure of the nearly flat bands at the chemical potential, which obey
\xi_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{k}},\eta}=\xi_{-\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{k}},-\eta}\equiv\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\eta}
due to TRS, the coupling of the TRS-breaking normal state order (in our case, valley imbalance
\Phi_{V}
) and of the superconducting order parameter to the electrons, respectively. For notational simplicity, we suppress the spin index in Eq. (
1
) and in the following, but emphasize that our results apply both for spin polarized bands
Morissette et al. 2022
;
Zondiner et al. 2020
as well as in the spinful case, with singlet or triplet pairing.

To stabilize superconductivity, we take an attractive (
g_{c}>0
) interaction
\mathcal{H}_{g_{c}}=-\frac{g_{c}}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q},\eta,\eta^{\prime}}C^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{q};\eta,\eta^{\prime}}C^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{q};\eta,\eta^{\prime}}
with
C_{\boldsymbol{q};\eta,\eta^{\prime}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2,\eta}c_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}/2,\eta^{\prime}}
and perform a decoupling in the intervalley pairing channel,
\braket{C_{\boldsymbol{q};\eta,\eta^{\prime}}}=\eta\delta_{\eta,\eta^{\prime}}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}/g_{c}
; we focus on intervalley pairing not only because it is favored as a consequence of TRS for a finite range of
\Phi_{V}

Scammell et al. 2022
but also because intravalley pairing is more susceptible to impurities and, hence, likely less relevant experimentally. The resulting mean-field Hamiltonian for superconductivity for given valley polarization
\Phi_{V}
then reads as
\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}=\mathcal{H}_{c}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}/g_{c}
. While we will compute the supercurrent systematically below, we start for illustration purposes with a semi-phenomenological approach based on a Ginzburg-Landau expansion. Integrating out the electrons in
\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}
and expanding up to second order in
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
, the change
\delta\mathcal{F}_{\text{S}}:=\mathcal{F}[\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}},\Phi_{V}]-\mathcal{F}[0,\Phi_{V}]
of the free-energy with superconductivity reads as

\delta\mathcal{F}_{\text{S}}\sim\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\,|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}+b^{\text{S}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}_{i}}\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}}\Delta^{*}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{2}}\Delta^{\phantom{*}}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{3}}\Delta^{\phantom{*}}_{\boldsymbol{q}_{4}}\delta_{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}+\boldsymbol{q}_{2},\boldsymbol{q}_{3}+\boldsymbol{q}_{4}},

where we neglected the momentum dependence of the quartic term. It holds
a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=1/g_{c}-\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{q}}
with
Scammell et al. 2022

\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{\tanh{\frac{E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}}{2T}}+\tanh{\frac{E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}}{2T}}}{E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}+E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}},

(2)

where
N
is the number of unit cells and
E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\eta}=\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}+\eta\boldsymbol{q}/2}+\eta\,\Phi_{V}
encodes the normal-state dispersion, associated with the first line of Eq. (
1
).

The equilibrium superconducting state at given
\Phi_{V}
is found by minimizing
\delta\mathcal{F}_{\text{S}}
. Restricting the analysis to single-
\boldsymbol{q}
states,
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}\propto\delta_{\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{q}_{0}}
, the value of
\boldsymbol{q}_{0}
is determined by the minimum of
a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
(the maximum of
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{q}}
). As expected, the current
Daido et al. 2022
;
Yuan and Fu 2022
;
Scammell et al. 2022

\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{q})=2e\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{q}},\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{q}}a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}},

(3)

vanishes in equilibrium,
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{q}_{0})=0
; a supercurrent-carrying state, therefore, corresponds to pairing with
\boldsymbol{q}\neq\boldsymbol{q}_{0}
. We define the critical current
J_{c}(\hat{n})
along
\hat{n}
as the maximal magnitude of
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{q})
oriented along
\hat{n}
.

For
\Phi_{V}=0
, it holds
E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\eta}=E_{\boldsymbol{k},-\boldsymbol{q},-\eta}
and thus
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\Gamma_{-\boldsymbol{q}}
in Eq. (
2
). This, in turn, immediately implies
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{q})=-\boldsymbol{J}(-\boldsymbol{q})
and
J_{c}(\hat{n})=J_{c}(-\hat{n})
, i.e., there is no SDE. This was expected as a consequence of TRS or two-fold rotational symmetry,
C_{2z}
, for that matter—both have to be broken to get
J_{c}(\hat{n})\neq J_{c}(-\hat{n})
. This is achieved by
\Phi_{V}\neq 0

Scammell et al. 2022
, leading to a finite SDE efficiency,

\eta=\max_{\hat{n}}\frac{|J_{c}(\hat{n})-J_{c}(-\hat{n})|}{J_{c}(\hat{n})+J_{c}(-\hat{n})}\in[0,1].

(4)

Although generically non-zero by symmetry, it does not guarantee that
\eta
can reach high values corresponding to large current asymmetries,
J_{c}(\hat{n})\gg J_{c}(-\hat{n})
—as mentioned above, a property desirable for applications of the SDE and relevant fundamentally to understand recent experiments
Lin et al. 2022
.

Phenomenological coupling.—
The main result of this work is that the diode-effect efficiency
\eta
can be significantly enhanced by taking into account that the strength of the valley polarization is affected by the supercurrent or, put differently,
\Phi_{V}
entering
E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\eta}
in Eq. (
2
) also depends on the center of mass momentum
\boldsymbol{q}
of the Cooper pairs,
\Phi_{V}\rightarrow\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})
. Postponing a systematic microscopic computation that treats
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
and
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})
on equal footing, we start with a simpler phenomenological analysis. In this simplified description, we determine
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})
by minimizing the free energy

\mathcal{F}_{\text{V}}\sim a_{\Phi}\Phi_{V}^{2}+b_{\Phi}\Phi_{V}^{4}+\Phi_{V}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}\left[F(\boldsymbol{q})+\alpha^{\prime\prime}\Phi_{V}\right]

(5)

with respect to
\Phi_{V}
, where terms involving fifth and higher powers of the order parameters
\Phi_{V}
,
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
are neglected.
The first two terms in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{V}}
determine the value of
\Phi_{V}
in the absence of superconductivity. Meanwhile, the remaining terms in Eq. (
5
) capture the key coupling between the momentum of the Cooper pairs and valley polarization. The last term,
\propto\alpha^{\prime\prime}
, simply describes the fact that increasing (decreasing) the superconducting order parameter magnitude suppresses (enhances) valley polarization. The other term with
F(\boldsymbol{q})=-F(-\boldsymbol{q})
describes the fact that superconductivity at finite
\boldsymbol{q}
breaks TRS and
C_{2z}
and can, hence, couple to the first (and in general an odd) power of
\Phi_{V}
. Motivated by twisted graphene systems, which exhibit
C_{3z}
rotational symmetry, we write

F(\boldsymbol{q})=\alpha\,\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\epsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol{X}(\boldsymbol{q})+\alpha^{\prime}\,\chi(\boldsymbol{q}),

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{X}(\boldsymbol{q})
and
\chi(\boldsymbol{q})
are real-valued, periodic on the Brillouin zone, odd in
\boldsymbol{q}
, and transform as a vector and scalar under
C_{3z}
, respectively; in the limit of small
\boldsymbol{q}
, it holds
\boldsymbol{X}\sim(q_{x},q_{y})^{T}
and
\chi(\boldsymbol{q})\sim q_{x}(q_{x}^{2}-3q_{y}^{2})
, using a convention where one of the primitive translation vectors,
\boldsymbol{a}_{j}
, of the underlying triangular Bravais lattice points along the
x
direction. For the following calculations, we extend these functions to the Brillouin zone using the leading lattice harmonics (see Appendix
A
). Importantly, the first term in Eq. (
6
), requires finite strain or nematic order which break
C_{3z}
symmetry explicitly. These phenomena, which seem to be ubiquitous in graphene moiré systems
Jiang et al. 2019
;
Kerelsky et al. 2019
;
Cao et al. 2021
;
Rubio-Verdú et al. 2022
;
Samajdar et al. 2021
;
Sobral et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2022b
, define a preferred in-plane direction, captured by the unit vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\epsilon}
. For instance, in the case of strain,
\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\epsilon}\propto(\epsilon_{xx}-\epsilon_{yy},\epsilon_{xy}+\epsilon_{yz})^{T}
, where
\epsilon_{ij}
are the strain-tensor elements. We note that the coupling would also be present when
\Phi_{V}
is replaced by another order parameter that itself already breaks both TRS and
C_{3z}
simultaneously
Zhang et al. 2022b
;
Lin et al. 2023
.
The second term in Eq. (
6
), albeit subleading for small
\boldsymbol{q}
, is finite regardless of whether
C_{3z}
is broken.

To study the SDE, we self-consistently minimize
\mathcal{F}_{V}[\Phi_{V},\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}]
and
\delta\mathcal{F}_{\text{S}}[\Phi_{V},\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}]
with respect to
\Phi_{V}
and
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
, respectively, for given
\boldsymbol{q}
, and compute the current according to Eq. (
3
). For concreteness, we use a nearest-neighbor dispersion with finite flux
\phi
on the triangular lattice,
\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}t_{j}\cos(\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}-\phi/3)
where
\boldsymbol{a}_{j}
are three
C_{3z}
-related primitive vectors. We choose
t_{1}=t
,
t_{2}=t_{3}=t(1+\beta)
to parameterize the impact of strain (
\propto\beta
) on the dispersion and, for notational simplicity, measure all energies in units of

in the following.

6
), i.e., set
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime\prime}=0
in

. As can be seen in Fig.

Self-consistent theory.—
Having established a phenomenological understanding of the enhancement of the SDE from the back action mechanism, we next turn our attention to a systematic self-consistent formalism. We switch to an action formalism and redefine
c_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}
in Eq. (
1
) as Grassmann variables depending on imaginary time
\tau
. We start from the effective action

\mathcal{S}=\int\mathrm{d}\tau\Biggl[\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}\partial_{\tau}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}+\mathcal{H}_{c}+\mathcal{S}_{\Delta}+\mathcal{S}_{\Phi}\Biggr],

(7)

that captures the desired phenomenology in a minimal setting. In Eq. (
7
),
\mathcal{S}_{\Delta}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}(\frac{1}{g_{c}}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}+u_{\Delta}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{4})
and
\mathcal{S}_{\Phi}=\frac{1}{g_{v}}\Phi_{V}^{2}+v_{\Phi_{V}}\Phi_{V}^{4}
are the bare actions of the Hubbard-Stratonovich fields associated with superconductivity and valley polarization, respectively. We include terms up to quartic order (which, e.g., arise after having integrated out electronic degrees of freedom at higher energies) to stabilize coexistence of these two orders, as is seen in experiment
Lin et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023b
;
Scammell et al. 2022
.

The saddle-point equations for
\Phi_{V}
and
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
read as

\frac{\Phi_{V}}{g_{v}}+2v_{\Phi}\Phi_{V}^{3}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},p=\pm}p\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},p}}{2T}},

(8a)

\frac{1}{g_{c}}+2u_{\Delta}|\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}|^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{(\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}}{2T}}+\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}}{2T}})}{2(\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}+\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-})},

(8b)

or
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}=0
, where
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},p}=\sqrt{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}^{2}+|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}}+p\,\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}
with
\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\pm}=(E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}\pm E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-})/2
. As required, Eq. (
8b
) becomes equivalent to
a^{\text{S}}_{\boldsymbol{q}}=0
when expanded to leading order in
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
.

We solve Eq. (
8b
) self-consistently for both
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
and
\Phi_{V}
for given
\boldsymbol{q}
. This captures the mutual influence of these two orders, as
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
and
\Phi_{V}
enter each other’s saddle-point equation, Eqs. (
8a
) and (
8b
), respectively, via the dispersion
\mathcal{E}
. Broken
C_{3z}
symmetry is entirely encoded in the parameter
\beta
entering the bare normal-state dispersion
\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. Generically, the two coupled saddle-point equations are expected to capture any symmetry-allowed coupling term between
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
and
\Phi_{V}
in an effective free-energy expansion, including those in Eq. (
5
) but also terms involving higher powers of the order parameters. It turns out, though, that for the simple coupling in
\mathcal{H}_{c}
, where valley polarization only enters as an imbalance of the chemical potential in the two valleys, we find
\alpha=0
in Eq. (
6
) for any
\beta
. This is readily shown by expanding Eq. (
8a
) in terms of
\delta\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})=\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})-\Phi_{V}(0)
at fixed
\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})
, see Appendix
C
. This is why the back action is dominated by the term
\alpha^{\prime\prime}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}\Phi_{V}^{2}
in Eq. (
6
) for small
\boldsymbol{q}
as can be seen in Fig.
3
(a), where
\delta\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})\propto\delta\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\boldsymbol{q}^{2}
for small
\boldsymbol{q}
. We have checked that generalizing the coupling to
\Phi_{V}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}\eta f_{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\eta}c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta}
, with
C_{3z}
-invariant form factors
f_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, leads to finite
\alpha
; this is clearly visible in Fig.
3
(b), where
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})
is found to have a finite slope at the maximum (red hexagon) of
\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})
.

SDE in self-consistent theory.—
To demonstrate that the enhancement mechanism for the SDE based on the back action is a robust phenomenon, we here discuss it for the original from of the order parameter
\Phi_{V}
without form factors,
f_{\boldsymbol{k}}=1
, where the leading coupling (
\propto\alpha
) in the phenomenological theory in Eq. (
5
) is absent. In Fig.
4
(a), we plot
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})
and
\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})
, obtained by solving Eq. (
8b
). We can clearly see the supercurrent-induced change of valley polarization, which is manifestly not
C_{3z}
invariant, due to
\beta\neq 0
, and exhibits the strongest asymmetry under
\boldsymbol{q}\rightarrow-\boldsymbol{q}
for
\boldsymbol{q}
along
q_{x}
. Due to the additional coupling terms beyond those in Eq. (
5
),
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})
looks different at large
\boldsymbol{q}
from that shown in Fig.
2
(a). However, the for our purpose more important
\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})
looks very similar to
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{q}}
in Fig.
2
(b). Evaluating the thermal expectation value of the current operator within the full theory (see Appendix
D
) as a function of
\boldsymbol{q}
, we compute the directional dependence of the critical current, shown in Fig.
4
(b) and (c) for
\beta=0
and
\beta\neq 0
, respectively. Exactly as in the phenonemological theory, we find that broken
C_{3z}
symmetry can enhance the SDE and that the current asymmetry first increases with small
\Phi_{V}
(as significant TRS breaking is needed to generate a large current asymmetry) but then eventually decreases with large
\Phi_{V}
(as it destabilizes superconductivity and also weakens the back action). This is also visible in Fig.
4
(d), where we plot the SDE efficiency
\eta
of the full self-consistent theory (black line) as a function of valley polarization at
\boldsymbol{q}=0
and compare it with the situation without back action (red dashed line); for the latter, we just fix
\Phi_{V}
at the indicated value
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)
, independent of
\boldsymbol{q}
and solve for superconductivity via Eq. (
2
). We observe that the back action not only enhances, as before, the maximum value of
\eta
that can be achieved, but also increases the efficiency significantly at smaller values of valley polarization. The reason for this amplification is the aforementioned dominance of the
\alpha^{\prime\prime}
-like couplings in the self-consistent theory, which enhance
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})
when suppressing
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
with finite
\boldsymbol{q}
, see also Fig.
3
(a). So even if we have very small
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)
, valley polarization can reach sizable values at non-zero
\boldsymbol{q}
due to the coupling to the supercurrent, which in turn enhances the diode effect. This is why
\eta
reaches finite values in the limit
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)\rightarrow 0^{+}
with back action, while it has to vanish linearly with
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)
without it in Fig.
4
(d). It shows that the proposed back action mechanism also enhances the
typical

\eta
which can reach large values without the need of fine-tuning
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)
to its optimal strength.

Conclusion.—
We have shown, both using a simple free-energy expansion and a self-consistent theory, that the supercurrent can couple to and, hence, affect the valley polarization,
\Phi_{V}
, which in turn can enhance the SDE efficiency significantly, see Fig.
1
(d) and Fig.
4
(d). Motivated by Ref.
Lin et al. 2022
, this was formulated for a theory applicable to graphene-based systems; however, we expect that the basic mechanism based on coupling of the supercurrent to a TRS-breaking order parameter is more generally valid for zero-field superconducting diodes.

Appendix B
Derivation of the saddle-point equations

To derive saddle point equations, we generalize Eq. (
7
) to include non-trivial form factors,
f_{\boldsymbol{k}}\neq\text{const.}
, for the coupling of valley polarization to the electrons. The associated action in Matsubara-frequency notation becomes

\mathcal{S}[\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}},\Phi_{V}]=\frac{1}{g_{v}}\Phi_{V}^{2}+\frac{1}{g_{c}}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}+v_{\Phi_{V}}\Phi_{V}^{4}+u_{\Delta}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{4}+\sum_{k}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{G}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{k,q}},

(11)

where the sum is done over
k=(\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n})
, comprising momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
and Matsubara frequencies
\omega_{n}
.
\mathcal{G}_{k,\boldsymbol{q}}
and
\Psi_{k,\boldsymbol{q}}
are given by

\mathcal{G}^{-1}_{k,\boldsymbol{q}}=\begin{bmatrix}-i\omega_{n}+\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}+\frac{\boldsymbol{q}}{2},+}+f_{\boldsymbol{k}+\frac{\boldsymbol{q}}{2},+}\Phi_{V}&\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}\\
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}&-i\omega_{n}-\xi_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\frac{\boldsymbol{q}}{2},-}+f_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\frac{\boldsymbol{q}}{2},-}\Phi_{V}\end{bmatrix},\quad\Psi_{k,\boldsymbol{q}}=\binom{c_{\mathbf{k+\frac{q}{2},+}}}{c_{\mathbf{-k+\frac{q}{2},-}}^{\dagger}}.

(12)

Integrating out the fermions, we obtain the effective action as,

\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}=\frac{\beta}{g_{v}}\Phi_{V}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{g_{c}}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}+\beta v_{\Phi_{V}}\Phi_{V}^{4}+\beta u_{\Delta}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{4}-\sum_{k}\text{tr}\ln{\mathcal{G}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}}.

(13)

Now, we calculate the saddle point equation for
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
we set
\delta S_{\text{eff}}/\delta\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}\overset{!}{=}0

\displaystyle\beta\frac{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}}{g_{c}}+2\beta u_{\Delta}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}-\sum_{k}\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}\frac{\delta}{\delta\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}}\begin{pmatrix}-i\omega_{n}+E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}&\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}\\
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}&-i\omega_{n}-E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}\end{pmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle=0

(14)

\displaystyle\beta\frac{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}}{g_{c}}+2\beta u_{\Delta}|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}-\sum_{k}\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle=0

(15)

Now,

\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}=\frac{-1}{(-i\omega_{n}+E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+})(-i\omega_{n}-E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-})-\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}i\omega_{n}+E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}&\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}\\
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}&i\omega_{n}-E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}\end{bmatrix}

(16)

where we have redefined our energies as,

E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}=\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}+\frac{\boldsymbol{q}}{2},+}+f_{\boldsymbol{k}+\frac{\boldsymbol{q}}{2},+}\Phi_{V},\quad E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}=\xi_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\frac{\boldsymbol{q}}{2},-}-f_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\frac{\boldsymbol{q}}{2},-}\Phi_{V},

(17)

which is the generalization of the energies defined below Eq. (
2
) of the main text to include form factors.
Therefore, the trace term reduces to

\sum_{k}\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right]=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},n}\frac{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}}{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}+\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}^{2}+(\omega_{n}+i\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-})^{2}},\quad\text{with}\quad\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},\pm}=(E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}\pm E_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-})/2.

(18)

Performing the sum over Matsubara frequencies, we get

\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right]=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}(\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}}{2T}}+\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}}{2T}})}{2T(\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}+\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-})}

(19)

where
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k,q},p}=\sqrt{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}^{2}+|\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}}+p\,\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}
.
Consequentially, this leads us to our saddle point equation for
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}
,

\frac{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}}{g_{c}}+2u_{\Delta}|\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}|^{2}\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}(\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}}{2T}}+\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}}{2T}})}{2(\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}+\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-})}

(20)

as stated in Eq. (
8b
) of the main text.
Moving onto the saddle point equation for valley polarization, we start similarly by setting
\delta S_{\text{eff}}/\delta\Phi_{V}\overset{!}{=}0

\displaystyle\frac{2\beta\Phi_{V}}{g_{v}}+4v_{\Phi}\beta\Phi_{V}^{3}-\sum_{k}\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}\frac{\delta}{\delta\Phi_{V}}\begin{pmatrix}-i\omega_{n}+E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}&\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}\\
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}&-i\omega_{n}-E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}\end{pmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle=0

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{2\beta\Phi_{V}}{g_{v}}+4v_{\Phi}\Phi_{V}^{3}-\sum_{k}\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}&0\\
0&\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}\end{pmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle=0

(22)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}=f_{\boldsymbol{k}+\frac{\boldsymbol{q}}{2},+}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}=f_{-\boldsymbol{k}+\frac{\boldsymbol{q}}{2},-}
.
Evaluating the trace, we get

\sum_{k}\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}&0\\
0&\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}\end{pmatrix}\right]=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},n}\frac{(i\omega_{n}+E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-})\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}+(i\omega_{n}-E_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+})\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}}{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}+\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}^{2}+(\omega_{n}+i\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-})^{2}}

(23)

If we now evaluate the Matsubara sums, we arrive at

\sum_{k}\text{tr}\left[\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}&0\\
0&\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}\end{pmatrix}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left(F_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}}{2T}}-F_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}}{2T}}\right)

(24)

where the coefficients are given by

F_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}=\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}+}l_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}+\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}-}m_{\boldsymbol{k,q}},\quad F_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}=\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}-}l_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}+\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{k,q}+}m_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}

(25)

l_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}}{\sqrt{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}^{2}+\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}}}\right),\quad m_{\boldsymbol{k,q}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}}{\sqrt{\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}^{2}+\Delta_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}}}\right)

(26)

Therefore, we obtain our saddle point equation for valley polarization as,

\frac{\Phi_{V}}{g_{v}}+2v_{\Phi}\Phi_{V}^{3}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left(F_{\boldsymbol{k,q},+}\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},+}}{2T}}-F_{\boldsymbol{k,q},-}\tanh{\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q},-}}{2T}}\right)

(27)

which reduces to Eq. (
8a
) when the form factors are trivial [
f_{\boldsymbol{k},\pm}=1
in Eq. (
12
)].

Appendix C
Coupling of supercurrent and valley polarization within full self-consistent theory

In this part of the appendix, we elucidate the impact of finite
\boldsymbol{q}
on superconductivity and valley polarization within the coupled non-linear self-consistency equations in Eq. (
8b
) of the main text. We choose a gauge where
\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})\in\mathbb{R}
, for notational simplicity, and begin with Eq. (
8a
) which we expand to linear order in
\boldsymbol{q}
,
\delta\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q}):=\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})-\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)
, and
\delta\Delta(\boldsymbol{q}):=\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})-\Delta(\boldsymbol{q}=0)
. This leads to

C_{\Phi\Phi}\delta\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})+C_{\Phi\Delta}\delta\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})=\boldsymbol{n}_{\Phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}+\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{q}^{2},\delta\Phi_{V}^{2},\delta\Delta^{2}),

(28)

where

\displaystyle C_{\Phi\Phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g_{v}}+6v_{\Phi}\Phi_{V}^{2}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)-\frac{1}{8T}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},p}\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}=0,p}}{2T})},

(29)

\displaystyle C_{\Phi\Delta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta(\boldsymbol{q}=0)}{8T}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},p}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)}}\frac{p}{\cosh^{2}(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}=0,p}}{2T})},

(30)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}_{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16T}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},p}\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{\cosh^{2}(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}=0,p}}{2T})}.

(31)

In our case,
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}=0,p}=\sqrt{\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)}+p\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)
only depends on
\boldsymbol{k}
through
\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, leading to
\boldsymbol{n}_{\Phi}=0
. Note this would generically be different if the order parameter of superconductivity or valley polarization [
f_{\boldsymbol{k}}\neq\text{const.}
in Eq. (
12
)] depended on momentum. We thus conclude that
\delta\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})\propto\delta\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})+\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{q}^{2})
. This is consistent with Fig.
3
(a) and
\alpha=0
in Eq. (
6
) of the main text.

We can learn more by expanding Eq. (
8b
) in the same way,

C_{\Delta\Delta}\delta\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})+C_{\Delta\Phi}\delta\Phi(\boldsymbol{q})=\boldsymbol{n}_{\Delta}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}+\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{q}^{2},\delta\Phi_{V}^{2},\delta\Delta^{2}),

(32)

where the coefficients are given by

\displaystyle C_{\Delta\Delta}

\displaystyle=4u_{\Delta}\Delta(\boldsymbol{q}=0)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},p}\tanh\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}=0,p}}{2T}\right)\frac{\Delta(\boldsymbol{q}=0)}{(\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{q}=0))^{\frac{3}{2}}}-\frac{1}{4T}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},p}\frac{\Delta(\boldsymbol{q}=0)}{\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)}\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}=0,p}}{2T})},

(33)

\displaystyle C_{\Delta\Phi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2T}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},p}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)}}\frac{p}{\cosh^{2}(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}=0,p}}{2T})},

(34)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8T}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},p}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)}}\frac{p\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{\cosh^{2}(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}=0,p}}{2T})}.

(35)

The same comments as those above for
\boldsymbol{n}_{\Phi}
also apply for
\boldsymbol{n}_{\Delta}
, which thus vanishes,
\boldsymbol{n}_{\Delta}=0
.
Without fine-tuning, we expect
C_{\Phi\Phi}C_{\Delta\Delta}\neq C_{\Phi\Delta}C_{\Delta\Phi}
and Eqs. (
28
) and (
32
) therefore imply
\delta\Delta(\boldsymbol{q}),\delta\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})=\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{q}^{2})
exactly as we find numerically, see Fig.
3
(a). This is the reason why the maximum of
\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})
[minimum of
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})
] can be pinned to
\boldsymbol{q}=0
even when
C_{3z}
is broken. As
\boldsymbol{n}_{\Phi},\boldsymbol{n}_{\Delta}
become non-zero when form factors are introduced, the maximum of
\Delta(\boldsymbol{q})
[minimum of
\Phi_{V}(\boldsymbol{q})
] move to finite values when
C_{3z}
is broken as is observed in Fig.
3
(b).

Appendix D
Microscopic calculation of current

We can write a BdG Hamiltonian for our system as,

H_{\text{BdG}}=\begin{bmatrix}E_{\mathbf{k+\frac{q}{2}},+}&\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}\\
\Delta_{\mathbf{q}}^{*}&-E_{\mathbf{-k+q/2},-}\end{bmatrix},\quad\Psi=\binom{c_{\mathbf{k+\frac{q}{2},+}}}{c_{\mathbf{-k+\frac{q}{2},-}}^{\dagger}},\quad\Psi^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{\mathbf{k+\frac{q}{2},+}}^{\dagger}&c_{\mathbf{-k+\frac{q}{2},-}}\end{pmatrix}

(36)

and

\binom{c_{\mathbf{k+\frac{q}{2},+}}}{c_{\mathbf{-k+\frac{q}{2},-}}^{\dagger}}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{\mathbf{k,q}}&v_{\mathbf{k,q}}\\
-v_{\mathbf{k,q}}^{*}&u_{\mathbf{k,q}}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\binom{\gamma_{\mathbf{k,q},+}}{\gamma^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{-k,q},-}}.

(37)

The BdG Hamiltonian in diagonal basis can be now written as,

H_{\text{BdG}}=UH_{\text{diag}}U^{\dagger},\quad U=\begin{pmatrix}u_{\mathbf{k,q}}&v_{\mathbf{k,q}}\\
-v_{\mathbf{k,q}}^{*}&u_{\mathbf{k,q}}^{*}\end{pmatrix},\quad U^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{\mathbf{k,q}}^{*}&-v_{\mathbf{k,q}}\\
v_{\mathbf{k,q}}^{*}&u_{\mathbf{k,q}}\end{pmatrix}

(38)

The expression for the current (see, e.g.,
Daido et al. 2022
) reads as

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\text{tr}\left[\partial_{\mathbf{q}}H_{\text{N}}n_{F}(H_{\text{BdG}})\right]

(39)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\text{tr}\left[\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\mathbf{q}}E_{\mathbf{k+\frac{q}{2}},+}&0\\
0&-\partial_{\mathbf{q}}E_{\mathbf{-k+q/2},-}\end{pmatrix}n_{F}(H_{\text{BdG}})\right],

(40)

where

n_{F}(\epsilon)=(1+e^{\beta\epsilon})^{-1}
is the Fermi distribution function and

H_{\text{N}}=\begin{pmatrix}E_{\mathbf{k+\frac{q}{2}},+}&0\\
0&-E_{\mathbf{-k+q/2},-}\end{pmatrix}.

(41)

Therefore we have,

\displaystyle n(H_{\text{BdG}})

\displaystyle=Un_{F}(H_{\text{diag}})U^{\dagger}

(42)

\displaystyle=U\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta E_{1,\mathbf{k,q}}}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta E_{2,\mathbf{k,q}}}}\end{pmatrix}U^{\dagger}

(43)

Plugging this into the expression for current we get:

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\text{tr}\left[U^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\mathbf{q}}E_{\mathbf{k+\frac{q}{2}},+}&0\\
0&-\partial_{\mathbf{q}}E_{\mathbf{-k+q/2},-}\end{pmatrix}Un_{F}(H_{\text{diag}})\right]

(44)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\text{tr}\left[\begin{pmatrix}u_{\mathbf{k,q}}^{*}&-v_{\mathbf{k,q}}\\
v_{\mathbf{k,q}}^{*}&u_{\mathbf{k,q}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_{\mathbf{k,q}}\partial_{\mathbf{q}}E_{\mathbf{k+\frac{q}{2}},+}&v_{\mathbf{k,q}}\partial_{\mathbf{q}}E_{\mathbf{k+\frac{q}{2}},+}\\
v_{\mathbf{k,q}}^{*}\partial_{\mathbf{q}}E_{\mathbf{-k+\frac{q}{2}},-}&-u^{*}_{\mathbf{k,q}}\partial_{\mathbf{q}}E_{\mathbf{-k+q/2},-}\end{pmatrix}n_{F}(H_{\text{diag}})\right]
