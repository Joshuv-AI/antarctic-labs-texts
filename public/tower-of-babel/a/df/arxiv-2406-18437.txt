Towards odd-sunflowers: temperate families and lightnings
Jan Petr, Pavel Turek

Abstract
Motivated by odd-sunflowers, introduced recently by Frankl, Pach, and Pálvölgyi, we initiate the study of temperate families: a family ℱ ⊆ 𝒫 ⁡ ( [ n ] ) \mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}([n]) is said to be temperate if each A ∈ ℱ A\in\mathcal{F} contains at most | A | |A| elements of ℱ \mathcal{F} as a proper subset. We show that the maximum size of a temperate family is attained by the middle two layers of the hypercube { 0 , 1 } n \{0,1\}^{n} . As a more general result, we obtain that the middle t + 1 t+1 layers of the hypercube maximise the size of a family ℱ \mathcal{F} such that each A ∈ ℱ A\in\mathcal{F} contains at most ∑ j = 1 t ( | A | j ) \sum_{j=1}^{t}\binom{|A|}{j} elements of ℱ \mathcal{F} as a proper subset. Moreover, we classify all such families consisting of the maximum number of sets. In the case of intersecting temperate families, we find the maximum size and classify all intersecting temperate families consisting of the maximum number of sets for odd n n . We also conjecture the maximum size for even n n .



1
Introduction

Let
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}([n])
and a set
A\in\mathcal{P}([n])
, let
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}(A)=|\mathcal{P}(A)\cap\mathcal{F}|
.
Given a non-negative integer
t
, we say
\mathcal{F}
is
t
-temperate
if
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}(A)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom{|A|}{j}
for all
A\in\mathcal{F}
. Note that
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}(A)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom{|A|}{j}
is trivially satisfied for all
A
with
|A|\leq t
.
Of particular interest to us will be
1
-temperate families, i.e. families
\mathcal{F}
where
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}(A)\leq 1+|A|
for all
A\in\mathcal{F}
.
For brevity, we will refer to
1
-temperate families as
temperate
families.

Simple examples of
t
-temperate families include antichains (for
t\geq 0
), chains (for
t\geq 1)
and the union of any
t+1
consecutive layers of the hypercube (for
t\geq 0
) since any set
A
of size
|A|\geq l
has precisely
\sum_{j=0}^{l}\binom{|A|}{j}
subsets which lie at most
l
layers below
A
.

In this short paper, we begin the investigation of properties of
t
-temperate families by considering the following two extremal questions. Recall that a family is called
intersecting
if the intersection of any two elements of it is non-empty.

Question 1.1
.

What is the maximum size of a
t
-temperate family on
[n]
?

Question 1.2
.

What is the maximum size of an intersecting
t
-temperate family on
[n]
?

1.1
Main results

As we shall see, the answer to
Question

1.1
is given by the total size of the
t+1
largest layers of the hypercube.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
t\leq n
be non-negative integers. The maximum size of a
t
-temperate family on
[n]
is
\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom{n}{\lfloor(n-t)/2\rfloor+j}
.

Moreover, we classify all the
t
-temperate families which attain the maximal size. We use
\binom{[n]}{k}
to denote the set of subsets of
[n]
of size
k
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
t\leq n
be non-negative integers.

1.

If
t\equiv n\pmod{2}
, the only
t
-temperate family of maximal size is
\bigcup_{j=0}^{t}\binom{[n]}{(n-t)/2+j}
.

2.

If
t=n-1
, any family of size
2^{n}-1
is a
t
-temperate family of maximal size.

3.

If
t\not\equiv n\pmod{2}
and
t<n-1
, then there are two
t
-temperate families of maximal size:
\bigcup_{j=0}^{t}\binom{[n]}{(n-t-1)/2+j}
and
\bigcup_{j=0}^{t}\binom{[n]}{(n-t+1)/2+j}
.

The case of
Question

1.2
turns out to be more convoluted. In this paper, we restrict our attention to the case of intersecting temperate families. In such a setting, we obtain the following answer for odd
n
.

Theorem 1.5
.

The maximum size of an intersecting temperate family on
[n]
for odd
n=2k-1
is
\binom{2k-1}{k}+\binom{2k-1}{k+1}
.

As with the non-intersecting case, we provide the full classification of the extremal families.

Theorem 1.6
.

For odd
n=2k-1
the unique intersecting temperate family of maximal size is
\binom{[2k-1]}{k}\cup\binom{[2k-1]}{k+1}
unless
n=3
, in which case there are three additional such families. These are
\{A\in\mathcal{P}([3]):i\in A\}
for each
i\in[3]
.

For even
n
, we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.7
.

The maximum size of an intersecting temperate family on
[n]
for even
n=2k
is
\frac{1}{2}\binom{2k}{k}+\binom{2k}{k+1}+\binom{2k-1}{k+2}
.

We believe one of the extremal constructions supporting
Conjecture

1.7
is the following one, whose name comes from the shape the contained elements resemble when drawn in the Hesse diagram of the hypercube, with rows ordered lexicographically. By a
lightning
, we mean the family on
[2k]
which contains:

•

all elements of
\binom{[2k]}{k}
containing
1
, and

•

all elements of
\binom{[2k]}{k+1}
, and

•

all elements of
\binom{[2k]}{k+2}
not containing
1
.

1.2
Background and motivation

The motivation for investigating temperate families and the two questions above in particular comes from odd-sunflowers as introduced by Frankl, Pach and Pálvölgyi
[
10
]
. These are in turn inspired by sunflowers, as originally studied by Erdős and Rado
[
9
]
.

Let us give a brief historical overview on sunflowers and odd-sunflowers. A family of
r\geq 3
sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
is an
r
-sunflower
if
S_{i}\cap S_{j}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{r}S_{k}
for all
i\neq j
. A
sunflower
is a family which is an
r
-sunflower for some
r\geq 3
. A family is said to be
r
-sunflower-free
if it contains no
r
-sunflower.
Analogously, it is said to be
sunflower-free
if it contains no sunflower.

In 1960, Erdős and Rado
[
9
]
showed that an
r
-sunflower-free family
\mathcal{F}
where every set
A\in\mathcal{F}
satisfies
|A|\leq w
satisfies
|\mathcal{F}|<w!\cdot(r-1)^{w}
. Furthermore, they conjectured that there exists
c=c(r)
such that this bound may be improved to
|\mathcal{F}|<c^{w}
. This conjecture, known as the Sunflower Conjecture, is unresolved to this date. In view of recent developments due to Alweiss, Lovett, Wu and Zhang
[
2
]
, Rao
[
12
]
, Tao
[
15
]
and Bell, Chueluecha and Warnke
[
4
]
, the current best known bound is of the form
O(r\cdot\log(w))^{w}
.

A second main direction in which sunflower-free families have been studied, and the direction closer to our questions, is that of the maximum size of a sunflower-free family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}([n])
. Denote this quantity
f(n)
, and let
\phi=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)^{1/n}
. Erdős and Szemerédi
[
8
]
conjectured in 1978 that
\phi<2
. Naslund and Sawin
[
11
]
finally proved this almost 40 years later, building on the techniques of Croot, Lev and Pach
[
5
]
, Ellenberg and Gijswijt
[
7
]
and Tao
[
14
]
. Naslund and Sawin gave
\phi<1.890
, which remains the state of art. In the opposite direction, the best known lower bound
\phi>1.551
is due to Deuber, Erdős, Gunderson, Kostochka and Meyer
[
6
]
.

As variants on sunflowers, Frankl, Pach and Pálvölgyi
[
10
]
introduced the following notion of odd-sunflowers and even-sunflowers.

A non-empty family of non-empty sets is an
even-degree sunflower
or, simply, an
even-sunflower
, if every element of the underlying set is contained in an even number of sets (or in none). Analogously, a family of at least two non-empty sets forms an
odd-degree sunflower
or, simply, an
odd-sunflower
, if every element of the underlying set is contained in an odd number of sets, or in none
[
10
]
.

It should be noted that neither an even-sunflower nor an odd-sunflower need be a sunflower. However, any
3
-sunflower is an odd-sunflower. Thus every family that does not contain an odd-sunflower is guaranteed to be sunflower-free.

Let
f_{\mathrm{even}}(n)
be the maximum size of a family of sets in
[n]
that contains no even-sunflower. Analogously, let
f_{\mathrm{odd}}(n)
be the maximum size of a family of sets in
[n]
that contains no odd-sunflower. These two quantities exhibit a diametrically opposite behaviour. On one hand, the authors of
[
10
]
observe that, as a variant of the “odd-town” problem (see, for example, a textbook by Babai and Frankl
[
3
]
for a reference), the following holds.

Theorem 1.8
.

f_{\mathrm{even}}(n)=n
.

On the other hand, Frankl, Pach and Pálvölgyi
[
10
]
give the following exponential lower bound on
f_{\mathrm{odd}}
.

Theorem 1.9
.

f_{\mathrm{odd}}(n)>1.502^{n}
.

However, there is no better known upper bound than the one on sunflower-free families by Naslund and Sawin
[
11
]
, i.e.,
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\mathrm{odd}}(n)^{1/n}\leq\phi<1.890
.

Our two questions came from pursuit of a better upper bound for
f_{\mathrm{odd}}(n)
and two observations.
First, note that every family
\mathcal{F}
that does not contain an odd-sunflower is a temperate family. Indeed, any set
A
together with an even sunflower
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(A)\setminus\{A\}
form an odd-sunflower, and thus by
Theorem

1.8
, we need
\mu_{\mathcal{F}}(A)\leq|A|+1
for all
A\in\mathcal{F}
. Furthermore, as two disjoint sets are considered an odd-sunflower, every family that does not contain an odd-sunflower is intersecting.

The maximum size of a
t
-temperate family

The first step of our proof of
Theorem

1.3
is
Observation

2.1
, for which the following notation will be helpful. For a family
\mathcal{F}
on
[n]
, we let
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{A\in\mathcal{F}:|A|=i\}

Observation 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be (any) family on
[n]

\mathcal{C}
be a random maximal chain on
[n]

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}}[w(\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C})]=|\mathcal{F}|

Proof.

We simply compute
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}}[w(\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C})]=\sum_{i=0}^{n}w([i])\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{F}_{i}\cap\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}w([i])\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{i}|/\binom{n}{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}|\mathcal{F}_{i}|=|\mathcal{F}|
.
∎

The maximum size of an intersecting temperate family

The statement is clear if
n\leq 3
, so we suppose that
n\geq 5
onwards. If
\mathcal{F}
is an intersecting temperate family of maximal size, then
equality in (
7
) holds. Morevoer, the final paragraph of the proof of
Theorem

1.5
implies that
\mathcal{F}
consists of one half of the elements of
\binom{[2k-1]}{k+1}\cup\binom{[2k-1]}{k-2}
and one half of the elements of
\binom{[2k-1]}{k}\cup\binom{[2k-1]}{k-1}
.

As it is intersecting, it contains at least one element of
\binom{[2k-1]}{k+1}
, which for
n>5
(that is,
k>3
) by the ‘moreover’ part of
Lemma

3.1
contains all its subsets of size
k
. Using the same argument as in the proof of
Theorem

1.4

3
we deduce that in fact
\mathcal{F}
contains all sets of size
k+1
and thus it must be
\binom{[2k-1]}{k}\cup\binom{[2k-1]}{k+1}
.

This remains true for
n=5
unless there is a singleton set, say
\{5\}
in
\mathcal{F}
. But then all sets of
\mathcal{F}
have
5
as an element and thus
\{A\setminus\{5\}:A\in\mathcal{F}\}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
is a temperate family on

of size

, which contradicts

1.3
.
∎

For even
n=2k
, the same proof technique one gives an upper bound of
\frac{1}{2}\binom{2k}{k}+\binom{2k}{k+1}+\frac{1}{2}\binom{2k}{k+2}
for the maximum size of an intersecting temperate family. However, using ad-hoc arguments it can be shown that this bound is not attained for any sufficiently large
n
.

Instead, as stated in
Conjecture

1.7
, we believe that this maximum size is
\frac{1}{2}\binom{2k}{k}+\binom{2k}{k+1}+\binom{2k-1}{k+2}
, with a lightning being one of the extremal constructions. Note that a lightning would not be a unique extremal construction, as it could be possible to exchange some of the
(k+1)
-sized sets not containing
1
with their complements.

A possible first step towards
Conjecture

1.7
is proving it with an extra assumption that the temperate family lies in the union
\binom{[2k]}{k}\cup\binom{[2k]}{k+1}\cup\binom{[2k]}{k+2}
.

Concluding remarks

In this paper, we studied
t
-temperate families, with the hope of achieving a better understanding of families which contain no odd-sunflowers. We have given a robust answer to

1.1
. However, the case of
Question

1.2

n
even and

We finish on a property of temperate families (and therefore also of families which contain no odd-sunflowers) that could be of further interest, the “Bubbling-up Lemma”.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a temperate family and
C\subset B\subset A
be sets such that
A
and
C
lie in
\mathcal{F}
but
B
does not lie in
\mathcal{F}
, and
|B|=|A|-1
. Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}=(\mathcal{F}\cup\{B\})\setminus\{C\}
is a temperate family.

Proof.

Let
D
be a set in
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
. If
D\neq B
, then the number of subsets of
D
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
is at most the number of subsets of
D
in
\mathcal{F}
since
C\subset B
. If
D=B
, then
\mu_{\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}}(B)\leq\mu_{\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}}(A)-1\leq|A|+1-1=|B|+1
. Thus
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
is a temperate family.
∎
