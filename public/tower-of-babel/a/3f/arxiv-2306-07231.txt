Introduction

Let
G
be a countable, discrete group. If
G
is torsion free and amenable, then the Kadison-Kaplansky conjecture holds
[
22
, Thm. 1.3]
, so
C^{*}(G)\cong C^{*}_{r}(G)
has no non-trivial projections. However, if
G
is not torsion free, then for every
g\in G
with finite order
n
, the element
\frac{1+g+g^{2}+...+g^{n-1}}{n}\in C^{*}(G)
is a projection. If, in addition the group is locally finite, then
C^{*}(G)
is an AF-algebra.

In
[
9
]
, Effros conjectured that if
C_{r}^{*}(G)
is an AF-algebra, then
G
has to be locally finite. The conjecture is still open. Actually, for amenable groups, we can strengthen the conjecture by replacing the AF-algebra assumption, with the weaker property of real rank zero:

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
G
be a discrete, amenable group such that
C^{*}(G)
has real rank zero. Then
G
is locally finite.

In the non-amenable case the situation is more complicated. For instance,
in
[
7
]
, Dykema and Rørdam proved that
C^{*}_{r}({\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\raisebox{-2.15277pt}[6.45831pt][0.0pt]{\rm*}}_{n=2}^{\infty}}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}))
has real rank zero. Note that the free product
{\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\raisebox{-2.15277pt}[6.45831pt][0.0pt]{\rm*}}_{n=2}^{\infty}}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
is a non-amenable and non-periodic group.

Conjecture
1.1
is open (even in the non-amenable case for full group
C^{*}
-algebras) but it has been verified for several classes of groups, including the ones below:

•

Discrete, amenable groups that have a subgroup of finite index that is torsion free. This is a direct Corollary of the proof of the Kadison-Kaplansky conjecture for amenable groups. For the sake of completion, we present the argument in Proposition
4.1
.

•

Discrete, nilpotent groups. It was proved by Kaniuth in
[
15
]
. (Actually, this result is more general and provides a characterization for when (non-necessarily discrete), locally compact nilpotent groups have group
C^{*}
-algebras that have real rank zero. But, because on this paper we will talk only about discrete groups, we restrict on that special case).

•

Finitely generated, discrete, elementary amenable groups. This was proved by Scarparo in
[
19
]

The purpose of this paper is to come up with more obstructions that prevent
C^{*}(G)
from having real rank zero. Our main result is the following;

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a discrete, amenable group and assume that
C^{*}(G)
has real rank zero. Then every normal subgroup of
G
that is elementary amenable and has finite Hirsch length, has to be locally finite.

Recall that because real rank zero is preserved under taking quotients, we can weaken the assumption of the Theorem above, by assuming that
H
is a normal subgroup of some quotient of
G
.

The Hirsch length was defined for elementary amenable groups by Hillman in
[
13
]
. We will explain more in Section 6, but for now we will only say that virtually polycyclic groups have finite Hirsch length. So, the following Corollary is automatic.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
G
be a discrete, amenable group and assume that
C^{*}(G)
has real rank zero. Then every normal subgroup of
G
that is virtually polycyclic has to be finite.

Theorem
1.2
yields new examples of groups verifying Conjecture
1.1
. For instance, notice that if
G
is amenable and has a normal subgroup isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then
C^{*}(G)
does not have real rank zero. Hence, we can start with an amenable group
H
that is infinitely generated (or not elementary amenable). Every group homomorphism
\sigma:H\rightarrow Aut(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\cong GL(n,\mathbb{Z})
gives rise to a semidirect product
G=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rtimes H
. Notice that
C^{*}(G)
does not have real rank zero and
G
is infinitely generated (or not elementary amenable).

For the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we generalize ideas from
[
19
]
and
[
1
]
. A key step is Proposition
5.1
. For its proof, we need to work with continuous fields. More specifically, we define a quantity for elements of continuous fields, called oscillation (see beginning of Section 2). This quantity is zero for self-adjoint elements of finite spectrum (Lemma
2.2
) and it cannot increase much under small perturbations (Lemma
2.1
). This strategy allows us to find self-adjoint elements on a group
C^{*}
-algebra that are far away from elements of finite spectrum. After proving Proposition
5.1
, we use group theory tools, like Tits Alternative
[
21
]
and a characterization of groups with finite Hirsch length from
[
14
]
, to show our main result.

The main difficulty when trying to extend our results even further (e.g dropping the assumption of finite Hirsch length on Theorem
1.2
, or generalize Scarparo’s result to all elementary amenable groups), arises from the fact that we can find increasing sequences of
C^{*}
-algebras with real rank greater than zero (e.g matrices over
C(\mathbb{T})
), such that the inductive limit has real rank zero. Actually, whether the inductive limit has real rank zero heavily depends on the structure of the connecting maps.

To bypass this issue, in Definition
7.1
we define a property for groups, whose presence implies that the full group
C^{*}
-algebra is not of real rank zero. A crucial feature of this property is that it is preserved when taking increasing unions. This will allow us to show that locally nilpotent groups whose group
C^{*}
-algebra has real rank zero, are locally finite (see Proposition
7.5
).

All groups will be discrete, unless clearly stated otherwise. We will denote with
e
the identity of a group. For

we define
\beta(g)

:=1-\frac{g+g^{-1}}{2}\in C^{*}(G)
. For a topological space

, we denote with

the set of connected components of
X
. The center (of a
C^{*}
-algebra, or a group) will be denoted by
Z(.)
, while the set of projections of a
C^{*}
-algebra, with

.

3.
Basics on groups

Let
G
be a group. We say that a subgroup
H
is
characteristic in
G
if for every
\sigma\in Aut(G)
, we have
\sigma(H)\subset H
. Note that this is equivalent to assume
\sigma(H)=H
for every
\sigma\in Aut(G).
It is well-known that the commutator subgroup
[G,G]
and the center
Z(G)
are characteristic in
G
. If
G
is abelian, the torsion subgroup
T(G)
is characteristic in
G
. Moreover, if
H
is characteristic in
G
and
G\trianglelefteq L
, then
H\trianglelefteq L
. Also, being a characteristic subgroup is a transitive relation.

Let
G
be a group. Its
derived series
, indexed
G^{(\alpha)}
, is defined as follows:

•

G^{(0)}=G

•

G^{(n+1)}=[G^{(n)},G^{(n)}]

•

G^{(\alpha)}=\bigcap_{\beta<\alpha}G^{(\beta)}

G
is
solvable
if its derived series terminates in finitely many steps. We say that a group is
virtually solvable
if it has a subgroup of finite index that is solvable. Let
G
be a virtually solvable group. By
[
16
, p.355]
, it has a characteristic subgroup of finite index that is solvable. Hence, it has a normal series, where each quotient is either abelian or finite.

The class of
elementary amenable groups
is the smallest class of groups that contains
\mathbb{Z}
, all finite groups, and it is closed under subgroups, quotients, extensions and increasing unions. Thus all locally virtually solvable groups are elementary amenable. The converse is not true. For more information on elementary amenable groups, we refer the reader to a survey by A.Garrido (
[
10
]
).

We say that a group is
periodic
if every element of the group has finite order and
locally finite
if it is an increasing union of finite groups. Every locally finite group is periodic, while the converse holds for a large class of groups, including elementary amenable ones by
[
3
, Thm 2.3]
. However, the converse does not hold in general. For instance, the Grigorchuk group, which was defined in
[
11
]
is periodic but not locally finite. Actually, it is an amenable group that is not elementary amenable.

The following observation is known to experts, but we present a proof for the sake of completion.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a virtually solvable group. Then there is a normal series

1=G_{0}\trianglelefteq G_{1}\trianglelefteq...\trianglelefteq G_{n}=G

such that

i.

for every
i
,
G_{i+1}/G_{i}
is either locally finite or abelian.

ii.

There is no
i
such that both
G_{i+1}/G_{i}
and
G_{i+2}/G_{i+1}
are locally finite.

Proof.

Consider a normal series

1=G_{0}\trianglelefteq G_{1}\trianglelefteq...\trianglelefteq G_{n}=G

that satisfies
(i)
. and has minimal length (in the sense that there is no normal series of length
m<n
that satisfies
(i)
). Recall that by the aforementioned, there exists at least one such series. Hence, we can take a minimal one. We will show that such a sequence has to satisfy
(ii)
.
For the sake of contradiction assume that there exists
i
such that both
G_{i+1}/G_{i}
and
G_{i+2}/G_{i+1}
are locally finite. Because the series is normal,
G_{i}\trianglelefteq G_{i+2}
. Because, both
G_{i+1}/G_{i}
and
G_{i+2}/G_{i+1}
are locally finite and the class of locally finite groups is closed under taking extensions,
G_{i+2}/G_{i}
is locally finite. So the normal series

1=G_{0}\trianglelefteq G_{1}\trianglelefteq..\trianglelefteq G_{i}\trianglelefteq G_{i+2}\trianglelefteq..\trianglelefteq G_{n}=G

satisfies (i). But also it has length

, contradicting minimality.
∎

of

.
It can be shown (see

for more details) that there exists a faithful *-homomorphism

\Phi:C^{*}(G)\rightarrow M_{n}(C^{*}(N))

that satisfies

(\Phi(a))_{h^{\prime},h}=E(g_{h^{\prime}}ag_{h}^{-1}).

Let

. Observe that
\pi(g_{h^{\prime}}ag_{h}^{-1})=e
iff

Hence

\Phi(a)=diag(g_{h}ag_{h}^{-1}:\hskip 5.69054pth\in H).

We have

for every

. Indeed, if

, then
\tau_{G}(x)=\lambda_{e}
. On the other hand,
tr_{n}\otimes\tau_{N}(\Phi(x))=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{N}(\Phi(x)_{ii})
. Note that
(\Phi(g))_{ii}=0
for every
i
, if

. Also,
(\Phi(e))_{ii}=e
and
\tau_{N}(g_{h}xg_{h}^{-1})=0
for

, because

. Hence,
tr_{n}\otimes\tau_{N}(\Phi(x))=\lambda_{e}
. Because
\mathbb{C}[G]
is dense in

we have that
tr_{n}\otimes\tau_{N}(\Phi(x))=\tau_{G}(x)
for every

. Combining this with the fact that Baum-Connes conjecture holds for torsion free amenable groups and Lemma

, we deduce the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
G
be a discrete, infinite, amenable group that has a torsion free subgroup of finite index. Then
C^{*}(G)
cannot have real rank zero.

Proof.

Let
N\trianglelefteq G
be a normal subgroup of finite index that is torsion free. Because the Baum-Connes conjecture holds for
N
,
Tr_{n}\otimes\tau_{N}(p)\in\mathbb{N}
for every
p\in P_{n}(C^{*}(N))
(see
[
22
, Prop. 6.3.1, Thm. 1.3]
). Note that
Tr_{n}
is the unnormalized trace on
n\times n
matrices. By the above,
\tau_{G}(q)\geq\frac{1}{n}
for every
0\neq q\in P(C^{*}(G))
. Result follows from Lemma
2.6
.
∎

Now assume that the extension in (

) is central and
G
is amenable (H is no longer assumed to be finite). Then, by
[

, Thm. 1.2]
(see also
[

)

is a continuous field over
\widehat{N}
, where
\widehat{N}
is the Pontryagin dual of

. Moreover the fiber on the trivial character of

is isomorphic to

, while more generally each fiber is isomorphic to some twisted group
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(H,\sigma)
.

5.
A class of groups whose

The following Proposition is a generalization of the ideas in the proof of

.

6.
Hirsch length and the main result

The Hirsch length was first defined for polycyclic groups, and in
[
13
]
it was generalized to all elementary amenable groups. More specifically, define
EG_{0}
to be the set containing
\mathbb{Z}
and finite groups. Also define
EG_{\alpha}
to be
\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}EG_{\beta}
if
\alpha
is a limit ordinal. Otherwise, define it to be the set that arises from the elements of
EG_{\alpha-1}
after taking increasing unions and extensions. By
[
3
, Prop. 2.2]
, the set of elementary amenable groups is
\bigcup_{\alpha}EG_{\alpha}
. Define the Hirsch length inductively via the following relations:

•

h(\mathbb{Z})=1

•

h(G)=0
for every finite group
G
.

•

h(H)+h(G/H)=h(G)\hskip 11.38109pt\text{ for every }H\trianglelefteq G.

•

h(\varinjlim G_{n})=\sup h(G_{n})
for every increasing net of groups
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}.

By
[
13
, Theorem 1.]
, the Hirsch length is well-defined for every elementary amenable group
G
.

Observe that
h(G)=0
iff
G
is locally finite. Moreover,
h(\mathbb{Z}^{n})=n
and more generally, in a polycyclic group,
h(G)
counts the (finite) number of infinite (cyclic) factors. So, virtually polycyclic (and hence finitely generated nilpotent) groups have finite Hirsch length. There exist groups that have finite Hirsch length but are not finitely generated, e.g
h(\mathbb{Q}^{n})=n
. There exist groups that have infinite Hirsch length, like
\bigoplus_{\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
, where
\wr
denotes the reduced wreath product. The latter is an example of a finitely generated group that has infinite Hirsch length.

We need the following Lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
G
be a torsion free abelian group with finite Hirsch length. Then
Aut(G)
is a linear group.

Proof.

Because
G
is torsion free,
Aut(G)\subset Aut(G\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}).
Moreover,
G\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space. Observe that
G\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}=\varinjlim G
, where the n-th connecting map is multiplication with
n
. Hence
h(G\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})=h(G)<+\infty.
So,
G\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}\cong\mathbb{Q}^{l}
for
l=h(G)
. Thus
Aut(G)\subset Aut(G\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})=GL(l,\mathbb{Q})
. Proof is complete.
∎

The following Lemma is stated without proof on
[
14
, p. 238]
. We will prove it for the sake of completion.

Lemma 6.2
.

Every group
G
has a unique maximal normal locally finite subgroup.

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{E}:=\{H\trianglelefteq G\hskip 8.53581pt|\hskip 8.53581ptH\text{ is locally finite }\}.

Let
(L_{i})_{i\in I}
be an increasing sequence of locally finite normal subgroups of
G
. Then
L:=\bigcup_{i\in I}L_{i}
is a locally finite normal subgroup of
G
. Hence, by Zorn’s Lemma,
\mathcal{E}
has a maximal element. We will show that
\mathcal{E}
actually has a unique maximal element. For the sake of contradiction, let
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}
be distinct maximal elements in
\mathcal{E}
. Because
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}
are normal in
G
, the product
\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2}
is a normal subgroup of
G
. It is not difficult to verify that it is also locally finite. Finally,
\Lambda_{1}\subsetneq\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{2}
, contradicting maximality. Proof is complete.
∎

For the rest of the paper, for a group
G
, we will write
\Lambda(G)
to denote the unique maximal, normal locally finite subgroup of
G
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For every group
G
,
\Lambda(G)
is characteristic in
G.

Proof.

Let
\sigma:G\rightarrow G
be an automorphism of
G
. Then
\sigma(\Lambda(G))
is a locally finite, normal subgroup of
G
. Hence, by Lemma
6.2
and its proof, we get that
\sigma(\Lambda(G))\subset\Lambda(G)
. Thus
\Lambda(G)
is characteristic in
G.

∎

Observe that
G/\Lambda(G)
does not have any non-trivial, normal, locally finite subgroups.
Now we are ready to prove our main result:

Proof of Theorem
1.2

We will actually show the contrapositive: i.e we will show that if
G
is discrete, amenable and has a normal subgroup
H
which is elementary amenable with finite Hirsch length and is not locally finite, then
C^{*}(G)
does not have real rank zero.

By
[
14
, Theorem]
, we have that
H/\Lambda(H)
is virtually solvable. Because
\Lambda(H)
is characteristic in
H
, we have that
\Lambda(H)\trianglelefteq G
and
H/\Lambda(H)\trianglelefteq G/\Lambda(H).
Note that amenability and finite Hirsch length is preserved under taking quotients. Moreover the property of having real rank zero is preserved under taking quotients. Hence, we may assume that
H
is non-trivial, is virtually solvable with finite Hirsch length and has no non-trivial normal locally finite subgroups.

By
[
16
, p.355]
, there exists
L
characteristic in
H
that is solvable and it has finite index in
H
. Note that
L\trianglelefteq G
so we may assume that
H
is non-trivial, is solvable with finite Hirsch length and has no normal, non-trivial, locally finite subgroups.

Because
H
is solvable, its derived series has finite length. Because the commutator of a group is a characteristic subgroup, the last non-zero group on the derived series, let
S
is an abelian characteristic subgroup of
H
. Moreover, by assumption, the fact that in an abelian group the torsion subgroup is characteristic, and the fact that being a characteristic subgroup is a transitive property, we deduce that
S
is torsion free. Otherwise the torsion subgroup would be a non-zero normal locally finite subgroup of
H
. Note that
S\trianglelefteq G
, so we may assume that
H
is non-trivial, is abelian, torsion free with finite Hirsch length. But now result yields from Lemma
6.1
and Theorem
5.3
. ∎

We will end the section with one more Corollary of Theorem
1.2
.

We say that a group
G
is with
infinite conjugacy classes
, or
icc
if it is not trivial and for every
a\neq e
, the set
\{g^{-1}ag\hskip 8.53581pt|\hskip 8.53581ptg\in G\}
is infinite. Examples of icc groups are
S_{\infty}
and all free groups. On the other hand, nilpotent groups cannot be icc as they have non-trivial center.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
G
be a discrete amenable group such that
C^{*}(G)
has real rank zero.
There exists a locally finite, normal subgroup
H\trianglelefteq G
such that
G/H
is either trivial or icc.

Proof.

Assume that
G
is not locally finite. We will show that
\{e\}\neq G/\Lambda(G)
is icc, which is enough to yield the result. For the sake of contradiction, assume that
G/\Lambda(G)
is not icc. By construction, it has no non-trivial, locally finite, normal subgroups. So, by
[
18
, Proposition 1.1]
,
G/\Lambda(G)
has a normal subgroup isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for some
n
. By assumption
C^{*}(G/\Lambda(G))
has real rank zero. Thus, we have a contradiction by Theorem
1.2
.
∎

7.
Groups where real part of all non-torsion elements is far away from elements of finite spectrum in the group
C^{*}
-algebra

Our main difficulty in proving Conjecture
1.1
, at least in the case of elementary amenable groups, arises from the fact that we can find increasing sequences of
C^{*}
-algebras with real rank greater than zero (e.g matrices over
C(\mathbb{T})
), such that the inductive limit has real rank zero). That’s why define a property for groups, whose presence implies that the full group
C^{*}
-algebra does not have real rank zero. A crucial feature of this property is that it is preserved when taking increasing unions (Prop.
7.2
).

Set
d(a):=\inf\{||a-v||,\hskip 14.22636ptv=v^{*}\in C^{*}(G)\text{ and }v\text{ has finite spectrum}\}.
Note that
d(a)\leq||a||
and
d(a\pm\lambda\cdot 1)=d(a)
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. A
C^{*}
-algebra
A
has real rank zero, iff
d(a)=0
for every
a=a^{*}\in A
.

Definition 7.1
.

We say that a group
G
is
strongly not (FS)
if it is not periodic and for every
g\in G
that is non-torsion, we have that
d(\frac{g+g^{-1}}{2})=1.

Obviously, if
G
is strongly not (FS),
C^{*}(G)
cannot have real rank zero. We do not know if the converse holds. If it holds, then
[
19
, Thm 2.3]
would automatically yield that Conjecture
1.1
would be true for all elementary amenable groups. Also Proposition
5.1
and Theorem
1.2
would yield that the group
C^{*}
-algebras of more (non-elementary) amenable groups do not have real rank zero.

Proposition 7.2
.

If
G_{n}
is an increasing union of strongly not (FS) groups, then
G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}G_{n}
is also strongly not (FS).

Proof.

Let
g\in G
non-torsion,
\varepsilon>0
and
w=w^{*}\in C^{*}(G)
that has finite spectrum. By the spectral theorem, there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g\in G_{n}
and
v=v^{*}\in C^{*}(G_{n})
with finite spectrum such that
||w-v||<\varepsilon.
Because
G_{n}
is strongly not (FS),
||\frac{g+g^{-1}}{2}-v||\geq 1.
Thus
||\frac{g+g^{-1}}{2}-w||\geq 1-\varepsilon.
Because
\varepsilon>0
,
g
and
w
were selected at random,
d(\frac{g+g^{-1}}{2})=1
for every
g\in G
non-torsion. Hence
G
is strongly not (FS).
∎

By Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
2.1
, every countable abelian group that is not locally finite is strongly not (FS).

Lemma 7.3
.

Let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{e\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\{e\}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}

be short exact sequence of groups, with
N
periodic and
H\neq\{e\}
torsion free amenable. Then
G
is strongly not (FS).

Proof.

Let
g\in G
non-torsion. Because
N
is periodic,
g\notin G
. Hence
\pi(g)\neq e
. Because
H
is torsion free amenable, the Kadison-Kaplansky conjecture holds for
H
by
[
22
, Thm. 1.3]
. Hence
C^{*}(H)
does not have any non-trivial projections. Hence, we have
d(\frac{g+g^{-1}}{2})\geq d(\frac{\pi(g)+\pi(g)^{-1}}{2})=1.
Thus
d(\frac{g+g^{-1}}{2})=1
. Because
g
is a random non-torsion element, we deduce that
G
is strongly not (FS).
∎

Lemma 7.4
.

Every nilpotent group is periodic by torsion free.

Proof.

Let
G
be a nilpotent group and set

N:=\{x\in G\hskip 8.53581pt|\hskip 8.53581pto(g)<\infty\}.

By
[
20
, Ch. 1, Cor. 10]
,
N
is a periodic, normal subgroup of
G
and
G/N
is torsion free. Result follows.
∎

Combining Proposition
7.2
, Lemma
7.3
, Lemma
7.4
and the fact that an elementary amenable, periodic group is locally finite, we deduce the following:

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
G
be a locally nilpotent group that is not locally finite. Then it is strongly not (FS). In particular,
C^{*}(G)
does not have real rank zero.

The following example from
[
13
]
yields a new example not covered by Theorem
5.3
.

Example 7.6
.

Let
x_{k}\in\bigoplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}
be the sequence that has 1 on the
k
-th position and 0 elsewhere. For
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, consider the automorphism
e_{i}\in Aut(\bigoplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z})
that satisfies
e_{i}(x_{i})=x_{i}+x_{i+1}
and
e_{i}(x_{j})=x_{j}
if
i\neq j.
Let
G\leq Aut(\bigoplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z})
be the group generated by
\{e_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. As
G
is an increasing union of subgroups isomorphic to groups of upper triangular matrices, it is locally nilpotent. So, by Proposition
7.5
,
C^{*}(G)
does not have real rank zero. On the other hand, it can be shown that

has no non-trivial normal, abelian subgroups and it has infinite Hirsch length.
