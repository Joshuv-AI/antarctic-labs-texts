Rado’s criterion over squares and higher powers
Sam Chow, Sofia Lindqvist, Sean Prendiville

Abstract
. We establish partition regularity of the generalised Pythagorean equation in five or more variables. Furthermore, we show how Rado’s characterisation of a partition regular equation remains valid over the set of positive k k th powers, provided the equation has at least ( 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ k ​ log ⁡ k (1+o(1))k\log k variables. We thus completely describe which diagonal forms are partition regular and which are not, given sufficiently many variables. In addition, we prove a supersaturated version of Rado’s theorem for a linear equation restricted either to squares minus one or to logarithmically-smooth numbers.

1.
Introduction

Schur’s theorem
[
Sch1916
]
is a foundational result in Ramsey theory, asserting that in any finite colouring of the positive integers there exists a monochromatic solution to the equation
x+y=z
(a solution in which each variable receives the same colour). A notorious question of Erdős and Graham asks if the same is true for the Pythagorean equation
x^{2}+y^{2}=z^{2}
, offering $250 for an answer
[
Grah07
,
Grah08
]
. The computer-aided verification
[
HKM16
]
of the two colour case of this problem is reported to be the largest mathematical proof in existence, consuming 200 terabytes
[
Lam16
]
. We provide an affirmative answer to the analogue of the Erdős–Graham question for generalised Pythagorean equations in five or more variables.

Theorem 1.1

(Schur-type theorem in the squares)
.

In any finite colouring of the positive integers there exists a monochromatic solution to the equation

x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}=x_{5}^{2}.

(1.1)

This is a consequence of a more general phenomenon. Given enough variables, we completely describe which diagonal forms have the above property and which do not.

Definition 1.2

(Partition regular)
.

Given a polynomial
P\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{s}]
and a set
S
call the equation
P(x)=0

partition regular over
S
if, in any finite colouring of
S
, there exists a solution
x\in S^{s}
whose coordinates all receive the same colour. We say that the equation is
non-trivially partition regular
if every finite colouring of
S
has a monochromatic solution in which each variable is distinct.

Rado
[
Rad33
]
established an elegant algebraic characterisation of partition regular homogeneous linear equations.

Rado’s criterion for one equation
.

Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, where
s\geqslant 3
. Then the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{i}x_{i}=0
is (non-trivially) partition regular over the positive integers if and only if there exists a non-empty set
I\subset[s]
such that
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
.

A number of authors
[
Ber96
,
Ber16
,
Grah08
,
DNB18
]
have sought algebraic characterisations of partition regularity within families of non-linear Diophantine equations. The example of the Fermat equation shows that one cannot hope for something as simple as Rado’s criterion for diagonal forms. Nevertheless, provided that the number of variables
s
is sufficiently large in terms of the degree
k
, we establish that the same criterion characterises partition regularity for equations in
k
th powers.

Theorem 1.3

(Rado over
k
th powers)
.

There exists
s_{0}(k)\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for
s\geqslant s_{0}(k)
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
the following holds. The equation

\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{i}x_{i}^{k}=0

(1.2)

is (non-trivially) partition regular over the positive integers if and only if there exists a non-empty set
I\subset[s]
such that
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
. Moreover, we may take
s_{0}(2)=5
,
s_{0}(3)=8
and

s_{0}(k)=k\left(\log k+\log\log k+2+O(\log\log k/\log k)\right).

(1.3)

Notice that Rado’s criterion for a linear equation shows that the condition
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
is necessary for (
1.2
) to be partition regular. The content of Theorem
1.3
is that this condition is also sufficient.

For higher-degree equations one cannot avoid the assumption of some lower bound on the number of variables, as the example of the Fermat equation demonstrates. Given current knowledge on the solubility of diagonal Diophantine equations
[
Woo92
]
, the bound (
1.3
) is at the cutting edge of present technology. Indeed, it is unlikely that one could improve this condition without making an analogous breakthrough in Waring’s problem, since partition regularity implies the existence of a non-trivial integer solution to the equation (
1.2
).

We remark that one could use the methods of this paper to establish the weaker but explicit bound

s_{0}(k)\leqslant k^{2}+1.

This follows by utilising the work of Bourgain–Demeter–Guth
[
BDG16
]
on Vinogradov’s mean value theorem, eschewing smooth numbers, as in
[
Cho17
]
.

We are also able to establish the sufficiency of Rado’s criterion for other sparse arithmetic sets of interest, such as logarithmically-smooth numbers and shifted squares. For these sets we avoid certain local issues which must be surmounted for perfect powers, and thereby prove stronger quantitative variants of partition regularity, analogous to work of Frankl, Graham and Rödl
[
FGR88
]
counting monochromatic solutions to a linear equation.

1.1.
Non-triviality

It may be that (
1.2
) possesses a wealth of monochromatic solutions for ‘trivial’ reasons. For instance, if
c_{1}+\dots+c_{s}=0
then taking
x_{1}=\dots=x_{s}
yields many uninteresting solutions. We have delineated between partition regularity and non-trivial partition regularity to ensure that Rado’s criterion still has content in such a situation. However, since Rado’s criterion is necessary for ‘trivial’ partition regularity, the two notions are in fact equivalent.

1.2.
Previous work

To the knowledge of the authors, work on non-linear partition regularity begins with papers of Furstenberg and Sárközy
[
Fur77
,
Sár78
]
, independently resolving a conjecture of Lovász—a line of investigation which culminates in the polynomial Szemerédi theorem of Bergelson–Leibman
[
BL96
]
, proved using ergodic methods. Such methods have also established colouring results for which no density analogue exists, such as partition regularity of the equation
x-y=z^{2}

[
Ber96
, p.53]
. Interestingly, the story is more complicated for the superficially similar equation
x+y=z^{2}
studied in
[
KS06
,
CGS12
,
GL16
,
Pac18
]
.

A recent breakthrough of Moreira
[
Mor17
]
resolves a longstanding conjecture of Hindman
[
Hin79
]
, proving partition regularity of the equation
x+y^{2}=yz
. More intuitively: in any finite colouring of the positive integers there exists a monochromatic configuration of the form
\left\{a,a+b,ab\right\}
. This result is a consequence of a general theorem which also yields partition regularity of equations of the form
x_{0}=c_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\dots+c_{s}x_{s}^{2}
, subject to the condition that
c_{1}+\dots+c_{s}=0
.

Notice that all of the above results involve an equation with at least one linear term. There are fewer results in the literature concerning genuinely non-linear equations such as (
1.2
). Certain diagonal quadrics are dealt with in Lefmann
[
Lef91
, Fact 2.8]
, using Rado’s theorem to locate a long monochromatic progression whose common difference possesses a (well-chosen) multiple of the same colour. This results in the following sufficient condition for partition regularity.

Lefmann’s criterion
.

Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, and suppose that
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
with
I\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, suppose that the auxiliary system

\begin{split}\Bigl(\sum_{i\notin I}c_{i}\Bigr)x_{0}^{2}+\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}x_{i}^{2}&=0,\\
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}x_{i}&=0\end{split}

(1.6)

possesses a rational solution with
x_{0}\neq 0
. Then the equation

c_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\dots+c_{s}x_{s}^{2}=0

(1.7)

is partition regular.

This result reduces the combinatorial problem of establishing partition regularity of (
1.7
) to a task in number theory: find a rational point of a certain form on a variety determined by a diagonal quadric and linear equation. In Appendix
F
we derive general algebraic criteria guaranteeing such a rational point using the Hardy–Littlewood circle method.

Theorem 1.8

(Lefmann + Hardy–Littlewood circle method)
.

Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, and suppose that
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
with
I\neq\emptyset
. Suppose in addition that
|I|\geqslant 6
and at least two
c_{i}
are positive and at least two are negative. Then

c_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\dots+c_{s}x_{s}^{2}=0

(1.8)

is partition regular.

This result does not encompass all equations amenable to Lefmann’s criterion: fewer variables may suffice, for instance

x^{2}+9y^{2}=2z^{2}+8w^{2}\qquad\text{or}\qquad 4x^{2}+y^{2}=2z^{2}+2w^{2}.

We emphasise that Lefmann’s criterion cannot hope to be a necessary condition for partition regularity, as there are partition regular equations for which the auxiliary Lefmann system (
1.6
) has no rational point of the required form. Such equations include the generalised Pythagorean equation (
1.1
), as well as the ‘convex’ equation

x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}=4x_{5}^{2}

(1.9)

addressed in
[
BP17
]
.

In the same article, Lefmann
[
Lef91
, Theorem 2.6]
established Rado’s criterion for reciprocals.

Theorem 1.9

(Lefmann)
.

Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{s}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}
. Then

\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{i}x_{i}^{-1}=0

is partition regular over
{\mathbb{N}}
if and only if
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
for some non-empty
I\subset[s]
.

This demonstrates the partition regularity of

\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{y}=\frac{1}{z},

answering a question of Erdős and Graham.

If one is prepared to relax the definition of partition regularity, so that certain variables are not constrained to receive the same colour as the remainder, then specific homogeneous equations of arbitrary degree are dealt with in Frantzikinakis–Host
[
FH14
]
. For instance, one consequence of their methods is that in any finite colouring of the positive integers there exist distinct
x,y
of the same colour, along with
\lambda
(possibly of a different colour) such that

9x^{2}+16y^{2}=\lambda^{2}.

(1.10)

However for these techniques to succeed, not only must one variable of (
1.10
) be free to take on any colour, but it is also necessary for the solution set to possess a well-factorable parametrisation, allowing for the theory of multiplicative functions to come into play.

When the coefficients of (
1.2
) sum to zero, partition regularity follows easily, since any element of the diagonal constitutes a monochromatic solution. However, there are results in the literature which also guarantee
non-trivial
partition regularity in this situation, provided that
s\geqslant k^{2}+1
. This was first established for quadrics in
[
BP17
]
and for general
k
in
[
Cho17
]
. In fact in
[
Cho17
]

it is established that, under these assumptions, dense subsets of the
primes
contain many solutions to (
1.2
). Density results were obtained for non-diagonal quadratic forms in at least 9 variables by Zhao
[
Zha17
]
, subject to the condition that the corresponding matrix has columns which sum to zero.

We believe that when the solution set of a given equation contains the diagonal it is more robust with respect to certain local issues—indeed one expects dense sets (such as congruence classes) to contain solutions under this assumption. As a consequence, the local issues for such equations are easier to handle using elementary devices, such as passing to a well-chosen subprogression. The novelty in our methods is that for general equations, instead of tackling the somewhat thorny local problem head on, we show how we may assume our colouring possesses a certain homogeneous structure, and this structure allows the same devices available in the dense regime to come into play.

We remark that it appears to be a challenging problem to decrease
s_{0}(k)
substantially below
k^{2}+1
for the density analogue of Theorem
1.3
. In order to show that
s_{0}(k)=(1+o(1))k\log k
is admissible in our partition result we make heavy use of the fact that a colouring of the positive integers induces a colouring of the smooth positive integers, and we obtain a monochromatic solution to our equation in the smooths. Sets of positive density, however, may not contain any smooth numbers. We are therefore in the curious situation where we can prove that relatively dense sets of smooth numbers possess solutions to certain diagonal equations, but cannot say the same for dense sets of integers.

It is interesting to compare our results with partition regularity results over the primes. Here congruence obstructions mean that one cannot hope to establish a Rado-type criterion. For example, a parity obstruction prohibits Schur’s equation from being partition regular over the primes. The situation is markedly different if one considers modifications of the primes with no local obstructions, such as the set of primes minus one. Partition regularity of the Schur equation over this set was established by Li–Pan
[
LP12
]
, then generalised to the full Rado criterion for systems of linear equations by Lê
[
Lê12
]
. This latter result utilised the full strength of Green and Tao’s asymptotic for linear equations in primes
[
GT10a
]
, together with a characterisation of so called ‘large’ sets due to Deuber
[
Deu73
]
. Neither of these tools are available, or reasonable to expect, for
k
th powers.

The argument of Li–Pan for Schur’s theorem in primes minus one is a direct application of the Fourier-analytic transference principle pioneered by Green
[
Gre05
]
, elucidated by the same author in the context of partition regularity in a comment
2
2

2

https://goo.gl/Yjookp
on MathOverflow. This approach cannot hope to succeed for perfect powers, at least when the coefficients of the equation do not sum to zero, since one can no longer pass to the same (affine) subprogression in all of the variables. The introduction of homogeneous sets (Definition
2.2
) allows us to circumvent these difficulties. However, for squares minus one, or smooth numbers, one need only pass to projective subprogressions when enacting the transference principle. The methods of Part
III
therefore use a direct form of the transference principle analogous to Li–Pan. We include the argument to illustrate the subtleties which must be overcome for perfect powers.

Methods

All of the essential ideas required for Theorem

Theorem 2.1

Proof of Schur’s theorem.

We induct on the number of colours

N_{r}\in{\mathbb{N}}

From the definition of homogeneity it follows that there exists a positive integer

Since

x_{1}^{\prime}+x_{2}^{\prime}=x_{3}^{\prime}

The homogeneous case: All colour classes are

It may seem excessive to employ a density result in the proof of a colouring result, since (typically) density results lie deeper and require more work to prove.

5

One can give an alternative argument for Schur’s theorem based on these ideas, replacing Szemerédi’s theorem with van der Waerden’s. However, this approach does not seem to generalise to the non-linear situation.
We have described this approach to motivate our proof of Theorem
1.1

why
Schur’s theorem is true: there is always a long homogeneous arithmetic progression on which one of the colour classes is multiplicatively syndetic. This exemplifies a well-used philosophy in Ramsey theory that underlying every partition result there is some notion of largeness.

To prove partition regularity of the generalised Pythagorean equation we induct on the number of colours as in our proof of Schur’s theorem. The inhomogeneous case follows with minimal change to the argument. In the remaining case we may assume that all colour classes are homogeneous. In this situation we are able to show that every colour class contains many solutions to our non-linear equation by employing the following density result.

Theorem 2.3

(Non-linear homogeneous Sárközy)
.

For any
\delta>0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
N_{0}
and
c_{0}>0
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
the following holds. Let
A\subset[N]
have density at least
\delta
in
[N]
, and let
B
be an
M
-homogeneous subset of the positive integers. Then

\begin{split}\#\left\{({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\in A^{2}\times B^{3}:x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}=y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+y_{3}^{2}\right\}\geqslant c_{0}N^{3}.\end{split}

Using Green’s Fourier-analytic transference principle
[
Gre05
]
, as elucidated for squares in
[
BP17
,
Pre17a
]
, the deduction of Theorem
2.3
is reduced (in §§
5
–
6
) to a linear analogue in which the squares have been removed from the dense variables. This can be thought of as a generalisation of the Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem
[
Fur77
,
Sár78
]
, extended to homogeneous sets.

Theorem 2.4

(Supersaturated homogeneous Sárközy)
.

For any
\delta>0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
N_{0},c_{0}>0
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
the following holds. Let
A\subset[N]
have density at least
\delta
in
[N]
and let
B
be an
M
-homogeneous subset of the positive integers. Then there are at least
c_{0}N^{\frac{5}{2}}
tuples
(x,y)\in A^{2}\times B^{3}
satisfying the equation

\begin{split}x_{1}-x_{2}=y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+y_{3}^{2}.\end{split}

(2.2)

Our ability to remove the squares from the dense variables is intrinsically linked to the fact that the coefficients corresponding to these variables sum to zero. One consequence of this is that we may restrict all of the dense variables to lie in the same congruence class, without destroying solutions to the equation in the process.

Theorem
2.4
is ultimately derived (in §
8
) from the following result, which is both more general and at the same time slightly weaker than Theorem
2.4
. It is weaker in that it yields only one solution to (
2.2
), yet it applies to the more general context of multidimensional sets of integers. The increase in dimension allows us to deduce a supersaturation result for (
2.2
) by bootstrapping the existence of a single solution to the existence of many solutions, using an averaging argument first implemented by Varnavides
[
Var59
]
.

Theorem 2.5

(Multidimensional homogeneous Sárközy)
.

For any
\delta>0
and
d,M\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
N_{0}
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
the following holds.
If
A\subset[N]^{d}
is at least
\delta
-dense in
[N]^{d}
and
B_{1},\dots,B_{d}
are
M
-homogeneous sets of positive integers, then there exist
x,x^{\prime}\in A
and
y_{1}\in B_{1},\dots,y_{d}\in B_{d}
such that

x-x^{\prime}=(y_{1}^{2},\dots,y_{d}^{2}).

(2.3)

In §
7
this theorem is proved using the Fourier-analytic density increment strategy pioneered by Roth
[
Rot53
]
, a proof which yields quantitative bounds on
N_{0}
. One can deduce the qualitative statement in a few lines from the multidimensional polynomial Szemerédi theorem of Bergelson and Leibman
[
BL96
]
, see Corollary
9.1
. The general Rado criterion of Theorem
1.3
requires a more complicated density result for which Fourier analysis does not appear sufficient and which therefore necessitates the invocation of this deep result.

3.
Open problems

3.1.
The supersaturation result

Frankl, Graham and Rödl
[
FGR88
]
establish that for any
r
-colouring of
[N]
, a linear equation
\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{i}x_{i}=0
satisfying Rado’s criterion has
\gg_{r}N^{s-1}
monochromatic solutions. Our methods do not yield the analogous supersaturation result for equation (
1.2
). We instead find that if
N
is sufficiently large in terms of
M
then
[N]
contains a homogeneous arithmetic progression of length
M
which possesses at least
\gg_{r}M^{s-k}
monochromatic solutions to (
1.2
). This deficiency is an artefact of our method where, to avoid tackling certain local issues, we iteratively pass to a well-chosen homogeneous subprogression.

It may be possible to establish a supersaturation result if one is prepared to replace the homogeneous arithmetic progressions appearing in this paper with quadratic Bohr sets. Informally, let us call a set
quadratic Bohr homogeneous
if it has large intersection with all quadratic Bohr sets (centred at zero). Then our methods reduce to showing that if
A
is a dense subset of a quadratic Bohr set and if
B
is quadratic Bohr homogeneous, then there are many solutions to the equation

x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}=y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+y_{3}^{2}

with
x_{i}\in A
and
y_{i}\in B
. A promising strategy for obtaining such a result proceeds by decomposing
1_{A}
according to a variant of the arithmetic regularity lemma developed by Green and Tao
[
GT10b
]
. It is in fact this strategy which informs the simpler approach developed in this paper.

3.2.
Quantitative bounds

Define the
Rado number
(see
[
GRS90
, p.103]
) of the equation (
1.2
) to be the smallest positive integer
R_{\mathbf{c},k}(r)
such that any
r
-colouring of the interval
\left\{1,2,\dots,R_{\mathbf{c},k}(r)\right\}
results in at least one monochromatic tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})
satisfying (
1.2
) with all
x_{i}
distinct. For linear equations, this quantity has been extensively studied by Cwalina and Schoen
[
CS17
]
, with near optimal bounds extracted for certain choices of coefficients. In
[
BP17
]
it is shown that when
k=2
,
c_{1}+\dots+c_{s}=0
and
s\geqslant 5
then there exists a constant
C_{\mathbf{c}}
such that

R_{\mathbf{c},2}(r)\leqslant\exp\exp\exp(C_{\mathbf{c}}r).

(3.1)

It is feasible that the methods of this paper lead to quantitative bounds for the Rado number of the equation (
1.2
) provided that there exist coefficients with
c_{i}=-c_{j}
. In this situation, all of the results we employ in our argument can be proved using Fourier-analytic methods, where the quantitative machinery is well-developed. However, these bounds are sure to be of worse quality than (
3.1
) due to our induction on the number of colours, a feature of the argument not present in
[
BP17
]
.

If there are no coefficients satisfying
c_{i}=-c_{j}
, then any hope of extracting quantitative bounds on
R_{\mathbf{c},k}(r)
is diminished, since the methods of this paper invoke the multidimensional (polynomial) Szemerédi theorem, a result for which there are no quantitative bounds presently known. It would be interesting if one could avoid calling on such a deep result.

3.3.
Systems of equations

Rado
[
Rad33
]
characterised when
systems
of linear equations are partition regular. This criterion says that a system
Ax=0
is partition regular if and only if the integer matrix
A
satisfies the so-called
columns condition
(see
[
GRS90
, p.73]
). We conjecture that the columns condition is sufficient for systems of equations in
k
th powers, provided that the number of variables is sufficiently large in terms of the degree and the number of equations, and that the matrix of coefficients is sufficiently generic. For instance, in analogy with results of Cook
[
Coo71
]
we posit the following.

Conjecture 3.1
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s},b_{1},\ldots,b_{s}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}
. Then the system of equations

\displaystyle a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\dots+a_{s}x_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle b_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\dots+b_{s}x_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle=0

is non-trivially partition regular, provided that

(i)

s\geqslant 9
;

(ii)

the matrix
A:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&\dots&a_{s}\\
b_{1}&\dots&b_{s}\end{pmatrix}
satisfies the columns condition;

(iii)

for any real numbers
\lambda,\mu
that are not both zero, the vector
(\lambda,\mu)A
has at least five non-zero entries, not all of which have the same sign.

Condition (ii) is certainly necessary for partition regularity, by Rado’s criterion. Weakening conditions (i) and (iii) would presumably require improvements in circle method technology.

3.4.
Roth with logarithmically-smooth common difference

Using the arguments of §
9
one can prove the following (see Remark
9.3
).

Theorem 3.2
.

If
A\subset[N]
lacks a three-term arithmetic progression with
R
-smooth common difference, where
10\leqslant R\leqslant N
, then

|A|\ll N\frac{(\log\log R)^{4}}{\log R}.

(3.2)

When
R=\log^{K}N
for some fixed absolute constant
K
, the set of
R
-smooth numbers in
[N]
has cardinality
N^{1-K^{-1}+o(1)}
. Common differences arising from such a set are therefore polynomially sparse, and Theorem
3.2
results in a density bound of the form
(\log\log N)^{-1+o(1)}
.

The argument for Theorem
3.2
really only uses the fact that the
R
-smooths contain the interval
[R]
, and that
A
must be dense on a translate of this set, so we are in fact locating a ‘short’ arithmetic progression. Since smooth arithmetic progressions are much more abundant than short arithmetic progressions, it would be interesting if one could obtain a better density bound by exploiting this.

The only other bound known for Roth’s theorem with common difference arising from a polynomially sparse arithmetic set can be found in
[
Pre17b
]
, which deals with perfect
k
th powers. This also results in a double logarithmic bound, of the form
(\log\log N)^{-c_{k}}
for some small
c_{k}>0
. Breaking the double logarithmic barrier for the smooth Roth problem may be a tractable intermediate step towards improving bounds in the polynomial Roth theorem.

Part I
The generalised Pythagorean equation

In this part we establish partition regularity of the 5-variable Pythagorean equation
x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}=x_{5}^{2}
. The proof contains all of the essential ideas required for Theorem
1.3
but is more transparent, avoiding notational complexities and the need for smooth number technology. Unlike the general case, we show that all requisite steps can be established using Fourier analysis, avoiding recourse to deeper results involving higher-order uniformity and the multidimensional Szemerédi theorem. This may be of use to those interested in quantitative bounds and supersaturation.

Throughout this part we assume familiarity with the high-level schematic outlined in §
2
.

4.
Induction on colours

We first derive Theorem
2.1
from Theorem
2.3
by induction on the number of colours. We deduce Theorem
2.3
from Theorem
2.4
in §§
5
–
6
, and prove Theorem
2.4
in §§
7
–
8
.

4.1.
The inductive base: one colour

Definition 4.1

(
T
counting operator)
.

Given functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{s}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
with finite support, define the counting operator

\displaystyle T(f_{1},\dots,f_{5}):=\sum_{x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}=x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}+x_{5}^{2}}f_{1}(x_{1})f_{2}(x_{2})f_{3}(x_{3})f_{4}(x_{4})f_{5}(x_{5}).

We write
T(f)
for
T(f,f,\dots,f)
.

By Theorem
B.1
, there exist
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{1}>0
such that for
N\geqslant N_{1}
we have

T(1_{[N]})\geqslant c_{1}N^{3}.

Since the latter quantity is positive, Theorem
2.1
follows for 1-colourings (the base case of our induction).

4.2.
The inductive step

Let
[N]=C_{1}\cup\dots\cup C_{r}
be an
r
-colouring.
We split our proof into two cases depending on the homogeneity of the
C_{i}
.

4.2.1.
The inhomogeneous case

Let
M:=N_{0}(r-1)
be the quantity whose existence is guaranteed by our inductive hypothesis. We first suppose that some
C_{i}
is not
M
-homogeneous in
[N]
(see Definition
2.2
). Consequently there exists
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\begin{split}q\cdot[M]\subset[N]\quad\text{and}\quad C_{i}\cap q\cdot[M]=\emptyset.\end{split}

(4.1)

For
j\neq i
let us define

C_{j}^{\prime}:=\left\{x\in[M]:qx\in C_{j}\right\}.

Then it follows from (
4.1
) that
\bigcup_{j\neq i}C_{j}^{\prime}=[M]
. By the induction hypothesis, there exist
y_{k}\in C_{j}^{\prime}
for some
j\neq i
such that
y_{1}^{2}-y_{2}^{2}=y_{3}^{2}+y_{4}^{2}+y_{5}^{2}
. Setting
x_{k}:=qy_{k}
we obtain elements of
C_{j}
which solve the generalised Pythagorean equation.

4.2.2.
The homogeneous case

In this case every colour class is
M
-homogeneous in
[N]
. We claim that Theorem
2.3
then implies that each
C_{i}
contains a solution to the generalised Pythagorean equation. First we observe that each colour class is dense.

Lemma 4.2

(Homogeneous sets are dense)
.

If
B\subset[N]
is
M
-homogeneous in
[N]
then

|B|\geqslant\frac{1}{M}\left\lfloor\frac{N}{M}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

We proceed by a variant of Varnavides averaging
[
Var59
]
. For each
q\leqslant N/M
the definition of homogeneity gives

B\cap q\cdot[M]\neq\emptyset.

Summing over
q
then yields

\sum_{q\leqslant N/M}|B\cap q\cdot[M]|\geqslant\left\lfloor N/M\right\rfloor.

Interchanging the order of summation, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in B}\#\left\{(q,m)\in[N/M]\times[M]:x=qm\right\}\geqslant\left\lfloor N/M\right\rfloor.

The result follows on noting that

\#\left\{(q,m)\in[N/M]\times[M]:x=qm\right\}\leqslant M.

∎

Setting
A=B=C_{i}
in Theorem
2.3
we deduce that if
N\geqslant N_{0}(M)
then

T(1_{C_{r}})\geqslant c_{0}(M)N^{s-k}.

Since the latter quantity is positive the induction step follows, completing the proof of Theorem
2.1
. Note that a quantity dependent on
M=N_{0}(r-1)
is ultimately dependent only on
r
.

5.
A pseudorandom Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem

In §
4
we reduced partition regularity of the generalised Pythagorean equation (
1.1
) to Theorem
2.3
. In §
6
we deduce the latter result from Theorem
2.4
. To prepare the ground for this deduction, we first modify Theorem
2.4
to accommodate sets which are relatively dense in a suitably pseudorandom set. The goal is to find the weakest possible pseudorandomness conditions required for such a result to hold. Our primary quantity of interest is the following.

Definition 5.1

(
T_{1}
counting operator)
.

Given functions
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
with finite support and
B\subset\mathbb{Z}
, define

\displaystyle T_{1}(f_{1},f_{2};B):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}-x_{2}=y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+y_{3}^{2}\end{subarray}}f_{1}(x_{1})f_{2}(x_{2})1_{B}(y_{1})1_{B}(y_{2})1_{B}(y_{3}).

We write
T_{1}(f;B)
for
T_{1}(f,f;B)
and
T_{1}(A;B)
for
T_{1}(1_{A};B)
.

We begin by showing how Theorem
2.4
implies a result in which the indicator function
1_{A}
can be replaced by a function
f:[N]\to[0,1]
with sufficiently large average.

Lemma 5.2

(Functional Sárközy)
.

For any
\delta>0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{0}>0
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
the following holds. Let
f:[N]\to[0,1]
with
\left\|f\right\|_{1}\geqslant\delta N
and let
B
be an
M
-homogeneous subset of the positive integers. Then

\begin{split}T_{1}(f;B)\geqslant c_{0}N^{\frac{5}{2}}.\end{split}

Proof.

Let
A=\{x\in[N]:f(x)\geqslant{\delta}/2\}
. As
\|f\|_{1}\geqslant{\delta}N
and
f\leqslant 1
, we have
|A|\geqslant{\delta}N/2
. Since
f\geqslant{\delta}1_{A}/2
, we deduce that

T_{1}(f;B)\geqslant({\delta}/2)^{2}T_{1}(A;B),

and an application of Theorem
2.4
completes the proof.
∎

Our next step is to weaken the assumptions of Theorem
2.4
even further, replacing bounded functions with unbounded functions which are sufficiently pseudorandom. The pseudorandomness we enforce posits the existence of a ‘random-like’ majorising function
\nu
, whose properties are given in the following two definitions.

Definition 5.3

(Fourier decay)
.

We say that
\nu:[N]\to[0,\infty)
has
Fourier decay of level
\theta
(with respect to
1_{[N]}
) if

\bigg\|\frac{\hat{\nu}}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}}-\frac{\hat{1}_{[N]}}{\left\|1_{[N]}\right\|_{1}}\biggr\|_{\infty}\leqslant\theta.

Definition 5.4

(
p
-restriction)
.

We say that
\nu:[N]\to[0,\infty)
satisfies a
p
-restriction estimate with constant
K
if

\sup_{|\phi|\leqslant\nu}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\hat{\phi}(\alpha)\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}\alpha\leqslant K\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}^{p}N^{-1}.

Theorem 5.5

(Pseudorandom Sárközy)
.

For any
\delta>0
and
K,M\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
N_{0},c_{0},\theta>0
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
the following holds. Let
B
be an
M
-homogeneous set of positive integers. Let
\nu:[N]\to[0,\infty)
satisfy a
4.995
-restriction estimate with constant
K
, and have Fourier decay of level
\theta
. Then for any
f:[N]\to[0,\infty)
with
f\leqslant\nu
and
\left\|f\right\|_{1}\geqslant\delta\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}
we have

\begin{split}T_{1}(f;B)\geqslant c_{0}\left\|\nu\right\|^{2}_{1}N^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

Proof.

Since
\nu
has Fourier decay of level
\theta
, we may apply the dense model lemma recorded in
[
Pre17a
, Theorem 5.1]
, rescaling as appropriate, to conclude the existence of
g:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
0\leqslant g\leqslant 1_{[N]}
and

\bigg\|\frac{\hat{f}}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}}-\frac{\hat{g}}{N}\biggr\|_{\infty}\ll\log(\theta^{-1})^{-3/2}.

(5.1)

Provided that
\theta\leqslant\exp(-C\delta^{-1})
with
C
a large positive constant, we can compare Fourier coefficients at 0 to deduce that
\left\|g\right\|_{1}\gg\delta N
. Applying Lemma
5.2
then gives

T_{1}(g;B)\gg_{\delta,M}N^{\frac{5}{2}}.

(5.2)

Let
h
denote the indicator function of the set
\{x^{2}:x\in B\cap[\sqrt{N}]\}
. Then for functions
h_{1},h_{2}:[N]\to\mathbb{C}
we have

T_{1}(h_{1},h_{2};B)=\sum_{x_{1}-x_{2}=x_{3}+x_{4}+x_{5}}h_{1}(x_{1})h_{2}(x_{2})h(x_{3})h(x_{4})h(x_{5}).

(5.3)

The function
h
is majorised by the indicator function of the set

\{x^{2}:x\in[\sqrt{N}]\}

which, by Lemma
B.3
, satisfies a
4.995
-restriction estimate with constant
O(1)
.

The function
g
is majorised by
1_{[N]}
, which satisfies a 4.995-restriction estimate with constant
O(1)
. Employing the generalised von Neumann lemma (Lemma
C.3
), together with (
5.1
) and (
5.3
), we deduce that

\left|\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}^{-2}T_{1}(f;B)-N^{-2}T_{1}(g;B)\right|\ll KN^{1/2}\log(\theta^{-1})^{-3/400}.

Combining this with (
5.2
) and choosing
\theta\leqslant{\theta}_{0}({\delta},M,K)
completes the proof.
∎

6.
The
W
-trick for squares: a simplified treatment

In this section we deduce our non-linear density result (Theorem
2.3
) from its pseudorandom analogue (Theorem
5.5
). The heart of the matter is massaging the set of squares to appear suitably pseudorandom. This is accomplished using a version of the
W
-trick for squares, simplified from that developed in Browning–Prendiville
[
BP17
]
.

It is useful to have a non-linear version of the operator
T_{1}
introduced in §
5
.

Definition 6.1

(
T_{2}
counting operator)
.

Given functions
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
with finite support and
B\subset\mathbb{Z}
, define

\displaystyle T_{2}(f_{1},f_{2};B):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}=y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+y_{3}^{2}\end{subarray}}f_{1}(x_{1})f_{2}(x_{2})1_{B}(y_{1})1_{B}(y_{2})1_{B}(y_{3}).

We write
T_{2}(f;B)
for
T_{2}(f,f;B)
and
T_{2}(A;B)
for
T_{2}(1_{A};B)
.

Assuming the notation and premises of Theorem
2.3
, our objective is to obtain a lower bound for
T_{2}(A;B)
by relating it to an estimate for
T_{1}(f;B)
, where
f
is a function bounded above by a pseudorandom majorant
\nu
, as in Theorem
5.5
.

Let

W=2\prod_{p\leqslant w}p^{2},

(6.1)

where
w=w(\delta,M)
is a constant to be determined, and the product is over primes. By Lemma
A.4
, applied with
S=[N]
, there exists a
w
-smooth positive integer
\zeta\ll_{\delta,w}1
, and
\xi\in[W]
with
(\xi,W)=1
, such that

|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\zeta(\xi+Wx)\in A\}|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\delta|\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\zeta(\xi+Wx)\in[N]\}|.

Set

A_{1}:=\{\tfrac{1}{2}Wx^{2}+\xi x:\zeta(\xi+Wx)\in A\}\setminus\left\{0\right\}

and, noting that
(2W)^{1/2}
is a positive integer, set

B_{1}:=\bigl\{y\in{\mathbb{N}}:\zeta(2W)^{1/2}y\in B\bigr\}.

One may check that
B_{1}
is
M
-homogeneous, and that there exists an absolute constant
C
such that if
N\geqslant C(\delta\zeta W)^{-1}
then

|A_{1}|\geqslant\frac{\delta N}{8\zeta W}.

(6.2)

By the binomial theorem

T_{2}(A;B)\geqslant T_{1}(A_{1};B_{1}).

(6.3)

We note that although the squares are not equidistributed in arithmetic progressions with small modulus, the same cannot be said of the set

\left\{\tfrac{1}{2}Wx^{2}+\xi x:x\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

(6.4)

This is the reason for our passage from
A
to
A_{1}
; the latter is a subset of the more pseudorandom set (
6.4
). Unfortunately, the (truncated) Fourier transform of (
6.4
) still does not behave sufficiently like that of an interval: they decay differently around the zero frequency, reflecting the growing gaps between consecutive elements of (
6.4
). To compensate for this, we must work with a weighted indicator function of
A_{1}
that counteracts this increasing sparsity.

We first observe that
A_{1}
is contained in the interval
[X]
, where

X:=\frac{1}{2W}\cdot\left(\frac{N}{\zeta}\right)^{2}.

Define a weight function
\nu:[X]\to[0,\infty)
by

\nu(n)=\begin{cases}Wx+\xi,&\text{if }n=\tfrac{1}{2}Wx^{2}+\xi x\text{ for some }x\in[N/\zeta]\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(6.5)

Since the results we are about to invoke are independent of the normalisation of
\nu
, we note that we could replace the weight
Wx+\xi
in the above definition by
x
, or even by
\sqrt{n}
. We have chosen to incorporate the more complicated weight in order to make calculations a little cleaner. The weight
\nu(\cdot)
has average value 1, since

\sum_{n\in[X]}\nu(n)=\sum_{x\leqslant\frac{N}{\zeta W}}Wx+O(N/\zeta)=X+O\left(W^{1/2}X^{1/2}\right).

(6.6)

Lemma 6.2

(Density transfer)
.

For
N
large in terms of
w
and
{\delta}
we have

\sum_{n\in A_{1}}\nu(n)\geqslant\tfrac{\delta^{2}}{256}\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}.

Proof.

For
N
sufficiently large in terms of
\delta
and
w
the estimate (

7.
Multidimensional homogeneous Furstenberg–Sárközy

It remains to establish Theorem
2.4
. In §
8
we derive this supersaturated counting result from a multidimensional ‘existence’ result, Theorem
2.5
, whose proof is the aim of this section. One can prove Theorem
2.5
succinctly using the multidimensional polynomial Szemerédi theorem of Bergelson–Leibman
[
BL96
]
, see Corollary
9.1
for such an argument. One may regard such an approach as overkill, and of little utility if one is interested in quantitative bounds. In this section we opt for a more circuitous approach which demonstrates how Fourier analysis suffices for Theorem
2.5
. More precisely, we adapt the Fourier-analytic density increment strategy originating with Roth
[
Rot53
]
and Sárközy
[
Sár78
]
, and show how it may accommodate the presence of homogeneous sets. The structure of our argument is based on Green
[
Gre02
]
.

Lemma 7.1

(Density increment lemma)
.

Let
B_{i}
be
M
-homogeneous sets of positive integers, and let
A\subset[N]^{d}
have size at least
\delta N^{d}
. Suppose that
A\times A
lacks
(x,x^{\prime})
satisfying

x-x^{\prime}=(y_{1}^{2},\dots,y_{d}^{2})

(7.1)

with
(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in B_{1}\times\dots\times B_{d}
. Then either

N\leqslant C_{d}(\delta^{-1}M^{d})^{C},

(7.2)

or there exist

(i)

M
-homogeneous sets
B_{i}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{N}
;

(ii)

a positive integer
N_{1}\geqslant N^{c}
, where
c>0
is an absolute constant;

(iii)

a multidimensional set
A_{1}\subset[N_{1}]^{d}
such that

(ia)

A_{1}\times A_{1}
lacks tuples satisfying (
7.1
) with
(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in B_{1}^{\prime}\times\dots\times B_{d}^{\prime}
;

(ib)

|A_{1}|\geqslant(\delta+c_{d}(\delta M^{-d})^{6})N_{1}^{d}
.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
given Lemma
7.1
.

Let us assume that
A\subset[N]^{d}
has size at least
\delta N^{d}
and lacks solutions to (
7.1
) with
y_{i}\in B_{i}
, where the
B_{i}
are
M
-homogeneous sets. Setting
A_{0}:=A
, we iteratively apply Lemma
7.1
to obtain a sequence of sets
A_{0},A_{1},A_{2},\dots
, each contained in an ambient grid
[N_{n}]^{d}
with

N_{n}\geqslant N^{c^{n}},\qquad|A_{n}|\geqslant\left(\delta+nc_{d}(\delta M^{-d})^{6}\right)N_{n}^{d}.

If this iteration continues until
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
d,\delta,M
, we obtain a density exceeding 1, which would be impossible. Hence for some
n\ll_{d,\delta,M}1
the inequality (
7.2
) is satisfied with
N_{n}
in place of
N
therein. Therefore

N\leqslant N_{n}^{C^{n}}\leqslant\left(C_{d}(\delta^{-1}M^{d})^{C}\right)^{C^{n}}\ll_{d,\delta,M}1.

∎

We henceforth proceed with the proof of Lemma
7.1
. Put

B:=B_{1}\times\dots\times B_{d},

let
A\subset[N]^{d}
with
|A|=\delta N^{d}
, and suppose
A\times A
lacks tuples
(x,x^{\prime})
satisfying (
7.1
) with
(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in B
.

For
f,g:[N]^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
, define the counting operator

T_{B}(f,g):=\sum_{x-x^{\prime}=(y_{1}^{2},\dots,y_{d}^{2})}f(x)g(x^{\prime})1_{B}(y_{1},\dots,y_{d}).

Write
T_{B}(f)
for
T_{B}(f,f)
. With this notation, our assumption is that

T_{B}(1_{A})=0.

Let
f=1_{A}-\delta 1_{[N]^{d}}
denote the
balanced function
of
A
in
[N]^{d}
. Then by bilinearity

\begin{split}T_{B}(1_{A})=T_{B}(1_{A},f)+T_{B}(f,\delta 1_{[N]^{d}})+\delta^{2}T_{B}(1_{[N]^{d}}).\end{split}

Hence there exists
g:[N]\to[0,1]
with
\left\|g\right\|_{2}\leqslant\sqrt{\delta N}
and such that

|T_{B}(g,f)|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^{2}T_{B}(1_{[N]^{d}})\quad\text{or}\quad|T_{B}(f,g)|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^{2}T_{B}(1_{[N]^{d}}).

(7.3)

Since the balanced function
f
has average value
0
, one can regard (
7.3
) as exhibiting the fact that
f
displays some form of non-uniformity. In order to demonstrate this formally we require the following lemmas.

Lemma 7.2

(Homogeneous counting lemma)
.

Let
B=B_{1}\times\dotsm\times B_{d}
be a product of
M
-homogeneous sets. Then for
N\geqslant 64M^{2}
we have

T_{B}(1_{[N]^{d}})\geqslant\left(\frac{N^{\frac{3}{2}}}{8M^{2}}\right)^{d}.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result for
d=1
, since

T_{B}(1_{[N]^{d}})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}T_{B_{i}}(1_{[N]}).

If
y\in\left[\sqrt{N/2}\right]
then
y^{2}\in[N/2]
, so for
y
in this interval we have

\begin{split}\sum_{x-x^{\prime}=y^{2}}1_{[N]}(x)1_{[N]}(x^{\prime})=\#\left\{y^{2}+x:1\leqslant x\leqslant N-y^{2}\right\}\geqslant\frac{N}{2}.\end{split}

Summing over
y
lying in the intersection of this interval with a homogeneous set
B
, we apply Lemma
4.2
to deduce that

\begin{split}T_{B}(1_{[N]})&\geqslant\sum_{y\in B\cap[\sqrt{N/2}]}\sum_{x-x^{\prime}=y^{2}}1_{[N]}(x)1_{[N]}(x^{\prime})\\
&\geqslant\frac{1}{M}\bigg\lfloor\frac{\lfloor\sqrt{N/2}\rfloor}{M}\bigg\rfloor\frac{N}{2}.\end{split}

The result follows provided that
N
is sufficiently large.
∎

Lemma 7.3

(Generalised von Neumann theorem)
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2}:[N]^{d}\to[-1,1]
. Then for
i=1,2
we have

|T_{B}(f_{1},f_{2})|\ll_{d}N^{3d/2}\left(\frac{\big\|\hat{f_{i}}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}}{N^{d}}\right)^{1/3}.

Proof.

We prove the result for
i=1
, the other case being similar. For
{\alpha}=({\alpha}_{1},\ldots,{\alpha}_{d})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, define

S_{B}(\alpha):=\sum_{y\in B\cap[\sqrt{N}]^{d}}e(\alpha_{1}y_{1}^{2}+\dots+\alpha_{d}y_{d}^{2}).

By orthogonality and Hölder’s inequality, we have

\begin{split}|T_{B}(f_{1},f_{2})|&=\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}S_{B}(\alpha)\hat{f}_{1}(-\alpha)\hat{f}_{2}(\alpha)\mathrm{d}\alpha\right|\\
&\leqslant\left\|S_{B}\right\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\big\|\hat{f}_{1}\big\|^{1/3}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\big\|\hat{f}_{1}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2/3}\big\|\hat{f}_{2}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.\end{split}

The result now follows on incorporating Parseval’s identity

\big\|\hat{f}_{i}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=\big\|f_{i}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leqslant N^{d/2}

together with the estimate

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|S_{B}(\alpha)|^{6}\mathrm{d}\alpha\ll_{d}N^{2d}.

The latter mean value estimate follows from orthogonality and Theorem
B.1
.
∎

When taken in conjunction with (
7.3
), Lemmas

and

imply that for
N\geqslant 64M^{2}
there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
for which

\begin{split}\bigl|\hat{f}(\alpha)\bigr|\gg_{d}\left(\delta M^{-d}\right)^{6}N^{d}.\end{split}

(7.4)

Lemma 7.4

(Fragmentation into level sets)
.

If
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
Q\geqslant 1
and
P\in{\mathbb{N}}
then there exist positive integers
q_{i}\leqslant Q
and a partition of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
into sets
R
of the form

\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(a_{i}+q^{2}_{i}\cdot(-P,P]\right)

(7.5)

such that for any
g:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to[-1,1]
with finite
L^{1}
norm we have the estimate

\begin{split}\left|\hat{g}(\alpha)\right|\leqslant\sum_{R}\Bigl|\sum_{x\in R}g(x)\Bigr|+O_{d}\left(\left\|g\right\|_{1}PQ^{-1/3}\right).\end{split}

(7.6)

Proof.

By a weak form of a result of Heilbronn
[
Hei48
]
, there are
q_{1},\dots,q_{d}\leqslant Q
such that

\begin{split}\left\|\alpha_{i}q_{i}^{2}\right\|\ll Q^{-1/3}\qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant d).\end{split}

(7.7)

We partition
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
into congruence classes of the form

\prod_{i}\left(a_{i}+q_{i}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{Z}\right),

then partition each copy of
\mathbb{Z}
appearing in this product into a union of intervals of the form
2nP+(-P,P]
with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. This yields a partition of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
into sets
R
of the form (
7.5
).

If
x,x^{\prime}
lie in the same

then
x-x^{\prime}=(q_{1}^{2}y_{1},\dots,q_{d}^{2}y_{d})
for some

, and so

|e(\alpha\cdot x)-e(\alpha\cdot x^{\prime})|\ll\sum_{i=1}^{d}P\left\|q_{i}^{2}\alpha_{i}\right\|\ll_{d}PQ^{-1/3}.

It then follows from the triangle inequality that

\begin{split}\left|\hat{g}(\alpha)\right|&\leqslant\sum_{R}\biggl|\sum_{x\in R}g(x)e(\alpha\cdot x)\biggr|\\
&=\sum_{R}\biggl|\sum_{x\in R}g(x)\biggr|+O_{d}\Bigl(\sum_{R}\sum_{x\in R}|g(x)|PQ^{-1/3}\Bigr).\end{split}

∎

Let us take
P:=\left\lfloor N^{1/9}\right\rfloor
and
Q:=N^{3/8}
. Then, provided that (

) fails to hold, we have

PQ^{-1/3}\leqslant c_{d}(\delta M^{-d})^{6},\qquad PQ^{2}N^{-1}\leqslant c_{d}(\delta M^{-d})^{6}.

(7.8)

With these bounds in hand, we claim that we may apply Lemma
7.4
to (
7.4
) and conclude that there exists a set
R
contained in
[N]^{d}
and of the form (
7.5
) for which

\sum_{x\in R}f(x)\gg_{d}(\delta M^{-d})^{6}|R|.

(7.9)

Let us presently set about showing this.

The first bound in (

), together with (

), implies that

\sum_{R}\biggl|\sum_{x\in R}f(x)\biggr|\gg_{d}(\delta M^{-d})^{6}N^{d}.

By definition, the balanced function has average value

, so adding this quantity to either side of the inequality gives

\sum_{R}\max\Bigl\{0,\sum_{x\in R}f(x)\Bigr\}\gg_{d}(\delta M^{-d})^{6}N^{d}.

Inspection of the proof of Lemma

(7.10)

Similarly, the number of

contained in

Therefore

The second inequality in (

By (

. An application of the pigeonhole principle finally confirms (

The estimate (

8.
Varnavides averaging for supersaturation

We complete the proof of Theorem
2.1
by deducing the counting result, Theorem
2.4
, from the multidimensional existence result, Theorem
2.5
. The deduction proceeds by collecting a single configuration from many subprogressions, then establishing that these configurations don’t coincide too often. This random sampling argument originates with Varnavides
[
Var59
]
.

Proposition 8.1

(Varnavides argument)
.

For any
\delta>0
and
d,M\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{0}>0
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
the following holds.
If
A\subset[N]^{d}
is at least
\delta
-dense in
[N]^{d}
and
B_{1},\dots,B_{d}
are
M
-homogeneous sets of natural numbers, then there are at least
c_{0}N^{\frac{3d}{2}}
tuples
(x,x^{\prime},y)\in A^{2}\times B_{1}\times\dots\times B_{d}
satisfying (
2.3
).

Proof.

For
q,n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
write
q^{\otimes 2}\otimes n
for the tuple
(q_{1}^{2}n_{1},\dots,q_{d}^{2}n_{d})
and write
q^{\otimes 2}\otimes[N]^{d}
for the set

\left\{q^{\otimes 2}\otimes n:n\in[N]^{d}\right\}.

Let
N_{0}=N_{0}(\delta/2^{1+d},d,M)
be the quantity given by Theorem
2.5
. Suppose that
N\geqslant N_{0}
and write
Q:=\left\lfloor\sqrt{N/N_{0}}\right\rfloor
. Averaging, we have

\begin{split}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{q\in[Q]^{d}}|A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes 2}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)|\geqslant\delta(NQN_{0})^{d}.\end{split}

By the definition of
Q
, there are at most
(2N)^{d}
choices for
z
for which there exists
q\in[Q]^{d}
such that

|A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes 2}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)|\neq 0.

Hence there are at least
\tfrac{1}{2}\delta(NQ)^{d}
choices for
(z,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
for which

\begin{split}|A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes 2}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)|\geqslant 2^{-1-d}\delta N_{0}^{d}.\end{split}

(8.1)

Call each such choice of
(z,q)
a
good
tuple. Define

A_{z,q}:=\left\{y\in[N_{0}]^{d}:z+q^{\otimes 2}\otimes y\in A\right\}.

If
(z,q)
is good then
|A_{z,q}|\geqslant 2^{-1-d}\delta N_{0}^{d}
. Applying Theorem
2.5
we see that there exist
x,x^{\prime}\in A_{z,q}
satisfying (
2.3
) with the
y_{i}
restricted to the
M
-homogeneous sets

\left\{y_{i}:q_{i}y_{i}\in B_{i}\right\}.

Translating and dilating, we deduce that each set
A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes 2}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right))
satisfying (
8.1
) contains a solution to (
2.3
) with
y_{i}\in B_{i}
.

For fixed
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
define
R(x,y)
to be the quantity

\#\left\{(z,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times[Q]^{d}:\left\{x,\ x+y^{\otimes 2}\right\}\subset\left(z+q^{\otimes 2}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)\right\}.

Claim
.

R(x,y)\leqslant N_{0}^{2d}.

To see this, observe that if
\left\{x,\ x+y^{\otimes 2}\right\}\subset z+q^{\otimes 2}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}
then for each
i
there exists
m_{i}\in[N_{0}]
such that

y_{i}^{2}=q_{i}^{2}m_{i}.

As there are at most
N_{0}
choices for
m_{i}
for fixed
y_{i}
, there are at most
N_{0}^{d}
choices for
q
.
Once one has fixed this choice of
q
we have

z\in x-q^{\otimes 2}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d},

so there are at most
N_{0}^{d}
choices of
z
for fixed
x
. This establishes the claim.

Invoking the claim gives

N_{0}^{2d}\#\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times B_{1}\times\dotsm\times B_{d}:\left\{x,\ x+y^{\otimes 2}\right\}\subset A\right\}\\
\geqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},y\in B_{1}\times\dotsm B_{d}\\
x,\ x+y^{\otimes 2}\in A\end{subarray}}R(x,y).

Next we interchange the order of summation to find that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},y\in B_{1}\times\dotsm B_{d}\\
x,\ x+y^{\otimes 2}\in A\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle R(x,y)

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{q\in[Q]^{d}}\#\left\{(x,y):\left\{x,\ x+y^{\otimes 2}\right\}\subset A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes 2}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\#\left\{(z,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times[Q]^{d}:(z,q)\text{ is good}\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\delta(NQ)^{d}.

It follows that

\#\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times B_{1}\times\dotsm\times B_{d}:\left\{x,\ x+y^{\otimes 2}\right\}\subset A\right\}\\
\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\delta N_{0}^{-2d}N^{d}\left\lfloor\sqrt{N/N_{0}}\right\rfloor^{d}.

The result follows since
N_{0}\ll_{\delta,d,M}1
.
∎

Proof that Proposition
8.1
implies Theorem
2.4
.

We prove a more general result for sums of
d
squares. First note that, by translation, Proposition
8.1
remains valid for dense subsets of
[-N,N]^{d}
.
Given
A\subset[N]
of density at least
\delta
, define

A^{\prime}:=\left\{x\in[-N,N]^{d}:x_{1}+\dots+x_{d}\in A\right\}

For every element
n
of
[-N,N]
there are at least
N
pairs
(n_{1},n_{2})\in[-N,N]^{2}
such that
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
. An induction then shows that for each
n\in[-N,N]
we have

\#\left\{(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})\in[-N,N]^{d}:n=n_{1}+\dots+n_{d}\right\}\geqslant N^{d-1}.

Consequently

|A^{\prime}|\geqslant\delta N^{d}\gg_{d}\delta|[-N,N]^{d}|.

Applying Proposition
8.1
with
B_{i}:=B
for all
i
, we deduce that there are at least
c_{0}N^{3d/2}
tuples
(x,y)\in A^{\prime}\times B^{d}
such that
x+(y_{1}^{2},\dots,y_{d}^{2})\in A^{\prime}
.
For each such tuple the sum
x=x_{1}+\dots+x_{d}
is an element of the one-dimensional set
A
, as is
x+y_{1}^{2}+\dots+y_{d}^{2}
. As each element of
A
has at most
(2N+1)^{d-1}
representations of the form
x_{1}+\dots+x_{d}
, it follows that the number of solutions to

x-x^{\prime}=y_{1}^{2}+\dots+y_{d}^{2}

is at least

c_{0}N^{\frac{3d}{2}-(d-1)}=c_{0}N^{1+\frac{d}{2}},

as required.
∎

Part II
Rado’s criterion over squares and higher powers

In this part we prove Theorem
1.3
. Let
\eta=\eta_{k}>0
be a fixed constant, where
\eta_{2}=1
, and
\eta_{k}
is sufficiently small when

9.
The smooth homogeneous Bergelson–Leibman theorem

We begin our investigation of Rado’s criterion in
k
th powers by generalising Theorem
2.4
, which asserts that dense multidimensional sets contain configurations of the form

(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}),\quad(x_{1}+y_{1}^{2},\dots,x_{d}+y_{d}^{2})

with the
y_{i}
lying in a homogeneous set. We require a version of this result which concerns affine configurations determined by
k
th powers, similar in flavour to the following special case of the multidimensional polynomial Szemerédi theorem of Bergelson–Leibman
[
BL96
]
.

Bergelson–Leibman
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta>0
and let
F\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a finite set. There exists
N_{0}=N_{0}(k,\delta,F)
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
, if
A\subset[N]^{d}
has size
|A|\geqslant\delta N^{d}
then there exists
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
y\in\mathbb{N}
such that

x+y^{k}\cdot F\subset A.

We require a version of this result in which the
k
th power comes from a homogeneous set. Fortunately, this strengthening can be deduced from the original. It is convenient to set up the following notation.

Notation
.

Given
q,y,k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
define

q\otimes y:=(q_{1}y_{1},\dots,q_{d}y_{d}),\qquad y^{\otimes k}:=(y_{1}^{k_{1}},\dots,y_{d}^{k_{d}}).

For
F\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, write
q\otimes F
for the set

\left\{q\otimes y:y\in F\right\}.

Here is our version of the Bergelson–Leibman theorem with common difference arising from a homogeneous set.

Corollary 9.1

(Homogeneous Bergelson–Leibman)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta>0
and let
F\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a finite set. There exists
N_{0}
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
, if
A\subset[N]^{d}
has size
|A|\geqslant\delta N^{d}
and
B_{1},\dots,B_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}
are
M
-homogeneous, then there exists
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
y_{1}\in B_{1},\dots,y_{d}\in B_{d}
such that

x+y^{\otimes k}\otimes F\subset A.

(9.1)

Proof.

Let
K:=\prod_{i}k_{i}
and consider the finite set

F^{\prime}:=[M^{K}]^{d}\otimes F.

By the Bergelson–Leibman theorem, provided that
N\gg_{M,K,F,\delta}1
, there exist
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that

x+t^{K}\cdot F^{\prime}\subset A.

The result follows if the progression
t^{K}\cdot[M^{K}]
contains an element of the form
y_{i}^{k_{i}}
for some
y_{i}\in B_{i}
.

Let
z_{i}:=t^{K/k_{i}}
. Then

\left\{z_{i}^{k_{i}},(2z_{i})^{k_{i}},\dots,(Mz_{i})^{k_{i}}\right\}=t^{K}\cdot\left\{1^{k_{i}},2^{k_{i}},\dots,M^{k_{i}}\right\}\subset t^{K}\cdot[M^{K}].

Since each
B_{i}
is
M
-homogeneous, it intersects the set
z_{i}\cdot[M]
.
∎

Next we require a counting analogue of this result. In fact, we need to count the number of configurations arising from a smooth common difference.
Before stating the theorem, we remind the reader of what it means for a set to be
M
-homogeneous in the
N^{\eta}
-smooths (see Definitions
1.6
and
2.2
).

Theorem 9.2

(Varnavides averaging)
.

Let
k_{1},\dots,k_{d},M\in\mathbb{N}
,
\eta,\delta\in(0,1]
, and let
F\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a finite set. There exist
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{0}>0
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
, if
A\subset[N]^{d}
has
|A|\geqslant\delta N^{d}
and
B\subset\mathbb{N}
is
M
-homogeneous in the
N^{\eta}
-smooths, then the number of tuples
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times B^{d}
for which (
9.1
) holds is at least

c_{0}N^{d+\frac{1}{k_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{k_{d}}}.

Proof.

Increasing the size of
F
if necessary, we may assume that
F
contains two elements which differ in the
i
th coordinate for each
i\in[d]
. Let
N_{0}
be the quantity given by Corollary
9.1
with respect to the density
\delta/2^{d+1}
. Suppose that

N\geqslant N_{0}^{1/\eta},

(9.2)

and define the following sets of smooths:

S_{i}:=S\bigl(\bigl\lfloor\sqrt[k_{i}]{N/N_{0}}\bigr\rfloor;N^{\eta}\bigr).

Interchanging the order of summation, we have

\begin{split}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{q_{1}\in S_{1}}\dots\sum_{q_{d}\in S_{d}}|A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes k}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)|\geqslant\delta|S_{1}|\dotsm|S_{d}|(NN_{0})^{d}.\end{split}

Notice that there are at most
(2N)^{d}
choices for
z
for which there exists
q\in S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{d}
such that

|A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes k}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)|\neq 0.

Hence there are at least
\tfrac{1}{2}\delta N^{d}|S_{1}|\dotsm|S_{d}|
choices for
(z,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\prod_{i}S_{i}
for which

\begin{split}|A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes k}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)|\geqslant 2^{-d-1}\delta N_{0}^{d}.\end{split}

(9.3)

Call such a choice of
(z,q)
a
good
tuple.

Claim 1.
For each good tuple
(z,q)
the set
A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes k}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)
contains a configuration of the form
x+y^{\otimes k}\otimes F
for some
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and some
y\in B^{d}
.

To see this, define

A_{z,q}:=\left\{x\in[N_{0}]^{d}:z+q^{\otimes k}\otimes x\in A\right\}.

Then
|A_{z,q}|\geqslant 2^{-d-1}\delta N_{0}^{d}
. Let

B_{i}=\left\{y_{i}\in[N_{0}]:q_{i}y_{i}\in B\right\}\cup(N_{0},\infty).

Using the fact that
B
is
N^{\eta}
-smoothly
M
-homogeneous, together with (
9.2
), one can check that each
B_{i}
is
M
-homogeneous (not just smoothly homogeneous).
Invoking Corollary
9.1
, we see that there exist
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
y\in B_{1}\times\dots\times B_{d}
such that

x+y^{\otimes k}\otimes F\subset A_{z,q}.

Translating and dilating, we deduce that
A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes k}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)
contains a configuration of the form
x^{\prime}+(q\otimes y)^{\otimes k}\otimes F
. By definition of the
B_{i}
and the fact that
F
is non-constant in each coordinate, we see that
y\in[N_{0}]^{d}
and thus each coordinate of
q\otimes y
lies in
B
. This establishes Claim 1.

For fixed
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
let
G(x,y)
denote the number of tuples
(z,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
satisfying

x+y^{\otimes k}\otimes F\subset z+q^{\otimes k}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}.

(9.4)

Define

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times B^{d}:x+y^{\otimes k}\otimes F\subset A\right\}.

(9.5)

Then interchanging the order of summation shows that the sum
\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}}G(x,y)
is at least

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{q_{1}\in S_{1}}\dots\sum_{q_{d}\in S_{d}}\Bigl|\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times B^{d}:x+y^{\otimes k}\otimes F\subset A\cap\left(z+q^{\otimes k}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}\right)\right\}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\geqslant\Bigl|\Bigl\{(z,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\prod_{i}S_{i}:(z,q)\text{ is good}\Bigr\}\Bigr|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\delta N^{d}|S_{1}|\dotsm|S_{d}|.

Applying Lemma
A.2
(for
N
sufficiently large) we deduce that

\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}}G(x,y)\gg_{k,\delta,\eta,N_{0}}\ N^{d+\frac{1}{k_{1}}+\dots+\frac{1}{k_{d}}}.

Since the theorem asserts a lower bound on the size of
\mathcal{A}
, the result is proved provided we have the following upper bound on
G(x,y)
.

Claim 2.
Suppose that
F
contains two elements which differ in the
i
th coordinate for each
i\in[d]
. Then
G(x,y)\leqslant N_{0}^{2d}
.

To see this, first note that if
x+y^{\otimes k}\otimes F\subset z+q^{\otimes k}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d}
then, since
F
contains two elements differing in their
i
th coordinate, there exist integers
f_{i}<f_{i}^{\prime}
such that

x_{i}+y_{i}^{k_{i}}f_{i},\ x_{i}+y_{i}^{k_{i}}f_{i}^{\prime}\in z_{i}+q_{i}^{k_{i}}\cdot[N_{0}].

Subtracting these elements, we deduce that there exists
n_{i}\in[N_{0}]
for which

q_{i}^{k_{i}}=\frac{y_{i}^{k_{i}}(f_{i}^{\prime}-f_{i})}{n_{i}}.

As there are at most
N_{0}
choices for
n_{i}
, and
y_{i}
is fixed, there are at most
N_{0}^{d}
choices for
q
.
Once one has fixed this choice of
q
, for any
f\in F
we have

z\in x+y^{\otimes k}\otimes f-q^{\otimes k}\otimes[N_{0}]^{d},

so there are at most
N_{0}^{d}
choices for
z
. In summary
G(x,y)\leqslant N_{0}^{2d}
, which establishes Claim 2.
∎

Remark 9.3

(Roth’s theorem with logarithmically-smooth common difference)
.

The above argument remains valid for much stronger levels of smoothness. For instance, one can use it to establish that if
A\subset[N]
lacks a three-term progression with common difference equal to an
R
-smooth number then

|A|\ll\frac{r_{3}(R)}{R}N.

(9.6)

Here
r_{3}(N)
denotes the size of a largest subset of
[N]
lacking a non-trivial three-term arithmetic progression.

10.
A supersaturated generalisation of both Roth and Sárközy’s theorems

In this section we deduce a one-dimensional counting result analogous to Theorem
2.4
by projecting down the multidimensional Theorem
9.2
. Again we remind the reader of what it means to be
M
-homogeneous in
S(N^{1/k};N^{\eta})
(see Definition
2.2
).

Theorem 10.1

(Supersaturated smooth homogeneous Roth–Sárközy)
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{t}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
with
\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{s}=0
. For any
\eta,\delta>0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{0}>0
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
the following holds.
If
A
is at least
\delta
-dense in
[N]
and
B
is
M
-homogeneous in
S(N^{1/k};N^{\eta})
, then there are at least
c_{0}N^{s+\frac{t}{k}-1}
tuples
(x,y)\in A^{s}\times B^{t}
solving the equation

\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{s}x_{s}=\mu_{1}y_{1}^{k}+\dots+\mu_{t}y_{t}^{k}.

(10.1)

Notice that Theorem
10.1
is a common generalisation of both the Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem (take
s=2
and
t=1
) and Roth’s theorem (take
\lambda=(1,-2,1)
and
t=0
).

Proof.

Given
A\subset[N]
of density at least
\delta
, let us define

\tilde{A}:=\Bigl\{x\in[N]^{s+t-2}:\sum_{i}x_{i}\in A\Bigr\}

A stars and bars argument shows that for
n\in[N]
we have

\#\left\{(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})\in[N]^{d}:n=n_{1}+\dots+n_{d}\right\}=\binom{n-1}{d-1}.

Since there are at most
\tfrac{1}{2}|A|
elements
x
of
A
satisfying the inequality
x\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}|A|
, it follows that for
N\geqslant C_{s,t}\delta^{-1}
we have

|\tilde{A}|=\sum_{n\in A}\binom{n-1}{s+t-3}\gg_{s,t}\delta^{s+t-2}N^{s+t-2}.

(10.2)

In the statement of Theorem
10.1
, at least one of the coefficients
\lambda_{i}
must be positive. Relabelling indices, we may assume that
\lambda_{s}>0
. For a technical reason, it will be useful in a later part of the argument if we can ensure that

\tilde{A}-\tilde{A}\subset\left[-\tfrac{N}{\lambda_{s}},\tfrac{N}{\lambda_{s}}\right]^{s+t-2}.

(10.3)

This follows on partitioning the hypercube
[N]^{s+t-2}
into subhypercubes of sufficiently small side length and applying the pigeonhole principle to ensure that
\tilde{A}
has large density on one such part (worsening the density (
10.2
) by a factor of
O_{s,t,\lambda_{s}}(1)
in the process).

Define
F\subset\mathbb{Z}^{s+t-2}
to be the set consisting of the zero vector together with the rows of the following matrix

\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda_{s}&\ &\ &\ &\ &\ &\ \\
\ &-\lambda_{s}&\ &\ &\ &\ &\ \\
\ &\ &\ddots&\ &\ &\ &\ \\
\ &\ &\ &-\lambda_{s}&\ &\ &\ \\
\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{2}&\dots&\lambda_{s-2}&\lambda_{s}^{k-1}\mu_{1}&\dots&\lambda_{s}^{k-1}\mu_{t}\end{pmatrix}.

(10.4)

Consider the set

\tilde{B}:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{N}:\lambda_{s}y\in B\right\}\cup(N^{1/k}\lambda_{s}^{-1},\infty).

Provided that
N^{\eta}\geqslant\max\left\{\lambda_{s},M\right\}
(as we may assume), we see that
\tilde{B}
is
M
-homogeneous in the
N^{\eta}
-smooths.
Applying Theorem
9.2
, we find that there are at least
c_{0}N^{s+t-2+s-2+\frac{t}{k}}

tuples
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{Z}^{s+t-2}\times\tilde{B}^{s-2}\times\tilde{B}^{t}
such that
\tilde{A}
contains the configuration

x+(y_{1},\dots,y_{s-2},z_{1}^{k},\dots,z_{t}^{k})\otimes F.

By (
10.3
) and (
10.4
) we have
\lambda_{s}^{k}\mu_{i}z_{i}^{k}\in[-N,N]
, hence by definition of
\tilde{B}
we deduce that
\lambda_{s}z_{i}\in B
.
Projecting down to one dimension and taking into account the multiplicities of representations, we obtain
\gg N^{s+\frac{t}{k}-1}
tuples
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}^{s-2}\times\mathbb{N}^{t}
with
\lambda_{s}z_{i}\in B
and such that
A
contains the configuration

\begin{split}x,\quad x-\lambda_{s}y_{1},\quad\dots,\quad x-\lambda_{s}y_{s-2},\quad x+\sum_{i=1}^{s-2}\lambda_{i}y_{i}+\lambda_{s}^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mu_{j}z_{j}^{k}.\end{split}

Let us set
x_{i}:=x-\lambda_{s}y_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,s-2
, along with
x_{s-1}=x
and

x_{s}:=x+\sum_{i=1}^{s-2}\lambda_{i}y_{i}+\lambda_{s}^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mu_{j}z_{j}^{k}.

One can then check that the tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{s},\lambda_{s}z_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s}z_{t})
is an element of
A^{s}\times B^{t}
satisfying
(
10.1
). By construction there are
\gg N^{s+\frac{t}{k}-1}
such tuples.
∎

11.
Pseudorandom Roth–Sárközy

In this section we develop a pseudorandom variant of Theorem
10.1
. As in Part
I
, we begin by relaxing Theorem
10.1
to encompass general bounded functions. In order to count solutions to our equation weighted by general functions, we use the following notation.

Definition 11.1

(
T_{\ell}
counting operator)
.

Fix
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{t}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
with
\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{s}=0
. Given functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{s}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
and
B\subset\mathbb{Z}
, write (when defined)

\displaystyle T_{\ell}(f_{1},\dots,f_{s};B):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1}x_{1}^{\ell}+\dots+\lambda_{s}x_{s}^{\ell}=\\
\mu_{1}y_{1}^{k}+\dots+\mu_{t}y_{t}^{k}\end{subarray}}f_{1}(x_{1})\dotsm f_{s}(x_{s})1_{B}(y_{1})\dotsm 1_{B}(y_{t}).

We write
T_{\ell}(f;B)
for
T_{\ell}(f,f,\dots,f;B)
and
T_{\ell}(A;B)
for
T_{\ell}(1_{A};B)
.

Remark 11.2

(Dependence on constants)
.

In the sequel we regard the coefficients
\lambda_{i}
and
\mu_{j}
as fixed, and suppress their dependence in any implied constants. Similarly for the degree
k
and the number of variables
s+t
. We also fix
\eta=\eta_{k}
globally: recall that this is 1 if
k=2
, and a small positive constant if
k\geqslant 3
. We opt to keep any dependence on the following explicit: the level of homogeneity
M
, and the density
\delta
.

Lemma 11.3

(Functional Roth–Sárközy)
.

For any
\delta>0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{0}>0
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
the following holds. Let
f:[N]\to[0,1]
with
\left\|f\right\|_{1}\geqslant\delta N
, and let
B
be
M
-homogeneous in
S(N^{1/k};N^{\eta})
. Then

\begin{split}T_{1}(f;B)\geqslant c_{0}N^{s+\frac{t}{k}-1}.\end{split}

Proof.

Let
A=\{x\in[N]:f(x)\geqslant{\delta}/2\}
. As
\|f\|_{1}\geqslant{\delta}N
, we must necessarily have
|A|\geqslant{\delta}N/2
. Since
f\geqslant{\delta}1_{A}/2
, we deduce that

T_{1}(f;B)\geqslant({\delta}/2)^{s}T_{1}(A;B),

and an application of Theorem
10.1
completes the proof.
∎

Our next step is to weaken the assumptions of Theorem
10.1
even further, replacing bounded functions with unbounded functions which are sufficiently pseudorandom, in that they possess a majorant with good Fourier decay (Definition
5.3
) and
p
-restriction (Definition
5.4
).

Theorem 11.4

(Pseudorandom Roth–Sárközy)
.

There exists
s_{0}(k)
such that for
s+t\geqslant s_{0}(k)
,
\delta>0
and
K,M\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{0},\theta>0
such that for
N\geqslant N_{0}
the following holds.

•

Let
\nu:[N]\to[0,\infty)
satisfy a
(s+t-10^{-8})
-restriction estimate with constant
K
, and have Fourier decay of level
\theta
;

•

let
B
be
M
-homogeneous in
S(N^{1/k};N^{\eta})
;

•

let
f:[N]\to[0,\infty)
with
f\leqslant\nu
and
\left\|f\right\|_{1}\geqslant\delta\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}
.

Then

\begin{split}T_{1}(f;B)\geqslant c_{0}\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}^{s}N^{\frac{t}{k}-1}.\end{split}

(11.1)

Moreover, we may take
s_{0}(2)=5
,
s_{0}(3)=8
, and
s_{0}(k)
satisfying (
1.3
).

Proof.

By replacing
B
with
B\cap S(N^{1/k};N^{\eta})
, we may freely suppose that
B\subset S(N^{1/k};N^{\eta})
. Deploying the dense model lemma
[
Pre17a
, Theorem 5.1]
, there exists
g:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
0\leqslant g\leqslant 1_{[N]}
and

\bigg\|\frac{\hat{f}}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}}-\frac{\hat{g}}{N}\biggr\|_{\infty}\ll\log(\theta^{-1})^{-3/2}.

(11.2)

Provided that
\theta\leqslant\exp(-C\delta^{-1})
with
C
a large positive constant, we can compare Fourier coefficients at 0 to deduce that
\left\|g\right\|_{1}\gg\delta N
. Lemma
11.3
then gives

T_{1}(g;B)\gg_{\delta,M}N^{s+\frac{t}{k}-1}.

(11.3)

Let
h
denote the indicator function of the set
\{x^{k}:x\in B\}
. Then for functions
h_{1},\dots,h_{s}:[N]\to[0,\infty)
we have

T_{1}(h_{1},\dots,h_{s};B)=\sum_{\lambda\cdot x=\mu\cdot y}h_{1}(x_{1})\dotsm h_{s}(x_{s})h(y_{1})\dotsm h(y_{t}).

(11.4)

The function
h
is majorised by the indicator function of the set

\{x^{k}:x\in S(N^{1/k};N^{\eta})\}

which, by Lemma
B.3
, satisfies an
(s+t-10^{-8})
-restriction estimate with constant
O_{\eta}(1)
.

Observe that
g
is majorised by
1_{[N]}
, which also satisfies an
(s+t-10^{-8})
-restriction estimate with constant
O(1)
. The generalised von Neumann theorem (Lemma
C.3
), together with (
11.2
) and (
11.4
), yields

\displaystyle\Biggl|\frac{T_{1}(f;B)}{\|\nu\|_{1}^{s}}-\frac{T_{1}(g;B)}{N^{s}}\Biggr|

\displaystyle\ll\frac{K|S(N^{1/k};N^{\eta})|^{t}}{N}\log({\theta}^{-1})^{1.5\times 10^{-8}}

\displaystyle\leqslant KN^{\frac{t}{k}-1}\log({\theta}^{-1})^{1.5\times 10^{-8}}.

Pairing this with (
11.3
), and choosing
\theta\leqslant{\theta}_{0}({\delta},M,K)
, completes the proof.

∎

12.
The
W
-trick for smooth powers and a non-linear Roth–Sárközy theorem

Our objective in this section is to use Theorem
11.4
to deduce the following non-linear density result. Recall that
\eta=\eta_{k}
is 1 if
k=2
, and a small positive constant if
k\geqslant 3
.

Theorem 12.1

(Non-linear Roth–Sárközy)
.

There exists
s_{0}(k)
such that the following holds. Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{s},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{t}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
with
s+t\geqslant s_{0}(k)
and
\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{s}=0
. For any
\delta>0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{0}>0
such that for any
N\geqslant N_{0}
the following holds. Let
A
have density at least
\delta
in
S(N;N^{\eta})
and let
B
be
M
-homogeneous in
S(N;N^{\eta})
. Then

\begin{split}\#\Bigl\{(x,y)\in A^{s}\times B^{t}:\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}x_{i}^{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mu_{j}y_{j}^{k}\Bigr\}\geqslant c_{0}N^{s+t-k}.\end{split}

Moreover, we may take
s_{0}(2)=5
,
s_{0}(3)=8
, and
s_{0}(k)
satisfying (
1.3
).

This deduction proceeds by developing a
W
-trick for smooth
k
th powers, analogous to that developed for prime powers in
[
Cho17
]
. Let

W=k^{k-1}\prod_{p\leqslant w}p^{k},

(12.1)

where
w=w(\eta,\delta,M)
is a constant to be determined, and the product is over primes.
We apply Lemma
A.4
with
S=S(N;N^{\eta})
, using Lemma
A.2
in the process. This allows us to conclude that there exists a
w
-smooth positive integer
\zeta\ll_{\eta,\delta,w}1
and
\xi\in[W]
with
(\xi,W)=1
such that

\#\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\zeta(\xi+Wx)\in A\}\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\#\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\zeta(\xi+Wx)\in S(N;N^{\eta})\}.

(12.2)

Define

P:=\frac{N}{\zeta},\qquad X:=\frac{P^{k}}{kW}

(12.3)

and set

A_{1}:=\left\{\tfrac{(Wx+\xi)^{k}-\xi^{k}}{kW}:\zeta(Wx+\xi)\in A\text{ and }Wx+\xi\in S(P;P^{\eta})\right\}\setminus\left\{0\right\}.

(12.4)

Then
A_{1}\subset[X]
. Combining (
12.2
) and Lemma
A.5
, we have the lower bound

|A_{1}|\geqslant\tfrac{\delta}{2}\#\left\{x\in S(P;P^{\eta}):x\equiv\xi\bmod W\right\}-O_{\eta,\delta,w}\left(N(\log N)^{-1}\right).

(12.5)

Noting that
(kW)^{1/k}
is a positive integer, let

B_{1}:=\bigl\{y\in{\mathbb{N}}:\zeta(kW)^{1/k}y\in B\bigr\}.

(12.6)

Provided that
N\geqslant\max\left\{k,w,\zeta\right\}^{1/\eta}
, one may check that
B_{1}
is
M
-homogeneous in
S(X^{1/k};X^{\eta})
. Recalling that
\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_{i}=0
, we have

T_{k}(A;B)\geqslant T_{1}(A_{1};B_{1}).

Part III
Supersmooths and shifted squares

In this part we establish Rado’s criterion for a linear equation in logarithmically-smooth numbers (Theorem
1.7
). Furthermore, we show how a direct application of the transference principle yields a supersaturated version of this result, and analogously for a linear equation in the set of squares minus one (Theorem
1.4
). Both of these results are established without recourse to properties of homogeneous sets. This reflects the fact that supersmooths and shifted squares possess subsets which can be projectively transformed to obtain equidistribution in congruence classes to small moduli, ruling out possible local obstructions to partition regularity—obstructions which must be surmounted when working with perfect squares and higher powers. This phenomenon manifests itself when massaging the perfect powers to obtain equidistribution; this can be done, but requires an
affine
transformation, as opposed to a projective one. Unfortunately, a typical equation satisfying Rado’s criterion is only projectively invariant, so the methods of this part do not succeed in establishing partition regularity for equations in perfect powers.

14.
Modelling a pseudorandom partition with a colouring

As described above, the proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.7
proceed by first passing to a subset of the sparse arithmetic set of interest (supersmooths or shifted squares). We then projectively transform this subset to obtain a set which is well distributed in arithmetic progressions to small moduli. We can then define a weight
\nu:[N]\to\infty
supported on our equidistributed set which has nice pseudorandomness properties.

Given a finite colouring of our original arithmetic set, the above procedure induces a finite partition of our pseudorandom weight function into non-negative functions
f_{i}
, so that

\nu=\sum_{i}f_{i}.

Deducing supersaturation then amounts to showing that the count of solutions to our equation weighted by some
f_{i}
is within a constant factor of the maximum possible.

The main tool in deriving this lower bound is to model the
f_{i}
with functions
g_{i}
whose sum dominates the indicator function of the interval
1_{[N]}
. It is a short step to show that, in essence, we may assume that the
g_{i}
correspond to indicator functions of a colouring of
[N]
. For such colourings there is already a supersaturation result in the literature due to Frankl, Graham and Rödl
[
FGR88
, Theorem 1]
. Employing this theorem and then (quantitatively) retracing our steps yields Theorems
1.4
and
1.7
.

In this section we establish the modelling part of the above procedure: non-negative functions
f_{i}
with pseudorandom sum
\sum_{i}f_{i}
have approximants
g_{i}
whose sum dominates the constant function
1_{[N]}
. This ‘transference principle’
6
6

6

This is also referred to as a ‘dense model’ or ‘bounded approximation’ lemma in the literature.
for colourings is based on Green’s transference principle for dense sets
[

]
, as exposited in
[

]
. We recall the concepts of Fourier decay and

5.3

Proposition 14.1

(Modelling lemma)
.

Suppose that
\nu:[N]\to[0,\infty)
satisfies a
p
-restriction estimate with constant
K
, and has Fourier decay of level
1/M
with
M\geqslant M_{0}(p,K)

\bigg\|\frac{\hat{f}_{i}}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}}-\frac{\hat{g}_{i}}{N}\biggr\|_{\infty}\ll_{r,p,K}(\log M)^{-\frac{1}{p+2}}\qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant r).

Let
{\kappa},\varepsilon>0
be parameters, to be determined later. In proving this result we utilise the large spectrum of
f_{i}
, which we take as

S_{i}:=\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{T}:|\hat{f}_{i}(\alpha)|\geqslant{\kappa}\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}\right\}.

(14.1)

Define the Bohr set with frequencies

and width

Next define

g_{i}:=\frac{Nf_{i}*1_{B}*1_{B}}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}|B|^{2}}\qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant r-1),

where, for finitely supported

We first estimate

\widehat{f_{1}*f_{2}}=\hat{f}_{1}\hat{f}_{2}.

If

14.1
) of the large spectrum we have

\biggl|\frac{\hat{f}_{i}(\alpha)}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}}-\frac{\hat{g}_{i}(\alpha)}{N}\biggr|=\biggl|\frac{\hat{f}_{i}(\alpha)}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}}\biggr|\left|1-\tfrac{\hat{1}_{B}(\alpha)^{2}}{|B|^{2}}\right|\leqslant 2{\kappa}.

If

Hence

Combining both cases gives

\bigg\|\frac{\hat{f}_{i}}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}}-\frac{\hat{g}_{i}}{N}\biggr\|_{\infty}\ll\varepsilon+{\kappa}.

\sum_{\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}=0}g_{i}(x_{1})\dotsm g_{i}(x_{s})\gg_{r,{\mathbf{c}}}N^{s-1}.

Our assumption that
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
ensures that

. We may in fact assume that

is trivial. Hence

-restriction estimate with constant 1, and majorises each

. Employing the generalised von Neumann lemma (Lemma

), with

\frac{N}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}^{s}}\sum_{\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}=0}f_{i}(x_{1})\dotsm f_{i}(x_{s})\geqslant c_{0}(r,{\mathbf{c}})-O_{r,{\mathbf{c}},K}\left(\left(\log M\right)^{-\frac{1}{400s}}\right).

Assuming that
M\geqslant M_{0}(r,{\mathbf{c}},K)
completes the proof of Proposition

.

Define

17.
Supersaturation for logarithmically-smooth numbers

The proof of Theorem

follows in analogy with the argument of the prior section. The situation is somewhat simpler in this context, as there is no need to massage the set of smooths to exhibit sufficient pseudorandomness.

Define the indicator function

(
[

)

\begin{split}\sup_{|\phi|\leqslant\nu}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\hat{\phi}(\alpha)\right|^{2.995}\mathrm{d}\alpha\ll\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}^{2.995}N^{-1}.\end{split}

Lemma 17.2

(
[

, §5]
)
.

We have the Fourier decay estimate

\bigg\|\frac{\hat{\nu}}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}}-\frac{\hat{1}_{[N]}}{N}\biggr\|_{\infty}\ll\frac{\log\log N}{\log R}.

.

Appendix A
Results on smooth numbers

Definition A.1

(
R
-smooth numbers)
.

We say that a positive integer is
R
-smooth
if all of its prime divisors are at most
R
. We denote the set of such numbers in the interval
[N]
by

S(N;R):=\left\{n\in[N]:p\mid n\implies p\leqslant R\right\}.

The following estimate was proved by de Bruijn; see
[
Gran08
, Eq. (1.8)]
. Here
\rho:[0,\infty)\to(0,1]
denotes the Dickman–de Bruijn
\rho
-function. Note that
\rho
is decreasing and has bounded derivative.

Lemma A.2
.

We have

|S(N;N^{\eta})|=\rho(1/\eta)N+O_{\eta}\left(N/\log N\right).

In particular, there exists
N_{0}=N_{0}(\eta)
such that for
N\geqslant N_{0}
we have

|S(N;N^{\eta})|\gg_{\eta}N.

Lemma A.3
.

There are at most
10^{w}NM^{-1/2}
elements of
[N]
divisible by a
w
-smooth number greater than
M
.

Proof.

It follows from Rankin’s trick that the
number of integers in
[N]
divisible by a
w
-smooth number exceeding
M
is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>M\\
\text{$m$ is $w$-smooth}\end{subarray}}\frac{N}{m}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$m$ is $w$-smooth}\end{subarray}}\frac{N}{m}\left(\frac{m}{M}\right)^{1/2}=NM^{-1/2}\prod_{p\leqslant w}\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{1/2}-1}\right).

The result follows on noting that
1+\frac{1}{p^{1/2}-1}\leqslant 10
.
∎

Notice that if
W
is a
w
-smooth positive integer divisible by the primorial
\prod_{p\leqslant w}p
, then every positive integer can be written in the form
\zeta(\xi+Wy)
for a unique choice of a
w
-smooth positive integer
\zeta
and a unique
\xi\in[W]
with
(\xi,W)=1
.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
W
be a
w
-smooth positive integer divisible by the primorial
\prod_{p\leqslant w}p
. For any sets
A\subset S\subset[N]
with
|A|\geqslant\delta|S|
and
|S|\geqslant\eta N
, there exist a
w
-smooth number

\zeta\ll_{{\delta},\eta,w}1,

and
\xi\in[W]
with
(\xi,W)=1
, such that

\#\left\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\zeta(\xi+Wx)\in A\right\}\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\#\left\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\zeta(\xi+Wx)\in S\right\}.

Proof.

For
\zeta,\xi\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
T\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, write

T_{\zeta,\xi,W}:=\left\{x\in T:x=\zeta(\xi+Wy)\text{ for some }y\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

Let
M=4({\delta}\eta)^{-2}10^{2w}
, so that
N10^{w}M^{-1/2}=\frac{{\delta}}{2}\eta N\leqslant\frac{{\delta}}{2}|S|
. By the remarks preceding this lemma, together with Lemma
A.3
, we have

\displaystyle{\delta}|S|\leqslant|A|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\leqslant M\\
\zeta\text{ is $w$-smooth}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in[W]\\
\ (\xi,W)=1\end{subarray}}|A_{\zeta,\xi,W}|+N10^{w}M^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\leqslant M\\
\zeta\text{ is $w$-smooth}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in[W]\\
\ (\xi,W)=1\end{subarray}}|A_{\zeta,\xi,W}|+\frac{{\delta}}{2}|S|.

Therefore

\delta\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\leqslant M\\
\zeta\text{ is $w$-smooth}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in[W]\\
\ (\xi,W)=1\end{subarray}}|S_{\zeta,\xi,W}|\leqslant{\delta}|S|\leqslant 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\leqslant M\\
\zeta\text{ is $w$-smooth}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in[W]\\
\ (\xi,W)=1\end{subarray}}|A_{\zeta,\xi,W}|,

and the pigeonhole principle completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

For any
K\geqslant 1
we have

S(N;KN^{\eta})-S(N;N^{\eta})\ll_{K,\eta}\frac{N}{\log N}.

Proof.

By Lemma
A.2
, we have

\frac{S(N;KN^{\eta})-S(N;N^{\eta})}{N}=\rho\Bigl(\frac{\log N}{\eta\log N+\log K}\Bigr)-\rho(1/\eta)+O(1/\log N).

The estimate now follows from the mean value theorem, since
\rho^{\prime}
is bounded and

\frac{\log N}{\eta\log N+\log K}-\frac{1}{\eta}\ll\frac{1}{\log N}.

∎

Appendix B
The unrestricted count and mean values estimates

Recall that
\eta
is 1 if
k=2
and a small positive constant if
k\geqslant 3
. The following is a consequence of the current state of knowledge in Waring’s problem.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
with
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
for some non-empty subset
I
of
[s]
. Then, for
k\geqslant 2
, there exists
s_{0}(k)\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that if
s\geqslant s_{0}(k)
and
N\geqslant N_{0}
then

\#\Bigl\{{\mathbf{x}}\in S(N;N^{\eta})^{s}:\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{i}x_{i}^{k}=0\Bigr\}\asymp_{{\mathbf{c}},\eta,k}N^{s-k}.

Moreover, one can take
s_{0}(2)=5
,
s_{0}(3)=8
, and
s_{0}(k)
satisfying (
1.3
).

The
k=2
case was known to Hardy and Littlewood. In an influential paper, Kloosterman
[
Klo27
]
opens with a discussion of this, then adapts the Hardy–Littlewood method to address the quaternary problem. Details of a proof may be found in
[
Dav2005
, Ch. 8]
.

As we cannot find the precise statement that we require for
k\geqslant 3
in the literature, we outline a proof below. The conclusion largely follows from the earlier techniques of Vaughan and of Wooley
[
Vau89
,
VW91
,
Woo92
]
, but we find it convenient to also draw material from other sources. Indeed, the aforementioned articles on Waring’s problem involve a combination of smooth and full-range variables, so for our lower bound the results cannot be imported directly. Theorem
B.1
is an indefinite version of a special case of
[
DS16
, Theorem 2.4]
; the latter is more profound, as it tackles a more challenging smoothness regime. One approach would be simply to imitate the proof of that theorem, until needing to treat the local factors—this is approximately what we do below.

Proof.

Let
k\geqslant 3
, and let
\eta=\eta_{k}
be a small positive constant. By orthogonality, our count is

\int_{0}^{1}g_{1}({\alpha})\cdots g_{s}({\alpha}){\mathrm{d}}{\alpha},

where

g({\alpha})=\sum_{x\in S(N;N^{\eta})}e({\alpha}x^{k}),\qquad g_{i}({\alpha})=g(c_{i}{\alpha})\quad(1\leqslant i\leqslant s).

Let
A\geqslant A_{0}(k)
, and put
Q=(\log N)^{A}
. Now perform a Hardy–Littlewood dissection
[
Vau97
]
: define major arcs

{\mathfrak{M}}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant a<q\leqslant Q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}{\mathfrak{M}}(q,a),\qquad{\mathfrak{M}}(q,a)=\{{\alpha}\in[0,1]:|q{\alpha}-a|\leqslant QN^{-k}\}

and minor arcs
{\mathfrak{m}}=[0,1]\setminus{\mathfrak{M}}
. It follows from
[
DS16
, Lemma 8.6]
, by slightly adjusting the parameters therein to allow for constant multiples, that

\int_{\mathfrak{m}}|g_{i}({\alpha})|^{s}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}=c_{i}^{-1}\int_{c_{i}{\mathfrak{m}}}|g({\beta})|^{s}{\mathrm{d}}{\beta}\ll N^{s-k}Q^{-c},

for some
c=c(k)>0
. Therefore

\int_{0}^{1}g_{1}({\alpha})\cdots g_{s}({\alpha}){\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}=\int_{\mathfrak{M}}g_{1}({\alpha})\cdots g_{s}({\alpha}){\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}+o(N^{s-k}).

First we prune our major arcs down to a lower height. Set
Q_{1}=\sqrt{\log N}
. Let

{\mathfrak{N}}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant a<q\leqslant Q_{1}\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}{\mathfrak{N}}(q,a),\qquad{\mathfrak{N}}(q,a)=\{{\alpha}\in[0,1]:|q{\alpha}-a|\leqslant Q_{1}N^{-k}\},

and put
{\mathfrak{n}}=[0,1]\setminus{\mathfrak{N}}
. Let
{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{M}}(q,a)
with
0\leqslant a<q\leqslant Q
and
(a,q)=1
and, by Dirichlet’s approximation theorem
[
Vau97
, Lemma 2.1]
, choose relatively prime
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
b\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
r\leqslant 2Q
and
|rc_{s}{\alpha}-b|\leqslant(2Q)^{-1}
. The triangle inequality gives

\Bigl|\frac{a}{q}-\frac{b}{rc_{s}}\Bigr|\leqslant\frac{Q}{qN^{k}}+\frac{1}{2rc_{s}Q}<\frac{1}{qrc_{s}},

so
\frac{a}{q}=\frac{b}{rc_{s}}
. As
(a,q)=1
and
(rc_{s},b)\ll 1
, we have
q\asymp r
,
|rc_{s}{\alpha}-b|\asymp|q{\alpha}-a|
, and it now follows from
[
VW91
, Lemma 8.5]
that

g_{s}({\alpha})\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon}N(q+N^{k}|q{\alpha}-a|)^{-1/k}+N\exp(-c\sqrt{\log N})(1+N^{k}|{\alpha}-a/q|),

where
c=c(A,\eta)
is a small positive constant. In particular, if
{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{M}}\setminus{\mathfrak{N}}
then

g_{s}({\alpha})\ll NQ_{1}^{\varepsilon-1/k}.

Furthermore, the sharp mean value estimate
[
DS16
, Theorem 2.3]
implies

\int_{0}^{1}|g_{i}({\alpha})|^{s-0.1}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}\ll N^{s-0.1-k}\qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant s).

(B.1)

Using Hölder’s inequality, we now obtain

\int_{{\mathfrak{M}}\setminus{\mathfrak{N}}}|g_{1}({\alpha})\cdots g_{s}({\alpha})|{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}=o(N^{s-k}),

and so

\int_{0}^{1}g_{1}({\alpha})\cdots g_{s}({\alpha}){\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}=\int_{\mathfrak{N}}g_{1}({\alpha})\cdots g_{s}({\alpha}){\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}+o(N^{s-k}).

(B.2)

For
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, define

S(q,a)=\sum_{x\leqslant q}e_{q}(ax^{k}),\qquad w({\beta})=\sum_{N^{\eta k}<m\leqslant N^{k}}\frac{1}{k}m^{\frac{1}{k}-1}\rho\Bigl(\frac{\log m}{\eta k\log N}\Bigr)e({\beta}m)

and

W({\alpha},q,a)=q^{-1}S(q,a)w({\alpha}-a/q),

where as before
\rho
denotes the Dickman–de Bruijn
\rho
-function. Next, we apply
[
Vau89
, Lemma 5.4]
to
c_{i}{\alpha}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant s
and
{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{N}}(q,a)\subset{\mathfrak{N}}
, where
0\leqslant a<q\leqslant Q_{1}
and
|q{\alpha}-a|\leqslant Q_{1}N^{-k}
. With
c^{\prime}_{i}=c_{i}/(c_{i},q)
and
q_{i}=q/(c_{i},q)
, this gives

g_{i}({\alpha})=W(c_{i}{\alpha},q_{i},c^{\prime}_{i}a)+O((\log N)^{-1/2}),

and furthermore

W(c_{i}{\alpha},q,c_{i}a)=W(c_{i}{\alpha},q_{i},c_{i}^{\prime}a)\ll q^{-1/k}\min(N,|{\alpha}-a/q|^{-1/k}).

(B.3)

By (
B.1
) and (
B.2
), together with Hölder, we now have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}g_{1}({\alpha})\cdots g_{s}({\alpha}){\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{q\leqslant\sqrt{\log N}}q^{-s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leqslant q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\int_{|{\beta}|\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{\log N}}{qN^{k}}}\Bigl(\prod_{i\leqslant s}S(q,c_{i}a)w(c_{i}{\beta})\Bigr){\mathrm{d}}{\beta}+o(N^{s-k}).

The bound (
B.3
) enables us to extend the integral to
[-1/2,1/2]^{s}
and then the outer sum to infinity with
o(N^{s-k})
error, as is usual for a major arc analysis
[
Dav2005
,
Vau97
]
. We thus obtain

\int_{0}^{1}g_{1}({\alpha})\cdots g_{s}({\alpha}){\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}={\mathfrak{S}}J+o(N^{s-k}),

where

{\mathfrak{S}}=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leqslant q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}q^{-s}S(q,c_{1}a)\cdots S(q,c_{s}a)

and

J=\int_{[-1/2,1/2]^{s}}w(c_{1}{\beta})\cdots w(c_{s}{\beta}){\mathrm{d}}{\beta}.

As discussed in
[
Dav2005
, Ch. 8]
, the singular series is a product of
p
-adic densities,

{\mathfrak{S}}=\prod_{p}\chi_{p},

and is strictly positive if and only if
\chi_{p}>0
for all
p
. The positivity of the
p
-adic densities
\chi_{p}
follows from the assumption that
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
for some non-empty
I\subseteq[s]
: one takes a non-trivial solution in
\{0,1\}^{s}
, and this is a non-singular
p
-adic zero.

Our final task is to show that
J\asymp N^{s-k}
. By orthogonality

J=k^{-s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{m}}\in(N^{\eta k},N^{k}]^{s}\\
{\mathbf{c}}\cdot{\mathbf{m}}=0\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\leqslant s}m_{i}^{\frac{1}{k}-1}\rho\Bigl(\frac{\log m_{i}}{\eta k\log N}\Bigr).

With
c>0
small, we have the crude lower bound

J\gg N^{s(1-k)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{m}}\in(cN^{k},N^{k}]^{s}\\
{\mathbf{c}}\cdot{\mathbf{m}}=0\end{subarray}}1\gg N^{s(1-k)}(N^{k})^{s-1}=N^{s-k},

since the
c_{i}
are not all of the same sign. We also have the complementary upper bound

J\ll N^{s(1-k)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{m}}\in[1,N^{k}]^{s}\\
{\mathbf{c}}\cdot{\mathbf{m}}=0\end{subarray}}1\ll N^{s-k}.

∎

Remark B.2
.

By working harder, we could have obtained a main term
{\lambda}N^{s-k}
, for some positive constant
{\lambda}={\lambda}({\mathbf{c}})
, similarly to Drappeau—Shao
[

]
.

We also need the following bounded restriction inequalities.

Lemma B.3
.

Let

f:[N]\to\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|z|\leqslant 1\}.

If

then

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Bigl|\sum_{x\in S(N;N^{\eta})}f(x)e({\alpha}x^{2})\Bigr|^{p}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}\ll_{p}N^{p-2}.

For
k\geqslant 3
, there exists
s_{0}(k)
such that if
s\geqslant s_{0}(k)
then

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Bigl|\sum_{x\in S(N;N^{\eta})}f(x)e({\alpha}x^{k})\Bigr|^{s-10^{-8}}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}\ll N^{s-10^{-8}-k}.

Moreover, one may take
s_{0}(3)=8
, and
s_{0}(k)
satisfying (

).

Proof.

The quadratic statement is a direct consequence of
[
Bou89
, Eq. (4.1)]
. Assuming for the time being that
k\geqslant 4
, write
2t
for the smallest even integer greater than or equal to the integer

appearing in Theorem

. Note that modifying

), and so we will prove the statement for

By orthogonality, the triangle inequality and Theorem

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Bigl|\sum_{x\in S(N;N^{\eta})}f(x)e({\alpha}x^{k})\Bigr|^{2t}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\#\Bigl\{({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\in S(N;N^{\eta})^{t}\times S(N;N^{\eta})^{t}:\sum_{i\leqslant t}x_{i}^{k}=\sum_{i\leqslant t}y_{i}^{k}\Bigr\}\ll_{t,\eta}N^{2t-k}.

The trivial estimate
\Bigl|\sum_{x\in S(N;N^{\eta})}f(x)e({\alpha}x^{k})\Bigr|\leqslant N

.

For

[

]

\phi:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{C}}

for some

\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\hat{\phi}({\alpha})|^{8-10^{-8}}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}\ll N^{5-10^{-8}}.

In the present appendix, we let
{\delta}
denote a parameter in the range

and consider the large spectra

\mathcal{R}_{\delta}=\{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{T}}:|\hat{\phi}({\alpha})|>{\delta}N\}.

By the dyadic pigeonholing argument in
[

, §6]
, it suffices to prove that

\mathrm{meas}(\mathcal{R}_{\delta})\ll\frac{1}{{\delta}^{8-10^{-7}}N^{3}}.

By orthogonality, Wooley’s estimate
[

, Theorem 1.2]
implies that

\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\hat{\phi}({\alpha})|^{6}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}\ll N^{3.25-10^{-4}}.

Thus, we may assume without loss of generality that

N^{10^{-5}-\frac{1}{8}}<{\delta}<1.

(B.4)

Indeed, if
{\delta}\leqslant N^{10^{-5}-\frac{1}{8}}
then

\mathrm{meas}(\mathcal{R}_{\delta})\leqslant({\delta}N)^{-6}\int_{\mathcal{R}_{\delta}}|\hat{\phi}({\alpha})|^{6}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}\ll({\delta}N)^{-6}N^{3.25-10^{-4}}\leqslant\frac{1}{{\delta}^{8-10^{-7}}N^{3}}.

Let
{\theta}_{1},{\theta}_{2},\ldots,{\theta}_{R}
be
N^{-3}
-separated points in
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
. As
8-10^{-7}\geqslant 6.3
it suffices to show that

R\ll{\delta}^{-6.3}.

(B.5)

Let
\mu(n)=1
if
n=x^{3}
for some
x\in[N]
, and zero otherwise. For some
a_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|a_{n}|\leqslant 1
, we then have
\phi(n)=a_{n}\mu(n)
; this ‘throws away’ smoothness. With
{\gamma}=3.1
, the calculation in
[
BP17
, §6]
yields

{\delta}^{2{\gamma}}N^{\gamma}R^{2}\ll\sum_{r,r^{\prime}\leqslant R}|\hat{\mu}({\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r})|^{\gamma}.

(B.6)

Consider the value of
{\theta}={\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}
in the right-hand side of (
B.6
). Define a set of ‘minor arcs’

{\mathfrak{n}}=\{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{T}}:|\hat{\mu}({\alpha})|\leqslant N^{10^{-8}+3/4}\}.

In light of (
B.4
), the contribution from
{\theta}\in{\mathfrak{n}}
to the right-hand side of (
B.6
) is
o({\delta}^{2{\gamma}}N^{\gamma}R^{2})
, and so

{\delta}^{2{\gamma}}N^{\gamma}R^{2}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r,r^{\prime}\leqslant R:\\
{\theta}\notin{\mathfrak{n}}\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mu}({\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r})|^{\gamma}.

(B.7)

Next, suppose
{\theta}\in{\mathbb{T}}\setminus{\mathfrak{n}}
, and fix a small
\varepsilon>0
. By
[
Cho16
, Lemma 2.3]
, there exist relatively prime
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

q\leqslant N^{3/4},\qquad|q{\theta}-a|\leqslant N^{-9/4}

and

\hat{\mu}({\theta})\ll q^{\varepsilon-\frac{1}{3}}N(1+N^{3}|{\theta}-a/q|)^{-1/3}.

With
C
a large positive constant, put
Q=C+{\delta}^{-9}
. The contribution to the right-hand side of (
B.7
) from denominators
q>Q
is
O(R^{2}N^{\gamma}Q^{{\gamma}(\varepsilon-\frac{1}{3})})
, which is negligible compared to the left-hand side.

Hence

{\delta}^{2{\gamma}}R^{2}\ll\sum_{1\leqslant r,r^{\prime}\leqslant R}G({\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}),

(B.8)

where

G({\theta})=\sum_{q\leqslant Q}\sum_{a=0}^{q-1}\frac{q^{{\gamma}(\varepsilon-\frac{1}{3})}}{(1+N^{3}|\sin({\theta}-a/q)|)^{{\gamma}/3}}.

The inequality (
B.8
) is a cubic version of
[
Bou89
, Eq. (4.16)]
. As
{\gamma}(\varepsilon-\frac{1}{3})>1
, Bourgain’s argument carries through, and yields (
B.5
).
∎

Finally, we need an upper bound on the number of trivial solutions.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
, and let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{s}
be non-zero integers summing to zero. Then there exists
s_{0}(k)
such that if
s\geqslant s_{0}(k)
then

\#\Bigl\{{\mathbf{x}}\in S(N;N^{\eta})^{s}:\sum_{i=1}^{s}c_{i}x_{i}^{k}=0\text{ and }x_{i}=x_{j}\text{ for some }i\neq j\Bigr\}=o\left(N^{s-k}\right).

Moreover, one can take
s_{0}(2)=5
,
s_{0}(3)=8
, and
s_{0}(k)
satisfying (
1.3
).

Proof.

Let
s_{0}(k)
be as in Lemma
B.3
. By the union bound, it suffices to prove an estimate of the required shape for the number of solutions with
x_{s-1}=x_{s}
. In this case we are estimating

\#\Bigl\{x\in S(N;N^{\eta})^{s-1}:\sum_{i=1}^{s-2}c_{i}x_{i}^{k}+(c_{s-1}+c_{s})x_{s-1}^{k}=0\Bigr\}.

It may be that
c_{s-1}+c_{s}=0
, so we estimate the contribution from the
x_{s-1}
variable trivially. Using orthogonality and Hölder’s inequality, it therefore suffices to prove that

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Bigl|\sum_{x\in S(N;N^{\eta})}e(\alpha x^{k})\Bigr|^{s-2}\mathrm{d}\alpha=o(N^{s-1-k}).

(B.9)

Let
p=s_{0}(k)-10^{-8}
. When
s-2\geqslant p
, the estimate (
B.9
) follows from Lemma
B.3
and the trivial estimate
\Bigl|\sum_{x\in S(N;N^{\eta})}e(\alpha x^{k})\Bigr|\leqslant N
. When
s-2<p
, we apply Hölder’s inequality and Lemma
B.3
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Bigl|\sum_{x\in S(N;N^{\eta})}e(\alpha x^{k})\Bigr|^{s-2}\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Bigl|\sum_{x\in S(N;N^{\eta})}e(\alpha x^{k})\Bigr|^{p}\mathrm{d}\alpha\right)^{\frac{s-2}{p}}

\displaystyle\ll N^{s-2-\frac{k(s-2)}{p}}.

It remains to check that

s-2-\frac{k(s-2)}{p}<s-1-k,

or equivalently that

2+p(1-\tfrac{1}{k})<s.

Since
s>p
, this follows if
p/k\geqslant 2
, which we can certainly ensure without affecting the bound (
1.3
).
∎

Appendix C
A generalised von Neumann lemma

Recall the notion of
p
-restriction introduced in Definition
5.4
.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}:[N]\to[0,\infty)
. If both
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
satisfy a
p
-restriction estimate with constant
K
, then so does
\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
|\phi|\leqslant\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}
. Then
\phi=\psi\times\theta
, where
\psi:[N]\to[0,\infty)
satisfies
\psi\leqslant\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}
and
\theta:[N]\to\mathbb{C}
satisfies
|\theta|\leqslant 1
. Put
\psi_{1}:=\min\left\{\psi,\nu_{1}\right\}
and
\psi_{2}:=\psi-\psi_{1}
. On setting
\phi_{i}:=\psi_{i}\theta
, we have
\phi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
with
|\phi_{i}|\leqslant\nu_{i}
. Applying the triangle inequality and restriction estimates for each
\nu_{i}
gives

\displaystyle\big\|\hat{\phi}\big\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\big\|\hat{\phi}_{1}\big\|_{p}+\big\|\hat{\phi}_{2}\big\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant(K/N)^{1/p}\left(\left\|\nu_{1}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\nu_{2}\right\|_{1}\right).

Positivity gives that
\left\|\nu_{1}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\nu_{2}\right\|_{1}=\left\|\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}\right\|_{1}
, and the result then follows on taking
p
th powers.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and suppose that
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{s}:[N]\to[0,\infty)
each satisfy a
(s-\delta)
-restriction estimate with constant
K
. Then for any
|f_{i}|\leqslant\nu_{i}
we have

\Biggl|\sum_{\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}=0}\frac{f_{1}(x_{1})}{\left\|\nu_{1}\right\|_{1}}\dotsm\frac{f_{s}(x_{s})}{\left\|\nu_{s}\right\|_{1}}\Biggr|\leqslant\frac{K}{N}\min_{i}\bigg\|\frac{\hat{f}_{i}}{\left\|\nu_{i}\right\|_{1}}\biggr\|_{\infty}^{\delta}.

(C.1)

Proof.

We prove the upper bound with
i=1
, the remaining cases following by re-labelling indices. Let
p=s-\delta
. By orthogonality and Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl|\sum_{\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}=0}f_{1}(x_{1})\dotsm f_{s}(x_{s})\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\hat{f}_{1}(c_{1}\alpha)\dotsm\hat{f}_{s}(c_{s}\alpha)\mathrm{d}\alpha\Bigr|\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Bigl|\hat{f}_{1}(c_{1}\alpha)\dotsm\hat{f}_{s}(c_{s}\alpha)\Bigr|\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle\leqslant\big\|\hat{f}_{1}\big\|_{\infty}^{\delta}\big\|\hat{f}_{1}\big\|_{p}^{1-\delta}\big\|\hat{f}_{2}\big\|_{p}\dotsm\big\|\hat{f}_{s}\big\|_{p}.

Inequality (
C.1
) then follows from our
p
-restriction assumption.
∎

Lemma C.3

(Generalised von Neumann)
.

Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and suppose that
\nu_{i},\mu_{i}:[N]\to[0,\infty)
each satisfy a
(s-\delta)
-restriction estimate with constant
K
. Then for any
|f_{i}|\leqslant\nu_{i}
and
|g_{i}|\leqslant\mu_{i}
we have

\Biggl|\sum_{\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{x}=0}\left(\frac{f_{1}(x_{1})}{\left\|\nu_{1}\right\|_{1}}\dotsm\frac{f_{s}(x_{s})}{\left\|\nu_{s}\right\|_{1}}\ -\ \frac{g_{1}(x_{1})}{\left\|\mu_{1}\right\|_{1}}\dotsm\frac{g_{s}(x_{s})}{\left\|\mu_{s}\right\|_{1}}\right)\Biggr|\\
\leqslant\frac{sK}{N}\max_{i}\bigg\|\frac{\hat{f}_{i}}{\left\|\nu_{i}\right\|_{1}}-\frac{\hat{g}_{i}}{\left\|\mu_{i}\right\|_{1}}\biggr\|_{\infty}^{\delta}.

Proof.

Let
p=s-\delta
. By Lemma
C.1
, the weight

\frac{\nu_{i}}{\left\|\nu_{i}\right\|_{1}}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{\left\|\mu_{i}\right\|_{1}}

satisfies a
p
-restriction estimate with constant
K
and majorises the difference

\frac{f_{i}}{\left\|\nu_{i}\right\|_{1}}-\frac{g_{i}}{\left\|\mu_{i}\right\|_{1}}.

Observing that this weight has
L^{1}
norm equal to two, the lemma follows on applying the telescoping identity

a_{1}\dotsm a_{s}-b_{1}\dotsm b_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}(a_{i}-b_{i})\prod_{j<i}a_{j}\prod_{j>i}b_{j},

together with Lemma
C.2
.
∎

The primary objective of this section is to establish the Fourier decay estimates in Lemmas

a small positive number when

Our goal is to prove the inequality (

), using the Hardy–Littlewood circle method. More explicitly, we wish to show that if

then

\frac{\hat{\nu}({\alpha})}{\left\|\nu\right\|_{1}}=\frac{\hat{1}_{[X]}({\alpha})}{X}+O_{\eta}(w^{-1/k}).

(D.1)

We treat the

D.1.
Smooth Weyl sums

We first consider the case

, recalling that here we choose
\eta=\eta_{k}
sufficiently small. The idea is to consider a rational approximation
a/q
to
\alpha
; there will ultimately be four regimes to consider, according to the size of

. We begin with a variant of
[
Vau89
, Lemma 5.4]
, which is useful for low height major arcs. Let

S_{q,a}=\sum_{r{\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,q}}e\Bigl(\frac{a}{q}\cdot\frac{(Wr+\xi)^{k}-\xi^{k}}{kW}\Bigr),\qquad I({\beta})=\int_{0}^{X}e({\beta}z){\mathrm{d}}z.

Lemma D.1

(First level)
.

Suppose

and
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, with
q\leqslant R/W
and
\|q{\alpha}\|=|q{\alpha}-a|
. Then

\hat{\nu}({\alpha})=\rho(1/\eta)q^{-1}S_{q,a}I\Bigl({\alpha}-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)+O_{\eta}\Bigl(\frac{P^{k}}{\log P}(q+P^{k}\|q{\alpha}\|)\Bigr).

Proof.

The start of the proof of
[
Vau89
, Lemma 5.4]
yields

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in S(m;R)\\
x\equiv Wr+\xi{\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,W}q\end{subarray}}1=\frac{1}{Wq}\sum_{x\in S(m;R)}1+O\Bigl(\frac{P}{\log P}\Bigr),

valid for

and

. Therefore

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in S(m;R)\\
x\equiv\xi{\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,W}\end{subarray}}e\Bigl(\frac{a}{q}\cdot\frac{x^{k}-\xi^{k}}{kW}\Bigr)=\frac{S_{q,a}}{Wq}\sum_{x\in S(m;R)}1+O\Bigl(\frac{qP}{\log P}\Bigr).

In particular, if
{\alpha}(x)
equals
e\Bigl(\frac{a}{q}\cdot\frac{x^{k}-\xi^{k}}{kW}\Bigr)
when

is
R
-smooth and 0 otherwise, then

\sum_{x\leqslant m}\Bigl({\alpha}(x)-\frac{S_{q,a}}{Wq}\rho\Bigl(\frac{\log m}{\log R}\Bigr)\Bigr)\ll\frac{qP}{\log P}.

By partial summation and the boundedness of
\rho^{\prime}
, we also have

\sum_{x\leqslant m}\rho\Bigl(\frac{\log x}{\log R}\Bigr)=m\rho\Bigl(\frac{\log m}{\log R}\Bigr)+O\Bigl(\frac{P}{\log P}\Bigr)\qquad(1\leqslant m\leqslant P),

and so

\sum_{x\leqslant m}\Bigl({\alpha}(x)-\frac{S_{q,a}}{Wq}\rho\Bigl(\frac{\log x}{\log R}\Bigr)\Bigr)\ll\frac{qP}{\log P}.

Next, observe that with
{\beta}={\alpha}-a/q
we have
|{\beta}|=q^{-1}\|q{\alpha}\|
and

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}({\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in S(P;R)\\
x\equiv\xi{\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,W}\end{subarray}}e\Bigl(\frac{a}{q}\cdot\frac{x^{k}-\xi^{k}}{kW}\Bigr)\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{q,a}}{Wq}\sum_{x\leqslant P}\rho\Bigl(\frac{\log x}{\log R}\Bigr)\phi(x)+E,

(D.2)

where

\phi(x)=x^{k-1}e\Bigl({\beta}\frac{x^{k}-\xi^{k}}{kW}\Bigr)

E\ll\frac{qP}{\log P}(\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}([1,P])}+P\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}([1,P])})\ll\frac{qP^{k}}{\log P}(1+P^{k}|{\beta}|),

and with the boundedness of
\rho^{\prime}
it also implies that

\sum_{x\leqslant P}\rho\Bigl(\frac{\log x}{\log R}\Bigr)\phi(x)=\rho(1/\eta)\sum_{x\leqslant P}\phi(x)+O\Bigl(\frac{P^{k}}{\log P}\Bigr).

Meanwhile, Euler–Maclaurin summation
[
Vau97
, Eq. (4.8)]
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{x\leqslant P}\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{P}\phi(x){\mathrm{d}}x+O(P^{k-1}(1+P^{k}|{\beta}|))

\displaystyle=WI({\beta})+O(P^{k-1}(1+P^{k}|{\beta}|)).

Substituting these estimates into (
D.2
) concludes the proof.

∎

We supplement this by bounding

and

Lemma D.2
.

If

then
S_{1,0}=1
,

S_{q,a}=0\qquad(2\leqslant q\leqslant w)

Plainly

and we note that

(

). Write

, where

is
w
-smooth and
(v,W)=1
. Since

, a standard calculation reveals that

S_{q,a}=S_{u,a_{1}}S_{v,a_{2}},

(D.5)

where

and

Put

, where

\displaystyle S(u,a_{1})

Lemma D.3
.

We have

(Minor arcs)

Lemma D.6

E.8
) from denominators
q>Q_{1}:=C+{\delta}^{-3k}
is therefore bounded, up to a constant, by

R^{2}X^{\gamma}(Q_{1}^{-{\gamma}/k}+W^{\gamma}(\log X)^{-{\gamma}/2})

which, by (
E.5
), is negligible compared to the left-hand side of (
E.8
). Therefore

{\delta}^{2{\gamma}}R^{2}\ll\sum_{1\leqslant r,r^{\prime}\leqslant R}G({\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}),

(E.9)

where

G({\alpha})=\sum_{q\leqslant Q_{1}}\sum_{a=0}^{q-1}\frac{q^{-{\gamma}/k}}{(1+X|\sin({\alpha}-\frac{a}{q})|)^{\gamma}}.

The inequality (
E.9
) is very similar to
[
Bou89
, Eq. (4.16)]
, but with
N^{2}
replaced by
X
, and with
Q_{1}\sim{\delta}^{-3k}
rather than
Q_{1}\sim{\delta}^{-5}
. The exponents differ but, since
{\gamma}>k
, Bourgain’s argument carries through, and we obtain (
E.6
) in the case
k\geqslant 4
.

Now suppose
k=2
. Consider
{\theta}={\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}
in the summand on the right-hand side of (
E.7
). By Lemma
D.10
, the contribution from
{\theta}\in{\mathfrak{m}}_{2}
is

O(R^{2}(X^{1-\frac{\tau}{2}+\tau^{2}})^{\gamma}),

and by (
E.5
) this is
o({\delta}^{2{\gamma}}X^{\gamma}R^{2})
. Hence

{\delta}^{2{\gamma}}X^{\gamma}R^{2}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant r,r^{\prime}\leqslant R:\\
{\theta}={\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}\in{\mathfrak{M}}\end{subarray}}|\hat{\nu}({\theta})|^{\gamma}.

(E.10)

If
{\theta}\in{\mathfrak{M}}_{2}(q,a)
with
(a,q)=1
and
q\leqslant X^{\tau}
then, by Lemmas
D.2
,
D.3
and
D.9
, we have

\hat{\nu}({\theta})\ll q^{-1/2}\min\Bigl\{X,\Bigl\|{\theta}-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr\|^{-1}\Bigr\}+O_{w}(X^{\frac{1}{2}+2\tau})\ll\frac{Xq^{-1/2}}{1+X|{\theta}-\frac{a}{q}|}.

We obtain (
E.9
), but with
k=2
in the definition of
G(\cdot)
, and Bourgain’s argument again completes the proof.

E.2.
An intermediate exponent

In this subsection let
k=3
, and let
\eta
be a small positive constant as before. We proceed in two steps, effectively ‘pruning’ the large spectrum. In the first step, we use a power-saving minor arc estimate for an auxiliary majorant to come close to a sharp restriction estimate. In the second step, we no longer require a power saving on the minor arcs, so we are able to obtain a sharp restriction estimate by reverting to the majorant
\nu
.

E.2.1.
A close estimate

Here we concede a small loss. By slightly increasing the exponent, we will recover it in the next subsection. Our goal for the time being is to establish the following.

Lemma E.3
.

We have

\sup_{|\phi|\leqslant\nu}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\hat{\phi}({\alpha})|^{8-10^{-6}}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}\ll X^{-1}(WX)^{8-10^{-6}}.

Similarly to the
k\neq 3
case, it suffices to prove that

\mathrm{meas}(\mathcal{R}_{\delta})\ll\frac{1}{{\delta}^{8-10^{-5}}X},

where it is now convenient to redefine

\mathcal{R}_{\delta}=\{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{T}}:|\hat{\phi}({\alpha})|>{\delta}WX\}.

Note that in this setting
{\delta}\ll W^{-1}
. Since Lemma
E.1
implies that

({\delta}WX)^{6}\mathrm{meas}(\mathcal{R}_{\delta})\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\hat{\phi}({\alpha})|^{6}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}\ll P^{15.25-10^{-4}},

we may assume without loss that

{\delta}>P^{10^{-5}-\frac{1}{8}}.

(E.11)

Let
{\theta}_{1},{\theta}_{2},\ldots,{\theta}_{R}
be
X^{-1}
-spaced points in
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
. It suffices to show that

R\ll{\delta}^{-6.3}.

(E.12)

For some
a_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|a_{n}|\leqslant 1
, we have
\phi(n)=a_{n}\mu(n)
, wherein we employ the majorant

\mu(n)=\begin{cases}x^{2},&\text{if }n=\frac{x^{3}-\xi^{3}}{3W}\text{ for some }x\leqslant P\text{ with }x\equiv\xi{\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,W}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

With
{\gamma}=3.1
, the calculation in
[
BP17
, §6]
implies

{\delta}^{2{\gamma}}(WX)^{\gamma}R^{2}\ll\sum_{r,r^{\prime}\leqslant R}|\hat{\mu}({\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r})|^{\gamma}.

(E.13)

Consider
{\theta}={\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}
in the summand. We require a circle method analysis. The majorant
\mu
is very similar to the ‘auxiliary majorant’ from
[
Cho17
, §5]
. Therein, the calculations are based on partial summation and Roger Baker’s estimates, as packaged in
[
Cho16
, §2]
. The same approach yields the following major arc estimate, where the corresponding set of minor arcs is

{\mathfrak{n}}:=\{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{T}}:|\hat{\mu}({\alpha})|\leqslant P^{2.75+10^{-6}}\}.

Lemma E.4
.

If
{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{T}}\setminus{\mathfrak{n}}
then there exist
q,a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
0\leqslant a\leqslant q-1
and

\hat{\mu}({\alpha})\ll WXq^{\varepsilon-\frac{1}{3}}\Bigl(1+X\Bigl|{\alpha}-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr|\Bigr)^{-1/3}.

We continue the proof of Lemma
E.3
. In light of (
E.11
), the contribution from
{\theta}\in{\mathfrak{n}}
to the right-hand side of (
E.13
) is
o({\delta}^{2{\gamma}}(WX)^{\gamma}R^{2})
, and so

{\delta}^{2{\gamma}}(WX)^{\gamma}R^{2}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r,r^{\prime}\leqslant R:\\
{\theta}\notin{\mathfrak{n}}\end{subarray}}|\hat{\mu}({\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r})|^{\gamma}.

With
C
a large positive constant and
Q_{2}=C+{\delta}^{-9}
, we can use Lemma
E.4
to obtain

{\delta}^{2{\gamma}}R^{2}\ll\sum_{1\leqslant r,r^{\prime}\leqslant R}G_{1}({\theta}_{r}-{\theta}^{\prime}_{r}),

where

G_{1}({\theta})=\sum_{q\leqslant Q_{2}}\sum_{a=0}^{q-1}\frac{q^{{\gamma}(\varepsilon-\frac{1}{3})}}{(1+X|\sin({\theta}-\frac{a}{q})|)^{{\gamma}/3}}.

As
{\gamma}(\varepsilon-\frac{1}{3})>1
, the proof may be completed by Bourgain’s argument, as in the previous subsection.

E.2.2.
A sharp estimate

We are ready to prove (
E.1
); recall that
p=8-10^{-8}
. This time let

\mathcal{\mathcal{R}}_{\delta}=\{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{T}}:|\hat{\phi}({\alpha})|>{\delta}X\},

where
{\delta}\ll 1
. Following the same strategy, it suffices to prove that

\mathrm{meas}(\mathcal{R}_{\delta})\ll\frac{1}{{\delta}^{8-10^{-7}}X}.

From Lemma
E.3
we have

({\delta}X)^{8-10^{-6}}\mathrm{meas}(\mathcal{R}_{\delta})\ll W^{8}X^{7-10^{-6}},

so we may assume without loss that

W^{-10^{7}}<{\delta}\ll 1.

With
1,2,\ldots,R
being
X^{-1}
-spaced points in
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
, it again suffices to prove (
E.12
). This time put
\phi(n)=a_{n}\nu(n)
, where
a_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|a_{n}|\leqslant 1
. The calculation in
[
BP17
, §6]
then gives (
E.7
), and by the method of subsection
E.1
(in the
k\geqslant 4
case) we obtain (
E.9
); once again Bourgain’s argument carries through.

We have considered all cases, thereby completing the proof of Lemmas
6.4
,
12.5
and
16.2
.

Appendix F
Lefmann’s criterion

In this section we prove Theorem
1.8
, which is a consequence of Lefmann’s lemma
[
Lef91
, Fact 2.8]
. The theorem is a special case of Theorem
1.3
, but can be established more simply, and we presently provide a proof. By rearranging the variables, we may suppose that for some
t\in\{6,7,\ldots,s\}
we have

c_{1}+\cdots+c_{t}=0.

(F.1)

Let

a:=c_{t+1}+\cdots+c_{s}.

The case
a=0
was treated by Browning and Prendiville
[
BP17
]
so, for simplicity, we assume henceforth that
a\neq 0
.

The following obscure fact was shown by Lefmann
[
Lef91
, Fact 2.8]
.

Lemma F.1

(Lefmann)
.

Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{s}
be non-zero integers. Assume that there exists
t\in[s]
for which we have (
F.1
).
Assume further that there exist
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{t}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

c_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+c_{t}y_{t}=0

(F.2)

and

ay^{2}+c_{1}y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+c_{t}y_{t}^{2}=0.

(F.3)

Then (
1.8
) is partition regular over
{\mathbb{N}}
.

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.8
, it remains to prove that the system has a solution
(y,{\mathbf{y}})\in({\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\})\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{t}
. The number of such solutions in
[-P,P]^{t+1}
is
{\mathcal{N}}_{1}-{\mathcal{N}}_{2}
, where
{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
is the total number of integer solutions
(y,{\mathbf{y}})\in[-P,P]^{t+1}
and
{\mathcal{N}}_{2}
is the number of integer solutions
{\mathbf{y}}\in[-P,P]^{t}
to

c_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+c_{t}y_{t}=c_{1}y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+c_{t}y_{t}^{2}=0.

Here
P
is a large positive real number.

Lemma F.2
.

We have

{\mathcal{N}}_{2}\ll P^{t-3}\log P.

Proof.

Rogovskaya
[
Rog86
]
showed that the system

\displaystyle x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}

\displaystyle=y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}

\displaystyle x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle=y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+y_{3}^{2}

has
\frac{18}{\pi^{2}}P^{3}\log P+O(P^{3})
solutions
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\in[P]^{6}
. By orthogonality, one can deduce from this that

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}\Bigl|\sum_{|x|\leqslant P}e({\alpha}_{1}x+{\alpha}_{2}x^{2})\Bigr|^{6}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}_{1}{\mathrm{d}}{\alpha}_{2}\ll P^{3}\log P.

As
t\geqslant 6
, the lemma now follows from orthogonality, Hölder’s inequality, and the trivial bound
\sum_{|x|\leqslant P}e({\alpha}_{1}x+{\alpha}_{2}x^{2})\ll P
.
∎

Lemma F.3
.

We have

{\mathcal{N}}_{1}\gg P^{t-2}.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle Q(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t-1})

\displaystyle=c_{t}^{-1}(c_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+c_{t-1}y_{t-1})^{2}+\sum_{i\leqslant t-1}c_{i}y_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leqslant t-1}(c_{i}+c_{i}^{2}/c_{t})y_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant t-1}(c_{i}c_{j}/c_{t})y_{i}y_{j},

and put

C=|c_{1}|+\cdots+|c_{t}|.

Now
{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
is greater than or equal to the number of integer solutions

(y,y_{1},\ldots,y_{t-1})\in[-P/C,P/C]^{t}

to

ay^{2}+Q(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t-1})=0

with
c_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+c_{t-1}y_{t-1}\equiv 0\mod c_{t}
. By considering only multiples of
c_{t}
, we find that
{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
is greater than or equal to the number of integer solutions
{\mathbf{x}}\in[-P/C^{2},P/C^{2}]^{t}
to

Q_{1}({\mathbf{x}}):=Q(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1})+ax_{t}^{2}=0.

For the sake of brevity, we appeal to Birch’s very general theorem
[
Bir61
, Theorem 1]
. The
Birch singular locus
is the set
{\mathcal{S}}
of
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{t}
at which the gradient of
Q_{1}
vanishes identically. (In this instance, the Birch singular locus coincides with the usual singular locus.) We compute that

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}Q(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t-1})=(c_{i}+c_{i}^{2}/c_{t})y_{i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leqslant t-1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}c_{i}c_{j}y_{j}/c_{t},

and so

\frac{c_{t}}{2c_{i}}\partial_{i}Q=(c_{t}+c_{i})y_{i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leqslant t-1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}c_{j}y_{j}=c_{t}(y_{i}-y_{t})+\sum_{j\leqslant t}c_{j}y_{j}=c_{t}(y_{i}-y_{t}),

where
y_{t}:=-c_{t}^{-1}(c_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+c_{t-1}y_{t-1})
. Therefore

{\mathcal{S}}=\{(x,x,\ldots,x,0)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{t}\},

and in particular
\dim{\mathcal{S}}=1
.

As
t-\dim{\mathcal{S}}>4
, Birch’s theorem
[
Bir61
, Theorem 1]
gives

{\mathcal{N}}_{1}={\mathfrak{S}}{\mathfrak{J}}P^{t-2}+O(P^{t-2-{\delta}}),

(F.4)

for some constant
{\delta}>0
, where
{\mathfrak{S}}
and
{\mathfrak{J}}
are respectively the
singular series
and
singular integral
arising from the circle method analysis. Birch notes in
[
Bir61
, §7]
that
{\mathfrak{S}}
is positive as long as
Q_{1}
has a non-singular
p
-adic zero for each prime
p
, and that
{\mathfrak{J}}
is positive as long as
Q_{1}
has a real zero outside of
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
. Note that
Q
has a zero
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{t-1}
with pairwise distinct coordinates; this follows from
[
Kei14
, Theorem 1.1]
, or from a circle method analysis. Now
({\mathbf{x}}^{*},0)
is a real zero of
Q_{1}
outside of
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
, and is also a non-singular
p
-adic zero for each
p
. Hence
{\mathfrak{S}}{\mathfrak{J}}>0
, and by (
F.4
) the proof is now complete.
∎

The previous two lemmas yield
{\mathcal{N}}_{1}>{\mathcal{N}}_{2}
, and this completes the proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Remark F.4
.

Lefmann’s lemma generalises straightforwardly to higher degrees. We do not explore this avenue further, as any results thus obtained are likely subsumed by Theorem
1.3
.
