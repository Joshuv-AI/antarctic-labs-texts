Abstract
. We study nonnegative and sums of squares symmetric (and even symmetric) functions of fixed degree. We can think of these as limit cones of symmetric nonnegative polynomials and symmetric sums of squares of fixed degree as the number of variables goes to infinity. We compare these cones, including finding explicit examples of nonnegative polynomials which are not sums of squares for any sufficiently large number of variables, and compute the tropicalizations of their dual cones in the even symmetric case. We find that the tropicalization of the dual cones is naturally understood in terms of the overlooked superdominance order on partitions. The power sum symmetric functions obey this same partial order (analogously to how term-normalized power sums obey the dominance order [ CGS11 ] ).

1.
Introduction

The relationship between nonnegative polynomials and sums of squares plays an important role in real algebraic geometry, and symmetric polynomials form a distinguished family of examples. The study of inequalities in real symmetric polynomials goes back to Newton
[
HLP52
,
New32
]
. We are interested in inequalities in symmetric polynomials which hold regardless of the number of variables; we can view such inequalities as inequalities between
symmetric functions
(see e.g.
[
Mac98
, Chapter I.2]
). We can also regard symmetric functions as natural limits of symmetric polynomials as the number of variables increases. Therefore symmetric functions can be seen as functions on the
image at infinity
of the so-called
Vandermonde map
, which has been studied extensively by Arnold, Givental, Kostov, and Ursell in finitely many variables
[
Arn86
,
Giv87
,
Kos89
,
Kos99
,
Kos04
,
Urs59
]
and at infinity
[
Kos04
,
Kos07
,
Ace+23
]
.

Inequalities in symmetric functions can be naturally expressed in the power sum basis. Examining such inequalities leads us to the
superdominance order
on partitions, and we find extensions of inequalities in
\ell^{p}
-norms for integer exponents and Lyapunov’s inequality (
[
HLP52
, Sec. 2.9 and 2.10]
). We find explicit uniform examples of power sum inequalities valid for any number of variables, which do not have a sum of squares certificate, e.g. the quartic
4p_{1}^{4}-5p_{2}p_{1}^{2}-\frac{139}{20}p_{3}p_{1}+4p_{2}^{2}+4p_{4}
and the decic
\frac{1}{18}p_{2}^{5}+3p_{8}p_{2}+6p_{6}p_{4}-3p_{6}p_{2}^{2}
. Moreover, we prove that for symmetric functions, the cones of symmetric (and even symmetric) nonnegative forms are not semialgebraic for any degree
2d\geq 4
, (respectively
2d\geq 6
), while the cones of symmetric, and even symmetric sums of squares are semialgebraic, hence proving their difference (Theorem
3.12
).
We then study the tropicalizations of the dual cones of the limit cones, and discover hidden combinatorial structure, also naturally expressed in terms of the superdominance order.

Recently
tropicalization
has been applied beyond classical algebraic geometry in extremal combinatorics
[
Ble+22
,
BR22
]
and real algebraic geometry
[
Ble+22a
,
AB24
]
. Using tropicalization we study the dual cones to nonnegative and sums of squares homogeneous even symmetric functions. We show that the tropicalization of the
Vandermonde cell
(the limit of the image of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under the even power sum map) is a rational polyhedral cone, and give an explicit list of facet-defining inequalities (Theorem
4.5
). This allows us to compute the tropicalization of the dual cone to even symmetric nonnegative functions (Proposition
5.4
) via
tropical convexity

[
DS04
]
similar to
[
Ble+22a
,
AB24
]
. Furthermore, the inequalities defining the tropical Vandermonde cell can be described in terms of the superdominance order, a partial order on partitions which is also related to power sum inequalities (Theorem
2.3
), analogous to how
term-normalized power sums
obey the dominance order (see
[
CGS11
]
). We give a combinatorial description of the tropicalization of the dual cone to even symmetric nonnegative functions (Theorem
5.9
). The superdominance order also helps us compute the tropicalization of the dual cone to symmetric sums of squares via a lemma on tropical spectrahedra (Lemma
5.14
). We also use symmetry-adapted bases constructed from higher Specht polynomials (as in
[
BR21
]
) and
partial symmetry reduction
(a technique introduced in
[
AB24
]
). We conjecture that the tropicalization of the dual cone to symmetric sums of squares has a combinatorial characterization in terms of the superdominance order (Conjecture
5.23
).
We now introduce background and notation, and discuss our results in detail.

1.1.
Background and notation

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denote the
symmetric group
on
n
letters, i.e. the group of permutations of
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Symmetric polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
are invariant polynomials under the action of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
that permutes the variables.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{n}
denote the
hyperoctahedral group
on
n
letters, i.e. the group of signed permutations of
[n]
. We call a polynomial in
\mathbb{R}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]

even symmetric
if it is invariant under the action of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
that permutes the variables (multiplying by the sign of the permutation). Equivalently,
n
-variate even symmetric polynomials are symmetric polynomials in the ring
\mathbb{R}[X_{1}^{2},\dots,X_{n}^{2}]
.

Let
H_{n,d}:=\mathbb{R}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]_{d}
be the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space of
n
-variate homogeneous polynomials (forms) of degree
d
in the variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. With the above actions, each vector space
H_{n,d}
, for fixed
n
and any
d
, is both an
\mathcal{S}_{n}
-module and a
\mathcal{B}_{n}
-module. Denote by
H_{n,d}^{\mathcal{S}}
the subspace of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
-invariant forms in
H_{n,d}
, and by
H_{n,d}^{\mathcal{B}}
the subspace of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
-invariant forms in
H_{n,d}
.

It is well-known that the dimension of
H_{n,d}^{\mathcal{S}}
(resp.
H_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{B}}
) as an
\mathbb{R}
-vector space is the number of partitions of
d
with at most
n
parts. A
partition

\lambda
of a positive integer
d
is a decreasing sequence of positive integers that add up to
d
, i.e.
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})
where
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{k}
,
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\dots+\lambda_{k}=d
, and
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
for
i=1,\dots,k
. The numbers
\lambda_{i}
are called the
parts
of
\lambda
, and the number of parts of
\lambda
is called its
length
, which we denote by
\ell(\lambda)
. The sum of the parts of a partition
\lambda
, denoted
|\lambda|
, is called its
size
. We call a partition
even
if all its parts are even numbers. For example, if
\lambda=(4,2,2)
then
\lambda
is even,
\lambda_{1}=4,\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}=2
,
\ell(\lambda)=3
and
|\lambda|=8
. We condense the number of repeated parts in an exponent, for example,
(4,2^{3},1^{2}):=(4,2,2,2,1,1)
. We consider the empty set
\emptyset
to be the unique partition of
0
, so that
\ell(\emptyset)=|\emptyset|=0
.

We denote by
\Lambda_{d}
the set of partitions of
d
and by
\Lambda_{2d}^{\varepsilon}
the set of even partitions of
2d
. We will often use the notation

\lambda\vdash d\text{ if }\lambda\in\Lambda_{d},\text{ and }\lambda\vDash 2d\text{ if }\lambda\in\Lambda_{2d}^{\varepsilon}.

The vector spaces
H_{n,d}^{\mathcal{S}}
and
H_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{B}}
have many well-known bases, some of them being the
power sum polynomials
, the
monomial symmetric polynomials
, and the
elementary symmetric polynomials
. For a positive integer
r
,
p_{r}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}):=X_{1}^{r}+\dots+X_{n}^{r}
is the
r
-th power sum polynomial, and, if
r\leq n
,
e_{r}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}):=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq n}X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{r}}
is the
r
-th elementary symmetric polynomial. We will write
p_{r}
and
e_{r}
when the number of variables is clear from the context.
For a partition
\lambda
we define

\displaystyle p_{\lambda}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}p_{\lambda_{i}},\quad e_{\lambda}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}e_{\lambda_{i}},\quad m_{\lambda}=\sum X^{\lambda},

the power sum, elementary, and monomial symmetric polynomials respectively, and where we sum over all monomials whose exponents are precisely
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}
in
m_{\lambda}
. For
n\geq d
the sets
\{p_{\lambda}:\lambda\vdash d\}
,
\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\vdash d\}
,
\{m_{\lambda}:\lambda\vdash d\}
are bases for
H_{n,d}^{\mathcal{S}}
, and the sets
\{p_{\lambda}:\lambda\vDash 2d\}
,
\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\vDash 2d\}
,
\{m_{\lambda}:\lambda\vDash 2d\}
are bases for
H_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{B}}
. Hence, for
n\geq d
the dimension of
H_{n,d}^{\mathcal{S}}
(and the dimension of
H_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{B}}
) is the number of partitions of
d
, which we denote by
\pi(d)
.
The transition matrices between all three bases do not depend on the number of variables for all
n\geq d

[
Mac98
, Sec. I.6.]
.

A
symmetric function
is a formal power series in countably many variables that is invariant under the permutation of the variables and of bounded degree. We consider the
power sum functions

\mathfrak{p}_{k}:=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}X_{i}^{k}
,
elementary symmetric functions

\mathfrak{e}_{k}=\sum_{I\subset\mathbb{N},|I|=k}\prod_{i\in I}X_{i}
and
monomial symmetric functions

\mathfrak{m}_{\lambda}=\sum X^{\lambda}
. An
even symmetric function
is a symmetric function in which the exponent vector of every monomial contains only even integers.

We will usually write
\mathbb{R}^{\pi(d)}
, indicating that the coordinates are labeled with the partitions of
d
ordered according to the
reverse lexicographic order
. The reverse lexicographic order
>_{revlex}
is a total order on the set of partitions. For any partition
\lambda
let
\lambda_{(1)}\leq\lambda_{(2)}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{(\ell(\lambda))}
denote the parts of
\lambda
in increasing order, i.e.,
\lambda_{(i)}=\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)+1-i}
for
i=1,\dots,\ell(\lambda)
. For distinct partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
we say that
\lambda>_{revlex}\mu
if for the smallest
i
such that
\lambda_{(i)}\neq\mu_{(i)}
we have
\lambda_{(i)}<\mu_{(i)}
. For example,
(1^{4})>(2,1^{2})>(3,1)>(2^{2})>(4)
where
>
is
>_{revlex}
. We list coordinates of vectors
c\in\mathbb{R}^{\pi(d)}
in this order, from largest to smallest, e.g. for
c\in\mathbb{R}^{\pi(4)}
:
c=(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4},c_{5})=(c_{(1^{4})},c_{(2,1^{2})},c_{(3,1)},c_{(2^{2})},c_{(4)})
.

Definition 1.1
.

The
Vandermonde map
of degree
d
in
n
variables is the function

\displaystyle\nu_{n,d}:\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\longmapsto(x_{1}+\dots+x_{n},x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2},\dots,x_{1}^{d}+\dots+x_{n}^{d}).

We write
\mathcal{M}_{n,d}=\nu_{n,d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for the image of the Vandermonde map. In our work, the Vandermonde map appears naturally in the study of nonnegative symmetric forms. For fixed degree
d
and increasing number of variables the images of these maps form an ascending chain, i.e., we have
\mathcal{M}_{n,d}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n+1,d}
for all
n\geq 1
. We are particularly interested in the image of the
Vandermonde map at infinity
, i.e. in the closure of the union of all the images

\mathcal{M}_{d}:=\clo\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{n,d}\right)~.

We also consider the
even Vandermonde map

\displaystyle\nu_{n,d}^{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\longmapsto(x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2},x_{1}^{4}+\dots+x_{n}^{4},\dots,x_{1}^{2d}+\dots+x_{n}^{2d}).

its image
\mathcal{N}_{n,d}=\nu_{n,d}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
and its
image at infinity

\mathcal{N}_{d}:=\clo\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{N}_{n,d}\right)
. Note that
\mathcal{N}_{n,d}
equals the image of
\nu_{n,d}
restricted to the nonnegative orthant. We call the image of the even Vandermonde map
the Vandermonde cell
.

When working in finitely many variables we use the power sum basis of
H^{S}_{n,d}
. With the restriction
p_{\lambda}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n+1})\mapsto p_{\lambda}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},0)
we identify the vector spaces
H_{n,d}^{\mathcal{S}}
for all
n\geq d
. Analogously, we identify the vector spaces
H_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{B}}
. This defines an inverse system whose limit can be identified with the homogeneous component of degree
d
(resp.
2d
) of the ring of (resp. even) symmetric functions.

Denoting by
\Sigma_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}
the cones of sums of squares and nonnegative forms in
H_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}
, we have the inclusions
\Sigma_{n+1,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}\subset\Sigma_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n+1,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}\subset\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}
.
The cones

\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}=\bigcap_{n\geq 2d}\Sigma_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}=\bigcap_{n\geq 2d}\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}

then contain any homogeneous symmetric function of degree
2d
that is a sum of squares/nonnegative in any number of variables.
Analogously, we define
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}
and
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
for the subspace of even symmetric forms.

1.2.
Main results in detail

In Section
2
we introduce the
superdominance order

\succeq
on the set
\Lambda_{d}
(see Definition
2.1
) and observe that the power sum polynomials are ordered in the same way as partitions with respect to
\succeq
.

Theorem
2.3
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\vdash d
. Then
p_{\lambda}\geq p_{\mu}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
for all
n
if and only if
\lambda\succeq\mu
.

Furthermore, if
\lambda
and
\mu
are even partitions then
\lambda\succeq\mu
is equivalent to
p_{\lambda}-p_{\mu}
being a sum of squares for all number of variables (Proposition
2.15
). By exploiting combinatorial properties of the superdominance order we prove one direction of Theorem
2.3
in Section
2
, and use properties of tropicalization to finally prove the theorem in Section
4
. In Section
5
we show that the superdominance order is tightly related to the tropicalizations of the dual cones to
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}
and
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
.

In Section
3
we study the general convex geometry of the cones
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}
,
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
,
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}
,
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
.
Our first observation in Theorem
3.2
is that these cones are full-dimensional pointed closed convex cones and that these are the Kuratowski limits of the cones in finitely many variables. We study the symmetric quartics in detail and find a form in
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{4}\setminus\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{4}
.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first example of a symmetric nonnegative form that is not a sum of squares
regardless of the number of variables
(
n\geq 4
), where the coefficients are independent of the number of variables when expressed in the power sum basis.

Theorem
3.6
.

The symmetric quartic
4p_{1}^{4}-5p_{2}p_{1}^{2}-\frac{139}{20}p_{3}p_{1}+4p_{2}^{2}+4p_{4}
is nonnegative for all number of variables. Moreover, these quartic forms are not sums of squares for any number of variables
n\geq 4
.

We are able to construct this family by finding a
test set for nonnegativity
(and a test set for being a sum of squares), similar to
[
CLR87
]
, for symmetric quartics.
The test set for nonnegativity is derived from
[
Kos07
]
, while the test set for being a sum of squares is obtained from examining the extremal rays of the dual cone to
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{4}
.

Theorem
3.5
.

Let
f(\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2},\mathfrak{p}_{3},\mathfrak{p}_{4})
be a homogeneous even symmetric function of degree
4
. Then,
f
is nonnegative in all number of variables if and only if
f
is nonnegative on the discrete set of parallel lines

\{(x,1,n^{-1/2},n^{-1})\,\,|\,\,x\in\mathbb{R},\,n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\}.

Moreover,
f
is a sum of squares for all number of variables if and only if
f
is nonnegative on

\{(x,1,u,u^{2})\,\,|\,\,x\in\mathbb{R},\,0\leq u\leq 1\}.

In Section
5
we find, with the help of tropicalization, an example of an even symmetric nonnegative decic that is not a sum of squares for any sufficiently large number of variables.

Proposition
5.27
.

The even symmetric form
\frac{1}{18}p_{(2^{5})}+3p_{(8,2)}+6p_{(6,4)}-3p_{(6,2^{2})}

is nonnegative for all number of variables but not a sum of squares for any sufficiently large number of variables
n\geq N
for some
N
.

We also show that the cones of sums of squares and nonnegative symmetric functions are distinct for all even degrees
\geq 6
.

Theorem
3.12
.

For all
d\geq 2
we have
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}\subsetneq\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
and for all
d\geq 3
we have
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}\subsetneq\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
. Moreover, the sets
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
and
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
are non-semialgebraic in all these cases, and the sets
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}
,
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}
are semialgebraic.

The
Vandermonde cell

\mathcal{N}_{d}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n})
is closed under addition and under coordinatewise (Hadamard) multiplication (see Lemma
4.11
). This, together with the two families of classical binomial inequalities between power sums (Remark
4.2
), and the aid of a
tropical Farkas lemma
(Lemma
4.6
), allows us to compute the tropicalization of the Vandermonde cell
\mathcal{N}_{d}
, which is a rational polyhedron.

Theorem
4.5
.

The set
\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d})
is a rational polyhedron with the facet-defining inequalities:

\displaystyle\trop\left(\mathcal{N}_{d}\right)=\left\{\left.y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\begin{cases}y_{k}+y_{k+2}\geq 2y_{k+1}\;,\quad k=1,\ldots,d-2\\
dy_{d-1}\geq(d-1)y_{d}\;.\end{cases}\right\}\right\}.

We then use the
H
-representation of
\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d})
to immediately prove that power sums obey the superdominance order (Theorem
2.3
).

We turn to the tropicalization of the dual cones
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
and
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*}
in Section
5
. Using the superdominance order we define a decreasing chain of rational polyhedra
T^{(k)}_{2d}
(see Definition
5.6
) and prove that the sequence stabilizes and it does so at
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
(Theorem
5.9
). With the aid of Lemma
5.14
(bounds the tropicalization of a spectrahedral cone), using properties of symmetric functions and the superdominance order, we find that
\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*})
is a polyhedral cone sitting in between
T_{2d}^{(1)}
and
T_{2d}^{(2)}
. We finally conclude the strict inclusion between the tropicalizations of the dual cones.

Theorem
5.21
.

\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*})\supsetneq\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
for all
d\geq 5
.

Although we have
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{6}^{*}\subsetneq\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{6}^{*}
and
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{8}^{*}\subsetneq\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{8}^{*}
we find that their tropicalizations coincide.

In Appendix
A
we briefly explain symmetry and partial symmetry reduction techniques for representing symmetric sums of squares.
Using partial symmetry reduction we find that the cones
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
and
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
are spectrahedra, and construct an explicit spectrahedral representation for
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{4}^{*}
and each
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
for
d=3,4,5
.

2.
Superdominance Order

In this section, we introduce a partial order on partitions of the same size, which we call the
superdominance order
. We will see that the inequalities on power sums
p_{\lambda}\geq p_{\mu}
that hold for all number of variables on the nonnegative orthant, where
\lambda
and
\mu
are partitions of the same size, are precisely those for which
\lambda
superdominates
\mu
(Theorem
2.3
). We show that
\lambda
superdominates
\mu
implies
p_{\lambda}\geq p_{\mu}
, delaying the proof of the reverse implication to Section
4
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\vdash d
. We say that
\lambda

superdominates

\mu
, denoted
\lambda\succeq\mu
, if

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j}\lambda_{(i)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mu_{(i)}

for each
j=1,\dots,\min\{\ell(\lambda),\ell(\mu)\}
. We write
\lambda\succ\mu
if
\lambda\succeq\mu
and
\lambda\neq\mu
.

Observe that
\succeq
defines a partial order on
\Lambda_{d}
. For
d\leq 5
the order
\succeq
is a total order on
\Lambda_{d}
, e.g. for
d=5
,

\displaystyle(1^{5})\succ(2,1^{3})\succ(3,1^{2})\succ(2^{2},1)\succ(4,1)\succ(3,2)\succ(5)

but
\succeq
is no longer a total order on
\Lambda_{d}
for all
d\geq 6
.

Remark 2.2
.

Definition
2.1
is motivated by the following definition in the literature
[
MOA79
, A.2. Definition]
for real vectors. For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
it is said that
x
is
weakly supermajorized
by
y
, denoted
x\prec^{\mathbf{w}}y
, if

\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{(i)}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{(i)}

for all
k=1,\dots,n
, where
(z_{(1)},\dots,z_{(n)})
is obtained by permuting the entries of
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
z_{(1)}\leq\dots\leq z_{(n)}
.

Using properties of the superdominance order and Theorem
4.5
we show that power sums of a fixed degree are ordered in precisely the same way as partitions with respect to the superdominance order.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\vdash d
. Then
p_{\lambda}\geq p_{\mu}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
for all
n
if and only if
\lambda\succeq\mu
.

An analogous result was obtained by Cuttler, Greene, and Skandera
[
CGS11
, Theorem 4.2]
for
term-normalized power sums
and the
dominance order
.

For a partition
\lambda
the
\lambda
-th

term-normalized power sum
is
P_{\lambda}:=\frac{p_{\lambda}}{n^{\ell(\lambda)}}
. The dominance order
\unrhd
on
\Lambda_{d}
, is defined as follows:
\lambda\unrhd\mu
if

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j}\lambda_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mu_{i}

for each
j=1,\dots,\min(\ell(\lambda),\ell(\mu))
.

Remark 2.4
.

Observe that the dominance order is equivalent to the superdominance order on partitions of the same length, i.e., if
\lambda,\mu\vdash d
satisfy
\ell(\lambda)=\ell(\mu)
then
\lambda\succeq\mu
if and only if
\lambda\unrhd\mu
.

Remark 2.5
.

In analogy to Theorem
2.3
, the binomial inequalities in elementary symmetric polynomials
e_{k}=\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leqslant n}X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}}
are characterized by the reverse-dominance order. For partitions
\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda_{d}
we have
e_{\lambda}\geq e_{\mu}
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if
\lambda\unlhd\mu
.
This characterization is the same as for term-normalized elementary symmetric polynomials
E_{\lambda}:=\frac{e_{\lambda}}{e_{\lambda}(1,\ldots,1)}
(see
[
CGS11
, Theorem 3.2]
). One observes that
e_{(k-1,l+1)}(\underline{X}^{2})-e_{(k,l)}(\underline{X}^{2})
is a sum of squares and thus nonnegative, where
\underline{X}^{2}:=(X_{1}^{2},\ldots,X_{n}^{2})
. The converse direction can be proved exactly as in
[
CGS11
]
for
E_{\lambda}\geq E_{\mu}
.

2.1.
Properties of the superdominance order

It is well-known that the lexicographic order
refines
the dominance order. We show that the reverse lexicographic order refines the superdominance order, among other elementary properties.

Proposition 2.6
.

If
\lambda,\mu\vdash d
and
\lambda\succ\mu
, then
\ell(\lambda)\geq\ell(\mu)
and
\lambda>_{revlex}\mu
.

Proof.

Since both are partitions of
d
then one cannot finish adding the parts of
\lambda
first, so
\ell(\lambda)\geq\ell(\mu)
.

If
\mu>_{revlex}\lambda
then the smallest index
j
such that
\lambda_{(j)}\neq\mu_{(j)}
also satisfies
\mu_{(j)}<\lambda_{(j)}
, and then
\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mu_{(i)}<\sum_{i=1}^{j}\lambda_{(i)}
, so
\lambda\not\succeq\mu
.
∎

The converse of Proposition
2.6
is not true: for example
\lambda=(3^{4},1)
and
\mu=(7,2^{3})
are incomparable in the superdominance order.

For
\lambda,\mu\vdash d
denote
\lambda\gtrdot\mu
if
\lambda

covers

\mu
in the superdominance order, i.e.,
\lambda\succ\mu
and there is no
\nu\vdash d
such that
\lambda\succ\nu\succ\mu
.

Definition 2.7
.

If
\ell(\lambda)>1
denote by
\lambda^{*}
the partition obtained from
\lambda
by merging
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
into a single part
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
.

For example
(3^{2})^{*}=(6)
and
(4,3,1)^{*}=(7,1)
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\vdash d
with
\ell(\lambda)>\ell(\mu)
. Then

(i)

\lambda\succ\mu\Rightarrow\lambda^{*}\succeq\mu
,

(ii)

\lambda\gtrdot\mu\Rightarrow\mu=\lambda^{*}
,

(iii)

\lambda\gtrdot\lambda^{*}\iff\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\leq 1
.

Proof.

(i) Let
\ell(\lambda)=l
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{(i)}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{(i)}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{(i)}

for each
k=1,\dots,l-2
. Since
\ell(\lambda^{*})=l-1
and
\ell(\mu)\leq l-1
then
\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\lambda_{(i)}^{*}=d=\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}\mu_{(i)}
, therefore
\lambda^{*}\succeq\mu
.

(ii) By (i)
\lambda\gtrdot\mu
implies
\lambda^{*}\succeq\mu
. But
\lambda\succ\lambda^{*}
, so
\lambda\succ\lambda^{*}\succeq\mu
, and since
\lambda\gtrdot\mu
we must have
\mu=\lambda^{*}
.

(iii) If
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}>1
the partition
\nu
with
\nu_{1}=\lambda_{1}-1
and
\nu_{2}=\lambda_{2}+1
and the rest of its parts coinciding with
\lambda
lies strictly between
\lambda
and
\lambda^{*}
. If
\lambda
does not cover
\lambda^{*}
then we have
\lambda\succ\nu\succ\lambda^{*}
for some
\nu\vdash d
, which, due to (i), is only possible if
\ell(\nu)=\ell(\lambda)
. But then
\lambda_{i}=\nu_{i}
for
i=3,\dots,\ell(\lambda)
, and so
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\rhd(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
and
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}
so
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}>1
.
∎

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\vdash d
. Then
\lambda\gtrdot\mu
if and only if one of the following holds:

(i)

\ell(\lambda)=\ell(\mu)
and
\lambda
covers
\mu
in the dominance order.

(ii)

\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\leq 1
and
\mu=\lambda^{*}
.

Proof.

By Remark
2.4
, if
\ell(\lambda)=\ell(\mu)
, then
\lambda\gtrdot\mu
is equivalent to
\lambda
covering
\mu
in the dominance order.

If
\ell(\lambda)>\ell(\mu)
then by Proposition
2.8
,
\lambda\gtrdot\mu
implies
\mu=\lambda^{*}
and
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\leq 1
. The other implication follows from Proposition
2.8
(iii).
∎

Definition 2.10
.

The
fusion
of partitions
\lambda,\mu,\dots,\omega
, denoted by
\lambda\mu\cdots\omega
, is the unique partition whose parts are precisely the parts of
\lambda,\mu,\dots,\omega
put together. Denote by
\mu^{\circ k}
the fusion of
k
copies of
\mu

For example, if
\lambda=(2^{2},1)
and
\mu=(3,2,1)
then
\lambda\mu=(3,2^{3},1^{2})
and
\mu^{\circ 2}=\mu\mu=(3^{2},2^{2},1^{2})
. Observe that
\ell(\lambda\mu\cdots\omega)=\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\mu)+\dots+\ell(\omega)
, and
|\lambda\mu\cdots\omega|=|\lambda|+|\mu|+\dots+|\omega|
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
\lambda^{i},\mu^{i}\vdash d_{i}
such that
\lambda^{i}\succeq\mu^{i}
for

, then
\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}\succeq\mu^{1}\cdots\mu^{k}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show the result for
k=2
. Let
\lambda:=\lambda^{1}\lambda^{2}
,
\mu:=\mu^{1}\mu^{2}
and
m:=\ell(\mu)
.

Since
\lambda^{i}\succeq\mu^{i}
, by Proposition
2.6
we have
\ell(\lambda^{i})\geq\ell(\mu^{i})
and so
\ell(\lambda)\geq m
.

Define
\tilde{\mu}^{i}
to be the partition of length
\ell(\lambda^{i})
such that
\tilde{\mu}^{i}_{(j)}=\mu_{(j)}^{i}
for
j=1,\dots,\ell(\mu^{i})
, and
\tilde{\mu}^{i}_{(j)}=r
for all
j>\ell(\mu^{i})
, where
r
is larger than any part of
\lambda^{i}
and
\mu^{i}
for
i=1,2
. So
\tilde{\mu}^{i}\prec^{\mathbf{w}}\lambda^{i}
and, using

MOA79
, 5.A.7]
: if

on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
a\prec^{\mathbf{w}}b
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
then
(x,a)\prec^{\mathbf{w}}(y,b)
on
\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
, we obtain
\tilde{\mu}\prec^{\mathbf{w}}\lambda
where
\tilde{\mu}:=\tilde{\mu}^{1}\tilde{\mu}^{2}
. Therefore

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{(j)}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\tilde{\mu}_{(j)}

for each

, and in particular for each

. But then
\lambda\succeq\mu
because by construction of
\tilde{\mu}
, the parts
\tilde{\mu}_{(1)},\dots,\tilde{\mu}_{(m)}
are all smaller than

, so they must be precisely the parts of
\mu
.
∎

The following is a cancellation property of the superdominance order.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\vdash d
and
\nu
be any partition. Then
\lambda\succeq\mu\iff\lambda\nu\succeq\mu\nu
.

Proof.

The forward implication follows from Lemma
2.11
. For the other implication it is enough to consider the case
\ell(\nu)=1
, so let
\nu=(t)
with
t\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. If
t
occurs first in
\mu\nu
than in
\lambda\nu
, i.e., if
t=\mu\nu_{(i)}
and
t=\lambda\nu_{(j)}
with
i\leq j
, then we can cancel
t
without altering the order. The only remaining case is when
t=\mu\nu_{(i)}
and
t=\lambda\nu_{(j)}
with
i>j
. If
t\geq\lambda_{1}
then, since both partitions are of the same size, it is clear we can cancel
t
from both partitions, and the inequality is preserved. So suppose
\lambda_{(j-1)}\leq t<\lambda_{(j)}
. For each

denote
\check{\lambda}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\lambda_{(i)}
, and define
\check{\lambda}_{j}=|\lambda|
for all

. Since
\lambda\nu\succeq\mu\nu
we have

\displaystyle\check{\lambda}_{j-1}+t+\lambda_{(j)}

\displaystyle\leq\check{\mu}_{j-1}+\mu_{(j)}+\mu\nu_{(j+1)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\check{\lambda}_{j-1}+\lambda_{(j)}

\displaystyle\leq\check{\mu}_{j-1}+\mu_{(j)}+\mu\nu_{(j+1)}-t

\displaystyle\leq\check{\mu}_{j-1}+\mu_{(j)}

the last inequality because
\mu\nu_{(j+1)}\leq t
. Hence
\check{\lambda_{j}}\leq\check{\mu_{j}}
, and similarly
\check{\lambda}_{k}\leq\check{\mu}_{k}
as long as
\mu\nu_{(k+1)}\leq t
. But after
t
has appeared in both sides of the inequalities
\check{\lambda\nu}_{r}\leq\check{\mu\nu}_{r}
then the inequalities
\check{\lambda}_{r}\leq\check{\mu}_{r}
are immediate because we can just cancel
t
on both sides.
∎

3.
Limit cones

In this section, we analyze the cones of homogeneous nonnegative symmetric functions and sums of squares, i.e. the symmetric functions which are nonnegative/sums of squares in any number of variables. In Subsection
3.1
we show that these cones are full-dimensional. Subsection
3.3
is dedicated to studying symmetric quartics, where we identify test sets for determining whether a symmetric quartic is nonnegative or a sum of squares for all number of variables (Theorem
3.5
). Moreover, we present a family of nonnegative polynomials that are never sums of squares for any number of variables
n\geq 4
(Theorem
3.6
). This shows that the cone of symmetric sum of squares quartics is strictly contained in the cone of symmetric nonnegative quartics, in contrast to the term-normalized case
[
BR21
, Theorem 2.9]
.
Finally, in Subsection
3.4
, we show that for all higher degrees, the cone of symmetric sums of squares is also strictly contained in the cone of symmetric nonnegative forms (Theorem
3.12
).

3.1.
Sums of squares and nonnegative symmetric functions

Definition 3.1
.

The limit sets of sums of squares and nonnegative symmetric forms in
n
variables of degree
2d
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}:=\bigcap_{n\geq 2d}\Sigma_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}:=\bigcap_{n\geq 2d}\mathcal{P}_{n,{2d}}^{\mathcal{S}}~.

We consider the sets
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d},\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
as convex cones in the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{\pi(2d)}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The sets
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}
and
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
are full-dimensional pointed closed convex cones and

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Sigma_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}~,\quad\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}

are the Kuratowski limits.

Proof.

To show that the limit sets defined in Definition
3.1
are indeed the Kuratowski limits of the sequences of convex sets
(\Sigma_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}})_{n}
and
(\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}})_{n}
we need to prove the inclusions

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\Sigma_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}\subset\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}\subset\liminf_{n}\Sigma_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}},\quad\limsup_{n}\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}\subset\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}\subset\liminf_{n}\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}

which follow from the nestedness of the sequences.

The limit sets are closed, pointed convex cones as intersections of closed, pointed convex cones. We need to show that they are also full-dimensional.
The homogeneous component of the ring of symmetric functions of degree
2d
is spanned by the set

(3.1)

\displaystyle\{\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}\mathfrak{p}_{\mu}:\lambda,\mu\vdash d\}\cup\{\mathfrak{p}_{a+b}\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}\mathfrak{p}_{\mu}:1\leqslant a,b\leqslant d,\lambda\vdash d-a,\mu\vdash d-b\}.

This is because the partitions
\omega
of
2d
can be divided into two types: those that are the fusions of partitions
\lambda,\mu\vdash d
, and those that are the fusion of three partitions
(a+b),\lambda,\mu
for some integers
1\leqslant a,b\leqslant d
and
\lambda\vdash d-a,\mu\vdash d-b
. To see this, let
\omega\vdash 2d
be a partition that is not the fusion of two partitions of
d
. Let
0\leqslant k\leqslant d-1
be the maximal integer such that
\omega
is the fusion of a partition
\mu^{1}\vdash k
and a partition
\nu\vdash 2d-k\geq d+1
. We have
2d-k-\nu_{1}<d
since otherwise
d\geq k+\nu_{1}
, and since
\lambda
does not contain a partition of
d
, we have
d>k+\nu_{1}
which contradicts the maximality of
k
. We have
\nu=(\nu_{1})\mu^{2}
for a partition
\mu^{2}\vdash 2d-k-\nu_{1}
. We obtain the fusion representation of
\omega
by setting
a=d-k,b=k+\nu_{1}-d
and have
\omega=(a+b)\mu^{1}\mu^{2}
, where
1\leqslant a,b\leqslant d
and
\mu^{1}\vdash d-a,\mu^{2}\vdash d-b
.

We show that
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}
is full-dimensional by proving that any element from (
3.1
) is contained in the linear span of
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}
. Note that
\mathfrak{p}_{(\lambda,\lambda)}
,
\mathfrak{p}_{(\mu,\mu)}
,
(\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}-\mathfrak{p}_{\mu})^{2}=\mathfrak{p}_{(\lambda,\lambda)}-2\mathfrak{p}_{(\lambda,\mu)}+\mathfrak{p}_{(\mu,\mu)}\in\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}
which implies
\{\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}\mathfrak{p}_{\mu}:\lambda,\mu\vdash d\}
is contained the linear span of
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}
.

For some integers
1\leqslant a,b\leqslant d
and partitions
\lambda\vdash d-a,~\mu\vdash d-b
we have
(X_{1}^{a}-X_{2}^{a})p_{\lambda},(X_{1}^{b}-X_{2}^{b})p_{\mu}\in H_{n,d}
are equivariants of the Specht module
\mathbb{S}^{(n-1,1)}
. For
a\neq b
we have

\displaystyle f_{1}:=\frac{n}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\frac{1}{n!}\sigma\cdot\left((x_{1}^{a}-x_{2}^{a})^{2}p_{\lambda}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\quad p_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}\left(p_{2a}-\frac{p_{(a,a)}-p_{2a}}{n-1}\right),

\displaystyle f_{2}:=\frac{n}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\frac{1}{n!}\sigma\cdot\left((x_{1}^{a}-x_{2}^{a})(x_{1}^{b}-x_{2}^{b})p_{\lambda}p_{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=\quad p_{\lambda}p_{\mu}\left(p_{a+b}-\frac{p_{(a,b)}-p_{a+b}}{2(n-1)}\right),

\displaystyle f_{3}:=\frac{n}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\frac{1}{n!}\sigma\cdot\left((x_{1}^{a}-x_{2}^{a})(x_{1}^{a}-x_{2}^{a})p_{\lambda}p_{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle=\quad p_{\lambda}p_{\mu}\left(p_{2a}-\frac{p_{(a,a)}-p_{2a}}{n-1}\right).

In particular, for any integer
n\geq 2d
we have by
[
BR21
, Theorem 4.15]

\displaystyle f_{1}-2f_{2}+p_{(\mu,\mu)}\left(p_{2b}-\frac{p_{(b,b)}-p_{2b}}{n-1}\right),\quad f_{1}-f_{3}+p_{(\mu,\mu)}\left(p_{2a}-\frac{p_{(a,a)}-p_{2a}}{n-1}\right)\in\Sigma_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}.

Thus,
\mathfrak{p}_{2a}\mathfrak{p}_{(\lambda,\lambda)}-2\mathfrak{p}_{a+b}\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}\mathfrak{p}_{\mu}+\mathfrak{p}_{2b}\mathfrak{p}_{(\mu,\mu)}
,
\mathfrak{p}_{2a}\mathfrak{p}_{(\lambda,\lambda)}-2\mathfrak{p}_{2a}\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}\mathfrak{p}_{\mu}+\mathfrak{p}_{2a}\mathfrak{p}_{(\mu,\mu)}\in\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}
. Since also
\mathfrak{p}_{(\lambda,\lambda)}
,
\mathfrak{p}_{2a}\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}
, the cone
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}
spans the same linear space as the set (
3.1
) which shows that the cones
\mathcal{S}\Sigma_{2d}\subset\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
are full-dimensional.
∎

It can be seen more directly that the limit cones
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}
,
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
are full-dimensional in the graded component of even symmetric functions of degree
2d
. Any even power sum is already a sum of squares. Therefore, in the basis of even power sums, the convex cone
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}
contains the nonnegative orthant as a full-dimensional subcone.

3.2.
Dual cones and the Vandermonde map

Let
K
be a cone in a finite-dimensional real vector space
V
. The
dual cone
to
K
is

\displaystyle K^{*}:=\{\ell\in V^{*}:\ell(v)\geq 0\text{ for all }v\in K\}

where
V^{*}
is the dual vector space to
V
.

As explained in
[
AB24
, Section 4]
,
(\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}})^{*}
and
(\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{B}})^{*}
are the convex hulls of all point evaluations. When working with the power sum basis, the point evaluations can be identified with the sets
\nu_{2d}(\mathcal{M}_{n,2d})
and
\nu_{d}(\mathcal{N}_{n,d})
, respectively. By homogeneity, we can restrict to the fiber obtained by setting
p_{2}=1
. Since these are compact sets that do not contain the origin, their convex conical hulls are closed cones. Consequently, we have
(\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}})^{*}=\cone(\nu_{2d}(\mathcal{M}_{n,2d}))
and
(\mathcal{P}_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{B}})^{*}=\cone(\nu_{d}(\mathcal{N}_{n,d}))
.

Remark 3.3
.

Observe that
\mathcal{M}_{d}
and
\mathcal{N}_{d}
are
weighted homogeneous sets
, i.e. if
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
then
(ta_{1},t^{2}a_{2},\dots,t^{d}a_{d})\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and similarly for
\mathcal{N}_{d}
with
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Further observe that the first coordinate of
\mathcal{M}_{d}
is free (but it is not the case for
\mathcal{N}_{d}
), which is to say
\mathcal{M}_{d}
is a
prism
with respect to the first coordinate, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}_{d}=\mathcal{M}_{d}+\spn(1,0,\dots,0)
. To see this let
a\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the sequence
x_{n}=(\frac{a}{n},\frac{a}{n},\ldots,\frac{a}{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so

\mathcal{M}_{d}\ni\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})(x_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(a,\frac{a^{2}}{n},\ldots,\frac{a^{d}}{n^{d-1}})=(a,0,\ldots,0)

which follows since the set
\mathcal{M}_{d}
is closed under addition (see Lemma
4.11
).

Lemma 3.4
.

For any degree
d
we have
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*}=\cone(\nu_{2d}(\M_{2d}))
and
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*}=\cone(\nu_{d}(\mathcal{N}_{d}))
.

Proof.

We restrict to the cone
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*}
since the proof for
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*}
works analogously.
The convex cone
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
is closed by Theorem
3.2
, and a symmetric function lies in
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
if and only if it is nonnegative on
\nu_{2d}(\mathcal{M}_{n,2d})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
which is equivalent to the nonnegativity on the closure of the union of those sets. Therefore, we have
\cone(\nu_{2d}(\mathcal{M}_{2d}))^{*}=\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
. Moreover,
\cone(\nu_{2d}(\mathcal{M}_{2d}))
is closed, since the fiber of
\nu_{2d}(\mathcal{M}_{2d})
by
\mathfrak{p}_{2}=1
is a compact set (
[
Kos07
, Prop. 4]
) whose convex hull does not contain the origin, and its convex conical hull is
\cone(\nu_{2d}(\mathcal{M}_{2d}))
. This shows the claim by conical duality.
∎

3.3.
Symmetric quartics at infinity

For quartics, we present test sets for verifying nonnegativity and being a sum of squares in Theorem
3.5
. This allows us to construct a symmetric nonnegative quartic that is not a sum of squares for any number of variables
n\geq 4
(Theorem
3.6
).

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f(\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2},\mathfrak{p}_{3},\mathfrak{p}_{4})
be a homogeneous even symmetric function of degree
4

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\left(x_{i}^{4},x_{i}^{2},\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}_{i}},1,\frac{1}{n_{i}}\right)=\left(x^{4},x^{2},\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}},1,\frac{1}{n}\right)

\mathcal{X}
. Recall that any subset of distinct points on the real moment curve
t\mapsto(t,t^{2})
is in convex position (
[
Zie12
, Prop. 0.7]
). Looking at the first two coefficients of the elements of any point we obtain that all
x_{i}
are equal up to sign.
Then we consider the third and last coordinates in the convex combination and conclude
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{i}\sqrt{n}_{i}},\frac{1}{n_{i}})=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{1}{n})
, where
\epsilon_{i}:=1
if
x_{i}=x
, and
\epsilon_{i}:=-1
otherwise. Again, these are points on the moment curve
(t,t^{2})
.
This implies
n_{i}=n
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
and either all
\epsilon_{i}
are
1
or
-1
.

Therefore, the set
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}^{*}
has infinitely many isolated extremal rays.
Thus the set
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{4}^{*}
cannot be semialgebraic (see
[
Ace+23
, Corollary 2.20]
).
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

The dual cone of a semialgebraic set

is semialgebraic, i.e., the set
S^{*}=\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}w_{i}\geq 0,\forall w\in S\}

Proof.

We observe

\displaystyle S^{*}

\displaystyle=\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\exists w\in S,\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}w_{i}<0\}^{c}=\pi\left(\{(a,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:w\in S,\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}w_{i}<0\}\right)^{c},

Proposition 3.14

For any even degree

the cones
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{P}_{2d}
and
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}

Proof.

By

an

if and only if

Now let

4.
Tropicalization of
\mathcal{N}_{d}
and proof of Theorem
2.3

Our main result in this section is a full description of the tropicalization of the Vandermonde cell
\mathcal{N}_{d}
(Theorem
4.5
).
Utilizing the polyhedral representation of
\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d})
, we then prove Theorem
2.3
, i.e. that valid pure binomial inequalities in power sums on the nonnegative orthant are captured by the superdominance order.

The
tropicalization
, or
logarithmic limit
, of a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
has often been called a
combinatorial shadow
of it, given some of the structural properties it retains from
S
while being an apparently simpler geometrical object.

For a scalar
a>0
we consider the
logarithm map

\log_{a}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}\to\mathbb{R}^{s},{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})}\mapsto{\left(\log_{a}(x_{1}),\ldots,\log_{a}(x_{s})\right)}.

We write
\log:=\log_{e}
and
\trop:\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{s}
denotes the
tropicalization
which sends
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}
to the limit set
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\log_{t}(S)
. We extend this definition for sets
S\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{s}
where
\log\left(S\right):=\log\left(S\cap\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}\right)
and
\trop(S):=\trop(S\cap\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s})
. The set
\trop(S)
is a closed cone for any set
S\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{s}
and
y\in\trop(S)
if and only if there exist sequences
(x^{(n)})\in S\cap\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}
and
(\tau_{n})\in(0,1)
with
\tau_{n}\to 0
for
n\to\infty
such that
y=\lim_{n\to\infty}\log_{\frac{1}{\tau_{n}}}(x^{(n)})
(
[
Ale13
, Proposition 2.2]
).

Definition 4.1
.

A set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
the Hadamard property
if whenever
u
and
v
belong to
S
then their Hadamard product
u\circ v:=(u_{1}v_{1},\dots,u_{n}v_{n})
also lies in
S
.

In other words, a set has the Hadamard property if it is closed under coordinatewise multiplication.

Given its logarithmic nature, tropicalization captures
pure binomial inequalities
valid on subsets of the nonnegative orthant, i.e. if

x^{\alpha}=\prod_{i=1}^{s}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\geq x^{\beta}=\prod_{i=1}^{s}x_{i}^{\beta_{i}}

is valid on
S\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{s}
, then the linear inequality

\alpha\cdot y=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}y_{i}\geq\beta\cdot y=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}y_{i}

is valid on
\trop(S)
. Furthermore, when
S
has the Hadamard property,
\trop(S)
is a closed convex cone that is determined by the pure binomial inequalities valid on
S

[
Ble+22
, Lemma 2.2]
. More precisely, the extreme rays of the dual cone
\trop(S)^{*}
generate all of the pure binomial inequalities valid on
S

[
BR22
]
. This idea was recently fruitfully applied in extremal graph theory
[
Ble+22
,
BR22
]
where it was used to understand pure binomial inequalities in homomorphism numbers and densities from the tropicalizations of graph homomorphism profiles. It was also used in real algebraic geometry to study the cones of nonnegative and sums of squares polynomials
[
Ble+22a
,
AB24
]
.

The set
\mathcal{N}_{d}
is closed under addition and has the Hadamard property (Lemma
4.11
). A compact level set of it has the structure of a
d
-dimensional
infinite curvy cyclic polytope
(see
[
Ace+23
]
for context). However, its tropicalization turns out to have a much simpler description.

Remark 4.2
.

We show in Theorem
4.5
that all binomial inequalities valid on
\mathcal{N}_{d}
come from two families of classical inequalities on power sums, namely

(1)

p_{2k}^{k+1}\geq p_{2k+2}^{k}
and

(2)

p_{2k}\cdot p_{2k+4}\geq p_{2k+2}^{2}
for all positive integer
k
.

The former comes from the monotonicity of
\ell^{p}
-norms and the latter from Lyapunov’s inequality (
[
HLP52
, Sections 2.9 and 2.10]
).

Denote by
\oplus
the
tropical addition
, i.e. for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

x\oplus y:=\left(\max{(x_{1},y_{1})},\ldots,\max{(x_{n},y_{n})}\right),

and by
\odot
the
tropical scalar multiplication
, i.e. for
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

a\odot x:=(a+x_{1},\ldots,a+x_{n}).

Definition 4.3
.

A set
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
max-closed
if
x\oplus y\in M
for all
x,y\in M
.
The
max-closure

\overline{M}
of
M
is the smallest max-closed set containing
M
.

Observe that the max-closure of a set
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the intersection of all max-closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing
M
. Since
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a max-closed set containing
M
the max-closure
\overline{M}
is well-defined.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The
double hull
of
S
is defined as the smallest closed and max-closed convex cone containing
S
. We write
\dhu(S)
for the double hull of
S
.

Theorem 4.5
.

The set
\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d})
is the double hull of the vectors
(1,\dots,1)
,
(1,2,\dots,d)
and
-(1,2,\dots,d)
. It is a rational polyhedral cone with the facet-defining inequalities:

\displaystyle\trop\left(\mathcal{N}_{d}\right)=\left\{\left.y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\begin{cases}y_{k}+y_{k+2}\geq 2y_{k+1}\;,\quad k=1,\ldots,d-2\\
dy_{d-1}\geq(d-1)y_{d}\;.\end{cases}\right\}\right\}.

Moreover,
\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d})
is the Minkowski sum of the linear span of
(1,2,\ldots,d)
and the convex cone with the
d-1
extremal rays
(1,0,\ldots,0),(2,1,0,\ldots,0),\ldots,(d-2,d-3,\ldots,1,0,0)
and
(1,\ldots,1)
.

Before proving the Theorem we study some general properties of tropicalizations.

4.1.
Properties of max-closed sets

We prove some general properties of max-closed sets, and in particular, examine the structure of extremal rays of convex cones that are max-closed.

In the following lemma, we provide a description of the max-closure of sets
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
which contain a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
in their
lineality space
, i.e.,
x+\lambda v\in M
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and all
x\in M
. We denote by
\mathcal{Q}_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the orthant where the
i
-th coordinate is non-positive and any other nonnegative, i.e.,

\mathcal{Q}_{i}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,x_{i}\leqslant 0,x_{j}\geq 0\,~\forall j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}\}.

The following is a variant of what has been called the
tropical Farkas lemma

[
BR22
, Lemma 2.6]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
v\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
be contained in the lineality space of
M
. Then, the max-closure of
M
equals
\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}(M+\mathcal{Q}_{i})
.

We write
\widehat{M}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}(M+\mathcal{Q}_{i})
.

Proof.

First, we prove that any
x\in\widehat{M}
is the tropical sum of elements in
M
, i.e.
x=y_{1}\oplus y_{2}\oplus\ldots\oplus y_{m}
for some
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\in M
. Since
x\in\widehat{M}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
there exist
y_{i}=(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{in})\in M
and
g_{i}=(g_{i1},\ldots,g_{in})\in\mathcal{Q}_{i}
such that
x=y_{i}+g_{i}
. Since
g_{ii}\leqslant 0,g_{ij}\geq 0
for
j\neq i
we have
y_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus y_{n}=(y_{11},\ldots,y_{nn})
and
x_{i}\leqslant y_{ii}
. Suppose that for some
i\in[n]
we actually have
y_{ii}>x_{i}
, and let
\lambda>0
be such that
x_{i}=y_{ii}-\lambda v_{i}
. By assumption
\widetilde{y}_{i}:=y_{i}-\lambda v\in M
and
x=\widetilde{y}_{i}+g_{i}+\lambda v\in M+\mathcal{Q}_{i}
since
g_{ii}+\lambda v_{i}=0
and any other coordinate is positive. Recursively, we obtain the existence of
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in M
with
x=y_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus y_{n}
.

Second, we prove that any tropical sum of elements in
M
is contained in
\widehat{M}
. Let
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\in M
and
x:=y_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus y_{k}
. For any
j\in[n]
let
i_{j}\in[k]
be such that
x_{j}=y_{i_{j}j}
. Since
x_{k}\geq y_{i_{j}k}
for any
k\in[n]
we have
x\in y_{i_{j}}+\mathcal{Q}_{j}
. In particular,
x\in\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}(M+\mathcal{Q}_{j})
.

Finally, we show that
\widehat{M}
is max-closed. For vectors
a,b\in\widehat{M}
we have already seen that these vectors can be written as tropical sums of elements of
M
. Then
a\oplus b
is the tropical sum of all those elements and lies in
\widehat{M}
, since
\widehat{M}
is closed under tropical summation with elements in
M
.

Thus,
\widehat{M}
is max-closed and the elements in
\widehat{M}
are precisely the tropical sums of elements in
M
which proves the claim.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex cone containing some vector
v\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
in its lineality space. Then, the max-closure of
S
is a convex cone.

Proof.

The max-closure of
S
is the intersection of convex cones by Lemma
4.6
. Thus the max-closure is again a convex cone.
∎

We denote the all ones vector
(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
\mathbf{1}
.

Definition 4.8
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a set. We define the
tropical conical hull
of
M
as the set of all tropical linear combinations of points in
M
. We write
\tcone(M)
for the tropical conical hull of
M
, i.e.,

\tcone(M)=\{(a_{1}\odot x_{1})\oplus\ldots\oplus(a_{l}\odot x_{l})\,\,\mid\,\,l\in\mathbb{N},a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}\in\mathbb{R},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in M\}.

M
is called
tropically convex
if

equals its tropical conical hull.

We refer to (
[
DS04
, Proposition 4]
) for a proof of the set theoretical description of

in Definition

.
The set

is the max-closure of

. This follows from the fact that
Z:=M+\mathbb{R}\cdot\mathbf{1}
contains the vector
\mathbf{1}
in its lineality space and

(4.1)

\displaystyle\tcone(M)=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}(M+\mathbb{R}\cdot\mathbf{1}+\mathcal{Q}_{j})

.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed convex cone containing a point from

in its lineality space. Then, the linear inequalities characterizing the max-closure of

where we use

and

for closed convex cones

Lemma 4.11
.

The sets
\mathcal{M}_{d}
and
\mathcal{N}_{d}
are closed under addition, and have the Hadamard property.

Proof.

We show that the set
\mathcal{M}_{d}
satisfies both properties and analogous arguments work for
\mathcal{N}_{d}

Let
u,v\in\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\nu_{n,d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, so
u=\nu_{m,d}(x)
and
v=\nu_{n,d}(y)
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Hence
u+v=\nu_{m+n,d}(x,y)\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
where
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
is the Cartesian product of
x
and
y
. Hence the set
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\nu_{n,d}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\mathcal{M}_{d}
.

Also
u\circ v=\nu_{mn,d}(x\otimes y)\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
, where
x\otimes y:=(x_{1}y_{1},x_{1}y_{2},\dots,x_{1}y_{n},x_{2}y_{1},\dots,x_{m}y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
is the
Kronecker product
of the row vectors
x
and
y
. Hence the set
\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu_{k,d}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
has the Hadamard property, and, by continuity of the coordinatewise product, also its closure
\mathcal{M}_{d}
.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

The set
\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d})

2\lambda(1,2,\ldots,d)=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\log_{\frac{1}{\tau_{m}}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\tau_{m}^{\lambda}}\right)^{2},\left(\frac{1}{\tau_{m}^{\lambda}}\right)^{4},\ldots,\left(\frac{1}{\tau_{m}^{\lambda}}\right)^{2d}\right)

Proof of Theorem
4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
denote the polyhedron on the right-hand side in the theorem. We first prove

and divide its proof into three steps.

Claim 1:

\trop\left(\mathcal{N}_{d}\right)\subset\mathcal{Q}
.

By (
[
Ble+22

) we have
\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d})=\clo\left(\mbox{conv}(\log(\mathcal{N}_{d}))\right)
since
\mathcal{N}_{d}
has Hadamard property. Thus, by taking logarithms the families of binomial inequalities in power sums

p_{2k}\cdot p_{2k+4}\geq p_{2k+2}^{2}\mbox{ and }p_{2k}^{k+1}\geq p_{2k+2}^{k}

from Remark
4.2
transfer to valid linear inequalities on
\log(\mathcal{N}_{d})
which are preserved under taking the convex hull. We have

(k+1)\cdot(y_{k}-2y_{k+1}+y_{k+2})+((k+2)y_{k+1}-(k+1)y_{k+2})=(k+1)y_{k}-ky_{k+1}

for all

which shows that

Lemma 5.2

, Lemma 2.2 (2)]
) Claim 1 is equivalent to

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}_{d}\left(\clo(\conv(\log(\mathcal{N}_{d})))\right)=\clo\left(\conv(\log(\nu_{d}(\mathcal{N}_{d})))\right).

However,

\displaystyle\clo(\conv(\log(\nu_{d}(\mathcal{N}_{d}))))=\clo(\conv(\tilde{\nu}_{d}(\log(\mathcal{N}_{d}))))=\clo(\tilde{\nu}_{d}(\conv(\log(\mathcal{N}_{d})))),

where the first equality follows from the definition of
\log
and the second equality follows because taking convex hull and applying a linear map commute.
Moreover, since
\tilde{\nu}_{d}
is injective and linear we have
\tilde{\nu}_{d}(\clo(A))=\clo(\tilde{\nu}_{d}(A))
for all sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{\pi(d)}
. Therefore, Claim 1 follows.

Claim 2:

\trop(\cone(S))=\tcone(\trop(S))
for
S=\nu_{d}(\mathcal{N}_{d}).

This follows from Proposition
5.1
since the set
S
has Hadamard property by Lemma
5.3
.

Finally, by combining the assertions from Claim 1 and Claim 2 we have

\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})=\trop(\cone(\nu_{d}(\mathcal{N}_{d})))=\tcone(\trop(\nu_{d}(\mathcal{N}_{d})))=\tcone(\tilde{\nu}_{d}(\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d}))).

∎

Corollary 5.5
.

The set
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
is a rational polyhedral cone and tropically convex with lineality space spanned by
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\pi(d)}
. Moreover, any extreme ray of
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})^{*}
has exactly one negative coordinate.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
4.5
and Proposition
5.4
that the cone
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
is rational polyhedral and tropically convex.

Also observe that the
d-1
facet-defining inequalities of
\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, given by Theorem
4.5
, are linearly independent, so the lineality space of
\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d})
is one-dimensional, and one can see it is the line
\spn(1,2,\dots,d)
(because there all inequalities are tight). This implies that the lineality space of
\tilde{\nu}(\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d}))
is the line
\spn(1,\dots,1)=\tilde{\nu}(\spn(1,2,\dots,d))
in
\mathbb{R}^{\pi(d)}
, because
\tilde{\nu}
is injective. Now,
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
is the tropical conical hull of
\tilde{\nu}(\trop(\mathcal{N}_{d}))
(Proposition
5.4
), which preserves the lineality space.

Since the lineality space of
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
is the line spanned by
\mathbf{1}
then
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})^{*}
is contained in the orthogonal complement of
\spn\mathbf{1}
, which is the subspace where the coordinates add up to zero. But, by Lemma
4.6
, the extreme rays of
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})^{*}
have at most one negative coordinate, and then it must be exactly one negative coordinate.
∎

5.2.
Characterization of
\operatorname{trop}(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
by the superdominance order

Using the superdominance order, we define a decreasing chain of polyhedra
T_{2d}^{(k)}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\pi(d)}
. We prove that this sequence stabilizes and that it does so at the tropicalization of
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*}
(see Theorem
5.9
).

Definition 5.6
.

For a positive integer
k
let
T_{2d}^{(k)}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\pi(d)}
denote the cone given by the intersection of the half-spaces

y_{\lambda^{1}}+\dots+y_{\lambda^{k}}\geq ky_{\mu}

where
\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{k},\mu\in\Lambda_{2d}^{\varepsilon}
and
\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}\succeq\mu^{\circ k}
.

Example 5.7
.

We have
T_{6}^{(1)}=\{(y_{(2^{3})},y_{(4,2)},y_{(6)})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,\,\mid\,\,y_{(2^{3})}\geq y_{(4,2)}\geq y_{(6)}\}
since
(2^{3})\succ(4,2)\succ(6)
. Furthermore, we find that
T_{6}^{(2)}=\left\{(y_{(2^{3})},y_{(4,2)},y_{(6)})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,\,\mid\,\,y_{(2^{3})}+y_{(6)}\geq 2y_{(4,2)},\,y_{(4,2)}\geq y_{(6)}\right\}
is equal to
\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{6}^{*})
(see Example
5.15
).

By the fusion lemma (Lemma
2.12
), the sequence
(T_{2d}^{(k)})_{k\geq 1}
is nested non-increasing, i.e.,
T_{2d}^{(k)}\supseteq T_{2d}^{(k+1)}
for each positive integer
k
. Now we show that this sequence stabilizes, and the stabilization is
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{k},\mu^{1},\dots,\mu^{k}\in\Lambda_{2d}^{\varepsilon}
. If the inequality
y_{\lambda^{1}}+\dots+y_{\lambda^{k}}\geq y_{\mu^{1}}+\dots+y_{\mu^{k}}
is valid on
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
, then
\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}\succeq\mu^{1}\cdots\mu^{k}
.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*}\supset\nu(\mathcal{N}_{d})
, so
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})\supset\trop(\nu(\mathcal{N}_{d}))
. Hence

(*)\,\,y_{\lambda^{1}}+\dots+y_{\lambda^{k}}\geq y_{\mu^{1}}+\dots+y_{\mu^{k}}

valid on
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
implies that (
*
) valid on
\trop(\nu(\mathcal{N}_{d}))
. Now
\nu(\mathcal{N}_{d})\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\pi(d)}
has the Hadamard property, so Lemma 2.2 of
[
Ble+22
]
implies
\trop(\nu(\mathcal{N}_{d}))=\clo(\cone(\log(\nu(\mathcal{N}_{d}))))
. Therefore (
*
) is valid on
\log(\nu(\mathcal{N}_{d}))
. This means that
\log(p_{\lambda^{1}})+\dots+\log(p_{\lambda^{k}})\geq\log(p_{\mu^{1}})+\dots+\log(p_{\mu^{k}})
, hence
p_{\lambda^{1}}\cdots p_{\lambda^{k}}\geq p_{\mu^{1}}\cdots p_{\mu^{k}}
, i.e.
p_{\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}}\geq p_{\mu^{1}\cdots\mu^{k}}
by multiplicativeness of power sums, and so by Theorem
2.3
we obtain
\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}\succeq\mu^{1}\cdots\mu^{k}
.
∎

Theorem 5.9
.

For each positive integer
d\geq 3
there exists a positive integer
\tau_{d}\geq 2
such that
T_{2d}^{(k)}=T_{2d}^{(\tau_{d})}
for all
k\geq\tau_{d}
,
T_{2d}^{(\tau_{d}-1)}\supsetneq T_{2d}^{(\tau_{d})}
, and
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})=T_{2d}^{(\tau_{d})}
.

Proof.

We divide the proof into three steps.

In (i) we show that every inequality of the form
y_{\lambda^{1}}+\dots+y_{\lambda^{k}}\geq ky_{\mu}
, such that
\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}\succeq\mu^{\circ k}
, and
k\geq 1
, is valid on
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
.

In (ii) we show that each facet of
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
is of the form
y_{\lambda^{1}}+\dots+y_{\lambda^{k}}\geq ky_{\mu}
with
\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}\succeq\mu^{\circ k}
for some
k\geq 1
.

Finally in (iii) we show that
\tau_{d}\geq 2
.

This suffices because we have
T_{2d}^{(k)}\subset\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
for all
k
, by (i) and since the rational polyhedron
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
has a finite number of facets, there exists an upper bound on the integer
\tau_{d}
by (ii) depending on
d
. Combining these observations with (iii) we can deduce the claimed statement.

(i) Let
\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{k},\mu\in\Lambda_{2d}^{\varepsilon}
such that
\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}\succeq\mu^{\circ k}
. By Theorem
2.3
then
p_{\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}}\geq p_{\mu^{\circ k}}
, or equivalently, by the multiplicativeness of power sums,
p_{\lambda^{1}}\cdots p_{\lambda^{k}}\geq p_{\mu}^{k}
. This is a binomial inequality which is valid on
\nu(\mathcal{N}_{d})
, and, by Lemma
5.2
, it is precisely the kind of binomial inequality that remains valid on
\cone\nu(\mathcal{N}_{d})=\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*}
, therefore
y_{\lambda^{1}}+\dots+y_{\lambda^{k}}\geq ky_{\mu}

is valid on
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
.

(ii) By Corollary
5.5
the extreme rays of
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})^{*}
have exactly one negative coordinate and the coordinates add up to zero. Further, one can take an integer point on each extreme ray since
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
is a rational polyhedral cone, therefore the facets of
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
are of the form
y_{\lambda^{1}}+\dots+y_{\lambda^{k}}\geq ky_{\mu}
, where
\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{k},\mu\in\Lambda_{2d}^{\varepsilon}
(observe that the
\lambda^{i}
are not necessarily distinct).
By Lemma
5.8
if
y_{\lambda^{1}}+\dots+y_{\lambda^{k}}\geq ky_{\mu}
is valid on
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
then
\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}\succeq\mu^{\circ k}
.

(iii) For
d\geq 3
it holds that
(2^{d})\succ(4,2^{d-2})\succ(6,2^{d-3})
and
(2^{d})(6,2^{d-3})\succ(4,2^{d-2})(4,2^{d-2})
, so
y_{(2^{d})}+y_{(6,2^{d-3})}\geq 2y_{(4,2^{d-2})}
is not implied by the inequalities in
T_{2d}^{(1)}
since these are just
y_{\lambda}\geq y_{\mu}
if and only if
\lambda\succeq\mu
. Therefore
T_{2d}^{(1)}\supsetneq T_{2d}^{(2)}
, and so
\tau_{d}\geq 2
.
∎

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{k},\mu^{1},\dots,\mu^{k}\in\Lambda_{2d}^{\varepsilon}
. If
\lambda^{1}\cdots\lambda^{k}\not\succeq\mu^{1}\cdots\mu^{k}
then the inequality
y_{\lambda^{1}}+\dots+y_{\lambda^{k}}\geq y_{\mu^{1}}+\dots+y_{\mu^{k}}
is not valid on

for any

.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.9

T_{2d}^{(r)}\supseteq\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
, and so the statement follows from the contrapositive of Lemma
5.8
.
∎

The following proposition is not only essential for proving Theorem

and

for all

in Theorem

Recall that we choose the even partitions of

Proposition 5.11

If

then

, i.e.

Proof.

Denote by

\displaystyle y_{1}

\displaystyle:=y_{(2^{d})},\quad\quad\,\,\,\,\,y_{2}:=y_{(4,2^{d-2})},\quad y_{3}:=y_{(6,2^{d-3})},

5.3.
The tropicalization of
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
and the superdominance order

In this subsection, we nearly describe the set
\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*})
in terms of the superdominance order. First, we prove the technical Lemma
5.14
, which bounds the tropicalization of a
spectrahedral cone
. Second, we discuss how partial symmetry reduction (as in
[
AB24
, Section 3.2]
) can be used to give a spectrahedral representation of
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
that works well for tropicalizing the cone. Third, we combine partial symmetry reduction with the lemma to prove that
\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*})
is a rational polyhedron (Proposition
5.19
), the inclusions
T_{2d}^{(1)}\supseteq\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*})\supseteq T_{2d}^{(2)}
(Proposition
5.20
) and strict containment of
\trop(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{P}_{2d}^{*})
in
\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*})
for all
2d\geq 10
(Theorem
5.21
).

Recall that we work with
\{p_{\lambda}\,\,\mid\,\,\lambda\in\Lambda_{2d}^{\varepsilon}\}
as a linear basis of
H_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{B}_{n}}
. Observe that
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}
contains the nonnegative orthant because every even power sum is a sum of squares, implying that its dual cone is contained in the nonnegative orthant, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\pi(d)}
.

In the following proposition, we observe that the coordinates of points in
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
are partially ordered, corresponding to the superdominance order.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
and
\lambda,\mu\vDash 2d
. If
\lambda\succeq\mu
then
\varphi_{\lambda}\geq\varphi_{\mu}
. In particular,
T_{2d}^{(1)}\supseteq\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*})
.

Proof.

Let
\{\rho_{\nu}\}_{\nu\vDash 2d}
be a dual basis for
\{\mathfrak{p}_{\nu}\}_{\nu\vDash 2d}
and let
\lambda\succeq\mu
. By Corollary
2.14
then
\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}-\mathfrak{p}_{\mu}\in\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}
and so
\varphi(\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}-\mathfrak{p}_{\mu})\geq 0
. Hence

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}-\mathfrak{p}_{\mu})=\sum_{\nu\vDash 2d}\varphi_{\nu}\rho_{\nu}(\mathfrak{p}_{\lambda}-\mathfrak{p}_{\mu})=\varphi_{\lambda}-\varphi_{\mu}\geq 0.

We have
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\pi(d)}
, and
\log
is an increasing function, so
\log\varphi_{\lambda}\geq\log\varphi_{\mu}
and
T_{2d}^{(1)}\supseteq\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*})
.
∎

The sets
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{n,2d}^{*}
have spectrahedral representations that can be computed using symmetry reduction and a symmetry adapted basis
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{(\lambda,\mu),1},\ldots,f_{(\lambda,\mu),m_{(\lambda,\mu)}}\,\,\mid\,\,(\lambda,\mu)\vDash n\}
of
H_{n,d}
(see e.g.
[
BR21
, Sec. 4]
). We present a brief explanation in the Appendix
A
.

Let
\operatorname{sym}_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}:H_{n,d}\to H_{n,d}^{\mathcal{B}},f\mapsto\frac{1}{\mid\mathcal{B}_{n}\mid}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{B}_{n}}\sigma\cdot f
denote the Reynolds operator of the group
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, and define the matrix polynomial

\mathbf{N}_{n}=(\operatorname{sym}_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(f_{(\lambda,\mu),i}f_{(\lambda^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}),j}))_{(\lambda,\mu),i,(\lambda^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}),j},

obtained by the symmetrization of pairwise products of elements in
\mathcal{F}
. Then,
f\in\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{n,2d}
if and only if there exists a positive semidefinite matrix
A
such that
f=\operatorname{Tr}(A\cdot\mathbf{N}_{n})
(see
[
BR21
, Cor. 4.4]
). Thus, the set
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{n,2d}
is the
psd projection
with respect to the matrix
\mathbf{N}_{n}
(recall Definition
3.7
). Moreover, the matrix
\mathbf{N}_{n}
can be block-diagonalized because the symmetrization of the product of two basis elements from two non-isomorphic irreducible representations is zero.

For the hyperoctahedral group (analogously to the symmetric group), one can uniformly choose the matrices
\mathbf{N}_{n}
. This is possible because there exists a symmetry-adapted basis
\mathcal{F}
of
H_{d,d}
, which can be extended to
H_{n,d}
by replacing any power sum in
\mathcal{F}
with the identified power sum in
n\geq d
variables (see Corollary
A.4
for details). Then, for
n\to\infty
any coefficient of the matrix
\mathbf{N}_{n}
converges so that we can speak about the limit
\mathbf{N}
of the matrices. The set
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}
is then the psd projection of
\mathbf{N}
.

The following lemma bounds the tropicalization of a spectrahedral cone contained in the nonnegative orthant. It will be a key ingredient for computing the tropicalization of the cones
\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*}
.

Let
L=(L_{ij}(\underline{X}))_{ij}
an
N\times N
symmetric matrix whose entries are linear forms

L_{ij}(\underline{X})=L_{ji}(\underline{X})=\sum_{k=1}^{s}a_{ijk}X_{k},\quad a_{ijk}\in\mathbb{R},

and let
\ell_{ij}^{+}:=\{k:a_{ijk}>0\},\ell_{ij}^{-}:=\{k:a_{ijk}<0\}
and
\ell_{ij}:=\{k:a_{ijk}\neq 0\}
. We use the convention
\max\{a\in\emptyset\}:=-\infty
.

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
K:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{s}:L(x)\succeq 0\}
and
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}
be the set of points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
satisfying

(1)

\max\{x_{k}:k\in\ell_{ii}^{+}\}\geq\max\{x_{k}:k\in\ell_{ii}^{-}\}
, for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, and

(2)

\max\{x_{k}:k\in\ell_{ii}^{+}\}+\max\{x_{k}:k\in\ell_{jj}^{+}\}\geq 2\max\{x_{k}:k\in\ell_{ij}\}
, for each
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N
.

If for all
v\in\inti(T)
all the inequalities in
(1)
and
(2)
are strict then
\clo\left(\inti(T)\right)\subset\trop\left(K\right)\subset T
.

Proof.

The conditions
(1)
and
(2)
follow for every element

from the positive semidefiniteness of the
1\times 1
and
2\times 2
principal minors of the defining matrix
L
. Thus, we have
\trop\left(K\right)\subset T
.

Next, we prove
\clo\left(\inti(T)\right)\subset\trop(K)
. It suffices to show that any
v\in\inti(T)
is contained in
\trop(K)
, since
\trop(K)
is a closed set (
[
Ale13
, Proposition 2.2]
). Let
v\in\inti(T)
. We show that
t^{v}:=(t^{v_{1}},\ldots,t^{v_{s}})\in K
for all
t>0
sufficiently large. By assumption, all the inequalities in
(1)
and
(2)
are strict at
v
. We consider the diagonal entries of
L
at
t^{v}
. For
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
let
q_{i}\in[n]
be an index at which the maximum over all
v_{j}
with
j\in\ell_{ii}^{+}
is attained. Further be
m_{i}
be the multiplicity of
v_{q_{i}}
in
v
. Then
v_{k}-v_{q_{i}}<0
for all
k
with
a_{iik}<0
. Thus, for all
k\in\ell_{ii}^{-}
we have
t^{v_{k}-v_{q_{i}}}\to 0
for
t\to\infty
. Hence, for sufficiently large
t

\displaystyle\frac{L_{ii}(t^{v})}{t^{v_{q_{i}}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\ell_{ii}}a_{iik}t^{v_{k}-v_{q_{i}}}=\sum_{k\in\ell_{ii}^{+}}a_{iik}t^{v_{k}-v_{q_{i}}}\geq m_{i}a_{iiq_{i}}>0.

Now let
k\geq 2
and consider the Leibniz formula of the leading
k\times k
principal minor of
L(t^{v})
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\mbox{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{k}L_{i\sigma(i)}(t^{v}).

We treat the product of linear forms in
t^{v}
as univariate exponential polynomials in
t
and claim

(5.2)

\displaystyle\deg_{t}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}L_{ii}(t^{v})\right)>\deg_{t}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}L_{i\sigma(i)}(t^{v})\right)

for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mbox{id}\}
. Equivalently to (
5.2
), since
L
is symmetric

\deg_{t}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}L_{ii}^{2}(t^{v})\right)>\deg_{t}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}L_{i\sigma(i)}(t^{v})L_{\sigma(i)i}(t^{v})\right).

The leading coefficient of the univariate exponential polynomial
\prod_{i=1}^{k}L_{ii}^{2}(t^{v})
equals a product of positive coefficients of each
L_{ii}
by assumption (1). Combining (1) and (2) we obtain at
t^{v}

\displaystyle\deg_{t}\left(L_{ii}L_{\sigma(i)\sigma(i)}\right)

\displaystyle=\deg_{t}\left(L_{ii}\right)+\deg_{t}\left(L_{\sigma(i)\sigma(i)}\right)

\displaystyle>2\deg_{t}\left(L_{i\sigma(i)}\right)=\deg_{t}\left(L_{i\sigma(i)}L_{\sigma(i)i}\right).

Therefore,
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\mbox{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{k}L_{i\sigma(i)}(t^{v})>0
for all sufficiently large
t
.
Analogously, for any
k\times k
principal minor of
L(t^{v})
the product of the diagonal entries has a degree larger than the product of any other generalized diagonal obtained from a permutation of the indices. Thus, for all
t
sufficiently large we have
t^{v}\in K
.
∎

Instead of considering full symmetry reduction, we apply the idea of partial symmetry reduction
[
AB24
, Section 3.2]
. Together with the superdominance order this allows us to prove that
\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*})
is always a rational polyhedron.

The idea of partial symmetry reduction is as follows. For
n\geq d
, up to replacing power sums by those in another number of variables, the same symmetry adapted basis of the
\mathcal{B}_{n}
-modules
H_{n,d}
can be used (Corollary
A.4
). Any polynomial in this symmetry-adapted basis can be written as a sum of terms of the form
X^{\alpha}p_{\lambda}
, where
\lambda
is an even partition. The form
p_{\lambda}
is in
n
variables,
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and
\alpha_{d+1}=\ldots=\alpha_{n}=0
, but
\alpha
is not necessarily in
(2\mathbb{N})^{n}
. Therefore, products of any two such terms from symmetry basis elements remain of the same form. We have

\operatorname{sym}_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(X^{\alpha}p_{\lambda})=\operatorname{sym}_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(X^{\alpha})p_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}M_{\alpha}p_{\lambda},&\text{if $\alpha\in(2\mathbb{N})^{n}$},\\
0,&\text{else},\end{cases}

where
M_{\alpha}
is the corresponding term-normalized monomial symmetric polynomial. To
denormalize

M_{\alpha}
we instead rescale the terms as
n^{\ell(\alpha)/2}X^{\alpha}p_{\lambda}
, where
\ell(\alpha)
denotes the size of the support of
X^{\alpha}
. We then express the monomial symmetric polynomial
m_{\alpha}
in the power sum basis. When expressing
p_{\lambda}
in the monomial basis we get precisely
\lambda
and partitions that are refined by
\lambda
which one observes by expanding
p_{\lambda}=(X_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}+\dots)\cdots(X_{1}^{\lambda_{l}}+\dots)
. Therefore
\lambda
is the maximum in the superdominance order among the partitions appearing in
p_{\lambda}
. This implies the same happens for monomial symmetric functions expressed in power sums. Then when expressing
m_{\alpha+\beta}p_{\lambda\mu}
in the power sum basis, the maximum partition in the superdominance order that occurs is the partition
(\alpha+\beta)\lambda\mu
which we verify in Corollary
5.18
.

We verify that the entries of the partial symmetrization matrix are well-defined at the limit when
n\to\infty
. For terms
X^{\alpha}p_{\lambda}
and
X^{\alpha^{\prime}}p_{\lambda^{\prime}}
we define
\varepsilon_{(\alpha,\lambda),(\alpha^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})}
as
1
, if
\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}\in(2\mathbb{N})^{n}
, and otherwise as
0
. For
n
variables this matrix is

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{n}

\displaystyle:=(\varepsilon_{(\alpha,\lambda),(\alpha^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})}\cdot n^{(\ell(\alpha)+\ell(\alpha^{\prime}))/2}M_{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}p_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}})_{(\alpha,\lambda),(\alpha^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\sim(\varepsilon_{(\alpha,\lambda),(\alpha^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})}\cdot n^{(\ell(\alpha)+\ell(\alpha^{\prime}))/2}\frac{c_{\alpha}c_{\alpha^{\prime}}}{n^{\ell(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime})}}m_{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}p_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}})_{(\alpha,\lambda),(\alpha^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})}

since
M_{\alpha}\sim\frac{c_{\alpha}}{n^{\ell(\alpha)}}m_{\alpha}
for a constant
c_{\alpha}>0
depending only on
\alpha
. But
\frac{1}{2}(\ell(\alpha)+\ell(\alpha^{\prime}))\leq\ell(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime})
with equality if and only if
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
have the same support, so we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{sym}_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(n^{(\ell(\alpha)+\ell(\alpha^{\prime}))/2}X^{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}p_{\lambda}p_{\lambda^{\prime}})=0

otherwise. Moreover, as observed above, the symmetrization
\operatorname{sym}_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}(n^{(\ell(\alpha)+\ell(\alpha^{\prime}))/2}X^{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}p_{\lambda}p_{\lambda^{\prime}})
is zero also when
\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}\notin 2\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 5.17

Let
\lambda\vdash k

m_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu\vdash k}E_{\lambda\mu}p_{\mu}
where

is a constant depending only on
\lambda
and
\mu

if and only if
\mu=\lambda
or
\mu
is obtained by successively merging parts of
\lambda

For
\lambda\vDash 2d

A_{\lambda}^{-}:=\{\mu\vDash 2d:E_{\lambda\mu}<0\}

A_{\lambda}:=\{\mu\vDash 2d:E_{\lambda\mu}\neq 0\}

Corollary 5.18
.

With respect to the superdominance order, the largest partition in
A_{\lambda}^{+}
and in
A_{\lambda}
is
\lambda
, and the largest partition in
A_{\lambda}^{-}
is
\lambda^{*}

Proof.

By Lemma
5.17
, we have
E_{\lambda\lambda}>0
. Since all other partitions

\lambda

\lambda\succ\mu

and hence in

Among the partitions

of length

is the largest, because these come from merging exactly two parts of
\lambda
. Partitions of smaller length in
A_{\lambda}^{-}
come from merging partitions of length at most

\lambda^{*}
.

\trop(\mathcal{B}\Sigma_{2d}^{*})

Lemma 5.26
.

Let

such that

a_{1}p_{(2^{5})}+a_{2}p_{(6,4)}+a_{3}p_{(8,2)}-3p_{(6,2^{2})}

Proof.

We fix a number of variables. By Lyapunov’s inequality (Remark

Therefore

is valid for any number of variables.
∎

A.1.
Symmetric sums of squares and symmetry reduction

In general, using representation theory, one can describe the invariant sums of squares forms more efficiently
[
GP04
]
. We refer to
[
BR21
, Section 4]
and
[
DR23
, Section 3]
for background on the description of the cone of (even) symmetric sums of squares in an increasing number of variables.

In characteristic zero we have a correspondence between the irreducible
\mathcal{S}_{n}
-modules and the partitions
\lambda\vdash n
. For
\mathcal{B}_{n}
the irreducible modules are in correspondence with bipartitions
(\lambda,\mu)
of
n
, i.e. pairs of partitions of nonnegative integers
k
and
n-k
. We also write
(\lambda,\mu)\vdash n
for bipartitions
(\lambda,\mu)
of
n
. For both groups, the irreducible representations are called
Specht modules
and we denote them by
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}
, resp.
\mathbb{S}^{(\lambda,\mu)}
(see e.g.
[
Sag01
,
Mus93
]
for background on representation theory of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
\mathcal{B}_{n}
).
Asymptotically, the isotypic decomposition of the modules
H_{n,d}
stabilizes in a certain sense for
n\geq 2d
. For any partition
\alpha
let
\alpha+ke_{1}
denote the partition obtained from
\alpha
by adding
k
to its first part, where
k
is a positive integer.

Proposition A.1

(
[
Rie+13
,
DR23
]
)
.

Let
\lambda\vdash 2d
, and
(\mu,\nu)\vdash d
, then

(i)

the multiplicity of
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}
in
H_{2d,d}
equals the multiplicity of
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda+ke_{1}}
in
H_{2d+k,d}
, and all
\mathcal{S}_{2d+k}
-irreducible representations in
H_{2d+k,d}
are of this form,

(ii)

the multiplicity of
\mathbb{S}^{(\mu,\nu)}
in
H_{d,d}
equals the multiplicity of
\mathbb{S}^{(\mu+ke_{1},\nu)}
in
H_{d+k,d}
and all
\mathcal{B}_{d+k}
-irreducible representations in
H_{d+k,d}
are of this form.

Hence, for each positive integer
d
there exist positive integers
d_{1},\dots,d_{k}
corresponding to the multiplicities of the distinct
\mathbb{S}^{\lambda}
in
H_{2d,d}
. And similarly for each positive integer
d
there exist positive integers
d_{1}^{\prime},\dots,d_{l}^{\prime}
corresponding to the multiplicities of the distinct
\mathbb{S}^{(\mu,\nu)}
in
H_{d,d}
.

Proposition A.2

(
[
BR21
]
, Cor. 4.4)
.

For any
n\geq 2d
there exist symmetric matrices
A_{n}^{(i)}\in\left({H_{n,2d}^{\mathcal{S}}}\right)^{d_{i}\times d_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,k
, such that any
f\in\Sigma^{\mathcal{S}}_{n,2d}
can be written as

f=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Tr(A_{n}^{(i)}B^{(i)})

for some positive semidefinite matrices
B^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times d_{i}}
.

The same is true verbatim for
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and
n\geq d
. The matrices
A_{n}^{(i)}
are obtained in the following way. One finds a symmetry-adapted basis
\mathcal{F}
of
H_{n,d}
and the matrices contain the symmetrization of pairwise products of elements belonging to the same isotypic component of
\mathcal{F}
.

A.2.
A uniform symmetry adapted basis for partial symmetry reduction

In view of partial symmetry reduction as in
[
AB24
]
we are interested in a symmetry-adapted basis of
H_{n,d}
where any two polynomials belonging to the same isotypic component have the same support and the size of the support is at most
d
for all
n\geq d
. There we discard all factors of the basis elements that are power sums, since they vary in their number of variables. We prove the existence of such a symmetry-adapted basis with this property in Lemma
A.3
. Moreover, we deduce that the same symmetry-adapted basis can be used for the
\mathcal{B}_{n}
-modules
H_{n,d}
in Corollary
A.4
and only the power sums are varying in the number of variables. As a consequence, we see that the matrices
A_{n}^{(i)}
in Proposition
A.2
can be chosen uniformly and converge asymptotically.

The
coinvariant algebra

\mathbb{R}[\underline{X}]_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}
of the hyperoctahedral group
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is defined as the quotient ring of the real polynomial ring modulo the ideal generated by all invariant polynomials of positive degrees, i.e.,
\mathbb{R}[\underline{X}]_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}=\mathbb{R}[\underline{X}]/(p_{2},\ldots,p_{2n})
. The ideal
(p_{2},\ldots,p_{2n})
is closed under the action of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and therefore
\mathbb{R}[\underline{X}]_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}
is itself a
\mathcal{B}_{n}
-module which is isomorphic to the regular representation of the hyperoctahedral group as
\mathcal{B}_{n}
-modules
[
Ber09
, Section 8.1]
. Moreover, we have an isomorphism of graded
\mathcal{B}_{n}
-modules
\mathbb{R}[\underline{X}]\cong\mathbb{R}[p_{2},\ldots,p_{2n}]\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}[\underline{X}]_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}
. Therefore a symmetry-adapted basis of the coinvariant algebra lifts to a symmetry-adapted basis of the polynomial ring by multiplication with products of even power sums.

Lemma A.3
.

There exists a symmetry-adapted basis of the coinvariant algebra
\mathbb{R}[\underline{X}]_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}
for which the support of any two polynomials belonging to the same isotypic component is equal. Moreover, the support equals the support of a Specht polynomial belonging to the same isotypic component.

Proof.

It was proven by Ariki, Terasoma and Yamada
[
ATY97
]
that there exists a symmetry-adapted basis of
\mathbb{R}[\underline{X}]_{\mathcal{B}_{n}}
where the part with respect to the isotypic component of
(\lambda,\mu)
is the set of all higher Specht polynomials
F_{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathbf{S}}
where
\mathbf{S}
ranges over the set of standard bitableaux of shape
(\lambda,\mu)
and
\mathbf{T}
is a fixed bitableau of same shape. Then,
F_{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathbf{T}}
is actually equal to the Specht polynomial
\spe_{\mathbf{T}}
up to multiplication by some scalar in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
\operatorname{supp}(\spe_{\mathbf{T}})
denote the support of
\spe_{\mathbf{T}}
. Without loss of generality we can suppose
\operatorname{supp}(\spe_{\mathbf{T}})=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}
. For any standard bitableau
\mathbf{S}
of same shape as
\mathbf{T}
we have

F_{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathbf{S}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}}c_{\alpha}\prod_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\prod_{m+1}^{n}X_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}}f_{\beta}(\underline{X})\prod_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{\beta_{i}},

where the
f_{\beta}
are some polynomials with
\operatorname{supp}(f_{\beta})\subset\{X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
, for all
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
.
Since
\spe_{\mathbf{T}}\mapsto F_{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathbf{S}}
defines a
\mathcal{B}_{n}
-equivariant linear isomorphism, we observe that permuting any of the variables, which do not lie in
\operatorname{supp}(\spe_{\mathbf{T}})
, does not change
F_{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathbf{S}}
. Analogously, the change of sign of any of these variables does not change
\spe_{\mathbf{T}}
and therefore also
F_{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathbf{S}}.
Therefore, each polynomial
f_{\beta}
must be an invariant polynomial with respect to the reflection action of the hyperoctahedral group
\mathcal{B}_{n-m}
acting on
\mathbb{R}[X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{n}]
by permutation and change of signs.

Then for any
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
we have
f_{\beta}(\underline{X})=g_{\beta}(p_{2}(X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{n}),\ldots,p_{2(n-m)}(X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{n}))
for some
(n-m)
-variate polynomials
g_{\beta}
.
Since

p_{2k}(X_{m+1},\ldots,X_{n})\equiv-p_{2k}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})\mod(p_{2}(\underline{X}),\ldots,p_{2n}(\underline{X}))

we have

F_{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathbf{S}}\equiv\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}}g_{\beta}(-p_{2}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}),\ldots,-p_{2(n-m)}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}))\prod_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{\beta_{i}}=:\widetilde{F}_{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathbf{S}}\mod(p_{2},\ldots,p_{2n}).

The polynomial
\widetilde{F}_{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathbf{S}}
has the same support as
\spe_{\mathbf{T}}
and can be used instead of
F_{\mathbf{T}}^{\mathbf{S}}
in the symmetry adapted basis which proves the Lemma.
∎

Corollary A.4
.

There exists a symmetry adapted basis
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{{(\lambda,\mu)},i_{(\lambda,\mu)}}\,\,\mid\,\,\text{$(\lambda,\mu)\vdash d$},i_{(\lambda,\mu)}=1,\ldots,m_{(\lambda,\mu)}\}
of the
\mathcal{B}_{d}
-module

such that any

is a sum of terms of the form
x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}\cdot p_{\kappa}
for exponents
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and even partitions
\kappa
, and the union of the supports of the monomials
x^{\alpha}
in any term

of
f_{{(\lambda,\mu)},i_{(\lambda,\mu)}}
equals the support of a fixed Specht polynomial of shape
{(\lambda,\mu)}
. Moreover, if one replaces the
d
-variate even symmetric polynomials by
n
-variate even symmetric polynomials in any term

of
f_{{(\lambda,\mu)},i_{(\lambda,\mu)}}
, then
\mathcal{F}
is a symmetry adapted basis of

in any number of variables

.

Proof.

By Lemma
A.3
there exists a symmetry adapted basis of the direct sum of the graded components of degree at most
d
of the coinvariant algebra
\mathbb{R}[\underline{X}]_{\mathcal{B}_{d}}
which consists only of polynomials with same support as a fixed corresponding
\mathcal{B}_{d}
-Specht polynomial
F_{\mathbf{T}_{(\lambda,\mu)}}^{\mathbf{T}_{(\lambda,\mu)}}
for each
(\lambda,\mu)\vdash d
.
By multiplying the polynomials with even power sums such that the obtained degree equals
d
, we obtain a symmetry-adapted basis

\mathcal{F}=\{f_{{(\lambda,\mu)},i_{(\lambda,\mu)}}\,\,\mid\,\,{(\lambda,\mu)}\vdash n,i_{(\lambda,\mu)}=1,\ldots,m_{(\lambda,\mu)}\}

of
H_{d,d}
.
Moreover, we can suppose that

\mathcal{B}_{d}

All terms of basis elements in
