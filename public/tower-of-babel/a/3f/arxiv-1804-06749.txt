Abstract
Asymptotic approximations of Jacobi polynomials are given for large values of the β \beta -parameter and of their zeros. The expansions are given in terms of Laguerre polynomials and of their zeros. The levels of accuracy of the approximations are verified by numerical examples.

Introduction

This paper fits in a series of our papers on asymptotic aspects of Gauss quadrature for the classical polynomials. In
[
2
]
we have considered large degree asymptotics of Hermite and Laguerre polynomials, including asymptotic expansions of their nodes and weights for Gauss quadrature. In a recently submitted paper
[
3
]
we study similar aspects for large degree asymptotics of the Jacobi polynomials and in the present paper we do the same for large values of
\beta
.

We describe two rather similar methods, the second one gives more concise coefficients and better approximations. Our methods are different from the large
\beta
approach used in
[
1
]
, where the starting point is a power series representation of the Jacobi polynomial. We start with an integral representation.

Similar expansions can be obtained for large values of
\alpha
with other parameters fixed. This follows from the symmetry relation

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=(-1)^{n}P_{n}^{(\beta,\alpha)}(-x).

2
A first expansion

For details and properties of the orthogonal polynomials, we refer to
[
4
]
. The following limit is well known

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(1-\frac{2x}{\beta}\right)=L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x).

The expansions in this paper give asymptotic details of this limit.

For deriving the results we use the Cauchy integral that follows from the Rodrigues formula for the Jacobi polynomial

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}w(x)}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\cal C}}\frac{w(z)\big(1-z^{2}\big)^{n}}{(z-x)^{n+1}}{\rm d}z,\qquad x\in(-1,1),

(2.1)

where
w(z)=(1-z)^{\alpha}(1+z)^{\beta}
and
{{\cal C}}
is a contour around
x
with
\pm 1
outside the contour. Similarly, for the Laguerre polynomial

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\cal L}}(s+1)^{n+\alpha}e^{-xs}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s^{n+1}},

where
{{\cal L}}
is a contour around
0
with
-1
outside the contour.

By changing the variable in (
2.1
), writing
z=x-(1-x)s
, it follows that

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=\frac{(1+x)^{n}}{2^{n}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\cal C}}(s+1)^{n+\alpha}\left(1-\frac{1-x}{1+x}s\right)^{n+\beta}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s^{n+1}},

(2.2)

where
{{\cal C}}
is a contour around
0
with
-1
and
(1+x)/(1-x)
outside the contour.

Next, replacing
x
by
1-2x/\beta
we find for
0<x<\beta

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(1-\frac{2x}{\beta}\right)=\frac{(1-x/\beta)^{n}}{2\pi i}\int_{{\cal C}}(s+1)^{n+\alpha}\left(1-\frac{x}{\beta-x}s\right)^{n+\beta}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s^{n+1}},

(2.3)

where the contour is around
0
with
-1
and
(\beta-x)/x
outside the contour.

We expand

\displaystyle(1-x/\beta)^{n}\left(1-\frac{x}{\beta-x}s\right)^{n+\beta}=e^{-xs}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{k}(n,x;s)}{\beta^{k}},

(2.4)

and we obtain

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(1-\frac{2x}{\beta}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\Phi_{k}(n,\alpha,x)}{\beta^{k}},

(2.5)

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(n,\alpha,x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\cal C}}(s+1)^{n+\alpha}e^{-xs}c_{k}(n,\alpha,x;s)\frac{{\rm d}s}{s^{n+1}}.

(2.6)

The same expansion has been derived in
[
1
]
, starting from a polynomial
{}_{2}F_{1}
-representation of the Jacobi polynomial, and explicit forms of
\Phi_{k}(n,\alpha,x)
are given in terms of symmetric functions. The first
\Phi_{k}(n,\alpha,x)
are shown in terms of derivatives of
L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)
.

Remark 2.1
.

The expansion in (
2.4
) is convergent for
\beta>|x|\left(1+|s|\right)
, and initially we obtain an infinite expansion in (
2.5
) of the Jacobi polynomial. Because this expansion defines an analytic function of
\beta
for large enough values of
\beta
(depending on
x
and
s
), the relation in (
2.5
) (with the infinite series) defines an analytical identity. But when we expand the left-hand side into an
n
th degree power series by using one of the available power series of a
{}_{2}F_{1}
-representation, we can rearrange that expansion into a finite
n
th degree expansion with negative powers of
\beta
. Because of the analytical relationship the series in (
2.5
) is finite, as shown.

Another point is that the Rodrigues formula and the Cauchy integral in (
2.1
) are not only valid for
x\in(-1,1)
, as mentioned because of convention. But the relation in (
2.5
) shows two polynomials in
x
on both sides. Again, by using the analytical relationship we can take for
x
any complex value. But the large
\beta
asymptotic property holds uniformly for bounded values of
n
,
\alpha
and
x
.

The first coefficients
c_{k}(n,x;s)
are

\displaystyle c_{0}(n,x;s)=1,

\displaystyle c_{1}(n,x;s)=-\tfrac{1}{2}x\bigl(2xs+xs^{2}+2ns+2n\bigr),

\displaystyle c_{2}(n,x;s)=\tfrac{1}{24}x^{2}\bigl({-}24xs^{2}-24xs-8xs^{3}-24ns-12ns^{2}+12x^{2}s^{2}+12x^{2}s^{3}+36xs^{2}n

\displaystyle\hphantom{c_2(n,x;s) =}{}+3x^{2}s^{4}+12xs^{3}n+12n^{2}s^{2}+24nxs+24n^{2}s-12n+12n^{2}\bigr).

(2.7)

These are polynomials in
s
of degree
2k
. The
\Phi_{k}(n,\alpha,x)
are combinations of Laguerre polynomials, because a power
s^{j}
in
c_{k}(n,x;s)
gives

\displaystyle L_{n-j}^{(\alpha+j)}(x)=(-1)^{j}\frac{{\rm d}^{j}}{{\rm d}x^{j}}L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x).

(2.8)

That is, when we write

\displaystyle c_{k}(n,x;s)=\sum_{j=0}^{2k}c_{jk}s^{j},

(2.9)

we obtain

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(n,\alpha,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n,2k)}c_{jk}L_{n-j}^{(\alpha+j)}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n,2k)}c_{jk}(-1)^{j}\frac{{\rm d}^{j}}{{\rm d}x^{j}}L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x).

(2.10)

For expansions of the zeros of the Jacobi polynomial when
\beta
is large, it is convenient to have a representation in the form

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(1-\frac{2x}{\beta}\right)=L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)U(n,\alpha,\beta,x)+L_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}(x)V(n,\alpha,\beta,x),

(2.11)

with expansions

\displaystyle U(n,\alpha,\beta,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{u_{k}(n,\alpha,x)}{\beta^{k}},\qquad V(n,\alpha,\beta,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{v_{k}(n,\alpha,x)}{\beta^{k}}.

(2.12)

These coefficients can be obtained by writing the Laguerre polynomials
L_{n-j}^{(\alpha+j)}(x)
in (
2.8
) as (see also
[
1
, Lemma 1]
)

\displaystyle L_{n-j}^{(\alpha+j)}(x)=P_{j}(n,\alpha,x)L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)+Q_{j}(n,\alpha,x)L_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}(x),

where
j=0,1,2,\dots,n
and the
P_{j}
and
Q_{j}
follow from

\displaystyle P_{0}(n,\alpha,x)=1,\qquad Q_{0}(n,\alpha,x)=0,\qquad xP_{1}(n,\alpha,x)=-n,\qquad xQ_{1}(n,\alpha,x)=n+\alpha,

\displaystyle xP_{j+1}(n,\alpha,x)=(\alpha+j-x)P_{j}(n,\alpha,x)+(j-n-1)P_{j-1}(n,\alpha,x),

\displaystyle xQ_{j+1}(n,\alpha,x)=(\alpha+j-x)Q_{j}(n,\alpha,x)+(j-n-1)Q_{j-1}(n,\alpha,x).

These relations follow from the standard recurrence relations of the Laguerre polynomials. An extra useful relation is (for
j=0,1,\dots,n-1
),

\displaystyle xL_{n-j-1}^{(\alpha+j+1)}(x)+(x-j-\alpha)L_{n-j}^{(\alpha+j)}(x)+(n-j+1)L_{n-j+1}^{(\alpha+j-1)}(x)=0.

The coefficients
u_{k}(n,\alpha,x)
and
v_{k}(n,\alpha,x)
are given by

\displaystyle u_{k}(n,\alpha,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n,2k)}c_{jk}P_{j}(n,\alpha,x),\qquad v_{k}(n,\alpha,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n,2k)}c_{jk}Q_{j}(n,\alpha,x).

(2.13)

The first coefficients are

\displaystyle u_{0}(n,\alpha,x)=1,\qquad v_{0}(n,\alpha,x)=0,

\displaystyle u_{1}(n,\alpha,x)=\tfrac{1}{2}n(2n+\alpha+1),\qquad v_{1}(n,\alpha,x)=-\tfrac{1}{2}(n+\alpha)(\alpha+2n+x+1).

We give a numerical example. We take
n=10
,
\alpha=\frac{1}{3}
,
x=1
, and several values of
\beta
. We use the expansions in (
2.12
) summing up till
k=k_{\max}
. In Table
1
we give the relative errors. We compared the results with computed results by Maple with Digits = 16.

2.1
Expansions of the zeros

We use the method described in our previous papers
[
2
,
3
]
. We denote the zeros of

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)\qquad

\displaystyle{\rm by}\quad z_{k},\quad z_{1}<z_{2}<\cdots<z_{n},

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(1-2x/\beta)\qquad

\displaystyle{\rm by}\quad x_{k},\quad x_{1}>x_{2}>\cdots>x_{n},

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)\qquad

\displaystyle{\rm by}\quad\ell_{k},\quad\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots<\ell_{n}.

Clearly, for large
\beta
,
x_{1}
can be approximated by
\ell_{n}
,
x_{2}
by
\ell_{n-1}
, and in general
x_{k}
by
\ell_{n-k+1}

We assume for
x_{k}
an expansion of the form

\displaystyle x_{k}=\ell_{n-k+1}+\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon_{k}}{\beta^{k}},\qquad\beta\to\infty,

(2.14)

and expand the right-hand side of (
2.11
), denoted by
W(x)
, at
x=\ell_{n-k+1}
for small values of
\varepsilon
:

\displaystyle W(x_{k})=W(\ell_{n-k+1}+\varepsilon)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon^{k}}{k!}\frac{{\rm d}^{k}}{{\rm d}x^{k}}W(x)=0,

where the derivatives are evaluated at

\varepsilon

to obtain

,

are the coefficients in (
2.12

. In terms of the original variables:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}=-\frac{x}{2}(\alpha+2n+x+1),

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}=\frac{x}{24}\bigl(5+7\alpha^{2}+12\alpha+24n(1+\alpha+n)+13(1+\alpha+2n)x+4x^{2}\bigr),

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{3}=-\frac{x}{48}\bigl(9\alpha^{3}+42n\alpha^{2}+23x\alpha^{2}+21\alpha^{2}+42x\alpha+72n^{2}\alpha+84xn\alpha+15\alpha+14x^{2}\alpha+72n\alpha

3
An alternative expansion

The first expansion in Section
2
gives the expansion derived in
[
1
]
, although obtained by a different method. In this section we derive another expansion which performs better and has simpler coefficients. First observe that the factor
(1-x/\beta)^{n}
in front of the integral (
2.3
) is included in the expansion in (
2.4
). We have included it to obtain the same expansion as in
[
1
]
, but there is no need to do so. Next, we can obtain simpler coefficients when we take
b=n+\beta
as a new parameter. Because the large
\beta
parameter is replaced by a larger parameter, this may have influence on the accuracy of the results.

With this new parameter we write the representation in (
2.2
) in the form

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(1-\frac{2x}{b}\right)=\frac{(1-x/b)^{n}}{2\pi i}\int_{{\cal C}}(s+1)^{n+\alpha}\left(1-\frac{x}{b-x}s\right)^{b}\frac{{\rm d}s}{s^{n+1}},

(3.1)

and we expand first in powers of
s
:

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{x}{b-x}s\right)^{b}=e^{-xs}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(b,x)\xi^{k}s^{k},\qquad\xi=x/(b-x),\qquad b=\beta+n.

(3.2)

We find for the first coefficients

\displaystyle a_{0}(b,x)=1,\qquad a_{1}(b,x)=-x,\qquad a_{2}(b,x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(x^{2}-b\big),

\displaystyle a_{3}(b,x)=\tfrac{1}{6}\big(3bx-2b-x^{3}\big),\qquad a_{4}(b,x)=\tfrac{1}{24}\big(3b^{2}-6b+8bx-6bx^{2}+x^{4}\big),

\displaystyle a_{5}(b,x)=\tfrac{1}{120}\big(30bx+20b^{2}-20bx^{2}-15b^{2}x-10bx^{3}-24b-x^{5}\big),

and obtain the finite expansion

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(1-\frac{2x}{b}\right)=(1-x/b)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi^{k}a_{k}(b,x)L_{n-k}^{(\alpha+k)}(x)

\displaystyle\hphantom{P_n^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(1-\frac{2x}{b}\right)}{}=(1-x/b)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-\xi)^{k}a_{k}(b,x)\frac{{\rm d}^{k}}{{\rm d}x^{k}}L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x).

(3.3)

The expansion is finite because powers
s^{k}
in (
3.2
) with
k>n
absorb the pole at
s=0
in (
3.1
). In addition, because

\displaystyle\xi^{2k}a_{2k}(b,x)={\cal O}\big(b^{-k}\big),\qquad\xi^{2k+1}a_{2k+1}(b,x)={\cal O}\big(b^{-{k-1}}\big),\qquad b\to\infty,

the expansion in (
3.3
) has an asymptotic character for large values of
b=\beta+n
.

To obtain an expansion in negative powers of
b
we expand

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{x}{b-x}s\right)^{b}=e^{-xs}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{d_{k}(x;s)}{b^{k}},

and we find

\displaystyle d_{0}(x;s)=1,\qquad d_{1}(x;s)=-\tfrac{1}{2}sx^{2}(s+2),

\displaystyle d_{2}(x;s)=\tfrac{1}{24}sx^{3}\big({-}24-24s-8s^{2}+12xs+12xs^{2}+3xs^{3}\big).

These are the alternative ones of the
c_{k}(n,x;s)
in (
2.7
) and they are much simpler because
n
is not explicitly included.

We write as in (
2.9
)

\displaystyle d_{k}(x;s)=\sum_{j=0}^{2k}d_{jk}s^{j},

and obtain

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(1-\frac{2x}{b}\right)=(1-x/b)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\Psi_{k}(n,\alpha,x)}{b^{k}},

where

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(n,\alpha,x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\cal C}}(s+1)^{n+\alpha}e^{-xs}d_{k}(x;s)\frac{{\rm d}s}{s^{n+1}},

and, as in (
2.10
),

\displaystyle\Psi_{k}(n,\alpha,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n,2k)}d_{jk}L_{n-j}^{(\alpha+j)}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n,2k)}d_{jk}(-1)^{j}\frac{{\rm d}^{j}}{{\rm d}x^{j}}L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x).

Finally, we write

\displaystyle\displaystyle{P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(1-\frac{2x}{b}\right)}=(1-x/b)^{n}W(n,\alpha,\beta,x),

\displaystyle W(n,\alpha,\beta,x)=L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)Y(n,\alpha,\beta,x)+L_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}(x)Z(n,\alpha,\beta,x),

with expansions

\displaystyle Y(n,\alpha,\beta,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{y_{k}(n,\alpha,x)}{b^{k}},\qquad Z(n,\alpha,\beta,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{z_{k}(n,\alpha,x)}{b^{k}}.

(3.4)

The coefficients
y_{k},z_{k}
can be obtained with the summations as in (
2.13
), and the first few are

\displaystyle y_{0}(n,\alpha,x)=1,\qquad z_{0}(n,\alpha,x)=0,

\displaystyle y_{1}(n,\alpha,x)=\tfrac{1}{2}n(2x+\alpha+1),\qquad z_{1}(n,\alpha,x)=-\tfrac{1}{2}(n+\alpha)(\alpha+x+1).

We give a numerical example. We take
n=10
,
\alpha=\frac{1}{3}
,
x=1
, and several values of
\beta
. We use the expansions in (
3.4
) summing up till
k=k_{\max}
