Euclidean Steiner Spanners: Light and Sparse
Sujoy Bhore, Csaba D. Tóth

Abstract
Lightness and sparsity are two natural parameters for Euclidean ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -spanners. Classical results show that, when the dimension d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} and ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 are constant, every set S S of n n points in d d -space admits an ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -spanners with O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) edges and weight proportional to that of the Euclidean MST of S S . In a recent breakthrough, Le and Solomon (2019) established the precise dependencies on ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , for constant d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} , of the minimum lightness and sparsity of ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -spanners, and observed that Steiner points can substantially improve the lightness and sparsity of a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -spanner. They gave upper bounds of O ~ ( ε − ( d + 1 ) / 2 ) \tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-(d+1)/2}) for the minimum lightness in dimensions d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 , and O ~ ( ε − ( d − 1 ) / 2 ) \tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-(d-1)/2}) for the minimum sparsity in d d -space for all d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 . Subsequently, Le and Solomon (2020) constructed Steiner ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -spanners of lightness O ⁡ ( ε − 1 ​ log ⁡ Δ ) O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\Delta) in the plane, where Δ ∈ Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Delta\in\Omega(\sqrt{n}) is the spread of S S , defined as the ratio between the maximum and minimum distance between a pair of points. In this work, we improve several bounds on the lightness and sparsity of Euclidean Steiner ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -spanners. We establish lower bounds of Ω ( ε − d / 2 ) \Omega(\varepsilon^{-d/2}) for the lightness and Ω ( ε − ( d − 1 ) / 2 ) \Omega(\varepsilon^{-(d-1)/2}) for the sparsity of such spanners in Euclidean d d -space for all constant d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . Our lower bound constructions generalize previous constructions by Le and Solomon, but the analysis substantially simplifies previous work, using new geometric insight, focusing on the directions of edges. Next, we show that for every finite set of points in the plane and every ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \varepsilon\in(0,1] , there exists a Euclidean Steiner ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -spanner of lightness O ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) O(\varepsilon^{-1}) ; this matches the lower bound for d = 2 d=2 . We generalize the notion of shallow light trees, which may be of independent interest, and use directional spanners and a modified window partitioning scheme to achieve a tight weight analysis.

Euclidean Steiner spanners.

Steiner points are additional vertices in a network (via points) that are not part of the input, and a
t

A classical problem on Steiner points arises in the context of minimum spanning trees. The
Steiner ratio
is the supremum ratio between the weight of a

of a finite point set, and it is at least
\frac{1}{2}
in any metric space due to the triangle inequality.

Le and Solomon
[
34

noticed that Steiner points can substantially improve the bound on the lightness and sparsity of an
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner. Previously, Elkin and Solomon
[
26
]
and Solomon

(

[
26
]

[
47
]

Le and Solomon, used Steiner points to improve the bounds for lightness and sparsity of Euclidean spanners. For minimum sparsity, they gave an upper bound of

[

For minimum lightness, Le and Solomon

Moreover, Le and Solomon

We improve the bounds on the lightness and sparsity of Euclidean Steiner

-spanners; see Table

Theorem 1
.

Let

,

, be a constant and let

.
For every integer

, there exists a set

of
n

\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that any Euclidean Steiner
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner for

has

\Omega(\varepsilon^{-d/2})
and sparsity
\Omega(\varepsilon^{(1-d)/2})
.

For lightness in dimension

, this improves the earlier bound of
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}/\log\varepsilon^{-1})
by Le and Solomon
[
34
]
by a logarithmic factor; and it is the first lower bound in dimensions

. The point set

in Theorem

is fairly simple: It consists of two square grids in two parallel hyperplanes in

. However, our lower-bound analysis is significantly simpler than that of
[

]
. In particular, our analysis does not depend on planarity, and it generalizes to higher dimensions. The key new insight pertains to a geometric property of Steiner

-spanners: If the length of an

between points

is at most

, then “most” of the edges of

are almost parallel to

.

Then, in Section
4
we prove the following theorem on light spanners.

Theorem 2
.

For every set
S
of
n
points in Euclidean plane and every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
there exists a Steiner
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner of

.

This result improves on an earlier bound of
O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\Delta)
by Le and Solomon
[
35
]
, where

is the

of the point set, defined as the ratio between the maximum and minimum distance between a pair of points. Note that

The tight bound in Theorem
2
relies on three new ideas, which may be of independent interest:
First, we generalize Solomon’s

Second, we reduce the proof of Theorem

Combining the first two ideas, we show how to construct light directional spanners for points on a staircase path (Section

In each direction, we start with a rectilinear MST of

We refine the classical window partition of a rectilinear polygon into histograms by subdividing each histogram into special histograms (called tame histograms), whose boundary does not oscillate wildly; this is the final piece of the puzzle. \displaystyle\|P_{ab}\|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{e\in E(i\,\sqrt{\varepsilon})}\|e\|+\sum_{e\in F(i\,\sqrt{\varepsilon})}\|\text{proj}_{ab}(e)\|\left(1+\frac{i^{2}\,\varepsilon}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\|E(i\,\sqrt{\varepsilon})\|+\big(\|ab\|-\|E(i\,\sqrt{\varepsilon})\|\big)\left(1+\frac{i^{2}\,\varepsilon}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{i^{2}\,\varepsilon}{2}\right)\|ab\|-\frac{i^{2}\,\varepsilon}{2}\,\|E(i\,\sqrt{\varepsilon})\|

\displaystyle>\left(1+\frac{i^{2}\,\varepsilon}{2}\right)\|ab\|-\frac{i^{2}\,\varepsilon}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{i^{2}}\right)\,\|ab\|

\displaystyle\geq\left(1+\frac{i^{2}\,\varepsilon}{2}\right)\|ab\|-\left(\frac{i^{2}}{2}-1\right)\varepsilon\,\|ab\|

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon)\|ab\|,

which is a contradiction, and completes the proof.
∎

Proof.

Let

as follows.

\displaystyle\|P_{ab}\|-\|ab\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\|e\|-\sum_{e\in E}\|\mathrm{proj}_{ab}e\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\|e\|(1-\cos\angle(ab,e))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|E((i-1)\sqrt{\varepsilon\kappa},i\,\sqrt{\varepsilon\kappa})\|(1-\cos(i\cdot\sqrt{\varepsilon\kappa}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|E((i-1)\sqrt{\varepsilon\kappa},i\,\sqrt{\varepsilon\kappa})\|\cdot\frac{i^{2}\,\varepsilon\kappa}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\|F((i-1)\sqrt{\varepsilon})\|-\|F(i\,\sqrt{\varepsilon})\|\right)\cdot\frac{i^{2}\,\varepsilon\kappa}{2}

\displaystyle=F(0)\cdot\frac{1^{2}\varepsilon\kappa}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|F(i\,\sqrt{\varepsilon\kappa})\|\left(\frac{(i+1)^{2}\,\varepsilon\kappa}{2}-\frac{i^{2}\,\varepsilon\kappa}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\kappa\cdot\|P_{ab}\|\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{2i+1}{i^{2+\delta}}\right).

Construction of a Tiling

The so-called
window partition
of rectilinear simple polygon
P
is a recursive subdivision of
P
into histograms
[
25
,
38
,
39
,
49
]
. It can be computed in
O(n\log n)
time if
P
has
n
vertices. It was originally designed for data structures that support orthogonal visibility and minimum-link path queries in
P
. Importantly, every axis-parallel chord of
P
intersects (
stabs
) at most three histograms of the decomposition. The stabbing property implies that the total perimeter of the histograms is
O(\mathrm{per}(P))
. For completeness, we present the standard window partition and the weight analysis here.

(a) The standard window partition of a rectilinear polygon
P
into histograms, starting from a horizontal edge
e_{0}

(b) A decomposition of an
x
-monotone histogram into tame histograms.

Lemma 13
.

Every rectilinear (weakly) simple polygon
P
can be subdivided into a collection
\mathcal{H}
of histograms such that

\sum_{H\in\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{per}(H)\leq O(\mathrm{per}(P))
.

Proof.

We describe a recursion on instances
(R,e)
, where
R
is a rectilinear (weakly) simple polygon, and
e
is an edge of
R
.
We also maintain the invariant that
\partial R\setminus e\subset\partial P
. Initially, let
R=P
and
e
an arbitrary horizontal edge of
P
; clearly
\partial P\setminus e\subset\partial P
. In one iteration, consider an instance
(R,e)
; see Fig.
4
(a) for example.

Assume w.l.o.g. that
(R,e)
is an instance where
e
is horizontal edge of
R
. We define a histogram
H\subset R
as the set of all points that can be connected to a point in
e
by a vertical line segment in
R
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be the collection of connected components
C
of
R\setminus H
. Each component
C\in\mathcal{C}
is a weakly simple rectilinear polygon that has a unique vertical edge (
window
)
w(C)
along the boundary of
H
. Recurse on the instances
(C,w(C))
for all
C\in\mathcal{C}
if
\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset
.

Weight analysis.

Consider the input rectilinear polygon
P
, and the subdivision created by the above algorithm. Each iteration of the recursion handles an instance
(R,e)
, and creates an
x
- or a
y
-monotone histogram
H_{e}
, which is not partitioned further. The cost of creating
H_{e}
equals to the weight of the windows on boundary between
H_{e}
and
R\setminus H_{e}
. Each component of
\partial H_{e}\cap\partial(R\setminus H_{e})
is the edge
w(C)
of an instance
(C,w(C))
in a recursive call.

At the next level of the recursion, for each instance
(C,w(C))
, the algorithm constructs a histogram
H_{w(C)}\subset C
. By our invariant, we have
\partial C\setminus w(C)\subset\partial R
; and we charge the weight of
w(C)
to the boundary of
R
as follows. When
w(C)
is a horizontal (vertical) side of
C
, then we charge the orthogonal projection of
w(C)
to horizontal (vertical) edges of
\partial C\setminus w(C)\subset\partial R
. The projection consists of one or more horizontal (vertical) line segments of total weight
\|w(C)\|
. We charge
w(C)
to the common boundary of
R
and
H_{w(C)}
, which is not partitioned further, hence every line segment
s
on the boundary of the input polygon
P
receives a charge of at most
\|s\|
, and the overall weight of all windows is bounded by
\mathrm{per}(P)
. As each window contributes to the perimeter of precisely two faces in
\mathcal{C}
, then

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{per}(C)=O(\mathrm{per}(P))
, as claimed.
∎

Subdivision of Histograms into Tame and Thin Histograms

Let

used similar methods to partition a histogram into histograms of constant geometric dilation.

Lemma 14

For every

of

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{vper}(T)=O(\mathrm{vper}(H))
.

Proof.

Let
H
be an
x
-monotone histogram bounded by a horizontal line segment
pq
from below, and an
x
-monotone
pq
-path

from above.
We describe a sweepline algorithm that recursively subdivides
H
with horizontal lines; see Fig.
4

\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
. Sweep

\ell
, and incrementally update

,

, and
\mathcal{T}
as follows. Whenever the sweepline

contains a chord

of

such that the subpath

of

between
a
and

has weight
(1+\tau)\|ab\|
, then we add the simple polygon bounded by

and

into
\mathcal{T}
, and replace

with the line segment

in both

and

.

First note that each polygon added into

is a

-tame histogram. By construction,
\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{per}(T)
is proportional to the sum of
\mathrm{per}(H)
and the total weight of all horizontal chords

inserted by the algorithm. At the time when the algorithm creates a tame histogram bounded by

and

, we can charge the weight

of the chord

to the vertical edges of the path

. Since the algorithm inserts only horizontal edges, all vertical edges along this path lie on the boundary of the input polygon. Consequently, the total weight of vertical edges of

is

; and are not charged in subsequent steps of the recursion.
Each vertical segment

on the boundary of

receives a charge of at most

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{per}(T)\leq\mathrm{per}(H)+O(\tau^{-1}\,\mathrm{vper}(H))\leq O(\tau^{-1}\,\mathrm{per}(H))

Since all subdivision edges are horizontal, then

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{vper}(T)=O(\mathrm{vper}(H))
.
∎

In this section we prove Lemma

Conclusion and Outlook

We have studied Euclidean Steiner
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanners under two optimization
criteria,
lightness
and
sparsity
, and obtained improved lower and upper bounds. In Euclidean
d
-space, the same point sets (grids in two parallel hyperplanes) establish the lower bounds
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-d/2})
for lightness and
\Omega(\varepsilon^{(1-d)/2})
for sparsity, for Steiner
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanners
(cf. Theorem
1
).
For lightness, we obtained a matching lower bound of
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
in the plane (cf. Theorem
2
). However, in dimensions
d\geq 3
, a
\tilde{\Theta}(\varepsilon^{-1/2})
-factor gap remains between the current upper bound
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-(d+1)/2})

[
37
, Theorem 1.7]
and the lower bound
\Theta(\varepsilon^{-d/2})
of Theorem
1
.
Le and Solomon
[
34
, Theorem 1.3]
constructed spanners with sparsity
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{(1-d)/2})
, matching the lower bound up to lower-order factors in every dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

Without Steiner points, the greedy-spanner achieves the worst-case lower bounds of
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-d})
and
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-d+1})
for lightness and sparsity, resp., in every dimension
d\geq 2
. When Steiner points are allowed, however, it is unclear whether a
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner can meet both optimization criteria. The current best constructions for sparsity (
[
34
, Theorem 1.3]
) and lightness (Theorem
2
and
[
37
, Theorem 1.7]
) place Steiner points in
d
-space to optimize one criterion, but not the other.
We conjecture that a Euclidean Steiner
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner cannot simultaneously attain both lower bounds (lightness and sparsity) of Theorem
1
. Exploring the trade-offs between lightness and sparsity in Euclidean
d
-space remains an open problem.

In the plane, in particular, we have proved a tight upper bound of
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
on the lightness of Euclidean Steiner
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanners (cf. Theorem
2
). Our proof is constructive: For every finite set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we describe a Euclidean Steiner
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner of weight
O(\varepsilon^{-1}\,\|\mathrm{MST}(S)\|)
. However, we do not control the number of Steiner points. This immediately raises two questions: What is the minimum number of Steiner points and what is the minimum sparsity of a Euclidean Steiner
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner of lightness
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
that can be attained for all finite point sets in the plane?

Planarity is an important aspect of any geometric network in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
It is desirable to construct Euclidean
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanners that are plane, i.e., no two edges of the spanner cross. Any Steiner spanner can be turned into a plane spanner (planarized), with the same weight and the same spanning ratio between the input points, by introducing Steiner points at all edge crossings. However, planarization may substantially increase the number of Steiner points. Bose and Smid
[
13
, Sec. 4]
note that Arikati et al.
[
2
]
constructed a Euclidean plane
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanner with
O(\varepsilon^{-4}n)
Steiner points for
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; see also
[
40
]
. Borradaile and Eppstein
[
10
]
improved the bound to
O(\varepsilon^{-3}n\log\varepsilon^{-1})
in certain special cases where all Delaunay faces of the point set are fat. It remains an open problem to find the optimum dependence of
\varepsilon
for plane Steiner
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanners; and for plane Steiner
(1+\varepsilon)
-spanners of lightness
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
.
