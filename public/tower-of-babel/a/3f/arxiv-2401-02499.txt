Multivariate Quantiles: Geometric and Measure-Transportation-Based Contours
Marc Hallin and Dimitri Konen

Abstract
Quantiles are a fundamental concept in probability and theoretical statistics and a daily tool in their applications. While the univariate concept of quantiles is quite clear and well understood, its multivariate extension is more problematic. After half a century of continued efforts and many proposals, two concepts, essentially, are emerging: the so-called (relabeled) geometric quantiles , extending the characterization of univariate quantiles as minimizers of an L 1 loss function involving the check functions , and the more recent center-outward quantiles based on measure transportation ideas. These two concepts yield distinct families of quantile regions and quantile contours . Our objective here is to present a comparison of their main theoretical properties and a numerical investigation of their differences.



1
Introduction

Quantiles are a fundamental concept in probability and theoretical statistics and a familiar tool in their applications, ranging from descriptive statistics and data analysis to statistical inference. The univariate concept of a quantile function (the inverse of a distribution function) is well understood and well studied in dimension
d=1
. Despite half a century of efforts, however, the multivariate extension (dimension
d\geq 2
) of this essential notion remains quite problematic—the obvious difficulty being the absence of a canonical ordering of the real space for dimension two and higher.

Various proposals have been made in the literature, none of which is fully agreed upon. Two concepts, essentially, are emerging from these many attempts: the so-called
(relabeled) geometric quantiles
(Chaudhuri 1996), extending the characterization of univariate quantiles as minimizers of an L
1
loss function involving the
check functions
, and the more recent
center-outward quantiles
(Hallin et al. 2021) based on measure transportation ideas.

The objective of this note is a brief comparison of some of the properties of the quantile regions and quantile contours associated with these two concepts.

Quantiles: from univariate to multivariate

Throughout, the inner product of two vectors
x,y\in{\mathbb R}^{d}
is denoted as
x^{\prime}y
;
\mathbb{B}_{d}
and
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
stand for the open unit ball and the
(d-1)
-dimensional unit sphere, respectively, in
{\mathbb R}^{d}
.

2.1
Definitions and basic properties

Let
X
denote a Lebesgue-absolutely continuous real-valued random variable with distribution
\rm P
over
{\mathbb R}
and distribution function

F_{\rm P}:x\mapsto F_{\rm P}(x)\coloneqq{\rm P}(X\leq x).

To simplify the exposition, assume that
X
admits a nonvanishing density
f\coloneqq{\rm dP/d\lambda}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
so that, in particular,
F_{\rm P}
is continuous and strictly monotone increasing, hence invertible. The quantile function of
\rm P
(equivalently, the quantile function of
X\sim\rm P
) then is defined as the inverse
Q_{\rm P}\coloneqq F_{\rm P}^{-1}
of the distribution function
F_{\rm P}
and the quantile of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
of
\rm P
(of
X\sim{\rm P}
) is the value
Q_{\rm P}(\alpha)
of
Q_{\rm P}
at
\alpha
.

Along with quantiles of given order
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it is often convenient to consider central quantile regions and contours. In dimension
d=1
,
call
quantile region of order
\tau\in~\![0,1)
of
\rm P
(of
X\sim{\rm P}
) the closed interval

\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}(\tau)\coloneqq\Big[Q_{\rm P}\Big(\frac{1-\tau}{2}\Big),\,Q_{\rm P}\Big(\frac{1+\tau}{2}\Big)\Big]

(an interquantile interval) and
quantile contour of order
\tau
of
\rm P
(of
X\sim{\rm P}
) the boundary

{\mathscr{C}_{\rm P}(\tau)}\coloneqq\Big\{Q_{\rm P}\Big(\frac{1-\tau}{2}\Big),\,Q_{\rm P}\Big(\frac{1+\tau}{2}\Big)\Big\}

of
\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}(\tau)
.

The quantile region of order 0 is the degenerate interval
\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}({0})=\{Q_{\rm P}(1/2)\}
and has Lebesgue measure zero: call it the
median region
. Similarly, the quantile region of order 1/2 is the traditional interquartile interval:
\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}({1/2})=[Q_{\rm P}(1/4),Q_{\rm P}(3/4)]
. Contrary to the quantile halflines
(-\infty,Q_{\rm P}(\alpha)]
, which very much depend on the orientation of the real line (an orientation that is no longer meaningful in
{\mathbb R}^{d}
for
d\geq~\!2
), the concepts of quantile regions and contours of order
\tau
are
“center-outward”
concepts (the “center” being the median
Q_{\rm P}(1/2)
) and are invariant under a change of the orientation of
{\mathbb R}
(an orthogonal transformation). The collection
\left\{\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}(\tau):\,\tau\in[0,1)\right\}
of quantile regions is stricly nested (in the sense of set inclusion) as
\tau
ranges from zero to one, with
\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}({0})=\bigcap_{\tau\in[0,1)}{\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}}(\tau)
. It is easily seen that
{\mathscr{C}_{\rm P}(|2F_{\rm P}(x)-1|)}
is the unique quantile contour running through
x\in\mathbb{R}
; actually,

{\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}(\tau)}=\left\{x:|2F_{\rm P}(x)-1|\leq\tau\right\}\ \text{ and }\ {\mathscr{C}_{\rm P}(\tau)}=\left\{x:|2F_{\rm P}(x)-1|=\tau\right\}\quad\tau\in[0,1).

(1)

An essential property of a quantile function is that the
\rm P
-probability content of a quantile region
\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}(\tau)
is
\tau
irrespective of
\rm P
: namely,
{\rm P}(\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}(\tau))=\tau
for all
\rm P
. What would be the relevance, indeed, of an interquartile region
{\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}(1/2)}
with probability content 0.5 under
{\rm P=P}_{1}
(as should be) but probability content 0.6 under
{\rm P=P}_{2}
, and probability content 0.7 under
{\rm P=P}_{3}
? This property, in dimension
d=1
, is equivalent to the property of
F_{\rm P}(X)
being uniform over
[0,1)
for
X\sim{\rm P}
.

When the center-outward quantile regions
\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}(\tau)
and contours
\mathscr{C}_{\rm P}(\tau)
are the main points of interest, it is natural to replace
F_{\rm P}
and
Q_{\rm P}
by center-outward counterparts: call
F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}\coloneqq 2F_{\rm P}-1
and
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}\coloneqq(F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P})^{-1}
the
center-outward distribution
and
quantile functions
of
\rm P
(or
X\sim{\rm P}
), respectively. Just like
F_{\rm P}
and
Q_{\rm P}
, the maps
F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
and
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
are monotone increasing and carry the same information about
\rm P
, which they fully characterize. Instead of being uniformly distributed over
[0,1)
,
F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}(X)
, when
X\sim{\rm P}
, is uniform over
(-1,1)
, which is the open unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{1}
in
{\mathbb R}
. In fact, denoting by
{\rm U}_{1}
the uniform distribution over
\mathbb{B}_{1}
, we have
F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}(X)\sim U_{1}
when
X\sim{\rm P}
, hence
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}(U)\sim{\rm P}
if
U\sim{\rm U}_{1}
. In the terminology of measure transportation, we say that the
transport

F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
is
pushing
\rm P
forward to
{\rm U}_{1}
and that its inverse
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
is
pushing
{\rm U}_{1}
forward to
{\rm P}
, which we conveniently denote as

F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}\#{\rm P}={\rm U}_{1}\quad\text{and }\quad Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}\#{\rm U}_{1}={\rm P},

respectively. The quantile region
\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}(\tau)
and quantile contour
\mathscr{C}_{\rm P}(\tau)

then take the simple forms (equivalent to (
1
))

\mathscr{R}_{\rm P}(\tau)=Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}(\tau\mathbb{B}_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{C}_{\rm P}(\tau)=Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}(\tau\mathbb{S}^{0}),\quad\tau\in[0,1),

(2)

respectively, where
\mathbb{S}^{0}\coloneqq\{-1,1\}
stands for the (
0
-dimensional) unit sphere in
{\mathbb R}
.

2.2
Characterization of univariate quantiles as minimizers of expected check functions

When
\rm P
is a probability measure over
{\mathbb R}
and
X\sim{\rm P}
, and given a level
\alpha\in[0,1)
, a quantile of order
\alpha
of
X
, by definition, is any
x\in{\mathbb R}
satisfying

{\rm P}(X<x)\leq\alpha\leq{\rm P}(X\leq x).

It turns out that such
x
’s are exactly the minimizers (with respect to
\theta\in{\mathbb R}
) of the objective function

\theta\mapsto M_{\alpha}^{\rm P}(\theta):={\rm E}_{\rm P}[\rho_{\alpha}(\theta-X)-\rho_{\alpha}(X)]

(3)

where, denoting by
z^{+}
and
z^{-}
the positive and negative parts, respectively, of
z\in\mathbb R
,

\rho_{\alpha}:z\mapsto\rho_{\alpha}(z)\coloneqq|z|+(2\alpha-1)z=2\big\{\alpha z^{+}+(1-\alpha)z^{-}\big\},\quad z\in{\mathbb R},\ \alpha\in[0,1)

stands for the so-called
check function
. Noting that
M_{\alpha}^{\rm P}(\theta)
rewrites as

M_{\alpha}^{\rm P}(\theta)={\rm E}_{\rm P}\big[|\theta-X|-|X|\big]-(2\alpha-1)\theta

(4)

for all
\theta\in{\mathbb R}
, it follows from the triangular inequality that
\big||X-\theta|-|X|\big|\leq|\theta|
, which is integrable. The objective function
M_{\alpha}^{\rm P}
, hence the correspondence between quantiles and the minimizing procedure in (
3
), thus remains well defined for an
arbitrary
probability measure
{\rm P}
, without any moment assumption. In this general setting, we have, even in the absence of a finite moment of order one,

Q_{\rm P}(\alpha)={\rm argmin}_{\theta\in{\mathbb R}}M_{\alpha}^{\rm P}(\theta),\qquad\alpha\in[0,1).

For
\alpha=0.5
,
M^{\rm P}_{\alpha}(\theta)={\rm E}_{\rm P}[|\theta-X|-|X|]
; in case
{\rm E_{P}}(X)<\infty
, thus, we recover the familiar characterization of the median
Q_{\rm P}(1/2)
as an
{\rm L}_{1}
location parameter, the minimizer of the expected absolute deviation
{\rm E}_{\rm P}[|\theta-X|]
.

Recalling that
F_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}\coloneqq 2F_{\rm P}-1
, we see that the natural labeling for center-outward quantiles is provided by
\beta:=2\alpha-1\in(-1,1)
. Therefore, the center-outward version of the characterization of quantiles as minimizers is obtained by relabeling
2\alpha-1
as
\beta
in (
3
). This leads to the characterization of center-outward quantiles
Q_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\beta)
as minimizers with respect to
\theta\in{\mathbb R}
of the objective function

\theta\mapsto O_{\beta}^{\rm P}(\theta)\coloneqq{\rm E}_{\rm P}\big[|\theta-X|-|X|\big]-\beta\theta,\qquad\beta\in(-1,1).

(5)

We then have (still, without any moment assumption)

Q_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\beta)={\rm argmin}_{\theta\in{\mathbb R}}O_{\beta}^{\rm P}(\theta),\qquad\beta\in(-1,1).

The value
\beta=0
yields the median
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}(0)=Q_{\rm P}(1/2)
of
\rm P
, while positive (resp. negative) values of
\beta
are associated to quantiles sitting to the right-hand (resp. left-hand) side of the median.

2.3
Characterization of univariate quantile functions as monotone transports

Consider a probability measure
{\rm P}
with non-vanishing density
f
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb R}
. An alternative to the characterization, in dimension
d=1
, of
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
(hence of
Q_{\rm P}
and
F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
) as minimizers of expected check functions is obtained by noting that
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
is monotone increasing and pushes
{\rm U}_{1}
forward to
\rm P
. This fully characterizes
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
up to a set of values with Lebesgue measure zero.

As we shall see, the existence and (almost everywhere) uniqueness, in dimension
d=1
, of a monotone non-decreasing mapping (that is, the derivative, hence the gradient, of a convex function) pushing

forward to

is a particular case of a famous and more general measure transportation result by

The center-outward quantile function

, thus, can be characterized as the almost everywhere unique monotone map from the open unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{1}=(-1,1)
to

such that
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}\#{\rm U}_{1}={\rm P}
.

3
Multivariate quantile functions, regions, and contours

Extending to dimension
d>1
the characterizations of quantile functions developed in Section
2.2
leads to the concept of
geometric quantiles
first proposed by
Chaudhuri 1996
while the measure-transportation-based characterization developed in Section
2.3
leads to the concept of multivariate
center-outward quantiles
proposed in
Hallin et al. 2021
.

3.1
Geometric quantiles and geometric quantile contours

Consider a probability measure
{\rm P}
supported on
{\mathbb R}^{d}
, with
d\geq 2
. Extending toa
d
-dimensional framework the characterization of univariate quantiles as minimizers of expected check functions is obtained by replacing, in (
5
), the absolute values with Euclidean norms and
\beta\in(-1,1)=\mathbb{B}_{1}
with
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{B}_{d}
. Letting
{\bf v}\eqqcolon\tau{\bf u}\in\mathbb{B}_{d}
with
\tau=\|{\bf v}\|\in(0,1)
and
{\bf u}={\bf v}/\|{\bf v}\|\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, define the
geometric quantile
of order
\tau>0
in direction
{\bf u}={\bf v}/\|{\bf v}\|
of
\rm P
(of
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
) as an arbitrary minimizer, over
{\bf z}\in{\mathbb R}^{d}
, of the objective function

{\bf z}\mapsto O_{\tau,{\bf u}}^{\rm P}({\bf z}):={\rm E}_{\rm P}\big[\|{\bf z}-{\bf X}\|-\|{\bf X}\|\big]-\tau{\bf u}^{\prime}{\bf z}

(6)

where
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
; throughout, boldface is used to stress that the variables take values in
{\mathbb R}^{d}
. The objective function
O_{\tau,{\bf u}}^{\rm P}
being convex, it can be shown that the geometric quantiles of
{\rm P}
are unique as soon as
\rm P
is not supported on a single line of
{\mathbb R}^{d}
—a situation which essentially boils down to a univariate setting: see Theorem 1 in
Paindaveine and
Virta 2020
for a discussion. For
\tau=0
, define the geometric median as the minimizer of
{\rm E}_{\rm P}\big[\|{\bf z}-{\bf X}\|-\|{\bf X}\|\big]
which, still in view of the triangular inequality, exists without any moment assumption and is better known as the
Fréchet median
.

When uniqueness holds (
\rm P
not supported on a line), we denote by
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}(\tau{\bf u})
the geometric quantile of order
\tau\in[0,1)
in direction
\bf u
of
{\rm P}
(of
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
) and by
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}({\boldsymbol{0}})
the geometric or Fréchet median, which can be interpreted as the geometric quantile of order
\tau=0
in any direction
\bf u
. Here again, boldface is used to stress that
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
takes values in
{\mathbb R}^{d}
.

Strongly related to the gradient of
O_{\tau,{\bf u}}^{\rm P}
, we define the
geometric distribution function

{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
of
{\rm P}
as the mapping

{\bf z}\mapsto{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}({\bf z})\coloneqq{\rm E}_{\rm P}\Big[\frac{{\bf z}-{\bf X}}{\|{\bf z}-{\bf X}\|}{\mathbb 1}[{\bf X}\neq{\bf z}]\Big],\qquad{\bf z}\in{\mathbb R}^{d},

(7)

where
{\mathbb 1}[A]
stands for the indicator function of
A
. While the definition of
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
is motivated by the characterization of univariate center-outward quantiles as minimizers of an objective function, let us stress that the geometric distribution function in (
7
) is a natural analogue of the univariate center-outward distribution as well. Indeed, recalling that, for
d=1
,
F_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}\coloneqq 2F_{\rm P}-1
and observing that
2{\mathbb 1}[X\leq z]-1={\rm sign}(z-X)
, one obtains

F_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(z)={\rm E}_{\rm P}[{\rm sign}(z-X)],\quad z\in{\mathbb R},

(8)

to which
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
in (
7
) reduces when
d=1
. The direction
({\bf z}-{\bf X})/\|{\bf z}-{\bf X}\|
, thus, can be interpreted as a multivariate sign for
{\bf z}-{\bf X}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
.

Provided that
{\rm P}
is non-atomic and is not supported on a single line of
{\mathbb R}^{d}
, Theorem 6.2 in
Konen and
Paindaveine 2022
entails that
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
is a homeomorphism between
{\mathbb R}^{d}
and the open unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
of
{\mathbb R}^{d}
, with inverse
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
. Consequently, one also can define
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
as
\left({\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}\right)^{-1}
with
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
defined in (
7
), or define
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
as
\left({\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}\right)^{-1}
with
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
defined as the minimizer of
O_{\tau,{\bf u}}^{\rm P}
in (
6
). Further results about the regularity of
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
and
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
are provided in
Konen 2022
.

With this, one can define
geometric quantile regions
and
contours
, which are multivariate extensions of the univariate center-outward quantile regions and contours, by letting

\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{{\rm P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau):={\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}(\tau\mathbb{B}_{d})\quad\textrm{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau):={\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}(\tau\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),\quad\tau\in[0,1).

(9)

For
\tau=0
,
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(0)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(0)
reduce to the singlepoint set consisting of the
geometric
or
Fréchet median
; as in the univariate case, the geometric median is an
{\rm L}_{1}
location parameter that minimizes, when finite first-order moments exist, the expected absolute deviation (
absolute
deviations, here, are to be understood as
Euclidean norms
of deviations).

3.2
Measure-transportation-based quantiles and quantile contours

Denote by
{\rm U}_{d}
the spherical uniform over the open unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
, that is, the product of a uniform distribution over the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and a (univariate) uniform over the distances from the origin; in other words,
{\rm U}_{d}

f_{{\rm U}_{d}}({\bf x})
proportional to
1/\|{\bf x}\|^{d-1}{\mathbb 1}[{\boldsymbol{0}}<\|{\bf x}\|\leq 1]
for
{\boldsymbol{0}}<\|{\bf x}\|
and

A celebrated result by
McCann 1995
implies that, for any distribution
\rm P
over
{{\mathbb R}}^{d}

\rm P

{\rm U}_{d}
over the (open) unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
of
{\mathbb R}^{d}

d=1

\rm P
forward to
{\rm U}_{1}
is the center-outward distribution function
F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}\coloneqq 2F_{\rm P}-1
. For arbitrary dimension

to define the
center-outward distribution function
of
\rm P
(or
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
) as the almost everywhere unique gradient of a convex function
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
pushing
\rm P
to the spherical uniform
{\rm U}_{d}
.

belongs to the class

of Lebesgue-absolutely continuous distributions with density bounded away from zero and infinity on any compact

4
Main properties

4.1
Geometric quantile regions and contours

Consider a probability measure
{\rm P}
which is non-atomic and is not supported on a single line of
{\mathbb R}^{d}
; in particular,
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
and
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
are homeomorphisms with
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}=({\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P})^{-1}
. Then, geometric quantile regions and contours have the following topological properties:

(i)

the regions
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)
are compact and arc-connected for all
\tau\in[0,1)
;

(ii)

the regions
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)
are stricly nested as
\tau
increases: for any
0\leq\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<1
, we have
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau_{1})\subsetneq\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau_{2})
;

(iii)

if
{\rm P}
has a bounded density
f
, then the contour
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional manifold of class
\mathcal{C}^{d-1}
for all
\tau\in(0,1)
; if, in addition,
f
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{d+k}
for all
\tau\in(0,1)
;

(iv)

for
d=1
,
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
,
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
reduce to
F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
,
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
,
{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
, and
{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
.

While properties (i)-(ii) are straightforward consequences of the continuity of
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
, property (iii) requires more advanced mathematical tools. That property was established in
Konen 2022
, where it is shown that geometric distribution functions satisfy a linear partial differential equation, which was the key to te derivation of the regularity properties of geometric contours. Among other things, this PDE implies that geometric distribution functions fully characterize
arbitrary
probability measures (no moment or non-atomicity assumptions required), namely,

(v)

if
{\rm P_{1}}
and
{\rm P_{2}}
are
arbitrary
probability measures such that
{\bf F}_{\rm P_{1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}={\bf F}_{\rm P_{2}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
, then
{\rm P}_{1}={\rm P}_{2}
.

This characterization result has been long known, though: the first proof was provided in Theorem 2.5 of
Koltchinski 1997
and was refined recently in
Konen 2022
.

Geometric quantile and distribution functions further enjoy the following natural equivariance properties; see
Girard and
Stupfler 2017
and
Konen and
Paindaveine 2022
.

(vi)

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb R}^{d}
and denote by
{\bf O}
an arbitrary
d\times d
orthogonal matrix. For
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
, write
{\bf F}_{{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}},{\bf F}_{{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
, etc. instead of
{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}},{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
, etc. We have

\displaystyle{\bf F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf x})={\bf O}{\bf F}_{\bf X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}({\bf x})\quad

\displaystyle{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle{\bf Q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}({\bf O}{\bf u})=\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf Q}_{\bf X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}({\bf u})\quad

\displaystyle{\bf u}\in\mathbb{B}_{d},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)\quad

\displaystyle\tau\in[0,1),

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\bf X}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)\quad

\displaystyle\tau\in[0,1).

In particular, if
\rm P
enjoys symmetry properties (rotational, central, or with respect to some hyperplanes),
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
,
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)
enjoy
the same
symmetry properties as
\rm P
for all
\tau\in~\![0,1)
.

Recall that an essential property of distribution functions in dimension
d=1
is their non-decreasing monotonicity. In higher dimensions, the concept of monotonicity still makes sense; we distinguish two notions of monotonicity, which both reduce to the usual concept of (non-decreasing) monotonicity when
d=1
:
monotonicity
and
cyclical monotonicity
. We say that a map
{\bf G}:{\mathbb R}^{d}\to{\mathbb R}^{d}
is
monotone
if

({\bf G}({\bf y})-{\bf G}({\bf x}))^{\prime}({\bf y}-{\bf x})\geq 0

for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in{\mathbb R}^{d}
. We say that
G
is
cyclically monotone
if for any
m\geq 1
we have

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\big({\bf G}({\bf x}_{k+1})-{\bf G}({\bf x}_{k})\big)^{\prime}{\bf x}_{k+1}\geq 0

for every cycle
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m+1}:=x_{1}
. For
m=2
, cyclical monotonicity yields

\big({\bf G}({\bf x}_{2})-{\bf G}({\bf x}_{1})\big)^{\prime}{\bf x}_{2}+\big({\bf G}({\bf x}_{1})-{\bf G}({\bf x}_{2})\big)^{\prime}{\bf x}_{1}\geq 0,

and cyclical monotonicity thus implies monotonicity.
Theorem 24.8 in
Rockafellar 1970
essentially entails that
{\bf G}
is cyclically monotone precisely when it writes as the gradient
{\bf G}=\nabla\psi
of some convex (potential) function
\psi:{\mathbb R}^{d}\to{\mathbb R}
.

The following then holds.

(vii)

The maps
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
and
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
are cyclically monotone, hence also monotone, over
{\mathbb R}^{d}
.

Indeed, similarly to the center-outward distribution functions based on measure transportation introduced in Section 2.1, geometric distribution functions are gradients of convex functions. This is clear from (
7
), which immediately yields
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}=\nabla\phi_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
, where

{\bf z}\mapsto\phi_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}({\bf z}):={\rm E}\big[\|{\bf z}-{\bf X}\|-\|{\bf X}\|\big],\quad{\bf z}\in{\mathbb R}^{d}

is a convex function and
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
. It follows that
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
is cyclically monotone, which implies that its inverse
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
is cyclically monotone too (hence the gradient of a convex function).

However, the geometric distribution function
{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
, in general, does not push
{\rm P}
forward to the spherical uniform
{\rm U}_{d}
over
\mathbb{B}_{d}
. Would this be case,
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
and
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
both being gradients of convex functions, the a.s. uniqueness result of McCann (1955) would imply
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}={\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
a.s. for all
{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{+}
(see Section 2.2), which cannot hold in general. Indeed,
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
can be defined as the solution of the previously mentioned
linear
PDE (see
Konen 2022
), while
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
can be defined as the solution of a PDE of the Monge-Ampère type which is
nonlinear
(see
Hallin et al. 2021
). Therefore, the solutions
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
and
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
, for general
\rm P
, do not coincide and
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
, in general, does not push
{\rm P}
forward to
{\rm U}_{d}
.

Nevertheless, recalling that
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
is a homeomorphism between
{\mathbb R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{B}_{d}
, we see that each point
{\bf x}\in{\mathbb R}^{d}
belongs to exactly one geometric contour, namely
{\bf x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)
where
\tau=\|{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}({\bf x})\|
. Therefore, we can relabel quantile regions so that, in the new labeling, the region associated with an order
\tau\in[0,1)
has
{\rm P}
-probability content
\tau
. For this purpose, it is enough to observe that the map

\tau\mapsto\kappa_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau):={\rm P}[\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)],\quad\tau\in[0,1),

from
[0,1)\to[0,1)

is a continous and monotone increasing bijection. In fact,
\kappa_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
is the univariate distribution function of the random variable
\|{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}({\bf X})\|
with
{\bf X}\sim~\!{\rm P}
.Consequently, we can define the
relabeled geometric distribution function
of
\rm P
(of
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
) as

{\bf x}\mapsto\tilde{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}({\bf x}):={\bf B_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}}\big({\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}({\bf x})\big),

(11)

where

{\bf B_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}}({\bf x}):=\begin{cases}\kappa_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\|{\bf x}\|)\frac{{\bf x}}{\|{\bf x}\|}&\textrm{ if }{\bf x}\neq 0\\
0&\textrm{ if }{\bf x}=0\end{cases}

is a homeomorphism.
Consequently, the relabeled geometric distribution function
\tilde{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
is also a homeomorphism, hence continuously invertible: define the
relabeled geometric quantile function
as its inverse
\tilde{\bf Q}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}:=(\tilde{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}})^{-1}
. The collections of regions and contours associated with
\tilde{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
are the same as those associated with
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
—only the labels have changed.
Call
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau):=\tilde{\bf Q}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau\mathbb{B}_{d})
the
relabeled geometric quantile region of order
\tau
. Then,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)
satisfies the essential property of a quantile region of order
\tau
:

(viii)

the relabeled geometric quantile region

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)

has
{\rm P}
-probability content
\tau
for all
\tau\in~\![0,1)
, irrespective of
\rm P
.

We similarly define the
relabeled geometric quantile contours

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau):=\tilde{\bf Q}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),\quad\tau\in[0,1).

One may wonder whether
\tilde{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
now coincides with
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
(hence,
\tilde{\bf Q}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
with
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
). First, let us stress that, even though
\|\tilde{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}({\bf X})\|
, just like
\|{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}({\bf X})\|
, is uniformly distributed over
[0,1)
when
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
, the behavior of
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}({\bf X})/\|{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}({\bf X})\|
, to the best of our knowledge, remains unknown, and has not been investigated so far. Second, observe that
{\bf B}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
writes as the gradient
\nabla h_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
of the convex function

{\bf x}\mapsto h_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}({\bf x}):=\int_{0}^{\|{\bf x}\|}\kappa_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(s)\,ds,\quad{\bf x}\in{\mathbb R}^{d}.

It follows that
\tilde{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}=\nabla h_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}\circ\nabla\phi_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
. However, it is well known that the composition of two gradients of convex functions, in general, is not the gradient of a convex function. For these reasons, which do not constitute rigorous proofs, we believe that there is no obvious reason why the equality
\tilde{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}={\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
should hold for general
\rm P
. The simulations in Section
5
) below—which do not constitute mathematical proofs—actually suggest that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)
, for some distributions
\rm P
, very strongly differ.

4.2
Center-outward quantile regions and contours

Let
{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{+}
. It is shown in
Hallin et al. 2021
that

(i)

the regions
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)
are compact and arc-connected for all
\tau\in(0,1)

;

(ii)

the regions
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)
are stricly nested as
\tau
increases:
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau_{1})\subsetneq\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau_{2})
for any
0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<1
;

(iii)

the median region
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(0)
is compact, convex, and has Hausdorff dimension at most
(d-1)
;

(iv)

if, for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the density of
{\rm P}
is locally of class
\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}
, then the contour
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional manifold of class
\mathcal{C}^{k+1,\alpha}
for all
\tau\in(0,1)
;

(v)

for
d=1
,
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
,
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
reduce to
F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
,
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
,
{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
, and
{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
.

Note that (iv) was first established in
del
Barrio et al. 2020
when the support of
{\rm P}
is convex, then refined in
del Barrio
and Gonzáles-Sanz 2023
. By construction, we also have the following:

(vi)

the
\rm P
-probability content of
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)
equals the
{\rm U}_{d}
-probability content of
\tau\mathbb{B}_{d}
, which is
\tau
:
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)
, thus, qualify as the center-outward quantile region and quantile contour
of order

\tau
of
\rm P
;

(vii)

if
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
, then
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}({\bf X})\sim{\rm U}_{d}
; if
{\bf U}\sim{\rm U}_{d}
, then
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}({\bf U})\sim{\rm P}
: both
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
and
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
, thus, fully characterize
\rm P
.
3
3

3

{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
or

{\mathbb R}^{d}

\rm P

(viii)

the maps
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}

.

Finally,
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
and
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
enjoy the following invariance/equivariance properties (see Hallin et al. (2022)).

(ix)

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\mathbb R}^{d}
and denote by
{\bf O}
an arbitrary
d\times d
orthogonal matrix. Letting
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
, write
{\bf F}_{{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
,
{\bf Q}_{{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
, etc. for
{\bf F}_{{\rm P}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
,
{\bf Q}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
, etc. Then,

\displaystyle{\bf F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf x})={\bf O}{\bf F}_{\bf X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}({\bf x})\quad

\displaystyle{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle{\bf Q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}({\bf O}{\bf u})=\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf Q}_{\bf X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}({\bf u})\quad

\displaystyle{\bf u}\in\mathbb{B}_{d},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)\quad

\displaystyle\tau\in[0,1),

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}{\bf X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{\theta}+{\bf O}\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\bf X}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)\quad

\displaystyle\tau\in[0,1).

In particular, if
\rm P
enjoys symmetry properties (rotational, central, or with respect to some hyperplanes),
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
,
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{R}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)
enjoy the same symmetry properties as
\rm P
for all
\tau\in~\![0,1)
. This is not the case for the distribution and quantile functions resulting from a transport to the Lebesgue uniform over the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
which are favored by some authors.

These properties, which for dimension
d=1
reduce to those of
F^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
and
Q^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
and constitute the essence of the concepts of distribution and quantile functions, fully justify the terminology
center-outward distribution and quantile functions
for
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
and
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
. The
only
difference, here, between the properties of
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
and
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}
and those of
{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
and
{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}
is (vi) above, and this difference disappears if the relabeled functions
\tilde{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
and
\tilde{\bf Q}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
are substituted for
{\bf F}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
and
{\bf Q}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
, respectively.

In view of this, the relabeled geometric and the center-outward concepts, which, it seems, do not coincide, apparently both qualify as equally satisfactory multivariate concepts of distribution and quantile functions. Is there a way to break that tie and select one of them rather than the other? Since analytical expressions are impossible, one has to recur to numerical approximations—in statistical terms, simulations—in order to perform comparisons. This is what we are doing in the next section, where comparisons are based on simulations of the quantile contours
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{g}(\tau)
.

5
Geometric and center-outward quantile contours: an empirical comparative study

5.1
Numerical computation of contours

Except for some very special distributions, such as the spherical ones, geometric and center-outward quantiles in dimension
d\geq 2
, unfortunately, are sharing the inconvenient absence of analytical expressions. Numerical solutions are possible, though, based on simulations with high numbers of replications. For all
N\in\mathbb N
, denote by
{\rm P}_{N}
the empirical distribution of a random sample of size
N
drawn from
{\rm P}
. The accuracy of these numerical solutions relies on consistency results for the empirical counterparts
{\bf F}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
and
{\bf Q}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
in the geometric case (obtained by substituting
{\rm P}_{N}
for
{\rm P}
in (
7
)), for
{\bf F}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
and
{\bf Q}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
in the measure-transportation setting (see
Hallin et al. 2021
for precise definitions and details).

In the geometric case,
Mottonen
et al. 1997
established a Glivenko-Cantelli result of the form

\sup_{{\bf z}\in{\mathbb R}^{d}}\|{\bf F}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}({\bf z})-{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}({\bf z})\|\to 0

(12)

with
{\rm P}
-probability one as
N\to\infty
, provided that
{\rm P}
admits a bounded density. In fact, one can show that the bounded density assumption is superfluous: similarly to distribution functions in dimension
d=1
, the Glivenko-Cantelli result (
12
) is valid for an
arbitrary
probability measure. The same convergence holds for the sequence of relabeled counterparts
\tilde{{\bf F}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
of
{{\bf F}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
and
\tilde{{\bf F}}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
of
{{\bf F}}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}
; see
Konen 2022
.

In the measure transportation approach,
Hallin et al. 2021
proved a similar Glivenko-Cantelli result for an interpolated version
\overline{{\bf F}}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\,\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
of their empirical center-outward distribution functions
{\bf F}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}
:

\sup_{{\bf z}\in{\mathbb R}^{d}}\|\overline{{\bf F}}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\,\scriptscriptstyle\pm}({\bf z})-{\bf F}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}_{\rm P}({\bf z})\|\to 0

with
{\rm P}
-probability one as
N\to\infty
, when
{\rm P}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}^{+}
(see Section 2.2 for the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{+}
).

First, the contours obtained by measure transportation seem to better reflect the geometry of the underlying distributions. This is particularly obvious for the banana-shaped distribution (iv), where the concave shape of the mixture is well picked up by the center-outward contours but completely missed by the relabeled geometric ones. The same phenomenon also takes place, to a less spectacular extent, under distributions (i)-(iii): the relabeled geometric quantile region of order .75, under the exponential distribution (ii), largely extends beyond the exponential support, something the center-outward contours do not; the same geometric contours, for the skew-
t
distribution (iii), contrary to the center-outward ones, hardly exhibit any skewness.

(b)

Second, while relabeled geometric quantiles (as a direct consequence of their re-labelization rather than because of their L
1
characterization) do provide the desired control over the probability content of their regions, hence yield, for each
{\bf X}_{i}\in\mathbb{X}_{N}
, a sound concept
R^{(N)}_{i}\!\!\!\coloneqq\text{rank}(\|{\bf F}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}({\bf X}_{i})\|)
of center-outward multivariate rank, they do not provide any clear notion of signs. Such signs, quite on the contrary, are well defined, in the measure-transportation-based approach, for the sample points
{\bf X}_{i}\in~\!\mathbb{X}_{n}
, as the unit vectors
{\bf F}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}({\bf X}_{i})/\|{\bf F}_{{\rm P}_{N}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}({\bf X}_{i})\|
,
i=1,\ldots,N
, which are i.i.d. (uniform over the unit vectors associated with the
N
gridpoints in
{\mathfrak{G}}_{N}
and independent of the ranks
R^{(N)}_{1},\ldots,R^{(N)}_{N}
).

(c)

On the other hand, geometric contours are more regular than their measure-transportation-based counterparts. This is a consequence of (iii) and (iv) in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, respectively: geometric contours are continuous as soon as
{\rm P}
is non-atomic and smooth (at least
\mathcal{C}^{d-1}
) while the oscillations, hence also the variability, of the interpolated measure-transportation-based contours are further increased by the interpolation scheme.

While points (a) and (b) above are pleading for the measure-transportation-based concepts, the decisive argument in favor of the latter follows from a theoretical result by
Girard and
Stupfler 2017
on the “intriguing behavior” (a clear understatement) of high-order geometric quantile contours.

5.3
Numerical comparison of extreme contours

Girard and
Stupfler 2017
establish the following disturbing result for extreme geometric quantiles: if
{\rm P}
is a probability measure on
{\mathbb R}^{d}
with finite second-order moments, i.e.
{\rm E}_{\rm P}[\|{\bf X}\|^{2}]<\infty
for
{\bf X}\sim{\rm P}
with covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, then, denoting by Tr
(\bf A)
the trace of a square matrix
\bf A
,

\|{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}(\tau{\bf u})\|^{2}(1-\tau)\to\frac{1}{2}\Big(\Tr{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-{\bf u}^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf u}\Big)

as
\tau\to 1
for any
{\bf u}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. First, let us stress that the quantity
\Tr{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-{\bf u}^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf u}
is always non-negative, and is strictly positive as soon as
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is non-degenerate. In addition to providing the rate at which extreme geometric quantiles are “escaping to infinity,” this result also provides valuable insights on the shape of the corresponding extreme contours. For instance, taking for
{\bf u}
a unit eigenvector
{\bf u}_{\text{\rm max}}
associated with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
’s
largest
eigenvalue yields a norm
\|{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}(\tau{\bf u}_{\text{\rm max}})\|
for the contour
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{C}}_{\rm P}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}(\tau)
of order
\tau
in direction
\bf u
which is
minimal
for
\tau\to 1
; the same contour, in the direction
{\bf u}_{\text{\rm min}}
associated with the smallest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, asymptotically yields the
largest
norm
\|{\bf Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{\rm g}}}_{\rm P}(\tau{\bf u}_{\text{\rm min}})\|
. More generally, due to the fact that the shape factor
\Tr{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-{\bf u}^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf u}
is a decreasing function of
{\bf u}^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf u}
, the asymptotic behavior of extreme quantiles—moving away fast from the center along the eigendirections of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
associated with small eigenvalues, and slowly along the eigendirections of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
associated with large eigenvalues—is exactly the opposite of what it is expected to be. This is highly counterintuitive and, actually, totally unacceptable.

Figure 2 provides a finite-sample empirical illustration of this theoretical fact. We generated a sample of size
N=1,000
from a centered bivariate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
with covariance matrix

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}1/8&0\\
0&3/4\end{pmatrix}.

Then, for
\tau\in\{.90,.95,.99\}
, we computed
the empirical relabeled geometric and transportation-based quantile contours of order
\tau
, as described in Sections
5.1
and
5.2
, with
K=50
,
n_{R}=20
, and
n_{S}=50
for the latter (see Section
5.2
for notation).

Figure 2:
Geometric (red) and measure-transportation-based (blue) contours of order
\tau=.90,.95,.99
for samples of size
N=1,000
drawn from a (nonspherical) bivariate Gaussian distribution on
{\mathbb R}^{2}
. Empirical quantiles, both geometric and center-outward, are connected by lines in the plots.

Figure 2 perfectly illustrates the pathological phenomenon described by
Girard and
Stupfler 2017
: the geometric contours are much wider in the (horizontal) directions associated with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
’s smaller eigenvalue, and narrower in the (vertical) directions associated with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
’s larger eigenvalue. This pathological behaviour is increasingly severe as
\tau
approaches one: for
\tau=.90
, the contour is approximately circular. In sharp contrast, the center-outward contours, irrespective of
\tau
, are correctly accounting for the shape of the distribution and the relative magnitudes of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
’s eigenvalues.

6
Conclusions

Among all concepts of multivariate quantiles proposed in the literature, geometric quantiles (after due re-labelization) and measure-transportation-based center-outward quantiles are the most convincing ones: they both reduce to classical concepts in dimension one, and both satisfy most properties expected from quantiles, quantile regions, and quantile contours. Empirical comparisons and a highly pathological property of extreme geometric quantiles established by
Girard and
Stupfler 2017
, however, are tilting the balance in favour of the measure-transportation-based concept.
