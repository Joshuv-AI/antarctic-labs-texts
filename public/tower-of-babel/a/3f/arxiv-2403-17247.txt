DASA : Delay-Adaptive Multi-Agent Stochastic Approximation
Nicolò Dal Fabbro, Arman Adibi, H. Vincent Poor, Sanjeev R. Kulkarni, Aritra Mitra, George J. Pappas

Abstract
We consider a setting in which N N agents aim to speedup a common Stochastic Approximation (SA) problem by acting in parallel and communicating with a central server. We assume that the up-link transmissions to the server are subject to asynchronous and potentially unbounded time-varying delays. To mitigate the effect of delays and stragglers while reaping the benefits of distributed computation, we propose DASA , a Delay-Adaptive algorithm for multi-agent Stochastic Approximation. We provide a finite-time analysis of DASA assuming that the agents’ stochastic observation processes are independent Markov chains. Significantly advancing existing results, DASA is the first algorithm whose convergence rate depends only on the mixing time τ m ​ i ​ x \tau_{mix} and on the average delay τ a ​ v ​ g \tau_{avg} while jointly achieving an N N -fold convergence speedup under Markovian sampling. Our work is relevant for various SA applications, including multi-agent and distributed temporal difference (TD) learning, Q-learning and stochastic optimization with correlated data.

I
Introduction

Motivated by emerging applications in data-hungry algorithms like distributed large-scale reinforcement learning (RL), we study an instance of Stochastic Approximation (SA) in which multiple agents act in parallel to cooperatively solve a common SA problem. The main goal of splitting the computational effort across
N
agents is to achieve an
N
-fold convergence speedup in the sample complexity relative to when a single agent acts alone. Only recently, theoretical studies have shown the beneficial effect of distributed computing in multi-agent and federated SA when agents’ observations are temporally correlated (Markov) processes
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. However, in real-world large-scale communication networks and client-server architectures, inter-node communications are commonly subject to (potentially large) delays.
While several algorithms have been provided to study and mitigate the effect of delays in distributed asynchronous optimization and federated learning
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
under i.i.d. sampling, very little is known about distributed SA under Markovian sampling. Existing works, indeed, either provide algorithms that are heavily impacted by the maximum delay
[
8
,
9
]
(which can be potentially very large), or are not able to provide convergence speedups under Markovian sampling
[
10
]
. In the recent work in
[
8
]
, the authors study collaborative gains of parallel learners in RL under Markovian sampling, but they obtain convergence rates affected by the maximum network delay and provide convergence speedups only when the data are collected in an i.i.d. fashion. However, note that the main challenges in proving multi-agent convergence speedups of SA algorithms emerge when agents’ samples form temporally correlated trajectories. Indeed, even for single-agent settings, non-asymptotic convergence rates for SA under Markovian sampling have only recently been established
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. In our recent work
[
10
]
, we provide finite-time convergence rates for an SA setting under Markovian sampling in which the SA operator is computed with delayed updates, and we propose a delay-adaptive algorithm that replaces the dependence on the maximum delay
\tau_{max}
with a dependence on the average delay
\tau_{avg}
. However, the work in
[
10
]
only considers a single-agent setting. In this paper, we bridge this gap proposing and analyzing a new multi-agent algorithm for distributed asynchronous SA,
DASA
. The main contributions of this paper are as follows.

Contributions:

We propose and provide a finite-time analysis of
DASA
, that has the following unprecedented features: (i)
DASA
has a convergence rate that depends on the network delays only via the average delay
\tau_{avg}
and (ii) at the same time,
DASA
achieves an
N
-fold linear convergence speedup with the number of agents under Markovian sampling. Providing
jointly
(i) and (ii) presents major technical challenges that we carefully address in our analysis in Section
III
-
IV
.
Note that we do not need conditions on the maximum delay: it can be as large as the global number of iterations
T
and our results would still hold.
To validate our theoretical findings, we provide simple simulations on a distributed TD learning problem in Section
V
.

II
Problem Setting and
DASA

The goal of SA is to solve a root-finding problem formulated as follows:

\textrm{Find }{\boldsymbol{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\textrm{ such that }\bar{\mathbf{g}}({\boldsymbol{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{*}})=0,\hskip 5.69046pt

(1)

where, for a given approximation parameter
{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the deterministic function
\bar{\mathbf{g}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
represents the expectation of a noisy operator
\mathbf{g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},o_{k})
taken over distribution
\pi
, and
\{o_{k}\}
denotes a stochastic observation process. It is commonly assumed that
\{o_{k}\}
converges in distribution to
\pi

[
14
,
15
]
.

Distributed Asynchronous SA.
We consider a setting in which
N
agents communicate with a central server to collaboratively estimate
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
. To achieve this goal, the agents compute in parallel their versions of the noisy operator
\mathbf{g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k},o_{i,k})
, where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
is the approximation parameter at iteration
k
and
\{o_{i,k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is the local observation process of agent
i
. At each iteration, the agents transmit to a server their local operators via up-link communications subject to asynchronous time-varying delays. We denote by
\tau_{i,k}
the delay of agent
i
at iteration
k
. At each iteration
k
, the server can use the (delayed) update directions
\{\mathbf{g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{i,k}},o_{i,t_{i,k}})\}_{i=1}^{N}
to update the SA parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
, where
t_{i,k}=k-\tau_{i,k}
is the iteration at which the SA update direction of agent
i
was computed. On the other hand,
we assume that the down-link communication from the server to the agents is not subject to delays
. This assumption is practically motivated by the fact that in most client-server architectures the main communication bottleneck comes from up-link transmissions, rather than from down-link broadcasting.
We anticipate that this assumption can be removed and we plan to do so in future work.
The aggregation protocol is illustrated in Figure
1
. SA with delayed updates can estimate
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
, but with significant performance degradation in the rate, as theoretically shown, e.g., in
[
9
,
7
,
10
]
especially in the presence of large delays. To mitigate the impact of stragglers on the performance, we propose
DASA
, in which, using available delayed operators, the server updates the parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
according to a delay-adaptive update rule, which we describe next.

DASA
:
We now present
DASA
.
The server updates the parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
according to the following rule:

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k+1}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}+\alpha\mathbf{v}_{k},

(2)

where
\alpha
is a constant step-size/learning rate, and

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{k}\triangleq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{k}|}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\mathbf{g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{i,k}},o_{i,t_{i,k}})&\textrm{if}\hskip 2.84544pt\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{i_{med,k},k}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\|\leq{\epsilon}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases},

(3)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm, while
i_{med,k}
,
\mathcal{I}_{k}
and
\epsilon
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle i_{med,k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\arg\text{median}(\{\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{j,k}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\|\}_{j=1}^{N}),

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\{i:\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{i,k}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\|\leq\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{i_{med,k},k}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\|\},

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\triangleq\max\{\alpha^{1.5},\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{M}}\},

with
M=|\mathcal{I}_{k}|=\lceil\frac{N}{2}\rceil,\forall k\geq 0
.

Remark 1
.

Note that the size of
\mathcal{I}_{k}
is
|\mathcal{I}_{k}|=\lceil\frac{N}{2}\rceil
. Here, we assume without loss of generality that
N
is even. Therefore,
|\mathcal{I}_{k}|=\frac{N}{2}
. The proof is equivalent for odd
N
.

Remark 2
.

If
i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
, then we have

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{i,k}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\|\leq\epsilon.

(5)

The rationale behind
DASA
is to control the error introduced by delays, using, at each iteration, the information from the set of
M=N/2
agents in
\mathcal{I}_{k}
whose available operators were computed with iterates that are
the least stale at iteration
k
, as dictated by the median delay error index
i_{med,k}
. At the same time, averaging (
N/2
) noisy operators, we wish to benefit from a variance reduction effect, achieving an
N
-fold convergence speedup thanks to the independence of the different stochastic observation processes.
DASA
allows the server to control the error of the aggregated operator
\mathbf{v}_{k}
that is used to update
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
, via the threshold parameter
\epsilon
. When carefully chosing
\epsilon
, we obtain the convergence guarantees that we want:
a convergence rate that does not depend on the maximum delay and, jointly, a linear convergence speedup with the number of agents N
. Providing these convergence guarantees presents key technical challenges that we address in the next section.

III
Convergence Analysis

In this section, we provide the convergence analysis of
DASA
, under standard assumptions that we illustrate next. These assumptions are common in most finite-time analyses of SA algorithms
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

Assumption 1
.

Problem (
1
) admits a solution
{\boldsymbol{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{*}}
, and
\exists\,\mu>0
such that for all
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we have

{\langle{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*},\bar{\mathbf{g}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\bar{\mathbf{g}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*})\rangle\leq-\mu\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}\|^{2}.}

(6)

Assumption 2
.

There exists
L>0
such that for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
o\in\{o_{t}\}
, we have

\|\mathbf{g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},o)-\mathbf{g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2},o)\|\leq L\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}\|.

(7)

Furthermore, there exists
\sigma\geq 1
such that for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},o)\|\leq L(\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|+\sigma).

(8)

Assumption 3
.

For each agent
i=1,\dots,N
, the stochastic observation process
\{o_{i,k}\}
is an aperiodic and irreducible Markov chain. For each
i\neq j
and for each iteration
k
,
o_{i,k}
and
o_{j,k}
are statistically independent.

Assumption
1
ensures the strong monotonicity of the expected operator
\bar{\mathbf{g}}(\cdot)
, which is a standard requirement for proving linear convergence rates.
Assumption
2
imposes Lipschitz continuity on the noisy operator
\mathbf{g}(\cdot,\cdot)
, which is also common in SA analyses.
Assumption
3
implies that the Markov chain
\{o_{k}\}
mixes at a geometric rate.

Definition 1
.

Let
\tau_{mix}
be such that, for every
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for all
i=1,...,N
and time-step
\bar{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta},o_{i,t})|o_{i,\bar{t}}\right]-\bar{\mathbf{g}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|\leq\alpha^{2}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|+\sigma\right),

(9)

\displaystyle{\forall t:t-\bar{t}\geq\tau_{mix}},\forall o_{i,\bar{t}}.

Definition
1
introduces the mixing time
\tau_{mix}
, which quantifies the number of steps after which the conditional expectation of
\mathbf{g}({\boldsymbol{\theta}},o_{i,t})
is close to its stationary expectation
\mathbf{\bar{g}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, after conditioning over any past observation
o_{i,\bar{t}}
. Note that, thanks to Assumption
3
, for any
i=1,...N
, the Markov chain
\{o_{i,k}\}
mixes at a geometric rate, and hence there exists some
K\geq 1
such that
\tau_{mix}\leq K\log(\frac{1}{\alpha})
.
Under the assumptions above, we are now ready to state the main result of this paper, which is the non-asymptotic convergence analysis of
DASA
. We provide the convergence result in the following theorem, whose proof requires a novel and careful analysis, which we defer to the next section. In all of our results, we use the notation
y=O(x)
to describe the relation between positive terms
x
and
y
, meaning that the value
y
is at most equal to some positive constant multiplying the quantity
x
. We define
\delta_{k}\triangleq\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}\|
, and assume without loss of generality that
\sigma\geq\max\{1,\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}\|,\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}\|\}
,
L\geq 1
and
\mu\leq 1
. We also define an indicator function
\mathcal{X}_{k}
, that is equal to 1 when the median agent’s parameter is not too far away from the server’s parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}
, and the average delay
\tau_{avg}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if}\hskip 2.84544pt\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{i_{med,k},k}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}\|\leq{\epsilon}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases},

(10)

\displaystyle\tau_{avg}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i,t}.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the update rule of
DASA
in (
2
) and let
T\geq 0
be such that
\mathcal{X}_{T}=1
. There exists a universal constant
C_{1}\geq 1
such that for
\alpha\leq\frac{\mu}{C_{1}L^{2}\tau_{mix}}
the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{T}^{2}\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{-\alpha\mu T}{8\left(\tau_{avg}+1\right)}\right)O\left(\sigma^{2}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}L^{2}\alpha\tau_{mix}}{\mu}\right)\left(\frac{1}{N}+\alpha\right)

Main Takeaways:
We now discuss the main takeaways from Theorem
1
. We first note that, with a constant step size
\alpha
satisfying the theorem conditions,
DASA
guarantees mean-square exponential convergence of the approximation parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}
to a ball around
{\boldsymbol{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{*}}
. We now compare this result against existing related work and comment on the effect of the delays on the convergence rate. We also illustrate explicitly the linear convergence speedup achieved by
DASA
.

Effect of Delays and Dependence on
\tau_{avg}
.
Note that the exponential convergence rate of
DASA
depends on the average delay
\tau_{avg}
, while neither the rate nor the convergence ball are affected by the maximum delay
\tau_{max}
. The convergence ball, in fact, does not depend on the delay sequence at all. In this regard, note that convergence rates of non-adaptive SA algorithms under delayed updates are impacted by the maximum delay
\tau_{max}
both in the convergence rate and in the noise ball of convergence,
even in a single-agent setting
[
10
]
. At the same time, the step size choice that guarantees the convergence of
DASA
does not require any knowledge of the delay sequence, while it requires knowledge of the mixing time of the Markov sampling process, which is commonly required for finite-time convergence results in SA
[
12
,
11
,
13
]
. With respect to the single-agent SA setting with delayed updates in
[
10
]
,
DASA
manages to remove the dependence on the maximum delay
\tau_{max}
from both the convergence rate and from the step size choice even in the multi-agent setting
while jointly achieving a linear convergence speedup with the number of agents under Markovian sampling
. We now comment on the convergence speedup.

Linear Convergence Speedup.

We now compare the convergence bound of
DASA
with the convergence bound obtained by the single-agent delay-adaptive algorithm provided in
[
10
]
. Note that the noise ball is deflated by
\frac{1}{N}+\alpha
, which, for a proper choice of step size
\alpha
and for a number of iterations
T
large enough, provides a linear convergence speedup with the number of agents
N
. In particular, we can easily show that if we set
\alpha
and
T
as follows:

\alpha=O\left(\frac{\log(NT)\tau_{avg}}{T\mu}\right),\hskip 5.69046ptT\geq\frac{CN\log(NT)\tau_{avg}\tau_{mix}L^{2}}{\mu^{2}},

with
C\geq 2
a constant, then the mean-square error after
T
iterations is

\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{T}^{2}\right]\leq O\left(\frac{\log(NT)\tau_{avg}\tau_{mix}\sigma^{2}L^{2}}{NT\mu}\right),

(12)

which implies that
DASA
is
sample efficient
and that, despite the joint presence of asynchronous delays and Markovian sampling, we can achieve an
N
-fold linear convergence speedup when
N
agents act in parallel, without dependence on the maximum delay.
DASA
is the first algorithm for multi-agent/distributed SA with these convergence guarantees.

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{k}

\displaystyle\triangleq\{\tau_{i,k}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\},

Outline of the Analysis:
As a first step, we here provide a high-level outline of the convergence analysis of

. We start by writing the following recursion, which holds for the iterations

in which

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{k+1}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{k}^{2}\right]-2\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\langle{\mathbf{v}_{k}},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}\rangle\right]+\alpha^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{k}^{2}\right]-2\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\langle{\bar{\mathbf{g}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k})},{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}\rangle\right]

. The main objective of the analysis is to obtain a one-step bound of the following form (for iterations

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{k+1}^{2}\right]

(15)

\displaystyle+O\left(\sigma^{2}L^{2}\alpha^{2}\tau_{mix}\right)\left(\frac{1}{N}+\alpha\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\left({\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}}\right)^{2}

We start the convergence analysis by proving a lemma, partially inspired by Lemma 4 in

Lemma 1
.

Let
\tau_{avg}
be the average delay experienced by agents up to iterate

\tau_{avg}=\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i,t}
. The number of updates that

\lceil{\frac{T}{8\tau_{avg}+8}}\rceil
.

Proof.

Recall the definition of the average delay up to time
T

\tau_{avg}=\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i,t}
.
Consider
U_{2\tau_{avg}}
, the number of steps

for which the delay
\tau_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i,t}
is at least
2\tau_{avg}
. We must have
U_{2\tau_{avg}}\leq\frac{T}{2}
(otherwise the total sum of delays exceeds

, contradicting the definition of

). On the other hand, let

be the number of updates that the algorithm makes. Let

be the steps in which an update is made. Denote

and

. Now, fix

and consider the steps at times

. In all those steps no update takes place and

. We must have
\tau_{i,s_{n}}>n
for all

for at least
\frac{N}{2}
agents (otherwise

for more than

agents and an update occurs since the median becomes zero). In particular, we have that

for at least

of agents in at least

\tau_{s_{n}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i,s_{n}}\geq 2\tau_{avg}
in at least

\displaystyle\text{\# of steps in $[t_{i},t_{i+1}]$ with delay bigger or equal to $2\tau_{avg}$}

(19)

\displaystyle\geq\max\{0,t_{i+1}-t_{i}-1-4\tau_{avg}\}

\displaystyle\geq t_{i+1}-t_{i}-1-4\tau_{avg}.

Hence,
U_{2\tau_{avg}}\geq\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(t_{i+1}-t_{i}-1-4\tau_{avg})=T-m(1+4\tau_{avg}).

Finally, it follows that

which implies

.

Lemma 2
.

For

, the following holds,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle D

Proof.

V
Experiments

In this section, we provide simulation results to validate our theoretical analysis and show the superior performance of
DASA
when compared to a non-adaptive baseline of asynchronous SA. We consider a simple instance of linear SA, i.e., temporal difference (TD) learning with linear function approximation. The setting that we consider is similar to the one adopted for the simulations in
[
18
,
2
]
of federated TD learning, with the difference that we let the agents’ TD update directions be subject to delays, i.e., the server updates the TD approximation parameter with
\mathbf{v}_{k}
defined in (
3
), where
\mathbf{g}\left({{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t_{i,k}},o_{t_{i,k}}}\right)
is the TD direction computed by agent
i
at (delayed) time
t_{i,k}
. For the TD learning setting, the SA approximation setting consists of estimating the best approximation
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
of a value function associated to a policy
\mu
that induces a Markov chain when applied to a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma)
, where we consider a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
of dimension
n=30
. With respect to the other notation,
\mathcal{A}
is a finite set of actions,
\mathcal{P}
is the set of action-dependent Markov transition kernels,
\mathcal{R}
is a reward function, and the discount factor is
\gamma=0.5
. We approximate the value function with a set of
r=10
basis vectors. For the considered TD learning setting, we have
\mu=(1-\gamma)\omega
, where
\omega>0
is an eigenvalue that we compute explicitly to set the step sizes. We set the maximum delay to
\tau_{max}=50
. In Figure
2
-(a), we consider a distributed SA setting with
N=10
agents and compare
DASA
against (i) a version of distributed SA with no delays, which we refer to as “Non-delayed”, and (ii) a non-adaptive version of distributed asynchronous SA, which we simply call “Delayed”. Convergence guarantees for “Delayed” (in a single-agent setting) have been studied in
[
7
]
, where it was shown that tight convergence bounds are achievable for a step size
\alpha
inversely proportional to
\tau_{mix}\left({\alpha}\right)+\tau_{max}
. The plot clearly shows the advantage of the delay-adaptive solution (
DASA
) when compared to the non-adaptive SA algorithm: for the same number of updates, the approximation error achieved by
DASA
is almost two orders of magnitude smaller than that of the non-adaptive algorithm (“Delayed”). We can also notice how the approximation error to which
DASA
converges is quite close to the approximation error of the “Non-delayed” version. In Figure

comparing a single-agent configuration (
N=1

N=20

N=1,20

N=20
agents are estimating the value function in parallel, the approximation error is improved by more than one order of magnitude, which validates the convergence speedup effect. We can indeed note that the speedup obtained by
DASA
has a similar performance to the speedup obtained for distributed SA without delays.

VI
Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced
DASA
, a novel algorithm that effectively mitigates the effect of asynchronous and potentially unbounded network delays and ensures collaborative speedups in multi-agent SA. Future works include the design of more advanced delay-adaptive algorithms, extensions to fully distributed (peer-to-peer) network architectures and further empirical evaluations.
