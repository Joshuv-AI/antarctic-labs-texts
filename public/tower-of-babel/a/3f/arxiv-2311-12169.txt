Optimal retirement choice under age-dependent force of mortality
Giorgio Ferrari And Shihao Zhu

Abstract
. This paper examines the retirement decision, optimal investment, and consumption strategies under an age-dependent force of mortality. We formulate the optimization problem as a combined stochastic control and optimal stopping problem with a random time horizon, featuring three state variables: wealth, labor income, and force of mortality. To address this problem, we transform it into its dual form, which is a finite time horizon, three-dimensional degenerate optimal stopping problem with interconnected dynamics. We establish the existence of an optimal retirement boundary that splits the state space into continuation and stopping regions. Regularity of the optimal stopping value function is derived and the boundary is proved to be Lipschitz continuous, and it is characterized as the unique solution to a nonlinear integral equation, which we compute numerically. In the original coordinates, the agent thus retires whenever her wealth exceeds an age-, labor income- and mortality-dependent transformed version of the optimal stopping boundary. We also provide numerical illustrations of the optimal strategies, including the sensitivities of the optimal retirement boundary concerning the relevant model’s parameters.



Keywords:
Optimal retirement time; Optimal consumption; Optimal portfolio choice; Duality; Optimal stopping; Free boundary; Stochastic control.

91B70, 93E20, 60G40.

1.
Introduction

The timing of retirement is a crucial financial decision that individuals must face as they approach later stages of life. However, determining the appropriate moment to retire is a complex undertaking, as it is influenced by numerous factors. One of the most prominent and evident predictors of retirement decisions is an individual’s chronological age, as highlighted in empirical studies such as
Wang and Shi 2014
. Additionally, research indicates that individuals typically postpone retirement until they possess the necessary financial means to do so, with retirement timing being linked to both wealth and labor income, as explored in
Honig 1998
. In line with rational decision-making principles and the life-cycle model in economics, subjective life expectancy (how long one expects to live or expected mortality) also plays a significant role in retirement timing.
Szinovacz et al. 2014
used data from the Health and Retirement Study and found that expected mortality risk influenced retirement plans, and the effects were especially strong for expectations to work beyond age 65. Furthermore, factors like health status and familiar caregiving responsibilities contribute to the complexity of retirement decision-making. For a comprehensive examination of these factors, please refer to
Fisher et al. 2016
’s insightful review.

In addition to the aforementioned empirical studies, the field of optimal retirement time decision-making has been enriched by various theoretical models from a financial perspective. The historical origins of the optimal retirement time problem can be traced back to seminal works such as
Jin Choi and Shim 2006
,
Farhi and Panageas 2007
,
Choi et al. 2008
,
Dybvig and Liu 2010
, and
Dybvig and Liu 2011
. These foundational contributions paved the way for investigating the optimal investment and consumption behavior of individuals facing retirement decisions, resulting in significant advancements across various contexts. Notable advancements include the introduction of features like mandatory retirement dates and early retirement options
Yang and Koo 2018
, the consideration of consumption ratcheting
Jeon and Park 2020
, the exploration of partial information
Chen et al. 2022
, the incorporation of habit persistence
Chen et al. 2018
,
Guan et al. 2020
, the examination of return ambiguity in risky asset prices
Park and Wong 2023
, the analysis of non-Markovian environments
Yang et al. 2021
, and the study of heterogeneous consumption patterns involving basic and luxury goods
Jang et al. 2022
.

However, an important characteristic shared by much of the existing literature is the absence of consideration for mortality risk or the assumption of a constant force of mortality. This requirement, although common, can impose significant restrictions on the modeling framework. In reality, mortality has long been studied in the fields of mathematics and demography. Abraham De Moivre, in 1725, proposed perhaps the earliest mathematical mortality model, suggesting that the probability of surviving from birth to age
x
follows a linear function of age. Moreover, the most successful and influential mortality law is known as Gompertz’s law, formulated by
Gompertz 1825
. This law describes a function with exponentially increasing mortality rates and has found application in insurance economics as well (see, e.g.,
Milevsky and Young 2007
and
De Angelis and
Stabile 2019b
for the optimal annuitization problem with a Gompertz force of mortality).

In this paper, we aim to quantitatively examine the interplay between age, wealth, labor income, and mortality on the optimal retirement time. We extend the existing model by incorporating an age-dependent force of mortality, specifically the Gompertz model, to comprehensively analyze these influences. Our study focuses on an individual who confronts a mandatory retirement date but possesses the option to retire earlier, assuming irreversibility in retirement decisions. The individual can allocate her wealth to consumption and invest in a risky asset. Additionally, the labor income of the individual is stochastic until retirement, and post-retirement utility increases due to the availability of more leisure time.

As highlighted by
Chen et al. 2021
, “the optimal retirement problem of a sophisticated individual with an age-dependent force of mortality has not been solved analytically.” Consequently, the primary objectives of our paper are twofold: first, to determine optimal consumption and portfolio-choice strategies considering realistic longevity risks stemming from an age-dependent force of mortality, and second, to fully characterize the optimal retirement time. By addressing these objectives, our study intends to fill the existing gap in the literature.

Notably, our findings, which are collected in Section 5 and Section 6, are somewhat intuitively convincing. For example, we show that it is optimal to retire when the agent’s wealth first reaches an endogenously determined boundary surface, which depends on the agent’s age, labor income and mortality. Intuitively, if the agent is sufficiently rich (her wealth exceeds the corresponding boundary), then retirement should be performed immediately; otherwise, it is optimal to wait for an increase of the wealth. Moreover, we show that both consumption and portfolio choices jump at the endogenous retirement time, which is consistent with
Dybvig and Liu 2010
and
Chen et al. 2018
. We also provide some interesting economic implications of the optimal retirement boundary through a numerical study in Section 6.

1.1.
Overview of the mathematical analysis

From a mathematical point of view, we model the previous problem as a
random time horizon, three-dimensional stochastic control problem with discretionary stopping.
The three coordinates of the state process are the wealth process
W
, the force of mortality process
M
, and the stochastic labor income
Y
. The dominant feature of the wealth process
W
is that it is not the same before and after the retirement time
\tau
. The agent’s aim is to choose consumption rate
c
, portfolio
\pi
, and the retirement time
\tau
in order to maximize the total expected utility of consumption
c
, up to the random death time
\eta
.

In the literature, the derivation of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation is a frequently employed approach when analyzing stochastic optimization problem with Markovian processes. In our specific case, the corresponding HJB equation (cf. (
A.7
)) turns out to be an involved combination of an HJB-type equation (reflecting the investment and consumption optimization) with a variational inequality (reflecting the retirement optimization). However, studying the properties of the value function and the optimal strategies, particularly the optimal stopping boundary, proves to be challenging when directly studying the equation. This difficulty arises due to the presence of a three-dimensional state process, rendering the classical “guess and verify” method ineffective.

In order to tame the intricate mathematical structure of our problem, where the consumption and portfolio choices nontrivially interact with the retirement decision, we combine a duality and a free-boundary approach, and proceed in our analysis as it follows.

Step 1.
First, we conduct successive transformations (see Section 3) that connect the original stochastic control-stopping problem (with value function
V
) with its dual problem (with value function
J
) by martingale and duality methods (similar to
Karatzas and Wang 2000
or
Yang and Koo 2018
). The dual problem (with value function
J
) is a
finite time-horizon, three-dimensional optimal stopping problem with interconnected dynamics
, which is difficult to tackle directly. Due to the intrinsic homogeneous structure of the considered power utility function, we reduce the dimensionality by applying a measure change method, and reach a reduced-version dual stopping problem (with value function
\widetilde{J}
).

Step 2.
We then study the reduced-version dual problem, which is a
finite time-horizon, two-dimensional optimal stopping problem with interconnected dynamics
. The newly introduced state variable
X
(depending on the dual process
Z
and the labour income process
Y
) evolves as a geometric Brownian motion, where the drift depends on the mortality force
M
. The coupling between the two components of the state process makes the optimal stopping problem quite intricate.

It is also worth pointing out that the force of mortality process
M
does not possess any diffusive term, which leads to a novel analysis of the regularity of
\widehat{J}
(given by the difference of
\widetilde{J}
and the smooth payoff of immediate stopping; see (
4.12
)). As a matter of fact, the process (
X,M
) is a degenerate diffusion process (in the sense that the differential operator of (
X,M
) is a degenerate parabolic operator) so that the study of the regularity of
\widehat{J}
in the interior of its continuation region cannot hinge on classical results for parabolic PDEs (see, e.g.,
Friedman 1982
).

Additional technical difficulties arise when trying to infer properties of the optimal stopping boundary
b
. In fact, since the regularity of
\widehat{J}
in the interior of its continuation region
\mathcal{W}
cannot be established a priori via classical PDE results, we were unable to establish continuity for the mapping
(t,m)\mapsto b(t,m)
, even if monotonicity properties of the latter could be easily proved. It is indeed well known in optimal stopping and free-boundary theory that interior regularity of
\widehat{J}
in
\mathcal{W}
together with the monotonicity of
b
are the key ingredients for a rigorous study of the continuity of the boundary (for a deeper discussion please refer to
De Angelis 2015
).

We overcome those major technical hurdles by proving that the optimal boundary is in fact a locally Lipschitz-continuous function of time
t
and force of mortality
m
, without employing neither monotonicity of the boundary nor classical results on interior regularity for parabolic PDEs. In order to achieve this goal, we rely only upon probabilistic methods borrowed from
De Angelis and
Stabile 2019a
, which are then specifically adjusted to tackle our problem.

As a matter of fact, we first prove that
\widehat{J}
is locally Lipschitz continuous and obtain probabilistic representations of its weak-derivatives (cf.
De Angelis and
Stabile 2019a
). Then, through a suitable application of the method developed in
De Angelis and
Stabile 2019b
, by means of a version of the implicit function theorem for Lipschitz mappings (cf.
Papi 2005
), we can show that the free boundary surface
(t,m)\mapsto b(t,m)
is locally-Lipschitz continuous. This enables us to prove that the optimal stopping time
(t,x,m)\mapsto\tau^{*}(t,x,m)
is continuous, which in turn gives that
\widehat{J}
is a continuously differentiable functions of its three variables. Being that the process
X
is the only diffusive one, the
C^{1}
-property of
\widehat{J}
implies that
\widehat{J}_{xx}
admits a continuous extension to the closure of the continuation region. Notice that it is in fact this regularity that could had not been derived from standard results on PDEs nor from
Peskir 2022
, and it is in fact this regularity that allows (via an application of a weak version of Dynkin’s formula) to derive an integral equation which is uniquely solved by the free boundary.

Step 3
. After proving the strict convexity of
{J}
, we can come back to the original coordinates’ system and via the duality relations we obtain the optimal consumption and portfolio policies, as well as the optimal retirement time, in terms of the optimal stopping boundary and value function (cf. Section 5).

Overall, we believe that the contributions of this paper are the following. As we discussed before, even though the literature on optimal retirement time problems is extensive from different perspectives, the introduction of the age-dependent force of mortality constitutes a novelty. From a mathematical point of view, we provide a rigorous theoretical analysis of the optimal retirement time in terms of the optimal boundary
\widehat{b}
. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first work providing a complete analytical characterization of the value function and of the optimal strategies in the optimal retirement time problem with age-dependent force of morality. Furthermore, we believe that the dual optimal stopping problem with the degenerate parabolic operator, studied as a device to characterize the optimal solution of the optimal retirement time problem, is of interest of its own. By performing a thorough analysis on the regularity of
\widehat{J}
(a transformed version of the dual value function) and of the free boundary, we are able to provide a complete characterization of the optimal retirement time strategy through a nonlinear integral equation. The analysis in this paper is completed by solving numerically the integral equation and studying its sensitivity to variations in the model’s parameters, thus extending the results of the related optimal retirement time model.

1.2.
Organization of the paper

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the optimal retirement time decision model with an age-dependent force of mortality. We transform the original stochastic control-stopping problem into a pure stopping problem in Section 3, while in Section 4 we study the optimal stopping problem. In Section 5, we provide the optimal retirement time boundary, optimal consumption plan and optimal portfolio in primal variables, and in Section 6 we present a numerical study and provide some economic implications. Appendix A collects the proofs of some results of Sections 4 and 5, whereas Appendix B includes some auxiliary results needed in the paper. In Appendix C, we give the details of the numerical method used in Section 6.

2.
Setting and problem formulation

2.1.
The age-dependent mortality rate

Let
({\Omega},{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
be a complete probability space, endowed with a filtration
\mathbb{F}:=\{\mathcal{F}_{s},s\geq t\}
satisfying the usual conditions, where
t\geq 0
is a fixed initial time. The remaining lifetime of an agent
\eta
is an exogenous non-negative continuous random variable and is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
. Moreover,
\eta
has the cumulative distribution function
F
, density function
f
, so that, for any
s\geq t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\eta\geq s)=1-{F}(s)=\int^{\infty}_{s}f(u)du

is the probability that the agent is living at least another
s
years. The force of mortality (also called the hazard function) represents the instantaneous death rate for the agent surviving to time
s
, and it is defined by

\displaystyle M_{s}:=\lim_{\Delta s\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}(s\leq\eta<s+\Delta s|\eta\geq s)}{\Delta s}.

Then the force of mortality
M
is expressed by

\displaystyle M_{s}=\frac{f(s)}{1-F(s)},

which means that
F(s)=1-\exp\{-\int^{s}_{t}M_{u}du\}
, for
s\geq t
.

Consider an agent whose force of mortality rate evolves according to the standard Gompertz model (see, e.g.,
Gompertz 1825
). The process
M:=\{M_{s},s\geq t\}
thus follows the dynamics

(2.1)

\displaystyle dM_{s}=aM_{s}ds,\ M_{t}=m>0,

with
a>0
. Notice that the usual form for the Gompertz model is
M_{s}=a_{0}^{-1}e^{(x_{0}+s-m_{0})/{a_{0}}}
, hence we are using
a=\frac{1}{a_{0}}
and
m=a_{0}^{-1}e^{(x_{0}-m_{0})/{a_{0}}}
. Here,
x_{0}
denotes the agent’s age at initial time
t
,
m_{0}
is called the modal value, and
a_{0}
is the dispersion coefficient for the Gompertz model. This model is simple, and takes advantage of a long experience of calibration to real populations.

2.2.
The financial market and labour income

We assume that the agent invests in a financial market with two assets. One of them is a risk-free bond, whose price
S^{0}:=\{S^{0}_{s},s\geq t\}
evolves as

\displaystyle dS^{0}_{s}=rS^{0}_{s}ds,\quad S^{0}_{t}=s^{0}>0,

where
r>0
is a constant risk-free rate. The second one is a stock, whose price is denoted by
S:=\{S_{s},s\geq t\}
and it satisfies the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle dS_{s}

\displaystyle=\mu S_{s}dt+\sigma S_{s}dB_{s},\quad S_{t}=s>0,

where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma>0
are given constants. Here,
B:=\{B_{s},s\geq t\}
is an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted standard Brownian motion under
\mathbb{P}
.

For the agent, there is a mandatory retirement time
T\in[t,\infty)
. Let
\tau\in[t,T]
be an
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time representing the time at which the agent chooses to retire. The agent receives a stochastic labor income
Y:=\{Y_{s},t\leq s\leq\tau\}
as long as she is not retired. We assume the labor income to be spanned by the market and that we can express it as a geometric Brownian motion, similar to e.g.
Bodie et al. 2004
and
Dybvig and Liu 2010
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle Y_{s}=ye^{(\mu_{y}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{y}^{2})(s-t)+\sigma_{y}(B_{s}-B_{t})},\ \text{for}\ t<s\leq\tau,\ Y_{t}=y>0,

with
Y_{s}=0
for
s>\tau
. Here
\mu_{y}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma_{y}>0
are constants, representing the instantaneous growth rate and the volatility of the labor income, respectively.

We define the market price of risk
\theta:=\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}
and the state-price-density process
\xi_{s,t}:=e^{-r(s-t)-\theta(B_{s}-B_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}(s-t)}
. Since the market is complete and the labor income is perfectly correlated with the market, the present value of the future labour income
g(s)
at time
s<T
(also called “human capital” as in
Dybvig and Liu 2010
), under the assumption that the agent does not choose early retirement and the agent is always alive, is given by

\displaystyle g(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int^{T}_{s}\xi_{u,s}Y_{u}du\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{s}\bigg]=Y_{s}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int^{T}_{s}\xi_{u,s}\frac{Y_{u}}{Y_{s}}du\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{s}\bigg]=Y_{s}\bigg[\int^{T}_{s}e^{(-r+\mu_{y}-\sigma_{y}\theta)(u-s)}du\bigg]

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &Y_{s}\frac{1-e^{-\kappa(T-s)}}{\kappa},\ &\text{if}\ \kappa\neq 0,\\
&Y_{s}(T-s),\ &\text{if}\ \kappa=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\kappa:=r-\mu_{y}+\sigma_{y}\theta
is the effective discount rate for labor income, which is assumed to be positive (see also
Dybvig and Liu 2011
and
Guan et al. 2020
for a similar requirement). Then, for
s\leq T
, we define

\displaystyle q(s)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1-e^{-\kappa(T-s)}}{\kappa},\ &\text{if}\ \kappa\neq 0,\\
&T-s,\ &\text{if}\ \kappa=0,\end{aligned}\right.

so that
g(s)=q(s)Y_{s}
.

The agent also consumes from her wealth, while investing in the financial market. Denoting by
\pi_{s}
the amount of wealth invested in the stock at time
s
, the agent then also chooses the rate of spending in consumption
c_{s}
at time
s
. Therefore, the agent’s wealth
W:=\{W^{c,\pi,\tau}_{s},s\geq t\}
evolves as

(2.4)

\displaystyle dW^{c,\pi,\tau}_{s}=[\pi_{s}(\mu-r)+rW^{c,\pi,\tau}_{s}-c_{s}+Y_{s}\mathds{1}_{\{s\leq\tau\}}]ds+\pi_{s}\sigma dB_{s},\quad W_{t}^{c,\pi,\tau}=w.

In the following, we shall simply write
W
to denote
W^{c,\pi,\tau}
, where needed.

2.3.
The optimization problem

Here and in the sequel, we write
\mathcal{O}:=[t,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}
,
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}:=[t,T]\times(-g(t),+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
and
\mathcal{U}:=[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, with
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=(0,\infty)
. We denote by
\mathcal{S}_{t,s}
the class of
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times
\tau:\Omega\to[t,s]
for
t\leq s\leq T
, and let
\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{S}_{t,T}
. Then we introduce the set of admissible strategies as it follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(t,w,m,y)\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
be given and fixed. The triplet of choices
(c,\pi,\tau)
is called an
admissible strategy
for
(t,w,m,y)
, and we write
(c,\pi,\tau)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,y)
, if it satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

c
and
\pi
are progressively measurable with respect to
\mathbb{F}
,
\tau\in\mathcal{S}
;

(ii)

c_{s}\geq 0
for all
s\geq t
and
\int_{0}^{\Theta}(c_{s}+|\pi_{s}|^{2})ds<\infty

\mathbb{P}
-a.s., for any
\Theta>0
;

(iii)

W^{c,\pi,\tau}_{s}>-g(s)\mathds{1}_{\{s<\tau\}}
for all
s\geq t
, where
g(s)
is defined in (
2.2
) if
s<T
and
g(s)=0
if
s\geq T
.

Condition (iii) means that the agent is allowed to borrow money and hold a negative wealth level, while the amount of money she borrows cannot exceed the total value of future labour income. Once the agent retires, she is prohibited from any borrowing.

The preferences of the agent are described through a power utility function. Due to a larger amount of free time when retired, the agent assigns a higher utility value to consumption during retirement, represented by a constant weighting
K>1
. The utility of the agent for consumption
c_{s}
at time
s
is

\displaystyle U(c_{s})=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\Big[(\mathds{1}_{\{s<\tau\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{\tau\leq s\}}K)\cdot c_{s}\Big]^{1-\gamma},\ \gamma\neq 1,\ \gamma>0.

Specifically, we call
U_{1}
the utility function before retirement, i.e., for
s<\tau
,

\displaystyle U_{1}(c_{s}):=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}c_{s}^{1-\gamma};

on the other hand, we call
U_{2}
the utility function after retirement, i.e., for
s\geq\tau
,

\displaystyle U_{2}(c_{s}):=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}(K\cdot c_{s})^{1-\gamma}.

From the perspective of time
t
, the agent’s aim is then to maximize over all
(c,\pi,\tau)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,y)
the expected intertemporal utility functional

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\eta}e^{-\beta(s-t)}U(c_{s})ds\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t}\bigg],

where
\beta>0
is a constant representing the discount rate. Thanks to Fubini’s Theorem and independence between
\eta
and
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
, we can disentangle the market risk and mortality risk and write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\eta}e^{-\beta(s-t)}U(c_{s})ds\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U(c_{s})ds\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t}\bigg].

Hence, given the Markovian setting, the agent aims at determining

(2.5)

\displaystyle V(t,w,m,y)=\sup_{(c,\pi,\tau)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,y)}\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U(c_{s})ds\bigg],

where
\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}
denote the expectation under
\mathbb{P}
conditioned on
W_{t}=w,M_{t}=m
and
Y_{t}=y
. In the rest of the paper, we shall focus on (
2.5
).

3.
From control-stopping to pure stopping

3.1.
The static budget constraint

An application of Itô’s formula yields

\displaystyle e^{-rs-\theta B_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}s}(W_{s}+g(s))+\int^{s}_{t}e^{-ru-\theta B_{u}-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}u}c_{u}du

(3.1)

\displaystyle=e^{-rt-\theta B_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}t}(w+g(t))+\int^{s}_{t}e^{-ru-\theta B_{u}-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}u}\Big(\pi_{u}\sigma-(W_{u}+g(u))\theta+g(u)\sigma_{y}\Big)dB_{u},\ s\leq\tau,

and

\displaystyle e^{-rs-\theta B_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}s}W_{s}+\int^{s}_{t}e^{-ru-\theta B_{u}-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}u}c_{u}du

(3.2)

\displaystyle=e^{-rt-\theta B_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}t}w+\int^{s}_{t}e^{-ru-\theta B_{u}-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}u}(\pi_{u}\sigma-W_{u}\theta)dB_{u},\ s>\tau.

Since
W_{s}+g(s)\mathds{1}_{\{s<\tau\}}>0
for any
s\geq t
, we can deduce that
W_{s}+g(s)\mathds{1}_{\{s\leq\tau\}}>0
for any
s\geq t
from the continuity of
W+g(\cdot)
.
For an admissible plan
(c,\pi,\tau)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,y)
, the left-hand side of (
3.1
) is nonnegative for
s\leq\tau
, so that the Itô’s integral on the right-side is not only a continuous
\mathbb{P}
-local martingale, but it is also a supermartingale by Fatou’s Lemma. Thus, recalling that
\xi_{s,t}=e^{-r(s-t)-\theta(B_{s}-B_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}(s-t)}
, the optional sampling theorem implies the so-called budget constraint:

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\big[{\xi_{s,t}}(W_{s}+g(s))\big]+\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{s}\xi_{u,t}c_{u}du\bigg]\leq w+g(t),\ \text{if}\ t\leq s\leq\tau.

By similar arguments on
(\ref{3-2})
we also have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}\xi_{u,t}c_{u}du\bigg]\leq w,\ \text{if}\ t=\tau\leq s.

3.2.
The agent’s optimization problem after retirement

In this subsection we will consider the agent’s optimization problem after retirement, and over this time period only consumption and portfolio choice have to be determined. Formally, the model in the previous section accommodates this case if we let
\tau=t
, where
t
is the fixed starting time. That is, the agent chooses immediate retirement. Then, letting
\mathcal{A}_{t}(t,w,m):=\{(c,\pi):(c,\pi,t)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,0)\},
where the subscript
t
indicates that the retirement time is equal to
t
, the agent’s value function after retirement is

(3.5)

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(t,w,m):

\displaystyle=\sup_{(c,\pi)\in\mathcal{A}_{t}(t,w,m)}\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{2}(c_{s})ds\bigg],

where
M
as defined in (
2.1
) and
\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}
denotes the expectation conditioned on
W_{t}=w
and
M_{t}=m
.

From the budget constraint (
3.4
), recalling that
\xi_{s,t}=e^{-r(s-t)-\theta(B_{s}-B_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}(s-t)}
and for a Lagrange multiplier
z>0
, we have for any
(c,\pi)\in\mathcal{A}_{t}(t,w,m)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{2}(c_{s})ds\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{2}(c_{s})ds\bigg]-z\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}\xi_{s,t}c_{s}ds\bigg]+zw

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{2}(c_{s})ds\bigg]-\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}zP_{s}c_{s}ds\bigg]+zw

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\Big(U_{2}(c_{s})-zP_{s}c_{s}\Big)ds\bigg]+zw

(3.6)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{2}^{*}(zP_{s})ds\bigg]+zw,

where

(3.7)

\displaystyle P_{s}:=\xi_{s,t}e^{\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{2}^{*}(z):=\sup_{c\geq 0}[U_{2}(c)-cz].

Let then
Z_{s}:=zP_{s}
. By Itô’s formula, we obtain that the dual variable
Z
satisfies

(3.8)

\displaystyle dZ_{s}=(\beta-r+M_{s})Z_{s}ds-\theta Z_{s}dB_{s},\quad Z_{t}=z,

and we set

(3.9)

\displaystyle Q(t,z,m):=\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{2}^{*}(Z_{s})ds\bigg].

Assumption 3.1
.

We assume
\beta\geq(1-\gamma)(r+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2})+\frac{(\gamma-1)^{2}\theta^{2}}{2\gamma}
throughout the paper.

Assumption
3.1
gives a sufficient condition to ensure the finiteness of
Q
defined in (
3.9
).

Proposition 3.1
.

Q
is independent of time
t
, it is finite, and one has
Q\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}).
Moreover,
Q
satisfies

(3.10)

\displaystyle-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}Q=U_{2}^{*},

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}Q:=\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}z^{2}Q_{zz}+(\beta-r+m)zQ_{z}+amQ_{m}-(\beta+m)Q.

Proof.

First, we compute the convex dual of
U_{2}
in (
3.7
); that is,

\displaystyle U^{*}_{2}(z)=K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}z^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}},\quad z\geq 0.

From (
2.1
), we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle M_{s}=me^{a(s-t)},\ \forall s\geq t.

Therefore, by (
3.9
) and (
3.11
) we rewrite
Q(t,z,m)
as follows

\displaystyle Q(t,z,m)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}{Z_{s}}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}ds\bigg]

\displaystyle=z^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+me^{a(u-t)})du}e^{[-\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}(r+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\gamma-1)^{2}\theta^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}](s-t)}ds,

(3.12)

\displaystyle=z^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}\beta s^{\prime}-\frac{1}{\gamma}\int^{s^{\prime}}_{0}me^{au^{\prime}}du^{\prime}}e^{[-\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}(r+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\gamma-1)^{2}\theta^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}]s^{\prime}}ds^{\prime},

where we have used the definition of
P_{s}
as in (
3.7
) and the fact that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{P_{s}}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[{(\xi_{s,t}e^{\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du})}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}]=e^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\mathbb{E}[{(e^{-r(s-t)-\theta(B_{s}-B_{t})-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}(s-t)})}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}]

\displaystyle=e^{\int^{s}_{t}(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}(\beta+M_{u}-r-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\gamma-1)^{2}\theta^{2}}{\gamma^{2}})du}.

This shows that
Q
is indeed time-independent.

Moreover, due to Assumption
3.1
, we can verify that
Q(z,m)<\infty
by (
3.2
), and that
Q\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
. Finally it satisfies (
3.10
) by the well-known Feynman-Kac formula (see, e.g., Chapter 4 in
Karatzas and
Shreve 1998a
).∎

Given that
Q
is time-independent, with a slight abuse of notation in the sequel we then simply write
Q(z,m)
.
It is possible to deduce properties of
\widehat{V}
through those of
Q
by the following duality relation.

Theorem 3.1
.

The following dual relations holds:

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(t,w,m)=\inf_{z>0}[Q(z,m)+zw],\quad Q(z,m)=\sup_{w>0}[\widehat{V}(t,w,m)-zw].

Proof.

Since
(c,\pi)\in\mathcal{A}_{t}(t,w,m)
is arbitrary, taking the supremum over
(c,\pi)\in\mathcal{A}_{t}(t,w,m)
on the left-hand side in (
3.2
) and recalling (
3.5
), we get, for any
z>0
,

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(t,w,m)\leq Q(z,m)+zw,

and thus

\displaystyle\widehat{V}(t,w,m)\leq\inf_{z>0}[Q(z,m)+zw]\quad\text{and}\quad Q(z,m)\geq\sup_{w>0}[\widehat{V}(t,w,m)-zw].

For the reverse inequalities, observe that the equality in (
3.2
) holds if and only if

(3.13)

\displaystyle c_{s}=\mathcal{I}_{2}^{u}(Z_{s}),

and

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}\xi_{s,t}c_{s}ds\bigg]=w,

where we denote by
\mathcal{I}_{2}^{u}
the inverse of the marginal utility function
U_{2}^{\prime}(\cdot).

Then, assuming (
3.14
) (we will prove its validity later), we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}(t,z,m):=\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}\xi_{s,t}\mathcal{I}_{2}^{u}(Z_{s})ds\bigg],\quad\mathcal{Y}(t,w,m):=\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{2}(c_{s})ds\bigg],

and notice that (
3.2
), (
3.13
) and (
3.14
) yield

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}(t,\mathcal{X}(t,z,m),m)

\displaystyle=Q(z,m)+z\mathcal{X}(t,z,m)\leq\widehat{V}(t,w,m),

where the last inequality is due to
\mathcal{Y}(t,\mathcal{X}(t,z,m),m)\leq\widehat{V}(t,w,m)
. The last display inequality thus provides

\displaystyle Q(z,m)\leq\sup_{w>0}[\widehat{V}(t,w,m)-zw]\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{V}(t,w,m)\geq\inf_{z>0}[Q(z,m)+zw].

It thus remains only to show that equality (
3.14
) indeed holds. As a matter of fact, Lemma
B.1
guarantees the existence of a candidate optimal portfolio process
\pi^{*}
such that
(c^{*},\pi^{*})\in\mathcal{A}_{t}(t,w,m)
and (
3.14
) holds, where
c_{s}^{*}=\mathcal{I}_{2}^{u}(Z_{s})
is a candidate optimal consumption process. By Theorem 3.6.3 in
Karatzas and
Shreve 1998b
or Lemma 6.2 in
Karatzas and Wang 2000
, one can then show that
(c^{*},\pi^{*})
is indeed optimal for the optimization problem
\widehat{V}
.
∎

From Theorem
3.1
we see that
\widehat{V}
is time-independent, so that in the following, with a slight abuse of notation, we simply write
\widehat{V}(w,m)
.

3.3.
The pure optimal stopping problem

From the agent’s problem in (
2.5
), by the dynamic programming principle we can deduce that for any
(t,w,m,y)\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
,

(3.15)

\displaystyle V(t,w,m,y)=\sup_{(c,\pi,\tau)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,y)}\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{1}(c_{s})ds+e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\widehat{V}(W_{\tau},M_{\tau})\bigg].

Now, for any
(t,w,m,y)\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
and Lagrange multiplier
z>0
, from the budget constraint (
3.3
) and (
3.15
), recalling
P_{s}
in (
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U(c_{s})ds\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(c,\pi,\tau)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,y)}\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{1}(c_{s})ds+e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\widehat{V}(W_{\tau},M_{\tau})\bigg]

\displaystyle-z\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\xi_{\tau,t}\Big(W_{\tau}+g(\tau)\Big)+\int_{t}^{\tau}\xi_{s,t}c_{s}ds\bigg]+z(w+g(t))

\displaystyle=\sup_{(c,\pi,\tau)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,y)}\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\Big(U_{1}(c_{s})-zP_{s}c_{s}\Big)ds

\displaystyle+e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\widehat{V}(W_{\tau},M_{\tau})-e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}zP_{\tau}(W_{\tau}+g(\tau))\bigg]+z(w+g(t))

\displaystyle=\sup_{(c,\pi,\tau)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,y)}\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\Big(U_{1}(c_{s})-zP_{s}c_{s}\Big)ds

\displaystyle+e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\Big(\widehat{V}(W_{\tau},M_{\tau})-zP_{\tau}W_{\tau}-zP_{\tau}g(\tau)\Big)\bigg]+z(w+g(t))

(3.16)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}{U}_{1}^{*}(Z_{s})ds+e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\Big(Q(Z_{\tau},M_{\tau})-Z_{\tau}g(\tau)\Big)\bigg]+z(w+g(t)),

where
Z_{s}
is defined in (
3.8
) and

(3.17)

\displaystyle{U}_{1}^{*}(z):=\sup_{c\geq 0}[U_{1}(c)-cz].

Hence, defining

(3.18)

\displaystyle J(t,z,m,y)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}{U}^{*}_{1}(Z_{s})ds+e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\Big(Q(Z_{\tau},M_{\tau})-Z_{\tau}g(\tau)\Big)\bigg],

we have

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U(c_{s})ds\bigg]\leq J(t,z,m,y)+z(w+g(t)).

In the following sections, we perform a detailed probabilistic study of (
3.18
). Before doing that, we have the following theorem that establishes a dual relation between the original problem (
2.5
) and the optimal stopping problem (
3.18
).

Theorem 3.2
.

The following duality relations holds:

\displaystyle V(t,w,m,y)=\inf_{z>0}[{J}(t,z,m,y)+z(w+g(t))],\ J(t,z,m,y)=\sup_{w>-g(t)}[V(t,w,m,y)-z(w+g(t))].

Proof.

Since
(c,\pi,\tau)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,y)
is arbitrary, taking the supremum over
(c,\pi,\tau)\in\mathcal{A}(t,w,m,y)
on the left-hand side of (
3.19
), we get, for any
z>0,w>-g(t)
,

\displaystyle{V}(t,w,m,y)

\displaystyle\leq J(t,z,m,y)+z(w+g(t)),

so that
V(t,w,m,y)\leq\inf_{z>0}[{J}(t,z,m,y)+z(w+g(t))]
and
J(t,z,m,y)\geq\sup_{w>-g(t)}[V(t,w,m,y)-z(w+g(t))]
.

For the reverse inequality, observe that equality holds in (
3.19
) if and only if (see also (
3.3
))

\displaystyle c_{s}=\mathcal{I}_{1}^{u}(Z_{s}),\quad Q(z,m)=\sup_{w>0}[\widehat{V}(w,m)-zw],

and

(3.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\xi_{\tau,t}\Big(W_{\tau}+g(\tau)\Big)+\int_{t}^{\tau}\xi_{s,t}c_{s}ds\bigg]=w+g(t),

where
\mathcal{I}_{1}^{u}
denotes the inverse of the marginal utility function
U_{1}^{\prime}(\cdot).
From Lemma
B.2
, we know that there exists a portfolio process
\pi^{*}
such that (
3.20
) holds. From Theorem
3.1
, we also know that
Q(z,m)=\sup_{w>0}[\widehat{V}(w,m)-zw]
.
Next we define

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{X}}(t,z,m,y):=\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}\xi_{s,t}\mathcal{I}^{u}_{1}(Z_{s})ds\bigg],\quad\bar{\mathcal{Z}}(t,w,m,y):=\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}[\xi_{\tau,t}(W_{\tau}+g(\tau))],

and

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Y}}(t,w,m,y):=\mathbb{E}_{t,w,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{1}(c_{s})ds+e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\widehat{V}(W_{\tau},M_{\tau})\bigg].

Then by (
3.19
) and (
3.20
) we have

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Y}}(t,\bar{\mathcal{X}}(t,z,m,y)+\bar{\mathcal{Z}}(t,w,m,y)-g(t),m,y)

\displaystyle=J(t,z,m,y)+z(\bar{\mathcal{X}}(t,z,m,y)+\bar{\mathcal{Z}}(t,w,m,y))

\displaystyle\leq{V}(t,w,m,y),

where the last inequality is due to
\bar{\mathcal{Y}}(t,\bar{\mathcal{X}}(t,z,m,y)+\bar{\mathcal{Z}}(t,w,m,y)-g(t),m,y)\leq{V}(t,w,m,y)
. This in turn gives

\displaystyle V(t,w,m,y)\geq\inf_{z>0}[{J}(t,z,m,y)+z(w+g(t))],

which completes the proof.

∎

4.
Study of the dual optimal stopping problem

4.1.
Dimensionality reduction

Notice that the dual optimal stopping problem in
(\ref{3-13})
has three state variables and a finite time-horizon. However, due to the homogeneous structure of the utility function
U
, we can apply a measure change method to reduce the dimensionality of the problem from three state variables
(z,m,y)
to two state variables
(x,m)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Consider the exponential martingale

\displaystyle\zeta:=\bigg\{\zeta_{s}=\exp\bigg(\int^{s}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{y}-\theta)^{2}du+\int^{s}_{t}(\sigma_{y}-\theta)dB_{u}\bigg):t\leq s\leq T\bigg\}.

Let
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
be the probability measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T})
such that
\frac{d\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}{d\mathbb{P}}=\zeta_{T}
, and denote by
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}
the expectation under
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
. Then we have

(4.1)

\displaystyle J(t,z,m,y)=zy\widetilde{J}(t,z^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}y^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}},m)

with

(4.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}(t,x,m):=\sup_{t\leq\tau\leq T}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}{U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})ds+e^{-\kappa(\tau-t)}\Big(Q(X_{\tau},M_{\tau})-q(\tau)\Big)\bigg],

and the underlying Markov process
X
being defined as
X:=Z^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}Y^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}
(with
Z
and
Y
as in (
3.8
) and (
2.2
), respectively).

Proof.

First, from (
3.17
) and (
3.7
) we compute the convex dual functions
U_{1}^{*}(z)
and
U^{*}_{2}(z)
:

(4.3)

\displaystyle U^{*}_{1}(z)=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}z^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}},\quad U^{*}_{2}(z)=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}z^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}=K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}U^{*}_{1}(z),\quad z\geq 0.

Plugging
U^{*}_{2}
into (
3.9
) we have

\displaystyle Q(z,m)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}{Z_{s}}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}ds\bigg]

\displaystyle=z^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\mathbb{E}_{z,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}{P_{s}}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}ds\bigg]

and

\displaystyle Q(z^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}y^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}},m)

\displaystyle=(z(zy)^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}})^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\mathbb{E}_{z,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}{P_{s}}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}ds\bigg]

\displaystyle=z^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}(zy)^{-1}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\mathbb{E}_{z,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}{P_{s}}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}ds\bigg]

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q(z,m)}{zy}.

Direct calculations (cf. (
3.8
) and (
2.2
)) show

\displaystyle Z_{s}Y_{s}

\displaystyle=zP_{s}Y_{s}=ze^{\int^{s}_{t}(-r-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}+\beta+M_{u})du-\int^{s}_{t}\theta dB_{u}}ye^{(\mu_{y}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{y}^{2})(s-t)+\sigma_{y}(B_{s}-B_{t})}

\displaystyle=zye^{\int^{s}_{t}(-r-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}+\beta+M_{u}+\mu_{y}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{y}^{2})du-\int^{s}_{t}(\theta-\sigma_{y})dB_{u}}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=zye^{\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u}-\kappa)du}\zeta_{s}.

Then, by the continuous-time Bayes’ rule (cf. Lemma 3.5.3 in
Karatzas and
Shreve 1998a
), we have for any fixed
\tau\in\mathcal{S}
, and for
x=z^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}y^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m,y}[e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}(Q(Z_{\tau},M_{\tau})-Z_{\tau}g(\tau))]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m,y}[e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}(Q(Z_{\tau},M_{\tau})-Z_{\tau}Y_{\tau}q(\tau))]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m,y}[e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}Z_{\tau}Y_{\tau}(Q(Z_{\tau},M_{\tau})/(Z_{\tau}Y_{\tau})-q(\tau))]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m,y}[e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}Z_{\tau}Y_{\tau}(Q(X_{\tau},M_{\tau})-q(\tau))]

\displaystyle=zy\mathbb{E}_{t,x,m}[e^{-\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}e^{\int^{\tau}_{t}(\beta+M_{u}-\kappa)du}\zeta_{\tau}(Q(X_{\tau},M_{\tau})-q(\tau))]

(4.6)

\displaystyle=zy\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}[e^{-\kappa(\tau-t)}(Q(X_{\tau},M_{\tau})-q(\tau))],

where we have used (
4.1
), the definition of
X
and (
4.1
).
Based on the same arguments, we also have for
x=z^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}y^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}{U}^{*}_{1}(Z_{s})ds\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}{U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})Z_{s}Y_{s}ds\bigg]

\displaystyle=zy\mathbb{E}_{t,z,m,y}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}{U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})e^{\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u}-\kappa)du}\zeta_{s}ds\bigg]

\displaystyle=zy\zeta_{t}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}{U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})ds\bigg]

(4.7)

\displaystyle=zy\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}{U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})ds\bigg],

due to
\zeta_{t}=1
. Here and in the sequel,
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}
denotes the expectation under
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
conditioned on
X_{t}=x
and
M_{t}=m
. Combining (
4.1
) and (
4.1
), together with (
3.18
), we have
J(t,z,m,y)=zy\widetilde{J}(t,z^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}y^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}},m)
with

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}(t,x,m):=\sup_{t\leq\tau\leq T}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}{U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})ds+e^{-\kappa(\tau-t)}\Big(Q(X_{\tau},M_{\tau})-q(\tau)\Big)\bigg].

∎

Before closing this subsection, we introduce the dynamic equation of
X
defined in Lemma
4.1
. By Itô’s formula and Girsanov’s Theorem,
X:=\{X_{s},t\leq s\leq T\}
under the new measure
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
evolves as

(4.8)

\displaystyle dX_{s}=X_{s}[(\rho+1)(\beta-r+M_{s})+\mu_{1}]ds+X_{s}\sigma_{1}d\widehat{B}_{s},\quad X_{t}=x(=z^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}y^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}),

where
\mu_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}(\rho+1)\rho\theta^{2}+\rho\mu_{y}+\frac{1}{2}(\rho-1)\rho\sigma_{y}^{2}-(\rho+1)\rho\theta\sigma_{y}+\sigma_{1}(\sigma_{y}-\theta),\sigma_{1}:=\rho\sigma_{y}-(\rho+1)\theta
with
\rho:=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}
, and
\widehat{B}
is a standard Brownian motion under the measure
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
. Then, the solution to (
4.8
) may be expressed as

\displaystyle X_{s}=x\exp\bigg(\int^{s}_{t}\Big[(\rho+1)(\beta-r+M_{u})+\mu_{1}-\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{2}\Big]du+\int^{s}_{t}\sigma_{1}d\widehat{B}_{u}\bigg),\quad\text{for}\ s\geq t,

so that
X
depends on both initial values
x
and
m
.

For future frequent use, we also introduce here the new probability measure
\mathbb{\widetilde{P}}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T})
such that

(4.9)

\displaystyle H_{T}:=\frac{d\mathbb{\widetilde{P}}}{d\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}\bigg|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}=\exp\bigg\{{\int^{T}_{t}\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\sigma_{1}d\widehat{B}_{u}-\int^{T}_{t}\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma})^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}du}\bigg\}

and notice that

(4.10)

\displaystyle(X_{s})^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}=x^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}H_{s}N(s,m),

where

(4.11)

\displaystyle N(s,m):=\exp\bigg(\int^{s}_{t}{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\Big[(\rho+1)(\beta-r+M^{m}_{u})+\mu_{1}-\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{2}\Big]du+\int^{s}_{t}\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma})^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}du\bigg).

By Girsanov’s Theorem, the process
\widetilde{B}:=\{\widehat{B}_{s}-\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\sigma_{1}s,s\in[t,T]\}
is a new standard Brownian motion under the measure
\mathbb{\widetilde{P}}
. Moreover, it is easy to check that
N(s,m)
is uniformly bounded in time, i.e., there exist
\underline{L},\overline{L}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto(0,\infty)
such that
0<\underline{L}(m)\leq\sup_{s\in[0,T]}N(s,m)\leq\overline{L}(m)<\infty
. In fact,
N(s,m)=\exp[(-\frac{1}{\gamma}(\beta-r)+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}(\mu_{1}-\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{2})+\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma})^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2})(s-t)]\exp(-\frac{1}{\gamma}\int^{s}_{t}me^{a(u-t)}du)
is continuously differentiable in
[t,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{+},t\in[0,T]
.

4.2.
Preliminary properties of the value function

To study the optimal stopping problem (
4.2
), we find it convenient to introduce the function

(4.12)

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(t,x,m):=\widetilde{J}(t,x,m)-\widehat{W}(t,x,m)

with

(4.13)

\displaystyle\widehat{W}(t,x,m):=Q(x,m)-q(t).

Applying Itô’s formula to
\{e^{-\kappa(s-t)}[Q(X_{s},M_{s})-q(s)],s\in[t,\tau]\}
, and taking conditional expectations we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\Big[e^{-\kappa(\tau-t)}\big(Q(X_{\tau},M_{\tau})-q(\tau)\big)\Big]=Q(x,m)-q(t)+

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}\mathcal{L}\Big(Q(X_{s},M_{s})-q(s)\Big)ds\bigg],

where, for any
F\in C^{1,2,1}(\mathcal{U})
, the second order differential operator
\mathcal{L}
is such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}F:=F_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}x^{2}F_{xx}+[(\rho+1)(\beta-r+m)+\mu_{1}]xF_{x}+amF_{m}-\kappa F.

Combining (
4.2
), (
4.12
) and (
4.13
), we have

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(t,x,m)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\leq\tau\leq T}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}{U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})ds+e^{-\kappa(\tau-t)}\Big(Q(X_{\tau},M_{\tau})-q(\tau)\Big)\bigg]-\widehat{W}(t,x,m)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\leq\tau\leq T}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}{U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})ds\bigg]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}\mathcal{L}\Big(Q(X_{s},M_{s})-q(s)\Big)ds\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\leq\tau\leq T}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}\Big({U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})-U^{*}_{2}(X_{s})+1\Big)ds\bigg]

(4.14)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\leq\tau\leq T}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}\Big[(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})+1\Big]ds\bigg],

where we have used the fact that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(Q(X_{s},M_{s})-q(s))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}Q(X_{s},M_{s})-\mathcal{L}(q(s))=-U^{*}_{2}(X_{s})+1.

Indeed, arguing similarly to the proof of Lemma
4.1
(cf. (
4.1
) and (
4.1
)), one finds (recalling that
x=x(z,y)=z^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}y^{\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}}
)

(4.15)

\displaystyle Q(z,m)=\mathbb{E}_{z,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\int^{s}_{t}(\beta+M_{u})du}U_{2}^{*}(Z_{s})ds\bigg]=zy\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}U_{2}^{*}(X_{s})ds\bigg]=zyQ(x,m),

and
\mathcal{L}Q(x,m)=-U_{2}^{*}(x)
. Moreover,
\mathcal{L}(q(s))=-e^{\kappa(T-s)}-\kappa(\frac{1-e^{-\kappa(T-s)}}{\kappa})=-1
.

Notice now that the process
(X_{t},M_{t})
is time-homogeneous, so that

\displaystyle\text{Law}[(u,X_{u},M_{u})_{u\geq t}|X_{t}=x,M_{t}=m]=\text{Law}[(t+s,X_{s},M_{s})_{s\geq 0}|X_{0}=x,M_{0}=m].

Let
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}
be the expectation under
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
conditional on
X_{0}=x,M_{0}=m
. Hence, from (
4.2
),

(4.16)

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(t,x,m)=\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq T-t}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa s}\Big[(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})+1\Big]ds\bigg].

As usual in optimal stopping theory, we let

(4.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}:=\{(t,x,m)\in\mathcal{U}:\widehat{J}(t,x,m)>0\},\quad\mathcal{I}:=\{(t,x,m)\in\mathcal{U}:\widehat{J}(t,x,m)=0\}

be the so-called continuation (waiting) and stopping (retiring) regions, respectively. We denote by
\partial\mathcal{W}
the boundary of the set
\mathcal{W}.

Since, for any stopping time
\tau
, the mapping
(x,m)\mapsto\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa s}[(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})+1]ds]
is continuous,
\widehat{J}
is lower-semicontinuous on
\mathcal{U}
. Hence,
\mathcal{W}
is open,
\mathcal{I}
is closed, and introducing the stopping time

\displaystyle\tau^{*}(t,x,m):=\inf\{s\geq 0:(t+s,X_{s},M_{s})\in\mathcal{I}\}\wedge(T-t),\quad\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{x,m}-a.s.,

with
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
, one has that
\tau^{*}(t,x,m)
is optimal for
\widehat{J}(t,x,m)
(see e.g. Corollary I.2.9 in
Peskir and Shiryaev 2006
).

Proposition 4.1
.

The value function
\widehat{J}
is such that
0\leq\widehat{J}(t,x,m)\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}(1-e^{-\kappa(T-t)})
for all
(t,x,m)\in\mathcal{U}
and it satisfies the following properties:

(i)

When
0<\gamma<1
,
x\mapsto\widehat{J}(t,x,m)
is non-decreasing for all
(t,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
; when
\gamma>1
,
x\mapsto\widehat{J}(t,x,m)
is non-increasing for all
(t,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
;

(ii)

t\mapsto\widehat{J}(t,x,m)
is non-increasing for all
(x,m)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
;

(iii)

m\mapsto\widehat{J}(t,x,m)
is non-decreasing for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Proof.

The proof is given in Appendix
A.1
.
∎

The next lemma shows that
\mathcal{I}
as in (
4.17
) is nonempty.

Lemma 4.2
.

One has
\mathcal{I}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{I}=\emptyset
, then for all
(t,x,m)\in\mathcal{U}
we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\widehat{J}(t,x,m)=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T-t}e^{-\kappa s}\Big[(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})+1\Big]ds\bigg].

However, when

, taking

, the term

converges monotonically to

, leading to a contraction. Similarly, when
\gamma>1
, taking
x\uparrow\infty
, the term
(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})
converges monotonically to

, which brings to a contradiction again.
∎

The next technical result states some properties of
\widehat{J}
that will be useful in the study of the regularity of the boundary

.

Recall

, (

) and (

). The function

\displaystyle\widehat{J}_{m}(t,x,m)

\displaystyle=(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)\frac{x^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}}{1-\gamma}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}}e^{-\kappa s}e^{as}H_{s}N(s,m)ds\bigg].

Moreover, there exists a constant

(4.20)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{T-t}(Cx^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}])\leq\widehat{J}_{t}(t,x,m)\leq 0.

where

.

Proof.

, let
(t,x,m)\in\mathcal{U}

, let
(t,x,m)\in\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(t,x,m):=\inf\{s\geq 0:X_{s}>b(t+s,M_{s})\}\wedge(T-t).

\hat{\tau}(t,x,m)=\tau^{*}(t,x,m)

.

\tau^{*}
, which will then be fundamental in the proof of Proposition

below.

Proposition 4.4
.

One has that
\mathcal{U}\ni(t,x,m)\mapsto\tau^{*}(t,x,m)\in[0,T-t]

Proof.

The proof exploits Lemma

and arguments completely analogous to those employed in the proof of Proposition 5.2 in

Proposition 4.5
.

The value function
\widehat{J}\in C^{1,2,1}(\overline{\mathcal{W}})\cap C^{1,1,1}(\mathcal{U})
and solves the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}\widehat{J}(t,x,m)&=-(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(x)-1,\ (t,x,m)\in\mathcal{W},\\
\widehat{J}(t,x,m)&=0,\ (t,x,m)\in\mathcal{I}\cap\{t<T\},\\
\widehat{J}(T,x,m)&=0,\ (x,m)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2},\\
\widehat{J}_{t}(t,x,m)&=\widehat{J}_{x}(t,x,m)=\widehat{J}_{m}(t,x,m)=0\ \text{on}\ \partial\mathcal{W}\cap\{t<T\}.\end{aligned}\right.

Proof.

First we show that the function
\widehat{J}
is continuously differentiable over
\mathcal{U}
. From the representations of
\widehat{J}_{x},\widehat{J}_{t}
and
\widehat{J}_{m}
in Proposition
4.2
, and the continuity of
(t,x,m)\mapsto\tau^{*}(t,x,m)
(cf. Proposition
4.4
), we conclude that those weak derivatives are in fact continuous and therefore that
\widehat{J}\in C^{1,1,1}(\mathcal{W})\cap C^{1,1,1}(\mathring{\mathcal{I}})
, where
\mathring{\mathcal{I}}
denotes the interior of
\mathcal{I}
. In particular,
\widehat{J}_{t}=\widehat{J}_{x}=\widehat{J}_{m}=0
on
\mathring{\mathcal{I}}
. It thus remains to analyze the regularity of
\widehat{J}
across
\partial{\mathcal{W}}
.

Fix a point
(t_{0},x_{0},m_{0})\in\partial\mathcal{W}\cap\{t<T\}
and take a sequence
(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
with
(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})\rightarrow(t_{0},x_{0},m_{0})
as
n\to\infty.
Continuity of
(t,x,m)\mapsto\tau^{*}(t,x,m)
implies that
\tau^{*}(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})\to\tau^{*}(t_{0},x_{0},m_{0})=0,\ \mathbb{P}
-a.s. as
n\to\infty.
Again, from Proposition
4.2
, dominated convergence yields that
\widehat{J}_{m}(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})\to 0,\widehat{J}_{x}(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})\to 0
and
\widehat{J}_{t}(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})\to 0
. Since
(t_{0},x_{0},m_{0})
and the sequence
(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})
were arbitrary, we get
\widehat{J}\in C^{1,1,1}(\mathcal{U})
.

Let us now turn to study the regularity of
\widehat{J}
in
\mathcal{W}
. First of all notice that, because the solution to (
4.8
) is linear with respect to the initial datum
x
, we then have that
x\mapsto U^{*}_{1}(X_{s})
is convex, being
U^{*}_{1}
from (
4.3
) clearly convex. This fact implies that
x\mapsto\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{t,x,m}[\int_{t}^{\tau}e^{-\kappa(s-t)}{U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})ds+e^{-\kappa(\tau-t)}(Q(X_{\tau},M_{\tau})-q(\tau))]
is convex, as
Q(\cdot,m)
is strictly convex. Hence,
x\mapsto\widetilde{J}(t,x,m)
is convex. In particular, by Alexandrov’s Theorem,
\widetilde{J}
admits second order derivatives with respect to
x
in a dense subset of
\mathcal{U}
. Further, since
Q(x,m)
is
C^{2}
with respect to
x
, we know that
\widehat{J}
admits second order derivatives with respect to
x
in a dense subset of
\mathcal{U}
(cf. (
4.12
) and (
4.13
)).

By Corollary 6 in
Peskir 2022
, we also know that
\widehat{J}
solves in the sense of distributions

(4.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\widehat{J}(t,x,m)

\displaystyle=-(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(x)-1,\ (t,x,m)\in\mathcal{W},

and it is such that

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(t,x,m)

\displaystyle=0,\ (t,x,m)\in\mathcal{I}\cap\{t<T\},

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(T,x,m)

\displaystyle=0,\ (x,m)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}.

By writing (
4.23
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}x^{2}}{2}\widehat{J}_{xx}=-\widehat{J}_{t}+\kappa\widehat{J}-am\widehat{J}_{m}-[(\rho+1)(\beta-r+m)+\mu_{1}]x\widehat{J}_{x}-(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(x)-1,

we then see that
\widehat{J}_{xx}
admits a continuous extension to
\overline{\mathcal{W}}
, that we denote
\bar{J}_{xx}.
Then, by taking arbitrary
(t_{0},x_{0},m_{0})\in\mathcal{W}
we can write

\displaystyle\widehat{J}_{x}(t_{0},x,m_{0})=\widehat{J}_{x}(t_{0},x_{0},m_{0})+\int^{x}_{x_{0}}\bar{J}_{xx}(t_{0},u,m_{0})du,\quad\forall\ (t_{0},x,m_{0})\in\mathcal{I},

and the latter yields that
\widehat{J}_{x}(t,\cdot,m)
is continuous for any
x
such that
(t,x,m)\in\overline{\mathcal{W}}
. Since we already know that
\widehat{J}\in C^{1,1,1}(\mathcal{U})
, we conclude that
\widehat{J}\in C^{1,2,1}(\overline{\mathcal{W}})
.

∎

Corollary 4.1
.

Recall (
4.12
). The function
\widetilde{J}\in C^{1,2,1}(\overline{\mathcal{W}})\cap C^{1,1,1}(\mathcal{U})
and solves the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\widetilde{J}(t,x,m)

\displaystyle=-{U}^{*}_{1}(x),\ (t,x,m)\in\mathcal{W},

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}(t,x,m)

\displaystyle=\widehat{W}(t,x,m),\ (t,x,m)\in\mathcal{I}\cap\{t<T\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}(T,x,m)

\displaystyle=\widehat{W}(T,x,m),\ (x,m)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2},

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{t}(t,x,m)

\displaystyle=\widehat{W}_{t}(t,x,m),\ \widetilde{J}_{x}(t,x,m)=\widehat{W}_{x}(t,x,m),\ \widetilde{J}_{m}(t,x,m)=\widehat{W}_{m}(t,x,m)\ \text{on}\ \partial\mathcal{W}\cap\{t<T\}.

Remark 4.1
.

It is worth noting that standard PDE arguments typically require uniform ellipticity of the underlying second-order differential operator and thus could not be directly applied in the proof of Proposition
4.5
, due to the fully degenerate diffusion process
(X,M)
. Therefore, we had to hinge on a novel series of intermediate results. First, we find the locally Lipschitz continuity of
\widehat{J}
(cf. Proposition
4.2
) and then establish the locally-Lipschitz continuity of free boundary without relying upon the continuity of
\widehat{J}_{x},\widehat{J}_{m}
and
\widehat{J}_{t}
(cf. Theorem
4.1
). Finally, we upgrade the regularity of
\widehat{J}
using the continuity of the optimal stopping time (cf. Propositions
4.4
and
4.5
).

We are now in the conditions of determining a nonlinear integral equation that characterizes uniquely the free boundary. Such a characterization results from an integral representation of the value function
\widehat{J}
. This is accomplished by the next theorem, which exploits the regularity properties of
\widehat{J}
proved so far.

.

\gamma<1

.

If

,
\widehat{J}
from (
4.16
) has the representation

(4.26)

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(t,x,m)=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int^{T-t}_{0}e^{-\kappa s}\Big[(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})+1\Big]\mathds{1}_{\{X_{s}\leq b(t+s,M_{s})\}}ds\bigg],\ (t,x,m)\in\mathcal{U}.

Moreover, the optimal boundary
b
is the unique continuous solution bounded from below by
L
to the following nonlinear integral equation: For all
(t,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

(4.27)

\displaystyle 0=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{b(t,m),m}\bigg[\int^{T-t}_{0}e^{-\kappa s}\Big[(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(X_{s})+1\Big]\mathds{1}_{\{X_{s}\leq b(t+s,M_{s})\}}ds\bigg],

with
\lim_{t\uparrow T}b(t,m)=L
.

Proof.

Step 1
. In this step, we only need to prove (
4.24
), since the derivation in the case of

is similar. Let
(t,x,m)\in\mathcal{U}
be given and fixed, let

be a sequence of compact sets increasing to

and define

\displaystyle\tau_{n}:=\inf\{s\geq 0:(t+s,X^{x}_{s},M^{m}_{s})\notin K_{n}\}\wedge(T-t),\quad n\geq 0.

Since

,

, and

, we can apply a weak version of Dynkin’s formula (see, e.g.,

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(t,x,m)=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[e^{-\kappa\tau_{n}}\widehat{J}(t+\tau_{n},X^{x}_{\tau_{n}},M^{m}_{\tau_{n}})-\int^{\tau_{n}}_{0}e^{-\kappa s}\mathcal{L}\widehat{J}(t+s,X^{x}_{s},M^{m}_{s})ds\bigg].

Therefore, using (
4.23
), we also find

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(t,x,m)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[e^{-\kappa\tau_{n}}\widehat{J}(t+\tau_{n},X^{x}_{\tau_{n}},M^{m}_{\tau_{n}})

\displaystyle+\int^{\tau_{n}}_{0}e^{-\kappa s}\bigg((1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(X^{x}_{u})+1\bigg)\mathds{1}_{\{X_{s}^{x}\geq b(t+s,M^{m}_{s})\}}ds\bigg],

where we have used again that
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}[(t+s,X^{x}_{s},M^{m}_{s})\in\partial\mathcal{W}]=0
.

Finally, we take

, apply the dominated convergence theorem, and use that
\tau_{n}\uparrow(T-t)
and
\widehat{J}(T,x,m)=0
(cf. Proposition
4.5
) to obtain (
4.24
).

Step 2.
Next, for
\gamma<1
, we find the limit value of
b(t,m)
when
t\to T
. The argument is inspired by the proof of Proposition 4.10 in
De Angelis and
Stabile 2019b
. The case of
\gamma>1
can be treated similarly and we thus omit it. Firstly, the limit
b(T-,m):=\lim_{t\to T}b(t,m)
exists, since
b
is monotone on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Notice that
b(t,m)\leq L
for all
(t,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
(cf. Lemma
4.3
) and therefore
b(T-,m)\leq L
. Arguing by contradiction, we assume
b(T-,m)<L.
Then we pick
a,c
such that

and

such that
(a,c)\times[t^{\prime},T)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
. Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(a,c)
with
\varphi\geq 0
such that
\int^{c}_{a}\varphi(y)dy=1.
Recall (
4.2
) and define
F_{\varphi}(s):=\int^{c}_{a}\widehat{J}_{t}(s,y,m)\varphi(y)dy
. Now, denoting by
\mathbb{L}^{*}
the adjoint of the operator
\mathbb{L}
, where
\mathbb{L}f:=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}x^{2}f_{xx}+[(\rho+1)(\beta-r+m)+\mu_{1}]xf_{x}+amf_{m}
. Therefore, we have
\mathcal{L}\widehat{J}=\widehat{J}_{t}+\mathbb{L}\widehat{J}-\kappa\widehat{J}=-(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}){U}^{*}_{1}(x)-1

\displaystyle\lim_{s\uparrow T}F_{\varphi}(s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\uparrow T}\int^{c}_{a}\Big((K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1){U}^{*}_{1}(y)-1-\mathbb{L}\widehat{J}+\kappa\widehat{J}\Big)\varphi(y)dy

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\uparrow T}\int^{c}_{a}\Big(\Big((K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1){U}^{*}_{1}(y)-1\Big)\varphi(y)+\widehat{J}(s,y,m)(-\mathbb{L}^{*}+\kappa)\varphi(y)\Big)dy

Given that (

,
(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}})\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}<0
and
\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}>0
. Therefore,

is non-increasing for all
(t,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Monotonicity in
t
.
Monotonicity in
t
is a trivial consequence of the fact that the

is independent of

Monotonicity in

When

\rho+1=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}>0

is increasing

When

, thus

is decreasing

-a.s. for all

is therefore non-decreasing for all

∎

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}b_{\epsilon}(t,m)=b(t,m),\quad\text{for all}\ (t,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Arguing symmetrically, we also have that (
A.10
) holds true for
\gamma>1
.

Step 2.
We here prove that
b_{\epsilon}(t,m)
is bounded uniformly in
\epsilon
. Clearly, we can restrict the attention to
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
for some
\epsilon_{0}>0
. From Lemma

, we know that, when

, which provides the desired uniform bound. When
\gamma>1
, we have
b(t,m)\leq b_{\infty}(m)
. Hence, similarly, we have that
L\leq b_{\epsilon}(t,m)\leq 1+b_{\infty}(m),\forall\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
, which provides the desired uniform bound.

Step 3.

Here we show that
b_{\epsilon}
is locally-Lipshitz continuous in
m
, uniformly with respect to
\epsilon
.

Step 3-(a).
Here we determine an
upper bound for

as in Proposition

we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\widehat{J}_{m}(t,b_{\epsilon}(t,m),m)

\displaystyle=(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}(\rho+1)\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}e^{-\kappa s}e^{as}H_{s}N(s,m)ds\bigg]

where

. Then,

\displaystyle|\widehat{J}_{m}(t,b_{\epsilon}(t,m),m)|

\displaystyle\leq(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)(\rho+1)(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}e^{-\kappa s}e^{as}H_{s}N(s,m)ds\bigg]

(A.11)

\displaystyle\leq(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)(\rho+1)(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}e^{aT}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}e^{-\kappa s}H_{s}N(s,m)ds\bigg]:=L^{\epsilon}_{2}(t,m).

Step 3-(b).
Here we determine a
lower bound for

4.18

(A.12)

\displaystyle\widehat{J}_{x}(t,b_{\epsilon}(t,m),m)=(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{-1}{\gamma}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}e^{-\kappa s}H_{s}N(s,m)ds\bigg],

so that from (
A.12

(A.13)

\displaystyle|\widehat{J}_{x}(t,b_{\epsilon}(t,m),m)|

\displaystyle=|(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)|(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{-1}{\gamma}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}H_{s}N(s,m)ds\bigg]=:\frac{1}{\lambda^{\epsilon}_{1}(t,m)}.

Step 3-(c).
From (
A.9
) (with
\lambda^{\epsilon}=\lambda^{\epsilon}_{1}
), (
A.5
) and (
A.13
) we conclude that when

the family of weak derivatives

is uniformly bounded; i.e.,

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon\geq 0}|\partial_{m}b_{\epsilon}(t,m)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\epsilon\geq 0}({\lambda_{1}^{\epsilon}(t,m)L_{2}^{\epsilon}(t,m)})

(A.14)

\displaystyle=|\rho+1|b_{\epsilon}(t,m)e^{aT}\leq|\rho+1|(1+L)e^{aT}.

Similarly, when

the family of weak derivatives

is uniformly bounded; i.e.,

(A.15)

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon\geq 0}|\partial_{m}b_{\epsilon}(t,m)|\leq|\rho+1|(1+b_{\infty}(m))e^{aT}

Step 4.
We show that
b_{\epsilon}
is locally-Lipschitz continuous in

, uniformly with respect to
\epsilon

Step 4-(a).
Here we find an
upper bound for

.
Recalling that

-\frac{1}{T-t}(Cx^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}])\leq\widehat{J}_{t}(t,x,m)\leq 0

\displaystyle|\widehat{J}_{t}(t,b_{\epsilon}(t,m),m)|\leq\frac{1}{T-t}(C{b_{\epsilon}(t,m)}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]):=L^{\epsilon}_{1}(t,m).

In the sequel a change of probability measures through a Girsanov argument will be needed in order to take care of the expectation on the right-hand side of (
A.16
). To this end, recall the probability measure
\mathbb{\widetilde{P}}
on

defined as

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{\widetilde{P}}}{d\widehat{\mathbb{P}}}=\exp\bigg\{{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\sigma_{1}\widehat{B}_{T}-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma})^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}T}\bigg\}.

.

Step 4-(b).
We here determine another
lower bound
for

\displaystyle|\widehat{J}_{x}(t,b_{\epsilon}(t,m),m)|

\displaystyle=|1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}|(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{-1}{\gamma}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}e^{-\kappa s}H_{s}N(s,m)ds\bigg]

\displaystyle=|1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}|(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{-1}{\gamma}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}e^{-\kappa s}N(s,m)ds\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq|1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}|(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{-1}{\gamma}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}e^{-\kappa T}\underline{L}(m)ds\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq C_{1}|1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}|(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{-1}{\gamma}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}[{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}]:=\frac{1}{\lambda^{\epsilon}_{2}(t,m)},

where

.

Step 4-(c).
Our aim here is to find a bound of
\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]}
, uniformly with respect to
\epsilon
when
\gamma>1
. This term arises from the ratio of the two expectations in (
A.16
) and (
A.5
).

Since the dynamics of
X^{\epsilon}_{s}
under
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
are

\displaystyle dX_{s}^{\epsilon}=X_{s}^{\epsilon}[(\rho+1)(\beta-r+M_{s})+\mu_{1}]ds+X_{s}^{\epsilon}\sigma_{1}d\widehat{B}_{s},\ X_{t}^{\epsilon}=b_{\epsilon}(t,m),

those become under
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
(remember that
\widetilde{B}:=\{\widehat{B}_{s}-\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\sigma_{1}s,s\in[0,T]\}
)

\displaystyle dX_{s}^{\epsilon}=X_{s}^{\epsilon}[(\rho+1)(\beta-r+M_{s})+\mu_{1}+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\sigma_{1}^{2}]ds+X_{s}^{\epsilon}\sigma_{1}d\widetilde{B}_{s},\quad X^{\epsilon}_{t}=b_{\epsilon}(t,m).

Now, if on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
we define

\displaystyle d\widetilde{X}_{s}^{\epsilon}=\widetilde{X}_{s}^{\epsilon}[(\rho+1)(\beta-r+M_{s})+\mu_{1}+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\sigma_{1}^{2}]ds+\widetilde{X}_{s}^{\epsilon}\sigma_{1}d\widehat{B}_{s},\quad\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\epsilon}=b_{\epsilon}(t,m).

and
\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{\epsilon}:=\inf\{s\in[0,T-t]:(t+s,\widetilde{X}_{s}^{\epsilon},M_{s})\in\mathcal{I}\}
, then we see that

\displaystyle\text{Law}(X^{\epsilon}_{s}|\widetilde{\mathbb{P}})=\text{Law}(\widetilde{X}^{\epsilon}_{s}|\widehat{\mathbb{P}}),\quad\text{Law}(\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}|\widetilde{\mathbb{P}})=\text{Law}(\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{\epsilon}|\widehat{\mathbb{P}}),

where
\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}:=\inf\{s\in[0,T-t]:(t+s,{X}^{\epsilon}_{s},M_{s})\in\mathcal{I}\}
. Moreover, by the comparison principles for SDEs, we have that
X^{\epsilon}_{s}\leq\widetilde{X}^{\epsilon}_{s},\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s., for all
s\in[0,T-t]
since
\gamma>1
, and, therefore, we have
\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}\leq\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{\epsilon},\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s., and

(A.19)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\widetilde{\tau}^{*}_{\epsilon}]\geq\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[{\tau}^{*}_{\epsilon}].

Hence, when
\gamma>1
, combining (
A.9
) (with
\lambda^{\epsilon}=\lambda^{\epsilon}_{2}
), (
A.16
), (
A.5
) and (
A.19
) we find

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon\geq 0}|\partial_{t}b_{\epsilon}(t,m)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\epsilon\geq 0}(\lambda_{2}^{\epsilon}(t,m)L_{1}^{\epsilon}(t,m))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{1}{T-t}(C{b_{\epsilon}(t,m)}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}])}{C_{1}(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}})(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{-1}{\gamma}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}[{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}]}\leq\frac{Cb_{\epsilon}(t,m)}{(T-t)C_{1}(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}})}+\frac{(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}{(T-t)C_{1}(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}})}

(A.20)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Cb_{\infty}(m)+(b_{\infty}(m))^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}{(T-t)C_{1}(1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}})}.

Step 4-(d).
Our next task is to find a bound for the ratio
\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]}
, uniformly with respect to
\epsilon
, when
\gamma<1
. The following proof arguments are borrowed from
De Angelis and
Stabile 2019b
. Due to Lemma
4.3
, there exists
l>0
such that
2l\leq L^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}
, and we denote

\displaystyle\tau_{l}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\leq l\}.

For this part of the proof, it is convenient to think of
\Omega
as the canonical space of continuous paths
\omega=\{\omega(t),t\geq 0\}
and denote by
\vartheta_{s}
the shifting operator
\vartheta_{s}\omega=\{\omega(s+t),t\geq 0\}
. Moreover, we recall that
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}[\cdot]=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\cdot|X_{0}=x,M_{0}=m]
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}[\cdot]=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\cdot|X_{0}=x,M_{0}=m]
. With this notation, for fixed
(x_{\epsilon},m)
and any
s\geq 0
, we have
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}[\tau_{\epsilon}^{*}>s]=\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{\epsilon,m}}[\tau^{*}>s]
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}[\tau_{\epsilon}^{*}>s]=\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{x_{\epsilon,m}}[\tau^{*}>s]
, because

\displaystyle\tau_{\epsilon}^{*}:=\tau^{*}(t,x^{\epsilon},m)=\inf\{s\geq 0:(t+s,X^{x_{\epsilon}}_{s},M^{m}_{s})\in\mathcal{I}\}\wedge(T-t),\quad\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{x^{\epsilon},m}\ \text{and}\ \widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{x^{\epsilon},m}-a.s..

Our first estimate gives (cf. (
A.17
))

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[\tau^{*}]

\displaystyle=x_{\epsilon}^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{\tau^{*}}N({\tau^{*}},m)^{-1}\tau^{*}]

(A.21)

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}x_{\epsilon}^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{{\tau^{*}}}\tau^{*}]

for a suitable constant
c_{1}>0
, independent of
(t,x_{\epsilon})
. Next we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{{\tau^{*}}}\tau^{*}]=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{{\tau^{*}}}\tau^{*}(\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}\leq\tau_{l}\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}})]

(A.22)

\displaystyle\geq l\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[\tau^{*}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}\leq\tau_{l}\}}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{\tau^{*}}\tau^{*}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}].

The last term can be further estimated via

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{\tau^{*}}\tau^{*}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}]=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{\tau^{*}}\tau^{*}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}(\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}<T-t\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T-t\}})]

(A.23)

\displaystyle\geq c_{2}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[\tau^{*}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}<T-t\}}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{\tau^{*}}\tau^{*}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T-t\}}],

where we have used that
\{\tau^{*}<T-t\}\subseteq\{X_{\tau^{*}}\leq L\}=\{X_{\tau^{*}}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\geq L^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}:=c_{2}\}
under
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{x^{\epsilon},m}
. The last term in the above expression may be controlled by using iterated conditioning and the strong Markov property as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{\tau^{*}}\tau^{*}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T-t\}}]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[(T-t)\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{\tau^{*}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T-t\}}|\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{l}}]]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[(T-t)\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{\tau_{l}+\tau^{*}\circ\vartheta_{\tau_{l}}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}\circ\vartheta_{\tau_{l}}=T-t-\tau_{l}\}}|\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{l}}]]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[(T-t)\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{\tau_{l}},M_{\tau_{l}}}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{\tau^{*}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T-t\}}]]

\displaystyle\geq\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[(T-t)\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T-t\}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{\tau_{l}},M_{\tau_{l}}}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{\tau^{*}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T-t\}}]]

(A.24)

\displaystyle\geq c_{3}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[(T-t)\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T-t\}}],

where

\displaystyle c_{3}:=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{\tau_{l}},M_{\tau_{l}}}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{T-t}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T-t\}}]>0.

Notice that the strictly positivity of
c_{3}
may be verified by using the known joint law of the Brownian motion and its running supremum. Indeed, recalling that
l\leq\frac{L^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{\tau_{l}},M_{\tau_{l}}}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{T-t}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T-t\}}]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{\tau_{l}},M_{\tau_{l}}}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{T-t}\mathds{1}_{\{\inf_{s\in[0,T-t]}X_{s}\geq b(t+s,M_{s})\}}]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{\tau_{l}},M_{\tau_{l}}}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{T-t}\mathds{1}_{\{[\inf_{s\in[0,T-t]}X_{s}]^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\leq(b(t+s,M_{s}))^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\}}]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{\tau_{l}},M_{\tau_{l}}}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{T-t}\mathds{1}_{\{\sup_{s\in[0,T-t]}X_{s}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\leq(b(t+s,M_{s}))^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\}}]

\displaystyle\geq\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{\tau_{l}},M_{\tau_{l}}}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{T-t}\mathds{1}_{\{\sup_{s\in[0,T-t]}X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{s}\leq L^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\}}]

\displaystyle\geq\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{X_{\tau_{l}},M_{\tau_{l}}}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{T-t}\mathds{1}_{\{\sup_{s\in[0,T-t]}X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{s}\leq 2l\}}].

Hence, overall, from (
A.5
), (
A.5
), (
A.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[X^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}_{{\tau^{*}}}\tau^{*}]

\displaystyle\geq l\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[\tau^{*}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}\leq\tau_{l}\}}]+c_{2}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[\tau^{*}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}<T\}}]+c_{3}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[T\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}>\tau_{l}\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tau^{*}=T\}}]

\displaystyle\geq c_{4}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{\epsilon},m}[\tau^{*}]=c_{4}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]

where
c_{4}=l\wedge c_{2}\wedge c_{3}
. Now we plug the latter into (
A.5
) and get

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]}{\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]}\leq\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{4}}x_{\epsilon}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}.

If we now let
x_{\epsilon}=b_{\epsilon}(t,m)
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon\geq 0}|\partial_{t}b_{\epsilon}(t,m)|\leq\sup_{\epsilon\geq 0}(\lambda_{2}^{\epsilon}(t,m)L_{1}^{\epsilon}(t,m))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{1}{T-t}(C{b_{\epsilon}(t,m)}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}]+\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}])}{C_{1}(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)(b_{\epsilon}(t,m))^{\frac{-1}{\gamma}}\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}_{x,m}[{\tau^{*}_{\epsilon}}]}\leq\frac{Cb_{\epsilon}(t,m)}{(T-t)C_{1}(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)}+\frac{b_{\epsilon}(t,m)}{(T-t)C_{1}(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)c_{1}c_{4}}

(A.25)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(C+\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{4}})(1+L)}{(T-t)C_{1}(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)},

which gives the required uniform bound.

Step 5.
Combining the findings of the previous steps, by (
A.9

b_{\epsilon}
is locally-Lipschitz continuous, with Lipschitz constants that are independent of
\epsilon
(see (
A.5
), (
A.13
) and (
A.5
)). Furthermore, the family
(b_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon}
is also uniformly bounded (cf. Step 2).

Hence, by Ascoli-Arzelà theorem we can extract a subsequence

such that

uniformly, with

. However,

A.6.
Proof of Lemma
4.4

Proof.

It is easy to check that
\hat{\tau}(t,x,m)\geq\tau^{*}(t,x,m)
by their definitions. In order to show the reverse inequality, the rest of the proof is organized in two steps.

Step 1.
We claim that

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(t,b(t,m),m)=0,\quad\widehat{\mathbb{P}}-a.s.

due to the Lipschitz continuity of
b(t,m)
and the law of the iterated logarithm of Brownian motion. As a matter of fact, we fix a point
(t_{0},x_{0},m_{0})\in\partial\mathcal{W}\cap\{t<T\}
and take a sequence
(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
with
(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})\to(t_{0},x_{0},m_{0})
as
n\to\infty.
We also fix
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
, with
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{0})>0
, and assume that
\lim\sup_{n\to\infty}\hat{\tau}(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})(\omega)=:\lambda>0
. Now we need to distinguish two cases:
\gamma<1
and
\gamma>1
.

Step 1-(a).
We start with the case
\gamma<1
. The equality
\hat{\tau}(t,x,m)=\tau^{*}(t,x,m)
is trivial for
(t,x,m)
such that
x<b(t,m)
, hence we fix
(t,x,m)\in\mathcal{U}
with
x\geq b(t,m)
in the subsequent proof. Then, if
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\widehat{\tau}(t_{n},x_{n},m_{n})=\lambda>0
on
\Omega_{0}
, for any
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
we have,

\displaystyle X_{s}^{x_{n}}(\omega)\geq b(t_{n}+s,M^{m_{n}}_{s}),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\ \forall s\in[0,\frac{\lambda}{2}].

Upon using that
(t,m)\mapsto b(t_{n}+s,M_{s}^{m_{n}})
is Lipschitz continuous (cf. Theorem
4.1
), we let
n\to\infty
and obtain

\displaystyle X_{s}^{x_{0}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\geq b(t_{0},m_{0})+b(t_{0}+s,M^{m_{0}}_{s})-b(t_{0},M^{m_{0}}_{s})+b(t_{0},M^{m_{0}}_{s})-b(t_{0},m_{0})

(A.26)

\displaystyle=b(t_{0},m_{0})+\int^{s}_{0}\partial_{t}b(t_{0}+u,M^{m_{0}}_{s})du+\int^{m_{0}e^{as}}_{m_{0}}\partial_{m}b(t_{0},u)du.

However, from (
A.5
) and (
A.5
) we have

\displaystyle\partial_{m}b(t_{0},u)

\displaystyle\geq-|\rho+1|(1+L)e^{aT},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}b(t_{0}+u,M^{m_{0}}_{s})

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{(C+\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{4}})(1+L)}{(T-t_{0}-u)(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)},

which used in (
A.6
) give

\displaystyle X_{s}^{x_{0}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\geq b(t_{0},m_{0})-\int^{s}_{0}\frac{(C+\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{4}})(1+L)}{(T-t_{0}-u)(K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1)}du-\int^{m_{0}e^{as}}_{m_{0}}|\rho+1|(1+L)e^{aT}du

\displaystyle=b(t_{0},m_{0})-\frac{(C+\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{4}})(1+L)}{K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1}\int^{s}_{0}\frac{1}{T-t_{0}-u}du-(m_{0}e^{as}-m_{0})|\rho+1|(1+L)e^{aT}

\displaystyle\geq b(t_{0},m_{0})+\frac{(C+\frac{1}{c_{1}c_{4}})(1+L)}{K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}}-1}\frac{s}{s+t_{0}}-(m_{0}e^{as}-m_{0})|\rho+1|(1+L)e^{aT}

\displaystyle=b(t_{0},m_{0})+\overline{C}_{1}\frac{s}{s+t_{0}}-\overline{C}_{2}(e^{as}-1),

and
\bar{b}^{*}(\xi)=\bar{b}(T-t-\xi)
, we can show that
\bar{b}^{*}(\xi)
satisfies the following integral equation:

(C.2)

\displaystyle 0=\int^{\xi}_{0}G(\xi,s,\bar{b}^{*}(\xi),\bar{b}^{*}(\xi-s))ds,

where

\displaystyle G(\xi,s,\bar{b}^{*}(\xi),\bar{b}^{*}(\xi-s)):

\displaystyle=e^{-\kappa s}\bigg((1-K^{\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}})\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}{\bar{b}^{*}(\xi)}^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}N(s,m)\Phi\Big(d^{2}(s,\frac{\bar{b}^{*}(\xi-s)}{\bar{b}^{*}(\xi)})\Big)

\displaystyle+\Phi\Big(d^{1}(s,\frac{\bar{b}^{*}(\xi-s)}{\bar{b}^{*}(\xi)})\Big)\bigg).

In order to solve the above integral equation (
C.2
), the recursive iteration method proceeds as follows.

We divide the interval
[0,\xi]
into
n
subintervals with end points
\xi_{j},j=0,1,2,...,n
where
\xi_{0}=0,\xi_{n}=\xi
and
\Delta\xi=\frac{\xi}{n}
. Let
\bar{b}^{*}_{j}
denote the numerical approximation to
\bar{b}^{*}(\xi_{j}),j=0,1,...,n
. For
\xi=\xi_{1}
, by the trapezoidal rule, integral equation (
C.2
) is approximated by

(C.3)

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\Delta\xi}{2}[G(\xi_{1},\xi_{0},\bar{b}^{*}_{1},\bar{b}^{*}_{1})+G(\xi_{1},\xi_{1},\bar{b}^{*}_{1},\bar{b}^{*}_{0})].

Since
\bar{b}^{*}_{0}=b(T,M^{m}_{T-t})=L
, the only unknown in (
C.3
) is
\bar{b}^{*}_{1}
. We can solve the algebraic equation (
C.3
) by applying the bisection method.
Similarly, for
\xi=\xi_{2}
, we have

(C.4)

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\Delta\xi}{2}[G(\xi_{2},\xi_{0},\bar{b}^{*}_{2},\bar{b}^{*}_{2})+2G(\xi_{2},\xi_{1},\bar{b}^{*}_{2},\bar{b}^{*}_{1})+G(\xi_{2},\xi_{2},\bar{b}^{*}_{2},\bar{b}^{*}_{0})].

Since
\bar{b}_{1}^{*}
is known from previous step, equation (
C.4
) can be solved for
\bar{b}_{2}^{*}
by the same procedure. Hence, for
\bar{b}_{k}^{*},k=2,3,...,n
, we can obtain
\bar{b}_{k}^{*}
recursively as the solution of the following algebraic equation,

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\Delta\xi}{2}[G(\xi_{k},\xi_{0},\bar{b}^{*}_{k},\bar{b}^{*}_{k})+2\sum^{k-1}_{j=1}G(\xi_{k},\xi_{k-j},\bar{b}^{*}_{k},\bar{b}^{*}_{j})+G(\xi_{k},\xi_{k},\bar{b}^{*}_{k},\bar{b}^{*}_{0})].

Now from the values of
\{\bar{b}^{*}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
\widehat{J}
in (
4.26
) can be approximated by

\displaystyle\widehat{J}(\xi,x,m)\approx\widehat{J}_{n}(\xi,x,m):=\frac{\Delta\xi}{2}[G(\xi_{n},\xi_{0},x,\bar{b}^{*}_{n})+2\sum^{n-1}_{j=1}G(\xi_{n},\xi_{n-j},x,\bar{b}^{*}_{j})+G(\xi_{n},\xi_{n},x,\bar{b}^{*}_{0})].

As shown by
Huang et al. 1996
, for sufficiently large number of subintervals
n
, the approximated free boundary
\bar{b}^{*}_{n}
converges to
\bar{b}^{*}(\xi)
, and therefore,
\widehat{J}_{n}
converges to
\widehat{J}
as well.
