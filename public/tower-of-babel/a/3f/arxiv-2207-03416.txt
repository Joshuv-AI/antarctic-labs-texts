Onsager’s Conjecture for Subgrid Scale α \alpha -Models of Turbulence
Daniel W. Boutros and Edriss S. Titi

Abstract
The first half of Onsager’s conjecture states that the Euler equations of an ideal incompressible fluid conserve energy if u ⁡ ( ⋅ , t ) ∈ C 0 , θ ​ ( 𝕋 3 ) u(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{3}) with θ > 1 3 \theta>\frac{1}{3} . In this paper, we prove an analogue of Onsager’s conjecture for several subgrid scale α \alpha -models of turbulence. In particular we find the required Hölder regularity of the solutions that ensures the conservation of energy-like quantities (either the H 1 ​ ( 𝕋 3 ) H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}) or L 2 ​ ( 𝕋 3 ) L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}) norms) for these models. We establish such results for the Leray- α \alpha model, the Euler- α \alpha equations (also known as the inviscid Camassa-Holm equations or Lagrangian averaged Euler equations), the modified Leray- α \alpha model, the Clark- α \alpha model and finally the magnetohydrodynamic Leray- α \alpha model. In a sense, all these models are inviscid regularisations of the Euler equations; and formally converge to the Euler equations as the regularisation length scale α → 0 + \alpha\rightarrow 0^{+} . Different Hölder exponents, smaller than 1 / 3 1/3 , are found for the regularity of solutions of these models (they are also formulated in terms of Besov and Sobolev spaces) that guarantee the conservation of the corresponding energy-like quantity. This is expected due to the smoother nonlinearity compared to the Euler equations. These results form a contrast to the universality of the 1 / 3 1/3 Onsager exponent found for general systems of conservation laws by (Gwiazda et al., 2018; Bardos et al., 2019).

Introduction

It is well-known that the
L^{2}
spatial norm (the kinetic energy) of smooth solutions of the Euler equations of an ideal incompressible fluid is conserved. On the other hand, turbulent flows, for high Reynolds numbers, in particular for an infinite Reynolds number, are not expected to be smooth. Therefore, they might not conserve energy. This observation was made by Lars Onsager
[
49
]
which led to the following conjecture (which is now a theorem).

Conjecture 1.1

(Onsager’s conjecture)
.

Let
v\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
be a weak solution of the Euler equations.
If
v\in L^{3}((0,T);C^{0,\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
\theta>\frac{1}{3}
, then the spatial
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
norm of the solution stays constant.

Furthermore, for
\theta<1/3
there exist weak solutions of the Euler equation that do not conserve energy.

In this context
\frac{1}{3}
is often referred to as the Onsager exponent. The importance of Onsager’s conjecture in mathematical fluid mechanics was pointed out in
[
30
]
. The first half of Onsager’s conjecture, i.e. about conservation of energy, was proved in
[
30
]
, but with slightly stronger assumptions than
C^{0,\theta}
with
\theta>\frac{1}{3}
. Subsequently, this part of Onsager’s conjecture was proved (i.e. if
\theta>\frac{1}{3}
) in
[
23
]
using Besov spaces and commutator estimates. A different proof of the first half was given in
[
29
]
, which relied on a local equation of energy balance.

The paper
[
50
]
studies the first half of the conjecture in the upper-half space
\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, subject to non-permeability boundary conditions on the wall/boundary
x_{3}=0
. This part of the conjecture was then proven in bounded domains with
C^{2}
boundary in
[
4
]
. The results of
[
4
]
were improved in
[
5
]
by only requiring interior Hölder regularity (instead of on the whole domain), for this the regularity of the pressure needed to be studied more carefully (which was also done in
[
6
]
). Finally,
[
2
,
34
]
considered the analogue of the first half of Onsager’s conjecture for the conservation of entropy and other companion laws in the general class of conservation laws. In particular, it established the universality of the exponent
\frac{1}{3}
for this case (see also
[
3
]
and references therein).

To establish the second half of the Onsager conjecture, namely the existence of non energy-conserving Hölder continuous weak solutions to the three-dimensional Euler equations, with Hölder exponent
\theta<\frac{1}{3}
techniques from convex integration were implemented.
In
[
40
]
weak solutions with regularity up to
\frac{1}{3}
were constructed which do not conserve energy, after gradual success in a sequence of papers
[
51
,
53
,
27
,
25
,
26
,
28
,
39
,
11
,
10
,
24
]
. In
[
12
]
energy-dissipating solutions were constructed with regularity less than
\frac{1}{3}
, while in
[
47
]
this was achieved in Besov spaces using an intermittent convex integration scheme.

It is natural to ask whether Onsager’s conjecture can be generalised to other models, as was done in
[
2
,
3
,
34
,
7
,
9
]
(see also references therein and
[
35
]
). Moreover, in recent years active scalar equations (including the surface quasi-geostrophic equation) have been the focus of several works, see for example
[
13
,
1
,
41
,
46
,
21
]
(and references therein). For many of these equations the Onsager conjecture remains open in the full range of exponents, including the 2D Euler equations
[
14
,
13
]
.

In this paper we study several subgrid scale turbulence models (also referred to as the
\alpha
models), such as the Euler-
\alpha
equations. This model is the inviscid version of the Navier-Stokes-
\alpha
equations (also known as the viscous Camassa-Holm equations)
[
31
,
32
]
.

The subgrid scale turbulence models have been studied because they approximate the Navier-Stokes equations. In the viscous case these models where shown to be successful subgrid scale models of turbulence when tested against experimental data. In particular, they have been shown to capture the right statistics of the Navier-Stokes equations for scales larger than the regularisation parameter
\alpha
.

Moreover, the Navier-Stokes-
\alpha
equations have been found to be a good closure model of turbulence for channel and pipe flow (if one chooses a Helmholtz smoothing kernel), as there is agreement with experimental data
[
17
]
. Further details can be found in
[
32
,
31
,
20
,
18
,
19
]
.

Analytically, the viscous
\alpha
-models have been shown to be globally well-posed. Moreover, when one lets the regularisation parameter
\alpha
go to zero they formally converge to the Navier-Stokes equations. This limit can be made rigorous. In fact, one can show that for a subsequence
(\alpha_{j})
of the regularisation parameter going to zero, the unique solutions of the Navier-Stokes-
\alpha
equations converge to a Leray-Hopf weak solution of the Navier-Stokes equations (globally in time). In addition, one can prove that the solution of the Navier-Stokes-
\alpha
model converges to the corresponding strong solution of the Navier-Stokes equations on its interval of existence. For further details see
[
31
,
32
]
.

The Euler-
\alpha
equations also appear in several contexts. For example, the equations arise as a description of the geodesics on the volume-preserving diffeomorphism group of the
H^{1}
norm
[
36
,
37
,
52
]
. Finally, they also can be derived from a specific choice of stress tensor for inviscid second-grade fluids
[
15
]
.

Over the years other subgrid scale
\alpha
-models have been considered. Examples include the Bardina model
[
17
]
, the Leray-
\alpha
model
[
22
]
, the modified Leray-
\alpha
model
[
38
]
, the Clark-
\alpha
model
[
16
]
and
the Navier-Stokes-Voigt model
[
44
]
. Finally, one can consider
\alpha
-type models for the MHD (magnetohydrodynamic) equations
[
45
]
.

In this paper we will study the conservation of energy-like quantities for the inviscid versions of these models and in particular deduce the corresponding Onsager exponents, which are the threshold Besov or Hölder exponents for energy conservation. The conserved quantity is not always the
L^{2}
norm, but can also be the
H^{1}
norm instead. What is important to emphasise is that in several cases, the exponent found is smaller than
1/3
, unlike for the models considered in
[
2
,
3
]
(and references therein).

First we observe that the results of
[
3
,
2
]
do not apply because the nonlinearity in the
\alpha
-models is nonlocal. For these models the advective velocity is more regular compared to the incompressible Euler equations, i.e. the nonlinearity is smoother. This means that compared to the Euler equations the
\alpha
-models have lower Onsager exponents and therefore weaker regularity conditions for energy conservation are expected.

The different subgrid scale models have different regularity thresholds due to several factors, such as the type of conserved quantity, the way the nonlinearity is regularised etc. This will be discussed further in the conclusion and in Remark
4.4
below.

It is also important to mention that for the Leray-
\alpha
MHD model
[
45
]
it is possible to ‘trade regularity assumptions’, i.e. there are several ways to formulate the criterion for energy conservation. To be more precise, it is possible to weaken the conditions on the magnetic field
B
if one strengthens the conditions on the velocity field
v
such that energy conservation is still ensured (and vice versa). In other words, there is some flexibility when the conditions for conservation of the energy-like quantity are formulated.
A similar mechanism can be observed for the ordinary MHD equations
[
14
]
.

In this paper we eventually consider the inviscid models:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\nabla\cdot(u\otimes v)+\nabla p=0,

(Leray-
\alpha
model)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\nabla\cdot(u\otimes v)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}v_{j}\nabla u_{j}+\nabla p=0,

(Navier-Stokes-
\alpha
model)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)+\nabla p=0,

(Navier-Stokes-Voigt)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)+\nabla p=0,

(Bardina model)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\nabla\cdot(v\otimes u)+\nabla p=0,

(Modified Leray-
\alpha
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\nabla\cdot(u\otimes v)+\nabla\cdot(v\otimes u)-\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(\nabla u\cdot\nabla u^{T})+\nabla p=0,

(Clark-
\alpha
model)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v+(u\cdot\nabla)v+\nabla p+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\lvert B\rvert^{2}=(B\cdot\nabla)B,\\
\partial_{t}B+(u\cdot\nabla)B-(B\cdot\nabla)v=0.\end{cases}

(Leray-
\alpha
MHD model)

Throughout this paper the models will be subject to periodic boundary conditions on the three-dimensional flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. Therefore the domains will sometimes be omitted from now on. These models are incompressible, i.e.

\nabla\cdot u=\nabla\cdot v=0.

In addition
u
is a Helmholtz regularisation of
v
, which is

v=(1-\alpha^{2}\Delta)u.

(1.1)

We also introduce the following Sobolev norm

\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}=\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\alpha^{2}\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{2}}.

(1.2)

For all these models we consider the inviscid case
\nu=0
(and the irresistive case
\eta=0
for the Leray-
\alpha
MHD model). Under that assumption the Navier-Stokes-
\alpha
equations are called the Euler-
\alpha
equations and the Navier-Stokes-Voigt model is called the Euler-Voigt model. Note that the inviscid Bardina model and the Euler-Voigt model are the same.

The Euler-Voigt model is of interest because it is an inviscid regularisation of the Euler equations. To be more precise, it has the same form as the Euler equations but with an additional term
-\alpha^{2}\Delta\partial_{t}u
. Note that this additional term is independent of viscosity and that the Euler-Voigt model is globally well-posed with and without viscosity. It can be shown by classical methods that the unique weak solution of the Euler-Voigt model conserves energy
[
17
,
42
]
. For this reason, there is no analogue of the Onsager conjecture for this model, which is why the Euler-Voigt model will not be considered any further in this work.

Our approach in this paper will be based on
[
29
]
, and the results were part of
[
8
]
. We first derive an equation describing the local energy balance. This equation contains a ‘defect term’ which captures a (potential) lack of smoothness of the solution and physically describes the energy flux. In particular, it captures the possible dissipation of energy for weak solutions when the solutions are not smooth enough.

In our framework (cf.
[
8
]
), we will show that under sufficiently strong regularity assumptions, this term will be zero. This in turn rules out the dissipation (or non-conservation) of energy.

The procedure of deriving the Onsager exponent will be exactly the same for all these models. Therefore we will only work out the detailed proof for the inviscid Leray-
\alpha
model and the Euler-
\alpha
equations. These models have a different type of conserved quantity (the
L^{2}
norm and the
H^{1}
norm respectively) and their weak formulations are quite different in nature. In the main text we just state the results for the three other models (the Modified Leray-
\alpha
, Clark-
\alpha
and Leray-
\alpha
MHD models) for the sake of brevity and because the cases of the Leray-
\alpha
model and the Euler-
\alpha
equations are illustrative of how the proofs work for the other models. The detailed proofs for the other models are then given in the appendices.

The conserved quantity for the Leray-
\alpha
model is the
L^{2}
norm in space of
v
. The conserved quantity for the Euler-
\alpha
, Modified Leray-
\alpha
and Clark-
\alpha
models is the
H^{1}
norm in space of
u
given by equation (
1.2
). The conserved quantity for the inviscid and irresistive Leray-
\alpha
MHD model is given by

\lVert v(\cdot,t)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+\lVert B(\cdot,t)\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}.

The different types of conserved quantities do not really alter the proof significantly. However, it is important to mention that the regularity assumptions for the turbulence models differ. For the inviscid Leray-
\alpha
model we assume that the weak solutions are in the space
v\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
, while for the Euler-
\alpha
equations, the modified Leray-
\alpha
and Clark-
\alpha
models the regularity assumption is
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. For the MHD Leray-
\alpha
model the assumption is
v,B\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
.

We now outline the rest of the paper. In section
2
we prove the first half of the Onsager conjecture for the Leray-
\alpha

we prove the result for the Euler-
\alpha

We conclude in section

In the appendices we provide the detailed proofs for the other models we consider. Namely, in section
A

\alpha
model, while in section
B
we consider the Clark-
\alpha
model. Finally, in section

\alpha

We emphasise that in this paper we focus on the first half of Onsager’s conjecture. The recent paper
[

]
treats both the first as well as the second half for the Euler-
\alpha

2
The Leray-
\alpha
model

In this section, we prove the first half of Onsager’s conjecture for the Leray-
\alpha
model. We start by deriving an equation of local energy balance. This equation describes the time evolution of the conserved quantity in terms of an energy flux (which will be referred to as the defect term). We will first introduce some notation. We let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a standard
C^{\infty}_{c}
radial mollifier satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\phi(x)=1
and let
\phi_{\epsilon}
be defined by

\phi_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\epsilon^{3}}\phi\bigg(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\bigg).

We also introduce the notation
v^{\epsilon}\coloneqq v*\phi_{\epsilon}
. Throughout this paper we will be using the Einstein summation convention. Finally we will use the brackets
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to denote the action of a distribution on a test function. We will first introduce the notion of a weak solution for the inviscid Leray-
\alpha
model.

Definition 2.1
.

A pair of functions
v,p\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
is called a weak solution of the inviscid Leray-
\alpha
model if for all
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\chi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R})
it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}v_{i}\partial_{t}\psi_{i}dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}v_{j}u_{i}\partial_{i}\psi_{j}dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\partial_{i}\psi_{i}dxdt=0,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\partial_{i}\chi dx=0.

(2.2)

Note that the pressure is defined up to a constant. We fix this constant by assuming that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p(x,t)dx=0.

Remark 2.2
.

For the Leray-
\alpha
model we can derive the following equation for the pressure (which holds in the sense of distributions for test functions in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T))
)

\Delta p=-(\nabla\otimes\nabla):(u\otimes v),

where we recall that
v\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and
u=(I-\alpha^{2}\Delta)^{-1}v\in L^{\infty}((0,T);H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. By elliptic regularity it follows that

\displaystyle\lVert p\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq c\lVert u\otimes v\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq c\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq c\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2}}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq c\lVert v\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

for some constant
c
(which may change from line to line). Note that we have used the Sobolev embedding theorem in three dimensions,
H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
, in the above. For this reason we introduced the assumption that
p\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
in the definition of a weak solution for the Leray-
\alpha
model.

In fact, instead of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T))
one can consider a larger space of test functions, as we prove in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

A weak solution of the inviscid Leray-
\alpha
model also satisfies identity (
2.1
) for functions
\psi\in W^{1,1}_{0}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
.

Proof.

For every
\psi\in W^{1,1}_{0}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
we know that there exists a sequence of test functions
\psi_{m}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T))
that converge to
\psi
in
W^{1,1}_{0}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. For each of the functions
\psi_{m}
, identity (
2.1
) holds. We observe that
v\cdot\partial_{t}\psi_{m}
converges to
v\partial_{t}\psi
in
L^{1}((0,T);L^{3/2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
as
m\rightarrow\infty
. In particular, this implies that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}v\cdot\partial_{t}\psi_{m}dxdt\xrightarrow{m\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}v\cdot\partial_{t}\psi dxdt.

Similarly, one can see that
v_{j}u_{i}\partial_{i}(\psi_{m})_{j}
converges to
v_{j}u_{i}\partial_{i}\psi_{j}
in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{3})
as
m\rightarrow\infty
(by using that
H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
). Therefore, we have

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}v_{j}u_{i}\partial_{i}(\psi_{m})_{j}dxdt\xrightarrow{m\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}v_{j}u_{i}\partial_{i}\psi_{j}dxdt.

Finally, as mentioned in Remark
2.2
, we know that
p\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. Therefore
p\partial_{i}(\psi_{m})
converges to
p\partial_{i}\psi
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T))
as
m\rightarrow\infty
. Thus

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\partial_{i}(\psi_{m})dxdt\xrightarrow{m\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\partial_{i}\psi_{i}dxdt.

As a result identity (
2.1
) also holds for test functions
\psi\in W^{1,1}_{0}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
.
∎

We now turn to establishing the equation of local energy balance.

Theorem 2.4

(Equation of energy balance)
.

Let
v\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
be a weak solution of the inviscid Leray-
\alpha
model (as introduced in Definition
2.1
). Then
v
satisfies the following equation of energy (which holds in the sense of distributions for test functions in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T))
)

\partial_{t}(\lvert v\rvert^{2})+2\nabla\cdot(pv)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)+D_{1}(u,v)=0,

(2.3)

where the defect term
D_{1}(u,v)
is

D_{1}(u,v)(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta v(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}d\xi,

and the notation means

\delta w(\xi;x,t)\coloneqq w(x+\xi,t)-w(x,t).

Note that the defect term is independent of the choice of mollifer.

Proof.

Mollifying the equation of the Leray-
\alpha
model by convolution with
\phi_{\epsilon}
gives that

\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}+\partial_{i}(u_{i}v)^{\epsilon}+\nabla p^{\epsilon}=0.

(2.4)

This equation holds pointwise a.e. in
\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T)
. This is because
v^{\epsilon}
is
C^{\infty}
in space and we can use the equation to gain time regularity for
v^{\epsilon}
. In particular, we observe that
\partial_{i}(u_{i}v_{j})^{\epsilon}+\nabla p^{\epsilon}
is in
L^{\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
which implies that
v^{\epsilon}\in W^{1,\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. We take a test function
\chi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R})
and take the dot product of equation (
2.4
) with
v\chi\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. The resulting equation is

\chi v\cdot\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}+\chi v_{j}\partial_{i}(u_{i}v_{j})^{\epsilon}+\chi v\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}=0,

(2.5)

which holds almost everywhere in
\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T)
. Now we let the test function
\chi v^{\epsilon}
act on the original inviscid Leray-
\alpha
equation. Note that we can use
\chi v^{\epsilon}
as a test function since it lies in
W^{1,\infty}_{0}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))\subset W^{1,1}_{0}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
(so we can apply Lemma
2.3
). We subtract the resulting equation from the integrated version of equation (
2.5
), which gives (where we have used the weak formulation from Definition
2.1
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\chi v\cdot\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}-v\partial_{t}(\chi v^{\epsilon})+\chi v_{j}\partial_{i}(u_{i}v_{j})^{\epsilon}-v_{j}u_{i}\partial_{i}(\chi v_{j}^{\epsilon})+\chi v\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}-p\partial_{i}(v^{\epsilon}_{i}\chi)\bigg]dxdt=0.

Now we rewrite the different parts of the above equation, we see that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\chi v\cdot\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}-v\cdot\partial_{t}(\chi v^{\epsilon})\bigg]dxdt=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(v\cdot v^{\epsilon})\partial_{t}\chi dxdt=\langle\partial_{t}(v\cdot v^{\epsilon}),\chi\rangle.

To handle the advective terms, we introduce a defect term

D_{1,\epsilon}(u,v)(x,t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta v(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}d\xi,

observe that

D_{1,\epsilon}(u,v)=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}(u_{i}v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2}u_{i}\partial_{i}(v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}+v_{j}\partial_{i}(v_{j}u_{i})^{\epsilon}-u_{i}v_{j}\partial_{i}v_{j}^{\epsilon},

which holds almost everywhere in
\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T)
. Now we can rewrite the advective terms as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\chi v_{j}\partial_{i}(u_{i}v_{j})^{\epsilon}-v_{j}u_{i}\partial_{i}(\chi v_{j}^{\epsilon})\bigg]dxdt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\chi v_{j}\partial_{i}(u_{i}v_{j})^{\epsilon}-\chi v_{j}u_{i}\partial_{i}v_{j}^{\epsilon}-v_{j}u_{i}v_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\chi D_{1,\epsilon}(u,v)+\frac{1}{2}\chi\partial_{i}(u_{i}v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}-\frac{1}{2}\chi u_{i}\partial_{i}(v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}-v_{j}u_{i}v_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\chi D_{1,\epsilon}(u,v)+\frac{1}{2}\bigg((v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}u_{i}-(u_{i}v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg)\partial_{i}\chi-v_{j}u_{i}v_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle D_{1,\epsilon}(u,v)+\nabla\cdot((\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon}-(\lvert v\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u)+\nabla\cdot((v\cdot v^{\epsilon})u),\chi\bigg\rangle.

Next, we show that
(v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}u_{i}-(u_{i}v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0
in
L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. One can see that since
u_{i}(\cdot,t)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
and
v_{j}(\cdot,t)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
then
u_{i}v_{j}v_{j}(\cdot,t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
, thus

\displaystyle\lVert(u_{i}v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}-u_{i}(v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}\rVert_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert(u_{i}v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}-u_{i}v_{j}v_{j}\rVert_{L^{1}}+\lVert u_{i}v_{j}v_{j}-u_{i}(v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}\rVert_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert(u_{i}v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}-u_{i}v_{j}v_{j}\rVert_{L^{1}}+\lVert u_{i}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert v_{j}v_{j}-(v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}\rVert_{L^{1}}\xrightarrow{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}0.

Consequently, the advective terms converge to
\langle D_{1}(u,v)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u),\chi\rangle
in the sense of distributions as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, where
D_{1}(u,v)\coloneqq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}D_{1,\epsilon}(u,v)
.
Finally, we get that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\chi v\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}-p\partial_{i}(v^{\epsilon}_{i}\chi)\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\psi v\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}-pv^{\epsilon}_{i}\partial_{i}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[p^{\epsilon}v_{i}\partial_{i}\chi+pv^{\epsilon}_{i}\partial_{i}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\cdot(p^{\epsilon}v+pv^{\epsilon}),\chi\rangle.

Combining these results we get the following equation of energy (which holds in the sense of distributions with test functions in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T))
)

\partial_{t}(v\cdot v^{\epsilon})+D_{1,\epsilon}(u,v)+\nabla\cdot((v\cdot v^{\epsilon})u)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot((\lvert v\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon}+\nabla\cdot(p^{\epsilon}v+pv^{\epsilon})=0.

From this equation we can obtain that the limit
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}D_{1,\epsilon}(u)
is a well-defined distributional limit, as all the other terms converge. As was argued before, we will have that
-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot((\lvert v\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon}\xrightarrow{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}0
as a distributional limit. Moreover, since
v\cdot v^{\epsilon}\rightarrow\lvert v\rvert^{2}
in
L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
, it follows that
\partial_{t}(v\cdot v^{\epsilon})\rightarrow\partial_{t}\lvert v\rvert^{2}
in the distributional limit.
We observe that we can write the following equation for the defect term

D_{1}(u,v)=-\partial_{t}\lvert v\rvert^{2}-\nabla\cdot(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)-\nabla\cdot(2pv).

Observe that the right-hand side is completely independent of the choice of mollifier, as a result the defect term is independent of the choice of mollifier.
We end up with the equation

\partial_{t}\lvert v\rvert^{2}+D_{1}(u,v)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)+\nabla\cdot(2pv)=0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
v
be a weak solution of the inviscid Leray-
\alpha
model. Moreover, we assume that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u\rvert\lvert\delta v\rvert^{2}dx\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{1}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

(2.6)

where
C\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
\sigma_{1}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, with the property that
\sigma_{1}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\rightarrow 0
as
\lvert\xi\rvert\rightarrow 0
. Then
D_{1}(u,v)=0
.

Proof.

By the change of variable
\xi=\epsilon z
one can calculate that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}

\displaystyle\lvert D_{1,\epsilon}(u,v)(x,t)\rvert dx\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\rvert\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u\rvert\lvert\delta v\rvert^{2}dxd\xi

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}C(t)dt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\rvert\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{1}(\lvert\xi\rvert)d\xi=\int_{0}^{T}C(t)dt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\lvert\nabla_{z}\phi(z)\rvert\lvert z\rvert\sigma_{1}(\epsilon\lvert z\rvert)dz\xrightarrow{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}0,

where we used the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem to justify the limit in the last step as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. Moreover, note that
\lvert\delta u\rvert\lvert\delta v\rvert^{2}\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
so one can swap the order of integration in the first inequality. Consequently, one has
D_{1}(u,v)=0
.
∎

In order to prove the sufficient condition for energy conservation, we will use the following inequality for functions in Besov spaces (see, e.g.,
[
23
,
43
]
)

\lVert f(\cdot+\xi)-f(\cdot)\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq C\lvert\xi\rvert^{\theta}\lVert f\rVert_{B^{\theta}_{p,\infty}},

(2.7)

which holds for
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
\theta>0
and some constant
C
.

Proposition 2.6
.

If
v
is a weak solution of the Leray-
\alpha
model such that
v\in L^{3}((0,T);B^{\theta}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
\theta>0
, then it holds that
D_{1}(u,v)=0
. In particular, the solution conserves energy.

Proof.

Condition (
2.6
) can be verified quite easily to see that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u\rvert\lvert\delta v\rvert^{2}dx\leq\lvert\xi\rvert^{1+2\theta}\lVert v\rVert_{B^{\theta}_{3,\infty}}^{3},

which follows from using inequality (
2.7
).
Then we can take
\sigma_{1}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\coloneqq\lvert\xi\rvert^{2\theta}
, which tends to zero as
\lvert\xi\rvert\to 0
. Therefore, the proof can be completed thanks to Proposition
2.5
.
∎

Having analysed the defect term, we can prove the conservation of energy. This comes down to a suitable choice of test function (which ensures that the divergence term vanishes) and then using the Lebesgue differentiation theorem to prove conservation of the energy.

Theorem 2.7

(Conservation of energy)
.

Suppose
v\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
is a weak solution of the inviscid Leray-
\alpha
model and in addition it satisfies the conditions of Proposition
2.5
(for example when
v\in L^{3}((0,T);B^{s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
s>1
, by Proposition
2.6
). Then conservation of energy holds, i.e.

\lVert v(t_{1},\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}}=\lVert v(t_{2},\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}},

for almost all
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,T)
.

Proof.

Using Proposition
2.5
together with the local equation of energy established in Theorem
2.4
gives us that

\partial_{t}(\lvert v\rvert^{2})+2\nabla\cdot(pv)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)=0.

This equation is valid in the distributional sense, i.e. for any test function
\psi\in\mathcal{D}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{3})
it holds that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\frac{1}{2}\lvert v\rvert^{2}\partial_{t}\psi dxdt=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla\psi\cdot\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\lvert v\rvert^{2}u+vp\bigg)\bigg)dxdt.

(2.8)

We want to show that the
L^{2}
norm of
v
at time
t_{1}
equals the
L^{2}
norm at
t_{2}
(where
t_{1}<t_{2}
). Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a standard
C^{\infty}_{c}
mollifier with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(t)dt=1
with support contained in
[-1,1]
. We introduce the notation

\varphi_{\epsilon}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\varphi\bigg(\frac{t}{\epsilon}\bigg).

We make the following choice of test function

\psi_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{\epsilon}(t^{\prime}-t_{1})-\varphi_{\epsilon}(t^{\prime}-t_{2})dt^{\prime},

for
\epsilon
sufficiently small. Because of the properties of mollifiers, one can see that the function
\psi_{1}(t)=0
for
t\in(0,t_{1}-\epsilon)\cup(t_{2}+\epsilon,T)
, and
\psi_{1}(t)=1
for
t\in(t_{1}+\epsilon,t_{2}-\epsilon)
. This means in particular that the function has compact support in
(0,T)
. Therefore equation (
2.8
) becomes

\int_{t_{1}-\epsilon}^{t_{1}+\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert v\rvert^{2}\varphi_{\epsilon}(t-t_{1})dxdt=\int_{t_{2}-\epsilon}^{t_{2}+\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert v\rvert^{2}\varphi_{\epsilon}(t-t_{2})dxdt.

Then we apply the Lebesgue differentiation theorem (cf.,
[
33
,
54
]
), by taking the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, which yields

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert v(x,t_{1})\rvert^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert v(x,t_{2})\rvert^{2}dx

for almost every
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,T)
.
Therefore we conclude that the
L^{2}
norm
\lVert v(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}}
is conserved for almost all
t\in(0,T)
. Thus we proved conservation of energy for weak solutions (i.e. the analogue of the first half of Onsager’s conjecture for this model) under the assumptions of Proposition
2.5
.
∎

3
The Euler-
\alpha
equations

We now prove a similar result for the Euler-
\alpha
equations, we recall that the equations are given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\nu\Delta v+\nabla\cdot(u\otimes v)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}v_{j}\nabla u_{j}+\nabla p=0,

\displaystyle v=u-\alpha^{2}\Delta u,\quad\nabla\cdot u=\nabla\cdot v=0.

The definition of a weak solution is slightly different compared to the Leray-
\alpha
model, since the regularity assumption is now
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
instead of
v\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
.

Definition 3.1
.

A pair of functions
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and
p\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
is called a weak solution of the Euler-
\alpha
equations if it satisfies the equations (for all
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\chi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R})
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\cdot\partial_{t}\psi dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u:\nabla\partial_{t}\psi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla\psi dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(\partial_{i}u\otimes\partial_{i}u):\nabla\psi dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes\partial_{i}u):\nabla\partial_{i}\psi dxdt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\psi_{i}dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}\psi_{i}dxdt+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}\psi dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot\psi dxdt=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\partial_{i}\chi dxdt=0.

Once again, the pressure will be determined uniquely by assuming that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p(x,t)dx=0.

As before, it is possible to extend the space of test functions for these models, like was done in Lemma
2.3
.

Remark 3.2
.

We observe that for the Euler-
\alpha
equations it is possible to write down the following distributional equation for the pressure (where
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R})
)

\displaystyle\langle\Delta p,\psi\rangle+\langle(\nabla\otimes\nabla):(u\otimes u),\psi\rangle+\alpha^{2}\langle(\nabla\otimes\nabla):(\partial_{i}u\otimes\partial_{i}u),\psi\rangle-\alpha^{2}\langle\partial_{i}(\nabla\otimes\nabla):(u\otimes\partial_{i}u),\psi\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\Delta(\lvert u\rvert^{2}),\psi\rangle-\alpha^{2}\langle\partial_{k}\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}),\psi\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\langle\Delta(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}),\psi\rangle=0.

We can conclude from this equation that if
u\in L^{3}((0,T);W^{1,3}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
then by elliptic regularity theory it follows that
p\in L^{3/2}((0,T);L^{3/2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
, this observation will be used later.

As we did with the Leray-
\alpha
model, we now enlarge the space of test functions in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

The weak formulation of the Euler-
\alpha
equations still holds if
\psi\in W^{1,1}_{0}((0,T);\linebreak H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))\cap L^{1}((0,T);H^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
.

Proof.

The proof goes the same way as the proof of Lemma
2.3
, by using a density argument.
∎

Now we will establish the equation of local energy balance.

Theorem 3.4

(Equation of energy balance)
.

Let
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
be a weak solution of the Euler-
\alpha
equations such that
u\in L^{3}((0,T);W^{1,3}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. Then the following equation of local energy balance holds in the sense of distributions,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2})-2\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+D_{2}(u)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)

\displaystyle+2\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u_{j}u_{j}\partial_{k}u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{3}(u)+2\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}u)+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}u_{j})=0,

where the defect terms are given by

\displaystyle D_{2}(u)(x,t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert^{2}d\xi,

\displaystyle D_{3}(u)(x,t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{i}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\delta u_{i}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{j}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{j}(\xi;x,t)d\xi,

where the limits above also hold in the sense of distribution. Moreover, the defect terms,
D_{2}(u),D_{3}(u)
, are independent of the choice of mollifier.

Proof.

By mollifying the Euler-
\alpha
equations we find that
\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon},\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. As a result, we can conclude that
u^{\epsilon}\in W^{1,\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. As a result, we are able to apply Lemma
3.3
and take
u^{\epsilon}\chi
as our test function. Mollifying our equation and multiplying it by
u\chi
and subtracting it from the weak formulation gives us that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon})dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u:\nabla\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla(u\chi):\partial_{t}\nabla u^{\epsilon}dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi u\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u):\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot(\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes\partial_{k}u):\nabla\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}(u\chi)\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes\partial_{k}u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}(u^{\epsilon}_{i}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\chi(u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}_{i}\chi)dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\chi\partial_{k}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}(u^{\epsilon}_{i}\chi)dxdt+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}\chi dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}dxdt=0.

We first deal with the time derivative terms, we get that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon})dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u:\nabla\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla(u\chi):\partial_{t}\nabla u^{\epsilon}dxdt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[(u\cdot u^{\epsilon})\partial_{t}\chi+\alpha^{2}\nabla u:\nabla u^{\epsilon}\partial_{t}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\nabla u:\nabla\chi\otimes\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}+\nabla u:\partial_{t}\nabla\chi\otimes u^{\epsilon}-\nabla\chi\otimes u:\partial_{t}\nabla u^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[(u\cdot u^{\epsilon})\partial_{t}\chi+\alpha^{2}\nabla u:\nabla u^{\epsilon}\partial_{t}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\nabla u:\nabla\chi\otimes\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}+\nabla u:\partial_{t}\nabla\chi\otimes u^{\epsilon}+\partial_{t}\nabla\chi\otimes u:\nabla u^{\epsilon}+\nabla\chi\otimes\partial_{t}u:\nabla u^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

This can be rewritten as

-\bigg\langle\partial_{t}(u\cdot u^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\nabla u:\nabla u^{\epsilon})-\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg)+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg),\chi\bigg\rangle.

Later we will choose
\chi
such that it only depends on time, for which we will have

\bigg\langle-\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg)+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg),\chi\bigg\rangle=0.

We introduce a defect term given by

D_{2,\epsilon}(u)(x,t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert^{2}d\xi,

then by similar reasoning to the case of the Leray-
\alpha
model we find that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi u\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[-\chi D_{2,\epsilon}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\bigg((u_{j}u_{j})^{\epsilon}u_{i}-(u_{i}u_{j}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg)\partial_{i}\chi+u_{j}u_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=-\bigg\langle D_{2,\epsilon}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot((\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon}-(\lvert u\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u)+\nabla\cdot((u\cdot u^{\epsilon})u),\chi\bigg\rangle.

We have the terms

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\chi\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}(u_{j}\chi)\partial_{i}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}_{i}\chi)dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\chi\partial_{k}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi\bigg[\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}-\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}\chi

\displaystyle-\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}u_{i}^{\epsilon}\partial_{k}\chi\bigg]dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi\bigg[\partial_{i}u_{j}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})^{\epsilon}-\partial_{k}u_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[u_{j}\partial_{i}\chi(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})^{\epsilon}-u_{i}\partial_{k}\chi(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi\bigg[-\frac{1}{2}D_{3,\epsilon}(u)-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2}u_{i}\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}\chi\partial_{k}u^{\epsilon}_{j}+u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{k}\chi+u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}\chi+\partial_{k}\partial_{i}\chi u_{j}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+\partial_{k}\chi\partial_{i}u_{j}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt.

The defect term that was used above is given by

\displaystyle D_{3,\epsilon}(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

\displaystyle\big[\partial_{i}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\delta u_{i}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{j}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{j}(\xi;x,t)d\xi=-\partial_{i}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+u_{i}\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}+2\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}-2u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\big]d\xi.

Now we can write that

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}(u^{\epsilon}_{i}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\chi(u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi\partial_{i}\big[u_{i}^{\epsilon}u_{j}u_{j}\big]dxdt-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi\partial_{i}\big[u_{i}(u_{j}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\big]dxdt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg\langle\nabla\cdot\big(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u^{\epsilon}+(u\cdot u)^{\epsilon}u\big),\chi\bigg\rangle.

The next part we can write as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}(u^{\epsilon}_{i}\chi)dxdt+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}\chi dxdt

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\bigg\langle\nabla\cdot\big[\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}u^{\epsilon}+(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}u\big],\chi\bigg\rangle.

The contribution of the pressure terms can subsequently be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}dxdt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}[pu^{\epsilon}+up^{\epsilon}]\cdot\nabla\chi dxdt

\displaystyle=-\bigg\langle\nabla\cdot(pu^{\epsilon}+p^{\epsilon}u),\chi\bigg\rangle.

Combining all these derivations, we obtain the following equation

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\partial_{t}(u\cdot u^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\nabla u:\nabla u^{\epsilon})-\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg)+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg)

\displaystyle+D_{2,\epsilon}(u)+\nabla\cdot((\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon}-(\lvert u\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u)+\nabla\cdot((u\cdot u^{\epsilon})u)-\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u_{j}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{k}u)-\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}u_{i}^{\epsilon})-\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}u_{j}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})^{\epsilon}+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}u_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(u_{j}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u)^{\epsilon})-\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}(u_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon})+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{3,\epsilon}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}(u_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}^{\epsilon}u)+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}u_{j}^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}\partial_{i}(u_{j}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}(\partial_{i}u_{j}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}),\chi\bigg\rangle=0.

We observe that
D_{2,\epsilon}(u)
and
D_{3,\epsilon}(u)
are well-defined for any
\epsilon>0
. It is now possible to express the sum
\big[D_{2,\epsilon}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{3,\epsilon}(u)\big]
using the other terms in the above equation. Since the limit of all the other terms in the above equation is a well-defined as distributional limit and is independent of the choice of mollifier, we conclude that the limit
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\big[D_{2,\epsilon}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{3,\epsilon}(u)\big]
exists as a distribution and will be denoted by
\big[D_{2}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{3}(u)\big]
. Then by taking the limit we obtain the following equation of local energy balance

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\partial_{t}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2})-2\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+\big[D_{2}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{3}(u)\big]

\displaystyle+\nabla\cdot(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)+2\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u_{j}u_{j}\partial_{k}u)+2\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}u)+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}u_{j}),\chi\bigg\rangle=0.

In fact, we will prove, in what follows, that
D_{2}(u):=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}D_{2,\epsilon}(u)=0
for all weak solutions of the Euler-
\alpha
equations, without any additional regularity assumption on
u
. This implies that
D_{3}(u):=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}D_{3,\epsilon}
exists as a distribution. For this reason we used a separate notation for the terms
D_{2}
and
D_{3}
in the statement of the theorem.
∎

Now we state a sufficient condition for the defect terms,
D_{2}(u),D_{3}(u)
, to be zero.

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume that
u
is a weak solution of the Euler-
\alpha
equations such that it satisfies the following bounds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{3}dx\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{2}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert\lvert\delta\nabla u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}dx\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{3}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

where
C\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
\sigma_{k}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
has the property that
\sigma_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\rightarrow 0
as
\lvert\xi\rvert\rightarrow 0
, for
k=2,3
. Then
D_{2}(u)=D_{3}(u)=0
.

Proof.

The proof is completely analogous to the proof of Proposition
2.5
.
∎

Now we state the regularity assumption that ensures that the sufficient conditions of Proposition
3.5
are satisfied.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of the Euler-
\alpha
equations such that
u\in L^{3}((0,T);\linebreak B^{s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
s>1
. Then
D_{2}(u)=D_{3}(u)=0
. In particular, the weak solution conserves energy.

Proof.

We need to verify that the assumptions of Proposition
3.5
are satisfied. We first observe that by the Besov embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})=B^{1}_{2,2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\subset B^{1/2}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
(see, e.g.,
[
43
]
for futher details). Therefore, by the above and (
2.7
) we have

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{3}dx\leq\lvert\xi\rvert^{3/2}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{3}.

We can then take
\sigma_{2}(\lvert\xi\rvert)=\lvert\xi\rvert^{1/2}
, which indeed converges to zero as
\lvert\xi\rvert\to 0
. We therefore conclude that
D_{2}(u)=0
for all weak solutions of the Euler-
\alpha
equations, without any additional regularity assumption on
u
. The second assumption of Proposition
3.5
is also satisfied since

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert\lvert\delta\nabla u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}dx\leq\lvert\xi\rvert^{2s-1}\lVert u\rVert_{B^{s}_{3,\infty}}^{3},

where we have applied inequality (
2.7
). Since
s>1
, then
\sigma_{3}(\lvert\xi\rvert)=\lvert\xi\rvert^{2s-2}
tends to zero as
\lvert\xi\rvert\to 0
.
∎

Finally, we prove the conservation of energy.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of the Euler-
\alpha
equations with
u\in L^{3}((0,T);B^{s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
s>1
. Then
u
conserves energy, which means that

\lVert u(\cdot,t_{1})\rVert_{H^{1}}=\lVert u(\cdot,t_{2})\rVert_{H^{2}},

for almost every
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,T)
.

Proof.

The proof works the same way as the proof of Theorem
2.7
.
∎

[
7
]
is developing a convex integration scheme for the Euler-
\alpha
equations to construct weak solutions that do not conserve energy. In this paper we provide a study of several subgrid scale
\alpha

In addition, the paper
[
7

[
23

[

we will consider what happens to the Onsager exponent if the Helmholtz regularisation in equation (
1.1
) for the inviscid Leray-
\alpha

. This leads to a linear relationship between the Onsager exponent and
\theta
. Deriving such a relation by using commutator estimates is possible, but would be more tedious.

To conclude this section, we make a remark on what ‘determines’ the Onsager exponent for a given PDE.

Remark 4.4

(‘Origin’ of the Onsager exponent)
.

Observe that the bound (
2.6
) in Proposition
2.5
is of the form
|\xi|\sigma_{1}(|\xi|)
, where
\sigma_{1}=o(1)
, as
|\xi|\to 0
. This is the essence of why different models have different Onsager exponents. The required regularity is to ensure bounds on the defect terms of the form
|\xi|\sigma_{k}(|\xi|)
, where the
|\xi|
factor is ‘distributed’ into fractional regularities among the three terms in the product, and where the relevant
\sigma_{k}=o(1)
, as
|\xi|\to 0
. Therefore, the more regular these terms are, the lower the Onsager exponent will be. For instance, for the case of the Euler equations one has the following condition for energy conservation
[
29
]

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta v(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{3}dx\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{1}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

which implies an Onsager (Besov) exponent of
\frac{1}{3}
since all the three terms are the same and hence the factor
\lvert\xi\rvert
is equally distributed among the three terms. In the case of the Leray-
\alpha
model, the term
\delta v
has two degrees less regularity than the term
\delta u
. Therefore assuming a Besov exponent of more than
0
for
v
implies that
u
has a Besov exponent of more than
2
which combines to give a total exponent of more than 1.

So the crucial difference between the Euler equations and the Leray-
\alpha
model is that in the defect term one factor of
\delta v
is replaced by a factor of
\delta u
, which has two degrees more regularity. Therefore under less assumptions on the weak solution than for the Euler equations, the regularity of the factor
\delta u
still can ensure that
\sigma_{1}=o(1)
, as
\lvert\xi\rvert\to 0
, which implies that the defect term is zero.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we have studied several subgrid scale
\alpha
-models of turbulence, obtained equations of local energy balance and found Onsager exponents in terms of Besov, Hölder and Sobolev spaces. Several of these exponents were different from
1/3
, which is the Onsager exponent for the Euler equations. This means that the Onsager exponent is not universal and is plausibly determined by the regularity of the coefficient functions of the nonlinearity.

It was already discussed in Remark
4.4
what mechanism seems to lead to different values of the Onsager exponent for the different models. We mention that it is straightforward to predict the Onsager exponent for a given PDE of this type without doing a complete analysis by establishing an equation of local energy balance with a precise defect term.

If a PDE of this type has a formally conserved quantity, the way one obtains the conservation law is by multiplying by a given function (such as
u
or
v
) and then invoking the divergence theorem to show the time derivative of the conserved quantity is zero.

This already tells you what kind of products the defect term will contain. From Lemma
2.5
one can conclude that the function
\sigma
in equation (
2.6
) needs to be
o(1)
. Therefore one can already tell which regularity assumption will assure a condition of type (
2.6
) is satisfied. The difference in Onsager exponents therefore is caused by the different forms of the defect terms.

For the Leray-
\alpha
model one has a product
\lvert\delta v\rvert^{2}\lvert\delta u\rvert
. For the Euler-
\alpha
equations it is
\lvert\delta\nabla u\rvert^{2}\lvert\delta u\rvert
. Since
u
is more regular than
v
, it follows that the Euler-
\alpha
model has a lower Onsager exponent than the Leray-
\alpha
model (namely a Besov exponent of 1 instead of
2
for
u
).

Several other MHD-
\alpha
models exist and a similar analysis for these should be possible. Examples include the modified Leray-
\alpha
MHD model, see
[
45
]
for more details. In addition, one can consider what happens if one uses a different regularisation instead of the standard Helmholtz regularisation which was used throughout the paper (see also
[
48
]
). In the following remark we look at this point for the Leray-
\alpha
model.

Remark 5.1
.

A possibly interesting generalisation of the results in this work is the following: Suppose that instead of the Helmholtz regularisation
v=u-\alpha^{2}\Delta u
we consider the regularisation

v=u+\alpha^{2\theta}(-\Delta)^{\theta}u,

for
\theta>0
positive. For such a regularisation
v(\cdot,t)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
implies that
u(\cdot,t)\in H^{2\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
. Note that a general case involving viscosity given by a fractional Laplacian was studied in
[
48
]
. Assume in addition that
u
and
v
are incompressible and satisfy the equation

\partial_{t}v+\nabla\cdot(u\otimes v)+\nabla p=0.

For the choice
\theta=1
we recover the Leray-
\alpha
model. One can then ask how the Onsager exponent changes as
\theta\rightarrow 0
. In the formal limit we recover the Euler equations. That means that one would expect the Onsager exponent of this model to converge to
\frac{1}{3}
as
\theta\rightarrow 0
(formally at least). By similar reasoning as in the proof of Theorem
2.4
we can establish an equation of local energy balance. One can make the following observations.

\bullet

If
\theta>\frac{1}{2}
the Onsager exponent is at most 0 (we are referring to the Hölder exponent here). This is because
v(\cdot,t)\in C^{0+}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
implies that
u(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,2\theta+}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
, here the notation
u(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,\beta+}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
means that
u(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,\beta^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
for some
\beta^{\prime}>\beta
. Therefore the fact that
v(\cdot,t)\in C^{0+}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
implies that the condition from Lemma
2.5
is satisfied and the energy is conserved by Theorem
2.7
.

\bullet

If
0<\theta<\frac{1}{2}
then we can deduce the Onsager exponent as follows. Suppose
v(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,\gamma}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
, then we have that
u(\cdot,t)\in C^{0,\gamma+2\theta}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
. Together the product
\lvert\delta u\rvert\lvert\delta v\rvert^{2}
must have a little bit more than Lipschitz decay as
\lvert\xi\rvert\rightarrow 0
, this leads to the equation

3\gamma+2\theta=1\implies\gamma=\frac{1}{3}-\frac{2}{3}\theta.

This means that if
\gamma>\frac{1}{3}-\frac{2}{3}\theta
, energy is conserved by Theorem
2.7
. Like in the case of the original Onsager exponent for the Euler equations, this inequality is strict. Observe that in the limit
\theta\rightarrow 0
we indeed recover the Onsager exponent
\frac{1}{3}
for the Euler equations. Hence we have justified that the Onsager exponent indeed converges formally to
\frac{1}{3}
as
\theta\rightarrow 0
.

Appendix A
The Modified Leray-
\alpha
model

The Modified Leray-
\alpha
model is given by

\partial_{t}v+\nabla\cdot(v\otimes u)+\nabla p=0,\quad v=u-\alpha^{2}\Delta u,\quad\nabla\cdot u=\nabla\cdot v=0.

The formally conserved quantity is again
\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}
.

Definition A.1
.

We say that
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and
p\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
is a weak solution of the Modified Leray-
\alpha
model if it satisfies the following equations for all
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\chi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\cdot\partial_{t}\psi dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u:\nabla\partial_{t}\psi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\otimes u:\nabla\psi dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla\psi dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes u:\nabla\partial_{k}\psi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot\psi dxdt=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\partial_{i}\chi dxdt=0.

The pressure is determined up to a constant, which we fix by

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p(x,t)dx=0.

As before, we now state a result stating that we can take a larger space of test functions.

Lemma A.2
.

The weak formulation of the Modified Leray-
\alpha
model still holds for test functions
\psi\in W^{1,1}_{0}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))\cap L^{1}((0,T);H^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))

Proof.

The proof proceeds in the same fashion as the proof of Lemma
2.3
. Again one relies on a limiting argument by using the approximation of Sobolev functions by smooth functions.
∎

Now we establish the equation of local energy balance.

Theorem A.3
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of the Modified Leray-
\alpha
model, such that
u\in L^{3}((0,T);W^{1,9/4}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. Then the equation of local energy balance holds

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2})-2\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+2\nabla\cdot(pu)

\displaystyle+D_{4}(u)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)+2\nabla\cdot\big(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u\big)+\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}(\lvert u\rvert^{2}\partial_{k}u))+D_{5}(u)=0.

This equation holds in the sense of distributions. Here the defect terms are given by

\displaystyle D_{4}(u)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert^{2}d\xi,

\displaystyle D_{5}(u)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{i}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\delta u_{j}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{i}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{j}(\xi;x,t)d\xi.

Note that the limits hold in the sense of distributions and are independent of the choice of mollifier.

Proof.

Mollifying the equation of the Modified Leray-
\alpha
model in space (with
\phi_{\epsilon}
) gives us that
\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. Therefore we can conclude that
u^{\epsilon},v^{\epsilon}\in W^{1,\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
.

By Lemma
A.2
we can take
u^{\epsilon}\chi
(for
\chi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R})
) as a test function in the weak formulation. If we subtract the mollified equation multiplied by
u\chi
from this equation, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon})dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u:\nabla\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla(u\chi):\partial_{t}\nabla u^{\epsilon}dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi u\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)-

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot(\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u)^{\epsilon})dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes u:\nabla\partial_{k}\chi dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}(u\chi)\cdot(\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}dxdt=0.

One can prove that
p\in L^{3/2}((0,T);L^{9/8}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and
\partial_{t}v\in L^{3/2}((0,T);W^{-2,9/5}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
, which implies that
\partial_{t}u\in L^{3/2}((0,T);L^{9/5}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. The time derivative terms work the same way as before, we can write that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon})dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u:\nabla\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla(u\chi):\partial_{t}\nabla u^{\epsilon}dxdt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[(u\cdot u^{\epsilon})\partial_{t}\chi+\alpha^{2}\nabla u:\nabla u^{\epsilon}\partial_{t}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\nabla u:\nabla\chi\otimes\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}+\nabla u:\partial_{t}\nabla\chi\otimes u^{\epsilon}-\nabla\chi\otimes u:\partial_{t}\nabla u^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[(u\cdot u^{\epsilon})\partial_{t}\chi+\alpha^{2}\nabla u:\nabla u^{\epsilon}\partial_{t}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\nabla u:\nabla\chi\otimes\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}+\nabla u:\partial_{t}\nabla\chi\otimes u^{\epsilon}+\partial_{t}\nabla\chi\otimes u:\nabla u^{\epsilon}+\nabla\chi\otimes\partial_{t}u:\nabla u^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt.

This can be written as

-\bigg\langle\partial_{t}(u\cdot u^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\nabla u:\nabla u^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg)+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg),\chi\bigg\rangle.

In the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
this converges to

-\bigg\langle\partial_{t}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2})+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg),\chi\bigg\rangle.

The pressure terms can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}dxdt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}[pu^{\epsilon}+up^{\epsilon}]\cdot\nabla\chi dxdt

\displaystyle=-\bigg\langle\nabla\cdot(pu^{\epsilon}+p^{\epsilon}u),\chi\bigg\rangle.

The most involved part are the advective (cubic in
u
) terms. We introduce the defect term given by

D_{4,\epsilon}(u)(x,t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert^{2}d\xi,

As before we can write that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi u\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[-\chi D_{4,\epsilon}(u)(x,t)-\frac{1}{2}(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi+\frac{1}{2}u(\lvert u\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla\chi\otimes u^{\epsilon}dxdt,

and observe that as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
this converges to

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla\chi\otimes udxdt.

The other advective terms can be written as (omitting the factor
\alpha^{2}
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\chi\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}(u_{j}\chi)\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{i}\chi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{i}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{i}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{k}\chi dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\partial_{k}\chi\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\chi\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{j}\chi\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}.

Now we can check that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}\chi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{i}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{k}\chi dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\partial_{k}\chi\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}\chi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{i}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{k}\chi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}\chi(\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{i}\chi(\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\xrightarrow{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}\lvert u\rvert^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}\chi dxdt

\displaystyle=-\bigg\langle\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}(\lvert u\rvert^{2}\partial_{k}u)),\chi\bigg\rangle.

We have more divergence terms, which are given by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{i}\chi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{i}\chi dxdt

\displaystyle=-\bigg\langle\nabla\cdot\big(u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u+u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{k}u\big),\chi\bigg\rangle.

We compute a new defect term which is given by

\displaystyle D_{5,\epsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{i}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\delta u_{j}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{i}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{j}(\xi;x,t)d\xi=-\partial_{i}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})^{\epsilon}+\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{i}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})^{\epsilon}+u_{j}\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})^{\epsilon}-\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}-u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}.

Finally, we are left with

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\chi\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{j}\chi\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi\bigg[-D_{5,\epsilon}(u)-\partial_{i}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})^{\epsilon}+\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{i}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=-\bigg\langle D_{5,\epsilon}(u)-\nabla\cdot(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u)^{\epsilon}+\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}),\chi\bigg\rangle.

Now we put everything together and write down the equation of local energy balance which is given by (in the limit as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
)

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\partial_{t}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2})-2\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+2\nabla\cdot(pu)+D_{4}(u)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)

\displaystyle+2\nabla\cdot\big(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u\big)+\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}(\lvert u\rvert^{2}\partial_{k}u))+D_{5}(u),\chi\bigg\rangle=0.

We can argue as before that the limiting distribution
D_{4}(u)+D_{5}(u)
is independent of the choice of mollifier. Like in the case for the Euler-
\alpha
equations, we will show that
D_{4}(u)=0
for all weak solutions of the modified Leray-
\alpha
model. Therefore
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}D_{5,\epsilon}
makes sense as a distribution.
∎

Now we state the usual sufficient conditions for the defect terms to be zero.

Proposition A.4
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of the inviscid modified Leray-
\alpha
model such that
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T);W^{1,9/4}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. Moreover, we assume that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{3}dx

\displaystyle\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{4}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert\lvert\delta\nabla u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{5}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

where
C\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
\sigma_{k}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\sigma_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\rightarrow 0
as
\lvert\xi\rvert\rightarrow 0
, for
k=4,5
. Then
D_{4}(u)=D_{5}(u)=0
. In particular, the weak solution conserves the energy.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in the same fashion as the proof of Proposition
2.5
.
∎

Now we formulate sufficient conditions for the defect term to be zero.

Proposition A.5
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of the modified Leray-
\alpha
model, such that
u\in L^{3}((0,T);B^{s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
s>1
, then
D_{4}(u)=D_{5}(u)=0
, which implies that energy is conserved.

Proof.

We need to prove that the conditions of Proposition
A.4
are satisfied. We observe that the first inequality in Proposition
A.4
is already satisfied for any weak solution of the model. The second inequality can be observed to hold because

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert\lvert\delta\nabla u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}dx\leq\lvert\xi\rvert^{3s-2}\lVert u\rVert_{B^{s}_{3,\infty}}^{3}.

Note that in the above we have used inequality (
2.7
).
∎

Now we can conclude that energy is indeed conserved.

Theorem A.6
.

Let
u\in L^{3}((0,T);B^{s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
s>1
be a weak solution of the modified Leray-
\alpha
model, then the energy is conserved, which means that

\lVert u(\cdot,t_{1})\rVert_{H^{1}}=\lVert u(\cdot,t_{2})\rVert_{H^{1}},

for almost all times
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,T)
.

Proof.

The proof goes the same way as the proof of Theorem
2.7
.
∎

Appendix B
The Clark-
\alpha
model

The equations for the Clark-
\alpha
model are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\nabla\cdot(u\otimes v)

\displaystyle+\nabla\cdot(v\otimes u)-\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)-\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(\nabla u\cdot\nabla u^{T})+\nabla p=0,

\displaystyle v=u-\alpha^{2}\Delta u,\quad\nabla\cdot u=\nabla\cdot v=0.

The conserved quantity is the norm
\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}
.

Definition B.1
.

We call
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and
p\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
a weak solution of the Clark-
\alpha
model if for all
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\chi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R})
satisfies the following equations

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\cdot\partial_{t}\psi dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u:\nabla\partial_{t}\psi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla\psi dxdt

\displaystyle+2\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla\psi dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla\partial_{k}\psi dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes u:\nabla\partial_{k}\psi dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u\cdot(\nabla u)^{T}:\nabla\psi dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot\psi dxdt=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\partial_{i}\chi dxdt=0.

The pressure is determined by

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p(x,t)dx=0.

Now we extend the space of test functions.

Lemma B.2
.

The weak formulation of the Clark-
\alpha
model still holds for test functions

\psi\in W^{1,1}_{0}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))\cap L^{1}((0,T);H^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
.

Proof.

The proof is the same as for the other models considered in this paper.
∎

Now we establish the equation of local energy balance.

Theorem B.3
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of the Clark-
\alpha
model such that
u\in L^{3}((0,T);\linebreak W^{1,9/4}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. Then the following equation of local energy balance holds

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2})-2\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)

\displaystyle+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})+3\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}(\partial_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})+\bigg[D_{6}(u)+\alpha^{2}D_{7}(u)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{8}(u)\bigg]+2\nabla\cdot(pu)=0,

which holds in the sense of distributions. The defect terms are given by

\displaystyle\bigg[D_{6}(u)+\alpha^{2}D_{7}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{8}(u)\bigg](x,t)\coloneqq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{i}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\delta u_{j}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{i}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{j}(\xi;x,t)d\xi

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{i}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\delta u_{i}(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta\partial_{k}u_{j}(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}d\xi\bigg],

where the limit holds in the sense of distributions and is independent of the choice of mollifier. Note that
\bigg[D_{6}(u)+\alpha^{2}D_{7}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{8}(u)\bigg]
should be regarded as a single distribution, but we will write it as a sum of the defect terms
D_{6},D_{7}
and
D_{8}
(which formally correspond to the limits of the different integrals) for later notational convenience.

Proof.

We observe that
u^{\epsilon},v^{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. Moreover, we get that
\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon},\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
by mollifying the weak formulation. Then by applying Lemma
B.2
and taking the general test function
u^{\epsilon}\chi
, we get that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\cdot\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u:\nabla\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle+2\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes u:\nabla\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u\cdot(\nabla u)^{T}:\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt=0.

Then we mollify the equation of the Clark-
\alpha
model, multiply it by
u\chi
and subtract it from the previous expression, which gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\cdot\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u:\nabla\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla(u\chi):\nabla\partial_{t}u^{\epsilon}dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle+2\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-2\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u^{\epsilon}\chi\cdot(\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}(u\chi)\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes\partial_{k}u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes u:\nabla\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}(u\chi)\cdot(\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u\cdot(\nabla u)^{T}:\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot(\nabla\cdot(\nabla u\cdot(\nabla u)^{T})^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}dxdt=0.

First we look at the time derivative parts, for which we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon})dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla u:\nabla\partial_{t}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla(u\chi):\partial_{t}\nabla u^{\epsilon}dxdt=-\bigg\langle\partial_{t}(u\cdot u^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\nabla u:\nabla u^{\epsilon})-\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg)

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg),\chi\bigg\rangle.

We note that under the assumption that
u\in L^{3}((0,T);W^{1,9/4}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
, we can prove that
p\in L^{3/2}((0,T);L^{9/8}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and
\partial_{t}v\in L^{3/2}((0,T);W^{-1,9/8}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and hence
\partial_{t}u\in L^{3/2}((0,T);W^{1,9/8}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))\subset L^{3}((0,T);L^{9/5}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. This means that
\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. In the limit as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
this is the same as

-\bigg\langle\partial_{t}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2})+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg),\chi\bigg\rangle.

The first part of the advective terms can be dealt with by introducing a defect term

D_{6,\epsilon}(u)(x,t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert^{2}d\xi,

As before we can write that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi u\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[-\chi D_{6,\epsilon}(u)(x,t)-\frac{1}{2}(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi+\frac{1}{2}u(\lvert u\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(u\otimes u):\nabla\chi\otimes u^{\epsilon}dxdt.

Next we look at the terms coming from the advective term
\nabla\cdot(v\otimes u)
, we have that

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi u^{\epsilon}\cdot(\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u\otimes u:\nabla\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}\chi)\cdot(\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})dxdt

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi\bigg[-D_{7,\epsilon}(u)-\partial_{i}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})^{\epsilon}+\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{i}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla\chi\otimes u^{\epsilon}+\partial_{k}u\otimes u:\bigg(\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\partial_{k}\chi)+\nabla\chi\otimes\partial_{k}u^{\epsilon}\bigg)

\displaystyle-\partial_{k}\chi u^{\epsilon}\cdot(\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})\bigg]dxdt=-\alpha^{2}\bigg\langle D_{7,\epsilon}(u)-\nabla\cdot(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u)^{\epsilon}+\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}),\chi\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\partial_{k}u\otimes\partial_{k}u:\nabla\chi\otimes u^{\epsilon}+\partial_{k}u\otimes u:\bigg(\nabla(u^{\epsilon}\partial_{k}\chi)+\nabla\chi\otimes\partial_{k}u^{\epsilon}\bigg)

\displaystyle-\partial_{k}\chi u^{\epsilon}\cdot(\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u\otimes u)^{\epsilon})\bigg]dxdt.

In the above we have introduced the following defect term

D_{7,\epsilon}(u)(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{i}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\delta u_{j}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{i}(\xi;x,t)\delta\partial_{k}u_{j}(\xi;x,t)d\xi.

Finally, we are left with the remaining terms

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\chi\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi)dxdt-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}(u_{j}\chi)\partial_{i}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\chi)dxdt+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{j}\chi\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}dxdt

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{k}\chi)+u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{i}\chi+\partial_{k}\partial_{i}\chi u_{j}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}+\partial_{k}\chi\partial_{i}u_{j}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\chi\bigg[-\frac{1}{2}D_{8,\epsilon}(u)-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2}u_{i}\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt.

In the above we have introduced the defect term given by

\displaystyle D_{8,\epsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\partial_{i}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\delta u_{i}(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta\partial_{k}u_{j}(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}d\xi=-\partial_{i}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+u_{i}\partial_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}+2\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}-2u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}^{\epsilon}.

One can write the pressure terms as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot(u^{\epsilon}\chi)dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u\chi\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}dxdt=-\bigg\langle\nabla\cdot(pu^{\epsilon}+p^{\epsilon}u),\chi\bigg\rangle.

Finally, this allows us to write down the local equation of energy

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\partial_{t}(u\cdot u^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\nabla u:\nabla u^{\epsilon})-\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg)+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}^{\epsilon}\bigg)

\displaystyle+D_{6,\epsilon}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\big((\lvert u\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u-(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon}\big)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}D_{7,\epsilon}(u)-\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u)^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\nabla\cdot(\partial_{k}u(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}(u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}(\partial_{i}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}(\partial_{i}u^{\epsilon}_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}(u_{j}(\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}(\partial_{i}u_{j}(\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})^{\epsilon})+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{8,\epsilon}(u)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}(u_{i}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}(u_{i}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j})^{\epsilon})+\nabla\cdot(pu^{\epsilon}+p^{\epsilon}u),\chi\bigg\rangle=0.

As before, we know that
D_{6,\epsilon}(u),D_{7,\epsilon}(u)
and
D_{8,\epsilon}(u)
all make sense for fixed
\epsilon>0
as an element in
L^{1}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
due to the assumption that
u\in L^{3}((0,T);W^{1,9/4}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. We can write the above equation as an equation holding in the sense of distributions for
[D_{6,\epsilon}(u)+\alpha^{2}D_{7,\epsilon}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{8,\epsilon}(u)]
. Now we observe that all the other terms in the equation converge in the sense of distributions as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and therefore by arguing as before
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\big[D_{6,\epsilon}(u)+\alpha^{2}D_{7,\epsilon}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{8,\epsilon}(u)\big]
makes sense as a distribution. We will denote this limit, abusing notation, by
[D_{6}(u)+\alpha^{2}D_{7}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{8}(u)]
, even though we have not shown at this stage that each individual defect term is converging. Later, we will show that under more restricted condition that each defect term converges to 0. In particular, from above we arrive at the following equation of local energy balance

\displaystyle\bigg\langle\partial_{t}(\lvert u\rvert^{2})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}(\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2})-2\alpha^{2}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\bigg(u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\bigg(\partial_{t}u_{j}\partial_{i}u_{j}\bigg)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert u\rvert^{2}u)

\displaystyle+2\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i})+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{k}(u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})+3\alpha^{2}\partial_{k}(\partial_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{i}u_{j})

\displaystyle+\bigg[D_{6}(u)+\alpha^{2}D_{7}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}D_{8}(u)\bigg]+2\nabla\cdot(pu),\chi\bigg\rangle=0.

∎

We now state the usual sufficient condition for the defect terms to be zero.

Proposition B.4
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of the inviscid Clark-
\alpha
model such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{3}dx\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{6}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert\lvert\delta\nabla u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}dx\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{7}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

where we assume that
C\in L^{1}(0,T)
, while
\sigma_{j}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\sigma_{j}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\rightarrow 0
as
\lvert\xi\rvert\rightarrow 0
, for
j=6,7
. Then
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}D_{k,\epsilon}(u)=D_{k}(u)=0
, for
k=6,7,8
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in the same fashion as the proof of Proposition
2.5
.
∎

We now specify a sufficient regularity assumption for the sufficient conditions in Proposition
B.4
to be satisfied.

Proposition B.5
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of the inviscid Clark-
\alpha
model with
u\in L^{3}((0,T);\linebreak B^{s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
s>1
, then
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}D_{k,\epsilon}(u)=D_{k}(u)=0
for
k=6,7,8
. This implies that the weak solution conserves energy.

Proof.

To prove the first inequality in Proposition
B.4
we observe that the Besov embedding theorem gives us that
H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\subset B^{1/2}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
and hence we get that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{3}dx\leq\lvert\xi\rvert^{3/2}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{3}.

One can observe that the second inequality in Proposition
B.4
holds because

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert\lvert\delta\nabla u(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}dx\leq\lvert\xi\rvert^{3s-2}\lVert u\rVert_{B^{s}_{3,\infty}}^{3}.

This follows from using property (
2.7
) for functions in Besov spaces.
Therefore the assumptions of Proposition
B.4
are satisfied and we conclude that
D_{6}(u)=D_{7}(u)=D_{8}(u)=0
.
∎

This proposition allows us to prove conservation of energy.

Theorem B.6
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of the inviscid Clark-
\alpha
model with
u\in L^{3}((0,T);B^{s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
s>1
, then the solution conserves energy. In particular, it holds that

\lVert u(\cdot,t_{1})\rVert_{H^{1}}=\lVert u(\cdot,t_{2})\rVert_{H^{1}},

for almost every
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,T)
.

Appendix C
The Leray-
\alpha
MHD model

The equations for the Leray-
\alpha
MHD model are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+(u\cdot\nabla)v+\nabla p+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\lvert B\rvert^{2}=(B\cdot\nabla)B,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}B+(u\cdot\nabla)B-(B\cdot\nabla)v=0,

\displaystyle v=u-\alpha^{2}\Delta u,\quad\nabla\cdot u=\nabla\cdot v=\nabla\cdot B=0.

Definition C.1
.

A weak solution of the Leray-
\alpha
MHD model is a triplet
v\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})),B\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and
p\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
if for all
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\chi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R})
the following equations hold

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}v\cdot\partial_{t}\psi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}v_{j}u_{i}\partial_{i}\psi_{j}dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p\nabla\cdot\psi dxdt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert B\rvert^{2}\nabla\cdot\psi dxdt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}(B\otimes B):\nabla\psi dxdt=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}B\cdot\partial_{t}\psi dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}B_{j}u_{i}\partial_{i}\psi_{j}dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}v_{j}B_{i}\partial_{i}\psi_{j}dxdt=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}u_{i}\partial_{i}\chi dxdt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}B_{i}\partial_{i}\chi dxdt=0.

We fix the constant in the definition of the pressure by demanding that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}p(x,t)dx=0.

Once again, we extend the space of test functions.

Lemma C.2
.

The weak formulation of the Leray-
\alpha
MHD model still holds for
\psi\in W^{1,1}_{0}((0,T);\linebreak L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))\cap L^{1}((0,T);H^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
.

Proof.

The proof works the same way as for the other models.
∎

Now we establish the equation of local energy balance.

Theorem C.3
.

Let
(v,B)
be a weak solution of the Leray-
\alpha
MHD model such that
v,B\in L^{3}((0,T);L^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. Then the following equation of local energy balance holds in the sense of distributions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\lvert v\rvert^{2}+\lvert B\rvert^{2})+2\nabla\cdot(pv)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)+2\nabla\cdot(\lvert B\rvert^{2}v)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert B\rvert^{2}u)-2\nabla\cdot((v\cdot B)B)

\displaystyle+\big[D_{9}(u,v)+D_{10}(u,B)-D_{11}(v,B)\big]=0.

Here the sum of the three defect terms is given by the limit

\displaystyle\big[D_{9}(u,v)+D_{10}(u,B)-D_{11}(v,B)\big](x,t)\coloneqq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta v(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta B(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}d\xi-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta B(\xi;x,t)(\delta B(\xi;x,t)\cdot\delta v(\xi;x,t))d\xi\bigg].

Note that the limit holds in the sense of distributions and is independent of the choice of mollifier. Like in the case of the Clark-
\alpha
model,
\big[D_{9}(u,v)+D_{10}(u,B)-D_{11}(v,B)\big]
should be regarded as a single distribution.

Proof.

Mollifying the equations of the Leray-
\alpha
MHD model in space (with
\phi_{\epsilon}
) gives that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}+\nabla\cdot(u\otimes v)^{\epsilon}+\nabla p^{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla(\lvert B\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}-\nabla\cdot(B\otimes B)^{\epsilon}=0,

(C.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}B^{\epsilon}+\nabla\cdot(u\otimes B)^{\epsilon}-\nabla\cdot(B\otimes v)^{\epsilon}=0.

(C.2)

It follows that
v^{\epsilon},B^{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
, as well as that
\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon},\partial_{t}B^{\epsilon}\in L^{\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. As a result we can conclude that
v^{\epsilon},B^{\epsilon}\in W^{1,\infty}((0,T);C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. This allows us to apply Lemma
C.2
and use
v^{\epsilon}\chi
and
B^{\epsilon}\chi
as test functions in the weak formulations, where
\chi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T);\mathbb{R})
. Subtracting the mollified equations multiplied by
v\chi
and
B\chi
we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[v\cdot\partial_{t}(v^{\epsilon}\chi)-v\chi\cdot\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}+B\cdot\partial_{t}(B^{\epsilon}\chi)-B\chi\cdot\partial_{t}B^{\epsilon}+u\otimes v:\nabla(v^{\epsilon}\chi)-v\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes v)^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle-\chi v\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}+p\partial_{i}(v^{\epsilon}_{i}\chi)+\frac{1}{2}\lvert B\rvert^{2}\nabla\cdot(v^{\epsilon}\chi)-\frac{1}{2}v\chi\nabla(\lvert B\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}-B\otimes B:\nabla(v^{\epsilon}\chi)+v\cdot(\nabla\cdot(B\otimes B)^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle+u\otimes B:\nabla(\chi B^{\epsilon})-\chi B\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes B)^{\epsilon})-B\otimes v:\nabla(\chi B^{\epsilon})+B\chi\cdot(\nabla\cdot(B\otimes v)^{\epsilon})\bigg]dxdt=0.

The time derivative terms then become

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[v\cdot\partial_{t}(v^{\epsilon}\chi)-v\chi\cdot\partial_{t}v^{\epsilon}+B\cdot\partial_{t}(B^{\epsilon}\chi)-B\chi\cdot\partial_{t}B^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\big[v\cdot v^{\epsilon}+B\cdot B^{\epsilon}\bigg]\partial_{t}\chi dxdt=-\bigg\langle\partial_{t}\big(v\cdot v^{\epsilon}+B\cdot B^{\epsilon}\big),\chi\bigg\rangle.

For the pressure terms we get that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[-\chi v\cdot\nabla p^{\epsilon}+p\partial_{i}(v^{\epsilon}_{i}\chi)\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[p^{\epsilon}v_{i}\partial_{i}\chi+pv^{\epsilon}_{i}\partial_{i}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=-\langle\nabla\cdot(p^{\epsilon}v+pv^{\epsilon}),\chi\rangle.

Note that
p\in L^{3/2}((0,T);L^{3/2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and therefore
p^{\epsilon}v+pv^{\epsilon}\xrightarrow{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}2pv
in
L^{1}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. Several of the advective terms can be written down straightforwardly (analogously to the Leray-
\alpha
model)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[u\otimes v:\nabla(v^{\epsilon}\chi)-v\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes v)^{\epsilon})\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\chi D_{9,\epsilon}(u,v)-\frac{1}{2}\bigg((v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}u_{i}-(u_{i}v_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg)\partial_{i}\chi-v_{j}u_{i}v_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=-\bigg\langle D_{9,\epsilon}(u,v)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot((\lvert v\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u-(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon})+\nabla\cdot((v\cdot v^{\epsilon})u),\chi\bigg\rangle,

where we have introduced the defect term

D_{9,\epsilon}(u,v)(x,t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta v(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}d\xi,

Now we can write that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\lvert B\rvert^{2}\nabla\cdot(v^{\epsilon}\chi)-v\chi\nabla(\lvert B\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\lvert B\rvert^{2}v^{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi+v(\lvert B\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=-\bigg\langle\nabla\cdot\big(\lvert B\rvert^{2}v^{\epsilon}+(\lvert B\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}v\big),\chi\bigg\rangle=0.

For the other advective terms we find that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[u\otimes B:\nabla(\chi B^{\epsilon})-\chi B\cdot(\nabla\cdot(u\otimes B)^{\epsilon})\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\chi D_{10,\epsilon}(u,B)-\frac{1}{2}\bigg((B_{j}B_{j})^{\epsilon}u_{i}-(u_{i}B_{j}B_{j})^{\epsilon}\bigg)\partial_{i}\chi-B_{j}u_{i}B_{j}^{\epsilon}\partial_{i}\chi\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=-\bigg\langle D_{10,\epsilon}(u,B)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot((\lvert B\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u-(\lvert B\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon})+\nabla\cdot((B\cdot B^{\epsilon})u),\chi\bigg\rangle,

where we have introduced the defect term

D_{10,\epsilon}(u,B)(x,t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta u(\xi;x,t)\lvert\delta B(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}d\xi,

Finally, we are left with

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[v\cdot(\nabla\cdot(B\otimes B)^{\epsilon})-B\otimes B:\nabla(v^{\epsilon}\chi)-B\otimes v:\nabla(\chi B^{\epsilon})+B\chi\cdot(\nabla\cdot(B\otimes v)^{\epsilon})\bigg]dxdt.

We introduce another defect term, which can be calculated to be

\displaystyle D_{11,\epsilon}(v,B)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)\cdot\delta B(\xi;x,t)(\delta B(\xi;x,t)\cdot\delta v(\xi;x,t))d\xi

\displaystyle=-\partial_{i}(B_{i}B_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}+B_{i}\partial_{i}(B_{j}v_{j})^{\epsilon}+B_{j}\partial_{i}(B_{i}v_{j})^{\epsilon}+v_{j}\partial_{i}(B_{i}B_{j})^{\epsilon}-B_{i}v_{j}\partial_{i}B_{j}^{\epsilon}-B_{i}B_{j}\partial_{i}v_{j}^{\epsilon}.

Therefore we can write the different terms as follows

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[v\cdot(\nabla\cdot(B\otimes B)^{\epsilon})-B\otimes B:\nabla(v^{\epsilon}\chi)-B\otimes v:\nabla(\chi B^{\epsilon})+B\chi\cdot(\nabla\cdot(B\otimes v)^{\epsilon})\bigg]dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[-B\otimes B:\nabla\chi\otimes v^{\epsilon}-B\otimes v:\nabla\chi\otimes B^{\epsilon}+\chi D_{11,\epsilon}(v,B)+\chi\nabla\cdot((B\cdot v)B)^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle-\chi B\cdot\nabla(B\cdot v)^{\epsilon}\bigg]dxdt=\bigg\langle\nabla\cdot((v^{\epsilon}\cdot B)B)+\nabla\cdot((B^{\epsilon}\cdot v)B)+D_{11,\epsilon}(v,B)+\nabla\cdot((B\cdot v)B)^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot((B\cdot v)^{\epsilon}B),\chi\bigg\rangle.

From this we can conclude that we have the following equation of local energy balance

\displaystyle\bigg\langle

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\big(v\cdot v^{\epsilon}+B\cdot B^{\epsilon}\big)-\nabla\cdot(p^{\epsilon}v+pv^{\epsilon})-D_{9,\epsilon}(u,v)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot((\lvert v\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u-(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot((v\cdot v^{\epsilon})u)-\nabla\cdot\big(\lvert B\rvert^{2}v^{\epsilon}+(\lvert B\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}v\big)-D_{10,\epsilon}(u,B)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot((\lvert B\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u-(\lvert B\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot((B\cdot B^{\epsilon})u)+\nabla\cdot((v^{\epsilon}\cdot B)B)+\nabla\cdot((B^{\epsilon}\cdot v)B)+D_{11,\epsilon}(v,B)+\nabla\cdot((B\cdot v)B)^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot((B\cdot v)^{\epsilon}B),\chi\bigg\rangle=0.

Now we observe that for fixed
\epsilon>0
, the defect terms
D_{9,\epsilon},D_{10,\epsilon}
and
D_{11,\epsilon}
are all individually well-defined as elements in
L^{1}((0,T);L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
. Moreover, we can write the following equation for the sum of the defect terms

\displaystyle-D_{9,\epsilon}(u,v)-D_{10,\epsilon}(u,B)+D_{11,\epsilon}(v,B)=\partial_{t}\big(v\cdot v^{\epsilon}+B\cdot B^{\epsilon}\big)+\nabla\cdot(p^{\epsilon}v+pv^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot((\lvert v\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u-(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon})+\nabla\cdot((v\cdot v^{\epsilon})u)+\nabla\cdot\big(\lvert B\rvert^{2}v^{\epsilon}+(\lvert B\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}v\big)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot((\lvert B\rvert^{2})^{\epsilon}u

\displaystyle-(\lvert B\rvert^{2}u)^{\epsilon})+\nabla\cdot((B\cdot B^{\epsilon})u)-\nabla\cdot((v^{\epsilon}\cdot B)B)-\nabla\cdot((B^{\epsilon}\cdot v)B)-\nabla\cdot((B\cdot v)B)^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+B\cdot\nabla(B\cdot v)^{\epsilon},

which holds in the sense of distributions. Now we observe that the right-hand converges in the sense of distributions to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\lvert v\rvert^{2}+\lvert B\rvert^{2})+2\nabla\cdot(pv)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert v\rvert^{2}u)+2\nabla\cdot(\lvert B\rvert^{2}v)+\nabla\cdot(\lvert B\rvert^{2}u)-2\nabla\cdot((v\cdot B)B).

The limit exists as a distribution and is independent of the choice of mollifier, therefore
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\bigg[D_{9,\epsilon}(u,v)+D_{10,\epsilon}(u,B)-D_{11,\epsilon}(v,B)\bigg]
exists as a distribution and the equation of local energy balance holds in the sense of distributions.
∎

Now we state sufficient conditions for the defect term to be zero.

Proposition C.4
.

Assume that
(v,B)\in L^{3}((0,T);L^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
is a weak solution of the inviscid and irresistive Leray-
\alpha
MHD model. Morever, we assume that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert\lvert\delta v(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}dx\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{9}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta u(\xi;x,t)\rvert\lvert\delta B(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}dx\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{10}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta v(\xi;x,t)\rvert\lvert\delta B(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}dx\leq C(t)\lvert\xi\rvert\sigma_{11}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

where
C\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
\sigma_{j}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\sigma_{j}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\rightarrow 0
as
\lvert\xi\rvert\rightarrow 0
, for
j=9,10,11
. Then
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}D_{k,\epsilon}(u,v,B)=D_{k}(u,v,B)=0
for
k=9,10,11
. This then implies that the weak solution conserves energy.

Proof.

The proof is completely analogous to the proof of Proposition
2.5
.
∎

We now prove a proposition which states that under given regularity assumptions these conditions are met.

Proposition C.5
.

Let
(v,B)
be a weak solution of the inviscid and irresistive Leray-
\alpha
MHD model such that
v\in L^{3}((0,T);B^{s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and
B\in L^{3}((0,T);B^{r}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
s,r>0
and
s+2r>1
. Then
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}D_{k,\epsilon}(u,v,B)=D_{k}(u,v,B)=0
for
k=9,10,11
(which implies conservation of energy).

Proof.

The proof for
D_{9}
and
D_{10}
is analogous to the case for the Leray-
\alpha
model, because
v(\cdot,t)\in B^{s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
implies that
u(\cdot,t)\in B^{2+s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
. For
D_{11}(u,v)
we observe that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\lvert\delta v(\xi;x,t)\rvert\lvert\delta B(\xi;x,t)\rvert^{2}dx\leq\lvert\xi\rvert^{s+2r}\lVert v\rVert_{B^{s}_{3,\infty}}\lVert B\rVert_{B^{r}_{3,\infty}}^{2}.

To obtain this bound we have used inequality (
2.7
).
Then we can take
\sigma_{11}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\coloneqq\lvert\xi\rvert^{s+2r-1}
, which satisfies the conditions of Proposition
C.4
. Therefore it follows from the proposition that
D_{11}(v,B)=0
.
∎

Finally, we can prove that the weak solution conserves energy.

Theorem C.6
.

Let
(v,B)
be a weak solution of the inviscid and irresistive Leray-
\alpha
MHD model, then if
v\in L^{3}((0,T);B^{s}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
and
B\in L^{3}((0,T);B^{r}_{3,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
with
s,r>0
and
s+2r>1
the weak solution conserves energy. So for almost all
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,T)
it holds that

\lVert v(t_{1},\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert B(t_{1},\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\lVert v(t_{2},\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert B(t_{2},\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Proof.

The proof works the same way as the proof of Theorem
2.7
.
∎
