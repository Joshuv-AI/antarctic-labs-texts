Well-Ordered Flag Spaces as Functors of Points
Nathaniel Gallup

Abstract
. Using Grothendieck’s “functor of points” approach to algebraic geometry, we define a new infinite-dimensional algebro-geometric flag space as a k k -functor (for k k a ring) which maps a k k -algebra R R to the set of certain well-ordered chains of submodules of an infinite rank free R R -module. This generalizes the well known construction of a k k -functor that is represented by the classical (i.e. finite-dimensional) full flag scheme. We prove that as in the finite-dimensional case, there is an action of a general linear group on our flag space, that the stabilizer of the standard flag is the subgroup B B of upper triangular matrices, and that the Bruhat decomposition holds, meaning that our space is covered by the disjoint Schubert cells sh ⁡ ( B ​ σ ​ B ) / B \operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)/B indexed by permutations σ \sigma of an infinite set. Finally, in the case of flags indexed by the ordinal ω + 1 \omega+1 , we define an analog of the Bruhat order on this infinite permutation group and prove that when k k is a domain, Ehresmann’s closure relations still hold, i.e. that the closure sh ⁡ ( B ​ σ ​ B ) / B ¯ \overline{\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)/B} is covered by the Schubert cells indexed by permutations smaller than σ \sigma in the infinite Bruhat order.

Our definition of an infinite-dimensional flag space is of course by no means the first. In
[
DP04
]
(see also
[
IP17
]
), Ivan Dimitrov and Ivan Penkov defined flag spaces very similar to ours, except that their

are indexed by countable totally ordered sets in which every element has an immediate successor or an immediate predecessor (this, of course, is satisfied by any well-ordered set), and are compatible with a given fixed flag. These spaces are filtered colimits of flag varieties with closed immersions as the inclusions, i.e. they are
ind-varieties

[
FP15
]
Penkov and Lucas Fresse proved a Bruhat decomposition for these generalized flag spaces as well as many geometric properties of the Schubert ind-varieties. The approach in this research program is certainly topically different from ours (they do not use functors of points), but their definitions actually do give a fundamentally different object from the one that we study. If we restrict to the case of flags indexed by
\omega+1
, their generalized flag space consists of maximal ascending chains of subspaces which are
eventually equal
to a given fixed flag. The group which acts transitively in this case is a filtered colimit of the groups

\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}(k)

\mathbb{N}

\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})

[

]
, about discrete infinite-dimensional vector bundles (which is the topic of this paper), his results there are about Tate vector bundles (i.e. those which have locally linearly compact fibers).

Background

In this section we fix notation and explain some background needed for the rest of the paper. It is quite long and the reader is encouraged to skip to Section
3
and refer back when needed. This material will all be well-known to experts and we give proofs only when we could not find an appropriate reference.

2.1.
Categories

Categories will be denoted by bold letters, such as
C
, and functors will usually be denoted by calligraphic letters, such as
\mathcal{F}
. If
C
is a locally small category containing objects
A,B
, we denote by
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(A,B)
the set of morphisms in
C
from
A
to
B
. If
D
is another category we denote the category of functors from
C
to
D
by
[\textbf{C},\textbf{D}]
. Morphisms in this category are natural transformations. In particular, for any category
C
we have the identity functor
\text{id}_{\textbf{C}}:\textbf{C}\to\textbf{C}
. If
\mathcal{F}:\textbf{C}\rightleftarrows\textbf{D}:\mathcal{G}
are functors such that
\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{F}=\text{id}_{\textbf{C}}
and
\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{G}=\text{id}_{\textbf{D}}
, then we say that
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are
inverses
and that
C
and
D
are
isomorphic categories
, although this situation is not very common.

The
opposite category

\textbf{C}^{\text{op}}
is obtained from
C
by keeping the objects the same and reversing all the arrows, so that every morphism
f:A\to B
in
C
corresponds to a unique morphism
f^{\text{op}}:B\to A
in
\textbf{C}^{\text{op}}
. In this way, a contravariant functor from
\textbf{C}\to\textbf{D}
is exactly the same as a covariant functor
\textbf{C}^{\text{op}}\to\textbf{D}
.

2.2.
Natural Transformations

If
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}:\textbf{C}\to\textbf{D}
are functors, we denote the (possibly proper) class of natural transformations from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
by
\text{Nat}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
. We denote the
identity natural transformation
from a functor
\mathcal{F}
to itself by
1_{\mathcal{F}}
.

There are two ways to compose a natural transformation and a functor to get another natural transformation. Suppose that
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}:\textbf{B}\to\textbf{C}
and
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}:\textbf{C}\to\textbf{D}
are functors and
\eta:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
and
\epsilon:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
are natural transformations. Then we define the natural transformation
\mathcal{G}\eta:\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{F}
by specifying for each object
x
in
B
the morphism
\mathcal{G}(\eta_{x})
. Similarly we define the natural transformation
\epsilon\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{H}\circ\mathcal{E}
by specifying for each object
x
in
B
the morphism
\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}(x)}
.

Lemma 1
.

If
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}:\textbf{B}\to\textbf{C}
and
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}:\textbf{C}\to\textbf{D}
are functors and
\eta:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}
and
\epsilon:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
are natural isomorphisms, then
\mathcal{G}\eta
and
\epsilon\mathcal{G}
are also natural isomorphisms.

Proof.

Recall that a natural transformation is an isomorphism if and only if all of its components are isomorphisms. Then the first result follows because functors map isomorphisms to isomorphisms and the second is trivial.
∎

2.3.
Fully Faithful Functors

A functor is called
full
if it is surjective on hom-sets,
faithful
if it is injective on hom-sets, and
fully faithful
if it is both full and faithful.

Lemma 2
.

If
\mathcal{F}:\textbf{C}\to\textbf{D}
is fully faithful then a morphism
f:x\to y
in
C
is an isomorphism if and only if
\mathcal{F}(f):\mathcal{F}(x)\to\mathcal{F}(y)
is an isomorphism in
D
. In particular we have that
x\cong y
if and only if
\mathcal{F}(x)\cong\mathcal{F}(y)
.

Lemma 3
.

If
\mathcal{F}:\textbf{C}\to\textbf{D}
is fully faithful then a square in
C
commutes if and only if its image in
D
commutes as well.

2.4.

If
\mathcal{F}:\textbf{C}\to\textbf{D}
is a functor and
B

of

\mathcal{F}_{*}:[\textbf{B},\textbf{C}]\to[\textbf{B},\textbf{D}]

If

2.5.
Products of Categories

If
C
and
D
are categories, one can form their
product

\textbf{C}\times\textbf{D}
. The objects in this category are ordered pairs
(A,B)
where
A
is an object in
C
and
B
is an object in
D
, and a morphism from
(A,B)
to
(X,Y)
consists of a pair of morphisms
(f,g)
where
f:A\to X
is a morphism in
C
and
g:B\to Y
is a morphism in
D
. One can take the product of two functors
\mathcal{F}:\textbf{A}\to\textbf{C}
and
\mathcal{G}:\textbf{B}\to\textbf{D}
to obtain a functor
\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{G}:\textbf{A}\times\textbf{B}\to\textbf{C}\times\textbf{D}
which is defined in the obvious way, and furthermore if
\eta:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and
\epsilon:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
are natural transformations, one can take their product as well to obtain a natural transformation
\eta\times\epsilon:\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.

If
C
is locally small, then there is a functor
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(-,-):\textbf{C}^{\text{op}}\times\textbf{C}\to\textbf{Set}
which sends an object
(A,B)
to
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(A,B)
and morphism
(f^{\text{op}},g):(A,B)\to(X,Y)
to the function
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(A,B)\to\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(X,Y)
which sends
[\varphi:A\to B]\mapsto[g\circ\varphi\circ f:X\to Y]
.

2.6.
The Yoneda Lemma

If
A
is an object in a category
C
, we denote by
h^{A}:\textbf{C}\to\textbf{Set}
the functor sending an object
B\mapsto\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(A,B)
and a morphism
\varphi:B\to C
to the function
[f:B\to C]\mapsto[\varphi\circ f:A\to C]
. We therefore obtain a functor
h^{-}:\textbf{C}^{\text{op}}\to[\textbf{C},\textbf{Set}]
by sending an object
A\mapsto h^{A}
and a morphism
\varphi:A\to B
to the natural transformation
h^{B}\to h^{A}
defined on
C\in\textbf{C}
by
[f:B\to C]\mapsto[f\circ\varphi:A\to C]
. This functor is named the
(contravariant) Yoneda embedding
, so called because by the ubiquitous
(weak) Yoneda lemma
, it is fully faithful, i.e. the map
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(C,B)\to\text{Nat}(h^{B},h^{C})
induced by the Yoneda embedding is a bijection. One says that a functor
\mathcal{X}:\textbf{C}\to\textbf{Set}
is
representable
if it is isomorphic to
h^{A}
for some
A\in\textbf{C}
.

The
(strong) Yoneda lemma
says that for any functor
\mathcal{Y}:\textbf{C}\to\textbf{Set}
and any object
A\in\textbf{C}
, the map
\text{Nat}(h^{A},\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{Y}(A)
given by
\Phi\mapsto\Phi_{A}(1_{A})
is a bijection. Its inverse is obtained by sending
y\in\mathcal{Y}(A)
to the natural transformation
\Phi^{y}:h^{A}\to\mathcal{Y}
given by
\Phi^{y}_{B}(\varphi:A\to B)=\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)(y)
.

2.7.
Limits and Colimits

Let
\textbf{I},\textbf{C}
be categories. A
diagram of shape
I
in
C
is a functor
\mathcal{D}:\textbf{I}\to\textbf{C}
. If
X\in\textbf{C}
is an object, then
\Delta_{X}:\textbf{I}\to\textbf{C}
denotes the
constant functor
which sends all the objects of
I
to
X
and all the morphisms of
I
to the identity
1_{X}
. A
cone
(resp.
cocone
) over
\mathcal{D}
is a pair
(X,\eta)
where
X\in\textbf{C}
is an object and
\eta:\Delta_{X}\to\mathcal{D}
(resp.
\eta:\mathcal{D}\to\Delta_{X}
) is a natural transformation. A
morphism
of cones (resp. cocones)
(X,\eta)\to(Y,\rho)
is a morphism
f:X\to Y
such that
\rho\circ\Delta_{f}=\eta
(resp.
\Delta_{f}\circ\eta=\rho
), where
\Delta_{f}:\Delta_{X}\to\Delta_{Y}
is the natural transformation induced by
f
. A cone (resp. cocone)
(X,\eta)
over
\mathcal{D}
is a
limit of
\mathcal{D}
(resp.
colimit of
\mathcal{D}
) if it is a terminal (resp. initial) object in the category of cones over
\mathcal{D}
.

The following proposition states that limits and colimits in functor categories can be computed component-wise. In other words, suppose that
C
and
D
are categories. For every object
X\in\textbf{C}
we have a functor
\text{Eval}_{X}:[\textbf{C},\textbf{D}]\to\textbf{D}
which sends an object
\mathcal{F}
to
\mathcal{F}(X)
and a morphism (i.e. a natural transformation)
\eta:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{G}
to
\eta_{X}
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{D}:\textbf{I}\to[\textbf{C},\textbf{D}]
is a diagram of functors and
(\mathcal{F},\rho)
is a cone (resp. cocone) over
\mathcal{D}
. Then
(\mathcal{F}(X),\text{Eval}_{X}\rho)
is a cone (resp. cocone) over
\text{Eval}_{X}\circ\mathcal{D}:\textbf{I}\to\textbf{D}
, and furthermore
(\mathcal{F},\rho)
is a limit (resp. colimit) of
\mathcal{D}
if
(\mathcal{F}(X),\text{Eval}_{X}\circ\rho)
is a limit (resp. colimit) of
\text{Eval}_{X}\circ\mathcal{D}
for all objects
X\in\textbf{C}
. The converse holds if
D
has all limits of shape
I
.

2.8.
Filtered Colimits

A category
C
is
filtered
if every finite diagram in
C
has a cocone.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
I
is a small filtered category,
\mathcal{D}:\textbf{I}\to\textbf{Set}
is a diagram,
X
is a set, and
\eta:\mathcal{D}\to\Delta(X)
is a natural transformation. Then
(X,\eta)
is a colimit of
\mathcal{D}
if and only if

(FC1)

The functions
\eta_{i}
are injective for all objects
i\in\textbf{I}
.

(FC2)

The functions
\eta_{i}
are jointly surjective, i.e.
X=\bigcup_{i\in I}\operatorname{im}\eta_{i}
.

Proof.

We prove only the reverse direction, as that is all we will need. Let
(Y,\mu)
be another cocone over
\mathcal{D}
. We define a function
f:X\to Y
as follows. Given any
x\in X
, by (FC2) there exists
i\in\textbf{I}
and
s\in\mathcal{D}(i)
such that
\eta_{i}(s)=x
. We define
f(x)=\mu_{i}(s)
, and we now check that this is well-defined. Suppose that there is
j\in\textbf{I}
and
t\in\mathcal{D}(j)
such that
\eta_{j}(t)=x
. Because
I
is filtered, the diagram
\textbf{Dis}\{i,j\}\hookrightarrow\textbf{I}
has a cocone, i.e. there exists an object
k\in\textbf{I}
and maps
\alpha:i\to k
,
\beta:j\to k
. Then we have that

\eta_{k}(\alpha(s))=\eta_{i}(s)=x=\eta_{j}(t)=\eta_{k}(\beta(t)).

However by (FC1),
\eta_{k}
is injective, hence
\alpha(s)=\beta(t)
. Then we compute:

\mu_{i}(s)=\mu_{k}(\alpha(s))=\mu_{k}(\beta(t))=\mu_{j}(t).

So indeed
f
is well-defined. It is clear from the definition of
f
that
\Delta(f)\circ\eta=\mu
. Finally if
g:X\to Y
also satisfies
\Delta(g)\circ\eta=\mu
, then for any
x\in X
using (FC2) choose
i\in\textbf{I}
and
s\in\mathcal{D}(i)
such that
\eta_{i}(s)=x
, and compute

g(x)=g(\eta_{i}(s))=\mu_{i}(s)=f(x).

∎

2.9.
Discrete Categories

A
discrete category
is a category whose only morphisms are the identity maps. If
S
is a set, there is an associated discrete category, denoted by
\textbf{Dis}(S)
. The set of objects of this category is simply
S
, and for
x,y\in S
,
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{Dis}(S)}(x,y)
is empty unless
x=y
in which case this set has a single element labeled
1_{x}
. A limit (resp. colimit) of a diagram whose shape is discrete is called a
product
(resp.
coproduct
).

2.10.
Equalizers

A diagram in a category
C
of the form

X\overset{f}{\to}Y\underset{h}{\overset{g}{\rightrightarrows}}Z

is called an
equalizer
if
g\circ f=h\circ f
and
X
together with the maps
f
and
g\circ f=h\circ f
is a limit of the diagram

Proposition 3

A diagram

(EQ1)

The function

(EQ2)

If

2.12.
Adjoint Functors

A
hom-set adjunction
between a pair of categories

and
D
consists of functors
\mathcal{G}:\textbf{C}\rightleftarrows\textbf{D}:\mathcal{F}
and a natural isomorphism
\Phi:\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(\mathcal{F}-,-)\to\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{G}-)
. Here
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(\mathcal{F}-,-):\textbf{D}^{\text{op}}\times\textbf{C}\to\textbf{Set}
is the composition of functors
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(-,-)\circ(\mathcal{F}^{\text{op}}\times\text{id}_{\textbf{C}})
and similarly
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{G}-)=\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,-)\circ(\text{id}_{\textbf{D}}^{\text{op}}\times\mathcal{G})
. In this case we say that
\mathcal{F}
is
left adjoint
to
\mathcal{G}
, or that
\mathcal{G}
is
right adjoint
to
\mathcal{F}
, and write
\mathcal{F}\dashv_{\Phi}\mathcal{G}
or simply
\mathcal{F}\dashv\mathcal{G}
.

Theorem 2
.

If
\mathcal{G}:\textbf{B}\rightleftarrows\textbf{C}:\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{H}:\textbf{C}\rightleftarrows\textbf{D}:\mathcal{E}
are functors with
\mathcal{F}\dashv_{\Phi}\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{E}\dashv_{\Psi}\mathcal{H}
, then
\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{E}\dashv\mathcal{H}\circ\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

We have that
\Phi:\text{Hom}_{\textbf{B}}(\mathcal{F}-,-)\to\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(-,\mathcal{G}-)
and
\Psi:\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(\mathcal{E}-,-)\to\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{H}-)
are natural isomorphisms. Therefore by Lemma
1
, we have that
\Phi(\mathcal{E}^{\text{op}}\times\text{id}_{\textbf{B}}):\text{Hom}_{\textbf{B}}(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{E}-,-)\to\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(\mathcal{E}-,\mathcal{G}-)
and
\Psi(\text{id}_{\textbf{D}}^{\text{op}}\times\mathcal{G}):\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(\mathcal{E}-,\mathcal{G}-)\to\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{H}\circ\mathcal{G}-)
are also natural isomorphisms. Composing these yields the desired natural isomorphism:

\Psi(\text{id}_{\textbf{D}}^{\text{op}}\times\mathcal{G})\circ\Phi(\mathcal{E}^{\text{op}}\times\text{id}_{\textbf{B}}):\text{Hom}_{\textbf{B}}(\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{E}-,-)\to\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{H}\circ\mathcal{G}-)

∎

The following theorem shows that right adjoints of a given functor are unique up to a unique isomorphism (a similar theorem holds for left adjoints).

Theorem 3
.

If
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}:\textbf{C}\rightleftarrows\textbf{D}:\mathcal{F}
are functors with
\mathcal{F}\dashv_{\Phi}\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{F}\dashv_{\Psi}\mathcal{H}
, then there exists a unique natural isomorphism
\theta:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
such that
\Psi\circ\Phi^{-1}=\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,-)(1_{\text{id}_{\textbf{D}}^{\text{op}}}\times\theta)
.

Proof.

We have that
\Phi:\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(\mathcal{F}-,-)\to\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{G}-)
and
\Psi:\text{Hom}_{\textbf{C}}(\mathcal{F}-,-)\to\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{H}-)
are natural isomorphisms, hence
\Psi\circ\Phi^{-1}:\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{G}-)\to\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{H}-)
is an isomorphism in
[\textbf{D}^{\text{op}}\times\textbf{C},\textbf{Set}]
. Let
E:[\textbf{D}^{\text{op}}\times\textbf{C},\textbf{Set}]\to[\textbf{C},[\textbf{D}^{\text{op}},\textbf{Set}]]
be the obvious isomorphism. Then
E(\Psi\circ\Phi^{-1}):E(\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{G}-))\to E(\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{H}-))
is also an isomorphism. Consider the Yoneda embedding
h_{-}:\textbf{D}\to[\textbf{D}^{\text{op}},\textbf{Set}]
and its pushforward
(h_{-})_{*}:[\textbf{C},\textbf{D}]\to[\textbf{C},[\textbf{D}^{\text{op}},\textbf{Set}]]
. We note that
E(\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{G}-))=(h_{-})_{*}\mathcal{G}
and similarly
E(\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,\mathcal{H}-))=(h_{-})_{*}\mathcal{H}
. Therefore
E(\Psi\circ\Phi^{-1}):(h_{-})_{*}\mathcal{G}\to(h_{-})_{*}\mathcal{H}
is an isomorphism. Since the Yoneda embedding is fully faithful, by Theorem
1
, its pushforward is also fully faithful. Hence there exists a unique natural transformation
\theta:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
such that
(h_{-})_{*}(\theta)=E(\Psi\circ\Phi^{-1})
. By Lemma
2
,
\theta
is an isomorphism. The desired result follows since
E^{-1}((h_{-})_{*}(\theta))
is equal to
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{D}}(-,-)(1_{\text{id}_{\textbf{D}}^{\text{op}}}\times\theta)
.
∎

2.14.
Posets

A
preordered set
consists of a set
P
and a relation
\leq
on
P
satisfying the following conditions: (P1) (
Reflexivity
) For all
p\in P
, we have
p\leq p
, (P2) (
Transitivity
) If
p\leq q
and
q\leq r
, then
p\leq r
. A
filtered set
is a preordered set satisfying the extra condition: (FIL1) Every pair of elements has an upper bound. A
poset
is a preordered set satisfying the extra condition: (P3) (
Antisymmetry
) If
p\leq q
and
q\leq p
, then
p=q
. A poset
(P,\leq)
is
totally (or linearly) ordered
if it satisfies the following additional condition: (T1) Given
p,q\in P
either
p\leq q
or
q\leq p
. If
P
and
Q
are posets, a function
f:P\to Q
is
order-preserving
if for all
x,y\in P
with
x\leq y
we have
f(x)\leq f(y)
. It is an
order-embedding
if for all
x,y\in P
,
x\leq y\iff f(x)\leq f(y)
.

Let
(P,\leq)
be a poset with
p<q\in P
, and suppose that
p\leq r\leq q
implies
r=p
or
r=q
. Then we say that
q
is a
successor
of
p
, and
p
is a
predecessor
of
q
. If
q
has a predecessor, we say that
q

is a successor
. If
p
has a successor, we say that
p

is a predecessor
. An element
p\in P
is called an
upper limit element
(resp.
lower limit element
) if does not have a predecessor (resp. successor). We denote the set of successor elements (resp. predecessor elements) in
P
by
P_{s}
(resp.
P_{p}
). Note that if
(I,\leq)
is a totally ordered set, a successor (resp. predecessor) of
i\in I
is unique if it exists, although this may not be true for general posets.

We say that a poset
P
is
well-founded
if every nonempty subset of
P
has a minimal element. If
I
is linear and well-founded we call it
well-ordered
.

2.15.
Thin Category associated to a Poset

A category
C
is

if for any objects
X
and
Y
in
C
, there is at most one morphism
X\to Y
. If
(P,\leq)
is a preordered set, we can form its
associated thin category

\textbf{Thin}(P)
whose objects are exactly the elements of
P
,
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{Thin}(P)}(x,y)
is equal to
\{i_{x,y}\}
if

and
\emptyset
otherwise, and composition is given by
i_{y,z}\circ i_{x,y}=i_{x,z}
where the latter exists by transitivity of the preorder. For any

, by reflexivity

exists and then it is clear from the definition of composition that this morphism is the identity

. Note that if
(P,\leq)
is a filtered set, then
\textbf{Thin}(P)
is a filtered category.

2.16.

All rings in this paper are commutative and have a multiplicative identity, denoted by

Throughout this paper,

. By abuse of notation, we usually suppress the morphism

2.17.

The poset of submodules of an
R
-module

. Suppose that

is a set and

-module. We say that a family

and elements

for all

spans

. Note that if

and

is also independent. We say that

of the family

if (DS1) it is independent and (DS2) it spans
M
. In this case we write

. We say that a submodule
N\subseteq M
is a

if there exists
N^{\prime}\subseteq M
such that
M=N\oplus N^{\prime}
. The poset of summands of

will be denoted by
\text{Sum}(M)
.

Let

be an
R
-module and

be a set. A family
(b_{i}:i\in I)
of elements of

is called a

of

if

is the internal direct sum of the family

of submodules. In this case every element of

can be written as a unique (finite!) linear combination of the elements

. If a module

has a basis, it is called a

R
-module.

We define

to be the set of functions

, which is an
R
-module, and indeed a product of

copies of

in

. A function

. In general

is not
a free

-module when

is infinite. We also define

to be the submodule of

consisting of the functions

with finite support. The module

is

standard basis
for this module is
(e_{i}:i\in I)
where we define

by sending

for

and

.

The category theory perspective on this is as follows. The forgetful functor
\mathcal{U}_{\textbf{Mod}_{R}}:\textbf{Mod}_{R}\to\textbf{Set}
has a left adjoint
\textbf{Mod}_{R}\leftarrow\textbf{Set}:R^{\oplus-}
which sends a set

to the free

-module

. The free

-modules are exactly those which are isomorphic to something in the image of the functor

.

2.27.
(Faithfully) Flat Modules

An
R
-module

is
flat
if the functor
-\otimes_{R}N:\textbf{Mod}_{R}\to\textbf{Mod}_{R}
is exact, i.e. if whenever

is an exact sequence of
R
-modules,
K\otimes_{R}N\overset{f\otimes 1_{N}}{\to}L\otimes_{R}N\overset{g\otimes 1_{N}}{\to}M\otimes_{R}N
is exact. Since
-\otimes_{R}N
is always right exact for any

,

is flat if and only if whenever
\varphi:L\to M
is an injective
R
-module homomorphism,
\varphi\otimes 1_{N}:L\otimes_{R}N\to M\otimes_{R}N
is also injective.

The module
N
is
faithfully flat
if a sequence of
R
-modules
K\overset{f}{\to}L\overset{g}{\to}M
is exact if and only if the sequence
K\otimes_{R}N\overset{f\otimes 1_{N}}{\to}L\otimes_{R}N\overset{g\otimes 1_{N}}{\to}M\otimes_{R}N
is exact.

2.28.

A ring homomorphism

Proposition 8
.

Let

•

\varphi
is faithfully flat.

•

Whenever
\varphi_{!}M=M\otimes_{R}S=0

.

•

The induced map

•

\varphi

is injective for every
R
-module

.

Lemma 6
.

If

Proof.

First we show that

-modules. Then we form the sequence

∎

2.30.
k
-Functors

A
k
-functor
is a functor
\mathcal{X}:\textbf{Alg}_{k}\to\textbf{Set}
. A
morphism of
k
-functors

\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is just a natural transformation. The category of
k
-functors is simply the functor category
[\textbf{Alg}_{k},\textbf{Set}]
. Here it must be said that as Demazure and Gabriel do in
[
DG70
]
in order for the foundations to be rigorous, we should fix two Grothendieck universes
\mathbb{U}\subseteq\mathbb{V}
with
\mathbb{N}\in\mathbb{U}
and restrict all objects in any category we work with (such as
Set
) to be elements of
\mathbb{V}
, and restrict the elements of
\textbf{Alg}_{k}
to only those
k
-algebras in
\mathbb{U}
with
k
also in
\mathbb{U}
(Demazure and Gabriel call such rings
models
). We will be loose with these foundations, but modifications can be made if desired by the reader.

A
subfunctor
of
\mathcal{X}
is a
k
-functor
\mathcal{Y}
such that for any
k
-algebra
A
,
\mathcal{Y}(A)
is a subset of
\mathcal{X}(A)
, and for any
k
-algebra morphism
\varphi:A\to B
,
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)=\mathcal{X}(\varphi)|_{\mathcal{Y}(A)}
. If
\mathcal{Z}
is a subfunctor of a
k
-functor
\mathcal{Y}
, and
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is a morphism, then the
inverse image

f^{-1}\mathcal{Z}
is the subfunctor of
\mathcal{X}
defined by
R\mapsto f_{R}^{-1}(\mathcal{Z}(R))\subseteq\mathcal{X}(R)
. We also define
\operatorname{im}f
to be the subfunctor of
\mathcal{Y}
given by
R\mapsto\operatorname{im}f_{R}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}(R)
.

Via the Yoneda embedding, any
k
-algebra

defines a
k
-functor
h^{R}:\textbf{Alg}_{k}\to\textbf{Set}
which sends
A\mapsto\text{Hom}_{k}(R,A)
. Sometimes, to emphasize that we are thinking of

as a
k
-algebra, we write

. Then as before a

-functor

is representable if there exists a

-algebra

.

There is also a slightly more general notion of representability in the context of

-functors. We say that a

-functor

is

if there exists a scheme

over

such that
\mathcal{X}
is isomorphic to the
k
-functor

. If

, then we have
\text{Hom}_{\textbf{Sch}/k}(\text{Spec}(R),\text{Spec}(A))\cong\text{Hom}_{k}(A,R)
, so any representable

-functor is automatically representable by schemes.

We now give a few classic examples of
k
-functors, and discuss their representability.

Example 1

(The Affine Line)
.

The

is the functor
\mathbb{A}^{1}_{k}:\textbf{Alg}_{k}\to\textbf{Set}
defined by sending
R\mapsto R
and
[\varphi:R\to S]\mapsto\varphi
, i.e. it is the forgetful functor. It is represented by the polynomial ring

since we have a natural isomorphism
\Phi:h^{k[x]}\to\mathbb{A}^{1}_{k}
given by
\Phi_{R}(\psi)=\psi(x)
for
\psi\in h^{k[x]}(R)=\text{Hom}_{k}(k[x],R)
.

Example 2

(Affine
n
-Space)
.

Extending the example above,
affine
n
-space
is the functor
\mathbb{A}^{n}_{k}:\textbf{Alg}_{k}\to\textbf{Set}
defined by sending

and
[\varphi:R\to S]\mapsto[\varphi^{n}:(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})\mapsto(\varphi(r_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(r_{n}))]

, since there is a natural isomorphism
\Phi:h^{k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}_{k}
given by
\Phi_{R}(\psi)=(\psi(x_{1}),\ldots,\psi(x_{n}))
for
\psi\in h^{k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]}(R)=\text{Hom}_{k}(k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}],R)

Example 3

(Affine
I
-Space)
.

Let

be an infinite set. For any

-algebra

and

are not equal (the former is a strict submodule of the latter), so we have two possible generalizations of the previous examples:

•

There is a functor
\textbf{Alg}_{k}\to\textbf{Set}
which sends
R\mapsto R^{I}
and
[\varphi:R\to S]\to\varphi^{I}
. This functor is represented by the polynomial ring

for the same reason as above, and is studied e.g. in
[

]
.

•

We call the functor
\mathbb{A}^{I}_{k}:\textbf{Alg}_{k}\to\textbf{Set}
which sends

and

affine

-space
. It turns out that this functor is not representable, though it is an ind-scheme.

2.32.
Pullbacks in the category of

-Functors

Since limits and colimits in functor categories can be computed component-wise (Proposition
1
), in particular the pullback of a diagram
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}\leftarrow\mathcal{Z}
of
k
-functors is given by the functor which sends a
k
-algebra

to the set
\mathcal{X}(A)\times_{\mathcal{Y}(A)}\mathcal{Z}(A)
. The category of

-functors has a terminal object, namely the functor
\mathbb{A}^{0}
which sends a
k
-algebra

to the set

. It follows that the product of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
is given by the pullback of the diagram
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{A}^{0}\leftarrow\mathcal{Y}

k
-algebra

, we have that

has just a single element, namely the structure homomorphism

, hence

, i.e.
\mathbb{A}^{0}
is representable.

Suppose that
\mathcal{X}\cong S
,
\mathcal{Y}\cong h^{R}
, and
\mathcal{Z}\cong h^{T}
are representable
k
-functors and we have a diagram
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}\leftarrow\mathcal{Z}
of

-functors. Taking the coordinate ring yields a diagram of

-algebras isomorphic to

. The pushout of this diagram in the category of

-algebras is given by

together with the maps

defined by

and a similarly defined map

. Applying the Yoneda embedding to this pushout diagram of

-algebras yields a diagram of

-functors

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h^{S\otimes_{R}T}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h^{T}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h^{S}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h^{R}}

which is a pullback since the Yoneda embedding is a right adjoint and hence preserves limits. Therefore we have that the fiber product

]

Proposition 9
.

(1)

If

2.37.
Quotients and Sheafification

Suppose that
C
is a full subcategory of the category of
k
-functors that is closed under finite products, and suppose that
\mathcal{G}
is a
k
-group functor which, by composing with the forgetful functor
\textbf{Grp}\to\textbf{Set}
is in
C
, and
\alpha:\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is an action of
\mathcal{G}
on a
k
-functor
\mathcal{X}
also in
C
. We define also a natural transformation
\beta:\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
by
\beta_{R}(g,x)=x
. Then a morphism
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is constant on
\mathcal{G}
-orbits if and only if
f\circ\alpha=f\circ\beta
.

It can happen that the naive choice for a quotient of
\mathcal{X}
by the action of
\mathcal{G}
, i.e. the
k
-functor
R\mapsto\mathcal{X}(R)/\mathcal{G}(R)
, is not an element of
C
. However we can still define a
k
-functor
\mathcal{Y}\in\textbf{C}
to be the
quotient of
\mathcal{X}
by
\mathcal{G}
in

C
if
\mathcal{Y}
is a colimit of the diagram
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X}\underset{\beta}{\overset{\alpha}{\rightrightarrows}}\mathcal{X}
in
C
. (Note that because
C
is closed under finite products,
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X}
is also in
C
.) This tells us that if the quotient exists, it is unique (up to a unique isomorphism), but the problem is that it may not always exist.

The functor
\mathcal{Y}:R\mapsto\mathcal{X}(R)/\mathcal{G}(R)
, together with the natural transformation
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
mapping
x\in\mathcal{X}(R)
to its
\mathcal{G}(R)
-orbit in
\mathcal{X}(R)/\mathcal{G}(R)
, is the quotient of
\mathcal{X}
by
\mathcal{G}
in the entire category of
k
-functors. Suppose that the inclusion
i:\textbf{C}\to[\textbf{Alg}_{k},\textbf{Set}]
admits a left adjoint
\mathcal{S}
. Then because left adjoints preserve colimits,
(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{Y}),\mathcal{S}(\pi))
will be the colimit of
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X})\underset{\mathcal{S}(\beta)}{\overset{\mathcal{S}(\alpha)}{\rightrightarrows}}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{X})
. However because
C
is a full subcategory, the inclusion
i:\textbf{C}\to[\textbf{Alg}_{k},\textbf{Set}]
is fully faithful, hence by a result originally due to Gabriel-Zisman
[
GZ12
]
the counit
\epsilon:\mathcal{S}\circ i\to 1_{\textbf{C}}
of the adjunction
\mathcal{S}\vdash i
is an isomorphism. So we have a diagram in
C
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{X})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{Y})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}(\alpha)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}(\beta)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}(\pi)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\epsilon_{\mathcal{X}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

where the vertical maps are isomorphisms, and hence
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{Y}),\mathcal{S}(\pi)\circ\epsilon_{X}^{-1}
is the quotient of
\mathcal{X}
by
\mathcal{G}
in
C
. Such a left adjoint
does
exist (see
[
DG70
]
III §1, 1.8-1.12) when

is the category of fppf
k
-sheaves (which is indeed closed under finite products because limits commute with limits, see
[
ML13
]
IX.2), it is called the
fppf sheafification functor
and is denoted by
\operatorname{sh}:[\textbf{Alg}_{k},\textbf{Set}]\to\textbf{Sh}_{\text{fppf}}
. This implies that if an fppf
k
-group
G
acts on an fppf
k
-sheaf
\mathcal{X}
, the quotient of
\mathcal{X}
of
\mathcal{X}
by
\mathcal{G}
in
\textbf{Sh}_{fppf}
always exists. We denote it by
\mathcal{X}/\mathcal{G}
.

The following theorem gives an easy description of the fppf sheafification of certain nice subfunctors of fppf sheaves (see
[
Jan03
]
I.5.4 (4)).

Theorem 9
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{Y}
is a fppf-
k
-sheaf and that
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
is a subfunctor which satisfies (FP1). Then
\operatorname{sh}\mathcal{X}
is the subfunctor of
\mathcal{Y}
given by

\operatorname{sh}\mathcal{X}(R)=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}(R)\mid\exists\text{ an fppf ring homomorphism }\varphi:R\to S\text{ with }\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)(y)\in\mathcal{X}(S)\}.

One consequence of Theorem
9
is the following.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{Y}
is an fppf
k
-sheaf, and
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
are subfunctors such that
\mathcal{X}
satisfies (FP1) and
\mathcal{Z}
is an fppf-sheaf. If
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
then
\operatorname{sh}(\mathcal{X})\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
.

Proof.

Given
y\in\operatorname{sh}\mathcal{X}(R)
, by Theorem
9
there exists an fppf ring map
\varphi:R\to S
such that
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)(y)\in\mathcal{X}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(S)
. But because
\mathcal{Y}
is an fppf-sheaf, we have that
\mathcal{Y}(\delta)\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)(y)=\mathcal{Y}(\epsilon)\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)(y)
which implies that
\mathcal{Z}(\delta)\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)(y)=\mathcal{Z}(\epsilon)\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)(y)
. So since
\mathcal{Z}
is an fppf-sheaf, there exists
z\in\mathcal{Z}(R)
such that
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)(z)=\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)(y)
. However since
\mathcal{Y}
is an fppf-sheaf,
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)
is injective, and hence
z=y
, so
y\in\mathcal{Z}(R)
.
∎

Another important consequence of Theorem
9
is the fact that closed subfunctors of fppf-sheaves are themselves fppf-sheaves.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be an fppf-
k
-sheaf and
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
a closed subfunctor. Then
\mathcal{X}
is also an fppf-sheaf.

Proof.

By Proposition
12
,
\mathcal{Y}
is a Zariski sheaf, and so by Proposition
11
,
\mathcal{X}
is too. Then by Proposition
10
,
\mathcal{X}
satisfies (FP1), hence Theorem
9
gives a nice description of
\operatorname{sh}\mathcal{X}
as a subfunctor of
\mathcal{Y}
. We will show that
\mathcal{X}=\operatorname{sh}\mathcal{X}
thereby proving that
\mathcal{X}
is an fppf-sheaf. Let
R
be a
k
-algebra and let
y\in\mathcal{Y}(R)
be such that there exists an fppf ring map
\varphi:R\to S
such that
\mathcal{Y}(\varphi)(y)\in\mathcal{X}(S)
. Then by the Yoneda lemma,
y
corresponds to a natural transformation
\eta:h^{R}\to\mathcal{Y}
which for any
k
-algebra
A
sends
\psi:R\to A
to
\mathcal{Y}(\psi)(y)\in\mathcal{Y}(A)
. However by the definition of a closed subfunctor, there exists an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq R
such that
\eta^{-1}(\mathcal{X})=V(\mathfrak{a})
. But note that
\varphi\in\eta^{-1}(\mathcal{X})(S)=\{\psi:R\to S\mid\mathcal{Y}(\psi)(y)\in\mathcal{X}(S)\}
. Hence we must have
\varphi\in V(\mathfrak{a})(S)=\{\psi:R\to S\mid\psi(\mathfrak{a})=0\}
. So
\varphi(\mathfrak{a})=0
. But
\varphi
is faithfully flat, and hence injective by Proposition
8
. So it must be that
\mathfrak{a}=0
, and hence that
V(\mathfrak{a})=h^{R}
. In particular this means that the identity map
\text{id}_{R}:R\to R
is in

, so
\mathcal{Y}(\text{id}_{R})(y)\in\mathcal{X}(R)
. But functors take identity maps to identity maps, hence
\mathcal{Y}(\text{id}_{R})=\text{id}_{\mathcal{Y}(R)}
, so we have that

∎

Now suppose that a

\mathcal{G}

We have an isomorphism
\mathcal{G}/\text{Stab}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)\overset{\sim}{\to}\text{Orb}_{\mathcal{G}}(x)

3.
The Grassmannian

3.1.
Definitions

Let
k
be a ring and
I
be a set. We define a
k
-functor
\text{Gr}_{I}:\textbf{Alg}_{k}\to\textbf{Set}
by sending a
k
-algebra
R
to the set of
R
-submodules of
R^{\oplus I}
which are direct summands (equivalently submodules
N
where
R^{\oplus I}/N
is projective), and by sending a
k
-algebra homomorphism
\varphi:R\to S
to the function which sends
N\in\text{Gr}_{I}(R)
to the image of
\varphi_{!}(i_{N})[\varphi_{!}N]\subseteq\varphi_{!}(R^{\oplus I})
under the canonical isomorphism
\varphi_{!}(R^{\oplus I})=R^{\oplus I}\otimes_{R}S\overset{\sim}{\to}S^{\oplus I}
from Proposition
5
. Here
i_{N}:N\to R^{\oplus I}
denotes the inclusion map, and we note that
\varphi_{!}(i_{N})
is injective by Proposition
4
so that
\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)
is isomorphic to
\varphi_{!}N=N\otimes_{R}S
.

3.2.
Representability and the Case of Finite
I

If
I
is finite,
\text{Gr}_{I}
is isomorphic to the
full Grassmannian
functor from
[
Kar00
]
Section 9. There it is proved that this functor is represented by a scheme, hence it is an fppf-sheaf. However we will be interested in the case when
I
is infinite, and the proof of representability does not extend so we must prove the sheaf property using a more direct approach.

3.3.
The Grassmannian is an fppf-Sheaf

We begin with a few lemmas about the functor
\text{Gr}_{I}
and then move on to prove it is an fppf sheaf.

Lemma 8
.

If
\varphi:R\to S
is a
k
-algebra homomorphism, then
\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)
preserves direct sums, i.e. if
N\in\text{Gr}_{I}(R)
and
(M_{j}\mid j\in J)
is a family of elements of
\text{Gr}_{I}(R)
such that
N=\bigoplus_{j\in J}M_{j}
, then
\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)=\bigoplus_{j\in J}\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(M_{j})
.

Proof.

Since
\varphi_{!}:\textbf{Mod}_{R}\to\textbf{Mod}_{S}
preserves direct sums (Equation
2
), we have that

\varphi_{!}N=\bigoplus_{j\in J}\varphi_{!}(i_{M_{j},N})[\varphi_{!}M_{j}],

where
i_{M_{j},N}:M_{j}\to N
is the inclusion map. Applying the injective map
\varphi_{!}(i_{N,R^{\oplus I}}):\varphi_{!}N\to\varphi_{!}(R^{\oplus I})
yields

\displaystyle\varphi_{!}(i_{N,R^{\oplus I}})[\varphi_{!}N]=\bigoplus_{j\in J}\varphi_{!}(i_{N,R^{\oplus I}})\circ\varphi_{!}(i_{M_{j},N})[\varphi_{!}M_{j}]=\bigoplus_{j\in J}\varphi_{!}(i_{N,R^{\oplus I}}\circ i_{M_{j},N})[\varphi_{!}M_{j}]=\bigoplus_{j\in J}\varphi_{!}(i_{M_{j},R^{\oplus I}})[\varphi_{!}M_{j}].

Finally, applying the isomorphism
\varphi_{!}(R^{\oplus I})\to S^{\oplus I}
, we obtain that
\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)=\bigoplus_{j\in J}\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(M_{j})
as desired.
∎

Lemma 9
.

If
N\in\text{Gr}_{I}(R)
and
\varphi:R\to S
is a
k
-algebra homomorphism, then
\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)
is the
S
-submodule of
S^{\oplus I}
generated by the image of
N
under the
R
-module homomorphism
\varphi^{I}:R^{\oplus I}\to S^{\oplus I}
.

Proof.

By definition
\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)
is equal to the image of

\varphi_{!}(i_{N})[\varphi_{!}N]=\{\sum(n_{i})_{i\in I}\otimes s\mid(n_{i})_{i\in I}\in N,s\in S\}\subseteq R^{\oplus I}\otimes S

under the isomorphism
\Phi_{R,S}(\varphi^{I}):R^{\oplus I}\otimes S\to S^{\oplus I}
which sends
(r_{i})_{i\in I}\otimes s\mapsto s(\varphi(r_{i}))_{i\in I}
. Therefore
\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)=\{\sum s(\varphi(n_{i}))_{i\in I}\mid(n_{i})_{i\in I}\in N,s\in S\}
which is clearly equal to the
S
-submodule of
S^{\oplus I}
generated by the image of
N
under
\varphi^{I}
.
∎

Lemma 10
.

If
\varphi:R\to S
is a
surjective

k
-algebra homomorphism and
N\in\text{Gr}_{I}(R)
, then
\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)=\varphi^{I}(N)
.

Proof.

The inclusion (
\supseteq
) is immediate. For the reverse inclusion (
\subseteq
), by Lemma
9
it is enough to show that
\varphi^{I}(N)
is already an
S
-submodule of
S^{\oplus I}
. Given
(n_{i})_{i\in I}\in N
and
s\in S
, since
\varphi
is surjective there exists
r\in R
such that
\varphi(r)=s
. Then the following computation gives the desired result.

s\varphi^{I}((n_{i})_{i\in I})=\varphi(r)(\varphi(n_{i}))_{i\in I}=(\varphi(r)\varphi(n_{i}))_{i\in I}=(\varphi(rn_{i}))_{i\in I}=\varphi^{I}((rn_{i})_{i\in I})

∎

Lemma 11
.

Let
\varphi:R\to S
be a ring homomorphism and let
N\in\text{Gr}_{I}(R)
. The map
\varphi^{I}:N\to\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)
is adapted to
\varphi
(see Definition
1
).

Proof.

It is clear that
\varphi^{I}:N\to\varphi^{*}\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)
is an
R
-module homomorphism. Furthermore, we have previously shown that the composition of the maps

\varphi_{!}N\overset{\varphi_{!}(i_{N})}{\longrightarrow}\varphi_{!}R^{\oplus I}\overset{\Phi_{R,S}(\varphi^{I})}{\longrightarrow}S^{\oplus I}

is an isomorphism onto its image, which is by definition
\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)
. But notice that this isomorphism is equal to the map
\varphi_{!}N\to\text{Gr}_{I}(\varphi)(N)
induced by the adjunction
\varphi_{!}\vdash\varphi^{*}
.
∎

Theorem 11
.

\text{Gr}_{I}
is an fppf sheaf.

Proof.

We use Theorem
7
and we begin with (FP1). Given
k
-algebras
R_{1},\ldots,R_{n}
, we need to show that the map
\text{Gr}_{I}(R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n})\to\text{Gr}_{I}(R_{1})\times\ldots\times\text{Gr}_{I}(R_{n})
is a bijection.

To prove injectivity, suppose that
M,N\in\text{Gr}_{I}(R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n})
are such that
\text{Gr}_{I}(\pi_{j})(M)=\text{Gr}_{I}(\pi_{j})(N)
for
1\leq j\leq n
. Since the projection maps
\pi_{j}
are surjective, it follows from Lemma
10
that
\text{Gr}_{I}(\pi_{j})(M)=\pi_{j}^{I}(M)
and similarly for
N
. Therefore given
(m_{i})_{i\in I}\in M
, because
\pi_{j}^{I}(M)=\pi_{j}^{I}(N)
for each
1\leq j\leq n
, there exists
(n^{j}_{i})_{i\in I}\in N
such that
(\pi_{j}(m_{i}))_{i\in I}=(\pi_{j}(n^{j}_{i}))_{i\in I}
. Therefore we can write

(m_{i})_{i\in I}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}(n^{j}_{i})_{i\in I}\in N

where
e_{j}
is the idempotent
(0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0)\in R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n}
with a
1
in the
j
th entry and
0
’s everywhere else. Hence
M\subseteq N
. A similar computation shows the other containment, so
M=N
.

For surjectivty, suppose we are given
M_{j}\in\text{Gr}_{I}(R_{j})
, with direct sum complements
M_{j}\oplus M_{j}^{\prime}=R_{j}^{\oplus I}
. We note that as
(R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n})
-modules we have
R_{1}^{\oplus I}\times\ldots\times R_{n}^{\oplus I}=(M_{1}\times\ldots\times M_{n})\oplus(M_{1}^{\prime}\times\ldots\times M_{n}^{\prime})
. Furthermore, under the
(R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n})
-module isomorphism

\displaystyle R_{1}^{\oplus I}\times\ldots\times R_{n}^{\oplus I}

\displaystyle\overset{\sim}{\to}(R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n})^{\oplus I}

\displaystyle((x_{i}^{1})_{i\in I},\ldots,(x_{i}^{n})_{i\in I})

\displaystyle\mapsto((x^{1}_{i},\ldots,x^{n}_{i}))_{i\in I}

M_{1}\times\ldots\times M_{n}
corresponds to
L=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}(\pi^{I}_{j})^{-1}(M_{j})
and
M_{1}^{\prime}\times\ldots\times M_{n}^{\prime}
corresponds to
L^{\prime}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}(\pi^{I}_{j})^{-1}(M_{j}^{\prime})
. Then we have that
L\oplus L^{\prime}=(R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n})^{\oplus I}
, so that
L\in\text{Gr}_{I}(R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n})
. Furthermore, given
(m_{i})_{i\in I}\in M_{j}
, we note that
(m_{i}e_{j})_{i\in I}\in(R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n})^{\oplus I}
is in fact in

, since

and

for all

. But this computation also shows that

, as desired.

Now we show (FP2). Suppose that

is a

4.
Submodule Filtrations

The goal of this section is to introduce the very general notions of a poset filtration and an (almost) gradation which are necessary for defining flags and which will also be used as technical tools during our proof of the Bruhat decomposition.

4.1.
Poset Filtrations

Definition 2
.

A
poset filtration
of an
R
-module
M
consists of a partially ordered set
(P,\leq)
, and a function
F:P\to\text{Sub}(M)
satisfying the following.

(F1)

There exists
p\in P
such that
F_{p}=\{0\}
.

(F2)

There exists
q\in P
such that
F_{q}=M
.

(F3)

F
is an order-preserving function (i.e.
p\leq q\implies F_{p}\subseteq F_{q}
).

A poset filtration
(F,P)
is called
linear
if
(P,\leq)
is a totally ordered set. In this case we typically use “
I
” or “
J
” instead of “
P
”. Furthermore, we define a poset filtration
(F,P)
to be an
embedding
if the map
F:P\to\text{Sub}(M)
is an order embedding (i.e. for any
p,q\in P
,
p\leq q\iff F_{p}\subseteq F_{q}
). Note that this condition implies that
F
is injective, and indeed an order-isomorphism into its image. If
(F,I)
is linear, then it is an embedding if and only if
F
is injective. Finally we say
(F,P)
is
well-founded
if
(P,\leq)
is a well-founded poset. Well-founded filtrations which are linear are called
well-ordered
.

Example 5
.

Let
R^{\oplus\mathbb{N}}:=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}R
. This is often known as
finitely supported sequence space
. Furthermore, let
I=\omega+1
have the usual total order. We define a filtration
E:I\to\text{Sub}(R^{\oplus\mathbb{N}})
as follows.

\displaystyle E_{\omega}

\displaystyle=R^{\oplus\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle E_{k}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},0,\ldots)\}

\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle=0

Then
(E,I)
is a linear, embedded, well-ordered filtration.

Example 6
.

Define
J=\{-\infty\}\cup\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
with the usual linear order. Define
F:J\to\text{Sub}(R^{\oplus\mathbb{N}})
as follows.

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=R^{\oplus\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle F_{-k}

\displaystyle=\{(\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{k},a_{k+1},\ldots)\}

\displaystyle F_{-\infty}

\displaystyle=0

Then
(F,J)
is a linear, embedded filtration of
R^{\oplus\mathbb{N}}
, but note that it is not well-ordered.

Example 7
.

Let
R^{\mathbb{N}}:=\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}R
. This is often known as “sequence space”, and strictly contains
R^{\oplus\mathbb{N}}
. As before we let
J=\{-\infty\}\cup\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, and define
G:J\to\text{Sub}(R^{\mathbb{N}})
by

\displaystyle G_{0}

\displaystyle=R^{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle G_{-k}

\displaystyle=\{(\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{k},a_{k+1},\ldots)\}

\displaystyle G_{-\infty}

\displaystyle=0

Note that
(G,J)
is basically the same as
(F,J)
, the only difference is the underlying module has been enlarged.

4.2.
Gradations and Almost Gradations

Definition 3
.

A
gradation
of a poset filtration
(F,P)
of an
R
-module
M
is a function
C:P\to\text{Sub}(M)
satisfying:

(G1)

For all
p\in P
we have
F_{p}=\bigoplus_{q\leq p}C_{q}
.

One can weaken (G1) so that the submodules
C_{p}
only complement successive submodules of the filtration, resulting in the following definition.

Definition 4
.

An
almost gradation
of a poset filtration
(F,P)
of an
R
-module
M
is a function
C:P\to\text{Sub}(M)
satisfying:

(AG1)

For all
p\in P
,
F_{p}=F_{<p}\oplus C_{p}
,

where we define
F_{<p}=\sum_{q<p}F_{q}
.

As the names suggest and the following lemma and proposition show, every gradation of a poset filtration
(F,P)
is also an almost gradation of
(F,P)
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
C:P\to\text{Sub}(M)
be a gradation of a poset filtration
(F,P)
of an
R
-module
M
. Then for all
p\in P
,
F_{<p}=\bigoplus_{q<p}C_{q}
.

Proof.

Let
p\in P
. By definition we have that
F_{p}=\bigoplus_{q\leq p}C_{q}
. Therefore the family of submodules
(C_{q}:q\leq p)
is independent. Hence the subfamily
(C_{q}:q<p)
is independent as well, so (DS1) is satisfied. To prove (DS2), note that we have
F_{<p}=\sum_{q<p}F_{q}=\sum_{q<p}\sum_{r\leq q}C_{r}=\sum_{r<p}C_{r}
.
∎

Proposition 14
.

Every gradation of a poset filtration
(F,P)
is an almost gradation of
(F,P)
.

Proof.

Let
C:P\to\text{Sub}(M)
be a gradation of
(F,P)
. Let
p\in P
. definition we have that
F_{p}=\bigoplus_{q\leq p}C_{q}
. Since
\{q\leq p\}=\{q<p\}\sqcup\{p\}
, it follows that
F_{p}=\left(\sum_{q<p}C_{q}\right)\oplus C_{p}=F_{<p}\oplus C_{p}
, where the second equality follows from Lemma
12
. Hence (AG1) is satisfied.
∎

4.3.
Independent Almost Gradations

Recall the definition of an independent function
C:P\to\text{Sub}(M)
from the background section. Of course even in the case when
R
is a field, almost gradations need not be independent in general. However, linearity gives one simple condition on the poset
P
to guarantee that any almost gradation of any filtration indexed by
P
will be independent.

Theorem 12
.

Every almost gradation
C:I\to\text{Sub}(M)
of a linear filtration
(F,I)
of
M
is independent.

Proof.

Suppose that
J\subseteq I
is a finite set and
c_{j}\in C_{j}
are elements such that
\sum_{j\in J}c_{j}=0
. Because
I
is linearly ordered, we let
J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}
with
j_{1}<j_{2}<\ldots<j_{n}
. Then we have that
c_{j_{1}},\ldots,c_{j_{n-1}}\in F_{<j_{n}}
, and by definition,
F_{j_{n}}=C_{j_{n}}\oplus F_{<j_{n}}
. Hence we obtain that
c_{j_{n}}=0
and also
c_{j_{1}}+\ldots+c_{j_{n-1}}=0
. The result follows by induction on
n
.
∎

In fact we’ll now show that the theorem above is also true for the intersection of two linear filtrations, though not for three!

4.4.
Intersections of Poset Filtrations

Let
(P,\leq_{P})
and
(Q,\leq_{Q})
be posets. We define the
product order
on
P\times Q
, denoted here by
\leq_{\times}
, by letting
(p,q)\leq_{\times}(x,y)
if and only of
p\leq_{P}x
and
q\leq_{Q}y
. From now on we may drop the subscripts on the orders. The
intersection
of two poset filtrations
(E,P)
and
(F,Q)
of a
k
-module
M
consists of the poset
(P\times Q,\leq_{\times})
and the function
E\cap F:P\times Q\to\text{Sub}(M)
defined by
[E\cap F]_{(p,q)}=E_{p}\cap F_{q}
. If
(E,P)
and
(F,Q)
are poset filtrations of
M
then so is their intersection.

First we prove some technical lemmas.

Lemma 13
.

If
(F,I)
is a linear filtration of an
R
-module
M
, and
J\subseteq I
is a nonempty subset, then
\sum_{j\in J}F_{j}=\bigcup_{j\in J}F_{j}
.

Proof.

To prove (
\subseteq
), note that since
J
is nonempty, every element of
\sum_{j\in J}F_{j}
is of the form
v_{j_{1}}+\ldots+v_{j_{n}}
with
v_{j_{m}}\in F_{j_{m}}
. Because
I
is totally ordered, we may assume without loss of generality that
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}
. Then
F_{j_{m}}\subseteq F_{j_{n}}
for all
1\leq m\leq n
, hence
v_{j_{1}}+\ldots+v_{j_{n}}\in F_{j_{n}}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in J}F_{j}
. Since
F_{k}\subseteq\sum_{j\in J}F_{j}
for all
k\in J
, the containment (
\supseteq
) follows trivially.
∎

Lemma 14
.

If
(E,I)
and
(F,J)
are linear filtrations of an
R
-module
M
, then for each
(i,j)\in I\times J
, we have that

[E\cap F]_{<(i,j)}=E_{i}\cap F_{<j}+E_{<i}\cap F_{j}.

Proof.

By definition, we have that
[E\cap F]_{<(i,j)}=\sum_{(k,\ell)<(i,j)}E_{k}\cap F_{\ell}
. We now show mutual inclusion of the desired equality.

For the containment (
\subseteq
), suppose that
(k,\ell)<(i,j)
, and note that in the product order this holds if and only if
k<i
and
\ell\leq j
, or
k\leq i
and
\ell<j
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
k<i
and
\ell\leq j
. Then we have that
E_{k}\subseteq E_{<i}
, and
F_{\ell}\subseteq F_{j}
. Therefore
E_{k}\cap F_{\ell}\subseteq E_{<i}\cap F_{j}
.

To show the containment (
\supseteq
), we prove only that
E_{i}\cap F_{<j}\subseteq[E\cap F]_{<(i,j)}
, the other case being similar. First of all, if
j
is the smallest element of
J
, then
F_{<j}=0
and so the containment holds trivially. If not, given
v\in E_{i}\cap F_{<j}
, in particular we have that
v\in F_{<j}=\sum_{\ell<j}F_{\ell}
. Since
(J,\leq)
is totally ordered and
\{\ell\in J\mid\ell<j\}
is nonempty by hypothesis, by Lemma
13
we have that
\sum_{\ell<j}F_{\ell}=\bigcup_{\ell<j}F_{\ell}
. Therefore there exists some
\ell^{\prime}<j
such that
v\in F_{\ell^{\prime}}
. Note that this implies
(i,\ell^{\prime})<(i,j)
, hence
v\in E_{i}\cap F_{\ell^{\prime}}\subseteq\sum_{(k,\ell)<(i,j)}E_{i}\cap F_{j}=[E\cap F]_{<(i,j)}
.
∎

Now we prove the theorem.

Theorem 13
.

Let
(E,I)
and
(F,J)
be linear filtrations of an
R
-module
M
. Every almost gradation of the intersection
(E\cap F,I\times J)
is independent.

Proof.

Let
C:I\times J\to\text{Sub}(V)
be an almost gradation of
(E\cap F,I\times J)
. We prove by induction on
m
and
n
that if
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{m}\in I
and
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\in J
and for
1\leq k\leq m
,
1\leq\ell\leq n
we have
c_{k,\ell}\in C_{(i_{k},j_{\ell})}
such that
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq m\\
1\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}c_{k,\ell}=0
, then
c_{k,\ell}=0
for all
1\leq k\leq m
and
1\leq\ell\leq n
.

If
m=n=1
, then by hypothesis we have that
c_{1,1}=0
. Now suppose that the result holds for
m=1
and for
n-1
. Given
c_{1,1}+\ldots+c_{1,n}=0
, this implies that
c_{1,1}+\ldots+c_{1,n-1}=-c_{1,n}
.
Note that
(i_{1},j_{1}),\ldots,(i_{1},j_{n-1})<(i_{1},j_{n})
, and hence for
1\leq\ell\leq n-1
,

c_{1,\ell}\in C_{(i_{1},j_{\ell})}\subseteq[E\cap F]_{(i_{1},j_{\ell})}\subseteq[E\cap F]_{<(i_{1},j_{n})}.

Therefore
c_{1,1}+\ldots+c_{1,n-1}\in[E\cap F]_{<(i_{1},j_{n})}
, and since
c_{1,n}\in C_{(i_{1},j_{n})}
, and by definition we have that
[E\cap F]_{(i_{1},j_{n})}=[E\cap F]_{<(i_{1},j_{n})}\oplus C_{(i_{1},j_{n})}
, it follows that
c_{1,n}=0
and that
c_{1,1}+\ldots+c_{1,n-1}=0
. By our induction hypothesis, we then have that
c_{1,\ell}=0
for all
1\leq\ell\leq n-1
as well. Hence we have shown that the result holds for
m=1
and for arbitrary
n
.

Now suppose the result holds for
m-1
and arbitrary
n
. Given
c_{k,\ell}
such that
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq m\\
1\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}c_{k,\ell}=0
, we’ll prove that
c_{m,p}=0
for
1\leq p\leq n
by induction on
r=n-p
. If
r=0
, then
p=n
. First of all, we have that
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq m\\
1\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}c_{k,\ell}=0
, which we rewrite as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq m-1\\
1\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}c_{k,\ell}+\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq n-1}c_{m,\ell}=-c_{m,n}.

However, we note that the left hand side of this equation is contained in
[E\cap F]_{<(i_{m},j_{n})}
. Since
c_{m,n}\in C_{(i_{m},j_{n})}
and by definition we have that
[E\cap F]_{(i_{m},j_{n})}=[E\cap F]_{<(i_{m},j_{n})}\oplus C_{(i_{m},j_{n})}
, it follows that
c_{m,n}=0
.

Now suppose that
c_{m,n-q}=0
for
0\leq q\leq r-1
. We’ll show that
c_{m,n-r}=c_{m,p}=0
as well. By hypothesis, we have that
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq m-1\\
1\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}c_{k,\ell}+\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq p}c_{m,\ell}=0
which we rewrite as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq m-1\\
1\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}c_{k,\ell}=-\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq p}c_{m,\ell}.

We note that the left hand side of this equation is contained in
E_{i_{m-1}}
, and the right hand side is contained in
F_{j_{p}}
. As the two sides are equal, both sums are contained in
E_{i_{m-1}}\cap F_{j_{p}}
, so in particular, the left hand side is contained in
[E\cap F]_{<(i_{m},j_{p})}
. Furthermore, leaving off the
c_{m,p}
term, we also have that the sum
\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq p-1}c_{m,\ell}
is contained in
[E\cap F]_{<(i_{m},j_{p})}
. But by hypothesis, we have the equation

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq m-1\\
1\leq\ell\leq n\end{subarray}}c_{k,\ell}+\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq p-1}c_{m,\ell}=-c_{m,p}.

We have just shown that the left hand side is contained in
[E\cap F]_{<(i_{m},j_{p})}
, while the RHS is contained in
C_{(i_{m},j_{p})}
. Since by definition we have that
[E\cap F]_{(i_{m},j_{p})}=[E\cap F]_{<(i_{m},j_{p})}\oplus C_{(i_{m},j_{p})}
, it follows that
c_{m,p}=0
.
∎

4.5.
Spanning Almost Gradations

Let
(F,P)
be a poset filtration of an
R
-module
M
and
C:P\to\text{Sub}(M)
a function. If for all
p\in P
we have that
F_{p}=\sum_{q\leq p}C_{q}
, then we say
C

spans

(F,P)
. Clearly an almost gradation of
(F,P)
is a gradation if and only if it is independent and spans
(F,P)
.

If
M
is a finite-dimensional vector space, then obviously all almost gradations of
F
span. However, perhaps surprisingly, this is not true in infinite-dimensions (even for vector spaces). In the latter case we have three possibilities, even in the linear case: (1) No almost gradations of
F
are gradations, (2) Some almost gradations of
F
are gradations and some are not, and (3) all almost gradations of
F
are gradations. We now give examples of (1) and (2), and for simplicity we work over a field
\mathbb{F}
. Note that an equivalent example of (1) can be found in
[
BLP11
]
.

Example 8

(A Linear Filtration that does not have a Gradation)
.

Define
V=\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots):a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}\}
, and for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
define
F_{i}=\{(\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{-i},a_{-i+1},\ldots)\}
, and
F_{-\infty}=\{0\}
, and
I=\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}\cup\{-\infty\}
with the usual order. Then
(F,I)
is a maximal embedded linear filtration of
V
. For any almost gradation
C:I\to\text{Sub}(V)
, we have that
\dim(C_{i})=1
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, and
\dim(C_{-\infty})=0
. Therefore
\sum_{i\in I}C_{i}
has countable dimension, whereas
\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}
has uncountable dimension. Hence
\sum_{i\leq 0}C_{i}\subsetneq F_{0}
, so
C
cannot be a gradation.

Example 9

(An Almost Gradation that is not a Gradation)
.

Define
V=\mathbb{F}^{\oplus\mathbb{N}}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)\in\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}:a_{i}=0\text{ for all but finitely many }i\}
and similarly to the example above for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
define
F_{i}=\{(\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{-i},a_{-i+1},\ldots)\}
, and
F_{-\infty}=\{0\}
. Define
I=\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}\cup\{-\infty\}
with the usual order. Then
(F,I)
is a maximal embedded linear filtration of
V
. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, define
e_{k}
to be the
k
th standard basis vector, and let
f_{k}=e_{k}-e_{k+1}
.

Firstly, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, define
C_{i}=\text{span}(f_{-i+1})
and
C_{-\infty}=\{0\}
. For each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, we have that

\displaystyle F_{i}=F_{i-1}\oplus C_{i},

hence
C:I\to\text{Sub}(V)
is an almost gradation of
(F,I)
. However for all
i\in I
,
C_{i}
is contained in the kernel of the linear functional
\varphi:V\to\mathbb{F}
defined by
\varphi(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)=a_{1}+a_{2}+\ldots
, which has codimension
1
inside
V
. Therefore
F_{0}\neq\sum_{i\leq 0}S_{i}
, and hence the almost gradation does not span
V
and is therefore not a gradation.

However, secondly, if for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, we define
C^{\prime}_{i}=\text{span}(e_{-i+1})
and
C^{\prime}_{-\infty}=\{0\}
, then
C^{\prime}:I\to\text{Sub}(V)
is a gradation.

Hence filtrations satisfying (1) and (2) exist. We now give a characterization of those filtrations satisfying (3) in terms of the well-founded property of the poset
P
.

Theorem 14
.

If
C:P\to\text{Sub}(V)
is an almost gradation of a well-founded poset filtration
(F,P)
of an
R
-module
M
, then
C
spans
(F,P)
.

Proof.

Let
T=\{p\in P:F_{p}=\sum_{q\leq p}C_{q}\}
, and suppose for some
n\in P
we have that
m\in T
for all
m<n
. By definition of an almost gradation, we have
F_{n}=F_{<n}\oplus C_{n}
. Then we compute

F_{n}=F_{<n}+C_{n}=\left[\sum_{p<n}F_{p}\right]+C_{n}=\left[\sum_{p<n}\sum_{q\leq p}C_{q}\right]+C_{n}=\left[\sum_{q<n}C_{q}\right]+C_{n}=\sum_{q\leq n}C_{q}.

Therefore
n\in T
as well. By transfinite induction we have that
T=P
.
∎

Corollary 4
.

Every almost gradation of a linear well-ordered filtration
(F,I)
is in fact a gradation. Similarly, if
(E,I)
and
(F,J)
are linear well-ordered filtrations of
V
, then every almost gradation of their intersection
(E\cap F,I\times J)
is a gradation.

Proof.

If
C
is an almost gradation of
(F,I)
, then because
(F,I)
is linear, by Theorem
12

C
is independent, and because
(F,I)
is well-ordered, by Theorem
14
,
C
spans
(F,I)
. Therefore
C
is a gradation of
(F,I)
.

If
C
is an almost gradation of
(E\cap F,I\times J)
, then by Theorem
13
,
C
is independent, and because
I
and
J
are well-ordered, by Lemma
15
,
I\times J
is well-founded, hence
(E\cap F,I\times J)
is a well-ordered poset filtration. By Theorem
14
,
C
spans
(E\cap F,I\times J)
, and hence
C
is a gradation of
(E\cap F,I\times J)
.
∎

In the case when
I
and
J
are finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
(and hence automatically well-ordered), a proof of Corollary
4
can be found in
[
Rin16
]
.

Lemma 15
.

Let
(P,\leq_{P})
and
(Q,\leq_{Q})
be well-founded posets. Then
(P\times Q,\leq_{\times})
is a well-founded poset.

Proof.

Let
S
be a nonempty subset of
P\times Q
. We denote by
\pi_{P}:P\times Q\to P
and
\pi_{Q}:P\times Q\to Q
the projections onto the first and second coordinates respectively. Then
\pi_{P}(S)
is a nonempty subset of
P
, so it has a minimal element, call it
p_{0}
. Then
\pi_{Q}(\pi_{P}^{-1}(p_{0})\cap S)
is a nonempty subset of
Q
, and therefore has a minimal element as well, call it
q_{0}
. Suppose that
(p,q)\in S
were such that
(p,q)\leq_{P\times Q}(p_{0},q_{0})
. Then we have that
p\leq p_{0}
and that
p\in\pi_{P}(S)
, hence by minimality of
p_{0}
, it follows that
p=p_{0}
. Then
q\in\pi_{Q}(\pi_{P}^{-1}(p_{0})\cap S)
, and we also have that
q\leq q_{0}
. Hence by minimality of
q_{0}
, we have
q=q_{0}
. Therefore
(p,q)=(p_{0},q_{0})
, so
(p_{0},q_{0})
is a minimal element of
S
.
∎

4.6.
Embedded Filtrations

A poset filtration
(F,P)
of an
R
-module
M
is called
embedded
if
F:P\to\text{Sub}(M)
is an order embedding. Note that a linear filtration
(F,I)
is embedded if and only if
F
is injective.

Proposition 15
.

If
(F,I)
is an embedded linear filtration of an
R
-module
M
, then
I
has a least element
i_{0}
and a greatest element
i_{\infty}
and we have
F_{i_{0}}=0
,
F_{i_{\infty}}=M
.

Proof.

By (F1) there exists
i_{0}\in I
such that
F_{i_{0}}=0
. We claim that
i_{0}
is the least element of
I
. If not, there exists
i\in I
such that
i<i_{0}
, and by (F3) we must have
F_{i}=0
as well. However embedded filtrations are injective, hence
i=i_{0}
. This implies that
i_{0}
is a minimal element, but since
I
is totally ordered, it is in fact the least element. A similar argument shows that there exists a greatest element
i_{\infty}
in
I
with
F_{i_{\infty}}=M
.
∎

5.
Flags

The goal of this section is to develop a theory of full flags in an infinite dimensional vector space and more generally in an infinite rank free module. Let
R
be a ring, let
M
be a
free

R
-module, and let
I
be a well-ordered set.

Definition 5
.

An
I
-flag
in
M
is a well-ordered linear filtration
F:I\to\text{Sub}(M)
satisfying the following conditions.

(FL1)

For each
i\in I
,
F_{i}
is a direct summand of
M
, i.e. the image of
F
lies in
\text{Sum}(M)
.

(FL2)

For each
i\in I
, we have
F_{i+1}/F_{i}
is finitely generated projective of rank
1
.

(FL3)

For each upper limit element
i\in I
we have
F_{<i}=F_{i}
.

Note that (FL1) implies that for each
i\in I
, both
F_{i}
and
M/F_{i}
are projective. The following lemma shows that this implies the quotient
F_{i+1}/F_{i}
is also automatically projective, hence the condition (FL2) simply imposes that this module is finitely generated and has projective rank
1
.

Lemma 16
.

Let
N\subseteq L
be submodules of a free
R
-module
M
, with the property that
M/N
and
M/L
are projective. Then
L/N
is projective as well, and in fact
N
is complemented inside
L
, i.e. there exists a (necessarily projective)
R
-submodule
C\subseteq M
such that
N\oplus C=L
.

Proof.

Because
M/L
is projective,
L
is complemented inside
M
, i.e. there exists a submodule
K\subseteq M
such that
L\oplus K=M
. Since
N\subseteq L
, we have inclusions
L/N\to M/N
and
K\to M/N
, and it follows that
L/N\oplus K=M/N
. But
M/N
is projective and direct summands of projective modules are projective, hence
L/N
is projective. This implies that
N
is complemented inside
L
, say by
C
. Finally we have that
C\cong L/N
, and we have shown that the latter is projective, so
C
is projective as well.
∎

We now collect a few properties of
I
-flags that we will require later.

Corollary 5
.

Let
R
be a ring,
M
a free
R
-module. Every
I
-flag of
M
has a gradation.

Proof.

If
F
is an
I
-flag of
M
, then we define
C:I\to\text{Sub}(M)
as follows. If
i
is an upper limit element then we define
C_{i}=0
, and since in this case
F_{<i}F_{i}
, indeed we have
F_{i}=F_{<i}\oplus C_{i}
. If
i
is a successor, say
i=j+1
,
F_{i}/F_{j}
is projective hence there exists a submodule
C_{i}\subseteq F_{i}
such that
F_{i}=F_{j}\oplus C_{i}
. Since
j
is the predecessor of
i
, we have that
F_{j}=F_{<i}
, so
F_{i}=F_{<i}\oplus C_{i}
. Then we have shown that
C
is an almost gradation of
F
. By Corollary
4
, it follows that
C
is a gradation of
F
.
∎

Lemma 17
.

I
-flags are embedded.

Proof.

Suppose that
F:I\to\text{Sub}(M)
is an
I
-flag in
M
. Since
I
is totally ordered, to show
F
is an order embedding, it is enough to show that it is injective. Given
i\neq j\in I
, without loss of generality we can assume that
i<j
. Then because
I
is well-ordered, and
i
is not the maximal element,
i
has an immediate successor
k
. Since
F
is order-preserving, we have that
F_{i}\subseteq F_{k}\subseteq F_{j}
and
F_{k}/F_{i}
has projective rank
1
so in particular it is non-zero. Therefore
F_{i}
is properly contained in
F_{k}
, hence in
F_{j}
as desired.
∎

Together with Proposition
15
, this lemma shows the following.

Corollary 6
.

If
F
is an
I
-flag of
M
, then
I
has a least element
i_{0}
and a greatest element
i_{\infty}
and we have
F_{i_{0}}=0
,
F_{i_{\infty}}=M
.

6.
The Full Flag Functor

6.1.
Definition

Let
k
be a ring and
I
a well-ordered set. We define a functor
\text{Fl}_{I}:\textbf{Alg}_{k}\to\textbf{Set}
, by sending a
k
-algebra
R
to the set of
I
-flags in
R^{\oplus I_{s}}
, and a
k
-module homomorphism
\varphi:R\to S
to the function which takes an
I
-flag
F:I\to\text{Sum}(R^{\oplus I_{s}})
to the composition
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ F:I\to\text{Sum}(S^{\oplus I_{s}})
. We now confirm that
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ F
is indeed an
I
-flag of
S^{\oplus I_{s}}
.

Lemma 18
.

If
F:I\to\text{Sum}(R^{\oplus I_{s}})
is a filtraion in
R^{\oplus I_{s}}
and
\varphi:R\to S
is a
k
-algebra homomorphism, then the composition
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ F:I\to\text{Sum}(S^{\oplus I_{s}})
is a filtration of
S^{\oplus I_{s}}
.

Proof.

We first show (F1). By hypothesis there exists
i\in I
such that
F_{i}=0
, and we have that
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)F_{i}\cong F_{i}\otimes_{R}S=\{0\}\otimes_{R}S=0
. Similarly, to show (F2), we note that by hypothesis there exists
j\in I
such that
F_{j}=R^{\oplus I}
, and we have that
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(R^{\oplus I_{s}})=S^{\oplus I_{s}}
. For (F3), if
i\leq j
, then
F_{i}\subseteq F_{j}
, so we consider the following commutative diagram in
\textbf{Mod}_{R}
where all of the arrows are inclusion maps, along with its image under the functor
\varphi_{!}
which is a commutative diagram in
\textbf{Mod}_{S}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{j}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{!}F_{j}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{!}F_{i}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R^{\oplus I_{s}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{!}R^{\oplus I_{s}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{F_{j}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{!}(i_{F_{j}})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{F_{i}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{!}(i_{F_{i}})}

It follows that
\operatorname{im}\varphi_{!}(i_{F_{i}})\subseteq\operatorname{im}\varphi_{!}(i_{F_{j}})
, and hence that
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(F_{i})\subseteq\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(F_{j})
.
∎

Lemma 19
.

If
F:I\to\text{Sum}(R^{\oplus I_{s}})
is a filtration in
R^{\oplus I_{s}}
,
C:I\to\text{Sum}(R^{\oplus I_{s}})
is a gradation of
F
, and
\varphi:R\to S
is a
k
-algebra homomorphism, then
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ C
is a gradation of the filtration
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ F
of
S^{\oplus I_{s}}
.

Proof.

Because
C
is a gradation of
F
, given any
i\in I
, we have that
F_{i}=\oplus_{j\leq i}C_{j}
. By Lemma
8
it follows that
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(F_{i})=\oplus_{j\leq i}\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(C_{j})
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 16
.

If
F:I\to\text{Sum}(R^{\oplus I_{s}})
is an
I
-flag in
R^{\oplus I_{s}}
and
\varphi:R\to S
is a
k
-algebra homomorphism, then the composition
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ F:I\to\text{Sum}(S^{\oplus I_{s}})
is an
I
-flag in
S^{\oplus I_{s}}
.

Proof.

(FL1) is clearly satisfied because by Lemma
8
,
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)
takes direct summands to direct summands. We now prove (FL2). If
i\in I
is a successor with predecessor
j
, then because
F_{i}/F_{j}
is projective,
F_{j}
is a direct summand of
F_{i}
, say
F_{i}=F_{j}\oplus N
. By Lemma
8
it follows that
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(F_{i})=\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(F_{j})\oplus\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(N)
. Since
N
is finitely generated projective of rank
1
, it follows from Propositions
6
and
7
that
\varphi_{!}(N)\cong\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(N)\cong\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(F_{i})/\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(F_{j})
are all also finitely generated projective of rank
1
.

For (FL3), let
C:I\to\text{Sub}(R^{\oplus I_{s}})
be a gradation of
F
. Then by Lemma
19
,
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ C
is a gradation of
\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ F
. For any
i\in I
, we have that
F_{<i}=\bigoplus_{j<i}C_{j}
and also
(\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ F)_{<i}=\bigoplus_{j<i}\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(C_{j})
. Now if
i
is an upper limit element, then
F_{i}=F_{<i}
, hence we compute as follows.

(\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ F)_{i}\overset{(1)}{=}\bigoplus_{j<i}\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(C_{j})=(\text{Gr}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)\circ F)_{<i}

Here (1) follows from Lemma
8
.

∎

6.2.
The Finite Flag Functor

Now let us discuss the case when
I
is a finite set. Since all well-ordered finite sets are order-isomorphic to
\{0,\ldots,n\}
with the usual order, we assume that
I
is equal to this set. Then
I_{s}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\text{Fl}_{I}
is isomorphic to the usual full flag functor
\text{Fl}_{n}
which sends a
k
-algebra
R
to the set of sequences of submodules
0\subseteq M_{1}\subseteq M_{2}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq M_{n-1}\subseteq R^{\oplus n}
where each
M_{i}
is a summand of
R^{\oplus n}
and is finitely generated projective of rank
i
(see
[
DG80
]
I.2.6.5 and
[
Kar00
]
). In this case
\text{Fl}_{n}
is represented by a scheme, and hence is automatically an fppf-sheaf. However in the case when
I
is infinite, the proof of representability (again) does not extend, and therefore (again) we spend the remainder of this section proving the sheaf condition directly.

6.3.
The Full Flag Functor is an fppf-Sheaf

We begin with some technical lemmas.

Lemma 20
.

Let
R_{1},\ldots,R_{n}
be rings and let
\pi_{i}:R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n}\to R_{i}
denote the projection map. For each
i
let
M_{i}
be an
R_{i}
module and note that the projection
p_{i}:M_{1}\times\ldots\times M_{n}\to\pi_{i}^{*}M_{i}
is an
(R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{n})
-module homomorphism. Then
p_{i}
is adapted to
\pi_{i}
.

Proof.

Given
m\in M_{i}
, we have that
(\pi_{i})_{!}((0,\ldots,m,\ldots,0)\otimes 1)=m
, so
(\pi_{i})_{!}
is surjective. On the other hand, consider the map
g:M_{i}\to(\pi_{i})_{!}(M_{1}\times\ldots\times M_{n})
which sends
m\mapsto(0,\ldots,m,\ldots,0)\otimes 1
. An easy computation shows that this map is
R_{i}
-linear.
Furthermore another easy computation shows that

g\circ(\pi_{i})_{!}=1_{(\pi_{i})_{!}(M_{1}\times\ldots\times M_{n})}
. Hence
(\pi_{i})_{!}
has a left inverse and is therefore injective.
∎

Lemma 21
.

Let
R
be a ring,
M
an
R
-module,
I
a totally ordered set,
\mathcal{E}:\textbf{Thin}(I)\to\textbf{Mod}_{R}
a functor, and
\eta:\mathcal{E}\to\Delta(M)
a natural transformation. Suppose that for every
j
in
I
,
\eta_{j}
is injective. Then
(\sum_{i\in I}\eta_{i}(\mathcal{E}(i)),\nu)
is a colimit of
\mathcal{E}
, where
\nu:\mathcal{E}\to\Delta(\sum_{i\in I}\eta_{i}(\mathcal{E}(i)))
is defined by letting
\nu_{i}
be the factorization of
\eta_{i}
through
\sum_{i\in I}\eta_{i}(\mathcal{E}(i))
.

Proof.

First of all,
(\sum_{i\in I}\eta_{i}(\mathcal{E}(i)),\nu)
is a cocone over
\mathcal{E}
because
(M,\eta)
is. Now given
j,k\in I
, since
I
is totally ordered without loss of generality we may assume that
j\leq k
. Then there exists a morphism
i_{j,k}:j\to k
in
I
, and by hypothesis
\mathcal{E}(i_{j,k})
is injective. Since
\eta_{j}
and
\eta_{k}
are injective as well, we have that
\eta_{j}(\mathcal{E}(j))\subseteq\eta_{k}(\mathcal{E}(k))
, hence the sum
\sum_{i\in I}\eta_{i}(\mathcal{E}(i))
is nested, so it is in fact equal to
\bigcup_{i\in I}\eta_{i}(\mathcal{E}(i))
. Now suppose that
N
is an
R
-module and
\mu:\mathcal{E}\to N
is a cocone over
\mu
. We define a morphism
f:\bigcup_{i\in I}\eta_{i}(\mathcal{E}(i))\to N
as follows. If
x\in\bigcup_{i\in I}\eta_{i}(\mathcal{E}(i))
, then there exists some
j\in I
such that
x\in\eta_{j}(\mathcal{E}(j))
, and hence there exists
y\in\mathcal{E}(j)
with
\eta_{j}(y)=x
. We define
f(x)=\mu_{j}(y)
.

This is well-defined because if
x\in\eta_{k}(\mathcal{E}(k))
with
\eta_{k}(z)=x
as well, then because
I
is totally ordered, without loss of generality we have that
j\leq k
, and
(M,\eta)
is a cocone over
\mathcal{E}
, we have that

\eta_{k}(z)=\eta_{j}(y)=\eta_{k}(i_{j,k}(y)).

But because
\eta_{k}
is injective, it follows that

7.1.
Matrices

Let
I
and
J
be sets and
R
be a ring. An
I\times J

matrix with entries in
R
is a function
x:I\times J\to R
. We denote the value of such a function at
(i,j)
by
x_{i,j}
, and sometimes we denote the matrix
x
by
[x_{i,j}]
. A matrix
x
is said to be
column finite
if for each
j\in J
, the function
x_{-,j}:I\to R
has finite support. We denote the set of column finite
I\times J
matrices by
\text{Hom}_{I,J}(R)
, and in particular the set of
I\times I
matrices by
\text{End}_{I}(R)
. The latter is a monoid with law of composition given by usual matrix multiplication:

(xy)_{i,j}=\sum_{n\in I}x_{in}y_{nj}.

We note that because
y
is column finite, for a fixed
i
and
j
, only finitely many of the terms
x_{in}y_{nj}
are nonzero, hence this sum actually does make sense.

We get a functor
\text{Hom}_{I,J}:\textbf{CRing}\to\textbf{Set}
which sends a ring
R\mapsto\text{Hom}_{I,J}(R)
and a ring homomorphism
\varphi:R\to S
to the function sending
[x_{i,j}]\mapsto[\varphi(x_{i,j})]
. Note that indeed
[\varphi(x_{i,j})]
has finitely supported columns, since
\varphi(0)=0
. Notice
\text{Hom}_{I,J}
is a subfunctor of the functor
\text{Mat}_{I,J}
which maps
R\in\textbf{CRing}
to the set of
all

I\times J
matrices. The latter functor is representable, with
\text{Mat}_{I,J}\cong h^{k[x_{i,j}\mid i\in I,j\in J]}
, but the former is not. However, we now show that
\text{Hom}_{I,J}
is a filtered colimit of representable functors.

Proposition 17
.

\text{Hom}_{I,J}
is an ind-affine ind-scheme.

Proof.

Let
X:J\to\mathcal{P}_{\text{fin}}(I)
be a function that assigns to each
j\in J
a finite subset
X_{j}\subseteq I
. Let
C_{I,J}
denote the set of all such functions. We write
X\subseteq Y
if
X_{j}\subseteq Y_{j}
for all
j\in J
. Then
(C_{I,J},\subseteq)
is a poset and in fact a filtered set because the function
X\cup Y
is an upper bound for
X
and
Y
in
C_{I,J}
. Define a functor
\mathcal{D}:\textbf{Thin}(C_{I,J})\to[\textbf{CRing},\textbf{Set}]
by sending
X
to the subfunctor
\text{Mat}_{X}
of
\text{Mat}_{I,J}
which assigns to a ring
R
, the set

\{x\in\text{Mat}_{I,J}(R)\mid x_{i,j}=0\text{ if }i\notin X_{j}\},

and by sending the arrow
i_{X,Y}:X\to Y
(which exists if and only if
X\subseteq Y
) to the inclusion map
\text{Mat}_{X}\hookrightarrow\text{Mat}_{Y}
. Since
X_{j}
is finite for all
j\in J
, it follows that
\text{Mat}_{X}\subseteq\text{Hom}_{I,J}
. Furthermore we have that
\text{Mat}_{X}\cong h^{k[x_{i,j}\mid i\in X_{j}]}

For each function

\text{Mat}_{X}\hookrightarrow\text{Hom}_{I,J}
by
\eta_{X}
. Then

is a cocone over the diagram

and by Proposition
1
it is a colimit if and only if for every ring

, the cocone

over
\text{Mat}_{-}(R)
is a colimit in

. We use Proposition
2
to show that this is in fact the case. First of all, (FC1) is satisfied trivially because for each

,

defined by letting

is in
C_{I,J}
, and

.

7.2.
The General Linear Functor

We define the

associated to

\text{GL}_{I}:\textbf{Alg}_{k}\to\textbf{Set}
, by letting
\text{GL}_{I}(R)
be the subset of

consisting of the invertible matrices, i.e. column-finite matrices

with

is the Kronecker delta function (i.e. the identity matrix). Note that if

is a ring homomorphism and

[\varphi(x_{i,j})]
is also invertible since if

. Hence defining
\text{GL}_{I}(\varphi)=\text{End}_{I}(\varphi)
gives that
\text{GL}_{I}
is a subfunctor of
\text{End}_{I}

is a group for all rings

and
\text{GL}_{I}(\varphi)
is a group homomorphism for all ring homomorphisms

is actually a
k
-group functor, i.e. takes values in

We define a subfunctor of the

-functor
\text{End}_{I}\times\text{End}_{I}

\mathcal{G}_{I}(R)=\{(x,y)\in\text{End}_{I}(R)\times\text{End}_{I}(R)\mid xy=yx=\delta\}.

Proposition 18
.

\mathcal{G}_{I}
is an ind-affine ind-scheme.

Proof.

Using the language of the proof of Proposition
17
, given functions

in

, we define
\mathcal{G}_{X,Y}=(\text{Mat}_{X}\times\text{Mat}_{Y})\cap\mathcal{G}_{I}
. In other words,

is the subfunctor of
\mathcal{G}_{I}
defined by

\mathcal{G}_{X,Y}(R)=\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{G}_{I}(R)\mid x_{i,j}=0\text{ if }i\notin X_{j}\text{ and }y_{i,j}=0\text{ if }i\notin Y_{j}\}.

Then

is a closed subfunctor of the representable functor
\text{Mat}_{X}\times\text{Mat}_{Y}\cong h^{k[x_{i,j},y_{n,m}\mid i\in X_{j},n\in Y_{m}]}

\displaystyle\sum_{\{\ell\in Y(b)|a\in X(\ell)\}}x_{a\ell}y_{\ell b}

\displaystyle\sum_{\{\ell\in X(b)|a\in Y(\ell)\}}y_{a\ell}x_{\ell b}

\displaystyle\text{ for all }a,b\in I\text{ with }a\neq b

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{\{\ell\in Y(c)|c\in X(\ell)\}}x_{c\ell}y_{\ell c}

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{\{\ell\in Y(c)|c\in X(\ell)\}}y_{c\ell}x_{\ell c}

and therefore

where

generated by the polynomials above. A similar argument to that in the proof of Proposition

shows that

is a filtered colimit of the representable functors

, as desired.

.

For any set

Lemma 22

Let

Next we prove injectivity of the map
\text{GL}(M)\backslash\text{CRP}_{I}^{\text{free}}(M)\to\text{Perm}(I_{s})
. Suppose that
[E,F]
and
[E^{\prime},F^{\prime}]
are such that
w_{E,F}=w_{E^{\prime},F^{\prime}}
. We’ll call this permutation
w
for short. Let
C:I\times I\to\text{Sub}(M)
and
D:I\times I\to\text{Sub}(M)
be gradations of the filtration intersections
E\cap F
and
E^{\prime}\cap F^{\prime}
respectively. Define

A=\{(i,j)\in I_{s}\times J_{s}:j=w(i)\},

And recall that
C_{(i,j)},D_{(i,j)}\neq 0
if and only if
(i,j)\in A
. For each
(i,j)\in A
let
(e_{i,j})
be a basis for the free module
C_{(i,j)}
(it is here that we use the fact that
E,F
are in constant
free
relative position). Similarly we define
f_{i,j}\in D_{(i,j)}
. Then we have that

(e_{i,j}:(i,j)\in A)\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ (f_{i,j}:(i,j)\in A)

are bases for
V
. Define an
R
-module homomorphism
g:M\to M
by
g(e_{i,j})=f_{i,j}
. Since
g
sends a basis to a basis, it is invertible. We claim that
gE=E^{\prime}
and
gF=F^{\prime}
, from which it will follow that
[E,F]=[E^{\prime}.F^{\prime}]
. Given any
i\in I
, we have

gE_{i}=g\left(\bigoplus_{(k,\ell)\leq(i_{\infty},j)}C_{(k,\ell)}\right)=\bigoplus_{(k,\ell)\leq(i_{\infty},j)}gC_{(k,\ell)}=\bigoplus_{(k,\ell)\leq(i_{\infty},j)}D_{(k,\ell)}=E^{\prime}_{i}

where the first and last equalities hold because
C
and
D
are gradations of
E\cap F
and
E^{\prime}\cap F^{\prime}
respectively. A similar computation shows
gF=F^{\prime}
.

Finally we prove surjectivity of the map
\text{GL}(M)\backslash\text{CRP}_{I}^{\text{free}}(M)\to\text{Perm}(I_{s})
. Let
w\in\text{Perm}(I_{s})
. Let
(e_{i}:i\in I_{s})
be a basis for
M
. Define functions
E,F:I\to\text{Sub}(M)
by

E_{i}=\text{span}(e_{w(j)}\mid j\in I_{s},j\leq i)\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ F_{i}=\text{span}(e_{j}\mid j\in I_{s},j\leq i).

Then
E
and
F
are
I
-flags in
M
. Furthermore, we define a function
C:I\times I\to\text{Sub}(M)
by

C_{(i,j)}=\left\{\begin{matrix}\text{span}(e_{j})&\text{ if }&i\in I_{s}\text{ and }j=w(i)\\
0&\text{ otherwise }&\end{matrix}\right..

Note first that because
w
is a bijection,
(C_{(i,j)}\mid(i,j)\in I\times I)
is an independent family. We now show that
C
satisfies the conditions of part (2) of Lemma
22
for the filtrations
E
and
F
.

Given
i\in I
, we wish to show that
E_{i}=\bigoplus_{(k,\ell)\leq(i,j_{\infty})}C_{(k,\ell)}
. First of all (DS1) is satisfied because the family
(C_{(k,\ell)}\mid(i,j_{\infty})\in I\times I)
is a subfamily of an independent family and hence independent. Furthermore (DS2) follows from the computation

\sum_{(k,\ell)\leq(i,j_{\infty})}C_{(k,\ell)}=\sum_{k\leq i,k\in I_{s}}\text{span}(e_{w(k)})=E_{i}.

Similarly, given
j\in I
, we wish to show that
F_{j}=\bigoplus_{(k,\ell)\leq(i_{\infty},j)}C_{(k,\ell)}
. The proof of (DS1) is the same as before, and for (DS2), we compute

\sum_{(k,\ell)\leq(i_{\infty},j)}C_{(k,\ell)}=\sum_{\ell\leq j,\ell\in I_{s}}\text{span}(e_{\ell})=F_{j}.

Therefore by Lemma
22
,
C:I\times I\to\text{Sub}(V)
is a gradation of the filtration intersection
E\cap F
. By construction of
C
, given any
i\in I_{s}
, the unique
j\in I_{s}
such that
C_{(i,j)}
is nonzero is given by
j=w(i)
. Hence
w_{E,F}=w
, so indeed the map is surjective.

∎

If
V
is a vector space then by Lemma
23
we have that
\text{CRP}^{\text{free}}_{I}(V)=\text{Fl}_{I}(V)\times\text{Fl}_{I}(V)
, and so in this case Theorem
16
can be stated as follows.

Corollary 8
.

If
V
is a vector space, then the formula
[E,F]\mapsto w_{E,F}
gives a well-defined bijection
\text{GL}(V)\backslash[\text{Fl}_{I}(V)\times\text{Fl}_{I}(V)]\to\text{Perm}(I_{s})
.

8.2.
The Flag Bruhat Decomposition

Fix an
I
-flag
E\in\text{Fl}_{I}(M)
and denote by
\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)
the set of
I
-flags in
\text{Fl}_{I}(M)
that are in constant relative position with
E
. Furthermore denote by
B_{E}
the stabilizer of
E
under the action of
\text{GL}(M)
on
\text{Fl}_{I}(M)
. Suppose that
F\in\text{CRP}(M,E)
and let
C
be a gradation of
F\cap E
. Then for any
b\in B_{E}
,
bC
is a gradation of
bF\cap bE=bF\cap E
. Since
(bC)_{(i,j)}\cong C_{(i,j)}
for all
i,j\in I
, it follows that
bF
is in constant relative position with
E
as well and indeed that
w_{bF,E}=w_{F,E}
. Hence we have a left action of
B_{E}
on
\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)
, and the map
\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)\to\text{Perm}(I_{s})
defined by
F\mapsto w_{F,E}
lifts to a well-defined map on the quotient
B_{E}\backslash\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)\to\text{Perm}(I_{s})
.

We are now in a position to state the Bruhat decomposition.

Theorem 17

(The Bruhat Decomposition)
.

Suppose that
I
is a well-ordered set,
M
is a free
R
-module of rank
|I_{s}|
and
E
is a successively free
I
-flag in
M
. Then the map
B_{E}\backslash\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)\to\text{Perm}(I_{s})
defined by
[F]\mapsto w_{F,E}
is a bijection.

Proof.

Note first that by Proposition
24
, every flag
F\in\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)
is successively free and
F
and
E
are in constant free relative position.

Now suppose that
w_{F,E}=w_{F^{\prime},E}
. Then under the map
\text{GL}(M)\backslash\text{CRP}^{\text{free}}_{I}(M)\to\text{Perm}(I_{s})
we have that
[F,E]
and
[F^{\prime},E]
map to the same permutation. Hence by Theorem
16
, there exists some
g\in\text{GL}(M)
such that
g(F,E)=(F^{\prime},E)
, which implies that
(gF,gE)=(F^{\prime},E)
. In particular we have
gE=E
, so
g\in B_{E}
, but then since
gF=F^{\prime}
, this implies that
[F]=[F^{\prime}]
in
B_{E}\backslash\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)
, so indeed the map is injective.

Furthermore, given
w\in\text{Perm}(I_{s})
, again by Theorem
16
, there exists a pair
(F,F^{\prime})\in\text{CRP}^{\text{free}}_{I}(M)
such that
w_{F,F^{\prime}}=w
. However by Proposition
22
, there exists
g\in\text{GL}(M)
such that
gF^{\prime}=E
. But we have
[gF,E]=[gF,gF^{\prime}]=[F,F^{\prime}]
in
\text{GL}(M)\backslash\text{CRP}^{\text{free}}_{I}(M)\to\text{Perm}(I_{s})
, so
w_{gF,E}=w
. Then
gF\in\text{CRP}_{I}(M)
, and we have that
[gF]\mapsto w
, so the map is surjective as well.
∎

The Bruhat decomposition is less complicated over a field. Indeed by Lemma
23
, every
I
-flag
F
in a vector space
V
is in constant relative position to a fixed flag
E\in\text{Fl}_{I}(V)
, so we obtain the following.

Corollary 9

(The Bruhat Decomposition for Vector Spaces)
.

If
I
is a well-ordered set,
V
is a vector space of dimension
|I_{s}|
, and
E
is any
I
-flag in
V
, then the map
B_{E}\backslash\text{Fl}_{I}(V)\to\text{Perm}(I_{s})
defined by
[F]\mapsto w_{F,E}
is a bijection.

8.3.
The General Linear Bruhat Decomposition

Let
I
be a well-ordered set,
M
a free
R
-module of rank
|I_{s}|
and
E
a fixed successively free
I
-flag in
M
. Define a map
m_{E}:\text{GL}(M)\to\text{Fl}_{I}(M)
by sending
g\mapsto gE
. The inverse image
\text{GL}_{E}(M):=m_{E}^{-1}(\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E))
is closed under both left and right multiplication by
B_{E}
, because given
g\in\text{GL}_{E}(M)
and
b\in B_{E}
, first of all we have
gbE=gE\in\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)
, and second of all we have
bgE\in\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)
by Subsection
8.2
. Therefore we have a left and right action of
B_{E}
on
\text{GL}_{E}(M)
by multiplication. The following theorem describes the double cosets, i.e. the orbits under the corresponding
B_{E}\times B_{E}
action.

Theorem 18

(The General Linear Bruhat Decomposition)
.

Let
I
be a well-ordered set,
M
be a vector space of rank
|I_{s}|
, and
E
be a successively free
I
-flag in
M
. The formula
[g]\mapsto w_{gE,E}
gives a well-defined bijection

B_{E}\backslash\text{GL}_{E}(M)/B_{E}\to\text{Perm}(I_{s}).

Proof.

If
[g]=[g^{\prime}]
, then by definition
g^{\prime}=bgc
for
b,c\in B_{E}
, and we have that
w_{g^{\prime}E,E}=w_{bgcE,E}=w_{bgE,E}
. But by the Bruhat decomposition (Theorem
17
),
w_{bgE,E}=w_{gE,E}
, so indeed our formula gives a well-defined map.

Suppose that
w_{gE,E}=e_{g^{\prime}E,E}
. Then again by the Bruhat decomposition, we have that
[gE]=[g^{\prime}E]
in
B_{E}\backslash\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)\to\text{Perm}(I_{s})
, so by definition there exists
b\in B_{E}
such that
bgE=g^{\prime}E
. But this implies that
(g^{\prime})^{-1}bg\in B_{E}
, and hence that
bgc=g^{\prime}
for some
c\in B_{E}
. Hence our map is injective.

Finally, given
w\in\text{Perm}(I_{s})
, by the Bruhat decomposition, there exists an
I
-flag
F\in\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)
such that
w=w_{F,E}
. But because the
\text{GL}(M)
action on the set of successively free
I
-flags is transitive (Proposition
22
), there exists
g\in\text{GL}(M)
such that
gE=F
. However
F\in\text{CRP}_{I}(M,E)
, so by definition
g\in\text{GL}_{E}(M)
. Hence we have
[g]\mapsto w_{gE,E}=w_{F,E}=w
, and our map is surjective as well.
∎

Again the situation is much simpler for vector spaces. For a vector space
V
, every
I
-flag is successively free and
\text{CRP}_{I}(V,E)=\text{Fl}_{I}(V)
, so we have
\text{GL}_{E}(V)=\text{GL}(V)
. Hence we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 10

(The General Linear Bruhat Decomposition for Vector Spaces)
.

Let
I
be a well-ordered set,
V
be a vector space of dimension
|I_{s}|
, and
E
be any
I
-flag in
V
. The formula
[g]\mapsto w_{gE,E}
gives a well-defined bijection

B_{E}\backslash\text{GL}(V)/B_{E}\to\text{Perm}(I_{s}).

Let
R
be a ring and let
E
be the standard flag in
R^{\oplus I_{s}}
. Note that
E
is successively free. Then define
\text{GL}_{I_{s},E}(R)
to be the group of
I_{s}\times I_{s}
matrices
g
with entries in
R
such that
gE\in\text{CRP}_{I}(R^{\oplus I_{s}},E)
. The following corollary follows from applying Theorem
10
to the
R

R^{\oplus I_{s}}
.

Corollary 11

(The General Linear Bruhat Decomposition over a Ring)
.

Let
I
be a well-ordered set and
R
be a ring. Then

\text{GL}_{I_{s},E}(R)=\bigsqcup_{\sigma\in\text{Perm}(I_{s})}B_{I_{s}}(R)\sigma B_{I_{s}}(R)

Proof.

Under the isomorphism
\text{GL}(R^{\oplus I_{s}})\cong\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(R)
given by the standard basis, the stabilizer subgroup
B_{E}\subseteq\text{GL}(R^{\oplus I_{s}})
corresponds to the subgroup
B_{I_{s}}(R)
of upper triangular matrices (see Proposition
21

\sigma\in\text{Perm}(I_{s})
(and by abuse of notation we denote the corresponding permutation matrix in
\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(R)
also by
\sigma

w_{\sigma E,E}=\sigma
. Therefore the double coset
B_{I_{s}}(R)\sigma B_{I_{s}}(R)
corresponds to the permutation
\sigma
under the bijection of Theorem
10
, so the desired result follows.
∎

If
k
is a field, then
\text{GL}_{I_{s},E}(k)=\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(k)
so from Corollary
11
we obtain the following:

Corollary 12

(The General Linear Bruhat Decomposition over a Field)
.

Let

be a well-ordered set and
\mathbb{F}

\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(k)=\bigsqcup_{\sigma\in\text{Perm}(I_{s})}B_{I_{s}}(k)\sigma B_{I_{s}}(k)

.

It is
not
true for general rings

that
\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(R)=\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(R)
because the LHS will typically be larger (see e.g.
[
OPV06
]
).

8.4.
The Functorial Bruhat Decomposition

Let
I
be a well-ordered set. We collect the previous results to give statements about the
k

\text{Fl}_{I}
and
\text{GL}_{I_{s}}
. For the rest of the paper we define
B=B_{I_{s}}
when there is no chance of confusion. There is both a left and right action of the
k
-group functor
B
on the
k
-functor
\text{GL}_{I_{s}}
by left and right multiplication respectively, which gives a left action of
B\times B
on
\text{GL}_{I_{s}}
, explicitly defined by
b,c\in B(R),g\in\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(R)\mapsto bgc^{-1}
. If
\sigma\in\text{Perm}(I_{s})
, then the permutation matrix of
\sigma
(which we also denote by
\sigma

\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(k)

B\sigma B\subseteq\text{GL}_{I_{s}}
by

R\mapsto B(R)\sigma B(R)=\{b\sigma c\mid b,c\in B(R)\}.

By definition the fppf-orbit of
\sigma
under the
B\times B
orbit is the fppf-image of the natural transformation
B\times B\to\text{GL}_{I_{s}}
given by
b,c\in B(R)\mapsto b\sigma c^{-1}

\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)
and by Equation

(7)

R\mapsto\{g\in\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(R)\mid\exists\text{ an fppf-$R$-algebra }\varphi:R\to S\text{ with }\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(g)=b\sigma c\text{ for }b,c\in B(S)\}.

Clearly we have
B\sigma B\subseteq\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)
.

Recall that two subfunctors
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Z}
of a
k
-functor
\mathcal{X}
are called
disjoint
if for every
k
-algebra
R
, the subsets
\mathcal{Y}(R)
and
\mathcal{Z}(R)
of

are disjoint. Also recall that a family of subfunctors
(\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in A)
of a
k

\mathcal{X}
are said to

\mathcal{X}
if for all
k
-algebras
R

which are fields
, we have that
\mathcal{X}(R)=\bigcup_{\alpha\in A}\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha}(R)

Theorem 19

(The Functorial General Linear Bruhat Decomposition)
.

Let
I
be a well-ordered set. The subfunctors
\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)
are disjoint and cover the
k
-functor
\text{GL}_{I_{s}}
.

Proof.

First of all, it follows from Corollary
11
that
B\sigma B
and
B\tau B
are disjoint. Suppose that
g\in\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)(R)\cap\operatorname{sh}(B\tau B)(R)
. By definition there exist fppf-ring maps
\varphi:R\to S
and
\psi:R\to T
such that
\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(\varphi)(g)\in B(S)\sigma B(S)
and
\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(\psi)(g)\in B(T)\sigma B(T)
. By Lemma
6
,
\iota_{S}:S\to S\otimes_{R}T
and
\iota_{T}:T\to S\to S\otimes_{R}T
are both faithfully flat and by Lemma
4
they are finitely presented, hence they are fppf ring maps. As
B\sigma B
and
B\tau B
are subfunctors, we obtain that

B(S\otimes_{R}T)\sigma B(S\otimes_{R}T)\ni\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(\iota_{S}\circ\varphi)(g)=\text{GL}_{I_{s}}(\iota_{T}\circ\varphi)(g)\in B(S\otimes_{R}T)\tau B(S\otimes_{R}T).

But
B(S\otimes_{R}T)\sigma B(S\otimes_{R}T)
and
B(S\otimes_{R}T)\tau B(S\otimes_{R}T)
are disjoint, so we obtain the desired contradiction.

The fact that the subfunctors
(B\sigma B\mid\sigma\in\text{Perm}(I_{s}))

\text{GL}_{I_{s}}

∎

There is a left action of the

Let

The fact that

9.
The Infinite Bruhat Order

For the remainder of the paper, we turn our attention to describing the closures of the subfunctors
B\sigma B\subseteq\text{GL}_{I_{s}}
and the Schubert cells
\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)/B\subseteq\text{GL}_{I_{s}}/B
. We shall specialize to the case of
I=\omega+1=\{0,1,\ldots,\omega\}
so that
I_{s}=\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
. Many of the proofs here rely heavily on this specialization, and the extension of these results to the case of general well-ordered sets
I
is a potential direction for future research.

9.1.
A Topology on the Space of Permutations

If
I
is a set, we place a topology on
\text{Perm}(I)
as follows. There is an injective map
\text{Perm}(I)\to I^{I}
sending
\sigma\mapsto(\sigma(i))_{i\in I}
, so we can identify
\text{Perm}(I)
as a subset of
I^{I}
. We now give
I
the discrete topology,
I^{I}
the product topology, and
\text{Perm}(I)\subseteq I^{I}
the subspace topology. In fact, since
I
has the discrete topology, all functions
I\to I
are continuous, hence we can identify the space
C(I,I)
of continuous functions
I\to I
with
I^{I}
, and the product topology on
I^{I}
gives the
compact-open
topology on
C(I,I)
. Therefore we call our subspace topology on
\text{Perm}(I)
the compact-open topology as well.

Proposition 26
.

A sequence
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots
in
\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
converges to
\sigma
in the compact-open topology if and only if the sequence is
eventually equal
to
\sigma
, i.e. if and only if for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
n\geq N
then
\sigma_{n}(\ell)=\sigma(\ell)
for all
\ell\leq m
.

Proof.

Recall that there is a base for the product topology on
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
which consists of sets of the form
U_{1}\times U_{2}\times\ldots
such that each
U_{i}
is open in
\mathbb{N}
and
U_{i}=\mathbb{N}
for all but finitely many
i
. Then the sequence
(\sigma_{n})
converges to
\sigma
if and only if for all basic open subsets
U=U_{1}\times U_{2}\times\ldots
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
n\geq N
, then
\sigma_{n}\in B
.

Now suppose that
(\sigma_{n})
converges to
\sigma
. Then given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, since
\mathbb{N}
carries the discrete topology, the set
U=\{\sigma(1)\times\sigma(2)\times\ldots\times\{\sigma(m)\}\times\mathbb{N}\times\ldots
is a basic open set containing
\sigma
, so there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N
,
\sigma_{n}\in U
, which implies in particular that if
n\geq N
the
\sigma_{n}(\ell)=\sigma(\ell)
for all
\ell\leq m
.

Conversely, suppose
(\sigma_{n})
is eventually equal to
\sigma
and let
U=U_{1}\times U_{2}\times\ldots\times U_{m}\times\mathbb{N}\times\ldots
be a basic open set containing
\sigma
. Then by definition there exists some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
n\geq N
then
\sigma_{n}(\ell)=\sigma(\ell)
for all
\ell\leq m
. Since
\sigma\in B
, this implies that
\sigma_{n}\in U
for all
n\geq N
, so
(\sigma_{n})\to\sigma
as desired.
∎

Corollary 13
.

Suppose that
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots
is a sequence in
\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
, that converges to
\sigma
. Then
\sigma_{1}^{-1},\sigma_{2}^{-1},\ldots
converges to
\sigma^{-1}
.

Proof.

Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, because
\sigma
is a bijection,
\sigma^{-1}(\{1,\ldots,m\})\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a set of cardinality
m
. Let
m^{\prime}
be the maximal element of this set. Then because
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots
converges to
\sigma
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
n\geq N
then
\sigma_{n}(\ell)=\sigma(\ell)
for all
\ell\leq m^{\prime}
. But then for any such
n\geq N
, given
j\leq m
, we know that
\sigma^{-1}(j)\leq m^{\prime}
, hence
\sigma_{n}(\sigma^{-1}(j))=\sigma(\sigma^{-1}(j))
, hence
\sigma_{n}(\sigma^{-1}(j))=j
, so
\sigma^{-1}_{n}(j)=\sigma^{-1}(j)
. So indeed
(\sigma_{n})
converges to
\sigma
.
∎

It is well known that
S_{n}:=\text{Perm}([n])
is generated by transpositions, i.e. permutations swapping two elements of
[n]
, but this is no longer the case for
\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
. Given any
\sigma\in\text{Perm}(I)
for any set
I
, we define
\text{Supp}(\sigma)=\{i\in I\mid\sigma(i)\neq i\}
. Then because all of the transpositions in
\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
have finite support, the subgroup they generate is contained inside the subgroup
\text{Perm}_{\text{fin}}(\mathbb{N})
of finitely supported permutations. This is a proper subgroup since there exist permutations with infinite support, for example the permutation
\rho
which sends all odd
n
to
n+2
, all even
m
with
m\neq 2
to
m-2
, and
2\mapsto 1
. However as we shall see, it is true that every
\sigma\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
is a limit (in the compact-open topology) of a sequence of the form
(t_{1},t_{2}t_{1},t_{3}t_{2}t_{1},\ldots)
where each
t_{i}
is a transposition.

9.2.
The Bruhat Order

Suppose that
X
and
Y
are subsets of
\mathbb{N}
both of cardinality

, and that the elements of
X
are denoted by
x_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
such that
x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots<x_{n}
and similarly for
Y
. Then we say that
X\leq Y
if and only if
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all

. This defines a partial order on the set
\mathcal{P}_{n}(\mathbb{N})
of subsets of
\mathbb{N}
of cardinality
n
. Using this, we define a partial order, called the
Bruhat order
, on
\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
by setting
\tau\leq\sigma
if and only if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
\{\tau(1),\ldots,\tau(n)\}\leq\{\sigma(1),\ldots,\sigma(n)\}
.

, all even

with
m\neq 2
to
m-2
, and

has one-line notation and associated tableau given respectively by

Then for another permutation

we have that
\tau\leq\sigma
if and only if the entry in each box of the tableau associated to

is less than or equal to the corresponding box in the tableau associated to

.

There is an alternate description of Bruhat order that will be useful to us. If
g\in\text{End}_{\mathbb{N}}(R)
, any ordered pair of subsets
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{N}
defines a
|X|\times|Y|
submatrix of
g
by
g_{X,Y}=[g_{i,j}]_{i\in X,j\in Y}
. Let
r_{X,Y}(g)
denote the rank of the submatrix
g_{X,Y}
. If
\sigma
is a permutation matrix, then
r_{X,Y}(\sigma)
is simply the number of
1
’s in the submatrix
\sigma_{X,Y}
. We adopt the following notational shorthand: for
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\leq n
, we let
[m,n]=\{m,m+1,\ldots,n\}
, if
m<n
, we let
(m,n]=\{m+1,\ldots,n\}
and
[m,n)=\{m,\ldots,n-1\}
, and we further shorten
[1,n]
to

. Then as a special case of above, for any

and

, define
r_{p,q}(g)=r_{[p],[q]}(g)
and
r_{\geq p,q}(g)=r_{[p,\infty),[q]}(g)
. Note that if
\sigma\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
then
r_{p,q}(\sigma)=|\{n\leq q\mid\sigma(n)\leq p\}|
and
r_{\geq p,q}(\sigma)=|\{n\leq q\mid\sigma(n)\geq p\}|
. The former is the number of

\sigma
, and the latter is the number of
1
’s in the lower left submatrix of
\sigma
with upper right corner
(p,q)
.

Proposition 27
.

Given
\sigma,\tau\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
, we have that
\sigma\leq\tau
if and only if
r_{i,j}(\sigma)\geq r_{i,j}(\tau)
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

To prove (
\Rightarrow
), suppose that
\sigma\leq\tau
and let
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
. Then by definition we have that
\{\sigma(1),\ldots,\sigma(j)\}\leq\{\tau(1),\ldots,\tau(j)\}
. Let
n_{1},\ldots n_{j}
be a rearrangement of the numbers
1,\ldots,j
such that
\sigma(n_{1})<\ldots<\sigma(n_{j})
and similarly define
m_{1},\ldots m_{j}
for
\tau
. Then by definition
\sigma(n_{\ell})\leq\tau(n_{\ell})
for all
1\leq\ell\leq j
.

Now suppose for contradiction that
a:=r_{i,j}(\sigma)<r_{i,j}(\tau):=b
. Then we have that
\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{a}\}=\{n\leq j\mid\sigma(n)\leq i\}
and
\{m_{1},\ldots m_{b}\}=\{m\leq j\mid\tau(m)\leq i\}
. But then we have

\sigma(n_{a+1})\overset{(1)}{>}i\overset{(2)}{\geq}\tau(m_{a+1})

where (1) follows from the definition of
a
, and (2) follows because
a<b
so
a+1\leq b
. This contradicts that
\sigma\leq\tau
, as desired.

Now we prove (
\Leftarrow
). Suppose that
r_{i,j}(\sigma)\geq r_{i,j}(\tau)
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
q\in\mathbb{N}
. As before, let
n_{1},\ldots n_{q}
be a rearrangement of the numbers
1,\ldots,q
such that
\sigma(n_{1})<\ldots<\sigma(n_{q})
, and similarly define
m_{1},\ldots m_{q}
for
\tau
. We prove that
\sigma(n_{\ell})\leq\tau(n_{\ell})
for all
1\leq\ell\leq q
. By hypothesis we have
r_{\tau(m_{\ell}),q}(\sigma)\geq r_{\tau(m_{\ell}),q}(\tau)=\ell
, therefore
n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in\{n\leq q\mid\sigma(n)\leq\tau(m_{\ell})\}
, so in particular we have
\sigma(n_{\ell})\leq\tau(m_{\ell})
. Therefore
\{\sigma(1),\ldots,\sigma(q)\}\leq\{\tau(1),\ldots,\tau(q)\}
. Since
q
was arbitrary, we have that
\sigma\leq\tau
.
∎

Note that for any two permutations
\sigma
and
\tau
, we have that
\text{Supp}(\sigma^{-1}\tau)=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\sigma(n)\neq\tau(n)\}
. If
\sigma\neq\tau
, then
\text{Supp}(\sigma^{-1}\tau)
is nonempty, so it has a minimal element which we call
d(\sigma,\tau)
.

Lemma 25
.

Suppose that
\sigma,\tau\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
are such that
\sigma<\tau
, and let
q=d(\sigma,\tau)
. Then
\sigma(q)<\tau(q)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
27
we must have
r_{\tau(q),q}(\sigma)\geq r_{\tau(q),q}(\tau)
. However since the first
q-1
columns of
\sigma
and
\tau
are the same and
\tau
has a
1
in the
(\tau(q),q)
entry,
\sigma
must have that
\sigma(q)\leq\tau(q)
. But by hypothesis
\sigma
and
\tau
differ in the
q
th column so it must be that
\sigma(q)<\tau(q)
.
∎

Lemma 26
.

Suppose that
\tau\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
and
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
with
p<q
. Then
\tau(p,q)<\tau
if and only if
\tau(p)>\tau(q)
.

Proof.

Let
\rho=\tau(p,q)
. It is clear from the definition of the transposition
(p,q)
that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{p,q\}
, we have
\rho(n)=\tau(n)
.

First we prove (
\Rightarrow
). Suppose that
\tau(p,q)<\tau
. Then
p=d(\tau,\tau(p,q))
and so by Lemma
25
we have that
\tau(q)=\tau(p,q)(p)<\tau(p)
as desired.

Now we prove (
\Leftarrow
). Suppose that
\tau(p)>\tau(q)
. Since the matrix of
\tau(p,q)
is obtained from that of
\tau
by swapping columns
p
and
q
, notice that
r_{i,j}(\tau(p,q))=r_{i,j}(\tau)
as long as at least one of the following conditions is satisfied: (1)
j<p
, (2)
j\geq q
, (3)
i<\tau(q)
, or (4)
i\geq\tau(p)
. If
\tau(q)\leq i<\tau(p)
and
p\leq j<q
, then the submatrix
(\tau(p,q))_{i,j}
is identical to that of
\tau_{i,j}
except with an extra
1
in the
(\tau(q),p)
position, so we have
r_{i,j}(\tau(p,q))=r_{i,j}(\tau)+1
. Therefore for any
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
r_{i,j}(\tau(p,q))\geq r_{i,j}(\tau)
, which implies by Proposition
27
that
\tau(p,q)<\tau
.
∎

Lemma 27
.

If
\sigma,\tau\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
with
\sigma<\tau
, let
p=d(\sigma,\tau)
. Then there exists
q\in\mathbb{N}
with
p<q
such that if
t=(p,q)
then

(1)

\sigma\leq\tau t<\tau
,

(2)

\sigma(p)\leq\tau t(p)<\tau(p)
, and

(3)

\tau t(m)=\tau(m)
for all
m<p
.

Proof.

First of all, we let
r=\tau^{-1}(\sigma(p))
, and we claim that
p<r
. Indeed if
p=r
, then
\sigma^{-1}\tau(r)=p=r
, and hence
p=r\notin\text{Supp}(\sigma^{-1}\tau)
, a contradiction. On the other hand, if
r<p
then
\sigma^{-1}\tau(r)=p\neq r
, hence
r\in\text{Supp}(\sigma^{-1}\tau)
contradicting the minimality of
p
.

Now we note that Lemma
25
implies that
\sigma(p)<\tau(p)
. Therefore we may consider the set
\{n>p\mid\sigma(p)\leq\tau(n)<\tau(p)\}
. As we have just shown,
r>p
and
\tau(r)=\sigma(p)
, so this set contains
r
, and is therefore nonempty. Hence it has a minimal element, which we call
q
. By definition we have
p<q
and also
\tau(p)>\tau(q)
, hence by Lemma
26
it follows that
\tau(p,q)<\tau
. Additionally (3) is clear since
p<q
. Furthermore, by definition of
q
we have
\sigma(p)\leq\tau(q)<\tau(p)
, so (2) follows because
\tau t(p)=\tau(q)
.

Now we claim that
\sigma\leq\tau(p,q)
. On the one hand, since the matrix of
\tau(p,q)
is obtained from that of
\tau
by swapping columns
p
and
q
, notice that
r_{i,j}(\tau(p,q))=r_{i,j}(\tau)
as long as at least one of the following conditions is satisfied: (1)
j<p
, (2)
j\geq q
, (3)
i<\sigma(p)
, or (4)
i\geq\tau(p)
.

Therefore as
\sigma\leq\tau
, for any such
i
and
j
by Proposition
27
we have that
r_{i,j}(\sigma)\geq r_{i,j}(\tau(p,q))
. Now suppose that
\sigma(p)\leq i<\tau(p)
and
p\leq j<q
. We break up the submatrices of
\sigma
and
\tau(p,q)
consisting of the first
i
rows and
j
columns into three disjoint regions: (a)
[1,\sigma(p))\times[j]
, (b)
(\sigma(p),i]\times[1,p)
, and (c)
[\sigma(p),i]\times[p,j]
so that we have

r_{i,j}(\sigma)=r_{[1,\sigma(p)),[j]}(\sigma)+r_{(\sigma(p),i],[1,p)}(\sigma)+r_{[\sigma(p),i],[p,j]}(\sigma).

and similarly for
\tau
and
\tau(p.q)
.

For region (a), we have that
r_{[1,\sigma(p)),[j]}(\tau(p,q))=r_{[1,\sigma(p)),[j]}(\tau)\geq r_{[1,\sigma(p)),[j]}(\sigma)
, where the equality follows because this submatrix satisfies condition (3) from above, and the inequality from the fact that
\sigma\leq\tau
. For region (b), we note that the
m
th columns of
\sigma
,
\tau
, and
\tau(p,q)
are all the same for
1\leq m<p
, hence
r_{[\sigma(p),i],[1,p)}(\sigma)=r_{(\sigma(p),i],[1,p)}(\tau(p,q))
. Finally for region (c),
\sigma
has a
1
in the
(\sigma(p),p)
-entry, hence
r_{[\sigma(p),i],[p,j]}(\sigma)\geq 1
. However by minimality of
q
, we have that
r_{[\sigma(p),i],[p,j]}(\tau)=0
. Since the matrix of
\tau(p,q)
is obtained from that of
\tau
by swapping columns
p
and
q
, we have that
r_{[\sigma(p),i],[p,j]}(\tau(p,q))\leq 1
. Combining our results for all three regions together yields
r_{i,j}(\sigma)\geq r_{i,j}(\tau(p,q))
, hence this inequality holds for every
i
and
j
, so by Proposition
27
, we have that
\sigma\leq\tau(p,q)
.

∎

Corollary 14
.

Suppose that
\sigma,\tau\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
are such that
\sigma<\tau
. There exists transpositions
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
such that
\sigma\leq\tau t_{1}\ldots t_{n}<\ldots<\tau t_{1}\leq\tau
and

.

Proof.

Let

. We repeatedly apply Lemma
27
to obtain transpositions

such that
\sigma\leq\tau t_{1}\ldots t_{n}<\ldots<\tau t_{1}<\tau
and
\sigma(p)=\tau t_{1}\ldots t_{n}(p)<\ldots<\tau t_{1}(p)<\tau(p)
. Then because
\sigma
,
\tau
, and
\tau t_{1}\ldots t_{n}
all agree on all
m<p
, and
\sigma
and
\tau t_{1}\ldots t_{n}
agree at

as well, we have

as desired.
∎

10.
Closure of the Schubert Cells

In this section we define
B:=B_{\mathbb{N}}
. Given
\sigma\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
, let
B\sigma B
denote the image subfunctor of the natural transformation
m_{\sigma}:B\times B\to\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}
defined by sending
b,c\in B(R)
to
b\sigma c
for any
k
-algebra
R
.

If
I
and
J
are sets,
R
is a
k
-algebra, and
x\in\text{Hom}_{I,J}(R)
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\mathfrak{d}_{\ell}(x)
to be the ideal of
R
generated by the
\ell\times\ell
minors of

.

Lemma 28
.

Given any
\sigma\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
, there exist functions
X,Y:\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{P}_{\text{fin}}(\mathbb{N})
such that
X_{i}=[1,\ldots,n_{i}]
and if
i\leq j
then
n_{i}\leq n_{j}
and similarly for
Y
, and such that the natural transformation
m_{\sigma}:B\times B\to\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}
factors through the inclusion map
\text{GL}_{X,Y}\hookrightarrow\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

Let
X(n)=[1,\max\{\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\ldots,\sigma(n)\}]
and
Y=[1,\max\{\sigma^{-1}(1),\sigma^{-1}(2),\ldots,\sigma^{-1}(n)\}]
. Then for any
k
-algebra
R
and any
b,c\in B(R)
, note that the
i
th column of
b\sigma
is the
\sigma(i)
th column of
b
which is upper triangular, therefore if
j>i
then the
i,j
entry of
b\sigma
is zero if
j>\sigma(i)
. Furthermore,
i
th column of
b\sigma c
is obtained by taking linear combinations of columns
1,2,\ldots,i
of
b\sigma
. Therefore the
i,j
entry

is zero as long as
j>[1,\max\{\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\ldots,\sigma(i)\}]
. Hence
b\sigma c\in\text{Mat}_{X}(R)\subseteq\text{End}_{\mathbb{N}}(R)
. A similar argument shows that
(b\sigma c)^{-1}=c^{-1}\sigma^{-1}b^{-1}\in\text{Mat}_{Y}(R)
. Therefore
b\sigma c\in\text{GL}_{X,Y}(R)
, so
m_{\sigma}
factors through
\text{GL}_{X,Y}
.
∎

Definition 7
.

For
\sigma\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
, we say that a pair of functions
X,Y:\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{P}_{\text{fin}}(\mathbb{N})
satisfying the conditions of Lemma
28
is

to

.

Corollary 15
.

(1)

For any
\sigma\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
, there exists functions
X,Y:\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{P}_{\text{fin}}(\mathbb{N})
such that
\overline{B\sigma B}\subseteq\text{GL}_{X,Y}
.

(2)

\overline{B\sigma B}
is affinely representable and hence an fppf-sheaf.

(3)

\overline{B\sigma B}=\overline{\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)}
.

Proof.

(1)

By Lemma
28
, we have that
B\sigma B\subseteq\text{GL}_{X,Y}
which is a closed subfunctor of
\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}
. Therefore
\overline{B\sigma B}\subseteq\text{GL}_{X,Y}
as well.

(2)

Recall that
\text{GL}_{X,Y}
is affinely representable and as discussed in the background section, all closed subfunctors of affinely representable functors are also affinely representable. By Theorem
8
all representable functors are fppf-sheaves.

(3)

For one containment, we have

, and hence
\overline{B\sigma B}\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)}
. For the other containment, by the previous part,
\overline{B\sigma B}
is an fppf-sheaf, and it contains

, hence by Corollary

it contains
\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)
, but it is also closed so it contains

as desired.

∎

Proposition 29
.

A upper triangular matrix

is invertible if and only if
b_{i,i}\in R^{\times}
for all

.

Proof.

To prove (
\Rightarrow

are isomorphisms, so are
\bigwedge^{\ell}(b^{\prime})
and
\bigwedge^{\ell}(c^{\prime})
. Therefore
\bigwedge^{\ell}(b^{\prime})\bigwedge^{\ell}(\pi_{p}g\iota_{q})\bigwedge^{\ell}(c^{\prime})=0
if and only if
\bigwedge^{\ell}(\pi_{p}g\iota_{q})=0
, which holds if and only if all
\ell\times\ell
minors of
\pi_{p}g\iota_{q}
vanish, which holds by definition of
\ell
. Therefore indeed all
\ell\times\ell
minors of
\pi_{p}h\iota_{q}
vanish, so
h\in Y_{\sigma}
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 33
.

Let
k
be a
domain
. For any
\sigma,\tau\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
we have that
B\tau B\subseteq Y_{\sigma}
if and only if
\tau\leq\sigma
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\tau\leq\sigma
. Since
\sigma\in Y_{\sigma}
, by Lemma
30
we have that
B\sigma B\subseteq Y_{\sigma}
. Then because
Y_{\sigma}
is a closed subfunctor, this implies
\overline{B\sigma B}\subseteq Y_{\sigma}
, and hence by Corollary
17
, we obtain
B\tau B\subseteq Y_{\sigma}
.

Conversely, suppose that
B\tau B\subseteq Y_{\sigma}
. Then for any
k
-algebra
R
, we have that
\tau\in Y_{\sigma}(R)
, which implies that for any
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\ell>r_{\geq p,q}(\sigma)
, we have that all
\ell\times\ell
minors of
\pi_{p}\tau\iota_{q}
vanish. This implies that
r_{\geq p,q}(\tau)\leq r_{\geq p,q}(\sigma)
and hence
r_{p,q}(\tau)\geq r_{p,q}(\sigma)
. Since
p
and
q
were arbitrary, by Proposition
27
it follows that
\tau\leq\sigma
.
∎

Proposition 34
.

Suppose that
f:R^{n}\to R^{\oplus\mathbb{N}}
is an
R
-linear map. Then all
\ell\times\ell
minors of
f
vanish if and only if
\bigwedge^{\ell}f=0
.

Proof.

Denote the standard bases of
R^{n}
and
R^{\oplus\mathbb{N}}
by
\{d_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}
and
\{e_{j}\mid j\in\mathbb{N}\}
respectively. Then a basis for
\bigwedge^{\ell}(R^{n})
is
\{d_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge d_{i_{\ell}}\mid 1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell}\leq n\}
and a basis for
\bigwedge^{\ell}(R^{\oplus\mathbb{N}})
is
\{e_{j_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{j_{\ell}}\mid 1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{\ell}\}
. In particular these exterior powers are both free, so we can compute the matrix of
\bigwedge^{\ell}f
with respect to them. The entry of this matrix in the
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}),(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})
position is precisely the
\ell\times\ell
minor
\det_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}\},\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\}}f
. It therefore follows that all
\ell\times\ell
minors of
f
vanish if and only if
\bigwedge^{\ell}f=0
.
∎

Proposition 35
.

Let
k
be a
domain
. Let
\sigma\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
. Then
Y_{\sigma}
is covered by the subfunctors
B\tau B
for
\tau\leq\sigma
.

Proof.

We have just shown (Proposition
33
) that
B\tau B\subseteq Y_{\sigma}
for all
\tau\leq\sigma
. Now suppose that
A
is a
k
-algebra which is a field and let
g\in Y_{\sigma}(A)
. Since
A
is a field, by the Bruhat decomposition we have that
g=b\tau c
for
b,c\in B(A)
and
\tau\in\text{Perm}(\mathbb{N})
. By Lemma
30
, it follows that
\tau\in Y_{\sigma}(A)
hence by the proof of Proposition
33
, it follows that
\tau\leq\sigma
. Therefore
g\in B\tau B(A)
as desired.
∎

10.1.
Closure in the Quotient

We now turn our attention to the closure of the Schubert cells in the quotient
\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}/B
. Suppose that
k
is a
domain
. We call the closure
\overline{\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)/B}\subseteq\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}/B
a
Schubert subfunctor
(in the finite-dimensional case it is called a
Schubert variety
). Suppose that
\mathcal{X}
is a closed subfunctor of
\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}/B
containing
\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)/B
. Then
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{X})
is a closed subfunctor of
\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}
which contains
B\sigma B
, where
\pi:\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}\to\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}/B
denotes the fppf quotient map. Hence by Corollary
17
, if
\tau\leq\sigma
then we have that
\overline{\operatorname{sh}(B\tau B)}=\overline{B\tau B}\subseteq\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{X})
. It therefore follows that
\operatorname{sh}(B\tau B)/B
is contained in
\operatorname{sh}(\mathcal{X})
. But the latter is equal
\mathcal{X}
because
\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}/B
is an fppf sheaf and closed subfunctors of fppf sheaves are again fppf sheaves (Corollary
3
). Since
\mathcal{X}
was an arbitrary closed subfunctor containing
\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)/B
, it follows that if
\tau\leq\sigma
then
\operatorname{sh}(B\tau B)/B\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)/B}
. The proof of the following proposition is trivial.

Proposition 36
.

Y_{\sigma}/B
is a closed subfunctor of
\text{GL}_{\mathbb{N}}/B
.

It follows from Proposition
36
that
\overline{\operatorname{sh}(B\sigma B)/B}\subseteq Y_{\sigma}/B
. The proof of the following result is similar to that of Proposition
35
. It is an easy application of the Bruhat decomposition.

As a consequence of this we obtain our closure result.
