The Traveling Salesman Theorem for Jordan Curves in Hilbert Space
Jared Krandel

Abstract
Given a metric space X X , an Analyst’s Traveling Salesman Theorem for X X gives a quantitative relationship between the length of a shortest curve containing any subset E ⊆ X E\subseteq X and a multi-scale sum measuring the “flatness” of E E . The first such theorem was proven by Jones for X = ℝ 2 X=\mathbb{R}^{2} and extended to X = ℝ n X=\mathbb{R}^{n} by Okikiolu, while an analogous theorem was proven for Hilbert space, X = H X=H , by Schul. Bishop has since shown that if one considers Jordan arcs, then the quantitative relationship given by Jones’ and Okikioulu’s results can be sharpened. This paper gives a full proof of Schul’s original necessary half of the traveling salesman theorem in Hilbert space and provides a sharpening of the theorem’s quantitative relationship when restricted to Jordan arcs analogous to Bishop’s aforementioned sharpening in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} .

Introduction

Given a metric space
X
and a set
E\subseteq X
, how can one tell if there is a curve
\gamma
of finite length containing
E
? If one does exist, how can one estimate its length in terms of the geometry of
E
, and how can one construct such a curve with length as short as possible? The problem of answering these questions in
X
is commonly referred to as the
Analyst’s Traveling Salesman Problem
for
X
. The study of these problems began when Peter Jones introduced and solved the problem in the standard Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}

[
Jon90
]
. Okikiolu later extended the result to curves in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
Oki92
]
, and Schul managed to give an analogue of Okikiolu’s and Jones’s results in Hilbert space
H

[
Sch07b
]
. Full solutions have been given for sets in Carnot groups
[
Li22
]
and graph inverse limit spaces
[
DS16
]
, and for Radon measures in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
BS17
]
and Carnot groups
[
BLZ22
]
. Partial results are also available in Banach spaces
[
BM22a
]
,
[
BM22b
]
and general metric spaces
[
Hah05
]
,
[
DS21
]
.

Many authors have also studied traveling salesman-type problems for higher-dimensional sets. This includes Hölder curves
[
BNV19
]
,
[
BZ20
]
,
C^{1,\alpha}
surfaces
[
Ghi20
]
, lower content
d
-regular sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
AS18
]
and Hilbert space
[
Hyd21
]
, analogues of Jordan curves in higher dimensions
[
Vil20
]
, and even general sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
Hyd22
]
. Many of these approaches are closely tied to results on parameterization of Reifenberg flat-type sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
DT12
]
and Banach spaces
[
ENV19
]
.

One of the central matters in traveling salesman problems is finding a specific quantitative relationship between the Hausdorff measure of the set in question and some measure of its local geometry. The traditional traveling salesman theorems in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\ell_{2}
provide a relationship which holds for general subsets of the ambient space. Therefore, it seems plausible that one could find a tighter relationship when one restricts their attention to a more geometrically regular class of subsets. A result in this direction was recently achieved by Bishop
[
Bis22
]
as part of his study of Weil-Petersson curves
[
Bis20
]
. His result is a sharpening of this quantitative relationship for the class of Jordan arcs in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Bishop posed a natural question: Does a similar relationship exist for Jordan arcs in Hilbert space?

This paper has two primary goals: First, provide a full proof of Schul’s necessary condition in the Hilbert space traveling salesman theorem, filling in gaps and correcting errors present in the original presentation in
[
Sch07b
]
. Second, we answer Bishop’s question in the affirmative by providing an analogous sharpening of the Hilbert space traveling salesman theorem when restricted to Jordan arcs.

The proof of our analogue of Bishop’s result differs significantly from Bishop’s proof because the latter relies heavily on dimension-dependent estimates. We use dimension-independent pieces of Bishop’s argument where possible, but largely focus on implementing an extension of the Hilbert space methods introduced in
[
Sch07b
]
. The first goal is motivated by the discovery of several technical errors in Schul’s original proof as presented in
[
Sch07b
]
. The proof presented here has largely the same outline and general structure as Schul’s proof while implementing several new ideas to correct the identified errors. We also mention that the work on the traveling salesman problem in Banach spaces by Badger and McCurdy
[
BM22a
]
,
[
BM22b
]
provides another proof of the Hilbert space necessary condition via methods which diverge more significantly from ours and those of Schul’s original proof.

1.1
Overview

Jones’ solution to the traveling salesman problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is based on measuring how close a subset
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is to being linear locally. In order to do this, he defined what is now called
Jones’ beta number
.

Definition 1.1

(
Jones’ beta number
)
.

Fix a Hilbert space
H
and let
E,Q\subseteq H
where
Q
has finite diameter. We define the
\beta
-number for
E
in the “window”
Q
by

\beta_{E}(Q)\vcentcolon=\inf_{L}\sup_{x\in Q\cap E}\frac{\text{dist}(x,L)}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)},

where
L
ranges over all affine lines in
H
.

One can interpret the number
\beta_{E}(Q)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)
as the radius of the minimal width cylinder in
H
which contains the set
E\cap Q
. The factor of
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)
on the right-hand side in the definition ensures that
\beta_{E}(Q)
is scale-invariant. We always have
0\leq\beta_{E}(Q)\leq 1
where
\beta_{E}(Q)=0
implies
E\cap Q\subseteq L
for some line
L
while
\beta_{E}(Q)\geq\epsilon
for some constant
\epsilon>0
implies that for every choice of
L
, there exists a point in
E\cap Q
of distance
\epsilon\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)
from
L
. Jones and Okikiolu used these numbers to characterize subsets of rectifiable curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
respectively in the following theorem:

Theorem 1.2
.

(Jones for
n=2

[
Jon90
]
, Okikiolu for
n\geq 2

[
Oki92
]
)
Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
E
is contained in a rectifiable curve if and only if

\beta_{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\vcentcolon=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(E)+\sum_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\beta_{E}(3Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)<\infty

where
\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the set of all dyadic cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
3Q
is the cube with the same center as
Q
but three times the side length. If
\Sigma
is a connected set of shortest length containing
E
, then

\beta_{\Sigma}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\lesssim_{n}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)

(1.1)

and

\beta_{E}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\gtrsim_{n}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma).

(1.2)

It is important to take
3Q
rather than
Q
so that the family
\{3Q\}_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
“covers”
\mathbb{R}^{n}
sufficiently well. More precisely, any subset
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is contained in a cube
3Q
of comparable diameter, while there may not exist such a standard dyadic cube
Q
with this property. The exponent
2
appears in Theorem
1.2
because the Pythagorean theorem allows one to estimate the difference in length between a line segment and a slight perturbation of the segment by a small distance
d
in a perpendicular direction by a factor proportional to
d^{2}
(See Remark
1.3
). We recommend the reader sees the introduction of
[
BM22a
]
for further intuition on the behavior of
\beta
-numbers for subsets of rectifiable curves in
H
(and in Banach spaces).

Remark 1.3

(The Pythagorean theorem and triangle inequality excess)
.

Figure 1.1:

Let
x,y,z\in\Sigma
. Applying the Pythagorean theorem in Figure
1.1
gives
d^{2}=c_{i}^{2}-a_{i}^{2}=(c_{i}-a_{i})(c_{i}+a_{i})
for
i=1,2
. If we assume that
R>0
is such that
c_{i}\simeq a_{i}\simeq R
, then

2d^{2}=(c_{1}-a_{1})(c_{1}+a_{1})+(c_{2}-a_{2})(c_{2}+a_{2})\simeq 2R(c_{1}+c_{2}-a_{1}-a_{1}).

If we further assume that there exists a dyadic cube
Q
such that
x,y,z\in 3Q,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\simeq R
, and
d\simeq\beta_{\Sigma}(Q)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)
, then

\beta_{\Sigma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\simeq c_{1}+c_{2}-a_{1}-a_{2}=|x-y|+|y-z|-|x-z|.

This final equality demonstrates why beta numbers are often said to measure the
triangle inequality excess
inside a cube
3Q
.

Bishop’s results say that if we restrict our attention to
Jordan arcs
then (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) can be improved.

Definition 1.4

(
Jordan arcs and curves
)
.

For a metric space
X
, we say that
\Gamma\subseteq X
is a
rectifiable arc
if
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma)<\infty
and there exists a surjective continuous map
\gamma:I\rightarrow\Gamma
for some closed interval
I\vcentcolon=[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. We also refer to the map
\gamma
as a rectifiable arc. Whenever we refer to such
\Gamma
, we implicitly have a particular parameterizing map
\gamma
in mind and really mean the pair
(\Gamma,\gamma)
. We refer to the points
\gamma(a),
and
\gamma(b)
as the
endpoints
of
\gamma
(or
\Gamma
) and we define

\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)\vcentcolon=\mathrm{crd}(\gamma)\vcentcolon=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(\gamma(a),\gamma(b)).

We refer to
\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)
as the
chord length
of
\Gamma
and refer to the line (segment) which passes through the two endpoints of
\Gamma
as its
chord
or its
chord line (segment)
. We additionally define

\ell(\Gamma)\vcentcolon=\ell(\gamma)

as the length of
\Gamma
(also see
1.14
). We call
\Gamma
a
Jordan arc
if we can take
\gamma
to be bijective. We call
\Gamma
a
Jordan curve
if there exists a map
\gamma:[a,b]\rightarrow\Gamma
which is injective on
[a,b)
, but has
\gamma(a)=\gamma(b)
. In general, any rectifiable arc
\gamma
with
\gamma(a)=\gamma(b)
is called
closed
. For Jordan arcs and curves
\ell(\Gamma)=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma)
, but this does not hold for general rectifiable arcs (for more on this, see Remark
1.12
).

The chord length is an integral part of Bishop’s following improvement:

Theorem 1.5

(
[
Bis22
]
Theorem 1.2)
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a Jordan arc. Then

\sum_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\beta_{\Gamma}(3Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\simeq_{n}\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma).

(1.3)

This result has the following corollary for Jordan curves:

Corollary 1.6

(
[
Bis22
]
Corollary 1.3)
.

If
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Jordan curve, then

\sum_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\beta_{\Gamma}(3Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\simeq_{n}\ell(\Gamma).

One can think of deriving this by applying Theorem
1.5
while taking
\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)\rightarrow 0
. The significance of the inequalities in (
1.3
) is easier to see if we compare each directly with its corresponding inequality in Theorem
1.2
.

Remark 1.7

(The
\lesssim
improvement in (
1.3
))
.

The inequality (
1.1
) and the
\lesssim
direction of (
1.3
) applied to a Jordan arc
\Gamma
are respectively equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\beta_{\Gamma}(3Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(n)\ell(\Gamma),

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\beta_{\Gamma}(3Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}(n)(\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma))

(1.5)

for some constants
C_{1}(n),C_{2}(n)>0
depending on
n
where we used the fact that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\Gamma)\leq\ell(\Gamma)
. This means (
1.3
) improves the inequality by replacing
\ell(\Gamma)
with
\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)
on the right-hand side. One can see how this improvement manifests by considering the case when
\Gamma
is a line segment. In this case,
\beta_{\Gamma}(3Q)=0
for all
Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})
while
\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)=\ell(\Gamma)
. This means (
1.4
) becomes
0\leq C_{1}(n)\ell(\Gamma)
while (
1.5
) becomes
0\leq 0
. Bishop’s improvement pulls the slack out of Jones and Okikiolu’s inequality by recognizing that
\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)
is a global measure that accounts for how much
\Gamma
looks like its chord segment: the beta numbers do not see the “component” of
\Gamma
along its chord.

Remark 1.8

(The
\gtrsim
improvement in
1.3
)
.

The inequality (
1.2
) and the
\gtrsim
direction of (
1.3
) applied to a Jordan arc
\Gamma
and
E\subseteq\Gamma
are respectively equivalent to

\displaystyle(1+\delta)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(E)+C(\delta,n)\sum_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\beta_{E}(3Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

\displaystyle\geq\ell(\Gamma),

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)+C_{2}(n)\sum_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\beta_{\Gamma}(3Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

\displaystyle\geq\ell(\Gamma).

(1.7)

where in (
1.6
),
\delta>0
is arbitrary but
C(\delta,n)\rightarrow\infty
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
. This is not exactly as was stated in (
1.2
), but Jones shows that one can arrange the inequality this way. This means (
1.7
) changes (
1.6
) by replacing the
C_{1}(n)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(E)
term on the left-hand side with
\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)
and exchanges the
\beta_{E}
numbers for the (larger)
\beta_{\Gamma}
numbers.

Bishop
[
Bis22
]
shows how this change manifests by considering the example set
E=\{0,1,i\beta\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
with
0<\beta\ll 1
. One can calculate that
\sum_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\beta_{E}(3Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\leq C\beta^{2}
while
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(E)=\sqrt{1+\beta^{2}}\leq 1+c\beta^{2}
and the shortest curve
\Gamma=[i\beta,0]\cup[0,1]
containing
E
satisfies
\ell(\Gamma)=1+\beta
. By taking
\beta\rightarrow 0
, this configuration gives a family of examples showing that one cannot take
\delta=0
in (
1.6
). The only cubes
Q
which contribute to
\sum\beta_{E}(3Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)
are those for which
E\subseteq 3Q
because
E
only contains three points total. But, for any cube
Q
which contains
0
,
\beta_{\Gamma}(3Q)\gtrsim 1
so that one can show that
\sum\beta_{\Gamma}(3Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\geq 1+c\beta
. The point is that the connectedness of
\Gamma
adds more geometry with more locations and scales to measure curvature at, increasing the contribution of the
\beta
-numbers and loosening the requirement on the
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(E)
term.

In each of the previously stated traveling salesman theorems, the implicit constants in the given inequalities increase exponentially as
n\rightarrow\infty
. The constants’ exponential blowup can be attributed to the exponential increase in the relative number of dyadic cubes on each scale as
n
increases. To formulate a version of the traveling salesman theorem in Hilbert space
H
, Schul invented a replacement for the set of dyadic cubes called a
multiresolution family
.

Definition 1.9

(
Multiresolution family
)
.

Fix a connected set
\Sigma\subseteq H
. For
\epsilon>0
, we call a set
E\subseteq\Sigma
an
\epsilon
-net
of
\Sigma
if both

(i)

For all
x,y\in E
,
|x-y|>\epsilon
,
and

(ii)

\Sigma\subseteq\bigcup_{x\in E}B(x,\epsilon)

Any subset satisfying
(i)
can be extended to an
\epsilon
-net since it can be extended to satisfy
(ii)
by adding a maximal number of appropriately spaced points. Fixing an integer
n_{0}
, let
X_{n_{0}}\subseteq\Sigma
be a
2^{-n_{0}}
-net. The extension property implies the existence of a sequence of
2^{-n}
-nets
\{X_{n}\}
satisfying
X_{n}\subseteq X_{n+1}
. Fix a constant
A>1
and put

\mathscr{H}\vcentcolon=\{B(x,A2^{-n}):x\in X_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{Z},\ n\geq n_{0}\}

(1.8)

where
B(x,A2^{-n})
is the closed ball of radius
A2^{-n}
around
x
. We call
\mathscr{H}
a
multiresolution family
for
\Sigma
and refer to an element
Q\in\mathscr{H}
as a ball. Given
Q=B(x,A2^{-n})
, define
x_{Q}\vcentcolon=x
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{rad}(Q)\vcentcolon=A2^{-n}
. For
\lambda>0
, we let
\lambda Q\vcentcolon=B(x_{Q},\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{}}{rad}(Q))
. The starting point
n_{0}
is of no consequence; all of the results obtained will be independent of it.

Remark 1.10

(Reduction to
H=\ell_{2}
)
.

If
\Sigma\subseteq H
is a closed, connected set with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)<\infty
, then
\Sigma
is compact and hence a separable subset of
H
. This gives the existence of a countable set of vectors
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots
such that
\Sigma\subseteq\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{span}\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots\}}=\vcentcolon V
. Hence,
\Sigma
is contained in the separable subspace
V\subseteq H
which is isometric (via a linear transformation) to
\ell_{2}
. Therefore, it suffices to fix
H=\ell_{2}
in the following theorems concerning Hilbert space.

The important difference between a multiresolution family and the set of dyadic cubes is that the former is centered on the set, while the latter is a partition of the ambient space. In infinite dimensional space (and general metric spaces), it is necessary to concentrate on the intrinsic properties of the set in question rather than where the set happens to lie relative to pre-defined pieces of the ambient space. We can now state Schul’s result:

Theorem 1.11

(
[
Sch07b
]
Theorem 1.1, Theorem 1.5)
.

Let
E\subseteq\ell_{2}
and let
\mathscr{H}
be a multiresolution family for
E
with inflation factor
A>200
. Then
E
is contained in a rectifiable curve if and only if

\beta_{E}^{2}(\mathscr{H})\vcentcolon=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(E)+\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{E}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)<\infty.

If
\Sigma\subseteq H
is a connected set of shortest length containing
E
, then

\beta_{\Sigma}^{2}(\mathscr{H})\lesssim_{A}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)

(1.9)

and

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)\lesssim_{A}\beta_{E}^{2}(\mathscr{H}).

(1.10)

The exponent 2 can again be attributed to the fact that the Pythagorean theorem holds in Hilbert space. The inflation factor
A
given in the definition of
\mathscr{H}
is the analogue of taking
3Q
rather than
Q
in the Euclidean space traveling salesman theorems. Schul’s proof of (
1.10
) closely parallels Jones’ constructive proof of (
1.2
), replacing dimension-dependent estimates with dimension-independent estimates needed. We mention here that Badger, Naples, and Vellis provide a refined constructive proof of this result in
[
BNV19
]
which produces a nice sequence of parameterizations which they used to prove traveling salesman sufficient conditions for Hölder curves.

On the other hand, Schul’s proof of (
1.9
) differs significantly from Jones’s original proof, incorporates some key ideas from Okikiolu’s proof in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and introduces several ingenious new constructions to remove dimension-dependent estimates. Unfortunately, several errors have since been discovered in the original presentation in
[
Sch07b
]
, leaving gaps in the proof. The results of this paper will fill in these gaps, providing a full proof of (
1.9
) in parallel with the following new results:

Theorem A
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\ell_{2}
be a Jordan arc. For any multiresolution family
\mathscr{H}
associated to
\Gamma
with inflation factor
A>200
, we have

\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\lesssim_{A}\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma).

(1.11)

The second main result is the other side of the inequality in Theorem
1.5
for
\ell_{2}
:

Theorem B
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\ell_{2}
be a Jordan arc. For any multiresolution family
\mathscr{H}
associated to
\Gamma
with inflation factor
A>30
, we have

\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\gtrsim_{A}\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma).

(1.12)

These results are to Hilbert space what Bishop’s Theorem
1.5
is to Euclidean space. One can again look to Remarks
1.7
and
1.8
to gain intuition about the nature of these improvements over the estimates in Theorem
1.11
.

Remark 1.12

(General rectifiable arcs)
.

Theorems
A
and
B
raise a natural question: Do similar results hold for general rectifiable arcs? In this case, we must be careful about the definitions. If
\gamma:[0,\ell(\gamma)]\rightarrow\Sigma
is any constant arc length parameterization of a compact, connected set
\Sigma\subseteq\ell_{2}
, then we interpret
\ell
as the pushforward of Lebesgue measure onto
\Sigma
which does not necessarily coincide with
\mathcal{H}^{1}|_{\Sigma}
as it does for a Jordan arc or curve. If
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a rectifiable arc, then
\ell(\Gamma^{\prime})\geq\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{\prime})
, so Theorem
A
is weaker than the more natural inequality

\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\lesssim_{A}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{\prime})-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma^{\prime}).

(1.13)

Whether or not (
1.13
) holds remains open. In Remark
4.39
we give some ideas on how one might modify some of our methods in this direction.

1.2
Related Results and Questions

1.2.1
Weil-Petersson Curves

Theorem
1.5
arose as an improvement to the traveling salesman theorem necessary to connect some of the geometric characterizations of
Weil-Petersson
curves discovered in
[
Bis20
]
(a few out of 26 total definitions given!). The Weil-Petersson curves are defined to be the closure of smooth curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in the Weil-Petersson metric on universal Teichmüller space introduced in
[
TT06
]
by Takhtajan and Teo for studying problems related to string theory. This class of curves has also been studied in relation to computer vision
[
FKL14
]
,
[
FN17
]
,
[
SM06
]
, and Schramm-Loewner evolutions
[
Wan19b
]
,
[
Wan19a
]
.

The following result gives the aforementioned characterizations when
n=2
. We say that a curve
\Gamma
is
chord-arc
if any two points
x,y\in\Gamma
are connected by a subarc
\gamma\subseteq\Gamma
with
\ell(\gamma)\leq C|x-y|
for some constant
C
independent of
x
and
y
.

Theorem 1.13

(
[
Bis22
]
Theorem 1.4)
.

The following are equivalent for a closed Jordan curve
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},\ n\geq 2
,

(i)

\Gamma
satisfies

\sum_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\beta_{\Gamma}(3Q)^{2}<\infty.

(ii)

\Gamma
is chord-arc, and for any dyadic decomposition of
\Gamma
, the inscribed polygons
\{\Gamma_{n}\}
defined by taking the
n
-th generation points as vertices satisfy

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{n}[\ell(\Gamma)-\ell(\Gamma_{n})]<\infty

with a bound that is independent of the choice of decomposition.

(iii)

\Gamma
has finite Möbius energy. That is,

\mathrm{\mathop{M\ddot{o}b}}(\Gamma)\vcentcolon=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2}}-\frac{1}{\ell(x,y)^{2}}\right)dxdy<\infty

where
\ell(x,y)
is the length of the shortest arc contained in
\Gamma
connecting
x
and
y
and the integration is with respect to arc length measure.

The Möbius energy in
(iii)
was one of several functionals introduced by O’Hara to study knots
[
O’H91
]
,
[
O’H92
]
. One can interpret
(i)
as a bound on the total curvature of the curve
\Gamma
over all locations and scales. The missing factor of
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)
when compared with the sums that appear in the traveling salesman theorems makes this condition much harder to satisfy in general. For instance, a curve satisfying
(i)
cannot have a “corner” (conical type singularity) because this would give an infinite collection of cubes
Q
such that
\beta_{\Gamma}(3Q)\gtrsim 1
. In
(ii)
, a dyadic decomposition is an ordered collection of points contained in
\Gamma
which divide
\Gamma
into
2^{n}
intervals of equal length. We let
\Gamma_{n}
be the polygon with these points as vertices. Hence, we interpret
(ii)
as measuring the rate of convergence of the length of inscribed polygonal approximations to
\Gamma
to the length of
\Gamma
itself. The term
\ell(\gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\gamma)
a subarc
\gamma
can be expected to appear because it measures exactly this form of difference in length.

One of the corollaries of Theorem
1.5
that Bishop uses to prove Theorem
1.13
translates directly to our setting:

Corollary 1.14

(
[
Bis22
]
Corollary 5.2 in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
)
.

If
\Gamma\subseteq\ell_{2}
is a closed Jordan curve and
S\vcentcolon=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}<\infty
, then
\Gamma
is chord-arc, i.e.. any pair of points
z,w\in\Gamma
are connected by a subarc
\gamma
with
\ell(\gamma)\lesssim|z-w|
.

One can check that Bishop’s proof of the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-version is independent of the dimension
n
so that this result follows if one replaces usages of Theorem
1.5
there with Theorem
B
. For more on how the traveling salesman theorem applies to Weil-Petersson curves and related subjects, the reader should see Section 4 of
[
Bis20
]
. The rest of the paper gives connections between these curves and a plethora of objects such as conformal maps, Schwarzian derivatives, quasiconformal mappings, Sobolev spaces, and minimal surfaces in hyperbolic 3-space.

1.2.2
Traveling salesman in Banach spaces

A separate, related branch of research is that of traveling salesman problems in more general metric spaces. Recent success has been achieved by Matthew Badger and Sean McCurdy
[
BM22a
]
,
[
BM22b
]
in attaining traveling salesman-type necessary and sufficient conditions in Banach spaces. Much of their work was inspired by the paper of Edelen, Naber, and Valtorta
[
ENV19
]
which implemented the Reifenberg algorithm in Banach spaces.

Roughly speaking,
[
ENV19
]
gave a Banach space version of Reifenberg’s topological disk theorem
[
Rei60
]
, which states that any subset
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is sufficiently bilaterally close to an affine
k
-dimensional plane at all locations in
\Sigma
and all sufficiently small scales is locally homeomorphic to an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, hence is locally topologically a
k
-dimensional disk. Edelen, Naber, and Valtorta extended this result to infinite-dimensional Banach spaces, and gave a traveling salesman-type application in the form of a structure theorem for measures in Banach spaces (
[
ENV19
]
Theorem 2.1). They give a sufficient condition on a Borel measure
\mu
to be well concentrated around a
k
-dimensional set in terms of the pointwise boundedness of a sum of integral beta numbers
\beta_{\mu}^{k}
which measure how close
\mu
is locally to a
k
-dimensional affine plane. An important aspect of their result which is particularly relevant to Badger and McCurdy’s work is that the exponent on
\beta_{\mu}^{k}
appearing in Edelen, Naber, and Valtorta’s sum differs based on the geometric structure of the Banach space. The exponent
2
appears in the Hilbert space case, but one must make other assumptions on the geometry in more general Banach spaces to say something stronger.

Indeed, Badger and McCurdy use the well-studied notions of
modulus of smoothness
and
modulus of convexity
to estimate the triangle inequality excess (recall Remark
1.3
) from above and below respectively. They apply their results to prove necessary and sufficient conditions in
\ell_{p}
spaces for
1<p<\infty
. A major difference between
\ell_{p},\ p\not=2
and
\ell_{2}
is that the sharp necessary and sufficient conditions they prove in
\ell_{p}
using the standard Jones beta number diverge from one another. One reason this result might be expected is that the triangle inequality excess for orthogonally (in the
\ell_{2}
sense) perturbed vectors differs based on the direction of the perturbed vector.

To illustrate this point, if
e_{1},e_{2}
are standard unit basis vectors for
\ell_{p}
and
0<\delta\ll 1
, then

|e_{1}+\delta e_{2}|_{p}-|e_{1}|_{p}=\left(1+\delta^{p}\right)^{1/p}-1\simeq_{p}\delta^{p}.

On the other hand, suppose we take a “diagonal” vector
v=\frac{1}{2^{1/p}}(e_{1}+e_{2})
and perturb it by the orthogonal (in the
\ell_{2}
sense) vector
w=\frac{1}{2^{1/p}}(e_{1}-e_{2})
. We have

|v+\delta w|_{p}-|v|_{p}=\frac{1}{2^{1/p}}\left((1+\delta)^{p}+(1-\delta)^{p}\right)^{1/p}-1\simeq_{p}\delta^{2}.

The length gain by small orthogonal perturbation in
\ell_{p}
varies depending on the direction of the perturbed vector in contrast to the
\ell_{2}
case.

Theorem 1.15

(
[
BM22a
]
Theorem 1.6)
.

(sharp sufficient conditions in
\ell_{p}
)
Let
1<p<\infty
. If
E\subseteq\ell_{p}
and
S_{E,\min(p,2)}(\mathscr{G})<\infty
for some multiresolution family
\mathscr{G}
for
E
with inflation factor
A_{\mathscr{G}}\geq 240
, then
E
is contained in a curve
\Gamma
in
\ell_{p}
with

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma)\lesssim_{p,A_{\mathscr{G}}}S_{E,\min(p,2)}(\mathscr{G}).

The exponent
\min(p,2)
on beta numbers is sharp.

Theorem 1.16

(
[
BM22a
]
Theorem 1.7)
.

(sharp necessary conditions in
\ell_{p}
)
Let
1<p<\infty
. If
\Sigma\subseteq\ell_{p}
is a connected set and
\mathscr{H}
is a multiresolution family for
\Sigma
with inflation factor
A_{\mathscr{H}}>1
, then

S_{\Sigma,\max(2,p)}(\mathscr{H})\lesssim_{p,A_{\mathscr{H}}}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma).

The exponent
\max(2,p)
on beta numbers is sharp.

Badger and McCurdy’s results give a similar proof of Theorem
1.11
by taking the case
p=2
in their above results.

Remark 1.17

(Banach space Jordan arcs)
.

Given the results of this paper, it is natural to ask whether there is any analogue of Theorems
A
and
B
in
\ell_{p}
. That is, for a Jordan arc
\Gamma\subseteq\ell_{p}
and multiresolution family
\mathscr{H}
for
\Gamma
, could one show that

S_{\Gamma,\max(2,p)}(\mathscr{H})\lesssim_{p,A_{\mathscr{H}}}\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)

or

S_{\Gamma,\min(p,2)}(\mathscr{H})\gtrsim_{p,A_{\mathscr{H}}}\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)

by combining the methods of
[
BM22a
]
,
[
BM22b
]
and those given here? If these inequalities do not hold, can one find a different geometric function of the endpoints of
\Gamma
which could replace
\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)
? What about for general rectifiable arcs?

1.2.3
Traveling salesman in general metric spaces

Some success has also been achieved in the setting of general metric spaces by Hahlomaa
[
Hah05
]
and David and Schul
[
DS21
]
. Because there is no ambient linear structure in a general metric space which one can use to define the standard Jones beta number, the work in metric spaces uses replacements which directly measure the triangle inequality excess. Hahlomaa originally defined a general
metric beta number
using the notion of Menger curvature, but this definition is equivalent to the following given by David and Schul. Let
E
be a metric space,
p\in E
and
r>0
. Let
Q=B(p,r)
and define the
metric beta number
by

\displaystyle\beta_{\infty}^{E}(Q)^{2}\vcentcolon=r^{-1}\sup\{

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(x,y)+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,z)-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(z,x):

\displaystyle x,y,z\in E\cap B(p,r)\text{ and }\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(z,y)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,z)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(z,x)\}.

If
E
is
\ell_{2}
, then this is proportional to the normalized length difference between the line segment
[x,z]
and its perturbed version given by
[x,y]\cup[y,z]
. The exponent
2
is added in the definition as a convention to preserve the form of Theorem
1.2
. Hahlomaa was the first to give a sufficient condition in general metric spaces:

Theorem 1.18

(
[
Hah05
]
Theorem 5.3)
.

Let
E
be a metric space and let
\mathscr{G}
be a multiresolution family for
E
with inflation factor
A\simeq 1
. If

\beta_{\infty}^{E}(\mathscr{G})\vcentcolon=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(E)+\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{G}}\beta_{\infty}^{E}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)<\infty,

then there exists a set
F\subseteq[0,1]
and a surjective Lipschitz map
f:F\rightarrow E
with Lipschitz constant
\mathrm{\mathop{Lip}}(f)\lesssim\beta_{\infty}^{E}(\mathscr{G})
.

See
[
Sch07a
]
Example 3.3.1 for a counterexample to the converse to Hahlomaa’s result in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the
\ell_{1}
metric. Schul notes however that this counterexample is not fully satisfactory, as Hahlomaa’s result can be strengthened, for instance, by defining the metric beta number to be a supremum taken over more restrictive triples. In any case, David and Schul have recently achieved a partial converse to this result. Their result concerns
doubling
metric spaces. We say that a metric space is doubling if there exists a constant
N
such that every ball of radius
r>0
can be covered by at most
N
balls of radius
\frac{r}{2}
.

Theorem 1.19

(
[
DS21
]
Theorem A)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a connected doubling metric space with doubling constant
N
and let
\mathscr{H}
be a multiresolution family for
\Sigma
with inflation factor
A>1
. For every
\epsilon>0
,

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)+\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\infty}^{\Sigma}(Q)^{2+\epsilon}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\lesssim_{\epsilon,A,N}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma).

The authors conjecture that the doubling hypothesis can be dropped by utilizing the techniques of
[
Sch07b
]
.

Remark 1.20

(Metric space Jordan arcs)
.

It would again be interesting to know whether these results could be strengthened in the special case of a Jordan arc. That is, let
\Gamma
be a metric space which is the image of a continuous injective map
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\Gamma
. Suppose
\mathscr{G}
is a multiresolution family for
\Gamma
. Do the naïve inequalities

\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{G}}\beta_{\infty}^{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\gtrsim_{A}\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)

or, for
\Gamma
with doubling constant
N
,

\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{G}}\beta_{\infty}^{\Gamma}(Q)^{2+\epsilon}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\lesssim_{\epsilon,A,N}\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)

hold? As our methods rely heavily on linear structure, these seem further from proof than the proposed extension to Banach space. But even if these do not hold, can one find a different geometric function of the endpoints of
\Gamma
to replace
\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)
in the equations above? What about for general rectifiable arcs?

1.4.1
Parameterizations of finite-length continua and arcs associated to a parameterization in Hilbert space

From this point on, fix a connected, compact set
\Sigma\subseteq\ell_{2}
and a rectifiable Jordan arc
\Gamma\subseteq\ell_{2}
. We are guaranteed that
\Gamma
has an injective arc length parameterization
\gamma:[0,\ell(\Gamma)]\rightarrow\Gamma
by definition. It is a vital fact that we also have access to an arc length parameterization of
\Sigma
. We deduce the existence of this map as a consequence of the more general results on parameterization of finite-length continua in metric spaces carried out by Alberti and Ottolini
[
AO17
]
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
I=[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a closed interval. Following Alberti and Ottolini, for a continuous map
\gamma:I\rightarrow X
(often referred to as a
path
) and a point
x\in X
, define the
multiplicity
of
\gamma
at
x
as

m(\gamma,x)\vcentcolon=\#(\gamma^{-1}(x))

where for any set
A
,
\#A
denotes the cardinality of
A
. We define the
length
of
\gamma
as

\ell(\gamma)=\ell(\gamma,I)\vcentcolon=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i})):n\geq 0,\ t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n},\ t_{j}\in I\text{ for all $j$}\right\}.

(1.14)

Additionally,
\gamma
has
constant speed
if there exists a finite constant
c
such that

\ell(\gamma,[t_{0},t_{1}])=c(t_{1}-t_{0})\text{ for every $[t_{0},t_{1}]\subseteq I$}.

We will refer to
\gamma
as an
arc length
parameterization if
\gamma
has constant speed with
c=1
. We will only consider constant speed parameterizations, and given a fixed parameterization
\gamma:I\rightarrow\Sigma
with constant speed
c
, we define a finite Borel measure
\ell
supported on
\Sigma
by

d\ell\vcentcolon=c\gamma_{*}(dt)

where
\gamma_{*}
denotes the pushforward measure so that
\ell(A)=c\int_{\gamma^{-1}(A)}dt
. Alberti and Ottolini prove the following general parameterization result:

Theorem 1.21

(
[
AO17
]
Theorem 4.4)
.

Let
X
be a connected, compact metric space with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(X)<\infty
. Then there exists a path
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow X
with the following properties:

(i)

\gamma
is closed, Lipschitz, surjective, and has degree zero;

(ii)

m(\gamma,x)=2
for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in X
, and
\ell(\gamma)=2\mathcal{H}^{1}(X)
; and,

(iii)

\gamma
has constant speed, equal to
2\mathcal{H}^{1}(X)
.

See
[
AO17
]
Section 4.1 for the definition of degree zero. (Essentially, the path passes through almost every point thee same number of times in one direction as in the opposite direction.) Fix a multiresolution family
\mathscr{H}
for
\Sigma
, and let
\gamma
be a constant speed parameterization of
\Sigma
, the existence of which is guaranteed by Theorem
1.21
. We will use
\gamma
to properly study the geometry of
\Sigma
inside of the balls of
\mathscr{H}
.

Definition 1.22

(Arcs)
.

We define an
arc

\tau\vcentcolon=\gamma|_{[a,b]}
to be the restriction of
\gamma
to a subinterval
[a,b]\subseteq I
. Given a ball
Q\subseteq\ell_{2}
, we define the family of arcs of
\Sigma
inside
Q
as

\Lambda(Q)\vcentcolon=\{\gamma|_{[a,b]}:[a,b]\subseteq[0,1],\ [a,b]\ \text{is a connected component of }\gamma^{-1}(2Q\cap\Sigma)\}.

These are arcs inside
2Q
which intersect
Q
in the style of
[
BM22a
]
. Fix an arc
\tau
as above. Further following
[
BM22a
]
, we use bold terms to refer to operators acting on arcs and define

\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Domain}\nolimits(\tau)\vcentcolon=[a,b],\ \mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\tau)\vcentcolon=\tau(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Domain}\nolimits(\tau)),\ \mathop{\mathsf{}}{Diam}\nolimits(\tau)\vcentcolon=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\tau)),

\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Edge}\nolimits(\tau)\vcentcolon=[\tau(a),\tau(b)],\ \mathop{\mathsf{}}{Line}\nolimits(\tau)\vcentcolon=\{\tau(a)+t(\tau(b)-\tau(a)):t\in\mathbb{R}\},\ \mathop{\mathsf{}}{Crd}\nolimits(\tau)\vcentcolon=|\tau(a)-\tau(b)|,

\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Start}\nolimits(\tau)\vcentcolon=\tau(a),\ \mathop{\mathsf{}}{End}\nolimits(\tau)\vcentcolon=\tau(b).

where
[\tau(a),\tau(b)]\subseteq\ell_{2}
is the line segment connecting the endpoints of
\tau
, and hence
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Line}\nolimits(\tau)
is the line passing through the endpoints of
\tau
. We will often use the term
arc
to refer to both
\tau
and
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\tau)
, but the referent should be clear from context. If
\xi=\gamma|_{[c,d]}
for
[c,d]\subseteq[a,b]
, then we will often call
\xi
a
subarc
of
\tau
. For two general arcs
\xi
and
\tau
we define shorthand notation by defining (in the sense of logical formulas)

\displaystyle(\xi\subseteq\tau)\vcentcolon=(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Domain}\nolimits(\xi)\subseteq\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Domain}\nolimits(\tau))\text{ and }(x\in\tau)\vcentcolon=(x\in\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\tau)),

and we define

\displaystyle\xi\cap\tau\vcentcolon=\gamma|_{\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Domain}\nolimits(\tau)\cap\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Domain}\nolimits(\xi)}\text{ and }\xi\cup\tau\vcentcolon=\gamma|_{\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Domain}\nolimits(\tau)\cup\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Domain}\nolimits(\xi)}.

If
E\subseteq\ell_{2}
and
\mu
is a Borel measure on
\ell_{2}
then we set

\displaystyle\tau\cap E\vcentcolon=\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\tau)\cap E\text{ and }\mu(\tau)\vcentcolon=\mu(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\tau)).

Definition 1.23

(Almost flat and non-flat arcs)
.

In order to measure the flatness of an arc, we define the
arc beta number

\tilde{\beta}(\tau)\vcentcolon=\sup_{x\in\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\tau)}\frac{\text{dist}(x,\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Edge}\nolimits(\tau))}{\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Diam}\nolimits(\tau)}.

We also set

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Max}\nolimits(\tau)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\{y\in\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\tau):\tilde{\beta}(\tau)\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Diam}\nolimits(\tau)=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Edge}\nolimits(\tau))\}\not=\varnothing,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Drift}\nolimits(\tau)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\tilde{\beta}(\tau)\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Diam}\nolimits(\tau)=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(y,\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Edge}\nolimits(\tau))\text{ for any $y\in\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Max}\nolimits(\tau)$}.

Fix a constant
\epsilon_{2}>0
whose specific value will be set in Section
1.5
Given a ball
Q\in\mathscr{H}
, we define the set of
almost flat arcs
for
Q
as

S(Q)\vcentcolon=\{\tau\in\Lambda(Q):\tilde{\beta}(\tau)\leq\epsilon_{2}\beta_{\Sigma}(Q)\}

and refer to any arc
\tau\in S(Q)
as an
almost flat arc
. We will commonly refer to
\Lambda(Q)\setminus S(Q)
as the set of
non-flat arcs
. For any collection of arcs
\mathscr{T}

\beta_{\mathscr{T}}(Q)\vcentcolon=\beta_{\cup_{\tau\in\mathscr{T}}\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\tau)}(Q),

and for a single arc

.

Considering different configurations of almost flat and non-flat arcs will give us useful ways of classifying balls

. For
\eta\in S(Q)
, we get that
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\eta)
lies

on the scale of

\epsilon_{2}
will be fixed small enough such that this approximation will work well on all small scales relative to

.

Proof.

Proposition 3.14

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
Q=B(x_{Q},R)
be a ball and let

be such that

We will first give a lower bound for the function

, then apply this to a Pythagorean theorem estimate. Let

\sqrt{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-1)\cdots(\frac{1}{2}-n+1)}{n!}x^{n}\geq 1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x^{n}\geq 1-x-\frac{x}{1-x}\geq 1-2x

we define the collection of
children
of
Q
in
T
by

C(Q)\vcentcolon=\{Q^{\prime}\in T:\text{$Q^{\prime}$ is maximal in $T$ such that }U^{c}_{Q^{\prime}}\subsetneq U^{c}_{Q}\}.

We also think of
C^{1}(Q)\vcentcolon=C(Q)
as the first generation descendants of
Q
. Given the set
C^{n}(Q)
for some
n\geq 1
, we define the
n+1
-th generation descendants of
Q
as

C^{n+1}(Q)\vcentcolon=\{Q^{\prime\prime}\in C(Q^{\prime}):Q^{\prime}\in C^{n}(Q)\}.

Because each ball is either a root or its core is contained in the core of some root ball, we have

U_{\mathscr{L}}^{c}\vcentcolon=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{T}^{c}_{\mathscr{L}}}U^{c}_{Q(T)}=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{L}}U^{c}_{Q}.

If
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathscr{Q}_{j}
for some
j,\ 1\leq j\leq J
, then the union above over trees is disjoint. Otherwise it is a union in which each point is contained in at most
J
constituent sets.

We now give the definition of a martingale and relevant notions from probability theory.

Definition 4.6

(Martingales)
.

Given a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
, we define a
filtration
of
\mathcal{A}
to be an increasing sequence
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of sub-
\sigma
-algebras of
\mathcal{A}
. We say that a collection of real-valued random variables
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
\Omega
is a
martingale
with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
if for all
n\geq 0
,

(i)

X_{n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable,

(ii)

\mathbb{E}(X_{n})<\infty
,

(iii)

\mathbb{E}(X_{n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n})=X_{n}
.

where
\mathbb{E}(X_{n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n})
denotes the conditional expectation of
X_{n+1}
with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Importantly, it is well-known that positive martingales converge pointwise almost surely. That is, if
X_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\geq 0
, then there exists a positive random variable
X
such that
X(\omega)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}X_{n}(\omega)
for
\mathbb{P}
almost all
\omega\in\Omega
. We will only consider positive martingales.

Remark 4.7

(Schul’s martingales)
.

Let
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathscr{G}
and form the forest
\mathcal{T}^{c}_{\mathscr{L}}
which gives
\mathscr{L}
a partial order, hence a child-parent structure as defined above. For each
Q\in\mathscr{L}
, Schul constructs a martingale
(w^{n}_{Q})_{n\geq 0}
supported inside
U^{c}_{Q}\cap\Sigma
. We define the remainder

R_{Q}\vcentcolon=U_{Q}\cap\Sigma\setminus\left(\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\in C(Q)}U^{c}_{Q^{\prime}}\cap\Sigma\right)

so that

U^{c}_{Q}\cap\Sigma=\left(\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\in C(Q)}U^{c}_{Q^{\prime}}\cap\Sigma\right)\cup R_{Q}

where the collection
\{U^{c}_{Q^{\prime}}\cap\Sigma\}_{Q^{\prime}\in C(Q)}\cup\{R_{Q}\}
is pairwise disjoint. Applying this partitioning scheme iteratively to each
U^{c}_{Q^{\prime}}\cap\Sigma
in the union above, we see that for any
n\geq 0
, we can write
U^{c}_{Q}\cap\Sigma
as the partition.

U^{c}_{Q}\cap\Sigma=R_{Q}\cup\left(\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\in C^{1}(Q)}R_{Q^{\prime}}\right)\cup\ldots\cup\left(\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\in C^{n}(Q)}R_{Q^{\prime}}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{Q^{\prime}\in C^{n+1}(Q)}U^{c}_{Q^{\prime}}\cap\Sigma\right)

(4.5)

This gives a decomposition of
U^{c}_{Q}\cap\Sigma
into “atoms” at the
(n+1)
-th level, from which we will define a filtration by setting
\mathcal{F}_{n}
to be the sigma algebra generated by
\cup_{k\leq n}\{U^{c}_{Q^{\prime}}\cap\Sigma\}_{Q^{\prime}\in C^{k}(Q)}
. We will form the martingale
(w_{Q}^{n})_{n\geq 0}
by setting
w_{Q}^{0}
to be constant on
U^{c}_{Q}\cap\Sigma
and defining
w_{Q}^{n+1}
by distributing the mass that
w_{Q}^{n}
assigns to
U^{c}_{Q^{\prime}}\cap\Sigma
for any
Q^{\prime}\in C^{n}(Q)
onto its constituent pieces
R_{Q^{\prime}}\cup\bigcup_{Q^{\prime\prime}\in C(Q^{\prime})}U^{c}_{Q^{\prime\prime}}\cap\Sigma=U^{c}_{Q^{\prime}}\cap\Sigma
with weighting factors depending on the size and number of children in
C(Q^{\prime})
and the length of the remainder
R_{Q^{\prime}}
.

Lemma 4.8

(Martingale construction)
.

Fix a constant
D>0,\ c<\frac{1}{4A}
and let
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathscr{G}\cap\mathscr{Q}_{j}
. Suppose that there exists a constant
q<1
such that for any
Q\in\mathscr{L}

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U^{c}_{Q})}{\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in C(Q)}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U^{c}_{Q^{\prime}})+D\ell(R_{Q})}\leq q.

(4.6)

Then, there exists a collection of positive real-valued functions
\{w_{Q}\}_{Q\in\mathscr{L}}
satisfying

(i)

\int_{\Sigma}w_{Q}d\ell=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U^{c}_{Q}),

(ii)

\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{L}}w_{Q}(x)\leq\frac{D}{1-q}\chi_{U^{c}_{\mathscr{L}}}(x)\ \ \text{ for almost every $x\in\Sigma$}
,

(iii)

\text{supp}(w_{Q})\subseteq U^{c}_{Q}\cap\Sigma
.

Proof.

We will suppress the superscript of the cores and write
U_{Q}=U_{Q}^{c}
. Fix
Q\in\mathscr{L}
. For any set
E
and function
w:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we let
w(E)=\int_{E}wd\ell
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\cup_{k\leq n}\{U_{Q^{\prime}}\cap\Sigma\}_{Q^{\prime}\in C^{k}(Q)}
. We construct the function
w_{Q}
as the pointwise limit of a martingale
(w^{n}_{Q})_{n\geq 0}
adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with underlying finite measure
\ell|_{U_{Q}\cap\Sigma}
.
We begin by defining the function
w^{0}_{Q}
:

w^{0}_{Q}(x)\vcentcolon=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})}{\ell(U_{Q}\cap\Sigma)}\ \text{ for any $x\in U_{Q}\cap\Sigma$}.

The martingale sequence will have fixed total mass
w^{0}_{Q}(U_{Q})=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})
. We next define

s_{Q}\vcentcolon=\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in C(Q)}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime}})+D\ell(R_{Q})<\infty.

Given the function
w^{n}_{Q}
, we define
w^{n+1}_{Q}
by readjusting the distribution of mass inside the cores
\{U_{Q^{\prime}}\}_{Q^{\prime}\in C^{n+1}(Q)}
and leaving the remainders
R_{Q^{\prime\prime}}
constant for any ancestor balls
Q^{\prime\prime}\in C^{j}(Q)
for
j<n
. Let
Q^{\prime}\in C^{n}(Q),\ Q^{\prime\prime}\in C(Q^{\prime})\subseteq C^{n+1}(Q)
, and
Q_{0}\in C^{j}(Q)
for some
j<n
. We define
w_{Q}^{n+1}(x)
by declaring
w_{Q}^{n+1}
to be constant on each of
R_{Q_{0}},R_{Q^{\prime}},
and
U_{Q^{\prime\prime}}\cap\Sigma
and imposing

\displaystyle w^{n+1}_{Q}(R_{Q_{0}})

\displaystyle=w^{n}_{Q}(R_{Q_{0}}),

(4.7)

\displaystyle w^{n+1}_{Q}(R_{Q^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=w^{n}_{Q}(U_{Q^{\prime}})\cdot\frac{D\ell(R_{Q^{\prime}})}{s_{Q^{\prime}}},

(4.8)

\displaystyle w^{n+1}_{Q}(U_{Q^{\prime\prime}})

\displaystyle=w^{n}_{Q}(U_{Q^{\prime}})\cdot\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime\prime}})}{s_{Q^{\prime}}}.

(4.9)

We could find the pointwise value for
w^{n+1}
on each set by dividing the three above equations by
\ell(R_{Q_{0}}),\ell(R_{Q^{\prime}}),
and
\ell(U_{Q^{\prime\prime}}\cap\Sigma)
respectively. It follows from the definition that
w_{Q}^{n}(U_{Q})
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
measurable. In order to show that
(w^{n}_{Q})
is a martingale adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})
, we must prove

\mathbb{E}(w_{Q}^{n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n})=w_{Q}^{n}.

It suffices to show that
w^{n+1}_{Q}(U_{Q^{\prime}})=w_{Q}^{n}(U_{Q^{\prime}})
for any
Q^{\prime}\in\cup_{k\leq n}C^{k}(Q^{\prime})
. First, suppose
Q^{\prime}\in C^{n}(Q)
. Then, using (
4.9
) and (
4.8
),

\displaystyle w_{Q}^{n+1}(U_{Q^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=w_{Q}^{n+1}(R_{Q^{\prime}})+\sum_{Q^{\prime\prime}\in C(Q^{\prime})}w_{Q}^{n+1}(U_{Q^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=w^{n}_{Q}(U_{Q^{\prime}})\cdot\frac{D\ell(R_{Q^{\prime}})}{s_{Q^{\prime}}}+\sum_{Q^{\prime\prime}\in C(Q^{\prime})}w^{n}_{Q}(U_{Q^{\prime}})\cdot\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime\prime}})}{s_{Q^{\prime}}}=w_{Q}^{n}(U_{Q^{\prime}}).

On the other hand, if
Q^{\prime}\in C^{k}(Q)
for some
k<n
, we can apply (
4.5
) to
Q^{\prime}
to write
U_{Q^{\prime}}
in terms of the remainders down to level
n-1
and the cores at level
n
inside of
U_{Q^{\prime}}
:

\displaystyle w^{n+1}_{Q}(U_{Q^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}\sum_{Q^{\prime\prime}\in C^{j}(Q^{\prime})}w^{n+1}_{Q}(R_{Q^{\prime}})+\sum_{Q^{\prime\prime}\in C^{n-k}(Q^{\prime})}w_{Q}^{n+1}(U_{Q^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}\sum_{Q^{\prime\prime}\in C^{k}(Q^{\prime})}w^{n}_{Q}(R_{Q^{\prime}})+\sum_{Q^{\prime\prime}\in C^{n-k}(Q^{\prime})}w_{Q}^{n}(U_{Q^{\prime}})=w^{n}_{Q}(U_{Q^{\prime}})

using the previous two cases. This also shows that
w^{n+1}_{Q}(U_{Q})=w_{Q}^{n}(U_{Q})=\ldots=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})
, verifying the finite expectation condition. Hence,
(w_{Q}^{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a positive martingale so that it converges pointwise
\ell
almost everywhere to a function

w_{Q}(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}w_{Q}^{n}(x).

By definition,
\text{supp}(w_{Q})\subseteq\text{supp}(w_{Q}^{0})=U_{Q}\cap\Sigma
and
\int_{Q}w_{Q}d\ell=w_{Q}(U_{Q})=w_{Q}^{0}(U_{Q})=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})
verifying properties
(i)
and
(iii)
above. We now prove
(ii)
. Fix
x\in U_{Q}\cap\Sigma
for which
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}w_{Q}^{n}(x)
exists and suppose that
x\in R_{Q_{N}}\cap U_{Q_{N}}\subset U_{Q_{N-1}}\subset\cdots\subset U_{Q_{0}}=U_{Q}
. Then (
4.9
) and (
4.6
) imply

\displaystyle\frac{w_{Q}(U_{Q_{N}})}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q_{N}})}=\frac{w_{Q}(U_{Q_{N-1}})}{s_{Q_{N-1}}}=\frac{w_{Q}(U_{Q_{N-1}})}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q_{N-1}})}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q_{N-1}})}{s_{Q_{N-1}}}<q\frac{w_{Q}(U_{Q_{N-1}})}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q_{N-1}})}.

Applying this
N
times, we get

\frac{w_{Q}(U_{Q_{N}})}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q_{N}})}<q^{N}\frac{w_{Q}(U_{Q})}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})}=q^{N}.

Therefore, using (
4.8
), we conclude

w_{Q}(x)=\frac{w_{Q}(R_{Q_{N}})}{\ell(R_{Q_{N}})}\leq\frac{w_{Q}(U_{Q_{N}})}{\ell(R_{Q_{N}})}\frac{D\ell(R_{Q_{N}})}{s_{Q_{N}}}\leq D\frac{w_{Q}(U_{Q_{N}})}{s_{Q_{N}}}<Dq^{N}.

In particular, if
x
is contained in an infinite sequence of nested cores, then
w_{Q}(x)=0
for all
Q
. Applying the above calculation for each
Q_{k},\ 0\leq k\leq N
, we see that
w_{Q_{k}}(x)\leq Dq^{N-k}
. . Because
\text{supp}(w_{Q})=U_{Q}\cap\Sigma
, we also know that
\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{L}}\text{supp}(w_{Q})\subseteq U_{\mathscr{L}}
and we can compute

\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{L}}w_{Q}(x)=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{L}}w_{Q}(x)\chi_{U_{Q}}(x)\leq\left(\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{L}}w_{Q}(x)\right)\chi_{U_{\mathscr{L}}}(x)\leq\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Dq^{n}\right)\chi_{U_{\mathscr{L}}}(x)\leq\frac{D}{1-q}\chi_{U_{\mathscr{L}}}(x).

This concludes the proof of
(ii)
.
∎

The goal of this section is to finish the proof of Proposition
4.1

. Finally, in Section

.

4.2.1
New Definitions and Tools

Recall that
\Gamma\subseteq\ell_{2}
is a Jordan arc with an injective arc length parameterization
\gamma:I\rightarrow\Gamma
where we fix
I\vcentcolon=[0,\ell(\Gamma)]
. We assume without loss of generality that the chord line of
\Gamma
is the
e_{1}
-axis. Let
\pi:\ell_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the orthogonal projection onto the
e_{1}
-axis and let
\pi^{\perp}:\ell_{2}\rightarrow\ell_{2}
be the orthogonal projection onto the orthogonal hyperplane to the
e_{1}
-axis. For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
the function
\gamma_{i}(t)\vcentcolon=\langle\gamma(t),e_{i}\rangle
is
1
-Lipschitz, hence differentiable almost everywhere. We let
\gamma_{i}^{\prime}(t)
denote the derivative and write

\gamma(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{i}(t)e_{i}\text{ and }\gamma^{\prime}(t)\vcentcolon=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{i}^{\prime}(t)e_{i}.

The fact that
\gamma
is an arc length parameterization means that
|\gamma^{\prime}(t)|=1
almost everywhere. In particular,
\gamma^{\prime}(t)
gives an almost everywhere well-defined notion of tangent vector to
\Gamma
at
\gamma(t)
. For
x\in\Gamma
, we let
t(x)\in I
be the unique number such that
\gamma(t(x))=x
.

We begin by defining a new measure
\mu\ll\ell
which quantifies how much subsets of
\Gamma
contribute to the value of
\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)
.

Definition 4.18

(
\mu
measure)
.

Let
\rho:I\rightarrow[0,2]
be given by

\rho(t)\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}1-\gamma^{\prime}_{1}(t),\ &\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(t)\ \text{exists}\\
1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Define the finite Borel measure
\mu
supported on
\Gamma
as

d\mu\vcentcolon=\gamma_{*}(\rho\ dt).

where
\mu(A)=\gamma_{*}(\rho\ dt)(A)\vcentcolon=\int_{\gamma^{-1}(A)}\rho(t)dt
is the pushforward of
\rho(t)dt
by
\gamma
.

The definition of
\mu
is motivated by the fundamental theorem of calculus in the following way:

Lemma 4.19
.

Let
a,b\in I
with
a\leq b
. Then

\mu(\gamma|_{[a,b]})=\ell(\gamma|_{[a,b]})-(\pi(\gamma(b))-\pi(\gamma(a))).

In particular,

\mu(\Gamma)=\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma).

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle\mu(\gamma|_{[a,b]})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mu(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\gamma_{[a,b]}))=\int_{\gamma([a,b])}\gamma_{*}(\rho\ dt)=\int_{a}^{b}\rho(t)\ dt=\int_{a}^{b}1-\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle=(b-a)-(\gamma_{1}(b))-\gamma_{1}(a))=\ell(\gamma|_{[a,b]})-(\pi(\gamma(b))-\pi(\gamma(a))).

Setting
a=0
and
b=\ell(\Gamma)
gives
\mu(\Gamma)=\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)
.
∎

Remark 4.20

(Null sets and examples)
.

Fix
x,y\in\Gamma
with
t(x)<t(y)
and suppose
\xi
is a subarc of
\Gamma
such that
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Start}\nolimits(\xi)=x
and
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{End}\nolimits(\xi)=y
. If
\mu(\xi)=0
, then

\ell(\xi)=\pi(y)-\pi(x)=y_{1}-x_{1}.

This forces
y_{1}>x_{1}
and forces
\xi
to be a parameterization of the line segment
[x,y]=[x,x+(y_{1}-x_{1})e_{1}
which is parallel to the chord line of
\Gamma
. Now, suppose
\tau(t)\vcentcolon=x+t\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}
for
t(x)\leq t\leq t(x)+|y-x|
. That is,
\tau
parameterizes
[x,y]\subseteq\Gamma
. In general, we have the formula

\mu(\tau)=\ell(\tau)-(\pi(y)-\pi(x))=|y-x|-(y_{1}-x_{1}).

(4.21)

If
y_{1}<x_{1}
, then
\mu
is larger than
\ell
on
\tau
; this measure assigns “bonus” length to arcs which “backtrack” along the direction of the chord line of
\Gamma
. If
y_{1}>x_{1}
, the right side of (
4.21
) bears resemblance to triangle inequality excess estimates. Indeed, let
x,y,z\in\Gamma
and suppose there exists a subarc
\eta
such that

\eta(t)\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}x+t\frac{y-x}{|y-x|},\ &t(x)\leq t\leq t(x)+|y-x|\\
y+t\frac{z-y}{|z-y|},\ &t(x)+|y-x|t\leq t(x)+|y-x|+|z-y|.\end{cases}

The arc
\eta
injectively parameterizes the line segments
[x,y]
and
[y,z]
. We compute

\mu(\eta)=\ell(\eta)-(\pi(x)-\pi(z))=|x-y|+|y-z|-(z_{1}-x_{1}).

When
x_{1}<y_{1}<z_{1}
, this is something like a triangle inequality excess estimate (see Remark
1.3
) where instead of subtracting the length of the triangle base
[x,z]
, we subtract the length of the projection of
[x,z]
along the chord line of
\Gamma
.

Our goal for proving Theorem
A
is to bound the beta sums above by
\mu(\Gamma)
rather than
\ell(\Gamma)
. Intuitively, this is plausible since
\mu
assigns small measure only to those regions of
\Gamma
which are nearly parallel to the chord and are directed via the parameterization
\gamma
towards the terminal endpoint of
\Gamma
, i.e., have
\gamma^{\prime}_{1}>1-\delta
for
\delta>0
small. One would expect
\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)
to be small on average for
Q
centered in such a region. There is a problem with this definition of
\mu
, however. We would like to have a bound of the form

\mu(U_{Q})\gtrsim\ell(U_{Q})

(4.22)

for individual cores in some family because this would allow a translation of the preceding martingale arguments to this setting. However, such a result cannot hold, as
\mu(U_{Q})=0
may hold even for
U_{Q}
with
\beta_{\Gamma}(U_{Q})\approx 1
(see Figure
4.3
). However, in order for a situation like Figure
4.3
to occur, there must be some “backtracking” arc (given in the figure by the bottom-most horizontal piece of
\Gamma
outside of
U_{Q}
). On this arc,
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}<0
so that
d\mu\geq d\ell
. To recover inequalities like (
4.22
), we will construct a new, larger measure
\tilde{\mu}
that fills in the
\mu
measure gaps in Figure
4.3
by “borrowing” mass from backtracking arcs. We begin by isolating these regions of change as maximal disjoint arcs where
\Gamma
“bends” back on itself along the
e_{1}
axis in the sense that the projection map
\pi
is non-injective. This is made more precise with the following definition.

A core with
\beta_{\Gamma}(U_{Q})\approx 1

Definition 4.21

(Multiplicity)
.

For
t\in I
, define

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{perp}(t)\vcentcolon=\gamma^{-1}(\pi^{-1}(\pi(\gamma(t)))\cap\Gamma).

This is the set of points in
I
which map to points in
\Gamma
that have the same first coordinate as
\gamma(t)
. Define
M:I\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, the multiplicity function, by

M(t)\vcentcolon=\#\mathop{\mathrm{}}{perp}(t).

Additionally, we let
E\vcentcolon=\{t\in I:\pi(\gamma(t))=\min(\pi(\Gamma))\text{ or }\pi(\gamma(t))=\max(\pi(\Gamma))\}
and set

\displaystyle S_{\pi}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\{t\in I:M(t)=1\},

\displaystyle M_{\pi}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\{t\in I:M(t)\geq 2\}\cup E.

S_{\pi}
is the set where
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{perp}(t)=\{t\}
is a singleton while
M_{\pi}
is the set where either
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{perp}(t)
has multiple elements or
\gamma(t)
is an extremal point of
\Gamma
along the
e_{1}
-axis. If one of these latter points is also a member of
S_{\pi}
, then it is isolated by connectedness.

We will add mass to
\mu
by raising the value of
\rho
in a carefully chosen neighborhood of
M_{\pi}
. In order to define this neighborhood, we first provide a decomposition of
M_{\pi}
into the maximal arcs promised above. The following structure lemma for
M_{\pi}
will aid us:

Lemma 4.22
.

Suppose
a,b\in I
with
\pi(\gamma(a))=\pi(\gamma(b))
and
a\leq b
. Then
[a,b]\subseteq M_{\pi}
.

Proof.

Let
r\in(a,b)
. If
\pi(\gamma(r))=\pi(\gamma(a))
, then
r\in M_{\pi}
. Otherwise,
\pi(\gamma(r))\not=\pi(\gamma(a))
and

\inf_{t\in[a,b]}\pi(\gamma(t))\leq\pi(\gamma(r))\leq\sup_{t\in[a,b]}\pi(\gamma(t)).

Since
\pi
is continuous, there exist points
s,u\in[a,b]
on which
\pi\circ\gamma
achieves the infimum and supremum above respectively. Suppose first that
\pi(\gamma(r))=\pi(\gamma(s))
. If there exists
s^{\prime}\in I,\ s^{\prime}\not=r
such that
\pi(\gamma(s^{\prime}))=\pi(\gamma(r))
, then
r\in M_{\pi}
by definition. Otherwise,
\pi(x)>\pi(\gamma(r))
for all
x\in\Gamma\setminus\gamma(r)
so that
\gamma(r)=\min(\pi(\Gamma))\in E\subseteq M_{\pi}
. Therefore, it suffices to consider the case when
\pi(\gamma(r))>\pi(\gamma(s))
. By a similar argument, we can also assume
\pi(\gamma(r))<\pi(\gamma(u))
. Hence, the function
f:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
f(t)=\pi(\gamma(t))-\pi(\gamma(a))
is continuous and satisfies

(i)

f(r)\not=0
,

(ii)

f(s)<f(r)<f(u)
,
and

(iii)

f(a)=f(b)=0
.

We claim that the intermediate value theorem implies the existence of a point
r^{\prime}\in[a,b],\ r\not=r^{\prime}
with
f(r^{\prime})=f(r)
so that
\gamma(r)\in M_{\pi}
. Indeed, suppose without loss of generality that
f(r)>0
. If
u<r
, then
f(a)<f(r)<f(u)
so that there exists such
r^{\prime}\in[a,u]
. Otherwise,
u>r
and
f(b)<f(r)<f(u)
so that there exists such
r^{\prime}\in[u,b]
.
∎

Definition 4.23

(Bends)
.

It follows from Lemma
4.22
that for any
t\in M_{\pi}
, the family

\mathcal{C}_{t}\vcentcolon=\{[a,b]\subseteq I:t\in[a,b]\subseteq M_{\pi}\}

contains a non-degenerate interval so that the union
I_{t}\vcentcolon=\bigcup\mathcal{C}_{t}
is also a non-degenerate interval. The union
\bigcup_{t\in I}I_{t}=M_{\pi}
has countably many disjoint connected components, each of which is a non-degenerate subinterval of
I
(which is possibly open). Thus, the closure
\overline{M}_{\pi}
has connected components that are closed, non-degenerate intervals which we enumerate as
\overline{M}_{\pi}=\bigcup_{k\in K}[s_{k},u_{k}]
. We define

\Phi\vcentcolon=\{\gamma|_{[s_{k},u_{k}]}:k\in K\}.

We refer the elements of
\Phi
as
bends
.

An important fact is that these regions contribute a proportionally large amount of measure to
\mu
.

Lemma 4.24
.

Let
\phi\in\Phi
. Then

\mu(\phi)\geq\frac{1}{2}\ell(\phi).

Proof.

Let
\phi\in\Phi
. Lemma
4.19
implies

\mu(\phi)=\ell(\phi)-\left[\pi(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{End}\nolimits(\phi))-\pi(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Start}\nolimits(\phi))\right].

But
\pi
is at least two-to-one almost everywhere on
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi)\subseteq\overline{M}_{\pi}
, implying
\ell(\phi)\geq 2|\pi(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{End}\nolimits(\phi))-\pi(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Start}\nolimits(\phi))|
.
∎

Lemma
4.24
says that the bends are arcs on which
\mu
measure is globally comparable to length measure. This allows us to promote
\mu
to a bigger measure
\tilde{\mu}
which is pointwise comparable to length inside bends at the cost of increasing the total measure by a bounded factor independent of
\Gamma
. In fact, at the cost of further increasing
\mu
’s mass by a bounded factor, we can take our proposed larger measure
\tilde{\mu}
to be comparable to length on regions of
\Gamma
which extend a distance comparable to
\ell(\phi)
out from
\phi
in the
e_{1}
direction.

Definition 4.25

(
\tilde{\mu}
measure)
.

For any
\phi\in\Phi
, define

\displaystyle N_{\phi}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\{t\in I:\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(\pi(\gamma(t)),\pi(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi)))\leq 100\ell(\phi)\},

and set

N(\Phi)\vcentcolon=\bigcup_{\phi\in\Phi}N_{\phi}.

We define a new weight
\tilde{\rho}:I\rightarrow[0,2]
as

\tilde{\rho}(t)\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}2,\ &t\in\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Domain}\nolimits(\phi)\text{ for some $\phi\in\Phi$}\\
1,\ &t\in N(\Phi)\setminus\bigcup_{\phi\in\Phi}\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Domain}\nolimits(\phi)\\
\rho(t),&t\in I\setminus N(\Phi).\end{cases}

We use the value
2
in the first case so that
\tilde{\rho}(t)\geq\rho(t)
for all
t\in I
. Put

d\tilde{\mu}\vcentcolon=\gamma_{*}(\tilde{\rho}\ dt).

In words,
\tilde{\mu}
is equal to twice length measure on bends, equal to length measure just outside of bends, and equal to
\mu
measure far away from bends.

Looking back at Figure
4.3
, we can see that
\tilde{\mu}(U_{Q})=2\ell(U_{Q})
because
\Gamma\cap U_{Q}
is contained in a single bend
\phi
. We will use
\tilde{\mu}
as our primary accounting tool for bounding the beta-squared sum from above. We begin by verifying that the total mass of
\tilde{\mu}
is controlled by the total mass of
\mu
.

Lemma 4.26
.

\tilde{\mu}(\Gamma)\lesssim\mu(\Gamma).

Proof.

Fix
\phi\in\Phi
and consider the region

U_{\phi}\vcentcolon=\gamma\left(N_{\phi}\setminus\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi\\
\phi^{\prime}\not=\phi\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi^{\prime})\right)

Observe that we can bound the mass added in
U_{\phi}
as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(U_{\phi})-\mu(U_{\phi})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}(U_{\phi}\setminus\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi))+\tilde{\mu}(\phi)-\left[\mu(U_{\phi}\setminus\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi))+\mu(\phi)\right]

\displaystyle=\ell(U_{\phi}\setminus\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi))-\mu(U_{\phi}\setminus\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi))+2\ell(\phi)-\mu(\phi)

\displaystyle\leq 2\ell(\phi)+\int_{U_{\phi}\setminus\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi)}\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(t)\ dt

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\pi(U_{\phi}\setminus\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi)))+2\ell(\phi)\leq 200\ell(\phi)+2\ell(\phi)\leq 404\mu(\phi)

where the final inequality follows from Lemma
4.24
. The fact that
\tilde{\rho}(t)\geq\rho(t)
for all
t\in I
implies
\tilde{\mu}-\mu
is a positive measure so that, because
\tilde{\rho}(t)=\rho(t)
for all
t\in I\setminus N(\Phi)
and
N(\Phi)=\bigcup_{\phi\in\Phi}\gamma^{-1}(U_{\phi})
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(\Gamma)-\mu(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\mu}\left(\bigcup_{\phi\in\Phi}U_{\phi}\right)-\mu\left(\bigcup_{\phi\in\Phi}U_{\phi}\right)=(\tilde{\mu}-\mu)\left(\bigcup_{\phi\in\Phi}U_{\phi}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\phi\in\Phi}(\tilde{\mu}-\mu)(U_{\phi})=\sum_{\phi\in\Phi}\tilde{\mu}(U_{\phi})-\mu(U_{\phi})\leq 404\sum_{\phi\in\Phi}\mu(\phi)\leq 404\mu(\Gamma).\qed

The final lemma we will prove in this section gives sufficient conditions for an inequality like
\tilde{\mu}(B)\gtrsim\ell(B)
to hold for any Borel set
B
. The fact that
\tilde{\mu}
is comparable to
\ell
on
\gamma(M_{\pi})
means that in order for
\tilde{\mu}(B)\ll\ell(B)
to hold, most of
\Gamma\cap B
must be contained in
\gamma(S_{\pi})
. Even then, it must be true that
\tilde{\rho}\ll 1
on most of
\Gamma\cap B
so that
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}\approx 1
inside
B
, constraining the total amount of length that
B
is allowed to contain. Lemma
4.28
proves a sort of contrapositive of this observation, showing that a lower bound on
\ell(B)
translates into a lower bound on
\tilde{\mu}(B)
in terms of
\ell(B)
. First, we will need a version of the area formula:

Lemma 4.27
.

(Area formula)
Let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz map and let
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an integrable function. Then the map

z\mapsto\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(\{z\})}g(x),

is measurable, and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(y)|f^{\prime}(y)|dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(\{z\})}g(x)dz.

For proof of this result, see
[
Fed69
]
Theorem 3.2.5.

Lemma 4.28
.

Fix
\delta<\frac{1}{10}
and let
B\subseteq\ell_{2}
be Borel. If either

(i)

\ell(B)\geq(1+\delta)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(B),

or

(ii)

\tilde{\mu}(B)\geq\delta\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(B)
,

then,

\tilde{\mu}(B)\geq\frac{\delta^{3}}{2}\ell(B).

Proof.

We first prove that
(i)
implies the conclusion. Consider the set
E_{\delta^{2}}=\{t\in I:\tilde{\rho}(t)<\delta^{2}\}\cap\gamma^{-1}(B)
. Observe that
\tilde{\rho}(t)<\delta^{2}
implies
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(t)>1-\delta^{2}
so that
E_{\delta^{2}}\subseteq S_{\pi}
. The former inequality directly implies
t\in S_{\pi}
so that
E_{\delta^{2}}\subseteq S_{\pi}
. Applying the area formula (Lemma
4.27
) with the Lipschitz function
\gamma_{1}:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and the integrable function
\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}\chi_{E_{\delta^{2}}}
gives

\int_{E_{\delta^{2}}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}|\gamma_{1}^{\prime}|\ dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{s\in\gamma_{1}^{-1}(u)}\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s)}\chi_{E_{\delta^{2}}}(s)\ du=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{s\in\gamma^{-1}(\pi^{-1}(u))\cap E_{\delta^{2}}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s)}\ du.

Because
E_{\delta^{2}}\subseteq S_{\pi}
and
\gamma
is injective, the set
\gamma^{-1}(\pi^{-1}(u))\cap E_{\delta^{2}}
contains at most one element, and is nonempty only if
u\in\pi(\gamma(E_{\delta^{2}}))\subseteq B
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\gamma^{-1}(\pi^{-1}(u))\cap E_{\delta^{2}}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s)}=\sum_{s\in\gamma^{-1}(\pi^{-1}(u))\cap E_{\delta^{2}}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s)}\chi_{\gamma_{1}(E_{\delta^{2}})}(u)\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta^{2}}\chi_{\gamma_{1}(E_{\delta^{2}})}(u).

Using these statements, the area formula simplifies to

\displaystyle\ell(E_{\delta^{2}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{s\in\gamma^{-1}(\pi^{-1}(u))\cap E_{\delta^{2}}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s)}\ du\leq\int_{\gamma_{1}(E_{\delta^{2}})}\frac{1}{1-\delta^{2}}\ du\leq\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(B)}{1-\delta^{2}}.

Now, define
C_{\delta^{2}}\vcentcolon=B\cap\Gamma\setminus\gamma(E_{\delta^{2}})
. We have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(B)\geq\tilde{\mu}(C_{\delta^{2}})\geq\delta^{2}\ell(C_{\delta^{2}})=\delta^{2}(\ell(B)-\ell(E_{\delta^{2}})).

Adding in the lemma’s hypothesis, we have both

\displaystyle\ell(B)

\displaystyle\geq(1+\delta)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(B),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\ell(E_{\delta^{2}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(B)}{1-\delta^{2}}.

This means

\displaystyle\frac{\ell(E_{\delta^{2}})}{\ell(B)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(B)}{1-\delta^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\delta)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(B)}=1+\left(\frac{1}{(1+\delta)(1-\delta^{2})}-1\right)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\delta-\delta^{2}-\delta^{3}}{(1+\delta)(1-\delta^{2})}\leq 1-\frac{\delta-\frac{\delta}{10}-\frac{\delta}{100}}{1+\frac{1}{10}}\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}.

using the fact that
\delta<\frac{1}{10}
. Rearranging this inequality gives
\ell(B)-\ell(E_{\delta^{2}})\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\ell(B)
. Therefore

\tilde{\mu}(B)\geq\delta^{2}(\ell(B)-\ell(E_{\delta^{2}}))\geq\frac{\delta^{3}}{2}\ell(B).

This concludes the proof that
(i)
implies the conclusion. We now show that
(ii)
implies the conclusion. From
(i)
, it suffices to assume
\ell(B)<(1+\delta)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(B)
. Then,

\tilde{\mu}(B)\geq\delta\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(B)\geq\delta\frac{1+\delta}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(B)\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\ell(B).\qed

Let us begin the proof. Define

Lemma 4.32

Let

We begin by proving

\gamma^{\prime\prime}\vcentcolon=\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Edge}\nolimits(\gamma_{Q}),\ \gamma^{\prime}\vcentcolon=\gamma^{\prime\prime}\cap c_{0}Q=\vcentcolon[x,y]

\gamma^{\prime}
makes angle of order
\epsilon_{3}
with
\eta_{Q}

, and then use that to bound
\frac{\tilde{\mu}(\varphi)}{\ell(\varphi)}
from below. First, observe that
\tilde{\beta}(\gamma_{Q})\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(2Q)\leq 2\epsilon_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

4.3

\gamma_{Q}\subseteq B(\gamma^{\prime\prime},2\epsilon_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q))\text{ and }\gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq B(\gamma_{Q},2\epsilon_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)).

(4.23)

Let

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(z^{\prime\prime},\eta_{Q})\geq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(z,\eta_{Q})-|z-z^{\prime\prime}|\geq 99\epsilon_{3}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

We can define the angle
\theta\vcentcolon=\angle(\gamma^{\prime},\eta_{Q})=\angle(\gamma^{\prime\prime},\eta_{Q})
by translating the segment
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
so that one of its endpoints lies in
\eta_{Q}
and measuring the angle in the (at most
2
-dimensional) plane containing
\eta_{Q}
and this translated segment. The previous estimates then imply
\tan(\theta)\geq\frac{99\epsilon_{3}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(2Q)}\geq 45\epsilon_{3}
. Using the Pythagorean theorem, we get
|x-y|^{2}=|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|^{2}+|\pi^{\perp}(x)-\pi^{\perp}(y)|^{2}
, and the lower bound on
\tan(\theta)
implies
|\pi^{\perp}(x)-\pi^{\perp}(y)|\geq 45\epsilon_{3}|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|

\displaystyle\frac{|x-y|-|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|}{|x-y|}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\pi^{\perp}(x)-\pi^{\perp}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|(|x-y|+|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|)}\geq 45^{2}\epsilon_{3}^{2}\frac{|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|(|x-y|+|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|)}

\displaystyle\geq 45^{2}\epsilon_{3}^{2}\frac{\frac{1}{4}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})^{2}}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})\cdot 2\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})}\geq 100\epsilon_{3}^{2}

(4.24)

Now, let

\varphi

\displaystyle|x-x^{\prime}|\leq 8\epsilon_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\text{ and }|y-y^{\prime}|\leq 8\epsilon_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q).

We estimate

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mu}(\varphi)}{\ell(\varphi)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu(\varphi)}{\ell(\varphi)}\geq 1-\frac{|\pi(x^{\prime})-\pi(y^{\prime})|}{|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|}=\frac{|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|-|\pi(x^{\prime})-\pi(y^{\prime})|}{|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|x-y|-|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|-32\epsilon_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)}{|x-y|+16\epsilon_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{|x-y|-|\pi(x)-\pi(y)|}{|x-y|}-\frac{32\epsilon_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)}{|x-y|+16\epsilon_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)}\geq 50\epsilon_{3}^{2}-64c_{0}^{-1}\epsilon_{2}\geq 40\epsilon_{3}^{2}

where we used (

{}\ell(\gamma_{Q}\cap U_{Q}\setminus\varphi)\leq\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\pi(\gamma_{Q}\cap U_{Q}\setminus\varphi))}{1-\epsilon_{3}^{2}}\leq\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})}{1-40\epsilon_{3}^{2}}\leq 2\ell(\varphi)

Definition 4.34

(Levels and inner/outer cores)
.

Because we would prefer to work with pairwise disjoint subfamilies of
\Delta_{2.1.2}
in view of Lemma
4.33
, we will divide
\Delta_{2.1.2}
into pairwise disjoint “levels” using its tree structure. Indeed, fix
j,\ 1\leq j\leq J
and recall
\mathscr{Q}_{j}
is one of the
J
families of balls ordered by inclusion of cores constructed in Proposition
1.25
. Consider the family
\Delta_{2.1.2}^{j}\vcentcolon=\Delta_{2.1.2}\cap\mathscr{Q}_{j}
. We define the
k
-th level of
\Delta_{2.1.2}^{j}
for
k\geq 0
as

\mathscr{L}_{k}\vcentcolon=\{Q\in\Delta_{2.1.2}^{j}:Q\in C^{k}(Q(T)),\ T\in\mathcal{T}_{\Delta_{2.1.2}^{j}}^{c_{0}}\}

where we set
C^{0}(Q(T))=\{Q(T)\}
. The family
\mathscr{L}_{k}
is pairwise disjoint for any
k\geq 0
and
\Delta_{2.1.2}^{j}=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\mathscr{L}_{k}
. We additionally want to single out balls which live away from from the boundary of the core of their parent in the tree structure. We define the inner and outer balls:

\displaystyle\Delta_{I}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathscr{L}_{0}\cup\{Q\in\Delta_{2.1.2}^{j}\setminus\mathscr{L}_{0}:2Q\subseteq(1-5\epsilon_{1})c_{0}P(Q)\},

\displaystyle\Delta_{O}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\Delta_{2.1.2}^{j}\setminus\Delta_{I}.

We can show that the diameters of the outer cores are controlled by the diameters of the inner cores using Lemma
4.33
and some algebra.

Lemma 4.35
.

\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{O}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{I}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q}).

Proof.

Fix
Q\in\Delta^{j}_{2.1.2}
and let
Q^{\prime}\in C(Q)
. Recall that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(2Q^{\prime})\leq\epsilon_{1}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})
as in (
4.16
). Hence, if
Q^{\prime}\in\Delta_{O}
, then Lemma
4.32
implies

(\Gamma\setminus\gamma_{Q})\cap 2Q^{\prime}\cap\pi^{-1}\left((1-10\epsilon_{1})c_{0}Q\right)=\varnothing.

(4.27)

Because the cores of balls in
C(Q)
are pairwise disjoint, the projection lemma
4.33
implies

\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in C(Q)\cap\Delta_{O}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime}})\leq 5\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in C(Q)\cap\Delta_{O}}\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\pi\left(\frac{c_{0}}{4}Q^{\prime}\right)\right)\leq 200\epsilon_{1}c_{0}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\leq 200\epsilon_{1}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q}).

Summing this inequality over
Q\in\mathscr{L}_{k}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{L}_{k+1}\cap\Delta_{O}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{L}_{k}}\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in C(Q)\cap\Delta_{O}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime}})\leq 200\epsilon_{1}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{L}_{k}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})

\displaystyle\leq 200\epsilon_{1}\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{L}_{k}\cap\Delta_{O}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime}})+200\epsilon_{1}\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{L}_{k}\cap\Delta_{I}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime}}).

(4.28)

In order to simplify the notation, define
S_{k}^{O}:=\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{L}_{k}\cap\Delta_{O}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime}})
and
S_{k}^{I}:=\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{L}_{k}\cap\Delta_{I}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime}})
. The lemma will follow from some algebraic manipulations of (
4.28
). We can restate (
4.28
) in this notation as

S_{k+1}^{O}\leq 200\epsilon_{1}S_{k}^{O}+200\epsilon_{1}S_{k}^{I}.

Iterating this inequality over
k
, we get

S_{k}^{O}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{k}(200\epsilon_{1})^{n}S_{k-n}^{I}

which gives

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{O}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}S_{k}^{O}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{k}(200\epsilon_{1})^{n}S_{k-n}^{I}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k,n)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\\
n\leq k\end{subarray}}(200\epsilon_{1})^{n}S_{k-n}^{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(200\epsilon_{1})^{n}S_{m}^{I}\lesssim\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}S_{m}^{I}=\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{I}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q}).\qed

With this lemma, we now concentrate on proving
\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{I}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})\lesssim\tilde{\mu}(\Gamma)
. Because
\Delta_{I}\subseteq\Delta_{2.1}
, any
Q\in\Delta_{I}
has the existence of an arc
\tau_{Q}
as described in the section following Lemma
4.13
. It follows from the neighborhood containment of
\gamma_{Q}
in Lemma
4.32
that there exists a subarc
\zeta_{Q}\subseteq\tau_{Q}\cap(1-c_{0})2Q\subseteq\gamma(\overline{M}_{\pi})
such that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Diam}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{10}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(2Q).

These properties imply

\tilde{\mu}(\zeta_{Q})\geq\frac{1}{5}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(2Q).

(4.29)

Fix a level
\mathscr{L}_{k}^{I}:=\mathscr{L}_{k}\cap\Delta_{I}
and define an equivalence relation on
\mathscr{L}_{k}^{I}
by putting
Q\sim Q^{\prime}
if and only if there exists a collection
\{Q_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subseteq\mathscr{L}_{k}^{I}
such that
\zeta_{Q_{i}}\cap\zeta_{Q_{i+1}}\not=\varnothing
while both
\zeta_{Q}\cap\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\zeta_{Q_{n}}\not=\varnothing
and
\zeta_{Q^{\prime}}\cap\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\zeta_{Q_{n}}\not=\varnothing
. That is,
Q\sim Q^{\prime}
if and only if
\zeta_{Q}
and
\zeta_{Q^{\prime}}
can be connected by a connected path of
\zeta
arcs from
\mathscr{L}_{k}^{I}
. This partitions
\mathscr{L}_{k}^{I}
into equivalence classes
\mathscr{L}_{k}^{I}=\bigcup_{i\in I_{k}}\mathscr{C}_{k,i}
. In each equivalence class
\mathscr{C}_{k,i}
, there exists a ball
Q^{M}_{k,i}
of maximal diameter. The arc
\zeta_{Q^{M}_{k,i}}
dominates the sum of diameters of balls in this equivalence class in the sense of the following lemma:

Lemma 4.36
.

\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{C}_{k,i}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})\lesssim\tilde{\mu}(\zeta_{Q^{M}_{k,i}}).

Proof.

Define
\zeta_{k,i}\vcentcolon=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{C}_{k,i}}\zeta_{Q}
. We claim that for any
Q\in\mathscr{C}_{k,i}
,

\displaystyle 0<\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(\pi(x_{Q}),\pi(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{k,i})))

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(2Q)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(2Q^{M}_{k,i}).

Indeed, to prove the left inequality, suppose that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(\pi(x_{Q}),\pi(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{k,i})))=0
. Because
\zeta_{Q}\subseteq\gamma(\overline{M}_{\pi})
for any
Q\in\mathscr{L}_{k}
, we know that
\zeta_{k,i}\subseteq\gamma(\overline{M}_{\pi})
. Therefore, there exists
\phi\in\Phi
such that
\zeta_{k,i}\subseteq\phi
and
\ell(\phi)\geq\ell(\zeta_{Q^{M}_{k,i}})\geq\frac{1}{10}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(2Q^{M}_{k,i})\geq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})
. Recalling the definition of
N(\Phi)
(see Definition
4.25
), it follows from the fact that
\sup_{x\in U_{Q}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(\pi(x),\pi(\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi)))\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})
that
\gamma^{-1}(U_{Q})\subseteq N(\Phi)
, implying
\tilde{\mu}(U_{Q})\geq\ell(U_{Q})
which contradicts the fact that
Q\in\Delta_{2.1.2}
. For the right inequality above, notice that
\zeta_{Q}\subseteq 2Q
so that
\zeta_{k,i}\cap 2Q\not=\varnothing
.
Therefore, by Lemma
4.33
,
\{\pi\left(\frac{c_{0}}{4}Q\right)\}_{Q\in\mathscr{C}_{k,i}}
is a collection of pairwise disjoint intervals of total length less than
10\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q^{M}_{k,i})
. This means

\displaystyle\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{C}_{k,i}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})\leq 5\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{C}_{k,i}}\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\pi\left(\frac{c_{0}}{4}Q\right)\right)\leq 50\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q^{M}_{k,i})\leq 250\tilde{\mu}(\zeta_{Q^{M}_{k,i}})

using (
4.29
) in the final inequality.
∎

The following lemma gives the reason for restricting this argument to the inner cores.

Lemma 4.37
.

The arcs in the collection
\{\zeta_{Q^{M}_{k,i}}\}_{k\geq 0,i\in I_{k}}
have pairwise disjoint images. As a result,

\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{I}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})\lesssim\tilde{\mu}(\Gamma).

Proof.

Fix
k,k^{\prime}\geq 0
and
i\in I_{k},\ i^{\prime}\in I_{k^{\prime}}
. Because the cores of balls in
\mathscr{L}_{k}
are pairwise disjoint for any
k\geq 0
, we can assume
k>k^{\prime}\geq 0
. For ease of notation, let
Q_{1}:=Q^{M}_{k,i},\ Q_{2}:=Q^{M}_{k^{\prime},i^{\prime}}
, and
Q^{\prime}=P(Q_{1})
which exists because
k>0
. Suppose by way of contradiction that
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{1}})\cap\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{2}})\not=\varnothing
. We first claim that
U_{Q^{\prime}}\cap U_{Q_{2}}=\varnothing
. Indeed, further suppose by way of contradiction that
U_{Q_{2}}\cap U_{Q^{\prime}}\not=\varnothing
. Then
k<k^{\prime}
implies
Q^{\prime}\in C^{m}(Q_{2})
for some
m\geq 0
. Because
Q_{1}\in\Delta_{I}
and because
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q_{2})\geq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q^{\prime})>\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q_{1})
, we have

\displaystyle 2Q_{1}\subseteq(1-5\epsilon_{1})c_{0}Q^{\prime},\text{ and }

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(2Q_{1})\leq\epsilon_{1}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime}}).

We conclude
2Q_{1}\subseteq U_{Q^{\prime}}\subseteq U_{Q_{2}}
, which contradicts
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{1}})\cap\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{2}})\not=\varnothing
because
\zeta_{Q_{2}}\cap U_{Q_{2}}=\varnothing
by definition as a subarc of
\tau_{Q_{2}}
.

From this claim, we see that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(2Q_{1},x_{Q_{2}})\geq 4\epsilon_{1}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q^{\prime}})
so that we can again conclude
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q_{2})\geq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q^{\prime})
, for otherwise we would have
2Q_{1}\cap 2Q_{2}=\varnothing
which is in contradiction to our starting assumption that
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{1}})\cap\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{2}})\not=\varnothing
. Now, because
\zeta_{Q_{2}},\zeta_{Q_{1}}\subseteq\gamma(M_{\pi})
and
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{2}})\cap\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{1}})\not=\varnothing
, we can conclude that there exists a bend
\phi\in\Phi
such that
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{1}})\cup\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{2}})\subseteq\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\phi)
, hence
\ell(\phi)\geq\ell(\zeta_{Q_{2}})\geq\frac{1}{10}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(2Q_{2})
. Because
2Q_{1}\cap 2Q_{2}\not=\varnothing
, this implies
\gamma^{-1}(Q_{1})\subseteq N(\Phi)
so that
\tilde{\mu}(U_{Q_{1}})\geq\ell(U_{Q_{1}})
, contradicting the fact that
Q\in\Delta_{2.1.2}
and implying our assumption that
\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{1}})\cap\mathop{\mathsf{}}{Image}\nolimits(\zeta_{Q_{2}})\not=\varnothing
must be false. This proves the first claim of the lemma. Using Lemma
4.36
, we get

\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{I}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{i\in\mathscr{C}_{k,i}}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{C}_{k,i}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})\lesssim\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{i\in\mathscr{C}_{k,i}}\tilde{\mu}(\zeta_{Q^{M}_{k,i}})\leq\tilde{\mu}(\Gamma).\qed

Now that we have controlled the inner cores, we can finish the proof of the bound for
\Delta_{2.1.2}
and of the proof of Theorem
A
.

Proposition 4.38
.

\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{2.1.2}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\lesssim_{A}\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{2.1.2}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})\lesssim_{J}\tilde{\mu}(\Gamma).

Proof.

Using Lemmas
4.35
and
4.37
, we get

\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{2.1.2}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{2.1.2}^{j}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})\lesssim_{J}\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{I}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})+\sum_{Q\in\Delta_{O}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(U_{Q})\lesssim\tilde{\mu}(\Gamma).\qed

This completes the proof of Theorem
A
.

Remark 4.39

(General rectifiable arcs)
.

Given a rectifiable arc
\Gamma_{r}
with arc length parameterization
\gamma_{r}
, one can proceed as for Jordan arcs and define the measure
\mu
by
d\mu\vcentcolon=(\gamma_{r})_{*}(\rho\ d\ell)
. Lemma
4.22
likely holds, and one can define bends and likely carry out a similar program to that of Section
4.2
to show that
\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{A}}\beta_{\Gamma_{r}}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\lesssim_{A}\ell(\Gamma_{r})-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma_{r})
. This is importantly weaker than the more desirable inequality

\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{A}}\beta_{\Gamma_{r}}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\lesssim_{A}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma_{r})-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma_{r}).

(4.30)

If one would like to achieve
4.30
via methods similar to those used here, one likely needs a stronger definition of
\mu
. For any
t\in I
, set
m(t)\vcentcolon=\inf\{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s):\gamma(s)=\gamma(t)\}
. A more prudent choice of
\mu
might be something like

\displaystyle d\mu_{r}\vcentcolon=\sigma_{r}d\mathcal{H}^{1}

where

\sigma_{r}(x)\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}1-\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(t),&\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(t)\text{ exists and $\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(t)=\inf\{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(s):s\in\gamma^{-1}(x)\}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

That is, we assign to
x
the maximal
\rho
value achieved on
\gamma^{-1}(x)
. We do not investigate this approach further here.

5
Theorem
B

In this section, we show how making minor modifications to the proof of the
\gtrsim
direction of Theorem 1.3 in
[
Bis20
]
gives a proof of Theorem
B
. First, we need a slightly weaker version of Theorem
B
.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
Bis20
]
Theorem 1.1 in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
)
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\ell_{2}
be a rectifiable Jordan arc. For any multiresolution family
\mathscr{H}
associated to
\Gamma
with inflation factor
A>30
, we have

\ell(\Gamma)-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\Gamma)\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q).

Proof.

The proof is similar in form to Bishop’s proof in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but we construct coverings of the curve by pieces of
\Gamma
inside Voronoi cells centered at net points rather than dividing convex hulls of pieces of the curve along diameter segments. Assume
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\Gamma)=1
. Define

\mathcal{V}_{n}\vcentcolon=\{V_{n}(x):x\in X_{n}\}

where, given any
x\in X_{n}
,

V_{n}(x)\vcentcolon=\{y\in\Gamma:\forall z\in X_{n},\ |x-y|\leq|z-y|\}.

Since
X_{n}
is a
2^{-n}
-net centered on
\Gamma
, it is clear that

2^{-n-1}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x))<2^{-n+1}.

(5.1)

By definition, for any
n\geq 0
we also have
\Gamma=\bigcup_{x\in X_{n}}V_{n}(x)
so that

\ell(\Gamma)=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma)\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{x\in X_{n}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x)).

This means is suffices to prove

\sum_{x\in X_{n}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x))\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\Gamma)+C\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

(5.2)

for any
n\geq 0
and some
C>0
. We will show

\sum_{x\in X_{n}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x))\leq\sum_{y\in X_{n-1}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n-1}(y))+C^{\prime}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{n}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

(5.3)

where each ball
Q\in\mathscr{H}
will only appear in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
for a bounded number of values of
n
. We can prove Theorem
5.1
by repeatedly applying inequality (
5.3
). Indeed,

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X_{n}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y\in X_{n-1}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n-1}(y))+C^{\prime}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{n}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y\in X_{n-2}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n-2}(y))+C^{\prime}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{n-1}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)+C^{\prime}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{n}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

\displaystyle\ \vdots

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{0}(x_{0}))+C^{\prime}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{k}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\Gamma)+C\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q).

We now turn to proving (
5.3
). Fix
\epsilon<2^{-10}
. For any net point
x\in X_{n}
, call
x

flat
if
\beta_{\Gamma}(B(x,10\cdot 2^{-n}))<\epsilon
and call
x

non-flat
if
\beta_{\Gamma}(B(x,10\cdot 2^{-n}))\geq\epsilon
. We will construct a function
P:\cup_{n}X_{n}\rightarrow\cup_{n}X_{n}
which assigns each
y\in X_{n+1}
to a parent
P(y)\in X_{n}
. Since
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\Gamma)=1
,
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\{V_{0}(x_{0})\}
so for any
y\in X_{1}
, define
P(y)=x_{0}
. Fix
n>0
and a point

.
If there exists

is non-flat and

, then define

. Otherwise, every

is flat. Choose one such

and let

. We define

\displaystyle X_{n}\cap B(x,10\cdot 2^{-n})

\displaystyle=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\}

\displaystyle X_{n+1}\cap V_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{M}\}

\displaystyle\pi_{d_{x}}(v_{1})

\displaystyle<\ldots<\pi_{d_{x}}(v_{N}),

\displaystyle\pi_{d_{x}}(u_{1})

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in X_{n+1}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n+1}(y))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in X_{n+1}\\

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{seg}(v_{j+1})
. With these sets constructed, we can now write

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in X_{n+1}\\
P(y)\ \text{flat}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n+1}(y))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in X_{n+1}\\
P(y)\ \text{flat}\end{subarray}}d_{y}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in X_{n}\\
x\ \text{flat}\end{subarray}}\sum_{s\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{seg}(x)}\mathcal{H}^{1}(s).

(5.5)

With (
5.5
) in place, we only need to give an appropriate bound for
\sum_{s\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{seg}(x)}\mathcal{H}^{1}(s)
. Define
Q_{x}=B(x,A2^{-n})
. We claim

\sum_{s\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{seg}(x)}\mathcal{H}^{1}(s)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x))+C\beta_{\Gamma}(Q_{x})^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q_{x})

(5.6)

for some large
C>0
. In order to prove this statement, we first state a lemma given in
[
BS17
]
:

Lemma 5.2
.

(
[
BS17
]
Lemma 8.3)
Suppose that
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
1
-separated set with
\#V\geq 2
and there exist lines
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
and a number
0\leq\alpha\leq 1/16
such that

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}(v,\ell_{i})\leq\alpha\quad\text{for all $v\in V$ and $i=1,2$}.

Let
\pi_{i}
denote the orthogonal projection onto
\ell_{i}
. There exist compatible identifications of
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
with
\mathbb{R}
such that
\pi_{1}(v^{\prime})\leq\pi_{1}(v^{\prime\prime})
if and only if
\pi_{2}(v^{\prime})\leq\pi_{2}(v^{\prime\prime})
for all

. If
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are consecutive points in
V
relative to the ordering of

\mathcal{H}^{1}([u_{1},u_{2}])<(1+3\alpha^{2})\cdot\mathcal{H}^{1}([\pi_{1}(u_{1}),\pi_{1}(u_{2})])\quad\text{for all }[u_{1},u_{2}]\subseteq[v_{1},v_{2}].

(5.7)

Moreover,

\mathcal{H}^{1}([y_{1},y_{2}])<(1+12\alpha^{2})\cdot\mathcal{H}^{1}([\pi_{1}(y_{1}),\pi_{1}(y_{2})])\quad\text{for all }[y_{1},y_{2}]\subseteq\ell_{2}.

(5.8)

Applying this lemma with
\ell_{1}=d_{x}
and
\ell_{2}=d_{y}
, for any segment
[s_{1},s_{2}]\subseteq d_{y}
, we have

\mathcal{H}^{1}([s_{1},s_{2}])<(1+C\beta_{\Gamma}(Q_{x})^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q_{x}))\mathcal{H}^{1}([\pi_{d_{x}}(s_{1}),\pi_{d_{x}}(s_{2})]).

Enumerate
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{seg}(x)=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}\}
. With this, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{seg}(x)}\mathcal{H}^{1}(s)

\displaystyle<(1+C\beta_{\Gamma}(Q_{x})^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q_{x}))\sum_{s\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{seg}(x)}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{d_{x}}(s))

\displaystyle\leq(1+C\beta_{\Gamma}(Q_{x})^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q_{x}))\left(\mathcal{H}^{1}(d_{x})+2\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{d_{x}}(s_{i})\cap\pi_{d_{x}}(s_{i+1}))\right)

\displaystyle=(1+C\beta_{\Gamma}(Q_{x})^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q_{x}))\left(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x))+2\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{d_{x}}(s_{i})\cap\pi_{d_{x}}(s_{i+1}))\right).

(5.9)

Because
x
is flat,
\#\mathop{\mathrm{}}{seg}(x)
is bounded above by a universal constant, so we only need to show that

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{L}(s_{i})\cap\pi_{L}(s_{i+1}))\leq C^{\prime}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q_{x})^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q_{x}).

(5.10)

for some

. It suffices to bound the length of the overlap between the projections of consecutive Voronoi cells

within the tube of radius

around

. The boundary between the cells is the intersection of this tube with the hyperplane of points of equal distance from both

and

]

) (also see Figure

). Combining (

). Applying (

), we can finally write

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in X_{n+1}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n+1}(y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in X_{n+1}\\
P(y)\ \text{flat}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n+1}(y))+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in X_{n+1}\\
P(z)\ \text{non-flat}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n+1}(z))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in X_{n}\\
x\ \text{non-flat}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x))+C^{\prime}\sum_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{rad}(Q)=A2^{-n-1}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in X_{n}\\
x\ \text{flat}\end{subarray}}\sum_{s\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{seg}(x)}\mathcal{H}^{1}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in X_{n}\\
x\ \text{non-flat}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x))+C^{\prime}\sum_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{rad}(Q)=A2^{-n-1}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in X_{n}\\
x\ \text{flat}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x))+C^{\prime\prime}\sum_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{rad}(Q)=A2^{-n}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in X_{n}}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(V_{n}(x))+C\sum_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{rad}(Q)\in\{A2^{-n-1},A2^{-n}\}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q).

This proves inequality (

) and finishes the proof of Theorem

.

Proof.

Assume the
\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)<\infty
and that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\Gamma)=1
. Let

Therefore, by Theorem

\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)+C\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q)\lesssim\sum_{Q\in\mathscr{H}}\beta_{\Gamma}(Q)^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(Q).

\gamma_{2}=\gamma(t(y),\ell(\Gamma))

\gamma_{1}
is the subarc of
\Gamma
from the beginning of
\Gamma
to

\gamma_{2}
is the subarc from
y
to the end of
\Gamma
. Observe that

\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)\geq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diam}(\Gamma)-\ell(\gamma_{1})-\ell(\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle\ell(\Gamma)-\mathrm{crd}(\Gamma)
