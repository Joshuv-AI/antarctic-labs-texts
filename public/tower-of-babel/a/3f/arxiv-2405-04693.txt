Abstract
. A Wright function based framework is proposed to combine and extend several distribution families. The α \alpha -stable distribution is generalized by adding the degree of freedom parameter. The PDF of this two-sided super distribution family subsumes those of the original α \alpha -stable, Student’s t distributions, as well as the exponential power distribution and the modified Bessel function of the second kind. Its CDF leads to a fractional extension of the Gauss hypergeometric function. The degree of freedom makes possible for valid variance, skewness, and kurtosis, just like Student’s t. The original α \alpha -stable distribution is viewed as having one degree of freedom , that explains why it lacks most of the moments. A skew-Gaussian kernel is derived from the characteristic function of the α \alpha -stable law, which maximally preserves the law in the new framework. To facilitate such framework, the stable count distribution is generalized as the fractional extension of the generalized gamma distribution. It provides rich subordination capabilities, one of which is the fractional χ \chi distribution that supplies the needed ’degree of freedom’ parameter. Hence, the ”new” α \alpha -stable distribution is a ”ratio distribution” of the skew-Gaussian kernel and the fractional χ \chi distribution. Mathematically, it is a new form of higher transcendental function under the Wright function family. Last, the new univariate symmetric distribution is extended to the multivariate elliptical distribution successfully.

Framework of Continuous Gaussian Mixture

The bulk of this paper is focused on the construction of a symmetric two-sided distribution
in the form of a continuous Gaussian mixture. Both the ratio and product distribution methods are used.
In the case of the symmetric
\alpha
-stable distribution (SaS)
[
5
]
,
the exponential power distribution comes from its characteristic function (CF).
We would like to present a unified framework and familiarize the reader with the notations,
which would be otherwise subtle and confusing.

Assume the PDF of a two-sided symmetric distribution is
L(x)
where
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
It has zero mean,
\mathbb{E}(X|L)=0
.
Assume the PDF of a one-sided distribution is
\chi(x)\,(x>0)
such that

(3)

\displaystyle L(x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,\mathcal{N}(xs)\,\chi(s)

This is nothing but the definition of a ratio distribution with a standard normal variable
\mathcal{N}
.
This is the first form of the Gaussian mixture:
L\sim\mathcal{N}/\chi
.

It also has the equivalent expression in terms of a product distribution
by way of
the inverse distribution

\chi^{\dagger}
such
that
L\sim\mathcal{N}\chi^{\dagger}
.
This is the second form of the Gaussian mixture.

\chi^{\dagger}
is closer to our typical understanding of
the marginal distribution of a volatility process.
For example, the Brownian motion process
dX_{t}=\sigma_{t}\,dW_{t}
,
measured in a particular time interval
\Delta t
,
we have
\Delta X_{t}\sim L
and
\sigma_{t}\sim\chi^{\dagger}
.

However,
\chi
in the first form is more natural in the expression of

\alpha
-stable distribution.
So we are more inclined to use the ratio distribution.
The reader should keep this subtlety in mind.

.

Definition 3.1

(Generalized stable count distribution (GSC))
.

GSC is a four-parameter one-sided distribution family, whose PDF is defined as

(14)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(x;\sigma,d,p)

\displaystyle:=C\,{\left(\frac{x}{\sigma}\right)}^{d-1}\,{W}_{{-\alpha},0}\left(-{{\left(\frac{x}{\sigma}\right)}^{p}}\right)

\displaystyle(x\geq 0)

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
controls the shape of the Wright function;

\sigma
is the scale parameter;

p
is also the shape parameter controlling the tail behavior
(p\neq 0,dp\geq 0)
;

d
is the
degree of freedom
parameter.
When
\alpha\to 1
, the PDF becomes a Dirac delta function:
\delta(x-\sigma)

assuming
\sigma
is finite.
When
d\geq 1
, all the moments of GSC exist and have closed forms.
See Section
5

for more detail.

The normalization constant
C
will be derived in Section
5.2

and is shown below:

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{|p|}{\sigma}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{\alpha d}{p})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{p})}&,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\alpha\neq 0,d\neq 0.\\
\frac{|p|}{\sigma\alpha}&,\,\text{for}\,\,\alpha\neq 0,d=0.\end{array}\right.

The original stable count distribution (
12
)
is simply
\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(x)=\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(x;\sigma=1,d=1,p=\alpha)
.
See Section
5.7
.

It is important to note that
d
and
p
are allowed to be negative,
as long as
dp\geq 0
.
Several examples are found in Table
0
and Table
2
. This feature is used to define the characteristic distribution of FCM below.

Since the Wright function extends an exponential function to the fractional space,
GSC is the fractional extension
of the generalized gamma (GG) distribution
[
29
]
,
whose PDF is defined as:

(17)

f_{\text{\tiny GG}}(x;a,d,p)=\frac{|p|}{a\Gamma(\frac{d}{p})}{\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)}^{d-1}e^{-(x/a)^{p}}.

The parallel use of parameters is obvious,
except that
a
in GG is replaced with
\sigma
in GSC to avoid confusion with
\alpha
.

GG is subsumed to GSC in two ways:

\displaystyle f_{\text{\tiny GG}}(x;\sigma,d,p)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathfrak{N}_{0}(x;\sigma,d=d-p,p)&,\,\,\text{at}\,\,\alpha=0.\\
\mathfrak{N}_{\frac{1}{2}}\left(x;\sigma=\frac{\sigma}{2^{2/p}},d=d-\frac{p}{2},p=\frac{p}{2}\right)&,\,\,\text{at}\,\,\alpha=\frac{1}{2}.\end{array}\right.

The first line is treated as the definition of GSC at
\alpha=0
.
See Sections
5.3
and
5.4

for more detail.

Although the first line is more obvious,
it is the second line that leads to the fractional extension of the
\chi
distribution.

The readers are reminded that

{W}_{{-\alpha},0}\left(-{x}\right)
used here is fully supported
by existing software packages. This is due to the fact that
it can be converted to
L_{\alpha}(x)

by

Definition 3.2

(Fractional

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{\alpha})}\,{\left(\frac{1}{{\sigma}_{\alpha,k}}\right)}^{k-1}{x}^{k-2}\,W_{-\frac{\alpha}{2},0}\left(-{\left(\frac{x}{{\sigma}_{\alpha,k}}\right)}^{\alpha}\right)

Lemma 3.1
.

(FCM Reflection Formula)
Assume
k>0
, FCM has the reflection formula that resembles (
10
):

(23)

\displaystyle\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,-k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x^{3}\,\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)

\displaystyle\,\,\text{where}\,\,\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})={\sigma}_{\alpha,k}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{\alpha})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}\,\,\text{(See \eqref{eq:fcm-moment})}

It is equivalent to the following relation in terms of the moments:

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,-k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}(X^{-n+1}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})},\,\,k>0.

In particular, when
n=1
,
\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,-k})=1/\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})
.
The first moment is reciprocal.

When
k
is positive,

k

On the other hand,

The parameter space outside

Lemma 3.2
.

(FCM Asymptotics)
As
k\to\pm\infty
, FCM becomes a delta function at its asymptotic mean.
For
k\gg 1
,

(25)

\displaystyle\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(x)

\displaystyle\sim\delta(x-m_{\alpha}),\,\,\,\,\text{where}\,\,m_{\alpha}={\alpha}^{-1/\alpha}.

On the other hand,
\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,-k}(x)\sim\delta(x-1/m_{\alpha})
.

This property ensures that the two-sided distributions constructed from FCM
become a normal distribution at
k\to\pm\infty
. Hence, this lemma is associated with
the Central Limit Theorem.

Note that
m_{1}=1
is preserved for Student’s t;
m_{2}=1/\sqrt{2}
is preserved for
\alpha
-stable.

The speed of approaching a delta function is determined by how fast the variance decreases:

\mathbb{E}(X^{2}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})-\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})^{2}\to 0
.
It is faster for larger
\alpha
, and can be very slow for small
\alpha
below 1.

Definition 3.4

(The skew-Gaussian kernel)
.

The skew-Gaussian kernel
g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)
is derived from the
\alpha
-stable law.

The minus-log of the stable CF is defined
in Feller’s parameterization
[

]
as

\psi_{\alpha}^{\theta}(\zeta):=\exp\left(\text{sgn}(\zeta)\frac{i\theta\pi}{2}\right){|\zeta|}^{\alpha}
,
where
\theta
is the skewness parameter confined by the so-called

Feller-Takayasu diamond

\theta\leq\min\{\alpha,2-\alpha\}
and
\alpha\in(0,2]
[
20

19
]
.

The stable PDF is
L_{\alpha}^{\theta}({x}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\zeta\,\exp(-\psi_{\alpha}^{\theta}(\zeta))\,e^{-ix\zeta}
.
We want it to be decomposed according to

L_{\alpha}^{\theta}({x})=\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1}(s)
.
The solution of
g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)
is

(28)

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\cos\left(\tau\,{(st)}^{\alpha}+\frac{x}{q}\,st\right)e^{-t^{2}/2}

\displaystyle(x\in\mathbb{R},s\geq 0)

\displaystyle\,\,\text{where}\,\,q=\cos(\theta\pi/2)^{1/\alpha},\,\,\tau=\tan(\theta\pi/2),\,\,\theta\neq 1.

Feller’s parameterization provides a clean environment to dissect the subordination structure
in the stable CF.
See Section
6
for more detail.

In general,
g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)

is not a distribution, since some parts of the function can be negative.
But it satisfies
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)dx=1/s
.
One exception is
g_{\alpha}^{0}(x,s)=\mathcal{N}(xs)
.
Also note that
g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,0)=1/q\sqrt{2\pi}
, independent of
x
.

\alpha

The range of the integral is confined by

But when the oscillation frequency is high, it gets harder to integrate with

\alpha

L_{\alpha}^{\theta}\sim L_{\alpha,1}^{\theta}
,
and subsumes Student’s t as
t_{k}\sim L_{1,k}^{0}
.
See Section
10
for more detail.

GAS admits valid variance, skewness, and kurtosis,

as long as there are a few degrees of freedom
,
just like you would expect in Student’s t.
(See Lemma
3.5
about the validity of kurtosis.)
It inherits the skewness from the
\alpha
-stable law - a property that
very few two-sided skew distributions have, if any.

Most real-world data analyses begin with the summary statistics of the first four moments.
Plus a few more, such as the peak PDF, and the 95% and 99% confidence levels.
GAS can address all of them. This is a large improvement to
\alpha
-stable.

4.
Multivariate Distribution

The appearance of
\mathcal{N}(xs)

makes it possible to extend GSaS to
an
n
-dimensional multivariate symmetric distribution
with the concept of the elliptical distribution
[
31
]
.
We propose two viable options.
And we use the daily return distributions of the S&P 500 Index (SPX) and CBOE VIX Index (VIX) since 1990
to examine the bivariate use case.

We need to upgrade
\mathcal{N}(xs)
to a multivariate normal distribution properly.
To set it up, we vectorize
x
to
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
and introduce the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
which is invertible to
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}
. Its determinant is
|\mathbf{\Sigma}|
.
There are two routes to go from here.

4.1.
Multivariate of the First Kind - Elliptical

The first route
is simpler and produces very meaningful results.
All dimensions share the same shape from a single FCM.
So
(xs)^{2}
in
\mathcal{N}(xs)
is replaced to

\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\,s^{2}
.
Such elliptical distribution closely resembles that of a multivariate normal.
The ellipse structure
\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}
is perserved.

We use an extended notation
\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)
to represent
the PDF of a multivariate normal distribution.
But for the purpose of exploring fundamental properties, we don’t want to be bothered
with the location parameter. Therefore, we set its
\mathbf{\mu}=0
.

Definition 4.1
.

(GSaS Elliptical Multivariate Distribution)
The PDF of the first kind is

(38)

\displaystyle L_{\alpha,k}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\,ds\,\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)\,\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x};0,\frac{\mathbf{\Sigma}}{s^{2}}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}{|\mathbf{\Sigma}|}^{1/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{n}\,ds\,\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)\left[\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2}\,\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\right)\right]

The
scipy.stats.multivariate_normal
function is used in our prototype implementation.
It is obvious from the first line that the total density is integrated to 1.

When
\alpha=1,k>0
,
L_{1,k}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{\Sigma})
becomes the multivariate t distribution.
This is validated numerically with
scipy.stats.multivariate_t
.
On the other hand, due to Lemma
3.4
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 2\,\text{or}\,k\to\infty}L_{\alpha,k}\left(\mathbf{x};{\alpha^{-2\alpha}}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x};0,\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)

These two validations are critically
important
.
They show that the univariate GSaS extends naturally to multivariate
through the combination of the elliptical distribution and FCM.

From (
39
), its peak PDF is

(40)

\displaystyle L_{\alpha,k}(\mathbf{0};\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}{(2\pi)^{n/2}{|\mathbf{\Sigma}|}^{1/2}}

Its variance (aka the second moment) is

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{i}X_{j})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(X^{-2}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i,j}

Its
i
-th dimension marginal PDF is a GSaS:

(42)

\displaystyle L_{\alpha,k}^{(i)}(x;\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}L_{\alpha,k}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma_{i}}\right),\quad\,\,\text{where}\,\,\sigma_{i}=\sqrt{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i,i}}

Proof.

The proof of (
42
) is straightforward.
The marginal distribution of a multivariate normal distribution is
the down-sized multivariate normal distribution with the covariance matrix from
those selected dimensions. See Section 3.3.1 of
[
31
]
.
∎

Hence, the
i
-th marginal variance is
\mathbb{E}(X^{2}|L_{\alpha,k})\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i,i}
,
the same as
\mathbb{E}(X_{i}^{2})
in (
41
) since

\mathbb{E}(X^{2}|L_{\alpha,k})=\mathbb{E}(X^{-2}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})
from Lemma
8.1
.
They match nicely.

The marginal peak density is

L_{\alpha,k}^{(i)}(0;\mathbf{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{i}}\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})
.

The multivariate extension of GEP (aka
k<0
) has not been validated
since there is no implementation in the
scipy
package.
But we do find it useful as an alternative model in Section
4.3
.

4.2.
Multivariate of the Second Kind - Adaptive

The second route
is more complex, but also more adaptive for practical use.
For instance, in a multi-asset scenario, it allows each asset
to have its own shape according to its marginal distribution.
The downside is that the elliptic structure is distorted.

In the previous method, we learn the advantage of using multivariate normal to
construct our multivariate PDF. The
\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x};...\right)
term
can be integrated away easily to make sure the total density is equal to 1.
We follow the same methodology but expand on the FCM structure.

For simplicity, we use
\Theta_{i}
to represent
the
i
-th parameterization:
(\alpha_{i},k_{i})
.
And
\Theta
is the collection of all
\Theta_{i}
’s.
We vectorize
s
to
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Each
s_{i}
comes from a distinct
\overline{\chi}_{\Theta_{i}}
in that dimension.

The covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is sandwiched by
1/\mathbf{s}
such that
the covariance matrix becomes

(\mathbf{s}^{\dagger})^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{s}^{\dagger}

where
\mathbf{s}^{\dagger}=\diag(\mathbf{s})^{-1}
.
It is then enveloped by the integrals of the multi-dimensional FCMs.
To put it together -

Definition 4.2
.

(GSaS Adaptive Multivariate Distribution)
The PDF of the second kind is

(43)

\displaystyle L_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,ds_{i}\,\,\overline{\chi}_{\Theta_{i}}(s_{i})\,\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x};0,(\mathbf{s}^{\dagger})^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{s}^{\dagger}\right),\quad\,\,\text{where}\,\,\mathbf{s}^{\dagger}=\diag(\mathbf{s})^{-1}

This object is far more computationally intense than
L_{\alpha,k}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{\Sigma})

due to the multi-dimensional integral on
\mathbf{s}
.

Note that, in the case of identity
\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
L_{\Theta}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{\Sigma})

does factorize into the product of the marginal one-dimensional distributions.
This is a positive feature.
However, this PDF retains the circular contour only in the center region.
The contour could be distorted into rectangle-barbell shapes with round edges in the tail regions.

Its peak PDF is

(44)

\displaystyle L_{\Theta}(\mathbf{0};\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{i}\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\Theta_{i}})}{(2\pi)^{n/2}{|\mathbf{\Sigma}|}^{1/2}}

Its variance (aka the second moment) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{i}X_{j})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{E}(X^{-2}|\overline{\chi}_{\Theta_{i}})\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i,i}&,\,\text{for}\,\,i=j.\\
\mathbb{E}(X^{-1}|\overline{\chi}_{\Theta_{i}})\,\mathbb{E}(X^{-1}|\overline{\chi}_{\Theta_{j}})\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i,j}&,\,\text{for}\,\,i\neq j.\end{array}\right.

Its
i
-th dimension marginal PDF is a GSaS:

(47)

\displaystyle L_{\Theta}^{(i)}(x;\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}L_{\alpha_{i},k_{i}}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma_{i}}\right),\quad\,\,\text{where}\,\,\sigma_{i}=\sqrt{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i,i}}

Such marginal distribution can adapt to each sample’s shape.
This is the major improvement over (
42
).
The
i
-th marginal variance is
\mathbb{E}(X^{2}|L_{\alpha,k})\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i,i}=\mathbb{E}(X_{i}^{2})

in (
4.2
). They match nicely.

The
i
-th marginal peak density is

L_{\Theta}^{(i)}(0;\mathbf{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{i}}\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha_{i},k_{i}})
.
Hence, the peak density ratio between marginal and multivariate is

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{i}L_{\Theta}^{(i)}(0;\mathbf{\Sigma})}{L_{\Theta}(\mathbf{0};\mathbf{\Sigma})}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{|\mathbf{\Sigma}|}{\prod_{i}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i,i}}\right]^{1/2}

In the bivariate case, this ratio becomes
\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}

where
\rho
is the correlation and
|\rho|\leq 1
.
On one hand, when
\rho=0
, this ratio is 1.
On the other hand, the closer
\rho
is to 1, the smaller this ratio is.
Conversely, the ratio from the sample data implies the model correlation.

In conclusion, the differences between the elliptical version and the adaptive version
are (a) how the peak density is formed; (b) the shapes of the marginal distributions;
and (c) the off-diagonal elements of the variance.

5.1.
GSC PDF and Explanation

We recap the PDF of GSC (

and compare it with that of GG (

)

The CDF of a GG is a regularized incomplete gamma function
\Gamma(\cdot,\cdot)
.
Hence, it can also be parameterised such as

\text{GenGamma}(\frac{x}{a};s=\frac{d}{p},c=p):=\Gamma\left(s,{\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)}^{c}\right)

side by side:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(x;\sigma,d,p)

\displaystyle:=C\quad\quad{\left(\frac{x}{\sigma}\right)}^{d-1}\,W_{-\alpha,0}\left(-{\left(\frac{x}{\sigma}\right)}^{p}\right)

The parallel in notations between the two PDFs should be obvious.

GSC is the fractional extension of GG.
The exponential function in GG is

with
the Wright function:

.
The additional parameter

The other two parameters

is the second shape parameter controlling the tail behavior
(p\neq 0,dp\geq 0)
.
In our use cases,

is either
\alpha

In the later case,

is exactly the stability index.

The range of
\alpha

[0,1]
due to the analytic property of
W_{-\alpha,0}(-z)
.
When
\alpha\to 1

\delta(z-1)
.
This is a well known result.

On the other hand, when
\alpha\to 0

See Section

5.2.
Determination of C

We derive the value of the normalization constant
C
in (
3.1
) as following.

Proof.

The normalization constant
C
in (
14

that the integral of the PDF must be 1:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\,N_{\alpha}(x;\sigma,d,p)\,dx

\displaystyle=\,\frac{C\sigma}{|p|}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{p})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{p}\alpha)}=1

where the integral is carried out
by the moment formula of the Wright function (
116
).

Hence,
C
is determined as

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{|p|}{\sigma}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{p}\alpha)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{p})}

\displaystyle(\alpha\neq 0,d\neq 0)

We typically constrain
dp\geq 0
and
p
is typically positive.
But it becomes negative in the inverse distribution and/or characteristic distribution types.
So we need
|p|
to ensure
C
is positive.

For the case of
\alpha\neq 0
and
d\to 0
,
due to (

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{|p|}{\sigma\alpha}

\displaystyle(\alpha\neq 0,d=0)

These two cases are combined to form (

Lemma 5.1

\displaystyle\,\,\text{for}\,\,\alpha\neq 0,d\neq 0.

∎

6.1.
Derivation of the Skew-Gaussian Kernel

We prove how the kernel
g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)
in (

)
comes into existence as following.

Proof.

The minus-log of
\alpha
-stable CF in Feller’s parameterization is defined as
(Section XVII.6, p.581-583 of
[
9
]

\displaystyle\psi_{\alpha}^{\theta}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\text{sgn}(\zeta)\frac{i\theta\pi}{2}\right){|\zeta|}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\left(\cos(\Theta)+i\sin(\Theta)\right){|\zeta|}^{\alpha},\quad\,\,\text{where}\,\,\Theta=\text{sgn}(\zeta)\frac{\theta\pi}{2}

\displaystyle\quad\text{and}\,\,0<\alpha\leq 2,|\theta|\leq\min\{\alpha,2-\alpha\}.

Put it into the integral of the
\alpha
-stable PDF (See (F.35) of
[
18
]
), we get

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}^{\theta}({x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\zeta\,\exp(-\psi_{\alpha}^{\theta}(\zeta))\,e^{-ix\zeta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\zeta\,e^{-\cos(\Theta){|\zeta|}^{\alpha}}\,e^{-i(B+x\zeta)},\quad\,\,\text{where}\,\,B=\sin(\Theta){|\zeta|}^{\alpha}

The imaginary part of the integral cancels out due to the sign of
\zeta
.
The real part of the integral can be simplified to
the integral on the positive axis
(\zeta\geq 0)
.
We can remove
\text{sgn}(\zeta)
from
\Theta
. Hence,

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}^{\theta}({x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\zeta\,e^{-\cos(\Theta){\zeta}^{\alpha}}\,\cos(B+x\zeta),\quad\,\,\text{where}\,\,\Theta=\frac{\theta\pi}{2},B=\sin(\Theta){\zeta}^{\alpha}

Next, we apply the Lambda decomposition (
5.7
) for the stable-vol
V_{\alpha}(\zeta)

on the
e^{-\cos(\Theta){\zeta}^{\alpha}}
term.
Let
u=\cos(\Theta)^{1/\alpha}\zeta
, then

e^{-\cos(\Theta){\zeta}^{\alpha}}=e^{-{u}^{\alpha}}
. We have

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}^{\theta}({x})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\zeta\,\cos(B+x\zeta)\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(u/s)^{2}/2}\right)\left(\frac{2\Gamma(\frac{1}{\alpha}+1)}{\pi}V_{\alpha}(s)\right)

(56)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,\left(\frac{1}{\pi s}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\zeta\,\cos(B+x\zeta)e^{-(u/s)^{2}/2}\right)\left(\frac{2\Gamma(\frac{1}{\alpha}+1)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}s^{-1}V_{\alpha}(s)\right)

The terms inside the first big parenthesis of (
56
)
is the skew-Gaussian kernel in its raw format:

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi s}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\zeta\,\cos(B+x\zeta)e^{-(u/s)^{2}/2},\quad\,\,\text{where}\,\,u=\cos(\Theta)^{1/\alpha}\zeta

Make one more change of variable:

,

we arrive at the final form presented in (

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\cos\left(\tau\,{(st)}^{\alpha}+\frac{x}{q}\,st\right)e^{-t^{2}/2}\quad(s\geq 0)

\displaystyle\,\,\text{where}\,\,q=\cos(\theta\pi/2)^{1/\alpha},\,\,\tau=\tan(\theta\pi/2)

∎

Next, we prove that

is indeed

\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1}(s)
.

Proof.

The terms inside the second big parenthesis of (

) is simplified by
(

(57)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2\Gamma(\frac{1}{\alpha}+1)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}s^{-1}V_{\alpha}(s)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{N}_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(s;\sigma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},d=0,p=\alpha)

\displaystyle=\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1}(s)

So according to (
57
) and (

),
the
\alpha
-stable PDF (

) becomes a ratio distribution

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}^{\theta}({x})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1}(s)

∎

6.2.
Some Properties of the Skew Kernel

Some properties of
g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)
are analyzed in this section.
This will help us better understand its behavior intuitively, as well as
provide a better implementation.

First, the integral inside
g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)
is bounded by the Gaussian integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-t^{2}/2}
, where
e^{-t^{2}/2}
is the upper bound
to the absolute of the integrand since
|\cos(z)|\leq 1
. Therefore,
the contribution of the integrand decreases rapidly as
t
gets larger.
This is the main reason such representation is chosen.
Implementation-wise, it is better to cut off the integral at a certain large
t
(e.g. 100).

Indeed, when
\theta=0
, the Gaussian integral becomes exact, that is,

g_{\alpha}^{0}(x,s)=\mathcal{N}(xs)
; and
L_{\alpha}^{0}(x)
becomes symmetric.

Proof.

When
\theta=0
,
q=1
and
\tau=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}^{0}(x,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\cos\left(xst\right)e^{-t^{2}/2}

From (
135
), this is
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(xs)^{2}/2}=\mathcal{N}(xs)
.
∎

When
\theta\neq 0
, some parts of
g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)
can be negative.
Hence, it is not a distribution per se.

When
\alpha=1
, we have

g_{1}^{\theta}(x,s)=\frac{1}{q}\,\mathcal{N}\left(\left(\tau+\frac{x}{q}\right)s\right)
,
and
L_{1}^{\theta}(x)
becomes symmetric over
x_{0}=-q\tau=-\sin(\theta\pi/2)
.
That is, the effect of
\theta
is no longer skewness, but a shift in location.

Proof.

When
\alpha=1
, the argument of
\cos()
in (
28
)
becomes
\tau\,{(st)}+\frac{x}{q}\,st=Yt

where
Y=\left(\tau+\frac{x}{q}\right)s
.
Therefore, from (
135
),

\displaystyle g_{1}^{\theta}(x,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\cos\left(Yt\right)e^{-t^{2}/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}\mathcal{N}(Y)=\frac{1}{q}\,\mathcal{N}\left(\left(\tau+\frac{x}{q}\right)s\right)

∎

At
s=0
,
g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,0)
is simply a constant
1/\sqrt{2\pi}q
.
This is straightforward since
g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,0)=\frac{1}{q\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-t^{2}/2}
. Furthermore, for a given
(\alpha,\theta)
pair,

|g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)|\leq 1/\sqrt{2\pi}q
for all
x
’s and
s
’s.

Next we prove:

Lemma 6.1
.

For a given
s>0
,
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)\,dx=1/s
.

Proof.

We integrate the
\alpha
-stable PDF (
58
)
by the full range of
x
, and it should be one:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}L_{\alpha}^{\theta}({x})\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,dx\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1}(s)=1

Rearranging the double integrals, it becomes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)\,dx\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1}(s)

\displaystyle=1

Since
\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1}(s)
is a PDF, we have
\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1}(s)\,ds=1
.
This forces the integral inside the big parenthesis to be

, which is

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g_{\alpha}^{\theta}(x,s)\,dx=1/s
.

∎

7.1.
Derivation of Fractional Chi

First of all, after many experiments (elaborated here
and also the subordination results in Appendix

I’ve come to realization that we need to use
a ratio distribution (
115
),
instead of a product distribution (
114
), to describe
the inner structures of both Student’s t and
\alpha
-stable.
This is a paradigm change.

Secondly, after many analyses, I’ve settled down on using a normal distribution,
instead of other alternatives, such as Laplace or Cauchy, as the unit distribution in the middle.
This has been formalized into the continuous Gaussian mixture in Section
2
.

The advantage of a normal distribution is obvious from the perspective of stochastic calculus.
Whatever distributional equation we come up with can be translated to the random variable generation directly.
Symbolically,

Section

random variable generation, that is, how

Thirdly, for the expression of the normal distribution, we’ve evaluated two options:
(a) use the notion of variance (which we like to use

for it in (

));
or (b) use the notion of volatility (that is, squared root of variance,
which we like to use
s
for it in (
5.7
)).
After many thoughts, we didn’t chose the variance route.

We wouldn’t say the last point is totally set in stone.
Searches on ”chi-squared distribution” vs ”chi distribution” showed that
people like to talk about sum of square of variables,
instead of squared root of that sum.
But we find that our formula is better presented by the later, instead of the former.

During the following discussions, we assume

.

degrees of freedom
.

and
F_{R}(r)
be the PDF and CDF of
R
. Their relation is

\displaystyle F_{R}(r+dr)-F_{R}(r)

\displaystyle=f_{R}(r)dr

\displaystyle=\int_{V}\prod_{i=1}^{k}S_{\alpha}(x_{i})dx_{i}

where
S_{\alpha}(x_{i})
is the PDF of the
S_{\alpha}
distribution,
and
V
is the volume on the
(k-1)
-dimensional surface in the
k
-space
at the radius
r
.

Assume we can refactor the above integral such that

\displaystyle f_{R}(r)dr

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{V}dx_{1}...dx_{k}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}S_{\alpha}(x_{i})\right)

where we deal with the two big parentheses separately.

The first part is an integral of the surface area of the
(k-1)
-dimensional sphere
with radius
r
, which is

(61)

\displaystyle A(r)dr

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{V}dx_{1}...dx_{k}\right)=2\,r^{k-1}\frac{\pi^{k/2}}{\Gamma(k/2)}\,dr

For the second part, we argue it should just be
\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1}
in (
60
):

(62)

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}S_{\alpha}(x_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\sim\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1}(r)=\frac{2}{r}\,W_{-\frac{\alpha}{2},0}\left(-{(\sqrt{2}r)}^{\alpha}\right)

Combining (
61
) and (
62
),
we arrive at the PDF for
R
(apart from a normalization constant):

\displaystyle f_{R}(r)

\displaystyle\propto r^{k-2}\,W_{-\frac{\alpha}{2},0}\left(-{(\sqrt{2}r)}^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{N}_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(r;\sigma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},d=k-1,p=\alpha)

Therefore,
f_{R}(r)
is the fractional-chi
\chi_{\alpha,k}(r)
that we seek.

Note that we glossed over the constant term.
We picked up the
r^{k-1}
factor from
A(r)
but ignored the rest of it.
This ”surface area of the
(k-1)
-dimensional sphere” is more complicated than
a simple Euclidean sphere.
This topic is left for future research.

7.4.
From Fractional-Chi to Fractional Chi-Mean

Now we have
\chi_{\alpha,k}(x)=\mathfrak{N}_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x;\sigma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},d=k-1,p=\alpha)
.
We need to ”standardize” its mean, so to speak.
Our cue is that
\overline{\chi}_{k}=\chi_{k}/\sqrt{k}
works for Student’s t.
A simple multiplier of
1/\sqrt{k}
for
\sigma
suffices.
The question is: What works for
\chi_{\alpha,k}
in general?

We examine the mean of
\chi_{\alpha,k}
. From (
51
), its mean is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X|\chi_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{\alpha})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}

At
k=1
,
\mathbb{E}(X|\chi_{\alpha,1})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+1\right)
,
which we want to preserve for
\alpha
-stable.

Its large
k
asymptotic is

(63)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X|\chi_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle\sim k^{1/\alpha-1/2}\,\alpha^{-1/\alpha},\quad k\gg 1;

whose
k
dependency we would like to eliminate so that the mean doesn’t go to 0 or infinity.

So our requirement for FCM is to modify the
\sigma
parameter in
\chi_{\alpha,k}
,
according to the
k
component of
\chi_{\alpha,k}
’s asymptotic mean,
such that FCM has an asymptotic mean independent of
k
.

The solution is the following. Define

(64)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha,k}

\displaystyle:=\frac{k^{1/2-1/\alpha}}{\sqrt{2}}

which has the desired properties:
\sigma_{1,k}=1/\sqrt{2k}
,
and
\sigma_{\alpha,1}=1/\sqrt{2}
, in order to preserve both Student’s t
and
\alpha
-stable distributions.

Hence, FCM is constructed as

(65)

\displaystyle\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathfrak{N}_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x;\sigma=\sigma_{\alpha,k},d=k-1,p=\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{N}_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(x;\sigma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(k^{1/2-1/\alpha}\right),d=k-1,p=\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{\alpha})}\,{\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{k^{1/2-1/\alpha}}\right)}^{k-1}{x}^{k-2}\,W_{-\frac{\alpha}{2},0}\left(-{\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\,x}{k^{1/2-1/\alpha}}\right)}^{\alpha}\right)

To verify, its large
k
asymptotic mean doesn’t have
k
dependency:
(See Section
7.8
)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-1/\alpha}

This meets our design goal.

\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,1})=\mathbb{E}(X|\chi_{\alpha,1})

and
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{1,k})=1
as expected.
And it is nice to have
\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{2,k})=1/\sqrt{2}
for every
k
.

Interpretation:
The
\alpha
-stable law states that

\displaystyle X_{1}+X_{2}+...+X_{k}

\displaystyle\sim k^{1/\alpha}X

Therefore, the
\left(k^{1/2-1/\alpha}\right)
term can be viewed as the relative scale (ratio)
between the normal scaling factor
k^{1/2}
and
\alpha
-stable scaling factor
k^{1/\alpha}
.
That is what we added to
\sigma_{\alpha,k}
as the way to ”demean” the
k
dependency.

7.5.
FCM Hankel Integral

From (
50
), make the following substitutions:

\alpha/p=1/2,d=k-1,\alpha d/p=(k-1)/2,p=\alpha
,
and
\sigma=\sigma_{\alpha,k}
.
The Hankel integral of FCM for
k>0
is

(66)

\displaystyle\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}({x})

\displaystyle=\,\Gamma\left(\frac{k-1}{2}\right)\,\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{H}dt\,\left(\frac{e^{t}}{t^{(k-1)/2}}\right)\,f_{\text{\tiny GG}}\left(x;a=\frac{\sigma_{\alpha,k}}{\sqrt{t}},d=k-1,p=\alpha\right)

This expression requires
k\neq 1
.

FCM Moments

FCM’s moment is a special case of GSC’s moment, which is carried out in Section
5.6
.

For
k>0
, based on (
20
) and (

),
substitute
\alpha/p=1/2,d=k-1,p=\alpha

7.7.
FCM Reflection Formula

We prove the reflection formula shown in (
23
)
and (
24
).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,-k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}(X^{-n+1}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})},\,\,k>0.

The approach is to show that the moments evaluated from the RHS via (
67
)
is the same as
those of the LHS from (
68
) with
k
replaced with
-k
.

Proof.

Assume
k>0
, the
n
-th moment on the LHS is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\text{LHS})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,-k})={\left(\frac{{k}^{1/2-1/\alpha}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}^{-n}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{\alpha})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-n}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-n}{2})}

The
n
-th moment on the RHS is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\text{RHS})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n-3}}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\,dx

Make a change of variable
t=1/x
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\text{RHS})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{-n+1}}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}\left(t\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}(X^{-n+1}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}

Hence, (
24
) is true
if
\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\text{RHS})
=
\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\text{LHS})
.

Plug (
67
) to both the numerator and denominator,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\text{RHS})

\displaystyle=\left({\left(\frac{{|k|}^{1/2-1/\alpha}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}^{-n+1}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{\alpha})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{-n+k}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\frac{-n+k}{2})}\right)\bigg/\left({\left(\frac{{|k|}^{1/2-1/\alpha}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{\alpha})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}\right)

\displaystyle={\left(\frac{{|k|}^{1/2-1/\alpha}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}^{-n}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{\alpha})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-n}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-n}{2})}

It is clear that
\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\text{RHS})
and
\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\text{LHS})
are the same.

∎

7.8.
FCM at Infinite Degrees of Freedom

First, we establish the following lemma:

Lemma 7.1
.

We use (
131
) to simplify (
67
) and
obtain the asymptotic value of the
n
-th moment for
k\gg 1
:

(69)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle\sim{\left(\frac{{|k|}^{1/2-1/\alpha}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}^{n}\,{\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)}^{-n/2}{\left(\frac{k}{\alpha}\right)}^{n/\alpha}={\alpha}^{-n/\alpha}

It follows from the reflection rule that

\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,-k})\sim{\alpha}^{n/\alpha}
.
Notice that the asymptotic value has no dependency on
k
.

The proof for Lemma
3.2
becomes very easy,
which states that FCM becomes a delta function at
x=m_{\alpha}
when
k\gg 1
,
where
m_{\alpha}
is its first moment.
We prove it by showing

m_{\alpha}
is
{\alpha}^{-1/\alpha}

and its variance is zero.

Proof.

Let
m_{\alpha}={\alpha}^{-1/\alpha}
, we have

\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})\sim{(m_{\alpha})}^{n}
.
Hence, the first moment is obviously

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})\sim{\alpha}^{-1/\alpha}

The second moment is
\mathbb{E}(X^{2}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})\sim m_{\alpha}^{2}
.
Therefore, its variance is
\mathbb{E}(X^{2}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})-m_{\alpha}^{2}=0
.
FCM becomes a delta function as
k\to\infty
.

On the negative side, the reflection rule ensures the same proof applies
such that

\mathbb{E}(X^{2}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,-k})-\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,-k})^{2}=0
.
FCM becomes a delta function as
-k\to-\infty
.

∎

8.
GSaS: Generalized Symmetric alpha-Stable Distribution

The PDF of a GSaS takes the form of an elegant ratio distribution:

\displaystyle L_{\alpha,k}({x})

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,\mathcal{N}(xs)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

This two-sided distribution is one of the most important outcomes of this work.
We prove some closed form results in this section.

8.1.
GSaS Peak PDF

The peak PDF of a GSaS at
x=0
comes from the first moment of FCM
in Section
7.6
:

\displaystyle L_{\alpha,k}({0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{k^{1/2-1/\alpha}}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{\alpha})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})},&\,\,k>0.\\[14.45377pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}{\left({|k|}^{1/2-1/\alpha}\right)}}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{|k|}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{|k|}{\alpha})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{|k|-1}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\frac{|k|-1}{2})},&\,\,k<0.\end{array}\right.

When
k={\pm}1
, it requires the typical special handling of the gamma function at zero.

The
standardized peak PDF
is the peak PDF
L_{\alpha,k}({0})
multiplied by
the standard deviation,
\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(X^{2}|L_{\alpha,k})}
, if it exists.
We argue that the standardized peak PDF is an important statistic since it is invariant
to the scale of the sample data. In finance, this means that the standardized peak PDF
measures the similarity of two data sets
taken from two periods of different volatility environments,
or from two different assets altogether.

Aligning the peak PDF was often impossible in the past due to the limited
one dimensional parameter space in either
\alpha
or
k
.
GSaS overcomes it with a two-dimensional parameter space.
This opens up much greater flexibility,
e.g., to match the standard deviation, the peak density, and the kurtosis simultaneously.

The first validation of
L_{\alpha,k}({0})
is that
it becomes the peak PDF of the original
\alpha
-stable distribution when
k\to 1
:

\displaystyle L_{\alpha,1}(0)=P_{\alpha}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\Gamma\left(1+1/\alpha\right)

The second validation is that
it becomes the peak PDF of the exponential power distribution when
k\to-1
:

\displaystyle L_{\alpha,-1}(0)=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\Gamma\left(1+1/\alpha\right)}

8.2.
GSaS Moments

GSaS has closed form solution for all the moments. This is a great improvement over
the original
\alpha
-stable distribution.

Lemma 8.1
.

The
n
-th moment of GSaS is

(72)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|L_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\,\mathbb{E}(X^{-n}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}),\quad k\neq 0

when
n
is even. The odd moments are zero.

Its closed form is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|L_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{2^{n}}{\sqrt{\pi}\left(k^{1/2-1/\alpha}\right)^{n}}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\,\left(\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-1}{\alpha})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k-n-1}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\frac{k-n-1}{2})}\right),&\,\,k>0.\\[14.45377pt]
\displaystyle\frac{{({|k|}^{1/2-1/\alpha})}^{n}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\,\left(\frac{\Gamma(\frac{|k|}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{|k|}{\alpha})}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{|k|+n}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\frac{|k|+n}{2})}\right),&\,\,k<0.\end{array}\right.

When
k={\pm}1,n+1,-n
, it requires the typical special handling of the gamma function at zero.

This formula is continuous and well-behaves when
k
is

sufficiently large
, but it gets very complicated when
k
is small.
This is a complexity inherent from the
\alpha
-stable distribution.

Proof.

The integral of the
n
-th moment is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|L_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,x^{n}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,\mathcal{N}(xs)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,x^{n}\,\mathcal{N}(xs)\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

Inside the big parenthesis is the
n
-th moment of a normal distribution
(see (
137
)), which is

\frac{2^{n/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}s^{-n-1}\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)
.
Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|L_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\,\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{-n}\,ds\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}({s})

The remaining integral is the ”
-n
”-th moment of FCM.
Hence, we get (
72
).

To obtain the first line of (
8.1
),
copy (
67
) into (
72
),
and replace every
n
with
-n
.

Similarly, copy (
68
) into (
72
),
replace every
n
with
-n
,
and we reach the second line of (
8.1
).

∎

GSaS Variance:

The second moment
m_{2}
, aka the variance, is

\displaystyle m_{2}=\mathbb{E}(X^{2}|L_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(X^{-2}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})

In general,
m_{2}\neq 1
for finite
k
.

At
k\gg 1
, according to (
69
),
the variance of the normal-like distribution is

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}m_{2}

\displaystyle={\alpha}^{2/\alpha}

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to-\infty}m_{2}

\displaystyle={\alpha}^{-2/\alpha}

Both are independent of
k
.
This is the intended result from Section
7.8
.

8.3.
GSaS Kurtosis

Let
m_{4}=\mathbb{E}(X^{4}|L_{\alpha,k})
, it is straightforward to get
the kurtosis as

(75)

\displaystyle\frac{m_{4}}{m_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=3\,\frac{\mathbb{E}(X^{-4}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}{\mathbb{E}(X^{-2}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})^{2}}

The excess kurtosis is
\text{exKurt}=m_{4}/m_{2}^{2}-3
.

For
k>0
, the closed form is

\displaystyle\frac{m_{4}}{m_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle 3\,{\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{k-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{k-1}{\alpha}\right)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{k-5}{\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{k-5}{2}\right)}\right)}\bigg/{{\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{k-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{k-1}{\alpha}\right)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{k-3}{\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{k-3}{2}\right)}\right)}^{2}}

(76)

\displaystyle=3\,\left(\frac{k-5}{k-3}\right)\frac{\Gamma(s(k-5))\Gamma(s(k-1))}{\Gamma(s(k-3))^{2}},\quad\,\,\text{where}\,\,\,s=1/\alpha.

We prove the linear relation of excess kurtosis in (
35
)
for
k\gg 1
as follows.

Proof.

In Figure
5
, notice the singular point at
s=1/2,k=3
.
Hence we make a change of variable by
k_{3}:=k-3
:

\displaystyle\frac{m_{4}}{m_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=3\,\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{3}}\right)\frac{\Gamma(s(k_{3}-2))\Gamma(s(k_{3}+2))}{\Gamma(sk_{3})^{2}}

Next, the gamma functions are expanded via the Sterling’s formula (
132
)
for large
k_{3}
. It is simplified to

\displaystyle\frac{m_{4}}{m_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=3\,{\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{3}}\right)}^{sk_{3}-2s+1/2}\,{\left(1+\frac{2}{k_{3}}\right)}^{sk_{3}+2s-1/2}

Take the log on both sides, and retain the Taylor series of
\log(1+x)

up to
k_{3}^{-2}
. We have

\displaystyle\log{\left(\frac{m_{4}}{3\,m_{2}^{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{k_{3}}{\left(s-\frac{1}{2}\right)}

Since
m_{4}/m_{2}^{2}=3+\text{exKurt}
where
exKurt
is the excess kurtosis,
we arrive at the proof that

\displaystyle{\left(s-\frac{1}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{k-3}{4}\right)\log{\left(1+\frac{\text{exKurt}}{3}\right)}

∎

As
k\to\infty
, the excess kurtosis approaches zero.
GSaS becomes a normal distribution asymptotically,

\mathcal{N}(0,m_{2})
.
Only when
\alpha=1
,
\lim_{k\to\pm\infty}m_{2}=1
.
It becomes a
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

8.4.
GSaS CDF

The CDF formula of GSaS is straightforward since

\int_{-\infty}^{x}\,\mathcal{N}\left(z\right)dz

=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\erf\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
.

Lemma 8.2
.

The CDF of a GSaS is

(77)

\displaystyle\Phi[L_{\alpha,k}](x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\,L_{\alpha,k}\left(z\right)dz=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,ds\,\erf\left(\frac{xs}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

Proof.

We begin with the double integral via the PDF, such that

\displaystyle\Phi[L_{\alpha,k}](x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\,L_{\alpha,k}\left(z\right)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\,dz\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,\mathcal{N}(zs)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

The order of the two integrals is switched, and we make a change of variable
u=zs
:

\displaystyle\Phi[L_{\alpha,k}](x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,\left(\int_{-\infty}^{x}\,dz\mathcal{N}(zs)\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\,ds\,\left(\int_{-\infty}^{xs}\,du\mathcal{N}(u)\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

The inner integral is replaced with the error function:

\displaystyle\Phi[L_{\alpha,k}](x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\,ds\,\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\erf\left(\frac{xs}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,ds\,\erf\left(\frac{xs}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

We arrive at the desired formula for the CDF.
∎

8.5.
Fractional Hypergeometric Function

Due to the connection between the CDF of Student’s t and
the Gauss hypergeometric function

\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(a,b;c;z\right)
(defined in 15.2.1 of DLMF
[
6
]
),
we propose a framework to
extend
\,{}_{2}F_{1}
fractionally.

Definition 8.1

(Fractional hypergeometric function (FHF))
.

Let
M(b,c;z)
be the confluent hypergeometric function, or called the Kummer function,
defined in 13.2.2 of DLMF.
(Note we avoid using
a
because it is confusing to
\alpha
.)

The fractional hypergeometric function is defined in a ratio-distribution style as

(78)

\displaystyle M_{\alpha,k}(b,c;x)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{k}{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}s\,ds\,M\left(b,c;\frac{xks^{2}}{2}\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

Note that
x
usually takes a negative value. This is due to the asymptotic behavior:

M(b,c;z)\sim e^{z}z^{b-c}/\Gamma(b)
as
z\to\infty
(13.2.23 of DLMF).

Lemma 8.3
.

The CDF of a GSaS can be expressed by the fractional hypergeometric function as

The exponential power distribution

with the

We have shown that

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}(0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}/\mathbb{E}(X|{\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}})

The reader can take a closer look at Figure

Its moment formula is straightforward:

Lemma 9.1
.

The

-th moment of
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}
is

(87)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\mathcal{N})\,\mathbb{E}(X^{n+1}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}

Proof.

The integral of the

) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{n}\,dx\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\,ds\,\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

With a change of variable

, we can separate the two integrals:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^{n}\mathcal{N}\left(z\right)\,dz\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\,s^{n+1}\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}(X^{n}|\mathcal{N})\,\mathbb{E}(X^{n+1}|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}

∎

The excess kurtosis of
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}
in (
36

\mathbb{E}(X^{4}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k})/\mathbb{E}(X^{2}|\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k})^{2}

The CDF of
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}
is derived below by following
the same logic in Section
8.4

Lemma 9.2
.

The CDF of
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}
is

(88)

\displaystyle\Phi[\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}](x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\,\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}\left(z\right)dz=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,s\,ds\,\erf\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}\,s}\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

Proof.

We begin with the double integral via the PDF, such that

\displaystyle\Phi[\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}](x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\,dz\int_{0}^{\infty}\,ds\,\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{z}{s}\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

The order of the two integrals are switched, and we make a change of variable

\displaystyle\Phi[\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}](x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,ds\,\left(\int_{-\infty}^{x}\,dz\,\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{z}{s}\right)\right)\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

\displaystyle\Phi[\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}](x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}(X|\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,s\,ds\,\left[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\erf\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}\,s}\right)\right]\,\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}(s)

∎

Lemma 11.1
.

The

\displaystyle=\frac{(x\frac{d}{dx}+1)\,\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(x;\sigma,d,p)}{2\,\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(x;\sigma,d,p)}

As

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sigma_{u}^{2}\,\mu\left(x\right)\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(x;\sigma,d,p)\right]

\displaystyle\mu\left(x\right)\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(x;\sigma,d,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{(pz\frac{d}{dz}+1)\,\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(z;\sigma=1,d^{\prime},p=1)}{2\,\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(z;\sigma=1,d^{\prime},p=1)}

The critical algebra is

\displaystyle pz\frac{d}{dz}\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(z;\sigma=1,d^{\prime},p=1)

\displaystyle=Cp(d^{\prime}-1){z}^{d^{\prime}-1}\,W_{-\alpha,0}\left(-z\right)+Cp({z}^{d^{\prime}-1})\,\left(z\frac{d}{dz}W_{-\alpha,0}(-z)\right)

\displaystyle=C{z}^{d^{\prime}-1}\left[p(d^{\prime}-1)\,W_{-\alpha,0}\left(-z\right)+\frac{p}{-\alpha}\left(W_{-\alpha,-1}(-z)+W_{-\alpha,0}(-z)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{\alpha}C{z}^{d^{\prime}-1}W_{-\alpha,-1}(-z)+\left(pd^{\prime}-\frac{p}{\alpha}-p\right)\,C{z}^{d^{\prime}-1}\,W_{-\alpha,0}\left(-z\right)

We use the following recurrence relation:

(97)

\displaystyle-\alpha z\frac{d}{dz}W_{-\alpha,0}(-z)

\displaystyle=W_{-\alpha,-1}(-z)+W_{-\alpha,0}(-z)

to move from the second line to the third line above.
(To derive it, set
\lambda=-\alpha,\mu=0

in (
117
) and (
118
)).

The
\mu(x)
solution becomes

\displaystyle\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{-p}{\alpha}\frac{W_{-\alpha,-1}(-z)}{W_{-\alpha,0}(-z)}+\left(pd^{\prime}-\frac{p}{\alpha}-p\right)\right]+\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{2\alpha}\frac{W_{-\alpha,-1}\left(-{\left(\frac{x}{\sigma}\right)}^{p}\right)}{W_{-\alpha,0}\left(-{\left(\frac{x}{\sigma}\right)}^{p}\right)}+\left(\frac{d}{2}-\frac{p}{2\alpha}\right)

which is the intended solution.

∎

The next question is how to compute
Q_{\alpha}(z)
.
In addition to going through the series representation of
W_{-\alpha,-1}(-z)
,
the following lemma addresses such issue.

Lemma 11.3
.

There are two methods to compute
Q_{\alpha}(z)
depending on the knowledge of

F_{\alpha}(z)
or
M_{\alpha}(z)
. They are

(98)

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha}(z)=-\frac{W_{-\alpha,-1}\left(-z\right)}{W_{-\alpha,0}\left(-z\right)}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\alpha z\,\frac{d}{dz}F_{\alpha}(z)}{F_{\alpha}(z)}

(99)

\displaystyle=(\alpha+1)+\frac{\alpha z\,\frac{d}{dz}M_{\alpha}(z)}{M_{\alpha}(z)}

The second line (
99
) allows for high-precision implementation
through
M_{\alpha}(z)
and its first derivative (
126
),
both of which have nice analytic properties.

Proof.

Note that (
97
) is the same as

\displaystyle W_{-\alpha,-1}(-z)

\displaystyle=-\left(\alpha z\frac{d}{dz}+1\right)W_{-\alpha,0}(-z)

Divide both sides by
F_{\alpha}(z)=W_{-\alpha,0}(-z)
,
we arrive at (
98
).

To move from (
98
) to (
99
),
simply replace
F_{\alpha}(z)
by
\alpha z\,M_{\alpha}(z)
, and expand
the derivative into it.

∎

To validate
\mu(x)
, first we note that, by setting
\sigma=1,d=1,p=\alpha
,
it is reduced to that of a SC.
Secondly, by setting
\sigma=1/\sqrt{2},d=1,p/\alpha=2

\alpha

, when the closed form of

) is known,

(100)

\displaystyle\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{pz}{2}\,\frac{d}{dz}\left[\log\mathfrak{N}_{\alpha}(z;\sigma=1,d^{\prime},p=1)\right]+\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha=\frac{1}{2}}\left(x\right)

\mu_{\alpha,1}(x)

11.3.
Generation of Random Variables for Inverse FCM

The simulation of GEP is complicated by two options: ratio vs product.
If we go with the ratio distribution, we have to deal
evaluating
F_{\alpha}(z)
at very large
z
,
which is technically more difficult.

To simulate
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,k}
, we set
\sigma=1/\sigma_{\alpha,k},d=-k,p/\alpha=-2

and
\alpha
replaced with
\alpha/2
in (
95
), we get

(105)

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha,-k}(x)

\displaystyle=-Q_{\alpha}^{(\chi)}\left({\left(x\,\sigma_{\alpha,k}\right)}^{-1}\right)+\left(1-\frac{k}{2}\right)

\displaystyle(k>0)

The
x^{-1}
term is the added complication.

To avoid such complication, it is more straightforward to take the product distribution route,
that is,
X_{t}=S_{t}\,\mathcal{N}
. And
S_{t}
is generated from an inverse FCM:

(106)

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha,-k}^{\dagger}(x)

\displaystyle=Q_{\alpha}^{(\chi)}\left(\frac{x}{\sigma_{\alpha,k}}\right)+\left(\frac{k}{2}-1\right)

\displaystyle(k>0)

And
\mu_{\alpha,-1}^{\dagger}(x)
provides a method to simulate
an exponential power distribution
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha,-1}^{\dagger}(x)

\displaystyle=Q_{\alpha}^{(\chi)}\left(\sqrt{2}x\right)-\frac{1}{2}

As is expected, this is identical to
\mu(x)
of SV.

The simplest validation is
\mu_{1,-1}^{\dagger}(x)=1-\frac{x^{2}}{2}
which can simulate
an exponential distribution
\mathcal{E}_{1}
.
On the other hand, to simulate
\mathcal{E}_{1}
by a ratio distribution,
the polynomial solution is
\mu_{1,-1}(x)=1/(2x^{2})-1
.
The divergence at
x=0
is a very different behavior.

In summary,
\mu_{\alpha,k}(x)
is an
amazing function
that generates
not only
\overline{\chi}_{\alpha,k}
but also
L_{\alpha,k}
.
In some cases, they are just simple polynomials. This is very impressive.
