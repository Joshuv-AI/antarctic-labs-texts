Runs in Random Sequences over Ordered Sets
Tanner Reese

Abstract
We determine the distributions of lengths of runs in random sequences of elements from a totally ordered set (total order) or partially ordered set (partial order). In particular, we produce novel formulae for the expected value, variance, and probability generating function (PGF) of such lengths in the case of an arbitrary total order. Our focus is on the case of distributions with both atoms and diffuse (absolutely or singularly continuous) mass which has not been addressed in this generality before. We also provide a method of calculating the PGF of run lengths for countably series-parallel partial orders. Additionally, we prove a strong law of large numbers for the distribution of run lengths in a particular realization of an infinite sequence.

Introduction

A
run
in a sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
is a maximal subsequence of consecutive monotonic elements.
We say
\langle a_{i},\ldots,a_{i+n-1}\rangle
is a
strict run
when

\begin{split}a_{i-1}&\nless a_{i}<a_{i+1}<\ldots<a_{i+n-1}\nless a_{i+n}\quad\text{ or }\\
a_{i-1}&\ngtr a_{i}>a_{i+1}>\ldots>a_{i+n-1}\ngtr a_{i+n}\end{split}

and
\langle a_{i},\ldots,a_{i+n-1}\rangle
is a
non-strict run
when

\begin{split}a_{i-1}&\nleq a_{i}\leq a_{i+1}\leq\ldots\leq a_{i+n-1}\nleq a_{i+n}\quad\text{ or }\\
a_{i-1}&\ngeq a_{i}\geq a_{i+1}\geq\ldots\geq a_{i+n-1}\ngeq a_{i+n}\end{split}

where
a_{i-1}
or
a_{i+n}
may not exist if the run starts at zero or extends to infinity.
Here, we say the runs begin at index
i
and have length
n
(if the run extends to infinity we say
n=\infty
).
When the values are ascending (resp. non-descending), the run is a strict (resp. non-strict)
run up

and if the values are descending (resp. non-ascending) then it is a strict (resp. non-strict)
run down
.
Because runs up and down may be swapped by reversing the ordering relation,
we will just consider runs up for the remainder of the paper.

Suppose
T
is a partial order and
\mu
is a probability measure on
T
.
Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables
on the probability space
\langle\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\rangle
distributed according to
\mu
.
We are interested in the distribution of the lengths of runs in the sequence
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots

particularly when
\mu
has atoms and/or
T
is a countably series-parallel partial order
(definition in
section

2.2
).

Runs have been studied extensively in the context of permutations beginning with André
[
2
]
.
Of particular interest has been the enumeration of permutations with a given number of runs
and the generating functions associated with these numbers
[
5
,
11
,
6
]
.
A general treatment of runs and other patterns in permutations can be found in Bóna’s book
[
4
]

with a review of probabilistic methods in chapter six.
The distribution of the length of runs in permutations can be used to calculate
the distribution of the length of runs in a sequence when
\mu
is diffuse (continuous).
However, since permutations do not allow duplicate entries,
the behavior of runs in permutations will not reflect the behavior of
runs in sequences where
\mu
contains atoms (and perhaps diffuse mass).

Runs in random sequences are also closely related to ascents, descents,
and records (running maxima and minima) which have been explored extensively.
The asymptotic theory of order statistics has been well studied.
For example, a theorem of Rényi
[
14
]
regarding records
can be used to calculate the distribution of run lengths
when
\mu
is diffuse (continuous).
However, there are fewer results in the non-diffuse case.
Existing theory for records in the non-diffuse case
is focused on the mean, variance, and position of records for discrete distributions,
but does not address runs or mixed distributions
(see
[
7
]
, chapter 6 of
[
1
]
, or for a survey
[
13
]
).
In this paper, we will be considering not just the discrete case,
but cases in which the distribution contains both discrete and continuous parts.
We will also consider a broad class of partial orders which we call countably series-parallel
(see
section

2.2
for a definition).

Research into runs in permutations has found practical applications in computer science.
The number of runs of a given length in a permutation was used as a test of randomness in section 3.3.2 of
[
8
]
.
Runs and their lengths are also important for sorting algorithms as described in section 5.1.3 of
[
9
]
.
Given these applications, it seems valuable to investigate runs in the context of i.i.d. random sequences
where the distribution may contain atoms.

We will now define the run length as a random variable.
For each index
i\geq 0
, we define
\accentset{\circ}{S}_{i}
(resp.
\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{i}
)
as the event that a strict (resp. non-strict) run begins at
i
.
More precisely, the events will be

\begin{split}\accentset{\circ}{S}_{i}:=\begin{cases}\Omega&\text{ when }i=0\\
\{X_{i-1}\nless X_{i}\}&\text{ when }i\geq 1\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{i}:=\begin{cases}\Omega&\text{ when }i=0\\
\{X_{i-1}\nleq X_{i}\}&\text{ when }i\geq 1\end{cases}.\end{split}

We also define the random variable
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
(resp.
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
)
as the length of the strict (resp. non-strict) run beginning at
i
assuming one is present.
More precisely, for any
n\in\{1,2,\ldots,\infty\}
,

\begin{split}\left\{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}=n\right\}&:=\begin{cases}\{X_{0}<\ldots<X_{n-1}\nless X_{i+n}\}&\text{ when }i=0\text{ and }n<\infty\\
\{X_{0}<X_{1}<\ldots\}&\text{ when }i=0\text{ and }n=\infty\\
\{X_{i-1}\nless X_{i}<\ldots<X_{i+n-1}\nless X_{i+n}\}&\text{ when }i\geq 1\text{ and }n<\infty\\
\{X_{i-1}\nless X_{i}<X_{i+1}<\ldots\}&\text{ when }i\geq 1\text{ and }n=\infty\end{cases}\\
\left\{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}=n\right\}&:=\begin{cases}\{X_{0}\leq\ldots\leq X_{n-1}\nleq X_{i+n}\}&\text{ when }i=0\text{ and }n<\infty\\
\{X_{0}\leq X_{1}\leq\ldots\}&\text{ when }i=0\text{ and }n=\infty\\
\{X_{i-1}\nleq X_{i}\leq\ldots\leq X_{i+n-1}\nleq X_{i+n}\}&\text{ when }i\geq 1\text{ and }n<\infty\\
\{X_{i-1}\nleq X_{i}\leq X_{i+1}\leq\ldots\}&\text{ when }i\geq 1\text{ and }n=\infty\end{cases}.\end{split}

Notice that
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
are
not
defined on the entire probability space
\Omega
.
Instead, they are restricted to
\accentset{\circ}{S}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{i}
, respectively.
In order for
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
to be well-defined,
\{X_{0}<X_{1}\}
must be measurable.
We will assume that this is the case throughout the rest of the paper.
The probability generating functions (PGFs) of
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
are the formal power series

G_{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}}(Z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}=n\,\middle|\,\accentset{\circ}{S}_{i}\right)Z^{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad G_{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}}(Z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}=n\,\middle|\,\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{i}\right)Z^{n}.

Our main interest in this paper will be calculating the PGFs,
G_{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}}
and
G_{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}}
,
and from these determining the means and variances of the run lengths.
Because of the generality of
T
and
\mu
, there are many examples of random sequences
that can be modelled this way.

•

Suppose we have a block of radioactive material
and we let
X_{i}
be the amount of time between the
i
-th and
(i+1)
-th decay.
We can take
T=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\mu
is exponential with
\mu([0,t))=1-e^{-\lambda t}
.
Then,
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
(resp.
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
) is the number of decay events starting with the
i
-th
for which the amount of time between decays is strictly increasing (resp. non-decreasing).

•

Suppose we have an evenly weighted
n
-sided die.
We let
X_{i}
represent the value of the
i
-th roll.
In this case,
T=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\mu(\{k\})=\frac{1}{n}
for each
k\in T
.
Then,
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
(resp.
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
) is the number of rolls starting with the
i
-th
for which the value is strictly increasing (resp. non-decreasing).

•

Suppose we have a dart board with a bullseye.
For any two throws that land outside the bullseye, we say the one that is closer to the bullseye is better.
However, for any throws landing in the bullseye, we say they are all equally good.
The total order
T
can be described as
(0,1]
.
Here,
1
represents a throw landing in the bullseye and
(0,1)
represents throws landing outside
with
x>y
when
x
is closer to the bullseye.
The probability measure
\mu
assigns mass
p\in(0,1)
to the bullseye
\mu(\{1\})=p

and the remaining mass is uniformly distributed over
(0,1)
so
\mu(A)=(1-p)m(A)

for any Borel set
A
where
m
is the Lebesgue measure.
Then,
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
(resp.
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
) is the number of throws starting with the
i
-th throw
for which each throw is better (resp. not worse) than the previous.

•

Suppose there is a light bulb socket
whose bulb has a probability
p\in(0,1)
of going out each day.
We keep track of the number of days each light bulb lasts.
If we let
X_{i}
be the number of days the
i
-th bulb lasts
then
T=\{1,2,\ldots\}
and
\mu
is geometric with
\mu(\{k\})=p(1-p)^{k-1}
.
Then,
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
(resp.
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
) is the number of bulbs starting with the
i
-th bulb
for which every bulb lasts longer than (resp. at least as long as) the previous.

•

Suppose one has an evenly weighted
2n
-sided die.
We want to require that runs are not just increasing (non-decreasing),
but that the elements of a run are all of the same parity.
That is the sequence
1,3,7,8,2,4,5,9
would have runs
1,3,7\;|\;8\;|\;2,4\;|\;5,9
.
This can be achieved by taking
T=\{1,2,\ldots,2n\}
to be a partial order where

1<3<\ldots<2n-1\quad\text{ and }\quad 2<4<\ldots<2n

and
i,j\in T
are incomparable whenever
i
and
j
are of different parity.
As above, we take
\mu(\{k\})=\frac{1}{2n}
for each
k\in T
.
Then,
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
(resp.
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
) represents the number of rolls starting with the
i
-th roll
for which the value is strictly increasing (resp. non-decreasing) and the parity is the same.

We will present a method of calculating the PGFs
for
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
in all of these cases.
The PGFs, means, and variances for these examples can be found in
section

3
.
This method is applicable whenever
T
is countably series-parallel (which includes total orders)
and
\mu
is a probability measure (potentially containing atoms).

Results

This section contains the statements of the theorems and corollaries.
Their proofs can be found in the later sections.
First, we will state our primary results for total orders
and then, in
section

2.2
, we will consider partial corders.

2.1
Total Orders

We say
x\in T
is an
atom
of
\mu
if
\mu(\{x\})>0
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the set of atoms of
\mu
.
For each
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, we say
m_{\alpha}=\mu(\{\alpha\})
.
The mass of
\mu
not containing atoms is the
diffuse mass
,
m_{d}=\mu(T\setminus\mathcal{A})
.
We will be considering
\mu
that contain both atoms and diffuse mass.
In particular, when
T=\mathbb{R}
, this means that
\mu

may contain discrete, singularly continuous, and absolutely continuous sections.
If there exists
x\in T
such that
\mu(\{x\})=\mu(T)
then we say
\mu
is
degenerate

and otherwise
\mu
is
non-degenerate
.

Theorem 1
.

If
T
is a total order and
\mu
is a non-degenerate probability measure on
T
then
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,
the PGFs of
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
will fulfill

G_{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{0}}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\left(1+(z-1)e^{m_{d}z}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}(1+m_{\alpha}z)\right)\quad\text{ and for }|z|\leq 1,\quad G_{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{0}}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\left(1+(z-1)e^{m_{d}z}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha}z}\right)

(1)

G_{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}}(z)=1+\frac{2(z-1)}{z\left(1+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}\right)}G_{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{0}}(z)\quad\text{ and for }|z|\leq 1,\quad G_{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}}(z)=1+\frac{2(z-1)}{z\left(1-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}\right)}G_{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{0}}(z)\text{ when }i\geq 1.

(2)

From the PGFs, it is possible to immediately calculate the means and variances.

Corollary 2
.

For any total order
T
and non-degenerate probability measure
\mu
on
T
,
the means of the run lengths will be

\mathbb{E}\left[\accentset{\circ}{N}_{0}\right]=-1+e^{m_{d}}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}(1+m_{\alpha})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{0}\right]=-1+e^{m_{d}}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha}}

\mathbb{E}\left[\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}\,\middle|\,\accentset{\circ}{S}_{i}\right]=\frac{2}{1+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}\,\middle|\,\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{i}\right]=\frac{2}{1-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}}\quad\text{ when }i\geq 1.

Further, the variances of the run lengths will be

\begin{split}\mathrm{var}\left(\accentset{\circ}{N}_{0}\right)&=\left(e^{m_{d}}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}(1+m_{\alpha})\right)\left(3-e^{m_{d}}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}(1+m_{\alpha})-2\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{m_{\alpha}^{2}}{1+m_{\alpha}}\right)\\
\mathrm{var}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{0}\right)&=\left(e^{m_{d}}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha}}\right)\left(3-e^{m_{d}}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha}}+2\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{m_{\alpha}^{2}}{1-m_{\alpha}}\right)\end{split}

(3)

\begin{split}\mathrm{var}\left(\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}\,\middle|\,\accentset{\circ}{S}_{i}\right)&=\frac{2}{1+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}}\left(-3+2e^{m_{d}}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}(1+m_{\alpha})-\frac{2}{1+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}}\right)\quad\text{ for }i\geq 1\\
\mathrm{var}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}\,\middle|\,\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{i}\right)&=\frac{2}{1-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}}\left(-3+2e^{m_{d}}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha}}-\frac{2}{1-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}}\right)\quad\text{ for }i\geq 1.\end{split}

(4)

2.2
Countably Series-Parallel Partial Orders

In this subsection, we will introduce a method for calculating the PGFs of the run lengths
whenever
T
is a countably series-parallel partial order (defined below).
First, however, we will state some results which apply to all partial orders.
To do this, we will introduce two very useful generating functions using
Z
as a formal indeterminate.
For any finite non-negative measure
\mu
on a partial order
T
,
we define the
strict run function

\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)
as

\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z):=1+\mu(T)Z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mu^{n}(\{\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in T^{n}\;:\;a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\})Z^{n}

(5)

and the
non-strict run function

\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z)
as

\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z):=1+\mu(T)Z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mu^{n}(\{\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in T^{n}\;:\;a_{1}\leq\ldots\leq a_{n}\})Z^{n}.

(6)

In particular, when
\mu
is a probability measure and
\{X_{i}\}
is defined as above, we have

\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)=1+Z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}<\ldots<X_{n}\right)Z^{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z)=1+Z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}\leq\ldots\leq X_{n}\right)Z^{n}.

The usefulness of these generating functions is justified by the following result
which holds for
any
partial order.

Theorem 3
.

For any partial order
T
and probability measure
\mu
on
T
,
the PGFs of
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
will be

G_{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{0}}(Z):=\frac{1+(Z-1)\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)}{Z}\quad\text{ and }\quad G_{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{0}}(Z):=\frac{1+(Z-1)\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z)}{Z}.

(7)

G_{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}}(Z)=1+\frac{2(Z-1)}{Z(2-\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0))}G_{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{0}}(Z)\quad\text{ and }\quad G_{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}}(Z)=1+\frac{2(Z-1)}{Z(2-\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0))}G_{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{0}}(Z)\text{ when }i\geq 1.

(8)

Further, the radius of convergence of
G_{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}}(Z)
will be infinite
and the radius of convergence of
G_{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}}(Z)
will be greater than or equal to
\frac{1}{\|\mu\|}

with equality when
\mu
is degenerate.

As before, we can use the PGFs to derive formulae for the means and variances of the run lengths.

Corollary 4
.

Suppose
T
is a partial order and
\mu
is a non-degenerate probability measure over
T
.
The run lengths
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}
are almost surely finite and their means are

\mathbb{E}\left[\accentset{\circ}{N}_{0}\right]=\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(1)-1\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{0}\right]=\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(1)-1

(9)

\mathbb{E}\left[\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}\,\middle|\,\accentset{\circ}{S}_{i}\right]=\frac{2}{2-\accentset{\circ}{P}^{\prime\prime}(0)}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}\,\middle|\,\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{i}\right]=\frac{2}{2-\accentset{\bullet}{P}^{\prime\prime}(0)}\quad\text{ for }i\geq 1.

(10)

Also, the variances of the run lengths will be

\mathrm{var}\left(\accentset{\circ}{N}_{0}\right)=\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(1)-\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(1)^{2}+2\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}^{\prime}(1)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{var}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{0}\right)=\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(1)-\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(1)^{2}+2\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}^{\prime}(1)

(11)

\mathrm{var}\left(\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}\,\middle|\,\accentset{\circ}{S}_{i}\right)=\frac{4\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(1)-6}{2-\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0)}-\frac{4}{(2-\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0))^{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{var}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}\,\middle|\,\accentset{\bullet}{S}_{i}\right)=\frac{4\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(1)-6}{2-\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0)}-\frac{4}{(2-\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0))^{2}}\quad\text{ for }i\geq 1.

(12)

While
3
holds for any partial order,
it remains to actually calculate the run functions.
In the case of total orders, we have the following formulae for the run functions.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose
T
is a total order and
\mu
is a finite non-negative and non-degenerate measure on
T
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be the atoms of
\mu
with masses
(m_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}

and
m_{d}
be the diffuse mass of
\mu
.
Then, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(z)=e^{m_{d}z}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}(1+m_{\alpha}z)\quad\text{ and when }|z|\leq\frac{1}{\|\mu\|},\quad\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(z)=e^{m_{d}z}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha}z}.

(13)

Using
5
together with
3
and
4
,
we can derive the results for total orders described in
1
and
2
.
We can also calculate the run functions in the case of countably series-parallel partial orders.
In order to define this class of partial orders, we will introduce the following operations.

Definition 1
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a totally ordered index set
and
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
is an indexed collection of partial orders.
We define the
series composition
and
parallel composition
of
\mathcal{T}

as the partial orders

\bigotimes\mathcal{T}=\bigotimes_{\gamma\in\Gamma}T_{\gamma}\quad\text{ and }\quad\bigoplus\mathcal{T}=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}T_{\gamma},

respectively.
The underlying set of
\bigotimes\mathcal{T}
and
\bigoplus\mathcal{T}
is the disjoint union
\bigsqcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}T_{\gamma}
.
For any
\gamma,\tilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma
with
\gamma\neq\tilde{\gamma}
, the ordering relation of
\bigotimes\mathcal{T}
is

\begin{split}\text{for all }&t_{1},t_{2}\in T_{\gamma},\qquad t_{1}\leq_{\bigotimes\mathcal{T}}\,t_{2}\iff t_{1}\leq_{T_{\gamma}}t_{2}\quad\text{ and}\\
\text{for all }&t\in T_{\gamma},\tilde{t}\in T_{\tilde{\gamma}},\qquad t\leq_{\bigotimes\mathcal{T}}\,\tilde{t}\iff\gamma<_{\Gamma}\tilde{\gamma}.\end{split}

and the ordering relation of
\bigoplus\mathcal{T}
is

\begin{split}\text{for all }&t_{1},t_{2}\in T_{\gamma},\qquad t_{1}\leq_{\bigoplus\mathcal{T}}\,t_{2}\iff t_{1}\leq_{T_{\gamma}}t_{2}\quad\text{ and}\\
\text{for all }&t\in T_{\gamma},\tilde{t}\in T_{\tilde{\gamma}},\qquad t\text{ is incomparable to }\tilde{t}.\end{split}

Note that the ordering on
\Gamma
has no effect on the parallel composition.

If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are partial orders, we can think of
T_{1}\otimes T_{2}
as the partial order
in which we place all of the elements of
T_{1}
below all of the elements of
T_{2}
.
Simiarly,
T_{1}\oplus T_{2}
is the partial order
in which we place all of the elements of
T_{1}
beside all of the elements of
T_{2}

so that every
x\in T_{1}
is incomparable to every
y\in T_{2}
.
The above operations have been studied and rediscovered a number of times.
As a result, there are a variety of different notations and names for them.
The above “series composition” is sometimes referred to as the
“linear sum”, “ordinal sum”, or “lexicographic sum”.
Furthermore the above “parallel composition” is sometimes referred to as the

\omega\in\Omega

of length

and
\accentset{\circ}{S}_{j}

and
\accentset{\circ}{S}_{j}

(

)
for any

.
That is to say the asymptotic distribution of lengths of runs in a particular infinite sequence
is also given by

.

and a permutation

over

such that

and

.

\langle\mu_{xy},T_{xy}\rangle

\langle\mu_{yx},T_{yx}\rangle

We are interested in the distribution of run lengths for

Proposition 8
.

For any
n\geq 0
and any

,
the events
\{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}=n\}
and
\{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}=n\}

are insensitive to rearrangement.

Proof.

Suppose
T
and
T^{\prime}
are total orders with finite non-negative measures
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
.
Also, assume that
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
are rearrangements.
If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
are their sets of atoms
then there must exist a bijection
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.
Further,
\mu(\{\alpha\})=\mu^{\prime}(\{\phi(\alpha)\})
for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.
Thus, the weights of the atoms are the same and

\mu(T\setminus\mathcal{A})=\mu(T)-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(\{\alpha\})=\mu(T)-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(\{\phi(\alpha)\})=\mu^{\prime}(T^{\prime})-\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\mu^{\prime}(\{\alpha^{\prime}\})=\mu^{\prime}(T^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{\prime})

so their diffuse masses are the same.
Therefore, by
5
, we know
\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)=\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu^{\prime}}(Z)
and
\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z)=\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z)
.
Then, by

and
4
, the PGFs of
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}

will be the same for
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
.
Because the PGFs determine the probabilities of
\{\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}=n\}
and
\{\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}=n\}
, we know

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}=n\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\prime}}\left(\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}=n\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}=n\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\prime}}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}=n\right).

∎

Rearrangement insensitivity may not seem like an especially unusual property.
However, most events that one encounters are
sensitive
to rearrangement.
In the following proposition, we will look at a class of examples of rearrangement sensitive events.

Proposition 9
.

Suppose
\mu
is a probability measure on a total order
T

and
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
is a random sequence in
T

independently and identically distributed as
\mu
.
Then, for any
n\geq 2
, the event

R_{n}:=\{X_{n}\geq\max(X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{n-1})\}

is sensitive to rearrangement.

Proof.

It suffices to consider a single counter-example.
We will use
\mu_{xy}
and
\mu_{yx}
from above.
Now, for any
n\geq 2
, using independence of
X_{0},\ldots,X_{n-1}
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{xy}}\left(R_{n}\right)&=\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{xy}}\left(X_{n}\geq X_{0},X_{n}\geq X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\geq X_{n-1}\right)\\
&=p\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{xy}}\left(X_{n}\geq X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}\geq X_{n-1}\,\middle|\,X_{n}=x\right)+(1-p)\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{yx}}\left(X_{n}\geq X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}\geq X_{n-1}\,\middle|\,X_{n}=y\right)\\
&=p\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{xy}}\left(x\geq X_{0}\right)\cdots\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{xy}}\left(x\geq X_{n-1}\right)+(1-p)\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{xy}}\left(y\geq X_{0}\right)\cdots\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{xy}}\left(y\geq X_{n-1}\right)\\
&=p\cdot p^{n}+(1-p)\cdot 1^{n}=p^{n+1}+(1-p).\end{split}

By a similar calculation, one concludes that
\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{yx}}\left(R_{n}\right)=(1-p)^{n+1}+p
.
Then, setting
p=\frac{1}{3}
and taking their difference

\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{xy}}\left(R_{n}\right)-\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{yx}}\left(R_{n}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{3^{n+1}}+\frac{2}{3}\right)-\left(\frac{2^{n+1}}{3^{n+1}}+\frac{1}{3}\right)=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1-2^{n+1}}{3^{n+1}}=\frac{3^{n}-2^{n+1}+1}{3^{n+1}}.

For all
n\geq 2
,
3^{n}>2^{n+1}
so
3^{n}-2^{n+1}+1>0
implying
\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{xy}}\left(R_{n}\right)\neq\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{yx}}\left(R_{n}\right)
.
∎

Notice from

Lemma 10
.

For any finite non-negative measure
\mu
on a partial order
T
,
the radius of convergence of
\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)
is infinite
and the radius of convergence of
\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z)

\sqrt{\frac{2}{\|\mu\|\cdot\left(\|\mu\|+\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(\{\alpha\})\right)}}\geq\frac{1}{\|\mu\|}

where equality occurs only if
\mu
is degenerate.

Proof.

First, we consider the strict case.
Let
n\geq 2
and let
\Sigma_{n}
be the set of permutations of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
For each
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
, we define

A_{\sigma}:=\{a_{\sigma(1)}<a_{\sigma(2)}<\ldots<a_{\sigma(n)}\}_{T^{n}}.

Now, if
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{n}
with
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
then
A_{\sigma}\cap A_{\sigma^{\prime}}=\emptyset
,
because if
a_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,a_{\sigma(n)}
is an ascending sequence then
a_{\sigma^{\prime}(1)},\ldots,a_{\sigma^{\prime}(n)}
will not be.
Therefore, using symmetry

n!\cdot\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}=n!\cdot\mu^{n}(\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{T^{n}})=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}\mu^{n}(A_{\sigma})=\mu^{n}\left(\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}A_{\sigma}\right)\leq\mu^{n}(T^{n})=\|\mu\|^{n}

which implies
\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}\leq\frac{\|\mu\|^{n}}{n!}
.
Thus, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(|z|)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}(\mu)|z|^{n}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\|\mu\|^{n}\cdot|z|^{n}}{n!}=e^{\|\mu\|\cdot|z|}<\infty

so
\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)
has an infinite radius of convergence.

Second, we consider the non-strict case.
Let
n\geq 4
be an integer then

\{a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq a_{3}\leq\ldots\leq a_{n}\}_{T^{n}}\subseteq\{a_{1}\leq a_{2}\}_{T^{2}}\times\{a_{3}\leq\ldots\leq a_{n}\}_{T^{n-2}}

\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{n}=\mu^{n}(\{a_{1}\leq\ldots\leq a_{n}\}_{T^{n}})\leq\mu^{2}(\{a_{1}\leq a_{2}\}_{T^{2}})\cdot\mu^{n-2}(\{a_{3}\leq\ldots\leq a_{n}\}_{T^{n-2}})=\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{2}\cdot\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{n-2}.

Similarly for
n=3
, we get

\{a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq a_{3}\}_{T^{3}}\subseteq\{a_{1}\leq a_{2}\}_{T^{2}}\times T

\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{3}=\mu^{3}(\{a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq a_{3}\}_{T^{3}})\leq\mu^{2}(\{a_{1}\leq a_{2}\}_{T^{2}})\cdot\mu(T)=\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{2}\cdot\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{1}.

Applying this inductively, we get that for all

\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{2n}\leq\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{2}^{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{2n+1}\leq\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{2}^{n}\cdot\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{1}.

(15)

Let
x\in S
then by definition
\mu(\{x\})\leq C:=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(\{\alpha\})
and so

\mu^{2}(\{a_{1}=a_{2}\}_{T^{2}})=\int\mu(\{a_{1}=x\}_{T})\;\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\int\mu(\{x\})\;\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq\int C\;\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=C\|\mu\|.

Since

and

are disjoint possibilities, we know

and by symmetry

2\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{2}=\mu^{2}(\{a_{1}\leq a_{2}\}_{T^{2}})+\mu^{2}(\{a_{1}=a_{2}\})+\mu^{2}(\{a_{1}<a_{2}\}_{T^{2}})\leq\|\mu\|^{2}+C\|\mu\|.

Thus, we have the bound
\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{2}\leq\|\mu\|\cdot(\|\mu\|+C)/2
.
Let

Then, using

15

will have a radius of convergence greater than

in particular

Thus, as

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}=\infty\right)&=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}\leq X_{i+1}\leq\ldots\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}\leq\ldots\leq X_{i+k}\right)=\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{k+1}\to 0\quad\text{ and}\\

implying

so

is almost surely finite.

Now, to find the expected value, we can take the derivative of the PGF at one.
Using
eq.

7
and
eq.

8
, we know that the PGFs for
\accentset{\circ}{N}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{N}_{i}

can be expressed in terms of the run functions.
Because the radius of convergence of

is greater than one,

at

.
Thus,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{0}\right]=\left.\left(z\frac{d}{dz}\right)G_{N_{0}}(z)\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}&=\left.\left(z\frac{d}{dz}\right)\frac{1+(z-1)P_{\mu}(z)}{z}\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}\\
&=\left.z\frac{z(P_{\mu}(z)+(z-1)P^{\prime}_{\mu}(z))-(1+(z-1)P_{\mu}(z))}{z^{2}}\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}\\
&=\left.\frac{z(z-1)P^{\prime}_{\mu}(z)+P_{\mu}(z)-1}{z}\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}=\frac{1\cdot(1-1)P^{\prime}_{\mu}(1)+P_{\mu}(1)-1}{1}=P_{\mu}(1)-1\end{split}

proving
eq.

9
.
Similarly for the variance, we will use the PGF to find

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{0}^{2}\right]=\left.\left(z\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{2}G_{N_{0}}(z)\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}&=\left.\left(z\frac{d}{dz}\right)\frac{z(z-1)P^{\prime}_{\mu}(z)+P_{\mu}(z)-1}{z}\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}\\
&=\left.z\frac{z\left((2z-1)P^{\prime}_{\mu}(z)+z(z-1)P^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}(z)+P^{\prime}_{\mu}(z)\big.\right)-\left(z(z-1)P^{\prime}_{\mu}(z)+P_{\mu}(z)-1\big.\right)}{z^{2}}\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}\\
&=\left.\frac{z^{2}(z-1)P^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}(z)+(z+z^{2})P^{\prime}_{\mu}(z)-P_{\mu}(z)+1}{z}\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}\\
&=\frac{1^{2}\cdot(1-1)P^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}(1)+(1+1^{2})P^{\prime}_{\mu}(1)-P_{\mu}(1)+1}{1}=2P^{\prime}_{\mu}(1)-P_{\mu}(1)+1.\end{split}

Then, the variance will be

\mathrm{var}\left(N_{0}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[N_{0}^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[N_{0}\right]^{2}=(2P^{\prime}_{\mu}(1)-P_{\mu}(1)+1)-(P_{\mu}(1)-1)^{2}=P_{\mu}(1)-P_{\mu}(1)^{2}+2P^{\prime}_{\mu}(1)

proving
eq.

11
.

Now, we consider
i\geq 1
.
Again because the radius of convergence of

is one,
we know
P_{\mu}
is infinitely differentiable at zero and

.
Using

8

, we can take the first derivative to get

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{i}\,\middle|\,S_{i}\right]=\left.\left(z\frac{d}{dz}\right)G_{N_{i}}(z)\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}&=\left.\left(z\frac{d}{dz}\right)\left[1+\frac{z-1}{z(1-L_{2})}G_{N_{0}}(z)\right]\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}\\
&=\left.z\frac{z-1}{z(1-L_{2})}G^{\prime}_{N_{0}}(z)+z\frac{1}{z^{2}(1-L_{2})}G_{N_{0}}(z)\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}\\
&=\frac{1-1}{1-L_{2}}G^{\prime}_{N_{0}}(z)+\frac{1}{1-L_{2}}G_{N_{0}}(1)=\frac{1}{1-L_{2}}=\frac{2}{2-P_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0)}\end{split}

proving
eq.

.
From

9
, we get

so

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{i}^{2}\,\middle|\,S_{i}\right]=\left.\left(z\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{2}G_{N_{i}}(z)\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}&=\frac{1}{1-L_{2}}\left(\left.\left(z\frac{d}{dz}\right)\left[(z-1)G^{\prime}_{N_{0}}(z)+\frac{1}{z}G_{N_{0}}(z)\right]\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{1-L_{2}}\left(\left.z(z-1)G^{\prime\prime}_{N_{0}}(z)+zG^{\prime}_{N_{0}}(z)+\frac{z}{z}G^{\prime}_{N_{0}}(z)-\frac{z}{z^{2}}G_{N_{0}}(z)\Bigg.\right|_{z=1}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{1-L_{2}}\left(\Big.(1-1)G^{\prime\prime}_{N_{0}}(1)+G^{\prime}_{N_{0}}(1)+G^{\prime}_{N_{0}}(1)-G_{N_{0}}(1)\right)\\
&=\frac{2G^{\prime}_{N_{0}}(1)-1}{1-L_{2}}=\frac{2(P_{\mu}(1)-1)-1}{1-L_{2}}=\frac{2P_{\mu}(1)-3}{1-L_{2}}.\end{split}

Thus, the variance for

\begin{split}\mathrm{var}\left(N_{i}\,\middle|\,S_{i}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[N_{i}^{2}\,\middle|\,S_{i}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[N_{i}\,\middle|\,S_{i}\right]^{2}&=\frac{2P_{\mu}(1)-3}{1-L_{2}}-\frac{1}{(1-L_{2})^{2}}\\
&=\frac{1}{1-L_{2}}\left(-3+2P_{\mu}(1)-\frac{1}{1-L_{2}}\right)\\
&=\frac{2}{2-P_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0)}\left(-3+2P_{\mu}(1)-\frac{2}{2-P_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0)}\right)\end{split}

proving

Strong Law of Large Numbers

Proof of
7
.

To prove this, we will use a generalization of the law of large numbers for weakly correlated random variables from Lyons
[
10
]
.
We define
W_{b}
to be the number of runs in the sequence
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
starting in
[0,b)

and
U_{b,n}
to be the number of runs starting in
[0,b)
of length
n
.
First, notice that no run can have length zero so for any
b
,
U_{b,0}=0
always.
Thus,

\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{U_{b,0}}{W_{b}}=0=\mathbb{P}\left(N_{i}=0\,\middle|\,S_{i}\right).

Now, for each
n\geq 1
and
i\geq 0
, we define

R_{i}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }S_{i}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad Q_{i,n}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }S_{i}\text{ and }N_{i}=n\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Let
n\geq 0
and
b\geq 0
then

W_{b}=\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}R_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad U_{b,n}=\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}Q_{i,n}.

Now, suppose
j\geq i\geq 0
are indices with
|i-j|>1
.
If
i=0
then
R_{0}=1
is always true and
\mathbb{P}\left(R_{0}=1\text{ and }R_{j}=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(R_{j}=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(R_{0}=1\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(R_{j}=1\right)
.
Otherwise,
i\neq 0
with
j-1>i
so by independence of the sequence
\{X_{i}\}

\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\circ}{R}_{i}=1\text{ and }\accentset{\circ}{R}_{j}=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i-1}\nless X_{i}\text{ and }X_{j-1}\nless X_{j}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i-1}\nless X_{i}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j-1}\nless X_{j}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\circ}{R}_{i}=1\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\circ}{R}_{j}=1\right)

and similarly for
\accentset{\bullet}{R}_{i}
and
\accentset{\bullet}{R}_{j}
.
Thus,
R_{i}
and
R_{j}
are independent if
|i-j|>1
.
Similarly, suppose
j\geq i\geq 0
are indices with
|i-j|>n+1
.
Then,
j-1>i+n
so by independence,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\circ}{Q}_{0,n}=1\text{ and }\accentset{\circ}{Q}_{j,n}=1\right)&=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}<\ldots<X_{n-1}\nless X_{n}\text{ and }X_{j-1}\nless X_{j}<\ldots<X_{j+n-1}\nless X_{j+n}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}<\ldots<X_{n-1}\nless X_{n}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j-1}\nless X_{j}<\ldots<X_{j+n-1}\nless X_{j+n}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\circ}{Q}_{0,n}=1\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\circ}{Q}_{j,n}=1\right)\quad\text{ and}\\
\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\circ}{Q}_{i,n}=1\text{ and }\accentset{\circ}{Q}_{j,n}=1\right)&=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i-1}\nless X_{i}<\ldots<X_{i+n-1}\nless X_{i+n}\text{ and }X_{j-1}\nless X_{j}<\ldots<X_{j+n-1}\nless X_{j+n}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i-1}\nless X_{i}<\ldots<X_{i+n-1}\nless X_{i+n}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j-1}\nless X_{j}<\ldots<X_{j+n-1}\nless X_{j+n}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\circ}{Q}_{i,n}=1\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\accentset{\circ}{Q}_{j,n}=1\right)\quad\text{ when }i\neq 0\end{split}

and the same arguments apply for the non-strict case.
Therefore,
Q_{i,n}
and
Q_{j,n}
are independent if
|i-j|>n+1
.

We will be using corollary 4 from Lyons
[
10
]

which states that for a sequence of random variables
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
with
|Y_{i}|\leq 1
a.s.,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}=0\quad\text{ a.s.}

if there exists some non-negative function
\Phi(k)
on the integers such that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}Y_{j}\right]\leq\Phi(|i-j|)
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Phi(k)/k<\infty
.

For each
i\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
, we define
A_{i}:=R_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[R_{i}\right]
and
B_{i,n}:=Q_{i,n}-\mathbb{E}\left[Q_{i,n}\right]

so that
\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i,n}\right]=0
.
Since
R_{i},Q_{i,n}\in[0,1]
surely, we know
|A_{i}|,|B_{i,n}|\leq 1
.
Observe that for any indices
i
and
j
,

\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i}A_{j}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|A_{i}|\cdot|A_{j}|\right]\leq 1\quad\text{ when }|i-j|\leq 1\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i,n}B_{j,n}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|B_{i,n}|\cdot|B_{j,n}|\right]\leq 1\quad\text{ when }|i-j|\leq n+1

\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i}A_{j}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[A_{j}\right]=0\quad\text{ when }|i-j|>1\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i,n}B_{j,n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i,n}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[B_{j,n}\right]=0\quad\text{ when }|i-j|>n+1.

We then define

\Phi_{A}(k):=\begin{cases}1&k\leq 1\\
0&k>1\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Phi_{B,n}(k):=\begin{cases}1&k\leq n+1\\
0&k>n+1\end{cases}

which fulfill the requirements of Lyons’ corollary 4.
Therefore, the strong law of large numbers holds for
A_{i}
and
B_{i,n}
so almost surely

\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{W_{b}-\mathbb{E}\left[W_{b}\right]}{b}=\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}A_{i}=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{U_{b,n}-\mathbb{E}\left[U_{b,n}\right]}{b}=\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}B_{i,n}=0.

From
eq.

16
and
eq.

17
,
we know
\mathbb{P}\left(R_{i}=1\right)=1-L_{2}
and
\mathbb{P}\left(R_{i}=1\text{ and }N_{i}=n\right)=L_{n+2}-2L_{n+1}+L_{n}
for all
i\geq 1
so

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{W_{b}}{b}\right]=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}\mathbb{E}\left[R_{i}\right]&=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(R_{0}=1\right)}{b}+\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b-1}\mathbb{P}\left(R_{i}=1\right)=\frac{1}{b}+\frac{b-1}{b}(1-L_{2})\quad\text{ and}\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{U_{b,n}}{b}\right]=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=0}^{b-1}\mathbb{E}\left[Q_{i,n}\right]&=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(N_{0}=n\right)}{b}+\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b-1}\mathbb{P}\left(R_{i}=1\text{ and }N_{i}=n\right)\\
&=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(N_{0}=n\right)}{b}+\frac{b-1}{b}(L_{n+2}-2L_{n+1}+L_{n}).\end{split}

Using these expected values in the limits, we know almost surely that

\begin{split}\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{W_{b}}{b}&=\lim_{b\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{b}+\frac{b-1}{b}(1-L_{2})\right)=1-L_{2}\qquad\text{ and}\\
\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{U_{b,n}}{b}&=\lim_{b\to\infty}\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(N_{0}=n\right)}{b}+\frac{b-1}{b}(L_{n+2}-2L_{n+1}+L_{n})\right)=L_{n+2}-2L_{n+1}+L_{n}\end{split}

which implies that their quotient is almost surely

\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{U_{b,n}}{W_{b}}=\lim_{b\to\infty}\frac{U_{b,n}/b}{W_{b}/b}=\frac{L_{n+2}-2L_{n+1}+L_{n}}{1-L_{2}}=\mathbb{P}\left(N_{i}=n\,\middle|\,S_{i}\right)\quad\text{ for any }i\geq 1.

∎

Run Functions of Limits of Measures

In this section, we will prove that under certain circumstances
the convergence of a sequence of measures
implies the uniform convergence of their run functions
on bounded intervals.

Lemma 11
.

Suppose
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are finite positive measures on
T
.
For all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, if
P_{\mu_{1}}(|z|)
and
P_{\mu_{2}}(|z|)
exist then

\left|P_{\mu_{1}}(z)-P_{\mu_{2}}(z)\right|\leq\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|\cdot|z|\cdot P_{\mu_{1}}(|z|)\cdot P_{\mu_{2}}(|z|).

Proof.

First, we will bound the distance of
L_{n}(\mu_{1})
and
L_{n}(\mu_{2})
.
Let
n\geq 1
and observe that the following sum telescopes

\begin{split}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mu_{1}^{k}\times(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\times\mu_{2}^{n-k-1}&=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mu_{1}^{k+1}\times\mu_{2}^{n-k-1}\right)-\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mu_{1}^{k}\times\mu_{2}^{n-k}\right)\\
&=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{1}^{k}\times\mu_{2}^{n-k}\right)-\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mu_{1}^{k}\times\mu_{2}^{n-k}\right)=\mu_{1}^{n}-\mu_{2}^{n}\end{split}

Thus, we can bound
\left|\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}(\mu_{1})-\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}(\mu_{2})\right|
as

\begin{split}\left|\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}(\mu_{1})-\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}(\mu_{2})\right|&\leq\left|\mu_{1}^{n}-\mu_{2}^{n}\right|(\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{T^{n}})\\
&\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left|\mu_{1}^{k}\times(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\times\mu_{2}^{n-k-1}\right|(\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{T^{n}})\\
&\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left|\mu_{1}^{k}\times(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\times\mu_{2}^{n-k-1}\right|(\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k},a_{k+2}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{T^{n}})\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left|\mu_{1}^{k}\right|(\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}\}_{T^{k}})\cdot\left|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right|(T)\cdot\left|\mu_{2}^{n-k-1}\right|(\{a_{k+2}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{T^{n-k-1}})\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\accentset{\circ}{L}_{k}(\mu_{1})\cdot\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|\cdot\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n-k-1}(\mu_{1})\end{split}

(18)

and the same argument holds for
\left|\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{n}(\mu_{1})-\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{n}(\mu_{2})\right|
.

Now, let
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
P_{\mu_{1}}(|z|)
and
P_{\mu_{2}}(|z|)
converge.
Note that
L_{0}(\mu_{1})=1=L_{0}(\mu_{2})
so they will cancel in the difference.
Then, using
eq.

18

\begin{split}\left|P_{\mu_{1}}(z)-P_{\mu_{2}}(z)\right|&=\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(L_{n}(\mu_{1})-L_{n}(\mu_{2}))z^{n}\right|\\
&\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|L_{n}(\mu_{1})-L_{n}(\mu_{2})\right|\cdot|z|^{n}=|z|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|z|^{n-1}\cdot\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}L_{j}(\mu_{1})\cdot L_{n-j-1}(\mu_{2})\\
&=\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|\cdot|z|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}(L_{j}(\mu_{1})\cdot L_{n-j}(\mu_{2}))|z|^{n}.\end{split}

Using the substitution
j=n-k
, we can say

\begin{split}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}(L_{j}(\mu_{1})\cdot L_{n-j}(\mu_{2}))|z|^{n}&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(L_{j}(\mu_{1})\cdot L_{k}(\mu_{2}))|z|^{j+k}\\
&=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}L_{j}(\mu_{1})|z|^{j}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L_{k}(\mu_{2})|z|^{k}\right)=P_{\mu_{1}}(|z|)\cdot P_{\mu_{2}}(|z|)\end{split}

proving the desired bound.

Proposition 12
.

Suppose that
\mu,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots
are finite non-negative measures on
T

with
\mu_{i}\leq\mu_{j}
for
i\leq j
and
\mu_{k}\to\mu

R

then for all

with
|z|<R
,
P_{\mu_{k}}(|z|)
exists for all
k
.
Additionally, for any
0<b<R
,
P_{\mu_{k}}(z)\to P_{\mu}(z)
uniformly on
\{|z|\leq b\}
.

Proof.

First, we will show that
P_{\mu_{k}}(|z|)
exists when
|z|<R
.
For the sctrict case, by
10
, the radii of convergence of
\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)

and
\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu_{1}}(Z),\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu_{2}}(Z),\ldots
are all infinite

\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu_{k}}(|z|)
always exists.
For any
n
, by monotonicity, we know

\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{n}(\mu_{k})=\mu_{k}^{n}(\{a_{1}\leq\ldots\leq a_{n}\}_{T^{n}})\leq\mu^{n}(\{a_{1}\leq\ldots\leq a_{n}\}_{T^{n}})=\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{n}(\mu).

.

.

In this section, we will show how the run functions
behave under series and parallel composition.
We begin by proving the result for finite
\Gamma
.
It is sufficient to consider when
\Gamma=\{1,2\}

because
\otimes
and

are associative and we can extend by induction.

Lemma 13
.

If
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are finite measures over partial orders
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, respectively,
then
P_{\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2}}(Z)=P_{\mu_{1}}(Z)\cdot P_{\mu_{2}}(Z)
as formal power series.

Proof.

For all integers
n\geq 1
and
0\leq k\leq n
, we define
A_{n},B_{n}\subseteq(T_{1}\otimes T_{2})^{n}
as

\begin{split}A_{k,n}&:=\begin{cases}\hfil T_{2}&\text{ for }n=1\text{ and }k=0\\
\hfil T_{1}&\text{ for }n=1\text{ and }k=1\\
\{\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in T_{1}^{k}\times T_{2}^{n-k}\;:\;a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}\\
B_{n}&:=\begin{cases}\hfil T_{1}\otimes T_{2}&\text{ for }n=1\\
\{\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in(T_{1}\otimes T_{2})^{n}\;:\;a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.\end{split}

Here, we are taking
T_{1}^{0}\times T_{2}^{n}
and
T_{1}^{n}\times T_{2}^{0}
to mean
T_{2}^{n}
and
T_{1}^{n}
, respectively.
Next, we define
\tilde{B}_{n}\subseteq(T_{1}\otimes T_{2})^{n}
as

\tilde{B}_{n}:=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}A_{k,n}.

We will now prove that
B_{n}=\tilde{B}_{n}
.
When
n=1
, we have
B_{0}=T_{1}\otimes T_{2}=T_{1}\cup T_{2}=\tilde{B}_{0}
.
When
n\geq 2
, let
a=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in B_{n}
so that
a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}
.
We define

k:=\max\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\;:\;c_{i}\in T_{1}\}

or

if

.
For all
i\leq k
,

so
a_{i}\in T_{1}
.
Also for all
i>k
, by maximality,
a_{i}\notin T_{1}
so
a_{i}\in T_{2}
.
Thus,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in T_{1}
and
a_{k+1},\ldots,a_{n}\in T_{2}

implying that
a\in T_{1}^{k}\times T_{2}^{n-k}
.
Because
a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}
, this means
a\in A_{k,n}\subseteq\tilde{B}_{n}
and so
B_{n}\subseteq\tilde{B}_{n}
.
Conversely, let
a=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in\tilde{B}_{n}
with
a\in A_{k,n}
.
Then,
a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}
meaning
a\in B_{n}
and so
\tilde{B}_{n}\subseteq B_{n}
.
Hence,
B_{n}=\tilde{B}_{n}
.

Next, we show that the collection
\{A_{k,n}\}_{k=0}^{n}
is disjoint.
Let
j,k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
with
j<k
.
Assume for sake of contradiction that
a\in A_{j,n}\cap A_{k,n}
.
Since
a\in A_{k,n}
with
k>0
,
a_{k}\in T_{1}
.
However since
a\in A_{j,n}
, we know
a_{j+1},\ldots,a_{k},\ldots,a_{n}\in T_{2}
which is a contradiction.
We conclude that
A_{j,n}\cap A_{k,n}=\emptyset
.
Thus,
\accentset{\circ}{L}_{0}(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2})=1=\accentset{\circ}{L}_{0}(\mu_{1})\cdot\accentset{\circ}{L}_{0}(\mu_{2})
and for
n\geq 1
,

\begin{split}\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2})&=(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2})^{n}(B_{n})=(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2})^{n}\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}A_{k,n}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2})^{n}(A_{k,n})\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2})^{k}\left(\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}\}_{T_{1}^{k}}\right)\cdot(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2})^{n-k}\left(\{a_{k+1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{T_{2}^{n-k}}\right)\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mu_{1}^{k}\left(\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}\}_{T_{1}^{k}}\right)\cdot\mu_{2}^{n-k}\left(\{a_{k+1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{T_{2}^{n-k}}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\accentset{\circ}{L}_{k}(\mu_{1})\cdot\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n-k}(\mu_{2}).\end{split}

We can apply the same argument to
\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{n}(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2})
as well.
Therefore, using the substitution
n=j+k
, the run functions become

\begin{split}P_{\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2}}(Z)&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}L_{n}(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{2})Z^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}(L_{j}(\mu_{1})\cdot L_{n-j}(\mu_{2}))Z^{n}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(L_{j}(\mu_{1})\cdot L_{k}(\mu_{2}))Z^{j+k}=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}L_{j}(\mu_{1})Z^{j}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L_{k}(\mu_{2})Z^{k}\right)=P_{\mu_{1}}(Z)\cdot P_{\mu_{2}}(Z).\end{split}

∎

Lemma 14
.

If
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are finite measures over partial orders
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, respectively,
then
P_{\mu_{1}\oplus\mu_{2}}(Z)-1=(P_{\mu_{1}}(Z)-1)+(P_{\mu_{2}}(Z)-1)
as formal power series.

Proof.

For all integers
n\geq 1
, we define
A_{n},B_{n},C_{n}\subseteq T
as

A_{n}:=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{T_{1}^{n}}\quad\text{ and }\quad B_{n}:=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{T_{2}^{n}}\quad\text{ and }\quad C_{n}:=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{(T_{1}\oplus T_{2})^{n}}

Immediately, we have
A_{n},B_{n}\subseteq C_{n}
.
Let
a=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in C_{n}
then
either
a_{1}\in T_{1}
or
a_{1}\in T_{2}
.
If
a_{1}\in T_{1}
then since
a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
are comparable to
a_{1}
,
we know
a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}\in T_{1}
and so
a\in A_{n}
.
Otherwise, if
a_{1}\in T_{2}
then
a\in B_{n}
.
Hence, we have
C_{n}\subseteq A_{n}\cup B_{n}
and so
C_{n}=A_{n}\cup B_{n}
.

Notice that
A_{n}\cap B_{n}\subseteq T_{1}^{n}\cap T_{2}^{n}=\emptyset
thus

\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}(\mu_{1}\oplus\mu_{2})=(\mu_{1}\oplus\mu_{2})^{n}(C_{n})=(\mu_{1}\oplus\mu_{2})^{n}(A_{n})+(\mu_{1}\oplus\mu_{2})^{n}(B_{n})=\mu_{1}^{n}(A_{n})+\mu_{2}^{n}(B_{n})=\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}(\mu_{1})+\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}(\mu_{2}).

We may apply this same argument to the non-strict case
so
L_{n}(\mu_{1}\oplus\mu_{2})=L_{n}(\mu_{1})+L_{n}(\mu_{2})
for
n\geq 1
.
Therefore, the run functions will be

P_{\mu_{1}\oplus\mu_{2}}(Z)-1=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}L_{n}(\mu_{1}\oplus\mu_{2})Z^{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}L_{n}(\mu_{1})Z^{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}L_{n}(\mu_{2})Z^{n}=(P_{\mu_{1}}(Z)-1)+(P_{\mu_{2}}(Z)-1).

∎

Proposition 15
.

Suppose
M=\{\mu_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
is a collection of finite non-negative measures on
the collection of partial orders
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
.
We assume that the sum of the norms of
\{\mu_{\gamma}\}
is finite.
Let
R_{\otimes}
and
R_{\oplus}
be the radii of convergence of
P_{\bigotimes M}
and
P_{\bigoplus M}
, respectively.
If
\Gamma
is countable then for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

P_{\bigotimes M}(z)=\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma}P_{\mu_{\gamma}}(z)\;\text{ when }|z|<R_{\otimes}\quad\text{ and }\quad P_{\bigoplus M}(z)-1=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}(P_{\mu_{\gamma}}(z)-1)\;\text{ when }|z|<R_{\oplus}.

Proof.

Since
\Gamma
is countable, let
\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots\}
be an enumeration for
\Gamma
.
We will say
\mu^{\otimes}=\bigotimes M
and
\mu^{\oplus}=\bigoplus M
.
For every integer
k\geq 1
, we define a new collection of measures
\tilde{M}_{k}:=\{\tilde{\mu}_{k,\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
where

\tilde{\mu}_{k,\gamma}=\begin{cases}\mu_{\gamma}&\text{ when }\gamma\in\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k}\}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

Then, we can define
\mu^{\otimes}_{k}:=\bigotimes\tilde{M}_{k}
and
\mu^{\oplus}_{k}:=\bigoplus\tilde{M}_{k}

as finite non-negative measures on
\bigotimes\mathcal{T}
and
\bigoplus\mathcal{T}
, respectively.
Using
13
and
14
, we know

P_{\mu^{\otimes}_{k}}(Z)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}P_{\mu_{\gamma_{j}}}(Z)\quad\text{ and }\quad P_{\mu^{\oplus}_{k}}(Z)-1=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(P_{\mu_{\gamma_{j}}}(Z)-1).

Now, notice that for
j\leq k
,
\mu^{\otimes}_{j}\leq\mu^{\otimes}_{k}
and
\mu^{\oplus}_{j}\leq\mu^{\oplus}_{k}
as well as
when
k\to\infty
,

\left\|\mu^{\otimes}_{k}-\mu_{k}\right\|,\left\|\mu^{\oplus}_{k}-\mu_{k}\right\|\leq\left\|\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\mu_{\gamma_{j}}\right\|\to 0.

since
\sum_{\gamma}\|\mu_{\gamma}\|<\infty
.
Thus by
12
, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

\begin{split}P_{\mu^{\otimes}}(z)&=\lim_{k\to\infty}P_{\mu^{\otimes}_{k}}(z)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\prod_{j=1}^{k}P_{\mu_{\gamma_{j}}}(z)=\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma}P_{\mu_{\gamma}}(z)\quad\text{ when }|z|<R^{\otimes}\text{ and}\\
P_{\mu^{\oplus}}(z)-1&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left[P_{\mu^{\oplus}_{k}}(z)-1\right]=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(P_{\mu_{\gamma_{j}}}(z)-1\right)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\left(P_{\mu_{\gamma}}(z)-1\right)\quad\text{ when }|z|<R^{\oplus}.\end{split}

∎

9
Run Lengths in Total Orders and Countably Series-Parallel Partial Orders

First, we will consider the case of a total order with no atoms
and then generalize to the atomic case.

Lemma 16
.

Suppose
T
is a total order and
\mu
is a finite non-negative measure on
T
.
If
\mu
has no atoms then
\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)=\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z)=e^{\|\mu\|Z}
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma_{n}
be the set of permutations on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Then, for every
\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}
, we define the subset

A_{\sigma}:=\{a_{\sigma(1)}<\ldots<a_{\sigma(n)}\}_{T^{n}}

so that
\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}=\mu^{n}(A_{\sigma})
.
Notice that if
a_{\sigma(1)}<\ldots<a_{\sigma(n)}
then no other permutation of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}

will be strictly ascending so
A_{\sigma}
is disjoint from all other
A_{\sigma^{\prime}}
.
Next, we define

D:=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\text{ are pairwise distinct}\}_{T^{n}}.

Because
T
is a total order, for any tuple
a=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in D
, there is a permutation
\sigma

such that
a_{\sigma(1)}<\ldots<a_{\sigma(n)}
and therefore
a\in A_{\sigma}
.
Conversely, for any
a\in A_{\sigma}
,
a_{\sigma(1)}<\ldots<a_{\sigma(n)}
so
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are distinct and
a\in D
.
Thus,
D=\bigcup_{\sigma}A_{\sigma}
and by disjointness

\mu^{n}(D)=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}\mu^{n}(A_{\sigma})=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}=|\Sigma_{n}|\cdot\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}=n!\cdot\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}.

Now, for every
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we define
E_{i,j}:=\{a_{i}=a_{j}\}_{T^{n}}
.
By symmetry, we know
\mu^{n}(E_{i,j})=\mu^{n}(E_{1,2})
and by diffuseness (lack of atoms)

\mu^{n}(E_{1,2})=\mu^{n}(\{a_{1}=a_{2}\}_{T^{2}}\times T^{n-2})=\mu^{2}(\{a_{1}=a_{2}\}_{T^{2}})\cdot\mu^{n-2}(T^{n-2})=\|\mu\|^{n-2}\cdot\int\mu(\{t\})\;\mathrm{d}\mu(t)=0.

Next, for every
a=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in D^{c}
,
there exist
i,j\in n
with
a_{i}=a_{j}
so
a\in E_{i,j}
.
Thus,

D^{c}\subseteq\bigcup_{i,j\in[1,n]}E_{i,j}\qquad\text{ implying }\qquad\mu^{n}(D^{c})\leq\mu^{n}\left(\bigcup_{i,j}E_{i,j}\right)=0

and so
\|\mu\|^{n}=\mu^{n}(T^{n})=\mu^{n}(D)+\mu^{n}(D^{c})=n!\cdot\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}+0

showing that
\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}=\frac{\|\mu\|^{n}}{n!}
.

Now, we address the case of
\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{n}
.
We define
A=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}
and
B=\{a_{1}\leq\ldots\leq a_{n}\}
.
From above, we know
\mu^{n}(A)=\frac{\|\mu\|^{n}}{n!}
.
If
a=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in B\setminus A
then
there must exist
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
a_{i}=a_{i+1}
so
a\in E_{i,i+1}
.
Thus,
B\setminus A\subseteq\bigcup_{i,j}E_{i,j}
and
\mu^{n}(B\setminus A)=0
.
Therefore, we have

\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{n}=\mu^{n}(B)=\mu^{n}(A)+\mu^{n}(B\setminus A)=\frac{\|\mu\|^{n}}{n!}+0.

Finally, the run functions will be

\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z)=\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\|\mu\|^{n}}{n!}Z^{n}=e^{\|\mu\|Z}.

∎

As before, we assume
\mathcal{A}
is the set of atoms of
\mu

and that for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, the mass of
\alpha
is
m_{\alpha}=\mu(\{\alpha\})
.
Note that because
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}\leq\|\mu\|<\infty
, we must have at most countably many atoms.

Proof of
5
.

First, we will prove the result when
\mathcal{A}
is finite.
We enumerate the atoms as
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\}=\mathcal{A}
.
Because
T
is total, we can define
R_{0},\ldots,R_{k}\subseteq T
to separate
T
into

R_{0}\cup\{\alpha_{1}\}\cup R_{1}\cup\{\alpha_{2}\}\cup\ldots\cup\{\alpha_{k}\}\cup R_{k}:=(-\infty,\alpha_{1})\cup\{\alpha_{1}\}\cup(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\cup\{\alpha_{2}\}\cup\ldots\cup\{\alpha_{k}\}\cup(\alpha_{k},\infty)=T.

Treating each section as its own total order, we have
T=R_{0}\otimes\{\alpha_{1}\}\otimes\ldots\otimes\{\alpha_{k}\}\otimes R_{k}
.
Next, we take
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
to be the restrictions of
\mu
to
R_{0},\ldots,R_{k}

and
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k}
to be the restrictions of
\mu
to
\{\alpha_{1}\},\ldots,\{\alpha_{k}\}
.
Then,
\mu=\lambda_{0}\otimes\delta_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\delta_{k}\otimes\lambda_{k}
so by
13
,

P_{\mu}(Z)=P_{\lambda_{0}}(Z)\cdot P_{\delta_{1}}(Z)\cdots P_{\delta_{k}}(Z)\cdot P_{\lambda_{k}}(Z).

Because
R_{0}\cup\ldots\cup R_{k}\subseteq T\setminus\mathcal{A}
,
we know
R_{0}\cup\ldots\cup R_{k}
will contain no atoms of
\mu
.
Thus, every one of
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
must be diffuse and so
16

tells us that
P_{\lambda_{i}}(Z)=e^{\|\lambda_{i}\|Z}=e^{\mu(R_{i})Z}
for all
0\leq i\leq k
.

Next, let
1\leq i\leq k
and we will consider
\delta_{i}
.
For the strict case,
we know
\accentset{\circ}{L}_{0}(\delta_{i})=1
and
\accentset{\circ}{L}_{1}(\delta_{i})=\|\delta_{i}\|=\mu(\{\alpha_{i}\})=m_{\alpha_{i}}
.
Then, for all
n\geq 2
, because
\{\alpha_{i}\}
does not have
n
distinct elements,

\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}(\delta_{i})=\delta_{i}^{n}(\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{n}\}_{\{\alpha_{i}\}^{n}})=0.

Thus,
\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\delta_{i}}(Z)=1+m_{\alpha_{i}}Z
.
Next, for the non-strict case,
we have
\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{0}(\delta_{i})=1
and for all
n\geq 1
, all elements of
\{\alpha_{i}\}
are equal so

\accentset{\bullet}{L}_{n}(\delta_{i})=\delta_{i}^{n}(\{a_{1}\leq\ldots\leq a_{n}\}_{\{\alpha_{i}\}^{n}})=\delta_{i}^{n}(\{\alpha_{i}\}^{n})=\|\delta_{i}\|^{n}=m_{\alpha_{i}}^{n}.

Thus,
\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\delta_{i}}(Z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}m_{\alpha_{i}}^{n}Z^{n}=\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha_{i}}Z}
.
Finally, using these results in the above product, we get

\begin{split}\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z)&=e^{\mu(S_{0})Z}\cdot\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha_{1}}Z}\cdots\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha_{k}}Z}\cdot e^{\mu(S_{k})Z}=e^{\mu(S_{0}\cup\ldots\cup S_{k})Z}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha_{i}}Z}=e^{m_{d}Z}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha}Z}\quad\text{ and}\\
\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)&=e^{\mu(S_{0})Z}\cdot(1+m_{\alpha_{1}}Z)\cdots(1+m_{\alpha_{k}}Z)\cdot e^{\mu(S_{k})Z}=e^{\mu(S_{0}\cup\ldots\cup S_{k})Z}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+m_{\alpha_{i}}Z)=e^{m_{d}Z}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}(1+m_{\alpha}Z).\end{split}

which proves the result in the finite case.

Second, we will extend our result to countable atoms using a limit.
We enumerate the atoms as
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots\}=\mathcal{A}
.
For each
k\geq 1
, we define the measurable set
F_{k}=T\setminus\{\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k+1},\ldots\}
.
Next, we define
\mu_{k}=\mu\big|_{F_{k}}
so that
\mu_{k}
is the restriction of
\mu
to
F_{k}
.
Then,
\mu_{k}
will have exactly the atoms
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1}\}
.
So the diffuse mass of
\mu_{k}
will be

\|\mu_{k}\|-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}m_{\alpha_{i}}=\|\mu\|-\sum_{i=k}^{\infty}m_{\alpha_{i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}m_{\alpha_{i}}=\|\mu\|-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}m_{\alpha_{i}}=m_{d}

where
m_{d}
is the diffuse mass of
\mu
.
Using the results for the finite case, we have

\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu_{k}}(Z)=e^{m_{d}Z}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(1+m_{\alpha_{i}}Z)\quad\text{ and }\quad\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu_{k}}(Z)=e^{m_{d}Z}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha_{i}}Z}.

Next, we observe that as
k\to\infty

\|\mu-\mu_{k}\|=\left\|\mu\big|_{F_{k}^{c}}+\mu\big|_{F_{k}}-\mu\big|_{F_{k}}\right\|=\left\|\mu\big|_{F_{k}^{c}}\right\|=\mu(F_{k}^{c})=\sum_{i=k}^{\infty}m_{\alpha_{i}}\to 0

so
\mu_{k}\to\mu
.
Because
\{F_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is an increasing sequence of sets,
we know
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is an increasing sequence of measures.
From
10
, we know that the radius of convergence of
\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(Z)
is infinite
and the radius of convergence of
\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(Z)
is greater than
\frac{1}{\|\mu\|}
.
Thus by
12
, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

\begin{split}\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(z)&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu_{k}}(z)=\lim_{k\to\infty}e^{m_{d}z}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(1+m_{\alpha_{i}}z)=e^{m_{d}z}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}(1+m_{\alpha}z)\quad\text{ and}\\
\text{if }|z|\leq\frac{1}{\|\mu\|}\text{ then }\quad\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}(z)&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu_{k}}(z)=\lim_{k\to\infty}e^{m_{d}z}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha_{i}}z}=e^{m_{d}z}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha}z}.\end{split}

∎

Lemma 17
.

For any finite non-negative measure
\mu
on a total order,

\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\|\mu\|^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\accentset{\bullet}{P}_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\|\mu\|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}.

(19)

Proof.

We define
\theta:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}
.
From
5
, we know

\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\accentset{\circ}{L}_{n}z^{n}=e^{m_{d}z}\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}(1+m_{\alpha}z)

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
and the product converges uniformly on bounded sets.
So we define
\accentset{\circ}{Q}(z):=\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}(1+m_{\alpha}z)

and take the derivative

\accentset{\circ}{Q}^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathcal{A}\\
\beta\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}(1+m_{\beta}z)\quad\text{ so the constant term is }\quad\accentset{\circ}{Q}^{\prime}(0)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}=\|\mu\|-m_{d}.

The second derivative and its constant term will be

\begin{split}\accentset{\circ}{Q}^{\prime\prime}(z)&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}\\
\alpha\neq\beta\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathcal{A}\\
\gamma\neq\alpha,\beta\end{subarray}}(1+m_{\gamma}z)\quad\text{ and}\\
\accentset{\circ}{Q}^{\prime\prime}(0)&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}\\
\alpha\neq\beta\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}\\
\alpha=\beta\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\\
&=\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}\right)^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}=(\|\mu\|-m_{d})^{2}-\theta.\end{split}

Thus, the second derivative of
\accentset{\circ}{P}_{\mu}
at zero is

\begin{split}\accentset{\circ}{P}^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}(0)&=m_{d}^{2}e^{m_{d}\cdot 0}\;\accentset{\circ}{Q}(0)+2m_{d}e^{m_{d}\cdot 0}\;\accentset{\circ}{Q}^{\prime}(0)+e^{m_{d}\cdot 0}\;\accentset{\circ}{Q}^{\prime\prime}(0)\\
&=m_{d}^{2}+2m_{d}(\|\mu\|-m_{d})+(\|\mu\|-m_{d})^{2}-\theta\\
&=m_{d}^{2}+2\|\mu\|m_{d}-2m_{d}^{2}+\|\mu\|^{2}-2\|\mu\|m_{d}+m_{d}^{2}-\theta=\|\mu\|^{2}-\theta.\end{split}

proving the first part of
eq.

19
.

Now, we will address the non-strict case.
We define
\accentset{\bullet}{Q}(z):=\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\alpha}z}

for
z\in\mathbb{C}
sufficiently close to zero and the first derivative near zero is

\accentset{\bullet}{Q}^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{m_{\alpha}}{(1-m_{\alpha}z)^{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathcal{A}\\
\beta\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\beta}z}\quad\text{ so the constant term is }\quad\accentset{\bullet}{Q}^{\prime}(0)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{m_{\alpha}}{(1-0)^{2}}=\|\mu\|-m_{d}.

For the second derivative, we may differentiate the
\alpha
term again or differentiate a different term

\accentset{\bullet}{Q}^{\prime\prime}(z)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\frac{2m_{\alpha}^{2}}{(1-m_{\alpha}z)^{3}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathcal{A}\\
\beta\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\beta}z}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathcal{A}\\
\beta\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}\frac{m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}}{(1-m_{\alpha}z)^{2}(1-m_{\beta}z)^{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathcal{A}\\
\gamma\neq\alpha,\beta\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{1-m_{\gamma}z}\right]

and its constant term will be

\begin{split}\accentset{\bullet}{Q}^{\prime\prime}(0)&=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\frac{2m_{\alpha}^{2}}{(1-0)^{3}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathcal{A}\\
\beta\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}\frac{m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}}{(1-0)^{2}(1-0)^{2}}\right]=2\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}\right)+\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}\\
\alpha\neq\beta\end{subarray}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}^{2}+\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}}m_{\alpha}m_{\beta}=\theta+(\|\mu\|-m_{d})^{2}.\end{split}
