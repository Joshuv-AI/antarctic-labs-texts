On Nominal Syntax and Permutation Fixed Points Thanks: Work supported by FAPDF grant 193001369/2016. M. Ayala-Rincón partially funded by CNPq research grant number 307672/2017-4.
Mauricio Ayala-Rincón, Maribel Fernández, Daniele Nantes-Sobrinho

Abstract
. We propose a new axiomatisation of the alpha-equivalence relation for nominal terms, based on a primitive notion of fixed-point constraint. We show that the standard freshness relation between atoms and terms can be derived from the more primitive notion of permutation fixed-point, and use this result to prove the correctness of the new α \alpha -equivalence axiomatisation. This gives rise to a new notion of nominal unification, where solutions for unification problems are pairs of a fixed-point context and a substitution. Although it may seem less natural than the standard notion of nominal unifier based on freshness constraints, the notion of unifier based on fixed-point constraints behaves better when equational theories are considered: for example, nominal unification remains finitary in the presence of commutativity, whereas this is not the case when unifiers are expressed using freshness contexts. We provide a definition of α \alpha -equivalence modulo equational theories that takes into account 𝖠 \mathsf{A} , 𝖢 \mathsf{C} and 𝖠𝖢 \mathsf{AC} theories. Based on this notion of equivalence, we show that 𝖢 \mathsf{C} -unification is finitary and we provide a sound and complete 𝖢 \mathsf{C} -unification algorithm, as a first step towards the development of nominal unification modulo 𝖠𝖢 \mathsf{AC} and other equational theories with permutative properties.

1.
Introduction

This paper presents a new approach for the definition of nominal languages, based on the use of permutation fixed points. More precisely, we give a new axiomatisation of the
\alpha
-equivalence relation for nominal terms using permutation fixed-points, and revisit nominal unification in this setting.

In nominal syntax
[
UPG04
]
,

atoms
are used to represent object-level variables
and
atom permutations
are used to implement renamings, following the nominal-sets approach advocated by Gabbay and Pitts
[
Gab00
,
GP02
,
Pit13
]
. Atoms can be abstracted over terms; the syntax
[a]s
represents the abstraction of
a
in
s
. To rename an abstracted atom
a
to
b
, a
swapping

permutation
\pi=(a\,b)
is applied.
Thus, the action of
\pi
over
[a]s
, written as
(a\,b)\cdot[a]s
, produces the nominal term
[b]s^{\prime}
, where
s^{\prime}
is the result of
swapping
a
and
b
in
s
. The
\alpha
-equivalence relation between nominal terms is specified using swappings together with a
freshness relation
between atoms and terms, written
b\#s
,
which roughly corresponds to
b
not occurring free in
s
.

In this setting, checking
\alpha
-equivalence requires another
first-order specialised calculus to check freshness constraints.
For instance, checking whether
[a]s\approx_{\alpha}[b]t
reduces to checking whether
s\approx_{\alpha}(b\,a)\cdot t

and
a\#t
.

The action of a permutation propagates
down the structure of nominal terms, until a variable is reached: permutations suspend over variables. Thus,
\pi\cdot s
represents the action of a permutation
over a nominal term, but is not itself a nominal term unless
s
is a
variable; for instance,
\pi\cdot X
is a
suspension
(also called
moderated variable
), which is a nominal term.

The presence of moderated variables and atom-abstractions makes reasoning about equality
of nominal terms more involved than in standard first-order syntax.
For example,
\pi\cdot X\approx_{\alpha}^{?}\rho\cdot X
is only true
when
X
ranges over nominal terms, say
s
, for
which all atoms in the difference set of
\pi
and
\rho
(i.e., the
set
\{a:\pi(a)\neq\rho(a)\}
) are fresh in
s
.

If the domain of a permutation
\pi
is fresh for
X
then
\pi\cdot X\mathrel{{\approx}_{\scriptstyle{\alpha}}}Id\cdot X
.
Thus a set of freshness constraints (i.e., a freshness context) can be used to specify
that a permutation will have no effect on the instances of
X
.
This is why in
nominal unification

[
UPG04
]
, the solution for a problem is a pair consisting of a freshness context and a substitution.

The use of freshness contexts is natural when dealing with “syntactic” nominal unification, but in the presence of equational axioms (i.e., equational nominal unification) it is not straightforward.
For example, in the case of
\mathsf{C}
-nominal unification (nominal unification modulo commutativity), to specify that a permutation has no effect on the instances of
X
modulo
\mathsf{C}
, in other words, to specify that the permutation does not affect a given
\mathsf{C}
-equivalence class, we need something more than a freshness constraint (note that

(a\ b)\cdot(a+b)=b+a=_{\mathsf{C}}a+b
, so the permutation
(a\ b)
fixes the term
a+b
, despite the fact that
a
and
b
are not fresh).

In this paper, we propose to axiomatise
\alpha
-equivalence of nominal terms using permutation fixed-point constraints: we write
\pi\curlywedge t
(read “
\pi
fixes
t
”) if
t
is a fixed-point of
\pi
. We show how to derive fixed-point constraints
\pi\curlywedge t
from primitive constraints of the form
\pi\curlywedge X
, and show the correctness of this approach by proving that the
\alpha
-equivalence relation generated in this way coincides with the one axiomatised via freshness constraints.
We then show how fixed-point constraints can be used to specify
\alpha
-equivalence modulo equational theories containing
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{C}
, and
\mathsf{AC}
operators, and provide an algorithm to solve nominal unification problems modulo
\mathsf{C}
, which outputs a finite set of most general solutions.

Related Work

Equational reasoning has been extensively explored since the early development of modern abstract algebra (see, e.g., the
E
-unification surveys by Siekmann
[
Sie90
]
and Baader et al
[
BS94
]
and
[
BSN+01
]
). For
\mathsf{AC}
-equality checking,
\mathsf{AC}
matching and
\mathsf{AC}
unification, refined techniques have been applied. For instance,
\mathsf{AC}
-equality check and linear
\mathsf{AC}
-matching problems can be reduced
to searching a perfect matching in a bipartite
graph
[
BKN87
]
, whereas
\mathsf{AC}
unification
problems can be reduced to solving a system of Diophantine equations
[
Sti81
,
Fag87
]
.

Techniques to deal with
\alpha
-equivalence modulo the equational theories
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{AC}
were proposed in
[
AdCSFN17
,
AdCSMFR19
,
ARdCSFNS17
,
ARdCSFNS18
]
, using the standard nominal approach via freshness constraints.
Solving nominal
\mathsf{C}
-unification problems requires to deal with fixed-point equations, for which there is no finitary representation of the set of solutions using only freshness constraints and substitutions
[
ARdCSFNS17
,
ARdCSFNS18
]
. A combinatorial algorithm permits to find all the solutions of fixed-point equations
[
ARdCSFNS18
]
.

Fixed-point constraints arise also in other contexts: Schmidt-Schauß et al
[
SKLV17
]
show how nominal unification problems in a language with recursive let operators give rise to freshness constraints and nominal fixed-point equations. The approach to nominal unification via permutation fixed-points proposed in this paper could also be used to reason about equality in this language.

This paper is a revised and extended version of
[
AFN18
]
, where nominal terms with fixed-point permutation constraints were first presented. In this paper we prove the correctness of the approach, by showing it is equivalent to the standard presentation via freshness constraints. We also provide proofs of soundness and completeness of the unification algorithm. A generalisation of the notion of
\alpha
-equivalence to take into account equational theories including
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{AC}
is also provided (only
\mathsf{C}
was considered in
[
AFN18
]
), as well as a
\mathsf{C}
-unification algorithm.

Overview

The paper is organised as follows. Section
2
provides the necessary background material on nominal syntax and semantics. Section
3
introduces fixed-point constraints and
\alpha
-equivalence, and shows that these relations behave as expected. In particular, we show that there is a two-way translation between the freshness-based
\alpha
-equivalence relation and its permutation fixed-point counter-part, which confirms that the fixed-point approach is equivalent to the standard approach via freshness constraints.
Section
4
presents a nominal unification algorithm specified by a set of simplification rules, and proves its soundness and completeness. In Section
5
we generalise the approach to take into account equational theories: we define a notion of permutation fixed-point
\alpha
-equivalence modulo
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{AC}
theories, and develop a
\mathsf{C}
-unification algorithm. Finally, Section
6
concludes and discusses future work.

Let

A permutation on

\mathbb{A}
with finite domain.

Fix a countably infinite set
\mathcal{X}=\{X,Y,Z,\ldots\}
of variables and a countable set
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathsf{f},\mathsf{g},\ldots\}
of function symbols.

{defi}

[Nominal grammar] Nominal terms are generated by the following grammar.

s,t:=a\mid[a]t\mid(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\mid\mathsf{f}^{\mathsf{E}}\ t\mid\pi\cdot X

where
a
is an
atom term
,
[a]t
denotes the
abstraction

of the atom
a
over the term
t
,
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
is a tuple, function symbols are equipped with an equational theory
\mathsf{E}
, hence
\mathsf{f}^{\mathsf{E}}\ t
denotes the
application of
\mathsf{f}^{\mathsf{E}}
to
t

and
\pi\cdot X
is a
moderated variable
or
suspension
, where
\pi
is an atom permutation. We write
\mathsf{f}^{\emptyset}
, or simply
\mathsf{f}
, to emphasise that no equational theory is assumed for
\mathsf{f}
, that is,
\mathsf{f}
is just a function symbol.

We follow the
permutative convention

[
GM08
, Convention 2.3]
for atoms
throughout the paper, i.e., atoms
a,b,c
range permutatively over
\mathbb{A}
so that they are always pairwise different, unless stated otherwise.

Atom
permutations
are represented by finite lists of
swappings
, which are
pairs of different atoms
(a\ b)
; hence, a permutation
\pi
is generated by the following grammar:

\pi:=Id\ |\ (a\ b)\pi.

where
Id
is the identity permutation, usually omitted from the list of swappings representing a permutation
\pi
.
Suspensions of the form
Id\cdot X
will be represented just by
X
.
We write
\pi^{-1}
for the
inverse
of
\pi
,
and use
\circ
to denote the composition of permutations. For example, if

\pi=(a\ b)(b\ c)
then
\pi(c)=a
and
c=\pi^{-1}(a)
.

The
difference set
of two permutations
\pi
,
\pi^{\prime}
is

\texttt{ds}(\pi,\pi^{\prime})=\{a\mid\pi(a)\neq\pi^{\prime}(a)\}
.

We write
\texttt{Var}(t)
for the set of variables occurring in
t
. Ground terms are terms
without variables, that is,
\texttt{Var}(t)=\emptyset
. A ground term may still contain atoms,
for example
a
is a ground term and
X
is not.

{defi}

[Permutation action] The action of a permutation
\pi
on a term
t
is
defined by induction on the number of swappings in
\pi
:

Id\cdot t=t
and
((a\ b)\pi)\cdot t=(a\ b)\cdot(\pi\cdot t),

where

\begin{array}[]{@{\hspace{-1mm}}c@{\hspace{-.2mm}}c@{\hspace{-.2mm}}c}\hskip-2.84526pt\lx@intercol\hfil\begin{array}[]{c}(a\ b)\cdot a=b\\
(a\ b)\cdot b=a\\
(a\ b)\cdot c=c\end{array}\hfil\hskip-0.56905pt&\begin{array}[]{c}(a\ b)\cdot(\pi\cdot X)=((a\ b)\circ\pi)\cdot X\\
(a\ b)\cdot\mathsf{f}\ t=\mathsf{f}\ (a\ b)\cdot t\\
(a\ b)\cdot(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=((a\ b)\cdot t_{1},\ldots,(a\ b)\cdot t_{n})\end{array}\hfil\hskip-0.56905pt&\begin{array}[]{c}(a\ b)\cdot[a]t=[b](a\ b)\cdot t\\
(a\ b)\cdot[b]t=[a](a\ b)\cdot t\\
(a\ b)\cdot[c]t=[c](a\ b)\cdot t\\
\end{array}\end{array}

{defi}

[Substitution]

Substitutions
are generated by the grammar

\sigma::=id\ |\ [X\mapsto s]\sigma.

Postfix notation is used for substitution application and

for composition:
t(\sigma\circ\sigma^{\prime})=(t\sigma)\sigma^{\prime}

t\ id=t,\ t[X\mapsto s]\sigma

=

\begin{array}[]{cc}\begin{array}[]{c}a[X\mapsto s]=a\\
(\mathsf{f}\ t)[X\mapsto s]=\mathsf{f}(t[X\mapsto s])\\
([a]t)[X\mapsto s]=[a](t[X\mapsto s])\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{c}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})[X\mapsto s]=(t_{1}[X\mapsto s],\ldots,t_{n}[X\mapsto s])\\

(\pi\cdot Y)[X\mapsto s]=\pi\cdot Y\end{array}\end{array}

The following well-known property of substitution and permutation justifies the notation

(without brackets), see

Substitution and permutation commute:

A set

if for all
\pi\in\texttt{Perm}(\mathbb{A})

((\forall a\in A)\ \pi(a)=a)\Rightarrow\pi\cdot x=x

(1)

A

is a set equipped with an action of the group

, that is, a

As in

2.3.
The “new” quantifier

In Nominal Logic
[
Pit03
]
, the “new” quantifier

\mathsf{N}

is used to quantify over new names.
Given two elements
x
,
y
of a nominal set, write
x\#y
as an abbreviation of
\texttt{supp}(x)\cap\texttt{supp}(y)=\emptyset
; then
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}a.P(a,x)
(read “for some/any new atom
a
,
P(a,x)
”) abbreviates
\forall a\in\mathbb{A}.a\#x\Rightarrow P(a,x)
or equivalently
\exists a\in\mathbb{A}.a\#x\wedge P(a,x)
(see Theorem 3.9 in
[
Pit13
]
for more details).

Intuitively, the “new” quantifier is used to indicate that a predicate holds for some (any) new atoms. The formula
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}a.\phi
means “for a fresh name
a
,
\phi
holds”. Since there is an infinite supply of names and elements of nominal sets have a finite support, it is always possible to pick a fresh name. Moreover, it can be shown that if a property
\phi(a)
holds for some fresh name
a
then it holds for all fresh names, so the way in which the fresh atom is chosen is not important.

In proof systems for Nominal Logic, several approaches have been proposed to define rules to introduce and eliminate the

\mathsf{N}

quantifier (see, e.g.,
[
Pit03
,
Che05
,
CU08
]
). We follow Cheney’s approach
[
Che05
]
, where the formula
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}a.\phi
(read “
a
is a
newly quantified atom
in
\phi
”) is well formed if
\phi
is a well-formed formula and
a\in\mathbb{A}
is semantically fresh. In other words, the introduction of a

\mathsf{N}

quantifier for
a
in a formula
\phi
requires
a
to be a new atom, fresh for all the variables in
\phi
(see
[
Che05
]
, Figure 5, where the well-formedness rules are given). We implicitly assume that
a
is a newly generated atom when we introduce

\mathsf{N}

in our formulas in the next section.
Operationally, proving a formula
\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}a.\phi
boils down to picking a fresh atom
c
and proving that
\phi
holds when the occurrences of
a
are replaced by the fresh atom
c
.

3.
Fixed-point Constraints

The native notion of equality on nominal terms is
\alpha
-equivalence, written
s\mathrel{{\approx}_{\scriptstyle{\alpha}}}t
. As mentioned in section
2.1
, this relation is usually axiomatised using the
freshness relation
between atoms and terms. The notion of freshness is derived from the notion of support, which in turn is defined using permutation fixed-points (see Definition
2.2
). We can define freshness using the quantifier

\mathsf{N}

combined with a notion of fixed-point, as shown by Pitts
[
Pit13
]
(page 53):

a\#X\Leftrightarrow\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}a^{\prime}.(a\ a^{\prime})\cdot X=X.

In this paper, instead of defining
\alpha
-equivalence using freshness, we define it using the more primitive notion of
fixed-point
under the action of permutations. We will denote this relation
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}
, and show that it coincides with
\mathrel{{\approx}_{\scriptstyle{\alpha}}}
on ground terms, i.e., the relation defined using permutation fixed points corresponds to the relation defined using freshness. For non-ground terms, there is also a correspondence, but under different kinds of assumptions (fixed-point constraints vs. freshness constraints).

3.1.
Fixed-points of permutations and term equality

We start by defining a binary relation that describes which elements of a nominal set
S
are fixed-points of a permutation
\pi\in\texttt{Perm}(\mathbb{A})
:

{defi}

[Fixed-point relation]

Let
S
be a nominal set. The
fixed-point relation

\curlywedge\subseteq\texttt{Perm}(\mathbb{A})\!\times\!S
is defined as:

\pi\curlywedge x\Leftrightarrow\pi\cdot x=x.

Read “
\pi\curlywedge x
” as
“
\pi
fixes
x
”
.

It is easy to see that if
\texttt{dom}(\pi)\cap\texttt{supp}(x)=\emptyset
then
\pi\curlywedge x
holds (this follows directly from the definition of support, see Definition
2.2
). However, the other direction does not hold in general. For example, consider expressions built using atoms and a binary commutative operator
+
; the permutation
\pi=(a\ b)
fixes the equivalence class of the expression
a+b
despite the fact that its support coincides with
\texttt{dom}(\pi)
.

Permutation fixed-points will play an important role in the definition of
\alpha
-equality of nominal terms. Below we define
fixed-point constraints
and
equality constraints
using predicates
\curlywedge
and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}
and then give deduction rules to derive fixed-point and equality judgements.

Intuitively, for
s
and
t
ground nominal terms

•

s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}t
will mean that
s
and
t
are
\alpha
-equivalent, i.e., equivalent modulo renaming of abstracted atoms.

•

\pi\curlywedge t
will mean that the permutation
\pi
fixes the nominal term
t
, that is,
\pi\cdot t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}t
. This means that
\pi
has “no effect” on
t
except for the renaming of bound names, for instance,
(a\ b)\curlywedge[a]a
but not
(a\ b)\curlywedge\mathsf{f}\ a
.

In the case of non-ground terms, a fixed-point or
\alpha
-equality constraint has to be evaluated in a context, which provides information about permutations that fix the variables.

{defi}

[Fixed-point and equality constraints]
A
fixed-point constraint
is a pair
\pi\curlywedge t
of a permutation
\pi
and a term
t
. An
\alpha
-
equivalence constraint
is a pair of the form
s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}t
.

We call a fixed-point constraint of the form
\pi\curlywedge X
a
primitive fixed-point constraint
and a finite set of such constraints is called a
fixed-point context
.

\Upsilon,\Psi,\ldots
range over fixed-point contexts.

We write
\pi\curlywedge\texttt{Var}(t)
as an abbreviation for the set of constraints
\{\pi\curlywedge X\mid X\in\texttt{Var}(t)\}
.

We now introduce some notation:

The set
\texttt{Var}(\Upsilon)
of variables is defined as expected: it contains all the variables mentioned in the fixed-point context
\Upsilon
.

The set of permutations of a fixed-point context
\Upsilon
with respect to the variable
X\in\texttt{Var}(\Upsilon)
, denoted by
\texttt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X})
, is defined as
\texttt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X}):=\{\pi\ |\ \pi\curlywedge X\in\Upsilon\}
.

For a substitution
\sigma
and a fixed-point context
\Upsilon
we define
\Upsilon\sigma:=\{\pi\curlywedge X\sigma\,|\,\pi\curlywedge X\in\Upsilon\}
.

To axiomatise the relation
\curlywedge
, we rely on the notion of
conjugation
of permutations.
Conjugacy plays an important role in group theory
[
Hun80
]
. Recall that in a group
G
, the element
b
is a conjugate of
a
if there exists an element
g
such that
gag^{-1}=b
. It can be easily shown that conjugacy is an equivalence relation: if
b
is a conjugate of
a
then
a
is a conjugate of
b
. The conjugacy class of
a
consists of the elements of the form
gag^{-1}
, for
g\in G
. In the case of permutation groups,
the conjugate of
\pi
with respect to
\rho
, denoted as
\pi^{\rho}
, is the result of the composition:
\rho\circ\pi\circ\rho^{-1}
.

\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}\pi^{\rho}:&A&\overset{\rho^{-1}}{\rightarrow}&A&\overset{\pi}{\rightarrow}&A&\overset{\rho}{\rightarrow}&A\\
&a&\mapsto&\rho^{-1}(a)&\mapsto&\pi(\rho^{-1}(a))&\mapsto&\rho(\pi(\rho^{-1}(a)))\end{array}

The notion of
support
of a permutation or nominal term will be necessary for the next results. Considering permutations as elements of a nominal set, it follows from Definition
2.2
that
\texttt{supp}(\pi)=\texttt{dom}(\pi)
; similarly, in the case of a ground term
t
, the support coincides with its set of free names, i.e.,
\texttt{supp}(t)={\tt fn}(t)
. In the case a term
t
is not ground, it should be considered in a context, say
\Upsilon
, which gives information about its variables. We define it after introducing fixed-point and
\alpha
-equality judgements.

{defi}

[Judgements]
A
fixed-point judgement
is a tuple
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\ \pi\curlywedge t
of a fixed-point context and a fixed-point constraint where the atoms
\overline{c}
are newly quantified.
Here
\overline{c}=c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
, and
n=0
stands for empty quantification; in that case we may omit the

\mathsf{N}

quantifier and write simply
\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge t
.

An
\alpha
-equivalence judgement
is a tuple

\Psi\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\ s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}t
of a fixed-point context and an equality constraint
where the atoms
\overline{c}
are newly quantified.

The derivable fixed-point and
\alpha
-equivalence judgements are defined by the rules in Figures
3
and
4
.
We give an example before explaining the rules in more detail.

{exa}

The term
[a]\mathsf{f}a
is a fixed-point for the permutation
(a\ b)
, since
(a\ b)\cdot[a]\mathsf{f}a=[b]\mathsf{f}b
, which is
\alpha
-equivalent to
[a]\mathsf{f}a
. As expected, we can derive
\vdash(a\ b)\curlywedge[a]\mathsf{f}a
. For this we use the rule
(\mathbf{\curlywedge abs})
, which requires to prove
\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}c_{1}.(a\ b)\curlywedge(a\ c_{1})\cdot\mathsf{f}a
. Since
(a\ c_{1})\cdot\mathsf{f}a=\mathsf{f}c_{1}
, we need to prove
\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}c_{1}.(a\ b)\curlywedge c_{1}
, which holds by
(\mathbf{\curlywedge a})
.
However,
\mathsf{f}a
is not a fixed-point for
(a\ b)
: we cannot derive
\vdash(a\ b)\curlywedge\mathsf{f}a
. This is to be expected, since
\mathsf{f}a
and
\mathsf{f}b
are not
\alpha
-equivalent; we cannot derive
\vdash(a\ b)\cdot\mathsf{f}a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}\ \mathsf{f}a
.

The premise of rules
(\mathbf{\curlywedge abs})
and

introduces a newly quantified atom

. Recall that the introduction of a

\mathsf{N}

2.3
). Therefore, in rules
(\mathbf{\curlywedge abs})

c_{1}

In rule
(\mathbf{\curlywedge var})

\texttt{supp}(\pi^{{\pi^{\prime}}^{-1}})\setminus\{\overline{c}\}\subseteq\texttt{supp}(\texttt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X}))

means that the permutation
\pi^{{\pi^{\prime}}^{-1}}
can only affect atoms in
\texttt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X})
and new atoms, hence it fixes
X
.
Rules

(\mathbf{\curlywedge abs})
is the most interesting one.
The intuition behind this rule is the following:
[a]t
is a fixed-point of
\pi
if
\pi\cdot[a]t
is
\alpha
-equivalent to
[a]t
, that is,
[\pi(a)]\pi\cdot t
is
\alpha
-equivalent to
[a]t
;
the latter means that the only free atom in
t
that could be affected by
\pi
is
a
, hence, if we replace occurrences of
a
in
t
with another, new atom
c
,
\pi
should have no effect.
Note that if a judgement
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi\curlywedge t

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},\overline{c^{\prime}}.\pi\curlywedge t
for a new set of atoms
\overline{c^{\prime}}
(there is an infinite supply of new atoms).

The

-equivalence relation is defined using fixed-point judgements (see rule

). Rules
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}{\mathsf{a}})
,
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}{\mathsf{f}})
,
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}{\mathsf{[a]}})

are defined as expected, whereas the intuition behind rule
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}{\mathsf{var}})
is similar to the corresponding rule in Figure
3
.
The most interesting rule is
(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}{\mathsf{ab}})
. Intuitively, it states that for two abstractions
[a]s
and
[b]t
to be equivalent, we must obtain equivalent terms if we rename in one of them, in our case
t
, the abstracted atom
b
to
a
, so that they both use the same atom. Moreover, the atom
a
should not occur free in
t
, which is checked by stating that
(a\ c_{1})
fixes
t
for some newly generated atom
c_{1}
.

{exa}

Notice that
(b\,e)\curlywedge X\vdash(a\,b)\curlywedge[a](\mathsf{f}a,(c\,d)\cdot X)
. This is derived as follows: by rule
(\mathbf{\curlywedge{\mathsf{a}bs}})
we have to show
(b\,e)\curlywedge X\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}g.(a\,b)\curlywedge(\mathsf{f}g,(a\,g)(c\,d)\cdot X)
; by rule
(\mathbf{\curlywedge{\mathsf{t}uple}})
this requires
(b\,e)\curlywedge X\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}g.(a\,b)\curlywedge\mathsf{f}g
and
(b\,e)\curlywedge X\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}g.(a\,b)\curlywedge(a\,g)(c\,d)\cdot X
; the former holds by application of rules
(\mathbf{\curlywedge\mathsf{f}})
and
(\mathbf{\curlywedge{\mathsf{a}}})
since
(a\,b)\cdot g=g
, and the latter by rule
(\mathbf{\curlywedge{\mathsf{v}ar}})
, since
\texttt{supp}((a\ b)^{(c\ d)(a\ g)})\setminus\{g\}=\{g,b\}\setminus\{g\}=\{b\}
and
\texttt{supp}((b\ e))=\{b,e\}
.

However, it is not possible to derive
(a\,e)\curlywedge X\vdash(a\,b)\curlywedge[a](\mathsf{f}a,(c\,d)\cdot X)
since in this case
b
is not in the support of the permutations that fix
X
(only
(a\ e)
fixes
X
according to our context).

We prove below that
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}
is indeed an equivalence relation, for which we need to study the properties of the relations
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}
and
\curlywedge
, starting with
inversion
and
equivariance
.

Lemma 2

(Inversion)
.

The inference rules for
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}
are invertible.

Proof 3.1
.

Straightforward since there is only one rule for each syntactic class of terms. Note that in the case of rule
(\mathbf{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}\!{\mathsf{a}b}})
, the permutative convention ensures that
a
and
b
are different atoms.

Equivariance is an important notion in nominal logic: equivariant subsets of nominal sets are closed under permutation (see Definition 1.8 in
[
Pit13
]
). Below we show that the fixed-point and
\alpha
-equivalence relations are equivariant.

Lemma 3

(Equivariance)
.

(1)

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi\curlywedge\ t
iff
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi^{\rho}\curlywedge\rho\cdot t
, for any permutation
\rho
.

(2)

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}t
iff
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi\cdot s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}\pi\cdot t
, for any permutation
\pi
.

Proof 3.2
.

For both parts the “if” statement is trivial, by taking the identity permutation,
\mathit{Id}
. We prove the “only if” statements.

(1)

The proof is by rule induction using the rules in Figure
3
. We consider cases depending on the last rule applied in the derivation.

(a)

The rule applied is
(\mathbf{\curlywedge a})
.

In this case,
t=a
. Notice that,

\Upsilon\vdash\pi^{\prime}\curlywedge a
if, and only if,
\pi^{\prime}(a)=a
. We want to show that
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi^{\rho}\curlywedge\rho(a)
, for any permutation
\rho
. Notice that
\pi^{\rho}(\rho(a))=(\rho\circ\pi\circ\rho^{-1})(\rho(a))=(\rho\circ\pi)(a)=\rho(\pi(a))=\rho(a)
, and the result follows.

(b)

The rule applied is
(\mathbf{\curlywedge var})
.

In this case one has
t=\pi_{1}\cdot X
and
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi\curlywedge\pi_{1}\cdot X
.
We need to prove that

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi^{\rho}\curlywedge\rho\cdot(\pi_{1}\cdot X)
,
which requires

\texttt{supp}((\rho\circ\pi_{1})^{-1}\circ\pi^{\rho}\circ(\rho\circ\pi_{1}))\setminus\{\overline{c}\}\subseteq\texttt{supp}(\texttt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X}))
.

Note that, since
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi\curlywedge\pi_{1}\cdot X
, it follows that
\texttt{supp}(\pi_{1}^{-1}\circ\pi\circ\pi_{1})\setminus\{\overline{c}\}\subseteq\texttt{supp}(\texttt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X}))
.
By definition of
\pi^{\rho}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\texttt{supp}((\rho\circ\pi_{1})^{-1}\circ\pi^{\rho}\circ(\rho\circ\pi_{1}))

\displaystyle=\texttt{supp}((\rho\circ\pi_{1})^{-1}\circ(\rho\circ\pi\circ\rho^{-1})\circ(\rho\circ\pi_{1}))

\displaystyle=\texttt{supp}((\pi_{1}^{-1}\circ\rho^{-1}))\circ(\rho\circ\pi\circ\rho^{-1})\circ(\rho\circ\pi_{1})

\displaystyle=\texttt{supp}(\pi_{1}^{-1}\circ\pi\circ\pi_{1})

Therefore, the result follows.

(c)

The rule applied is
(\mathbf{\curlywedge abs})
.

In this case,
t=[a]t^{\prime}
and
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi\curlywedge[a]t^{\prime}
, therefore, there exists a proof
\Pi
:

\displaystyle\penalty\Pi

\displaystyle\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.\ \pi\curlywedge(a\ c_{1})\cdot t^{\prime}

(\mathbf{\curlywedge abs})

\displaystyle\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\ \pi\curlywedge[a]t^{\prime}

By induction hypothesis, for an arbitrary permutation
\rho
, there exists a derivation
\Pi^{\prime}
for :

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.\ \pi^{\rho}\curlywedge\rho\cdot((a\ c_{1})\cdot t^{\prime}).

Since
\rho\cdot((a\ c_{1})\cdot t^{\prime})=(\rho(a)\ \rho(c_{1}))\cdot(\rho\cdot t^{\prime})=(\rho(a)\ c_{1})\cdot(\rho\cdot t^{\prime})
because
c_{1}
is a new name, we have

\displaystyle\penalty\Pi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.\ \pi^{\rho}\curlywedge(\rho(a)\ c_{1})\cdot(\rho\cdot t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\ \pi^{\rho}\curlywedge[\rho(a)]\rho\cdot t^{\prime}

and the result follows.

(d)

The rule applied is
(\mathbf{\curlywedge\mathsf{f}})
or
(\mathbf{\curlywedge tuple})
.

These cases follow directly by induction hypothesis.

(2)

The proof is by rule induction using the rules in Figure
4
.

(a)

The rule applied is
(\mathbf{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}a})
.

In this case,
s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}t
is an instance of
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}a
. It is straightforward to check that
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi\cdot a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}\pi\cdot a
, via an application of the rule
(\mathbf{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}a})
.

(b)

The rule applied is
(\mathbf{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}var})
.

In this case,
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\ \pi_{1}\cdot X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}\pi_{2}\cdot X
and
\texttt{supp}(\pi_{2}^{-1}\circ\pi_{1})\setminus\{\overline{c}\}\subseteq\texttt{supp}(\texttt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X}))
.

Notice that
\texttt{supp}((\pi\circ\pi_{2})^{-1}\circ\pi\circ\pi_{1})=\texttt{supp}(\pi_{2}^{-1}\circ\pi_{1})
. Therefore, by rule

The following correctness property states that
\curlywedge
is indeed the fixed-point relation.

Theorem 5

(Correctness)
.

Let
\Upsilon
,
\pi
and
t
be a fixed-point context, a permutation and a nominal term, respectively.
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\ \pi\curlywedge t
iff
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\ \pi\cdot t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}t
.

Proof 3.8
.

In both directions the proof is by induction on the structure of the term
t
, distinguishing cases according to the last rule applied in the derivation.

(
\Rightarrow
)
The interesting cases correspond to variables and abstractions; the other cases follow directly by the induction hypothesis.

(1)

The last rule is
(\mathbf{\curlywedge var})
.

In this case,
t=\pi^{\prime}\cdot X
and
\texttt{supp}((\pi^{\prime})^{-1}\circ\pi\circ\pi^{\prime})\setminus\{\overline{c}\}\subseteq\texttt{supp}(\texttt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X}))

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\ \pi\cdot(\pi^{\prime}\cdot X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}\pi^{\prime}\cdot X

(\mathbf{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}var})
.

(2)

The last rule is
(\mathbf{\curlywedge abs})
.
In this case,
t=[a]t^{\prime}
and by assumption there is a derivation of the form:

\displaystyle\penalty\Pi

\displaystyle\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.\ \pi\curlywedge(a\ c_{1})\cdot t^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi\curlywedge[a]t^{\prime}

We need to prove that
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\ [\pi(a)]\pi\cdot t^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}[a]t^{\prime}

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\ \pi\cdot t^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}(\pi(a)\ a)\cdot t^{\prime}
and also

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.(\pi(a)\ c_{1})\curlywedge t^{\prime}
.

By IH, there exists a proof
\Pi^{\prime}
for
\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.\pi\cdot((a\ c_{1}).t^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}(a\ c_{1})\cdot t^{\prime}
.
The following equivalence holds, since
c_{1}
is newly quantified:

\displaystyle\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.\pi\cdot((a\ c_{1})\cdot t^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}(a\ c_{1})\cdot t^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.(\pi(a)\ c_{1})\cdot(\pi\cdot t^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}(a\ c_{1})\cdot t^{\prime}

(3)

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c}.\pi\cdot t^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}(\pi(a)\ a)\cdot t^{\prime}

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.\ \pi\curlywedge(a\ c_{1})\cdot t^{\prime}

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.\ \pi^{(a\ c_{1})}\curlywedge t^{\prime}

and we deduce

\Upsilon\vdash\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}\overline{c},c_{1}.(\pi(a)\ c_{1})\curlywedge t^{\prime}

3.3.
From freshness to fixed-point constraints and back again

In this section we show that the
\alpha
-equivalence relation defined in terms of

\mathrel{{\approx}_{\scriptstyle{\alpha}}}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}

from primitive freshness to primitive fixed-point constraints and a transformation
[\_]^{\#}
from primitive fixed-point to primitive freshness constraints can be defined.

Below we denote by

the family of freshness contexts, and by
\mathfrak{F}_{\curlywedge}
the family of fixed-point contexts.
The mapping
[\_]_{\curlywedge}
associates each primitive freshness constraint with a fixed-point constraint; it extends to freshness contexts in the natural way.

\begin{array}[]{cccl}[\_]_{\curlywedge}:&\mathfrak{F}_{\#}&\longrightarrow&\mathfrak{F}_{\curlywedge}\\
&a\#X&\mapsto&(a\ c_{a})\curlywedge X\mbox{ where }c_{a}\mbox{ is a new name}.\end{array}

We denote by
[\Delta]_{\curlywedge}
the image of
\Delta
under
[\_]_{\curlywedge}
.

The mapping
[\_]_{\#}
associates each primitive fixed-point constraint with a freshness constraint; it extends to fixed-point contexts in the natural way.

\begin{array}[]{cccl}[\_]_{\#}:&\mathfrak{F}_{\curlywedge}&\longrightarrow&\mathfrak{F}_{\#}\\
&\pi\curlywedge X&\mapsto&\texttt{supp}(\pi)\#X.\end{array}

We denote by
[\Upsilon]_{\#}
the image of
\Upsilon
under
[\_]_{\#}
.

Below we abbreviate the set of constraints

Using the above-specified translation of primitive constraints, we translate freshness judgements into fixed-point judgements with newly quantified constraints:

\Delta\vdash a\#t

[\Upsilon]_{\#}\vdash\overline{\texttt{supp}(\pi)\setminus\{\overline{c}\}\#t}

4.
Nominal Unification via fixed-point constraints

In this section we address the problem of unifying nominal terms. Solutions for unification problems will be represented using fixed-point constraints and substitutions. After defining unification problems, we present a simplification algorithm that computes the most general unifier for a unification problem, provided the problem has a solution (otherwise the algorithm stops indicating that there is no solution).
To specify the nominal unification algorithm, in this section we follow the approach to dealing with new atoms that relies on a name generator.

{defi}

A

Matching and unification are closely related notions. While unification is the basis of logic programming languages, matching is at the heart of rewriting and functional programming. A matching algorithm can be easily derived from a unification algorithm. First we recall the definition of matching.

{defi}

A
matching problem
is a particular kind of unification problem
Pr
in which
the variables in right-hand sides of equality constraints are disjoint from the variables in left-hand sides. A solution
\langle\Phi,\sigma\rangle
for a matching problem
Pr
satisfies
\Phi\vdash s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}t\sigma
and
\Phi\vdash\pi\curlywedge t\sigma
, for each
s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}^{?}t,\pi\curlywedge^{?}t\in\texttt{Pr}
(i.e., in a matching problem, only the variables in right-hand sides of terms can be instantiated.

A nominal matching algorithm can be obtained from the unification algorithm specified in Table

by restricting the instantiation rules, so that only variables which were in left-hand sides of equality constraints in the initial problem can be instantiated.

5.
Nominal alpha-equivalence modulo equational theories

In this section an extension of
\alpha
-equality to take into account equational theories will be proposed.
It is well-known that first-order unification modulo an arbitrary equational theory
\mathsf{E}
is undecidable, therefore, it is expected that nominal unification modulo
\mathsf{E}
(nominal
\mathsf{E}
-unification, for short) inherits this undecidability property. In this work, we propose an approach to deal with particular classes of equational theories, such as, associativity (
\mathsf{A}
), commutativity (
\mathsf{C}
) and associativity-commutativity (
\mathsf{AC}
), using fixed-point constraints.

In the case in which
\mathsf{E}
=
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{C}
,
\mathsf{AC}
, or a combination of these theories, an algorithm to check
\mathsf{E}
-
\alpha
-equality via freshness constraints was proposed in
[
AdCSFN17
,
AdCSMFR19
]
, where correctness results were formally verified using the Coq proof assistant, and an implementation in Ocaml was given. Unification was considered only for
\mathsf{C}
theories, for which it was shown that in general there is no finitary representation of the set of solutions if solutions are represented by freshness contexts and substitutions.

We argue that the approach of fixed-point constraints is convenient when dealing with unification problems in equational theories that involve some notion of permutation of elements (such as commutativity), as it was shown in the previous version of this paper
[
AFN18
]
.

5.3.

-unification problems via fixed-point constraints

In this section we propose an approach to nominal unification modulo commutativity via the notion of fixed-point constraints.

For example, assuming
+

X+Y=Y+X
, a problem of the form

+((a\ b)\cdot X,a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}^{?}+(Y,X)

(5)

can be solved by unifying
(a\ b)\cdot X
with
Y
and
a
with
X
, or

(a\ b)\cdot X
with
X
and
a
with
Y
.

In
[
ARdCSFNS18
]
, a simplification algorithm for solving nominal
\mathsf{C}
-unification was proposed. This algorithm was based on the standard nominal unification algorithm
[
UPG04
]
where
\alpha
-equivalence is defined w.r.t. the notion of freshness. Upon the input of a unification problem
Pr
, the algorithm outputs a finite family of triples of the form
\langle\nabla,\sigma,P\rangle
, where
\nabla
is a freshness context,
\sigma
a substitution and
P
is a set of fixed-point equations, which are solved using a separate procedure.

In
[
ARdCSFNS17
]
it is proved that even a simple
unification problem such as
(a\ b)\cdot X\mathrel{{\approx}_{\scriptstyle{\alpha}}}X
(i.e., a problem consisting of just one fixed-point equation) could produce an infinite and independent set of solutions, whenever the signature contains commutative function symbols. For example, if
f
is commutative, the following substitutions solve this equation:
\{X\mapsto a+b,X\mapsto f(a+b),X\mapsto[e](a+b,b+a),\ldots\}
. Therefore, it is not possible to obtain a finite and complete set of solutions for every solvable unification problem if solutions are expressed using freshness constraints and substitutions.
However, we remark that the problem
+((a\ b)\cdot X,a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\alpha}^{?}(Y,X)
mentioned above has in fact a finite number of most general solutions (indeed, two) if we solve it using fixed-point constraints. The most general unifiers are
\{X\mapsto a,Y\mapsto b\}
and
\{Y\mapsto a,(a\ b)\curlywedge X\}
. This observation led us to use fixed-point constraints instead of freshness constraints to express solutions.

4

-unification in terms of
\mathsf{C}
-fixed-point constraints, and provide a nominal

-unification unification algorithm specified by means of simplification rules.

Lemma 19

(Termination of simplification for
\mathsf{C}
-unification problems)
.

There is no infinite chain of reductions
\Longrightarrow_{\mathsf{C}}
starting from a
\mathsf{C}
-unification problem
Pr
.

Proof 5.4
.

Termination of the simplification rules follows directly from the fact that the following measure of the size of
Pr
is strictly decreasing:

[\texttt{Pr}]=(n_{1},M)
where
n_{1}
is the number of different variables
used in
Pr
,
and
M
is the multiset of
heights
of equality constraints and non-primitive fixed-point constraints occurring in
Pr
.

Each simplification step either eliminates one variable (when an instantiation rule is used) and therefore decreases the first component of the interpretation, or leaves the first component unchanged but replaces a constraint with primitive ones and/or constraints where terms have smaller height.

The simplification rules (Table
2
) specify a
\mathsf{C}
-
unification algorithm
: we apply the simplification rules in a problem
Pr
until we reach normal forms. In the case of a term rooted by a commutative symbol, two rules can be applied, so a tree of derivations is built. The termination property (Lemma
19
) guarantees the tree is finite.

For the leaves in the tree (i.e., normal forms), the notions of
consistency, failure, correctness
can be defined as in Section
4
(see Definition
4
). So, if a normal form contains equality constraints, or inconsistent fixed-point constraints of the form

\pi\curlywedge_{\mathsf{C}}^{?}a
such that
\pi(a)\neq a

then this normal form is a failure. Only leaves containing consistent fixed-point constraints produce solutions.

We now prove that the
\mathsf{C}
-unification algorithm is sound and complete. The proof is done in two stages, first we show that the non-instantiating rules preserve solutions if we consider all the branches of the derivation tree (here it is important to consider all the branches: due to the non-deterministic application of rules involving commutative operators, if we consider just one branch we may loose solutions). Then we show that the set of solutions computed from all the successful leaves is a complete set of solutions for the initial problem.

Lemma 20

(Correctness of non-instantiating rules)
.

Let
Pr
be a
\mathsf{C}
-unification problem and

a natural number. Assume
\texttt{Pr}\overset{{n}}{\Longrightarrow_{\mathsf{C}}}\texttt{Pr}^{\prime}_{i}

(i\in I)
are all the

starting from

that do not use

(\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\curlywedge}}{{\approx}}_{\scriptstyle{\alpha,\mathsf{C}}}}inst1)

(1)

(2)

For part (1),

Theorem 21

(Soundness and Completeness)
.

Let
\texttt{Pr}=\langle\Upsilon,P\rangle
be a
\mathsf{C}
-unification problem and let

\{\texttt{Pr}^{\prime}_{i}\mid\texttt{Pr}\overset{*}{\Longrightarrow_{\mathsf{C}}}\texttt{Pr}^{\prime}_{i}~and~\texttt{Pr}^{\prime}_{i}\mbox{ successful normal form}\}
be the set of all the successful normal forms of
Pr
(i.e., leaves in the derivation tree without equality constraints or inconsistent fixed-point constraints).

(1)

If
\langle\Phi,\sigma\rangle\in\bigcup\langle\texttt{Pr}^{\prime}_{i}\rangle_{\mathtt{\it{sol}}}
then
\langle\Phi,\sigma\rangle\in\mathcal{U}(\texttt{Pr})
, and

(2)

If
\langle\Phi,\sigma\rangle\in\mathcal{U}(\texttt{Pr})
, there exists

\langle\Phi^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}\rangle
such that
\langle\Phi^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}\rangle\in\bigcup\langle\texttt{Pr}^{\prime}_{i}\rangle_{\mathtt{\it{sol}}}
and
\langle\Phi^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}\rangle\preceq\langle\Phi,\sigma\rangle
,
that is, the set
\bigcup\langle\texttt{Pr}^{\prime}_{i}\rangle_{\mathtt{\it{sol}}}
is a complete set of solutions.

Proof 5.6
.

The proof is by induction on the length of a derivation
\texttt{Pr}{\Longrightarrow}^{*}\texttt{Pr}^{\prime}_{i},
distinguishing cases according to the first rule used.
By Lemma
20
, it is sufficient to check generality of solutions after each application of instantiation rules.
If the first step uses a non-instantiating rule, then the previous lemma, together with the induction hypothesis, ensures that the set of solutions of

-unification algorithm was presented in

, which outputs solutions represented as triples

consisting
of a freshness context
\nabla
, a substitution
\sigma
and a set

of the form
\pi\cdot X\approx_{\{\alpha,\mathsf{C}\}}^{?}X
.

As with standard nominal unification, one can use the functions
[\_]_{\#}
and
[\_]_{\curlywedge}
to translate solutions

.

A set of simplification rules generalising the
\mathsf{C}
-unification algorithm to take into account
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{AC}
symbols was proposed in Ribeiro’s thesis
[
dCS19
]
following the freshness constraint approach. The main difficulty is in the rules to deal with the
\mathsf{AC}
symbols and with treatment of fixed-point equations of the form
\pi\cdot X\approx^{?}_{\{\alpha,\mathsf{AC}\}}X
. Analytical proofs of soundness and completeness of such rules were given, and a formalisation in Coq was developed for the
\mathsf{C}
-unification algorithm presented in
[
ARdCSFNS18
]
. In future work we will consider and relate
\mathsf{AC}
-nominal unification with freshness and fixed-point constraints.

Regarding the complexity of the
\mathsf{C}
-unification algorithm based on fixed-point constraints, we observe that in the syntactic case (i.e., without
\alpha
-equivalence rules), the
\mathsf{C}
-unification problem is NP-complete so it is expected that the algorithm will be exponential (Chapter 10 on Equational Unification in
[
BN98
]
surveys in detail the cases of
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{AC}
unification). Comparing the nominal unification modulo
\mathsf{C}
based on freshness and on fixed-point constraints, we can again notice that there is a one-to-one correspondence in the simplification rules, and thus the algorithms have the same behaviour during the simplification phase. The main difference is that using the freshness approach, a second algorithm is needed to solve fixed-point equations (generating an infinite number of solutions in general), which is avoided with the fixed-point approach.

6.
Conclusions and Future Work

The notion of fixed-point constraint allowed us to obtain a finite representation of solutions for nominal
\mathsf{C}
-unification problems.
This brings a novel alternative to standard nominal unification approaches in which just the algebra of atom permutations and the logic of freshness constraints are used to implement equational reasoning (e.g.,
[
AK16
,
Cal13
,
CF08
,
Che10
,
FGM04
]
), and in particular to their extensions modulo commutativity, for which only infinite representations were possible in the standard approach. We have shown that with the new proposed approach the development of an algorithm to solve nominal equational problems modulo
\mathsf{C}
is simpler, avoiding, thanks to the use of fixed-point constraints, the development of procedures for the generation of infinite independent sets of solutions.

In future work we plan to extend this approach to matching and unification modulo different equational theories as well as to the treatment of equational problems in nominal rewriting modulo. Future study will also address handling the case of Mal’cev permutative theories, which include
n
-ary functions with permutative arguments
[
Com93
]
, as well as the more general and complex case of permutative equational theories
[
Sch89
]
. Finally, exploring the relation between Higher-Order Pattern unification in the style of Levy and Villaret
[
LV08
]
and nominal unification with fixed-point constrains would be also of great interest.
