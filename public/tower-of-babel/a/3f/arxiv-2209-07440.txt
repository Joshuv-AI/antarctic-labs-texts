Envy-freeness in 3D Hedonic Games This work was supported by the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (Doctoral Training Award 2125850), OTKA grant K128611, and the János Bolyai Research Fellowship.
Ágnes Cseh, Michael McKay, David Manlove

Abstract
We study the problem of partitioning a set of agents into coalitions based on the agents’ additively separable preferences, which can also be viewed as a hedonic game. We apply three successively weaker solution concepts, namely envy-freeness, weakly justified envy-freeness, and justified envy-freeness. In a model in which coalitions may have any size, trivial solutions exist for these concepts, which provides a strong motivation for placing restrictions on coalition size. In this paper, we require feasible coalitions to have size three. We study the existence of partitions that are envy-free, weakly justified envy-free, and justified envy-free, and the computational complexity of finding such partitions, if they exist. We present a comprehensive complexity classification, in terms of the restrictions placed on the agents’ preferences. From this, we identify a general trend that for the three successively weaker solution concepts, existence and polynomial-time solvability hold under successively weaker restrictions.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background and motivation

We study a model that involves partitioning a set of agents into disjoint coalitions of size three. In relation to the existing literature, this model could be described as either a
hedonic game with fixed size coalitions
or a model of
three dimensional roommates
. We assume agents’ preferences are additively separable, or equivalently that each agent assigns a numerical valuation to every other agent. This assumption means that an instance of our model can be represented as an
Additively Separable Hedonic Game
(ASHG), which is a type of cooperative game
[
1
,
2
]
. The “hedonic” aspect refers to the fact that “every agent only cares about which agents are in its coalition, but does not care how agents in other coalitions are grouped together”
[
3
,
4
]
, which is a natural assumption to make in the study of multi-agent systems
[
5
]
.

Our main interest in this model is the existence of, and complexity of finding, partitions that are envy-free, meaning there exists no pair of agents where the one would prefer to swap places with the other. In fact, we consider three successively weaker formalisms of this notion, namely
envy-freeness
,
weakly justified envy-freeness
, and
justified envy-freeness
.

Informally, we say that agent
i
has
envy
for agent
j
if agent
i
would gain a higher utility by swapping places with
j
in the partition. We say that such envy is
justified
if the other agents in
j
’s coalition strictly prefer
j
to
i
, and that such envy is
weakly justified
if the other agents in
j
’s coalition either prefer
j
to
i
or are indifferent between the two. We shall define these concepts formally in Section
2
(and illustrate them in Figure
2
).

A strong motivation exists for combining these solution concepts with the setting of fixed-size coalitions (and specifically of size three). In particular, in a model in which coalitions have arbitrary size, such as a hedonic game, it can be trivial to construct partitions that are envy-free by, for example, placing all agents into a single coalition
[
6
,
7
]
. Moreover, much less is known about models in which coalitions have a fixed size compared to hedonic games. Most of the existing work involving fixed-size coalitions involves finding partitions that are
stable
, meaning there exists no coalition
S
in which each agent in
S
prefers
S
to their assigned coalition in the partition.
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. It has also been argued in the literature that the assumption made in hedonic games that coalitions may have any size is unrealistic in practice
[
13
]
.

In fact, many of the the computational problems associated with stability in this setting are
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
so it makes sense in particular to study justified envy-freeness, which (as we shall see in Section
2
) is a relaxation of stability. This also provides a justification for beginning with coalitions of size three, since any hardness result in this setting is likely to give an indication for larger coalition sizes.

1.2
Related work

1.2.1
Overview of the subsection

We discuss three sets of related literature. The first is work involving both restricted coalition sizes and envy-freeness. The second is work involving envy-freeness and general settings of coalition formation, with no restriction on coalition size (this includes hedonic games). The third is work on coalition formation involving coalitions of fixed size but not necessarily involving envy-freeness (this includes three- and
multidimensional
roommates).

For more general models of coalition formation and hedonic games that involve solution concepts other than those related to envy, and coalitions of arbitrary size, a wealth of literature exists
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
.

1.2.2
Envy-freeness and restricted coalition size

We introduce three existing works.

The first is by Li et al.
[
18
]
, who considered a strictly more general model. Rather than a fixing the size of a coalition, a partition of the set of
n
agents must be
balanced
, meaning the number of coalitions
k\leq n
is fixed and
\lfloor n/k\rfloor\leq|S|\leq\lceil n/k\rceil
for any coalition
S
in a feasible partition. They studied an approximation of envy-freeness, termed EF-
r
, in which the utility gained by any envious agent may be up to
r
in a feasible partition, for some fixed
r\geq 0
(our definition of envy-freeness is thus EF-
0
). Interestingly, they applied results from discrepancy theory to show that an approximate-envy free partition with a particular fixed asymptotic bound must exist, and can be found in polynomial time. They also considered restricted sets of instances, such those in which the underlying structure is a tree. They showed that in such an instance, an EF-
1
partition must exist and can be found in polynomial time. A coalition partition in which every coalition has size three is by definition balanced, so some of the algorithmic results relating to approximate envy-free partitions
[
18
, Theorems 9 and 10]
also apply to our model.

The second is by Li and Li
[
19
]
. The authors defined a very general model in which a set of resources is to be assigned in a fair way by a set of agents. In this model, they characterised a relationship between
proportional
and
envy-free
assignments. They also defined
Pareto envy-freeness
(PEF), which is a relaxation of envy-freeness as defined in that setting. The authors’ main contribution relates to a restricted case of the general model, in which agents are assigned to dormitories (dorms). Each agent is assigned to exactly one dorm and each dorm is shared by exactly two agents. Agents have binary and symmetric preferences over other agents, as dorm-mates, and additively separable preferences over possible dorms. The main result is that a PEF assignment must exist and can be found in polynomial time. The authors also show that if dorms are shared by exactly three agents then a PEF may not exist. The model we study in this paper can be seen as a special case of the latter model, in which agents are indifferent between possible dorms.

The third is by Boehmer and Elkind
[
20
]
. The authors considered a model in which a set of agents is to be partitioned into sets of equal size. Notably, the desired coalition size is supplied as part of the input. In their model, each agent’s preference between possible coalitions is highly constrained. Each agent belongs to one of a number of types and compares two possible coalitions considering only the proportion of agents in each coalition of each type. The authors presented results for a number of solution concepts, including envy-freeness as we define it here. In particular, they showed that envy-free partitions may not exist and the associated decision problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete, even for two possible further restrictions on the agents’ preferences. Notably, they also showed that if the coalition size is fixed, and not part of the input, then an Integer Programming-based algorithm can be used to decide, in polynomial time, if a given instance contains an envy-free coalition partition.

1.2.3
Envy-freeness and arbitrary coalition size

In a comprehensive study on ASHGs, Aziz et al.
[
16
]
defined envy-freeness as a solution concept in ASHGs. They note that the “singleton” coalition partition
\{\{1\},\{2\},\dots,\{{|N|}\}\}
is trivially envy-free and thus considered the existence of partitions that simultaneously satisfy envy-freeness and other established solution concepts. The existence of trivially envy-free partitions provides a strong motivation for restricting the size of coalitions, by for example requiring that each coalition has a fixed size.

Ueda
[
7
]
also considered envy-freeness and justified envy-freeness in hedonic games, in a setting in which agents have ordinal preferences over all possible coalitions. He too observed that the singleton coalition partition and the “grand” coalition partition
\{N\}
are trivially envy-free, although there exist instances in which no non-trivial coalition partition is envy-free, and additional instances in which no non-trivial partition is justified envy-free. He also observed that any so-called
core stable
partition
[
3
]
is also justified envy-free.

Barrot and Yokoo
[
6
]
noted Ueda’s observation in 2019, and continued exploring the existence of coalition partitions that satisfy a combination of solution concepts, including envy-freeness, weakly justified envy-freeness, and justified envy-freeness. As well as some non-existence results relating to ASHGs, they also considered more general systems of preference representation. Notably, they presented results relating to the existence of such coalition partitions in a setting in which preferences satisfy
top responsiveness
and
bottom responsiveness
, two restrictions already well-established in the hedonic games literature.

1.2.4
Other solution concepts and restricted coalition size

A central solution concept in hedonic games is
core stability
(or simply
stability
)
[
3
]
. We provided an informal definition of stability in Section
1.1
(and provide a formal definition later, in Section
2
). Informally, a partition is core stable if there exists no coalition
S
in which each agent in
S
prefers
S
to their assigned coalition in the partition. The
Stable Roommates problem
, which can be viewed as a hedonic game, involves matching a set of agents into pairs. It is a classical problem of matching under preferences and has been studied extensively
[
21
]
. Its generalisation to higher dimensions creates a model that can be equally viewed as a hedonic game with a fixed coalition size. In particular, the three-dimensional case has received much attention, and a number of specific variants have been studied in the literature
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.

In 2018, Sless et al.
[
13
]
proposed a model that can be viewed as a type of ASHG with symmetric preferences. They argued that a strong practical motivation exists for considering coalitions of restricted size, and thus focused on the existence of coalition partitions that contain exactly
k
coalitions, for some fixed
k\geq 1
. They presented a number of theoretical and empirical results relating to this model. They showed that the problem of finding a partition with maximum utilitarian welfare can be solved in polynomial time in the restricted case in which
k
is fixed and there are, in a precise sense, relatively few negative edges. Otherwise, they showed that this construction problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard. They also presented other polynomial-time solvability results for a problem in which a central organiser can add edges to the instance.

In 2019, Cseh et al.
[
22
]
considered Pareto optimal partitions in a general model that is comparable to a hedonic game. In this model there is a set of rooms with integer sizes, and any coalition must be allocated to exactly one room where the size of the room is exactly the size of the coalition. They studied two specific variants of this model, applying
\mathscr{B}
- and
\mathscr{W}
-preferences. They showed if
\mathscr{B}
-preferences are used and the agents’ preference lists are strict then a polynomial-time algorithm based on serial dictatorship can be used to construct a Pareto optimal partition in polynomial time. They also showed that, in a number of other circumstances, a Pareto optimal partition may not exist and that in many cases the associated existence problems are either
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard or
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.

In 2022, Bilò et al.
[
23
]
studied ASHGs in which the coalition size is fixed and agents have binary and symmetric preferences, considering
swap stability
and two related concepts, namely
strict swap stability
and
swap stability under transferable utilities
. In the following definitions, we use
u_{i}(\pi)
to denote the utility of
i
in a partition
\pi
(see Section
2
for a formal definition). In a partition that is
strictly swap stable
, no two agents
i,j
exist where if
j
and
j
swap places then the utilities of both agents strictly increase. In a partition that is
swap stable
, no two agents
i,j
exist where if
j
and
j
swap places then the utility of
i
strictly increases and the utility of
j
does not decrease. In a partition
\pi
that is
swap stable under transferable utilities
, no two agents
i,j
exist where if
i
and
j
swap places, in a new partition
\pi^{\prime}
, then
u_{i}(\pi^{\prime})+u_{j}(\pi^{\prime})>u_{i}(\pi)+u_{j}(\pi)
. They remarked that a variation of these solution concepts had been previously studied in the context of the Stable Marriage problem as
exchange stability

[
24
]
. We remark here that envy-freeness implies strict swap stability.

We consider the existence of envy-free, weakly justified envy-free, and justified envy-free partitions into triples and the complexity of the associated decision and construction problems. For each of the three solution concepts, we consider various restrictions on the agents’ valuations.

Our results, summarised in Table

In Section

, we show that an envy-free partition into triples may not exist, even if the preferences are symmetric and binary and the maximum degree of the underlying graph is
2

(Theorem

).

In Section

that do not contain weakly justified envy-free partitions into triples is a strict subset of the set of ASHGs with maximum degree

that do not contain envy-free partitions into triples. We describe a more complex polynomial-time algorithm for this case that can decide if a weakly justified envy-free partition into triples exists and construct such a partition if so (Theorem
3

In Section

{\mathsf{NP}}

6

, and the associated decision problem is

-complete (Theorem

In Section 6 we conclude the paper and consider possible directions for future work.

Preliminaries

An ASHG comprises a set
N
of
3n
agents with

over coalitions, which we define as follows. Each agent

valuation function

v_{\alpha_{i}}:N\setminus\{{\alpha_{i}}\}\mapsto\mathbb{Z}

. An agent’s preference between two possible partitions depends only on the partners of that agent in each partition, so given a partition
\pi

. If

then preferences are
ternary
. If preferences are symmetric and binary then they reveal an undirected graph, which we refer to as the

(N,E)
. We define the

The three solution concepts that we explore are all related to “envy”. Suppose

. We say that an agent

Suppose

all prefer the envying agent
\alpha_{i}
to
\alpha_{j}
. Similarly, we say that envy is

(termed wj-envy) if
v_{\alpha_{k}}(\alpha_{i})\geq v_{\alpha_{k}}(\alpha_{j})
for each
\alpha_{k}\in\pi(\alpha_{j})\setminus\{\alpha_{j}\}

. The definitions of

(j-envy-free) and

3
Envy-freeness

3.1
Introduction

In this section we consider consider envy-freeness in ASHGs with binary and symmetric preferences.

We first show, in Section
3.2
, that if the maximum degree of a given ASHG is
2
then, in polynomial time, we can either construct an envy-free partition into triples or report that no such partition exists (Theorem
1
).

We then show, in Section
3.3
, that it is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete to decide if a given ASHG with binary and symmetric preferences has an envy-free partition into triples even when the maximum degree is
3
(Theorem
2
).

3.2
Symmetric and binary preferences with maximum degree
2

Our first result is a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of an envy-free partition into triples in an ASHG with binary and symmetric preferences and maximum degree
2
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider an ASHG with binary and symmetric preferences and maximum degree
2
. Let
P
be the set of isolated agents,
\mathcal{Q}
be the set of connected components of size
3{k_{1}}-2
for any
{k_{1}}>1
, and
\mathcal{R}
be the set of connected components of size
3{k_{2}}-1
for any
{k_{2}}\geq 1
. An envy-free partition into triples exists if and only if
2|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|\leq|P|
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary ASHG with binary and symmetric preferences, represented by its underlying graph
(N,E)
. Label
P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{|P|}\}
,
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{1},Q_{2},\dots,Q_{|\mathcal{Q}|}\}
, and
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{1},R_{2},\allowbreak\dots,R_{|\mathcal{R}|}\}
. Let
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{|\mathcal{S}|}\}
be the set of connected components of size
3{k_{3}}
for any
{k_{3}}\geq 1
.

To show the first direction, suppose
2|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|\leq|P|
. We shall construct a partition into triples
\pi
and demonstrate that it is envy-free. First, observe that if any agent has utility
1
or more then that agent is not envious, since the maximum degree of the underlying graph is
2
.

Construct
\pi
as follows. First, consider each
S=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{3{k_{3}}})
in
\mathcal{S}
. For each
i
where
1\leq i\leq{k_{3}}
, add
\{s_{3i-2},s_{3i-1},s_{3i}\}
to
\pi
. It follows that each agent in
S
has utility at least

in
\mathcal{R}
. For each
i
where
1\leq i\leq{k_{2}}-1
, add
\{r_{3i-2},r_{3i-1}r_{3i}\}
to
\pi
. Next, add
\{r_{3{k_{2}}-2},r_{3{k_{2}}-1},p_{2|\mathcal{Q}|+i}\}
to
\pi
(recall that
|P|\geq 2|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|
). It follows that each agent in
R
is not envious. Now consider each
Q=(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{3{k_{1}}-2})
in
\mathcal{Q}
. For each
i
where
1\leq i\leq{k_{1}}-2
, add
\{q_{3i-2},q_{3i-1},q_{3i}\}
to
\pi
. Next, add to
\pi

\{q_{3{k_{1}}-5},q_{3{k_{1}}-4},p_{i}\}

Finally, arbitrarily add the remaining agents in

to triples in

To show the second direction, suppose for a contradiction that

Now consider some

Theorem 1
.

Consider an ASHG with binary and symmetric preferences and maximum degree
2
. There exists an
O(|N|)
-time algorithm that can either find an envy-free partition into triples or report that no such partition exists.

Proof.

Lemma
1
gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of an envy-free partition into triples in
(N,E)
, based on the number of connected components of different sizes. Define
P
,
\mathcal{Q}
, and
\mathcal{R}
as before in Lemma
1
. We outline a linear-time algorithm based on the constructive proof in Lemma
1
. The algorithm outputs either
\bot
, if no envy-free partition into triples exists, or a labelling
\tau
of each agent
\alpha_{i}
with some index

that represents the index of
\pi(\alpha_{i})
in some arbitrary ordering of the triples in an envy-free partition into triples
\pi
.

The algorithm has three phases. In the first phase, the algorithm constructs a stack
P
that contains all isolated agents in
(N,E)
. It also constructs a stack
T
, which contains exactly one agent per connected component of size two or more, such that if a connected component is a path then
T
contains one of its endpoints. The construction of
T
can thus be performed in linear time.

The algorithm now enters the second phase, which is as follows. The algorithm maintains a counter
r
to track the label of the agent last labelled. Initially,
r=1
. The algorithm pops an unmarked agent
m_{i}
from the stack
T
and marks
\tau(m_{i})=1
. It sets a counter
c
to
1
, which will track the size of the connected component that contains
m_{i}
. It then identifies successive adjacent agents and labels each one with
r
, incrementing
r
by one every third agent, following the path or cycle in the underlying graph. The successive agents are therefore marked
1,1,1,2,2,2,3,3,3\dots
. The counter
c
is updated to ensure that
c
is the size of this connected component. Eventually, either some agent with degree
1
or some previously labelled agent is discovered. In this case, there are three possibilities.

The first possibility is that
c=3{k_{3}}
for some
{k_{3}}\geq 1
. In this case the algorithm pops some yet unlabelled
m_{i}
from the stack
T
and repeats the above process.

The second possibility is that
c=3{k_{2}}-1
for some
{k_{2}}\geq 1
. In this case the algorithm pops some isolated agent
p_{i}
from the stack
P
and labels
\tau(p_{i})=r
. If the stack
P
is empty then it must be that
2|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|>|P|
and thus the algorithm returns
\bot
. The algorithm then pops some yet unlabelled
m_{i}
from the stack
T
and repeats the above process.

The third possibility is that
c=3{k_{1}}-2
for some
{k_{1}}>1
. In this case it must be that exactly one agent
\alpha_{j}
has been marked with
r
. The algorithm identifies the last agent
\alpha_{l}
that was labelled with
r-1
, which must be adjacent to
\alpha_{j}
. It relabels
\tau(\alpha_{l})=r
. It follows that exactly two adjacent agents are labelled with
r-1
and exactly two adjacent agents are labelled with
r
. The algorithm then pops two isolated agents
p_{g},p_{h}
from the stack
P
and labels
\tau(p_{g})=r
and
\tau(p_{h})=r-1
. If
|P|<2
then it must be that
2|\mathcal{Q}|+|\mathcal{R}|>|P|
and thus the algorithm returns
\bot
. The algorithm then pops some yet-unlabelled
m_{i}
from the stack

and repeats the above process.

In the third phase, since the algorithm has not yet returned
\bot

or more has been labelled and therefore assigned to some triple in

to triples in
\pi
by popping successive agents
p_{i}
from the stack

and labelling

∎

3.3
Symmetric and binary preferences with maximum degree
3

We now consider ASHGs with binary and symmetric preferences in which the maximum degree of the underlying graph is

. In contrast with Theorem

{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete, even when preferences are binary and symmetric and the maximum degree is

We present a polynomial-time reduction from a variant of
Exact Satisfiability
(XSAT)
[

. An instance of XSAT is a boolean formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF). We write

to represent such a formula as the set of its clauses. We represent each clause

to mean the set of variables contained in the formula. A
truth assignment

is an assignment of values to the set of variables. We say that an

is a truth assignment to the variables such that each clause contains exactly one true literal (and therefore exactly two false literals). Given an instance

is
exactly satisfiable
. Deciding if an instance

of XSAT is exactly satisfiable is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete
[
27
]
even when each clause contains exactly three literals. Porschen et al.
[

-complete even for the restricted case every literal is positive and each variable occurs in exactly three clauses, which they denote by

\text{CNF}_{+}^{3}

. We shall refer to this variant as
\text{X3SAT}_{+}^{\,=3}
. Note that in this problem

Theorem 2
.

Deciding if a given ASHG contains an envy-free partition into triples is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete, even when preferences are binary and symmetric and the underlying graph has maximum degree

.

Proof.

It is straightforward to show that this decision problem belongs to

we can test if
\alpha_{i}

\alpha_{j}

We have presented a polynomial-time reduction from

. Given an arbitrary instance

∎

4
Weakly justified envy-freeness

4.1
Introduction

In this section we consider consider wj-envy-freeness in ASHGs with binary and symmetric preferences.

In Section
4.2
, we consider ASHGs with maximum degree
2
. We show that if such an ASHG has binary and symmetric preferences then in polynomial time we can either construct a wj-envy-free partition into triples or report that no such partition into triples exists (Theorem
3
). In fact, we present a necessary and sufficient condition for the non-existence of a wj-envy-free partition into triples in such an ASHG (in Definition
1
).

We then show, in Section
4.3
, that it is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete to decide if a wj-envy-free partition into triples exists, given an ASHG, even when the preferences are binary and symmetric and the instance has maximum degree
3
.

4.2
Symmetric and binary preferences with maximum degree
2

We begin by defining a class of ASHGs,

-cycles and a single isolated agent.

does not contain a wj-envy-free partition into triples. Consider an ASHG with binary and symmetric preferences that belongs to

Lemma 7

Suppose

Building on Lemma

is a necessary and sufficient condition for the non-existence of a wj-envy-free partition into triples in ASHGs with with binary and symmetric preferences and maximum degree

In some respects the approach taken by Algorithm

is straightforward. For example, paths or cycles that contain a number of agents divisible by

are broken up into triples of three successively adjacent agents. Other paths and cycles, except

-cycles, are broken up in a similar fashion leaving one or two surplus agents per connected component. More care is required in the assignment of the agents in
4
-cycles to triples. The
12
agents in three

-cycles can be assigned to four triples in a relatively straightforward way that ensures no agent is wj-envied in some resulting partition into triples. The main complexity of Algorithm

stems from the case when the number of

-cycles is not divisible by

Algorithm

contains calls to five subroutines, which are presented separately in order to simplify the overall presentation. Four of the subroutines take as input some agents in

and constructs a set of triples containing some or all of the agents in that set. The final subroutine is a helper function used to shorten the pseudocode of the main algorithm.

5
Justified envy-freeness

5.1
Introduction

In this section we consider consider j-envy-freeness.

In Section
5.2
, we consider ASHGs with binary preferences. We first observe that since a stable partition into triples must exist, and can be found in polynomial time, a j-envy-free partition into triples must also exist, and can be found in polynomial time (Observation
1
). We then show that a justified envy-free partition into triples must exist and can be found in polynomial time, even if preferences are binary and not necessarily symmetric (Theorem
5
). Interestingly, this tractability result applies to a set of ASHGS in which a stable partition into triples may not exist, and the associated decision problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete
[
11
]
.

We then complement this result with two hardness results. The first, shown in Section
5.3
, is that a given ASHG may not contain a justified envy-free partition into triples even when preferences are ternary but not symmetric, and the associated existence problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete (Theorem
6
).

The second, shown in Section
5.4
, is that a given ASHG may not contain a justified envy-free partition into triples even when preferences are non-binary and symmetric, and the associated existence problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete (Theorem
7
).

5.2
Binary preferences

It is straightforward to show that if a partition into triples is stable then it is j-envy-free. A corollary is that if we are given an ASHG in which preferences are binary and symmetric then we can find a j-envy-free partition into triples by finding a stable partition into triples, which must exist and can be found in polynomial time

Observation 1

Theorem 6
.

Deciding if a given ASHG contains a j-envy-free partition into triples is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete, even when preferences are ternary.

Proof.

It is straightforward to show that this decision problem belongs to
{\mathsf{NP}}

we can test if
\alpha_{i}
j-envies
\alpha_{j}

We have presented a polynomial-time reduction from a restricted version of Directed Triangle Cover (DTC), which is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete
[
29

. Given a directed antisymmetric graph

(N,V)
with ternary preferences. Lemmas

(N,V)
contains a j-envy-free partition into triples if and only if

contains a directed triangle cover and thus that this decision problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}

∎

5.4
Symmetric non-binary preferences

From Theorems
5
and
6
, a natural question arises: is it the symmetry of agents’ preferences that guarantees the existence of a j-envy-free partition into triples?
In this section we show that this is not the case, and a j-envy-free partition into triples may not exist even when agents’ preferences are symmetric, and the associated existence problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete. We remark, however, that the instances shown that do not contain j-envy-free partitions into triples are relatively contrived and involve non-negative integer valuations up to
6
. We leave open the case in which preferences are symmetric and the maximum valuation is strictly less than
6
.

To show that this existence problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete, we present a polynomial-time reduction from PIT. This reduction is similar to the reduction we presented in Section
5.3
for the analogous problem involving ternary preferences that are not (necessarily) symmetric. In that section we reduced from Directed Triangle Packing (DTC) but here we reduce from Partition Into Triangles (PIT, Problem
3
).

\{\{\alpha_{b_{1}},\alpha_{b_{2}}\},\{\alpha_{b_{3}},\alpha_{b_{4}}\}\}=\{\{l_{1},l_{4}\},\{l_{2},l_{3}\}\}
. As before,
l_{1}
has j-envy for
h_{a_{2}}
since
u_{l_{1}}(\{h_{a_{1}},l_{4}\})=1<3\leq u_{l_{1}}(\{l_{2},l_{3}\})
,
v_{l_{2}}(h_{a_{2}})=0<2=v_{l_{2}}(l_{1})
, and
v_{l_{3}}(h_{a_{2}})=0<1\leq v_{l_{3}}(l_{1})
.

It remains that
\{\{\alpha_{b_{1}},\alpha_{b_{2}}\},\{\alpha_{b_{3}},\alpha_{b_{4}}\}\}=\{\{l_{1},l_{2}\},\{l_{3},l_{4}\}\}
.
∎

By Lemma
50
, either
\{\alpha_{b_{1}},\alpha_{b_{2}}\}=\{l_{1},l_{2}\}
or
\{\alpha_{b_{1}},\alpha_{b_{2}}\}=\{l_{3},l_{4}\}
. Without loss of generality assume that
\{\alpha_{b_{1}},\alpha_{b_{2}}\}=\{l_{1},l_{2}\}
.

Lemma 51
.

If
(N,V)
contains a j-envy-free partition into triples then
u_{c_{i}}(\pi)=6
for each
i
where
1\leq i\leq 3q
.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that some
1\leq i\leq 3q
exists where
u_{c_{i}}(\pi)<6
. Then
c_{i}
has j-envy for
h_{a_{1}}
since
u_{c_{i}}(\pi)\leq 5<6\leq u_{c_{i}}(\{l_{1},l_{2}\})
,
v_{l_{1}}(h_{a_{1}})=0<3=v_{l_{1}}(c_{i})
, and
v_{l_{2}}(h_{a_{1}})=0<3=v_{l_{2}}(c_{i})
. This contradicts our supposition that
\pi
is j-envy-free.
∎

Lemma 52
.

If
(N,V)
contains a j-envy-free partition into triples then
G
contains a partition into triangles.

Proof.

Suppose
(N,V)
contains a j-envy-free partition into triangles
\pi
. Lemma
51
shows that
u_{c_{i}}(\pi)=6
for each
i
where
1\leq i\leq 3q
. By construction, it follows that
\pi(c_{i})
contains two agents
c_{j},c_{k}
such that
v_{c_{i}}(c_{j})=v_{c_{i}}(c_{k})=3
. By construction,
c_{j}
and
c_{k}
therefore correspond to vertices
w_{j},w_{k}\in W
where
\{w_{i},w_{j}\}\in E
and
\{w_{i},w_{k}\}\in E
. It follows thus that there are exactly
q
triples in
\pi
each containing three agents
\{c_{i},c_{j},c_{k}\}
, where the three corresponding vertices
w_{i},w_{j},w_{k}
are pairwise adjacent in
G
. From these triples a partition into triangles
X
can be easily constructed.
∎

We have now shown that the ASHG
(N,V)
contains a j-envy-free partition into triples if and only if the PIT instance
G
contains a partition into triangles. This shows that the reduction is correct.

Theorem 7
.

Deciding if a given ASHG contains a j-envy-free partition into triples is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete, even when preferences are symmetric and the maximum possible valuation is
6
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to show that this decision problem belongs to
{\mathsf{NP}}
, since for any two agents
\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}\in N
we can test if
\alpha_{i}
j-envies
\alpha_{j}
in constant time.

We have presented a polynomial-time reduction from Partition Into Triangles (PIT), which is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete
[
26
]
. Given a graph
G
, the reduction constructs an ASHG
(N,V)
with symmetric preferences in which the maximum valuation is
6
. Lemmas
42
and
52
show that
(N,V)
contains a j-envy-free partition into triples if and only if
G
contains a partition into triangles and thus that this decision problem is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard.
∎

Remark
.

The authors designed the reduction in Theorem
7
around a particular instance that contains no j-envy-free partition into triples (which can be constructed by removing
l_{2},l_{3},l_{4}
and

from any instance produced by the reduction). This particular instance was discovered by a guided computer search, using an Integer Programming
[
30
]
model to test candidate solutions. One assumption made was the valuations of

, in the spirit of an “undesired guest”
[

]
. A similar technique has been applied in another setting of hedonic games
[

. We believe that this was an effective technique to identify ASHGs that have no partition into triples satisfying a particular solution concept, although it remains open if the instance described contains a minimal number of agents.

6
Conclusion

We considered three successively weaker solution concepts: envy-freeness, weakly justified envy-freeness, and justified envy-freeness, and studied the existence of partitions into triples that satisfy each concept together with associated decision and construction problems.

From a practical perspective, the polynomial-time algorithms for envy-freeness and wj-envy-freeness impose a strict requirement on the structure of the agents’ preferences. While such restrictions may not necessarily be achievable in practice, the identification of this frontier between solvability and
{\mathsf{NP}}
-completeness is a first step. On the other hand, the polynomial-time algorithm for justified envy-freeness can be applied more generally to situations involving “friends” and “neutrals”.

The scope for future work is wide.

One immediate open problem relates to justified envy-freeness and preferences that are symmetric and ternary (a strengthening of Theorem
7
). Another immediate direction is to what extent our results generalise to other restrictions on coalition size, for example a model in which coalitions must have size
k
, for some
k\geq 3
. We conjecture that all of our
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness reductions can be generalised to this setting. Although the restriction of maximum degree
2
is a strong one, it is unclear if our positive results can be similarly generalised.

Studying weaker solution concepts, such as
r
-approximate envy-freeness, denoted EF-
r
, may lead to practical and interesting efficient algorithms. Li et al.
[
18
]
pose a similar question in their related model, asking if every instance with binary and symmetric preferences contains a balanced EF-
2
partition.

Similarly, it could also be interesting to investigate ordinal preferences, for example considering complete,
\mathscr{B}
-,
\mathscr{W}
-, or lexicographic preferences
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
. We conjecture that in a hedonic game model with
\mathscr{B}
-preferences
[
32
]
, an envy-free partition may not exist, and the associated existence problem is solvable in polynomial time.

Another possibility is to identify other restrictions on the agents’ valuations in which an envy-free, wj-envy-free or j-envy-free partition into triples can be found in polynomial time. The gadgets used in our reductions are highly regular and it might be that there exist interesting classes of instances that must contain an envy-free, wj-envy-free, or j-envy-free partition into triples. Alternatively, we could study these problems from the perspective of parameterised complexity. For example, in the case of binary and symmetric preferences, one could consider the tree-width
[
35
]
of the underlying graph.

We believe that it would be very interesting to estimate the probability that a randomly chosen ASHG contains an envy-free, wj-envy-free, or j-envy-free partition into triples, or to estimate the same probability in a random ASHG with binary or ternary preferences. Our complexity results indicate that, among instances with binary and symmetric preferences and maximum degree
2
, the set of ASHGs that contain a j-envy-free partition into triples (i.e. all instances) is larger than the set of ASHGs that contain a wj-envy-free partition into triples, which is in turn larger than the set of ASHGs that contain an envy-free partition into triples. We conjecture that, in general, the probability that a given ASHG contains an envy-free partition into triples is smaller than the probability that it contains a wj-envy-free partition into triples, which is in turn smaller than the probability that it contains a j-envy-free partition into triples.
In this direction, it might be possible to apply probabilistic techniques from graph theory, such as the Erdös-Rényi model of a random graph. Of course, the probabilistic events in which agents have envy for other agents are not independent, which complicates the analysis. Alternatively, an empirical approach might be informative.
