Diffusion Model Based Posterior Sampling for Noisy Linear Inverse Problems
Xiangming Meng, Yoshiyuki Kabashima

Abstract
With the rapid development of diffusion models and flow-based generative models, there has been a surge of interests in solving noisy linear inverse problems, e.g., super-resolution, deblurring, denoising, colorization, etc, with generative models. However, while remarkable reconstruction performances have been achieved, their inference time is typically too slow since most of them rely on the seminal diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) framework and thus to approximate the intractable likelihood score, time-consuming gradient calculation through back-propagation is needed. To address this issue, this paper provides a fast and effective solution by proposing a simple closed-form approximation to the likelihood score. For both diffusion and flow-based models, extensive experiments are conducted on various noisy linear inverse problems such as noisy super-resolution, denoising, deblurring, and colorization. In all these tasks, our method (namely DMPS) demonstrates highly competitive or even better reconstruction performances while being significantly faster than all the baseline methods.

Introduction

Many problems in science and engineering such as computer vision and signal processing can be cast as the following noisy linear inverse problems:

\displaystyle{\bf{y}}={\bf{Ax}}_{0}+{\bf{n}},

(1)

where
{\bf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
is a (known) linear mixing matrix,
{\bf{n}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf{n}};0,\sigma^{2}{\bf{I}})
is an i.i.d. additive Gaussian noise, and the goal is to recover the unknown target signal
{\bf{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
from the noisy linear measurements
{\bf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times 1}
. Notable examples include a wide class of image restoration tasks like super-resolution (SR)
Ledig et al. 2017
, colorization
Zhang et al. 2016
, denoising
Buades et al. 2005
, deblurring
Yuan et al. 2007
, inpainting
Bertalmio et al. 2000
, as well as the well-known compressed sensing (CS)
Candès et al. 2006
;
Candès and Wakin 2008
in signal processing. One big challenge of these linear inverse problems is that they are ill-posed
O’Sullivan 1986
, i.e., the solution to (
1
) is not unique (even in the noiseless case). This problem can be tackled from a Bayesian perspective: suppose that the target signal
\bf{x}
follows a proper
prior distribution

p(\bf{x})
, given noisy observations
\bf{y}
, one can perform posterior sampling from
p({\bf{x}}_{0}|\bf{y})
to recover
{\bf{x}}_{0}
. Hence, an accurate prior
p({\bf{x}}_{0})
is crucial in recovering
{\bf{x}}_{0}
. Various kinds of priors or structure constraints have been proposed, including sparsity
Candès and Wakin 2008
, low-rank
Fazel et al. 2008
, total variation
Candès et al. 2006
, just to name a few. However, such handcrafted priors might fail to capture the capture more rich structure of natural signals
Ulyanov et al. 2018
.

;
Ma et al. 2024

Kadkhodaie and Simoncelli 2020
;
Kadkhodaie and Simoncelli 2021
;
Jalal et al. 2021a
;
Jalal et al. 2021b
;
Kawar et al. 2021
;

;

;

;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Meng and Kabashima 2023
;
Meng and Kabashima 2024
;
Pokle et al. 2023
. One fundamental challenge in this field is computing the score of
noise-perturbed likelihood

p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t})
, i.e.,
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t})}
, where
{\bf{x}}_{t}
is a noise-perturbed version of
{\bf{x}}_{0}
at time instance

defined by the forward process of DM

;
Song and Ermon 2019
. This is because while
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t})}
is easily obtained for
t=0
from (
1
), it is intractable for general
t>0
. To address this challenge, most diffusion and flow-based methods adopt the diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) framework
Chung et al. 2022a
which leverages the Tweedie’s formula
Robbins 1992
to obtain a posterior estimate of
\bf{x}_{0}
. While DPS and its variants achieve excellent reconstruction performances, they suffer from a big disadvantage that their inference speed is very slow due to the time-consuming gradient calculation through back-propagation.

In this paper, we take an alternative perspective and provide a simple fast solution for solving the noisy linear inverse problems with diffusion or flow-based models by proposing a closed-from approximation to the intractable function
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t})}
. Our primary goal is to reduce the inference time of existing methods with minimal degradation, rather than to compete with state-of-the-art performance. The key observation is that, the noise-perturbed likelihood

uninformative
prior
p({\bf{x}}_{0})

{\bf{x}}_{t}
negligibly small. The resultant algorithm is denoted as Diffusion Model based Posterior Sampling (DMPS), one approach that applies to both diffusion and flow-based models. Compared with the seminal DPS and its variants such as PGDM, thanks to the proposed closed-from approximation, no back-propagation through the pre-trained model is needed, thus significantly reducing the inference time. To verify its efficacy, a variety of experiments on different linear inverse problems such as image super-resolution, denoising, deblurring, colorization, are conducted. Remarkably, as shown in Figure

Background

Diffusion models (DM)
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
;
Song et al. 2023
and Flow-based models (such as flow matching, rectified flow)
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Albergo et al. 2023
;
Ma et al. 2024
can be seen as a unified class of probabilistic generative models that learn to turning random noise into data samples
{\bf{x}}_{0}\sim p({\bf{x}}_{0})
. The forward time-dependent process
{\bf{x}}_{0}\to{\bf{x}}_{1}\to\cdots\to{\bf{x}}_{T}
can be described as follows:

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}_{t}=a_{t}{\bf{x}}_{0}+b_{t}{\bf{\epsilon}},

(2)

where
a_{t}
is a decreasing function of
t
,
b_{t}
is an increasing function of
t
, and
{\bf{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bf{0},\bf{I})
is an i.i.d. standard Gaussian noise. Equivalently, the forward process (
2
) is modeled as

\displaystyle p({\bf{x}}_{t}|{\bf{x}}_{0})=\mathcal{N}({\bf{x}}_{t};a_{t}{\bf{x}}_{0},b_{t}^{2}{\bf{I}}).

(3)

Both diffusion models and flow-based models aim to reverse the forward process (
2
) and generate new samples from a distribution that approximates the target data distribution
p({\bf{x}}_{0})
.

Diffusion Models
: Diffusion models reverse the forward process (
2
) by performing a denoising task for each step, i.e., predicting the noise
\epsilon
from
{\bf{x}}_{t}
. In the seminal work of DDPM
Ho et al. 2020
,
a_{t}=\sqrt{{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}},\;b^{2}_{t}=1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}
, where
{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}
,
\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t}
, and
0<\beta_{1}<\beta_{1}<\cdots<\beta_{T}<1

Ho et al. 2020
. Denote
{\rm{s}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf{x}}_{t},t)
as the noise approximator from
{\bf{x}}_{t}
, one can generate samples following the estimated reverse process
Ho et al. 2020
as

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\big({\bf{x}}_{t}-\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}}_{t}}{\rm{s}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf{x}}_{t},t)\big)+{\beta}_{t}{\bf{z}}_{t},

(4)

where
{\bf{z}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bf{0},\bf{I})
is an i.i.d. standard Gaussian noise. Note that in the variant ADM in
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
, the reverse noise variance
\beta_{t}
is learned as
\{\tilde{\sigma}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, which further improves the performances of DDPM.

Diffusion models are also known as score-based generated models since the denoising process is equivalent to approximating the score function
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})

Song and Ermon 2019
;
Song et al. 2020
. For example, for DDPM, there is a one-to-one mapping between
{\rm{s}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf{x}}_{t},t)
and
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}}_{t}}{\rm{s}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf{x}}_{t},t).

(5)

Flow-based Models
: Flow-based models can be viewed as a generalization of diffusion models
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Albergo et al. 2023
;
Ma et al. 2024
, which introduce a probability ODE with a velocity field
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Ma et al. 2024
：

\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t),

(6)

where
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
can be obtained as the conditional expectation
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbb{E}[\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{x}]
. Flow-based models solve the probability ODE (
6
) backwards by learning the velocity field
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
using a neural network
{\rm{v}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf{x}},t)
, and a first-order ODE solver can be realized as follows:

\mathbf{x}_{t-1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-{\rm{v}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf{x}}_{t},t)\Delta_{t},

(7)

where
\Delta_{t}
is the sampling time interval.
Interestingly, the score function
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})
can also be expressed in terms of the velocity field
Ma et al. 2024

\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})=b_{t}^{-1}\frac{a_{t}{\rm{v}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bf{x}}_{t},t)-\dot{a}_{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}}{\dot{a}_{t}b_{t}-a_{t}\dot{b}_{t}}.

(8)

Previous Methods with Diffusion and Flow-based Models
:
The problem of reconstructing
{\bf{x}}_{0}
from noisy
\bf{y}
in (
1
) can be cast as performing
posterior
inference, i.e.,

\displaystyle p({\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{y}})=\frac{p({\bf{x}}_{0})p(\bf{y}|{\bf{x}}_{0})}{p({\bf{y}})},

(9)

where
p({\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{y}})
is the
posterior
distribution.
Ideally, one can directly train diffusion or flow-based models using samples from
p({\bf{x}}|{\bf{y}})
. However, such a supervised approach is neither efficient nor flexible and most previous methods adopt an unsupervised approach
Jalal et al. 2021a
;
Chung et al. 2022a
;
Song et al. 2022
;
Pokle et al. 2023
: given a pre-trained diffusion model or flow-based model, one treats it as an implicit prior
p({\bf{x}}_{0})
and then performs posterior sampling through a reverse sampling process
{\bf{x}}_{T}\to\cdots{\bf{x}}_{t}\to{\bf{x}}_{t-1}\to\cdots\to{\bf{x}}_{0}
. The main challenge is thus how to incorporate information of
\bf{y}
within such reverse sampling process. Interestingly, while diffusion models and flow-based models admit slightly different forms, there exists a principled way thanks to the simple relation from the Bayes’ rule (
9
),

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{x}}_{t}|{\bf{y}})}=\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{x}}_{t})}+\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}}),

(10)

where
p({{\bf{x}}_{t}}|{\bf{y}})
is the score of posterior distribution (we call
posterior score
), which is the sum of the prior score
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{x}}_{t})}
, and the likelihood score
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
. Given a pre-trained diffusion model or flow-based model, the prior score
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{x}}_{t})}
can be readily obtained from the pre-trained model outputs thanks to the intrinsic connections (
5
) (
8
). However, while
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
can be readily obtained from (
1
) when
t=0
, it becomes intractable in the general case for
t>0

Chung et al. 2022a
. To see this, one can equivalently write
{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
as

\displaystyle p({\bf{y}}|{{\bf{x}}}_{t})=\int p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{0})p({\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{x}}_{t})d{\bf{x}}_{0},

(11)

where from the Bayes’ rule,

\displaystyle p({\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{x}}_{t})=\frac{p({\bf{x}}_{t}|{\bf{x}}_{0})p({\bf{x}}_{0})}{\int p({\bf{x}}_{t}|{\bf{x}}_{0})p({\bf{x}}_{0})d{\bf{x}}_{0}}.

(12)

For both diffusion and flow-based models, although the forward transition probability
p({\bf{x}}_{t}|{\bf{x}}_{0})
is exactly known as (
3
), the reverse transition probability
p({\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{x}}_{t})
is difficult to obtain. Consequently, the remaining key challenge is the calculation of the
noise-perturbed likelihood score

\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
. A variety of methods
Jalal et al. 2021a
;
Chung et al. 2022a
;
Song et al. 2022
;
Pokle et al. 2023
have been proposed to approximate
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
(or equivalently
{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
) and most of them build on the seminal work DPS
Chung et al. 2022a
which leverages the Tweedie’s formula to obtain the posterior estimate of
{\bf{x}}_{0}

Robbins 1992
;
Chung et al. 2022a
：

\displaystyle\hat{\bf{x}}_{0}({\bf{x}}_{t}):=\mathbb{E}[{\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{x}}_{t}]=\frac{1}{a_{t}}\left({\bf{x}}_{t}+b_{t}^{2}\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log p_{t}({\bf{x}}_{t})\right),

(13)

where
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log p_{t}({\bf{x}}_{t})
is approximated by the neural network as (
5
) and (
8
) for diffusion and flow-based models, respectively.
In particular, DPS uses a Laplace approximation
p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t})\approx p({\bf{y}}|\hat{\bf{x}}_{0}({\bf{x}}_{t}))=\mathcal{N}({\bf{A}}\hat{\bf{x}}_{0}({\bf{x}}_{t});\sigma_{y}^{2}{\bf{I}})
, while both PGDM
Song et al. 2022
and OT-ODE
Pokle et al. 2023
use an approximation
p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t})\approx\mathcal{N}({\bf{A}}\hat{\bf{x}}_{0}({\bf{x}}_{t});\gamma^{2}_{t}{\bf{A}}{\bf{A}}^{T}+\sigma_{y}^{2}{\bf{I}})
, where
\gamma_{t}
is a hyper-parameter for the variance term. Nevertheless, while DPS and its variants can achieve excellent reconstruction performances, they suffer from a significant drawback: the inference speed is very slow due to the time-consuming gradient of the pre-trained model output w.r.t.
{\bf{x}}_{t}
in calculating the likelihood
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
.

3
Method

In this section, we propose a fast and efficient closed-form solution for the intractable likelihood score
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
, termed as noise-perturbed pseudo-likelihood score. We first derive the results of
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
under the general settings (
2
-
3
), and then apply our results in diffusion and flow-based models, respectively.

3.1
Noise-Perturbed Pseudo-Likelihood Score

To tackle the intractability difficulty of
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
, we introduce a simple approximation under the following assumption:

Assumption 1
.

(uninformative prior)

The prior
p({\bf{x}}_{0})
(
12
) is uninformative (flat) w.r.t.
{\bf{x}}_{t}
so that
p({\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{x}}_{t})\propto p({\bf{x}}_{t}|{\bf{x}}_{0})
, where
\propto
denotes equality up to a constant scaling.

Note that while the uninformative prior assumption appears crude at first sight, it is asymptotically accurate when the perturbed noise in
{\bf{x}}_{t}
becomes negligible, as verified in Appendix
A
.

Under Assumption
1
, we obtain a simple
closed-form
approximation of
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
called noise-perturbed
pseudo-likelihood score
and denote as
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{\tilde{p}}({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
, as shown in Theorem
1
.

Theorem 1
.

(noise-perturbed pseudo-likelihood score for (
2
))

For the general forward process (
2
), under Assumption
1
, the noise-perturbed likelihood score
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})

in (
1

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})\simeq\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{\tilde{p}}({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{t}}{\bf{A}}^{T}{\Big(\sigma_{y}^{2}{\bf{I}}+\frac{b^{2}_{t}}{a^{2}_{t}}{\bf{A}}{\bf{A}}^{T}\Big)^{-1}}\big({\bf{y}}-\frac{1}{a_{t}}{{\bf{A{\bf{x}}}}_{t}}\big).

(14)

\bf{Proof}
.
From Assumption
1

p({\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{x}}_{t})\propto p({\bf{x}}_{t}|{\bf{x}}_{0})

2

2

{\bf{x}}_{0}

\displaystyle p({\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{x}}_{t})\simeq\mathcal{N}({\bf{x}}_{0};\frac{{\bf{x}}_{t}}{a_{t}},\frac{b_{t}^{2}}{a_{t}^{2}}\bf{I}),

(15)

whereby

{\bf{x}}_{0}=\frac{{\bf{x}}_{t}}{a_{t}}+{\frac{b_{t}}{{a_{t}}}}{\bf{w}}
, where
\bf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bf{0,I})
. Thus, from (
1

\displaystyle{\bf{y}}=\frac{{\bf{Ax}}_{t}}{a_{t}}+{\frac{b_{t}}{{a_{t}}}}{\bf{Aw}}+\bf{n}.

(16)

\displaystyle\tilde{p}({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t})=\mathcal{N}({\bf{y}};\frac{{\bf{Ax}}_{t}}{a_{t}},\sigma_{y}^{2}{\bf{I}}+\frac{b^{2}_{t}}{a_{t}^{2}}{{\bf{AA}}^{T}}),

\tilde{p}({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t})

Using (
17

14
),
which completes the proof.
\hfill\blacksquare

As shown in Theorem

Corollary 2
.

(efficient computation via SVD)

For the general forward process (
2

3.2
DMPS: Diffusion Model Based Posterior Sampling

After obtaining the approximate results of the likelihood score function
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})
, we can easily modify the sampling equations of the original diffusion and flow-based models from a unified Bayesian perspective. Here we introduce a simple yet universal procedure demonstrating how we can achieve this for both diffusion and flow-based models.

Step 1
: Reformulate the original sampling equations for unconditional generation in the terms of the prior score
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})
.
This step requires building connections between the generative model (either diffusion or flow-based models) output with the score function
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})
. For example, given the connections (
5
) (
8
), the original sampling equation (
4
) for DDPM and (
7
) for flow-based models can be rewritten using
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})
as follows

\displaystyle\text{DDPM:\;\;}{\bf{x}}_{t-1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\big({\bf{x}}_{t}+({1-\alpha_{t}})\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})\big)+{\beta}_{t}{\bf{z}}_{t},

(20)

\displaystyle\text{Flow-based:\;\;}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}-\big(\frac{\dot{a}_{t}}{a_{t}}{\bf{x}}_{t}+\frac{b_{t}(\dot{a}_{t}b_{t}-a_{t}\dot{b}_{t})}{a_{t}}\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})\big)\Delta_{t},

(21)

\displaystyle\text{DDPM:\;\;}{\bf{x}}_{t-1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\big({\bf{x}}_{t}+({1-\alpha_{t}}){{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})}})\big)}+{\beta}_{t}{\bf{z}}_{t},

(22)

\displaystyle\text{Flow-based:\;\;}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}-\big(\frac{\dot{a}_{t}}{a_{t}}{\bf{x}}_{t}+\frac{b_{t}(\dot{a}_{t}b_{t}-a_{t}\dot{b}_{t})}{a_{t}}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})}})\big)\Delta_{t},

(23)

Step 2
: Replace the prior score
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})
in the sampling equations obtained in Step 1 with the posterior score
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{x}}_{t}|{\bf{y}})}
as (
10
). For example, for DDPM and flow-based models, the corresponding sampling equations (
22
-
21
) become

\displaystyle\text{DDPM:\;\;}{\bf{x}}_{t-1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\big({\bf{x}}_{t}+({1-\alpha_{t}})(\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}}){{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})}})\big)+{\beta}_{t}{\bf{z}}_{t},

(24)

\displaystyle\text{Flow-based:\;\;}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}-\big(\frac{\dot{a}_{t}}{a_{t}}{\bf{x}}_{t}+\frac{b_{t}(\dot{a}_{t}b_{t}-a_{t}\dot{b}_{t})}{a_{t}}(\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}}){{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{p({\bf{y}}|{\bf{x}}_{t}})}})\big)\Delta_{t},

(25)

Step 3
: Replace the prior score
\nabla_{{\bf{x}}_{t}}\log{{{p}}({\bf{x}}_{t}})
back in terms of the generative model outputs in the obtained sampling equations in Step 2. Subsequently, taking into account the additional terms due to the addition of likelihood score, we can easily obtain the final posterior sampling equations.
For example, for DDPM and flow-based models, the corresponding sampling equations (
24
-
25
) finally become

\displaystyle\text{DDPM:\;\;}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}s_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\right)+\beta_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}}_{\text{original sampling equation}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\underbrace{\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{t})}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\text{additional part}}}},

(26)

\displaystyle\text{Flow-based:\;\;}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\Delta_{t}}_{\text{original sampling equation}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\underbrace{\frac{b_{t}(\dot{a}_{t}b_{t}-a_{t}\dot{b}_{t})}{a_{t}}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{t})\Delta_{t}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\text{additional part}}}},

(27)

where the blue part is the addition terms required to incorporate into the original sampling equations to enable posterior sampling from
p({\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{y}})
given
\bf{y}
.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we propose fast and effective closed-form approximation of the intractable noise-perturbed likelihood score, leading to the Diffusion Model based Posterior Sampling (dubbed DMPS).
For both diffusion and flow-based models, we evaluate the effectiveness of DMPS on multiple linear inverse problems including image super-resolution, denoising, deblurring, colorization. Despite its simplicity, DMPS achieves highly competitive or even better reconstruction performances, while its inference time of DMPS is significantly faster.

Limitations
\&
Future Work
: While DMPS apparently reduces the inference time and achieves competitive reconstruction performances, it still suffers several limitations. First, although memory efficient SVD exists for most practical matrices
\bf{A}
of practical interests
Kawar et al. 2022
, the SVD operation in DMPS still has some implementation difficulty for more general matrices
\bf{A}
. Second, it can not be directly applied to the popular latent diffusion models such as stable diffusion
Rombach et al. 2022
, which is widely used due to its efficiency. Addressing these limitations are left as future work.

Appendix A
Verification of Assumption
1

Here we provide a theoretical support of the uninformative prior assumption
1
, or, equivalently, the following Gaussian approximation of the posterior
p(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})\approx\mathcal{N}(\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}}{a_{t}},b_{t}^{2}\bf{I}),

(28)

Throughout the following derivations, we will drop any additive constants in the log (which translate to normalizing factors), and drop all terms of order
O(t)
.

Let us start with the original Bayes’ formula (using the log form):

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})=\log p(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0})+\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),

(29)

where
p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
and
p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
denote the marginal distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, respectively.

Since
p_{t-\Delta t}(\cdot)=p_{t}(\cdot)+\Delta t\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t}(\cdot)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)
for
|t|\ll 1
, there is

\displaystyle\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})=\log p(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0})+\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})+\mathcal{O}(t)-\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}).

(30)

For (
30
), we perform a first order Taylor expansion of
\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
around
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, which yields

\displaystyle\log p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\log p(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0})+\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(t)-\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\log p(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0})+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(t).

(31)

Substituting
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(a_{t}\mathbf{x}_{0},b_{t}^{2})
and completing the squares, we obtain:

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{t}-a_{t}\mathbf{x}_{0}\rVert^{2}}{2b_{t}^{2}}+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{t}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2b_{t}^{2}}\lVert\mathbf{x}_{0}-\bm{\mu}\rVert^{2}+C,

(32)

where
C
is a constant value and the mean value
\bm{\mu}
is:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}}{a_{t}}+\frac{b_{t}^{2}}{a_{t}^{2}}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})

(33)

Therefore, we obtain that the posterior distribution
p(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
can be approximated as a Gaussian

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t})\approx\mathcal{N}(\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}}{a_{t}}+\frac{b_{t}^{2}}{a_{t}^{2}}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t}),b_{t}^{2}\bf{I})

(34)

Comparing eqs. (
28
) and (
34
), we can see that in our result (1), we further ignore the term
\frac{b_{t}^{2}}{a_{t}^{2}}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
in the mean value. This is valid for sufficiently small
t
since the variance
b^{2}_{t}
is sufficiently small following the special design principle in forward diffusion process. For example, for DDPM and flow-based model considered in our manuscript,
b^{2}_{t}=1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}
,
b^{2}_{t}=t^{2}
, respectively.

Reflecting on this derivation, the main idea is that for a sufficiently small
t
, the Bayes’ rule expansion of
p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})
(recall that this is what we need to compute the likelihood score) is primarily influenced by the term
p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})
from the forward process, regardless of the prior of
p(\mathbf{x}_{0})
. As a result, the uninformative prior assumption is reasonable for sufficiently small
t
. In fact, this insight is exactly why in the diffusion models the reverse process and the forward process share the same functional form for sufficiently small time interval. It is worth pointing out that, the validity of the above results does not depend on the underlying distribution
p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, whether it being a simple Gaussian or a complex distribution as that of a face image.

.

A toy example:
We further consider a toy example to illustrate this where the exact form of
p({\bf{x}}_{0}|{\bf{x}}_{t})
in (

) can be computed exactly. Assume that
\bf{x}
reduces to a scalar random variable
x
and the associated prior
p(x)
follows a Gaussian distribution, i.e.,
p(x)=\mathcal{N}(x;0,\sigma^{2}_{0})
, where
\sigma^{2}
is the prior variance. The likelihood
p({\bf{x}}_{t})|{\bf{x}}_{0})
(
3
) in this case is simply
p({{x}}_{t}|{{x}}_{0})=\mathcal{N}({x_{t}};\sqrt{{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}}x_{0},(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}))
.

Then, from (
12
), after some algebra, it can be computed that the posterior distribution
p({{x}}_{0}|{{x}_{t}})
is

\displaystyle p({{x}_{0}}|{{x}_{t}})=\mathcal{N}({x}_{0};m_{\textrm{exact}},v_{\textrm{exact}})

(35)

where

\displaystyle m_{\textrm{exact}}=\frac{\sqrt{{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}}\sigma_{0}^{2}}{(1-{\bar{\alpha}}_{t})+{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}\sigma^{2}_{0}}{x}_{t},\;v_{\textrm{exact}}=\frac{(1-{\bar{\alpha}}_{t})\sigma^{2}_{0}}{(1-{\bar{\alpha}}_{t})+{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}\sigma^{2}_{0}}.

(36)

Under the Assumption
1
, i.e.,
p({{x}_{0}}|{{x}_{t}})\propto p({{x}_{t}}|{{x}_{0}})
, we obtain an approximation of
p({{x}}|{{x}_{t}})
as follows

\displaystyle p({{x}_{0}}|{{x}_{t}})\simeq\tilde{p}({{x}_{0}}|{{x}_{t}})=\mathcal{N}({x}_{0};m_{\textrm{pseudo}},v_{\textrm{pseudo}}),

(37)

where

\displaystyle m_{\textrm{pseudo}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}}}{x}_{t},\;v_{\textrm{pseudo}}=\frac{1-{{\bar{\alpha}}_{t}}}{\bar{\alpha}}.

(38)

By comparing the exact result (
36
) and approximation result (
38
), it can be easily seen that for a fixed
\sigma^{2}_{0}>0
, as
\bar{\alpha}_{t}\to 1
, we have
m_{\textrm{pseudo}}\to m_{\textrm{post}}
and
v_{\textrm{pseudo}}\to v_{\textrm{post}}
, which is exactly the case for DDPM as
t\to 1
. To see this, we anneal
\bar{\alpha}_{t}
as
\bar{\alpha}_{t}={\bar{\alpha}}_{\rm{max}}(\frac{{\bar{\alpha}}_{\rm{min}}}{{\bar{\alpha}}_{\rm{max}}})^{\frac{t-1}{T-1}}
geometrically and compare
m_{\textrm{pseudo}},v_{\textrm{pseudo}}
with
m_{\textrm{exact}},v_{\textrm{exact}}
as
t
increase from
1
to
T
. Assume that
{\bar{\alpha}}_{\rm{min}}=0.01
and
{\bar{\alpha}}_{\rm{min}}=0.99
, and
\sigma_{0}=25,x_{t}=5,T=500
, we obtain the results in Fig.

. It can be seen in Fig.

m_{\textrm{pseudo}},v_{\textrm{pseudo}}

under this toy example.
