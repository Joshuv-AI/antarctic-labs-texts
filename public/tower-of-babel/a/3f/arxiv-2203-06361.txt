Preserving Lagrangian structure in data-driven reduced-order modeling of large-scale dynamical systems
Harsh Sharma, Boris Kramer

Abstract
This work presents a nonintrusive physics-preserving method to learn reduced-order models (ROMs) of Lagrangian systems, which includes nonlinear wave equations. Existing intrusive projection-based model reduction approaches construct structure-preserving Lagrangian ROMs by projecting the Euler-Lagrange equations of the full-order model (FOM) onto a linear subspace. This Galerkin projection step requires complete knowledge about the Lagrangian operators in the FOM and full access to manipulate the computer code. In contrast, the proposed Lagrangian operator inference approach embeds the mechanics into the operator inference framework to develop a data-driven model reduction method that preserves the underlying Lagrangian structure. The proposed approach exploits knowledge of the governing equations (but not their discretization) to define the form and parametrization of a Lagrangian ROM which can then be learned from projected snapshot data. The method does not require access to FOM operators or computer code. The numerical results demonstrate Lagrangian operator inference on an Euler-Bernoulli beam model, the sine-Gordon (nonlinear) wave equation, and a large-scale discretization of a soft robot fishtail with 779 ​ ; ​ 232 779\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}232 degrees of freedom. The learned Lagrangian ROMs generalize well, as they can accurately predict the physical solutions both far outside the training time interval, as well as for unseen initial conditions.

Introduction

In today’s world of ever-increasing computational power, many engineering disciplines and the physical sciences rely on numerical simulations for design and control of complex dynamical systems. Modeling and simulation of dynamical systems using the Lagrangian mechanics framework has become essential in diverse areas such as structural mechanics, aerospace engineering, biomedical engineering, high-energy physics, quantum mechanics, solid-state physics, and soft robotics. Lagrangian systems exhibit physically interpretable quantities such as momentum, energy, or vorticity; the behavior of these quantities in numerical simulation provides an important measure of accuracy of the model.

During the past three decades, significant advances have been made in the field of structure-preserving numerical methods
[
1
,
2
]
for Hamiltonian and Lagrangian systems. In fact, the class of mechanical integrators
[
3
]
has been developed specifically for Lagrangian systems to ensure that the numerical solution captures the underlying physics accurately. However, numerical simulations of large-scale (e.g., with thousands to millions of states) Lagrangian systems using these structure-preserving methods can take days or weeks on standard computing workstations. As a result, there is a great need for computational savings in time-critical applications such as structural design optimization, real-time simulation, control, and uncertainty quantification. This need has produced several different frameworks for faster simulation, and we next review related existing approaches in reduced-order modeling and learning of structured Lagrangian systems.

The equations of motion for Lagrangian systems are given by the Euler-Lagrange equations. Since these equations are second-order differential equations, a straightforward approach is to rewrite the Lagrangian system as a first-order system and then using existing ROM techniques to reduce their dimensionality. However, this approach destroys the Lagrangian structure, see
[
4
]
for an example. Thus, other approaches were developed for this situation. The first approach to deriving ROMs for second-order systems goes back to 1960s where the modal truncation method was extended to the second-order setting
[
5
,
6
]
. The dominant pole algorithms for large-scale second-order systems were developed in
[
7
]
. Moreover, standard model reduction methods based on balanced truncation were extended to linear systems that are second order in time in
[
8
]
and general second-order systems in
[
9
,
10
]
. Recently a variety of data-driven approaches for second-order systems have been developed, e.g. the interpolatory Loewner framework
[
11
,
12
]
and vector fitting
[
13
]
. However, most of these articles focus on frequency-response data and do not derive ROMs from time-domain data.

While the field of symplectic model reduction of Hamiltonian systems has grown considerably in recent years
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
, progress on structure-preserving model reduction of Lagrangian systems has been less rapid. The intrusive model reduction for Lagrangian systems was introduced in
[
4
]
where the authors showed that performing a Galerkin projection on the Euler-Lagrange equations preserves the Lagrangian structure. Building on this idea, the work in
[
19
]
presented an efficient structure-preserving model reduction strategy for nonlinear Lagrangian systems with parameter dependence. The authors applied their method to a geometrically nonlinear parametrized truss structure with 3,000 degrees of freedom (DOFs) in the FOM. These structure-preserving model reduction approaches are intrusive in that they assume full knowledge about governing equations and their space-time discretization, and require access to full model operators in order to derive Lagrangian reduced-order models (ROMs) via intrusive projection. This limits the scope of intrusive approaches, as in many situations, the full model operators are either not accessible or the complexity of the FOM source code makes the process of obtaining the full model operators very labor-intensive. In contrast, we propose a lightweight nonintrusive method to construct a Lagrangian ROM directly from data.

A variety of machine learning papers have developed structure-preserving neural networks for Lagrangian systems by endowing neural networks with
physics-motivated
inductive biases, e.g. Deep Lagrangian Networks (DeLaNs)
[
20
]
, Lagrangian Neural Networks (LNNs)
[
21
]
, Structured Mechanical Models (SMMs)
[
22
]
. Although these structure-preserving learning methods have been applied to various learning and control tasks, a majority of these approaches are only concerned with learning Lagrangian systems when the data is coming from very low-dimensional systems, i.e. 3-4 dimensions.

In another research direction, symbolic regression has been employed to discover governing equations and conservation laws for low-dimensional systems in
[
23
,
24
]
. Sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy)
[
25
]
and its modifications
[
26
,
27
]
have also been developed for discovering conservation laws
[
28
]
, Hamiltonians
[
29
]
, and Lagrangians
[
30
]
from data. These approaches discover governing equations from a dictionary of candidate functions that are chosen either by expert knowledge or through sparse approximation techniques. The number of candidate functions grow factorially with the state dimension, and hence, SINDy-based methods are ill-suited for large-scale dynamical systems unless additional knowledge about low-dimensionality is used.

A promising method for learning interpretable low-dimensional models from high-dimensional data is the operator inference framework
[
31
]
. This nonintrusive method for data-driven model reduction is applicable to FOMs with linear or low-order polynomial nonlinear terms and can be extended to a broader class of dynamical systems with nonpolynomial nonlinear terms via lifting transformations
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
. The approach has also been extended to a gray-box setting in
[
36
]
where knowledge about the nonpolynomial nonlinear terms in analytic form are used to learn ROMs. However, operator inference applied in its standard form to Lagrangian systems does not preserve the underlying geometric structure and therefore produces systems whose energy grows in time (see Section
3.1
), rendering them unphysical. We recently developed a structure-preserving operator inference method, Hamiltonian operator inference, for canonical Hamiltonian systems in
[
37
]
. That method is designed for Hamiltonian FOMs obtained from spatial discretizations of conservative Hamiltonian PDEs. In contrast, the present work focuses on developing a structure-preserving operator inference method for Lagrangian mechanical systems with external nonconservative forcing and spatial discretizations of Lagrangian PDEs. The preservation of the underlying Lagrangian structure in such systems leads to new constraints on the operator inference problem which can not be tackled with the Hamiltonian operator inference method.

The main goal of this work is to develop a structure-preserving model reduction method that can learn Lagrangian ROMs nonintrusively from high-dimensional data. We focus particularly on large-scale models that arise from semi-discretization of partial differential equations (PDEs), which arise for instance in structural dynamics, nonlinear wave equations, and soft robotics applications. We approach this problem by proposing the nonintrusive
Lagrangian operator inference
(
L-OpInf
), a structure-preserving data-driven model reduction method for Lagrangian systems that preserves the underlying geometric structure. The method can work with high-dimensional snapshot data from a Lagrangian system. We first project this data onto a low-dimensional basis as in classical projection-based model reduction and then obtain reduced time-derivative data using an appropriate finite difference scheme. We postulate a form and parametrization of a Lagrangian ROM and then learn the reduced operators from the reduced data using a constrained operator inference procedure. This ensures that the ROMs preserve the Lagrangian structure. The main contributions of this work are:

1.

We develop a nonintrusive physics-preserving method to learn Lagrangian ROMs of large-scale models derived from spatial discretization of Lagrangian PDEs. The proposed method exploits knowledge about the space-time continuous Lagrangian at the PDE level, specifically of the nonlinear potential energy terms, to define and parametrize a Lagrangian ROM form. The ROM can then be learned from trajectory data via a constrained linear least-squares problem;

2.

We present numerical results that demonstrate the learned models’ ability to provide accurate predictions outside the training time interval for the Euler-Bernoulli beam model and the nonlinear sine-Gordon equation. Unlike the structure-preserving Hamiltonian approaches that require both trajectory and momentum data, the presented structure-preserving approach can learn accurate and stable ROMs with bounded energy error from high-dimensional trajectory data alone;

3.

We learn Lagrangian ROMs for a high-dimensional soft-robotic fishtail model with dissipation and time-dependent control input to demonstrate the proposed method’s versatility and robustness to unknown control inputs.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews the basics of Lagrangian FOMs and describes projection-based intrusive structure-preserving model reduction for Lagrangian mechanical systems and nonlinear wave equations. Section
3
presents the proposed structure-preserving operator inference for deriving Lagrangian ROMs. In Section
4
we present numerical experiments where reduced Lagrangian models are inferred from data of conservative and forced Lagrangian systems. In particular, we apply the proposed structure-preserving operator inference method to a linear beam model from structural dynamics, a nonlinear space-discretized sine-Gordon equation, and a fishtail model from soft robotics. Finally, Section
5
summarizes the contributions and suggests future research directions.

2.1
Lagrangian full-order models

We consider a Lagrangian system with a finite-dimensional configuration manifold
\mathsf{Q}
, state space
\mathsf{T}\mathsf{Q}
and a Lagrangian
L:\mathsf{T}\mathsf{Q}\to\real
. For a conservative system Hamilton’s principle (see, e.g.,
[
38
]
) characterizes the solution trajectory
\mathbf{q}(t)
which passes through
\mathbf{q}(t_{0})=\mathbf{q}_{0}
at initial time
t=t_{0}
to reach
\mathbf{q}(t_{K})=\mathbf{q}_{K}
at the final time
t_{K}>t_{0}
as that which satisfies the variational principle

\delta\mathfrak{B}[\mathbf{q}]=\delta\int^{t_{K}}_{t_{0}}L(\mathbf{q}\mathchar 59\relax\dot{\mathbf{q}})\ \text{d}t=0.

(1)

Here,
\mathfrak{B}[\mathbf{q}]
is the action functional and the variation of the action functional
\mathfrak{B}
at a trajectory
\mathbf{q}
for a variation
\delta\mathbf{q}
is defined as

\langle\delta\mathfrak{B}[\mathbf{q}]\mathchar 59\relax\delta\mathbf{q}\rangle=\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\epsilon}\mathfrak{B}[\mathbf{q}^{\epsilon}]\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\mathchar 59\relax

(2)

where
\mathbf{q}^{\epsilon}
is any of the one-parameter families that defines the variation
\delta\mathbf{q}
.
The corresponding Euler-Lagrange equations are

\frac{\partial L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})}{\partial\mathbf{q}}-\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(\frac{\partial L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}}\right)=\mathbf{0}.

(3)

Conservative dynamical systems governed by the Euler-Lagrange equations exhibit important qualitative properties. For autonomous Lagrangian systems the total energy
E(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{q}}-L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})
is conserved along solution trajectories. In addition, by Noether’s theorem
[
39
]
, there exists an invariant of the motion corresponding to each symmetry of the system Lagrangian. The flow map for these Lagrangian systems also preserves the symplectic Lagrangian form.

For Lagrangian systems with external nonconservative forcing
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)
, the Lagrange-d’Alembert principle (see, e.g.,
[
38
]
) seeks
\mathbf{q}(t)
satisfying

\delta\mathfrak{B}(\mathbf{q})=\delta\int^{t_{K}}_{t_{0}}L(\mathbf{q}\mathchar 59\relax\dot{\mathbf{q}})\ \text{d}t+\int^{t_{K}}_{t_{0}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)\cdot\delta\mathbf{q}\ \text{d}t=0\mathchar 59\relax

(4)

which leads to the forced Euler-Lagrange equations

\frac{\partial L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})}{\partial\mathbf{q}}-\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(\frac{\partial L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}}\right)+\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)=\mathbf{0}.

(5)

For this work we focus on two types of Lagrangian FOMs. First, we consider
simple mechanical systems
under Rayleigh viscous damping and external time-dependent forcing in Section
2.1.1
. Second, we consider spatial discretizations of nonlinear Lagrangian PDEs in Section
2.1.2
.

2.1.1
Mechanical systems with nonconservative forcing

Simple mechanical systems are defined in terms of three components: a configuration manifold
\mathsf{Q}
, a Riemannian metric
g(\dot{\mathbf{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})
where
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
belongs to the tangent bundle of
\mathsf{Q}
, i.e.
\mathsf{T}\mathsf{Q}
, and a scalar potential function
U(\mathbf{q})
defined on the configuration manifold
\mathsf{Q}
. See
[
40
]
for more details. In particular, we consider simple mechanical systems with configuration manifold
\mathsf{Q}=\real^{n}
where
n
denotes the large number of degrees of freedom in the FOM. The Lagrangian
L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})=T(\dot{\mathbf{q}})-U(\mathbf{q})
for these systems represents the difference between the kinetic energy
T(\dot{\mathbf{q}})
and potential energy
U(\mathbf{q})
. The notion of Riemannian metric plays a key role in describing the kinetic energy of simple mechanical systems. In geometric terms, a Riemannian metric
g(\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{2})
is a positive-definite, symmetric covariant 2-tensor field on
\mathsf{Q}
, where
\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\in\mathsf{T}\mathsf{Q}
. The kinetic energy
T(\dot{\mathbf{q}})
of simple mechanical systems can be written in terms of the Riemannian metric as

T(\dot{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}g(\dot{\mathbf{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\mathchar 59\relax

(6)

where
\mathbf{M}\in\real^{n\times n}
is the symmetric positive-definite mass matrix, i.e.
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{\top}\succ 0
. The Lagrangian for simple mechanical systems can be expressed as

L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})=T(\dot{\mathbf{q}})-U(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\dot{\mathbf{q}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{q}\mathchar 59\relax

(7)

where
\mathbf{K}
is the stiffness matrix. As a result of structure-preserving space discretization via finite element methods, the stiffness matrix is often symmetric and positive-definite, i.e.
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}^{\top}\succ 0
.
If the system is subjected to a nonconservative external forcing, i.e.,
\mathbf{f}\neq\mathbf{0}
, that force is often modeled via a dissipative force and an external time-dependent input as

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)=-\mathbf{C}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t)\mathchar 59\relax

(8)

where
\mathbf{C}\in\real^{n\times n}
is the damping matrix,
\mathbf{B}\in\real^{n\times m}
is the input matrix, and
\mathbf{u}(t)\in\real^{m}
is the vector of time-dependent control inputs. The dissipative behavior for these mechanical systems is modeled using Rayleigh damping, i.e., the damping matrix
\mathbf{C}
is proportional to the mass and stiffness matrix:

\mathbf{C}=\alpha\mathbf{M}+\beta\mathbf{K}\mathchar 59\relax\quad\quad\alpha\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\beta\in\real^{+}.

(9)

Substituting expressions for the system Lagrangian
L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})
from equation (
7
) and the nonconservative external forcing
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)
from equation (
8
) into the forced Euler-Lagrange equations (
5
) yields the resulting governing equations of motion

\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}(t)+\mathbf{C}\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t)+\mathbf{K}\mathbf{q}(t)=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t).

(10)

The corresponding output equation is given by

\mathbf{y}(t)=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{q}(t)\mathchar 59\relax

(11)

where
\mathbf{y}(t)\in\real^{p}
is the output vector and
\mathbf{E}\in\real^{p\times n}
is the output matrix.

Although the nonconservative external forcing
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)
from equation (
8
) violates the symplectic structure and, in general, breaks the symmetries of the Lagrangian, the variational approach reveals how the external forcing affects the time evolution of these quantities. This plays a crucial role in developing time integrators for Lagrangian FOMs that track the change in energy or some conserved quantity accurately.

2.1.2
Nonlinear wave equations

The governing PDEs for many space-time continuous physical systems can be derived from a space-time continuous Lagrangian using the Euler-Lagrange equations. We consider nonlinear FOMs obtained via structure-preserving spatial discretization of infinite-dimensional nonlinear Lagrangian PDEs. For illustration purposes, we focus on one-dimensional nonlinear wave equations of the type

\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial t^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\text{d}U_{\text{nl}}(q)}{\text{d}q}=0\mathchar 59\relax

(12)

where
x
is the spatial variable and
U_{\text{nl}}(q)
is the nonlinear component of the potential energy. The corresponding space-time continuous Lagrangian is

\mathcal{L}(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}q\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}q_{x}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}q_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}\right)^{2}\right)-U_{\text{nl}}(q).

(13)

This specific form of the space-time continuous Lagrangian covers a number of important nonlinear wave equations found in engineering and science applications, see
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
. Space-discretized nonlinear Lagrangian FOMs are usually derived from the nonlinear wave equation (
12
) by symmetric finite differences or pseudo-spectral methods
[
44
]
. Those methods discretize the spatial dimension in an accurate and structure-preserving way, so that the resulting space-discretized FOM is a finite-dimensional Lagrangian system. One way to do this is to discretize the Lagrangian density directly and then derive Euler-Lagrange equations for the space-discretized Lagrangian. Direct discretization of the space-time continuous Lagrangian (
13
) with
n
equally spaced points leads to

L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left(\frac{\partial q_{i}}{\partial t}\right)^{2}-\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}D_{ik}q_{k}\right)^{2}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{\text{nl}}(q_{i})\mathchar 59\relax

where
\mathbf{q}=[q_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}q_{2}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax q_{n}]^{\top}
with
q_{i}:=q(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}x_{i})
, and the derivative of
q
with respect to
x
is approximated by an appropriate differentiation matrix
\mathbf{D}=(D_{ij})_{i\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}j=1}^{n}
, i.e.,
\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}(x_{i})\approx\sum_{k=1}^{n}D_{ik}q_{k}
. For
\Delta x\to 0
with
n\Delta x=\ell
, the term
L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})\Delta x
converges to
\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathcal{L}(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}q\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}q_{x}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}q_{t})\ \text{d}x
. The Euler-Lagrange equations for the space-discretized Lagrangian are

\ddot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{K}\mathbf{q}+\frac{\text{d}U_{\text{nl}}(\mathbf{q})}{\text{d}\mathbf{q}}

(14)

where the linear Lagrangian FOM operator
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}^{\top}
is always symmetric regardless of the spatial approximation. The nonlinear FOM described by (
14
) conserves the total energy

E(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{q}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{\text{nl}}(q_{i}).

(15)

The solution trajectories of (
14
) also preserve the Lagrangian symplectic form. Additionally, if the system Lagrangian possesses symmetries then the solution trajectories exhibit additional invariants of motion.

2.2
Structure-preserving time integration of Lagrangian FOMs

Variational integrators provide a systematic way of deriving structure-preserving numerical integrators for Lagrangian systems. We closely follow
[
45
]
to give a brief review of the construction of variational integrators for Lagrangian systems with nonconservative external forcing. These time integrators are based on a discrete version of Lagrange-d’Alembert principle where the basic idea is to first construct discrete approximations of both the action integral and virtual work terms in (
4
) and then use concepts from discrete mechanics to derive variational integrators.

For a fixed time step
\Delta t
, the discrete trajectory
\{\mathbf{q}_{k}\}^{k=K}_{k=0}
is defined by the configuration of the Lagrangian system at the discrete time values
\{t_{k}=k\Delta t\}^{k=K}_{k=0}
. We introduce the discrete Lagrangian function
L_{\text{d}}(\mathbf{q}_{k}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k+1})
along with discrete forcing terms
\mathbf{f}^{+}
and
\mathbf{f}^{-}
, which approximate the action integral and virtual work terms between
t_{k}
and
t_{k+1}
in the following sense

\displaystyle L_{\text{d}}(\mathbf{q}_{k}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k+1})

\displaystyle\approx\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})\text{d}t\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{+}_{\text{d}}(\mathbf{q}_{k}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k+1})\cdot\delta\mathbf{q}_{k+1}+\mathbf{f}^{-}_{\text{d}}(\mathbf{q}_{k}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k+1})\cdot\delta\mathbf{q}_{k}

\displaystyle\approx\int^{t_{k+1}}_{t_{k}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)\cdot\delta\mathbf{q}\ \text{d}t.

Using these discrete approximations of integral terms in (
4
), we consider a discrete version of the Lagrange-d’Alembert principle that seeks
\{\mathbf{q}_{k}\}^{k=K}_{k=0}
that satisfy

\delta\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}L_{\text{d}}(\mathbf{q}_{k}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k+1})+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}[\mathbf{f}_{\text{d}}^{+}(\mathbf{q}_{k}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k+1})\cdot\delta\mathbf{q}_{k+1}+\mathbf{f}_{\text{d}}^{-}(\mathbf{q}_{k}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k+1})\cdot\delta\mathbf{q}_{k}]=0\mathchar 59\relax

(16)

which yields the following discrete forced Euler-Lagrange equations

\frac{\partial L_{\text{d}}(\mathbf{q}_{k-1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k})}{\partial\mathbf{q}_{k}}+\frac{\partial L_{\text{d}}(\mathbf{q}_{k}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k+1})}{\partial\mathbf{q}_{k}}+\mathbf{f}_{\text{d}}^{+}(\mathbf{q}_{k-1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k})+\mathbf{f}_{\text{d}}^{-}(\mathbf{q}_{k}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{q}_{k+1})=\mathbf{0}\quad k=1\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}...\mathchar 59\relax K-1.

(17)

For conservative Lagrangian systems with
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{0}
in (
8
), these variational integrators are automatically symplectic and exhibit bounded energy error for exponentially long times. For Lagrangian systems with nonconservative external forcing, these integrators have been shown to track the change in energy accurately
[
46
,
47
]
. In addition to their excellent energy behavior, variational integrators also conserve invariants of the dynamics associated with the symmetries of the FOM Lagrangian via a discrete version of Noether’s theorem
[
45
]
.

The conservative Lagrangian FOMs and ROMs for the linear Euler-Bernoulli beam example in Section
4.2
and the nonlinear sine-Gordon equation in Section
4.3
are numerically integrated using a variational integrator based on the midpoint rule. For the nonconservative soft-robotic fishtail example in Section
4.4
, we use the Newmark integrator with
\gamma=0.5
for all FOMs and ROMs. The Newmark integrator is a variational integrator for Lagrangian systems which preserves the symplectic structure and exhibits bounded energy error for both FOM and ROM simulations. Details about the geometric properties of this variational integrator can be found in
[
46
]
.

2.3
Projection-based intrusive structure-preserving model reduction of Lagrangian FOMs

The proposed data-driven Lagrangian-preserving model reduction method is strongly motivated by the intrusive projection-based structure-preserving model reduction of Lagrangian FOMs
[
4
,
19
]
which we briefly review in this section.
Galerkin projection-based methods first replace the FOM configuration space
\mathsf{Q}
by a reduced-order configuration space
\mathsf{Q}_{r}
, define an intrusive reduced Lagrangian
\tilde{L}
on the reduced state space
\mathsf{T}\mathsf{Q}_{r}
, and then derive governing equations for the Lagrangian ROM using a set of reduced-dimension coordinates. The state is approximated via
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}
where
\mathbf{V}_{r}=[\mathbf{v}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{v}_{r}]\in\real^{n\times r}
is an orthogonal basis matrix whose columns span an
r
-dimensional reduced-order configuration space
\mathsf{Q}_{r}
. Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)
[
48
]
computes the basis matrix
\mathbf{V}_{r}
from the snapshot matrix
\mathbf{Q}=[\mathbf{q}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{q}_{K}]
via singular value decomposition (SVD). The intrusive reduced Lagrangian
\tilde{L}_{r}
is defined as

\tilde{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}):=L(\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}})=T(\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}})-U(\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}).

(18)

The intrusive reduced nonconservative forcing
\mathbf{\tilde{f}}
is

\mathbf{\tilde{f}}(\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t):=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t).

(19)

Following the variational derivation for forced Lagrangian systems outlined in Section
2.1
, the forced Euler-Lagrange equations in
r
dimensions are

\frac{\partial\tilde{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}})}{\partial\mathbf{\tilde{q}}}-\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}})}{\partial\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}}\right)+\mathbf{\tilde{f}}(\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)=\mathbf{0}.

(20)

Since the reduced equations are derived from a Lagrangian system, the resulting reduced equations of motion preserve the underlying Lagrangian structure. In this work, we are mainly interested in two types of Lagrangian systems.

1.

Mechanical systems with external nonconservative forcing.

For the Lagrangian FOMs of the form (
10
), the intrusive reduced Lagrangian
\tilde{L}_{r}
is

\tilde{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}^{\top}(\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{V}_{r})\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}^{\top}(\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{V}_{r})\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathchar 59\relax

(21)

and the intrusive reduced forcing
\mathbf{\tilde{f}}
is

\mathbf{\tilde{f}}(\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}\mathchar 59\relax t)=-(\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{V}_{r})\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}+(\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{u}(t)\mathchar 59\relax

(22)

where the last equality results from the specific form of the external forcing, see (
8
).
Substituting the intrusive reduced Lagrangian
\tilde{L}_{r}
from equation (
21
) and the intrusive reduced forcing
\mathbf{\tilde{f}}
from (
22
) into (
20
) results in the reduced equations of motion

\mathbf{\tilde{M}}\mathbf{\ddot{\tilde{q}}}(t)+\mathbf{\tilde{C}}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}(t)+\mathbf{\tilde{K}}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}(t)=\mathbf{\tilde{B}}\mathbf{u}(t)\mathchar 59\relax

(23)

where
\mathbf{\tilde{M}}:=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{V}_{r}
,
\mathbf{\tilde{C}}:=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{V}_{r}
,
\mathbf{\tilde{K}}:=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{V}_{r}
, and
\mathbf{\tilde{B}}:=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{B}
are the intrusive reduced operators. It is clear from these expressions for the intrusive reduced operators
\mathbf{\tilde{M}}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{\tilde{C}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{K}}
that they are all symmetric and positive-definite matrices. The corresponding output equation is

\mathbf{y}(t)=\mathbf{\tilde{E}}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}(t)\mathchar 59\relax

(24)

where
\mathbf{\tilde{E}}=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{V}_{r}
is the reduced output operator.

2.

Nonlinear wave equations.
For the nonlinear Lagrangian FOMs of the form (
14
), the intrusive reduced Lagrangian
\tilde{L}
is

\tilde{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\tilde{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}^{\top}\mathbf{\dot{\tilde{q}}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}^{\top}(\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{V}_{r})\mathbf{\tilde{q}}-U_{\text{nl}}(\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}).

(25)

Substituting the intrusive reduced Lagrangian
\tilde{L}_{r}
from equation (
25
) into (
20
) gives us the reduced equations of motion

\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{q}}}(t)=\mathbf{\tilde{K}}\mathbf{\hat{q}}(t)+\frac{\text{d}U_{\text{nl}}(\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\tilde{q}})}{\text{d}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}}\mathchar 59\relax

(26)

where
\mathbf{\tilde{K}}:=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{V}_{r}
is the intrusive reduced operator that retains the symmetric property of the structure-preserving spatial discretizations.

It was shown in
[
4
]
that Galerkin projection carried out on the forced Euler-Lagrange equations (
10
) lead to the same reduced equations, and hence, preserve the underlying Lagrangian structure.

3.2
Lagrangian operator inference

In this work, our goal is to learn a Lagrangian ROM for FOMs of the form (
10
) and (
14
), where the trajectories and outputs can be computed, but where the FOM operators are unavailable. We consider the situation that we have knowledge about the governing equations at the PDE level and we have simulated data from a convergent FOM time integrator. The proposed physics-preserving inference-based approach learns a Lagrangian ROM from data of a large-scale mechanical system with nonconservative external forcing or a nonlinear wave equation so that the learned ROM:

1.

is a Lagrangian system;

2.

retains the physical interpretation of state variables;

3.

preserves the Riemannian metric for large-scale mechanical systems;

4.

respects the symmetric property of system matrices.

3.2.1
Lagrangian operator inference for mechanical systems with nonconservative forcing

Next, we introduce a novel
L-OpInf
framework for Lagrangian FOMs described in
2.1.1
. Given known control inputs
\mathbf{u}(t)
and initial conditions
(\mathbf{q}(0)\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{q}}(0))
, let
\mathbf{q}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{q}_{K}
be the solutions to the Lagrangian FOM (
10
) at
t_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax t_{K}
computed using a variational integrator. Let
\mathbf{y}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{y}_{K}
be the corresponding outputs at those time instances. We collect this data in the snapshot matrices

\mathbf{Q}=[\mathbf{q}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{q}_{K}]\in\real^{n\times K}\mathchar 59\relax\quad\quad\mathbf{Y}=[\mathbf{y}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{y}_{K}]\in\real^{p\times K}.

(28)

We also define the input snapshot matrix

\mathbf{U}=[\mathbf{u}(t_{1})\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\cdots\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{u}(t_{K})]\in\real^{m\times K}.

(29)

Given these snapshot matrices of Lagrangian FOM state and output trajectories along with the input snapshot matrix, our goal is to learn a Lagrangian ROM directly from the data. To learn the reduced operators, we first prepare the data, i.e., we project FOM trajectories onto low-dimensional subspaces of the high-dimensional state spaces and compute the reduced time-derivative data. We then propose to fit operators to the projected trajectories in a structure-preserving way.

As a projection bases, we use the POD basis, which can be computed via the SVD of the snapshot data matrix
\mathbf{Q}
. We compute

\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{V}\mbox{\boldmath$\Xi$}\mathbf{W}^{\top}

(30)

where
\mathbf{V}\in\real^{n\times n}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\Xi$}\in\real^{n\times n}
, and
\mathbf{W}\in\real^{K\times n}
, and we assume that the singular values
\xi_{1}\geq\xi_{2}\geq\cdots
in
\Xi
are ordered from largest to smallest.
Then POD basis matrix is
\mathbf{V}_{r}\in\real^{n\times r}
, the leading
r
columns of
\mathbf{V}
. The projected state
\mathbf{\hat{q}}(t)\in\real^{r}
of the FOM state
\mathbf{q}(t)\in\real^{n}
is
\mathbf{\hat{q}}(t)=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{q}(t)
. Using this relation, we obtain reduced snapshot data via the projections onto the POD basis matrix
\mathbf{V}_{r}
as

\mathbf{\hat{Q}}=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=[\mathbf{\hat{q}}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{\hat{q}}_{K}]\in\real^{r\times K}.

(31)

We also compute
\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}
and
\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{q}}}
from the reduced trajectory data
\mathbf{\hat{q}}
using a finite difference scheme, e.g., via the eighth-order central finite difference scheme

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{k}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{4(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k+1}-\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k-1})}{5\Delta t}-\frac{(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k+2}-\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k-2})}{5\Delta t}+\frac{4(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k+3}-\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k-3})}{105\Delta t}-\frac{(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k+4}-\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k-4})}{280\Delta t}\mathchar 59\relax

(32)

\displaystyle\ddot{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}_{k}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{205\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k}}{72\Delta t^{2}}+\frac{8(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k+1}+\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k-1})}{5\Delta t^{2}}-\frac{(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k+2}+\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k-2})}{5\Delta t^{2}}+\frac{8(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k+3}+\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k-3})}{315\Delta t^{2}}-\frac{(\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k+4}+\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{k-4})}{560\Delta t^{2}}.

(33)

We derive ROMs for the Euler-Bernoulli beam example using reduced time-derivative data obtained via finite difference schemes of increasing accuracy and observe that the learned ROM accuracy improves marginally with higher accuracy in second-order time derivatives. Based on the results from this study (not shown here), we use the eighth-order central finite difference scheme to compute the reduced time-derivative data. These time-derivative approximations are used to build the snapshot matrices of the reduced first-order and second-order time-derivative data

\mathbf{\dot{\hat{Q}}}=[\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}_{K}]\in\real^{r\times K}\mathchar 59\relax\quad\quad\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{Q}}}=[\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{q}}}_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{q}}}_{K}]\in\real^{r\times K}.

(34)

We postulate the form of the reduced Lagrangian (motivated by (
21
)) as

\hat{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\hat{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}^{\top}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\hat{q}}^{\top}\mathbf{\hat{K}}\mathbf{\hat{q}}\mathchar 59\relax

(35)

where
\mathbf{\hat{K}}\in\real^{r\times r}
is the symmetric positive-definite reduced stiffness matrix that is learned from data. We postulate the form of the nonintrusive reduced forcing
\mathbf{\hat{f}}
based on (
8
) and (
22
) as

\mathbf{\hat{f}}(\mathbf{\hat{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)=-\mathbf{\hat{C}}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}+\mathbf{\hat{B}}\mathbf{u}(t)\mathchar 59\relax

(36)

where
\mathbf{\hat{C}}\in\real^{r\times r}
is the symmetric positive-definite reduced damping matrix that is learned from data and
\mathbf{\hat{B}}\in\real^{r\times m}
is the reduced input matrix that is learned from data. Based on the assumed model form for
\hat{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\hat{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}})
and nonintrusive reduced forcing
\mathbf{\hat{f}}
, we derive the governing equations for the reduced system via the forced Euler-Lagrange equations (
5
) and obtain

\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{q}}}(t)+\mathbf{\hat{C}}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}(t)+\mathbf{\hat{K}}\mathbf{\hat{q}}(t)=\mathbf{\hat{B}}\mathbf{u}(t)\mathchar 59\relax

(37)

along with the reduced output equation

\mathbf{y}(t)=\mathbf{\hat{E}}\mathbf{\hat{q}}(t)\mathchar 59\relax

(38)

where
\mathbf{\hat{E}}\in\real^{p\times r}
is the reduced output operator that is learned from data.

The Lagrangian ROM form leads us to propose the following optimization problems to compute
\mathbf{\hat{C}}\in\real^{r\times r}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\hat{K}}\in\real^{r\times r}
, and
\mathbf{\hat{B}}\in\real^{r\times m}
:

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{\hat{K}}=\mathbf{\hat{K}}^{\top}\succ 0\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{\hat{C}}=\mathbf{\hat{C}}^{\top}\succ 0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\\
\mathbf{\hat{B}}\end{subarray}}\lVert\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{Q}}}+\mathbf{\hat{C}}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{Q}}}+\mathbf{\hat{K}}\mathbf{\hat{Q}}-\mathbf{\hat{B}}\mathbf{U}\rVert_{F}.

(39)

The symmetric positive-definite constraints on
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{C}}
ensure that the learned reduced operators respect the underlying geometric structure of FOMs, and hence, the ROMs learned via
L-OpInf
are Lagrangian systems. The constrained optimization problem (
39
) has a unique minimizer if and only if
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}
,
\mathbf{\dot{\hat{Q}}}
, and
\mathbf{U}
have full column rank; see
[
50
]
for more details about linear least-squares problems with symmetric positive definite constraints.

For the inference of the reduced output operator
\mathbf{\hat{E}}\in\real^{p\times r}
we solve the least-squares problem

\min_{\mathbf{\hat{E}}}\lVert\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{\hat{E}}\mathbf{\hat{Q}}\rVert_{F}.

(40)

Although the
L-OpInf
framework for mechanical systems is strongly motivated by analogies to the intrusive projection-based approach described in Section
2.3
, the nonintrusive ROM model form (
37
) differs from (
23
) in the following sense. The proposed nonintrusive Lagrangian (
35
) can be interpreted as a special case of the more general nonintrusive Lagrangian
\hat{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\hat{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}^{\top}\mathbf{\hat{M}}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\hat{q}}^{\top}\mathbf{\hat{K}}\mathbf{\hat{q}}
with
\mathbf{\hat{M}}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
. While this specific choice for the reduced mass matrix restricts the search of reduced Lagrangian operators to a subset of the full solution space, it plays a key role in making the constrained optimization problem for inferring the reduced Lagrangian operators tractable. We emphasize that even though we search for models with reduced mass matrix
\mathbf{\hat{M}}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
, the nonintrusive ROM model form (
37
) is not equivalent to premultiplying (
23
) by
\mathbf{\hat{M}}^{-1}
. The specific choice of
\mathbf{\hat{M}}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
ensures that the ROM model form retains all the relevant geometric properties and the Lagrangian structure whereas premultiplying (
23
) by
\mathbf{\hat{M}}^{-1}
loses the symmetric property of the reduced stiffness matrix
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
and reduced damping matrix
\mathbf{\hat{C}}
and therefore violates the Lagrangian structure.

3.2.2
Lagrangian Operator Inference for Nonlinear Wave Equations

We use knowledge about the nonlinear potential energy
U_{\text{nl}}
at the PDE level to develop a gray-box approach to derive nonintrusive Lagrangian ROMs for spatial discretizations of nonlinear wave equations. For the nonlinear Lagrangian FOMs discussed in Section
2.1.2
, the nonlinear potential energy term
U_{\text{nl}}(q)
in (
13
) is assumed to be given explicitly, whereas the quadratic terms in (
13
) and details about their structure-preserving spatial discretization are unavailable. We define the nonlinear forcing

\mathbf{f}_{\text{nl}}(\mathbf{q})=\left[\frac{\text{d}U_{\text{nl}}}{\text{d}q}(q_{1})\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\frac{\text{d}U_{\text{nl}}}{\text{d}q}(q_{n})\right]^{\top}\in\real^{n}.

(41)

We build the nonlinear forcing snapshot data matrix

\mathbf{F}_{\text{nl}}=\left[\mathbf{f}_{\text{nl}}(\mathbf{q}_{1})\mathchar 59\relax\cdots\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{f}_{\text{nl}}(\mathbf{q}_{K})\right]\in\real^{n\times K}\mathchar 59\relax

(42)

where we compute
\mathbf{f}_{\text{nl}}
in (
41
) at different time instances using the FOM snapshot data. We compute the POD basis
\mathbf{V}_{r}\in\real^{n\times r}
via the SVD of the augmented snapshot data matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{\text{aug}}=[\mathbf{Q}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{F}_{\text{nl}}]\in\real^{n\times 2K}
. We obtain projections of the snapshot data
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{F}_{\text{nl}}
as

\mathbf{\hat{Q}}=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\in\real^{r\times K}\mathchar 59\relax\quad\quad\mathbf{\hat{F}}_{\text{nl}}=\mathbf{V}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{\text{nl}}\in\real^{r\times K}.

(43)

For the nonlinear Lagrangian FOMs discussed in Section
2.1.2
, we postulate the form of the reduced Lagrangian

\hat{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\hat{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}^{\top}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\hat{q}}^{\top}\mathbf{\hat{K}}\mathbf{\hat{q}}-\hat{U}_{\text{nl}}(\mathbf{\hat{q}})\mathchar 59\relax

(44)

where
\mathbf{\hat{K}}\in\real^{r\times r}
is the symmetric reduced operator that is learned from data and
\hat{U}_{\text{nl}}(\mathbf{\hat{q}}):=U_{\text{nl}}(\mathbf{V}_{r}\mathbf{\hat{q}})
is the reduced nonlinear potential energy. Based on the assumed model form for
\hat{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\hat{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}})
in (
44
), we derive the governing equations for the nonlinear reduced system via the Euler-Lagrange equations (
3
) and obtain

\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{q}}}(t)=\mathbf{\hat{K}}\mathbf{\hat{q}}(t)+\frac{\text{d}\hat{U}_{\text{nl}}(\mathbf{\hat{q}})}{\text{d}\mathbf{\hat{q}}}.

(45)

We solve the following constrained optimization problem

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{\hat{K}}=\mathbf{\hat{K}}^{\top}\end{subarray}}\lVert\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{Q}}}-\mathbf{\hat{F}}_{\text{nl}}-\mathbf{\hat{K}}\mathbf{\hat{Q}}\rVert_{F}.

(46)

to infer the symmetric ROM operator
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
. We impose a symmetry constraint on
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
to ensure that the learned ROM operator retains the symmetric property of the linear FOM operator introduced during the structure-preserving spatial discretization, see Section
2.1.2
. The symmetric linear least-squares problem (
46
) has a unique solution if and only if the reduced snapshot data matrix
\mathbf{\hat{Q}}
has full column rank.

Remark 1
.

The space-discretized FOM for nonlinear wave equations (
14
) can also be written in the Hamiltonian form where the governing equations are a set of
2n
coupled first-order ODEs. The Hamiltonian operator inference method
[
37
]
for learning Hamiltonian ROMs requires both trajectory and momentum data whereas the proposed Lagrangian operator inference approach has the advantage that it learns Lagrangian ROMs purely from trajectory data.

3.3.2
Practical considerations for solving the constrained optimization problems

For a nonlinear wave equation with FOM equations described by (
14
) the proposed inference method requires solving the symmetric linear least-squares problem (
46
). Since CVX supports declaration of symmetric matrix variables, we declare
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
as a symmetric matrix and then solve for
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
. For a mechanical system with FOM equations described by (
10
) the proposed inference method requires solving the constrained linear least-squares problem (
39
). To ensure that the reduced operators satisfy the hard constraints required for preserving the Lagrangian structure we utilize the semidefinite programming (SDP) mode provided by CVX. Similar to the nonlinear wave equation problem, we first declare both
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{C}}
as symmetric matrices. In floating point arithmetic, the difference between a positive-definite and positive-semidefinite matrices become blurred due to rounding issues, so we impose the following constraints in SDP mode

\mathbf{\hat{K}}-\epsilon_{\texttt{tol}}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{r}\succeq 0\mathchar 59\relax\quad\quad\mathbf{\hat{C}}-\epsilon_{\texttt{tol}}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{r}\succeq 0\mathchar 59\relax

(47)

to ensure that the smallest allowed eigenvalue for both
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{C}}
is
\epsilon_{\texttt{tol}}
.

We make two additional remarks on practical aspects of solving the constrained optimization problem in
L-OpInf
. First, the CVX solver performance may depend on scaling of the input data. Input data that spans many orders of magnitude is often seen in multiphysics models where the FOM state vector contains different physical quantities with different scale of magnitudes
[
32
,
53
,
54
]
. Thus, it is important to exploit any available information about the FOM system to improve the numerical scaling of the snapshot data
\mathbf{Q}
. Second, in practice, CVX is not guaranteed to yield reduced operators that satisfy the positive-definite constraint (
47
), and in some cases, CVX gives solution status as ‘
Inaccurate/Solved
’. This indicates that the CVX solver was unable to find a solution within the default numerical tolerance. However, the CVX solver still returns inferred reduced operators that satisfy a relaxed tolerance value instead, and these reduced operators may still be useful as ROMs. However, extra care is needed in these situations, e.g., monitoring the eigenvalues of the inferred reduced operators
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{C}}
can help in detecting if the hard constraints are satisfied or not. In the numerical examples below we always ensure that the hard constraints are satisfied by testing the validity of the inferred ROM operators before using them for simulating the ROMs.

3.3.3
Alternate constrained optimization problems and their challenges

For a more general reduced Lagrangian model form
\hat{L}_{r}(\mathbf{\hat{q}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}^{\top}\mathbf{\hat{M}}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\hat{q}}^{\top}\mathbf{\hat{K}}\mathbf{\hat{q}}
, the Lagrangian ROM dynamics are

\mathbf{\hat{M}}\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{q}}}+\mathbf{\hat{C}}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{q}}}+\mathbf{\hat{K}}\mathbf{\hat{q}}=\mathbf{\hat{B}}\mathbf{u}\mathchar 59\relax

(48)

where
\mathbf{\hat{M}}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{\hat{C}}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}
and
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
are symmetric and positive-definite matrices. The corresponding constrained operator inference problem

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{\hat{M}}=\mathbf{\hat{M}}^{\top}\succ 0\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{\hat{K}}=\mathbf{\hat{K}}^{\top}\succ 0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\\
\mathbf{\hat{C}}=\mathbf{\hat{C}}^{\top}\succ 0\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{B}\end{subarray}}\lVert\mathbf{\hat{M}}\mathbf{\ddot{\hat{Q}}}+\mathbf{\hat{C}}\mathbf{\dot{\hat{Q}}}+\mathbf{\hat{K}}\mathbf{\hat{Q}}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}\rVert_{F}\mathchar 59\relax

(49)

is, however, challenging to solve. In all of the numerical experiments, we observe that the CVX solver fails to find a reduced mass matrix
\mathbf{\hat{M}}
that satisfies the positive definite constraint. For low-dimensional ROMs with
r<8
, the CVX solver often gives solution status as ‘
Infeasible
’ or ‘
Inaccurate/Infeasible
’. For ROMs of size
r>10
, we observe that the CVX solver fails to make sufficient progress towards a solution, even to within the “relaxed” tolerance setting. To overcome this problem, we simplify the optimization problem by restricting the solution space of (
49
) to reduced mass matrix
\mathbf{\hat{M}}=\mathbf{I}_{r}
which still leads to a structure-preserving Lagrangian ROM that is inferred by fitting a Lagrangian ROM of the form (
37
) to the projections of the FOM data from (
10
).

One way to circumvent the numerical challenges associated with solving (
49
) is to premultiply the ROM form in (
48
) by
\mathbf{\hat{M}}^{-1}
and then solve for
\mathbf{\hat{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{K}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{C}}
. However, this premultiplication by
\mathbf{\hat{M}}^{-1}
destroys the Lagrangian structure as we do not have a unique way of recovering
\mathbf{\hat{M}}^{-1}
,
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
, and
\mathbf{\hat{C}}
from
\mathbf{\hat{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{K}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{C}}
. Another approach to tackling (
49
) is to solve it iteratively by breaking it into two constrained optimization problems. The idea is to start with an initial guess for
\mathbf{\hat{M}}=\mathbf{\hat{M}}_{0}
and solving for
\mathbf{\hat{C}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{K}}
followed by solving for
\mathbf{\hat{M}}
. We observe that this iterative approach converges only when we start with an initial guess
\mathbf{\hat{M}}_{0}
close to the intrusive reduced mass matrix. However, such an approach would require access to FOM operators which is not possible in the nonintrusive setting considered herein.

4.2
Transverse vibrations of an Euler-Bernoulli beam

The Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, also known as the classical beam theory, is most commonly used for calculating load-carrying and deflection characteristics of beams in structural and mechanical engineering. This linear beam theory is based on the observation that bending effect plays a key role in modeling transverse vibrations of beams. This theory is a simplification of the linear elasticity theory and ignores the effects of shear deformation and rotary inertia.

4.2.1
PDE formulation

We consider the transverse vibrations of an unforced 1-D linear beam. The general dynamic equation for a conservative Euler-Bernoulli beam is given by

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\left(EI\frac{\partial^{2}w(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)}{\partial x^{2}}\right)=-\mu\frac{\partial^{2}w(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)}{\partial t^{2}}\mathchar 59\relax

where
x\in[0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\ell]
is the spatial variable,
w(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)
is the transverse deflection, the product
EI
is the flexural rigidity, and
\mu
is the mass per unit length. For a homogeneous beam with constant flexural rigidity, the governing PDE for modeling the transverse vibrations is

EI\frac{\partial^{4}w(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)}{\partial x^{4}}=-\mu\frac{\partial^{2}w(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)}{\partial t^{2}}.

(52)

The beam, as shown in Figure
2
, is simply-supported at both ends, i.e.,

w(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)=0\mathchar 59\relax\quad w(\ell\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)=0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\quad\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial x^{2}}(0\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)=0\mathchar 59\relax\quad\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial x^{2}}(\ell\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)=0.

(53)

In this study, we consider the following stationary initial condition

w(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0)=0.001\left(x^{4}-2\ell x^{3}+\ell^{3}x\right)\mathchar 59\relax\quad\quad\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0)=0.

4.2.2
FOM and ROM implementation

We consider a steel beam of length
\ell=1

\mathrm{m}
with mass per unit length
\mu=6.28\times 10^{-3}

\mathrm{kg}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}
and flexural rigidity
EI=9.81\times 10^{-3}

\mathrm{N}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{2}
. Using Hermite shape functions for beam finite elements, the governing PDE is spatially discretized to yield a Lagrangian FOM of the form

\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{ww}&\mathbf{M}_{w\theta}\\
\mathbf{M}_{w\theta}&\mathbf{M}_{\theta\theta}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{M}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\ddot{w}}\\
\boldsymbol{\ddot{\theta}}\end{bmatrix}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}_{ww}&\mathbf{K}_{w\theta}\\
\mathbf{K}_{w\theta}&\mathbf{K}_{\theta\theta}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{K}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}\\
\boldsymbol{\theta}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{0}\mathchar 59\relax

(54)

where
\mathbf{w}\in\real^{n}
is the deflection perpendicular to the beam length and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\real^{n}
is the rotation in the deformation plane. The state vector
\mathbf{q}
for this beam FOM is partitioned as
\mathbf{q}=[\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}]^{\top}
where both
\mathbf{w}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
carry specific physical meaning. The FOM mass and stiffness matrices in (
54
) also possess a block structure that reflects the partitioning of the state vector
\mathbf{q}\in\real^{2n}
.

We choose
n=200
equally spaced grid points leading to a discretized state
\mathbf{q}\in\real^{400}
. The FOM is numerically integrated for total time
T=0.18

\mathrm{s}
using a variational integrator based on the midpoint rule with
\Delta t=10^{-5}

\mathrm{s}
. The resulting time-marching equations require solving a linear system of
2n=400
equations at every time step.

For the projection step in Step 3 of Algorithm
1
, we choose a projection matrix with a block diagonal structure, i.e. we approximate

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}\\
\boldsymbol{\theta}\end{bmatrix}\approx\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}_{w}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{V}_{\theta}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{V}_{2r}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{w}}\\
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}\end{bmatrix}.

(55)

This specific block diagonal structure of the basis matrix
\mathbf{V}_{2r}\in\real^{2n\times 2r}
retains the physical meaning of the
\mathbf{w}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
variables in the reduced dimensions. The same basis matrix is also used for the intrusive projection-based ROMs which we show for comparison of our results.

4.3
Sine-Gordon equation

The sine-Gordon equation is a nonlinear hyperbolic PDE with a nonpolynomial nonlinearity. Its name is a wordplay on its similarity with the well-known Klein-Gordon wave equation. The sine-Gordon equation is a universal model for combining the wave dispersion and the nonlinearity which is a periodic function of the field variable. The sine-Gordon equation is used for modeling nonlinear phenomena in a wide variety of physical applications such as the self-induced transparency in nonlinear optics
[
56
]
, propagation of fluxons in Josephson junctions between superconductors
[
57
]
, relativistic field theory
[
58
]
, hydrodynamics
[
59
]
, and charge-density-wave conductors
[
60
]
.

4.3.1
PDE formulation

We consider the one-dimensional sine-Gordon equation

\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial t^{2}}=\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial x^{2}}-\sin(q)\mathchar 59\relax

(56)

where
t
is the nondimensional time unit and the field variable
q(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}t)
has the meaning of phase in the respective physical setting. This equation can be formulated as a Lagrangian PDE with the following space-time continuous Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}q\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}q_{x}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}q_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}\right)^{2}\right)-(1-\cos(q)).

In this study, we consider periodic boundary conditions with the following initial conditions

q(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0)=0\mathchar 59\relax\quad\quad\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}(x\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}0)=\frac{4}{\cosh(x)}.

4.3.2
FOM and ROM implementation

We study the sine-Gordon equation over
x\in[-L/2\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}L/2]
with
L=40
. The nonlinear PDE is spatially discretized using
n=2\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}000
equally spaced grid points leading to a discretized state
\mathbf{q}\in\real^{2\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}000}
. We discretize the space-time continuous Lagrangian which yields the following space-discretized Lagrangian

L(\mathbf{q}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\dot{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{\text{fd}}\mathbf{q}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\cos(q_{i})\right)\mathchar 59\relax

where
\mathbf{K}_{\text{fd}}
denotes the symmetric finite difference approximation for the spatial derivative
\partial_{xx}
. The resulting Lagrangian FOM is represented by the following second-order nonlinear ODE system

\ddot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{K}_{\text{fd}}\mathbf{q}-\begin{bmatrix}\sin(q_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\sin(q_{n})\end{bmatrix}.

The FOM is numerically integrated until time
T=25
using a variational integrator based on the midpoint rule with
\Delta t=0.005
. The resulting time-marching equations for the FOM require solving a system of
n=2\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}000
coupled nonlinear equations at every time step. The intrusive Lagrangian ROMs are derived by projecting the FOM onto
\mathbf{V}_{r}
( see equation (
26

4.4
Soft-robotic fishtail model

Autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) have become indispensable in a wide range of civilian and military applications. Due to their fast, agile and efficient underwater movement, AUVs mimicking a fish’s swimming behavior have gained increasing interest for underwater surveillance and exploration. Soft-bodied robots designed for these applications utilize an actuation concept called fluid muscle, where the actuation is distributed over the whole fishtail via an array of fluid elastomers. The compliant nature of these soft-bodied robots allows them to achieve continuum motion and perform escape response maneuvers involving rapid body accelerations of very short duration.

The soft-robotic fishtail model considered here is based on the CAD model created in
[
61
]
. More details about the design idea behind this model can be found in
[
62
]
.
The fishtail design considered in this CAD model uses two separate fluid chambers to mimic the natural antagonistic muscle pair interaction. To minimize equipment in the experimental setup, both fluid chambers are pre-pressurized with
u_{\text{pre}}
and a difference pressure
u(t)
is used as the control input. This setup leads to an effective pressure of
u_{\text{pre}}+u(t)
in one chamber and
u_{\text{pre}}-u(t)
in the other.
Figure
13(a)
shows one of the two identical fluid chambers where the main tube at the center along with the side tubes provide a single pressure supply to the chambers. The complete fishtail model shown in Figure
13(b)
is divided into two parts: (i) carbon center beam and (ii) silicone hull. The smooth silicone hull design is generated by three ellipses and a thick carbon beam is placed in the center of the silicone hull to increase stability of the soft-robotic fishtail against torsional moments. The fluid chamber systems are placed on both sides of the carbon center beam.

In
[
63
]
, various frequency-domain based second-order reduced-order modeling methods have been applied to the soft-robotic fishtail model. However, all the ROMs considered therein are intrusive in that they assume access to the FOM operators. In contrast, the proposed approach does not need access to the FOM operators as it learns the reduced operators nonintrusively directly from data. We only require solution of a linear least-squares problem with symmetric positive-definite constraints at the ROM level, whereas
[
63
]
requires the solution of Lyapunov equations at the FOM level.

4.4.1
PDE formulation

The governing PDE for both parts of the soft-robotic fishtail is derived using the theory of linear elastic materials; all equations in this subsection hold for both parts with different material properties. The first-order displacement tensor
\underline{q}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})
and the symmetric second-order strain tensor
\underline{\epsilon}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})
are related by the kinetic equation

\underline{\epsilon}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\underline{q}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})+\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}\underline{q}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})\right)\mathchar 59\relax

where
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}z_{2}\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}z_{3})
is the three-dimensional spatial variable. Using the stress-strain relationship for isotropic elastic materials, the stress tensor
\underline{\penalty\ \sigma}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})
is
given by

\underline{\penalty\ \sigma}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})=\lambda\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\underline{\epsilon}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})\right)\underline{I}+2\mu\underline{\epsilon}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})\mathchar 59\relax

where
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\cdot\right)
is the trace operator,
\underline{I}
is the identity tensor and
\lambda
and
\mu
are the Lamé parameters. The governing PDE can be written in terms of the second time-derivative of the displacement and divergence of the stress tensor

\rho\frac{\partial^{2}\underline{q}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})}{\partial t^{2}}=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\cdot\underline{\penalty\ \sigma}(t\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}\mathbf{z})\mathchar 59\relax

(57)

where
\rho
is the constant density of a material with a homogeneous mass distribution.

4.4.2
FOM implementation

Space discretization of the governing PDE (
57
) using tetrahedral finite elements yields

\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}(t)+\mathbf{K}\mathbf{q}(t)=\mathbf{B}u(t)\mathchar 59\relax

where
\mathbf{M}\mathchar 59\relax\mathbf{K}\in\real^{n\times n}
are symmetric positive-definite matrices,
\mathbf{B}\in\real^{n}
is the input vector, and
u(t)\in\real
in the scalar control input. Since the original mathematical model does not account for any dissipative effects, Rayleigh damping
[
65
]
is introduced in form of a damping matrix
\mathbf{C}\in\real^{n\times n}
to obtain realistic behavior of the soft structure, i.e.,

\mathbf{C}=\alpha\mathbf{M}+\beta\mathbf{K}\in\real^{n\times n}\mathchar 59\relax

(58)

where
\alpha=10^{-4}
and
\beta=2\times 10^{-4}
. The state vector
\mathbf{q}
for the FOM considered in this example has dimension
n=779\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}232
which results from a computational grid of
259\mathord{\mathchar 59\relax}744
points and three spatial DOFs at each grid point. We consider a stationary initial condition, i.e.
\mathbf{q}(0)=\dot{\mathbf{q}}(0)=\mathbf{0}
, as the system is forced for
t>0
.

In order to achieve a fish-like movement, we require control over the displacement of the fish’s fin. Thus, as an output, we are interested in the location (
0

\mathrm{m}
,
0

\mathrm{m}
,
0.21

\mathrm{m}
) which is denoted as the point of interest (POI) and is equal to a single mesh point at the end of the center beam, see Figure
13(b)
. This then defines the output

\mathbf{y}(t)=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{q}(t)\in\real^{3}\mathchar 59\relax

which represents displacement of the rear tip of the carbon center beam in the three spatial directions.

Remark 3
.

The high-dimensional fishtail model reflects a typical gray-box setting where we have knowledge about the Lagrangian nature of the mechanical system, but details about the spatial discretization are unavailable. Unlike the beam example in Section
4.2

Moreover, we do not have have access to the finite element code or meshing data used for generating these system matrices. The system matrices defining the Lagrangian FOM for this example are obtained from

5
Conclusions

We presented a data-driven model reduction method,
Lagrangian operator inference
(
L-OpInf
), that learns Lagrangian ROMs directly from high-dimensional data via nonintrusive structure-preserving operator inference. The data we considered came from large-scale semi-discretized PDE models that are marched forward in time using a time integrator. The presented
L-OpInf
method exploits the underlying geometric structure of Lagrangian systems and applies to mechanical systems with nonconservative forcing and nonlinear wave equations. The inference of the reduced operators is based on a constrained optimization problem that ensures that the reduced models are Lagrangian and also respect the symmetric (positive definite) property of system matrices. For Lagrangian systems that do not fall into the class of Lagrangian FOMs considered in Section
2.1
, our work may be used as a template to define a problem-specific constrained optimization problem that yields Lagrangian ROMs that preserve the structure of the high-dimensional Lagrangian system.
The proposed method only assumes a Lagrangian nature of the large-scale system and does not require access to FOM operators or require information about the spatial discretization used to derive the high-dimensional systems. This setting is common in many applications where the full model is either given as a black box or the source code is very complicated and understanding these implementation details is tedious and time consuming. In those settings, intrusive model reduction may be less appealing to novice users.

The numerical experiments demonstrate the advantages of preserving the underlying geometric structure for both conservative and forced Lagrangian systems. The conservative Euler-Bernoulli beam example shows that the proposed method learns stable ROMs with bounded energy error, while facilitating accurate long-time predictions far outside the training time interval and demonstrating robustness to unseen initial conditions that are outside the training dataset. The nonlinear sine-Gordon wave equation example shows that the proposed method can learn accurate and stable ROMs of semi-discretized nonlinear wave equations purely from the trajectory data. For the high-dimensional soft-robotic fishtail model, the learned Lagrangian ROMs track the change in system energy accurately in the presence of dissipation and time-dependent control input. Notably, the learned Lagrangian ROMs work well even for unknown control inputs. Moreover, the proposed method achieves significant reduction in state dimension, which makes the learned ROMs ideal for real-time control and state estimation.

Future research directions motivated by this work are: combining
L-OpInf
with structure-preserving machine learning methods to learn Lagrangian ROMs of dynamical systems with unknown nonlinear operators; studying connection between reduced operators and predicted energy of the intrusive and nonintrusive ROMs; and applying the proposed method to noisy data coming from experiments instead of the simulated data setting considered in this work.
