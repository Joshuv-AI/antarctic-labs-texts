A note on powerful numbers in short intervals
Tsz Ho Chan

Abstract
In this note, we are interested in obtaining uniform upper bounds for the number of powerful numbers in short intervals ( x , x + y ] (x,x+y] . We obtain unconditional upper bounds O ⁡ ( y log ⁡ y ) O(\frac{y}{\log y}) and O ⁡ ( y 11 / 12 ) O(y^{11/12}) for all powerful numbers and y 1 / 2 y^{1/2} -smooth powerful numbers respectively. Conditional on the a ​ b ​ c abc -conjecture, we prove the bound O ⁡ ( y log 1 + ϵ ⁡ y ) O(\frac{y}{\log^{1+\epsilon}y}) for squarefull numbers and the bound O ⁡ ( y ( 2 + ϵ ) / k ) O(y^{(2+\epsilon)/k}) for k k -full numbers when k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . They are related to Roth’s theorem on arithmetic progressions and the conjecture on non-existence of three consecutive squarefull numbers.



1
Introduction and Main Result

A number
n
is
squarefull
if its prime factorization
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{r}^{a_{r}}
satisfies
a_{i}\geq 2
for all
1\leq i\leq r
. Similarly, a number
n
is
k
-
full
if the exponents
a_{i}\geq k
for all
1\leq i\leq r
. For example,
72=2^{3}\cdot 3^{2}
is squarefull and
243=3^{5}
is
5
-full. Let
Q_{k}(x)
denote the number of
k
-full numbers which are less than or equal to
x
. It is known that

Q_{k}(x)=\prod_{p}\Bigl(1+\sum_{m=k+1}^{2k-1}\frac{1}{p^{m/h}}\Bigr)x^{1/k}+O(x^{1/(k+1)})

(1)

where the product is over all primes (see
[
4
]
or
[
1
]
for example.) There are also interests in counting the number of
k
-full numbers in short intervals
(x,x+y]
with
y=o(x)
. For moderate size
y
, there are some asymptotic results: For example, Trifonov
[
8
]
and Liu
[
6
]
obtained,

Q_{2}\bigl(x+x^{1/2+\theta}\bigr)-Q_{2}(x)\sim\frac{\zeta(3/2)}{2\zeta(3)}x^{\theta}\;\;\text{ for }\;\;19/154=0.12337...<\theta<1/2,

(2)

and

Q_{3}\bigl(x+x^{2/3+\theta}\bigr)-Q_{3}(x)\sim\frac{\zeta(4/3)}{3\zeta(4)}x^{\theta}\;\;\text{ for }\;\;5/42=0.11904...<\theta<1/3

(3)

respectively.

What happens when
y
is very small such as
y\ll x^{1/2}
or even
y\ll\log x
? For such short intervals, one can only expect suitable upper bounds rather than asymptotic formulas. Thus, in this note, we are interested in finding uniform upper bounds for
Q_{k}(x+y)-Q_{k}(x)
with
1\leq y\leq x
that are independent of
x
. By comparing
k
-full numbers with perfect
k
-th powers, we suspect the following to be true.

Conjecture 1

Given an integer
k\geq 2
and a real number
x\geq 1
. There exists some constant
C_{k}\geq 1
such that

Q_{k}(x+y)-Q_{k}(x)\leq C_{k}y^{1/k}

uniformly over
1\leq y\leq x
.

We are far from proving this at the moment. The current best upper bound

Q_{k}(x+y)-Q_{k}(x)\ll\frac{y\log\log(y+2)}{\log(y+2)}

(4)

was obtained by De Koninck, Luca and Shparlinski
[
3
]
. In this note, we improve (
4
) slightly.

Theorem 1

Given an integer
k\geq 2
and a real number
x\geq 1
. We have

Q_{k}(x+y)-Q_{k}(x)\ll\frac{y}{\log(y+1)}

(5)

uniformly over
1\leq y\leq x
.

In fact, we shall prove the following more general result concerning squarefull numbers in arithmetic progressions over short intervals which gives Theorem
1
immediately as
k
-full numbers are included in squarefull numbers.

Theorem 2

Given real numbers
x\geq 1
and
0<\alpha<1
. Suppose
q>0
and
r
are two integers with
\text{gcd}(r,q)=1
. We have

\mathop{\mathop{\sum_{x<n\leq x+y}}_{n\text{ squarefull}}}_{n\equiv r\;(\bmod{q})}1\ll_{\alpha}\frac{y}{\phi(q)\log(y+1)}

uniformly over
1\leq y\leq x
and
1\leq q\leq y^{1-\alpha}
.

Using similar technique, one can obtain some power savings over (
5
) for
smooth

k
-full numbers in short intervals.

Theorem 3

Given an integer
k\geq 2
and a real number
x\geq 1
. We have

\mathop{\mathop{\sum_{x<n\leq x+y}}_{n\text{ $k$-full}}}_{p^{+}(n)\leq y^{1/2}}1\leq\mathop{\mathop{\sum_{x<n\leq x+y}}_{n\text{ squarefull}}}_{p^{+}(n)\leq y^{1/2}}1\ll y^{11/12}.

(6)

uniformly over
1\leq y\leq x
. Here
p^{+}(n)
stands for the largest prime factor of
n
. Remark: One may increase the exponent
1/2
up to
1
and obtain similar power saving upper bound.

The bound (
6
) lends evidence towards Conjecture
1
and shows that the difficulty lies with
non-smooth

k
-full numbers. Another piece of evidence comes from the famous
abc
-conjecture. It was proved in
[
3
]
that, given any
\epsilon>0
, the interval

(x,x+x^{1-(2+\epsilon)/k}]

(7)

contains at most one
k
-full number for sufficiently large
x
under the
abc
-conjecture. From this, one has

Theorem 4

Given an integer
k\geq 3
and real numbers
\epsilon>0
and
x\geq 1
. We have

Q_{k}(x+y)-Q_{k}(x)\ll_{\epsilon}y^{(2+\epsilon)/k}

(8)

uniformly over
1\leq y\leq x
.

Unfortunately, the proof in
[
3
]
concerning (
7
) was a little inaccurate as the
a
,
b
,
c
in its application of the
abc
-conjecture might not be relatively prime. So, we shall give a complete proof of Theorem
4
later. Furthermore, (
7
) gives us nothing when
k=2
. To remedy this, we shall prove the following conditional result which improves on (
5
) slightly by a small power of logarithm.

Theorem 5

The
abc
-conjecture implies that, for some absolute constant
c>0
,

Q_{2}(x+y)-Q_{2}(x)\ll\frac{y}{\log^{1+c}(y+1)}

uniformly over
1\leq y\leq x
.

Its proof relies on the following recent breakthrough result of Bloom and Sisask
[
2
]
on density of integer sequence without three term arithmetic progressions.

Theorem 6
(Bloom-Sisask)

Let
N\geq 2
and
A\subset\{1,2,...,N\}
be a set with no non-trivial three-term arithmetic progressions, i.e. solutions to
x+y=2z
with
x\neq y
. Then

|A|\ll\frac{N}{(\log N)^{1+c}},

where
c>0
is an absolute constant.

This paper is organized as follows. First, we will prove Theorems
2
and
3
using Brun-Titchmarsh inequality and ideas from Shiu’s generalization
[
7
]
. Then we will prove Theorem
4
using the
abc
-conjecture. Finally, we will prove Theorem
5
by establishing non-existence of three term arithmetic progressions for squarefull numbers in short intervals.

Notation.
We use
|A|
to denote the number of elements in a finite set
A
and
\lfloor x\rfloor
to denote the greatest integer less than or equal to
x
. We let
p_{-}(n)
and
p^{+}(n)
be the smallest and the largest prime factor of
n
respectively. The symbols
f(x)=O(g(x))
,
f(x)\ll g(x)
and
g(x)\gg f(x)
are equivalent to
|f(x)|\leq Cg(x)
for some constant
C>0
. Also,
f(x)=O_{\lambda}(g(x))
,
f(x)\ll_{\lambda}g(x)
or
g(x)\gg_{\lambda}f(x)
mean that the implicit constant may depend on
\lambda
. Furthermore,
f(x)=o(g(x))
means
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f(x)/g(x)=0
and
f(x)\sim g(x)
means
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f(x)/g(x)=1
. Finally, the summation symbol
\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\prime}}
signifies that a sum is over squarefull numbers only.

2
Some Preparations

Lemma 1

For any
X\geq 1
,

\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\prime}}_{X<n\leq X^{2}}\frac{1}{n}\ll X^{-1/2}.

Proof: From (
1
), we have
Q_{2}(X)\ll X^{1/2}
. By partial summation, the above sum is

=\int_{X}^{X^{2}}\frac{1}{u}dQ(u)=\frac{Q(X^{2})}{X^{2}}-\frac{Q(X)}{X}+\int_{X}^{X^{2}}\frac{Q(u)}{u^{2}}du\ll\frac{1}{X^{1/2}}+\int_{X}^{X^{2}}\frac{1}{u^{3/2}}du\ll\frac{1}{X^{1/2}}.

Lemma 2
(Brun-Titchmarsh inequality)

Let
q\geq 1
and
r
be integers satisfying
\text{gcd}(r,q)=1
. Suppose
q<y\leq x
and
z\geq 2
. Then

\mathop{\mathop{\sum_{x<n\leq x+y}}_{n\equiv r\;(\bmod{q})}}_{p_{-}(n)>z}1\ll\frac{y}{\phi(q)\log z}+z^{2}.

Remark: The above bound is still true when
y\leq q
or
y<1
since there is at most one term in the sum.

Proof: This follows from Selberg upper bound sieve method (see
[
5
]
, page 104 for example).

Finally, let us recall the
abc
-conjecture. For any nonzero integer
m
, let

\kappa(m):=\prod_{p|m}p

be the kernel of
m
.

Conjecture 2
(
abc
-conjecture)

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\epsilon}>0
such that, for any integers
a,b,c
with
a+b=c
and
\text{gcd}(a,b)=1
, the bound

\max\{|a|,|b|,|c|\}\leq C_{\epsilon}\kappa(abc)^{1+\epsilon}

holds.

3
Proof of Theorem
2

Our proof is inspired by Shiu
[
7
]
on Brun-Titchmarsh theorem for multiplicative functions. We may assume that
y\geq 2^{2/\alpha}
for the theorem is clearly true when
1\leq y<2^{2/\alpha}
by choosing a large enough implicit constant. Recall that
1\leq q\leq y^{1-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
. Let
z=y^{\alpha/2}\geq 2
. Any squarefull number
n
in
[x,x+y]
can be factored as

n=\underbrace{p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{j}^{a_{j}}}_{b_{n}}\underbrace{p_{j+1}^{a_{j+1}}\cdots p_{s}^{a_{s}}}_{d_{n}}\;\;\text{ with }\;\;p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{s}

where
j
is the greatest index such that
p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{j}^{a_{j}}\leq z
. Hence,
b_{n}\leq z<b_{n}p_{j+1}^{a_{j+1}}
. Note that
j
may be
0
(the product is an empty product) if
p_{1}^{a_{1}}>z
. In this case,
b_{n}=1
and
d_{n}=n
. Also, since
n\equiv r\pmod{q}
with
\text{gcd}(r,q)=1
, we must have
\text{gcd}(b_{n},q)=1=\text{gcd}(d_{n},q)
.

Case 1:
b_{n}>z^{1/2}
. As
q\leq y^{1-\alpha}
and
z=y^{\alpha/2}
, the number of such squarefull numbers is bounded by

\mathop{\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\prime}}_{z^{1/2}<b\leq z}}_{\text{gcd}(b,q)=1}\mathop{\mathop{\sum_{x<n\leq x+y}}_{b|n}}_{n\equiv r\;(\bmod{q})}1\leq\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\prime}}_{z^{1/2}<b\leq z}\Bigl(\frac{y/b}{q}+1\Bigr)\ll\frac{y}{qz^{1/4}}+z^{1/2}\ll_{\alpha}\frac{y}{\phi(q)\log y}

(9)

by (
1
) and Lemma
1
.

Case 2:
b_{n}\leq z^{1/2}
and
p_{-}(d_{n})\leq z^{1/2}
. Then
p_{j+1}\leq z^{1/2}
and
p_{j+1}^{a_{j+1}}>z^{1/2}
which implies
p_{j+1}^{-a_{j+1}}\leq\min(z^{-1/2},p_{j+1}^{-2})
as
a_{j+1}\geq 2
. Hence, the sum

\sum_{p_{j+1}\leq z^{1/2}}\frac{1}{p_{j+1}^{a_{j+1}}}\leq\sum_{p_{j+1}\leq z^{1/4}}z^{-1/2}+\sum_{z^{1/4}<p_{j+1}\leq z^{1/2}}\frac{1}{p_{j+1}^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{z^{1/4}}.

Therefore, by replacing
p_{j+1}^{a_{j+1}}
with a generic
p^{a}
, the number of squarefull numbers in this case is bounded by

\mathop{\sum_{p\leq z^{1/2}}}_{\text{gcd}(p,q)=1}\mathop{\mathop{\sum_{x<n\leq x+y}}_{p^{a}|n}}_{n\equiv r\;(\bmod{q})}1\leq\sum_{p\leq z^{1/2}}\Bigl(\frac{y/p^{a}}{q}+1\Bigr)\ll\frac{y}{qz^{1/4}}+z^{1/2}\ll_{\alpha}\frac{y}{\phi(q)\log y}

(10)

since
q\leq y^{1-\alpha}
and
z=y^{\alpha/2}
.

Case 3:
b_{n}\leq z^{1/2}
and
p_{-}(d_{n})>z^{1/2}
. As
q\leq y^{1-\alpha}
and
z=y^{\alpha/2}
, the number of such squarefull numbers is bounded by

\mathop{\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\prime}}_{b\leq z^{1/2}}}_{\text{gcd}(b,q)=1}\mathop{\mathop{\sum_{x/b<n/b\leq(x+y)/b}}_{p_{-}(n/b)>z^{1/2}}}_{(n/b)\equiv r\overline{b}\;(\bmod q)}1\ll\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\prime}}_{b\leq z}\Bigl(\frac{y/b}{\phi(q)\log z}+z\Bigr)\ll\frac{y}{\phi(q)\log z}+z^{3/2}\ll_{\alpha}\frac{y}{\phi(q)\log y}

(11)

by (
1
), Lemma
2
and the convergence of the sum of reciprocal of squarefull numbers (which follows from Lemma
1
for instance). Here
\overline{b}
denotes the multiplicative inverse of
b\;(\bmod{q})
, i.e.
b\overline{b}\equiv 1\pmod{q}
.

Combining (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
), we have Theorem
2
.

4
Proof of Theorem
3

It is very similar to the proof of Theorem
2
. So, we just highlight the necessary adjustments. We set
q=1
and
z=y^{1/3}
. The arguments for Case 1 and Case 2 are exactly the same as (
9
) and (
10
) and we get the bound

\frac{y}{z^{1/4}}+z^{1/2}\ll y^{11/12}.

It remains to deal with Case 3 where
b_{n}\leq z^{1/2}
and
z^{1/2}<p_{-}(d_{n})\leq y^{1/2}
as the squarefull numbers are assumed to be
y^{1/2}
-smooth. Thus, with
p:=p_{-}(d_{n})
and
d_{n}:=p^{2}d
, the number of squarefull numbers in this case is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\prime}}_{b\leq z^{1/2}}\sum_{z^{1/2}<p\leq y^{1/2}}\mathop{\sum_{x/b<p^{2}d\leq(x+y)/b}}_{p_{-}(d)>z^{1/2}}1=

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\prime}}_{b\leq z^{1/2}}\sum_{z^{1/2}<p\leq y^{1/2}}\mathop{\sum_{\frac{x}{bp^{2}}<d\leq\frac{x+y}{bp^{2}}}}_{p_{-}(d)>z^{1/2}}1

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\nolimits^{\prime}}_{b\leq z^{1/2}}\sum_{z^{1/2}<p\leq y^{1/2}}\Bigl(\frac{y/(bp^{2})}{\log z}+z\Bigr)\ll\frac{y}{z^{1/2}\log z}+\frac{z^{5/4}y^{1/2}}{\log y}\ll y^{11/12}

by (
1
), Lemma
2
and the convergence of the sum of reciprocal of squarefull numbers. The above bounds together yield Theorem
3
.

5
Proof of Theorem
4

Given integer
k\geq 3
and small real number
\epsilon>0
. We claim that, for some sufficiently small constant
c_{\epsilon}>0
, the interval

(x,x+c_{\epsilon}x^{1-(2+\epsilon)/k}]

contains at most one
k
-full number for all sufficiently large
x\geq C
(in terms of
\epsilon
and
c_{\epsilon}
). We shall abbreviate
c_{\epsilon}x^{1-(2+\epsilon)/k}
as
z
.

Suppose the contrary that the interval
(x,x+z]
contains two
k
-full numbers
b<c
. Then
c=a+b
for some integer
0<a\leq z
. Let
d=\text{gcd}(a,b)
. Then the integers
a/d
,
b/d
and
c/d
are pairwise relatively prime. Note that
\kappa(n)\leq n^{1/k}
for any
k
-full numbers. Apply the
abc
-conjecture to the equation
a/d+b/d=c/d
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{x}{d}<\frac{c}{d}

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon}\Bigl(\kappa\Bigl(\frac{a}{d}\frac{b}{d}\frac{c}{d}\Bigr)\Bigr)^{1+\epsilon/2}=\Bigl(\kappa\Bigl(\frac{a}{d}\frac{b}{d}\Bigr)\kappa\Bigl(\frac{c}{d}\Bigr)\Bigr)^{1+\epsilon/2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\kappa\Bigl(\frac{ab}{d}\Bigr)\kappa(c)\Bigr)^{1+\epsilon/2}\leq\Bigl(\frac{a^{1/k}b^{1/k}}{d^{1/k}}c^{1/k}\Bigr)^{1+\epsilon/2}\leq\frac{z^{(1+\epsilon/2)/k}(2x)^{2(1+\epsilon/2)/k}}{d^{(1+\epsilon/2)/k}}

as
ab/d=\text{lcm}(a,b)
is still
k
-full,
0<a\leq z
and
b,c\leq 2x
. Since
0<d\leq a\leq z
, the above implies

x^{1-(2+\epsilon)/k}\ll_{\epsilon}z

which contradicts with
z=c_{\epsilon}x^{1-(2+\epsilon)/k}
when
c_{\epsilon}>0
is small enough. Thus, the claim is true.

Clearly, Theorem
4
is true for
1\leq y\leq C
by picking an appropriate implicit constant. Now, for
C\leq y\leq x
, the above claim implies that the interval

(x,x+c_{\epsilon}y^{1-(2+\epsilon)/k}]

contains at most one
k
-full number. By dividing the interval
(x,x+y]
into subintervals of length
c_{\epsilon}y^{1-(2+\epsilon)/k}
, we obtain

Q_{k}(x+y)-Q_{k}(x)\ll\frac{y}{c_{\epsilon}y^{1-(2+\epsilon)/k}}\cdot 1\ll_{\epsilon}y^{\frac{2+\epsilon}{k}}

which gives Theorem
4
.

6
Proof of Theorem
5

First, we suppose
y\leq x^{0.2}
. We claim that there is no non-trivial three term arithmetic progression among the squarefull numbers in the interval
(x,x+y]
under the abc-conjecture. Suppose the contrary. Then we have three squarefull numbers
x<a_{1}^{2}b_{1}^{3}<a_{2}^{2}b_{2}^{3}<a_{3}^{2}b_{3}^{2}\leq x+y
such that

a_{1}^{2}b_{1}^{3}=a_{2}^{2}b_{2}^{3}-d\;\;\text{ and }\;\;a_{3}^{2}b_{3}^{3}=a_{2}^{2}b_{2}^{3}+d

for some positive integer
d
with
2d\leq y
. Multiplying the above two equations, we get

a_{1}^{2}a_{3}^{2}b_{1}^{3}b_{3}^{3}=a_{2}^{4}b_{2}^{6}-d^{2}\;\;\text{ or }\;\;a_{1}^{2}a_{3}^{2}b_{1}^{3}b_{3}^{3}+d^{2}=a_{2}^{4}b_{2}^{6}.

Say
D^{2}=\text{gcd}(a_{2}^{4}b_{2}^{6},d^{2})
as the numbers are perfect squares. Then, the three integers

\frac{a_{1}^{2}a_{3}^{2}b_{1}^{3}b_{3}^{3}}{D^{2}},\;\;\frac{d^{2}}{D^{2}},\;\;\frac{a_{2}^{4}b_{2}^{6}}{D^{2}}

are pairwise relatively prime and we have the equation

\frac{a_{1}^{2}a_{3}^{2}b_{1}^{3}b_{3}^{3}}{D^{2}}+\frac{d^{2}}{D^{2}}=\frac{a_{2}^{4}b_{2}^{6}}{D^{2}}.

Now, by the abc-conjecture, we have

\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}}{D^{2}}\leq\frac{a_{2}^{4}b_{2}^{6}}{D^{2}}

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon}\kappa\Bigl(\frac{a_{1}^{2}a_{3}^{2}b_{1}^{3}b_{3}^{3}}{D^{2}}\frac{d^{2}}{D^{2}}\frac{a_{2}^{4}b_{2}^{6}}{D^{2}}\Bigr)^{1+\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\bigl(a_{1}^{2}a_{3}^{2}b_{1}^{3}b_{3}^{3}\bigr)^{1+\epsilon}\kappa\Bigl(\frac{d^{2}}{D^{2}}\Bigr)^{1+\epsilon}\kappa\bigl(a_{2}^{4}b_{2}^{6}\bigr)^{1+\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq(a_{1}b_{1}a_{2}b_{2}a_{3}b_{3})^{1+\epsilon}\Bigl(\frac{d}{D}\Bigr)^{1+\epsilon}\ll x^{3/2+3\epsilon/2}\frac{y^{1+\epsilon}}{D^{1+\epsilon}}.

Since
1\leq D\leq d\leq y
, this implies
x^{1/2-3\epsilon/2}\ll_{\epsilon}D^{1-\epsilon}y^{1+\epsilon}\ll y^{2}\leq x^{0.4}
which is a contradiction for small enough
\epsilon
, say
\epsilon=0.01
, and sufficiently large
x>C
(in terms of the implicit constant).

Clearly, the theorem is true for
1\leq y\leq C
by picking an appropriate implicit constant. So, we may assume
y>C
. Since arithmetic progressions are invariant under translation, we may shift the interval
(x,x+y]
to
(0,y]
. Therefore, by Theorem
6
, we have

Q_{2}(x+y)-Q_{2}(x)\ll\frac{y}{\log^{1+c}y}

which gives the theorem.

Now, if
y>x^{0.2}
, one can simply divide the interval
(x,x+y]
into subintervals of length
x^{0.2}
:

(x,x+x^{0.2}]\cup(x+x^{0.2},x+2x^{0.2}]\cup\cdots\cup\Bigl(x+\Bigl\lfloor\frac{y}{x^{0.2}}\Bigr\rfloor x^{0.2},x+\Bigl(\Bigl\lfloor\frac{y}{x^{0.2}}\Bigr\rfloor+1\Bigr)x^{0.2}\Bigr]

Then, over each interval
(x+ix^{0.2},x+(i+1)x^{0.2}]
, we have the bound

Q_{2}(x+(i+1)x^{0.2})-Q_{2}(x+ix^{0.2})\ll\frac{x^{0.2}}{\log^{1+c}x}.

Summing over
\lfloor\frac{y}{x^{0.2}}\rfloor+1
of these intervals, we have

Q_{2}(x+y)-Q_{2}(x)\ll\frac{y}{x^{0.2}}\cdot\frac{x^{0.2}}{\log^{1+c}x}\ll\frac{y}{\log^{1+c}y}

which gives the theorem as well.
