Preponderant Orbital Polarization in Relativistic Magnetovortical Matter
Kenji Fukushima, Koichi Hattori, Kazuya Mameda

Abstract
We establish thermodynamic stability and gauge invariance in the magnetovortical matter of Dirac fermions under the coexistent rotation and strong magnetic field. The corresponding partition function reveals that the orbital contribution to bulk thermodynamics preponderates over the conventional contribution from anomaly-related spin effects. This orbital preponderance macroscopically manifests itself in the sign inversion of the induced charge and current in the magnetovortical matter, and can be tested experimentally as the flip of the angular momentum polarization of magnetovortical matter when the magnetic field strength is increased.

This Letter aims to challenge the conventional spin-dominant argument for the polarization effects as sketched in Fig.
1

In magnetovortical matter, the crucial piece is the significant contribution from the orbital angular momentum around the guiding center of each cyclotron orbit, as illustrated in Fig.

The polarization of the orbital angular momentum is opposite to that of the spin, as understood from Lenz’s law.
In quantum theory, we show that this orbital polarization preponderates over the spin polarization in magnitude, and thus the direction of the total angular momentum is flipped.
Such a sign inversion definitely affects thermodynamic quantities;
for example, the charge density becomes

\rho=-\frac{eB\Omega}{4\pi^{2}},

(1)

with an opposite sign to the aforementioned result.

In this Letter, we formulate the thermodynamics of the magnetovortical matter, and derive the charge density [Eq. (

We reveal that reaching the correct answer requires careful implementation of thermodynamic stability and gauge invariance, which were not encompassed in the previous thermodynamic function

These fundamental requirements turn out to be two sides of the same coin, and essential to resolving the unphysical divergence originating from large angular momenta
[

Besides, the thermodynamic function constructed in this way elucidates the relation among the family of the anomaly-related transport including Eq. (

Angular momentum in the Landau-level basis—
In the quantum theory of charged particles, one of the fundamental concepts is the gauge invariance of kinematical variables.
For instance, the physical momentum of a charged particle is not
{\boldsymbol{p}}=-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\nabla}
but
\boldsymbol{\Pi}={\boldsymbol{p}}-e\boldsymbol{A}
with an external electromagnetic field
\boldsymbol{A}
and a charge
e
.
A similar distinction is found in angular momenta.
The gauge-invariant and thus physical quantity is not the
canonical
angular momentum,
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{x}\times{\boldsymbol{p}}
, but the
kinetic
angular momentum,
\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathrm{kin}}=\boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{\Pi}
.
The difference between them may well be interpreted as the electromagnetic contribution.
Indeed, in the symmetric gauge, the canonical angular momentum is a Noether conserved charge for the rotational symmetry and is related to the kinetic one as
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathrm{kin}}+\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathrm{EM}}
with
\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathrm{EM}}=\boldsymbol{x}\times(e\boldsymbol{A})

[
36
]
.
The gauge dependence in
\boldsymbol{L}
and
\boldsymbol{L}_{\mathrm{EM}}
cancels out.

Quantum mechanics under
\boldsymbol{B}=B\hat{z}
is conveniently described by the Landau-level basis, that is,
|n,m\rangle=(a^{\dagger})^{n}(b^{\dagger})^{m}|0,0\rangle/\sqrt{n!m!}
, where
a=(\Pi_{x}+\mathrm{i}s\Pi_{y})/\sqrt{2|eB|}
,
b=\sqrt{|eB|/2}(X-\mathrm{i}sY)
, and
s=\mathrm{sgn}(eB)
.
Here, the coordinate variables,
X=x+\Pi_{y}/(eB)
and
Y=y-\Pi_{x}/(eB)
, are the guiding center of the cyclotron motion, and they are constants of motion if further interactions are turned off.
The longitudinal component of the kinetic angular momentum is represented as
L_{\mathrm{kin}}=x\Pi_{y}-y\Pi_{x}=\Lambda+\Delta
with

\begin{split}&\Lambda=(x-X)\Pi_{y}-(y-Y)\Pi_{x}=-s(2a^{\dagger}a+1),\\
&\Delta=X\Pi_{y}-Y\Pi_{x}=\mathrm{i}s(a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}-ab).\end{split}

(2)

The derivation is reviewed in the Supplemental Material
[
37
]
.
The diagonal operator
\Lambda
quantifies the local orbital motion around the guiding center, while the purely off-diagonal
\Delta
describes the global orbital motion around the origin of the coordinate system.
The form of
\Lambda
can be understood as follows.
For
eB>0
, the sign of the
\Lambda
eigenvalue should be negative as the cyclotron motion is clockwise on the
xy
plane.
The magnitude should be characterized by
a^{\dagger}a

so that higher Landau levels have larger
L_{\mathrm{kin}}
.
Also,
\Lambda
as well as the energy levels are independent of
b
and
b^{\dagger}
, as a result of the translational symmetry that leads to the Landau degeneracy.
Contrarily,
\Delta
obviously breaks the translational symmetry, and thus does not play any role in characterizing conventional magnetized systems without rotation;
see also Ref.
[
38
]
, in which
\Lambda
enters the kinetic theory and the hydrodynamics describing the guiding center motion, while
\Delta
does not.

Gauge invariance in the partition function—
The traditional approach to installing the rotational effect in thermodynamics is based on the maximum entropy principle.
First, let us examine rigidly rotating matter (i.e., systems with rotational symmetry) in the absence of a gauge field.
Then, the angular velocity enters a statistical ensemble as the Lagrange multiplier for the angular momentum conservation, in parallel to those of inverse temperature and chemical potential for the conservation of energy and charge, respectively.
The partition function is thus given as
[
39
]

Z=\mathrm{tr}\exp\Bigl[-\beta(\mathcal{H}-\Omega\mathcal{J})\Bigr]\quad\text{(no gauge field)}

(3)

with the inverse temperature
\beta=1/T
and the angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\Omega\hat{z}
.
Here,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{J}
are the field operators of a Hamiltonian and a conserved Noether charge, i.e., the
z
component of the angular momentum in our setup, respectively.
Although the partition function [Eq. (
3
)] was originally formulated in a nonrelativistic form, it yields the correct form of the chiral vortical effect
[
40
,
*Vilenkin:1980zv
]
, which was later confirmed with the linear response theory under a metric perturbation
[
19
]
.

However, the partition function [Eq. (
3
)] needs refinement for gauge theories.
The subtlety stems from the difference between the canonical and kinetic momenta elaborated above.
Whereas the conserved
\mathcal{J}
is built with the canonical angular momentum,
L=xp_{y}-yp_{x}
, it causes a problem that the partition function can become gauge dependent.
Instead, the correct partition function should read

Z=\mathrm{tr}\exp\Bigl[-\beta(\mathcal{H}-\Omega\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{kin}})\Bigr],

(4)

where
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{kin}}
is the field operator built with the kinetic angular momentum,
L_{\mathrm{kin}}=x\Pi_{y}-y\Pi_{x}
.
The partition function [Eq. (
4
)] is one particular form of the covariant density operator
[
42
,
*Becattini:2012tc
]
.

Let us now derive the gauge-invariant partition function [Eq. (
4
)].
The derivation is based on the balance condition within electromagnetism
[
44
]
.
Suppose that a particle with charge
e
is rotating on the circle
with a radius
r=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{1/2}
and an angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\Omega\hat{z}
.
This particle has a velocity
\boldsymbol{v}=r\Omega\hat{\theta}
along the azimuthal angle
\theta
direction.
If we apply a magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}=B\hat{z}
, the particle feels a radial drift force,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{drift}}=e\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}=eB\Omega\boldsymbol{r}
.
Then, the circular motion is no longer closed since the particle flows outwardly (inwardly) when
eB>0
(
eB<0
).
The particle motion can reach a steady state only when an additional electric field offsets the radial drift force.
Such an additional electric field is generated by an effective scalar potential
eA_{0}=\Omega L_{\mathrm{EM}}
involved in
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{kin}}
.
In the symmetric gauge
\boldsymbol{A}=(A^{1},A^{2},A^{3})=(-By/2,Bx/2,0)
,
one finds that
e\boldsymbol{E}=-e\boldsymbol{\nabla}A_{0}=-e\boldsymbol{\nabla}(xA^{2}-yA^{1})=-\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{drift}}
.
In many-body physics, the stability of the circular motion has a close connection to equilibration of magnetovortical systems.
That is, while a usual global equilibrium state allows for a global rotation and translation, the magnetovortical systems need to satisfy the additional balance condition along the radial direction (see Table
1
).
The covariant form of the local equilibrium condition in Ref.
[
45
]
also results in the present condition between
\boldsymbol{B}
and
\boldsymbol{E}
in its special case
1
1

1

The condition is written as
\partial_{\mu}\zeta+\beta^{\lambda}F_{\lambda\mu}=0
, where
\zeta
and
\beta^{\mu}
are the chemical potential and fluid velocity (multiplied by the inverse temperature), respectively.
The condition
\boldsymbol{E}=-B\Omega\boldsymbol{r}
corresponds to
\zeta=0
,
\beta^{\mu}=\beta(1,\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{x})
,
F_{21}=-F_{12}=B
, and
F_{0i}=E^{i}
.
.

As shown in the Supplemental Material

(5)

where we defined
D_{i}=\partial_{i}+\mathrm{i}eA_{i}
, a mass
M
, and the spin operator
S=\mathrm{i}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}/2

The same expression is obtained when the gauge field in the rotating coordinate
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
is employed as
A_{\mu}^{\prime}=(0,By^{\prime}/2,-Bx^{\prime}/2,0)
.

Orbital preponderance in the strong magnetic field limit—
A standard manner to evaluate Eq. (
4
) with the Hamiltonian [Eq. (
5
)] is to diagonalize the Dirac operator.
However, the Landau-level basis is not the eigenstate of the Dirac operator due to
\Delta
.
In a technical perspective, the difficulty of the present eigenvalue problem is more obvious in the corresponding differential equation of motion in the symmetric gauge; a potential term
\propto r^{4}=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{2}
arises and the anharmonic oscillator problem must be solved.
Also the translational noninvariance due to
\Delta
requires the careful treatment of the boundary effect in the cylindrical coordinate, and this inhomogeneity makes the analysis more complicated.

Nevertheless, in the lowest Landau level (LLL) approximation, we can obtain the analytic expression of Eq. (
4
) and illustrate the importance of the orbital motion.
Inserting the complete set of the Ritus basis for the spin-degenerate Landau levels
|n,m\rangle_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathcal{P}_{+}|n,m\rangle+\mathcal{P}_{-}|n-1,m\rangle
with
\mathcal{P}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm 2sS)

(see, e.g., Refs.
[
48
,
49
]
), one can show that

\begin{split}\ln Z=\mathrm{tr}\ln(\gamma^{0}\partial_{\tau}-\mathrm{i}\gamma^{3}\partial_{z}+M-\gamma^{0}\nu),\end{split}

(6)

where we used Eq. (
2
) and defined the rotation-induced effective chemical potential

\nu=\Omega\langle L_{\mathrm{kin}}+S\rangle_{\mathrm{LLL}}=-\frac{s\Omega}{2}

(7)

with
\langle O\rangle_{\mathrm{LLL}}={}_{\mathrm{R}}\langle 0,m|O|0,m\rangle_{\mathrm{R}}
.

A crucial remark is that
\nu
involves not only the spin-rotation coupling (spin polarization) but also the orbital-rotation coupling (orbital polarization).
This is why the conventional picture in Fig.
1
(a) is insufficient in the strong magnetic field case.
The negative sign in Eq. (
7
) is due to the orbital contribution,
\nu_{\mathrm{orb}}=\Omega\langle L_{\mathrm{kin}}\rangle_{\mathrm{LLL}}=-s\Omega
preponderating over the spin contribution,
\nu_{\mathrm{spin}}=\Omega\langle S\rangle_{\mathrm{LLL}}=s\Omega/2
.
Such a sign inversion is a unique feature in the coupling to
\Omega
.
For instance, the LLL energy dispersion incorporates a similar combination, i.e., the magnetization coupling, but this vanishes for the LLL due to the
g
factor:

eB\langle L_{\mathrm{kin}}+2S\rangle_{\mathrm{LLL}}=0
.

The evaluation of Eq. (
6
) is similar to that in the magnetized system at finite density.
After performing the Matsubara summation, the pressure is obtained as

\begin{split}P=\frac{|eB|}{2\pi}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{z}}{2\pi}\left[\epsilon+T\sum_{\eta=\pm}\ln\big(1+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\epsilon-\eta\nu)}\big)\right],\end{split}

(8)

where
\epsilon=\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+M^{2}}
.
The summation over
m
in the Landau-level basis was taken as
\sum_{m}=|eB|R^{2}/2
with the radial system size
R
, so that the usual Landau degeneracy is reproduced.
Equation (
8
) is derived without considering the boundary effects, which have to be taken into account in usual rotating systems because of the causality constraint
R\leq|\Omega|^{-1}
.
This is justified for bulk thermodynamics as long as we impose
\sqrt{|eB|}\gg R^{-1}
.
The physical meaning of the inequality is that the Landau wave function is localized too tightly to perceive the existence of the boundary
[
34
]
.

The pressure [Eq. (
8
)] implies a nontrivial property of the magnetovortical matter. To see this,
let us specifically look into the zero temperature limit of Eq. (
8
):

P=\frac{|eB|\Omega^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}\Biggl[\sqrt{1-\alpha}-\alpha\ln\frac{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\Biggr]\theta(1-\alpha),

(9)

where
\alpha=(2M/\Omega)^{2}
and
\theta(x)
is the step function.
For
|\Omega|/2>M
, hence, the rotational effect is visible even at the vacuum.
This property emerges from the formation of the Landau levels that remain low-energy modes.
Indeed, the rotational effect is unseen in a magnetic-free
vacuum, due to the infrared energy gap demanded by the causality constraint
[
34
]
.

Charge density and angular momenta—
The negative sign in Eq. (
7
) turns out to be crucial in charge-dependent thermodynamic quantities.
As a prominent example, let us compute the charge density in the massless limit
M\to 0
.
It is now convenient to add a real chemical potential
\mu
through the shift
\Omega\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{kin}}\to\Omega\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{kin}}+\mu\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\bar{\psi}\gamma^{0}\psi
in Eq. (
5
), and accordingly
\nu\to\nu+\mu
in Eq. (
8
).
The pressure is then analytically evaluated as

P=\frac{|eB|}{4\pi^{2}}\left[\biggl(\mu-\frac{s\Omega}{2}\biggr)^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3}\right].

(10)

The charge density is calculated as
\partial P/\partial\mu=|eB|\mu/(2\pi^{2})+\rho
with the magnetovortical contribution
\rho
in Eq. (
1
).
The negative sign in
\rho
originates in the same way as the effective chemical potential [Eq. (
7
)].
Namely, the charge density
\rho
is given as
the sum of the spin and orbital contributions:

\rho_{\mathrm{spin}}=\frac{eB\Omega}{4\pi^{2}},\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{orb}}=-\frac{eB\Omega}{2\pi^{2}}.

(11)

We make comparisons with related studies.
First, in Refs.
[
34
,
35
]
, we perform a similar analysis with the partition function [Eq. (
3
)], instead of Eq. (
4
).
Then,
\rho_{\mathrm{spin}}
was correctly found, but the orbital contribution corresponding to
\rho_{\mathrm{orb}}
was divergent in the large
R
limit, as
\simeq eB\Omega N/(4\pi^{2})
with the magnetic flux
N=eBR^{2}/2\gg 1

3
3

3

The derivation is done almost parallelly to the present case, by replacing
\langle L_{\mathrm{kin}}\rangle_{\mathrm{LLL}}=-s
with
\langle L\rangle_{\mathrm{LLL}}=sm
, which follows from the ladder-operator form in the symmetric gauge,
L=xp_{y}-yp_{x}=b^{\dagger}b-a^{\dagger}a
.
This leads to the thermal average with
\sum_{m=0}^{N}\langle L\rangle_{\mathrm{LLL}}\simeq sN^{2}/2
, instead of
\sum_{m=0}^{N}\langle L_{\mathrm{kin}}\rangle_{\mathrm{LLL}}=-sN
.
Such an extra factor of
N
results in the divergent orbital part of the charge density.
.
This unphysical divergence is identified as the thermodynamic instability because of the nonvanishing drift force in the partition function [Eq. (
3
)].
Second, the Kubo formula based on the diagrammatic approach led to the answer that looked like only
\rho_{\mathrm{spin}}

[
21
]
.
The sign of
\rho
was skewed due to the lack of clear recognition of the orbital-rotation coupling.
As we discuss in the Supplemental Material
[
37
]
, the careful analysis of the linear response theory agrees with Eq. (
1
)
4
4

4

The linear response theory under a slow rotation should be equivalent to the analysis from the partition function. On the other hand, this is not the case under a perturbation not realizing an equilibrium state, as is found in the chiral magnetic conductivity under an ac magnetic field
[
75
]
.
.

Besides, the chiral kinetic theory (CKT) up to
O(\hbar^{2})
also reproduces only the spin part
\rho_{\mathrm{spin}}

[
22
,
*Mameda:2023ueq
,
*Yang:2024sfp
]
.
This, however, does not contradict our analysis in the LLL approximation.
In the usual CKT, background electromagnetic fields are assumed to be small compared with other scales.
Since a weak magnetic field cannot yield the Landau quantization
[
52
]
, only the spin-rotation coupling contributes to the induced charge density [see Fig.

(a)].
On the other hand, the CKT constructed with the Landau-level basis

should pick up the orbital contribution
\rho_{\mathrm{orb}}
.
Our result appears to be consistent with the “total” charge density obtained from the “vortical solution” in Ref.

The above observations elucidate the distinct origins of
\rho_{\mathrm{spin}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{orb}}
.
The former origin persists irrespective of the magnitude of the magnetic field.
The latter emerges only under a strong magnetic field.
Hence, it is deduced that only
\rho_{\mathrm{spin}}
is related to a quantum anomaly.
We confirm this identification with the thermodynamic potential.
From the pressure [Eq. (
10
)], the total angular momentum is derived as
\partial P/\partial\Omega=J_{\mathrm{spin}}+J_{\mathrm{orb}}+|eB|\Omega/(8\pi^{2})
, where the last term is the contribution from the effective chemical potential
\nu=-s\Omega/2
and the others are from the real one
\mu
:

\begin{split}J_{\mathrm{spin}}=\frac{eB\mu}{4\pi^{2}},\quad J_{\mathrm{orb}}=-\frac{eB\mu}{2\pi^{2}}.\end{split}

(12)

The Maxwell relation for
\partial^{2}P/\partial\mu\partial\Omega
shows that
\rho_{\mathrm{spin}}
and
J_{\mathrm{spin}}
share the same origin of the coefficient
eB/4\pi^{2}
; see also a similar correspondence in Ref.
[
54
]
.
We recall that the fermionic spin is also connected with the axial current as
J_{\mathrm{spin}}=j^{z}_{5}/2
.
Therefore, both
\rho_{\mathrm{spin}}
and
J_{\mathrm{spin}}
are anomaly related, and so is the chiral separation current
j^{z}_{5}=eB\mu/(2\pi^{2})
.
We note that they receive the mass correction, which is again the same as the chiral separation effect.

Finally, we discuss an associated transport phenomenon in magnetovortical matter.
When the induced charge density
\rho
is
rotating with the velocity
v_{\theta}=r\Omega
, the azimuthal current density is given as
j_{\theta}=\rho v_{\theta}=-eB\Omega^{2}r/(4\pi^{2})
.
The stability condition for electromagnetic fields,
\boldsymbol{E}=-B\Omega\boldsymbol{r}
, leads to the vector form
\boldsymbol{j}=-e\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}/(4\pi^{2})
, which agrees with the spatial component of the Chern-Simons current
[
26
]
, but with the opposite sign for the same reason as
\rho
.
This explains the physical picture behind the Chern-Simons current derived from the discussion about quantum anomaly.

Outlook—
Our work sheds light on the orbital angular momentum carried by magnetized Dirac fermions.
Lattice QCD simulation is a first-principles method to extend our analysis to the strongly coupled magnetovortical matter with dynamical gluons.
A benchmark quantity is magnetization, which has been simulated without rotation
[
55
,
56
,
*Bonati:2013lca
,
*Levkova:2013qda
,
*Bali:2020bcn
]
.
In the magnetovortical matter, magnetization is obtained from our pressure [Eq. (
10
)] as

\begin{split}\frac{\partial P}{\partial(eB)}=\frac{s}{4\pi^{2}}\biggl(\mu^{2}-s\mu\Omega+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}+\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3}\biggr).\end{split}

(13)

The sign of the term
\propto\mu\Omega
provides a crucial test.
Our analysis is also inspiring enough to revisit the phase structures of magnetovortical matter
[
33
,
60
,
*Cao:2019ctl
,
*Chen:2019tcp
]
with the correct thermodynamic formulation in lattice QCD.
In general, numerical simulations for rotating systems depend on details of boundary conditions implemented for causality
[
63
,
*Braguta:2021jgn
,
*Yang:2023vsw
]
.
Investigating the magnetovortical matter is technically advantageous in this respect because the wave functions in strong magnetic fields are tightly localized within a cyclotron radius.

Our finding in Eq. (
12
) at
\Omega=0
implies that the strongly magnetized matter plays a role of the storage of the negative angular momentum
J=J_{\mathrm{spin}}+J_{\mathrm{orb}}<0
for
eB\mu>0
.
If we adiabatically turn off
B
, a stored
J
is distributed in a rigid body so that the total angular momentum is conserved.
This evokes the Einstein-de Haas (EdH) effect, but the rotation occurs in an opposite direction to the conventional one, which we call the anti-EdH effect.
This novel phenomenon can be tested in parallel to the conventional one by using Weyl or Dirac semimetals, which are good environments for the direct applicability of the present relativistic analysis, the avoidability of the mass correction, and the controllability of the chemical potential
[
66
]
.
The maximum induced angular momentum is evaluated from
\Omega_{\mathrm{ind}}=|J|/I
with
I
being the density of moment of inertia for a cylindrical material.
The estimated magnitude is as large as

\begin{split}\Omega_{\mathrm{ind}}=\frac{\displaystyle\biggl(\frac{\mu}{10^{-1}\,\text{eV}}\biggr)\biggl(\frac{B}{10^{2}\,\text{T}}\biggr)}{\displaystyle\biggl(\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{mass}}}{1\,\text{g}/\text{cm}^{3}}\biggr)\biggl(\frac{R}{10^{-2}\,\text{cm}}\biggr)^{2}}\times 7.76\times 10^{-5}\,\text{rad/s},\end{split}

(14)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{mass}}
is the mass density and the Fermi velocity is introduced as
v_{\mathrm{F}}=10^{6}\,\text{m}/\text{s}
.
This
\Omega_{\mathrm{ind}}
is smaller by only 1 order of magnitude than the conventional EdH, and sufficiently testable;
for example, a realistic set of parameters
\mu=10^{-1}\,\text{eV}
,
B=10^{2}\,\text{T}
, and
R=10^{-2}\,\mathrm{cm}
leads to
\mu/\sqrt{2\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}eB}=0.39
and
eBR^{2}/2\hbar=1.52\times 10^{9}
, which justify the LLL approximation with the large degeneracy, and thus the estimate based on Eq. (
12
).
Further studies open up a new avenue to the orbitronics and related phenomena of Dirac electrons.

Preponderant Orbital Polarization in Relativistic Magnetovortical Matter

Kenji Fukushima, Koichi Hattori, and Kazuya Mameda

S1
Landau quantization

This section is devoted to a review of the nonrelativistic Landau problem (see also Ref.
[
49
]
);
the following argument is also applied to the energy dispersion of Dirac fermions.
The one-particle Hamiltonian under a magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}=B\hat{z}
is given by

H=\frac{1}{2M}(\Pi_{x}^{2}+\Pi_{y}^{2}),\qquad\Pi_{i}=p_{i}-eA_{i}(x,y),

(S1)

where
A_{i}
is the gauge field such that
\partial_{x}A_{y}-\partial_{y}A_{x}=B
.
Here
\Pi_{i}
is called the kinetic (linear) momentum;
‘kinetic’ means this is the gauge-invariant and thus physical momentum.
Another fundamental quantity to characterize the cyclotron motion is
(X,Y)
defined as

X=x+\frac{\Pi_{y}}{eB},\qquad Y=y-\frac{\Pi_{x}}{eB}.

(S2)

From a solution of the classical equation of motion,
\dot{p}_{i}=-\partial H/\partial x_{i}
and
\dot{x}_{i}=\partial H/\partial p_{i}
, one finds that the set of
(X,Y)
is the center coordinate of the cyclotron motion, which is referred to as the guiding center coordinate.

An important advantage of introducing the guiding center
(X,Y)
is that with the help of the canonical commutation relation
[x_{i},p_{j}]=\mathrm{i}\delta_{ij}
, one can construct the following algebra:

[\Pi_{x},\Pi_{y}]=\mathrm{i}eB,\qquad[X,Y]=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{eB},\qquad[X,\Pi_{i}]=[Y,\Pi_{i}]=0.

(S3)

This enables us to introduce the following two types of the ladder operators:

a:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2|eB|}}(\Pi_{x}+\mathrm{i}s\Pi_{y}),\qquad b:=\sqrt{\frac{|eB|}{2}}(X-\mathrm{i}sY),\qquad s:=\mathrm{sgn}(eB),

(S4)

which satisfies
[a,a^{\dagger}]=[b,b^{\dagger}]=1
and
[a,b]=0
.
We inversely solve Eq. (
S4
) in terms of
\Pi_{i}
,
X
and
Y
as

\Pi_{x}=\sqrt{\frac{|eB|}{2}}(a+a^{\dagger}),\qquad\Pi_{y}=-\mathrm{i}s\sqrt{\frac{|eB|}{2}}(a-a^{\dagger}),\qquad X=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2|eB|}}(b+b^{\dagger}),\qquad Y=\frac{\mathrm{i}s}{\sqrt{2|eB|}}(b-b^{\dagger}).

(S5)

The Landau-level basis is defined as the eigenstate for
a^{\dagger}a
and
b^{\dagger}b
:

|n,m\rangle:=\frac{(a^{\dagger})^{n}(b^{\dagger})^{m}}{\sqrt{n!m!}}|0,0\rangle,

(S6)

where the ground state is normalized as
\langle 0,0|0,0\rangle=1
.

Let us explain the physical meaning of the quantum numbers
n
and
m
.
It is straightforward to show

\Pi_{x}^{2}+\Pi_{y}^{2}=|eB|(2a^{\dagger}a+1).

(S7)

This representation implies that
n
corresponds to the transverse momentum on the
xy
-plane, or equivalently, to the quantum number to specify the Landau level, as is clear from

H|n,m\rangle=\frac{|eB|}{M}\biggl(n+\frac{1}{2}\biggr)|n,m\rangle.

(S8)

An important observation here is that the Landau level is degenerate in terms of
m
.
The reason is understandable if we notice the following relation:

X^{2}+Y^{2}=\frac{1}{|eB|}(2b^{\dagger}b+1).

(S9)

This indicates that
m
characterizes the guiding center position, which is obviously not invariant under the translation on the
xy
-plane.
The degeneracy of
m
hence reflects the translational invariance of the Landau problem.
More precisely, each state
|n,m\rangle
corresponds to the cyclotron motion where the magnitude of the momentum is
\sqrt{|eB|(2n+1)}
and the center position is located at the distance
\sqrt{(2m+1)/|eB|}
from the origin.

Using the above ladder operators, we represent the kinetic angular momentum as

\begin{split}L_{z}^{\mathrm{kin}}&:=x\Pi_{y}-y\Pi_{x}=\Lambda+\Delta,\end{split}

(S10)

where
\Lambda
and
\Delta
are the diagonal and off-diagonal operator defined as

\displaystyle\Lambda:=(x-X)\Pi_{y}-(y-Y)\Pi_{x}=-s(2a^{\dagger}a+1),

(S11)

\displaystyle\Delta:=X\Pi_{y}-Y\Pi_{x}=-\mathrm{i}s(ab-a^{\dagger}b^{\dagger}).

(S12)

The operator
\Lambda
corresponds to the usual cyclotron motion, i.e., the local circular motion around the guiding center.
The overall sign of
\Lambda
is met to the classical Lenz’s law, and it is plausible that the magnitude is proportional to the transverse momentum.
Another piece
\Delta
is regarded as the angular momentum due to the global circular motion around the origin.
For this reason,
\Delta
is nothing to do with the usual Landau problem respecting the translational symmetry, while it generally contributes under rotation, which washes out the translation invariance.
We emphasize that in the lowest Landau level approximation, the off-diagonal operator
\Delta
does not enter the thermodynamics of magnetovortical matter.
This is physically because the strong magnetic field limit effectively recovers the translation invariance even under rotation.

S2
Field operator of angular momentum

We present the brief derivation of the gauge-invariant angular momentum operator composed of Dirac fields (see also Ref.
[
67
]
for more detailed discussion), providing Eq. (5) in the main text.
We start from the following Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}=\bar{\psi}(\mathrm{i}\gamma^{\mu}D_{\mu}-m)\psi-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}.

(S13)

with
D_{\mu}\psi=(\partial_{\mu}+\mathrm{i}eA_{\mu})\psi
and
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
.
Here and hereafter the raising and lowering of indices are performed using the Minkowski metric
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)
.
The canonical energy-momentum tensor is computed as

\begin{split}\Theta^{\mu\nu}&=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}\psi)}\partial^{\nu}\psi+\partial^{\nu}\bar{\psi}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}\bar{\psi})}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}A_{\rho})}\partial^{\nu}A_{\rho}-\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{L}\\
&=\bar{\psi}\mathrm{i}\gamma^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\psi-F^{\mu\rho}\partial^{\nu}A_{\rho}-\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{L}.\end{split}

(S14)

The above energy-momentum tensor is not gauge-invariant obviously, but the situation can be cured through the Belinfante prescription;
the energy momentum tensor can be shifted by
\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho\mu\nu}
such that
B^{\mu\rho\nu}=-B^{\rho\mu\nu}
because the conservation law still holds as
\partial_{\mu}(\Theta^{\mu\nu}+\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho\mu\nu})=0
.
One of such Belinfante tensors above is

B^{\rho\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}(M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\rho\mu\nu}-M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mu\rho\nu}+M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\nu\mu\rho})\qquad M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mu\rho\sigma}:=\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Sigma^{\rho\sigma}\psi+F^{\mu\rho}A^{\sigma}-F^{\mu\sigma}A^{\rho},

(S15)

where
\Sigma^{\mu\nu}=(\mathrm{i}/4)[\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}]
.
This
M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\mu\rho\sigma}
is a spin-like part of the total angular momentum tensor, as we will show below.
Thanks to the identity
\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\rho}=\eta^{\mu\nu}\gamma^{\rho}+\eta^{\nu\rho}\gamma^{\mu}-\eta^{\rho\mu}\gamma^{\nu}-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\gamma^{5}\gamma_{\sigma}
with
\gamma_{5}=\mathrm{i}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}\gamma^{3}
and
\varepsilon^{0123}=1
, the Belinfante term is written as

B^{\rho\mu\nu}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\bar{\psi}\biggl(\eta^{\nu\rho}\gamma^{\mu}-\eta^{\nu\mu}\gamma^{\rho}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\varepsilon^{\rho\mu\nu\sigma}\gamma_{5}\gamma_{\sigma}\biggr)\psi-F^{\rho\mu}A^{\nu}.

(S16)

Then, the improved energy-momentum tensor is

\begin{split}T^{\mu\nu}&:=\Theta^{\mu\nu}+\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho\mu\nu}\\
&=\bar{\psi}\mathrm{i}\gamma^{\mu}\overleftrightarrow{D^{\nu}}\psi+\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\partial_{\rho}(\bar{\psi}\gamma_{5}\gamma_{\sigma}\psi)-F^{\mu\rho}{F^{\nu}}_{\rho}+\frac{1}{4}\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}F^{\rho\sigma},\end{split}

(S17)

where we define
\overleftrightarrow{D_{\mu}}:=(D_{\mu}-\overleftarrow{D_{\mu}})/2
with
\bar{\psi}\overleftarrow{D_{\mu}}:=\bar{\psi}(\overleftarrow{\partial_{\mu}}-\mathrm{i}eA_{\mu})
.
In deriving the last line, we utilize the U(1) current conservation
\partial_{\mu}(\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\psi)=0
and the equations of motions
(\mathrm{i}\gamma^{\mu}D_{\mu}-m)\psi=0
and
\partial_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=e\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\nu}\psi
.

Similarly, the canonical angular momentum tensor is derived as

\begin{split}M^{\lambda\mu\nu}&=x^{\mu}\Theta^{\lambda\nu}-x^{\nu}\Theta^{\lambda\mu}-\mathrm{i}\biggl[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\lambda}\psi)}\Sigma^{\mu\nu}\psi+\bar{\psi}\Sigma^{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\lambda}\bar{\psi})}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\lambda}A_{\rho})}\Xi^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}A_{\sigma}\biggr]\\
&=x^{\mu}\Theta^{\lambda\nu}-x^{\nu}\Theta^{\lambda\mu}+M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\lambda\mu\nu}.\end{split}

(S18)

with
\Xi^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}=\eta^{\mu\alpha}\eta^{\nu\beta}-\eta^{\mu\beta}\eta^{\nu\alpha}
.
Again the above form is not gauge-invariant, but can be improved by the Belinfante tensor;
the angular momentum can be shifted by
\partial_{\rho}(x^{\mu}B^{\rho\lambda\nu}-x^{\nu}B^{\rho\lambda\mu})
because
B^{\lambda\rho\nu}=-B^{\rho\lambda\mu}
ensures the conservation law
\partial_{\lambda}[M^{\lambda\mu\nu}+\partial_{\rho}(x^{\mu}B^{\rho\lambda\nu}-x^{\nu}B^{\rho\lambda\mu})]=0
.
From
B^{\mu\lambda\nu}-B^{\nu\lambda\mu}=-M_{\mathrm{s}}^{\lambda\mu\nu}
, we find

\begin{split}J^{\lambda\mu\nu}&:=M^{\lambda\mu\nu}+\partial_{\rho}(x^{\mu}B^{\rho\lambda\nu}-x^{\nu}B^{\rho\lambda\mu})\\
&=x^{\mu}T^{\lambda\nu}-x^{\nu}T^{\lambda\mu}.\end{split}

(S19)

The conserved energy density and angular momentum density along the
z
-axis are
{T^{0}}_{0}
and
J^{0xy}
, respectively.
Keeping only the fermionic contributions, we arrive at the following expressions of the Hamiltonian and the angular momentum:

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}&:=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,{T^{0}}_{0}=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\bar{\psi}(-\mathrm{i}\gamma^{i}D_{i}-m)\psi,\\
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{kin}}&:=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,J^{0xy}=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\bar{\psi}\gamma^{0}\biggl[-\mathrm{i}(xD_{y}-yD_{x})+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}\biggr]\psi,\end{split}

(S20)

where we utilize the Dirac equation and perform the integration by parts.
These gauge-invariant operators correspond to those in Eq. (5) in the main text.

S3
Linear response theory

S3.1
Correspondence between the flow and metric perturbations

We revisit the linear response theory for the charge density induced in the magnetovortical matter, and clarify the correspondence between our result in the main text and that in Ref.
[
21
]
.
Within the linear-response regime, the current induced by the Hamiltonian density
H_{\mathrm{ext}}
is given as
[
68
]

\delta j^{\mu}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=-\mathrm{i}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x^{\prime}\langle[j^{\mu}(t,\boldsymbol{x}),H_{\mathrm{ext}}(t^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})]\rangle,

(S21)

where the angle brackets represent the thermal average.
The perturbation Hamiltonian density is identified as follows.

We first check the equivalence between a flow perturbation and a metric perturbation used in Ref.
[
21
]
.
The Hamiltonian for the rest fluid
\mathring{u}^{\mu}=(1,\boldsymbol{0})
is given as

\mathring{H}=T^{0\mu}\mathring{u}_{\mu}=T^{00}.

(S22)

Let us consider a nontrivial background fluid velocity
u^{\mu}=(1,\boldsymbol{v})
, i.e,
u_{\mu}=(1,-\boldsymbol{v})
, where we ignore the terms of
O(|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2})
.
Then, the Hamiltonian at the comoving frame reads

H=T^{0\mu}u_{\mu}=\mathring{H}-T^{0i}v^{i}.

(S23)

In particular, let us consider a flow
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{x}
with a constant
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
that realizes the rotating system with the angular velocity
\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}
.
Then, the above Hamiltonian becomes

H=\mathring{H}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}\times\boldsymbol{P}),

(S24)

where
P^{i}=(\boldsymbol{P})^{i}=T^{0i}
.

This Hamiltonian is mimicked by a background geometry.
That is, we consider a static curved spacetime quantified by the metric tensor
g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}
, where the Minkowskian metric is given as
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)
.
We suppose that the metric perturbation
h_{\mu\nu}
has nonzero components only in
h_{0i}
.
The Hamiltonian for the rest fluid in this geometry is specified by
u^{\mu}=(1,\boldsymbol{0})
, i.e.,
u_{\mu}=\mathring{u}_{\mu}+h_{0\mu}
and reads

H=T^{0\mu}u_{\mu}=\mathring{H}+T^{0i}h_{0i}.

(S25)

According to Eqs. (
S23
) and (
S25
),
the perturbation Hamiltonian is now identified as

H_{\mathrm{ext}}=-T^{0i}v^{i}=T^{0i}h_{0i}.

(S26)

This implies the correspondence between the perturbations

v^{i}=-h_{0i},

(S27)

and thus that

\Omega^{i}=-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_{j}h_{0k},

(S28)

where the Levi-Civita symbol is normalized by
\epsilon^{123}=-\epsilon_{123}=1
.
The same expression of the vorticity is shown in Ref.
[
69
]
and required to reproduce the chiral vortical effect in Ref.
[
70
]
with the correct sign.
However, above Eq. (8) of Ref.
[
21
]
, the relation between the perturbations has an opposite sign.

S3.2
Linear response under a metric perturbation

Let us analyze the induced charge under the metric perturbation.
In the following, all indices can be raised and lowered by
\eta_{\mu\nu}
, granted that we consider the linear response to the metric perturbation.
The linear response theory (
S21
) tells that, under a weak metric perturbation (
S25
), the induced current is expressed with the retarded correlator as

\begin{split}\delta j^{\mu}(q)=\frac{1}{2}G^{\mu\nu\rho}_{\mathrm{R}}(q)h_{\nu\rho}(q),\quad G^{\mu\nu\rho}_{\mathrm{R}}(x-x^{\prime})=-\mathrm{i}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle[j^{\mu}(x),T^{\nu\rho}(x^{\prime})]\rangle,\end{split}

(S29)

where we defined
q^{\mu}=(\omega,{\boldsymbol{q}})
and the Fourier transform

f(x)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\cdot x}f(q).

(S30)

The factor of
1/2
is attached in front of the correlator, in order to cancel a doubled contribution from the symmetrized contraction.
The microscopic definitions of the current and the energy-momentum tensor are given by

j^{\mu}(x)=\bar{\psi}(x)\gamma^{\mu}\psi(x),\quad T^{0i}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\bar{\psi}(x)(\gamma^{0}D^{i}+\gamma^{i}D^{0})\psi(x).

(S31)

The covariant derivative
D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}+\mathrm{i}eA_{\mu}
contains
A_{\mu}
for a constant magnetic field.
Based on the analytic continuation, we relate the retarded correlator to the imaginary-time correlator as

G^{\mu\nu\rho}_{\mathrm{E}}(q_{0}\to\omega+\mathrm{i}\delta,{\boldsymbol{q}})=G_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mu\nu\rho}(q),\quad G_{\mathrm{E}}^{\mu\nu\rho}(x-x^{\prime})=-\langle T_{\tau}j^{\mu}(x)T^{\nu\rho}(x^{\prime})\rangle,

(S32)

where
\delta
is a positive infinitesimal number for the analytic continuation and
T_{\tau}
is the imaginary-time ordering.
The momentum in the imaginary-time correlator is
q^{\mu}=(q_{0},{\boldsymbol{q}})
with the bosonic Matsubara frequency
q_{0}=2\mathrm{i}\pi Tm
(
m
is an integer).
Focusing on the charge density, we have

\delta j^{0}=h_{0k}(q)\lim_{\omega\to 0}G_{\mathrm{E}}^{00k}(q_{0}\to\omega+\mathrm{i}\delta,{\boldsymbol{q}}).

(S33)

The limit of
\omega\to 0
is required in order to obtain a static transport coefficient.

We compute the above correlator in the lowest Landau level (LLL) limit at the one-loop order.
One should start with the coordinate-space representation

\begin{split}G_{\mathrm{E}}^{00k}(x-x^{\prime})&=(-1)\cdot(-1)\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathrm{tr}\Bigl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}S_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x,x^{\prime})(\gamma^{0}D^{k}_{x^{\prime}}+\gamma^{k}D^{0}_{x^{\prime}})S_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x^{\prime},x)\Bigr]\\
&=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathrm{tr}\Bigl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}S_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x,x^{\prime})\gamma^{0}D^{k}_{x^{\prime}}S_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x^{\prime},x)\Bigr],\end{split}

(S34)

where the two minus signs are due to the definition of
G_{\mathrm{E}}^{\mu\nu\rho}
in Eq. (
S32
) and the fermion loop.
The LLL propagator
S_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x^{\prime},x)
is given by

S_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x^{\prime},x)=\langle\bar{\psi}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x^{\prime})\psi_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x)\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi(x^{\prime},x)}\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x^{\prime}-x)

(S35)

with
\psi_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x)
is the field operator for the Dirac fermion in the LLL.
The translational invariance is broken by the Schwinger phase

\phi(x^{\prime},x)=-e\int_{x}^{x^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}z^{\mu}\biggl[A_{\mu}(z)+\frac{1}{2}F_{\mu\nu}(z^{\nu}-x^{\nu})\biggr],

(S36)

which fulfills

\begin{split}D^{\mu}_{x^{\prime}}S_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x^{\prime},x)&=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi(x^{\prime},x)}\biggl[\partial^{\mu}_{x^{\prime}}-\frac{\mathrm{i}e}{2}F^{\mu\nu}({x}^{\prime}_{\nu}-x_{\nu})\biggr]\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x^{\prime}-x).\end{split}

(S37)

The translation-invariant part of the imaginary-time propagator is given as

\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(p)=2\,\mathrm{e}^{-|p_{\perp}|^{2}/|eB|}S_{1+1}(p_{\parallel}),\quad S_{1+1}(p_{\parallel})=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{+}}{\gamma^{\mu}_{\parallel}p_{\mu\parallel}}=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{+}}{\gamma^{0}p_{0}+\gamma^{3}p_{3}},

(S38)

where
p^{\mu}=(p_{0},{\boldsymbol{p}})
,
p_{0}=\mathrm{i}\pi T(2n-1)
,
{\boldsymbol{p}}=(p^{1},p^{2},p^{3})
,
p^{\mu}_{\parallel}=(p^{0},0,0,p^{3})
,
p^{\mu}_{\perp}=(0,p^{1},p^{2},0)
and
\mathcal{P}_{+}=(1+\mathrm{i}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2})/2
.
The field strength is now specified as
F^{21}=\partial^{2}A^{1}-\partial^{1}A^{2}=\partial_{1}A^{2}-\partial_{2}A^{1}=B
, namely,

F^{kl}=-\epsilon^{kl}_{\perp}B.

(S39)

The transverse Levi-Civita symbol is defined by
\epsilon_{\perp}^{12}=-\epsilon_{\perp}^{21}=1
, i.e.,
\epsilon^{jk}_{\perp}=\epsilon^{3jk}
.

From the above preparation, the correlation function is written as

\begin{split}G_{\mathrm{E}}^{00k}(x-x^{\prime})&=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathrm{tr}\Bigl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x-x^{\prime})\gamma^{0}\Bigl(\partial^{k}_{x^{\prime}}+\mathrm{i}(eB/2)\epsilon_{\perp}^{kl}({x}^{\prime}_{l}-x_{l})\Bigr)\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x^{\prime}-x)\Bigr]\\
&=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\,T\sum_{n}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,T\sum_{l}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(q-p)\cdot(x-x^{\prime})}\mathrm{tr}\Bigl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(q)\gamma^{0}(-\mathrm{i}p^{k}+(eB/2)\epsilon_{\perp}^{kl}\partial^{p}_{l})\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(p)\Bigr]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\,T\sum_{n}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,T\sum_{l}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\cdot(x-x^{\prime})}\mathrm{tr}\Bigl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(p+q)\gamma^{0}(p^{k}+\mathrm{i}(eB/2)\epsilon_{\perp}^{kl}\partial^{p}_{l})\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(p)\Bigr].\end{split}

(S40)

The last line is obtained with the change of the variable
q-p\to q
.
Here, we define
\partial_{p}^{j}=\partial/\partial p_{j}=-\partial/\partial p^{j}
, and accordingly
\partial^{p}_{j}=-\partial_{p}^{j}
.
From Eq. (
S40
), the correlation function in the momentum space reads

\begin{split}G_{\mathrm{E}}^{00k}(q)&=\frac{1}{2}\,T\sum_{m}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\mathrm{tr}\Bigl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(p+q)\gamma^{0}(p^{k}+\mathrm{i}(eB/2)\epsilon_{\perp}^{kl}\partial^{p}_{l})\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(p)\Bigr].\end{split}

(S41)

Hereafter, we focus only on
k=1,2
, that is, the transverse component of
G^{00k}_{\mathrm{E}\perp}(q)
.
The sign in front of
eB
is opposite to that in Eq. (15) of Ref.
[
21
]
for
k=2
.

The phase space integral in
G^{00k}_{\mathrm{E}\perp}(q)
is decomposed into those over the transverse and longitudinal momenta, as follows:

G^{00k}_{\mathrm{E}\perp}(q)=I^{k}_{\perp}(q_{\perp})I_{\parallel}(q),

(S42)

I^{k}_{\perp}(q_{\perp})=2\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}p_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{2}}(p_{\perp}^{k}+\mathrm{i}s\epsilon_{\perp}^{lk}p_{\perp}^{l})\,\mathrm{e}^{-(|p_{\perp}+q_{\perp}|^{2}+|p_{\perp}|^{2})/|eB|},

(S43)

\begin{split}I_{\parallel}(q_{\parallel})=T\sum_{n}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{z}}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{tr}\Bigl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}S_{1+1}(p_{\parallel}+q_{\parallel})\gamma^{0}S_{1+1}(p_{\parallel})\Bigr],\end{split}

(S44)

where
s={\rm sgn}(eB)
.
The former integration can be performed as

\begin{split}I^{k}_{\perp}(q_{\perp})&=2\mathrm{e}^{-|{\boldsymbol{q}}_{\perp}|^{2}/(2|eB|)}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}p_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{2}}(p_{\perp}^{k}+\mathrm{i}s\epsilon_{\perp}^{lk}p_{\perp}^{l})\mathrm{e}^{-2|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\perp}+{\boldsymbol{q}}_{\perp}/2|^{2}/|eB|}\\
&=-\mathrm{e}^{-|{\boldsymbol{q}}_{\perp}|^{2}/(2|eB|)}(q_{\perp}^{k}+\mathrm{i}s\epsilon_{\perp}^{lk}q_{\perp}^{l})\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}p^{\prime}_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-2|{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\perp}^{\prime}|^{2}/|eB|}\\
&=-\frac{|eB|}{8\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-|{\boldsymbol{q}}_{\perp}|^{2}/(2|eB|)}(q_{\perp}^{k}+\mathrm{i}s\epsilon_{\perp}^{lk}q_{\perp}^{l}).\end{split}

(S45)

For the latter integral, one can replace the Matsubara summation
by a contour integral.
By the use of identities
\tanh z=\pm[1-2/(\mathrm{e}^{\pm 2z}+1)]
,
one can separate the vacuum and thermal parts as
I_{\parallel}=I_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{vac}}+I_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{mat}}
with

\begin{split}I_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{vac}}(q_{\parallel})&=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{z}}{2\pi}\int_{-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\mathrm{i}\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{0}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{tr}\biggl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}\frac{1}{\gamma^{\mu}_{\parallel}(p_{\mu\parallel}+q_{\mu\parallel})}\gamma^{0}\frac{1}{\gamma^{\nu}_{\parallel}p_{\nu\parallel}}\biggr],\\
I_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{mat}}(q_{\parallel})&=-\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{z}}{2\pi}\sum_{\eta=\pm}\int_{C_{\eta}}\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{0}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}n_{\mathrm{F}}(\eta p_{0})\,\mathrm{tr}\biggl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}\frac{1}{\gamma^{\mu}_{\parallel}(p_{\mu\parallel}+q_{\mu\parallel})}\gamma^{0}\frac{1}{\gamma^{\nu}_{\parallel}p_{\nu\parallel}}\biggr],\end{split}

(S46)

where we defined
p_{z}=p^{3}
and the thermal distribution function
n_{\mathrm{F}}(p_{0})=1/[\mathrm{e}^{\beta p_{0}}+1]
.
The contours
C_{\pm}
go along
-\mathrm{i}\infty\pm\varepsilon\to\mathrm{i}\infty\pm\varepsilon
with a positive infinitesimal number
\varepsilon
, and pick up the residues at the positive- and negative-energy poles, respectively.

It is here important to notice that the thermal contribution
I_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{mat}}(q_{\parallel})
vanishes totally, as shown in the following.
First, we compute the spinor trace as
\mathrm{tr}[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{\mu}_{\parallel}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{\nu}_{\parallel}]=2(-\eta^{\mu\nu}_{\parallel}+2\eta^{\mu 0}_{\parallel}\eta^{\nu 0}_{\parallel})
with
\eta^{\mu\nu}_{\parallel}
being the longitudinal components of the Minkowskian metric tensor.
The contour integral is then evaluated with the residues as

\begin{split}I_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{mat}}&=\sum_{\eta=\pm}\eta\!\int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{z}}{2\pi}\biggl[n_{\mathrm{F}}(|p_{z}|)\frac{\eta p_{z}(\eta p_{z}+q_{0})+p_{z}(p_{z}+q_{z})}{2\eta p_{z}[(\eta p_{z}+q_{0})^{2}-(p_{z}+q_{z})^{2}]}+n_{\mathrm{F}}(|p_{z}+q_{z}|)\frac{(\eta(p_{z}+q_{z})-q_{0})\eta(p_{z}+q_{z})+p_{z}(p_{z}+q_{z})}{2[(\eta(p_{z}+q_{z})-q_{0})^{2}-p_{z}^{2}]\eta(p_{z}+q_{z})}\biggr]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\eta=\pm}\frac{1}{q_{z}-\eta q_{0}}\cdot\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{z}}{2\pi}\Bigl[n_{\mathrm{F}}(|p_{z}+q_{z}|)-n_{\mathrm{F}}(|p_{z}|)\Bigr],\end{split}

(S47)

where we used
\mathrm{e}^{\eta\beta q_{0}}=\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\pi m}=1
for
q_{0}=2\mathrm{i}\pi Tm
.
Since the above
p_{z}
-integrals are convergent, we can freely shift the integral variable.
This manipulation leads to the cancellation between the two integrals in Eq. (
S47
), and thus the conclusion that
I_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{mat}}(q_{\parallel})=0
.

The remaining piece is the vacuum contribution.
This can be computed with the standard Feynman-parameter trick as
[
71
,
*Baier:1991gg
,
*Fukushima:2015wck
,
74
]

\begin{split}I_{\parallel}(q_{\parallel})=I^{\mathrm{vac}}_{\parallel}(q_{\parallel})&=-\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}p_{\parallel}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\mathrm{tr}\biggl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\gamma^{\mu}_{\parallel}(p_{\mu\parallel}+q_{\mu\parallel})}\gamma^{0}\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\gamma^{\nu}_{\parallel}p_{\nu\parallel}}\biggr]\\
&=-\frac{1}{\pi}\cdot\frac{q^{2}_{\parallel}-q_{0}^{2}}{q_{\parallel}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\cdot\frac{q_{z}^{2}}{q_{0}^{2}-q_{z}^{2}},\end{split}

(S48)

where we rotate the contour of the
p_{0}
-integral from
-\mathrm{i}\infty\to\mathrm{i}\infty
to
-\infty\to\infty
.
This result is equal to the polarization function in the
(1+1)
-dimensional QED
divided
by a factor of
(-\mathrm{i}e)^{2}

[
74
]
.
However, Eq. (19) of Ref.
[
21
]
has an opposite sign.

Performing the analytic continuation and taking the static limit, we have

\lim_{\omega\to 0}I_{\parallel}(q_{0}\to\omega+\mathrm{i}\delta,q_{z})=-\frac{1}{\pi}.

(S49)

Finally, the correlation function reads

\lim_{\omega\to 0}G_{\mathrm{E}}^{00k}(q_{0}\to\omega+\mathrm{i}\delta,{\boldsymbol{q}})=\biggl(-\frac{1}{\pi}\biggr)\cdot\biggl(-\frac{eB}{8\pi}\biggr)(q_{\perp}^{k}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon^{3jk}q_{\perp}^{j})+O(|q_{\perp}|/\sqrt{|eB|}).

(S50)

Therefore, the vorticity-induced term reads

\delta j_{\mathrm{vortical}}^{0}=\frac{eB}{8\pi^{2}}\epsilon^{3jk}\partial_{j}h_{0k}=-\frac{eB\Omega}{4\pi^{2}}

(S51)

with
\Omega=-\epsilon^{3jk}\partial_{j}h_{0k}/2
.
This is consistent with the isothermal transport coefficient that is derived from the partition function in the main text.
In summary, the three opposite signs in Eqs. (
S28
), (
S40
), and (
S48
) explain why Eq. (
S51
) has the extra minus sign compared with the main result in Ref.
[
21
]
.
This concludes that the linear-response analysis in the strong magnetic field contains both the orbital and spin contributions.

S3.3
Linear response under a perturbative rotation

We show that the same charge density is reproduced from the linear-response theory in terms of a weak angular velocity
\Omega
based on the correspondence in Eq. (
S27
).
The kinetic angular momentum operator explicitly appears in this analysis.

Let us consider the charge density
\delta j^{0}=\lambda\Omega
generated by the perturbation Hamiltonian (
S24
), i.e.,

H_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)=-\Omega J_{\mathrm{kin}}(x),\quad J_{\mathrm{kin}}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\epsilon^{3jk}\,x^{j}\bar{\psi}(\gamma^{0}D^{k}+\gamma^{k}D^{0})\psi.

(S52)

As in the above analysis, the transport coefficient
\lambda
is computed from the imaginary-time correlator as

\lambda=\lim_{{\boldsymbol{q}}\to\boldsymbol{0}}\Bigl[\lim_{\omega\to 0}G_{\mathrm{E}}(q_{0}\to\omega+\mathrm{i}\delta,{\boldsymbol{q}})\Bigr],\quad G_{\mathrm{E}}(x,y)=-\Bigl\langle T_{\tau}j^{0}(x)\Bigl(-J_{\mathrm{kin}}(y)\Bigr)\Bigr\rangle.

(S53)

We compute the above correlator in the LLL limit at the one-loop order.
A similar correlator has been directly evaluated with the covariant derivative
D_{\mu}
in the above and in Ref.
[
21
]
.
Here, it is more useful to evaluate the covariant derivative as the kinetic angular momentum operator.
That is, we can utilize the relation

\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\epsilon^{3jk}\,x^{j}\bar{\psi}_{\mathrm{LLL}}\gamma^{0}D^{k}\psi_{\mathrm{LLL}}=\frac{1}{2}\bar{\psi}_{\mathrm{LLL}}\gamma^{0}L_{\mathrm{kin}}\psi_{\mathrm{LLL}}=-\frac{s}{2}\bar{\psi}_{\mathrm{LLL}}\gamma^{0}\psi_{\mathrm{LLL}}.

(S54)

The kinetic angular momentum is evaluated as
L_{\mathrm{kin}}\psi_{\mathrm{LLL}}=-s\psi_{\mathrm{LLL}}+\text{(higher LL)}
based on Eq. (2) in the main text.
Thus, the angular momentum is expressed with the charge density:

\begin{split}J_{\mathrm{kin}}(y)&=-\frac{s}{2}j^{0}(y)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\epsilon^{3jk}\,x^{j}\bar{\psi}\gamma^{k}D^{0}\psi.\end{split}

(S55)

Since the second term should vanish due to the spinor trace, the correlator
G_{\mathrm{E}}
takes the same form as the density-density correlator:

\begin{split}G_{\mathrm{E}}(x,y)&=(-1)\cdot\biggl(-\frac{s}{2}\biggr)\mathrm{tr}\Bigl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}S_{\mathrm{LLL}}(x,y)\gamma^{0}S_{\mathrm{LLL}}(y,x)\Bigr],\end{split}

(S56)

where the additional minus sign is from the fermion loop.
The momentum representation of the correlator reads

\begin{split}G_{\mathrm{E}}(q)&=\frac{s}{2}\,T\sum_{n}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\mathrm{tr}\Bigl[\mathcal{P}_{+}\gamma^{0}\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(p+q)\gamma^{0}\tilde{S}_{\mathrm{LLL}}(p)\Bigr].\end{split}

(S57)

One can further decompose the integral as
G_{\mathrm{E}}(q)=\tilde{I}_{\perp}(q_{\perp})I_{\parallel}(q_{\parallel})
with the same
I_{\parallel}(q_{\parallel})
as in Eq. (
S44
).
The transverse part
\tilde{I}_{\perp}(q_{\perp})
is slightly different from Eq. (
S45
), but is readily evaluated in the same manner as

\begin{split}\tilde{I}_{\perp}(q_{\perp})=2s\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}p_{\perp}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-(|p_{\perp}|^{2}+|p_{\perp}+q_{\perp}|^{2})/|eB|}=\frac{eB}{4\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-|q_{\perp}|^{2}/|eB|}.\end{split}

(S58)

Plugging Eqs. (
S49
) and (
S58
) into Eq. (
S53
), we finally arrive at

\lambda=-\frac{eB}{4\pi^{2}}.

(S59)

This result again agrees with the induced charge density derived in the main text.
The origin of the negative sign is clear from Eq. (
S54
).
We reiterate that, according to the correspondence in Eq. (
S27
), this analysis is equivalent to the analysis leading to Eq. (
S51
) with the metric perturbation.
