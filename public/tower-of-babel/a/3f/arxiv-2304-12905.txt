FASTER THAN FAST: ACCELERATING THE GRIFFIN-LIM ALGORITHM
Rossen Nenov, Dang-Khoa Nguyen, Peter Balazs

Abstract
The phase retrieval problem is found in various areas of applications of engineering and applied physics. It is also a very active field of research in mathematics, signal processing and machine learning. In this paper, we present an accelerated version of the well known Fast Griffin-Lim algorithm (FGLA) for the phase retrieval problem in a general setting. It has increased the speed of convergence, and most importantly, the limit points of the generated sequence can reach a significantly smaller error than the ones generated by FGLA. We will give a motivation of the acceleration and compare it numerically to its predecessors and other algorithms typically used to solve similar problems.

The
Griffin-Lim algorithm
(GLA) is a famous method to solve the phase retrieval problem in the time-frequency setting
[
1
]
. It aims to solve two related but different problems, namely constructing a signal from a valid spectrogram with no phase information (Phase recovery) and constructing a signal from a synthetic or modified
Short-Time Fourier transform
(STFT) magnitude. These problems can be seen as an optimization task and many different algorithms from convex and nonconvex optimization have been applied to them
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

Beyond acoustics, phase retrieval problems are found in many different fields involving transformations of signals or data
[
6
,
7
]
. In a general setting, the phase retrieval problem can be formulated as the task to find elements in the intersection of the linear subspace
C_{1}
, i.e. the transformation domain, and of the nonconvex set
C_{2}
, i.e. those coefficients having a fixed magnitude. The GLA projects the iterates alternatingly onto
C_{1}
and onto
C_{2}
. In
[
8
]
applying the
Fast iterative shrinkage-threshold algorithm
(FISTA) was considered for this problem, which resulted in the formulation of FGLA. It outperforms GLA in numerical experiments in speed of convergence and converges to points with smaller error. Since its introduction FGLA was applied for the phase retrieval in many different types of applications
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.

Over time different variations of FISTA have been designed for problems beyond the convex setting. This inspired us to use a more flexible algorithm that better fitted for this problem setting and apply it to phase retrieval. It resulted in the formulation of the
Accelerated Griffin-Lim algorithm
(AGLA), which outperforms the established FGLA.

2
THE PHASE RETRIEVAL PROBLEM

Let
T
be a matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{M\times L}
with full column rank. We will call
T
the transformation matrix, like the discrete short time Fourier transform
[
12
]
or the analysis operator of a finite frame
[
13
]
. Let
s\in[0,\infty)^{M}
be the vector of given magnitudes. The phase retrieval problem can be expressed as finding a signal
x^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{L}
, such that the magnitude of the coefficients of the transformation of
x^{*}
, namely
\left|Tx^{*}\right|
are as close as possible to
s
, where
\left|\cdot\right|
is understood componentwise.
The STFT and other finite frames used in applications are redundant, i.e.
M>L
and
T
maps to a true subspace of the coefficient space
\mathbb{C}^{M}
. Therefore, a nontrivial dependence between the magnitudes and the phases arises, which make the questions about feasibility, uniqueness and stability of the phase retrieval problem difficult to answer. These problems have their own interest and they have been studied in numerous works such as
[
14
,
15
]
.
For results regarding critically sampled transforms, i.e.
M=L
, such as the Discrete Fourier Transform we refer to
[
16
]
.

The phase retrieval problem can be formulated mathematically in the following form: The signal
x^{*}
is the solution of

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{L}}\left\|\left|Tx\right|-s\right\|_{2}.

(1)

Following the formulation of
[
8
]
, this problem can be rewritten as finding the intersection or the closest points of two sets.

By
C_{1}
we denote the set of possible sequences of coefficients of our transformation, i.e. the range of the transformation matrix
T
:

\displaystyle C_{1}=\{c\in\mathbb{C}^{M}\mid\exists x\in\mathbb{C}^{L}:c=Tx\}.

This set is a linear subspace and therefore convex. Using the pseudo-inverse
T^{\dagger}
, which is well defined, the orthogonal projection onto
C_{1}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle P_{C_{1}}(c)=TT^{\dagger}c.

By
C_{2}
we denote the set of coefficients, whose magnitude coincide with the given magnitude measurements:

\displaystyle C_{2}=\{c\in\mathbb{C}^{M}\mid\left|c_{i}\right|=s_{i}\quad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,M\}\}.

This set is compact by definition.
The projection
P_{C_{2}}
onto
C_{2}
is equivalent to scaling the elements of
c
to have magnitude
s
, without changing the phase, which can be written as

\displaystyle(P_{C_{2}}(c))_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{s_{i}c_{i}}{\left|c_{i}\right|}&\text{ if }c_{i}\neq 0,\\
s_{i}&\text{ if }c_{i}=0.\end{cases}

One can verify that
P_{C_{2}}(c)
maps
c
onto
C_{2}
and gives its closest point in
C_{2}
.
The problem (
1
) can be seen as the task of finding coefficients in
C_{1}
that are as close as possible to
C_{2}
, which motivates rewriting the objective as

\displaystyle\min_{c\in\mathbb{C}^{M}}\delta_{C_{1}}(c)+\frac{1}{2}d_{C_{2}}^{2}(c),

(2)

where the indicator function
\delta_{C}
of a set
C
is defined as

\displaystyle\delta_{C}(c)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }c\in C,\\
+\infty&\text{else,}\end{cases}

and the distance function
d_{C}
to a compact set
C
is defined as

\displaystyle d_{C}(c)=\min_{y\in C}\left\|c-y\right\|.

Solving the problem (
1
) is equivalent to finding a solution
c^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
of (
2
) since
x^{*}=T^{\dagger}c^{*}
is then a solution to (
1
).

3
GRIFFIN-LIM BASED ALGORITHMS

The algorithms will be initialized by a vector
c_{0}

\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
which matches the magnitudes of
s
and the phases of each component can be zero or chosen freely. We fix an integer
N
, which denotes the amount of iterations of the algorithms.

The paper
[
1
]
aims to solve the phase retrieval problem via alternating projections and proposed the following algorithm:

Algorithm 1

Griffin-Lim algorithm

Fix
phase
\angle c_{0}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}

Initialize
c_{0}=s\cdot e^{i\cdot\angle c_{0}}

\textbf{Iterate for }n=1,\dots,N

c_{n}=P_{C_{1}}(P_{C_{2}}(c_{n-1})).

Return

T^{\dagger}c_{N}

The method of alternating projections is a well-known method, which converges in various settings
[
17
]
, and for
GLA
it was proven that the function values of the iterates are nonincreasing and therefore converging
[
1
]
.

Motivated by the algorithm FISTA
[
18
]
, an inertial step was added in
[
8
]
, which resulted in the formulation of
FGLA
. It can be seen as
GLA
but with an enlarged effective stepsize, depending on the last iterates. The case
\alpha=0
coincides with
GLA
. The numerical experiments suggest that
\alpha\in[0,1)
has to hold to have stability in the algorithm, which is coherent with the bounds on the constant
\alpha
in FISTA for the convex case.
It seems that the choice
\alpha=0.99
achieves the best empirical behavior
[
8
]
.

Algorithm 2

Fast Griffin-Lim algorithm

Fix
phase
\angle c_{0}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha>0

Initialize
c_{0}=s\cdot e^{i\cdot\angle c_{0}}
and
t_{0}=P_{C_{1}}(P_{C_{2}}(c_{0}))

\textbf{Iterate for }n=1,\dots,N

t_{n}=P_{C_{1}}(P_{C_{2}}(c_{n-1})),

c_{n}=t_{n}+\alpha_{n}(t_{n}-t_{n-1}).

Return

T^{\dagger}c_{N}

Recent developments of new algorithms based on FISTA have lead to many variations of the algorithm, which expanded the applications of inertial methods to certain nonconvex problems
[
19
,
20
]
. The paper
[
21
]
investigates a relaxation of the FISTA algorithm with two inertial sequences in connection with the minimization of the sum of a convex nonsmooth and a nonconvex differentiable function.

Our function
\delta_{C_{1}}
is nonsmooth and convex, while
d_{C_{2}}^{2}
is a nonconvex function and smooth everywhere except on

\displaystyle\{c\in\mathbb{C}^{M}\mid\exists i\in\{1,...,{M}\}:c_{i}=0\wedge s_{i}\neq 0\}.

We can still apply the algorithm to solve (
2
), but nothing can be deduced from the theoretical statements of previous works.

Algorithm 3

Accelerated Griffin-Lim algorithm

Fix
phase
\angle c_{0}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0

Initialize
c_{0}=s\cdot e^{i\cdot\angle c_{0}}
and
t_{0}=d_{0}=P_{C_{1}}(P_{C_{2}}(c_{0}))

\textbf{Iterate for }n=1,\dots,N

t_{n}=(1-\gamma)d_{n-1}+\gamma P_{C_{1}}(P_{C_{2}}(c_{n-1})),

c_{n}=t_{n}+\alpha(t_{n}-t_{n-1}),

d_{n}=t_{n}+\beta(t_{n}-t_{n-1}).

Return

T^{\dagger}c_{N}

GLA
converges to or stops at a point
c^{*}
with the property
c^{*}=P_{C_{1}}(P_{C_{2}}(c^{*}))

[
1
]
, which is not necessarily a global solution. The inertial step of
c_{n}
in
FGLA
and
AGLA
helps surpassing such points, if the last step
t_{n}-t_{n-1}
was sufficiently large. Therefore they are expected to converge to points of lower error than
GLA
. Combining with the nonprojected sequence
d_{n}
in
AGLA
will enhance this behavior even further, if the distance between the projected
c_{n}
and the nonprojected
d_{n}
is sufficiently large. In other words, we are not only taking the direction of the projected iterates into account, but also the direction of the iterates themselves. As can be observed in the numerical experiment, the additional inertia allows
FGLA
and
AGLA
to converge to points of lower error, and the relaxation in
AGLA
gives more flexibility in the choice of the inertial parameters.

4
FURTHER PROJECTION BASED ALGORITHMS

In this section we present two other algorithms, which also aim to save the phase retrieval problem, to compare them to
AGLA
. It was observed in
[
22
]
that these two in particular perform better than
GLA
and
FGLA
.

The
Relaxed Averaged Alternating Reflections
(
RAAR
) was proposed in
[
23
]
to solve the phase retrieval problem in Diffraction Imaging. It is a relaxation of the
Douglas-Rachford algorithm
, a well-known method for finding the intersection of two sets using reflections. Especially in the case, where the two sets intersect at a small angle, reflection-based methods converge faster than projection-based methods
[
22
]
. Numerical experiments suggest that
\lambda=0.9
is the best choice for speech signals
[
24
]
.

Algorithm 4

Relaxed Averaged Alternating Reflections

Fix
phase
\angle c_{0}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\lambda\leq 1

Initialize
c_{0}=s\cdot e^{i\cdot\angle c_{0}}

\textbf{Iterate for }n=1,\dots,N

c_{n+1}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(c_{n}+R_{C_{1}}(R_{C_{2}}(c_{n}))\right)+(1-\lambda)P_{C_{2}}(c_{n}),

where
R_{C}=2P_{C}-Id

Return

T^{\dagger}c_{N}

We will also compare our algorithm with the
Difference Map
(DM) as proposed in
[
25
]
for phase retrieval problems.

Algorithm 5

Difference Map

Fix
phase
\angle c_{0}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\rho\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

Initialize
c_{0}=s\cdot e^{i\cdot\angle c_{0}}

\textbf{Iterate for }n=1,\dots,N

t_{n}=P_{C_{2}}(c_{n})+\frac{1}{\rho}(P_{C_{2}}(c_{n})-c_{n}),

s_{n}=P_{C_{1}}(c_{n})+\frac{1}{\rho}(P_{C_{1}}(c_{n})-c_{n})

c_{n+1}=c_{n}+\rho(P_{C_{1}}(t_{n})-P_{C_{2}}(s_{n})).

Return

T^{\dagger}c_{N}

The parameter
\rho
can be chosen arbitrary, but numerical experiments show that the values close to 1 yield the best convergence. In
[
22
]
the optimal value was observed to be at
\rho=0.8
, which we also observed in our simulations. For the choice
\rho=1
,
DM
is nothing else than
RAAR
with
\lambda=1
. It is important to note, that the
DM
algorithm computes twice as many different projections per iteration than the other algorithms presented in this paper.

\displaystyle\text{SSNR}(c)=-10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\left\|\left|c\right|-s\right\|}{\left\|s\right\|}\right).

6
CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

In this paper we presented a new algorithm to solve the phase retrieval problem.
Compared to
GLA
and
FGLA
,
AGLA
converges in numerical examples faster and to better points with hardly any extra computational cost. We also saw that depending on the signal,
AGLA
can perform better than
DM
and
RAAR
. While these two algorithms sometimes find better critical points, they tend to have strong oscillations in their function values after a certain amount of iterations compared to
AGLA
. This sparks the interest in investigating the performance of hybrid regimes as proposed in
[
22
]
, where one runs either
DM
or
RAAR
for a set amount of iterations to escape the neighbourhood of bad local minima and then applies
AGLA
for the rest of the iterations. Furthermore, the question arises how well
AGLA
performs with different initialization, other window functions and lower redundancies, and if the optimal choices for the parameters might vary.

In our future research we will look into stating convergence results of
FGLA
and
AGLA
, namely proving convergence of the iterates
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and of the objective function values
\left(d_{C_{2}}^{2}(c_{n})\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
