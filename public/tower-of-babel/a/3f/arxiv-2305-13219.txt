Bicomplex Matrices and Operators: Jordan Forms, Invariant Subspace Lattice Diagrams, and Compact Operators
William Johnston, Rebecca G. Wahl

Abstract
This paper extends topics in linear algebra and operator theory for linear transformations on complex vector spaces to those on bicomplex Hilbert and Banach spaces. For example, Definition 3 for the first time defines a bicomplex vector space, its dimension, and its basis in terms of a corresponding vectorial idempotent representation, and the paper shows how an n × n n\times n bicomplex matrix’s idempotent representation leads to its bicomplex Jordan form and a description of its bicomplex invariant subspace lattice diagram. Similarly, the paper rigorously defines for the first time “bicomplex Banach and Hilbert” spaces, and then it expands the theory of compact operators on complex Banach and Hilbert spaces to those on bicomplex Banach and Hilbert spaces. In these ways, the paper shows that complex linear algebra and operator theory are not necessarily built upon the broadest and most natural set of scalars to study. Instead, when using the idempotent representation, such results surprisingly generalize in a straightforward way to the higher-dimensional case of bicomplex values.

.

A bicomplex vector space, taken over bicomplex scalars, is a set of well-defined elements, called “bicomplex vectors,” that may be decomposed using the bicomplex idempotent structure into complex idempotent components as

\{b_{k,j}\}=\{b_{1,k}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+b_{2,j}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\}

This paper examines such vector spaces with well-defined complete norms defined on each idempotent component, so that each component vector space forms a Banach space, calling such vector spaces “bicomplex Banach spaces.” In addition, it examines such vector spaces with well-defined inner products, so that each component vector space forms a Hilbert space, calling such vector spaces “bicomplex Hilbert spaces.” Examining the bicomplex Banach and Hilbert spaces’ idempotent structure, it will also define and examine types of linear transformations on such spaces.

Bicomplex Matrices

We first consider the extension of complex linear algebra to linear transformations on finite
n
-dimensional bicomplex vector spaces. In what follows in this section, each matrix is

A

Theorem 1
.

The Fundamental Theorem for Bicomplex Matrices:
Given bicomplex matrices
A=A_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+A_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
of size
m\times k
and
B=B_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+B_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
of size
k\times n
, then
A\cdot B=A_{1}B_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+A_{2}B_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
is
m\times n
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}^{2}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}
,
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}^{2}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}=0
,

A\cdot B=(A_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+A_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})(B_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+B_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})\\
\hskip 56.0pt=A_{1}B_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}^{2}+A_{1}B_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}+A_{2}B_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+A_{2}B_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}^{2}\\
\hskip 56.0pt=A_{1}B_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+A_{2}B_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
.

For an equivalent proof, denote
AB=[(ab)_{ij}]
, with
A=[a_{ik}]
and
B=[b_{kj}]
. Each term is

(ab)_{ij}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}a_{ik}b_{kj}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}(\hat{a}_{ik}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\tilde{a}_{ik}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})(\hat{b}_{kj}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\tilde{b}_{kj}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})\\
\hskip 60.5pt=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}(\hat{a}_{ik}\hat{b}_{kj}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\tilde{a}_{ik}\tilde{b}_{kj}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}(\hat{a}_{ik}\hat{b}_{kj})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}(\tilde{a}_{ik}\tilde{b}_{kj})\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Bicomplex Jordan Form:
Write
A=A_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+A_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
and put
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
in their (complex) Jordan forms as
A_{1}=P_{1}J_{1}P_{1}^{-1}
and
A_{2}=P_{2}J_{2}P_{2}^{-1}
. Then a Jordan form of
A
is
A=PJP^{-1}
, where
P=P_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
and
J=J_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+J_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are not unique (for example, the diagonal blocks for

and
J_{2}
may appear in any order), this Jordan form representation of

Proof.

Express the matrix in its idempotent form:

A=A_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+A_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=P_{1}J_{1}P_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}J_{2}P_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\\
\hskip 39.6pt=(P_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})(J_{1}P_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+J_{2}P_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})\\
\hskip 39.6pt=(P_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})(J_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+J_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})(P_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})=PJP^{-1}
.
∎

Corollary 4
.

Writing any vector
\vec{\zeta}\in\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}^{n}

\vec{\zeta}=\vec{\zeta}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\vec{\zeta}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

The subspace

An analysis that is similar to the development of the Jordan form also applies to other representations of bicomplex matrices, for example, to form a diagonalization of a self-adjoint bicomplex matrix. Begin with the following definitions.

Definition 4
.

The adjoint of a bicomplex matrix
A=[a_{ij}]
is its conjugate-transpose
A^{*}=[\overline{a}_{ji}]
.

Note this definition generalizes the adjoint of a complex matrix. Furthermore, if
\vec{\zeta},\vec{\omega}\in\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we define as in, for example,
[
11
]
, a bicomplex inner product as

\langle\vec{\zeta},\vec{\omega}\rangle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}\overline{\omega}_{i}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\zeta_{i1}\overline{\omega}_{i1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\zeta_{i2}\overline{\omega}_{i2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})=\langle\vec{\zeta}_{1},\vec{\omega}_{1}\rangle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\langle\vec{\zeta}_{2},\vec{\omega}_{2}\rangle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
.

The following theorem results.

Theorem 3
.

For any
n\times n
bicomplex matrix
A
and
\vec{\zeta},\vec{\omega}\in\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

\langle A\vec{\zeta},\vec{\omega}\rangle=\langle\vec{\zeta},A^{*}\vec{\omega}\rangle
.

Proof.

By Theorem 1, the definition of the bicomplex inner product, and the adjoint properties of complex matrices,

\langle A\vec{\zeta},\vec{\omega}\rangle=\langle A_{1}\vec{\zeta}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+A_{2}\vec{\zeta}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}},\vec{\omega}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\vec{\omega}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\rangle=\langle A_{1}\vec{\zeta}_{1},\vec{\omega}_{1}\rangle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\langle A_{2}\vec{\zeta}_{2},\vec{\omega}_{2}\rangle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\\
\indent\hskip 37.5pt=\langle\vec{\zeta}_{1},A_{1}^{*}\vec{\omega}_{1}\rangle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\langle\vec{\zeta}_{2},A_{2}^{*}\vec{\omega}_{2}\rangle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=\langle\vec{\zeta},A^{*}\vec{\omega}\rangle

∎

Theorem 4
.

The Spectral Theorem for Self-adjoint Matrices:
Any self-adjoint bicomplex matrix is unitarily diagonalizable, in the sense that

A=PDP^{-1}

for a (bicomplex) diagonal matrix
D
and a unitary matrix
P
.

Proof.

When
A=A_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+A_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, by the definition of a self-adjoint bicomplex matrix, both
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are complex self-adjoint, and then the spectral theorem implies

A_{1}=P_{1}D_{1}P_{1}^{-1}
and
A_{2}=P_{2}D_{2}P_{2}^{-1}

A=A_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+A_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=P_{1}D_{1}P_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}D_{2}P_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\\
\hskip 40.0pt=(P_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})(D_{1}P_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+D_{2}P_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})\\
\indent\hskip 28.0pt=(P_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})(D_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+D_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})(P_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}})=PDP^{-1}
,

with each matrix having the properties required in the theorem statement. For example,

P_{2}
may be chosen as (complex) unitary, and then

P^{*}=P_{1}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}^{*}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=P_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+P_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=P^{-1}
.

∎

Examples

Example 1:
Let
A=\left[\begin{matrix}0&\zeta\\
\overline{\zeta}&0\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}0&\zeta_{1}\\
\overline{\zeta_{1}}&0\end{matrix}\right]\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\left[\begin{matrix}0&\zeta_{2}\\
\overline{\zeta_{2}}&0\end{matrix}\right]\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

\lambda_{1}^{2}-|\zeta_{1}|^{2}=0
, or
\lambda_{1}=\pm|\zeta_{1}|
, and similarly

\left[\begin{matrix}|\zeta|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}\\
\overline{\zeta}\end{matrix}\right]

\overline{\zeta}\end{matrix}\right]

4
Bicomplex Compact Operators on Banach Spaces

This section shows how the theory of operators on complex Banach spaces extends, via an operator’s idempotent representation, to a corresponding theory of so-called bicomplex compact operators on bicomplex Banach spaces.

Definition 5
.

A bicomplex Hilbert space is of the form
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\mathcal{H}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are complex Hilbert spaces with the same dimension. The bicomplex inner product on any elements
\vec{\zeta},\vec{\omega}\in\mathcal{H}
is defined via complex inner products on the idempotent components of
\vec{\zeta}
and
\vec{\omega}
as

\langle\vec{\zeta},\vec{\omega}\rangle=\langle\vec{\zeta}_{1},\vec{\omega}_{1}\rangle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\langle\vec{\zeta}_{2},\vec{\omega}_{2}\rangle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}

A set of bicomplex vectors
\{\vec{\zeta}_{k}\}
is orthogonal in
\mathcal{H}
when
\langle\vec{\zeta}_{n},\vec{\zeta}_{m}\rangle=0
for
n\neq m
and orthonormal when in addition the hyperbolic norm of each vector in the set, defined via idempotent component norms for
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
according to
\|\vec{\zeta}_{n}\|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}=\|\vec{\zeta}_{n,1}\|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\|\vec{\zeta}_{n,2}\|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, equals 1.

Similarly, a bicomplex Banach space is of the form
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\mathcal{B}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, where
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are complex Banach spaces with the same dimension.

Such spaces are often called “
\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}
-modules” in the literature (see, for example,
[
10
]
), where they have been studied in detail. Definition 5 appropriately names such a space as a Hilbert and/or Banach space, as the idempotent arrangement is uniquely structured to produce broad general theories for linear operators on such spaces. This and the next section develops such theory for bicomplex compact operators, defined here:

Definition 6
.

An operator
K:\ \mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
mapping a bicomplex Banach space
\mathcal{B}
to itself is compact if for every bounded set
S\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, the set
K(S)
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{B}
, which means that every infinite subset of
K(S)\equiv\{K\vec{s}:\vec{s}\in S\}
has at least one limit point in its (hyperbolic) norm closure. This is equivalent to every bounded sequence
\{x_{n}\}
of vectors in
\mathcal{B}
producing the sequence
\{Kx_{n}\}
with a hyperbolic norm-convergent subsequence.

Note importantly that hyperbolic norm convergence in
\mathcal{B}
happens exactly when norm convergence of the idempotent components happen in both complex spaces
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
. In such a way, the actions of any operator in
\mathcal{B}
follows from actions of the corresponding operator elements acting on
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
. Here,
T
acts on
v=v_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+v_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\in\mathcal{B}
according to
Tv=T_{1}v_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+T_{2}v_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
. The following lemma notes the impact of those facts on compact operators.

Lemma 1
.

For any operator
T=T_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+T_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
on a bicomplex Banach space
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\mathcal{B}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, the operators
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are compact on
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
respectively iff
T
is compact.

Proof.

For
i=1,2
, the compactness of
T_{i}
implies for every bounded set
S_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{i}
, the set
T_{i}(S_{i})
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{B}_{i}
; i.e., every infinite subset of
T_{i}(S_{i})
has at least one limit point
x_{i}
in its (complex Banach space) norm closure. But then every hyperbolic bounded set
S=S_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+S_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, which must have
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
bounded in the corresponding complex Banach space norm, has at least one limit point
x=x_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+x_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
in its (hyperbolic) norm closure. Hence
T
is compact by definition.

Conversely, assume
T=T_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+T_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
is compact and take any bounded sequences
\{x_{1,n}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\{x_{2,n}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}
. Then
\{x_{n}\}=\{x_{1,n}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+x_{2,n}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\}
is a bounded set of vectors in
\mathcal{B}
(in the hyperbolic norm sense), producing the sequence
\{Tx_{n}\}
with a hyperbolic norm-convergent subsequence
\{Tx_{{n}_{k}}\equiv T_{1}x_{{1,n}_{k}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+T_{2}x_{{2,n}_{k}}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\}
. But the hyperbolic norm structure then implies that each idempotent component
T_{1}x_{{1,n}_{k}}
and
T_{2}x_{{2,n}_{k}}
is norm-convergent in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
, respectively. Hence
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
each satisfy the definition of a compact operator.
∎

The (hyperbolic) operator norm of any (bicomplex) bounded operator
T
is naturally defined as
\|T\|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}=\|T_{1}\|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\|T_{2}\|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
. Also, for example, it is easy to see that the identity operator
I
is not compact on any infinite-dimensional bicomplex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. For we can choose an infinite sequence
x_{n}
of
\mathcal{H}
to be the orthonormal set of basis elements for the Hilbert space, which are each distance (in hyperbolic norm) 1 apart. Then
I(x_{n})=x_{n}
has no convergent subsequence. A similar result is seen true for the identity operator on any infinite-dimensional bicomplex Banach space by applying Lemma 2 below.

The spectrum of a bicomplex operator is defined similarly to the spectrum of a complex operator.

Definition 7
.

For a bounded operator
T
:

1.

The spectrum of
T
is
\sigma(T)=\{\zeta\in\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}\ \colon\zeta I-T\mbox{ is not invertible }\}
.

2.

The point spectrum of
T
is the set of eigenvalues
\sigma_{p}(T)
; in other words, the set of
\lambda\in\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}
that satisfy

(\lambda I-T)v=0

for some
v=v_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+v_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\in\mathcal{B}
with both
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
nonzero, where
v
is called an eigenvector for
\lambda
. Any eigenvalue
\lambda=\lambda_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
produces for
T=T_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+T_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
for
T_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
for
T_{2}
on the associated spaces
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
, respectively, with associated eigenvectors
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, respectively.

A spectral theory for bicomplex compact operators holds on bicomplex Banach spaces. The following theorem describes that fact, and is well known in the case of compact operators on complex Banach spaces (see, for example

[
8
, pp. 377–378]
).

Theorem 5
.

For a bicomplex compact operator
K:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}
with
\mathcal{B}
a bicomplex Banach space,

1.

Every invertible
\lambda\in\sigma(K)
is an eigenvalue of
K
.

2.

The eigenvalues can only accumulate at noninvertible values. If the dimension of
\mathcal{B}
is not finite, then
\sigma(K)
must contain 0, even if 0 is not an eigenvalue.

3.

\sigma(K)
is countable; in other words, its elements can be listed as either a finite list or as
\sigma(K)=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots\}
.

The theorem’s proof follows from the fact that
K
on
\mathcal{B}
decomposes into
K=K_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+K_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, where
K_{i}
is compact on
\mathcal{B}_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Applying the fact that the theorem is well known and true on complex Banach spaces, the proof of each part follows immediately.

Furthermore, the next two lemmas, which are historically important and true for operators on complex Banach spaces, are true for bicomplex Banach spaces as well. The first uses the concept of an infimum of a set of hyperbolic numbers
\eta=\eta_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\eta_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
for which
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, defined as
\inf\{\eta\}=\inf\{\eta_{1}\}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\inf\{\eta_{2}\}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
.

Lemma 2
.

Riesz’s Lemma.
For a non-dense subspace
X=X_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+X_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
of a bicomplex Banach space
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\mathcal{B}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, given
0<r<1
, there is an element
y\in\mathcal{B}
with
\|y\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1
and
\inf\limits_{x\in X}\|x-y\|_{\mathbb{H}}=r
.

Proof.

As mentioned, the lemma holds for each of the associated operators on the complex Banach spaces:
X_{1}
in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
X_{2}
in
\mathcal{B}_{2}
, in the sense that it produces a vector
y_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
y_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}
with
\|y_{i}\|=1
and
\inf\limits_{x\in X_{i}}\|x-y_{i}\|=r
for
i=1,2
. Then the vector
y=y_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+y_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
satisfies the lemma, since
\|y\|_{\mathbb{H}}=1\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+1\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=1
and
\inf\limits_{x\in X}\|x-y\|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}=r\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+r\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}=r
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

For a compact operator
K=K_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+K_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
on a bicomplex Banach space
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\mathcal{B}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
and for which
I-K
is one-to-one, the range of
I-K
is closed in the hyperbolic norm.

Proof.

The lemma holds for operators on a complex Banach space; see, for example,
[
7
]
. Apply that fact to each of
K_{1}
on
\mathcal{B}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}
and
K_{2}
on
\mathcal{B}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, producing, since
I-K_{1}
must be one-to-one on
\mathcal{B}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}
and
I-K_{2}
must be one-to-one on
\mathcal{B}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, closed ranges for
I-K_{1}
and
I-K_{2}
. But then the range of
I-K
is closed as desired.
∎

Bicomplex Compact Operators on Hilbert Spaces

Similar as for the material in the last section, important theorems describe the structure of a bicomplex compact operator
K
on a bicomplex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\mathcal{H}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
. The theorems result from corresponding theory for each complex compact operator
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
that form the idempotent components of
K
and act on the complex Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, respectively.

Theorem 6
.

For any operator
T=T_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+T_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
on a bicomplex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\mathcal{H}_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, if there is a sequence
\{K_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of bicomplex compact operators with
\|T-K_{n}\|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}\to 0
, then
T
is compact.

Proof.

Since the (hyperbolic) operator norm of any bicomplex operator
Y
is calculated via idempotent complex operator norms as
\|Y\|_{{}_{\mathbb{H}}}=\|Y_{1}\|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\|Y_{2}\|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
, there are two (idempotent component) sequences of complex compact operators
K_{n,i}
such that
\|T_{i}-K_{n,i}\|\to 0
for
i=1,2
. Then, since the theorem is true for any operator on a complex Hilbert space (cf.
[
8
, p. 367]
), it must be the case that the idempotent components
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are both compact. But then
T
is compact by Lemma 1.
∎

Since every finite-rank bicomplex operator in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
(of the form

T(f)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\langle f,g_{i}\rangle g_{i}

for hyperbolic nonnegative bicomplex
\sigma_{i}
and
g_{i}
chosen as orthogonal in
\mathcal{H}
) is compact (as it splits into compact operators on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
), Theorem 6 says any bicomplex operator norm limit of a bicomplex finite-rank operator is compact. But just as for complex compact operators on complex Hilbert spaces, the converse is also true, as the next theorem points out. See
[
5
, p. 11]
for a parallel statement of the next theorem.

Theorem 7
.

Every compact operator
K
on a bicomplex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is an operator norm limit of finite-rank bicomplex operators. In fact,
K
takes the form

K(f)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}\langle f,g_{i}\rangle g_{i}

for hyperbolic nonnegative bicomplex
\sigma_{i}
, and with
g_{i}
chosen as orthogonal in
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Write
K=K_{1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+K_{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
in its idempotent complex Hilbert space compact operator components, and express both
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
as operator norm limits (in each idempotent complex Hilbert space) of finite-rank operators:

K_{j}(f)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i,j}\langle f,g_{i,j}\rangle g_{i,j}
, for
j=1,2
.

Note
\sigma_{i,j}
may be chosen as nonnegative and each set of vectors
\{g_{i,j}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
may be chosen as an orthogonal family of vectors for
j=1,2
. Recombining the idempotent structures gives

K(f)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}\langle f,g_{i}\rangle g_{i}
,

where
\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i,1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+\sigma_{i,2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}
are nonnegative hyperbolic numbers and the set of vectors
\{g_{i}=g_{i,1}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{1}}+g_{i,2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}_{2}}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
forms an orthogonal family in the bicomplex inner product structure.
∎

6
Conclusion

This paper’s results continue analysis found already in many other articles that show how natural it is to use the idempotent structure on both the vector elements of a Hilbert or Banach space as well as the idempotent structure of operators. The idempotent approach, as opposed to misguided efforts that continue to examine the Euclidean rectangular coordinates, easily extends results in linear algebra and operator theory for complex Hilbert or Banach spaces to similar results on bicomplex spaces. This paper’s theory decidedly confirms that a full study of bicomplex operators is possible as a complete generalization of operator theory on complex operators when using the idempotent structure. The authors urge continued investigations into bicomplex spaces and bicomplex function theory, using the idempotent structures and hyperbolic norms to extend known complex-valued results.
