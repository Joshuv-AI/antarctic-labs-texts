2
Additional introduction of weighted energy distance

2.1
Definition of energy distance

The energy distance
(
Székely and
Rizzo 2013
)
is a measure of distance between two multivariate distributions. The energy distance between the
d
-dimensional independent random variables
\boldsymbol{G}
and
\boldsymbol{H}
where
\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{U}^{\prime}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\boldsymbol{G}
and
\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{V}^{\prime}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\boldsymbol{H}
is defined as

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H})=2\mathbb{E}(|\boldsymbol{U}-\boldsymbol{V}|_{d})-\mathbb{E}(|\boldsymbol{U}-\boldsymbol{U}^{\prime}|_{d})-\mathbb{E}(|\boldsymbol{V}-\boldsymbol{V}^{\prime}|_{d}),

(1)

where
\mathbb{E}(|\boldsymbol{U}|_{d})<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}(|\boldsymbol{V}|_{d})<\infty
so that the expectations are well-defined.

With the energy distance between two random vectors defined, the energy distance between two empirical CDFs can be similarly defined. Let
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{n}
be the empirical CDF of sample
\{\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
where
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\boldsymbol{G}
and
\boldsymbol{H}_{m}
be the empirical CDF of sample
\{\boldsymbol{V}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
where
\boldsymbol{V}_{i}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\boldsymbol{H}
. The energy distance between
\boldsymbol{G}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{H}_{n}
is defined as

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}({\boldsymbol{G}}_{n},\boldsymbol{H}_{m})&=\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}||\boldsymbol{U}_{i}-\boldsymbol{V}_{j}||_{2}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}||\boldsymbol{U}_{i}-\boldsymbol{U}_{j}||_{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}||\boldsymbol{V}_{i}-\boldsymbol{V}_{j}||_{2}.\end{split}

(2)

Note that, the definition of the energy distance ensures that it is rotation invariant in
d
-dimensional space. Here we provide some of the other key properties of the energy distance described in
Székely and
Rizzo 2013
. The first property, made rigorous in the following Lemma, is that the energy distance
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{H})
is non-negative and equals zero if and only if
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{V}
are identically distributed.

Lemma 2.1
(Proposition 1 from
Székely and
Rizzo 2013
).

If the
d
-dimensional random variables
\boldsymbol{U}\sim\boldsymbol{G}
and
\boldsymbol{V}\sim\boldsymbol{H}
are independent with
\mathbb{E}(|\boldsymbol{U}|_{d})+\mathbb{E}(|\boldsymbol{V}|_{d})<\infty
, and
\varphi_{G}
,
\varphi_{H}
denote their respective characteristic functions, then the energy distance can be expressed as

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H})=\frac{1}{c_{d}}\int_{R^{d}}\frac{|\varphi_{G}(t)-\varphi_{H}(t)|^{2}}{|t|^{d+1}_{d}}dt,

(3)

where

c_{d}=\frac{\pi^{(d+1)/2}}{\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}

(4)

and
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the complete gamma function. Thus
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H})\geq 0
with equality if and only if
\boldsymbol{G}
and
\boldsymbol{H}
are identical.

2.2
Alternative metric: maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)

Here, we introduce the maximum mean discrepancy as an alternative metric that can be used to construct the optimal weights. Maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), originally proposed in the context of machine learning, is defined as the distance between embeddings of distributions to reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS). With a different choice of kernel function, MMD provides a flexible definition of the distance between two distributions. In fact, the energy distance may be interpreted as a special case of MMD with a particular kernel function
(
Sejdinovic
et al. 2013
)
. As such, it is an attractive alternative to our use of the energy distance to characterize distributional imbalance, however we do not prove the analytical properties of the balancing weights with MMD as the metric. One can simply switch the energy distance to the MMD in our methods. The MMD between two empirical CDFs
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{H}_{m}
is defined as

\begin{split}\gamma^{2}_{ker}&=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k({\boldsymbol{U}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j})+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k({\boldsymbol{V}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{V}}_{j})-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k({\boldsymbol{U}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{V}}_{j}),\end{split}

(5)

where
k(\cdot,\cdot)
is a valid kernel function. A common choice is the Gaussian kernel

k_{v}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})=\exp\left(-\frac{||{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}||^{2}}{2v^{2}}\right),

(6)

where
v
is a key tuning parameter that must be specified. In the simulation experiments of this paper, we use this kernel with the median heuristic
v=\textnormal{median}\{||{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{V}}_{j}||^{2}|1<i<m,1<j<n\}

(
Garreau, Jitkrittum and
Kanagawa 2017
)
as the choice of the tuning parameter. However, the use of the median heuristic is not fully justified and may be suboptimal in many scenarios.

3
Generalization to the stochastic interventions

Stochastic interventions
(
Muñoz and Van
Der Laan 2012
)
are a type of alternative causal parameter for estimating the effects of either standard or continuous interventions. Instead of fixing the shifted intervention
q({\boldsymbol{X}},A)
to one single value, stochastic interventions view the shifted intervention as a random draw from a user-specified shifted intervention distribution. In
Muñoz and Van
Der Laan 2012
, the authors define the shifted intervention distribution as
\textnormal{Pr}(A=a|{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}})=f_{A|{\boldsymbol{X}}}(a-\delta({\boldsymbol{x}})|{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}})
where
f_{A|{\boldsymbol{X}}}(a|{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}})
is the conditional distribution of the treatment, and
\delta({\boldsymbol{x}})
is a known function that characterize the stochastic intervention. Stochastic interventions are a general framework that can incorporate a variety of estimands, such as the incremental propensity score intervention proposed by
Kennedy 2019
. Thus, extensions of our framework to stochastic interventions greatly increases the generality of our proposed methods.

Letting
Y^{\delta}
denote the counterfactual outcome under the stochastic intervention
\delta
, the
Muñoz and Van
Der Laan 2012
give the following identification result:

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(Y^{q})=&\int_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)f_{A|{\boldsymbol{X}}}(a-\delta({\boldsymbol{x}})|{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}})f_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}({\boldsymbol{a}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}da\\
=&\int_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{{\boldsymbol{X}}\in\mathcal{X}}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)dF_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},a+\delta({\boldsymbol{x}})).\end{split}

(7)

It is straightforward to prove that
F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},a+\delta({\boldsymbol{x}}))
is the CDF of random variable
({\boldsymbol{X}},Q({\boldsymbol{X}},A))=({\boldsymbol{X}},A-\delta({\boldsymbol{X}}))
where
Q({\boldsymbol{X}},A)=A-\delta({\boldsymbol{X}})
. Therefore, our result of the estimation of the causal effect of MTP can be easily generalized to stochastic interventions with the particular shifted distribution based on the function
q({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=a-\delta({\boldsymbol{x}})
.

In
(
Díaz
et al. 2021
)
, the authors considered a more general rule for stochastic intervention effect in the longitudinal studies and showed its connection to modified treatment policies. For simplicity, we only consider the scenario with one time point. For any user-given function
{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
, Theorem 1 of
(
Díaz
et al. 2021
)
gives the following identification result:

\mathbb{E}(Y^{{d}})=\mathbb{E}(\mu({\boldsymbol{X}},{d}({\boldsymbol{X}},A)))=\int_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{{\boldsymbol{X}}\in\mathcal{X}}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))dF_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},a).

(8)

We further require that
{d}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
(as a function of
a
) to be at least piece-wise invertible (assumption A3 in section
of the manuscript). With this assumption, we will have exactly the same identification result as in (
) with
q={d}
.

With the identification results for stochastic interventions, we can straightforwardly extend the error decomposition for MTPs to stochastic interventions. Assume we have the weighted estimator
\hat{\mu}^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}Y_{i}
, and denote
\mu^{q}=\mathbb{E}(Y^{q})
then we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}-\mu^{q}={}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu(\boldsymbol{x},a)d(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{X}},A}-F_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q})(\boldsymbol{x},a)}_{\text{error due to confounding bias}}

(9)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu(\boldsymbol{x},a)d(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}-F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q})}_{\text{sampling error}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\epsilon_{i},

(10)

which is identical to the error decomposition of MTP with a prespecified, deterministic function
q({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
.

Additional illustration of using weighted energy distance for MTPs

4.1
Simulation results for the method of choosing a feasible MTP scale

Here, we provide the simulation results to demonstrate the control of type I error for our method of choosing a feasible MTP scale. We use the same data-generating mechanism as the first simulation scenario in the manuscript Section
with sample size
n=100,200,400,800
, and number of covariates
p=10,20,40,80
with continuous treatment. The detailed data-generating mechanism is described in the Supplementary Material Section
6
. Under the null hypothesis, there is no difference between the observed population and the shifted population, i.e.,
q({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=a
. For the alternative hypothesis, we adopt
q({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=a-10
as our shift function. Two permutation strategies are under evaluation in this simulation study, the first strategy is the one we present in the manuscript section
where we randomly partition the dataset
\{{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
into two subsamples
\{{\boldsymbol{X}}_{I_{k}},A_{I_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{n/2}
and
\{{\boldsymbol{X}}_{J_{k}},A_{J_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{n/2}
. The permutation is conducted on the combined sample
\{\tilde{w}_{i},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{i},\tilde{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}=(\{w_{I_{k}},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{I_{k}},A_{I_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{n/2},\{w_{J_{k}},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{J_{k}},q({\boldsymbol{X}}_{J_{k}},A_{J_{k}})\}_{k=1}^{n/2})
of size
n
. The second strategy is to use the entire dataset in the permutation without any partition, i.e., we use the sample
\{\tilde{w}_{i},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{i},\tilde{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2}n=(\{w_{i},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{w_{j},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{j},q({\boldsymbol{X}}_{j},A_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n})
of size
2n
for the permutation. We explore the second strategy to assess the challenges presented by the samples being correlated.

We run 2000 experiments for each simulation condition. For each simulation experiment, the p-value for the test is calculated as the proportion of times that
T_{b}>T
among the
b=1,...,B
permutations, where
T_{b}
is the weighted energy distance of the two permuted samples for the
b
th permutation and
T
is the weighted energy distance of the weighted observed sample and the MTP-shifted sample (see the main manuscript Section
for the definition). Under the null hypothesis of no population shift, we examine the control of type I error rate for our test by evaluating whether the p-values are uniformly distributed among
[0,1]
. Under the alternative hypothesis of existing population imbalance, we compare the power of the two tests with different permutation strategies. The power is estimated by the proportion of p-values smaller than
0.05
.

and

[0,1]

Under the null hypothesis, both strategies 1 and 2 control the type I error around the nominal level of 0.05. Under the alternative hypothesis of existing population imbalance, the first method has the larger power in most scenarios, especially when the power is not close to 1. From the simulation results, although the second strategy includes a larger sample size for the permutation test, the correlation among the two samples
\{w_{i},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{w_{j},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{j},q({\boldsymbol{X}}_{j},A_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
may actually decrease the power. Therefore, we adopt the first strategy which is formally presented in the main manuscript.

4.2
Evaluation of arbitrary weights for a given MTP and dataset

This subsection provides additional illustration of the correlation between the weighted energy distance after covariate balancing and the corresponding estimation error. This demonstrates the effectiveness our method of choosing balancing methods with the smallest weighted energy distance. Figures

depicts the correlation between the weighted energy distance and the estimation error for four distinct weights: naive weights (which equally weight all sample points), random forest weights, logistic class weights, and Poisson density weights (which match the true data generating mechanism). The figure displays simulation results for

and

Each point on the graph represents the energy distance (x-axis) and the estimation error (y-axis) of one weight, and a line corresponds to a particular simulation dataset. Within each simulation scenario, there is a noticeable trend between the weighted energy distance and the absolute value of the estimation error, suggesting that the method with the smallest energy distance tends to yield the smallest estimation error for a given dataset.

We further demonstrate the performance of this method using all 16 simulation conditions (with

From the figure, we can also see that our method for choosing different balancing methods generally performs better for larger sample sizes and more complex scenarios (with more covariates). However, the energy distance still serves as a good criterion to determine the best weighting approach for a given MTP and dataset.

5
Energy balancing estimator

5.1
An unpenalized energy balancing estimator

In this section, we provide an unpenalized version of our energy balancing estimator (which we refer as energy balancing estimator). As stated in the paper, the existence of extreme weights is a key hurdle for a weighted estimator to achieve root-
n
consistency. Therefore, the following unpenalized energy balancing estimator requires additional restrictions for extreme weights in order to be root-n consistent. The energy balancing weights
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n}
are defined as the weights
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n}=\{w^{e}_{i,n}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n}\in&\underset{{\boldsymbol{w}}=\{w_{1},...,w_{n}\}}{\arg\min}\mathcal{E}(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{X}},A},F_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q})\\
&\textrm{subject to}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=n\textrm{ ,and }w_{i}\geq 0\textrm{ for all }i.\end{split}

(11)

The corresponding energy balancing estimator of the mean potential outcome under the MTP is

\hat{\mu}^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i,n}^{e}Y_{i}.

(12)

Like the penalized energy balancing weights, the purpose of the energy balancing weights is to minimize the energy distance between the weighted empirical CDF and the MTP-shifted empirical CDF. As shown in (
) and Theorem
in the main text, the energy distance is a measure of the discrepancy between two distributions. Thus, by using the energy balancing weights, the weighted empirical distribution and the shifted empirical distribution (under MTP) are balanced. Consequently, we can expect that the corresponding energy balancing estimate will in general have a small bias due to the connection between the distributional balance and bias in estimating MTP effects. Analogous to the penalized energy balancing weights, we prove the following properties of the energy balancing weights. Theorem
proves that by minimizing the weighted energy distance, the energy balancing weights makes the weighted empirical CDF converges to the true CDF under the MTP.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume
\mathbb{E}(||{\boldsymbol{X}}||_{2}|A)<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}(||{\boldsymbol{X}}||_{2})<\infty
, and assumption A0-A3 in the main text Section
hold. Let
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}^{e}
be the energy balancing weights. Then, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}^{e},{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)

(13)

almost surely for every continuity point
({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\in(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})
. Furthermore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}^{e},{\boldsymbol{X}},A},F_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q})=0

(14)

holds almost surely.

Following the above result, we directly have the asymptotic unbiasness of the energy balancing estimator
\hat{\mu}^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n}}
by applying the Portmanteau Theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
5.1
hold, and the conditional mean (potential) outcome
\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
is bounded and continuous on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})
. Then,
\hat{\mu}^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n}}
is an asymptotically unbiased estimator of
\mu^{q}
.

To ensure root-
n
consistency of the energy balancing estimator one can impose a restriction to avoid these extreme weights. One approach is to introduce an additional penalization term for the sum of squared weights, resulting in penalized energy balancing weights (see the main paper for more detail). Alternatively, one could also assume or impose in the optimization problem that the energy balancing weights meet the condition
w^{e}_{i,n}\leq Cn^{1/3}
for all
i=1,...,n
, where
C
is a real number. This assumption of no extreme weights is essential for the following theorem demonstrating the root-
n
consistency of the energy balancing estimator. Although we assume this holds, it is straighforward to impose this condition in the optimization problem as all theoretical results can straightforwardly be proved with such a modified optimization problem as in
Huling, Greifer and
Chen 2023
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Assume the conditions in Theorem
5.1
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the native space induced by the radial kernel
\Phi(.)=-||.||_{2}
on
\mathcal{X}
. Suppose the following mild conditions hold:

•

C-1:

\mu(\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}

•

C-2:
Var
[\mu({\boldsymbol{X}},A)]<\infty

•

C-3:
Var
[Y|{\boldsymbol{X}}={\boldsymbol{x}},A=a]
is bounded over
({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{a}})\in({\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{A}})
.

•

C-4:

\mathbb{E}[g^{2}({\boldsymbol{W}},{\boldsymbol{W}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{W}}^{\prime\prime},{\boldsymbol{W}}^{\prime\prime\prime})]\leq\infty
where
{\boldsymbol{W}},{\boldsymbol{W}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{W}}^{\prime\prime},{\boldsymbol{W}}^{\prime\prime\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}
is a vector with
{\boldsymbol{W}}=({\boldsymbol{X}},A)
,
h({\boldsymbol{w}})=\frac{f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)}{f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)}
, and the kernel function
g(.)
is defined as:

5.2
Implementation detail for minimizing the weighted energy distance

In this section, we provide the implementation details of our algorithm for solving the unpenalized and penalized energy balancing weights. First, consider the optimization problem
11
for unpenalized energy balancing weights:

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n}\in&\underset{{\boldsymbol{w}}=\{w_{1},...,w_{n}\}}{\arg\min}\mathcal{E}(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{X}},A},F_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q})\\
&\textrm{subject to}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=n\textrm{ ,and }w_{i}\geq 0\textrm{ for all }i.\end{split}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(

\displaystyle F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{X}},A},F_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q})={}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bigg\{2\times\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{k}}(A_{k})w_{i}||({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i})-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},q_{j}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},A_{k}))||_{2}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{i}w_{k}||({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i})-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},A_{k})||_{2}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i})}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}}(A_{i})I_{j^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{k}}(A_{k})||({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},q_{j}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i}))-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},q_{j^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},A_{k}))||_{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bigg\{2\times\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ w_{i}||({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i})-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},q({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},A_{k}))||_{2}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{i}w_{k}||({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i})-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},A_{k})||_{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}||({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},q({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i}))-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},q({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},A_{k}))||_{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}\mathbb{Q}{\boldsymbol{w}}+2{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}\mathbf{b}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}||({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},q({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i}))-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},q({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},A_{k}))||_{2}

where
q({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{x}})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(a)q_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
,
{\boldsymbol{w}}=\{w_{1},...,w_{n}\}
,
\mathbb{Q}
be a matrix with

the element of the
i
-th row and
k
-th column, and
\mathbf{b}
be a vector with the
i

\sum_{k=1}^{n}||({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i})-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},q({\boldsymbol{X}}_{k},A_{k}))||_{2}

Therefore, the optimization problem becomes:

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n}\in&\underset{{\boldsymbol{w}}=\{w_{1},...,w_{n}\}}{\arg\min}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}\mathbb{Q}{\boldsymbol{w}}+2{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}\mathbf{b}\\
&\textrm{subject to}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=n\textrm{ ,and }w_{i}\geq 0\textrm{ for all }i.\end{split}

which is a linear optimization problem with linear restrictions that can be handled efficiently by the existing algorithms. For example, we use the R package OSQP

Similarly, for the penalized energy balancing weights, the optimization problem can be transformed as:

7.2
Valid CDF function

Here we prove that
F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
is the CDF of
({\boldsymbol{X}},q({\boldsymbol{X}},A))
.

Assume the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and the probability density function of
({\boldsymbol{X}},A)
is
F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
and
f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
, respectively. Since
q({\boldsymbol{X}},A)=\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{X}})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{X}}}(A)q_{j}({\boldsymbol{X}},A)
is piece-wise invertible. From the theorem of piece-wise invertible transformation of a random variable, the density function for the random variables
({\boldsymbol{X}},q({\boldsymbol{X}},A))
is

\begin{split}f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Q}({\boldsymbol{x}},q)=\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{x}})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},q))f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},q))h^{\prime}_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},q).\end{split}

(17)

On the other hand, by differentiating
F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\mathbf{A}}^{q}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{x}})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))
, we have

\begin{split}\frac{d}{d{\boldsymbol{x}}da}&F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\mathbf{A}}^{q}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=\frac{d}{d{\boldsymbol{x}}da}\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{x}})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{x}})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))h^{\prime}_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\\
&=f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Q}({\boldsymbol{x}},a).\end{split}

Note that, here,
f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Q}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
is identical to
f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},Q}({\boldsymbol{x}},q)
which is the density function of random variable
({\boldsymbol{X}},q({\boldsymbol{X}},A))
. Therefore, in order to prove

is the CDF of
({\boldsymbol{X}},q({\boldsymbol{X}},A))

\begin{split}\lim_{({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\to\boldsymbol{-\infty}}F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\mathbf{A}}^{q}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=\lim_{({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\to\boldsymbol{-\infty}}\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{x}})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))=0\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\lim_{({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\to\boldsymbol{\infty}}F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\mathbf{A}}^{q}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=\lim_{({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\to\boldsymbol{\infty}}\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{x}})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))=1.\end{split}

We demonstrate that these limiting properties can be satisfied through standard assumptions, namely that
\lim_{a\to-\infty}q({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=-\infty
and
\lim_{a\to\infty}q({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=\infty
for
\forall{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Since
\forall j
,
q_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
are invertible (strictly increasing or decreasing) functions and
h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
is the inverse function of
q_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
for
\forall{\boldsymbol{x}}
, it is easy to prove that
\lim_{({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\to-\infty}h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=-\infty
and
\lim_{({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\to\infty}h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=\infty
. Thus, since
F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
is the CDF of the random vector
({\boldsymbol{X}},A)
, we have

\lim_{({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\to\boldsymbol{-\infty}}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))=0

\lim_{({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\to\boldsymbol{\infty}}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))=I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))

\lim_{({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\to\boldsymbol{-\infty}}F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},\mathbf{A}}^{q}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)=\lim_{({\boldsymbol{x}},a)\to\boldsymbol{-\infty}}\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{x}})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{x}},h_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}},a))=0

&+\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)d(F^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}-F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{i,n}\epsilon_{i}\\
={}&\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)d(F^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}-F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{X}},A})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{i,n}\epsilon_{i}.\end{split}

The absolute bias then can be written as:

\begin{split}\left|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}^{e}}^{q}]-\mu^{q}\right|&=\left|\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)d(F^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}-F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{X}},A})\right|.\end{split}

(33)

By (
), we know that
F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{X}},A}
converges to
F^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}
as
n
goes to infinity. By the Portmanteau Theorem, it follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)dF_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{X}},A}=\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)dF^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}.

(34)

Hence, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}^{e}}^{q}]-\mu^{q}|=0,

(35)

which demonstrates the asymptotic unbiasedness of the estimator
\hat{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}^{e}}^{q}
.

(36)

holds almost surely.

Consider the non-normalized Radon-Nikodym derivative weights

, which is a function of

in the sense that

(37)

Denote

Then

is a two sample V statistic with kernel
g(\cdot)
and can be written as

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{h}}^{nnrn},{\boldsymbol{X}},A},F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A})={}&\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{l}\sum_{j}h^{nnrn}_{j}||(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{i},\tilde{A}_{i})-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{j},A_{j})||_{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}h^{nnrn}_{i}h^{nnrn}_{j}||({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i})-({\boldsymbol{X}}_{j},A_{j})||_{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{l}\sum_{m}||(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{i},\tilde{A}_{i})-(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{j},\tilde{A}_{j})||_{2}\\
={}&\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\sum_{l}\sum_{m}g({\boldsymbol{W}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j};\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{l},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{m}),\end{split}

where
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{i}=({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i})
and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{i}=(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{i},\tilde{A}_{i})
.
From positivity and strong ignorability, it can be shown that
\mathcal{E}(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{h}}^{nnrn},{\boldsymbol{X}},A},F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A})
is first-order degenerate in the sense that
\mathbb{E}g(\mathbf{W},{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j};\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{l},\tilde{\mathbf{W}})=0
for any
\tilde{\mathbf{W}}
and
\mathbf{W}
.

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}g(\mathbf{W},{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j};\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{l},\tilde{\mathbf{W}})\\
={}&\mathbb{E}(h({\boldsymbol{W}})||{\boldsymbol{W}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{l}||_{2})+\mathbb{E}(h({\boldsymbol{W}}_{j})||{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}||_{2})\\
&+\mathbb{E}(h({\boldsymbol{W}})h({\boldsymbol{W}}_{j})||{\boldsymbol{W}}-{\boldsymbol{W}}_{j}||_{2})+\mathbb{E}(||\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{W}}}_{l}||_{2})\\
={}&\int_{{\boldsymbol{w}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}}\frac{f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}})}{f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}})}||{\boldsymbol{w}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}||_{2}f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}})f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l})d{\boldsymbol{w}}d\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}\\
&+\int_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}\frac{f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{j})}{f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{j})}||{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}||_{2}f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{j})f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}})d{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}d\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
&-\int_{{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}}\frac{f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}})}{f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}})}\frac{f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{j})}{f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{j})}||{\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}||_{2}f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}})f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{j})d{\boldsymbol{w}}d{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}\\
&-\int_{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}}||\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}||_{2}f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}})f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l})d\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}d\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}\\
={}&\int_{{\boldsymbol{w}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}}f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}})f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l})||{\boldsymbol{w}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}||_{2}d{\boldsymbol{w}}d\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}\\
&+\int_{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{j})f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}})||{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}||_{2}d{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}d\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\\
&-\int_{{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}}f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}})f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{j})||{\boldsymbol{w}}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}||_{2}d{\boldsymbol{w}}d{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}\\
&-\int_{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}}f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}})f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l})||\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}||_{2}d\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}d\tilde{{\boldsymbol{w}}}_{l}\\
={}&0.\end{split}

(44)

almost surely. Thus, we have:

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}|\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}(\boldsymbol{t})-\varphi_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}^{p},{\boldsymbol{X}},A}(\boldsymbol{t})|^{2}v(\boldsymbol{t})d\boldsymbol{t}\\
&\leq\bigg(\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}|\varphi_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}(\boldsymbol{t})-\varphi_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}^{p},{\boldsymbol{X}},A}(\boldsymbol{t})|^{2}v(\boldsymbol{t})d\boldsymbol{t}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}|\varphi_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}(\boldsymbol{t})-\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}(\boldsymbol{t})|^{2}v(\boldsymbol{t})d\boldsymbol{t}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)^{2}\\
&=\bigg([\mathcal{E}(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q},F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}^{p},{\boldsymbol{X}},A})]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}|\varphi_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}(\boldsymbol{t})-\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}(\boldsymbol{t})|^{2}v(\boldsymbol{t})d\boldsymbol{t}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)^{2}\\
&\leq\bigg(\bigg[\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q},F_{n,\hat{{\boldsymbol{h}}},{\boldsymbol{X}},A})]+\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}^{2}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}+\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}|\varphi_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}(\boldsymbol{t})-\varphi_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q}(\boldsymbol{t})|^{2}v(\boldsymbol{t})d\boldsymbol{t}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg)^{2},\end{split}

which converges to
0
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Lemma 7.5
.

(Partially Conditional Central Limit Theorem of
Wong and Chan 2017
) Let
(W_{1},\mathcal{B}_{1}),...,(W_{n},\mathcal{B}_{n})
be indipendent and identically distributed where
W_{1},...,W_{n}
are random variables and
\mathcal{B}_{1},...,\mathcal{B}_{n}
are sets of random variables. Let
\{C_{1},...,C_{n}\}
be another set of random variables. Write
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{W_{1},...,W_{n},\mathcal{B}_{1},...,\mathcal{B}_{n}\}
. Assume these variables satisfy, for each
j=1,...,n

E(W_{j})=0

E(C_{j}|\mathcal{D}_{n})=0

\sum_{j=1}^{n}var(W_{j})=1

\sum_{j=1}^{n}var(C_{j}|\mathcal{D}_{n})=1

and there exists
q>0
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}E(|C_{j}|^{2+q}|\mathcal{D}_{n})\to 0
in probability. Moreover,
C_{1},...,C_{n}
are conditionally independent given
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Let
g
be a (non-random) function mapping from the support of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that there exists a constant
M>0
such that
E(g^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{n}))\leq M
and
g^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\leq M+o_{p}(1)
. For any positive real number
\tau
, consider two random variables:

Z_{n}=\tau\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{j}+g(\mathcal{D}_{n})\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}

Z_{n}^{*}=\tau F+g(\mathcal{D}_{n})\sum_{j=1}^{n}\{var(C_{j}|\mathcal{D}_{j})\}^{1/2}G_{j},

where
F,G_{1},...,G_{n}
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables independent of
C_{1},...,C_{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Let
\phi_{n}
and
\phi_{n}^{*}
be the corresponding characteristic function of
Z_{n}
and
Z_{n}^{*}
respectively. Then

|\phi_{n}(t)-\phi_{n}^{*}(t)|\to 0

for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
E(Z^{*2}_{n})=\tau^{2}+E\{g^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\}\leq\tau^{2}+M
and therefore
\phi^{*}_{n}
is twice differentiable.

Now we start to prove Theorem
:

From the error decomposition, we have

\begin{split}\hat{\mu}_{AG}^{q}-\mu^{q}=&\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}[\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)-\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)]d(F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}-F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n},{\boldsymbol{X}},A})\\
&+\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)d(F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A}-F_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}^{q})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{i}\epsilon_{i}.\end{split}

For the element
\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}[\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)-\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)]d(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n},{\boldsymbol{X}},A}-F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A})
, note that by Lemma 3.3, since
\hat{\mu}-\mu\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\bigg[\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}[\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)-\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)]d(F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n},{\boldsymbol{X}},A}-F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A})\bigg]^{2}\leq C_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}(F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A},F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n},{\boldsymbol{X}},A}),

where
C_{\alpha}\geq 0
is a constant depending on only
\mu(x,a)
. From Lemma
7.3
, we have

C_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}(F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A},F_{n,{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{n},{\boldsymbol{X}},A})\leq C_{\alpha}\mathcal{O}(1/n).

(48)

Note that here
C_{\alpha}=\langle\hat{\mu}-\mu,\hat{\mu}-\mu\rangle
. Since
\hat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
is a consistent estimator of
\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)
, we have
C_{\alpha}={O}_{p}(n^{-1})
. Thus, the first element would be
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
which does not influence the efficiency result.

Now, let’s consider the term
\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)d(F^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}-F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{i}\epsilon_{i}
.
Its variance can be obtained as

\begin{split}{\bf Var}(J_{n})={}&{\bf Var}(n^{1/2}(\hat{\mu}_{AG}^{q}-\mu^{q}))\\
={}&n{\bf Var}\bigg(\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)d(F^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}-F^{q}_{n,{\boldsymbol{X}},A})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{p}_{i}\epsilon_{i}\bigg)\\
={}&n{\bf Var}\bigg(\mu^{q}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}}(A_{i})\mu({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},q_{j}({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i}))+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{p}_{i}\epsilon_{i}\bigg)\\
={}&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Var}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}}(A_{i})\mu({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},Q_{j})\bigg)+\frac{1}{n}\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}^{p})^{2}\bigg)\\
\leq{}&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf Var}\{\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}}(A_{i})\mu({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},Q_{j})\}+\sigma^{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}^{2}+n{O}_{p}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\bigg).\end{split}

where the second to last equality is obtained since
\mathbb{E}(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{p}_{i}\epsilon_{i})=0
, the last inequality is due to the definition of the penalized energy balancing weights, see the proof of Theorem
.
Therefore, by the central limit theorem of the Radon-Nikodym weights, we have the following limiting property:

\limsup_{n}{\bf Var}(J_{n})\leq{\bf Var}(\mu({\boldsymbol{X}},Q))+\sigma^{2}\bigg({\bf E}\left(\frac{f^{q}_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{X}},A)}{f_{{\boldsymbol{X}},A}({\boldsymbol{X}},A)}\right)^{2}+B\bigg),

(49)

where
B
is some real number.

Note that we have

{W}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{J({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i})}I_{j,{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}}(A_{i})\mu({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},Q_{i})-E_{q}(\mu({\boldsymbol{X}},A))}{\bigg(n{\bf Var}(\mu({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},Q_{i}))\bigg)^{1/2}}

\mathcal{B}_{i}=({\boldsymbol{X}}_{i},A_{i})

C_{i}=\frac{w_{i}^{e}\epsilon_{i}}{(\sigma^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(w^{e}_{j})^{2})^{1/2}}

\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{W_{1},...,W_{n},\mathcal{B}_{1},...,\mathcal{B}_{n}\}

and let
\tau^{2}=Var(\mu(X,q({\boldsymbol{X}},A)))
,
g^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{n})=\sigma^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(w^{e}_{j})^{2}
, by assumption CA-2, we have
1\leq w_{i}^{e}\leq Bn^{1/3}
. Therefore,
(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(w^{e}_{i})^{2})^{-1}=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{n})

\begin{split}0&\leq E\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|C_{i}|^{3}|\mathcal{D}_{n}\bigg)\leq\frac{\max_{i}E(|\epsilon_{i}|^{3})\sum_{j=1}^{n}(w_{j}^{e})^{3}}{\sigma^{3}(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(w^{e}_{j})^{2})^{3/2}}\\

By Lemma
7.4

E(g^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{n}))\leq M
and
g^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{n})\leq M+o_{p}(1)
by taking

. Then, let

\begin{split}Z_{n}&=\tau\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{j}+g(\mathcal{D}_{n})\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}\\
&=n^{1/2}\bigg(\int_{(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})}\mu({\boldsymbol{x}},a)d(F^{q}_{X,A}-F^{q}_{n,X,A})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{w}}^{e}_{i}\epsilon_{i}\bigg)\end{split}

\begin{split}Z_{n}^{*}=\tau F+g(\mathcal{D}_{n})\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bf Var}(C_{j}|\mathcal{D}_{n})^{1/2}G_{j}=[{\bf Var}(\mu(X,Q))]^{1/2}F+\sigma n^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{e}G_{j},\end{split}

where
F,G_{1},...,G_{n}
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables independent of
C_{1},...,C_{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Let
\phi_{n}
and
