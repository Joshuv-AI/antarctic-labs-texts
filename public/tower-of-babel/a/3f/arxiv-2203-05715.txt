Unitary rotation of pixellated polychromatic images Alejandro R. Urzúa and Kurt Bernardo Wolf


Abstract
Unitary rotations of polychromatic images on finite two-dimensional pixellated screens provide invertibility, group composition, and thus conservation of information. Rotations have been applied on monochromatic image data sets, where we now examine closer the Gibbs- like oscillations that appear due to discrete ‘discontinuities’ of the input images under unitary transformations. Extended to three-color images we examine here the display of color at the pixels where, due to the oscillations, some pixel color values may fall outside their required common numerical range [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , between absence and saturation of the red, green, and blue formant color images.

1
Introduction

In geometric and in wave optics, transformations that respect the
Hamiltonian structure of the models are called canonical; they are
termed linear when the phase space coordinates are linearly mapped.
Images displayed on screens can undergo linear maps representing
free propagation, rotation, gyration, squeezing,
fractional Fourier and other transformations for one-
or two-dimensional images on line or plane screens.

The
discrete
version of these systems and transformations, are based on the
finite oscillator model
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, which considers finite
pixellated

line or plane screens that bear the set of generally complex data values that form
the image. The requirement that the transformations be
unitary
to conserve
Hilbert space properties of the image data sets (orthogonality, reality), requires
them to be elements of a
group
, of which screen rotations form a one-parameter
subgroup, to guarantee invertibility and proper concatenation for two or more such
transformations. In particular, when the pixel values are real, they should remain
so under rotation.

For this article to be essentially self-contained, in Sect.
2

we remind the reader of the basic finite oscillator
model for
D=1
and
D=2
-dimensional finite pixellated images along Cartesian
coordinates
[
4
]
. We emphasize that this rotation
algorithm provides the
only
unitary rotation coefficients for the
image pixels on an
N_{x}\times N_{y}
generally rectangular screen
[
5
]
.
For simplicity we address here only
square

N\times N
images and screens.

In this paper we extend unitary rotations to
polychromatic
pixellated arrays that
carry the three component color values (for red, green and blue) as generally used in
the applied literature. In the polychromatic case, the ranges of the pixel values
are restricted to the
common
interval
[0,1]
, between absence and saturation
of each color. The main problem that appears now is that of Gibbs-
like

oscillations in one or more color component images of pixel values
that may fall
outside
that basic
[0,1]
range. This must be addressed with an
appropriate display tactic, which is laid out in Sect.
3
.
Finally, in the concluding Sect.
4
, we comment further on
linear unitary transformations of pixellated data that are required to conserve information
and Hamiltonian structure.

2
Finite one- and two-dimensional images

The finite oscillator model
[
6
]
is based
on the Lie algebra of spin
su(
2
)
, with generators
\{J_{1},J_{2},J_{3}\}

of commutation relations
[J_{i},J_{j}]=\varepsilon_{i,j,k}J_{k}
(
i,j,k
cyclic),
whose representation multiplets, of dimension
N=2j+1
(with
j

a non-negative integer or half-integer), are interpreted to contain its

N
equidistant eigenstates. The eigenvalues of the
generator
Q:=J_{1}
characterize the
N
pixel
positions
on
a screen,
P:=J_{2}
is momentum, while those of
K:=J_{3}
number the

N
‘energy’ states.
The overlap between the position and mode eigenbases yields
the finite oscillator
wavefunctions
. This finite model mimics
the usual ‘continuous’ harmonic oscillator based on the
Heisenberg-Weyl algebra (of commutator

[\overline{Q},\overline{P}]={\rm i}\hbar{\it 1}
, where the
spectrum of position
\overline{Q}
and of momentum

\overline{P}
are the full real line), whose energy eigenstates of

\overline{H}:={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(\overline{P}^{2}+\overline{Q}^{2})

are equally-spaced with only a lower bound.

2.1
The one-dimensional finite oscillator and screen

For
D=1
-dimensional screens of
N
pixels in a line, the

finite quantization
of the harmonic oscillator replaces
the commutator
[\overline{Q},\overline{P}]={\rm i}{\it 1}

of the continuous model with
[Q,P]={\rm i}K
in their

N=2j+1
-dimensional representation
[
1
]
.
Finite quantization thus assigns
N\times N

su(
2
)

matrix representations
\bf Q
and
\bf P
to position and
momentum
q
and
p
, as follows:

\displaystyle{\hskip-15.0pt}\hbox{position}:\quad q\mapsto Q:=J_{1},\quad{\bf Q}=\|Q_{q,q^{\prime}}\|,

(1)

\displaystyle Q_{q,q^{\prime}}=q\,\delta_{q,q^{\prime}},\qquad q,q^{\prime}\in\{-j,-j{+}1,\ldots\,,j\},\quad N=:2j+1,

\displaystyle{\hskip-15.0pt}\hbox{momentum}:\quad p\mapsto P:=J_{2},\quad{\bf P}=\|P_{q,q^{\prime}}\|,

(2)

\displaystyle P_{q,q^{\prime}}=-{\rm i}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{(j{-}q)(j{+}q{+}1)}\,\delta_{q+1,q^{\prime}}+{\rm i}{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{(j{+}q)(j{-}q{+}1)}\,\delta_{q-1,q^{\prime}},

\displaystyle{\hskip-15.0pt}\hbox{and their commutator}:\quad K:=J_{3},\quad{\bf K}=\|K_{q,q^{\prime}}\|,

(3)

\displaystyle K_{q,q^{\prime}}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{(j{-}q)(j{+}q{+}1)}\,\delta_{q+1,q^{\prime}}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{(j{+}q)(j{-}q{+}1)}\,\delta_{q-1,q^{\prime}}.

These three operators are thus represented by self-adjoint
N\times N

matrices in the vector space with inner product
\sum_{q=-j}^{j}f_{q}g_{q}^{*}
,
and generate the Lie algebra
su(
2
)
with commutation relations

[J_{i},J_{j}]={\rm i}\,\varepsilon_{i,j,k}J_{k},\quad\hbox{i.e.},\quad[K,Q]=-{\rm i}P,\ [K,P]={\rm i}Q,\ [Q,P]={\rm i}K,

(4)

where the first two will stand in place of the two Hamiltonian commutators
in the continuous model,
[\overline{H},\overline{Q}]=-{\rm i}\overline{P}

and
[\overline{H},\overline{P}]={\rm i}\overline{Q}
.
The eigenvalue spectra of the three
su(
2
)
generators
are
\Sigma(Q)=\Sigma(P)=\Sigma(K)=\{-j,-j{+}1,\ldots\,,j\}=:|_{-j}^{j}
,
and we remark that the position operator
Q=J_{1}
, is here
diagonal
.
Finally, the eigenvalues of the
mode
operator
K+j{\it 1}

will count its eigenstates by the
mode number

n:=\kappa+j
as

\ \Sigma(K+j{\it 1}):=\kappa+j=n\in\{0,\,1,\,\ldots,\,2j\}.

(5)

Using Dirac notation, let the orthogonal and complete eigenbases of the
position and mode operators,
J_{1}
and
J_{3}
, be

Q|j,q\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}=q|j,q\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},\ q|_{-j}^{j},\qquad K|j,n\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 3}=\kappa|j,n\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 3}=(n{-}j)|j,n\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 3},\ n|_{0}^{2j}.

(6)

One-dimensional finite signals or discrete
images

{\bf F}=\{F_{q}\}_{q=-j}^{j}

are then given by
N=(2j{+}1)
-dimensional vectors of components

F_{-j}={}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle j,-j|{\bf F}\rangle,\quad F_{-j+1}={}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle j,-j{+}1|{\bf F}\rangle,\quad\cdots\quad F_{j}={}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle j,j|{\bf F}\rangle.

(7)

The overlap between the two bases in (
6
) leads to a three-term difference
equation that yields the Wigner
‘little-
d
’ functions
[
7
]
for the angle
{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\pi

between the two axes, which are the
finite oscillator
wavefunctions

[
1
]
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Psi_{n}(q)&:=&{}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle j,q|j,n\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 3}=d^{j}_{n-j,q}({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\pi)=\Psi_{q+j}(n{-}j)\\[7.0pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{j}}\sqrt{\bigg({2j\atop n}\bigg)\bigg({2j\atop q{+}j}\bigg)}\,K_{n}(q{+}j;{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}};2j),\end{array}

(8)

that are given in terms of the square root of a binomial coefficient
in
q
, a discrete version of a Gaussian, and symmetric Kravchuk
polynomials of degree
n
,

K_{n}(q{+}j;{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}};2j)=K_{q+j}(n;{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}};2j)={}_{2}F_{\!1}(-n,-2j{-}q;-2j;2)
,
that are discrete analogues of Hermite polynomials. The set of
functions
\{\Psi_{n}(q)\}_{n=0}^{2j}
, and also the set
\{\Psi_{n}(q)\}_{q=-j}^{j}
,
form orthonormal bases for the
N
-dimensional space of signals (i.e.,
one-dimensional pixellated images on linear screens), so

\sum_{q=-j}^{j}\Psi_{n}(q)\Psi_{n^{\prime}}(q)=\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}
and

\sum_{n=0}^{2j}\Psi_{n}(q)\Psi_{n}(q^{\prime})=\delta_{q,q^{\prime}}
.

Finally, we write the general form of the Wigner ‘little-
d
’
functions
[
7
, Sect. 3.6]
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d^{j}_{m^{\prime},m}(\beta)&:=&d^{j}_{m,m^{\prime}}(-\beta)=\sqrt{(j+m^{\prime})(j-m^{\prime})(j+m)(j-m)}\\[5.0pt]
&\times&\displaystyle\sum_{k}\frac{(-1)^{m^{\prime}-m+k}(\cos{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\beta)^{2j+m-m^{\prime}-2k}(\sin{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\beta)^{m^{\prime}-m+2k}}{k!\,(j+m-k)!\,(m^{\prime}-m+k)!\,(j-m^{\prime}-k)!},\end{array}

(9)

where the summation extends over the integer range

\hbox{max}(0,m{-}m^{\prime})\leq k\leq\hbox{min}(j{-}m)

for
m>m^{\prime}
, while for
m<m^{\prime}
the reflection formula
in (
8
) applies.

2.2
Finite two-dimensional images

In
D=2
dimensions, the finite oscillator Lie algebra is

\hbox{\sf su($2$)}_{x}\oplus\;\hbox{\sf su($2$)}_{y}=\hbox{\sf so($4$)}
,
i.e., the four-dimensional orthogonal Lie algebra, which
contains in particular the generator of an
SO(
2
)

group of rotations in the
x
-
y
plane. (Lie algebras are denoted by
lower-case letters, their generated Lie groups by upper-case letters.)
On a square screen, the
so(
4
)
algebra represents
finite images
\bf F
by
N\times N
matrices of entries

\{F_{q_{x},q_{y}}\}
, where
-j\leq q_{x},q_{y}\leq j
are integer-spaced.
Generic rectangular pixellated screens
N_{x}\times N_{y}
have
been considered in
[
5
]
with general representations
of
so(
4
)
, but square ones are simpler and suffice for
this study.

Finite 2D images can be characterized
also by a
second
set of
N^{2}
pixel values
that follow polar coordinates responding to the subalgebra chain

\hbox{\sf so($4$)}\supset\hbox{\sf so($3$)}\supset\hbox{\sf so($2$)}

[
2
]
. The rotation of pixellated images in a square
screen is then accomplished by transforming from the Cartesian
to the polar basis, rotating through multiplicative phases in the
polar basis, and transforming back to the Cartesian basis.

In two dimensions marked by orthogonal axes
x,\,y
, the
algebra
\hbox{\sf su($2$)}_{x}\oplus\;\hbox{\sf su($2$)}_{y}
provides
two sets of generators (
1
)–(
3
) that mutually commute
and provide, as in (
6
), their common eigenbases with
j_{x}=j=j_{y}

(where we omit writing
j
henceforth),

\begin{array}[]{ll}Q_{x}|q_{x},q_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}=q_{x}|q_{x},q_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},\ q_{x}|_{-j}^{j},&K_{x}|n_{x},n_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 3}=(n_{x}{-}j)|n_{x},n_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 3},\ n_{x}|_{0}^{2j},\\
Q_{y}|q_{x},q_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}=q_{y}|q_{x},q_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},\ q_{y}|_{-j}^{j},&K_{y}|n_{x},n_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 3}=(n_{y}{-}j)|n_{x},n_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 3},\ n_{y}|_{0}^{2j},\end{array}

(10)

defining the
N^{2}
states
\{|q_{x},q_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\}
of the
position basis and the
N^{2}
states
\{|n_{x},n_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 3}\}

of the mode basis. The overlap between these two orthogonal
eigenbases provides the 2D finite
Cartesian
oscillator
wavefunctions,

\Psi_{n_{x},n_{y}}(q_{x},q_{y})={}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle q_{x},q_{y}|n_{x},n_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 3}=\Psi_{n_{x}}(q_{x})\,\Psi_{n_{y}}(q_{y}).

(11)

As before, the set of functions
\{\Psi_{n_{x},n_{y}}(q_{x},q_{y})\}
forms
orthonormal bases for the
N^{2}
-dimensional space of pixellated
two-dimensional images with respect to the indices
q_{x},q_{y}
and
with respect to
n_{x},n_{y}
.

Two-dimensional images
{\bf F}=\{F_{q_{x},q_{y}}\}_{q_{x},q_{y}=-j}^{j}
are thus
represented by the values of

N\times N
matrices built with the adjoint of the position basis
|q_{x},q_{y}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}

in (
10
), as was done in (
7
) for one dimension,

{\bf F}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle-j,-j|&\cdots&{}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle-j,j|\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phantom{-}{}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle j,-j|&\cdots&\phantom{-}{}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle j,j|\end{array}\right)\!|{\bf F}\rangle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}F_{-j,-j}&\cdots&F_{-j,j}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
F_{j,-j}&\cdots&F_{j,j}\end{array}\right),

(12)

Next, according to the commutation relations (
4
),
K_{x}

and
K_{y}
generate independent rotations in the
(Q_{x},P_{x})
and

(Q_{y},P_{y})
phase planes. Their sum and difference have the eigenvalue sets

\displaystyle K:=K_{x}+K_{y},

\displaystyle\quad n:=n_{x}+n_{y}\in\{0,1,\ldots,4j\},

(13)

\displaystyle M:=K_{x}-K_{y},

\displaystyle\quad\!m:=n_{x}-n_{y}\in\{-2j,-2j{+}1,\ldots,2j\}.

(14)

There follows the definition of a
distinct
position-momentum
basis
[
2
]
that is classified by the
so(
4
)
generators

Q^{\circ}_{x}:=Q_{x}+Q_{y},\quad Q^{\circ}_{y}:=P_{x}-P_{y},\qquad P^{\circ}_{x}:=P_{x}+P_{y},\quad P^{\circ}_{y}:=-Q_{x}+Q_{y}.

(15)

With
K
and
M
, these operators satisfy the commutation relations

\begin{array}[]{l}\Big[K,\,\Big\{{Q_{x}^{\circ}\atop P_{x}^{\circ}}\Big]={\rm i}\Big\{{\phantom{-}P_{x}^{\circ}\atop-Q_{x}^{\circ},}\quad\Big[K,\Big\{{Q_{y}^{\circ}\atop P_{y}^{\circ}}\Big]={\rm i}\Big\{{\phantom{-}P_{y}^{\circ}\atop-Q_{y}^{\circ},}\\[5.0pt]
\Big[M,\Big\{{Q_{x}^{\circ}\atop Q_{y}^{\circ}}\Big]={\rm i}\Big\{{\phantom{-}Q_{y}^{\circ}\atop-Q_{x}^{\circ},}\quad\Big[M,\Big\{{P_{x}^{\circ}\atop P_{y}^{\circ}}\Big]={\rm i}\Big\{{\phantom{-}P_{y}^{\circ}\atop-P_{x}^{\circ},}\end{array}\qquad[K,M]=0,

(16)

that interpret
K
as the generator of
isotropic fractional Fourier-Kravchuk transforms

[
2
,
3
]
, and
M
as the generator of
rotations

of the phase space operators in (
15
). The commuting operators

K,\,M
define the orthonormal
polar
eigenbasis
\{|n,m\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle P}\}
,

K|n,m\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle P}=(n-2j)|n,m\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle P},\ n|_{0}^{4j},\quad M|n,m\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle P}=m|n,m\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle P},\ m|_{-2j}^{2j}.

(17)

The overlap between the position basis and the polar basis determines
the 2D finite
polar
oscillator wavefunctions
[
2
]
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}&&\Lambda^{j}_{n,m}(q_{x},q_{y}):={}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle q_{x},q_{y}|n,m\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle P}\\[5.0pt]
&&{\qquad}=\displaystyle(-1)^{(|m|-m)/2}\!\!\!\sum_{n_{x}+n_{y}=n}\!\!\!(-{\rm i})^{n_{y}}d^{n/2}_{\frac{1}{2}(n_{x}-n_{y}),\frac{1}{2}m}({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\pi)\,\Psi_{n_{x},n_{y}}(q_{x},q_{y}).\end{array}

(18)

Here also, the set of functions
\{\Lambda^{j}_{n,m}(q_{x},q_{y})\}
provide
orthonormal bases for the
N^{2}
-space of images, with respect to
the indices
q_{x},q_{y}
and with respect to
n,m
.

By construction, the functions (
18
) transform
under rotations through multiplication by phases,

{\cal R}(\theta)\Lambda^{j}_{n,m}(q_{x},q_{y}):=e^{-{\rm i}\theta M}\Lambda^{j}_{n,m}(q_{x},q_{y})=e^{-{\rm i}m\theta}\Lambda^{j}_{n,m}(q_{x},q_{y}),

(19)

The rotation of finite images
{\cal R}(\theta):|{\bf F}\rangle\mapsto|{\bf F}^{(\theta)}\rangle
from the set

\{F_{q_{x},q_{y}}\}
to
\{F_{\!q_{x},q_{y}}^{(\theta)}\}
can thus proceed as

\displaystyle F_{\!q_{x},q_{y}}^{(\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle q_{x},q_{y}|\,{\cal R}(\theta)\,|{\bf F}\rangle

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\sum_{n,m;q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime}}\!\!\!\!{}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle q_{x},q_{y}|n,m\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle\!P}\,{}_{\scriptscriptstyle P\!}\langle n,m|{\cal R}(\theta)|n,m\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle\!P}{}_{\scriptscriptstyle P\!}\langle n,m|q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\,{}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime}|{\bf F}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\sum_{n,m;q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime}}\!\!\!\!{}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle q_{x},q_{y}|n,m\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle\!P}\,e^{-{\rm i}m\theta}{}_{\scriptscriptstyle P\!}\langle n,m|q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}{}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\langle q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime}|{\bf F}\rangle{\qquad}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime}}R(q_{x},q_{y};q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime};\theta)\,F_{q^{\prime}_{x},q^{\prime}_{y}}

(21)

with
n|_{0}^{2j}
,
m|_{-2j}^{2j}
and
q^{\prime}_{x},q^{\prime}_{y}|_{-j}^{j}
,
where the kernel of rotation is given by

R(q_{x},q_{y};q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime};\theta):=\sum_{n,m}\Lambda^{j}_{n,m}(q_{x},q_{y})\,e^{-{\rm i}m\theta}\,\Lambda^{j}_{n,m}(q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime})^{*}.

(22)

An alternative expression for this kernel can be written as
[
2
]

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle R(q_{x},q_{y};q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime};\theta):=\sum_{\mu,\mu^{\prime}}\Psi_{n_{x},n_{y}}(q_{x},q_{y})\,d^{n/2}_{\mu,\mu^{\prime}}(2\theta)\,\Psi_{n_{x}^{\prime},n_{y}^{\prime}}(q_{x}^{\prime},q_{y}^{\prime})^{*},\\
\mu={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(n_{x}-n_{y}),\ \mu^{\prime}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(n_{x}^{\prime}-n_{y}^{\prime});\quad n_{x}+n_{y}=n=n_{x}^{\prime}+n_{y}^{\prime}.\end{array}

(23)

When the input image is a matrix of real numbers, after rotation it
will remain a real matrix, because the rotation kernel
(
22
) is real. Due to the orthonormality of the bases,
from (
22
) or (
23
), we see that
R(q_{x},q_{y};q_{x^{\prime}},q_{y^{\prime}};0)=\delta_{q_{x},q_{x^{\prime}}}\delta_{q_{y},q_{y^{\prime}}}
is the unit transformation.
Similarly, the product of two rotations satisfies group composition

\sum_{q_{x^{\prime}},q_{y^{\prime}}}R(q_{x},q_{y};q_{x^{\prime}},q_{y^{\prime}};\theta_{1})\*R(q_{x^{\prime}},q_{y^{\prime}};q_{x^{\prime\prime}},q_{y^{\prime\prime}};\theta_{2})=R(q_{x},q_{y};q_{x^{\prime\prime}},q_{y^{\prime\prime}};\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})
, and the inverse
is
R(q_{x},q_{y};q_{x^{\prime}},q_{y^{\prime}};-\theta)=R(q_{x^{\prime}},q_{y^{\prime}};q_{x},q_{y};\theta)^{*}
.
This shows that the matrices in the set (
22
)
are unitary,
{\bf R}(-\theta)={\bf R}(\theta)^{\dagger}
, and is a
representation of the group
SO(
2
)
of plane rotations; it is reducible in
the polar basis but irreducible in the Cartesian basis. As an example of
this rotation algorithm we provide Fig.
1
that we proceed
to examine and comment.

2.3
The ‘Gibbs-
like
’ oscillation phenomenon

As is evident in Fig.
1
, a
ubiquitous consequence of the unitarity of the rotation
algorithm is that rotated images display
oscillations

due to ‘discrete discontinuities’, i.e., large differences
between values of neighboring pixels.
This oscillation pattern reminds us of that appearing
in another context: the summation of truncated Fourier series,
known as the Gibbs phenomenon. Because their origin is different
(i.e., it is not a
type
of Gibbs oscillation),
we shall call these oscillations Gibbs-
like
;
here we address this phenomenon for the first time.

Figure 1:
Left
: A
50\times 50
monochromatic image (letter “
R
”) of
pixel values 1, over a background of pixel values 0.

Center
: Rotation of
this image by
\frac{1}{4}\pi
(
45^{\circ}
) with
clipping
of the over-
and under-shoots beyond the obligate range
[0,1]
.
Right
: Same rotation with

normalization
of the pixel values to fit this range. Both output
images exhibit ‘Gibbs-
like
’ oscillations due to the sharp
boundaries of the input image. Whereas clipping leads to a more contrasted
image, it also entails loss of information in pixel values; normalization
retains information subject to translation and scale, although it appears
less contrasted.

Linear normalization is the main tactic to keep the grey-tone scale within
the specified range
[0,1]
, between absence and saturation of the
pixel values. We thus distinguish between the
data-image

whose pixel values can have any range, and the
screen-image

which is output for display, where pixel values are all within
[0,1]
.
This tactic maps the data- to screen-values,
{\bf F}\mapsto\overline{\bf F}
,
using the extreme values,

F_{q_{x},q_{y}}\mapsto\overline{F}_{q_{x},q_{y}}=\frac{F_{q_{x},q_{y}}-s_{N}}{S_{N}-s_{N}},\quad\begin{array}[]{l}s_{N}\,:=\min_{q_{x},q_{y}}\,\{F_{q_{x},q_{y}}\}<0,\\
S_{N}:=\max_{q_{x},q_{y}}\,\{F_{q_{x},q_{y}}\}>1.\end{array}

(24)

The screen-image will thus have a minimum of complete blacks
and whites, with no information loss.

Whereas in the familiar trigonometric Gibbs phenomenon,
a unit step function
\Theta(q):={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(1+\hbox{sign}\,q)
entails
a maximal overshoot of
{\sim}8.95\%
at a positions ever closer
to the discontinuity as the number of series terms grows
(see
[
9

), to
calibrate the Gibbs-
like
phenomenon for

N\times N
images under unitary rotation, let us consider a
Kronecker delta at the center of the screen with an odd
number

4
Conclusions

We remarked above that the unitary rotation algorithm for pixellated images
based on the finite oscillator model is unique, and can be inverted or
concatenated. Yet we should underline that the unitary rotation algorithm
requires that the value at every pixel in the rotated image depend on the
values of
all
pixels of the input image. We are thus dealing
with an
\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
dependence in the number of operations
needed to compute a rotation.
The obvious advantage of information conservation comes thus at the
price of a lengthier computation time. For commercial
applications, where
N\approx 10^{3}
is common, the more common
image rotation algorithms based on interpolation in smaller
regions of an image, have none of the former advantages, nor the
last disadvantage.

The results presented for three basic colors of course also apply
to any number of quantities that may be attached to the pixel
values in pre-ordained ranges. The unitary rotations can also be made
to act on the latter quantities
covariantly
, such as would be the case
for a discrete and finite
vector field
, whose two components will
rotate together with the data-image. Also, three- and higher-dimensional
pixellated (‘voxellated’) images, subject to rotations
[
22
]

can be subject to considerations similar to those analyzed here.

Lastly, we should place the group of rotations of pixellated
screens within the wider context of the finite oscillator model,
which uses basically the rotation Lie subalgebra chain
\hbox{\sf so($3$)}\supset\hbox{\sf so($2$)}
to provide the pixel coordinates and
a phase space interpretation for the system, as
opposed to their usual numbering through the
N
-point Fourier
transform. In the latter, the position space forms a discrete torus
whose two discrete
(x,y)
coordinates curl into circles, but
where rotations between them cannot be applied without breaking
the torus. In the finite oscillator model, the
manifold harboring the coordinates is a sphere, which can be
freely rotated and also gyrated in phase space, among other
linear canonical transformations.
