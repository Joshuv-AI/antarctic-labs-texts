An efficient characterization of complex-balanced, detailed-balanced, and weakly reversible systems
Gheorghe Craciun, Jiaxin Jin, Polly Y. Yu

Abstract
Very often, models in biology, chemistry, physics, and engineering are systems of polynomial or power-law ordinary differential equations, arising from a reaction network. Such dynamical systems can be generated by many different reaction networks. On the other hand, networks with special properties (such as reversibility or weak reversibility) are known or conjectured to give rise to dynamical systems that have special properties: existence of positive steady states, persistence, permanence, and (for well-chosen parameters) complex balancing or detailed balancing. These last two are related to thermodynamic equilibrium, and therefore the positive steady states are unique and stable. We describe a computationally efficient characterization of polynomial or power-law dynamical systems that can be obtained as complex-balanced, detailed-balanced, weakly reversible, and reversible mass-action systems.

In order to describe our main results, we need to introduce some definitions and notations (these notions will be described in further detail in Section 2). For our purposes here, a

One of our main results is the following.

Theorem.

A mass-action system

is dynamically equivalent to some

mass-action system if and only if it is dynamically equivalent to a complex-balanced mass-action system

that only uses the vertices of

This theorem is useful not only for finding complex-balanced realizations of mass-action systems but also because for the first time, it gives us

We will see in Section

Moreover, our results are shown for

Reaction networks and mass-action systems, along with all other relevant terms, are defined in Section

Chemical reaction networks appear at the intersection of biology, biochemistry, chemistry, engineering, and mathematics. Different notations are used in the literature; here we explain the notations used throughout this paper. Introductions to chemical reaction network theory can be found in

Definition 2.1
.

A
reaction network
(or simply a
network
) is a directed graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
embedded in Euclidean space, with no self-loops, i.e.,
V_{G}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

When there is no ambiguity, we simply write

Remark.
Vertices are points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We denote an edge

as

, which is associated to a

{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G
instead of
{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in E

The dimension
n
of the ambient Euclidean space is the number of chemical species involved in the reaction network
G
. An edge in the set
E
is called a
reaction
. A vertex in
V
is also known as a
reaction complex
.
The
source vertex
of a reaction
{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}
is the vertex
{\boldsymbol{y}}
, while
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}
is the
product vertex
. Let
V_{s}\subseteq V
denote the
set of source vertices
, i.e., the set of vertices that is the source of some reaction.

The vector space spanned by the reaction vectors is the
stoichiometric subspace

S=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}\{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\colon{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G\}
. For any positive vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
, the affine polytope
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)_{>}=({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}+S)\cap\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is known as the
stoichiometric compatibility class
of

. A reaction network

is
reversible
if
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G
whenever
{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G
; for simplicity, we denote such a pair of reactions by
{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}
. It is

if every connected component of
G
is strongly connected, i.e., every reaction
{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G

Let

\displaystyle\frac{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}\,\,=\!\!\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G}k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{y}}).

\displaystyle\frac{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}\,\,=\!\!\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G}k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{y}})={\boldsymbol{0}}.

(5)

3
Fluxes on Reaction Networks

Most dynamical systems associated to reaction networks are nonlinear
[
30
,
14
,
43
]
. While nonlinear dynamical systems are generally difficult to study, the analysis of reaction networks is sometimes facilitated by the linear constraints arising from the network structure and stoichiometry.

To illustrate what we mean, consider mass-action kinetics. The (generally nonlinear) dynamical system under mass-action kinetics has the form

\displaystyle\frac{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}\,\,=\!\!\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G}\nu_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{x}})({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{y}}),

where
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}}
. Once the nonlinearity is hidden inside the reaction rate function
\nu_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
, the linear structure remaining becomes apparent.

Enumerate the set of reactions,
E=\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}^{\prime}\}_{j=1}^{|E|}
, and let
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=(\nu_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{x}}))_{j=1}^{|E|}
be a vector consisting of the reaction rate functions. Define the
stoichiometric matrix

N\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|E|}
as the matrix whose
j
th column is the
j
th reaction vector
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
. Then the dynamical system above can be written succinctly as
\frac{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{dt}=N{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
.

In order to deal with the underlying linear structure, we do not keep track of the concentrations that give rise to
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
but leave it as a vector of unknowns. For this reason, we denote the value
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
simply as
{\boldsymbol{J}}
and call it a
flux vector
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
flux vector

{\boldsymbol{J}}=(J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}})_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{E}
on a reaction network
G=(V,E)
is a vector of positive numbers. The number
J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}
is called the
flux
of the reaction
{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}
, and the pair
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is called a
flux system
.
3
3

3

The word “system” in “flux system” is in the sense of a system of linear equations, rather than a dynamical system.

As with the rate constants, it may be convenient to refer to
J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}
even when
{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\not\in G
, in which case
J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}=0
.

This idea of fluxes on a reaction network may be familiar to anyone who has worked with stoichiometric network analysis or flux balance analysis. One form of the analysis is to solve the linear equation
N{\boldsymbol{J}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
, where the unknown vector
{\boldsymbol{J}}
has nonnegative coordinates
[
39
,
49
]
. Since we are interested in relating network structure with dynamics, if
{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G
, we impose that
J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}>0
. Also if
{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}
is a reversible reaction in
G
, then
J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}
and
J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}}
are two positive components of the vector
{\boldsymbol{J}}
. A solution
{\boldsymbol{J}}>{\boldsymbol{0}}
of the equation
N{\boldsymbol{J}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
corresponds to a positive steady state if
{\boldsymbol{J}}={\boldsymbol{\nu}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
for some
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
. We define the flux analogues of positive steady state, detailed-balanced steady state, and complex-balanced steady state.

Definition 3.2
.

A
steady state flux
on a network
G=(V,E)
is a flux vector
{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{E}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{y}})={\boldsymbol{0}}.

(8)

A flux
{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{E}
is said to be
detailed-balanced
if for every
{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G
, we have

\displaystyle J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}=J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}}.

(9)

A flux
{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{E}
is said to be
complex-balanced
if for every
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}\in V
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}\in G}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}}.

(10)

A steady state flux is a positive vector
{\boldsymbol{J}}
in
\ker N
, where the stoichiometric matrix
N
has the reaction vectors as its columns. As a shorthand, we refer to the flux system
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
as detailed-balanced if
{\boldsymbol{J}}
is a detailed-balanced flux on
G
. Similarly defined is a complex-balanced flux system on
G
. It will be clear from context whether a complex-balanced system refers to a mass-action system or a flux system.

4
Complex balancing without additional vertices

The identification of possible network structures associated to a biochemical system, say, from experimental data, is closely related to identifying key players in the system (e.g., enzymes in metabolic networks, genes in genetic networks). While the general nonuniqueness implies that network identification may often be impossible, it may still be desirable to compute equivalent systems — whether that be dynamical equivalence or flux equivalence — in order to conclude that the system has better properties than first suspected, e.g., weak reversibility or complex balance. This problem is not new
[
47
,
16
]
.

In recent years, the engineering community has utilized properties of mass-action systems in novel ways to designing and analyzing control systems
[
4
,
44
,
36
,
48
]
. For example, the controllers can be added in such a way that the resulting system is a complex-balanced mass-action system; from this, one can conclude that the control system has a unique positive steady state and local stability
[
36
,
48
]
. Moreover, very general results have been obtained on the stability of complex-balanced systems with delay
[
35
]
.

Thus, there is strong incentive for developing effective computational methods to find structurally better dynamically equivalent systems. One approach uses linear programming, but an objective function must be chosen. To reduce the search space, one can decide to search for a realization with the maximal and minimal number of edges
[
46
,
33
]
. Nonetheless, the set of vertices to be included in the reaction network must be chosen ahead of time.

In the examples of Figure
3
, the mass-action systems systems are dynamically equivalent, but one uses an additional source vertex, whose weighted vectors sum to zero. Intuition may say that additional vertices can only improve the chance to find a network with desirable properties, as additional parameters provide extra degrees of freedom. Even if that is the case, the question of
computability
arises. Even if by adding new vertices to the network, one can produce an equivalent complex-balanced system, there is no a priori bound on the number of new vertices needed. One cannot realistically add new vertices ad infinitum.

Fortunately, we prove that
no additional vertices are needed
in order to check if a given system admits complex-balanced realizations. Thus, to check whether or not a network can admit a complex-balanced realization becomes a finite calculation, one that can be done by searching through the admissible domain as done in linear programming. Although the motivation came from mass-action systems, we prove our results in the more general setting of flux systems.

Our approach is to show that any such additional vertices in the network can be removed without changing the properties desired, namely, complex-balanced or weak reversibility. Such additional vertices will be called
virtual sources
.

{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
from one incoming edge.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
be a complex-balanced flux system on reaction network
G=(V,E)
. Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in V
is a virtual source. Then there exists an equivalent complex-balanced flux system
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
with
V_{G^{\prime}}=V\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}}=J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}}+J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}}\frac{J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}

(14)

for any
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
such that
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G
and any
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}
such that
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}\in G
, and
J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}=J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}
for all other edges
{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
N
be the number of reactions with
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
as target, i.e.,
N=|\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G\}|
. Enumerate the sources as
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2},\dots,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{N}
. Let
M
be the number of reactions with
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
as sources, i.e.,
M=|\{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G\}|
. Enumerate the targets as
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2},\dots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{M}
. Since
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
is a virtual source, it is in the relative interior of the convex hull of the targets
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
. From complex balancing, we have
P_{(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})=0
, or

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}.

Let
\theta=\frac{J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}{\sum_{i}J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}
be the fraction of flux to be redirected from
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
. We apply the construction described in Lemma
4.3
to the incoming edge
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
, and the outgoing edges
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
for
j=1,2,\dots,M
. Let
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
denote the flux system after the diversion. More precisely,
J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}=0
,

\displaystyle J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}-J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\theta J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}},

\displaystyle J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}-J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}

\displaystyle=-\theta J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}},

and the fluxes on all other edges unchanged from
{\boldsymbol{J}}
.

Checking for flux equivalence at
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}
before and after the diversion, we see that

\displaystyle\left(\text{Final flow from ${\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}$}\right)-\left(\text{Initial flow from ${\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}$}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{M}J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})-J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})

\displaystyle=\theta\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{M}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})}_{=\,\,{\boldsymbol{0}}}+\theta\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{M}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}}_{=\,\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})-J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{0}}.

At all other vertices, the net flux is unchanged.

In terms of potentials, at
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle P_{(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})-P_{(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{M}J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}+J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}=-\theta\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}+J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}=0.

At each
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
:

\displaystyle P_{(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j})-P_{(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j})

\displaystyle=\left(J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}+J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}\right)-\left(J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}+J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}\right)=0.

At
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
:

\displaystyle P_{(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})-P_{(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})=-J_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}+\theta\sum_{j=1}^{M}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}=0.

The new flux system
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
after diverting the flux from
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
is still complex-balanced, as the potential is unchanged from those of
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
. Moreover,
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
and
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
are flux equivalent. In addition, at
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G^{\prime}}J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})

\displaystyle=(1+\theta)\!\!\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G^{\prime}}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})={\boldsymbol{0}},

i.e,
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
is a virtual source for
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
.

Thus we have recovered all the hypotheses stated in the theorem. The only difference between
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
and
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
is that
G^{\prime}
contains
N-1=|\left\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G^{\prime}\right\}|
reactions with
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
as target vertex. By induction on the number
|\left\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\rightarrow{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G^{\prime}\right\}|
, there exists a flux system
(G^{*},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{*})
that is flux equivalent to
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
, and for which
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{*}
is a complex-balanced flux on
G^{*}
. Finally, because
P_{(G^{*},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{*})}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})=0
, but there are no incoming reactions to
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
, it follows that there are no outgoing reactions from
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\not\in V_{G^{*}}
.
∎

When does a flux system (or a reaction network) admit a complex-balanced realization? Theorem
4.4
implies that virtual sources do not need to be considered. Theorem
4.5
below is the basis behind several relevant numerical methods in Section
5
for determining if a flux system (or a reaction network) is equivalent to complex-balanced.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
be a flux system, and
V_{G,s}
its set of source vertices. Then
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is flux equivalent to some complex-balanced flux system if and only if
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is flux equivalent to some complex-balanced flux system
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
where
V_{G^{\prime}}\subseteq V_{G,s}
.

Proof.

One direction is trivial. To prove the other direction, suppose
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is a flux system that is flux equivalent to some complex-balanced flux system
(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{J}}})
. If
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in V_{\widetilde{G}}\setminus V_{G,s}
, the set
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G\}
is empty; flux equivalent demands that

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{0}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\widetilde{G}}}\widetilde{J}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}).

Theorem
4.4
implies we can maintain flux equivalence and complex balance even after dropping the vertex
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
from
V_{\widetilde{G}}
. Repeating this process for all vertices not in
V_{G,s}
ultimately implies that there is a complex-balanced flux system
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
such that
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})\sim(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
and in addition
V_{G^{\prime}}\subseteq V_{G,s}
.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
G
be a reaction network, and
V_{G,s}
its set of source vertices. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a flux vector
{\boldsymbol{J}}
such that
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is flux equivalent to some complex-balanced flux system.

(ii)

There exists a flux vector
{\boldsymbol{J}}
such that
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is flux equivalent to some complex-balanced flux system
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
, where
V_{G^{\prime}}\subseteq V_{G,s}
.

Proof.

The proof follows immediately from Theorem
4.5
.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

A mass-action system
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is dynamically equivalent to some complex-balanced system if and only if it is dynamically equivalent to a complex-balanced system
G^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
that only uses the source vertices, i.e.,
V_{G^{\prime}}\subseteq V_{G,s}
.

Proof.

This theorem follows from Proposition
3.8
and Theorem
4.5
. Suppose
G_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is dynamically equivalent to some complex-balanced mass-action system
\widetilde{G}_{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}
. Define the appropriate fluxes
{\boldsymbol{J}}
on
G
and
\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{J}}}
on
\widetilde{G}
; by Proposition
3.8
, the two flux systems are flux equivalent. Theorem
4.5
holds if and only if
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is flux equivalent to some complex-balanced flux system
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
where
V_{G^{\prime}}\subseteq V_{G,s}
. Define the appropriate mass-action system
G^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
(see Proposition
3.9
); we have one direction of this theorem. The other direction is trivially true.
∎

All of our theorems thus far have been concerned with flux systems; in the case of mass-action systems, implicit in everything is the existence of a complex-balanced steady state. However, the idea of redirecting fluxes can be adapted to show the surprising result that weak reversibility can be accomplished (if at all) with no extra vertices.

Theorem 4.8
.

A mass-action system
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is dynamically equivalent to some weakly reversible mass-action system if and only if it is dynamically equivalent to a weakly reversible mass-action system
G^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
that only uses its source vertices, i.e.,
V_{G^{\prime}}\subseteq V_{G,s}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may suppose that
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is a weakly reversible mass-action system for which there exists a virtual source
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
.
As in Theorem
4.4
, we remove the vertex
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
by redirecting the reactions flowing through it. Since
G
is weakly reversible, there exists some vertex
{\boldsymbol{z}}
such that
{\boldsymbol{z}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G
. As before, we will try to replace pairs of reactions
{\boldsymbol{z}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}
with
{\boldsymbol{z}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}
.

Enumerate the set
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G\}
as
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
, and enumerate the set
\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G\}
as
\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{N}
. For simplicity, let
\alpha_{j}=k_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}
, and let
\beta_{i}=k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}
. Informally speaking, in place of the reactions
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
, we shall have the reaction
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
with rate constant
k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}=\alpha_{j}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\sum\beta_{s}}
. More precisely, let
G^{\prime}
be the graph after deleting the vertex
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
and its adjacent edges from
G
, and (if needed) the edges
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
added for all
i=1,2,\dots,M
and
j=1,2,\dots,N
. On
G^{\prime}
, take the rate constants to be
k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}=k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}=0
and

\displaystyle k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}=k_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}+\alpha_{j}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\sum\beta_{s}},

and all other rate constants same as in
G_{\boldsymbol{k}}
.

The assumption that
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
is a virtual source can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\beta_{i}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\beta_{i}{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}.

Now to check for dynamical equivalence at
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}
, we consider the differences due to the reactions
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}(k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}-k_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}})({\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\alpha_{1}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\sum\beta_{s}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\sum\beta_{s}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}\beta_{i}{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{M}\beta_{i}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}),

which is the contribution from the reaction
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
. Since other reactions were untouched, we have dynamical equivalence at
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}
. There is nothing special about
j=1
; the same holds for all source vertices
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{3},\dots,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{N}
.

Finally, given any cycle
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}\to{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2}\to\cdots\to{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\ell}\to{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}
in
G^{\prime}
, whenever an edge
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
appears in the cycle, replace it with two edges
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
, and obtain a cycle in
G
. Therefore,
G^{\prime}
is still weakly reversible.
∎

We extend the above results (Theorems
4.4
-
4.8
) to detailed-balanced fluxes and/or reversible networks. We summarize these results in the following theorems.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
be a detailed-balanced flux system on a reaction network
G=(V,E)
. Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in V
is a virtual source.
Then there exists an equivalent detailed-balanced flux system
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
with
V_{G^{\prime}}=V\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}}=J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}}+J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}}\frac{J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}

(15)

for any
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}
connected to
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
in
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
. Let other fluxes remain unchanged from
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
. In particular,
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is flux equivalent to some detailed-balanced flux system if and only if
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is flux equivalent to some detailed-balanced flux system
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
where
V_{G^{\prime}}\subseteq V_{G,s}
.

Proof.

As in Theorem
4.4
, we divert fluxes away from
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
. We only need to check detail balancing. Consider any two vertices
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\neq{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}
where
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G
. Using the fact that the flux system was originally detailed-balanced, i.e.,
J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}}=J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}}

\displaystyle=J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}}+J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}}\frac{J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}

\displaystyle=J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}+J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}\frac{J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}}

\displaystyle=J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}.

For any other pairs of reversible reaction, detail balancing is inherited from
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
. In other words,
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
is detailed-balanced.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

A mass-action system
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is dynamically equivalent to some reversible system if and only if it is dynamically equivalent to a reversible system
G^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
that only uses its source vertices, i.e.,
V_{G^{\prime}}\subseteq V_{G,s}
.

Proof.

We assume that
G_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is reversible and has a virtual source
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in V_{G}
. We will replace the reactions
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\in G\}
by modifying/adding the reactions
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}\colon{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G\}
. For any
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}
,
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
such that
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
,
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in G
, let
k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}=k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}=0
and

\displaystyle k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}=k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}+k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}\left(\frac{k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}}{\sum k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{s}}}\right).

Similar to Theorem
4.8
, it can be shown that
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
and
G^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
are dynamically equivalent. Moreover, by symmetry of construction,
G^{\prime}
is reversible.
∎

Note that related results have been obtained recently for the problem of kinetic feedback design involving complex-balanced and weakly reversible systems
[
36
]
.
Here, for the problem of dynamical equivalence, we show that a given system admits a dynamically equivalent system that is complex-balanced (or weakly reversible, or detailed-balanced, or reversible) if and only if such a system exists using
only
the complexes that are already present in the original system.

4.1
Connection to deficiency theory

Within the reaction network theory literature,
deficiency
is a well-known quantity defined for a network
G
. Equipped with mass-action kinetics, networks with low deficiency are known to enjoy special dynamical properties under mass-action kinetics. For example, the famous deficiency zero theorem says that a weakly reversible deficiency zero network is complex-balanced for any choices of rate constants
[
30
,
25
]
. As we have introduced, complex-balanced systems enjoy properties such as uniqueness and stability of steady states, existence of a Lyapunov function, and the steady states admit a monomial parametrization
[
28
,
24
,
50
,
30
,
26
]
. Despite the strong implications, deficiency has a relatively simple definition.

Definition 4.11
.

Let
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
be a reaction network with
\ell_{G}
connected components. Suppose the dimension of the stoichiometric subspace
S
is
s=\dim S
; then the
deficiency
of the network
G
is the nonnegative integer

\displaystyle\delta_{G}=|V_{G}|-\ell_{G}-s.

(16)

It can be shown that
\delta_{G}=\dim(\Ker Y\cap\Img I_{G})
, where
Y
is the stoichiometric matrix, with the vertices as its columns, and
I_{G}
the incidence matrix of
G

[
34
]
. It follows that
\delta_{G}
is a nonnegative integer. When the network is weakly reversible, we also have
\delta_{G}=\dim(\Ker Y\cap\Img A_{{\boldsymbol{k}}})
, where
-A_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{T}
is the Laplacian of the weighted graph
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}

[
28
,
24
]
.

Deficiency continues to play an important role in the analysis of reaction networks and mass-action systems. In our procedure for removing virtual vertices, deficiency always decreases.
This is similar to a result obtained in
[
36
]
, where the removal of additional monomials that function as controls in a feedback system also leads to a decrease in deficiency.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
be a weakly reversible mass-action system with deficiency
\delta_{G}
. Suppose it has a virtual source
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
.
Let
G^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
be the weakly reversible mass-action system as produced in Theorem
4.8
, dynamically equivalent to
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
with
V_{G^{\prime}}=V_{G}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\}
. Then the deficiency of
G^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
is
\delta_{G^{\prime}}=\delta_{G}-1
.

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem
4.8
, we replaced the reactions
{\boldsymbol{z}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}
with the reaction
{\boldsymbol{z}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}
by choosing appropriate rate constants. It is clear that
|V_{G^{\prime}}|=|V_{G}|-1
, and the number of linkage classes stays the same. We claim that the stoichiometric subspace
S
remains unchanged. Thus, the drop in deficiency is due to the removal of the vertex
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
, and
\delta_{G^{\prime}}=\delta_{G}-1
.

First enumerate the reactions coming out of
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
as
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
, and enumerate the reactions going into
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
as
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
. Let
S_{0}
be the span of the reaction vectors “untouched” by our procedure, more precisely,

\displaystyle S_{0}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}\{{\boldsymbol{y}}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\in G\colon{\boldsymbol{y}}\neq{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\text{ or }{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}\neq{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\}.

Let
S_{G}
be the stoichiometric subspace of
G
, in particular,

\displaystyle S_{G}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}\{S_{0},\,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\}_{i,j},

and
S_{G^{\prime}}
be the stoichiometric subspace of
G^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle S_{G^{\prime}}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}\{S_{0},\,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\}_{i,j}.

Clearly,
S_{G^{\prime}}\subseteq S_{G}
, since
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}=({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})+({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i})\in S_{G}
. Moreover, because
G
is weakly reversible, the edge
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
is a part of a cycle; therefore,
S_{G}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}\{S_{0},\,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\}_{i}
. Finally, we note that
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
is in the convex hull of the vertices
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
, and thus
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\in\Span_{\mathbb{R}}\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\}_{j}
, which implies
S_{G}\subseteq S_{G^{\prime}}
. In other words,
S_{G}=S_{G^{\prime}}
and
\delta_{G^{\prime}}=\delta_{G}-1
.
∎

5
Numerical methods

In this section, we characterize when a flux system or a mass-action system is equivalent to a complex-balanced system. We also describe a method to determine when a mass-action system is dynamically equivalent to a complex-balanced or weakly reversible system.

5.1
Flux equivalence to complex-balancing

Is a steady state flux system
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
flux equivalent to a complex-balanced one? The answer lies in the following linear feasibility problem for an unknown vector
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime}
. Enumerate the set of source vertices in
G
as
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2},\dots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}
. Search for
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime}=(J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}})_{i\neq j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}-N}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G}J_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})\qquad\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,N,

(17)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}J^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}\qquad\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,N,

(18a)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

(19)

If such a flux vector
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime}
exists, then
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is flux equivalent to a complex-balanced system. If no such flux vector
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime}
exists, then
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
is not flux equivalent to a complex-balanced system.

Equation (
17
) is the flux equivalence condition, while (
18a
) ensures that the new flux system is complex-balanced. Equation (
17
) alone checks for flux equivalence between any two given systems
(G,{\boldsymbol{J}})
and
(G^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{J}}^{\prime})
.

(20)

5.2
Dynamical equivalence to complex balancing

We considered above a set of equalities and inequalities necessary and sufficient for a flux system to be equivalent to a complex-balanced one. If the flux system arises from mass-action kinetics, we can write down an analogous system of equalities and inequalities necessary and sufficient for dynamical equivalence to a complex-balanced system.

Consider a mass-action system
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
, whose vertices are points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and enumerate the set of source vertices in
G
as
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2},\dots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}
. We set up a nonlinear feasibility problem for unknowns
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Search for vectors
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}=(k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}})_{i\neq j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}-N}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G}k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})\qquad\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,N,

(21)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}\qquad\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,N,

(22)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(23)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle>0.

(24)

If such
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}
exist, then
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is dynamically equivalent to a complex-balanced system with
{\boldsymbol{x}}
a complex-balanced steady state. If no such rate constants and steady state exist, then
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is not dynamically equivalent to a complex-balanced system.

Equation (
21
) enforces dynamical equivalence. Equations (
22
) and (
24
) imply that
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a positive complex-balanced steady state for an equivalent mass-action system; hence
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a positive steady state of
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
. Note that in the inequality (
23
), some
k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}
can be zero, which implies that
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}
is not a reaction in the equivalent network.

Equations (
21
)-(
24
) generally form a nonlinear problem. Despite that, for networks with additional structure, one may be able to extract more information about the rate constants. One such example is the network
G
in Figure
2
(a). For this network we can completely characterize the parameter values for which the associated mass-action system has a complex-balanced realization.

Example 5.2
.

Consider a mass-action system on the network
G
of Figure
2
(a) and Example
2.7
, with rate constants

\displaystyle k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{5}}

\displaystyle=k_{1},\quad k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{5}}=k_{2},\quad k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{3}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{6}}=k_{3},\quad\text{and}\quad k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{4}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{6}}=k_{4}.

By a calculation, (
21
)-(
24
) hold if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{1}{25}<\frac{k_{1}k_{3}}{k_{2}k_{4}}<25.

(25)

Again, a complex-balanced realization is a subgraph of
G^{\prime}
in Figure
2
(b). More precisely, it is the reversible square with one pair of reversible diagonal (either
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}_{3}
or
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2}\rightleftharpoons{\boldsymbol{y}}_{4}
); which diagonal is needed depends on the magnitudes of
k_{1}k_{3}
and
k_{2}k_{4}
. The details of this characterization can be found in an upcoming paper
[
13
]
.

The complex-balanced realization described (the subgraph of
G^{\prime}
in Figure

(b)) has deficiency
\delta_{G^{\prime}}=1
. It is known that if its eight rate constants lie in a toric ideal of codimension
\delta_{G^{\prime}}=1
, then the mass-action system is complex-balanced
[

]
. While these eight rate constants are related to
k_{1},\,k_{2},\,k_{3}
, and
k_{4}
by several linear equations, we found one explicit condition (
25
) for when the mass-action system
G_{\boldsymbol{k}}
of Figure

(a) is dynamically equivalent to a complex-balanced system.

Finally, note that the network of Example
5.2
gives rise to systems that are equivalent to complex-balanced for certain choices of rate constants, but
not
for other choices of rate constants. In a follow-up paper we will show that an entire class of networks give rise to systems that are equivalent to complex-balanced
for all choice of rate constants
. More precisely, we will prove that systems generated by single-target networks that have their (unique) target vertex in the strict relative interior of the convex hull of its source vertices are dynamically equivalent to detailed-balanced mass-action systems for any choice of rate constants

5.3
Existence of a weakly reversible realization for a mass-action system

While complex-balanced mass-action systems are weakly reversible, not all weakly reversible mass-action systems are complex-balanced. There has been much work on determining when a weakly reversible mass-action system is complex-balanced or not. Nonetheless, weakly reversible mass-action systems always have at least one positive steady state within each stoichiometric compatibility class
[
9
]
and are conjectured to be persistent, and even permanent
[
15
]
.

We present a simple nonlinear feasibility problem to determine when a mass-action system is dynamically equivalent to a weakly reversible one. Recall that a mass-action system is weakly reversible if and only if it is complex-balanced for
some
choice of rate constants. We introduce a scaling factor
\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}
in order to decouple the dynamical equivalence condition from the complex-balanced condition.

Consider a mass-action system
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
, whose vertices are points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and enumerate the set of source vertices in
G
as
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2},\dots,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{N}\}
. We set up a nonlinear feasibility problem for unknown rate constants
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}
and a scaling factor
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. Search for vectors
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}=(k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}})_{i\neq j}
and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}})_{i\neq j}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}-N}
satisfying.

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G}k_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i})\qquad\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,N,

(26)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}}\qquad\text{ for }i=1,2,\dots,N,

(27)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(28)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

\displaystyle>0.

(29)

If such
{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}
and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
exist, then
G_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is dynamically equivalent to a weakly reversible mass-action system. If no solution exists, then
G_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is
not
dynamically equivalent to a weakly reversible system.

Equation (
26
) enforces dynamical equivalence. Equation (
27
) can be regarded as a complex balancing condition that uses a different set of rate constants
\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}
. Since
\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}\neq 0
if and only if
k^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}\to{\boldsymbol{y}}_{j}}\neq 0
, we preserve the graph structure of
G^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
. It is well-known that a reaction network is weakly reversible if and only if it is complex-balanced for some choice of rate constants
[
10
]
. The scaling factor
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
frees the rate constants from the dynamical equivalence constraint.

Note that while (
26
)-(
29
) are simple to describe, more sophisticated, computationally efficient methods have been developed
[
42
,
46
]
. Weak reversibility is a condition of the underlying directed graph. Ultimately one is imposing conditions on the incidence matrix or the Kirchhoff matrix of the network. Algorithms to find weakly reversible realization for a fixed vertex set have been proposed initially using mixed-integer linear programming
[
46
,
33
]
and later by a
polynomial time
algorithm based on linear programming
[
42
]
. However, as with previous work on complex-balanced realizations, one must fix the set of vertices to be used in the computation. According to Theorem
4.8
, it suffices to find an equivalent network using the existing source vertices. Therefore, the mixed-integer linear programming algorithms proposed in
[
46
,
33
]
and the polynomial time algorithm in
[
42
]
can be used in conjunction with Theorem
4.8
to completely characterize whether or not a mass-action system
G_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is dynamically equivalent to a weakly reversible one.

6
Conclusion

If we are looking for a complex-balanced realization of a given polynomial (or power-law) dynamical system, there exists no a priori limit on the number of vertices in the objective network. Moreover, there are no a priori choices for the locations of the vertices. Here we prove that a solution exists if and only if the objective network can be constructed by using only the vertices that are already present in the original system (i.e., the exponents of the monomial terms present in the original system). We also prove that the same is true for detailed-balanced, reversible and weakly reversible systems.
