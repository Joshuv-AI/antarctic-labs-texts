A sharp, structure preserving two-velocity model for two-phase flow
Ronald A. Remmerswaal, Arthur E.P. Veldman

Abstract
The numerical modelling of convection dominated high density ratio two-phase flow poses several challenges, amongst which is resolving the relatively thin shear layer at the interface. To this end we propose a sharp discretisation of the two-velocity model of the two-phase Navier–Stokes equations. This results in the ability to model the shear layer, rather than resolving it, by allowing for a velocity discontinuity in the direction(s) tangential to the interface. In a previous paper (Remmerswaal and Veldman (2022), arXiv:2209.14934 ) we have discussed the transport of mass and momentum, where the two fluids were not yet coupled. In this paper an implicit coupling of the two fluids is proposed, which imposes continuity of the velocity field in the interface normal direction. The coupling is included in the pressure Poisson problem, and is discretised using a multidimensional generalisation of the ghost fluid method. Furthermore, a discretisation of the diffusive forces is proposed, which leads to recovering the continuous one-velocity solution as the interface shear layer is resolved. The proposed two-velocity formulation is validated and compared to our one-velocity formulation, where we consider a multitude of two-phase flow problems. It is demonstrated that the proposed two-velocity model is able to consistently, and sharply, approximate solutions to the inviscid Euler equations, where velocity discontinuities appear analytically as well. Furthermore, the proposed two-velocity model is shown to accurately model the interface shear layer in viscous problems, and it is successfully applied to the simulation of breaking waves where the model was used to sharply capture free surface instabilities.

Introduction

The numerical simulation of two-phase flow is of great interest to engineering problems (e.g. liquid sloshing), as well as to fundamental research into fluid flow (e.g. to study the physics of liquid impacts).
One of the main challenges in performing such simulations is the computational effort required to resolve the shear layer (SL) present at the phase interface.
The shear layer is a thin (relative to other length scales of interest, we will soon justify this by comparing such length scales) region at the interface where viscous effects play a role, and is for example present on the wave crest of a breaking wave, as illustrated by the green region in
fig.

1
.

The accurate prediction of impact pressures resulting from breaking wave impacts is challenging, in particular because it was found
[
14
]
that the fragmentation of the interface prior to impact results in a high variability of the local interface shape, and thus a high variability of the resulting impact pressures.
This fragmentation is due to the development of free surface instabilities, such as the Kelvin–Helmholtz (KH) instability, resulting from the shearing gas flow
U_{\tau}
past the wave crest, as illustrated by the large red arrow in
fig.

1
.
In order to numerically capture such free surface instabilities, a sharp and accurate model for two-phase flow is required, and we must moreover have a sufficiently fine mesh to resolve the most unstable KH wavelength, which will be denoted by
\lambda_{\text{KH}}
.
To obtain a relative impression of the length scales mentioned, we will now compare the most unstable KH wavelength with an estimate of the shear layer thickness
\delta_{\text{SL}}
.
Doing so we are able to judge whether or not additional mesh refinement is required if we want to resolve the shear layer as well.

Using the single-phase boundary layer theory by
Blasius, 1907
we find that the
.99
thickness of the shear layer in the gas phase can be estimated by

\delta_{\text{SL}}(\tau)=4.91\sqrt{\frac{\mu^{g}\tau}{\rho^{g}U_{\tau}}},

(2)

where
\tau
is the coordinate along the length of the shear layer which starts at
\tau=0
,
\rho^{\pi}
denotes the density of the
\pi
-phase (for
\pi\in\{l,g\}
) and
\mu^{\pi}
denotes the corresponding dynamic viscosity.
Here
U_{\tau}
denotes the magnitude of the free stream velocity, see also
fig.

1
.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of a breaking wave moving towards the left.
The shear layer thickness
\delta_{\text{SL}}(\tau)
and most unstable wavelength
\lambda_{\text{KH}}
are indicated.
Note that for illustrational purposes the shear layer is shown much thicker, relative to the most unstable wavelength, than it would usually be.

According to linearised potential flow theory (see e.g.
Lamb, 1932
) the most unstable KH wavelength is given by

\lambda_{\text{KH}}=\frac{3\pi\sigma(\rho^{l}+\rho^{g})}{\rho^{g}\rho^{l}U_{\tau}^{2}},

(3)

where
\sigma
denotes the surface tension coefficient.
It follows that the ratio of viscous and inertial length scales is given by

\frac{\delta_{\text{SL}}(\lambda_{\text{KH}})}{\lambda_{\text{KH}}}=4.91\sqrt{\frac{\mu^{g}}{\rho^{g}U_{\tau}\lambda_{\text{KH}}}}=4.91\sqrt{\frac{\rho^{l}\mu^{g}}{3\pi\sigma(\rho^{g}+\rho^{l})}}\sqrt{U_{\tau}}\approx\frac{\sqrt{U_{\tau}}}{40},

(4)

where we have substituted the fluid properties of water and air.
In particular, if we consider velocities up to
30\%
of the speed of sound in air, in order to justify our assumption of incompressibility, we find that

\frac{\delta_{\text{SL}}(\lambda_{\text{KH}})}{\lambda_{\text{KH}}}\approx\frac{\sqrt{0.3\times 330}}{40}\approx\frac{1}{4}.

(5)

Hence we find, according to this admittedly simplified analysis, that for incompressible flow the shear layer is at least a factor four thinner than the most unstable wavelength, and therefore resolving the shear layer always yields a much more stringent resolution restriction than one would obtain when wanting to resolve only the most unstable wavelength
\lambda_{\text{KH}}
.

The aforementioned analysis motivates the use of a two-velocity model, wherein each phase is described by its own momentum equation, resulting in two distinct velocity fields which will be denoted by
\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{l},\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{g}
.
If the shear layer is not resolved, then numerically the velocity component(s) tangential to the interface can be viewed as discontinuous.
Therefore a two-velocity model can be utilised to permit such a velocity discontinuity, thereby modelling the shear layer rather than resolving it.
We impose that only the interface normal component of the velocity field is continuous (hence assuming that no phase change can occur)

\left\llbracket u_{\eta}\right\rrbracket=0,

(6)

thereby allowing for a discontinuity in the tangential component(s) of the velocity field.
Here the jump in a quantity is defined as the difference between the gas and liquid value

\left\llbracket\varphi\right\rrbracket\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\varphi^{g}-\varphi^{l},\quad\{\kern-3.125pt\{\varphi\}\kern-3.125pt\}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\varphi^{g}+\varphi^{l},

(7)

where we have introduced a complimentary notation for the sum over the phases.
In one-velocity models continuity of the full velocity field is imposed instead

\left\llbracket\bm{\mathbf{u}}\right\rrbracket=\bm{\mathbf{0}}.

(8)

Several multi-equation models for two-phase flow can be found in the literature, resulting in at most two mass conservation equations, two momentum conservation equations, two energy equations and an equation that governs the advection of the interface.
In
Saurel and Abgrall, 1999
a hyperbolic seven equation model (2-2-2-1) is considered for the simulation of two-phase compressible flow.
The energy equations are omitted in
Preisig and Zimmermann, 2010
, who therefore consider a five equation model (2-2-0-1), and in
Favrie et al., 2014
a six equation model (2-1-2-1) is considered where a momentum equation for the mixture is considered.

For incompressible two-phase flow not much can be found in the literature regarding multi-equation models (in particular two-velocity models).
Note that for incompressible flow the mass conservation equations are implied by the equation that governs the advection of the interface, together with a suitable incompressibility constraint (which we will count as a mass conservation equation).
In
Desjardins and Moureau, 2010
and
Vukčević et al., 2017
two separate equations are used for the transport of momentum, but after each time step the velocity jump is removed in the pressure Poisson problem by imposing
eq.

8
.
In our terminology this constitutes as a one-velocity model (1-1-0-1).
Our proposed two-velocity model is a sharp four equation model (1-2-0-1) wherein the velocity is permitted to be discontinuous according to
eq.

6
.

3
Poisson problem

We will now focus our attention on the most crucial part of the discretisation for our proposed two-velocity formulation: the coupling of the two fluids via the pressure Poisson equation.
Contrary to the approach followed in
[

We start by briefly discussing the pressure Poisson problem discrete in time, but continuous in space.
Then we discuss discretisation approaches for the divergence as well as gradient operator in the presence of a velocity discontinuity, resulting in a generalisation of the ghost fluid method (GFM)
[
19
]

The resulting method is then validated using an academic test case, and slightly altered to ensure that conservation properties are adhered to.
We then discuss the relation between the resulting one- and two-velocity formulations.

3.1
The pressure Poisson problem

Before we discuss the full discretisation of the pressure Poisson problem shown in

T5.II

Continuous in space, the velocity update due to the pressure is given by

The cut-cell divergence operator is illustrated in

The advantage of using such a cut-cell approximation as opposed to using, e.g., the GFM, is that the cut-cell method naturally satisfies the discrete equivalent of Gauss’s theorem (assuming

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathbf{u}}\hskip 4.30554ptdV=\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{n}\hskip 4.30554ptdS\quad\xrightarrow[\text{equivalent}]{\text{discrete}}\quad\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\mathfrak{D}\{\kern-3.125pt\{au\}\kern-3.125pt\}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\Gamma}}|f|u_{f},

where

The image of the linear operator is nontrivial because this linear operator has a nontrivial null space spanned by

The imposition of

We will now define the jump interpolant
\mathfrak{J}
which, for a staggered velocity field
u^{\pi}\in\mathcal{F}^{h}
, approximates the vector-valued jump at the face centroid

\mathfrak{J}:[\mathcal{F}^{h}]^{2}\rightarrow[\mathcal{F}^{h}]^{d},\quad(\mathfrak{J}(u^{g},u^{l}))_{f}\approx\left\llbracket\bm{\mathbf{u}}\right\rrbracket(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{f}).

(30)

The staggered velocity
u^{\pi,(n)}_{f}
is defined if and only if the corresponding staggered volume fraction is non-zero
\widetilde{\alpha}^{\pi,(n)}_{f}>0
, and therefore a mixed face is defined as one for which both staggered volume fractions are non-zero.
It follows that for a mixed face

we can easily compute the face normal component of the jump as
\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket_{f}
, resulting in the face normal part
\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket_{f}\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}
of the vector-valued jump.
The jump interpolant
\mathfrak{J}
is then defined by specifying how we approximate the parts of the vector-valued jump which are tangential to
\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}
: the face tangential parts.

For each face
f
we define a subset
\mathcal{F}^{\perp_{k}}(f)\subset\mathcal{F}
, for
k=1,\ldots,d-1
, as the set of neighbouring faces whose face normal is perpendicular to that of
f
, as shown by the horizontal arrows in
fig.

3

These subsets provide a way to interpolate the face tangential parts of the velocity jump, resulting in the following definition of the jump interpolant for mixed faces

\mathfrak{J}^{1}(u^{g},u^{l})_{f}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\underbrace{\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket_{f}\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}}_{\text{face normal part}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\underbrace{\sum_{h\in\mathcal{F}^{\perp_{k}}(f)}w^{\perp_{k}}_{f,h}\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket_{h}\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{h}}_{\text{$k$-th face tangential part}},

(31)

where face tangential parts are averaged using the weights
w^{\perp_{k}}_{f,h}
, resulting in a first-order accurate interpolant.
The weight
w^{\perp_{k}}_{f,h}
is defined such that it is non-zero only for mixed faces
h
(for which the jump
\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket_{h}
is defined)

w^{\perp_{k}}_{f,h}=\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}^{g}_{h}\widetilde{\alpha}^{l}_{h}}{\sum_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{\perp_{k}}(f)}\widetilde{\alpha}^{g}_{h^{\prime}}\widetilde{\alpha}^{l}_{h^{\prime}}},

(32)

and moreover is such that the interpolant varies continuously in time, as a consequence of the (staggered) volume fractions varying continuously in time.

Given the jump interpolant
\mathfrak{J}^{1}
we are now able to compute the interface normal component of the jump in a staggered field.
We will use this in conjunction with the ghost fluid method (GFM), which will be introduced next.

3.4
The ghost fluid method

In
Liu et al., 2000

We denote the value and derivative jumps as

\zeta_{f}=\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}^{I}_{f}),\quad\xi_{f}=\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}\cdot\left\llbracket\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p\right\rrbracket(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}^{I}_{f}),

(33)

respectively, where
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}^{I}_{f}
is located on the line connecting

\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{c_{2}}

We let
\widetilde{\phi}^{\pi}
denote the unsigned and nondimensional face projected distance (for which

\pi

\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}^{I}_{f}
.

Imposing the jump conditions at first-order accuracy results in the GFM gradient (a derivation is given in

\mathfrak{g}^{\pi}_{f}(p,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{(\mathfrak{S}p)_{f}+\zeta(\mathfrak{S}\chi^{l})_{f}+{\Xi}^{\pi}_{f}\xi_{f}}{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\phi}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}_{f}}\approx\frac{1}{\rho^{\pi}}\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}\cdot(\nabla p^{\pi})(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{f}),

(34)

where
\chi^{l}\in\mathcal{C}^{h}
denotes the liquid indicator function for which
\chi^{l}_{c}\in\{0,1\}

{\Xi}^{l}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\widetilde{\phi}^{g}\rho^{g},\quad{\Xi}^{g}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\widetilde{\phi}^{l}\rho^{l},

(35)

such that

\left\llbracket\mathfrak{g}(p,\xi)\right\rrbracket=\xi

The GFM requires the jump of the face normal derivative of the pressure, rather than the interface normal derivative given by
eq.

22
.
We can write the jump of the face normal derivative in terms of the interface normal and tangential jumps as follows (for simplicity in 2D)

\xi_{f}=\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}\cdot\left[\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}\left\llbracket\frac{1}{\rho}\partial_{\eta}p\right\rrbracket(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{I}_{f})+\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}}_{f}\left\llbracket\frac{1}{\rho}\partial_{\tau}p\right\rrbracket(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{I}_{f})\right],

(36)

where
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}}_{f}
denotes the vector which is orthogonal to the staggered interface normal
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}
.
Neglecting the interface tangential component (which is multiplied by
\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}}_{f}
) of the pressure gradient jump, as proposed by

\xi^{\text{GFM}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\bm{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}}{\delta t}\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}\cdot\mathfrak{J}(u^{g},u^{l})^{***},

(37)

where for notational convenience we let
\mathfrak{J}(u^{g},u^{l})^{***}=\mathfrak{J}(u^{g,***},u^{l,***})
.
Note that
eq.

37

\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\tau}}}_{f}\neq 0
, and therefore results in undesired smearing of the jump condition given by
eq.

22
.

In
Egan and Gibou, 2020
the xGFM is proposed as a generalisation to the GFM which resolves the aforementioned inconsistency.
The xGFM solves a sequence of Poisson problems, where each such Poisson problem is discretised using the GFM method.
This approach will not be considered here.
Instead we will propose a method to impose

6

22
.

3.5
A multidimensional variant of the ghost fluid method

We will now introduce a multidimensional generalisation of the GFM (MDGFM) which consistently imposes
eq.

22
, that is, without neglecting the interface tangential components of the pressure gradient jump.

Using the jump interpolant
eq.

31
we can directly (and consistently) impose
eq.

22
, resulting in

\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}\cdot\mathfrak{J}\left(\mathfrak{g}^{g}(p,\xi),\mathfrak{g}^{l}(p,\xi)\right)=\frac{\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}\cdot\mathfrak{J}(u^{g},u^{l})^{***}}{\delta t},

(38)

where the gradient
\mathfrak{g}^{\pi}(p,\xi)
is given by
eq.

34
with unknown
p,\xi
.
Note that
\left\llbracket\mathfrak{g}(p,\xi)\right\rrbracket=\xi
and therefore, according to the definition of the jump interpolant
eq.

31
, the left hand-side of
eq.

38
will depend only on the gradient jump
\xi
and in particular not on the pressure
p
.
This implies that the gradient jump can be found independently from the pressure, which is remarkable since we have not imposed any condition on the interface tangential component of the gradient jump.

Regardless of this remarkable independence, we can use
eq.

38
to express the jump
\xi_{f}
in terms of its neighbouring values
\xi_{h}

\xi_{f}=\delta t^{-1}{\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket^{***}_{f}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{F}^{\perp_{k}}(f)}w^{\perp_{k}}_{f,h}\left(\delta t^{-1}\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket^{***}_{h}-\xi_{h}\right)\ratio{f,h},

(39)

where the ratio of face normal components of the interface normal
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}
is given by

\ratio{f,h}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{h}}{\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}}.

(40)

We find that the ratio
\ratio{f,h}
tends to infinity
\ratio{f,h}\rightarrow\pm\infty
if the interface normal
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}
is tangent to the face
f
.
The aforementioned unboundedness results in an ill-posed linear system of equations when solving for the jump
\xi
, making it impossible to satisfy
eq.

38

eq.

31

The remarkable independence of the jump
\xi
on the pressure

We propose the following redefinition of the jump interpolant (cf.
eq.

31

\mathfrak{J}^{\beta}(u^{g},u^{l})_{f}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left[\beta_{f}^{-1}\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket_{f}+(1-\beta_{f}^{-1})(u^{g}_{h^{g}}-u^{l}_{h^{l}})\right]\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{F}^{\perp_{k}}(f)}w^{\perp_{k}}_{f,h}\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket_{h}\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{h},

(41)

where
\beta_{f}\geq 0
is some non-negative coefficient (when solving
eq.

41

\xi_{f}

eq.

42

\beta_{f}
appears rather than
\beta_{f}^{-1}
, and therefore
\beta_{f}=0
is permitted) and
h^{\pi}\in\mathcal{F}
are neighbouring faces with
\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}=\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{h^{\pi}}
,
\rho^{l}\widetilde{\alpha}^{l}_{h^{l}}>\rho^{g}\widetilde{\alpha}^{g}_{h^{l}}
and
\rho^{g}\widetilde{\alpha}^{g}_{h^{g}}>\rho^{l}\widetilde{\alpha}^{l}_{h^{g}}
, as shown in
fig.

3

We let either
h^{g}=f
or
h^{l}=f
depending on which of the corresponding masses is largest inside
\omega_{f}

Thus we average two different approximations of the face normal component of the jump.
Note that when
\beta\equiv 1

eq.

31

We will now construct the coefficient
\beta_{f}
such that the jump
\xi_{f}

To this end, we again (as we did with
eq.

31

38

\xi_{f}
in terms of the neighbouring jump values, which now results in

\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\xi_{f}^{\text{MDGFM}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\delta t^{-1}{\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket^{***}_{f}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{F}^{\perp_{k}}(f)}w^{\perp_{k}}_{f,h}\left(\delta t^{-1}\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket^{***}_{h}-\xi_{h}\right)\beta_{f}\ratio{f,h}\\

The previously (when we used

\beta_{f}\ratio{f,h}

Note that whereas the reciprocal

Suppose that
\beta_{f}
is a function of the absolute value of the face normal component of the interface normal

\beta_{f}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\beta(\left\lvert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}\right\rvert).

(43)

We are now free to model the function
\beta(x)

First and foremost we require that
\beta_{f}\left\lvert\ratio{f,h}\right\rvert
is bounded at all times.
The ratio
\ratio{f,h}
can be bounded by (with equality in 2D)

\left\lvert\ratio{f,h}\right\rvert=\frac{\left\lvert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{h}\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{\sqrt{1-(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f})^{2}}}{\left\lvert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{\eta}}}_{f}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}\right\rvert},

3.7
The conservative nondimensional distance function

In the derivation of the GFM
[
19
]
the distance function
\widetilde{\phi}
is indeed an actual distance function, as illustrated in
fig.

4
.
Besides the local consistency of the gradient operator, global conservation properties are also of interest.
To this end we compute the contribution to linear momentum by the (MD)GFM gradient operator

\sum_{\omega(\mathcal{F})}\bm{\mathbf{n}}\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\mathfrak{g}(p,\xi)\}\kern-3.125pt\}=\sum_{\omega(\mathcal{F})}\bm{\mathbf{n}}\frac{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}(\mathfrak{S}p+\zeta\mathfrak{S}\chi^{l})+{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho{\Xi}\}\kern-3.125pt\}\xi}}{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\phi}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}}.

(52)

Whereas usually, when considering the one-velocity formulation in the absence of a value jump, we would find

\sum_{\omega(\mathcal{F})}\bm{\mathbf{n}}\mathfrak{S}p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eqn:notation:adjointness}}}{{=}}-\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\mathfrak{D}\bm{\mathbf{n}}=\bm{\mathbf{0}},

(53)

we now find that none of the contributions in the right-hand side of
eq.

52
vanish.
The fact that we do not end up with a result such as
eq.

53
is due to the fact that the GFM uses a definition of mass, given by
\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\phi}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}
, which is different from the definition of mass used by the convection operator, which was given by
\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}
.
Therefore we propose to use a novel definition of distance, given by
\widetilde{\phi}^{\pi}=\widetilde{\alpha}^{\pi}
, such that the contribution to linear momentum is now given by

\sum_{\omega(\mathcal{F})}\bm{\mathbf{n}}\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}^{(n+1)}\rho(u^{(n+1)}-u^{***})\}\kern-3.125pt\}=-\delta t\sum_{\omega(\mathcal{F})}\bm{\mathbf{n}}\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\mathfrak{g}(p,\xi)\}\kern-3.125pt\}=-\delta t\sum_{\omega(\mathcal{F})}\bm{\mathbf{n}}\zeta\mathfrak{S}\chi^{l}.

We will use this alternative choice for the distance function, and we refer to the resulting methods as the conservative GFM (CGFM) and the conservative MDGFM (CMDGFM) respectively.

\mathfrak{g}^{\pi}(p,\xi)=\frac{\mathfrak{S}p+\zeta\mathfrak{S}\chi^{l}+{\Xi}^{\pi}\xi}{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}},\quad{\Xi}^{l}=-\widetilde{\alpha}^{g}\rho^{g},\quad{\Xi}^{g}=\widetilde{\alpha}^{l}\rho^{l},

3.8
Comparison to the one-velocity formulation

We now discuss the discretisation of the Poisson problem for the one-velocity formulation, as given by

For the one-velocity formulation we use the mass weighted average of the CGFM gradient operator, which we denote as

\bar{\mathfrak{g}}(p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\mathfrak{g}(p,\xi)\}\kern-3.125pt\}}{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}}=\frac{\mathfrak{S}p+\zeta\mathfrak{S}\chi^{l}}{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}},

and no longer depends on the gradient jump
\xi

Using this gradient operator in

\bar{u}^{(n+1)}=\bar{u}^{***}-\delta t\frac{\mathfrak{S}p+\zeta\mathfrak{S}\chi^{l}}{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}}.

Note that the right-hand side of

(59)

Furthermore, the numerical models for diffusion and gravity, which will be discussed in

It follows that the one-velocity formulation can alternatively be obtained by simply letting

within the two-velocity formulation.
The difference being that usually we first impose continuity of the velocity field
\left\llbracket\bm{\mathbf{u}}\right\rrbracket=0
and subsequently manipulate the analytical equations
before
discretisation, whereas now we see that we can also interpret the one-velocity model as a special instance of the two-velocity model, where we have substituted

This is a very important property, since, when comparing our proposed one- and two-velocity models in

Such an approach is in the same vein as

discretise first, substitute next

4
Diffusion

Thus far we have addressed
1.1
and
1.2
and therefore we now have a sharp model for the two-phase Euler equations.
We however do not completely want to neglect viscosity, and in particular if the mesh is sufficiently fine, we want our two-velocity model to converge to the viscous one-velocity model which has a continuous velocity field (as given by
1.3
).
This will be achieved by the diffusion model, but before we discuss this, we will introduce the diffusion model for the one-velocity formulation.

4.1
One-velocity formulation

For the discretisation of diffusion we choose to use a diffuse (i.e. not sharp) approach, where we simply let the dynamic viscosity be a weighted (using the volume fraction) average of the dynamic viscosities per phase, and subsequently use standard single-phase operators for the discretisation of the strain tensor as well as divergence operator.
Using the implicit midpoint rule for time integration results in the following stress tensor

(see also

T3.I
)

(60)

where the gradient and strain

The diagonal components of the strain tensor are located on the centroids of the centred control volumes, whereas the off-diagonal components are located on the edges of the centred control volumes.
In particular, the

It follows that the definition of the symmetric part of the gradient tensor

\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}^{T}

The gradient operator is defined as the negative adjoint of

\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\widetilde{\mathfrak{D}}^{T}

The volume fraction at the edges of a control volume are interpolated from the centred volume fractions, thereby allowing us to compute the spatially varying dynamic viscosity

as the weighted geometric average of the dynamic viscosity per phase

The weighted arithmetic average of the dynamic viscosity was found to lead to continuity of the velocity too quickly under mesh refinement for a viscous test problem using the two-velocity formulation, and we have therefore chosen to use the weighted geometric average instead.
We also use the weighted geometric average in the one-velocity formulation to ensure that any observed differences, between the one- and two-velocity formulations, are in fact due to the velocity jump condition at the interface, and not due to a different viscosity averaging.

4.2
Two-velocity formulation

In developing a two-velocity generalisation for the diffusion model we require the following: total (i.e. the sum over the phases) linear momentum should be conserved, and if the velocity field is continuous we want to recover exactly the same diffusion model as used for the one-velocity formulation.

A simple way to ensure that both of these conditions hold is to let the diffusion operator act on the equivalent velocity field one would obtain in the one-velocity model.
That is, we define (cf.
eq.

59
)

\bar{u}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}^{(n+1)}\rho u^{*}\}\kern-3.125pt\}}{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}^{(n+1)}},

(63)

and compute
\bar{u}^{**}
using the implicit midpoint rule as given by
eq.

T3.I
, where
T^{\mu}
is defined by
eq.

60
.
Provided with the stress tensor
T^{\mu}
, the velocity
u^{\pi,**}
can be computed according to
eq.

T3.II
.

This model is not based on consistent and sharp modelling of the diffusive stresses at the interface, but does result in the desired behaviour: the velocity discontinuity vanishes under mesh refinement for viscous flows.
This will be demonstrated in
section

6.6
.

5
Gravity

The nonconservative gravity force (where
\chi^{\pi}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\in\{0,1\}
is the phase indicator, and
\bm{\mathbf{g}}
the gravitational acceleration)

\bm{\mathbf{F}}=\{\kern-3.125pt\{\chi\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}\bm{\mathbf{g}},

(64)

is deceptively simple, but its discretisation is nontrivial nonetheless.
Besides consistency, well-balancedness is of importance, which refers here to the existence of a steady state solution.
That is, we consider the existence of some pressure
p^{\dagger}
, such that

\nabla p^{\dagger}=\bm{\mathbf{F}}.

(65)

Here we consider a steady state in the absence of surface tension, and thus consider a linear interface given by the zero level set of

q(\bm{\mathbf{x}})=\bm{\mathbf{N}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{x}}-S,\quad\bm{\mathbf{N}}=-\frac{\bm{\mathbf{g}}}{\left\lvert\bm{\mathbf{g}}\right\rvert_{2}},

(66)

where we permit the gravitational acceleration
\bm{\mathbf{g}}
to be in any direction.
It follows that the following pressure

p^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})=-\{\kern-3.125pt\{\chi(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}\left\lvert\bm{\mathbf{g}}\right\rvert_{2}q(\bm{\mathbf{x}}),

(67)

results in an exact balance between pressure and the gravity force.
A discretisation of the gravity force
eq.

64
for which such an exact balance always (for any
\bm{\mathbf{g}}
) exists is called well-balanced
[
22
]
.

Note that the pressure given by
eq.

67
is continuous, despite the discontinuous density, since
q
vanishes at the interface.

5.1
Overview

Integration over a staggered control volume of the face normal component of the gravity force
eq.

64
results in

\frac{1}{|\omega_{f}|}\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}\cdot\int_{\omega_{f}}\bm{\mathbf{F}}\hskip 4.30554ptdV=\{\kern-3.125pt\{\widetilde{\alpha}\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}_{f}\bm{\mathbf{n}}_{f}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{g}},

(68)

where we make use of
|c|\alpha^{l}_{c}=\int_{c}\chi^{l}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\hskip 4.30554ptdV
(cf.
eq.

9
).
This is the most straightforward discretisation of the gravity force.
It turns out, however, that this model is ill-balanced (i.e. not well-balanced) whenever the interface is not aligned with the grid.

The model can be made well-balanced by modifying the definition of mass that appears inside the gravity force
\bm{\mathbf{F}}

[
36
]
.
The resulting ‘gravity consistent averaging’ of the density is such that the discrete equivalent of
\nabla\times\bm{\mathbf{F}}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}
holds in a steady state, which implies that
\bm{\mathbf{F}}=\nabla p^{*}
for some scalar potential
p^{*}
.
Modification of the mass however implies that the mass appearing in the pressure gradient differs from the one used in the definition of momentum, and thus linear momentum is no longer conserved by the pressure gradient, which is undesirable.

Gravity can also be modelled by analytically subtracting the hydrostatic pressure from the pressure
[
22
]

P^{\pi}=p^{\pi}-\rho^{\pi}\bm{\mathbf{g}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{x}},

(69)

which results in a modified jump condition on the newly defined reduced pressure

\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket p\right\rrbracket-\left\llbracket\rho\right\rrbracket\bm{\mathbf{g}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{x}}=-\sigma\kappa-\left\llbracket\rho\right\rrbracket\bm{\mathbf{g}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{x}},\quad\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in I.

(70)

This jump condition can then be taken into account as a value jump in the C(MD)GFM method.
This model is well-balanced
[
22
]
and reproduces the steady state
eq.

67
exactly provided that the globally linear interface is exactly reconstructed.

Each of the three methods (firstly
eq.

68
, secondly the gravity consistent method from
Wemmenhove et al., 2015
and thirdly the reduced pressure approach given by
eq.

70
) are consistent, but the first lacks well-balancedness, and all of them lack conservation properties.
In what follows we therefore propose a new well-balanced and mimetic gravity model, which yields the correct contribution to linear momentum, as well as a correct exchange between kinetic and gravitational potential energy.

5.2
A mimetic gravity model

We propose a new gravity model based on the consistent evolution of the gravitational potential energy density
G\in\mathcal{C}^{h}

G_{c}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\frac{\bm{\mathbf{g}}\cdot\{\kern-3.125pt\{\rho\bm{\mathbf{M}}_{1,c}\}\kern-3.125pt\}}{|c|},

6.3
Kelvin–Helmholtz instability

We now consider two fluids which initially have a velocity discontinuity tangential to the interface:
U^{g}=\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{g},U^{l}=\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{x}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{l}
with
\left\llbracket U\right\rrbracket\neq 0
, in the absence of gravity and viscosity, see also
section

6.1.2
.
The Weber number, which is a nondimensional measure of the importance of inertia relative to that of capillary forces, is given by

\text{We}=\frac{\rho^{g}\rho^{l}\left\llbracket U\right\rrbracket^{2}\lambda}{2\pi\sigma(\rho^{g}+\rho^{l})}.

(93)

For small amplitude perturbations of a given wavenumber
k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}
, the evolution of the interface amplitude is as follows
[
15
, Art. 267 & 268]

\delta(t)={\frac{e^{-i\aleph_{+}t}+e^{-i\aleph_{-}t}}{2}}\delta_{0},

(94)

where the frequency
\aleph_{\pm}
can be found from a dispersion relation.
This dispersion relation yields an expression for the frequency
\aleph_{\pm}
as function of the wavenumber, and is given by (recall that the capillary time scale is given by
eq.

92
)

\aleph_{\pm}=k\frac{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\rho U\}\kern-3.125pt\}}{\{\kern-3.125pt\{\rho\}\kern-3.125pt\}}\pm\frac{2\pi}{T_{\sigma}}\sqrt{1-\text{We}}.

(95)

It follows that if
\text{We}>1
we find that
\Im\aleph_{\pm}\neq 0
and therefore results in exponential growth of the interface amplitude given by
eq.

94
.
Here
\Re\aleph_{\pm}
and
\Im\aleph_{\pm}
denote the real and imaginary part of
\aleph_{\pm}
respectively.

We use
\alpha(L)
-AMR with
h_{0}=2^{-3}\lambda
and a domain of size
[0,\lambda]\times[-1.5\lambda,1.5\lambda]
, the initial amplitude is given by
\delta_{0}=10^{-2}
and the wavelength is given by
\lambda=2\pi
.

6.4

We now consider the effect which gravity has on the stability of the interface.
Again, we consider the motion of the interface between two inviscid fluids, where the interface is initially as shown in

[

, Art. 267]

T_{g}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho^{l}+\rho^{g}}{\rho^{l}-\rho^{g}}\frac{2\pi\lambda}{\left\lvert g\right\rvert}}.

(100)

98
, the interface is unstable w.r.t. an infinitesimal perturbation if
\text{Bo}>1

6.5
Plateau–Rayleigh instability

\captionbox

Example interface contours resulting from a Plateau–Rayleigh instability, for

A liquid jet (a cylindrical column of water), in the absence of gravity, can become unstable due to surface tension and evolve into a sequence of droplets, as shown in
section

6.5
.
Such a Plateau–Rayleigh instability happens when the resulting surface area of the droplets is smaller than that of the original liquid jet.

With the Plateau–Rayleigh instability we find that only a small part of the interface is greatly deformed (see
section

6.5
), suggesting a localised mesh refinement criterion based on the interface curvature.
The refinement criterion
\kappa(L)
-AMR is such that the following condition is ensured to hold

\varepsilon_{\text{coarsen}}^{\kappa}\leq\max_{c\in\mathcal{B}}h_{c}|\kappa_{c}|\leq\varepsilon_{\text{refine}}^{\kappa},

(101)

where
\varepsilon_{\text{coarsen}}^{\kappa}
and
\varepsilon_{\text{refine}}^{\kappa}
are nondimensional tolerances, and
h_{c}=|c|^{1/d}
.
This means that if
h_{c}|\kappa_{c}|>\varepsilon_{\text{refine}}^{\kappa}
for at least one
c\in\mathcal{B}
then the block is refined, and if
h_{c}|\kappa_{c}|<\varepsilon_{\text{coarsen}}^{\kappa}
for all
c\in\mathcal{B}
then the block is coarsened.
Coarsening will likely double the nondimensional curvature and therefore we must ensure that

2\varepsilon_{\text{coarsen}}^{\kappa}<\varepsilon_{\text{refine}}^{\kappa},

(102)

such that the coarsened block isn’t directly eligible for refinement in the next time step.
We use
\varepsilon_{\text{refine}}^{\kappa}=0.2
and
\varepsilon_{\text{coarsen}}^{\kappa}=0.06
.

This test case was also considered in
Popinet, 2009
.
Therein the interface along the axis of the cylinder, the
x
-axis, is perturbed according to

r(t,x)=R(1+\epsilon e^{i(\aleph t+kx)}),

(103)

where
r(x,t)
denotes the radius of the jet and
k
denotes the wavenumber of the perturbation.
The dispersion relation for perturbations on a liquid jet, for an inviscid fluid without the presence of a surrounding gas, is given by
[
26
]

(\aleph T_{\sigma})^{2}+kR\frac{I_{1}(kR)}{I_{0}(kR)}\left(1-(kR)^{2}\right)=0,

(104)

where
I_{n}
are the (non-negative) modified Bessel functions of the first kind.
It follows that the jet is stable w.r.t. perturbations of wavenumber
k
provided that

kR\geq 1\quad\iff\quad\lambda\leq 2\pi R.

(105)

That is, the inviscid jet is stable if the wavelength of the perturbation is at most the circumference of the jet.
For finite values of the Laplace number (defined as in
eq.

86
) the following approximate generalisation of
eq.

104
was proposed in
Eggers and Villermaux, 2008

(\aleph T_{\sigma})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(kR)^{2}\left(1-(kR)^{2}\right)+(\aleph T_{\sigma})i(kR)^{2}{\text{La}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=0.

(106)

We use the two-velocity model with the fluid properties as given in
[
21
]
.
Importantly, the Laplace number is given by
\text{La}=23.834
and
kR=0.2\pi
such that the liquid jet should become unstable.
We nondimensionalise time using the capillary timescale, which is given by

T_{\sigma}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho^{l}R^{3}}{\sigma}}.

(107)

The temporal evolution of the interface is shown in
section

6.5
.
Indeed we find that the liquid jet becomes unstable, and eventually results in the pinch-off of a sequence of droplets.
We find that the
\kappa
-AMR refinement criterion successfully refines the interface where needed, resulting in an efficient refinement strategy.
Furthermore, the results illustrate that topological changes of the interface are no problem for the jump conditions discussed in
section

3
.

7
Discussion

We have motivated the use of, and proposed a discretisation of, a sharp two-velocity model for two-phase flow.
The numerical model, which imposes continuity of only the interface normal component of velocity, is of particular interest in the simulation of high density ratio and convection dominated two-phase flow, such as the simulation of breaking wave impacts.
Such flow problems result in thin shear layers, where viscous effects dominate, and which are infeasible to resolve entirely.
In a previous paper
[
28
]
we discussed the discretisation of the equations governing conservation of mass and momentum, whereas in this paper we considered the implicit coupling of the two fluids via the pressure Poisson equation as well as diffusion.

We have proposed a generalisation of the GFM, referred to as the MDGFM, which allows for the consistent imposition of the
interface normal
component of the pressure gradient, rather than the
face normal
component which is imposed in the GFM.
The MDGFM relies on the definition of a jump interpolant which yields the vector valued jump of our staggered velocity field.
Naive construction of such a jump interpolant results in unboundedness of the resulting interpolation coefficients, which we have resolved by a novel interpolation between two approximations of the face normal component of the jump.
The resulting gradient operator is combined with a cut-cell approximation of the divergence operator, resulting in the convergent approximation of the pressure Poisson problem, for a large range of density ratios.

Furthermore, we have introduced a simple model for diffusion in the presence of a velocity discontinuity.
In particular, the proposed model, which is implicit in time, is defined such that it coincides exactly with the diffusion model for the one-velocity formulation in case the velocity is continuous across the interface.
This approach aims at obtaining the one-velocity formulation when the viscous shear layer is resolved.

Also, a mimetic gravity model was proposed which is shown to be well-balanced, mimics the correct transfer between kinetic and gravitational potential energy, and moreover yields the correct contribution to linear momentum.

We have illustrated the efficacy of the proposed two-velocity formulation by directly comparing it to our one-velocity model.
It has been demonstrated that the well-balancedness of the model is hardly affected by introducing the new jump condition.
Moreover, we have illustrated convergence under mesh refinement of viscous as well as inviscid wave problems, in the presence of surface tension, inertial forces and/or gravity, in both the linear and nonlinear regime.
As can be expected, our two-velocity model performs better than the one-velocity model on inviscid problems, since the latter results in a numerical shear layer, which results in visible oscillations of the velocity, and in some cases results in a lack of convergence.

Topological changes are trivially dealt with, also in 3D, as is demonstrated by the simulation of the Plateau–Rayleigh instability as well as the 3D third-order Stokes wave impact.
The former test problem moreover illustrates the efficiency of the curvature based refinement criterion.

Finally we considered the simulation of breaking waves.
For the third-order Stokes wave we found that, due to the low Reynolds number, the shear layer was easy to resolve, resulting in very similar results when comparing the one- and two-velocity formulations.
We did find that the velocity profile inside the shear layer converges faster for the two-velocity model: a coarser mesh can be used with the two-velocity model.
The simulation of the large gas-pocket impact shows that the two-velocity formulation can effectively be used to model free surface instabilities on breaking waves, without the need for resolving the thin shear layer present at the interface.

In future work it would be interesting to further develop a subgrid model for diffusive stresses at the interface.
The two-velocity model will be used to study the effect of scaling on the development of free surface instabilities in 3D breaking wave impacts.
Therein we want to use an AMR criterion based on resolving the most unstable wavelength.
