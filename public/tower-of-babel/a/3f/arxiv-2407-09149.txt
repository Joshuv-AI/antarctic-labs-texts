Introduction

This is a follow-up of the paper
[
12
]
,
where we studied band projections in spaces of regular operators. Our
aim here is to explore analogous results in the more general context
of Banach lattice algebras. Besides contributing to the theory of
these structures, this serves the purpose of showing that some
properties of spaces of regular operators hold for arbitrary Banach
lattice algebras.

Given a Banach lattice
X
, we can order the space of
operators on
X
using the cone of positive operators,
that is, setting an operator
T\colon X\to X
to be positive if

T(X_{+})\subseteq X_{+}
. In general, however, not every operator can be
expressed as the difference of two positive operators, so the positive
cone need not be generating. Those operators that are
in the span of the positive cone are called regular operators.

The space of regular operators
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
is an ordered vector space, but
it need not be a lattice. It is a remarkable result by Freudenthal,
Kantorovich and Riesz that when
X
is order complete,
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)

is again a Banach lattice when equipped with the regular norm (defined
as
\|T\|_{r}=\|\,|T|\,\|
for
T\in\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
). Since the composition of positive operators
is again positive,
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
is also a Banach algebra.

So in
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
a lattice and an algebraic structure coexist, with the
compatibility condition that the product of positive elements is
positive. In
[
12
]
we used this structure
to study band projections on
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
. More precisely,
if
\{P_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is a family of pairwise disjoint band
projections on
X
, and
\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda
, we
defined for positive operators
T\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(X)
the map

\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}(T)=\bigvee_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Gamma}P_{\alpha}TP_{\beta}.

(1)

We showed that
\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}\colon\mathcal{L}_{+}(X)\to\mathcal{L}_{+}(X)

extends to a unique band projection on
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
, and called band
projections of this form inner.

Even though the definition of inner band projections uses only the
lattice and algebraic operations of
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
, the proof that

Eq.

1
actually defines a band projection relied
heavily on the expression of the Riesz–Kantorovich formulae for the
lattice operations in
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
. One of
the main motivations for this paper is to determine whether we can
extend inner band projections to more general spaces in which a
lattice and an algebraic operation coexist in a manner analogous to
that of
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
.

This leads to the notion of Banach lattice algebras. Formally, a Banach lattice algebra is a Banach lattice together with a
Banach algebra structure in which the product of positive elements is
positive. One of the most important open problems in the theory of
Banach lattice algebras is the following:

Question 1.1

(
[
17
, Question 5.3]
)
.

Is every Banach lattice algebra isometrically (isomorphic) to a
closed subalgebra and sublattice of some space of regular
operators?

It is relevant to this question, and therefore to the theory, to
understand which properties of spaces of regular operators depend only
on the Banach lattice algebra structure, and not on the fact that we
are actually dealing with operators. It is in this line of
investigation where our paper fits in.

The first step towards an abstract formulation of inner band
projections is to determine which elements of a Banach lattice algebra

A
should play the role of the band projections
\{P_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
. It is a classical result
that an operator
P\colon X\to X
on a Banach lattice
X
is a band
projection if and only if
P^{2}=P
and
0\leq P\leq I_{X}
. When
A

has an identity
e
, it seems then
natural to regard the elements
p\in A
satisfying
p^{2}=p
and
0\leq p\leq e
as the analogue of band projections. These
elements, called order idempotents, were already considered by E. A. Alekhno in the context of ordered Banach algebras (see

[
4
]
). For
instance, Alekhno showed that the set of order idempotents forms a Boolean
algebra, just like the set of band projections on a Banach lattice
does. We
shall give an alternative proof of this fact, and show that
the set of order idempotents is order closed.

But for this we will
need to study the ideal generated by the identity element in
A
. This
ideal can be regarded as
the abstract analogue of the center of an order complete Banach
lattice. We will collect results by L. Martignon

[
10
]
, C. B. Huijsmans
[
7
]
and
E. Scheffold
[
14
]
to show that this ideal posseses
the most fundamental
properties of the center: it is lattice and algebra isometric to a
space of continuous functions on a compact Hausdorff space, it is a
projection band, and it is an inverse closed subalgebra of
A
.

And what happens if we don’t have an identity? Can we still define
elements that play the role of band projections? Recently, it was
proved in
[
12
]
that band projections are characterized by their
multiplication operators in the following way: a positive operator

P\colon X\to X
defined on an order complete Banach lattice
X
is a
band projection if and only if the multiplication operator

\begin{array}[]{cccc}L_{P}R_{P}\colon&\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)\\
&T&\longmapsto&PTP\\
\end{array}

is a band projection. Band projections could therefore be defined as those
elements
p\in A_{+}
for which the operator

\begin{array}[]{cccc}L_{p}R_{p}\colon&A&\longrightarrow&A\\
&a&\longmapsto&pap\\
\end{array}

is a band projection. This is analogue to the approach taken
by K. Vala in
[
16
]
to define compact elements in normed
algebras.

Several properties of band projections, in this sense, are
studied. It is shown that the set of band projections
need not be order closed or even commute; in particular, when we have
an identity, band projections and order idempotents may not be the
same. The relation between order idempotents and band projections
appears to be subtle, as it is illustrated with several
finite-dimensional examples. Some conditions guaranteeing that a band
projection is order idempotent are also given.

Much better behaved is the set of left and right band projections,
defined as those positive elements in
A
for which both the left and right
multiplication operators are band projections.
These elements coincide with order idempotents when
an identity is present, and they always commute. Moreover, they
provide the appropiate generalization of band projections that allows
for the abstract construction of inner band projections we were looking
for. Our main result (
Theorem

3.29
) is that (
1
) defines a band
projection in any order complete Banach
lattice algebra as long as the
\{P_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}

are taken to be left and right band projections satisfying
P_{\alpha}P_{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}P_{\alpha}
(where
\delta_{\alpha\beta}
is
the Kronecker delta). To prove it, we cannot resort directly to the
Riesz–Kantorovich formulae, as done in

[
12
]
; instead, we have to work with the
multiplication operators
L_{P_{\alpha}}R_{P_{\beta}}
to translate
properties from elements in
A
to operators acting on
A
(i.e., to elements
in
\mathcal{L}_{r}(A)
). Even before reaching inner band projections, many of our
results will use this technique, that allows for the generalization of
properties from spaces of regular operators to Banach lattice
algebras. We are not aware of any other systematic use of this idea in
the context of Banach lattice algebras, but one can find it already mentioned or
implicitly used in some parts of the literature (for instance, in

[
6
,
9
,
10
,
17
]
).

Spaces of regular operators

In this section we review some properties of the space of
regular operators over a Banach lattice that will motivate
our study of Banach lattice algebras. The reader familiar with the
topic can therefore skip it, except maybe for

2.4
,
2.5
and
2.5
. With these
exceptions, all the contents are classical, and may be found in

[
1
,

,

.

2.1
Regular operators

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach lattices, and let
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
denote
the set of (linear and bounded) operators from
X
to
Y
. We say that an operator
T\colon X\to Y
is
positive
if
T(X_{+})\subseteq Y_{+}
. The set of
positive operators
\mathcal{L}_{+}(X,Y)
forms a cone in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
. With
this cone,
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
is an ordered Banach space, but it need
not be a lattice; in fact, it may even be the case that
\mathcal{L}_{+}(X,Y)-\mathcal{L}_{+}(X,Y)
is a
proper subset of
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
. The elements of

\mathcal{L}_{+}(X,Y)-\mathcal{L}_{+}(X,Y)
are called
regular operators
, and the set
of regular operators is denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X,Y)
. Regular operators are
those operators that can be written as the difference of two positive
operators; equivalently, an operator
T
is regular if there exists a
positive operator
S\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(X,Y)
such that
T\leq S
.

The space of regular operators
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X,Y)
need not be a Banach lattice either.
It was first shown by Freudenthal,
Kantorovich and Riesz that, when
Y
is
order complete
(that is, when any bounded above
set has supremum),
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X,Y)
is a vector lattice. Later, an
appropiate norm was introduced in this
space, so as to make it a Banach lattice. More precisely:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach lattices. If
Y
is order complete, then

\mathcal{L}_{r}(X,Y)
is a Banach lattice, with supremum and infimum
given by

\displaystyle(S\vee T)x

\displaystyle=\sup\{\,Su+Tv:u,v\in X_{+},\,u+v=x\,\},

(2)

\displaystyle(S\wedge T)x

\displaystyle=\inf\{\,Su+Tv:u,v\in X_{+},\,u+v=x\,\},

(3)

where
S,T\in\mathcal{L}_{r}(X,Y)
and
x\in X_{+}
, and with norm

\|T\|_{r}=\|\,|T|\,\|.

(4)

Moreover,
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X,Y)
is order complete.

Equations
Eq.

2
and
Eq.

3
and their derivatives (e.g.,
|T|=\sup\{\,|Tu|:-x\leq u\leq x\,\}
) are called the
Riesz–Kantorovich
formulae
. The norm defined in
Eq.

4
is called the

regular norm
.

When
X=Y
, we will simplify our notation from
\mathcal{L}(X,X)
to

\mathcal{L}(X)
, from
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X,X)
to
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
, and so on. In this case, the space
\mathcal{L}(X)

is a Banach algebra with multiplication given by composition, and norm
given by operator norm. It is clear that the composition of positive operators is again
positive. From this, and the fact that the modulus is
submultiplicative (i.e.,
|TS|\leq|T||S|
), it follows
that
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
is a Banach algebra with respect to the regular norm. If
X
is order
complete, we have seen that
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
is a Banach lattice. The
simultaneous existence of these two structures will be our object of
study in
Section

3
.

2.2
Band projections

The interaction between the lattice and algebraic structures of
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
manifests itself clearly when we study band projections. A subspace
B
of
X
that is closed
under lattice operations (i.e., if
y\in B
then
|y|\in B
)
is called a
sublattice
. A sublattice
that is solid (i.e., if
|x|\leq|y|
and
y\in B
then
x\in B
) is
called an
ideal
. An ideal that is order closed (i.e., if

(y_{\gamma})_{\gamma}\subseteq B
is an increasing net and

\sup_{\gamma}y_{\gamma}
exists in
X
, then
\sup_{\gamma}y_{\gamma}\in B
) is called a
band
. A band for which
X=B\oplus B^{d}
, where

B^{d}=\{\,x\in X:|x|\wedge|y|=0\text{ for all }y\in B\,\},

is called a
projection band
. In this case we have a positive
projection
P\colon X\to X
onto
B
, called a
band projection
.
It turns out that band
projections can be nicely characterized in terms of the algebraic and
order structure in
\mathcal{L}(X)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. An operator
P\colon X\to X
is a band projection if and only if
P^{2}=P
and

0\leq P\leq I_{X}
.

In general, not all bands are projection bands. When
X
is order complete,
however, every band is a projection band. In this case, we will talk
indistinguishably about bands, projection bands, and band projections.

Finite infima and suprema of band projections yield new band projections.
Surprisingly enough, lattice operations between band projections
are determined by the algebraic and linear operations only.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. The set of band projections on
X

forms a Boolean algebra with operations

\overline{P}=I_{X}-P,\quad P_{1}\vee P_{2}=P_{1}+P_{2}-P_{1}P_{2},\quad P_{1}\wedge P_{2}=P_{1}P_{2},

minimum the zero map, and maximum the identity map.
Moreover, suprema and infima coincide with those in
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
.

A direct consequence of previous theorem is that band projections
commute. Note here the interplay between the lattice and algebraic
operations: we are able to express the lattice operations in purely
algebraic terms, and from this we are able to deduce an algebraic
property.

It will be central to our discussion that band projections are
characterized among positive operators by the multiplication
operators they define. Let
A,B\in\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
be regular operators on an
order complete Banach lattice
X
. The operators

\begin{array}[]{cccc}L_{A}\colon&\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)\\
&T&\longmapsto&AT\\
\end{array}\quad\begin{array}[]{cccc}R_{B}\colon&\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)\\
&T&\longmapsto&TB\\
\end{array}

are called
left
and
right multiplication operators
,
respectively. More generally, any operator of the form
L_{A}R_{B}
, for
some
A,B\in\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
, is
called a
multiplication operator
. As promised, using multiplication
operators we can characterize those elements in
\mathcal{L}_{+}(X)
that are band
projections as follows.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
12
, Theorem 4.5]
)
.

Let
X
be an order complete Banach lattice, and let
A,B\in\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
be nonzero regular operators. The multiplication operator

L_{A}R_{B}
is a band projection if and only if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\lambda A
and
B/\lambda
are
band projections.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
X
be an order complete Banach lattice, and let
A\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(X)
. The multiplication operator
L_{A}R_{A}
is a band projection
if and only if
A
is a band projection.

Proof.

Suppose that
L_{A}R_{A}
is a band projection. If
A=0
the result
follows,
so assume
A\neq 0
. By previous theorem, there exists

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\lambda A
and

A/\lambda
are band projections. Since
A
is positive,
\lambda
must also be positive. Using that
\lambda A
is a
projection,

\left(\frac{\lambda A}{\lambda^{2}}\right)^{2}=\frac{(\lambda A)^{2}}{\lambda^{4}}=\frac{\lambda A}{\lambda^{4}}.

But
\lambda A/\lambda^{2}=A/\lambda
is also a projection, so

(\lambda A/\lambda^{2})^{2}=A/\lambda
. Join both equations to
arrive at
\lambda^{2}A=A
. Since
A\neq 0
, it
must be the case that
\lambda^{2}-1=0
. But
\lambda>0
implies
\lambda=1
, and therefore
A
is a band projection.
∎

2.3
The center of a Banach lattice

Central operators and the center of a Banach lattice have been
extensively studied in the literature (see
[
1
, Section
3.3]
). Here we only recall some basic
definitions and a couple of results that will be useful later.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. An operator
T\colon X\to X
is
called
central
if there exists some scalar
\lambda>0

such that
|Tx|\leq\lambda|x|
for all
x\in X
. The collection
of all central operators is denoted
\mathcal{Z}(X)
and is called
the
center of
X
.

For example, central operators on
C(K)
are precisely the operators
of the form
M_{f}(g)=fg
, where
f,g\in C(K)
. Note also that band
projections are positive central operators.

Central operators have nice properties: they are regular and order
continuous, and positive central operators are also lattice
homomorphisms. The following theorem collects some of the most
important properties of the center.

Theorem 2.7

(Wickstead
[
18
]
)
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice.

(i)

The center
\mathcal{Z}(X)
, equipped with the operator
norm, is an AM-space with unit
I_{X}
. In particular,

\|T\|=\|\,|T|\,\|=\inf\{\,\lambda>0:|T|\leq\lambda I_{X}\,\}

for every
T\in\mathcal{Z}(X)
, and
\mathcal{Z}(X)
is
lattice isometric to
C(K)
for a certain compact
Hausdorff space
K
.

(ii)

The center
\mathcal{Z}(X)
is an inverse closed
*
*

*

A
subalgebra
B
of an algebra
A
is said to be

inverse closed
if for every
b\in B
invertible in
A
,
the inverse
b^{-1}
is contained in
B
.

subalgebra of
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
.

(iii)

When
X
is order complete, the center
\mathcal{Z}(X)
is
the band generated by
I_{X}
in
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
. In particular, it
is a projection band.

An operator
T\colon X\to X
on a Banach lattice
X
is said to be

(i)

an
orthomorphism
if

|x|\wedge|y|=0
implies
|x|\wedge|Ty|=0
for every
x,y\in X
;

(ii)

band preserving
if
T(B)\subseteq B
for every
band
B
in
X
.

It is immediate that central operators are
orthomorphisms, and that orthomorphisms are band preserving. Not
trivial, but true, is that for Banach lattices these three classes of
operators coincide.

Theorem 2.8

(Abramovich–Veksler–Koldunov

[
2
]
)
.

For an operator
T\colon X\to X
on a Banach lattice
X
the
following are equivalent.

(i)

T
is a central operator.

(ii)

T
is an orthomorphism.

(iii)

T
is band preserving.

2.4
Complexification and spectra

We want to introduce the “complex version” of the spaces of regular
operators. But first we need to recall the notion of a complex
Banach lattice, following
[
11
, Section 2.2]
. Let
X
be a Banach lattice. The

complexification
of
X
, as a vector space, is the additive
group
X\times X
with scalar multiplication

(\alpha+i\beta)(x,y)=(\alpha x-\beta y,\alpha y+\beta x),

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
x,y\in X
. The group
X\times X
with this scalar multiplication is a complex vector
space, usually denoted by
X_{\mathbb{C}}
. Identifying
x\in X
with
(x,0)\in X\times X
and
ix
with
(0,x)
, we write
x+iy
instead of

(x,y)\in X\times X
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice. For
z=x+iy\in X_{\mathbb{C}}
define

|z|=\sup\{\,x\cos\phi+y\sin\phi:0\leq\phi\leq 2\pi\,\}.

This supremum exists in
X
, and it satisfies:

(i)

|z|=0
if and only if
z=0
,

(ii)

|\lambda z|=|\lambda||z|
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and

z\in X_{\mathbb{C}}
,

(iii)

|z+w|\leq|z|+|w|
for all
z,w\in X_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Moreover, the map
z\mapsto\||z|\|
defines a norm on
X_{\mathbb{C}}
,
and
X_{\mathbb{C}}
with this norm is a complex Banach space.

Definition 2.10
.

A
complex Banach lattice
is a Banach space of the form

X_{\mathbb{C}}
, for a certain Banach lattice
X
, with the norm and
modulus introduced in previous proposition.

We now introduce regular operators between complex Banach lattices
X_{\mathbb{C}}

and
Y_{\mathbb{C}}
. For every (complex) linear operator
T\colon X_{\mathbb{C}}\to Y_{\mathbb{C}}

there exist unique (real) linear operators
T_{1},T_{2}\colon X\to Y
such
that
Tx=T_{1}x+iT_{2}x
for all
x\in X
. The operator
T_{1}
is called the
real
part
of
T
, while the operator
T_{2}
is called the
imaginary part
of
T
.
Thus
\mathcal{L}(X_{\mathbb{C}},Y_{\mathbb{C}})
is
isomorphic to the complexification of
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
.
An operator
T\colon X_{\mathbb{C}}\to Y_{\mathbb{C}}
is said to be

real valued
if
T(X)\subseteq Y
. If
T
is real valued and

T(X_{+})\subseteq Y_{+}
, then
T
is said to be
positive
. An
operator
T\colon X_{\mathbb{C}}\to Y_{\mathbb{C}}
is said to be
regular
if both
its real and imaginary parts are regular. When
Y
is order complete, the space of regular
operators between
X_{\mathbb{C}}
and
Y_{\mathbb{C}}
may be identified with the
complexification of the Banach lattice
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X,Y)
.

Given a regular operator
T\colon X_{\mathbb{C}}\to X_{\mathbb{C}}
, denote by
\sigma(T)
its spectrum. The following result
characterizes the lattice isomorphisms contained in the center in
terms of their spectrum, and will be needed later in the discussion.

Theorem 2.11

(Schaefer–Wolff–Arendt

[
13
]
)
.

Let
T
be a lattice isomorphism of a complex Banach lattice
X
.
Then
T\in\mathcal{Z}(X)
if and only if
\sigma(T)\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

2.5
Inner band projections

For an order complete Banach lattice
X
, we have introduced the
Banach lattice
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
of regular operators over
X
. We can then consider
band projections on this space. Since
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
is again order complete,
any band will have an associated band projection. We have already
encountered an example of such a band in
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
, namely the center.
Another example are inner band projections, a particular
class of band projections on spaces of regular operators introduced by
the author together with P. Tradacete in

[
12
]
. The construction of these band
projections is summarized in the following result.

Theorem 2.12

(
[
12
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Let
X
be an order complete Banach lattice and let
\{P_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be a family of pairwise disjoint band
projections on
X
. Let
\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda

be any subset. Then the map

\ \begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}\colon&\mathcal{L}_{+}(X)&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{L}_{+}(X)\\
&T&\longmapsto&\displaystyle\bigvee_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Gamma}P_{\alpha}TP_{\beta}\\
\end{array}

extends to a unique band projection
\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}\colon\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)\to\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
X
be an order complete Banach lattice. We say that a band
projection
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
is an
inner band projection

if there exists a family of pairwise disjoint band projections

\{P_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
on
X
such that

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}
, as defined in

Theorem

2.12
, for a certain
\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda
. We will call
inner projection bands
the
bands associated with inner band projections, and we will denote

\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma}={P}_{\Gamma})
.

In
Section

3.5
we will be able to generalize this
construction to arbitrary order complete Banach lattice algebras.
Together with the construction, we will generalize some of the
properties of inner band projections.
In particular, we will study the following property, regarding the
structure of the set of inner band projections.

Proposition 2.14

(
[
12
, Proposition
2.10]
)
.

Let
X
be an order complete Banach lattice and let
\{P_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be a family of pairwise disjoint
band projections on

. The inner band projections
\{\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda\}
(resp. the inner projection bands
\{\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda\}
) form a Boolean algebra with the same order,
suprema and infima as in
\mathcal{L}_{r}(\mathcal{L}_{r}(X))
. Moreover, this Boolean algebra is
isomorphic to

through the map

\Gamma\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}
(resp.
\Gamma\mapsto\mathcal{B}_{\Gamma}
).

Inner band projections are also used to characterize
atomic and order continuous Banach lattices.

Theorem 2.15

(
[

, Theorem
3.1]
)
.

Let

be an order complete Banach lattice. All band projections
on

are inner if and only if

is both atomic and order

3
Banach lattice algebras

The goal of this section is to generalize the
properties studied in the first section to Banach lattice algebras.

Sections

3.1
and
3.2
introduce Banach lattice algebras
and some known results that will be useful throughout.
The reader familiar with the literature can therefore have a quick
look at the notation in these sections, and then go directly to

Section

3.3
.

3.1
Banach lattice algebras

In the space of regular operators over an order complete Banach
lattice, a Banach lattice structure (given by the cone of positive
operators) and a Banach algebra structure (given by composition)
coexist. We want to study more general spaces in which these two
structures coexist, and to extend (when possible) the properties of
the first
part of the paper to this abstract setting. This will elucidate which
properties of the space of regular operators do not really depend on
the fact that they are regular operators over a Banach lattice, but
rather on the algebraic and lattice structures of the space.

But first, we need to define Banach lattice algebras.
It seems like there is no consensus in the literature on the notion of
Banach lattice algebra with identity, so we follow the one suggested
by A. W. Wickstead in
[
17
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
Banach lattice algebra
is a Banach lattice
A
together
with a product
\ast\colon A\times A\to A
such that

(i)

(A,\ast)
is a Banach algebra,

(ii)

if
x,y\in A_{+}
, then
x\ast y\in A_{+}
.

When the product
\ast
has an identity
e
of norm one, we say that

A
is a
Banach lattice algebra with identity
e
. When the product

\ast
is commutative, we say that
A
is a
commutative
Banach lattice algebra
.

It is important to note the following consequence of the definition.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
6
, Theorem 2]
)
.

The identity in a Banach lattice algebra is positive.

Let us pause here to present some classical examples of Banach lattice
algebras. We shall resort to them when presenting examples and
counterexamples in our exposition.

Example 3.3
.

(i)

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space. The space

C(K)
of real-valued continuous functions on
K
is a
commutative Banach lattice algebra with pointwise order
and product, and supremum norm. The identity is the constant one function.

(ii)

Let
X
be an order complete Banach lattice. We have
seen in
Section

2.1
that the
space of regular operators
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
is a Banach lattice,
and at the same time a Banach algebra, in which the
composition of positive operators is positive. It is,
therefore, a Banach lattice algebra, with
identity
I_{X}
. This Banach lattice algebra need not be
commutative.

(iii)

Let
G
be a locally compact group, let
\lambda
be a
left invariant Haar measure, and consider the Banach
lattice
L_{1}(G)=L_{1}(\lambda)
. The convolution
multiplication on
L_{1}(G)
, defined for
\phi,\psi\in L_{1}(G)
by

(\phi\ast\psi)(g)=\int_{G}\phi(gh)\psi(h^{-1})\,d\lambda(h),\quad\text{where }g\in G,

makes
L_{1}(G)
into a Banach algebra. It is easy to check that
the product of positive elements is positive, so
L_{1}(G)

is Banach lattice algebra. This Banach lattice
algebra is commutative if and only if
G
is abelian, and
has an identity if and only if
G
is discrete.

(iv)

Let
M(G)
be the space of signed regular Borel measures
on a locally compact group
G
. Then
M(G)
is a Banach
lattice if we set
\mu\geq\nu
,
\mu,\nu\in M(G)
,
whenever
\mu(A)\geq\nu(A)
for every Borel set
A
, and give it the
norm
\|\mu\|=|\mu|(G)
. The convolution multiplication
defined by

(\mu\ast\nu)(A)=\int_{G}\int_{G}\chi_{A}(st)\,d\mu(s)\,d\nu(t),

where
\chi_{A}
denotes the characteristic function of the
Borel set
A
, gives
M(G)
a Banach algebra structure. Again, this
space is a Banach lattice algebra. Its identity is the point mass measure on the identity of
G
.

(v)

Let
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of Banach lattice
algebras idexed by a set
I
, and let
{1\leq p\leq\infty}
.
Then their
\ell_{p}
-sum
\ell_{p}(A_{i})
, equipped with
coordinate-wise order and
product, is again a Banach lattice algebra, for if

x=(x_{i})
and
y=(y_{i})
are elements of
\ell_{p}(A_{i})
, then

\|xy\|_{p}\leq\|x\|_{p}\|y\|_{\infty}\leq\|x\|_{p}\|y\|_{p}.

It is clear
that
\ell_{p}(A_{i})
is commutative if and
only if
A_{i}
is commutative for each
i\in I
. Also,

\ell_{\infty}(A_{i})
has identity if and only if
A_{i}
has
identity for each
i\in I
.

3.2
The ideal generated by the identity
element

As explained in
Section

2.3
, the ideal generated by the identity
element in
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
is the center of the Banach lattice
X
, an ideal of operators
extensively studied in the literature. It turns out that the
notion of the ideal generated by the identity element in Banach
lattice algebras has also been studied, and that properties analogous
to those in
Theorem

2.7
hold in general.

If
A
is a Banach lattice algebra with identity

, we adopt the
convention of
[
14
]
and denote
by
A_{e}
the (order) ideal generated by
e
in

. The main
properties of this ideal are collected in the following result.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra with identity
e
.

(i)

The space
(A_{e},\|{\cdot}\|_{e})
, with the order and
product of
A
, is a Banach lattice
algebra, lattice and algebra isometric to
C(K)
,
for a certain compact Hausdorff
K
.
†
†

†

Note that
the analogy with
Theorem

2.7
is not complete:
here we are endowing the ideal generated by the
identity with its associated norm, whereas in

Theorem

2.7
one works with the operator norm. As
it turns out, assumption
\|e\|=1
implies
that both norms are equal. Yet we need spectra to show
this and it will have to wait until

Section

3.4
. For the time being, it suffices to
know that
A_{e}
is closed in
A
. This apparent inconvenience
has an advantage: all the contents of this section are valid
under the weaker hypothesis

, which is another common
definition of Banach lattice algebras with identity.

(ii)

The ideal
A_{e}
is a principal projection band in
A
.

(iii)

The space

is an inverse closed subalgebra of

.

The contents of previous theorem are scattered through the literature:
the first assertion follows from the results by L. Martignon in
[
10
]
,
the second assertion is due to E. Scheffold
[
14
, Theorem 3]
, while the third is
mentioned by C. B. Huijsmans in
[
7
]
without any further reference.
Since this result will be central in our exposition, we devote the
rest of this section to present a full proof of it.

To prove the first part of the theorem, we need to talk about Banach lattice algebras
that are AM-spaces with unit, in which the order
unit coincides with the algebraic identity.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra with identity
e
. If
A
is also
an AM-space with unit
e
(i.e., if
A_{e}=A
and
\|x\|=\inf\{\,\lambda>0:|x|\leq\lambda e\,\}
for every
x\in A
), we say that
A
is an

AM-algebra with unit
e
.

The following theorem asserts that AM-algebras with unit are nothing
but algebras
of continuous functions.

Theorem 3.6
.

Every AM-algebra with unit is algebra and lattice isometric to

C(K)
, for a certain compact Hausdorff
K
.

Previous result is a direct consequence of Kakutani’s representation
theorem for AM-spaces with unit, and the following
characterization of the pointwise product in
C(K)
due to L. Martignon.

Proposition 3.7

(
[
10
, Proposition 1.4]
)
.

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space and let
\star\colon C(K)\times C(K)\to C(K)
be a binary operation such that

(C(K),\star)

with pointwise order is a Banach lattice algebra.
Suppose also that the constant one function is the identity for

\star
. Then
\star
is the pointwise multiplication.

The proof of this last result relies heavily on Banach lattice techniques.
Here we want to provide an alternative proof of
Theorem

3.6
in the style
of the Gelfand transform: working with maximal (order and algebraic)
ideals and real-valued (lattice and algebra) homomorphisms. This
argument follows closely the proof of Kakutani’s representation
theorem from
[
15
, Chapter 4]
.

There are two kinds of ideals present in Banach lattice algebras: the
ideals as a Banach lattice and the ideals as a Banach algebra. There
are also subspaces that are ideals in both senses. There does not seem
to be a common terminology for these spaces in the literature. We will
refer to ideals in the Banach lattice sense as

order ideals
, and to ideals in the Banach algebra sense as

algebraic ideals
(these, in turn, can be left or right ideals,
or both).
Ideals
will be the subspaces that are
at the same time an order ideal and a (two-sided) algebraic ideal. The
following is a simple characterization of the ideal generated
by an element (that is, the intersection of all the ideals containing
the element) in a Banach lattice algebra with identity.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra with identity and let
a\in A
. Then
the ideal generated by
a
is

\{\,x\in A:|x|\leq r|a|r^{\prime}\text{ for some }r,r^{\prime}\in A_{+}\,\}.

Proof.

Call the displayed set
I
. Let us check that this set is indeed
an ideal. If
x,y\in I
, say
|x|\leq rar^{\prime}
and
|y|\leq sas^{\prime}
for some

r,r^{\prime},s,s^{\prime}\in A_{+}
, then

|x+y|\leq|x|+|y|\leq rar^{\prime}+sas^{\prime}\leq(r+s)a(r^{\prime}+s^{\prime})

so
x+y\in I
. Clearly,
-x
and
|x|
are elements of
I
, so
I
is a
sublattice. From the definition it follows that
I
is an
order ideal. To check that it is also an algebraic ideal, let
z\in A
. Then we have

|zx|\leq|z||x|\leq(|z|r)ar^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad|xz|\leq|x||z|\leq ra(r^{\prime}|z|)

so both
zx,xz\in I
, and we have thus shown that
I
is an
ideal.

Since
A
has an identity,
a\in I
.
Let
J
be any ideal of
A
containing
a
. Then, since
J
is a
sublattice and an
algebraic ideal,
r|a|r^{\prime}\in J
for all
r,r^{\prime}\in A_{+}
. Since

J
is an order ideal,
x\in J
whenever
|x|\leq rar^{\prime}
. Hence

I\subseteq J
, and so
I
is the ideal generated by
a
in
A
.
∎

As usual, we say that an ideal in a Banach lattice algebra is

maximal
if it is not contained in any proper ideal.
The following is pure routine.

Lemma 3.9
.

In a Banach lattice algebra with identity, any proper ideal is contained in a maximal ideal.

Proof.

Let
I
be a proper ideal in a Banach lattice algebra
A
with
identity
e
. Let
\mathcal{I}
be the
family of proper ideals in
A
containing
I
, ordered by
inclusion. Let
\{J_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
be a chain in

\mathcal{I}
.
Then it is straightforward to check that
J=\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}J_{\gamma}
is again an ideal containing
I
. Moreover, if

J=A
, then
e\in J
and so
e\in J_{\gamma}
for a certain

\gamma\in\Gamma
. Being
J_{\gamma}
an algebraic ideal, this
would imply
J_{\gamma}=A
, contradicting the definition. So
J
is
proper, and therefore is an upper bound of the chain in
\mathcal{I}
. By Zorn’s
lemma, there exists a maximal element in
\mathcal{I}
, and this
element is a maximal ideal containing
I
.
∎

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra and let
I
be a closed ideal. Perform
the usual quotient
A/I
. Being
I
a two-sided closed ideal,
A/I
is
again a Banach algebra, and the quotient map
Q\colon A\to A/I
is an algebra homomorphism.
Since
I
is an order ideal,
Q(A_{+})
is a cone in
A/I
, and the
order it induces makes
A/I
into a Banach lattice, and
Q
into a
lattice homomorphism. It is direct to check that the product of
positive elements in
A/I
is again positive. Hence
A/I
admits a
natural Banach lattice algebra structure, making
Q
a lattice and
algebra homomorphism.

Since the closure of an algebraic ideal is again an
algebraic ideal, and the closure of an order ideal is again an order
ideal, the closure of an ideal in a Banach lattice algebra is again an
ideal. It is then routine to check that maximal ideals in
Banach lattice algebras with identity are closed. Moreover, we have the following.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
A
be an AM-algebra with unit
e
and let
M
be a maximal ideal
in
A
. Then
A/M
is lattice and algebra isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

By the discussion preceding the lemma,
A/M
is a Banach lattice
algebra. Suppose that
\dim(A/M)>1
. Then there would
exist two non-zero disjoint elements
Qx,Qy\in A/M
. We are
going to show that the ideal
I
generated by
|Qx|
in
A/M

must be proper. Indeed, if
Qy\in I
, by

Lemma

3.8
there would exist
r,r^{\prime}\in A_{+}
such
that
|Qy|\leq Q(r)|Q(x)|Q(r^{\prime})
, and then

|Qy|=(Q(r)|Q(x)|Q(r^{\prime}))\wedge|Qy|\leq\|r\|\|r^{\prime}\||Q(x)|\wedge|Q(y)|=0,

which is a contradiction. Hence
I
is proper. It is not difficult
to check that
Q^{-1}(I)\subseteq A
is again an ideal, because

Q
is both a lattice and algebra homomorphism. Moreover,

M\subseteq Q^{-1}(I)\subsetneq A
, and since
x\in Q^{-1}(I)
,
the first inclusion is proper. This contradicts that
M
is
maximal. So
\dim(A/M)
must be
1
. Then
A/M
is spanned by

Qe
and the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\mathbb{R}&\longrightarrow&A/M\\
&\lambda&\longmapsto&\lambda Qe\\
\end{array}

is a lattice and algebra isomorphism.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
A
be an AM-algebra with unit
e
and let
x_{0}\in A_{+}
. If

x_{0}\not\leq e
, there exists a lattice and algebra homomorphism

\phi\colon A\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\phi(x_{0})>1
.

Proof.

We have
(x_{0}-e)_{+}\neq 0
. Let
I
be the ideal generated by

(x_{0}-e)_{-}
. We claim that
(x_{0}-e)_{+}\not\in I
. Indeed, if it
were the case, according to
Lemma

3.8
there would exist
r,r^{\prime}\in A_{+}
such that

(x_{0}-e)_{+}\leq r(x_{0}-e)_{-}r^{\prime}\leq\|r\|\|r^{\prime}\|(x_{0}-e)_{-}

which contradicts the fact that
(x_{0}-e)_{+}
and
(x_{0}-e)_{-}
are
disjoint. Hence
I
is
proper, and by
Lemma

3.9
it is contained in a maximal
ideal
M
. The quotient map associated
with
M
is a lattice and algebra homomorphism
A\to A/M
. Let
\phi\colon A\to\mathbb{R}
be the composition of this quotient map with the
isomorphism between
A/M
and
\mathbb{R}
given in

Lemma

3.10
. This homomorphism satisfies

\phi(x_{0}-e)=\phi((x_{0}-e)_{+})>0,

so
\phi(x_{0})>\phi(e)=1
.
∎

With this set of rather standard tools, we can proceed to the proof of

Theorem

3.6
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.6
.

Let
A
be an AM-algebra with unit
e
. Let
K
be the set of all
lattice and algebra
homomorphisms
\phi\colon A\to\mathbb{R}
with
\phi(e)=1
. We can view
K
as a subset
of
\mathbb{R}^{A}
. Equip
\mathbb{R}^{A}
with the product topology and
K
with the
subspace topology. This topology is Hausdorff, because the
product topology is. Note that, for every
\phi\in K
,

|\phi(x)|\leq\phi(\|x\|e)=\|x\|
holds, which
means that
K\subseteq\prod_{x\in A}[-\|x\|,\|x\|]
. By
Tychonoff’s theorem, the latter product is compact. Moreover,
K

is closed in this subspace, for if
(\phi_{\lambda})
is a net in

K
pointwise convergent to
\phi
, this means that
\phi(x)=\lim\phi_{\lambda}(x)
for all
x\in A
, and then by
continuity of the algebraic and lattice operations it is easy
to check that

\phi
is a again a lattice and algebra homomorphism with
\phi(e)=1
. Therefore
K
is a compact Hausdorff space.

It is useful to note that
K
has sufficiently many elements.
Indeed, for every
x\in A_{+}
with
\|x\|>1
, we have
x\not\leq e
,
and by
Lemma

3.11
there is some element
\phi\in K

such that
\phi(x)>1
.

Now consider the map
T\colon A\to C(K)
where
Tx(\phi)=\phi(x)
for
x\in A
and
\phi\in K
. It is easy to check that

Tx
is indeed continuous. Since
operations in
C(K)
are computed pointwise, and
Te(\phi)=\phi(e)=1
for all
\phi\in K
, it
follows that
T
is a lattice and algebra homomorphism that
preserves the identity.

We claim
\|Tx\|=\|x\|
for all
x\in A
. On one hand,

|Tx(\phi)|=|\phi(x)|\leq\|x\|
, so
\|Tx\|\leq\|x\|
. On the other
hand, if
\|x\|>1
, we have seen that there exists some
\phi\in K
such that
\phi(|x|)>1
, so that
\|Tx\|\geq|Tx(\phi)|=\phi(|x|)>1
. If
\|Tx\|<\|x\|
for some

, the
element
|x|/\|Tx\|
would contradict the previous observation. So

\|Tx\|=\|x\|
for all
x\in A
.

Finally, note that
T(A)
is a closed
subalgebra of

that contains the constants
and separates points: if
\phi\neq\psi
in
K
, there is some

such that
(Tx)(\phi)=\phi(x)\neq\psi(x)=(Tx)(\psi)
. By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem,

, and the result
is proved.
∎

Some preliminary remarks before presenting the proof of
Theorem

3.4
. For the second
assertion we will provide an argument similar to

[

, Theorems 1 and 2]
instead of
the original one appearing in
[
14
]
because it serves
as a first illustration of what will be
our main trick in the coming sections, namely, to consider multiplication
operators on a Banach lattice algebra, and then exploit the
known properties of the space of regular operators. On a Banach lattice
algebra

, operators of the form

\begin{array}[]{cccc}L_{a}\colon&A&\longrightarrow&A\\
&x&\longmapsto&ax\\
\end{array},\quad\begin{array}[]{cccc}R_{b}\colon&A&\longrightarrow&A\\
&x&\longmapsto&xb\\
\end{array},

for certain

, are called
left
and

operators
, respectively. Operators of the form
L_{a}R_{b}
, for some

, are called

.

Proof of
Theorem

3.4
.

(i)

We are going to check that
(A_{e},\|{\cdot}\|_{e})
is an
AM-algebra with unit
e
. Then the result will follow from

Theorem

3.6
. That

(A_{e},\|{\cdot}\|_{e})
is an AM-space with unit
e
is
clear. Moreover, it is a subalgebra, for if

:

|xy|\leq|x||y|\leq\|x\|_{e}\|y\|_{e}e.

From this also follows that
\|xy\|_{e}\leq\|x\|_{e}\|y\|_{e}
,
so it is a Banach algebra. Its algebraic identity is
obviously
e
.

(ii)

We prove this fact in two steps. First, we show that

B_{e}
, the band generated by
e
, is a projection band.
And then we show that
A_{e}=B_{e}
.

To prove that
B_{e}
is a projection band, it suffices to
check that
\sup_{n}a\wedge ne
exists for every
a\in A_{+}

(in fact, we show a little more, because we will need it
later on). Denote
a_{n}=a\wedge ne
, and observe that for
any
m\geq n\geq 1
:

(a_{m}-a_{n})\wedge(ne-a_{n})=(a_{m}\wedge ne)-a_{n}=a_{n}-a_{n}=0.

Using the representation of
I_{e}
as a
C(K)
space, and
seeing
a_{m}
,
a_{n}
and

as continuous functions in

K
, we are saying that
(a_{m}-a_{n})(t)\wedge(ne-a_{n})(t)=0
for every
t\in K
. It follows that
a_{m}-a_{n}
and
a_{m}-n^{-1}a_{m}a_{n}=n^{-1}a_{m}(ne-a_{n})
are also disjoint, so

(a_{m}-a_{n})\wedge(a_{m}-n^{-1}a_{m}a_{n})=0

which implies

a_{m}=a_{n}\vee(n^{-1}a_{m}a_{n}).

It follows that

0\leq\|a_{m}-a_{n}\|=\|(n^{-1}a_{m}a_{n}-a_{n})_{+}\|\leq n^{-1}\|a_{m}a_{n}\|\leq n^{-1}\|a\|^{2},

(5)

so
(a_{n})
is a Cauchy sequence. It therefore converges to
some

. Since
(a_{n})
is increasing,
we must have
b=\sup_{n}a\wedge ne
, and this shows that

B_{e}
is a projection band.

To prove that
A_{e}
is a band we need to show that
A_{e}=B_{e}
, and for this it
suffices to check that
a\in A_{e}
whenever
a\in(B_{e})_{+}
. For every

, inequality

implies that

is a central
operator for every

. In particular, it is an orthomorphism. If

are such that

, then
|x|\wedge|(a\wedge ne)y|=0
for every

3.3
Band projections and order idempotents

Band projections play a prominent role in the theory of Banach
lattices. These operators are characterized among
regular operators by
Theorem

2.2
. The goal of this section
is to define elements in a general Banach lattice algebra that play
the same role as band projections do in spaces of regular operators. When
an identity is present, this generalization is straightforward
from
Theorem

2.2
, and has already been
considered by E. A. Alekhno in the context of ordered Banach
algebras (see
[
3
,
4
]
).

Definition 3.13
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra with identity
e
. We say
that
p\in A
is
order idempotent
if
p^{2}=p
and
0\leq p\leq e
. We denote by
OI(A)
the set of order idempotents in
A
.

What follows is the generalization of
Theorem

2.3
. This
result appears, with a different proof, in
[
4
, Corollary
2.2]
.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra with identity
e
. The set of
order idempotents
OI(A)
forms a Boolean algebra with operations

\overline{p}=e-p,\ p\vee q=p+q-pq,\ p\wedge q=pq,\quad\text{for }p,q\in OI(A),

with zero as the minimum and the identity
e
as the maximum. Moreover,
suprema and infima coincide with those in
A
.

Proof.

By definition,
OI(A)
is contained in
A_{e}
. Represent

A_{e}
as a
C(K)
space using
Theorem

3.4
. Then
p\in OI(A)
, seen
as a continuous function in
K
, satisfies
p^{2}=p
; that is,
p

is a characteristic function of a clopen set in
K
. Conversely, any
characteristic function of a clopen set is an order idempotent in

C(K)
, and therefore in
A
. This exhibits an identification of

OI(A)
with
\{\,\chi_{C}:C\subseteq K\text{ clopen}\,\}.

The result now clearly follows.
∎

Proposition 3.15
.

In a Banach lattice algebra with identity,
the set of order idempotents is norm and order closed.

Proof.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra with identity
e
. Let
(p_{n})
be a sequence of order idempotents converging to
p
in
norm. Then
p_{n}^{2}=p_{n}
and
0\leq p_{n}\leq e
for
each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and since the product is continuous and the
positive cone is closed, it follows that
p
is an order
idempotent. This shows that the set of order idempotents is norm
closed.

To show that it is also order closed, let
(p_{\alpha})
be a net
of order idempotents converging to
p
in order. The product need
not be order continuous, so one has to proceed with more care now.
Since the positive cone is always order closed,
0\leq p\leq e
follows from
0\leq p_{\alpha}\leq e
for each
\alpha
. By

Theorem

3.4
,
p
and
p_{\alpha}
commute, so

|p^{2}-p_{\alpha}^{2}|\leq(p+p_{\alpha})|p-p_{\alpha}|\leq 2|p-p_{\alpha}|,

where the first inequality follows from the submultiplicativity of
the modulus. This implies that
p_{\alpha}^{2}
converges in order to

p^{2}
. Since also
p_{\alpha}^{2}=p_{\alpha}
for every
\alpha
, and the order limit is unique,
p^{2}=p
. So
p
is an order
idempotent, and this shows that the set of order idempotents is
also order closed.
∎

The notion of order idempotent seems to abstract some of the
most important properties of band projections, but it relies on the
existence of an identity element. Of course, this poses no limitation in
spaces of regular operators, where the identity operator is always
present. However, this is not the case for general Banach lattice algebras. Can we
somehow still abstract the
definition and properties of band projections to Banach
lattice algebras without identity?

Faced with a similar problem, K. Vala gives in
[
16
]
a notion of compact and finite rank elements in
arbitrary normed algebras as follows:
for a normed algebra
A
, he defines an element

to be

compact
(resp.
of finite rank
) if the operator

L_{a}R_{a}
is compact (resp. of finite rank). In view of

Corollary

2.5
, it makes sense to take Vala’s approach and set
the following definition.

Definition 3.16
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra. An element

is
called a

band projection
if
L_{a}R_{a}\colon A\to A
is a band
projection. The set of band projections in
A
is denoted

BP(A)=\{\,a\in A_{+}:L_{a}R_{a}\text{ is a band projection}\,\}.

It is also convenient to have the following notions.

Definition 3.17
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra. An element
a\in A_{+}
is
called a
left band projection
(resp.
right band
projection
) if
L_{a}
(resp.

sets of left and right band projections in

are denoted

BP_{l}(A)=\{\,a\in A_{+}:L_{a}\text{ is a band projection}\,\},

BP_{r}(A)=\{\,a\in A_{+}:R_{a}\text{ is a band projection}\,\}.

Remark
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra. According to

Theorem

2.2
,

a\in A_{+}
is a band projection if and only if

0\leq axa\leq x\text{ and }a^{2}xa^{2}=axa\quad\text{for all }x\in A_{+}.

Similarly,
a\in A_{+}
is a left band projection if and only if

0\leq ax\leq x\text{ and }a^{2}x=ax\quad\text{for all }x\in A_{+},

and a right band projection if and only if

0\leq xa\leq x\text{ and }xa^{2}=xa\quad\text{for all }x\in A_{+}.

In particular, the sets
BP(A)
,
BP_{l}(A)
and
BP_{r}(A)
are all
norm closed.

If the multiplication in

is jointly order continuous, it follows
from previous remark that

and

following example shows.

Example 3.18

Consider the Banach lattice

of convergent real sequences, with

x\star y=\big(\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}\big)\big(\lim_{n\to\infty}y_{n}\big)e\quad\text{where }e=(1,1,1,\ldots).

Associativity of this product follows from

\lim_{n\to\infty}(x\star y)_{n}=\big(\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}\big)\big(\lim_{n\to\infty}y_{n}\big),

It is clear that

Complexification and spectra

To talk about spectra and see their relation with band
projections and order idempotents, we need to work over the complex
field. So first we need to “complexify” some of the notions introduced
so far.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra. Applying the procedure for the
complexification of Banach lattices, as exposed in

Section

2.4
, one
obtains the complex Banach lattice
A_{\mathbb{C}}
with norm
\|z\|_{\mathbb{C}}=\||z|\|
. With the natural product

(x_{1}+ix_{2})(y_{1}+iy_{2})=(x_{1}y_{1}-x_{2}y_{2})+i(x_{1}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{1})\quad\text{where
}x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in A,

the space
A_{\mathbb{C}}
becomes a complex algebra, that has an identity precisely
when
A
does. This product is compatible
with the complex lattice structure in the sense that
|z_{1}z_{2}|\leq|z_{1}||z_{2}|
, for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in A_{\mathbb{C}}
. This fact is not trivial,
and a proof may be found in
[
8
]
. The submultiplicativity of the
norm on
A
immediately implies
\|z_{1}z_{2}\|_{\mathbb{C}}\leq\|z_{1}\|_{\mathbb{C}}\|z_{2}\|_{\mathbb{C}}
for
z_{1},z_{2}\in A_{\mathbb{C}}
. Hence
A_{\mathbb{C}}
is also a complex
Banach algebra with norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{\mathbb{C}}
. The space
A_{\mathbb{C}}
with its
structures of complex Banach lattice and complex Banach algebra is
called a
complex Banach lattice algebra
. When
A
has an identity, we
emphasize it by saying that
A_{\mathbb{C}}
is a
complex Banach
lattice algebra with identity
.

Example 3.23
.

(i)

If
K
is a compact Hausdorff space, the
complexification of
C(K)
may be identified with

C(K,\mathbb{C})
, the space of continuous complex-valued
functions on
K
.

(ii)

If
X
is an order complete Banach lattice, the
complexification of
\mathcal{L}_{r}(X)
may be identified with the
space of regular operators over
X_{\mathbb{C}}
(see

Section

2.4
).

Next we want to present a result analogous to
Theorem

3.4
in
complex Banach lattice algebras. If
X
is a Banach lattice, a
subspace
I\subseteq X_{\mathbb{C}}
is called an
ideal
if
x\in I
and

|y|\leq|x|
imply
y\in I
.
It is not difficult to check that
I
is the complexification of the
ideal
I\cap X
of
X
, and that the intersection of all the ideals
containing
z\in X_{\mathbb{C}}
is given by

(I_{z})_{\mathbb{C}}=\{\,w\in X_{\mathbb{C}}:|w|\leq\lambda|z|\text{ for some }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\,\}.

This is called the
ideal generated by
z
.

An ideal
B\subseteq X_{\mathbb{C}}
is called a

band
(resp.
projection band
) if
B\cap X
is a band
(resp. projection band) in
X
. Again, one can check that an ideal

B\subseteq X
is a projection band if and only if
X_{\mathbb{C}}=B\oplus B^{d}
, where

B^{d}=\{\,z\in X_{\mathbb{C}}:|w|\wedge|z|=0\text{ for all }w\in B\,\}.

Finally, some common notation: for
z\in A_{\mathbb{C}}
denote its spectrum by

\sigma(z)
and its spectral radius by
r(z)
.

Theorem 3.24
.

Let
A_{\mathbb{C}}
be a complex Banach lattice algebra with identity
e
and let

(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{e}
be the ideal generated by
e
in
A_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then

(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{e}=(A_{e})_{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.,
(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{e}
is the complexification of
the Banach lattice algebra
A_{e}
. Moreover:

(i)

The space
(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{e}
can
be isometrically identified with
C(K,\mathbb{C})
, for some compact Hausdorff
K
.

(ii)

The ideal
(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{e}
is a projection band in
A_{\mathbb{C}}
.

(iii)

The space
(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{e}
is an inverse closed subalgebra of
A_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

Since
(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{e}\cap A=A_{e}
, it follows that
(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{e}=(A_{e})_{\mathbb{C}}
.
It is then a direct consequence of
Theorem

3.4
that
(A_{\mathbb{C}})_{e}
, with the
complexification of the norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{e}
, is isometric to

C(K,\mathbb{C})
. To prove
(i)
it only remains to check that the norm

\|{\cdot}\|_{e,\mathbb{C}}
is equal to
\|{\cdot}\|_{\mathbb{C}}
.
Being
A_{e}
an inverse closed
subalgebra, it is easy to check that the spectrum of an element in

A_{e}
(i.e., its range when viewed as a function in
C(K,\mathbb{C})
) is
the same as its spectrum in
A_{\mathbb{C}}
. Therefore
\|z\|\geq r(z)=\|z\|_{e}
,
and since we are assuming
\|e\|=1
in our definition of Banach
lattice algebras,
\|z\|\leq\|z\|_{e}
. Joining both inequalities,

\|z\|=\|z\|_{e}=r(z)
and
(i)
is proved.
From
Theorem

3.4
clearly follows
(ii)
, and exactly the
same proof as in
Theorem

3.4
can be used to show
(iii)
.
∎

.

In

We also need the generalization of

Corollary 3.26
.

Let
A_{\mathbb{C}}
be a complex Banach lattice algebra with identity. Let
a\in A_{+}
be an
invertible element with positive inverse. Then

a\in A_{e}
if and only if
\sigma(a)\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in A_{+}
be an invertible element such that
a^{-1}\in A_{+}
. Then
R_{a}\colon A\to A
, being a positive operator with
positive inverse

, is a lattice isomorphism. Note that

(R_{a}-\lambda I_{A_{\mathbb{C}}})(x)=x(a-\lambda e)=R_{a-\lambda e}(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in A_{\mathbb{C}},

where

denotes the identity element of

. It follows that

\sigma(R_{a})\subseteq\sigma(a)
, since
R_{a-\lambda e}
is
invertible whenever
a-\lambda e
is.
Suppose
\sigma(a)\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then
\sigma(R_{a})\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
and

R_{a}
is a lattice isomorphism. By

,

, which readily implies

.
The converse is direct if we see

as a positive
continuous function on a compact space.
∎

Proposition 3.27
.

Let
A_{\mathbb{C}}
be a complex Banach lattice algebra with identity. Let

be an invertible element such that:

(i)

,

(ii)

\sigma(a)\subseteq\{\lambda,\lambda+1\}
for some

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Then

, where

is the identity of

.

Proof.

Since

has positive spectrum, and

, from

. Then

, and its

∎

3.5
Inner band projections

In this section the construction of inner band projections
explained
in
Section

2.5
is extended to general Banach
lattice algebras. Let
A
be an
order complete Banach lattice algebra and let
\{p_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\subseteq BP_{l}(A)\cap BP_{r}(A)
be such that
p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
(in particular, if
A
has an identity,
we can take the
p_{\lambda}
to be pairwise disjoint order
idempotents).
For
\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda
define the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}P_{\Gamma}\colon&A_{+}&\longrightarrow&A_{+}\\
&x&\longmapsto&\displaystyle\bigvee_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Gamma}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}\\
\end{array}.

(6)

Note that this is well-defined, because
p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}=L_{p_{\alpha}}R_{p_{\beta}}(x)\leq x
(recall that
L_{p_{\alpha}}R_{p_{\beta}}

is a band projection on
A
), and
A
is order complete. If we
do not assume that
A
is order complete, the coming results work just
fine, as long as we ask for this supremum to exist. In particular, if

\Gamma
is finite, everything works out.

Lemma 3.28
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra, and let
(p_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\subseteq BP_{l}(A)\cap BP_{r}(A)
be such
that
p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}
. If

\Phi\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda
is finite, then

\bigvee_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}=\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta},\quad\text{for all }x\in A_{+}.

Proof.

For every
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi
,
L_{p_{\alpha}}R_{p_{\beta}}

is a band projection; to ease the notation, denote it by

L_{\alpha}R_{\beta}
. Whenever
(\gamma,\delta)\neq(\alpha,\beta)
:

(L_{\alpha}R_{\beta})(L_{\gamma}R_{\delta})(x)=p_{\alpha}p_{\gamma}xp_{\delta}p_{\beta}=0

because either
p_{\alpha}p_{\gamma}=0
or
p_{\delta}p_{\beta}=0
.
Therefore
(L_{\alpha}R_{\beta})(L_{\gamma}R_{\delta})=0
. Being band
projections, this implies that the bands determined by
L_{\alpha}R_{\beta}
and
L_{\gamma}R_{\delta}
are disjoint. Thus

p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}\wedge p_{\gamma}xp_{\delta}=(L_{\alpha}R_{\beta})(x)\wedge(L_{\gamma}R_{\delta})(x)=0,

or, equivalently,
p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}\vee p_{\gamma}xp_{\delta}=p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}+p_{\gamma}xp_{\delta}
. Since this is true for
any pair of indices, the result follows by induction.
∎

Theorem 3.29
.

The map
P_{\Gamma}
, as defined in (
6
), extends to
a unique band projection on
A
.

Proof.

Note first that, using previous lemma,

P_{\Gamma}x=\bigvee_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Gamma}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}=\sup_{\Phi\in\Fin(\Gamma)}\bigvee_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}=\sup_{\Phi\in\Fin(\Gamma)}\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}.

(7)

It follows from the definition that
P_{\Gamma}(x+y)\leq P_{\Gamma}(x)+P_{\Gamma}(y)
. For every
\Phi,\Psi\in\Fin(\Gamma)
:

\displaystyle\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}+\sum_{(\gamma,\delta)\in\Psi}p_{\gamma}yp_{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi\cup\Psi}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}+\sum_{(\gamma,\delta)\in\Phi\cup\Psi}p_{\gamma}yp_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi\cup\Psi}p_{\alpha}(x+y)p_{\beta}\leq P_{\Gamma}(x+y).

Taking suprema separately in
\Phi
and
\Psi
we conclude that

P_{\Gamma}
is additive. Hence it extends to a unique positive
operator
P_{\Gamma}\colon A\to A
. It is clear from the definition that
0\leq P_{\Gamma}\leq I_{A}
. To show that it is a band projection, it only remains to
prove that it is idempotent. By previous inequality,

P_{\Gamma}^{2}\leq P_{\Gamma}
. To see the other inequality, note that
for an arbitrary
\Phi\in\Fin(\Gamma)
:

\displaystyle P_{\Gamma}(P_{\Gamma}(x))\geq\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}p_{\alpha}P_{\Gamma}(x)p_{\beta}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}p_{\alpha}\bigg(\sum_{(\gamma,\delta)\in\Phi}p_{\gamma}xp_{\delta}\bigg)p_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}\sum_{(\gamma,\delta)\in\Phi}p_{\alpha}p_{\gamma}xp_{\delta}p_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}.

Inequality
P_{\Gamma}^{2}\geq P_{\Gamma}
follows from taking supremum
on
\Phi\in\Fin(\Gamma)
.
∎

Definition 3.30
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra and let
P\colon A\to A
be a
band projection. If there exist
(p_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\subseteq BP_{l}(A)\cap BP_{r}(A)
such that
p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
, and such that
P=P_{\Gamma}
for some
\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda
as defined in
Eq.

6
, then we
say that
P
is an
inner band projection
. We say that the
band
P(A)
is an
inner projection band
.

Example 3.31
.

Let

Remark
.

Let
A
be an order complete Banach lattice algebra.
Since in
\mathcal{L}_{r}(A)
the supremum of an increasing net is taken pointwise,
equation (
7
) implies

P_{\Gamma}=\olim_{\Phi\in\Fin(\Gamma)}\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}L_{\alpha}R_{\beta},

where

In particular, if

Proposition 3.32
.

Let
A
be an order complete Banach lattice algebra, and let
(p_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\subseteq BP_{l}(A)\cap BP_{r}(A)
be such
that
p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}
.
The set
\{\,P_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda\,\}
forms a Boolean algebra with maximum element
P_{\Lambda\times\Lambda}
, minimum
P_{\varnothing}
and operations

P_{\Gamma}\wedge P_{\Delta}=P_{\Gamma\cap\Delta},\;P_{\Gamma}\vee P_{\Delta}=P_{\Gamma\cup\Delta},\;\overline{P}_{\Gamma}=P_{\overline{\Gamma}},\quad\text{for
}\Gamma,\Delta\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda,

where
\overline{\Gamma}=\Lambda\times\Lambda\setminus\Gamma
denotes the complementary set.

Proof.

On the set of inner band projections, consider the usual order of
operators. Let
\Gamma,\Delta\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda
.
We first want to prove that
P_{\Gamma}\wedge P_{\Delta}=P_{\Gamma\cap\Delta}
. For every
x\in A_{+}
, the net
(\sum_{(\gamma,\delta)\in\Psi}p_{\gamma}xp_{\delta})_{\Psi\in\Fin(\Delta)}
increases to
P_{\Delta}(x)
.
For every
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Gamma
, using that band
projections are order continuous:

L_{p_{\alpha}}R_{p_{\beta}}(P_{\Delta}(x))=\olim_{\Psi\in\Fin(\Delta)}\sum_{(\gamma,\delta)\in\Psi}L_{p_{\alpha}}R_{p_{\beta}}(p_{\gamma}xp_{\delta})=\begin{cases}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}&\text{if }(\alpha,\beta)\in\Delta,\\
0&\text{if }(\alpha,\beta)\not\in\Delta.\end{cases}

Therefore

P_{\Gamma}(P_{\Delta}(x))=\bigvee_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Gamma}L_{p_{\alpha}}R_{p_{\beta}}(P_{\Delta}(x))=\bigvee_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Gamma\cap\Delta}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}=P_{\Gamma\cap\Delta}(x),

as wanted

To check the formula for the supremum, first consider the
situation
\Gamma\cap\Delta=\varnothing
. In this case,
P_{\Gamma}P_{\Delta}=0
, and

P_{\Gamma}+P_{\Delta}=P_{\Gamma}\vee P_{\Delta}\leq P_{\Gamma\cup\Delta}

because both
P_{\Gamma}\leq P_{\Gamma\cup\Delta}
and
P_{\Delta}\leq P_{\Gamma\cup\Delta}
. To see the reverse inequality, take
finite sets
\Phi\in\Fin(\Gamma)
and
\Psi\in\Fin(\Delta)
.
Using that they are disjoint:

P_{\Gamma}(x)+P_{\Delta}(x)\geq\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}+\sum_{(\gamma,\delta)\in\Psi}p_{\gamma}xp_{\delta}=\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Phi\cup\Psi}p_{\alpha}xp_{\beta}.

The right hand side is an arbitrary finite subset of
\Gamma\cup\Delta
; taking supremum over these yields
P_{\Gamma}+P_{\Delta}\geq P_{\Gamma\cup\Delta}
. In particular, when

\Delta=\overline{\Gamma}
, we get the desired formula for the complementary.
Finally, for arbitrary
\Gamma
and
\Delta
(not necessarily
disjoint), write their union as the three disjoint unions
\Gamma\cup\Delta=(\overline{\Gamma}\cap\Delta)\cup(\Gamma\cap\overline{\Delta})\cup(\Gamma\cap\Delta)
and apply the previous formulae.
∎
