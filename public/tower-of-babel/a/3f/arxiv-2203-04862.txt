Information recoverability of noisy quantum states
Xuanqiang Zhao, Benchi Zhao, Zihan Xia, Xin Wang

Abstract
Extracting classical information from quantum systems is an essential step of many quantum algorithms. However, this information could be corrupted as the systems are prone to quantum noises, and its distortion under quantum dynamics has not been adequately investigated. In this work, we introduce a systematic framework to study how well we can retrieve information from noisy quantum states. Given a noisy quantum channel, we fully characterize the range of recoverable classical information. This condition allows a natural measure quantifying the information recoverability of a channel. Moreover, we resolve the minimum information retrieving cost, which, along with the corresponding optimal protocol, is efficiently computable by semidefinite programming. As applications, we establish the limits on the information retrieving cost for practical quantum noises and employ the corresponding protocols to mitigate errors in ground state energy estimation. Our work gives the first full characterization of information recoverability of noisy quantum states from the recoverable range to the recovering cost, revealing the ultimate limit of probabilistic error cancellation.

Background

The dynamics of a closed quantum system is described as a unitary evolution
[
1
]
. However, quantum systems are
rarely
closed in practice as they unavoidably interacts with the environment. Quantum channels, stemming from the unitary dynamics in a larger Hilbert space, are considered as the proper mathematical formalism depicting the evolution of general quantum systems
[
2
]
. Quantum channels represent the quantum information manipulation processes and are essential to quantum computation
[

,

.

A central
subroutine
of quantum computation is to extract classical information from a quantum system. The expectation value of some chosen observable, also known as shadow information,
characterizes
physical properties of the quantum system, making estimating expectation values the main goal of many quantum algorithms
[
2
,
9
]
, such as variational quantum eigensolver (VQE)
[

]
. The importance of expectation values has motivated the study of shadow tomography and related problems
[

,
12
,
13
]
.
However, inevitable noises modeled as quantum channels can corrupt the shadow information as they undesirably change the state of the quantum system, preventing us from estimating the expectation value accurately.

It is natural to ask how a quantum channel
{\cal N}
affects the information stored in quantum states. The minimum fidelity
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
of some initial state
|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
and the final state
{\cal N}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)
is the one we often use to answer this question.
But this method is not appropriate for quantifying how well a quantum channel preserves shadow information. Take the completely phase damping channel
{\cal N}_{\rm PD}(\rho)=Z\rho Z
as an example. While
F_{\min}({\cal N}_{\rm PD})=0
, the expectation value of a diagonal observable (e.g., observable
Z
) is unaffected. Similarly, other fidelity-based measures such as average fidelity and entanglement fidelity
[
17
,
14
]
are not suitable to depict the shadow information preservation either. Some other measures for information preservation are various channel capacities (e.g., classical capacity
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
), where each channel capacity quantifies a quantum channel’s utility to transfer information for a certain purpose.
However, these capacities are derived in asymptotic settings with multiple uses of the channel, and thus are not proper guides to practical tasks with finite quantum resources to some extent. Naturally, the following key questions then arise:

1.

What is a proper way to quantify the level of shadow information preservation by a

quantum channel?

2.

How to quantify the cost of retrieving a certain piece of shadow information given

it is preserved?

1.2
Contributions

To address these two questions in an operational way, we introduce a framework for retrieving shadow information from a noisy state.
Both practically relevant and theoretically interesting, this framework combines an efficient way to manipulate quantum states and general quantum operations. Note that similar protocols are employed in predicting properties of quantum states
[
21
,
13
]
and mitigating quantum errors
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26

{\cal N}(\rho)
are available. For each copy we apply a quantum channel randomly sampled from an ensemble of channels

and then measure the observable of interest, as shown in Fig.

preserves the classical information inquired by an observable

such that

\displaystyle\sum_{j}c_{j}\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal D}_{j}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O].

(1)

We establish a necessary and sufficient condition for the preservation of the desired shadow information in terms of a relation between
O

{\cal N}
that should be satisfied. This condition motivates a measure called
shadow destructivity
characterizing a quantum channel’s level of destroying shadow information.

We define a measure called

to quantify the minimum cost of recovering
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
, which also quantifies how well the channel preserves the shadow information queried by a specific observable. This measure, along with the concrete retrieving protocol, is efficiently computable with respect to the system dimension via semidefinite programming
[
27
]
. We analytically obtain the values of this measure and the retrieving protocols for generalized amplitude damping (GAD) channels and depolarizing channels. Our retrieving costs set ultimate limits on the cost required for shadow retrieving, and the corresponding protocols outperform existing probabilistic error cancellation (PEC) methods
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
.
Specifically, we employ our method to estimate the ground state energies of several molecules with VQE. The results show that the gap of sampling overhead between our method and a conventional PEC method gets larger as the size of the quantum system grows, implying potential applications of our method in implementing near-term algorithms.

1.3
Preliminaries

Before introducing our results, we set the notations and define several quantities that will be used throughout this paper.
We use symbols such as
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
to denote finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces associated with systems
A
and
B
, respectively.
We use
d_{A}
to denote the dimension of the system
A
. The sets of linear and Hermitian operators acting on
A
are denoted by
{\cal L}_{A}
and
{\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A}
, respectively.

In this work, we focus on linear maps having the same input and output dimension. A linear map
{\cal N}_{A\to A^{\prime}}
transforms linear operators in
{\cal L}_{A}
to linear operators in
{\cal L}_{A^{\prime}}
, where
{\cal H}_{A^{\prime}}
is isomorphic to
{\cal H}_{A}
.
We call a linear map
{\cal N}_{A\to A^{\prime}}
a quantum channel if it is completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP). By saying
{\cal N}
is completely positive (CP), we mean
{\operatorname{id}}_{R}\otimes{\cal N}
is a positive map with a reference system
R
of arbitrary dimension. By saying
{\cal N}
is trace-preserving (TP), we mean
\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal N}(X)]=\operatorname{Tr}[X]
for all
X\in{\cal L}_{A}
.
A linear map
{\cal N}
is called Hermitian-preserving (HP) if
{\cal N}(X)\in{\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A^{\prime}}
for all
X\in{\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A}
. For any linear map
{\cal N}_{A\to A^{\prime}}
, its Choi-Jamiołkowski matrix is given by
J_{{\cal N}}\equiv\sum_{i,j=0}^{d_{A}-1}|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes{\cal N}_{A\to A^{\prime}}(|i\rangle\langle j|)
, where
\{|i\rangle\}_{i=0}^{d_{A}-1}
is an orthonormal basis in
{\cal H}_{A}
.

Main Results

2.1
A Necessary and Sufficient Condition

We study the preservation of shadow information by considering an operational task: recovering the expectation value
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
of an observable
O
from multiple copies of a corrupted state
{\cal N}(\rho)
, where
{\cal N}
is the noisy channel of interest and
\rho
is an unknown state. A usual way
[
25
]
is to simulate a Hermitian-preserving and trace-preserving (HPTP) map
{\cal D}
, which we call a retriever in this work, such that
{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}={\operatorname{id}}
, where
{\operatorname{id}}
is the identity map. While this approach is sufficient for recovering the target information, its requirement on the retriever
{\cal D}
is generally not necessary as we are only concerned with the expectation value of a specific observable. Hence, the only necessary requirement for a retriever
{\cal D}
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]

(2)

for any state
\rho
and a fixed observable
O
. Our framework extends the set of potential retrievers from HPTP maps to Hermitian-preserving and trace-scaling (HPTS) maps for that HPTS maps are physically simulatable in the way prescribed by Eq. (
1
), as shown in Lemma
6
. We say a linear map
{\cal N}
is trace-scaling (TS) if
\operatorname{Tr}_{A^{\prime}}[J_{\cal N}]=pI_{A}
for some real number
p
, and HPTP maps are special cases of HPTS maps when
p=1
.

\displaystyle{\cal N}^{\dagger}\circ{\cal D}^{\dagger}(O)=O,

Lemma 1

Given an observable
O
, an HP map
{\cal M}
satisfies
\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal M}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
for any state
\rho
if and only if it holds that
{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)=O
.

Proof.

First, we assume that
{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)=O
is true. For
{\cal M}
being HP, its adjoint map
{\cal M}^{\dagger}
is also HP (see Remark
2.1
). Hence,
\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal M}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho({\cal M}^{\dagger}(O^{\dagger}))^{\dagger}]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)]
for both
O
and
{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)
being Hermitian. Then, with our assumption, we have
\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal M}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
.

For the other direction, given
\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal M}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
for all states
\rho
, we immediately have
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)\neq O
. If we define
\Delta O={\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)-O
, then
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\Delta O]=0
for all
\rho
. However, this implies that
\Delta O=0
since otherwise we can choose a
\sigma
from the nonzero eigenspace of
\Delta O
so that
\operatorname{Tr}[\sigma\Delta O]\neq 0
. As
\Delta O=0
contradicts with the assumption that
{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)\neq O
, we conclude that
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
holds for all
\rho
only if
{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)=O
.

Remark 1

A linear map
{\cal N}
is Hermitian-preserving (HP) if and only if
J_{\cal N}
is Hermitian. The adjoint of an HP map
{\cal N}
is also HP. This can be seen by checking the Hermiticity of its Choi matrix:
J_{{\cal N}^{\dagger}}^{\dagger}=(J_{\cal N}^{T})^{\dagger}=J_{\cal N}^{T}=J_{{\cal N}^{\dagger}}
for
J_{\cal N}
being Hermitian.

Lemma
1
sets the criterion that we should refer to when searching for a retriever. We say a channel
{\cal N}
preserves the shadow information queried by an observable
O
if there exists a retriever
{\cal D}
satisfying Eq. (
3
). It is crucial to ask for what observables the channel preserves their corresponding shadow information.
In Theorem
2
, we establish a necessary and sufficient condition for the preservation of the shadow information by introducing the adjoint image of
{\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A^{\prime}}
under the channel
{\cal N}_{A\to A^{\prime}}
, which we define to be the image of
{\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A^{\prime}}
under
{\cal N}^{\dagger}_{A^{\prime}\to A}
and is denoted by
{\cal N}^{\dagger}({\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A^{\prime}})
.

Theorem 2

(Necessary and sufficient condition for information preservation).

Given a quantum channel
{\cal N}_{A\to A^{\prime}}
and an observable
O
, there exists an HPTS map
{\cal D}
such that
{\cal M}\equiv{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}
satisfies
{\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)=O
if and only if
O\in{\cal N}^{\dagger}({\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A^{\prime}})
.

The proof for the necessity part is rather straightforward, while that for the sufficiency part is nontrivial. To prove the “if” part, we denote by
Q
a Hermitian operator that evolves into the observable
O
under the map
{\cal N}^{\dagger}
, i.e.,
{\cal N}^{\dagger}(Q)=O
. Then, it is sufficient to construct an HPTS map
{\cal D}
such that
{\cal D}^{\dagger}(O)=Q
. According to Lemma
S1
, we can instead construct an HP and unit-scaling map
{\cal D}^{\dagger}
, which guarantees that the map
{\cal D}
is HPTS. By saying that
{\cal D}^{\dagger}
is unit-scaling, we mean that
{\cal D}^{\dagger}
scales the identity operator. The main difficulty of this construction is to ensure that
{\cal D}^{\dagger}
is unit-scaling. To overcome this difficulty, we split all the observables into three categories by their rank and trace.

We first consider observables that do not have full rank and are not traceless, i.e.,
\operatorname{Tr}[O]\neq 0
. For these observables, we exploit their kernel so that the constructed
{\cal D}^{\dagger}
is unit-scaling. With some manipulation, the other two cases can be reduced to this one. The detailed proof is given below.

Proof.

For the “only if” part, suppose such a map
{\cal D}
exists for the given channel
{\cal N}
and non-zero observable
O
. Note that
{\cal D}^{\dagger}
is also a Hermitian-preserving map since its Choi operator is Hermitian, as we have noted in Remark

{\cal N}^{\dagger}(Q)={\cal N}^{\dagger}\circ{\cal D}^{\dagger}(O)={\cal M}^{\dagger}(O)=O

2.2
Quantifying the Level of Shadow Information Preservation

Following Theorem
2
, for a quantum channel
{\cal N}_{A\to A^{\prime}}
, we define the dimension of
{\cal N}^{\dagger}({\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A^{\prime}})
as the channel
{\cal N}
’s
effective shadow dimension

d_{\rm s}({\cal N})
, which quantifies how much shadow information is preserved by
{\cal N}
. To compute the effective shadow dimension, we note that the set of all Hermitian operators
{\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A^{\prime}}
can be viewed as a vector space over the field of real numbers, and the set of all linear operators
{\cal L}_{A^{\prime}}
is a vector space over the field of complex numbers. Both vector spaces are with dimension
d_{A^{\prime}}^{2}
, where
d_{A^{\prime}}=\dim({\cal H}_{A^{\prime}})
. Note that a basis for
{\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A^{\prime}}
is also a basis for
{\cal L}_{A^{\prime}}
, which implies that
\dim({\cal N}^{\dagger}({\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A^{\prime}}))=\dim({\cal N}^{\dagger}({\cal L}_{A^{\prime}}))
. Then, by the properties of the adjoint
[
28
]
, the image of
{\cal N}^{\dagger}
is the space orthogonal to the null space of
{\cal N}
. Hence, the dimension of
{\cal N}^{\dagger}({\cal L}^{\rm H}_{A^{\prime}})
is
d_{A^{\prime}}^{2}-\dim({\rm null}({\cal N}))=\dim({\rm Image}({\cal N}))
, which equals the rank of a matrix of
{\cal N}
as a linear map.

A matrix of
{\cal N}
as a linear map can be obtained from the channel’s Kraus representation. Given
{\cal N}(|i\rangle\!\langle j|)=\sum_{k}E_{k}|i\rangle\!\langle j|E_{k}^{\dagger}
, we have
{\rm vec}({\cal N}(|i\rangle\!\langle j|))=\sum_{k}\overline{E_{k}}|j\rangle\otimes E_{k}|i\rangle=(\sum_{k}\overline{E_{k}}\otimes E_{k})|ji\rangle
, where
\overline{E_{k}}
is the complex conjugate of
E_{k}
. Hence,
M_{\cal N}\equiv\sum_{k}\overline{E_{k}}\otimes E_{k}
is a matrix representation of
{\cal N}
as a linear map. The effective shadow dimension
d_{\rm s}({\cal N})
is efficiently computable with respect to the system dimension as the matrix rank of
M_{\cal N}
.

Based on the effective shadow dimension, we define a measure called
shadow destructivity
:

\displaystyle\zeta({\cal N})\equiv\log\frac{d^{2}}{d_{\rm s}({\cal N})},

(20)

where
d
is the dimension of the Hilbert space that the input linear operators in
{\cal L}_{A}
act on. The shadow destructivity quantifies a quantum channel’s
capability
to destruct shadow information and has some meaningful properties:

1.

(
Faithfulness
)
\zeta({\cal N})=0
if and only if all the shadow information is recoverable. In other words,
\zeta
is strictly larger than
0
if and only if some shadow information is irreversibly lost.

Proposition 3

(Faithfulness of shadow destructivity).

The shadow destructivity is faithful in the sense that
\zeta({\cal N})\geq 0
and it vanishes, i.e.,
\zeta({\cal N})=0
, if and only if for each observable
O
, there exists a retriever
{\cal D}
such that
\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
for every quantum state
\rho
.

Proof.

First suppose
\zeta({\cal N})\equiv\log(d^{2}/d_{\rm s}({\cal N}))=0
, which is only true when
d_{\rm s}({\cal N})=d^{2}
and thus implying that
{\cal N}^{\dagger}
is surjective, i.e.,
{\cal L}^{\rm H}
being the image of
{\cal N}^{\dagger}
. In other words, the image of
{\cal N}^{\dagger}
consists of all observables. Then, according to Theorem
2
and Lemma
1
, for every observable
O
, there is a retriever
{\cal D}
such that
\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
for every state
\rho
.

Now suppose that for every observable
O
, there is a retriever
{\cal D}
such that
\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
for every state
\rho
. By Theorem
2
, this implies that all observables are in the image of
{\cal N}^{\dagger}
. Since the image can only be a subspace of
{\cal L}^{\rm H}
, we know that the image is
{\cal L}^{\rm H}
itself, whose dimension is
d^{2}
. Hence,
d_{\rm s}({\cal N})\equiv\dim({\cal N}^{\dagger}({\cal L}^{\rm H}))=d^{2}
, and we conclude that
\zeta({\cal N})\equiv\log(d^{2}/d_{\rm s}({\cal N}))=0
.

We have shown that
\zeta({\cal N})=0
if and only if all the shadow information is recoverable. In addition, the shadow destructivity is non-negative by its definition. Therefore, the shadow destructivity is faithful.

Additivity

\zeta({\cal M}\otimes{\cal N})=\zeta({\cal M})+\zeta({\cal N})
for two quantum channels

Proposition 4

(Additivity of shadow destructivity).

The shadow destructivity is additive with respect to tensor product, i.e.,

\displaystyle\zeta({\cal M}\otimes{\cal N})=\zeta({\cal M})+\zeta({\cal N}),

(21)

where
{\cal M}_{A\to A^{\prime}}
and
{\cal N}_{B\to B^{\prime}}
are two quantum channels.

Proof.

We have showed that the effective shadow dimension of a channel equals the rank of the matrix of it as a linear map, that is:
d_{\rm s}({\cal M})={\rm rank}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{\cal M}})

d_{\rm s}({\cal N})={\rm rank}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{\cal N}})
, and
d_{\rm s}({\cal M}\otimes{\cal N})={\rm rank}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{{\cal M}\otimes{\cal N}}})
. Hence,
\zeta({\cal M}\otimes{\cal N})\equiv\log((d_{A}d_{B})^{2}/d_{\rm s}({\cal M}\otimes{\cal N}))=\log(d_{A}^{2}d_{B}^{2}/{\rm rank}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{{\cal M}\otimes{\cal N}}}))

(22)

By this property, we have

\displaystyle\zeta({\cal M}\otimes{\cal N})=

\displaystyle\log(d_{A}^{2}d_{B}^{2}/({\rm rank}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{\cal M}}){\rm rank}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{\cal N}})))

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log(d_{A}^{2}/{\rm rank}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{\cal M}}))+\log(d_{B}^{2}/{\rm rank}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{\cal N}}))

(25)

3.

(
Data processing inequality
) The shadow destructivity cannot increase when a quantum state is sent through more channels, that is,
\zeta({\cal N}\circ{\cal M})\geq\max(\zeta({\cal N}),\zeta({\cal M}))
. This property implies that we can only lose shadow information by sending quantum states through quantum channels.

Proposition 5

(Data processing inequality of shadow destructivity).

For two quantum channels
{\cal M}
and
{\cal N}
, it holds that
\zeta({\cal N}\circ{\cal M})\geq\max(\zeta({\cal N}),\zeta({\cal M}))
.

Proof.

If we write the channels
{\cal M}
and
{\cal N}
in their matrix representations
M_{\cal M}
and
M_{\cal N}
, the the matrix representation of the composite channel
{\cal N}\circ{\cal M}
is
M_{\cal N}M_{\cal M}
, which is known to have a rank
{\rm rank}(M_{\cal N}M_{\cal M})\leq\min({\rm rank}(M_{\cal N}),{\rm rank}(M_{\cal M}))
. This is equivalent to
d_{\rm s}({\cal N}\circ{\cal M})\leq\min(d_{\rm s}({\cal N}),d_{\rm s}({\cal M}))
, which implies
\zeta({\cal N}\circ{\cal M})\geq\max(\zeta({\cal N}),\zeta({\cal M}))
.

\sqcap
\sqcup

As the shadow destructivity derived from Theorem
2
can be computed from the matrix rank of
M_{\cal N}
, Theorem
2
endows the matrix rank of
M_{\cal N}
an operational meaning in shadow information recoverability, where a higher rank indicates that more shadow information is recoverable. It is well known that a matrix is invertible if and only if it has full rank. Since full rank corresponds to the total recoverability of shadow information, invertibility of a channel is equivalent to its total shadow information recoverability.

2.3
Quantifying the Cost of Retrieving Shadow Information

By saying that a quantum channel preserves some shadow information, we mean this information can be retrieved after a state is corrupted by this channel. Though the effective shadow dimension of a quantum channel captures its overall capability of preserving shadow information, a piece of shadow information being preserved does not mean that retrieving it is cost-free, and different pieces of information can have different retrieving cost. In the following part, we derive a measure called
retrieving cost
from the number of copies of the corrupted state required to estimate the desired shadow information within an acceptable accuracy.

Note that the retriever
{\cal D}
is an HPTS map but not necessarily CPTP, i.e., not necessarily a quantum channel. While such a map
{\cal D}
is not physically implementable, we can simulate its action by decomposing it as a linear combination of CPTP maps:
{\cal D}=\sum_{j}c_{j}{\cal D}_{j}
, and utilizing Eq. (
1
). As Lemma
6
implies, a linear map is physically simulatable in this framework if and only if it is HPTS.

Lemma 6

A linear map
{\cal D}
is HPTS if and only if it can be written as a linear combination of quantum channels
\{{\cal D}_{j}\}
with real numbers
\{c_{j}\}
:
{\cal D}=\sum_{j}c_{j}{\cal D}_{j}
.
Furthermore, for any HPTS map
{\cal D}
, there exist two quantum channels
{\cal D}_{1},{\cal D}_{2}
with real numbers
c_{1},c_{2}
such that

{\cal D}=c_{1}{\cal D}_{1}+c_{2}{\cal D}_{2}.

(26)

Proof.

We first prove that a linear combination of quantum channels is HPTS. The Choi-Jamiołkowski matrix of such a linear combination
{\cal D}=\sum_{j}c_{j}{\cal D}_{j}
is
J_{\cal D}=\sum_{j}c_{j}J_{{\cal D}_{j}}
, where
J_{{\cal D}_{j}}\geq 0
and
\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[J_{{\cal D}_{j}}]=I_{A}
for each
j
. As a linear combination of Hermitian operators is also Hermitian,
{\cal D}
is Hermitian-preserving. Also noting that
\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[J_{\cal D}]=\sum_{j}c_{j}\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[J_{{\cal D}_{j}}]=\sum_{j}c_{j}I_{A}
, we have
\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[J_{\cal D}]=cI_{A}
, where
c=\sum_{j}c_{j}
. Hence,
{\cal D}
is HPTS.

For the other direction, if a map
{\cal D}
is HPTS, its Choi-Jamiołkowski matrix
J_{\cal D}
satisfies
J_{\cal D}
being Hermitian and
\operatorname{Tr}_{B}[J_{\cal D}]=cI_{A}
for some real coefficient
c
. If
c\neq 0
,
{\cal D}/c
is an HPTP map and there exist two CPTP maps
{\cal D}_{1},{\cal D}_{2}
and two real numbers
c_{1}\geq 0,c_{2}\leq 0
such that
{\cal D}/c=c_{1}{\cal D}_{1}+c_{2}{\cal D}_{2}

[
25
]
. Then we have
{\cal D}=c\cdot c_{1}{\cal D}_{1}+c\cdot c_{2}{\cal D}_{2}
. On the other hand, if
c=0
,
{\cal D}+{\operatorname{id}}
is an HPTP map and there still exist two CPTP maps
{\cal D}_{1},{\cal D}_{2}
and two real numbers
c_{1}\geq 0,c_{2}\leq 0
such that
{\cal D}+{\operatorname{id}}=c_{1}{\cal D}_{1}+c_{2}{\cal D}_{2}
. Hence,
{\cal D}
can be written as
{\cal D}=c_{1}{\cal D}_{1}+(c_{2}-1){\cal D}_{2}^{\prime}
, where
{\cal D}_{2}^{\prime}=c_{2}{\cal D}_{2}/(c_{2}-1)-{\operatorname{id}}/(c_{2}-1)
is a quantum channel.

\sqcap
\sqcup

Now let
{\cal D}
be an HPTS map whose decomposition is
\sum_{j}c_{j}{\cal D}_{j}
.
With this decomposition, we can simulate its action on the expectation value by the probabilistic sampling method prescribed by Eq. (
1
). Specifically, in the
s
-th round of total S times of sampling, we first sample a quantum channel
{\cal D}^{(s)}
from
\{{\cal D}_{j}\}
with probabilities
\{|c_{j}|/\gamma\}
, where
\gamma=\sum_{j}|c_{j}|
, and apply it to a copy of the corrupted state to obtain
{\cal D}^{(s)}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)
. Then, we measure this state in the eigenbasis
\{|\psi_{j}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{j}|\}_{j}
of the given observable
O=\sum_{j}o_{j}|\psi_{j}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{j}|
, where
o_{j}\in[-1,1]
, and obtain the measurement value
o^{(s)}
. After
S
rounds of sampling, we attain an estimation for the expectation value
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
as

\displaystyle\xi=\frac{\gamma}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}{\rm sgn}(c^{(s)})o^{(s)}.

(27)

By the Hoeffding’s inequality
[
30
]
, the number of rounds required to obtain the estimation within an error
\varepsilon
with a probability no less than
1-\delta
is

S=2\gamma^{2}\log(2/\delta)/\varepsilon^{2}.

(28)

It can be seen that the required number of copies
S
of the corrupted state is directly related to
\gamma
, the sum of absolute values of all
c_{j}
. Hence,
\gamma
can be used to characterize the cost of simulating the HPTS map
{\cal D}
. It is desirable to find a decomposition of
{\cal D}
making
\gamma
as small as possible, and we denote the minimum possible value of
\gamma
as
\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})
. In Ref.
[
25
]
, the authors define the logarithm of
\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})
as the physical implementability of an HPTP map
{\cal D}
.

Naturally, given a channel
{\cal N}
, we define the
retrieving cost
with respect to an observable
O
to be

\displaystyle\gamma_{O}({\cal N})=\min\{\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})|{\cal D}\text{ is HPTS},\penalty\ {\cal N}^{\dagger}\circ{\cal D}^{\dagger}(O)=O\},

(29)

which is the
\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})
minimized over
{\cal D}
that can retrieve the shadow information queried by
O
from the noisy channel
{\cal N}
.

Notably, Lemma
6
states that any HPTS map can be decomposed as a linear combination of two quantum channels (Eq. (
26
)), and we show in the Supplemental Material that the minimum retrieving cost can be achieved by such a decomposition. This is true because all channels with non-negative coefficients can be grouped into a single channel with a non-negative coefficient, and we can do the same thing for all channels with negative coefficients. The existence of such an optimal decomposition with two CPTP maps leads to an efficient way to compute this measure through a semidefinite program (SDP) in terms of linear maps’ Choi-Jamiołkowski matrices:

\displaystyle\gamma_{O}({\cal N})=\min

\displaystyle\;c_{1}+c_{2}

(30a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;J_{\cal D}\equiv J_{{\cal D}_{1}}-J_{{\cal D}_{2}}

(30b)

\displaystyle\;J_{{\cal D}_{1}}\geq 0,\;J_{{\cal D}_{2}}\geq 0

(30c)

\displaystyle\;\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{o}^{\prime}}[J_{{\cal D}_{1{A^{\prime}_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}}}}]=c_{1}I_{A^{\prime}_{i}},\;\operatorname{Tr}_{A^{\prime}_{o}}[J_{{\cal D}_{2{A^{\prime}_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}}}}]=c_{2}I_{A^{\prime}_{i}}

(30d)

\displaystyle\;J_{{\cal M}_{A_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}}}\equiv\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{o}A^{\prime}_{i}}[(J_{{\cal N}_{A_{o}A^{\prime}_{i}}}^{T}\otimes I_{A_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}})(J_{{\operatorname{id}}_{A_{i}A_{o}}}\otimes J_{{\cal D}_{A^{\prime}_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}}})]

(30e)

\displaystyle\;\operatorname{Tr}_{A^{\prime}_{o}}[(I_{A_{i}}\otimes O_{A^{\prime}_{o}}^{T})J_{{\cal M}_{A_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}}}^{T}]=O_{A_{i}},

(30f)

where
J_{\operatorname{id}}
is the Choi-Jamiołkowski matrix of an identity channel.
Eq. (
30c
) corresponds to the condition that
{\cal D}_{1}
and
{\cal D}_{2}
are CP, and Eq. (
30d
) requires them to be TP. Eq. (
30f
) corresponds to the minimum requirement for a valid retriever as given in Eq. (
3
).
Note that we can establish approximate versions of Eq. (
30
) by relaxing the requirement in Eq. (
30f
) such that
\operatorname{Tr}_{A^{\prime}_{o}}[(I_{A_{i}}\otimes O_{A^{\prime}_{o}}^{T})J_{{\cal M}_{A_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}}}^{T}]
is close to
O
within a certain tolerance.
This relaxation provides a remedy to cases where a perfect retriever does not exist as well as a trade-off between the retrieving cost and the estimation precision.

2.4
Case Study and Applications in Error Mitigation

To intuitively understand effective shadow dimension and shadow destructivity, we study two non-invertible channels as examples:
{\cal N}_{1}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}I(\cdot)I+\frac{1}{2}X(\cdot)X
and
{\cal N}_{2}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}I(\cdot)I+\frac{1}{4}X(\cdot)X+\frac{1}{4}Y(\cdot)Y
. For a quantum state

\rho=\begin{pmatrix}a&b+ci\\
b-ci&1-a\end{pmatrix}
, where
a,b,c
are real numbers and
i\equiv\sqrt{-1}
, it can be easily verified that

{\cal N}_{1}(\rho)=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&b\\
b&1/2\end{pmatrix}
and

{\cal N}_{2}(\rho)=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&(b+ci)/2\\
(b-ci)/2&1/2\end{pmatrix}
.
While both channels are non-invertible, it is intuitive to see that channel
{\cal N}_{2}
preserves more information than channel
{\cal N}_{1}
since the imaginary part of off-diagonal elements is preserved. This intuition coincides with the effective shadow dimension of these two channels,
d_{\rm s}({\cal N}_{2})=3>d_{\rm s}({\cal N}_{1})=2
, which implies that
{\cal N}_{2}
preserves one more shadow dimension than
{\cal N}_{1}
. The shadow destructivity of these channels,
\zeta({\cal N}_{1})=\log(4/2)=1
and
\zeta({\cal N}_{2})=\log(4/3)\approx 0.415
, has similar implications.

In error mitigation, PEC methods are promising to suppress noises in quantum circuits run on near-term quantum computers and return us unbiased estimation of expectation values

{\cal N}

{\cal N}^{-1}=\sum_{j}a_{j}{\cal A}_{j}

a_{j}

{\cal A}_{j}

O
with respect to a state
\rho

{\cal N}(\rho)

\gamma_{\rm con}\sum_{j}p(j)\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal A}_{j}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal N}^{-1}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)O]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O].

(31)

\gamma_{\rm con}

\gamma_{\rm con}
is usually used to evaluate the efficiency of an error mitigation protocol.

3
Conclusions

In this work, we establish two measures from an operational perspective to quantify a quantum channel’s influence on shadow information encoded in quantum states, answering the questions posed at the beginning.
Our work delivers a systematic framework of quantifying the information preservativity and destructivity of a quantum channel. Meanwhile it establishes an optimal way of extracting noiseless classical information from noisy states, which could be useful for near-term quantum information processing tasks.

While the shadow destructivity and the retrieving cost capture different aspects of shadow information recoverability, a single unified measure that captures both aspects may be worth further studies.
For future work, it would be interesting to see how the techniques presented in this work can be combined with quantum error correction
[

,
48
]
or quantum error mitigation
[
41
,
37
,

,
43
,
44
]
. We also expect that our ideas could be applied to near-term quantum tasks or applications on noisy quantum devices
[

]

The adjoint of a trace-scaling map is unit-scaling,

Proof.

{\cal M}^{\dagger}

Retrieving Cost

Recall that, given a quantum channel
{\cal N}

O

\displaystyle\gamma_{O}({\cal N})=\min\{\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})|{\cal D}\text{ is HPTS},\penalty\ {\cal N}^{\dagger}\circ{\cal D}^{\dagger}(O)=O\},

(S1)

where
\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})
is the minimum cost for simulating
{\cal D}
. Here, we give a proof that the minimum cost of simulating an HPTS map can be achieved by a decomposition with two CPTP maps. The same thing has been proved for HPTP maps in Ref.
[
25

Proposition S2

For any HPTS map
{\cal D}
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})=\min\left\{|c_{1}|+|c_{2}|\big|{\cal D}=c_{1}{\cal D}_{1}+c_{2}{\cal D}_{2};\penalty\ {\cal D}_{1},{\cal D}_{2}\text{ are CPTP};\penalty\ c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

(S2)

Proof.

Lemma
6
implies that
\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})
is finite as
{\cal D}
can be decomposed into a linear combination of two CPTP maps. Suppose
\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})
is achieved by
{\cal D}=\sum_{j=1}^{T}c^{\prime}_{j}{\cal D}^{\prime}_{j}
, where
T\geq 3
so that
\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})=\sum_{j=1}^{T}|c^{\prime}_{j}|
. Then, it is always possible to construct two CPTP maps
{\cal D}_{1},{\cal D}_{2}
so that
{\cal D}=c_{1}{\cal D}_{1}+c_{2}{\cal D}_{2}
and
|c_{1}|+|c_{2}|=\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})
. In particular, let
c_{1}=\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}c^{\prime}_{j}\geq 0}c^{\prime}_{j}
,
{\cal D}_{1}=\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}c^{\prime}_{j}\geq 0}c^{\prime}_{j}/c_{1}\cdot{\cal D}^{\prime}_{j}
,
c_{2}=\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}c^{\prime}_{j}<0}c^{\prime}_{j}
, and
{\cal D}_{2}=\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}c^{\prime}_{j}<0}c^{\prime}_{j}/c_{2}\cdot{\cal D}^{\prime}_{j}
. One can check that
{\cal D}_{1}
and
{\cal D}_{2}
are CPTP and
c_{1}{\cal D}_{1}+c_{2}{\cal D}_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{T}c^{\prime}_{j}{\cal D}^{\prime}_{j}
. Thus, this decomposition of
{\cal D}
is valid. Plus,
|c_{1}|+|c_{2}|=\sum_{j=1}^{T}|c^{\prime}_{j}|
, ensuring that this decomposition achieves
\gamma_{\min}({\cal D})
.

3
Retrieving Cost for GAD channel

A GAD channel can be used to describe the energy dissipation to the environment at finite temperature
[
45
]
. It is one of the realistic sources of noise in superconducting quantum computing. For single-qubit cases, a GAD channel can be characterized by the following Kraus operators:

\displaystyle E_{0}=\sqrt{p}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-\epsilon}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle,E_{1}=\sqrt{p}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\sqrt{\epsilon}\\
0&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle E_{2}=\sqrt{1-p}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{1-\epsilon}&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle,E_{3}=\sqrt{1-p}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
\sqrt{\epsilon}&0\end{array}\right),

where
\epsilon
is the damping factor and
p
is the indicator of the temperature of the environment. A quantum state
\rho
after going through the GAD channel is given by
{\cal N}_{\rm GAD}(\rho)=\sum_{i=0}^{3}E_{i}\rho E_{i}^{\dagger}
.
Note that the amplitude damping channel is a special case of the GAD channel when
p=1
.
A single qubit state

\rho=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{00}&\rho_{01}\\
\rho_{10}&\rho_{11}\end{pmatrix}

after going through the GAD channel is

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}={\cal N}_{\rm GAD}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(1-\epsilon)\rho_{00}+p\epsilon(\rho_{00}+\rho_{11})&\sqrt{1-\epsilon}\rho_{01}\\
\sqrt{1-\epsilon}\rho_{10}&\rho_{11}+\epsilon\rho_{00}-p\epsilon(\rho_{00}+\rho_{11})\end{array}\right),

where
\epsilon
and
p
are noise parameters.

Proposition S3

Given an observable
O\in\{X,Y\}
and a GAD channel
{\cal N}
, the minimum cost
\gamma
to retrieve the information
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
is
\gamma_{O}({\cal N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\epsilon}}
, and the corresponding retriever
{\cal D}
can be written in the form of a Choi matrix

J_{{\cal D}}=c_{1}J_{{\cal D}_{1}}+c_{2}J_{{\cal D}_{2}},

where
c_{1}=-c_{2}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-\epsilon}}
,
J_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}O^{T}\otimes O+\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I
, and
J_{\mathcal{D}_{2}}=-\frac{1}{2}O^{T}\otimes O+\frac{1}{2}I\otimes I
.

Proof.

First, we are going to prove
\gamma_{O}({\cal N})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\epsilon}}
using SDP (
S3
).
We show that the retriever
{\cal D}
above is a feasible solution with a cost of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\epsilon}}
. So
to
be specific, we have

\displaystyle{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)

\displaystyle={\cal D}(\rho^{\prime})

(S29)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{A}[(\rho^{\prime T}\otimes I)\cdot J_{{\cal D}}]

(S30)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-\epsilon}}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}[(\rho^{\prime T}\otimes I)(O^{T}\otimes O)]

(S31)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-\epsilon}}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}[\rho^{\prime T}O^{T}\otimes IO]

(S32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-\epsilon}}O\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}O].

(S33)

This means that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)O]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-\epsilon}}\operatorname{Tr}[O^{2}]\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}O]

(S34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\epsilon}}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}O]

(S35)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[O\rho],

(S36)

where the second equality follows from the fact that

for

Second, we use the dual SDP (

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{i}A_{o}}[(K^{T}_{A_{i}}\otimes J^{T}_{{\cal N}_{A_{o}A^{\prime}_{i}}}\otimes O_{A^{\prime}_{o}})(J_{{\operatorname{id}}_{A_{i}A_{o}}}\otimes I_{A^{\prime}_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}})]

(S61)

4
Retrieving Cost for Mixed Pauli Channel

The mixed Pauli channel is a common noise model in quantum computers. For single-qubit cases, a quantum state corrupted by mixed Pauli becomes

{\cal N}_{\rm Pauli}(\rho)=p_{i}\rho+p_{x}X\rho X+p_{y}Y\rho Y+p_{z}Z\rho Z,

(S81)

where
p_{i},p_{x},p_{y},p_{z}
are the corresponding probabilities with
p_{i}+p_{x}+p_{y}+p_{z}=1
and
0\leq p\leq 1
for each
p\in\{p_{i},p_{x},p_{y},p_{z}\}
. For general
n
-qubit cases, the noisy state is

{\cal N}_{\rm Pauli}(\rho)=\sum_{\sigma}p_{\sigma}\sigma\rho\sigma,

(S82)

where the sum is over all the
n
-qubit Pauli operators
\{\sigma\}
, and
\{p_{\sigma}\}
are the corresponding probabilities with
\sum_{\sigma}p_{\sigma}=1
and
0\leq p_{\sigma}\leq 1
. Note that the depolarizing channel is a special case of the mixed Pauli channels, where
p_{x}=p_{y}=p_{z}
.

Proposition S5

Given an
n
-qubit observable
O=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}
, where
\sigma_{i}\in\{X,Y,Z,I\}
, and a mixed Pauli channel
{\cal N}
. The minimum cost
\gamma_{O}({\cal N})
to retrieve the information
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]
is
\gamma_{O}({\cal N})=\frac{1}{\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}}
, and the corresponding retriever
{\cal D}
can be written in the form of a Choi matrix
J_{{\cal D}}=c_{1}J_{{\cal D}_{1}}+c_{2}J_{{\cal D}_{1}}
, where
J_{{\cal D}_{1}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}I^{\otimes 2n}+\frac{1}{2^{n}}(O^{T}\otimes O),J_{{\cal D}_{2}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}I^{\otimes 2n}-\frac{1}{2^{n}}(O^{T}\otimes O)
and
c_{1}=-c_{2}=\frac{1}{2(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}})}
. The
\{\sigma^{+}\}
are all the Pauli operators that commute with the observable, i.e.,
\sigma^{+}\in P_{n},[\sigma^{+},O]=0
, and correspondingly
\sigma^{-}\in P_{n},\{\sigma^{-},O\}=0
.

Proof.

First, we are going to prove
\gamma_{O}({\cal N})\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}}
. We prove this by utilizing SDP (
S3
) and showing that the retriever
{\cal D}
is a feasible solution.

For an arbitrary state
\rho
, after the mixed Pauli channel, it becomes
\rho^{\prime}={\cal N}(\rho)=\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}\sigma^{+}\rho\sigma^{+}+\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}\sigma^{-}\rho\sigma^{-}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)

\displaystyle={\cal D}(\rho^{\prime})

(S83)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{A}[(\rho^{\prime T}\otimes I)\cdot J_{{\cal D}}^{O}]

(S84)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\cdot(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}})}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}[(\rho^{\prime T}\otimes I)\cdot(O^{T}\otimes O)]

(S85)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\cdot(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}})}\operatorname{Tr}_{A}[\rho^{\prime T}O^{T}\otimes IO]

(S86)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\cdot(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}})}O\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{\prime T}O^{T}]

(S87)

Since the transpose is linear, we have
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{\prime T}O^{T}]=\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho^{\prime}O)^{T}]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}O]
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\cdot(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}})}O\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}O]

(S89)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\cdot(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}})}O\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}\sigma^{+}\rho\sigma^{+}+\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}\sigma^{-}\rho\sigma^{-}\right)O\right]

(S90)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\cdot(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}})}O\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}\sigma^{+}\rho O\sigma^{+}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}\sigma^{-}\rho O\sigma^{-}\right)\right]

(S91)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\cdot(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}})}O\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}\rho O\sigma^{+}\sigma^{+}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}\rho O\sigma^{-}\sigma^{-}\right)\right]

(S92)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}\cdot(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}})}O\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}\right)\rho O\right]

(S93)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}O\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O],

(S94)

where the third equality follows from the commutative properties of
\{\sigma_{+}\}
and
\{\sigma_{-}\}
with respect to the observable, the fourth equality follows from the cyclic property of trace, and the fifth equality follows from the identities that
\sigma^{+}\sigma^{+}=\sigma^{-}\sigma^{-}=I^{\otimes n}
.
Then, it is easy to check that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[O{\cal D}\circ{\cal N}(\rho)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\operatorname{Tr}[O^{2}]\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]

(S95)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\operatorname{Tr}[I^{\otimes n}]\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O]

(S96)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho O],

(S97)

which means that the retriever
{\cal D}
is a feasible protocol, and thus we have
\gamma_{O}({\cal N})\leq|c_{1}|+|c_{2}|=\frac{1}{\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}}
.

Second, we use the dual SDP (
S18
) to show that
\gamma_{O}({\cal N})\geq\frac{1}{\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}}
. We set the dual variables as
M=N=\frac{1}{2^{n}}I,K=qO
, where
n
is the number of qubits and
q\equiv-\frac{1}{2^{n}(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}})}
. We will show the variables
\{M,N,K\}
is a feasible solution to the dual problem.

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{i}A_{o}}[(K^{T}_{A_{i}}\otimes J^{T}_{{\cal N}_{A_{o}A^{\prime}_{i}}}\otimes O_{A^{\prime}_{o}})(J_{{\operatorname{id}}_{A_{i}A_{o}}}\otimes I_{A^{\prime}_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}})]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{i}A_{o}}\left[\left(qO^{T}\otimes\sum_{i,j}|j\rangle\langle i|\otimes\sum_{\sigma}p_{\sigma}\sigma^{T}|j\rangle\langle i|\sigma^{T}\otimes O\right)\left(\sum_{m,n}|m\rangle\langle n|\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|\otimes I\otimes I\right)\right]

(S98)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{i}A_{o}}\left[\sum_{i,j,m,n}\sum_{\sigma}qO^{T}|m\rangle\langle n|\otimes|j\rangle\langle i|m\rangle\langle n|\otimes p_{\sigma}\sigma^{T}|j\rangle\langle i|\sigma^{T}\otimes O\right]

(S99)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,n}\sum_{\sigma}qp_{\sigma}\operatorname{Tr}[O^{T}|i\rangle\langle n|\otimes|j\rangle\langle n|]\sigma^{T}|j\rangle\langle i|\sigma^{T}\otimes O

(S100)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,n}\sum_{\sigma}qp_{\sigma}\operatorname{Tr}[O^{T}|i\rangle\langle n|]\operatorname{Tr}[|j\rangle\langle n|]\sigma^{T}|j\rangle\langle i|\sigma^{T}\otimes O

(S101)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\sum_{\sigma}qp_{\sigma}O^{T}_{ji}\sigma^{T}|j\rangle\langle i|\sigma^{T}\otimes O

(S102)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}qp_{\sigma}\sigma^{T}O^{T}\sigma^{T}\otimes O

(S103)

\displaystyle=q\left(\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}O^{T}\otimes O-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}O^{T}\otimes O\right)

(S104)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2^{n}}O^{T}\otimes O,

(S105)

where
O^{T}_{ji}\equiv\langle j|O^{T}|i\rangle
.
This implies that

\displaystyle M_{A^{\prime}_{i}}\otimes I_{A^{\prime}_{o}}+\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{i}A_{o}}[(K_{A_{i}}^{T}\otimes J_{{\cal N}_{A_{o}A^{\prime}_{i}}}^{T}\otimes O_{A^{\prime}_{o}})(J_{{\operatorname{id}}_{A_{i}A_{o}}}\otimes I_{A^{\prime}_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}I\otimes I-\frac{1}{2^{n}}O^{T}\otimes O\geq 0,

(S106)

\displaystyle N_{A^{\prime}_{i}}\otimes I_{A^{\prime}_{o}}-\operatorname{Tr}_{A_{i}A_{o}}[(K_{A_{i}}^{T}\otimes J_{{\cal N}_{A_{o}A^{\prime}_{i}}}^{T}\otimes O_{A^{\prime}_{o}})(J_{{\operatorname{id}}_{A_{i}A_{o}}}\otimes I_{A^{\prime}_{i}A^{\prime}_{o}})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}I\otimes I+\frac{1}{2^{n}}O^{T}\otimes O\geq 0,

(S107)

which means that
\{M,N,K\}
is a feasible solution to the dual SDP (
S18
). Therefore, we have
\gamma_{O}({\cal N})\geq-\operatorname{Tr}[KO]=\frac{1}{\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}}
. Combining this with the primal part, we conclude that
\gamma_{O}({\cal N})=\frac{1}{\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}}
.

From the proof, we also know that the above retriever
{\cal D}
is optimal.
Moreover, from the given Choi matrix, one could derive that the corresponding Kraus operators of
{\cal D}_{1}
and
{\cal D}_{2}
are

E_{{\cal D}_{1}}=\left\{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{2n-1}}}E_{i}\middle|E_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n},E_{i}O=OE_{i}\right\}

E_{{\cal D}_{2}}=\left\{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{2n-1}}}E_{i}\middle|E_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n},E_{i}O=-OE_{i}\right\}.

Note that the depolarizing channel is a special case of the mixed Pauli channels, where all noise coefficients are the same. For the single-qubit case, the depolarizing channel is
{\cal N}_{\rm depo}(\rho)=(1-\epsilon)\rho+\epsilon\frac{I}{2}
, which is equivalent to Eq. (
S81
) by setting
p_{x}=p_{y}=p_{z}=\frac{\epsilon}{4}
and
p_{i}=1-p_{x}-p_{y}-p_{z}=1-\frac{3\epsilon}{4}
. The
n
-qubit depolarizing channel is
{\cal N}_{n-{\rm depo}}(\rho)=(1-\epsilon)\rho+\epsilon\frac{I}{2^{n}}
, by setting the parameters in Eq. (
S82
) as
p_{\sigma}=\frac{\epsilon}{4^{n}}
and
p_{I}=1-\sum_{\sigma}p_{\sigma}=1-\epsilon\frac{4^{n}-1}{4^{n}}
, where
\{\sigma\}
are the
n
-qubit Pauli operators excluding
I^{\otimes n}
, and
\{p_{\sigma}\}
are the corresponding probabilities. It is obvious that
I^{\otimes n}
commutes with any observable, i.e.,
I\in\sigma^{+}
. Since the rest probabilities are identical, we have
\sum_{\sigma^{+}}p_{\sigma^{+}}-\sum_{\sigma^{-}}p_{\sigma^{-}}=1-\epsilon
, which means that the retrieving cost for depolarizing channel is
\gamma_{O}({\cal N}_{n-{\rm depo}})=\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}
for
O
being an
n
-qubit Pauli operator.
