Abstract
. In this work, we study the solvability of a boundary value problem for the magneto-hydrostatic equations originally proposed by Grad and Rubin in [ 16 ] . The proof relies on a fixed point argument which combines the so-called current transport method together with Hölder estimates for a class of non-convolution singular integral operators. The same method allows to solve an analogous boundary value problem for the steady incompressible Euler equations.



1.
Introduction and prior results

In this paper we consider some boundary value problem for the two dimensional magneto-hydrostatic equation
(MHS)
given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}j\times B&=\nabla p,\quad\mbox{in }\Omega\\
\nabla\times B&=j,\quad\mbox{in }\Omega\\
\nabla\cdot B&=0,\quad\mbox{in }\Omega\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

where
B
denotes the magnetic field,
j=\nabla\times B
the current density and
p
the fluid pressure on a suitable two dimensional manifold
\Omega
. The MHS equations are a particular case of the ideal steady magneto-hydrodynamics equations with trivial fluid flow
v=0
. Magneto-hydrostatics is relevant in a wide variety of problems in astrophysical plasmas describing coronal field structures and stellar winds as well as in the study of plasma confinement fusion, (cf.
[
14
,
15
,
22
]
). Using the vector identity
j\times B=(\nabla\times B)\times B=B\cdot\nabla B-\frac{1}{2}\nabla(\left|B\right|^{2})
and defining the magnetic pressure or total pressure
p_{m}=p+\frac{1}{2}\left|B\right|^{2}
, equations (
1.1
) recast into

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}B\cdot\nabla B&=\nabla p_{m},\quad\mbox{in }\Omega\\
\nabla\cdot B&=0,\quad\mbox{in }\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

Using the appropriate identification of variables, equations (
1.2
) are equivalent to the well-known equations of steady incompressible Euler equations, namely,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}v\cdot\nabla v&=-\nabla p,\quad\mbox{in }\Omega\\
\nabla\cdot v&=0,\quad\mbox{in }\Omega\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

where
v:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the velocity fluid vector field and
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the fluid pressure. Indeed, a quick inspections shows that (
1.2
) is equivalent to (
1.3
) using the transformations of variables
v\leftrightarrow B
and
-p\leftrightarrow p_{m}
.

In this paper we are interested in studying some specific boundary value problems for (
1.1
) where information about the magnetic field
B
is given in different parts of the boundaries. Hereafter we will describe in detail the boundary value conditions into consideration for the case of the MHS equations (
1.1
). Since from the mathematical point of view systems (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are identical, a similar analysis and results can be shown for the steady Euler equations (
1.3
). Nevertheless a specific boundary value problem for one of the equations might not be physically relevant for the other and vice-versa.

Let
\Omega
be a two dimensional orientable manifold with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. We will denote by
n
the outer normal to the boundary and assume that the normal component of the magnetic field
B\cdot n
is given. We now decompose the boundary
\partial\Omega=\partial\Omega_{+}\cup\partial\Omega_{-}
where

\partial\Omega_{+}=\{\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega:(n\cdot B)(\bm{x})\geq 0\}\mbox{ and }\partial\Omega_{-}=\{\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega:(n\cdot B)(\bm{x})\leq 0\}.

The boundary problem which we treat in this paper consists in prescribing in addition to the normal component
B\cdot n
on
\partial\Omega
, the tangential component
B\cdot\tau
in one part of the boundary, namely on
\partial\Omega_{-}
. Here and in the following we denote by
\tau
a unit vector tangent to the boundary. This boundary value problem was introduced in the seminal paper of Grad and Rubin
[
16
]
. To the best of our knowledge, the well-posedness of this boundary value problem remains open even in the two-dimensional case. Furthermore, in
[
16
]
the authors also suggested different boundary value problems for the MHS equations in two dimensional and three dimensional cases. A relevant feature of the solutions constructed in this article is that the current
j
is different from zero for generic choices of the boundary values. For the construction of zero current density solutions, i.e.
j=0
, it is well-known that system (
1.1
) reduces to the study of the Laplace equation where the theory of harmonic functions can be applied to study the existence of solutions.

In this work, we will restrict ourselves to a very particular geometric setting, namely we will assume that

\Omega=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,L],

(1.4)

with
L>0.
The reason to choose this manifold is the following: for
\Omega
as in (
1.4
) we can choose the values of
B\cdot n
in such a way that
\partial\Omega_{+}\cap\partial\Omega_{-}=\emptyset
and in particular we can guarantee that
B\cdot n\neq 0
at all points
\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega
. As it has been discussed in
[
2
]
at the points of the set
\partial\Omega_{+}\cap\partial\Omega_{-}
some singular behaviour for
B
arise for generic domains
\Omega
. In order to avoid the technical difficulties that should be considered in that situation, we will just work on the particular manifold (
1.4
).

It is worth to notice that several boundary value problems for the steady Euler or MHS equations have been considered in the literature
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
20
,
25
,
26
]
. We refer the interested reader to
[
2
]
for a thorough description of the currently available results considering the well-posedness of the different boundary value problems for the steady Euler or MHS equations.

In order to solve boundary value problems for both equations, two main methods have been considered in the literature: the Grad-Shafranov method
[
17
,
24
]
and the vorticity transport method introduced by Alber
[
1
]
. The former is restricted to two dimensional settings or to problems with particular symmetries, for instance axisymmetric or toroidal symmetries. The main idea behind the Grad-Shafranov method relies on reducing the steady Euler or the MHS equations to an elliptic equation where large number of techniques are available. See for instance,
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
18
]
for ideas closely related to the Grad-Shafranov approach that have been recently applied to derive properties solutions of the steady Euler equation and MHS equation.

A different approach to obtain solutions with non-vanishing vorticity (since it was originally applied for the steady Euler equation) was introduced by Alber
[
1
]
. Roughly speaking, he constructed solutions where the velocity field
v
can be split into
v=v_{0}+V
where
v_{0}
is an irrotational solution to (
1.3
) and
V
a small perturbation. The boundary value problem for the Euler equations is reduced to a fixed point problem for a function
V
combining the fact that the vorticity satisfies a suitable transport equation and that the velocity can be recovered from the vorticity using the Biot-Savart law. This idea will be discussed later in more detail. In particular, we will explain why Alber’s method cannot be directly applied to solve the boundary value problem we are interested in and more importantly what are the new key tools we implement to address the problem.

\left[f\right]_{\alpha,\Omega}:=\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{x}\neq\bm{y};\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\overline{\Omega}}\frac{\left|f(\bm{x})-f(\bm{y})\right|}{\left|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right|^{\alpha}}

1.2.
Main result

The main result in this article deals with the well-posedness of a boundary value problem for the MHS equations suggested by Grad-Rubin in
[
16
]
. Specifically we prescribe the normal component
B\cdot n
on
\partial\Omega
and the tangential component
B\cdot\tau
in one part of the boundary, namely on
\partial\Omega_{-}
. In particular, our result reads as follows

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,L]\}
, with
L>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. There exists
M=M(\alpha,L)>0
sufficiently small such that for

f\in C^{{2,\alpha}}(\partial\Omega)
and
g\in C^{{2,\alpha}}(\partial\Omega_{-})
satisfying

\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{C^{{2,\alpha}}(\partial\Omega_{-})}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C^{{2,\alpha}}(\partial\Omega)}\leq M,

(1.5)

and

\int_{\partial\Omega_{-}}f\ dx=\int_{\partial\Omega_{+}}f\ dx,

(1.6)

there exists a unique
(B,p)\in C^{{2,\alpha}}(\Omega)\times C^{{2,\alpha}}(\Omega)
with
B=(B_{1},B_{2})
to (
1.1
) with

\left\lVert B-(0,1)\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)}\leq M

such that

B\cdot n=1+f\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega\mbox{ and }\ B\cdot\tau=g\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{-}.

(1.7)

Remark 1.2
.

Notice that the solutions
(B,p)
are obtained as small perturbations around the particular vertical constant magnetic field
B_{0}=(0,1)
. The constant magnetic fields of the form
B_{0}=(0,a)
for
a>0
can be reduced by a re-scaling argument to the unitary magnetic field
B_{0}=(0,1)
. On the other hand, it is not a priori clear if it is possible to perturb around more general non-constant magnetic fields.

Remark 1.3
.

A question that could be interesting to explore is whether one can generalize Theorem
1.1
to more general domains
\Omega=\{(x_{1},x_{2}):\gamma_{1}(x_{1})<x_{2}<\gamma_{2}(x_{1})\}

where
\gamma_{j}
are smooth functions satisfying the periodicity condition
\gamma_{j}(x_{1}+2\pi)=\gamma_{j}(x_{1})
for
j=1,2
. In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, several computations which can be made in a explicit manner in the case of the domain
\Omega=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,L]
, will become more involved for more general domains.

Remark 1.4
.

In the three dimensional setting
\Omega=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,L]
, we believe that the same ideas developed in this paper can be carried out, although the computations are more involved. In particular, we will need to derive Hölder estimates for non-convolution singular integral operators that in the three dimensional case are more delicate.

Notice that using the change of variables
B\leftrightarrow v
and
p_{m}\leftrightarrow-p
the following result can be obtained for the steady Euler equations

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\Omega=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,L]\}
, with
L>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. There exists
M=M(\alpha,L)>0
sufficiently small such that for

f\in C^{{2,\alpha}}(\partial\Omega)
and
g\in C^{{2,\alpha}}(\partial\Omega_{-})
satisfying

\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{C^{{2,\alpha}}(\partial\Omega_{-})}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C^{{2,\alpha}}(\partial\Omega)}\leq M,

and

\int_{\partial\Omega_{-}}f\ dx=\int_{\partial\Omega_{+}}f\ dx,

there exists a unique
(v,p)\in C^{{2,\alpha}}(\Omega)\times C^{{2,\alpha}}(\Omega)
with
v=(v_{1},v_{2})
to (
1.3
) with

\left\lVert v-(0,1)\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)}\leq M

such that

v\cdot n=1+f\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega\mbox{ and }\ v\cdot\tau=g\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{-}.

1.3.
Strategy behind the proof and novelties

The strategy of the proof is based on two ingredients, namely the transport equation for the current and the div-curl problem that recovers the magnetic field in terms of the current. Suppose that we have a magnetic field with the form
(B_{1},B_{2})=(0,1)+b
where
b
is a small perturbation of the vertical base magnetic field.

For magnetic fields for which the magnetic vector is contained always in a given plane, the current
j
is a vector in the direction of the normal to the plane. However, in these two dimensional settings it is more convenient to assume that the current is a scalar quantity and therefore we will use the notation
j=\nabla\times B=-\partial_{y}B_{1}+\partial_{x}B_{2}
.

It is well-known that if
B
solves (
1.1
), the current density
j
solves the following transport equation

B\cdot\nabla j=((0,1)+b)\cdot\nabla j=0,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega.

(1.8)

On the other hand, assuming that we have a current
j
we can recover the corresponding magnetic field
B
solving the following system of equations

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\nabla\times B=\nabla\times b=j,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\mbox{div }B=\mbox{div }b=0,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\end{array}\right.

(1.9)

The equations (
1.8
)-(
1.9
) must be solved under suitable boundary value conditions. It turns out that given the function
j
we can obtain a unique solution
B
to (
1.9
) if we prescribe the normal component of the magnetic field on the two connected components of the boundary of
\Omega

\displaystyle B\cdot n=f,\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega

(1.10)

as well as the horizontal flux for the magnetic field

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}B_{1}(0,y)\ dy=J.

(1.11)

We will see later, that the value of
J
has to be chosen in a very specific way to obtain a uni-valued pressure
p
on
\Omega
.

On the other hand, if we assume that
b
is sufficiently small (in a sense to be precise later), the current
j
is uniquely determined in
\Omega
if we prescribe it in any of the two connected components of
\partial\Omega
. For instance, if

j(x,0)=j_{0}(x),\ \mbox{on }\partial\Omega_{-}

(1.12)

is given, we can obtain
j
in
\Omega
just by using the method of characteristics. Notice however that the boundary conditions for the problem (
1.1
)-(
1.7
) do not allow to compute the value of
j_{0}
in (
1.12
).

On the other hand, we have an additional boundary condition that yields the tangential component of the magnetic field

B\cdot\tau=g\mbox{ on }\ \partial\Omega_{-}.

(1.13)

The structure of the problem suggests to use a fixed point argument in order to construct the solution. More precisely, given a vector field
B
defined in
\Omega
as a well as a function
j_{0}
on
\partial\Omega_{-}
we can solve (
1.8
) with the boundary condition (
1.12
) to construct a current field
j[B;j_{0}](\cdot)
defined in
\Omega
. Using this current function we can solve (
1.9
) with boundary conditions (
1.10
) and (
1.11
) to find a new vector field
\widetilde{B}[B;j_{0}](\cdot)
in
\Omega
. Notice that the new vector field
\widetilde{B}
does not satisfy in general the boundary condition (
1.13
). However, this equation can be reformulated as

\widetilde{B}[B;j_{0}]\cdot\tau=g\ \mbox{ on }\partial\Omega_{-},

(1.14)

that turns out to be an integral equation for the function
j_{0}
on
\partial\Omega_{-}
. We can prove that this integral equation can be solved by means a fixed point argument using regularity estimates for non-convolution singular integral operators in Hölder spaces. The solution of this equation yields an operator
B\to j_{0}[B]
. Notice that this operator depends also on the boundary value conditions
f,g
, but we will not write this dependence explicitly. We can now define an operator
B\to\Gamma[B]=\widetilde{B}[B,j_{0}[B]]
. A fixed point argument for the operator
\Gamma(\cdot)
solves the problem (
1.8
), (
1.9
), (
1.10
), (
1.11
) and (
1.13
). Using now the fact that
\nabla\times(j\times B)=B\cdot\nabla j=0
one can show, arguing as in
[
2
]
, that there exists a pressure function
p
such that
(B,p)
satisfies (
1.1
) and (
1.7
).

It is worth to notice that there are several important differences regarding the problem treated here and previous works
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
20
,
25
,
26
]
. For a more detailed description of the different boundary value problems mentioned previously, we refer the reader to
[
2
]
. In the case treated by Alber
[
1
]
for the steady incompressible Euler equation, the vorticity
\omega_{0}
(or current
j_{0}
in our case) on
\partial\Omega_{-}
can be readily obtained from the boundary values given in the problem, so roughly speaking
\omega_{0}
(or
j_{0}
) on
\partial\Omega_{-}
is already prescribed. On the other hand, this is not the case for the boundary value type problems solved in
[
2
]
where the vorticity
\omega_{0}
(or current
j_{0}
in our case) is not fully prescribed by the boundary values. Instead in those cases,
\omega_{0}
(or
j_{0}
) is part of the solution. Nevertheless, it can be obtained by means of the fixed point argument. More precisely, the value of
\omega_{0}
(or
j_{0}
) can be computed using the Euler equation (
1.3
) and is given in terms of
v
(or
B
), its derivative and the boundary value conditions. Using the characteristics one can solve the transport equation (
1.8
) to construct
\omega[v;\omega_{0}](\cdot)
(or
j[B;j_{0}](\cdot)
) and then equation (
1.9
) to construct the new velocity field
\widetilde{v}[v;\omega_{0}](\cdot)
or magnetic field
\widetilde{B}[B;j_{0}](\cdot)
. The crucial point is that the new velocity field or magnetic field already satisfies the required boundary value conditions, since
\omega_{0}
or
j_{0}
has been chosen in terms of the boundary conditions and
v
or
B
in a precise way.

To deal with the boundary value conditions imposed in (
1.7
), we have to use a more sophisticated argument to compute the value of
j_{0}
on
\partial\Omega_{-}
. As we have explained above, this reduces to study an integral equation containing singular integral operators. To show the existence and uniqueness of the integral equation, we derive some general results providing Hölder estimates for a class of non-convolution singular integral operators which are of independent interest (cf. Section
4
). The use of Hölder spaces instead of Sobolev spaces (as in
[
1
]
for instance) is an important detail. Indeed, the value
j_{0}
at the boundary
\partial\Omega_{-}
depends on the value of
B
(and the boundary data) and therefore, if the estimates for
B
are given in terms of Sobolev spaces, we obtain less regularity for
j_{0}
due to the classical regularity trace theorem. Once
j_{0}
is obtained we can compute
j
along
\Omega
using the transport equation (
1.8
) which does not improve the regularity due its hyperbolic character. Therefore, the new function
B
computed via the div-curl problem (
1.9
) has a loss of regularity which prevents to close a fixed point argument. This obstructions can be avoided by making use of Hölder spaces.

2.
The linearized problem

In this section, we will describe the formal idea behind the method to construct solutions
(B,p)
to (
1.1
) satisfying the boundary value conditions (
1.7
). As we have mentioned in the introduction, the proof is based on defining an adequate operator
\Gamma
on a subspace of
C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
which has a fixed point
b
such that
B=(0,1)+b
is a solution to (
1.1
) and (
1.7
). We define the operator
\Gamma:B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))\to C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
in two steps. First, given
b\in B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))
we define
j\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)
solving the following the transport type problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}((0,1)+b)\cdot\nabla j=0,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
j=j_{0},\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{-}\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

where
j_{0}
is a priori an unknown quantity. As a second step, we define
W\in C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
as the unique solution to the div-curl problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\nabla\times W=j,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\mbox{div }W=0,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
W\cdot n=f,\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega\\
W\cdot\tau=g,\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{-}\\
\int_{0}^{L}W_{1}(0,y)\ dy=J.\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

Thus we define
\Gamma(b,J)=W
. We remark that
J

is a degree of freedom of the problem, since there exists non trivial solutions
(W,J)
of the homogeneous problem (
2.2
) with
f=g=0
given by

W=(\frac{2Jy}{L^{2}},0)\mbox{ and }j=-\frac{2J}{L^{2}}.

(2.3)

This degree of freedom will be used later to obtain a uni-valued function pressure
p
in
\Omega
.

We are interested in obtaining solutions of the form
B=(0,1)+b
where
b=(b_{1},b_{2})
is a small perturbation, i.e.
\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)}\leq M
with
M\leq M_{0}
and
M_{0}
sufficiently small. Therefore, in the lowest order (dropping the
small
nonlinear terms of order
M^{2}
), the transport equation
(
2.1
) reduces to

\partial_{y}j(x,y)=0,\mbox{ in }\Omega

(2.4)

and hence
j(x,y)=j_{0}(x)
. Then, with this approximation, the div-curl problem (
2.2
) becomes

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\nabla\times W=j_{0}(x),\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\mbox{div }W=0,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
W\cdot n=f,\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega\\
W\cdot\tau=g,\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{-}\\
\int_{0}^{L}W_{1}(0,y)\ dy=J.\end{array}\right.

(2.5)

Notice that (
2.5
) is a non-homogeneous linear problem for
W
.
To solve (
2.5
), we examine the following auxiliary problem (cf.
[
2
, §3.1.1]
), namely

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Delta\psi=j_{0}(x),\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\psi(x,L)=-J+h^{+}(x),\ x\in\mathbb{R}\\
\psi(x,0)=h^{-}(x),\ x\in\mathbb{R}\\
\partial_{y}\psi(x,0)=-g,\ x\in\mathbb{R}\end{array}\right.

(2.6)

where

h^{+}(x)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}(f(\xi,L)-A)\ d\xi,\quad h^{-}(x)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}(f(\xi,0)-A)\ d\xi

(2.7)

and

A=\int_{\partial\Omega_{+}}f\ dS=\int_{\partial\Omega_{-}}f\ dS.

(2.8)

For a sufficiently smooth stream function
\psi
, the function
W=(0,A)+\nabla^{\perp}\psi
, where
\nabla^{\perp}\psi=(-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y},\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x})
, solves (
2.5
). However, for any fixed
j_{0}(x)
the problem (
2.6
) is over-determined. This fact will be used in order to obtain the a priori unknown function
j_{0}(x)
.

In order to obtain a complete linearized version of the problem (
1.1
) satisfying boundary conditions (
1.7
), it remains to add a condition that guarantees that the pressure is a uni-valued function on
\Omega
. Indeed, a linearized version of (
1.1
)
with
B=(0,1)+b
(with
b
small) is given by

\displaystyle-j(x,y)=\partial_{x}p,\quad 0=\partial_{y}p.

(2.9)

A necessary condition for the solvability of this problem is that
\partial_{y}j(x,y)=0
and hence
j(x,y)=j_{0}(x)
, similar as the condition derived in (
2.4
). Therefore, (
2.9
) reduces to

\displaystyle-j_{0}(x)=\partial_{x}p,\quad 0=\partial_{y}p.

(2.10)

Then, we can obtain a solution to (
2.10
) given by

p(x,y)=\int_{\bm{0}}^{\bm{x}}j_{0}(x)\ dx

(2.11)

where the integral on the right hand side is the line integration computed along any contour connecting
\bm{0}=(0,0)
and
\bm{x}\in\Omega
. Notice that a necessary and sufficient condition to ensure that
p(x,y)
is a uni-valued function in
\Omega
is that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}j_{0}(x)\ dx=0.

(2.12)

To this end, we apply the Fourier transform in the
x
variable to equation (
2.6
). This transforms the PDE (
2.6
) into the following second-order non-homogeneous ODEs with constant coefficients

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-n^{2}\widehat{\psi}(n,y)+\partial_{yy}\widehat{\psi}(n,y)=\widehat{j_{0}}(n),\quad(n,y)\in\mathbb{Z}\times(0,L)\\
\widehat{\psi}(n,L)=-J\delta_{0,n}+\widehat{h^{+}}(n),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\widehat{\psi}(n,0)=\widehat{h^{-}}(n),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\partial_{y}\widehat{\psi}(n,0)=-\widehat{g}(n),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.\end{array}\right.

(2.13)

Above,
\widehat{h^{+}}(n),\widehat{h^{-}}(n)
are the Fourier coefficients associated with the function
h^{+},h^{-}
respectively, and
\widehat{g}(n)
the Fourier coefficients of the function
g
.

After a straightforward calculation using variation of parameters method we find that

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}(n,y)

\displaystyle=-J\frac{y}{L}\delta_{0,n}+\widehat{h^{+}}(n)\frac{\sinh(\left|n\right|y)}{\sinh(\left|n\right|L)}+\widehat{h^{-}}(n)\frac{\sinh(\left|n\right|(L-y))}{\sinh(\left|n\right|L)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\widehat{j_{0}}(n)\frac{\sinh(\left|n\right|(L-y))-\sinh(\left|n\right|L)+\sinh(\left|n\right|y)}{\left|n\right|^{2}\sinh(\left|n\right|L)},

(2.14)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In the case
n=0
, the functions multiplying
\widehat{h^{+}}(n),\widehat{h^{-}}(n)
and
\widehat{j_{0}}(n)
must be understood as the limit when
n
tends to zero. More precisely, for
n=0
, we use the replacements

\frac{\sinh(\left|n\right|y)}{\sinh(\left|n\right|L)}\longmapsto\frac{y}{L},\quad\frac{\sinh(\left|n\right|(L-y))}{\sinh(\left|n\right|L)}\longmapsto\frac{L-y}{2},

and

\frac{\sinh(\left|n\right|(L-y))-\sinh(\left|n\right|L)+\sinh(\left|n\right|y)}{\left|n\right|^{2}\sinh(\left|n\right|L)}\longmapsto\frac{L^{2}y}{2}\left(\frac{y}{L}-1\right).

This convention of understanding several combinations of trigonometric hyperbolic functions when
n=0
as the limit when
n
tends to zero will be used throughout the paper. Imposing the last boundary condition
\partial_{y}\widehat{\psi}(n,0)=-\widehat{g}(n)
in (
2.13
), we find that

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\widehat{\psi}(n,0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{J}{L}\delta_{0,n}+\widehat{h^{+}}(n)\frac{\sinh(\left|n\right|y)}{\sinh(\left|n\right|L)}+\widehat{h^{-}}(n)\frac{\sinh(\left|n\right|(L-y))}{\sinh(\left|n\right|L)}+\widehat{j_{0}}(n)\frac{1-\cosh(\left|n\right|L)}{\left|n\right|\sinh(\left|n\right|L)}=-\widehat{g}(n).

Taking the inverse Fourier transform in the first variable we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\psi(x,0)=-\frac{J}{L}+\mathcal{Z}(x)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{1-\cosh(\left|n\right|L)}{\left|n\right|\sinh(\left|n\right|L)}e^{in(x-\eta)}j_{0}(\eta)\ d\eta=-g(x)

(2.15)

with

\mathcal{Z}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\left(\widehat{h^{+}}(n)\frac{\left|n\right|}{\sinh(\left|n\right|L)}-\widehat{h^{-}}(n)\frac{\left|n\right|}{\tanh(\left|n\right|L)}\right)e^{inx}.

(2.16)

Using the symmetry in
n
and denoting the kernel

\mathcal{G}^{L}(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{\cosh(nL)-1}{n\sinh(nL)}e^{inx}

(2.17)

and

\widetilde{g}(x)=-g(x)-\mathcal{Z}(x),

(2.18)

we have that (
2.15
) can be expressed as the following convolution equation for
j_{0}
,

\mathcal{T}^{L}j_{0}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{G}^{L}(x-\eta)j_{0}(\eta)d\eta=\widetilde{g}(x)+\frac{J}{L}.

(2.19)

Notice that the function
\widetilde{g}
depends only on the boundary values
g
and
f
. Using the fact that the Fourier coefficients in (
2.17
) are different than zero, we can use standard Fourier techniques to invert the operator yielding

j_{0}(x)=(\mathcal{T}^{L})^{-1}\widetilde{g}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}^{L}}(x-\eta)\widetilde{g}(\eta)\ d\eta+\frac{2J}{L^{2}}

(2.20)

where the kernel function
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}^{L}}(x)
can be explicitly computed as

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}^{L}}(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{n\sinh(nL)}{\cosh(nL)-1}e^{inx}.

The value of
J
that until now is undetermined is obtained by means of the previous derived formula (
2.12
). Using (
2.20
) we find that

J=\frac{L}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\widetilde{g}(\eta)d\eta=-L\widehat{g}(0)-(\widehat{h}^{+}(0)-\widehat{h}^{-}(0)).

(2.21)

Once we have obtained the value of
j_{0}
and
J
, we can use formula (
2
) which combined with Fourier inverse formula yields
\psi(x,y)

and hence
W
since
W=(0,A)+\nabla^{\perp}\psi
.

In the following sections we will show how to solve the full non-linear problem (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and (
1.7
) by using a perturbative argument with respect to the linear problem.

3.
The non-linear problem: an integral equation for the current

In this section we will derive an integral equation for the current
j
on
\partial\Omega_{-}
, namely
j_{0}=j(x,0)
,
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. As expected, this integral equation will be a perturbation of equation (
2.20
) that we have obtained for the linearized problem. The solution of this equation will give
j_{0}(x)
in terms of the perturbation magnetic field
b
and the boundary values
f
and
g
. In the following subsection, using a formal argument that assumes the convergence of some Fourier series, we show how to arrive to an integral equation for
j_{0}(x)
. We will not consider in detail the convergence of the Fourier series and the precise definitions of the operators that appeared in this section will be given later (cf. Subsection
3.3
).

3.1.
The formal argument using Fourier series

Proceeding as in the Section
2
, we define the operator
\Gamma:B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))\to C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
using two building blocks: a transport type problem and a div-curl problem. Given
b\in B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))
we define
j\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)
as the solution to the transport type problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}((0,1)+b)\cdot\nabla j=0,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
j=j_{0},\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{-}\end{array}\right.

(3.1)

where
j_{0}
is a priori an unknown quantity. As a second step, we define
W\in C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
as the unique solution to the following div-curl problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\nabla\times W=j,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\mbox{div }W=0,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
W\cdot n=f,\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega\\
W\cdot\tau=g,\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{-}\\
\int_{0}^{L}W_{1}(0,y)\ dy=J.\end{array}\right.

(3.2)

Then, we define
\Gamma(b)=W
.
By the theory of transport equations (cf.
[
2
, Proposition 3.8]
), it is well-known that system (
3.1
) can be solved by using the integral curves of the vector field
B=(0,1)+b
. More precisely, the explicit solution to (
3.1
) is given

j(x,y)=j_{0}(X^{-1}(x,y))

(3.3)

where
X^{-1}
is the inverse of the mapping
\xi\to X(\xi,y)
solving the ordinary differential equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\partial_{y}X(\xi,y)=\frac{b_{1}(X(\xi,y),y)}{1+b_{2}(X(\xi,y),y)}\\
X(\xi,0)=\xi.\end{array}\right.

(3.4)

Arguing as in (
2.6
) in Section
2
using the stream function
\psi
, the div-curl problem (
3.2
) becomes

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Delta\psi=j_{0}(X^{-1}(x,y)),\ \mbox{in }\Omega\\
\psi(x,L)=-J+h^{+}(x),\ x\in\mathbb{R}\\
\psi(x,0)=h^{-}(x),\ x\in\mathbb{R}\\
\partial_{y}\psi(x,0)=-g(x),\ x\in\mathbb{R}\end{array}\right.

(3.5)

where we recall that
h^{+}(x),h^{-}(x)
and
A
are given in (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) respectively. To solve (
3.5
) we do not use variation of parameters but compute directly the fundamental solution
\Phi(x,y,y_{0})
solving the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Delta\Phi(x,y,y_{0})=\delta(x)\delta(y-y_{0}),\ \mbox{in }\Omega\\
\Phi=0,\ \mbox{on }\partial\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.

(3.6)

Using Fourier transform and imposing the continuity jump conditions we infer that

\Phi(x,y,y_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y_{0}))\sinh(ny)}{n\sinh(nL)}e^{inx},\ \mbox{for }y<y_{0},\\
-\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y))\sinh(ny_{0})}{n\sinh(nL)}e^{inx},\ \mbox{for }y>y_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(3.7)

Moreover, the normal derivative at
y=0
is given by

\partial_{y}\Phi(x,0,y_{0})=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y_{0}))}{\sinh(nL)}e^{inx}.

Computing an homogeneous solution and imposing the boundary value conditions

\psi(x,L)=-J+h^{+}(x),\quad\psi(x,0)=h^{-}(x)\mbox{ and }\partial_{y}\psi(x,0)=-g(x),

we conclude (similarly as in Section
2
) that

\partial_{y}\psi(x,0)=-\frac{J}{L}+\mathcal{Z}(x)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{L}dy_{0}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y_{0}))}{\sinh(nL)}e^{in(x-\xi)}j_{0}(X^{-1}(\xi,y_{0}))\ d\xi

(3.8)

where
\mathcal{Z}(x)
is defined in (
2.16
). Therefore we have that for
\widetilde{g}
as in (
2.18
) we can write (
3.8
) as the following integral equation for
j_{0}

\mathcal{T}^{NL}j_{0}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{L}dy_{0}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y_{0}))}{\sinh(nL)}e^{in(x-\xi)}j_{0}(X^{-1}(\xi,y_{0}))\ d\xi=\widetilde{g}(x)+\frac{J}{L}.

(3.9)

Notice that the operator in (
3.9
) reduces to (
2.19
) in the particular case where
X^{-1}(\xi,y_{0})=\xi
, which corresponds to the linearized case considered in Section
2
. From now on, in integral expressions like (
3.9
) which results in functions depending only on
x
we replace the integration variable
y_{0}
for
y
for the sake of simplicity. We rewrite the operator equation (
3.9
) into a more convenient form. To that purpose, we define

\Theta(\xi,y)=X^{-1}(\xi,y)-\xi

(3.10)

and plugging (
3.10
) in (
3.9
) we infer that the operator
\mathcal{T}^{NL}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{NL}j_{0}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{L}dy\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y))}{\sinh(nL)}e^{in(x-\xi)}j_{0}(\xi+\Theta(\xi,y))\ d\xi.

(3.11)

Using the following changes of variables

X^{-1}(\xi,y)=\xi+\Theta(\xi,y)=\eta,\quad d\xi=\frac{d\eta}{(1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))},

(3.12)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{NL}j_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{L}dy\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y))}{\sinh(nL)}e^{in(x-X(\eta,y))}j_{0}(\eta)\frac{1}{(1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\ d\eta.

(3.13)

Defining,
\Lambda(\eta,y)=X(\eta,y)-\eta
we notice that the operator (
3.13
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{NL}j_{0}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{G}^{NL}(x-\eta,\eta)j_{0}(\eta)\ d\eta.

(3.14)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{NL}(x,\eta)=\ \displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}a_{n}(\eta)e^{inx}

(3.15)

with

a_{n}(\eta)=\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y))}{\sinh(nL)}\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}}{(1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}dy.

(3.16)

Thus the integral equation (
3.9
) for
j_{0}
becomes

\mathcal{T}^{NL}j_{0}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{G}^{NL}(x-\eta,\eta)j_{0}(\eta)\ d\eta=\widetilde{g}(x)+\frac{J}{L}

(3.17)

3.2.
Decomposing the operator
\mathcal{T}^{NL}

In this subsection, we will decompose
the operator
\mathcal{T}^{NL}
defined in (
3.14
)-(
3.16
) into several operators which are more tractable and easier to estimate. In particular, we will split the operator into one main term which is a convolution operator and several remainder terms which are perturbations of convolution operators.

To that purpose we first notice that the coefficients in (
3.16
) can be written as

\displaystyle a_{n}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y))}{\sinh(nL)}dy+\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y))}{\sinh(nL)}\left[\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}-1\right]dy

\displaystyle=a_{n}^{0}+a_{n}^{1}(\eta).

The first term can be easily integrated since it does not depend on
\eta
, giving

a_{n}^{0}=\frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{\cosh(nL)-1}{\sinh(nL)}\right]

(3.18)

and the second term is split as

\displaystyle a_{n}^{1}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y))}{\sinh(nL)}\left[\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}-1}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy-\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\sinh(n(L-y))}{\sinh(nL)}\left[\frac{\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy

\displaystyle=a_{n}^{2}(\eta)+a_{n}^{3}(\eta).

Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\sinh(n(L-y))}{\sinh(nL)}=e^{-\left|n\right|y}-M(n,y)

(3.19)

where

M(n,y)=\frac{e^{-2\left|n\right|L}(e^{\left|n\right|y}-e^{-\left|n\right|y})}{(1-e^{-2\left|n\right|L})}.

(3.20)

By means of this computation, we find that

a_{n}^{2}(\eta)=\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\left|n\right|y}\left[\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}-1}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy+R_{n}^{2}(\eta)

(3.21)

where

R_{n}^{2}(\eta)=\int_{0}^{L}M(n,y)\left[\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}-1}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy.

(3.22)

Similarly, we have that

a_{n}^{3}(\eta)=\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\left|n\right|y}\left[\frac{\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy+R_{n}^{3}(\eta)

(3.23)

where

R_{n}^{3}(\eta)=\int_{0}^{L}M(n,y)\left[\frac{\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy.

(3.24)

Therefore, collecting the expressions (
3.18
)-(
3.24
)

\displaystyle a_{n}(\eta)=a_{n}^{0}+a^{2}_{n}(\eta)+a^{3}_{n}(\eta)+R^{2}_{n}(\eta)+R^{3}_{n}(\eta)

and using the definition of
\mathcal{G}^{NL}(x,\eta)
given in (
3.15
) we can rewrite
\mathcal{G}^{NL}(x,\eta)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{NL}(x,\eta)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\left(a_{n}^{0}+a_{n}^{1}(\eta)\right)e^{inx}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\left(a^{0}_{n}+a^{2}_{n}(\eta)+a^{3}_{n}(\eta)+R^{2}_{n}(\eta)+R^{3}_{n}(\eta)\right)e^{inx}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}^{NL}_{0}(x)+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathcal{G}^{NL}_{i}(x,\eta)

where the main term is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{NL}_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{\cosh(nL)-1}{\sinh(nL)}\right]e^{inx},

(3.25)

and the remainder terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{NL}_{1}(x,\eta)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}e^{inx}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\left|n\right|y}\left[\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}-1}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{NL}_{2}(x,\eta)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}e^{inx}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\left|n\right|y}\left[\frac{\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{NL}_{3}(x,\eta)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}e^{inx}\int_{0}^{L}M(n,y)\left[\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}-1}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{NL}_{4}(x,\eta)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}e^{inx}\int_{0}^{L}M(n,y)\left[\frac{\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy.

Using this decomposition we write the operator
\mathcal{T}^{NL}
in (
3.14
) as

\mathcal{T}^{NL}j_{0}(x)=\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0}j_{0}(x)+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{i}j_{0}(x)

(3.26)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0}j_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{G}^{NL}_{0}(x-\eta)j_{0}(\eta)\ d\eta,

(3.27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{i}j_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{G}^{NL}_{i}(x-\eta,\eta)j_{0}(\eta)d\eta,\quad\mbox{for }i=1,\ldots,4.

(3.28)

Remark 3.1
.

Notice that the main term
\mathcal{G}^{NL}_{0}(x)
does not depend on
\eta
and coincides with the linearized kernel
\mathcal{G}^{L}(x)
in (
2.17
). Therefore,
\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0}
is a convolution operator that can be inverted using Fourier series.

We can formally rewrite the integral equation (
3.17
) for
j_{0}
in the form a second order Fredholm integral equation. Indeed, using the fact that the operator
\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0}
is a convolution that can be inverted using Fourier series, we can write equation (
3.17
) as

j_{0}(x)+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathsf{T}_{i}j_{0}(x)=\mathsf{G}(x)+\frac{2J}{L^{2}}

(3.29)

where

\mathsf{T}_{i}=\big[\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0}\big]^{-1}\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{i},\quad\mbox{for }i=1,\ldots,4\quad\mbox{and }\mathsf{G}=\big[\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0}\big]^{-1}\widetilde{g},

(3.30)

with
\widetilde{g}
defined in (
2.18
). We now argue as in the case of the linearized problem and explain how to choose
J
in order to obtain a uni-valued pressure function
p
on
\Omega
. To this end, we use equation (
3.1
) to construct the pressure
p
by means of the following identity

p(\bm{x})=\int_{\bm{0}}^{\bm{x}}\big[j\times B\big](\bm{y})\cdot d\bm{y}

(3.31)

where the integral on the right hand side is the line integration computed along any curve connecting
\bm{0}=(0,0)
and
\bm{x}\in\Omega
. The function
p
given by (
3.31
) is uni-valued in
\Omega
if and only if

\int_{0}^{2\pi}[j\times B]_{1}(x,0)\ dx=0,

(3.32)

where
(j\times B)_{1}
denotes the first component of the vector
j\times B
. Moreover, we notice that
(j\times B)_{1}(x,0)=-j_{0}(x)(1+b_{2}(x,0))=-j_{0}(x)(1+f^{-}(x))
where
f^{-}=f|_{\partial\Omega_{-}}
. Then, (
3.32
) is equivalent to

\int_{0}^{2\pi}j_{0}(x)(1+f^{-}(x))\ dx=0.

(3.33)

Using (
3.29
) we find that

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}j_{0}(x)dx=\frac{2J}{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathsf{G}(x)dx-\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathsf{T}_{i}j_{0}(x)\ dx.

(3.34)

Combining (
3.33
) and (
3.34
) we obtain that

\frac{2J}{L^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathsf{G}(x)dx+\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathsf{T}_{i}j_{0}(x)\ dx\ dx-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}j_{0}(x)f^{-}(x)\ dx

(3.35)

Plugging (
3.35
) into (
3.29
) and denoting by
\langle h\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(x)\ dx
we have that

j_{0}(x)=-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}(\mathsf{T}_{i}j_{0}(x)-\langle\mathsf{T}_{i}j_{0}\rangle)+\mathsf{G}(x)-\langle\mathsf{G}\rangle-\langle j_{0}f^{-}\rangle.

(3.36)

The problem (
3.36
) is a fixed point type of equation which will be shown to be equivalent to the solution
(B,p)
. Indeed, after solving equation (
3.36
), we can obtain the value of
j(x,y)
in
\Omega
using the transport type problem (
3.1
) and recover the new magnetic field
W
using the div-curl system (
3.2
).

3.3.
A rigorous formulation of the problem

The previous computations in Subsections
3.1
and
3.2
are purely formal, since we did not consider in a rigorous manner the convergence of the Fourier series. In this subsection, we will give a precise meaning of the integral equation (
3.36
) for
j_{0}
.
To this end, we
first give a detailed definition of the operators
\mathsf{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{T}_{4}
in (
3.30
). We defined the operators
\mathsf{T}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{1}j_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathfrak{G}_{1,\epsilon}(x-\eta,\eta)j_{0}(\eta)\ d\eta,

(3.37)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{2}j_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathfrak{G}_{2,\epsilon}(x-\eta,\eta)j_{0}(\eta)\ d\eta,

(3.38)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}_{1,\epsilon}(x,\eta)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{n\sinh(nL)}{(\cosh(nL)-1)}e^{inx}\int_{\epsilon}^{L}e^{-\left|n\right|y}\left[\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}-1}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy,

(3.39)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}_{2,\epsilon}(x,\eta)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{n\sinh(nL)}{(\cosh(nL)-1)}e^{inx}\int_{\epsilon}^{L}e^{-\left|n\right|y}\left[\frac{\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy.

(3.40)

On the other hand, the operators
\mathsf{T}_{3},\mathsf{T}_{4}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{3}j_{0}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathfrak{G}_{3}(x-\eta,\eta)j_{0}(\eta)\ d\eta,

(3.41)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{4}j_{0}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathfrak{G}_{4}(x-\eta,\eta)j_{0}(\eta)\ d\eta,

(3.42)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}_{3}(x,\eta)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{n\sinh(nL)}{(\cosh(nL)-1)}e^{inx}\int_{0}^{L}M(n,y)\left[\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}-1}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy,

(3.43)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}_{4}(x,\eta)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\frac{n\sinh(nL)}{(\cosh(nL)-1)}e^{inx}\int_{0}^{L}M(n,y)\left[\frac{\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y))}\right]dy,

(3.44)

with
M(n,y)
as in (
3.20
). The operators
\mathsf{T}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,4
will act on functions
j_{0}
on some suitable Hölder spaces. The fact that the operators
\mathsf{T}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,4
in this spaces are well-defined operators will be shown in Section
4
. For instance the reason why operators
\mathsf{T}_{3},\mathsf{T}_{4}
are well defined acting on Hölder functions
j_{0}
readily follows from the fact that
\mathfrak{G}_{3}(x,\eta),\mathfrak{G}_{4}(x,\eta)
are
C^{\infty}
in
x
due with the exponential decay of the function
M(n,y)
as
\left|n\right|\to\infty.
To deal with operators
\mathsf{T}_{1},\mathsf{T}_{2}
some refined estimates for perturbations of non-convolution singular integral operators will be required.

We now define in a precise manner the operator
\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0}
in (
3.27
). On the one hand notice that

\frac{1}{\left|n\right|}\frac{\cosh(nL)-1}{\sinh(nL)}=\frac{1}{\left|n\right|}+Q_{n}\mbox{ for }n\neq 0,\mbox{ and }\frac{1}{\left|n\right|}\frac{\cosh(nL)-1}{\sinh(nL)}=Q_{0}

(3.45)

where

Q_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{\cosh(nL)-1}{\sinh(nL)}-\mbox{sgn}(n)\right),\mbox{ for }n\neq 0,\mbox{ and }Q_{0}=\frac{L}{2}.

We recall that the periodic Hilbert transform denoted by
\mathcal{H}
is given in Fourier side as

\widehat{\mathcal{H}f}(n)=-i\mbox{sgn}(n)\widehat{f}(n),n\in\mathbb{N}

(3.46)

and define the linear operator
\partial_{x}^{-1}:L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
by means of

\partial_{x}^{-1}\psi(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\psi(\xi)\ d\xi-\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi(\xi)\xi\ d\xi,\mbox{ if }\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi(x)\ dx=0,\\
0,\mbox{ if }\psi(x)=1.\end{cases}

(3.47)

Then it is natural to define
\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0}
in (
3.27
) as

\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0}\psi(x)=-\mathcal{H}\partial_{x}^{-1}\psi(x)+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}Q(x-\eta)\psi(\eta)\ d\eta,

(3.48)

where

Q(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}Q_{n}e^{inx}.

On the other hand, notice that the derivative operator
\partial_{x}
is the inverse of
\partial_{x}^{-1}
, i.e.
\partial_{x}\circ\partial_{x}^{-1}=\mathcal{I}
, where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the identity operator. Hence, we find that the inverse operator
(\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0})^{-1}
is given by

(\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0})^{-1}\psi(x)=\mathcal{H}\partial_{x}\psi(x)-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\widetilde{Q}(x-\eta)\psi(\eta)\ d\eta,

(3.49)

where

\widetilde{Q}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\widetilde{Q}_{n}e^{inx}

and

\widetilde{Q}_{n}=\left(n\frac{\sinh(nL)}{\cosh(nL)-1}-\left|n\right|\right),\mbox{ for }n\neq 0,\quad\widetilde{Q}_{0}=\frac{2}{L}\mbox{ for }n=0.

Thus, we have that
(\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0})^{-1}\circ(\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0})=(\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0})\circ(\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0})^{-1}=\mathcal{I}
. The easiest way to check this identity is to use the Fourier expression for
\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{0}
on the left hand side in (
3.45
). With these definitions at hand we have that the function
\mathsf{G}(x)
in (
3.30
) is given by

\mathsf{G}(x)=(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL})^{-1}\widetilde{g}(x)=\mathcal{H}\partial_{x}\widetilde{g}(x)-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\widetilde{Q}(x-\eta)\widetilde{g}(\eta)\ d\eta

(3.50)

where

\widetilde{g}(x)=-g(x)-\mathcal{Z}(x)

where
\mathcal{Z}
is defined in (
2.16
).
Roughly, speaking the function
\widetilde{g}
and hence
\mathsf{G}
take into account the given boundary value conditions
f
on
\partial\Omega
,
g
on
\partial\Omega_{-}
satisfied by the magnetic fields.

To conclude, we will define the operators given in (
3.28
) for
i=1,\ldots,4
as

\mathcal{T}_{i}^{NL}\psi(x)=\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}\mathsf{T}_{i}\psi(x)

(3.51)

where
\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}
is given by (
3.48
) and
\mathsf{T}_{i}
are given as (
3.37
), (
3.38
), (
3.41
) and (
3.42
).

Remark 3.2
.

The fact that the operators
\mathcal{T}^{NL}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,4
can be written as in (
3.28
) acting on spaces of Hölder functions will be proved at the end of the paper, cf. Corollary
8.1
in Subsection

.

4.
Hölder estimates for non-convolution singular integral operators

In order to show the existence and uniqueness of solutions of equation (
3.36
), we will need to derive bounds for the operators
\mathsf{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{T}_{4}
in the functional spaces
C^{1,\alpha}
and
C^{\alpha}
. To that purpose, we will first derive in this section some general lemmas showing
C^{1,\alpha}
and
C^{\alpha}
Hölder estimates for non-convolution singular integral operators. These operators differ from convolutions because they contain a function
\Lambda:\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Estimates for these operators in Hölder norms will be shown assuming a suitable smallness condition on
\Lambda
which will be used repeatedly in the rest of the paper. More precisely, the assumptions reads

Assumption 4.1
.

Let us assume that the function
\Lambda:\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
has
C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
regularity and satisfies that
\Lambda(\eta,0)=0
. Moreover, there exists
\delta_{0}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
such that

\left\lVert\Lambda\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)}\leq\delta_{0}.

Let us start with the following calculus lemma that will be used throughout this section.

Lemma 4.2
.

Then there exists a numerical constant
c_{0}>0
such that for any
\Lambda
satisfying Assumption
4.1
the following inequality holds

\left|1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)}\right|\geq\displaystyle\min\{\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{(x-\eta)^{2}+y^{2}},c_{0}\}

(4.1)

for
x\in[-\pi,\pi]
,
\eta\in[x-\pi,x+\pi]
and
y\in[0,L]
.

Proof.

Denoting by
z=i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)
, we have that for
\left|z\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}

\left|e^{z}-1\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\left|z\right|,\mbox{ for }z\in\mathbb{C}.

(4.2)

Indeed, a straightforward calculation shows that

\left|e^{z}-z-1\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{z}(e^{\xi}-1)\ d\xi\right|\leq\frac{\sqrt{e}}{2}\left|z\right|^{2},\mbox{ for }\left|z\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}

and hence

\left|e^{z}-1\right|\geq\left|z\right|-\int_{0}^{z}(e^{\xi}-1)d\xi\geq(1-\frac{\sqrt{e}}{4})\left|z\right|,\mbox{ for }\left|z\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(4.3)

Therefore, (
4.2
) follows.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\left|i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)\right|=\sqrt{(x-\eta)^{2}-2\Lambda(x-\eta)+y^{2}+\Lambda^{2}}.

(4.4)

Since by assumption
\Lambda(\eta,0)=0
and
\left\lVert\Lambda\right\rVert_{C^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{2}
we find that

\left|\Lambda(\eta,y)\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}y,\mbox{ for }0\leq y\leq L.

(4.5)

Applying Young’s inequality in (
4.4
) yields

\displaystyle\left|i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)\right|\geq\sqrt{(x-\eta)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(x-\eta)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}+y^{2}},

(4.6)

and hence

\left|i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)\right|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{(x-\eta)^{2}+y^{2}}

(4.7)

\mbox{ for }x\in[-\pi,\pi],\eta\in[x-\pi,x+\pi]\mbox{ and }y\in[0,L].

On the other hand for
\left|z\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}
one can readily check that for
\left\lVert\Lambda\right\rVert_{C^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\delta_{0}
we have that

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{A}}\left|1-e^{z}\right|\equiv c_{0},\mbox{ for }\mathcal{A}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|\geq\frac{1}{2},\left|Im(z)\right|\leq\pi\}

(4.8)

where the constant
c_{0}
is independent of
\Lambda
. Combining (
4.3
), (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) we conclude that estimate (
4.1
) follows.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Notice that in Assumption
4.1
we imposed that
\Lambda\in C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
, however the proof of Lemma
4.2
can be shown only assuming
\Lambda\in C^{1}(\Omega)
. However, later in the application we will use this stronger regularity assumption and therefore we prefer to already state the calculus lemma for
\Lambda\in C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
.

4.1.
C^{\alpha}
Hölder estimates

In this subsection, we provide a
C^{\alpha}
Hölder estimates for a type of non-convolution singular integral operators.

Proposition 4.4

(
C^{\alpha}
estimate)
.

Let
H(\eta,y)\in C^{\alpha}(\Omega)
and let Assumption
4.1
hold. Then for any
x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
the following limit exists

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\Xi_{\epsilon}(x)=\Xi(x),

(4.9)

where

\Xi_{\epsilon}(x)=\int_{\epsilon}^{L}dy\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d\eta\ \partial_{x}\mathcal{A}(x,y,\eta)H(\eta,y),\ \epsilon>0

(4.10)

with

\mathcal{A}(x,y,\eta)=\left(\frac{e^{i(x-\eta)-y}y}{(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y})(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})}\right).

(4.11)

Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Xi\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)}

(4.12)

with
C>0
.

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

In order to check that the left hand side in (
4.9
) exists, we first notice that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\mathcal{A}(x,y,\eta)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}(x,y,\eta)-\mathcal{R}(x,y,\eta)

(4.13)

where

\mathcal{R}(x,y,\eta)=iy\frac{e^{i(x-\eta)-y}\partial_{\eta}\Lambda(\eta,y)e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)}}{(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y})(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})^{2}}.

(4.14)

Therefore, using the fact that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d\eta\ \partial_{\eta}(\ldots)=0

we can rewrite (
4.9
) as

\Xi_{\epsilon}(x)=\Xi_{1,\epsilon}(x)+\Xi_{2,\epsilon}(x),

where

\displaystyle\Xi_{1,\epsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\epsilon}^{L}dy\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d\eta\ \partial_{\eta}\mathcal{A}(x,y,\eta)\left(H(\eta,y)-H(x,0)\right),

(4.15)

\displaystyle\Xi_{2,\epsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\epsilon}^{L}dy\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d\eta\ \mathcal{R}(x,y,\eta)H(\eta,y).

(4.16)

Expanding the derivative in (
4.15
) and manipulating the corresponding expression, we have that

\Xi_{1,\epsilon}(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{4}I_{j,\epsilon}

(4.17)

where

I_{j,\epsilon}=\int_{\epsilon}^{L}dy\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d\eta\ i_{j,\epsilon}(x,\eta,y),\mbox{ for }j=1,\ldots,4

(4.18)

where

\displaystyle i_{1,\epsilon}(x,\eta,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{iye^{i(x-\eta)-y}}{(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y})(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})}\left(H(\eta,y)-H(x,0)\right),

\displaystyle i_{2,\epsilon}(x,\eta,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{iy(e^{i(x-\eta)-y})^{2}}{(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y})^{2}(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})}\left(H(\eta,y)-H(x,0)\right),

\displaystyle i_{3,\epsilon}(x,\eta,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{iye^{i(x-\eta)-y}e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)}}{(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y})(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})^{2}}\left(H(\eta,y)-H(x,0)\right),

\displaystyle i_{4,\epsilon}(x,\eta,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{iye^{i(x-\eta)-y}e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)}\partial_{\eta}\Lambda(\eta,y)}{(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y})(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})^{2}}\left(H(\eta,y)-H(x,0)\right).

Identifying
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with
I_{x}=[x-\pi,x+\pi]
for
x\in[-\pi,\pi]
and using the bound (
4.1
) as well as the Hölder regularity for
H
we obtain

\displaystyle\left|i_{1,\epsilon}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)}\frac{y(\left|x-\eta\right|^{\alpha}+y^{\alpha})}{(x-\eta)^{2}+y^{2}},

(4.19)

\displaystyle\left|i_{2,\epsilon}\right|+\left|i_{3,\epsilon}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)}\frac{y(\left|x-\eta\right|^{\alpha}+y^{\alpha})}{\left((x-\eta)^{2}+y^{2}\right)^{3/2}}

(4.20)

where
C>0
is independent on
\epsilon
.
Therefore, using the dominated convergence it follows
\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}I_{j,\epsilon}
exists, for
j=1,\ldots,3.

On the other hand, we can combine
I_{4,\epsilon}
and
\Xi_{2,\epsilon}
as

I_{4,\epsilon}+\Xi_{2,\epsilon}=\int_{\epsilon}^{L}dy\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d\eta\ i_{4,\epsilon}(x,\eta,y),

(4.21)

where

i_{4,\epsilon}(x,\eta,y)=-H(x,0)\frac{iye^{i(x-\eta)-y}e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)}\partial_{\eta}\Lambda(\eta,y)}{(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y})(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})^{2}}.

(4.22)

Hence using again (
4.1
) and Assumption
4.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|i_{4,\epsilon}\right|\leq C\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\frac{y^{2}}{((x-\eta)^{2}+y^{2})^{3/2}}.

(4.23)

Similarly using dominated convergence it follows that the limit

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\left(I_{4,\epsilon}+\Xi_{2,\epsilon}\right)
exists.
Therefore, the limit on the left hand side of (
4.9
) exists and the function
\Xi(x)
is well-defined. Moreover, we have the pointwise bounds

\displaystyle\left|\Xi_{\epsilon}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)},\quad\mbox{for }x\in\mathbb{S}^{1},\epsilon>0,

(4.24)

\displaystyle\left|\Xi(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)},\quad\mbox{for }x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

(4.25)

We now proceed with the
\alpha
-Hölder semi-norm. More precisely, we will show that

\left|\Xi(x_{1})-\Xi(x_{2})\right|\leq C\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|^{\alpha}\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)},\quad\mbox{for }x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

Due to the translation invariance of the estimate it suffices to check, without loss of generality, that the bound holds for
x_{2}=0
and
x_{1}=x
, namely

\left|\Xi(x)-\Xi(0)\right|\leq C\left|x\right|^{\alpha}\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)},\quad\mbox{for }x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

(4.26)

To that purpose, by means of equation (
4.15
)-(
4.16
), we compute the difference

\Xi_{\epsilon}(x)-\Xi_{\epsilon}(0)=\bigg[\Xi_{1,\epsilon}(x)-\Xi_{1,\epsilon}(0)\bigg]+\bigg[\Xi_{2,\epsilon}(x)-\Xi_{2,\epsilon}(0)\bigg]=J_{1,\epsilon}+J_{2,\epsilon}

(4.27)

where

\displaystyle J_{1,\epsilon}=

\displaystyle-\int_{\epsilon}^{L}dy\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d\eta\bigg[\partial_{\eta}\left(\frac{e^{i(x-\eta)-y}y}{(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y})(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})}\right)\left(H(\eta,y)-H(x,0)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\hskip 56.9055pt-\partial_{\eta}\left(\frac{e^{-i\eta-y}y}{(1-e^{-i\eta-y})(1-e^{-i\eta-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})}\right)\left(H(\eta,y)-H(0,0)\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle J_{2,\epsilon}=

\displaystyle-\int_{\epsilon}^{L}dy\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d\eta\bigg[\frac{e^{i(x-\eta)-y}y\partial_{\eta}\Lambda(\eta,y)e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)}}{(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y})(1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})^{2}}H(\eta,y)

\displaystyle\quad\hskip 85.35826pt-\frac{e^{-i\eta-y}y\partial_{\eta}\Lambda(\eta,y)e^{-i\eta-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)}}{(1-e^{-i\eta-y})(1-e^{-i\eta-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})^{2}}H(\eta,y)\bigg].

Notice that the functions
J_{1,\epsilon}
and
J_{2,\epsilon}
depend on
x
, but do not write it explicitly for the sake of simplicity. Moreover, recall that using the arguments above we have that the limits
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}J_{1,\epsilon}
and
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}J_{2,\epsilon}
exist.
Expanding the derivative we can split the integral in the following manner

\displaystyle\left|K_{7,\epsilon}\right|

\displaystyle\left|K_{2,\epsilon}\right|

To conclude the proof of the

bound (
4.12
), it only remains to estimate the more singular terms, namely

Using this decomposition,

\displaystyle K_{4,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=-\left(H(x,0)-H(0,0)\right)\int_{R^{\epsilon}_{\Omega,>}}dy\ d\eta\frac{iy(e^{-i\eta-y})^{2}}{(1-e^{-i\eta-y})^{2}(1-e^{-i\eta-y-i\Lambda(\eta,y)})}

\displaystyle=-\left(H(x,0)-H(0,0)\right)\int_{R^{\epsilon}_{\Omega,>}}dy\ d\eta\frac{iy(e^{-i\eta-y})^{2}}{(1-e^{-i\eta-y})^{2}(1-e^{-i\eta-y-iA(\eta)y}\left(1+r_{1}(\eta,y)\right))}

\displaystyle K_{4,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=-\left(H(x,0)-H(0,0)\right)\int_{R^{\epsilon}_{\Omega,>}}dy\ d\eta\frac{iy(e^{-i\eta-y})^{2}}{(1-e^{-i\eta-y})^{2}(1-e^{-i\eta-y-iA(\eta)y})}\left(1+r_{2}(\eta,y)\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(H(x,0)-H(0,0)\right)\left(\int_{R^{\epsilon}_{\Omega,>}}dy\ d\eta\big[\ldots\big]+\int_{R^{\epsilon}_{\Omega,>}}dy\ d\eta\big[\ldots\big]r_{2}(\eta,y)\right)=K_{41,\epsilon}+K_{42,\epsilon},

\displaystyle K_{41,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=-\left(H(x,0)-H(0,0)\right)\int_{R^{\epsilon}_{\Omega,>}}dy\ d\eta\frac{iy}{(-i\eta-y)^{2}(-i\eta-y-iA(\eta)y)}(1+r_{3}(\eta,y))

Therefore,

\displaystyle K_{411,\epsilon}=-\left(H(x,0)-H(0,0)\right)\int_{R^{\epsilon}_{\Omega,>}}dy\ d\eta\frac{iy}{(-i\eta-y)^{2}(-i\eta-y-iA(0)y)}+K_{4112,\epsilon}

In order to estimate this integral, we consider two different cases, the case for

and
\epsilon\leq y<2\left|x\right|

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}^{L}\frac{dy}{y}\int_{\max\{1,\left|\zeta\right|\}>\frac{2\left|x\right|}{y}}\frac{d\zeta}{(i\zeta+1)^{2}(i\zeta+1+iA(0))}

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

By Proposition
4.4
, it is clear that the function
\Xi(x)

We provide a similar type of estimate for a simplified operator that do not have the dependence on the function

Proposition 4.6
.

Let

and let Assumption
4.1
hold. Then for any

the following limit exists

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\widetilde{\Xi}_{\epsilon}(x)=\widetilde{\Xi}(x),

(4.148)

Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widetilde{\Xi}\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widetilde{\Xi}\right\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

The proof follows closely the ideas of Proposition
4.4
and Proposition
4.5
and the estimates can be shown mimicking the arguments used there. For the sake of completeness, we include the computations to derive bound (
4.149
), being the
C^{1,\alpha}
bound (
4.150
) analogous. To show that the left hand side of (

\partial_{x}\left(\frac{e^{ix-y}}{1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y}}\right)=-\partial_{\eta}\left(\frac{e^{ix-y}}{1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y}}\right).

Using the fact that

, we rewrite (

\widetilde{\Xi}_{\epsilon}(x)=-\int_{\epsilon}^{L}dy\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d\eta\ \partial_{\eta}\left(\frac{e^{ix-y}}{1-e^{i(x-\eta)-y}}\right)\left(H(\eta,y)-H(x,0)\right).

Expanding the derivative and using Lemma
4.2
(for the trivial case of
\Lambda\equiv 0
), where for

with the symmetric interval

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{\Xi}_{\epsilon}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)}\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\epsilon}^{L}dy\int_{I_{x}}d\eta\frac{(\left|x-\eta\right|^{\alpha}+y^{\alpha})}{(x-\eta)^{2}+y^{2}}\leq C\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)}.

is well-defined.

To obtain the Hölder estimate, we compute the difference

\displaystyle=\{(\eta,y)\in\Omega:\displaystyle\max\{\left|y\right|,\left|\eta\right|\}\leq 2\left|x\right|\mbox{ and }\epsilon\leq y\leq L\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Xi}_{\epsilon}(x)-\widetilde{\Xi}_{\epsilon}(0)

\displaystyle\left|D_{1,\epsilon}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\Omega)}\int_{R_{\Omega,\leq}}dy\ d\eta\bigg[\frac{(\left|x-\eta\right|^{\alpha}+y^{\alpha})}{(x-\eta)^{2}+y^{2}}+\frac{(\left|\eta\right|^{\alpha}+y^{\alpha})}{\eta^{2}+y^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle D_{2,\epsilon}

\displaystyle D_{22,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=-i\left(H(x,0)-H(0,0)\right)\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\bigg[\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}d\widetilde{\eta}\int_{2\left|x\right|}^{L}dy\frac{e^{i\widetilde{\eta}-y}}{(1-e^{i\widetilde{\eta}-y})^{2}}+\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{-2\left|x\right|}d\widetilde{\eta}\int_{\epsilon}^{L}dy\frac{e^{i\widetilde{\eta}-y}}{(1-e^{i\widetilde{\eta}-y})^{2}}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathsf{T}_{1}[\Lambda,\vartheta]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1}))}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathsf{T}_{1}[\Lambda,\vartheta]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1}))}

(5.4)

\mathfrak{G}^{+}_{1,\epsilon}(x,\eta)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{n=\infty}ne^{inx}\int_{\epsilon}^{L}e^{-ny}\left[\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}-1}{1+\vartheta(\eta,y)}\right]dy+\mathfrak{G}_{12,\epsilon}^{+}(x,\eta)

Lemma 5.11
.

Let

\displaystyle h^{+}(x)=-J+\int_{0}^{x}(f(\xi,L)-A)\ d\xi,\quad h^{-}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}(f(\xi,0)-A)\ d\xi,\mbox{ for }x\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

Proof.

Adding and subtracting

in (
5.72
) we obtain that

\mathcal{Z}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\left(\left|n\right|\widehat{h^{-}}(n)e^{inx}+W_{1}(n)\widehat{h^{+}}(n)e^{inx}+W_{2}(n)\widehat{h^{-}}(n)e^{inx}\right)

(5.74)

where
\left|W_{i}(n)\right|\leq Ce^{-Ln}
for

. Therefore, recalling that

\partial_{x}\mathcal{H}h^{-}(x)=\mathcal{H}\partial_{x}h^{-}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}\left|n\right|\widehat{h^{-}}(n)e^{inx}

where
\mathcal{H}
is the periodic Hilbert transform we arrive at

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{Z}\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert\mathcal{H}\partial_{x}h^{-}\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}+\left\lVert h^{-}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}+\left\lVert h^{+}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert\mathcal{H}h^{-}\right\rVert_{C^{3,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}+\left\lVert h^{+}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right).

Moreover, using the fact that the Hilbert transform is a bounded operator in the class of Hölder spaces (cf.
[
21
]
) namely

\left\lVert\mathcal{H}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(C^{3,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1}))}\leq C,

and that

we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{Z}\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert\mathcal{H}h^{-}\right\rVert_{C^{3,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}+\left\lVert h^{+}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right)\leq C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}.

∎

Lemma 5.12

7.
The fixed point argument

In this section we will provide the fixed point argument, this is, we will define an adequate operator
\Gamma
on a subspace of
C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
which has a fixed point
b
such that
B=(0,1)+b
is a solution to (

) and (

We define the operator
\Gamma:B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))\to C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
using several intermediate building blocks. Given

we define the flow map associated with the vector field
B=(0,1)+b
as the mapping
X[b]:B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))\to C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
which satisfies the ordinary differential equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\partial_{y}X[b](\xi,y)=\frac{b^{1}(X(\xi,y),y)}{1+b^{2}(X(\xi,y),y)}\\
X[b](\xi,0)=\xi.\end{array}\right.

(7.1)

Moreover, we denote by
X[b]^{-1}(\xi,y)
the inverse function of
X[b]
in the first variable, namely
X[b](X[b]^{-1}(\xi,y),y)=\xi
. Then we define
\Lambda:B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))\to C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
as

\Lambda[b](\eta,y)=X[b](\eta,y)-\eta

(7.2)

Actually, in Lemma
7.2
we will show the stronger result
Im(\Lambda)\subset B_{CM}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))
for
C>0
.
We define also the function
\Theta:B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))\to C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
as

\Theta[b](\xi,y)=X[b]^{-1}(\xi,y)-\xi.

(7.3)

Finally
\vartheta[b]:B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))\to C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)
is given by

\vartheta[b](\eta,y)=\partial_{\xi}\Theta[b](X[b](\eta,y),y).

(7.4)

Notice that combining (
7.3
)-(
7.4
) we can write

\vartheta[b](\eta,y)=\partial_{\xi}X^{-1}[b]\left(X[b](\eta,y),y)\right)-1.

(7.5)

Moreover, arguing as in Lemma
7.2
we prove that
Im(\vartheta)\subset B_{CM}(C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega))
.

Therefore, we now introduce the following operator

\Psi[b]:B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))\to B_{CM}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))\times B_{CM}(C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega))

defined by
\Psi[b]=(\Lambda[b],\vartheta[b])
and choose
\widetilde{M}=CM\leq M_{0}
.
Next, notice that the function
j_{0}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{-})
given by (
6.11
) solving the integral equation (
6.2
) can be expressed as the following composition of operators

j_{0}=\Pi[\Psi[b]](\mathsf{G}-\langle\mathsf{G}\rangle).

(7.6)

The condition
\widetilde{M}=CM\leq M_{0}
must be satisfied so that Proposition
6.5
can be applied.
To conclude the construction, we use two additional building blocks. First, for
b\in B_{M}(C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega))
and
j_{0}=\Pi[\Psi[b]](\mathsf{G}-\langle\mathsf{G}\rangle)
, we define

where

is the unique solution to the transport type problem

T[b,j_{0}]:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}((0,1)+b)\cdot\nabla j=0,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
j=j_{0},\ \mbox{on}\ \partial\Omega_{-}.\end{array}\right.

(7.7)

To conclude, the new velocity field
W\in C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
is recovered by means of the div-curl problem (also known as Biot-Savart operator) given by

W=B_{s}[j,f,J]:C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)\times C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)\times\mathbb{R}\to C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)

is the unique solution to

B_{s}[j,f,J]:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\nabla\times W=j,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\
\mbox{div }W=0,\ \mbox{in}\ \Omega\\

\int_{0}^{L}W_{1}(0,y)\ dy=J.\end{array}\right.

(7.8)

(7.9)

(cf. (
3.35
)) with

.
Then, we define
\Gamma(b)=W
. In particular, the full operator can be expressed as the following composition of operators

(7.10)

Corollary 8.1
.

Let
j_{0}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{-})
. Then, for

\mathcal{T}_{i}^{NL}j_{0}(x)=\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}\mathsf{T}_{i}j_{0}(x):C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{-})\to C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{-})

Proof.

By means of Proposition
5.4
, Proposition
5.5
and Proposition
5.6

j_{0}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{-})
the operators
\mathsf{T}_{i}j_{0}
are well defined and
\mathsf{T}_{i}j_{0}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{-})
,

On the other hand, for

\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}j_{0}
given as in (
3.48
) is well defined and
\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}j_{0}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{-})

\mathcal{T}_{i}^{NL}=\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}\mathsf{T}_{i}:C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{-})\to C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{-}),\quad i=1,\ldots,4.

We now show that we can express
\mathcal{T}_{i}^{NL}j_{0}(x)
as the convolution operators given in (
3.28
), for
i=1,\ldots,4.
We will just provide the proof for
i=1
, since the cases
i=2,3,4
are very similar. Recalling that the
\mathsf{T}_{1}
is understood as the limit operator (
3.37
)

\mathsf{T}_{1}j_{0}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathfrak{G}_{1,\epsilon}(x-\eta,\eta)j_{0}(\eta)\ d\eta:=\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathsf{T}_{1,\epsilon}j_{0}(x)

(8.3)

we have that

\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}\mathsf{T}_{1}j_{0}=\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathsf{T}_{1,\epsilon}j_{0}(x).

By means of Proposition
5.4
, we have shown the uniform estimate

\left\lVert\mathsf{T}_{1,\epsilon}j_{0}\right\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega_{-})}\leq C,\mbox{ and }\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathsf{T}_{1,\epsilon}j_{0}(x)=\mathsf{T}_{1}j_{0}(x)

(8.4)

Therefore, by the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem and the fact that

\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}
has an integrable kernel, we conclude that

\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathsf{T}_{1,\epsilon}j_{0}(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}\mathsf{T}_{1,\epsilon}j_{0})(x)

(8.5)

Using the formal Fourier computations in Section

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}^{NL}\mathsf{T}_{1,\epsilon}j_{0}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{G}_{1,\epsilon}^{NL}(x-\eta,\eta)\omega_{0}(\eta)d\eta

where

\mathcal{G}_{1,\epsilon}^{NL}(x,\eta)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{n=\infty}e^{inx}\int_{\epsilon}^{L}e^{-\left|n\right|y}\left[\frac{e^{-in\Lambda(\eta,y)}-1}{1+\partial_{\xi}\Theta(X(\eta,y),y)}\right]dy.
