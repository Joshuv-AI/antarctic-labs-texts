Surprising occurrences of order structures in mathematics
Gunnar Fløystad

Abstract
. Order and symmetry are main structural principles in mathematics. We give five examples where on the face of it order is not apparent, but deeper investigations reveal that they are governed by order structures. These examples are finite topologies, associative algebras, subgroups of matrix groups, ideals in polynomial rings, and classes of bipartite graphs.

1.
Introduction

Proving a mathematical statement is to give an argument assuming
simpler mathematical statements. These statements are again proven from
even simpler ones. So it goes on, until reaching the basis, the axioms.

Classifying and unravelling mathematical structure should be to understand
it in terms of simpler structures. For instance, how it is
built up or understood in terms of groups, partial orders, graphs and so on.
Of course, these structures are also complicated, and understanding and
classifying subclasses of such provides in itself deep research into
structure.

Given two objects
a
and
b
, the simplest way they can relate is
that one ”precedes” the other, or that there is a “direction” from one
to the other, say
ab
or the other way
ba
.
They could also be unrelated, or we could allow both
ab
and
ba
to
occur. This most basic of ways to relate should be
fundamental. If
V
is a set of objects,
the simple mathematical notion for it is a relation
R\subseteq V\times V
.
It may also be reasonable to have the trivial
a
precedes
a
, and
that the notion composes:
Given
ab
and
bc
one has
ac
.
The relation
R
is then a
preorder
.

One may enrich and allow
ab
, that is
a
precedes
b
, to occur in several
ways. This gives a set of such,
\text{Hom}(a,b)
, for each ordered pair
(a,b)
.
Equivalently this is a map
E\rightarrow V\times V
with
\text{Hom}(a,b)
the
inverse image of
(a,b)
. This structure is a directed graph.
Again, if one would like to compose ordered ways, this gives a

category

We give five examples of well-known and richly studied structures which
on the face of it do not involve order structures, but deeper investigations
reveal that order structures classify or describe essential features
of them.

Topologies.
Consider topologies on a finite set
X
.
For topologists is is well-known that giving such a topology is equivalent
to give a preorder on
X
, but this may not be so well-known outside that
community.

Associative algebras.
Associative algebras make a large class.
For a field
k
, the
free

k
-algebra on a set
X
has basis
all words on the set
X
. A word is a sequence of elements of
X
and
as such ordered. Going to the opposite in size, we consider the class
of associative
k
-algebras which are finite-dimensional as
k
-vector spaces.
Essential in classifying them are directed graphs, or quivers as they
are called in that community. The associated category and path algebra,
together with relations give essentially all associative
k
-algebras
which are finite-dimensional.

Subgroups of matrix groups.
The general linear group

GL_{k}(n)
is the group of invertible
n\times n
-matrices with entries
in the field
k
. We consider subgroups of this containing the diagonal
matrices. Surprisingly, and maybe not well-known, connected such
subgroups are precisely classified by preorders on a set with

n
elements.

Ideals in polynomial rings.
These are the classical
objects in algebra and the fundamental algebraic objects in algebraic geometry,
as their zero set gives varieties. A polynomial ring is commutative and
so in contrast to associative algebras, there is no hint of order
relation.

Polynomial ideals may be degenerated to monomial ideals. And monomial
ideals may be degenerated to even simpler types of monomial ideals.
But at the bottom there is a class of monomial ideals which are
as degenerate as possible. Which are these?
To any monomial ideal is associated a preorder. This is a simple
observation which in its explicit form may be new here. And the monomial
ideal is maximally degenerate iff the associate preorder is a total preorder.

Bipartite graphs.
To a combinatorial object one may often
associate an algebraic object. Thus, one can use the machine and notions
of algebra to investigate combinatorial objects.
To simplicial complexes one may for instance associate Stanley-Reisner
ideals, to graphs one may associate edge ideals in polynomial rings.

A basic niceness condition on commutative rings is that of being
Cohen-Macaulay. For graphs it is as difficult question as anything to
to give a general description of when a graph has Cohen-Macaulay edge ring.
But for bipartite graphs this is completely understood. An amazing
result of J.Herzog and T.Hibi
[
21
]
says that this holds
if and only if the bipartite graphs are constructed from a partial order.

2.
Categories, groups, and preorders

Category theory started with the 1945 article “A general theory of
natural equivalences”
[
9
]
by Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders MacLane.
They defined the noitions of category, functors, and natural transformations
to get clear, abstract and pervasive notions to describe a variety
of settings and workings in mathematics. Category theory really
started to gain a life of its own from the late 1950’s,
especially with notion of adjoint
functors (see the appendix) introduced by Daniel Kan in 1958.
The language of category theory has now advanced into most domains
of pure mathematics. Since the 1990’s it has also been
comprehensively in use in theoretical computer
science. In the last 10-15 years Applied Category Theory has surfaced

[
17
]
. For instance, categorical notions are realized to be natural
in database and network modeling.

To put it in popular terms, category theory concerns the “social life”
of mathematical objects. It is not a priori concerned with inner structure, but
rather how the objects relate to each other. But (just as with people),
how they relate reflects much on their inner structure. So,
it also becomes a tool (a universal such) to understand inner structure.

.

Consider the mathematical notion of

.
Groups form a collection of mathematical objects.
Between any two groups

•

Morphisms may be composed. Given homomorphisms

we get the composition

.

•

For each group

there is an identity homomorphism

.

The homomorphisms fulfill two properties:

•

If
h:G_{3}\rightarrow G_{4}
is a third homomorphism, we have equality of
the two compositions

h\circ(g\circ f),\quad(h\circ g)\circ f.

•

The compositions

.

2.2.
Groups as categories

Example 2.4
.

Let a category have a single object

This fulfills

This categorical view of a group in fact displays the basic idea that

definition of a group the categorical viewpoint adds the classical
intuition.

Our first example was the category of groups. And now in fact we
see that each single group itself may be considered a category.
The inner structure of the object (group) is described as a category.

2.3.
Preorders as categories

Consider a category whose objects form a set, and
where between any pair of objects
a,b
there is
at most one

morphism.
Write
a\leq b
if there is a morphism.
The existence of an identity morphism says that
a\leq a
for
every object

. If
a\leq b
and
b\leq c
, there must
be a composition, so
a\leq c
.

The requirements of associativity of composition and on the identity
morphism are automatic when there is at most a single morphism between
two objects.
Thus, such a category corresponds precisely to a set with a
preorder
.

Definition 2.5
.

A relation
\leq
on a set
V
is

if it is:

•

Reflexive:

a\leq a
for every
a\in V
,

•

Transitive:

a\leq b
and
b\leq c
implies
a\leq c
.

We may have extra conditions.

a. The preorder is a

,
and
(V,\leq)
is a

if it is:

•

Anti-symmetric:

a\leq b
and
b\leq a
implies
a=b
.

In categorical language this says that each
isomorphism class consists of a single
object (element).

b. The preorder is an
equivalence relation
if it is:

•

Symmetric:

a\leq b
implies
b\leq a
.

In categorical language this is a
groupoid
: Every morphism
is invertible, so is an isomorphism.

c. The preorder is
total
if:

•

Comparability:

For any
a,b\in V
either
a\leq b
or
b\leq a
(or both).

d. The preorder is
discrete

if
a\leq b
implies
a=b
, so any two distinct elements
are not comparable.

e. The preorder is
coarse

if
a\leq b
for any
a,b\in V
.

A preorder
\leq
induces an equivalence relation
\sim

where
a\sim b
if

a\leq b
and
b\leq a
. The equivalence classes of

are
the

of the preordered set
(V,\leq)
.
Letting
\overline{V}
be these equivalence classes, the preorder
induces a poset
(\overline{V},\leq)
.

3.
Directed graphs, categories, and preorders

An even simpler type of order structure is provided by the notion of

. This induces both a preorder
and an enrichment of this preorder, a category, as we now shall see.

Let

an ordered pair of maps

It is tempting to think this could be a category with objects

.
At least an edge

But it seems it should be the germ of a category and of a preorder.

Consider paths of edges in this directed graph

Letting

This gives a partial associative product on the set of paths

4.
Topologies

Most of us have likely seen the basic definition of a topology on a
set
X
in
terms of open sets. Usually in topology, the set
X

is infinite, and one puts extra conditions on
the topology to develop richer theory. For instance, one may require each
point of
X
to have an open set neighbourhood which looks like an
open set in some
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. This gives the notion of topological manifold.
The modern notion of a topology was in an equivalent form introduced by
Felix Hausdorff in his influential book
Grundzüge der Mengenlehre

in 1914:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be a family of subsets of a set
X
. This is a
topology

on
X
if:

•

\emptyset\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
X\in{\mathcal{T}}
,

•

If
U,V\in{\mathcal{T}}
, then
U\cap V\in{\mathcal{T}}
,

•

If
\{U_{i}\}
is any family of sets with
U_{i}\in{\mathcal{T}}
, then
their union
\cup_{i}U_{i}\in{\mathcal{T}}
.

The elements of
{\mathcal{T}}
are the
open sets
of the topology.
A subset of
X
is
closed
if its complement set is open.

No order structure is apparent on
X
in this definition. Indeed,
any sphere with the standard notion of open subsets, forms
a topology, and a sphere has a priory no natural order structure.
Now assume
X
is finite. Digging into
what a topology on
X
is, gives a surprise. First an example
with finite
X
:

Example 4.2
.

Let
(X,\leq)
be a set with a preorder. Am
up-set

U
of this
preorder, is a subset
of
X
such that
x\in U
and
x\leq y
in
X
implies
y\in U
.
Both
X
and the empty set are up-sets of
X
. We also see that taking
an arbitrary union or intersection of up-sets of
X
we still get a
up-set.
Hence up-sets fulfill the requirement of being
a topology.

Now comes an amazing correspondence and result.
Given a topology

{\mathcal{T}}
on a set
X
. For
x,y\in X
, define
x\leq y
if
any open subset
U
containing
x
also contains
y
.
Clearly i)
x\leq x
and ii)
x\leq y
and
y\leq z
implies
x\leq z
.
So this gives a preorder. With Example
4.2
we thus have correspondences:

\text{Pre}(X)\overset{{\tt{t}}}{\underset{{\tt{pre}}}{\rightleftarrows}}\text{Top}(X).

Theorem 4.3
.

For a finite set
X
, these correspondences are inverse bijections.
Thus, to give a topology
{\mathcal{T}}
on
X
is
equivalent
to give
a
preorder

\leq
on
X
. The topology is then precisely the up-sets
of the preorder.

Proof.

If a preorder
\leq
induces a topology
{\mathcal{T}}
, it is immediate that
this topology again induces the preorder
\leq
, so
{\tt{pre}}\circ{\tt{t}}=\text{{id}}
.

Let a topology
{\mathcal{T}}
induce a preorder
\leq
. This preorder induces
a topology
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}
.
We show
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{T}}
.
Let
U
be an open subset of
X
.
If
x\in U
and
x\leq y
, then
y\in U
by definition of the preorder.
Hence
U
is an up-set for
\leq
and
U\in{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}
.
Thus
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}
.

Conversely let
U^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}
. We show
U^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}
.
For each
x\in U^{\prime}
, let

U_{x}
be the intersection of all
U
in
{\mathcal{T}}
containing
x
. Since

X
is finite, this is a finite intersection and so
U_{x}
is in
{\mathcal{T}}
.
As
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}
,
U_{x}
is also in
{\mathcal{T}}^{\prime}
.

Let
y\in U_{x}
. By definition of the preorder
\leq
, we have
x\leq y
.
But then
y\in U^{\prime}
as
x\in U^{\prime}
and
U^{\prime}
is an up-set.
Hence
U_{x}\subseteq U^{\prime}
. So

U^{\prime}=\cup_{x\in U^{\prime}}U_{x}
and so
U^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}
.
∎

Birkhoff’s theorem

[

dates from 1937
and is very closely related to Theorem

They are essentially the same. Birkhoff’s theorem says that a

A lattice of subsets of a finite set

Such a lattice is also
automatically distributive.
So, Birkhoff’s theorem produces a poset. It is in fact the
poset

induced on the set

5.2.
Finite dimensional algebras and module categories

Definition 5.5

A

From the definition of associative algebras there is no apparent order

The following shows however that basic order relations underlie

or more implicit

Theorem 5.6

Let

Any finite dimensional associative

is Morita equivalent to a

with relations

Morita equivalence dates back to

were then widely circulated in lectures by Hyman Bass

Due to above theorem, in the representation theory of finite-dimensional

The representation theory of finite-dimensional associative algebras

The setting for the above theory is now the greater generality of

7.
Ideals in polynomial rings

Polynomial rings
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and ideals
I
in polynomial
rings are the classical and fundamental algebraic objects in
algebraic geometry and commutative algebra.
Ideals may be degenerated to simpler ideals. The question we address here
is: What are the most degenerate ideals in a polynomial ring?

Let
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}_{k}
be the affine
n
-space, consisting of all
n
-tuples

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
where the
a_{i}\in k
.
To the ideal
I
we associated its geometric object, the algebraic set

V(I)=\{{\mathbf{a}}\in{\mathbb{A}}^{n}_{k}\,|\,f({\mathbf{a}})=0\text{ for every }f\in I\}.

When
k
is algebraically closed, Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz says that this
is a bijection between algebraic sets in
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}_{k}
and
radical
ideals in

k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. The ideal
I
is radical if
f^{n}\in I
implies

. An algebraic set which is
irreducible
, i.e.
not the union of two
proper algebraic subsets is a

. Varieties are in bijection with
prime ideals in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

7.1.
Families of ideals and algebraic sets

Varieties and ideals come in families. Consider the ideal in

k[x,y]
generated by the polynomials

p=x^{2}+2xy+y,\quad q=y^{2}-x-y.

Each of them defines a conic in the affine plane
{\mathbb{A}}^{2}_{k}
and
their intersection is a set of four points.

We introduce a new parameter
t
, and perform the coordinate change

x\mapsto\frac{1}{t^{3}}x,\quad y\mapsto\frac{1}{t^{2}}y.

The polynomials then become:

p_{t}=\frac{1}{t^{6}}x^{2}+2\frac{1}{t^{5}}xy+\frac{1}{t^{2}}y,\quad q_{t}=\frac{y^{2}}{t^{4}}-\frac{1}{t^{3}}x-\frac{1}{t^{2}}y.

They generate a family of ideals as

varies. As long as
t\neq 0
,
we may multiply with
t^{6}
and

and they generate the same ideals as:

\overline{p_{t}}=x^{2}+2txy+t^{4}y,\quad\overline{q_{t}}=y^{2}-tx-t^{2}y.

Now let

. We then get the degenerate ideal generated by:

(4)

x^{2},y^{2}.

This is not a radical ideal anymore. Its zero set is now only the origin.
Geometrically it is nevertheless fruitful to
think of this as the origin “fattened” up
to a fat point of size
4
.
The ideal generated by
p
and
q
has now been
degenerated
to
the ideal

generated by
x^{2},y^{2}
.

Question.
Is the ideal
I
as degenerate as
it gets, or is it possible to degenerate it still further?

It turns out one can degenerate even more.

, acts on the set of monomials in

k[X]
and so also on its set of monomial ideals. The symmetry group
of a monomial ideal

is its stabilizer, i.e. the permutations
g\in S(X)

such that
g.I=I
.

Example 7.1
.

The preorder associated to
I=(x^{2},y^{2})

is the discrete preorder. More generally the preorder associated to

I=(x^{2}\,|\,x\in X)
is the discrete preorder. However,
the symmetry group of this ideal is the full group

of
permutations of
X
.

Example 7.2
.

The preorder associated to the power ideal
I=(x\,|\,x\in X)^{r}

is the coarse preorder where
x\leq y
for all
x,y\in X
.
The symmetry group of this ideal is the full group
S(X)
of
permutations of

.

Example 7.3
.

Let
X=\{x,y,z\}
. To the ideal

I=(x,y^{2},yz,z^{3})
is associated the total order

.
The symmetry group of this ideal only consists of the identity map.

7.3.
Families of ideals and algebraic sets, continued

On the ideal
I=(x^{2},y^{2})

perform the coordinate change
y\rightarrow y+\frac{1}{t}x
.
The gives now the ideal
I_{t}
generated by:

(6)

x^{2},\quad(y+\frac{1}{t}x)^{2}=\frac{1}{t^{2}}x^{2}+2\frac{1}{t}xy+y^{2}.

Multiplying with
t^{2}
and let

the limit of both polynomials
is
x^{2}
. This is not good geometrically. The zero set is now blown up
to a line.
There is a good limit ideal
I_{0}
with the same “size” as the
I_{t}
’s,
but to see its generators we
need to throw extra generators into (
6
).

Note that the generators also give the following combination:

(y-2\frac{1}{t}x)(\frac{1}{t^{2}}x^{2}+2\frac{1}{t}xy+y^{2})+(2\frac{x}{t^{3}}+3\frac{y}{t^{2}})x^{2}=y^{3}.

The ideal generated by (
6

t\neq 0

is the same as the ideal generated by the three polynomials:

x^{2},xy+\frac{t}{2}y^{2},y^{3}.

Now let
t\rightarrow 0
. We get the generators:

(7)

x^{2},xy,y^{3}.

This
is
a generating set for the correct limit ideal
I_{0}
.
This ideal is also

more
degenerate than the ideal
I
generated by
x^{2},y^{2}
:
One can
not
perform coordinate changes on
I_{0}
and get the ideal

I
as a limit ideal.

Question.
Can one further degenerate the ideal
I_{0}

generated by (

)?

The answer turns out to be no. The ideal
I_{0}
cannot be further degenerated.
Why is this so, and which ideals have this property?

7.4.
Parameter spaces: The Hilbert schemes

First let us give some theoretical insight into what is going on.
There is a good notion of parameter spaces for graded ideals in a
polynomial ring
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
These are the
Hilbert schemes

H
, which are themselves algebraic sets.
Each point
p\in H
corresponds to a graded ideal
I_{p}
in

k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

The general linear group
GL(n)
acts by coordinate changes on
ideals. For an ideal
I
in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, a
g\in GL(n)

gives by coordinate change a new ideal
g.I
.
Acting on
I_{p}
, the ideal
g.I_{p}
will be a new graded ideal
I_{q}

parametrized by the point
q
. We can then define an action of
GL(n)

on
H
by
g.p=q
.

Algebraic sets are endowed with a topology, the
Zariski topology
,
and so also the Hilbert scheme
H
. For a subset
Z
of
H
, denote by

\overline{Z}
its closure.
If the orbit
\mathcal{O}(p)
by
GL(n)
is not closed, its boundary

\overline{\mathcal{O}(p)}\backslash\mathcal{O}(p)
contains a point
p^{\prime}
and
the ideal
I_{p^{\prime}}
corresponds to a more degenerate ideal than
I_{p}
.
The
most degenerate ideals

I_{p}
are then those ideals such that the
orbit
\mathcal{O}(p)
is closed.
As any ideal can be degenerated to a monomial
ideal, any such closed orbit contains a monomial ideal.
The monomial ideals in such orbits
can be described precisely.

7.5.
Most degenerate ideals and Borel-fixed ideals

An ideal which is as degenerate as possible, is a monomial
ideal with a “direction”:

Theorem 7.4
.

(char.
k=0
) A monomial
ideal is a most degenerate ideal
(i.e. it cannot be degenerated further, its orbit on the Hilbert scheme
is closed), iff the associated preorder by (
5
)
is a
total preorder
.

This formulation is new. It follows from Theorem
7.9

below, and we now give the traditional presentation of this.

Put a total order
x_{1}>x_{2}>\cdots>x_{n}
on the variables.
By Section
5
, the associated
subgroup
B(n)=GL_{\geq}(X)
of
GL(n)

is the subgroup of upper triangular matrices.

Definition 7.5
.

A graded ideal
I
in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

is
Borel-fixed
if
g.I=I
for all
g
in the Borel group
of upper triangular matrices in
GL(n)
.

Definition 7.6
.

A monomial ideal

in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is

strongly stable
if whenever
m
is a monomial in
I
and

m=x_{j}n
and
x_{i}>x_{j}
(i.e.
i<j
), then
x_{i}n\in I
.

As it turns out, in characteristic zero Definition
7.6

is a combinatorial
characterization of Definition
7.5
:

Fact 7.7
.

When
k
has characteristic zero, a graded ideal

is
Borel-fixed iff it is monomial and strongly stable,
[
10
, Thm. 15.23]

or
[

, Prop. 4.24]
.

Example 7.8
.

The ideal
J=(x^{2},xy,y^{3})
is strongly stable for the
monomial order
x>y
.

We see that a monomial ideal is strongly stable iff the associated
preorder is a total preorder in
\text{Pre}(X)
which is
{\mathbf{\succeq}}
the total order

x_{1}>x_{2}>\cdots>x_{n}

in
\text{Pre}(X)
.
Note that the associated preorder of a monomial ideal is a total preorder
iff it is given by permuting the variables of a strongly stable ideal.

The following is shown in
[
13
]
, but is really an old
and known result which follows from standard theory, Borel’s fixed point
theorem
[
23
, Thm. 21.2]
. It is equivalent to Theorem

.

Theorem 7.9
.

(char.
k=0
) An orbit of

on
the Hilbert scheme

is closed iff it is the orbit of a Borel-fixed

Borel-fixed ideals or strongly stable ideals are standard topics in
textbooks on computational or combinatorial commutative algebra,

[
10
,
22
,
31
]
. They
are essential for establishing results on the numerical behavior of
homogeneous ideals

in polynomial rings, for instance the classical
results of Macaulay on what are the possible
Hilbert functions
h(n)=\dim_{k}I_{n}
(dimension of the

of such ideals. They are essential in

[

where they are used to understand numerical behavior of

[
5
]
for a strong classification

7.6.
Monomial orders

For any homogeneous ideal in a polynomial ring there is a standard
way of getting directly to a degenerate ideal, by introducing a
monomial order
: This is a total order on the monomials in a polynomial ring with the
following two simple extra requirements:

•

x_{i}>1
for each variable
x_{i}
,

•

If
m,n
are monomials with
m>n
, then
x_{i}m>x_{i}n
for each variable
x_{i}
.

With a mononomial order specified,
one gets the theory of Gröbner bases for ideal in polynomial rings,

[
10
, Section 15]
,
[
25
]
.
This is the central tool of computational algebra. We must however
emphasize that there are many monomial orders to choose from (a priori
none is canoncial), and
different orders have different advantages for computational tasks.
The setting of a monomial order is thus quite orthogonal to the
philosophy of this article. We aim at uncovering
naturally
occurring order
structures, while a monomial order must be
chosen
.

A monomial order gives for any ideal
I
a distinguished direct
monomial ideal degeneration
(one arrives at it by a single one-parameter
t
degeneration),
the
initial ideal

\text{in}(I)
.
By performing a slight perturbation of

I
we have the following classical result by André Galligo from
1974
[
19
]
,
[
10
, Thm. 15.20]
, which brings one
directly to a maximally degenerate ideal.

Theorem 7.10
.

Choose any monomial order on a polynomial ring

k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

such that
x_{1}>x_{2}>\cdots>x_{n}
.
Let
I
be a homogenous ideal in this ring and
perform a general coordinate change on
I

to get
g.I
. The initial ideal
\text{in}(g.I)
is Borel-fixed.
This is called the generic initial ideal of
I
with respect
to the monomial order.

That one can thus directly arrive at a Borel-fixed ideal (and possibly
many by varying the monomial order) has given the idea that these
could give an approach to classify the components of the parameter
spaces, Hilbert schemes, for homogeneous ideals
[
16
,
27
,
34
,
35
]
.
A classification appears very intricate however, and must
be said to be at a rudimentary level.

7.7.
Strongly stable ideals as up-sets

Monomials in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are in bijection with
{\mathbb{N}}^{n}

where
{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, or equivalently with

\text{Hom}(\overline{n},{\mathbb{N}})
where
\overline{n}
is the anti-chain

\overline{n}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. For
{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})
the corresponding monomial is
x^{\mathbf{u}}=x_{1}^{u_{1}}x_{2}^{u_{2}}\ldots x_{n}^{u_{n}}
. Monomial ideals in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are in
bijection with up-sets
U\subseteq\text{Hom}(\overline{n},{\mathbb{N}})
, with the monomial
ideal consisting of all
x^{{\mathbf{u}}}
with
{\mathbf{u}}\in U
.

Let
[n]=\{1<2<\ldots<n\}
. The monomials in
k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

are also in bijection with order-preserving maps
\text{Hom}([n],{\mathbb{N}})
.
For
u_{1}\leq u_{2}\leq\cdots\leq u_{n}
in the latter, the corresponding
monomial is
x_{1}^{u_{1}}x_{2}^{u_{2}-u_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{u_{n}-u_{n-1}}
. As it turns out
strongly stable ideals are precisely
in bijection with up-sets
U\subseteq\text{Hom}([n],{\mathbb{N}})
,

[
12
,
13
]
or first observed in
[
14
]
.
Moreover, given a total preorder
T
, the strongly stable
ideals whose associated preorder is
\succeq\,T
, correspond
precisely to the up-sets in
\text{Hom}(T^{\text{op}}\,,{\mathbb{N}})
.

8.
Bipartite graphs

To combinatorial objects one may often associate algebraic objects.
In particular, simplicial complexes have an associated Stanley-Reisner
ring,
[
22
, Section 1]
. So, one may consider algebraic conditions on the ring and investigate
what this means for the combinatorial object.
For commutative rings a basic niceness condition is that of being

Cohen-Macaulay
. It has various equivalent definitions, often adapted
to various specializations.
We give one of them which does not require much technical background, and
investigate this in a basic combinatorial setting.

8.1.
Graph ideals

Let
G=(E,X)
be a
simple
graph with edges
E
and vertices
X
.
Let
k[X]
be the polynomial ring whose variables are the
x\in X
.
To each edge
\{v,w\}\in E
there is a quadratic monomial
vw\in k[X]
.
Let
I(G)
be the monomial ideal generated by all such
vw
coming from
edges in
G
, the
edge ideal
of
G
, and let
k[G]=k[X]/I(G)

be the quotient algebra, the
edge ring
of
G
.

Example 8.1
.

The graph

a
b
c
d

gives the edge ideal

I_{1}=(ab,bc,cd)\subseteq k[a,b,c,d]

and the edge ring
A_{1}=k[a,b,c,d]/(ab,bc,cd)
.

We may associate geometry to this ideal, by taking the common zeros

Z(ab,bc,cd)
of the monomials in
I_{1}
. This is an algebraic set
living in
{\mathbb{A}}^{4}_{k}
, the affine four-space.
Denoting such points by

{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{a},u_{b},u_{c},u_{d})
, note that if
{\mathbf{u}}
is in the zero set
then also
t{\mathbf{u}}
is in the zero set, so we may consider the zero set
to live in the projective three-space
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
.
It is easy to see that the zero set is then the union of the three
projective lines

Z(a,c)\cup Z(c,b)\cup Z(b,d)

where for instance
Z(a,c)
is the projective line where the
coordinates
a=0
and
c=0
, so it consists of points

(0,t,0,s)
(
s,t
not both zero) in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
.

Example 8.2
.

The graph

a
b
c
d

gives the edge ideal
I_{2}=(ab,bc,cd,da)
and
edge ring
A_{2}=k[a,b,c,d]/I_{2}
.
And the zero set of
I_{2}
is the union of two disjoint lines in

{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
:

Z(a,c)\cup Z(b,d).

Since the generators of the ideals
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are homogeneous polynomials,
these are graded ideals, and the quotient rings are graded rings

A_{1}
and
A_{2}

(more specifically graded

-algebras).

8.2.
Some notions from commutative algebra

Let us recall a few notions from commutative algebra.
A ring
A
is
graded
if it can be written

A=A_{0}\oplus A_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{n}\oplus\cdots

where the
A_{i}
are abelian groups, and the products
A_{i}\cdot A_{j}\subseteq A_{i+j}
. If
A
is a
k
-algebra, the
A_{i}
are
k
-vector spaces.

An element
a
in a commutative ring is a
nonzerodivisor
if

ab=0
implies
b=0
. A sequence of elements of
A
:

a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
is
regular
if each
a_{i}
is a nonzerodivisor
in the quotient ring
A/(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1})
and these quotient rings
are all non-zero, for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
.

A graded
k
-algebra
A
is
Cohen-Macaulay
if there is a regular
sequence
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
such that
A/(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})

is finite-dimensional as a vector space over
k
. This is a basic
niceness

condition on graded
k
-algebras. The Krull dimension of
A
is then said
to be
n
, the length of the regular sequence.

Example 8.3
.

For the ring
A_{1}
above,
a-b,c-d
is a regular
sequence. The quotient ring

A_{1}/(a-b,c-d)\cong k[a,c]/(a^{2},ac,c^{2})\cong k\oplus\langle a,c\rangle

is a three-dimensional vector space.

Example 8.4
.

For the ring
A_{2}
, for the sequence
a-b,c-d
, the
first
a-b
is a nonzerodivisor in
A_{2}
, but
c-d
is a zerodivisor
in

A_{2}/(a-b)\cong k[a,c,d]/(a^{2},ac,ad,cd)

as
(c-d)a=0
but
a\neq 0
in
A_{2}/(a-b)
.

One may show there is no regular sequence in
A_{2}
giving a quotient
ring which is a finite-dimensional vector space, so
A_{2}
is not
Cohen-Macaulay.

Geometrically the reason why
A_{2}
is not Cohen-Macaulay is that the
zero set of the ideal is two non-intersecting lines. In general, if
the zero set is an algebraic subset which is not ”sufficiently connected”,
this implies it is not Cohen-Macaulay.

Edge ideals of graphs
I(G)
were first introduced in 1990 by Raphael Villarreal

[
38
]
. Since then, it has been an active topic in combinatorial
commutative algebra to understand algebraic properties of edge rings
and connect it to combinatorial properties of graphs
[
32
]
,
to build a dictionary between commutative algebra and graph theory.
For instance,
minimal vertex covers of a graph corresponds to minimal associated
prime ideals of the edge ideal
I(G)
.
Somewhat surprisingly, for a graph
G
in general,
it seems as difficult question as anything
to find a general criterion on
G
for the edge ring
k[G]
to be
Cohen-Macaulay.
But, for one class of graphs we have a good answer: Bipartite graphs.

8.3.
When are bipartite graphs Cohen-Macaulay?

A simple graph is
bipartite
if there is a disjoint union

X=A\sqcup B
such that every edge in
G
has one vertex in
A

and the other in
B
. An edge
\{a,b\}
(with
a\in A,b\in B
)
may be identified with the ordered pair
(a,b)
.
Hence a bipartite graph is equivalent to a subset
R\subseteq A\times B
,
that is, a
relation
between
A
and
B
.
We now characterize when
k[G]
is Cohen-Macaulay for a bipartite graph.

Let
(V,\geq)
be a partially ordered set.
The partial order is relation

R\subseteq V\times V
, and we write
v\leq w
if
(v,w)\in R
.
For
i=1,2
write
V_{i}=V\times\{i\}
.
It induces a bipartite
graph with vertices
X=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}
, and edges
\{(v,1),(w,2)\}
when

v\leq w
. Let
L
be the associated edge ideal.

Theorem 8.5
.

The edge ring
k[V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}]/L
is a Cohen-Macaulay
ring. Its Krull dimension equals the cardinality of
V
.

The proof of this is rather straightforward. It is not hard to show
that the sequence of variable differences
(v,1)-(v,2)
for
v\in V

forms a regular sequence. The final quotient ring is isomorphic to

k[V]/(vw\,|\,v\leq w).

This quotient ring is finite-dimensional as a vector space over
k
,
as the quotienting ideal contains the second power

v^{2}
of each variable
v\in V
.
The amazing thing is that
every
Cohen-Macaulay edge ideal is
of this kind: The edge ring is Cohen-Macaulay precisely when the relation between

A
and
B
is a partial order
[
21
]
,
[
22
, Sec.9.1.3]

Theorem 8.6
.

Let
G
be a bipartite graph with vertex partition
A\sqcup B
.
The edge ring
k[G]
is a Cohen-Macaulay ring iff there is a bijection

A\cong B
such that the bipartite graph, considered as a relation
between
A
and
B
, may be identified with a
partial
order
on
A
.

Example 8.7
.

Let
A=\{a,c\}
and
B=\{b,d\}
. Let
V=\{v,w\}
with the order relation
v\leq w
.
We identify
B
with
V_{1}
and
A
with
V_{2}
.
The edge ring in Example
8.1

becomes the edge ring

k[V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}]/((v,1)(v,2),(v,1)(w,2),(w,1)(w,2))

which is Cohen-Macaulay by Theorem
8.5
above.

Remark 8.8
.

A related niceness condition on monomial ideals is that
of having a
linear resolution
. When does this hold for edge
ideals of bipartite graphs? This is easier
than the theorem above. This happens when
A
and
B
can be given total
orders such that the relation
R\subseteq A\times B
is a up-set for
the partial order on
A\times B
.

8.4.
Letterplace ideals

Theorems
8.5
and

8.6
have generated much activity in
combinatorial commutative algebra.
We briefly sketch one direction having led to considerable generalizations,
which the author has been active in.
For a set
R
let
k[R]
be the polynomial ring whose variables are the
elements of
R
. Each subset
S\subseteq R
gives a monomial

m_{S}=\prod_{s\in S}s
.

For two preorders
P
and
Q
, we have the polynomial ring
k[P\times Q]
.
Let
\phi:P\rightarrow Q
be an order preserving map and let
\text{Hom}(P,Q)
be the set
of such. The graph of
\phi
is
\Gamma\phi=\{(p,\phi(p))\,|\,p\in P\}
,
which is a subset of
P\times Q
. It gives a monomial
m_{\Gamma\phi}

in
k[P\times Q]
. These monomials generated an ideal denoted
L(P,Q)
.

When
P=[n]
and
Q=V
, a partially ordered set,

is called a
letterplace ideal

[
14
]
.
These are Cohen-Macaulay ideals
[
11
]
,
[
14
]
,
and are generated by monomials of degree

.

Example 8.9
.

The ideals
L([2],V)
are precisely the ideals given
in Theorem
8.5
.

A more general framework is given in
[
12
,
14
]
. Consider
a
finite
down-set
\mathcal{I}
in
\text{Hom}(V,{\mathbb{N}})
. The ideal

generated
by all
m_{\Gamma\phi}
for
\phi\in\mathcal{I}
is called a
co-letterplace
ideal
. Its Alexander dual monomial ideal

is a
(generalized)
letterplace ideal
, a class considerably generalizing the

L([n],V)
. These ideals are also Cohen-Macaulay, and the simplicial complex
associated via the Stanley-Reisner correspondence is always a

triangulated ball

[
8
]
.
In the recent
[
15
]
this led to a fundamental theorem on the
combinatorial geometry of monomial ideals: Any polarization of
an artin monomial ideal gives, via the Stanley-Reisner correspondence,
a simplicial complex which is a triangulated ball.

Appendix A
Functors and adjunctions

In line with the general philosophy in mathematics, there should
be structure preserving maps between objects, and so also between
categories.

A.1.
Functors

Definition A.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
be categories.
A
functor

F:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
:

•

Associates to each object
c
in
\mathcal{C}
an object
F(c)
in
\mathcal{D}
.

•

To each pair of objects
c_{1},c_{2}
of
\mathcal{C}
there is a function

\text{Hom}(c_{1},c_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle F}}{{\longrightarrow}}\text{Hom}(F(c_{1}),F(c_{2}))

such that for morphisms

c_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\longrightarrow}}c_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\longrightarrow}}c_{3}
we have

F(g\circ f)=F(g)\circ F(f)
.

•

F({1}_{C})={1}_{F(c)}
.

Example A.2
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a category with one object
c
and similarly
\mathcal{D}
with
one object
d
, a functor
F:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
is a map of
monoids
F:\text{Hom}(c,c)\rightarrow\text{Hom}(d,d)
. If
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
are groups,
this is a group homomorphism.

Example A.3
.

If
P
and
Q
are preorders, then considering them
as categories, a functor
F:P\rightarrow Q
is simply an order preserving map
from
P
to
Q
, i.e. if
a\leq b
in
P
, then
F(a)\leq F(b)
in
Q
.

A.2.
Adjunctions

A map of sets
f:X\rightarrow Y
has an inverse iff it is a bijection.
Often this will not be so, but there may be maps
g:Y\rightarrow X
which
are approximations to an inverse. Furthermore, the approximation may be
from ”above” or from ”below”.

Let
P,Q
be preorders and consider an order-preserving map
g:P\rightarrow Q
.
There may then be a map
f:Q\rightarrow P
which approximates
an inverse of
g
from ”above”. Precisely this is formulated as follows.
For
q\in Q
and
p\in P
we have:

f(q)\leq p\text{ iff }q\leq g(p).

So given
g
and
q\in Q
we consider all
p
with
q\leq g(p)

and make
f(q)
as large as possible
\leq
these
p
.

Similarly, we may consider
h:Q\rightarrow P
which may be an approximation
from ”below”. Then

g(p)\leq q\text{ iff }p\leq h(q).

Example A.4
.

Let
P
and
Q
be the natural numbers
{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,2,\cdots\}
.
The map
f:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
given by
f(n)=2n
is an order preserving
map. If one wants an approximate “inverse” to this,
one should of course send

6
to
3
and
8
to
4
. However, for
7
it is a question whether one
should choose
3
of
4
. There are two consistent choices:

•

g^{l}:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
given by
g^{l}(m)=\lceil\frac{m}{2}\rceil
,

•

g^{r}:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
given by
g^{r}(m)=\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\rfloor
,

These are both order preserving maps, and are respectively the left
and the right adjoint to the map
f
.

The general categorical notion of this is the following.

Definition A.5
.

Let
F:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
and
G:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
be
functors. These are
adjoint functors
, with
F
left adjoint and
G

right adjoint, if there for each
c
in
\mathcal{C}
and
d
in
\mathcal{D}
are
bijections

\text{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(F(c),d)\cong\text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(c,G(d)).

Moreover, these bijections must be natural in the sense that if
c^{\prime}\rightarrow c

and
d\rightarrow d^{\prime}
are morphisms, the diagrams derived from this
are commutative diagrams.
