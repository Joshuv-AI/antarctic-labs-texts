Order statistics from exchangeable random variables are always sufficient
Nickos Papadatos

Abstract
Let ( X 1 , … , X n ) (X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}) be an exchangeable random vector with distribution function F F , and denote by Y 1 ≤ ⋯ ≤ Y n Y_{1}\leq\cdots\leq Y_{n} the corresponding order statistics. We show that the conditional distribution of ( X 1 , … , X n ) (X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}) given ( Y 1 , … , Y n ) (Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}) does not depend on F F .

For a Borel set
B\in{\cal B}(\mathds{R}^{n})
we set

B^{+}:=\cup_{\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}\{(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)}):\ {\textrm{\boldmath$x$}}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in B\}
, where the union is taken over the
n!
permutations

\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}:=(\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(n))
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Define the conic Borel set
\Gamma_{0}:=\{{\textrm{\boldmath$x$}}\in\mathds{R}^{n}:x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n}\}
.
If
{\textrm{\boldmath$X$}}:=(X_{1},\ldots X_{n})
is a random vector with order statistics

Y_{1}\leq\cdots\leq Y_{n}

then, setting
{\textrm{\boldmath$Y$}}:=(Y_{1},\ldots Y_{n})
, it is readily
checked that
{\textrm{\boldmath$Y$}}\in B
if and only if (iff)
{\textrm{\boldmath$Y$}}\in B\cap\Gamma_{0}

iff
{\textrm{\boldmath$X$}}\in(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}
for any Borel subset
B
of
\mathds{R}^{n}
.
This verifies that
{\cal B}^{+}:=\{(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}:B\in{\cal B}(R^{n})\}

is a
\sigma
-field in
\mathds{R}^{n}
which we shall call

the symmetric
\sigma
-field
.
A Borel subset
B
of
\mathds{R}^{n}
is called
symmetric
if
\textrm{\boldmath$x$}\in B

implies
(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)})\in B
for any permutation

\pi
. Under this definition,

{\cal B}^{+}
is the smallest
\sigma
-field that contains the symmetric
Borel subsets of
\mathds{R}^{n}
.

Using the preceding terminology we have the following

Theorem 1
.

If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are exchangeable random variables and

g:\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}
is Borel measurable with
\operatorname{\mathds{E}}|g({\textrm{\boldmath$X$}})|<\infty

then

\operatorname{\mathds{E}}[g({\textrm{\boldmath$X$}})\ |\ {\textrm{\boldmath$Y$}}]=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{{\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}}g(Y_{\pi(1)},\ldots,Y_{\pi(n)}),\ \ \mbox{\rm a.s.}

Proof.

For every
{\textrm{\boldmath$x$}}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
we fix a permutation
{\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}={\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}_{\textrm{\boldmath$x$}}

such that

x_{\pi(1)}\leq\cdots\leq x_{\pi(n)}
.
Define
{\textrm{\boldmath$f$}}({\textrm{\boldmath$x$}})=(f_{1}({\textrm{\boldmath$x$}}),\ldots,f_{n}({\textrm{\boldmath$x$}})):\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}^{n}
where

f_{1}({\textrm{\boldmath$x$}})=y_{1},\ldots,f_{n}({\textrm{\boldmath$x$}})=y_{n}
, with

y_{i}=x_{\pi(i)}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. The mapping
\textrm{\boldmath$f$}:\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}^{n}

is continuous, hence Borel. Then, the
\sigma
-field generated by
Y
,

\sigma({\textrm{\boldmath$Y$}})
, equals to

\sigma({\textrm{\boldmath$Y$}})=({\textrm{\boldmath$f$}}\circ{\textrm{\boldmath$X$}})^{-1}({\cal B}(\mathds{R}^{n}))={\textrm{\boldmath$X$}}^{-1}\Big({\textrm{\boldmath$f$}}^{-1}({\cal B}(\mathds{R}^{n}))\Big)={\textrm{\boldmath$X$}}^{-1}({\cal B}^{+}).

Let
(\Omega,{\cal A},\operatorname{\mathds{P}})
be the probability space where
the random vector
X
is defined. Clearly,

A\in\sigma({\textrm{\boldmath$Y$}})
iff
A=\{\omega:{\textrm{\boldmath$Y$}}(\omega)\in B\}=\{\omega:{\textrm{\boldmath$X$}}(\omega)\in(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}\}
for some

B\in{\cal B}(\mathds{R}^{n})
. Thus, we may write

I_{A}(\omega)=I_{(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}}({\textrm{\boldmath$X$}}(\omega))
, where

I
denotes an indicator function. The result
will be proved if we verify the identity

\int_{A}g({\textrm{\boldmath$X$}})\ {\rm d}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}\int_{A}g(Y_{\pi(1)},\ldots,Y_{\pi(n)})\ {\rm d}\operatorname{\mathds{P}},\ \ \ \ A\in\sigma({\textrm{\boldmath$Y$}}).

(1)

Obviously,

\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}g(Y_{\pi(1)},\ldots,Y_{\pi(n)})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}g(X_{\pi(1)},\ldots,X_{\pi(n)}),

(2)

because these sums contain the same summands. Since
I_{A}=I_{(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}}(\textrm{\boldmath$X$})
,
we can rewrite the left-hand side of (
1
) as

\int I_{A}\ g({\textrm{\boldmath$X$}})\ {\rm d}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}=\int I_{(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}}(\textrm{\boldmath$X$})\ g({\textrm{\boldmath$X$}})\ {\rm d}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}h(\textrm{\boldmath$X$}),

where
h(\textrm{\boldmath$x$}):=I_{(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}}(\textrm{\boldmath$x$})g(\textrm{\boldmath$x$})
.
From (
2
), the right-hand side of (
1
) equals to

\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}\int I_{(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}}(\textrm{\boldmath$X$})\ g(X_{\pi(1)},\ldots,X_{\pi(n)})\ {\rm d}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}.

Since the set
(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}
is symmetric,
it holds
I_{(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}}(\textrm{\boldmath$X$})=I_{(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}}(X_{\pi(1)},\ldots,X_{\pi(n)})
for every
\pi
.
Using exchangeability we obtain

\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}\int I_{(B\cap\Gamma_{0})^{+}}(\textrm{\boldmath$X$})\ g(X_{\pi(1)},\ldots,X_{\pi(n)})\ {\rm d}\operatorname{\mathds{P}}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}\operatorname{\mathds{E}}h(X_{\pi(1)},\ldots,X_{\pi(n)})=\operatorname{\mathds{E}}h(\textrm{\boldmath$X$}),

and the proof is complete.
∎

Corollary 1
.

The conditional distribution of
X
given
Y
is given by

\operatorname{\mathds{P}}({\textrm{\boldmath$X$}}\in B\ |\ \textrm{\boldmath$Y$})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\textrm{\boldmath$\pi$}}I_{B}(Y_{\pi(1)},\ldots,Y_{\pi(n)}),\ \ \ \ B\in{{\cal B}(\mathds{R}^{n})}.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
1
with
g=I_{B}
.
∎

Corollary
1
verifies that the order statistics are
sufficient

within the entire family,
{\cal F}
,
of exchangeable probability measures on
\mathds{R}^{n}
. This result cannot be
proved by means of the well-known Fisher-Neyman factorization criterion,
since there is no
\sigma
-finite measure dominating
{\cal F}
; see
Shao (2003), Lemma 2.1, Theorem 2.2 and Exercise 31.
Moreover, let

{\cal F}^{\prime}\subset{\cal F}
be the family
of
n
-variate probability measures
of independent, identically distributed, random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
Clearly,
{\cal F}^{\prime}
contains the Dirac measures
\delta_{(x,\ldots,x)}
,
x\in\mathds{R}
,
and therefore, there is no
\sigma
-finite measure dominating
{\cal F}^{\prime}
, too.
