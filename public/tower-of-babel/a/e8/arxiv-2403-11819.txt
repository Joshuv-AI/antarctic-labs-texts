Detecting immersed obstacle in Stokes fluid flow using the coupled complex boundary method
J. F. T. Rabago, L. Afraites, H. Notsu

Abstract
A non-conventional shape optimization approach is introduced to address the identification of an obstacle immersed in a fluid described by the Stokes equation within a larger bounded domain, relying on boundary measurements on the accessible surface. The approach employs tools from shape optimization, utilizing the coupled complex boundary method to transform the over-specified problem into a complex boundary value problem by incorporating a complex Robin boundary condition. This condition is derived by coupling the Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions along the accessible boundary. The identification of the obstacle involves optimizing a cost function constructed based on the imaginary part of the solution across the entire domain. The subsequent calculation of the shape gradient of this cost function, rigorously performed via the rearrangement method, enables the iterative solution of the optimization problem using a Sobolev gradient descent algorithm. The feasibility of the method is illustrated through numerical experiments in both two and three spatial dimensions, demonstrating its effectiveness in reconstructing obstacles with pronounced concavities under high-level noise-contaminated data, all without perimeter or volume functional penalization. Keywords: coupled complex boundary method, obstacle problem, shape inverse problem, Stokes equation, shape optimization, rearrangement method, and adjoint method

1.2
Shape optimization techniques for shape recovery

To recover the shape of the obstacle
\omega
, a common strategy involves minimizing a cost function.
Various types of cost functions may be considered for this purpose; see
[
BCD11
,
Cau13
,
CDKT13
]
for examples of existing cost functions.
A conventional approach is to consider the Neumann-least-squares-fitting cost function given by
[
BCD11
,
Cau13
]

J_{N}(\omega)=\cfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\sigma(\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{D}(\omega),p_{D}(\omega))\bm{\mathbf{n}}-{\bm{\mathbf{g}}}|^{2},

(3)

where
{\bm{\mathbf{g}}}
is the boundary measurement and
(\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{D},p_{D})
is the solution of the pure Dirichlet BVP:

-{\alpha}\Delta\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{D}+\nabla p_{D}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}\ \text{ in }\Omega,\qquad\operatorname{div}{\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{D}}=0\ \text{ in }\Omega,\qquad\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{D}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}\ \text{ on }\Gamma,\qquad\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{D}=\bm{\mathbf{f}}\ \text{ on }\Sigma.

(4)

Given the compatibility condition (
2
), problem (
4
) admits a unique solution once a normalization condition on the pressure
p
is imposed (see, e.g.,
[
Tem01
, Chap. 1]
).
In the minimization setting ‘
J_{N}(\omega)\to\inf
’, one can choose the normalization
\langle\sigma(\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{D},p_{D})\bm{\mathbf{n}},\bm{\mathbf{n}}\rangle_{\Sigma}=\langle\bm{\mathbf{g}},\bm{\mathbf{n}}\rangle_{\Sigma}
.
To ensure that the cost function
J_{N}
is well-defined, it is necessary to assume that the Dirichlet data input is such that
\bm{\mathbf{f}}\in H^{3/2}(\Sigma)^{d}
.
To avoid this higher regularity assumption, it is usually preferable to fit Dirichlet data rather than Neumann data.
In this case, one evaluates the misfit of the Dirichlet data using the least-squares method:

J_{D}(\omega)=\cfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{N}(\omega)-\bm{\mathbf{f}}|^{2},

(5)

where
(\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{N},p_{N})
satisfies the mixed Dirichlet-Neumann BVP:

-{\alpha}\Delta\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{N}+\nabla p_{N}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}\ \text{ in }\Omega,\quad\operatorname{div}{\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{N}}=0\ \text{ in }\Omega,\quad\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{N}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}\ \text{ on }\Gamma,\quad\sigma(\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{N},p_{N})\bm{\mathbf{n}}={\bm{\mathbf{g}}}\ \text{ on }\Sigma.

(6)

It can be shown that system (
6
) admits a unique solution once a normalization condition on
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{N}
is imposed.

Another cost function one may take into consideration is the called Kohn-Vogelius functional defined as follows
[
CDK13
]
:

J_{KV}(\omega)=\cfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{{\alpha}|{\nabla(\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{D}(\omega)-\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{N}(\omega))}|^{2}}{\,{d}x},

(7)

where
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{N}
is the solution of Neumman problem (
6
) and
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{D}
is the solution of the Dirichlet one (
4
).
The cost function
J_{KV}
measures the energy-gap between the solutions of (
4
) and (
6
).
This function vanishes only if
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{D}=\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{N}
which is the case when the shape
\omega
fits the exact inclusion.

The optimization problems and shape functionals mentioned above were studied in the context of closely related shape inverse problems for the Stokes equations, utilizing tools from shape optimization, as discussed in
[
CDK13
,
CDKT13
]
.
This involves employing the shape derivative of the associated cost functional with respect to the shape of the region to be reconstructed.
In the three works mentioned above, the authors characterized the shape gradient of the same functionals to facilitate numerical resolution.
Inspired by these works, we will do the same here, but through a proposal of a new shape optimization formulation.

Remark 1.3

(Existence, uniqueness, and regularity)
.

For detailed discussions on the existence, uniqueness, and regularity of solutions to the Stokes problem with Dirichlet boundary conditions, we recommend referring to
[
BF06
]
or
[
Gal94
]
.
Meanwhile, for the existence, uniqueness, and local regularity results for the Stokes’ problem with mixed conditions (
6
), one may consult Theorem A.1 and Theorem A.2 in
[
CDKT13
, Appx. A]
.

In this study, we propose the use of the Coupled Complex Boundary Method (CCBM) in a shape optimization setting to numerically approximate a solution to the inverse problem. We first emphasize that, in the classical approach (
3
) and (
5
), the required data for fitting is carried out on the boundary.
However, this condition often renders the numerical solution unstable.
In contrast, in the case of CCBM, the data needed for fitting is transferred from the boundary to the interior of the domain – similar to (
7
).
This is because CCBM couples the Neumann and Dirichlet data to derive a Robin condition.
Consequently, the Neumann data and the Dirichlet data are represented as the real and imaginary parts, respectively, of the Robin boundary condition.
As a result, this method is more favorable in terms of numerical realization.
However, we note that the new boundary value problem is complex, leading to an increase in the dimension of the corresponding discrete system.
Nevertheless, the method proves to be more robust, and effective numerical methods could be explored based on the new framework.

1.3
The CCBM and contribution to the literature

Initially introduced by Cheng et al. in
[
CGHZ14
]
for an inverse source problem, CCBM has found applications in various problmes.
Gong et al. utilized it for an inverse conductivity problem in
[
GCH17
]
, and Cheng et al. applied it to parameter identification in elliptic problems in
[
ZCG20
]
.
Afraites extended the method to shape inverse problems for the Laplace equation in
[
Afr22
]
and geometric inverse source problems in
[
AMN22
]
.
Rabago pioneered its use in free boundary problems, specifically solving the exterior case of the Bernoulli problem in
[
Rab22
]
.
Rabago and Notsu further extended the use of CCBM to free surface problems for the Stokes equations in
[
RN24
]
.
Notably, there has been no prior application of CCBM to obstacle problems except in
[
OCNN22
]
, where it was used to solve an inverse Cauchy Stokes problem.
In this study, the authors employed CCBM as a numerical strategy to determine fluid velocity and flux over a specific boundary portion, reconstructing an obstacle immersed in a fluid described by the Stokes equation through numerical shape optimization techniques.
The novelty of our work lies in applying CCBM to shape inverse obstacle problems in fluid flows.
Emphasizing the motivation, it is worth noting that, in the context of free boundary problems
[
Rab22
]
and free surface problems
[
RN24
]
, CCBM outperforms the classical least-squares approach in terms of stability and accuracy in obtaining the expected optimal shape solution.
Additionally, in terms of computational cost, CCBM is roughly comparable to the Kohn-Vogelius method, see
[
Rab22
]
.

We highlight the contribution of this examination to the literature.
From a theoretical point of view, we emphasize the following.

•

This study introduces a novel cost functional (
14
) to address the shape inverse obstacle Problem
1.1
.
The formulation is derived from the CCBM formulation (
8
).
Notably, this functional has not been explored in the current literature within the context of the present problem.

•

The study is centered on the computation of the first-order shape derivative of the cost functional associated with CCBM (Theorem
3.1
).
This computation is achieved only through the Hölder continuity of state variables (Lemma
3.6
), following the methodology outlined in
[
RN24
]
.
Our approach deviates from the conventional chain rule method (see, e.g.,
[
BCD11
,
CDKT13
]
) and the traditional Lagrangian formulation technique (see
[
KO22
]
), eliminating the need to calculate material or shape derivatives of states.

Meanwhile, from a numerical standpoint, we depart innovatively from the methodology applied in
[
CDKT13
]
.
Our approach distinguishes itself in three key aspects.

•

In the previously mentioned work, the authors addressed the ill-posedness of the problem by employing parametric regularization, incorporating a parametric model of shape variations.
This involved imposing restrictions on domains, confining them to star-shaped configurations, and using polar coordinates for parametrization.
This approach led to a unique method for calculating deformation fields.
In contrast, our approach relies on a straightforward Sobolev gradient-based method inspired by
[
DMNV07
]
.
Unlike the parametric method, we avoid the complexities associated with determining the number of parameters needed for a satisfactory approximation of the target shape.
The parametric approach, as presented in
[
CDKT13
]
, faces challenges in accurately determining this number.
Insufficient parameters may hinder the detection of non-trivial shapes, while an excess may result in functional degeneracy.
Consequently, this approach, in our view, poses implementation difficulties, especially in three dimensions (3D).

•

In our numerical simulations, we use lower-degree finite elements, in contrast to
[
CDKT13
]
, which employed higher-degree elements (refer to Remark
4.3
).
It is crucial to highlight that utilizing higher-degree finite elements, like
P4/P3
finite elements, for computing velocity-pressure variables in the Stokes equations, especially in 3D cases, is computationally expensive.

•

The work in
[
KO22
]
solely computes the shape derivative of a Lagrangian functional without including any numerical experiments.
Additionally, to the best of our knowledge, prior numerical investigations on shape inverse obstacle problems have exclusively concentrated on two-dimensional (2D) settings (refer to, e.g.,
[
CDKT13
]
).
In contrast, our current study extends this scope by evaluating the effectiveness of our proposed methodology in 3D cases.
This further warrants the execution of the present work.

The outline of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we present the coupled complex boundary method appropriate for our inverse obstacle problem.
We also reformulate it into a shape optimization setting, introducing the least-squares fitting for the imaginary part of the complex PDE’s solution.
Then, in Section

After that, in Section
4

We conclude the paper with a brief summary in Section

Finally, Appendix

is dedicated to proving the key lemmas used to derive the shape gradient via the rearrangement method.

can equivalently be formulated as follows:

Problem 2.2
.

Find
\omega\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta}
such that
{\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}
and
{p_{i}}=0
in
\Omega
, where
(\bm{\mathbf{u}},p)
satisfy (
8
).

2.2
Well-posedness of the CCBM formulation

Here, we briefly examine the well-posedness of the complex PDE system (
8
).
To do so, we introduce the following forms:

\left\{\begin{aligned} a({\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}},{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}})&=\int_{\Omega}{{\alpha}\nabla{\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}:\nabla{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}}}{\,{d}x}+i\int_{\Sigma}{{\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}\cdot{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}}}{\,{d}\sigma},\quad\text{where}\ {\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}},{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}\in{V_{\Gamma}},\\
b(\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}},\lambda)&=-\int_{\Omega}{\lambda(\nabla\cdot\overline{\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}})}{\,{d}x},\quad\text{where}\ {\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}\in{V_{\Gamma}},\ \lambda\in{Q},\\
F(\bm{\mathbf{\psi}})&=\int_{\Sigma}{(\bm{\mathbf{g}}+i\bm{\mathbf{f}})\cdot\overline{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}}{\,{d}\sigma},\quad\text{where}\ {\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}\in{V_{\Gamma}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(11)

With the above forms, we can state the weak formulation of (
8
) as follows:

\text{Find\quad$({\bm{\mathbf{u}}},p)\in V_{\Gamma}\times Q$\quad such that \quad}\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a({\bm{\mathbf{u}}},{\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}})+b(\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}},p)&=&F(\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}),&\ \forall{\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}\in{V_{\Gamma}},\\
b(\bm{\mathbf{u}},\lambda)&=&0,&\ \forall\lambda\in{Q}.\\
\end{array}\right.

(12)

The well-posedness of the above variational problem is issued in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(\bm{\mathbf{f}},{\bm{\mathbf{g}}})\in H^{1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}\times H^{-1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}
be given.
Then, problem (
12
) admits a (unique) solution
(\bm{\mathbf{u}},p)\in{V_{\Gamma}}\times Q
.
This solution depends continuously on the data and we have

{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\bm{\mathbf{u}}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{X},\ {|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|p|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{Q}\lesssim{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\bm{\mathbf{f}}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{H^{1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}}+{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\bm{\mathbf{g}}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{H^{-1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}}=:\left\|{(\bm{\mathbf{f}},\bm{\mathbf{g}})}\right\|_{1/2,\Sigma}.

The above claim can be validated through standard steps (cf.
[
RN24
, Appx. A]
).
In fact, it follows from the continuity of the sesquilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
on
V_{\Gamma}\times V_{\Gamma}
, its coercivity on
V_{\Gamma}\subset X
(i.e., there is a constant
c_{a}>0
such that the relaxed
V_{\Gamma}
-ellipticity assumption
\Re(a(\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}},\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}))\geqslant c_{a}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{X}
, for all
\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\in{V_{\Gamma}}
), the boundedness of
F
, and on an inf-sup condition; that is, there exists a constant
\beta_{0}>0
such that

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\lambda}\in{Q}\\
{\lambda}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\in{V_{\Gamma}}\\
\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\neq\bm{\mathbf{0}}\end{subarray}}\frac{b(\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}},{\lambda})}{{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{X}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|{\lambda}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{Q}}\geqslant\beta_{0}.

(13)

2.3
The shape optimization problem

We now present the solution method we use to resolve the shape inverse problem
2.2
.
To this end, we introduce the cost functional

J(\Omega):=J(D\setminus\overline{\omega})=\frac{1}{2}\Big\{\big\|{\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}}^{2}+\big\|{p_{i}}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big\}

(14)

and consider the following shape optimization problem:

Problem 2.4
.

Find
\omega^{\ast}\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta}
such that
\omega^{\ast}=\displaystyle\inf_{\omega\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta}}J(\Omega)
.

Remark 2.5
.

CCBM enables us to define the cost function
J
across the entire domain
\Omega
, offering advantages in robustness for reconstruction, such as the Kohn-Vogelius method, when compared to the cost functionals of boundary-integral-least-squares fitting, namely
J_{N}
and
J_{D}
.

To find the shape derivative of the cost function (
14
) with respect to
\omega
(or equivalently, to
\Omega
), we can use the classical chain rule approach (see, e.g.,
[
DZ11
,
HP18
]
). This method relies on having the expression for the shape derivative of the state problem (
8
).
However, obtaining the shape derivative of the states requires higher regularity assumptions on the unknown variables, typically derived from the regularities imposed on the domain and on the boundary data.
Our goal here is to avoid such unnecessary requirements for the first-order shape sensitivity of
J
.
Consequently, we establish the shape gradient of
J
– following the Hadamard-Zolésio structure theorem
[
DZ11
, p. 479]
– using only a mild
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
regularity assumption on the domain, combined with the assumption that
(\bm{\mathbf{f}},\bm{\mathbf{g}})\in H^{3/2}(\Sigma)^{d}\times H^{1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}
.
This task is achieved through the application of an alternative technique in computing shape derivatives of shape functionals known as the
rearrangement method

[
IKP06
,
IKP08
]
.
This approach bypasses the computation of the material and shape derivative of the states and utilizes only the Hölder continuity of the state variables to obtain the expression for the shape derivative of the cost.
It is worth noting that, despite its application in computing shape derivatives for various problems, such as shape optimization reformulations of free boundary problems
[
IKP06
,
HIK+09
,
BP13
,
RN24
]
, shape optimal problems
[
IKP08
,
KK11
,
KK14
]
, and shape design problems
[
SN22b
,
SN22a
]
, there is, to the best of our knowledge, no documented demonstration of the method applied to shape inverse problems.
This observation serves as a compelling motivation for applying this method to our present problem.

3
First-order shape sensitivity analysis

To iteratively solve Problem
2.4
through a numerical procedure, our goal is to characterize the shape gradient of the cost function
J
, as enunciated in Theorem
3.1
.
To facilitate this, we will make necessary preparations by recalling some relevant concepts from shape calculus.

3.1
Some concepts from shape calculus

Let us define
T_{t}
as the
perturbation of the identity

id
given by the map

T_{t}=T_{t}({\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})=id+t\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\qquad(T_{t}:D\longmapsto D),

(15)

where
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
is a
t
-independent deformation field belonging to the admissible space

\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}:=\{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d})^{\top}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}(\overline{D})^{d}\mid\operatorname{supp}{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}\subset{\overline{D}}_{\delta}\},

(16)

where
\{x\in{D}\mid d(x,\partial{D})>\delta/2\}\subset{D}_{\delta}\subset\{x\in{D}\mid d(x,\partial{D})>\delta/3\}
.
Clearly, by definition, we have that
\Sigma_{t}:=T_{t}(\Sigma)\equiv\Sigma
and
\Gamma_{t}:=T_{t}(\Gamma)
.
In addition,
\Omega_{0}=\Omega
and
\Sigma_{0}=\Sigma
.

Hereinafter, we assume that
t>0
is sufficiently small such that
T_{t}
is a diffeomorphism from
\Omega\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
onto its image.
Specifically, we let
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
be small enough so that
[t\mapsto T_{t}]\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{C}^{1,1}(\overline{D})^{d})
, where
\mathcal{I}:=[0,\varepsilon]
.
Accordingly, we define the set of all admissible perturbations of
\Omega
denoted by
\mathcal{O}_{ad}
as follows:

\mathcal{O}_{ad}=\left\{T_{t}({\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})(\overline{\Omega})\subset D\mid\Omega=D\setminus{\overline{\omega}}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1},\omega\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta},t\in\mathcal{I},\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}\right\}.

(17)

Note that we do not necessarily require the fixed boundary
\Sigma=\partial\Omega
to be
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
regular (it is sufficient for it to be Lipschitz), but for the sake of simplifying the discussion, we assume the stated regularity.

The functional
J:\mathcal{O}_{ad}\to\mathbb{R}
has a directional
first-order

Eulerian derivative
at
\Omega
in the direction of the field
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}
if the limit
\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{J(\Omega_{t})-J(\Omega)}{t}=:{d}J(\Omega)[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}]
exists (see, e.g.,
[
DZ11
, Sec. 4.3.2, Eq. (3.6), p. 172]
).
The function
J
is considered
shape differentiable
at
\Omega
in the direction of
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
if the mapping
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\mapsto{d}J(\Omega)[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}]
is both linear and continuous.
In such cases, we call it the
shape gradient
of
J
.

We introduce a few more notations for ease of writing.
The Jacobian matrix of
T_{t}
, its inverse, and its inverse transpose are denoted respectively by
DT_{t}
,
(DT_{t})^{-1}
, and
(DT_{t})^{-\top}:=((DT_{t})^{\top})^{-1}
.
For convenience, we define

I_{t}:=\det\,DT_{t},\quad A_{t}:=I_{t}(DT_{t}^{-1})(DT_{t})^{-\top},\quad\text{and}\quad B_{t}:=I_{t}|{M_{t}\bm{\mathbf{n}}}|,\quad M_{t}:=(DT_{t})^{-\top},

and we assume that, for all
t\in\mathcal{I}
, we have

0\leqslant\Lambda_{1}\leqslant I_{t}\leqslant\Lambda_{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0<\Lambda_{3}|{\xi}|^{2}\leqslant A_{t}\xi\cdot\xi\leqslant\Lambda_{4}|{\xi}|^{2},

(18)

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for some constants
\Lambda_{1}
,
\Lambda_{2}
,
\Lambda_{3}
, and
\Lambda_{4}
(
\Lambda_{1}<\Lambda_{2}
,
\Lambda_{3}<\Lambda_{4}
).

At
t=0
,
I_{0}=1
,
DT_{0}^{-1}=id
,
(DT_{0})^{-\top}=id
,
A_{0}=id
, and
B_{0}=1
.
The maps
t\mapsto(DT_{t})^{\pm 1}
,
t\mapsto I_{t}
, and
t\mapsto A_{t}
are differentiable (see (
A1
)), and their respective derivative are given as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(DT_{t})^{\pm 1}\big\rvert_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\dfrac{(DT_{t})^{\pm 1}-id}{t}=\pm D\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},\qquad\qquad\frac{d}{dt}I_{t}\big\rvert_{t=0}=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{I_{t}-1}{t}=\operatorname{div}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}},

(19)

\displaystyle\quad\frac{d}{dt}A_{t}\big\rvert_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{A_{t}-id}{t}=(\operatorname{div}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}){id}-{D}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}-({D}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}})^{\top}=:A.

We also introduce the following notations for economy of space:

\mathfrak{i}_{t}:=I_{t}-1,\qquad\mathfrak{m}_{t}:=M_{t}^{\top}-id,\qquad\mathfrak{a}_{t}:=A_{t}-id,\qquad\text{and}\quad\theta_{n}:=\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{n}}.

(20)

Since we intend to map a function
\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}_{t}:\Omega_{t}\to D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
to a reference domain using the transformation
T_{t}
, we will utilize the following notation:

\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}^{t}:=\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}_{t}\circ T_{t}:\Omega\to D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

To conclude this review and preparation section, we present some results regarding the limits of functions composed with the map
T_{t}
.

For a vector-valued function
\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}=(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{d})^{\top}\in H^{2}(D)^{d}
, we have

\lim_{t\to 0}\|\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\circ T_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\|_{H^{1}(D)^{d}}=\lim_{t\to 0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|\varphi_{i}\circ T_{t}-\varphi_{i}\|_{H^{1}(D)}\right)^{1/2}=0.

(21)

Moreover, the maps
t\mapsto\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\circ T_{t}
from
\mathcal{I}\to H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
and
t\to I_{t}\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\circ T_{t}
from
\mathcal{I}
to
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
are differentiable at
t=0
, and we have

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\left(\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\circ T_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\right)=D\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\left(I_{t}\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\circ T_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\right)=\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}\cdot(\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\otimes\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}),

(22)

where
\bm{\mathbf{\nabla}}\cdot(\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\otimes\bm{\mathbf{\theta}})=(\operatorname{div}(\varphi_{1}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}),\operatorname{div}(\varphi_{2}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}),\ldots,\operatorname{div}(\varphi_{d}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}))^{\top}
, and
\otimes
denotes the outer product; i.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\otimes\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}=\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{\top}=(\varphi_{j}\theta_{k})_{jk}
where
j,k=1,\ldots,d
.

The proofs of the above results are omitted since they are standard and follow similar arguments used for the case of scalar functions, see, e.g.,
[
DZ11
,
IKP06
]
.

3.2
The shape gradient of
J

Our main result in this section is the expression for the shape gradient of
J
, which we present in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\bm{\mathbf{f}},\bm{\mathbf{g}})\in H^{3/2}(\Sigma)^{d}\times H^{1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}
be a given Cauchy pair,
\Omega
is an admissible domain, and
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}
.
Then,
J
is shape differentiable with respect to
\Omega
in the direction of the deformation field
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
.
Its shape derivative is given by

\displaystyle{d}J(\Omega)[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}]

\displaystyle=\left\langle{{G}\bm{\mathbf{n}}},{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}\right\rangle_{\Gamma}=\int_{\Gamma}{\left(\Im\left\{\overline{\sigma(\bm{\mathbf{v}},q)\bm{\mathbf{n}}}\cdot\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}\right\}+\frac{1}{2}|p_{i}|^{2}\right)\bm{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}{\,{d}\sigma},

(23)

where
\Im
denotes the imaginary part,
(\bm{\mathbf{u}},p)
is the unique pair of solution to (
8
), while the pair of adjoints
(\bm{\mathbf{v}},q)=(\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{r}+i\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{i},q_{r}+iq_{i})
uniquely solves the adjoint system

-{\alpha}\Delta\bm{\mathbf{v}}+\nabla q=\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\ \text{in $\Omega$},\quad-\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathbf{v}}=p_{i}\ \text{in $\Omega$},\quad\bm{\mathbf{v}}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}\ \text{on $\Gamma$},\quad\sigma(\bm{\mathbf{v}},q)\bm{\mathbf{n}}-i\bm{\mathbf{v}}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}\ \text{on $\Sigma$},

(24)

with compatibility condition
\langle\bm{\mathbf{v}},\bm{\mathbf{n}}\rangle_{\Sigma}=-(p_{i},1)_{\Omega}
.

We emphasize here that if, instead,
\Omega
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{2,1}
,
(\mathbf{f},\bm{\mathbf{g}})\in H^{5/2}(\Sigma)^{d}\times H^{3/2}(\Sigma)^{d}
, and
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2,1}
smooth deformation field, then the existence of the material and shape derivatives of the states is guaranteed.
Because then we will have
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{r}
,
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\in H^{3}(\Omega)^{d}
and
p_{r}
,
p_{i}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
.
With this regularity, the shape gradient of the cost can be computed with ease using Hadamard’s domain differentiation formula
(see, e.g.,
[
DZ11
, Thm. 4.2, p. 483]
),
[
HP18
, eq. (5.12), Thm. 5.2.2, p. 194]
or
[
SZ92
, eq. (2.168), p. 113]
):

\displaystyle\left.\left\{\frac{{d}}{{d}t}\int_{\Omega_{t}}{f(t,x)}{\,{d}x_{t}}\right\}\right|_{t=0}=\int_{\Omega}{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(0,x)}{\,{d}x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}{f(0,\sigma)\theta_{n}}{\,{d}\sigma}

(25)

Before we provide a rigorous proof of Theorem
3.1
, let us exhibit shortly the shape gradient of
J
via the chain rule approach.
Let us assume, for now, that
\Omega
is an admissible domain of class
\mathcal{C}^{2,1}
, and
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\mathcal{C}^{2,1}(\overline{D})^{d}
such that
\operatorname{supp}{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}\subset{\overline{D}}_{\delta}
, and that
(\bm{\mathbf{f}},\bm{\mathbf{g}})\in H^{5/2}(\Sigma)^{d}\times H^{3/2}(\Sigma)^{d}
.
Then, we can apply formula (
25
) to obtain – noting that
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}
on
\Sigma
and
\bm{\mathbf{u}}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}
on
\Gamma
– the derivative

\displaystyle{{d}}J(\Omega)[\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}]=\int_{\Omega}{\left(\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\prime}+p_{i}p_{i}^{\prime}\right)}{\,{d}x}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma}{|p_{i}|^{2}\theta_{n}}{\,{d}\sigma}=:L_{1}+L_{2}.

(26)

Here, the shape derivative
(\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime},p^{\prime})
of the states
(\bm{\mathbf{u}},p)
is characterized by the PDE system

-{\alpha}\Delta\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}+\nabla p^{\prime}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}\ \text{in $\Omega$},\quad\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}\ \text{in $\Omega$},\quad\sigma(\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime},p^{\prime})+i\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}\ \text{on $\Sigma$},\quad\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}=-\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}\theta_{n}\ \text{on $\Gamma$}.

(27)

We can confirm this claim using standard techniques from shape calculus
[
DZ11
,
HP18
]
, with arguments similar to the proof in
[
RN24
, Thm. C.1]
, so we omit it.

We focus on eliminating the derivatives in the first integral
L_{1}
.
To do this, we make use of the adjoint method, utilizing the adjoint system (
24
).

Let us multiply by
\overline{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}^{\prime}
equation (
24
), apply integration by parts (IBP), and then use the boundary conditions in (
24
) to obtain

\int_{\Omega}{{\alpha}\nabla{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}:\nabla{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}^{\prime}}}{\,{d}x}-i\int_{\Sigma}{\bm{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\overline{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}^{\prime}}{\,{d}\sigma}-\int_{\Gamma}{({\alpha}\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}-q\bm{\mathbf{n}})\cdot\overline{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}^{\prime}}{\,{d}\sigma}=\int_{\Omega}{\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\cdot\overline{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}^{\prime}}{\,{d}x}.

Taking the complex conjugate on both sides of the above equation, we get (using the sesquilinear form in (
11
))

a(\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathbf{v}})-\int_{\Gamma}{\overline{({\alpha}\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}-q\bm{\mathbf{n}})}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}}{\,{d}\sigma}=\int_{\Omega}{\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}}{\,{d}x}.

Meanwhile, multiplying equation (
27
) by
\overline{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}
and then employing IBP, while taking into account the boundary conditions in (
27
), we get

a(\bm{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathbf{v}})=-\int_{\Omega}{p_{i}p^{\prime}}{\,{d}x}.

Subtracting this equation from the previous one, we see that

\displaystyle L_{1}=\Im\left\{\int_{\Omega}{\left(\bm{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\prime}+pp_{i}^{\prime}\right)}{\,{d}x}\right\}

\displaystyle=\Im\left\{\int_{\Gamma}{\overline{\sigma(\bm{\mathbf{v}},q)\bm{\mathbf{n}}}\cdot\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{n}}}{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}\theta_{n}}{\,{d}\sigma}\right\},

from which, after combining with
L_{2}
, we recover the expression in (
23
).

Our next objective is to demonstrate that applying the rearrangement method yields the same expression, with the only requirement being mild
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
regularity assumptions on
\Omega
, with perturbations in
\mathcal{O}_{ad}
, and regularity conditions
(\mathbf{f},\bm{\mathbf{g}})\in H^{3/2}(\Sigma)^{d}\times H^{1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}
.

Remark 3.2
.

The conclusion in Theorem
3.1
holds even when
(\mathbf{f},\bm{\mathbf{g}})\in H^{1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}\times H^{-1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}
.
In this case, the expression of
\left\langle{{G}},{\theta_{n}}\right\rangle_{\Gamma}
has to be seen as a duality product
H^{1/2}\times H^{-1/2}
.

3.3
Computation of the shape gradient: Proof of Theorem
3.1

We are now in the position to compute the shape derivative of
J
rigorously via rearrangement method given that
(\bm{\mathbf{f}},\bm{\mathbf{g}})\in H^{3/2}(\Sigma)^{d}\times H^{1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}
,
\Omega=D\setminus\overline{\omega}
of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
such that
\omega\in\mathcal{O}_{\delta}
, and
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\bm{\mathbf{\Theta}}
.
The discussion then lays out the proof of Theorem
3.1
, closely following
[
RN24
]
.

We start with the weak formulation of (
24
) which is given as follows:

\text{find \quad$({\bm{\mathbf{v}}},q)\in V_{\Gamma}\times Q$\quad such that \quad}\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\tilde{a}({\bm{\mathbf{v}}},{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}})+b(\bm{\mathbf{\psi}},q)&=&\tilde{F}(\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}),&\ \forall{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}\in{V_{\Gamma}},\\
b(\bm{\mathbf{v}},\mu)&=&(\mu,p_{i}),&\ \forall\mu\in{Q},\end{array}\right.

(28)

where

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{a}({\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}},{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}})&=\int_{\Omega}{{\alpha}\nabla{\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}:\nabla{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}}}{\,{d}x}-i\int_{\Sigma}{{\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}\cdot{\overline{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}}}{\,{d}\sigma},\quad\text{with}\ {\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}},{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}\in{V_{\Gamma}},\\
\tilde{F}(\bm{\mathbf{\psi}})&=\int_{\Omega}{\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\cdot\overline{\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}}{\,{d}x},\quad\text{with $\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}$ and ${\bm{\mathbf{\psi}}}\in{V_{\Gamma}}$}.\end{aligned}\right.

(29)

The demonstration of the well-posedness of (
28
) follows a standard procedure. It mirrors the proof outlined in Proposition
2.3
, anchoring on the distinct solvability of (
12
) within the space
V_{\Gamma}\times Q
.
Notably, it can be established that the variational problem satisfies an inf-sup condition.

At this juncture, we note that for
\Omega
of class
\mathcal{C}^{k+1,1}
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and assuming
(\bm{\mathbf{f}},\bm{\mathbf{g}})\in H^{k+3/2}(\Sigma)^{d}\times H^{k+1/2}(\Sigma)^{d}
, it can be demonstrated that the weak solution
(\bm{\mathbf{u}},p)\in V_{\Gamma}\times Q
of the variational problem (
12
) also belongs to
\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{k+2}(\Omega)^{d}\times\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{k+1}(\Omega)
– specifically,
\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{r},\bm{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\in H^{k+2}(\Omega)^{d}\cap H^{1}_{\Gamma,0}(\Omega)^{d}
and
p_{r},p_{i}\in H^{k+1}(\Omega)
. Consequently, the weak solution
(\bm{\mathbf{v}},q)
of problem (
28
) is not only in
V_{\Gamma}\times Q
but also lies within
\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{k+2}(\Omega)^{d}\times\bm{\mathbf{\mathsf{H}}}^{k+1}(\Omega)
.

Next, we introduce the sesquilinear forms
a^{t},\tilde{a}^{t}\in V_{\Gamma}\times V_{\Gamma}\to\mathbb{R}
and linear forms,
b^{t}:V_{\Gamma}\times Q\to\mathbb{R}
and

4
Numerical experiments

To numerically solve the optimization problem
J(\Omega)\to\inf
, we will apply a preconditioned shape-gradient-based descent method based on FEM.

Remark 4.1
.

Newton-type methods, as shown in
[
EH05
,
ADEK07
,
RA20
]
, could be developed for the numerical procedure using shape Hessian information.
However, we refrain from applying the method for two main reasons.
Firstly, computing second-order shape derivatives is challenging, involving substantial effort, preparations, and technical intricacies; see, e.g.,
[
ADK08
]
.
In the computation of the shape Hessian of
J
, two additional systems of complex PDEs, which correspond to the new adjoints, must be introduced.The second reason is primarily numerical.
In general, the implementation and computational effort required for computing the Hessian are not justified by a significant reduction in the number of iterations.
Indeed, it is important to note that evaluating the shape Hessian requires the solution of (at least) four variational problems (two for the states and another two for the adjoints), making it prohibitively costly.
Despite these drawbacks, we defer the examination of the shape Hessian of
J
for future work.

4.1
Numerical algorithm

Our main algorithm follows a standard approximation procedure (see, e.g.,
[
CDKT13
,
RN24
]
), the important details of which we provide as follows.

Choice of descent direction.

We will employ a Riesz representation of the shape gradient
G
to avoid undesired oscillations on the unknown boundary, which could result in instabilities in the approximation.
To do this, we fix
\eta\in(0,1]
and generate a smooth descent direction for the cost function
J
by seeking a vector
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in H_{\Sigma,0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
that solves the variational equation

\eta\int_{\Omega}{\nabla\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}:\nabla\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}{\,{d}x}+(1-\eta)\int_{\Gamma}{\nabla_{\Gamma}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}:\nabla_{\Gamma}\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}{\,{d}\sigma}=-\int_{\Gamma}{{{G}}\bm{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}}{\,{d}\sigma},\ \text{for all $\bm{\mathbf{\varphi}}\in H_{\Sigma,0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}$}.

(40)

Here, we choose
\eta=0.5
. In this way we obtain a
Sobolev gradient

[
Neu97
]
representation
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
of
G
.
More importantly, this approach produces a smoothed preconditioned extension of
-{G}\bm{\mathbf{n}}
over the entire domain
\Omega
.
Such extensions allow us to deform the discretized computational domain by moving the (movable) nodes of the computational mesh and not only the boundary nodes that are subject for perturbation.
The boundary integral appearing on the right side of (
40
) corresponds to the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\Gamma
.
Its purpose is to ensure an additional level of smoothness for the descent direction
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
within
\Gamma
.
Further discussion about discrete gradient flows for shape optimization problems are issued in
[
DMNV07
]
.

Remark 4.2
.

We reiterate here that our approach differs completely from that applied in
[
CDKT13
]
.
In the cited work, the authors employed parametric regularization to address the ill-posedness of the problem, utilizing a parametric model of shape variations for numerical realization.
This process involved restricting the domains to star-shaped configurations and using polar coordinates for parametrization, resulting in an explicit computation of
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
.

To compute the
k
th domain
\Omega^{k}
, we carry out the following procedures:

1.
Initilization

Choose an initial guess
\Omega^{0}
.

2.
Iteration

For
k=0,1,2,\ldots
, do the following:

2.1

Solve the state’s and adjoint’s variational equations on the current domain
\Omega^{k}
.

2.2

Choose

2.3

3.

Repeat

Step-size computation and stopping condition.
In Step 2.2,

is computed using a backtracking line search procedure inspired by
[
RA20
, p. 281]
with the formula
t^{k}=\mu J(\Omega^{k})/|\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{k}|^{2}_{\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega^{k})}
at each iteration, where

is a scaling factor.
This step size is further reduced to avoid reversed triangles within the mesh after the update.
Meanwhile, we terminate the algorithm as soon as the condition that
|{J^{k-1}-J^{k}}|<\varepsilon_{J}
or
t^{k}<\varepsilon_{T}
is satisfied where
\varepsilon_{J},\varepsilon_{T}>0
are sufficiently small real numbers, or the algorithm reached the maximum allowable number of iterations.
In all experiments, we set
\mu=0.5
,
\varepsilon_{J}=\varepsilon_{T}=10^{-12}
, and the maximum number of iterations to

4.2
Forward problem

We let
\alpha=1.0
and consider the unit circle as the shape of the medium.
For the input data, we will consider
\bm{\mathbf{g}}=(\sin\theta,-\cos\theta)^{\top}

represents the prescribed flux.
To obtain additional boundary measurements on the accessible part
\Sigma

Specifically, given a prescribed Neumann flux

\bm{\mathbf{f}}
on the accessible boundary

To avoid ‘inverse crimes’ (as discussed by Colton and Kress

This involves using a larger number of discretization points (with finer meshes of uniform mesh width

{P}{3}/{P}{2}
finite element basis functions in the
FreeFem++

[
Hec12
]
code than in the inversion process.
For experiments with noise-contaminated data with a specified noise level

4.3
Inversion procedure

In the inversion procedure, all variational problems are solved using Hood-Taylor (
{P}{2}/{P}{1}
) finite elements, and we discretize the domain with coarse meshes.
Specifically, in all experiments, the initial domain is discretized with uniform mesh size having
70

100

Remark 4.3
.

We emphasize here that we are, in fact, using lower-degree finite elements in our numerical simulations, in contrast to the computational setup in

5
Conclusion

The coupled complex boundary method is employed to address a shape-inverse obstacle problem in Stokes fluid flow.
The study reformulates the inverse problem as a shape optimization setup, utilizing least-squares fitting of the imaginary part of the complex PDEs’ solution in the flow domain.
The unknown inclusion is treated as a nonparametric shape.
The shape gradient of the associated cost functional is rigorously characterized using the rearrangement method, enabling effective numerical resolution through a Sobolev gradient descent method in a finite element setting.
Numerical results in both two and three-dimensional cases demonstrate the method’s effectiveness in detecting non-trivial obstacles, even under high-level noise-contaminated data.
