Constructing NP # ​ P \mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}} -complete problems and # ​ P {\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}} -hardness of circuit extraction in phase-free ZH
Piotr Mitosek

Abstract
The ZH calculus is a graphical language for quantum computation reasoning. The phase-free variant offers a simple set of generators that guarantee universality. ZH calculus is effective in MBQC and analysis of quantum circuits constructed with the universal gate set Toffoli+H. While circuits naturally translate to ZH diagrams, finding an ancilla-free circuit equivalent to a given diagram is hard. Here, we show that circuit extraction for phase-free ZH calculus is # ​ P \mathrm{\#P} -hard, extending the existing result for ZX calculus. Another problem believed to be hard is comparing whether two diagrams represent the same process. We show that two closely related problems are NP # ​ P \mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}} -complete . The first problem is: given two processes represented as diagrams, determine the existence of a computational basis state on which they equalize. The second problem is checking whether the matrix representation of a given diagram contains an entry equal to a given number. Our proof adapts the proof of Cook-Levin theorem to a reduction from a non-deterministic Turing Machine with access to # ​ P {\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}} oracle.

ZH calculus, circuit extraction,
\mathrm{\#P}
, computational complexity, oracle complexity

1
Introduction

Graphical representation of the quantum processes via string diagrams
[
1
]
is a quickly developing area with a new view on the most important problems of theoretical quantum computation. String diagrams offer a concise and precise representation of quantum processes and transformations they can undergo
[
2
]
. Graphical calculi such as ZX calculus
[
2
,
3
]
have been successfully used to tackle the circuit optimization
[
4
,
5
]
, error correction
[
6
]
, and many other problems. ZH calculus, introduced in
[
7
]
, has been applied when reasoning about measurement-based quantum computation
[
8
]
and circuits constructed with the Toffoli gate
[
9
]
. Normally, ZH features generators with a phase label that ranges from
0
to
2\pi
. The phase-free variant
[
10
]
offers a simple set of generators that guarantee universality and closely relate to circuits constructed with the Toffoli+H universal gate set, without arbitrary phases in spider generators.

The standard notation of quantum computation uses circuits. Rewrite rules for quantum circuits exist (for example, see
[
11
]
), but can get very complicated, while diagram rewriting rules are usually compact. In graphical calculi, a few generators are sufficient for universality, while the addition of appropriate rewrite rules may offer completeness for various parts of quantum mechanics
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
10
]
. Thus, one can often benefit from the translation of the quantum process to a string diagram. Typical uses of graphical calculi follow the same pattern. First, a circuit or other presentation of quantum computation like an MBQC scheme is translated to a graphical calculus. Next, the obtained diagram is modified via rewrite rules. Finally, a circuit is extracted from the new diagram. The circuit extraction, in general, is
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}
-hard
[
15
]
. The existence of various flow structures guarantees fast extraction
[
4
,
16
,
17
]
. Sometimes these properties can be preserved while rewriting diagrams
[
18
]
. Circuit extraction is vital, as circuits are the machine language for most existing physical quantum computers.

The most natural problem to ask about any model of computation is to say whether two computation schemes are equivalent. For graphical calculi, this problem is comparing diagrams:

\mathbf{CompareDiagrams}

Input:
two phase-free ZH diagrams
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
.

Output:

True
if and only if
\left\llbracket D_{1}\right\rrbracket=\left\llbracket D_{2}\right\rrbracket
.

One of the ways to check whether two diagrams represent the same quantum process is to attempt rewriting one to the other. Completeness of graphical calculi means, that if two diagrams represent the same process, one can be transformed to the other by a series of rewrites. For languages complete for quantum computation, the proof goes through exponential in size normal forms. It means that, concerning the current knowledge, the number of rewrites transforming one diagram to another not only can be exponential, but the diagrams on the way can be exponential as well. The best known upper bound for comparing diagrams is
\rm{coNP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}

[
15
]
.

The goal of the research is to establish a tight bound for comparing diagrams. The main motivations come from
[
15
]
, where an upper bound for comparing diagrams is used to bound circuit extraction from above with
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}}
. Further, the hardness of comparing diagrams would extend to the comparison of circuits with measurement post-selection and thus, it would connect to
\mathrm{PostBQP}
(Bounded-error Quantum Polynomial-time with Post-selection
[
19
]
) and
\mathrm{PP}
(Probabilistic Polynomial-time
[
20
]
) computation schemes. Another motivation was exploring the encoding of
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}
-hard problems as ZH diagrams, and to look for interesting completeness results.

In this work, we make progress towards the above goal. We show that two closely related problems, arising in phase-free ZH calculus
[
10
]
, are
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-complete. These are some of the first examples of
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-complete problems (other examples that do not directly relate to quantum can be found in
[
21
,
22
]
). The problems relate to comparing diagrams in the following way. Comparing diagrams asks that a certain property holds for all entries in the matrix representation, while the presented problems check whether there exists an entry satisfying the property.

The first of the problem mentioned above is:

\mathbf{StateEq}

Input:
a pair of phase-free ZH diagrams
D_{1},D_{2}
with matching number of dangling edges.

Output:

True
if and only if there exists a computational basis state
\left|v\right\rangle
such that
\left\llbracket D_{1}\right\rrbracket\left|v\right\rangle=\left\llbracket D_{2}\right\rrbracket\left|v\right\rangle
.

By interpreting a diagram with dangling edges as a quantum state, this problem can be viewed as checking whether there exists a choice of measurement outcomes (on the computational basis) that appears with the same probability for both quantum states given as diagrams. Another way to understand this problem is by checking whether matrix interpretations of given diagrams are equal in some positions.

The second problem is as follows, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1}{2}]
(dyadic rationals):

\mathbf{ContainsEntry_{k}}

Input:
a phase-free ZH diagram
D

Output:

True
if and only if
k
appears in the matrix interpretation of
D
.

In particular, when
k=0
, the problem can be interpreted as: given a diagram representing a quantum state, does there exist a measurement choice (on the computational basis) that happens with probability
0
? Our result also works when the number
k
is part of the input, rather than a fixed number on which the problem depends. Our main contribution is proving the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

\mathbf{StateEq}
and
\mathbf{ContainsEntry_{k}}
are both
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-complete.

We also give a construction showing
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}
-hardness of circuit extraction from phase-free ZH – the original paper
[
15
]
showing
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}
-hardness of circuit extraction did not work for the phase-free variant – the construction used in the proof required
\frac{\pi}{2}
phase spider, which does not exist in phase-free ZH.

\mathbf{CircuitExtraction}

Input:
a phase-free ZH diagram
D_{1}
proportional to a unitary and a (finitely presented) set of quantum gates
\mathcal{G}
.

Output:
a polynomial in size of
D_{1}
quantum circuit constructed with gates from
\mathcal{G}
representing a process proportional to
\left\llbracket D_{1}\right\rrbracket
or a message that no such circuit exists.

Theorem 1.2
.

\mathbf{CircuitExtraction}
is
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}
-hard.

Our proofs work for phase-free ZH calculus. These results also generalise to other graphical calculi, like ZX, ZH and ZW. This is because the phase-free ZH diagrams can always be represented in those other graphical calculi, and the translation from phase-free ZH to other calculi is polynomial in size.

Computational Complexity

We assume knowledge of Turing Machines and familiarity with standard classes such as
\mathrm{NP}
and the concept of oracle classes. The names of problems are written in bold text, while the names of classes are in standard, non-italic text. TM stands for Turing Machine and ND for non-deterministic.
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{Paths}(M,w)
for a NDTM
M
and its input
w\in\Sigma^{*}
stands for the total number of paths
M
runs on
w
. Similarly,
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{AccPaths}(M,w)
and
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{RejPaths}(M,w)
stand for the total number of respectively accepting and rejecting paths
M
has on
w
.
q_{ACC}
and
q_{REJ}
stand for the accepting and rejecting states of a TM respectively. Finally,
q_{ASK}
stands for the state used to communicate with the oracle, i.e. when an oracle TM enters its
q_{ASK}
state, then the oracle is called.

Some of the classes we work with are non-standard. For clarity, in the appendix
A
, we present all of the complexity classes that we use or mention.

We are going to talk about boolean formulae and their valuations. Boolean formulae are constructed with variables, two logical values
True
and
False
, and connectives
\wedge,\vee,\neg,\to,\leftrightarrow
. A valuation is a function mapping some variables to logical values
True
and
False
. For instance,
(x_{1}\vee x_{2})
under the valuation
(x_{1},False),(x_{2},True)
is
(False\vee True)=True
, and under the valuation
(x_{1},False)
it is
(False\vee x_{2})=x_{2}
.

Throughout, we use the following two problems, based on
\mathbf{SAT}
problem, the canonical
\mathrm{NP}
-complete problem
[
23
]
.

\mathbf{\#SAT}

Input:
a boolean formula
\phi
and the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
on which
\phi
is defined.

Output:
a number of satisfying assignments of
\phi
, i.e. number of
True/False
assignments to
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
for which
\phi
evaluates to
True
.

We will also use
\mathbf{\#SAT}
as the corresponding function mapping the formula and variables to the number of satisfying assignments. When the variables are explicit, for instance, all variables appearing in the formula and nothing else, we omit writing them. For instance:
\mathbf{\#SAT}((x_{1}\wedge x_{2})\wedge(x_{1}\wedge\neg x_{3}))=1
as the only satisfying assignment of
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
is
(True,True,False)
The
\mathbf{\#SAT}
problem is the canonical
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}
-complete problem
[
24
]
.

\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}

Input:
Two boolean formulae defined on
n
variables each:

•

\phi
on variables
X:=x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
,

•

\psi
on variables
Y:=y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
.

Output:

True
if and only if
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi,X)=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi,Y)
.

Similarly, we omit writing variables when they are explicit. For example, the answer for instance consisting of formulae
((x_{1}\wedge x_{2})\wedge(x_{1}\wedge\neg x_{3})
and
(y_{1}\vee y_{2}\vee y_{3})
is
False
, as
\mathbf{\#SAT}((x_{1}\wedge x_{2})\wedge(x_{1}\wedge\neg x_{3}))=1\neq 7=\mathbf{\#SAT}(y_{1}\vee y_{2}\vee y_{3})
. This problem is
\mathrm{C_{=}P}
-complete. While the proof follows immediately from the definitions of
\mathrm{C_{=}P}
and
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}
, we present it below, as we could not find any publications with the proof (or this problem definition).

Theorem 2.1
.

\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
is
\mathrm{C_{=}P}
-complete.

Proof.

Containment:

Define a NDTM that given an input
(\phi,\psi)
, where the formulae are on
n
variables each, runs
2^{n+1}
paths. It uses
2^{n}
paths to evaluate all possible assignments of
\phi
and returns
True
precisely for satisfying assignments of
\phi
. Similarly, on the other
2^{n}
paths it evaluates and returns
True
precisely for unsatisfying assignments of
\psi
. Thus, in total
M
accepts input on
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi)+\mathbf{\#SAT}(\neg\psi)=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi)+2^{n}-\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi)
paths and rejects on
2^{n}-\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi)+\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi)
paths. The two numbers equal precisely when
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi)=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi)
, as required.

Hardness:

Given a problem
A\in\mathrm{C_{=}P}
, let
M
be the corresponding NDTM. For any instance
w
for
A
, we use the Cook-Levin method
[
23
]
to construct a boolean formula
\phi
corresponding to running
M
on
w
. This construction is parsimonious (or at least it can be made parsimonious, check
[
25
]
), thus
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi)=\mathord{\#}\mathrm{AccPaths}(M,w)
. Similarly, we construct a boolean formula
\psi
corresponding to running
M^{\prime}
on
w
, where
M^{\prime}
is
M
with accepting and rejecting states flipped. Then, again by parsimony of the Cook-Levin method,
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi)=\mathord{\#}\mathrm{RejPaths}(M,w)
. We extend both formulae to
\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime}
that operate on the same number of variables, without changing the number of satisfying assignments (for instance, by appending a clause
(y_{1}\wedge y_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge y_{l})
where
y_{1},\dots,y_{l}
are newly introduced variables). Finally, the instance
w
of
A
is equivalent to
(\psi^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})
instance of
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
, as
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi^{\prime})=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow\mathord{\#}\mathrm{AccPaths}(M,w)=\mathord{\#}\mathrm{RejPaths}(M,w)
.
∎

The trouble of proving
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-hardness of
\mathbf{StateEq}
and
\mathbf{ContainsEntry_{k}}
comes from the lack of a simple
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-complete problem that can be used in reductions. Crafting such a problem is the most difficult part and we will dedicate it most of the proof section.

From now on, given a boolean formula
\phi
on some variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, its diagram representation is denoted
D_{\phi}
.
D_{\phi}
has
n
left dangling edges corresponding to the variables and one right dangling edge, corresponding to the valuation of
\phi
. Attaching
\left|0\right\rangle
s or
\left|1\right\rangle
s to the left dangling edges, results in the evaluation of the formula for some given assignment. However, we can also efficiently represent the number of satisfying assignments of a formula. By attaching white spiders to edges corresponding to the variables, we evaluate the diagram on the state
\sqrt{2}^{n}\left|+\dots+\right\rangle
, which equals the superposition of all states from computational basis, i.e. we evaluate the formula on all possible assignments simultaneously, as captured in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\phi
be a boolean formula on variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and
D_{\phi}
be the phase-free ZH encoding of
\phi
. The diagram obtained by attaching white spiders to the dangling edges corresponding to the variables has matrix representation
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi)\left|1\right\rangle+(2^{n}-\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi))\left|0\right\rangle
.

Proof.

We have:

\displaystyle\left\llbracket D_{\phi}\right\rrbracket(\sqrt{2}^{n}\left|+\dots+\right\rangle)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\{0,1\}}\left\llbracket D_{\phi}\right\rrbracket\left|v_{1}\dots v_{n}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\{0,1\}}\left|v(\phi)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi)\left|1\right\rangle+(2^{n}-\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi))\left|0\right\rangle

where
v
stands for the valuation of
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
mapping
x_{i}
to
True
when
v_{i}=1
and to
False
when
v_{i}=0
.
∎

In this section, we prove theorem

1
in brackets means that the oracle is called precisely once during the computation. In the third subsection, we craft an artificial

4.1
Containment

We need the following problem:

\mathbf{ScalarDiagram}

Input:
a phase-free ZH diagram
D
with no dangling edges (i.e. a scalar diagram).

Output:
the number
\left\llbracket D\right\rrbracket
.

It is known that this problem is in
\mathrm{FP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}

[
28
]
. A version that works for some tensor networks other than only phase-free ZH follows from the Holant framework
[
30
, page 212]
and
[
31
, Lemma 54]
. Yet another explanation was given in
[
15
]
, where authors used the method introduced in
[
32
]
to obtain a similar bound for circuits rather than a diagram, though as the authors point out the method works for diagrams as well. By pushing the deterministic polynomial time operations to instead be performed by the NDTM:
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{FP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}}\subseteq\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
. Hence, for the containment of
\mathbf{StateEq}
and
\mathbf{ContainsEntry_{k}}
in
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
, it suffices to show containment in
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathbf{ScalarDiagram}}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

The problem
\mathbf{StateEq}
is in
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
.

Proof.

We construct a polytime NDTM
M
with
\mathbf{ScalarDiagram}
oracle that takes input
(D_{1},D_{2})
and non-deterministically chooses a computational basis state
\left|v\right\rangle
. Next, using the oracle,
M
computes both
\left\llbracket D_{1}\right\rrbracket\left|v\right\rangle
and
\left\llbracket D_{2}\right\rrbracket\left|v\right\rangle
. Finally,
M
accepts if the two values are equal.
∎

For the second problem, we proceed similarly.

Theorem 4.2
.

The problem
\mathbf{ContainsEntry_{k}}
is in
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
.

Proof.

We construct a polytime NDTM
M
with
\mathbf{ScalarDiagram}
oracle that takes input
D
and non-deterministically chooses a computational basis state
\left|v\right\rangle
. Next, using the oracle,
M
computes
\left\llbracket D\right\rrbracket\left|v\right\rangle
. Finally,
M
accepts if the obtained value equals
k
.
∎

4.2
Hardness – oracle change

To simplify proofs of
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-hardness of
\mathbf{StateEq}
and
\mathbf{ContainsEntry_{k}}
, we use the following fact.

Theorem 4.3
.

\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}=\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
. Further, the equality holds if the answer to the single call to the
\mathrm{C_{=}P}
oracle is returned as an answer.

The idea is as follows: instead of calling
\mathbf{\#SAT}
oracle, a NDTM can non-deterministically choose the answer. Then, at the end of the computation, it can verify all answer choices with a single call to the
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
oracle.

To prove theorem
4.3
formally, we need a few additional lemmas.

Lemma 4.4

(Concat formulae)
.

Given two boolean formulae
\phi
and
\psi
, it is possible to deterministically construct a boolean formula
\rho
, such that:

•

\rho
can be constructed in time polynomial in the sizes of
\phi
and
\psi
,

•

\mathbf{\#SAT}(\rho)
written in binary is a concatenation of
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi)
and
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi)
written in binary, possibly with some additional leading zeros.

Proof.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be variables of
\phi
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
be variables of
\psi
. By substituting variables in one formula with new ones, we can assume that
x_{i}\neq y_{j}
for all
i,j
. We define
\rho
as a boolean formula on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{m},z,z^{\prime}
as follows:

\rho=(\phi\wedge z)\vee(x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{n}\wedge\psi\wedge\neg z\wedge z^{\prime})

Let:

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=x_{1},\dots,x_{n}

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=y_{1},\dots,y_{m}

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=z,z^{\prime}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=X,Y,Z

Then, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\#SAT}(\rho,A)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi\wedge z,A)+\mathbf{\#SAT}(x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{n}\wedge\psi\wedge\neg z\wedge z^{\prime},A)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi,X)\cdot 2^{m+1}+\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi,Y)

The first equality holds as
\rho
is a conjunction of two formulae that cannot be simultaneously satisfied due to the requirement of different valuations of
z
. The second equality follows from the fact, that
\phi\wedge z
is independent of valuations of
Y,z^{\prime}
. Thus, the last
m+1
digits of
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\rho,A)
in binary represent
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi,Y)
possibly with leading zeros, and the other first digits of
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\rho,A)
represent
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi,X)
.
∎

Lemma 4.5

(Concat
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
instances)
.

Let
(\phi,\psi)
and
(\zeta,\xi)
be two instances for problem
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
. They can be combined to a single instance for which the answer is
True
if and only if the answers are
True
for the two given instances.

Proof.

Let
\rho
be a formula constructed from
\phi
and
\zeta
in the previous lemma and
\tau
be a formula constructed from
\psi
and
\xi
. Then the instance
(\rho,\tau)
can be constructed in polytime and by construction:
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\rho)=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi)\cdot 2^{m+1}+\mathbf{\#SAT}(\zeta)
and
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\tau)=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi)\cdot 2^{m+1}+\mathbf{\#SAT}(\xi)
, where

is the number of variables of
\zeta
(and, thus, also of

and
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\xi)

\mathbf{\#SAT}(\rho)=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\tau)\Leftrightarrow(\mathbf{\#SAT}(\xi)=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\zeta))\wedge(\mathbf{\#SAT}(\phi)=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi))

∎

Another necessary construction is the creation of a boolean formula with a given number of satisfying assignments.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be variables and
k
a number in
[0..2^{n}]
. Then, it is possible to construct a boolean formula
\psi
on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
with
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi,(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))=k
.

Proof of lemma
4.6
.

Let
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
be the digits of
k
written in binary, possibly with leading zeros. Thus, we have
k=\sum_{i\in[0..n]}2^{n-i}\cdot a_{i}=\overline{(a_{0}\dots a_{n})}_{2}
. Now, define the following formula:

\displaystyle\psi:=\neg l(a_{0})\to(x_{1}\mathbin{{\wedge}\mkern-5.0mu{\vee}}_{a_{1}}(x_{2}\mathbin{{\wedge}\mkern-5.0mu{\vee}}_{a_{2}}\dots(x_{n-1}\mathbin{{\wedge}\mkern-5.0mu{\vee}}_{a_{n-1}}(x_{n}\wedge l(a_{n})))\dots))

where
\mathbin{{\wedge}\mkern-5.0mu{\vee}}_{0}=\wedge
and
\mathbin{{\wedge}\mkern-5.0mu{\vee}}_{1}=\vee
, and
l(0)=False
and
l(1)=True
.
We prove that
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\rho)=k
.

When
n=0
, then the formula simplifies to
\neg l(a_{0})\to l(a_{0})
, which is equivalent to
True\to False=False
for
a_{0}=0
and
False\to True=True
for
a_{0}=1
. So
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi)=a_{0}=k
.

When
a_{0}=1
, then
k\geq 2^{n}
. Since
k\in[0..2^{n}]
, we must have
k=2^{n}
. On the other hand, when
a_{0}=1
, then
\rho
simplifies to a tautology
False\to(\dots)
, and thus
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi)=2^{n}=k
, as required.

The remaining cases have
n\geq 1
and
a_{0}=0
(so
k<2^{n}
). Thus, we can ignore the “
\neg l(a_{0})\to
” part of the formula. For these cases, we proceed by induction on
n
.

When
n=1
, then
\psi
simplifies to
x_{1}\wedge l(a_{1})
, which has
a_{1}=\overline{(0a_{1})}_{2}=k
satisfiable assignments.

For the induction step, we assume that the thesis holds up to some
n-1
, and we prove it also holds for
n
. Let
\tau=x_{2}\mathbin{{\wedge}\mkern-5.0mu{\vee}}_{a_{2}}(x_{3}\dots\mathbin{{\wedge}\mkern-5.0mu{\vee}}_{a_{n-1}}(x_{k}\wedge l(a_{n}))\dots)
be the part of the formula after
x_{1}\mathbin{{\wedge}\mkern-5.0mu{\vee}}_{a_{1}}
. Thus, by induction hypothesis
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\tau,(x_{2},\dots,x_{n}))=\overline{(a_{2}\dots a_{n})}_{2}
.

When
a_{1}=0
, then
\psi=x_{1}\wedge\tau
and
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi,(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\tau,(x_{2},\dots,x_{n}))=\overline{(a_{2}\dots a_{n})}_{2}=\overline{(0a_{2}\dots a_{n})}_{2}=k
.

When
a_{1}=1
, then
\psi=x_{1}\vee\tau
. Since
x_{1}
does not appear in
\tau
, we have
\mathbf{\#SAT}(\psi,(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))=2^{n-1}+\mathbf{\#SAT}(\tau,(x_{2},\dots,x_{n}))=2^{n-1}+\overline{(a_{2}\dots a_{n})}_{2}=\overline{(1a_{2}\dots a_{n})}_{2}=k
, which ends the proof.
∎

Proof of theorem

.

We start by proving the right containment
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}\subseteq\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
.

\mathbf{\#SAT}
is complete for class
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}
, thus
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}=\mathrm{NP}^{\mathbf{\#SAT}}
. Let

be a problem in
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathbf{\#SAT}}
. It suffices to show that

. Let

be a polytime NDTM with access to the
\mathbf{\#SAT}
oracle that recognizes

. Define NDTM

as follows.
M^{\prime}
contains one extra tape over

and for all inputs

, the transition function of
M^{\prime}
matches that of
M
(ignoring the extra tape of
M^{\prime}
) for all configurations except for the three states: the oracle ask
q_{ASK}
, the accepting state
q_{ACC}
and the rejecting state
q_{REJ}
. Further,
M^{\prime}
has access
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
oracle rather than
\mathbf{\#SAT}
oracle.

When

enters the oracle query state

and sends
\phi,(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
to the
\mathbf{\#SAT}
oracle, then

instead non-deterministically chooses an answer

to the oracle query. Next, it creates a boolean formula
\psi
on

such that

by using the lemma

. Then,

\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}

on its extra tape. Finally,

would be in if it received the answer

from the oracle (except for the extra tape).

When

instead constructs two boolean formulae

such that

for all pairs

stored in the extra tape of

. Finally,

.

When

The NDTM

. Then,

The left containment

is immediate. Instead of sending

, a Turing Machine can ask for

and

∎

We could not find a shorter proof of the above fact. However, it is worth mentioning how a shorter proof could proceed. By corollary of Toda’s theorem
[
33
]
:
\mathrm{P}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}=\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{PP}}
and hence
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}=\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{PP}}
. Then it may be possible to adjust Torán’s work
[
21
, Corollary 3.13 and Theorem 4.1]
to obtain
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{PP}}=\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}}
. Finally,
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}}=\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
by, for instance, paring functions. We would skip the Turing Machines here, however, they are necessary in the remaining parts of the proof anyway.

Thanks to the above theorem, instead of showing
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-hardness, we can show
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
-hardness.

4.3
Hardness – crafting complete problem

We want to show
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-hardness. By theorem
4.3
, it is equivalent to showing
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
-hardness. There are no natural
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
-complete problems suitable for a reduction, so we craft an artificial
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
-complete problem
\mathbf{SAT\&Compare\#SAT}
.

\mathbf{SAT\&Compare\#SAT}

Input:
Natural numbers
n,m
and two boolean formulae:

•

\psi
, defined on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
,

•

\rho
, defined on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},z_{1},\dots,z_{m}

Output:

True
if and only if there exists valuation
v\colon\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\to\{True,False\}
such that:

\mathbf{\#SAT}(v(\psi),y_{1},\dots,y_{m})=\mathbf{\#SAT}(v(\rho),z_{1},\dots,z_{m})

Note, that in the problem definition,
v(\psi)
and
v(\rho)
are formulae on variables
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
and
z_{1},\dots,z_{m}
respectively.

While the definition of the problem is lengthy, we could argue that it is the most natural problem for
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
– it combines
\mathbf{SAT}
and
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
so the natural problems for
\mathrm{NP}
and
\mathrm{C_{=}P}
.

The other existing complete problems for
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}

[
21
,
22
]
are based on checking which answer to two
\mathbf{\#SAT}
instances is greater, or how to maximize an answer, rather than asking for exact equality. Comparison of which value is greater likely cannot be directly represented in phase-free ZH.

Example 4.8
.

Let
n=1,m=2
and:

•

\psi=x_{1}\vee y_{1}\vee\neg y_{2}
,

•

\rho=\neg(z_{1}\wedge(z_{2}\vee x_{1}))
.

Then, under valuation
v
mapping
x_{1}
to
False
, we have:

\displaystyle v(\psi)

\displaystyle=False\vee y_{1}\vee\neg y_{2}=y_{1}\vee\neg y_{2}

\displaystyle v(\rho)

\displaystyle=\neg(z_{1}\wedge(z_{2}\vee False))=\neg(z_{1}\wedge z_{2})=\neg z_{1}\vee\neg z_{2}

Since:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\#SAT}(v(\psi))=\mathbf{\#SAT}(y_{1}\vee\neg y_{2})=3=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\neg z_{1}\vee\neg z_{2})=\mathbf{\#SAT}(v(\rho))

the answer to such an instance of
\mathbf{SAT\&Compare\#SAT}
is
True
. For valuation
v^{\prime}
mapping
x_{1}
to

, the two values do not equalize:

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(\psi)

\displaystyle=True\vee y_{1}\vee\neg y_{2}=True

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\neg(z_{1}\wedge(z_{2}\vee True))=\neg(z_{1}\wedge True)=\neg z_{1}

and:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\#SAT}(v^{\prime}(\phi),y_{1},y_{2})=\mathbf{\#SAT}(True,y_{1},y_{2})=4\neq 2=\mathbf{\#SAT}((\neg z_{1}),z_{1},z_{2})=\mathbf{\#SAT}(v^{\prime}(\rho),z_{1},z_{2}).

The rest of this subsection is dedicated to showing the
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
-completeness of the above problem. The proof involves typical transformations of Turing machines. The next part of the proof is in the subsection
4.4
.

We put a plethora of constraints on TMs for problems in
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
. One of these constraints describes how a TM should represent a boolean formula used in the query. We want TMs to work solely with
0
and

Definition 4.9

(CNF)
.

A boolean formula on

is in

(

) if it is a conjunction of clauses, where each clause is a disjunction of some of the literals

.

Definition 4.10

(

.

Let

be a boolean formula in CNF on variables

. We define

as a word

, defined as follows, where

:

•

,

•

Extend
\phi
with
k-m
clauses containing literals
x_{1}
and
\neg x_{1}
,

•

Extend each clause with literals
x_{n+1},\dots,x_{k}

•

Translate each clause

of
\phi
to
2k

\{0,1\}
, where symbols at the positions

and

correspond to variable

for

as follows:

–

The first symbol is

when

does not contain literal

and

otherwise,

–

The second symbol is
0
when
c_{j}
does not contain literal
\neg x_{i}
and
1
otherwise.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
\Phi

be variables on which it is considered. Then:

\mathbf{\#SAT}(\Phi,(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\textsc{Formula}(e_{\Phi}),(x_{1},\dots,x_{\max(n,m)}))

Proof.

Let
k=\max(m,n)
. Consider steps in the construction of

:

•

Extension of
\Phi
by the clauses containing literals
x_{1}
and
\neg x_{1}
does not change satisfiable assignments – any assignment satisfies such clauses,

•

Extension of set of variables to
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
, while extending each clause with literals
x_{n+1},\dots,x_{k}
does not change number of satisfiable assignments – the only satisfiable assignments after the extension are those with
x_{n+1},\dots,x_{k}
set to
True

x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
set to satisfiable assignment of the original
\Phi
.

Let
\Psi
be the formula

\mathbf{\#SAT}(\Phi,(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\Psi,(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}))

The encoding of literals preserves the satisfiable assignments when the number of clauses equals the number of variables. Therefore:
\textsc{Formula}(e_{\Psi})=\Psi
. By construction
e_{\Psi}=e_{\Phi}
and thus the thesis follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\#SAT}(\textsc{Formula}(e_{\Phi}),(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\textsc{Formula}(e_{\Psi}),(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\Psi,(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\#SAT}(\Phi,(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))

We can now put constraints on a TM for some

.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
A\in\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
. Then, there exists a polytime NDTM
\mathcal{M}
with access to
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
oracle, that recognized
A
and satisfies the following conditions:

1.

\mathcal{M}
calls the oracle
A
precisely once, at the very end of the computation, and
\mathcal{M}
returns the oracle answer,

2.

the boolean formulae that
\mathcal{M}
sends to the oracle are all in CNF,

3.

\mathcal{M}
has two dedicated tapes used for communication with the oracle. When
\mathcal{M}
enters its
q_{ASK}
state to ask an
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
query
(\zeta,\xi)
, then on the first tape it contains a representation of the first boolean formula
\zeta
as a sequence of zeros and ones
e_{\zeta}
, and on the second tape it contains a representation
e_{\xi}
of
\xi
,

4.

there exists a polynomial
p
such that for any input
w
, when
\mathcal{M}
calls the oracle with
(\Phi,\Psi)
query then
|e_{\Phi}|=|e_{\Psi}|=p(|w|)
. In other words, the size of the query depends only on the size of the input, not on the input itself.

5.

there exist a polynomial
q
such that for any input
w
,
\mathcal{M}
runs in precisely
q(|w|)
steps.

Proof.

By theorem
4.3
, there exists polytime NDTM
M
with access to
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
oracle satisfying the first condition.

Since every boolean formula can be transformed into CNF in polynomial time, we can assume that
M
also satisfies the second condition.

The third condition is just a formal description of how the machine should communicate with the oracle. By theorem
4.14
, the encoding of the formula does not alter the number of satisfying assignments, and thus we can assume that
M
further satisfies the third condition.

The fourth condition is more involved. Let
p_{M}
be the polynomial bounding number of steps of
M
, i.e. for input
w
,
M
terminates in at most
p_{M}(|w|)
steps. Define
M^{\prime}
as a TM that follows
M
, however, when
M
enters
q_{ASK}
state,
M^{\prime}
instead changes the contents of oracle tapes: the boolean formulae are transformed to have encoding sizes
2p_{M}(|w|)^{2}
each – via the same procedure as in the construction of the encoding, we add clauses and variables to have
p_{M}(|w|)
of each.

Therefore,
M^{\prime}
satisfies the first three conditions, as
M
does, and it satisfies the fourth condition with
p=2p_{M}^{2}
. Further,
M^{\prime}
is polytime, as
M
is, and extra steps of
M^{\prime}
take at most
O(p_{M}(|w|))
steps. Let
p_{M^{\prime}}
be the polynomial bounding number of steps of
M^{\prime}
, i.e. for input
w
,
M^{\prime}
terminates in at most
p_{M^{\prime}}(|w|)
steps.

Finally, we define
\mathcal{M}
as a TM that follows
M^{\prime}
. However, if
M^{\prime}
enters
q_{ASK}
state, then
\mathcal{M}
stalls for some number of steps so that it always calls the oracle in the
q(|w|)
step, where
q
is some polynomial. This can be achieved, for example, by equipping
\mathcal{M}
with an extra ‘counter’ tape on which it initially fills
p_{M^{\prime}}(|w|)
cells with
1
. Then,
\mathcal{M}
starts computation, following
M^{\prime}
and removing
1
cell from the counter during each step. When the oracle should be called,
\mathcal{M^{\prime}}
first continues to clear the counter and only then calls the oracle. Note that
p_{M^{\prime}}(|w|)
can be computed based on the input size but not the input itself. Thus, the counter creation and clearance require the number of steps depending only on
|w|
but not
w
. Therefore, the total number of steps on input
w
is indeed given by some
q(|w|)
.
∎

We can now prove that the crafted problem is
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
-complete.

Theorem 4.16
.

\mathbf{SAT\&Compare\#SAT}
is
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
-complete.

Proof.

Containment:
given an instance
(n,m,\psi,\rho)
of
\mathbf{SAT\&Compare\#SAT}
, a NDTM with
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
oracle can do the following:

•

Non-deterministically choose a valuation of
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
,

•

Call the oracle on
(v(\psi),v(\rho))
and returns its answer.

Hardness:

-complete problem that could be used for reduction. To define such a problem, we showed that
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}=\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
and proceeded in a way similar to the proof of the Cook-Levin theorem.

6.1
Comparing Diagrams

Our results could suggest that comparing diagrams should be
\rm{coNP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-complete. At the same time, comparing quantum circuits is simpler, in the sense that upper bounds are tighter. Determining whether a quantum circuit is almost equivalent to identity is
\rm{QMA}
-complete
[
34
]
(Quantum Merlin-Arthur
[
35
]
), while an exact non-identity check is
\mathrm{NQP}
-complete
[
36
]
(Nondeterministic Quantum Polynomial-time
[
32
]
). Both these classes are within
\mathrm{PP}
, and thus within
\mathrm{P}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
. This way, graphical calculi like phase-free ZH can be understood as more powerful than quantum circuits.

The problems we studied closely relate to problems
\mathbf{CompareDiagrams}
and the following problem:

\mathbf{IsZero}

Input:
a phase-free ZH diagram
D
.

Output:

True
if and only if
\left\llbracket D\right\rrbracket
is the zero matrix.

The two problems defined above are essential. Not only the question
“Are two diagrams equal”
is the most natural question one can ask about diagrams, but the problem
\mathbf{CompareDiagrams}
was indirectly used to obtain the upper bound of
\mathbf{CircuitExtraction}
in
[
15
]
, i.e.
\mathbf{CircuitExtraction}
is in
\mathrm{FNP}^{\mathbf{CompareDiagrams}}
.

These problems are very similar to the two problems we have presented.
\mathbf{StateEq}
asks whether the two matrix representations agree
somewhere
, while
\mathbf{CompareDiagrams}
asks whether the two matrices agree
everywhere
. Similarly,
\mathbf{ContainsEntry}\mathbf{{}_{0}}
asks whether the matrix agrees with zero matrix
somewhere
, and
\mathbf{IsZero}
asks for agreement
everywhere
. The problems defined here are therefore asking whether a property is
universal
rather than
existential
. The upper bounds for these problems follow a similar idea as the upper bounds for
\mathbf{StateEq}
and
\mathbf{ContainsEntry}
. A NDTM can pick an entry, and using the
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}
oracle it accepts when the property holds. It leads to upper bounds
\mathrm{coNP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
.

However, the same hardness proof does not work. The main trick in our hardness proof was that an NDTM with
\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}
oracle could instead guess answers to the
\mathbf{\#SAT}
instances and verify them at the end with a single
\mathrm{C_{=}P}
oracle query. However, the transformation creates lots of new non-deterministic paths on which NDTM chooses at least one answer incorrectly. Informally, there is no method in ZH to
use
answer to the question of whether boolean formulae have different numbers of satisfying assignments or to verify that two amplitudes are different. Similarly, it is unclear whether
\mathrm{coNP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
must equal to
\mathrm{coNP}^{\mathrm{C_{=}P}[1]}
if for the second class we demand an oracle answer to be returned as the answer to the entire computation.

It would be interesting to research whether
\mathbf{CompareDiagrams}
and
\mathbf{IsZero}
are
\mathrm{coNP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-complete, or at least obtain hardness better than
\mathrm{C_{=}P}
. Such immediate hardness can be obtained by reducing
\phi,\psi
instance of
\mathbf{Compare\#SAT}
to diagrams
D_{\phi},D_{\psi}
with white spiders attached to all dangling edges. One of the possible avenues is to pay more attention to known complexity results, such as
\mathrm{NQP}=\mathrm{coC_{=}P}

[
37
]
, and use
\mathrm{NQP}
oracle instead of
\mathrm{C_{=}P}
.

6.2
Tensor networks and complexity classes

When proving the hardness of
\mathbf{StateEq}
and
\mathbf{ContainsEntry_{k}}
, we touched on the equivalent problems defined for tensors instead of diagrams. It may be interesting to classify complexity bounds for tensors constructed with different generator sets. Tensors required for phase-free ZH are sufficient for hardness results. In particular, generators of phase-free ZH can all be achieved in other related graphical calculi, like ZX. The upper bound is a bit more tricky. For instance, whether the presented problems are
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
-complete for ZX diagrams depends on the allowed phases and presentations of such phases in the input. The infinite number of generators can lead to
\mathbf{ScalarDiagram}
not being in
\mathrm{FP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
, and hence the
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
can also fail.

Besides tensor networks, there is an interesting connection to the class
\mathrm{PostBQP}
, i.e. quantum computation with post-selection of measurement outcomes. Graphical calculi can be viewed as circuits with post-selection of measurement outcomes, or as quantum circuits where arbitrary linear maps rather than unitaries can be used as gates. Thus, diagrams represent computation schemes for the class
\mathrm{PostBQP}
. Further, it is known that
\mathrm{PostBQP}=\mathrm{PP}
and hence
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{PostBQP}}=\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{PP}}=\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
, where the last equality follows from Toda’s theorem
[
33
]
. It would be interesting to see whether the approach of interpreting diagrams as
\mathrm{PostBQP}
computations could lead to new complexity bounds for problems appearing when working with graphical calculi. Further, the study of the class
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathrm{PostBQP}}
could lead to new observations about the equal
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
. The problem
\mathbf{SAT\&Compare\#SAT}
we crafted can be viewed as a new natural complete problem for the mostly unexplored class
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
.

6.3
Counting complexity

In
[
27
]
, phase-free ZH was successfully used to develop a new algorithm for a variant of
\mathbf{\#SAT}
with small clause density, beating other existing algorithms. Another connection of phase-free ZH with counting complexity was demonstrated in
[
28
]
, where ZH rewriting rules simplified proofs of known results from counting complexity. However, only scalar diagrams were used. It would be interesting to check whether the inclusion of dangling edges may be used for other counting complexity proofs, for instance, those involving
\mathrm{NP}^{\mathord{\#}\mathrm{P}}
.
