Abstract




Abstract.

We develop an anomaly-detection method when systematic anomalies, possibly statistically very similar to genuine inputs, are affecting control systems at the input and/or output stages. The method allows anomaly-free inputs (i.e., those before contamination) to originate from a wide class of random sequences, thus opening up possibilities for diverse applications. To illustrate how the method works on data, and how to interpret its results and make decisions, we analyze several actual time series, which are originally non-stationary but in the process of analysis are converted into stationary. As a further illustration, we provide a controlled experiment with anomaly-free inputs following an ARMA time series model under various contamination scenarios.

Key words and phrases
: control systems, anomaly detection, systematic errors, time series.

Computer systems monitor and control a myriad of physical processes, and their protection against random errors, deliberate intrusions

For complementary reviews on anomaly detection, we refer to

The emphasis in the present paper is on temporal aspects and dependence structures that arise in this area of research. There have been a number of studies tackling these issues from several perspectives. For example,
Barahona and Poon 1996
present a computational procedure capable of robust and sensitive statistical detection of deterministic and chaotic dynamics in short and noisy time series.
Hu et al. 2017
explore the role of dependence when assessing quantities such as system-compromise probabilities and the cost of attacks, which are then used to develop optimization strategies.
Dasgupta and Li 2018
tackle the problem of assessing whether temporal clusters in randomly occurring sequences of events are genuinely random.

Furthermore,
Fisch et al. 2018
propose what is called the collective and point anomalies (CAPA) method for detecting point anomalies (i.e., outliers in the statistical language) as well as anomalous segments, or collective anomalies. The method is suitable when collective anomalies are characterised by either a change in mean, variance, or both, ant it is capable of distinguishing collective anomalies from point anomalies. This and several other methods have been implemented in an R package by
Fisch et al. 2020b
, where we also find
the multi-variate collective and point anomaly (MVCAPA) method of
Fisch et al. 2019

and also sequential versions of CAPA and MVCAPA by
Fisch et al. 2020a

Fisch 2020
provides the state of the art on statistical anomaly detection, together with a guide for computational implementation.

The present paper is devoted to another anomaly-detection method that works irrespective of whether systems are being affected at the input or output stage, or at both stages simultaneously.
The important feature that distinguishes our method from the earlier ones is that it can detect persistent anomalies that may not change the regime (e.g., mean, variance, and/or autocorrelations) of data in an abrupt fashion during the period of observation. Hence, those statistical techniques that have been designed to detect outliers and other aberrations become ineffective in such situations.

As in many previous studies, we also consider dependent random inputs and, in turn, dependent outputs. This enables us to use the method in a myriad of applications. We have carefully proven the underlying theoretical results and illustrated the method using stationary time series under various contamination by anomalies scenarios. It is useful to recall at this point that historical data as well as subject-matter knowledge are helpful in deciding how to reduce non-stationary random sequences to stationary ones, and transformations such as differencing and de-periodization can especially be helpful
(

Brockwell and Davis 1991
, see, e.g.,)

The departure from the earlier explored by

2
Setting the stage: basic notation

Throughout the paper, we assume the existence of a function
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{1+d}\to\mathbb{R}
, called transfer function, that connects inputs
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
and outputs
Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
via the equation

(2.1)

Y_{t}=h(X_{t},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}),

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
d
-dimensional exogenous random variables, called anomalies. They can of course be equal to
\mathbf{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, meaning that the system is free of anomalies. In this case the transfer function reduces to
h_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

h_{0}(x)=h(x,\mathbf{0}),

which we call the baseline function. When we wish to emphasize that outputs
Y_{t}
arise from this anomaly-free case, we use the notation

(2.2)

Y^{0}_{t}=h_{0}(X_{t}).

To illustrate, let
d=2
, in which case we have
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}=(\varepsilon_{1,t},\varepsilon_{2,t})
. We may think of
\varepsilon_{1,t}
as anomalies affecting the inputs
X_{t}
before they enter the control system, and
\varepsilon_{2,t}
as anomalies affecting the (already affected) inputs when they exit the system. Thinking in this fashion, we arrive at the following transfer functions
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
, which we use in our numerical experiment later in the paper:

TF1:

h(x,y,0)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=h_{0}(x+y)
when the system is affected by anomalies only at the input stage;

TF2:

h(x,0,z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=h_{0}(x)+z
when the system is affected by anomalies only at the output stage;

TF3:

h(x,y,z)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=h_{0}(x+y)+z
when the system is affected by anomalies at the input and output stages.

Besides the additive model, there are other models and thus other transfer functions that link inputs with exogenous variables
(
Finkelshtain et al. 1999
;
Franke et al. 2006
;
Franke et al. 2011
;
Guo et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. Arguments in favour of using one model over another can be found in studies by, e.g.,
Perote et al. 2015
,
Su 2016
,
Semenikhine et al. 2018
,
Guo et al. 2018
, and
Guo et al. 2019
.

Model (
2.1
) arises in many areas, including regression analysis, classification, and, generally, in machine learning
(
Hastie et al. 2009
, e.g.,)
. It also relates our research to the so-called strategy-proof estimation in regression
(
Perote and Perote-Peña 2004
;
Perote et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
.

Note 2.1
.

Visually, model (
2.1
) may give the impression that the outputs depend only on the current value of inputs, but
X_{t}
, at least in the case of causal time series, is a linear combination of the contemporary and historical values of the underlying white noise. That is,
X_{t}
is the inner product
\langle\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{Z}_{t}\rangle
of a (finite or infinite) sequence
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{i})_{i\geq 0}
of parameters and a (finite or infinite) sequence
\mathbf{Z}_{t}=(Z_{t-i})_{i\geq 0}
of uncorrelated random variables. We shall elaborate more on this topic in Section
9
, in the context of potential future work.

Although the function
h
might be known to, e.g., the control system’s manufacturer, its precise formula may not be known to those working in the area of anomaly detection (e.g., company’s IT personnel). The transfer function might even deviate from its original specifications due to, e.g., wear and tear. Furthermore, in the context of, say, economic variables, which we shall encounter in the next section, their relationships might be postulated by academics but in actuality, the true relationships (i.e., the transfer mechanisms from one to another) usually deviate from any model. In addition, the relationships might be, and usually are, affected by exogenous economic and other variables. Hence, to accommodate various scenarios associated with model uncertainty, we shall aim at deriving results for very large classes of transfer functions, that is, under very mild assumptions.

3
Two actual illustrations

This section is devoted to two real-world illustrations, which make up the contents of the following two subsections. The illustrations are based on pairs
(X_{t},Y_{t})
of economic variables, which are observed only for
1\leq t\leq n
for some sample sizes
n
. The inputs and outputs are dependent. With some luck, one of these variables can be assessed from the values of another variable, although not precisely because the transfer mechanism (i.e., the transfer function
h
) is not known, except of course in academic models. This, however, is not of concern to us because our primary interest is in finding out whether exogenous economic or other variables are systematically affecting the relationship between
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
. That is, we want to answer the following question:

Question 3.1
.

Are there
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
’s in model (
2.1
)?

At this point, we may instinctively start to debate as to the extent of smoothing of the scatterplot
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
, assuming that we want to do it: extreme undersmoothing would result in a wiggly function
h
with no errors
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
, whereas too much smoothing would result in a nice function
h
but with large errors
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
.
Hence, the researcher’s subjectively chosen level of smoothing determines whether or not there are errors in the model, and how large they are. We therefore do not do any smoothing. Our task is to find out if the hypothetical transfer function from one economic variable to another is affected by exogenous systematic variables, whatever they might be.

5
Anomaly detection: a foundation

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be the observable part of a stationary sequence of inputs
X_{t}
, whose marginal cumulative distribution functions (cdf’s) are the same; we denote them by
F
. With these observable inputs, there are associated outputs
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
, and so we are dealing with the random input-output pairs
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
. Based on them, we wish to determine whether the system transferring the inputs into the outputs is functioning as intended or is systematically affected by anomalies. To successfully tackle this problem, we first need to rigorously define (dis)orderly systems.

Let the cdf
F
be continuous, which allows us without loss of generality to state that all the inputs
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are different. Hence, their order statistics

X_{1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}<X_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}<\cdots<X_{n\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}

are strictly increasing. This facilitates unambiguous definition of the concomitants of the outputs
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
, which are denoted by
Y_{1,n},\dots,Y_{n,n}
and defined by the equation

Y_{t,n}=\sum_{s=1}^{n}Y_{s}\mathds{1}\{X_{s}=X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\},

where
\mathds{1}
is the indicator: it is equal to
1
when the condition
X_{s}=X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
is satisfied and
0
otherwise. We are now in the position to define (dis)orderly systems.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that the outputs and thus the system are
in
p
-reasonable order
with respect to the inputs for some
p>0
if

B_{n,p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={1\over n^{1/p}}\sum_{t=2}^{n}|Y_{t,n}-Y_{t-1,n}|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

when
n\to\infty
. If, however,
B_{n,p}\to_{\mathbb{P}}\infty
,
then we say that the outputs and thus the system are
out of
p
-reasonable order
with respect to the inputs.

Although this definition is a technicality that is necessary for our anomaly-detection method, it is also natural from the practical point of view. Indeed, detection of anomalies in disorderly systems can hardly be a task worth pursuing. As to the parameter
p
, its role in Definition
5.1
is to control tail heaviness of the outputs, and we shall later see that this is achieved by controlling tail heaviness of the inputs. Roughly speaking, we can view
p
as the order of finite moments. Note that when the outputs are in
p
-reasonable order, the outputs are in
r
-reasonable order for all
r\leq p
. On the other hand, if the outputs are out of
p
-reasonable order, the outputs are out of
r
-reasonable order for all
r\geq p
. Hence, we can say that for any given system, there is a threshold
p
delineating the sets of in-order and out-of-order outputs.

To successfully detect anomalies affecting a system, we of course need to know that the brand new system was in orderly state. For a rigorous definition of the latter notion, we slightly adjust Definition
5.1
as follows.

Definition 5.2
.

The anomaly-free outputs and thus the anomaly-free system are
in
p
-reasonable order
with respect to the inputs for some
p>0
if

B^{0}_{n,p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={1\over n^{1/p}}\sum_{t=2}^{n}|Y^{0}_{t,n}-Y^{0}_{t-1,n}|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

when
n\to\infty
. (For obvious reasons, we do not consider systems that are out of order when they are free of anomalies.)

To illustrate the anomaly-free case, that is, when all
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
’s are equal to
\mathbf{0}
, if all the inputs happen to be equal to the same constant, say
c
, then the system is in
p
-reasonable order for every
p>0
, because
B^{0}_{n,p}=0
. More generally, next Theorem
5.1
will show that if the transfer function
h
is sufficiently smooth (e.g., Lipschitz continuous), then the system is in
p
-reasonable order for some
p>0
even when the inputs are random, although not too heavy tailed. We need to introduce additional notation before we can formulate the theorem.

Let
a_{X}
and
b_{X}
be the endpoints of the support of the cdf
F
, that is,

\displaystyle a_{X}

\displaystyle=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}F(x)=0\},

\displaystyle b_{X}

\displaystyle=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}F(x)=1\}.

These endpoints can of course be infinite, but they never coincide because the cdf
F
is assumed to be continuous. Therefore, the open interval
(a_{X},b_{X})
is never empty.

Next, we recall that
h_{0}
is called absolutely continuous if there is a function
h^{*}_{0}
, called the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
h_{0}
, that satisfies the equation

h_{0}(v)-h_{0}(u)=\int_{u}^{v}h^{*}_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x

for all
u\leq v
. Now we are ready to formulate the theorem that describes the circumstances under which the anomaly-free system is orderly.

Theorem 5.1
.

The anomaly-free outputs are in
p
-reasonable order with respect to the inputs for some
p\geq 1
if there is
\alpha\in[1,p]
such that
\mathbb{E}(|X_{1}|^{p/\alpha})<\infty
and one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

If
\alpha=1
, then the baseline function
h_{0}
is Lipschitz continuous, that is, there is a constant
K\geq 0
such that, for all
x,y\in[a_{X},b_{X}]
,

|h_{0}(x)-h_{0}(y)|\leq K|x-y|.

(ii)

If
\alpha>1
, then the baseline function
h_{0}

Next are two facts (to be proven later) upon which we base our anomaly-detection method:

Fact 1:

If the anomaly-free outputs are in

(5.1)

Fact 2:

If, due to anomalies, the outputs are out of

-reasonable order with respect to the inputs, then (details in Section

(5.2)

I_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\sum_{i=2}^{n}(Y_{i,n}-Y_{i-1,n})_{+}\over\sum_{i=2}^{n}|Y_{i,n}-Y_{i-1,n}|}

7
Anomaly-free systems

In this section we specify conditions under which the index

z_{+}
of any real number

can be expressed as

, the index
I^{0}_{n}
can be re-written as

(7.1)

I^{0}_{n}={1\over 2}\bigg(1+{Y^{0}_{n,n}-Y^{0}_{1,n}\over\sum_{i=2}^{n}|Y^{0}_{i,n}-Y^{0}_{i-1,n}|}\bigg).

Hence, our goal becomes to understand when and where the numerator and the denominator of the ratio

(7.2)

\Lambda_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={Y^{0}_{n,n}-Y^{0}_{1,n}\over\sum_{i=2}^{n}|Y^{0}_{i,n}-Y^{0}_{i-1,n}|}

7.1
Asymptotics of the
\Lambda_{n}
numerator

We start with a definition.

Definition 7.1
.

We say that the inputs
X_{t}
having the same continuous marginal cdf
F
are
temperately dependent
if, for every
x\in(a_{X},b_{X})
and when
n\to\infty
,

(7.3)

\mathbb{P}(X_{1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\geq x)\to 0\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(X_{n\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\leq x)\to 0.

A few clarifying notes follow. First, the open interval
(a_{X},b_{X})
is not empty because the cdf
F
is continuous. Second, our use of the term
temperately dependent
is natural because property (
7.3
) is simultaneously related to
1
-minimally and
n
-maximally dependent random variables in the terminology used by
Gascuel and Caraux 1992
. To clarify, consider two extreme cases:

•

If
X_{t}
is a sequence of iid random variables, then

\mathbb{P}(X_{1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\geq x)=(1-F(x-))^{n}

and

\mathbb{P}(X_{n\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\leq x)=F(x)^{n}
, where
x\mapsto F(x-)
is the left-continuous version of
F
. Since for all
x\in(a_{X},b_{X})
, both
F(x)
and
F(x-)
are in the interval
(0,1)
, property (
7.3
) holds.

•

If
X_{t}
is a sequence of super-dependent random variables,
that is, if there is a random variable
X
such that
X_{t}=X
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
X_{1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}=X=X_{n\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
and so neither of the two probabilities in property (
7.3
) converges to
0
.

Finally, we note that the concept of temperate dependence is closely related to the existence of phantom distributions, which originate from the work of
O’Brien 1987
. For further details, examples, and extensive references on this topic, we refer to
Jakubowski 1991
,
Bradley 2007
, and
Doukhan et al 2015
. Phantom distributions for non-stationary random sequences have been tackled by
Jakubowski 1993
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let the inputs
X_{t}
be strictly stationary and temperately dependent. If the baseline function
h_{0}
is absolutely continuous on the interval
[a_{X},b_{X}]
and its Radon-Nikodym derivative
h_{0}^{*}
is integrable on
[a_{X},b_{X}]
, then

(7.4)

Y^{0}_{n,n}-Y^{0}_{1,n}\to_{\mathbb{P}}h_{0}(b_{X})-h_{0}(a_{X})

when
n\to\infty
.

Recall that a strictly stationary time series
X_{t}
is
\alpha
-mixing (i.e., strongly mixing) if

\alpha_{X}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup\big|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)\big|\to 0

when
t\to\infty
, with the supremum taken over all
A\in\mathscr{F}^{0}_{-\infty}
and
B\in\mathscr{F}^{\infty}_{t}
, where the two
\sigma
-algebras are defined as follows:

\mathscr{F}^{0}_{-\infty}=\sigma(X_{u},u\leq 0)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathscr{F}^{\infty}_{k}=\sigma(X_{v},v\geq t).

We refer to
Lin and Lu 1996
,
Bradley 2007
, and
Rio 2017
for details and references on various notions of mixing. In the context of the present paper, a particularly important random sequence is the strictly stationary ARMA(
p,q
) time series
X_{t}
. We refer to
Mokkadem 1988
who has shown, among other things, that a strictly stationary ARMA time series is
\beta
-mixing (i.e., completely regular) and thus
\alpha
-mixing (i.e., strongly mixing).

Theorem 7.2
.

If the inputs
X_{t}
are strictly stationary and
\alpha
-mixing, then they are temperately dependent.

The proofs of Theorems
7.1
and
7.2
are in Appendix
B
.

7.2
Asymptotics of the
\Lambda_{n}
denominator

We start with a definition, which is a weak (i.e., in probability) form of the classical Glivenko-Cantelli theorem.

Definition 7.2
.

We say that the inputs
X_{t}
having the same marginal cdf
F
satisfy the Glivenko-Cantelli property if

(7.5)

\|F_{n}-F\|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|F_{n}(x)-F(x)\big|\to_{\mathbb{P}}0,

where
F_{n}
is the empirical cdf based on the random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
.

The classical Glivenko-Cantelli theorem says that statement (
7.5
) (with convergence in probability replaced by almost surely) holds for iid random sequences. Establishing the Glivenko-Cantelli property for dependent sequences has been a challenging but fruitful task. In particular, results by
Cai and Roussas 1992

and
Rio 2017
tell us that if a strictly stationary sequence
X_{t}
is
\alpha
-mixing and there exists a constant
\nu>0
such that

(7.6)

\alpha_{X}(t)=O(t^{-\nu})

when
t\to\infty
, then the Glivenko-Cantelli property holds.

Consider now the stationary
\text{ARMA}(p,q)
time series
X_{t}
that follows the dynamical model

\sum^{p}_{i=0}\phi_{i}X_{t-i}=\sum^{q}_{j=0}\theta_{j}\eta_{t-j},\quad t\in\mathbb{Z},

with
\phi_{0}=1
and some parameters
\phi_{i},\theta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
such that the absolute values of all the roots of the characteristic polynomial
z\mapsto\sum^{p}_{i=0}\phi_{i}z^{i}
are (strictly) greater than
1
. Hence, the time series is causal.

Note 7.1
.

There is a clash of notation between the
p
in
\text{ARMA}(p,q)
and the
p
in the earlier introduced definition of
p
-reasonable order. The two
p
’s are unrelated, and we do not expect them to cause any confusion. We have simply run out of different notation, especially given the deeply rooted traditions in the literature, such as those in time series analysis.

Mokkadem 1988
has proved that if the white noise sequence
\eta_{t}
is iid with absolutely continuous (with respect to the Lebesgue measure) marginal distributions, then the time series
X_{t}
is geometrically completely regular. That is, there exists
\rho\in(0,1)
such that

(7.7)

\beta_{X}(t)=O(\rho^{t})

when
t\to\infty
, where
\beta_{X}(t)
is the complete regularity coefficient
(
Davydov 1973
)
defined by

\beta_{X}(t)=\mathbb{E}\Big(\sup\big|\mathbb{P}(B\mid\mathscr{F}^{0}_{-\infty})-\mathbb{P}(B)\big|\Big),

where the supremum is taken over all
B\in\mathscr{F}^{\infty}_{t}
.
As noted by
Mokkadem 1988
, the bound

\alpha_{X}(t)\leq\beta_{X}(t)

holds, and thus statement (
7.7
) implies (
7.6
) for any
\nu>0
, which in turn establishes the Glivenko-Cantelli property for the sequence
X_{t}
.

We are now in the position to formulate the main result of this subsection concerning the denominator on the right-hand side of equation (
7.1
).

Theorem 7.3
.

Let the inputs
X_{t}
be strictly stationary, temperately dependent, satisfy the Glivenko-Cantelli property, and have finite
p
-th moments
\mathbb{E}(|X_{t}|^{p})<\infty
for some
p>2
. Let the cdf
F
and its quantile function
F^{-1}
be continuous. Finally, assume that the baseline function
h_{0}
is absolutely continuous on
[a_{X},b_{X}]
and such that its Radon-Nikodym derivative
h^{*}_{0}
is continuous on a finite interval
[a,b]\subseteq[a_{X},b_{X}]
and vanishes outside
[a,b]
. Then

(7.8)

\sum_{t=2}^{n}|Y^{0}_{t,n}-Y^{0}_{t-1,n}|\to_{\mathbb{P}}\int_{a}^{b}|h^{*}_{0}(x)|\mathrm{d}x

when
n\to\infty
.

The restriction of the support of the Radon-Nikodym derivative
h^{*}_{0}
to only a finite interval
[a,b]
is due to two reasons:

1)

those real-life examples (automatic voltage regulators, insurance layers, etc.) that initiated our current research are based on finite transfer windows;

2)

dealing with finite intervals
[a,b]
considerably simplifies mathematical technicalities, which is an appealing feature, especially because we do not have a solid practical justification that would warrant further technical complexities.

7.3
Back to the index
I^{0}_{n}

The following corollary to Theorems
7.1
and
7.3
is the main result of entire Section
7
. Since the conditions of Theorem
7.1
make up only a subset of the conditions of Theorem

, we thus impose the latter set of conditions when formulating the corollary.

Corollary 7.1
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
7.3
, we have

(7.9)

I^{0}_{n}\to_{\mathbb{P}}I^{0}_{\infty}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\int_{a}^{b}(h^{*}_{0}(x))_{+}\mathrm{d}x\over\int_{a}^{b}|h^{*}_{0}(x)|\mathrm{d}x}

when

.

Note the representation (recall equation (
7.1
))

(7.10)

I^{0}_{\infty}={1\over 2}\big(1+\Lambda(h_{0})\big),

where

\Lambda(h_{0})={h_{0}(b)-h_{0}(a)\over\int_{a}^{b}|h^{*}_{0}(x)|\mathrm{d}x}.

We shall see in the next section that for anomaly-affected systems, the empirical index
I_{n}
converges to
0.5
. To distinguish this case from the limit
I^{0}_{\infty}
in the currently discussed anomaly-free case, we need to assume
\Lambda(h_{0})\neq 0
, which is tantamount to assuming
h_{0}(b)\neq h_{0}(a)
, because the numerator of
\Lambda(h_{0})
is positive. This is natural from the practical point of view as it excludes those transfer functions (which are usually non-decreasing) whose values at the end-points of the transfer window

coincide. Moreover, given model uncertainty, we need to ensure that
h_{0}
belongs to a class of functions for which
\Lambda(h_{0})
is sufficiently distant from

so that in the presence of statistical uncertainty we could still – with high confidence – be able to see whether the empirical index
I_{n}
converges to
0.5
or some other number.

Note 7.2
.

The ratio on the right-hand side of statement (

) arises as a normalized distance in a functional space
(
Davydov and Zitikis 2017
)
, which after a discretization gives rise to the index
I^{0}_{n}
and thus, in turn, to the index
I_{n}

(
Chen et al 2018
)
. For a generalization of these indices to multi-argument functions with further applications, we refer to
Davydov et al 2019
. For related mathematical considerations, we refer to

.

Assuming that the system with anomaly-free outputs

is in

, we need to show that the system becomes out of

are equal to

with non-degenerate at
\mathbf{0}
random anomalies
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
. In other words, assuming
B^{0}_{n,p}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

when

when
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}

.

To avoid overloading arguments with mathematical complexities, from now on we set

and work with the three transfer functions TF

–TF

(Section

). Consequently, in the anomaly-free case we have

(8.1)

B^{0}_{n,p}={1\over n^{1/p}}\sum_{t=2}^{n}|h_{0}(X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n})-h_{0}(X_{t-1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n})|.

We refer to Theorem
5.1
for a description of those inputs
X_{t}
and the baseline function
h_{0}
for which the anomaly-free system is in
p
-reasonable order. Our next theorem deals with the case when the input anomalies
\delta_{t}
are absent.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
\delta_{t}=0
for all

, and let the anomaly-free outputs
Y_{t}^{0}
be in
p
-reasonable order with respect to the inputs for some

. The outputs
Y_{t}
are out of

-reasonable order with respect to the inputs if and only if the output anomalies
\epsilon_{t}
are out of
p
-reasonable order with respect to the inputs.

To illustrate Theorem
8.2
, consider the case when the output anomalies
\epsilon_{t}
and the inputs
X_{t}
are independent. Assume also that the output anomalies
\epsilon_{t}
are iid and have finite first moments. (Such anomalies can be interpreted as genuinely unintentional.) In this case, the joint distribution of the concomitants
(\epsilon_{1,n},\dots,\epsilon_{n,n})
is the same as the joint distribution of the anomalies
(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})
themselves. Consequently, the output anomalies
\epsilon_{t}
are out of
p

9
A summary and potential extensions

In this paper we have explored a method for detecting systematic anomalies affecting systems when genuine anomaly-free inputs belong to a large class of stationary time series, or can be reduced to such. The anomalies may mimic (from the distributional point of view) the genuine inputs so closely that the contaminated system may not exhibit any visual aberrations, yet they can be detected using the herein proposed method. Supporting probabilistic and statistical results have been rigorously derived, and conditions under which they hold carefully specified. This rigour facilitates confidence when interpreting results and thus when making decisions.

To illustrate how the method works in practice, we have illustrated it using actual time series and also included a numerical experiment under various model and anomaly specifications. The results have shown that the method is robust and is able to detect even tiny systematic anomalies, although, naturally, under longer periods of observation. The method covers light- and heavy-tailed inputs, thus showing its versatility in applications, including those that are associated with data traffic and cyber risks.

Several interesting topics for future study naturally arise from the present paper, throughout which we have so far concentrated on the model

(9.1)

Y_{t}=h(X_{t},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t})

with one-dimensional inputs
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
and outputs
Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
d
-dimensional anomalies
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. With the time series structure of inputs, contemporary and historical observations enter into the model via the equation
X_{t}=\langle\boldsymbol{\beta},\mathbf{Z}_{t}\rangle
. This point of view together with naturally occurring multidimensional predictors in regression, classification, and, generally, in machine learning lead us to the model

(9.2)

Y_{t}=h(\mathbf{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t})

with
k
-dimensional predictors
\mathbf{X}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
. More generally, problems associated with anomaly detection in parallel computer systems, electrical grid, and wireless communication architectures such as SISO, SIMO, etc.
(
Tse and Viswanath 2005
;
Kshetrimayum 2017
, e.g.,)
lead us to the model

(9.3)

\mathbf{Y}_{t}=h(\mathbf{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t})

with
q
-valued (
q\in\mathbb{N}
) transfer function
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{k+d}\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
and thus
q
-dimensional outputs
\mathbf{Y}_{t}
.

The transition from the univariate inputs
X_{t}
to the multivariate ones
\mathbf{X}_{t}
gives rise to serious mathematical challenges, particularly because of the lack of total ordering in multi-dimensional Euclidean spaces. We feel that the coordinate-wise ordering might lead to a useful anomaly-detection method, but at this moment it looks ad hoc, lacking geometric interpretation and thus intuitive appeal. The optimization problems tackled by
Davydov and Zitikis 2017
, and
Davydov et al 2019
might give a clue as to what path to take. Alternatively, studies by
Koshevoy 1997
and
Mosler 2002
on ordering multi-dimensional elements could give rise to an effective solution.

The multivariate nature of outputs
\mathbf{Y}_{t}
also creates serious statistical-testing and decision-making problems, but we feel that with some effort, such problems can be tackled with the help of e-values studied by
Vovk and Wang 2021
and the multiple testing procedures developed by
Wang and Ramdas 2022
. The e-values are expectation-based versions of the classical p-values. They are simpler to use, thus facilitating multiple hypothesis testing and, in turn, decision making. Hence, the e-values can give rise to impressively powerful and convenient statistical tools in the context of systematic-anomaly detection in, e.g., parallel computer systems, electrical grid, wireless communication architectures, and so on.

Finally, we conclude with the note that anomaly detection problems involve adversarial aspects (e.g., adversarial signal processing, adversarial hypothesis testing) which involve adversaries (intruders) who change their strategies over time. For a glimpse of such research areas, we refer to

Barni and Pérez-González 2013
,
Biggio and Roli 2018
,
Barni and Tondi 2018
,
Alhajjar et al. 2021
. Naturally, machine learning techniques play a pivotal role in these areas.

To prove Theorem

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\xi_{t}
,
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, be identically distributed random variables such that
\mathbb{E}(|\xi_{t}|^{p})<\infty
for some
p\geq 1
. Then

(B.1)

n^{-1/p}\big(\mathbb{E}(\xi_{n\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n})-\mathbb{E}(\xi_{1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n})\big)=O(1)

when
n\to\infty
, and thus
n^{-1/p}(\xi_{n\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}-\xi_{1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n})=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, where
\xi_{1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\leq\xi_{2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\leq\cdots\leq\xi_{n\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
are the order statistics of
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{n}
.

Proof.

Using bounds (7) of
Gascuel and Caraux 1992
and then applying Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\xi_{n\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n})-\mathbb{E}(\xi_{1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n})

\displaystyle\leq n\int_{1-1/n}^{1}F_{\xi}^{-1}(y)\mathrm{d}y-n\int_{0}^{1/n}F_{\xi}^{-1}(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq n\int_{1-1/n}^{1}|F_{\xi}^{-1}(y)|\mathrm{d}y+n\int_{0}^{1/n}|F_{\xi}^{-1}(y)|\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq cn\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}|F_{\xi}^{-1}(y)|^{p}\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{1/p}n^{-1/q}

\displaystyle\leq cn^{1/p}\big(\mathbb{E}(|\xi_{1}|^{p})\big)^{1/p},

where
F_{\xi}
denotes the cdf of
\xi_{1}
, and
q\in[1,\infty]
is such that
p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1
. (When
p=1
, we set

B.1
). To prove the concluding part of the lemma, we choose any constant
\lambda>0
and write the bounds

\mathbb{P}(n^{-1/p}(\xi_{n\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}-\xi_{1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n})>\lambda)\leq{n^{-1/p}\over\lambda}\big(\mathbb{E}(\xi_{n\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n})-\mathbb{E}(\xi_{1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n})\big)\leq{c\over\lambda}

with a finite constant

that does not depend on
n
and
\lambda
.
This finishes the entire proof of Lemma
B.1
.

F_{n}^{-1}((t-1)/n)\leq b
is equivalent to
(t-1)/n\leq F_{n}(b)
. Due to the Glivenko-Cantelli property, we therefore conclude that for any (small)
\delta>0
and for all sufficiently large
n
, all those
t
’s for which the bounds
X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}>a
and
X_{t-1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\leq b
hold are such that
(1-\delta)F(a)n\leq t\leq(1+\delta)F(a)n
. For typographical simplicity, we rewrite the latter bounds as
\alpha n\leq t\leq\beta n
, where
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(1-\delta)F(a)
and
\beta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(1+\delta)F(a)
. Hence, statement (
B.8
) follows if

(B.9)

\max_{\alpha n\leq t\leq\beta n}\big(Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n}\big)\to_{\mathbb{P}}0.

Since
Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n}\leq X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}-X_{t-1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}
for all
t=2,\dots,n
, statement (
B.9
) follows if

(B.10)

\max_{\alpha n\leq t\leq\beta n}\big(X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}-X_{t-1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\big)\to_{\mathbb{P}}0.

To prove the latter statement, we write

\displaystyle\max_{\alpha n\leq t\leq\beta n}\big(X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}-X_{t-1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\big)

\displaystyle=\max_{\alpha n\leq t\leq\beta n}\big(F_{n}^{-1}(t/n)-F_{n}^{-1}((t-1)/n)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\alpha n\leq t\leq\beta n}\big(F^{-1}(t/n)-F^{-1}((t-1)/n)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\max_{\alpha n\leq t\leq\beta n}\big|F_{n}^{-1}(t/n)-F^{-1}(t/n)\big|

(B.11)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\max_{\alpha n\leq t\leq\beta n}\big|F_{n}^{-1}((t-1)/n)-F^{-1}((t-1)/n)\big|.

The first maximum on the right-hand side of bound (
B.11
) converges to
0
because
F^{-1}
is continuous on
(0,1)
and thus uniformly continuous on every closed subinterval of
(0,1)
. As to the second and third maxima on the right-hand side of bound (
B.11
), they converge to
0
in probability because

(B.12)

\Gamma_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\big|F_{n}^{-1}(t)-F^{-1}(t)\big|\to_{\mathbb{P}}0

for every closed interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]\subset(0,1)
, because the Glivenko-Cantelli property holds. To show that the just noted implication is true, we proceed as follows.

Statement (
B.12
) means that, for any fixed
\gamma>0
, the probability of the event
\Gamma_{n}\leq\gamma
converges to
1
when
n\to\infty
. This event
has at least the same, if not larger, probability as the event

(B.13)

F^{-1}(t)-\gamma<F_{n}^{-1}(t)\leq F^{-1}(t)+\gamma\quad\textrm{for all}\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{1}],

which is equivalent to

F_{n}(F^{-1}(t)-\gamma)<t\leq F_{n}(F^{-1}(t)+\gamma)\quad\textrm{for all}\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{1}].

The latter event has at least the same, if not larger, probability as the event

(B.14)

F(F^{-1}(t)-\gamma)+\|F_{n}-F\|<t\leq F(F^{-1}(t)+\gamma)-\|F_{n}-F\|\quad\textrm{for all}\quad t\in[t_{0},t_{1}].

Since
t=F(F^{-1}(t))
, event (
B.14
) has at least the same, if not larger, probability as the event

(B.15)

-\Delta_{1}(\gamma)+\|F_{n}-F\|<0\leq\Delta_{2}(\gamma)-\|F_{n}-F\|,

where

\Delta_{1}(\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\big(F(F^{-1}(t))-F(F^{-1}(t)-\gamma)\big)

and

\Delta_{2}(\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\big(F(F^{-1}(t)+\gamma)-F^{-1}(F^{-1}(t))\big).

Since the cdf
F
is strictly increasing (because we have assumed that
F^{-1}
is continuous), the quantities
\Delta_{1}(\gamma)
and
\Delta_{2}(\gamma)
are (strictly) positive for every
\gamma>0
. We therefore conclude that statement (
B.15
) holds with as large a probability as desired, provided that
n
is sufficiently large. This, in turn, implies that event (
B.13
) can be made as close to
1
as desired, provided that
n
is sufficiently large. The proof of Lemma
B.2
is finished.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.3
.

Since the baseline function
h_{0}
is absolutely continuous on
[a_{X},b_{X}]
and its Radon-Nikodym derivative
h^{*}_{0}
vanishes outside the interval
[a,b]
, we have

\displaystyle|Y^{0}_{t,n}-Y^{0}_{t-1,n}|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}}^{X_{t-1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}}h^{*}_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{[X_{t-1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n},X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}]\cap[a,b]}h^{*}_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x\bigg|=|h^{*}_{0}(\xi_{t,n})|(Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n}),

where, due to the mean-value theorem, the right-most equation holds for some
\xi_{t,n}\in[Z_{t-1,n},Z_{t,n}]
with
Z_{t,n}
’s defined in Lemma
B.2
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=2}^{n}|Y^{0}_{t,n}-Y^{0}_{t-1,n}|-\int_{a}^{b}|h^{*}_{0}(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=2}^{n}|h^{*}_{0}(\xi_{t,n})|(Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n})-\int_{a}^{b}|h^{*}_{0}(x)|\mathrm{d}x.

Obviously,
Z_{t,n}\in[a,b]
for all
t=2,\dots,n
. We also have
Z_{0,n}=a
and
Z_{n+1,n}=b
. By Lemma
B.2
,

(B.16)

\max_{1\leq t\leq n+1}(Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n})\to_{\mathbb{P}}0.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n+1}|h^{*}_{0}(\xi_{t,n})|(Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n})-\int_{a}^{b}|h^{*}_{0}(x)|\mathrm{d}x-\sum_{t\in\{1,n+1\}}|h^{*}_{0}(\xi_{t,n})|(Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n})

(B.17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n+1}|h^{*}_{0}(\xi_{t,n})|(Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n})-\int_{a}^{b}|h^{*}_{0}(x)|\mathrm{d}x+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

where the last equation holds when
n\to\infty
because the function
h^{*}_{0}
is bounded, and
Z_{1,n}-a\to_{\mathbb{P}}0
and
b-Z_{n,n}\to_{\mathbb{P}}0

when
n\to\infty
, which we verified at the beginning of the proof of Lemma
B.2
. Hence, equation (
B.17
) holds, and in order to prove
\Theta_{n}\to_{\mathbb{P}}0
, we need to show

(B.18)

\Theta_{n}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{t=1}^{n+1}|h^{*}_{0}(\xi_{t,n})|(Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n})-\int_{a}^{b}|h^{*}_{0}(x)|\mathrm{d}x\to_{\mathbb{P}}0.

In other words, we need to show that, for every
\gamma>0
,

(B.19)

\mathbb{P}\big(|\Theta_{n}^{*}|\geq\gamma\big)\to 0

when
n\to\infty
. For this, we first rewrite statement (
B.16
) explicitly: for every
\lambda>0
,

(B.20)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\leq t\leq n+1}(Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n})\geq\lambda\Big)\to 0

when
n\to\infty
. Hence, statement (
B.19
) follows if, for any
\gamma>0
, we can find
\lambda>0
such that

(B.21)

\mathbb{P}\Big(|\Theta_{n}^{*}|\geq\gamma,\max_{1\leq t\leq n+1}(Z_{t,n}-Z_{t-1,n})<\lambda\Big)\to 0

when
n\to\infty
. We now recall the very basic definition of Riemann integral, according to which, for any
\gamma>0
, we can find
\lambda>0
such that

\bigg|\sum_{t=1}^{n+1}|h^{*}_{0}(\zeta_{t,n})|(z_{t,n}-z_{t-1,n})-\int_{a}^{b}|h^{*}_{0}(x)|\mathrm{d}x\bigg|<\gamma

whenever

(B.22)

\max_{1\leq i\leq n+1}(z_{t,n}-z_{t-1,n})<\lambda,

where
z_{0,n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=a
,
z_{n+1,n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=b
,
z_{t-1,n}\leq z_{t,n}
for
t=1,\dots,n+1
, and
\zeta_{t,n}\in[z_{t-1,n},z_{t,n}]
. Hence, with the same
\lambda>0
as in statement (
B.22
), probability (
B.21
) is equal to
0
. This establishes statement (
B.19
) and finishes the proof of Theorem
7.3
.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

The concomitants
Y_{1,n},\dots,Y_{n,n}
of the outputs
Y_{t}=h(X_{t},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t})
,
t=1,\dots,n
, with respect to the inputs

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{1,n},\dots,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n,n}

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{n}
with respect to
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, that is,

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t,n}=\sum_{s=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s}\mathds{1}\{X_{s}=X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\}.

Consequently,

(B.23)

B_{n,p}={1\over n^{1/p}}\sum_{t=2}^{n}\big|h(X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t,n})-h(X_{t-1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1,n})\big|.

Proof.

Since the cdf
F
of each input

is continuous, we can assume without loss of generality that all the inputs

are unequal. Hence, we can write the equation

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s}\mathds{1}\{X_{s}=X_{t}\}.

This implies that the concomitants of the outputs
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
with respect to the inputs

can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle Y_{t,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{n}Y_{s}\mathds{1}\{X_{s}=X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{n}h(X_{s},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s})\mathds{1}\{X_{s}=X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{n}h(X_{s\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s,n})\mathds{1}\{X_{s\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}=X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\}

\displaystyle=h(X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t,n}).

This establishes equation (
B.23
) and concludes the proof of Lemma
B.3
.

Lemma B.4
.

-dependent, splitting the sum into the sums with respect to even and odd

’s yields statement (

) for every

if the moment

Proof of Theorem
8.3
.

Since the baseline function

is Lipshitz continuous, Theorem
5.1
implies that the anomaly-free outputs
Y_{t}^{0}
are in
p
-reasonable order with respect to the inputs
X_{t}
. Consequently,
B^{0}_{n,p}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. By Lemma

B_{n,p}={1\over n^{1/p}}\sum_{t=2}^{n}|h(X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n},\delta_{t,n},0)-h(X_{t-1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n},\delta_{t-1,n},0)|.

Using Lipschitz continuity of
h_{0}
and also Lemma
B.1
with
X_{t}
instead of
\xi_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle B_{n,p}

\displaystyle={1\over n^{1/p}}\sum_{t=2}^{n}|h_{0}(X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}+\delta_{t,n})-h_{0}(X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}+\delta_{t-1,n})+h_{0}(X_{t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}+\delta_{t-1,n})-h_{0}(X_{t-1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}+\delta_{t-1,n})|

\xi_{t}=|h_{0}(X_{t}+\delta_{t})-h_{0}(X_{t}+\delta_{t-1})|,

(B.25)

{1\over n^{1/p}}\sum_{t=2}^{n}\xi_{t}\to_{\mathbb{P}}\infty.

That is, we need to show that for every
\lambda<\infty

\mathbb{P}\bigg({1\over n^{1/p}}\sum_{t=2}^{n}\xi_{t}\leq\lambda\bigg)\to 0

n\to\infty

\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}(\xi_{1})=\mathbb{E}(\xi_{t})

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg({1\over n^{1/p}}\sum_{t=2}^{n}\xi_{t}\leq\lambda\bigg)

{1\over n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg(\bigg|\sum_{t=2}^{n}(\xi_{t}-\mu)\bigg|^{2}\bigg)\to 0.

\xi_{t}

\displaystyle=(n-1)\mathrm{Var}(\xi_{0})+2\sum_{t=1}^{n-2}(n-1-t)\mathrm{Cov}(\xi_{0},\xi_{t})

where

h_{0}
.

\displaystyle\alpha_{\xi}(t)

for all

and
B

C

D

C

D

\displaystyle{1\over n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg(\bigg|\sum_{t=2}^{n}(\xi_{t}-\mu)\bigg|^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq{c\over n}\alpha_{X}(0)+{c\over n}\alpha_{X}(1)+{c\over n^{2}}\sum_{t=2}^{n-2}(n-1-t)\alpha_{X}(t)
