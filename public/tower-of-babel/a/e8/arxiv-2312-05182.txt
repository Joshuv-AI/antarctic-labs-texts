Scaling properties of a random Yule tree embedded in ℝ 𝔇 \mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{D}}
Lukas Schoug

Abstract
. We study a random tree, which was introduced in [ 4 ] as part of a model of a neuronal network. Realising a scaling relation for the law of the tree, we can use elementary techniques to derive asymptotic results on the geometry as time goes to infinity. Keywords: Scaling, birth process, random growth model. MSC2020 subject classifications: Primary 60J80, Secondary 60G50.



1.
Introduction

We study the growth of a random tree,
(\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{D}}
,
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}=\{1,2,\dots\}
, starting from the origin and growing at unit speed in a random direction, branching after a random exponentially distributed time. Each new interval formed by branching evolves independently of the others as the first interval and branches in the same manner. (This tree can be realised in the same way as a branching Brownian motion, where the branching rate for a particle is
\lambda
, the Brownian trajectories replaced by lines growing at speed
1
and drawn in a uniform direction, and where one keeps track of the entire history of the process.) In particular, this is an embedding of a Yule process with rate
\lambda
(that is, a birth process with individual birth rate
\lambda
) into
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{D}}
as a tree, such that each time interval between births is embedded as a line in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{D}}
, attached to the endpoint of the line corresponding to the previous birth of that particular branch, and growing in a uniformly randomly chosen direction. As such, we call
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
a Yule tree.

This model was introduced in two dimensions in
[
4
]
to model a neuronal network and was studied further in
[
3
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
7
]
(the latter paper also considering a three-dimensional version). In those papers each such random tree was supposed to simulate a neuron and the authors studied networks consisting of many such trees (we will not touch upon the biology background — for more information on that, see
[
4
]
and the references therein) and the growth of the trees define a directed graph. The model is studied both analytically and statistically: in
[
4
]
the moment generating function of the total length of the network, as well as a functional equation for connection probabilities are derived and simulations predicting features of the model, such as density, length distribution etc. are carried out. In
[
3
]
, connectivity properties related to the graph structure of the network were studied, deriving expectations of in- and out-degrees, and numerical experiments were performed, estimating frequencies of connection, shortest paths, in- and out-degrees and clustering coefficients. Next, in
[
8
]
, the authors study the decay of the connection probabilities, by numerically solving the functional equations describing the connection probabilities obtained in
[
4
]
and the corresponding equations in three dimensions (which they derive in the paper). Finally, in Paper IV of
[
7
]
, the author studies a the distance between branching points and the origin, as well as a version where the branches of the tree stop growing randomly at a prescribed rate.

Extensive work has also been put into simulating neuronal networks, providing statistical properties of the models. A recent simulation environment is NETMORPH, developed in
[
11
]
and studied and evaluated further in
[
1
]
and
[
12
]
. The tree
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}

However, interesting as this process is, it is not yet well understood. While simulations and statistical analyses have been carried out, relatively few results about the model have been rigorously proved. Natural questions, such as the radius or shape turn out to be cumbersome much due to the fact that each branching penalises the growth of a branch (but can still help the “average” growth of the tree, as in the growth in all directions simultaneously, since there will be more branches growing). Indeed, the behaviour of a branch depends heavily on how many times it branches and increasing the branching intensity to infinity actually makes an entire branch (grown in a finite time) converge to
\mathbf{0}=\{(0,\dots,0)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{D}}
in the topology determined by the Hausdorff distance (see Lemma
3.4
). Moreover, the heavy dependence of a branch on the number times it branches makes it difficult to use many-to-one and many-to-few formulas, which are powerful tools in the theory of branching processes used to deduce results on the whole branching structure from the law of an individual branch (see e.g.
[
10
]
and
[
9
]
).

In this paper, we study the geometry of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}
from a completely analytical perspective. The main features of interest in this paper are the radius and shape of the tree, for fixed intensity
\lambda>0
and large times
t>0
and for fixed time and high intensities. As we shall see, there is a certain correspondence between these regimes and the parts of Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
are deduced from the structure of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(1)
in the high intensity regime, by virtue of scaling (Lemma
4.1
). We now state our main results. Let
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)}|x|
be the radius of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}
at time
t
. Our first main result concerns
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
\alpha>0
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(\alpha,\varepsilon)>0
such that for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\leq 1/2-\varepsilon)\leq(1+\alpha)e^{-\varepsilon\lambda}.

Moreover, for any
\lambda>0
, almost surely,
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{LI}}\coloneqq\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)/t\geq 1/2
and the law of
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{LI}}
does not depend on
\lambda
.

For an explicit bound on
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\leq 1/2-\varepsilon)
for all
\lambda>0
and
\varepsilon>0

Remark 1.2
.

We remark that the model exhibits a

1

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{H}(A,A^{\prime})=\inf\{\varepsilon>0:A\subset B(A^{\prime},\varepsilon)\ \text{and}\ A^{\prime}\subset B(A,\varepsilon)\},

Theorem 1.3
.

\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(1)\cap B(0,1/2)
converges to

for all

We expect there to be some

2.
The model
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
: a random tree in Euclidean space

Fix
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{D}}
, let
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{x}(t)=\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{x,\mathfrak{D}}(t)
be a random set defined as follows.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{x}(0)=x
. Draw a vector
\nu\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{D}-1}
uniformly at random and an exponential random variable
\tau
with expectation
1/\lambda
, independently of
\nu
, and for
t<\tau
let
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{x}(t)=\{x+s\nu\}_{s\in[0,t]}
, i.e., for
t<\tau
it is an interval of length
t
drawn in the direction
\nu
. The end of the interval is called an active end, or a leaf. At time
\tau
, two new, independent, intervals start to grow from the leaf in two new, independently and uniformly randomly drawn directions, and what was the leaf is now no longer growing, hence is for time
t>\tau
referred to as a
branching point
. Both of the new intervals branch independently in the same manner as the first interval, with intensity
\lambda
. We write
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)=\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{0}(t)
. We denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(t)
the set of branching points of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
.

While, in the case
\mathfrak{D}\leq 2
, this process is self-intersecting and hence does form loops, we still refer to it as a tree. This is because if we define the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(t)=(\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}(t),\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(t))
, to be such that
\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}(t)
is the union of
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(t)
and the leaves of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
and
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}\in\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(t)
if
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}(t)
and there is an interval in
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
connecting
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, then
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(t)
is a tree.

Every path
\gamma:[0,t]\to\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
, starting at
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(0)
and ending at some leaf of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
, travelling at unit speed and leaving an interval in
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
only at the point where it branches into two new intervals, is called a
branch
of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
. Note here that almost surely, no two branching points coincide, so for each time
t>0
and each leaf
v
of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
there is a unique branch of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
starting at
0
and ending at
v
. Moreover, each branch of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
has length
t
. In (the equivalent) Definitions
3.1
and
3.2
we define a process
(\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t))_{t\geq 0}
which has the law of a branch of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
and we henceforth denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
a branch of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}
. Moreover, we denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}([s_{1},s_{2}])
the set of points traced out by
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
in the time interval
[s_{1},s_{2}]
.

We say that a point
y\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
is a
descendant
of a point
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
if there is a branch
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
and times
t_{1}<t_{2}\leq t
such that
x=\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t_{1})
and
y=\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t_{2})
. We say that
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
is an
ancestor
of
y\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
if
y
is a descendant of
x
. The
descending tree
at time
t
of a point
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
is the set of descendants
y\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
of
x
. A
double point

\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
is a point
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
for which there are two branches
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{1}
,
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{2}
of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
and times
0\leq t_{1},t_{2}\leq t
such that
x=\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{1}(t_{1})=\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{2}(t_{2})
and
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{1}([0,t_{1}])\neq\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{2}([0,t_{2}])
. We let
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(t)
denote the set of double points of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
. Note that this definition allows for
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{2}
to be the same, provided that
x
is hit by the branch at two different points in time. If this is the case, we say that
x
is
hit twice by the same branch
. When we refer to a point
x\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(t)

hit by two different branches
, we mean a double point which is not hit twice by the same branch (even though most points of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
will actually be hit by more than one branch, in the sense that there are several branches that hit the same points before they branch). Note that the set of triple points of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
(that is, the set of points
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
such that there exist branches
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{1}
,
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{2}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{3}
of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}
and times
0\leq t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\leq t
, such that
x=\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{1}(t_{1})=\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{2}(t_{2})=\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{3}(t_{3})
and such that
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{i}([0,t_{i}])\neq\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{j}([0,t_{j}])
whenever
i\neq j
) is almost surely empty. Moreover,
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(t)\cap\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(t)=\emptyset
almost surely. Note that there are four different cases for the law of the descending tree of a point
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
.

(1)

The descending tree of a point
x\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(t)
hit at time
s
has the law of the union of two independently sampled trees with law
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{x}(t-s)
. We refer to those two trees as the descending trees of
x
and it will be clear to the reader what is meant.

(2)

The descending tree of a point
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)\setminus(\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(t)\cup\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(t))
hit at time
s
has the law of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{x}(t-s)
, conditioned to start growing in the direction
\nu
in which the branch hitting
x
was growing at time
s
.

(3)

A point
x\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(t)
that is hit by two different branches at times
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
have two descending trees, with the laws of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{x}(t-s_{1})
and
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{x}(t-s_{2})
, conditioned to start growing in the directions that their respective branches grew when hitting
x
.

(4)

A point
x\in\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(t)
that is hit twice by the same branch, at times
s_{1}<s_{2}
, has two descending trees, with the laws of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{x}(t-s_{1})
and
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}^{x}(t-s_{2})
conditioned to start growing in the directions that the branch hitting
x
grew at times
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, respectively, and the latter is a subset of the former.

Remark 2.1
.

We emphasise here that in case (
1
), the two descending trees are conditionally independent of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(s-)
given
x
and similarly in the cases (
2
)–(
4
) the descending trees are conditionally independent the past given
x
and their initial angles of growth. This independence is key in deriving bounds for the radius of the tree as well as functional equations for connection probabilities and expectations of densities, see Section
4.2
.

2.1.
Previous results

In this subsection we recall the previous mathematical results on the process
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}
. Let
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(t)
be the number of leaves of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
for
t\geq 0
(with the understanding that
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(0)=1
). We note that
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(t)
is a Yule process with rate
\lambda
, that is, a birth process with individual birth rate
\lambda
. Thus, the following holds, see Exercise 2.5.1 of
[
14
]
or Proposition 6 of
[
16
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(t)\sim\mathrm{Fs}(e^{-\lambda t})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(t)=k)=e^{-\lambda t}(1-e^{-\lambda t})^{k-1},

for
k=1,2,\dots

Thus,
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(t)]=e^{\lambda t}
and
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(t)
typically grows exponentially. This is a key property which drastically changes several geometric features, compared to if the growth had been slower. Indeed, this makes sure that very rare events for a branch actually occurs all the time for the tree, changing, for example, the radius of the tree significantly, as we will see in Section
4.1
.

Denote the total length of every interval in
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
by
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(t)
. The following was proved in Section A.1 of
[
4
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The moment generating function
\psi_{\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(t)}
of
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(t)}(x)=\frac{x-\lambda}{xe^{(\lambda-x)t}-\lambda}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(t)]=\frac{e^{\lambda t}-1}{\lambda}.

Denote by
\mathcal{A}_{r}^{\lambda}(t)=\mathrm{Area}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathrm{dist}(x,\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t))\leq r\})
. It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\pi r^{2}\leq\mathcal{A}_{r}^{\lambda}(t)\leq 2r\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(t)+\frac{\pi r^{2}}{2}(\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(t)+1).

We will see that the probability that
\pi/4\leq\mathcal{A}_{r}^{\lambda}(1)\leq\pi(1+r)^{2}
converges exponentially fast to
1
as
\lambda\to\infty
, for any value of
r>0
. Also, a functional equation for the function
q_{r}^{\lambda}(t,x)=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t),x)>r)
was derived in Section 3.2 of
[
4
]
, but this will be stated in Section
4.2
.

2.2.
Differences in the model compared to previous work

While the model is mostly the same, there are some key differences compared to the works
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
and
[
8
]
. One obvious restriction in this note is that we consider the growth of only one tree, whereas the main goal of the papers
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
and
[
8
]
is to study the network composed of many trees. A restriction that is in a way imposed in said papers is that the network of trees lives in a bounded region and growth of branches is killed off upon reaching the boundary of said region. We stress, however, that some properties are also derived without considering a bounded region and when only considering one tree. Finally, we mention that in
[
7
]
, the author studies the model when leaves stop growing at random.

Another difficulty that arises in the perspective of the above mentioned papers is the fitting of parameters to approximate the properties of actual neuronal networks. This is an aspect that we will not consider in this paper.

2.3.
Comparison to potentially similar branching processes

There are other branching processes which might seem similar at first glance. Branching Brownian motion is naturally the one of first that come to mind and it has been studied extensively, see e.g.
[
5
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
13
]
and
[
2
]
. However, it satisfies very different large scale behaviour. Both of the processes typically have a radius of order
t
(recall Theorem
1.1
and see Theorem 1.1 of
[
13
]
), however, deducing a scaling rule for branching Brownian motion shows that this is not the case. Indeed, if
(\mathrm{BBM}_{\lambda}(t))_{t\geq 0}
denotes a branching Brownian motion, where the branching rate is
\lambda
(so that ordinary branching Brownian motion is
\mathrm{BBM}_{1}
), then we have by Brownian scaling and scaling of exponential random variables, that
\sqrt{\lambda}\mathrm{BBM}_{\lambda}(t)
has the same law as
\mathrm{BBM}_{1}(\lambda t)
. Then, by Theorem 1.1 of
[
13
]
, the radius of
\mathrm{BBM}_{\lambda}(t)
has order
\sqrt{2\lambda}t
(plus a logarithmic term), so that increasing
\lambda
pushes the maximal displacement of branching Brownian motion further from the origin. In the model
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
, the same does not hold true, as
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)\subset\overline{B(0,t)}
for any
\lambda\geq 0
.

One might also consider branching random walks, for which one has good control of the speed of growth (or in fact
speeds
of growth, see e.g.
[
6
]
). However, typically when studying branching random walks, one is concerned with what happens at a certain step, whereas in the present model, the number of steps taken at a finite time
t>0
depends on the length of the steps, adding extra difficulty which to our best knowledge is not considered before in this context.

For more on branching random walks and branching Brownian motions, see
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
.

3.
Branches and their approximate projections

We now give two equivalent definitions of a stochastic process which describes the law of a branch of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
,
t\geq 0
. We denote by
\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{D}-1})
the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{D}-1}
. In what follows, we consider a branch
(\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t))_{t\geq 0}
to be a stochastic process (in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{D}}
) and write
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}([s,t])
to be the set of points traced out by
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
in the time interval
[s,t]
for
0\leq s<t
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
t_{i}\in\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)
and
\nu_{i}\in\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{D}-1})
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We let
(\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be the stochastic process

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t):=(\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{1}(t),\dots,\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{\mathfrak{D}}(t))=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)}t_{i}\nu_{i}+\left(t-\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)}t_{i}\right)\nu_{N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)+1},

where
N_{\mathcal{B}}(t):=\min\{j\geq 0:\sum_{i=0}^{j}t_{i}\leq t<\sum_{i=0}^{j+1}t_{i}\}
.

The second definition is the following.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)
be a Poisson process with intensity
\lambda
. Define the stochastic process
(\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t))_{t\geq 0}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t):=(\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{1}(t),\dots,\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}^{\mathfrak{D}}(t))=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)}t_{i}\nu_{i},

where, conditionally on
N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)=n
,
t_{0}
is the time of the first jump of
N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)
and
t_{i}
is the time between the
i
th and the
i+1
st jump of
N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)
, for
i\in\{1,...,n-1\}
and
t_{n}=t-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}t_{i}
.

Simulation of
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t)
for
\mathfrak{D}=2
, with
t=20
and
\lambda=2
.
Right:
Simulation of
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t)
for
\mathfrak{D}=2
, with
t=2\times 10^{6}
and
\lambda=2
.

However, studying the sums
\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)}t_{i}\nu_{i}
in the above definitions is not straightforward at all, as the number of summands depends on the magnitude of the
t_{i}
’s. In studying a branch of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
, we consider the simpler process,
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=(X_{k}^{1},\dots,X_{k}^{\mathfrak{D}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, defined by

\displaystyle X_{k}^{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{k}t_{i}\nu_{i}^{n},

where
(t_{i})
and
(\nu_{i})
are independent sequences such that
t_{i}\in\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)
and
\nu_{i}=(\nu_{i}^{1},\dots,\nu_{i}^{\mathfrak{D}})\sim\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{D}-1})
for all integers
i\geq 1
and the
t_{i}
(resp.
\nu_{i}
) is independent of
t_{j}
(resp.
\nu_{j}
) whenever
j\neq i
. Let
\mathscr{F}_{k}=\sigma(t_{j},\nu_{j}:1\leq j\leq k)
. For each
n=1,\dots,\mathfrak{D}
, the process
(X_{k}^{n})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
will behave approximately as the projection of a branch onto the
n
th coordinate axis. We will begin by stating some basic properties which are straightforward to check.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have that

(i)

\mathbb{E}[X_{k}^{n}]=0
,

(ii)

\mathrm{Var}[X_{k}^{n}]\leq\frac{2(k+1)}{\lambda^{2}}
,

(iii)

X_{k}^{m}
and
X_{k}^{n}
are uncorrelated for
m\neq n
,

(iv)

X_{k}^{n}
is a martingale with respect to the filtration
(\mathscr{F}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
.

Suppose that
X_{k}=(X_{k}^{1},\dots,X_{k}^{\mathfrak{D}})
, then we would like to know the radius of
X_{k}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle R_{X}(k)=\max_{j\leq k}|X_{j}|=\max_{j\leq k}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathfrak{D}}(X_{j}^{n})^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

We note that if
R_{X}^{n}(k)=\max_{j\leq k}|X_{j}^{n}|
, then

(3.1)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq n\leq\mathfrak{D}}R_{X}^{n}(k)\leq R_{X}(k)\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\mathfrak{D}}R_{X}^{n}(k).

The above observations will be used to describe the behaviour of a branch
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
when
\lambda\to\infty
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For each
t>0
, the range of the branch
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}([0,t])
converges to
\mathbf{0}
in probability with respect to the topology determined by the Hausdorff distance as
\lambda\to\infty
. That is, the random variable
\max_{0\leq s\leq t}|\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(s)|
converges to
0
in probability as
\lambda\to\infty
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that we can couple
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
with
(X_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
so that if
(t_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are the times between the changes of direction in the branch
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
and
t_{k}^{*}=\sum_{j\leq k}t_{j}
are the times at which the changes are made, then
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t_{k}^{*})=X_{k}
, and such that the direction that the branch grows in a given time interval is the same as that of
X
in the corresponding one. That is, the processes
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
and
(X_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
change direction at the same times and the growth directions are the same. We note that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq s\leq t_{k}^{*}}|\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(s)|=\max_{j\leq k}|\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(t_{j}^{*})|=R_{X}(k).

For
n=1,\dots,\mathfrak{D}
, we have by the Kolmogorov martingale inequality and Lemma
3.3
, that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{X}^{n}(k)\geq\varepsilon)\leq\max_{j\leq k}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(X_{k}^{n})}{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{2(k+1)}{\varepsilon\lambda^{2}}.

Consequently, by (
3.1
),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{X}(k)\geq\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\mathfrak{D}}\mathbb{P}(R_{X}^{n}(k)\geq\varepsilon/\mathfrak{D})\leq\frac{2\mathfrak{D}^{2}(k+1)}{\varepsilon\lambda^{2}}.

Let
N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)
be as in Definition
3.1
and recall that
N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)\sim\mathrm{Po}(\lambda t)
. Thus, we have that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{0\leq s\leq t}|\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(s)|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(R_{X}(k)\geq\varepsilon)+\mathbb{P}(N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)>k).

Next we note that for any integer
k>\lambda t
, we have by a Chernoff bound that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{\mathcal{B}}(t)\geq k)\leq\frac{(e\lambda t)^{k}e^{-\lambda t}}{k^{k}},

and hence, choosing
k=\lceil(\lambda t)^{3/2}\rceil
, we have by (
3.4
), (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{0\leq s\leq t}|\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(s)|\geq\varepsilon\right)=O(\lambda^{-1/2}).

Thus
\max_{0\leq s\leq t}|\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}(s)|\to 0
in probability as
\lambda\to\infty
and hence the proof is done.
∎

For more on the processes
X
and
\mathcal{B}_{\lambda}
in the case
\mathfrak{D}=2
, see
[
16
]
.

4.
On the geometry of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)

In this section, we study the geometry of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
. We begin by noting the following scaling relation, which is the key observation for the proofs of the present paper.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix
s>0
. Then,
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)/s
and
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda s}(t/s)
have the same law. In particular, the three sets
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)/t
,
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda t}(1)
and
\mathcal{T}_{1}(\lambda t)/\lambda
have the same law.

Proof.

This follows since if
\tau\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)
and
s>0
, then
\tau/s\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda s)
. In other words, rescaling the lengths of the intervals by dividing by a number
s>0
, corresponds to increasing the splitting intensity by a factor of
s
and dividing the growth time of the tree by
s
(since the length of any branch is then divided by
s
).
∎

Thus, by Lemma
4.1
, in studying
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
for large
t
, we can (for some properties) instead study
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(1)
for large
\lambda
. Note here that any behaviour that is of scale
o(t)
, such as lower order radius terms or possibly fluctuations of the outer boundary, will not show up when rescaling as above.

Left:
Simulation of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
for
\mathfrak{D}=2
, with
t=100
and
\lambda=0.1
.
Right:
Simulation of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
for
\mathfrak{D}=2
, with
t=20
and
\lambda=0.5
. Note that by Lemma
4.1
, the laws of the simulations are the same, up to scaling the tree by
5
.

4.1.
Radius of the tree

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)=\mathrm{rad}\,\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)}|x|
denote the radius of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t)
. It is natural to consider the asymptotics of
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)
as
t\to\infty
and by virtue of Lemma
4.1
(which implies that
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)/t
and
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda t}(1)
have the same law), we may instead study
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)
as
\lambda\to\infty
. We begin by noting that the law of
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)
is continuous in
\lambda
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For each
t,r>0
, the function
\lambda\mapsto\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)\leq r)
is continuous for all
t,r>0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
,
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)
has the same distribution as
\mathcal{R}_{1}(\lambda t)/\lambda
, which is almost surely a continuous function of
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
for each fixed
t>0
. To see that
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)
is continuous at
\lambda=0
, note that
\mathbb{P}(\text{no branching occurs before time}\ t)=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)>t)=e^{-\lambda t}\to 1
as
\lambda\to 0
.
∎

Next we note the following immediate consequence of Lemma
4.1
, namely that any limit of
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)/t
is independent of
\lambda
. Note, that at this point, it is not clear that this means anything for the radius, since
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)
could be of size
o(t)
. However, we shall see below that
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)
is actually of order
t
as
t\to\infty
(as opposed to a typical branch, which has radius of order
o(t)
).

Corollary 4.3
.

The laws of
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)/t
and
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)/t
do not depend on
\lambda
.

Proof.

Follows immediately from Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Now we shall lower bound the radius of the tree. Seeing as a branch will converge to the point
\mathbf{0}
as
\lambda\to\infty
(by Lemma
3.4
), it might seem reasonable to expect
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)
to decrease to
0
as
\lambda\to\infty
as well. However, we shall see that the sheer number of branches forces the radius of the tree to be positive.

Proposition 4.4
.

Fix
\alpha>0
. For each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists some
\lambda^{*}=\lambda^{*}(\alpha,\varepsilon)>0
, such that for all
\lambda\geq\lambda^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\leq 1/2-\varepsilon)\leq(1+\alpha)e^{-\varepsilon\lambda}.

Proof.

Note that by Proposition
2.2
and Lemma
A.1
(with
y=e^{-\lambda(1/2+\varepsilon)}
,
a=\tfrac{1}{1+2\varepsilon}=1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1/2+\varepsilon}
and
b=1
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(1/2+\varepsilon)\leq e^{\lambda/2})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(-(n-1+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1/2+\varepsilon})\lambda(1/2+\varepsilon))}{n}

\displaystyle=e^{-\varepsilon\lambda}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(-n\lambda(1/2+\varepsilon))}{n+1}\right)\!.

Note that the sum
C_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{\exp(-n\lambda(1/2+\varepsilon))}{n+1}
converges to
0
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Thus, with probability at least
1-e^{-\varepsilon\lambda}(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-n\lambda(1/2+\varepsilon)}/(n+1))
, there are at least
e^{\lambda/2}
leaves at time
1/2+\varepsilon
. Moreover, if the tree branches at the point
x\in B(0,1/2+\varepsilon)
, then if the direction of growth for any of the two new branches belongs to the set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{half}}^{\mathfrak{D}-1}(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{D}-1}:x\cdot y\geq 0\}
, that is, the half of
\mathcal{S}^{\mathfrak{D}-1}
that is the closest to
x/|x|
(this has probability
1/2
for each branch), and the branch grows for time at least
1/2-\varepsilon
from
x
before branching, then
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq 1/2-\varepsilon
. We say that a branch grows in the
good
direction if its direction of growth belongs to
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{half}}^{\mathfrak{D}-1}(x)
, where
x
denotes the most recent point of branching for the branch. Assuming that we are on the event that
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(1/2+\varepsilon)\geq e^{\lambda/2}
, we have by Hoeffding’s bound, that the conditional probability that fewer than
\tfrac{1}{4}e^{\lambda/2}
of the (at least)
e^{\lambda/2}
active ends are growing in the the good direction is upper bounded by
\exp(-e^{\lambda/2}/8)
. On the event that at least
\tfrac{1}{4}e^{\lambda/2}
leaves at time
1/2+\varepsilon
are growing in the good direction, we have by the memoryless property of the exponential distribution and Lemma
A.2
, that the conditional probability that none of the
\tfrac{1}{4}e^{\lambda/2}
leaves produces an interval of length at least
1/2-\varepsilon
, is at most
\widetilde{C}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{4}e^{\varepsilon\lambda})
, where

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{4}e^{\lambda/2}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-n(1/2+\varepsilon)\lambda}}{n}\right)/\left(1-e^{-(1/2+\varepsilon)\lambda}\right)\!.

Note that for each fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
\widetilde{C}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\to 1
as
\lambda\to\infty
. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq 1/2-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq(1-e^{-\varepsilon\lambda}(1+C_{\lambda,\varepsilon}))\times(1-\exp(-e^{\lambda/2}/8))\times\left(1-\widetilde{C}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{4}e^{\varepsilon\lambda}\right)\right)\!.

Since for large enough
\lambda
(depending on
\alpha
and
\varepsilon
), this is lower bounded by
1-(1+\alpha)e^{-\varepsilon\lambda}
, the result follows.
∎

It turns out that a bound as in Proposition
4.4
is the key to obtaining the main results in Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
. However, other than the calculations working nicely, there is no obvious reason why the value
1/2
should be special or optimal for the model. Below, we shall introduce a condition which is a priori weaker than the exponential decay of the probability
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\leq d-\varepsilon)
and might be easier to check, which turns out to imply bounds which are strong enough to give the main results of the paper with
d
in place of
1/2
.

By virtue of Proposition
4.4
, we now know that
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)
will be of constant order for all
\lambda
large, in fact, it is extremely unlikely that it is smaller than
1/2-\varepsilon
for large
\lambda
. Looking at small
\lambda
, we see that increasing
\lambda
slightly actually increases the probability
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\leq 1/2-\varepsilon)
, since
\mathcal{R}_{0}(1)=1
almost surely, and increasing the intensity increases the rate of branching, which gives
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(1)
the chance to branch and possibly keep its branches inside
B(0,1/2-\varepsilon)
. However, as the intensity and hence the average number of branches increases, it eventually gets less likely that
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(1)
stays contained in
B(0,1/2-\varepsilon)
(despite the fact that the average distance from the origin travelled by a branch decreases to zero, by Lemma
3.4
). We therefore find it believable that for each fixed
d\in[0,1]
,
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\leq d)
should either be lower bounded away from zero for all
\lambda
large enough or converge to zero as
\lambda\to\infty
. Further evidence of this is discussed in Section
4.2.1
.

With the above discussion in mind, it is reasonable to expect that there is some maximal number
d\in[1/2,1]
such that there exists some
p>0
for which
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq d)\geq p
for all
\lambda
large enough. (Indeed, by Proposition
4.4
, this holds for all
d<1/2
.) We now prove a
0
-
1
-law type result, which states that the bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq d)\geq p
holding for all
\lambda
large enough implies a bound which is similar to that of Proposition
4.4
and is hence strong enough to imply the main results with
d
in place of
1/2
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Fix
d\in[0,1]
. Suppose that there exist
p>0
and
\lambda_{0}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq d)\geq p
for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
. Then, for each
\alpha>0
,
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\lambda_{*}=\lambda_{*}(\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon,\lambda_{0},p)>0
such that for each
\lambda\geq\lambda_{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\leq d-\varepsilon)\leq(1+\alpha)e^{-\varepsilon\beta\lambda/2}.

For a bound that holds for all
\lambda>0
,
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
, see (
4.4
).

Proof.

By the assumption of the lemma and Lemma
4.1
, we have that if
\varepsilon>0
, then

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1-\varepsilon/2)\geq d-\varepsilon/2)\geq p,

for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}/(1-\varepsilon/2)
and that if any descending tree of a point in
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(\varepsilon/2)
has radius at least
d-\varepsilon/2
within time
1-\varepsilon/2
of its starting time, then
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq d-\varepsilon
. Moreover, for fixed
\beta\in(0,1)
, we note that by Lemma
A.1
(with
y=e^{-\varepsilon\lambda/2}
,
a=1-\beta
and
b=1
),

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(\varepsilon/2)\geq e^{(1-\beta)\varepsilon\lambda/2})\geq 1-e^{-\varepsilon\beta\lambda/2}\!\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-n\varepsilon\lambda/2}}{n+1}\right)\!.

By (
4.1
), we have the following lower bound on the conditional probability that that
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq d-\varepsilon
given the event
\{\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(\varepsilon/2)\geq e^{(1-\beta)\varepsilon\lambda/2}\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq d-\varepsilon\,|\,\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(\varepsilon/2)\geq e^{(1-\beta)\varepsilon\lambda/2})

\displaystyle\geq 1-(1-p)^{\exp((1-\beta)\varepsilon\lambda/2)}

(4.3)

\displaystyle=1-\exp\!\left(e^{(1-\beta)\varepsilon\lambda/2}\log(1-p)\right)\!.

Consequently, by (
4.2
) and (
4.1
), we have that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq d-\varepsilon)\geq\left(1-\exp\!\left(e^{(1-\beta)\varepsilon\lambda/2}\log(1-p)\right)\right)\!\left(1-e^{-\varepsilon\beta\lambda/2}\!\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-n\varepsilon\lambda/2}}{n+1}\right)\right)\!.

Since
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-{n\varepsilon\lambda/2}}/(n+1)
converges to
0
as
\lambda\to\infty
and
\exp(e^{(1-\beta)\varepsilon\lambda/2}\log(1-p))=o(e^{-\varepsilon\beta\lambda/2})
as
\lambda\to\infty
, we have that there exists
\lambda_{*}=\lambda_{*}(\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon,\lambda_{0},p)\geq\lambda_{0}/(1-\varepsilon/2)
such that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\leq d-\varepsilon)\leq(1+\alpha)e^{-\varepsilon\beta\lambda/2}
for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{*}
.
∎

Since the condition that there exist
d,p,\lambda_{0}>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq d)\geq p
is satisfied for each
d<1/2
and by Lemma
4.5
guarantees a bound of similar sort as that of Proposition
4.4
, we now take this assumption as the basis of our proofs. This makes it easier to strengthen the results, should one manage to prove said condition for larger
d
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Suppose that
d,p,\lambda_{0}>0
are such that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(1)\geq d)\geq p
for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
. Then, almost surely,

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)/t\geq d.

Proof.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
we consider the event
E_{k}=\{\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(k)/k\leq d-\varepsilon\}
. We note that if
E_{k}
does not occur, then

(4.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)}{t}\geq\left(d-\varepsilon\right)\frac{k}{k+1},\quad k\leq t\leq k+1.

Let
F_{k}=\{\exists j\geq k:E_{j}\ \text{occurs}\}
and note that by (
4.5
), we have for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle\left\{\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)/t\leq(d-\varepsilon)\tfrac{k}{k+1}\right\}\subset F_{k}.

Moreover, we note that
F_{k}\subset\cup_{j\geq k}E_{j}
and by Lemma
4.5
with, say,
\alpha=1
and
\beta=1/2
, we have for
k\geq\lambda_{*}(\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon,\lambda_{0},p)/\lambda
, that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{k})=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{R}_{k\lambda}(1)\leq d-\varepsilon)\leq 2e^{-k\varepsilon\lambda/4}.

Consequently, for
k\geq\lambda_{*}(\alpha,\beta,\varepsilon)/\lambda
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(F_{k})\leq\sum_{j\geq k}\mathbb{P}(E_{j})\leq\sum_{j\geq k}2e^{-j\varepsilon\lambda/4}=\frac{2e^{-k\varepsilon\lambda/4}}{1-e^{-\varepsilon\lambda/4}}.

This converges to
0
as
k\to\infty
and hence
\mathbb{P}(\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(t)/t\leq d-\varepsilon)=0
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The first part is Proposition
4.4
and the second part is Corollaries
4.6
and
4.3
.
∎

4.2.
Connection probabilities

Fix some point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{D}}
. Let

\displaystyle p_{r}^{\lambda}(t,x)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t),x)\leq r),

and note that
p_{r}^{\lambda}(t,x)
is radially symmetric in
x
, that is, its dependence on
x
is only on
|x|
. Thus, we may and will sometimes write
p_{r}^{\lambda}(t,d)
when
|x|=d
. Furthermore, we let

\displaystyle q_{r}^{\lambda}(t,x)=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t),x)>r)=1-p_{r}^{\lambda}(t,x).

Assume for now that
\mathfrak{D}=2
. Then, we have from Section 3.2 of
[
4
]
, the following functional equation for
q_{r}^{\lambda}(t,x)
. For
|x|=d
,
t>d-r
and
d>r
,

\displaystyle q_{r}^{\lambda}(t,d)=

\displaystyle\int_{-\alpha_{0}}^{\alpha_{0}}\int_{0}^{s_{0}(\alpha)}\frac{\lambda e^{-\lambda s}}{2\pi}q_{r}^{\lambda}(t-s,D(s,\alpha))^{2}dsd\alpha

(4.6)

\displaystyle+\int_{\alpha_{0}}^{2\pi-\alpha_{0}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\lambda e^{-\lambda s}}{2\pi}q_{r}^{\lambda}(t-s,D(s,\alpha))^{2}dsd\alpha+e^{-\lambda t}f_{r}^{\lambda}(t,d),

where
\alpha_{0}=\arcsin\tfrac{r}{d}
,
s_{0}(\alpha)=d\cos\alpha-\sqrt{r^{2}-d^{2}\sin^{2}\alpha}
,
D(s,\alpha)=\sqrt{d^{2}+s^{2}-2ds\cos\alpha}
, and

\displaystyle f_{r}^{\lambda}(t,d)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(t),x)>r\,|\,\textup{no branching has occurred before time}\,t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1-\frac{1}{\pi}\arccos\!\left(\frac{d^{2}+t^{2}-r^{2}}{2td}\right)\!,&\textup{if }d-r\leq t\leq\sqrt{d^{2}-r^{2}},\\
1-\frac{1}{\pi}\arcsin\frac{r}{d},&\textup{if }t>\sqrt{d^{2}-r^{2}},\end{cases}

with boundary conditions

\displaystyle q_{r}^{\lambda}(t,d)=0,\quad\textup{if }d\leq r,\ t\geq 0,

\displaystyle q_{r}^{\lambda}(t,d)=1,\quad\textup{if }d>r,\ 0\leq t<d-r.

While this does not yield an analytic solution, it does allow for numerical computation. Similar equations can be derived in any dimension. For the case
\mathfrak{D}=3
, see Section 3.2 of
[
8
]
.

Now, allowing for general
\mathfrak{D}
, we have (see equation (12) of
[
8
]
) that

\displaystyle p_{r}^{\lambda}(t,x)\geq e^{-\lambda t}p_{r}^{0}(t,x).

Moreover, it is obvious from the definition that if
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
and
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
, then

(4.7)

Again, let us first focus on the case

. Thus, taking the limit as

in (

) it is reasonable to expect that

is a continuity point of

. The same analysis can be carried out in the case
\mathfrak{D}=3
. Next we prove that there is at least an interval of

where it is true for arbitrary dimension

(and we expect the statement to be true for all

Remark 4.9

Appendix A
Asymptotics of first success and exponential distributions

In this appendix we prove the necessary asymptotics for first success and exponential distributions, that are used to prove the radius bounds in Section
4.1
. For each
y\in(0,1)
, let
N_{y}\sim\mathrm{Fs}(y)
.

Lemma A.1
.

We have that
yN_{y}\to\mathrm{Exp}(1)
in distribution as
y\to 0
. Moreover,
if
0<a<1
and
b>0
, then

(A.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{y}\leq by^{-a})\leq b\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{n-a}}{n}.

Proof.

Fix
0<a\leq 1
and
b>0
, and recall that
\log(1-y)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}y^{n}/n
whenever
|y|<1
. We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{y}\leq by^{-a})

\displaystyle=1-(1-y)^{b/y^{a}}(1-y)^{\lfloor b/y^{a}\rfloor-b/y^{a}}

\displaystyle=1-e^{by^{-a}\log(1-y)}(1-y)^{\lfloor b/y^{a}\rfloor-b/y^{a}}

\displaystyle=1-\exp\!\left(-b\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{n-a}}{n}\right)(1-y)^{\lfloor b/y^{a}\rfloor-b/y^{a}}.

Since
1\leq(1-y)^{t}\leq 1/(1-y)
whenever
-1\leq t\leq 0
, we have that

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{1-y}\exp\!\left(-b\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{n-1}}{n}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(yN_{y}\leq b)\leq 1-e^{-b},

and thus
yN_{y}\to\mathrm{Exp}(1)
in distribution. Similarly, using that
e^{-t}>1-t
for all
t>0
, we have for
0<a<1
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{y}\leq by^{-a})\leq b\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{n-a}}{n}.

∎

For each
x>0
, let
(T_{x}^{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}}
be an i.i.d. collection of
\mathrm{Exp}(x)
-distributed random variables.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
a>c>0
and
b>0
. Then, for all
x>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{j\leq be^{ax}}\,T_{x}^{j}\leq c\right)\leq C_{a,b,c,x}\exp(-be^{(a-c)x}),

where
C_{a,b,c,x}=\exp(-be^{ax}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\tfrac{e^{-ncx}}{n})/(1-e^{-cx})
.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\max_{j\leq be^{ax}}\,T_{x}^{j}\leq c\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(T_{x}^{1}\leq c)^{\lfloor be^{ax}\rfloor}=(1-e^{-cx})^{be^{ax}}(1-e^{-cx})^{\lfloor be^{ax}\rfloor-be^{ax}}

\displaystyle=\exp(be^{ax}\log(1-e^{-cx}))(1-e^{-cx})^{\lfloor be^{ax}\rfloor-be^{ax}}

\displaystyle=\exp\!\left(-be^{ax}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-ncx}}{n}\right)(1-e^{-cx})^{\lfloor be^{ax}\rfloor-be^{ax}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\!\left(-be^{ax}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-ncx}}{n}\right)/(1-e^{-cx})

\displaystyle\leq C_{a,b,c,x}\exp(-be^{(a-c)x}),

where, in the second to last inequality, we again used the inequality
(1-y)^{t}\leq 1/(1-y)
which holds for
t\in[-1,0]
.
∎
