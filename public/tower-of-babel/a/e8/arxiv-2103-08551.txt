A fully local hybridised second-order accurate scheme for advection-diffusion equations
Hanz Martin Cheng

Abstract
. In this paper, we present a fully local second-order upwind scheme, applicable on generic meshes. This is done by hybridisation, which is achieved by introducing unknowns on each edge of the mesh. By doing so, fluxes only depend on values associated to a single cell, and thus, this scheme can easily be applied even on cells near the boundary of the domain. Another advantage of hybridised schemes is that static condensation can be employed, leading to a very efficient implementation. A convergence analysis, which also covers a flux-limited TVD variant of the scheme, is then presented. Numerical results are also given in order to compare this with a hybridised first-order upwind scheme and a classical cell-centered second-order upwind type scheme.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study a family of finite volume methods for stationary advection-diffusion equations. We start by presenting the choice of discretisation for the diffusive fluxes. This will be done via the hybrid mimetic mixed (HMM) method
[
6
]
, which is equivalent to the SUSHI method
[
9
]
. For the advective fluxes, we propose a fully local second-order scheme. To motivate the problem, we start with a revision of the first-order upwind scheme, which is the easiest to implement. One of the main disadvantages of the first-order upwind scheme is that it may easily introduce too much numerical diffusion into the solution of the system
[
11
]
. High-order schemes have been proposed in order to help mitigate the introduction of too much numerical diffusion. However, in order to achieve a high-order discretisation, more degrees of freedom (DOFs) are needed, which translates into a higher computational cost. In this work, we focus on second-order schemes. Classical second-order upwind schemes on Cartesian meshes
[
16
]
involve a 9-point stencil; hence, some interpolation techniques or introduction of ghost cells are needed in order to apply them on cells near the boundary of the domain.

The novelty of this work is the introduction of a hybridised fully local second-order scheme, inspired by the ideas in
[
3
]
. To do so, in addition to unknowns at the cell center, we introduce one additional unknown to each cell face. This results to more unknowns in the system: now having
\sharp
of cells
+

\sharp
of faces (edges) unknowns, as compared to
\sharp
of cells for cell-centered schemes. However, this allows us to reduce the dependence of the fluxes on values from neighboring cells. In the case of square cells (see Figure
1
), we only need one cell value, and four interface values. Since we remove the direct dependence of the fluxes on the values from neighboring cells, this allows us to directly apply this hybridised second-order scheme even near the boundaries of the domain. Moreover, static condensation can be employed to make the implementation more efficient. Following this, we then present some convergence results, which cover, for the advective component, the classical second-order upwind type schemes, and also include some nonlinear total variation diminishing (TVD) methods
[
4
,
13
]
.

Figure 1
.
Values needed for computing a hybridised second-order flux for edge
\sigma
.

The paper is organised into the following sections: We start by considering a stationary advection-diffusion equation, and present it in its finite volume form. Afterwards, we provide a discretisation of the diffusive fluxes, which will be done via the HMM method. We then give a short review of the cell-centered first and second-order upwind schemes on Cartesian meshes and give their extension onto generic polygonal meshes. Following this, we propose a hybridised fully local second-order scheme, applicable on generic meshes. Here, we show that by introducing additional unknowns along the faces (edges) of each cell, we can create a stencil that does not depend on values from neighboring cells. Convergence results will then be presented. Numerical tests will then be performed to illustrate the accuracy of the scheme, and also to compare this with a hybridised first-order upwind scheme and a classical cell-centered second-order upwind type scheme.

2.
The model problem

Consider the following stationary, linear scalar advection-diffusion equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions on a polytopal domain
\Omega
: Find
c\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(-\Lambda\nabla c+c\mathbf{V})

\displaystyle=f\qquad\mathrm{in}\quad\Omega,

(1)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=g\qquad\mathrm{in}\quad\partial\Omega.

Here,
\Lambda
is a diffusion tensor,
\mathbf{V}
is a velocity field, and
f
is a source term. We begin by stating the assumptions on the data:

(
A1
)

\Lambda
is a measurable function from
\Omega
to the set of
d\times d
symmetric positive definite matrices, and there exists
\underline{\lambda},\overline{\lambda}>0
such that, for a.e.
x\in\Omega
, the eigenvalues of
\Lambda(x)
are in
[\underline{\lambda},\overline{\lambda}]
;

(
A2
)

\mathbf{V}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})^{d}
with
{\mathop{\rm div}}(\mathbf{V})\geq 0
;

(
A3
)

f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

2.1.
Finite volume discretisation of the advection-diffusion equation

We now write a finite volume discretisation of the model (
1
). First, we define a mesh in the simplest intuitive way: a partition of
\Omega
into polygonal (in 2D) or polyhedral (in 3D) sets. Following the notations in
[
5
, Definition 7.2]
, we denote
\mathcal{T}=({\mathcal{M}};{\mathcal{E}})
to be
the set of cells
K
and faces (edges in 2D)
\sigma
of our mesh, respectively. For each cell
K\in{\mathcal{M}}
, we denote by
|K|
its
d
dimensional measure,
\mathrm{diam}(K)
its diameter,
{\mathcal{E}}_{K}\subset{\mathcal{E}}
the set of faces (edges) of cell
K
, and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}
the cell center of gravity. The collection of faces is a disjoint union of two sets,
{\mathcal{E}}={\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{int}}\cup{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{ext}}
, where
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{int}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{ext}}
denote the set of interior and exterior faces, respectively. For each interior face
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
, we denote by
K^{\prime}
the cell that shares the face
\sigma
with
K
. Also, for
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}
, we denote by
|\sigma|
its
d-1
dimensional measure,
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}
its center, and
\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}
its normal direction pointing out of
K
. We also denote by
d_{K,\sigma}
the orthogonal distance from
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}
to
\sigma
(see Figure
2
).

Denoting by
{\mathcal{M}}_{h}
the mesh such that,
\max_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}(\mathrm{diam}(K))=h
, the analysis performed in Section
5
will require the following assumptions on the mesh.

(
MR1
)

Every cell
K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}
is star-shaped with respect to
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}
.

(
MR2
)

The mesh regularity parameter, which is defined as

\mathrm{regul}({\mathcal{M}}_{h}):=\max\bigg(\max_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h,\mathrm{int}}}\frac{d_{K,\sigma}}{d_{K^{\prime},\sigma}},\max_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h,\mathrm{ext}}}\frac{\mathrm{diam}(K)}{d_{K,\sigma}},\max_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\mathrm{card}({\mathcal{E}}_{K})\bigg),

where
\mathrm{card}({\mathcal{E}}_{K})
denotes the number of edges of a cell
K
, is uniformly bounded as
h\rightarrow 0
.

Figure 2
.
Notations in a mesh cell.

We now denote the unknowns of the system, one on each cell and one on each edge by

X_{\mathcal{D}}:=\{\big((w_{K})_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}},(w_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}},w_{K},w_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}\big)\}.

Now, we take the integral of (
1
) over a cell
K
, and use Gauss’ Theorem to obtain

\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\int_{\sigma}(-\Lambda\nabla c+c\mathbf{V})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\,\,ds=\int_{K}f\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}.

Following the ideas in
[
3
]
, we write
F_{K,\sigma}^{D}\approx\frac{1}{|\sigma|}\int_{\sigma}\Lambda\nabla c\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\,\,ds
and
F_{K,\sigma}^{A}\approx\frac{1}{|\sigma|}\int_{\sigma}c\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\,\,ds
approximations of the average diffusive and advective fluxes along
\sigma
, respectively. The discrete equation for flux balance in each cell
K
reads

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|(F_{K,\sigma}^{D}+F_{K,\sigma}^{A})=|K|f_{K},

where
f_{K}
is the average value of the source term at cell
K
.
Here, we see that the main ingredients for obtaining an approximation for the solution
c
is the definition of the diffusive and advective fluxes
F_{K,\sigma}^{D}
and
F_{K,\sigma}^{A}
.

3.
Diffusive fluxes

In this section, we discuss the discretisation of the diffusive fluxes. This will be done via the hybrid-mimetic-mixed (HMM) method
[
6
]
, for which the diffusive fluxes are based on the bilinear form
a(u,w):=\int_{\Omega}\Lambda\nabla u\cdot\nabla w\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}
, which stems from the weak formulation of the advection-diffusion equation (
1
). In particular, we define the diffusive fluxes such that for
c\in X_{\mathcal{D}}
, we have for all
K\in{\mathcal{M}},v\in X_{\mathcal{D}}
,

\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{D}(v_{K}-v_{\sigma})=\int_{K}\Lambda_{K}\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}}c\cdot\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}}v\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}},

(2)

where
\Lambda_{K}
is an approximation of
\Lambda
at cell
K
, and
\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}}
is a
stabilised discrete gradient
. The discrete gradient is stabilised in the sense that it consists of a consistent term, added to a stabilisation term. The consistent term is linearly exact, and is defined via a cellwise discrete gradient
\overline{\nabla}_{\mathcal{D}}
, such that for all
q\in X_{\mathcal{D}}
and for all
K\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
(\overline{\nabla}_{\mathcal{D}}q)_{|K}=\overline{\nabla}_{K}q
, where

\overline{\nabla}_{K}q:=\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|(q_{\sigma}-q_{K})\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma},\

(3)

and
\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}
is the unit outward normal vector of
\sigma
. In order to ensure the coercivity of the diffusive flux, a stabilisation term needs to be added to the discrete gradient (
3
). For the HMM method, the stabilisation term is defined such that for all
q\in X_{\mathcal{D}}
and for all
K\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
S_{K}:X_{\mathcal{D}}\rightarrow L^{2}(K)
is given by

S_{K}(q):=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}S_{K,\sigma}\mathbbm{1}_{D_{K,\sigma}},

(4)

where
(D_{K,\sigma})_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}
are convex hulls of
\sigma
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}
(see Figure
3
), and

S_{K,\sigma}:=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{d_{K,\sigma}}[q_{\sigma}-q_{K}-\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})]\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}.

(5)

Here,
d_{K,\sigma}
is the orthogonal distance from
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}
to
\sigma
, and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}
is the center of the edge
\sigma
.

Figure 3
.
Notations on cell
K
.

The stabilised discrete gradient is then defined such that for all
q\in X_{\mathcal{D}}
and for all
K\in{\mathcal{M}}

(\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}}q)_{|K}:=\overline{\nabla}_{K}q+S_{K}(q).

(6)

One important property of the stabilisation term, which will be needed to establish the coercivity of the scheme, is the following orthogonality condition.

Lemma 3.1

(Orthogonality of the stabilisation term.)
.

Let
q\in X_{\mathcal{D}}
and let
K\in{\mathcal{M}}
. Then, the stabilisation
S_{K}:X_{\mathcal{D}}\rightarrow L^{2}(K)
defined as in (
4
) satisfies the following orthogonality condition.

\int_{K}S_{K}q\cdot\phi\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}=0\quad\forall\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(7)

A proof of Lemma
3.1
can be found in
[
5
, Chapter 13]
, but for completeness, we present an alternative proof below.

Proof.
Since
S_{K}q
is piecewise constant with value
S_{K,\sigma}
on each convex hull
D_{K,\sigma}
, we can write

\displaystyle\int_{K}S_{K}q\cdot\phi\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\int_{D_{K,\sigma}}S_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|D_{K,\sigma}|S_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi.

Now, using the definition of
S_{K,\sigma}
in (
5
) and the geometric relation

|D_{K,\sigma}|=\frac{1}{d}|\sigma|d_{K,\sigma},

(8)

we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|D_{K,\sigma}|S_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|[q_{\sigma}-q_{K}-\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})]\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|(q_{\sigma}-q_{K})\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|[\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})]\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi.

Using (
3
), we obtain

\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|(q_{\sigma}-q_{K})\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi=|K|\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot\phi.

(9)

We then note that
\overline{\nabla}_{K}q
is a constant vector, and hence,
\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})
is a polynomial of degree one. This means that if
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}
is the edge midpoint of
\sigma
, then

|\sigma|[\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})]\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi=\int_{\sigma}\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi\,\,ds.

Taking the sum over
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}
, using Green’s Theorem and the fact that
\phi
is constant, we then get

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|[\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})]\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\int_{\sigma}\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi\,\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{K}{\mathop{\rm div}}(\phi\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}))\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot\phi\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}},

or equivalently,

\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|[\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})]\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\cdot\phi=|K|\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot\phi.

(10)

The proof is then concluded by combining the expressions (
9
) and (
10
).

4.
Advective fluxes

4.1.
Cell-centered first and second-order upwind fluxes

In this section, we discuss the advective fluxes, starting with the standard first order upwind scheme for Cartesian meshes. For each control volume
K
, we assign one discrete unknown
c_{K}
, which approximates the average value of
c
at the center
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}
of
K
. Consider now a cell
K
with eastern edge
\sigma
. Adapting the compass notation, the cells to the west and east of
K
are denoted by
W,E
respectively. The cell to the east of
E
is then denoted by
EE
(see Figure
4
).

Figure 4
.
Cells involved in computing a second-order upwind flux for edge
\sigma
.

Denoting by
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}
the midpoint of the edge
\sigma
and using the midpoint rule for computing the integral
\int_{\sigma}c\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\,\,ds
, the upwind fluxes are then given by

F_{K,\sigma}^{A}=c_{K}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}-c_{E}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-},

(11)

where
\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}=\frac{1}{|\sigma|}\int_{\sigma}\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\,\,ds
,
\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}=\max(\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma},0)
,
\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-}=\max(-\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma},0)
, and the value
c_{\sigma}=c({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma})
is approximated from the upwind direction.

Essentially, this tells us that if material is flowing out (into) cell
K
through the edge
\sigma
, corresponding to
\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}
being positive (negative), then the value of
c_{\sigma}
is approximated by
c_{K}
(
c_{E}
), which makes sense since the material traveling through
\sigma
comes from cell
K
(
E
). From this, we see that the first-order upwind scheme has a very natural physical interpretation, and is fairly simple to implement. Moreover, the formulation (
11
) can straightforwardly be extended onto generic meshes, by treating
E
as a generic neighboring cell that shares
\sigma
with
K
. However, the upwind scheme may introduce too much numerical diffusion. Several methods can be employed in order to mitigate this, but for this work, we focus on second-order methods.

We now discuss the second-order upwind scheme. Again, we look at the eastern edge
\sigma
of cell
K
. The second-order upwind scheme approximates the advective flux
F_{K,\sigma}
in the following manner:

F_{K,\sigma}^{A}=\bigg(c_{K}+\frac{1}{2}(c_{K}-c_{W})\bigg)\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}-\bigg(c_{E}+\frac{1}{2}(c_{E}-c_{EE})\bigg)\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-}.

(12)

Here, we see that the main difference between the second-order upwind flux (
12
) and the first-order upwind flux is that (
11
) approximates the value of
c_{\sigma}
by a first-order Taylor expansion centered at
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}
(
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{E}
) when
\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}
is positive (negative), whereas (
12
) gives a second-order Taylor expansion in the same direction. Another thing we notice is that due to the presence of
-c_{W}
and
-c_{E}
in the second-order upwind flux (
12
), it is no longer guaranteed that the discrete maximum principle is satisfied. This is typically the case for linear schemes which are second-order or higher. We now note that

\frac{1}{2}(c_{K}-c_{W})\approx\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}|=\nabla c\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}),

and similarly

\frac{1}{2}(c_{EE}-c_{E})\approx\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{E})|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{E}|=\nabla c\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{E}).

Here, the gradient of
c
is approximated from the upwind direction. To be specific, we see that if
\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}>0
, then
\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})
is approximated by values from cells
K
and
W
, which are located along the upwind direction.

To generalise, second-order fluxes are obtained from first-order upwind fluxes by adding a correction term, where the correction term is related to a discrete gradient. In particular, the fluxes
F_{K,\sigma}^{A}
for second-order schemes can be written in the following manner:

F_{K,\sigma}^{A}=\bigg(c_{K}+\widetilde{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}}c_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\bigg)\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}-\bigg(c_{E}+\widetilde{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}}c_{E}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{E})\bigg)\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-},

(13)

where
\widetilde{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}}c_{K}
is a discrete gradient which approximates the value of
\nabla c
at cell
K
.
Different choices on how to reconstruct the discrete gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}}c
then leads to different schemes. For example, we can take
\widetilde{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}}c_{K}
to be
\overline{\nabla}_{K}c
defined as in (
3
). For schemes with cell-centered unknowns, the value of
c_{\sigma}
in (
3
) needs to be chosen. Taking the value of
c_{\sigma}
from the upwind direction then leads to the second-order upwind scheme. On the other hand, taking the value of
c_{\sigma}
from the downwind direction leads to the second-order centered scheme. To extend the description of the fluxes (
13
) onto non-Cartesian meshes, we treat
E
as a generic neighboring cell that shares the edge
\sigma
with
K
. As with Cartesian meshes, using a second-order cell-centered scheme leads to a much wider stencil. The main point to consider is how the term
\widetilde{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}}c_{K}
in (
13
) is computed on generic meshes. Here, we use
\overline{\nabla}_{K}c
defined as in (
3
) as our discrete gradient. This gives us

\widetilde{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}}c_{K}=\frac{1}{|K|}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|(c_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{up}}-c_{K})\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma},

(14)

where the value of
c_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{up}}
is then taken from the upwind direction. That is, we take
c_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{up}}=c_{K}
if
\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}>0
and
c_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{up}}
from the neighboring cell otherwise.

As an example, consider a cell
K
of a triangular mesh with edges
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
being shared with cells
E,W,S
respectively. Taking a velocity field
\mathbf{V}
such that
\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma_{1}}>0
and
\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma_{i}}<0
for the other edges
\sigma_{i},i=2,3
of
K
(see Figure
5
), the discrete derivative is then computed to be

\widetilde{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}}c_{K}=\frac{1}{|K|}\big(|\sigma_{2}|(c_{W}-c_{K})\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma_{2}}+|\sigma_{3}|(c_{S}-c_{K})\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma_{3}}\big).

Figure 5
.
Cell values needed for constructing
\overline{\nabla}_{K}c
for cell-centered schemes.

Since the construction of the advective fluxes
F_{K,\sigma}^{A}
on an edge
\sigma
of cell
K
in (
13
) requires a linear reconstruction both in cell
K
and from its neighboring cell, this requires information not only from the neighboring cells of
K
, but also from the neighbors of its neighbors. Moreover, this generalisation of the second-order upwind fluxes onto arbitrary polygonal meshes comes with an additional computational cost/storage. In particular, the sign of
\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}
needs to be computed/stored for each of the edges of cell
K
and its neighbors in order to determine the value of
c_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{up}}
in computing the discrete gradient (
14
).

4.2.
Fully local second-order fluxes

In this section, we introduce the idea of having a fully local second-order scheme. In order to do so, we employ the concept of hybridisation, as in
[
1
]
. This was done for first order upwind schemes in
[
3
]
by the introduction of additional unknowns, one on each face, leading to the hybridised first-order upwind fluxes

F_{K,\sigma}^{A}=c_{K}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}-c_{\sigma}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-}.

(15)

By doing so, the direct dependence of the fluxes on values from neighboring cells is eliminated. The main idea for this section is to extend the hybridised first-order upwind fluxes into second-order fluxes by writing

F_{K,\sigma}^{A}=\bigg(c_{K}+\widetilde{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}}c_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\bigg)\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}-c_{\sigma}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-}.

(16)

One of the main advantages of using a hybridised scheme is, if information is expected to have arrived from another cell, we keep the value of
c
at
\sigma
to be implicit. Moreover, for second-order schemes, if the discrete gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\mathcal{D}}c
is taken such that
(\widetilde{\nabla}_{\mathcal{D}}c)_{|K}=\overline{\nabla}_{K}c
as defined in (
3
), then the values of
c_{\sigma}
in the discrete gradient are determined naturally, without having to choose between the values in the upwind and downwind direction. This also allows us to save in terms of the computational cost associated with computing
(\widetilde{\nabla}_{\mathcal{D}}c)
.

On the other hand, since we introduced additional unknowns along the edges, we also need to introduce additional equations (one corresponding to each edge). Denoting by
N_{K}
and
N_{e}
denote the number of cells and edges in the mesh, respectively, we then have a scheme with
N_{K}+N_{e}
equations and
N_{K}+N_{e}
unknowns. Using definitions (
2
) and (
16
) for the diffusive and advective fluxes, and denoting

F_{K,\sigma}=F_{K,\sigma}^{D}+F_{K,\sigma}^{A},

the first
N_{k}
equations are given by the balance of fluxes:

\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}=|K|f_{K}.

(17a)

Following this, we impose the conservation of fluxes for interior edges. That is, for cells
K
and
L
that share a common edge
\sigma
,

F_{K,\sigma}+F_{L,\sigma}=0.

(17b)

Finally, on each boundary edge, an equation is needed for imposing the Dirichlet boundary conditions. In particular, for each
\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{ext}}
, we take
c_{\sigma}
to be the average value of
g
over
\sigma
, given by

c_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{|\sigma|}\int_{\sigma}g\,\,ds.

(17c)

Note here that the scheme (
17a
)-(
17c
) consists of
N_{e}
more equations than cell-centered schemes. The main advantage, however, of the hybridised formulation is that the fluxes no longer depend directly on values from neighboring cells, which enables us to use these second-order fluxes even on cells at the boundary of the domain. Moreover, we can employ static condensation for solving the system of equations, which essentially leads to solving only a system of
N_{e}
equations in
N_{e}
unknowns.

5.
Convergence of the scheme

In this section, we study the convergence of a family of hybridised second-order finite volume schemes, with diffusive and advective fluxes as in (
2
) and (
16
), respectively. Since the diffusive fluxes used here come from the HMM method, the convergence analysis for these terms are more or less the same as those found in
[
3
,
6
,
9
]
. For our proofs, we focus on the advective fluxes, and how to deal with the correction term. For simplicity of exposition, we assume that the discrete gradient for the correction term in (
16
) is taken from (
6
). A typical norm used for measuring errors is a discrete
L^{2}
norm. For this, we start by defining a function reconstruction
\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}:X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
, such that for all
q_{h}\in X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}

(\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h})_{|K}=q_{K},\quad\forall K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}.

(18)

The discrete
L^{2}
norm is then defined as

\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}:=\left\|\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}|K|q_{K}^{2}\bigg)^{1/2},\quad\forall q_{h}\in X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}.

(19)

Following the ideas in
[
3
,
5
]
, it is also useful to perform analysis for hybridised schemes by using a
discrete
H^{1}
-like norm on
X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}
:

\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}=\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\frac{|\sigma|}{d_{K,\sigma}}|q_{K}-q_{\sigma}|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2},\quad\forall q_{h}\in X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}.

(20)

We start by specifying the following estimate on the discrete gradient
\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
q_{h}\in X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}
, then

\left\|\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}},

(21)

where
a\lesssim b
means that there is a constant
C
, independent of
h
, such that
a\leq Cb
.

Proof.
Using the definition (
6
) of the discrete gradient and the orthogonality of the stabilisation term (
7
), we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\cdot\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\int_{K}\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\cdot\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\int_{K}\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\cdot\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\int_{K}S_{k}(q_{h})\cdot S_{K}(q_{h})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}.

We start by looking at the consistent term and use the definition (
3
) to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\overline{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\cdot\overline{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\int_{K}\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\cdot\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|(q_{\sigma}-q_{K})\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}.

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz, we then have

\displaystyle\left\|\overline{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\bigg(\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\frac{|\sigma|}{d_{K,\sigma}}(q_{\sigma}-q_{K})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|d_{K,\sigma}(\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\frac{|\sigma|}{d_{K,\sigma}}(q_{\sigma}-q_{K})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|d_{K,\sigma}(\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|d_{K,\sigma}(\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(22)

Using the fact that

(\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma})^{2}\leq\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\cdot\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}

and

\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|d_{K,\sigma}=d|K|,

we then have

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|d_{K,\sigma}(\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}|K|\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\cdot\overline{\nabla}_{K}q_{h}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d}\left\|\overline{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

which upon substitution to (
22
), leads to

\left\|\overline{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq d\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}^{2}.

(23)

Now, for the stabilisation term, we have from (
4
), (
5
), and (
8
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\int_{K}S_{k}(q_{h})\cdot S_{K}(q_{h})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|D_{K,\sigma}|S_{K,\sigma}\cdot S_{K,\sigma}

\displaystyle=d\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\frac{|D_{K,\sigma}|}{d_{K,\sigma}^{2}}(q_{\sigma}-q_{K}-\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\frac{|\sigma|}{d_{K,\sigma}}(q_{\sigma}-q_{K}-\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}))^{2}.

Using the fact that
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
for any real numbers
a,b
, we then have

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\int_{K}S_{k}(q_{h})\cdot S_{K}(q_{h})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\frac{|\sigma|}{d_{K,\sigma}}(q_{\sigma}-q_{K})^{2}

\displaystyle+2\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\frac{|\sigma|}{d_{K,\sigma}}(\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}))^{2}.

Now, the first term is simply
\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}^{2}
, so we only need to deal with the second term, which we will denote by
T_{2}
. Using Cauchy-Schwarz, the regularity of the mesh
(MR2)
, and (
23
) we have that

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}(\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot\overline{\nabla}_{K}q)\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}\frac{|\sigma|}{d_{K,\sigma}}\big(({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}|K|(\overline{\nabla}_{K}q\cdot\overline{\nabla}_{K}q)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}^{2}.

This allows us to conclude that

\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\int_{K}S_{k}(q_{h})\cdot S_{K}(q_{h})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}\lesssim\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}^{2}.

(24)

Combining the inequalities (
23
) and (
24
) then concludes the proof.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let us assume that
(A1)
–
(A3)
hold. Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{h}
be an admissible discretization of
\Omega
such that

\theta>\mathrm{regul}({\mathcal{M}}_{h})
, and let
F_{K,\sigma}^{A}
be the advective flux of
q_{h}\in X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}
given by (
16
) for the velocity
field
\mathbf{V}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})^{d}
. Then there exists a non-negative
constant
C_{1}\geq 0
that only depends on
\theta
and
\mathbf{V}
such that

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}:

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h})^{2}{\mathop{\rm div}}(\mathbf{V})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}\leq\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{A}(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})+C_{1}h\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}^{2}.

Proof.

We start by taking note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}+\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}+\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-}),

so that we can write the fluxes (
16
) as

F_{K,\sigma}^{A}=\frac{1}{2}(q_{K}+q_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}+\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-})(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})+\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}.

(26)

Using (
26
) and the fact that
\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}+\mathbf{V}_{K^{\prime},\sigma}=0
for two cells
K
and
K^{\prime}
sharing
\sigma
, we then obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{A}(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\frac{|\sigma|}{2}(q_{K}^{2}-q_{\sigma}^{2})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\frac{|\sigma|}{2}(\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}+\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-})(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\frac{|\sigma|}{2}q_{K}^{2}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\frac{|\sigma|}{2}(\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}+\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-})(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\frac{|\sigma|}{2}q_{K}^{2}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}.

Using the fact that

\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}=\int_{K}{\mathop{\rm div}}(\mathbf{V})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}},

we then have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}q_{h})^{2}{\mathop{\rm div}}(\mathbf{V})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

(27)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{K}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{A}(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma})(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}.

Now, upon applying Cauchy-Schwarz, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma})(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}

\displaystyle\,\,\leq\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\bigg(\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\bigg(\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\,\,\leq\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\bigg(|\sigma|\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\,\,\lesssim\bigg(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|(\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K}\cdot\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K})\big(({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma})\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma})\big)\bigg)^{1/2}h^{1/2}\left\|(q_{h},q_{{\mathcal{E}}_{h}})\right\|_{1,D_{h},\mathcal{E}_{h}}

\displaystyle\,\,\lesssim h\left\|\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}},

which, together with (
21
), leads to

\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\nabla_{\mathcal{D}}q_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})(q_{K}-q_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}\lesssim h\left\|q_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}^{2}.

Substituting the above inequality into (
27
) then leads us to (
25
).

Lemma 5.3
.

Let us assume that
(A1)
-
(A3)
hold. Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{h}
be an admissible discretization of
\Omega

such that
\theta>\mathrm{regul}(D_{h})
, and let
F_{K,\sigma}^{A}
be the advective flux given by (
16
) for

\mathbf{V}\in C^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
. Then, for all solutions

c_{h}
to the scheme (
17a
)-(
17c
) with diffusive and advective fluxes defined as in (
2
) and (
16
), we have

\left\|c_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}^{2}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|c_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+h\left\|c_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}^{2}.

(28)

Proof.
Take

\int_{\Omega}f\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\int_{K}fc_{K}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

and let
c_{h}
be the solution to the scheme. Then, using the conservation of fluxes, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|(F_{K,\sigma}^{D}+F_{K,\sigma}^{A})c_{K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{D}(c_{K}-c_{\sigma})+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{A}(c_{K}-c_{\sigma}).

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz then leads us to

\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}F_{K,\sigma}^{D}(c_{K}-c_{\sigma})+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}F_{K,\sigma}^{A}(c_{K}-c_{\sigma})\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|c_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Using the definition (
2
) for the diffusive fluxes, we have

\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{D}(c_{K}-c_{\sigma})=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\int_{K}\Lambda_{K}\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\cdot\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}.

We then use the assumption
(A1)
on
\Lambda
and
[

, Lemma 13.11]
to establish that

\left\|c_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{D}(c_{K}-c_{\sigma}).

Combining this with the inequality (

), and using the fact that

then allows us to conclude the proof.

5.2.
Convergence result

In this section, we show that the numerical solution of the scheme given by (
17a
)-(
17c
), with diffusive and advective fluxes defined as in (
2
),(
16
) converges to the weak solution of (
1
). For simplicity of exposition, we consider homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Definition 5.4

(Weak solution of the advection-diffusion problem (
1
))
.

We say that
c\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is the weak solution of (
1
) if for any
\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)

\int_{\Omega}\Lambda\nabla c\cdot\nabla\psi\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}-\int_{\Omega}c\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla\psi\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}=\int_{\Omega}f\psi\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(29)

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
c\in H^{1}(\Omega)
be the weak solution to (
1
). Under assumptions
(A1)
–
(A3)
,
let
c_{h}\in X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}
be the numerical solution to the scheme (
17a
)-(
17c
), with diffusive and advective fluxes constructed as in (
2
) and (
16
). Then, for
h\rightarrow 0
, the following hold:

(1)

\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\rightarrow c
in
L^{r}(\Omega)
for all
r<\frac{2d}{d-2}

(2)

\overline{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\rightarrow\nabla c
in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
.

Proof.
Using Lemma
5.3
, we have
\left\|c_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}^{2}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
when
h
is small. In view of Lemma
9.1
, we obtain an upper bound on
\left\|c_{h}\right\|_{1,{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}
. Then the result of Lemma
9.2
implies
the existence of a function
c\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that, up to a subsequence,
\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\rightarrow c
in
L^{r}(\Omega)
for all

r<\frac{2d}{d-2}
and
\overline{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\rightarrow\nabla c
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
. Now,
consider
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and for
K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h},\sigma\in{\mathcal{E}}_{h}
write
\varphi_{K}=\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}),\varphi_{\sigma}=\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma})
. Let
c_{h}\in X_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}
be the numerical solution to (
17a
)-(
17c
).
We multiply the balance of flux equations (
17a
) by
\varphi_{K}
and take the sum over
K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\int_{K}f\varphi_{K}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}\varphi_{K}.

Using the conservation of internal fluxes, we then have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f\varphi_{h}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}(\varphi_{K}-\varphi_{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{D}(\varphi_{K}-\varphi_{\sigma})

\displaystyle+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\bigg(\big(c_{K}+\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\big)\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}-c_{\sigma}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-}\bigg)(\varphi_{K}-\varphi_{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{D}(\varphi_{K}-\varphi_{\sigma})+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|c_{K}\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}(\varphi_{K}-\varphi_{\sigma})

\displaystyle+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|(c_{K}-c_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{-}(\varphi_{K}-\varphi_{\sigma})

\displaystyle+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\big(\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{K}\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\big)\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}^{+}(\varphi_{K}-\varphi_{\sigma}).

We then write the right hand side of the above equation as
T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4}
.
For the term
T_{1},
we write a Taylor expansion

\varphi_{\sigma}=\varphi_{K}+\nabla\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})+R_{K,\sigma}(\varphi),

where
R_{K,\sigma}(\varphi)\lesssim h^{2}\left\|\nabla^{2}\varphi\right\|_{\infty}
. This leads to

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{D}\nabla\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\cdot({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\sigma})+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|F_{K,\sigma}^{D}R_{K,\sigma}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=T_{1,1}+T_{1,2}.

Using the definition (
2
) of the diffusive flux and the orthogonality of the stabilisation term (
7
), we then get

\displaystyle T_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}}\int_{K}\nabla_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\cdot\nabla\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\overline{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\cdot(\nabla\varphi)_{h}\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}},

where
(\nabla\varphi)_{h}
is defined such that
\big((\nabla\varphi)_{h}\big)_{|K}=\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{K})
for each
K\in{\mathcal{M}}_{h}
.
Using the weak convergence of
\overline{\nabla}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}
and the strong convergence of
(\nabla\varphi)_{h}
, we have that as
h\rightarrow 0
,

T_{1,1}\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}\Lambda\nabla c\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(30)

Also, as
h\rightarrow 0
,
T_{1,2}\rightarrow 0
.
Now, considering
T_{2}
, we have that

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}c_{K}\varphi_{K}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}-\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}c_{K}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}|\sigma|\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}\varphi_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}\int_{K}c_{K}\varphi_{K}{\mathop{\rm div}}(\mathbf{V})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}-\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}c_{K}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\int_{\sigma}\varphi\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\,\,ds

\displaystyle+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}c_{K}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\int_{\sigma}(\varphi-\varphi_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\,\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}\varphi_{h}{\mathop{\rm div}}(\mathbf{V})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}-\int_{\Omega}\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}{\mathop{\rm div}}(\varphi\mathbf{V})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}c_{K}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\int_{\sigma}(\varphi-\varphi_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\,\,ds.

Due to the strong convergence of
\Pi_{{\mathcal{D}}_{h}}c_{h}
, we see that the first two terms on the right hand side converges to

\int_{\Omega}c\varphi{\mathop{\rm div}}(\mathbf{V})\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}-\int_{\Omega}c{\mathop{\rm div}}(\varphi V)\,\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
We now consider the third term. Due to the fact that
\mathbf{V}_{K,\sigma}

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{M}}}c_{K}\sum_{\sigma\in{{{\mathcal{E}}}_{K}}}\int_{\sigma}(\varphi-\varphi_{\sigma})\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{K,\sigma}\,\,ds\bigg|

6.1.

We start by performing a test over a one dimensional domain

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a fully local hybridised second-order finite volume scheme for advection-diffusion equations. We then presented a convergence proof for these hybridised second-order schemes, which also cover flux-limited variants. Numerical results were then presented to compare the hybridised second-order scheme with the hybridised upwind scheme, and the classical cell-centered second-order scheme. Firstly, we note that the hybridised second-order scheme provided solutions which are second-order convergent, and gradients which are first-order convergent. It was also shown that the hybridised second-order scheme had a good
\epsilon
-sensitivity. Upon comparison with the cell-centered scheme, the hybridised scheme can achieve the same level or even better accuracy on much coarser meshes. Moreover, the hybridised scheme can straightforwardly be extended onto generic meshes, whereas further improvement on the approximate gradient (
14
) is needed in order to apply the cell-centered schemes onto generic meshes, otherwise the convergence reduces to first order. Another advantage of the hybridised scheme over the cell-centered second-order schemes is that the stencil only depends on local values, and does not need information from neighboring cells, which allows it to be straightforwardly implemented near the boundaries of the domain. Moreover, static condensation can be employed in order to implement the hybridised scheme efficiently. Comparison with hybridised upwind schemes also shows the advantage of the hybridised second-order schemes in terms of the convergence of the solution and the gradient. However, in some instances, for solutions with boundary layers, the solution from the hybridised second-order scheme exhibited non-physical oscillations. This is expected from linear second-order schemes. In comparison, the hybridised upwind scheme does not encounter such problems. In order to mitigate these non-physical oscillations, we propose an idea which involves introducing artificial vanishing diffusion to the hybridised second-order scheme. This reduces the order of convergence to 1.5, but still offers an improvement over the hybridised upwind scheme. One prospect for future work would involve trying to determine when artificial diffusion is needed, and locate on which cells it needs to be introduced, so that the second-order accuracy is preserved over regions for which the solution is smooth. Another avenue for future work would involve extending these ideas to hybrid high order schemes
[
15
]
, with the aim of obtaining order
h^{(k+2)}
estimates for polynomials of degree
k
in advection-dominated regimes. We also aim to extend these ideas to time-dependent advection diffusion equations.
