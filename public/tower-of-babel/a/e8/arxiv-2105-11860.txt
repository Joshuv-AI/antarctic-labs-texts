Abstract
. The notion of dichotomous matrices is introduced as a natural generalization of essentially Hermitian matrices. A criterion for arrowhead matrices to be dichotomous is established, along with necessary and sufficient conditions for such matrices to be unitarily irreducible. The Gau–Wu number (i.e., the maximal number k ⁡ ( A ) k(A) of orthonormal vectors x j x_{j} such that the scalar products ⟨ A ​ x j , x j ⟩ \langle Ax_{j},x_{j}\rangle lie on the boundary of the numerical range of A A ) is computed for a class of arrowhead matrices A A of arbitrary size, including dichotomous ones. These results are then used to completely classify all 4 × 4 4\times 4 matrices according to the values of their Gau–Wu numbers.

1.
Introduction

The
numerical range

W(A)
of an
n\times n
matrix
A
is by definition the image of the unit sphere of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
under the mapping
f_{A}\colon x\mapsto\langle Ax,x\rangle
, where
\langle.,.\rangle
is the standard scalar product
on the space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

The set
W(A)
is compact and convex and has a piecewise smooth boundary
\partial W(A)
. The maximal number
k(A)

of orthonormal vectors
x_{j}
such that
z_{j}:=f_{A}(x_{j})\in\partial W(A)
was introduced in
[
4
]
and called
the
Gau–Wu number
of
A
in
[
2
]
. Clearly,
k(A)\leq n
, and it is not hard to see (
[
4
, Lemma 4.1]
) that
k(A)\geq 2
. Thus
k(A)=2
for all
2\times 2
matrices.

A description of matrices for which the bound
k(A)=n
is attained was given in
[
10
, Theorem 2.7]
; see also

[
2
, Section 4]
. In Section
3
we derive a more constructive version of this description for the case of arrowhead matrices and also provide conditions sufficient for
k(A)
to attain its smallest possible value 2. Some preliminary results about these matrices, along with the definition, are provided in Section
2
. Building on the result for
3\times 3
matrices from
[
10
, Proposition 2.11]
, in Section
4
we complete the classification of Gau–Wu numbers of arbitrary
4\times 4
matrices, making a step forward.

2.
Unitary (ir)reducibility of arrowhead matrices

An
arrowhead matrix
is a matrix that has all its non-zero entries on the main diagonal, in the last row, and in the last column. In other words, it is a matrix of the form

\quad A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&0&\ldots&0&b_{1}\\
0&a_{2}&\ldots&0&b_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&a_{n-1}&b_{n-1}\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&\ldots&c_{n-1}&a_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

(1)

When
n\leq 2
, every matrix is an arrowhead matrix. We will therefore focus on the situation when
n\geq 3
in this paper.

Sometimes it will be convenient for us to write (
1
) as

\quad A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{0}&b\\
c&\mu\end{bmatrix},

(2)

where
A_{0}=\operatorname{diag}[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}]
,
\mu=a_{n}
,
b
is the column
[b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n-1}]^{T}
, and
c
is the row
[c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n-1}]
.

Our first observation is trivial, and we put it in writing (without proof) for convenience of reference only.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of (
1
) different from its diagonal entries
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}
. Then

\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{b_{j}c_{j}}{\lambda-a_{j}}+a_{n}-\lambda=0,

(3)

the respective eigenspace is one-dimensional, and it is spanned by the vector
x
with the
coordinates

\quad x_{j}=\frac{b_{j}}{\lambda-a_{j}},\ j=1,\ldots,n-1;\quad x_{n}=1.

(4)

Recall that
\lambda
is a
normal eigenvalue
of a given matrix
A
if there is a corresponding eigenvector
x

of
A
which at the same time is an eigenvector of
A^{*}
;
x
is then called a
reducing eigenvector
of
A
, and it corresponds to the eigenvalue
\overline{\lambda}
of
A^{*}
. Of course, a matrix having a normal eigenvalue is unitarily reducible; the converse is true for
n=2
or 3 but not in higher dimensions.

Arrowhead matrices having at least one normal eigenvalue are characterized by the following statement.

Proposition 2
.

The matrix (
1
) has a normal eigenvalue if and only if at least one of the following (not mutually exclusive) conditions holds:

(i)

b_{j}=c_{j}=0
for at least one
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
;

(ii)

a_{i}=a_{j}
and
b_{i}\overline{c_{j}}=b_{j}\overline{c_{i}}
for some
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
,
i\neq j
;

(iii)

a_{i}=a_{j}=a_{k}
for some triple of distinct
i,j,k\in\{1,\ldots.n-1\}
;

(iv)

\left|b_{j}\right|=\left|c_{j}\right|\neq 0
for all
j=1,\ldots,n-1
, and the lines
\ell_{j}
passing through
a_{j}
and forming the angle
(\arg{b_{j}}+\arg{c_{j}})/2
with the positive real half-axis either all coincide or all intersect at (then defined uniquely)
\lambda
satisfying (
3
).

Proof.

First we will prove that
A
has a normal eigenvalue
\lambda
associated with a respective eigenvector
x
, the last coordinate of which is equal to zero, if and only if (i), (ii), or (iii) holds.

Indeed, for such
x
, conditions

\quad Ax=\lambda x\text{ and }A^{*}x=\overline{\lambda}x

(5)

can be rewritten as the homogeneous system of linear equations

\quad\begin{aligned} (a_{j}-\lambda)x_{j}&=0,&j=1,\ldots,n-1,\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}c_{j}x_{j}&=0,&\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\overline{b}_{j}x_{j}=0.\end{aligned}

(6)

For
\lambda
different from all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}
, the first
n-1
equations imply that
x=0
, and so these
\lambda
’s cannot be normal eigenvalues.

If
\lambda=a_{j}
for exactly one
j
, we arrive at a non-trivial solution if and only if the respective
b_{j},c_{j}
are equal to zero. This is case (i).

If
\lambda=a_{i}=a_{j}\neq a_{k}
for some
i\neq j
and all other
k
, with
i,j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, then the first line of (
6
) is equivalent to
x_{k}=0
if
k\neq i,j
, and a non-trivial solution of (
6
) exists if and only if this is true for

c_{i}x_{i}+c_{j}x_{j}=0,\text{ and }\overline{b_{i}}x_{i}+\overline{b_{j}}x_{j}=0,

i.e., if and only if we are in case (ii).

Finally, if
a_{i}=a_{j}=a_{k}
with distinct
i,j,k
—that is, we are in case (iii)—we have that a non-trivial solution
\{x_{i},x_{j},x_{k}\}
to

c_{i}x_{i}+c_{j}x_{j}+c_{k}x_{k}=0,\text{ and }\overline{b_{i}}x_{i}+\overline{b_{j}}x_{j}+\overline{b_{k}}x_{k}=0,

with all other coordinates of
x
set to zero, delivers a non-trivial solution to (
6
).

In what follows, we may suppose that (i) does not hold, i.e., for every
j=1,\ldots,n-1
at least one of the off diagonal entries
b_{j},c_{j}
is different from zero. Equations (
5
) then imply that

a_{j}x_{j}+b_{j}x_{n}=\lambda x_{j},\quad\overline{a_{j}}x_{j}+\overline{c_{j}}x_{n}=\overline{\lambda}x_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,n-1.

The reducing eigenvector
x
with
x_{n}\neq 0
may therefore correspond only to an eigenvalue different from
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}
. Applying Lemma
1
(also with
A
replaced by
A^{*}
) we reach the conclusion that (
5
) holds if and only if (
3
) is satisfied, and

b_{j}/(\lambda-a_{j})=\overline{c_{j}/(\lambda-a_{j})},\quad j=1,\ldots,n-1.

Equivalently,

\left|b_{j}\right|=\left|c_{j}\right|\text{ and }\arg(\lambda-a_{j})=(\arg b_{j}+\arg c_{j})/2\mod\pi.

This is in turn equivalent to (iv). ∎

From the proof of Proposition
2
it is clear that
{\rm(i)}\vee{\rm(ii)}\vee{\rm(iii)}
holds if and only if there exists a joint eigenvector of
A
and
A^{*}
orthogonal to
e_{n}
. Interestingly, the absence of such eigenvectors implies certain restrictions on reducing subspaces of
A
.

Lemma 3
.

Let an
n\times n
arrowhead matrix
A
be such that its reducing eigenvectors (if any) are not orthogonal to
e_{n}
. Then a reducing subspace of
A
that contains at least one of the standard basis vectors
e_{j}
coincides with the whole space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
L
be a subspace invariant both under
A
given by (
1
) and
A^{*}
. If
L
contains
e_{j}
for some
j<n
, then also
b_{j}e_{n}\in L
and
\overline{c_{j}}e_{n}\in L
. Since the negation of (i) from Proposition
2
holds, we conclude that
e_{n}\in L
. Thus, this case is reduced to
e_{n}\in L
.

Using notation (
2
), observe that from
e_{n}\in L
it follows that
Ae_{n}-\mu e_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}b\\
0\end{bmatrix}

and
A^{*}e_{n}-\overline{\mu}e_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}c^{*}\\
0\end{bmatrix}
also lie in
L
. Moreover, along with every
x=\begin{bmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
x_{n}\end{bmatrix}\in L
we also have
\begin{bmatrix}A_{0}x^{\prime}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\in L
.
Using this property repeatedly, we see that

\begin{bmatrix}A_{0}^{m}b\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}A_{0}^{m}c^{*}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\in L\text{ for all }m=0,1,\ldots

The negation of
{\rm(i)}\vee{\rm(ii)}\vee{\rm(iii)}
means exactly that the system of vectors
\{A_{0}^{m}b,A_{0}^{m}c^{*}\colon m=0,1,\ldots\}
has the full rank
n-1
, thus implying that
L
contains all
the vectors
e_{j}
and not just
e_{n}
.
In other words,
L
is the whole space.
∎

We note that if in the setting of Proposition
2
case (iv) all the lines
\ell_{j}
coincide, condition (
3
) then implies that
\arg(\lambda-a_{n})
is the same as all
\arg(\lambda-a_{j})
,
j=1,\ldots,n-1
. According to
[
5
, Proposition 2]
, this is exactly the situation when
A
is normal and, moreover,
essentially Hermitian
; the latter means that
A
is a linear combination of a Hermitian matrix and the identity.

Another way to look at essentially Hermitian matrices is as follows: given
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, consider the minimal number of distinct eigenvalues attained by the Hermitian (equivalently: skew-Hermitian) part of the “rotated” matrix
e^{i\theta}A
. A matrix is essentially Hermitian if and only if this number is the minimal possible, i.e. equals one.

From this point of view it is very natural to introduce the class of matrices for which this number equals two. Let us call such matrices
dichotomous
.
We want to describe all dichotomous arrowhead matrices. Let us start with the simplest case when the matrix itself is Hermitian, with 0 and 1 as its eigenvalues. In other words,
A=P
is a self-adjoint involution
(in operator terms, an orthogonal projection).

Lemma 4
.

An arrowhead matrix
P
is such that
P=P^{*}=P^{2}
if and only if it is either
(i)
diagonal, with all the diagonal entries equal 0 or 1, or
(ii)
has the form

\quad P=\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}&0&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&0\\
0&\ddots&&&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&p_{i-1}&&&&&0\\
\vdots&&&t&&&&\alpha\\
\vdots&&&&p_{i+1}&&&0\\
\vdots&&&&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&&&p_{n-1}&0\\
0&\dots&0&\overline{\alpha}&0&\ldots&0&1-t\end{bmatrix}.

(7)

Here
t\in(0,1)
,
\left|\alpha\right|=\sqrt{t(1-t)}
,
\alpha
and
\overline{\alpha}
are the
i
th elements of the last column and row, respectively, and
p_{j}\in\{0,1\}
for all
j=1,\ldots n-1
with
j\neq i
.

Proof.

Sufficiency
can be checked by a simple direct computation. To prove
necessity
, observe first of all that if an arrowhead matrix
P
is self-adjoint, then its diagonal entries are real while the block representation takes the form

P=\begin{bmatrix}P_{0}&\omega\\
\omega^{*}&\mu\end{bmatrix}.

Condition
P^{2}=P
implies in particular that

\quad\omega\omega^{*}=P_{0}-P_{0}^{2}.

(8)

Since the right hand side of (
8
) is diagonal along with
P_{0}
, the entries of
\omega
are such that
\omega_{j}\overline{\omega_{k}}=0
whenever
j\neq k
. Thus, at most one of them can be different from zero. In the case that
\omega=0
, the matrix
P
is diagonal, and so (i) holds.

Suppose therefore that for some
i
we have
\omega_{i}:=\alpha\neq 0
. Then, up to a permutational similarity,
P
is the direct sum of the diagonal
matrix
\operatorname{diag}[p_{1},\ldots,p_{i-1},p_{i+1},\ldots,p_{n-1}]
and the
2\times 2
block
P^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{i}&\alpha\\
\overline{\alpha}&\mu\end{bmatrix}
. Since
P^{\prime}
is an orthogonal projection with a non-zero off diagonal entry,
P^{\prime}
has rank one and is unitarily similar to
\operatorname{diag}[0,1]
. Denoting
p_{i}:=t
, we thus see that
\mu=1-t
and
\left|\alpha\right|^{2}=p_{i}\mu=t(1-t)
, as described in case (ii).
∎

The description of all dichotomous arrowhead matrices follows.

Proposition 5
.

The arrowhead matrix (
1
) is dichotomous if and only if for some angle
\theta
and distinct real
h_{0},h_{1}
either

(a)

\arg{b_{j}}+\arg{c_{j}}=:2\theta+\pi\text{ does not depend on }j,\quad\left|b_{j}\right|=\left|c_{j}\right|

(9)

for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
, and

\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\theta}a_{j})\text{ equals }h_{0}\text{ or }h_{1}

(10)

for
j=1,\ldots,n
, with both values attained,

or

(b)

Conditions (
10
) and (
9
) hold for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
, except for one missing
j=i
, and

\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\theta}a_{i})=(1-t)h_{0}+th_{1},\quad\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\theta}a_{n})=th_{0}+(1-t)h_{1},

(11)

\left|e^{-i\theta}b_{i}+e^{i\theta}\overline{c_{i}}\right|=2\left|h_{1}-h_{0}\right|\sqrt{t(1-t)}

for some
t\in(0,1)
.

By convention, the argument condition in (
9
) is considered satisfied whenever
b_{j}=c_{j}=0
. Condition (
10
) means that the respective elements
a_{j}
lie on two parallel lines
forming the angle
\theta
with the positive direction of the
y
-axis. The slope of these lines is defined uniquely by (
9
) if there is at least one non-zero pair
b_{j},c_{j}
.

Proof.

Any matrix
A
is dichotomous if and only if for some
\theta
the self-adjoint matrix
\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\theta}A)
has exactly two distinct eigenvalues, say
h_{0}
and
h_{1}
.
This is equivalent to

\quad\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\theta}A)=h_{0}I+(h_{1}-h_{0})P,

(12)

where
P\neq 0,I
is a self-adjoint idempotent. If this
A
is an arrowhead matrix, then so is
P
. Invoking Lemma
4
completes the proof.
∎

Note that case (a) corresponds to diagonal
P
, while (b) stems from
P
given by (
7
). In the latter case
\arg\alpha
is defined uniquely as
\arg(e^{-i\theta}b_{i}+e^{i\theta}\overline{c_{i}})
.

For our purposes, the case of unitarily irreducible dichotomous matrices is of special interest.

Theorem 6
.

An arrowhead matrix (
1
) is unitarily irreducible and dichotomous if and only if it is as described by Proposition
5
with

\quad a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}\text{ pairwise distinct and }b_{j},c_{j}\neq 0\text{ whenever \eqref{offdiag} holds},

(13)

except when
A
is of type
(b)
with

\quad\operatorname{Im}(e^{-i\theta}A):=K=\begin{bmatrix}k_{1}&0&\ldots&0&m_{1}\\
0&k_{2}&\ldots&0&m_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&k_{n-1}&m_{n-1}\\
\overline{m_{1}}&\overline{m_{2}}&\ldots&\overline{m_{n-1}}&k_{n}\end{bmatrix}

(14)

such that
m_{i}/\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
(where
i
is determined by the position of the entry
t
in (
7
)) and either

(b’)
all
a_{j}
(
j\neq i,n
) lie on the same line (equivalently: all of the respective
p_{j}
in (
7
) take the same value
p_{0}\in\{0,1\}
), and

k_{n}=\lambda-\sum_{j\neq i,n}\frac{\left|m_{j}\right|^{2}}{\lambda-k_{j}}\text{ with }\lambda=k_{i}{+(p_{0}-t)}\frac{m_{i}}{\alpha},

or

(b”)

n=4
,
p_{\sigma(1)}=0
,
p_{\sigma(2)}=1
for some permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,2,3\}
satisfying
\sigma(3)=i
,
while

\quad k_{i}=(1-t)k_{\sigma(1)}+tk_{\sigma(2)},

(15)

\quad(1-t)m_{\sigma(1)}^{2}=tm_{\sigma(2)}^{2}\text{ if }m_{i}+(k_{\sigma(1)}-k_{\sigma(2)})\alpha=0,

\quad\det\begin{bmatrix}k_{\sigma(1)}-k_{i}+t{m_{i}}/{\alpha}&0&m_{\sigma(1)}\\

\overline{m_{\sigma(1)}}&\overline{m_{\sigma(2)}}&k_{4}-k_{i}-(1-2t){m_{i}}/{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}=0.

Proof.

An arrowhead dichotomous matrix
A
meets the description provided by Proposition
5
. Let
S
denote the set of indices

for which (
9
) holds.
In other words,
S=\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
in case (a) and

If one of

equals zero for some

, then the other one is also zero, due to the first condition in (

2
(i) would then imply that

has a normal eigenvalue. Moreover, (

. So, if

, then

We have established that, if

is unitarily irreducible, then indeed

are all distinct for

In what follows, we therefore suppose that (

) holds (this implies that (

) holds for all

) and (

Also, when

.

Observe also that instead of treating unitary (ir)reducibility of

\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\left|m_{j}\right|^{2}}{\lambda-k_{j}}+k_{n}-\lambda=0,

.

We now treat several possible situations separately.

I.

is diagonal.

Applying a permutational similarity (not involving the last row and column so that the arrowhead structure of

If

Choose

. Consequently,

.

II.

To simplify the notation, we use the transposition similarity switching the rows and columns numbered

\quad P=\operatorname{diag}[p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-2}]\oplus\begin{bmatrix}t&\alpha\\

II.b’.

All

Passing from

allows us to switch between the cases

and

, while leading to the change

,
\alpha\mapsto-\alpha

in the second direct summand of (

Let us determine when

3.
Gau–Wu numbers of some arrowhead matrices

According to
[

, a unitarily irreducible

matrix

has Gau–Wu number

if and only if it is dichotomous. The results of Section
2
therefore immediately imply

Theorem 7
.

An arrowhead matrix (
1
) is unitarily irreducible with the Gau–Wu number
k(A)=n
if and only if it is as described in Proposition
5
, where, in addition,
a_{j}
are distinct and

in (
9
) are non-zero, and we also are not in either of the situations b’, b” of Theorem
6
.

Condition (
9
) of Proposition

guarantees the “diagonalization after rotation”
of the Hermitian part of

, while condition (
11
) is responsible for the fact that the resulting matrix has at most two distinct eigenvalues. A more detailed analysis shows that

can be computed explicitly under condition (

) alone (provided that it holds for all
j=1,\ldots,n-1
). Let us first consider the leading case when all

are different from zero.

Theorem 8
.

Let an arrowhead matrix (
1
) satisfy (
9
) with
b_{j},c_{j}\neq 0
;

. Then

equals the sum of multiplicities of the largest and the smallest eigenvalues of

\operatorname{Im}(e^{-i\theta}A)

Proof.

Multiplying
A
by an appropriate non-zero scalar, we may without loss of generality suppose that in (
9
)
\theta=0
, i.e.,
c_{j}=-\overline{b_{j}}
. The matrix

the set of indices corresponding to the extremal (maximal and minimal)

lie on the vertical supporting lines

of

, and thus on its boundary, for all

, where

is the cardinality of

Before proving the reverse inequality, we observe that an appropriate diagonal unitary similarity allows us to adjust the arguments of all

to the value

, thus guaranteeing
c_{j}=b_{j}=im_{j}
with
m_{j}>0
for

and define

as in equation (
14

and

respectively denote the diagonal entries of

For every

\xi_{+}(t)
of the matrix

\quad H+tK=\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}+tk_{1}&0&\ldots&0&tm_{1}\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&h_{n-1}+tk_{n-1}&tm_{n-1}\\
tm_{1}&tm_{2}&\ldots&tm_{n-1}&h_{n}+tk_{n}\end{bmatrix}

is strictly bigger than the diagonal entries
h_{j}+tk_{j}
,

. Formulas (
4

) take the form

implying that all the entries of the respective eigenvector

are positive.
So
\langle x^{+}(t_{1}),x^{+}(t_{2})\rangle>0

.

Similarly, the minimal eigenvalue
\xi_{-}(t)
of the matrix (

) is strictly smaller than
h_{j}+tk_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,n-1
, the respective eigenvector
x^{-}(t)
has the entries

x^{-}_{j}(t)=\frac{t^{2}m_{j}^{2}}{\xi_{-}(t)-h_{j}-tk_{j}},\ j=1,\ldots,n-1;\quad x^{-}_{n}=1,

the first

of them being negative, and so

Denote by

the eigenspace of

, the set

∎

Theorem 11

This means that each support line of

{x}(\theta)=\left[\frac{\left(e^{-i\theta}\overline{c_{1}}+e^{i\theta}b_{1}\right)}{2\left(\xi(\theta)-\operatorname{Re}\left(a_{1}e^{i\theta}\right)\right)},\ldots,\frac{\left(e^{-i\theta}\overline{c_{n-1}}+e^{i\theta}b_{n-1}\right)}{2\left(\xi(\theta)-\operatorname{Re}\left(a_{n-1}e^{i\theta}\right)\right)},1\right]^{T}.
