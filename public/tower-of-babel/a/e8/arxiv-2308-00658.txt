Abstract
The emerging fifth generation and the upcoming sixth generation communication technologies introduce the use of space- and airborne networks in their architectures under the scope of non-terrestrial networks. With this integration of satellite and aerial platform networks, better coverage, network flexibility and easier deployment can be achieved. Correspondingly, satellite broadband internet providers have launched an increasing number of small satellites operating in low earth orbit. These recent developments imply an increased electromagnetic field exposure to humans and the environment. In this work, we provide a short overview of the state of consumer-grade satellite networks including broadband satellites and future non-terrestrial network services. We also consider the regulatory state governing their operation within the context of electromagnetic field exposure. Finally, we highlight the aspects that are relevant to the assessment of electromagnetic field exposure in relation to non-terrestrial networks.

I
Introduction

Discussions about the next sixth generation (6G) of communications systems are well on course in both the academia and industry communities. Innovative solutions and disruptive technologies are being proposed to account for the shortcomings and challenges that faced previous generations. One of the challenges faced by current terrestrial networks is the difficulty and non-feasibility of providing global coverage to users in remote areas. The infrastructure deployment for terrestrial networks is often costly, rigid, and requires meticulous planning. Not to mention, in the case of natural disasters, how prone terrestrial networks are to breakdowns and how challenging it could be to restore their operation.

Non-terrestrial networks (NTNs) are seen as one of the appealing solutions to complement terrestrial networks services and expand their coverage to remote areas around the globe. Recent advancements in the satellite and space industries lowered launch costs for satellite systems and advanced the markets for consumer-service based deployments. Nowadays, the Third Generation Partnership Project is laying out the foundations for integrating non-terrestrial networks in the next generation of communication networks. With sixth generation, two connectivity scenarios would be possible. Namely, direct connectivity between a satellite or an aerial platform and handset devices as well as indirect connectivity that exploit the satellites’ connectivity in the backhaul
[
1
]
.

On the other hand, over the past decade, several companies announced revolutionary plans to provide high-speed internet access using constellations of a large number of satellites to serve user terminals around the globe.
This increasing interest in bringing non-terrestrial networks closer to end-users raises questions about how would such deployments affect the overall level of electromagnetic field (EMF) exposure experienced by humans and the environments.

For terrestrial networks, organizations such as the World Health Organization, International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection, Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, and International Telecommunication Union are concerned with evaluating research and studies on human electromagnetic field exposure
[
2
,
3
,

. The outcomes of their evaluation is used to derive safe limits and guidelines to restrict the levels of electromagnetic field exposure. In this work, we consider previous efforts done to study electromagnetic field exposure related to satellite communications.

II
Non-Terrestrial Networks

Interest in leveraging satellite and airborne networks for civilian applications has been gaining momentum over the recent years. Compared to terrestrial networks, satellite networks have the advantage of a larger coverage area which translates to better accessibility to remote and rural areas where the deployment of terrestrial networks can be challenging, not feasible, or not possible. non-terrestrial networks could also ensure a fallback solution in natural disasters situations on the occasion that terrestrial networks’ services are disrupted.
Within the scope of sixth generation networks, the introduction and integration of non-terrestrial networks in the overall network architecture play an essential role in providing global network coverage and improving the network’s resiliency.
This will not be the first attempt to extend the coverage of terrestrial networks via satellite connectivity
[
7
]
, nor to provide mobile satellite services to users around the globe
[
8
]
. However, the advent of the new low-cost space- and air-borne vehicles as well as the substantial development of communications technologies and markets makes the reintroduction of non-terrestrial networks to support terrestrial networks quite attractive.

In this section we provide an overview of the state of the current and upcoming satellite networks, focusing particularly on those providing direct links of two-way satellite communications to user terminals and handheld devices. We first consider the Third Generation Partnership Project’s ongoing work on non-terrestrial networks, followed by existing examples of mega-constellations of non-geostationary satellite orbit satellite systems.

As per the Third Generation Partnership Project’s vision, the scope of non-terrestrial networks includes spaceborne systems operating in the geostationary earth orbit, non-geostationary satellite orbit (including low earth orbit (LEO) and medium earth orbit), as well as airborne platforms such as high altitude platform station and unmanned aerial systems

Whereas the latter is concerned with massive machine type communication applications, providing satellite connectivity to Internet of Things devices.

II-B

Satellite Networks

Over the last decade, we witnessed a surge in the number of satellites being launched into space. With companies such as SpaceX, OneWeb, Telesat, and Blue Origin racing to kick-off their mega-constellations by sending hundreds of low earth orbit satellites to space
[
16
]
. The main goal of low earth orbit mega-constellations is to provide global broadband internet access to end-users over a fixed satellite service. The architecture of mega-constellation satellite systems consists of three main components: the constellation of satellites in low earth orbit, a network of ground gateway stations, and user terminals; in the form of very small aperture terminals. We consider here aspects of two of the currently largest constellations, namely, SpaceX and OneWeb.

In 2015, SpaceX announced its Starlink program with initial plans to deploy more than 4000 low earth orbit satellites in orbit. Starlink satellites are operated on circular orbits at an altitude of 550 km. As of the time of this writing, more than 3500 of Starlink’s mini-satellites are in-orbit. The Ku and Ka satellite frequency bands are used for the satellite-to-user and satellite-to-ground links, respectively
[
17
]
. The satellite and earth terminals support beamforming transmissions using phase-array antennas. Each satellite could support at least 8 beams. The peak effective isotropic radiated power values for the user’s downlink could reach 36.71 dBW
[
17
]
.

OneWeb’s constellation is planned to operate polar orbit planes at an altitude of 1200 km. A total number of 648 satellites are to be delivered in orbit to establish a global coverage of OneWeb’s satellite network. Between the satellite and user terminals, the Ku band is used for communications while the Ka band is allocated for the satellite to gateway links. OneWeb satellites can support up to 16 beams. The maximum effective isotropic radiated power for the user’s downlink is around 34.6 dBW.

III
Measurements considerations and challenges

The recent growing interest in ubiquitous satellite communications with direct connectivity to user equipments calls for more measurement and simulation studies on the exposure levels to users and the environment. Measurement studies on electromagnetic field exposure near satellite earth stations are scarce and the emerging technologies to be implemented in both terrestrial networks and non-terrestrial networks require more attention to their implications on the electromagnetic field exposure levels. Table
III
summarizes some of the aspects that might prove relevant when comparing between electromagnetic field exposure assessment methods for terrestrial networks and non-terrestrial networks.

Hankin in
[
22
]
, supported by the US Environmental Protection Agency, provides one of the earliest and few measurement studies assessing the electromagnetic field exposure levels from satellite communication systems. He first evaluated mathematically the electromagnetic field exposure in terms of power density as a function of distance from the source, then he performed measurements in the vicinity of the sources to determine the actual exposure levels. However, the study considers satellite communication earth terminals transmitting at an effective isotropic radiated power in the orders of megawatts, unlike the case for non-terrestrial networks which will transmit at considerably lower powers. Moreover, such terminals are normally located away from the general population and are within sites that are restricted to public access. Besides, electromagnetic field measurement equipment and satellite communication technologies have evolved significantly since the 1970s, when this study took place.

More recent measurement studies were conducted in
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
, where the authors evaluated the electromagnetic field exposure using broadband electromagnetic field meters in the vicinity of maritime satellite transmitters and satellite earth terminals, respectively. They later on compared it to the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection guidelines levels for occupational and general public scenarios.
In
[
25
]
, the authors consider the exposure from large-phased array antennas used to communicate with low earth orbit satellite systems and provide a device-based time-averaging method to estimate the electromagnetic field exposure based on the time-averaged power density.

In this section we present a few considerations on electromagnetic field exposure measurements for non-terrestrial networks communications scenarios.

III-A

Downlink: space-to-earth

Path loss
. By the time the signal arrives at the earth terminal, it would have experienced high losses due to several factors such as, free space path loss, atmospheric loss, and ionospheric and tropospheric scintillation losses. Free space path loss is dependant on the distance between the transmitter and receiver, which in the case of non-terrestrial networks can be anywhere between 300 km for low earth orbit satellites and could reach up to 35,786 km for geostationary earth orbit satellites. III-B

Uplink: earth-to-space

Path loss
The uplink undergoes similar free space path loss to that experienced by the downlink. As a result, higher antenna gains are required at the user terminals transmitting from earth to space.

Terminal type
. Depending on the earth terminal type, the maximum transmitted power is defined accordingly. For instance, in
[
6
]
the Third Generation Partnership Project has assigned the user equipment power class 3 to user equipments for non-terrestrial network use-cases, which include handheld devices and allows a maximum transmit power of 26 dBm with a power tolerance level of +/- 2 dB for any transmission bandwidth within the channel bandwidth. user equipment power class 3 is the default power class for terrestrial networks as well.
Other terminal types such as very small aperture terminals and earth stations in motions are configured to transmit at higher powers. very small aperture terminals and earth stations in motions would provide users indirect access to non-terrestrial network services. They are planned to be used for FR2 operation in non-terrestrial network.

Antenna type
. In contrast to user equipments, very small aperture terminals and earth stations in motions would use directional antennae such as parabolic antennae, i.e., the resulting electromagnetic field exposure is concentrated in their antennae’s main transmission lobe. Thus additional safety measures need to be taken into account when mounting such terminals to ensure the safety of users.

Service usage profiles
. For handheld user equipment devices, a major component of the electromagnetic field exposure occurs in the antenna’s near-field. Adequate specific absorption rate assessments methods are thus necessary in this case to determine the level of radio frequency power absorbed by the human body
[
3
]
. The usage profile (voice calls, texting, browsing, etc.) influences factors such as the proximity of the device to the body, the duration of usage, and consequently the intensity of electromagnetic field exposure
[
28
]
.

IV
Regulations and standardization

The recent advancements of wireless communications networks and their pervasiveness in our daily lives raised few calls from the public concerning the increased levels of electromagnetic field exposure in the environment. Several organizations, such as the World Health Organization, International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection, Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, International Telecommunication Union and International Electrotechnical Commission, evaluate continuously the research studying the influence of electromagnetic field exposure on humans. They use the outcome of their evaluations to define and maintain the guidelines and recommendations with the goal to protect humans and the environment against any possible harmful effects caused by the exposure to radio-frequency electromagnetic fields
[
3
,
4
,
29
,
30
]
.

The electromagnetic field exposure limits set by the regulatory organizations target the overall maximum possible radiation at a given frequency and location. This maximum level should take into consideration the electromagnetic field exposure due to all different sources and radio access technologys surrounding the point of evaluation. Also, it has to take into account characteristics of the transmissions present, for instance, whether they are continuous or pulsed
[
3
]
. In practice, such assessments are not necessarily straight-forward with regular measurement equipment as they only provide the instantaneous and average values of electromagnetic field radiation at a given location.

The International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection guidelines are the most widely adopted recommendations internationally. They define the reference levels for limiting the electromagnetic field exposure from sources operating at frequencies up to 300 GHz
[
3
]
.
The reference levels are frequency dependant and can be evaluated in terms of the electric field, magnetic field, or the power flux density. V
Conclusions

In this work, we presented a short overview of the state of the next generation non-terrestrial networks and their position in future sixth generation networks. Given this growing interest in non-terrestrial networks, questions on how to assess their possible contribution to the electromagnetic field exposure on humans and the environment need to be taken into account. non-terrestrial networks will bring direct connectivity of user equipments, such as smartphones and tablets, to satellite and air-bone communication systems, which raises questions on the levels of electromagnetic field exposures the users are subject to experience in such scenarios. We provided considerations regarding the implications of the spread of these technologies on electromagnetic field exposure levels in the environment from the downlink and uplink perspectives. We observed there is a shortage of electromagnetic field assessment studies concerned with non-terrestrial network scenarios. More measurement and simulation studies are required to better understand the possible electromagnetic field exposure levels on the uplink and to ensure the compliance of such communications links with the recommended reference levels defined by the respective organizations.
