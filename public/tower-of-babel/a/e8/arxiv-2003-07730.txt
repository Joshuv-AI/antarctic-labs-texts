The Non-Iterative Transformation Method
Riccardo Fazio

Abstract
The Blasius flow is the idealized flow of a viscous fluid past an infinitesimally thick, semi-infinite flat plate. The definition of a non-iterative transformation method for the celebrated Blasius problem is due to Töpfer and dates more than a century ago. Here we define a non-iterative transformation method for Blasius equation with a moving wall, a slip flow condition or a surface gasification. The defined method allows us to deal with classes of problems in boundary layer theory that, depending on a parameter, admit multiple or no solutions. This approach is particularly convenient when the main interest is on the behaviour of the considered models with respect to the involved parameter. The obtained numerical results are found to be in good agreement with those available in literature.



Keywords:
Initial value methods, non-iterative transformation method, BVPs on infinite intervals, Blasius equation, moving wall, slip flow condition, surface gasification.

Recently, Boyd

Thereafter, the quest for a good approximation of

This is seldom the case for the most important problems of applied mathematics:

2
Fluid flow on a flat plate

The model describing
the steady plane flow of a fluid past a thin plate, provided the boundary layer assumptions are verified (the flow has a very thin layer attached to the plate and
v\gg w
), is given by

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}}+{\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial z}}=0

\displaystyle v{\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}}+w{\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}}=\nu{\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial z^{2}}}

(1)

\displaystyle v(y,0)=w(y,0)=0\ ,\quad v(y,z)\rightarrow V_{\infty}\quad\mbox{as}\quad z\rightarrow\infty\ ,

where the governing differential equations, namely conservation of mass and momentum, are the
steady-state 2D Navier-Stokes
equations under the boundary layer approximations,

v
and
w
are the velocity components of the fluid in the

y
and
z
direction,
V_{\infty}

represents the main-stream velocity, see the draft in figure
1
, and
\nu
is the viscosity of the fluid.

Figure 1:
Sketch of boundary layer over a thin plate.

The boundary conditions at
z=0
are based on the
assumption that neither slip nor mass
transfer are permitted at the plate whereas the remaining boundary condition
means that the velocity
v
tends to the main-stream velocity

V_{\infty}
asymptotically.

In order to study this problem, it is convenient to introduce a
potential (stream function)
\psi(y,z)
defined by

v={\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}}\ ,\qquad w=-{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}}\ .

(2)

The physical motivation for introducing this function is that constant

\psi
lines are steam-lines.
The mathematical motivation for introducing such a new variable is that the equation of continuity is satisfied identically, and we have to deal only with the transformed momentum equation.
In fact, introducing the stream function the problem can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle\nu{\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{3}\psi}{\partial z^{3}}}+{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}}{\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial z^{2}}}-{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}}{\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial y\partial z}}=0

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}}(y,0)={\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}}(y,0)=0

(3)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}}(y,z)\rightarrow V_{\infty}\quad\mbox{as}\quad z\rightarrow\infty\ .

2.1
Blasius problem

Blasius
[
4
]
used the following similarity transformation

\eta=z\left(\frac{V_{\infty}}{\nu y}\right)^{1/2}\ ,\qquad f(\eta)=\psi(y,z)\left(\nu yV_{\infty}\right)^{-1/2}\ ,

(4)

that reduces the partial differential model (
2
) to

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}f}{d\eta^{3}}}+\frac{1}{2}f{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}}=0

(5)

\displaystyle f(0)={\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(0)=0\ ,\quad{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\eta)\rightarrow 1\quad\mbox{as}\quad\eta\rightarrow\infty\ ,

i.e., a boundary value problem (BVP) defined on a semi-infinite interval.
Blasius solved this BVP by patching a power series to an asymptotic approximation at some finite value of
\eta
.

3
Töpfer transformation

In order to clarify Töpfer
[
33
]
derivation of a further transformation of variables that reduces the BVP into an initial value problem (IVP) we consider the derivation of the series expansion solution. Of course, some of the coefficients of the series can be evaluated by imposing the boundary conditions at
\eta=0
.
Moreover, we set

\lambda=\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}(0)

(6)

where
\lambda
is a constant different from zero.
So that, we look for a series solution defined as

f(\eta)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\eta^{2}+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}C_{n}\eta^{n}

(7)

where the coefficients
\lambda
and
C_{n}
, for
n=3,4,\dots
, are constants to be determined.
In fact, the boundary values at the plate surface, at
\eta=0
,
require that
C_{0}=C_{1}=0
, and we also have
C_{2}=\lambda/2
by the definition of
\lambda
.
Now, we substitute this series expansion into the governing differential equation, whereupon we find

\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}n(n-1)(n-2)C_{n}\eta^{n-3}\\
+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\eta^{2}+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}C_{n}\eta^{n}\right)\left[\lambda+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}n(n-1)C_{n}\eta^{n-2}\right]=0

(8)

or in expanded form

\left[3\;2\;C_{3}\right]+\left[4\;3\;2\;C_{4}\right]\eta+\left[5\;4\;3\;C_{5}+\frac{1}{2}\;2\;\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{\lambda}{2}\right]\eta^{2}\\
+\left[6\;5\;4\;C_{6}+\frac{1}{2}\;2\;\frac{\lambda}{2}C_{3}+\frac{1}{2}\;\frac{\lambda}{2}\;3\;2\;C_{3}\right]\eta^{3}\\
+\cdots=0\ .

(9)

According to a standard approach, we have to require that all coefficients of the powers of
\eta
to be zero.
It is an easy matter to compute the coefficients of the series expansion in terms of
\lambda
:

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=C_{4}=0\ ,\qquad C_{5}=-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\;5!}

\displaystyle C_{6}

\displaystyle=C_{7}=0\ ,\qquad C_{8}=11\frac{\lambda^{3}}{2^{2}\;8!}

\displaystyle C_{9}

\displaystyle=C_{10}=0\ ,\qquad C_{11}=-375\frac{\lambda^{4}}{2^{3}\;11!}

and so on …

The solution can be written as

f=\frac{\lambda\eta^{2}}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}\eta^{5}}{2\;5!}+\frac{11\;\lambda^{3}\eta^{8}}{2^{2}\;8!}-\frac{375\;\lambda^{4}\eta^{11}}{2^{3}\;11!}+\cdots

(10)

where the only unknown constant is
\lambda
.
In principle,
\lambda
can be determined by imposing the boundary condition at the second point, but, in this case, this cannot be done because the left boundary condition is given at infinity.
However, by modifying the powers of
\lambda
we can rewrite the series expansion as

\lambda^{-1/3}f=\frac{\left(\lambda^{1/3}\eta\right)^{2}}{2}-\frac{\left(\lambda^{1/3}\eta\right)^{5}}{2\;5!}+\frac{11\;\left(\lambda^{1/3}\eta\right)^{8}}{2^{2}\;8!}-\frac{375\;\left(\lambda^{1/3}\eta\right)^{11}}{2^{3}\;11!}+\cdots

(11)

which suggests a transformation of the form

f^{*}=\lambda^{-1/3}f\ ,\qquad\eta^{*}=\lambda^{1/3}\eta\ .

(12)

In the new variables the series expansion becomes

f^{*}=\frac{\eta^{*2}}{2}-\frac{\eta^{*5}}{2\;5!}+\frac{11\;\eta^{*8}}{2^{2}\;8!}-\frac{375\;\eta^{*11}}{2^{3}\;11!}+\cdots

(13)

which does not depend on
\lambda
.
We notice that the governing differential equation and the initial conditions at the free surface, at
\eta=0
, are left invariant by the new variables defined above.
Moreover, the first and second order derivatives transform in the following way

\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}=\lambda^{-2/3}\frac{df}{d\eta}\ ,\qquad\frac{d^{2}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*2}}=\lambda^{-1}\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}\ .

(14)

As a consequence of the definition of
\lambda
we have

\frac{d^{2}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*2}}(0)=1\ ,

(15)

and this explains why in these variables the series expansion does not depend on
\lambda
.
Furthermore, the value of
\lambda
can be found on condition that we have an approximation for
\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\infty)
, say
\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\eta_{\infty})
where
\eta^{*}_{\infty}
is a suitable truncated boundary.
In fact, by the above relation we get

\lambda=\left[\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\eta^{*}_{\infty})\right]^{-3/2}\ .

(16)

From a numerical viewpoint, BVPs must be solved within the computational domain simultaneously (a “stationary” problem), whereas IVPs can be solved by a stepwise procedure (an “evolution” problem).
Somehow, numerically, IVPs are easier than BVPs.

3.1
Töpfer algorithm

Let us list the steps necessary to solve the Blasius problem by the Töpfer algorithm.
In this way, we define a non-iterative (I)TM.
We have to:

1.

solve the auxiliary IVP

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}f*}{d\eta^{*3}}}+\frac{1}{2}f^{*}{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*2}}}=0

(17)

\displaystyle f^{*}(0)={\displaystyle\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}}(0)=0,\qquad{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*2}}}(0)=1

and, in particular, get an approximation for

\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\infty)
;

2.

compute
\lambda
by equation (
16
);

3.

obtain
f(\eta)
,
{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\eta)
, and
{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}}(\eta)
by the inverse transformation of (
12
).

Indeed, Töpfer solved the IVP for the Blasius equation once.
At large but finite
\eta_{j}^{*}
, ordered so that
\eta_{j}^{*}<\eta_{j+1}^{*}
, we can compute by equation (
16
) the corresponding
\lambda_{j}
.
If two subsequent values of
\lambda_{j}
agree within a specified accuracy, then

is approximately equal to the common value of the
\lambda_{j}
, otherwise, we can march to a larger value of
\eta
and try again.
Using the classical fourth order Runge-Kutta method and a grid step
\Delta\eta^{*}=0.1

Töpfer was able to determine

with an error less than

.
He used the two truncated boundaries
\eta_{1}^{*}=4
and
\eta_{2}^{*}=6
.
We notice that for
\eta^{*}\in[0,4]
we get
\lambda_{1}=0.333233336
.
In figure

We notice that this figure shows the solutions of the auxiliary IVP (
1
) and of the BVP (

).

4
Rubel error analysis

The boundary condition at infinity is certainly not suitable for a numerical treatment.
This condition has usually been replaced by the same condition applied at a truncated boundary, see Collatz
[
7
, pp 150-151]
or Fox
[
18
, p. 92]
.
In the truncated boundary formulation
f_{M}(\eta)
is defined by

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}f_{M}}{d\eta^{3}}}+f_{M}{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f_{M}}{d\eta^{2}}}=0

(18)

\displaystyle f_{M}(0)={\displaystyle\frac{df_{M}}{d\eta}}(0)=0,\qquad{\displaystyle\frac{df_{M}}{d\eta}}(M)=1

where
M
represents the truncated boundary.
It is evident that also in (
18
) the governing DE and the two boundary
conditions at the origin are left invariant by the scaling transformation (
12
).

For the error related to the truncated boundary solution
f_{M}(\eta)
defined by

e(\eta)=|f(\eta)-f_{M}(\eta)|,\qquad\eta\in[0,M]\ ,

(19)

the following theorem holds true.

Teorema 1

(due to Rubel
[
27
]
).
A truncated boundary formulation of the Blasius problem introduces an error which verifies the following inequality

e(\eta)\leq M{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f_{M}}{d\eta^{2}}}(M)[f_{M}(M)]^{-1}\quad.

(20)

Outline of the proof.

As proved by Weyl
[
35
]
, it is true that

{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}}(\eta)>0,\qquad\mbox{for \ \ }\eta\in(0,\infty)\ .

(21)

By (
21
) and taking into account the boundary conditions in (
2.1
), we have that

{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\eta)\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ f(\eta)\ \ \mbox{are increasing functions on}\ \ \eta\in(0,\infty)\quad.

(22)

As a consequence the function
\lambda^{2}{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\lambda M)
is zero for
\lambda=0
, increases with
\lambda
and tends to infinity as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
.
For some value of
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
we must have

\lambda^{2}{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\lambda M)=1\quad.

(23)

This value verifies
\lambda>1
because
\lambda M
is a
finite value,
{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\eta)
is an
increasing function and

{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\eta)\rightarrow 1\ \ \mbox{as}\ \ \eta\rightarrow\infty
.
For this particular value of
\lambda
, due to the scaling properties, we have found that

f_{M}(\eta)=\lambda f(\lambda\eta)

(24)

because
\lambda f(\lambda\eta)
verifies the BVP (
18
) that defines
f_{M}(\eta)
uniquely.

Hence, the error for
\eta\in[0,M]
is given by

e(\eta)=|\lambda f(\lambda\eta)-f(\eta)|\leq|(\lambda-1)f(\lambda\eta)|+|f(\lambda\eta)-f(\eta)|\quad.

(25)

By applying the mean value theorem of differential calculus and taking
into account that
{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\eta)\leq 1

we get the relations
f(\lambda\eta)\leq\lambda\eta
and

|f(\lambda\eta)-f(\eta)|\leq(\lambda-1)\eta
. As a result

e(\eta)\leq M(\lambda^{2}-1),\qquad\eta\in[0,M]

(26)

where
\lambda^{2}-1>0
because
\lambda>1
.
Naturally,

{\displaystyle\frac{df_{M}}{d\eta}}(\eta\rightarrow\infty)=\lambda^{2}
, so that

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda^{2}-1&={\displaystyle\frac{df_{M}}{d\eta}}(\eta\rightarrow\infty)-{\displaystyle\frac{df_{M}}{d\eta}}(M)\\
&={\displaystyle\int_{M}^{\infty}}{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f_{M}}{d\eta^{2}}}d\eta\quad.\end{array}

To complete the proof Rubel used some manipulations,
involving a first integral of the governing differential equation, to find that

\lambda^{2}-1\leq{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f_{M}}{d\eta^{2}}}(M)[f_{M}(M)]^{-1}\ .

(28)

\Box

Remark.
As a consequence of this theorem in order to control the error we can modify either the value of
M
or the value of
{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f_{M}}{d\eta^{2}}}(M)
.
Classically the value of
M
has been chosen to this end.
The above Theorem shows that the error is directly proportional to
M
.
In this context Fazio defines a free boundary formulation of the Blasius problem where the second order derivative of the solution with respect to
\eta
at the free boundary can be chosen as small as possible, see
[
12
]
for details.
The free boundary can be interpreted as a truncated boundary, see Fazio
[
15
]
.

5
Similarity analysis

Motivated by several problems in boundary layer theory, let us consider the class of BVPs defined by

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}f}{d\eta^{3}}}=\phi\left(\eta,f,{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta},\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}}\right)

(29)

\displaystyle f(0)=a\,\quad{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(0)=b+c{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}}(0)\ ,\quad{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\eta)\rightarrow d\quad\mbox{as}\quad\eta\rightarrow\infty\ ,

where
a
,
b
,
c
and
d
are given constants, with
d\neq 0
.
Introducing the scaling group

f^{*}=\lambda f\ ,\qquad\eta^{*}=\lambda^{\delta}\eta\ ,

(30)

we require the invariance of (
5
), but the asymptotic boundary condition so that
\delta\neq 1
, with respect to (
30
).
The requested invariance is granted on condition that
a=b=c=0
and

\phi\left(\eta,f,{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta},\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}}\right)=\eta^{1-3\delta}\Phi\left(\eta^{1/\delta}f,\eta^{(1-\delta)/\delta}{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}},\eta^{(1-2\delta)/\delta}{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}}\right)\ .

(31)

As a consequence of the above scaling invariance we can define a non-ITM.

5.1
The non-iterative algorithm

In order to define the numerical method for the characterized class of problems we have to consider the auxiliary IVP

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*3}}}=\eta^{*(1-3\delta)}\Phi\left(\eta^{*(1/\delta)}f^{*},\eta^{*(1-\delta)/\delta}{\displaystyle\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}},\eta^{*(1-2\delta)/\delta}{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*2}}}\right)

(32)

\displaystyle f^{*}(0)={\displaystyle\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}}(0)=0\ ,\qquad{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*2}}}(0)=p\ .

where
p
is defined by the user, we usually set
p=\pm 1
, but it is also possible to consider different values.
For instance, in Fazio
[
12
]
for the Blasius problem we used
p=1000
.
We have to solve (
5.1
) on
[0,\eta_{\infty}^{*}]
, where
\eta_{\infty}^{*}
is a suitable truncated boundary chosen under the condition

{\displaystyle\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}}(\eta_{\infty}^{*})\approx{\displaystyle\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}}(\infty)\ .

(33)

As
d\neq 0
, we have

\lambda=\left[{\displaystyle\frac{\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\eta_{\infty}^{*})}{d}}\right]^{1/(1-\delta)}\ .

(34)

Computed the value of
\lambda
we can apply the inverse transformation of (
30
) to get

\displaystyle\eta=\lambda^{-\delta}\eta^{*}\ ,\quad f(\eta)=\lambda^{-1}f^{*}(\eta^{*})\ ,

(35)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}(\eta)=\lambda^{\delta-1}\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\eta^{*})}\ ,\quad{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}(\eta)=\lambda^{2\delta-1}\frac{d^{2}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*2}}(\eta^{*})}\ .

In particular, we are interested to compute the missing initial condition
{\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}(0)}
.

We are now ready to present the method of solution in the form of an algorithm.

The non-iterative algorithm.

1. Input
p
,
\delta
,
\eta_{\infty}^{*}
,
d
.

2. Solve (
5.1
) in
[0,\eta_{\infty}^{*}]
.

3. Compute
\lambda
by (
34
).

4. Rescale the numerical solution according to (
5.1
).

The above algorithm defines a non-ITM for the numerical solution of the class of problems characterized by (
31
) and
a=b=c=0
.

6
The extension due to Na

We report here an important extension of the non-ITM due to Na.
For this extension we have to require the invariance of physical parameters.
The boundary conditions at
\eta=0
involve the parameters
a
,
b
and
c
.
Those boundary conditions are left invariant by the scaling transformation on condition
that the involved parameters transform as

a^{*}=\lambda a\ ,\quad b^{*}=\lambda^{1-\delta}b\ ,\quad c^{*}=\lambda^{\delta}c\ .

(36)

6.1
Moving wall

According to Ishak et al.
[
21
]
the moving wall boundary conditions are given by

f(0)=0\ ,\qquad\frac{df}{d\eta}(0)=b\ ,\qquad{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\eta)\rightarrow d\quad\mbox{as}\quad\eta\rightarrow\infty\ ,

(37)

where
d=1-b
and
b
is a non-dimensional parameter given by the ration of the wall to the flow velocities.
The boundary conditions of the Blasius problem are recovered from (
37
) by setting
b=0
.

6.1.1
The non-ITM

The applicability of a non-ITM to the Blasius problem is a consequence of its invariance with respect to a scaling transformation; note that the asymptotic boundary condition is not invariant.
In order to apply the non-ITM we consider
b
as a parameter involved in the scaling invariance, i.e., we define the extended scaling group

f^{*}=\lambda f\ ,\qquad\eta^{*}=\lambda^{-1}\eta\ ,\qquad b^{*}=\lambda^{2}b\ .

(38)

By setting a value of
P^{*}
, we can integrate the Blasius equation (
2.1
) in the star variables with initial conditions

f^{*}(0)=0\ ,\quad\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(0)=b^{*}\ ,\quad\frac{d^{2}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*2}}(0)=\pm 1\ ,

(39)

in order to compute an approximation
\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\eta_{\infty})
for
\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\infty)
and the corresponding value of
\lambda
according to the equation

\lambda=\left[\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\eta_{\infty})+b^{*}\right]^{1/2}\ .

(40)

Once the value of
\lambda
is computed by equation (
40
), then we can find the missed initial condition by the equation

\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}(0)=\lambda^{-3}\frac{d^{2}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*2}}(0)\ ,

(41)

For the application of the method defined above, we remark that the plus (for
b<0.5
) or minus (when
b>0.5
) sign must be used for the second derivative in (
39
).
Moreover, the computation of a value at infinity is unsuitable from a numerical viewpoint and therefore we use a truncated boundary
\eta^{*}_{\infty}
instead of infinity.

The boundary layer separate from the surface at

We have separation for a positive value of the skin friction coefficient and not at the point where this coefficient vanish as in the classical boundary layer theory.
The zero value of the skin friction coefficient when
b=1/2
corresponds to equal velocity of the plate and the free stream and does not mark separation.

6.2
Slip flow condition

We consider now the case of a rarefied flow where the no-slip condition at the wall, considered in the previous section, must be replaced by a slip-flow condition, see for instance Gad-el-Hak
[
8
]
.
For an isothermal wall, the slip condition can be defined as

v(y,0)=\frac{2-\sigma}{\sigma}\ell\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}(y,0)\ ,

(42)

where
\ell
is the mean free path, and
\sigma
is the tangential momentum accommodation coefficient.
Within a similarity transformation this slip boundary condition becomes

\frac{df}{d\eta}(0)=c\;\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}(0)\ ,

(43)

where
c
is a non-dimensional parameter, that takes into account the behaviour at the surface, defined by

c=\frac{2-\sigma}{\sigma}Kn\;Re\;y^{1/2}\ ,

(44)

where
Kn
and
Re
are the Knudsen and Reynolds numbers based on
y
.

For the Blasius problem with slip condition we implemented the extended non-ITM.

f^{*}=\lambda f\ ,\qquad\eta^{*}=\lambda^{-1}\eta\ ,\qquad c^{*}=\lambda^{-1}c\ .

(45)

Henceforth,
\lambda
is defined, once again, by equation (
16
).

7
Surface gasification boundary condition

In this section we consider a problem that does not belong to the framework used so far.
This is the surface gasification flow that, with and without subsequent gas-phase flame-sheet burning, was first formulated and analysed by Emmons
[
9
]
.
For such a flow we have to consider the variant of the celebrated Blasius problem

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}f}{d\eta^{3}}}+f{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}}=0

(46)

\displaystyle f(0)=-s\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}(0)\ ,\qquad\frac{df}{d\eta}(0)=0\ ,\qquad{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\eta)\rightarrow 1\quad\mbox{as}\quad\eta\rightarrow\infty\ ,

where
s
is the classical Spalding heat transfer number
[
31
]
.
This transfer number for slow vaporization belongs to the interval
[0,0.1]
and varies from
s=O(1)
to
s\approx 20
for strong burning.
This problem has been studied recently by Lu and Law
[
22
]
.
These authors define an iterative method that has been shown to produce more accurate numerical results than the classical approximate solutions.

7.1
The non-ITM

In the present case we consider the extended scaling group

f^{*}=\lambda f\ ,\qquad\eta^{*}=\lambda^{-1}\eta\ ,\qquad s^{*}=\lambda^{-2}s\ .

(47)

Let us notice that the governing differential equation and the two boundary conditions at
\eta=0
in (
7
) are left invariant under (
47
) and, on the contrary, the asymptotic boundary condition is not invariant.
By setting a value of

, we can integrate the Blasius governing differential equation in (

[0,\eta^{*}_{\infty}]
with initial conditions

f^{*}(0)=-s^{*}\ ,\quad\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(0)=0\ ,\quad\frac{d^{2}f^{*}}{d\eta^{*2}}(0)=1\ ,

(48)

in order to compute
\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\eta^{*}_{\infty})\approx\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\infty)
.
Here
\eta^{*}_{\infty}
is a suitable truncated boundary.
The value of
\lambda
can be found by

\lambda=\left[\frac{df^{*}}{d\eta^{*}}(\eta_{\infty}^{*})\right]^{1/2}\ .

(49)

After using (
49

\lambda

f(0)=\lambda^{-2}s^{*}\ ,\quad\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}(0)=\lambda^{-3}\ .

(50)

8
Concluding remarks

The main contribution of this paper is the extension of the non-ITM proposed by Töpfer
[
33
]
for the numerical solution of the celebrated Blasius problem
[
4
]
to classes of problems depending on a parameter.
By requiring the invariance of the involved parameter we are able to solve the given BVP non-iteratively but for a different value of the parameter.
This kind of extension was considered first by Na
[
24
]
, see also NA
[
25
, Chapters 8-9]
.
Here we defined a non-ITM for Blasius equation with moving wall, surface gasification or slip boundary conditions.

Finally, the reader should be advised that non-ITM cannot be applied to all problems of boundary layer theory.
As an example, let us consider the Falkner-Skan model
[
11
]

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}f}{d\eta^{3}}}+f{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}f}{d\eta^{2}}}+P\;\left[1-\left({\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}\right)^{2}\right]=0\ ,

(51)

\displaystyle f(0)={\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(0)=0\ ,\qquad{\displaystyle\frac{df}{d\eta}}(\eta)\rightarrow 1\quad\mbox{as}\quad\eta\rightarrow\infty\ ,

where
f
and
\eta
are similarity variables and
P
is a parameter related to the functional form of the fluid mainstream velocity.
If we test the invariance of the governing differential equation in (
8
) under the extended scaling group

\eta^{*}=\lambda^{\alpha_{1}}\eta\ ,\qquad f^{*}=\lambda^{\alpha_{2}}f\ ,\qquad P^{*}=\lambda^{\alpha_{3}}P\ ,

(52)

where
\lambda
is, again, the group parameter and
{\alpha_{j}}
, for
j=1,2,3
, are constant to be determined, then we get three invariant conditions

\alpha_{2}-3\alpha_{1}=2(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})=\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{3}+2(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1})\ .

(53)

Now, it is a simple matter to show that the linear system defined by (
53
) has the unique solution
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}=0
.
However, as a last word on this topic we can mention an iterative extension of our transformation method that has been developed in
[
13
,
14
]
and successfully applied to the Falkner-Skan model
[
13
,
16
]
.
