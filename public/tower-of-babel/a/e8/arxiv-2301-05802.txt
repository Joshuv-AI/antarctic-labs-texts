Kippenhahn’s construction revisited
Stephan Weis 1

Abstract
. Kippenhahn discovered that the numerical range of a complex square matrix is the convex hull of a plane real algebraic curve. Here, we present an example of a convex set, which has a similar algebraic description as the numerical range, whereas the analogue of Kippenhahn’s construction fails regarding isolated, singular points of the curve. This example prompted us to carefully review Kippenhahn’s assertion and to highlight aspects of a complete proof that was achieved with methods of convex geometry and real algebraic geometry.

1.
Introduction

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix with complex coefficients.
The
numerical range
, also known as
field of values
,
of
A
is the subset of the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
defined by

W(A)=\{\langle\eta\mid A\eta\rangle:\langle\eta\mid\eta\rangle=1,\eta\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\}\,,

where

\langle\eta\mid\xi\rangle=\eta_{1}\overline{\xi_{1}}+\eta_{2}\overline{\xi_{2}}+\dots+\eta_{n}\overline{\xi_{n}}

denotes the inner product of two vectors
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\dots,\eta_{n})^{T}

and
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{n})^{T}
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
The numerical range
W(A)
is invariant under unitary similarity. It is used to
study spectra and norms of matrices
[
14
]
and of operators

[
8
,
15
]
.

The shape of the numerical range is well understood
[
17
]

for matrices of sizes
n=2
and
n=3
. The shape remains a research topic
in matrix theory
[
4
,
1
,
16
]

and quantum information theory
[
9
]
for matrices of sizes

n\geq 4
. Among the basic key-theorems is the
Toeplitz-Hausdorff theorem
[
28
,
10
]
from the years
1918/19, which asserts that
W(A)
is a convex set, and
Kippenhahn’s assertion
[
18
, Sec. 3, Nr. 10]
from 1951, which
states that
W(A)
is the convex hull of a plane real algebraic curve
C_{A}
.
The curve
C_{A}
has been called
boundary generating curve

[
18
,
3
]
, and was renamed to

Kippenhahn curve

[
6
,
8
,
1
,
16
]

recently.

In this paper, we take a closer look at Kippenhahn’s proof that the curve
C_{A}

is included in
W(A)
. We illustrate Kippenhahn’s construction of
C_{A}
and his
assertion with a
3\times 3
matrix
A
in Section
2
.
In Section
3
we present a convex set
W
​ for which
a similar construction is feasible, but the curve analogous to
C_{A}
has isolated,
singular points outside of
W
​. These examples are supported by the computer
algebra system
Wolfram Mathematica
. Despite this flaw, Kippenhahn’s
proof is still advocated
[
8
, Thm. 1.3]
,

[
6
, Thm. 13.4]
without paying attention that it is incomplete.
An exception is the paper
[
3
]
where Chien and Nakazato
analyze singular points of the numerical range.

During the last fifteen years or so, we witnessed an increasing research
activity at the crossroads of convex geometry and real algebraic geometry,
a field called
convex algebraic geometry

[
12
,
13
,
24
,
20
,
22
,
2
,
27
,
25
]
.
To our surprise, Sinn
[
27
, Example 3.15]
had proven an assertion
similar to Kippenhahn’s already in 2014. By translating Sinn’s result
to the numerical range, and by showing that all singular points of
C_{A}
lie
inside
W(A)
, we obtained a complete proof of Kippenhahn’s assertion

[
21
, Thm. 1.1]
.

The set
W
mentioned above is not the numerical range of a matrix since the
analogue of Kippenhahn’s assertion fails for
W
​. This follows also from
the fact that the dual convex set
W^{\ast}
to
W
is bounded by the

Fermat curve

x_{1}^{6}+x_{2}^{6}=1
. All lines
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
through this curve intersect it in two points instead of six, see
Fig.
2
b). In other words,
W^{\ast}
is not

rigidly convex

[
12
]
and therefore it cannot be
the dual convex set to the numerical range of a matrix

[
13
,
11
]
. This is, of course, a peculiarity of
the finite dimensionality. Any bounded convex nonempty subset of the plane is
the numerical range of a bounded linear operator on a Hilbert space

[
23
]
.

Section
5
highlights some aspects of the proof
that the curve
C_{A}
is included in
W(A)
. The preliminary
Sec.
4
changes perspective from points outside of

W(A)
to lines crossing the dual convex set.

In this paper, we do not address the converse part of Kippenhahn’s assertion,
that
W(A)
is included in the convex hull of
C_{A}
. This follows
from
[
27
, Cor 3.14]
, and is adapted to the setting of the numerical
range in Thm. 4.5 and Sec. 6 in
[
21
]
.

2.
Construction of the Kippenhahn Curve

Following Kippenhahn
[
18
]
, we construct the curve
C_{A}

in three steps along the drawings in Fig.
1
. For the sake
of clarity, we consider only irreducible curves defined by irreducible
polynomials in the sequel. A general reference for algebraic curves is
Fischer
[
7
]
.

We identify
\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\mathrm{i}
is the imaginary unit,
and we view the numerical range
W(A)
as a subset of
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}
, the
dual vector space to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Writing
A=K+\mathrm{i}L
, where
K,L\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

are hermitian matrices, we identify the numerical range
W(A)
with the set

(1)

W=\{(\langle\eta\mid K\eta\rangle,\langle\eta\mid L\eta\rangle):\langle\eta\mid\eta\rangle=1,\eta\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\}\subset(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}\,.

Step 1.

The
support function

[
26
]
of the numerical range

W\subset(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}
at the direction
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is

h(x)=\min_{y\in W}\langle x,y\rangle\,,

where the pairing of
x=(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

y=(y_{1},y_{2})\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}
is given by
\langle x,y\rangle=x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}
.
The number
h(x)
is the signed distance of the origin
(0,0)\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}

from the
supporting line
with inner normal vector
x
,

(2)

\{y\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}:\langle x,y\rangle=h(x)\}\,.

This means that
x
is perpendicular to this line and points into the
half-space
\{y\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}:\langle x,y\rangle\geq h(x)\}
, which contains

W(A)
. Toeplitz
[
28
]
showed that
h(x)
is the smallest
eigenvalue of the hermitian matrix
x_{1}K+x_{2}L
. Let

(3)

K=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-1&0\\
-1&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
\end{array}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad L=-\frac{1}{4}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&2&-4\\
2&1&2\\
-4&2&1\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

Some supporting lines for this example are depicted in Fig.
1
a).
Note that
h(x)
is negative as
x_{1}K+x_{2}L
has a negative eigenvalue for
nonzero
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The rounded triangle in the center of
Fig.
1
a), without lines crossing, depicts the numerical range.

Step 2.

To obtain an algebraic equation for the set of supporting lines, we use
a one-to-one correspondence between the points in the complex projective
plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}
and lines in the dual projective plane
(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{\ast}
. The

polar
of a point
(x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
is the line

\{(y_{0}:y_{1}:y_{2})\in(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{\ast}\mid x_{0}y_{0}+x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}=0\}\,.

Conversely, the point
(x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2})
is the
pole
of this line.
The line is a
real line
if its pole can be written with real numbers

x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}
. (In this paper we use poles and polars with respect to the
quadric
\{x\in\mathbb{P}^{2}:x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}=0\}
.) We also use the analogous
one-to-one correspondence between points in
(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{\ast}
and lines in

\mathbb{P}^{2}
that comes with the biduality of
\mathbb{P}^{2}=((\mathbb{P}^{2})^{\ast})^{\ast}
.
We will often abuse notation and identify a line with its pole.
Furthermore, we employ the embedding of the affine plane
\mathbb{C}^{2}
into the
projective plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}

via

(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\mapsto(1:x_{1}:x_{2})
and
the embedding of the dual affine plane
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\ast}
into the dual
projective plane
(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{\ast}

via

(y_{1},y_{2})\mapsto(1:y_{1}:y_{2})
.

For all nonzero
(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the pole of the supporting line in equation (
2
) is

\ell=(-h(x):x_{1}:x_{2})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\,.

The line
\ell
satisfies the algebraic equation
p(\ell)=0
, where

p\in\mathbb{R}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]
is the homogeneous polynomial defined by the determinant

p=\det(x_{0}\mathds{1}+x_{1}K+x_{2}L)\,,

and where
\mathds{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is the identity matrix. This means that

\ell
lies on the curve

D=\{x\in\mathbb{P}^{2}:p(x)=0\}\,.

The
real, affine part
of this curve is

\{(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid(1:x_{1}:x_{2})\in D\}
.

The polynomial
p
in the example (
3
) is provided in
Sec.
6
. The real, affine part of
D
has a connected component
of the shape of a rounded triangle, see Fig.
1
b), and three
peripheral, unbounded, connected components outside the rounded triangle,
see Fig.
1
c). The inner rounded triangle is the set of supporting
lines of the numerical range
W
. The peripheral components do not contain any
supporting lines of
W
.

Step 3.

The final step is to pass from the curve
D
to its dual curve. A point

x\in D
is a
regular point
of
D
if at least one of the partial
derivatives
\partial_{x_{0}}p
,
\partial_{x_{1}}p
,
\partial_{x_{2}}p
is
nonzero at
x
(the polynomial
p
is assumed to be irreducible).
Otherwise,
x
is a
singular point
of
D
. If
x
is a regular
point, then the tangent line to
D
at
x
is

(\partial_{x_{0}}p(x):\partial_{x_{1}}p(x):\partial_{x_{2}}p(x))\in(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{\ast}\,.

The
dual curve

D^{\vee}
to
D
is the closure in the norm topology
of the set of tangent lines to the curve
D
at all regular points, see

[
7
, Sec. 5.1 and 3.6]
. This closure comprises exactly the
set of all tangents to the curve
D
, at regular and singular points.
It is also possible to replace the norm closure with the Zariski closure

[
19
, Thm. 2.33]
.

One can use Gröbner bases
[
5
]
to compute the algebraic equation
for the dual curve
D^{\vee}
​. In the above example, we have

D^{\vee}=\{y\in(\mathbb{P}^{3})^{\ast}:q(y)=0\}
, where
q\in\mathbb{R}[y_{0},y_{1},y_{2}]
is an
irreducible, homogeneous polynomial of degree six. The polynomial and the code
from
Wolfram Mathematica
used for its calculation are provided in
Sec.
6
. The real, affine part

C_{A}=\{(y_{1},y_{2})\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}\mid(1:y_{1}:y_{2})\in D^{\vee}\}

of the dual curve is the Kippenhahn curve.

Fig.
1
d) depicts the Kippenhahn curve
C_{A}
of the example
(
3
). Here,
C_{A}
has two connected components, a rounded triangle
and a curve inside with three cusps
[
18
,
17
]
. The
rounded triangle contains the tangent lines at the central, rounded triangle
in the real, affine part of
D
, shown in Fig.
1
b) and
Fig.
1
c). The inner curve of
C_{A}
comprises the tangent lines
at the three peripheral components of the real, affine part of
D
shown in
Fig.
1
c).

3.
A Revealing Example

Here, we present a convex set, which has a similar algebraic description
as the numerical range, whereas the analogue of Thm.
2.1

fails.

Step 1.

Using the notation from the prior section, we define a compact, convex set
W\subset(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}
in terms of its support function

(4)

h(x)=-\sqrt[6]{x_{1}^{6}+x_{2}^{6}}\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,.

Some supporting lines of
W
are depicted in Fig.
2
a).
The rounded rhomb in the center of Fig.
2
a), without
lines crossing, depicts the convex set
W
.

Step 2.

For every nonzero
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the supporting line of
W
with inner normal
vector
x
has the pole
\ell=(-h(x):x_{1}:x_{2})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
, which lies on the
curve
D=\{x\in\mathbb{P}^{2}:p(x)=0\}
, where

p=x_{0}^{6}-x_{1}^{6}-x_{2}^{6}\,.

Fig.
2
b) depicts a rounded square, which is the real,
affine part of
D
, and which is the set of all supporting lines of
W
.

Step 3.

The dual curve to
D
is the variety
D^{\vee}=\{y\in(\mathbb{P}^{3})^{\ast}:q(y)=0\}

of an irreducible, homogeneous polynomial
q\in\mathbb{R}[y_{0},y_{1},y_{2}]
of degree
30
,
see Sec.
6
for the polynomial and the code from

Wolfram Mathematica
used for the calculation. The curve
D^{\vee}
has

228
singular points, eight of which are real. These are
(1,0)
,
(0,1)
,

(-1,0)
,
(0,-1)
,

(5)

(\omega,\omega),\quad(\omega,-\omega),\quad(-\omega,\omega),\quad\text{and}\quad(-\omega,-\omega)\,,

where
\omega=\left((11+5\sqrt{5})/2)\right)^{1/6}\approx 1.49\,
. The real,
affine part of
D^{\vee}
consists of a rounded rhomb, which contains the first
four singular points, and of the four isolated, singular points, which are
depicted in Fig.
2
c). The analogue of
Thm.
2.1
fails because the isolated singular points lie
outside of
W
. Their polars are real double tangents of the curve
D
,
depicted in Figure
2
d). For example, the polar of

(1:\omega:\omega)\in D^{\vee}
is tangent to
D
at the pair of complex
conjugate points

\frac{1}{2\omega}(-1\pm\mathrm{i}\alpha,-1\mp\mathrm{i}\alpha)^{T}\,,\qquad\alpha=\sqrt{5+2\sqrt{5}}\,.

4.
The Dual Picture of Points Outside the Numerical Range

Some singular points of the dual curve
D^{\vee}
lie outside of
W
in the
example of Sec.
3
. Here we capture the location
of points in- or outside of
W
in terms of whether their polars intersect
the dual convex set to
W
.

We consider a compact, convex subset
W
of
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}
that contains the
origin
(0,0)\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}
as an interior point. The
dual convex set

to
W
is defined by

W^{\ast}=\{(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}:1+x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}\geq 0\,\,\forall y\in W\}\,.

The set
W^{\ast}
is a compact, convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that contains the
origin
(0,0)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as an interior point, and we have
(W^{\ast})^{\ast}=W
.
See
[
26
, Thm. 1.6.1]
for these statements.

If the origin is not an interior point
W
​, then conic duality
instead of convex duality makes the ideas of the following
Sec.
5
work
[
21
, Sec. 5]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

A real point in
(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{\ast}
lies outside of
W
if and only if
its polar intersects the interior of
W^{\ast}
.

Proof.

Since we associated the support function
h
with
W
, we prove the equivalent
dual statement that a real line in
(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{\ast}
intersects the interior of
W

if and only if its pole lies outside of
W^{\ast}
.

We consider a line in
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\ast}
, which is not incident with the origin. By
this we mean a real line in
(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{\ast}
, such that its pole

m=(x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
has the affine coordinates

(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{0}},\frac{x_{2}}{x_{0}})^{T}
for some
x_{0}\neq 0
. If we fix a nonzero
tuple
x=(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then the supporting line of
W
with inner normal
vector
x
has the pole
\ell=(-h(x):x_{1}:x_{2})
with the affine coordinates

\,-(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}/h(x)\,
. These real tuples parametrize the boundary of the
dual convex set
W^{\ast}
if
(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}
varies in a closed curve about the
origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, according
to
[
26
, Sec. 1.7.2, Eq. (1.52)]
.

Let
x_{0}>0
. Then the polar of
m=(x_{0}:x_{1}:x_{2})
lies on the same side of the
origin as the polar of
\ell=(-h(x):x_{1}:x_{2})
. Clearly, the polar of
m

intersects the interior of the set
W
if and only if
x_{0}<-h(x)
if and only
if
(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{0}},\frac{x_{2}}{x_{0}})^{T}
lies outside of
W^{\ast}
. The claim
follows from setting
x_{0}=1
.

The claim extends to real lines in
(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{\ast}
through the origin, as their
poles lie on the line at infinity. Having its pole at the origin, the line at
infinity is also consistent with the claim.
∎

Under the assumptions of Lemma
4.1
, it follows that an algebraic curve

D
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
has a real tangent that intersects the interior of the set
W^{\ast}
,
if and only if a real point of the dual curve
D^{\vee}
lies outside of
W
.

Example 4.2
.

As an example, let us verify that the convex set

S=\{(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\mid x_{1}^{6}+x_{2}^{6}\leq 1\}

is the dual convex set to
W
defined in equation (
4
).
We verified in Step 2 of Sec.
3
that the
point
(-h(x):x_{1}:x_{2})\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
is a root of
p=x_{0}^{6}-x_{1}^{6}-x_{2}^{6}
for all
nonzero
(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Hence the tuples
\,-(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}/h(x)\,

parametrize the boundary
x_{1}^{6}+x_{2}^{6}=1
of
S
. We saw in the proof of
Lemma
4.1
that these tuples also parametrize the boundary of

W^{\ast}
, so we have
S=W^{\ast}
.

We corroborate that the isolated, singular points of the real, affine part of

D^{\vee}
, depicted in Fig.
2
c), lie outside of
W

because their polars, depicted in Fig.
2
d), intersect
the interior of the convex set
S
.

5.
Aspects of the Proof of Kippenhahn’s Assertion

Following
[
21
, Thm. 4.9]
, we discuss aspects of a proof that
the Kippenhahn curve
C_{A}
is included in the numerical range
W=W(A)
.
Here we use the determinant
p=\det(x_{0}\mathds{1}+x_{1}K+x_{2}L)
and matrices

A=K+\mathrm{i}L
as in Sec.
2
, whereas
[
21
]

employs hyperbolic polynomials

[
12
,
20
,
22
,
27
]
.

Observation 1.

The convex dual set to the numerical ranges is a well-known object.
A set of the form

S=\{(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,\mathds{1}+x_{1}K+x_{2}L\,\text{ is positive semi-definite}\}

is a
spectrahedron
, see
[
20
]
and the references
therein. This spectrahedron is the convex dual to the numerical range

[
11
]
,

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,\langle\eta,(\mathds{1}+x_{1}K+x_{2}L)\eta\rangle\geq 0\,\,\forall\eta\in\mathbb{C}^{n},\langle\eta\mid\eta\rangle=1\,\}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,1+x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}\geq 0\,\,\forall(y_{1},y_{2})\in W\,\}

\displaystyle=W^{\ast}\,.

Observation 2.

A real line that intersects an interior point of the spectrahedron
S

intersects the curve
D
only in real points.
Let
\{e+\lambda x\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}
be such a line, where
e=(e_{1},e_{2})^{T}

is an interior point of
S
and
x=(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}
is nonzero. This line has the
point at infinity
(0:x_{1}:x_{2})
. The line intersects the curve
D
at the points

e+\lambda x
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, for which

\det(\underbrace{\mathds{1}+e_{1}K+e_{2}L}_{A}+\lambda\underbrace{(x_{1}K+x_{2}L)}_{B})=\det(A+\lambda B)

is zero, and possibly at the point
(0:x_{1}:x_{2})
at infinity if
\det(B)=0
.
Since
e
is an interior point of
S
, the matrix
A
is positive definite.
Substituting
\lambda=-\frac{1}{\mu}
, we obtain

\det(A+\lambda B)=\det({\textstyle-\frac{1}{\mu}}A)\det(-\mu\,\mathds{1}+A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}})\,.

As the matrix
A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is hermitian, its eigenvalues
are real. This shows that the line intersects
D
only in real points.

Roots at infinity can occur. For example, when
n=2
and

K=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad L=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\,,

then
p=x_{0}^{2}+x_{0}x_{2}-x_{1}^{2}
. The affine part of the curve
D
is the graph
of the function
x_{2}=x_{1}^{2}-1
, which is a parabola.
The
