Random coordinate descent methods for nonseparable composite optimization
Flavia Chorobura, Ion Necoara

Abstract
In this paper we consider large-scale composite optimization problems having the objective function formed as a sum of two terms (possibly nonconvex), one has (block) coordinate-wise Lipschitz continuous gradient and the other is differentiable but nonseparable. Under these general settings we derive and analyze two new coordinate descent methods. The first algorithm, referred to as coordinate proximal gradient method, considers the composite form of the objective function, while the other algorithm disregards the composite form of the objective and uses the partial gradient of the full objective, yielding a coordinate gradient descent scheme with novel adaptive stepsize rules. We prove that these new stepsize rules make the coordinate gradient scheme a descent method, provided that additional assumptions hold for the second term in the objective function. We present a complete worst-case complexity analysis for these two new methods in both, convex and nonconvex settings, provided that the (block) coordinates are chosen random or cyclic. Preliminary numerical results also confirm the efficiency of our two algorithms on practical problems.



keywords

Composite minimization, nonseparable objective function, random coordinate descent, adaptive stepsize, convergence rates.

Introduction

In this paper we consider solving large-scale composite optimization problems of the form:

Contributions
. This paper deals with large-scale composite optimization problems of the form (
1

3

(i) We introduce a coordinate proximal gradient method, (

\nabla f

(ii) We also present a coordinate gradient method, (

2.1
Assumptions/setup

We consider the following problem settings. Let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a column permutation of the identity matrix

and further let

be a decomposition of

and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_{i}=n
. Hence, any

can be written as

, where
x^{(i)}=U_{i}^{T}x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
.
Throughout the paper the following assumptions hold:

Assumption 1
.

For composite optimization problem (
1
) the following hold:

A.1: Gradient of

is block coordinate-wise Lipschitz continuous with constants
L_{i}
:

\displaystyle\|U_{i}^{T}(\nabla f(x+U_{i}h)-\nabla f(x))\|\leq L_{i}\|h\|\quad\forall h\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},i=1:N.

(5)

A.2:
\psi
is twice continuously differentiable (possibly nonseparable and nonconvex).

A.3: A solution exists for (
1
) (hence, the optimal value
F^{*}>-\infty
).

If Assumption
1
[A1] holds, then we have the relation
[
32
]
:

|f(x+U_{i}h)-f(x)-\langle U_{i}^{T}\nabla f(x),h\rangle|\leq\frac{L_{i}}{2}\|h\|^{2}\quad\forall h\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}},\quad i=1,\cdots,N.\vskip-2.84544pt

(6)

The basic idea of our algorithms consist of choosing
i\in\{1,...,N\}
uniformly at random or cyclic and update

as follows:

x^{+}=x+U_{i}d.
We consider two choices for the directions

. In Coordinate Proximal Gradient (CPG) algorithm, the direction

is computed by a proximal operator of

restricted to the subspace
U_{i}
. In Coordinate Gradient Descent (CGD) algorithm,

is given by a multiple of a block of components of the gradient
\nabla F(x_{k})

Definition 2
.

For any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

denote
\phi^{x}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
as:

\phi^{x}_{i}(d)=\psi(x+U_{i}d).\vskip-2.84544pt

(7)

We say that the function

is convex along coordinates if the partial functions
\phi^{x}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
are convex for all

and

.

One can easily notice that there are nonconvex functions

which are convex along coordinates. Note that if
\psi
is twice differentiable, then it is convex along coordinates if
U_{i}^{T}\nabla^{2}\psi(x)U_{i}
is positive semidefinite matrix for any

U_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{i}}
, with
i=1:N
.

Below, we use the following mean value inequality (see Appendix for a proof).

Lemma 3
.

Let
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a continuously differentiable function and
J:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be its Jacobian. Consider
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
a fixed matrix and
x,x+Ud\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with

\|G(x+Ud)-G(x)\|\leq\|J(y)U\|\|d\|.

2.2
KL property

Let us recall the definition of the Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (KL) property for a function, see e.g.,
[
7
]
. Note that the KL property is defined for general functions (possibly nondifferentiable). Below, we adapt this definition to the differentiable case, since in this paper we consider only differentiable objective functions.

Definition 4
.

A differentiable function
F
satisfies KL property on a compact set
\Omega
on which
F
takes a constant value
F_{*}
if there exist
\gamma,\epsilon>0
such that one has:

\kappa^{\prime}(F(x)-F_{*})\|\nabla F(x)\|\geq 1\quad\forall x\!:\text{dist}(x,\Omega)\leq\gamma,\;F_{*}<F(x)<F_{*}+\epsilon,\vskip-2.84544pt

where
\kappa:[0,\epsilon]\to\mathbb{R}
is a concave differentiable function satisfying
\kappa(0)=0
and
\kappa^{\prime}>0
.

The KL property holds for semi-algebraic functions (e.g., real polynomial functions), vector or matrix (semi)norms (e.g.,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
with
p\geq 0
rational number), logarithm functions, exponential functions and uniformly convex functions, see
[
7
]
for a comprehensive list.

3
A coordinate proximal gradient algorithm

In this section we assume that the function
\psi
is simple, i.e.,
\psi
restricted to any subspace generated by
U_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{i}}
is proximal easy. For minimizing the composite problem (
1
), where
f
and
\psi
are possibly nonseparable and nonconvex, we propose a pure coordinate proximal gradient algorithm that requires some block of components of the gradient
\nabla f(x)
and computes the prox of
\psi
also along these block of coordinates. Hence, our Coordinate Proximal Gradient (CPG) algorithm is as follows:

Algorithm 1 (CPG)
:

Given a starting point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For
k\geq 0
do:

1.   Choose
i_{k}\in\{1,...,N\}
uniformly at random or cyclic and
\eta_{i_{k}}>0
. Set:

H_{f,U_{i_{k}}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{L_{i_{k}}+\eta_{i_{k}}}{2}\;\text{ if }\psi\;\text{convex along coordinates}\\
L_{i_{k}}+\eta_{i_{k}}\;\text{ otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(8)

2.   Find
d_{k}
solving the following subproblem:

\;\;d_{k}\in\arg\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i_{k}}}}f(x_{k})+\langle U^{T}_{i_{k}}\nabla f(x_{k}),d\rangle+\dfrac{H_{f,U_{i_{k}}}}{2}\|d\|^{2}+\psi(x_{k}+U_{i_{k}}d)\vskip-2.84544pt

(9)

3.   Update
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+U_{i_{k}}d_{k}
.

Note that for
U_{i_{k}}=I_{n}
, CPG recovers the
full
proximal gradient method, algorithm (46) in
[
33
]
, while for
f\equiv 0
we get a Gauss-Seidel type algorithm similar to
[
17
]
. However,
[
33
]
derives rates only in the convex settings, while there are very few results ensuring that the iterates of a Gauss-Seidel method converges to a global minimizer, even for strictly convex functions, e.g.,
[
17
]
presents only assymptotic convergence results. In this paper we derive convergence rates for the general algorithm CPG in both convex and nonconvex settings. An important fact concerning our approach is that the convergence of CPG works for any
\eta_{i_{k}}
greater than a fixed positive parameter which can be chosen arbitrarily small. In particular, in CPG we can choose a larger stepsize when
\psi
is convex along coordinates (see Definition
2
), since
H_{f,U_{i_{k}}}
must satisfy in this case
H_{f,U_{i_{k}}}>L_{i_{k}}/2
.
When
\psi
is
\rho
-weakly convex along coordinates, the subproblem (
9
) is convex, provided that
H_{f,U_{i}}\geq\rho
. Our algorithm requires computation of the proximal operator only of the
partial
function
\phi_{i_{k}}^{x_{k}}
(defined in (
7
)) at
U^{T}_{i_{k}}\nabla f(x_{k})
:

d_{k}\in\text{prox}_{H_{f,U_{i_{k}}}^{-1}\phi_{i_{k}}^{x_{k}}}\left(-H_{f,U_{i_{k}}}^{-1}\;U^{T}_{i_{k}}\nabla f(x_{k})\right).\vskip-2.84544pt

(10)

Regardless of the properties of the two functions
f
and
\psi
, the subproblem (
9
) in CPG is convex provided that
\psi
is (weakly) convex along coordinates and then the prox operator (
10
) is well-defined (and unique) in this case, while for general nonconvex
\psi
, the prox (
10

p
regularizers. Note that the prox restricted to some subspace (as required in CPG) is much less expensive computationally than the full prox (as required in the literature
[
16
,
18
,
20

]
). More precisely, if
\psi
is differentiable, then solving the subproblem e.g., in the full proximal gradient method (algorithm (46) in
[

]
), is equivalent to finding a full vector
d_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the system of

\nabla f(x_{k})+\nabla\psi(x_{k}+d_{k})+H_{f}d_{k}=0.\vskip-2.84544pt

(11)

On other hand, when

U_{i}=e_{i}
, where
e_{i}
is the

th vector of the canonical basis of

, at each iteration of our algorithm CPG, solving the subproblem (

to finding a scalar

Clearly, there are very efficient methods for finding the root of a scalar equation (

5
Sufficient conditions for bounded iterates

Note that Lemmas
5
and
9
prove that the sequence
\left(F(x_{k})\right)_{k\geq 0}
generated by the algorithms CPG or CGD (with appropiate stepsize rules) is nonincreasing, i.e.,

for all

. However, in order to prove Lemmas

and
10
, we need to assume that the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is bounded. In this section we present sufficient conditions for boundedness. One natural example is when the level set is bounded:

\mathcal{L}_{F}(x_{0})=\left\{x:\,F(x)\leq F(x_{0})\right\}.\vskip-5.69046pt

Note that uniformly convex functions have bounded level sets. Indeed, if
F
is uniformly convex, with constant
q>1
, then it satisfies
[
33
]

F(y)\geq F(x)+\langle\nabla F(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\sigma_{q}(q-1)}{q}\|y-x\|^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\vskip-2.84544pt

(52)

Then, for
x=x^{*}
and

, we have

\frac{\sigma_{q}(q-1)}{q}\|x^{*}-y\|^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\leq F(y)-F(x^{*})\leq F(x^{0})-F(x^{*})<\infty.\vskip-2.84544pt

Moreover, note that if
F
is uniformly convex, then it has a unique minimizer. In the next lemma we show that if
f
is nonconvex and
\psi
uniformly convex with constant

, then the level set
\mathcal{L}_{F}(x_{0})
is bounded.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{X}^{*}
be the set of optimal solutions of problem (
1
). Assume
\psi
differentiable and uniformly convex function with constant

and the function

\|x-x^{*}\|\leq\max\left\{\!\left(\dfrac{(2(F(x_{0})-F(x^{*}))+L)q}{2(q-1)\sigma_{q}}\right)^{\frac{q-1}{2-q}}\!\!,1\right\}\\

We prove (

Then,

\|\bar{x}-x^{*}\|^{\frac{2-q}{q-1}}>\dfrac{(2(F(x_{0})-F(x^{*}))+L)q}{2(q-1)\sigma_{q}},

\dfrac{\sigma_{q}(q-1)}{q}\|\bar{x}-x^{*}\|^{\frac{q}{q-1}}\leq\psi(\bar{x})-\psi(x^{*})-\langle\nabla\psi(x^{*}),\bar{x}-x^{*}\rangle.\vskip-2.84544pt

-\dfrac{L}{2}\|\bar{x}-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq f(\bar{x})-f(x^{*})-\langle\nabla f(x^{*}),\bar{x}-x^{*}\rangle,\vskip-2.84544pt

Recall that in Lemmas

II) If

(60)

6.1
Sublinear convergence

Based on the descent inequalities above, which we proved without requiring the full gradient
\nabla F
to be Lipschitz continuous, as it is usually considered in the existing literature, we derive in this section convergence rates for our algorithms depending on the properties of
F
.

Theorem 12
.

Choose accuracy level
\varepsilon>0
and confidence level
\rho\in(0,1)
. Let the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be generated by the algorithms CPG or CGD with
i_{k}
chosen uniformly at random and satisfying (
59
). If

k\geq\dfrac{F(x_{0})-F^{*}}{\varepsilon\rho C},

(61)

then in probability we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\displaystyle\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-\rho.

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle k\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}
,
we have that

\mathbb{P}\left[\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\dfrac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right].

(62)

Further, from Markov inequality and basic properties of expectation, we get:

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}\geq k\varepsilon\right]\leq\dfrac{1}{k\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}\right]\leq\dfrac{1}{k\varepsilon}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}\right].

(63)

On the other hand, taking the expectation in the inequality (
59
), w.r.t.
\{x_{0},...,x_{k-1}\}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k+1})]\leq\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k})]-C\cdot\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}]
.
This implies that:

C\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}]\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(\mathbb{E}[F(x_{i})]-\mathbb{E}[F(x_{i+1})]\right)\leq F(x_{0})-F^{*},

(64)

Combining the previous relations, we obtain:

\mathbb{P}\left[\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq\dfrac{F(x_{0})-F^{*}}{k\varepsilon C}\leq\rho,

which proves our statement.

Remark 13
.

Note that from inequality (
64
), we also obtain:

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}\right]\leq\dfrac{F(x_{0})-F^{*}}{kC}.

Remark 14
.

Using a similar reasoning, in the cyclic case, we also have:

\min_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\|\nabla F(x_{i})\|^{2}\leq\dfrac{N\left(F(x_{0})-F^{*}\right)}{Ck}.

6.2

In this section we derive convergence rates for our algorithms when the objective function

satisfies the KL property, see Definition

\kappa(t)=\sigma_{q}^{\frac{1}{q}}\frac{q}{q-1}t^{\frac{q-1}{q}}

In the next lemma, we derive

Convergence analysis: convex case

In this section we assume that the composite objective function
F=f+\psi
is convex. Note that we do not need to impose convexity on
f
and
\psi
separately. Denote the set of optimal solutions of (
1
) by
X^{*}
and let
x^{*}
be an element of this set. Define also:

R=\max_{k\geq 0}\min_{x^{*}\in X^{*}}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|<\infty.\vskip-2.84544pt

Theorem 21
.

Let
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be generated by algorithm CPG or CGD, with
i_{k}
chosen uniformly at random. If the descent (
59
) holds and
F
is convex, then the following sublinear rate in function values holds:

\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})]\leq\dfrac{\left(F(x_{0})-F(x^{*})\right)R^{2}}{C\left(F(x_{0})-F(x^{*})\right)k+R^{2}}.

(77)

Proof.

Since
F
is convex, we have:

F(x^{*})-F(x_{k})\geq\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),x^{*}-x_{k+1}\rangle\geq-\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|\geq-\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|R.

Hence,

\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|\geq\dfrac{F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})}{R}.

(78)

Combining this inequality with (
59
), we get:

\left(F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})\right)-\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k+1})-F(x^{*})\ |\ x_{k}]\geq C\dfrac{\left(F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})\right)^{2}}{R^{2}}.

(79)

Since
t\mapsto t^{2}
is convex function, then taking expectation on both sides of the inequality (
79
) w.r.t.
\{x_{0},...,x_{k-1}\}
, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})]-\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k+1})-F(x^{*})]\geq C\dfrac{\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})]^{2}}{R^{2}}.

Multiplying both sides by
C/R^{2}
, we further get:

\dfrac{C\cdot\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})]}{R^{2}}-\dfrac{C\cdot\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k+1})-F(x^{*})]}{R^{2}}\geq\left[\dfrac{C\cdot\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})]}{R^{2}}\right]^{2}.

Denote

\Delta_{k}=\dfrac{C\cdot\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})]}{R^{2}}
. Therefore, we obtain the following recurrence:

\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1}\geq\left(\Delta_{k}\right)^{2}.

From Lemma 9 in
[
27
]
, we obtain:

\dfrac{C\cdot\mathbb{E}[F(x_{k})-F(x^{*})]}{R^{2}}\leq\dfrac{C\left(F(x_{0})-F(x^{*})\right)}{C\left(F(x_{0})-F(x^{*})\right)k+R^{2}}.

which proves our statement.

[
37
]

Implementation details for CPG algorithm:

Note that at each iteration of CPG for solving problem (

. Once we compute a positive root

we have that the equation (

Matrix factorization

Finally, we consider the penalized orthogonal matrix factorization problem, see also
[
2
]
:

\min_{(W,V)}F(W,V)=\min_{(W,V)}\dfrac{1}{2}\|X-WV\|^{2}_{F}+\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\|I-VV^{T}\|^{2}_{F},\vskip-5.69046pt

(84)

with
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
. Let us define:

f(W,V)=\dfrac{1}{2}\|X-WV\|^{2}_{F}
and
\psi(W,V)=\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\|I-VV^{T}\|^{2}_{F}.

Then, one can easily compute:

\nabla_{W}f(W,V)=WVV^{T}-XV^{T}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\nabla^{2}_{WW}f(W,V)Z=ZVV^{T}.

Thus,
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous w.r.t.
W
, with
L_{1}(V)=\|VV^{T}\|_{F}
. Similarly:

\nabla_{V}f(W,V)=W^{T}WV-W^{T}X\text{ and }\nabla^{2}_{VV}f(W,V)Z=W^{T}WZ.\vskip-5.69046pt

Therefore,
\nabla f
is also Lipschitz continuous w.r.t.
V
, with constant Lipschitz
L_{2}(W)=\|W^{T}W\|_{F}
. On the other hand,
\nabla_{V}\psi(W,V)=2\lambda(VV^{T}V-V)
and thus

\nabla^{2}_{VV}\psi(W,V)Z=2\lambda(ZV^{T}V+VZ^{T}V+VV^{T}Z-Z).\vskip-5.69046pt

Therefore, we get the following bound on the Hessian:

\langle Z,\nabla^{2}_{V}\psi(W,V)Z\rangle=\langle Z,2\lambda(ZV^{T}V+VZ^{T}V+VV^{T}Z-Z)\rangle\leq 6\lambda\|Z\|_{F}^{2}\|V\|_{F}^{2}.\vskip-5.69046pt

This shows that
\nabla^{2}_{V}\psi(\cdot)
satisfies condition [A.4] in Assumption
8
, with
p=2
and
H_{\psi}=6\lambda
. Note that if we assume that there exist
\bar{L}_{1},\bar{L}_{2}>0
such that
L_{1}(V)\leq\bar{L}_{1}
and
L_{2}(W)\leq\bar{L}_{2}
, then Lemmas
9
and
10
are still valid.

84

\nabla F
is Lipschitz continuous w.r.t.
W

9
Conclusions

In this paper we have considered composite problems having the objective formed as a sum of two terms, one smooth and the other twice differentiable, both possibly nonconvex and nonseparable. For solving this problem we have proposed two algorithms, a coordinate proximal gradient method and a coordinate gradient descent method, respectively. For the second algorithm we have designed several novel adaptive stepsize strategies which guarantee descent. For both algorithms we derived convergence bounds in both convex and nonconvex settings. Preliminary numerical results confirm the efficiency of our algorithms on real applications.

\theta_{k}:=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{\epsilon}]

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}^{\epsilon}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{\epsilon}]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{\epsilon}]^{\zeta+1},\quad\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k+1}^{\epsilon}]\leq\left(1-\epsilon^{\zeta}\right)\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{\epsilon}].\vskip-5.69046pt
