Index of minimal hypersurfaces in real projective spaces
Shuli Chen

Abstract
. We prove that for an embedded unstable one-sided minimal hypersurface of the ( n + 1 ) (n+1) -dimensional real projective space, the Morse index is at least n + 2 n+2 , and this bound is attained by the cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces. We also show that there exist closed embedded two-sided minimal surfaces in the 3-dimensional real projective space of each odd index by computing the index of the Lawson surfaces.



1.
Introduction

Minimal hypersurfaces in the unit round sphere
S^{n+1}
have been intensively studied. For instance, Lawson
[
LJ70
]
constructed three families of minimal surfaces in
S^{3}
, now called the Lawson surfaces, and used them to show that every closed surface but the projective plane can be minimally immersed into
S^{3}
. However, determining the Morse index of a minimal hypersurface is in general a difficult problem which has been fully solved only in a few cases. For example, Solomon
[
Sol90a
,
Sol90b
]
computed the index of cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces via representation theory. Kapouleas and Wiygul
[
KW20
]
computed the index of the Lawson surfaces
\xi_{m-1,1}
using their symmetries.

On the other hand, certain bounds can be given on the index of minimal hypersurfaces in
S^{n+1}
. In the late ’60s, Simons
[
Sim68
]
proved that there do not exist closed stable minimal hypersurfaces in
S^{n+1}
and also characterized the totally geodesic hyperspheres as the only closed minimal hypersurfaces
of index one. Later, Urbano
[
Urb90
]
proved that any closed two-sided minimal surface in
S^{3}
that is not totally geodesic has index at least 5 and the equality occurs only for the minimal Clifford surface. A direct computation shows that minimal Clifford hypersurfaces have index
n+3
in
S^{n+1}
, and it can be shown that any closed two-sided minimal non-totally geodesic hypersurface in
S^{n+1}
has
index bigger than or equal to
n+3
(see e.g.,
[
Per01
]
). An open question is whether the minimal Clifford hypersurfaces are the only minimal immersed hypersurfaces in
S^{n+1}
with index
n+3
. For a two-sided minimal non-totally geodesic hypersurface
\Sigma^{n}
in
S^{n+1}
, Savo
[
Sav10
]
showed that its index is bounded below by
\frac{2}{(n+1)(n+2)}b_{1}(\Sigma)+n+2
, which is a linear function of its first Betti number
b_{1}(\Sigma)
. In this paper, we observe that Savo’s bound can be extended to the one-sided case as well (see Theorem
3.6
).

Since the real projective space
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
is the quotient of
S^{n+1}
by the antipodal map, it is also natural to study minimal hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
. Notice that
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
has no closed stable two-sided minimal hypersurfaces because it has positive Ricci curvature. However, the real projective subspaces
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
are closed stable one-sided minimal hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
, and Ohnita
[
Ohn86
]
proved that they are the only stable ones; this demonstrates that the stability condition in the one-sided case in general becomes more subtle.
For two-sided minimal hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
, do Carmo, Ritoré, and Ros
[
DCRR00
]
proved that the totally geodesic sphere and the Clifford hypersurfaces are the only closed two-sided minimal hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
with index 1, and Batista and Martins
[
BM22
]
further showed that they are the only closed two-sided minimal and constant scalar curvature
hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
with index less than or equal to two. Ambrozio, Carlotto, Sharp
[
ACS18
]
extended Savo’s result on hypersurfaces in
S^{n+1}
to hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
, and showed that for a closed embedded minimal hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
, its index is bounded below by
\frac{2}{(n+1)(n+2)}b_{1}(\Sigma)
.

In this paper, we consider one-sided minimal hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
and show that unlike the two-sided case, there is a large gap in the index.
We also compute the index of the cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
using works of Solomon
[
Sol90a
,
Sol90b
]
. See Section
4.2
for the definition of cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces.

Theorem 1.1
.

For an embedded unstable one-sided minimal hypersurface
\Sigma^{n}
of
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
, the Morse index is at least
n+2
. Further, this bound is attained by the cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces.

In addition, we establish the following theorem by computing the index of the Lawson surfaces, using work of Kapouleas and Wiygul
[
KW20
]
:

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist closed embedded two-sided minimal surfaces of every odd index in
\mathbb{R}P^{3}
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Schrödinger-type operators on involutive manifolds.

Let
(\Sigma^{n},g)
be a closed Riemannian manifold. Let
V\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
and consider the Schrödinger operator
-\Delta+V
. Then standard elliptic theory tells us that the eigenvalues of
L=-\Delta+V
are discrete and bounded from below, and we can list them in increasing order as
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\dots\to\infty
. It is well-known that we have the following variational characterization of the eigenvalues:

(1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\max_{S\in S_{k-1}}\min_{u\in S^{\perp},u\neq 0}\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+Vu^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}u^{2}\,d\Sigma},

where
S_{k-1}
denotes the collection of all the subspaces of
W^{1,2}(\Sigma)
of dimension
k-1
and
S^{\perp}
denotes the
L^{2}
-orthogonal complement of
S
in
W^{1,2}(\Sigma)
.
This value can be attained and any nonzero function
u
attaining this value is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
V_{1},V_{2}\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
be such that
V_{1}\leq V_{2}
everywhere and
V_{1}\not\equiv V_{2}
. Consider two Schrödinger operators
L_{1}=-\Delta+V_{1}
and
L_{2}=-\Delta+V_{2}
. Let
\lambda_{k}(L_{i})
be the
k^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue of the operator
L_{i}
. Then we have
\lambda_{k}(L_{1})<\lambda_{k}(L_{2})
for all
k
.

Proof.

Since for any function
u\in W^{1,2}(\Sigma)
, we have

\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+V_{1}u^{2}\,d\Sigma\leq\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+V_{2}u^{2}\,d\Sigma
, it follows from the above variational characterization of the eigenvalues that
\lambda_{k}(L_{1})\leq\lambda_{k}(L_{2})
for all
k
.

Now for the sake of contradiction suppose for some
k
that
\lambda_{k}(L_{1})=\lambda_{k}(L_{2}):=\Lambda
.

Let
\mathcal{S}:=\{S\in S_{k-1}\mid S\text{ attains the value $\Lambda$ in \eqref{eqn: max-min} for $L_{2}$}\}
.

Then for any space
\tilde{S}\in S_{k-1}
,
\tilde{S}\not\in\mathcal{S}
, there exists
0\neq\tilde{u}\in\tilde{S}
such that

\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\tilde{u}|^{2}+V_{2}\tilde{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}\tilde{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}<\Lambda
. Since
V_{1}\leq V_{2}
, we have that
\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\tilde{u}|^{2}+V_{1}\tilde{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}\tilde{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}<\Lambda
as well. This shows
\tilde{S}
cannot attain the value
\Lambda
in (
1
) for
L_{1}
.

However, since
\Lambda
is also the
k^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue of
L_{1}
, there exists
S\in\mathcal{S}
such that
S
attains the value
\Lambda
in (
1
) for
L_{1}
. That is,

(2)

\displaystyle\min_{u\in S^{\perp},u\neq 0}\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla{u}|^{2}+V_{1}{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}=\Lambda.

On the other hand, from the definition of
\mathcal{S}
we have that
S
also attains the value
\Lambda
in (
1
) for
L_{2}
. So there exists
0\neq\hat{u}\in S^{\perp}
such that

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\hat{u}|^{2}+V_{2}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}=\min_{u\in S^{\perp},u\neq 0}\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla{u}|^{2}+V_{2}{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}=\Lambda.

Combining equations (
2
) and (
3
) with the inequality
V_{1}\leq V_{2}
shows that

\Lambda=\min_{u\in S^{\perp},u\neq 0}\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla{u}|^{2}+V_{1}{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}\leq\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\hat{u}|^{2}+V_{1}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}\leq\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\hat{u}|^{2}+V_{2}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}=\Lambda,

so we must have that

\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\hat{u}|^{2}+V_{1}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}=\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\hat{u}|^{2}+V_{2}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}{\int_{\Sigma}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma}=\Lambda,

i.e.,
\hat{u}
attains the value
\Lambda=\lambda_{k}(L_{1})=\lambda_{k}(L_{2})
in (
1
) for both
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
.

Thus
\hat{u}
is an eigenfunction of both
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, and
\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\hat{u}|^{2}+V_{1}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma=\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\hat{u}|^{2}+V_{2}\hat{u}^{2}\,d\Sigma
.
Hence we have
\int_{\Sigma}(V_{2}-V_{1})\hat{u}^{2}d\Sigma=0
. This shows
\hat{u}
vanishes on the open set
\{V_{2}\neq V_{1}\}
, which by the unique continuation principle (see, e.g.,
[
Aro56
]
) implies
\hat{u}\equiv 0
, a contradiction.

∎

Now suppose there exists a Riemannian
involution
\tau:(\Sigma,g)\to(\Sigma,g)
, namely a smooth isometry such that

\tau\circ\tau=\operatorname{id},\,\tau\neq\operatorname{id}.

Then given a linear function space
X
consisting of functions defined on
\Sigma
we introduce the subspaces of even and odd functions with respect to the action of
\tau
:

X_{\mathcal{E}}=\{\phi\in X\mid\phi\circ\tau=\phi\},\,X_{\mathcal{O}}=\{\phi\in X\mid\phi\circ\tau=-\phi\}.

In particular, with respect to the involution
\tau
, we have the
L^{2}
-orthogonal decompositions

\displaystyle C^{\infty}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=C^{\infty}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{E}}\oplus C^{\infty}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle W^{1,2}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=W^{1,2}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{E}}\oplus W^{1,2}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{O}},

Given a function
V\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{E}}
we study the operator
L=-\Delta+V
. For each eigenvalue
\lambda
, let
W_{\lambda}
be the corresponding space of eigenfunctions. Then it is easy to see that we have the
L^{2}
-orthogonal decomposition
W_{\lambda}=(W_{\lambda})_{\mathcal{E}}\oplus(W_{\lambda})_{\mathcal{O}}
.

Let
spec(L)
denote the set of eigenvalues of
L
, and let
spec_{\mathcal{E}}(L)
and
spec_{\mathcal{O}}(L)
denote the set of even eigenvalues and odd eigenvalues, respectively. That is,

\lambda\in spec_{\mathcal{E}}(L)\Leftrightarrow\exists u\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{E}}\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }Lu=\lambda u,

\lambda\in spec_{\mathcal{O}}(L)\Leftrightarrow\exists u\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{O}}\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }Lu=\lambda u.

Then
spec(L)=spec_{\mathcal{E}}(L)\cup spec_{\mathcal{O}}(L)
(for proof, see, e.g.,
[
ABCS19
, Lemma 16]
).
Let
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{E}}(L)
(resp.
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{O}}(L)
) be the
k^{\text{th}}
even (resp. odd) eigenvalue of the operator
L
. For
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{E}}(L)
(resp.
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{O}}(L)
), we also have the variational characterization as in (
1
), with
W^{1,2}(\Sigma)
replaced by
W^{1,2}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{E}}
(resp.
W^{1,2}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{O}}
).
Using this, it is straightforward to deduce

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(\Sigma^{n},g)
be an
n
-dimensional closed Riemannian manifold with a Riemannian involution
\tau
. Let
V_{1},V_{2}\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{E}}
be such that
V_{1}\leq V_{2}
everywhere and
V_{1}\not\equiv V_{2}
. Consider two Schrödinger operators
L_{1}=-\Delta+V_{1}
and
L_{2}=-\Delta+V_{2}
. Let
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{E}}(L_{i})
(resp.
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{O}}(L_{i})
) be the
k^{\text{th}}
even (resp. odd) eigenvalue of the operator
L_{i}
. Then we have
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{E}}(L_{1})<\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{E}}(L_{2})
and
\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{O}}(L_{1})<\lambda_{k}^{\mathcal{O}}(L_{2})
for all
k
.

2.2.
Morse index of minimal submanifolds

Let
\Phi:(\Sigma^{n},g)\rightarrow(M^{m},\bar{g})
be a minimal isometric immersion of an
n
-dimensional closed Riemannian manifold
(\Sigma,g)
. Let
C^{\infty}(\Phi^{*}TM))
denote the space of all
C^{\infty}
-vector fields along
\Phi
. For any
V\in C^{\infty}(\Phi^{*}TM)
, let
\{\Phi_{t}\}
be a
C^{\infty}
-one-parameter family of immersions of
\Sigma
into
M
such that
\Phi_{0}=\Phi
and
\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{t}(x)|_{t=0}=V_{x}
for each
x\in\Sigma
. Then we have the classical second variational formula

Theorem 2.3

(Second variation formula)
.

The second variation of the volume
|\Phi_{t}(\Sigma)|
at time
t=0
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}|\Phi_{t}(\Sigma)|\biggr|_{t=0}=-\int_{\Sigma}\langle\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}V^{N},V^{N}\rangle d\Sigma,

where
V^{N}
is the normal
component of
V
,
\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
is the elliptic Jacobi operator on the normal bundle
N(\Sigma)
defined by

\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}(X):=\Delta^{\perp}X+(\sum_{i=1}^{n}R^{M}(X,e_{i})e_{i})^{\perp}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle{\rm II}_{\Sigma}(e_{i},e_{j}),X\rangle{\rm II}_{\Sigma}(e_{i},e_{j}),

and
{\Delta}^{\perp}
is the normal Laplacian on the normal bundle
N(\Sigma)
given by

{\Delta}^{\perp}X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\overline{\nabla}^{\perp}_{e_{i}}\overline{\nabla}^{\perp}_{e_{i}}X-\overline{\nabla}^{\perp}_{(\overline{\nabla}_{e_{i}}e_{i})^{T}}X).

Here,
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
T\Sigma
,
\overline{\nabla}
is the connection of
M
,
\overline{\nabla}^{\perp}
is the normal connection of
\Sigma
in
M
,
{\rm II}_{\Sigma}
is the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
in
M
, and
R^{M}
is the curvature tensor of
M
.

Now for
V\in C^{\infty}(N\Sigma)
, we define the quadratic form
Q_{\Sigma}
on
C^{\infty}(N\Sigma)
by

Q_{\Sigma}(V,V):=-\int_{\Sigma}\langle\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}V,V\rangle d\Sigma.

For
\Sigma
, we define its
Morse index

\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)
to be the largest dimension of a linear subspace of
C^{\infty}(N\Sigma)
where
Q_{\Sigma}
is negative definite.

2.3.
Morse index of minimal hypersurfaces

In the special case where
\Sigma
is a two-sided closed minimal hypersurface of
M
, we let
N
be a unit normal vector field of
\Sigma
. Notice that if the ambient space
M
is orientable, then two-sideness of
\Sigma
is equivalent to
\Sigma
being orientable. For any
V
in
C^{\infty}(\Phi^{*}TM)
, let
\{\Phi_{t}\}
be a
C^{\infty}
-one-parameter family of immersions of
\Sigma
into
M
such that
\Phi_{0}=\Phi
and
\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{t}(x)|_{t=0}=V_{x}
for each
x\in\Sigma
.
Then there exists a unique
C^{\infty}
function
u_{V}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
such that
V^{N}=u_{V}N
. And the second variation formula simplifies to

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}|\Phi_{t}(\Sigma)|\biggr|_{t=0}=-\int_{\Sigma}\langle\mathcal{L}_{scal}u_{V},u_{V}\rangle d\Sigma
,

where
\mathcal{L}_{scal}:=\Delta+\operatorname{Ric}_{M}(N,N)+|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|^{2}
is the Jacobi
operator on
\Sigma
acting on scalar-valued functions.

Now for
u\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
, we can define the quadratic form
Q_{\Sigma,scal}
on
C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
by

Q_{\Sigma,scal}(u,u):=-\int_{\Sigma}\langle\mathcal{L}_{scal}u,u\rangle d\Sigma.

For
\Sigma
, we define its
Morse index
,
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)
, to be the largest dimension of a linear subspace of
C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
where
Q_{\Sigma,scal}
is negative definite. By the theory of elliptic operators on closed manifolds, the Morse index also equals the number of negative eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity) of the Jacobi operator
\mathcal{L}_{scal}
.
It is straightforward to check that the definition given here agrees with the definition of Morse index in the general case. In the following, we will omit the subscript
scal
when there is no confusion.

We call a
C^{2}
-function
u\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
a
Jacobi field
if it satisfies
\mathcal{L}u=0
on
\Sigma
.
We define the
nullity
of
\Sigma
,
\operatorname{Nul}(\Sigma)
, as the dimension of the space of Jacobi fields on
\Sigma
. Equivalently, this is the number of
0
-eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity) of
\mathcal{L}
. It is well-known that Killing vector fields of the ambient manifold
M
induce Jacobi fields of
\Sigma
. We call a
Jacobi field on
\Sigma

non-exceptional
if it is induced by a Killing field; otherwise we call it
exceptional
.

Now suppose on
\Sigma
there exists a Riemannian
involution
\tau:(\Sigma,g)\to(\Sigma,g)
.
We define the
even Morse index

\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{E}}(\Sigma)
(resp. the
odd Morse index

\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\Sigma)
) as the largest dimension
of a linear subspace of
C^{\infty}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{E}}
(resp.
C^{\infty}(\Sigma)_{\mathcal{O}}
) where
Q_{\Sigma}
is negative definite. Again, the even (resp. odd) Morse index equals the number of even (resp. odd) negative eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
, counted with multiplicity.
We also define the
even nullity

\operatorname{Nul}_{\mathcal{E}}(\Sigma)
(resp. the
odd nullity

\operatorname{Nul}_{\mathcal{O}}(\Sigma)
) as the dimension of the space of even (resp. odd) Jacobi fields on
\Sigma
.
Then it is not hard to deduce that (see, e.g.,
[
ABCS19
, Section 2.1]
)

\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{E}}(\Sigma)+\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\Sigma),

\operatorname{Nul}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Nul}_{\mathcal{E}}(\Sigma)+\operatorname{Nul}_{\mathcal{O}}(\Sigma).

3.
Minimal hypersurfaces in
S^{n+1}

Let
({\Sigma}^{n},g)\to(S^{n+1},\overline{g})
be a closed connected orientable (hence two-sided) immersed minimal hypersurface of the
(n+1)
-dimensional
Euclidean unit sphere. Let
N
denote a unit normal vector field on
\Sigma
. Let
\overline{\nabla}
denote the Levi-Civita connection on
S^{n+1}
, let
\nabla
denote the induced Levi-Civita connection on
\Sigma
, and let
\Delta
denote the Laplacian on
\Sigma
.
Then
\operatorname{Ric}_{S^{n+1}}(N,N)=n
and the Jacobi operator on
\Sigma
becomes
\mathcal{L}=\Delta+|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|^{2}+n
.

Definition 3.1

(Test functions
l_{w}
,
f_{w}
,
r_{v,w}
)
.

For every fixed vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, we define functions
l_{w}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
and
f_{w}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
by

l_{w}(x)=\langle w,x\rangle,

f_{w}(x)=\langle w,N(x)\rangle

for all
x\in\Sigma
. Here
\langle,\rangle
denotes the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
.
For
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, we further define
r_{v,w}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
by

r_{v,w}:=l_{v}f_{w}-l_{w}f_{v}.

A standard computation using Gauss and Weingarten formulas shows that

\displaystyle\nabla l_{w}

\displaystyle=w^{\top},

\displaystyle\nabla f_{w}

\displaystyle=-A(w^{\top}),

where
\top
denotes the projection onto
T\Sigma
and
A(X)=-(\overline{\nabla}_{X}N)^{\top}
is the shape operator of
\Sigma
.

Using the minimality of
\Sigma
and the Codazzi equations, we have

\displaystyle\Delta l_{w}

\displaystyle=-nl_{w},

\displaystyle\Delta f_{w}

\displaystyle=-|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|^{2}f_{w}.

Then each nonzero
f_{w}
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}
with eigenvalue
-n
.

We define the linear subspaces
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3}
of
C^{\infty}(M)
by

\Gamma_{1}:=\{l_{w}\mid w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\},

\Gamma_{2}:=\{f_{w}\mid w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\},

\Gamma_{3}:=\{r_{v,w}\mid v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\}.

We have the following three simple lemmas regarding these spaces:

Lemma 3.2
.

For
0\neq w\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, we have
Q_{\Sigma}(l_{w},l_{w})\leq 0
, and
Q_{\Sigma}(l_{w},l_{w})=0
if and only if
\Sigma
is an equatorial hypersphere. Consequently, for
\Sigma
not an equatorial hypersphere,
Q_{\Sigma}
is negative definite on
\Gamma_{1}
and
\dim\Gamma_{1}=n+2
.

Proof.

Using
\Delta l_{w}=-nl_{w}
, we have

\mathcal{L}l_{w}=\Delta l_{w}+|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|^{2}l_{w}+nl_{w}=|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|^{2}l_{w},

so

Q_{\Sigma}(l_{w},l_{w})=-\int|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|^{2}l_{w}^{2}\leq 0.

If
Q_{\Sigma}(l_{w},l_{w})=0
, then
|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|^{2}l_{w}^{2}\equiv 0
. Thus at every point
x\in\Sigma
, either
|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|=0
or
l_{w}=\langle w,x\rangle=0
. If
|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|\not\equiv 0
, then consider the open neighborhood
U
where
|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|>0
. In
U
we have
\langle w,x\rangle=0
, so
U
is contained in the equatorial hypersphere orthogonal to
w
. Then
U
is totally geodesic, showing
|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|=0
on
U
, which is a contradiction. Hence the condition
Q_{\Sigma}(l_{w},l_{w})=0
implies
|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|\equiv 0
, that is,
\Sigma
is totally geodesic. The only such immersed hypersurface is an equator.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

[
Per01
, Lemma 3.1]

For
\Sigma
not an equatorial hypersphere, we have
\dim\Gamma_{2}=n+2
.

Lemma 3.4
.

r_{v,w}
are non-exceptional Jacobi fields of
\Sigma
.

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle\Delta(l_{v}f_{w})

\displaystyle=(\Delta l_{v})f_{w}+l_{v}\Delta f_{w}+2\langle\nabla l_{v},\nabla f_{w}\rangle

\displaystyle=-nl_{v}f_{w}-|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|^{2}l_{v}f_{w}+2\langle v^{\top},-A(w^{\top})\rangle

\displaystyle=-nl_{v}f_{w}-|{\rm II}_{\Sigma}|^{2}l_{v}f_{w}-2\,{\rm II}_{\Sigma}(v^{\top},w^{\top}).

Thus
\mathcal{L}(l_{v}f_{w})=-2\,{\rm II}_{\Sigma}(v^{\top},w^{\top})
. Using the symmetry of the second fundamental form, we have
\mathcal{L}(r_{v,w})=\mathcal{L}(l_{v}f_{w}-l_{w}f_{v})=-2\,{\rm II}_{\Sigma}(v^{\top},w^{\top})+2\,{\rm II}_{\Sigma}(w^{\top},v^{\top})=0
as desired.
∎

Recall that every connected manifold
\Sigma
admits a double cover
\tilde{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
by the manifold
\tilde{\Sigma}=\{(p,\mathcal{O}_{p})\mid\text{$p\in\Sigma$ and $\mathcal{O}_{p}$ is an orientation of $T_{p}\Sigma$}\}
. We call
\tilde{\Sigma}
the orientation double cover of
\Sigma
. When
\Sigma
is non-orientable,
\tilde{\Sigma}
is connected, and
\tilde{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
is a two-sheeted smooth covering map. Using the above lemmas, we can give a bound on the index of one-sided (hence non-orientable) minimal hypersurfaces in
S^{n+1}
and its orientation double cover:

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\Phi:\Sigma\to S^{n+1}
be a one-sided closed connected immersed minimal hypersurface in
S^{n+1}
and let
\tilde{\Phi}:\tilde{\Sigma}\to S^{n+1}
be its orientation double cover. Then
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq n+2
and
\operatorname{Ind}(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq 2n+5
.

Proof.

Since
\Phi:\Sigma\to S^{n+1}
is a one-sided closed connected immersed minimal hypersurface in
S^{n+1}
, we have that
n\geq 2
and
\Sigma
is non-orientable, and we can consider the connected orientation double cover
p:\tilde{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
. The immersion
\Phi:\Sigma\to S^{n+1}
lifts to a two-sided immersion
\tilde{\Phi}:\tilde{\Sigma}\to S^{n+1}

that is also minimal. Since
\tilde{\Phi}
factors through the covering map
p
, it cannot be an embedding. In particular, this implies
\tilde{\Sigma}
cannot be the equatorial hypersphere, so
|{\rm II}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}|\not\equiv 0
. Let
N
denote a unit normal vector field of
\tilde{\Sigma}
and let
s:\tilde{\Sigma}\to\tilde{\Sigma}
be the change of sheet involution induced by the covering. Then
N
is odd with respect to the involution
s
. Normal vector fields on
\Sigma
are in one-to-one correspondence with normal vector fields on
\tilde{\Sigma}
that are even, i.e., vector fields of the form
\phi N
where
\phi
is an
odd
function with respect to
s
. Hence
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\tilde{\Sigma})
. Note that the functions
l_{w}
are even with respect to
s
, while the functions
f_{w}
are odd with respect to
s
. That is,
l_{w}\circ s=l_{w}
and
f_{w}\circ s=-f_{w}
.

Now consider the operator
L_{2}=-\Delta-n
. It has even eigenvalue
-n
with eigenfunction
1
, and even eigenvalue
0
with eigenfunctions
l_{w}
. Thus by Lemma
3.2
,
L_{2}
has at least
n+3
even eigenvalues less than or equal to
0
. Since
-n\leq-|{\rm II}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}|^{2}-n
and
|{\rm II}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}|^{2}\not\equiv 0
, by Lemma
2.2
, we have that
\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
has at least
n+3
even eigenvalues strictly less than
0
. In particular, this shows
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq n+3
.

By Lemma
3.3
,
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq\dim\Gamma_{2}=n+2
. Consequently,
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq n+2
and
\operatorname{Ind}(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{E}}(\tilde{\Sigma})+\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq 2n+5
.
∎

In
[
Sav10
]
, Savo proved a lower bound on the index of closed orientable minimal hypersurfaces
\Sigma^{n}
in
S^{n+1}
in terms of the first Betti number. Specifically, he proved that for
\Sigma
not totally geodesic, the number of eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
less than
-n
is bounded from below by

\frac{2}{(n+2)(n+1)}\cdot\text{(dimension of the space of harmonic 1-forms on $\Sigma$)}.

By Hodge theory, the dimension of the space of harmonic 1-forms on
\Sigma
equals
b_{1}(\Sigma)
.
Since
-n
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
with multiplicity at least
n+2
(Lemma

), it follows that
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)\geq\frac{2}{(n+2)(n+1)}b_{1}(\Sigma)+n+2
.

Now suppose that
\Sigma
is non-orientable and consider its orientation double cover
\tilde{\Sigma}
with change of sheet involution
s
. Looking through Savo’s proof, we see that every step carries over if we restrict only to functions and forms on
\tilde{\Sigma}
that can pass down to
\Sigma
; that is, we replace the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
by odd eigenvalues and we replace the eigen 1-forms of

(the rough Laplacian on 1-forms) by the eigen 1-forms of

invariant under

. Then we obtain that the number of odd eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\Sigma}}
less than

is bounded from below by

\frac{2}{(n+2)(n+1)}\cdot\text{(dimension of the space of harmonic 1-forms on $\tilde{\Sigma}$ that pass down to $\Sigma$)}.

This quantity is just
\frac{2}{(n+2)(n+1)}b_{1}(\Sigma)
. Since
-n
is an odd eigenvalue of

with multiplicity at least

(Lemma

), it follows that
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq\frac{2}{(n+2)(n+1)}b_{1}(\Sigma)+n+2
.
Combining with Proposition
3.5
, we can extend Savo’s result to the non-orientable setting as below.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\Phi:\Sigma\to S^{n+1}
be a one-sided closed connected immersed minimal hypersurface in
S^{n+1}
, and let
\tilde{\Phi}:\tilde{\Sigma}\to S^{n+1}
be its orientation double cover. Let

be the first Betti number of
\Sigma
. Then
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq\frac{2}{(n+2)(n+1)}b_{1}(\Sigma)+n+2

\operatorname{Ind}(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq\frac{2}{(n+2)(n+1)}b_{1}(\Sigma)+2n+5
.

4.
Minimal hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}

Define
\tau=-\operatorname{Id}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
. Then
\tau
restricted to the antipodal map on
S^{n+1}
. Let
({\widehat{\Sigma}}^{n},g)\to(S^{n+1},\overline{g})
be a closed connected orientable (hence two-sided) immersed minimal hypersurface with antipodal symmetry.

Let
p
denote the quotient map
p:S^{n}\to\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}=S^{n+1}/(x\sim\tau(x))
. Then
\widehat{\Sigma}
passes through the quotient map to a closed connected minimal hypersurface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
, and
\widehat{\Sigma}
is a double cover of
\Sigma
, with
\tau:\widehat{\Sigma}\rightarrow\widehat{\Sigma}
the change of sheet involution of
\widehat{\Sigma}
. Let
N
be a unit normal vector field on
\widehat{\Sigma}
.

If
(\Sigma^{n},g)
is two-sided, then
\tau:\widehat{\Sigma}\rightarrow\widehat{\Sigma}
must satisfy
d\tau_{x}(N(x))=N(\tau(x))
for all
x\in\widehat{\Sigma}
. Since
d\tau_{x}=-\operatorname{Id}
on
T_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})
, if we consider the normal vector field
N
of
\widehat{\Sigma}
as a map to
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
then
N(\tau(x))=d\tau_{x}(N(x))=-N(x)
, showing that
N
is odd under
\tau
. Since
\Sigma
is two-sided, normal vector fields on
\Sigma
are in one-to-one correspondence with normal vector fields on
\tilde{\Sigma}
that are odd with respect to
\tau
, i.e., vector fields of the form
\phi N
where
\phi
is an
even
function with respect to
\tau
.
The Morse index of
\Sigma
thus equals the index of the quadratic form
Q_{\widehat{\Sigma}}
over the space of functions
\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\widehat{\Sigma})
satisfying
\varphi\circ\tau=\varphi
. Thus
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{\Sigma})
.

If instead
(\Sigma^{n},g)
is one-sided, then
\tau:\widehat{\Sigma}\rightarrow\widehat{\Sigma}
must satisfy
d\tau_{x}(N(x))=-N(\tau(x))
for all
x\in\widehat{\Sigma}
. In this case the normal vector field
N
of
\widehat{\Sigma}
, considered as a map to
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, is even under
\tau
; i.e.,
N(\tau(x))=N(x)
.
The Morse index of
\Sigma
equals the index of the quadratic form
Q_{\widehat{\Sigma}}
over the space of functions
\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\widehat{\Sigma})
satisfying
\varphi\circ\tau=-\varphi
. Thus
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{\Sigma})
.

4.1.
Lower bound on the index of one-sided minimal hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}

When
n
is even,
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
is orientable. Then a connected one-sided hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
is non-orientable, while a connected two-sided hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
is orientable.
When
n
is odd,
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
is non-orientable. Then a connected one-sided hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
is orientable, while a connected two-sided hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
is non-orientable, unless it contains
no loop noncontractible in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
. For details, see
[
Vir98
, Section 1]
.

Since
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
has positive Ricci curvature, it does not have closed stable connected two-sided minimal hypersurfaces. On the other hand, Ohnita
[
Ohn86
, Theorem C]
showed the only closed stable connected one-sided minimal hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
are the projective subspaces
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
. Here we show that in many cases there is a large gap on the index of one-sided minimal hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\Phi:\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
be an unstable connected one-sided immersed minimal hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
. Suppose either

•

n
is odd, or

•

n
is even and
\Phi
lifts to an immersion of
\Sigma^{n}
into
S^{n+1}
, or

•

n
is even and there exist a connected
orientable twofold covering
\widehat{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
and an isometric minimal immersion
\widehat{\Phi}:\widehat{\Sigma}\to S^{n+1}
locally
congruent to
\Phi
such that
\widehat{\Sigma}
is invariant under the antipodal symmetry
\tau:S^{n+1}\to S^{n+1}
,

Then
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)\geq n+2
.

Proof.

Case 1:
Suppose
n
is odd. Then since
\Sigma
is one-sided,
\Sigma
is orientable. There are two subcases: either (1)
\Phi
lifts to an immersion of
\Sigma^{n}
into
S^{n+1}
, or (2) there exist a connected twofold covering
\widehat{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
and an isometric minimal immersion
\widehat{\Phi}:\widehat{\Sigma}\to S^{n}
locally congruent to
\Phi
such that
\widehat{\Sigma}
is invariant under the antipodal symmetry
\tau:S^{n+1}\to S^{n+1}
.

Subcase (1):
If
\Phi
lifts to an immersion of
\Sigma
into
S^{n+1}
, then
\Sigma
is a one-sided minimal hypersurface in the sphere. However, one-sided minimal hypersurfaces in the sphere are non-orientable, contradicting the fact that
\Sigma
is orientable. So this subcase cannot happen.

Subcase (2):
Since Subcase (1) cannot happen, there exist a connected twofold covering
\widehat{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
and an isometric minimal immersion
\widehat{\Phi}:\widehat{\Sigma}\to S^{n+1}
locally
congruent to
\Phi
such that
\widehat{\Sigma}
is invariant under the antipodal symmetry
\tau:S^{n+1}\to S^{n+1}
,
x\mapsto-x
.
Since
\Sigma
is orientable,
\widehat{\Sigma}
is also orientable (hence two-sided in
S^{n+1}
).
Then
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{\Sigma})
.

If
\widehat{\Sigma}
is an equatorial sphere, then by
[
Sim68
]
,
\operatorname{Ind}(\widehat{\Sigma})=1=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{\Sigma})
, and
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{\Sigma})=0
. This shows
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{\Sigma})=0
. This is impossible since we assume
\Sigma
to be unstable.

Thus
\widehat{\Sigma}
is a non-equatorial minimal hypersurface in
S^{n+1}
. Notice that the functions
l_{w}\in\Gamma_{1}
as defined in Definition
3.1
are odd with respect to the antipodal map
\tau
. By Lemma
3.2
,
Q_{\widehat{\Sigma}}
is negative definite on
\Gamma_{1}
, so
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{\Sigma})\geq\dim(\Gamma_{1})=n+2
.

Thus
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{\Sigma})\geq n+2
in this subcase.

Case 2:
Suppose
n
is even and
\Phi
lifts to an immersion of
\Sigma^{n}
into
S^{n+1}
. Then the normal bundles of
\Sigma^{n}
in the two ambient spaces
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
and
S^{n+1}
are isomorphic, so the index of
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
equals the index of
\Sigma
in
S^{n+1}
. By Theorem
3.5
,
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)\geq n+2
.

Case 3:
Suppose
n
is even and there exist a connected orientable twofold covering
\widehat{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
and an isometric minimal immersion
\widehat{\Phi}:\widehat{\Sigma}\to S^{n+1}
locally
congruent to
\Phi
such that
\widehat{\Sigma}
is invariant under the antipodal symmetry
\tau
. In this case the reasoning is exactly the same as in Case 1 Subcase (2), and we have
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)\geq n+2
.

∎

The only possibility not included in the previous theorem is when
n
is even and the twofold covering
\widehat{\Sigma}\to\Sigma
invariant under the antipodal symmetry is non-orientable. Note that since embedded hypersurfaces in
S^{n+1}
(or any simply-connected manifold) are two-sided (see, e.g.,
[
Sam69
]
), if we assume
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
to be embedded, then this case actually cannot happen. Therefore we have

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\Phi:\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
be an unstable connected one-sided embedded minimal hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
. Then
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)\geq n+2
.

Remark 4.3
.

In
[
ACS18
]
, Ambrozio, Carlotto, and Sharp proved for a closed embedded minimal hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
,
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)\geq\frac{2}{(n+1)(n+2)}b_{1}(\Sigma)
. They remarked that it is not possible to obtain an extra
n+2
in the lower bound (see
[
ACS18
, Remark 5.2]
), unlike the case when the ambient space is a sphere (see
[
Sav10
]
and also Theorem

).
Indeed, as mentioned earlier, the projective subspace
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
is one-sided and has index 0 in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}

[
Ohn86
]
, and
the Clifford hypersurfaces are two-sided and have index
1
in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}

[
DCRR00
]
.
Even when the hypersurface is unstable and one-sided, we have been unable to incorporate the lower bounds
\frac{2}{(n+1)(n+2)}b_{1}(\Sigma)
and
n+2
together as in the case of Theorem
3.6
. This is because the functions
l_{w}
only produce test functions but not eigenfunctions for the Jacobi operator.

4.2.
Index of cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces

Next we consider the isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces in
S^{n+1}
. A hypersurface
\Sigma^{n}
in
S^{n+1}
is called
isoparametric
if its principal curvature functions,

\kappa_{1}(x)\leq\kappa_{2}(x)\leq\dots\leq\kappa_{n}(x),

are constants. In particular the norm of the second fundamental form of
\Sigma^{n}
is constant. Let
g
be the number of distinct principal curvatures of
\Sigma^{n}
. Isoparametric hypersurfaces were first studied by Élie Cartan
[
Car38
,
Car39a
,
Car39b
]
in the late 1930’s. The interest in isoparametric hypersurfaces was revived in the 1970s, and in 1973, Münzner
[
Mün80
]
established a breakthrough result. He showed that the number of distinct principal curvature
g
could only be 1, 2, 3, 4, or 6, and there exists a homogeneous polynomial
F
, later called the Cartan–Münzner
polynomial, of degree
g
over
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
satisfying

(4)

\displaystyle|DF(x)|^{2}=g^{2}|x|^{2g-2},

(5)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{n+2}}F=c|x|^{g-2}\,\text{ for some constant $c$}

such that
\Sigma=F^{-1}(a)\cap S^{n+1}
for some
a\in(-1,1)
. Conversely, each Cartan–Münzner polynomial
F
gives a family of isoparametric hypersurfaces; for any
a\in(-1,1)
, the level set
F^{-1}(a)\cap S^{n+1}
is smooth and isoparametric. The mean curvature of the hypersurface
F^{-1}(a)\cap S^{n+1}
varies monotonically between
-\infty
and
+\infty
as
a
varies between
-1
and
1
. In particular, there is a unique
t\in(-1,1)
such that the hypersurface
\Sigma=F^{-1}(t)\cap S^{n+1}
has zero mean curvature, i.e., it is minimal. Such hypersurfaces are called
isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces
. For odd
g
, the isoparametric minimal hypersurface in the family is given by
t=0
. We refer the reader to the book
[
CR15
]
or the surveys
[
Tho00
,
Chi20
]
for more details and references on isoparametric hypersurfaces.

Let
\Sigma^{n}=F^{-1}(t)\cap S^{n+1}
be an isoparametric minimal hypersurface with
g
distinct principal curvatures. Since the Cartan–Münzner
polynomial
F
is homogeneous and
t=0
for odd
g
, we see that
\Sigma
is invariant under the antipodal map
\tau
. On
\Sigma
, one can take a unit normal vector field as
N=\frac{1}{g}DF
, where
DF
is the gradient of
F
as a function on
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
. When
g=2,4,6
, we see that
N
is odd under
\tau
as a function to
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, while when
g=1,3
,

N
is even under
\tau
. Thus when
g=2,4,6
,
\Sigma
quotients through
\tau
to a two-sided hypersurface
\Sigma
of
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
and when
g=1,3
,
\Sigma
quotients through
\tau
to a one-sided hypersurface
\Sigma
of
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

[
PT83
, Corollary 1]

Let
\Sigma^{n}
be an isoparametric minimal hypersurface with
g
distinct principal curvatures. Then the squared norm of the second fundamental form
|{\rm II}|^{2}
equals
(g-1)n
.

Below we focus on cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces
\Sigma
, so
g=3
. In this case, Cartan
[
Car39a
,
Car39b
]
showed that the three principal curvatures must have equal multiplicity
m=1,2,4,
or
8
. Up to rotations, there are exactly four such hypersurfaces; one each of dimension
n=3,6,12
, and
24
. These four hypersurfaces are homogeneous, and the full isometry group is
G=SO(3)
,
SU(3)
,
Sp(3)
, and
F_{4}
, respectively. The action of
G
on
\Sigma
is the restriction of a
linear
action
G\to GL(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})
. Further,
\Sigma=F^{-1}(0)\cap S^{n+1}
is the zero level set of
F|_{S^{n+1}}
and
c=0
in equation (
5
) for the Cartan-Münzner polynomial
F:\mathbb{R}^{n+2}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\Sigma^{n}\subset S^{n+1}
be a cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurface and let
G
be its full isometry group. Let
\tau
be the antipodal map. Let
\gamma\in G
and consider the function
\gamma:G\to G
. Then
\gamma
commutes with
\tau
.

Thus if
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
is even (respectively, odd) with respect to
\tau
, then
\gamma^{\#}f
is also even (respectively, odd) with respect to
\tau
, where
\gamma^{\#}f(x)=f(\gamma^{-1}(x))
.

Proof.

Since the action of
G
on
\Sigma
is the restriction of a linear action
G\to GL(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})
, we can view
\gamma
as an element of
GL(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})
. Then it is clear that
\gamma
commutes with
\tau=-\operatorname{Id}_{n+2}
as elements of
GL(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})
.
∎

Let
\Sigma^{n}\subset S^{n+1}
be a cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurface. By Lemma
4.4
, on
\Sigma
, the squared norm of the second fundamental form
|{\rm II}|^{2}
is constant and equals
2n
. The Jacobi operator on
\Sigma^{n}
is thus
\mathcal{L}=\Delta+3n
, and study of the Jacobi operator becomes study of the Laplacian. In
[
Sol90a
,
Sol90b
]
, Bruce Solomon explicitly computed the spectrum of the Laplacian of the four cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces. The functions
1
,
f_{w}
,
l_{w}
,
r_{v,w}
(as defined in Definition
3.1
) are eigenfunctions corresponding to certain low eigenvalues of the Laplacian, and Solomon showed that all the eigenfunctions can be built up from these functions. Using his result, we can compute the index of the quotients of the four cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}P^{n+1}
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
n=3,6,12,24
and let
{\widehat{\Sigma}}^{n}\subset S^{n+1}
be a cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurface. Then the quotient
\Sigma={\widehat{\Sigma}}^{n}/\{x\sim-x\}
is a one-sided minimal hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
of index
n+2
.

Proof.

By earlier discussions in this section,
\Sigma
is one-sided in
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
and
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{\Sigma})
. So we just need to show
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{\Sigma})=n+2
.
Solomon showed that the only eigenvalues of
\Delta_{\widehat{\Sigma}}
less than
3n
(i.e., the index eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{\widehat{\Sigma}}
) are precisely

0
,
n
,
2n
,
2n+2

[
Sol90a
, (2.20)]
. Of these eigenvalues,
0
has multiplicity 1 with corresponding eigenfunctions being the constant functions,
n
has multiplicity
n+2
with eigenspace
\Gamma_{1}
, and
2n
has multiplicity
n+2
with eigenspace
\Gamma_{2}
. The functions
l_{w}\in\Gamma_{1}
are odd with respect to
\tau
. Since
N
is even under the antipodal map
\tau
, the functions
f_{w}=\langle w,N\rangle\in\Gamma_{2}
are also even with respect to
\tau
. Thus
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{\Sigma})\geq\dim\Gamma_{1}=n+2
, and it only remains to show all the eigenfunctions with eigenvalue
2n+2
are even.

Solomon
[
Sol90a
, (2.20)]
showed that there exist complex-valued functions
\zeta\in\Gamma_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
and
\nu\in\Gamma_{2}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
such that

\Delta\zeta^{2}=-(2n+2)\zeta^{2}-2\nu^{2},

\Delta\nu^{2}=-(8+4n)\nu^{2},

so
\Delta(\zeta^{2}-\frac{1}{n+3}\nu^{2})=-(2n+2)(\zeta^{2}-\frac{1}{n+3}\nu^{2})

. See Section 2 of
[

]
for definition of

and
\nu
. Further, let

.
The complex eigenspace corresponding to eigenvalue

It is easy to see that the function

is even. Then by Lemma
4.5
, all functions in

are even.

Therefore the only odd eigenvalues of

less than

are
n
with multiplicity
n+2
, so
\operatorname{Ind}(\Sigma)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{\Sigma})=n+2
as desired.

∎

From the above theorem we deduce

.

The bound

and therefore in Corollary

is achieved by the cubic isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces.
