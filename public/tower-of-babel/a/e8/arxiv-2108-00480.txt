Realised Volatility Forecasting: Machine Learning via Financial Word Embedding
Eghbal Rahimikia, Stefan Zohren, Ser-Huang Poon

Abstract
This study develops a financial word embedding using 15 years of business news. Our results show that this specialised language model produces more accurate results than general word embeddings, based on a financial benchmark we established. As an application, we incorporate this word embedding into a simple machine learning model to enhance the HAR model for forecasting realised volatility. This approach statistically and economically outperforms established econometric models. Using an explainable AI method, we also identify key phrases in business news that contribute significantly to volatility, offering insights into language patterns tied to market dynamics.



Keywords:
Realised Volatility Forecasting, Machine Learning, Natural Language Processing, Language Models, Explainable AI.

We develop FinText, a financial word embedding compiled from fifteen years of business news archives. The performance of FinText is compared with two widely known general-purpose word embeddings from Google and Facebook using standard industry benchmarks. Since FinText is developed using specialist business news archives, we propose further evaluation using a new gold-standard financial benchmark introduced in this study. We demonstrate that FinText, our specialist financial word embedding, is more attuned to financial context than the general-purpose word embeddings. The financial benchmark includes 2,660 unique analogies, and results show that FinText is approximately 8 (512) times more accurate than Google Word2Vec (WikiNews) word embeddings. Despite being based on a smaller corpus, FinText outperforms widely recognised general-purpose word embeddings in this benchmark.

As an application, we introduce an ensemble model for realised volatility forecasting that combines outputs from an NLP model and a HAR model. Specifically, we compute the daily volatility forecast by averaging predictions from these two models, thereby integrating both historical realised volatility patterns and recent market news. Our analysis, conducted on 23 NASDAQ stocks from July 27, 2007, to January 27, 2022, demonstrates improved forecasting performance with this approach. These findings underscore the complementary contributions of financial metrics and news content in volatility forecasting. The results further suggest that HAR models benefit from the improved performance associated with stock-related news, particularly on high volatility days, compared to general hot news covering broad topics such as economic, financial, political, and geopolitical events. Finally, we identify a list of top phrases (n-grams) in stock-related and general hot news that can act as volatility drivers using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), an XAI method. These phrases are then manually organised into n-gram groups. For stock-related news, categories such as analyst opinions, company events, numbers, and announcements are identified as potential volatility drivers. For general hot news, person names, places, and legal entities emerge as key volatility drivers. This detailed insight into the key phrases influencing volatility is difficult to obtain with traditional dictionary-based approaches.

Word Embedding

Word embedding is one of the most significant recent developments in NLP, wherein each token
2
2

2

In NLP, a token is a sequence of characters representing a single unit of meaning, such as a word, number, or punctuation mark.
is represented by a real-valued vector. In this representation, words that are closer in the vector space are more similar in meaning. Prior to the development of word embeddings, each token in one-hot encoding was defined by a binary vector consisting of zeros, except at the index representing that token. Thus, the vectors of any two tokens in one-hot encoding are orthogonal to each other, limiting their ability to capture semantic similarity. In contrast, word embeddings provide a more meaningful representation of semantics. Specifically, a word embedding is represented by an
M\times N
matrix, where
M
is the embedding dimension (often around 300 in the literature), and
N
is the number of unique tokens in the vocabulary. Each token is represented by a vector of
M
values. Word2Vec
(
Mikolov
et al. 2013
)
and FastText
(
Bojanowski et al. 2017
)
are among the most efficient algorithms for training word embeddings.

and

provide brief overviews of these two simple and efficient algorithms.

2.1
Word2Vec

Mikolov
et al. 2013
proposed unsupervised learning models utilising skip-gram and continuous bag-of-words (CBOW) log-likelihoods for the Word2Vec algorithm, as shown below:

\displaystyle L_{skip-gram}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg[\sum_{j=-k\textbackslash 0}^{k}log\;p(w_{t+j}|w_{t})\bigg],

(1a)

\displaystyle L_{CBOW}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigg[\sum_{j=-k\textbackslash 0}^{k}log\;p(w_{t}|w_{t+j})\bigg],

(1b)

where
T
is the total number of tokens in the sequence
X=\{t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{T}\}
,
k
is the window size around the chosen token
w_{t}
, and
p(w_{t+j}|w_{t})
and
p(w_{t}|w_{t+j})
represent the probability of correct predictions in the skip-gram and CBOW models, respectively. In skip-gram, the input (middle) token is used to predict the context (surrounding tokens), whereas in CBOW, the context (surrounding) tokens are used to predict the middle token. It is generally agreed that the faster CBOW model is better suited for training larger datasets, while the skip-gram model is more efficient for training smaller datasets. Both models aim to maximise the aggregate predictive probability, as shown in
Equation

1a
and
Equation

1b
, based on a simple neural network.

For both skip-gram and CBOW models, the softmax function used to calculate the conditional probability,
p
, is defined as follows:

\displaystyle p_{skip-gram}(w_{c}|w_{t})=\frac{exp(u_{w_{c}}^{T}u_{w_{t}})}{\sum_{l=1}^{N}exp(u_{l}^{T}u_{w_{t}})},

(2a)

\displaystyle p_{CBOW}(w_{t}|w_{c})=\frac{exp(u_{w_{t}}^{T}\bar{u}_{w_{c}})}{\sum_{l=1}^{N}exp(u_{l}^{T}\bar{u}_{w_{c}})},

(2b)

where
w_{c}
and
w_{t}
are context and target tokens,
u_{w_{c}}
and
u_{w_{t}}
are the trainable vectors of
w_{c}
and
w_{t}
, and
N
is the number of tokens in the vocabulary. For the CBOW model in
Equation

2b

\bar{u}_{w_{c}}
, is used. Both models are trained using stochastic gradient descent. When
N
is large,
Equation

2a
and
Equation

2b
are computationally expensive. In this case, hierarchical softmax
(
Morin and
Bengio 2005
)
can be used instead. An alternative is the negative sampling method with binary logistic regression
(
Mikolov et al. 2013
)
.

2.2
FastText

FastText is an extension of Word2Vec, particularly useful for rare words by incorporating character-level information. Each word is represented as a bag of character n-grams in addition to the word itself. Take ‘profit’ as an example, and set
n=3
for character n-grams; the FastText representation is {<pr, pro, rof, ofi, fit, it>, profit}. The boundary symbols, <and >, are included to help preserve the meaning of shorter words that may appear as n-grams of other words, e.g., ‘fit’ vs <fit>. Inherently, FastText also allows for the capture of meaning in suffixes and prefixes. More formally, for a token
w
,
u_{w}=\sum_{i=0}^{N}u_{i}
where
u_{w}
is the vector of token
w
,
N
is the number of n-grams of this token, and
u_{i}
is the vector of each sub-token. Compared with the Word2Vec algorithm, this approach is more powerful for learning rare and out-of-vocabulary (OOV) tokens and morphologically rich languages (MRLs)
(
Bojanowski et al. 2017
)
. In contrast, Word2Vec can only generate embeddings for words that are present in the training data, making it less effective for handling OOV tokens.

3
Financial Word Embedding: FinText

At the time of writing, several well-known word embeddings are available. These include the three-million-unique-token word embedding by
Mikolov et al. 2018
, trained using the Word2Vec algorithm and the Google news dataset with approximately 100 billion words, and the one-million-unique-token word embedding by
Joulin et al. 2016
, trained using the FastText algorithm and datasets such as Wikipedia 2017, the UMBC webbase corpus, and the statmt.org news dataset. It is arguable whether these general word embeddings are suitable for specialist financial research, as certain words may have distinctly different meanings in a financial context, e.g., ‘apple’ as fruit versus ‘Apple’ as the technology company. To address this concern, we train a word embedding, called FinText, using the Dow Jones Newswires Text News Feed database, covering data from 1 January 2000 to 14 September 2015. This large, business-specialised news database is among the most comprehensive, covering diverse news agencies worldwide.
Section

3.1
details the database, the preprocessing steps, and the properties of the developed word embedding. Additionally,
Section

3.2
compares this word embedding with the general word embeddings mentioned above.

3.1
News Data, Preprocessing Steps and Model Properties

This study uses all news stories from the Dow Jones Newswires Text News Feed for the period from 1 January 2000 to 14 September 2015 to develop different word embeddings. All duplicate news stories, as well as those without headlines and bodies, are removed. Extensive pre-processing of the text is required to eliminate redundant characters, sentences, and structures.
Table

A1
provides a brief overview of the cleaning rules applied. Each rule is defined by a regular expression and may include different variations. For brevity, only one variation is shown in this table. The text cleaning procedures fall into five main categories: 1) Primary, 2) Begins with, 3) Ends with, 4) General, and 5) Final checks. The ‘Primary’ rules extract the body of news from the Extensible Markup Language (XML), remove XML-encoding characters (XMLENCOD), convert XML to text (parsing), convert uppercase to lowercase letters, and remove tables. ‘Begins with’ and ‘Ends with’ categories remove, respectively, parts that begin and end with specified structures. The ‘General’ category addresses patterns that may appear in any part of the news stories. Lastly, the ‘Final checks’ remove links, email addresses, phone numbers, short news stories (fewer than 25 characters), and leading and trailing spaces. These five sets of rules are applied separately to news headlines and bodies.

Relatedness refers to an associative connection; words may be conceptually linked even if they do not share a direct meaning. For example, ‘doctor’ and ‘hospital’ are related because they appear in similar contexts but are not synonyms. Similarity, on the other hand, requires words to have a close or nearly identical meaning. For instance, ‘doctor’ and ‘physician’ are similar, as they essentially refer to the same concept. Understanding these differences is crucial for language models, as it allows them to capture the full range of semantic relationships between words. 4.2
NLP Model Structure

Figure

2
represents an abstract representation of the NLP model. The vector
\{X_{(t,1)},X_{(t,2)},\dots,X_{(t,k_{t})}\}
denotes the vector of
k_{t}
tokens extracted from news headlines on day
t
. When
k_{t}
is fewer than 500, padding is applied to extend the vector to a length of 500, using the token ‘NONE’. This ensures that daily inputs to the neural network maintain a consistent length. We use only the news headline and not the full news body because aggregating all news bodies creates a sequence of tokens that is extremely long, even for a single day. Such a lengthy token sequence places a high demand on computational resources and may result in overfitting, particularly given the relatively small size of our training sample. Additionally, it is often considered that the headline provides the most crucial summary of the news body.

An Abstract Representation of the NLP Model

Notes:

The subsequent layer is a convolutional neural network (CNN) block. CNNs are a type of deep learning architecture primarily used for processing structured grid data, such as images, by leveraging their spatial hierarchy of patterns. Designed with layers that include convolutional operations, pooling, and fully connected layers, CNNs are particularly effective at recognising local and global features within input data through feature extraction and abstraction. This structure enables CNNs to capture spatial relationships within data, allowing them to generalise well across various tasks
(
LeCun
et al. 2015
)

.

This is a simple yet flexible and powerful framework because it connects textual data as input (independent variable) with the RV forecast as output (dependent variable). Depending on the research question, RV can be easily substituted with other target variables in empirical asset pricing. This NLP model is also free from statistical assumptions and capable of handling high degrees of nonlinearity.

Fourth, for the sake of comparability, models in this study were selected to maintain a similar structure and complexity, allowing for consistent and meaningful comparisons. Lastly, training a word embedding model from scratch is considerably faster and requires less hardware compared to more advanced language models, making it a preferred option when resources are limited.

Economic Gain

To evaluate the economic value of RV forecasting models, we apply the methodology inspired by
Li and
Tang 2024
. This approach examines the return spreads of the variance risk premium (VRP) strategy by employing various RV forecasting models. For a given stock
i
on day
t
, where
m
represents the RV forecasting model, the VRP is defined as follows:

\text{VRP}_{i,t}^{m}=\text{IV}_{i,t}-\mathbb{E}_{t}^{m}(\text{RV}_{i,t+1}),

(13)

where
{IV}_{i,t}
is the annualised daily implied variance (IV) from at-the-money call options, and
\mathbb{E}_{t}^{m}(\text{RV}_{i,t+1})
is the annualised daily expected RV from the forecasting model. OptionMetrics was employed to extract option data for the 23 tickers analysed in this study. We just consider the options that their time to expiration is one day to match it with our daily RV forecasting framework. For each trading day and each stock, we identify the top at-the-money call option, defined as the option with with a strike price equal to or closely aligned with the current market price of the underlying asset. In the out-of-sample period, there are 325 days with available IV data due to option expiration dates occurring on specific days each week. We construct an equal-weight zero-net investment portfolio based on VRP results with a one-day holding period. Specifically, for each stock and each trading day, we sort the stocks in our out-of-sample set, taking long positions in the top 10% of stocks with the highest VRP values and short positions in the bottom 10% with the lowest VRP values.

It reports the Sharpe ratio (SR) separately for long, short, and long-short portfolios, as well as the average SR across various number of filters specified for each NLP model. In the benchmark, CHAR achieves SRs of 0.5741, -0.2898, and 0.5004 for long, short, and long-short portfolios, respectively. All NLP models enhance the average SRs for these portfolio types. Explainable AI

Over recent years, considerable efforts have been made to better understand ML models, which are often described as black-box. In this paper, we explore one of the prominent XAI methods, SHAP, to analyse the explanatory power of specific phrases in RV forecasting.
Lundberg and
Lee 2017
proposed the SHAP method based on coalition game theory. Shapley values, denoted as
\phi_{i}
and defined below, reveal the importance of a model input
S
(a set of tokens in daily news headlines) given the model output
f(S)
, which in this case is the volatility forecast. Specifically:

\phi_{i}=\frac{1}{\lvert N\rvert!}\;\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}}\lvert S\rvert!\;\;(\lvert N\rvert-\lvert S\rvert-1)!\;\;[f(S\cup\{i\})-f(S)],

(14)

where
f(S\cup\{i\})-f(S)
captures the marginal contribution in volatility forecast of adding token
i
to the set
S
,
N
contains all model inputs,
\lvert S\rvert!
shows the number of different ways the chosen set of tokens may be presented, and
(\lvert N\rvert-\lvert S\rvert-1)!
is the number of different ways that the remaining tokens could have been added. The Shapley values
\phi_{i}
indicate the magnitude and sign of the average contribution of token
i
; these satisfy three properties, namely local accuracy (additivity)
35
35

35

This means that the sum of individual token attributions is equal to the forecasted RV.
, missingness (nonexistence or null effect)
36
36

36

This implies that a missing token has no attributed impact (
\phi_{i}=0
).
, and consistency (symmetry)
37
37

37

This means that if a change in a specific token has a greater impact on the first model compared to the second model, the importance of this token should be higher for the first model than for the second model.
. As tokens are added to the set, changes in the RV forecast reflect their relevance. The advantages of the SHAP method include a solid theoretical foundation in game theory and no requirement for differentiable models. However, it is computationally intensive and, like other permutation-based approaches, does not consider feature dependencies, potentially leading to misleading results. Here, we use a high-speed approximation algorithm, Deep SHAP based on DeepLIFT
(
Shrikumar
et al. 2017
)
, to calculate Shapley values.

Following
Zhao
et al. 2020
, SHAP is applied to the classification block of the model (FCNN). In this instance, the inputs are outputs from the global max pooling layer, and the output is the forecasted RV in
Figure

3
. Consequently, the number of inputs is equal to
3h
, where
h
is the number of filters, and 3 corresponds to the three filter sizes (unigram, bigram, and trigram). Applying SHAP to the full model can lead to misleading results because non-existent words may mistakenly receive high scores. However, by applying SHAP specifically to the classification block, the permutation operates over features extracted from the filters, meaning the Shapley values are attributed directly to the unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams. Moreover, the duplication of filters is common in CNNs, particularly for large
h
values
(
RoyChowdhury et al. 2017
)
, which could result in similar n-grams being generated. To prevent this, a de-duplication step is implemented by removing repeated n-grams and recalculating the Shapley value by summing the corresponding Shapley values.
38
38

38

Zhao
et al. 2020
proposed two steps for de-duplication, namely ‘exact de-duplication’ (employed in this study) and ‘merge de-duplication’. In ‘merge de-duplication’, overlapping n-grams are merged, and their Shapley value is determined by summing the Shapley values of the constituent n-grams. Given the generally shorter input text length, our experiments indicate that incorporating ‘exact de-duplication’ alone is sufficient to achieve more granular results.

For each stock, we obtained the Shapley values for the constituent n-grams of all the textual information used to forecast RV for that stock during the out-of-sample period. To identify the volatility drivers for the entire sample of 23 stocks, we first store, for each stock, the top five n-grams with the highest absolute Shapley values across the full out-of-sample forecast period. This process results in 23 groups of five n-grams, with some overlapping n-grams among them. Next, for each n-gram, we count the number of occurrences
t
, where
t=1:23
. An n-gram with
t=23
indicates that this specific n-gram appears among the top five n-grams for all 23 stocks. In the analysis of stock-related news in
Section

5.1

8
Conclusions

This study introduces a specialised financial word embedding, developed using the Word2Vec and FastText algorithms and trained on a curated corpus of business news archives from 2000 to 2015. Although this financial word embedding underperforms on general-purpose language benchmarks relative to widely used general word embeddings, it demonstrates superior sensitivity to financial terminology and nuanced relationships within the domain. According to the financial benchmark developed in this research, the model achieves a notable improvement, with approximately eight times higher accuracy than Google’s Word2Vec and 512 times higher accuracy compared to the WikiNews word embedding.

To advance our research, the next objective is to test our approach within an asset pricing framework. Volatility forecasting literature suggests that news impacts stock market volatility. Accordingly, we developed a straightforward NLP model that integrates various word embeddings to generate RV forecasts based on news stories. These forecasts are then combined with a traditional HAR model for RV forecasting, allowing us to leverage both news information and historical financial data simultaneously. Analysing data from 23 NASDAQ stocks over the period from July 27, 2007, to January 27, 2022, alongside stock-related news, we observed that this ensemble model enhances RV forecasting performance. Notably, our specialised word embedding model outperforms general word embeddings, despite being derived from a smaller, domain-specific corpus. This result underscores the importance of the quality of domain-specific corpus over mere corpus size, highlighting the relevance of tailored word embeddings—and, by extension, large language models—in financial research. The results also indicate that HAR models benefit significantly from the enhanced performance provided by stock-specific news, especially on high volatility days. When the model input is switched to general hot news—encompassing economic, financial, political, and geopolitical events—these inputs are more effective in enhancing forecasting power during periods of normal volatility. Therefore, HAR models do not benefit as much from such news inputs compared to stock-related news.

We additionally evaluated the economic gains generated by the models and found that the statistical forecasting results align with those obtained from economic gain assessments. A comparative analysis with results from the LM dictionary approach further demonstrated that the proposed models yield superior outcomes, both statistically and economically. Finally, we identified key phrases in stock-related news and general hot news that influence volatility, using an XAI method. In stock-related news, volatility is driven by analyst opinions, company events, numbers, and announcements, whereas in general news, person names, places, and legal entities are primary volatility drivers. Lastly, robustness tests indicate that neither increasing model complexity nor extending the historical news input period improves forecasting performance.

Further studies could enhance the results by decoupling numerical data, such as the historical record of RV, within the same model, allowing for the integration of multiple data sources. Additionally, future research could explore incorporating this information to improve models such as those proposed in
Bollerslev et al. 2018
and
Patton and
Zhang 2022
. Another avenue for future work is to broaden the application of these specialised models to other areas, including return forecasting, credit risk assessment, and sentiment analysis. Finally, creating universal RV forecasting models that incorporate news stories to generate a single model for forecasting RV across the entire universe of stocks represents another promising direction.
