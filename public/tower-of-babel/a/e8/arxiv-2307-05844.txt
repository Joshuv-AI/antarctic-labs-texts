Self-consistent interaction of linear gravitational and electromagnetic waves in non-magnetized plasma
Deepen Garg, I. Y. Dodin

Abstract
This paper explores the hybridization of linear metric perturbations with linear electromagnetic (EM) perturbations in non-magnetized plasma for a general background metric. The local wave properties are derived from first principles for inhomogeneous plasma, without assuming any symmetries of the background metric. First, we derive the effective (“oscillation-center”) Hamiltonian that governs the average dynamics of plasma particles in a prescribed quasimonochromatic wave that involves metric perturbations and EM fields simultaneously. Then, using this Hamiltonian, we derive the backreaction of plasma particles on the wave itself and obtain gauge-invariant equations that describe the resulting self-consistent gravito-electromagnetic (GEM) waves in a plasma. The transverse tensor modes of gravitational waves are found to have no interaction with the plasma and the EM modes in the geometrical-optics limit. However, for longitudinal GEM modes with large values of the refraction index, the interplay between gravitational and EM interactions in plasma can have a strong effect. In particular, the dispersion relation of the Jeans mode is significantly affected by electrostatic interactions. As a spin-off, our calculation also provides an alternative resolution of the so-called Jeans swindle.

\displaystyle S_{\rm m}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}}{p^{0}}\,f_{s}(x,\boldsymbol{p})

\displaystyle\times\bigg\{h_{\alpha\beta}h_{\gamma\delta}\left[g^{\beta\gamma}p^{\alpha}p^{\delta}-\frac{k_{\mu}}{4}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{\mu}}\left(\frac{p^{\alpha}p^{\beta}p^{\gamma}p^{\delta}}{k_{\lambda}p^{\lambda}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\hphantom{\times\bigg\lx@text@lbrace}+2e_{s}a_{\alpha}h_{\beta\gamma}\left[g^{\alpha\gamma}p^{\beta}-\frac{k_{\mu}}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{\mu}}\left(\frac{p^{\alpha}p^{\beta}p^{\gamma}}{k_{\lambda}p^{\lambda}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\hphantom{\times\bigg\lx@text@lbrace}+e_{s}^{2}a_{\alpha}a_{\beta}\left[g^{\alpha\beta}-k_{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{\mu}}\bigg(\frac{p^{\alpha}p^{\beta}}{k_{\lambda}p^{\lambda}}\bigg)\right]\bigg\},

(36)

where the space averaging on the right-hand side is dropped as it has no impact on the integral within the GO limit. Also note that the first term in the third line above (

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}}{p^{0}}\,f_{s}(x,\boldsymbol{p})\,e_{s}\,p^{\alpha}=0.

(37)

III.3
Dispersion functions

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-Q_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\bigg],

(42)

(43)

(Note that

Also, as a side remark, note that within the model considered here, EM and gravitational perturbations couple only via plasma and vanish in the limit when the plasma density is negligible. This is because the direct contribution of the photons to the spacetime curvature is assumed negligible compared to that of massive plasma particles. This means that the coupling considered here is different from the known direct photon–graviton conversion in the presence of magnetic fields

IV
Equation for gravito-electromagnetic waves

IV.1
Assumptions

In what follows, we use normal coordinates, in which the first-order derivatives of the background metric vanish. Then,

\displaystyle g_{\alpha\beta}=\eta_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\,(\partial_{\sigma}\partial_{\rho}g_{\alpha\beta})\,x^{\rho}x^{\sigma}+\mathcal{O}(\ell_{g}^{-3}),

(46)

and metric’s second-order derivatives can be expressed through the Riemann tensor
\smash{R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}}
as
Brewin 1998

\displaystyle\partial_{\sigma}\partial_{\rho}g_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{1}{3}\left(R_{\alpha\rho\beta\sigma}+R_{\alpha\sigma\beta\rho}\right).

(47)

As discussed in
(
Garg and Dodin 2022a
)
, the interaction of GWs with matter (in our case, plasma) is significant only when the Weyl tensor is not the dominant contributor to the curvature and
R_{\alpha\beta}
, which is
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
by Einstein field equations, is of the same order as
R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
, which is
\ell_{g}^{-2}
. Thus, below we assume that

\displaystyle\mathbb{h}\ll\epsilon\sim\ell_{h}/\ell_{g}\sim\ell_{h}\sqrt{\rho}\ll 1,

(48)

where
\rho
is the total mass density. Note that, in typical plasmas, the ions are the dominant contributors to the mass density. That is,

\displaystyle\rho\simeq\rho_{i}\ll\rho_{e},

(49)

where the index
i
denotes ions, the index
e
denotes electrons, and
m_{i}\gg m_{e}
. (For simplicity, we assume that, in addition to electrons, plasma contains ions of only one type.) Our results are also applicable at smaller densities, but then wave’s gravitational coupling with matter is beyond the accuracy of our approximation and thus will be neglected.

Another small parameter that we will use is
m_{s}/e_{s}
. In typical plasmas, the values of
\smash{m_{s}/e_{s}}
for various species are very small compared to unity. For example, electrons have
m_{e}/e_{e}\sim 10^{-21}
, and protons (denoted with index
p
) have
m_{p}/e_{p}\sim 10^{-18}
. We will also assume

\displaystyle\frac{m_{s}^{2}}{e_{s}^{2}}\ll\epsilon

(50)

for all species. Like in the case with
Eq.

48
, our results are also applicable at arbitrarily small
\smash{\epsilon}
, which corresponds to vanishingly small densities, but then wave’s gravitational coupling with matter must be neglected.

IV.2
Euler–Lagrange equations for the EM field

The first set of GEM equations is obtained by considering the variation of the action (
14
) with respect to the EM four-potential:

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\delta S^{(2)}}{\delta a_{\alpha}}=\frac{\delta S^{(2)}_{\rm EM}}{\delta a_{\alpha}}+\frac{\delta S^{(2)}_{\rm m}}{\delta a_{\alpha}}.

(51)

The first term on the right-hand side is well known
(
Landau and Lifshitz 1971
, Sec. 30)
:

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\alpha\beta}}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta S^{(2)}_{\rm EM}}{\delta a_{\beta}}=-{a_{\beta,\alpha}}^{\beta}+{a_{\alpha,\beta}}^{\beta}.

(52)

Using
Eq.

25
, this can also be expressed as

\frac{g_{\alpha\beta}}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta S^{(2)}_{\rm EM}}{\delta a_{\beta}}=k_{\alpha}k^{\beta}a_{\beta}-g^{\beta\gamma}\mathfrak{R}^{\rm EM}_{\beta\alpha\gamma}\\
-k^{2}a_{\alpha}+g^{\beta\gamma}\mathfrak{R}^{\rm EM}_{\alpha\beta\gamma},

(53)

where we have introduced

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}^{\rm EM}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\doteq\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}(\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,\beta\gamma}+\mathrm{i}k_{\gamma}\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,\beta}+\mathrm{i}k_{\beta}\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,\gamma}+\mathrm{i}k_{\gamma,\beta}\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha}).

(54)

Note that
\smash{\mathfrak{R}^{\rm EM}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\sim\epsilon}
, while

\displaystyle D^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\beta}\sim e^{2}\rho_{e}/m_{e}^{2}\sim e^{2}\epsilon^{2}/m_{e}m_{i},

(55)

where
e=|e_{e}|=-e_{e}
is the elementary charge, and we used
\rho_{e}/\rho_{i}\sim m_{e}/m_{i}
due to the background-plasma neutrality. Then, under the assumption (
50
),
\smash{\mathfrak{R}^{\rm EM}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}
can be ignored compared with
\smash{D^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\beta}}
. This leads to

\displaystyle\frac{g_{\alpha\beta}}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta S^{(2)}_{\rm EM}}{\delta a_{\beta}}\simeq k_{\alpha}k^{\beta}a_{\beta}-k^{2}a_{\alpha}.

(56)

In conjunction with Eqs. (
40
) or (
44
) for
S^{(2)}_{\rm m}
,
Eq.

56
can be used to rewrite
Eq.

51
as

\displaystyle k_{\alpha}k^{\beta}a_{\beta}-k^{2}a_{\alpha}+D^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\beta}a^{\beta}+D^{\rm mGEM}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}h^{\beta\gamma}=0.

(57)

Equation

57
determines the GO dispersion relation and the local polarization of GEM waves. (For this, it must be combined with the equation for
\smash{h^{\beta\gamma}}
, which is derived in
Section

IV.3
.) In the absence of gravitational interactions,
Eq.

57
coincides with the well-known equation for dispersive waves in relativistic nonmagnetized plasma, which is usually expressed through the corresponding dielectric tensor; see, e.g., Eq. (9.42) in
(
Dodin 2022
)
.

Note that, although approximate,
Eq.

57
honors symmetries of the exact equations that describe the whole system. Indeed, as one can check by direct calculation [with
Eq.

37
taken into account], the dispersion functions satisfy

\displaystyle k^{\alpha}D^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\beta}=0,

(58a)

\displaystyle k^{\alpha}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=0,

(58b)

\displaystyle k^{\beta}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=0.

(58c)

From here, one can see that
Eq.

57
is invariant with respect to the EM gauge transformations

\displaystyle a_{\alpha}\to a_{\alpha}+k_{\alpha}\varphi,

(59)

where
\varphi
is any scalar function.
Equation

58c
also ensures invariance of
Eq.

57
with respect to the gauge transformation of the metric perturbation,

\displaystyle h_{\alpha\beta}\to h^{\prime}_{\alpha\beta}=h_{\alpha\beta}-\mathrm{\text{\pounds}}_{\xi}g_{\alpha\beta},

(60)

where
\mathrm{\text{\pounds}}_{\xi}g_{\alpha\beta}=2\Lambda_{(\alpha}k_{\beta)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
is the Lie derivative of the background metric with respect to an arbitrary vector wave field
\xi^{\alpha}=\text{Re}\,(-\mathrm{i}\Lambda^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})=\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{h})
in the GO limit.

Equations (
58
) can be considered as a test for the accuracy of approximate models of the GW dispersion in plasma, like in
(
Garg and Dodin 2022a
, Eq. (4.20))
. As shown in
(
Garg and Dodin 2022a
)
,
certain terms in the dispersion operator may not pass this test if derived under the GO approximation and, thus, contain spurious coordinate effects that should be dealt with.

IV.3
Euler–Lagrange equations for the metric perturbation

The other set of GEM equations can be obtained by considering the variation of the action (
14
) with respect to the metric perturbation:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta S^{(2)}}{\delta h^{\alpha\beta}}=0.

(61)

These equations can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{D}^{\rm vac}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}+\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}+D^{\rm mG}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\right)h^{\gamma\delta}+a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\alpha\beta}=0,

(62)

with the operators
\widehat{D}^{\rm vac}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
defined as
Garg and Dodin 2022a

\widehat{D}^{\rm vac}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}h^{\gamma\delta}\doteq\frac{1}{4}\big(\partial^{\rho}\partial_{\rho}h_{\alpha\beta}-g_{\alpha\beta}g^{\rho\sigma}\partial^{\lambda}\partial_{\lambda}h_{\rho\sigma}+g^{\rho\sigma}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}h_{\rho\sigma}\\
+g_{\alpha\beta}\partial^{\rho}\partial^{\sigma}h_{\rho\sigma}-\partial^{\rho}\partial_{\alpha}h_{\beta\rho}-\partial^{\rho}\partial_{\beta}h_{\alpha\rho}\big),

(63)

\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}h^{\gamma\delta}\doteq\frac{1}{4}\big(-Gh_{\alpha\beta}-G_{\alpha\beta}h+2G_{\alpha\rho}{h_{\beta}}^{\rho}\\
+2G_{\rho\beta}{h^{\rho}}_{\alpha}-2g_{\alpha\beta}R_{\rho\sigma}h^{\rho\sigma}+2R_{\rho\alpha\sigma\beta}h^{\rho\sigma}\big).

(64)

For quasimonochromatic waves [
Eq.

25
],
Eq.

62
can also be written as

\displaystyle 4D^{(0)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}h^{\gamma\delta}+M_{\alpha\beta}+4a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\alpha\beta}=0.

(65)

Here,
D^{(0)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
is defined as

D^{(0)}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\doteq\frac{1}{4}\,\big(-k^{2}g_{\alpha\gamma}g_{\beta\delta}+g_{\alpha\beta}g_{\gamma\delta}k^{2}-k_{\alpha}k_{\beta}g_{\gamma\delta}\\
-g_{\alpha\beta}k_{\gamma}k_{\delta}+k_{\alpha}k_{\gamma}g_{\beta\delta}+k_{\beta}k_{\delta}g_{\alpha\gamma}\big),

(66)

M_{\alpha\beta}
describes wave’s gravitational coupling to the matter,

\displaystyle M_{\alpha\beta}\doteq

\displaystyle\,\,{\mathfrak{R}_{\alpha\beta\rho}}^{\rho}-g_{\alpha\beta}g^{\rho\sigma}{\mathfrak{R}_{\rho\sigma\lambda}}^{\lambda}+g^{\rho\sigma}\mathfrak{R}_{\rho\sigma\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle+g_{\alpha\beta}{\mathfrak{R}_{\rho\sigma}}^{\rho\sigma}-{\mathfrak{R}_{\rho\beta\alpha}}^{\rho}-{\mathfrak{R}_{\rho\alpha\beta}}^{\rho}+2h^{\rho\sigma}C_{\rho\alpha\sigma\beta}

\displaystyle-g_{\alpha\beta}G_{\rho\sigma}h^{\rho\sigma}+{h_{\beta}}^{\rho}G_{\alpha\rho}+{h^{\rho}}_{\alpha}G_{\rho\beta}

\displaystyle-(h_{\alpha\beta}-hg_{\alpha\beta})G/3+4D^{\rm mG}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}h^{\gamma\delta},

(67)

and
\mathfrak{R}_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}\doteq\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}(\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\mu}\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha\beta}+2\mathrm{i}k_{(\mu}\partial_{\nu)}\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha\beta}+\mathrm{i}\mathfrak{h}_{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\mu}k_{\nu})
.

Note that
\smash{D^{\text{mG}}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}}
and
\smash{D^{\text{mGEM}}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}}
do not have any particular symmetries in the general case. Hence, the symmetry considerations that are commonly used to study waves in vacuum are not necessarily applicable in the presence of plasma. This means that, to find the wave polarization, in the general case one actually has to solve
Eq.

65
. In particular, this requires calculating
\smash{M_{\alpha\beta}}
, which can also be written as follows:

\displaystyle M_{\alpha\beta}=4D^{\text{mG}}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}h^{\gamma\delta}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\mathbb{h}).

(68)

The term
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon\mathbb{h})
, which contains the derivatives of the amplitudes, is not necessarily small compared with
D^{\text{mG}}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}h^{\gamma\delta}
, which is
\mathcal{O}(\rho\mathbb{h})
in the general case (
48
). Hence, one must either give up the GO approximation and consider the amplitude derivatives along with
D^{\text{mG}}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}h^{\gamma\delta}
, or neglect the gravitational coupling to the matter,
M_{\alpha\beta}
, altogether. However, as already noted in
(
Garg and Dodin 2022a
)
,
M_{\alpha\beta}
can be retained in special cases where additional large parameters are present. In particular, one such case is the quasistatic limit, where waves have a large refraction index. This limit is discussed in detail in
Section

V
.

Like
Eq.

57
, equation (
65
) is invariant with respect to EM gauge transformations (
59
), as one can verify by a straightforward calculation using
Eq.

58b
. To address invariance with respect to gravitational gauge transformations (
60
), one can use the corresponding argument from
(
Garg and Dodin 2022a
)
and adapt it to the case when EM interactions are present. Specifically, let us decompose
Eq.

65
into the longitudinal part (cf. Eq. (4.20) from
(
Garg and Dodin 2022a
)
)

\displaystyle k^{\alpha}M_{\alpha\beta}+4k^{\alpha}a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\alpha\beta}=0

(69)

and the transverse part (cf. Eq. (4.21) from
(
Garg and Dodin 2022a
)
)

\Pi^{\gamma\rho}\Pi^{\delta\sigma}\big(k^{2}h_{\rho\sigma}-\bar{M}_{\rho\sigma}\\
-4a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\rho\sigma}+2g_{\rho\sigma}a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\alpha\beta}g^{\alpha\beta}\big)=0,

(70)

where
\smash{\Pi^{\alpha\beta}\doteq g^{\alpha\beta}-k^{\alpha}k^{\beta}/k^{2}}
is a projection tensor,
5
5

5

Here, we assume
k^{2}\neq 0
, which is a valid assumption in the presence of plasma with nonzero density. The vacuum case can be considered within this approach as the limit in which the plasma density is nonzero but vanishingly small.
and the overbar represents the trace-reverse of the corresponding rank-2 tensor. According to
Eq.

70
, one has

k^{2}h_{\rho\sigma}-\bar{M}_{\rho\sigma}-4a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\rho\sigma}+2g_{\rho\sigma}a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\alpha\beta}g^{\alpha\beta}\\
=\lambda_{\rho}k_{\sigma}+k_{\rho}\lambda_{\sigma},

(71)

where
\smash{\lambda_{\alpha}}
is some vector field. Comparing the trace-reverse of
Eq.

65

-k^{2}h_{\alpha\beta}+k_{\alpha}k_{\gamma}{\bar{h}_{\beta}}^{\gamma}+k_{\beta}k_{\gamma}{\bar{h}_{\alpha}}^{\gamma}+\bar{M}_{\alpha\beta}\\
+4a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\alpha\beta}-2g_{\alpha\beta}a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\gamma\delta}g^{\gamma\delta}=0

(72)

one immediately finds from
Eq.

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha}=k^{\beta}\bar{h}_{\alpha\beta},

(73)

which are the degrees of freedom always afforded by the metric gauge invariance

Schutz 2009
, Sec. 8.3)
. Thus, up to gauge freedom, the transverse part encodes all the physical information required to determine the solution for the metric perturbation. Then,
Eq.

69
can be used as a test of whether the model for the dispersion function
D^{\rm mG}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
preserves gauge invariance. Using
Eq.

58c

\displaystyle k^{\alpha}M_{\alpha\beta}=0,

(74)

which coincides with the corresponding equation for neutral matter in
(
Garg and Dodin 2022a
)

(
Garg and Dodin 2022a
)

\displaystyle M_{\rho\sigma}[2\Lambda_{(\alpha}k_{\beta)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}]=0,

(75)

where the square brackets denote the argument on which

6

6

and

Then, it is readily seen that

that are negligible within the assumed approximation. This makes Eqs. (
76
) a satisfactory model that retains gauge invariance both with respect to the EM gauge as well as the coordinate gauge. The modes that satisfy this approximation can be called
gravito-electrostatic
by analogy with electrostatic waves such as Langmuir mode and the gravitostatic waves
Garg and Dodin 2022a
such as the Jeans mode. Similarly, Eqs. (
44
) for the dispersion functions can be approximated as

\displaystyle D_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}^{\rm mG}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}}{4(p^{0})^{2}}\frac{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}f_{s}}{\omega-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathcal{T}_{\alpha\beta}\mathcal{T}_{\gamma\delta},

(78a)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}^{\rm mGEM}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}}{2(p^{0})^{2}}\,e_{s}\frac{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}f_{s}}{\omega-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathcal{T}_{\alpha\beta}p_{\gamma},

(78b)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha\beta}^{\rm mEM}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}}{(p^{0})^{2}}\,e^{2}_{s}\frac{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{p}}f_{s}}{\omega-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathcal{T}_{\alpha\beta}.

(78c)

These are equivalent to Eqs. (
76
) up to subdominant terms
\mathcal{O}(N^{0})
, as can be seen by comparing
Eqs.

40
and
44
.

V.2
Newtonian limit

Now, let us consider the Newtonian limit,
7
7

7

Some effects of Newtonian gravity on plasma dynamics have been addressed in the literature; see, for example,
Coppi 2023
;
Pandey et al. 1999
;
Lundin et al. 2008
;
Prajapati et al. 2012
.
in which
N\to\infty
and the background energy–momentum tensor is nonrelativistic,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{T}_{\alpha\beta}}{(p^{0})^{2}}\simeq\delta^{0}_{\alpha}\delta^{0}_{\beta}.

(79)

(This approximation does not exclude all thermal effects; see below.) Pursuing an approach similar to the one used in
(
Garg and Dodin 2022a
)
, let us change the normalization of the distribution functions
f_{s}(x,\boldsymbol{p})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}\to N_{s}(x)\,f_{s}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}
, as common in nonrelativistic plasma theory
Stix 1992
, with
N_{s}=\rho_{s}/m_{s}
. Then, Eqs. (
78
) can be written as

\displaystyle D_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}^{\rm mG}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{s}\mathcal{X}_{s}\delta^{0}_{\alpha}\delta^{0}_{\beta}\delta^{0}_{\gamma}\delta^{0}_{\delta},

(80a)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}^{\rm mGEM}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s}\frac{e_{s}}{m_{s}}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}\delta^{0}_{\alpha}\delta^{0}_{\beta}\delta^{0}_{\gamma},

(80b)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha\beta}^{\rm mEM}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\frac{e_{s}^{2}}{m_{s}^{2}}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}\delta^{0}_{\alpha}\delta^{0}_{\beta},

(80c)

where we introduced

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{s}\doteq\rho_{s}(x)\int_{\mathcal{L}}\frac{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}f_{s}(\boldsymbol{v})}{\omega-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}.

(81)

Then,
Eq.

77
can be used to write

\displaystyle M_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{s}\mathcal{X}_{s}\,\delta^{0}_{\alpha}\delta^{0}_{\beta}h^{00},

(82a)

whence

\displaystyle\bar{M}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\mathcal{X}_{s}h^{00}\left(\delta^{0}_{\alpha}\delta^{0}_{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\,\eta_{\alpha\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}h^{00}I_{\alpha\beta},

(82b)

where
I_{\alpha\beta}
is a unit matrix. Using the EM and gravitational gauge invariance, let us adopt the Lorenz gauge both for the EM potential and the metric perturbation, i.e.,

\displaystyle k^{\alpha}a_{\alpha}=0,\qquad k^{\beta}\bar{h}_{\alpha\beta}=0.

(83)

Then,
Eqs.

57
and
72
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle-k^{2}a_{\alpha}+\sum_{s}\left(\frac{e_{s}^{2}}{m_{s}^{2}}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}\ a^{0}-\frac{e_{s}}{2m_{s}}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}h^{00}\right)\delta^{0}_{\alpha}=0,

(84a)

\displaystyle-k^{2}h_{\alpha\beta}+\sum_{s}\left(\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}h^{00}-\frac{e_{s}}{m_{s}}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}\ a^{0}\right)I_{\alpha\beta}=0.

(84b)

It is seen from here that gravito-electrostatic modes have the longitudinal polarization similar to that found for the Langmuir mode and the Jeans mode, i.e.,

\displaystyle h_{\alpha\beta}=I_{\alpha\beta}\mathfrak{h},\qquad a_{\alpha}=\delta^{0}_{\alpha}\mathfrak{a},

(85)

where
\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathfrak{a}
denote the scalar amplitudes of the respective wave fields. Substituting this into
Eq.

84
readily yields

\displaystyle k^{2}\mathfrak{a}+\sum_{s}\frac{e_{s}}{m_{s}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}\mathfrak{h}+\frac{e_{s}}{m_{s}}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}\mathfrak{a}\right)=0,

(86)

\displaystyle-k^{2}\mathfrak{h}+\sum_{s}\left(\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}\mathfrak{h}+\frac{e_{s}}{m_{s}}\,\mathcal{X}_{s}\mathfrak{a}\right)=0,

(87)

so the wave polarization is found to be

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\mathfrak{h}}=\frac{k^{2}-W_{0}/2}{W_{1}},

(88)

where we have introduced the notation

\displaystyle W_{n}\doteq\sum_{s}\left(\frac{e_{s}}{m_{s}}\right)^{n}\mathcal{X}_{s}.

(89)

The corresponding dispersion relation is given by

\displaystyle k^{2}=\frac{W_{0}}{4}-\frac{W_{2}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{W_{0}}{4}+\frac{W_{2}}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{W_{1}^{2}}{2}}.

(90)

Below, we explore this result in several limits.

V.3
Cold plasma limit

In the cold-plasma limit, where all particle velocities satisfy
v\ll\omega/\mathsf{k}
and can be neglected completely, one has

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{L}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}\,\frac{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}f(\boldsymbol{v})}{\omega-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle\simeq\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}\left(1+\frac{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}{\omega}\right)\frac{\boldsymbol{k}}{\omega}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}f(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}\,\left(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\right)\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}f(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}\,f(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}.

(91)

This readily yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{s}=-\rho_{s}\,\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}}{\omega^{2}},

(92)

Due to the assumed background neutrality
\smash{\sum_{s}\left(e_{s}\rho_{s}/m_{s}\right)=0}
[
Eq.

37
], this gives
W_{1}=0
. Remember also that
k^{2}\simeq\mathsf{k}^{2}
, since
N\gg 1
is assumed. Then, it is readily seen that
Eq.

90
predicts the following two modes. One of them satisfies

\displaystyle k^{2}=-W_{2},

(93)

whence
\omega^{2}=\omega_{\rm p}^{2}
, where
\omega_{\rm p}
is the plasma frequency:

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm p}^{2}\doteq\sum_{s}\frac{e^{2}_{s}}{m^{2}_{s}}\,\rho_{s}.

(94)

The corresponding polarization is
(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{a})=(0,1)\times\text{const}
. This is the familiar Langmuir mode
Stix 1992
. The other mode predicted by
Eq.

90
satisfies

\displaystyle k^{2}=\frac{W_{0}}{2}.

(95)

This leads to
\omega^{2}=-\omega_{\rm J}^{2}
, where
\omega_{\rm J}
is the Jeans frequency:

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm J}^{2}\doteq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s}\rho_{s}.

(96)

The corresponding polarization is
(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{a})=(1,0)\times\text{const}
. This is the familiar Jeans mode
Trigger et al. 2004
;
Lima et al. 2002
;
Ershkovich and Israelevich 2008
. Notice that

\displaystyle\omega^{2}_{\rm p}\simeq e^{2}\rho_{e}/m_{e}^{2},\qquad\omega^{2}_{\rm J}\simeq\rho_{i}/2,

(97)

so
\smash{\omega^{2}_{\rm J}/\omega^{2}_{\rm p}\ll 1}
by
Eq.

50
.

As a side remark, note that our derivation of the Jeans mode also presents an alternative resolution of the “Jeans swindle”, the problem that the traditional derivation of the Jeans mode arbitrarily ignores the background gravitational field, which is necessarily present in the presence of matter
Binney and Tremaine 2008
;
Ershkovich 2011
. The proposed resolutions to the Jeans swindle are to assume infinite homogeneous background
Ershkovich 2011
or to cancel out the background effect by the Hubble expansion
Falco et al. 2013
. In contrast, our derivation arrives at the local Jeans dispersion relation using formal asymptotic analysis of the linear GWs even for a Minkowski background with an inhomogeneous mass distribution.

Also note that the absence of coupling between the electrostatic and gravitational modes in the cold-plasma limit is a general statement that is valid also beyond the quasistatic approximation. This is seen from the fact that this interaction is determined by the “cross term”
\smash{\langle a^{\alpha},D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}^{\rm mGEM}h^{\beta\gamma}\rangle}
in the action (
39
). As easily seen from
Eq.

40b
, the dispersion function
D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}^{\rm mGEM}
vanishes in the cold limit due to neutrality of the background plasma. Thus, in the cold limit, gravitational modes and EM modes cannot interact (within the model assumed in this paper) irrespective of the value of
k^{\alpha}
. This can be considered as an extension of the results obtained in
(
Flauger and Weinberg 2018
)
, where no dispersion of the tensor modes was observed for their propagation through cold neutral matter.

V.4
Warm plasma

In a warm plasma, when the characteristic speeds of particles are small compared to
\omega/\mathsf{k}
but not entirely negligible, the coupling term
W_{1}
is small but nonzero and can be treated perturbatively. In this case, the square root in
Eq.

90
can be Taylor-expanded in
W_{1}
. This results in small modifications of the Jeans mode and the Langmuir mode. For the modified Jeans mode, one obtains

\displaystyle k^{2}=-\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\,\omega_{\rm J}^{2}-\frac{W_{1}^{2}}{2W_{2}},

(98a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\mathfrak{h}}=-\frac{W_{1}}{2W_{2}},

(98b)

where we used
Eq.

96
, and the modified Langmuir mode satisfies

\displaystyle k^{2}=\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\,\omega_{\rm p}^{2}+\frac{W_{1}^{2}}{2W_{2}},

(99a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\mathfrak{h}}=-\frac{W_{2}}{W_{1}}+\frac{W_{1}}{2W_{2}},

(99b)

where we used
Eq.

94
and
W_{0}\ll W_{2}
.

For example, let us consider a specific example of a warm Maxwellian plasma consisting of electrons with temperature
T_{e}=m_{e}v_{Te}^{2}
and ions with comparable (or smaller) temperature
T_{i}=m_{i}v_{Ti}^{2}
. Due to the smallness of the ratio
m_{e}/m_{i}
and the assumed condition
v_{s}\ll\omega/\mathsf{k}
for all
s
, one has
v_{Ti}\ll v_{Te}\ll\omega/\mathsf{k}
. This renders the ion temperature negligible, while the electron temperature can be treated as a small but nonvanishing correction to the case considered in
Section

V.3
. Then, one obtains
Stix 1992

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{e}\simeq-\rho_{e}\,\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\left(1+\frac{3\mathsf{k}^{2}v_{Te}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right),

(100)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{i}\simeq-\rho_{i}\,\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}}{\omega^{2}},

(101)

whence

\displaystyle W_{1}\simeq 3\,eN_{e}\,\frac{\mathsf{k}^{4}v_{Te}^{2}}{\omega^{4}}.

(102)

Using this in
Eqs.

98a
and
99a
immediately leads to the dispersion relation,
\omega^{2}=-\omega^{2}_{\rm J}+\Delta^{2}
for the modified Jeans mode, and
\omega^{2}=\omega^{2}_{\rm p}-\Delta^{2}
for the modified Langmuir mode respectively, with

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\omega_{\rm J}^{2}}\simeq\frac{9m_{e}}{m_{i}}\,\frac{\mathsf{k}^{4}v_{Te}^{4}}{\omega^{4}},\quad\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\omega_{\rm p}^{2}}\simeq\frac{9m_{e}^{2}}{2e^{2}}\,\frac{\mathsf{k}^{4}v_{Te}^{4}}{\omega^{4}},

(103)

where we have retained only the leading-order terms and neglected corrections
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{k}^{2}v_{s}^{2}/\omega^{2})
. The relative strength of the correction for the Jeans mode is very small, which is in line with the cold plasma limit of no interaction at all. Similarly, the relative strength of the modification of the Langmuir mode due to gravitational effects is further suppressed by an additional factor of
m_{i}m_{e}/e^{2}\ll 1
, due to the gravitational forces being weaker compared to the EM forces.

V.5
Plasma with cold ions and hot electrons

A more significant interaction between the Jeans mode and plasma electrostatic modes can be found at low frequencies that satisfy

\displaystyle v_{Ti}\ll\omega/\mathsf{k}\ll v_{Te}.

(104)

In this regime,
Eq.

81
can be approximated as
(
Stix 1992
, Sec. 8-13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{e}=\frac{\rho_{e}}{v_{Te}^{2}},\qquad\mathcal{X}_{i}=-\frac{\rho_{i}\mathsf{k}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}.

(105)

In the absence of gravitational interactions, such a plasma supports ion acoustic oscillations

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\simeq\frac{k^{2}c_{\rm s}^{2}}{1+k^{2}\lambda_{De}^{2}}\to k^{2}c_{\rm s}^{2}.

(106)

Here,
\smash{\lambda_{De}\doteq v_{Te}/\omega_{\rm p}}
is the electron Debye length,
c_{\rm s}\doteq(ZT_{e}/m_{i})^{1/2}
is the ion sound speed (assuming
e_{i}=Ze
), and the second part of
Eq.

106
corresponds to the limit
\smash{k^{2}\lambda_{De}^{2}\ll 1}
, where the dispersion relation becomes particularly simple (sound-like). Although electric charge
e
does not enter
Eq.

106
explicitly, the electrostatic interactions are important in that they tie cold ions, which provide inertia, to hot electrons, which carry pressure.

Now, let us reinstate gravitational interactions, assuming
\mathsf{k}^{2}c_{\rm s}^{2}\sim\omega^{2}_{\rm J}
. In this case, the square root in the dispersion relation (
90
) can still be Taylor-expanded in
W_{1}
as per
Eqs.

98
and
99
, since

\displaystyle\frac{W_{1}}{W_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(-1-\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}v_{Te}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{e}{m_{e}}-\frac{Ze\mathsf{k}^{2}v_{Te}^{2}}{m_{i}\omega^{2}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{m_{i}}{e}\left(Z-\frac{m_{i}/m_{e}}{\mathsf{k}^{2}v_{Te}^{2}/\omega^{2}}\right)^{-1}

(107)

is small, as can be verified a posteriori [using
Eq.

109
]. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{W_{1}^{2}}{W_{2}}

\displaystyle\simeq-\rho_{e}\,\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\left(-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\mathsf{k}^{2}v_{Te}^{2}}+\frac{Zm_{e}}{m_{i}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\rho_{i}\,\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}c_{\rm s}^{2}}{\omega^{2}-\mathsf{k}^{2}c_{\rm s}^{2}}\right),

(108)

so
Eq.

98a
leads to the following dispersion relation:

\displaystyle\omega^{2}=-\omega_{\rm J}^{2}+\mathsf{k}^{2}c_{\rm s}^{2},

(109)

where we have again used the approximation
\omega_{\rm J}^{2}\simeq\rho_{i}/2
. One can understand this as the Jeans mode hybridized with the ion-acoustic branch. (For comparison: in a neutral gas, the ion sound speed in the Jeans mode’s dispersion relation is replaced by the particle thermal speed
Garg and Dodin 2022a
.)

One can similarly calculate the correction to the ion acoustic waves that result from their hybridization with the Jeans mode. It is easy to see that this correction is of order
\sim m_{e}m_{i}/e^{2}
. Thus, the effect of gravitational interactions on electrostatic waves is negligible, which is again due to the relative weakness of the gravitational forces compared with the EM forces.

VI
Transverse waves

Now let us consider transverse waves. To the extent that gravitational effects can be neglected, transverse EM waves in nonrelativistic plasma satisfy the well-known GO dispersion relation

\displaystyle k^{2}=-\omega^{2}_{\rm p},

(110)

and GWs satisfy the GO dispersion relation

\displaystyle k^{2}=0,

(111)

because corrections due to the interaction with the matter are beyond the GO approximation
Garg and Dodin 2022a
. Let us consider the interaction of these waves perturbatively.

Assuming the Lorenz gauge (
83
) again,
Eqs.

57
and
72
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle-k^{2}a_{\alpha}+D^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\beta}a^{\beta}+D^{\rm mGEM}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}h^{\beta\gamma}=0,

(112)

\displaystyle-k^{2}h_{\alpha\beta}+\bar{M}_{\alpha\beta}+4a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\alpha\beta}-2g_{\alpha\beta}a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\gamma\delta}g^{\gamma\delta}=0.

(113)

Let us first consider the mode whose frequency is closest to that given by the EM dispersion relation (
110
). For this mode, the last term in
Eq.

112
is a small perturbation and
h_{\alpha\beta}
, can be found perturbatively from
Eq.

113
by substituting
k^{2}\simeq-\omega^{2}_{\rm p}
there, i.e., adopting

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm p}^{2}h_{\alpha\beta}+\bar{M}_{\alpha\beta}+4a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\alpha\beta}-2g_{\alpha\beta}a^{\mu}D^{\rm mGEM}_{\mu\gamma\delta}g^{\gamma\delta}=0.

(114)

Since
N\sim 1
for such waves, one has
M_{\alpha\beta}\sim\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, so
M_{\alpha\beta}
can be ignored. Let us also simplify our notation by introducing
D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\doteq D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}^{\rm mGEM}
. Then, one can write that

\displaystyle h_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{1}{\omega_{\rm p}^{2}}\left(4a^{\mu}D_{\mu\alpha\beta}-2g_{\alpha\beta}a^{\mu}D_{\mu\gamma\delta}g^{\gamma\delta}\right).

(115)

By substituting this into
Eq.

112
, one obtains

\displaystyle-k^{2}a_{\alpha}+\mathcal{D}^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\mu}a^{\mu}=0,

(116)

where
\smash{\mathcal{D}^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\mu}}
can be understood as the EM dispersion function dressed by gravitational interactions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\mu}\doteq D^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\mu}-2\omega_{\rm p}^{-2}D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\left(2{D_{\mu}}^{\beta\gamma}-g^{\beta\gamma}{D_{\mu\delta}}^{\delta}\right).

(117)

Since
D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
vanishes in the the cold-plasma limit, the difference between
\smash{\mathcal{D}^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\mu}}
and
\smash{D^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\mu}}
is entirely due to finite particle speeds (in the frame specified in
Section

IV.1
). This difference can be estimated as follows. Assuming nonrelativistic speeds, one has
\smash{p_{\alpha}\simeq-m\delta^{0}_{\alpha}}
, so
\smash{a^{\mu}D_{\mu\alpha\beta}\simeq a^{0}D_{0\alpha\beta}}
. Then, like in
Section

V.4
, one finds that

\displaystyle D_{0\alpha\beta}\sim\frac{\mathsf{k}^{2}v_{Te}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\,Ne,

(118)

and
\mathsf{k}v_{Te}\ll\omega
in nonrelativistic plasma, because
{\omega/\mathsf{k}\gtrsim 1}
. Also note that
D^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\beta}\sim\omega_{\rm p}^{2}
and
\omega^{2}_{\rm p}\sim Ne^{2}/m_{e}
. Then, the second term on the right-hand side of
Eq.

117
scales relative to the first term as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathsf{k}v_{Te}}{\omega}\right)^{4}\frac{N^{2}e^{2}}{\omega_{p}^{4}}\lesssim\frac{m_{e}^{2}}{e^{2}}.

(119)

In other words, the frequency shift of an EM waves in plasma due to gravitational effects is of order
\smash{\mathcal{O}(m_{e}^{2}/e_{e}^{2})}
, which is extremely small. Note that this conclusion holds even in the limit of vanishingly small plasma density, where GWs and EM waves have the same dispersion relation,
k^{2}=0
. This is due to the fact that both the deviation from the linear resonance between these waves and their coupling (
\sim D^{2}/\omega_{\rm p}^{2}
) are equally proportional to
N
, so these waves remain nonresonant even at
N\to 0
.

A similar procedure can be used to obtain the correction to the dispersion relation for the GWs with
a_{\alpha}
treated as a perturbation. Substituting
k^{2}=0
in
Eq.

112
yields

\displaystyle D^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\beta}a^{\beta}=-D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}h^{\beta\gamma},

(120)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha\beta}^{\rm mEM}=-\omega^{2}_{\rm p}g_{\alpha\beta}+\sum_{s}e^{2}_{s}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{p}}{p^{0}}\,f_{s}(x,\boldsymbol{p})\,\frac{k_{\alpha}p_{\beta}}{k^{\mu}p_{\mu}},

(121)

where
Eq.

40c
is used to obtain the expression for
D_{\alpha\beta}^{\rm mEM}
. Let us denote the inverse of
\smash{D^{\rm mEM}_{\alpha\beta}}
as
\smash{D_{-1}^{\alpha\beta}}
. Then,

\displaystyle a^{\rho}=-D_{-1}^{\rho\alpha}D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}h^{\beta\gamma}.

(122)

By substituting this to
Eq.

113
, one obtains

-k^{2}h_{\alpha\beta}+\bar{M}_{\alpha\beta}-4D_{-1}^{\mu\rho}D_{\rho\sigma\tau}h^{\sigma\tau}D_{\mu\alpha\beta}\\
+2g_{\alpha\beta}D_{-1}^{\mu\rho}D_{\rho\sigma\tau}h^{\sigma\tau}{D_{\mu\gamma}}^{\gamma}=0.

(123)

The last two terms in
Eq.

123
represent the correction caused by GW coupling with EM field. Like in the case with EM waves, let us use that
\smash{D_{\alpha\beta}\sim\omega_{\rm p}^{2}}
and
\smash{D_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\ll Ne}
. Then, said correction is much less than
\smash{\rho_{e}}
, which is negligible compared with
\smash{M_{\alpha\beta}\sim\rho_{i}}
[see
Eq.

92
, with
\smash{\mathsf{k}^{2}\simeq\omega^{2}}
]. This means that EM effects in nonrelativistic nonmagnetized have a much smaller (at least by the factor
m_{e}/m_{i}\ll 1
) effect on GWs than gravitational interactions with the plasma. Furthermore, as we have pointed out earlier, even the effect of the latter is negligible within the GO approximation. In this sense, our results agree with the existing literature in that,
within the GO limit
, there is no linear coupling between the gravitational modes and the EM modes in any smooth background. The modifications of the dispersion relations of tensor gravitational modes that have been earlier reported for specific backgrounds
Chesters 1973
;
Madore 1973
;
Madore 1972
;
Barta and Vasúth 2018
;
Flauger and Weinberg 2018
;
Baym et al. 2017
;
Asseo et al. 1976
are beyond GO and therefore are not in disagreement with our findings.

VII
Conclusions

In summary, here we explore the hybridization of linear metric perturbations with their electromagnetic (EM) counterpart in non-magnetized plasma without assuming any special symmetry of the background. We begin, in
Section

III
, with the derivation of the effective (“oscillation-center”) Hamiltonian (
39
) that governs the average dynamics of plasma particles in a prescribed quasimonochromatic wave that involves metric perturbations and EM fields simultaneously. Then, using this Hamiltonian, in
Section

IV
, we derive the backreaction of plasma particles on the wave itself and obtain the equations [(
57
) and (
65
)] that describe the resulting self-consistent gravito-electromagnetic (GEM) waves in a plasma. We also demonstrate the gauge invariance of these equations and discuss how this coordinate invariance can be used to test (
58
) other approximate models of gravitational-wave (GW) dispersion in plasma.

We find that in the cold plasma limit (
Section

V.3
), linear gravitational modes and EM modes do not interact within the model assumed here, necessitating thermal effects for any such interaction. In a sufficiently dense plasma,
transverse
GEM modes consist of modes similar to the familiar transverse EM waves in plasma and the tensor modes of the GWs in vacuum, respectively. The shift of the GW frequency due to plasma is found to be generally of the same order as diffraction caused by plasma’s curving the background spacetime; therefore, it is beyond the accuracy of the geometrical-optics (GO) approximation, and the transverse tensor modes have no significant interaction with the plasma and the EM modes in the GO limit. However, for
longitudinal
GEM modes with large values of the refraction index, the interplay between gravitational and EM interactions in plasma can have a strong effect. In the case of purely gravitational interactions, these longitudinal modes contain the so-called scalar modes that also yield the Newtonian Jeans mode in the limit of large refraction index. In particular, the dispersion relation of the Jeans mode is significantly affected by electrostatic interactions. The approach used in this work can also be readily extended to magnetized plasma, an endeavour that we leave to future work.
