On the distribution of eigenvalues in families of Cayley graphs
Matilde Lalin, Anwesh Ray

Abstract
. We consider the family of undirected Cayley graphs associated with odd cyclic groups, and study statistics for the eigenvalues in their spectra. Our results are motivated by analogies between arithmetic geometry and graph theory.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
05C25, 11H06, 11N60, 11M38

1.
Introduction

It is well known that there are deep analogies between the arithmetic of curves in positive characteristic and the combinatorial properties of graphs. Ihara
[
12
]
and Sunada
[
20
]
defined the zeta function associated to a locally finite connected graph. The definition closely resembles the
Selberg zeta function
and encodes asymptotics for closed walks in the graph. There is a close analogy between the zeta functions of curves in positive characteristic and the Ihara zeta functions associated to graphs. Further analogies have been realized by Baker and Norine
[
1
]
, who etablished an analogue of the Abel-Jacobi map and the Riemann-Roch theorem in graph theory. Recently, statistical questions have been framed and studied for curves varying in certain naturally occurring ensembles, see for instance,
[
18
,
14
,
26
,
6
,
11
,
23
,
8
,
5
,
7
,
19
,
17
]
. There has also been a growing interest in graph theoretic questions that are motivated by arithmetic statistics. The sandpile group (also known as the Jacobian group, the critical group, or the Picard group) is an abelian group naturally associated to a graph that codifies structural information of the graph. The statistics of such groups have been studied by various authors
[
9
,
25
]
. More recently, the arithmetic statistics in the context of the Iwasawa theory of graphs has been studied in
[
10
]
.

It is natural to study statistical questions for the poles of the Ihara zeta function
[
12
,
2
,
22
]
. For a finite regular graph, Ihara related the poles of the zeta function to the spectrum of the adjacency matrix. The spacing between the eigenvalues of regular graphs has been studied via numerical computation, and related to the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble, cf.
[
13
,
15
]
. Cayley graphs are central objects in the combinatorial and geometric group theory. They allow us to visualize a group with respect to a generating set. Its spectrum can be described very concretely
[
24
]
. The main focus of this work is to study distribution questions for the family of eigenvalues that arise from Cayley graphs of finite odd cyclic groups.

1.1.
Main result

We now state our main result. Let
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and let
\mathcal{F}
be an infinite family of
r
-regular graphs with real eigenvalues. A
counting function

h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
is a function such that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, there are only finitely many graphs
X\in\mathcal{F}
that satisfy
h(X)\leq x
. Note that any eigenvalue of an
r
-regular graph in
\mathcal{F}
lies in the interval
[-r,r]
. Given an interval
J=[a,b]
contained in
[-r,r]
, we wish to evaluate the probability that a random eigenvalue of a graph in
\mathcal{F}
does lie in
J
. We consider families of Cayley graphs associated with cyclic groups with odd order. Given an odd natural number
n
, let
C_{n}
denote the cyclic group with
n
elements, which we identify with
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. For a pair
\gamma=(G,S)
such that
S=S^{-1}
, recall that
X_{\gamma}
is the associated Cayley graph. The association
\gamma\mapsto X_{\gamma}
gives us a way to parametrize Cayley graphs. In greater detail, we set

\mathcal{F}_{r}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{X_{\gamma}\mid\gamma=(G,S);G=C_{n}\text{ for some odd number }n>1,\#S=r,S=S^{-1}\right\}.

Thus,
\mathcal{F}_{r}
is an infinite family of
r
-regular graphs; define a height function by setting
h(X_{\gamma})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=n
, where
\gamma=(C_{n},S)
. There are only finitely many Cayley graphs for a given group, and hence,
h
is a counting function.

For
x\geq 0
, let
\sqrt{x}
be the non-negative square root of
x
. Set
\delta
denote the normalized arcsine distribution

\delta(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}&\text{ if }u\in(-1,1);\\
0&\text{ otherwise;}\\
\end{cases}

and let
\delta^{(k)}(u)
be the
k
-fold convolution

\delta^{(k)}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{-1}^{1}\dots\int_{-1}^{1}\delta(u_{1})\delta(u_{2})\cdots\delta(u_{k-1})\delta\left(u-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}u_{i}\right)du_{1}\cdots du_{k-1}.

Given a graph
X
, let
\operatorname{Sp}(X)
denote its spectrum, i.e., the set of eigenvalues for its adjacency matrix.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
and set
k\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\rfloor
. Let
J=[a,b]
be an interval contained in
[-r,r]
. Set
[c,d]\subseteq[-k,k]
to denote the interval
[a/2,b/2]
(resp.
[(a-1)/2,(b-1)/2]
) if
r
is even (resp.
r
is odd). Then, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\begin{split}\frac{\#\{(X,\alpha)\mid X\in\mathcal{F}_{r},\alpha\in\operatorname{Sp}(X),h(X)=n,\alpha\in J\}}{\#\{(X,\alpha)\mid X\in\mathcal{F}_{r},\alpha\in\operatorname{Sp}(X),h(X)=n\}}=&\int_{c}^{d}\delta^{(k)}(u)du+O_{r}(n^{-1+\epsilon}),\end{split}

where the implicit constant in the error term only depends on
r
.

The above theorem is a direct consequence of Theorem
5.5
. We note that the eigenvalues in the spectrum of a Cayley graph in
\mathcal{F}_{1}
are all equal to
1
, hence, we only consider the case when
r\geq 2
. The above probabilities are reinterpreted in terms of certain lattice point counts in certain regions in Euclidean space. These lattice point counts are estimated via a combination combinatorial and analytic techniques.

The above result motivates the study of eigenvalue distributions in more general families of
r
-regular Cayley graphs, ordered by the size of the underlying group. The authors expect that the study of such questions shall lead to many interesting developments in the future.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Basic definitions and properties of graphs

In this section, we recall a few preliminary notions in graph theory. For a more detailed exposition, we refer to
[
21
]
. The data of a directed graph
X
consists of a tuple
(V_{X},\mathbf{E}_{X})
, where
V_{X}
is the set of vertices and
\mathbf{E}_{X}
is the set of directed edges. The map
\operatorname{inc}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{E}_{X}\rightarrow V_{X}\times V_{X}
is the incidence map. The source and target maps
o\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{E}_{X}\rightarrow V_{X}
and
t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{E}_{X}\rightarrow V_{X}
send a directed edge to the source and target vertices respectively, and
\operatorname{inc}(e)=(o(e),t(e))
. Given
e\in\mathbf{E}_{X}
, we say that
e
joins
o(e)
to
t(e)
. We shall make the following assumptions on our graphs
X
.

(1)

The sets
V_{X}
and
\mathbf{E}_{X}
are finite.

(2)

Given vertices
v_{1}\in V_{X}
and
v_{2}\in V_{X}
(not necessarily distinct), there is at most one edge joining
v_{1}
to
v_{2}
.

(3)

Given any edge
e\in\mathbf{E}_{X}
, there is an edge
\bar{e}
such that
o(\bar{e})=t(e)
and
t(\bar{e})=o(e)
. Note that if
o(e)=t(e)
(i.e.,
e
is a loop), then
\bar{e}=e
.

Consider an equivalence relation on the set
\mathbf{E}_{X}
of directed edges, identifying
e
with
\bar{e}
. The equivalence classes consist of undirected edges of the graph and are denoted by
E_{X}
. Given
v\in V_{X}
, we consider the set of directed edges that orginate at
v
,

\mathbf{E}_{X,v}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{e\in\mathbf{E}_{X}\mid o(e)=v\}.

The valency of
v
is defined as follows
\operatorname{val}_{X}(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\#\mathbf{E}_{X,v}
. Alternatively, the valency is also the number of undirected edges which contain
v
as one of its vertices. The graph is
r
-regular if
\operatorname{val}_{X}(v)=r
for all
v\in V_{X}
. Let
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\#V_{X}
and choose a labelling on the vertices
V_{X}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{g}\}
. Consider the
g\times g
diagonal matrix defined by

d_{i,j}=\begin{cases}&0\text{ if }i\neq j;\\
&\operatorname{val}_{X}(v_{i})\text{ if }i=j.\\
\end{cases}

The adjacency matrix
A=(a_{i,j})
is defined as follows

a_{i,j}=\begin{cases}&\text{number of undirected edges between }v_{i}\text{ and }v_{j}\text{ if }i\neq j;\\
&\text{twice the number of undirected loops at }v_{i}\text{ if }i=j.\\
\end{cases}

The spectrum
\operatorname{Sp}(X)
is the set of eigenvalues
\alpha
of the adjacency matrix
A
. The matrix
Q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=D-A
is called the
Laplacian matrix
of the graph
X
. Let us explain the explicit relationship between the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix and the poles of
Ihara zeta function
associated to
X
. A path in
X
is a sequence
C=a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
, where
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
is a tuple of directed edges of
X
such that
t(a_{i})=o(a_{i+1})
. The length of
C
is the number of edges
\nu(C)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=k
. A closed path is a path for which
o(a_{1})=t(a_{k})
, i.e. a path starting and ending at the same vertex. The path is said to have a
backtrack
if
a_{j+1}=\overline{a_{j}}
for some
j
in the range
1\leq j\leq k-1
. The path is said to have a
tail
if
a_{k}=\overline{a_{1}}
. Given a closed path
D
, and an integer
m>0
,
D^{m}
is the path obtained by moving around
D

m
-times. A closed path
C
is said to be
primitive
if
C
has no backtracks or tails and
C\neq D^{m}
for any closed path
D
and
m>1
. Two closed paths are equivalent if one can be obtained from the other by changing the starting vertex. Note that if
C
is primitive, there are exactly
\nu(C)
paths that are equivalent to
C
. A
prime
path is an equivalence class of primitive paths. We shall sometimes denote the equivalence class of a primitive path
C
by
[C]
, however, when there is no cause for confusion, we simply use
C
itself to denote the prime path with primitive representative
C
.

The
Ihara polynomial

h_{X}(u)
denotes the determinant

h_{X}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\det(I-Au+Qu^{2}).

Suppose that
X
is connected with no vertex of degree
1
.
With these definitions in mind, the Ihara zeta function is defined to be the product

\zeta_{X}(u)=\prod_{C}(1-u^{\nu(C)})^{-1},

where the product ranges over all primes in
X
, ordered according to length. Then the Ihara
3
-term determinant formula states that

\zeta_{X}(u)^{-1}=(1-u^{2})^{r(X)-1}h_{X}(u),

where
r(X)
is rank of the fundamental group,
r(X)=|E|-|V|+1
. Note that when
X
is
r
-regular, where
r\geq 2
. Then
Q=(r-1)\operatorname{Id}
, and

h_{X}(u)=\prod_{\alpha\in\operatorname{Sp}(X)}\left(1-\alpha u+(r-1)u^{2}\right).

Therefore, the roots of
h_{X}(u)
are given by

r_{\alpha}^{\pm}=\frac{\alpha\pm\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-4(r-1)}}{2(r-1)},

where
\alpha\in\operatorname{Sp}(X)
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
S
be a subset of
G
such that
S^{-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{s^{-1}\mid s\in S\}
is equal to
S
. Associated to the pair
\gamma=(G,S)
is the undirected Cayley graph
X_{\gamma}
. The vertices
V_{\gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=V_{X_{\gamma}}
is the set
\{v_{g}\mid g\in G\}
, and there is an undirected edge joining
v_{g}
to
v_{h}
if and only if
gh^{-1}
is contained in
S
. Consider the special case when
G
is a finite abelian group and
\widehat{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{Hom}(G,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
be the group of characters defined on
G
. A Cayley graph
X_{\gamma}
is
r
-regular, where,
r\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\#S
. Given a character,
\chi\in\widehat{G}
, set
\lambda_{\chi}(X_{\gamma})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{s\in S}\chi(s)
.
As
\chi
ranges over
\widehat{G}
, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\chi}(X_{\gamma})
are all distinct (see Section 1.4.9 on Cayley graphs in
[
4
]
).
The spectrum
\operatorname{Sp}(X_{\gamma})
is the set of eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix and is equal to the set
\{\lambda_{\chi}(X_{\gamma})\mid\chi\in\widehat{G}\}
. Since
S=S^{-1}
, we find that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\chi}(X_{\gamma})
are all real and contained in
[-r,r]
.

3.
Eigenvalues of Cayley graphs

Let
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
, and recall that
\mathcal{F}_{r}
is the family of Cayley graphs

\mathcal{F}_{r}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{X_{\gamma}\mid\gamma=(G,S);G=C_{n}\text{ for some odd number }n>1,\#S=r,S=-S\right\},

where we are writing
C_{n}
with additive notation, since we identify it with the group
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
.

Since
n
is assumed to be odd, we find that
r=\#S
is odd precisely when
S
contains
0
. In this case, we write,

\begin{cases}r=2k&\text{ if }r\text{ is even};\\
r=2k+1&\text{ if }r\text{ is odd.}\\
\end{cases}

In either case, we may then write
S\backslash\{0\}=T\cup\left(-T\right)
, where
T=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
and
1\leq a_{i}\leq\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)
for
i=1,\dots,k
. We shall assume without loss of generality that
1\leq a_{1}<a_{2}<\dots<a_{k}\leq\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)
.

Let
S
be a subset of
G=C_{n}
such that
\#S=r
and
S=-S
. We set
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(G,S)
. Recall that the spectrum of
X_{\gamma}
is given by
\operatorname{Sp}(X_{\gamma})=\{\lambda_{\chi}(X_{\gamma})\mid\chi\in\widehat{G}\}
.
For an integer
m\in[0,n-1]
, let
\chi_{m}\in\widehat{C_{n}}
be the character defined by
\chi_{m}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{exp}\left(\frac{2\pi imx}{n}\right)
. We set
\lambda_{m}(\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lambda_{\chi_{m}}(X_{\gamma})
and we find that

(3.1)

\lambda_{m}(\gamma)=\begin{cases}\sum_{j=1}^{k}2\operatorname{cos}\left(\frac{2\pi ma_{j}}{n}\right)&\text{ if }r\text{ is even};\\
1+\sum_{j=1}^{k}2\operatorname{cos}\left(\frac{2\pi ma_{j}}{n}\right)&\text{ if }r\text{ is odd.}\end{cases}

In particular, we note that
\lambda_{0}(\gamma)=r
is the largest eigenvalue.

We set
A_{k}(n)
to denote the set of all vectors
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
such that
1\leq a_{1}<a_{2}<\dots<a_{k}\leq\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)
. Given
\vec{a}\in A_{k}(n)
, let
\gamma_{\vec{a}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(C_{n},S_{\vec{a}})
, where
S_{\vec{a}}
is the set with
r
elements such that
S\backslash\{0\}=\{a_{1},-a_{1},a_{2},-a_{2},\dots,a_{r},-a_{r}\}
. We set

\tau_{m}(\vec{a})=\tau_{m}(\gamma_{\vec{a}})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{cos}\left(\frac{2\pi ma_{i}}{n}\right).

Therefore, from (
3.1
), we find that

\lambda_{m}(\vec{a})=\lambda_{m}(\gamma_{\vec{a}})=\begin{cases}2\tau_{m}(\vec{a})&\text{ if }r\text{ is even};\\
1+2\tau_{m}(\vec{a})&\text{ if }r\text{ is odd.}\end{cases}

We find that
\tau_{m}(\vec{a})\in[-k,k]
, and that
\lambda_{m}(\vec{a})\in[-r,r]
.

Let
\mathcal{S}(n,k)
be the set of pairs
\left(\gamma_{\vec{a}},\tau_{m}(\vec{a})\right)
, where
\vec{a}\in A_{k}(n)
, and
m\in[0,n-1]
.
Let
\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)
be the subset of
\mathcal{S}(n,k)
where
m
is fixed. Given and interval
[e,f]\subset\mathbb{R}
, we set
[e,f]_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{Z}\cap[e,f]
. We identify
\mathcal{S}(n,k)
with
A_{k}(n)\times\left[0,n-1\right]_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Moreover, we identify
\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)
with
A_{k}(n)
, upon identifying
\vec{a}\in A_{k}(n)
with
(\vec{a},m)\in A_{k}(n)\times\left[0,n-1\right]_{\mathbb{Z}}
. We refer to
\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)
as the
m
-slice
in
\mathcal{S}(n,k)
. Let
J=[a,b]\subseteq[-r,r]
be a closed interval and
I=[c,d]\subseteq[-k,k]
defined by

[c,d]=\begin{cases}[a/2,b/2]&\text{ if }r\text{ is even};\\
[(a-1)/2,(b-1)/2]&\text{ if }r\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Note that
\lambda_{m}(\vec{a})\in J
if and only if
\tau_{m}(\vec{a})\in I
.
Set
\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k)\subseteq\mathcal{S}(n,k)
(resp.
\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k,m)\subseteq\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)
) to denote the subset for which
\tau_{m}(\vec{a})\in I
.

We have the following identifications

(3.2)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{S}(n,k)=\{(X,\alpha)\mid X\in\mathcal{F}_{r},\alpha\in\operatorname{Sp}(X),h(X)=n\},\\
&\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k)=\{(X,\alpha)\mid X\in\mathcal{F}_{r},\alpha\in\operatorname{Sp}(X),h(X)=n,\alpha\in J\}.\\
\end{split}

It is easy to see that

\begin{split}&\#\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)=\binom{\frac{n-1}{2}}{k},\\
&\#\mathcal{S}(n,k)=n\binom{\frac{n-1}{2}}{k}.\\
\end{split}

For
n,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that
n
is odd and
k\leq\frac{n-1}{2}
, set

\operatorname{Prob}_{I}\left(n,k\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\#\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k)}{\#\mathcal{S}(n,k)}.

Note that after making the identifications (
3.2
), we have that

(3.3)

\operatorname{Prob}_{I}\left(n,k\right)=\frac{\#\{(X,\alpha)\mid X\in\mathcal{F}_{r},\alpha\in\operatorname{Sp}(X),h(X)=n,\text{ and }\alpha\in J\}}{\#\{(X,\alpha)\mid X\in\mathcal{F}_{r},\alpha\in\operatorname{Sp}(X),h(X)=n\}}.

For a fixed integer
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, we wish to compute the limit

\begin{split}\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(k)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=&\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{Prob}_{I}\left(n,k\right),\\
=&\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{(2^{k}k!)\#\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k)}{n^{k+1}}\right).\end{split}

We express
\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(k)
as an average

(3.4)

\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k,m),

where

\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k,m)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\#\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k,m)}{\#\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)}=\frac{\#\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k,m)}{\binom{\frac{n-1}{2}}{k}}.

In the next section, we shall set up a geometric interpretation for the probability
\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k,m)
. Let
B_{k}
denote the
k
-dimensional box

B_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mid 0\leq x_{i}<1/2\text{ for all }i\},

and note that
\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k})=2^{-k}
. Let
B_{k}(I)
be the subset of
B_{k}
defined by

(3.5)

B_{k}(I)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in B_{k}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{cos}(2\pi x_{i})\in I\}.

Let us compute
\operatorname{Vol}\left(B_{k}(I)\right)
for an interval
I=[c,d]
. Let
D_{k}(I)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\in[-1,1]^{k}\mid\sum_{i}u_{i}\in I\}
, and
\Phi
be the function taking
D_{k}(I)
to
B_{k}(I)
defined by
\Phi(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\operatorname{cos}^{-1}(u_{1}),\dots,\frac{1}{2\pi}\operatorname{cos}^{-1}(u_{k})\right)
. For
x\geq 0
, let
\sqrt{x}
be the non-negative square root of
x
. Recall that

\delta(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}&\text{ if }u\in(-1,1);\\
0&\text{ otherwise;}\\
\end{cases}

and let
\delta^{(k)}(u)
be the
k
-fold convolution

\delta^{(k)}(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{-1}^{1}\dots\int_{-1}^{1}\delta(u_{1})\delta(u_{2})\cdots\delta(u_{k-1})\delta\left(u-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}u_{i}\right)du_{1}\cdots du_{k-1}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
I=[c,d]\subseteq[-k,k]
be an interval,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, and let
B_{k}(I)\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be the region defined by (
3.5
). Then, its volume is given by

\operatorname{Vol}\left(B_{k}(I)\right)=2^{-k}\int_{c}^{d}\delta^{(k)}(u)du.

Proof.

By the change of variables theorem, we find that

\begin{split}\operatorname{Vol}\left(B_{k}(I)\right)=&\int_{D_{k}(I)}|\operatorname{det}D\Phi|\\
=&(2\pi)^{-k}\int_{D_{k}(I)}\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{1-u_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{1-u_{2}^{2}}\dots\sqrt{1-u_{r}^{2}}\right)}du_{1}\dots du_{r}\\
=&2^{-k}\int_{c}^{d}\delta^{(k)}(u)du.\end{split}

∎

4.
A geometric interpretation

We wish to provide a geometric reinterpretation for the quantities
\#\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k,m)
and
\#\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)
introduced in the previous section. Given an integer
n\geq 1
, let
L_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be the lattice consisting of all vectors of the form
(\frac{a_{1}}{n},\dots,\frac{a_{k}}{n})
, where
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. Let
L_{n}^{\prime}
be the subset of
L_{n}
consisting of such vectors
(\frac{a_{1}}{n},\dots,\frac{a_{k}}{n})
for which the coordinates are all mutually distinct. Set
\Omega_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=B_{k}\cap L_{n}
,
\Omega_{n}(I)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=B_{k}(I)\cap L_{n}
,
\Omega_{n}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=B_{k}\cap L_{n}^{\prime}
and
\Omega_{n}^{\prime}(I)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=B_{k}(I)\cap L_{n}^{\prime}
and let
\kappa\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1/2]
be the function defined as follows

(4.1)

\kappa(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}\{x\}&\text{ if }\{x\}\leq 1/2;\\
1-\{x\}&\text{ if }\{x\}>1/2.\end{cases}

Here,
\{x\}
denotes the fractional part of
x
. We note that
\operatorname{cos}(2\pi x)=\operatorname{cos}\left(2\pi\kappa(x)\right)
. For a pair
\gamma=(G,S)
, such that
G=C_{n}
and
S
a subset of
G
with
S=S^{-1}
, we recall from the previous section that
S\backslash\{0\}=T\cup(-T)
, where
T=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
, and
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in A_{k}(n)
. Let
\sigma
be a permutation of
\{1,\dots,k\}
and
m\in[1,n-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Write
m=dm_{1}
,
n=dn_{1}
, where
d\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(m,n)
. Consider a point
(\vec{a},m)\in\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)
. Associate to this point and to the permutation
\sigma
, the point

(4.2)

\begin{split}P(\vec{a},\sigma,m,n)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=&\left(\kappa\left(\frac{ma_{\sigma(1)}}{n}\right),\kappa\left(\frac{ma_{\sigma(2)}}{n}\right),\dots,\kappa\left(\frac{ma_{\sigma(k)}}{n}\right)\right),\\
=&\left(\kappa\left(\frac{m_{1}a_{\sigma(1)}}{n_{1}}\right),\kappa\left(\frac{m_{1}a_{\sigma(2)}}{n_{1}}\right),\dots,\kappa\left(\frac{m_{1}a_{\sigma(k)}}{n_{1}}\right)\right).\end{split}

It is clear that
P(\vec{a},\sigma,m,n)
is contained in
\Omega_{n_{1}}
.

For ease of notation, identify the
m
-slice
\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)
with
A_{k}(n)
, by identifying
\vec{a}
with
(\vec{a},m)
.
For
m\in[0,n-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
, it conveniences us to further subdivide
\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)
into two disjoint subsets
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m)
as follows. Set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,0)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,0)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{S}(n,k,0)
. For
m\in[1,n-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
, let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
consist of all
\vec{a}\in A_{k}(n)
such that for
i<j
,

a_{i}\not\equiv\pm a_{j}\mod{n_{1}},

and set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m)
to be the complement
\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)\backslash\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
. We shall set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k)
(resp.
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k)
) to denote the disjoint union of slices
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
(resp.
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m)
) as
m
ranges through
[0,n-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
. It shall turn out that the slices
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
are better suited to combinatorial arguments than
\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)
. Lemma
4.2
will show that when
k
is fixed,
\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k)
is small in comparison to
\#\mathcal{S}(n,k)
, as
n
goes to
\infty
. Given an interval
I\subseteq[-k,k]
, we set
\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
(resp.
\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m)
) to be the subset of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
(resp.
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m)
) for which
\tau_{m}(\vec{a})\in I
.

Lemma 4.1
.

There is a constant
C_{k}>0
(depending on
k
), independent of
m
and
n
such that

\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m)<\frac{C_{k}n^{k}}{n_{1}}.

The constant
C_{k}
can be taken to be a polynomial in
k
.

Proof.

Given a pair
\tau=(i,j)
such that
i<j
, let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}_{\tau}(n,k,m)
be the subset of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m)
such that

a_{i}\equiv\pm a_{j}\mod{n_{1}}.

The total number of subsets
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{j-1},a_{j+1},\dots,a_{k}\}
is at most
\binom{\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}{k-1}
. Given one such choice,

\begin{split}a_{j}\in&\{a_{i}+rn_{1}\mid-\lfloor n/n_{1}\rfloor-1\leq r\leq\lfloor n/n_{1}\rfloor+1\}\\
\cup&\{-a_{i}+rn_{1}\mid-\lfloor n/n_{1}\rfloor-1\leq r\leq\lfloor n/n_{1}\rfloor+1\}.\end{split}

Therefore, we find that

\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}_{\tau}(n,k,m)\leq 4\left(\lfloor n/n_{1}\rfloor+1\right)\binom{\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}{k-1}.

Thus, we have that

\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m)\leq 4\binom{k}{2}\left(\lfloor n/n_{1}\rfloor+1\right)\binom{\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}{k-1}<\frac{C_{k}n^{k}}{n_{1}}.

∎

Given
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, let
d(n)
denote the number of divisors of
n
. Note that for any
\epsilon>0
,
d(n)=O(n^{\epsilon})
. Given non-negative functions
f
,
g
and
h
, we write

f=g+O_{k}(h)

if there is a constant
C_{k}>0
which depends only on
k
such that

|f-g|\leq C_{k}h.

Lemma 4.2
.

There is a constant
C_{k}>0
, independent of
n
, and depending on
k
, such that

\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k)<C_{k}n^{k}\left(\sum_{d|n}\frac{\varphi(d)}{d}\right)<C_{k}d(n)n^{k},

where
d(n)
is the number of divisors of
n
. As a result, we find that for any
\epsilon>0
,

\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k)=O_{k}(n^{k+\epsilon}),

and that

\frac{\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k)}{\#\mathcal{S}(n,k)}=O_{k}(n^{-1+\epsilon}).

Proof.

Let
m\in[1,n-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
, set
d=(m,n)
. Then, Lemma
4.1
implies that

\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m)<C_{k}\frac{n^{k}}{n_{1}}=C_{k}n^{k-1}d.

We find that

\begin{split}\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k)&=\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,0)+\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m),\\
&=\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,0)+\sum_{d|n}\sum_{(m,n)=d}\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m),\\
&<n^{k}+C_{k}\sum_{d|n}n^{k-1}d\#\{m\in[1,n-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}\mid(m,n)=d\},\\
&=n^{k}+C_{k}\sum_{d|n}n^{k-1}d\varphi\left(\frac{n}{d}\right),\\
&=n^{k}+C_{k}n^{k}\left(\sum_{d|n}\frac{\varphi(d)}{d}\right).\\
\end{split}

Replace
C_{k}
above with
(C_{k}+1)
to obtain the result.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The function
g(n)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{d|n}\varphi(d)\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)
is known as Pillai’s arithmetical function
[
16
]
. Set
\omega(n)
to denote the number of prime divisors of
n
. We note that a bound of

g(n)\leq 27n\left(\frac{\log n}{\omega(n)}\right)^{\omega(n)}

is explicitly proven by Broughan
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\sigma\in S_{k}
,
m\in[1,n-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
, and
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
. Let
I
be an interval contained in
[-k,k]
. Then, the point
P(\vec{a},\sigma,m,n)
defined above in (
4.2
) lies in
\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}
. Furthermore, the following are equivalent

(1)

\tau_{m}(\vec{a})\in I
,

(2)

P(\vec{a},\sigma,m,n)\in\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}(I)
.

Proof.

Suppose that

\kappa\left(\frac{m_{1}a_{\sigma(i)}}{n_{1}}\right)=\kappa\left(\frac{m_{1}a_{\sigma(j)}}{n_{1}}\right),

where we recall that
\kappa(x)
is given by (
4.1
). Then,

m_{1}a_{\sigma(i)}\equiv\pm m_{1}a_{\sigma(j)}\mod{n_{1}}.

Since
m_{1}
is coprime to
n_{1}
, we find that

a_{\sigma(i)}\equiv\pm a_{\sigma(j)}\mod{n_{1}}.

However, since
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
,

a_{\sigma(i)}\not\equiv\pm a_{\sigma(j)}\mod{n_{1}}

unless
i=j
. This proves that all the coordinates of the point
P(\vec{a},\sigma,m,n)
are distinct. Furthermore, all coordinates are of the form
b_{i}/n_{1}
, where
b_{i}
is an integer, and these numbers lie in the range
[0,1/2]
. Therefore, the point
P(\vec{a},\sigma,m,n)
is contained in
\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}
.

Write
P(\vec{a},\sigma,m,n)=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
, where,
y_{i}=\kappa\left(\frac{ma_{\sigma(i)}}{n}\right)
. Recall that
B_{k}(I)
consists of all tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in B_{k}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{cos}(2\pi x_{i})\in I
. The point
(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\in\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}
lies in
\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}(I)
if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{cos}(2\pi y_{i})\in I
. Observe that

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{cos}(2\pi y_{i})\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{cos}\left(2\pi\kappa\left(\frac{m_{1}a_{\sigma(i)}}{n_{1}}\right)\right)\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{cos}\left(\frac{2\pi m_{1}a_{\sigma(i)}}{n_{1}}\right)\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{cos}\left(\frac{2\pi m_{1}a_{i}}{n_{1}}\right)=\tau_{m}(\vec{a}).\\
\end{split}

This means that the conditions are equivalent.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

With respect to notation above,

\begin{split}&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1}),\\
&\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1}).\\
\end{split}

Proof.

Given a tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1})
and
(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{k}^{\prime})\in\left[0,\left(\frac{(n,m)-1}{2}\right)\right]^{k}
, set
(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})
be given by
b_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=a_{i}+a_{i}^{\prime}m_{1}
. Note that since
a_{i}\in[1,\frac{m_{1}-1}{2}]
,

b_{i}<\left(\frac{m_{1}-1}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{(n,m)-1}{2}\right)m_{1}=\frac{m-1}{2}.

Let
(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
be the permutation of the coordinates of
(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})
such that
1\leq c_{1}<c_{2}<\dots<c_{k}\leq\frac{m-1}{2}
. Note that
a_{i}\not\equiv\pm a_{j}\mod{m_{1}}
for
i\neq j
, and therefore,
c_{i}\not\equiv\pm c_{j}\mod{m_{1}}
for
i\neq j
. This implies that
(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
. The association

\left((a_{1},\dots,a_{k}),(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{k}^{\prime})\right)\mapsto(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})

sets up a bijection

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1})\times\left[0,\left(\frac{(n,m)-1}{2}\right)\right]^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m),

which restricts to a bijection

\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1})\times\left[0,\left(\frac{(n,m)-1}{2}\right)\right]^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n,k,m).

Therefore, we conclude that

\begin{split}&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1}),\\
&\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1}).\end{split}

∎

Proposition 4.6
.

For
m\in[1,n-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
, and
I\subseteq[-k,k]
be a subset. Then the following assertions hold.

(1)

There is a surjection

\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1})

whose fibres all have cardinality
k!
.

(2)

The map
\Phi
restricts to a map

\Phi_{I}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}(I)\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1}),

whose fibers also have cardinality
k!
.

Proof.

We set
d\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(m,n)
,
m_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=m/d
and
n_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=n/d
. Consider a point
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}
. By definition, there exist distinct integers
b_{i}\in[0,\frac{n_{1}-1}{2}]
such that
x_{i}=b_{i}/n_{1}
. Let
c_{i}\in[0,n_{1}-1]
be such that
m_{1}c_{i}\equiv b_{i}\mod{n_{1}}
for all
i
. Then, set

a_{i}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}c_{i}&\text{ if }c_{i}\in[0,\frac{n_{1}-1}{2}]\\
(n_{1}-c_{i})&\text{ if }c_{i}\in[\frac{n_{1}+1}{2},n_{1}-1].\end{cases}

Note that
a_{i}^{\prime}\in[0,\frac{n_{1}-1}{2}]
and that
\kappa\left(\frac{m_{1}a_{i}^{\prime}}{n_{1}}\right)=\frac{m_{1}c_{i}}{n_{1}}=\frac{b_{i}}{n_{1}}
. There is a tuple
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1})
and a permutation
\sigma\in S_{k}
such that
(a_{\sigma(1)},\dots,a_{\sigma(k)})=(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{k}^{\prime})
. Thus, there is a unique permutation
\sigma
such that

(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\left(\kappa\left(\frac{m_{1}a_{\sigma(1)}}{n_{1}}\right),\dots,\kappa\left(\frac{m_{1}a_{\sigma(i)}}{n_{1}}\right),\dots,\kappa\left(\frac{m_{1}a_{\sigma(k)}}{n_{1}}\right)\right).

We map the tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
to
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
. Consider the action of
S_{k}
on
\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}
via permutation of coordinates. The fibres of this map have cardinality
k!=\#S_{k}
. By Lemma
4.4
, it is easy to see that
\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1}))=\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}(I)
, and the second assertion follows.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
m\in[1,n-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
I
be an interval contained in
[-k,k]
. Then we have that

\begin{split}&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}}{k!},\\
&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}(I)}{k!}.\end{split}

Proof.

Lemma
4.5
asserts that the following relations hold

\begin{split}&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1}),\\
&\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1}).\\
\end{split}

By Proposition
4.6
, we have that

\begin{split}&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1})=\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}}{k!},\\
&\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n_{1},k,m_{1})=\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}(I)}{k!},\end{split}

and combining the above relations, the result follows.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
m\in[1,n-1]
, we have that

\begin{split}&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}}{k!}+O_{k}((n,m)n^{k-1}),\\
&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}(I)}{k!}+O_{k}((n,m)n^{k-1}).\end{split}

Proof.

Let
\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Omega_{n_{1}}\backslash\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}
and
\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime\prime}(I)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Omega_{n_{1}}(I)\backslash\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}(I)
. The set
\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime\prime}
consists of all tuples
\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\right)
such that for some
i<j
,
x_{i}=x_{j}
. Clearly, the cardinality of this set can be bounded as follows

\#\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime\prime}\leq\binom{k}{2}\left(\frac{n_{1}-1}{2}\right)^{k-1}.

It follows from Lemma
4.7
that

\begin{split}&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}}{k!}-\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime\prime}}{k!},\\
&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I}(n,k,m)=\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}(I)}{k!}-\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime\prime}(I)}{k!}.\end{split}

Therefore, the error term can be bounded as follows

\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime\prime}}{k!}\leq\frac{1}{k!}\binom{k}{2}\left(\frac{n_{1}-1}{2}\right)^{k-1}\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}=O_{k}((n,m)n^{k-1}),

and the result follows.
∎

5.
Proof of the main theorem

In the previous section, we obtained a geometric interpretation for the quantities
\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)
and
\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I}(n,k,m)
, in terms of the quantities
\#\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}
and
\#\Omega_{n_{1}}^{\prime}(I)
respectively. In order to effectively bound
\#\Omega_{n}^{\prime}(I)
, it suffices bound
\#\Omega_{n}(I)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
I=[c,d]
is an interval contained in
[-k,k]
, and suppose that
(\frac{a_{1}}{n},\dots,\frac{a_{k}}{n})\in B_{k}(I)
. Then,
(\frac{a_{1}+1}{n},\frac{a_{2}+1}{n},\dots,\frac{a_{k}+1}{n})\in B_{k}\left(\left[c-\frac{2\pi k}{n},d+\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right]\right)
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi a_{i}}{n}\right)\in[c,d]\Longrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{k}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi(a_{i}+1)}{n}\right)\in\left[c-\frac{2\pi k}{n},d+\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right].

To see the above, notice that for
0\leq x\leq\pi
and
\epsilon>0
such that
x+\epsilon\leq\pi
, it follows from the mean value theorem that

\operatorname{cos}(x)-\epsilon\leq\cos(x+\epsilon)\leq\operatorname{cos}(x)+\epsilon.

∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
I=[c,d]
be an interval contained in
[-k,k]
. With respect to notation above,

\#\Omega_{n}(I)=n^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}(n^{k-1}).

Proof.

For a given element
(\frac{a_{1}}{n},\dots,\frac{a_{n}}{n})\in\Omega_{n}(I)
, let
\mathcal{B}_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})}
denote the box

\mathcal{B}_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})}=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\left|\frac{a_{j}}{n}\leq x_{j}\leq\frac{a_{j}+1}{n}\right.\right\}.

It is clear that
\operatorname{Vol}(\mathcal{B}_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})})=\frac{1}{n^{k}}
. Now we consider the set
U_{k,n}
which is the union of
\mathcal{B}_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})}
, where
(\frac{a_{1}}{n},\dots,\frac{a_{n}}{n})\in\Omega_{n}(I)
. This is a set containing
B_{k}(I)
. We have

\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))\leq\frac{\#\Omega_{n}(I)}{n^{k}}=\operatorname{Vol}(U_{k,n}).

From Lemma
5.1
, it follows that

U_{k,n}\subseteq B_{k}\left(\left[c-\frac{2\pi k}{n},d+\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right]\right).

Thus we conclude that

\begin{split}n^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))&\leq\#\Omega_{n}(I)\\
&\leq n^{k}\operatorname{Vol}\left(B_{k}\left(\left[c-\frac{2\pi k}{n},d+\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right]\right)\right)\\
&=n^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))\\
+&n^{k}\operatorname{Vol}\left(B_{k}\left(\left[c-\frac{2\pi k}{n},c\right]\right)\right)+n^{k}\operatorname{Vol}\left(B_{k}\left(\left[d,d+\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right]\right)\right).\end{split}.

To estimate the error term, it suffices to bound

\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{k}\int^{c}_{c-\frac{2\pi k}{n}}\delta^{(k)}(u)du\text{ and }\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{k}\int^{d+\frac{2\pi k}{n}}_{d}\delta^{(k)}(u)du.

Note that the support of
\delta
is contained in
[-1,1]
, and hence the support of
\delta^{(k)}
is contained in
[-k,k]
. Therefore, if
c=-k
, then,
\int^{c}_{c-\frac{2\pi k}{n}}\delta^{(k)}(u)du=0
. Without loss of generality, assume that
c\in(-k,k)
, and let
c^{\prime}\in(-k,c)
. Set
M\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{sup}\{|\delta^{(k)}(u)|\mid u\in[c^{\prime},c]\}
. Note that for
n>\frac{2\pi k}{(c-c^{\prime})}
,

\int^{c}_{c-\frac{2\pi k}{n}}\delta^{(k)}(u)du\leq\frac{2\pi kM}{n}.

Therefore, we have shown that

\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{k}\int^{c}_{c-\frac{2\pi k}{n}}\delta^{(k)}(u)du=O_{k}(n^{k-1}).

The same reasoning shows that

\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{k}\int^{d+\frac{2\pi k}{n}}_{d}\delta^{(k)}(u)du=O_{k}(n^{k-1}).

Therefore, we have shown that

\#\Omega_{n}(I)=n^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}\left(n^{k-1}\right).

∎

Proposition 5.3
.

With respect to notation above,

\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I}(n,k,m)=\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)2^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}((n,m)n^{k-1}).

Proof.

We have

\begin{split}\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I}(n,k,m)=&\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{\#\Omega_{n_{1}}(I)}{k!}+O_{k}((n,m)n^{k-1}),\\
=&\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\frac{n_{1}^{k}}{k!}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}((n,m)n^{k-1})\\
=&\left(\frac{(n,m)+1}{2}\right)^{k}\#\Omega_{n_{1}}\frac{2^{k}}{k!}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}((n,m)n^{k-1})\\
=&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)2^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}((n,m)n^{k-1}).\end{split}

For the above set of inequalities, we use Corollary
4.8
and Proposition
5.2
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

With respect to notation above,

\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I}(n,k)=\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k)2^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}(n^{k+\epsilon}).

Proof.

Note that
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,0)
is empty. By an argument similar to the one employed in Lemma
4.2
,

\begin{split}\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n,k)&=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n,k,m),\\
&=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k,m)2^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}\left(n^{k-1}\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}(n,m)\right),\\
&=\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k)2^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}\left(n^{k-1}\sum_{d|n}d\varphi\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\right),\\
&=\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k)2^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}(n^{k+\epsilon}),\end{split}

where in the second line, we have applied Proposition
5.3
.
∎

Theorem 5.5
.

For
k\geq 1
fixed, and
I=[c,d]\subseteq[-k,k]
,

\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k)=\int_{c}^{d}\delta^{(k)}(u)du+O_{k}(n^{-1+\epsilon}).

Proof.

Recall from (
3.4
) that

\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k,m),

where

\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k,m)=\frac{\#\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k,m)}{\#\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)}=\frac{\#\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k,m)}{\binom{\frac{n-1}{2}}{k}}.

Note that
\#\mathcal{S}_{I}(n,k,m)=\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n,k,m)+\#\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime\prime}(n,k,m)
, and
therefore,

\begin{split}\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k)=&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k,m)\\
=&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\frac{\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n,k,m)}{\binom{\frac{n-1}{2}}{k}}+\frac{\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime\prime}(n,k)}{n\binom{\frac{n-1}{2}}{k}}.\\
\end{split}

By Lemma
4.2
, we find that

\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime\prime}(n,k)\leq\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(n,k)=O_{k}(d(n)n^{k})=O_{k}(n^{k+\epsilon}),

and therefore,

\begin{split}\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k)=&\frac{1}{n}\frac{\mathcal{S}_{I}^{\prime}(n,k)}{\binom{\frac{n-1}{2}}{k}}+O_{k}(n^{-1+\epsilon}).\end{split}

By Proposition
5.4
,

\begin{split}\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{I}(n,k)=&\#\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(n,k)2^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}(n^{k+\epsilon}),\\
=&\#\mathcal{S}(n,k)2^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}(n^{k+\epsilon}),\\
=&n\binom{\frac{n-1}{2}}{k}2^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}(n^{k+\epsilon}),\\
\end{split}

where we recall that for fixed
k
,
\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))
is bounded uniformly in
I
, which follows from Lemma
3.1
. Moreover, by Lemma
3.1
,

\begin{split}\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k)=&2^{k}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{k}(I))+O_{k}(n^{-1+\epsilon}),\\
=&\int_{c}^{d}\delta^{(k)}(u)du+O_{k}(n^{-1+\epsilon}).\end{split}

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

It follows from (
3.3
) that

\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k)=\frac{\#\{(X,\alpha)\mid X\in\mathcal{F}_{r},\alpha\in\operatorname{Sp}(X),h(X)=n,\alpha\in J\}}{\#\{(X,\alpha)\mid X\in\mathcal{F}_{r},\alpha\in\operatorname{Sp}(X),h(X)=n\}}.

By Theorem
5.5
,

\operatorname{Prob}_{I}(n,k)=\int_{c}^{d}\delta^{(k)}(u)du+O_{k}(n^{-1+\epsilon}),

which proves the result.
∎
