Controlled crossed Andreev reflection and elastic co-tunneling mediated by Andreev bound states
Alberto Bordin, Guanzhong Wang, Chun-Xiao Liu, Sebastiaan L. D. ten Haaf, Grzegorz P. Mazur, Nick van Loo, Di Xu, David van Driel, Francesco Zatelli, Sasa Gazibegovic, Ghada Badawy, Erik P. A. M. Bakkers, Michael Wimmer, Leo P. Kouwenhoven, Tom Dvir

Abstract
A short superconducting segment can couple attached quantum dots via elastic co-tunneling (ECT) and crossed Andreev reflection (CAR). Such coupled quantum dots can host Majorana bound states provided that the ratio between CAR and ECT can be controlled. Metallic superconductors have so far been shown to mediate such tunneling phenomena, albeit with limited tunability. Here we show that Andreev bound states formed in semiconductor-superconductor heterostructures can mediate CAR and ECT over mesoscopic length scales. Andreev bound states possess both an electron and a hole component, giving rise to an intricate interference phenomenon that allows us to tune the ratio between CAR and ECT deterministically. We further show that the combination of intrinsic spin-orbit coupling in InSb nanowires and an applied magnetic field provides another efficient knob to tune the ratio between ECT and CAR and optimize the amount of coupling between neighboring quantum dots.

The Kitaev chain is a prime example of condensed-matter toy models exhibiting a topological superconducting phase
Kitaev.2001
.
Practical proposals to construct an artificial Kitaev chain require a set of quantum dots (QDs) separated by narrow superconducting segments
Sau.2012
;
Leijnse.2012
;
Fulga.2013
.
Such QDs interact via two mechanisms: elastic co-tunneling (ECT) and crossed Andreev reflection (CAR).
ECT occurs when a single electron tunnels between the two QDs via the superconductor (see schematic in Fig.
1
a).
In CAR, electrons from two separate QDs tunnel into the superconductor forming a Cooper pair; or in its reversed process, a Cooper pair is split into two electrons, tunneling to different QDs (see schematic in Fig.
1
b)
beckmann.2004
;
Russo2005
;
Recher.2001
.
The balance between CAR- and ECT-induced couplings is crucial for observing the sought-after Majorana zero modes at the boundaries of the Kitaev chain
Leijnse.2012
.

Andreev bound states (ABSs) in hybrid semiconductor-superconductor heterostructures can mediate CAR and ECT between two neighboring QDs
Liu.2022
.
Such states form when a confined semiconducting level is tunnel-coupled to a superconductor.
Importantly, an ABS excitation can be smoothly tuned from electron-like to hole-like using electrostatic gating
Schindele.2014
;
Danon.2020
;
Menard.2020
.
The interplay between the electron and hole components of an ABS is predicted to be a key element in controlling CAR and ECT
Liu.2022
.
Moreover, an external magnetic field affects the ABS energy via Zeeman splitting
Lee.2014
and influences CAR and ECT amplitudes thereby.
In the presence of spin-orbit coupling, the amplitudes further develop an anisotropic dependence on the magnetic field direction
Wang.2022
.

In this work, we report on gate tunability of CAR and ECT in hybrid semiconductor-superconductor heterostructures.
In particular, both processes are correlated with the presence of ABSs in the hybrid.
By comparing experimental data and our theoretical model, we further show that the observed CAR and ECT amplitudes, respectively, result from constructive and destructive interference of tunneling paths.
The interference pattern is linked to the charge of the mediating Andreev bound state and can be controlled via tuning the hybrid’s chemical potential.
Finally, we report on the magnetic field dependence of CAR and ECT.
We show how the CAR and ECT interference patterns are modified through the interplay of the orientation of the magnetic field, the direction of the spin-orbit coupling, the energy of the ABS and its spin-splitting.

Next, to measure currents induced by CAR and ECT (

We characterize the QDs by measuring the gate-dependent and magnetic-field-dependent transport through them and focus in the remainder of this paper on two charge transitions of each QD: from

The CAR- and ECT-induced currents are measured using a method introduced in our previous work

We briefly summarize the full descriptions in the Methods here.
To measure CAR-induced currents at a given value of
V_{\mathrm{PG}}

We take

To measure the amount of ECT-induced currents, we repeat this procedure when applying anti-symmetric bias on both leads:
V_{\mathrm{L}}=-V_{\mathrm{R}}=$70\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$

We take

To understand the role of ABSs in mediating CAR- and ECT-induced currents, we consider a model with two QDs on each side of a single ABS confined in the central hybrid segment, as shown in Fig.
2
a,b. Considering only one orbital state in each QD, this reduces to a simple three-site model
Dominguez.2016
;
Liu.2022
;
Tsintzis.2022
.
For simplicity, we treat the ABS as one pair of semiconducting states tunnel-coupled to the superconductor in the atomic limit
Bauer.2007
, simplifying the general expressions derived in Ref.
Liu.2022
(see Supplementary Information for details).
Andreev reflection at the semiconductor-superconductor interface hybridizes the two electronic states with even charge occupation,
\ket{0}
and
\ket{2}
, with hybridization rate
\Gamma
.
The ground state of the ABS is a spin singlet of the form
\ket{\mathrm{S}}=u\ket{0}-v\ket{2}
, where
u,v>0
are the normalized superposition coefficients determined by
\Gamma
and
\mu
, the chemical potential of the electronic level before hybridization.
Positive
\mu
results in
u>v
and negative
\mu
leads to
u<v

Bauer.2007
.
The excited states of the ABS form a doublet
\ket{\mathrm{D}\!\uparrow},\ket{\mathrm{D}\!\downarrow}
where
\uparrow/\downarrow
indicates, in the absence of spin-orbit coupling, the spin state of the single electron occupying the ABS (see the Supplementary Information for general spin-orbit-coupled scenarios).

Under zero external magnetic field, the doublet states are degenerate and the energy difference between
\ket{\mathrm{S}}
and
\ket{\mathrm{D}}
is
E_{\mathrm{ABS}}
, which reaches a minimum around
\mu=0
(Fig.
2
c)
Bauer.2007
.
An excitation from the ground state of the ABS to an excited state is said to be a Bogoliubov quasiparticle, having an electron-like part
u
and a hole-like part
v
in superposition.
The effective charge of the ABS is defined as the net charge character of this excitation,
-e(u^{2}-v^{2})
, where
e>0
is the elementary charge
Danon.2020
;
Menard.2020
.
This quantity ranges from
-e
(electron-like) to
+e
(hole-like).

We consider both CAR and ECT as coherent second-order processes that involve the virtual occupation of an ABS doublet as the intermediate state.
ECT can take place through two paths.
The first, marked in grey in Fig.
2
a, involves the occupation of the ABS by adding an electron from one lead with a hopping amplitude proportional to
u
, followed by emptying of the ABS via ejection of the electron to the other lead, with an amplitude also proportional to
u
.
The second, marked in green in Fig.
2
a, occurs in the opposite order: an ABS is excited to
\ket{\mathrm{D}}
by accepting a hole from one lead, with an amplitude proportional to
v
, and then relaxes to
\ket{\mathrm{S}}
by ejecting a hole to the other lead, also with an amplitude proportional to
v
.
As presented in Ref.
Liu.2022
and briefly here in Supplementary Information, these two paths interfere destructively due to fermion exchange statistics and the ECT-induced current,
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
, is:

I_{\mathrm{ECT}}=I_{0}\absolutevalue{\frac{u^2-v^2}{\EABS/\Gamma}}^{2}

(1)

where
I_{0}
is a proportionality constant given by
I_{0}=\frac{e}{\hbar}\cdot\frac{t^{2}_{\mathrm{L}}t^{2}_{\mathrm{R}}}{\Gamma^{2}\gamma_{\mathrm{DL}}}
and depends on the coupling between the QDs and the ABS (
t_{\mathrm{L}}
and
t_{\mathrm{R}}
) as well as the lifetime of QDs due to coupling to the leads (
\gamma_{\mathrm{DL}}
) in the limit of electron temperature and tunnel couplings much smaller than bias voltage.
Strikingly, the destructive interference results in a suppression of
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
near
\mu=0
where
u^{2}=v^{2}=\frac{1}{{2}}
(Fig.
2
e).

The process of CAR, depicted in Fig.
2
b, can take place via two paths as well.
In the first path (marked in green), an electron from the left lead populates the ABS with an amplitude proportional to
u
, followed by emptying of the ABS via accepting an electron from the right lead, with an amplitude proportional to
v
.
In the second path, the roles of the left and right QDs are reversed.
The two paths interfere constructively, yielding

I_{\mathrm{CAR}}=I_{0}\absolutevalue{\frac{2uv}{\EABS/\Gamma}}^{2}

(2)

where
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
is the CAR-induced current, shown in Fig.
2
e.
The term
uv
is significant only when
\absolutevalue{\mu}
is small, leading to the peak in
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
around
\mu=0
(Fig.
2
e).
This is also where ECT is diminished, allowing CAR to dominate over ECT.
Far away from ABS charge neutrality, ECT decays slower than CAR and becomes the dominant coupling mechanism, as it does not require electron-hole conversion to take place.
The distinct dependencies of CAR/ECT on
\mu
thus enable us to tune the relative strengths between them via electrostatic gating.

To study our model experimentally, we focus on the range of
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
values between 0 and
70\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{V}
where a single ABS dominates the subgap spectrum (Fig.
2
d).
The ABS reaches a minimum around
V_{\mathrm{PG}}=$35\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{V}$
and merges with the superconducting gap below
V_{\mathrm{PG}}=$10\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{V}$
and above
V_{\mathrm{PG}}=$60\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{V}$
.
Fig.
2
f shows
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
and
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
measured in the same
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
range with higher resolution in
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
than
Fig.
1
e. As predicted,
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
features a narrow peak centered around the ABS energy minimum.

I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
is non-zero in a wider range of
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
values and, as predicted, shows a dip when the ABS energy is minimal.
We interpret this suppression as resulting from the destructive interference of the two ECT paths.
We emphasize that this quantum mechanical interference is distinct from the cancellation between electron and hole currents as observed in three-terminal spectroscopy of hybrid nanowires
Menard.2020
;
Poschl.2022
.
Note that, contrary to our theoretical model,
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
is not fully suppressed when
v>u
.
This could be due to other ABSs at higher
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
that contribute to
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
or higher
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
increasing tunneling rates via gate cross-coupling.
Similar observations of the
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
dependence reported here are reproduced in two more devices (Fig.
ED6
and Fig.
ED7
).

Application of a Zeeman field lifts the Kramers’ degeneracy of the ABS and the QDs.
The spin splitting of the QDs makes their charge transitions spin-polarized: the addition energy from
N
to
N+1
electrons becomes lower (spin-down,
\downarrow
), and that from
N+1
to
N+2
becomes higher (spin-up,
\uparrow
)
Hanson.2007
.
We thus control the spins of the electrons participating in CAR and ECT by selecting the corresponding charge transitions
Wang.2022
.
The odd states of the ABS split in energy, leading to two possible excitations from the ground state
\ket{\mathrm{S}}
: either to
\ket{\mathrm{D}\!\downarrow}
with an energy
E_{\downarrow}=E_{\mathrm{ABS}}-E_{\mathrm{Z}}/2
, or to
\ket{\mathrm{D}\!\uparrow}
with an energy
E_{\uparrow}=E_{\mathrm{ABS}}+E_{\mathrm{Z}}/2
, where
E_{\mathrm{Z}}
is the Zeeman splitting of the ABS
Lee.2014
.

Fig.
3
a shows schematically the process of ECT in the presence of a Zeeman field when both QDs are tuned to the
\uparrow
transition.
Again, this process can take place via two paths.
In the first path (marked in grey), an
\uparrow
electron from one lead populates the
\ket{\mathrm{D}\!\uparrow}
state of the ABS.
Then the ABS is emptied by emitting an
\uparrow
electron to the other lead through the QD.
In the second process (marked in green), a hole from one lead hops into the ABS, exciting it into the
\ket{\mathrm{D}\!\downarrow}
state.
The ABS then relaxes by emitting a hole to the other lead.
The energies of the intermediate states in the two paths,
\ket{\mathrm{D}\!\uparrow}
and
\ket{\mathrm{D}\!\downarrow}
, are split and the interference pattern is thus modified.
The ECT-induced current is now of the form:

I_{\mathrm{ECT}}^{\uparrow\uparrow}\propto\absolutevalue{ \frac{u^2}{E_\uparrow} - \frac{v^2}{E_\downarrow} }^{2}

(3)

Since
E_{\downarrow}<E_{\uparrow}
, ECT is stronger when the ABS is hole-like (large
v
) as seen in the
\uparrow\uparrow
panel of Fig.
3
c.
Analogously, the ECT is higher when the ABS is electron-like (
u>v
) and both QDs are tuned to the
\downarrow
transition.

CAR-induced currents are also modified by the Zeeman splitting of the ABS doublet state.
CAR takes place in two paths involving both levels (shown schematically in Fig.
3
b).
In one path (marked in green), the ABS occupies the
\ket{\mathrm{D}\!\downarrow}
state by receiving a
\downarrow
electron from one lead and is emptied by receiving an
\uparrow
electron from the other lead.
In the second path (marked in grey), the order is reversed and the ABS passes through the
\ket{\mathrm{D}\!\uparrow}
state.
The probability for the CAR process is now:

I_{\mathrm{CAR}}^{\uparrow\downarrow}\propto\absolutevalue{ \frac{uv}{E_\downarrow} + \frac{uv}{E_\uparrow} }^{2}

(4)

This probability peaks at the ABS energy minimum, as seen in the relevant panel of Fig.
3
c. Note that the expected CAR peak remains symmetric in
\mu
, in contrast to ECT.
Fig.
3
d shows the measured
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
and
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
under the application of
\absolutevalue{\vec{B}}=$80\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{T}$
along the nanowire direction, sufficient to fully spin-polarize the QDs (
E_{\mathrm{Zeeman}}^{\mathrm{QD}}\approx$200\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
) and split the energy of the ABS (
E_{\mathrm{Z}}\approx$100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
, see Fig.
ED3
).
Spin-orbit coupling in the nanowire allows for spin flipping processes — equal-spin CAR and opposite-spin ECT — to take place

, allowing us to measure ECT and CAR in all possible spin configurations.

I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
is symmetric around the ABS energy minimum and is generally larger for opposite-spin than equal-spin configurations.

I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
in the
\uparrow\uparrow

) and is suppressed when it is electron-like (large

The destructive interference dip is shifted from the ABS minimum towards lower

The opposite trend is observed in the
\downarrow\downarrow

is slightly larger when the ABS is electron-like, and the interference dip is shifted towards higher values of
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
.

I_{\mathrm{ECT}}

I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
in the equal-spin configuration.
Thus, all of the qualitative predictions of the model

\vec{B}
, at fixed
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
.
We measure a second device, B, with a longer superconducting segment (
\approx$350\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
, much larger than the superconducting coherence length in the Al film) and no Pt layer on top of the Al.

and

.
The QDs are set to the
\downarrow\uparrow

is selected such that ECT is stronger than CAR when the field is parallel to the nanowire.

and

Below, we examine the rotational dependence of CAR and ECT along two exemplary meridians of the globe (dashed and dotted lines in panels a to c) in order to separate anisotropy due to ABS energy from that caused by spin.

As discussed above, CAR and ECT amplitudes are inversely proportional to the ABS energy.

,
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
and
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
along the meridian with
\varphi=-50^{\circ}
(dashed line in Fig.
4
a-c).
Here,
E_{\downarrow}
is significantly modulated between
\sim$170\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
and
\sim$50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro}$
and, accordingly, both
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
and
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
increase drastically at the energy minimum.
In contrast, very different pattern can be obtained when we rotate the magnetic field along the meridian of
\varphi=40^{\circ}
(dotted line in Fig.
4
a-c).
Fig.
4
f shows that, along this meridian,
E_{\downarrow}
changes by a small amount.
As before,
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
is enhanced where
E_{\downarrow}
is minimal.
However,
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
varies in the opposite way and becomes completely suppressed around
\theta=0
(perpendicular to the nanowire axis).
This suppression is generic across various
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
values and therefore not explained by either the energy or the charge of the ABS.
We attribute the reduction of opposite-spin ECT along this specific direction to spin blockade
Wang.2022
.
When the QDs select opposite spins, spin precession due to spin-orbit coupling enables the presence of some
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}

Liu.2022
.
However, if the applied
\vec{B}
is parallel to the effective spin-orbit field
\vec{B}_{\mathrm{SO}}
, no spin precession occurs and therefore ECT is suppressed between QDs with opposite spins
Hofmann.2017
;
Wang.2018
.
The observation of this type of spin blockade reveals the orientation of the spin-orbit field.
Compared to prior works measuring the spin-orbit field direction in hybrid nanowires via superconducting gap size anisotropy, the method presented here using spin conservation to detect
\vec{B}_{\mathrm{SO}}
direction is less prone to other effects such as orbital depairing and
g
-factor anisotropy (Fig.
ED9
).

With these two effects in mind, we summarize the angle dependence of CAR and ECT over the entire sphere as follows.
First, there exists one special
\vec{B}
direction along which equal-spin CAR and opposite-spin ECT are strongly suppressed (see Fig.
ED8
for other spin combinations).
We interpret this as a spin-blockade effect and its direction as that of the spin-orbit field.
Away from this blockaded direction, multiple factors compete to influence the amplitudes of CAR and ECT, such as the angle between
\vec{B}
and
\vec{B}_{\mathrm{SO}}
and the energy of the mediating ABS.

This combination of anisotropic ABS energy and spin-orbit coupling makes the
\vec{B}
direction dependence of CAR and ECT very rich, enabling further tuning of their relative amplitudes.
Fig.
4
d shows the ratio between
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
and
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
as a function of
\vec{B}
orientation.
Here, due to the aforementioned influence of the ABS charge,
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
is larger than
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
on most of the sphere.
However, since ECT is suppressed along a specific direction, the ratio between
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
and
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
can be inverted.
Such tunability allows for
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}=I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
, the sweet spot essential for the realization of Poor Man’s Majoranas in a minimal Kitaev chain
Leijnse.2012
;
Dvir.2022
.
Fig.
4
d shows with continuous lines the locus of points where
\frac{I_{\mathrm{ECT}}}{I_{\mathrm{CAR}}}=1
and Fig.
4
f reports the corresponding current values, highlighting with crosses the points where
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}(=I_{\mathrm{ECT}})
is maximal.
It is therefore evident that the
\vec{B}
dependence of CAR and ECT not only enables the tuning to the
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}=I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
sweet spot, but also allows optimization of their strengths.

In summary, we have measured ECT- and CAR-induced currents mediated by ABSs formed in a proximitized InSb nanowire.
We show that the amplitudes of both processes depend on the charge of the ABSs, and are thus highly tunable via electrostatic gating. Particularly, we show that ECT is significantly suppressed when the ABS is charge-neutral due to destructive interference originating from fermionic exchange statistics.
Furthermore, we examine how the interference pattern and the balance between ECT and CAR is shifted when the applied magnetic field spin-polarizes the QDs and splits the energy of the ABS.
Finally, we measure how the magnetic field orientation modifies both the energy of the ABS and the effect of spin-orbit coupling, adding another independent knob to tune CAR and ECT.
These results demonstrate deterministic control of the relative amplitudes of CAR and ECT, forming the foundation of realizing an artificial Kitaev chain
Dvir.2022
.

4.1
Theoretical model

In this section, we show analytically how to obtain the CAR/ECT couplings/currents based on our theoretical model. The calculation is based on Ref.
Liu.2022
, but specialized to the atomic limit.

The model Hamiltonian for the dot-hybrid-dot system is

H=H_{\text{dot}}+H_{\text{hybrid}}+H_{\text{tunnel}}

(1)

We first introduce the Hamiltonian for two quantum dots,
H_{\text{dot}}
:

H_{\text{dot}}=\varepsilon_{L}d^{\dagger}_{L\eta}d_{L\eta}+\varepsilon_{R}d^{\dagger}_{R\sigma}d_{R\sigma}

(2)

Here,
\varepsilon_{L/R}
are the dot energies relative to the Fermi energy, and
d_{L\eta},d_{R\sigma}
denote the spin-polarized dot levels in the presence of a large magnetic field.
Note that no summation is taken over the spin indices
\eta
and
\sigma
.

Next,
H_{\text{hybrid}}
describes the hybrid segment with two Andreev bound states.
In general, we can write down its Hamiltonian in diagonalized form using Bogoliubov quasiparticle operators
\gamma_{+}
and
\gamma_{-}
:

H_{\text{hybrid}}=E_{+}\gamma^{\dagger}_{+}\gamma_{+}+E_{-}\gamma^{\dagger}_{-}\gamma_{-}

(3)

More concretely, we consider a superconducting-atomic-limit model
Bauer.2007
under weak spin-orbit coupling compared to Zeeman field.
This means we use the pseudospin labels
+,-
instead of
\uparrow,\downarrow
but treat the spin-splitting between the two as approximately
E_{\mathrm{Z}}
.
We also set charging energy of the ABS to zero, as it is strongly screened by the grounded Al film in the experiment.
Using electron annihilation operators
a_{+},a_{-}
, the atomic-limit Hamiltonian is

H_{\mathrm{hybrid}}\approx\mu\left(a_{+}^{\dagger}a_{+}+a_{-}^{\dagger}a_{-}\right)+\frac{E_{\mathrm{Z}}}{2}\left(a_{+}^{\dagger}a_{+}-a_{-}^{\dagger}a_{-}\right)+\Gamma a_{+}^{\dagger}a_{-}^{\dagger}+\rm{H.c.}

(4)

This model can be solved exactly. The ABS energies are

\displaystyle E_{\pm}\approx\sqrt{\mu^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}\pm\frac{E_{\mathrm{Z}}}{2},

(5)

where
\mu
is the chemical potential in the hybrid segment which is controlled by the plunger gate voltage
V_{\mathrm{PG}}
in the experimental device,
\Gamma
is the superconducting coupling strength and
E_{\mathrm{Z}}
is the Zeeman spin splitting.
The corresponding wavefunctions of the ABSs are

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}=ua_{+}+va^{\dagger}_{-},\quad\gamma_{-}=va_{+}-ua_{-},

(6)

where
u^{2}=1-v^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}}\right)
are the BCS coherence factors characterizing the electron and hole components of the ABSs.

Finally,
H_{\text{tunnel}}
is the tunnel Hamiltonian between the dots and the hybrid segment:

\displaystyle H_{\text{tunnel}}=\sum_{a=L,R}

\displaystyle t_{a}\left[\left(\cos\theta_{a}a^{\dagger}_{+}-\sin\theta_{a}a^{\dagger}_{-}\right)d_{a\uparrow}+\left(\sin\theta_{a}a^{\dagger}_{+}+\cos\theta_{a}a^{\dagger}_{-}\right)d_{a\downarrow}\right]

\displaystyle+\rm{H.c.}

(7)

Here we only consider the scenario where the spin-orbit field (
\propto\sigma_{y}
) is perpendicular to the globally applied magnetic field (
\propto\sigma_{z}
).
In particular,
\theta_{R}=-\theta_{L}=k_{so}L/2
describes the spin precession in the hybrid region due to spin-orbit interaction, where
k_{so}=m\alpha_{R}/\hbar^{2}
is the spin-orbit wave-vector and
L
is the length of the hybrid segment.
In the weak spin-orbit interaction regime (
k_{so}L/2\ll 1
), the ABS of
\gamma_{+}
is mainly spin-up, but it can also accommodates spin-down QD electrons with amplitude
\sim\sin(k_{so}L/2)
. Similar pictures hold for
\gamma_{-}
as well.
Note that although we include both
\uparrow
and
\downarrow
in
H_{\text{tunnel}}
for dots, in the calculation, we only include one spin species depending on the choice of
\eta
and
\sigma
in
H_{\text{dot}}
, as the experimental measurements are spin-polarized.

In the tunneling regime
t_{L/R}\ll\Gamma
, the effective Hamiltonian of the coupled quantum dots can be obtained using the perturbation theory as below

\displaystyle H_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=H_{\text{dot}}-H_{\text{tunnel}}\frac{1}{H_{\text{hybrid}}}H_{\text{tunnel}}+O(H^{3}_{\text{tunnel}})

\displaystyle\approx H_{\text{dot}}-\Gamma^{\text{ECT}}_{\eta\sigma}d^{\dagger}_{L\eta}d_{R\sigma}-\Gamma^{\text{CAR}}_{\eta\sigma}d_{L\eta}d_{R\sigma}+h.c.,

(8)

where
\Gamma^{\text{CAR}}_{\eta\sigma}
and
\Gamma^{\text{ECT}}_{\eta\sigma}
are the spin-selective CAR and ECT couplings between quantum dots.
Interestingly, as shown in Ref.
Liu.2022
and explained in the main text of this work, the strengths of the effective couplings can be extracted from the resonant current measured in a three-terminal setup, that is

\displaystyle I^{\text{max}}_{\text{CAR/ECT}}\propto\absolutevalue{\Gamma^{\text{CAR/ECT}}_{\eta\sigma}}^{2}.

(9)

In the
\uparrow\uparrow
channel, from Eq. (
8
), the CAR coupling is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\uparrow\uparrow}^{\text{CAR}}/t_{L}t_{R}

\displaystyle=-\left(-\sin\theta_{L}\cos\theta_{R}\frac{uv}{E_{+}}-\sin\theta_{R}\cos\theta_{L}\frac{(-u)v}{E_{-}}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(-\sin\theta_{R}\cos\theta_{L}\frac{uv}{E_{+}}-\sin\theta_{L}\cos\theta_{R}\frac{(-u)v}{E_{-}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sin( \theta_R - \theta_L )\left(\frac{uv}{E_{+}}+\frac{uv}{E_{-}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sin( k_{so}L )\left(\frac{uv}{E_{+}}+\frac{uv}{E_{-}}\right),

(10)

which gives

\displaystyle I_{\uparrow\uparrow}^{\text{CAR}}=I_{0}\cdot\sigma\cdot\absolutevalue{ \frac{uv}{E_+} + \frac{uv}{E_-} }^{2},

(11)

where
\sigma=\sin^{2}(k_{so}L)
is the flipping rate due to spin-orbit interaction, and
I_{0}=et^{2}_{L}t^{2}_{R}/(\hbar\Gamma^{2}\gamma_{DL})
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\uparrow\uparrow}^{\text{ECT}}/t_{L}t_{R}

\displaystyle=\left(\cos\theta_{L}\cos\theta_{R}\frac{u^{2}}{E_{+}}+\sin\theta_{L}\sin\theta_{R}\frac{(-u)^{2}}{E_{-}}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\cos\theta_{L}\cos\theta_{R}\frac{v^{2}}{E_{-}}+\sin\theta_{L}\sin\theta_{R}\frac{v^{2}}{E_{+}}\right)

\displaystyle=\cos(k_{so}L)\left(\frac{u^{2}}{E_{+}}-\frac{v^{2}}{E_{-}}\right)-\sin^{2}(k_{so}L/2)\frac{2E_{\mathrm{Z}}}{E_{+}E_{-}}

giving

\displaystyle I_{\uparrow\uparrow}^{\text{ECT}}\approx I_{0}\cdot(1-\sigma)\cdot\absolutevalue{ \frac{u^2}{E_+} - \frac{v^2}{E_-} }^{2}.

(13)

Here, the ECT current in Eq. (
13
) shows a destructive interference between two virtual paths.
In the first path, an electron first hops from the right dot into the hybrid before it hops out to the left (
\propto u^{2}
).
In the second path, an electron first escapes from the hybrid segment to the left dot, leaving behind a hole-like ABS excitation which is later annihilated when a second electron jumping in from the right dot (
\propto-v^{2}
).
The minus sign responsible for the destructive interference stems from fermionic statistics when switching the order of two hopping events.
In contrast, the CAR current in Eq. (
11
) shows a constructive interference pattern.
Because the CAR process is proportional to
uv
instead of
u^{2}
or
v^{2}
, an additional minus sign in the ABS wavefunctions (see Eq. (
6
)) cancels the minus sign from fermionic statistics, yielding a constructive interference between the two virtual paths.
A similar analysis and calculation gives the current in the
\uparrow\downarrow
channel:

\displaystyle I_{\uparrow\downarrow}^{\text{CAR}}=I_{0}\cdot(1-\sigma)\cdot\absolutevalue{ \frac{uv}{E_+} + \frac{uv}{E_-} }^{2},

\displaystyle I_{\uparrow\downarrow}^{\text{ECT}}=I_{0}\cdot\sigma\cdot\absolutevalue{\frac{\mu}{E_+E_-}}^{2}.

(14)

The currents in the remaining channels are readily obtained using the following symmetry relation
Liu.2022
:

\displaystyle I_{\downarrow\uparrow}^{\text{CAR}}(E_{\mathrm{Z}})=I_{\uparrow\downarrow}^{\text{CAR}}(E_{\mathrm{Z}}),

\displaystyle I_{\downarrow\uparrow}^{\text{ECT}}(E_{\mathrm{Z}})=I_{\uparrow\downarrow}^{\text{ECT}}(E_{\mathrm{Z}}),

\displaystyle I_{\downarrow\downarrow}^{\text{CAR}}(E_{\mathrm{Z}})=I_{\uparrow\uparrow}^{\text{CAR}}(E_{\mathrm{Z}}),

\displaystyle I_{\downarrow\downarrow}^{\text{ECT}}(E_{\mathrm{Z}})=I_{\uparrow\uparrow}^{\text{ECT}}(-E_{\mathrm{Z}}).

(15)

In the absence of Zeeman field and when the dot occupancy is tuned at the transition of
N
to
N+1
, currents from all the four spin channels are allowed, yielding the total current

\displaystyle I^{\text{CAR}}=\sum_{\eta,\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow}I_{\eta\sigma}^{\text{CAR}}(E_{\mathrm{Z}}=0)=2\cdot I_{0}\cdot\absolutevalue{\frac{2uv}{E}}^{2},

\displaystyle I^{\text{ECT}}=\sum_{\eta,\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow}I_{\eta\sigma}^{\text{ECT}}(E_{\mathrm{Z}}=0)=2\cdot I_{0}\cdot\absolutevalue{\frac{u^2 - v^2}{E}}^{2},

(16)

where
E=\sqrt{\mu^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}
.

4.2.3
Extraction of CAR and ECT amplitudes

ECT-induced currents are measured with fixed voltage biases such that
V_{\mathrm{L}}\neq V_{\mathrm{R}}
.
Due to energy conservation, ECT-induced currents arise when
V_{\mathrm{LD}}
and
V_{\mathrm{RD}}
fulfill the condition that the chemical potentials of both QDs are aligned and within the bias window (shown schematically in Fig.
ED1
b). ECT-induced current is detected as correlated current flowing from one lead to the other.
Similarly, CAR-induced currents are measured with fixed voltage biases such that
V_{\mathrm{L}}\neq-V_{\mathrm{R}}
. CAR-induced current arise when the QD chemical potentials are equal in magnitude with an opposite sign with respect to the Fermi energy (shown schematically in Fig.
ED1
a). CAR currents flow jointly from the leads to the superconductor or vice-versa.
For each CAR and ECT measurement,
V_{\mathrm{LD}}
and
V_{\mathrm{RD}}
are swept around a charge degeneracy point of each dot. We measure
I_{\mathrm{L}}
and
I_{\mathrm{R}}
and calculate the correlated current
I_{\mathrm{corr}}\equiv\mathrm{sign}\left(I_{\mathrm{L}}I_{\mathrm{R}}\right)\sqrt{\absolutevalue{ \IL\IR}}
.
In this manuscript, as in
Wang.2022
, the maximum of
I_{\mathrm{corr}}
is taken as a proxy of the CAR strength
I_{\mathrm{CAR}}\equiv\max(I_{\mathrm{corr}})
, and minus the minimum of
I_{\mathrm{corr}}
is taken as a proxy of the ECT strength
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}\equiv-\min(I_{\mathrm{corr}})
. Notice that in the absence of CAR or ECT signal,
\max(I_{\mathrm{corr}})
and
-\min(I_{\mathrm{corr}})
return the background noise extrema. Background noise level is
\sim$30\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{A}$
for device A and
\sim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{A}$
for devices B and C. We note that, instead of taking the bare maximum and minimum of
I_{\mathrm{corr}}
, averaging procedures can improve the signal-to-noise ratio
Wang.2022.2DEG
, although come at the price of having to set an arbitrary threshold.
Every CAR data point in Fig.
1
e,
2
f,
3
d is taken from a
V_{\mathrm{LD}}
-
V_{\mathrm{RD}}
sweep with symmetric biases, while every ECT data point is taken from a subsequent
V_{\mathrm{LD}}
-
V_{\mathrm{RD}}
sweep with anti-symmetric biases.
Every data point of Fig.
4
a-b comes from a single
V_{\mathrm{LD}}
-
V_{\mathrm{RD}}
sweep with finite
V_{\mathrm{L}}>0
while
V_{\mathrm{R}}=0
; in this case, positive
\mu_{\mathrm{LD}}
allows for CAR and negative
\mu_{\mathrm{LD}}
allows for ECT.
For every CAR and ECT measurement, we make sure that the bias voltages
V_{\mathrm{L}}
and
V_{\mathrm{R}}
are smaller than the ABS energy.

I_{\mathrm{CAR}}
,
I_{\mathrm{ECT}}
and
E_{\downarrow}
values along specific lines shown in Fig.
4

4

Superconducting gap dependence on magnetic field direction.

Local tunneling spectroscopy as in Fig.

c but at negative superconducting gate:

Since no ABSs are present at this

value, this is a direct measurement of the hybrid superconducting gap as a function of magnetic field orientation.

\absolutevalue{\vec{B}}=$150\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{T}$
.
Notably, the direction along which the gap is reduced the most is different from that along which the ABS of Fig.
4
c reaches its energy minimum.
The gap-suppression direction in this strongly metallized regime
Mazur.2022
is likely where orbital depairing in the Al film is the strongest, considering the size of
\absolutevalue{\vec{B}}
and that it is the angle that maximizes the flux incident on the Al-covered facets.
Superficially, previous work on hybrid nanowires has also observed maximal gap suppression along similar angles and interpreted it as the measured spin-orbit direction
Bommer.2019
.
However, the analysis there relied on the superconducting film made of NbTiN experiencing almost no orbital depairing along all magnetic field directions.
The same interpretation is not valid in the case of Al here and thus gap spectroscopy cannot be used to measure the effect of spin-orbit coupling.
Therefore, using CAR and ECT to measure the spin-orbit direction is less prone to complications by orbital effects compared to gap-size spectroscopy.
