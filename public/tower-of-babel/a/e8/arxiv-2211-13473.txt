1
Introduction

We introduce and study a class of communication problems parameterized by a norm
N
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
— we will denote those problems as
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
(for
inner product
). Here two parties (traditionally called Alice and Bob) are given two vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively
v
and
w
, with guarantees that
\|v\|_{N}\leq 1
and
\|w\|_{N^{*}}\leq 1
, where
N^{*}
is the
dual norm
defined as
\|w\|_{N^{*}}:=\sup_{\|v\|_{N}\leq 1}\left\langle v,w\right\rangle
. Those players wish to use small amount of communication to compute
\left\langle v,w\right\rangle
. We will focus exclusively on randomized, approximate protocols for
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
— for given
\varepsilon,\delta
we denote by
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{N})
the smallest communication cost of a private-coin protocol computing some function
f(x,y)
such that
|f(u,v)-\left\langle v,w\right\rangle|\leq\varepsilon
with probability
1-\delta
. Note that, by the definition of the dual norm we always have
|\left\langle v,w\right\rangle|\leq\|v\|_{N}\|w\|_{N^{*}}\leq 1
, making this a natural normalization for the approximate version of the problem.

As it turns out this setting is not only very natural, but also quite powerful — it is a common generalization of few seemingly different problems that have been studied in communication complexity on their own right, and are of tremendous importance in the field. We will discuss some of these connections in the next few paragraps. To start with a simple, yet consequential example, consider a finite universe
\mathcal{U}
, and a scenario where Alice has an arbitrary probability distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{U}
and Bob has a bounded function
f:\mathcal{U}\to[-1,1]
, and they wish to compute
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}f(x)\pm\varepsilon
. The problem they are solving is equivalent to
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{1}}
. As it turns out, there is an easy protocol for this problem using
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log|\mathcal{U}|}{\varepsilon^{2}})
communication — it is enough for Alice to sample
\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
elements from the distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and send their names to Bob.

Gap Hamming Distance problem and
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{2}}
.

The Gap Hamming Distance problem has been introduced by Indyk and Woodruff in order to study the space complexity of the streaming
Distinct-Elements
problem
[
IW03
]
. Here, Alice and Bob have two vectors
x,y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, and they wish to distinguish whether
\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\leq-\varepsilon n
or
\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\geq+\varepsilon n
.

After its introduction, this problem turned out to be extremely convenient as a reduction target — communication complexity lower bound for the Gap Hamming Distance implies space lower bounds for numerous streaming and data structures problems — and often optimal ones (we refer to the monograph
[
Rou16
]
for a detailed exposition of those consequences). As such, understanding the communication complexity of this problem has become very important — a sequence of lower bounds for followed
[
Woo04
,
JKS08
,
Woo09
,
BC09
,
BCR+10
]
, culminating in the optimal
\Omega(\min(\varepsilon^{-2},n))
lower bound in the public-coin model by Chakrabarti and Regev
[
CR11
]
— subsequently simplified and expanded upon
[
She12
,
Vid11
]
.

Quite clearly, the Gap-Hamming-Distance problem is in fact a special case of the
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{2}}
problem — in particular the
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2})
lower bound for Gap Hamming Distance readily implies that
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{2}})\geq\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2})
.

From the upper-bound perspective, the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
protocol in the public-coin model is very simple for the Gap Hamming Distance, implying
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}+\log n)
protocol in the private coin model, by Newmans theorem
[
New91
]
— it is enough for both parties to uniformly subsample
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
coordinated and evaluate the inner product on this subset of coordinates. That was understood throughout the work on the Gap Hamming Distance.

In general, if
x,y
are not restricted to the binary vectors, but only are restricted to be bounded in
\ell_{2}
norm, the ideas of Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projections
[
Vem05
]
can provide a protocol with communication complexity
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
. Specifically, when
\|u\|_{2}=\|v\|_{2}=1
both Alice and Bob will use a common random projection on a subspace of dimension
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}})
— the inner product between their two vectors is preserved up to an additive error
\varepsilon
. In fact, as noticed by Canonne et. al. the space complexity of this protocol can be improved again to
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}+\log n)

[
CGMS17
]
— if Alice and Bob can use shared randomness, it is enough for them to draw
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}})
random Gaussian vectors
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots g_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and compare how often
\textnormal{sgn}(\left\langle g_{i},x\right\rangle)=\textnormal{sgn}(\left\langle g_{i},y\right\rangle)
. The additive
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
term comes again from the Newman’s theorem, in order to simulate this protocol in the private coin model.

Quantum Communication Complexity.

Another impactful example of a well-studied problem that can be phrased as
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
for some norm
N
is a complete problem for
quantum communication complexity
(interested reader can look at
[
Aar13
,
Kre95
]
for relevant definitions and discussion). Specifically, let us consider the communication problem where Alice is given a symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with bounded operator norm, and Bob a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|v\|_{2}=1
, and they wish to compute
v^{T}Av\pm\varepsilon
. With the proper definitions in hand, it is not difficult to see that this problem has a quantum protocol with complexity
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log d}{\varepsilon^{2}})
, and on the other hand if one has a classical protocol for this problem, one can use it as a black-box to simulate arbitrary quantum communication protocol
[
Kre95
]
. As such, understanding its communication complexity has drawn significant attention: in particular Raz showed that it has a protocol with complexity
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})

[
Raz99
]
, whereas Klartag and Regev
[
KR11
]
proved
\Omega(d^{1/3})
lower bound. As it turns out, this problem can be easily seen to be equivalent to the
\mathrm{IP}_{\mathcal{S}_{\infty}}
, where
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
is an operator norm on the space of symmetric
d\times d
matrices over
\mathbb{R}
. In short, to reduce the evaluation of the quadratic form to the
\mathrm{IP}_{\mathcal{S}_{\infty}}
, Bob given vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
can construct a matrix
vv^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, such that
\left\langle A,vv^{T}\right\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}A^{T}vv^{T}=v^{T}Av
. It can be shown that
\|vv^{T}\|_{\mathcal{S}_{1}}=1
where
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is a
Shatten 1-norm
, dual to the operator norm. The other reduction is also relatively simple.

Extension Complexity.

In the seminal and celebrated work Yannakakis
[
Yan91
]
defined the
extension complexity
of a convex polytope: extension complexity of
P
, denoted by
\mathrm{xc}(P)
is the smallest number of inequalities needed to specify some higher-dimensional polytope
Q
, such that
P
is a linear projection of
Q
. He showed a striking connection between extension complexity of a polytope
P
and communication complexity measures of the so-called
slack matrix
of
P
— opening a road to a number of beautiful results showing non-conditional lower bounds for extension complexity of various explicit polytopes of interests, using communication complexity methods (see
[
GJW18
,
Rou16
]
as well as
[
CCZ14
, Chapter 4.10]
and references therein).

If a polytope
P
is origin-symmetric (i.e. satisfying
P=-P
), there is a unique norm (which we denote as
\|\cdot\|_{P}
by a slight abuse of notation) for which
P
is a unit ball. We observe that a short direct argument can be used to prove
R_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log\mathrm{xc}(P)}{\varepsilon^{2}})
— norms associated with polytopes that have small extension complexity admit efficient communication protocols for the
\mathrm{IP}_{P}
problem. More interestingly, by contrapositive, lower bounds for communication complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{P}
can be used to obtain lower bounds for extension complexity of
P
.

1.1
Our results

We initiate a systematic study of the
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
problem, and in particular focus on one-way and two-way communication complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
for symmetric norms
N
in a private-coin model. We show that the two-way communication complexity for symmetric norm is small,
\textnormal{poly}(\log n,\varepsilon^{-1})
. In contrast, we show that one-way communication complexity is (nearly completely) determined by whether the norm contains up to small distortion
\ell_{\infty}^{k}
— the
k
-dimensional space equipped with the
\ell_{\infty}
norm. Finally, we show connections to extension complexity, which holds for arbitrary (non necessarily symmetric) norms.

Communication Complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}}
.

Given the importance of some of the communication problems
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
, it is natural to ask whether we can characterize the communication complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
in terms of geometric properties of the norm
N
. This is quite an ambitious task: to our knowledge even the complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}}
has not been studied before, except for
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
, and it is not intuitive at a first glance what answer should we expect for those norms.

It turns out to be quite easy to see that
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{1}})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
— if the vector
v
with
\|v\|_{1}=1
is non-negative Alice can treat it as probability distribution over
[n]
and sample
\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
coordinates from this probability distribution. In the general case, they can apply the same protocol separately for the negative and positive part of the vector
v
.

Interestingly, the first protocol that comes to mind for the
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{2}}
is using Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma — if Alice and Bob share public randomness, they can use a projection on random subspace of dimension
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
. With some care this idea can be used to get a
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{pub}}_{\varepsilon,\frac{1}{3}}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{2}})\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})

[
CGMS17
]
, and by Newmans theorem
[
New91
]

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\frac{1}{3}}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{2}})\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}+\log n)
. Unfortunately, these two protocols for
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{1}}
and
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{2}}
seem to be very different — and it is not clear if they can be used to obtain an efficient protocol for
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}}
for general
p
. As it turns out, the first of the aforementioned protocols — the coordinate-sampling based protocol — can actually be generalized, leading to the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

For any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we have
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\frac{1}{3}}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
.

On the other hand a simple reduction from the
Gap-Hamming
problem can be used to show an almost matching lower bound
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}})\geq\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2})
for any
\varepsilon\geq\Omega(n^{-1/2})
(
Lemma

26
).

Communication Complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
for symmetric norms.

The next step in the quest of trying to characterize the complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
in terms of the geometry of the normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{N})
is to consider a wide class of
symmetric norm
— norms that are invariant under permutation of coordinates and negation of any coordinate — for example all the
\ell_{p}
norms are symmetric according to this definition.

As it turns out, Andoni et. al
[
ANN+17
]
showed that any finite dimensional normed space equipped with a symmetric norm has
near-isometric embedding
into a relatively “simple” (or at least explicit) normed space of only polynomially higher dimension (see Section
5
and specifically
Theorem

29
for details).

Definition 2
.

We say that a normed
(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})

embeds into

(Y,\|\cdot\|_{Y})
with distortion
D
if and only if there is a linear mapping
i:X\to Y
, such that

\forall x\in X\qquad\|i(x)\|_{Y}\leq\|x\|_{X}\leq D\|i(x)\|_{Y}.

We will use notation
X\hookrightarrow^{D}Y
to signify that
X
embeds in
Y
with distortion
D
.

Perhaps surprisingly, it is not immediately obvious that such an embedding is useful for designing an efficient protocol for our problem
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
— in contrast with the Approximate Nearest Neighbours Problem for which this technique was developed. Specifically, let us consider a norm
N
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, such that
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
problem already has an efficient protocol, and a linear subspace
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with an induced norm
N|_{U}
— restriction of the norm
N
to the subspace
U
. Can we use the protocol for
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
to obtain an efficient protocol for
\mathrm{IP}_{N|_{U}}
? Alice, with a vector
u\in U
can treat it as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but what should Bob do with his vector in
U^{*}
(equipped with the norm dual to
N|_{U}
)? This dual space cannot be interpreted in a natural way as a subspace of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},N^{*})
. As it turns out,
U^{*}
is actually isometric with the quotient
U^{*}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/U^{\perp}
(where
U^{\perp}:=\{v:\forall u\in U,\left\langle u,v\right\rangle=0\}
), and importantly the unit ball of a dual norm
(N|_{U})^{*}
is just an image of the ball for the norm
N^{*}
under the projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}/U^{\perp}
— this is essentially a finite dimensional Hahn-Banach theorem (or equivalently separating hyperplane theorem). We can use this fact to show that
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{N|_{U}})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{N})
— Alice can just treat her vector
v\in U
as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with norm
\|\cdot\|_{N}
, whereas Bob given a vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}/U^{\perp}
can find a vector
\tilde{w}\in\pi^{-1}(w)
such that
\|\tilde{w}\|_{N^{*}}=\|w\|_{(N|_{U})^{*}}
. Using the protocol for
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
they can approximate
\left\langle v,\tilde{w}\right\rangle
, which is equal to
\left\langle v,w\right\rangle
by the choice of
\tilde{w}
.

It turns out that handling embeddings with distortion
D\geq 1
does not provide any additional difficulty, and we can prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

If
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|_{X})\hookrightarrow^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{Y})
, then for any
\varepsilon,\delta
we have
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{X})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon\alpha^{-1},\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{Y})
.

With this proposition in hand, one way to show that there is an efficient protocol for
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
for any symmetric norm
N
, is just to provide an efficient protocol for the explicit target space of the aforementioned embedding. We actually managed to implement this plan, showing the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

For any symmetric norm
N
, we have
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{N})\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-6})\log n
.

One-way communication and sparsification for
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
for symmetric norms.

An interesting feature of the simple protocol for
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{1}}
is that it is a
one-way protocol
: instead of full bidirectional communication, Alice is the only party sending single message to Bob based on her vector (i.e. she sends a multiset of coordinates sampled according to the probability distribution specified by
v
), and Bob can report the answer right away, based on the message he received and his own vector
w
. We will denote by
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{N})
the one-way communication complexity for the problem
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
up to additive error
\varepsilon
, with probability
1-\delta
.

In fact, the protocol for
\ell_{1}
is a special kind of one-way protocol, which we will call
sparsification
for
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
— Alice, based on her vector
v
, can produce a random sparse vector
\phi(v)
, such that for any fixed
w
that Bob could have, with probability
2/3
we have
\left\langle\phi(v),w\right\rangle=\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\pm\varepsilon
, and just send an encoding of this sparse vector to Bob.

In what follows let
B_{N}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x\|_{N}\leq 1\}
be the unit ball for a norm
N
.

Definition 5
.

We say that a norm
N
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
admits a
(\varepsilon,\delta,D)
-sparsification, if there exist a randomized mapping
\phi:B_{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, such that

•

For any
v\in B_{N}
, and any
w\in B_{N^{*}}
we have
\left\langle\phi(v),w\right\rangle=\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\pm\varepsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

•

For any
v
, we always have
\|\phi(v)\|_{0}\leq D
(i.e.
\phi(v)
has at most
D
non-zero coordinates).

It is not difficult to see that if a norm
N
admits an
(\varepsilon,\delta,D)
-sparsification, then
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{N})\leq\mathcal{O}(D\cdot\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon})
(see
Proposition

27
), but such a sparsification can potentially be much more useful than arbitrary one-way protocol — in particular, note that Bob in order to compute his answer does not have to access the entire vector
w
, just
D
of its coordinates.

As it turns out, the protocol for
\ell_{p}
norms is actually achieved via such sparsification.

Theorem 6
.

For any
1\leq p<\infty
, and any
\varepsilon>0
, the norm
\ell_{p}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
admits a
(\varepsilon,1/3,\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-\max(2,p)}))
-sparsification.
In particular,
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}})\leq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-\max(2,p)}\cdot\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon})
.

Interestingly, the guaranteed sparsity in the theorem above does not depend on the ambient dimension
n
; for a fixed
p
and
\varepsilon
, we could approximate
\left\langle v,w\right\rangle
by accessing only constant number of coordinates of
w
.

In fact it is not difficult to show that this sparsification result is almost optimal. A direct reduction from the
index
problem can be used to show
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\ell_{\infty})\geq\Omega(n)
for any
\varepsilon<1
. Since Proposition
3
has a direct analog for one-way communication complexity (with the same proof), we have as a consequence

Proposition 7
.

For any norm
N
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\ell_{\infty})\hookrightarrow^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},N)
, then
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{N})\geq\Omega(k)
.

We use this, together with known embeddings of low-dimensional
\ell_{\infty}
into
\ell_{p}
, to deduce lower bounds
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,2/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}})\geq\Omega(\varepsilon^{-\max(2,p)})
— nearly matching (up to the
\log n
multiplicative factor) the upper bound from Theorem
6
obtained via sparsification.

Perhaps surprisingly, among all symmetric norms
N
, embedding of
\ell_{\infty}^{k}
with small distortion is the only obstruction for a good sparsification of a norm — we prove the following weak converse of Proposition
7
.

Theorem 8
.

If
N
is a symmetric norm such that
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\ell_{\infty})\not\hookrightarrow^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},N)
, then
N
admits a
(\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),1/3,k^{\mathcal{O}(\log\log k)}\log^{2}n)
-sparsification.

This theorem is in fact the most technically challenging part of this paper, as well as most unexpected — while Proposition
7
says that existence of an embedding implies a communication complexity lower bound, which is relatively common phenomenon, here we claim to be able to provide an efficient protocol given
non-existence
of any embedding from one space to another (or, by contrapositive, claim an existence of embedding assuming non-existence of efficient protocol).

Theorem
8
together with Proposition
7
essentially say that the one-way communication complexity of any symmetric norm is characterized by the possibility of finding low-distortion embedding of finite-dimensional
\ell_{\infty}
spaces into this norm.

We believe that both the weak dependency on
\log n
and the unfortunate dependency on
k
are just artifacts of our analysis. In fact, we conjecture that

Conjecture 9
.

If
N
is a symmetric norm such that
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\ell_{\infty})\not\hookrightarrow^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},N)
, then
N
admits an
(\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),1/3,k^{\mathcal{O}(1)})
-sparsification.

Connections with extension complexity.

For any origin symmetric convex polytope
P
(i.e. satisfying
P=-P
), we can define the associated norm
\|w\|_{P}:=\inf\{\lambda:w/\lambda\in P\}
— this is a unique norm for which
P
is a unit ball. As mentioned earlier, it is easy to directly show the following relation between extension complexity of
P
and communication complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{P}
.

Proposition 10
.

For any origin symmetric convex polytope
P
we have
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log\mathrm{xc}(P)}{\varepsilon^{2}})
.

We can sketch the proof in a single paragraph — first by dual of the Proposition
3
, we show that if
P
is a projection of a polytope
Q
, then
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q})
, by symmetry
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q})=\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q^{*}})
, and finally with a sampling based argument we can show that if the unit ball for a norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q^{*}}
has at most
M
vertices, then
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q^{*}})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log M}{\varepsilon^{2}})
— this step is essentially the same as the proof that
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{1}})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}})
. Finally vertices of the dual polytope
Q^{*}
correspond exactly to facets of
Q
.

For a convex polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, specified by matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times n}
via
P=\{v:\forall i\leq M,\left\langle A_{i},v\right\rangle\leq b_{i}\}
, we define the
slack matrix

S_{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times M}
with rows indexed by vertices
V
of the polytope
P
, to be
(S_{P})_{v,i}:=b_{i}-\left\langle A_{i},v\right\rangle
— clearly this matrix is non-negative. Yannakakis showed that
\mathrm{xc}(P)=\mathrm{rk}^{+}(S_{P})+\mathcal{O}(1)
, where
\mathrm{rk}^{+}(S)
is a
non-negative rank
of
S
— i.e. the smallest number
k
such that we can decompose
S=\sum_{i\leq k}v_{i}w_{i}^{T}
for coordinatewise non-negative vectors
v_{i},w_{i}

[
Yan91
]
. Therefore, Proposition
10
can be equivalently stated as bounding the communication complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{P}
by the non-negative rank of the corresponding slack matrix, i.e.
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log\mathrm{rk}^{+}(S_{P})}{\varepsilon^{2}})
.

It turns out that
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})
is in turn lower bounded (up to the dependency on
\varepsilon
) by a closely related quantity, the
approximate non-negative rank
of the same matrix
S_{P}
.

Definition 11
.

[
KMSY14
]

For a non-negative matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, we define
approximate non-negative rank
as

\widetilde{\mathrm{rk}^{+}_{\varepsilon}}(S):=\inf_{S^{\prime}:\forall_{ij}|S_{ij}-S^{\prime}_{ij}|\leq\varepsilon}\mathrm{rk}^{+}(S^{\prime}).

Clearly
\widetilde{\mathrm{rk}^{+}_{\varepsilon}}(S)\leq\mathrm{rk}^{+}(S)
. We will show in Section
7
that this former quantity turns out to lower bound the communication complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{P}
.

Proposition 12
.

For any origin-symmetric convex polytope
P
we have

\widetilde{\mathrm{rk}^{+}_{2\varepsilon}}(S_{P})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})\leq\mathcal{O}(\log\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})).

Preliminaries and notation

All the results and notations in this section are standard.

In the sequel we write
w=v\pm\varepsilon
to indicate that
|w-v|\leq\varepsilon
. We will use notation
A\lesssim B
to denote that there exist a universal constant
K
such that
A\leq KB
.

For a norm
N
, we write
B_{N}:=\{v:\|v\|_{N}\leq 1\}
to denote the unit ball with respect to norm
N
, and if
N=\ell_{p}
we usually abbreviate
B_{p}
instead of
B_{\ell_{p}}
.

We will also use
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
notation to supress polylogartihmic factors, i.e.
g=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(f)
if and only if
g=\mathcal{O}(f\log^{\mathcal{O}(1)}f)
.

2.1
Symmetric norms

The following notion of symmetric norm will be used throughout the paper.

Definition 13
.

A norm
N
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
symmetric norm
if and only if it satisfies the following two properties:

1.

Invariant under permutations:
\|v\|_{N}=\|\pi(v)\|_{N}
for any permutation
\pi:[n]\to[n]
, where
\pi(v)
is a vector with
\pi(v)_{i}=v_{\pi(i)}
.

2.

Invariant under coordinate negation:
\|v\|_{N}=\||v|\|_{N}
where
|v|
is a vector with
|v|_{i}=|v_{i}|
.

We will deal only with symmetric norms normalized as
\|e_{1}\|_{N}=1
for a standard basis vector
e_{1}
, and this normalization will not be mentioned explicitly anymore.

Claim 14
.

Any symmetric norm is coordinate-wise monotone: if vectors
v
and
w
satisfy
\forall_{i}|v_{i}|\leq|w_{i}|
, then
\|v\|_{N}\leq\|w\|_{N}
.

Proof.

It is enough to show this inequality in the special case where
v_{i}=w_{i}
for
i\geq 2
, and
|v_{1}|\leq|w_{1}|
. In this case, we can define
w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
w^{\prime}_{1}:=-w_{1}
, and
w^{\prime}_{i}:=w_{i}
for
i\geq 2
. Clearly,
\|w^{\prime}\|_{N}=\|w\|_{N}
, and moreover
v
is a convex combination of
w
and
w^{\prime}
:
v=\lambda w+(1-\lambda)w^{\prime}
where
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{|v_{1}|}{2|w_{1}|}
. Hence, by triangle inequality
\|v\|_{N}\leq\lambda\|w\|_{N}+(1-\lambda)\|w^{\prime}\|_{N}=\|w\|_{N}

∎

2.2
Probabilistic Tools

For an
\mathbb{R}
-valued random variable
Z
and
p\geq 1
, we will consider the
p
-norm
\|Z\|_{p}:=(\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p})^{1/p}
, and the weak
p
-norm:

\|Z\|_{p,\infty}:=\sup_{\lambda}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(|Z|\geq\lambda)^{1/p}\lambda.

Fact 15

(Monotonicity of
L_{p}
)
.

For
1\leq p\leq p^{\prime}\leq\infty
, we have
\|Z\|_{p}\leq\|Z\|_{p^{\prime}}
and
\|Z\|_{p,\infty}\leq\|Z\|_{p^{\prime},\infty}
.

Markov inequality says that
\|Z\|_{p,\infty}\leq\|Z\|_{p}
, and this can be weakly inverted:

Lemma 16
.

For any
1\leq p^{\prime}<p
, we have

\|Z\|_{p^{\prime}}\leq\left(\frac{p}{p-p^{\prime}}\right)^{1/p^{\prime}}\|Z\|_{p,\infty}

Proof.

We assume that
\|Z\|_{p,\infty}<\infty
, because otherwise the inequality is trivial.

Integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf E\/}|Z|^{p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(|Z|\geq\lambda)\lambda^{p^{\prime}}+p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{p^{\prime}-1}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(|Z|\geq\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

We can bound
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(|Z|\geq\lambda)\lambda^{p^{\prime}}\leq\|Z\|_{p,\infty}^{p}\lambda^{p^{\prime}-p}\to^{\lambda\to\infty}0
.

For the second term

\displaystyle p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{p^{\prime}-1}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(|Z|\geq\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\leq p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{p^{\prime}-1}\min(1,\|Z\|_{p,\infty}^{p}\lambda^{-p})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\leq p^{\prime}\left(\int_{0}^{\|Z\|_{p,\infty}}\lambda^{p^{\prime}-1}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\|Z\|_{p,\infty}^{p}\int_{\|Z\|_{p,\infty}}^{\infty}\lambda^{p^{\prime}-1-p}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\leq p^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\|Z\|_{p,\infty}^{p^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{p-p^{\prime}}\|Z\|_{p,\infty}^{p^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{p-p^{\prime}}\|Z\|_{p,\infty}^{p^{\prime}}.

∎

The following lemma is also standard, we include the proof for completeness.

Lemma 17
.

Let
1\leq q\leq 2
, and
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots Z_{s}
be independent random variables in
\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\mathop{\bf E\/}Z_{i}=0
. Then

\|\sum_{i}Z_{i}\|_{q}\lesssim(\sum_{i}\|Z_{i}\|_{q}^{q})^{1/q}.

In particular, if
Z_{i}
are i.i.d., we have
\|\sum_{i\leq s}Z_{i}\|_{q}\lesssim s^{1/q}\|Z_{1}\|_{q}
.

Proof.

Let
Z^{\prime}_{i}
be all independent, and
Z^{\prime}_{i}
distributed identically as
Z_{i}
, and let
\varepsilon_{i}
be again independent Rademacher random variables. We have

\|\sum_{i}Z_{i}\|_{q}\overset{(1)}{=}\|\sum_{i}Z_{i}-\mathop{\bf E\/}Z_{i}\|_{q}\overset{(2)}{\leq}\|\sum_{i}Z_{i}-Z^{\prime}_{i}\|_{q}\overset{(3)}{=}\|\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}(Z_{i}-Z^{\prime}_{i})\|_{q}\overset{(4)}{\leq}2\|\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}Z_{i}\|_{q}.

The
(1)
equality follows as
Z_{i}
are mean
0
.
For the
(2)
inequality, since
q\geq 1
, the function
x^{q}
is convex, we can pull out the inner expectation (denote
Z=\sum Z_{i}
, and
Z^{\prime}=\sum Z_{i}^{\prime}
, then by Jensen we have
\|Z-\mathbb{E}_{Z^{\prime}}Z^{\prime}\|_{q}=(\mathbb{E}_{Z}(\mathbb{E}_{Z^{\prime}}[Z-Z^{\prime}])^{q})^{1/q}\leq(\mathbb{E}_{Z}\mathbb{E}_{Z^{\prime}}[(Z-Z^{\prime})^{q}]))^{1/q}=\|Z-\mathbb{E}_{Z^{\prime}}Z^{\prime}\|_{q}
).
For equality
(3)
, note that since
Z_{i}
and
Z_{i}^{\prime}
have the same distribution and are independent, then distribution of
Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}
is symmetric. I.e.
Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime}
has the same distribution as
-(Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime})=Z_{i}^{\prime}-Z_{i}
. In particular,
also
\varepsilon_{i}(Z_{i}-Z_{i}^{\prime})
has also the same distribution.
Finally inequality
(4)
is just a triangle inequality.

The bound
\|\sum\varepsilon_{i}Z_{i}\|_{q}\leq(\sum\|Z_{i}\|_{q}^{q})^{1/q}
is just the type-
q
inequality for the norm
L_{q}
. To see why it is true, we can condition on
Z_{i}
and apply the Khintchine inequality (which says that for fixed numbers
a_{1},\ldots a_{n}
and independent Rademacher random variables
\varepsilon_{i}
, we have
\mathop{\bf E\/}\left\langle\varepsilon,a\right\rangle^{q}\lesssim(\sum a_{i}^{2})^{q/2})
to get

(\mathop{\bf E\/}\left\langle\varepsilon,Z\right\rangle^{q})^{1/q}=(\mathop{\bf E\/}_{Z}\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\varepsilon}\left\langle\varepsilon,Z\right\rangle^{q})^{1/q}\lesssim(\mathop{\bf E\/}_{Z}(\sum Z_{i}^{2})^{q/2})^{1/q},

Since
q\leq 2
, we have
(\sum Z_{i}^{2})^{q/2}\leq\sum Z_{i}^{q}
, hence

\|\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}Z_{i}\|_{q}\lesssim(\sum_{i}\mathop{\bf E\/}Z_{i}^{q})^{1/q}.

∎

2.3
Duality

Lemma 18

(Finite dimensional Hahn-Banach theorem)
.

Consider a normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{N})
, and a subspace
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the induced norm given by the restriction of
\|\cdot\|_{N}
to this subspace.

Then the dual
V^{*}
is isometric with the quotient
\mathbb{R}^{n}/V^{\perp}
, with the norm given by
\|w+V^{\perp}\|_{V^{*}}=\min_{v^{\perp}\in V^{\perp}}\|w+v^{\perp}\|_{N^{*}}
.

In other words: for any vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\max_{v\in V\cap B_{N}}\left\langle v,w\right\rangle=\min_{v^{\perp}\in V^{\perp}}\|w+v^{\perp}\|_{N^{*}}
.

Proof.

As usual in the duality statement, one inequality is easy: clearly, for any
v\in V\cap B_{N}
, and any
v^{\perp}\in V^{\perp}
, we have

\left\langle v,w\right\rangle=\left\langle v,w+v^{\perp}\right\rangle\leq\|v\|_{N}\|w+v^{\perp}\|_{N^{*}}\leq\|w+v^{\perp}\|_{N^{*}},

so

\max_{v\in V\cap B_{N}}\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\leq\min_{v^{\perp}\in V^{\perp}}\|w+v^{\perp}\|_{N^{*}}.

For the other direction, consider a projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}/V^{\perp}
. Take a vector
w
such that
\max_{v\in V\cap B_{N}}\left\langle v,w\right\rangle=1
, and assume for the sake of reaching a contradiction that
\min_{v^{\perp}\in V^{\perp}}\|w+v^{\perp}\|_{N^{*}}>1
. Equivalently, this is saying that
\pi(w)\not\in\pi(B_{N^{*}})
, hence by a separating hyperplane theorem we can find a hyperplane separating
\pi(w)
from
\pi(B_{N^{*}})
. Such a hyperplane will be parametrized by
r\in V
, say with
\|r\|_{N}=1
, s.t.
\left\langle r,w\right\rangle>\max_{w^{\prime}\in B_{N^{*}}}\left\langle v,w^{\prime}\right\rangle=\|r\|_{N}=1
. But this is a contradiction with
\max_{v\in V\cap B_{N}}\left\langle v,w\right\rangle=1
.
∎

The following is basically a restatement of the more involved direction in the above duality.

Claim 19
.

If
p:X\to Y
is an isometric embedding, then for any
w\in X^{*}
we can find
w^{\prime}\in Y^{*}
, with
\|w^{\prime}\|_{Y^{*}}=\|w\|_{X^{*}}
, s.t. for any
x\in X
we have
\left\langle p(x),w^{\prime}\right\rangle=\left\langle x,w\right\rangle
.

Remark 20
.

Note that the map which sends
w\in X^{*}
to
w^{\prime}\in Y^{*}
described above is usually not linear.

For a convex, origin-symmetric body
K\subset V
, we define a dual body
K^{*}\subset V^{*}
as
K^{*}:=\{w:\forall v\in K,\,\left\langle w,v\right\rangle\leq 1\}
. If
N
is a norm, we have
B_{N}^{*}=B_{N^{*}}
, moreover for any pair of bodies we have
K_{1}\subset K_{2}\iff K_{2}^{*}\subset K_{1}^{*}
, and
K^{**}=K
. Finally, for arbitrary subset
S\subset V
define
\textnormal{conv}(S)
to be the convex hull of
S
.

Lemma 21
.

If
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are convex origin-symmetric bodies, then

(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{*}=\textnormal{conv}(K_{1}^{*}\cup K_{2}^{*}).

Proof.

We will first show that
(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{*}\subset\textnormal{conv}(K_{1}^{*}\cup K_{2}^{*})
. Equivalently we can check that
\textnormal{conv}(K_{1}^{*}\cup K_{2}^{*})^{*}\subset K_{1}\cap K_{2}
. To this end, take arbitrary
v\in\textnormal{conv}(K_{1}^{*}\cup K_{2}^{*})^{*}
. By definition, for any
w\in\textnormal{conv}(K_{1}^{*}\cup K_{2}^{*})
we have
\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\leq 1
. In particular since
K_{1}^{*}\subset\textnormal{conv}(K_{1}^{*}\cup K_{2}^{*})
, we have
\sup_{w\in K_{1}^{*}}\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\leq 1
, therefore
v\in K_{1}^{**}=K_{1}
, and symmetrically
v\in K_{2}
.

For the other direction, we wish to show that
\textnormal{conv}(K_{1}^{*}\cup K_{2}^{*})\subset(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{*}
. Note that since
K_{1}\cap K_{2}\subset K_{1}
, we have
K_{1}^{*}\subset(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{*}
, and symmetrically
K_{2}^{*}\subset(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{*}
.

Since
(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{*}
is a convex body such that
K_{1}^{*}\cup K_{2}^{*}\subset(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{*}
, we also have
\textnormal{conv}(K_{1}^{*}\cup K_{2}^{*})\subset(K_{1}\cap K_{2})^{*}
.
∎

Corollary 22
.

Consider two arbitrary norms
N_{1},N_{2}
on a linear space
V
, and a norm
N=\max(N_{1},N_{2})
. We can decompose any
w\in B_{N^{*}}
as
w=w_{1}+w_{2}
, where
\|w_{1}\|_{N_{1}^{*}}+\|w_{2}\|_{N_{2}^{*}}\leq 1
.

3
Reductions and communication complexity lower bounds

In this section we prove that a bounded distortion embedding of a normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|_{X})
into another normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{Y})
provides a reduction between the corresponding
\mathrm{IP}_{X}
and
\mathrm{IP}_{Y}
communication problems. We use this reduction to deduce lower bounds for one-way communication complexity of the
\mathrm{IP}_{X}
problems for spaces
X
that contain a (distorted) copy of
\ell_{\infty}
.

Proposition 23

(Extended version of Proposition
3
)
.

If
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|_{X})\hookrightarrow^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{Y})
, then for any
\varepsilon,\delta
we have
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{X})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\alpha^{-1}\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{Y})
, and moreover
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{X})\leq\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\alpha^{-1}\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{Y})
.

Proof.

We can decompose any embedding
f:X\to Y
with distortion
\alpha
as a composition of linear maps
f=f_{2}\circ f_{1}
, where
f_{1}:X\to f(X)
is an isomorphism of underlying vector spaces (it preserves the dimension) and has distortion
\alpha
and
f_{2}:f(X)\to Y
is an isometric embedding (i.e. it has distortion
1
).

As such it is enough to show the statement of the proposition in those two special cases — if the distortion is
1
, or map
f
is in an isomorphism (perhaps with non-trivial distortion).

For isometric embedding
p:X\to Y
the result
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{X})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{Y})
(and the same for
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}
) follows from
19
— Alice, given a vector
u\in X
, can instead consider a vector
p(u)\in Y
, Bob on the other hand given
w\in X^{*}
can find a vector
w^{\prime}\in Y^{*}
as in
19
— such that
\left\langle u,w\right\rangle=\left\langle p(u),w^{\prime}\right\rangle
. They can now apply the protocol for
\mathrm{IP}_{Y}
on the pair of inputs
(p(u),w^{\prime})
.

Now, consider the assumption that
f
is an isomorphism of underlying vector spaces, with distortion
\alpha
. In this case we can basically think of a single vector space
V
, on which we have two norms
X
and
Y
, s.t. for all
v
we have
\alpha^{-1}\|v\|_{X}\leq\|v\|_{Y}\leq\|v\|_{X}
. It is easy to check that in this case, for any
w\in V^{*}
we have
\|w\|_{X^{*}}\leq\|v\|_{Y^{*}}\leq\alpha\|v\|_{X^{*}}
.

Finally, if Alice has
v\in B_{X}
(unit ball with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
), and Bob has
w\in B_{X^{*}}
, we have
v\in B_{Y}
and
\alpha^{-1}w\in B_{Y^{*}}
. They can use the protocol for the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Y}
with an error
\alpha^{-1}\varepsilon
applied to
(v,\alpha^{-1}w)
to compute jointly
C=\alpha^{-1}\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\pm\alpha^{-1}\varepsilon
. Knowing this
C
they can easily compute
\alpha C=\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\pm\varepsilon
.
∎

Lemma 24
.

For any
\alpha<1
, we have
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\alpha,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{\infty}})\geq\Omega(n).

Proof.

We will use reduction from the
index
problem on
n
bits — in this problem Alice is given an
n
bit string
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and Bob an index
i\in[n]
, and he wants to output a value
x_{i}
with probability at least
2/3
. It is known that one-way communication of the
index
problem is
\Omega(n)
— Alice needs to essentially send her entire bit-string to Bob
[
KNR95
,
Rou16
]
.

Given an instance
(x,i)
of an indexing problem we can easily encode it as an equivalent instance of
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{\infty}}
: Alice takes her bit-string
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
encodes it as
v_{x}=\sum_{i}x_{i}e_{i}
, with
\|v_{x}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. Bob can recover arbitrary bit
x_{i}
if he can approximate
\left\langle v_{x},e_{i}\right\rangle=x_{i}
up to an error smaller than
1
. His test vector
w=e_{i}
clearly has
\|w\|_{1}=1
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
7
announced in the introduction.

Proof of Proposition
7
.

We need to show that if
\ell_{\infty}^{k}\hookrightarrow^{\varepsilon^{-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{X})
, then
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{X})\geq\Omega(k)
. It follows as a direct corollary by composing Lemma
24
together with Proposition
23
.
∎

Finally, we can use Proposition
7
to show a lower bound for the communication complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}}
.

Lemma 25
.

We have
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}})\geq\Omega(\min(n,\varepsilon^{-\max(p,2)}))
.

Proof.

Let
r=\varepsilon^{-\max(p,2)}
, and assume
r<n
. We wish to show that
\ell_{\infty}^{r}\hookrightarrow^{\varepsilon^{-1}}\ell_{p}
.
If
p\geq 2
, we can just take a subspace spanned by the first
r
basis vectors, and observe that for
\alpha\in\ell_{\infty}^{r}
, we have
\|\alpha\|_{\infty}\leq\|\sum_{i\leq r}\alpha_{i}e_{i}\|_{p}\leq\|\alpha\|_{\infty}r^{1/p}=\varepsilon^{-1}\|\alpha\|_{\infty}
— this inclusion gives an embedding with distortion
\varepsilon^{-1}
.

For
p<2
, it is known that the Banach-Mazur distance between
\ell_{p}^{r}
and
\ell_{\infty}^{r}
is at most
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{r})=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})

[
JL01
, Chapter 1, Section 8]
— which is just the same as saying that there exists a linear isomorphism between
\ell_{\infty}^{r}
and
\ell_{p}^{r}
with distortion at most
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{r})=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
.
∎

Lemma 26
.

We have
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}})\geq\Omega(\min(n,\varepsilon^{-2}))
.

Proof.

We will use a reduction from the
Gap-Hamming
problem — in this problem Alice and Bob are given two vectors
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, and they wish to check if
\Delta(x,y)<\frac{k}{2}-C\sqrt{k}
or
\Delta(x,y)>\frac{k}{2}+C\sqrt{k}
, where
\Delta(x,y)=|\{i:x_{i}\not=y_{i}\}|
— it is known that any randomized protocol solving this problem with probability
2/3
needs to use
\Omega(k)
communication
[
CR11
,
She12
]
.

Alice and Bob will interpret their vectors
x,y
directly as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(padding with zeros if necessary). Note that since
\Delta(x,y)=\|x\|_{2}^{2}+\|y\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\langle x,y\right\rangle
, if Alice and Bob can compute
\left\langle x,y\right\rangle
up to an additive error smaller than
2C\sqrt{k}
, they can solve the
Gap-Hamming
problem. Using the
\varepsilon
-approximate protocol for
\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}}
they can compute
\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\pm\varepsilon\|x\|_{p}\|y\|_{q}=\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\pm\varepsilon k^{1/p}k^{1/q}=\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\pm\varepsilon k
. Choosing
\varepsilon=\frac{2C}{\sqrt{k}}
, we get the desired lower bound:
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{\ell_{p}})\geq\Omega(k)=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2})
.
∎

Finally, we will show we can solve the one-way communication problem
\mathrm{IP}_{N}
if the norm
N
admits a sparsification.

Proposition 27
.

If a norm
N
admits a
(\varepsilon,\delta,D)
-sparsification, then
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{N})\leq\mathcal{O}(D\cdot\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon})
.

Proof.

Alice, given a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|v\|_{N}\leq 1
can just apply a sparsification procedure to obtain a vector
\phi(v)
and try to send an encoding of
\phi(v)
to Bob. In order to efficiently encode this vector, for each of at most
D
non-zero coefficients she needs to send the index of the corresponding coefficient (spending at most
\log n
bits), and an encoding of its value up to precision
(\varepsilon/n)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
— for which she needs to spend

Sparsification for

In this section we prove

Proof.

We have

5
Efficient protocol for arbitrary symmetric norm

The goal of this section is the proof of Theorem
4
.

See
4

The main ingredient used by us is the embedding theorem, saying that any symmetric norm can be with low distortion embedded into an explicit, relatively simple space. Before we can state this theorem, we need to introduce a sum operation of normed spaces — it is necessary to describe the target space in the embedding theorem.

For a sequence of normed spaces
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots V_{d}
, and a norm
h
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can construct a normed space
V
called the
h
-sum of
V_{i}
, denoted as

V=\bigoplus_{i\leq d}^{h}V_{i},

such that the underlying vector space is
V:=\bigoplus_{i\leq d}V_{i}
and norm is defined by the following formula. For an arbitrary vector
v\in V
, with decomposition
v=\sum v_{i}
where
v_{i}\in V_{i}
, we take

\|v\|_{V}:=h(\|v_{1}\|_{V_{1}},\|v_{2}\|_{V_{2}},\ldots\|v_{d}\|_{V_{d}}).

We also need to define the Top-k norm
T^{(k)}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, given by

\|v\|_{T^{(k)}}:=\sum_{j\leq k}|v|_{(i)}

Where
|v|_{(1)}\geq|v|_{(2)}\geq\ldots\geq|v|_{(n)}
is a non-increasing (in the magnitude) rearrangement of entries of
v
.

With those definition in hand, the aforementioned embedding theorem can finally be stated.

Theorem 29

(
[
ANN+17
, Theorem 4.2]
)
.

For any symmetric norm
N
, and any
\delta>0
, the normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{N})
embedds with distortion
1+\delta
into
\bigoplus_{i\leq t}^{l_{\infty}}\bigoplus_{k\leq n}^{\ell_{1}}T^{(k)}
, where
t=n^{\mathcal{O}(\delta^{-1}\log\delta^{-1})}
.

We will use this Theorem
29
together with Proposition
3
to prove Theorem
4
. To this end, all we need to do is to show an explicit protocol for the inner product problem on a space of form
\bigoplus_{i\leq t}^{l_{\infty}}\bigoplus_{k\leq n}^{\ell_{1}}T^{(k)}
.

5.1
Compositions of norm

We will show here that if all normed spaces

admit an efficient protocol for the inner product problem, and

has an efficient protocol for the inner product problem.

Lemma 30
.

If
V=\bigoplus_{i\leq d}^{h}V_{i}
, then
V^{*}=\bigoplus_{i\leq d}^{h^{*}}V_{i}^{*}
.

Proof.

Consider
w\in V^{*}
, such that
w=w_{1}+\ldots+w_{d}
.
We have

\displaystyle\|w\|_{V^{*}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{v\in V,\|v\|_{V}\leq 1}\left\langle v,w\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\alpha\|_{h}\leq 1}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{1},\ldots v_{k}\\
\|v_{i}\|_{V_{i}}=\alpha_{i}\end{subarray}}\left\langle\sum v_{i},w\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\alpha\|_{h}\leq 1}\sum_{i}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{i}\\
\|v_{i}\|_{V_{i}}=\alpha_{i}\end{subarray}}\left\langle v_{i},w_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\alpha\|_{h}\leq 1}\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\|w_{i}\|_{V_{i}^{*}}

\displaystyle=h^{*}(\|w_{1}\|_{V_{1}^{*}},\ldots\|w_{d}\|_{V_{d}^{*}}).

∎

Lemma 31
.

If for each

Proof.

Alice is given a vector

We claim that with probability

Indeed, let us first consider the vector

(1)

\displaystyle\left\langle\phi(q),\eta\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\tau_{i}\frac{\phi(q)_{i}}{\|v_{i}\|_{V_{i}}}\pm\sum_{i}\varepsilon\phi(q)_{i}\|w\|_{V_{i}^{*}}

6.1
Proof of Theorem
8

Let us consider an arbitrary vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
\|v\|_{N}\leq 1
, and a random variable
t\in[n]
, drawn according to some distribution
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t=i)=p_{i}

is a standard basis vector. Clearly, by construction
\mathop{\bf E\/}\tilde{v}=v

We will specify a suitable distribution for a random variable

, such that we can upper bound the quantity
\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}
, where

is the dual exponent to

(i.e.

). If we have an upper bound
\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}\leq K

\mathcal{O}((pK/\varepsilon)^{2p})
-sparsification for a vector

— in short because we can bound
\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q^{\prime}}\leq O(p)\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}=O(Kp)
for a slightly smaller

, and therefore by Lemma

it is enough to sample

independently at random for an average of

to concentrate within

of its expectation.

In order to implement this proof idea, let us first show a convenient bound for the quantity
\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}
.

Lemma 36
.

For any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the random vector
\tilde{v}:=e_{t}v_{t}/p_{t}
where

is a random variable distributed according to
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t=i)=p_{i}
, and any
q\geq 1
, if
p_{i}=0
for all

with

, we have

\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}\leq\sup_{S\subset[n]}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{-1/p}\|v_{S}\|_{N}~,

where
v_{S}
is a restriction of
v
to coordinates in
S
, and
p,q
are dual exponents
(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1)

Proof.

Expanding the definition of
\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}

\displaystyle\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle|\geq\lambda)^{1/q}\lambda

\displaystyle=\sup_{S\subset[n]}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{1/q}(\min_{i\in S}\frac{|v_{i}w_{i}|}{p_{i}}).

We could now take
\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}
, to see that

\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}=\sup_{S\subset[n]}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{1/q}\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}\min_{i\in S}\frac{|v_{i}w_{i}|}{p_{i}}.

(3)

For any

, let

be a vector with
(w_{S})_{i}=\frac{p_{i}}{v_{i}}
for

, and
(w_{S})_{i}=0
otherwise. When the set

is fixed, the supremum is achieved for

, that is

\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}\min_{i\in S}\frac{|v_{i}w_{i}|}{p_{i}}=\frac{1}{\|w_{S}\|_{N^{*}}}.

(4)

Indeed, if we had any

such that at least one of the terms

was greater than the minimum, we could decrease the corresponding

, and without affecting

. Hence, there is an optimal vector

for which all terms

are equal to each other — that is all

Moreover, by the definition of the dual norm, we have

(5)

Plugging together inequalities (

\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}\leq\sup_{S\subset[n]}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{1/q}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{-1}\|v_{S}\|_{N}=\sup_{S\subset[n]}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{-1/p}\|v_{S}\|_{N}.

∎

We will leverage the assumption that

T_{i}
., Clearly, we have
v=\sum_{i}v_{T_{i}}
. We will pick probabilities
p_{i}=\frac{1}{R}\frac{1}{|T_{j}|}
for
i\in T_{j}
(and similarly for
T_{*}
). That is, in order to sample coordinate
t\in[n]
, we first sample uniformly one of the sets
T_{i}
or
T_{*}
, and then a uniformly random coordinate from this set. As before, let
\tilde{v}
be a random vector defined as
\tilde{v}=e_{t}\frac{v_{t}}{p_{t}}
with probability
p_{t}
.

Lemma 38
.

For a vector
v
and a random index
t
of the form described above, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{S\subset[n]}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{1/p}\|v_{S}\|_{N}\leq(kR)^{1/p},

(6)

where
\ell_{\infty}^{k}\not\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{N})
and
p:=\frac{\log k}{\log\varepsilon^{-1}}
.

Proof.

We will show that for any set
S
either the desired inequality (
6
) holds, or we can find another set
S^{\prime}
with
|S|<|S^{\prime}|
such that

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{1/p}\|v_{S}\|_{N}\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S^{\prime})^{1/p}\|v_{S^{\prime}}\|_{N}.

By the mathematical induction on
n-|S|
this is enough to prove the lemma.

Indeed, take
S_{i}=S\cap T_{i}
, and consider two cases. Either there is some
i_{0}
such that
|S_{i_{0}}|/|T_{i_{0}}|>\frac{1}{k}
, or for all
i
we have
|S_{i_{0}}|/|T_{i_{0}}|\leq\frac{1}{k}
.

In the former case, we can bound
\|v_{S}\|_{N}\leq\|v\|_{N}=1
, and

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(i\in S)=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{i}\frac{|S_{i}|}{|T_{i}|}\geq\frac{1}{R}\frac{|S_{i_{0}}|}{|T_{i_{0}}|}>(kR)^{-1}.

hence
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(i\in S)^{-1/p}\|v_{S}\|_{N}\leq(kR)^{1/p}
as desired.

On the other hand if for all
i
we have
\frac{|S_{i}|}{|T_{i}|}\leq\frac{1}{k}
, we can find a collection of disjoint sets
S^{(1)}=S,S^{(2)},S^{(3)},\ldots S^{(k)}
such that for all
i,j
we have
|S^{(i)}\cap T_{j}|=|S_{j}|
. Let us define
S^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i}S^{(i)}
, so that
v_{S^{\prime}}=\sum_{i}v_{S^{(i)}}
. We can now apply Lemma
37
to vectors
v_{S^{(i)}}
(they all have the same norm, since they are all permutations of each other), to deduce

\|v_{S^{\prime}}\|_{N}=\|\sum_{i\in[k]}v_{S^{(i)}}\|_{N}>\varepsilon^{-1}\min_{i}\|v_{S^{(i)}}\|_{N}=\varepsilon^{-1}\|v_{S}\|_{N}

On the other hand
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(i\in S^{\prime})=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{i\in R}\frac{|S^{\prime}\cap T_{i}|}{|T_{i}|}=k\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(i\in S)
. Hence,

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(i\in S)^{-1/p}\|v_{S}\|_{N}\leq k^{1/p}\varepsilon\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(i\in S^{\prime})^{-1/p}\|v_{S^{\prime}}\|_{N}=\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(i\in S^{\prime})^{-1/p}\|v_{S^{\prime}}\|_{N}.

∎

We will use this bound for structured vectors to show a similiar upper bound for arbitrary vectors with
R=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, essentially by rounding each coordinate to the nearest value
2^{-j}
.

Lemma 39
.

For any vector
v
with
\|v\|_{N}\leq 1
, there is a distribution
(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots p_{n})
of a random variable
t\in[n]
such that the random vector
\tilde{v}:=e_{t}\frac{v_{t}}{p_{t}}
satisfies

\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}\leq\mathcal{O}(k^{1/p}\log^{1/p}(n)).

Proof.

Consider sets
T_{i}=\{j:2^{-i}<v_{j}\leq 2^{-i+1}\}
for
i\leq R
where
R:=3\log_{2}(n)
and
T_{+}=\{j:v_{j}\leq 2^{-R}\}
. In particular each coordinate
j\in T_{+}
satisfies
v_{j}\leq\frac{1}{n^{3}}
, and therefore
\|v_{T_{+}}\|_{N}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}
.

We will set probabilities
p_{j}=\frac{1}{R+1}\frac{1}{|T_{i}|}
for
j\in T_{i}
(and similarly
\frac{1}{R+1}\frac{1}{|T_{+}|}
for
j\in T_{+}
). According to Lemma
36
, it is enough to bound

\sup_{S\subset[n]}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{-1/p}\|v_{S}\|_{N}.

Consider now a vector
v^{\prime}:=\sum_{i}2^{-i}\mathbf{1}_{T_{i}}
, and a random variable
t^{\prime}
where
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t^{\prime}=j)=\frac{1}{R}\frac{1}{|T_{i}|}
for
j\in T_{i}
(and
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t^{\prime}\in T_{+})=0
). We can apply Lemma
38
to the pair
(v^{\prime},t^{\prime})
and deduce

\sup_{S\subset[n]}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t^{\prime}\in S)^{1/p}\|v^{\prime}_{S}\|_{N}\leq(kR)^{1/p}.

Consider now arbitrary
S
and decompose
S=S^{\prime}\cup S^{\prime\prime}
where
S^{\prime\prime}=S\cap T_{+}
and
S^{\prime}=S-S^{\prime\prime}
, we have

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{-1/p}\|v_{S}\|_{N}\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{-1/p}\|v_{S^{\prime}}\|_{N}+\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{-1/p}\|v_{S^{\prime\prime}}\|_{N}

(7)

We can bound those two terms separately. On one hand
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)\geq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S^{\prime})=\frac{R}{R+1}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t^{\prime}\in S^{\prime})
and
\|v_{S^{\prime}}\|_{N}\leq 2\|v^{\prime}_{S^{\prime}}\|_{N}
(since we have coordinate-wise inequalities
|v_{j}|\leq 2|v^{\prime}_{j}|
for
j\in S^{\prime}
), therefore

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{-1/p}\|v_{S^{\prime}}\|_{N}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{R+1}{R}\right)^{1/p}\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t^{\prime}\in S^{\prime})^{-1/p}\|v^{\prime}_{S^{\prime}}\|_{N}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{R+1}{R}\right)^{1/p}(kR)^{1/p}=2(k(R+1))^{1/p}.

(8)

On the other hand

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(t\in S)^{-1/p}\|v_{S^{\prime\prime}}\|_{N}\leq(\frac{1}{R}\frac{1}{|T_{+}|})^{-1/p}\|v_{T_{+}}\|_{N}\lesssim(n\log n)^{1/p}\frac{1}{n^{2}}=o(1).

(9)

Combining now inequalities (
7
), (
8
) and (
9
), get the desired result

\sup_{w\in B_{N^{*}}}\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}\leq\mathcal{O}(k^{1/p}\log^{1/p}n).

∎

With this lemma in hand, we are ready to prove the Theorem
35
.

Proof of Theorem
35
.

For a vector
v\in B_{N}
, let us consider a random vector
\tilde{v}
distributed as in Lemma
39
, and
s
independent copies of
\tilde{v}
, say
\tilde{v}_{1},\ldots\tilde{v}_{s}
, for some
s
that will be specified later.

Take
\phi(v):=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i\leq s}\tilde{v}_{i}
. Since
\|\tilde{v}_{i}\|_{0}=1
, we have
\|\phi(v)\|_{0}\leq s
, and since
\mathop{\bf E\/}\tilde{v}_{i}=v
, we also have
\mathop{\bf E\/}\phi(v)=v
. We wish to show that for any given
w\in B_{N^{*}}
with probability
2/3
we have
\left\langle\phi(v),w\right\rangle=\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\pm\varepsilon
.

Let us define
Z_{i}=\left\langle\tilde{v}_{i},w\right\rangle-\left\langle v,w\right\rangle
, moreover let us take
p^{\prime}=2p
, and
q^{\prime}
a dual exponent (i.e. satisfying
1/q^{\prime}+1/p^{\prime}=1
).

By Lemma
16
, we have
\|Z_{i}\|_{q^{\prime}}\lesssim p\|Z_{i}\|_{q,\infty}
, and by Lemma
39
we have
\|Z_{i}\|_{q,\infty}\leq\|\left\langle\tilde{v},w\right\rangle\|_{q,\infty}+1\leq\mathcal{O}(k^{1/p}\log^{1/p}n)
.

Applying now Lemma
17
, we have
\|\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i\leq s}Z_{i}\|_{q^{\prime}}\lesssim s^{-1/p^{\prime}}\|Z_{1}\|_{q^{\prime}}\lesssim p\left(\frac{k\log n}{\sqrt{s}}\right)^{1/p}
.

If we now chose
s=\left(\frac{Cp}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2p}(k\log n)^{2}
for some universal constant
C
, we can ensure that
\|\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i\leq s}Z_{i}\|_{q^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon/5
, and by Markov inequality with probability
2/3
we have
|\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i\leq s}Z_{i}|\leq\varepsilon
, or equivalently

\left\langle\phi(v),w\right\rangle=\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\pm\varepsilon,

as desired.
∎

Remark 40
.

By the way, we can write
(Cp/\varepsilon)^{4p}\log^{2}n=k^{\mathcal{O}(\log\log k)}+\log^{3}n
, if we consider this bound to be more aesthetically pleasing.

7
Extension complexity and communication complexity of
\mathrm{IP}_{P}

In this section we prove Proposition
10
and Theorem
12
.

As a reminder, in this section we consider an arbitrary origin-symmetric polytope
P
(with
P=-P
) and the associated norm
\|\cdot\|_{P}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which
P
is a unit ball. We wish to connect extension complexity of the polytope
P
with the communication complexity of the related
\mathrm{IP}_{P}
problem.

We will first with a direct argument for
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log\mathrm{xc}(P)}{\varepsilon^{2}})
(
Proposition

10
) — more concretely, we wish to show that if a
P
is a projection of some polytope
Q
such that
Q
is defined by
M
inequalities, then
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\frac{1}{3}}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log M}{\varepsilon^{2}}).

Lemma 41
.

Let
P
be an origin-symmetric polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
P
is a projection of
Q

then
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q})
.

Proof.

Consider
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a subspace
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
P
is the image of
Q
for the projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}/U^{\perp}
.

By
Lemma

18
, the norm
\|\cdot\|_{P}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}/U^{\perp}
is isometrically isomorphic with the dual to the restriction of
\|\cdot\|_{Q^{*}}
to the subspace
U
. Therefore we have

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})=\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{P^{*}})=\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q^{*}|_{U}})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q^{*}})=\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,\delta}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q}),

where the inequality follows from
Proposition

3
.
∎

Lemma 42
.

If
P
is an origin-symmetric convex polytope defined by
M
inequalities, then
P^{*}
has at most
M
vertices.

Proof.

This follows from
Lemma

21
— for a polytope
P
defined by
P=\{x:\forall i-1\leq A_{i}x\leq 1\}
we have
P=\bigcap_{i}\{x:-1\leq\left\langle A_{i},x\right\rangle\leq 1\}
, and therefore
P^{*}=\textnormal{conv}(\bigcup_{i}K_{i}^{*})
, where
K_{i}:=\{x:-1\leq\left\langle A_{i},x\right\rangle\leq 1\}
. It is easy to check that
K_{i}^{*}=\textnormal{conv}(\{A_{i},-A_{i}\})
, and therefore
P^{*}=\textnormal{conv}(\{\pm A_{i}\}_{i})
.
∎

Lemma 43
.

If
P
is an origin-symmetric polytope with
M
vertices, then
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log M}{\varepsilon^{2}})
.

Proof.

In the communication problem Alice has arbitrary point
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|v\|_{P}\leq 1
, which is just a point
v\in P
. She can express it as a convex combination
v=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}v_{i}
where all
v_{i}
are vertices of the polytope
P
and
\sum\lambda_{i}=1
. Now consider a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over vertices of
P
specified by the vector which has
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}(x=v_{i})=\lambda_{i}
.

We have
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x\mathcal{D}}x=v
and therefore by linearity of expectation
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x\mathcal{D}}\left\langle x,w\right\rangle=\left\langle v,w\right\rangle
for Bobs vector
w
. Since
\|w\|_{P^{*}}\leq 1
, and all vertices of
P
by definition have
P
-norm equal to one, the random variable
\left\langle x,w\right\rangle
satisfies
|\left\langle x,w\right\rangle|\leq 1
. As ususal, by the Chebyshev inequality is enough to sample
t=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
independent copies of
x
from the distribution
\mathcal{D}
, to ensure that the average
\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i\leq t}\left\langle x^{(i)},w\right\rangle=\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\pm\varepsilon
with probability
2/3
.

Alice can now just perform the sampling herself and send the names of those vertices to Bob — she can specify a vertex of a polytope using
\mathcal{O}(\log M)
bits of communication per sample.
∎

With those lemmas in hand we are ready to show
Proposition

10
.

Proof of
Proposition

10
.

If
P
is a projection of
Q
by
Lemma

41
we have
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q})
, now by symmetry between Alice and Bob we have
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q})=\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q^{*}})
, and since
Q
was defined by
M
inequalities,
Q^{*}
has at most
M
vertices (by
Lemma

42
). Finally, since
Q^{*}
has at most
M
vertices we can apply
Lemma

43
to deduce
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{Q^{*}})\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log M}{\varepsilon^{2}})
.
∎

We will now concentrate on the proof of
Proposition

12
. To this end we will use the following convenient characterization of the non-negative rank of matrix
S
.

Theorem 44

(
[
FFGT12
]
)
.

For a non-negative matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{X\times Y}
, we say that the randomized communication protocol computes
S
in expectation, if Alice and Bob given
x\in X
and
y\in Y
respectively, output a non-negative value
f(x,y)
such that
\mathop{\bf E\/}f(x,y)=S_{x,y}
. Let
\mathrm{ecc}(S)
be the communication of the optimal protocol computing
S
in expectation, then

\mathrm{ecc}(S)=\lceil\log\mathrm{rk}^{+}(S)\rceil.

Proof of
Proposition

12
.

By Theorem
44
it is enough to show a protocol for Alice and Bob that computes in expectation a matrix
S^{\prime}
such that
\forall i,j|S_{i,j}-S^{\prime}_{i,j}|\leq 2\varepsilon
, assuming that they have an efficient protocol for
\mathrm{IP}_{P}
.

Here Alice is given a vertex
v\in P
, and Bob is given an inequality
i
among those defining
P
(either
\left\langle x,A_{i}\right\rangle\leq 1
or
\left\langle x,A_{i}\right\rangle\geq-1
). Note that
\|A_{i}\|_{P^{*}}\leq 1
, so they can in fact use the protocol for
\mathrm{IP}_{P}
to compute a value which is with probability
2/3
in a range
\left\langle v,A_{i}\right\rangle\pm\varepsilon
.

They can improve the failure probability by instantiating it independently
\mathcal{O}(\log\varepsilon^{-1})
times and taking the median of reported answers — this method provides an answer in range
\left\langle v,A_{i}\right\rangle
with probability
1-\varepsilon/2
— and hence they can attempt to compute slack as
1-\left\langle v,A_{i}\right\rangle)
(or
1+\left\langle x,A_{i}\right\rangle
) — and if the answer is outside the range
[0,2]
, they just project it to this range.

This way, the answer
f(v,A_{i})
they are computing is a non-negative, random variable bounded by
2
, such that with
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}(|f(v,i)-S_{v,i}|>\varepsilon)<\varepsilon/2
— this implies that for all
v,i
we have
\mathop{\bf E\/}f(v,i)=S_{v,i}\pm 2\varepsilon
, proving

\log\widetilde{\mathrm{rk}^{+}_{2\varepsilon}}(S_{P})\leq\mathcal{O}(\log\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon,1/3}(\mathrm{IP}_{P})).

∎
