Thermal Pair Production from Photon-Photon Collision: Breit-Wheeler Process at Finite Temperature
D. S. Cabral, A. F. Santos, R. Bufalo

Abstract
In this paper we examine the pair production through the Breit-Wheeler process γ ​ γ → e + ​ e − \displaystyle\gamma\penalty\ \gamma\to e^{+}e^{-} in a thermal background. We compute the thermal contribution to the Breit-Wheeler differential cross section within the thermofield dynamics formalism. We evaluate in details the cross section for this process, which possess a surprisingly simple expression valid for any temperature β \displaystyle\beta , from which we discuss some physically relevant aspects. We also consider the high temperature regime of the cross section in order to have a better understanding about its thermal behavior.

Breit-Wheeler Amplitude at finite temperature

The main purpose of this section is to calculate the differential cross section for the Breit-Wheeler (BW) process at finite temperature within the thermofield dynamics (TFD)
takahashi:1996
;
umezawa:1982
;
umezawa:1995
;

. The BW process consists of creating an electron-positron pair through the collision of two photons, i.e.
\displaystyle\gamma+\gamma=e^{+}+e^{-}
.
The Feynman diagrams that describe this process are depicted in Figure

.

Since we wish to compute the thermal cross section of the BW process, our starting point is

\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\frac{1}{64\pi^{2}(4\omega^{2})}\langle|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{fi}|^{2}\rangle

(1)

in which the temperature effects are solely contained in the transition amplitude
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\beta)
.
This thermal amplitude is formally defined as

\mathcal{\mathcal{M}}\left(\beta\right)=\left\langle f,\beta\left|\hat{S}\right|i,\beta\right\rangle

(2)

where the
\displaystyle\hat{S}
-matrix is given

\hat{S}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-i\right)^{n}}{n!}\int dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{n}\tau\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{i}\left(x_{1}\right)\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}\left(x_{2}\right)\cdots\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}\left(x_{n}\right)\right],

(3)

with
\displaystyle\tau
being the time ordering operator and

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}\left(x\right)=\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}\left(x_{n}\right)-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}\left(x_{n}\right)

(4)

describes the Lagrangian interaction part in the doubled notation of the TFD formalism.
In this context, the final and initial thermal states for the process
\displaystyle\gamma(k)+\gamma(k^{\prime})=e^{+}(p^{\prime})+e^{-}(p)
are given as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ket{i}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\ket{\gamma,\gamma^\prime}=d^{\dagger}_{\beta}(k)d^{\dagger}_{\beta}(k^{\prime})\ket{0(\beta)},

(5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ket{f}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\ket{e^{+},e^{-}}=b^{\dagger}_{\beta}(p)a^{\dagger}_{\beta}(p^{\prime})\ket{0(\beta)},

(6)

with
\displaystyle d^{\dagger}_{\beta}
,
\displaystyle b^{\dagger}_{\beta}
and
\displaystyle a^{\dagger}_{\beta}
being the thermal creation operators for the photons, electrons and positrons, respectively.

In order to calculate the thermal transition amplitude for the BW process, the second-order term of the scattering matrix (
3
) is considered,

\widehat{S}^{(2)}=\int d^{4}xd^{4}y\bra{f}\tau\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}(x)\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}(y)\right]\ket{i},

(7)

and the thermal transition amplitude for the BW process is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{fi}=\bra{f,\beta}\widehat{S}^{(2)}\ket{i,\beta}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{e^{2}}{2}\int d^{4}xd^{4}y\left\{\bra{f}\tau\left[\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}(x)\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}(y)\right]\ket{i}+\bra{f}\tau\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}(x)\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}(y)\right]\ket{i}\right.

(8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left.\bra{f}\tau\left[\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}(x)\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}(y)\right]\ket{i}-\bra{f}\tau\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}(x)\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}(y)\right]\ket{i}\right\}.

(9)

In the quantum electrodynamics, the interaction part of the Lagrangian is given as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=e\bar{\psi}(x)\gamma^{\mu}\psi(x)A_{\mu}(x),

(10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm int}(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=e\tilde{\bar{\psi}}(x)\gamma^{\mu}\tilde{\psi}(x)\tilde{A}_{\mu}(x),

(11)

where
\displaystyle e
is the electron charge,
\displaystyle\gamma^{\mu}
are the Dirac matrices,
\displaystyle\psi(x)
is the fermion field and
\displaystyle A_{\mu}(x)
is the photon field, while the tilde fields are the TFD dual (conjugated) fields.

In order to evaluate explicitly the matrix elements in (
9
), we should also consider the solution for the fermionic field (and its tilde dual)

\psi(x)=\int d^{3}pN_{p}\sum_{s}\left[b(p,s)u(p,s)e^{-ipx}+a^{\dagger}(p,s)\bar{u}(p,s)e^{ipx}\right],

(12)

where
\displaystyle N_{p}
is the normalization constant and
\displaystyle px=\xi t-\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}
, with the dispersion relation
\displaystyle\xi=\sqrt{\vec{p}^{2}+m^{2}}
and metric signature
\displaystyle\eta_{\mu\nu}={\rm diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)
.
In the TFD formalism it is worth to mention the (tilde) conjugation operations for the fermions
\displaystyle\tilde{u}(p,s)=u^{\dagger}(p,s)
.
Moreover, for the photon field

A_{\mu}(x)=\int d^{3}kM_{k}\sum_{\lambda}\left[d(k,\lambda)\epsilon_{\mu}(k,\lambda)e^{-ikx}+d^{\dagger}(k,\lambda)\epsilon^{\dagger}_{\mu}(k,\lambda)e^{ikx}\right]

(13)

with
\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mu}(k,\lambda)
being the polarization vector of the physical polarization states.
With these elements we are now in a position to compute the amplitude (
9
).
Let us start with the first term

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bra{f}\tau\left[\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}(x)\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}(y)\right]\ket{i}=\penalty\

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\bar{u}(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})\gamma^{\mu}S(x-y)\gamma^{\nu}v(p,s)\varepsilon_{\mu}(k,\lambda)\varepsilon_{\nu}(k^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times\left[e^{i(p-k)x+i(p^{\prime}-k^{\prime})y}+e^{i(p-k^{\prime})x+i(p^{\prime}-k)y}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times\bra{f}\tau\left[a^{\dagger}(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})b^{\dagger}(p,s)d(k,\lambda)d(k^{\prime},\lambda)\right]\ket{i}.

(14)

One should pay attention to the element matrix present in (
14
) because it involves both
\displaystyle T=0
and thermal creation (annihilation) operators (recall that the in and out states are thermal states, see eqs. (
5
) and (
6
)).
In order to evaluate this element matrix we should use the relation among these two sets of operators, which formally is given in terms of the following Bogoliubov transformation
khanna:2009

a(p)=Ua_{\beta}(p)+V\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}_{\beta}(p);\quad\quad\quad a^{\dagger}(p)=Ua^{\dagger}_{\beta}(p)+V\widetilde{a}_{\beta}(p),

(15)

for the case of fermions (analogous relations hold for
\displaystyle d(p,s)
) and

d(p)=U^{\prime}d_{\beta}(p)+V^{\prime}\widetilde{d}^{\dagger}_{\beta}(p);\quad\quad\quad d^{\dagger}(p)=U^{\prime}d^{\dagger}_{\beta}(p)+V^{\prime}\widetilde{d}_{\beta}(p)

(16)

for bosons.
The set of functions
\displaystyle(U,V)
and
\displaystyle(U^{\prime},V^{\prime})
are related with the Fermi-Dirac
\displaystyle n_{F}(E)
and Bose-Einstein
\displaystyle n_{B}(E)
distributions, respectively, defined as the following

\displaystyle\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+e^{-\beta E}}}=\sqrt{e^{\beta E}n_{F}\left(E\right)},\quad V=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+e^{\beta E}}}=\sqrt{n_{F}\left(E\right)},

(17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle U^{\prime}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^{-\beta E}}}=\sqrt{e^{\beta E}n_{B}\left(E\right)},\quad V^{\prime}=\frac{e^{-\beta E/2}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-\beta E}}}=\sqrt{n_{B}\left(E\right)}.

(18)

Thus, with help of these informations we cast the element matrix of Eq. (
14
) as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bra{f}\tau\left[a^{\dagger}(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})b^{\dagger}(p,s)d(k,\lambda)d(k^{\prime},\lambda)\right]\ket{i}=U^{2}(U^{\prime})^{2}\bra{f}\tau\left[a_{\beta}^{\dagger}(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})b_{\beta}^{\dagger}(p,s)d_{\beta}(k,\lambda)d_{\beta}(k^{\prime},\lambda)\right]\ket{i},

(19)

in which all operators are now thermal and this element can be evaluated.

From this analysis we can compute the remaining matrix elements of Eq. (
9
), i.e. those proportional to
\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\right\rangle
,
\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}{\mathcal{L}}\right\rangle
and
\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathcal{L}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\right\rangle
, and it follows that they contribute with the following thermal functions
\displaystyle V^{2}(V^{\prime})^{2}
,
\displaystyle-VU(V^{\prime})(U^{\prime})
and
\displaystyle-UV(U^{\prime})(V^{\prime})
, respectively.
Hence, taking all these results into consideration, the transition amplitude (
9

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{fi}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-ie^{2}F(\beta)\left(2\pi\right)^{4}\Big[\bar{u}(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})\gamma^{\mu}S_{\beta}\left(k-p\right)\gamma^{\nu}v(p,s)\varepsilon_{\mu}(k,\lambda)\varepsilon_{\nu}(k^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})

(20)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\bar{u}(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})\gamma^{\mu}S_{\beta}\left(k^{\prime}-p\right)\gamma^{\nu}v(p,s)\varepsilon_{\mu}(k,\lambda)\varepsilon_{\nu}(k^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})\Big]

(21)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times\bar{u}(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})\gamma^{\mu}S_{\beta}(q)\gamma^{\nu}v(p,s)\varepsilon_{\mu}(k,\lambda)\varepsilon_{\nu}(k^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})

(22)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times\bra{f}\tau\left[a_{\beta}^{\dagger}(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})b_{\beta}^{\dagger}(p,s)d_{\beta}(k,\lambda)d_{\beta}(k^{\prime},\lambda)\right]\ket{i},

(23)

where thermal distributions are contained in the factor

F(\beta)=(UU^{\prime}+VV^{\prime})^{2}

(24)

which can be written with help of (
17
) and (
18

F(\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)+2\sqrt{\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\cosh(\beta\omega)+1}}\sqrt{\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)+1}+\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)\right].

(25)

Moreover, we have that
\displaystyle S_{\beta}(q)
is the fermion propagator at finite temperature defined as

S_{\beta}(q)=S^{(0)}(q)+S^{(\beta)}(q),

(26)

in which
\displaystyle S^{(0)}(q)=\frac{\not{q}+m}{q^{2}-m^{2}}
is the usual
\displaystyle T=0
propagator, while
\displaystyle S^{(\beta)}(q)
is the finite temperature defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle S^{(\beta)}(q)=\frac{\pi i}{(e^{\beta q^{0}}+1)}\left[\frac{(\gamma^{0}\xi-\gamma^{i}q_{i}+m)}{2\xi}\Delta_{1}\delta(q^{0}-\xi)+\frac{(\gamma^{0}\xi+\gamma^{i}q_{i}-m)}{2\xi}\Delta_{2}\delta(q^{0}+\xi)\right],

(27)

with the definitions

\Delta_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{\beta q_{0}/2}\\
e^{\beta q_{0}/2}&-1\end{pmatrix};\quad\quad\Delta_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&e^{\beta q_{0}/2}\\
e^{\beta q_{0}/2}&1\end{pmatrix}.

(28)

Finally, taking these results into account, we are able to compute the squared modulus of the transition amplitude

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{fi}|^{2}\rangle=F^{2}(\beta)\left\{\langle|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{a}|^{2}\rangle_{\beta}+\langle|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{b}|^{2}\rangle_{\beta}+\left\langle 2\real\left[\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{a}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{b}\right]\right\rangle_{\beta}\right\}

(29)

with

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{a}|^{2}\rangle_{\beta}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{e^{4}}{4}\eta_{\mu\rho}\eta_{\nu\alpha}\Tr{(\slashed{p}^{\prime}+m)\gamma^\nu S_\beta(k-p)\gamma^\mu(\slashed{p}-m)\gamma^\rho S_\beta^\dagger(k-p)\gamma^\alpha};

(30)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{b}|^{2}\rangle_{\beta}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{e^{4}}{4}\eta_{\nu\rho}\eta_{\mu\alpha}\Tr{(\slashed{p}^{\prime}+m)\gamma^\mu S_\beta(k^\prime-p)\gamma^\nu(\slashed{p}-m)\gamma^\rho S_\beta^\dagger(k^\prime-p)\gamma^\alpha};

(31)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\langle 2\real\left[\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{a}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{b}\right]\right\rangle_{\beta}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{e^{4}}{2}\eta_{\mu\rho}\eta_{\nu\alpha}\Re{\Tr\left[(\slashed{p}-m)\gamma^\rho S_\beta(k-p)\gamma^\alpha(\slashed{p}^\prime+m)\gamma^\mu S_\beta^\dagger(k^\prime-p)\gamma^\nu\right]}.

(32)

where we have averaged over the polarization and spin states, with use of the completeness relations,

\sum_{s}u(p,s)\bar{u}(p,s)={\not{p}+m},\quad\quad\sum_{s}v(p,s)\bar{v}(p,s)={\not{p}-m}

(33)

and

\sum_{\lambda}\epsilon_{\mu}(k,\lambda)\epsilon^{\dagger}_{\rho}(k,\lambda)=\eta_{\mu\rho}.

(34)

in which we have introduced the parameter
\displaystyle\chi=\frac{\omega^{2}}{m^{2}}
by means of simplicity of notation. One can observe that the term
\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{1}
corresponds to the usual
\displaystyle T=0
contribution, while
\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{2}
corresponds to the purely thermal part.

It is important to observe that the delta functions present in
\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{2}
in the expression (
48
) play the role of filters for such values, this is a consequence of the approximation that has been used, a tree-level approximation.
One can naively find a singularity in
\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{2}
present in the term proportional to
\displaystyle\delta\left(\sqrt{\chi}\right)
or even that
\displaystyle\delta\left(3\chi-1\right)
leads to a imaginary contribution to the cross section.
However, these observations are simply apparent, because when we analyze the full expression for the cross-section (
48
) a natural condition arises upon these delta functions arise.
Once the cross-section represents a measurable physical quantity it must be real.
Therefore, applying this condition on (
48
), we realize that the terms
\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{1}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{2}
must be real.
This implies a strong constraint upon the parameter
\displaystyle\chi
, i.e.
\displaystyle\chi>1
(i.e.
\displaystyle\omega^{2}>m^{2}
).
Imposing this condition on
\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{2}
, we realize that both delta functions
\displaystyle\delta\left(\sqrt{\chi}\right)
and
\displaystyle\delta\left(3\chi-1\right)
become zero and the cross-section depends only on the term
\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{1}
.
Hence, the real and finite cross-section at finite temperature becomes

\sigma_{\rm BW}=\frac{e^{4}}{32\pi\omega^{2}}F^{2}\left(\beta\right)\mathbb{I}_{1}.

(52)

It is worth stress that the expression for the Breit-Wheeler process cross section (
52
) is valid for any value of
\displaystyle\beta
.
However, this thermal dependence is rather complicated in the factor
\displaystyle F^{2}\left(\beta\right)
.
Thus, one can consider, by means of elucidation, the high temperature limit, where one can get a better grasp of the thermal behavior of the cross section (
52
).
Hence, at the high temperature regime, we obtain that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm BW}^{\rm high}\simeq\frac{e^{4}}{8\pi\omega^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{1}e^{-\frac{3\beta\omega}{4}}.

(53)

This expression is accordance with our expectation, since it is well known that at the high-temperature limit the Fermi-Dirac and Bose-Einstein distributions reproduce the Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics:
\displaystyle\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}\mp 1}\simeq e^{-\beta\omega}
.

IV
Conclusion

In this paper we have studied thermal contributions to the Breit-Wheeler process within the thermofield dynamics approach.
Due to phenomenological interest (QED in intense electromagnetic fields) the Breit-Wheeler process, which consists of a pair production through the collision of two photons, has been considered in many contexts.
However, its description in a thermal bath is somewhat controversial, since many papers (results) disagree with each other.
Hence, we approach this topic within the thermofield dynamics formalism, which is very robust in order to shed some new light and clarity (some of) the controversies.

We started our analysis by reviewing the main aspects of the thermofield dynamics applied to the evaluation of the (thermal) differential cross section.
With this purpose, we discussed the second-order thermal transition amplitude related with the process
\displaystyle\gamma+\gamma\to e^{-}+e^{+}
, where the interacting part of the process is described by the usual QED coupling
\displaystyle e\bar{\psi}(x)\gamma^{\mu}\psi(x)A_{\mu}(x)
(and its dual).
One important point in the calculation of the transition amplitude is the use of the Bogoliubov transformation between the
\displaystyle T=0
and thermal creation/annihilation operators, this is necessary because the in/out states are thermal states (operators), while the operators in the interacting Lagrangian are not thermal.
However, this is not the only thermal contribution to the scattering matrix, but the fermionic propagator also has a finite temperature part.

After establishing the main operations of the thermofield dynamics formalism, we considered the center of mass frame to compute the squared transition amplitude.
With this step we finally obtained the desired expression for the differential cross section Breit-Wheeler process at finite temperature.
In the zero temperature limit (
\displaystyle\beta\rightarrow\infty
) our results reproduced the known literature
Bottcher:1997kf
;
torgrimsson:2018
;
Ribeyre
.
Remarkably, the thermal dependence of the differential cross section (
45
) had a simple form which allowed to compute the angular integration in order to obtain the Breit-Wheeler process cross section valid for any value of
\displaystyle\beta
.
Moreover, in order to have a better grasp about the thermal behavior of the cross section, we considered the high temperature regime, which led to a thermal dependence in terms of the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution as we would expect.
