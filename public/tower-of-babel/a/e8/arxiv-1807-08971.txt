ASYMPTOTICALLY OPTIMAL QUICKEST CHANGE DETECTION IN MULTISTREAM DATA—PART 1: GENERAL STOCHASTIC MODELS
Alexander G. Tartakovskylabel=e1]agt@phystech.edu t1

Abstract
Assume that there are multiple data streams (channels, sensors) and in each stream the process of interest produces generally dependent and non-identically distributed observations. When the process is in a normal mode (in-control), the (pre-change) distribution is known, but when the process becomes abnormal there is a parametric uncertainty, i.e., the post-change (out-of-control) distribution is known only partially up to a parameter. Both the change point and the post-change parameter are unknown. Moreover, the change affects an unknown subset of streams, so that the number of affected streams and their location are unknown in advance. A good changepoint detection procedure should detect the change as soon as possible after its occurrence while controlling for a risk of false alarms. We consider a Bayesian setup with a given prior distribution of the change point and propose two sequential mixture-based change detection rules, one mixes a Shiryaev-type statistic over both the unknown subset of affected streams and the unknown post-change parameter and another mixes a Shiryaev–Roberts-type statistic. These rules generalize the mixture detection procedures studied by Tartakovsky (2018) in a single-stream case. We provide sufficient conditions under which the proposed multistream change detection procedures are first-order asymptotically optimal with respect to moments of the delay to detection as the probability of false alarm approaches zero.

Introduction

In most surveillance applications with unknown points of change, including the classical Statistical Process Control sphere,
the baseline (pre-change, in-control) distribution of observed data is known, but the post-change out-of-control distribution is not completely known. There are
three conventional approaches in this case: (i) to select a representative value of the post-change parameter and apply efficient detection procedures tuned to this value such as the
Shiryaev procedure, the Shiryaev–Roberts procedure or CUSUM, (ii) to select a mixing measure over the parameter space and apply mixture-type procedures,
(iii) to estimate the parameter and apply adaptive schemes. In the present article, we consider a more general case where the change occurs in multiple data streams and more
general multi-stream double-mixture-type change detection procedures, assuming that the number and location of affected data streams are also unknown.

To be more specific, suppose there are
N
data streams
\{X_{i}(n)\}_{n\geqslant 1}
,
i=1,\dots,N
, observed sequentially in time subject to a change at an unknown time
\nu\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
,
so that
X_{i}(1),\dots,X_{i}(\nu)
are generated by one stochastic model and
X_{i}(\nu+1),X_{i}(\nu+2),\dots
by another model when the change occurs in the
i
th stream.
The change in distributions happens at a subset of streams

{\mathcal{B}}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
of a size (cardinality)
1\leqslant|{\mathcal{B}}|\leqslant K\leqslant N
, where
K
is an assumed maximal number of streams that can be affected, which can be and often is substantially smaller than
N
.
A sequential detection rule is a stopping time
T
with respect to an observed sequence

\{{\mathbf{X}}(n)\}_{n\geqslant 1}
,
{\mathbf{X}}(n)=(X_{1}(n),\dots,X_{N}(n))
, i.e.,
T
is an integer-valued random variable, such that the event
\{T=n\}

belongs to the sigma-algebra
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}=\sigma({\mathbf{X}}^{n})
generated by observations
{\mathbf{X}}(1),\dots,{\mathbf{X}}(n)
.
A false alarm is raised when the detection is declared before the change occurs.
We want to detect the change with as small a delay as possible while controlling for a risk of false alarms.

To begin, assume for the sake of simplicity that the observations are independent across data streams,
but have a fairly general stochastic structure within streams. So if we let
{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n}=(X_{i}(1),\dots,X_{i}(n))
denote the sample of size
n
in the
i
th stream and if

\{f_{\theta_{i},n}(X_{i}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})\}_{n\geqslant 1}
,
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
is a parametric family of conditional densities of
X_{i}(n)

given
{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1}
, then when
\nu=\infty
(there is no change) the parameter
\theta_{i}
is equal to the known value
\theta_{0,i}
, i.e.,

f_{\theta_{i},n}(X_{i}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})=f_{\theta_{i,0},n}(X_{i}({n}|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})
for all

n\geqslant 1
and when
\nu=k<\infty
, then
\theta_{i}=\theta_{i,1}\neq\theta_{i,0}
, i.e.,
f_{\theta_{i},n}(X_{i}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})=f_{\theta_{0,i},n}(X_{i}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})
for
n\leqslant k
and

f_{\theta,n}(X_{i}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})=f_{\theta_{i,1},n}(X_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
for
n>k
. Not only the point of change
\nu
, but also the subset
{\mathcal{B}}
, its size
|{\mathcal{B}}|
, and the post-change parameters

\theta_{i,1}
are unknown.

In the case where
f_{\theta_{i},n}(X_{i}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})=f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{i}(n))
, i.e., when the observations in the
i
th stream are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) according to a distribution
with density
f_{\theta_{0,i}}(X_{i}(n))
in the pre-change mode and with density
f_{\theta_{1,i}}(X_{n})
in the post-change mode this problem was considered in

[

,

,

,
16
,

]
.
Specifically, in the case of a known post-change parameter and
K=1
(i.e., when only one stream can be affected but it is unknown which one), Tartakovsky
[

proposed to use a multi-chart CUSUM procedure that raises an alarm when one of the partial CUSUM statistics exceeds a threshold. This procedure is very simple,

that are second-order minimax. Xie and Siegmund
[

They suggested a rule that combines mixture likelihood ratios that incorporate an assumption about the proportion of affected data streams with the generalized

detection procedure in terms of the ALR to false alarm and the expected delay as well as MC simulations. Chan
[

In the present paper, we consider a Bayesian problem with a general prior distribution of the change point and we generalize the results of Tartakovsky
[
12

for a single data stream and a general stochastic model to multiple data streams with an unknown pattern, i.e., when the size and location of the affected streams are unknown.
It is assumed that the observations can be dependent and non-identically distributed in data streams and even across the streams. We introduce two double-mixture detection procedures – the first one

The resulting statistics are then compared to appropriate thresholds. The main contribution of the present article (Part 1), as well as of the companion article (Part 2), is two-fold. In Part 1,
we present a general theory for very general stochastic models, providing sufficient conditions under which the suggested detection procedures are first-order asymptotically optimal.
In the companion article, we will consider the “i.i.d.” case, where data streams are mutually independent and also data
in each stream are independent, and we will provide higher-order asymptotic approximations to the operating characteristics – the average detection delay and the probability of false alarm.
We will also examine the accuracy of these approximations and compare the performance of several procedures by Monte Carlo simulations.

2
A multistream model and change detection procedures based on mixtures

2.1
The general multistream model

Consider the multistream scenario where the observations
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
are sequentially acquired in
N
streams (sources, channels), i.e.,
in the
i
th stream one observes a sequence
X_{i}=\{X_{i}(n)\}_{n\geqslant 1}
, where
i\in[N]:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Let
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
denote the probability measure corresponding to the
sequence of observations
\{{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
from all
N
streams when there is never a change (
\nu=\infty
) in any of the components and, for
k=0,1,\dots
and
{\mathcal{B}}\subset[N]
,
let
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}}}
denote the measure corresponding to the sequence
\{{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
when
\nu=k<\infty
and the change occurs in a subset
{\mathcal{B}}
of the set
{\mathcal{P}}

(i.e.,
X_{i}(\nu+1)
,
i\in{\mathcal{B}}
is the first post-change observation). It is convenient to parametrize the post-change distribution
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,\eta}

of
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
by an
N
-dimensional parameter vector,

\eta=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{N})
, where each component
\eta_{i}
takes values in the binary set
\{0,1\}
,
i\in[N]
.
Let
{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty}
denote the hypothesis that there is no change, under which all components of
\eta
are equal to 0. For any subset of components,
{\mathcal{B}}\subset[N]
, let
{\mathsf{H}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}}}

be the hypothesis according to which only the components of
\eta
in
{\mathcal{B}}
are non-zero after the change point
\nu=k
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta_{i}&=0,\qquad i\in[N]\qquad\text{under}\quad{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty}\\
\eta_{i}&=\begin{cases}1,&\quad i\in{\mathcal{B}},\\
0,&\quad i\notin{\mathcal{B}},\end{cases}\qquad\text{under}\quad{\mathsf{H}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}}}.\end{split}

(2.1)

The set
{\mathcal{P}}
is a class of subsets of
[N]
that incorporates available prior information regarding the subset of non-zero components of
\eta
. For example,
when it is known that
exactly

K
streams can be affected after the change occurs, then
{\mathcal{P}}=\widetilde{{\mathcal{P}}}_{K}
, and when it is known that
at most

K

channels can be affected, then
{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{P}}_{K}
, where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{{\mathcal{P}}}_{K}&=\{{\mathcal{B}}\subset[N]:|{\mathcal{B}}|=K\},\\
{\mathcal{P}}_{K}&=\{{\mathcal{B}}\subset[N]:1\leqslant|{\mathcal{B}}|\leqslant K\}.\end{split}

(2.2)

Hereafter we denote by
|{\mathcal{B}}|
the size of a subset
{\mathcal{B}}
, i.e., the number of non-zero components under
{\mathsf{H}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}}}
and
|{\mathcal{P}}|
denotes the size of class
{\mathcal{P}}
, i.e., the number of possible alternatives
in
{\mathcal{P}}
. Note that
|{\mathcal{P}}|
takes maximum value when there is no prior information regarding the subset of affected components of
\eta
, i.e., when
{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{P}}_{N}
, in which case
|{\mathcal{P}}|=2^{N}-1
.

We will write
{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n}=(X_{i}(1),\dots,X_{i}(n))
for the concatenation of the first
n
observations from the
i
th data stream and
{\mathbf{X}}^{n}=({\mathbf{X}}(1),\dots,{\mathbf{X}}(n))
for the concatenation of the first
n

observations from all
N
data streams. Let
\{g({\mathbf{X}}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}_{n\geqslant 1}
and
\{f_{{\mathcal{B}}}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}_{n\geqslant 1}
be sequences of conditional densities of
{\mathbf{X}}(n)

given
{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1}
, which may depend on
n
, i.e.,
g=g_{n}
and
f_{\mathcal{B}}=f_{{\mathcal{B}},n}
. For the general non-i.i.d. changepoint model,
which we are interested in, the joint density
p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|H_{k,{\mathcal{B}}})
under hypothesis
{\mathsf{H}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}}}
can be written as follows

\displaystyle p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|H_{k,{\mathcal{B}}})

\displaystyle=f_{\infty}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})=\prod_{t=1}^{n}g({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})\quad\text{for}~~\nu=k\geqslant n,

(2.3)

\displaystyle p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|H_{k,{\mathcal{B}}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{k}g({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})\times\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}f_{{\mathcal{B}}}({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})\quad\text{for}~~\nu=k<n,

(2.4)

where
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}
. Therefore,
g({\mathbf{X}}_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
is the pre-change conditional density and
f_{{\mathcal{B}}}({\mathbf{X}}_{n}|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
is the post-change conditional density given that the change
occurs in the subset
{\mathcal{B}}
.

In most practical applications, the post-change distribution is not completely known – it depends on an unknown (generally multi-dimensional) parameter
\theta\in\Theta
,
so that the model (
2.4
)
may be treated only as a benchmark for a more practical case where the post-change densities
f_{{\mathcal{B}}}({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})
are replaced by
f_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|H_{k,{\mathcal{B}}},\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{k}g({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})\times\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}f_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})\quad\text{for}~~\nu=k<n.

(2.5)

In what follows we assume that the change point
\nu
is a random variable independent of the observations with prior distribution

\pi_{k}={\mathsf{P}}(\nu=k)
,
k=0,1,2,\dots
with
\pi_{k}>0
for
k\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}=\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. We will also assume that a change point may take negative values, which means that the change has
occurred by the time the observations became available. However, the detailed structure of the distribution
{\mathsf{P}}(\nu=k)
for
k=-1,-2,\dots
is not important.
The only value which matters is the total probability
q={\mathsf{P}}(\nu\leqslant-1)
of the change being in effect before the observations become available.

2.2
Double-mixture change detection procedures

We begin by considering the most general scenario where the observations across streams are dependent and then go on tackling the scenario where the streams are mutually independent.

2.2.1
The general case

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(n)=f_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}({\mathbf{X}}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})/g({\mathbf{X}}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
. Note that in the general non-i.i.d. case the statistic

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(n)={\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{(k)}(n)
depends on the change point
\nu=k
since the post-change density
f_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}({\mathbf{X}}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})
may depend on
k
.
The likelihood ratio (LR) of the hypothesis “
{\mathsf{H}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}}}:\nu=k,\eta_{i}=1~\text{for}~i\in{\mathcal{B}}
” that the change occurs at
\nu=k
in the subset of streams
{\mathcal{B}}
against the no-change hypothesis
“
{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty}:\nu=\infty
” based on the sample
{\mathbf{X}}^{n}=({\mathbf{X}}(1),\dots,{\mathbf{X}}(n))
is given by the product

LR_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,n)=\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(t),\quad n>k

and we set
LR_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,n)=1
for
n\leqslant k
. For
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
and
\theta\in\Theta
, where
{\mathcal{P}}
is an arbitrary class of subsets of
[N]
, define the statistic

S_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{\pi}(n)=\frac{1}{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}\left[qLR_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(0,n)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\pi_{k}\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}LR_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,n)\right],~~n\geqslant 1,~~S_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{\pi}(0)=q/(1-q),

(2.6)

which is the Shiryaev-type statistic for detection of a change when it happens in a subset of streams
{\mathcal{B}}
and the post-change parameter is
\theta
.

Next, let

{\mathbf{p}}=\left\{p_{\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}\right\},\quad p_{\mathcal{B}}>0~\forall~{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}},\quad\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}=1

be the probability mass function on
[N]
(mixing measure), and define the mixture statistic

S_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}^{\pi}(n)=\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}S_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{\pi}(n),\quad S_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}^{\pi}(0)=q/(1-q).

(2.7)

This statistic can be also represented as

\begin{split}S_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}^{\pi}(n)&=\frac{1}{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}\left[q\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}(0,n)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\pi_{k}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}(k,n)\right],\end{split}

(2.8)

where

\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}(k,n)=\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}LR_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,n)

is the mixture LR.

When the parameter
\theta
is unknown there are two conventional approaches – either to maximize or average (mix) over
\theta
. Introduce a mixing measure

W(\theta)
,
\int_{\Theta}\rm{d}W(\theta)=1
, which can be interpreted as a prior distribution and
define the double LR-mixture (average LR)

\begin{split}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,n)&=\int_{\Theta}\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}LR_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,n)\,\mathrm{d}W(\theta)\\
&=\int_{\Theta}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}(k,n)\,\mathrm{d}W(\theta),\quad k<n\end{split}

(2.9)

and the double-mixture Shiryaev-type statistic

\begin{split}S_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(n)&=\int_{\Theta}\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}S_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{\pi}(n)\,\mathrm{d}W(\theta)\\
&=\frac{1}{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}\left[q\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(0,n)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\pi_{k}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,n)\right].\end{split}

(2.10)

The corresponding double-mixture LR-based detection procedure is given by the stopping rule which is the first time
n\geqslant 1
such that the statistic
S_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(n)
hits the level
A>0
, i.e.,

T_{A}^{W}=\inf\left\{n\geqslant 1:S_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(n)\geqslant A\right\}.

(2.11)

Another popular statistic for detecting a change from
\{g({\mathbf{X}}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}
to
\{f_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}({\mathbf{X}}(n)|{\mathbf{X}}^{n-1})\}
, which has certain optimality properties

[
8
,
15
,
9
,
14
]
, is the generalized Shiryaev–Roberts (SR) statistic

R_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(n)=\omega LR_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(0,n)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}LR_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,n),\quad n\geqslant 1,~~R_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(0)=\omega

(2.12)

with a non-negative head-start
\omega\geqslant 0
. For a fixed value of
\theta
, introduce the mixture statistic

\begin{split}R_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}(n)&=\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}R_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(n)\\
&=\omega\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}(0,n)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}(k,n)\quad n\geqslant 1,~~R_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}(0)=\omega,\end{split}

(2.13)

and the generalized double-mixture SR statistic

\begin{split}R_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(n)&=\int_{\Theta}\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}R_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(n)\,\mathrm{d}W(\theta)\\
&=\omega\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(0,n)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,n),\quad n\geqslant 1,~~R_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(0)=\omega\end{split}

(2.14)

(with a non-negative head-start
\omega
) as well as the corresponding stopping rule

\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W}=\inf\left\{n\geqslant 1:R_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(n)\geqslant A\right\},\quad A>0.

(2.15)

Note that we consider a very general stochastic model where not only the observations in streams may be dependent and non-identically distributed, but also the streams may be mutually dependent.
In this very general case, computing statistics
S_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(n)
and
R_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(n)
is problematic even when the statistics in data streams
S_{i}^{\pi}(n)
and
R_{i}(n)
,
i=1,\dots,N
,
can be computed. The computational problem becomes manageable when the data between data streams are independent, as discussed in the next subsection.

2.2.2
Independent streams

Consider now a special scenario where the data across streams are independent. Note that in the case of independent streams the post-change parameters can be assumed different in streams, i.e.,

\theta=\theta_{i}
for the
i
th stream,
i=1,\dots,N
. In contrast to the general case of dependent streams, this does not lead to an additional complication. Thus, we have

\begin{split}p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|H_{k,{\mathcal{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}})&=f_{\infty}({\mathbf{X}}^{n})=\prod_{t=1}^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}(X_{i}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{t-1})\quad\text{for}~\nu=k\geqslant n,\\
p({\mathbf{X}}^{n}|H_{k,{\mathcal{B}}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}})&=\prod_{t=1}^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}(X_{i}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{t-1})\times\\
&\quad\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{B}}}f_{i,\theta_{i}}(X_{i}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{t-1})\prod_{i\notin{\mathcal{B}}}g_{i}(X_{i}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{t-1})\quad\text{for}~\nu=k<n,\end{split}

(2.16)

where
g_{i}(X_{i}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{t-1})
and
f_{i,\theta_{i}}(X_{i}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{t-1})
are conditional pre- and post-change densities in the
i
th data stream, respectively, and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}=(\theta_{i},i\in{\mathcal{B}})
.
So the LRs are

LR_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta_{\mathcal{B}}}(k,n)=\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{B}}}LR_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,n),\quad LR_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,n)=\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i,\theta_{i}}(t),\quad n>k,

(2.17)

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{i,\theta_{i}}(t)=f_{i,\theta_{i}}(X_{i}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{t})/g_{i}(X_{i}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{t})
.

Assume in addition that the mixing measure is such that

p_{\mathcal{B}}=C({\mathcal{P}}_{K})\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{B}}}p_{i},\quad C({\mathcal{P}}_{K})=\left(\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{K}}\prod_{i\in{\mathcal{B}}}p_{i}\right)^{-1}.

Then the mixture LR is

\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(k,n)=C({\mathcal{P}}_{K})\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in\widetilde{{\mathcal{P}}}_{i}}\prod_{j\in{\mathcal{B}}}p_{j}LR_{j,\theta_{i}}(k,n),

and its computational complexity is polynomial in the number of data streams. Moreover, in the special, perhaps most interesting and difficult case of
K=N
and
p_{j}=p
, we obtain

\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(k,n)=C({\mathcal{P}}_{N})\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1+pLR_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,n)\right)-1\right],

(2.18)

so its computational complexity is only
O(N)
. The representation (
2.18
) corresponds to the case when each stream is affected independently with probability
p/(1+p)
,
the assumption that was made in
[
19
]
.

3
Asymptotic optimality problems and assumptions

Let
{\mathsf{E}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
and
{\mathsf{E}}_{\infty}
denote expectations under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
and
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
, respectively, where
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
corresponds to
model (
2.5
) with an unknown parameter
\theta\in\Theta
. Define the probability measure

{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}({\mathcal{A}}\times\mathcal{K})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\,\pi_{k}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left({\mathcal{A}}\right)
under which the change point
\nu
has distribution
\pi=\{\pi_{k}\}
and the model for the observations
is of the form (
2.3
),(
2.5
), i.e.,
{\mathbf{X}}(t)
has conditional density
g({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})
if
\nu\leqslant k
and conditional density

f_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})
if
\nu>k
and the change occurs in the subset
{\mathcal{B}}
with the parameter
\theta
. Let
{\mathsf{E}}^{\pi}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
denote the corresponding expectation.

For
r\geqslant 1
,
\nu=k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
, and
\theta\in\Theta
introduce the risk associated with the conditional
r
th moment of the detection delay

{\mathcal{R}}^{r}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(T)={\mathsf{E}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left[(T-k)^{r}\,|\,T>k\right]
.
In a Bayesian setting, the risk associated with the moments of delay to detection is

{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}^{r}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(T):={\mathsf{E}}^{\pi}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}[(T-\nu)^{r}|T>\nu]=\frac{{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}}\pi_{k}{\mathcal{R}}^{r}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(T){\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T>k)}{1-\mathsf{PFA}(T)},

(3.1)

where

\mathsf{PFA}(T)={\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(T\leqslant\nu)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant k)

(3.2)

is the weighted probability of false alarm (PFA) that corresponds to the risk associated with a false alarm. Note that in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) we used the fact that

{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(T\leqslant k)={\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant k)
since the event
\{T\leqslant k\}
depends on the observations
{\mathbf{X}}_{1},\dots{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
generated by the pre-change probability measure
{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}
since
by our convention
{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
is the last pre-change observation if
\nu=k
.

In this article, we are interested in the Bayesian (constrained) optimization problem

\inf_{\{T:\mathsf{PFA}(T)\leqslant\alpha\}}\,{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{r}(T)\quad\text{for all}~{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}},~~\theta\in\Theta.

(3.3)

However, in general this problem is intractable for every value of the PFA
\alpha\in(0,1)
. So we will focus on the asymptotic problem assuming that the PFA
\alpha
approaches zero.
Specifically, we will be interested in proving that the double-mixture detection procedure
T_{A}^{W}
is first-order uniformly asymptotically optimal for all possible subsets
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
where the change
may occur and all parameter values
\theta\in\Theta
, i.e.,

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{\displaystyle\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{r}(T)}{{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{r}(T_{A}^{W})}=1\quad\text{for all}~{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}},~~\theta\in\Theta

(3.4)

and

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{\displaystyle\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathcal{R}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{r}(T)}{{\mathcal{R}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{r}(T_{A}^{W})}=1\quad\text{for all}~{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}},~\theta\in\Theta~\text{and all}~\nu=k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(3.5)

where
{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)=\{T:\mathsf{PFA}(T)\leqslant\alpha\}
is the class of detection procedures for which the PFA does not exceed a prescribed number
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
A=A_{\alpha}
is suitably selected.

First-order asymptotic optimality properties of the double-mixture SR-type detection procedure
\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W}
under certain conditions will be also established.

Instead of the constrained optimization problem (
3.3
) one may be also interested in the unconstrained Bayes problem with the average (integrated) risk function

\begin{split}\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T)&={\mathsf{E}}\left[{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{T\leqslant\nu\}}}+c\,(T-\nu)^{r}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{T>\nu\}}}\right]\\
&=\mathsf{PFA}(T)+c\,\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{{\mathcal{B}}}\int_{\Theta}{\mathsf{E}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{\pi}[(T-\nu)^{+}]^{r}\,{\mathrm{d}}W(\theta),\end{split}

(3.6)

where
c>0
is the cost of delay per unit of time and
r\geqslant 1
. An unknown post-change parameter
\theta
and an unknown location of the change pattern
{\mathcal{B}}
are now assumed random and
the weight functions
W(\theta)
and
p_{\mathcal{B}}
are interpreted as the prior distributions of
\theta
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, respectively. The first-order asymptotic problem is

\lim_{c\to 0}\frac{\displaystyle\inf_{T\geqslant 0}\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T)}{\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T_{A}^{W})}=1,

(3.7)

where threshold
A=A_{c,r}
that depends on the cost
c
should be suitably selected.

While we consider a general prior and a very general stochastic model for the observations in streams and between streams, to study asymptotic optimality properties we still
need to impose certain constraints on the prior distribution
\{\pi_{k}\}
and on the general stochastic model (
2.3
)–(
2.4
) that guarantee
asymptotic stability of the detection statistics as the sample size increases.

In what follows, we assume that the prior distribution
\pi^{\alpha}=\{\pi_{k}^{\alpha}\}
may depend on
\alpha
and the following condition is imposed:

\mathbf{CP}\mathbf{1}
.
For some
0\leqslant\mu_{\alpha}<\infty
and
0\leqslant\mu<\infty
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left|\log\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\pi_{k}^{\alpha}\right|=\mu_{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\mu_{\alpha}=\mu.

(3.8)

The class of prior distributions satisfying condition
\mathbf{CP}\mathbf{1}
will be denoted by
{\mathbf{C}}(\mu)
.

For establishing asymptotic optimality properties of change detection procedures we will assume in addition that the following two condition hold:

\mathbf{CP}{\mathbf{2}}
.
If
\mu_{\alpha}>0
for all
\alpha
and
\mu=0
, then
\mu_{\alpha}
approaches zero at such rate that for some
r\geqslant 1

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}^{\alpha}|\log\pi_{k}^{\alpha}|^{r}}}{|\log\alpha|^{r}}=0.

(3.9)

\mathbf{CP}{\mathbf{3}}
.
For all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{|\log\pi_{k}^{\alpha}|}{|\log\alpha|}=0.

(3.10)

Note that if
\mu>0
, then the prior distribution has an exponential right tail (asymptotically) with the positive parameter
\mu
, in which case, condition (
3.9
) holds since

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}^{\alpha}|\log\pi_{k}^{\alpha}|^{r}<\infty
for all
r>0
. If
\mu=0
, the distribution has a heavy tail (at least asymptotically) and
we cannot allow this distribution to have a too heavy tail, which will generate very large time intervals between change points.
This is guaranteed by condition
\mathbf{CP}{\mathbf{2}}
. Note that condition
\mathbf{CP}{\mathbf{1}}
excludes light-tail distributions with unbounded hazard rates for which
\mu=\infty

and the time-intervals with a change point are very short (e.g., Gaussian-type or Weibull-type with the shape parameter
\kappa>1
).
In this case, prior information dominates information obtained from the observations, the change can be easily detected at early stages, and the asymptotic analysis is impractical. Note also that
if the prior distribution does not depend on
\alpha
, then in condition
\mathbf{CP}\mathbf{1}

\mu_{\alpha}=\mu
and
\mathbf{CP}\mathbf{2}
holds when
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}|\log\pi_{k}|^{r}<\infty

for some
r\geqslant 1
. These conditions were used in
[
11
]
.

For
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
and
\theta\in\Theta
, introduce the log-likelihood ratio (LLR) process
\{\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,n)\}_{n\geqslant k+1}
between the hypotheses
“
{\mathsf{H}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}}},\theta
” (
k=0,1,\dots
) and
{\mathsf{H}}_{\infty}
:

\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,n)=\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}\,\log\frac{f_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})}{g({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})},\quad n>k

(
\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,n)=0
for
n\leqslant k
).

Define

\beta_{M,k}(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{B}},\theta)={\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left\{\frac{1}{M}\max_{1\leqslant n\leqslant M}\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,k+n)\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\right\}

and for
\delta>0
define
\Gamma_{\delta,\theta}=\{\vartheta\in\Theta\,:\,|\vartheta-\theta|<\delta\}
and

\Upsilon_{r}(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{B}},\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{r-1}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\inf_{\vartheta\in\Gamma_{\delta,\theta}}\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}},\vartheta}(k,k+n)<I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}-\varepsilon\right\}.

Regarding the general model for the observations (
2.3
), (
2.5
) we assume that the following two conditions are satisfied:

{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
.
There exist positive and finite numbers
I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
(
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,
\theta\in\Theta
) such that the LLR
n^{-1}\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,k+n)\to I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
in

{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
-probability and for any
\varepsilon>0

\lim_{M\to\infty}\beta_{M,k}(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{B}},\theta)=0\quad\text{for all}~k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}},\theta\in\Theta;

(3.11)

{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta=\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that
W(\Gamma_{\delta,\theta})>0

and for any
\varepsilon>0
and some
r\geqslant 1

\Upsilon_{r}(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{B}},\theta)<\infty\quad\text{for all}~{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}},\theta\in\Theta.

(3.12)

4
Asymptotic lower bounds for moments of the detection delay and average risk function

In order to establish asymptotic optimality of detection procedures we first obtain, under condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
, asymptotic (as
\alpha\to 0
) lower bounds for moments
of the detection delay
{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}^{r}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(T)={\mathsf{E}}^{\pi}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left[\left(T-\nu\right)^{r}|T>\nu\right]
and
{\mathcal{R}}^{r}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}={\mathsf{E}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left[\left(T-k\right)^{r}|T>k\right]

of any detection procedure
T
from class
{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)
.
In the following sections, we show that under
condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
these bounds are attained for the double-mixture procedure
T_{A}^{W}
uniformly for all
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
and
\theta\in\Theta
and
that the same is true for the double-mixture procedures
\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W}
when the prior distribution is either heavy-tailed or
has an exponential tail with a small parameter
\mu
. We also establish the asymptotic lower bound for the integrated risk
\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T)
as
c\to 0
in the class of all Markov times and show that it is
attained by the double-mixture procedures
T_{A}^{W}
and
\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W}
.

Define

{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{r}(T)=\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\Theta}{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{r}(T)\,{\mathrm{d}}W(\theta)

(4.1)

and

D_{\mu,r}=\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}+\mu}\right)^{r}\,{\mathrm{d}}W(\theta).

(4.2)

Asymptotic lower bounds for all positive moments of the detection delay and the integrated risk are specified in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let, for some
\mu\geqslant 0
, the prior distribution belong to class
{\mathbf{C}}(\mu)
.
Assume that for some positive and finite numbers
I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
(
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,
\theta\in\Theta
) condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
holds. Then for all
r>0
and all

{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,
\theta\in\Theta

\liminf_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{{\displaystyle\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}}{\mathcal{R}}^{r}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(T)}{|\log\alpha|^{r}}\geqslant\frac{1}{(I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}+\mu)^{r}},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(4.3)

\liminf_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{{\displaystyle\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}}{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}^{r}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(T)}{|\log\alpha|^{r}}\geqslant\frac{1}{(I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}+\mu)^{r}},

(4.4)

and for all
r>0

\liminf_{c\to 0}\frac{{\displaystyle\inf_{T\geqslant 0}}\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T)}{c|\log c|^{r}}\geqslant D_{\mu,r}.

(4.5)

Proof.

The methodology of the proof is essentially analogous to that used in the proofs of the lower bounds in Tartakovsky
[
11
,
12
]
for a single stream change
detection problem with slightly different assumptions on the
prior distribution. In particular, since the vector
({\mathcal{B}},\theta)=\tilde{\theta}
is also an unknown parameter, the lower bound (
4.3
) follows from Lemma 1 in
[
12
]

if
\mu_{\alpha}=\mu
for all
\alpha
(i.e., when
\pi_{k}^{\alpha}
does not depend on
\alpha
) and from Lemma 3 in
[
12
]

if
\mu_{\alpha}\to\mu=0
by simple replacing
\theta
by
\tilde{\theta}
. A generalization of the proof under condition
\mathbf{CP}\mathbf{1}
introduced in the present article has several technical
details that are presented
below. We omit certain intermediate inequalities, which follow from the proofs given in
[
11
,
12
]
.

For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0
, define

N_{\alpha}=N_{\alpha}(\varepsilon,\delta,{\mathcal{B}},\theta)=\frac{(1-\varepsilon)|\log\alpha|}{I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}+\mu+\delta}

and let
\Pi^{\alpha}_{k}={\mathsf{P}}(\nu>k)
(
\Pi_{-1}^{\alpha}=1-q^{\alpha}
).
Using the fact
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(T>k)={\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T>k)
and Chebyshev’s inequality, as in (A.1) in
[
12
]
, we obtain

\begin{split}&\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathcal{R}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{r}(T)\\
&\geqslant N_{\alpha}^{r}\left[\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T>k)-\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k<T\leqslant k+N_{\alpha})\right]\\
&\geqslant N_{\alpha}^{r}\left[1-\alpha/\Pi_{k-1}^{\alpha}-\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k<T\leqslant k+N_{\alpha})\right],\end{split}

(4.6)

where we used the inequality

\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T>k)\geqslant 1-\alpha/\Pi_{k-1}^{\alpha},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(4.7)

which follows from the fact that for any stopping rule
T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)
,

\alpha\geqslant\sum_{i=k}^{\infty}\pi_{i}^{\alpha}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant i)\geqslant{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant k)\sum_{i=k}^{\infty}\pi_{i}^{\alpha}.

Thus, to prove the lower bound (
4.3
) it suffices to show that for arbitrary small
\varepsilon
and
\delta
and every fixed
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k<T\leqslant k+N_{\alpha})\to 0\quad\text{as}~\alpha\to 0.

(4.8)

Introduce

\displaystyle U_{N_{\alpha},k}(T)

\displaystyle=e^{(1+\varepsilon)I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}N_{\alpha}}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}\left(k<T\leqslant k+N_{\alpha}\right),

By inequality (3.6) in
[
17
]
, for any stopping time
T
,

{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left(k<T\leqslant k+N_{\alpha}\right)\leqslant U_{N_{\alpha},k}(T)+\beta_{N_{\alpha},k}(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{B}},\theta).

(4.9)

Using inequality (
4.7
) and the fact that by condition (
3.8
), for all sufficiently large
N_{\alpha}
(small
\alpha
), there exists a (small)
\delta
such that

\frac{|\log\Pi^{\alpha}_{k-1+N_{\alpha}}|}{k-1+N_{\alpha}}\leqslant\mu+\delta,

we obtain that for all sufficiently small
\alpha

\displaystyle U_{N_{\alpha},k}(T)

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{(1+\varepsilon)I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}N_{\alpha}}{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(T\leqslant k+N_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leqslant\alpha\,e^{(1+\varepsilon)I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}N_{\alpha}}/\Pi^{\alpha}_{k-1+N_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left\{(1+\varepsilon)I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}N_{\alpha}-|\log\alpha|+(k-1+N_{\alpha})\frac{|\log\Pi^{\alpha}_{k-1+N_{\alpha}}|}{k-1+N_{\alpha}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left\{(1+\varepsilon)I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}N_{\alpha}-|\log\alpha|+(k-1+N_{\alpha})(\mu+\delta)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\varepsilon^{2}+(\mu+\delta)\varepsilon}{I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}+\mu+\delta}|\log\alpha|+(\mu+\delta)(k-1)\right\},

where the last term is less or equal to

\exp\left\{-\varepsilon^{2}|\log\alpha|+(\mu+\delta)(k-1)\right\}:=\overline{U}_{\alpha,k}(\varepsilon,\delta)

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Thus, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

U_{N_{\alpha},k}(T)\leqslant\overline{U}_{\alpha,k}(\varepsilon,\delta).

(4.10)

Since
\overline{U}_{\alpha,k}(\varepsilon,\delta)
does not depend on the stopping time
T
and the value of
\overline{U}_{\alpha,k}(\varepsilon,\delta)
goes to
0
as
\alpha\to 0

for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and any
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
, it follows that

\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}U_{N_{\alpha},k}(T)\to 0\quad\text{as}~\alpha\to 0~~\text{for any fixed}~k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(4.11)

Also, by condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
,
\beta_{N_{\alpha},k}(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{B}},\theta)\to 0
for all
\varepsilon>0
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,
\theta\in\Theta
, and therefore, (
4.8
) holds. This completes
the proof of the lower bound (
4.3
).

We now prove the lower bound (
4.4
). Let
K_{\alpha}=K_{\alpha}(\varepsilon)=1+\lfloor\varepsilon^{2}\,|\log\alpha|\rfloor
.
Using (
4.9
) and (
4.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathsf{P}}^{\pi}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(\nu<T\leqslant\nu+N_{\alpha})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}^{\alpha}\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left(k<T\leqslant k+N_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathsf{P}}(\nu>K_{\alpha})+\sum_{k=0}^{K_{\alpha}}\pi_{k}^{\alpha}\beta_{N_{\alpha},k}(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{B}},\theta)+\max_{0\leqslant k\leqslant K_{\alpha}}\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}U_{N_{\alpha},k}(T)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathsf{P}}(\nu>K_{\alpha})+\sum_{k=0}^{K_{\alpha}}\pi_{k}^{\alpha}\beta_{N_{\alpha},k}(\varepsilon,{\mathcal{B}},\theta)+\overline{U}_{\alpha,K_{\alpha}}(\varepsilon,\delta).

(4.12)

If condition (
3.8
) holds with
\mu>0
, then
\log{\mathsf{P}}(\nu>K_{\alpha})\sim-\mu\,\varepsilon^{2}|\log\alpha|\to-\infty
as
\alpha\to 0
, so the probability

{\mathsf{P}}(\nu>K_{\alpha})
goes to
0
as
\alpha\to 0
and the same is true if
\mu_{\alpha}>0
for all
\alpha
and
\mu=0
since, in this case,

\log{\mathsf{P}}(\nu>K_{\alpha})\sim-\varepsilon^{3}|\log\alpha|\to-\infty
for a sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. If
\mu_{\alpha}=\mu=0
for all
\alpha
, then
{\mathsf{P}}(\nu>K_{\alpha})\to 0
as well since

\sum_{k=K_{\alpha}}^{\infty}\pi_{k}^{0}\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}0.

By condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
, the second term in (
4
) also goes to zero. Obviously,
\overline{U}_{\alpha,K_{\alpha}}(\varepsilon,\delta)\to 0
as
\alpha\to 0
, and therefore,
all three terms go to zero as
\alpha\to 0
for all
\varepsilon,\delta>0
,
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,
\theta\in\Theta
, so that

\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{\pi}(\nu<T\leqslant\nu+N_{\alpha})\to 0\quad\text{as}~\alpha\to 0.

(4.13)

Using Chebyshev’s inequality, similarly to (
4.6
) we obtain that

\begin{split}\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{r}(T)\geqslant N_{\alpha}^{r}\left[1-\alpha-\sup_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\mathsf{P}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{\pi}(\nu<T\leqslant\nu+N_{\alpha})\right].\end{split}

(4.14)

By (
4.13
) and (
4.14
), asymptotically as
\alpha\to 0

\inf_{T\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)}{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}^{r}(T)\geqslant\left[\frac{(1-\varepsilon)|\log\alpha|}{I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}+\mu+\delta}\right]^{r}(1+o(1)),

where
\varepsilon
and
\delta
can be arbitrarily small, so that the lower bound (
4.4
) follows.

In order to prove the lower bound (
4.5
) let us define the function

G_{c,r}(A)=\frac{1}{A}+D_{\mu,r}c(\log A)^{r}.

(4.15)

It is easily seen that
\min_{A>0}G_{c,r}(A)=G_{c,r}(A_{c,r})
, where
A_{c,r}
satisfies the equation

rD_{\mu,r}A(\log A)^{r-1}-1/c=0,

and goes to infinity as
c\to 0
so that
\log A_{c,r}\sim|\log c|
and that

\lim_{c\to 0}\frac{G_{c,r}(A_{c,r})}{D_{\mu,r}\,c\,|\log c|^{r}}=1.

Thus, it suffices to prove that

\frac{\displaystyle\inf_{T\geqslant 0}\rho_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T)}{G_{c,r}(A_{c,r})}\geqslant 1+o(1)\quad\text{as}~c\to 0.

(4.16)

If (
4.16
) is wrong, then there is a stopping rule
T_{c}
such that

\frac{\rho_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T_{c})}{G_{c,r}(A_{c,r})}<1+o(1)\quad\text{as}~c\to 0.

(4.17)

Let
\alpha_{c}=\mathsf{PFA}(T_{c})
. Since

\alpha_{c}\leqslant\rho_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T_{c})<G_{c,r}(A_{c,r})(1+o(1))\to 0\quad\text{as}~~c\to 0,

it follows that
\alpha_{c}\to 0
as
c\to 0
. Using inequality (
4.4
), we obtain that as
\alpha_{c}\to 0

{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{r}(T_{c})\geqslant D_{\mu,r}|\log\alpha_{c}|^{r}(1+o(1)),

and hence, as
c\to 0
,

\displaystyle\rho_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T_{c})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{c}+c\,(1-\alpha_{c}){\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{r}(T_{c})

\displaystyle\geqslant\alpha_{c}+c\,D_{\mu,r}|\log\alpha_{c}|^{r}(1+o(1)).

Thus,

\frac{\rho_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T_{c})}{G_{c,r}(A_{c,r})}\geqslant\frac{G_{c,r}(1/\alpha_{c})+c\,D_{\mu,r}|\log\alpha_{c}|^{r}o(1)}{\min_{A>0}G_{c,r}(A)}\geqslant 1+o(1),

which contradicts (
4.17
). Hence, (
4.16
) follows and the proof of (
4.5
) is complete.
∎

5
First-order asymptotic optimality of the detection procedures
T_{A}^{W}
and

in class

We now proceed with establishing asymptotic optimality properties of the double-mixture detection procedures

5.1
First-order asymptotic optimality of the procedure
T_{A}^{W}

The following lemma provides the upper bound for the PFA of the double-mixture procedure
T_{A}^{W}
defined in (
2.11
).

Lemma 5.1
.

For all
A>q/(1-q)
and any prior distribution of the change point, the PFA of the procedure
T_{A}^{W}
satisfies the inequality

\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A}^{W})\leqslant 1/(1+A),

(5.1)

so that if
A=A_{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)/\alpha
, then
\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A_{\alpha}}^{W})\leqslant\alpha
for
0<\alpha<1-q
, i.e.,
T_{A_{\alpha}}^{W}\in{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)
.

Proof.

Using the Bayes rule and the fact that
\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,n)=1
for
k\geqslant n
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}(\nu=k|{\mathcal{F}}_{n})=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}\pi_{k}\prod_{t=1}^{k}g({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})\int_{\Theta}\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}f_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}){\mathrm{d}}W(\theta)}{\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}\pi_{j}\prod_{t=1}^{j}g({\mathbf{X}}_{t}|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1})\int_{\Theta}\prod_{t=j+1}^{n}f_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}({\mathbf{X}}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}^{t-1}){\mathrm{d}}W(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,n)}{q\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(0,n)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\pi_{j}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(j,n)+{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}.

It follows that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n|{\mathcal{F}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\pi_{k}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,n)}{q\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(0,n)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\pi_{j}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(j,n)+{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}{q\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(0,n)+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\pi_{j}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(j,n)+{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{S_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(n)+1}.

(5.2)

By the definition of the stopping time
T_{A}^{W}
,
S_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(T_{A}^{W})\geqslant A
on
\{T_{A}^{W}<\infty\}
and
\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A}^{W})={\mathsf{E}}^{\pi}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}[{\mathsf{P}}(T_{A}^{W}\leqslant\nu|{\mathcal{F}}_{T_{A}^{W}});T_{A}^{W}<\infty]
, so that

\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A}^{W})={\mathsf{E}}^{\pi}_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left[(1+S_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(T_{A}^{W}))^{-1};T_{A}^{W}<\infty\right]\leqslant 1/(1+A),

which completes the proof of inequality (
5.1
).
∎

The following proposition, whose proof is given in the Appendix, provides asymptotic operating characteristics of the double-mixture procedure

for large values of threshold
A
.

5.2
Asymptotic optimality of the double-mixture procedure

Consider now the double-mixture detection procedure

Note that

{\mathsf{E}}_{\infty}[R_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(n)|{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}]=\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\Theta}\mathrm{d}W(\theta)+\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\Theta}R_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(n-1)\,\mathrm{d}W(\theta)=1+R_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(n-1),

{\mathsf{E}}_{\infty}[R_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(n)=\omega+n

{\mathsf{P}}_{\infty}(\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W}\leqslant k)\leqslant(\omega+k)/A,\quad k=1,2,\dots,

which implies the following lemma that establishes an upper bound for the PFA of the procedure
\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
A>0
and any prior distribution of
\nu
with finite mean

, the PFA of the procedure
\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W}

\mathsf{PFA}(\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W})\leqslant\frac{\omega b+\bar{\nu}}{A},

\alpha

depends on
\alpha

\alpha
and may go to infinity as
\alpha\to 0

and

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{{\log(\omega_{\alpha}+\bar{\nu}_{\alpha})}}{|\log\alpha|}=0.

.

Assume that the head-start
\omega=\omega_{\alpha}
of the statistic

6
Asymptotic optimality with respect to the average risk

In this section, instead of the constrained optimization problem (
3.3
) we are interested in the unconstrained Bayes problem (
3.7
)
with the average (integrated) risk function
\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T)
defined in
(
3.6
), where
c>0
is the cost of delay per time unit and
r\geqslant 1
.
Below we show that the double-mixture procedure
T_{A}^{W}
with a certain threshold
A=A_{c,r}
that depends on the cost
c
is asymptotically optimal, minimizing the average
risk
\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T)
to first order over all stopping times as the cost vanishes,
c\to 0
.

Recall that
{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{r}(T)
,
D_{\mu,r}
, and
G_{c,r}(A)
are defined in (
4.1
), (
4.2
), and (
4.15
), respectively.
Since
\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A}^{W})\approx 1/(1+A)
(ignoring an excess over the boundary), using the asymptotic formula (
5.4
) we obtain that for a large
A

{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{r}(T_{A})\approx\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\Theta}\left(\frac{\log A}{I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}+\mu}\right)^{r}\,{\mathrm{d}}W(\theta)=(\log A)^{r}D_{\mu,r}.

So for large
A
the average risk of the procedure
T_{A}
is approximately equal to

\displaystyle\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T_{A}^{W})=\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A}^{W})+c\,[1-\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A}^{W})]{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{r}(T_{A}^{W})\approx G_{c,r}(A).

The procedure
T_{A_{c,r}}
with the threshold value
A=A_{c,r}
that minimizes
G_{c,r}(A)
,
A>0
, which
is a solution of the equation (
6.2
) (see below), is a reasonable candidate for being asymptotically optimal in the Bayesian sense as
c\to 0
, i.e., in the asymptotic problem
(
3.7
).
The next theorem shows that this is true under conditions
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
when the set
\Theta
is compact and that the same is true for the procedure
\widetilde{T}_{A_{c,r}}^{W}

with certain threshold
A_{c,r}
in class of priors
{\mathbf{C}}(\mu)
with
\mu=0
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume that for some
0<I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}<\infty
,
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,
\theta\in\Theta
, right-tail and left-tail conditions

{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
are satisfied and that
\Theta
is a compact set.

(i)
If the prior distribution of the change point
\pi^{c}=\{\pi_{k}^{c}\}
satisfies condition (
3.8
) with

\lim_{c\to 0}\mu_{c}=\mu
and

\lim_{c\to 0}\frac{{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\pi_{k}^{c}|\log\pi_{k}^{c}|^{r}}}{|\log c|^{r}}=0,

(6.1)

and if threshold
A=A_{c,r}
of the procedure
T_{A}^{W}
is the solution of the equation

rD_{\mu,r}A(\log A)^{r-1}=1/c,

(6.2)

then

\inf_{T\geqslant 0}\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T)\sim D_{\mu,r}\,c\,|\log c|^{r}\sim\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T_{A_{c,r}})\quad\text{as}~~c\to 0,

(6.3)

i.e.,
T_{A_{c,r}}^{W}
is first-order asymptotically Bayes as
c\to 0
.

(ii)
If the head-start
\omega_{c}
and the mean of the prior distribution
\bar{\nu}_{c}
approach infinity at such rate that

\lim_{c\to 0}\frac{{\log(\omega_{c}+\bar{\nu}_{c})}}{|\log c|}=0

(6.4)

and if threshold
A=A_{c,r}
of the procedure
\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W}
is the solution of the equation

rD_{0,r}A(\log A)^{r-1}=(\omega_{c}b_{c}+\bar{\nu}_{c})/c,

(6.5)

then

\begin{split}\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(\widetilde{T}_{A_{c,r}})\sim D_{0,r}\,c\,|\log c|^{r}\quad\text{as}~~c\to 0,\end{split}

(6.6)

i.e.,
\widetilde{T}_{A_{c,r}}^{W}
is first-order asymptotically Bayes as
c\to 0
in the class of priors
{\mathbf{C}}(\mu=0)
.

Proof.

The proof is based on the technique used by Tartakovsky for proving Theorems 5 and 6 in
[
12
]
for the single stream problem with an unknown
post-change parameter for the prior with
the fixed
\mu_{c}=\mu
for all
c
in condition (
3.8
) and with positive
\mu_{c}
which vanishes when
c\to 0
. A more general prior considered in this article is
handled analogously.

The proof of part (i). Since
\Theta
is compact it follows from Proposition
5.1
(ii) (cf. the asymptotic approximation (
5.4
)) that under conditions
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
,
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
,
and (
6.1
) as
A\to\infty

\begin{split}{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{r}(T_{A})&\sim\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\Theta}\left(\frac{\log A}{I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}+\mu}\right)^{r}\,{\mathrm{d}}W(\theta)\\
&=D_{\mu,r}(\log A)^{r}.\end{split}

By Lemma
5.1
,
\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A}^{W})\leqslant 1/(A+1)
. Obviously, if threshold
A_{c,r}
satisfies equation (
6.2
), then
\log A_{c,r}\sim|\log c|
and

\mathsf{PFA}(T_{A_{c,r}}^{W})=o(c|\log c|^{r})
as
c\to 0
(for any
r\geqslant 1
and
\mu\geqslant 0
). As a result,

\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(T_{A_{c,r}})\sim D_{\mu,r}\,c\,|\log c|^{r}\quad\text{as}~~c\to 0,

which along with the lower bound (
4.5
) in Theorem
4.1
completes the proof of the assertion (i).

The proof of part (ii). Since
\Theta
is compact it follows from the asymptotic approximation (
5.11
) in Proposition
5.2
that under conditions
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
,
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
,
and (
6.4
) as
A\to\infty

\begin{split}{\overline{\mathcal{R}}}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{r}(\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W})&\sim\sum_{{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}}p_{\mathcal{B}}\int_{\Theta}\left(\frac{\log A}{I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}}\right)^{r}\,{\mathrm{d}}W(\theta)\\
&=D_{0,r}(\log A)^{r}.\end{split}

Define

\widetilde{G}_{c,r}(A)=(\omega_{c}b_{c}+\bar{\nu}_{c})/A+c\,D_{0,r}(\log A)^{r}.

By Lemma
5.2
,
\mathsf{PFA}(\widetilde{T}_{A})\leqslant(\omega_{c}b_{c}+\bar{\nu}_{c})/A
, so that for a sufficiently large
A
,

\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W})\approx\widetilde{G}_{c,r}(A).

Threshold
A_{c,r}
, which satisfies equation (
6.5
), minimizes
\widetilde{G}_{c,r}(A)
. By assumption (
6.4
),
\omega_{c}b_{c}+\bar{\nu}_{c}=o(|\log c|)
as
c\to 0
, so
that
\log A_{c,r}\sim|\log c|
and
\mathsf{PFA}(\widetilde{T}_{A_{c,r}})\leqslant(\omega_{c}b_{c}+\bar{\nu}_{c})/A_{c,r}=o(c|\log c|^{r})
as
c\to 0
. Hence, it follows that

\rho_{\pi,{\mathbf{p}},W}^{c,r}(\widetilde{T}_{A_{c,r}}^{W})\sim\widetilde{G}_{c,r}(A_{c,r})\sim D_{0,r}\,c\,|\log c|^{r}\quad\text{as}~~c\to 0.

This implies the asymptotic approximation (
6.6
). Asymptotic optimality of
\widetilde{T}_{A_{c,r}}^{W}
in the class of priors
{\mathbf{C}}(\mu=0)
follows from (
6.3
) and
(
6.6
).
∎

7
A remark on asymptotic optimality for a putative value of the post-change parameter

If the value of the post-change parameter
\theta=\vartheta
is known or its putative value
\vartheta
is of special interest,
representing a nominal change, then it is reasonable to turn the double-mixture procedures

T_{A}^{W}
and
\widetilde{T}^{W}
in single-mixture procedures
T_{A}^{\vartheta}
and
\widetilde{T}_{A}^{\vartheta}
by taking the degenerate weight function

concentrated at

These procedures are of the form

8
Asymptotic optimality in the case of independent streams

A particular, still very general scenario is where the data streams are mutually independent (but still have a quite general statistical structure) is of special interest for many applications.
In this case, the model is given by (
2.16
) and, as discussed in Subsection
2.2.2
, the implementation of detection procedures may be feasible since the LR process

\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},\theta}(k,n)
can be easily computed (see (
2.18
)). Moreover, in the case of independent data streams all the results obviously hold for different
values of the parameter
\theta=\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
in streams, which we will assume in this section. Specifically, we will write
\theta_{i}
for a post-change parameter in the
i
th stream and
bold
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}=(\theta_{i},i\in{\mathcal{B}})\in\mathbf{\Theta}_{\mathcal{B}}
for the vector of post-change parameters in the subset of streams
{\mathcal{B}}
.

Since the data are independent across streams, for an assumed
value of the change point
\nu=k
, stream
i\in[N]
, and the post-change parameter value in the
i
th stream
\theta_{i}
, the LLR of observations accumulated by time

\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)=\sum_{t=k+1}^{k+n}\log\frac{f_{i,\theta_{i}}(X_{i}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{t-1})}{g_{i}(X_{i}(t)|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{t-1})},\quad n\geqslant 1.

Let

\displaystyle\beta_{M,k}(\varepsilon,i,\theta_{i})

\displaystyle={\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left\{\frac{1}{M}\max_{1\leqslant n\leqslant M}\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)I_{i,\theta_{i}}\right\},

\displaystyle U_{r}(\varepsilon,i,\theta_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{r-1}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\inf_{\vartheta\in\Gamma_{\delta,\theta_{i}}}\lambda_{i,\vartheta}(k,k+n)<I_{i,\theta_{i}}-\varepsilon\right\}.

Assume that the following conditions are satisfied for local statistics in data streams:

{\mathbf{C}}_{1}^{(i)}
.
There exist positive and finite numbers
I_{i,\theta_{i}}
,
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
,
i\in[N]
, such that for any
\varepsilon>0

\lim_{M\to\infty}\beta_{M,k}(\varepsilon,i,\theta_{i})=0\quad\text{for all}~k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i},i\in[N];

(8.1)

{\mathbf{C}}_{2}^{(i)}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta=\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that
W(\Gamma_{\delta,\theta_{i}})>0

and for any
\varepsilon>0
and some
r\geqslant 1

U_{r}(\varepsilon,i,\theta_{i})<\infty\quad\text{for all}~\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i},i\in[N].

(8.2)

Let

. Since the LLR process

imply the following results on asymptotic properties of the double-mixture procedures

and

9
Examples

9.1
Detection of signals with unknown amplitudes in a multichannel system

In this subsection, we consider the
N
-channel quickest detection problem, which is
an interesting real-world example, arising in multichannel radar systems and electro-optic imaging systems where it is required to detect an unknown number of randomly appearing signals from
objects in clutter and noise (cf., e.g.,
[
14
,
13
,
1
]
).

Specifically,
we are interested in the quickest detection of deterministic signals
\theta_{i}S_{i,n}
with unknown amplitudes
\theta_{i}>0
that appear at an unknown time
\nu

in additive noises
\xi_{i,n}
in an
N
-channel system, i.e., observations in the
i
th channel have the form

X_{i,n}=\theta_{i}S_{i,n}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.2mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.6mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.2mul}_{\{n>\nu\}}}+\xi_{i,n},\quad n\geqslant 1,~i=1,\dots,N.

Assume that mutually independent noise processes
\{\xi_{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
are
p
th order Gaussian autoregressive processes AR
(p)
that obey recursions

\xi_{i,n}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{i,j}\xi_{i,n-j}+w_{i,n},\quad n\geqslant 1,

(9.1)

where
\{w_{i,n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
,
i=1,\dots,N
, are mutually independent i.i.d. normal
{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2})
sequences (
\sigma_{i}>0
), so the observations in channels

X_{1,n},\dots,X_{N,n}
are independent of each other. The initial values
\xi_{i,1-p},\xi_{i,2-p},\dots,\xi_{i,0}
are arbitrary random or deterministic numbers,
in particular we may set zero initial conditions
\xi_{i,1-p}=\xi_{i,2-p}=\cdots=\xi_{i,0}=0.

The coefficients
\beta_{i,1},\dots,\beta_{i,p}
and variances
\sigma_{i}^{2}
are known and all roots of the equation
z^{p}-\beta_{i,1}z^{p-1}-\cdots-\beta_{i,p}=0
are in the interior of the unit circle, so that
the AR(
p
) processes are stable.

Define the
p_{n}
-th order residual

\widetilde{Y}_{i,n}=Y_{i,n}-\sum_{j=1}^{p_{n}}\beta_{i,j}Y_{i,n-j},\quad n\geqslant 1,

where
p_{n}=p
if
n>p
and
p_{n}=n
if
n\leqslant p
. It is easy to see that the conditional pre-change and post-change densities in the
i
th channel are

\displaystyle g_{i}(X_{i,n}|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})

\displaystyle=f_{0,i}(X_{i,n}|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{i}^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\widetilde{X}_{i,n}^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right\},

\displaystyle f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{i,n}|{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{i}^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(\widetilde{X}_{i,n}-\theta_{i}\widetilde{S}_{i,n})^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right\},\quad\theta_{i}\in\Theta=(0,\infty),

and that for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
n\geqslant 1
the LLR in the
i
th channel has the form

\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)=\frac{\theta_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{i,j}\widetilde{X}_{i,j}-\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{i,j}^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

Since under measure
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\vartheta_{i}}
the random variables
\{\widetilde{X}_{i,n}\}_{n\geqslant k+1}
are independent Gaussian random variables
{\mathcal{N}}(\vartheta_{i}\widetilde{S}_{i,n},\sigma_{i}^{2})
,
under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\vartheta_{i}}
the LLR
\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)
is a Gaussian process (with independent non-identically distributed increments) with mean and variance

{\mathsf{E}}_{k,i,\vartheta}[\lambda_{i,\theta_{i},\vartheta_{i}}(k,k+n)]=\frac{2\theta_{i}\vartheta_{i}-\theta_{i}^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{i,j}^{2},\quad\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits_{k,i,\vartheta_{i}}[\lambda_{i,\theta_{i},\vartheta_{i}}(k,k+n)]=\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{i,j}^{2}.

(9.2)

Assume that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\widetilde{S}_{i,j}^{2}=Q_{i},

where
0<Q_{i}<\infty
. This is typically the case in most signal processing applications, e.g., in radar applications where the signals
\theta_{i}S_{i,n}
are the sequences of harmonic pulses.
Then for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\theta_{i}\in(0,\infty)

\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}-\text{a.s.}}\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}=I_{i,\theta_{i}},

so that condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}^{(i)}
holds. Furthermore, since all moments of the LLR are finite it can be shown (cf.
[
12
]
) that condition
\widetilde{{\mathbf{C}}}_{2}^{(i)}
(and hence, condition

{\mathbf{C}}_{2}^{(i)}
) holds for all
r\geqslant 1
.

Thus, by Corollary
8.1
, the double-mixture procedure
T_{A}^{W}
minimizes as
\alpha\to 0
all positive moments of the detection delay and asymptotic formulas
(
5.5
) and (
5.6
) hold with
I_{{\mathcal{B}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathcal{B}}}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{B}}}\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}Q_{i}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}
.

9.2
Detection of non-additive changes in mixtures

Assume that the observations across streams are independent. Let
p_{1,i}(X_{i,n})
,
p_{2,i}(X_{i,n})
, and
f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{i,n})
be distinct densities,
i=1,\dots,N
. Consider an example with non-additive changes where the observations in the
i
th stream
in the normal mode follow the pre-change joint density

g_{i}({\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n})=\beta_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{n}p_{1,i}(X_{i,j})+(1-\beta_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n}p_{2,i}(X_{i,j}),

which is the mixture density with a mixing probability
0<\beta_{i}<1
, and in the abnormal mode the observations follow the post-change joint density

f_{\theta_{i}}({\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{i,j}),\quad\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}.

Therefore, the observations
\{X_{i,n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
in the
i
th stream are dependent with the conditional probability density

g_{i}(X_{i,n}\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})=\frac{\beta_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{n}p_{1,i}(X_{i,j})+(1-\beta_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n}p_{2,i}(X_{i,j})}{\beta_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{1,i}(X_{i,j})+(1-\beta_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}p_{2,i}(X_{i,j})},\quad\nu>n

before the change occurs and i.i.d. with density
f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{i,n})
after the change occurs (
n\geqslant\nu
). Note that in contrast to the previous example, pre-change densities
g_{i}
do not belong to the
same parametric family as post-change densities
f_{\theta_{i}}
.

Define
{\mathcal{L}}_{i,n}^{(s)}(\theta_{i})=\log[f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{i,n})/p_{s,i}(X_{i,n})]
;
I_{\theta_{i}}^{(s)}={\mathsf{E}}_{0,\theta_{i}}[{\mathcal{L}}_{i,1}^{(s)}(\theta_{i})]
,
s=1,2
;
\Delta G_{i,n}=p_{1,i}(X_{i,n})/p_{2,i}(X_{i,n})
;

G_{i,n}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Delta G_{i,j}
; and
v_{i}=\beta_{i}/(1-\beta_{i})
. It is easily seen that

\frac{f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{i,n})}{g_{i}(X_{i,n}\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{n-1})}=\exp\left\{{\mathcal{L}}_{i,n}^{(2)}(\theta_{i})\right\}\frac{1-\beta_{i}+\beta_{i}G_{i,n-1}}{1-\beta_{i}+\beta_{i}G_{i,n}}.

Observing that

\prod_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\frac{1-\beta_{i}+\beta_{i}G_{i,j-1}}{1-\beta_{i}+\beta_{i}G_{i,j}}=\frac{1+v_{i}G_{i,k}}{1+v_{i}G_{i,k+n}},

we obtain

\prod_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\frac{f_{\theta_{i}}(X_{i,j})}{g_{i}(X_{i,j}\mid{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{j-1})}=\exp\left\{\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\theta_{i})\right\}\frac{1+v_{i}G_{i,k}}{1+v_{i}G_{i,k+n}},

and therefore,

\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)=\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\theta_{i})+\log\frac{1+v_{i}G_{i,k}}{1+v_{i}G_{i,k+n}}.

(9.3)

Assume that
I_{\theta_{i}}^{(1)}>I_{\theta_{i}}^{(2)}
. Then
{\mathsf{E}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}[\log\Delta G_{in}]=I_{\theta_{i}}^{(2)}-I_{\theta_{i}}^{(1)}<0
for
k\leqslant n
, and hence, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

G_{i,k+n}=G_{i,k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{k+n}\Delta G_{i,j}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\mbox{-a.s.}}0

and

\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1+v_{i}G_{i,k}}{1+v_{i}G_{i,k+n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\mbox{-a.s.}}0.

Since under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}
the random variables
{\mathcal{L}}_{i,n}^{(2)}(\theta_{i})
,
n=k+1,k+2,\dots
are i.i.d. with mean
I_{\theta_{i}}^{(2)}
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{i,\theta_{i}}(k,k+n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}-\text{a.s.}}I_{\theta_{i}}^{(2)},

and hence, condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}^{(i)}
holds with
I_{i,\theta_{i}}=I_{\theta_{i}}^{(2)}
.

Now, under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}
the LLR
\lambda_{i,\vartheta_{i}}(k,k+n)
can be written as

\lambda_{i,\vartheta_{i}}(k,k+n)=\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i},\theta_{i})+\psi_{i}(k,n),

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i},\theta_{i})
is the statistic
{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i})
under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}
and

\psi_{i}(k,n)=\log\frac{1+v_{i}G_{i,k}}{1+v_{i}G_{i,k+n}}\leqslant\log(1+v_{i}G_{i,k})=\psi_{i,k}^{\star}

for any
n\geqslant 1
. Since
\psi_{i,k}^{\star}\geqslant 0
and
\{{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i})\}_{j>k}
are i.i.d. under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\inf_{\vartheta_{i}\in\Gamma_{\delta,\theta_{i}}}\lambda_{\vartheta_{i}}(k,k+n)<I_{i,\theta_{i}}-\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\inf_{\vartheta_{i}\in\Gamma_{\delta,\theta_{i}}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i})<I_{i,\theta_{i}}-\varepsilon-\frac{1}{n}\psi_{i,k}^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathsf{P}}_{k,i,\theta_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\inf_{\vartheta_{i}\in\Gamma_{\delta,\theta_{i}}}\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i})<I_{i,\theta_{i}}-\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{P}}_{0,i,\theta_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\inf_{\vartheta_{i}\in\Gamma_{\delta,\theta_{i}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i})<I_{i,\theta_{i}}-\varepsilon\right)

and, consequently, conditions
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}^{(i)}
are satisfied as long as

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,n^{r-1}\,\sup_{\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i,c}}{\mathsf{P}}_{0,i,\theta_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\inf_{\vartheta_{i}\in\Gamma_{\delta,\theta_{i}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i})<I_{i,\theta_{i}}-\varepsilon\right)<\infty.

(9.4)

Typically condition (
9.4
) holds if the
(r+1)
th absolute moment of
{\mathcal{L}}_{i,1}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i})
is finite,

{\mathsf{E}}_{0,i,\theta_{i}}|{\mathcal{L}}_{i,1}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i})|^{r+1}<\infty
.

For example, let us consider the following Gaussian model:

\displaystyle f_{i,\theta_{i}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{i}^{2}}}\exp\left\{\frac{(x-\theta_{i})^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right\},\quad p_{s,i}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{i}^{2}}}\exp\left\{\frac{(x-\mu_{i,s})^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right\},\quad s=1,2,

where
\theta_{i}>0
,
\mu_{i,1}>\mu_{i,2}=0
. Then

{\mathcal{L}}_{i,n}^{(s)}(\theta_{i})=\frac{\theta_{i}-\mu_{i,s}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}X_{i,n}-\frac{(\theta_{i}-\mu_{i,s})^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}},

I_{i,\theta_{i}}^{(s)}=(\theta_{i}-\mu_{i,s})^{2}/2\sigma_{i}^{2}
,
I_{i,\theta_{i}}^{(2)}>I_{i,\theta_{i}}^{(1)}
and

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{L}}_{i,j}^{(2)}(\vartheta_{i},\theta_{i})=\frac{\vartheta_{i}\theta_{i}-\vartheta_{i}^{2}/2}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\vartheta_{i}}{\sigma_{i}n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta_{i,j},

where
\eta_{i,j}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
,

Discussion and remarks

1. Note that condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
holds whenever
\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,k+n)/n
converges almost surely to
I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
,

\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,k+n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}-\text{a.s.}}I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}

(10.1)

(cf. Lemma A.1 in
[
4
]
). However, the a.s. convergence is not sufficient for asymptotic optimality of the detection procedures with respect
to moments of the detection delay.
In fact, the average detection delay may even be infinite under the a.s. convergence (
10.1
). The left-tail condition
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
guarantees finiteness of first
r
moments of the detection delay
and asymptotic optimality of the detection procedures in Theorem
5.1
, Theorem
5.2
, and Theorem
6.1
.
Note also that the uniform
r
-complete convergence conditions for
n^{-1}\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,k+n)
and
n^{-1}\log\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,k+n)
to
I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
under
{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}
,
i.e., when for all
\varepsilon>0
,
{\mathcal{B}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
, and
\theta\in\Theta

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,n^{r-1}\,\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}(k,k+n)-I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\right|>\varepsilon\right)<\infty,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,n^{r-1}\,\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k,{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\log\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,k+n)-I_{{\mathcal{B}},\theta}\right|>\varepsilon\right)<\infty,

are sufficient for asymptotic optimality results presented in Theorems
5.1
–
6.1
. However, on the one hand these conditions are stronger than conditions

{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
, and on the other hand, verification of the
r
-complete convergence conditions is more difficult than checking conditions
{\mathbf{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{C}}_{2}
for the local values of
the LLR in the vicinity of the true parameter value, which is especially true for the weighted LLR
\log\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,k+n)
. Still the
r
-complete convergence conditions are intuitively
appealing since they define the rate of convergence in the strong law of large numbers (
10.1
).

2. Even for independent streams the computational complexity and memory requirements of the procedures
T_{A}^{W}
and
\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W}
can be quite high. For this reason, in practice,
it is reasonable to use window-limited versions of double-mixture detection procedures where the summation over potential change points
k
is restricted to the sliding window of size

m=m_{1}-m_{0}
. The idea of using a window-limited generalized likelihood ratio procedure for stochastic dynamic systems described by linear state-space models belongs to
Willsky and Jones
[
18
]
, and a general (mostly minimax) single-stream quickest changepoint detection theory for window-limited CUSUM-type procedures based on the maximization over

k
restricted to
n-m_{1}\leqslant k\leqslant n
was developed by Lai
[
5
,
6
]
who suggested a method of selection of
m_{1}
(depending on the given false alarm rate)
to make the detection procedures asymptotically optimal. The role of
m_{1}
is to reduce the memory requirements and computational complexity of stopping rules.
The values of
m_{0}
bigger than
0
can be used to protect against outliers, but
m_{0}=0
looks reasonable in most cases. To be more specific, in the window-limited versions of

T_{A}^{W}
and
\widetilde{T}_{A}^{W}
, defined in (
2.11
) and (
2.15
), the statistics
S_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(n)
and
R_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(n)
are replaced by the window-limited statistics

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(n)

\displaystyle=S_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(n)\quad\text{for}~n\leqslant m_{1};

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}^{\pi}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\mathsf{P}}(\nu\geqslant n)}\sum_{k=n-(m_{1}+1)}^{n-1}\pi_{k}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,n)\quad\text{for}~n>m_{1}

and

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(n)

\displaystyle=R_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(n)\quad\text{for}~n\leqslant m_{1};

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=n-(m_{1}+1)}^{n-1}\Lambda_{{\mathbf{p}},W}(k,n)\quad\text{for}~n>m_{1}.

Following guidelines of Lai
[
6
]
, it can be shown that these window-limited versions also have first-order asymptotic optimality properties as long as the size of the
window
m_{1}(A)
approaches infinity as
A\to\infty
with
m_{1}(A)/\log A\to\infty
but
\log m_{1}(A)/\log A\to 0
. Since thresholds
A=A_{\alpha}
in detection procedures should be selected
in such a way that
\log A_{\alpha}\sim|\log\alpha|
as
\alpha\to 0
, it follows that the value of the window size
m_{1}(\alpha)
should satisfy

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{m_{1}(\alpha)}{|\log\alpha|}=\infty,\quad\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{\log m_{1}(\alpha)}{|\log\alpha|}=0.

3. It is expected that first-order approximations to the moments of the detection delay are inaccurate in most cases, so higher-order approximations are in order. However, it is not feasible to
obtain such approximations in the general non-i.i.d. case considered in Part 1 of the article. The author is currently working on the companion paper “Asymptotically Optimal Quickest Change
Detection in Multistream Data—Part 2: Higher-Order Approximations to Operating Characteristics in the i.i.d. Case,” where we will derive higher-order approximations to the expected delay to detection
and the probability of false alarm in the “i.i.d.” scenario, assuming that the observations in streams are independent and also independent across streams. The results of the renewal theory and
nonlinear renewal theory will be used for this purpose. In the companion paper, we will also study the accuracy of asymptotic approximations and compare several detection schemes using MC simulations.

Appendix: An Auxiliary Lemma and Proofs

The following lemma is extensively used for obtaining upper bounds for the moments of the detection delay, which are needed for proving asymptotic optimality properties of
the introduced detection procedures. In this lemma,
{\mathsf{P}}
is a generic probability measure and
{\mathsf{E}}
is a corresponding expectation.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\tau
(
\tau=0,1,\dots
) be a non-negative integer-valued random variable and let
N
(
N\geqslant 1
) be an integer number. Then, for any
r\geqslant 1
,

{\mathsf{E}}[\tau]^{r}\leqslant N^{r}+r2^{r-1}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}n^{r-1}{\mathsf{P}}\left(\tau>n\right).

(A.1)

Proof.

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}\left[\tau\right]^{r}=\int_{0}^{\infty}rt^{r-1}{\mathsf{P}}\left(\tau>t\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{r}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{N+n}^{N+n+1}rt^{r-1}{\mathsf{P}}\left(\tau>t\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{r}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{N+n}^{N+n+1}rt^{r-1}{\mathsf{P}}\left(\tau>N+n\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=N^{r}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[(N+n+1)^{r}-(N+n)^{r}]{\mathsf{P}}\left(\tau>N+n\right)

\displaystyle=N^{r}+\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}[(n+1)^{r}-n^{r}]{\mathsf{P}}\left(\tau>n\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{r}+\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}r(n+1)^{r-1}{\mathsf{P}}\left(\tau>n\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{r}+r2^{r-1}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}n^{r-1}{\mathsf{P}}\left(\tau>n\right).

∎
