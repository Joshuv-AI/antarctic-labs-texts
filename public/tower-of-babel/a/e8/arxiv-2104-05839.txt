Sectionable Tournaments: Their topology and Coloring
Zakir Deniz

Abstract
. We provide a detailed study of topological and combinatorial properties of sectionable tournaments. 1.
Introduction

Topological methods and techniques have shed new lights on various combinatorial problems. In particular, the notion of topological connectivity of simplicial complexes associated to graphs plays a crucial role on either bounding or even exact determination of graph’s invariants (
[
11
]
). The choice for the associated complex may vary depending on the nature of concerned problems. Unlike the undirected graphs, problems surrounding directed graphs have not benefited much from such a rich interconnection. As in the undirected case, there exists a natural way to associate a simplicial complex to a directed graph. Recall that a subset
S
of a directed graph
G
is said to be
acyclic
(
transitive
) if the induced directed subgraph contains no dicycle, and the family of all acyclic sets in
G
forms a simplicial complex
\operatorname{Acy}(G)
, the
acyclic complex
of
G
.

The main purpose of our current work is to demonstrate that the family of acyclic complexes of directed graphs is a good choice for the investigation of a related coloring problem as well as decycling directed graphs, at least on the class of tournaments. Among all, the topology of acyclic complexes of tournaments has already received much attention. In particular,
Burzio and Demaria
[
3
]
have completely characterized those tournaments whose fundamental groups of acyclic complexes are non-trivial. They choose to call them as
simply disconnected tournaments
, and their characterization relies on a natural decomposition of such tournaments. In detail, if
T
is a simply disconnected tournament, then it can be written in the form
T\cong R_{m}(P^{1},\ldots,P^{m})
for some tournaments
P^{1},\ldots,P^{m}
, where
R_{m}
is a highly regular tournament on
m
vertices for some odd integer
m\geq 3
.
In such a decomposition, we call
P^{1},\ldots,P^{m}
as the
blocks
of
T
. Notice that when
P^{i}
is a block, its vertices have the same (out/in)-neighbors outside of
P^{i}
, i.e., if
u\in V(P^{i})
, then
uv\in E(T)
for some
v\in V(T-P^{i})
if and only if
wv\in E(T)
for every
w\in V(P^{i})\setminus\{u\}
. We inductively introduce a subfamily of tournaments by declaring that a tournament
T
is called
m
-
sectionable
if either it is itself a transitive tournament or else it is a simply disconnected tournament whose each block is a
t
-sectionable tournament for some
t\leq m
.

In the case when
m=3
, we have already displayed in
[
5
]
, the strong connection between the topology of acyclic complexes of
3
-sectionable tournaments and their acyclic chromatic numbers. Recall that a vertex coloring of a directed graph
G
is acyclic if each color class induces an acyclic set, and the least integer
k
for which
G
admits an acyclic coloring with
k
colors is the
acyclic chromatic number
of
G
, denoted by
\chi(G)
.
We proved in
[
5
]
that the acyclic complex of any
3
-sectionable tournament
T
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres. In particular, the chromatic number of every
3
-sectionable tournament
T
can be (tightly) bounded from above in terms of the highest dimension of a sphere occurring in such a decomposition.

We will show here that there is still a chance to bound or determine some combinatorial invariants of
m
-sectionable tournaments when
m\geq 3
, even if the methods developed in
[
5
]
are no longer sufficient. For topological calculations, we apply to a more stronger technique, namely discrete Morse theory of Forman
[
8
]
in order to describe fully the cell structure of
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
. As opposed to
3
-sectionable tournaments, the complex
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
is not known to be homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres when
m>3
. Instead, we formulate the highest dimension of a critical cell (called as the
depth
of
T
) occurring with respect to an acyclic matching of
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
by using the inductive nature of sectionable tournaments.

Theorem
4.12
.

Let
T=R_{m}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{m})
be a sectionable tournament with
0pt(P^{i})=d_{i}
,
m=2r+1
. Then,

0pt(T)=\max_{{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}}\{1,d_{i}+d_{i+1}+\ldots+d_{i+r}+r:\textnormal{either $d_{i},\ldots,d_{i+r-1}$ or $d_{i+1},\ldots,d_{i+r}$ are non-zero}\}

where the index of
d_{j}
is taken modulo
m
for
1\leq j\leq 2r+1
.

Unfortunately, the parameter
0pt(T)
is not the right invariant to bound the chromatic number of sectionable tournaments when
m>3
. In general, we verify that the dimension
\dim(T)
of the complex
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
takes its role:

Theorem
5.5
.

If
T=R_{m}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{m})
is an
m
-sectionable tournament with
m=2r+1
, then

\chi(T)\leq 2\left(2-\frac{1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(\dim(T)+1)}-1,

where the logarithm has two as its base.

We further show that the gap between
\dim(T)
and
0pt(T)
could be arbitrarily large, whereas they coincide when
m=3
. In fact, we completely characterize sectionable tournaments for which the equality
\dim(T)=0pt(T)
holds.

2.
Preliminaries

We first recall some general notions and notations needed throughout the paper, and repeat some of the definitions mentioned in the introduction more formally.

A
tournament

T
is a (finite) directed graph on a set
V=V(T)
such that either
uv
or
vu
is an edge (but not both) of
T

for each pair of distinct vertices
u
and
v
. If
S\subseteq V
, the tournament induced by
S
is written
T[S]
.
For a given subset
S\subseteq V
, the sets
N^{+}(S):=\{v\in V\colon uv\;\textrm{for\;some}\;u\in S\}
and

N^{-}(S):=\{w\in V\colon wu\;\textrm{for\;some}\;u\in S\}
are called the set of

out-neighbors
and
in-neighbors
of
S
in
T
respectively. Furthermore,
d^{+}(x):=|N^{+}(x)|
and
d^{-}(x):=|N^{-}(x)|
are called
out-degree
and
in-degree
of the vertex
x
.

A tournament
T
is said to be
regular
if
d^{+}(v)=d^{-}(v)
for any
v\in V
, and an
n
-vertex regular tournament
T_{n}
is called
highly-regular
(see
[
4
]
)
if
n=2k+1
for some
k\geq 1
and there exists a cyclic ordering
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{2k+1}
of the vertices such that
v_{i}v_{j}\in E(T_{n})

for any
i\in[n]
and
j\equiv i+l
(mod
n
) for some
l\in[k]
. A highly regular tournament of size
m
for
m\geq 3
is denoted by
R_{m}
.

For each
k\geq 3
, we denote the directed cycle (or
dicycle
) by
C_{k}
, where it is the directed graph on an ordered set of vertices

u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k}
such that
u_{i}u_{i+1}\in E(C_{k})
in the cyclic fashion, and a subdigraph
C
of a tournament
T
is called
a dicycle of
T
whenever
C\cong C_{k}
for some
k\geq 3
. We denote by
R_{1}
, the one point tournament.

A subset
S\subseteq V
is said to be an
acyclic
(or
transitive
) set in
T
if the induced subtournament
T[S]
contains no dicycle,
and a tournament
T
is called
transitive
whenever
V
is itself a transitive set in
T
. A map
\mu\colon V\to[k]
is said to be a

k
-
coloring
of a tournament
T
, if the set
\{v\in V\colon\mu(v)=i\}
is transitive in
T
for each
i\in[k]
, and the
chromatic number

of
T
is defined to be the least integer
k
for which
T
admits a
k
-coloring.

For any given
A,B\subseteq V
, we write
A\Rightarrow B
provided that
ab\in E(T)
for any
a\in A
and
b\in B
, and
a tournament
T
is said to be
reducible
if there exist disjoint subsets
A,B\subset V
satisfying
V=A\cup B

such that either
A\Rightarrow B
or
B\Rightarrow A
holds in
T
;
otherwise it is called
irreducible
.

A subset
P\subseteq V
is said to form an
equivalent
set of vertices in
T
, if for any
q\in V\backslash P
, we have either
q\Rightarrow P
or
P\Rightarrow q
. If the vertices of
T
can be partitioned into disjoint subtournaments

P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{m}
such that each
P^{i}
has an equivalent set of vertices,
and
Q_{m}
is a tournament on
w_{1},w_{2},...,w_{m}
in which
w_{i}w_{j}\in E(Q_{m})
if and only if
P^{i}\Rightarrow P^{j}
, then we write
T=Q_{m}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{m})
and call
T
as the
composition
of the components
P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{m}

with the
quotient

Q_{m}
. In particular, a tournament
T_{n}
is called
simple
, if
T_{n}=Q_{m}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{m})
implies that
either
m=1
or
m=n
. Note that every non-trivial tournament has precisely one non-trivial simple quotient tournament
[
6
]
.
Furthermore, if
T=Q_{m}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{m})
, then there exists a digraph homomorphism
\Gamma_{T,Q}\colon T\to Q
defined by

\Gamma_{T,Q}(P^{i})=w_{i}
for any
i\in[m]
.

Finally, we call a tournament
T
is
highly decomposable
if its non-trivial simple quotient is highly regular, that is,

T=R_{m}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{m})
, where
R_{m}
is a highly regular tournament for some
m\geq 3
.

2.1.
Simplicial complexes

An
(abstract) simplicial complex

\Delta
on a finite set
X
is a family
of subsets of
X
satisfying the following properties.

(i)

\{x\}\in\Delta
for all
x\in X
.

(ii)

If
F\in\Delta
and
H\subset F
, then
H\in\Delta
.

The elements of
\Delta
are called
faces
; the
dimension

of a face
F
is
\textrm{dim}(F):=|F|-1
, and the
dimension
of
\Delta

is defined to be
\textrm{dim}(\Delta):=\textrm{max}\{\textrm{dim}(F)\colon F\in\Delta\}
. The
0
and

1
-dimensional faces of
\Delta
are called
vertices
and
edges
while
maximal faces are called
facets
, and the set of facets of
\Delta
is denoted by
\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
.
A subset
C
of
X
is called a
circuit
of
\Delta
if
C
is a minimal non-face of
\Delta
.

The simplicial
join
of two complexes
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
on disjoint sets of vertices, denoted by
\Delta_{1}*\Delta_{2}
, is the simplicial complex defined by

\Delta_{1}\ast\Delta_{2}=\{\sigma\cup\tau\;\;|\>\>\sigma\in\Delta_{1},\tau\in\Delta_{2}\}

The following can be easily obtained by the definition of join operation on simplicial complexes.

Corollary 2.1
.

\dim(\Delta_{1}*\Delta_{2})=\dim(\Delta_{1})+\dim(\Delta_{2})+1
for simplicial complexes
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
.

A simplicial complex is said to be
simply connected
if its fundamental group is trivial, otherwise, it is called
simply disconnected
. We denote by
S^{j}
, the sphere of dimension
j
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex on the vertex set
V
, and consider
x,y\in V
such that
\{x,y\}\in\Delta

and
u_{xy}\notin V
. The
edge contraction

xy\mapsto u_{xy}
can be defined to be a map
f\colon V\to(V\backslash\{x,y\})\cup\{u_{xy}\}
by

f(v):=\begin{cases}v,&\textrm{if}\;v\notin\{x,y\},\\
u_{xy},&\textrm{if}\;v\in\{x,y\}.\end{cases}

We then extend
f
to all simplices
F=\{v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
of
\Delta
by setting

f(F):=\{f(v_{0}),f(v_{1}),\ldots,f(v_{k})\}.

The simplicial complex
\Delta\sslash xy:=\{f(F)\colon F\in\Delta\}
is called the contraction of
\Delta
with respect to the edge
\{x,y\}
.

Observe that the contraction of an edge may not need to preserve the homotopy type of the complex in general. However, under a suitable restriction on the contracted edge, we can guarantee this to happen.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
7
, Theorem 2.4]
,
[
1
, Theorem 1]

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex and let
\{x,y\}\in\Delta
be an edge. Then, the simplicial complexes
\Delta
and
\Delta\sslash xy
are homotopy equivalent
provided that no minimal non-face of
\Delta
contains the edge
\{x,y\}
.

2.2.
Discrete Morse Theory

We close this section by a short incomplete introduction to discrete Morse theory of Forman
[
8
]
, and refer readers to
[
11
]
for more details.

For a simplicial complex
\Delta
, we can associate to it a poset
P(\Delta)
called the face poset of
\Delta
, which is the set of faces of
\Delta
ordered by inclusion. We regard any finite poset
P(\Delta)
as a directed graph, by considering the Hasse diagram of
P(\Delta)
with edges pointing downward (that is, from large faces to smaller ones). A set
M
of pairwise disjoint edges of this digraph, where no face is contained in more than one pair in
M
, is called a
matching
of
P(\Delta)
. A face is
unmatched
(or
critical
) if it is not matched with any element in
P(\Delta)
. A matching is called
perfect
if it covers all elements of
P(\Delta)
, and a matching is said to be a
Morse matching
(or an
acyclic matching
) if the directed
graph obtained from
P(\Delta)
by reversing the direction of the edges in
M

is acyclic. We need the following classical results of discrete Morse theory
[
9
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite simplicial complex, seen as a poset,
and
M
a Morse matching on
\Sigma
,
such that the element
\emptyset
of
\Sigma
is matched. For
i\geq 0
, let
n_{i}
be
the number of unmatched
i
-dimensional elements of
\Sigma
.
Then there exists a CW-complex having
1+n_{0}

0
-dimensional cells and
n_{i}

i
-dimensional cells for
i\geq 1
that is homotopy equivalent to
\Sigma
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, if all
unmatched elements in
\Sigma
have the same dimension
i>0
and there
are
j
of them,

\Sigma
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of
j
spheres of dimension
i
.
In particular, if
\Sigma
is perfectly matched, then it is contractible.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\Delta_{0}
and
\Delta_{1}
be disjoint families of subsets of a finite set such that
\tau\not\subseteq\sigma
if
\sigma\in\Delta_{0}
and
\tau\in\Delta_{1}
. If
M_{i}
is an acyclic matching on
\Delta_{i}
for
i=0,1,
then
M_{0}\cup M_{1}
is an acyclic matching on
\Delta_{0}\cup\Delta_{1}
.

3.
Discrete Morse theory for Acyclic complexes of tournaments

In this section, we adapt the language of discrete Morse theory for its use in describing the CW-complex structure of acyclic complexes of sectionable tournaments. Our adaptation mainly follows the spirit of results presented in
[
2
,
12
]
.

We begin with outlining the general principle that we use to construct the Morse
matchings for acyclic complex
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
of a tournament
T
with a vertex set
V
. Pick a vertex
p\in V(T)
and define

\mathcal{D}(p)=\{I\in\operatorname{Acy}(T):\ \exists\ \{x,y\}\subseteq I\ \text{such that}\ T[p,x,y]\cong{R_{3}}\}

and

\Delta_{p}=\{I\in\operatorname{Acy}(T):\ \text{if}\ H\in\mathcal{D}(p),\text{ then}\ H\not\subseteq I\}.

It can be easily observed that
\Delta_{p}
is equal to
\operatorname{Acy}(T)\setminus\mathcal{D}(p)
.
The set of pairs
(I,I\cup\{p\})
, for
I\in\Delta_{p},
and
p\not\in I
, forms a perfect matching of
\Delta_{p}
, and hence it is a matching of
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
. The vertex
p
is called
pivot
of this matching. Note that
\mathcal{D}(p)
is exactly the set of unmatched elements of
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
. There may be many unmatched elements, but we can now choose
another pivot
p^{\prime}
to match some elements
of
\mathcal{D}(p)=\operatorname{Acy}(T)\setminus\Delta_{p}
, and we repeat this operation as long as we
can. Note that, the obtained matchings are always acyclic on the set
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
by Lemma
2.5
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{k}\}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{r}\}
be two families of sets. We define

of the families

and

as follows:

In particular,

As an example, if we consider the families

\mathcal{A}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\mathcal{B}=\left\{\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\{1,2,3\},\{1,2,4\},\{1,2,3,4\}\right\}

Definition 3.2
.

Let

4.
Topology of Sectionable Tournaments

This section is devoted to a complete description of the cell-structure of acyclic complexes of sectionable tournaments. Our main machinery is the powerful tool of discrete Morse theory. In particular, we provide an inductive calculation of the highest dimension of a critical cell occurring in a CW-complex structure of
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
.

We begin with a proper definition of sectionable tournaments.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
T
be a tournament and
m\geq 3
be an odd integer. We then call
T
an
m
-
sectionable tournament
, if either it is a transitive tournament or else it is a simply disconnected tournament
T=R_{m}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{m})
such that each block
P^{i}
is a
t
-sectionable tournament for some
t\leq m
. When there is no confusion, we sometimes drop the parameter
m
from our notation, and use the term sectionable tournament.

Notice that every set of ordered
r+1
elements create a face in a highly regular tournament
R_{2r+1}
. Thus, we conclude the following by using Corollary
2.1
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
T=R_{2r+1}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{2r+1})
be a tournament such that
\dim(P^{i})=d_{i}
for every
i\in[2r+1]
. Then,

\dim(T)=\max_{{i\in\{1,2,\ldots,2r+1\}}}\{d_{i}+d_{i+1}+\ldots+d_{i+r}+r\}

where the index of
d_{j}
is taken modulo
(2r+1)
for each
1\leq j\leq 2r+1
.

Since
\dim(T)\geq 0
for every sectionable tournament
T
, the following holds.

Corollary 4.3
.

If
\dim(P^{i})=d_{i}
for each
i\in[2r+1]
in
T=R_{2r+1}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{2r+1})
, then

\dim(T)\geq\max_{\underset{i\neq j}{i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,2r+1\}}}\{d_{i}+d_{j}+r\}.

Definition 4.4
.

We call a set of ordered integers
I:=(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{j})
as a
block sequence
in
T=R_{m}(P^{1},\ldots,P^{m})
with respect to a sectionable subtournament

of
T
if there exist sectionable tournaments
S_{0},S_{1},\ldots,S_{j-1}
such that
S_{r}
is the
(i_{r})^{th}
block of
S_{r-1}
for each
1\leq r\leq j
, where
S_{0}
is the
(i_{0})^{th}
block of
T
. If
I=(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{j})
is a block sequence in
T
with respect to
S_{j}
, and

is a sectionable tournament, we denote by

, the tournament obtained from

by replacing

with

.

For an example, consider the tournament
T^{\prime}=R_{5}(R_{1},T,R_{3},R_{5},R_{1})
, where

is the tournament depicted in Figure

. We observe that the tournament

in

. Indeed,

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
I=(i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{j})
be a block sequence in a sectionable tournament
T=R_{m}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{m})
with respect to a sectionable subtournament
S_{j}
of
T
. If

is isomorphic to a highly regular tournament

for some

, then
\operatorname{Acy}(T)\simeq\operatorname{Acy}(T(I;R_{3}))
.

Proof.

We first prove the claim when
j=0
. By using the symmetry of highly regular tournament, we may assume without loss of generality that
S_{0}=P^{1}
. Furthermore, it is sufficient to verify that
\operatorname{CS}(\operatorname{Acy}(T))=\operatorname{CS}(\operatorname{Acy}(T(I;R_{3})))

[
10
, Theorem 4.4]
. The main idea of our proof is based on creating an acyclic matching on
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
which allows us to divide
\operatorname{CS}(\operatorname{Acy}(T))
into two disjoint subsets. One of those will contribute only a critical cell of dimension

to
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
. On the other hand, the contribution of the other set to
\operatorname{Acy}(T)
is the same as with a tournament where
S_{0}
is replaced with

in

.

For the latter claim, we follow the proof of Lemma
3.5
. Suppose that
V(P^{1})=V(R_{s})=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}
, where

for some

. We will construct an acyclic matching on

. First, select
v_{1}
as a pivot, and let

be the set of pairs

for

with

. It follows that the set of unmatched elements with respect to

is

, where

and

. By Lemma

, we have

\displaystyle\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\}=\{v_{2}v_{t+2},v_{3}v_{t+2},

\displaystyle v_{3}v_{t+3},v_{4}v_{t+2},v_{4}v_{t+3},v_{4}v_{t+4},\ldots,

\displaystyle v_{t+1}v_{t+2},v_{t+1}v_{t+3},v_{t+1}v_{t+4},\cdots,v_{t+1}v_{2t+1}\}.

Observe that that when

Remark 4.6
.

In the view of Lemma

is itself a transitive tournament, we may think of it as

by repeated use of Theorem

Following the explanation given in Remark
4.6
, we may consider any subblock which is a highly regular tournament as a copy of

.

Definition 4.7

Let

\operatorname{CS}(\operatorname{Acy}(T))=\operatorname{CS}(\Sigma(wz:v))\cup\operatorname{CS}(\operatorname{Acy}(T(I;R_{1}))).

Since we have
\mathcal{D}(v)=\langle wz,\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{k}\rangle

(\gamma,\gamma\cup\{v\})
for the pivot

Note that for each

, since

is a non-face.

•

and

, since

contains some

as a subset such that

and

are non-face.

So, for every

and

. This in turn forces that

can not be a matched pair with respect to any pivot. Therefore, we may write

Since none of

contains any of the vertices in

Definition 4.16

Let

Let

5.
Coloring of Sectionable Tournaments

In this section, we construct an upper bound on the (acyclic) chromatic number of sectionable tournaments in terms of the dimension of their acyclic complexes as we promised. We begin with the following technical lemma.

Proposition 5.1
.

\Big\lceil\frac{k(2r+1)}{r+1}\Big\rceil=2k
for every pair of positive integers
r\geq k
.

Proof.

We will use the facts
\big\lceil\frac{a}{b}\big\rceil=\big\lfloor\frac{a+b-1}{b}\big\rfloor
and
\big\lfloor\frac{a}{b}\big\rfloor=\big\lceil\frac{a-b+1}{b}\big\rceil
for
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\displaystyle\Big\lceil\frac{k(2r+1)}{r+1}\Big\rceil

\displaystyle=\Big\lfloor\frac{k(2r+1)+r}{r+1}\Big\rfloor=\Big\lfloor\frac{k(r+1)+r(k+1)}{r+1}\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle=k+\Big\lfloor\frac{r(k+1)}{r+1}\Big\rfloor=k+\Big\lceil\frac{r(k+1)-r}{r+1}\Big\rceil=k+\Big\lceil\frac{rk}{r+1}\Big\rceil=k+\Big\lceil\frac{rk+k-k}{r+1}\Big\rceil

\displaystyle=k+\Big\lceil\frac{k(r+1)-k}{r+1}\Big\rceil=k+k+\Big\lceil\frac{-k}{r+1}\Big\rceil=2k

where we use
\Big\lceil\frac{-k}{r+1}\Big\rceil=0
, since
1\leq k\leq r
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
T=R_{2r+1}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{2r+1})
be a tournament. If
\chi(P^{i})=k
for each
1\leq i\leq 2r+1
,
then
\chi(T)\leq\Big\lceil\frac{k(2r+1)}{r+1}\Big\rceil
.

Proof.

The idea of our proof is based on creating a palette of colors for each block which contains sufficiently many colors for it to produce a legal coloring for the tournament
T
.

Let
\alpha=\lfloor\frac{k}{r+1}\rfloor
and
\ell=k-\lfloor\frac{k}{r+1}\rfloor(r+1)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that
\ell
is the remainder of
k
divided by
r+1
. We have three cases.

Case 1:

\alpha=0
. Then
k=\ell\leq r
. Consider a cyclic orientation of
T
and
k
-colorings of
P^{1}
and
P^{r+2}
from the sets
\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}\}
,
\{c_{k+1},c_{k+2},\ldots,c_{2k}\}
respectively. Each block
P^{i}
needs
k
colors and each color can be used in at most
r+1
consecutive blocks of
T
. We reserve the color set
\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}\}
for each of the blocks
P^{1},\ldots,P^{r+1}
, while the color set
\{c_{k+1},c_{k+2},\ldots,c_{2k}\}
is reserved for each of the blocks
P^{r+2},\ldots,P^{2r+1}
. Observe that there are exactly
k=\ell
colors for the block
P^{i}
with
1\leq i\leq 2r+1
, and we totally use
2k
colors for the proper coloring of
T
. Then, the claim follows from Proposition
5.1
.

Case 2:

\alpha\geq 1
and
\ell=0
. By considering a cyclic orientation of
T
, we create a set of palettes of colors. Throughout, the indices of the blocks are considered in modulo
(2r+1)
. We will reserve
(2r+1)\alpha
colors for a
k
-coloring of each block
P^{i}
. Note that each block
P^{i}
needs
k
colors, and each color can be used in at most
r+1
consecutive blocks. For each
i\in[2r+1]
, we apply to a cyclic iteration as follows; we reserve colors
c_{(i-1)\alpha+1},c_{(i-1)\alpha+2},\ldots,c_{(i-1)\alpha+\alpha}
for each block
P^{i},\ldots,P^{i+r}
. Observe that each block
P^{i}
receives exactly
\alpha.(r+1)=k
colors, and we totally use
(2r+1)\alpha
colors for the proper coloring of
T
. Then, the claim follows with the fact
(2r+1)\alpha\leq\Big\lceil\frac{k(2r+1)}{r+1}\Big\rceil
.

Case 3:

\alpha\geq 1
and
\ell\geq 1
. We will combine the first two cases in order to prove the claim. We begin with using
(2r+1)\alpha
colors as in Case 2, and then use
2\ell
more colors as in Case 1. That is, we first reserve the color palette
\{c_{(i-1)\alpha+1},c_{(i-1)\alpha+2},\ldots,c_{(i-1)\alpha+\alpha}\}
for each block
P^{i},\ldots,P^{i+r}
and for each
i\in[2r+1]
, and next we reserve the color palette
\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{\ell}\}
for each of the blocks
P^{1},\ldots,P^{r+1}
, while the color palette
\{d_{\ell+1},d_{\ell+2},\ldots,d_{2\ell}\}
is reserved for each of the blocks
P^{r+2},\ldots,P^{2r+1}
.
We totally use
(2r+1)\alpha+2\ell
colors. Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\chi(T)

\displaystyle\leq(2r+1)\alpha+2\ell=(2r+1)\Big\lfloor\frac{k}{r+1}\Big\rfloor+2\left(k-\Big\lfloor\frac{k}{r+1}\Big\rfloor(r+1)\right)

\displaystyle=2k+\Big((2r+1)-(2r+2)\Big).\Big\lfloor\frac{k}{r+1}\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle=2k-\Big\lfloor\frac{k}{r+1}\Big\rfloor=\Big\lfloor\frac{2k(r+1)-k}{r+1}\Big\rfloor\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{(2r+1)k}{r+1}\Big\rfloor.

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
T=R_{2r+1}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{2r+1})
be a tournament and let
\chi(P^{i})=k_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq 2r+1
. Suppose
k_{s}>k_{t}\geq k_{\ell}
for each
\ell\in[2r+1]\setminus\{s,t\}
. Then,
\chi(T)\leq\max\bigg\{\Big\lceil\frac{(2r+1)(k_{s}+k_{t})-1}{2r+2}\Big\rceil,k_{s}\bigg\}.

Proof.

We follow the same idea used in the proof of Lemma
5.2
. We prove the claim by dividing it into two separate cases. Once again the indices of the blocks are considered in modulo
(2r+1)
throughout.

Case 1:

k_{s}\geq 2k_{t}
. Then, we have
\chi(T)=\chi(P^{s})=k_{s}
. Indeed, we reserve all colors
\{1,2,\ldots,k_{t}\}
to each block in the sequence
P^{s},P^{s+1},\ldots,P^{s+r}
. On the other hand, colors in
\{k_{t}+1,k_{t}+2,\ldots,2k_{t}\}
are reserved for the blocks
P^{s},P^{s-1},\ldots,P^{s-r}
. Finally, we reserve
k_{s}-2k_{t}
more colors for the block
P^{s}
. Observe that each
P^{i}
with
i\in[2r+1]\setminus\{s\}
receives exactly
k_{t}
colors and the block
P^{s}
receives exactly
k_{s}
colors.
This creates a legal coloring, since each color is used in at most
r+1
consecutive blocks; hence,
\chi(T)=k_{s}
.

Case 2:

k_{t}<k_{s}<2k_{t}
. We may assume without loss of generality
s=1
so that
k_{s}=k_{1}=\chi(P^{1})
. Our aim is to provide a coloring in which
P^{1}
receives
k_{s}
colors, and each of the other blocks gets
k_{t}
colors. Let
\ell=k_{s}-\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor(r+1)
and
p_{i}=\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor(i-r-1)
. Note that
\ell
is the remainder of
k_{s}
divided by
r+1
, and so
\ell\leq r
.

Consider
r+1
consecutive blocks ending with
P^{s}=P^{1}
in clockwise order, which are
P^{r+2},P^{r+3},

\ldots,P^{2r+1},P^{1}
. We first reserve
k_{s}
colors to
P^{1}
as follows; start with the color
c_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq r+1
and reserve it for each of the block
P^{r+j+1},P^{r+j+2},\ldots,P^{j}
. We continue this process until the palette for
P^{1}
gets
k_{s}
colors, which consumes
\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor
-rounds if
r+1
divides
k_{s}
. Otherwise, we continue to reserve the color
c_{\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor+j}
with
1\leq j\leq\ell
for the palette of each of the block
P^{r+j+1},P^{r+j+2},\ldots,P^{j}
.

Now, the palette for
P^{1}
contains
k_{s}
colors within a color set
\{c_{1},c_{2}\ldots,c_{k_{s}}\}
. We remark that if
\ell=0
, then for each
r+2\leq i\leq 2r+1
, the palette for the block
P^{i}
receives
p_{i}
colors, while for each
2\leq i\leq r+1
, the palette for the block
P^{i}
gets
p_{2r+3-i}
colors. On the other hand, if
\ell\geq 1
, then the palette for the block
P^{2r+1}
receives exactly
\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor r+\ell
colors, and by the symmetry, the block
P^{2}
is colored with
\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor r+\ell-1
colors. In more detail, for
\ell\geq 0
, the number of colors in each palette for the blocks are

•

k_{s}
colors in the palette of
P^{1}
,

•

p_{2r+3-i}+\ell+1-i
colors in the palette of
P^{i}
for each
2\leq i\leq\ell+1
,

•

p_{2r+3-i}
colors in the palette of
P^{i}
for each
\ell+2\leq i\leq r+1
,

•

p_{i}+i-r-1
colors in the palette of
P^{i}
for each
r+2\leq i\leq r+\ell+1
,

•

p_{i}+\ell
colors in the palette of
P^{i}
for each
r+\ell+2\leq i\leq 2r+1
.

Let
\lambda=k_{t}-\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor
. We now reserve colors to the blocks that need extra colors. Observe that the block which needs the largest number of colors is
P^{r+1}
. This is because it has
\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor
colors for the moment. Notice that if
\ell=0
, then
P^{r+2}
has
\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor
colors as well. So far we have already used only
k_{s}
colors, and to extend this to a proper coloring, we need to create
\lambda=k_{t}-\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor
more colors in order to obtain a
k_{t}
coloring of all blocks except for
P^{1}
.

Next, consider
r+1
consecutive blocks starting with
P^{2}
in clockwise order, which are
P^{2},P^{3},\ldots,P^{r+2}
. We reserve
\lambda
colors to
P^{r+1}
as follows; start with the color
d_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq r
and reserve it for each of the block
P^{j+1},P^{j+2},\ldots,P^{j+r+1}
. We continue this process until the palette for
P^{r+1}
gets
\lambda
colors, which consumes
\lfloor\frac{\lambda}{r}\rfloor
-rounds if
r
divides
\lambda
. Otherwise, we continue to reserve the color
d_{\lfloor\frac{\lambda}{r}\rfloor r+j}
for

for the palette of each of the block
P^{j+1},P^{j+2},\ldots,P^{j+r+1}
.

Finally, the palette for
P^{r+1}
gets
\lambda=k_{t}-\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\rfloor
more colors from the set
\{d_{1},d_{2}\ldots,d_{\lambda}\}
. By the cyclic ordering, the palette for

also gets

totally gets

colors. In particular, we totally use

colors. Since

, it follows that

\displaystyle\chi(T)

\displaystyle\leq k_{s}+k_{t}-\Big\lfloor\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\Big\rfloor\leq\Big\lfloor k_{s}+k_{t}-\frac{k_{s}}{r+1}\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{(r+1)k_{s}+(r+1)k_{t}-k_{s}}{r+1}\Big\rfloor\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{2rk_{s}+(2r+2)k_{t}}{2r+2}\Big\rfloor\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{(2r+1)k_{s}+(2r+1)k_{t}-1}{2r+2}\Big\rfloor.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

For
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in\mathbb{R}
with
a=\frac{r+1}{2r+1}
and
p\geq 1
, the following inequality holds

a^{\log(1+p)}+a^{\log(1+\frac{1}{p})}\leq 2a

where the logarithm has two as its base.

Proof.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in\mathbb{R}
with
a=\frac{r+1}{2r+1}
and
p\geq 1
. Clearly
\frac{1}{2}<a\leq\frac{2}{3}
. For a fixed
a
, we define the function
f(p)=a^{\log(1+p)}+a^{\log(1+\frac{1}{p})}
on the interval
[0,\infty)
. The function
f
obtains it maximum value at the point
p=1
. Indeed, the function
f
is ascending on the interval
(0,1)
and descending on the interval
(1,\infty)
. Therefore,
p=1
is the maximum value point of
f
. Hence the claimed equality holds, since
f(p)\leq f(1)=2a
.
∎

We are now ready to prove our second main result.

Theorem 5.5
.

If
T=R_{2r+1}(P^{1},P^{2},\ldots,P^{2r+1})
is a
(2r+1)
-sectionable tournament, then

\chi(T)\leq 2\left(2-\frac{1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(\dim(T)+1)}-1

where the logarithm has two as its base.

Proof.

Suppose that
\dim(T)=d
. We use induction on the order of
T
. If
|V(T)|=3
, then
T
is just a
3
-dicycle so that
\chi(T)=2=2\frac{3}{2}-1
. For the case
|V(T)|>3
, we write
\dim(P^{i})=d_{i}
,
\chi(P^{i})=k_{i}
,
|V(P^{i})|=2r_{i}+1
, and assume that
\chi(P^{i})\leq 2\big(\frac{2r_{i}+1}{r_{i}+1}\big)^{\log(d_{i}+1)}-1

for each
1\leq i\leq m=2r+1
. Observe that the claim trivially holds if
\chi(T)\leq\chi(P^{i})
for some
i\in[2r+1]
.

Assume that
\max\{\chi(P^{i})+\chi(P^{j})\}=k_{s}+k_{t}
for
i,j\in[2r+1]
with
i\neq j
, and let
k_{s}\geq k_{t}
. By Corollary
4.3
, we have
d\geq\underset{i,j\in[2r+1]}{\max}\{d_{i}+d_{j}+1\}
. We set
r:=\max\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{m}\}
. It follows
d_{s}+d_{t}+1\leq d
and we set
\min\{d_{s},d_{t}\}=d_{\ell}
. Note that if
k_{s}=k_{t}
, then

\chi(P^{s})\leq 2\big(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\big)^{\log(d_{s}+1)}-1
, and similarly
\chi(P^{t})\leq 2\big(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\big)^{\log(d_{t}+1)}-1
.

Case
1
.

k_{s}=k_{t}
. We apply to Lemma
5.2
.

\displaystyle\chi(T)

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lceil\frac{(2r+1)k_{s}}{r+1}\Big\rceil=\Big\lfloor\frac{(2r+1)k_{s}+r}{r+1}\Big\rfloor\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\left(2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d_{s}+1)}-1\right)+\frac{r}{r+1}\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\left(2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d_{\ell}+1)}-1\right)+\frac{r}{r+1}\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lfloor 2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{1+\log(d_{\ell}+1)}-\frac{2r+1}{r+1}+\frac{r}{r+1}\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lfloor 2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(2d_{\ell}+2)}-1\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lfloor 2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d+1)}-1\Big\rfloor.

Case
2
.

k_{t}<k_{s}
. We set
w_{s}:=\log(\frac{d+1}{d_{s}+1})
and
w_{t}:=\log(\frac{d+1}{d_{t}+1})
, and apply to Lemma
5.3
.

\displaystyle\chi(T)

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lceil\frac{(2r+1)(k_{s}+k_{t})-1}{2r+2}\Big\rceil=\Big\lfloor\frac{(2r+1)(k_{s}+k_{t})+2r}{2r+2}\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{2r+1}{2r+2}\left(2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d_{s}+1)}-1+2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d_{t}+1)}-1\right)+\frac{r}{r+1}\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{2r+1}{2r+2}\left(2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d_{s}+1)+w_{s}-w_{s}}+2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d_{t}+1)+w_{t}-w_{t}}\right)-\frac{2r+1}{r+1}+\frac{r}{r+1}\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{2r+1}{2r+2}\left(2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d+1)-w_{s}}+2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d+1)-w_{t}}\right)-1\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{2r+1}{2r+2}\left(\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{-w_{s}}+\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{-w_{t}}\right)2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d+1)}-1\Big\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{2r+1}{2r+2}\left(\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(\frac{d+1}{d_{s}+1}\right)}+\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(\frac{d+1}{d_{t}+1}\right)}\right)2\left(\frac{2r+1}{r+1}\right)^{\log(d+1)}-1\Big\rfloor.

The claim follows once if we prove that

\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(\frac{d+1}{d_{s}+1}\right)}+\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(\frac{d+1}{d_{t}+1}\right)}\leq\frac{2r+2}{2r+1}.

If we write
p=\frac{d_{s}+1}{d_{r}+1}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(\frac{d+1}{d_{s}+1}\right)}+\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(\frac{d+1}{d_{t}+1}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(\frac{d_{s}+d_{t}+2}{d_{s}+1}\right)}+\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(\frac{d_{s}+d_{t}+2}{d_{t}+1}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(1+\frac{d_{t}+1}{d_{s}+1}\right)}+\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(1+\frac{d_{s}+1}{d_{t}+1}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)}+\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log(1+p)}\leq\frac{2r+2}{2r+1},

where the last equality is from Proposition
5.4
. Thus, we have

\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(\frac{d+1}{d_{s}+1}\right)}+\left(\frac{r+1}{2r+1}\right)^{\log\left(\frac{d+1}{d_{t}+1}\right)}\leq\frac{2r+2}{2r+1},

which completes the proof.
∎
