Representation theory of very non-standard quantum 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 2 ​ N − 1 ) {{\mathfrak{so}}}(2N-1)
Stefan Kolb, Jake Stephens

Abstract
. We classify the finite dimensional representations of the quantum symmetric pair coideal subalgebra ℬ 𝐜 {\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}} of type D ​ I ​ I DII corresponding to the symmetric pair ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 2 ​ N ) , 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 2 ​ N − 1 ) ) ({\mathfrak{so}}(2N),{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)) . For ℬ 𝐜 {\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}} defined over an arbitrary field k k and q ∈ k q\in k not a root of unity we establish a one-to-one correspondence between finite dimensional, simple ℬ 𝐜 {\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}} -modules and dominant integral weights for 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 2 ​ N − 1 ) {\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1) . We use specialisation to show that the category of finite dimensional ℬ 𝐜 {\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}} -modules is semisimple if char ⁡ ( k ) = 0 \mathrm{char}(k)=0 and q q is transcendental over ℚ \mathbb{Q} . In this case the characters of simple ℬ 𝐜 {\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}} -modules are given by Weyl’s character formula. This means in particular that the quantum symmetric pair of type D ​ I ​ I DII can be used to obtain Gelfand-Tsetlin bases for irreducible representations of the Drinfeld-Jimbo quantum group U q ​ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 ​ ( 2 ​ N ) ) U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N)) .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation and background

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}
denote the general linear Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{gl}}(n)
of complex
n\times n
matrices or the complex orthogonal Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{so}}(n)
. There are natural embeddings of Lie algebras
{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}\hookrightarrow{\mathfrak{g}}_{n+1}
.
In their seminal work
[
GT50a
]
,
[
GT50b
]
I. Gelfand and M. Tsetlin developed explicit formulas for representations of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}
in terms of combinatorial data. These Gelfand-Tsetlin patters can be interpreted as sequences
\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n-1},\dots,\lambda_{2},(\lambda_{1})
,
where
\lambda_{\ell}
is a dominant integral weight for
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\ell}
, such that the simple
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\ell}
-module of highest weight
\lambda_{\ell}
is contained inside the simple
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\ell+1}
-module of highest weight
\lambda_{\ell+1}
under restriction. As restriction from
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\ell+1}
to
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\ell}
is multiplicity free, the sequence
\lambda_{n},\lambda_{n-1},\dots,\lambda_{2},(\lambda_{1})
determines a basis of the simple
{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}
-module of highest weight
\lambda_{n}
up to scalar factors.

With the advent of quantum groups in the 1980s, it was natural to ask if similar bases exist for representations of Drinfeld-Jimbo quantised enveloping algebras
U_{q}({\mathfrak{g}}_{n})
. For
{\mathfrak{g}}_{n}={\mathfrak{gl}}(n)
this was settled early on
[
Jim86
]
,
[
UTS90
]
. However, for orthogonal Lie algebras there is no embedding of
U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(n-1))
into
U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(n))
and therefore Gelfand-Tsetlin bases for
U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(n))
were long deemed non-existent. This observation was the starting point for A. Gavrilik and A. Klimyk’s invention of non-standard
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(n))
in
[
GK91
]
. These algebras support inclusions
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(n-1))\subset U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(n))
and Gavrilik and Klimyk constructed finite dimensional, irreducible representations of
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(n))
in terms of Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns for
{\mathfrak{so}}(n)
.

M. Noumi observed in
[
Nou96
]
that Gavrilik and Klimyk’s
U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(n))
can be realised as a (right) coideal subalgebra of Drinfeld-Jimbo
U_{q}({\mathfrak{sl}}(n))
, and he established the pair of algebras
(U_{q}({\mathfrak{sl}}(n)),U^{\prime}_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(n)))
as one of the first examples of a quantum symmetric pair.
Noumi and collaborators constructed quantum symmetric pairs for all classical symmetric Lie algebras, that is, for all involutive Lie algebra automorphisms
\theta:{\mathfrak{g}}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{g}}
with
{\mathfrak{g}}
of classical type
[
Nou96
]
,
[
NS95
]
,
[
NDS97
]
. Their construction proceeds case by case and relies on explicit solutions of the reflection equation.

Independently, G. Letzter devised a uniform construction of quantum symmetric pairs for all semisimple symmetric Lie algebras
[
Let99
]
. Recall that involutive automorphisms
\theta
of a semisimple, complex Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
are classified up to conjugation in terms of Satake diagrams
(X,\tau)
, see also Section
2.1
. Here
X
is a subset of the set of nodes of the Dynkin diagram of
{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\tau
is a diagram automorphism preserving the subset
X
. For each Satake diagram, Letzter defined a right coideal subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})}
which is a quantum group analogue of
U({\mathfrak{k}})
for
{\mathfrak{k}}=\{x\in{\mathfrak{g}}\,|\,\theta(x)=x\}
. We refer to
({U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})},{\mathcal{B}})
as a quantum symmetric pair (QSP) and to the algebra
{\mathcal{B}}
as a QSP-coideal subalgebra of
{U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})}
. For
{\mathfrak{g}}
of classical type, Letzter’s construction reproduces the coideal subalgebras constructed by Noumi and collaborators, see
[
Let99
, Section 6]
.

({\mathfrak{so}}(n),{\mathfrak{so}}(n-1))
is a symmetric pair. If
n=2N

DII
, see

Quantum symmetric pairs provide a corresponding coideal subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{2N-1}\subset U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N))
which is a quantum group analogue of
U({\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1))
. The algebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{2N-1}
is different from Gavrilik and Klimyk’s non-standard
U_{q}^{\prime}({\mathfrak{so}}_{2N-1})
and hence we also refer to it as ‘very non-standard’ quantum
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
. By Letzter’s construction we obtain a chain of algebras

(1.1)

\displaystyle\dots\subset U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N-2))\subset{\mathcal{B}}_{2N-1}\subset U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N))\subset\dots

In the odd case
n=2N+1
, the Satake diagram corresponding to the symmetric pair
({\mathfrak{so}}(2N+1),{\mathfrak{so}}(2N))
is of type
BII
, see
Figure

1(B)
. Again, quantum symmetric pairs provide a coideal subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{2N}\subset U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N+1))
which is a quantum group analogue of
U({\mathfrak{so}}(2N))
, and we obtain a chain of algebras

(1.2)

\displaystyle\dots\subset U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1))\subset{\mathcal{B}}_{2N}\subset U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N+1))\subset\dots

It is natural to expect that the chains of algebras (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) lead to Gelfand-Tsetlin bases for representations of
U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(n))
. To this end, we need to understand the representation theory of the QSP-coideal subalgebras
{\mathcal{B}}_{2N-1}
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{2N}
. The two situations are quite different as they correspond to different Satake diagrams. In the present paper we consider the quantum symmetric pair
(U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N)),{\mathcal{B}}_{2N-1})
.

1.2.
Representations of QSP-coideal subalgebras

If
({\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{k}})
is a symmetric pair with semisimple
{\mathfrak{g}}
then
{\mathfrak{k}}
is a reductive Lie algebra.
Hence it is natural to expect that any QSP-coideal subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}
has a nice category of finite-dimensional representations. However, to this date, finite dimensional representations of
{\mathcal{B}}
have been classified only for a fairly limited class of examples. To put the results of the present paper into context, we provide a brief overview of known results.

In type
AI
, corresponding to the symmetric pair
({\mathfrak{sl}}(n),{\mathfrak{so}}(n))
, finite dimensional representations of
{\mathcal{B}}
were classified inductively by N. Iorgov and A. Klimyk
[
IK05
]
. There appear two types of irreducible representations, those of classical type which allow specialisation
q\rightarrow 1
and those of nonclassical type which don’t. The classification relies on the explicit realisation of these representations given in
[
GK91
]
. These realisations involve square roots of
q
-numbers and hence
[
IK05
]
work over the field
k={\mathbb{C}}
with
q\in{\mathbb{C}}
not a root of unity. The induction argument in
[
IK05
]
also shows that the category of finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}
-modules is semisimple in this case.

The classification in type
AI
was revisited by H. Wenzl who developed a Verma module approach in this case
[
Wen20
]
. In particular, Wenzl identifies large proper submodules of Verma modules and can show by an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2)
-argument similar to the classical case that the corresponding quotients are finite dimensional if the highest weight is dominant integral
[
Wen20
, Section 5.3]
. This is possible because the rank two case provides
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2)
-triples in type
AI
.

In type
AII
, corresponding to the symmetric pair
({\mathfrak{sl}}(2N)),\mathfrak{sp}(2N))
, irreducible representations of
{\mathcal{B}}
were classified by A. Molev in
[
Mol06
]
. Molev works essentially with Noumi’s construction of
{\mathcal{B}}
and again follows a Verma module approach. To show that simple quotients of Verma modules for dominant integral weights are finite dimensional, Molev uses Gelfand-Tsetlin bases for
U_{q}({\mathfrak{gl}}(2N))
to construct non-vanishing homomorphisms from Verma modules for
{\mathcal{B}}
to finite dimensional
U_{q}({\mathfrak{gl}}(2N))
-modules. Moreover, Molev uses specialisation to show that in type
AII
the characters of simple
{\mathcal{B}}
-modules are given by Weyl’s character formula.

A unifying approach to the classification of finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}
-modules was proposed by H. Watanabe in
[
Wat21
]
. To include all types, he works over the algebraic closure
\overline{{\mathbb{C}}(q)}
. Watanabe defines a class of modules of classical weight which excludes the nonclassical modules of type
AI
and which is not always closed under tensor products with 1-dimensional
{U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})}
-modules. For this class Watanabe develops a highest weight theory. To make this theory work, one needs to establish a triangular decomposition of
{\mathcal{B}}
in terms of analogues of root vectors in
{\mathcal{B}}
, which is quite challenging in general. In
[
Wat21
]
this program is performed for several classes of examples, reproving the results of
[
Mol06
]
, parts of the classification in
[
IK05
]
and
[
Wen20
]
, and leading to a classification of simple modules of classical weight in type AIII corresponding to the symmetric pairs
({\mathfrak{sl}}(2n),\mathfrak{s}({\mathfrak{gl}}(n)\oplus{\mathfrak{gl}}(n)))
and
({\mathfrak{sl}}(2n+1),\mathfrak{s}({\mathfrak{gl}}(n)\oplus{\mathfrak{gl}}(n+1)))
.
To show that simple quotients of Verma modules for dominant integral weights are finite dimensional, Watanabe uses specialisation early on. Specialisation is also employed to show that any finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}
-module is completely reducible in those cases.

1.3.
Results

In the present paper, we classify all finite dimensional simple
{\mathcal{B}}
-modules in type
DII
corresponding to the symmetric pair
({\mathfrak{so}}(2N),{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1))
.
We aim to emulate the approach in Jantzen’s textbook
[
Jan96
, Chapter 5]
. In particular, we work over any field
k
with
q\in k
not a root of unity. We follow the conventions in
[
Kol14
]
and write
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
to indicate the parameter dependence of the QSP-coideal subalgebra of type
DII
.

Let
U^{0}\subset U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N))
be the subalgebra generated by the group-like elements
K_{i}^{\pm 1}
for
i=1,\dots,N
. In type
DII
, the number of black dots of the Satake diagram
|X|=N-1
coincides with the rank of
{\mathfrak{k}}={\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
. Hence, the QSP-coideal subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
has a natural analogue of a Cartan subalgebra, namely
U^{0}_{X}:={\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}\cap U^{0}=k\langle K_{i}^{\pm 1}\,|\,i\in X\rangle
. This only happens for symmetric pairs of type
AII
,
DII
and
EIV
and simplifies the situation significantly.

The algebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
is generated by a single element
B_{1}
and the subalgebra
{\mathcal{M}}_{X}=U_{q}({\mathfrak{g}}_{X})
corresponding to the set
X
of black dots in the Satake diagram. Taking inspiration from the classical case, we define root vectors
for
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
in terms of the generator
B_{1}
and the Lusztig braid automorphism
T_{i}
for
i\in X
. The root vectors are ordered by a choice of longest element in the Weyl group
W
. Moreover, they split up into positive and negative root vectors depending on their behaviour under conjugation by
U^{0}_{X}
. Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{-}
be the span of all ordered monomials in the positive and negative root vectors of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
, respectively. We obtain a triangular decomposition for
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
.

Theorem A.
(Theorem
4.6
, Corollary
4.7
)

The multiplication map

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{-}\otimes U^{0}_{X}\otimes{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{+}\rightarrow{\mathcal{B}}

is a linear isomorphism. Moreover, the ordered monomials in the positive (negative) root vectors form a basis of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{+}
(
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{-}
).

Any finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module
V
can be considered as a
{\mathcal{M}}_{X}
-module by restriction, and hence the action of
U^{0}_{X}
on
V
is diagonalisable. The notion of type of a finite dimensional
{\mathcal{M}}_{X}
-module
[
Jan96
, 5.2]
lifts to a notion of type of a finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module. As in the quantum group case, it suffices to consider finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-modules of type
\mathbf{1}
, as all others can be obtained by tensoring by a one-dimensional
U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N))
-module, see Proposition
5.1
.

Let
P_{2N-1}
denote the weight lattice of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
and let
P_{2N-1}^{+}
be the subset of dominant integral weights. Finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-modules decompose into weight spaces and hence contain a highest weight vector of weight
\lambda\in P_{2N-1}
on which
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{+}
acts trivially. We need to show that
\lambda
is dominant. Let
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N-1}
be the simple roots of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
with
\gamma_{N-1}
being the short root. For
i=1,\dots,N-2
the triple
\{E_{i+1},F_{i+1},K_{i+1}^{\pm 1}\}\subset{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
generates a copy of
U_{q}({\mathfrak{sl}}(2))
corresponding to the root
\gamma_{i}
, and hence
\lambda(h_{\gamma_{i}})\geq 0
where
h_{\gamma_{i}}
is the corresponding coroot. We denote the positive and negative root vectors corresponding to the short root
\gamma_{N-1}
by
B_{\beta_{N-1}}
and
B_{\beta_{N}}
, respectively. We show in Proposition
5.10
that the triple
\{B_{\beta_{N-1}},B_{\beta_{N}},(K_{N}K_{N-1}^{-1})^{\pm 1}\}
acts on a suitable subspace
H(V)\subset V
as a copy of
U_{q}({\mathfrak{sl}}(2))
. Moreover,
H(V)
contains the highest weight vector. This implies that
\lambda(h_{\gamma_{N-1}})\geq 0
and hence
\lambda\in P^{+}_{2N-1}
is dominant.

Using the root vectors for
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
we define a Verma module
M(\lambda)
for each
\lambda\in P_{2N-1}
, see Section
5.5
. The Verma module
M(\lambda)
is generated by a highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}
and we show in Theorem
5.13
that the linear map

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{-}\rightarrow M(\lambda),\qquad b\mapsto bv_{\lambda}

is a linear isomorphism.
Any finite dimensional simple
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module is a quotient of
M(\lambda)
by a proper maximal submodule
N(\lambda)\subset M(\lambda)
for some
\lambda\in P^{+}_{2N-1}
. To complete the classification of finite dimensional, simple
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-modules, it remains to show that

\displaystyle L(\lambda)=M(\lambda)/N(\lambda)

is finite dimensional for all
\lambda\in P_{2N-1}^{+}
. We first consider the submodule

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}F_{i+1}^{n_{i}+1}v_{\lambda}+{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}B_{\beta_{N}}^{n_{N-1}+1}v_{\lambda}

with
n_{i}=\lambda(h_{\gamma_{i}})\geq 0
. As in the quantum group case, we show that

\widetilde{N}(\lambda)\subset M(\lambda)
is a proper submodule. However, it is unclear how to adapt the Weyl group invariance argument from
[
Jan96
, 5.9]
, since
\{B_{\beta_{N-1}},B_{\beta_{N}},(K_{N}K_{N-1}^{-1})^{\pm 1}\}
acts as an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2)
-triple only on the subspace
H(V)
. Instead we consider the submodule

\displaystyle\accentset{\approx}{N}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}F_{i+1}^{n_{i}+1}v_{\lambda}+{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}F_{N}^{n_{N}+1}v_{\lambda}

with
n_{\lambda}=\lambda(h_{\gamma_{N-2}}+h_{\gamma_{N-1}})
which contains
\widetilde{N}(\lambda)
, see Remark
5.19
. By Proposition
5.15
we know that
\accentset{\approx}{N}(\lambda)
is also a proper submodule of
M(\lambda)
. Using a filtered-graded argument, we show in Section
5.8
that
M(\lambda)/\accentset{\approx}{N}(\lambda)
is finite dimensional. This gives us the desired classification.

Theorem B.
(Theorem
5.22
)
Let
k
be any field and
q\in k
not a root of unity.
For each
\lambda\in P_{2N-1}^{+}
the simple
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module
L(\lambda)
has finite dimension. Each finite-dimensional simple
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module (of type
{\bf 1}
) is isomorphic to exactly one
L(\lambda)
with
\lambda\in P_{2N-1}^{+}
.

We would like to know the characters of the simple
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-modules
L(\lambda)
and we would like to show that all finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-modules are semisimple. At this point, we can only achieve this via specialisation, as in
[
Jan96
, 5.15, 5.17]
,
[
Mol06
]
,
[
Wat21
]
. We obtain the following result.

Theorem C.
(Corollary
6.8
, Theorem
6.11
) Assume that
k
is a field of characteristic zero and that
q
is transcendental over
\mathbb{Q}
. Then every finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module is semisimple. Moreover, for any
\lambda\in P^{+}_{2N-1}
the submodules
N(\lambda),\widetilde{N}(\lambda)
and
\accentset{\approx}{N}(\lambda)
coincide, and the dimensions of the weight spaces
L(\lambda)_{\mu}
for
\mu\in P_{2N-1}
are given by Weyl’s character formula.

Theorem C implies in particular that finite dimensional
U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N))
-modules decompose into irreducible
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-modules as in the classical case
q=1
. And similarly, finite-dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-modules decompose into irreducible
U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N-2))
-modules as in the classical case
q=1
. Hence, if
\mathrm{char}(k)=0
and
q
is transcendental over
\mathbb{Q},
then the chain of inclusions (
1.1
) provides a Gelfand-Tsetlin basis of any simple
U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N))
-module, up to scalar factors.

1.4.
Very recent developments

The theory of quantum symmetric pairs is presently developing at rapid speed. While we were putting final touches to the present paper, two preprints appeared which have bearing on our results. In
[
Wat24
]
H. Watanabe proposed a notion of integrable modules for QSP-coideal subalgebras. He shows that simple, integrable modules over QSP-coideal subalgebras of finite type are finite dimensional
[
Wat24
, Section 5.1]
. In the spirit of our paper to emulate the constructions of
[
Jan96
, Chapter 5]
as closely as possible, it would be desirable to show that the quotient
M(\lambda)/\accentset{\approx}{N}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in P^{+}_{2N-1}
is integrable in the sense of Watanabe’s paper.

M. Lu, R. Yang and W. Zhang proposed notions of root vectors and PBW bases for QSP-coideal subalgebras of finite type
[
LYZ24
]
. In type
DII
the root vectors given in
[
LYZ24
, 3.7]
coincide with the root vectors given in Section
4.2
. Moreover, the second part of Theorem A, Theorem
4.6
, is a special case of
[
LYZ24
, Theorem 4.4]
. We note that the definition of the root vectors in type
DII
and the PBW-Theorem
4.6
already appeared in the second named author’s PhD thesis
[
Ste23
]
.

2.
The symmetric pair
({\mathfrak{so}}(2N),{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1))

In this preliminary section we identify the orthogonal Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
explicitly as the Lie subalgebra pointwise fixed under a maximally split involution on
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
. This allows us to identify simple root vectors of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
inside
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
, which will in turn inform the choice of root vectors in the quantum setting in Section
4
.

2.1.
Symmetric pairs and Satake diagrams

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a semisimple complex Lie algebra with Cartan subalgebra
{\mathfrak{h}}
, root system
\Phi
, a fixed choice of simple roots
\Pi=\{\alpha_{i}\,|\,i\in I\}
, and corresponding triangular decomposition
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{n}}^{+}\oplus{\mathfrak{h}}\oplus{\mathfrak{n}}^{-}
. Let
e_{i},f_{i},h_{i}
for
i\in I
denote the Chevalley generators of
{\mathfrak{g}}
. For any subset
X\subset I
let
{\mathfrak{g}}_{X}
denote the Lie subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}
generated by
e_{i},f_{i},h_{i}
for all
i\in X
. Let
Q_{X}=\sum_{i\in X}{\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{i}
be the corresponding root lattice and
Q_{X}^{+}=\sum_{i\in X}{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\alpha_{i}
the positive cone. For
X=I
we write
Q
and
Q^{+}
instead of
Q_{I}
and
Q_{I}^{+}
, respectively.

Let
\theta:{\mathfrak{g}}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{g}}
be an involutive Lie algebra automorphism, i.e.
\theta^{2}={\mbox{id}}_{\mathfrak{g}}
, and
{\mathfrak{k}}=\{x\in{\mathfrak{g}}\,|\,\theta(x)=x\}
the pointwise fixed Lie subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}
. We refer to
({\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{k}})
as a symmetric pair. Up to conjugation by a Lie algebra automorphism of
{\mathfrak{g}}
, the involution
\theta
has the following properties, see
[
Dix96
, 1.13]
, also
[
Let02
, Section 7]
:

(1)

\theta({\mathfrak{h}})={\mathfrak{h}}
,

(2)

There exists
X\subset I
such that
\theta|_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{X}}={\mbox{id}}_{{\mathfrak{g}}_{X}}
,

(3)

\theta(e_{i})\in{\mathfrak{n}}^{-}
and
\theta(f_{i})\in{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}
if
i\in I\setminus X
, where
X
is the set given in (2).

Following
[
Let02
]
, we call the involution
\theta
maximally split if properties (1), (2) and (3) hold.
From now on we assume that
\theta
is maximally split. By condition (1), the map
\theta
induces an involution
\Theta:{\mathfrak{h}}^{\ast}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{h}}^{\ast}
which leaves the root system
\Phi
invariant. By construction we have
\theta({\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha})={\mathfrak{g}}_{\Theta(\alpha)}
for all roots
\alpha\in\Phi
. Moreover, if
\theta
is maximally split, then there exists an involutive diagram automorphism
\tau:I\rightarrow I
such that
\Theta(-\alpha_{i})-\alpha_{\tau(i)}\in Q^{+}_{X}
for all
i\in I\setminus X
, see also
[
Let02
, (7.5)]
. The pair
(X,\tau)
is called a Satake diagram and determines the involution
\theta
uniquely up to conjugation, see also
[
Kol14
, Theorem 2.7]
. The Satake diagram
(X,\tau)
is recorded in the Dynkin diagram of
{\mathfrak{g}}
by colouring all nodes in
X
black and indicating the diagram automorphism by a double-pointed arrow. See
[
Ara62
, p. 32/33]
for a list of all Satake diagrams for simple Lie algebras
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

2.2.
Root system and Chevalley generators for
{\mathfrak{so}}(n)

For any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
let
{\mathfrak{gl}}(n)
denote the Lie algebra of complex
n\times n
-matrices. For any
m\times n
matrix
M
we write
M^{t}
to denote its transpose matrix. The complex orthogonal Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{so}}(n)
can be defined as

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{gl}}_{S}(n)=\{M\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(n)\,|\,M=-SM^{t}S^{-1}\}

for any non-degenerate symmetric
n\times n
-matrix
S
. The Lie algebras

{\mathfrak{gl}}_{S}(n)
for different choices of
S
are all isomorphic by the classification of complex symmetric bilinear forms.
For the purpose of this paper, it is convenient to choose

(2.2)

\displaystyle S=S_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}&&1\\
&\iddots&\\
1&&\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{i,n-i+1}

where
E_{i,j}
denotes the elementary
n\times n
matrix with entry
1
in the
ij
-th position and zero entries elsewhere. For this choice of
S
, the set
{\mathfrak{h}}_{n}
of diagonal matrices in
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{S}(n)
is a Cartan subalgebra. Moreover, the definition of
S=S_{n}
is uniform for even and odd
n
. Throughout this paper, the symbol
{\mathfrak{so}}(n)
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
will always denote the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{S}(n)
for
S
given by (
2.2
).

For any
m\times n
matrix
A
we write
A^{\tau}=S_{n}A^{t}S_{m}
to denote the matrix obtained by reflection in the antidiagonal. With this notation we have

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{so}}(n)={\mathfrak{gl}}_{S}(n)=\{M\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(n)\,|\,M=-M^{\tau}\}.

Remark 2.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{K}}
be any field of characteristic
0
. Then (
2.1
) with
S
given by (
2.2
) defines a Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{n}(S)_{\mathbb{K}}
over
{\mathbb{K}}
. The set
{\mathfrak{h}}_{n}
of diagonal matrices in
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{S}(n)_{\mathbb{K}}
is a splitting Cartan subalgebra. Up to a change of basis this example is discussed in
[
Jac62
, IV, Theorems 7 and 9]
. Moreover, finite dimensional representations of
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{S}(n)_{\mathbb{K}}
are completely reducible, irreducible representations are given by dominant integral weights, and the characters of irreducible
{\mathfrak{gl}}_{n}(S)
-modules are given by Weyl’s character formula, see
[
Jac62
]
. We will need these statements in Section
6.3
when we prove analogous results for very non-standard quantum
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
over a field of characteristic
0
and
q
transcendental over
\mathbb{Q}
, see also
[
Jan96
, Remark 5.14]
.

For later reference, we describe root systems and Chevalley generators of
{\mathfrak{so}}(n)
for even and for odd
n
separately. Moreover, it is convenient to give block decompositions of
{\mathfrak{so}}(n)
for even and odd
n
which will allow us to realise explicit embeddings of
{\mathfrak{so}}(n-1)
into
{\mathfrak{so}}(n)
.

Case
n=2N
even:
In this case, we can write

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)={\mathfrak{gl}}_{S}(2N)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&-A^{\tau}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg|\,B=-B^{\tau},C=-C^{\tau}\right\}.

where
A,B,C\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(N)
. The elements
h_{i}=E_{i,i}-E_{n-i+1,n-i+1}
for

form a basis of the Cartan subalgebra
{\mathfrak{h}}_{2N}
. Let
\{\varepsilon_{i}\,|\,i=1,\dots N\}
be the dual basis of
{\mathfrak{h}}_{2N}^{*}
. The root system of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
is given by
\{\pm(\varepsilon_{i}\pm\varepsilon_{j})\,|\,1\leq i<j\leq N\}
. We choose the simple roots

(2.4)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i+1}&\mbox{for $1\leq i\leq N-1$,}\\
\varepsilon_{N-1}+\varepsilon_{N}&\mbox{for $i=N$.}\end{cases}

Corresponding Chevalley generators of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
are given by

(2.5)

\displaystyle e_{\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=E_{i,i+1}{-}E_{n-i,n-i+1},

\displaystyle f_{\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=E_{i+1,i}{-}E_{n-i+1,n-i},

\displaystyle h_{\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=h_{i}{-}h_{i+1}

for
1\leq i\leq N-1
, and

(2.6)

\displaystyle e_{\alpha_{N}}

\displaystyle{=}E_{N-1,N+1}{-}E_{N,N+2},

\displaystyle f_{\alpha_{N}}

\displaystyle{=}E_{N+1,N-1}{-}E_{N+2,N},

\displaystyle h_{\alpha_{N}}

\displaystyle{=}h_{N-1}{+}h_{N}.

Case
n=2N+1
odd:
In this case, we can write

(2.7)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{so}}(2N+1)={\mathfrak{gl}}_{S}(2N+1)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&b&B\\
d&0&-b^{\tau}\\
C&-d^{\tau}&-A^{\tau}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg|\,B=-B^{\tau},C=-C^{\tau}\right\}

where
b\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
is a column vector,
d\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
is a row vector and
A,B,C
are
N\times N
-matrices as before. As in the even case, the elements
h_{i}=E_{i,i}-E_{n-i+1,n-i+1}
for
i=1,\dots,N
form a basis of
{\mathfrak{h}}_{2N+1}
, and we still denote the dual basis by
\{\varepsilon_{i}\,|\,i=1,\dots,N\}
. The root system of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N+1)
is given by
\{\pm\varepsilon_{\ell},\pm(\varepsilon_{i}\pm\varepsilon_{j})\,|\,\ell=1,\dots,N,\,1\leq i<j\leq N\}
. We choose the simple roots

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}=\begin{cases}\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i+1}&\mbox{for $1\leq i\leq N-1$,}\\
\varepsilon_{N}&\mbox{for $i=N$.}\end{cases}

Corresponding Chevalley generators
e_{\gamma_{i}},f_{\gamma_{i}},h_{\gamma_{i}}
of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N+1)
are given by (
2.5
) for
1\leq i\leq N-1
, and

\displaystyle e_{\gamma_{N}}

\displaystyle=E_{N,N+1}{-}E_{N+1,N+2},

\displaystyle f_{\gamma_{N}}

\displaystyle=2(E_{N+1,N}{-}E_{N+2,N+1}),

\displaystyle h_{\gamma_{N}}

\displaystyle=2h_{N}.

In the case
n=2N+1
, it helps to define

(2.8)

\displaystyle b_{i}

\displaystyle=E_{i,N+1}{-}E_{N+1,2N-i+2},

\displaystyle d_{i}

\displaystyle=E_{N+1,i}{-}E_{2N-i+2,N+1}

for
i=1,\dots,N
.

With this notation, we have
e_{\gamma_{N}}=b_{N}
and
f_{\gamma_{N}}=2d_{N}
. Moreover, note that

(2.9)

\displaystyle[f_{\gamma_{i}},b_{i}]

\displaystyle=b_{i+1},

\displaystyle[e_{\gamma_{i}},b_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=b_{i},

\displaystyle[f_{\gamma_{i}},d_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=-d_{i},

\displaystyle[e_{\gamma_{i}},d_{i}]

\displaystyle=-d_{i+1}

for
1\leq i\leq N-1
. It is convenient to consider the Chevalley generators (
2.6
) of the copy of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
inside
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N+1)
.
We have

(2.10)

\displaystyle[f_{\alpha_{N}},b_{N-1}]

\displaystyle=-d_{N},

\displaystyle[f_{\alpha_{N}},b_{N}]

\displaystyle=d_{N-1},

(2.11)

\displaystyle[e_{\alpha_{N}},d_{N-1}]

\displaystyle=b_{N},

\displaystyle[e_{\alpha_{N}},d_{N}]

\displaystyle=-b_{N-1}.

The above formulas (
2.10
), (
2.11
) and (
2.9
) reflect the fact that the matrices
\{b_{i},d_{i}\,|\,i=1,\dots,N\}
form a basis of the vector representation of the Lie subalgebra
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)\subset{\mathfrak{so}}(2N+1)
under the adjoint action. This is also evident from the block matrix representation (
2.7
) of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N+1)
.

2.3.
Realising
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)\subset{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
as a symmetric pair

In the realisations (
2.3
), (
2.7
), there is an immediate embedding of Lie algebras
\varphi:{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)\hookrightarrow{\mathfrak{so}}(2N+1)
given by

\displaystyle\varphi\left(\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&-A^{\tau}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}A&{\mathbf{0}}&B\\
{\mathbf{0}}^{t}&0&{\mathbf{0}}^{t}\\
C&{\mathbf{0}}&-A^{\tau}\end{pmatrix}

where
{\mathbf{0}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
denotes the zero column vector. The embedding
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)\subset{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
is slightly less immediate. In the following, we will realise
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
for
N\geq 2
as the fixed Lie subalgebra for a maximally split involution on
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
. Consider the
2N\times 2N
-matrix

(2.12)

\displaystyle J=\begin{pmatrix}\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}1&&\\
\hline\cr&S_{2N-2}&\\
\hline\cr&&1\end{array}\end{pmatrix}

where all empty blocks are filled with zero-matrices of the appropriate size.
As
J
and the matrix
S_{2N}
in (
2.2
) commute, the map

(2.13)

\displaystyle\theta:{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)\rightarrow{\mathfrak{so}}(2N),\qquad\theta(M)=-JM^{t}J^{-1}

is well-defined. More explicitly, any
M\in{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
can be written as a block matrix

(2.14)

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}a&-s^{\tau}&0\\
t&P&s\\
0&-t^{\tau}&-a\end{pmatrix}

where
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
s,t\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2N-2}
are column vectors and
P\in{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-2)
. A direct calculation shows that

(2.15)

\displaystyle\theta(M)=\begin{pmatrix}-a&-t^{\tau}&0\\
s&P&t\\
0&-s^{\tau}&a\end{pmatrix}.

We consider the fixed Lie subalgebra

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)^{\theta}=\{M\in{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)\,|\,\theta(M)=M\}={\mathfrak{gl}}_{S}(2N)\cap{\mathfrak{gl}}_{J}(2N).

Comparing (
2.14
) and (
2.15
), we see that

(2.16)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)^{\theta}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&-s^{\tau}&0\\
s&P&s\\
0&-s^{\tau}&0\end{pmatrix}\,\Bigg|\,P\in{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-2)\right\}

where
s\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2N-2}
is a column vector.

Lemma 2.2
.

The map
\eta:{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)\rightarrow{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
defined by

\displaystyle\eta\left(\begin{pmatrix}A&b&B\\
d&0&-b^{\tau}\\
C&-d^{\tau}&-A^{\tau}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2}d&-b^{\tau}&0\\
b&A&2B&b\\
-\frac{1}{2}d^{\tau}&\frac{1}{2}C&-A^{\tau}&-\frac{1}{2}d^{\tau}\\
0&\frac{1}{2}d&-b^{\tau}&0\end{pmatrix}

is an injective homomorphism of Lie algebras with image
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)^{\theta}
.

Proof.

It follows from (
2.7
) and (
2.16
) that
\eta
is an injective linear map with image
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)^{\theta}
. A direct calculation shows that the map
\eta
respects the Lie brackets.
∎

The above Lemma shows that
({\mathfrak{so}}(2N),{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1))
is a symmetric pair.
We can identify the Chevalley generators of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
inside
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
via the embedding
\eta
.
Observe first that

(2.17)

\displaystyle\eta(e_{\gamma_{i}})

\displaystyle{=}e_{\alpha_{i+1}},

\displaystyle\eta(f_{\gamma_{i}})

\displaystyle{=}f_{\alpha_{i+1}},

\displaystyle\eta(h_{\gamma_{i}})

\displaystyle{=}h_{\alpha_{i+1}}

for
i=1,\dots,N-2
. Again, considering the Chevalley generators
e_{\alpha_{N-1}},f_{\alpha_{N-1}}
of
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-2)
inside
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
we have

(2.18)

\displaystyle\eta(e_{\alpha_{N-1}})

\displaystyle=2e_{\alpha_{N}},

\displaystyle\eta(f_{\alpha_{N-1}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}f_{\alpha_{N}}.

Next, recall the elements
b_{i}\in{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
defined by (
2.8
) for
i=1,\dots,N-1
. We have

(2.19)

\displaystyle f_{\alpha_{1}}+\theta(f_{\alpha_{1}})=\eta(b_{1})

and hence

\displaystyle\eta(e_{\gamma_{N-1}})

\displaystyle=\eta(b_{N-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:2Nin2N1}}}{{=}}\eta\big([f_{\gamma_{N-2}},[f_{\gamma_{N-3}},\dots,[f_{\gamma_{1}},b_{1}]\dots]]\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:etaxi}}}{{=}}[f_{\alpha_{N-1}},[f_{\alpha_{N-2}},\dots,[f_{\alpha_{2}},\eta(b_{1})]\dots]]

(2.20)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:f1+tf1}}}{{=}}[f_{\alpha_{N-1}},[f_{\alpha_{N-2}},\dots,[f_{\alpha_{2}},f_{\alpha_{1}}+\theta(f_{\alpha_{1}})]\dots]]

\displaystyle\eta(f_{\gamma_{N-1}})

\displaystyle=2\eta(d_{N-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:bc1}}}{{=}}-2\eta\big([f_{\alpha_{N-1}},b_{N-2}]\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:2Nin2N1}}}{{=}}-2\eta\big([f_{\alpha_{N-1}},[f_{\gamma_{N-3}},\dots,[f_{\gamma_{1}},b_{1}]\dots]]\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:etaxi},\eqref{eq:etaxN-1}}}{{=}}-[f_{\alpha_{N}},[f_{\alpha_{N-2}},\dots,[f_{\alpha_{2}},\eta(b_{1})]\dots]]

(2.21)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:f1+tf1}}}{{=}}-[f_{\alpha_{N}},[f_{\alpha_{N-2}},\dots,[f_{\alpha_{2}},f_{\alpha_{1}}+\theta(f_{\alpha_{1}})]\dots]].

Moreover, we have

(2.22)

\displaystyle\eta(h_{\gamma_{N-1}})=\eta\big(2(E_{N-1,N-1}-E_{N+1,N+1})\big)=2h_{N}=h_{\alpha_{N}}-h_{\alpha_{N-1}}.

We will use Equations (
2.20
) and (
2.21
) in Section
4.2
to identify
q
-analogues of
e_{\gamma_{N-1}}
and
f_{\gamma_{N-1}}
inside the QSP-subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
.

2.4.
The Satake diagram of the symmetric pair
({\mathfrak{so}}(2N),{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1))

The involution
\theta

{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
defined by (

) satisfies properties (1), (2), (3) in Section

with

.
Indeed, by (

\displaystyle\theta(e_{1})

\displaystyle=\theta(E_{1,2}-E_{N+2,N+1})=-E_{2N-1,1}+E_{2N,2}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N+1}[f_{2},[f_{3},{\dots}[f_{N-2},[f_{N},[f_{N-1},[f_{N-2},{\dots}[f_{2},f_{1}]{\dots}]]]]{\dots}]],

\displaystyle\Theta(-\alpha_{1})-\alpha_{1}=2(\alpha_{2}+\dots+\alpha_{N-2})+\alpha_{N-1}+\alpha_{N}\in Q_{X}^{+}

.

3.
Very non-standard quantum
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)

The theory of quantum symmetric pairs provides a coideal subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
of
U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N))
corresponding to the Satake diagram in Figure
1(A)
. The algebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
is a quantum group analogue of
U({\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1))
. We recall the definition of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
in the conventions of
[
Kol14
]
and its presentation in terms of generators and relations as given in
[
BK15
]
.

3.1.
Preliminaries on quantum groups

For the remainder of this paper we fix
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
. Let
k
be a field and fix an element
q\in k\setminus\{0\}
which is not a root of unity. Let
U={U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})}
be the corresponding Drinfeld-Jimbo quantum enveloping algebra defined over
k
in terms of generators
E_{i},F_{i},K_{i}^{\pm 1}
for
i=1,\dots,N
and relations given in
[
Jan96
, 4.3]
. For simplicity we write
I=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. Let
U^{+},U^{-}
and
U^{0}
denote the subalgebras of
U
generated by
\{E_{i}\,|\,i\in I\}
,
\{F_{i}\,|\,i\in I\}
and
\{K_{i}^{\pm 1}\,|\,i\in I\}
, respectively. By
[
Jan96
, 4.21]
the algebra
U
has a triangular decomposition in the sense that the multiplication map

(3.1)

\displaystyle U^{-}\otimes U^{0}\otimes U^{+}\rightarrow U

is a linear isomorphism. Recall the set of simple roots
\Pi=\{\alpha_{i}\,|\,i\in I\}
given by (
2.4
), the root lattice
Q={\mathbb{Z}}\Pi
and the positive cone
Q^{+}={\mathbb{N}}_{0}\Pi
. For any
\mu\in Q
let
U_{\mu}=\{u\in U\,|\,K_{i}uK_{i}^{-1}=q^{\mu(h_{\alpha_{i}})}u\mbox{ for all $i\in I$}\}
be the corresponding weight space. We also write
U^{\pm}_{\mu}=U_{\mu}\cap U^{\pm}
.
Set
\Pi^{\vee}=\{h_{\alpha_{i}}\,|\,i\in I\}
and let
Q^{\vee}={\mathbb{Z}}\Pi^{\vee}
be the coroot lattice. We can view
U^{0}
as the group algebra of
Q^{\vee}
and we write
K_{h}=\prod_{i\in I}K_{i}^{n_{i}}
if
h=\sum_{i\in I}n_{i}h_{\alpha_{i}}
. With this notation we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle K_{h}E_{i}K_{h}^{-1}=q^{\alpha_{i}(h)}E_{i},\quad K_{h}F_{i}K_{h}^{-1}=q^{-\alpha_{i}(h)}F_{i}\qquad\mbox{for all $h\in Q^{\vee},i\in I$.}

Recall that
U
is a Hopf algebra with coproduct
{\varDelta}
, counit
\varepsilon
and antipode
S
uniquely determined by

\displaystyle{\varDelta}(E_{i})=E_{i}\otimes 1+K_{i}\otimes E_{i},\quad{\varDelta}(F_{i})=F_{i}\otimes K_{i}^{-1}+1\otimes F_{i},\quad{\varDelta}(K_{i})=K_{i}\otimes K_{i}.

Let
T_{i}:U\rightarrow U
for
i\in I
denote the Lusztig braid group automorphisms on
U
in the conventions of
[
Jan96
, 8.14]
. In particular, for
i,j\in I
with
a_{ij}=-1
we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle T_{i}(E_{j})=[E_{i},E_{j}]_{q^{-1}}=T_{j}^{-1}(E_{i}),\qquad T_{i}(F_{j})=[F_{j},F_{i}]_{q}=T_{j}^{-1}(F_{i}).

Let
W
be the Weyl group of
{\mathfrak{g}}
generated by the simple reflections
s_{i}
corresponding to the simple roots
\alpha_{i}
for
i\in I
. For any
w\in W
with reduced expression
w=s_{i_{1}}\dots s_{i_{\ell}}
let
T_{w}=T_{i_{1}}\dots T_{i_{\ell}}
denote the corresponding Lusztig automorphism of
U
.

Finally, let
\sigma:U\rightarrow U
denote the involutive algebra antiautomorphism defined by
\sigma(E_{i})=E_{i}
,
\sigma(F_{i})=F_{i}
and
\sigma(K_{i})=K_{i}^{-1}
, see
[
Lus94
, 3.1.3]
.

3.2.
A very non-standard deformation of
U({\mathfrak{so}}(2n-1))

Let
X\subset I
be the subset
X=\{2,\dots,N\}
and let
{\mathcal{M}}_{X}\subset U
be the subalgebra generated by all
E_{j},F_{j},K_{j}^{\pm 1}
for
j\in X
. By construction, the Hopf-subalgebra
{\mathcal{M}}_{X}
is isomorphic to the Drinfeld-Jimbo quantum enveloping algebra
U_{q}({\mathfrak{so}}(2N-2))
. Let
W_{X}\subset W
be the parabolic subgroup corresponding to the subset
X
and let
w_{X}\in W_{X}
be the longest element.
Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}\subset U
be the subalgebra generated by
{\mathcal{M}}_{X}
and the element

\displaystyle B_{1}=F_{1}-c_{1}T_{w_{X}}(E_{1})K_{1}^{-1}.

The algebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
depends on a single parameter
c_{1}\in k\setminus\{0\}
.
We call
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
the very-nonstandard quantum deformation of
U({\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1))
. The pair
(U,{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})
is an example of a quantum symmetric pair as constructed by G. Letzter in
[
Let99
]
. Here we follow the conventions of
[
Kol14
]
. In particular,
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}\subset U
is a right coideal subalgebra, that is
{\varDelta}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})\subset U\otimes{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
. As in
[
Kol14
]
we also refer to the algebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
as a quantum symmetric pair (QSP) coideal subalgebra of
U
.

Remark 3.1
.

The algebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
was first defined by Noumi and Sugitani in
[
NS95
, Section 3]
as case ‘
BDI
for
\ell=1
and even
N
’. Their construction is more in the spirit of the FRT approach to quantum groups and relies on an explicit solution of the reflection equation which is a
q
-analogue of the matrix
J
in (
2.12
). Noumi and Sugitani denoted their QSP-coideal subalgebra by
U^{\mathrm{tw}}_{q}({\mathfrak{k}})
. Letzter showed in
[
Let99
, Section 6]
that
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
as defined here and
U^{\mathrm{tw}}_{q}({\mathfrak{k}})
as defined in
[
NS95
]
coincide for a suitable choice of the parameter
c_{1}
.

For
i=1,\dots,N-1
define reduced words
\sigma_{i}
in the Weyl group
W
by

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}=s_{i}\dots s_{N-2}s_{N-1}s_{N}s_{N-2}\dots s_{i}.

Then
w_{X}=\sigma_{2}\dots\sigma_{N-1}
is a reduced expression for the longest element
w_{X}\in W_{X}
. Using Equation (
3.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle T_{w_{X}}

\displaystyle(E_{1})=T_{\sigma_{2}}(E_{1})=T_{2}\dots T_{N-2}T_{N-1}T_{N}T_{N-2}\dots T_{2}(E_{1})

(3.5)

we have the iterative formula

\displaystyle\pi(\Omega_{j+1})=\pi(\Omega_{j})\frac{K_{j+1}-(2-q^{-2})K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}-q\Psi_{j}(\pi(\Omega_{j}))\frac{K_{j+1}-K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}.

Proof.

For
j\in\{1,\dots,N-2\}
define
A_{j}=T_{j}^{-1}\dots T_{2}^{-1}(F_{1})
and
C_{j}=T_{j}\dots T_{2}(E_{1})
, and in particular
A_{1}=F_{1}
and
C_{1}=E_{1}
. We have
\Omega_{j}=[A_{j},C_{j}]
and
\Omega_{j+1}=[[F_{j+1},A_{j}]_{q},[E_{j+1},C_{j}]_{q^{-1}}]
. Using the relations
[F_{j+1},C_{j}]=0=[E_{j+1},A_{j}]
we obtain

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle(\Omega_{j+1})=-\pi\big([E_{j+1},C_{j}]_{q^{-1}}\,[F_{j+1},A_{j}]_{q}\big)

\displaystyle=-\pi\big(E_{j+1}F_{j+1}C_{j}A_{j}{-}q^{-1}C_{j}E_{j+1}F_{j+1}A_{j}{-}qE_{j+1}C_{j}A_{j}F_{j+1}{+}C_{j}A_{j}E_{j+1}F_{j+1}\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{K_{j+1}-K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}\pi([C_{j},A_{j}])+q^{-1}\pi([C_{j},A_{j}])\frac{qK_{j+1}-q^{-1}K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}

\displaystyle\qquad+q\pi\big(E_{j+1}\pi([C_{j},A_{j}])F_{j+1}\big)-\pi([C_{j},A_{j}])\frac{K_{j+1}-K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}

\displaystyle=2\pi(\Omega_{j})\frac{K_{j+1}-K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}-\pi(\Omega_{j})\frac{K_{j+1}-q^{-2}K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}-q\Psi_{j}(\pi(\Omega_{j}))\frac{K_{j+1}-K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}

which gives the desired formula.
∎

The above lemma allows us to compare
\pi(\Omega_{j})
and
\Psi_{j}(\pi(\Omega_{j}))
.

Lemma 5.7
.

For all
j\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}
we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle\pi(\Omega_{j})-q\Psi_{j}(\pi(\Omega_{j}))=(-1)^{j-1}q^{j}K_{1}\dots K_{j}.

Proof.

We perform induction on
j
. For
j=1
we have
\Omega_{1}=\frac{K_{1}^{-1}-K_{1}}{q-q^{-1}}
and Equation (
5.8
) holds. For
j=1,\dots,N-2
we now use Lemma
5.6
and the fact that
\pi(\Omega_{j})
is invariant under
\Psi_{j+1}
to calculate

\displaystyle\pi(\Omega_{j+1})-

\displaystyle q\Psi_{j+1}(\pi(\Omega_{j+1}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi(\Omega_{j})\left[\frac{K_{j+1}-(2-q^{-1})K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}-q\frac{qK_{j+1}-(2-q^{-1})q^{-1}K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}\right]

\displaystyle-q\Psi_{j}(\pi(\Omega_{j}))\left[\frac{K_{j+1}-K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}-q\frac{qK_{j+1}-q^{-1}K_{j+1}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\pi(\Omega_{j})-q\Psi_{j}(\pi(\Omega_{j}))\big)(-q)K_{j+1}.

This formula provides the induction step.
∎

Using the three lemmas above, we obtain the desired expression for
\pi(\Omega)

Proposition 5.8

given by (

\displaystyle\pi(\Omega)=c_{1}(-1)^{N-1}q^{N-2}\frac{K_{N}K_{N-1}^{-1}-K_{N-1}K_{N}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}.

Using Lemmas

\displaystyle\pi(\Omega)=

\displaystyle c_{1}\big(\pi(\Omega_{N})K_{N-1}^{-1}-\pi(\Omega_{N-1})K_{N}^{-1}\big)K_{1}^{-1}\dots K_{N-2}^{-1}

\displaystyle c_{1}\Big[\pi(\Omega_{N-2})\frac{K_{N}{-}(2{-}q^{-2})K_{N}^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}

Remark 5.12

By Lemma (

5.8.

-modules

Fix

. Recall that we use the notation

Remark 5.19
.

Equation (

) implies that
{\mbox{Im}}(\varphi_{N-1})\subset{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}F_{N}^{n_{N}+1}v_{\lambda}
. Indeed, by an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2)
-argument and Equation (

{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}B_{\beta_{N}}^{n_{N-1}+1}v_{\lambda}
in the definition of
\accentset{\approx}{N}(\lambda)
.

By Propositions
5.14
and
5.15
we have
\accentset{\approx}{N}(\lambda)\neq M(\lambda)
. Consider the factor module

\displaystyle\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)=M(\lambda)/\accentset{\approx}{N}(\lambda).

We claim that
\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)
is finite-dimensional.

Proposition 5.20
.

For all
\lambda\in P^{+}_{2N-1}
the relation
0<\dim(\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda))<\infty
holds.

Proof.

The left-hand inequality holds because
v_{\lambda}\notin\accentset{\approx}{N}(\lambda)
. To verify the right-hand inequality define a left ideal
\accentset{\approx}{J}_{\lambda}\subset{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
by

\displaystyle\accentset{\approx}{J}_{\lambda}=J_{\lambda}+\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}F_{i+1}^{n_{i}+1}+{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}F_{N}^{n_{N}+1}.

By construction we have

(5.22)

\displaystyle\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)={\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}/\accentset{\approx}{J}_{\lambda}.

The standard filtration
{\mathcal{F}}_{\ast}
on
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
from Section
3.4
induces a filtration
{\mathcal{G}}_{\ast}
on
\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)
defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{n}(\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda))={\mathcal{F}}_{n}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})/\accentset{\approx}{J}_{\lambda}\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{n}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}).

In this way
\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)
is a filtered left module over the filtered algebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
, see e.g.
[
KL00
, Chapter 6]
. In particular, the associated graded space

is a graded module over the graded algebra

. By the isomorphism theorems we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{gr}}_{n}(\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda))=\frac{{\mathcal{F}}_{n}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})/\accentset{\approx}{J}_{\lambda}\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{n}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})}{{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})/\accentset{\approx}{J}_{\lambda}\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})}\cong{\mathcal{A}}_{n}\big/{\mathrm{gr}}_{n}(\accentset{\approx}{J}_{\lambda}).

Hence it suffices to show that
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathrm{gr}}(\accentset{\approx}{J}_{\lambda})
is a finite-dimensional vector space.
As
\accentset{\approx}{J}_{\lambda}
contains the elements

\displaystyle K_{i}-q^{\lambda(\eta^{-1}(h_{\alpha_{i}}))},E_{i},B_{1},F_{j}^{n_{j{-}1}+1},F_{N}^{n_{N}+1}\qquad\mbox{for $i\in X$, $j\in X\setminus\{N\}$},

the left ideal

contains the elements

(5.23)

\displaystyle K_{i}-q^{\lambda(\eta^{-1}(h_{\alpha_{i}}))},E_{i},F_{1},F_{j}^{n_{j{-}1}+1},F_{N}^{n_{N}+1}\qquad\mbox{for $i\in X$, $j\in X\setminus\{N\}$}.

be the left ideal generated by the elements in (

, we see that

is a quotient of

. By the triangular decomposition (

) of
{\mathcal{A}}
we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}/J^{\prime}_{\lambda}\cong U^{-}\Big/\Big(U^{-}F_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{N-1}U^{-}F_{j}^{n_{j{-}1}+1}+U^{-}F_{N}^{n_{N}+1}\Big).

Hence the quotient

is finite-dimensional by
[
Jan96
, Lemma 5.9]
. This implies that
{\mathrm{gr}}(\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda))
and hence
\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)
is finite-dimensional.
∎

Remark 5.21
.

In general, the inclusion

is proper. Indeed, the ideal
\accentset{\approx}{J}_{\lambda}

and hence Proposition

implies that

in this case. We can now adapt
[
Jan96
, Theorem 5.10]
to our setting.

Let

not a root of unity.
For each

\displaystyle\dots],f_{\alpha_{N-1}}],f_{\alpha_{N}}]=(-1)^{N-k+1}[E_{N,k}-E_{2N-k+1,N+1},f_{\alpha_{N}}]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N-k+1}(E_{N+2,k}-E_{2N-k+1,N-1})

in
{\mathfrak{so}}(2N)
.
Hence, for
N+2\leq j\leq 2(N-1)
we have

\displaystyle\overline{B_{\beta_{j}}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N-k+1}\big([E_{N+2,k}-E_{2N-k+1,N-1},\eta(b_{N-2})]\big)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N-k+1}\eta\big([E_{N+2,k-1}-E_{2N-k+1,N-2},E_{N-2,N}-E_{N,N+2}]\big)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N-k+1}\eta(2d_{k-1})

where
k=2N-j
. This translates into (
6.5
) for
j=(N-1)+\ell
and
k=N+1-\ell
.
∎

6.3.
Specialisation of highest weight
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-modules

Let
V
be a highest weight
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module of highest weight
\lambda\in P_{2N-1}
with highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}\in V
. We define

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{A}}=({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})_{\mathbf{A}}v_{\lambda}.

The triangular decomposition (
6.4
) of
({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})_{\mathbf{A}}
implies that
V_{\mathbf{A}}=({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{-})_{\mathbf{A}}v_{\lambda}
. The negative part
({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{-})_{\mathbf{A}}
of
({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})_{\mathbf{A}}
is a direct sum of
P_{2N-1}
-weight spaces under the adjoint action of
U^{0}_{X}
. Moreover, the weight spaces of
({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}^{-})_{\mathbf{A}}
are finite-dimensional, free
\mathbf{A}
-modules. Hence

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{A}}=\bigoplus_{\mu\leq\lambda}V_{\mathbf{A},\mu}

is a direct sum of
P_{2N-1}
-weight spaces. The weight spaces
V_{\mathbf{A},\mu}
are finitely generated, torsion-free and hence free
\mathbf{A}
-modules. By
[
Kol14
, Lemma 10.6]
we have

(6.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}_{\mathbf{A}}(V_{\mathbf{A},\mu})=\dim_{k}(V_{\mu}).

The tensor product
V^{1}:={\mathbb{K}}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}V_{\mathbf{A}}
is called the specialisation of
V
at
q=1
. For any
v\in V_{\mathbf{A}}
we write
{\overline{v}}=1\otimes v\in V^{1}
. The specialisation
V^{1}
is a module over the specialisation
{\mathbb{K}}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})_{\mathbf{A}}=\overline{{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}}=U({\mathfrak{k}})
. By construction we have
\overline{bv}={\overline{b}}\,{\overline{v}}
for all
b\in({\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}})_{\mathbf{A}}
and
v\in V_{\mathbf{A}}
. For any weight vector
v_{\mu}\in V_{\mathbf{A},\mu}
and any
h\in Q^{\vee}_{2N-1}
we have

\displaystyle\eta(h)\overline{v_{\mu}}=\overline{(K_{\eta(h)};0)_{q}}\,\overline{v_{\mu}}=\overline{\frac{q^{\mu(h)}-1}{q-1}v_{\mu}}=\mu(h)\overline{v_{\mu}}.

and hence
V^{1}
is a
{\mathfrak{k}}
-weight module. Proposition
6.6
implies that
V^{1}
is a highest weight module. Moreover, by (
6.6
) we have
\dim_{k}(V_{\mu})=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(V^{1}_{\mu})
for all
\mu\in P_{2N-1}
. We summarise the discussion in the following proposition.

Proposition 6.7
.

Assume that
k={\mathbb{K}}(q)
where
{\mathbb{K}}
is a field of characteristic
0
. Let
V
be a highest weight
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module of highest weight
\lambda\in P_{2N-1}
. Then the specialisation
V^{1}
of
V
at
q=1
is a highest weight
{\mathfrak{k}}
-module of highest weight
\lambda
and

\displaystyle\dim_{k}(V_{\mu})=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(V^{1}_{\mu})\qquad\mbox{for all $\mu\in P_{2N-1}$.}

We can apply Proposition
6.7
in particular to the simple
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module
L(\lambda)
from Theorem
5.22
. Moreover, as in
[
Jan96
, Theorem 5.15]
we can work over any field
k
of characteristic
0
with
q\in k
transcendental over
\mathbb{Q}
. Recall the definition of the submodules
\widetilde{N}(\lambda)
and
\accentset{\approx}{N}(\lambda)
over the Verma module
M(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in P^{+}_{2N-1}
, and the corresponding quotients
\widetilde{L}(\lambda)
and
\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)
, respectively.

Corollary 6.8
.

Assume that
k
is a field of characteristic
0
and
q\in k
is transcendental over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
\lambda\in P^{+}_{2N-1}
. Then
\widetilde{L}(\lambda)=\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)=L(\lambda)
and the dimension of the weight spaces
L(\lambda)_{\mu}
for
\mu\in P_{2N-1}
are given by Weyl’s character formula.

Proof.

Assume first that
k=\mathbb{Q}(q)
. Each of
\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)
and
L(\lambda)
are quotients of
\widetilde{L}(\lambda)=M(\lambda)/\widetilde{N}(\lambda)
. Hence the highest weight vector
v_{\lambda}
in each of
\widetilde{L}(\lambda)
,
\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)
and
L(\lambda)
satisfies
B_{\beta_{N}}^{n_{N-1}+1}v_{\lambda}=0=F_{i+1}^{n_{i}+1}v_{\lambda}
for
i=1,\dots,N-2

where
n_{i}=\lambda(h_{\gamma_{i}})
and
n_{N-1}=\lambda(h_{\gamma_{N-1}})
. This means that
f_{\gamma_{i}}^{n_{i}+1}\overline{v_{\lambda}}=0
for
i=1,\dots,N-1
in each of the three cases. Hence, each of
\widetilde{L}(\lambda)^{1}
,
\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)^{1}
and
L(\lambda)^{1}
are isomorphic to the unique simple
{\mathfrak{k}}
-module of highest weight
\lambda
. By Proposition
6.7
the surjective
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module homomorphisms
\widetilde{L}(\lambda)\twoheadrightarrow\accentset{\approx}{L}(\lambda)\twoheadrightarrow L(\lambda)
are isomorphisms. The result extends from
\mathbb{Q}(q)
to
k
as in
[
Jan96
, Theorem 5.15]
.
∎

We can also use Proposition
6.7
to show that every finite dimensional highest weight
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module is simple.

Corollary 6.9
.

Assume that
k
is a field of characteristic
0
and
q\in k
is transcendental over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
V
be a finite dimensional highest weight
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module of highest weight
\lambda\in P^{+}_{2N-1}
. Then
V\cong L(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Again we first consider the case
k={\mathbb{K}}(q)
. In this case, by Proposition
6.7
, the specialisation
V^{1}
is a finite dimensional highest weight
{\mathfrak{k}}
-module of highest weight
\lambda
. Hence
V^{1}\cong L(\lambda)^{1}
. Hence Proposition
6.7
implies that
\dim_{k}(V)=\dim_{k}(L(\lambda))
and as
L(\lambda)
is a quotient of
V
we get
V\cong L(\lambda)
. Again we can extend from
\mathbb{Q}(q)
to a general
k
of characteristic zero.
∎

Remark 6.10
.

Recall the definition of the dual
V^{\ast}
of a finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module
V
from (
5.3
). As
{\mathfrak{k}}\cong{\mathfrak{so}}(2N-1)
we have
\dim(L(\lambda)_{\mu}^{1})=\dim(L(\lambda)_{-\mu})
for all
\mu\in P_{2N-1}
. Hence we have
L(\lambda)\cong L(\lambda)^{\ast}
for all
\lambda\in P^{+}_{2N-1}
as they are simple
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-modules with weight spaces of the same dimension.

With Corollary
6.9
and Remark
6.10
we can show that every finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module is semisimple. This can be proved by a word by word translation of the proof of
[
Jan96
, Theorem 5.17]
.

Theorem 6.11
.

Assume that
k
is a field of characteristic
0
and
q\in k
is transcendental over
\mathbb{Q}
. Then every finite dimensional
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathbf{c}}
-module is semisimple.

Remark 6.12
.

We compare Theorem
5.22
and the results of the present section with Watanabe’s more general approach in

]

Set
