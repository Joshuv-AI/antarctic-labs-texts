Cauchy spinors on 3 3 -manifolds
Brice Flamencourt, Sergiu Moroianu

Abstract
. Let 𝒵 \mathcal{Z} be a spin 4 4 -manifold carrying a parallel spinor and M ↪ 𝒵 M\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Z} a hypersurface. The second fundamental form of the embedding induces a flat metric connection on T ​ M TM . Such flat connections satisfy a non-elliptic, non-linear equation in terms of a symmetric 2 2 -tensor on M M . When M M is compact and has positive scalar curvature, the linearized equation has finite dimensional kernel. Four families of solutions are known on the round 3 3 -sphere 𝕊 3 \mathbb{S}^{3} . We study the linearized equation in the vicinity of these solutions and we construct as a byproduct an incomplete hyperkähler metric on 𝕊 3 × ℝ \mathbb{S}^{3}\times\mathbb{R} closely related to the Euclidean Taub-NUT metric on ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} . On 𝕊 3 \mathbb{S}^{3} there do not exist other solutions which either are constant in a left (or right) invariant frame, have three distinct constant eigenvalues, or are invariant in the direction of a left (or right)-invariant eigenvector. We deduce from this last result an extension of Liebmann’s sphere rigidity theorem.



1.
Introduction

The Cauchy problem for parallel spinors

If
(M,g)
is an oriented hypersurface in a spin manifold
(\mathcal{Z},g_{\mathcal{Z}})
and
\Psi
is a parallel spinor on
\mathcal{Z}
, then the restriction

\psi:=\Psi|_{M}
is a spinor on
M
satisfying the initial condition

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}\psi=-\frac{A(X)}{2}\cdot\psi,

\displaystyle(\forall)X\in TM,

(1)

where
A
is the second fundamental form of
M
(see
[
8
, Proposition 1.4.1]
).

Conversely, given a spinor
\psi
on
M
satisfying the constraint (
1
) for some symmetric endomorphism
A
, is it always possible to embed
M
as a hypersurface in an ambient manifold
\mathcal{Z}
with second fundamental form
A
, and such that
\psi
is the restriction to
M
of a parallel spinor
\Psi
on
\mathcal{Z}
? In other words, is (
1
) the only constraint for the existence of
\mathcal{Z}
and
\Psi
? This is the so-called Cauchy problem for parallel spinors studied in
[
2
]
. The answer is positive when all the objects involved are real analytic, and negative in general in the smooth setting for
\dim(M)\geq 3
.

A spinor and symmetric
2
-tensor on
M
satisfying Equation (
1
) will be called below a
Cauchy spinor
, respectively a
Cauchy endomorphism
. Since (
1
) includes as a particular case the Killing spinor equation (i.e., when
A
is a constant multiple of the identity), Cauchy spinors were sometimes called
generalized Killing spinors
e.g. in
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Different generalizations of the notion of Killing spinors appear however in the literature, e.g. in the papers
[
10
]
or
[
6
,
7
]
. We believe that the current name should be more appropriate, as it describes more accurately the property of being the restriction of a parallel spinor to a hypersurface.

The classification problem for Cauchy spinors and endomorphisms on a given manifold
M
requires us to describe all pairs
(\psi,A)
verifying (
1
). In dimension
3
, partial results in this direction were found in
[
14
]
, where Cauchy spinors are characterized in terms of a triple of divergence-free vector fields on
M
. The same authors investigated in
[
13
,
15
]
the case of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, classifying all Cauchy endomorphisms having at most
2
distinct eigenvalues. This example illustrates the little understanding we have of Cauchy spinors in dimension
3
, as we are unable to classify them even on the round sphere. Note that a complete description can be given in several other dimensions
[
15
]
.

Cauchy spinors on 3-manifolds and flat connections

Spin geometry in dimension
3
is special because the Hodge
*
operator allows an exceptional identification between
1
- and
2
-forms, and moreover the real spinor bundle carries a quaternionic structure. Using these algebraic structures, on simply connected
3
-manifolds (which by the Poincaré conjecture must be diffeomorphic to the sphere) we can restate the classification problem for Cauchy spinors without mentioning spinors at all! Indeed, Equation (
1
) implies a constraint for the symmetric endomorphism field
A
:

\displaystyle 0=\mathrm{R}(X,Y)+*d^{\nabla}A(X,Y)+A(X)\wedge A(Y),

\displaystyle(\forall)X,Y\in TM.

(2)

Here
\mathrm{R}
is the Riemann curvature tensor and
d^{\nabla}
is the exterior covariant derivative on
M
mapping sections of
\Lambda^{1}M\otimes TM
to sections of
\Lambda^{2}M\otimes TM
. The set of symmetric endomorphisms satisfying (
2
) is denoted
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
.
If
M
is simply connected, every solution of Equation (
2
) also satisfies (
1
) for some Cauchy spinor, unique up to right multiplication by a quaternion. Our strategy below is to exploit Equation (
2
) in order to obtain new results on the Cauchy problem for parallel spinors, and also on the classification problem for Cauchy spinors.

Equation (
2
) amounts to the flatness of the modified metric connection
\nabla^{A}=\nabla+*A
on
TM
. Even in the compact and simply connected case, the structure of the set of flat connections
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
remains elusive, in part because Equation (
2
) is non-linear and not elliptic. For this reason, we first study the linearization of (
2
). We show that if the scalar curvature of
M
is positive, the space of infinitesimal deformations, defined as the space of symmetric endomorphism fields solution to the linearization of (
2
), is finite-dimensional (Theorem
3.2
). This can be interpreted as a finiteness result for the dimension of the “tangent space” of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
, with the caveat that this set of flat connections is a priori not a smooth manifold. The hypothesis on the sign of the scalar curvature is necessary: we exhibit flat compact
3
-manifolds for which the dimension of the space of deformations is infinite.

Cauchy endomorphisms on the round three-sphere

We view
\mathbb{S}^{3}
as the Lie group of unit-length quaternions. Four examples of symmetric endomorphisms
A
fulfilling the flatness condition (
2
) on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
are known from
[
14
, Example 3.2]
:
\pm\mathrm{Id}
, and the endomorphism fields constant in a left (resp. right)-invariant orthonormal frame, with eigenvalues
1,-3,-3
(respectively
-1,3,3
).
It was already shown in
[
15
]
that there are no deformations around
A=\pm\mathrm{Id}
. We prove that the space of infinitesimal deformations around the other two examples has dimension
2
, and corresponds to the Lie derivative of
A
in the direction of a left (or right-) invariant vector field from
\textrm{ker}(A\pm 3\mathrm{Id})
. In particular, there are no other deformations of the above solutions. If
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
were a smooth manifold, it would therefore necessarily have at least
4
connected components.

The examples of endomorphisms on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
given above are analytic, so by
[
2
]
they can be realized as second fundamental forms of the three-sphere embedded as a hypersurface in a generalized cylinder
\mathcal{Z}:=(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
carrying a parallel spinor. The cases
A=\pm\mathrm{Id}
both induce the standard embedding of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
into
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. We calculate in Section
4
an explicit expression of this metric in the other two cases, finding an extension of the family of Euclidean Taub-NUT metrics with a negative parameter. This computation solves the long-time Cauchy problem on the three-sphere for the four known examples of Cauchy spinors on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

Classification results

In the final section we prove three classification results for symmetric endomorphisms solving Equation (
2
) on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. The known examples of Cauchy endomorphisms have constant matrices in a left or right-invariant orthonormal basis of the tangent bundle of the Lie group
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. It is natural to ask if those are the only symmetric endomorphisms solutions to (
2
) with this property. We prove
in Proposition
6.1
that this is indeed the case. Moreover, since all the solutions of (
2
) with at most two eigenvalues are known
[
15
, Theorem 4.10]
, we investigate the case where
A
has three distinct constant eigenvalues. We show that there is no solution in this case, using a characterization of the Hopf fields on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
(Proposition
6.2
).
Finally, we show that the solutions which are constant only in the direction of a left or right-invariant eigenvector
\xi
of
A
, i.e., such that
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}A=0
, must also be constant in a left (respectively right) invariant orthonormal frame, so they fall in the class of previously known examples (Proposition
6.4
). This result turns out to imply (a slight extension of) the Liebmann rigidity theorem
[
11
]
.

Related results

Cauchy spinors in
3+1
Lorentzian signature were recently classified by Murcia and Shahbazi
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
, thanks to the fact that one of the elements in the global coframe defined by the Cauchy spinor on the hypersurface is privileged, and determines an integrable foliation. On the other hand, unlike in the Riemannian case, the Lorentzian initial value problem for parallel spinors is of hyperbolic nature and has solutions in the smooth category. Thus the two problems are in fact rather different, despite some formal similarities.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Spinors in dimension
3

The real Clifford algebra
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}
is canonically isomorphic to the quaternion algebra
\mathbb{H}
by the map sending
e_{1}
to
i
and
e_{2}
to
j
, where
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It follows that the even Clifford algebra
\mathrm{Cl}_{3}^{\mathrm{even}}
is also isomorphic to

\mathbb{H}
by the unique algebra map sending
e_{1}e_{3}
to
i
,
e_{2}e_{3}
to
j
, and hence
e_{2}e_{3}
to
k
.

Multiplication by the central element
P:=\frac{1-e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}}{2}
is a projector in
\mathrm{Cl}_{3}
. Let
\Sigma_{3}
be the image of this projector. Then
P
maps
\mathrm{Cl}_{3}^{\mathrm{even}}
isomorphically onto
\Sigma_{3}
, thus identifying
\Sigma_{3}
to
\mathbb{H}
. Since
P
is central,
\mathrm{Cl}_{3}^{\mathrm{even}}
acts on
\Sigma_{3}
by left multiplication, and this representation commutes with the right action of
\mathbb{H}\simeq\Sigma^{3}
on itself. The restriction of this quaternionic representation to the spin group
\mathrm{Spin}_{3}
is the spinor representation. By construction, the spinor representation is thus the restriction of a
\mathrm{Cl}_{3}
representation under which the volume element
e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}
acts as minus the identity.

The spinor representation is orthogonal with respect to the natural scalar product on
\Sigma_{3}
. The right multiplication by quaternions is also compatible with the scalar product, in the sense that
\langle\psi a,\phi a\rangle=|a|^{2}\langle\psi,\phi\rangle
for all
a\in\mathbb{H}
and
\psi,\phi\in\Sigma_{3}
.

Recall now that every oriented
3
-manifold is parallelizable, hence spin. Let
(M,g)
be an oriented Riemannian 3-manifold with a fixed spin structure. The real spinor bundle
\Sigma M
over
M
is the vector bundle associated to the spin bundle and the
\mathrm{Spin}_{3}
spinor representation
\Sigma_{3}
. It is endowed with a natural inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and a
\mathrm{Cl}(TM)
-action such that the Clifford product by vectors on
\Sigma M
is skew-symmetric. The right action of
\mathbb{H}
on
\Sigma_{3}
induces a right
\mathbb{H}
action on
\Sigma M
commuting with Clifford multiplication by tangent vectors, and satisfying
\langle\psi a,\phi a\rangle=|a|^{2}\langle\psi,\phi\rangle
for all
a\in\mathbb{H}
and
\psi,\phi\in\Sigma M
.

By construction, Clifford multiplication with the volume form acts as
-\mathrm{Id}
on
\Sigma M
. This choice implies that for a
2
-form
\omega\in\Lambda^{2}(M)
and a spinor
\psi\in\Sigma M
we have

\omega\cdot\psi=*\omega\cdot\psi,

(3)

where
*
denotes the Hodge star-operator.

2.2.
Cauchy spinors and endomorphisms

Let
E\to M
be a vector bundle endowed with a connection
\nabla
.
The exterior differential twisted by
\nabla
is defined on
E
-valued
p
-forms using Einstein’s summation convention:

d^{\nabla}(\omega\otimes V):=d\omega\otimes V+(-1)^{p}\omega\wedge X^{j}\otimes\nabla_{X_{j}}V,

where
(X_{j})_{1\leq j\leq n}
is any local orthonormal frame on
M
.
For an endomorphism field
A\in\mathrm{End}(TM)
, the above formula becomes

\displaystyle d^{\nabla}A(X,Y):=(\nabla_{X}A)Y-(\nabla_{Y}A)X,

\displaystyle(\forall)X,Y\in TM.

(4)

The divergence operator
\delta^{\nabla}
is the formal adjoint of
d^{\nabla}
with respect to the
L^{2}
-inner product on vector-valued forms.

Let now
\nabla
be the Levi-Civita covariant derivative on an oriented
3
-manifold
M
, and
\mathrm{R}=d^{\nabla}\circ\nabla
its Riemann curvature tensor.
The Levi-Civita covariant derivative on the spinor bundle
\Sigma M
is still denoted by
\nabla
, and its curvature tensor is written
\mathcal{R}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spin
3
-manifold.
A non-zero section
\psi\in\Gamma(\Sigma M)
is a
Cauchy spinor
if there exists a symmetric endomorphism field
A\in\Gamma(T^{*}M\times TM)
such that the pair
(\psi,A)
satisfies Equation (
1
). In this situation,
A
is called a
Cauchy endomorphism
.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{M}
the set of all Cauchy endomorphisms on
M
, and by
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
the set of all symmetric endomorphisms on
M
satisfying Equation (
2
).

In other words, an endomorphism field
A
on
M
is a Cauchy endomorphism if it is symmetric and there exists some non-zero spinor

\psi\in\Gamma(\Sigma M)
satisfying
\nabla_{X}\psi=-\frac{1}{2}A(X)\cdot\psi
for all vectors
X\in TM
.
We stress that the symmetry assumption on
A
is crucial here, since in dimension
3
every spinor of constant length determines uniquely some general endomorphism field
A
so that Equation (
1
) holds.

The sets
\mathcal{C}_{M}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
will form our main object of study in this paper.

2.3.
Parallel spinors in dimension
4

Let us review below the main results of
[
2
]
about parallel spinors in dimension
4
, Ricci-flat metrics and Cauchy spinors.

Let
(M,g)
be a hypersurface in a
4
-dimensional manifold
(\mathcal{Z},g^{\mathcal{Z}})
.
If
\mathcal{Z}
is Ricci-flat, the second fundamental form
W
of the embedding
M\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Z}
satisfies the contracted Codazzi and Gauss equations:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Scal}^{M}=\mathrm{tr}(A)^{2}-\mathrm{tr}(A^{2}),

\displaystyle\delta^{\nabla}A+d\mathrm{tr}(A)=0.

(5)

Conversely, if
(M,g^{M})
is real analytic and the constraints (
5
) hold on
M
for some analytic symmetric endomorphism
A
, then there exists a Ricci-flat real-analytic ambient manifold
(\mathcal{Z},g^{\mathcal{Z}})
in which
M
imbeds isometrically with second fundamental form
A
.

Upon replacing
\mathcal{Z}
by a collar neighborhood of
M
, we can assume that
\mathcal{Z}
is also parallelizable. Fix a spin structure on
\mathcal{Z}
. The restriction of each of the spinor bundles
\Sigma^{\pm}\mathcal{Z}
to
M
is isomorphic to
\Sigma M
.
If
\mathcal{Z}
admits a nonzero parallel spinor
\Psi
, then

must be Ricci flat. Up to reversing the orientation on
\mathcal{Z}
we can assume that the chiral component
\Psi^{+}
is nonzero, implying that the metric is self-dual (if
\Psi^{+}
and
\Psi^{-}
are both nonzero then
\mathcal{Z}
is flat). Moreover, the restriction of
\Psi^{+}
to
M
is a Cauchy spinor, and the second fundamental form of
M\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Z}
is a Cauchy endomorphism.

Conversely, if there exists a Cauchy spinor
\psi
and a Cauchy endomorphism
A
on
M
satisfying (
1
), then the identities (
5
) are satisfied (an alternate derivation of these identities can be found in
[
13
, Lemma 3.1, Equation (12)]
). If moreover
M
,
g^{M}
,
A
and
\psi
are real-analytic, then there exists a Ricci-flat, self-dual
4
-manifold
(\mathcal{Z},g^{\mathcal{Z}})
into which
(M,g^{M})
embeds isometrically with second fundamental form
A
, and locally there exists a parallel spinor of positive chirality on
\mathcal{Z}
extending
\psi
.

2.4.
The modified metric connection
\nabla^{A}

Throughout the paper we identify
1
-forms and vectors on
M
using the metric
g
.
Given any
A\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{1}M\otimes TM)
, we define
\overline{A}\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{1}M\otimes\Lambda^{2}M)
by

\overline{A}(X):=*(A(X))
for all
X\in TM
. We can view the
2
-form
\overline{A}(X)
as a skew-symmetric endomorphism of
TM
in the usual way: for
Y\in TM
,
\overline{A}(X)(Y)
is the unique vector
Z
satisfying
g(Z,W)=\overline{A}(X)(Y,W)
for all
W\in TM
.

We introduce the connection on
TM

\displaystyle\nabla^{A}_{X}Y:=\nabla_{X}Y+\overline{A}(X)(Y),

\displaystyle X,Y\in TM,

(6)

and we denote by
\mathrm{R}^{A}
its curvature tensor. Since by construction
\overline{A}(X)
is skew symmetric,
\nabla^{A}
is compatible with the Riemannian metric and hence induces a connection, still denoted
\nabla^{A}
, on the spinor bundle
\Sigma M

by pull-back from the orthonormal frame bundle:

\displaystyle\nabla^{A}_{X}\psi=\nabla_{X}\psi+\frac{A(X)}{2}\cdot\psi,

\displaystyle(\forall)X\in TM,\psi\in\Sigma M.

We denote by
\mathcal{R}^{A}
the curvature of
\nabla^{A}
on spinors.

The problem of finding solutions to (
1
) can be reduced to solving an equation involving the endomorphism field
A
alone, at least when
M
is simply-connected.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian
3
-manifold and
A\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{1}M\otimes TM)
a symmetric endomorphism field. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

Locally on
M
there exist nonzero Cauchy spinors with respect to
A
;

(2)

\mathcal{R}^{A}=0
;

(3)

\mathrm{R}^{A}=0
;

(4)

The symmetric endomorphism
A
satisfies the equation (
2
), i.e.,
A\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
.

When
M
is simply connected, the first condition is equivalent to the global existence of Cauchy spinors, hence
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}=\mathcal{C}_{M}
.

Proof
.

If there exists a locally defined, nonzero spinor
\psi
satisfying (
1
), then the four mutually orthogonal spinors
(\psi,\psi i,\psi j,\psi k)
, defined with the help of the quaternionic structure on
\Sigma M
, are parallel for the covariant derivative
\nabla^{A}
. This implies that the curvature endomorphism
\mathcal{R}^{A}
of
(\Sigma M,\nabla^{A})
vanishes.
Conversely, if
\mathcal{R}^{A}=0
, locally there exist non-zero spinors parallel with respect to the connection
\nabla^{A}
, i.e., Cauchy spinors. Moreover, if
M
is simply connected then such spinors exist globally on
M
.

Since the connection
\nabla^{A}
on
\Sigma M
is induced from the connection with the same name on
TM
and the spinor representation
\Sigma M
, it is well known that

\mathcal{R}^{A}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{R}^{A}(X,Y)\cdot
, where
\cdot
denotes Clifford multiplication (see e.g.
[
5
, Theorem 2.7]
). In dimension
3
the map associating to a
2
-form its action by Clifford multiplication on spinors is injective, thus
\mathcal{R}^{A}(X,Y)=0
if and only if
\mathrm{R}^{A}(X,Y)=0
.

For all
X,Y\in TM
, we compute from the definition of
\nabla^{A}
its curvature:

\mathrm{R}^{A}(X,Y)=\mathrm{R}(X,Y)+d^{\nabla}\overline{A}(X,Y)+[\overline{A}(X),\overline{A}(Y)]

Since the Hodge
*
operator is parallel, it commutes with
d^{\nabla}
, so
d^{\nabla}\overline{A}=*d^{\nabla}A
. Also, we check directly that

\overline{A}(X)\circ\overline{A}(Y)-\overline{A}(Y)\circ\overline{A}(X)=A(X)\wedge A(Y).

Hence
\mathrm{R}^{A}=\mathrm{R}+*d^{\nabla}A+A\wedge A
as claimed.
∎

Proposition
2.2
shows that
\mathcal{C}_{M}\subset\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
, with equality when
M
is simply connected.

3.
Deformation of endomorphism fields

There is not much one can say about the structure of the sets
\mathcal{C}_{M}
or
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
, even in the simply connected case when they coincide. Let us introduce the following definition:

Definition 3.1
.

The space of infinitesimal deformations of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
at
A\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
is

\displaystyle\{\dot{C}(0)|\>(\exists)\varepsilon>0,(\exists)C\in C^{\infty}((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon),\Gamma(\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(TM)),C(0)=A,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{R}^{C(t)}|_{t=0}=0\}.

In the case where
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
is a differentiable manifold, its tangent space
T_{A}\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
is the space of tangent vectors at
t=0
to smooth curves
C:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
with
C(0)=A
. In general, since we do not know any differentiable structure on
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
, the above cone might even not be a vector space. The space of infinitesimal deformations always contains this formal tangent cone.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact oriented Riemannian
3
-manifold with strictly positive scalar curvature and let
A\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{M}
. Then the space of infinitesimal deformations of
A
is finite-dimensional.

Proof
.

Let
C\in C^{\infty}((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon),\Gamma(\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(TM))
be a smooth curve as in Definition
3.1
, with
C(0)=A
. We define
\dot{A}:=\dot{C}(0)
. By differentiating with respect to
t
in (
2
) the condition
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{R}^{C(t)}|_{t=0}=0
rewrites

0=*(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla}\dot{A})(X,Y)+\dot{A}(X)\wedge A(Y)+A(X)\wedge\dot{A}(Y).

(7)

We recall some elementary identities about the Hodge star-operator in dimension
3
.
Let
X,Y\in TM
and
\alpha\in\Lambda^{2}M
. Then we easily check that

\displaystyle X\lrcorner*Y

\displaystyle=-*(X\wedge Y),

\displaystyle X\wedge*\alpha

\displaystyle=-*(X\lrcorner\alpha).

(8)

Using these identities, one has for all
X,Y\in TM

\displaystyle\dot{A}(X)\wedge A(Y)+A(X)\wedge\dot{A}(Y)

\displaystyle=-*\overline{A}(Y)(\dot{A}(X))+*\overline{A}(X)(\dot{A}(Y)).

Consequently, Equation (
7
) rewrites

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla}\dot{A})(X,Y)-\overline{A}(Y)(\dot{A}(X))+\overline{A}(X)(\dot{A}(Y))=(d^{\nabla^{A}}\dot{A})(X,Y).

We are hence led to the equation for the infinitesimal deformation of a flat connection:

d^{\nabla^{A}}\dot{A}=0.

(9)

According to Proposition
2.2
, the connection
\nabla^{A}
is flat, meaning that
\mathrm{R}^{A}=d^{\nabla^{A}}\circ\nabla^{A}=0
on
\Gamma(TM)
. But
d^{\nabla^{A}}\circ d^{\nabla^{A}}
is given by the action of
\mathrm{R}^{A}
also on
\Gamma(\Lambda^{k}M\otimes TM)
for all
k\geq 0
.
This implies that
d^{\nabla^{A}}\circ d^{\nabla^{A}}=0
on
\Gamma(\Lambda^{*}M\otimes M)
. The differential operators

d^{\nabla^{A}}:\Gamma(\Lambda^{*}M\otimes TM)\to\Gamma(\Lambda^{*+1}M\otimes TM)

form therefore an elliptic complex.
By Hodge theory, Equation (
9
) implies that there exists a vector field
X_{\dot{A}}\in\Gamma(TM)
and a
d^{\nabla^{A}}
-harmonic vector-valued
1
-form
B_{\dot{A}}\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{1}M\otimes TM)
(i.e.,
\delta^{\nabla^{A}}B_{\dot{A}}=0
and
d^{\nabla^{A}}B_{\dot{A}}=0
) such that
\dot{A}=\nabla^{A}X_{\dot{A}}+B_{\dot{A}}
. Notice that this equation still involves the symmetric endomorphism
A
which defines the flat connection

\nabla^{A}
.

The symmetry condition on
\dot{A}
can be rewritten as an equation on
X_{\dot{A}}
,
B_{\dot{A}}
and
A
:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X\in\Gamma(TM)
and
B\in\Gamma(\Lambda^{1}M\otimes TM)
. The endomorphism
d^{\nabla^{A}}X+B
is symmetric if and only if
X
, viewed as a 1-form, satisfies
dX-*(X\mathrm{tr}(A)-AX)+X_{k}\wedge B(X_{k})=0
, where
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})
is any orthonormal basis.

Proof
.

The endomorphism
d^{\nabla^{A}}X+B
is symmetric if and only if its skew-symmetric part is zero. Using the identities (
8
) we compute

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=X_{k}\wedge\nabla^{A}_{X_{k}}X+X_{k}\wedge B(X_{k})

\displaystyle=X_{k}\wedge(\nabla_{X_{k}}X+\overline{A}(X_{k})(X))+X_{k}\wedge B(X_{k})

\displaystyle=X_{k}\wedge(\nabla_{X_{k}}X+X\lrcorner\overline{A}(X_{k}))+X_{k}\wedge B(X_{k})

\displaystyle=X_{k}\wedge(\nabla_{X_{k}}X-*(X\wedge A(X_{k})))+X_{k}\wedge B(X_{k})

\displaystyle=X_{k}\wedge\nabla_{X_{k}}X-X_{k}\wedge*(X\wedge A(X_{k}))+X_{k}\wedge B(X_{k})

\displaystyle=X_{k}\wedge\nabla_{X_{k}}X+*(X_{k}\lrcorner X\wedge A(X_{k}))+X_{k}\wedge B(X_{k})

\displaystyle=dX+*(X(X_{k})A(X_{k})-A(X_{k})(X_{k})X)+X_{k}\wedge B(X_{k})

\displaystyle=dX+*(A(X)-X\textrm{tr}A)+X_{k}\wedge B(X_{k}).\qed

The space of
d^{\nabla^{A}}
-harmonic vector-valued
1
-forms
B
is finite dimensional by ellipticity. Lemma
3.3
implies the identity
dX_{\dot{A}}-*(X_{\dot{A}}\textrm{tr}(A)-AX_{\dot{A}})=-X_{k}\wedge B_{\dot{A}}(X_{k})
, and for a given
B
, the solutions
X\in TM
of

dX-*(X\mathrm{tr}(A)-AX)+X_{k}\wedge B(X_{k})=0

form an affine space of direction
\mathrm{ker}(X\mapsto dX-*(X\mathrm{tr}(A)-AX))
. Thus, to show that the space of deformations is finite dimensional, it is sufficient to prove that the solution space of

dX-*(X\mathrm{tr}(A)-AX)=0

(10)

is finite dimensional.

By applying the exterior derivative to equation (
10

0=d*(X\textrm{tr}(A)-AX)=*\delta(X\textrm{tr}(A)-AX)
, and then

\delta(X\textrm{tr}(A)-AX)=0.

We define the differential operator

\displaystyle\Xi:\Lambda^{1}M

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\Lambda^{2}M\oplus\Lambda^{0}M,

\displaystyle\Xi(X)=(dX-*(X\textrm{tr}(A)-AX),\delta(X\textrm{tr}A-AX)).

To compute the principal symbol of
\Xi
, let
f
be a smooth function on
M
. Again by using (
8
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{\Xi}(df)(X)

\displaystyle=\Xi(fX)-f\Xi X

\displaystyle=(d(fX)-fdX,\delta[f(X\textrm{tr}A-AX)]-f\delta[X\textrm{tr}A-AX])

\displaystyle=(df\wedge X,-\langle df,(X\textrm{tr}A-AX)\rangle).

Hence, the principal symbol of
\Xi
is given by

\sigma_{\Xi}(\alpha)(X)=(\alpha\wedge X,\langle\alpha,(X\textrm{tr}A-AX)\rangle).

We want to show that this principal symbol is injective in order to use the theory of elliptic operators. To do so, we first remark that by equation (
5
), the eigenvalues
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
of
A
satisfy
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Scal}^{M}
. We now use the hypothesis
\mathrm{Scal}^{M}>0
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
B\in M_{3}(\mathbb{R})
be a symmetric matrix with eigenvalues
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
such that
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}>0
. Then
B-\mathrm{tr}(B)\mathrm{Id}
is definite.

Proof
.

Since
B
is symmetric and real, there is
P\in\mathrm{O}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
such that

P^{T}BP=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}).

Thus,
P^{T}(B-\mathrm{tr}(B)\mathrm{Id})P=-\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3},\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3},\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})
. Moreover, one has

\displaystyle(\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3})(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{3}\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}^{2}>0

and similarly
(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3})(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})>0
,

(\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3})(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})>0
.
We conclude that
\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3},\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3},\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
have the same sign and are different from 0, so
B-\mathrm{tr}(B)\mathrm{Id}
is definite.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
3.4
,
A-\textrm{tr}(A)\textrm{Id}
is definite under the assumption that
\mathrm{Scal}^{M}>0
. Now, let
\alpha\in\Lambda^{1}M
such that there is a non-zero vector

with
\sigma_{\Xi}(\alpha)(X)=0
. In particular, we have
g(\alpha,[A-\mathrm{tr}(A)\mathrm{Id}]X)=0
and
\alpha\wedge X=0
. We deduce that

and
g(\alpha,[A-\mathrm{tr}(A)\mathrm{Id}]\alpha)=0
, so
\alpha=0
because the endomorphism
A-\textrm{tr}(A)\textrm{Id}
is invertible. Consequently, the principal symbol of
\Xi
is injective.

The operator
\Xi^{*}\Xi
has the same kernel as
\Xi
and is elliptic, so its kernel is finite dimensional (see e.g.
[

, Theorem 5.2]
). Thus the space of infinitesimal deformations of

is finite dimensional, ending the proof.
∎

The assumption
\mathrm{Scal}^{M}>0
is necessary, as shown by the following example:

Remark 3.5
.

We look at a flat Riemannian product

, where

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)-2\right)e_{2},

\displaystyle\nabla^{t}_{e_{3}}e_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)e_{2},

\displaystyle\nabla^{t}_{e_{2}}e_{3}

\displaystyle=e_{1},

\displaystyle\nabla^{t}_{e_{3}}e_{2}

\displaystyle=-e_{1}

\displaystyle\nabla^{t}_{e_{1}}e_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nabla^{t}_{e_{2}}e_{2}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nabla^{t}_{e_{3}}e_{3}

\displaystyle=0.

From this we immediately get

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{1},e_{2}}e_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a^{4}}{b^{4}}(t)e_{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{1},e_{2}}e_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)e_{1},

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{1},e_{2}}e_{3}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{1},e_{3}}e_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a^{4}}{b^{4}}(t)e_{3},

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{1},e_{3}}e_{2}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{1},e_{3}}e_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)e_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{2},e_{3}}e_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{2},e_{3}}e_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(3\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)-4\right)e_{3}

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{2},e_{3}}e_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(4-3\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)\right)e_{3}.

Thus, in the basis
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
one has

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{1},e_{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}&0\\
-\frac{a^{4}}{b^{4}}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{1},e_{3}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\\
0&0&0\\
-\frac{a^{4}}{b^{4}}&0&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{2},e_{3}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&4-3\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\\
0&3\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}-4&0\end{bmatrix}

so, in terms of
2
-forms, these

-dependent curvature matrices become

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}(e_{1},e_{2})=-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{4}}e_{1}\wedge e_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}(e_{1},e_{3})=-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{4}}e_{1}\wedge e_{3}

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}^{t}(e_{2},e_{3})=\frac{3a^{2}-4b^{2}}{b^{4}}e_{2}\wedge e_{3}.

Let us now analyze the Weingarten maps
A_{t}
. By
[
3
, Proposition 4.1]
,
A_{t}
is computed in the frame
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
by the formula
g_{t}(A_{t}(X),Y)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(g_{t}(X,Y)\right)
. We obtain

A_{t}=-\frac{\dot{a}}{a}(t)e_{1}\otimes e_{1}-\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)e_{2}\otimes e_{2}-\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)e_{3}\otimes e_{3}.

The twisted exterior differential of
A_{t}
is

\displaystyle(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla^{t}}A_{t})(e_{1},e_{2})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{t}_{e_{1}}(A_{t}e_{2})-A_{t}\nabla^{t}_{e_{1}}e_{2}-\nabla^{t}_{e_{2}}(A_{t}e_{1})+A_{t}\nabla^{t}_{e_{2}}e_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)\nabla^{t}_{e_{1}}e_{2}-\left(2-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)\right)A_{t}e_{3}+\frac{\dot{a}}{a}(t)\nabla^{t}_{e_{2}}e_{1}-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)A_{t}e_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)\left(\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)-\frac{\dot{a}}{a}(t)\right)e_{3}

\displaystyle(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla^{t}}A_{t})(e_{1},e_{3})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{t}_{e_{1}}(A_{t}e_{3})-A_{t}\nabla^{t}_{e_{1}}e_{3}-\nabla^{t}_{e_{3}}(A_{t}e_{1})+A_{t}\nabla^{t}_{e_{3}}e_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)\nabla^{t}_{e_{1}}e_{3}-\left(\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)-2\right)A_{t}e_{2}+\frac{\dot{a}}{a}(t)\nabla^{t}_{e_{3}}e_{1}+\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)A_{t}e_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(t)\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}(t)-\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)\right)e_{2}

\displaystyle(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla^{t}}A_{t})(e_{2},e_{3})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{t}_{e_{2}}(A_{t}e_{3})-A_{t}\nabla^{t}_{e_{2}}e_{3}-\nabla^{t}_{e_{3}}(A_{t}e_{2})+A_{t}\nabla^{t}_{e_{3}}e_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)\nabla^{t}_{e_{2}}e_{3}-A_{t}e_{1}+\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)\nabla^{t}_{e_{3}}e_{2}-A_{t}e_{1}

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}(t)-\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)\right)e_{1}.

Thus

\displaystyle*(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla^{t}}A_{t})(e_{1},e_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{b^{2}}(t)\left(\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)-\frac{\dot{a}}{a}(t)\right)\,e_{1}\wedge e_{2},

\displaystyle*(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla^{t}}A_{t})(e_{1},e_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{b^{2}}(t)\left(\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)-\frac{\dot{a}}{a}(t)\right)\,e_{1}\wedge e_{3},

\displaystyle*(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla^{t}}A_{t})(e_{2},e_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{2a}{b^{2}}(t)\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}(t)-\frac{\dot{b}}{b}(t)\right)\,e_{2}\wedge e_{3}.

Finally, Equation (
18
) rewrites as the system

\left\{\begin{aligned} 0&=\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{1},e_{2}}+*(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla^{t}}A_{t})(e_{1},e_{2})+A_{t}(e_{1})\wedge A_{t}(e_{2})\\
0&=\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{1},e_{3}}+*(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla^{t}}A_{t})(e_{1},e_{3})+A_{t}(e_{1})\wedge A_{t}(e_{3})\\
0&=\mathrm{R}^{t}_{e_{2},e_{3}}+*(\mathrm{d}^{\nabla^{t}}A_{t})(e_{2},e_{3})+A_{t}(e_{2})\wedge A_{t}(e_{3})\end{aligned}\right.

and taking into account the previous computations, this system reads

\left\{\begin{aligned} 0&=-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{4}}e_{1}\wedge e_{2}+\frac{a}{b^{2}}\left(\frac{\dot{b}}{b}-\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)e_{1}\wedge e_{2}+\frac{\dot{a}\dot{b}}{ab}e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\\
0&=-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{4}}e_{1}\wedge e_{3}+\frac{a}{b^{2}}\left(\frac{\dot{b}}{b}-\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)e_{1}\wedge e_{3}+\frac{\dot{a}\dot{b}}{ab}e_{1}\wedge e_{3}\\
0&=\frac{3a^{2}-4b^{2}}{b^{4}}e_{2}\wedge e_{3}+\frac{2a}{b^{2}}\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}-\frac{\dot{b}}{b}\right)e_{2}\wedge e_{3}+\left(\frac{\dot{b}}{b}\right)^{2}e_{2}\wedge e_{3}\end{aligned}\right.

so we are left with the two independent equations

\left\{\begin{aligned} 0&=-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{4}}-\frac{\dot{a}}{b^{2}}+\frac{a\dot{b}}{b^{3}}+\frac{\dot{a}\dot{b}}{ab}\\
0&=\frac{3a^{2}-4b^{2}}{b^{4}}+\frac{2\dot{a}}{b^{2}}-\frac{2a\dot{b}}{b^{3}}+\left(\frac{\dot{b}}{b}\right)^{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

(19)

Moreover, from the identity
g_{0}(A_{0}(X),Y)=-\frac{1}{2}\dot{g}_{0}(X,Y)
for all
X,Y\in TM_{0}
, we have the initial conditions

\displaystyle a(0)=b(0)=1

\displaystyle\dot{a}(0)=-1

\displaystyle\dot{b}(0)=3.

The first equation of (
19
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{ab}\left(\dot{b}-\frac{a}{b}\right)\left(\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}+\dot{a}\right),

thus either
\dot{b}-\frac{a}{b}=0
or
\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}+\dot{a}=0
. However, from the initial conditions one has
\dot{b}(0)-\frac{a}{b}(0)=2\neq 0
so the first case never occurs. Consequently, we get

\dot{a}=-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}.

Substituting this
\dot{a}
in the second equation of the system (
19
), one obtains by factorization

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b^{4}}(a-b\dot{b}-2b)(a-b\dot{b}+2b).

Thus, either
a-b\dot{b}-2b=0
or
a-b\dot{b}+2b=0
. The initial conditions give
a(0)-b\dot{b}(0)=1-3=-2

and we conclude that the second case occurs. We have reduced (
19
) to the simpler system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{a}=-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}\\
&\dot{b}=\frac{a}{b}+2\\
&a(0)=b(0)=1,\,\dot{a}(0)=-1,\,\dot{b}(0)=3.\end{aligned}\right.

In order to solve this system, we will find a conserved quantity and make a well-chosen change of variable. We begin by computing the derivative of
\frac{b}{a}
:

\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{\dot{b}a-\dot{a}b}{a^{2}}=\frac{\left(\frac{a}{b}+2\right)a+\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}b}{a^{2}}=\frac{2}{b}+\frac{2}{a},

and then,

0=\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{\prime}-2\left(\frac{1}{b}+\frac{1}{a}\right)\Leftrightarrow 0=ab\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{\prime}-2(a+b).

In addition, one has

(ab)^{\prime}=\dot{a}b+\dot{b}a=-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}b+a\left(\frac{a}{b}+2\right)=2a,

thus

2(a+b)=2a+2\frac{b}{a}a=2a\left(\frac{b}{a}+1\right)=(ab)^{\prime}\left(\frac{b}{a}+1\right),

and finally we have

0=ab\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{\prime}-(ab)^{\prime}\left(\frac{b}{a}+1\right)\Leftrightarrow\left(\frac{1}{ab}\left(\frac{b}{a}+1\right)\right)^{\prime}=0.

We conclude that the quantity
\frac{1}{ab}\left(\frac{b}{a}+1\right)
is constant, so

\frac{1}{ab}\left(\frac{b}{a}+1\right)=2.

A natural change of variable is to set
s=\phi(t):=a(t)b(t)
, so
\frac{b(t)}{a(t)}=2\phi(t)-1=2s-1
. Composing by
\phi^{-1}
on the right, we obtain for
s
in a neighborhood of
1

\displaystyle\dot{a}(\phi^{-1}(s))=-\frac{a^{2}}{b^{2}}(\phi^{-1}(s))

\displaystyle\dot{b}(\phi^{-1}(s))=\frac{a}{b}(\phi^{-1}(s))+2

\displaystyle a(\phi^{-1}(s))b(\phi^{-1}(s))=\phi(\phi^{-1}(s))

\displaystyle\frac{b}{a}(\phi^{-1}(s))=2\phi(\phi^{-1}(s))-1

and setting
\alpha:=a\circ\phi^{-1}
and
\beta:=b\circ\phi^{-1}
one arrives at

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}=-(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}}=-(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{1}{(2s-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}=(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}+2\right)=(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{4s-1}{2s-1}

\displaystyle\alpha\beta=s

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{\alpha}=2s-1.

Differentiating the last two equations one gets

\dot{\alpha}\beta+\alpha\dot{\beta}=1

(20)

and

\displaystyle\alpha\dot{\beta}-\dot{\alpha}\beta=2\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow 2\alpha=\dot{\beta}-\dot{\alpha}(2s-1)=(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{4s-1}{2s-1}+(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{1}{2s-1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\alpha=(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{2s}{2s-1}.

Re-injecting this last equation in (
20
) we have

\displaystyle\alpha\left(\dot{\alpha}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+\dot{\beta}\right)=1\Leftrightarrow(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{2s}{2s-1}\left(-(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{1}{(2s-1)^{2}}(2s-1)+(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{4s-1}{2s-1}\right)=1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left((\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\right)^{2}\frac{4s}{2s-1}=1\Leftrightarrow(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{2s-1}{4s}},

where we used that the derivative of
\phi
is positive.
Thus, one has

\displaystyle\dot{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{1}{(2s-1)^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{s}(2s-1)^{\frac{3}{2}}}

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}

\displaystyle=(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}\frac{4s-1}{2s-1}=\frac{4s-1}{2\sqrt{2s^{2}-s}}

and by integration we finally obtain

\displaystyle\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{s}{2s-1}},

\displaystyle\beta=\sqrt{s(2s-1)}.

We conclude that in terms of the new variable
s
, the metric on
S^{3}
introduced in (
17
) is

\displaystyle g_{s}=\frac{2s-1}{4s}\mathrm{d}s^{2}+\frac{s}{2s-1}\eta_{1}^{2}+s(2s-1)(\eta_{2}^{2}+\eta_{3}^{2}),

\displaystyle s\in(\tfrac{1}{2},\infty).

We can compute the interval to which the variable
t
belongs by calculating

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}(s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}\sqrt{\frac{2s-1}{4s}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[s\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{2s}}-\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(4s-1+\sqrt{16s^{2}-8s}\right)\right]_{\frac{1}{2}}^{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\ln(3+2\sqrt{2})=\frac{\sqrt{2}-\ln(1+\sqrt{2})}{2\sqrt{2}}\approx 0,1884,

and so the metric (
17
) admitting a parallel spinor exists for
t\in\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ln(1+\sqrt{2})-1\right),\infty\right)
.

5.2.
Link with the family of Euclidean Taub-NUT metrics

Let us first extend the previous study to the case where the initial sphere has radius
r>0
. It is easy to see that this does not change the form of the endomorphism
A_{0}
defined in (
14
). This modification results in the rescaling of the metric by a factor
r^{2}
. Subsequently, if we keep the same notations as in the case
r=1
, the metric
g
on
\mathcal{Z}
is given by

\displaystyle g=\frac{2s-1}{4s}r^{2}\mathrm{d}s^{2}+\frac{r^{2}s}{2s-1}\eta_{1}^{2}+r^{2}s(2s-1)(\eta_{2}^{2}+\eta_{3}^{2}),

\displaystyle s\in(\tfrac{1}{2},\infty).

(21)

With the change of variable
u:=rs
we can express this metric by

\displaystyle g=\frac{2u-r}{4u}\mathrm{d}u^{2}+\frac{r^{2}u}{2u-r}\eta_{1}^{2}+u(2u-r)(\eta_{2}^{2}+\eta_{3}^{2}),

\displaystyle u\in(\tfrac{r}{2},\infty).

This family of metrics is strikingly similar to the well-known family of Euclidean Taub-NUT metrics on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
(see e.g.
[
12
]
and the references therein).
In polar coordinates, the Euclidean Taub-NUT metrics are given (up to a constant) by the expression

g_{TN}=\frac{as+b}{s}\left(\mathrm{d}s^{2}+\frac{4b^{2}s^{2}}{(as+b)^{2}}\eta_{1}^{2}+4s^{2}(\eta_{2}^{2}+\eta_{3}^{2})\right)

(22)

where
a
and
b
are
positive
parameters and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3}
are the
1
-forms dual to the Hopf vector fields
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
.
Through a change of variable in the radial variable
s
, we can always normalize the parameter
a
to be equal to
2
.

The metric we found in Equation (
21
) belongs formally to the extension for
negative
values of the parameter
b=-r<0
of the family of Taub-NUT metrics normalized with
a=2
. Note that the parameter
r
cannot vanish, else the metric degenerates. The metrics in this family admit a nonzero parallel spinor. This implies that they are hyperkähler, hence Ricci-flat, and also (anti-) self-dual, according to the chirality of the nonzero parallel spinor.

In the presentation (
22
) of the Taub-NUT metric,
s=0
is an apparent singularity, but in fact the metric extends smoothly in the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. In contrast, the metric (
21
) has a true singularity at
s=\frac{1}{2}
. The horizontal directions
e_{2}
and
e_{3}
collapse, while the vertical direction of
e_{1}

explodes
in finite time as
s\searrow\frac{1}{2}
. As a result, the curvature operator is unbounded near
s=\frac{1}{2}
.

6.
Classification results on
\mathbb{S}^{3}

In this section we analyze the set of symmetric solutions of (
2
) in the case
M=\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Recall that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}=\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{loc}}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
because the sphere is simply-connected. Since the known examples of solutions can be expressed as constant matrices in a frame of left (or right-) invariant vector fields, we will investigate some classes of endomorphisms related to these vector fields.

6.1.
Endomorphisms constant in a left or right invariant orthonormal frame

The four examples we recalled in Remark
4.1
can be interpreted as constant matrices either in a left- or a right-invariant orthonormal frame. For this reason, it is legitimate to search for all symmetric endomorphisms in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
that verify this property. We shall prove that there exist no other examples besides the ones already known from Remark
4.1
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
. Assume that
A
is constant in a left (resp. right)-invariant orthonormal frame. Then, either
A=\pm\mathrm{Id}
or
A
has eigenvalues
1
,
-3
,
-3
(resp.
-1
,
3
,
3
). In particular,
A
is one of the endomorphism fields described in Remark
4.1
.

Proof
.

We recall that
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
are the three left-invariant vector fields corresponding to the quaternions
i,j,k
on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
(i.e.,
A
is symmetric and satisfies Equation (
13
) on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
), and assume that
A
is constant in a left-invariant orthonormal frame. Hence,
A
can be viewed as a real symmetric
3\times 3
matrix, in particular it is diagonalizable. From these considerations, up to an isometry of the sphere we can assume without loss of generality that
A=ae_{1}\otimes e_{1}+be_{2}\otimes e_{2}+ce_{3}\otimes e_{3}
for
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Equation (
13
) applied to
X,Y\in\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
together with (
4
) and (
12
) give the system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &a+b-2c=1-ab\\
&b+c-2a=1-bc\\
&c+a-2b=1-ca\end{aligned}\right.\Leftrightarrow\left\{\begin{aligned} &(a+1)(b+1)=2(c+1)\\
&(b+1)(c+1)=2(a+1)\\
&(a+1)(c+1)=2(b+1).\end{aligned}\right.

We easily see that
a+1=0\Leftrightarrow b+1=0\Leftrightarrow c+1=0
, and in this case
A=-\mathrm{Id}
.
We assume now that
a+1\neq 0
. The product of all the equations give

(a+1)(b+1)(c+1)=8,

and we conclude that
(a+1)^{2}=(b+1)^{2}=(c+1)^{2}=4
. Therefore
a+1,b+1,c+1\in\{-2,2\}
and moreover an even number among them are negative, concluding the proof when
A
is constant in a left-invariant frame.

The case of a right-invariant orthonormal frame is treated similarly and produces the additional solution
-1
,
3
,
3
.
∎

6.2.
Endomorphism fields with three distinct constant eigenvalues

The case of an endomorphism solution of (
13
) with at most two distinct eigenvalues was already studied in
[
15
]
, where it was shown that the only possibilities are the ones given in Remark
4.1
.

Proposition 6.2
.

There is no element of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
with three distinct constant eigenvalues.

Proof
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
with three constant eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{3}
. The associated unitary eigenvectors are global vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, which form an orthonormal frame, and are denoted by
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
. Equation (
13
) means that for every cyclic permutation
(a,b,c)
of the index set
(1,2,3)
one has

\lambda_{b}\nabla_{X_{a}}X_{b}-\lambda_{a}\nabla_{X_{b}}X_{a}-A[X_{a},X_{b}]=(1-\lambda_{a}\lambda_{b})X_{c}.

Projecting on
X_{a}
, we see that

\displaystyle\lambda_{b}g(\nabla_{X_{a}}X_{b},X_{a})-\lambda_{a}g(\nabla_{X_{b}}X_{a},X_{a})-\lambda_{a}g([X_{a},X_{b}],X_{a})=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle(\lambda_{b}-\lambda_{a})g(\nabla_{X_{a}}X_{b},X_{a})=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle(\lambda_{b}-\lambda_{a})g([X_{a},X_{b}],X_{a})=0.

This last equation is true for any
a,b\in\{1,2,3\}
, and this means
[X_{a},X_{b}]\in\mathrm{Span}(X_{c})
.

As a direct consequence of Koszul formula,
\nabla_{X_{a}}X_{a}=0
for
a\in\{1,2,3\}
. We can compute for any
a

\delta(X_{a})=-X_{k}\lrcorner\nabla_{X_{k}}X_{a}=g(X_{a},\nabla_{X_{k}}X_{k})=0.

This shows that the vector fields
X_{k}
are geodesic and divergence free. By a result of Gluck and Gu
[
9
, Theorem A]
, every geodesic and divergence free vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
is a Hopf vector field (i.e. a unit vector field tangent to the fiber of a Hopf fibration). Moreover, since they form an orthonormal basis at any point, they are all either left or right-invariant. However we can give a simpler argument in our case:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})
be a global orthonormal frame of geodesic vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
(i.e.
\nabla_{X_{k}}X_{k}=0
). Then
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})
is
either a left- or a right-invariant frame.

Proof
.

Using Koszul’s formula, one sees that the assumption
\nabla_{X_{k}}X_{k}=0
is actually equivalent to the existence of three real functions
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
such that
[X_{a},X_{b}]=\alpha_{c}X_{c}
for any cyclic permutation
(a,b,c)
of the indices
1,2,3
.

We define the real-valued functions
\beta_{k}=(-1)^{\delta_{1}^{k}}\alpha_{1}+(-1)^{\delta_{2}^{k}}\alpha_{2}+(-1)^{\delta_{3}^{k}}\alpha_{3}
. By the Koszul formula,

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}X_{b}=\beta_{a}X_{c},

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{a}}X_{c}=-\beta_{a}X_{b},

Moreover, the curvature tensor on the sphere satisfies
\mathrm{R}(X_{a},X_{b})X_{b}=X_{a}
. Since the vector fields
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
are geodesic, we also have

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}(X_{a},X_{b})X_{b}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{X_{b}}\nabla_{X_{a}}X_{b}-\nabla_{[X_{a},X_{b}]}X_{b}

\displaystyle=-X_{b}(\beta_{a})X_{b}-\beta_{a}\beta_{b}X_{a}+2\alpha_{c}\beta_{c}X_{a}

\displaystyle=-X_{b}(\beta_{a})X_{b}+(-\beta_{a}\beta_{b}+\beta_{a}\beta_{c}+\beta_{b}\beta_{c})X_{a},

so the projection of this equation on
X_{a}
yields

-\beta_{a}\beta_{b}+\beta_{a}\beta_{c}+\beta_{b}\beta_{c}=1.

Since this is true for any value of
(a,b,c)
in
\{(1,2,3),(2,3,1),(3,1,2)\}
, one has
\beta_{a}\beta_{b}=1
and we conclude that
\beta_{k}=\pm 1
for any
k\in\{1,2,3\}
.

Assume first that
\beta_{k}=1
for any
k\in\{1,2,3\}
. We define for any
X,Y\in T\mathbb{S}^{3}
the covariant derivative

\overline{\nabla}_{X}Y:=\nabla_{X}Y-*(X\wedge Y),

which was already considered in (
6
). The vector fields
X_{k}
are parallel for
\overline{\nabla}
and so are the left-invariant vector fields with value
(X_{k})_{e}
at
e
. Thus, these vector fields coincide.

In the case
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\beta_{3}=-1
, the same proof shows that
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
are right-invariant.
∎

Hence,
A
is constant in a left or right-orthonormal frame and according to Proposition (
6.1
) it must have at most 2 different eigenvalues, which contradicts the hypothesis.
∎

6.3.
Endomorphisms constant in the direction of a left-invariant vector field

We will now weaken the condition from Section
6.1
, and search for solutions
A
of (
13
) on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
that are constant in the direction of a left-invariant vector field
\xi
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}A=0
. Assuming this invariance, all the objects can be expressed on the basis of the Hopf fibration with fibers tangent to
\xi
.
We decompose
A
under the form:

A=f\xi\otimes\xi+v\otimes\xi+\xi\otimes v+B

(23)

where
f
is a function on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
,
v\in\xi^{\perp}
and
B
is the restriction of
A
to
\xi^{\perp}
. The condition
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}A=0
gives

\displaystyle 0=\mathcal{L}_{\xi}A=(\xi f)\xi\otimes\xi+\mathcal{L}_{\xi}v\otimes\xi+\xi\otimes\mathcal{L}_{\xi}v+\mathcal{L}_{\xi}B,

and we know that for all
X\in\xi^{\perp}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}X\in\xi^{\perp}
since
\xi
is a Killing field, so we deduce

\displaystyle\xi f=0,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi}v=0,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\xi}B=0.

(24)

As a direct consequence of equations (
24
) we can interpret
f
,
v
and
B
respectively as a function, a vector and an endomorphism on the basis
\mathbb{S}^{2}(\frac{1}{2})
of the Hopf fibration.

We define the endomorphism
J
of
\xi^{\perp}
by
JX:=-\nabla_{X}\xi
. This endomorphism is skew-symmetric and satisfies
J^{2}=-1
; it is actually the lift of the standard almost complex structure from
\mathbb{S}^{2}(\frac{1}{2})
through the Hopf fibration, so we will see it as an endomorphism of the base.

The invariance equations (
24
) give

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi}v

\displaystyle=\nabla_{v}\xi=-Jv

\displaystyle(\nabla_{\xi}B)X

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\xi}(BX)-B\nabla_{\xi}X=\nabla_{BX}\xi+[\xi,BX]+BJX-B[\xi,X]

\displaystyle=[B,J]X+\mathcal{L}_{\xi}(BX)-B\mathcal{L}_{\xi}X=[B,J]X+(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}B)X=[B,J]X.

Now, we express Equation (
13
) in terms of
f
,
v
and
B
by considering horizontal and vertical vectors for
X
and
Y
.

Let
X,Y
be two orthogonal vector fields in
\xi^{\perp}
. One has,

\displaystyle d^{\nabla}A(\xi,X)

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{\xi}A)X-(\nabla_{X}A)\xi

\displaystyle=-(Jv\otimes\xi+\xi\otimes Jv-[B,J])X-\nabla_{X}(A\xi)-AJX

\displaystyle=-g(Jv,X)\xi-JBX+BJX-\nabla_{X}(f\xi+v)-g(v,JX)\xi-BJX

\displaystyle=-JBX-\nabla_{X}(f\xi+v).

We now use the fact that for any
X
,
g(JX,X)=0
to infer that
*\xi\wedge X=JX
and
*v\wedge X=-g(v,JX)\xi
, so Equation (
13
) implies

\displaystyle-JBX-\nabla_{X}(f\xi+v)

\displaystyle=*\xi\wedge X-*(f\xi+v)\wedge(g(X,v)\xi+BX)

\displaystyle=JX-fJBX+g(v,JBX)\xi+g(X,v)Jv.

Projecting this equation on
\xi
, one has

-g(\nabla_{X}(f\xi+v),\xi)=g(v,JBX)

thus

g(BJv+Jv-df,X)=0.

Since this last equation is true for any
X\in\xi^{\perp}
we conclude

BJv=-Jv+df.

(25)

We define the orthogonal projector
P
on
\xi^{\perp}
. We now project Equation (
25
) on the orthogonal of
\xi
, and we obtain

-JBX+fJX-P\nabla_{X}v=JX-fJBX+g(X,v)Jv

thus

(f-1)(JBX+JX)=P\nabla_{X}v+g(X,v)Jv.

Thus, we have the system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &BJv=-Jv+df\\
&(f-1)(JBX+JX)=P\nabla_{X}v+g(X,v)Jv\end{aligned}\right.

We now compute:

\displaystyle(\nabla_{X}A)Y=

\displaystyle(X(f)\xi\otimes\xi-fJX\otimes\xi-f\xi\otimes JX+\nabla_{X}v\otimes\xi

\displaystyle+\xi\otimes\nabla_{X}v-JX\otimes v-v\otimes JX+(\nabla_{X}B))Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-fg(JX,Y)\xi+g(\nabla_{X}v,Y)\xi-g(JX,Y)v

\displaystyle-g(v,Y)JX+(\nabla_{X}B)Y.

We deduce that

\displaystyle d^{\nabla}A(X,Y)=

\displaystyle-fg(JX,Y)\xi+g(\nabla_{X}v,Y)\xi-g(JX,Y)v-g(v,Y)JX+(\nabla_{X}B)Y

\displaystyle+fg(JY,X)\xi-g(\nabla_{Y}v,X)\xi+g(JY,X)v+g(v,X)JY-(\nabla_{Y}B)X

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2fg(X,JY)\xi+dv(X,Y)\xi+2g(X,JY)v

\displaystyle-g(v,Y)JX+g(v,X)JY+d^{\nabla}B(X,Y).

Equation (
13
) leads to

\displaystyle 2fg(X,JY)\xi+dv(X,Y)\xi+2g(X,JY)v-g(v,Y)JX+g(v,X)JY+d^{\nabla}B(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle*X\wedge Y-*(g(X,v)\xi+BX)\wedge(g(Y,v)\xi+BY)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-[g(X,JY)-g(BX,JBY)]\xi-g(X,v)JBY+g(Y,v)JBX

which leads to

\displaystyle[-g(X,JY)+g(BX,JBY)-2fg(X,JY)-dv(X,Y)]\xi-2g(X,JY)v

\displaystyle=d^{\nabla}B(X,Y)+g(X,v)J(B+1)Y-g(Y,v)J(B+1)X.

Since

\displaystyle g(d^{\nabla}B(X,Y),\xi)

\displaystyle=g(\nabla_{X}(BY)-\nabla_{Y}(BX)-B[X,Y],\xi)

\displaystyle=g(\nabla_{X}(BY),\xi)-g(\nabla_{Y}(BX),\xi)

\displaystyle=-g(BX,JY)+g(BY,JX),

the projection on
\xi
gives

-g(X,JY)+g(BX,JBY)-2fg(X,JY)-dv(X,Y)=-g(BX,JY)+g(BY,JX)

and

2(1+f)g(X,JY)+dv(X,Y)=g((B+1)X,J(B+1)Y).

We remark that for any symmetric endomorphism
S
, one has
SJS=\det(S)J
and the last equation is rewritten

[2(1+f)-\det(B+1)]g(X,JY)=-dv(X,Y).

Moreover, the projection on
\xi^{\perp}
provides the equation

\displaystyle-Pd^{\nabla}B(X,Y)=g(X,v)J(B+1)Y-g(Y,v)J(B+1)X+2g(X,JY)v.

For the remainder of this section, we will denote by
\overline{\nabla}
the covariant derivative on
\mathbb{S}^{2}(\frac{1}{2})
(the basis of the Hopf fibration). We recall that if
U,V
are basic vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
we have the equation

\nabla_{U}V=\overline{\nabla}_{U}V+g(V,JU)\xi.

Now, we study the objects on
\mathbb{S}^{2}(\frac{1}{2})
. For any
X,Y\in T\mathbb{S}^{2}(\frac{1}{2})
, one has
dv(X,Y)=d^{*}(Jv)g(JX,Y)
and, using a unit vector field
X
at a point of
\mathbb{S}^{2}(\frac{1}{2})
and the fact that
J
is parallel, one has

*d^{\overline{\nabla}}B=d^{\overline{\nabla}}B(X,JX)=(\overline{\nabla}_{X}B)JX-(\overline{\nabla}_{JX}B)X=(\overline{\nabla}_{X}BJ)X+(\overline{\nabla}_{JX}BJ)JX=-\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}(BJ).

Consequently, we obtain four equations on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}(\frac{1}{2})

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(B+1)Jv=df\\
&(f-1)J(B+1)=\overline{\nabla}v+v\otimes Jv\\
&2(1+f)-\det(B+1)=d^{*}(Jv)\\
&\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}(BJ)=J(B+3)Jv.\end{aligned}\right.

(26)

The system (
26
) seems too difficult to solve in full generality for the time being, and is left as an open problem.

6.4.
A particular case:
v=0

We proceed by treating only the special case in the system (
26
) where
\xi
is an eigenvector of
A
, i.e.
v=0
in (
23
). In this situation, the system reduces to

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(f-1)J(B+1)X=0\\
&[2(1+f)-\det(B+1)]=0\\
&\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}(BJ)=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(27)

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
be the symmetric endomorphism corresponding to a Cauchy spinor on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Assume that there exists a left (resp. right)-invariant vector field
\xi
such that
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}A=0
and such that
\xi
is an eigenvector of
A
. Then
A=\pm\mathrm{Id}
or
A=\xi\otimes\xi-3P
(resp.
A=-\xi\otimes\xi+3P
), where
P
is the orthogonal projector on
\xi^{\perp}
.

Proof
.

We start with the case where
\xi
is left-invariant. We have seen that
A
must equal
f\xi\otimes\xi+B
where the Killing vector field
\xi
is an eigenvector of
A
, and
f
and
B
are
\xi
-invariant, hence they are pull-back of objects from the base
\mathbb{S}^{2}(\frac{1}{2})
of the Hopf fibration.
Define the open set
\mathcal{O}=\{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\,B(x)\neq-\mathrm{Id}\}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
. On
\mathcal{O}
, the first equation gives
f=1
, and the second one gives
\det(B+1)=4
. By continuity, this is still true on
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
, hence
\overline{\mathcal{O}}\subset\mathcal{O}
. We conclude that
\mathcal{O}
is open and closed, and by connectedness it is either empty or equal to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

If
\mathcal{O}
is empty,
B=-\mathrm{Id}
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, so
f=-1
and the only solution of the system is
A=-\mathrm{Id}
.

If
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we have seen that
f=1
so we are left with the two equations
\det(B+1)=4
and
\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}(BJ)=0
. Equivalently, we search for a symmetric endomorphism field
C=\frac{B+1}{2}
on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with
\det C=1
and
\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}(CJ)=0
.
Notice that
\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}(JCJ)=-J\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}(CJ)=0
, hence the symmetric endomorphism
U:=JCJ
satisfies

\det U=1
and
\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}U=0
.
The following proposition contains the result we need:

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
U
be a symmetric endomorphism on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
which satisfies
\det U=1
and
\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}U=0
. Then
U=\pm\mathrm{Id}
.

Proof
.

Let
t:=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}U
, and
S:=J(U-t\mathrm{Id})
. Since
U-t\mathrm{Id}
is symmetric and traceless, so is
S
. We will use several times below that traceless symmetric endomorphisms anticommute with
J
. One has
\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}S=\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}(JU)-\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}(Jt\mathrm{Id})=Jdt
. Since
U^{2}-2tU+\det U=0
by Cayley-Hamilton’s theorem, one has

S^{2}=(JS)^{2}=(U-tId)^{2}=U^{2}-2tU+t^{2}\mathrm{Id}=(t^{2}-1)\mathrm{Id}.

(28)

Thus,
|t|\geq 1
because
S^{2}=SS^{*}
is non-negative. The function
t
is continuous on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, so it cannot change sign. It follows that either
t\geq 1
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
or
t\leq-1
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. We shall show below that in the first case
U=\mathrm{Id}
. In the second case,
-U
also satisfies the hypotheses of the proposition and moreover
\mathrm{tr}(-U)>0
, so by the first case we get
-U=\mathrm{Id}
. It suffices therefore to solve the first case, i.e., we can assume in the rest of the proof that
t\geq 1
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

We want to show that the open set
E:=\{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2};t(x)>1\}
is empty. One has
S\neq 0
on
E
, thus we can define
T:=\frac{S}{\|S\|}
. Since
S
is symmetric and traceless, so are
T
and
JT
, hence
(T,JT)
is an orthonormal frame over
E
in the bundle of symmetric traceless endomorphisms of
T\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Therefore,

\overline{\nabla}T=:\alpha\otimes JT

(29)

for some
1
-form
\alpha\in\Lambda^{1}(E)
. By taking a further covariant derivative in this equation and skew-symmetrizing one has for any
X,Y\in T\mathbb{S}^{2}

d\alpha(X,Y)JT=\bar{\mathrm{R}}_{X,Y}T

where
\bar{\mathrm{R}}
is the curvature of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, which acts on any endomorphism field
W
as
\bar{\mathrm{R}}_{X,Y}W=[\bar{\mathrm{R}}_{X,Y},W]
. Then, using the identity
\bar{\mathrm{R}}_{X,Y}=-X\wedge Y
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we obtain
d\alpha=-2vol
, where
vol
is the Riemannian volume form.

We identify
\alpha
with a vector field
\alpha^{\sharp}
via the metric, and one has
\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}T=-JT\alpha^{\sharp}
by Equation (
29
). Since
T^{2}=(JT)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Id}
we obtain
\alpha^{\sharp}=-2JT(\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}T)
. The condition
\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}S=J\mathrm{d}t
gives
\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}T=\frac{\delta^{\overline{\nabla}}S}{\|S\|}+T\mathrm{grad}\ln(\|S\|)=\frac{Jdt}{\|S\|}+T\mathrm{grad}\ln(\|S\|)
, hence

\alpha^{\sharp}=-\frac{2S(\mathrm{grad}(t))}{\|S\|^{2}}-J\mathrm{grad}\ln(\|S\|).

We now differentiate this equation to obtain

d^{*}J\alpha^{\sharp}=-d^{*}\left(\frac{2JS(\mathrm{grad}(t))}{\|S\|^{2}}\right)+\Delta\ln(\|S\|)

where
\Delta=d^{*}d
is the positive Laplacian on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Using the fact that
d\alpha=-2V
, which is equivalent to
d^{*}J\alpha^{\sharp}=-2
, one has

-2=-d^{*}\left(\frac{2JS(\mathrm{grad}(t))}{\|S\|^{2}}\right)+\Delta\ln(\|S\|).

We know from (
28
) that
\|S\|^{2}=2(t^{2}-1)
, so
t=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\|S\|^{2}+1}
. This leads to
dt=\frac{\|S\|d(\|S\|)}{2\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\|S\|^{2}+1}}
, and finally

-2=-d^{*}\left(\frac{JSd\ln(\|S\|)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\|S\|^{2}+1}}\right)+\Delta\ln(\|S\|).

We define
\zeta:=\frac{1}{2}\|S\|^{2}
and we rewrite the above equation as

-1=-d^{*}(\frac{JSd\ln\zeta}{\sqrt{\zeta+1}})+\Delta\ln\zeta.

Let
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
be a point where
t
, and thus
\|S\|
, reaches its maximum. Clearly,
x\in E
. Let
(e_{1},e_{2})
be an orthonormal frame which is parallel at
x
. At this point, one has

\displaystyle d^{*}(\frac{JSd\ln\zeta}{\sqrt{\zeta+1}})

\displaystyle=-\langle J\overline{\nabla}_{e_{1}}(\frac{Sd\ln\zeta}{\sqrt{\zeta+1}}),e_{1}\rangle-\langle J\overline{\nabla}_{e_{2}}(\frac{Sd\ln\zeta}{\sqrt{\zeta+1}}),e_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\left[\langle\overline{\nabla}_{e_{1}}d\ln\zeta,SJe_{1}\rangle+\langle\overline{\nabla}_{e_{2}}d\ln\zeta,SJe_{2}\rangle\right]}{\sqrt{\zeta+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\mathrm{Hess\,}\ln\zeta,SJ\rangle}{\sqrt{\zeta+1}},

where the Hessian is defined for any function
\beta
by
\mathrm{Hess\,}\beta:=\overline{\nabla}d\beta
.
Thus,

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle=-\frac{\langle\mathrm{Hess\,}\ln\zeta,SJ\rangle}{\sqrt{\zeta+1}}-\langle\mathrm{Hess\,}\ln\zeta,\mathrm{Id}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle\mathrm{Hess\,}\ln\zeta,\mathrm{Id}+M\rangle,

where
M:=\frac{SJ}{\sqrt{\zeta+1}}
. Since
\zeta=\frac{1}{2}\|S\|^{2}
one has

\displaystyle\|M\|=\sqrt{\frac{2\zeta}{\zeta+1}}<\sqrt{2}.

Since the trace of
M
vanishes,
\|M\|=\sqrt{2}\rho(M)
, where
\rho(M)
is the spectral radius of
M
. Thus one has
\rho(M)<1
, so
\mathrm{Id}+M
is positive definite. We now use the following elementary result:

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
N_{1},N_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be two symmetric matrices such that
N_{1}
is positive and
N_{2}
is non-positive. Then,
\langle N_{1}N_{2}x,x\rangle\leq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof of the lemma.

By working in a eigenbasis
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
of
N_{1}
, we can suppose that
N_{1}
is diagonal. Thus, for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have
N_{1}f_{j}=:\lambda_{j}f_{j}
and

\langle N_{1}N_{2}f_{j},f_{j}\rangle=\langle N_{2}f_{j},N_{1}f_{j}\rangle=\lambda_{j}\langle N_{2}f_{j},f_{j}\rangle\leq 0.\qed

The matrix
\mathrm{Hess\,}\ln(\|S\|)
is non-positive because we are at a maximum point, and
\mathrm{Id}+M
is positive definite, so the previous lemma yields:

-1=-\langle\mathrm{Hess\,}\ln\zeta,\mathrm{Id}+M\rangle\geq 0

which is absurd. Thus
E=\emptyset
, so
t=1
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and hence
S=0
by (
28
). Therefore, in the case
t>0
we finally get
U=\mathrm{Id}
.

The solution
U=-\mathrm{Id}
is obtained in the case
t<0
, as explained in the beginning of the proof.
∎

Recall that in the case
B\neq-\mathrm{Id}
we defined
U=C^{-1}
where
C=\frac{B+\mathrm{Id}}{2}
.
As a consequence of Proposition
6.5
, we get the additional solutions
B=-3\mathrm{Id}
or
B=\mathrm{Id}
. We obtain therefore three solutions to equations (
27
), which lead to the endomorphism fields
A=\pm\mathrm{Id}
and
A=\xi\otimes\xi-3P
, where we recall that
P
is the orthogonal projector on
\xi^{\perp}
.

The previous analysis adapts as usual in the case where
\xi
is right-invariant, yielding the fourth solution
A=-\xi\otimes\xi+3P
.
∎

6.5.
Link with the sphere rigidity theorem

A classical result due to Liebmann
[
11
]
states that the only isometric immersions of the round sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the totally umbilical embeddings (hence they differ from the standard embedding by an isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
). Let us recall a property of Codazzi tensors in dimension
2
:

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\Sigma
be a surface endowed with a Riemannian metric
h
and
S
a field of endomorphisms on
\Sigma
. Then
S
is a Codazzi tensor (i.e.,
d^{\nabla}S=0
) if and only if
JSJ
is divergence-free, where
J
is the Hodge star on
1
-forms.

Proof
.

Let
X
be a locally-defined unit vector field on
\Sigma
. Using
\nabla J=0
we compute

\displaystyle-\delta^{\nabla}(JSJ)=

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}(JSJ)(X)+\nabla_{JX}(JSJ)(JX)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J\nabla_{X}(S)(JX)+J\nabla_{JX}(S)(JJX)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Jd^{\nabla}(S)(X,JX).\qed

Proposition
6.5
implies the following slight extension of the sphere rigidity theorem:

Proposition 6.8
.

Every isometric immersion of the round
2
-sphere in a flat
3
-manifold is totally umbilical.

Indeed, the Gauss and Codazzi equations of the embedding
\mathbb{S}^{2}\hookrightarrow M
with second fundamental form
S
tell us that
\det(S)=1
and
d^{\nabla}S=0
. By the above lemma, this is equivalent to
\det(JSJ)=1
and
\delta^{\nabla}(JSJ)=0
, so by Proposition
6.5
we deduce that

JSJ=\pm\mathrm{Id}
, which means that
S
itself is
\pm\mathrm{Id}
.

We refer to
[
1
]
for a modern proof of Liebmann’s theorem using the curvature of the metric defined by the second fundamental form
S
.

The interplay between solving the system (
27
) and a nontrivial classical result might explain why the more general system (
26
) is not so easy to solve.
