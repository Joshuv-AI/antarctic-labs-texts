Transformers are Expressive, But Are They Expressive Enough for Regression?
Swaroop Nath, Harshad Khadilkar, Pushpak Bhattacharyya

Abstract
Transformers have become pivotal in Natural Language Processing, demonstrating remarkable success in applications like Machine Translation and Summarization. Given their widespread adoption, several works have attempted to analyze the expressivity of Transformers. Expressivity of a neural network is the class of functions it can approximate. A neural network is fully expressive if it can act as a universal function approximator. We attempt to analyze the same for Transformers. Contrary to existing claims, our findings reveal that Transformers struggle to reliably approximate smooth functions, relying on piecewise constant approximations with sizable intervals. The central question emerges as: “ Are Transformers truly Universal Function Approximators ?” To address this, we conduct a thorough investigation, providing theoretical insights and supporting evidence through experiments. Theoretically, we prove that Transformer Encoders cannot approximate smooth functions. Experimentally, we complement our theory and show that the full Transformer architecture cannot approximate smooth functions. By shedding light on these challenges, we advocate a refined understanding of Transformers’ capabilities. Code Link: github.com/transformer-expressivity .

Necessary Definitions

In this section, we provide definitions necessary for the rest of the paper.

Definition 3.1

(

Let

A large

is constant is large, Figure

has a high rate of change (derivative).

Definition 3.2

(
Adequacy of Approximation
)
.

Let
g
be an approximation to a function

. We say that
g

adequately approximates

f
if the following holds, for some
\epsilon>0
and

d_{p}(f,g)=\bigg(\int\big\|g(x)-f(x)\big\|_{p}^{p}dx\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\epsilon

d_{p}(f,g)
defines a distance between
f
and
g
for different values of
x
, normalized by the
p
. Prior works
(
Yun et al. 2020a
;
Zaheer et al. 2020
)
have used this definition for adequacy of approximation.

Definition 3.3

(
Function with Compact Support
)
.

A function,
f
, is said to have a
compact support
iff it is non-zero for an input from a
compact set
. A set is compact iff it is
closed
and
bounded
.

3.3
Transformers as Universal Function Approximators

In this section, we outline the
3
-step process followed by
Yun et al. 2020a
. The work attempts to prove that Transformers,
\mathcal{T}^{h,d,r}
, approximate continuous permutation equivariant functions with compact support. We denote the set of such functions by

Step 1
.
Approximate

(

Step 2
.
Approximate
\mathcal{\overline{F}}_{PE}
with
modified

.
Yun et al. 2020a
show that
\mathcal{\overline{F}}_{PE}
can be approximated by a Transformer with some simplifying modifications, such as replacing the softmax operator with the hardmax operator.

Step 3
.
Approximate modified Transformers with (original) Transformers
. In this step,
Yun et al. 2020a
show that the original Transformers can approximate the modified Transformers.

Using this
3
-step process,
Yun et al. 2020a
;
Yun et al. 2020b
;
Zaheer et al. 2020
have analyzed the expressivity of Transformers. Additionally,
Yun et al. 2020a
also note that with a resolution factor
\delta
, in Step 1, the number of layers in the Transformer, needed to approximate a piecewise constant function, grows as:
\mathcal{O}(m(1/\delta)^{dm})
, where
m
is the input sequence length.

Expressivity of Transformers in the Continuous Space

Yun et al. 2020a

Transformers (

)

b

\mathcal{T}^{h,d,r}
and
\overline{f}

Does a change in

4

By similar effect we mean that adequacy of both approximations change in the same direction (worse or better). And, by dissimilar effect we mean that adequacy of both approximations change in the opposite directions.
We answer this in steps, by answering the following questions:

1.

What governs the choice of the resolution factor,

We provide an intuition (Section

2.

How does a well-chosen resolution factor affect the conclusion of

.

(

We use the Transformer Architecture proposed by

We perform experiments on sythetic datasets, to ensure that data quality does not affect our conclusions. Specifically, we perform two experiments:

Expt-I

, it is computed as the fraction of times the ground truth vector is not in the

-nearest-neighbor set of the generated vector.

The design of the metric is guided by our initial criteria of judging an approximation (Definition
3.2
). Mathematically,

is linked to the Adequacy of Approximation as follows:

\displaystyle\text{\tt failure-rate}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\Big(\sum_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T}^{h,d,r},\>x)\Big)

Where
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T}^{h,d,r},x)=\mathbbm{1}\big(\exists\>y\in\mathcal{D}:d_{1}(\mathcal{T}^{h,d,r}(x),f(y))<d_{1}(\mathcal{T}^{h,d,r}(x),f(x))\big)
.
\mathbbm{1}(z)
is
1
if and only if
z
evaluates to
true
, and
\mathcal{D}
is the test set.

6
Summary, Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we analyze the function approximation capabilities of the Transformer. We provide a theoretical treatment, analyzing the capability, in Section
4
. We prove that Transformer Encoders cannot practically approximate smooth functions. Following that, we provide experimental results on several settings to evaluate the capability of the full Transformer, in Section
5
. Experimentally, we complement our theoretical findings, and show that the full Transformer also cannot practically approximate smooth functions. Based on our theoretical and experimental findings, we thus conclude that:
Transformers are bad at approximating smooth functions
. However,
they are quite adept at approximating piecewise constant functions with moderately large-sized pieces
. Given this finding, our future work would attempt to: (
a
) Pinpoint the source of this limited expressivity, and (
b
) Modify the Transformer architecture to attain Universal Function Approximation. Additionally, for interested readers, we provide a few more future works along with limitations of our work in Appendix
A
.

Appendix A
Limitations

In this work, we extensively probe the function approximation capabilities of Transformers. First we theoretically show that Transformer Encoders are bad at approximating smooth functions. Then we experimentally show that the full Transformer architecture is also bad at approximating smooth functions, complementing our theory. However, our work has the following limitations:

1.

We do not theoretically analyse the function approximation capabilities of the full Transformer architecture. However, we believe that the presented analysis (theory
+
experiments) sufficiently supports our hypothesis.

2.

We highlight the limited expressivity of Transformers for smooth functions. However, we do not dive deeper into highlighting the varying extent of difficulty faced by the Transformer for different functions. For instance, a Transformer might be better at approximating
logarithmic functions
than
exponential functions
. We leave this as a future work.

3.

We do not provide any modification to the Transformer architecture to improve its expressivity. We leave this as a future work.

Appendix B
Assumptions of our Proofs

Our proofs, presented in Section
4.1
and Appendix
C
, are based on the following assumptions:

1.

Assumption

\mathbf{\mathit{1}}
:
The function to be approximated is smooth, which has continuous first-order derivatives over the entire compact support (
\mathcal{S}
) of the function
.

Effect
: This assumption limits the exploration space in deciding which functions cannot be reliably approximated by Transformers to smooth functions. We admit that the boundary may be broader, that is there can be non-smooth continuous functions, which are difficult for Transformers to approximate.

2.

Assumption

\mathbf{\mathit{2}}
:
The higher order terms in the Taylor series approximation (
\mu
) are negligible.

Validity
: This assumption is quite valid for smooth functions when the approximation region is small (
X
is near the approximation point
X^{0}
). In our case,
X
is indeed near
X^{0}
(owing to small
\delta
).

,
n

We have:

. Qualitatively, this means that
\overline{f}
is a good approximation of
f

\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{S}}\big\|\overline{f}(X)-f(X)\big\|_{p}^{p}dx\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\epsilon

Note that for

\overline{f}(X)=f(X)=0

\displaystyle\big\|\overline{f}(X)-f(X)\big\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big|\overline{f}(X)_{i}^{j}-f(X)_{i}^{j}\big|^{p}\bigg\}^{p\times{\frac{1}{p}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big|\overline{f}(X)_{i}^{j}-f(X)_{i}^{j}\big|^{p}

So, we have,

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{S}}\big\|\overline{f}(X)-f(X)\big\|_{p}^{p}dx\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big|\overline{f}(X)_{i}^{j}-f(X)_{i}^{j}\big|^{p}dX\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon

We look at the
\delta/2
neighborhood of
X^{0}
. Note that the neighborhood is a hypercube. As before, we have
\overline{f}(X)^{j}=f(X^{0})^{j}
in this neighborhood. Again, in this neighborhood,
f(X)^{j}
can be rewritten as:

f(X)_{i}^{j}=f(X^{0})_{i}^{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\dfrac{\partial f(X)^{j}_{i}}{\partial X_{l}^{k}}\bigg|_{X_{0}}\cdot(X_{l}^{k}-X_{l}^{k,0})+\mu_{X^{0}}^{i,j}\quad\forall i,j

Where
X_{l}^{k}
denotes the
l

k
-th sequence item in

, and
\mu_{X^{0}}^{i,j}
denotes the error of approximation for
f(X)_{i}^{j}
around
X^{0}
. Note that this approximation is the linear approximation of a function using first-order partial derivatives. For a function
z=g(x,y)

g(x_{0}+\Delta x,y_{0}+\Delta y)\approx g(x_{0},y_{0})+\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\cdot\Delta x+\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}\cdot\Delta y
. In our case,
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
, hence we have two summations: one over

and another over
m
. With
\overline{f}(X)^{j}=f(X^{0})^{j}

\overline{f}(X)_{i}^{j}-f(X)_{i}^{j}=-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\dfrac{\partial f(X)^{j}_{i}}{\partial X_{l}^{k}}\bigg|_{X_{0}}\cdot(X_{l}^{k}-X_{l}^{k,0})-\mu_{X^{0}}^{i,j}\quad\forall i,j

In the following mathematical statements, we will use
A
to denote
\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\dfrac{\partial f(X)^{j}_{i}}{\partial X_{l}^{k}}\bigg|_{X_{0}}\cdot(X_{l}^{k}-X_{l}^{k,0})

\delta/2

\displaystyle\int_{X^{0}-[\delta/2]_{m,d}}^{X^{0}+[\delta/2]_{m,d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big|\overline{f}(X)_{i}^{j}-f(X)_{i}^{j}\big|^{p}dX

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{X^{0}-[\delta/2]_{m,d}}^{X^{0}+[\delta/2]_{m,d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Bigg|A+\mu_{X^{0}}^{i,j}\Bigg|^{p}dX

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{X^{0}-[\delta/2]_{m,d}}^{X^{0}+[\delta/2]_{m,d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Bigg|A\cdot\bigg(1+\dfrac{\mu_{X^{0}}^{i,j}}{A}\bigg)\Bigg|^{p}dX

. Now, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{X^{0}-[\delta/2]_{m,d}}^{X^{0}+[\delta/2]_{m,d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|A|^{p}dX

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{X^{0}-[\delta/2]_{m,d}}^{X^{0}+[\delta/2]_{m,d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\dfrac{\partial f(X)^{j}_{i}}{\partial X_{l}^{k}}\bigg|_{X_{0}}\cdot(X_{l}^{k}-X_{l}^{k,0})\Bigg|^{p}dX

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{X^{0}-[\delta/2]_{m,d}}^{X^{0}+[\delta/2]_{m,d}}\Bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\dfrac{\partial f(X)^{j}_{i}}{\partial X_{l}^{k}}\bigg|_{X_{0}}\cdot(X_{l}^{k}-X_{l}^{k,0})\Bigg|^{p}dX

\displaystyle\int_{X_{l}^{k,0}-\delta/2}^{X_{l}^{k,0}+\delta/2}|B|^{p}\cdot|X_{l}^{k}-X_{l}^{k,0}|\>dX_{l}^{k}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\dfrac{\partial f(X)^{j}_{i}}{\partial X_{l}^{k}}\bigg|_{X_{0}}\Bigg|^{p}\cdot\dfrac{1}{p+1}\cdot 2^{-p}\cdot\delta^{p+1}\cdot\delta^{md-1}
