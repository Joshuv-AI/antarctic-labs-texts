Guess What Quantum Computing Can Do for Test Optimization Test Case Optimization with a Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm
Xinyi Wang, Shaukat Ali, Tao Yue, Paolo Arcaini

Abstract
In the near term, quantum approximate optimization algorithms (QAOAs) hold great potential to solve combinatorial optimization problems. These are hybrid algorithms, i.e., a combination of quantum and classical algorithms. Several proof-of-concept applications of QAOAs for solving combinatorial problems, such as portfolio optimization, energy optimization in power systems, and job scheduling, have been demonstrated. However, whether QAOAs can efficiently solve optimization problems from classical software engineering, such as test optimization, remains unstudied. To this end, we present the first effort to formulate a software test case optimization problem as a QAOA problem and solve it on quantum computer simulators. To solve bigger test optimization problems that require many qubits, which are unavailable these days, we integrate a problem decomposition strategy with the QAOA. We performed an empirical evaluation with five test case optimization problems and four industrial datasets from ABB, Google, and Orona to compare various configurations of our approach, assess its decomposition strategy of handling large datasets, and compare its performance with classical algorithms (i.e., Genetic Algorithm (GA) and Random Search). Based on the evaluation results, we recommend the best configuration of our approach for test case optimization problems. Also, we demonstrate that our strategy can reach the same effectiveness as GA and outperform GA in two out of five test case optimization problems we conducted.

Remark 1
.

This paper does not intend to demonstrate the quantum advantage of solving TCO problems with QAOA over classical approaches. This is practically impossible, since only small-scale noisy quantum computers are currently available. Rather, we demonstrate how TCO problems can be formulated and solved with QAOA by achieving comparable performance as classical approaches with manageable execution costs, which should be considered as a cornerstone for future quantum computing applications in solving software engineering optimization problems.

Qubits are different from classical bits since a classical bit can be in a state of either 0 or 1 at a time, while each qubit can exist in a superposition of both states until measured and collapses to a definite state (i.e., a classical one). A parameterized quantum circuit is at the core of QAOA, whose parameters are optimized with a classical optimizer. As depicted in Fig.

Initial state.

3.1
Ising formulation for test case optimization

We propose an Ising formulation for test case selection (TCS) and test case minimization (TCM) problems to be solved with QAOA.

Definition 1

(Test case selection (TCS))
.

Given a test suite
T=\{t_{0},

t_{1},

\ldots,t_{n-1}\}
with a set of testing attributes for the software under test and the corresponding testing objectives
F=\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{m-1}\}
, TCS aims to select a subset of test cases
T^{\prime}\subset T
to satisfy all testing objectives as much as possible. Each objective is associated with one of the attributes of the test cases.

Definition 2

(Test case minimization (TCM))
.

Given a test suite
T=\{t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-1}\}
with a set of testing attributes for the software under test and the corresponding testing objectives, TCM aims to select a subset of test cases with the minimized number of test cases
T^{\prime}\subset T
while satisfying all testing objectives as much as possible. The testing objectives
F=\{f_{0},

\ldots,

f_{m-1}\}
include ones associated with attributes of the test cases and the one specific objective aiming to minimize the size of
T^{\prime}
, which makes TCM different from TCS.

To construct the objective function of the TCO problem as an Ising formulation, we first represent
z_{i}
as the decision variable for selecting the test case
t_{i}
.
z_{i}=-1
denotes that
t_{i}
is
selected
and
z_{i}=+1
that is
not selected
. Thus, the selection of all test cases is represented with a bit string
\vec{z}\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
.

TCO problems maximize the effectiveness of the selected test cases in test suite
T^{\prime}
, while minimizing the cost when executing the selected test cases. We divide all testing objectives into two groups according to their corresponding testing attributes:
effectiveness objectives
and
cost objectives
.
Specially, for TCM problems, the objective of minimizing the size of selected test suite
T^{\prime}
belongs to the
cost objectives
group.
We minimize a cost-effective overall objective function for the TCO problem to seek the optimal solution
\vec{z}^{*}
, which finds an optimal trade-off between these two attribute groups to find the approximate optimal solution that minimizes the corresponding Ising formulation.

We represent the objective function for TCO problems as
\mathcal{O}(f_{0},

f_{1},

\ldots,

f_{m-1})
. Since different attribute values are obtained with various metrics, we normalize all objectives ranging from 0 to 1 to ensure comparability.

Given the
k
-th objective in the
effectiveness objectives
group, we represent the corresponding attribute values of all test cases as
\mathbf{v_{k}}=\{v_{0}^{k},\ldots,v_{n-1}^{k}\}
and build the objective as follows:

f_{k}(\vec{z})=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}v_{i}^{k}z_{i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}v_{i}^{k}}\right),\quad z_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}

(8)

We normalize the range of the objective between 0 and 1. If a test case
z_{i}
is selected (i.e.,
z_{i}=-1
), the corresponding attribute value
v_{i}^{k}
decreases, thus reducing the objective value. In contrast, if a test case is not selected (i.e.,
z_{i}=+1
), the attribute value is added to the objective value as a penalty.

Given the
k
-th objective in the
cost objectives
group, the corresponding attribute values of all test cases are represented as
\mathbf{c_{k}}=\{c_{0}^{k},\ldots,c_{n-1}^{k}\}
. For TCM problems, with respect to the objective of minimizing the size of the selected test suite, the attribute value for each test case is assigned as 1. We build the corresponding objective in the following:

f_{k}(\vec{z})=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}^{k}z_{i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}^{k}}\right),\quad z_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}

(9)

The value of the objective is still in the range of 0 and 1. If a test case
z_{i}
is selected (
z_{i}=-1
), the corresponding attribute value
c_{i}^{k}
will be added to the objective value as a penalty since a higher number of selected test cases will increase the cost; otherwise, the attribute value will contribute towards decreasing the objective value.

For each objective, to emphasize the larger deviation between the obtained value and the theoretical optimal value (i.e., 0), we square all objectives when combined to amplify the impact and penalize the improper selection.
For TCO problems, we formulate the overall objective function
\mathcal{O}(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{m-1})
as:

\min{\mathcal{O}_{TCO}(\vec{z})=w_{0}(f_{0})^{2}+\ldots+w_{m-1}(f_{m-1})^{2}}

(10)

w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{m-1}
are weights assigned to various objectives. Especially for TCM problems, one objective corresponds to the size of the selected test suite.

Suppose there are
s
objectives in the
effectiveness objectives
group and
q
objectives in the
cost objectives
group (
s+q=m
). Since
z_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
, it holds
z_{i}^{2}=1
. We expand the objective function as:

\mathcal{O}_{TCS}(\vec{z})=\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}X_{ij}z_{i}z_{j}+\sum_{i}Y_{i}z_{i}+\mathit{Const}

(11)

X_{ij}=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}w_{k}\frac{v_{i}^{k}v_{j}^{k}}{\left(\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}v_{l}^{k}\right)^{2}}-\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}w_{k+s}\frac{c_{i}^{k}c_{j}^{k}}{\left(\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}c_{l}^{k}\right)^{2}}

Y_{i}=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}w_{k}\frac{v_{i}^{k}}{\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}{v_{j}^{k}}}-\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}w_{k+p}\frac{c_{i}^{k}}{\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_{j}^{k}}

3.3
Process of
LOCH-QAOA

Currently, available quantum computers with limited qubits cannot directly solve TCO problems with many test cases with QAOA. Thus, we combine an impact-guided decomposition (IGDec) strategy adapted from
qbsolv

[
19
]
by D-Wave with QAOA to solve TCO problems as a classical-quantum hybrid approach with the Ising formulation and QAOA circuit described before.
Fig.
3
shows the overview of
LOCH-QAOA
that has the three main steps described below.

Figure 3:
Process of
LOCH-QAOA
. It has three steps: initialization, test case ordering by impact, and optimization

Step 1. Initialization.

Given a test suite containing
n
test cases, we initially assign random values (i.e.,
-1
or
+1
) to the decision variables associated with each test case. We represent the bit string as
\vec{z}
. With the objective function of Eq.
11
, we get an initial fitness value
\mathit{fval}
.

Step 2. Ordering test cases by
impact
.

We sort the test cases according to the
impact
value of each test case. This impact value indicates how much a test case contributes to reducing the fitness value of the TCO problem when its decision variable is altered. We use the calculation of the impact value of
t_{k}
as an example. Taking the bit string of the solution
\vec{z}
and the associated fitness value
\mathit{fval}
, for a test case
t_{k}
, we flip the value of its decision variable
z_{k}
(i.e.,
-1
to
+1
or
+1
to
-1
) to get a new bit string and the corresponding new fitness value
\mathit{fval}_{k}^{\prime}
. Next, we calculate the difference between
\mathit{fval}
and
\mathit{fval}_{k}^{\prime}
, which is the
\mathit{impact}
of
t_{k}
(i.e.,
\mathit{impact}_{k}=\mathit{fval}_{k}^{\prime}-\mathit{fval}
). In this way, we calculate the impact values of each test case and sort them based on the values.
Since we are solving a minimization problem, we arrange the test cases in ascending order of impact values. The test cases with lower impact values are positioned towards the front because they significantly decrease the fitness value when flipped.

Step 3. Optimization.

With the rearranged test cases, we decompose the problem and run QAOA iteratively to get the approximate optimal solution. We first determine the sub-problem size
N
that a quantum computer can afford. During the optimization process, we always optimize over a set of
N
variables in each QAOA execution, which belong to the
active
variable set
actSet
. For other variables, the values are fixed, maintaining the current values. Thus, with the overall objective function from Eq.
11
, irrespective of the constant term, we get the objective function:

\mathcal{O}_{TCO}(\vec{z})=\sum_{i,j\in\textit{actSet}}X_{ij}z_{i}z_{j}+\sum_{i\in\textit{actSet}}(Y_{i}+\sum_{j\notin\textit{actSet}}X_{j}z_{j}^{*})z_{i}

(17)

z_{j}^{*}
refers to the fixed values of variables not in set
actSet
. We optimize the variables (i.e., test cases) in order.
In the initial execution, we select the first
N
variables as the
active
variables. Then, we run the QAOA algorithm on the objective function to get optimized values of test cases in the
active
variables. Each variable in
actSet
corresponds to one qubit. Next, we optimize the next
N
variables, with the first
N
variables keeping the current optimized values. We continuously run the QAOA algorithm until
\mathit{num}
test case variables are optimized. With the new bit string
\vec{z}
, we get the new fitness value
\mathit{fval}
. If the stopping condition is not met, we return to
Step 2
and start a new iteration with the new bit string
\vec{z}
and
\mathit{fval}
, then arrange the test cases again.

Throughout the process of
LOCH-QAOA
, we identify and mark the best
\mathit{fval}
(i.e., the lowest fitness value) and the corresponding bit string
\vec{z}
(i.e., the solution). We repeat
Step 2
and
Step 3
until the stopping condition is achieved. Upon reaching this condition, the marked
\vec{z}
are recognized as the optimal solution to the problem.

4
Experiment Design

4.1
Research Questions

RQ1

How do the number of layers and the sub-problem size affect the performance of
LOCH-QAOA
?

RQ2

How is the performance of
LOCH-QAOA
compared with that of the baseline QAOA approach?

RQ3

How is the performance of
LOCH-QAOA
compared with that of classical approaches?

RQ1 studies how the two key parameters of
LOCH-QAOA
affect its performance. The first parameter is the depth of the QAOA circuit (i.e., the number of layers
p
). A higher value of
p
incurs higher cost in terms of execution. It is, therefore, important to study which number of layers is optimal for TCO problems. The second parameter is the sub-problem size (i.e.,
N
) to be solved on QAOA and how a particular sub-problem size affects the performance of
LOCH-QAOA
. This aspect helps us study which sub-problem size is optimal for TCO. In addition, we study whether a higher number of
N
incurs more execution time, i.e., studying the cost associated with
N
.

RQ2 studies whether
LOCH-QAOA
’s test case ordering by the IGDec strategy helps the QAOA to find approximately optimal solutions. To this end, we replace
LOCH-QAOA
’s IGDec strategy with a random strategy while keeping the QAOA part the same.

RQ3 compares
LOCH-QAOA
with two classical search algorithms: Random Search (RS) and a Genetic Algorithm (GA). We assess whether we can obtain optimal solutions close to, or even outperform, the classical search algorithm, i.e., GA, whereas comparison with RS ensures that we solve a complex problem that cannot be solved randomly.

4.2
Experiment Setup

This section discusses datasets we used for experiments, baselines, parameter settings, and execution environment.

4.2.1
Datasets

We employed four industrial datasets.

The

[

The first attribute contains a value telling how much time it was taken on average by a test case to execute, whereas the failure rate determines the possibility of a test case failing based on the historical ratio of failures to the total number of test case executions.
We run experiments on these datasets for the TCM problems. To do so, we encode the objective of minimizing the number of selected test cases. The overall fitness function objectives are to minimize the test execution cost and number of selected test cases while maximizing the fault detection rate of selected test cases.

The

The dataset has a total of 1925 test cases, and we selected two fitness functions to form the two TCS problems:

1)

ELEVATOR

2)

ELEVATOR

4.2.2
Baselines

We selected the following baselines for comparing
LOCH-QAOA

Div-QAOA
.
We implemented as a baseline approach a version of
LOCH-QAOA
without the IGDec strategy. The aim is to assess whether the IGDec strategy contributes to the overall performance of

in solving TCO problems. Specifically, we start with

active variables
and optimized at least once. We record the fitness values achieved after each run. Finally, the solution after the last sub-problem is solved is considered the final solution.

Genetic Algorithm.
We used the implementation of the Genetic Algorithm (GA) provided by the jMetalPy 1.6.0
[
6
]
framework. We represented each individual of the TCO problems as a bit string. We used the default parameter settings from jMetalPy, i.e., bit flip mutation operation being equal to the reciprocal of the size of the individual, the SPX crossover (the crossover rate=1.0). For each dataset, we experimented with population size
\textit{popSize}=\{10,

20,

30,

60,

. We ran 400,000 evaluations for each experiment to ensure that GA has sufficient time to converge. Note that in the literature, the default parameter settings of GA have shown good results
[

Random Search.
We used the existing random search (RS) implemented by jMetalPy 1.6.0
[
6
]
. It randomly selects binary values for each test case and calculates the fitness value repeatedly until a determined number of iterations has been reached, and we pick the lowest fitness value to compare with other approaches.

4.3
Evaluation metrics and statistical tests

4.3.1
Evaluation metrics

Approximation ratio (
ar
) is a common evaluation metric for assessing QAOA’s effectiveness in finding approximate optimal solutions
[
15
]
. We calculate
ar
as:

ar=\frac{\mathit{fval}(\vec{z})}{\mathit{fval_{min}}}

(18)

where
\mathit{fval_{min}}
is the optimal solution of the problem and
\mathit{fval}(\vec{z})
is the final solution obtained by QAOA. We use the lowest fitness value achieved in the whole experiment as the optimal fitness value for each case study. For
ar
, a lower value is indicative of a better performance. The optimal
ar
is 1.0 for all case studies.

To evaluate the time efficiency, we calculate the execution time
exTime
. For
LOCH-QAOA
, it contains both the QAOA execution time and classical computation time, including the time for the IGDec strategy.

In addition, we define fitness evaluations
numEva
to help analyze the performance of each approach.
For
LOCH-QAOA
, we calculate the number of QAOA runs (i.e., the sub-problems solved by QAOA) in all iterations until the stopping condition is satisfied as
numEva
. Similarly, for
Div-QAOA
, the number of randomly selected sub-problems is considered as
numEva
. For GA,
numEva
refers to the number of evaluations through all generations. For RS, each run (i.e., iteration) represents one evaluation, and the determined number of iterations represents the
numEva
value (each iteration contains one run).

4.3.2
Statistical tests

Following the guideline from
[
3
]
, we apply statistical tests to evaluate the performance (i.e.,
ar
) of
LOCH-QAOA
with different layers (i.e.,
p
) and sub-problem sizes (i.e.,
N
), and the baseline approaches. We first utilize a non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H-test to check whether there is a statistical difference among all groups of
ar
values obtained by
LOCH-QAOA
with different configurations or baseline approaches. We set the significance level as 0.05. A p-value smaller than 0.05 indicates that at least two groups of
ar
values are significantly different.

Next, we apply the Mann-Whitney U test and Vargha and Delaney’s
\hat{A}
12
effect size to compare all possible pairs of
LOCH-QAOA
with different configurations, and we also compare
LOCH-QAOA
with the other baseline approaches respectively. We set the significance level as 0.05. A p-value less than 0.05 indicates that there is no significant difference between the
ar
values of the two selected groups. In this case, we utilize Vargha and Delaney’s
\hat{A}
12
statistic as the effect size measure to quantify the magnitude of the difference between the two groups. If
\hat{A}
12
is 0.5, the result is achieved by chance. If
\hat{A}
12
is smaller than 0.5, the former corresponding approach performs better than that of the latter one during comparison and vice versa.

Generalizing results to datasets other than the ones used in this paper is a common threat to the validity of our experiments. We used four industrial datasets; therefore, the results are only valid for these datasets and other datasets of similar characteristics as ours. Nonetheless, additional experiments are warranted with more diverse datasets of varied sizes while also considering the limited number of qubits we could simulate on classical and real quantum computers currently.

Related Work

The computations performed by current quantum computers are noisy, thereby limiting their successful applications. To this end, hybrid algorithms are being advocated to maximize the use of scarce quantum resources and combine them with classical computations in near-term quantum computing applications. QAOA is one such hybrid algorithm that focuses on solving combinatorial optimization problems. Several problems, such as scheduling

For solving larger test optimization problems, we also integrate the IGDec strategy, which has previously been studied with quantum annealing

Quantum annealers exploit quantum principles, including superposition and entanglement, to solve combinatorial optimization problems efficiently. 7
Conclusion and Future Work

Quantum Computing (QC) holds great potential to solve many important and challenging problems in the long term. In the near future, their applications to solve optimization problems seem realistic. To this end, Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithms (QAOAs) are emerging as promising algorithms with several applications demonstrated in areas such as finance and scheduling. Along this line of investigation, we studied QAOAs in the context of test optimization for software systems. We contributed by providing a generic formulation of test optimization to solve it with QAOAs. Moreover, we integrated an existing test impact order strategy with QAOAs such that they can solve larger problems that cannot be solved with today’s limited number of available qubits. Finally, we performed an empirical study with five case studies with four industrial datasets. The results of experiments demonstrated the benefit of using QAOA’s for solving test optimization problems, which managed to reach similar or even better effectiveness compared to a classical Genetic Algorithm. In the future, we want to investigate solving other software engineering optimization problems with QAOAs.
