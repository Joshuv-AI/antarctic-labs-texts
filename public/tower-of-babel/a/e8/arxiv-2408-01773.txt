Chromo-natural inflation supported by enhanced friction from Horndeski gravity
Tomoaki Murata, Tsutomu Kobayashi

Abstract
We study the extension of the chromo-natural inflation model by incorporating nonminimal coupling between the axion field and gravity. Nonminimal coupling is introduced so that it enhances friction in the axion’s equation of motion and thus supports slow-roll inflation. This enhanced friction effectively delays the activation of the gauge field, thereby preventing the overproduction of gravitational waves in the CMB scale. We extend previous results by describing the nonminimal coupling in a general and unifying way utilizing Horndeski gravity. This allows us to explore systematically and comprehensively possible enhanced friction models of chromo-natural inflation consistent with observations. We find a novel enhanced friction model that shows better agreement (within 1 σ \sigma ) with CMB measurements than the previous nonminimally coupled chromo-natural inflation model. The gravitational-wave spectrum starts to rise at some wavenumber due to retarded activation of the gauge field in the late stage of inflation. We show how one can identify the wavenumber at which this occurs based on the background evolution and present a universal analytic formula for the gravitational-wave spectrum that can be used for any enhanced friction model of chromo-natural inflation.

II.2
Inflationary background dynamics

We consider the flat

\displaystyle A^{0}_{i}=0,\quad A^{a}_{i}=a(t)Q(t)\delta^{a}_{i}.

Using the FLRW metric and the ansatz for the gauge field,

(15)

\displaystyle{\cal I}\ddot{\phi}+3H{\cal I}\quantity(1 + \frac{\dot{\cali}}{3 H \cali} )\dot{\phi}+V_{,\phi}=-\frac{3g_{A}\lambda}{f}Q^{2}\quantity(\dot{Q}+HQ ),

(16)

(17)

Let us now consider the slow-roll inflationary dynamics of the above Horndeski-SU(2) system.
We assume that slow-roll inflation is supported by the gravitationally enhanced friction that arises if
\mathcal{I}\gg 1
rather than the interaction between the inflaton and the gauge field.
Although this is true throughout the entire duration of inflation, the energy fraction of the gauge field gradually increases toward the end of inflation.
The friction-dominated inflationary dynamics aims to fit our model to observations,
given that the original
CNI
model has already been ruled out by data.
As the energy fraction of the gauge field increases, its impact on the production of
PGW
gets stronger, possibly yielding a signature detectable by future gravitational-wave experiments.

We assume the slow-roll conditions to hold:

\displaystyle\epsilon:=-\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}\ll 1,\quad\eta:=\frac{\ddot{\phi}}{H\dot{\phi}}\ll 1.

(18)

It follows from these two conditions that

\displaystyle\dot{{\cal I}}\ll H{\cal I}.

(19)

We assume in addition that

\displaystyle\frac{H}{M}\gg 1,

(20)

so that the friction term in the axion equation of motion is enhanced:

\displaystyle{\cal I}\gg 1.

(21)

The effects of the gauge field are supposed to be subdominant
in the axion equation of motion,

\displaystyle{\cal I}\dot{\phi}\gg\frac{g_{A}\lambda}{f}Q^{3},

(22)

and thus, it is approximated as

\displaystyle 3{\cal I}H\dot{\phi}+V_{,\phi}\simeq 0.

(23)

This equation shows how the enhanced friction supports axion inflation without the help of the gauge field.

The gauge field may be considered as moving in the effective potential

\displaystyle U(Q)=H^{2}Q^{2}+\frac{1}{2}g_{A}^{2}Q^{4}-\frac{g_{A}\lambda}{3f}\dot{\phi}Q^{3}.

(24)

The shape of
U
depends on the value of
\dot{\phi}
, and it is easy to see that
the effective potential has a local minimum at
Q\neq 0
if

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}>\frac{4fH}{\lambda}

(25)

is satisfied.
The gauge field then tracks the slowly varying minimum:

\displaystyle g_{A}Q=g_{A}Q_{\rm min}:=\frac{\lambda\dot{\phi}}{4f}+\sqrt{\quantity(\frac{\lambda\dot{\phi}}{4f} )^{2}-H^{2}}.

(26)

The amplitude of the gauge field
Q
is thus controlled by the value of
\dot{\phi}
.

To see the background dynamics of inflation, it is convenient to introduce the density parameters representing a fraction of each term in the Friedmann equation (
12
),

\displaystyle\Omega_{X}

\displaystyle=\frac{X}{3M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}H^{2}},\quad\Omega_{V}=\frac{V}{3M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}H^{2}},\quad\Omega_{M}=\frac{M^{4}{\cal E}_{M}}{3M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}H^{2}},\quad\Omega_{B}=\frac{g_{A}^{2}Q^{4}}{2M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}H^{2}},\quad\Omega_{E}=\frac{\quantity(\dot{Q} + HQ )^{2}}{2M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}H^{2}}.

(27)

Under our slow-roll approximation,
Eq. (
13

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}\dot{H}\simeq-X{\cal I}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\epsilon\simeq\frac{X{\cal I}}{V}\gg\frac{X}{V},

(28)

and for
{\cal I}\gg 1
we have
X{\cal I}\sim M^{4}\mathcal{E}_{M}
.
We, therefore, expect the hierarchy of the density parameters

\Omega_{V}\gg\Omega_{M}\gg\Omega_{X},\Omega_{B},\Omega_{E}
during inflation.
We will confirm this hierarchy with the numerical calculations in the following subsection.

Upon doing so in the scalar sector, we find that

Substituting the solutions to the constraint equations back to the action, we arrive at the quadratic action for the coupled system of the three dynamical variables

24

III.1
Tensor perturbations

Let us derive the expression for the power spectrum of the primordial tensor perturbations from the Horndeski-SU(2) model.
We have two tensor variables
(h_{ij},T_{ia})
,
and each has two polarization degrees of freedom.
Therefore, the Horndeski-SU(2) system has four degrees of freedom in the tensor sector.

We decompose the tensor perturbations into the circular polarization basis.
Performing a Fourier decomposition, we write

\displaystyle h_{ij}(t,\bm{x})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}\quantity[ h^{+}(t,{\bm{k}})e^{+}_{ij}(\bm{k})
+ h^{-}(t,{\bm{k}})e^{-}_{ij}(\bm{k}) ],

(39)

and similarly for
T_{ij}
,
where
e^{\pm}_{ij}
is the circular polarization tensors satisfying

\displaystyle i\epsilon_{ilm}k^{l}e^{\pm}_{jm}=\pm ke^{\pm}_{ij}.

(40)

After straightforward calculations,
we obtain the quadratic action for the tensor perturbations as

\displaystyle S^{T}=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\left[\Delta_{I}^{\sigma\prime\dagger}\Delta_{I}^{\sigma\prime}+\Delta_{I}^{\sigma\prime\dagger}K_{t,IJ}\Delta_{J}^{\sigma}-\Delta_{I}^{\sigma\dagger}K_{t,IJ}\Delta_{J}^{\sigma\prime}-\Delta_{I}^{\sigma\dagger}\Omega_{t,IJ}^{2}\Delta_{J}^{\sigma}\right],

(41)

where
\tau
is the conformal time defined by
\mathrm{d}\tau=\mathrm{d}t/a(t)
,
a dash denotes differentiation with respect to
\tau
, and

\displaystyle\Delta^{\sigma}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix h^{\pol} \\ T^{\pol}\endlx@physics@matrix),

(42)

i.e.
\Delta^{\sigma}_{1}=h^{\sigma}
and
\Delta^{\sigma}_{2}=T^{\sigma}
.
Here,
K_{t,IJ}
is an anti-Hermitian matrix, and
\Omega^{2}_{t,IJ}
is a Hermitian matrix, the explicit forms of which are found in Appendix
D
.

Following the quantization procedure given in Ref.
[
67
]
and Appendix
C
,
we quantize
\Delta_{I}^{\sigma}

and express the operator
\hat{\Delta}_{I}^{\sigma}

in terms of the creation and annihilation operators
as

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{I}^{\sigma}(\tau,\bm{k})={\cal D}_{IJ}^{\sigma}(\tau,k)\hat{a}_{J}^{\sigma}(\bm{k})+{\cal D}^{\sigma*}_{IJ}(\tau,k)\hat{a}^{\sigma\dagger}_{J}(\bm{k}),

(43)

where the commutation relations

\displaystyle[\hat{a}_{I}^{\sigma}(\bm{k}),\hat{a}^{\sigma^{\prime}\dagger}_{J}(\bm{q})]=\delta^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\delta_{IJ}\delta(\bm{k}-\bm{q}),\quad\text{others}=0,

(44)

are understood.
The equation of motion for the mode functions
{\cal D}_{IJ}^{\sigma}(\tau,k)
are given by

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{\sigma\prime\prime}_{IL}+2K_{t,IJ}{\cal D}^{\sigma\prime}_{JL}+\quantity(K'_{t,IJ} + \Omega^2_{t,IJ} ){\cal D}_{JL}^{\sigma}=0.

(45)

We solve this equation numerically with the adiabatic vacuum initial conditions,

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{\sigma}_{IJ}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}c_{t,IJ}^{-1/2},\quad{\cal D}^{\sigma\prime}_{IJ}=-i\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}}c_{t,IJ}^{1/2},

(46)

where
c^{2}_{t,IJ}
is a diagonal matrix element corresponding to the propagation speeds of the tensor modes.
The general form of the power spectrum is given by Eq. (
101
) in Appendix
C
.
In the case of
PGW
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{h}(k)={\cal P}_{h}^{+}(k)+{\cal P}_{h}^{-}(k),\quad{\cal P}_{h}^{\sigma}(k)=\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{4}{a^{2}{\cal G}_{T}}\quantity(|\cald_{11}^{\pol}|^2 + |\cald_{12}^{\pol}|^2 ),

(47)

where
{\cal G}_{T}
is defined in terms of the Horndeski functions as

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{T}:=M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}+2M^{2}\quantity(
F_4-2\hat XF_4'-\frac{H}{M}\frac{\dot\phi}{M^2}\hat XF_5'
).

(48)

III.2
Scalar perturbations

We move to derive the scalar power spectrum in the Horndeski-SU(2) system.
The basic procedure is essentially the same as that for the tensor modes.
After integrating out the nondynamical variables,
the quadratic action for the scalar perturbations takes the form of

\displaystyle S^{S}=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left[\Delta_{I}^{\prime\dagger}\Delta_{I}^{\prime}+\Delta_{I}^{\prime\dagger}K_{s,IJ}\Delta_{J}-\Delta_{I}^{\dagger}K_{s,IJ}\Delta_{J}^{\prime}-\Delta_{I}^{\dagger}\Omega_{s,IJ}^{2}\Delta_{J}\right],

(49)

where

\displaystyle\Delta=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\dphi\\ \dQ\\ Z\endlx@physics@matrix),

(50)

i.e.
\Delta_{1}=\delta\phi
,
\Delta_{2}=\delta Q
, and
\Delta_{3}=Z
.
Here,
K_{s,IJ}
is an anti-Hermitian matrix, and
\Omega^{2}_{s,IJ}
is a Hermitian matrix, the explicit forms of which are found in Appendix
D
.

Following the same quantization procedure as in the case of the tensor perturbations,
we quantize
\Delta_{I}
and write

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{I}(\tau,\bm{k})={\cal D}_{IJ}(\tau,k)\hat{a}_{J}(\bm{k})+{\cal D}^{*}_{IJ}(\tau,k)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{J}(\bm{k}),

(51)

where the creation and annihilation operators satisfy

\displaystyle[\hat{a}_{I}(\bm{k}),\hat{a}^{{}^{\prime}\dagger}_{J}(\bm{q})]=\delta_{IJ}\delta(\bm{k}-\bm{q}),\quad\text{others}=0.

(52)

We then numerically solve the equations of motion for the mode functions,

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{\prime\prime}_{IL}+2K_{t,IJ}{\cal D}^{\prime}_{JL}+\quantity(K'_{s,IJ} + \Omega^2_{s,IJ} ){\cal D}_{JL}=0,

(53)

with the adiabatic vacuum initial conditions,

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{IJ}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}c_{s,IJ}^{-1/2},\quad{\cal D}^{\prime}_{IJ}=-i\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}}c_{s,IJ}^{1/2},

(54)

where
c^{2}_{s,IJ}
is a diagonal matrix element corresponding to the propagation speed of each degree of freedom.
The general form of the power spectrum (
101
) is now specialized to the present case, giving

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\delta\phi}(k)=\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{4}{a^{2}{\cal A}_{S}}\quantity(|\cald_{11}|^2 + |\cald_{12}|^2 + |\cald_{13}|^2),

(55)

where

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{S}

\displaystyle:=1+F_{2}^{\prime}+2\hat{X}F_{2}^{\prime\prime}+6\frac{H}{M}\frac{\dot{\phi}}{M^{2}}\left(F_{3}^{\prime}+\hat{X}F_{3}^{\prime\prime}\right)+6\frac{H^{2}}{M^{2}}\left(F_{4}^{\prime}+8\hat{X}F_{4}^{\prime\prime}+4\hat{X}^{2}F_{4}^{\prime\prime\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{H^{3}}{M^{3}}\frac{\dot{\phi}}{M^{2}}\left(6F_{5}^{\prime}+14\hat{X}F_{5}^{\prime\prime}+4\hat{X}^{2}F_{5}^{\prime\prime\prime}\right).

(56)

We evaluate the power spectrum of the curvature perturbation on uniform density slices
\zeta
.
The axion-field perturbation,
\delta\phi
, gives the dominant contribution to
\zeta
,
leading to
[
66
,
56
]

\displaystyle\zeta\simeq-\frac{H}{\dot{\phi}}\delta\phi.

(57)

(This is true in the present setup as well as in the previous models, as discussed in more detail in Appendix
E
.)
The power spectrum of
\zeta
is thus given by

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{\zeta}=\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{H^{2}}{\dot{\phi}^{2}}{\cal P}_{\delta\phi}.

(58)

We have thus derived the basic equations for evaluating the tensor and scalar power spectra in the Horndeski-SU(2) system.
Using those results, we can now compute the gravitational wave spectra and the CMB observables for the enhanced friction models of CNI.

IV
Gravitational wave spectra and CMB observables

In this section, we discuss how the choice of the Horndeski functions in the enhanced friction models of CNI affects the gravitational wave power spectra and the CMB observables and look for the models that can generate observable gravitational waves on small scales while avoiding their overproduction in the CMB scale.

IV.1
Power spectra

We show in the left panel of Fig.
2
the power spectra of
PGW
for the three different models introduced in Sec.
II.3
.
The parameters are the same as those used in Fig.
1
.
It can be seen that the power spectrum is enhanced for wavenumbers larger than some
k=k_{\star}
due to the gauge field.
We find that the mode with
k_{\star}
exits the horizon at the time when
\Omega_{B}
exceeds
\Omega_{X}
.
The precise value of
k_{\star}
thus depends on the choice of the Horndeski functions, as can be seen from Fig.
1
.
In the model (i), we see that
\Omega_{B}>\Omega_{X}
already at 70 e-folds before the end of inflation, resulting in the overproduction of
PGW
, similarly to the conventional
CNI
model.
The tensor power spectrum is, therefore, too large to be consistent with observations at the
CMB
scale.
In contrast, we have
\Omega_{B}\sim\Omega_{X}
in the model (ii) and
\Omega_{B}<\Omega_{X}
in the model (iii) at 70 e-folds before the end of inflation.
In these two cases, the tensor power spectrum is evaluated to be
{\cal P}_{h}\sim 7\times 10^{-11}
, leading to the tensor-to-scalar ratio
r\sim 0.03
, which is consistent with the
CMB
observations at
2\sigma

[
68
]
.
(A more detailed comparison with observations, including the scalar spectral index, will be performed shortly.)
One can understand these results by recalling how the value of the gauge field trapped at the minimum of the effective potential,
Q_{\rm min}
, depends on
\dot{\phi}
[Eq. (
26
)].
The more effective enhanced friction is, the smaller
\dot{\phi}
is and so is
Q_{\rm min}
. The growth of the gauge field is thus suppressed by enhanced friction.

In the right panel of Fig.
2
, we show the power spectra of the curvature perturbation
\zeta
.
In the model (i), the tilt of the spectrum is negatively large compared to the other two models.
This result reminds us of the original
CNI
model, where it has been known that
n_{s}
is negatively large
[
24
]
.
It implies that the effects of enhanced friction on the scalar perturbations are almost negligible in the model (i), yielding results close to those of the original
CNI
model.

Let us derive the analytic expression for the power spectrum of
GW
.
In the early stage of inflation, the effects of enhanced friction are dominant,
while those of the gauge field can be ignored.
The power spectrum is therefore well approximated by the expression for slow-roll inflation in Horndeski gravity derived in
[
63
]
.
In the present case, we have

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{h}^{\rm vacuum}=8\frac{\left[M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}+2M^{2}\left(F_{4}-2\hat{X}F_{4\hat{X}}-\frac{H}{M}\frac{\dot{\phi}}{M^{2}}\hat{X}F_{5\hat{X}}\right)\right]^{1/2}}{\left[M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}+2M^{2}\left(F_{4}-\frac{\ddot{\phi}}{M^{3}}\hat{X}F_{5\hat{X}}\right)\right]^{3/2}}\left.\quantity(\frac{H}{2\pi})^{2}\right|_{k=aH}.

(59)

This expression is mainly relevant to large scales with
k<k_{\star}
.
In the late stage, the gauge field is active, while the effects of enhanced friction can be ignored.
Therefore, the power spectrum can be approximated by the formula obtained in the original
CNI

[
25

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{h}^{\rm sourced}=\left.\frac{\epsilon_{B}H^{2}}{\pi^{2}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}}e^{3.6m_{Q}}\right|_{k=aH}.

(60)

where
\epsilon_{B}=g_{A}^{2}Q^{4}/(M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}H^{2})
and

.
This expression is mainly relevant to small scales with
k>k_{\star}
.
Thus, our analytic expression for the

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{h}^{\rm analytic}={\cal P}_{h}^{\rm vacuum}+{\cal P}_{h}^{\rm sourced},

(61)

where the first (second) term is dominant for
k<k_{\star}
(
k>k_{\star}
).
In Fig.
3
, we compare the numerical results with the analytic expression, which justifies the above argument.

Now we have a simplified way of deriving the approximate
GW
spectrum that can be used for any enhanced friction model of
CNI
.
First, one solves the background cosmological evolution to identify the time when
\Omega_{B}=\Omega_{X}
occurs, which fixes the wavenumber
k_{\star}
.
Then, the approximate
GW
spectrum is given by Eq. (
59

k<k_{*}
and Eq. (
60

k>k_{\star}
.
The tensor spectral index on small scales is given by

\displaystyle n_{t}\simeq 3.6\left(\epsilon+\frac{\dot{Q}_{\rm min}}{HQ_{\rm min}}\right)m_{Q}.

(62)

IV.3
Gravitational waves and backreaction

Let us move to discuss the detectability of
PGW
from the enhanced friction models of CNI.
The density parameter of
GW
per log frequency interval is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm GW}(k)=\frac{3}{128}\Omega_{r,0}{\cal P}_{h}\quantity[\frac{1}{2} \qty(\frac{k}{k_{\rm eq}})^2 + \frac{16}{9} ],

(64)

where
\Omega_{r,0}\simeq 2.47\times 10^{-5}
is the present radiation density parameter and
k_{\rm eq}\simeq 0.013\,{\rm Mpc}^{-1}
is the wavenumber of the mode reentering the horizon at matter-radiation equality with
\Omega_{m}h^{2}\simeq 0.141
(see e.g.
[
69
]
).
We express
\Omega_{\rm GW}
as a function of frequency by using the relation
f\simeq 1.5\times 10^{-15}(k/\rm{Mpc^{-1}})\,
Hz.
In Fig.

, we present
\Omega_{\rm GW}
for the models (i), (ii), and (iii).
The parameters are given again by Eqs. (
33
) and (
34
).
The sensitivity curves are taken from Ref.
[
70
]
.
As we already discussed, the amplitude of GWs in the model (i) is too large on the CMB scale.
Therefore, our main interest here is in the models (ii) and (iii).
Figure
6
shows that
PGW
in these models would be detectable by
experiments such as SKA, LISA, and DECIGO/BBO.
The SKA might be capable of observing circular polarization
[
71
]
.
If the circular polarization of the
PGW
were detected, our result could provide the physical interpretation of the observational results.

Some comments are now in order.
The first one is on the backreaction from tensor perturbations.
In the axion-SU(2) inflation model, tensor perturbations of the SU(2) gauge field are amplified by the tachyonic instability slightly before the horizon crossing.
These large tensor perturbations cause the backreaction to the background dynamics (see Refs.
[
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
]
for detailed discussions).
The effects of the backreaction are significant when
Q
becomes large enough to satisfy
[
72
]

\displaystyle e^{1.85m_{Q}}g_{A}\gtrsim 10,

(65)

where
m_{Q}=g_{A}Q/H
.
This condition can also be applied to the present model at late times when gravitational waves are sourced by tensor perturbations of the gauge field.
This backreaction may change the scalar and tensor spectra on small scales.
In Figs.
2
and
6
, we show by dashed lines the regimes where Eq. (
65
) is satisfied.

The backreaction effects are likely insignificant on large scales.
Therefore, the predictions on CMB observables,
n_{s}
and
r
, are robust even when the effects of the backreaction are taken into account.
The issue needs to be revisited when a more detailed treatment of backreaction becomes feasible in the future.
Further research such as lattice simulations is anticipated to advance our understanding of the impact of backreaction.

The second comment is on the perturbativity constraints on the axion-gauge dynamics.
Very recently, the authors of Ref.
[
76
]
put constraints on the allowed parameter space of axion-gauge inflation by requiring that loop corrections to the gauge-field propagator remain small.
The resultant constraints are competitive with the backreaction bounds and relevant to the present models as well.

Finally, the third comment is on
g_{A}
.
The parameter
g_{A}
is crucial in axion-gauge field interactions during inflation.
The dissipation term of the axion (the right-hand side of Eq. (
16
)) is proportional to
g_{A}^{-2}
, as we see from Eq. (
26
) the relation
Q\propto g_{A}^{-1}
(for nearly constant
\dot{\phi}
and
H
). Therefore, for smaller
g_{A}
, the dissipation from the axion to the gauge field is more efficient.
As a result,
for sufficiently small
g_{A}
, enhanced friction is less effective than the interaction with the gauge field in the axion field equation and hence it does not effectively suppress the growth of the gauge field.
For instance, for
g_{A}=10^{-3}
(used in Ref.
[
77
]
), the effects of enhanced friction are negligible, resulting in a large tensor-to-scalar ratio at the
CMB
scale.
On the other hand, if
g_{A}
is large enough, the aforementioned backreaction problem could arise. Thus, a careful choice of the value of
g_{A}
is important for building workable models of the axion-gauge field interaction during inflation and making reliable predictions that can be tested by future observations.

V
Conclusions

In this paper, we have generalized chromo-natural inflation (CNI) to allow the axion to be coupled nonminimally to gravity through the Horndeski-type interaction, extending the previous model
[
56
]
to a more general, unifying framework.
Due to the overproduction of gravitational waves (GWs), the original (minimally coupled)
CNI
model has already been ruled out by
CMB
observations
[
24
]
.
Nonminimal couplings to gravity can help to avoid this inconsistency with
CMB
observations by reducing the production of
GW
on large scales.
In the late stage of inflation, the effects of the SU(2) gauge field overwhelm this reduction mechanism and enhance the
GW
spectrum on small scales.

Inspired by
[
56
]
, we have considered general shift-symmetric Horndeski-type interactions of the axion field
\phi
and studied how they contribute to the enhancement factor
{\cal I}
of the friction term in
\phi
’s equation of motion.
The enhancement factor typically has the form

{\cal I}\sim H^{p}\dot{\phi}^{q}
, and thus is characterized by the powers
p
and
q
.
We have studied the background slow-roll dynamics of Horndeski-SU(2) inflation for several representative models with different
(p,q)
including the one proposed in Ref.
[
56
]
.
We have also considered cosmological perturbations and obtained the power spectra of
GW
and the curvature perturbation for each model.
Through these analyses, we have found that the two density parameters
\Omega_{B}
and
\Omega_{X}
defined in Eq. (
27
) are of particular importance in determining the shape of the
GW
spectrum.
In the early stage of inflation, one has
\Omega_{B}<\Omega_{X}
and enhanced friction is effective.
The
GW
production is suppressed in this stage.
The gauge field grows toward the end of inflation,
and hence one has
\Omega_{B}>\Omega_{X}
in the late stage of inflation.
Now enhanced friction is less effective and the inflationary dynamics becomes similar to that of usual
CNI
, leading to the efficient production of
GW
on small scales.
This is a universal nature of nonminimally coupled
CNI
models irrespective of
(p,q)
.
However, the time when
\Omega_{B}
catches up with
\Omega_{X}
crucially depends on
(p,q)
.
We have found that the onset of the late stage with
\Omega_{B}>\Omega_{X}
is delayed for larger
p
and smaller
q
.
For the
GW
spectrum on large scales to be consistent with
CMB
observations, the friction-dominated stage must be sustained sufficiently long.
Therefore, the model with
(p,q)=(3,0)
is most appropriate.

We have further investigated the observational predictions of each model in more detail by evaluating the scalar spectral index
n_{s}
and the tensor-to-scalar ratio
r
and looked for regions of the parameter space consistent with
CMB
observations.
We have found that the model with
(p,q)=(3,0)
(the model (iii)) is consistent with Planck
+
BICEP/Keck constraints at the
1\sigma
level, which is better than the results of the previous model
[
56
]
.
While consistent with observations on large scales,
the tensor amplitude is enhanced on small scales, which could be relevant to future
GW
experiments.
Though this could be an interesting observational signature, one must be careful about the issue of possible strong backreaction to the background dynamics caused on the same small scales.
Note that the predictions about
n_{s}
and
r
are not affected by this backreaction issue.

It would be interesting to study the reheating stage after inflation in the preset setup.
The nonminimal coupling considered in this paper leads to distinctive dynamics of the scalar field during reheating
[
78
]
, which would be helpful to distinguish between different enhanced friction models of inflation.
We hope to come back to this point in a future publication.

Appendix C
Quantization of a coupled system

Following Ref.
[
67
]
, we briefly summarize the quantization method for a coupled system.
First, we will introduce the general procedure and
then rewrite the results into formulas that can easily be applied to the specific system we are interested in.
In this appendix, we use the conformal time
\tau
.

The general procedure for quantizing a coupled system is as follows.
We consider a set of dynamical variables
v_{I}
, where
I=1,2,\dots,N
is a label of each component.
In general, the kinetic terms for
v_{I}
are not of the canonical form,
but we can always perform a rotation in field space,
\Delta_{I}={\cal R}_{IJ}v_{J}
, to obtain canonical kinetic terms.
Then, the action for
\Delta_{I}
is written in the form

\displaystyle S=\frac{1}{2}\int\differential{\tau}\differential[3]{x}\quantity[\Delta'_{I} \Delta'_{I}
+ \Delta'_{I} K_{IJ} \Delta_{J}
- \Delta_{I} K_{IJ} \Delta'_{J}
- \Delta_{I} \Omega_{IJ}^{2} \Delta_{J}],

(81)

where
K_{IJ}
is an anti-symmetric matrix and
\Omega^{2}_{IJ}
is a symmetric matrix. We start with the action written in this form.
The equation of motion that follows from the above action is given by

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime\prime}_{I}+2K_{IJ}\Delta^{\prime}_{J}+\quantity(K'_{IJ} + \Omega_{IJ}^{2} )\Delta_{J}=0.

(82)

We can eliminate the terms with the coefficients
K_{IJ}
by further performing a rotation in field space,
\psi_{I}={\cal Q}_{IJ}\Delta_{J}
,
where
{\cal Q}_{IJ}
is an orthogonal matrix that satisfies
{\cal Q}^{\prime}_{IJ}={\cal Q}_{IL}K_{LJ}
.
3
3

3

An orthogonal matrix
{\cal Q}
satisfies
{\cal Q}^{\top}{\cal Q}={\cal Q}{\cal Q}^{\top}=I,
where
\top
is a transpose and
I
is an identity matrix.
Differentiating this relation with repsect to time, we obtain
{\cal Q}^{\top}{\cal Q}^{\prime}=-({\cal Q}^{\top}{\cal Q}^{\prime})^{\top}
, which shows that

{\cal Q}^{\top}{\cal Q}^{\prime}
is an anti-symmetric matrix.
Choosing appropriately the
N(N-1)/2
independent components of
{\cal Q}
, one can find
{\cal Q}
satisfying
{\cal Q}^{\top}{\cal Q}^{\prime}=K
for any anti-symmetric matrix
K
.

Using the matrix
{\cal Q}_{IJ}
, we can write the action as

\displaystyle S=\int\differential{\tau}\differential[3]{x}{\cal L}=\frac{1}{2}\int\differential{\tau}\differential[3]{x}\quantity(\psi'_{I} \psi'_{I} - \psi_{I} {\cal S}^2_{IJ} \psi_{J}),\quad{\cal S}^{2}_{IJ}={\cal Q}_{IL}\quantity(\Omega^{2}_{LM} - K_{LN} K_{NM}){\cal Q}_{MJ}^{\top},

(83)

where
\top
denotes the transpose of the matrix.
The conjugate momentum is defined as

\displaystyle\Psi_{I}:=\partialderivative{\cal L}{\psi'_{I}}=\psi^{\prime}_{I},

(84)

and the Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{2}\int\differential[3]{x}\quantity(\Psi_{I} \Psi_{I} + \psi_{I} {\cal S}^2_{IJ} \psi_{J}).

(85)

In general, the matrix
{\cal S}^{2}_{IJ}
is nondiagonal.

One then proceeds to the usual quantization procedure for each set of the canonical variables
\Psi_{I}
and
\psi_{I}
.
We perform the decomposition

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{I}=C_{IJ}\int\frac{\differential[3]{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\quantity(u_{JK} \hat{a}_{K} + u^{*}_{JK} \hat{a}^{\dagger}_{K} )e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}},\quad\hat{\Psi}_{I}=C_{IJ}\int\frac{\differential[3]{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\quantity(\tilde{u}_{JK} \hat{a}_{K} + \tilde{u}^{*}_{JK} \hat{a}^{\dagger}_{K} )e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}},

(86)

where
C_{IJ}
, an orthogonal matrix diagonalizing
\mathcal{S}_{IJ}^{2}
:

\displaystyle C_{IK}^{\top}{\cal S}^{2}_{KL}C_{LJ}=\diag\quantity(\omega^{2}_{(1)},...,\omega^{2}_{(N)})=:\omega^{2}_{IJ},

(87)

is introduced for later convenience.
We impose the commutation relations

\displaystyle\quantity[\hat{\psi}_{I} (\bm{x}), \ \hat{\Psi}_{J} (\bm{y}) ]=i\delta^{3}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\delta_{IJ},\quad\text{others}=0,

(88)

for the conjugate fields
and

\displaystyle\quantity[\hat{a}_{I} (\bm{k}), \ \hat{a}_{J}^\dagger(\bm{p}) ]=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(\bm{k}-\bm{p})\delta_{IJ},\quad\text{others}=0,

(89)

for the creation and annihilation operators
\hat{a}_{I}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{I}
.
Equations (
88
) and (
89
) are satisfied if

\displaystyle u_{IK}\tilde{u}_{JK}^{*}-u_{IK}^{*}\tilde{u}_{JK}=i\delta_{IJ}.

(90)

The canonical equations of motion are given by

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}_{I}=\Psi_{I},\quad\Psi^{\prime}_{I}=-{\cal S}^{2}_{IJ}\psi_{J},

(91)

which yield

\displaystyle u^{\prime}_{IJ}=\tilde{u}_{IJ}-\Gamma_{IK}u_{KJ},\quad\tilde{u}^{\prime}_{IJ}=-\Gamma_{IK}\tilde{u}_{KJ}-\omega^{2}_{IK}u_{KJ},

(92)

where
\Gamma_{IJ}=C^{\top}_{IK}C^{\prime}_{KJ}
is anti-symmetric.
Now,
N
second-order differential equations (
82
) for
\Delta_{I}
have reduced to
N\times N
differential equations (
92
) for
u_{IJ}
.
While the
N
diagonal components of the Wronskian condition (
90
) are used to determine the normalization of the fields,
the remaining
N^{2}-N
components give constraints among
u_{IJ}
,
We are thus left with
N^{2}-(N^{2}-N)=N
independent components of
u_{IJ}
.
The number of dynamical degrees of freedom remains unchanged.

Next, let us discuss the initial adiabatic vacuum of this system, specializing to the case of inflationary cosmological perturbations with multiple degrees of freedom.
Now, we assume that the background field is constant in the early stage of the evolution of the system.
In the sub-horizon limit, we have
\Omega^{2}_{IJ}\simeq{\cal O}(k^{2})\delta_{IJ}\gg K_{IK}K_{KJ}
in the specific cases we are considering, as can be seen from the explicit expressions for these matrices (see Appendix
D
).
It then follows that
\Delta_{I}\simeq\psi_{I}
and
\mathcal{S}_{IJ}^{2}\simeq\Omega_{IJ}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(k^{2})\delta_{IJ}
in the early time.
Since
\mathcal{S}^{2}_{IJ}
is already diagonal in this limit, we have
C_{IJ}\simeq\delta_{IJ}
, and hence
\Gamma_{IJ}\simeq 0
, leading to

\displaystyle u^{\prime}_{IJ}\simeq\tilde{u}_{IJ},\quad\tilde{u}^{\prime}_{IJ}\simeq-\omega^{2}_{IK}u_{KJ}\quad\Rightarrow\quad u^{\prime\prime}_{IJ}\simeq-\omega^{2}_{IK}u_{KJ}.

(93)

The solution is given by

\displaystyle u_{IJ}=C_{1}\exp\quantity[-i \int^{\tau} \omega_{(I)} \D\tilde{\tau}]\delta_{IJ}+C_{2}\exp\quantity[i \int^{\tau} \omega_{(I)} \D\tilde{\tau}]\delta_{IJ},

(94)

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are integration constants.
We consider the adiabatic vacuum condition and hence set
C_{2}=0
.
Using the normalization condition (
90
),
we obtain the mode function at some initial moment as

\displaystyle u_{IJ}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{(I)}}}\delta_{IJ},\quad\tilde{u}_{IJ}=-i\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{(I)}}{2}}\delta_{IJ},

(95)

where we omitted irrelevant phase factors.

Having thus determined the initial conditions for
u_{IJ}
,
we can solve the equations of motion and accordingly find the time evolution of the original variables
v_{I}
by multiplying the (inverse of the) matrices
C_{IJ}
,
{\cal Q}_{IJ}
, and
\mathcal{R}_{IJ}
.
We therefore need to specify the form of these matrices explicitly.
These steps allow for some simplification, as shown in Refs.
[
79
,
66
,
56
]

and as presented below.

Practically, it is more convenient to deal with
\Delta_{I}
directly by performing the decomposition

\displaystyle\hat{\Delta}_{I}=\int\frac{\differential[3]{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\quantity[\cald_{IJ}(\tau,k) \hat{a}_{J}(\bm{k})
+ \cald^{*}_{IJ}(\tau,k) \hat{a}^{\dagger}_{J}(\bm{k})]e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}},

(96)

where
{\cal D}_{IJ}
is related to
u_{IJ}
as

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{IJ}={\cal Q}^{\top}_{IK}C_{KL}u_{LJ},\quad{\cal D}^{\prime}_{IJ}=-K_{IK}{\cal Q}^{\top}_{KL}C_{LM}u_{MJ}+{\cal Q}^{\top}_{IK}C_{KL}\tilde{u}_{LJ}.

(97)

The equation of motion is given by

\displaystyle{\cal D}^{\prime\prime}_{IL}+2K_{IJ}{\cal D}^{\prime}_{JL}+\quantity(K'_{IJ} + \Omega^2_{IJ} ){\cal D}_{JL}=0.

(98)

In the sub-horizon limit, the frequency matrix takes the form

\displaystyle\Omega^{2}_{IJ}\simeq\omega^{2}_{IJ}=k^{2}c^{2}_{IJ},

(99)

where
c^{2}_{IJ}
is a diagonal matrix whose
I
-th element corresponds to the propagation speed of the
I
-th field.
In this limit, the appropriate initial conditions are given by

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{IJ}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}c_{IJ}^{-1/2},\quad{\cal D}^{\prime}_{IJ}=-i\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}}c_{IJ}^{1/2},

(100)

where we assume that
c^{2}_{IJ}
is a slowly varying function of time in the early time.
In the case of the conventional
CNI
model, we have
c_{IJ}^{2}=\delta_{IJ}
.
However, the propagation speeds of the scalar modes may differ from unity in the Horndeski family of scalar-tensor theories.
In terms of
\mathcal{D}_{IJ}
, the power spectrum is evaluated as

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{IJ}(k)=\frac{k^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}{\cal R}_{IL}{\cal D}_{LK}{\cal D}_{KM}^{*}{\cal R}_{MJ}^{*}.

(101)

To summarize, in this Appendix we have introduced the quantization method for a coupled system.
One can quantize the system by identifying the matrices
{\cal R}_{IJ},K_{IJ},\Omega^{2}_{IJ}
, and
c^{2}_{IJ}
in the action of our system.
In practice,
c^{2}_{IJ}
can be read off from
\Omega^{2}_{IJ}
.

Appendix E

In the main text, the curvature perturbation on uniform density slices,

In this appendix, we discuss the validity of this approximation.

The curvature perturbation on uniform density slices is written as

\displaystyle\zeta=\frac{\delta\rho}{3(\rho+p)}=\frac{\rho_{\phi}}{\rho+p}\cdot\frac{\delta\rho_{\phi}}{3\rho_{\phi}}+\frac{\rho_{Q}}{\rho+p}\cdot\frac{\delta\rho_{Q}}{3\rho_{Q}},

where
\rho=\rho_{\phi}+\rho_{Q}

p=p_{\phi}+p_{Q}

\delta\rho=\delta\rho_{\phi}+\delta\rho_{Q}

\delta\rho_{\phi}
and
\delta\rho_{Q}
being the scalar-field and the gauge-field pieces, respectively.
In the present case, we have
\rho_{\phi}\gg\rho_{Q}

\zeta\simeq\delta\rho_{\phi}/3(\rho+p)
.

we show that the metric perturbations can be ignored under the slow-roll approximation.
Under the same approximation,
\delta\rho_{\phi}
is given by

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{\phi}

\displaystyle=-\quantity(G_{2\phi}
- \dot{\phi}^2 G_{2\phi X})\delta\phi+\dot{\phi}\quantity(G_{2X}
+ \dot{\phi}^2 G_{2XX})\delta\dot{\phi}+\dot{\phi}\quantity(
9 H \dot{\phi} G_{3X}
+ 3 H \dot{\phi}^3 G_{3XX})\delta\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle\quad+6H\quantity(
3 H \dot{\phi} G_{4X}
+ 6 H \dot{\phi}^3 G_{4XX}

\displaystyle\quad+{\cal O}(k^{2}),

(106)

where
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
terms are ignored on superhorizon scales.
In our inflation models, we have

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{\phi}\simeq V_{\phi}\delta\phi+\mathcal{O}(H{\cal I}\dot{\phi})\times\frac{\delta\dot{\phi}}{H}.

(107)

We have
\delta\phi\gg\delta\dot{\phi}/H
on superhorizon scales, as confirmed by numerical solutions, and hence the curvature perturbation on uniform density slices is approximately given by

\displaystyle\zeta\simeq\frac{V_{\phi}\delta\phi}{3(\rho+p)}\simeq-\frac{H}{\dot{\phi}}\delta\phi,

(108)

where we used the background equations with the slow-roll approximation.
