Asymptotic rates of convergence for compact reversible Markov chains
Yue Xu, Xiuwu Zhu

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish the main term governing the rate at which a compact reversible Markov chain converges to stationarity. As an application, we obtain the sharpest estimates for the corank distributions of uniform, skew centrosymmetric, symmetric, skew-symmetric, and Hermitian matrices.

60F17, 60J10

1.
Introduction

Studying the convergence rate of Markov chains is an important topic in probability theory and it has many important applications in
[
1
,
2
]
.
For an irreducible, aperiodic Markov chain, the convergence rate is exponential, which is called geometrically ergodic, if and only if it satisfies certain drift conditions
[
3
]
.
If the Markov chain is reversible, the exponential rate is closely related to the non-one eigenvalue of the corresponding transition matrix with largest absolute value
[
4
]
.
In this paper, we view the transition matrix as a bounded self-adjoint operator on a separable Hilbert space.
If it is further assumed that the operator is compact, we employ the spectral decomposition theorem to obtain the main term of the convergence rate.
Also, the mixing time of this reversible Markov chain can be obtained.

The main results of this paper are to prove that the operators related to certain classes of Markov chains are compact and to provide all of their eigenvalues. Consequently, we can demonstrate that Markov chains related to the corank distribution of several classes of random matrices over finite fields are also compact, thus obtaining the asymptotic expressions for their distributions.
These corank distributions play a significant role in the context of the Cohen-Lenstra conjecture for ideal class groups and Selmer groups
[
2
,
7
,
8
]
.

Let
P
be the transition matrix of an irreducible, aperiodic Markov chain on nonnegative integers.
Assume that
P
is reversible with respect to
\pi
.
Recall that the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\pi)
consists of the complex-valued sequences
\mu=(\mu(0),\mu(1),\cdots)
with inner product and norm defined by

\langle\mu,\nu\rangle_{\pi}:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(i)\overline{\nu(i)}}{\pi(i)},\quad\|\mu\|_{\pi}:=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu(i)|^{2}}{\pi(i)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Then
P
is a bounded, self-adjoint, linear operator on
\ell^{2}(\pi)
through the action
P\cdot\mu:=\mu P
.
Recall that the modified (without the factor
1/2
) total variation distance
\|\cdot\|_{tv}
between
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\ell^{2}(\pi)
is defined as follows:

\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|_{tv}:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|\mu_{1}(i)-\mu_{2}(i)|\leq\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|_{\pi}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume further that
P
is a compact operator on
\ell^{2}(\pi)

\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots
be all eigenvalues of

with non-increasing absolute value. Then for any

\|\mu P^{n}-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{n}\mu_{i}\|_{tv}=O(|\lambda_{k+1}|^{n}).

Here

is the
\lambda_{i}
-component in the spectral decomposition of

and the implicit constant is less than
\|\mu\|_{\pi}
. In particular,

\|\mu P^{n}-\mu_{0}\|_{tv}=\begin{cases}\|\mu_{1}\|_{tv}\cdot|\lambda_{1}|^{n}+O(|\lambda_{2}|^{n}),&\mbox{if }|\lambda_{1}|>|\lambda_{2}|\\
(\|\mu_{1}+(-1)^{n}\mu_{2}\|_{tv})\cdot|\lambda_{1}|^{n}+O(|\lambda_{3}|^{n}),&\mbox{if }|\lambda_{1}|=|\lambda_{2}|.\end{cases}

Here

,

To consider the corank distribution of random matrices

It’s irreducible, aperiodic and reversible with a stationary distribution

2.
Preliminaries

Let
P
be a transition matrix on nonnegative integers. Assume that
P
is irreducible and aperiodic, that
P
has a unique stationary distribution denoted by
\pi
. Then by the basic limit theorem, we know that for any nonzero initial distribution
\mu
,

\|\mu P^{n}-\pi\|_{tv}\rightarrow 0,\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Here the modified (without the factor
1/2
) total variation distance
\|\cdot\|_{tv}
between two distributions
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
is defined as follows:

\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|_{tv}:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|\mu_{1}(i)-\mu_{2}(i)|.

It is natural to ask how fast
\mu P^{n}
converges to
\pi
.
In general, if
P
satisfies certain drift conditions
[
3
]
, then the convergence rate is exponential. Now, can we give an explicit asymptotic estimation of the convergence rate for some
P
?

2.1.
Reversible Markov chain

Assume further that
P
is
reversible
with respect to
\pi
, i.e.,
\pi(j)P(j,i)=\pi(i)P(i,j)
for any
i,j\geq 0
.
Note that
\pi(i)>0
for all
i
since
P
is irreducible.
We define the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\pi)
of the complex-valued sequences by

\ell^{2}(\pi)=\left\{\mu=(\mu(0),\mu(1),\cdots)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}~\bigg|~\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu(i)|^{2}}{\pi(i)}<\infty\right\}.

The inner product on
\ell^{2}(\pi)
is given by

\langle\mu,\nu\rangle_{\pi}:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(i)\overline{\nu(i)}}{\pi(i)},\text{ and the norm is }\|\mu\|_{\pi}:=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu(i)|^{2}}{\pi(i)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have
\|\mu\|_{tv}\leq\|\mu\|_{\pi}
. In fact,

\|\mu\|_{tv}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu(i)|}{\sqrt{\pi(i)}}\cdot\sqrt{\pi(i)}\right)^{2}\leq\|\mu\|_{\pi}^{2}.

There is a natural action of
P
on
\ell^{2}(\pi)
through
P\cdot\mu:=\mu P
.

Usually, the following spaces are more frequently used literature (for example,
[
3
]
).
For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, consider

\ell^{p}_{\mathrm{old}}(\pi):=\left\{f=(f(0),f(1),\cdots)^{T}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}~\bigg|~\|f\|_{\ell^{p}}<\infty\right\},

and

P\cdot f:=Pf,\quad\|P\|_{\ell^{p}}:=\sup_{\|f\|_{\ell^{p}}=1}\|Pf\|_{\ell^{p}}.

Here,
\|f\|_{\ell^{p}}:=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|f(i)|^{p}\pi(i)\right)^{1/p}
for
p\neq\infty
and
\|f\|_{\ell^{\infty}}:=\sup_{i}|f(i)|
.
Note that both
\|P\|_{\ell^{1}}
and
\|P\|_{\ell^{\infty}}
are no greater than one.
Then by Hölder’s inequality, we have
\|P\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq 1
.
Meanwhile,
\ell^{2}_{\mathrm{old}}(\pi)
is a Hilbert space where the inner product is given by
\langle f,g\rangle_{\ell^{2}}:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}f(i)\overline{g(i)}\pi(i)
.
In particular, we have the following isomorphism between two Hilbert spaces which is also compatible with the action of
P
.

\phi:\ell^{2}(\pi)\rightarrow\ell^{2}_{\mathrm{old}}(\pi),\quad\mu\mapsto(\cdots,\mu(i)/\pi(i),\cdots)^{T}.

Then

\|P\|_{\pi}:=\sup_{\|\mu\|_{\pi}=1}\|\mu P\|_{\pi}=\|P\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq 1.

In other word,
P
is a linear contraction on
\ell^{2}(\pi)
.

On the other hand, the adjoint operator
P^{\ast}
on
\ell^{2}(\pi)
is defined by

P^{\ast}(i,j):=\frac{P(j,i)\pi(j)}{\pi(i)}.

Hence, as a operator,
P
is also
self-adjoint
since
P^{\ast}=P
. For similar reason, we have an isomorphism between
\ell^{2}(\pi)
and

\ell^{2}:=\left\{\nu=(\nu(0),\nu(1),\cdots)^{T}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}~\bigg|~\|\nu\|:=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|\nu(i)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}<\infty\right\}

by sending
\mu
to
\mu/\sqrt{\pi}
. Hence
\ell^{2}(\pi)
is separable.
In conclusion,
P
is a bounded, self-adjoint, linear operator on the separable space
\ell^{2}(\pi)
.

2.2.
Spectral theory

Definition 2.1

(The spectrum of a linear operator)
.

Let
T
be a linear operator on a complex Hilbert space
X
.
The spectrum denoted by
\sigma(T)
is
defined by

\sigma(T):=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:(\lambda I-T)\text{ is not bijective }\}.

The spectrum
T
is divided into
3
disjoint subsets.

(a)

The point spectrum, or the set of all eigenvalues of
T
, is defined by

\sigma_{p}(T)={\mathrm{EV}}(T)=\{\lambda\in\sigma(T):{\mathrm{Ker}}(\lambda I-T)\neq 0\}.

(b)

The continuous spectrum of
T
is the set defined by

\sigma_{c}(T)=\{\lambda\in\sigma(T):{\mathrm{Ker}}(\lambda I-T)=0,\text{ and }\overline{{\mathrm{Im}}(\lambda I-T)}=X\}.

(c)

The residual spectrum of
T
is the set defined by

\sigma_{r}(T)=\{\lambda\in\sigma(T):{\mathrm{Ker}}(\lambda I-T)=0\text{ and }\overline{{\mathrm{Im}}(\lambda I-T)}\subsetneq X\}.

Theorem 2.2

(Spectral theorem)
.

Let
T
be a bounded self-adjoint linear operator on an infinite dimensional separable complex Hilbert space
X
. Then

(1)

\sigma(T)
is a closed subset in
B(0,\|T\|)
;

(2)

\sigma_{r}(T)=\emptyset
;

(3)

all the eigenvalues are real;

(4)

eigenvectors associated with different eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Assume further that
T
is compact, then

(5)

all eigenspaces are finite dimensional;

(6)

For any
r>0
, there are only finitely many eigenvalues of
T
with absolute value greater than
r
.

(7)

\sigma(T)=\{0\}\cup\sigma_{p}(T)
and at least one of

-\|T\|,\|T\|
is an eigenvalue of
T
;

(8)

Arrange all eigenvalues by their absolute value:
\|T\|=|\lambda_{0}|\geq|\lambda_{1}|\geq|\lambda_{2}|\geq\cdots
.
Then

\ell^{2}(\pi)=\overline{\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}V_{\lambda_{i}}},

where
V_{\lambda_{i}}
are eigenspaces associated with
\lambda_{i}
.

For the completeness, we add some details of proof (see
[
11
,
12
]
).

Proof.

(1)

For
|\lambda|>\|T\|
,
\lambda I-T
is invertible with

\left(I-\frac{T}{\lambda}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{T}{\lambda}\right)^{k}.

If
\lambda I-T
is invertible, for any
\gamma\in B(\lambda,\|\lambda I-T\|)
,
\gamma I-T
is also invertible since

\gamma I-T=(\lambda I-T)(I+(\gamma-\lambda)(\lambda I-T)^{-1}).

(2)

See
[
12
, Theorem 9.2-4]
.

(3)

For non-zero element
x\in V_{\lambda}
, we have
\lambda\langle x,x\rangle=\langle Tx,x\rangle=\langle x,Tx\rangle=\overline{\lambda}\langle x,x\rangle
.

(4)

For non-zero elements
x\in V_{\lambda}
and
y\in V_{\gamma}
, we have
\lambda\langle x,y\rangle=\gamma\langle x,y\rangle
.

(5)

V_{\lambda}
is a closed subspace of
X
, hence a Hilbert space. Since
T/\lambda=I
is compact on
V_{\lambda}
, so is
\|\cdot\|
. Then the unit ball in
V_{\lambda}
is compact, hence
\dim V_{\lambda}<\infty
.

(6)

Suppose there are infinite eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
with
|\lambda_{i}|\geq r>0
.
For each
\lambda_{j}
, take
x_{j}\in V_{\lambda_{j}}
with
\|x_{j}\|=1
.
Since
\|Tx_{i}-Tx_{j}\|=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}^{2}+\lambda_{j}^{2}}>r
,
\{Tx_{i}\}
has no Cauchy subsequence.
It contradict to the compactness of
T
.

(7)

Recall the Fredholm alternative: suppose that
T:X\rightarrow X
is a compact operator on a Banach space
X
, and
\lambda\neq 0
.
Then
\lambda I-T
is surjective if and only if it is injective.
In particular, if
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue of
T
, and
\lambda\neq 0
, then
\lambda I-T
is an isomorphism.
On the other hand, if
0\notin\sigma(T)
, then
T,T^{-1}
, and
I=TT^{-1}
are compact.
This contradict to the assumption that
X
is infinite dimensional.
Recall that,

\|T\|=\sup_{\|x\|=1}\langle Tx,x\rangle\text{ or }-\inf_{\|x\|=1}\langle Tx,x\rangle.

In the first case, there is a sequence
x_{n}
, all with norm
1
, such that
\langle Tx,x\rangle\rightarrow\|T\|
, and then
Tx_{n}-\|T\|x_{n}\rightarrow 0
.
There must be a limit point
x
such that
Tx=\|T\|x
, i.e.
\|T\|
is an eigenvalue of
T
.
The second case deduce that
-\|T\|
is an eigenvalue of
T
.

(8)

We shall prove that
W:=\left(\oplus_{i\geq 0}V_{\lambda_{i}}\right)^{\perp}=\{0\}
. If not, then
\sigma(T|_{W})=\{0\}
without any eigenvalues.
Hence
T|_{W}=0
, i.e.,
\{0\}\neq W\subset{\mathrm{Ker}}~T
which means that
0
is an eigenvalue. Then it contradict to
W\perp{\mathrm{Ker}}~T
.

∎

1.1
, this theorem is a generalization of fact 3 in
[
1
]
.

Proof of theorem
1.1
.

Since
P
is irreducible, there is a unique stationary distribution
\pi
.
It is an eigenvector associated with the eigenvalue
1
.
Note that for any
\mu\in V_{\lambda},\lambda\neq 1
,
\mu\mathbf{1}=0
since
\mu P\mathbf{1}=\mu\mathbf{1}=\lambda\mu\mathbf{1}
. Now we prove that
\lambda_{1}\neq-1
.
If not, we take a nonzero eigenvector
\mu=\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}
where
\mu_{\pm}>0
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{+}(i)=1=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{-}(i)
since
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu(i)=0
. Then by the basic limit theorem,
\mu=(-1)^{2n}\mu=\mu P^{2n}=\mu_{+}P^{2n}-\mu_{-}P^{2n}\rightarrow\pi-\pi=0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Similarly, we know that
V_{1}=\langle\pi\rangle
.

Using the spectral theorem, for any
\mu\in\ell^{2}(\pi)
, we write
\mu=\sum_{i\geq 0}\mu_{i},\mu_{i}\in V_{\lambda_{i}}
. Note that
\mu_{0}=(\mu\cdot\mathbf{1})\pi
. Now we have

\|\mu P^{n}-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{n}\mu_{i}\|_{\pi}^{2}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{2n}\|\mu_{i}\|_{\pi}^{2}=\lambda_{k+1}^{2n}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\|\mu_{i}\|_{\pi}^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{k+1}}\right)^{2n}\right)\leq(\|\mu\|_{\pi}^{2}-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\|\mu_{i}\|_{\pi}^{2})\cdot\lambda_{k+1}^{2n}.

Thus

\|\mu P^{n}-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{n}\mu_{i}\|_{tv}=O(|\lambda_{k+1}|^{n}).

In particular, if
|\lambda_{1}|>|\lambda_{2}|
, we have

\|\mu P^{n}-\pi-\lambda_{1}^{n}\mu_{1}\|_{tv}=O(|\lambda_{2}|^{n}).

Then

\|\mu P^{n}-\pi\|_{tv}=\|\mu_{1}\|_{tv}\cdot|\lambda_{1}|^{n}+O(|\lambda_{2}|^{n}).

If
|\lambda_{1}|=|\lambda_{2}|
, we have

\|\mu P^{n}-\mu_{0}\|_{tv}=(\|\mu_{1}+(-1)^{n}\mu_{2}\|_{tv})\cdot|\lambda_{1}|^{n}+O(|\lambda_{3}|^{n}),\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

3.
Hilbert-Schmidt Markov chains

Definition 3.1
.

A bounded linear operator
T
on a separable Hilbert space
X
is Hilbert-Schmidt if there is an orthonormal basis
\{e_{n}:n\geq 0\}
such that
\|T\|_{\mathcal{HS}}:=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\|Te_{n}\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty
.

Hilbert-Schmidt operator is compact.
In fact, let
P_{N}
be the projection onto the finite dimension space spanned by
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{N}\}
. Then
P_{N}T
is compact (finite rank operator) and converge to
T
uniformly.

Let
\{\mu_{j}\}
be an orthonormal basis consist of eigenvectors. Note that

1=\|\mu_{j}\|^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\langle e_{n},\mu_{j}\rangle^{2}\text{ and }1=\|e_{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{j}\langle e_{k},\mu_{j}\rangle^{2}.

On the other hand, denoting
d_{i}=\dim V_{\lambda_{i}}
, then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|Te_{n}\|^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}^{2}\langle e_{n},\mu_{j}\rangle^{2}=\sum_{i}^{\infty}d_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2},

So
T
is Hilbert-Schmidt if and only if
T
is compact and
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}<\infty
. In particular,
\|T\|_{\mathcal{HS}}^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
P
be an irreducible, aperiodic, and reversible transition matrix with respect to
\pi
.
Then
P
is called Hilbert-Schmidt if it is Hilbert-Schmidt as an operator on
\ell^{2}(\pi)
.

For any two real numbers
q>1
and
m>-1
, consider the following transition matrix

P_{m}(i,j)=\begin{cases}q^{-1-2i-m},&\mbox{if }j=i+1\\
1-(1-q^{-i})(1-q^{-m-i})-q^{-1-2i-m},&\mbox{if }j=i\\
(1-q^{-i})(1-q^{-m-i}),&\mbox{if }j=i-1\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It is irreducible and aperiodic since
P_{m}(i,i)>0
for all
i\geq 0
.
Define
\pi_{m}
by

\pi_{m}(i)=\frac{\theta_{m}(q)}{q^{i(i+m)}\eta_{i}(q)\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-q^{-m-j})},\text{ where }\eta_{k}(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-q^{-i}),

and

\theta_{m}(q)^{-1}:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{i(i+m)}\eta_{i}(q)\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-q^{-m-j})}<\infty

Then
P_{m}
is also reversible with respect to
\pi_{m}
.
Now we prove that
P_{m}
is Hilbert-Schmidt.

Proof of theorem
1.2
.

Firstly, we prove that for any
k\geq 1
,
q^{-k}
is an eigenvalue.
Define
\pi_{m}\circ q^{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq}0}
as follows.

(\pi_{m}\circ q^{i})(k):=\pi_{m}(k)\cdot q^{ik}.

It’s easy to check that
\pi_{m}\circ q^{i}\in\ell^{2}(\pi_{m})
.
Now, we claim that
there exists some
a_{0},\cdots,a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\cdot(\pi_{m}\circ q^{i})
is a eigenvector associated with
q^{-k}
.
If so, for any
l\geq 0
, it must have

\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\cdot(\pi_{m}\circ q^{i})P_{m}\right)(l)=q^{-k}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\cdot\pi_{m}(l)\cdot q^{il}\right)

By the reversibility, it is equivalent to

\sum_{j=l-1}^{l+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i}a_{i}q^{ij}\right)P_{m}(l,j)=q^{-k}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}q^{il}\right)

Since
\sum_{j=l-1}^{l+1}P_{m}(l,j)=1
, we have

P_{m}(l,l-1)\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}(q^{-i}-1)q^{il}+P_{m}(l,l+1)\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}(q^{i}-1)q^{il}=\left(q^{-k}-1\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}q^{il}\right),

i.e.,

\left(1-(1+q^{-m})q^{-l}+q^{-m-2l}\right)\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}(q^{-i}-1)q^{il}+q^{-1-m-2l}\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}(q^{i}-1)q^{il}=\left(q^{-k}-1\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}q^{il}\right)

Compare the coefficient of
q^{il}
in two sides and denote
a_{k+1}=a_{k+2}=0
formally. Then

(q^{-i}-q^{-k})a_{i}-(1+q^{-m})(q^{-1-i}-1)a_{i+1}+(q^{-2-i-m}-q^{-m}+q^{i+1-m}-q^{-1-m})a_{i+2}=0,~0\leq i\leq k.

Hence, there are unique solution of
\{a_{0},\cdots,a_{k}\}
up to scalar.

Now we will prove these eigenvectors can generate whole space
\ell^{2}(\pi_{m})
.

One can find that
vP_{m}=\lambda v
has only one solution up to scalar, i.e. all
V_{\lambda}
are
1
-dimensional.
Then the
P
-invariant subspace

V:=\langle\pi\circ q^{i},i\geq 0\rangle=\overline{\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}V_{q^{-i}}}.

Now it is sufficient to show that
\delta_{0}\in V
, where for any
i\geq 0
,
\delta_{i}\in\ell^{2}(\pi_{m})
is given by
\delta_{i}(k)=1
if
k=i
and
0
otherwise.
In fact, if
\delta_{0}\in V
,
\delta_{1}
will belong to
V
since it’s a linear combination of
\delta_{0}
and
\delta_{0}P
.
By induction argument, all
\delta_{i}
, generators of
\ell^{2}(\pi_{m})
, lie in
V
.
From the identity of
q
-series (due to Euler
[
13
, eq(19)]
), we have

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}b_{i}t^{i}:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{\prod_{j=1}^{i}(q^{j}-1)}t^{i}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{-k}t\right).

Take
c_{k}=\frac{b_{k}}{\eta_{\infty}(q)\pi_{m}(0)}
.
We claim that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{k}(\pi_{0}\circ q^{k})=\delta_{0}\in\ell^{2}(\pi_{m}).

i.e.,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\pi_{m}(0)\cdot\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_{k}-\pi_{m}(0)^{-1}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\eta_{\infty}(q)^{2}\pi_{m}(0)^{2}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\pi_{m}(i)\cdot\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}q^{ki}\right|^{2}=0.

By the definition of
c_{k}
, the first summand converge to
0
.
It suffices to show

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}+mi}}\cdot\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}q^{ki}\right|^{2}=0.

Since
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}q^{ki}=0
,we have

\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}q^{ki}\right|=\left|\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}b_{k}q^{ki}\right|.

Note that

\left|\frac{b_{k}q^{ki}}{b_{k-1}q^{(k-1)i}}\right|=\frac{q^{i}}{q^{k}-1}.

According to the property of alternating series, we have

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}+mi}}\cdot\left|\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}b_{k}q^{ki}\right|^{2}&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}+mi}}b_{N+1}^{2}q^{2(N+1)i}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{\infty}(q)^{2}}\frac{1}{q^{(N+1)(N+2)}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}+mi}}q^{2(N+1)i}\\
&\ll\frac{1}{q^{(N+1)(N+2)}}\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}q^{2(N+1)i-mi-i^{2}}.\end{split}

Define
F(N):=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}q^{2Ni-mi-i^{2}}
and
f(N)=\frac{F(N)}{q^{N(N+1)}}
. Then

F(N+1)=1+q^{2N+1-m}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}q^{2N(i-1)-m(i-1)-(i-1)^{2}}=1+q^{2N+1-m}F(N).

Hence,

f(N+1)=\frac{1}{q^{(N+1)(N+2)}}+\frac{f(N)}{q^{m+1}}

and
f(N)\rightarrow 0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

On the other hand,

\begin{split}\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}+mi}}\cdot\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}q^{ki}\right|^{2}&\leq\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}+mi}}b_{N}^{2}q^{2Ni}\\
&\ll\sum_{i=N+1}^{\infty}q^{-i^{2}-mi+2Ni-N(N+1)}\\
&\ll\frac{1}{q^{(m+1)N}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}q^{-(i+m/2)^{2}}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }N\rightarrow\infty.\end{split}

Hence
\{\pi\circ q^{i}|i\geq 0\}
form an orthogonal basis of
\ell^{2}(\pi_{m})
and
\|P_{m}\|_{\mathcal{HS}}^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}q^{-2k}=(1-q^{-2})^{-1}
.
Therefore
P_{m}
is Hilbert-Schmidt.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

From a numerical perspective, we can study eigenvalues using the truncation method
[
6
]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
m>-1
be a real number. If
f(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{i}z^{i}
satisfying
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{i}^{2}q^{i^{2}+mi}<\infty
and for any
k\geq 0
,
f(q^{k})=0
, then
f=0
.

Proof.

Let
\mu=(\cdots,\mu_{i},\cdots)
. Note that

\frac{1}{q^{k(k+m)}}\ll\pi_{m}(k)\ll\frac{1}{q^{k(k+m)}}.

So
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{i}^{2}q^{i^{2}+im}<\infty
if and only if
\mu\in\ell^{2}(\pi_{m})
. On the other hand,
f(q^{k})=0,\forall k\geq 0
is equivalent to

\langle\mu,\pi_{m}\circ q^{i}\rangle_{\pi_{m}}=0
for all
i\geq 0
. Hence,

\mu\in\langle\pi_{m}\circ q^{i}|i\geq 0\rangle^{\perp}=\ell^{2}(\pi_{m})^{\perp}=\{0\}.

∎

Remark 3.5
.

(1)

It is equivalent to say that
the infinite Vandermonde matrix
\mathrm{Van}(1,q,q^{2},q^{3},\cdots)
is injective on
\ell^{2}(\pi_{m})
.

(2)

Unlike the conclusion of Carlson’s theorem
[
5
]
in complex analysis, the main difficulties here are that we cannot control the growth of
f(z)
and
q^{k}
are too sparse.

(3)

If
m<-1
, the lemma is not true. Recall that

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}b_{i}q^{i}\cdot t^{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}q^{i}}{\prod_{j=1}^{i}(q^{j}-1)}t^{i}=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{-k}t\right).

Take
\mu_{i}=b_{i}q^{i}
. Then
f\neq 0
but we always have

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{i}^{2}q^{i^{2}+im}<\infty
for any
m<-1
.

4.
Corank distribution of random matrices

In this section, we will investigate specific Markov chains that arise from the corank distributions of different types of matrices over finite fields. These problems have been extensively studied in many papers (see
[
2
,
14
]
). After proving that these Markov chains are all Hilbert-Schmidt, we can deduce the asymptotic expressions for the corank distributions of these matrices from the earlier theorem.

4.1.
Uniform case

Let
q
be a prime power and
m
a nonnegative integer. The first example we consider is chosen uniformly from all
n\times(n+m)
matrices over a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
Define
\mathrm{corank}~M=n-\mathrm{rank}~M
. From
[
14
, Section 1]
, we know that

{\mathrm{Prob}}\left(\mathrm{corank}~M=k~|~M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n\times(n+m)}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})\right)=(\delta_{0}P_{m}^{n})(k),

and that

\pi_{m}(i)=\frac{\eta_{\infty}(q)}{q^{i(i+m)}\eta_{i}(q)\eta_{i+m}(q)}.

(one can verify that this
\pi_{m}
is the same as the one defined in section 3).
According to theorems
1.2
and
1.1
, we get the corollary
1.5
.

Proof of corollary
1.5
.

By the theorem
1.2
, we know that the first non-one eigenvalue of
P_{m}
is
q^{-1}
, and

\nu:=\pi_{m}-\frac{\pi_{m}\circ q}{1+q^{-m}}

is an associated eigenvector (unique up to scalar since
V_{q^{-1}}
is
1
-dimensional). Recall that the
k
-moment of
\pi_{m}
is defined by
M(\pi_{m},k):=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\pi_{m}(i)q^{ki}
. It is known that (see
[
15
, Example 6.6]
)

M(\pi_{m},0)=1,M(\pi_{m},1)=1+q^{-m},M(\pi_{m},2)=1+(q+1)q^{-m}+q^{-2m}.

Then we have

\langle\nu,\nu\rangle_{\pi_{m}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\pi_{m}(i)-\frac{2}{1+q^{-m}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\pi_{m}(i)q^{i}+\frac{1}{(1+q^{-m})^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\pi_{m}(i)q^{2i}=\frac{(q-1)q^{m}}{(q^{m}+1)^{2}}

Thus the
q^{-1}
-component of
\delta_{0}
is

(\delta_{0})_{q^{-1}}=\frac{\langle\delta_{0},\nu\rangle_{\pi_{m}}}{\langle\nu,\nu\rangle_{\pi_{m}}}\nu=\frac{(q^{m}+1)^{-1}}{\langle\nu,\nu\rangle_{\pi_{m}}}\nu=\frac{q^{m}+1}{(q-1)q^{m}}\nu.

On the other hand, note that
\nu(0)>0
and
\nu(i)\leq 0
for any
i>0
. So
\|\nu\|_{tv}=\frac{2\pi_{m}(0)}{q^{m}+1}
since
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\nu(i)=0
.
Therefore

\|(\delta_{0})_{q^{-1}}\|_{tv}=\frac{2\pi_{m}(0)}{(q-1)q^{m}}

∎

Remark 4.1
.

(1)

In
[
2
]
, Jason Fulman and Larry Goldstein prove that (note that
\|\cdot\|_{tv}=2\|\cdot\|_{TV}
.)

\frac{1}{4q^{n+m+1}}\leq\|\delta_{0}P_{m}^{n}-\pi_{m}\|_{tv}\leq\frac{6}{q^{n+m+1}}.

(2)

Note that
M(\pi_{m},k)=(\pi_{m}\circ q^{k})\mathbf{1}
and
a_{0}\pi_{m}+a_{1}(\pi_{m}\circ q)\in V_{q^{-1}}
for some
a_{0},a_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. According to the fact that
V_{\lambda}\perp\mathbf{1}
if
\lambda\neq 1
, we can get
M(\pi_{m},1)
and by induction,
M(\pi_{m},k)
.

This distribution is closely related to the model of Cohen-Lenstra heuristic for ideal class groups
[
7
]
.

Proof of corollary
1.6
.

Recall that the
p^{i}
-rank of
G
is given by

\mathrm{rk}_{p^{i}}G=\dim_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}p^{i-1}G/p^{i}G.

By
[
9
, Proposition 1]
, or
[
10
, Proposition 14.1]
(they prove the case
m=0
and
1
and the proof of general situation is the same),

\begin{split}&\mu\left(\left\{M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n\times(n+m)}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\ \big|\ \mathrm{coker}~M\simeq G\right\}\right)\\
&=\frac{w_{m}(G)}{\pi_{m}(\mathrm{rk}_{p}(G))}\cdot{\mathrm{Prob}}(\mathrm{corank}~\overline{M}=\mathrm{rk}_{p}(G)~|~\overline{M}\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n\times(n+m)}({\mathbb{F}}_{p}))\\
&=\frac{w_{m}(G)}{\pi_{m}(\mathrm{rk}_{p}(G))}\cdot\left(\pi_{m}(\mathrm{rk}_{p}(G))+\frac{p^{m}+1}{(p-1)p^{m}}\nu(\mathrm{rk}_{p}(G))+O(p^{-2n})\right).\end{split}

∎

4.2.
Skew centrosymmetric case

Here we assume
q
is odd.
Consider the skew centrosymmetric matrices, i.e.,
{\mathrm{Mat}}^{\mathrm{scs}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}):=\left\{M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}):M_{ij}=-M_{ji}=M_{n+1-j,n+1-i}\right\}
.
It is known that the rank is even (see
[
2
]
).
Actually, we have

{\mathrm{Prob}}(\mathrm{corank}~M=2k|M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{2n}^{\mathrm{scs}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}))={\mathrm{Prob}}(\mathrm{corank}~M=k|M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}))

{\mathrm{Prob}}(\mathrm{corank}~M=2k+1|M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{2n+1}^{\mathrm{scs}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}))={\mathrm{Prob}}(\mathrm{corank}~M=k|M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n\times(n+1)}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}))

Therefore, this corank distribution follows from the uniform case.

4.3.
Symmetric case

Let
q
be a prime power. Now we discuss symmetric matrices, i.e.,
{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}^{\mathrm{sym}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}):=\left\{M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}):M^{T}=M\right\}
.
Similarly, from
[
14
]
, we have

{\mathrm{Prob}}\left(\mathrm{corank}~M=k~|~M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}^{\mathrm{sym}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})\right)=(\delta_{0}P_{\mathrm{sym}}^{n})(k).

Here

P_{\mathrm{sym}}(i,j)=\begin{cases}q^{-i-1},&\mbox{if }j=i+1\\
q^{-i}-q^{-i-1},&\mbox{if }j=i\\
1-q^{-i},&\mbox{if }j=i-1\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

and

\pi_{\mathrm{sym}}(n)=\frac{\alpha(q)}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(q^{i}-1)},\text{ where }\alpha(q)=\prod_{i=1,\text{odd}}^{\infty}(1-q^{-i}).

Theorem 4.2
.

P_{\mathrm{sym}}
is Hilbert-Schmidt on
\ell^{2}(\pi_{\mathrm{sym}})
and
{\mathrm{EV}}(P_{\mathrm{sym}})=\left\{\pm q^{-k}:k\geq 0\right\}
.

Proof.

Similarly, the eigenvectors associated with eigenvalue
\pm q^{-k}
can be obtained as a linear combination of vectors
\{\pi_{m}\circ(\pm q^{i}):i=0,\cdots,k\}
.
Now it remains to prove that if
\mu\in\ell^{2}(\pi_{\mathrm{sym}})
(i.e.,
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu(i)^{2}q^{\frac{i^{2}}{2}}<\infty
) such that
\mu\perp(\pi_{\mathrm{sym}}\circ(\pm q^{i}))
(i.e.,
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu(i)(\pm 1)^{i}q^{ki}=0
), for all
i
, then
\mu=0
. In other word, we have that for any
k\geq 0
,

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu(2i)(q^{2})^{ki}=0,\quad\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu(2i+1)(q^{2})^{ki}=0.

Note that

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu(2i)^{2}(q^{2})^{i^{2}}<\infty,~\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu(2i+1)^{2}(q^{2})^{i^{2}}<\infty.

So by the lemma
3.4
,
\mu=0
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

\|\delta_{0}P_{\mathrm{sym}}^{n}-\pi_{\mathrm{sym}}\|_{tv}=\begin{cases}\frac{2q\cdot\alpha(q)}{q^{2}-1}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{n}}+O\left(\frac{1}{q^{2n}}\right),&\mbox{if }n\text{ even}\\
\frac{2q\cdot\alpha(q)}{(q^{2}-1)(q-1)}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{n}}+O\left(\frac{1}{q^{2n}}\right),&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

where the implicit constant is less than
\alpha(q)^{-1}
.

Proof.

Similarly, we have

\nu_{+}:=\pi_{\mathrm{sym}}-\frac{1}{2}(\pi_{\mathrm{sym}}\circ q)\in V_{q^{-1}},\quad\nu_{-}:=\pi_{\mathrm{sym}}\circ(-q)\in V_{-q^{-1}}.

For the
k
-moment,

M(\pi_{\mathrm{sym}},0)=1,~M(\pi_{\mathrm{sym}},1)=2,~M(\pi_{\mathrm{sym}},2)=2+2q.

\langle\nu_{+},\nu_{+}\rangle_{\pi_{\mathrm{sym}}}=\frac{q-1}{2},~(\delta_{0})_{+}=\frac{1}{q-1}\nu_{+}.

\langle\nu_{-},\nu_{-}\rangle_{\pi_{\mathrm{sym}}}=2+2q,~(\delta_{0})_{-}=\frac{1}{2(q+1)}\nu_{-}.

Then

\|(\delta_{0})_{+}+(-1)^{n}(\delta_{0})_{-}\|_{tv}=\begin{cases}\frac{2q\cdot\alpha(q)}{q^{2}-1},&\mbox{if }n\text{ even}\\
\frac{2q\cdot\alpha(q)}{(q^{2}-1)(q-1)},&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

∎

4.4.
Alternating case

For alternating matrices (skew symmetric matrices), i.e. the set
{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}^{\mathrm{alt}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}):=\left\{M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}):M^{T}=-M,\text{ and the diagonal elements of $M$ are zero}\right\}
.
It is well-known that the rank is even (see
[
2
,
14
]
).
By calculation, we have

{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{corank}~M=2j+1~|~M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{2n+1}^{{\mathrm{alt}}})=(\delta_{0}P_{\mathrm{alt}}^{n})(j).

and

{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{corank}~M=2j~|~M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{2n}^{{\mathrm{alt}}})=(\delta_{0}Q_{\mathrm{alt}}^{n})(j).

Here

P_{\mathrm{alt}}(i,j)=\begin{cases}q^{-4i-3},&\mbox{if }j=i+1\\
1-q^{-4i-3}-(1-q^{-2i})(1-q^{-2i-1}),&\mbox{if }j=i\\
(1-q^{-2i})(1-q^{-2i-1}),&\mbox{if }j=i-1\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Q_{\mathrm{alt}}(i,j)=\begin{cases}q^{-4i-1},&\mbox{if }j=i+1\\
1-q^{-4i-1}-(1-q^{-2i})(1-q^{-2i+1}),&\mbox{if }j=i\\
(1-q^{-2i})(1-q^{-2i+1}),&\mbox{if }j=i-1\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

and

\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}(j)=\frac{\alpha(q)}{q^{2j^{2}+j}\eta_{2j+1}(q)},\quad\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}^{\prime}(j)=\frac{\alpha(q)}{q^{2j^{2}-j}\eta_{2j}(q)}.

Theorem 4.4
.

P_{\mathrm{alt}}
(resp.
Q_{\mathrm{alt}}
) is Hilbert-Schmidt on
\ell^{2}(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}})
(resp.
\ell^{2}(\pi^{\prime}_{\mathrm{alt}})
) and
{\mathrm{EV}}(P_{\mathrm{alt}})={\mathrm{EV}}(Q_{\mathrm{alt}})=\left\{q^{-2k}:k\geq 0\right\}
.

Proof.

The eigenvectors of
P_{\mathrm{alt}}
associated with eigenvalue
q^{-2k}
can be obtained as a linear combination of vectors
\{\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}\circ(q^{i}):i=0,\cdots,k\}
. Now it remains to prove that if

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu(i)^{2}(q^{2})^{i^{2}+i/2}<\infty\text{ and }\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu(i)(q^{2})^{ki}=0,

then
\mu=0
. This can be directly inferred from the lemma
3.4
. The proof of
Q_{\mathrm{alt}}
is similar.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

\|\delta_{0}P_{\mathrm{alt}}^{n}-\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}\|_{tv}=\frac{2\alpha(q)}{(q-1)^{2}(q+1)}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{2n}}+O\left(\frac{1}{q^{4n}}\right),

\|\delta_{0}Q_{\mathrm{alt}}^{n}-\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}^{\prime}\|_{tv}=\frac{\alpha(q)q}{(q-1)(q+1)}\frac{1}{q^{2n}}+O\left(\frac{1}{q^{4n}}\right),

where the implicit constants are less than
\eta_{1}(q)\alpha(q)^{-1}
and
\alpha(q)^{-1}
respectively.

Proof.

For
P_{\mathrm{alt}}
, we can find
\nu:=\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}-\frac{q}{q+1}(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}\circ q^{2})\in V_{q^{-2}}
and

(1+q^{2})(1+q^{-1})(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}-\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}\circ q^{2})+\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}\circ q^{4}\in V_{q^{-4}}

For the
k
-moment,

M(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}},0)=1,~M(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}},2)=1+q^{-1},~M(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}},4)=(1+q^{2})(1+q^{-1})q^{-1}.

Then

\langle\nu,\nu\rangle_{\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}}=q(q-1)(q+1)^{-1}\text{ and }\|\nu\|_{tv}=2\nu(0)=\frac{2\alpha(q)}{(q+1)\eta_{1}(q)}.

Then the
q^{-2}
-component of
\delta_{0}
is

(\delta_{0})_{q^{-2}}=q^{-1}(q-1)^{-1}\nu

and

\|(\delta_{0})_{q^{-2}}\|_{tv}=\frac{2\alpha(q)}{(q-1)^{2}(q+1)}.

For
Q_{\mathrm{alt}}
, we can find
\nu^{\prime}:=\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}-\frac{1}{q+1}(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}^{\prime}\circ q^{2})\in V_{q^{-2}}
and

\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{q}(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}^{\prime}\circ q^{2})+\frac{1}{q(q+1)(q^{2}+1)}(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}^{\prime}\circ q^{4})\in V_{q^{-4}}.

For the
k
-moment,

M(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}^{\prime},0)=1,~M(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}^{\prime},2)=q+1,~M(\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}^{\prime},4)=(q+1)(q^{2}+1).

Then

\langle\nu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}\rangle_{\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}^{\prime}}=\frac{q(q-1)}{q+1}\text{ and }\|\nu^{\prime}\|_{tv}=\frac{\alpha(q)q}{q+1}.

Then the
q^{-2}
-component of
\delta_{0}
is

(\delta_{0})_{q^{-2}}=(q-1)^{-1}\nu^{\prime}

and

\|(\delta_{0})_{q^{-2}}\|_{tv}=\frac{\alpha(q)q}{(q-1)(q+1)}.

∎

4.5.
Hermitian case

Let
q
be a power of odd prime.
Suppose that
\theta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}},\theta^{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, but
\theta\notin{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
(see
[
2
]
).
Then any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
can be written as
\alpha=a+b\theta
with
a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
By the conjugate of
\alpha
, we mean
\overline{\alpha}=a-b\theta
.
For
M=(\alpha_{ij})\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}})
, denote
M^{\ast}=(\overline{\alpha_{ij}})^{T}
.
Then
A
is said to be Hermitian if and only if
M^{\ast}=M
.
Write
{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}^{\mathrm{her}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}})=\left\{M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}):M^{\ast}=M\right\}

{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{corank}~M=k~|~M\in{\mathrm{Mat}}_{n}^{{\mathrm{her}}}({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}))=(\delta_{0}P_{\mathrm{her}}^{n})(k).

Here

P_{\mathrm{her}}(i,j)=\begin{cases}q^{-2i-1},&\mbox{if }j=i+1\\
q^{-2i}(1-q^{-1}),&\mbox{if }j=i\\
1-q^{-2i},&\mbox{if }j=i-1\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

and

\pi_{\mathrm{her}}(j)=\frac{\beta(q)}{q^{j^{2}}\eta_{j}(q^{2})},\text{ where }\beta(q)=\prod_{i=1,\text{odd}}^{\infty}(1+q^{-i})^{-1}.

Theorem 4.6
.

P_{\mathrm{her}}
is Hilbert-Schmidt on
\ell^{2}(\pi_{\mathrm{her}})
and
{\mathrm{EV}}(P_{\mathrm{her}})=\left\{(-q)^{-k}:k\geq 0\right\}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar. We need to show

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}}}\cdot\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}^{\prime}(-q)^{ki}\right|^{2}=0,\text{ where }b_{k}^{\prime}:=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}((-q)^{j}-1)}.

It suffices to prove

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1,odd}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}}}\cdot\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}^{\prime}(-q)^{ki}\right|^{2}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1,even}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}}}\cdot\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}^{\prime}(-q)^{ki}\right|^{2}=0

When
i
is odd,

{\mathrm{sign}}(b_{k}^{\prime}(-q)^{ki})\text{ is }\begin{cases}>0,&\mbox{if }k\equiv 3,4\bmod 4\\
<0,&\mbox{if }k\equiv 1,2\bmod 4.\end{cases}

Note that

\left|\frac{b_{k}^{\prime}(-q)^{ki}}{b_{k-1}^{\prime}(-q)^{(k-1)i}}\right|=\frac{q^{i}}{q^{k}-(-1)^{k}}.

According to the property of alternating series, we have

\sum_{i=1,odd}^{N}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}}}\cdot\left|\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}b_{k}^{\prime}(-q)^{ki}\right|^{2}\ll\frac{1}{q^{(N+1)(N+2)}}\sum_{i=1,odd}^{N}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}}}q^{2(N+1)i}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }N\rightarrow\infty.

and

\sum_{i=N+1,odd}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q^{i^{2}}}\cdot\left|\sum_{k=0}^{N}b_{k}^{\prime}(-q)^{ki}\right|^{2}\ll\frac{1}{q^{N}}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }N\rightarrow\infty.

The estimation for
i
even part is similar.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

\|\delta_{0}P_{\mathrm{her}}^{n}-\pi_{\mathrm{her}}\|_{tv}=\frac{2\beta(q)}{(q+1)\alpha(q^{2})}\cdot\frac{1}{q^{n}}+O\left(\frac{1}{q^{2n}}\right),

where the implicit constant is less than
\beta(q)^{-1}
.

Proof.

Similarly,
\nu:=\pi_{\mathrm{her}}\circ(-q)
is an eigenvector associated with
-q^{-1}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{her}}-\frac{1}{q+1}(\pi_{\mathrm{her}}\circ q^{2})\in V_{q^{-2}}
.
By calculation,
\langle\nu,\nu\rangle_{\pi_{\mathrm{her}}}=q+1
and
\|\nu\|_{tv}=\frac{2\beta(q)}{\alpha(q^{2})}
.
The
-q^{-1}
-component of
\delta_{0}
is

(\delta_{0})_{-q^{-1}}=\frac{1}{(q+1)}\nu\text{ and }\|(\delta_{0})_{-q^{-1}}\|_{tv}=\frac{2\beta(q)}{(q+1)\alpha(q^{2})}.

∎
