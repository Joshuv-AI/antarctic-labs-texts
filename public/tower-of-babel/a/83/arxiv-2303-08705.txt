Intrinsic optical absorption in Dirac metals
Adamya P. Goyal, Prachi Sharma, Dmitrii L. Maslov

Abstract
A Dirac metal is a doped (gated) Dirac material with the Fermi energy ( E F E_{\text{F}} ) lying either in the conduction or valence bands. In the non-interacting picture, optical absorption in gapless Dirac metals occurs only if the frequency of incident photons ( Ω \Omega ) exceeds the direct (Pauli) frequency threshold, equal to 2 ​ E F 2E_{\text{F}} . In this work, we study, both analytically and numerically, the role of electron-electron ( ee ) and electron-hole ( eh ) interactions in optical absorption of two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) Dirac metals in the entire interval of frequencies below 2 ​ E F 2E_{\text{F}} . We show that, for Ω ≪ E F \Omega\ll E_{\text{F}} , the optical conductivity, ℜ ⁡ σ ⁡ ( Ω ) \Re\sigma(\Omega) , arising from the combination of ee and certain eh scattering processes, scales as Ω 2 ​ ln ⁡ Ω \Omega^{2}\ln\Omega in 2D and as Ω 2 \Omega^{2} in 3D, respectively, both for short-range (Hubbard) and long-range (screened Coulomb) interactions. Another type of eh processes, similar to Auger-Meitner (AM) processes in atomic physics, starts to contribute for Ω \Omega above the direct threshold, equal to E F E_{\text{F}} . Similar to the case of doped semiconductors with parabolic bands studied in prior literature, the AM contribution to ℜ ⁡ σ ⁡ ( Ω ) \Re\sigma(\Omega) in Dirac metals is manifested by a threshold singularity, ℜ ⁡ σ ⁡ ( Ω ) ∝ ( Ω − E F ) d + 2 \Re\sigma(\Omega)\propto(\Omega-E_{\text{F}})^{d+2} , where d d is the spatial dimensionality and 0 < Ω − E F ≪ E F 0<\Omega-E_{\text{F}}\ll E_{\text{F}} . In contrast to doped semiconductors, however, the AM contribution in Dirac metals is completely overshadowed by the ee and other eh contributions. Numerically, ℜ ⁡ σ ⁡ ( Ω ) \Re\sigma(\Omega) happens to be small in almost the entire range of Ω < 2 ​ E F \Omega<2E_{\text{F}} . This finding may have important consequences for collective modes in Dirac metals lying below 2 ​ E F 2E_{\text{F}} .

An earlier result of Ref.

A quadratic scaling of the conductivity can be understood as the consequence of partially broken Galilean invariance in a Dirac-Fermi liquid (DFL). Indeed,
the optical conductivity can be cast into a Drude-like form

\displaystyle\Re\sigma(\Omega)\propto\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}\tau_{j}(\Omega)},

(4)

where
\tau_{j}(\Omega)
is the current relaxation time. If Galilean invariance is broken completely, e.g., by umklapp scattering,
\tau_{j}(\Omega)
is of the same order as the quasiparticle lifetime in a Fermi liquid (FL):
\tau_{j}(\Omega)\sim\tau_{\text{qp}}(\Omega)\propto\Omega^{-2}
.
In this case, Eq. (
4

\Re\sigma(\Omega)=\text{const}
. On the other hand, if Galilean invariance is intact, current cannot be relaxed in
ee
collisions: although
\tau_{\text{qp}}(\Omega)
is finite,
\tau_{j}(\Omega)=\infty
and thus
\Re\sigma(\Omega)=0
. A DFL
occupies an intermediate niche between the two limits described above. On one hand, its non-parabolic spectrum allows for current relaxation; on the other hand, the spectrum is still isotropic (at low doping) and current relaxation is impossible for electrons right on the Fermi surface (FS)
Sharma et al. 2021
. For states away from the FS, the current relaxation time is finite but long,
\tau_{j}(\Omega)\propto\Omega^{-4}
(modulo a
\ln\Omega
factor in 2D), while
\tau_{\text{qp}}(\Omega)
still
scales in a FL way, i.e., as

\Omega^{-2}
.
According to Eq. (
4

1/\tau_{j}(\Omega)
translates into the quadratic scaling of the conductivity.

In this paper, we extend the results of Ref.
Sharma et al. 2021
to the entire interval of frequencies below
\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
.
Such an extension necessarily requires to account for both
ee
and
eh
interaction processes. We consider
2D and 3D Dirac metals
with two types of interaction: Hubbard and Coulomb.
Our analytic results follow from the analysis of the Kubo formula and are applicable in two regions: i) for
\Omega\ll\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
, where

\displaystyle\omega_{\text{I}}=E_{\text{F}},

(5)

is the “indirect” threshold and ii) just above the indirect threshold, i.e, for
\Omega\gtrapprox\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
.
In the rest of the interval
0<\Omega<\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
, the conductivity is calculated numerically, but only for a Dirac metal with Hubbard interaction.
For
\Omega\ll\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
, we show that the
eh
contribution to the conductivity scales as
\Omega^{2}
, i.e., it is comparable to the
ee
one found in Ref.
Sharma et al. 2021

ee
one in 2D, but only in the leading logarithm sense.

This
\Omega^{2}
-scaling of the
eh

4

eh
scattering is not limited by (partially broken) Galilean invariance, so that
\tau_{j}(\Omega)\sim\tau_{\text{qp}}(\Omega)\propto\Omega^{-2}
. [Unlike
\tau_{\text{qp}}(\Omega)

\tau_{j}(\Omega)
does not have an extra logarithmic factor in 2D.] However, the energies of electrons and holes differ now by

rather than
\Omega

\Omega^{2}
in Eq. (
4

, and the conductivity scales as
\Omega^{2}
.

Another channel of absorption due to
eh
interaction opens up when
\Omega
exceeds the indirect threshold
\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
[Eq. (
5

Since the seminal 1969 paper by Gavoret et al.
Gavoret et al. 1969

similar to Auger-Meitner (AM) processes in atomic physics

Meitner 1922
;

;
Uenoyama and Sham 1990
;
Hawrylak 1991
;
Pimenov et al. 2017
.
Although we consider only gapless systems,
our result for the AM contribution just above
\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
exhibits a threshold singularity of the same type as found for a gapped spectrum

;
Ruckenstein and Schmitt-Rink 1987
;
Uenoyama and Sham 1990
;
Hawrylak 1991
;
Pimenov et al. 2017

\displaystyle\Re\sigma(\Omega)\propto\theta(\delta\Omega)\delta\Omega^{\beta_{\text{A}}},

(6)

where

\delta\Omega\equiv\Omega-\omega_{\mathrm{I}}\ll\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
and
\beta_{\text{A}}=d+2
with
d
being the spatial dimensionality and

is the Heaviside step function.
Equation (
6

\Re\sigma(\Omega)\propto\delta\Omega\mathcal{N}(\delta\Omega)/\tau_{\text{qp}}(\delta\Omega)
, where
\mathcal{N}(\epsilon)\propto\epsilon^{d-1}
is the density of states of a gapless Dirac metal and

.

More important, however, is the fact that for a non-parabolic spectrum the AM contribution occurs at the background of
ee
and other
eh
contributions, which start at the lowest frequencies (as
\Omega^{2}
and
\Omega^{2}\ln\Omega
in 3D and 2D, respectively) and are still present both near and above
\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
. Therefore, the AM threshold singularity is masked by these other contributions. These competing contributions were not taken into account in the previous work on AM processes
Gavoret et al. 1969
;
Ruckenstein and Schmitt-Rink 1987
;
Uenoyama and Sham 1990
;
Hawrylak 1991
;
Pimenov et al. 2017
, which considered two strictly parabolic bands separated by a gap (
2\Delta
).
To clarify the difference in absorption by materials with parabolic and Dirac bands, we invoke temporarily a gapped Dirac spectrum,
\epsilon_{\textbf{k}}=\pm\sqrt{v_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}k^{2}+\Delta^{2}}
. A gapped semiconductor with parabolic conduction and valence band
can be viewed as the
\Delta\to\infty
limit of this spectrum.
In this case, intra-band
ee
interaction does not affect the conductivity due to Galilean invariance, as we already discussed above. Moreover, inter-band absorption accompanied by electron-hole conversion processes, i.e., processes that do not conserve the numbers of electrons and holes separately,
is also forbidden in the parabolic limit, because the corresponding eigenstates are either purely electron-like or purely hole-like, with zero overlap between the two. Therefore, the interaction part of the corresponding Hamiltonian conserves the numbers of electrons and holes separately, and absorption is absent for
\Omega<\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
.
For a strongly non-parabolic, e.g., gapless Dirac spectrum, the
ee
contribution is not suppressed by Galilean invariance, while electron-hole conversion
processes are generically as important as other processes.

As far as the interval of
\Omega>\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
is concerned,
Abedinpour et al.
Abedinpour et al. 2011
showed that the conductivity of doped graphene (a 2D Dirac metal, in our terminology) with Coulomb interaction exhibits a logarithmic renormalization which, for
\Omega\gg\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
, is reduced to the well-studied case of undoped graphene, and is logarithmically enhanced for
\Omega\gtrapprox\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
both for Coulomb and Hubbard interactions Both of these effects arise already at first order in the corresponding interaction and reflect renormalization of the Dirac velocity and, consequently, of the coupling constant. To the best of our knowledge, the interval of
\Omega\gg\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
has not been studied for a 3D Dirac metal but, in analogy with the results for the undoped 3D case
Rosenstein and Lewkowicz 2013
;
Roy and Juričić 2017
, we would also expect a logarithmic renormalization starting at first order.
On the other hand, absorption processes studied in our paper correspond to real collisions between electrons, and between electrons and holes, which occur starting from the second order in the interaction. Therefore, these processes are subleading to the first-order effects described above studied in Ref.
Abedinpour et al. 2011
, and we will not extend our results above
\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
.

Our numerical results agree with analytic ones, where applicable, and allow one to trace the behavior of the conductivity for almost entire frequency range of interest,
0<\Omega<\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
,
except for a narrow interval of width

\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{\text{H,C}}^{2}E_{\text{F}})
around
\omega_{\text{D}}
,
where
\alpha_{\text{H,C}}\ll 1
is the dimensionless coupling constant of Hubbard and Coulomb interactions, respectively.
In this interval, our perturbative expansion breaks down and one needs to re-sum the diagrammatic series.

The density of states at the Fermi level per spin per valley is equal to

The velocity operator corresponding to

with its intra-band matrix element being the same as in Eq. (

while the inter-band matrix element is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{+,-}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{v}^{-,+}_{\mathbf{k}}\right)^{*}=\bra{\mathbf{k},+}\hat{\mathbf{v}}\ket{\mathbf{k},-}

(21)

\displaystyle=iv_{\text{D}}e^{-i\tau_{z}\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}\left(\hat{k}\times\hat{z}\right),

where

(\hat{x},\hat{y},\hat{z})

Lastly, similar to the 3D case, we consider two models of the interaction

\displaystyle[left={V_{\text{2D}}(\mathbf{q})=\empheqlbrace\,}]

\displaystyle\lambda_{2},

(2D, Hubbard)

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{q},

(2D, Coulomb)

III
Optical conductivity: general formalism

III.1
Kubo formula

In linear response, the real part of the optical conductivity is given by the Kubo formula

\displaystyle\Re\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\Omega)=-\frac{1}{\Omega}\Im\Pi_{\alpha\beta\text{,R}}(\mathbf{Q=0},\Omega),

(23)

where
\alpha,\beta=x,y(z)
in 2D and 3D, respectively, and
\Pi_{\alpha\beta\text{,R}}(\mathbf{Q=0},\Omega)
is the retarded current-current correlation function (denoted by subscript “R"), which is obtained by analytic continuation of its Matsubara counterpart:

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha\beta\text{,R}}(\mathbf{Q},\Omega)=\Pi_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{Q},i\Omega\rightarrow\Omega+i0^{+}),

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{Q},i\Omega)=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\int\limits_{0}^{1/k_{\text{B}}T}d\tau e^{i\Omega\tau}\left<T_{\tau}\hat{j}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{Q},\tau)\hat{j}_{\beta}(\mathbf{Q},0)\right>.

(24)

In the basis of conduction/valence bands, the current operator is written as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{j}}(\mathbf{Q},\tau)=-e\sum\limits_{\mathbf{k},s,s^{\prime}}{\textbf{v}}^{s,s^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{k}}\quad\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}-\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{2},s}(\tau)\hat{d}^{\phantom{}}_{\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{2},s^{\prime}}(\tau),

(25)

where
{\textbf{v}}_{{\textbf{k}}}^{s,s^{\prime}}
is given by Eq. (
12
).

For isotropic systems, considered in this paper, the conductivity tensor is diagonal and symmetric.
In this case, we define

\displaystyle\Pi(\mathbf{Q},i\Omega)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{\alpha}\Pi_{\alpha\alpha}(\mathbf{Q},i\Omega),

\displaystyle\Pi_{\text{R}}(\mathbf{Q},\Omega)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum\limits_{\alpha}\Pi_{\alpha\alpha,\text{R}}(\mathbf{Q},\Omega),

\displaystyle\Re\sigma(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\Omega}\Im\Pi_{\text{R}}(\mathbf{Q=0},\Omega).

(26)

We also assume that temperature is much smaller than any other energy scale of the problem and consider only the
T=0
limit.

III.2
Relevant diagrams

In Dirac metals,
optical absorption occurs already for non-interacting particles, if the frequency of incident light exceeds the direct threshold,
\omega_{\mathrm{D}}=2E_{\text{F}}
.
The main focus of this paper is the range of
0<\Omega<2E_{\text{F}}
, where absorption occurs only if electrons interact with other degrees in freedom, in particular, both among themselves and with holes. Dissipation occurs only if the interaction is dynamic, i.e., if the bare interaction, either Hubbard or Coulomb,
is dressed by particle-hole pairs.
Diagrammatically, this corresponds to renormalizing the interaction lines either by
particle-hole polarization bubbles or “Aslamazov-Larkin triangles” (cf. Fig.
2
).

III.2.1
Hubbard interaction

To make the analysis tractable, we assume that the number of identical Dirac points is large (
N\gg 1
) and
also adopt the
weak-coupling approximation, i.e, we assume that

\alpha_{\text{H}}N\ll 1
,
where

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{H}}=\lambda_{d}\mathcal{N}_{\text{F},d}

(27)

is the dimensionless coupling constant.
The first assumption allows us to retain only diagrams with the highest number of fermion loops, while the second one allows us to keep the lowest order in the interaction at which dissipation occurs, to wit: the second. The relevant diagrams for the current-current correlation function are shown in Fig.
2
. For the Hubbard case, the solid and broken interaction lines are identical and denote the Hubbard coupling
\lambda_{d}
.

III.2.2
Coulomb interaction

Within the random-phase approximation (RPA), the dynamically screened Coulomb interaction is given by

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{q},i\nu)=\frac{1}{V^{-1}_{0}(\mathbf{q})+\pi_{0}(\mathbf{q},i\nu)}

(28)

where

\displaystyle\pi_{0}({\textbf{q}},i\nu)=-\int_{\mathcal{K}}\tr\left[\hat{G}({\textbf{k}}+{\textbf{q}},i\omega+i\nu)\hat{G}({\textbf{k}},i\omega)\right],

(29)

is the polarization bubble,
\int_{\mathcal{K}}
is a short-hand for
(2\pi)^{-(d+1)}\int\text{d}^{d}k\int\text{d}\omega
, and
\hat{G}
is the free-electron Green’s function given by Eqs. (
10a
) and (
19a
) in 3D and 2D, respectively.
Since only the dynamic interaction contributes to dissipation, it is convenient to subtract off the static part of the interaction
and treat the remaining dynamic part as the effective interaction. The dynamic part is given by

\displaystyle V_{\text{dyn}}(\mathbf{q},i\nu)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle V({\textbf{q}},i\nu)-V({\textbf{q}},0)

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-V(\mathbf{q},i\nu)V(\mathbf{q},0)\pi_{0,\text{dyn}}(\mathbf{q},i\nu),

where

\displaystyle\pi_{0,\text{dyn}}(\mathbf{q},i\nu)=\pi_{0}(\mathbf{q},i\nu)-\pi_{0}(\mathbf{q},0)

(31)

is the dynamic part of the polarization bubble.
The lowest two-loop order diagrams in
V_{\text{dyn}}(\mathbf{q},i\nu)
are shown in Fig.
2
, where now the solid and broken wavy lines depict the dynamic and static parts of the interaction, respectively.

As opposed to the Hubbard case, the Coulomb one has an additional energy scale,

\displaystyle\omega_{\text{p}d}=v_{\mathrm{D}}\kappa_{d},

(32)

where

\displaystyle\kappa_{3}=\left(4\pi e^{2}N\mathcal{N}_{\text{F},3}\right)^{1/2}

(33a)

and

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}=2\pi e^{2}N\mathcal{N}_{\text{F},2}

(33b)

are the inverse screening radii in 3D and 2D, respectively. For
d=3
,
\omega_{\text{p}3}
is on the order of the plasmon frequency at
q=0
. For
d=2
,
\omega_{\text{p}2}
is on the order of the plasmon dispersion evaluated at
q\sim\kappa_{2}
. The condition for the Coulomb interaction to be treated via within RPA
is
\kappa_{d}\ll k_{\text{F}}
, which implies that
\omega_{\text{p}d}\ll E_{\text{F}}
. Correspondingly, the frequency region
0<\Omega\ll E_{\text{F}}
is divided into two subregions:
0<\Omega\ll\omega_{\text{p}d}
and
\omega_{\text{p}d}\ll\Omega\ll E_{\text{F}}
.
In the first subregion, a
typical energy transfer,
\nu
, is on the order of
\Omega
, while a typical momentum transfer,
q
, is on the order of
\kappa_{d}
. Therefore,
\nu\ll v_{\mathrm{D}}q\sim\omega_{\text{p}d}
.
In this case, one can set
\nu=0
in the first factor on the RHS of Eq. (
30
) with the result

\displaystyle V_{\text{dyn}}(\mathbf{q},i\nu)\approx-V^{2}(\mathbf{q},0)\pi_{0,\text{dyn}}(\mathbf{q},i\nu).

(34)

Diagrammatically, this amounts to replacing all the solid wavy lines by broken wavy ones in Fig.
2
.
Because
q\ll k_{\text{F}}
, the static screened potential is described by the usual Thomas-Fermi form:

\displaystyle[left={V(\mathbf{q},0)=\empheqlbrace\,}]

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi e^{2}}{q^{2}+\kappa_{3}^{2}},

for 3D

(35a)

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{q+\kappa_{2}},

\displaystyle\text{for 2D}.

(35b)

In the second subregion (
\omega_{\text{p}d}\ll\Omega\ll E_{\text{F}}
), typical energy and momentum transfers are
\nu\sim v_{\mathrm{D}}q\sim\Omega\gg\omega_{\text{p}d}
. In this case, screening is irrelevant and the effective dynamic interaction is given by

\displaystyle V_{\text{dyn}}({\textbf{q}},i\nu)=-V^{2}_{0}({\textbf{q}})\pi_{0,\text{dyn}}({\textbf{q}},i\nu),

(36)

where
V_{0}({\textbf{q}})
is the bare Coulomb potential.

Note that we do not need to use the large-
N
approximation for the Coulomb case, it is enough to require that the dimensional coupling constant of the Coulomb interaction

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{C}}=\frac{v_{\mathrm{D}}\kappa_{d}}{E_{\text{F}}}

(37)

is small, which is the condition for the validity of RPA. For the Coulomb case, therefore, we will restrict our analysis to the actual value of
N
for a specific system.

III.3
Current-current correlation function on the Matsubara axis

In this section, we describe the general structure of the diagrams for the current-current correlation function.
The set of diagrams in Fig.
2
includes two self-energy (SE) diagrams, SE
1
and SE
2

The contributions of individual diagrams to the current-current correlation function at the external

\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{SE}_{1}}(\mathcal{W})=\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\tr\left[\hat{{\textbf{v}}}\hat{S}(\mathcal{K+W})\cdot\hat{{\textbf{v}}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{K})\right],

(38a)

\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{SE}_{2}}(\mathcal{W})=\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathcal{K}}\tr\left[\hat{{\textbf{v}}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{K+W})\cdot\hat{{\textbf{v}}}\hat{S}(\mathcal{K})\right],

(38b)

\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{V}}(\mathcal{W})=\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathcal{K^{\prime}}}\tr\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\left(\mathcal{K^{\prime};W}\right)\hat{G}(\mathcal{K^{\prime}+W})\cdot\hat{{\textbf{v}}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{K^{\prime}})\right],

(38c)

(38d)

(38e)

\displaystyle\hat{S}(\mathcal{L})=\hat{G}(\mathcal{L})\hat{\Sigma}(\mathcal{L})\hat{G}(\mathcal{L}),

(39a)

(39b)

(39c)

(39d)

\displaystyle{\bf A}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{W})=-\int_{\mathcal{K}}\tr\left[\hat{G}(\mathcal{K})\hat{{\textbf{v}}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{K+W})\hat{G}(\mathcal{K-Q})\right],

(39e)

\displaystyle{\bf B}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{W})=-\int_{\mathcal{P}}\tr\left[\hat{G}(\mathcal{P+W})\hat{{\textbf{v}}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{P})\hat{G}(\mathcal{P-Q})\right],

(39f)

\displaystyle{\bf C}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{W})=-\int_{\mathcal{P}}\tr\left[\hat{G}(\mathcal{P})\hat{G}(\mathcal{P+W+Q})\hat{G}(\mathcal{P+W})\hat{{\textbf{v}}}\right],

\mathcal{K}\equiv(\mathbf{k},i\omega)

\mathcal{K^{\prime}}\equiv(\mathbf{k^{\prime}},i\omega)
,
\mathcal{P}\equiv(\mathbf{p},i\omega)

\mathcal{Q}\equiv(\mathbf{q},i\nu)

\pi_{0}(\mathcal{Q})
is defined by Eq. (

and

[Eqs. (

The expressions above are valid for Coulomb interaction

\Omega\ll E_{\text{F}}

Using the free rather than dressed Green’s functions is justified for any frequency except for a narrow region near the direct threshold (a precise condition will be formulated later, cf. Sec.

The total current-current correlation function is the sum of all the contributions displayed above:

\displaystyle\Pi(\mathcal{W})=\sum\limits_{J}\Pi^{J}(\mathcal{W}),

(40)

where

, and “SE” refers to both the self-energy diagrams collectively.

Equations (
38a

38e

become more transparent if written in the electron-hole basis, in which
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is diagonal.
Indeed, any diagram contains six Green’s functions,
each being the sum
of an electron and hole parts with helicities
s^{\prime}=\pm 1
, respectively.
This gives rise to a set of six helicities
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{s^{\prime}_{1}\dots s^{\prime}_{6}\}
that are to be summed over. Thus, each diagram is the sum of
2^{6}=64
terms

\displaystyle\Pi^{J}(\mathcal{W})=\sum\limits_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\Pi^{J}_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{W}),

(41)

where summation goes over all 64 configurations of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

Each
\Pi^{J}_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{W})
term in the sum contains a product of two integrals over the frequency

\displaystyle\int d\omega\prod\limits_{l=1}^{L}g_{s_{l}}(\mathbf{k}_{l},i\omega+i\nu_{l})\int d\omega^{\prime}\prod\limits_{l^{\prime}=1}^{L^{\prime}}g_{s_{l^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{k}_{l^{\prime}},i\omega^{\prime}+i\nu_{l^{\prime}}),

(42)

where
L=4
for all diagrams,

for SE

and V diagrams, and

is the Green’s function in the diagonal basis, defined by Eq. (

The integrals in Eq. (

Because
\xi_{\mathbf{k}_{l}}^{s_{l}}<0

Thus, instead of

terms we would, in general, have only
2^{4}=16
terms in the sum of helicities in Eq. (
41

V.3
High frequencies:
\Omega>\omega_{\mathrm{D}}

At the level of non-interacting electrons, the optical conductivity of undoped and gapless 3D Dirac metal scales linearly with frequency [see Eq. (
2
)].
In the doped case, the onset of the linear scaling is shifted to
\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
:

\displaystyle\Re\sigma_{\text{NI3}}(\Omega)=\frac{Ne^{2}\Omega}{24\pi\hbar v_{\mathrm{D}}}\theta(\Omega-\omega_{\text{D}}).

(102)

To the best of our knowledge, effects of electron-electron interaction in 3D Dirac systems were studied only for the undoped case.
In this case,
the Coulomb interaction is marginally irrelevant and, consequently, the Dirac velocity acquires an upward logarithmic renormalization while the coupling constant is renormalized downward
Abrikosov and Beneslavskii 1971
;
Rosenstein and Lewkowicz 2013
. The optical conductivity also experiences a logarithmic renormalization and, at
\Omega\to 0
, the slope of the linear scaling approaches a universal limit of
1+1/(N+1)

Roy and Juričić 2017
. By analogy with the 2D case, however (see Sec.
VI.3
), we expect the optical conductivity to exhibit a logarithmic singularity at
\Omega=\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
both for Coulomb and Hubbard interactions.
Renormalization of the optical conductivity is the first-order interaction effect, while the absorption processes studied in this paper are second-order ones. Therefore, the latter should be subleading to the former for
\Omega>\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
. Due to the lack of known first-order results for the doped case in this range, we will model the optical conductivity by its non-interacting value in Eq. (
102
).

VI
Optical conductivity of a 2D Dirac metal

Just as in 3D, we first discuss the lowest frequency regime for 2D, and then the intermediate and high-frequency regimes.

VI.1
Lowest frequencies:
\Omega\ll E_{\text{F}}

As in the 3D case, this regime corresponds to
s_{3}=s_{4}=+1
. The conductivity can be split into two contributions: a purely electron one and a contribution from processes that involve up to two holes.

VI.1.1
Intra-band absorption due to electron-electron interaction

The reasoning about partial cancellation of diagrams for an isotropic spectrum follows the same lines as for the 3D case, see Sec.
V.1.1
. We thus have exactly the same expressions for the conductivity as in Eqs. (
74
) and (
75
), but now with the momentum integrals being 2D rather than 3D.
The self-energy in 2D has an extra logarithmic factor; however, this factor cancels between different diagrams. Nevertheless, the
q
integrand in Eq. (
75
) has an extra factor of
q
in the denominator, which does lead to a logarithmic enhancement of the conductivity compared to the 3D case, cf. Ref.
Sharma et al. 2021
.

For Hubbard interaction,
we estimate the conductivity as

\displaystyle\Re\sigma_{\text{ee
}}(\Omega)\sim e^{2}(N\alpha_{\text{H}})^{2}\left(\frac{\Omega}{E_{\text{F}}}\right)^{2}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\text{F}}}{\Omega}\right),

(103)

whereas the actual calculation gives

\displaystyle\Re\sigma_{\text{ee}}(\Omega)=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}(N\alpha_{\text{H}})^{2}\left(\frac{1}{80\pi^{2}}\ln\frac{E_{\text{F}}}{\Omega}+\frac{30\ln 2-1}{1200\pi^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\Omega}{E_{\text{F}}}\right)^{2}.

(104)

Note that Eq. (
104
) contains not only the leading logarithmic term but also a subleading one.
Keeping the subleading term is necessary for comparison with the
eh1
and
eh2
contributions, which do not have a logarithmic enhancement.

As in 3D, the case of Coulomb interaction in the region
\Omega\ll\omega_{\text{p}2}\ll E_{\text{F}}
is similar to the Hubbard one. Explicit calculation shows that

\displaystyle\Re\sigma^{\text{C}}_{\text{ee1}}(\Omega)=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\left(\frac{1}{80\pi^{2}}\ln\frac{\omega_{\text{p}2}}{\Omega}+\frac{5\ln 2-2}{16\pi^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\Omega}{E_{\text{F}}}\right)^{2},

(105)

where
\omega_{\text{p}2}
is defined in Eq. (
32
).
The
leading logarithmic term in the last equation
coincides with the result of Ref.
Sharma et al. 2021
. Note that, in contrast to the 3D case, the conductivity depends on the coupling constant of the Coulomb interaction only via the cutoff of the logarithmic term.

In the range of frequencies
\omega_{\text{p}2}\ll\Omega\ll E_{\text{F}}
, the estimate for the self-energy in Eq. (
83
) is modified as

\displaystyle\Im\Sigma_{\text{ee}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa^{2}_{2}}{\mathcal{N}_{\text{F},2}v_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\int^{\Omega}_{0}\text{d}\nu\nu\int^{\infty}_{\max\{\Omega,\Omega-\nu\}/v_{\mathrm{F}}}\frac{\text{d}q}{q^{3}}

(106)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\omega_{\text{p}2}.

In contrast to the 3D case, the self-energy remains constant in this frequency interval and, according to Eq. (
78
), the same is true for the conductivity. The actual calculation gives

\displaystyle\Re\sigma^{\text{C}}_{\text{ee}2}(\Omega)=\frac{5}{576\pi^{2}}\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\frac{e^{2}}{v_{\mathrm{D}}}.

(107)

VI.1.2
Absorption processes involving up to two holes

Now we analyze the scattering processes which involve up two holes. Again, the general reasoning here is exactly the same as the 3D eh case. Namely, there are two types of such processes: with one hole (
eh1
,
s_{1}=-s_{2}=\pm 1
)
and with two holes (
eh2
,
s_{1}=s_{2}=-1
), with the same corresponding conditions on the helicities as in the 3D case.

The estimates for the
eh1
and
eh2
contributions to the conductivity are the same as in the 3D case, i.e., they are given by Eqs. (
87
) and (
90
),
modulo a replacement
e^{2}k_{\text{F}}\to e^{2}
.

Therefore, the estimates for the
eh1
and
eh2
contributions in 2D
read

\displaystyle\Re\sigma_{\text{eh1}}(\Omega)\sim\Re\sigma_{\text{eh2}}(\Omega)\sim e^{2}\alpha_{\text{H}}^{2}\left(\frac{\Omega}{E_{\text{F}}}\right)^{2},

(108)

whereas the actual calculation shows that

\displaystyle\Re\sigma_{\text{eh1
}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Re\sigma_{\text{eh2
}}=\frac{1}{96\pi^{2}}\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}(N\alpha_{\text{H}})^{2}\left(\frac{\Omega}{E_{\text{F}}}\right)^{2}.

(109)

Adding up the
eh1
and
eh2
contributions, we have

\displaystyle\Re\sigma_{\text{eh}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\Re\sigma_{\text{eh}1}(\Omega)+\Re\sigma_{\text{eh}2}(\Omega)

(110)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{48\pi^{2}}\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}(N\alpha_{\text{H}})^{2}\left(\frac{\Omega}{E_{\text{F}}}\right)^{2}.

While the combined
eh
contribution is smaller than the leading logarithmic term in
ee
contribution [cf. Eq. (
104
)], it is of the same order as the next-to-leading
ee
term. The total conductivity is then a sum of Eq. (
104
) and Eq. (
110
):

\displaystyle\Re\sigma(\Omega)=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}(N\alpha_{\text{H}})^{2}\left(\frac{1}{80\pi^{2}}\ln\frac{E_{\text{F}}}{\Omega}+\frac{5\ln 2+4}{200\pi^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\Omega}{E_{\text{F}}}\right)^{2}.

(111)

In terms of numbers, the logarithmic term becomes the leading one for
\Omega/E_{\text{F}}<0.05
.

As in the 3D case, the
eh
contribution for Coulomb interaction is smaller than the
ee
one by a factor of
\alpha_{\text{C}}
, and thus Eqs. (
105
) and (
107
) are the leading contributions to the conductivity in the corresponding frequency intervals. Note that the logarithmic term in Eq. (
105
) becomes the leading one only at very low frequencies:
\Omega/\omega_{\text{p}2}<6.6\times 10^{-4}
.

VI.2
Intermediate frequencies:
\omega_{\mathrm{I}}\leq\Omega<\omega_{\mathrm{D}}

The optical conductivity of a 2D Dirac metal in the intermediate frequency regime is completely analogous to the 3D case, discussed in Sec.
V.2
. As in 3D, the analytic results are attainable only for
\Omega\gtrapprox\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
. In fact, in Sec.
V.2.1
we have already derived a general expression for the scaling exponent
\beta_{\text{A}}
, see Eq. (
98
). For the case of
d=2
and
a=2
, we obtain
\beta_{\text{A}}=3
, in agreement with Ref.
Pimenov et al. 2017
. For our case of
d=2
and
a=1
, this equation gives
\beta_{\text{A}}=4
.
Without repeating the same steps as in 3D, we just present the results for the Hubbard case

\displaystyle\Re\sigma^{\text{IF}}=\frac{5}{108\sqrt{3}\pi}\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}(N\alpha_{\text{H}})^{2}\theta(\delta\Omega)\left(\frac{\delta\Omega}{E_{\text{F}}}\right)^{4}

(112)

and for the Coulomb case

\displaystyle\Re\sigma^{\text{IF,C}}=\frac{5}{108\sqrt{3}\pi}\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\alpha_{\text{C}}^{2}\theta(\delta\Omega)\left(\frac{\delta\Omega}{E_{\text{F}}}\right)^{4}.

(113)

As in the
3D case, the results for the Hubbard and Coulomb cases are identical for the reason explained in Sec.
V.2.1
.
Also, as in 3D, the estimates for the conductivity for a generic frequency within the interval
\{\omega_{\mathrm{I}},\omega_{\mathrm{D}}\}
and away from either of the thresholds,
can be obtained by replacing
\Omega
with
E_{\text{F}}
in Eq. (
104
) and assuming that Eq. (
107
) continues to be valid within an order of magnitude for
\Omega\sim E_{F}
. This gives

\displaystyle\Re\sigma_{\text{IF}}(\Omega)\sim\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(N\alpha_{\text{H}})^{2},&\\
\alpha_{\text{C}}^{2},&\end{array}\right.

for the Hubbard and Coulomb cases, respectively.

VI.3
High frequencies:
\Omega>\omega_{\text{D}}

The optical response of 2D Dirac metals, e.g., graphene, has been studied extensively; see, e.g., reviews
Peres 2010
;
Das Sarma et al. 2011
;
Kotov et al. 2012
and references therein. At the non-interacting level, the optical conductivity has a universal form, given by Eq. (
1
).
At finite doping, this result is modified to

\displaystyle\Re\sigma_{\text{NI2}}(\Omega)=\frac{e^{2}N}{16\hbar}\theta(\Omega-\omega_{\mathrm{D}}).

(117)

As in 3D, the Coulomb interaction is also marginally irrelevant in 2D, which leads to an upward logarithmic renormalization of the Dirac velocity and, consequently, to the downward renormalization of the coupling constant.
On the other hand, Hubbard interaction is irrelevant and can be neglected for frequencies below the ultraviolet cutoff of the model.

The optical conductivity of doped graphene
was studied by Abedinpour et al.
Abedinpour et al. 2011
to first order in both Coulomb and Hubbard interaction.
As expected, the results reduce to those for the undoped case in the limit of
E_{\text{F}}\ll\Omega
.
Near the direct threshold
\omega_{\mathrm{D}}=2E_{\text{F}}
, the conductivity is logarithmically enhanced compared to the non-interacting value for both Coulomb and Hubbard cases.
Because the absorption processes studied in this paper occur to second order in the interaction, they are subleading to those studied in Ref.
Abedinpour et al. 2011
and, therefore, we will not extend our results to the region
\Omega>\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
.

VII
Conclusions

We studied optical absorption is 2D and 3D Dirac metals due to electron-electron (
ee
) and electron-hole (
eh
) interactions. The latter were described by two models: a Hubbard-like interaction, with a radius shorter than the Fermi wavelength but longer than the lattice spacing, and a dynamically screened Coulomb potential. To keep the perturbation theory under control, both types of interactions were assumed to be weak. The optical conductivity,
\Re\sigma(\Omega)
, was obtained by computing the leading diagrams for the current-current correlation functions, in the large-
N
approximation for the Hubbard case and in the random-phase approximation for the Coulomb case. The main focus of this paper is the behavior of
\Re\sigma(\Omega)
in the range of frequencies
0<\Omega<\omega_{\mathrm{D}}=2E_{\text{F}}
, where absorption is blocked by the Pauli principle in the single-particle picture. This range is further split into two ranges:
0<\Omega<\omega_{\mathrm{I}}=E_{F}
(I) and
\omega_{\mathrm{I}}<\Omega<\omega_{\mathrm{D}}
(II).

In range I, absorption starts at the lowest frequencies. The conductivity in this range comes from purely
ee
scattering, which is allowed to contribute due to broken Galilean invariance, and from certain
eh
scattering processes, which involve up to two holes. For
\Omega\ll E_{\text{F}}
,
we derived the analytic results for the conductivity, which are presented in Tables
2
and
3
, for the Hubbard and Coulomb cases respectively. In both cases,
\Re\sigma(\Omega)

scales as
\Omega^{2}\ln\Omega
in 2D and as
\Omega^{2}
in 3D. In other words, the effective current relaxation rate,
1/\tau_{j}\equiv(k_{\text{F}}/v_{\mathrm{D}}ne^{2})\Omega^{2}\Re\sigma(\Omega)
scales as
\Omega^{4}\ln\Omega
in 2D and as
\Omega^{4}
in 3D. (Here,
n
is the carrier number density,
k_{\text{F}}
is the Fermi momentum, and
v_{\mathrm{D}}
is the Dirac velocity.)
The
ee
contribution to
\Re\sigma(\Omega)
has been derived in Ref.
Sharma et al. 2021
for the Coulomb case by a different method, via the Heisenberg equations of motion for the current operator, and our results for this contribution agree with those of Ref.
Sharma et al. 2021
(modulo a discrepancy in the numerical coefficient in 3D).
Remarkably, the
eh
contribution, studied in this paper, is comparable to the
ee
one in 3D and subleading to the
ee
in 2D only in the leading logarithmic sense. For the rest of range I,
\Re\sigma(\Omega)
was calculated numerically.

In range II, another type
eh
scattering processes, similar the Auger-Meitner (AM) processes in atomic physics
Meitner 1922
;
Auger 1923
;
Matsakis et al. 2019
, start to contribute to the conductivity. These processes have been studied extensively in the context of doped semiconductors (see, e.g., Refs.
Gavoret et al. 1969
;
Ruckenstein and Schmitt-Rink 1987
;
Uenoyama and Sham 1990
;
Hawrylak 1991
;
Pimenov et al. 2017
), but only in the model of parabolic bands, within which absorption in range I is absent, and the onset of absorption due to AM processes at
\Omega=\omega_{\mathrm{I}}
is manifested by a threshold singularity in
\Re\sigma(\Omega)
. We showed that a similar singularity also exists for Dirac metals. However, in contrast to the parabolic-bands case, the AM singularity occurs at the background of absorption due to
ee
and other
eh
processes, which start to contribute in region I, but continue to contribute in region II as well.
Our numerical calculations show that the AM threshold singularity is completely masked by these other processes.

In the range of
\Omega\sim E_{\text{F}}
(but not in the immediate vicinity of either
E_{\text{F}}
and
2E_{\text{F}}
), all
ee
and
eh
scattering processes give comparable contributions to
\Re\sigma(\Omega)
. As
E_{\text{F}}
is the only energy scale in this regime, the effective current relaxation rate is of order
gE_{\text{F}}
, where
g
is the dimensionless coupling constant for either type of interaction. However, our analytic and numerical results show that the numerical coefficient
C
in the relation
1/\tau_{j}=CgE_{\text{F}}
is anomalously small, on the order of
10^{-3}
, i.e., in reality
1/\tau_{j}\ll E_{\text{F}}
even at
g=1
. This may explain the observation of well-resolved collective modes below
2E_{\text{F}}
in the helical surface state of a doped 3D topological insulator
Kung et al. 2017
(see Ref.
Maslov et al. 2022
for more details).

As mentioned in Sec.
I
, experiments on monolayer graphene find significant optical absorption at frequencies above the Drude tail but below
2E_{\text{F}}

Li et al. 2008
;
Mak et al. 2008
;
Horng et al. 2011
;
Mak et al. 2012
;
Jenkins et al. 2016
and also significant Raman response in the same frequency range
Riccardi et al. 2016
. In real materials, absorption in this frequency range is not only due to
ee
and
eh
interactions, but also due to electron-impurity and electron-phonon scattering. Moreover, it was argued in Ref.
Peres et al. 2008
that the data of Ref.
Li et al. 2008
can be well explained by taking into account only electron-impurity and electron-phonon scattering (with an addition of excitonic effects
Peres et al. 2010
). We hope that future experiments on samples with higher mobilities will be able to resolve intrinsic,
ee
and
eh
contributions to absorption.

Appendix A
General structure of the diagrams for the optical conductivity

In this Appendix, we derive the general forms of the contributions from particular diagrams for the current-current correlation function in Fig.
2
. These forms are valid
for Hubbard interaction for all frequencies of interest,
\Omega<2E_{\text{F}}
, and for Coulomb interaction for
\Omega\ll E_{F}
.
We discuss only the self-energy and vertex diagrams; the analysis of the Aslamazov-Larkin diagrams follows the same lines.

A.1
Some common properties

As explained in Sec.
III.2
, the imaginary parts of the contributions from the self-energy (SE) and vertex (V) diagrams to the current-current correlation function contain a dynamic polarization bubble

\pi_{\text{dyn}}({\textbf{q}},i\nu)=\pi_{0}({\textbf{q}},i\nu)-\pi_{0}({\textbf{q}},0)
. For practical purposes, however, it is more convenient to replace
\pi_{\text{dyn}}({\textbf{q}},i\nu)
by the full bubble,
\pi_{0}({\textbf{q}},i\nu)
, with the understanding that its static part will drop out on taking the imaginary part. Then the self-energy and vertex diagrams can be written as

\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{SE,V}}(i\Omega)=\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbf{q}}T\sum\limits_{\nu}\pi^{\text{SE,V}}_{1}(\mathbf{q},i\nu;i\Omega)V^{2}_{\text{st}}({\textbf{q}})\pi_{0}({\textbf{q}},i\nu),

(118)

where SE stands for both SE
1
and SE
2
parts of the SE diagram,
V_{\text{st}}({\textbf{q}})
is either a (momentum-independent) Hubbard interaction or statically screened Coulomb potential,

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\mathbf{q},i\nu)=-\frac{1}{4}\sum\limits_{s^{\prime}_{4},s^{\prime}_{6}=\pm 1}\int_{\mathbf{p}}\tr\left(\hat{M}_{\mathbf{p}}^{s^{\prime}_{6}}\hat{M}_{\mathbf{p+q}}^{s^{\prime}_{4}}\right)\frac{\left[n_{\text{F}}(\xi^{s^{\prime}_{6}}_{\mathbf{p}})-n_{\text{F}}(\xi^{s^{\prime}_{4}}_{\mathbf{p+q}})\right]}{i\nu-\xi^{s^{\prime}_{4}}_{\mathbf{p+q}}+\xi^{s^{\prime}_{6}}_{\mathbf{p}}}

(119)

is the polarization bubble, and
\pi_{1}^{\text{SE,V}}({\textbf{q}},i\nu;i\Omega)
are given by

\displaystyle\pi_{1}^{\text{SE}_{1}}(\mathbf{q},i\nu;i\Omega)=-\int_{\mathcal{K}}\tr\left(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{K+W})\hat{G}(\mathcal{K+Q+W})\hat{G}(\mathcal{K+W})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{K})\right)

\displaystyle\pi_{1}^{\text{SE}_{2}}(\mathbf{q},i\nu;i\Omega)=-\int_{\mathcal{K}}\tr\left(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{K+W})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{K})\hat{G}(\mathcal{K+Q})\hat{G}(\mathcal{K})\right)

\displaystyle\pi_{1}^{\text{V}}(\mathbf{q},i\nu;i\Omega)=-\int_{\mathcal{K}}\tr\left(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{K+W})\hat{G}(\mathcal{K+Q+W})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}}\hat{G}(\mathcal{K+Q})\hat{G}(\mathcal{K})\right).

(120)

It will be convenient to introduce a quantity

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},i\omega)=T\sum\limits_{\nu}g_{s}(\mathbf{k},i\omega+i\nu)\pi_{0}(\mathbf{q},i\nu),

(121)

whose meaning is that
\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(\mathbf{k}+{\textbf{q}},\mathbf{q},i\omega)
is the self-energy with fixed momentum transfer
q
(but without the interaction potential).
Evaluating the Matsubara sum over
\nu
and performing analytic continuation as in
i\omega\to\omega+i0^{+}

A.2.3
Elimination of singular terms

In Sec.
A.2.1
, we argued that the singularities of the
\delta^{2}(x)
type, occurring in e.g., Eq. (
133
), can be ignored. Here, we prove this statement. Because the singularity occurs already for the case when all helicities are the same, we put

s_{1}=s_{2}=s_{3}=s_{5}\equiv s
and suppress the helicity index. Also, since the momentum-dependence of the self-energy is irrelevant for the present argument, we denote temporarily
\tilde{S}(i\omega)\equiv\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}({\textbf{k}}+{\textbf{q}},{\textbf{k}},i\omega)
.
Adding Eq. (
133
) to the corresponding contribution from diagram SE
2
, we obtain

\displaystyle W_{\text{SE}}(i\Omega)=W_{\text{SE}1}(i\Omega)+W_{\text{SE}2}(i\Omega)=T\sum\limits_{\omega}g^{2}(\mathbf{k},i\omega)\tilde{S}(i\omega)\left[g(\mathbf{k},i\omega-i\Omega)+g(\mathbf{k},i\omega+i\Omega)\right],

(144)

where we also made a change of variables
i\omega\to i\omega+i\Omega
in
W_{\text{SE}1}
. Applying the identity

\displaystyle g_{s}({\textbf{k}},i\varepsilon)g_{s^{\prime}}({\textbf{k}},i\varepsilon^{\prime})=\frac{1}{i(\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon)-\xi_{\textbf{k}}^{s^{\prime}}+\xi_{\textbf{k}}^{s}}\left[g_{s}({\textbf{k}},i\varepsilon)-g_{s^{\prime}}({\textbf{k}},i\varepsilon^{\prime})\right]

(145)

with
s=s^{\prime}
to Eq. (
144
) twice, we obtain

\displaystyle W_{\text{SE}}(i\Omega)=\frac{1}{(i\Omega)^{2}}T\sum_{\omega}\left[g({\textbf{k}},i\omega+i\Omega)+g({\textbf{k}},i\omega-i\Omega)-2g({\textbf{k}},i\omega)\right]\tilde{S}(i\omega).

(146)

Summing over
\omega
and performing analytic continuation, we get

\displaystyle W_{\text{SE,R}}(\Omega)=\frac{\mathcal{Z}(\Omega)}{(\Omega+i0^{+})^{2}},

(147)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\omega}{\pi}n_{\text{F}}(\omega)\left\{g_{\text{R}}({\textbf{k}},\omega+\Omega)\Im\tilde{S}_{\text{R}}(\omega)+\Im g_{\text{R}}({\textbf{k}},\omega)\tilde{S}_{\text{A}}(\omega-\Omega)+g_{\text{A}}({\textbf{k}},\omega-\Omega)\Im\tilde{S}_{\text{R}}(\omega)+\Im g_{\text{R}}({\textbf{k}},\omega)\tilde{S}_{\text{R}}(\omega+\Omega)\right.

(148)

\displaystyle\left.-2\Im\left[g_{\text{R}}({\textbf{k}},\omega)\tilde{S}_{\text{R}}(\omega)\right]\right\}.

Taking the imaginary part of Eq. (
147
), we find

\displaystyle\Im W_{\text{SE,R}}(\Omega)=\frac{\Im\mathcal{Z}(\Omega)}{\Omega^{2}}+\Im\frac{1}{(\Omega+i0^{+})^{2}}\Re\mathcal{Z}(\Omega).

(149)

It is the second term that contains an essential singularity. We re-write the singular part as

\displaystyle\Im\frac{1}{(\Omega+i0^{+})^{2}}\Re\mathcal{Z}(\Omega)=-\Im\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{\Omega+i0^{+}}\right)\Re\mathcal{Z}(\Omega)=\pi\delta^{\prime}(\Omega)\Re\mathcal{Z}(\Omega)=-\pi\delta(\Omega)\frac{\partial}{\partial\Omega}\Re\mathcal{Z}(\Omega)\Big|_{\Omega=0}.

(150)

The imaginary part of the retarded current-current correlator must be an odd function of
\Omega
, and the same is true for
\Im W_{\text{SE,R}}(\Omega)
and

\Im\mathcal{Z}(\Omega)
.
But then
\Re\mathcal{Z}(\Omega)
is odd in
\Omega
, and its derivative must vanish at
\Omega\to 0
at least as
\Omega
, which means that left-hand side of Eq. (
150
) is proportional to
\Omega\delta(\Omega)
. Recalling also that
\Re\sigma(\Omega)=-\Im\Pi_{\text{R}}(\Omega)/\Omega
, we see that the singular part of Eq. (
150
) only renormalizes the weight of the delta function term in the conductivity.
Such a term is rendered finite by taking momentum-relaxing scattering, e.g., scattering by impurities, into account, but is of no interest to our study and can be safely discarded.
