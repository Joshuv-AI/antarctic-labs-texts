On Ramanujan’s formula for ζ ⁡ ( 1 / 2 ) \zeta(1/2) and ζ ⁡ ( 2 ​ m + 1 ) \zeta(2m+1) Thanks: 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary 11M06; Secondary 11J81 . Keywords and phrases. Riemann zeta function, Odd zeta values, Ramanujan’s formula
Anushree Gupta, Bibekananda Maji

Abstract
. Page 332 of Ramanujan’s Lost Notebook contains a compelling identity for ζ ⁡ ( 1 / 2 ) \zeta(1/2) , which has been studied by many mathematicians over the years. On the same page, Ramanujan also recorded the series, 1 r exp ⁡ ( 1 s ​ x ) − 1 + 2 r exp ⁡ ( 2 s ​ x ) − 1 + 3 r exp ⁡ ( 3 s ​ x ) − 1 + ⋯ , \displaystyle\frac{1^{r}}{\exp(1^{s}x)-1}+\frac{2^{r}}{\exp(2^{s}x)-1}+\frac{3^{r}}{\exp(3^{s}x)-1}+\cdots, where s s is a positive integer and r − s r-s is any even integer. Unfortunately, Ramanujan doesn’t give any formula for it. This series was rediscovered by Kanemitsu, Tanigawa, and Yoshimoto, although they studied it only when r − s r-s is a negative even integer. Recently, Dixit and the second author generalized the work of Kanemitsu et al. and obtained a transformation formula for the aforementioned series with r − s r-s is any even integer. While extending the work of Kanemitsu et al., Dixit and the second author obtained a beautiful generalization of Ramanujan’s formula for odd zeta values. In the current paper, we investigate transformation formulas for an infinite series, and interestingly, we derive Ramanujan’s formula for ζ ⁡ ( 1 / 2 ) \zeta(1/2) , Wigert’s formula for ζ ⁡ ( 1 / k ) \zeta(1/k) as well as Ramanujan’s formula for ζ ⁡ ( 2 ​ m + 1 ) \zeta(2m+1) . Furthermore, we obtain a new identity for ζ ( − 1 / 2 ) \zeta(-1/2) in the spirit of Ramanujan.



1.
Introduction

The study of understanding the algebraic nature of the special values of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
has been a constant source of motivation for mathematicians to produce many beautiful results. In 1735, Euler established the following elegant formula for
\zeta(2m)
.
For every positive integer
m
,

\zeta(2m)=(-1)^{m+1}\frac{(2\pi)^{2m}B_{2m}}{2(2m)!},

(1.1)

where
B_{2m}
denotes the
2m
th Bernoulli number.
These are certain rational numbers,
defined by the generating function:

{\frac{z}{e^{z}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},\quad|z|<2\pi.}

Euler’s formula along with the transcendence of
\pi
, proved by F. Lindemann, immediately tells us that all even zeta values are transcendental. However, the arithmetic nature of the odd zeta values is yet to be determined. Surprisingly in 1979, Roger Apéry
[
2
,
3
]
proved the irrationality of
\zeta(3)
. But till today we do not know about the algebraic nature of
\zeta(3)
, that is, whether
\zeta(3)
is algebraic or transcendental.
In 2001, Rivoal
[
28
]
Ball and Rivoal
[
5
]
made a breakthrough by proving that there exist infinitely many odd zeta values which are irrational, but their result does not tells us anything about the algebraic nature of a specific odd zeta value.
Around the same time, Zudilin
[
32
]
proved that at least one of the members of
\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\zeta(9)
or
\zeta(11)
is irrational, which is the current best result in this direction.

Before going to England, Ramanujan, in his second notebook
[
26
, p. 173, Ch. 14, Entry 21(i)]
, noted down the following remarkable formula for odd zeta values
\zeta(2m+1)
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two positive numbers such that
\alpha\beta=\pi^{2}
. For any non-zero integer
m
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(4\alpha)^{m}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\zeta(2m+1)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-2m-1}}{(e^{2n\alpha}-1)}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{(4\beta)^{m}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\zeta(2m+1)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-2m-1}}{(e^{2n\beta}-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m+1}(-1)^{k-1}\frac{B_{2k}}{(2k)!}\frac{B_{2m+2-2k}}{(2m+2-2k)!}\alpha^{m+1-k}\beta^{k}.

(1.2)

This formula can also be found in Ramanujan’s Lost Notebook
[
27
, pp. 319-320, formula (28)]
.
In sharp contrast to Euler’s formula, it falls short of providing an explicit formula for odd zeta values, but it is indeed another wonderful discovery of Ramanujan that has attracted the attention of several mathematicians.
The first published proof was given by Malurkar
[
21
]
in 1925, who had no idea about its presence in Ramanujan’s Notebook.
Lerch, in 1901, proved a particular case of the formula (
1
), namely, corresponding to
\alpha=\beta=\pi
and
m
an odd positive integer. Replacing
m
by
2m+1
in (
1
), it reduces to

\displaystyle\zeta(4m+3)+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-4m-3}}{(e^{2\pi n}-1)}=\pi^{4m+3}2^{4m+2}\sum_{j=0}^{2m+2}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}B_{2j}B_{4m+4-2j}}{(2j)!(4m+2-2j)!}.

(1.3)

Note that the infinite series present in the left side of (
1.3
) is a rapidly convergent series, which indicates that
\zeta(4m+3)
is almost a rational multiple of
\pi^{4m+3}
. Moreover, one can say that atleast one of the expressions
\zeta(4m+3)
or
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-4m-3}}{(e^{2\pi n}-1)}
must be transcendental since the right side expression is transcendental. A folklore conjecture is that
\pi
and odd zeta values do not satisfy any non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients, which implies that all odd zeta values must be transcendental.

Ramanujan’s formula (
1
) has a deep connection with the theory of Eisenstien series over
SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and their Eichler integrals. To know more about this connection, one can see
[
12
]
,
[
18
]
. In 1977, Bruce Berndt
[
7
]
obtained a modular transformation formula for a generalized Eisenstien series from which he was able to derive Euler’s formula for
\zeta(2m)
as well as Ramanujan’s formula for
\zeta(2m+1)
. Over the years, many mathematicians found generalizations of (
1
) in various directions. Readers are encouraged to see the paper of Berndt and Straub
[
13
]
for more information on the history of Ramanujan’s formula for odd zeta values.

In 2001, Kanemitsu, Tanigawa and Yoshimoto
[
20
]
studied a generalized Lambert series defined by

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{N-2h}}{\exp(n^{N}x)-1},

(1.4)

where
h,N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
1\leq h\leq N/2
. They established an important modular transformational relation for this generalized Lambert series which enabled them to find a formula for the Riemann zeta function at rational arguments. Recently, Dixit and the second author
[
15
]
pointed out that the above generalized Lambert series is in fact present on page 332 of Ramanujan’s Lost Notebook
[
27
]
with more general conditions on the parameters. At the end of page 332, Ramanujan wrote

\displaystyle\frac{1^{r}}{\exp(1^{s}x)-1}+\frac{2^{r}}{\exp(2^{s}x)-1}+\frac{3^{r}}{\exp(3^{s}x)-1}+\cdots,

(1.5)

where
s
is a positive integer and
r-s
is
any even
integer. Unfortunately, Ramanujan does not give any formula for the series (
1.5
). Comparing (
1.4
) and (
1.5
), one can observe that Kanemitsu et al. studied (
1.5
) with the restriction that
r-s
is a
negative even
integer that lies in a restricted domain. This motivated Dixit and the second author to generalize the main result of Kanemitsu et al.
[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
and find a formula for (
1.5
). While extending the main result of Kanemitsu et al., Dixit and the second author
[
15
, Theorem 1.2]
obtained the following beautiful generalization of the Ramanujan’s formula for
\zeta(2m+1)
.

Let
N\geq 1
be an odd positive integer and
\alpha,\beta
be two positive real numbers such that
\alpha\beta^{N}=\pi^{N+1}
. Then for any integer non-zero integer
m\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{-\frac{2Nm}{N+1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\zeta(2Nm+1)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-2Nm-1}}{\textup{exp}\left((2n)^{N}\alpha\right)-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-\beta^{\frac{2N}{N+1}}\right)^{-m}\frac{2^{2m(N-1)}}{N}\Bigg(\frac{1}{2}\zeta(2m+1)+(-1)^{\frac{N+3}{2}}\sum_{j=\frac{-(N-1)}{2}}^{\frac{N-1}{2}}(-1)^{j}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{-2m-1}}{\textup{exp}\left((2n)^{\frac{1}{N}}\beta e^{\frac{i\pi j}{N}}\right)-1}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{m+\frac{N+3}{2}}2^{2Nm}\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N+1}{2N}+m\right\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{j}B_{2j}B_{N+1+2N(m-j)}}{(2j)!(N+1+2N(m-j))!}\alpha^{\frac{2j}{N+1}}\beta^{N+\frac{2N^{2}(m-j)}{N+1}}.

(1.6)

This formula is of interest as it establishes a relation between two distinct odd zeta values, namely
\zeta(2m+1)
and
\zeta(2Nm+1)
when
N>1
.
To derive the above generalization of Ramanujan’s formula for
\zeta(2m+1)
, Dixit and the second author studied the following line integration representation of (
1.4
):

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{N-2h}}{\exp(n^{N}x)-1}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c_{0}-i\infty}^{c_{0}+i\infty}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)\zeta(Ns-(N-2h))x^{-s}\mathrm{d}s,

where
c_{0}>\max(1,(N-2h+1)/N)
.

Inspired by the work of Dixit and the second author, in the current paper, we would like to investigate the following line integration:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}\mathrm{d}s,

where
k\in\mathbb{N},r\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c>\max\left(1/k,1+r\right)
.
Surprisingly, when
k-r
is an
even
integer, we obtain nice transformation formulas which allow us to derive many well-known formulas in the literature, for example, Ramanujan’s formula for
\zeta(1/2)
, Wigert’s formula for
\zeta(1/k)
for
k\geq
2 even, and Ramanujan’s formula for odd zeta values.
Furthermore, we also obtain a new identity for
\zeta(-1/2)
analogous to Ramanujan’s formula for
\zeta(1/2)
.

2.
Preliminaries

The study of the well-known divisor function
d(n)
plays an important role in analytic number theory. This function has been generalized in many directions, and one of the important generalizations is defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(m):=\sum_{d|m}d^{k},

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
In this paper, we encountered two new divisor functions that are defined by

\displaystyle D_{k,r}(n):=\sum_{d^{k}|n}\left(\frac{n}{d^{k}}\right)^{r},\quad{\rm and}\quad S_{k,r}(n):=\sum_{d^{k}|n}\left(\frac{n}{d^{k}}\right)^{-r}d^{k-1},

(2.1)

where
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}
. At this point, it may look artificial to define the above two divisor functions, but in due time we will see that these divisor functions naturally arise with the theory. One can easily observe that

\displaystyle D_{1,r}(n)=\sigma_{r}(n),\quad{\rm and}\quad S_{1,r}=\sigma_{-r}(n).

(2.2)

It is well-known that
\zeta(s)\zeta(s-k)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{k}(n)}{n^{s}}
, absolutely convergent for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>{\rm max}\{1,k+1\}
.
We now look for the Dirichlet series associated to
D_{k,r}(n)
and
S_{k,r}(n)
in terms of the Riemann zeta function. Using the definition (
2.1
), we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{D_{k,r}(n)}{n^{s}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n_{1}^{k}|n}\left(\frac{n}{n_{1}^{k}}\right)^{r}\right)\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{1}^{ks}}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n_{2}^{s-r}}=\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r),

(2.3)

valid for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\max\left(\frac{1}{k},1+r\right)
. Similarly, with the help of the definition (
2.1
), one can show that, for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\max(1,1-r)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{S_{k,r}(n)}{n^{s}}=\zeta(1+ks-k)\zeta(s+r).

(2.4)

We observe that the Lambert series
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{k}(m)e^{-mx}
associated to the divisor function
\sigma_{k}(m)
is in fact present in Ramanujan’s formula (
1
) as it can be clearly seen from the relation that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{k}(m)e^{-mx}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d|m}d^{k}\right)e^{-mx}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}d^{k}e^{-mdx}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{k}}{e^{nx}-1}.

Let us try to find a similar expression for the Lambert series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{k,r}(n)e^{-nx}
associated to the new divisor function
D_{k,r}(n)
.
We know that, for
x>0
,
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e^{-n^{k}mx}=\frac{1}{e^{n^{k}x}-1}
. Now differentiating both sides of this series
r
times, and then taking sum over
n
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{r}(n^{k}m)^{r}e^{-n^{k}mx}=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{r}}{\mathrm{d}x^{r}}\left(\frac{1}{e^{n^{k}x}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{r}e^{-n^{k}mx}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{n^{kr}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{r}}{\mathrm{d}x^{r}}\left(\frac{1}{e^{n^{k}x}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{k,r}(n)e^{-nx}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{r}}{n^{kr}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{r}}{\mathrm{d}x^{r}}\left(\frac{1}{e^{n^{k}x}-1}\right),\quad{\rm for}\,\,{r\geq 0}.

(2.5)

Next, we state another important special function, the Meijer
G
-function, which is the generalization of many well-known special functions in the literature.

Let
0\leq m\leq q
,
0\leq n\leq p
. Let
a_{1},\cdots,a_{p}
and
b_{1},\cdots,b_{q}
complex numbers such that
a_{i}-b_{j}\not\in\mathbb{N}
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq m
. Then the Meijer
G
-function
[
24
, p. 415, Definition 16.17]
is defined by the line integral:

\displaystyle G_{p,q}^{\,m,n}\!\left(\,\begin{matrix}a_{1},\cdots,a_{p}\\
b_{1},\cdots,b_{q}\end{matrix}\;\Big|z\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{m}\Gamma(b_{j}+s)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(1-a_{j}-s)z^{-s}}{\prod_{j=m+1}^{q}\Gamma(1-b_{j}-s)\prod_{j=n+1}^{p}\Gamma(a_{j}+s)}\mathrm{d}s,

(2.6)

where the vertical line of integration
L
, going from
-i\infty
to
+i\infty
, separates the poles of the factors
\Gamma(1-a_{j}-s)
from those of the factors
\Gamma(b_{j}+s)
. This integral converges if
p+q<2(m+n)
and
|\arg(z)|<(m+n-\frac{p+q}{2})\pi
.

Now we are ready to state the main results.

4.
Well-known results

An asymmetric form of the functional equation of
\zeta(s)
can be written as

\displaystyle\zeta(s)=2^{s}\pi^{s-1}\zeta(1-s)\Gamma(1-s)\sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right).

(4.1)

We know that
\zeta(s)
has a simple pole at
s=1
with residue
1
and the Laurent series expansion is

\displaystyle\zeta(s)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\gamma+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}\gamma_{n}(s-1)^{n}}{n!},

(4.2)

where
\gamma_{n}
are called the Stieltjes constants and defined by

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{(\log j)^{n}}{j}-\frac{(\log i)^{n+1}}{n+1}\right),

and

\gamma
is the well-known Euler–Mascheroni constant.
The Laurent series expansion of
\Gamma(s)
at
s=0
is

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)=\frac{1}{s}-\gamma+\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\right)s-\frac{1}{6}\left(\gamma^{3}+\gamma\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}+2\zeta(3)\right)s^{2}+\cdots.

(4.3)

More generally,
\Gamma(s)
has Laurent series expansion at every negative integer.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
r\leq-2
, we have

\Gamma(s)=\frac{a_{-1}}{s-(1+r)}+a_{0}+a_{1}(s-(1+r))+\cdots,

where

a_{-1}=\frac{(-1)^{r+1}}{(-(1+r))!}

and
a_{0}=\frac{(-1)^{r+1}}{(-(1+r))!}\left(-\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{-(1+r)}\frac{1}{k}\right)
.

Euler proved that
\Gamma(s)
satisfy the following beautiful reflection formula:

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1-s)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi s)},\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{C-Z}.

(4.4)

The multiplication formula for the gamma function stated as

Lemma 4.2
.

For any positive integer
k
,

\displaystyle\Gamma(ks)=\frac{k^{ks}}{\sqrt{k}(2\pi)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\Gamma(s)\Gamma\left(s+\frac{l}{k}\right).

(4.5)

In particular, substituting
k=2
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma(2s)=\frac{2^{2s-1}}{\pi}\Gamma(s)\Gamma\left(s+\frac{1}{2}\right).

This is known as duplication formula.

The next result gives an important information about the asymptotic expansion of the gamma function, popularly known as Stirling’s formula.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
s=\sigma+iT
in a vertical strip
\alpha\leq\sigma\leq\beta
,

|\Gamma(\sigma+iT)|=\sqrt{2\pi}|T|^{\sigma-1/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\pi|T|}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{|T|}\right)\right),

(4.6)

as
|T|\rightarrow\infty
.

The proof of this result can be found in
[
19
, p. 151]
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For
\sigma\geq\sigma_{0}
,
there exist a
constant
C(\sigma_{0})
, such that

|\zeta(\sigma+iT)|\ll|T|^{C(\sigma_{0})}

as
|T|\rightarrow\infty.

One can find the proof in
[
30
, p. 95]
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{e^{iuv}}{\exp(ae^{-iu})-1}\right)=\frac{\cos(a\sin(u)+uv)-e^{-a\cos(u)}\cos(uv)}{\cosh(a\cos(u))-\cos(a\sin(u))}.

In a similar vein, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
a,u,v
be three real numbers. Then

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{ie^{iuv}}{\exp(ae^{-iu})-1}\right)=\frac{-\sin(uv+a\sin(u))+e^{-a\cos(u)}\sin(uv)}{\cosh(a\cos(u))-\cos(a\sin(u))}.

The left hand side expression can be written as

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{ie^{iuv}}{\exp(ae^{-iu})-1}\right)

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{-\sin(uv)+i\cos(uv)}{e^{a\cos(u)}(\cos(a\sin(u))-i\sin(a\sin(u)))-1}\right),

now multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate of the denominator reduces to

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{(-\sin(uv)+i\cos(uv))({e^{a\cos(u)}\cos(a\sin(u))+ie^{a\cos(u)}\sin(a\sin(u)))-1})}{e^{2a\cos(u)}-2e^{a\cos(u)}(\cos(a\sin(u)))+1}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{-e^{a\cos(u)}(\cos(a\sin(u))\sin(uv))+\sin(uv)-e^{a\cos(u)}(\sin(a\sin(u))\cos(uv))}{e^{2a\cos(u)}-2e^{a\cos(u)}(\cos(a\sin(u)))+1}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{-e^{a\cos(u)}(\sin(uv+a\sin(u))+\sin(uv)}{e^{2a\cos(u)}-2e^{a\cos(u)}(\cos(a\sin(u)))+1}\right),

\displaystyle=\frac{-\sin(uv+a\sin(u))+e^{-a\cos(u)}\sin(uv)}{\cosh(a\cos(u))-\cos(a\sin(u))}.

In the final step we divided by

on the numerator as well as on the denominator.

5.
Proof of main results

It is well-known that
e^{-x}
is the inverse Mellin transform of the gamma function
\Gamma(s)
, that is, for any
x>0
,

\displaystyle e^{-x}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\Gamma(s)x^{-s}\mathrm{d}s,

(5.1)

valid for any
c>0
.
Replacing
x
by
nx
in (
5.1
) and using (
2.3
), one can easily show that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{k,r}(n)e^{-nx}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}\textrm{d}s,

(5.2)

provided with
\operatorname{Re}(s)=c>\max(\frac{1}{k},1+r)
.
In order to evaluate this line integral we choose a contour
\mathcal{C}
in a way so that all the poles of the integrand function lie inside the contour
\mathcal{C}
,
determined by the line segments
[c-iT,c+iT],[c+iT,-\lambda+iT],[-\lambda+iT,-\lambda-iT]
, and
[-\lambda-iT,c-iT]
, where we wisely take
\lambda>\max(0,-r)
and
T
is some large positive real number. The essence of this choice of
\lambda
is justified at the later stage in our proof.
Now applying Cauchy’s residue theorem, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-iT}^{c+iT}+\int_{c+iT}^{-\lambda+iT}+\int_{-\lambda+iT}^{-\lambda-iT}+\int_{-\lambda-iT}^{c-iT}

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho}\underset{s=\rho}{\operatorname{Res}}\,\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s},

(5.3)

where the sum over
\rho
runs through all the poles of
\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}
inside the contour
\mathcal{C}
.

First, let us analyse the poles of the integrand function.
We know that
\Gamma(s)
has simple poles at
s=0
and negative integers. The poles at negative integers are getting cancelled by the trivial zeros of
\zeta(ks)
as we are dealing with
k
even. Thus, the only simple poles of the integrand function
\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}
are at
s=0,\frac{1}{k}
and
1+r
, due to the simple pole of
\zeta(ks)
at
s=1/k
and the pole of
\zeta(s-r)
at
s=1+r
.
Here we would like to mention that, as we have considered
r
is any even integer, so it may happen that
1+r
is a negative integer, and in that case, it seems that
1+r
is a pole of order
2
due to the factor
\Gamma(s)\zeta(s-r)
, but
\zeta(ks)
will have a zero at
1+r
since
k
is even. This shows that
1+r
remains a simple pole of the integrand function as we are dealing with
k\geq 2
is even and
r
is even.

One can easily evaluate residues at
s=0
and
s=\frac{1}{k}
. Let
R_{\rho}
denotes the residue term at
\rho
. Using residue calculation methods, we have

\displaystyle R_{0}=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}s\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}=\zeta(0)\zeta(-r)=-\frac{1}{2}\zeta(-r).

(5.4)

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle R_{\frac{1}{k}}=\lim_{s\rightarrow\frac{1}{k}}\left(s-\frac{1}{k}\right)\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}=\frac{1}{k}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\zeta\left(\frac{1}{k}-r\right)x^{-\frac{1}{k}}.

(5.5)

Here we note that while calculating the residue at
s=1+r
, we have to make two different cases.
First, if
r\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle R_{1+r}=\lim_{s\rightarrow 1+r}(s-(1+r))\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}

\displaystyle=\Gamma(1+r)\zeta(k(1+r))x^{-(1+r)}

\displaystyle=r!\zeta(k(1+r))x^{-(1+r)}.

(5.6)

Second, if
r<0
is a negative even integer, then
1+r
is also a negative integer. Thus,
\Gamma(s)
satisfy the Laurent series expansion (
4.1
) at
1+r
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 1+r}(s-1-r)\Gamma(s)=\frac{(-1)^{1+r}}{(-(1+r))!}.

(5.7)

Again,
\zeta(ks)
has a trivial zero at
s=1+r
as
k
is even, so we have

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 1+r}\frac{\zeta(ks)}{(s-1-r)}=k\zeta^{\prime}(k(1+r)).

(5.8)

Combining (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) and together with the fact that
1+r
is a simple pole of
\zeta(s-r)
, we obtain

\displaystyle R_{1+r}=\lim_{s\rightarrow(1+r)}(s-(1+r))\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}=\frac{(-1)^{1+r}}{(-(1+r))!}k\zeta^{\prime}(k(1+r))x^{-(1+r)}.

(5.9)

We now proceed to show that the horizontal integrals

H_{1}(T,x):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c+iT}^{-\lambda+iT}\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}{\rm d}s

and

H_{2}(T,x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\lambda-iT}^{c-iT}\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}{\rm d}s

vanish as
T\rightarrow\infty
.
Replace
s
by
\sigma+iT
in
H_{1}(T,x)
to see

\displaystyle|H_{1}(T,x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c}^{d}\Gamma(\sigma+iT)\zeta(k\sigma+ikT)\zeta(\sigma+iT-r)x^{-\sigma+iT}{\rm d}\sigma\right|

\displaystyle\ll\int_{c}^{d}|\Gamma(\sigma+iT)\zeta(k\sigma+ikT)\zeta(\sigma+iT-r)|x^{-\sigma}{\rm d}\sigma

\displaystyle\ll|T|^{A}\exp\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}|T|\right),

for some constant
A
. In the final step, we used Stirling’s formula for
\Gamma(s)
i.e., Lemma
4.3
and bound for
\zeta(s)
i.e., Lemma
4.4
.
This immediately implies that
H_{1}(T,x)
vanishes as
T\rightarrow\infty
. Similarly, one can show that
H_{2}(T,x)
also vanishes as
T\rightarrow\infty
. Now letting
T\rightarrow\infty
in (
5
) and collecting all the residual terms, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=R_{0}+R_{\frac{1}{k}}+R_{1+r}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(-\lambda)}\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}{\rm d}s.

(5.10)

Here and throughout the paper we denote
\int_{(c)}
by
\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}
.
Now one of our main objectives is to evaluate the following vertical integral

\displaystyle V_{k,r}(x):=\int_{(-\lambda)}\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}{\rm d}s.

(5.11)

Utilize the asymmetric form (
4.1
) of the functional equation of
\zeta(s)
to write

\displaystyle\zeta(ks)

\displaystyle=2^{ks}\pi^{ks-1}\zeta(1-ks)\Gamma(1-ks)\sin\left(\frac{\pi ks}{2}\right),

(5.12)

\displaystyle\zeta(s-r)

\displaystyle=2^{s-r}\pi^{s-r-1}\zeta(1-s+r)\Gamma(1-s+r)\sin\left(\frac{\pi(s-r)}{2}\right).

(5.13)

Substituting (
5.12
) and (
5.13
) in (
5.11
), the vertical integral becomes

\displaystyle V_{k,r}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r}\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(-\lambda)}

\displaystyle\Gamma(s)\Gamma(1-ks)\Gamma(1-s+r)\zeta(1-ks)\zeta(1-s+r)

\displaystyle\times\sin\left(\frac{\pi ks}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi(s-r)}{2}\right)\left(\frac{(2\pi)^{k+1}}{x}\right)^{s}\textrm{d}s.

Now to shift the line of integration, we replace
s
by
1-s
. Then we have

\displaystyle V_{k,r}(x)=\frac{(2\pi)^{k+1-r}}{\pi^{2}x}\frac{1}{2\pi i}

\displaystyle\int_{(1+\lambda)}\Gamma(1-s)\Gamma(1-k+ks)\Gamma(s+r)\zeta(1-k+ks)\zeta(s+r)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{\pi k(1-s)}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi(1-s-r)}{2}\right)\left(\frac{(2\pi)^{k+1}}{x}\right)^{-s}\textrm{d}s.

(5.14)

Since
k
and
r
are both even, the following identities hold

\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{\pi k(1-s)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k}{2}-1}\sin\left(\frac{ks\pi}{2}\right),

\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{\pi(1-s-r)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-(k-1)}^{(k-1)}{}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}i^{j}e^{-\frac{i\pi j}{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{S_{k,-1}(n)}{n}\exp\left(-e^{\frac{i\pi j}{2k}}(2\pi)^{1+\frac{1}{k}}\left(\frac{n}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-(k-1)}^{(k-1)}{}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{d}\exp\left(-e^{\frac{i\pi j}{2k}}(2\pi)^{1+\frac{1}{k}}d\left(\frac{m}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=-(k-1)}^{(k-1)}{}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\log\left[1-\exp\left(-e^{\frac{i\pi j}{2k}}(2\pi)^{1+\frac{1}{k}}\left(\frac{m}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\right)\right].

(5.59)

Now substituting (
5.59
) in (
5.58
), one can finish the proof of (
3.8
).
∎

Plugging
k=1
in (
3.8
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{1,-1}(n)e^{-nx}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{x}{2\pi}\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6x}-\frac{x}{24}-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\log\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{4\pi^{2}m}{x}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d|n}\frac{1}{d}e^{-nx}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{x}{2\pi}\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6x}-\frac{x}{24}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{d}e^{-\frac{4\pi^{2}md}{x}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n(e^{nx}-1)}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{x}{2\pi}\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6x}-\frac{x}{24}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n\left(e^{\frac{4\pi^{2}n}{x}}-1\right)}.

Finally, replacing
x=2\alpha
and
\beta=\pi^{2}/\alpha
and simplifying we obtain (
3.9
).
∎

6.
Concluding Remarks

On page 332, Ramanujan recorded an intriguing formula for
\zeta(1/2)
and also mentioned the following interesting infinite series

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}\frac{n^{r}}{\exp(n^{N}x)-1},

where
N\in\mathbb{N},r\in\mathbb{Z}
and
N-r
is any even integer. This infinite series can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)\zeta(Ns-r)x^{-s}\textrm{d}s,

for
c>\max\left(1,\frac{1+r}{N}\right)
. Recently, Dixit and the second author obtained a beautiful generalization of Ramanujan’s formula for odd zeta values while studying the above integral.

In the current paper, we have studied a variant of the above integral,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{k,r}(n)e^{-nx}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\Gamma(s)\zeta(ks)\zeta(s-r)x^{-s}\textrm{d}s,

where for
k\in\mathbb{N},r\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
c>\max\left(1/k,1+r\right)
. The analysis of this integral can be divided into the following four cases.

Case 1:
When
k
and
r
are both are even integers, Theorem
3.1
gives us a transformation formula, which allows us to derive Ramanujan’s formula for
\zeta(1/2)
and Wigert’s formula for
\zeta(1/k)
.

Case 2:
When
k
is even and
r
is odd, i.e.,
k-r
is an odd integer. In this case, Theorem
3.4
allows us to obtain a new identity for
\zeta(-1/2)
analogous to Ramanujan’s formula for
\zeta(1/2)
.

Case 3:
When
k
and
r
are both odd, i.e.,
k-r
is an even integer. This is the most interesting case. Theorem
3.7
allows us to derive Ramanujan’s formula for odd zeta values. Corollary
3.8
can be conceived as a one-variable generalization of Ramanujan’s formula for odd zeta values, which connects two odd zeta values
\zeta(2m+1)
, and
\zeta(2km+1)
and a zeta value at a rational argument
\zeta\left(\frac{1}{k}+2m+1\right)
.

Case 4:
When
k
is odd and
r
is even, i.e.,
k-r
is an odd integer. In this case, the integrand function will have infinitely many poles, namely, at all odd negative integers. In the current paper, we have not discussed this case. It would be interesting to study this case too.

Ramanujan’s formula (
1
) for
\zeta(2m+1)
has been generalized by many mathematicians in various directions. Ramanujan himself gave a huge generalization
[
10
, Entry 20, p. 430]
. Berndt
[
7
]
derived Euler’s formula for
\zeta(2m)
as well as Ramanujan’s formula for
\zeta(2m+1)
from a single transformation formula for a generalized Eisenstein series. The Dirichlet
L
-function
L(s,\chi)
and the Hurwitz zeta function
\zeta(s,a)
are natural generalizations of the Riemann zeta function. A character analogue of Ramanujan’s formula was obtained by Merrill
[
22
]
and more generally, for any periodic function was obtained by Bradley
[
14
]
. Dixit, Kumar, Gupta and the second author
[
17
]
found a generalization for Hurwitz zeta function, which allowed them to connect many odd zeta values from a single formula. Dixit and Gupta
[
16
]
established an interesting analogue of Ramanujan’s formula which connects square of odd zeta values. Very recently, Banerjee and Kumar
[
6
]
found an analogue of Ramanujan’s formula (
1
) over an imaginary quadratic fields.
For more references on the generalizations of Ramanujan’s formula, readers are encouraged to see
[
13
]
.
