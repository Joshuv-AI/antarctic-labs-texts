Gradient flow structure and convergence analysis of the ensemble Kalman inversion for nonlinear forward models
Simon Weissmann

Abstract
The ensemble Kalman inversion (EKI) is a particle based method which has been introduced as the application of the ensemble Kalman filter to inverse problems. In practice it has been widely used as derivative-free optimization method in order to estimate unknown parameters from noisy measurement data. For linear forward models the EKI can be viewed as gradient flow preconditioned by a certain sample covariance matrix. Through the preconditioning the resulting scheme remains in a finite dimensional subspace of the original high-dimensional (or even infinite dimensional) parameter space and can be viewed as optimizer restricted to this subspace. For general nonlinear forward models the resulting EKI flow can only be viewed as gradient flow in approximation. In this paper we discuss the effect of applying a sample covariance as preconditioning matrix and quantify the gradient flow structure of the EKI by controlling the approximation error through the spread in the particle system. The ensemble collapse on the one side leads to an accurate gradient approximation, but on the other side to degeneration in the preconditioning sample covariance matrix. In order to ensure convergence as optimization method we derive lower as well as upper bounds on the ensemble collapse. Furthermore, we introduce covariance inflation without breaking the subspace property intending to reduce the collapse rate of the ensemble such that the convergence rate improves. In a numerical experiment we apply EKI to a nonlinear elliptic boundary-value problem and illustrate the dependence of EKI as derivative-free optimizer on the choice of the initial ensemble.

Ensemble Kalman inversion, Tikhonov regularization, derivative-free optimization, subspace property, covariance inflation

Introduction

In inverse problems we are concerned with the task of recovering some unknown quantity of interest, which can not be observed directly. Its research area has a wide range of applications in science and engineering, including among others medical imaging and geophysics. Since inverse problems are typically ill-posed, much research focuses on theoretical and practical analysis of regularization methods. Regularization techniques help to overcome instability issues arising for example through noise in the data. Beside deterministic regularization methods, the Bayesian approach formulates inverse problems in a statistical framework. By formulating a probabilistic approach, it incorporates uncertainty of the underlying model. The regularization can then be viewed as incorporating prior information through a probability distribution on the unknown parameter. The solution of the Bayesian inverse problem is given by the posterior distribution - the distribution of the unknown parameter conditioned on the realization of the observation. The resulting posterior distribution is often not accessible directly, such that sampling or suitable integration methods are needed.

In this document, we focus on a particle based method which is commonly used for data assimilation problems - the ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF). The EnKF has been introduced by Evensen
[
27
,
28
]
and more recently, formulated for solving inverse problems
[
38
]
. The application of the EnKF to inverse problem has been established as widely used tool and is known as ensemble Kalman inversion (EKI).
The aim of this manuscript is to analyse EKI as derivative-free optimization method for general nonlinear forward models.
Our analysis will be based on the continuous-time formulation of the scheme, which can be viewed as (stochastic) ordinary differential equation ((S)ODE) in time, with focus on the deterministic setting, i.e. without perturbed observations. We will quantify the approximation of derivatives through the ensemble spread and derive sufficient lower and upper bounds on the collapse in order to verify convergence of the scheme.

1.2
Main contributions

In this article we are going to quantify EKI as derivative-free optimization method in the context of general nonlinear forward maps. We employ a gradient flow structure and present a convergence analysis based on Lyapunov functions. With this document, we extend the convergence analysis for TEKI presented in
[
13
]
where the convergence result was based on linear forward models. We make the following contributions:

•

We present the well-posedness of the TEKI flow by proving unique existence of solutions for (
10
). Furthermore, we view the TEKI flow as approximate gradient flow by decomposing the flow into the preconditioned gradient direction and the approximation error resulting through Taylor expansion.

•

We quantify the approximation error through the spread in the particle system. While the ensemble collapses in time with rate
1/t
, we will see that the approximation error degenerates with rate
(1/t)^{3/2}
. In contrast, we prove that the sample covariance remains strictly-positive definite as operator acting on the subspace spanned by the initial ensemble spread
\mathcal{B}=u_{0}^{\perp}+\spn\{u_{0}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{0}\mid j=1,\dots,J\}
for a specific
u_{0}^{\perp}\in\mathcal{X}
.

•

We describe the effect of preconditioning through the sample covariance. Therefore, we view the minimization task of
\Phi_{R}
over the subspace
\mathcal{B}
as equality constrained optimization method and derive a Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL)-type inequality restricted to the subspace
\mathcal{B}
. Under strong convexity and smoothness of
\Phi_{R}
we are then able to prove convergence with rate
(1/t)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
,
\alpha\geq 1
.

•

We incorporate covariance inflation without breaking the subspace property. The inflation sufficiently slows down the ensemble collapse such that the convergence rate
\alpha
can be improved to
(1-\rho)\alpha
, where
\rho\in[0,1)
is the inflation factor. Furthermore, we view our proposed covariance inflation scheme as generalization of the ensemble square root filter (ESRF) applied to inverse problems.

2
Mathematical setup

In the following we will introduce the mathematical background. We consider the inverse problem of recovering
u\in\mathcal{X}
given noisy observations

y=G(u)+\eta,

(1)

where
\mathcal{X}
is some Hilbert space,
G:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
is the possibly nonlinear forward map and
\eta
denotes additive Gaussian noise, i.e.
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Gamma)
for some symmetric positive definite noise covariance matrix
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
. Due to ill-posedness of inverse problems, we typically consider the task of minimization of a regularized loss functional of the form

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}(G(x),y)+\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{X}}(x),

where we describe the discrepancy to the data through
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and incorporate prior information through the regularization function
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{X}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We refer to

[
24
,
3
]
for an overview of various types of regularization methods. Classical choices of regularization include Tikhonov regularization
[
25
]
and total variation regularization
[
15
,
54
]
. Throughout this document, our focus will be on the finite dimensional case
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
and the Tikhonov regularized lossfunction of the form

\Phi_{R}(x)=\Phi(x;y)+R(x),\quad R(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{C_{0}}^{2},

(2)

where we define the least-square data misfit by

\Phi(x;y)=\frac{1}{2}\|G(x)-y\|_{\Gamma}^{2}

(3)

and
R
denotes the regularization function with symmetric positive definite regularization matrix
C_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
. Here, we have introduced the notation
\|\cdot\|_{\Gamma}:=\|\Gamma^{-1/2}\cdot\|
,
\|\cdot\|_{C_{0}}=\|C_{0}^{-1/2}\cdot\|
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
respectively. Moreover, we will make use of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{HS}}
as notation for the Hilbert-Schmidt operator norm.

The Tikhonov regularization is closely connected to the Bayesian approach for inverse problems
[
58
]
which incorporates regularization from a statistical point of view. In a probabilistic model we let
u
be an
\mathcal{X}
-valued random variable with prior distribution
\mu_{0}
stochastically independent of the noise
\eta
. When viewing
(u,y)
as a jointly distributed random variable on
\mathbb{R}^{K}\times\mathcal{X}
, the solution of the Bayesian inverse problem is given by the posterior distribution of
u\mid y

\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp(-\Phi(x;y))\mu_{0}({\mathrm{d}}x),

(4)

with normalization constant

Z:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}\exp(-\Phi(x;y))\mu_{0}({\mathrm{d}}x).

We note for a Gaussian prior assumption
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,C_{0})
the maximum a-posteriori estimate computes as

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ \Phi(x;y)+\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{C_{0}}^{2},

which relates the Bayesian approach for inverse problems to the Tikhonov regularization through (
2
). Throughout this manuscript we assume that
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
is fixed and suppress the dependence of
\Phi
on
y
by writing
\Phi(x)=\Phi(x;y)
.

2.1
The ensemble Kalman filter applied to inverse problems

The EKI has been introduced as the application of the EnKF to inverse problems
[
38
]
. We will follow the derivation of EKI as sequential Monte Carlo method aiming to approximate the posterior distribution. Therefore, we introduce
the tempered distribution

\mu_{n+1}({\mathrm{d}}u)=\frac{1}{Z_{n}}\exp(-h\Phi(u;y))\mu_{n}({\mathrm{d}}u),{\quad n=1,\dots,N,\quad N\in\mathbb{N},}

(5)

with
h=1/N
and normalizing constants
Z_{n}
, where
\mu_{0}
is the prior distribution and
\mu_{N}
corresponds to the posterior distribution. This step incorporates an artificial discrete time system shifting weight from prior to posterior distribution. Given a sample from the prior distribution, the EKI evolves the sample as particle system through Gaussian approximation steps. Let
(u_{0}^{(j)})_{j\in\{1,\dots,J\}}
be the initial ensemble of size
J\geq 2
and consider the empirical approximation of the tempering distribution defined in (
5
)

\mu_{n}(\mathrm{d}u)\approx\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{u_{n}^{(j)}}(\mathrm{d}u).

Defining the following empirical means and covariances

\displaystyle C(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}(u^{(j)}-\bar{u})\otimes(u^{(j)}-\bar{u}),\quad\bar{u}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}u^{(j)},

\displaystyle C^{G,G}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}(G(u^{(j)})-\bar{G})\otimes(G(u^{(j)})-\bar{G}),\quad\bar{G}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}G(u^{(j)}),

\displaystyle C^{u,G}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}(u^{(j)}-\bar{u})\otimes(G(u^{(j)})-\bar{G}),

\displaystyle C^{G,u}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}(G(u^{(j)})-\bar{G})\otimes(u^{(j)}-\bar{u}),

the ensemble Kalman iteration for the current particle system
(u_{n}^{(j)})_{j\in\{1,\dots,J\}}
is given by

u_{n+1}^{(j)}=u_{n}^{(j)}+C^{u,G}(u_{n})(C^{G,G}(u_{n})+h^{-1}\Gamma)^{-1}(y_{n+1}^{(j)}-G(u_{n}^{(j)})),\quad j=,1\dots,J,

(6)

where
h>0
is a artificial step size and
y_{n+1}^{(j)}
are perturbed observations

y_{n+1}^{(j)}=y+\xi_{n+1}^{(j)},\quad\xi_{n+1}^{(j)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h^{-1}\Gamma)

with
\xi_{n+1}^{(j)}
being i.i.d. with respect to both
j
and
n
. Considering the limit
h\to 0
the discrete EKI (
6
) can be viewed as time discretization of the system of coupled SDEs

{\mathrm{d}}u_{t}^{(j)}=C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(y-G(u_{t}^{(j)}))\,{\mathrm{d}}t+C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1/2}\,{\mathrm{d}}W_{t}^{(j)},

(7)

where
W^{(j)}
are independent Brownian motions on
\mathbb{R}^{K}
. The continuous-time limit has been formulated in
[
56
]
and theoretical verified in
[
8
,
7
]
.

Since the EKI is not consistent with the posterior distribution for general nonlinear forward maps
[
26
]
, we will focus on EKI as derivative-free optimization method. Instead of considering perturbed observations, sometimes also referred to as stochastic EKI, the EKI is often analysed in its deterministic formulation represented by a system of coupled ODEs

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(y-G(u_{t}^{(j)})),

(8)

which describes the drift term of the SDE formulation
[
56
,
57
]
. Considering a linear forward map
G(\cdot)=A\cdot
with
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{K})
the deterministic EKI can be viewed as preconditioned gradient flow written through

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=-C(u_{t})\nabla\Phi(u_{t}^{(j)}).

(9)

For general nonlinear forward maps the representation as preconditioned gradient flow holds only approximatively. In particular, using Taylor approximations we can justify that

C^{u,G}(u)\approx C(u){\mathrm{D}}G^{\ast}(u),

where
{\mathrm{D}}G
denotes the (Fréchet) derivative of
G
, and we are going to quantify the error between the nonlinear EKI and a preconditioned gradient flow. Since our aim is to view the EKI as optimization method for minimizing the data misfit functional
\Phi
, the resulting scheme is ill-posed due to the ill-posed inverse and it is natural to consider a regularized modification of the EKI. Therefore, the authors in
[
13
]
proposed to incorporate Tikhonov regularization within EKI resulting in the deterministic continuous-time formulation as coupled system of ODEs

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(y-G(u_{t}^{(j)}))-C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}u_{t}^{(j)},

(10)

which can then be viewed as derivative-free approximation to the preconditioned gradient flow

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=-C(u_{t})\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)}).

(11)

We will see that this approximation is getting more accurate for a decreasing spread of the particle system. To do so, we are going to analyse the long-time behavior of (
10
) for a fixed number of particles. Therefore, we will extend the convergence analysis presented in
[
13
]
from the linear to the nonlinear setting. Here, it has been shown that assuming a linear forward model
G(\cdot)=A\cdot,

A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{K})
the resulting solution of the TEKI flow (
10
) minimizes the objective function
\Phi_{R}
in the subspace spanned by initial ensemble spread
\mathcal{B}=u_{0}^{\perp}+\spn\{u_{0}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{0}\mid j=1,\dots,J\}
for a specific
u_{0}^{\perp}\in\mathcal{X}
.

3
Main result: Convergence of TEKI for nonlinear forward models

In order to quantify the nonlinear EKI as derivative-free approximation we make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

We assume that the objective function
\Phi_{R}
is
C^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
\mu
-strongly convex, i.e.

\Phi_{R}(x_{1})-\Phi_{R}(x_{2})\geq\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(x_{2}),x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}

for some
\mu>0
and all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, we assume that
\Phi_{R}
is
L
-smooth, i.e.

\Phi_{R}(x_{1})-\Phi_{R}(x_{2})\leq\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(x_{2}),x_{1}-x_{2}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{2}

for some
L>0
and all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
and therefore satisfies the PL-inequality

\nu\|\nabla\Phi(x)\|^{2}\geq\Phi_{R}(x)-\Phi(x_{\ast}),\quad x\in\mathcal{X}

for some
\nu>0
, where
x_{\ast}
is a stationary point, i.e.
\nabla\Phi(x_{\ast})=0
.

In the above assumption, we suppose global
\mu
-strong convexity and
L
-smoothness, which is needed to prove convergence results as global optimizer. In the case, where those assumptions are only satisfied locally, we can only expect the results to hold in local neighborhoods around critical points. The necessary convergence analysis as local optimizer is work in progress.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that the forward map
G
is
C^{2}(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}})
, locally Lipschitz with constant
c_{\mathrm{lip}}>0
and can be approximated linearly by

G(x_{1})=G(x_{2})+{\mathrm{D}}G(x_{2})(x_{1}-x_{2})+{\mathrm{Res}}(x_{1},x_{2}),

(12)

where the approximation error
{\mathrm{Res}}
is bounded by

\|{\mathrm{Res}}(x_{1},x_{2})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}}\leq b_{\mathrm{res}}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}

(13)

for some
b_{\mathrm{res}}>0
independent from
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
.

We note that Assumption
2
is satisfied for linear forward maps
G(\cdot)=A\cdot
,
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R}^{K})
with
{\mathrm{Res}}(x_{1},x_{2})=0
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
. Furthermore, it is sufficient to assume that the Hessian of
G
is uniformly bounded in the sense that
\langle z,{\mathrm{D}}^{2}G(x)z\rangle\leq c\|z\|^{2}
for all
z\in\mathcal{X}
. Alternatively, the tangential cone condition

\|G(x_{1})-G(x_{2})-{\mathrm{D}}G(x_{2})(x_{1}-x_{2})\|\leq\tilde{b}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\|G(x_{1})-G(x_{2})\|

for some uniform
\tilde{b}>0
together with Lipschitz continuity implies Assumption
2
. This condition is often assumed (locally) when studying iterative methods for nonlinear inverse problems
[
55
]
. As soon as we are able to proof uniform boundedness of the solution of the TEKI flow (
10
), it is sufficient to consider Assumption
2
locally, i.e. to assume that for
B>0
there exists
b_{\mathrm{res}}(B)>0
such that (
13
) holds for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
with
\|x_{1}\|_{\mathcal{X}},\|x_{2}\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}\leq B
. While the proof of boundedness of the TEKI flow (
10
) is typically challenging, one can enforce boundedness through a smooth modification of the forward map
G
, such that
G(x)=0
for
\|x\|>B
, see for example
[
13
,
14
]
. Considering this type of modification might be reasonable for practical implementation if it is known that solutions of the corresponding inverse problem should be bounded.

Furthermore, we note that the above Assumption
2
can be weaken through the assumption
\|{\mathrm{Res}}(x_{1},x_{2})\|\leq b_{\mathrm{res}}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|^{p}
, for
p>2
. However, to avoid technical details we will keep the assumption with
p=2
for the main proof and state more details on the extension to
p>2
in Remark
11
after the proof of our main result.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose Assumption
2
is satisfied, then it holds true that

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\right)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)}),C(u_{t})\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+b_{1}JV_{e}(t)^{3/2}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\|^{2}+\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\right),

where
b_{1}>0
is independent from
J
and
V_{e}(t):=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\|u_{t}^{(k)}-\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2}
.

The above result quantifies the (approximate) gradient flow structure of TEKI. We will see, that
V_{e}(t)
is of the order
1/t
, which means that the approximation error of the gradient through the covariance degenerates in time. However, the degeneration of
C(u_{t})
influences the preconditioning effect for the gradient as well. Although we can show, that the degeneration of
C(u_{t})
is bounded from below by the order
1/t
, it is not possible to imply lower bounds on the eigenvalues
C(u_{t})
. This issue comes from the fact that
C(u_{t})
is a sample covariance and has at most rank
\min(n_{x},J-1)
. To alleviate this problem, we will show that
C(u_{t})
will remain positive definite in the subspace spanned through the initial ensemble spread. Along the solution of the TEKI flow the particle system
(u_{t}^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
remains in the subspace

\mathcal{B}:=u_{0}^{\perp}+\spn\{u_{0}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{0}\mid j=1,\dots,J\}\subset\spn\{u_{0}^{j}\mid j=1,\dots,J\}=:\mathcal{S},

where
u_{0}^{\perp}=\bar{u}_{0}-\mathcal{P}_{E}\bar{u}_{0}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{E}:=E(E^{\top}E)^{-1}E^{T}
with
E=((u_{0}^{(1)}-\bar{u}_{0})^{\top},\dots,(u_{0}^{(J)}-\bar{u}_{0})^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times J}
is the orthogonal projection on the subspace
\spn\{u_{0}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{0}\mid j=1,\dots,J\}
. This subspace property also holds for general nonlinear forward maps
G
, see Lemma 3.7 and Corollary 3.8 in
[
13
]
.
Hence, our aim is to quantify TEKI as gradient flow w.r.t. the constrained optimization problem

\min_{x\in{\mathcal{B}}}\ \Phi_{R}(x),

(14)

where
(u_{0}^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
is the initial ensemble for TEKI. In fact, a similar result has been shown in
[
13
]
for the linear setting, where the gradient approximation is exact. We are going to extend the theory to nonlinear forward maps satisfying Assumptions
1
and
2
. Note that we will reformulate the optimization problem (
14
) as equality constrained optimization problem, implying that there exists a unique minimizer
u_{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}
of (
14
). In the following we formulate our main convergence result.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
are satisfied, let
(u_{t}^{(j)},t\geq 0)_{j=1,\dots,J}
solve (
10
) with linearly independent initial ensemble
(u_{0}^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
and let
u_{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}
be the unique global minimizer of (
14
). Then there exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})-\Phi_{R}(u_{\ast})\leq\left(\frac{c_{1}}{t+c_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}},

(15)

where
0<\alpha<\frac{L}{\mu}(\sigma_{\max}+c_{\mathrm{lip}}\lambda_{\max}\|C(u_{0})\|_{\mathrm{HS}})
.

We note that the constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
depend on various constants arising in Assumption
1
-
2

4.1
Existence of solutions and ensemble collapse

Recall that we assume a finite dimensional parameter space
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
. We start this section with verifying the well-posedness of the scheme. Therefore, we state that there exists a unique solution for the considered TEKI flow.

Theorem 5
.

Let
(u_{0}^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
be the linearly independent initial ensemble and define its linear span
S=\spn\{u_{0}^{(j)}\mid j=1,\dots,J\}
. Then for all
T>0
the equation (
10
) has a unique solution
(u_{t}^{(j)},t\in[0,T])\in C([0,T],\mathcal{S})
. In particular, it holds true that

\langle z,u_{t}^{(j)}\rangle=0

for all
z\in S^{\perp}
and all
t\in[0,T]
,
j=1,\dots,J
.

Proof.

Due to local Lipschitz continuity local solutions follow from standard ODE theory, see e.g.
[
13
, Theorem 3.2]
for details. Moreover, we refer to
[
13
, Corollary 3.8]
for the subspace property, i.e. while a solution exists it holds true that
\langle z,u_{t}^{(j)}\rangle=0

for all
z\in S^{\perp}
and all
j=1,\dots,J
. In the following, we extend the existence of solutions globally. Therefore, we consider the Lyapunov function

V_{\bar{u}}(u_{t})=\varphi(\|\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2})+\frac{B_{1}}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2}+B_{2}

for
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\varphi(z)=\log(1+z)
and sufficiently large
B_{1},B_{2}>0
. We firstly observe that
z\varphi^{\prime}(z)=\frac{z}{1+z}\leq 1
for
z\geq 0
. The time evolution of
\varphi(\|\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2})
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\varphi(\|\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=2\varphi^{\prime}(\|\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2})\left(-\langle\bar{u}_{t},C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(\bar{G}_{t}-y)\rangle-\langle\bar{u}_{t},C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}\bar{u}_{t}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\varphi^{\prime}(\|\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2})\left(\|\bar{u}_{t}\|\|\bar{G}_{t}-y\|_{\Gamma}\|C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}+\|\bar{u}_{t}\|\|\bar{u}_{t}\|_{C_{0}}\|C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varphi^{\prime}(\|\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2})^{2}\|\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2}(2\|\bar{G}_{t}-G(\bar{u}_{t})\|_{\Gamma}^{2}+2\|G(\bar{u}_{t})-y\|_{\Gamma}^{2})+\|C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\varphi^{\prime}(\|\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2})^{2}\|\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2}\|\bar{u}_{t}\|_{C_{0}}^{2}+\|C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2},

where we have applied Young’s inequality and

\|\bar{G}_{t}-y\|_{\Gamma}^{2}\leq(\|\bar{G}_{t}-G(\bar{u}_{t})\|_{\Gamma}+\|G(\bar{u}_{t})-y\|_{\Gamma})^{2}\leq 2\|\bar{G}_{t}-G(\bar{u}_{t})\|_{\Gamma}^{2}+2\|G(\bar{u}_{t})-y\|_{\Gamma})^{2}

for the last inequality.
With Lipschitz continuity of
G
, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Jensen’s inequality we have that

\displaystyle\|\bar{G}-G(\bar{u})\|^{2}=\frac{1}{J^{2}}\sum_{j,l}^{2}\langle G(u^{(j)})-G(\bar{u}),G(u^{(l)})-G(\bar{u})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|u^{(j)}-\bar{u}\|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|u^{(j)}-\bar{u}\|^{2}.

Hence, using
z\varphi^{\prime}(z)\leq 1
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\varphi(\|\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2}+\tilde{c}_{2}\varphi^{\prime}(\|\bar{u}\|^{2})\Phi_{R}(\bar{u}_{t})

\displaystyle\quad+\|C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+\|C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2}+c_{2}+\|C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}+\|C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

for some constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
. Here we have used, that
\Phi_{R}(u)\leq c\|u\|^{2}
for local Lipschitz continuous
G
. On the other side, we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{J^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{J}\langle u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t},u_{t}^{(k)}-\bar{u}_{t}\rangle\langle G(u_{t}^{(k)})-\bar{G}_{t},G(u_{t}^{(j)})-\bar{G}_{t}\rangle_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2}{J^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{J}\langle u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t},u_{t}^{(k)}-\bar{u}_{t}\rangle\langle u_{t}^{(k)}-\bar{u}_{t},C_{0}^{-1}(u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\|C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}-2\|C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}.

It follows for
B_{1}>c_{1}
and
B_{2}>c_{2}
that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}V_{\bar{u}}(u_{t})\leq cV_{\bar{u}}(u_{t})

and by application of Gronwall’s inequality for all
T>0
the solution of (
10
) remains bounded.

L_{2}

Proposition 6

Let

On the one side the spread of the particle system describes the accuracy of the gradient approximation, which gets more accurate for a less spread particle system. In our first result, we prove that the ensemble collapses with rate

Lemma 7
.

Let
(u_{t}^{(j)},t\geq 0)_{j=1,\dots,J}

where
\sigma_{\min}
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of

Proof.

The evolution of

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2}{J^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{J}\langle u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t},u_{t}^{(k)}-\bar{u}_{t}\rangle\langle u_{t}^{(k)}-\bar{u}_{t},C_{0}^{-1}(u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\|C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}

Hence, we have shown

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}V_{e}(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\leq-\frac{2\sigma_{\min}}{J}V_{e}(t)^{2}

and the assertion follows by Lyapunov-type argument.

In the following result, we state that, although the sample covariance degenerates along the particle evolution, it remains a strictly positive definite operator acting on the subspace
\mathcal{B}

Lemma 8
.

Let
(u_{t}^{(j)},t\geq 0)_{j=1,\dots,J}
be the solution of (
8
) initialized by a linearly independent ensemble
(u_{0}^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
such that

\zeta_{0}=\min_{z\in{\mathcal{B}},\ \|z\|=1}\ \langle z,C(u_{0})z\rangle>0.

(17)

For each
z\in{\mathcal{B}}

\sigma_{\max}
and
\sigma_{\min}
of
C_{0}^{-1}

\lambda_{\max}
of
\Gamma^{-1}

Proof.

We note that the following proof is closely related to the proof of Theorem 3.5 in

, where the authors derived an upper bound on the sample covariance matrix.
The time evolution of the sample covariance is given by

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(u_{t})}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}e_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\left(e_{t}^{(j)}\right)^{\top}+\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}e_{t}^{(j)}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{d}}e_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=-2C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}C^{G,u}(u_{t})-2C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}C(u_{t}).

Let

with
\|z\|=1
, then we have that

\displaystyle\langle z,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(u_{t})}{{\mathrm{d}}t}z\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\|C^{G,u}(u_{t})z\|_{\Gamma}^{2}-2\|C(u_{t})z\|_{C_{0}}^{2}.

Since

\displaystyle\|C^{G,G}(u_{t})\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=\Tr(C^{G,G}(u_{t})C^{G,G}(u_{t}))^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}V_{e}(t)\leq c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}V_{e}(0)

and therefore it follows with

\|C^{G,u}(u_{t})z\|_{\Gamma}^{2}\leq c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\lambda_{\max}V_{e}(0)\|C(u_{t})z\|_{C_{0}}^{2}.

Hence, we have derived

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\langle z,C(u_{t})z\rangle}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\geq-2(c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\lambda_{\max}V_{e}(0)+\sigma_{\max})\|C(u_{t})z\|^{2}.

\varphi(t)\in{\mathcal{B}}

Since

0=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\|\varphi(t)\|^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=2\langle\varphi(t),\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\varphi(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\rangle.

Hence, we can describe the time-evolution of the smallest eigenvalue
\zeta_{t}
of

(initialized with
\zeta_{0}>0

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\zeta_{t}=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\langle\varphi(t),C(u_{t})\varphi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi(t),\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(u_{t})}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\varphi(t)\rangle+2\langle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\varphi(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t},C(u_{t})\varphi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi(t),\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(u_{t})}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\varphi(t)\rangle+2\zeta_{t}\langle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\varphi(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t},\varphi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi(t),\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(u_{t})}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\varphi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\geq-2(c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\lambda_{\max}V_{e}(0)+\sigma_{\max})\|C(u_{t})\varphi(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-2(c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\lambda_{\max}V_{e}(0)+\sigma_{\max})\zeta_{t}^{2}.

With the above computation we obtain the lower bound on the smallest eigenvalue of

\zeta_{t}\geq\frac{1}{2(c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\lambda_{\max}V_{e}(0)+\sigma_{\max})t+\zeta_{0}^{-1}}>0,

and the assertion follows with

m:=2(c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\lambda_{\max}V_{e}(0)+\sigma_{\max})>0.

4.2
Gradient approximation via Taylor expansion

In the following we will quantify how the TEKI flow (
10
) approximates gradients and hence can be viewed as gradient flow.

Lemma 9
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
is satisfied. Given a particle system
(u^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
it holds true that

\|C^{u,G}(u)\Gamma^{-1}(G(u^{(j)})-y)-C(u)\nabla\Phi(u^{(j)})\|\leq b_{1}J\sqrt{\Phi(u^{(j)})}\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\|u^{(k)}-\bar{u}\|^{2}\right)^{3/2},

for some
b_{1}
independent of
J
.

Proof.

We apply (
12
) and using bi-linearity of the inner product we obtain

\displaystyle C^{u,G}(u)\Gamma^{-1}(G(u^{(j)})-y)=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\langle G(u^{(k)})-\bar{G},G(u^{(j)})-y\rangle_{\Gamma}(u^{k}-\bar{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\langle G(u^{(j)})+{\mathrm{D}}G(u^{(j)})(u^{(k)}-u^{(j)})+{\mathrm{Res}}(u^{(k)},u^{(j)}),G(u^{(j)})-y\rangle_{\Gamma}(u^{(k)}-\bar{u})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{J}\sum_{l=1}^{J}\langle G(u^{(j)})+{\mathrm{D}}G(u^{(j)})(u^{(l)}-u^{(j)})+{\mathrm{Res}}(u^{(l)},u^{(j)}),G(u^{(j)})-y\rangle_{\Gamma}(u^{(k)}-\bar{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\langle{\mathrm{D}}G(u^{(j)})(u^{(k)}-\bar{u}),G(u^{(j)})-y\rangle_{\Gamma}(u^{(k)}-\bar{u})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\langle{\mathrm{Res}}(u^{(k)},u^{(j)})-\overline{\mathrm{Res}}^{(j)},G(u^{(j)})-y\rangle_{\Gamma}(u^{(k)}-\bar{u})

\displaystyle=C(u)\nabla\Phi(u^{(j)})+\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\langle{\mathrm{Res}}(u^{(k)},u^{(j)})-\overline{\mathrm{Res}}^{(j)},G(u^{(j)})-y\rangle_{\Gamma}(u^{(k)}-\bar{u}),

where we have defined

\overline{\mathrm{Res}}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{l=1}^{J}{\mathrm{Res}}(u^{(l)},u^{(j)}).

It follows with Assumption
2
and Cauchy-Schwarz that

\displaystyle\|C^{u,G}(u)\Gamma^{-1}(G(u^{(j)})-y)-C(u)\nabla\Phi(u^{(j)})\|

\displaystyle\leq b_{\mathrm{res}}\|\Gamma^{-1/2}\|\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}4\left(\|u^{(k)}-u^{(j)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{J}\sum_{l=1}^{J}\|u^{(l)}-u^{(j)}\|^{2}\right)\|u^{(k)}-\bar{u}\|\|G(u^{(j)})-y\|_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\leq b_{\mathrm{res}}\|\Gamma^{-1/2}\|\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}4\left((1+\frac{1}{J})\sum_{l=1}^{J}\|u^{(l)}-u^{(j)}\|^{2}\right)\|u^{(k)}-\bar{u}\|\|G(u^{(j)})-y\|_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\leq b_{\mathrm{res}}\|\Gamma^{-1/2}\|(8+\frac{2}{J})J\|G(u^{(j)})-y\|_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\|u^{(k)}-\bar{u}\|^{2}\right)^{3/2},

where we have used that

\|u^{(l)}-u^{(j)}\|^{2}\leq 2\|u^{(l)}-\bar{u}\|^{2}+2\|u^{(j)}-\bar{u}\|^{2}.

We are now ready to prove Proposition
3
in order to quantify the gradient flow structure of TEKI.

Proof of Proposition
3
.

We apply chain rule for deriving the time-evolution of
V_{\Phi_{R}}(t):=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})
as

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}V_{\Phi_{R}}(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}V_{\Phi_{R}}(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)}),\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)}),C(u_{t})\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)}),C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(G(u_{t}^{(j)})-y)-C(u_{t})\nabla\Phi(u_{t}^{(j)})\rangle.

We apply Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
9
such that

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}V_{\Phi_{R}}(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)}),C(u_{t})\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+b_{1}JV_{e}(t)^{3/2}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\|\sqrt{\Phi(u_{t}^{(j)})}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)}),C(u_{t})\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+b_{1}JV_{e}(t)^{3/2}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\|^{2}+\Phi(u_{t}^{(j)})\right).

The claim follows with
\Phi(x)\leq\Phi_{R}(x)
.

Considering the upper bound on the derived gradient approximation in Lemma
9
, we observe that it is increasing in the ensemble size
J
. Although we do not know whether this bound is sharp, it suggests that the gradient approximation might be less well-behaved in the large ensemble size regime. To alleviate this issue, we expect improvement when considering localised preconditioning covariance matrices
C^{u,G}(u^{(j)})
for each particle
u^{(j)}
. For the localised covariance we include more weights on particles in the neighborhood around
u^{(j)}
and less weight for particles far away. This type of localisation has been considered for the ensemble Kalman sampler, where we do not expect the particle system to collapse
[
53
]
. The convergence analysis of (T)EKI under localisation is work in progress.

4.3
TEKI as constrained optimization method

Before proving our main convergence result, we need to quantify the behavior of the preconditioning effect through the sample covariance, i.e. what does it mean to consider the direction
C(u_{t})\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})
instead of the steepest descent direction given through
\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})
. We first note that once
C(u_{t})
can be identified as strictly positive definite operator on the whole parameter space
\mathcal{X}
, the scheme can be viewed as global optimization method.
However, in general it is not true that
C(u_{t})
describes a strictly positive definite operator on the parameter space
\mathcal{X}
and indeed,
C(u_{t})
has at most rank
\min(n_{x},J-1)
and hence is positive semi-definite in case
J-1<n_{x}
. While
C(u_{t})
is only positive semi-definite on the whole space
\mathcal{X}
, we have verified in Lemma
8
that
C(u_{t})
is a strictly positive definite operator acting on the subspace
\mathcal{B}
spanned through the initial ensemble. We will make use of this property in order to quantify the TEKI flow (
10
) as optimizer for the constrained optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ \Phi_{R}(x),\quad x\in\mathcal{B}=u_{0}^{\perp}+\spn\{e_{0}^{(j)}\mid j=1,\dots,J\}.

Since we have assumed the finite dimensional setting
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
, we reformulate this problem as equality constrained optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}\ \Phi_{R}(x),\quad\text{s.t.}\quad h_{i}(x)=\langle q^{(i)},x\rangle=0,\ i=J+1,\dots,n_{x},

(19)

where
q^{(i)}\perp e_{0}^{(j)}
and
q^{(i)}\perp u_{0}^{\perp}
for all
j=1,\dots,J
and
i=J+1,\dots,n_{x}
. Since under Assumption
1

\Phi_{R}
is assumed to be strongly convex, there exists a unique global solution
u_{\ast}
of (
19
)
[
6
, Proposition 2.1.1]
. By the Lagrange multiplier theorem
[
6
, Proposition 3.1.1.]
there exist
\kappa_{J+1}^{\ast},\dots,\kappa_{n_{x}}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}

\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{\ast})+\sum_{i=J+1}^{n_{x}}\kappa_{i}\nabla h_{i}(x_{\ast})=0.

(20)

We will make use of this necessary condition in order to derive the following constrained PL-type inequality.

Lemma 10
.

Suppose Assumption
1
is satisfied and let
x_{\ast}
be the unique global minimizer of (
19
). Then there exists
\nu>0
such that for all
x\in{\mathcal{B}}
it holds true that

\nu\|\nabla\Phi_{R}(x)\|^{2}\geq\Phi_{R}(x)-\Phi_{R}(x_{\ast}).

Moreover, let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{B}
. Then it holds true that

{\nu\|\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}\nabla\Phi_{R}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}x)\|^{2}\geq\Phi_{R}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}x)-\Phi_{R}(x_{\ast})}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
.

Proof.

Consider some arbitrary
x\in\mathcal{B}
. By
L
-smoothness and the necessary optimality condition (
20
) we have that

\displaystyle\Phi_{R}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{R}(x_{\ast})+\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(x_{\ast}),x-x_{\ast}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x-x_{\ast}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{R}(x_{\ast})-\sum_{i=J+1}^{n_{x}}\kappa_{i}\langle\nabla h_{i}(x_{\ast}),x-x_{\ast}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x-x_{\ast}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{R}(x_{\ast})-\sum_{i=J+1}^{n_{x}}\kappa_{i}\langle q^{(i)},x-x_{\ast}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x-x_{\ast}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{R}(x_{\ast})+\frac{L}{2}\|x-x_{\ast}\|^{2},

where we have used
x,x_{\ast}\perp q^{(i)}

On the other side with

-strong convexity and Cauchy-Schwarz we obtain

\frac{\mu}{2}\|x_{\ast}-x\|\leq\|\nabla\Phi(x)\|

leading to

\Phi_{R}(x)-\Phi_{R}(x_{\ast})\leq\frac{L}{2}\|x-x_{\ast}\|^{2}\leq\frac{L}{\mu}\|\nabla\Phi_{R}(x)\|^{2}.

For the second claim, consider
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
. By
\mu
-strong convexity it follows

\langle{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}}\nabla\Phi_{R}(x),{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}}(x-x_{\ast})\rangle=\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(x),{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}}x-x_{\ast})\rangle\geq\Phi_{R}({\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}}x)-\Phi_{R}(x_{\ast})+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}}x-x_{\ast}\|^{2}.

The assertion follows again by applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
L
-smoothness.

Proof of Theorem

We are now ready to present the proof of our main convergence result.

Proof of Theorem

.

Let

\mathcal{B}=u_{0}^{\perp}+\spn\{e_{0}^{(j)}\mid j=1,\dots,J\}

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\right)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{3}{2}b_{1}J\left(\frac{1}{\frac{2\sigma_{\min}}{J}t+V_{e}(0)^{-1}}\right)^{3/2}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}(\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})-\Phi_{R}(u_{\ast}))

\displaystyle\quad+\tilde{b}_{1}J\left(\frac{1}{\frac{2\sigma_{\min}}{J}t+V_{e}(0)^{-1}}\right)^{3/2}\Phi_{R}(u_{\ast})),

where we have used strong convexity. Due to the subspace space property in Proposition

and with Lemma

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)}),C(u_{t})\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}}\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)}),C(u_{t}){\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}}\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{mt+\zeta_{0}^{-1}}\|{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{B}}}\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\|^{2},

where

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}V_{\Phi_{R}}(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})\right)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{\frac{L}{\mu}mt+\zeta_{0}^{-1}}V_{\Phi_{R}}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\tilde{b}_{1}J\left(\frac{1}{\frac{2\sigma_{\min}}{J}t+V_{e}(0)^{-1}}\right)^{3/2}V_{\Phi_{R}}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}V(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\leq-\frac{1}{\alpha t+a}V(t)+\left(\frac{z_{1}}{z_{2}+z_{3}t}\right)^{3/2}(V(t)+c)

\displaystyle V(t)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{c_{1}}{t+c_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}},

for some constants

Remark 11
.

We note that under the relaxed assumption
\|{\mathrm{Res}}(x,y)\|\leq b_{\mathrm{res}}\|x-y\|^{p}

\displaystyle\|C^{u,G}(u)\Gamma^{-1}(G(u^{(j)})-y)-C(u)\nabla\Phi(u^{(j)})\|

6
Stabilization via covariance inflation: A generalized ensemble square root filter for inverse problems

The incorporation of covariance inflation is a data assimilation tool in order to stabilize convergence and often used in practice
[
2
,
1
,
59
]
. In the following we present a form of covariance inflation for the TEKI flow for which it turns out to be a generalized formulation of the ensemble square root filter (ESRF)
[
52
]
applied to inverse problems.

Through our incorporation of covariance inflation we obtain in approximation a weighted gradient flow structure between the current particle position and the position of the ensemble mean. Roughly speaking, the TEKI flow can then be viewed as approximation of the equation:

\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=-C(u_{t})\left((1-\rho)\nabla\Phi_{R}(u_{t}^{(j)})+\rho\nabla\Phi_{R}(\bar{u}_{t})\right),

(21)

where
\rho\in[0,1)
describes the weight of the gradients of the particles and the mean. Based on
[
5
,
4
]
the continuous time formulation of the ESRF applied to inverse problems has been analysed in
[
12
]
for linear forward models and the special case
\rho=1/2
.
In the following, we will incorporate the covariance inflation and theoretical quantify the approximate gradient flow structure. Finally, we will observe an acceleration in convergence towards the optimal solution restricted to the subspace spanned through the initial ensemble spread.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of the effect through the incorporation of the covariance inflation. Each particle is getting moved into its own direction as well as into a joint direction.

6.1
Incorporation of covariance inflation

The covariance inflation is incorporated through the following modified TEKI flow

\begin{split}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}&=C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(y-G(u_{t}^{(j)}))-C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}u_{t}^{(j)}\\
&\quad+\rho C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(G(u_{t}^{(j)})-\bar{G})+\rho C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}(u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}).\end{split}

(22)

The idea behind this incorporation comes from the fact that the inflating term does not directly change the dynamical behavior of the particles mean, since the evolution

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\bar{u}_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(y-\bar{G}_{t})-C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}\bar{u}_{t}

(23)

coincides with the unmodified dynamic (i.e.
\rho=0
). However the inflating term decreases the force of collapsing the particle system and hence leads to a slower ensemble collapse without changing the collapse rate
1/t
but decreasing the constant. For the interpretation from the optimization point of view we rewrite (
22
) as

\begin{split}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}u_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}&=(1-\rho)\left\{C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(y-G(u_{t}^{(j)}))-C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}u_{t}^{(j)}\right\}\\
&\quad+\rho\left\{C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(y-\bar{G}_{t})-C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}\bar{u}_{t}\right\}.\end{split}

(24)

motivating the modified TEKI flow as approximation of (
21
). The movement of the particle system through (
24
) is described through two forces. The first force is given by

C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(y-G(u_{t}^{(j)}))-C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}u_{t}^{(j)}

moving each particle in its own direction and leading to a collapse of the ensemble and hence to a better gradient approximation as we have already seen in Lemma
9
. The second force

C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}(y-\bar{G}_{t})-C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}\bar{u}_{t}

moves the whole particle system into the same direction without changing the spread and hence does not improve the gradient approximation. However, it accelerates the overall convergence of the optimization process since it slows down the degeneration of the preconditioning through the sample covariance matrix. We will start the theoretical analysis by considering the ensemble spread and its collapse behavior.
We first note, that unique existence of solutions of (
22
) can be verified and the subspace property Proposition
6
is obviously satisfied.

Proposition 12
.

Let
(u_{0}^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
be the linearly independent initial ensemble and define the subspace

\mathcal{B}=u_{0}^{\perp}+\spn\{e_{0}^{(j)}\mid j=1,\dots,J\},

where
u_{0}^{\perp}=\bar{u}_{0}-\mathcal{P}_{E}\bar{u}_{0}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{E}=E(E^{\top}E)^{-1}E^{T}
with
E=((e_{0}^{(1)})^{\top},\dots,(e_{0}^{(J)})^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times J}
is the orthogonal projection on the subspace
\spn\{e_{0}^{(j)}\mid j=1,\dots,J\}
. Then for all
T\geq 0
and
\rho\in[0,T)
the unique solution
(u_{t}^{(j)},t\in[0,T])
of the flow (
22
) exists and remains in
\mathcal{B}
, i.e.

u_{t}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{B}
for all
t\in[0,T]
and all
j=1,\dots,J
.

The following result states, that the ensemble collapse occurs with reduced rate through the factor
1-\rho\in(0,1]
.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\rho\in[0,1)
and
(u_{t}^{(j)},t\geq 0)_{j=1,\dots,J}
be the solution of (
22
) initialized by a linearly independent ensemble
(u_{0}^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
. Then the mapping
V_{e}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
t\mapsto\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|e_{t}\|^{2}

is bounded by

V_{e}(t)\leq\frac{1}{\frac{2(1-\rho)\sigma_{\min}}{J}t+V_{e}(0)^{-1}},

where
\sigma_{\min}
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of
C_{0}^{-1}
.

Proof.

The evolution of
V_{e}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=-(1-\rho)2\|C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1/2}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{(1-\rho)2}{J^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{J}\langle u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t},u_{t}^{(k)}-\bar{u}_{t}\rangle\langle u_{t}^{(k)}-\bar{u}_{t},C_{0}^{-1}(u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2(1-\rho)\sigma_{\min}}{J}\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|u_{t}^{(j)}-\bar{u}_{t}\|^{2}\right)^{2},

and the assertion follows again by Lyapunov-type argument.

Similarly, we can derive the lower bound of the ensemble collapse.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\rho\in[0,1)
and
(u_{t}^{(j)},t\geq 0)_{j=1,\dots,J}
be the solution of (
22
) initialized by a linearly independent ensemble
(u_{0}^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
such that

\zeta_{0}=\min_{z\in{\mathcal{B}},\ \|z\|=1}\ \langle z,C(u_{0})z\rangle>0.

For each
z\in{\mathcal{B}}
with
\|z\|=1
it holds true that

\langle z,C(u_{t})z\rangle\geq\frac{1}{(1-\rho)mt+\zeta_{0}^{-1}},

where
m=2(c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\lambda_{\max}V_{e}(0)+\sigma_{\max})>0
depends on the smallest and largest eigenvalue
\sigma_{\max}
and
\sigma_{\min}
of
C_{0}^{-1}
, the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}
of
\Gamma^{-1}
and the Lipschitz constant
c_{\mathrm{lip}}
of
G
.

Proof.

The time evolution of the sample covariance is given by

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(u_{t})}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}e_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\left(e_{t}^{(j)}\right)^{\top}+\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}e_{t}^{(j)}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{d}}e_{t}^{(j)}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=-2(1-\rho)C^{u,G}(u_{t})\Gamma^{-1}C^{G,u}(u_{t})-2(1-\rho)C(u_{t})C_{0}^{-1}C(u_{t})

and similarly, for
z\in{\mathcal{B}}
with
\|z\|=1
we obtain

\displaystyle\langle z,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}C(u_{t})}{{\mathrm{d}}t}z\rangle

\displaystyle=-2(1-\rho)\|C^{G,u}(u_{t})z\|_{\Gamma}^{2}-2(1-\rho)\|C(u_{t})z\|_{C_{0}}^{2}

and therefore

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\langle z,C(u_{t})z\rangle}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\geq-2(1-\rho)(c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\lambda_{\max}V_{e}(0)+\sigma_{\max})\|C(u_{t})z\|^{2}.

We consider again the smallest eigenvalue
\zeta_{t}\geq 0
of
C(u_{t})
restricted to
\mathcal{B}
with unit-norm eigenvector
\varphi(t)\in{\mathcal{B}}
. For the time-evolution of
\zeta_{t}
we obtain

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\zeta_{t}\geq-2(1-\rho)(c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\lambda_{\max}V_{e}(0)+\sigma_{\max})\zeta_{t}^{2},

such that the assertion follows.

Our convergence result will be based on the mean of the particle system. Therefore we will need the following extension of Lemma
9
.

Lemma 15
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
is satisfied. Given a particle system
(u^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
it holds true that

\|C^{u,G}(u)\Gamma^{-1}(\bar{G}-y)-C(u)\nabla\Phi(\bar{u})\|\leq b_{1}J\sqrt{\Phi(\bar{u})}\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\|u^{(k)}-\bar{u}\|^{2}\right)^{3/2},

for some
b_{1}
independent of
J
.

Proof.

We apply triangular inequality

\displaystyle\|C^{u,G}(u)\Gamma^{-1}(\bar{G}-y)-C(u)\nabla\Phi(\bar{u})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|C^{u,G}(u)\Gamma^{-1}(\bar{G}-G(\bar{u}))\|

\displaystyle\quad+\|C^{u,G}(u)\Gamma^{-1}(G(\bar{u})-y)-C(u)\nabla\Phi(\bar{u})\|.

Using Lipschitz continuity and the inequality
\|C^{u,G}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq\sqrt{\|C^{u,u}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\|C^{G,G}\|_{\mathrm{HS}}}
we can bound the first term

\|C^{u,G}(u)\Gamma^{-1}(\bar{G}-G(\bar{u}))\|\leq\|\Gamma^{-1}\|c_{\mathrm{lip}}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}\|u^{(k)}-\bar{u}\|^{2}\right)^{3/2}.

The second term can be bounded similarly to Lemma
9
and the assertion follows.

Theorem 16
.

Suppose Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
are satisfied, let
(u_{t}^{(j)},t\geq 0)_{j=1,\dots,J}
solve (
22
) with
\rho\in[0,1)
and linearly independent initial ensemble
(u_{0}^{(j)})_{j=1,\dots,J}
and let
u_{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}
be the unique global minimizer of (
14

c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\Phi_{R}(\bar{u}_{t})-\Phi_{R}(u_{\ast})\leq\left(\frac{c_{1}}{t+c_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}},

(25)

where
0<\alpha<(1-\rho)\frac{L}{\mu}(\sigma_{\max}+c_{\mathrm{lip}}\lambda_{\max}\|C(u_{0})\|_{\mathrm{HS}})
.

Proof.

With the above presented results in Lemma
13
, Lemma
14
and Lemma
15

Through the incorporation of covariance inflation we have reduced the ensemble collapse by decreasing the constant of the degeneration rate through the constant inflation parameter
(1-\rho)\in(0,1]

(1-\rho)

7.2
Adaptive regularization

While in the previous experiment we have tested TEKI with fixed regularization parameter in order to verify our theoretical findings, we are providing a second experiment motivated from a more practical point of view. Here, we move away from the optimistic scenario, where the reference
u^{\dagger}
is chosen from the correct prior, instead we assume
u^{\dagger}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{C}_{0}^{\dagger})
with
{C}_{0}^{\dagger}:=\tilde{\beta}(-\Delta)^{-\alpha}
,
\tilde{\beta}=1
,
\alpha=2
. Hence, the incorporated smoothness information through the regularization matrix
C_{0}
may be misleading. We therefore propose to apply an adaptive regularization schemes introduced
[
62
]
for the stochastic formulation of TEKI. The idea of choosing a ”good” type of Tikhonov regularization is motivated from the hierarchical approach for Bayesian inverse problems. In the classical setting we are interested in the posterior distribution

u\mid y\sim\mu({\mathrm{d}}x)\propto\exp(-\Phi(x;y))\,\mu_{0}({\mathrm{d}}x)

whereas in the hierarchical setting we assume that the prior is depending on some unknown hyperparameter
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
such that we are interested in the posterior

(u,\Theta)\mid y\sim\mu({\mathrm{d}}(x,\theta))\propto\exp(-\Phi(x;y))\mu_{0}({\mathrm{d}}x,\theta)q_{0}({\mathrm{d}}\theta)

assuming a prior
\Theta\sim q_{0}({\mathrm{d}}\theta)
. We consider the case where
\mu_{0}
is given as Gaussian prior with pdf

\mu_{0}(x,\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\det(C_{0}(\theta))}}\exp(-\|x-m_{0}(\theta)\|_{C_{0}(\theta)}^{2},

where the hyperparameter
\theta
parametrizes the prior mean
m_{0}(\theta)
and prior covariance
C_{0}(\theta)
, and we assume an uniform prior on
\Theta\sim\mathcal{U}((0,M)^{n_{\theta}})
. When computing the joint MAP of
(u,\Theta)
the normalizing constant plays an important role such that the optimization problem reads as

8
Conclusion

In this work, we have quantified the EKI as derivative-free optimization method restricted to the finite dimensional subspace spanned through the initial ensemble spread. Our results are based on the deterministic continuous-time formulation represented by an ODE and the incorporation of Tikhonov regularization. The ensemble collapse has been quantified from above and below such that we were able to bound the approximation of gradients uniform in time and avoiding too fast degeneration of the preconditioner. We have further improved the convergence result through covariance inflation without breaking the subspace property. The considered covariance inflation can be viewed as generalization of the ESRF and gives an intuitive connection between the EKI and ESRF. In our numerical experiment we have illustrated the application of EKI as optimization method restricted to the initial subspace and specified the role of choosing the initial ensemble.

The next step for future work includes the extension of the presented results to the stochastic formulation of EKI represented as stochastic differential equation. The key challenge in this scenario will be the quantification of the ensemble collapse, since the resulting lower and upper bounds on the eigenvalues will be path-depending. In addition, a detailed complexity analysis of EKI needs to be done in order to quantify the advantage of applying EKI as derivative-free scheme compared to other commonly used optimization methods including derivatives such as gradient descent or (Quasi-)Newton method.
