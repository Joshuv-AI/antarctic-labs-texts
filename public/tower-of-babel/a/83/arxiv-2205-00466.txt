Categorical Semantics for Feynman Diagrams Conference: Quantum Physics and Logic 2022; June 27–July 1, 2022; Oxford, UK Proceedings of Quantum Physics and Logic 2022, June 27–July 1, 2022, Oxford, UK
Razin A. Shaikh, Stefano Gogioso

Abstract
. We introduce a novel compositional description of Feynman diagrams, with well-defined categorical semantics as morphisms in a dagger-compact category. Our chosen setting is suitable for infinite-dimensional diagrammatic reasoning, generalising the ZX calculus and other algebraic gadgets familiar to the categorical quantum theory community. The Feynman diagrams we define look very similar to their traditional counterparts, but are more general: instead of depicting scattering amplitude, they embody the linear maps from which the amplitudes themselves are computed, for any given initial and final particle states. This shift in perspective reflects into a formal transition from the syntactic, graph-theoretic compositionality of traditional Feynman diagrams to a semantic, categorical-diagrammatic compositionality. Because we work in a concrete categorical setting—powered by non-standard analysis—we are able to take direct advantage of complex additive structure in our description. This makes it possible to derive a particularly compelling characterisation for the sequential composition of categorical Feynman diagrams, which automatically results in the superposition of all possible graph-theoretic combinations of the individual diagrams themselves (cf. Figure 1 above).

The need for a rigorous handling of such infinities was the main motivation behind the non-standard approach to infinite-dimensional categorical quantum mechanics (CQM), a sequence of works of which the present paper is the latest instalment.
Non-standard analysis—one of the great 20th century triumphs of model theory—provides a way to work with infinite and infinitesimal quantities in an algebraic setting, without the need to work with limits or topological notions of convergence.
Calculations in the non-standard setting are performed in exact formal arithmetic, with the Transfer Theorem acting as the gateway through which results—such as the existence of interesting quantum states and maps—are transferred to and from the standard mathematical world.
In the aforementioned approach to infinite-dimensional CQM, NSA is used to define
{}^{\star}\!\operatorname{fHilb}
, a dagger compact category suitable for diagrammatic reasoning and able to accommodate the objects and morphisms necessary to talk about wave-functions and quantum fields.

On its own, the category
{}^{\star}\!\operatorname{fHilb}
is rather simple: it contains hyperfinite non-standard complex Hilbert spaces and
\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{C}
-linear maps between them. Hyper-finiteness is the key part of this definition: it means that the Hilbert spaces involved have dimension given by some non-standard natural number (i.e. they take the form
\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{C}^{d}
for some
d\in\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{N}
), so by Transfer Theorem they possess many of properties of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.
In particular:
{}^{\star}\!\operatorname{fHilb}
is a dagger compact category because so is
\operatorname{fHilb}
; it possesses special commutative dagger-Frobenius algebras (
\dagger
-SCFAs) because so does
\operatorname{fHilb}
; we can form group algebras in
{}^{\star}\!\operatorname{fHilb}
—that is, strongly complementary pairs of
\dagger
-SCFAs—because we can do so in
\operatorname{fHilb}
.
This last point turns out to be central to our endeavours: it allows the definition of quantised position and momentum spaces, connects them by Pontryagin duality and embodies the Weyl form of the Canonical Commutation Relations.

Thus said, the category
{}^{\star}\!\operatorname{fHilb}
is only part of the story: it provides a rich canvas for rigorous diagrammatic reasoning, but its objects don’t relate to any specific standard Hilbert space.
In fact, most constructions don’t rely on a specific choice of dimension

2.
Categorical wave-functions

2.1.
Non-standard lattice

Consider a non-standard lattice in the following form:

(1)

\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}\vcentcolon=\left\{\underline{x}\in\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\middle|\,\underline{x}=\frac{\underline{j}}{\omega_{uv}},\ \underline{j}\in{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}\right\}\subset\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{R}^{n}

where
\omega_{uv},\omega_{ir}\in\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{N}
are two odd non-standard natural numbers and
\omega\in\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{N}
is defined by
2\omega+1=\omega_{uv}\omega_{ir}
.
The Abelian group
{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}}
is defined by equipping the non-standard integers
\{-\omega,\dots,+\omega\}
with addition modulo
2\omega+1
. The lattice inherits this group structure by rescaling, structure which depends on the choice of
\omega
but not on the specific choice of
\omega_{uv},\omega_{ir}
such that
2\omega+1=\omega_{uv}\omega_{ir}
(were multiple choices to exist).
Using parlance from quantum field theory, we refer to
\omega_{uv}
as the
ultraviolet cut-off
and to
\omega_{ir}
as the
infrared cut-off
: the former determines the distance separating two points in the lattice—that is, the lattice
mesh
—while the latter determines the overall volume
\omega_{ir}^{n}=\left((2\omega+1)/\omega_{uv}\right)^{n}
of
\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{R}^{n}
spanned by the lattice.

2.2.
Standard quotient group

Now we restrict our attention to the near-standard elements, i.e. those
\underline{x}
which are infinitesimally close to a standard real number (necessarily unique and called
\operatorname{st}\left(\underline{x}\right)
):

(2)

\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}\right)_{fin}\hskip-0.56905pt\vcentcolon=\left\{\underline{x}\in\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}\,\middle|\,\underline{x}\text{ near-standard}\right\}

Near-standard elements form an (external) sub-group, and taking the standard part defines a group homomorphism:

(3)

\begin{array}[]{rrcl}\operatorname{st}:&\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}\right)_{fin}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&\underline{x}&\mapsto&\operatorname{std}(\underline{x})\end{array}

We thus obtain a quotient group:

(4)

G:=\operatorname{st}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}\right)_{fin}\right)

Different quotient groups arise depending on whether none, one or both of
\omega_{uv}
and
\omega_{ir}
are infinite:

•

if
\omega_{uv}
is finite and
\omega_{ir}
is finite, we obtain finite abelian groups
G=\frac{1}{\operatorname{st}\left(\omega_{uv}\right)}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}
;

•

if
\omega_{uv}
is finite and
\omega_{ir}
is infinite, we obtain the infinite group
G=\frac{1}{\operatorname{st}\left(\omega_{uv}\right)}\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}
;

•

if
\omega_{uv}
is infinite and
\omega_{ir}
is finite, we obtain the compact Lie group
G=\left(\mathbb{R}/\left(\operatorname{st}\left(\omega_{ir}\right)\mathbb{Z}\right)\right)^{n}\cong\mathbb{T}^{n}
;

•

if
\omega_{uv}
is infinite and
\omega_{ir}
is infinite, we obtain the locally compact Lie group
G=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.3.
Dual non-standard lattice

The dual lattice for
\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}}
is obtained by swapping
\omega_{uv}
(the small scale cut-off) and
\omega_{ir}
(the large scale cut-off):

(5)

\frac{1}{\omega_{ir}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}=\left\{\underline{p}\in\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\middle|\,\underline{p}=\frac{\underline{k}}{\omega_{ir}},\ \underline{k}\in{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}\right\}\subset\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{R}^{n}

Restricting to near-standard elements of the dual lattice and taking the standard part yields the Pontryagin dual of
G
:

(6)

G^{\wedge}=\operatorname{st}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{ir}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}\right)_{fin}\right)

In practice, we use
\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}
to model positions in certain
n
-dimensional spaces of quantum-mechanical interest:
the associated group structure is that of translations on position space, the dual lattice
\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}
models the corresponding momentum space, and its group structure is that of momentum boosts (the Pontryagin dual of translations).

2.4.
Wave-functions on position space

Given the above, the wave-functions on position space are modelled by the following object of
{}^{\star}\!\operatorname{fHilb}
:

(7)

\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{C}\left[\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}}\right]

Within this Hilbert space, we can restrict our attention to those near-standard vectors
\ket{\psi}
such that the inner product
\braket{\psi|\psi}
is itself near-standard.
Taking
\operatorname{st}\left(\ket{\psi}\right)
, we obtain exactly the functions of
L^{2}\left[G\right]
, that is we recover the standard picture of wave-functions on position space.
For this correspondence to hold, the inner product must correctly account for the volume element
d^{n}\underline{x}=1/\omega_{uv}^{n}
:

(8)

\braket{\psi|\varphi}\vcentcolon=\hskip-8.53581pt\sum_{\underline{x}\in\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}}\hskip-8.53581pt\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}^{n}}\psi^{*}(\underline{x})\varphi(\underline{x})

There also exist finite vectors—and, in particular, normalised ones—which are not near-standard: these are genuinely new objects made available by the non-standard framework.
Importantly, these objects can be used to rigorously model notoriously tricky mathematical notions used in everyday quantum physics calculations, such as delta functions (which are now genuine vectors) and plane-waves (which can now be normalised).
Delta functions and plane-waves are, in fact, related to a familiar object from categorical diagrammatics: a strongly complementary pair

of quasi-special commutative
\dagger
-Frobenius algebras.
Specifically, we can take

(9)

We then define a quasi-special commutative

\underline{p}\in\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{n}}

\displaystyle\ket{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\underline{p}}}

\braket{\delta_{\underline{x}}|\delta_{\underline{x}}}=\omega_{uv}^{n}\hskip 28.45274pt\braket{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\underline{p}}|\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\underline{p}}}=\omega_{ir}^{n}\hskip 28.45274pt\braket{\delta_{\underline{x}}|\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\underline{p}}}=e^{i2\pi\underline{p}\cdot\underline{x}}

3.
Categorical quantum fields

3.1.
Field quantisation

Consider the relativistic real scalar field whose Lagrangian is given by:

(12)

\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\phi^{2}

This field gives rise to the Klein-Gordon equation:

(13)

\partial_{\mu}\partial^{\mu}\phi+\left(\frac{mc}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\phi=0

In momentum space, the Klein-Gordon equation factorises:

(14)

\left(\hbar^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}+(|\underline{p}|c)^{2}+(mc^{2})^{2}\right)\phi(\underline{p},t)=0

This is the equation of motion for a field of independent simple harmonic oscillators at each point
\underline{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
of momentum space.
The oscillator at point
\underline{p}
oscillates at a frequency
\nu_{\underline{p}}=\frac{1}{\hbar}E_{\underline{p}}
, where
E_{\underline{p}}
is the energy given by the following
relativistic dispersion relation
:

(15)

E_{\underline{p}}\vcentcolon=\sqrt{(|\underline{p}|c)^{2}+(mc^{2})^{2}}

We now proceed to quantise this field in
{}^{\star}\!\operatorname{fHilb}
.

Traditionally,
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{C})
is considered as the space for quantised oscillators.
Its non-standard counterpart
\ell^{2}(\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{N};\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{C})
is, however, infinite-dimensional, and
{}^{\star}\!\operatorname{fHilb}
is limited (so to speak) to hyperfinite-dimensional non-standard Hilbert spaces.
To achieve our quantisation in
{}^{\star}\!\operatorname{fHilb}
, we consider versions of
\ell^{2}(\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{N};\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{C})
which are “truncated” at some infinite particle number
\kappa
:

(16)

\mathcal{H}^{\kappa}\vcentcolon=\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{C}^{\kappa}

Now, write
\left(|n\rangle\right)_{n=0}^{\kappa-1}
for the canonical orthonormal basis of
\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{C}^{\kappa}
.
In order to work around our (necessary) technical modification to the quantised oscillator space, we define the ladder operators to connect spaces truncated at different particle numbers:

\displaystyle a_{\kappa}^{\dagger}:\mathcal{H}^{\kappa}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}^{\kappa+1}

\displaystyle a_{\kappa}:\mathcal{H}^{\kappa}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{H}^{\kappa-1}

(17)

\displaystyle\ket{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}

\displaystyle\ket{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sqrt{n}\ket{n-1}

where the special case
\sqrt{0}\ket{-1}
for
n=0
on the right is set to the zero vector of the Hilbert space by convention.
The ladder operators satisfy the usual canonical commutation relation:

(18)

[a,a^{\dagger}]=id

Under the hood, ladder operators at different truncations are used to make dimensions match:

(19)

a_{\kappa+1}a^{\dagger}_{\kappa}-a^{\dagger}_{\kappa-1}a_{\kappa}=id_{\kappa}

Our 3-momentum space is modelled by the following non-standard lattice, in the case where
\omega_{uv},\omega_{ir}\in\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{N}
are both odd infinite naturals:

(20)

\Omega:=\frac{1}{\omega_{ir}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{3}}

Our 3-position space is modelled by the dual lattice:

(21)

\mathcal{X}=\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}{\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{Z}_{2\omega+1}^{3}}

Just as the quantisation
\mathcal{H}^{\kappa}
for each individual oscillator is truncated at some arbitrary particle number
\kappa
, so the overall field quantisation will be truncated at some arbitrary function
\tau:\Omega\rightarrow\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{N}
selecting an arbitrary particle number truncation
\tau_{\underline{p}}

for each momentum value

\underline{p}
.
Putting everything together, the following tensor product of quantised oscillators will be our quantised field space:

(22)

\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}\vcentcolon=\bigotimes_{\underline{p}\in\Omega}\mathcal{H}^{\tau_{\underline{p}}}

Its canonical product basis takes the following form, for all possible assignments
{\underline{n}\in\prod\limits_{\underline{p}\in\Omega}\{0,\dots,\tau_{\underline{p}}\}}
of particle numbers to individual momentum values:

(23)

\ket{\beta_{\underline{n}}}:=\bigotimes_{\underline{p}\in\Omega}\ket{n_{\underline{p}}}

3.2.
Ladder operators

For notational convenience, we define the following indicator functions for all momenta
\underline{p}\in\Omega
:

\displaystyle\delta_{\underline{p}}:\Omega

\displaystyle\to\{0,1\}

(24)

\displaystyle\underline{q}

\displaystyle\mapsto\delta_{\underline{p},\underline{q}}

where
\delta_{\underline{p},\underline{q}}
is 1 when
\underline{p}=\underline{q}
and 0 otherwise.
For each momentum value
\underline{p}\in\Omega
, we define
ladder operators
, acting non-trivially on the quantised harmonic oscillator factor for momentum value
\underline{p}
only:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rrcl}a_{\tau}^{\dagger}(\underline{p}):&\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{H}^{(\tau+\delta_{\underline{p}})}\\
&\ket{\beta_{\underline{n}}}&\mapsto&\sqrt{\omega_{ir}^{3}}\sqrt{n_{\underline{p}}+1}\ket{\beta_{\underline{n}+\delta_{\underline{p}}}}\\
\\
a_{\tau}(\underline{p}):&\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{H}^{(\tau-\delta_{\underline{p}})}\\
&\ket{\beta_{\underline{n}}}&\mapsto&\sqrt{\omega_{ir}^{3}}\sqrt{n_{\underline{p}}}\ket{\beta_{\underline{n}-\delta_{\underline{p}}}}\end{array}

By convention, we set
\ket{\beta_{\underline{n}}}
to be the zero vector if there exists
\underline{p}\in\Omega
such that
n_{\underline{p}}<0
.
Similarly, we set
\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}\vcentcolon=\!\,^{\star}\mathbb{C}^{0}
if there exists
\underline{p}\in\Omega
such that
\tau_{\underline{p}}<0
.
The operator
a^{\dagger}(\underline{p})
is also known as a
creation operator
, while the operator
a(\underline{p})
is known as an
annihilation operator
.

In the expressions above, the
\omega_{ir}^{3}
factor above comes from the Euclidean volume element for our discretised 3-momentum space:

(30)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}d^{3}\underline{p}\longleftrightarrow\sum_{\underline{p}\in\Omega}\frac{1}{\omega_{ir}^{3}}

In particular, the Euclidean
delta function
on discretised 3-momentum space takes the following form:

(31)

\delta(\underline{p}-\underline{q}):=\omega_{ir}^{3}\delta_{\underline{p},\underline{q}}

The above observation helps us make sense of the commutator for the relativistically normalised ladder operators:

(32)

\displaystyle\left[a(\underline{p}),a^{\dagger}(\underline{q})\right]

\displaystyle=\delta(\underline{p}-\underline{q})\,id

\displaystyle\left[a(\underline{p}),a(\underline{q})\right]

\displaystyle=0=\left[a^{\dagger}(\underline{p}),a^{\dagger}(\underline{q})\right]

Under the hood, ladder operators at different truncations are used to make dimensions match once again:

(33)

a_{\tau+\delta_{\underline{p}}}(\underline{p})a^{\dagger}_{\tau}(\underline{p})-a^{\dagger}_{\tau-\delta_{\underline{p}}}(\underline{p})a_{\tau}(\underline{p})=\omega_{ir}^{3}\,id_{\tau}

3.3.
Particle states

For conceptual and notational convenience, we also define the following orthogonal basis of
particle states
(in momentum space), obtained from the
free field vacuum

\ket{\underline{0}}:=\ket{\beta_{\underline{0}}}
by any hyperfinite number of applications of the creation operators:

\displaystyle\ket{\underline{n}}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\bigotimes_{\underline{p}\in\Omega}\sqrt{2E_{\underline{p}}\omega_{ir}^{3}}^{\,n_{\underline{p}}}\sqrt{n_{\underline{p}}!}\,\ket{n_{\underline{p}}}

(34)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\underline{p}\in\Omega}\left(\sqrt{2E_{\underline{p}}}a^{\dagger}(\underline{p})\right)^{n_{\underline{p}}}\ket{\underline{0}}

In other words, the particle state
\ket{\underline{n}}
is the one containing exactly
n_{\underline{p}}
particles with momentum
\underline{p}
for each
\underline{p}\in\Omega
, together with an additional
relativistic normalisation
factor
\sqrt{2E_{\underline{p}}}
.
In particular, the 1-particle states for each momentum value
\underline{p}\in\Omega
take the following simple form:

(35)

\ket{\delta_{\underline{p}}}=\sqrt{2E_{\underline{p}}}a^{\dagger}(\underline{p})\ket{\underline{0}}

where the particle number
\delta_{\underline{p}}
is 1 at
\underline{p}
and 0 everywhere else.
The relativistic normalisation factor
\sqrt{2E_{\underline{p}}}
comes from the Lorentz-invariant volume element for on-shell 4-momentum space (in its equivalent formulation for 3-momentum space):

(36)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}d^{4}p\delta(p_{0}-E_{\underline{p}})|_{p_{0}>0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}d^{3}\underline{p}\frac{1}{2E_{\underline{p}}}\longleftrightarrow\sum_{\underline{p}\in\Omega}\frac{1}{\omega_{ir}^{3}}\frac{1}{2E_{\underline{p}}}

In particular, the
Lorentz-invariant delta function
on discretised 3-momentum space appears as the inner product between 1-particle states:

(37)

\braket{\delta_{\underline{p}}|\delta_{\underline{q}}}=2E_{\underline{p}}\delta(\underline{p}-\underline{q})

3.4.
Field operator

3.8.
Complex scalar fields

The machinery developed in this section straightforwardly generalises to the case of complex scalar fields, governed by the following Lagrangian:

(60)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{complex}}=\partial_{\mu}\bar{\psi}\partial^{\mu}\psi-M^{2}\bar{\psi}\psi

This Lagrangian gives rise to two equations of motion:

(61)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\partial^{\mu}\psi+M^{2}\psi

\displaystyle=0

(62)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\partial^{\mu}\bar{\psi}+M^{2}\bar{\psi}

\displaystyle=0

As such, the complex scalar field is comprised of two species of particle—a particle
\psi
and the corresponding anti-particle
\bar{\psi}
—each with its own quantised field space:

(63)

\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{(\sigma)}

The particle states for such a complex scalar field arise by tensor product.
Each particle specie has its pair of ladder operators, commuting with the operator of the other specie:

(64)

\displaystyle\left[b(\underline{p}),b^{\dagger}(\underline{q})\right]

\displaystyle=\delta(\underline{p}-\underline{q})\,id

\displaystyle\left[c(\underline{p}),c^{\dagger}(\underline{q})\right]

\displaystyle=\delta(\underline{p}-\underline{q})\,id

\displaystyle\left[b(\underline{p}),c^{\dagger}(\underline{q})\right]

\displaystyle=0=\left[b^{\dagger}(\underline{p}),c(\underline{q})\right]

The propagator is now defined by commutation of the particle and antiparticle field operators, and the Feynman propagator is defined from it as in the real scalar case:

(65)

D(x-y)=\left[\psi(x),\psi^{\dagger}(y)\right]

3.10.
The interaction picture

In perturbative quantum field theory, it is useful to work in the
interaction picture
, a hybrid representation where the Heisenberg picture is used for the free-field Hamiltonian
H_{0}
and the Schrödinger picture is used for the interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\text{int}}
:

(71)

H=H_{0}+H_{\text{int}}

More specifically,
H_{0}
will govern the time dependence of operators while
H_{\text{int}}
will govern the time dependence of states.
Typically,
H_{0}
corresponds to a system of free fields and, as such, it is typically well-understood and exactly soluble.
In contrast,
H_{\text{int}}
is a perturbation to the system that represents interaction between the fields, typically hard to handle non-perturbatively.
The states and operators are transformed from Schrödinger to interaction picture as follows:

(72)

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t)}_{I}

\displaystyle=e^{iH_{0}t}\ket{\psi(t)}_{S}

(73)

\displaystyle O_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{iH_{0}t}O_{S}e^{-iH_{0}t}

where the subscripts
S
and
I
denote the Schrödinger and the interaction picture respectively.
The free field Hamiltonian
H_{0}
in the interaction picture remains the same as in the Schrödinger picture:

(74)

(H_{0})_{I}=e^{iH_{0}t}(H_{0})_{S}e^{-iH_{0}t}=(H_{0})_{S}

On the other hand—since
H_{0}
does not typically commute with
H_{\text{int}}
—we get a different expression for the interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\text{int}}
in the interaction picture, denoted by
H_{I}
:

(75)

H_{I}\vcentcolon=(H_{\text{int}})_{I}=e^{iH_{0}t}(H_{\text{int}})_{S}e^{-iH_{0}t}

From Schrödinger’s equation in the Schrödinger picture, one can derive the Schrödinger equation in the interaction picture:

(76)

\displaystyle i\frac{d}{dt}\left(\ket{\psi}_{I}\right)

\displaystyle=H_{I}(t)\ket{\psi}_{I}

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t)}_{I}

\displaystyle=U_{t_{0},t}\ket{\psi(t_{0})}_{I}

Above,
\left(U_{t_{0},t}\right)_{t_{0},t\in\mathcal{T}}
is a unitary representation responsible for time-translation on particle states, given by Dyson’s Formula:

(77)

U_{t_{0},t}=T\exp\left(-i\sum_{t^{\prime}=t_{0}}^{t}\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}H_{I}(t^{\prime})\right)id

The
time ordering operator

T
rearranges the operators evaluated at later times to the left of operators evaluated at earlier times, e.g.

(78)

T\{O_{1}(t_{1})O_{2}(t_{2})\}=\begin{cases}O_{1}(t_{1})O_{2}(t_{2})\qquad t_{1}>t_{2}\\
O_{2}(t_{2})O_{1}(t_{1})\qquad t_{2}>t_{1}\end{cases}

Moreover, note that the exponential in Dyson’s formula is defined in terms of a Taylor series expansion:

(79)

\displaystyle\exp\left(-i\sum_{t^{\prime}=t_{0}}^{t}\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}H_{I}(t^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-i\sum_{t^{\prime}=t_{0}}^{t}\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}H_{I}(t^{\prime})+\frac{(-i)^{2}}{2}\left(\sum_{t^{\prime}=t_{0}}^{t}\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}}H_{I}(t^{\prime})\right)^{2}+\dots

3.11.
Scattering

The process by which the incoming particles transition to the outgoing particles under the influence of an interaction is called
scattering
.
In quantum field theory, one is therefore interested in computing the associated
scattering amplitudes
, from which other quantities of physical interest—such as cross-sections and decay rates—can be derived.
To calculate amplitudes, we need to make a non-trivial assumption: that the initial state
\ket{i}
is (approximately) free in far past
t=-\infty
and that final state
\ket{f}
is (approximately) free in the far future
t=+\infty
.
As our values for
\pm\infty
we take:

(80)

\pm\infty:=\pm\frac{\omega}{\omega_{uv}}\approx\pm\frac{\omega_{ir}}{2}

The scattering amplitude from
\ket{i}
to
\ket{f}
is
\Braket{f|U_{-\infty,+\infty}|i}
, where the unitary operator
U_{-\infty,+\infty}
is itself known as the
S-matrix
.
Feynman diagrams represent individual terms in the Taylor expansion of the S-matrix: if we compute the amplitudes associated to progressively higher order diagrams, we obtain increasingly precise approximations
2
2

2

For perturbative theories, that is.
for the scattering amplitudes, and all quantities that depend on them.

4.1.
Split and merge maps

Key to our compositional, 2-dimensional interpretation of Feynman diagrams is the following observation: processes
a_{1},...,a_{k}
which act sequentially on a same space
\mathcal{H}
but commute with each other, can alternatively be thought as acting in parallel on different “portions” of
\mathcal{H}
, as a tensor product.
The only ingredient necessary to formalise this intuition is maps that “split” a single copy of a space
\mathcal{H}
into multiple copies that can be acted upon independently by the processes
a_{1},...,a_{k}
, and then “merge” the result back into a single copy.
This will reveal Feynman diagrams as a naturally “decompositional”—as opposed to compositional—processes: instead of arising by tensor product composition of simpler processes, their 2-dimensional description is actually the consequence of a decomposition of a single underlying space into non-interacting sectors.

In order to define suitable split and merge maps, we consider a particle state
\ket{\underline{n}}
—for some given choice of particle numbers
\underline{n}\in\prod_{\underline{p}\in\Omega}\{0,\dots,\tau(\underline{p})\}
—and define the set
\Theta_{\underline{n}}^{k}
of all possible ways of partitioning the particles of
\ket{\underline{n}}
into
k
independent copies of
\mathcal{H}
:

(86)

\Theta_{\underline{n}}^{k}\vcentcolon=\left\{(\underline{i_{1}},\dots,\underline{i_{k}})\in\left(\prod_{\underline{p}\in\Omega}\{0,\dots,\tau(\underline{p})\}\right)^{k}\,\middle|\,\underline{i_{1}}+\dots+\underline{i_{k}}=\underline{n}\right\}

The
split maps
are morphisms defined as follows:

(87)

\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\hbox{\lower 45.77783pt\hbox{{\lxSVG@includegraphics{}{}{figures/cache/main-figure29.pdf}}}}}$}}}\ \ :

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}\rightarrow\,\underbrace{\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}\,\otimes\,\dots\,\otimes\,\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}}_{k}

(88)

\displaystyle\ket{{\underline{n}}}\mapsto\sqrt{\braket{{\underline{n}}|{\underline{n}}}}\sum_{\uuline{i}\in\Theta_{\underline{n}}^{k}}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\braket{\underline{i_{j}}|\underline{i_{j}}}}}\ket{\underline{i_{j}}}

The
merge maps
are morphisms defined as follows:

(89)

\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.7}{${\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\immediate\hbox{\lower 12.22636pt\hbox{{\lxSVG@includegraphics{}{}{figures/cache/main-figure30.pdf}}}}}$}}}\ \ :

\displaystyle\,\underbrace{\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}\,\otimes\,\dots\,\otimes\,\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}}_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{(\tau)}

(90)

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}\ket{\underline{i_{j}}}\mapsto\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\sqrt{\Braket{\underline{i_{j}}|\underline{i_{j}}}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Braket{{\underline{n}}|{\underline{n}}}}}\ket{{\underline{n}}}

4.4.
Composing Feynman diagrams

As a calculation tool, traditional Feynman diagrams are inherently non-compositional.
The diagrams themselves can be composed as graphs, by gluing, but the rules that turn them into amplitudes don’t respect—at least, not directly—this compositional structure.
This is because Feynman rules pertain to the computation of amplitudes for specific initial and final states: in order to compose them sequentially, for example, we must integrate the individual amplitudes for all possible intermediate particle states, for all possible ways of connecting intermediate legs.

Categorical Feynman diagrams, on the other hand, are prima-facie compositional: they are morphisms in a symmetric monoidal category,
\!\,{}^{\star}\mathbb{C}
-linear endomorphism of a certain hyperfinite-dimensional Hilbert space.
Instead of representing amplitudes, each categorical diagram directly embodies the full process that turns initial and final states into amplitudes: connecting the output wire of one diagram to the input wire of the other amounts exactly to integration over all possible intermediate states.
In its simplicity, however, sequential composition of categorical Feynman diagrams turns out to be surprising: because of sliding and commutation rules, composition automatically generates all possible ways of connecting intermediate legs in the 2-dimensional diagrams.

To exemplify this phenomenon, we consider the composition of two 2nd order nucleon-nucleon scattering diagrams:

4.5.
Conclusions and Future Work

In Section
4
, we have shown how Feynman diagrams and amplitude calculations can be accommodated within the ever broadening universe of categorical diagrammatics.
This is a significant development, in that it paves the way for the application of such techniques to a variety of important areas of computer science, from the quantum simulation of particle physics to design and compilation in the context of photonic quantum computing.

In demonstrating this applicability, we have also provided the blueprint for a variety of works to follow: extensions of the categorical and diagrammatic primitives to Fermions and vector bosons, development of specialised calculi for photonic and continuous-variable quantum computation, translation of diagrams into computable quantum circuits (e.g. for simulation purposes).

A.1.
Wick’s Theorem

The calculation of scattering amplitudes requires the evaluation of time ordered products such as
T\{H_{I}(x_{1})\dots H_{I}(x_{n})\}
.
Wick’s theorem
(
Wick 1950
)
provides an algorithm to convert such time ordered products to sums of
normal ordered
products, which are easier to evaluate.

Given a string of operators
\phi_{1}(x_{1})\dots\phi_{n}(x_{n})
, their normal ordered product
:\phi_{1}(x_{1})\dots\phi_{n}(x_{n}):
is an arrangement where all annihilation operators are placed to the right of all creation operators: first we remove all incoming particles from the state—collapsing to the zero vector, if some required mode is present in insufficient number—and then we add back all outgoing ones.

Wick’s theorem is based on a simple but clever observation: that the only obstacle to time-ordering
T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}
of two field operators is the commutation of positive frequency components of a field across negative frequency components
of the same field
.
Expanding
\phi(x)=\phi^{+}(x)+\phi^{-}(x)
shows that the only commutation necessary when
x^{0}>y^{0}
is the one below:

(122)

\phi^{+}(x)\phi^{-}(y)=\phi^{-}(y)\phi^{+}(x)+D(x-y)

From this, we get an expression for the time-ordered product in the case where
x^{0}>y^{0}
:

(123)

T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}=\ :\phi(x)\phi(y):+\ D(x-y)

Analogously, for
x^{0}<y^{0}
we get:

(124)

T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}=\ :\phi(x)\phi(y):+\ D(y-x)

Putting the two cases together, a Feynman propagator appears:

(125)

T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}=\ :\phi(x)\phi(y):+\ \Delta_{F}(x-y)

Analogously, for the case of complex scalar fields:

(126)

T\{\psi(x)\psi^{\dagger}(y)\}=\ :\psi(x)\psi^{\dagger}(y):+\ \Delta_{F}(x-y)

The expression above represents an atomic step in the algorithm from Wick’s Theorem: normal ordering of a pair of fields by introduction of a Feynman propagator.
Because such a pair can appear anywhere in a string of field operators—typically involving multiple particle types—Wick introduces
contractions
, a notation to keep track of the pairs that have been normally ordered at any given step of the computation.

A.2.
Feynman diagrams

Wick’s theorem simplifies the calculation of scattering amplitudes, but the process of evaluating integrals is repetitive and tedious.
Feynman
(
Feynman 1949
)
introduced a diagrammatic shorthand, Feynman diagrams, with simple rules for the computation of amplitudes.

Feynman diagrams arise directly from the terms in Wick’s expansion.

(132)

H_{\text{int}}=g\sum_{\underline{x}}\frac{1}{\omega_{uv}^{3}}\psi^{\dagger}(\underline{x})\psi(\underline{x})\phi(\underline{x})

where
\psi
is a
nucleon
,
\bar{\psi}
the corresponding
anti-nucleon
and
\phi
is the
meson

interaction vertex

(133)

•

intermediate legs (connecting vertices) corresponding to contractions

A.3.
Feynman rules

The algorithm for translating Feynman diagrams into scattering amplitudes is known as
Feynman rules
, which we now describe for our running example of scalar Yukawa theory.
In general, a scattering amplitude takes the following form:

(139)

i\mathcal{A}\delta(p_{F}-p_{I})

where
p_{I}
and
p_{F}
are the sum of initial and final particle 4-momenta respectively.
The term
\delta(p_{F}-p_{I})
enforces conservation of 4-momentum, but does not otherwise contribute to the scattering: the interesting term is the amplitude term
i\mathcal{A}
itself
3
3

3

The factor of
i
is added as convention, to match amplitudes in non-relativistic quantum mechanics.
.
The following Feynman rules describe how to compute it:

(a)

Add a momentum variable
k
to each internal line.

(b)

Impose momentum conservation equations at each vertex.

(c)

For each vertex, write down a factor of
(-ig)
.

(d)

For each internal line with momentum
k
include the following factor, where
m
and
M
are the masses of meson and nucleon, respectively:

(140)

\begin{cases}\frac{i}{k^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}&\text{ for a meson}\\
\frac{i}{k^{2}-M^{2}+i\epsilon}&\text{ for a nucleon}\end{cases}

(e)

For each internal loop with the undetermined momentum
l
, integrate
l
over 4-momentum space:

4
4

4

Particles on internal edges are virtual, so their energy is not constrained by the relativistic dispersion relation.

(141)

\sum_{l}\frac{1}{\omega_{ir}^{4}}

Appendix D
Translating Feynman diagrams to categorical diagrams

We list here the practical steps to translate a Feynman diagram to a categorical Feynman diagram.

Step 1:
Make parallel wires using split and merge maps. The number of parallel wires should be the maximum of the number of incoming and outgoing external lines.

(161)

Step 2:
For incoming external lines, draw the corresponding annihilation operators in parallel on the left.

(162)

Step 3:
