Breaking the gridlock in Mixture-of-Experts: Consistent and Efficient Algorithms
Ashok Vardhan Makkuva, Sewoong Oh, Sreeram Kannan, Pramod Viswanath

Abstract
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) is a widely popular model for ensemble learning and is a basic building block of highly successful modern neural networks as well as a component in Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) and Attention networks. However, present algorithms for learning MoE, including the EM algorithm and gradient descent, are known to get stuck in local optima. From a theoretical viewpoint, finding an efficient and provably consistent algorithm to learn the parameters remains a long standing open problem for more than two decades. In this paper, we introduce the first algorithm that learns the true parameters of a MoE model for a wide class of non-linearities with global consistency guarantees. While existing algorithms jointly or iteratively estimate the expert parameters and the gating parameters in the MoE, we propose a novel algorithm that breaks the deadlock and can directly estimate the expert parameters by sensing its echo in a carefully designed cross-moment tensor between the inputs and the output. Once the experts are known, the recovery of gating parameters still requires an EM algorithm; however, we show that the EM algorithm for this simplified problem, unlike the joint EM algorithm, converges to the true parameters. We empirically validate our algorithm on both the synthetic and real data sets in a variety of settings, and show superior performance to standard baselines.

Introduction

In this paper, we study a popular gated neural network architecture known as Mixture-of-Experts (MoE). MoE is a basic building block of highly successful modern neural networks like Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) and Attention networks. A key interesting feature of MoE is the presence of a gating mechanism that allows for specialization of experts in their respective domains. MoE allows for the underlying expert models to be simple while allowing to capture complex non-linear relations between the data. Ever since their inception more than two decades ago
(
Jacobs et al. 1991
)
, they have been a subject of great research interest
(
Tresp 2001
;
Collobert et al. 2002
;
Ng & Deisenroth 2014
;
Theis & Bethge 2015
;
Le et al. 2016
;
Gross et al. 2017
;
Sun et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
across multiple domains such as computer vision, natural language processing, speech recognition, finance, and forecasting.

The interpretation behind (

\boldsymbol{x}
, the gating network chooses an expert based on the outcome of a multinomial random variable

, whose probability depends on
\boldsymbol{x}
in a parametric way, i.e.

\boldsymbol{x}

We want to learn the expert parameters
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(also referred to as the regressors) and
the gating parameters
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
assuming we know the non-linear activation

.

This problem of learning MoE
has been a long standing open problem for more than two decades,
even though it is a fundamental building block of several state-of-the-art gated neural network architectures.
Gated neural networks such as GRUs and Sparsely-gated-MoEs
have been widely successful in challenging tasks like machine translation
(
Chung et al. 2014
;
Shazeer et al. 2017
;
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
.
Parameters are typically learnt through (stochastic) gradient descent on a non-convex loss function.
However, these methods do not possess any theoretical guarantees, even for the simplest gated neural network, which is the MoE.

On the other hand, existing guarantees for simpler models without gating units do not extend to MoEs.
Consider the mixture of generalized linear models (M-GLMs)
(
Sedghi et al. 2014
;
Sun et al. 2014
;
Yi et al. 2016
;
Zhong et al. 2016
)
,
which is a strict simplification of
the
k
-MoE model in (
1
),
where
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\ast}=0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. The learning in M-GLMs is usually done through a combination of spectral methods and greedy methods such as EM. A major limitation of these methods is that they rely critically on the fact that the mixing probability is a constant and hence they do not generalize to MoEs (see Section
2
). In addition, the EM algorithm, which is the workhorse for learning in parametric mixture models, is prone to bad local minima
(
Sedghi et al. 2014
;
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
;
Zhong et al. 2016
)
(we independently verify this for MoEs in Section
4
).
These theoretical shortcomings and practical relevance of the MoE models lead to the following fundamental question:

Can we find an efficient and a consistent algorithm (with global initializations) that recovers the true parameters of the model with theoretical guarantees?

In this paper, we address this question precisely and make the following contributions:

1) First theoretical guarantees:
We provide the first (poly-time) efficient algorithm that recovers the true parameters of a MoE model with global initializations (Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
). We allow for a wide class of non-linearities which includes the popular choices of identity, sigmoid, and ReLU. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first work to give global convergence guarantees for MoE.

2) Algorithmic innovations:

Existing algorithms jointly or iteratively estimate the expert parameters and the gating paramters in the MoE and can get stuck in local minima. In this paper, we propose a novel algorithm that breaks the gridlock and can directly estimate the expert parameters by sensing its echo in a cross-moment tensor between the inputs and the output (Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
2
). Once the experts are known, the recovery of gating parameters still requires an EM algorithm; however, we show that the EM algorithm for this simplified problem, unlike the joint EM algorithm, converges to the true parameters. The proofs of global convergence of EM as well as the design of the cross-moment tensor are of independent mathematical interest.

3) Novel transformations:
In this paper, we introduce the novel notion of “Cubic and Quadratic Transform (CQT)". These are polynomial transformations on the output labels tailored to specific non-linear activation functions and the noise variance. The key utility of these transforms is to equip MoEs with a supersymmetric tensor structure in a principled way (Theorem
1
).

Related work.
While there is a huge literature on MoEs (
(
Yuksel et al. 2012
;
Masoudnia & Ebrahimpour 2014
)
are detailed surveys), there are relatively few works on its learning guarantees.
(

3

The simplified versions of MoE, M-GLMs, are widely studied in the literature. The key techiques for parameter inference in M-GLMs include EM algorithm, spectral methods, convex relaxations, and their variants.
(
Yi et al. 2014
;
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
)
prove convergence of EM for
2

(
Sedghi et al. 2014
)
construct a

-order supersymmetric tensor containing the regressors as its rank-
1
components. However, this approach fails to generalize for MoE.
(
Zhong et al. 2016
)
use a similar tensor construction followed by EM to learn the parameters; however, they can only handle linear noiseless mixtures and no gating parameters. In contrast, our algorithms can handle non-linearities and the gating parameters.
(
Chen et al. 2014
)
use a convex objective to learn the regressors for a special setting of
2
-mixtures of linear regressions. Similar to earlier approaches, this relaxation too does not generalize to

.

Issues with traditional tensor methods.

In the far simplified setting of the absence of the gating parameter, i.e.
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

2
-MoE reduces to
2

\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

-order tensor

by regressing the output

on the score transformation
\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})\triangleq\boldsymbol{x}\otimes\boldsymbol{x}\otimes\boldsymbol{x}-\sum_{i\in[d]}\mathrm{sym}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[y\cdot\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[g^{\prime\prime\prime}(\langle{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle)]\cdot(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\ast})^{\otimes 3}

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[g^{\prime\prime\prime}(\langle{\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle)]\cdot(\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\ast})^{\otimes 3}\;.

(3)

Here the second equality follows from the generalized Stein’s lemma that
\mathbb{E}[f(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(3)}f(\boldsymbol{x})]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[y\cdot\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1,2}\alpha_{i}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast})^{\otimes 3}+\beta_{i}\,\mathrm{sym}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}\otimes\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}\otimes\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt+\gamma_{i}\,\mathrm{sym}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}\otimes\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\otimes\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})+\delta(\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})^{\otimes 3},

(4)

where

Issues with EM algorithm.
EM algorithm is the workhorse for parameter learning in both the
k

(
Jordan & Jacobs 1994

. However, it is well known that EM is prone to spurious minima and existing theoretical results only establish local convergence for the regressors and the gating parameters. Indeed, our numerical experiments in Section

2.1
The proposed algorithm for learning MoE

In order to tackle these challenges, we take a different route and propose to estimate the regressors and gating parameters
separately
.
To gain intuition about our approach, let us consider 2-MoE model in (
2
) with
\sigma=0
and linear
g
. Then we have that
y
either equals
\langle{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle
with probability
\sigma(\langle{\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle)
or equals
\langle{\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle
with probability
1-\sigma(\langle{\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle)
, where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function. If we exactly know
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
, we can recover
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\ast}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\ast}
by solving a simple linear regression problem since we can recover the true latent variable
\boldsymbol{z}\in\{1,2\}
with high probability. Similarly, if we know
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\ast}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\ast}
, it is easy to see that we can recover
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
by solving a binary linear classification problem. Thus knowing either the regressors or the gating parameters makes the estimation of other parameters easier. However, how do we first obtain one set of parameters without any knowledge about the other?

Our approach precisely addresses this question and breaks the
grid lock
. We show that we can extract the regressors
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\ast}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\ast}
without knowing
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
at all, just using the samples.
Although we explain our approach with two mixtures,
all claims are made precise for general
k
in Theorems
1
and
2
,
and the algorithms are written for general
k
as well in Algorithms
1
and
2
.

To learn the regressors, we first pre-process
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
using the score transformations
\mathcal{S}_{3}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\triangleq\boldsymbol{x}\otimes\boldsymbol{x}\otimes\boldsymbol{x}-\sum_{i\in[d]}\mathrm{sym}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\right),

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\triangleq\boldsymbol{x}\otimes\boldsymbol{x}-I.

(6)

These score functions can be viewed as higher-order feature extractors from the inputs. As we have seen in (
3

To answer this question in a principled way, we introduce the notion of “Cubic and Quadratic Transform (CQT)" for the labels, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{3}(y)\triangleq y^{3}+\alpha y^{2}+\beta y,\quad\mathcal{P}_{2}(y)\triangleq y^{2}+\gamma y.

The coefficients

and
\mathcal{P}_{2}(y)=y^{2}

and the noise variance

\mathcal{P}_{3}(y)

In this section, we provide the theoretical guarantees for our algorithms in the population setting. We first formally state our assumptions and justify the rationale behind them:

1.

\boldsymbol{x}

Theorem 1

(Recovery of regression parameters)
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{x},y)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{P}_{2}(y)\cdot\mathcal{S}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c^{\prime}_{g}\mathbb{E}[P_{i|\boldsymbol{x}}]\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}\otimes\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{3}\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{P}_{3}(y)\cdot\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{g,\sigma}\mathbb{E}[P_{i|\boldsymbol{x}}]\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}\otimes\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}\otimes\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast},

where
c^{\prime}_{g}

are two non-zero constants depending on

. Hence the regressors
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}

and
\mathcal{T}_{3}

Proof.

(Sketch)
To highlight the central ideas behind the proof, first let
g=
linear. From (
1
) we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[y|\boldsymbol{x}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[k]}p_{i}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})\langle{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle,

where
p_{i}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})\triangleq P_{i|\boldsymbol{x}}
for
i\in[k]
. Taking the cross moment of

with
\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})
and using Lemma
2
we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[y\cdot\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}[p_{i}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})\langle{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\cdot\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(3)}(p_{i}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})\langle{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle)].

Notice that had
p_{i}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})
been a constant in the above equation, we would obtain a supersymmetric tensor easily as is the case with M-GLMs. However,
\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(3)}(p_{i}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})\langle{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle)]
now contains all the third-order rank-

\boldsymbol{w}_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{k-1}^{\ast}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}
for any fixed

. In particular, we consider a cubic transformation
\mathcal{P}_{3}(y)=y^{3}-3y(1+\sigma^{2})
and obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{P}_{3}(y)|\boldsymbol{x}]=\sum_{i\in[k]}p_{i}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})(\langle{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle^{3}-3\langle{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle)

Now it turns out that after using the orthogonality of
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\ast}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}
, and the fact
\mathbb{E}[p(Z)]=\mathbb{E}[p^{\prime}(Z)]=\mathbb{E}[p^{\prime\prime}(Z)]=0
for
3^{\mathrm{rd}}
-Hermite polynomial
p(z)=z^{3}-3z
and
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, we can nullify the cross-moments between
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\ast}
’s and
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}
’s to obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{P}_{3}(y)\cdot\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})]=6\sum_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}[p_{i}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})].(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast})^{\otimes 3}.

Similarly, we can show that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{P}_{2}(y)\cdot\mathcal{S}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})]=2\sum_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}[p_{i}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})].(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast})^{\otimes 2}
. For a general non-linearity
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we can similarly design cubic and quadratic polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{3}=y^{3}+\alpha y^{2}+\beta y
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}=y^{2}+\gamma y
such that we can still construct supersymmetric tensors involving the regressors. In order to obtain the unique set of coefficients
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, we need to solve a linear system of equations, which we describe in Appendix

∎

Once we obtain
\mathcal{T}_{2}
and
\mathcal{T}_{3}

follow from the standard tensor decomposition guarantees, for example, Theorem 4.3 and Theorem 5 of

\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
are such that
\max_{i\in[k]}\left\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}-\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}\right\|_{2}=\sigma^{2}\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Now we present our theoretical results for global convergence of EM. First we briefly recall the algorithm. Let
\Omega
denote the domain of our gating parameters, defined as

\displaystyle\Omega=\left\{\boldsymbol{w}=(\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{k-1}):\left\|\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\right\|_{2}\leq R,\forall i\in[k-1]\right\}.

Then the population EM for the mixture of experts consists of the following two steps:

•

E-step:
Using the current estimate
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}

to compute the function
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
,

•

M-step:

\boldsymbol{w}_{t+1}=\mathrm{argmax}_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega}Q(\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
,

where the function
Q(.|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
is the expected log-likelihood of the complete data distribution with respect to current posterior distribution. Mathematically,

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x},y)}\mathbb{E}_{P_{z|\boldsymbol{x},y,\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}}[\log P_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{x},z,y)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x},y)}\mathbb{E}_{P_{z|\boldsymbol{x},y,\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}}[\log P(\boldsymbol{x})P_{\boldsymbol{w}}(z|\boldsymbol{x})P(y|\boldsymbol{x},z)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x},y)}\mathbb{E}_{P_{z|\boldsymbol{x},y,\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}}[\log P_{\boldsymbol{w}}(z|x)]+\mathrm{const.}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i\in[k-1]}p_{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})-(1+\sum_{i\in[k-1]}e^{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}})]

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt+\mathrm{const.}

(8)

where
\mathrm{const}
refers to terms not depending on
\boldsymbol{w}
,
P_{\boldsymbol{w}}(z=i|x)=\exp(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})/\sum_{j}\exp(\boldsymbol{w}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})
and
p_{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}^{(i)}\triangleq\mathbb{P}\left[z=i|\boldsymbol{x},y,\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\right]
corresponds to the posterior probability for the
i^{\text{th}}
expert, given by

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}^{(i)}=\frac{p_{i,t}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathcal{N}(y|g(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}),\sigma^{2})}{\sum_{j\in[k]}p_{j,t}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathcal{N}(y|g(\boldsymbol{a}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}),\sigma^{2})},

(9)

\displaystyle p_{i,t}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{e^{(\boldsymbol{w}_{t})_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}}{1+\sum_{j\in[k-1]}e^{(\boldsymbol{w}_{t})_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}}.

In (
8
), the expectation is with respect to the true distribution of
(\boldsymbol{x},y)
, given by (
1
). Thus the EM can be viewed as a deterministic procedure which maps
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\mapsto M(\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
where

\displaystyle M(\boldsymbol{w})=\mathrm{argmax}_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in\Omega}Q(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{w}).

When the estimated regressors
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
equal the true parameters
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}
, it follows from the self-consistency property of the EM that the true parameter
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
is a fixed-point for the EM operator
M
, i.e.
M(\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})=\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}

(
McLachlan & Krishnan 2007
)
. However, this does not guarantee that EM converges to
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
. In the following theorem, we show that even when the regressors are known approximately, EM algorithm converges to the true gating parameters at a geometric rate upto an additive error, under
global
initializations. For the error metric, we define
\left\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\right\|\triangleq\max_{i\in[k-1]}\left\|\boldsymbol{w}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}_{i}\right\|_{2}
for any
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in\Omega
. We assume that
R=1
for simplicity. (Our results extend straightforwardly to general
R
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be such that
\max_{i}\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}-\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\ast}\|_{2}=\sigma^{2}\varepsilon
. There exists a constant
\sigma_{0}>0
such that whenever
0<\sigma<\sigma_{0}
, for any random initialization
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\in\Omega
, the population-level EM updates on the gating parameter
\{\boldsymbol{w}\}_{t\geq 0}
converge almost geometrically to the true parameter
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
upto an additive error, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\right\|\leq\left(\kappa_{\sigma}\right)^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{w}_{0}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\right\|+\kappa\varepsilon\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\kappa_{\sigma}^{i},

where
\kappa_{\sigma},\kappa
are dimension-independent constant depending on
g
and
\sigma
such that
\kappa_{\sigma}\xrightarrow{\sigma\rightarrow 0}0
and
\kappa\leq(k-1)\frac{\sqrt{6(2+\sigma^{2})}}{2}
for
g=
linear, sigmoid and ReLU.

Proof.

(Sketch) One can show that the
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
defined in (
8
) is a strongly concave function. Moreover, if we let
\varepsilon=0
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
, we have from the self-consistency of EM that
\mathrm{argmax}Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})=\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
. Thus if we can show that the functions are
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
and
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})
“sufficiently close" whenever
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
are close, we can use the EM convergence analysis tools from
(
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
)
to show that their corresponding maximizers also stay close upto a scaling factor determined by
\kappa_{\sigma}
above. Then it follows that the EM updates converge geometrically.
∎

Remark.
In the M-step of the EM algorithm, the next iterate is chosen so that the function
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
is maximized. Instead we can perform an ascent step in the direction of the gradient of
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
to produce the next iterate, i.e.
\boldsymbol{w}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{w}_{t}+\alpha\nabla Q(\boldsymbol{w}_{t}|\boldsymbol{w}_{t}))

.

Suppose that
(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}_{2})=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\ast})

(Sketch)

5
Discussion

In this paper we provided the first provable and globally consistent algorithm that can learn the true parameters of a MoE model. We believe that ideas from
(
Sedghi et al. 2014
)
can be naturally extended for the finite sample complexity analysis of the tensor decomposition to learn the regressors and similarly, techniques from
(
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
)
can be extended to the finite sample EM convergence analysis for the gating parameters. While we have focused here on parameter recovery, however, there are no statistical bounds on output prediction error when the data is not generated from the model. MoE models are known to be capable of fitting general functions, and getting statistical guarantees on learning in such regimes is an interesting direction for future work.

Appendix A
Toolbox for method of moments

In this section, we introduce the key techniques that are useful in parameter estimation of mixture models via the method of moments.

Stein’s identity (Stein’s lemma) is a well-known result in probability and statistics and is widely used in estimation and inference taks. A refined version of the Stein’s lemma
(
Stein 1972
)
for higher-order moments is the key to parameter estimation in mixture of generalized linear models. We utilize this machinery in proving Theorem
1
. We first recall the Stein’s lemma.

Lemma 1

(Stein’s lemma
(
Stein 1972
)
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that both
\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x})]
and
\mathbb{E}[g(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{x}]
exist and are finite. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{x}]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x})].

The following lemma, which can be viewed as an extension of Stein’s lemma for higher-order moments, is the central technique behind parameter estimation in M-GLMs.

Lemma 2

(
(
Sedghi et al. 2014
)
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and
\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})
be as defined in (
6
) and let
\mathcal{S}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\triangleq\boldsymbol{x}\otimes\boldsymbol{x}-I_{d}
. Then for any
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying some regularity conditions, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\mathcal{S}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(2)}g(\boldsymbol{x})],\quad\mathbb{E}[g(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\mathcal{S}_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(3)}g(\boldsymbol{x})].

Appendix B
Toolbox for EM convergence analysis

Recall that the domain of our gating parameters is
\Omega=\{\boldsymbol{w}:\left\|\boldsymbol{w}\right\|\leq 1\}
. Then the population EM for the mixture of experts consists of the following two steps:

•

E-step:
Using the current estimate
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}

to compute the function
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
.

•

M-step:

\boldsymbol{w}_{t+1}=\mathrm{argmax}_{\left\|\boldsymbol{w}\right\|\leq 1}Q(\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
.

Thus the EM can be viewed as a deterministic procedure which maps
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\mapsto M(\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
where

\displaystyle M(\boldsymbol{w})=\mathrm{argmax}_{\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in\Omega}Q(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{w}).

Our convergence analysis relies on tools from
(
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
)
where they provided local convergence results on both the EM and gradient EM algorithms.
In particular, they showed that if we initialize EM in a sufficiently small neighborhood around the true parameters, the EM iterates converge geometrically to the true parameters under some strong-concavity and gradient stability conditions.
We now formally state the assumptions in
(
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
)
under which the convergence guarantees hold. We will show in the next section that these conditions hold
globally
in our setting.

Assumption 1

(Convexity of the domain)
.

\Omega
is convex.

Assumption 2

(Strong-concavity)
.

Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})
is a
\lambda
-strongly concave function over a
r
-neighborhood of
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
, i.e.
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast},r)\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega:\left\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\right\|\leq r\}
.

Remark 1
.

An important point to note is that the true parameter
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
is a fixed point for the EM algorithm, i.e.
M(\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})=\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
. This is also known as
self-consistency
of the EM algorithm. Hence it is reasonable to expect that in a sufficiently small neighborhood around
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
there exists a unique maximizer for
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})
.

Assumption 3

(First-order stability condition)
.

Assume that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla Q(M(\boldsymbol{w})|\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})-\nabla Q(M(\boldsymbol{w})|\boldsymbol{w})\right\|\leq\gamma\left\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\right\|,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast},r).

Remark 2
.

Intuitively, the gradient stability condition enforces the gradient maps
\nabla Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w})
and
\nabla Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})
to be close whenever
\boldsymbol{w}
lies in a neighborhood of
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
. This will ensure that the mapped output
M(\boldsymbol{w})
stays closer to
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
.

Theorem 4

(Theorem 1,
(
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
)
)
.

If the above assumptions are met for some radius
r>0
and
0\leq\gamma<\lambda
, then the map
\boldsymbol{w}\mapsto M(\boldsymbol{w})
is contractive over
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast},r)
, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\|M(\boldsymbol{w})-\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\right\|\leq\left(\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\right\|,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast},r),

and consequently, the EM iterates
\{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
converge geometrically to
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\right\|\leq\left(\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\right)^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{w}_{0}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\right\|,

whenever the initialization
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\in\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast},r)
.

Appendix C
Class of non-linearities

In this section, we characterize the class of non-linearities for which our theoretical results for the recovery of regressors hold. Let
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
Y|Z\sim\mathcal{N}(g(Z),\sigma^{2})
, where
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. For
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{3}(y)

\displaystyle\triangleq Y^{3}+\alpha Y^{2}+\beta Y,\quad\mathcal{S}_{3}(Z)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{P}_{3}(y)|Z]=g(Z)^{3}+\alpha g(Z)^{2}+g(Z)(\beta+3\sigma^{2})+\alpha\sigma^{2},

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}(Y)

\displaystyle\triangleq Y^{2}+\gamma Y,\quad\mathcal{S}_{2}(Z)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{2}(Y)|Z]=g(Z)^{2}+\gamma g(Z)+\sigma^{2}.

Condition 1
.

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{3}^{\prime}(Z)]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{3}^{\prime\prime}(Z)]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{3}^{\prime\prime\prime}(Z)]\neq 0
.

Condition 2
.

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{2}^{\prime}(Z)]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{2}^{\prime\prime}(Z)]\neq 0
.

We are now ready to define the
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-valid class of non-linearities.

Definition 1
.

We say that the non-linearity
g
is
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-valid if there exists
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that both Condition
1
and Condition
2
are satisfied.

We have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{3}^{\prime}(Z)

\displaystyle=3g(Z)^{2}g^{\prime}(Z)+2\alpha g(Z)g^{\prime}(Z)+g^{\prime}(Z)(\beta+3\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=2\alpha g(Z)g^{\prime}(Z)+\beta g^{\prime}(Z)+3g(Z)^{2}g^{\prime}(Z)+3g^{\prime}(Z)\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{3}^{\prime\prime}(Z)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\left(g^{\prime}(Z)^{2}+g(Z)g^{\prime\prime}(Z)\right)+\beta g^{\prime\prime}(Z)+3g^{\prime\prime}(Z)(g(Z)^{2}+\sigma^{2})+6g(Z)g^{\prime}(Z)^{2}.

Thus
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{3}^{\prime}(Z)]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{3}^{\prime\prime}(Z)]=0
implies that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}2\mathbb{E}(g(Z)g^{\prime}(Z))&\mathbb{E}(g^{\prime}(Z))\\
2\mathbb{E}\left(g^{\prime}(Z)^{2}+g(Z)g^{\prime\prime}(Z)\right)&\mathbb{E}(g^{\prime\prime}(Z))\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-3\mathbb{E}(g(Z)^{2}g^{\prime}(Z)+g^{\prime}(Z)\sigma^{2})\\
-3\mathbb{E}(g^{\prime\prime}(Z)(g(Z)^{2}+\sigma^{2})+2g(Z)g^{\prime}(Z)^{2})\end{bmatrix}

To ensure Condition
1
, we need the pair
(\alpha,\beta)
obtained by solving the above linear equation to satisfy
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{3}^{\prime\prime\prime}(Z)]\neq 0
. Similarly,
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{2}^{\prime}(Z)]=0
implies that

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{-2\mathbb{E}[g(Z)g^{\prime}(Z)]}{\mathbb{E}[g^{\prime}(Z)]}.

Thus Condition
2
stipulates that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{2}^{\prime\prime}(Z)]\neq 0
with this choice of
\gamma
. It turns out that these conditions hold for a wide class of non-linearities and in particular, when
g
is either the identity function, or the sigmoid function, or the ReLU. For these three choices of popular non-linearities, the values of the tuple
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
are provided below (which are obtained by solving the linear equations mentioned above).

Example 1
.

If
g
is the identity mapping, then
\mathcal{P}_{3}(y)=y^{3}-3y(1+\sigma^{2})
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}(y)=y^{2}
.

Example 2
.

If
g
is the sigmoid function, i.e.
g(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
, then
\alpha
and
\beta
can be obtained by solving the following linear equation:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0.2066&0.2066\\
0.0624&-0.0001\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-0.1755-0.6199\sigma^{2}\\
-0.0936\end{bmatrix}

The second-order transformation is given by
\mathcal{S}_{2}(y)=y^{2}-y
(since
\gamma=-1
when
g
is sigmoid).

Example 3
.

If
g
is the ReLU function, i.e.
g(z)=\mathrm{max}\{0,z\}
, then
\alpha=-3\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}},\beta=3\left(\frac{4}{\pi}-\sigma^{2}-1\right)

Proof.

We first note that the EM iterates

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}_{t}=M(\boldsymbol{W}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{A}),\quad t\geq 1

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{W}_{t}-\boldsymbol{W}^{\ast}\right\|=\left\|M(\boldsymbol{W}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{A})-\boldsymbol{W}^{\ast}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|M(\boldsymbol{W}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{A})-M(\boldsymbol{W}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{A}^{\ast})\right\|

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}+(1-f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x},

(16)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}+f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\cdot(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}.

(17)

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[y|\boldsymbol{x}]-\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}{(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}).

Notice that in the above equation we have
(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}
in the denominator. But this equals zero with zero probability whenever
\boldsymbol{x}
is generated from a continuous distribution; in our case
\boldsymbol{x}
is Gaussian. Thus we may write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{y-\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}{(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{✗}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[y|\boldsymbol{x}]-\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}{(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}f^{\prime}(\left\|\boldsymbol{w}\right\|Z)\cdot\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle\propto\boldsymbol{w}.

However, it turns out that the above chain of equalities does not hold. Surprisingly, the first equality, which essentially is the law of iterated expectations, is not valid in this case as
\frac{y-\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}{(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}
is not integrable. To see this, notice that the model in (
13
) can also be written as

\displaystyle y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}Z(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})+(1-Z)(\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma N,\quad Z\sim\mathrm{Bern}(f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})),N\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ratio}\triangleq\frac{y-\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}{(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}Z+\frac{\sigma N}{(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}.

Since
Z
is independent of
N
and
\frac{N}{(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}
is a Cauchy random variable, it follows that the random variable
\mathrm{Ratio}
is not integrable. To deal with the non-integrability of
\mathrm{Ratio}
, we look at its conditional cdf, given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{Ratio}\leq z|\boldsymbol{x}\right]=f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\Phi\left((z-1)\frac{|\Delta_{x}|}{\sigma}\right)+(1-f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))\Phi\left(z\frac{|\Delta_{x}|}{\sigma}\right),\quad\Delta_{x}=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x},

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard Gaussian cdf. Substituting
z=0.5
and using the fact that
\Phi(z)+\Phi(-z)=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{Ratio}\leq 0.5|\boldsymbol{x}\right]

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\Phi\left(-\frac{|\Delta_{x}|}{2\sigma}\right)+(1-f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))\Phi\left(\frac{|\Delta_{x}|}{2\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\frac{|(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}|}{2\sigma}\right)+f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\left(1-2\Phi\left(\frac{|(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}|}{2\sigma}\right)\right).

Since
\Phi\left(\frac{|(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}|}{2\sigma}\right)
is a symmetric function in
\boldsymbol{x}
its first moment with
\boldsymbol{x}
equals zero. Furthermore, if we assume that
\boldsymbol{w}
is orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}\left\{\mathrm{Ratio}\leq 0.5\right\}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{Ratio}\leq 0.5|\boldsymbol{x}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\left(1-2\Phi\left(\frac{|(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}|}{2\sigma}\right)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(1-2\Phi\left(\frac{|(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}|}{2\sigma}\right)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{w}+

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\mathbb{E}[f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})]\cdot\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left(1-2\Phi\left(\frac{|(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}|}{2\sigma}\right)\right)\right]}_{=0,\text{ since derivative of a even function is odd}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(1-2\Phi\left(\frac{|(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}|}{2\sigma}\right)\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{w}

\displaystyle\propto\boldsymbol{w}.

Thus, if
\left\|\boldsymbol{w}\right\|=1
, we have that

\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}\left\{\mathrm{Ratio}\leq 0.5\right\}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\right]}{\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}\left\{\mathrm{Ratio}\leq 0.5\right\}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\right]\right\|}=\boldsymbol{w}.

In the finite sample regime,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}\left\{\mathrm{Ratio}\leq 0.5\right\}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\right]
can be estimated from samples using the empirical moments and its normalized version will be an estimate of
\boldsymbol{w}
.
∎

We need the following lemma which establishes the stability of the minimizers for strongly convex functions under Lipschitz perturbations.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a closed convex subset,

is a
\lambda

\lambda>0
and

\Omega

\boldsymbol{w}_{f}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega}f(\boldsymbol{w})
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{f+B}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega}f(\boldsymbol{w})+B(\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{w}_{f}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f+B}\right\|\leq\frac{L}{\lambda}.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}\in\Omega
be such that
\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|>\frac{L}{\lambda}
. Let
\boldsymbol{w}_{\alpha}=\alpha\boldsymbol{w}_{f}+(1-\alpha)\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
for
0<\alpha<1
. From the fact that
\boldsymbol{w}_{f}
is the minimizer of
f
on
\Omega
and that
f
is strongly convex, we have that

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})\geq f(\boldsymbol{w}_{f})+\frac{\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|^{2}}{2}.

Furthermore, the strong-convexity of
f
implies that

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{w}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq\alpha f(\boldsymbol{w}_{f})+(1-\alpha)f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)\lambda}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})+\alpha(f(\boldsymbol{w}_{f})-f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}))-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)\lambda}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})-\alpha\frac{\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|^{2}}{2}-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)\lambda}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})-\lambda\alpha\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|^{2}

(18)

Since
B
is
L
-Lipschitz, we have

\displaystyle B(\boldsymbol{w}_{\alpha})\leq B(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})+L\alpha\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|.

(19)

Adding (
18
) and (
19
), we get

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{w}_{\alpha})+B(\boldsymbol{w}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})+B(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})+L\alpha\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|-\lambda\alpha\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})+B(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})+\alpha\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|\left(\frac{L}{\lambda}-\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|\right)

By the assumption that
\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|>\frac{L}{\lambda}
, the term
\frac{L}{\lambda}-\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|
will be negative for sufficiently small
\alpha
. This in turn implies that
f(\boldsymbol{w}_{\alpha})+B(\boldsymbol{w}_{\alpha})<f(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})+B(\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime})
for such
\alpha
. Consequently
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
is not a minimizer of
f+B
for any
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
such that
\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{w}_{f}\right\|>\frac{L}{\lambda}
. The conclusion follows.

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\succcurlyeq}}\inf_{0\leq\alpha\leq 1}\min\left\{\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(\alpha Z)],\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(\alpha Z)]+\alpha^{2}\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime\prime\prime}(\alpha Z)]\right\}\cdot I

\displaystyle=\underbrace{0.14}_{\lambda}\cdot I

(28)

\boldsymbol{w}_{u}

Similarly,

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{x},y,\boldsymbol{w}_{u},\sigma)

(32)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{\sigma}}{\sqrt{3}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Ratio}(\boldsymbol{x},y,\boldsymbol{w}_{u},\sigma)^{2}]}du

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})^{2}(1-2Z)+\frac{2N}{n}(\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i\in[k-1]}p_{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})-\log\left(1+\sum_{i\in[k-1]}e^{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\right],

(52)

\displaystyle\lambda=\inf_{\left\|\boldsymbol{a}\right\|=1}\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{a}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}M_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\boldsymbol{x}}],

(53)

\boldsymbol{a}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{k-1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k-1}

M_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is given by

\displaystyle M_{\boldsymbol{x}}(i,j)=\begin{cases}p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})(1-p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})),\quad i=j\\
-p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})p_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad i\neq j\end{cases}

53

\boldsymbol{a}^{\ast}

\boldsymbol{x}

\boldsymbol{a}^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq 0

x

\boldsymbol{a}^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq 0

. For
i=1
, we have

\displaystyle R_{1}(\boldsymbol{x},y,\boldsymbol{w}_{u},\sigma)^{2}=\left(\frac{\sum_{j\geq 2}p_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})p_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{1}N_{j}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{i}\right)^{2}}\right)^{2}\leq k\sum_{j\geq 2}\left(\frac{p_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})p_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{1}N_{j}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{i}\right)^{2}}\right)^{2}\leq k\sum_{j\geq 2}\left(\frac{p_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})p_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{1}N_{j}}{(p_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{1}+p_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{j})^{2}}\right)^{2}

Similarly,

\displaystyle R_{i}(\boldsymbol{x},y,\boldsymbol{w}_{u},\sigma)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{p_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{1}N_{i}}{(p_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{1}+p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{i})^{2}}\right)^{2},\quad\forall i\neq 1,i\in[k-1].

For
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}_{u}
and
i\neq 1
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{p_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{1}N_{i}}{(p_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{1}+p_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})N_{i})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\boldsymbol{w}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}e^{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}e^{-\frac{(y-g(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{(y-g(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}{\left(e^{\boldsymbol{w}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}e^{-\frac{(y-g(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+e^{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}e^{-\frac{(y-g(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\boldsymbol{w}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}e^{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}e^{\frac{(y-g(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}-(y-g(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}{\left(e^{\boldsymbol{w}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}+e^{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}e^{\frac{(y-g(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}-(y-g(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sigma\rightarrow 0}0.

Thus, by Dominated Convergence Theorem,
\mathbb{E}[R_{i}(\boldsymbol{x},y,\boldsymbol{w}_{u},\sigma)^{2}]\rightarrow 0
for each
u\in[0,1]
. To show that
\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[R_{i}(\boldsymbol{x},y,\boldsymbol{w}_{u},\sigma)^{2}]du\rightarrow 0
, we can now follow the same analysis as in the proof of Theorem
2
from (
29
) on-wards (replacing
\boldsymbol{w}
there with
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
) which ensures that
\gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma}
in our case converges to zero. Similarly for other
i\in[k-1]
, we get that
\gamma^{(i)}\rightarrow 0
. Taking
\gamma_{\sigma}=\gamma^{(1)}_{\sigma}+\ldots+\gamma^{(k-1)}_{\sigma}
and
\kappa_{\sigma}=\frac{\gamma_{\sigma}}{\lambda}
completes the proof.

∎

Appendix G
Gradient EM algorithm

In this section, we provide the convergence guarantees for the gradient EM algorithm. For simplicity, we prove the results for
k=2
and
(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}_{2})=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\ast})
. Thus we want to learn the gating parameter
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
in this setting. The results for the general case follow essentially the same proof as that of Theorem
2
. In particular, our Theorem
5
can be viewed as a generalization of Lemma
3
. Together with Lemma
4
, extension to general
k
is straightforward.

Note that in the M-step of the EM algorithm, instead of maximizing
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
, we can chose an iterate so that it increases the
Q
value instead of fully maximizing it, i.e.
Q(\boldsymbol{w}_{t+1}|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})\geq Q(\boldsymbol{w}_{t}|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
. Such a procedure is termed as
generalized EM
.
Gradient EM
is an example of generalized EM in which we take an ascent step in the direction of the gradient of
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}_{t})
to produce the next iterate, i.e.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{w}_{t}+\alpha\nabla Q(\boldsymbol{w}_{t}|\boldsymbol{w}_{t}),

where
\alpha>0
is a suitably chosen step size and the gradient is with respect to the first argument. To account for the constrained optimization, we can include a projection step. Mathematically,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{t+1}=G(\boldsymbol{w}_{t}),\quad G(\boldsymbol{w})=\Pi_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{w}+\alpha\nabla Q(\boldsymbol{w}|)\boldsymbol{w}),

where
\Pi_{\Omega}
refers to the projection operator. Our next result establishes that the iterates of the gradient EM algorithm too converge geometrically for an appropriately chosen step size
\alpha
.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose that the domain
\Omega=\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}\leq 1\}
and
(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}_{2})=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{a}_{2}^{\ast})
. Then there exist constants
\alpha_{0}>0
and
\sigma_{0}>0
such that for any step size
0<\alpha\leq\alpha_{0}
and noise variance
\sigma<\sigma_{0}
, the gradient EM updates on the gating parameter
\{\boldsymbol{w}\}_{t\geq 0}
converge geometrically to the true parameter
\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\right\|\leq\left(\rho_{\sigma}\right)^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{w}_{0}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast}\right\|,

where
\rho_{\sigma}
is a dimension-independent constant depending on
g
and
\sigma
.

Remark 3
.

The condition
\sigma<\sigma_{0}
ensures that the Lipschitz constant
\rho_{\sigma}
for the map
G
is strictly less than
1
. The constant
\alpha_{0}
depends only on two universal constants which are nothing but the strong-concavity and the smoothness parameters for the function
Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})
.

Proof.

In addition to the assumptions of Appendix
B
, if we can ensure that the map
-Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})
is
\mu
-smooth, then the proof follows from Theorem
3
of
(
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
)
if we choose
\alpha_{0}=\frac{2}{\mu+\lambda}
where
\lambda
is the strong-convexity parameter of
-Q(\cdot|\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})
. The strong-convexity is already established in Appendix
D.3
. To find the smoothness parameter, note that

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}Q(\boldsymbol{w}|\boldsymbol{w}^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right],

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime\prime\prime}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}+\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})]\cdot I

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime\prime\prime}(\left\|\boldsymbol{w}\right\|Z)]\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}+\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(\left\|\boldsymbol{w}\right\|Z)]\cdot I,\quad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

\displaystyle\preceq\sup_{0\leq\alpha\leq 1}\min\left\{\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(\alpha Z)],\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(\alpha Z)]+\alpha^{2}\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime\prime\prime}(\alpha Z)]\right\}\cdot I

\displaystyle=\underbrace{0.25}_{\mu}\cdot I.

The contraction parameter is then given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\sigma}=1-\frac{2\lambda+2\gamma_{\sigma}}{\mu+\lambda}.

Since
\gamma_{\sigma}\xrightarrow{\sigma\rightarrow 0}0
,
\rho_{\sigma}<1
whenever
\sigma<\sigma_{0}
for a constant
\sigma_{0}
.
∎
