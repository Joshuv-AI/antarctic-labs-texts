Instantaneous time mirrors and wave equations with time-singular coefficients
Olivier Pinaud

Abstract
We study in this work the concept of instantaneous time mirrors that were recently introduced in the physics literature in [ 1 ] . Instantaneous time mirrors offer a new method for time reversal with a simplified experimental setup compared to classical techniques. At the mathematical level, instantaneous time mirrors are modeled by singularities in the time variable in the coefficients of a wave equation, and a prototype of such singularity is a Dirac delta. Our main goal in this work is to obtain refocusing estimates for the wavefield that quantify the quality of time reversal. This amounts to analyze the wave equation with Dirac-type singularities and develop a proper regularity theory as well as derive uniform estimates.



1
Introduction

This work is concerned with the mathematical analysis of
Instantaneous Time Mirrors (ITM)
that were introduced recently in
[
1
]
, and which offer a new avenue for time reversal. The latter is a technique developed by M. Fink and collaborators in the nineties, see e.g.
[
5
]
, based on the idea that if time is reversed in the wave equation
\partial_{t}^{2}u=\partial_{x}^{2}u
for instance, that is
t
becomes
-t
, then the equation is not changed. This fact was successfully exploited in order to focus waves: suppose that (i) a signal is emitted from a point source at a time
t=0
and propagates according to some time-reversible equations (e.g. a linear hyperbolic system without absorption such as acoustic, elastic, or electromagnetic wave equations), then (ii) is recorded and time-reversed at time
T
(i.e. what is recorded last is sent back first), and finally (iii) is re-emitted for back-propagation during a time
T
. Then, at time
2T
, the signal refocuses at the location of the point source. The quality of refocusing depends on various factors, such as how much of the signal was recorded during reversal and how heterogeneous is the underlying medium of propagation.

Time reversal has found many important applications in medical imaging, non-destructive testing, and telecommunications for instance. Its main practical difficulty is the measurement/reversal process: detectors (transducers) must operate both as recorders and emitters, and must occupy a sufficiently large domain of space for sharp focusing. This is often difficult to realize.

The groundbreaking nature of
[
1
]
is that time reversal can actually be achieved without any measurements and a complex experimental apparatus. The main idea is that sudden and strong perturbations in the medium of propagation generate back-propagating waves that refocus at the emission point. The procedure is referred to in
[
1
]
as creating an instantaneous time mirror, and opens interesting new perspectives as on the one hand the experimental procedure is simplified, and on the other some situations where time reversal was not feasible (e.g. quantum systems where phases are difficult to measure and the state of the system is modified by measurements) are amenable to reversal provided the background can be controlled. Note that ITM fall into the context of time refraction and time reflection where energy is in general not conserved, contrary to spatial refraction/reflection, see
[
8
]
.

At the mathematical level, ITM are modeled by time-singular coefficients in hyperbolic equations. The prototype of such singularity is a delta function at a given time
T
, that represents the (strong) perturbation due to the ITM at
T
. In
[
1
]
, this is the sudden and strong shaking of a water tank that changes abruptly the wave velocity of surface waves. A signal emitted at time
t=0
, perturbed by an ITM at
t=T
, will then refocus at its source at time
t=2T
.

The objective of the present work is to continue the analysis of ITM that we began in
[
2
]
. In the latter, we analyzed the refocusing wave in the context of wave equations with Dirac-type singularities. We proved refocusing estimates (the notion will be introduced further in the paper) for the wave equation with
spatially constant coefficients
. This allowed us to use the Fourier transform and pursue a fine analysis of the time-singularities in Fourier space. We generalize in this work the refocusing estimates to wave equations with smooth variable coefficients. Naturally, the use of Fourier techniques is not possible and we need to resort to different methods. A feature of ITM is that the refocusing wave is the time derivative of the original wave. This will be reflected in the estimates where there is a loss of a derivative.

The article is organized as follows: in Section
2
, we introduce some background on ITM and state our main results. The latter consists of two theorems: in the first one, we obtain refocusing estimates for the wave equation with varying coefficients when the ITM is modeled by an approximation of a Dirac delta; in the second theorem, we show that the system obtained by removing the approximation is well-posed. The proofs of these theorems are given in Sections
3
and
4
. We provide in the Appendix derivations of wave equations with time-dependent coefficients in the context of electromagnetics, elasticity, and fluids.

Main results

We start by introducing some background on ITM.

2.1
Preliminaries

Notations.

For
d\geq 1
,
p\in[1,\infty]
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the usual Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces.
BV(\Omega)
is the space of functions with bounded variations in
\Omega
, with
BV_{\rm{loc}}
and
L^{p}_{\rm{loc}}
the local versions of
BV
and
L^{p}
.
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space of infinitely differentiable functions, and
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
functions with compact support. For two real-valued functions
f
and
g
, we define
(f,g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)g(x)dx
.

The wave equation.

We consider the following wave equation (we suppose here all variables have been non-dimensionalized):

\partial_{t}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}=a(x)\nabla\cdot\Big(b(x)\big(1+\chi(x)\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)\big)\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\Big),\qquad(t,x)\quad\textrm{on}\quad\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1)

where
a
and
b
are two functions modeling a smooth, unperturbed background, and the term
\chi(x)\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)
models the action of the ITM. For simplicity of the analysis, we suppose that (
1
) is posed over the entire
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
. Our results can be generalized to bounded domains with appropriate boundary conditions without difficulty. For
T>0
and
0<\varepsilon\leq T
given, the function
\eta_{\varepsilon}
has the form

\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\eta_{0}\varepsilon^{-1}&\textrm{when}\quad|t-T|<\varepsilon/2\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

The ITM acts therefore at time
t=T
over a window
\varepsilon
and with amplitude
\eta_{0}\varepsilon^{-1}
. The function
\eta_{\varepsilon}
is an approximation of a delta function at
t=T
with weight
\eta_{0}
, and
\chi
is a regularized version of the characteristic function of the spatial domain where the ITM acts (we can have
\chi=1
if the ITM acts on the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).
Our analysis can be straightforwardly generalized to multiple ITM perturbations of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}(x)\eta_{\varepsilon}^{(i)}(t),

where
\eta_{\varepsilon}^{(i)}
has the same form as
\eta_{\varepsilon}
and is centered at
T_{i}
.

We will suppose that
a
and
b
are in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with bounded derivatives and positive constants
\underline{a}
,
\bar{a}
,
\underline{b}
,
\bar{b}
such that

\underline{a}\leq a(x)\leq\bar{a},\qquad\underline{b}\leq b(x)\leq\bar{b}.

(2)

We assume as well that
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with bounded derivatives, and that
\chi
is positive to ensure that the velocity
c^{2}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=a(x)b(x)(1+\chi(x)\eta_{\varepsilon}(t))
remains positive. All the
C^{\infty}
regularity assumptions are not crucial and can be relaxed to a control of just a few derivatives.

The wave equation equation (
1
) is complemented with the initial conditions

u(t=0,x)=u_{0}(x)\in H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad\partial_{t}u(t=0,x)=u_{1}(x)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(3)

As will be proved further, such a regularity is needed in order to obtain optimal refocusing estimates. We suppose that
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
are real-valued, and as a consequence the solution
u
is real-valued as well since the coefficients in (
1
) are real. The case of complex-valued initial conditions is simply obtained by separating real and imaginary parts.

Physical derivations of wave equations with time-dependent coefficients of the form (
1
) are given in the Appendix in the context of electromagnetics, elasticity, and surface waves. The solution
u_{\varepsilon}
is then the surface height for the latter, or related to the magnetic, electric, displacement field for the former.

Time reversal and refocusing.

We introduce in this paragraph some necessary background on ITM and some results from
[
2
]
. In order to investigate the refocusing induced by the ITM, it is convenient to recast (
1
) as a first-order system: let
\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\big[b(1+\chi\eta_{\varepsilon})\big]^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}+\nabla u_{\varepsilon}=0,\qquad a^{-1}\dfrac{\partial{u_{\varepsilon}}}{\partial{t}}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon}=0.

(4)

The system (
4
) can itself be stated as

A(x)\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}+D^{j}\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}}{\partial x_{j}}=V_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}],

(5)

where
V_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=b(x)(1+\chi(x)\eta_{\varepsilon}(t))
, and
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}=(\mathbf{v}_{\varepsilon},u_{\varepsilon})
,
\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}]=-(\nabla u_{\varepsilon},0)
(which are both considered as column vectors),
A=\textrm{Diag}(b^{-1},\cdots,b^{-1},a^{-1})
(
b^{-1}
repeated
d
times), and
(D^{j})_{mn}=\delta_{m,(d+1)}\delta_{n,j}+\delta_{n,(d+1)}\delta_{m,j}
, with
j=1,\cdots,d
, and
m,n=1,\cdots,d+1
. Here and below, we use the summation convention over repeated indices. Equation (
5
) is equipped with the initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{0}=(\mathbf{v}_{0},u_{0})
, where
\mathbf{v}_{0}
is such that
a^{-1}u_{1}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}_{0}=0
. We will need the Green’s function of the unperturbed equation (
5
), i.e. with
V=0
, defined by

A(x)\frac{\partial\mathbf{G}(t,x,y)}{\partial t}+D^{j}\frac{\partial\mathbf{G}(t,x,y)}{\partial x_{j}}=0,\qquad\mathbf{G}(0,x,y)=I\delta(x-y),

(6)

where
I
is the
(d+1)\times(d+1)
identity matrix and
\delta
the Dirac delta. We extend the Green’s function
\mathbf{G}_{t}
to negative values of
t
by solving (
6
) for
t<0
, and find
\mathbf{G}_{-t}\Gamma=\Gamma\mathbf{G}_{t}
, for
\Gamma=\textrm{Diag}(-1,\cdots,-1,1)
the time reversal matrix.
We will use the notation

\mathbf{G}_{t}(\mathbf{u})(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{G}(t,x,y)\mathbf{u}(y)dy.

The unperturbed solution to (
5
), i.e. with
V=0
, is denoted by
\mathbf{U}=(\mathbf{V},U)
. The following decomposition of
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}
, obtained in
[
2
]
, is key to understanding the refocusing effects of an ITM: the wavefield
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}
can be written as

\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\mathbf{U}(t)+\mathbf{u}_{R}(t)+\mathbf{u}_{F}(t)+\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}(t),

(7)

where
\mathbf{u}_{R}
is a backward (time-reversed) propagating wave,
\mathbf{u}_{F}
a forward propagating wave, and
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
a correction term. The precise expression of these terms can be found in
[
2
]
, and
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
will be our main focus. The decomposition (
7
) holds for any solution to the wave equation, and in general
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
has no reason to be small at all and can actually dominate. The latter reads

\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{G}_{t-s}\Big(V_{\varepsilon}(s)A^{-1}(\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}](s)-\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{U}](s))\Big)ds.

In the ITM context, the term
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
becomes negligible for small
\varepsilon
, and this is the mathematical explanation for the observation of a time-reversed focusing wave. The time-reversed field
\mathbf{u}_{R}
at the refocusing time
t=2T
is then the dominating term and reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{R}(2T,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{K}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\partial_{t}\mathbf{U}(y)dy

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta_{0}}{2\varepsilon}\int_{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\Gamma\mathbf{G}(2s,x,y)ds.

The kernel
\mathbf{K}_{\varepsilon}
is an approximation of
-\delta(x-y)\Gamma/2
when
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small since
\mathbf{G}(0,x,y)=\delta(x-y)I
. Some blurring in the refocusing is introduced when
\varepsilon
is not zero, and refocusing is perfect is the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. An important observation is that one reconstructs the time derivative of the initial condition
\partial_{t}\mathbf{U}(t=0)
and not the initial condition
\mathbf{U}(t=0)
. The ITM hence acts as a time differentiator, and this will be seen in the estimates.

The fact that
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
is negligible when
\varepsilon
is small is proved in
[
2
]
when the coefficients
a
,
b
and
\chi
are all constant. This allows for the use of the Fourier transform and (
1
) is reduced in Fourier space to

\partial_{t}^{2}\hat{u}_{\varepsilon}(t,\xi)+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)|\xi|^{2}\hat{u}_{\varepsilon}(t,\xi).

This is a one-dimensional Schrödinger equation in the variable
t
with the singular potential
-\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)|\xi|^{2}
. One can then precisely estimate
\hat{u}_{\varepsilon}
in terms of
\varepsilon
and
\xi
and obtain sufficient regularity to treat
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
.

Our goal in this work is to generalize this result to the case of variable
a
,
b
and
\chi
. The fact that
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)
is negligible when
t\geq T+\varepsilon/2
(the time right after the pertubation) follows from the following heuristical arguments: the non-zero component of
\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{U}]-\mathbf{S}[\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}]
in the definition of
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
reads, for any
s\in[T-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},T+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}]
,

\nabla(u_{\varepsilon}(s)-U(s))=\nabla(u_{\varepsilon}(s)-u_{\varepsilon}(s-\varepsilon))+\nabla(u_{\varepsilon}(s-\varepsilon)-U(s)).

As
s-\varepsilon<T-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, we are therefore before the perturbation, and the second term in the r.h.s. is actually equal to
\nabla U(s-\varepsilon)-\nabla U
. Since
\nabla U
is smooth (i.e. it has at least one time derivative), this second term is negligible provided
\varepsilon
is small compared to a parameter estimating
\nabla U
in some norm. The same applies to the first term provided
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}
has some regularity in time. The essential ingredient in estimating
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
is then a uniform bound for
\partial_{t}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}
in
L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, which will eventually provide us with an optimal control in terms of
\varepsilon
, that is
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}=O(\varepsilon)
in some appropriate sense. This bound is of course more difficult to obtain than in the constant coefficients case since the Fourier transform is not available. We refer to the relation
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}=O(\varepsilon)
as an optimal refocusing estimate. It is possible to obtain non optimal estimates of the form
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}=O(\varepsilon^{\gamma})
for
\gamma<1
assuming less regularity on the data.

We state now our main results.

2.2
Results

Using standard methods and assumptions (
2
)-(
3
), see e.g.
[
4
]
, Chapter 7, it is not difficult to establish that (
1
) admits a unique solution
u_{\varepsilon}
such that
u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

\partial_{t}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

. This estimate is used in the second statement of the theorem in order to obtain the optimal refocusing estimate
\mathbf{R}^{\varepsilon}=O(\varepsilon)
.

Theorem 2.1

Let
u_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to
(
1
)
with the initial conditions given in
(
3
)
. Then, we have the estimate, for all
\tau>0
,

\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau,H^{2})}+\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau,H^{1})}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{3}}+C\|u_{1}\|_{H^{2}},

(8)

where
C\equiv C(a,b,\chi,\eta_{0},\tau)
is independent of
\varepsilon
. Moreover, write
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}=(\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon})
, where
\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}
is a vector with
d
components. We have then the estimate, for all
\tau>0
,

\|\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau,H^{-1})}+\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau,L^{2})}\leq C\varepsilon,

(9)

where
C\equiv C(a,b,\chi,\eta_{0},\tau,u_{0},u_{1})
is independent of
\varepsilon
.

Note the loss of a spatial derivative in the uniform estimates (
8
): one needs
u_{\varepsilon}(t=0)\in H^{3}
and
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t=0)\in H^{2}
to obtain a uniform control of
u_{\varepsilon}(t)
and
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t)
in
H^{2}
and
H^{1}
, respectively. This is induced by the time singularity of the coefficients created by the ITM. This loss is optimal in the sense it is also there in the case of constant coefficients addressed in
[
2
]
where the Fourier transform allows for exact calculations. Owing to the heuristics that a time derivative is equivalent to a spatial derivative for the free the wave equation, this spatial loss can be related to the fact that the ITM acts as a time differentiator, as mentioned earlier.

The proof of (
8
) is based on three estimates obtained in six steps, and our main goal is to get the bound on
\partial_{t}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}
in
L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. In the first step, we derive a classical energy estimates for
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}
in
L^{2}
; now, this estimate is only uniform in
\varepsilon
at the location of the perturbation, modeled by
\chi
. In the second step, we extend the estimate to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at the price of losing one derivative. At the end of these first two steps, we have obtained uniform estimates for
u_{\varepsilon}
in
L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and for
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
in
L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. We then differentiate the equation twice, and use the same procedure as in steps 1 and 2 to arrive at the desired result for
\partial_{t}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}
.

Estimate (
9
) is a direct consequence of (
8
) and the equations satisfied by the different components of
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
.

Our second result concerns the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, and provides us with an existence and uniqueness theorem for the limiting wave equation with a Dirac delta at time
t=T
. The ITM is seen via a jump condition on the time derivative of the solution at
t=T
.

Theorem 2.2

Consider the wave equation, for
t>0
, and
t\neq T
,

\partial_{t}^{2}u=a(x)\nabla\cdot\big(b(x)\nabla u\big),

(10)

equipped with the initial conditions
(
3
)
and the following jump condition at

:

\partial_{t}u(T^{+},x)=\partial_{t}u(T^{-},x)+\eta_{0}a(x)\nabla\cdot\Big(b(x)\chi(x)\nabla u(T,x)\Big).

(11)

Then, the above system admits a unique solution
u
in
C^{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L_{\rm{loc}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
such that
\partial_{t}u\in L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap BV_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Equation
(
10
)
is verified almost everywhere in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and in
(T,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the jump condition
(
11
)
is satisfied in
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Moreover,
u_{\varepsilon}
converges to
u
as
\varepsilon\to 0
in
L_{\rm{loc}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
weak-
*
.

and

.

3
Proof of Theorem
2.1

We will consistenly use that
a,b
and
\chi
are smooth functions, and that
a,b
are positive and bounded below. We will not recall these facts for each estimate, and for simplicity will not make explicit the dependency of the various constants on
a,b,\chi
. The estimates will be derived for regular initial conditions
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to justify the calculations, in particular the integration by parts over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
using finite speed of propagation of the support. Hence, we will work with a solution
u_{\varepsilon}
that is infinitely differentiable with respect to the spatial variables and with bounded support for finite times, and that has two bounded derivatives with respect to
t
. The case
u_{0}\in H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
u_{1}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
follows by a simple limiting argument.

3.1
First estimate

We derive in this section uniform bounds for
u_{\varepsilon}
and
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, respectively. The first step is to obtain a uniform control at the location of the perturbation.

Step 1:

We begin with a classical energy estimate. Let

E_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}+c^{2}(x)|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\big)dx,

and

F_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}c^{2}(x)\chi(x)|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}dx,

where we have set
c^{2}(x)=a(x)b(x)
. All calculations in the proof are justified since
u_{\varepsilon}
has sufficient regularity. In particular, the wave equation (
1
) is satisfied everywhere on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and we find, by multiplying (
1
) by
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
and integrating in
x
, for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{dE_{\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)\frac{dF_{\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}b(x)(1+\chi(x)\eta_{\varepsilon}(t))\nabla a(x)\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\,\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)dx

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}(t).

We have, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for all
t>0
,

|A_{\varepsilon}(t)|\leq C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t))E_{\varepsilon}(t).

Let now
t_{\varepsilon}^{\pm}=T\pm\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon
, and
\eta_{\varepsilon,0}=\eta_{0}/\varepsilon
.
Then, since
\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)=\eta_{\varepsilon,0}
for
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
,

\displaystyle\frac{dE_{\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}+\eta_{\varepsilon,0}\frac{dF_{\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}\leq C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon,0})E_{\varepsilon}(t),\qquad t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+}),

which yields, for all
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
,

E_{\varepsilon}(t)+\eta_{\varepsilon,0}F_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq\big(E_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})+\eta_{\varepsilon,0}F_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\big)e^{C(\varepsilon+\eta_{0})}.

(12)

As a consequence, for all
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
,

F_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq\big(\varepsilon\eta_{0}^{-1}E_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})+F_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\big)e^{C(\varepsilon+\eta_{0})}=:(M_{0,\varepsilon})^{2}.

(13)

This last estimate is uniform in
\varepsilon
since
E_{\varepsilon}(t)
and
F_{\varepsilon}(t)
are continuous in time and independent of
\varepsilon
before the perturbation. Note that (
13
) only provides us with a uniform control at the location of the perturbation, and that estimate (
12
) does not yield a uniform bound over the entire
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For this, we need to exploit the latter estimate on
F_{\varepsilon}
and go back to the wave equation, at the price of losing one derivative.

Step 2.

We exploit here estimate (
13
) on
F_{\varepsilon}
to control
u_{\varepsilon}
and
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We rewrite (
1
) as

\partial_{t}^{2}u_{\varepsilon}=-aL_{0}u_{\varepsilon}+au_{\varepsilon}+a\eta_{\varepsilon}L_{1}u_{\varepsilon}\qquad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

where

L_{0}u=-\nabla\cdot\big(b\nabla u\big)+u,\qquad L_{1}u=\nabla\cdot\big(b\chi\nabla u\big).

Recalling that
b\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
0<\underline{b}\leq b(x)\leq\bar{b}
and bounded derivatives, the operator
L_{0}
is self-adjoint when equipped with the domain
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the inverse operator
L_{0}^{-1}
is an isomorphism from
H^{s-2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and its square root an isomorphism from
H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, We will use several times the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1

Let
\tau>0
,
f\in L^{\infty}(0,\tau,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, and
u\in L^{\infty}(0,\tau,H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with
\partial^{2}_{t}u\in L^{\infty}(0,\tau,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
such that

\partial_{t}^{2}u=-aL_{0}u+au+f,\qquad(t,x)\;a.e.

(14)

For all
t\in(0,\tau)
, we have then the estimate, for some
C=C(a,b,\tau)>0
independent of
u
and
f
:

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|

\displaystyle L_{0}^{-1/2}(a^{-1}\partial_{t}u(t))\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u(0)\|_{L^{2}}+C\|L_{0}^{-1/2}(a^{-1}\partial_{t}u(0))\|_{L^{2}}+C\int_{0}^{t}\|a^{-1}f(s)\|_{H^{-1}}ds.

Proof
. Consider first the weighted
L^{2}
norm,

\|u\|^{2}_{a}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(x)|^{2}a^{-1}(x)dx,

which is equivalent to the usual
L^{2}
norm since
a>0
is bounded below and above. The calculations below are justified since
u
has the required regularity. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a^{-1}\partial_{t}u,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(a^{-1}\partial_{t}u,L_{0}^{-1}a^{-1}\partial^{2}_{t}u)+(a^{-1}\partial_{t}u,L_{0}^{-1}u)+(a^{-1}\partial_{t}u,L_{0}^{-1}a^{-1}f),

where we used (
14
) to express
u
. With

G(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|u(t)\|^{2}_{a}+\|L_{0}^{-1/2}(a^{-1}\partial_{t}u)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\right),

we find

\frac{dG(t)}{dt}=B(t):=\left(L_{0}^{-1/2}(a^{-1}\partial_{t}u),L_{0}^{-1/2}(a^{-1}f)\right)+\left(L_{0}^{-1/2}a^{-1}\partial_{t}u,L_{0}^{-1/2}u\right).

We have, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle|B(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|L_{0}^{-1/2}(a^{-1}\partial_{t}u)\|_{L^{2}}\big(\|L_{0}^{-1/2}a^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}}+C\|u\|_{L^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CG(t)+CG^{1/2}(t)\|a^{-1}f\|_{H^{-1}},

where we exploited the fact that
L_{0}^{-1/2}
is an isomorphism from
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then,

\frac{dG(t)}{dt}\leq CG(t)+CG^{1/2}(t)\|a^{-1}f(t)\|_{H^{-1}},\qquad t.\;a.e.

which is equivalent to

\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{-Ct}G(t)\right)\leq Ce^{-Ct}G^{1/2}(t)\|a^{-1}f(t)\|_{H^{-1}},\qquad t.\;a.e.

This yields the estimate, for
t\in(0,\tau)
,

G^{1/2}(t)\leq CG^{1/2}(0)+C\int_{0}^{t}\|a^{-1}f(s)\|_{H^{-1}}ds,

which concludes the proof after direct algebra.

Using the previous lemma with
u(t)=u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-}+t)
,
f(t)=a\eta_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-}+t)L_{1}u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-}+t)
, we find the estimate, for
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
,

\displaystyle\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{L^{2}}+C\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{H^{-1}}+C\int_{t_{\varepsilon}^{-}}^{t}\eta_{\varepsilon}(s)\|b\chi\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(s)\|_{L^{2}}ds.

Together with (
13
), it follows, for all
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
,

\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{-1}}\leq C\|u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{L^{2}}+C\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{H^{-1}}+CM_{0,\varepsilon}=:M_{1,\varepsilon}.

(15)

This is our first uniform estimate over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that in order to control
u_{\varepsilon}
and
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, respectively, we need one more derivative for each via the constant
M_{0,\varepsilon}
. We iterate now twice in order to control higher spatial derivatives of
u_{\varepsilon}
and
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
. The method is similar as above.

3.2
Second estimate

Step 1.

We differentiate (
1
) with respect to
x_{j}
,
j=1,\cdots,d
, and introduce
v^{(j)}:=\partial_{x_{j}}u_{\varepsilon}
. We use the shorthand
f_{j}:=\partial_{x_{j}}f
to denote the partial derivative of a function
f
with respect to
x_{j}
. With the previous definitions of
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
at hand, we have

\partial_{t}^{2}v^{(j)}=-aL_{0}v^{(j)}+av^{(j)}+a\eta_{\varepsilon}L_{1}v^{(j)}+L_{2}u_{\varepsilon}+L_{3}u_{\varepsilon},

(16)

where

\displaystyle L_{2}u_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c^{2}+c^{2}\eta_{\varepsilon}\chi)_{j}\Delta u_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle L_{3}u_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\nabla(b+b\eta_{\varepsilon}\chi)_{j}\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}+a_{j}\nabla(b+b\eta_{\varepsilon}\chi)\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}.

With

E^{(j)}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(|\partial_{t}v^{(j)}(t,x)|^{2}+c^{2}(x)|\nabla v^{(j)}(t,x)|^{2}\big)dx,

and

F^{(j)}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}c^{2}(x)\chi(x)|\nabla v^{(j)}(t,x)|^{2}dx,

we obtain from (
16
) the standard energy estimate, for
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{dE^{(j)}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)\frac{dF^{(j)}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{t}v^{(j)},L_{2}u_{\varepsilon}+L_{3}u_{\varepsilon}\big).

(17)

We estimate now the right-hand side. Classical interpolation yields first

\|L_{2}u_{\varepsilon}+L_{3}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon})(\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\Delta u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}).

Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
u_{\varepsilon}
such that

\|\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}c^{2}(x)|\nabla v^{(j)}(t,x)|^{2}dx.

Introducing

E_{1,\varepsilon}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}E^{(j)}_{\varepsilon}(t),\qquad F_{1,\varepsilon}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}F^{(j)}_{\varepsilon},

we then find, together with (
15
) in order to control the
L^{2}
norm of
u_{\varepsilon}
, for
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
,

\|L_{2}u_{\varepsilon}(t)+L_{3}u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon})(M_{1,\varepsilon}+(E_{1,\varepsilon}(t))^{1/2}).

Going back to (
17
), we find for
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
, after using the Young inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{dE_{1,\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)\frac{dF_{1,\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t))E_{1,\varepsilon}+C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t))M_{1,\varepsilon}^{2}.

This provides us with the estimate, for
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
,

E_{1,\varepsilon}(t)+\eta_{\varepsilon,0}F_{1,\varepsilon}(t)\leq C\big(E_{1,\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})+\eta_{\varepsilon,0}F_{1,\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})+M_{1,\varepsilon}^{2}\big),

leading to

F_{1,\varepsilon}(t)\leq C\big(\varepsilon\eta_{0}^{-1}E_{1,\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})+F_{1,\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})+\varepsilon\eta_{0}^{-1}M_{1,\varepsilon}^{2}\big)=:(M_{3,\varepsilon})^{2}.

(18)

Again, the uniform estimate only holds at this point at the location of the perturbation, and it is extended below to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by using Lemma
3.1
.

Step 2.

We have first the following estimate, that is a consequence of (
18
),

\|L_{1}v^{(j)}\|_{H^{-1}}\leq C\|b\chi\nabla v^{(j)}\|_{L^{2}}\leq CM_{3,\varepsilon}.

The next two estimates are straightforward:

\displaystyle\|L_{3}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon})\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon})M_{1,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\|L_{2}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon})\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}.

Using now Lemma
3.1
for (
16
), and summing from
j=1
to
d
, we find for
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
:

\displaystyle\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{t}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{-1}}\leq

\displaystyle\quad C\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{L^{2}}+C\|\partial_{t}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle+C\int_{t_{\varepsilon}^{-}}^{t}(1+\eta_{\varepsilon}(s))(M_{1,\varepsilon}+M_{3,\varepsilon}+\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(s)\|_{L^{2}})ds.

Gronwall’s Lemma then yields the estimate, for
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
:

\displaystyle\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{t}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\big(\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{t}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{H^{-1}}+M_{1,\varepsilon}+M_{3,\varepsilon}\big)

(19)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle M_{4,\varepsilon}.

We have therefore just obtained a uniform bound in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
u_{\varepsilon}
and
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
, respectively. We iterate one last time to control
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is what is needed to prove refocusing. We do not detail some of the calculations since the method is similar to what is done is the previous steps.

3.3
Third estimate

Step 1.

We differentiate (
16
) with respect to
x_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,d
, and denote
v^{(ij)}:=\partial^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}u_{\varepsilon}
. We have

\partial_{t}^{2}v^{(ij)}=-aL_{0}v^{(ij)}+av^{(ij)}+a\eta_{\varepsilon}L_{1}v^{(ij)}+L_{2}v^{(i)}+L_{3}v^{(i)}+L_{2}^{\prime}v^{(j)}+L_{3}^{\prime}v^{(j)}+L_{4}u_{\varepsilon}+L_{5}u_{\varepsilon},

(20)

where

\displaystyle L_{2}^{\prime}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c^{2}+c^{2}\eta_{\varepsilon}\chi)_{i}\Delta u

\displaystyle L_{3}^{\prime}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\nabla(b+b\eta_{\varepsilon}\chi)_{i}\cdot\nabla u+a_{i}\nabla(b+b\eta_{\varepsilon}\chi)\cdot\nabla u

\displaystyle L_{4}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c^{2}+c^{2}\eta_{\varepsilon}\chi)_{ij}\Delta u

\displaystyle L_{5}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{i}\nabla(b+b\eta_{\varepsilon}\chi)_{j}\cdot\nabla u+a\nabla(b+b\eta_{\varepsilon}\chi)_{ij}\cdot\nabla u+a_{ij}\nabla(b+b\eta_{\varepsilon}\chi)\cdot\nabla u.

With

\displaystyle E^{(ij)}_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(|\partial_{t}v^{(ij)}(t,x)|^{2}+c^{2}(x)|\nabla v^{(ij)}(t,x)|^{2}\big)dx

\displaystyle F^{(ij)}_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}c^{2}(x)\chi(x)|\nabla v^{(ij)}(t,x)|^{2}dx,

we obtain from (
20
) the energy estimate

\frac{dE^{(ij)}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)\frac{dF^{(ij)}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}=\left(\partial_{t}v^{(ij)},S_{\varepsilon}\right),

(21)

where

S_{\varepsilon}=L_{2}v^{(i)}+L_{3}v^{(i)}+L_{2}^{\prime}v^{(j)}+L_{3}^{\prime}v^{(j)}+L_{4}u_{\varepsilon}+L_{5}u_{\varepsilon}.

We now estimate
R_{\varepsilon}
. Let

\|D^{3}u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}:=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\partial^{3}_{x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}}u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}dx.

Direct calculations yield

\|S_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon})(\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}+\|D^{3}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}).

(22)

Let moreover

E_{2,\varepsilon}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}E^{(ij)}_{\varepsilon}(t),\qquad F_{2,\varepsilon}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}F^{(ij)}_{\varepsilon}.

For
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
, we then find, combining (
21
) and (
22
), after using the Young inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{dE_{2,\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)\frac{dF_{2,\varepsilon}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t))E_{2,\varepsilon}+C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon}(t))M_{1,\varepsilon}^{2}.

This provides us with the estimate, for
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
,

E_{2,\varepsilon}(t)+\eta_{\varepsilon,0}F_{2,\varepsilon}(t)\leq C\big(E_{2,\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})+\eta_{\varepsilon,0}F_{2,\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})+M_{1,\varepsilon}^{2}\big),

leading to

F_{2,\varepsilon}(t)\leq C\big(\varepsilon\eta_{0}^{-1}E_{2,\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})+F_{2,\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})+\varepsilon\eta_{0}^{-1}M_{1,\varepsilon}^{2}\big)=:(M_{4,\varepsilon})^{2}.

(23)

Step 2.

We extend finally (
23
) to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and simply need for this to estimate
S_{\varepsilon}
in
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We find

\|S_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{-1}}\leq C(1+\eta_{\varepsilon})(\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\Delta u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}).

(24)

Also,

\|L_{1}v^{(ij)}\|_{H^{-1}}\leq C\|b\chi\nabla v^{(ij)}\|_{L^{2}}\leq CM_{4,\varepsilon}.

(25)

Combining (
24
), (
25
) with Lemma
3.1
, and summing over

, we obtain for
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
:

\displaystyle\|\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{t}\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{-1}}\leq

\displaystyle\quad C\|\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{L^{2}}+C\|\partial_{t}\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle+C\int_{t_{\varepsilon}^{-}}^{t}(1+\eta_{\varepsilon}(s))(M_{1,\varepsilon}+M_{4,\varepsilon}+\|\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(s)\|_{L^{2}})ds.

Gronwall’s Lemma then yields the estimate,
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
:

\displaystyle\|\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{t}\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{L^{2}}+

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{H^{-1}}+M_{1,\varepsilon}+M_{4,\varepsilon}\big).

(26)

3.4
Conclusion

We have everything needed now to conclude. Collecting (
15
)-(
19
)-(
26
), we find, for
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{-},t_{\varepsilon}^{+})
,

\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{2}}+\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}\leq C\|u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{H^{3}}+C\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{H^{2}}.

(27)

For

, we have
\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)=0
and perturbation has not occured yet. Since, on the one hand,
u_{\varepsilon}
and
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
are continuous in time along with all of their spatial derivatives, and on the other, (

\|u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{H^{3}}+C\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{-})\|_{H^{2}}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{3}}+C\|u_{1}\|_{H^{2}}.

(28)

In the same way, we have after the perturbation, for any
t\in(t_{\varepsilon}^{+},\tau)
,

\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{2}}+C\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{1}}\leq C_{\tau}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}^{+})\|_{H^{2}}+C_{\tau}\|u_{1}(t_{\varepsilon}^{+})\|_{H^{1}}.

Together with (
27
)-(
28
), this gives, for any
\tau>0
,

\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau,H^{2})}+C\|\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau,H^{1})}\leq C_{\tau}\|u_{0}\|_{H^{3}}+C_{\tau}\|u_{1}\|_{H^{2}}.

This proves estimate (
8
) for smooth
u_{0}
,
u_{1}
. For
u_{0}
,
u_{1}
with the regularity given in (
3
), it suffices to proceed by density and a limiting argument. This concludes the proof of (
8
). We now turn to the remainder term
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
.

Estimates on
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
.

Denote by
w_{\varepsilon}
the last component of the vector
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
. From the definition of
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}
, we verify that is satisfies

\partial_{t}^{2}w_{\varepsilon}=a(x)\nabla\cdot\big(b(x)\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\big)+a(x)\nabla\cdot\big(b(x)\eta_{\varepsilon}(t)\nabla(u_{\varepsilon}-U)\big),\qquad(t,x)\quad\textrm{on}\quad\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

equipped with vanishing initial conditions. We recall that
u_{\varepsilon}
and
U
denote the perturbed and unperturbed solutions, respectively. Given that
u_{\varepsilon}
and
U
belong to
L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, it follows that the above equation admit a unique solution in
L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with second order time derivatives in
L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Applying Lemma
3.1
, we obtain, for

\|w_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|L_{0}^{-1/2}(a^{-1}\partial_{t}w_{\varepsilon}(t))\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\eta_{\varepsilon}(s)\|\nabla(u_{\varepsilon}-U)(s)\|_{L^{2}}ds.

(29)

This last term is equal to

\eta_{0}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{-\varepsilon/2}^{\varepsilon/2}\|\nabla(u_{\varepsilon}-U)(T+s)\|_{L^{2}}ds,

which we now estimate.
Since
u_{\varepsilon}(t)=U(t)
for

\nabla(u_{\varepsilon}-U)(T+s)=\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(T+s)-\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(T+s-\varepsilon)+\nabla U(T+s-\varepsilon)-\nabla U(T+s).

Now, we know that

\tau>0

8

\|\nabla(u_{\varepsilon}-U)(T+s)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon,

(30)

and as a consequence, together with (
29
)

\|w_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|L_{0}^{-1/2}(a^{-1}\partial_{t}w_{\varepsilon}(t))\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon,

(31)

for all
t>0
. This gives an estimate for
w_{\varepsilon}
.

We turn now to the remaining
d
components of
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}

\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}
.
We verify that
\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}
solves

with
\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}(t=0,x)=0
. Combining (
30

), we find, for all

\|\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{H^{-1}}\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\big(\|\nabla w_{\varepsilon}(s)\|_{H^{-1}}+\eta_{\varepsilon}(s)\|\nabla(U-u_{\varepsilon})(s)\|_{H^{-1}}\big)ds\leq C_{t}\varepsilon.

This concludes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

4
Proof of Theorem
2.2

We gather first standard compactness results. Estimate (
8
) leads to the existence of
u\in L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with
\partial_{t}u\in L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, and of a subsequence
\{u_{\varepsilon_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

*

\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon_{j}}\to\partial_{t}u
in
L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
weak-
*
as

. A consequence of such regularity for

is that
u\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

We pass now to the limit in (
1
). Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

\langle a^{-1}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon_{j}},\partial_{t}\varphi\rangle=-\langle u_{\varepsilon_{j}},\nabla\cdot[b(1+\chi\eta_{\varepsilon_{j}}\big)\nabla\varphi]\rangle,

(32)

where

\langle u,v\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(t,x)v(t,x)dtdx.

Let also

\langle u,v\rangle_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\langle u(t,\cdot),v(t,\cdot)\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}dt.

Since
\eta_{\varepsilon_{j}}(t)\to\eta_{0}\delta(t-T)
as

in the sense of measures, and estimate (
8
) implies that
u_{\varepsilon_{j}}\to u
strongly in
C^{0}([0,\tau],L^{2}(\Omega))
for any
\tau>0
and any open bounded set
\Omega
, we deduce from (
32
) and the various convergences of
\{u_{\varepsilon_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
stated above that

\displaystyle\langle a^{-1}\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}\varphi\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\langle u,\nabla\cdot[b\nabla\varphi]\rangle-\eta_{0}(u(T,\cdot),\nabla\cdot[b\chi\nabla\varphi(T,\cdot)])

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\nabla u,b\nabla\varphi\rangle+\eta_{0}(\nabla u(T,\cdot),b\chi\nabla\varphi(T,\cdot)).

Since
a>0

|\langle\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}\varphi\rangle_{0}|\leq C\|u\|_{C^{0}([0,\tau],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\|\varphi\|_{C^{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))},\qquad\forall\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\tau

[0,\tau]
.

The next step is to identify the jump condition at

\varphi_{k}(t,x)=\chi_{k}(t)\psi(x)

\chi_{k}

33
) holds for
\varphi=\varphi_{k}
. The first term in the r.h.s of (

goes to zero as

\chi_{k}(t)\to 0
pointwise for
t\neq T
. The second term in the r.h.s is simply

\eta_{0}(\nabla u(T,\cdot),b\chi\nabla\psi).

The term in the l.h.s of (

) reads

k\int_{T-2/k}^{T-1/k}\langle\partial_{t}u(s,\cdot),\psi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}ds-k\int_{T+1/k}^{T+2/k}\langle\partial_{t}u(s,\cdot),\psi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}ds.

Since
\partial_{t}u\in BV_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, we can take the limit
k\to\infty
above and obtain the expression

\langle a^{-1}\partial_{t}u(T^{-},\cdot),\psi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}-\langle a^{-1}\partial_{t}u(T^{+},\cdot),\psi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}.

Collecting results, we find

\langle a^{-1}\partial_{t}u(T^{-},\cdot),\psi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}-\langle a^{-1}\partial_{t}u(T^{+},\cdot),\psi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}=\eta_{0}(\nabla u(T,\cdot),b\chi\nabla\psi),

which is

\partial_{t}u(T^{+},x)=\partial_{t}u(T^{-},x)+\eta_{0}a(x)\nabla\cdot[b(x)\chi(x)\nabla u(T,x)]\qquad\textrm{in}\quad H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and we have obtained the jump condition.

We now show that the wave equation is satisfied almost everywhere. Taking a test function
\varphi
in (
33
) with time support in
(0,T)
, we find from (
33
), since
\partial_{t}u\in L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{1}))
,

\langle a^{-1}\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}\varphi\rangle=\langle\nabla u,b\nabla\varphi\rangle.

Since
u\in L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, this shows that
\partial^{2}_{t}u\in L^{\infty}(0,T,H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Proceeding in the same way, we find
\partial^{2}_{t}u\in L^{\infty}_{\rm{loc}}(T,+\infty,H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. As a consequence, the equation

\partial_{t}^{2}u=a\nabla\cdot(b\nabla u)

is satisfied almost everywhere in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and in
(T,\mathbb{R}_{+})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We turn finally to the uniqueness of solutions, which is straightforward: by standard uniqueness results for the wave equation, the solution
u
is unique up to any time
t<T
. In particular,
\partial_{t}u
and
\nabla u
are uniquely defined up to
t<T
. Since
\partial_{t}u\in BV_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, it follows that
\partial_{t}u(t,x)
admits a limit in
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as
t\to T^{-}
, and since
\nabla u
is continuous in time with values in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as obtained in the beginning of the proof, this shows that the term

\partial_{t}u(T^{-},x)+\eta_{0}a(x)\nabla\cdot[b(x)\chi(x)\nabla u(T,x)]

is uniquely defined in
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Standard uniqueness for the wave equation for times
t>T
with initial conditions
u(T^{+},x)=u(T,x)
and
\partial_{t}u(T^{+},x)
equal to the expression above yield finally a unique solution for all times
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

As a conclusion, the entire sequence
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}
converges to
u
since (
10
)-(
11
) admits a unique solution. This ends the proof.

5
Appendix

We describe here how the wave equation (
1
) with time-dependent coefficients arises in applications.

Electromagnetics.

In absence of free charges, the three dimensional Maxwell equations read

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}=-\nabla\times\mathbf{E},\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0\\
\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}=\nabla\times\mathbf{H},\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}=0,\end{array}\right.

augmented with the constitutive relations

\mathbf{D}(t,\mathbf{x})=\epsilon(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{E}(t,\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{B}(t,\mathbf{x})=\mu(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{H}(t,\mathbf{x}),

where
\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)
. The coefficients
\varepsilon(t,x)
and
\mu(t,x)
, supposed to be scalars here, are the permittivity and permeability of the underlying isotropic material of propagation, respectively. It is assumed that the dispersive effects of the material are neglected, and therefore that the relationship between
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{E}
, as well as between
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{H}
is local in time. It is explained in
[
6
]
how time-dependent
\varepsilon(t,x)
and
\mu(t,x)
can be engineered in applications.

Consider first the following transverse magnetic case where

\mathbf{E}(t,x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
E_{z}(t,x,y)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{B}(t,x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}B_{x}(t,x,y)\\
B_{y}(t,x,y)\\
0\end{pmatrix}

Maxwell’s equations then reduce to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}B_{x}=-\partial_{y}E_{z}\\
\partial_{t}B_{y}=\partial_{x}E_{z}\\
\partial_{t}\big(\varepsilon E_{z}\big)=\partial_{x}\big(\mu^{-1}B_{y}\big)-\partial_{y}\big(\mu^{-1}B_{x}\big).\end{array}\right.

According to the divergence free condition on
\mathbf{B}
, there exists a
u
such that
B_{x}=\partial_{y}u,
and
B_{y}=-\partial_{y}u
. Setting
E_{z}=\partial_{t}u
, we obtain the wave equation

\partial_{t}\big(\epsilon(t,x)\partial_{t}u\big)=\nabla\cdot(\mu^{-1}(t,x)\nabla u).

In the context of time reversal and ITM, it is shown in
[
2
]
that there is no refocusing when both coefficients
\epsilon(t,x)
and
\mu(t,x)
are time singular. This is because the wave equation does not admit smooth solutions with respect to the time variable in this case, and the quality of refocusing can be quantified in terms of
\partial_{t}u
as we have seen in Theorem
2.1
. Supposing therefore that
\epsilon(t,x)
is independent of time, we recover (
1
).

In the transverse electric case, we have,

\mathbf{B}(t,x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
B_{z}(t,x,y)\end{pmatrix}\qquad\mathbf{D}(t,x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}D_{x}(t,x,y)\\
D_{y}(t,x,y)\\
0\end{pmatrix}

and obtain the wave equation

\partial_{t}\big(\mu(t,x)\partial_{t}u\big)=\nabla\cdot(\epsilon^{-1}(t,x)\nabla u),

where
B_{z}=\partial_{t}u
,
D_{x}=-\partial_{y}u
, and
D_{y}=\partial_{y}u
. We recover now (
1
) when the permeability
\mu
is independent of time.

Elasticity.

In the case of an isotropic material with negligible shear modulus (i.e. a non-rigid material), the Navier-Cauchy equations reduce to

\partial_{t}\big(\rho(t,x)\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\big)=\nabla\big(\lambda(t,x)\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\big),

where
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the displacement field,
\rho
is the mass density, and
\lambda
the first Lamé coefficient. Again, one can find in
[
6
]
examples of mechanical systems with time-dependent
\rho
and
\lambda
.

Set
\mathbf{w}=\rho\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}
. If
\mathbf{w}
is irrotational at
t=0
, then
\mathbf{w}
remains irrotational because of the above equation, and we can write
\mathbf{w}(t,\mathbf{x})=\nabla\phi(t,\mathbf{x})
. With
p=-\lambda\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}
, we obtain the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\mathbf{w}=\nabla p\\
\partial_{t}\big(\lambda^{-1}p\big)=-\nabla\big(\rho^{-1}\mathbf{w}\big).\end{array}\right.

Defining
p=-\partial_{t}\phi
, we find the wave equation

\partial_{t}\big(\lambda^{-1}\partial_{t}\phi\big)=\nabla\big(\rho^{-1}\nabla\phi\big).

We then recover (
1
) when
\lambda^{-1}
does not depend on time.

Fluids.

In the seminal work
[
1
]
, waves at the surface of a water tank are modeled by the following wave equation, neglecting surface tension and therefore dispersive effects,

\partial^{2}_{t}u=c_{0}^{2}(1+\alpha\delta(t-T))\Delta u,\qquad\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Above,
u
is the surface height,
c_{0}
is the (constant) background velocity, and
\alpha\delta(t-T)
represents the action of the ITM at
t=T
. This wave equation can be derived from the Euler system, see e.g.
[
3
]
.

Note that the wave equation (
1
) with time singular coefficients seems more difficult to justify physically in the context of sound waves. Indeed, while it is possible to derive some type of wave equations with time-dependent coefficients for sound waves, see
[
7
]
, these models are justified for slowly varying (in time) coefficients, which is certainly not the case for an ITM.
