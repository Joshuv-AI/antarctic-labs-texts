Diffusion processes as Wasserstein gradient flows via stochastic control of the volatility matrix Thanks: I am grateful to Julio Backhoff-Veraguas and Walter Schachermayer for their useful comments. I also acknowledge support by the Austrian Science Fund (FWF) under grant P35519.
Bertram Tschiderer

Abstract
Abstract. We consider a class of time-homogeneous diffusion processes on ℝ n \mathds{R}^{n} with common invariant measure but varying volatility matrices. In Euclidean space, we show via stochastic control of the diffusion coefficient that the corresponding flow of time-marginal distributions admits an entropic gradient flow formulation in the quadratic Wasserstein space if the volatility matrix of the diffusion is the identity. After equipping ℝ n \mathds{R}^{n} with a Riemannian metric, we prove that the diffusion process can be viewed as a gradient flow in the inherited Wasserstein space if the volatility matrix is the inverse of the underlying metric tensor. In the Euclidean case, our probabilistic result corresponds to the gradient flow characterization of the Fokker–Planck equation, first discovered in a seminal paper by Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto. In the Riemannian setting, the corresponding result on the level of partial differential equations was established by Lisini, building on the metric theory developed by Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré. MSC 2020 subject classifications: Primary 60H30, 60G44; secondary 60J60, 94A17 Keywords and phrases: relative entropy dissipation, gradient flow, diffusion process, stochastic control, Riemannian metric

Introduction

On a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathds{P})
, equipped with filtration
\mathds{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
, we consider a class of time-homogeneous diffusion processes
(X_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
of the form

\textnormal{d}X_{t}^{\Sigma}=\Big(\operatorname{div}\Sigma(X_{t}^{\Sigma})-\Sigma(X_{t}^{\Sigma})\,\nabla V(X_{t}^{\Sigma})\Big)\,\textnormal{d}t+\sqrt{2\Sigma(X_{t}^{\Sigma})}\,\textnormal{d}B_{t},

(1.1)

for
0\leqslant t\leqslant T<\infty
; with initial condition
X_{0}^{\Sigma}=Y
, a square-integrable random vector with continuous probability density function
p_{0}^{\Sigma}\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
, which is independent of the standard
n
-dimensional Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
. We denote by
P_{t}^{\Sigma}\coloneqq(X_{t}^{\Sigma})_{\#}\mathds{P}
the distribution of the random vector
X_{t}^{\Sigma}
and by
p_{t}^{\Sigma}\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
the corresponding probability density function. The functions
V\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathds{R}
and
\Sigma\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{n\times n}
are smooth and sufficiently well-behaved to guarantee the existence of a pathwise unique, strong solution of the stochastic differential equation
(
1.1
)
in
L^{2}(\mathds{P})
, as well as a classical solution of the corresponding Fokker–Planck equation
[
Fri75
,
Gar09
,
Ris96
,
Sch80
]

\partial_{t}p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)=\operatorname{div}\Big(\Sigma(x)\,\big(\nabla\log p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)+\nabla V(x)\big)\,p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)\Big),\qquad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathds{R}^{n},

(1.2)

which is also known as Kolmogorov forward equation
[
Kol31
]
.

The function
\Sigma\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{n\times n}
takes values in the set
S_{++}^{n}
of symmetric and positive definite matrices and we denote by
\sqrt{\Sigma(x)}\in S_{++}^{n}
the unique square root of
\Sigma(x)\in S_{++}^{n}
. The divergence of the matrix-valued function
\Sigma
is the vector field
\operatorname{div}\Sigma\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{n}
with components
(\operatorname{div}\Sigma)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}\Sigma_{ij}
.

The aim of this paper is to study entropy dissipation and gradient flow properties of the diffusion equation
(
1.1
)
. The novelty of our stochastic analysis approach is that it is based on stochastic control of the volatility matrix. Our primary inspiration derives from
[
KST22
]
, where a similar endeavor has been established in the case when the volatility matrix
\Sigma
is equal to the identity matrix.

1.1
Invariant measure

The stationary version

\operatorname{div}\Big(\Sigma(x)\,\big(\nabla\log q(x)+\nabla V(x)\big)\,q(x)\Big)=0,\qquad x\in\mathds{R}^{n}

(1.3)

of the Fokker–Planck equation
(
1.2
)
has a strictly positive solution
q(x)\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}
. We denote by
\mathrm{Q}
the
\sigma
-finite measure on the Borel sets
\mathscr{B}(\mathds{R}^{n})
having the function
q
as density with respect to Lebesgue measure. This measure
\mathrm{Q}
is invariant for the diffusion process
(X_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
. If

\mathrm{Q}(\mathds{R}^{n})=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x<\infty,

(1.4)

the measure
\mathrm{Q}
can be normalized to become a probability measure (the so-called
Gibbs measure
from statistical mechanics). We emphasize that we do not impose the condition
(
1.4
)
and admit the possibility that the total mass
\mathrm{Q}(\mathds{R}^{n})
is infinite. This allows for the important case when the function
V
vanishes identically and
\mathrm{Q}
is equal to Lebesgue measure on
\mathds{R}^{n}
. Instead, we require the weaker integrability condition

\mathds{E}_{\mathrm{Q}}\Big[\big(x\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-|x|^{2}}\big)\Big]=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-|x|^{2}-V(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x<\infty.

(1.5)

If, for example,
V
is non-negative, this condition is automatically satisfied. Furthermore, we stress that no convexity assumptions are imposed on the function
V
.

We remark that, if
\operatorname{div}\Sigma=\Sigma\,\nabla V
, the process
(X_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
is a martingale. In this case, if
n=1
, the invariant measure
\mathrm{Q}
has density
q(x)=\frac{1}{\Sigma(x)}
.

1.2
Relative entropy and free energy

Given the flow of probability measures
(P_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
defined by the diffusion process of
(
1.1
)
, our goal is to study the
relative entropy function

[0,T]\ni t\longmapsto H(P_{t}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}).

(1.6)

The
relative entropy
or
Kullback–Leibler divergence
of
P_{t}^{\Sigma}=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})
with respect to the invariant measure
\mathrm{Q}
is equal to

H(P_{t}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})\big]=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\log\bigg(\frac{p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)}{q(x)}\bigg)\,p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)\,\textnormal{d}x,

(1.7)

where the
likelihood ratio function

(t,x)\mapsto\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)
is given by

\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)\coloneqq\frac{p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)}{q(x)}=p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)\,\mathrm{e}^{V(x)},\qquad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathds{R}^{n}.

(1.8)

The relative entropy
(
1.7
)
can also be interpreted as a
free energy
. Defining the
free energy functional
as the sum of the internal and potential energy functionals via

p\longmapsto\mathscr{F}(p)\coloneqq\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}p(x)\log p(x)\,\textnormal{d}x+\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}V(x)\,p(x)\,\textnormal{d}x,

(1.9)

for probability densities
p(\,\cdot\,)
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
, we have the relation
H(P_{t}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})=\mathscr{F}(p_{t}^{\Sigma})
between entropy and energy.

Since the diffusion process
(X_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
lies in
L^{2}(\mathds{P})
, the corresponding time-marginals
(P_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
are elements of
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, the collection of probability measures on
\mathscr{B}(\mathds{R}^{n})
with finite second moments. Together with the integrability condition
(
1.5
)
, this ensures that the relative entropy
(
1.7
)
with respect to the
\sigma
-finite reference measure
\mathrm{Q}
is well-defined and takes values in the interval
(-\infty,\infty]
; we refer to
[
KST20
, Appendix C]
or
[
Léo14
, Section 3]
for the details.

1.3
Riemannian structure

We fix a function
A\in\mathcal{S}
, which then induces a smooth and symmetric metric tensor

G\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathds{R}^{n\times n}\colon x\longmapsto G(x)\coloneqq A^{-1}(x).

This metric tensor
G
defines a Riemannian inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\cdot\,\rangle_{G}
on
\mathds{R}^{n}
, given by

\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathds{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathds{R}\colon(v,w)\longmapsto\langle v,w\rangle_{G}(x)\coloneqq\langle G(x)v,w\rangle

for
x\in\mathds{R}^{n}
. We denote the induced norm by
|\cdot|_{G}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{G}}
. Equipped with this Riemannian structure, we can regard the pair
(\mathds{R}^{n},G)
as a Riemannian manifold; we refer to
[
Lee18
]
for an introduction to Riemannian manifolds. The metric tensor
G
naturally induces a Riemannian metric
d_{G}
on
(\mathds{R}^{n},G)
defined by

d_{G}(x,y)\coloneqq\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(x,y)}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\Big\langle G\big(\gamma(t)\big)\dot{\gamma}(t)\,,\dot{\gamma}(t)\Big\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}t\bigg\},\qquad x,y\in\mathds{R}^{n},

where
\Gamma(x,y)
denotes the collection of absolutely continuous curves
\gamma\colon[0,1]\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{n}
with starting point
\gamma(0)=x
and endpoint
\gamma(1)=y
. We write
\mathds{R}_{G}^{n}
for the metric space
\mathds{R}^{n}
equipped with this metric
d_{G}
. As a consequence of
(
1.13
)
,
G
satisfies a uniform ellipticity condition of the form

\forall x,\xi\in\mathds{R}^{n}\colon\quad C_{A}^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\leqslant\langle G(x)\xi,\xi\rangle\leqslant c_{A}^{-1}|\xi|^{2}.

(1.10)

Note that
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}_{G}^{n})=\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
on account of
(
1.10
)
. In other words, for a probability measure on
\mathds{R}^{n}
, it is immaterial whether the finite second moment property is tested with respect to the metric
d_{G}
or the Euclidean metric, and by equivalence of norms the topology and the Borel sets remain the same.

1.4
Relative Fisher information

For a probability measure
P\in\mathscr{P}(\mathds{R}^{n})
and vector fields
\bm{v},\bm{w}\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{n}
we define the scalar product

\langle\bm{v},\bm{w}\rangle_{L_{G}^{2}(P)}\coloneqq\mathds{E}_{P}\big[\langle\bm{v},\bm{w}\rangle_{G}\big]=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\langle G\bm{v},\bm{w}\rangle\,\textnormal{d}P

and the induced norm
\|\bm{v}\|_{L_{G}^{2}(P)}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle\bm{v},\bm{v}\rangle_{L_{G}^{2}(P)}}
.

The gradient of a smooth function
f\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathds{R}
on the Riemannian manifold
(\mathds{R}^{n},G)
is given by
\nabla_{G}f\coloneqq A\nabla f
(see, e.g.,
[
Lee18
]
).

In our Riemannian setting, the
relative Fisher information
(see, e.g.,
[
OV00
,
Vil03
]
) of the probability measure
P_{t}^{\Sigma}=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})
with respect to the invariant measure
\mathrm{Q}
is equal to

I_{G}(P_{t}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})\coloneqq\|\nabla_{G}\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}\|_{L_{G}^{2}(P_{t}^{\Sigma})}^{2}=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\big\langle\nabla\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma},A\,\nabla\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}\big\rangle(x)\,p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)\,\textnormal{d}x.

We can also express the relative Fisher information as a
\mathds{P}
-expectation, namely

I_{G}(P_{t}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[|\nabla_{G}\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})|_{G}^{2}\big].

(1.11)

For the classical definition of Fisher information in the information theory literature we refer to the books
[
CL98
,
CT06
]
.

1.5
Wasserstein distance

The quadratic Wasserstein distance (see, e.g.,
[
AGS08
,
Vil03
]
) between two probability measures
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
in
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, with
\mathds{R}^{n}
being equipped with the Riemannian metric
d_{G}
, is defined by

W_{2,G}(P_{1},P_{2})\coloneqq\sqrt{\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P_{1},P_{2})}\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathds{R}^{n}}d_{G}(x,y)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\pi(x,y)}\,,

(1.12)

where
\Pi(P_{1},P_{2})
denotes the set of all couplings
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathds{R}^{n})
between
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, i.e., probability measures
\pi
on
\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathds{R}^{n}
with first marginal
P_{1}
and second marginal
P_{2}
.

1.6
Class of admissible volatilities

Definition 1.1
.

We define the
class of admissible volatilities

\mathcal{S}
as the collection of all matrix-valued functions
\Sigma\colon\mathds{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{n\times n}
such that

(i)

\Sigma(x)
is a symmetric and positive definite matrix, for every
x\in\mathds{R}^{n}
,

(ii)

the component functions
\mathds{R}^{n}\ni x\mapsto\Sigma_{ij}(x)\in\mathds{R}
are smooth, for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

(iii)

for some constants
c,C>0
the uniform ellipticity condition

\forall x,\xi\in\mathds{R}^{n}\colon\quad c|\xi|^{2}\leqslant\langle\Sigma(x)\xi,\xi\rangle\leqslant C|\xi|^{2}

(1.13)

is satisfied,

(iv)

the stochastic differential equation
(
1.1
)
admits a pathwise unique, strong solution
(X_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
, which is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathds{P})
,

(v)

the Fokker–Planck equation
(
1.2
)
has a classical solution
(p_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
,

(vi)

the curve of probability measures
(P_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
given by
P_{t}^{\Sigma}=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})
is absolutely continuous on the quadratic Wasserstein space
(\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n}),W_{2,G})
, i.e., there exists
m\in L^{1}([0,T])
such that

W_{2,G}(P_{t_{0}}^{\Sigma},P_{t}^{\Sigma})\leqslant\int_{t_{0}}^{t}m(u)\,\textnormal{d}u

for all
0\leqslant t_{0}\leqslant t\leqslant T
,

(vii)

and the initial relative entropy
H(P_{0}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})
is finite.

1.7
Main results

Under the assumptions of this section, we can state our first main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}
and
0\leqslant t_{0}\leqslant t\leqslant T
we have

\big|H(P_{t}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})-H(P_{t_{0}}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})\big|\leqslant\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sqrt{I_{G}(P_{u}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})}\ \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{W_{2,G}(P_{u+h}^{\Sigma},P_{u}^{\Sigma})}{|h|}\,\textnormal{d}u,

(1.14)

with equality if the diffusion matrix
\Sigma
is a constant multiple of
A
. In the case
\Sigma=A
we have

H(P_{t_{0}}^{A}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})-H(P_{t}^{A}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}I_{G}(P_{u}^{A}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})\,\textnormal{d}u+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\bigg(\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{W_{2,G}(P_{u+h}^{A},P_{u}^{A})}{|h|}\bigg)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}u.

(1.15)

The message of
Theorem
1.2
is that the “flow of probability measures”
(P_{t}^{A})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
, defined by the stochastic differential equation

\textnormal{d}X_{t}^{A}=\Big(\operatorname{div}A(X_{t}^{A})-A(X_{t}^{A})\,\nabla V(X_{t}^{A})\Big)\,\textnormal{d}t+\sqrt{2A(X_{t}^{A})}\,\textnormal{d}B_{t},

(1.16)

is the
gradient flow
of the
relative entropy functional

\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})\ni P\longmapsto H(P\,|\,\mathrm{Q})

(1.17)

with respect to the quadratic Wasserstein distance
W_{2,G}
. The gradient flow property in this Riemannian setting was established by Lisini in
[
Lis09
]
, where nonlinear diffusion equations with variable coefficients are identified as gradient flows in Wasserstein spaces with respect to a Riemannian metric. The approach of
[
Lis09
]
is based on the theory of minimizing movements and the theory of curves of maximal slope in metric spaces, taking as a reference
[
AGS08
]
. While in
[
Lis09
]
it is crucial to study the Fokker–Planck equation

\partial_{t}p_{t}^{A}(x)=\operatorname{div}\Big(A(x)\,\big(\nabla\log p_{t}^{A}(x)+\nabla V(x)\big)\,p_{t}^{A}(x)\Big),

(1.18)

our approach is based on the corresponding stochastic differential equation
(
1.16
)
via stochastic control of the diffusion coefficient. More precisely, we fix
A\in\mathcal{S}
, and for every
\Sigma
in the class of admissible volatilities
\mathcal{S}
, we vary the diffusion coefficient by considering a stochastic differential equation of the form
(
1.1
)
. After some stochastic analysis and taking expectations this yields the inequality
(
1.14
)
. In the language of analysis in metric spaces, the inequality
(
1.14
)
has the interpretation that the square root of the
relative Fisher information functional

P\longmapsto\sqrt{I_{G}(P\,|\,\mathrm{Q})}=\|\nabla_{G}\log(\tfrac{p}{q})\|_{L_{G}^{2}(P)}=\sqrt{\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}\big\langle\nabla\log(\tfrac{p}{q}),A\,\nabla\log(\tfrac{p}{q})\big\rangle(x)\,p(x)\,\textnormal{d}x}

(1.19)

is a
strong upper gradient
(see
[
AGS08
, Definition 1.2.1]
,
[
Lis09
, Definition 3.2]
) for the relative entropy functional
(
1.17
)
. The domain of these two functionals consists of the collection of absolutely continuous probability measures
P\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
, having a probability density function
p(\,\cdot\,)
with respect to
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. In the case
\Sigma=A
, we obtain the so-called
energy identity

(
1.15
)
(see
[
AGS08
, Remark 1.3.3]
,
[
Lis09
, Definition 3.3]
). This shows that
(P_{t}^{A})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
is a
curve of maximal slope
(see
[
AGS08
, Definition 1.3.2]
,
[
Lis09
, Definition 3.3]
) for the relative entropy functional
(
1.17
)
with respect to its strong upper gradient
(
1.19
)
in the Wasserstein space
(\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n}),W_{2,G})
. According to
[
AGS08
, Theorem 11.1.3]
,
curves of maximal slope coincide with gradient flows
, which verifies the stated gradient flow property of
(P_{t}^{A})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
.

We leave now the Riemannian setting and return to the standard Euclidean structure on
\mathds{R}^{n}
. In other words, we fix the identity matrix
A=I_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
and consider the quadratic Wasserstein distance

W_{2}(P_{1},P_{2})=\sqrt{\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P_{1},P_{2})}\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}\times\mathds{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2}\,\textnormal{d}\pi(x,y)}\,,\qquad P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n}),

(1.20)

together with the relative Fisher information functional

P\longmapsto I(P\,|\,\mathrm{Q})=\|\nabla\log(\tfrac{p}{q})\|_{L^{2}(P)}^{2}=\int_{\mathds{R}^{n}}|\nabla\log(\tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)})|^{2}\,p(x)\,\textnormal{d}x.

As an immediate consequence of
Theorem
1.2
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 1.3
.

For every
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}
and
0\leqslant t_{0}\leqslant t\leqslant T
we have

\big|H(P_{t}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})-H(P_{t_{0}}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})\big|\leqslant\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sqrt{I(P_{u}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})}\ \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{W_{2}(P_{u+h}^{\Sigma},P_{u}^{\Sigma})}{|h|}\,\textnormal{d}u,

with equality if the diffusion matrix
\Sigma
is a constant multiple of the identity matrix. In the case
\Sigma=I_{n}
we have

H(P_{t_{0}}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})-H(P_{t}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}I(P_{u}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})\,\textnormal{d}u+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\bigg(\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{W_{2}(P_{u+h},P_{u})}{|h|}\bigg)^{2}\,\textnormal{d}u.

Here, the probability measures
(P_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
denote the time-marginal distributions of the stochastic differential equation of Langevin–Smoluchowski type

\textnormal{d}X_{t}=-\nabla V(X_{t})\,\textnormal{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\textnormal{d}B_{t}.

(1.21)

The corresponding probability density functions
(p_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
satisfy the Fokker–Planck equation

\partial_{t}p_{t}(x)=\operatorname{div}\Big(\big(\nabla\log p_{t}(x)+\nabla V(x)\big)\,p_{t}(x)\Big).

(1.22)

Clearly the equations
(
1.21
)
and
(
1.22
)
are obtained by taking
A
to be the
n
-dimensional identity matrix in
(
1.16
)
and
(
1.18
)
, respectively. By analogy with the Riemannian setting considered above,
Corollary
1.3
has a gradient flow interpretation in Euclidean space. Namely, it establishes that the flow of probability measures
(P_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
is the gradient flow of the relative entropy functional
(
1.17
)
with respect to the quadratic Wasserstein distance
W_{2}
on the space
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathds{R}^{n})
. The gradient flow property in this context was first established in
[
JKO98
]
.

The special feature of our approach is that it is mainly based on stochastic analysis. In fact, most of
Theorem
1.2
, which is a deterministic result, will be a consequence of a stronger trajectorial result,
Theorem
2.1
. This constitutes the second main result of this paper, it is stated and proved in
Section
2
. Trajectorial approaches of a similar kind were initiated by
[
FJ16
,
KST22
]
. Several important consequences and ramifications of
Theorem
2.1
are developed in
Subsection
2.2
.

In
Section
3
we prove the gradient flow property as stated in
Theorem
1.2
. Most of the proof will be a consequence of
Theorem
2.1
. As a final ingredient, we need to compute the derivative of the Wasserstein distance

f(u)\coloneqq\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{W_{2,G}(P_{u+h}^{\Sigma},P_{u}^{\Sigma})}{|h|},

(1.23)

a quantity appearing in
(
1.14
)
as well as
(
1.15
)
. The derivative
(
1.23
)
is known as a
metric derivative
in
[
AGS08
,
Lis09
]
. By relying on the metric theory of
[
AGS08
,
Lis09
]
we show in
Subsection
3.1
that

f(u)=\sqrt{\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\Big[\big|\big(\Sigma\,G\,\nabla_{G}\log\ell_{u}^{\Sigma}\big)(X_{u}^{\Sigma})\big|_{G}^{2}\Big]}.

Finally, in
Subsection
3.2
we merge our findings and provide the proof of
Theorem
1.2
.

1.8
Literature review

In the seminal paper
[
JKO98
]
, Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto established a new relationship between a particular class of Fokker–Planck equations, the associated free energy functionals, and the quadratic Wasserstein distance. More precisely, they considered the flow of probability densities described by a stochastic differential equation of Langevin–Smoluchowski type as in
(
1.21
)
, for which the drift term is given by the gradient of a potential and the diffusion coefficient is constant. The authors of
[
JKO98
]
constructed a time discrete, iterative variational scheme, whose solutions converge to the solution of the corresponding Fokker–Planck equation
(
1.22
)
. Associated with this Fokker–Planck equation is a free energy functional, modeled as the sum of internal and potential energy as in
(
1.9
)
, and defined on the set of probability densities with finite second moments. The path-breaking result shown in
[
JKO98
]
is that the slope of this free energy functional along the Fokker–Planck probability density flow is the steepest possible, if one chooses the quadratic Wasserstein distance
(
1.20
)
as metric. In other words, this flow of probability densities can be regarded as a “curve of steepest descent”, or a “gradient flux”, for the free energy functional with respect to the Wasserstein distance.

The point of view taken in
[
JKO98
]
was further developed in the paper
[
Ott01
]

2
Dynamics of the relative entropy process

In this section we present and prove our second main result,
Theorem
2.1
. In contrast to
Theorem
1.2
it is not of a deterministic but of a trajectorial nature. Most of
Theorem
1.2
will then follow from
Theorem
2.1
by taking expectations.

Our eventual goal is to study the time evolution of the relative entropy function
(
1.6
)
. We will do this in a trajectorial fashion by modelling the relative entropy function as a stochastic process. This idea goes back to
[
FJ16
]
and was utilized in the context of gradient flows in
[
KST22
]
. In the latter work a stochastic analysis approach to the characterization of diffusions of Langevin–Smoluchowski type
(
1.21
)
as entropic gradient flows in Wasserstein space was developed. The main difference to our current work is that
[
KST22
]
is formulated in the setting of
[
JKO98
]
, where the diffusion coefficient is constant and the underlying metric is Euclidean. Along the lines of
[
Lis09
]
, we allow here for a variable diffusion coefficient as well as a Riemannian geometry (determined by
A\in\mathcal{S}
as in
Subsection
1.3
). As a consequence, while in
[
KST22
]
the drift coefficient
\nabla V
is controlled (or, put another way, perturbed), we vary the diffusion coefficient
\Sigma
in the class of admissible volatilities
\mathcal{S}
. In particular, we provide probabilistic interpretations of the corresponding results in
[
Lis09
]
by considering the associated diffusion process
(
1.16
)
rather than the underlying Fokker–Planck equation
(
1.18
)
. From this perspective, our work can be viewed as a generalization of
[
KST22
]
, in a similar way as
[
Lis09
]
generalizes the setting of
[
JKO98
]
and some of the results in
[
AGS08
]
. Clearly, by choosing the underlying metric to be Euclidean (i.e., by fixing the identity matrix
A=I_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
as a reference), we recover the results of
[
JKO98
,
KST22
]
; recall
Corollary
1.3
in this context. We also remark that the setting of
[
Lis09
]
allows for more general nonlinear partial differential equations, as opposed to the linear case
(
1.18
)
.

We fix
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}
and consider the diffusion process
(X_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
, governed by the stochastic differential equation
(
1.1
)
, with time-marginal distributions
P_{t}^{\Sigma}=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})
. With the above motivation in mind, we consider the likelihood ratio function
(
1.8
)
and its logarithm as stochastic processes. The resulting random objects are the
likelihood ratio process
(or
Radon–Nikodým derivative process
)

\frac{\textnormal{d}P_{t}^{\Sigma}}{\textnormal{d}\mathrm{Q}}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})=\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})=\frac{p_{t}^{\Sigma}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})}{q(X_{t}^{\Sigma})}=p_{t}^{\Sigma}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})\,\mathrm{e}^{V(X_{t}^{\Sigma})},\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T

(2.1)

and the
relative entropy process

\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})=\log\bigg(\frac{p_{t}^{\Sigma}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})}{q(X_{t}^{\Sigma})}\bigg)=\log p_{t}^{\Sigma}(X_{t}^{\Sigma})+V(X_{t}^{\Sigma}),\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant T.

(2.2)

Since taking the
\mathds{P}
-expectation in
(
2.2
)
leads to the relative entropy as in
(
1.7
)
, the idea is to compute the dynamics of the relative entropy process. More precisely, we will determine the stochastic differential of the process
(
2.2
)
under the probability measure
\mathds{P}
. Motivated from results like
[
DPP89
,
Pav89
]
, and most notably the work
[
FJ16
]
by Fontbona and Jourdain, as well as the stochastic approach of
[
KST22
]
, we expect that these dynamics will be most transparent when regarded in the backward direction of time. Thus we consider the time-reversed process

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}^{\Sigma}\coloneqq X_{T-s}^{\Sigma},\qquad 0\leqslant s\leqslant T.

(2.3)

Due to the strong solvability of the stochastic differential equation
(
1.1
)
, we can assume that the filtration
\mathds{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
is given by

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\big(X_{u}^{\Sigma},B_{u}\colon\,0\leqslant u\leqslant t\big)

modulo
\mathds{P}
-augmentation. We define the
backwards filtration

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathds{F}}\overline{\phantom{\mathds{F}}}$}\mathds{F}=(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathcal{F}}\overline{\phantom{\mathcal{F}}}$}\mathcal{F}_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
by

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathcal{F}}\overline{\phantom{\mathcal{F}}}$}\mathcal{F}_{s}\coloneqq\sigma\big(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma},B_{T-u}-B_{T-s}\colon\,0\leqslant u\leqslant s\big)

modulo
\mathds{P}
-augmentation.

The following
Theorem
2.1
describes the semimartingale dynamics of the time-reversed relative entropy process

\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\Sigma}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}^{\Sigma})\coloneqq\log\ell_{T-s}^{\Sigma}(X_{T-s}^{\Sigma}),\qquad 0\leqslant s\leqslant T

(2.4)

in terms of the
cumulative relative Fisher information process

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\Sigma}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{s}\Big\langle\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{\Sigma}\,,\,\Sigma\,\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{\Sigma}\Big\rangle(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma})\,\textnormal{d}u,

(2.5)

for
0\leqslant s\leqslant T
. In particular, we will see that the difference between the processes of
(
2.4
)
and
(
2.5
)
is a martingale. The proof of the theorem below is given in
Subsection
2.1
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}
, fix
A\in\mathcal{S}
and set
G=A^{-1}
. On the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathcal{F}}\overline{\phantom{\mathcal{F}}}$}\mathcal{F}_{T},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathds{F}}\overline{\phantom{\mathds{F}}}$}\mathds{F},\mathds{P})
we have

\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{T}^{\Sigma}\,\big]=\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\Big[\Big\langle\nabla\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}\,,\,\Sigma\,\nabla\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}\Big\rangle(X_{t}^{\Sigma})\Big]\,\textnormal{d}t<\infty

(2.6)

and the process

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{M}\overline{\phantom{M}}$}M_{s}^{\Sigma}\coloneqq\big(\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\Sigma}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}^{\Sigma})-\log\ell_{T}^{\Sigma}(X_{T}^{\Sigma})\big)-\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{\Sigma},\qquad 0\leqslant s\leqslant T

(2.7)

is a martingale bounded in
L^{2}(\mathds{P})
, with representation as a stochastic integral

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{M}\overline{\phantom{M}}$}M_{s}^{\Sigma}=\int_{0}^{s}\Big\langle\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{\Sigma}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma})\,,\sqrt{2\Sigma(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma})}\,\textnormal{d}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{B}\overline{\phantom{B}}$}B_{u}\Big\rangle,

(2.8)

where the process
(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{B}\overline{\phantom{B}}$}B_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
is
\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathds{F}}\overline{\phantom{\mathds{F}}}$}\mathds{F}
-Brownian motion
(
see
Lemma
2.2
)
.

We now consider the important special case
\Sigma=A
. In this situation, the cumulative relative Fisher information process
(
2.5
)
takes the form

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{A}=\int_{0}^{s}\Big\langle\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{A}\,,\,A\,\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{A}\Big\rangle(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma})\,\textnormal{d}u=\int_{0}^{s}|\nabla_{G}\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{A}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{A})|_{G}^{2}\,\textnormal{d}u

and in light of
(
1.11
)
we have

\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{T}^{A}\,\big]=\int_{0}^{T}\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\Big[|\nabla_{G}\log\ell_{t}^{A}(X_{t}^{A})|_{G}^{2}\Big]\,\textnormal{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}I_{G}(P_{t}^{A}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})\,\textnormal{d}t<\infty.

Again, the process

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{M}\overline{\phantom{M}}$}M_{s}^{A}=\big(\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{A}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}^{A})-\log\ell_{T}^{A}(X_{T}^{A})\big)-\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{F}\overline{\phantom{F}}$}F_{s}^{A},\qquad 0\leqslant s\leqslant T

is a martingale bounded in
L^{2}(\mathds{P})
, with representation as a stochastic integral

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{M}\overline{\phantom{M}}$}M_{s}^{A}=\int_{0}^{s}\Big\langle\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{A}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{A})\,,\sqrt{2A(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{A})}\,\textnormal{d}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{B}\overline{\phantom{B}}$}B_{u}\Big\rangle.

The proof of
Theorem
2.1

We place ourselves on the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathcal{F}}\overline{\phantom{\mathcal{F}}}$}\mathcal{F}_{T},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathds{F}}\overline{\phantom{\mathds{F}}}$}\mathds{F},\mathds{P})
. As already pointed out, our goal is to calculate the dynamics of the time-reversed relative entropy process
(
2.4
)
. In order to do this, the first question is how the forward dynamics
(
1.1
)
of the diffusion process
(X_{t}^{\Sigma})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
change when regarded in the backward direction of time. In other words, we need to know the stochastic differential of the time-reversed process
(
2.3
)
. This requires the classical theory of time-reversal; see, e.g.,
[
Föl85
,
Föl86
]
,
[
HP86
]
,
[
Mey94
]
,
[
Nel01
]

[

]
. We summarize the results relevant for us in the following lemma. For its proof we refer to Theorems G.2 and G.5 of Appendix G in
[

Lemma 2.2
.

The stochastic process
(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{B}\overline{\phantom{B}}$}B_{s})_{0\leqslant s\leqslant T}
given by

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{B}\overline{\phantom{B}}$}B_{s}\coloneqq B_{T-s}-B_{T}-\int_{0}^{s}\operatorname{div}\Big(\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{p}\overline{\phantom{p}}$}p_{u}^{\Sigma}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma})\,\sqrt{2\Sigma(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma})}\,\Big)\big(\,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{p}\overline{\phantom{p}}$}p_{u}^{\Sigma}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma})\,\big)^{-1}\,\textnormal{d}u

is a Brownian motion on the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathcal{F}}\overline{\phantom{\mathcal{F}}}$}\mathcal{F}_{T},\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\mathds{F}}\overline{\phantom{\mathds{F}}}$}\mathds{F},\mathds{P})
, and the time-reversed process
(
2.3
)
satisfies the stochastic differential equation

\textnormal{d}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}^{\Sigma}=\big(\operatorname{div}\Sigma-\Sigma\nabla V+2\Sigma\,\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\Sigma}\big)(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}^{\Sigma})\,\textnormal{d}s+\sqrt{2\Sigma(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}^{\Sigma})}\,\textnormal{d}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{B}\overline{\phantom{B}}$}B_{s},

(2.9)

for
0\leqslant s\leqslant T
.

Here, the bar over a deterministic function means that time is reversed, i.e., the time parameter
s\in[0,T]
is replaced by
T-s
. In the lemma above, this conventions means that
\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{p}\overline{\phantom{p}}$}p_{u}^{\Sigma}=p_{T-u}^{\Sigma}
and
\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\Sigma}=\ell_{T-s}^{\Sigma}
.

The following lemma describes the partial differential equation satisfied by the likelihood ratio function
(
1.8
)
. It will be crucial in order to compute the stochastic differential of the time-reversed likelihood ratio process

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\Sigma}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{s}^{\Sigma})=\ell_{T-s}^{\Sigma}(X_{T-s}^{\Sigma}),\qquad 0\leqslant s\leqslant T

(2.10)

and of its logarithm
(
2.4
)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The likelihood ratio function
(
1.8
)
satisfies the partial differential equation

\partial_{t}\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Sigma_{ij}\,\partial_{ij}^{2}\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}+\big\langle\operatorname{div}\Sigma-\Sigma\,\nabla V\,,\nabla\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}\big\rangle.

(2.11)

Reversing time, we derive from
(
2.11
)
the equation

-\partial_{s}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\Sigma}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Sigma_{ij}\,\partial_{ij}^{2}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\Sigma}+\big\langle\operatorname{div}\Sigma-\Sigma\,\nabla V\,,\nabla\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{s}^{\Sigma}\big\rangle.

(2.12)

This is exactly the
Kolmogorov backward equation
corresponding to the stochastic differential equation
(
1.1
)
, a circumstance indicating that the dynamics of the likelihood ratio process
(
2.1
)
are most descriptive under time reversal.

Proof of
Lemma
2.3

Differentiating with respect to the temporal and spatial variables the product

p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)=q(x)\,\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}\,\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}(x),

substituting these derivatives into the Fokker–Planck equation
(
1.2
)
, and using the stationary version
(
1.3
)
of this equation, one obtains the partial differential equation
(
2.11
)
for the likelihood ratio function.
∎

.

\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\log(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{T}^{\Sigma})^{-1}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{T}^{\Sigma})\big]=-H(P_{0}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})\in(-\infty,\infty).

On the other hand, for the initial value we have that

\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\log(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{0}^{\Sigma})^{-1}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{0}^{\Sigma})\big]=-H(P_{T}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})\in[-\infty,\infty).

(2.17)

Thus we can apply
[
KST22
, Proposition A.3]
to the local martingale
(
2.15
)
and the deterministic stopping time
\tau=T
. We conclude that the expectation in
(
2.17
)
is finite and we have the identity

\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\log(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{T}^{\Sigma})^{-1}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{T}^{\Sigma})\big]-\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[\log(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{0}^{\Sigma})^{-1}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{0}^{\Sigma})\big]=-\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\Big\langle\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{\Sigma}\,,\,\Sigma\,\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{\Sigma}\Big\rangle(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma})\,\textnormal{d}u\bigg].

This implies that the local martingale
\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{M}\overline{\phantom{M}}$}M^{\Sigma}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathds{P})
. In fact, by the Itô isometry, the
L^{2}(\mathds{P})
-norm of the stochastic integral

\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{M}\overline{\phantom{M}}$}M_{T}^{\Sigma}=\int_{0}^{T}\Big\langle\nabla\log\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{\ell}\overline{\phantom{\ell}}$}\ell_{u}^{\Sigma}(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma})\,,\sqrt{2\Sigma(\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{X}\overline{\phantom{X}}$}X_{u}^{\Sigma})}\,\textnormal{d}\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{B}\overline{\phantom{B}}$}B_{u}\Big\rangle

is finite and can be computed as

\tfrac{1}{2}\big\|\makebox[0.0pt]{$\phantom{M}\overline{\phantom{M}}$}M_{T}^{\Sigma}\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathds{P})}^{2}=\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\Big\langle\nabla\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}\,,\,\Sigma\,\nabla\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}\Big\rangle(X_{t}^{\Sigma})\,\textnormal{d}t\bigg]=H(P_{0}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})-H(P_{T}^{\Sigma}\,|\,\mathrm{Q}).

Recalling
(
2.5
)
, this shows the integrability condition
(
2.6
)
and finishes the proof of
Theorem
2.1
.
∎

In this section we prove the gradient flow property as formulated in

. In a nutshell, we show that the flow of probability measures
(P_{t}^{A})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
, defined by the stochastic differential equation
(

)

(
1.17
)
with respect to the quadratic Wasserstein distance

as defined in
(

)
.

3.1
Derivative of the Wasserstein distance

We define the vector field

[0,T]\times\mathds{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathds{R}^{n}\colon(t,x)\longmapsto\bm{v}_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)\coloneqq-\Sigma(x)\,\big(\nabla\log p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)+\nabla V(x)\big).

Then we can write the Fokker–Planck equation
(
1.2
)
as a
continuity equation
, to wit

\partial_{t}p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)+\operatorname{div}\big(\bm{v}_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)\,p_{t}^{\Sigma}(x)\big)=0,\qquad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathds{R}^{n}.

The derivative of the Wasserstein distance along an absolutely continuous “curve of probability measures” satisfying a continuity equation is a well known result; see, e.g.
[
AGS08
,
Lis09
,
San15
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}
, fix
A\in\mathcal{S}
and set
G=A^{-1}
. For Lebesgue-a.e.

, we have that

\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{W_{2,G}(P_{t+h}^{\Sigma},P_{t}^{\Sigma})}{|h|}=\|\bm{v}_{t}^{\Sigma}\|_{L_{G}^{2}(P_{t}^{\Sigma})}=\sqrt{\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\Big[\big|\big(\Sigma\,G\,\nabla_{G}\log\ell_{t}^{\Sigma}\big)(X_{t}^{\Sigma})\big|_{G}^{2}\Big]}.

(3.1)

Proof.

For a proof of this result we refer to
[
Lis09
, Theorem 2.4]
, which is an extension of
[
AGS08
, Theorem 8.3.1]
to the current Riemannian setting induced by
G
. Alternatively, the reader may consult
[
San15
, Theorem 5.14]
and adapt the arguments by means of
[
McC01
]
, so that they apply also in our Riemannian setting.
∎

In the case
\Sigma=A
, the derivative
(
3.1
)
takes the form

\lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{W_{2,G}(P_{t+h}^{A},P_{t}^{A})}{|h|}=\|\bm{v}_{t}^{A}\|_{L_{G}^{2}(P_{t}^{A})}=\sqrt{\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}}\big[|\nabla_{G}\log\ell_{t}^{A}(X_{t}^{A})|_{G}^{2}\big]}=\sqrt{I_{G}(P_{t}^{A}\,|\,\mathrm{Q})};

(3.2)

for the last equality in

Proof of

.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}
, fix
