Functional Central Limit Theorem for Two Timescale Stochastic Approximation
Fathima Zarin Faizal, Vivek S. Borkar

Abstract
Two time scale stochastic algorithms emulate singularly perturbed deterministic differential equations in a certain limiting sense, i.e., the interpolated iterates on each time scale approach certain differential equations in the large time limit when viewed on the ‘algorithmic time scale’ defined by the corresponding step sizes viewed as time steps. Their fluctuations around these deterministic limits, after suitable scaling, can be shown to converge to a Gauss-Markov process in law for each time scale. This turns out to be a linear diffusion for the faster iterates and an ordinary differential equation for the slower iterates.



1
Introduction

Stochastic approximation was introduced by
Robbins and Monro 1951
as a method to estimate
x^{*}
satisfying the equation
h(x^{*})=\theta\ (:=
the zero vector) under noisy measurements. Here
h(x)
denotes the expected value at level
x
of the response to a certain experiment and the user has access only to noisy measurements of
h(x)
for a particular level
x
in each experiment. Robbins and Monro provided the following scheme (which they dubbed ‘
stochastic approximation
’) for making successive experiments
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
so that under reasonable conditions,
x_{n}
converges in probability to
x^{*}
:

\displaystyle x(n+1)=x(n)+a(n)\left(h(x_{n})+M_{n+1}\right).

(1)

The dynamical systems approach, a popular method for analyzing stochastic approximation, involves viewing the algorithm as a noisy Euler scheme of a limiting o.d.e. (for ‘
ordinary differential equations
’). Here, the limiting o.d.e. is

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=h(x(t)).

(2)

With some mild assumptions on the step sizes and the noise, it has been shown that the iterates capture the asymptotic behaviour of the limiting o.d.e.
Derevitskii and Fradkov 1974
,
Ljung 1977
,
Meerkov 1972
. See
Benaim 1996
,
Borkar 2022
for a modern treatment. Over the years, this framework has found wide applications in communication networks, artificial intelligence and economic theory due to its incremental and adaptive nature.

In this work, we consider two timescale stochastic approximation, where different parts of the iteration run at different stepsizes. This introduces the notion of different timescales in the algorithm where each timescale corresponds to a specific choice of stepsizes. Some recent applications of this algorithm include distributed optimization
Doan et al. 2018
and reinforcement learning
Borkar and Meyn 2000
,
Konda 2002
.

The classical functional central limit theorem (FCLT), also called Donsker’s theorem, is an extension of the classical Lindeberg-Lévy central limit theorem to function spaces. Donsker’s theorem shows the weak convergence of the laws of suitably interpolated and scaled simple symmetric random walk to the law of a Brownian motion. More generally, functional central theorems provide convergence in law of scaled interpolations of deviations of discrete processes from their deterministic or ‘fluid’ limits, to a Gauss-Markov process. For the problem of interest here, viz., the two time scale stochastic approximation, while convergence guarantees and concentration bounds have been proved, a functional central limit theorem has not been shown. This is the gap we fill in here.

A functional central limit theorem for the single timescale stochastic approximation can be found in
Borkar 2022
, Chapter 7. It shows that the distribution of the interpolated deviations of the iterates from the o.d.e. it tracks, when scaled by
\sqrt{a(n)}
where
a(n)
is the step size at time step
n
, converges to a zero mean Gauss-Markov process. A straightforward extension of this result to the two timescale case would be an analogous result for each timescale as in the single timescale case, viz., that the distribution of the interpolated deviations of the iterates from the o.d.e. of each timescale, when scaled by the square-root of the step size for that particular timescale, converges to a zero mean Gaussian process. In our results, we show that the correct formulation is: for each timescale, when the deviations of the iterates from the limiting o.d.e. are scaled by the square-root of the step size of the
faster
timescale, we achieve convergence in distribution to a zero mean Gaussian diffusion.

The main novelty in our work is in characterizing an iterative equation for the fluctuations of the faster timescale. The other main challenge is in dealing with the additional fluctuation arising from the dependence of the iterates in the slower timescale on the iterates in the faster timescale. To control this deviation, a scaling by the square-root of the faster step size for both timescales was found to be required in our analysis and resulted in a functional central limit theorem that substantially differs from the aforementioned straightforward extension one might expect for the slower timescale (Theorem
3
below). Finally, we also derive a central limit theorem from the FCLT proved in Theorem
9
.

1.1
Prior work

After the Robbins-Monro algorithm was introduced in
Robbins and Monro 1951
, similar and more powerful results have been proven for this class of iterative algorithms such as almost sure convergence, conditions for convergence, convergence rates and so on
Benaim 1996
,
Borkar 2022
.

Several other limit theorems have also been proved for single timescale stochastic approximation. Rate of convergence for moments has been shown in
Gerencsér 1992
. An almost sure central limit theorem and a law of iterated logarithms were proved in
Pelletier 1999
and
Pelletier 1998
respectively. A functional central limit theorem for stochastic approximation with martingale noise can be found in Chapter 7 of
Borkar 2022
. Another functional central limit theorem for stochastic approximation with Markovian noise was proved in
Borkar et al. 2021
. (These references are only representative, see
Duflo 2013
for an excellent overview of the classical work on stochastic approximation.) For FCLT for single time scale, see
Lai and Robbins 1978
,
Solo 1982
,
Benveniste et al. 1990
,
Pezeshki-Esfahani and Heunis 1997
,
Borkar et al. 2021
.

Several analogous results exist in the literature for two timescale stochastic approximation. Almost sure convergence was proved in
Borkar 1997
. Concentration bounds for two timescale stochastic approximation were proved in
Borkar and Pattathil 2018
. Sufficient conditions for the stability of two timescale stochastic approximation were proved in
Lakshminarayanan and Bhatnagar 2017
. A central limit theorem for two timescale stochastic approximation when the iterations are linear was proved in
Konda and Tsitsiklis 2004
,
Basak and Dasgupta 2020
. A central limit theorem for suitably scaled iterates of two timescale stochastic approximation was proved in
Mokkadem and Pelletier 2006
. See
Li et al. 2023
for a recent work on FCLT for Q-learning with Polyak-Ruppert averaging.

1.2
Problem description

We consider the two timescale stochastic approximation scheme involving martingale and Markov noise. Precisely, we consider the following iterative equations for
\{x_{k}\}
,
\{y_{k}\}
where
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
,
h:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},g:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
and
k\geq 0
:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}+a(k)\left(h(x_{k},y_{k},Y_{k})+M_{k+1}^{f}\right),

(3)

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=y_{k}+b(k)\left(g(x_{k},y_{k},Y_{k})+M_{k+1}^{s}\right),

(4)

such that

\displaystyle\sum_{n}a(n)=\sum_{n}b(n)=\infty,\ \sum_{n}(a(n)^{2}+b(n)^{2})<\infty,\ \frac{b(n)}{a(n)}\rightarrow 0.

(5)

Here,
\{Y_{k}\}
is the so called Markov noise taking values in a finite space
S
and controlled by
\{x_{n},y_{n}\}
(see Assumption
(A12)
for the definition). Let
\pi_{x,y}
be the unique stationary distribution of an irreducible Markov chain on
S
with transition probability
p_{x,y}(\cdot|\cdot)
that governs the evolution of
\{Y_{n}\}
as described in Assumption
(A12)
.

The last condition in (
5
) essentially puts
\{x_{k}\}
on a much faster timescale compared to
\{y_{k}\}
. This also implies that there are essentially three timescales at play here. The process
\{Y_{n}\}
runs on the natural timescale, i.e., the clock indexed by
n=0,1,2,\cdots
. On the other hand, both iterates above run on slower timescales defined by time increments
a(n),b(n),n\geq 0,
resp.

The equations (
3
) and (
4
) can be thought of as a noisy Euler scheme for the coupled (‘singularly perturbed’) o.d.e.s

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{i}\pi_{x(t),y(t)}(i)h(x(t),y(t),i)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\overline{h}(x(t),y(t)),

\displaystyle\dot{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\pi_{x(t),y(t)}(i)g(x(t),y(t),i)=\overline{g}(x(t),y(t)),

where
0<\epsilon\downarrow 0
. Note that the averaging over
\{Y_{n}\}
which moves on a faster time scale can be seen here in the averaging of the right hand side w.r.t. its stationary distribution parametrized by
(x,y)=(x(t),y(t))
at time
t
. Assume that for a fixed
y
, the o.d.e.

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{i}\pi_{x(t),y}(i)h(x(t),y,i),

(6)

has a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium
\lambda(y)
for a Lipschitz
\lambda(\cdot)
, and the o.d.e.

\displaystyle\dot{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\pi_{\lambda(y(t)),y(t)}(i)g(\lambda(y(t)),y(t),i)

(7)

has a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium
y^{*}
. Since
\epsilon
does not affect the trajectories of
x(\cdot)
and affects only the speed with which they are traversed,
\lambda(\cdot)
is independent of
\epsilon
. If
\epsilon
is sufficiently small, then
y(\cdot)
can be considered to be quasi-static relative to
x(\cdot)
. Thus
x(t)
essentially tracks
\lambda(y(t))
from (
6
) and
y(t)
approximately tracks the solution of the o.d.e. (
7
). We thus expect
(x(t),y(t))
to approximately converge to
(\lambda(y^{*}),y^{*})
and by extension,
(x_{n},y_{n})
to converge almost surely to
(\lambda(y^{*}),y^{*})
. This is indeed so, refer to
Borkar 2022
, Chapter 8, for details. Note, however, that throughout the rest of the paper, we do not assume that (
7
) has a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium point.

Our aim is to prove a functional central limit theorem for both iterates along the lines of the functional central limit theorem for single timescale stochastic approximation, which will be discussed in more detail in section
1.5
.

1.3
Technical assumptions

We make the following assumptions to prove Theorem
3
:

(A1)

\{a(k)\},\{b(k)\}\subset(0,1)
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{k}a(k)=\sum_{k}b(k)

\displaystyle=\infty,

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\left(a(k)^{2}+b(k)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle<\infty,

\displaystyle\frac{b(k)}{a(k)^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\overset{n\uparrow\infty}{\rightarrow}0.

Also,
0<b(k)\leq a(k)^{3/2}<a(k)<1\,\forall\,k
.

(A2)

\varphi:=\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}(\frac{1}{a(n+1)}-\frac{1}{a(n)})
exists and equals 0. The case when
\varphi\neq 0
has been handled separately in Section
3
. An example of such a sequence would be
a(n)=\frac{C}{(n+1)^{c}},c\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)
. As mentioned in
Borkar 2022
, Chapter 7, some consequences of this assumption are:

\displaystyle\underset{n\uparrow\infty}{\lim}\ \frac{a(n)}{a(n+1)}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\underset{n\uparrow\infty}{\lim}\ \frac{\left(a(n)-a(n+1)\right)^{2}}{a(n)a(n+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=0.

We also assume that
\vartheta:=\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}(\frac{1}{b(n+1)}-\frac{1}{b(n)})
exists.

(A3)

\displaystyle\underset{n\uparrow\infty}{\lim}\ \frac{a(n)}{b(n)}\left(\frac{1}{a(n+1)}-\frac{1}{a(n)}\right)=0.

(A4)

The sequence
a(n)
decreases monotonically to 0.

(A5)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
T>0
, let
m^{f-}(n)=\max\left\{m\leq n:t^{f}(m)\leq t^{f}(n)-T\right\}
, where
t^{f}(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a(i)
. Then for any
n
,

\displaystyle\underset{T\uparrow\infty}{\lim}\frac{a(m^{f-}(n,T))}{a(n)e^{T}}<\zeta(T)\rightarrow 0,

where
\zeta(T)
is independent of
n
and is a decreasing function of
T
. In the above, we added a second argument to
m^{f-}(\cdot)
to highlight its dependence on
T
.

(A6)

\sup_{n}\|x_{n}\|<\infty
a.s.,
\sup_{n}E\left[\|x_{n}\|^{4}\right]<\infty
. Also,
\sup_{n}\|y_{n}\|<\infty
a.s.,
\sup_{n}E\left[\|y_{n}\|^{4}\right]<\infty
. Such stability assumptions are common in the stochastic approximation literature to prove convergence and limit theorems. One can consult Chapter 4 of
Borkar 2022
for examples of stability criteria.

(A7)

\{M_{k+1}^{i}\},i\in\{f,s\}
are martingale difference sequences with respect to the increasing
\sigma
-fields

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(x_{m},y_{m},Y_{m},M_{m}^{i};i\in\{f,s\};m\leq n),n\geq 0.

That is,
\mathbb{E}\left[M^{i}_{n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=0
a.s. for
i\in\{f,s\}
and
n\geq 0
.

(A8)

\{M_{k+1}^{i}\},i\in\{f,s\}
also satisfy:

\displaystyle E\left[M_{n+1}^{f}{M_{n+1}^{f}}^{\mathrm{T}}|M_{k}^{f},M_{k}^{s},x_{k},y_{k},Y_{k},k\leq n\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{f}(x_{n},y_{n}),

\displaystyle E\left[M_{n+1}^{s}{M_{n+1}^{s}}^{\mathrm{T}}|M_{k}^{s},M_{k}^{f},x_{k},y_{k},Y_{k},k\leq n\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{s}(x_{n},y_{n}),

\displaystyle E\left[\|M_{n+1}^{f}\|^{4}|M_{k}^{f},M_{k}^{s},x_{k},y_{k},Y_{k},k\leq n\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K^{\prime}\left(1+\|x_{n}\|^{4}+\|y_{n}\|^{4}\right),

\displaystyle E\left[\|M_{n+1}^{s}\|^{4}|M_{k}^{f},M_{K}^{s},x_{k},y_{k},Y_{k},k\leq n\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K^{\prime}\left(1+\|x_{n}\|^{4}+\|y_{n}\|^{4}\right),

where
K^{\prime}>0
is a suitable constant and
Q_{f}^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}}
,

that the least eigenvalues of

are bounded away from zero
uniformly in its arguments. In particular, the last assumption combined with
(A6)
gives you that the fourth moments of the martingale noise are bounded.

(A9)

The functions

and

are uniformly Lipschitz in each of their first two arguments for each value of

, i.e., for some

and
\forall\ i\in S,x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}},

\displaystyle\|h(x,y,i)-h(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},i)\|

\displaystyle\leq L(\|x-x^{\prime}\|+\|y-y^{\prime}\|),

\displaystyle\|g(x,y,i)-g(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},i)\|

\displaystyle\leq L(\|x-x^{\prime}\|+\|y-y^{\prime}\|).

We also assume that
\overline{h}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
\overline{g}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
are continuously differentiable and their Jacobian matrices
\nabla_{x}\overline{h},\nabla_{y}\overline{h},\nabla_{x}\overline{g},\nabla_{y}\overline{g}
respectively are uniformly bounded and uniformly Lipschitz continuous. Moreover, the functions
h(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and
g(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
are uniformly bounded.

(A10)

The o.d.e. (
6
) has a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium
\lambda(y)
where
\lambda(\cdot)
is continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives.

(A11)

For any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
, the solution of the o.d.e. (
6
) satisfies
\left\|x(t)-\lambda(y)\right\|\leq ce^{-dt}\|x(0)-\lambda(y)\|
, for some

(i.e., the equilibrium

is exponentially stable).

(A12)

\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is such that for
i\in S
and
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}=i|Y_{m},x_{m},y_{m},m\leq n)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}=i|Y_{n},x_{n},y_{n}),

\displaystyle=p_{x_{n},y_{n}}(i|Y_{n}),

where for each fixed

,
p_{x,y}(\cdot|\cdot)
is the transition probability of an irreducible Markov chain on the finite state space
S
with the unique stationary distribution
\pi_{x,y}
. Furthermore, the maps
(x,y)\mapsto p_{x,y}(\cdot|\cdot)
are continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives.

(A13)

The Markov noise
Y_{n}
and the martingale noises

are conditionally independent of each other and the past, i.e.,
\{x_{0},y_{0},Y_{k},M^{i}_{k+1},i=1,2;k\geq 0\}
, given the current iterates
(x_{n},y_{n})
.
Furthermore, the conditional covariance matrices of the martingale noises given the current iterates are positive definite and Lipschitz.

1.4
Notation

In this section, we define the various terms required to state our main result. We first define the terms required to state the functional central limit theorem for single timescale stochastic approximation shown in Chapter 7 of
Borkar 2022
. We begin with some background. An o.d.e. is said to be well-posed if there exists a unique solution for every initial condition that is continuous with respect to the latter. Recall that an invariant set
A
for a well-posed o.d.e. is said to be internally chain transitive if given any
x,y\in A
and
\epsilon,T>0
, we can find an
n\geq 2
and points
x_{0}=x,x_{1},\cdots x_{n-1},x_{n}=y,
such that there exist trejectories
\breve{x}_{i}(\cdot)
of the o.d.e. for
0\leq i<n,
satisfying:
\breve{x}_{i}(0)
is in the
\epsilon
-neghborhood of
x_{i}
and
\breve{x}_{i}(t)
is in the
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
x_{i+1}
for some
t\geq T
.

The broad idea behind the o.d.e. approach is to construct an interpolated trajectory on the algorithmic timescale from the iterates and show that it asymptotically approaches the solution set of the limiting o.d.e. The following theorem, which builds upon a classic result of Benaim
Benaim 1996
in the single time scale case, characterizes the asymptotic behaviour of
\{x_{n}\},\{y_{n}\}
:

Theorem 1

(
Borkar 2022
, Theorem 8.1)
.

Almost surely, the sequences
\{x_{n}\},\{y_{n}\}
generated by (
3
), (
4
) respectively converge to
\{(\lambda(y),y):y\in\chi\}
, where
\chi
is a (possibly sample path dependent) connected internally chain transitive invariant set of (
7
).

Consider the iterative equation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by (
1
). Define the algorithmic timescale

\displaystyle t(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}a(m),\ n\geq 1;\ t(0)=0.

Note that this timescale runs slower than the normal timescale as
1>a(n)>0,a(n)\to 0
. Fix a value of
T>0
. Let
m(n):=\min\{m\geq n:t(m)\geq t(n)+T\},n\geq 1
. We also define
x^{n}(t),t\geq t(n)
, to be the unique solution of (
2
) starting at
t(n)
:

\displaystyle\dot{x}^{n}(t)=h(x^{n}(t)),t\geq t(n);\ x^{n}(t(n))=x_{n}.

In
Borkar 2022
, the functional central limit theorem for the single timescale case is stated for linear interpolations of the scaled fluctuations of the iterates from the limiting o.d.e. For a particular
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the scaled fluctuations are defined as: for
j\geq n
,

\displaystyle z_{j}^{n}:=\frac{x_{j}-x^{n}(t(j))}{\sqrt{a(j)}}.

These are then linearly interpolated over the algorithmic timescale to form the piecewise linear function
z^{n}(t),t\in[t(n),t(n)+T]
. Specifically,
z^{n}(t(j))=z_{j}^{n}
and
z^{n}(t)
is a linear interpolation of
z_{j}^{n}
and
z_{j+1}^{n}
for
t\in[t(j),t(j+1)]
and
n\leq j<m(n)
. For ease of notation, we define
\tilde{z}^{n}(t)=z^{n}(t(n)+t)
.

Analogously, to study the functional central limit theorem for the two timescale case, we define the two ‘algorithmic timescales’

\displaystyle t^{f}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}a(m),\ n\geq 1;\ t^{f}(0)=0,

\displaystyle t^{s}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}b(m),\ n\geq 1;\ t^{s}(0)=0,

with

\displaystyle m^{f}(n)

\displaystyle:=\min\{m\geq n:t^{f}(m)\geq t^{f}(n)+T\},n\geq 1,

\displaystyle m^{s}(n)

\displaystyle:=\min\{m\geq n:t^{s}(m)\geq t^{s}(n)+T\},n\geq 1.

Clearly,
t^{i}(m^{i}(n))\in\left[t^{i}(n)+T,t^{i}(n)+T+1\right],\ i\in\{f,s\}
. Define
\overline{f}(y)=\overline{g}(\lambda(y),y)
. Let
y^{n}(t)
be the unique solution of the differential equation

\displaystyle\dot{y}^{n}(t)=\overline{f}(y^{n}(t)),t\geq t^{s}(n);\ y^{n}(t^{s}(n))=y_{n}.

The fluctuations that we consider for the functional central limit theorem are the following. For
n\geq 1
, let

\displaystyle\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=\lambda(y_{n})-x_{n}=v_{n}-x_{n},

\displaystyle u_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{j}}{\sqrt{a(j)}},

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}^{n}

\displaystyle=y_{j}-y^{n}(t^{s}(j)),

\displaystyle w_{j}^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{j}^{n}}{\sqrt{a(j)}}.

We then construct linear interpolations of these fluctuations over an interval of length
\approx T
as before. The piecewise linear function
u^{n}(t),t\in[t^{f}(n),t^{f}(n)+T]
is such that
u^{n}(t^{f}(j))=u_{j}
and is linearly interpolated on the intervals
[t^{f}(j),t^{f}(j+1)],n\leq j\leq m^{f}(n)
. Similarly, the piecewise linear function
w^{n}(t),t\in[t^{s}(n),t^{s}(n)+T]
is such that
w^{n}(t^{s}(j))=w_{j}
and is linearly interpolated on the intervals
[t^{s}(j),t^{s}(j+1)],n\leq j\leq m^{s}(n)
. For ease of notation, we also define
\tilde{u}^{n}(t)=u^{n}(t^{f}(n)+t),t\in[0,T]
,
\tilde{y}^{n}(t)=y^{n}(t^{s}(n)+t)
and
\tilde{w}^{n}(t)=w^{n}(t^{s}(n)+t),t\in[0,T]
. Also denote by
\overline{y}^{n}(t),t\in[0,T]
the linearly interpolated version of
\{y_{n}\}
on the slow algorithmic timescale, i.e., for
n\leq j\leq m^{s}(n)
,
\overline{y}^{n}(t^{s}(j)-t^{s}(n))=y_{j}
and linearly interpolated otherwise.

1.5
Main results

We first reproduce the functional central limit theorem for single timescale stochastic approximation from
Borkar 2022
and
Borkar et al. 2021
for comparison.

Theorem 2

(Theorem 7.1,
Borkar 2022
)
.

Consider the single timescale stochastic approximation iteration as in (
1
). The limit points in law
(z^{*}(\cdot),x^{*}(\cdot))
of
\{\tilde{z}^{n}(\cdot),\tilde{x}^{n}(\cdot)\}
are such that
x^{*}(\cdot)
is a solution of the o.d.e.
\dot{x}(t)=h(x(t))
belonging to an internally chain transitive invariant set thereof, and

In the first part of the proof, we show that the laws of the processes

Then the laws of

Tightness of the laws of

\displaystyle P\left(\left\|y^{n}(0)\right\|>a\right)\leq\frac{E\left[\left\|y^{n}(0)\right\|^{4}\right]}{a^{4}}\leq\frac{K_{3}}{a^{4}}.

\{E\left[\left\|u_{n}\right\|^{4}\right]\}_{n\geq 1}
is a bounded sequence.

2.3.2
Proof that
\left\{E\left[\left\|u_{n}\right\|^{4}\right]\right\}_{n\geq 1}
is a bounded sequence.

Let
x^{n}(t),t\geq t^{f}(n),
be the unique solution of
\dot{x}(t)=h(x(t),y_{n})
starting at
t^{f}(n)
:

\displaystyle\dot{x}^{n}(t)=\overline{h}(x^{n}(t),y_{n}),t\geq t(n);x^{n}(t(n))=x_{n}.

Define
\gamma^{n}_{j}=\frac{x_{j}-x^{n}(t^{f}(j))}{\sqrt{a(j)}},n\leq j\leq m^{f}(n)
. The following lemma shows the boundedness of the fourth moments thereof, the proof of which can be found in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 5
.

There exists a constant
\kappa
such that for each
n
,

\displaystyle\underset{n\leq k\leq m^{f}(n)}{\sup}E\left[\|\gamma^{n}_{k}\|^{4}\right]<\kappa<\infty.

Now, to show that the fourth moments of
\{u_{n}\}
are bounded using the above result, we have the following equation:

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}^{m^{f-}(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{n}-x^{m^{f-}(n)}(t^{f}(n))}{\sqrt{a(n)}}

\displaystyle=u_{n}+\frac{\lambda(y_{n})-x^{m^{f-}(n)}(t^{f}(n))}{\sqrt{a(n)}}.

By rearranging the above equation, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|\gamma_{n}^{m^{f-}(n)}-u_{n}\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|\lambda(y_{n})-x^{m^{f-}(n)}(t^{f}(n))\right\|}{\sqrt{a(n)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\|\lambda(y_{n})-\lambda(y_{m^{f-}(n)})\right\|+\left\|\lambda(y_{m^{f-}(n)})-x^{m^{f-}(n)}(t^{f}(n))\right\|}{\sqrt{a(n)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\|\lambda(y_{n})-\lambda(y_{m^{f-}(n)})\right\|}{\sqrt{a(n)}}+ce^{-dT}\frac{\left\|\lambda(y_{m^{f-}(n)})-x_{m^{f-}(n)}\right\|}{\sqrt{a(n)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{a(m^{f-}(n))}{a(n)}}\frac{\left\|\lambda(y_{n})-\lambda(y_{m^{f-}(n)})\right\|}{\sqrt{a(m^{f-}(n))}}+ce^{-dT}\sqrt{\frac{a(m^{f-}(n))}{a(n)}}u_{m^{f-}(n)},

(24)

where the penultimate step follows from
(A11)
. The fourth moment of the first term can be shown to converge to 0 uniformly in
T
as
n\uparrow\infty
as follows:

\displaystyle E\left[\left(\sqrt{\frac{a(m^{f-}(n))}{a(n)}}\frac{\left\|\lambda(y_{n})-\lambda(y_{m^{f-}(n)})\right\|}{\sqrt{a(m^{f-}(n))}}\right)^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq K_{8}\ E\left[\left(\frac{\sum^{n-1}_{k=m^{f-}(n)}\frac{b(k)}{a(k)}a(k)\left(\left\|g(x_{k},y_{k},Y_{k})\right\|+\left\|M^{s}(k+1)\right\|\right)}{\sqrt{a(m^{f-}(n))}}\right)^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq K_{9}\ T^{3}\ E\left[\frac{\sum^{n-1}_{k=m^{f-}(n)}\left(\frac{b(k)}{a(k)}\right)^{4}a(k)\left(\left\|g(x_{k},y_{k},Y_{k})\right\|^{4}+\left\|M^{s}(k+1)\right\|^{4}\right)}{a(m^{f-}(n))^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq K_{10}\ T^{3}\underset{m^{f-}(n)\leq k\leq n-1}{\sup}\left(\frac{b(k)}{a(k)}\right)^{4}E\left[\frac{\sum^{n-1}_{k=m^{f-}(n)}a(k)}{a(m^{f-}(n))^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K_{11}\ T^{4}}{a(m^{f-}(n))^{2}}\underset{m^{f-}(n)\leq k\leq n-1}{\sup}\left(\frac{b(k)}{a(k)}\right)^{4}

\displaystyle\leq K_{12}\ T^{4}\underset{m^{f-}(n)\leq k\leq n-1}{\sup}\left(\frac{b(k)}{a(k)^{3/2}}\right)^{4}\overset{n\uparrow\infty}{\rightarrow}0,

(25)

where the first step follows from the Lipschitz property of
\lambda(\cdot)
and
(A5)
, the second step follows from using the convexity of the map
x\mapsto x^{4}
map and that
(a+b)^{4}\leq 8(a^{4}+b^{4})
, the third step follows from the boundedness of
g(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and the fourth moment of
M^{s}(\cdot)
, the fifth step follows from
a(n)
decreasing to zero monotonically, and the last step follows from
b(n)=o(a(n)^{3/2})
. It follows that for some
K_{13}>0
and for a large enough
n
,

\displaystyle\underset{n\leq k\leq m^{f}(n)}{\max}E\left[\left(\frac{\left\|\lambda(y_{k})-\lambda(y_{m^{f-}(k)})\right\|}{\sqrt{a(k)}}\right)^{4}\right]<K_{\ref{const: firsttsCLTyerrorub}}.

The final step is to show that
\{E[\|u_{n}\|^{4}]\}_{n\geq 1}
is bounded.

From (
24
), we have that

\displaystyle\|u_{n}\|\leq\|\gamma_{n}^{m^{f-}(n)}\|+\frac{\left\|\lambda(y_{n})-\lambda(y_{m^{f-}(n)})\right\|}{\sqrt{a(n)}}+ce^{-dT}\sqrt{\frac{a(m^{f-}(n))}{a(n)}}\left\|u_{m^{f-}(n)}\right\|.

From
(A5)
, it also follows that we can choose a
T
large enough so that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle ce^{-dT}\sqrt{\frac{a(k)}{a(m^{f}(k))}}<\zeta(T)<\frac{1}{2^{5/4}},

where
\zeta(T)
is a decreasing function of
T
. Let
b^{u}_{n}:=\underset{n\leq k\leq m^{f}(n)}{\max}E\left[\|u_{k}\|^{4}\right]
. Combining the above results, boundedness of the fourth moment of
E[\|\gamma_{k}^{m^{f-}(k)}\|^{4}]
from Lemma
5
, and the fact that
(a+b+c)^{4}\leq 16(a^{4}+b^{4}+c^{4})
, we have that

\displaystyle b^{u}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq 16\left(\underset{n\leq k\leq m^{f}(n)}{\max}E[\|\gamma_{k}^{m^{f-}(k)}\|^{4}]+K_{\ref{const: firsttsCLTyerrorub}}+\zeta(T)^{4}\underset{m^{f-}(n)\leq k\leq n}{\max}E\left[\left\|u_{k}\right\|^{4}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq K_{14}+16\zeta(T)^{4}b^{u}_{m^{f-}(n)}\leq K_{15}+\frac{1}{2}b^{u}_{m^{f-}(n)}.

It follows that
\{E[\|u_{n}\|^{4}]\}_{n\geq 1}
is bounded.

2.3.3
Proving tightness for

The following lemma coupled with Lemma

Lemma 6
.

For
0\leq n\leq k<\ell\leq m^{f}(n)

\displaystyle E\left[\left\|\nu_{\ell}-\nu_{k}\right\|^{4}\right]=O\left(\left(t^{f}(\ell)-t^{f}(k)\right)^{2}\right).

Proof.

Recall that for
0\leq i\leq m^{f}(n)-n
,

\displaystyle\nu_{n+i}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=n}^{n+i-1}\sqrt{a(j)}\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{n+i-1}\left(I+a(k)\nabla_{x}\overline{h}(x_{k},y_{k})\right)\sqrt{\frac{a(k)}{a(k+1)}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\Biggl(e^{f,T}_{j}+\epsilon_{j}\nabla\lambda(y_{j})\overline{g}(x_{j},y_{j})-M^{f}_{j+1}+\epsilon_{j}\nabla\lambda(y_{j})M^{s}_{j+1}+\frac{e_{j+1}}{a(j)}

\displaystyle\hskip 140.00021pt+\epsilon_{j}\nabla\lambda(y_{j})\Delta^{s}_{j}(x_{j})-\Delta^{f}_{j}(y_{j})\Biggr)\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}.

(26)

By
(A2)
,
\sqrt{a(j)/a(j+1)}

is uniformly Lipschitz,

is uniformly bounded. As seen previously, for

\displaystyle\left\|\prod_{r=k+1}^{\ell}\left(1+a(r)\nabla_{x}\overline{h}(x_{r},y_{r})\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(K_{\ref{const: D_xh}}\sum_{r=k+1}^{\ell}a(r)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(K_{\ref{const: D_xh}}(T+1)\right),

for a suitable bound
K_{\ref{const: D_xh}}>0
on

. We define

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n,j}^{f,k}:=\prod_{r=j}^{k}\left(I+a(r)\nabla\overline{h}(x_{r},y_{r})\right)\sqrt{\frac{a(r)}{a(r+1)}}.

Recall that this quantity is bounded. Also, for some

,

\displaystyle\|e^{f,T}_{j}\|\leq K_{\ref{const: efT_fast}}\|\beta_{j}\|\leq\sqrt{a(j)}K_{\ref{const: efT_fast}}\|u_{j}\|,

for a large enough
j
. Thus for large enough
j
,

\displaystyle E\left[\left\|\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\sqrt{a(j)}\Gamma^{f,\ell}_{n,j+1}e^{f,T}_{j}\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}\right\|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq K_{17}E\left[\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\|u_{j}\|\right)^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\right)^{3}\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)E\left[\|u_{j}\|^{4}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\right)^{2},

using Jensen’s inequality and the result that the fourth moment of the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is bounded.

As
\lambda(\cdot)
is uniformly Lipschitz,
\nabla\lambda(\cdot)
is uniformly bounded. Thus, for some
K_{18}>0
,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{j}\overline{g}\left(x_{j},y_{j}\right)\nabla\lambda\left(y_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq K_{\ref{const: eta_j}}\epsilon_{j}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\sqrt{a(j)}\Gamma_{n,j+1}^{f,\ell}\epsilon_{j}\overline{g}\left(x_{j},y_{j}\right)\nabla\lambda\left(y_{j}\right)\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq K_{\ref{const: eta_j}}e^{K_{\ref{const: D_xh}}(T+1)}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\sqrt{a(j)}\frac{b(j)}{a(j)}\leq K_{\ref{const: eta_j}}e^{K_{\ref{const: D_xh}}(T+1)}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j),

by the assumption
b(j)\leq a(j)^{3/2}
.

We need the following consequence of the Burkholder inequality.

Lemma 7

(
Borkar 2022
, Lemma 7.1)
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}
be a zero mean martingale w.r.t. the increasing

\sigma
-fields
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\}
with
X_{0}=0
(say) and

\sup_{n\leq N}E\left[|X_{n}|^{4}\right]<\infty

Y_{n}\overset{\text{def}}{=}X_{n}-X_{n-1},n\geq 1
. For a suitable constant
K>0
,

E\left[\sup_{n\leq N}|X_{n}|^{4}\right]\leq K\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}E\left[Y_{m}^{4}\right]+E\left[(\sum_{m=1}^{N}E\left[Y_{m}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{m-1}\right])^{2}\right]\right).

From (
26
), define

\displaystyle\Psi_{k,m}^{(1)}=-\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}\sqrt{a(j)}\Gamma^{f,m}_{n,j+1}M^{f}_{j+1}\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}.

Then by Lemma
7
, we have that

\displaystyle E\left[\sup_{k+1\leq m<\ell}\|\Psi^{(1)}_{k,m}\|^{4}\right]\leq K_{19}\left(\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)^{2}\right).

Likewise, define

\displaystyle\Psi_{k,m}^{(2)}=\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}\sqrt{a(j)}\Gamma^{f,m}_{n,j+1}\epsilon_{j}\nabla\lambda(y_{j})M_{j+1}^{s}\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}.

By using
\epsilon_{j}\leq 1
and the fact that
\nabla\lambda(y_{j})
is uniformly bounded, by Lemma
7
, we have that,

\displaystyle E\left[\sup_{k+1\leq m<\ell}\|\Psi_{k,m}^{(2)}\|^{4}\right]\leq K_{20}\left(\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)^{2}\right).

Recall from (
18
) that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{e_{k+1}}{a(k)}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq K_{21}\left(\epsilon_{k}b(k)+\epsilon_{k}b(k)\left\|M_{k+1}^{s}\right\|+\epsilon_{k}b(k)\left\|M_{k+1}^{s}\right\|^{2}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle E\left[\left\|\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\sqrt{a(j)}\Gamma^{f,m}_{n,j+1}\frac{e_{j+1}}{a(j)}\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}\right\|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=E\left[\left\|\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\Gamma^{f,m}_{n,j+1}\frac{e_{j+1}}{a(j)^{3/2}}\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}\right\|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\right)^{4}E\left[\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\frac{a(j)}{\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\right)}\left\|\Gamma^{f,m}_{n,j+1}\frac{e_{j+1}}{a(j)^{3/2}}\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}\right\|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\right)^{3}E\left[\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{a(j)}\left\|\Gamma^{f,m}_{n,j+1}\frac{e_{j+1}}{a(j)}\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}\right\|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}27K_{22}T^{3}E\left[\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}b(j)^{3}\epsilon_{j}^{5}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\leq}K_{23}\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}b(j)\right)^{3}

\displaystyle\overset{(d)}{\leq}K_{24}\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\right)^{3},

where
(a)
follows from Jensen’s inequality,
(b)
follows from
(A8)
and the fact that
(a+b+c)^{p}\leq 3^{p-1}(a^{p}+b^{p}+c^{p}),p\geq 1
,
(c)
follows from
\epsilon_{j}\leq 1
and
\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}b(j)^{3}\leq\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}b(j)\right)^{3}
, and

follows from

.

As
\Delta^{s}_{j}(x_{j})=g(x_{j},y_{j},Y_{j})-\overline{g}(x_{j},y_{j})
, it follows from the boundedness of

and Jensen’s inequality that

\displaystyle E\left[\left\|\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\sqrt{a(j)}\Gamma_{n,j+1}^{f,\ell}\epsilon_{j}\nabla\lambda(y_{j})\Delta^{s}_{j}(x_{j})\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}\right\|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq K_{25}E\left[\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\sqrt{a(j)}\epsilon_{j}\right)^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq K_{26}\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}a(j)\right)^{4}.

Here, in the penultimate step, we used the assumption that

.

The term with

is the last one left to handle. Recall the decomposition for

shown in (

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}^{f}(y_{j})

\displaystyle=\zeta_{j}^{f}(y_{j})+\tau^{f}_{j}(y_{j})-\tau^{f}_{j+1}(y_{j})+e^{f,\Delta}_{j}(y_{j}).

(27)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\sqrt{a(j)}\Gamma_{n,j+1}^{f,\ell}\left(\tau^{f}_{j+1}(x_{j})-\tau_{j}^{f}(x_{j})\right)\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}

(28)

Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}\sqrt{a(j)}\Gamma_{n,j+1}^{f,\ell}\left(\tau_{j}^{f}(x_{j})-\tau^{f}_{j+1}(x_{j})\right)\sqrt{\frac{a(j)}{a(j+1)}}\right\|

2.5
Characterizing the limit

As proved in the previous section, the joint laws of
\{\tilde{u}^{n}(\cdot),\tilde{w}^{n}(\cdot),\tilde{y}^{n}(\cdot)\}
are tight in
\mathcal{P}(C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{3}
. We take a subsequence of
\{\tilde{u}^{n}(\cdot),\tilde{w}^{n}(\cdot),\tilde{y}^{n}(\cdot)\}
(which we denote using the same notation) that converges in law to a limit
\{u^{*}(\cdot),w^{*}(\cdot),y^{*}(\cdot)\}
. Using Skorohod’s theorem, without loss of generality we have that there is a sequence of random variables (which we again denote using the same notation) on some probability space, with identical laws as those of
\{\tilde{u}^{n}(\cdot),\tilde{w}^{n}(\cdot),\tilde{y}^{n}(\cdot)\}
, that converges almost surely and the limiting random variable has the same law as that of
\{u^{*}(\cdot),w^{*}(\cdot),y^{*}(\cdot)\}
