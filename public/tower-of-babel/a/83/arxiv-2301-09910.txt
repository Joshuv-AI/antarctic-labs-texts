Color-avoiding percolation of random graphs: between the subcritical and the intermediate regime
Lyuben Lichev

Abstract
Fix a graph G G in which every edge is colored in some of k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 colors. Two vertices u u and v v are CA-connected if u u and v v may be connected using any subset of k − 1 k-1 colors. CA-connectivity is an equivalence relation dividing the vertex set into classes called CA-components. In two recent papers, Ráth, Varga, Fekete, and Molontay, and Lichev and Schapira studied the size of the largest CA-component in a randomly colored random graph. The second of these works distinguished and studied three regimes (supercritical, intermediate, and subcritical) in which the largest CA-component has respectively linear, logarithmic, and bounded size. In this short note, we describe the phase transition between the intermediate and the subcritical regime.



Keywords: CA-percolation, Erdős-Rényi random graph, giant component, phase transition

MSC Class: 05C80, 60C05, 60K35, 82B43

Introduction

The model of color-avoiding percolation (or CA-percolation, for short) is defined as follows. Every edge in a graph
G
is colored in some of
k\geq 2
colors. Two vertices
u
and
v
in
G
are said to be
CA-connected
if
u
and
v
may be connected using any subset of
k-1
colors. CA-connectivity defines an equivalence relation on the vertex set of
G
whose classes are called
CA-components
.

CA-percolation was introduced by Krause, Danziger, and Zlatić
[
5
,
6
]
. They studied vertex-colored graphs and analyzed a vertex analog of CA-connectivity. While some empirical observations were made for scale-free networks, the focus was put on Erdős-Rényi random graphs due to their better CA-connectivity
[
5
]
. In a subsequent work, Kadović, Krause, Caldarelli, and Zlatić
[
4
]
defined mixed CA-percolation where both vertices and edges have colors. To a large extent, each of these foundational papers based their conclusions on experimental evidence.

A rigorous mathematical treatment of the subject was initiated by Ráth, Varga, Fekete, and Molontay
[
9
]
. They showed that under a certain subcriticality assumption, the size of the largest CA-component renormalized by
n
converges in probability to a fixed constant, and studied the behavior of that constant close to criticality. Recently, the author and Schapira
[
7
]
showed that one may naturally distinguish a supercritical, an intermediate, and a subcritical regime, in which the size of the largest CA-component is respectively linear, logarithmic, and bounded. In this short note, we give a precise description of the size of the largest CA-component between the subcritical and the intermediate regime.

1.1
Notation and terminology

We reuse the notation of
[
7
]
. Namely, for a positive integer
m
, we denote
[m]=\{1,\ldots,m\}
. We reserve the notation
[k]
to denote the set of colors, and the notation
[n]
for the set of vertices of our graphs.
Recall that for
p\in[0,1]
, the Erdős-Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
with parameters
n
and
p
, or ER random graph for short, is the graph on the vertex set
[n]
where the edge between any two distinct vertices is present with probability
p
, independently from all other edges.

Consider now a non-increasing sequence of positive real numbers
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{k}
, and define a family of
k
independent Erdős-Rényi random graphs
G_{i}=G(n,\tfrac{\lambda_{i}}{n})
for
i\in[k]
on the same vertex set
[n]
(alternatively, this family can be seen as a multigraph
([n],E_{1},\dots,E_{k})
where
E_{i}
is the edge set of
G_{i}
). In order to easily refer to and distinguish the graphs
(G_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
, we say that for every
i\in[k]
, the edges of
G_{i}
are given color
i
. Define further

\Lambda=\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{k},\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{i}^{*}=\Lambda-\lambda_{i}\quad\text{for every }i\in[k].

In particular,
\lambda_{1}^{*}\leq\lambda_{2}^{*}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{k}^{*}
. Also, we set

G=G_{1}\cup\ldots\cup G_{k},

and for
I\subseteq[k]
,

G_{I}=\bigcup_{i\in I}G_{i},\quad\text{and}\quad G^{I}=\bigcup_{i\in[k]\setminus I}G_{i},

with the shorthand notation
G_{i}
and
G^{i}
, respectively, when
I=\{i\}
.

Recall that two vertices
u
and
v
in
G
are CA-connected if
u
and
v
are connected in each of the graphs
(G^{i})_{i=1}^{k}
. Moreover, CA-connectivity is an equivalence relation with classes called CA-components. The CA-component of a vertex
u\in[n]
is denoted by
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(u)
, and
|\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(u)|
denotes its
size
, that is, the number of vertices it contains.

Finally, a sequence of events
(\mathcal{E}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is said to hold a.a.s. if
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. We use standard asymptotic notations. Namely, given two positive real sequences
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(g_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we write
f_{n}=o(g_{n})
, or
f_{n}\ll g_{n}
, or
g_{n}\gg f_{n}
if
f_{n}/g_{n}\to 0
when
n\to\infty
. Also, we write
f_{n}=\mathcal{O}(g_{n})
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
f_{n}\leq Cg_{n}
for all
n\geq 1
, and
f_{n}=\Theta(g_{n})
if
f_{n}=\mathcal{O}(g_{n})
and
g_{n}=\mathcal{O}(f_{n})
. Furthermore, we write
\xrightarrow{d}
to denote convergence in distribution of a sequence of random variables, and
\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}
for convergence in probability.

1.2
Main result

The following theorem from
[
7
]
distinguishes a supercritical, an intermediate and a subcritical regime, as mentioned in the introduction.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem 1.1 in
[
7
]
)
.

Suppose that
k\geq 2
.

(i)

There exists
a_{1}\in[0,1)
such that

\frac{\max_{u\in[n]}|\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(u)|}{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}a_{1}.

Moreover, one has
a_{1}>0
if and only if
\lambda_{1}^{*}>1
.

(ii)

If
\lambda_{k}^{*}>1>\lambda_{k-1}^{*}
, then there is a positive constant
a_{2}
such that

\frac{\max_{u\in[n]}|\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(u)|}{\log n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}a_{2}.

(iii)

If
\lambda_{k}^{*}<1
, then a.a.s.
\max_{u\in[n]}|\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(u)|\leq k
. Moreover, there are positive constants
\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{k}
, such that

(N_{2},\ldots,N_{k})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\bigotimes_{\ell=2}^{k}\mathrm{Po}(\beta_{\ell}),

that is, the random variables
(N_{\ell})_{\ell=2}^{k}
jointly converge in distribution to
k-1
independent Poisson variables with means
\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{k}
as
n\to\infty
.

Our main result fills the gap between the last two parts of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix a function
\zeta=\zeta(n)
tending to
0
as
n\to\infty
. Suppose that
\lambda_{k}^{*}=1+\zeta
and
\lambda_{k-1}^{*}<1
is a fixed constant. Then,

(i)

if
\tfrac{\log\zeta^{-1}}{\log n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then

\frac{\log(\zeta^{-1})\max_{u\in[n]}|\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(u)|}{\log n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}1.

(ii)

if there is
\varepsilon>0
such that
\zeta\leq n^{-\varepsilon}
for all sufficiently large
n
, then

\sup_{n\geq 1}\;\mathbb{P}(\max_{u\in[n]}|\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}(u)|\geq M)\xrightarrow[M\to\infty]{}0.

(1)

Note that Proposition 1.3 from
[
7
]
shows (
1
) when
\zeta=0
. In fact, the proof of the second point of Theorem
1.2
is inspired by the one of the above proposition, and is based on a combination of a first moment argument and a careful analysis of the connected components of
(G_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
. The proof of the first part combines very precise though by now standard results on the component structure of a subcritical ER random graph with a non-trivial optimization step.

\frac{\max_{u\in[n]}|\mathcal{C}(u)|}{(\lambda-1)n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}2,

(2)

[

are derived analogously, as mentioned in Section 2 in

2.2
On the intersection of a giant with independent subcritical clusters

For every
t>0
, define
I_{t}=t-1-\log t
. It is easy to check that
I_{t}
is a positive constant for every positive
t
except 1.

Now, fix
\lambda\in(0,1)
. For every
s\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathfrak{t}_{s}
the number of trees of size
s
in the random graph
G(n,\tfrac{\lambda}{n})
. The next lemma gathers our knowledge for
(\mathfrak{t}_{s})_{s\geq 1}
. Although it may be deduced by patching several already known results, we provide a sketch of proof for coherence and completeness.

Lemma 2.3
.

Fix
\lambda\in(0,1)
, a function
\omega=\omega(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
such that
\omega(n)=o(\log\log n)
, and
\ell=\ell(\lambda,n)=I_{\lambda}^{-1}\left(\log n-\tfrac{5}{2}\log\log n\right)-\omega
. Then, a.a.s. for every
s\leq\ell
,
\mathfrak{t}_{s}=(1+o(1))\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}\gg 1
. Moreover, a.a.s. there are at most
\mathrm{e}^{2I_{\lambda}\omega}
trees of size between
\ell
and
\ell+2\omega
in
G(n,\tfrac{\lambda}{n})
. Finally, a.a.s. there are no trees of size more than
\ell+2\omega
.

Sketch of proof.

To begin with, we recall that for every
s\geq 1
, there are
s^{s-2}
trees on
s
vertices (statement known under the name Cayley’s formula). Thus, for every positive integer
s\leq 2\ell
,

\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}=\binom{n}{s}s^{s-2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{s-1}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{s(n-s)+(s-1)(s-2)/2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-I_{\lambda}\cdot s}}{\lambda\sqrt{2\pi s^{5}}}\right),

(4)

where the second equality comes from an application of Stirling’s formula for
s!
and the fact that
s^{2}\ll n
. Note that if additionally
s\gg 1
,

\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}=(1+o(1))\frac{n\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-I_{\lambda}\cdot s}}{\lambda\sqrt{2\pi s^{5}}}.

(5)

In particular, for every
s\in[\ell]
,
\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}\geq\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{\ell}\gg 1
. Moreover, the second moment of
\mathfrak{t}_{s}
for
s\in[\ell]
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle=\;\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}+\binom{n}{s}\binom{n-s}{s}s^{2(s-2)}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{2(s-1)}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{2s(n-2s)+s^{2}+(s-1)(s-2)}

\displaystyle=\;\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}+(\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s})^{2}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-s^{2}}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{s-1}\frac{n-s-i}{n-i}\right)=(\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s})^{2}+\left(\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(s\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s})^{2}}{n}\right)\right).

Thus, for every
s\in[\ell]
, Chebyshev’s inequality implies that

\mathbb{P}(|\mathfrak{t}_{s}-\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}|\geq(\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s})^{2/3})\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\mathfrak{t}_{s})}{(\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s})^{4/3}}=\frac{1}{(\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s})^{1/3}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{s^{2}(\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s})^{2/3}}{n}\right)=\frac{1}{(\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s})^{1/3}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{1/4}}\right),

which together with a union bound over all
s\in[\ell]
and the fact that
\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}(\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s})^{-1/3}=\mathcal{O}((\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{\ell})^{-1/3})=o(1)
shows that a.a.s. for every
s\leq\ell
,
|\mathfrak{t}_{s}-\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}|\geq(\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s})^{2/3}
. Using that for every
s\in[\ell]
we have
\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}\geq\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{\ell}\gg 1
shows that in particular, a.a.s.
\mathfrak{t}_{s}=(1+o(1))\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}
for every
s\in[\ell]
.

Moreover, combining Markov’s inequality for the sum
\sum_{s=\ell}^{\ell+2\omega}\mathfrak{t}_{s}
with the fact that for every
s\in[\ell,\ell+2\omega]
,
\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{s}\leq\mathbb{E}\mathfrak{t}_{\ell}=\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{e}^{I_{\lambda}\omega})
, shows that there are a.a.s. at most
\mathrm{e}^{2I_{\lambda}\omega}
trees of size between
\ell
and
\ell+2\omega
. Finally, the last point follows by combining the fact that there are a.a.s. no connected components in
G(n,\tfrac{\lambda}{n})
of size more that
2\ell
(see e.g. Theorem 4.4 in
[
10
]
), (
5
) and Markov’s inequality for the sum of
\mathfrak{t}_{s}
over the interval
s\in[\ell+2\omega,2\ell]
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Recall that for
\lambda\in(0,1)
and for every function
\nu=\nu(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, a.a.s. there are only
\mathcal{O}(\nu)
vertices in components in
G(n,\tfrac{\lambda}{n})
that contain a cycle (see e.g. Lemma 2.11 from
[
2
]
). In particular, the number of components containing a cycle is a.a.s. negligible to
\mathfrak{t}_{s}
for every
s\in[\ell]
. Thus, to avoid clutter, we abuse notation and identify
\mathfrak{t}_{s}
and the number of components of size
s
for every
s\geq 1
.

Now, we fix
q=q(n)\leq 1
and consider a sequence
(X_{v})_{v\in[n]}
of independent Bernoulli random variables with parameter
q
, and a graph
G(n,\tfrac{\lambda}{n})
independent from them. We call a vertex
v\in[n]

black
if
X_{v}=1
, and
white
otherwise. The aim of the following lemma is to show that the components of
G(n,\tfrac{\lambda}{n})
whose size is very close to the size of the largest component do not typically contain abnormally many black vertices.

Lemma 2.5
.

Fix any
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
q=q(n)\to 0
such that
\tfrac{\log q^{-1}}{\log n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Also, define
M_{0}=\tfrac{\log n}{2\log q^{-1}}
. Then, with the notation of Lemma
2.3
, a.a.s. every component of
G(n,\tfrac{\lambda}{n})
of size at least
\ell
contains at most
M_{0}
black vertices.

Proof.

Firstly, suppose that
q\ell\leq(\log n)^{-1}
. By Lemma
2.3
a.a.s. there are
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{e}^{2I_{\lambda}\omega})=o(\log n)
vertices in components of size between
\ell
and
\ell+2\omega
, and none of size more than
\ell+2\omega
. We condition on this event. Then, by Markov’s inequality the probability that a vertex in some of these components is black is at most
\mathcal{O}(q\ell\mathrm{e}^{2I_{\lambda}\omega})=o(1)
. Thus, when
q\ell\leq(\log n)^{-1}
, the statement is trivially satisfied.

On the other hand, suppose that
q\ell\geq(\log n)^{-1}
. Then, if
q\ell\leq(\log n)^{1/3}
, Markov’s inequality applied for any component
\mathcal{C}
in
G
of size between
\ell
and
\ell+2\omega
gives that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}}X_{v}\geq\sqrt{\log n}\right)=\mathcal{O}((\log n)^{-1/6}).

Thus, a union bound over all at most
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{e}^{2I_{\lambda}\omega})=o((\log n)^{1/6})
such components and the fact that
M_{0}\geq\tfrac{\log n}{8\log\log n}\gg\sqrt{\log n}
show the lemma in this case.

Finally, if
q\ell\geq(\log n)^{1/3}
, Chernoff’s inequality implies that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}}X_{v}\geq(1+\varepsilon)q\ell\right)\leq\exp\left(-(1+o(1))\frac{\varepsilon^{2}q\ell}{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}((\log n)^{-1}).

However, using that
\tfrac{1}{\log q^{-1}}\gg q
shows that
M_{0}\gg q\ell
in this case. Thus, once again, a union bound over all
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{e}^{2I_{\lambda}\omega})=o(\log n)
components of size between
\ell
and
\ell+2\omega
finishes the proof.
∎

For every
s\geq 1
, we denote by
\widetilde{Z}_{s}
the number of connected components
\mathcal{C}
in
G(n,\tfrac{\lambda}{n})
such that
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}}X_{v}\geq s
. The following lemma characterizes the maximal
s
for which
\widetilde{Z}_{s}\geq 1
. The idea is that by Lemma
2.5
, a.a.s. this maximum is attained by a component of size at most
\ell
. Then, our precise knowledge of the number of components of size
s\in[\ell]
ensured by Lemma
2.3
(and Remark
2.4
) allows us to conclude by an elementary computation.

Lemma 2.6
.

Fix
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
q=q(n)\leq 1
such that
\tfrac{\log q^{-1}}{\log n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then,

\frac{(\log q^{-1})\max\{s:\widetilde{Z}_{s}\geq 1\}}{\log n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}1.

Proof.

To begin with, we condition on the events in Lemma
2.3
and on the sequence
(\mathfrak{t}_{s})_{s\geq 1}
defined therein. Recall that by Remark
2.4
,
\mathfrak{t}_{s}
is identified with the number of components in
G(n,\tfrac{\lambda}{n})
of size
s
.

For every
s\geq 1
, define
\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{\ell}
as the number of components
\mathcal{C}
of size at most
\ell
such that
\sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}}X_{v}\geq s
. By Lemma
2.5
a.a.s.
\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{\ell}=\widetilde{Z}_{s}
for all
s\geq M_{0}+1
, so we may concentrate our attention on components of size at most
\ell
.

Now, given
M\in[M_{0}+1,\ell]
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\exists s\in[M,\ell],\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{\ell}\geq 1)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{C}\in G(n,\lambda/n),\\
|\mathcal{C}|\leq\ell\end{subarray}}(1-\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(|\mathcal{C}|,q)\geq M))=\prod_{s=M}^{\ell}\bigg(1-\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(s,q)\geq M)\bigg)^{\mathfrak{t}_{s}},

(6)

where the first product is over the set of connected components of
G(n,\tfrac{\lambda}{n})
of size at least
\ell
. Thus, on the one hand, the fact that a.a.s. every component (even the ones of size more than
\ell
) has less than
M
black vertices shows that
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(s,q)\geq M)=o(1)
for every
s\in[\ell]
. On the other hand, the fact that
M\geq M_{0}\geq 2\ell q
shows that for every
s\in[\ell]
,
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(s,q)\geq M)=\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(s,q)=M))=\mathcal{O}(\tbinom{s}{M}q^{M}(1-q)^{s-M})
. This allows us to simplify (
6
) to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{Z}_{s}^{\ell}\geq 1)=\exp\left(-\Theta\left(\sum_{s=M}^{\ell}\binom{s}{M}q^{M}(1-q)^{s-M}\mathfrak{t}_{s}\right)\right).

Hence, using (
5
) and the fact that
M\gg 1
, for
s\in[M,\ell-1]
we have that
\tfrac{\mathfrak{t}_{s+1}}{\mathfrak{t}_{s}}=\mathrm{e}^{-I_{\lambda}}+o(1)
. Therefore, by comparing consecutive terms in the above sum, one may conclude that the maximal term is given by some
s_{1}
satisfying

\frac{(s_{1}+1)(1-q)}{s_{1}+1-M}\mathrm{e}^{-I_{\lambda}}=1+o(1),\text{ or equivalently }s_{1}=(1+o(1))\frac{M}{1-(1-q)\mathrm{e}^{-I_{\lambda}}}.

Furthermore, from
s=2s_{1}
on, say, the terms of the above sum decrease exponentially, and therefore

\binom{s_{1}}{M}q^{M}(1-q)^{s_{1}-M}\mathfrak{t}_{s_{1}}\leq\sum_{s=M}^{\ell}\binom{s}{M}q^{M}(1-q)^{s-M}\mathfrak{t}_{s}=\mathcal{O}\left(s_{1}\binom{s_{1}}{M}q^{M}(1-q)^{s_{1}-M}\mathfrak{t}_{s_{1}}\right).

(7)

Now, by (
4
) one may notice that both the lower and the upper bound are of the form

\mathrm{e}^{(\log q+\mathcal{O}(1))M}\mathfrak{t}_{s_{1}}=\mathrm{e}^{(\log q+\mathcal{O}(1))M}n.

Thus, for every
\varepsilon>0
, if
M\geq(1+\varepsilon)\tfrac{\log n}{\log q^{-1}}
, then a.a.s.
\widetilde{Z}_{M}^{\ell}=0
, while if
M\leq(1-\varepsilon)\tfrac{\log n}{\log q^{-1}}
, a.a.s.
\widetilde{Z}_{M}^{\ell}\geq 1
, which finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

On the other hand, if

. We condition on these two events and reveal the sizes of the connected components of
G_{k}
and

.

Now, note that for every
s\geq 1
, the probability that an

-tuple of vertices is connected in both

, all components in
G_{k}
and all

shows that the probability of having a set of
\lfloor 2\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor+1
vertices that are connected both in

and in

is bounded from above by

n^{2}\cdot n^{\lfloor 2\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor+1}\cdot\mathcal{O}\bigg(\bigg(\frac{n^{1-\varepsilon}}{n}\bigg)^{\lfloor 2\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor+1}\bigg(\frac{\log n}{n}\bigg)^{\lfloor 2\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor+1}\bigg)=o(1).

4
Concluding remarks

In this note, we extended a line of research dedicated to color-avoiding percolation in the context of ER random graphs. In particular, our Theorem
1.2
identifies the correct order of the size of the largest CA-component when
\lambda_{k}^{*}=1+o(1)
, and provides the constant in front of the first term when
\log(\lambda_{k}^{*}-1)=o(\log n)
.

Some intriguing questions remain open.

•

As mentioned in
[
7
]
, the range where the intermediate regime is well understood is contained in the second part of Theorem
1.1
. It would be interesting to identify the behavior of the largest CA-component when
\lambda_{k-1}^{*}\geq 1>\lambda_{1}^{*}
.

•

While we know that under the conditions of Theorem
1.1
(
ii
), the size of the largest CA-component is a tight random variable, we do not have good understanding of its distribution. Finding this distribution is another open problem deserving attention.
