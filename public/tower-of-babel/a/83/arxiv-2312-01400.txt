The Horizontal Tensor Complementarity Problem


Abstract
This article explores a new type of nonlinear complementarity problem, namely the horizontal tensor complementarity problem (HTCP), which is a natural extension of the horizontal linear complementarity problem studied in [ 11 ] . We extend the concepts of 𝐑 𝟎 {\bf R_{0}} , 𝐑 {\bf R} , and 𝐏 {\bf P} pairs from a pair of linear transformations given in [ 9 ] to a pair of tensors. When a given pair of tensors has these properties, we use degree-theoretic tools to discuss the existence and boundedness of solutions to the HTCP. Finally, we study an uniqueness result of the solution of the HTCP

Introduction

Given
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
{\bf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the horizontal linear complementarity problem HLCP(
A,B,{\bf{q}}
) is to find
({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

(1)

\displaystyle A{\bf{x}}-B{\bf{y}}

\displaystyle={\bf{q}},

where
{}^{\prime}\wedge^{\prime}
is a pointwise minimum map. The horizontal linear complementarity problem (HLCP) is one of the generalizations of the linear complementarity problem (LCP). The significance of this problem is due to a result by Eaves and Lemke, which states that any piecewise linear system can be expressed as the HLCP, as shown in
[
1
]
. Various numerical methods have been provided to solve the HLCP, as seen in
[
10
,
17
]
. The HLCP has been used for several years to study interior point algorithms for linear and convex programming, as demonstrated in
[
2
,
3
,
4
]

[
4
]
studied the convergence of infeasible interior-point methods for the HLCP. Guler
[
2
]
and Zhang
[
4
]
also indicated that the HLCP formulation is more useful than the LCP formulation for computing a convex quadratic problem.

Boundedness, existence, and uniqueness results for the HLCP were provided by Sznajder
[
11
]
. He established that the HLCP solution is bounded when the given matrix pair is an
{R_{0}}
pair. The matrix pair
\{A,B\}
is an
{R_{0}}
pair if HLCP(
A,B,{\bf 0}
) has only the trivial solution. Given that the degree of an
R_{0}
pair is nonzero, the HLCP’s solution exists. The
{P}
pair of matrices gives the existence and uniqueness of the solution of HLCP. The matrix pair
\{A,B\}
is said to be a
{P}
pair if and only if
A^{-1}B
is a
P
matrix. Similar types of outcomes for the HLCP’s solution structure on a Euclidean Jordan algebra have been provided by Gowda et al.
[
9
]
.

In Equation (
1
), the HLCP contains the standard LCP as a special case where
A=I
. The LCP has been extensively studied by several authors for several decades, and its numerous applications are well known. To explore the structure of solution sets for the LCP, various classes of matrices have been developed by different authors, for instance, refer to
[
8
,
6
]
. The class of
R_{0}
,
Q
, and
P
matrices is essential to the existence and uniqueness of the LCP solution. More information on these types of matrices can be found in a standard reference by Cottle and Pang
[
5
]
.

Among other generalizations of LCP, the tensor complementarity problem (TCP) is well known. For
{\bf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the TCP(
\mathcal{A},{\bf{q}}
) is to find a vector
{\bf{x}}
such that

{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},~{\bf{y}}=\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}+{\bf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~\text{and}~{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}={\bf 0},

where
\mathcal{A}
is an
m
-th order and
n
dimensional tensor given as

\hskip 93.89418pt{\bf\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}),~{\text{where}}~i_{j}\in[n]~\text{for all}~j\in[m]\text{~and~}a_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}\in\mathbb{R}.

For
m=2
, the TCP transforms into the LCP. The TCP is a specific class of nonlinear complementarity problems
[
28
]
. The outcomes of nonlinear complementarity theory are applicable to TCP theory. The monograph by Harker and Pang
[
28
]
on the nonlinear complementarity problem is a standard reference. Due to its remarkable structure, TCP has numerous practical applications in several mathematical domains, including economics, game theory, and mathematical engineering. The tensor complementarity problem was introduced by Song
[
22
]
. After that, the TCP was examined swiftly, and several conclusions describing the solution structure of the TCP were discovered by several authors. The notions of
{P},{R_{0}},{R}
, and
{Q}
matrices have been extended to
{P},{R_{0}},{R}
, and
{Q}
tensors, see
[
25
,
30
,
31
,
33
,
36
]
. Tensor complementarity problems have been increasingly studied in the last decade (for example, see
[
18
,
19
,
23
,
24
,
37
]
). Also, various generalizations of the TCP have been studied by several authors, (for example, see
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
). The vertical tensor complementarity problem (VTCP)
[
14
]
is one of many generalizations of the TCP. The vertical LCP and HLCP have been studied simultaneously in LCP theory. Movtivated by this one can ask whether the HLCP can be generalized to the HTCP. Is there a connection between HTCP and TCP?

The crux of our paper is the extension of the HLCP to the HTCP. We first extend the definition of the HLCP to the HTCP. We define the
{\bf R_{0}}
,
{\bf R}
, and
{\bf P}
pair for a pair of tensors and then study the boundedness of the solution set of the HTCP. Using degree theory and

tensor pair, we demonstrate the existence of a solution to the HTCP by stating and proving several results.

2.1
Degree theory

In our paper, we rely on degree theory results to demonstrate the existence of solutions for the HTCP. We present some properties of degree theory for our discussion. For further details on degree theory, we refer the readers to
[
21
,
6
,
7
]
.

Let
{\rm\Psi}:\bar{\Delta}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a continuous function, where
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open bounded set and let
{\bf p}\notin{\rm\Psi}(\partial\Delta)
be a vector. Here,
\partial\Delta
and
\bar{\Delta}
denote the boundary and closure of
\Delta
respectively. The degree of
{\rm\Psi}
with respect to
{\bf p}
over
\Delta
is denoted by
\deg({\rm\Psi},\Delta,{\bf p})
. The equation
{\rm\Psi}({\bf{x}})={\bf p}
has a solution if
\deg({\rm\Psi},\Delta,{\bf p})\neq 0
. The degree is an integer. If
{\rm\Psi}({\bf{x}})={\bf p}
has a unique solution, say
{\bf{y}}
in
\Delta
, then the degree is the same over any bounded open sets in
\Delta
containing the unique solution
{\bf{y}}
. The common degree is called the local degree of
{\rm\Psi}
and is denoted by
\text{deg}({\rm\Psi},{\bf{y}})
. If
{\rm\Psi}
is differentiable with nonsingular derivative at a point
{\bf{y}}
, then (
[
7
]
, page 869)

\text{deg}({\rm\Psi},{{\bf{y}}})=\text{sgn }{\rm det}({\rm\Psi}^{\prime}({{\bf{y}}})).

It is notable that if
{\rm\Psi}
is a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
{\rm\Psi}({\bf{x}})={\bf 0}
if and only if
{\bf{x}}={\bf 0}
, then

\text{deg}({\rm\Psi},{\bf 0})=\deg({\rm\Psi},\Delta,{\bf 0})
for
any bounded open set
\Delta
containing
{\bf 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.1.1
Properties of the degree

We list out the following properties for later use.

(D1)

deg(
I,\Delta,\cdot)=1
, where
I
is the identity function.

(D2)

Homotopy invariance: Let
F({\bf{x}},t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be continuous function and
Z:=\{{\bf{x}}:F({\bf{x}},t)={0}~\text{for some}~t\in[0,1]\}
is the zero set of
F({\bf{x}},t).

If
Z
is bounded, then for any bounded open set
\Delta
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing
Z
, we have

\text{deg}(F(\cdot,0),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\text{deg}(F(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0}).

(D3)

Cartesian product property: Let
{\rm\Psi}:\bar{\Delta}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\rm\Phi}:\bar{\Delta}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be two continuous functions, where
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
are open, bounded and nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
{\bf 0}\notin{\rm\Psi}({\partial\Delta})
and
{\bf 0}\notin{\rm\Phi}({\partial\Delta^{\prime}})
. Then the degree of the mapping
{\rm\Psi}\times{\rm\Phi}
is well defined and given by

\deg({\rm\Psi}\times{\rm\Phi},\Delta\times\Delta^{\prime},({\bf 0},{\bf 0}))=\deg({\rm\Psi},\Delta,{\bf 0})\deg({\rm\Phi},\Delta^{\prime},{\bf 0}).

Now we state the Borsuk’s theorem from (
[
12
]
, page 78) which will be used later.

Theorem 2
.

Let
D
be a symmetric bounded open set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing zero and
{\rm\Psi}:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an odd mapping
({\rm\Psi}(-{\bf{x}})=-{\rm\Psi}({\bf{x}})\text{~for all ~}{\bf{x}}\in D)
such that
{\bf 0}\notin{{\rm\Psi}}(\partial D)
. Then
\deg({\rm\Psi},D,{\bf 0})
is an odd number.

Note
: All the degree theoretic results and concepts are also applicable over any finite dimensional Hilbert space (like
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
etc).

3
The horizontal tensor complementarity problem

Motivated by equation (
1
), we first define the horizontal tensor complementarity problem (HTCP).

Definition 4
.

Given
\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\in T(m,n)
and
{\bf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the HTCP
(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf{q}})
is to find a pair of vectors
({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}

\displaystyle={\bf{q}}.

Here,
\text{SOL}(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf{q}})
denotes the solution set of HTCP(
\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf{q}}
) and is defined as the set of all such
({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})
.

It is noted that HTCP is turned into HLCP for
m=2
. Also, when
m
is even and
\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{I}}
, HTCP becomes TCP. Next, we define the
{\bf R_{0}}
,
{\bf R}
,
{\bf P}
tensor pair in the following subsections.

3.1
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair

This section defines
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair and HTCP degree.
The
R_{0}
pair of matrices
\{A,B\}
is given by

[{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}={\bf 0},A{\bf{x}}-B{\bf{y}}={\bf 0}]\implies({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})=({\bf 0},{\bf 0}).

The degree of HLCP with the help of the
R_{0}
pair has been defined and discussed in
[
11
]
. When
A=I
and
B
is an
R_{0}
matrix, the degree of HLCP and degree of LCP are same. In TCP(
{\mathcal{B}},{\bf{q}}
), if
{\mathcal{B}}
is an
R_{0}
tensor then
{\rm\psi}({\bf{x}})={\bf{x}}\wedge{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}={\bf 0}\iff{\bf{x}}={\bf 0}
. So the degree of
{\rm\psi}
is well defined for any bounded open set
\Delta
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing zero vector. This common degree is called TCP(
{\mathcal{B}},{\bf{q}}
)-degree and is denoted by
\deg({\mathcal{B}})
i.e.,
\deg({\mathcal{B}})=\deg({\rm\psi},{\bf 0}).
Now we define the
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair and its HTCP-degree.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\in T(m,n)
. We say
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is an
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair, if

\displaystyle\implies({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})=({\bf 0},{\bf 0}).

Equivalently HTCP
(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf 0})

has only the trivial solution.

It is noted that if
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is an
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair, then

{\rm\Psi}({\bf{z}})={\bf 0}\iff{\bf{z}}={\bf 0},

where

{\rm\Psi}({\bf{z}}):=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}\\
\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}\end{bmatrix},~{\bf{z}}=({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3)

Therefore
\text{deg}({\rm\Psi},{\bf 0})
is well defined. We define the HTCP-degree of tensor pair
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
as

\deg(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}):=\text{deg}({\rm\Psi},{\bf 0}).

Now we give a boundedness result of
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
with the help of the
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair. This result extends the boundedness results of the solution set of HLCP
[
11
]
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\in T(m,n)
. Then, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is an
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair.

(ii)

{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is a compact set for any
{\bf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

(i)
\implies
(ii): The set
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is always closed. Let
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
be an
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair. If
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is empty, then we are done. Let
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
be a nonempty set. Now we prove the boundedness of
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}.
On the contrary, assume
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is unbounded for some
{\bf{q}}
. Let there exist
{\bf{z}}_{k}:=({\bf{x}}_{k},{\bf{y}}_{k})
such that
\|{\bf{z}}_{k}\|\rightarrow\infty,
as
k\rightarrow\infty,
with

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}_{k}\wedge{\bf{y}}_{k}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}_{k}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}_{k}

\displaystyle={\bf{q}}.

Dividing by
\|{\bf{z}}_{k}\|
and
\|{\bf{z}}_{k}\|^{m-1}
respectively,

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\bf{x}}_{k}}{\|{\bf{z}}_{k}\|}\wedge\dfrac{{\bf{y}}_{k}}{\|{\bf{z}}_{k}\|}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\big(\dfrac{{\bf{x}}_{k}}{\|{\bf{z}}_{k}\|}\big)^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}\big(\dfrac{{\bf{y}}_{k}}{\|{\bf{z}}_{k}\|}\big)^{m-1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\bf q}{\|{\bf{z}}_{k}\|^{m-1}}.

As
k\rightarrow\infty
, let

\bar{{\bf z}}=(\bar{{\bf{x}}},\bar{{\bf{y}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},~\text{where}~\bar{{\bf{x}}}=\text{lim}\dfrac{{\bf{x}}_{k}}{\|{\bf{z}}_{k}\|},~\bar{{\bf{y}}}=\text{lim}\dfrac{{\bf{y}}_{k}}{\|{\bf{z}}_{k}\|}.

Therefore in equation (
4
), as
k\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle{\bar{\bf{x}}}\wedge{\bar{\bf{y}}}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\bar{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}\bar{\bf{y}}^{m-1}

\displaystyle={\bf 0}.

We see that
\|\bar{{\bf{x}}}\|^{2}+\|\bar{{\bf{y}}}\|^{2}=1.

But
{\bf{z}}=({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})=({\bf 0},{\bf 0})
as
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is an
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair. Thus we have a contradiction as
(\bar{{\bf{x}}},\bar{{\bf{y}}})\neq({\bf 0},{\bf 0}).
Hence
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is bounded for all
{\bf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(ii)
\implies
(i): Let
{\bf{z}}=({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\neq({\bf 0},{\bf 0})
with,

\displaystyle{{\bf{x}}}\wedge{{\bf{y}}}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}

\displaystyle={\bf 0}.

Multiplying by
\mu
and
\mu^{m-1}
respectively,

\displaystyle{\mu{\bf{x}}}\wedge{\mu{\bf{y}}}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\mu{\bf{x}})^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}(\mu{\bf{y}})^{m-1}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

where
\mu>{0}
.
For all
\mu>{0}
, we see
\mu{\bf{z}}=(\mu{\bf{x}},\mu{\bf{y}})\neq({\bf 0},{\bf 0})
and
\mu{\bf{z}}\in{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf 0}})}
. It contradicts the boundedness of
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf 0}})}.
Hence we have our claim.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\in T(m,n)
. If

(i)

\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is an
{\bf R}_{0}
tensor pair.

(ii)

\deg(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}})
is nonzero.

Then
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is a nonempty compact set for all
{\bf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
be an
{\bf R}_{0}
tensor pair. Due to Theorem
3
, it is enough to prove
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is nonempty. Let us consider a homotopy function
G
such that

G({{\bf{z}}},t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{{\bf{x}}}\wedge{{\bf{y}}}\\
{\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}-{{\mathcal{B}}}{{\bf{y}}}^{m-1}-t{\bf q}\end{array}\right],

where
{{\bf{z}}}=({{\bf{x}}},{{\bf{y}}})\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{n}},t\in[0,1].

Then
G({{\bf{z}}},0)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{{\bf{x}}}\wedge{{\bf{y}}}\\
{\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}-{{\mathcal{B}}}{{\bf{y}}}^{m-1}\end{array}\right]
and
G({{\bf{z}}},1)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{{\bf{x}}}\wedge{{\bf{y}}}\\
{\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}-{{\mathcal{B}}}{{\bf{y}}}^{m-1}-{\bf q}\end{array}\right]
.
As
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is an
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair, by the similar process in theorem
3
,
Z=\{{\bf z}:G({\bf z,t})={\bf 0}\text{ for some }t\in[0,1]\}
is uniformly bounded. For an open bounded set
\Delta
containing the set

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by the homotopy invariance property of the degree, we have

\deg({\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}})=\deg(G(\cdot,0),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\deg(G(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0}).

(5)

Due to the fact
\deg(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}})
is nonzero,
~\deg(G(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0})
is nonzero. Thus
G(\cdot,1)={\bf 0}
has solution in
\Delta
implying
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is nonempty. Thus
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is both nonempty and compact all
{\bf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
∎

Now we establish a relation between HTCP-degree and TCP-degree.

Proposition 2
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}\in T(m,n).
If
{\mathcal{B}}
is an
{R_{0}}
tensor, then the followings hold.

(i)

\{\mathcal{I},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is an
{\bf R_{\bf 0}}
tensor pair.

(ii)

If
m
is even, then
\deg({\mathcal{I}},{\mathcal{B}})=\deg({\mathcal{B}}).

Proof.

(i): Let
({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}

\displaystyle={\bf 0}.

This gives that
{\bf{x}}^{[m-1]}={\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}.
As
{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}={\bf 0}
, we have
{\bf{x}}^{[m-1]}\wedge{\bf{y}}={\bf 0}
which implies that
{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}\wedge{\bf{y}}={\bf 0}.
Since
{\mathcal{B}}
is an
{R_{\bf 0}}
tensor,
{\bf{y}}={\bf 0}.
So
({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})=({\bf 0},{\bf 0}).
Therefore
\{\mathcal{I},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is an
{\bf R_{\bf 0}}
tensor pair.

(ii): To prove item (ii), we consider the following maps:

\displaystyle F({\bf{z}},t)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf{y}}\wedge(t{\bf{x}}^{[m-1]}+(1-t){\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1})\\
\mathcal{I}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-t{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle H({\bf{z}},t)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}t({\bf{y}}\wedge{\bf{x}})+(1-t)({\bf{y}}\wedge{\bf{x}}^{[m-1]})\\
\mathcal{I}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}\end{bmatrix},

where
{\bf{z}}=({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}~{\text{and}}~t\in[{0},1]
. We claim that the zeros of functions
F
and
H
are contained in some open bounded set
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
when
t
varies from
0
to
1
.

To show this, we proceed further. Regarding to the function
F
, for
t=0
and
t=1,
we have

F({\bf{z}},0)=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf{y}}\wedge{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}\\
\mathcal{I}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}\end{bmatrix}\text{~and~}F({\bf{z}},1)=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf{y}}\wedge{\bf{x}}^{[m-1]}\\
\mathcal{I}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}\end{bmatrix}.

As
{\mathcal{B}}
is an
{R}_{0}
tensor, it is easy to see that
F({\bf{z}},t)={\bf 0}\iff{\bf{z}}={\bf 0}
for any
t\in[0,1]
. For an arbitrary open bounded set
\Delta
containing zero in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by the homotopy invariance of degree,

\deg\big(F(\cdot,{0}),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\deg(F(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0}).

(6)

As
m
is even, we see
{\bf{y}}\wedge{\bf{x}}
and
{\bf{y}}\wedge{\bf{x}}^{[m-1]}
always has same sign. Thus for
t\in[0,1]
,

t({\bf{y}}\wedge{\bf{x}})+(1-t)({\bf{y}}\wedge{\bf{x}}^{[m-1]})={\bf 0}\iff{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}={\bf 0}.

(7)

Due to the
{\bf R}_{0}
pair property of
\{\mathcal{I},{\mathcal{B}}\}
and from equation (
7
), we get

\{{\bf z}:H({\bf{z}},t)={\bf 0},\text{~for some~}t\in[0,1]\}=\{({\bf 0},{\bf 0})\}.

(8)

As
({\bf 0},{\bf 0})\in\Delta
, thus by the homotopy invariance of degree and equation (
8
),

\deg(H(\cdot,0),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\deg(H(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0}).

(9)

As
F(\cdot,1)=H(\cdot,0),
from equations (
6
) and (
9
), we get,

\deg\big(F(\cdot,{0}),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\deg(F(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\deg(H(\cdot,0),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\deg(H(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0}).

And

\deg({\mathcal{I}},{\mathcal{B}})=\deg(H(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\deg(F(\cdot,0),\Delta,{\bf 0}).

Now for two arbitrary open bounded sets
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
each containing
{\bf 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
letting
\Delta=\Delta_{1}\times\Delta_{2}
, by the Cartesian product (D3), we have

\deg({\mathcal{I}},{\mathcal{B}})=\deg(F(\cdot,0),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\deg({\mathcal{B}})\deg\big({\rm\Phi},\Delta_{2},{\bf 0}).

Here
{\rm\Phi}({\bf{x}})={\bf{x}}^{[m-1]}.
As
{\rm\Phi}
is a single variable odd function, from theorem 5.1 in
[

]
,
\deg\big({\rm\Phi},\Delta_{2},{\bf 0}\big)
will be one. Therefore,

\deg({\mathcal{I}},{\mathcal{B}})=\deg({\mathcal{B}}).

This completes the proof.
∎

3.2
R
tensor pair

In this subsection, we first extend the concept of
R
pair to tensors, and then we study the existence of the solution to the HTCP. To proceed further, we recall the concept of partial symmetrization for a tensor from
[
32
]
.

A tensor
{\mathcal{A}}=(a_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}})\in T(m,n)
can always be converted into a tensor
\bar{\mathcal{A}}=(\bar{a}_{{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{m}}})\in T(m,n)
with

\bar{a}_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{m}}=\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\sum\limits_{\tau(i_{2}\cdots i_{m})}{a}_{i_{1}\tau(i_{2}\cdots i_{m})},

where
\tau
is a permutation on the set
\{i_{2},...,i_{m}\}
and

{\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}={\bar{\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}},~\text{for all}~{{\bf{x}}}\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

(10)

\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is called partial symmetrize tensor. It is noted that,

\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1})=\nabla(\bar{{\mathcal{A}}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1})=(m-1){\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-2}
, where
\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1})
is Jacobain matrix of
{\rm\Phi}({\bf{x}})=\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}
and
N={\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

[
20
]
with

({\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-2})_{ij}=\displaystyle{\sum_{i_{3},...,i_{m}=1}^{n}}\bar{a}_{iji_{3}...i_{m}}x_{i_{3}}...x_{i_{m}}.

Also

N{\bf{x}}=({{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-2}){{\bf{x}}}={{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}={{\mathcal{A}}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}.

(11)

We write
\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1})
at the point
{{\bf{x}}}={\bar{{\bf{x}}}}
as
\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{\bar{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}})
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\in T(m,n)
and
{\bf q}\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. If
(\bar{{\bf{x}}},\bar{{\bf{y}}})
is a solution of the
\mathrm{HTCP}({\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}},{\bf q})
then
(\bar{{\bf{x}}},\bar{{\bf{y}}})
is a solution of the
\mathrm{HLCP}(\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{\bar{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}),\nabla({{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar{\bf{y}}}^{m-1}),(m-1){\bf q})
.

Proof.

Since
(\bar{{\bf{x}}},\bar{{\bf{y}}})
is a solution of the
\mathrm{HTCP}({\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}},{\bf q})
, we have
\bar{{\bf{x}}}\wedge\bar{{\bf{y}}}={\bf 0}
and
{\mathcal{A}}{\bar{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}-{{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar{\bf{y}}}^{m-1}={\bf q}
. For
({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, due to
10
and
11

\displaystyle(m-1)({\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}-{{\mathcal{B}}}{{\bf{y}}}^{m-1})

\displaystyle=(m-1)({\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}-{\bar{\mathcal{B}}}{{\bf{y}}}^{m-1}),

\displaystyle=(m-1)(({\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-2}){\bf{x}}-({\bar{\mathcal{B}}}{{\bf{y}}}^{m-2}){\bf{y}}),

\displaystyle=(\nabla({\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1})){{\bf{x}}}-(\nabla({\bar{\mathcal{B}}}{{\bf{y}}}^{m-1})){{\bf{y}}},

\displaystyle=(\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1})){{\bf{x}}}-(\nabla({{\mathcal{B}}}{{\bf{y}}}^{m-1})){{\bf{y}}}.

This gives,
(\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{\bar{\bf{x}}}^{m-1})){\bar{\bf{x}}}-(\nabla({{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar{\bf{y}}}^{m-1})){\bar{\bf{y}}}=(m-1){\bf q}
. Hence
(\bar{{\bf{x}}},\bar{{\bf{y}}})
is a solution of the
{\mathrm{HLCP}}(\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{\bar{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}),\nabla({{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar{\bf{y}}}^{m-1}),(m-1){\bf q})
.
∎

Definition 6
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\in T(m,n)
. We say
\{{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
is an
{\bf R}
tensor pair if

(i)

\{{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
is an
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair.

(ii)

There exists
{\bf q}\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
such that

(a)

\mathrm{HTCP}({\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}},{\bf q})
has a unique solution, say,
({\bar{{\bf{x}}}},{\bar{{\bf{y}}}})
,

(b)

{\bar{{\bf{x}}}}+{\bar{{\bf{y}}}}>{\bf 0}
, and

(c)

\mathrm{HLCP}(\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{\bar{{\bf{x}}}}^{m-1}),\nabla({{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar{{\bf{y}}}}^{m-1}),(m-1){\bf q})
has a unique solution.

Note that, if
\{{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
is an
{\bf R}
tensor pair, then
\bar{{\bf{x}}}\land\bar{{\bf{y}}}={\bf 0}
and
{\bar{{\bf{x}}}}+\bar{{\bf{y}}}>{\bf 0}
. Therefore, without loss of generality, we can say that
\bar{{\bf{x}}}
and
\bar{{\bf{y}}}
takes the form
{\bar{{\bf{x}}}}=(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},...,\bar{x}_{k},0,0,...,0)^{t}
and
{\bar{{\bf{y}}}}=(0,0,...,0,\bar{y}_{k+1},\bar{y}_{k+2},...,\bar{y}_{n})^{t}
, for some
k\leq n
. Let us write
\varphi({{\bf{x}}},{{\bf{y}}})={{\bf{x}}}\land{{\bf{y}}}

and the partial derivative of
\varphi({{\bf{x}}},{{\bf{y}}})
with respect to
{{\bf{x}}}
and
{{\bf{y}}}
, at the point
{\bar{{\bf{z}}}}=({\bar{{\bf{x}}}},{\bar{{\bf{y}}}})
, are denoted by
{\varphi}_{{\bf{x}}}^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}})
and
{\varphi}_{{\bf{y}}}^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}})
, respectively. We record the following fact about
\varphi({{\bf{x}}},{{\bf{y}}})
from (
[
9
]
, page 10) which will be used in further discussions. If for any
({\bf u},{\bf v})\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

{\varphi}_{{\bf{x}}}^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}}){{\bf{u}}}+{\varphi}_{{\bf{y}}}^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}}){\bf v}={\bf 0},

(12)

then
u_{i}=0
whenever
\bar{x}_{i}=0
and
v_{i}=0
whenever
\bar{y}_{i}=0
. Therefore
{\bf u}
and
{\bf v}
takes the form
{\bf u}=(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{k},0,0,...,0)^{t}
and
{\bf v}=(0,0,...,0,v_{k+1},v_{k+2},...,v_{n})^{t}.

We now give an example of an
{\bf R}
tensor pair.

Example 1
.

Let
m
be even and
{{\mathcal{B}}}\in T(m,n)
be an
R
tensor. We show that
\{{\mathcal{I}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
is an
{\bf R}
tensor pair. It is easy to verify that a vector
{{\bf{y}}}\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is a solution of the
\mathrm{TCP}({{\mathcal{B}}},{{\bf{q}}})
if and only if
(({{\mathcal{B}}}{{\bf{y}}}^{m-1}+{{\bf{q}}})^{[\frac{1}{m-1}]},{{\bf{y}}})
is a solution of
\mathrm{HTCP}({\mathcal{I}},{{\mathcal{B}}},{{\bf{q}}})
. From definition of
R
tensor,
\mathrm{HTCP}(\mathcal{I},{{\mathcal{B}}},{\bf 0})
has a unique solution
({\bf 0},{\bf 0})
and the
\mathrm{HTCP}(\mathcal{I},{{\mathcal{B}}},{\bf e})
has a unique solution,
({\bf e}^{[\frac{1}{m-1}]},{\bf 0})
. Therefore we get
\{{\mathcal{I}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
is an
{\bf R}_{0}
pair and
({\bar{{\bf{x}}}},{\bar{{\bf{y}}}})=({\bf e}^{[\frac{1}{m-1}]},{\bf 0})
is the unique solution of the
\mathrm{HTCP}({\mathcal{I}},{{\mathcal{B}}},{\bf e})
. Also
{\bar{{\bf{x}}}}+{\bar{{\bf{y}}}}>{\bf 0}
.
From Lemma
1
, we get
({\bar{{\bf{x}}}},{\bar{{\bf{y}}}})=({\bf e}^{[\frac{1}{m-1}]},{\bf 0})
is the only solution of
\mathrm{HLCP}(\nabla({\mathcal{I}}{\bar{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}}),\nabla({{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar{{\bf{y}}}}^{m-1}),(m-1){\bf e})
. Hence
\{{\mathcal{I}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
is an
{\bf R}
tensor pair.

Theorem 5
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\in T(m,n)
. If
\{{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
is an
{\bf R}
tensor pair then,
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is a nonempty compact set for all
{\bf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\{{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
be an
{\bf R}
tensor pair. This implies
\{{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
is an
{\bf R_{0}}
tensor pair. In view of Theorem
4
, it is enough to prove
\deg({\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}})
is nonzero.
Let
{{\bf{z}}}=({{\bf{x}}},{{\bf{y}}})\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
,
t\in[0,1]
and we consider the function

G({{\bf{z}}},t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi({{\bf{x}}},{{\bf{y}}})\\
{\mathcal{A}}{{\bf{x}}}^{m-1}-{{\mathcal{B}}}{{\bf{y}}}^{m-1}-t{\bf q}\end{array}\right],~\text{where}~\varphi({{\bf{x}}},{{\bf{y}}})={{\bf{x}}}\land{{\bf{y}}}.

Similar to theorem
4
, we get the the zero sets of the homotopy
G({{\bf{z}}},t)
are uniformly bounded as
t
varies from 0 to 1 and hence contained in some bounded open subset
\Delta
of
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. Therefore by the homotopy invariance property of the degree, we have

\deg({\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}})=\deg(G(\cdot,0),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\deg(G(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0}).

(13)

We claim that
\deg(G(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0})\neq 0
. Since
\{{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
is an
{\bf R}
tensor pair, therefore
G({{\bf{z}}},1)={\bf 0}
if and only if
{{\bf{z}}}={\bar{{\bf{z}}}}
, where
{\bar{{\bf{z}}}}=({\bar{{\bf{x}}}},{\bar{{\bf{y}}}})
is the unique solution of the
\mathrm{HTCP}({\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}},{\bf q})
. Therefore

\deg(G(\cdot,1),{\bf 0})=\deg(G(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0})=\deg(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}).

We now prove that
{\rm det}G^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}},1)\neq 0
. By taking derivative of
G
at
\bar{{\bf{z}}}
, we get

G^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}},1)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}{\varphi}_{{\bf{x}}}^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}})&{\varphi}_{{\bf{y}}}^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}})\\
\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{\bar{{\bf{x}}}}^{m-1})&~-\nabla({{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar{{\bf{y}}}}^{m-1})\end{array}\right].

Suppose that
G^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}},1)
is singular, so there exists nonzero
\tilde{{\bf{z}}}=({\bf u},{\bf v})
in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
such that
(G^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}},1))\tilde{{\bf{z}}}={\bf 0}
. This implies

{\varphi}_{{\bf{x}}}^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}}){{\bf{u}}}+{\varphi}_{{\bf{y}}}^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}}){\bf v}={\bf 0},~\text{and}

(14)

\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{\bar{{\bf{x}}}}^{m-1}){{\bf{u}}}-\nabla({{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar{{\bf{y}}}}^{m-1}){\bf v}={\bf 0}.

(15)

From equation (
14
), we get
{\bf u}=(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{k},0,0,...,0)

and
{\bf v}=(0,0,...,0,v_{k+1},v_{k+2},...,v_{n})
, where
k\leq n
. Therefore for all sufficiently small
\delta>0
, we have
({\bar{{\bf{x}}}}+\delta{{\bf{u}}})\land({\bar{{\bf{y}}}}+\delta{\bf v})={\bf 0}
. Due to
\{{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
being an
{\bf R}
tensor pair, we have (x bar, y bar ) is the unique solution of HTCP
(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf{q}})
and from lemma
1
, we see that the
\mathrm{HLCP}(\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{\bar{{\bf{x}}}}^{m-1}),\nabla({{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar{{\bf{y}}}}^{m-1}),(m-1){\bf q})
has a unique solution, which is
(\bar{{\bf{x}}},\bar{{\bf{y}}})
. However from equation (
15
), we get that
({\bar{{\bf{x}}}}+\delta{{\bf{u}}},{\bar{{\bf{y}}}}+\delta{\bf v})
is a solution of the
\mathrm{HLCP}(\nabla({\mathcal{A}}{\bar{{\bf{x}}}}^{m-1}),\nabla({{\mathcal{B}}}{\bar{{\bf{y}}}}^{m-1}),(m-1){\bf q})
, for all sufficiently small
\delta>0
. Hence we get a contradiction to
\{{\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}}\}
being an
{\bf R}
tensor pair. Therefore the matrix
G^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}},1)
is nonsingular and hence
{\rm sgn}~{\rm det}G^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}},1)\neq 0
. This implies

\deg({\mathcal{A}},{{\mathcal{B}}})={\rm sgn}~{\rm det}G^{{}^{\prime}}({\bar{{\bf{z}}}},1)\neq 0.

Hence
{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is a nonempty compact set for all
{\bf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
∎

3.3
P
tensor pair

For
{A,B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we say pair
\{A,B\}
is a
P
pair
[
8
]
if

[{\bf{x}}*{\bf{y}}\leq{\bf 0},~A{\bf{x}}-B{\bf{y}}={\bf 0}]\implies({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})={({\bf 0},{\bf 0})}.

From
[
8
]
, this is equivalent to
A^{-1}B
being a
P
matrix. The
P
pair gives the existence and uniqueness of the solution to the corresponding HLCP.

In this section, we define the
{\bf P}~tensor~pair
which extends the notion of
P
pair of matrix.

Definition 7
.

Let
\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\in T(m,n)
. We say
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is a
{\bf P}
tensor pair if

[{\bf{x}}*{\bf{y}}\leq{\bf 0},~\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}={\bf 0}]\implies({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})=({\bf 0},{\bf 0}).

Proposition 3
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be an order
2
left inverse of a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in T(m,n)
. Then
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is an even order
{\bf P}
tensor pair if and only if
M{\mathcal{B}}
is a
P
tensor.

Proof.

First assume
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is an even order
{\bf P}
tensor pair. To prove
M{\mathcal{B}}
is a
P
tensor, let
{\bf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
{\bf{y}}*(M{\mathcal{B}}){\bf{y}}^{m-1}\leq{\bf 0}.
As
m
is even,
{\bf{y}}^{[m-1]}*(M{\mathcal{B}}){\bf{y}}^{m-1}\leq{\bf 0}.
For some
{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
{\bf{x}}^{[m-1]}=\mathcal{I}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}=(M{\mathcal{B}}){\bf{y}}^{m-1}.
As
M
is the order
2
left inverse of
\mathcal{A}
then, by theorem
1
,
M\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{I}}
. Therefore
(M\mathcal{A}){\bf{x}}^{m-1}=(M{\mathcal{B}}){\bf{y}}^{m-1}.
From definition
3
,
\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}={\bf 0}
and
{\bf{x}}*{\bf{y}}\leq{\bf 0}.
Thus
({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})=({\bf 0},{\bf 0})
as
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is a
{\bf P}
tensor pair. Hence
M{\mathcal{B}}
is a
P
tensor.

For converse, let
({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
{\bf{x}}*{\bf{y}}\leq{\bf 0},~\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}={\bf 0}.
As
M
is the order 2 left inverse of
\mathcal{A}
,
{\mathcal{I}}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-(M{\mathcal{B}}){\bf{y}}^{m-1}={\bf 0}
. This implies
{\bf{x}}^{[m-1]}=(M{\mathcal{B}}){\bf{y}}^{m-1}.
Also
{\bf{x}}*{\bf{y}}\leq{\bf 0}\implies{\bf{x}}^{[m-1]}*{\bf{y}}\leq{\bf 0}.
From this,
(M{\mathcal{B}}){\bf{y}}^{m-1}*{\bf{y}}\leq{\bf 0}.
Thus we get

Theorem 6
.

\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\in E(m,n)

{\bf P}

Proof.

(i): If H-eigenvalue does not exist, it is trivial. Let

\lambda

[{\bf{x}}*{\bf 0}={\bf 0},~\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf 0}={\bf 0}]\implies({\bf{x}},{\bf 0})=({\bf 0},{\bf 0}).

It contradicts our assumption. Therefore,
\mathcal{A}
has no zero H-eigenvalues. Similarly,
{\mathcal{B}}

(ii): It follows as similar to item (i).

(iii): In case of the non-existence of
{\mathcal{B}}

(iv): Let

(i)

Theorem 9
.

Let
\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\in E(m,n)
. If
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is a
{\bf P}
tensor pair,
HTCP(
\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}
)
has a nonempty compact solution set for every
{\bf q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

To prove this, we use degree theory tools and homotopy function.
Let us consider a homotopy, for
t\in[{0},1]
,

F({\bf{z}},t)=t\begin{bmatrix}{\bf{y}}\\
\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}\end{bmatrix}+(1-t)\begin{bmatrix}{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}\\
\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}\end{bmatrix},

where
{\bf{z}}=({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. At
t=0
and
t=1
,

F({\bf{z}},{0})={\rm\Psi}({\bf{z}})~\text{and}~F({\bf{z}},1)=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf{y}}\\
\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}\end{bmatrix}.

To prove
\deg(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}})
is nonzero, first we show the set,
Z=\{{\bf{z}}:F({\bf{z}},t)={\bf 0},~\text{where}~t\in[{0},1]\}
, is bounded. To prove our claim, we discuss the following cases here.

Case: 1.
When
t={0},
we get
{\rm\Psi}({\bf{z}})={\bf 0}\implies{\bf{z}}={\bf 0}
as
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is a
{\bf P}
tensor pair. For
t=1,F({\bf{z}},1)={\bf 0}\iff{\bf{z}}={\bf 0}
.

Case: 2
. Now for
t\in({0},1)
,

F({\bf{z}},t)={\bf 0},

\implies\begin{bmatrix}{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}\\
\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}\end{bmatrix}=-\alpha\begin{bmatrix}{\bf{y}}\\
\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}\end{bmatrix},

where
\alpha=\dfrac{t}{1-t}>{0},
as
t\in({0},1).
From the first row of above equation and proposition
1
, we have

{\bf{x}}\wedge{\bf{y}}=-\alpha{{\bf{y}}}\implies\text{min}\{{\bf{x}}+\alpha{{\bf{y}}},(1+\alpha){{\bf{y}}}\}={\bf 0}.

From this, we get
{\bf{y}}\geq{\bf 0}
and
({{\bf{x}}}+\alpha{{\bf{y}}})*(1+\alpha){{\bf{y}}}={\bf 0}
which implies that
{{\bf{x}}}*{{\bf{y}}}\leq{\bf 0}.
From the second row,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}

\displaystyle=-\alpha\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1},

\displaystyle(1+\alpha)\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\alpha^{\prime}{\bf{x}})^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}

\displaystyle={\bf 0},

where
\alpha^{\prime}=(1+\alpha)^{\frac{1}{m-1}}.
Here
m
is even so from the above equations,

(\alpha^{\prime}{\bf{x}})*{\bf{y}}\leq{\bf 0},~\mathcal{A}(\alpha^{\prime}{\bf{x}})^{m-1}-{\mathcal{B}}{\bf{y}}^{m-1}={\bf 0}.

As
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is a
{\bf P}
tensor pair,
((\alpha^{\prime}{\bf{x}}),{\bf{y}})=({\bf 0},{\bf 0})\implies{\bf{z}}=({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})=({\bf 0},{\bf 0}).

Hence,
Z
contains only the zero vector. By the homotopy invariance property of degree (D2),

\deg(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}})=\deg\big(F(\cdot,{0}),\Delta,{\bf 0}\big)=\deg\big(F(\cdot,1),\Delta,{\bf 0}\big).

Let
{\rm\Phi}({\bf{x}})=\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}
. Now for two arbitrary open bounded sets

and
\Delta_{2}
containing
{\bf 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

, by the Cartesian product (D3) and (D1), we have,

As
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is
{\bf P}
tensor pair,
\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}={\bf 0}\iff{\bf{x}}={\bf 0}.
Thus by lemma

\deg(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}})=\deg\big({\rm\Phi},\Delta_{2},{\bf 0})\neq 0.

As
{\bf P}
tensor pair is an
{\bf R}_{0}

{\bf R}_{0}
tensor pair with

Due to theorem

{\mathop{\rm SOL}{(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}},{\bf q}})}
is nonempty compact for every

Strong P tensor pair

In TCP, global uniqueness solvable property is given by strong

Definition 8
.

We say
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is strong

tensor pair if for
{\bf{x}}_{i},{\bf{y}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},i\in\{1,2\}

\displaystyle.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
be a strong
{\bf P}
tensor pair. Then the followings hold:

(i)

\mathcal{A}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are even order tensors.

(ii)

F({\bf{x}})=\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}
and

(iii)

\deg(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}})

Proof.

(i):
Let
{\bf{x}}_{1}={\bf{x}},{\bf{x}}_{2}=-{\bf{x}}
and
{\bf{y}}_{i}={\bf 0},
for
i=1,2
. Assume contrary
m
is odd so
\mathcal{A}{\bf{x}}^{m-1}=\mathcal{A}(-{\bf x})^{m-1}

Therefore

But it contradicts our assumption i.e.,
{\bf{x}}_{1}={\bf{x}},{\bf{x}}_{2}=-{\bf{x}}
. Hence

is even.

(ii): Let

and
{\bf{y}}_{1}={\bf 0}={\bf{y}}_{2}
then we get

be a strong
{\bf P}
tensor pair,

(iii): As
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is a strong
{\bf P}
tensor pair,
\{\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}}\}
is a
{\bf P}
tensor pair. Therefore from Theorem

,
\deg(\mathcal{A},{\mathcal{B}})\neq 0.

∎

Proposition 5
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}\in T(m,n)

{\mathcal{B}}
is a strong

is a strong
{\bf P}
tensor pair.

Let
{\bf{x}}_{i},{\bf{y}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},i\in\{1,2\}

Conclusion

We introduced the HTCP in this paper, followed by a study of its boundedness and the existence of the solution. In order to prove the boundedness and existence of the solution to the HTCP, we introduced the concepts of
{\bf R_{0}}
,
{\bf R}
and
