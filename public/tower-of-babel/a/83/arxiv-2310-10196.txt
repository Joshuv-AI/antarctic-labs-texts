Large Models for Time Series and Spatio-Temporal Data: A Survey and Outlook
Ming Jin, Qingsong Wen, Yuxuan Liang, Chaoli Zhang, Siqiao Xue, Xue Wang, James Zhang, Yi Wang, Haifeng Chen, Xiaoli Li, Shirui Pan, Vincent S. Tseng, Yu Zheng, Lei Chen, Hui Xiong

Abstract
Temporal data, notably time series and spatio-temporal data, are prevalent in real-world applications. They capture dynamic system measurements and are produced in vast quantities by both physical and virtual sensors. Analyzing these data types is vital to harnessing the rich information they encompass and thus benefits a wide range of downstream tasks. Recent advances in large language and other foundational models have spurred increased use of these models in time series and spatio-temporal data mining. Such methodologies not only enable enhanced pattern recognition and reasoning across diverse domains but also lay the groundwork for artificial general intelligence capable of comprehending and processing common temporal data. In this survey, we offer a comprehensive and up-to-date review of large models tailored (or adapted) for time series and spatio-temporal data, spanning four key facets: data types, model categories, model scopes, and application areas/tasks. Our objective is to equip practitioners with the knowledge to develop applications and further research in this underexplored domain. We primarily categorize the existing literature into two major clusters: large models for time series analysis (LM4TS) and spatio-temporal data mining (LM4STD). On this basis, we further classify research based on model scopes (i.e., general vs. domain-specific) and application areas/tasks. We also provide a comprehensive collection of pertinent resources, including datasets, model assets, and useful tools, categorized by mainstream applications. This survey coalesces the latest strides in large model-centric research on time series and spatio-temporal data, underscoring the solid foundations, current advances, practical applications, abundant resources, and future research opportunities.

In this paper, we fulfill this need by providing a unified comprehensive and up-to-date review of large models dedicated to time series and spatio-temporal data analysis, encompassing both LLMs and PFMs across different data categories, model scopes, application domains, and representative tasks. Time series and spatio-temporal data are both temporal in nature and often share similarities in analytical methods. Combining them in a single survey allows practitioners to explore the synergies and commonalities between these two areas. In addition, integrating insights from both domains can foster cross-pollination of ideas, leading to innovative approaches that leverage the strengths of one domain to address challenges in the other, as well as facilitate a more comprehensive and interconnected understanding of large models for temporal data.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

First Comprehensive and Up-to-Date Survey.
To our knowledge, this is the first survey paper that comprehensively reviews the lastest advancements of large models for time series and spatio-temporal data analysis. We offer a detailed and current overview that spans the breadth of the field while also delving into the nuances of individual approaches, providing the reader with a thorough and up-to-date understanding of this topic.

•

Unified and Structured Taxonomy.
We introduce a unified and structured taxonomy that categorizes existing research into two principal clusters: large model for time series (LM4TS) and for spatio-temporal data (LM4STD), organized based on data categories. We further divide each cluster into two subgroups, i.e., LLMs and PFMs, according to model types. Subsequent categorizations are conducted through the lenses of model scopes, application domains, and specific tasks. This multi-faceted classification provides the reader with a coherent roadmap for understanding the field from multiple perspectives.

•

Abundant Resource Compilation.
We compile and summarize a wealth of resources in this field, encompassing datasets, open-source implementations, and evaluation benchmarks. Additionally, we outline the practical applications of pertinent large models across various domains. This compilation serves as a valuable reference point for future research and development endeavors.

•

Future Research Oppotunities.
We identify and elaborate on multiple promising avenues for future research, encompassing a range of perspectives such as data sources, model architectures, training and inference paradigms, as well as other potential oppotunities. This discussion equips the reader with a nuanced understanding of the current state of the field, while also highlighting prospective directions for future investigation.

II-B

Pre-Trained Foundation Models

Pre-trained foundation models refer to the large-scale pre-trained models that can be adapted to solve a wide range of downstream tasks
[
43
,
41
]
. In principle, LLMs (as well as MLLMs) also belong to PFMs, but they are more commonly used for solving natural language-oriented tasks. PFMs constitute a broader category of models that are characterized by their emergent capabilities and homogenization in effectively solving different tasks with the consolidation of methodologies in building AI systems
[
41
]
, which are significantly different from task-specific models. In general, the capabilities of PFMs are in three key dimensions:
modality bridging
,
reasoning and planning
, and
interaction
.

The first aspect refers to multimodal models, e.g., MLLMs like vision-language models, which have shown remarkable results in unifying the language and vision modalities
[
2
]
. For example, CLIP
[
11
]
initially proposed to bridge the gap between images and texts, and SAM
[
86
]
further extends the concept of textual prompting to visual prompting. Other recent works, such as NExT-GPT
[
87
]
, further enlarge the boundary and even allow for the bridging of multiple different modalities. It is to be expected that data are naturally multimodal in the real world, such as in clinical medicine
[
88
]
, where time series and spatio-temporal data are frequently involved like EGC and medical events. This also motivates recent research interests in multimodal time series and spatio-temporal data
[
57
,
55
]
. A brief roadmap of PFMs is given in
Figure 2
(
right
), where VLMs remain the most popular research topic in this area but other PFMs related to time series and spatio-temporal data are emerging, though still in the early stage of development.

The second aspect highlights the reasoning and planning capabilities of PFMs. Typical examples including CoT
[
89
]
, ToT
[
90
]
, and GoT
[
91
]
in LLMs, as well as task-planning agents
[
92
,
93
]
. The last aspect defines the interaction capabilities of PFMs, and this includes both actioning and communication.
In this survey, we mainly focus on PFMs for time series and spatio-temporal data, in which most of them remain in the early stage of development and far from reaching the second and third aspects mentioned above. We refer readers to
[
41
]
for more details on PFMs.

II-C

Time Series and Spatio-Temporal Data

Temporal data, notably time series and spatio-temporal data, serve as foundational data categories for a myriad of real-world applications
[
94
,
20
]
.
A time series is commonly defined as an ordered sequence of data points, organized by their occurrence in time. These sequences can be either
univariate
or
multivariate
. For example, daily temperature readings from a city would form a univariate time series, whereas combining daily temperature and humidity data would create a multivariate time series.
In the sequel, we use bold uppercase letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{X}
) for matrices, bold lowercase letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{x}
) for vectors, calligraphic uppercase letters (e.g.,
\mathcal{X}
) for sets, and standard lowercase letters (e.g.,
x
) for scalars.
Formally, we adhere to the definitions of time series data outlined in
[
32
]
, which we summarize below.

Definition 1
(Time Series Data)

A univariate time series
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{T}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
is a sequence of
T
data points indexed in time order, where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
is the value of the time series at time
t
. A multivariate time series
\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{T}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D}
is a sequence of
T
data points indexed in time order but with
D
dimensions, where
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}(1\leq t\leq T)
denotes the values of the time series at time
t
along
D
channels. We refer the reader to
[
32
]
for more in-depth discussions on time series data.

Spatio-temporal data, on the other hand, is a sequence of data points organized by both temporal and spatial dimensions, which encapsulates a wide variety of constructs, including
spatio-temporal graphs
(STGs)
[
32
]
,
temporal knowledge graphs
(TKGs)
[
95
]
,
videos
[
96
]
,
point cloud streaming
(PCS)
[
97
]
,
trajectories

[
98
]
, among others. In this study, our primary focus is on the first three categories, which are highly representative and align closely with the current research interests. Formally, we follow the definitions of STGs and TKGs in
[
32
]
and
[
95
]
, which we summarize as follows.

Definition 2
(Spatio-Temporal Graphs)

A spatio-temporal graph
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{G}_{T}\}
is a sequence of
T
static graph snapshots indexed in time order, where
\mathcal{G}_{t}=(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t})
denotes a snapshot at time
t
;
\mathcal{V}_{t}
and
\mathcal{E}_{t}
are sets of nodes and edges at time
t
. The corresponding adjacency and node feature matrices are defined as
\mathbf{A}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
\mathbf{X}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
, where
\mathbf{A}_{t}=\{a_{ij}^{t}\}
and
a_{ij}^{t}\neq 0
if there is an edge between node
i
and
j
, s.t.
e_{ij}^{t}\in\mathcal{E}_{t}
. Here,
N=|\mathcal{V}_{t}|
is the number of nodes and
D
is the dimension of node features.

Definition 3
(Temporal Knowledge Graphs)

A temporal knowledge graph
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{G}_{T}\}
is a sequence of
T
static knowledge graph snapshots indexed in time order, where
\mathcal{G}_{t}=(\mathcal{E}_{t},\mathcal{R}_{t})
is a snapshot consisting of the entity and relation sets at time
t
. Specifically,
\mathcal{E}_{t}
encapsulates both subject and object entities, and
\mathcal{R}_{t}
denotes the set of relations between them. In a temporal knowledge graph, entities and relations may possess different features, denoted by
\mathbf{X}^{e}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}_{t}|\times D_{e}}
and
\mathbf{X}^{r}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{R}_{t}|\times D_{r}}
, where
D_{e}
and
D_{r}
are feature dimensions.

Video data can also be interpreted as a type of spatio-temporal data, which is usually defined as a sequence of images indexed in time order; thus, we define it as follows in a similar manner.

Definition 4
(Video Data)

Let
\mathcal{V}=\{\mathbf{F}_{1},\mathbf{F}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{F}_{T}\}
be a video consisting of
T
frames indexed in time order, where
\mathbf{F}_{t}
denotes the
t
-th frame. For simplicity, we let each frame
\mathbf{F}_{t}
be a matrix of pixels, i.e.,
\mathbf{F}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}
, without considering other metadata, where
H
,
W
, and
C
are the height, width, and color channels of the frame, respectively.

•

Time Series Tasks.
This area typically includes four principal analytical tasks:
forecasting
,
classification
,
anomaly detection
, and
imputation
. In forecasting, the goal is to predict the future values of a time series, which can be further categorized into short-term and long-term forecasting depending on the prediction horizon. In classification, the goal is to classify the input time series into different categories. Time series anomaly detection can also be understood as a special type of classification task, where we aim to identify the abnormal time series from the normal ones. In imputation, the goal is to fill in the missing values in time series. Without loss of generality, we refer the reader to
[
32
]
for more details.

•

Spatio-Temporal Graph Tasks.
The predominant downstream task in spatio-temporal graph is
forecasting
, which aims to predict the future node features by referring to the historical attributive and structural information. Typical examples including traffic forecasting
[
20
]
and some on-demand services
[
32
]
. Other common tasks include
link prediction

[
99
]
and
node/graph classification

[
100
]
, where the former aims to predict the existence of an edge based on the historical information, and the latter aims to classify the nodes or graphs into different categories. A more in-depth discussion can be found in
[
101
]
.

•

Temporal Knowledge Graph Tasks.
There are two important tasks in temporal knowledge graphs:
completion
and
forecasting
. The former mainly aims to impute missing relations within the graph, while the latter focuses on predicting future relations. For an expanded discussion, please consult
[
102
,
103
]
.

Video Tasks.
In the field of computer vision, video data encompasses several core tasks such as
detection
,
captioning
,
anticipation
, and
querying
. Detection aims to identify specific objects or actions within the video. Captioning seeks to generate natural language descriptions for the video content. Anticipation involves predicting forthcoming frames in a video sequence. Lastly, querying aims to retrieve video segments pertinent to a specific query. Notably, these tasks often traverse multiple modalities and have received considerable attention compared to the previously mentioned data types. For additional insights, the reader is directed to
[

]
.

In this section, we provide an overview and categorization of the large models for time series and spatio-temporal data. Our survey is structured along four main dimensions: data categories, model architectures, model scopes, and application domains or tasks. A detailed synopsis of the related works can be found in

large models for time series data
(LM4TS) and
large models for spatio-temporal data
(LM4STD).

In the first category, we subdivide the existing literature into two types:
LLMs for time series data
(LLM4TS) and
PFMs for time series data
(PFM4TS). The former refers to leveraging LLMs for solving time series tasks, irrespective of whether LLMs are fine-tuned or frozen during the adaptation process. The latter, on the other hand, focuses on the development of PFMs explicitly designed for various time series tasks. It is important to note that the field of PFM4TS is relatively nascent; existing models may not fully encapsulate the potential of general-purpose PFMs as defined in
subsection II-B

general-purpose

domain-specific

In the category of LM4STD, we employ a similar taxonomy, defining
LLMs for spatio-temporal data
(LLM4STD) and
PFMs for spatio-temporal data
(PFM4STD). Unlike time series data, spatio-temporal data encompasses a broader array of entities spanning multiple domains; thus we explicitly categorize LLM4STD and PFM4STD by their relevant domains. While we offer a categorization based on model scopes in
Table II

Figure 3

Figure 3
), which is similar to LM4TS. Notably, PFM4STD has seen more extensive development than its time series counterpart. Current research is mainly geared towards STGs and video data, often featuring enhanced capabilities of PFMs such as multimodal bridging and reasoning.

IV
Large Models for Time Series Data

In this section, we delve into the advancements of large language models and pre-trained foundational models in time series analysis. Within each subsection, we further segment our discussion based on whether the models serve general or domain-specific purposes, as outlined in
Figure 3
.

IV-A

Large Language Models in Time Series

Time series analysis is a fundamental problem studied in many real-world applications, such as retail sales forecasting, imputation of missing data for economic time series, anomaly detection for industrial maintenance, and classification of time series from various domains. By witnessing a fast
growth of LLMs in the NLP field, we
are particularly interested in exploring the research question
of whether we can take advantage of these models and adapt them for time series analysis. In this subsection, we discuss LLM4TS in terms of general-purpose and domain-specific models in
the following parts, respectively.

IV-A
1
General Models

As one of the first efforts in approaching general time series forecasting from an LLM perspective,
[
57
]
formally introduces a novel task: prompt-based time series forecasting — PromptCast. With the input and output being natural language sentences, PromptCast presents a novel “code less” solution for time series forecasting, which could provide a new perspective rather than purely focusing on designing complicated architectures. An instruction dataset (PISA) for the newly introduced task has been released. Another similar research is LLMTime
[
107
]
, which demonstrates that LLMs are effective zero-shot time series learners with properly configured tokenization on time series data. As a concurrent work, to tackle the challenge of lacking the large scale of data for training,
[
48
]
proposes a unified framework based on partially frozen LLMs, i.e., only fine-tuning the embedding and normalization layers while keeping self-attention and feedforward layers frozen, to achieve a state-of-the-art or comparable performance in all major types of time series analysis tasks, including time series classification, short/long-term forecasting, imputation, anomaly detection, few-shot, and zero-sample forecasting. A similar work is TEMPO
[
104
]
, which focuses solely on time series forecasting but incorporates additional fine-grained designs such as time series decomposition and soft prompts.
[
106
]
leverages LLMs for time series forecasting with a two-stage fine-tuning process: start by guiding the LLM towards time-series data using supervised fine-tuning, and subsequently pivot to downstream fine-tuning geared towards time-series forecasting.
[
105
]
activates LLM’s ability for time series with a new embedding method, which tokenizes and encodes the data by instance-wise, feature-wise, and text-prototype-aligned contrast, and then creates prompts to pass to LLMs to perform the tasks. Different from the above methods, a recent work Time-LLM
[
49
]
is proposed to reprogram time series with the source data modality along with natural language-based prompting to unleash the potential of LLMs as effective time series machines, which achieves state-of-the-art performance in various forecasting scenarios, as well as excels in both few-shot and zero-shot settings.
Time-LLM is also lightweight and efficient, since it neither edits the input time series directly nor fine-tunes the backbone LLM.

IV-A
2
Domain-Specific Models

Transportation.
Time series forecasting plays a pivotal role in Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS).
[
108
]
proposes a novel AuxMobLCast pipeline that leverages LLM for transportation data mining, such as for human mobility forecasting tasks. AuxMobLCast transfers the human mobility data into natural language sentences so that the pre-trained LLM can be directly applied in the fine-tuning phase for predicting human mobility. This work presents the first attempt to fine-tune existing LLMs for forecasting data in the transportation domain.

Finance.
Several works of LLMs specialized in the financial domain have been reported in literature recently.
[
68
]
introduces a simple yet effective instruction tuning approach for sentiment analysis in the finance sector. Through this approach, the classification based sentiment analysis dataset is transformed into a generation task, thereby allowing LLMs to apply their extensive training and superior analytical capabilities more effectively. Apart from the NLP tasks, it is tempting to harness LLMs’ outstanding knowledge and reasoning abilities for financial time series forecasting.
[
109
]
experiments with zero-shot/few-shot inference with GPT-4 and instruction-based fine-tuning with Llama
[
71
]
to generate explainable forecasts, which achieved reasonable performance, albeit relatively inferior in comparison to GPT-4. Concurrent works
[
110
,
68
]
use similar approaches to predict the stock price movement based on textual data.

Event Prediction.
In contrast to synchronous (regular) time series data with equal sampling intervals, event sequence is asynchronous time series with irregular timestamps. Event sequence plays a vital role in real-world domains such as finance
[
153
]
, online shopping
[
154
]
, social networks
[
155
]
, and so on. Temporal point processes (TPPs)
[
156
,
157
,
158
,
159
]
have emerged as the standard method to model such data. Event prediction aims to predict the time and the type of future events given the past. For example, in the online shopping domain, we would like to model users sequences of time-stamped visits and predict their future shopping behavior given their past shopping reviews. LLMs are potentially useful in such settings because event sequences are often accompanied with rich text information which they excel at handling.
[
112
]
proposes a modeling framework where an LLM performs abductive reasoning to assist an event sequence model: the event model proposes predictions on future events; instructed by a few expert-annotated prompts, the LLM learns to suggest possible causes for each proposal; a search module finds out the previous events that match the causes; a scoring function learns to examine whether the retrieved events could actually cause the proposal. A similar example is
[
148
]
for LLM-based human mobility prediction.
[
147
]
explores the ability of LLMs to generate coherent, varied sequences of events in natural language that overlap substantially with human-curated events across several domains of interest. It tries to pave the way for future efforts looking at how explicit knowledge like schemas can be used in tandem with LLMs to make event predictions.

Healthcare.
This is one of the most important and applicable domains of event sequence. By forecasting the clinical events, i.e., patients’ sequences of time-stamped hospital visits with their symptoms, the clinical models can help physicians and administrators make decisions in everyday practice. Existing structured data-based clinical models have limited use owing to the complexity of data processing, as well as model development and deployment.
[
59
]
develops from scratch a clinical LLM — GatorTron — using more than
90
billion words of text from unstructured electronic health records (EHRs). GatorTron models scale up the clinical LLM to
8.9
billion parameters and improve five clinical NLP tasks. As a concurrent work,
[
58
]
trains a clinical LLM (NYUTron) based on NYU Langone EHRs and subsequently fine-tunes it across a wide range of clinical and operational predictive tasks. The results that NYUTron brings significant improvement over traditional models, show the potential for using clinical LLM in medicine to read alongside physicians and provide guidance at the point of care.
[
113
]
systematically demonstrates that with few-shot prompt tuning, LLMs can ground numerical time series data, derived from wearable and clinical-grade sensing devices, resulting in large improvements over zero-shot inferences and supervised baselines on health tasks. In clinical practice, the electrocardiogram (ECG) is one of the most commonly used non-invasive, convenient medical monitoring tools that assist in the diagnosis of heart diseases. To overcome the challenge of limited annotated data,
[
114
]
utilizes the auto-generated clinical reports from LLMs to guide ECG self-supervised pre-training framework.

IV-B

Pre-Trained Foundation Models in Time Series

Besides harnessing LLMs for time series analysis, the development of time series pre-training and related foundational models holds promise. They aid in discerning general time series patterns that are consistent across various domains and subsequent tasks. In this subsection, we explore PFM4TS, distinguishing between general-purpose and domain-specific models that similar in
subsection IV-A
.

IV-B
1
General Models

Since 2021, plenty of pre-trained foundation models have been proposed among which most of them are general purposed. Voice2Series
[
56
]
leverages the representation learning power of pre-trained speech processing models for time series classification using voice data as univariate temporal signals, and it is the first framework that enables reprogramming for time series tasks.

After Voice2Series, there emerged several pre-trained foundation models for time series data based on contrastive learning techniques, including TF-C
[
37
]
, TS2Vec
[
118
]
and CLUDA
[
119
]
. TF-C
[
37
]
contains a time-based component and a frequency-based component, and each component is individually trained by contrastive estimation, and the self-supervised signal is provided by the distance between time and frequency components, i.e., time-frequency consistency. TS2Vec
[
118
]
proposes a universal contrastive learning framework for learning contextual representations for arbitrary sub-series at various semantic levels in the time series domain through a hierarchical way over augmented context views. The framework supports multivariate input and can be used for various tasks in the time series domain. CLUDA
[
119
]
is an unsupervised domain adaptation model for time series based on contrastive learning. There are two novel components in CLUDA, custom contrastive learning and nearest-neighbor contrastive learning. The adversarial learning is used to align them across source and target domains. The contrastive learning component of CLUDA aims to learn a representation space where semantically similar samples are closer together and dissimilar samples are farther apart. Thus, CLUDA can learn domain-invariant contextual representations in multivariate time series for domain adaptation.

Besides these, many other techniques are also used in this area. STEP
[
39
]
model contains a pre-training model and a spatio-temporal graph neural network (STGNN). The pre-training model is designed to efficiently learn temporal patterns from very long-term historical time series and generate segment-level representations. These representations provide contextual information for short-term time series input to STGNNs and facilitate modeling dependencies between time series. MTSMAE
[
115
]
is a self-supervised pre-training approach for multivariate time series forecasting. The masked autoencoders (MAE)–based pre-training method involves a new patch embedding that reduces memory usage and allows the model to process longer sequences to address the challenge of processing multivariate time series with high information density. SimMTM
[
38
]
, which is also based on the masked modeling technique, extends pre-training methods to time series by uncovering the local structure of the manifold. To improve the performance of masked modeling, the masked time points are recovered by weighted aggregation of multiple neighbors outside the manifold, which allows SimMTM to assemble complementary temporal variations from multiple masked series and improve the quality of the reconstructed series. PatchTST
[
116
]
is a transformer-based model for long-term time series forecasting. To overcome the limitations of other models in capturing local semantic information, PatchTST introduces a patching mechanism that extracts local semantic information and a channel-independence design that allows each series to learn its own attention map for forecasting. It is also applied to pre-trained tasks. TSMixer
[
117
]
is a lightweight MLP-Mixer model for
multivariate time series forecasting. It introduces two novel online reconciliation heads to tune and improve the forecasts by leveraging hierarchical patch-aggregation’s intrinsic time series properties and cross-channel correlation. Different from transformer-based models, TSMixer significantly enhances the learning capability of simple MLP structures.

IV-B
2
Domain-Specific Models

PromptTPP
[
120
]
proposes a general method to pre-train a foundation model of event sequence and addresses the problem of continuous model monitoring by adopting continual learning (CL), which makes it capable of continuously learning a sequence of tasks without catastrophic forgetting under realistic constraints. Correspondingly, PromptTPP integrates the foundation model with a continuous-time retrieval prompt pool. The prompts, small learnable parameters, are stored in a memory space and jointly optimized with the foundation model, ensuring that the model learns event streams sequentially without buffering past examples or task-specific attributes.

V
Large Models for Spatio-Temporal Data

In this section, we examine the advancements of large models in spatio-temporal data analysis across three primary data categories: spatio-temporal graphs, temporal knowledge graphs, and videos. Each possesses a broad spectrum of real-world applications. We also delve deeper into the literature organized by model types and scopes (see
Figure 3
).

V-A

Spatio-Temporal Graphs

Individuals enter a planet imbued with inherent structure, where constituent elements engage in dynamic interactions across both spatial and temporal dimensions, culminating in a profound spatio-temporal composition. The conceptualization of this structural fabric within real-world problem-solving has been significantly enriched through the incorporation of
spatio-temporal graphs
(STGs)
[
32
,
20
]
. The application of STG forecasting, such as traffic prediction
[
160
,
161
,
162
]
, air quality forecasting
[
163
,
164
,
165
]
, stock price estimation
[
166
,
167
]
, and human skeleton analysis
[
168
,
121
]
, has evolved into an indispensable tool for fostering informed decision-making and advancing sustainability objectives. In the era of deep learning,
spatio-temporal graph neural networks
(STGNNs)
[
32
,
20
]
have emerged as the de-facto most popular approach for STG forecasting. They primarily use graph neural networks to capture spatial correlations among vertices and exploit other models (e.g., RNNs and CNNs) to learn temporal dependencies across different time steps.

Recently, the emergence of LLMs and PFMs has provided invaluable support to STGNNs within the domain of spatio-temporal representation learning. These models excel in processing and contextualizing textual data, equipping them with the capability to distill insights from diverse textual sources, ranging from news articles and social media content to reports. These insights can be seamlessly integrated into the spatio-temporal fabric, enhancing its contextual richness. Moreover, they facilitate the fusion of multiple modalities, including text, images, and structured data, thus amplifying the depth and breadth of spatio-temporal comprehension. With their capacity to generate human-interpretable explanations, these models foster transparency and reliability, especially in applications like urban planning or disaster response. Furthermore, they contribute to computational efficiency by encoding high-level information in concise representations, streamlining both training and inference processes.

V-A
1
Large Language Models in Spatio-Temporal Graphs

In contrast to PFMs, there exists a relatively limited body of literature dedicated to harnessing LLMs to enhance the learning capabilities of STGNNs. The first category is using LLMs to learn the relationships between vertices. An early study can be found in
[
122
]
, which proposes a novel framework that capitalizes on LLM’s admirable graph inference capabilities to enhance STGNNs for the prediction of stock movements. In this method, ChatGPT is used to distill evolving network structures from daily financial news, and these inferred graph structures are then seamlessly integrated into GNNs to generate node embeddings for each company. These embeddings, in turn, prove instrumental in enhancing the performance of downstream tasks related to stock movements. Besides, the second line of research leverages the prior knowledge of LLMs to bolster STGNNs for downstream applications, such as LA-GCN
[
121
]
for human action recognition. In LA-GCN, LLM-derived knowledge is translated into a priori global relationship (GPR) topology and a priori category relationship (CPR) topology, defining the interconnections between nodes. The GPR serves as a guiding framework for the generation of novel bone representations, with a primary objective of accentuating critical node information extracted from the data at the foundational level. In summary, LLMs have great potential in boosting STGNNs, by elevating their accuracy, contextual acumen, and semantic profundity in the pursuit of spatio-temporal representation learning.

V-A
2
Pre-Trained Foundation Models in Spatio-Temporal Graphs

We are currently witnessing a proliferation of pre-trained machine learning models tailored for mastering STGs across an expansive spectrum of real-world applications. They significantly aid STG learning through knowledge transfer. In other words, they enable the transfer of learned representations, thereby improving the ability of STGs to capture intricate patterns, leading to more effective and efficient learning. In the subsequent parts, we introduce PFMs in both the general and specific domains.

General Purposes.
Contrastive learning
[
169
]
, a well-established method for representation learning in the domains of images and text, has also proven to be highly effective in the realm of STG learning. A representative approach is STGCL
[
149
]
, which facilitates the extraction of rich and meaningful representations from complex STG data by contrasting positive pairs against negative pairs, enabling real-world applications in diverse fields such as traffic forecasting and power consumption prediction. To learn informative relations among vertices, SPGCL
[
150
]
maximizes the distinguishing margin between positive and negative neighbors and generates an optimal graph with a self-paced strategy.

Climate.
In recent years, there has been a burgeoning fascination with the advancement of AI-based climate forecasting models. These models harness the capabilities of deep learning algorithms to meticulously analyze extensive troves of meteorological data, extracting intricate patterns crucial for precise weather prediction
[
170
,
171
]
. The adoption of a pre-training strategy has gained prominence in this domain as well, aiming to enhance the generalization capabilities of AI-based climate models, as exemplified in
[
172
]
. Considering the imperative factors of computational efficiency and the demand for timely climate forecasting, Pathak et al. introduce FourCastNet
[
136
]
, a climate PFM that utilizes adaptive Fourier neural operators to achieve high-resolution predictions and fast inference. Its training process comprises two stages – pre-training and fine-tuning. During pre-training, FourCastNet is supervisedly trained to acquire the mapping from the preceding step to the subsequent one. In fine-tuning, it builds upon the pre-trained model, refining it to predict the next two time steps in an autoregressive manner. PanGu
[
50
]
further demonstrates stronger medium-range forecasting capabilities than FourCastNet through a multi-timescale model combination approach, by leveraging a novel 3D Earth-Specific Transformer. This 3D deep network, enhanced with Earth-specific priors, effectively handles intricate weather data patterns. ClimaX
[
51
]
is an adaptable deep learning model for weather and climate science, trained on diverse datasets. It extends the Transformer architecture with innovative components, optimizing computational efficiency. Initially pre-trained on CMIP6 climate data, ClimaX can be fine-tuned for various climate and weather tasks, even involving unseen variables and spatio-temporal scales. W-MAE
[
141
]
incorporates self-supervised pre-training methods (i.e., Masked Autoencoder
[
173
]
) into climate forecasting tasks. This integration empowers models to extract essential meteorological-related features and general knowledge from extensive, unlabeled meteorological data, facilitating improved handling of diverse data sources. Different from the above approaches, FengWu
[
139
]
addresses medium-range climate forecasting through a multi-modal and multi-task approach. It employs a deep learning architecture with model-specific Transformers and a cross-modal fusion strategy. This design is carefully crafted, incorporating an uncertainty loss for region-adaptive optimization of various predictors.

Transportation.
CPPBTR
[
142
]
is a new Transformer-based framework for crowd flow prediction, featuring a distinctive two-stage decoding process. In the first decoder stage, a preliminary sequence is generated. Subsequently, in the second decoder stage, each time step of this initial sequence is systematically masked and fed into the Transformer encoder to predict the refined flow for each masked position. In the domain of traffic flow forecasting, TrafficBERT
[
143
]
leverages key characteristics inspired by BERT. It employs a bidirectional transformer structure akin to BERT, allowing it to predict overall traffic flow instead of individual time steps. In contrast to conventional models that require distinct training for each specific road, TrafficBERT enhances model generalization by pre-training it using data from various roads. Furthermore, Wang et al. introduce the Transportation Foundation Model (TFM)
[
52
]
, which incorporates traffic simulation into the realm of traffic prediction. TFM leverages graph structures and dynamic graph generation algorithms to adeptly capture the intricate dynamics and interactions among participants within transportation systems. This data-driven and model-free simulation approach effectively tackles the long-standing challenges posed by conventional systems in terms of structural complexity and model accuracy, laying a robust foundation for addressing intricate transportation issues with real-world data.

V-B

Temporal Knowledge Graphs

A host of applications such as search engines, question-answering systems, conversational dialogue systems, and social networks require reasoning over underlying structured knowledge. In particular,
knowledge graphs
(KGs)
[
174
,
175
]
have gained much attention as an important model for studying complex multi-relational settings over such knowledge. KGs represent facts (events), usually extracted from text data, in the form of triples
(s,p,o)
where
s
and
o
denote subject and object entities respectively, and
p
as a relation type means predicate. However, in the real world, knowledge evolves continuously, inspiring the construction and application of the
temporal knowledge graphs
(TKGs)
[
176
,
177
]
, where the fact extends from a triple
(s,p,o)
to a quadruple with a timestamp
t
, i.e.,
(s,p,o,t)
. By effectively capturing temporal dependencies across facts in addition to the structural dependencies, TKGs help improve the understanding on the behavior of entities and how they contribute to the generation of facts over time.

As recently LLMs
[
76
]
have shown astonishing performance on a number of text-reasoning tasks, it is natural to take the initiative to investigate the effectiveness of LLMs in TKGs. Based on the tasks performed, LLM-based TKG models can be divided into two categories: forecasting and completion. TKG forecasting aims to predict future facts using knowledge of past facts.
[
125
]
approaches the problem by using in-context learning (ICL) of LLMs. The prediction is generated by LLMs in a zero-shot manner with the historical facts constructed as prompts. Note that
[
112
]
also covers event prediction tasks in TKGs, evaluated on GDELT dataset
[
178
]
. Please see
section IV
for more details on the methodology. The completion is also an important task because TKGs are known for their incompleteness. PPT
[
123
]
is a novel PLM-based model with prompts to solve the completion task. Different prompts for entities (i.e., ent-prompts), relations (i.e., rel-prompts), and timestamps (i.e., time-prompts) are constructed to convert quadruples into a form suitable for input to PLMs and then make the prediction for the mask. By this way, the TKG completion task is converted into a PLM model masked token prediction task.
[
124
]
represents the first attempt to apply LLMs to the completion tasks in e-commerce contexts, where it proposes to label the predicate of e-commerce KGs by prompting LLMs such as GPT-3.5 and PaLM.

Videos

Video refers to the digital representation of visual information, typically comprised of a sequence of images or frames that collectively convey motion and temporal changes. These data have become ubiquitous in various real-world applications, including surveillance systems, entertainment platforms, social media, and driver assistance systems
[
179
]
. Conventional deep learning approaches for video understanding primarily involve two key paradigms: (1) 2D CNNs in which each video frame is processed separately by 2D convolutions and then aggregated along the time axis at the top of the network
[
180
,
181
]
; (2) 3D CNNs which learn spatio-temporal video representation via 3D convolutions by aggregating spatial and temporal features
[
182
,
183
,
184
,
185

. Recently, Transformers have also been widely used in modeling spatio-temporal dependencies for video recognition
[
186
,

188

by virtue of their capability in capturing long-range dependencies.

However, recent advances in LLMs and PFMs have paved the way for enhanced video understanding by leveraging the inherent multimodal nature of videos. These models, such as OpenAI’s CLIP
[
190
]
and DALL-E
[
191
]
, can effectively extract rich contextual information from video data by jointly processing visual and textual modalities, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of complex scenes and events. Furthermore, these models have the potential to facilitate efficient transfer learning across diverse domains, leading to improved generalization and robustness in video analysis tasks. We will discuss LLMs and PFMs for video understanding in the following parts.

V-C
1
Large Language Models for Video Data

As previously noted, current video understanding models frequently exhibit task-specific limitations and do not possess a comprehensive capacity for managing a variety of tasks. Recent advancements in LLMs have facilitated the application of sequence reasoning capabilities, derived from pre-trained LLMs in natural language processing, to various video processing tasks as demonstrated in LAVENDER
[
135
]
. Unlike conventional LLMs that take words as inputs and are pre-trained on extensive natural language datasets comprising word sequences, VideoLLM
[
133
]
encodes video streams into token sequences and employs pre-trained LLMs for video understanding tasks. This approach contains a meticulously designed Modality Encoder and Semantic Translator, which facilitate the conversion of inputs from diverse modalities into a unified token sequence. This sequence is subsequently input into a decoder-only LLM, and with the assistance of a straightforward task head, VideoLLM generates an efficient, unified framework applicable to various video understanding tasks. Video-ChatGPT
[
151
]
is a multimodal model tailored for video-based conversation. It combines a visual encoder adapted for processing video content with LLMs, enabling the model to comprehend and generate in-depth discussions related to videos. In contrast to the above approaches that rely on language data, Zhang et al. propose Video-LLaMA
[
134
]
, a multi-modal framework that operates independently of textual data. Video-LLaMA empowers Llama
[
71
]
with visual and audio information via Q-Former to endow it with the ability to comprehend both visual and auditory information present in videos. To ensure alignment between the output of the visual and audio encoders and LLM’s embedding space, Video-LLaMA is trained on an extensive corpus of video/image-caption pairs, as well as visual-instruction-tuning datasets of moderate size but with superior quality. Valley
[
53
]
, a video assistant With LLMs, employs a spatio-temporal pooling strategy to extract a unified visual encoding from video and image inputs. It also curates a vast corpus of vision-text pairs for pre-training purposes, and subsequently generates a multi-task instruction-following video dataset, aided by ChatGPT for conversation design.

Meanwhile, a series of research literature focuses on video question answering (VQA) using LLMs. FrozenBiLM
[
127
]
utilizes frozen bidirectional LLMs for learning multi-modal inputs and enables zero-shot VQA via masked language modeling. Su et al. propose a question-answer generation framework termed CaKE-LM
[
131
]
to extract causal common-sense knowledge from LLMs for causal VQA. VideoChat
[
129
]
proposes to merge specific elements from video PFMs
[
192
]
and image PFMs
[
84
]
and integrates them with LLMs
[
74
]
using a few adaptable layers. This integration is fine-tuned through a two-stage lightweight training process. R2A (Retrieving-to-Answer)
[
128
]
is a two-stage VQA framework. In the first stage, it retrieves a collection of semantically similar texts from a generic text corpus, employing a pre-trained multi-modal model, e.g., CLIP
[
193
]
. In the second stage, using both the original question and the retrieved texts, LLMs are directly applied to generate the desired answer. In traffic-domain VQA, Qasemi et al. introduce an innovative automatic captioning method called TRIVIA
[
152
]
, which serves as a form of weak supervision to incorporate traffic-domain knowledge into extensive video-language models.

The utilization of web-scale image-text data for learning visual representations has proven to be a potent instrument in the realm of computer vision. Nevertheless, progress has been constrained by the relatively limited size of paired video-text corpora in comparison to billion-scale image-text datasets. To overcome this challenge, LAVILA
[
54
]
introduces a novel methodology for acquiring video-language representations by capitalizing on the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). LAVILA repurposes pre-trained LLMs to be conditioned on visual input and subsequently fine-tunes them, resulting in the creation of automated video narrators. The auto-generated narrations proffer numerous benefits, such as comprehensive coverage of extended videos, enhanced temporal synchronization between visual information and text, and a significantly increased diversity of textual content.

VII
Outlook and Future Opportunities

In this section, we discuss the potential limitations of current research and highlight the top six future research directions aimed at developing more powerful, transparent, and reliable large models for temporal data analysis.

VII-A

Theoretical Analysis of Large Models

Large models, especially large language models, are mainly designed for natural language processing with their ability to capture long-term dependencies in sequences.
Due to the sequential nature of text data and temporal data, recent research has extended LLMs to handle time series and spatio-temporal tasks. This means that the learned representations from text data of LLMs can be fine-tuned to capture the patterns of temporal data. However, this is still a very high-level understanding.
Like other deep learning models, LLMs are also ”black boxes” due to their complexity, making it hard to understand what data influenced their prediction and decisions.
More deep theoretical analyses of LLMs for temporal data need to be investigated, which involves understanding the underlying pattern similarities between language and temporal data and how to effectively use them for specific time series and spatio-temporal tasks like forecasting, anomaly detection, classification, and so on.

VII-B

Development of Multimodal Models

Many time series and spatio-temporal data in real-world applications often come with supplementary information like text descriptions. This is especially useful in the scenarios like economics and finance areas. For example, economic forecasting can be performed by leveraging information from both textual (like news articles or tweets) and numerical economic time series data.
Therefore, LLMs can be adapted to learn joint representation that considers the sequential nature of temporal data and the unique characteristics of other modalities.
Furthermore, different modalities may have different temporal resolutions. LLMs can be adapted to handle temporal data disparities from multiple modalities with varying temporal resolutions. Therefore, all information from different temporal resolutions can be fully leveraged for better performance.

VII-C

Continuous Learning and Adaptation

Real-world applications often face continuously changing scenarios. Therefore, it is necessary to study the ability of the models to adapt to non-stationary environments and avoid the catastrophic forgetting of old knowledge. Although some works have studied these problems in the common machine learning and deep learning models, research on how to enable large models to continuously adapt to changing temporal data, including strategies for online learning, adapting to concept drift, and accommodating evolving patterns in the data, is still an under-explored problem.

VII-D

Interpretability and Explainability

Understanding why a model made a particular forecast or prediction is also crucial for time series analysis, especially in critical domains like healthcare and finance. Currently, the internal understanding of LLMs is still limited. Therefore, it is important to develop theoretical frameworks for understanding what LLMs have learned and how they make predictions when applied to time series data. The interpretability and explainability can make LLMs more transparent, providing rationales for time series analysis, like highlighting why future values are forecasted, how specific points were seen as anomalies, or explaining the reasons behind a particular classification.
Investigate how large models can be enhanced to perform temporal reasoning and infer causality in time series data. This includes developing methods to identify cause-and-effect relationships, which are essential for applications like root cause analysis and intervention planning.

VII-E

Privacy and Adversarial Attacks on Large Models

Temporal data can be highly sensitive, especially in applications like healthcare and finance. When LLMs are trained/fine-tuned on such data, they can memorize specific details from the training data, posing the risk of leaking private data. Therefore, there are a lot of research opportunities on how to leverage privacy-preserving techniques, like differential privacy and federated learning, to ensure data privacy while still benefiting from the power of LLMs for time series and spatio-temporal analysis.

VII-F

Model Generalization and Vulnerabilities

LLMs are often pre-trained on general data and then fine-tuned on specific tasks. This process can introduce vulnerabilities if the fine-tuning data contains adversarial or noisy examples. If the temporal data used for fine-tuning is not curated carefully, the model might inherit biases or vulnerabilities from this data, leading to compromised robustness in real-world applications.
Furthermore, while LLMs are trained on vast datasets, they might not generalize well to unseen or out-of-distribution data. Time series and spatio-temporal data can exhibit sudden shifts or trends. If an LLM hasn’t encountered similar patterns during training, it might produce unreliable outputs, emphasizing the need for robust generalization.

VIII
Conclusion

We present an extensive and up-to-date survey of large models tailored for the analysis of time series and spatio-temporal data. Our aim is to offer a fresh perspective on this dynamic field by introducing a novel taxonomy that categorizes the reviewed models. We go beyond mere description by summarizing the most prominent techniques in each category, delving into their strengths and limitations, and finally, illuminating promising avenues for future research. Within this exciting topic, the scope for groundbreaking investigations is boundless. This survey will serve as a catalyst for stimulating further curiosity and fostering an enduring passion for research in the realm of large models for time series and spatio-temporal data analysis.
