Abstract
. The two squares theorem of Fermat is a gem in number theory, with a spectacular one-sentence “proof from the Book”. Here is a formalisation of this proof, with an interpretation using windmill patterns. The theory behind involves involutions on a finite set, especially the parity of the number of fixed points in the involutions. Starting as an existence proof that is non-constructive, there is an ingenious way to turn it into a constructive one. This gives an algorithm to compute the two squares by iterating the two involutions alternatively from a known fixed point.



1.
Introduction

Fermat’s two squares theorem, dated back to 1640, states that a prime
n
that is one more than a multiple of
4
can be uniquely expressed as a sum of odd and even squares (Section
2
, Theorem
2.1
).
Of the many proofs of this classical number theory result, this one-sentence proof by Zagier
(
Zagier 1990
)
caused a sensation in 1990:

The involution on the finite set

S=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}\mid\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{x}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;{\textrm{4}}{\textsf{$$}}\mathit{y}{\textsf{$$}}\mathit{z}\}

defined by

(1)

(\mathit{x}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{y}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{z})\longmapsto\begin{cases}(x+2z,z,y-z-x)&\text{if }x<y-z\\
(2y-x,y,x+z-y)&\text{if }y-z<x<2y\\
(x-2y,x+z-y,y)&\text{if }x>2y\end{cases}

has exactly one fixed point, so
|\mathit{S}|
is odd, and the involution defined by

(\mathit{x}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{y}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{z})\longmapsto(\mathit{x}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{z}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{y})

also has a fixed point.

Those who are perplexed by this multi-line sentence are not alone.
Even knowing involution, a self-inverse function, and fixed points, those values unchanged by a function, the proof is not obvious at a glance!

Listed as number 20 in Formalizing 100 Theorems
(
Wiedijk 2020
)
,
there are many formal proofs of this theorem.
Some are based on textbook proofs, others follow the ideas in Zagier’s proof.
All show the existence of the two squares, only a few (Coq
(
Théry 2004
)
and Lean
(
Hughes 2019
)
) include the uniqueness part.
Therefore, a formalisation of this one-sentence proof, in a constructive way, is an interesting exercise in theorem-proving.
As a bonus, the exercise is a path of discovery due to recent progress in understanding this proof.

As Don Zagier remarked after the one sentence, his proof was a condensed version of a 1984 proof by Roger Heath-Brown
(
Heath-Brown 1984
)
, who in turn acknowledged prior work in number theory taken up by Joseph Liouville
(
Williams 2010
)
.
This one-sentence proof invokes two involutions: the second one is obvious, but the first one in Equation (
1
) has been called “black magic”
(
Trimble and Lama 2008
)
. The algebraic formulation of this involution has been given a geometric interpretation by Alexander Spivak
(
Spivak 2007
)
in 2007. These are the windmills (Section
2.1
). They explain why the magic works, and suggest an interplay of the involutions to identify fixed points of each other. Moreover, this provides an algorithm to find the two squares in Fermat’s theorem.
Thus the one-sentence proof can be made constructive, as elucidated by Zagier
(
Malter
et al. 2013
)
in 2013.

1.1.
Contribution

This paper gives the first formal proof of an algorithm to compute the two squares in Fermat’s two squares theorem, by following a constructive version of Zagier’s proof in HOL4.

As noted before, Zagier’s proof has been formalised, in HOL Light
(
Harrison 2010
)
, in NASA PVS
(
Narkawicz 2012
)
and in Coq
(

All the ideas used in this paper can be found in Shiu

)
and Zagier
(

et al. 2013
)

The novel feature of this work is an elegant and pictorial approach for our formalisation.
The emphasis is in providing formal definitions and developing appropriate theories, not only for the present work, but also for supporting further work.

2.2.
Involution

We are going to study involutions on
{\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n}
, the set of windmills for
n
.
A function
f
is an involution on a set
S
, denoted by
\mathit{f}\;{\textsf{involute}}\;\mathit{S}
, when it is its own inverse:

\begin{array}[]{l}\;\;\mathit{f}\;{\textsf{involute}}\;\mathit{S}\;\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}{}\;{\textsf{$\forall\,${}}}\mathit{x}.\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\in${}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\;\mathit{f}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\in${}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{f}\;(\mathit{f}\;\mathit{x})\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{x}\end{array}

That is,
f
is a bijection
\mathit{f}\;:\;\mathit{S}\;\leftrightarrow\;\mathit{S}
, pairing up
x
and
\mathit{f}\;\mathit{x}
, both in
S
. When
\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{f}\;\mathit{x}
, the element

is fixed by the involution

We define the following sets:

Definition 2.6.

The pairs and fixes of an involution
f
on a set

.

\begin{array}[]{l}\;\;{\textsf{pairs}}\;\mathit{f}\;\mathit{S}\;\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}{}\;\left\{\right.{}\mathit{x}\;\mid{}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\in${}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{f}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\neq${}}}\;\mathit{x}\left.\right\}\\
\;\;{\textsf{fixes}}\;\mathit{f}\;\mathit{S}\;\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}{}\;\left\{\right.{}\mathit{x}\;\mid{}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\in${}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{f}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{x}\left.\right\}\\
\end{array}

Clearly they are disjoint.
The subset
{\textsf{pairs}}\;\mathit{f}\;\mathit{S}
consists of distinct involute pairs, so its cardinality is even:

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}{\textsf{{{finite}}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{f}\;{\textsf{involute}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\;{\textsf{{{even}}}}\;|{\textsf{pairs}}\;\mathit{f}\;\mathit{S}|\end{array}

So both
|\mathit{S}|
and
|{\textsf{fixes}}\;\mathit{f}\;\mathit{S}|
have the same parity. This leads to:

Theorem 2.7.

[script]

If two involutions act on the same finite set

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}{\textsf{{{finite}}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{f}\;{\textsf{involute}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{g}\;{\textsf{involute}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;({\textsf{{{odd}}}}\;|{\textsf{fixes}}\;\mathit{f}\;\mathit{S}|\;{\textsf{$\iff${}}}\;{\textsf{{{odd}}}}\;|{\textsf{fixes}}\;\mathit{g}\;\mathit{S}|)\end{array}

We shall meet the two involutions on
{\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n}
, a set which is finite for non-square
n
(by Theorem
2.4

4.
Two Squares Theorem

Now we have enough tools to formalise Fermat’s two squares theorem.

4.1.
Existence of Two Squares

For the Zagier map,
it is straightforward to verify, as indicated in Table
3
, that only a triple of case
3
can map to itself:

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\neq${}}}\;{\textrm{0}}\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\;({\textsf{zagier}}\;(\mathit{x}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{y}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{z})\;{\textsf{=}}\;(\mathit{x}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{y}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{z})\;{\textsf{$\iff${}}}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{y})\end{array}

Hence
S_{x\ {\textsf{=}}\ y}\ {\textsf{=}}\ {\textsf{fixes}}\;{\textsf{zagier}}\;({\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n})
.
Applying Theorem
2.5
which characterises such triples, for certain primes
S_{x\ {\textsf{=}}\ y}
is a singleton:

Theorem 4.1.

[script]

A prime of the form
{\textrm{4}}{\textsf{$$}}\mathit{k}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;{\textrm{1}}
has only
(1,1,k)
fixed by the Zagier map.

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}{\textsf{prime}}\;\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{n}\;\equiv\;{\textrm{1}}\;(\bmod\;{\textrm{4}})\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;{\textsf{fixes}}\;{\textsf{zagier}}\;({\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n})\;{\textsf{=}}\;\left\{\right.{}({\textrm{1}}{\textsf{,}}{\textrm{1}}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{{{div}}}}\;{\textrm{4}})\left.\right\}\end{array}

The fixed points of two involutions play crucial roles in the existence of two squares for Theorem
2.1
:

Theorem 4.2 (
Two Squares Existence
).

[script]

A prime of the form
{\textrm{4}}{\textsf{$$}}\mathit{k}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;{\textrm{1}}
is a sum of two squares of different parity.

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}{\textsf{prime}}\;\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{n}\;\equiv\;{\textrm{1}}\;(\bmod\;{\textrm{4}})\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;{\textsf{$\exists\,${}}}(\mathit{u}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{v}).\;{\textsf{{{odd}}}}\;\mathit{u}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;{\textsf{{{even}}}}\;\mathit{v}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{u}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;\mathit{v}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\end{array}

Proof.

A prime is not a square, so
{\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n}
is finite by Theorem
2.4
.
, and both Zagier and flip maps are involutions on
{\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n}
,
by Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.2
.
Note that Zagier map has a single fixed point by Theorem
4.1
.
Thus
|{\textsf{fixes}}\;{\textsf{zagier}}\;({\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n})|\;{\textsf{=}}\;{\textrm{1}}
,
so
|{\textsf{fixes}}\;{\textsf{flip}}\;({\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n})|
is odd by Theorem
2.7
.
Hence
{\textsf{fixes}}\;{\textsf{flip}}\;({\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n})\;{\textsf{$\neq${}}}\;{\textsf{$\emptyset${}}}
, containing a triple
(x,y,y)
.
Thus
\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{=}}\;{\textsf{windmill}}\;\mathit{x}\;\mathit{y}\;\mathit{y}

=

\mathit{x}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;{\textrm{4}}{\textsf{$$}}\mathit{y}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}
.
Take
\mathit{u}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{x}
, and
\mathit{v}\;{\textsf{=}}\;{\textrm{2}}{\textsf{$$}}\mathit{y}
, then
\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{u}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;\mathit{v}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}
.
Evidently

is even, and

4.2.
Uniqueness of Two Squares

The uniqueness of the two squares in Fermat’s Theorem
2.1

is a consequence of the following property of a prime:

Theorem 4.3 (
Two Squares Uniquenss
).

[script]

If a prime
n
can be expressed as a sum of two squares, the expression is unique up to commutativity.

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}{\textsf{prime}}\;\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{a}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;\mathit{b}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{c}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;\mathit{d}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\left\{\right.{}\mathit{a};\;\mathit{b}\left.\right\}{}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\left\{\right.{}\mathit{c};\;\mathit{d}\left.\right\}\end{array}

The proof is purely number-theoretic, which has also been formalised by Laurent Théry in Coq
(
Théry 2004
)
.
Moreover, we have:

Theorem 4.4.

[script]

A number of the form
{\textrm{4}}{\textsf{$$}}\mathit{k}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;{\textrm{3}}
cannot be expressed as a sum of two squares.

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}\mathit{n}\;\equiv\;{\textrm{3}}\;(\bmod\;{\textrm{4}})\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\;{\textsf{$\forall\,${}}}\mathit{u}\;\mathit{v}.\;\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{$\neq${}}}\;\mathit{u}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;\mathit{v}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\end{array}

This is an elementary result from possible remainders after division by 4:
while a number, such as
u
or
v
, may have a remainer
0,1,2
, or
3
,
a square, such as
u^{2}
or
v^{2}
, can only have a remainder
0
or
1
.
Thus the sum of such remainders can never be
3
.

Now we can complete the proof of Fermat’s two squares Theorem

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}{\textsf{prime}}\;\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;(\mathit{n}\;\equiv\;{\textrm{1}}\;(\bmod\;{\textrm{4}})\;{\textsf{$\iff${}}}\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;{\textsf{$\exists$!{}}}(\mathit{u}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{v}).\;{\textsf{{{odd}}}}\;\mathit{u}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;{\textsf{{{even}}}}\;\mathit{v}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{u}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}}\;{\textsf{$+$}}\;\mathit{v}{\textsf{${}^{2}$}})\end{array}

Proof.

For the only-if part

cannot be a sum of two squares by Theorem

6.
Permutation Orbits

In general, the composition of two involutions is no longer an involution, but just a permutation.
Let
\varphi\colon S\rightarrow S
be a permutation,
a bijection on the set
S
, denoted by
\mathit{\varphi}\;{\textsf{{{permutes}}}}\;\mathit{S}
.
For an element
\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\in${}}}\;\mathit{S}

the iteration sequence
\varphi(x)
,
\mathit{\varphi}^{{\textrm{2}}}(\mathit{x})
,
\mathit{\varphi}^{{\textrm{3}}}(\mathit{x})
,
etc.
, form its
orbit
.
The smallest positive index
n
such that
\mathit{\varphi}^{\mathit{n}}(\mathit{x})\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{x}
is called the
period
of
x
under
\varphi
.
If such a positive index does not exist, the period is defined to be
0
.
In HOL4, the definition makes use of
OLEAST
, the optional
LEAST
operator:

Definition 6.1.

The period of function iteration of an element is the least nonzero index for the element iterate to wrap around, otherwise zero.

\begin{array}[]{l}\;\;{\textsf{{{period}}}}\;\mathit{\varphi}\;\mathit{x}\;\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny def}}}{=}}\\
\;\;\;\;\mbox{{{case}}}\;{\textsf{OLEAST}}\;\mathit{k}.\;{\textrm{0}}\;{\textsf{$<${}}}\;\mathit{k}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{\varphi}^{\mathit{k}}(\mathit{x})\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{x}\;\mbox{{{of}}}\\
\;\;\;\;\mid{}\;{\textsf{{{none}}}}\;\triangleright\;{\textrm{0}}\\
\;\;\;\;\mid{}\;{\textsf{{{some}}}}\;\mathit{k}\;\triangleright\;\mathit{k}\end{array}

When the set
S
is finite, the iterates cannot be always distinct.
Thus the permutation orbit of any
x\in S
is finite, with a nonzero period,
denoted by
\mathit{p}\;{\textsf{=}}\;{\textsf{period}}\;\mathit{\varphi}\;\mathit{x}
:

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}{\textsf{{{finite}}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{\varphi}\;{\textsf{{{permutes}}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\in${}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;{\textsf{$\exists\,${}}}\mathit{p}.\;{\textrm{0}}\;{\textsf{$<${}}}\;\mathit{p}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{p}\;{\textsf{=}}\;{\textsf{{{period}}}}\;\mathit{\varphi}\;\mathit{x}\end{array}

and by definition the period is minimal, which means that there is no wrap around for element iterates when the index is less than the period:

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}{\textrm{0}}\;{\textsf{$<${}}}\;\mathit{j}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{j}\;{\textsf{$<${}}}\;{\textsf{{{period}}}}\;\mathit{\varphi}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\;\mathit{\varphi}^{\mathit{j}}(\mathit{x})\;{\textsf{$\neq${}}}\;\mathit{x}\end{array}

This implies a criterion for an exponent index to be divisible by period:

Theorem 6.2.

[script]

For a nonzero period
p
of
x
,
x
is fixed by the
k
-th iterate of
\varphi
if and only if
k
is a multiple of period
p
.

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}{\textrm{0}}\;{\textsf{$<${}}}\;\mathit{p}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{p}\;{\textsf{=}}\;{\textsf{{{period}}}}\;\mathit{\varphi}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;(\mathit{\varphi}^{\mathit{k}}(\mathit{x})\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\iff${}}}\;\mathit{k}\;\equiv\;{\textrm{0}}\;(\bmod\;\mathit{p}))\end{array}

Moreover, the period is the same for all iterates in the same orbit:

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}{\textsf{{{finite}}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{\varphi}\;{\textsf{{{permutes}}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\in${}}}\;\mathit{S}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{y}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{\varphi}^{\mathit{j}}(\mathit{x})\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;{\textsf{{{period}}}}\;\mathit{\varphi}\;\mathit{y}\;{\textsf{=}}\;{\textsf{{{period}}}}\;\mathit{\varphi}\;\mathit{x}\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{l}\>\>\vdash{}({\textsf{$\forall\,${}}}\mathit{j}.\;\mathit{j}\;{\textsf{$<${}}}\;\mathit{k}\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\;\mathit{G}\;(\mathit{B}^{\mathit{j}}(\mathit{x})))\;{\textsf{$\Rightarrow${}}}\\

Other algorithms

A prime has a finite set of windmill triples, by Theorem
2.4
.
Fermat’s two squares for a prime
n
with
\mathit{n}\;\equiv\;{\textrm{1}}\;(\bmod\;{\textrm{4}})
, which must exist by Theorem
4.2
,
can be found by a brute-force search: subtract
n
by successive odd squares, and check whether the difference is a square. Although there are better ways to test a square than the square-root test, they are not simple to implement.

Don Zagier, after his one-sentence proof, referred to an effective algorithm by Wagon
(
Wagon 1990
)
to compute the two squares. The algorithm requires finding a quadratic non-residue of the given prime
n
.

The advantage of our algorithm in Definition
7.4
over such alternative methods is that only addition and subtraction are performed.
The implementation is rather straightforward. The issue of termination is discussed next.

7.3.
Terminating Condition

As mentioned in Section
5.1
, for our algorithm the WHILE loop may or may not terminate. To gaurantee termination, convert the WHILE loop to a countdown loop, as follows. First, ensure that the input number
n
is not a square, so that
{\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n}
is finite (Theorem
2.4
), and check
\mathit{n}\;\equiv\;{\textrm{1}}\;(\bmod\;{\textrm{4}})
, so that
({\textrm{1}}{\textsf{,}}{\textrm{1}}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{{{div}}}}\;{\textrm{4}})\;{\textsf{$\in${}}}\;{\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n}
,
i.e.
,
{\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{$\neq${}}}\;{\textsf{$\emptyset${}}}
.

Obviously for any triple
(\mathit{x}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{y}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{z})\;{\textsf{$\in${}}}\;{\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n}
, each
x,y
or
y
is less than
n

|{\textsf{mills}}\;\mathit{n}|\;{\textsf{$<${}}}\;\mathit{n}{\textsf{${}^{3}$}}
.
Now, use a countdown loop from
\mathit{n}{\textsf{${}^{3}$}}
to
0
, start with the triple
({\textrm{1}}{\textsf{,}}{\textrm{1}}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{n}\;{\textsf{{{div}}}}\;{\textrm{4}})
for the
zagier

\circ

The iterations trace an orbit. At half-way point, the orbit hits either a flip fixed point, detected by
\mathit{y}\;{\textsf{=}}\;\mathit{z}
, when the period is odd (Theorem

), or another Zagier fixed point, detected by

7.4.
Lessons Learnt

This formalisation work can be a self-contained project in a theorem-proving workshop.
The ideas are simple, but formulating the theorems properly is not simple.
For example, at first the author would like to prove:

{\textsf{$\forall\,${}}}\mathit{x}\;\mathit{y}\;\mathit{z}.\;{\textsf{zagier}}\;({\textsf{zagier}}\;(\mathit{x}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{y}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{z}))\;{\textsf{=}}\;(\mathit{x}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{y}{\textsf{,}}\mathit{z}).

The interactive session produces several subgoals which he cannot resolve immediately.
A comparison of Definition
3.3
with Equation (
1
)
shows differences in boundary cases.
Finally, some insight from windmills resolves why the boundaries are ignored, and provides the pre-condition
\mathit{x}\;{\textsf{$\neq${}}}\;{\textrm{0}}\;{\textsf{$\wedge${}}}\;\mathit{z}\;{\textsf{$\neq${}}}\;{\textrm{0}}
, see Equation (
4
). The result is Theorem
3.4
.

Explaining the Zagier map is an involution through the mind of a windmill poses some challenges. Definition
3.3
of the Zagier map has
3
branches, so the initial effort is to treat just
3
cases. The first case is immediate, but the second case runs into a mess. It is only after drawing a lot of windmills that the author realises these finer points:

•

there are
3
types:

, and
x>y
for the windmill triple

•

the
3
types are further subdivided due to geometry of the mind, giving
5

•

the
5
cases can be condensed into
3
branches, as the definition shows.

The result is Table
3
in Section
3.3
.

For the permutation orbits in Section
6
,
the proofs about relations between iterates start as long-winded arguments treating if-part and only-if part separately.
Putting them in this paper prompts the author to rethink the logic.
The polished proofs simply employ a chain of logical equivalences.

Fixed point orbits have either even or odd period, as treated in Section
6.3
and Section
6.4
.
The drawing of the diagrams helps to refine the proofs to be short and sweet, making good use of theorems already proved.

About the correctness proof of the algorithm using a while-loop in Section

This is awkward, as pointed out by Michael Norrish who knows the HOL4 theorem-prover inside out.
The reason is that

is

The section had since been rewritten.

7.5.
Related Work

As noted in Section
1
, Fermat’s two squares theorem has been formalised. However, none of these formal proofs is constructive, in the sense that there is no formal proof of an algorithm to compute the two squares for a prime satisfying the theorem.

Fermat’s two squares theorem has two parts: existence and uniqueness.
All formal proofs include the existence part (see Theorem
4.2
) , using classic and modern existence proofs: the method of infinite descent is used in one system, Gaussian integers are employed in three systems, both Heath-Brown’s proof and Zagier’s proof are treated in two systems.

Only two formal proofs include the uniqueness part (see Theorem
4.3
): Théry
(
Théry 2004
)
proved by algebraic identities and divisibility, and Hughes
(
Hughes 2019
)
proved by unique factorisation of Gaussian integers.

Recently, Dubach and Muehlboeck
(
Dubach and
Muehlboeck 2021
)
formalised Zagier’s proof using involutions in Coq’s Mathematical Components Library. They illustrated their proof using the windmills as per this paper, and extended the use of involutions on the same set to formalise also an integer-partition proof of Fermat’s two squares theorem by Christopher
(
Christopher 2016
)
.

8.
Conclusion

About Fermat’s two squares theorem,
G. H. Hardy wrote in his 1940 essay
A Mathematician’s Apology

(
Hardy 1940
, Section 13)
:

This is Fermat’s theorem, which is ranked, very justly, as one of the finest of arithmetic. Unfortunately, there is no proof within the comprehension of anybody but a fairly expert mathematician.

This work has been a rewarding exercise in formalisation,
delivering a proof of Fermat’s Theorem
2.1
using only natural numbers, involutions, and counting.
There is a certain sense of mathematical beauty when a non-trivial result can be shown by elementary means, borrowing elegant ideas by Zagier and Spivak.
Moreover, by developing a theory of involution iteration, an algorithm to compute the two squares of the theorem can be formally shown to be correct.

One can exploit the symmetry in permutation orbits, especially for permutations arising from two involutions, to improve the algorithm, as shown in the analysis by Shiu

In HOL4, this direction can start from the algebra of group theory in Chan and Norrish

A formal analysis of the performance of the algorithm for two squares described in Definition
