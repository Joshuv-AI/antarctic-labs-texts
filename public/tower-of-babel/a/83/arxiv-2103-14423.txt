Introduction

The theory of discrete stochastic systems has been initiated by the work of
Shannon
[
8
]
and von Neumann
[
4
]
.
While Shannon has considered memory-less communication channels and their generalization by introducing states,
von Neumann has studied the synthesis of reliable systems from unreliable components.
The fundamental work of Rabin and Scott
[
6
]
about deterministic finite-state automata
has led to two generalizations.
First, the generalization of transition functions to conditional distributions
studied by Carlyle
[
2
]
and Starke
[
9
]
.
This in turn has led to a generalization of discrete-time Markov chains in which the chains are governed
by more than one transition probability matrix.
Second, the generalization of regular sets by introducing stochastic automata as described by Rabin
[
5
]
.
Stochastic automata are well-investigated
[
3
]
.

This report provides a short introduction to stochastic automata based on the valuable book of Claus
[
3
]
.
This includes the basic topics of the theory of stochastic automata:
equivalence, minimalization, reduction, coverings, observability, and determinism.
Then stochastic versions of Mealy and Moore automata are studied and finally
stochastic language acceptors are considered as a generalization of nondeterministic finite-state acceptors.

Chapter 2
Stochastic Automata

Stochastic automata are abstract machines with input and output behavior.
Such automata are also called transducers.
A stochastic automaton can be viewed as an extension of a nondeterministic automaton with probabilistic transitions.

A
stochastic automaton
(SA) is a quadruple
A=(S,\Sigma,\Omega,p)
, where

•

S
is a nonempty finite set of
states
,

•

\Sigma
is an alphabet of
input symbols
,

•

\Omega
is an alphabet of
output symbols
, and

•

for each pair
(a,s)\in\Sigma\times S
,
p(\cdot,\cdot\mid a,s)

is a conditional probability distribution on
\Omega\times S
.

Note that a conditional probability distribution
p(\cdot,\cdot\mid a,s)
on
\Omega\times S
consists of nonnegative numbers

p(b,s^{\prime}\mid a,s)
for all
s^{\prime}\in S
and
b\in\Omega
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in\Omega}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}p(b,s^{\prime}\mid a,s)=1,\quad a\in\Sigma,s\in S.

(2.1)

Given a conditional probability distribution
p(\cdot,\cdot\mid a,s)
on
\Omega\times S
,
we define a conditional probability distribution
\hat{p}(\cdot,\cdot\mid x,s)
with
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
s\in S

on
\Omega^{*}\times S
recursively as follows.

•

For all
s,s^{\prime}\in S
,

\displaystyle\hat{p}(\epsilon,s^{\prime}\mid\epsilon,s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if }s=s^{\prime},\\
0&\mbox{if }s\neq s^{\prime},\end{array}\right.

where
\epsilon
denotes both the empty word in
\Sigma^{*}
and
\Omega^{*}
.

•

For all
s,s^{\prime}\in S
,
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, and
y\in\Omega^{*}
with
|x|\neq|y|
,

\displaystyle\hat{p}(y,s^{\prime}\mid x,s)=0.

(2.5)

•

For all
s,s^{\prime}\in S
,
a\in\Sigma
,
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,
b\in\Omega
, and
y\in\Omega^{*}
,

\displaystyle\hat{p}(yb,s^{\prime}\mid xa,s)=\sum_{t\in S}\hat{p}(y,t\mid x,s)\cdot p(b,s^{\prime}\mid a,t).

(2.6)

Proposition 2.1
.

For each pair
(x,s)\in\Sigma^{*}\times S
,
\hat{p}(\cdot,\cdot\mid x,s)
is a conditional probability distribution
on
\Omega^{*}\times S
.

Therefore, for each pair
(x,s)\in\Sigma^{*}\times S
,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\Omega^{*}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}p(y,s^{\prime}\mid x,s)=1.

(2.7)

A stochastic automaton works serially and synchronously.
It reads an input word symbol by symbol and after reading an input symbol
it emits an output symbol and transits into another state.
More precisely, if the automaton starts in state
s
and reads the word
x
, then
with probability
\hat{p}(y,s^{\prime}\mid x,s)
it will end in state
s^{\prime}
emitting the word
y
by taking all intermediate states into account.

Note that the measures
p
and
\hat{p}
coincide on the set
\Omega\times S\times\Sigma\times S
if we put
x=y=\epsilon
in (
2.6
).
Therefore, we will write
p
instead of
\hat{p}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For all
x,x^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
,
y,y^{\prime}\in\Omega^{*}
, and
s,s^{\prime}\in S
with
|x|=|y|
,

p(yy^{\prime},s^{\prime}\mid xx^{\prime},s)=\sum_{t\in S}p(y,t\mid x,s)\cdot p(y^{\prime},s^{\prime}\mid x^{\prime},t).

Proof.

First, if
|x^{\prime}|\neq|y^{\prime}|
, then
|xx^{\prime}|\neq|yy^{\prime}|
and so both sides are zero by (
2.5
).
Second, if
x^{\prime}=\epsilon=y^{\prime}
, then both sides are equal to
p(y,s^{\prime}\mid x,s)
by (
•
‣
2
).

Third, if
x^{\prime}\in\Sigma
and
y^{\prime}\in\Omega
, then both sides are equal by (
2.6
).
Finally, suppose the assertion holds for words of length
|x^{\prime}|=|y^{\prime}|\leq k
for some
k\geq 1
.
Then consider words
x^{\prime}a
and
y^{\prime}b
of length
k+1
, where
a\in\Sigma
and
b\in\Omega
.
We have

\displaystyle p(yy^{\prime}b,s^{\prime}\mid xx^{\prime}a,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in S}p(yy^{\prime},t\mid xx^{\prime},s)\cdot p(b,s^{\prime}\mid a,t),\quad\mbox{by~(\ref{e-SA-phat3})},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in S}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in S}p(y,t^{\prime}\mid x,s)p(y^{\prime},t\mid x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cdot p(b,s^{\prime}\mid a,t),\;\mbox{by induction},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t^{\prime}\in S}p(y,t^{\prime}\mid x,s)\sum_{t\in S}p(y^{\prime},t\mid x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\cdot p(b,s^{\prime}\mid a,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t^{\prime}\in S}p(y,t^{\prime}\mid x,s)\cdot p(y^{\prime}b,s^{\prime}\mid x^{\prime}a,t^{\prime}),\quad\mbox{by~(\ref{e-SA-phat3})}.

The behavior of a stochastic automaton can be described by probability matrices.
To see this, let
A
be a stochastic automaton with state set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
.
For each pair of input and output symbols
a\in\Sigma
and
b\in\Omega
, put

\displaystyle p_{ij}(b\mid a)=p(b,s_{j}\mid a,s_{i}),\quad 1\leq i,j\leq n,

(2.8)

and define the real-valued
n\times n
matrix

\displaystyle P(b\mid a)=(p_{ij}(b\mid a))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

(2.9)

Note that the matrix
P(b\mid a)
is
substochastic
,
i.e., it is a square matrix with nonnegative entries and by (
2.7
) each row adds up to at most 1.
The elements of
P(b\mid a)
provide the transition probabilities between the states
if the symbol
a
is read and the symbol
b
is emitted.
This definition can be extended to strings of input and output symbols.
For this, note that by (
•
‣
2
) we have

\displaystyle P(\epsilon\mid\epsilon)=I_{n},

(2.10)

where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
unit matrix.
Moreover, if
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y\in\Omega^{*}
with
|x|\neq|y|
, then by (
2.5
) we have

\displaystyle P(x\mid y)=O_{n},

(2.11)

where
O_{n}
is the
n\times n
zero matrix.
Furthermore, if
a\in\Sigma
,
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,
b\in\Omega
, and
y\in\Omega^{*}
, then by (
2.6
) we have

\displaystyle P(yb\mid xa)=P(y\mid x)\cdot P(b\mid a).

(2.12)

By Prop.
2.2
and the associativity of matrix multiplication, we obtain the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

For all
x,x^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y,y^{\prime}\in\Omega^{*}
with
|x|=|y|
,

P(yy^{\prime}\mid xx^{\prime})=P(y\mid x)\cdot P(y^{\prime}\mid x^{\prime}).

It follows by induction that if
x=x_{1}\ldots x_{k}\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y=y_{1}\ldots y_{k}\in\Omega^{*}
, then

\displaystyle P(y\mid x)=P(y_{1}\mid x_{1})\cdots P(y_{k}\mid x_{k}).

(2.13)

Proposition 2.4
.

Each stochastic automaton
A
is uniquely characterized by the collection of substochastic matrices

\{P(b\mid a)\mid a\in\Sigma,b\in\Omega\}.

For each input word
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, the matrix

P(x)=\sum_{y\in\Omega^{*}}P(y\mid x)

is (row) stochastic, i.e., each row sums up to
1
.

Proof.

The substochastic matrices
P(b\mid a)
with
a\in\Sigma
and
b\in\Omega
describe the conditional probabilities of the automaton and so characterize the automaton uniquely.

By (
2.1
), the matrix
P(a)=\sum_{b\in\Omega}P(b\mid a)
is stochastic for each input symbol
a\in\Sigma
.
Moreover, the matrix
P(\epsilon)=P(\epsilon\mid\epsilon)=I_{n}
is stochastic.
Since the multiplication of stochastic matrices is again a stochastic matrix,
by (
2.13
) the matrix
P(x)
is stochastic for each
x\in\Sigma^{*}
.
∎

Chapter 3
Equivalence

In the following, the capabilities of stochastic automata will be compared.
Intuitively, two stochastic automata have the same performance if they exhibit the same input-output behavior.

More concretely, let
A=(S,\Sigma,\Omega,p)
be a stochastic automaton with state set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
.
The output behavior of the automaton being in state
s_{i}\in S
, reading the symbol
a\in\Sigma

and emitting the symbol
y\in\Omega
is given by the marginal probability

\displaystyle\eta_{i}(b\mid a)=\sum_{s\in S}p(b,s\mid a,s_{i}).

(3.1)

Take the column vector of marginals

\displaystyle\eta(b\mid a)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\eta_{1}(b\mid a)\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{n}(b\mid a)\end{array}\right).

Thus
\eta(b\mid a)
is the vector of row sums of the substochastic matrix
P(b\mid a)
.
Equivalently, if
\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}
denotes the all-one vector of length
n
, we have

\displaystyle\eta(b\mid a)=P(b\mid a)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}.

(3.6)

This can be generalized to words
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y\in\Omega^{*}
by setting

\displaystyle\eta(y\mid x)=P(y\mid x)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}.

(3.7)

The
i
th component
\eta_{i}(y\mid x)
is the conditional probability of transition
from state
s_{i}
to any state when the word
x
is read and the word
y
is emitted,
1\leq i\leq n
.
The vector
\eta(y\mid x)
is the
result vector
of the stochastic automaton
reading
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and emitting
y\in\Omega^{*}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

We have

•

\eta(\epsilon\mid\epsilon)=\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}.

•

For all words
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,

\sum_{y\in\Omega^{*}}\eta(y\mid x)=\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}.

•

For all words
x,x^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y,y^{\prime}\in\Omega^{*}
with
|x|=|y|
,

\eta(yy^{\prime}\mid xx^{\prime})=P(y\mid x)\cdot\eta(y^{\prime}\mid x^{\prime}).

Proof.

First, we have
p(\epsilon\mid\epsilon)=P(\epsilon\mid\epsilon)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}=I_{n}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}=\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}
.

Second, we have

\sum_{y\in\Omega^{*}}\eta(y\mid x)=\sum_{y\in\Omega^{*}}P(y\mid x)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}=P(x)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}=\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n},

since the matrix
P(x)
is stochastic.

Finally, by Prop.
2.3
and (
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle\eta(yy^{\prime}\mid xx^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(yy^{\prime}\mid xx^{\prime})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}=P(y\mid x)\cdot P(y^{\prime}\mid x^{\prime})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(y\mid x)\cdot\eta(y^{\prime}\mid x^{\prime}).

∎

A stochastic automaton can be thought of as an abstract machine which takes on a well-defined state at each step.
However, an external observer is unable to determine this state with certainty.
She can only consider the probability that the automaton is in a certain state.
Therefore, we introduce socalled state distributions.

Let
A=(S,\Sigma,\Omega,p)
be a stochastic automaton with state set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
.
A row vector
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n})
is a
state distribution
of
A
if the entries of
\pi
are nonnegative and add up to 1.
The
i
th component
\pi_{i}
is the probability that the automaton
A
is in state
s_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
.

By convention, a state distribution
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n})
of
A
will also be written
as a formal linear combination of states,

\displaystyle\pi=\pi_{1}s_{1}+\ldots+\pi_{n}s_{n}.

(3.8)

In particular,
\pi=s_{i}
is the state distribution given by the
i
th unit vector
e_{i}
which has 1 in position
i
and 0’s elsewhere,

1\leq i\leq n
.

Take the probability of emitting the word
y\in\Omega^{*}

if the automaton
A
is in state
s_{i}
with probability
\pi_{i}
and the word
x\in\Sigma^{*}
is read.
This probability is given by the matrix product

\displaystyle\pi\cdot\eta(y\mid x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}\cdot\eta_{i}(y\mid x).

(3.9)

For this, consider the mapping
\eta^{\pi}:\Omega^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow[0,1]
defined by

\displaystyle\eta^{\pi}(y\mid x)=\pi\cdot\eta(y\mid x),\quad x\in\Sigma^{*},y\in\Omega^{*}.

(3.10)

This mapping is well-defined, since
\sum_{i}\pi_{i}=1
,
0\leq\eta_{i}(y\mid x)\leq 1
for each
1\leq i\leq n

and so
0\leq\sum_{i}\pi_{i}\eta_{i}(y\mid x)\leq 1
.

In view of the result vectors, define the sets

\displaystyle\mbox{$\cal D$}_{A}=\{\eta^{\pi}\mid\pi\mbox{ state distribution of }A\}

(3.11)

and

\displaystyle\mbox{$\cal S$}_{A}=\{\eta^{s}\mid s\mbox{ state of }\;A\}.

(3.12)

We introduce several notions of equivalence of state distributions and stochastic automata.
For this, let
A=(S_{A},\Sigma,\Omega,p_{A})
and
B=(S_{B},\Sigma,\Omega,p_{B})
be stochastic automata with the same input and output alphabets.

•

A
and
B
are
equivalent
, written
A\equiv B
, if
\mbox{$\cal D$}_{A}=\mbox{$\cal D$}_{B}
.

•

A
and
B
are
S-equivalent
, written
A\equiv_{S}B
, if
\mbox{$\cal S$}_{A}=\mbox{$\cal S$}_{B}
.

•

A

covers

B
, written
A\geq B
, if
\mbox{$\cal D$}_{A}\supseteq\mbox{$\cal D$}_{B}
.

•

A state distribution
\pi_{A}
of
A
is
equivalent
to a state distribution
\pi_{B}
of
B
,
written
\pi_{A}\equiv\pi_{B}
, if

\displaystyle\eta_{A}^{\pi_{A}}(y\mid x)=\eta_{B}^{\pi_{B}}(y\mid x)

(3.13)

for all
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y\in\Omega^{*}
.

•

Let
k\geq 0
.
A state distribution
\pi_{A}
of
A
is
k
-equivalent
to a state distribution
\pi_{B}
of
B
,
written
\pi_{A}\equiv_{k}\pi_{B}
, if

\displaystyle\eta_{A}^{\pi_{A}}(y\mid x)=\eta_{B}^{\pi_{B}}(y\mid x)

(3.14)

for all
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y\in\Omega^{*}
with
|x|=|y|\leq k
.

These relations are equivalence relations on the class of stochastic automata resp. the class of state distributions.
The following result follows directly from the definitions.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A
and
B
be stochastic automata with the same input and output alphabets.
Then
A\equiv B
if and only if
A\geq B
and
B\geq A
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be stochastic automata.
Then
A
covers
B
if and only if for each state of
B
there is an equivalent state distribution of
A
.

Proof.

Let
S_{A}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
and
S_{B}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}
.

First, suppose
A
covers
B
.
Then for each state
t\in S_{B}
there is a state distribution
\pi
of
A
such that

\eta_{B}^{t}=\eta^{\pi}
.

Conversely, suppose that for each state
t\in S_{B}
there is an equivalent state distribution
\pi(t)
of
A
.
Then the state distribution
\pi^{\prime}=\sum_{j}\pi^{\prime}_{j}t_{j}
of
B

is equivalent to the state distribution
\pi=\sum_{j}\pi^{\prime}_{j}\pi(t_{j})
of
A
.
Thus
\mbox{$\cal D$}_{A}\supseteq\mbox{$\cal D$}_{B}
and hence
A
covers
B
.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
A
and
B
be stochastic automata with the same input and output alphabets.
If
A
and
B
are S-equivalent, then
A
and
B
are equivalent.

Proof.

Let
A
and
B
be S-equivalent.
Then for each state
s_{i}
in
A
there is an equivalent state
t_{i}
in
B
.
Thus the state distribution
\pi=\sum_{j}\pi_{j}s_{j}
of
A
is equivalent to the state distribution

\pi^{\prime}=\sum_{j}\pi^{\prime}_{j}t_{j}
of
B
.
Thus
\mbox{$\cal D$}_{A}\subseteq\mbox{$\cal D$}_{B}
and similarly
\mbox{$\cal D$}_{B}\subseteq\mbox{$\cal D$}_{A}
.
Hence,
A\equiv B
.
∎

Chapter 4
Reduction

The objective of reduction is to construct stochastic automata with the same performance but a reduced number of states.
For this, a stochastic automaton
A

is called
reduced
if
A
has no two distinct states which are equivalent.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be reduced and S-equivalent stochastic automata.
Then the state sets of
A
and
B
have the same cardinality.

Proof.

For each state
s\in S_{A}
there is an equivalent state
s^{\prime}\in S_{B}
in the sense that the induced state distributions are equivalent.
Since the automaton
B
is reduced, the state
s
cannot be equivalent to another state
s^{\prime\prime}\in S_{B}
, since then the states
s^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime}
would be equivalent.
This gives an injective mapping
S_{A}\rightarrow S_{B}
.
In the same way, we obtain an injective mapping
S_{B}\rightarrow S_{A}
.
But the state sets are finite and hence there is a bijection between
S_{A}
and
S_{B}
.
∎

For each stochastic automaton, there is an equivalent and reduced stochastic automaton
which can be constructed using the powerset construction.

Theorem 4.2
.

Each stochastic automaton is S-equivalent to a reduced stochastic automaton.

Proof.

Let
A=(S,\Sigma,\Omega,p_{A})
be a stochastic automaton with state set
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
.
Two states
s
and
t
of
A
are equivalent if
\eta^{s}(y\mid x)=\eta^{t}(y\mid x)
for all
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y\in\Omega^{*}
.
This relation is an equivalence relation on the state set
S
of
A
.
Let
Z=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{r}\}
denote the corresponding set of equivalence classes of the state set of
A
;
in particular, the set
Z
is a partition of the state set
S
.
For each class
Z_{i}
choose a fixed representative
z_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq r
.

Define the stochastic automaton
B=(Z,\Sigma,\Omega,p_{B})
with conditional probabilities

\displaystyle p_{B}(b,Z_{j}\mid a,Z_{i})=\sum_{z\in Z_{j}}p_{A}(b,z\mid a,z_{i}),\quad a\in\Sigma,\;b\in\Omega,\;1\leq i,j\leq r.

(4.1)

The automaton
B
is reduced, since if the states
Z_{i}
and
Z_{j}
are equivalent, then
z_{i}=z_{j}
and thus
i=j
by definition.

Claim that the automata
A
and
B
are S-equivalent.
Indeed, take the states
z_{i}\in Z_{i}
in
A
and
Z_{i}
in
B
,
1\leq i\leq r
.
Then for all
a\in\Sigma
and
b\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle\eta_{A}^{z_{i}}(b\mid a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in S_{A}}p_{A}(b,s\mid a,z_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{Z_{j}\in Z}\sum_{z\in Z_{j}}p_{A}(b,z\mid a,z_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{Z_{j}\in Z}p_{B}(b,Z_{j}\mid a,Z_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{B}^{Z_{i}}(b\mid a).

Thus the states
z_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are 1-equivalent.
Suppose the states
z_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are
k
-equivalent for some
k\geq 1
.
Then for all
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,
y\in\Omega^{*}
with
|x|=|y|=k
,
a\in\Sigma
, and
b\in\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle\eta_{A}^{z_{i}}(by\mid ax)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in S_{A}}p_{A}(b,s\mid a,z_{i})\eta_{A}^{s}(y\mid x),\quad\mbox{by Prop.~\ref{p-omega}},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{Z_{j}\in Z}\sum_{s\in Z_{j}}p_{A}(b,s\mid a,z_{i})\eta_{A}^{s}(y\mid x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{Z_{j}\in Z}p_{B}(b,Z_{j}\mid a,Z_{i})\eta_{A}^{z_{j}}(y\mid x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{Z_{j}\in Z}p_{B}(b,Z_{j}\mid a,Z_{i})\eta_{B}^{Z_{j}}(y\mid x),\quad\mbox{by induction},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{B}^{Z_{i}}(by\mid ax),\quad\mbox{by Prop.~\ref{p-omega}}.

Therefore, the states
z_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are
(k+1)
-equivalent.
Thus the states
z_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are equivalent,
1\leq i\leq r
, and hence the automata
A
and
B
are S-equivalent.
∎

The equivalence of two state distributions can be decided by considering inputs and outputs of restricted length.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
A
be a stochastic automaton with
n
states.
Two state distributions of
A
are equivalent if and only if they are
(n-1)
-equivalent.

Proof.

Let
k\geq 0
.
Two state distributions
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
of
A
are
k
-equivalent if and only if
for all words
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y\in\Omega^{*}
with
|x|=|y|\leq k
,

\pi\cdot\eta(y\mid x)=\pi^{\prime}\cdot\eta(y\mid x).

Equivalently,

(\pi-\pi^{\prime})\cdot\eta(y\mid x)=0.

Let
V_{k}
be the vector subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
generated by the result vectors

\eta(y\mid x)
, where
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y\in\Omega^{*}
with
|x|=|y|\leq k
.
We have
V_{0}={\mathbb{R}}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$1$}_{n}
and
V_{k}\subseteq V_{k+1}
for each

.
Thus
V_{k}
is a subspace of
V_{k+1}
for all
k\geq 0
.
Moreover, let
V_{A}
be the vector subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
generated by all the result vectors

\eta(y\mid x)
, where
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y\in\Omega^{*}
.
Then
V_{k}
is a subspace of
V_{A}
for each

Consider the ascending chain of subspaces of

V_{0}\subseteq V_{1}\subseteq V_{2}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq V_{A}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Since the subspaces are of finite dimension, there exists a smallest number

such that

.
Then we have

for each

since by Prop.

It follows that any two state distributions

and
\pi^{\prime}
of

are equivalent
if and only if

for all

and

are

∎

Theorem 4.8

The objective is to further decrease the state set of a reduced stochastic automaton.

and no state distribution

.

A
permutation matrix
is a quadratic matrix with entries 0 and 1 which has exactly one entry 1 in each row and each column.
An
n\times n
permutation matrix represents a bijection of a set of
n
elements.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
M
be a stochastic
m\times n
matrix and
N
be a stochastic
n\times m
matrix.
If
M\cdot N=I_{m}
and
N\cdot M=I_{n}
, then
m=n
and the matrices
M
and
N
are permutation matrices.

Proof.

Matrices can be viewed as linear mappings between vector spaces.
If
M\cdot N=I_{m}
, the linear mappings given by
M
and
N
are surjective and injective, respectively.
Similarly, if
N\cdot M=I_{n}
, the linear mappings given by
M
and
N
are injective and surjective, respectively.
Therefore, the linear mappings determined by
M
and
N
are bijective; i.e., the matrices
M
and
N
are invertible with
m=n
.

Let
M=(a_{ij})
and
N=(b_{ij})
be
n\times n
matrices with
M\cdot N=I_{n}
and
N\cdot M=I_{n}
.
Then for the
(i,i)
th diagonal element,
1\leq i\leq n
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}b_{ji}=1
.
Since
M
and
N
are nonnegative matrices, we have
a_{il}>0
and
b_{li}>0
for some
l
.
Moreover, for each
j\neq l
, we have
\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{jk}a_{kl}=0
.
Since
a_{il}\neq 0
, we have
b_{ji}=0
. Thus
1=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}b_{ji}=a_{il}b_{lj}
.
But
a_{il},b_{lj}\leq 1
and so
a_{il}=b_{lj}=1
.
Therefore, each row of
M
and each column of
N
has exactly one entry
1
and the remaining entries are
0
.
By exchanging the roles of
M
and
N
,
each row of
N
and each column of
M
has exactly one entry
1
and the remaining entries are
0
.
Hence,
M
and
N
are permutation matrices.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
A
and
B
be minimal stochastic automata with the same input and output alphabets.
Then
A
and
B
are equivalent if and only if
A
and
B
are S-equivalent.

Proof.

By Prop.
3.4
,
A
and
B
are equivalent if they are S-equivalent.

Conversely, let
A
and
B
be equivalent.
Let
S_{A}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\}
and
S_{B}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}
be the state sets of
A
and
B
, respectively.
Then for each state
s_{i}\in S_{A}
there is an equivalent state distribution
\pi^{\prime}_{i}
of
B
,
and for each state
t_{j}\in S_{B}
there is an equivalent state distribution
\pi_{j}
of
A
.
Write

\pi^{\prime}_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{ik}t_{k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\pi_{j}=\sum_{l=1}^{m}d_{jl}s_{l}.

Thus the state
s_{i}
is equivalent to the state distribution

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{m}c_{ik}d_{kl}s_{l}=\sum_{l=1}^{m}s_{l}\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{ik}d_{kl}.

Since the automaton
A
is minimal, the right-hand of the equation must be equal to
s_{i}
;
that is,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{ik}d_{kl}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if }i=l,\\
0&\mbox{if }i\neq l.\end{array}\right.

Take the
n\times m
matrix
C=(c_{ij})
and the
m\times n
matrix
D=(d_{jk})
.
Both matrices are stochastic, since their rows are state distributions.
Moreover, the above equation shows that
C\cdot D=I_{n}
and
D\cdot C=I_{m}
.
Therefore, by Lemma
5.1
, we have
m=n
and the matrices
C
and
D
are permutation matrices.
In particular, the state distributions
\pi_{j}
and
\pi^{\prime}_{i}
are unit vectors
and therefore correspond to the states of
A
and
B
, respectively.
Thus for each state in
A
there is an equivalent state in
S_{B}
, and vice versa.
Hence, the automata
A
and
B
are S-equivalent.
∎

The construction of minimal stochastic automata is sketched in the next result.

Theorem 5.3
.

Each reduced stochastic automaton

Proof.

\pi

Suppose

\pi^{\prime}_{1}
of

s_{1}

\pi^{\prime}_{1}

\pi_{i}
of

Take the stochastic automaton

Since the state

\displaystyle SR\cdot\eta_{A}(y\mid x)=\eta_{A}(y\mid x),\quad x\in\Sigma^{*},\;y\in\Omega^{*}.

\displaystyle\eta_{B}(by\mid ax)

\displaystyle P_{B}(b\mid a)\cdot\eta_{B}(y\mid x),\quad\mbox{by Prop.~\ref{p-omega}},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R\cdot P_{A}(b\mid a)\cdot S\cdot\eta_{B}(y\mid x),\quad\mbox{by~(\ref{e-RS})},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R\cdot P_{A}(b\mid a)\cdot SR\cdot\eta_{A}(y\mid x),\quad\mbox{by induction},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R\cdot P_{A}(b\mid a)\cdot\eta_{A}(y\mid x),\quad\mbox{by~(\ref{e-SR})},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R\cdot\eta_{A}(by\mid ax),\quad\mbox{by Prop.~\ref{p-omega}}.

It follows that state

of

Finally, the aim is to further reduce the state set of a minimal stochastic automaton.
For this, a stochastic automaton
A
is called

if equivalent state distributions of

are equal.
By definition, each strongly reduced stochastic automaton is minimal.
However, not every minimal stochastic automaton is strongly reduced.

In the example of Even, the state distributions

is not strongly reduced.

\diamondsuit

Strong reduction of a stochastic automaton can be tested by using the

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
A
be a stochastic automaton with
n

Then
A
is strongly reduced if and only if the matrix
H_{A}
has rank
n
.

Proof.

Suppose the
n\times d
matrix
H_{A}
has rank
d=n
.
Then
H_{A}
is an
n\times n
matrix which determines a bijective linear mapping.
Let
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
be equivalent state distributions of
A
.
Then by Prop.
4.5
, we have
\pi H_{A}=\pi^{\prime}H_{A}
; i.e.,
(\pi-\pi^{\prime})H_{A}=0
.
But the kernel of the bijective linear mapping provided by
H_{A}
is the zero space
\{0\}
.
Thus we have
\pi=\pi^{\prime}
and hence the automaton
A
is strongly reduced.

Conversely, suppose the
n\times d
matrix
H_{A}
has rank
d<n
.
Then the kernel of the linear mapping given by
H_{A}
has nonzero dimension.
That is, there is a nonzero vector
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
uH_{A}=0
.
Since the first column of the matrix
H_{A}
is the all-one vector,
we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}=0
.
Moreover, since the vector
u
is nonzero and the matrix
H_{A}
is nonnegative,
the vector
u
must have nonzero positive and nonzero negative components.
Let
u^{+}
be the vector, which results from
u
by setting all negative entries to 0.
Moreover, put
u^{-}=u^{+}-u
; i.e.,
u^{-}
results from
u
by setting all positive entries to 0 and then changing signs.
Then the vectors
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
are nonnegative with
u=u^{+}-u^{-}
.
For instance, if
u=(1,-4,3,0)
, then
u^{+}=(1,0,3,0)
and
u^{-}=(0,4,0,0)
.
Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}=0
, we have
\alpha=\sum_{i}u_{i}^{+}=\sum_{i}u_{i}^{-}

and so
\pi^{+}=\frac{1}{\alpha}u^{+}
and
\pi^{-}=\frac{1}{\alpha}u^{-}
are state distributions of

Chapter 6
Coverings

The previous chapters have shown that the state set of a stochastic automaton can eventually be decreased
without diminishing its performance.
Further reductions are possible if coverings of stochastic automata are considered.

For this, let
A=(S_{A},\Sigma,\Omega,p_{A})
and
B=(S_{B},\Sigma,\Omega,p_{B})
be stochastic automata, and
let
S_{A}
and
S_{B}
have
n
and
m
states, respectively.
The automaton
B
is a
stochastic image

of automaton
A
, written
A\rightarrow_{s}B
, if
there is a stochastic
m\times n
matrix
Q
such that for all
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y\in\Omega^{*}
,

\displaystyle\eta_{B}(y\mid x)=Q\cdot\eta_{A}(y\mid x).

(6.1)

Note that the matrix
Q
defines a linear mapping
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:v\mapsto Qv
such that
\phi
maps the result vectors

\eta_{A}(y\mid x)
to the result vectors
\eta_{B}(y\mid x)
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be stochastic automata with the same input and output alphabets.
Then
A
covers
B
if and only if
B
is a stochastic image of
A
.

Proof.

Let
S_{A}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
and
S_{B}=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}
.

Suppose
A
covers
B
.
Then by Lemma
3.3
, for each state
t\in S_{B}
there is an equivalent state distribution
q_{j}
of
A
.
Let
Q
be the matrix with rows
q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}
.
Then
\eta_{B}^{t_{j}}(y\mid x)=q_{j}\cdot\eta_{A}(y\mid x)
for all
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,
y\in\Omega^{*}
and
1\leq j\leq m
.
Thus

\eta_{B}(y\mid x)=Q\cdot\eta_{A}(y\mid x)
for all
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,
y\in\Omega^{*}
.
Hence,
B
is a stochastic image of
A
.

Conversely, let
B
be a stochastic image of
A
.
The
j
th row
q_{i}
of the matrix
Q
is a state distribution of
A
.
Then
\eta_{B}^{t_{j}}(y\mid x)=q_{j}\cdot\eta_{A}(y\mid x)
for all
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,
y\in\Omega^{*}
and
1\leq j\leq m
.
Thus the state
t_{j}
is equivalent to the state distribution
q_{j}
of
A
.
By Lemma
3.3
, it follows that
A
covers
B
.
∎

Stochastic homomorphy is reflected by the corresponding
H

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
A
and

be stochastic automata with the same input and output alphabets.
If there is an

matrix

such that

is a stochastic image of

then
H_{B}
is obtained from

by deleting linearly dependent columns.
If the columns of

H_{B}=Q\cdot H_{A}
.

Proof.

Let
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d}
be the columns of the

matrix
H_{A}
.
Then
\eta^{\prime}_{i}=Q\cdot\eta_{i}
for

Since each result vector
\eta_{A}(y\mid x)
of

is a linear combination of the vectors
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d}

the vector
\eta_{B}(y\mid x)=Q\cdot\eta_{A}(y\mid x)
is a linear combination of the vectors

\eta^{\prime}_{1}=Q\cdot\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta^{\prime}_{d}=Q\cdot\eta_{d}

We may assume that the columns of

are totally ordered as given by the set

in (

).
It is easy to check that the matrix

obtained from

by deleting in turn the columns

.
Moreover, if the columns of

are linearly independent, then

.
∎

Chapter 7
Observability and Determinism

This chapter considers stochastic automata with restricted transition probability distributions.

A stochastic automaton
A=(S,\Sigma,\Omega,p)
is called
observable

if there is a (partial) mapping
\gamma:\Omega\times\Sigma\times S\rightarrow S
such that for all

a\in\Sigma
,
b\in\Omega
, and
s,s^{\prime}\in S
,

\displaystyle p(b,s^{\prime}\mid a,s)\neq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad s^{\prime}=\gamma(b,a,s).

(7.1)

If in an observable stochastic automaton the current state, the input and the output are known,
the next state is uniquely determined.

The (partial) mapping
\gamma:\Omega\times\Sigma\times S\rightarrow S
can be extended
to the (partial) mapping
\gamma:\Omega^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}\times S\rightarrow S
such that
for all
a\in\Sigma
,
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,
b\in\Omega
,
y\in\Omega^{*}
,
s,s^{\prime}\in S
with
|x|=|y|
,

\displaystyle\gamma(\epsilon,\epsilon,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s,

(7.2)

\displaystyle\gamma(by,ax,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma(y,x,\gamma(b,a,s)).

(7.3)

Lemma 7.1
.

A stochastic automaton
A
is observable if and only if
for all
a\in\Sigma
and
b\in\Omega
,
the matrices
P(b\mid a)
contain in each row at most one nonzero entry.

Proof.

Let
A
be observable and let
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
.
Consider the
s_{i}
th row of the matrix
P(b\mid a)
.
Since
p(b,s_{j}\mid a,s_{i})\neq 0
implies
s_{j}=\gamma(b,a,s_{i})
, the
s_{i}
th row contains

p(b,s_{j}\mid a,s_{i})
as the only nonzero element.

Let the matrices
P(b\mid a)
with
a\in\Sigma
and
b\in\Omega
contain in each row at most one nonzero entry,
say
p(b,s_{j}\mid a,s_{i})
.
Then put
s_{j}=\gamma(b,a,s_{i})
.
This defines a mapping
\gamma:\Omega\times\Sigma\times S\rightarrow S
such that
A
is observable.
∎

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
A
be a stochastic automaton and
\gamma:\Omega^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}\times S\rightarrow S
be the mapping as above.
Then for all
s,s^{\prime}\in S
,
x\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y\in\Omega^{*}
with
|x|=|y|
,

\displaystyle p(y,s^{\prime}\mid x,s)\neq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad s^{\prime}=\gamma(y,x,s).

(7.4)

Proof.

Let
y=by^{\prime}
and
x=ax^{\prime}
with
a\in\Sigma
,
b\in\Omega
,
x^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
and
y^{\prime}\in\Omega^{*}
with

.

For

the assertion is clear. Let
k\geq 1
.
Suppose
p(by^{\prime},s^{\prime}\mid ax^{\prime},s)\neq 0
.
Then there is a state

p(b,s^{\prime\prime}\mid a,s)\neq 0
and
p(y^{\prime},s^{\prime}\mid x^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})\neq 0
.
By induction,

s^{\prime\prime}=\gamma(b,a,s)
and
s^{\prime}=\gamma(y^{\prime},x^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime})
.
Hence,
s^{\prime}=\gamma(y^{\prime},x^{\prime},\gamma(b,a,s)))=\gamma(by^{\prime},ax^{\prime},s)
.
∎

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be observable stochastic automata with mappings

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in\Omega}p(b,\delta(a,s)\mid a,s)=1.

Stochastic versions of the well-known Mealy and Moore automata are presented and

A stochastic automaton
A=(S,\Sigma,\Omega,p)
is called

if there are conditional probabilities

and

over
\Omega
and

,

and

\displaystyle p(b,s^{\prime}\mid a,s)=p_{1}(b\mid a,s)\cdot p_{2}(s^{\prime}\mid a,s).

(8.1)

Stochastic independence of state transition and output emission makes stochastic Mealy automata a
restricted class of stochastic automata.

Proposition 8.1
.

Each state-determined stochastic automaton

is a stochastic Mealy automaton.
Each output-determined stochastic automaton
A
is a stochastic Mealy automaton.

Proof.

Let
A
be state-determined.
Define

p_{1}(b\mid a,s)=p(b,\delta(a,s)\mid a,s)
and

p_{2}(s^{\prime}\mid a,s)=1
if

and
0
otherwise.
Then
p(b,s^{\prime}\mid a,s)=p_{1}(b\mid a,s)\cdot p_{2}(s^{\prime}\mid a,s)
as required.

Let
A
be output-determined.
Define

p_{1}(b\mid a,s)=1
if

and

otherwise, and

p_{2}(s^{\prime}\mid a,s)=p(\lambda(a,s),s^{\prime}\mid a,s)
.
Then
p(b,s^{\prime}\mid a,s)=p_{1}(b\mid a,s)\cdot p_{2}(s^{\prime}\mid a,s)
as required.
∎

.

A stochastic Mealy automaton

is observable if and only if

be observable.
Then for all

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in\Omega}p_{1}(b\mid a,s)p_{2}(\delta(a,s)\mid a,s)

\displaystyle=

is state-determined.
The converse is similar.

Proposition 9.4
.

For each deterministic acceptor
A
and each cut point
\lambda
, there is a deterministic acceptor
B
with

Proof.

Let
A=(S_{A},\Sigma,P_{A},\pi_{A},f_{A})
be a deterministic acceptor with

S_{A}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
and
\pi_{A}=(\pi_{A,1},\ldots,\pi_{A,n})
.
Write
\pi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{A,i}s_{i}
.

For each
1\leq i\leq n
,
define the acceptor
A_{i}
obtained from

by replacing the initial state distribution
\pi_{A}
with the
state (standard basis vector)
s_{i}
.
This acceptor is deterministic in the ordinary sense.

Let
x\in L_{A,\lambda}
. Then
\pi_{A}P_{A}(x)f_{A}>\lambda
or equivalently

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{A,i}s_{i}P_{A}(x)f_{A}>\lambda.

That is, there are states

s_{i_{1}},\ldots,s_{i_{k}}
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi_{A,i_{j}}>\lambda
and
x\in L_{A_{i_{j}},0}
for all

.
Equivalently, we have

where the union extends over all nonempty subsets
\{s_{i_{1}},\ldots,s_{i_{k}}\}
of

such that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi_{A,i_{j}}>\lambda
.
Thus the set

is a finite union of intersections of regular languages.

The
p
-adic languages provide an example of stochastic languages which are not regular and also demonstrate that the class of stochastic languages is uncountable.
To see this, let
p\geq 2
be an integer.
The stochastic acceptor
A=(\{s_{1},s_{2}\},\{0,\ldots,p-1\},P,\pi,f)
with

P(a)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1-\frac{a}{p}&\frac{a}{p}\\
1-\frac{a+1}{p}&\frac{a+1}{p}\end{array}\right),\quad 0\leq a\leq p-1,\quad\pi=(1,0),\quad\mbox{and}\quad f=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\end{array}\right)

is called
p
-adic acceptor
.

Each word
x=x_{1}\ldots x_{k}\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}^{*}
can be assigned the real number
whose
p
-adic representation is
0.x_{k}\ldots x_{1}
.

Proposition 9.5
.

Let
A
be an
p
-adic acceptor and let
\lambda
be a cut point.
Then

L_{A,\lambda}=\{x_{1}\ldots x_{k}\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}^{*}\mid 0.x_{k}\ldots x_{1}>\lambda\}.

Proof.

A word
x=x_{1}\ldots x_{k}\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}^{*}
lies in
L_{A,\lambda}

if and only if

\pi P(x)f>\lambda
.
Note that
\pi P(x)f
is the upper right entry of the matrix
P(x)
.

Claim that the matrix
P(x)
has
0.x_{k}\ldots x_{1}
as upper right entry.
Indeed, this is true for
k=1
.
The matrix
P(x_{1}\ldots x_{k}a)
with
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},a\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}
can be written as the product matrix
P(x_{1}\ldots x_{k})P(a)
.
By induction, the upper right entry of this matrix is

\displaystyle(1-0.x_{k}\ldots x_{1})\cdot\frac{a}{p}+0.x_{k}\ldots x_{1}\cdot\frac{a+1}{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a}{p}+\frac{0.x_{k}\ldots x_{1}}{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.a+0.0x_{k}\ldots x_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.ax_{k}\ldots x_{1}.

This proves the claim.
Hence,
x_{1}\ldots x_{k}\in L_{A,\lambda}
if and only if
0.x_{k}\ldots x_{1}>\lambda
.
∎

The following result makes use of the
Nerode equivalence relation

\equiv_{L}
of a language
L
over
\Sigma
.
Define for all words
x,y\in\Sigma^{*}
,

\displaystyle x\equiv_{L}y\quad:\Longleftrightarrow\quad\forall z\in\Sigma^{*}:xz\in L\Longleftrightarrow yz\in L.

(9.14)

This is an equivalence relation on the set
\Sigma^{*}
.
The theorem of Nerode-Myhill says that a language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is regular if and only if the number of equivalence
classes of the relation
\equiv_{L}
is finite.

For instance, the language
L=\{a^{n}b^{n}\mid n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
is not regular, since
the words
a^{i}b
and
a^{j}b
with
i\neq j
are not equivalent and so the
equivalence classes of the words
a^{i}b
,
i\geq 1
, are all distinct.

Proposition 9.6
.

Let
A
be an
p
-adic acceptor and
\lambda
be a cut point.
Then
L_{A,\lambda}
is regular if and only if
\lambda
is rational.

Proof.

In view of the Nerode equivalence relation of the language
L=L_{A,\lambda}
,
two words
x,y\in\Sigma^{*}
belong to different equivalence classes if and only if
there is a word
z\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
xz\in L
and
yz\not\in L
or
xz\not\in L
and
yz\in L
.
That is,
0.\mbox{$\mu$}(yz)\leq\lambda<0.\mbox{$\mu$}(xz)
or
0.\mbox{$\mu$}(xz)\leq\lambda<0.\mbox{$\mu$}(yz)
,
where
\mbox{$\mu$}(x_{1}\ldots x_{k})=x_{k}\ldots x_{1}
denotes the
mirror image
of the word
x=x_{1}\ldots x_{k}
.

Suppose without restriction that
0.\mbox{$\mu$}(yz)\leq\lambda<0.\mbox{$\mu$}(xz)
.
Since
\mu(yz)=\mbox{$\mu$}(z)\mbox{$\mu$}(y)
and
\mu(xz)=\mbox{$\mu$}(z)\mbox{$\mu$}(x)
, we have

0.\mbox{$\mu$}(z)\mbox{$\mu$}(y)\leq\lambda<0.\mbox{$\mu$}(z)\mbox{$\mu$}(x)
.
Thus the cut point must have the form
\lambda=0.\mbox{$\mu$}(z)a_{1}a_{2}\ldots
.
If we put
\lambda_{z}=0.a_{1}a_{2}\ldots
, then
0.\mbox{$\mu$}(y)\leq\lambda_{z}<0.\mbox{$\mu$}(x)
.
With this kind of numbers
\lambda_{z}
it is possible to separate the equivalence classes.
That is, the relation
\equiv_{L}
has the classes

\{y\in\Sigma^{*}\mid\lambda_{u}<0.\mbox{$\mu$}(y)\leq\lambda_{v}\mbox{ and there is no $w\in\Sigma^{*}$ with }\lambda_{u}<\lambda_{w}<\lambda_{v}\}.

We have

\lambda=0.\mbox{$\mu$}(z)+\frac{\lambda_{z}}{p^{l}},

where
l
is the length of the word
z
.
Therefore, there are only finitely many equivalence classes of
\equiv_{L}
if and only if
there are only finitely many
\lambda_{z}
with this property.
In this case, the cut point
\lambda
is eventually periodic and hence a rational number.
∎

First, stochastic languages are independent of the specific cut point.

Proposition 9.7
.

Let
A
be a stochastic acceptor and
\lambda
be a cut point.
Then for each number
\mu
with
0<\mu<1
, there is a stochastic acceptor
B
such that

L_{A,\lambda}=L_{B,\mu}
.

Proof.

Let
A=(S,\Sigma,P,\pi,f)
be a stochastic acceptor with state set

S_{A}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
and let
\lambda
be a cut point.

Let
\lambda=0
.
Then by Prop.
9.3
,
L_{A,0}
is regular
and thus there is a deterministic acceptor
B
which accepts this language for each cut point
\mu
with
0<\mu<1
.

Let
\lambda=1
.
Then we have
L_{A,1}=\emptyset
and it is clear that there is a stochastic acceptor
B
with the required property.

Let
0<\lambda<1
.
Define the stochastic acceptor
B
with alphabet
\Sigma
,
state set
S_{B}=S_{A}\cup\{s_{n+1}\}
, and transition matrices

P_{B}(a)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}&&&0\\
&P_{A}(a)&&\vdots\\
&&&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\ldots&0&1\end{array}\right),\quad a\in\Sigma.

Then for each
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,

P_{B}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}&&&0\\
&P_{A}(x)&&\vdots\\
&&&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\ldots&0&1\end{array}\right).

Consider a cut point
\mu
with
0<\mu<1
.
We will define a number
\nu
with
0\leq\nu<1
corresponding to
\lambda
and
\mu
.
Put

\pi_{B}=((1-\nu)\pi_{A,1},\ldots,(1-\nu)\pi_{A,n},\nu).

First, let
0<\mu\leq\lambda
.
Then put
\nu=1-\frac{\mu}{\lambda}
and

f_{B}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{A}\\
0\end{array}\right)
.
This defines a stochastic acceptor
B
with the property that for each word
x\in\Sigma^{*}
,

\pi_{B}P_{B}(x)f_{B}=(1-\nu)\pi_{A}P_{A}(x)f_{A}=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\pi_{A}P_{A}f_{A}.

Thus
\pi_{A}P_{A}(x)f_{A}>\lambda
if and only if
\pi_{B}P_{B}(x)f_{B}>\mu
.
Hence,
L_{A,\lambda}=L_{B,\mu}
.

Second, let
\lambda<\mu<1
.
Then put
\nu=\frac{\mu-\lambda}{1-\lambda}
and

f_{B}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{A}\\

.
This defines a stochastic acceptor
B
with the property that for each word
x\in\Sigma^{*}

\pi_{B}P_{B}(x)f_{B}=(1-\nu)\pi_{A}P_{A}(x)f_{A}+\nu.

We have
(1-\nu)\lambda+\nu=\mu
.
Thus
\pi_{A}P_{A}(x)f_{A}>\lambda

\pi_{B}P_{B}(x)f_{B}>\mu
.
Hence,
L_{A,\lambda}=L_{B,\mu}
.

.

, whose initial state distribution is given by a single state,

Let

be a stochastic acceptor with state set

S_{A}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}

\lambda
be a cut point.

Define the stochastic acceptor
B
with alphabet
\Sigma
,

S_{B}=S_{A}\cup\{s_{0}\}

Finally, the concept of isolated cut points is introduced.
For this, let
A
be a stochastic acceptor.
A cut point
\lambda
is called
isolated
for
A

if there is a number
\Delta>0
such that for all words
x\in\Sigma_{A}^{*}
,

\displaystyle|\pi_{A}P_{A}(x)f_{A}-\lambda|\geq\Delta.

(9.15)

Isolated cut points have some meaning when conducting probabilistic experiments with stochastic acceptors.

Theorem 9.9

(Rabin)
.

Let
A
be a stochastic acceptor with isolated cut point
\lambda
.
Then the language
L_{A,\lambda}
is regular.

Proof.

Let
A
have state set
S_{A}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
.
Then by Prop.
9.8
,
there is a stochastic acceptor
B
with state set
S_{A}=\{s_{0}\}\cup S_{A}
such that

L_{A,\lambda}=L_{B,\lambda}
.

Let
\equiv_{L}
be the Nerode equivalence relation of the language
L=L_{A,\lambda}
.
Suppose
x
and
y
are words in
\Sigma^{*}
such that
x\not\equiv_{L}y
.
Then there exists a word
z\in\Sigma^{*}
such that without restriction

xz\in L_{A,\lambda}
and
yz\not\in L_{A,\lambda}
.
Thus
\pi_{B}P_{B}(xz)f_{B}>\lambda
and
\pi_{B}P_{B}(yz)f_{B}\leq\lambda
.
Since
\lambda
is isolated,

\pi_{B}P_{B}(xz)f_{B}\geq\lambda+\Delta
and
\pi_{B}P_{B}(yz)f_{B}\leq\lambda-\Delta
.
Therefore,

\pi_{B}(P_{B}(x)-P_{B}(y))P_{B}(z)f_{B}\geq 2\Delta.

The column vector
P_{B}(z)f_{B}
has nonnegative entries and the initial state vector is
\pi_{B}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
.
Thus the above inequality remains valid if the summation is restricted to the positive entries
p_{0j}(x)-p_{0j}(y)
, i.e.

\sum_{j}^{+}p_{0j}(x)-p_{0j}(y)\geq 2\Delta,

where
\sum^{+}
denotes the summation over the positive terms.
Since the matrices
P_{B}(x)
and
P_{B}(y)
are stochastic, we obtain

\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{0j}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{0j}(y)=1
and thus

\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{0j}(x)-p_{0j}(y)=0
.
Hence,

\sum_{j}^{+}p_{0j}(x)-p_{0j}(y)=-\sum_{j}^{-}p_{0j}(x)-p_{0j}(y),

where
\sum^{-}
denotes the summation over the non-positive terms.
Therefore,

\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{0j}(x)-p_{0j}(y)|=2\sum_{j}^{+}p_{0j}(x)-p_{0j}(y).

We may assume that
x,y
are different from the empty word.
Then
p_{00}(x)=p_{00}(y)
and so

2\Delta\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|p_{0j}(x)-p_{0j}(y)|.

Thus in case of
x\not\equiv_{L}y
, the row vectors
\gamma_{x}=(p_{01}(x),\ldots,p_{0n}(x))

and
\gamma_{y}=(p_{01}(y),\ldots,p_{0n}(y))

have the
|\cdot|
(absolute) distance of at least
4\Delta
.

However, all vectors of length
n
with row sum
1
form a closed and limited (and thus compact) subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
which can be covered by finitely many
n
-dimensional cubes of length
2\Delta
.
The above considerations show that
each of these cubes can only contain those vectors
\gamma_{x}
, which belong to the same equivalence class of the Nerode
equivalence relation.
Thus there are only finitely many classes of the Nerode equivalence.
Hence, by the Nerode-Myhill theorem, the language
L_{B,\lambda}
is regular.
∎
