Qubit Geodesics on the Bloch Sphere from Optimal-Speed Hamiltonian Evolutions
Carlo Cafaro 1 and Paul M. Alsing 2

Abstract
In the geometry of quantum evolutions, a geodesic path is viewed as a path of minimal statistical length connecting two pure quantum states along which the maximal number of statistically distinguishable states is minimum. In this paper, we present an explicit geodesic analysis of the dynamical trajectories that emerge from the quantum evolution of a single-qubit quantum state. The evolution is governed by an Hermitian Hamiltonian operator that achieves the fastest possible unitary evolution between given initial and final pure states. Furthermore, in addition to viewing geodesics in ray space as paths of minimal length, we also verify the geodesicity of paths in terms of unit geometric efficiency and vanishing geometric phase. Finally, based on our analysis, we briefly address the main hurdles in moving to the geometry of quantum evolutions for open quantum systems in mixed quantum states.

Introduction

It is well-know that geometry plays a fundamental role in physics. Moreover,
it is not unusual to observe that fundamental physics theories can help
creating new mathematical structures of geometric flavor. For instance, the
noncommutative nature of quantum theory has significantly motivated the birth
of the newly established field of Quantum Riemannian Geometry (QRG,

beggs20
). This geometry is an extension of classical differential
geometry to curved quantum spaces with a noncommutative coordinate algebra.
For recent applications of QRG to models of quantum gravity and to
formulations of ordinary quantum mechanics in the spirit of classical gravity,
we refer to Refs.
majid19
and
beggs21
;
beggs22
, respectively.

In this paper, we consider the geometric formulations of quantum mechanics in
a more conventional way where the geometry on the space of quantum states

karol
, either pure
wootters81
or mixed
braunstein94
,
specifies limitations on our capacity of discriminating one state from another
by means of measurements. The geometry on the space of quantum states does not
express, in general, the actual dynamical evolution of a quantum system

braunstein95
. Indeed, not all Hamiltonian evolutions are shortest time
Hamiltonian evolutions and, therefore, do not coincide with the geodesic paths
on the underlying quantum state space equipped with a suitable metric.
However, focusing for simplicity on pure states, there exist optimum
Hamiltonians generating optimal-speed evolutions characterized by the shortest
duration
brody03
;
carlini06
;
brody06
;
brody07
;
bender07
along with the
maximal energy dispersion
uhlmann92
;
ali09
. For such quantum motions,
Hamiltonian curves (i.e., dynamical trajectories) traced by quantum states
undergoing actual physical evolutions can be formally shown to be geodesics
(i.e., geodesic lines or geodesic paths) on the underlying metricized manifolds.

Following Refs.
wootters81
;
diosi84
, a geodesic path in the above
mentioned geometric formulations of quantum mechanics can be regarded as a
path of minimal statistical length connecting two quantum states along which
the maximal number of statistically distinguishable states is minimum. In
particular, the larger the size of the statistical fluctuations in
measurements prepared to distinguish one state from another, the closer points
are together. Therefore, optimum Hamiltonian evolutions can be shown to happen
along geodesic paths of minimal statistical length. The system evolution
occurs while crossing the minimum number of statistically distinguishable
quantum states and, in addition, moves quickly through regions in which the
energy dispersion is large. The problem of finding a Hamiltonian operator that
achieves the minimum travel time (or, alternatively, the highest evolution
speed) has been considered for systems in either pure

brody03
;
carlini06
;
brody06
;
brody07
;
bender07
;
ali09
or mixed

carlini08
;
campaioli19
;
campbell21
;
hornedal22
quantum states. In these
works, the emphasis is on the Hamiltonian operator and not on the geodesicity
of the dynamical trajectory traced by the actual Hamiltonian evolution. In
this regard, it is important to keep in mind that geodesics are curves with a
preferred parametrization. Therefore, when characterizing geodesic paths, one
needs to specify its parametrization in terms of a coordinate (that is, the
affine parameter) along with the path (for instance, the great circle for a two-sphere).

The main goal of our paper is to help create more awareness of the interplay
between quantum mechanics and geometry by spelling out how the concept of
shortest time Hamiltonian evolution in the quantum information sense coincides
with the notion of geodesic path in the geometric sense. To achieve this goal,
we consider a shortest time Hermitian Hamiltonian evolution of a two-level
quantum system from a pure source state
\left|A\right\rangle
to a pure
target state
\left|B\right\rangle
. Then, we show that the dynamical
trajectory connecting
\left|A\right\rangle
and
\left|B\right\rangle
that emerges from the unitary evolution operator
U\left(t\right)=e^{-\frac{i}{\hslash}\mathrm{H}t}
coincides with the geodesic path
between the two single-qubit pure states when the two-dimensional Hilbert
space
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{1}
is viewed in terms of points on the complex
projective Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}P^{1}
(or, equivalently, the Bloch sphere
S^{2}\cong

\mathbb{C}P^{1}
) equipped with the Fubini-Study metric (or, equivalently, the round
metric on the sphere).

For completeness, we point out that the selection of the main goal of our
paper is also motivated by our recent geometrically oriented investigations on
quantum search algorithms
carlo12
;
carlo17
, on continuous-time quantum
search evolutions
carlo19A
;
carlo19B
;
gassner20
, on the efficiency of
quantum evolutions
carlo20pra
;
carlo21
, on the emergence of geodesic
paths in different areas of physics (including classical gravity)

carlo20
, and, finally, on the intriguing link between propagation of
light with maximal degree of coherence and optimal-speed unitary quantum time
evolutions
carlopra22
.

The layout of the rest of the paper is as follows. In Section II, we revisit

trajectory introduced in Section III by means of the concepts of geometric

II.1
Distance between two pure states

In what follows, we introduce the Fubini-Study metric tensor. Recall that the

\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle

d_{\text{{FS}}}\left(\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\text{, }\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right)
between two quantum

\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle

d_{\text{{FS}}}\left(\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\text{,
}\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\lambda\sqrt{1-\left|\left\langle\psi_{A}|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right|^{2}}\text{,}

(1)

with
\lambda
being an arbitrary real constant factor. Alternatively, the
Wootters distance
d_{\text{{W}}}\left(\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\text{, }\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right)
between two
quantum states
\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
is given by
laba17

d_{\text{{W}}}\left(\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\text{,
}\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\lambda\cos^{-1}\left[\left|\left\langle\psi_{A}|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right|\right]\text{.}

(2)

Interestingly, given two infinitesimally close neighboring pure quantum
states

\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle

and

\left|\psi\left(\xi+\Delta\xi\right)\right\rangle

that can be distinguished thanks to a real parameter

\xi
,
it happens that up to the second order in

\Delta\xi

with

\Delta\xi\ll 1
,
the differential forms of
the Wootters and the Fubini-Study distances are equivalent

ravicule97
;
dodonov99
.

Following the line of reasoning presented in Ref.
laba17
, let us
consider a set of quantum state vectors
\left\{\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right\}
parametrized by the parameters

\xi\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left(\xi^{1}\text{,..., }\xi^{m}\right)
. The
quantity

m

denotes the number of real parameters assumed
to parametrize a quantum state

\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle

in

\mathbb{C}P^{n-1}
.
For clarity, we assume here that

\mathcal{H}

is the

n
-dimensional complex Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{2}^{N}

of

N
-
qubit quantum states
with

n=2^{N}

and we focus on the simple case with

n=2
.
Then, regardless of the chosen definition of finite distance,
the infinitesimal line element
ds_{\text{{FS}}}^{2}
quantifying the
distance between two neighboring states
\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi\left(\xi+d\xi\right)\right\rangle

can be written as

ds_{\text{{FS}}}^{2}=g_{ab}\left(\xi\right)d\xi^{a}d\xi^{b}\text{.}

(3)

The quantity
g_{ab}\left(\xi\right)
in Eq. (
3
) is defined as

,

g_{ab}\left(\xi\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\lambda^{2}\left[\gamma_{ab}\left(\xi\right)-\beta_{a}\left(\xi\right)\beta_{b}\left(\xi\right)\right]\text{,}

(4)

where, given that
\partial_{a}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\partial/\partial\xi^{a}
, we have

\gamma_{ab}\left(\xi\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]\text{, and }\beta_{a}\left(\xi\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}-i\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\text{.}

(5)

Note from Eq. (
5
) that,

\beta_{a}\left(\xi\right)\beta_{b}\left(\xi\right)=\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\text{,}

(6)

since
\partial_{a}\left[\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]=0
implies that
\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle=-\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle
.
Therefore, using Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
), the metric tensor
components
g_{ab}\left(\xi\right)
defined in Eq. (
4
) become

g_{ab}\left(\xi\right)=\lambda^{2}\left\{\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]-\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right\}\text{.}

(7)

For notational simplicity, let us define

A_{ab}\left(\xi\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\text{.}

(8)

We shall prove that
A_{ab}\left(\xi\right)d\xi^{a}d\xi^{b}=\operatorname{Re}\left[A_{ab}\left(\xi\right)\right]d\xi^{a}d\xi^{b}
. Consider,

A_{ab}\left(\xi\right)=\operatorname{Re}\left[A_{ab}\left(\xi\right)\right]+i\operatorname{Im}\left[A_{ab}\left(\xi\right)\right]=A_{ab}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\xi\right)+iA_{ab}^{\left(2\right)}\left(\xi\right)\text{.}

(9)

Then, from Eq. (
8
) we have

\displaystyle A_{ab}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\xi\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[A_{ab}\left(\xi\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle^{\ast}\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle^{\ast}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\left[A_{ba}\left(\xi\right)\right]

\displaystyle=A_{ba}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\xi\right)\text{,}

(10)

and, in addition,

\displaystyle A_{ab}^{\left(2\right)}\left(\xi\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}\left[A_{ab}\left(\xi\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}\left[\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Im}\left[\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle^{\ast}\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle^{\ast}\right]

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Im}\left[\left\langle\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Im}\left[A_{ba}\left(\xi\right)\right]

From Eqs. (

A_{ab}\left(\xi\right)d\xi^{a}d\xi^{b}=\operatorname{Re}\left[A_{ab}\left(\xi\right)\right]d\xi^{a}d\xi^{b}

g_{ab}\left(\xi\right)=\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]-\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]\text{,}

g_{ab}\left(\xi\right)=\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle-\left\langle\partial_{a}\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\partial_{b}\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\right]\text{.}

(13)

Eq. (

\lambda=2
in Eq.

II.2
Quantum lines

This subsection is divided in two parts. In the first part, we discuss the
parametrization of quantum lines. In the second part, we show that geodesic
paths are quantum lines of minimal length between two initial and final pure
states on the Bloch sphere. Clearly, the notion of distance used here relies
on the concept of Fubini-Study metric introduced in the previous subsection.

II.2.1
Parametrization of quantum lines

Consider two normalized quantum state vectors
\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
belonging to a Hilbert
space
\mathcal{H}
such that,

\left\langle\psi_{A}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle=1\text{, and }\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{B}\right\rangle=1\text{.}

(14)

Note that we do not require
\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and

\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
to be orthogonal. Thus, in general,

\left\langle\psi_{A}|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\neq\delta_{AB}
with

\delta_{AB}
denoting the Kronecker

delta symbol. From
\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
, we can
consider a one-parameter
\xi\in\left(0\text{, }1\right)\subset\mathbb{R}
that specifies a parametric set of quantum state vectors
\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle
,

\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\left[\left(1-\xi\right)\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\xi\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]\text{.}

(15)

We point out that
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
is a relative phase to be properly selected by imposing that global phase
factors are not physically important in quantum mechanics and
\mathcal{N}_{\xi}
is a real normalization factor to be chosen in such a manner that

\left\langle\psi\left(\xi\right)|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle=1
. Furthermore, we note that
\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle
in Eq. (
15
) is the analogue of a straight line

\vec{r}\left(\xi\right)
in a flat Euclidean space that connects two
points
\vec{r}_{A}
and
\vec{r}_{B}
,

\vec{r}\left(\xi\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left(1-\xi\right)\vec{r}_{A}+\xi\vec{r}_{B}\text{.}

(16)

Therefore, it appears reasonable to regard the linear combination of the
states
\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
that defines
\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle
in Eq. (
15
) as a “geodesic” line in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
that connects these two state
vectors. To select the phase
\varphi
, we recall that unlike the classical
case in Eq. (
16
), the quantum case in Eq. (
15
) requires
that global phase factors are physically unimportant. Therefore, state vectors

\left|\psi_{j}\right\rangle
and
e^{i\varphi_{j}}\left|\psi_{j}\right\rangle
with
j\in\left\{A\text{, }B\right\}
are physically
indistinguishable and represent the same quantum state. For this reason, one
needs to impose that the “geodesic” line
connecting
\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
must coincide with the “geodesic” line connecting
\left|\tilde{\psi}_{A}\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}e^{i\varphi_{A}}\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\tilde{\psi}_{B}\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}e^{i\varphi_{B}}\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
.
Specifically, we require

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\left[\left(1-\xi\right)\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\xi\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\left[\left(1-\xi\right)\left|\tilde{\psi}_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\tilde{\varphi}}\xi\left|\tilde{\psi}_{B}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\left[\left(1-\xi\right)e^{i\varphi_{A}}\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\tilde{\varphi}}\xi e^{i\varphi_{B}}\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=e^{i\varphi_{A}}\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\left[\left(1-\xi\right)\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\tilde{\varphi}}\xi e^{i\left(\varphi_{B}-\varphi_{A}\right)}\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\left[\left(1-\xi\right)\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+\xi e^{i\left(\varphi_{B}-\varphi_{A}\right)}e^{i\tilde{\varphi}}\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]\text{,}

(17)

that is,

e^{i\varphi}=e^{i\left(\varphi_{B}-\varphi_{A}\right)}e^{i\tilde{\varphi}}\text{.}

(18)

Observe that in the last line of Eq. (
17
), the symbol
“
\sim
” denotes physical equivalence of
quantum states and not mathematical equivalence. Eq. (
18
) can be
satisfied by choosing

the phase factor
e^{i\varphi}
equal to

e^{i\varphi}=\frac{\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle}{\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|}\text{.}

(19)

Indeed
,
using Eqs. (
18
) and (
19
), we obtain

\displaystyle e^{i\left(\varphi_{B}-\varphi_{A}\right)}e^{i\tilde{\varphi}}

\displaystyle=e^{i\left(\varphi_{B}-\varphi_{A}\right)}\frac{\left\langle\tilde{\psi}_{B}|\tilde{\psi}_{A}\right\rangle}{\left|\left\langle\tilde{\psi}_{B}|\tilde{\psi}_{A}\right\rangle\right|}

\displaystyle=e^{i\left(\varphi_{B}-\varphi_{A}\right)}\frac{e^{-i\left(\varphi_{B}-\varphi_{A}\right)}\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle}{\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle}{\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|}

Therefore, employing the expression of the properly identified phase

in Eq. (

\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\left[\left(1-\xi\right)\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+\left(\frac{\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle}{\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|}\right)\xi\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]\text{.}

The last quantity that we need to specify in Eq. (

that is,

\mathcal{N}_{\xi}=\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\left(\xi\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2\xi\left(1-\xi\right)\left(1-\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|\right)}}\text{.}

\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle

\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\left[\left(1-\xi\right)\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+\frac{\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle}{\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|}\xi\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]}{\sqrt{1-2\xi\left(1-\xi\right)\left(1-\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|\right)}}\text{.}

(24)

At this point, to explicitly show that
\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle
in Eq. (

\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle

new parameter

\left|\psi\left(\theta\right)\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]\text{,}

where the normalization factor
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
can be obtained by
imposing the normalization constraint
\left\langle\psi\left(\theta\right)|\psi\left(\theta\right)\right\rangle=1

1=\left\langle\psi\left(\theta\right)|\psi\left(\theta\right)\right\rangle=\mathcal{N}_{\theta}^{2}\left[1+\sin\left(\theta\right)\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|\right]\text{,}

(26)

that is,

\mathcal{N}_{\theta}=\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\left(\theta\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sin\left(\theta\right)\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|}}\text{.}

(27)

Finally, using Eqs. (
25
) and (
27
),
\left|\psi\left(\theta\right)\right\rangle
becomes

\left|\psi\left(\theta\right)\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\left[\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]}{\sqrt{1+\sin\left(\theta\right)\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|}}\text{,}

(28)

where
e^{i\varphi}
in Eq. (
28
) equals
\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle/\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|
. For completeness, we emphasize that
\left|\psi\left(\xi\right)\right\rangle
in Eq. (
24
) and
\left|\psi\left(\theta\right)\right\rangle
in Eq. (
28
) are the same states. In
particular, the relation between the two parameters
\xi
and
\theta
can be
obtained as follows. From the condition,

\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\left[\left(1-\xi\right)\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\xi\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]=\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\right]\text{,}

(29)

we get

\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\left(1-\xi\right)=\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\text{, and }\mathcal{N}_{\xi}\xi=\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\text{.}

(30)

Manipulations of Eq. (
30
) yield,

\xi=\xi\left(\theta\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}{1+\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}\text{.}

(31)

Observe that
\xi\left(0\right)=0
,
\xi\left(\pi\right)=1
. Moreover,
for
\xi=0
and

\theta=0
,
\left|\psi\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
.

Finally, for
\xi=1
and
\theta=\pi
,

\left|\psi\right\rangle=e^{i\varphi}\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\sim\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
.

II.2.2
Geodesics as quantum lines of minimal length

We want to show here that the quantum line in Eq. (
28
) is a quantum
geodesic line. For simplicity, let us set
\left|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle=

\left|\dot{\psi}\left(\theta\right)\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left|\partial_{a}\psi\left(\theta\right)\right\rangle
where, in our case,
\partial_{a}=\partial_{\theta}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\partial/\partial\theta
(the path depends on a single
parameter). Then, we observe that the single Fubini-Study metric component in
Eq. (
13
) can be written in a number of alternative manners

\displaystyle g_{\text{{FS}}}\left(\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle-\left|\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle^{\ast}\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle-\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\psi\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle-\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\psi\right\rangle\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle+\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\psi\right\rangle\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\psi\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle+\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\psi\right\rangle^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle+\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle^{2}\right]\text{.}

(32)

In the third to last line of Eq. (
32
), we have used the fact that

\left\langle\psi|\psi\right\rangle=1
implies that
\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\psi\right\rangle+\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle=0
, that is,

\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\psi\right\rangle=-

\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle
.

Therefore
,
\operatorname{Re}\left(\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle\right)=0
and

\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle

is a pure imaginary
number

i\mathcal{A}

with

\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}
.
The real quantity

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}\left(t\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}-i\left\langle\psi\left(t\right)|\dot{\psi}\left(t\right)\right\rangle

is a very relevant
geometric quantity with a significant physical meaning. Indeed, it is
the

connection one form that specifies proper covariant
differentiation and, in addition, leads to the so-called horizontal lift
condition (i.e.,

i\mathcal{A}\left(t\right)=\left\langle\psi\left(t\right)|\dot{\psi}\left(t\right)\right\rangle=0
)

richard
. Moreover, we see later in Eq. (
88
) that the
connection one form is such that its line integral gives the geometric phase.
In what follows, we employ the relation

g_{\text{{FS}}}\left(\theta\right)=\lambda^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\left\langle\dot{\psi}|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle+\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle^{2}\right]\text{.}

(33)

We want to use Eq. (
28
) to evaluate
g_{\text{{FS}}}\left(\theta\right)
in Eq. (
33
). We proceed as follows. Note that,

\left|\psi\right\rangle=A\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi}B\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\text{, and }\left|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle=C\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi}D\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle\text{,}

(34)

where
A
,
B
,
C
, and
D
are given by,

\displaystyle A\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\text{, }B\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)

\displaystyle C\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathcal{\dot{N}}_{\theta}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)-\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}{2}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\text{, }D\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathcal{\dot{N}}_{\theta}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)+\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}{2}\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+a\sin\left(\theta\right)}}\text{, }a\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|\text{, }\mathcal{\dot{N}}_{\theta}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{-(a/2)\cos\left(\theta\right)}{\left[1+a\sin\left(\theta\right)\right]^{\frac{3}{2}}}\text{.}

(35)

Inserting Eq. (
34
) into Eq. (
33
), we obtain

g_{\text{{FS}}}\left(\theta\right)=\lambda^{2}\left\{C^{2}+D^{2}+2aCD+\left[AC+BD+a\left(AD+BC\right)\right]^{2}\right\}\text{.}

(36)

Then, using Eq. (
35
) along with performing a number of algebraic
manipulations,
g_{\text{{FS}}}\left(\theta\right)
in Eq.
(
36
) becomes

g_{\text{{FS}}}\left(\theta\right)=\frac{\lambda^{2}\left(1-a^{2}\right)}{8a\sin\theta-2a^{2}\cos 2\theta+2a^{2}+4}\text{,}

(37)

that is,

g_{\text{{FS}}}\left(\theta\right)=\lambda^{2}\frac{1-a^{2}}{4\left[1+a\sin\left(\theta\right)\right]^{2}}\text{.}

(38)

As a side remark, we point out that
\left\langle\psi|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle=\left[AC+BD+a\left(AD+BC\right)\right]^{2}=0
. Indeed, this is
expected since

i\mathcal{A}\left(t\right)=\left\langle\psi\left(t\right)|\dot{\psi}\left(t\right)\right\rangle=0

is the horizontal lift condition that yields geodesics on the Bloch sphere

richard
. Therefore, the length
s_{A\rightarrow B}
of the line
connecting the states
\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
defined as,

s_{A\rightarrow B}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\int_{0}^{\pi}g_{\text{FS}}^{1/2}\left(\theta\right)d\theta\text{,}

(39)

is given by,

s_{A\rightarrow B}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}{1+a\sin\left(\theta\right)}d\theta\text{.}

(40)

Performing a (Karl Weierstrass) change of variables (that is,
\theta\rightarrow t=t\left(\theta\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\tan\left(\theta/2\right)
),
s_{A\rightarrow B}
becomes

s_{A\rightarrow B}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\sqrt{1-a^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{t^{2}+2at+1}dt\text{.}

(41)

With the help of the Mathematica symbolic software, we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{t^{2}+2at+1}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\left[\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a+t}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\right)\right]_{t=0}^{t=\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\cos^{-1}\left(a\right)\text{.}

(42)

Finally, using Eq. (
42
) and recalling the definition of
a
in Eq.
(
35
), the length
s_{A\rightarrow B}
in Eq. (
41
) becomes

s_{A\rightarrow B}=\lambda\cos^{-1}\left[\left|\left\langle\psi_{B}|\psi_{A}\right\rangle\right|\right]\text{.}

(43)

Since the length
s_{A\rightarrow B}
in Eq. (
43
) of the line
connecting the states
\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
equals the Wootters distance. For
\lambda=2
, the
Wootters distance equals the angle between the vectors that identify the
initial and final states
\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
on the Bloch sphere. This angle represents the minimal
possible length of the path
\gamma
on the Bloch sphere connecting

\left|\psi_{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{B}\right\rangle
,

\mathrm{Length}\left(\gamma_{A\rightarrow B}^{\left(\mathrm{geodesic}\right)}\right)\leq\mathrm{Length}\left(\gamma_{A\rightarrow B}^{\left(\mathrm{non}\text{-}\mathrm{geodesic}\right)}\right)\text{, }

(44)

for any non-geodesic path
\gamma_{A\rightarrow B}^{\left(\mathrm{non}\text{-}\mathrm{geodesic}\right)}
. Therefore, we conclude that the quantum
line in Eq. (
28
) is indeed a quantum geodesic line.

III
Optimal-Speed Hamiltonian Evolution

In this section, having introduced the Fubini-Study metric tensor for pure
states along with the discussion of parametrizations of quantum geodesic paths
as paths of minimal length connecting given initial and final states on the
Bloch sphere, our two main tasks can be stated as follows. First, we introduce
the Hamiltonian operator H in Eq. (
53
) that achieves the fastest
possible unitary evolution between two given initial and final pure states

\left|A\right\rangle
and
\left|B\right\rangle
. Second, we
present the shortest time quantum dynamical trajectory in Eq. (
54
)
that emerges from H in Eq. (
53
) and that connects
\left|A\right\rangle
to
\left|B\right\rangle
.

III.1
The Hamiltonian

Following the work presented by Mostafazadeh in Ref.
ali09
, consider a
traceless and time-independent Hamiltonian H specified by a spectral
decomposition given by H
\overset{\text{def}}{=}E_{1}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle E_{1}\right|+E_{2}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle\left\langle E_{2}\right|
, where
\left\langle E_{2}|E_{1}\right\rangle=\delta_{21}
and
E_{2}\geq E_{1}
. Clearly,

\left\{E_{i}\right\}_{i=1,2}

and

\left\{\left|E_{i}\right\rangle\right\}_{i=1,2}

denote the eigenvalues and the
corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian

H.
Moreover
,
\delta_{ij}

with

1\leq i
,

j\leq 2
is the usual Kronecker delta symbol. One is interested in evolving a
state
\left|A\right\rangle
, not necessarily normalized, into a state

\left|B\right\rangle
in the shortest possible time by maximizing the
energy uncertainty
\Delta E
and obtain
\Delta E=\Delta E_{\max}
, with

\Delta E\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left[\frac{\left\langle A|\mathrm{H}^{2}\mathrm{|}A\right\rangle}{\left\langle A|A\right\rangle}-\left(\frac{\left\langle A|\mathrm{H|}A\right\rangle}{\left\langle A|A\right\rangle}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\text{.}

(45)

We maximize the energy uncertainty

\Delta E

since we see
later in Eq. (
81
) that the speed of quantum evolution

ds/dt

along

the curve is proportional to the energy
uncertainty

\Delta E
,
ds/dt\propto\Delta E
.
To
get the value of
\Delta E_{\max}
, we observe that an arbitrary unnormalized
initial state
\left|A\right\rangle
can be recast as
\left|A\right\rangle=\alpha_{1}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle+\alpha_{2}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle
where
\alpha_{1}\overset{\text{def}}{=}

\left\langle E_{1}|A\right\rangle
,
\alpha_{2}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left\langle E_{2}|A\right\rangle\in\mathbb{C}
. Then, after some straightforward algebra, we obtain

\Delta E=\frac{E_{2}-E_{1}}{2}\left[1-\left(\frac{\left|\alpha_{1}\right|^{2}-\left|\alpha_{2}\right|^{2}}{\left|\alpha_{1}\right|^{2}+\left|\alpha_{2}\right|^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\text{.}

(46)

We observe from Eq. (
46
) that the maximum value of
\Delta E
is
achieved when
\left|\alpha_{1}\right|=\left|\alpha_{2}\right|
and, in addition, is equal to

\Delta E_{\max}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left(\frac{E_{2}-E_{1}}{2}\right)\text{.}

(47)

A main idea underlying Mostafazadeh’s approach in Ref.
ali09
is
expressing H
\overset{\text{def}}{=}E_{1}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle E_{1}\right|+E_{2}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle\left\langle E_{2}\right|
by means of the initial and
final states
\left|A\right\rangle
and
\left|B\right\rangle
,
respectively, while keeping
\Delta E=\Delta E_{\max}
. To this end, note that

\left|A\right\rangle
and
\left|B\right\rangle
can be
decomposed as
\left|A\right\rangle=\alpha_{1}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle+\alpha_{2}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle
and
\left|B\right\rangle=\beta_{1}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle+\beta_{2}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle
, respectively. Moreover, we must put
\left|\alpha_{1}\right|=\left|\alpha_{2}\right|
and
\left|\beta_{1}\right|=\left|\beta_{2}\right|
to satisfy
\Delta E=\Delta E_{\max}
and, consequently, guarantee minimum travel time

T_{AB}^{\min}
. Therefore, set
\alpha_{2}=e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}\alpha_{1}

and
\beta_{2}=e^{i\varphi_{\beta}}\beta_{1}
with
\varphi_{\alpha}
and

\varphi_{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, states
\left|A\right\rangle
and
\left|B\right\rangle

can be recast as

\left|A\right\rangle=\alpha_{1}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle+\alpha_{2}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle=\alpha_{1}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}\alpha_{1}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle\text{,}

(48)

and,

\left|B\right\rangle=\beta_{1}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle+\beta_{2}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle=\beta_{1}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi_{\beta}}\beta_{1}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle\text{,}

(49)

respectively. Using Eqs. (
48
) and (
49
), let us introduce the
states
\left|\mathcal{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\mathcal{B}\right\rangle
defined by the relations
\left|E_{1}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle=\alpha_{1}^{-1}\left|A\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sqrt{2}\left|\mathcal{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|E_{1}\right\rangle+e^{i\varphi_{\beta}}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle=\beta_{1}^{-1}\left|B\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sqrt{2}e^{-i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}\left|\mathcal{B}\right\rangle
, respectively. The states

\left|\mathcal{A}\right\rangle

and

\left|\mathcal{B}\right\rangle

are being introduced to express the
Fubini-Study and the geodesic distances in terms of the modulus squared of
their quantum overlap and, in addition, to recast the optimal-speed quantum
Hamiltonian in a convenient form
.
After some matrix algebra
manipulations with Eqs. (
48
) and (
49
), we obtain

\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle\\
\left|E_{2}\right\rangle\end{array}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}+\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}-e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}+\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}&-e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}\\
-e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\frac{\alpha_{1}^{-1}}{\sqrt{2}}\left|A\right\rangle\\
\frac{\beta_{1}^{-1}}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}\left|B\right\rangle\end{array}\right)\text{.}

(50)

For completeness, we remark that

\left|\left\langle\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{B}\right\rangle\right|^{2}=\frac{\left|\left\langle A|B\right\rangle\right|^{2}}{\left\langle A|A\right\rangle\left\langle B|B\right\rangle}=\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)=\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{FS}}}{2}\right)\text{,}

(51)

where
\theta_{\mathrm{FS}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}=2s_{\text{{FS}}}=s_{\text{{geo}}}
, with

s_{\text{{FS}}}
and
s_{\text{{geo}}}
being the Fubini-Study
and the geodesic distances, respectively. Finally, observing that

E_{2}=-E_{1}\overset{\text{def}}{=}E
since the Hamiltonian H is
assumed to be traceless and employing Eq. (
50
), the spectral
decomposition of the Hamiltonian yields

\mathrm{H}=\frac{iE}{\sin\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)}\left[\left|\mathcal{B}\right\rangle\left\langle\mathcal{A}\right|-\left|\mathcal{A}\right\rangle\left\langle\mathcal{B}\right|\right]\text{.}

(52)

Finally, using Eq. (
50
) along with recalling the definitions of
states
\left|\mathcal{A}\right\rangle
and
\left|\mathcal{B}\right\rangle
, we note that the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
52
) can be
expressed in terms of the initial and final states
\left|A\right\rangle
and
\left|B\right\rangle
, respectively, as

\mathrm{H}=iE\cot\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)\left[\frac{\left|B\right\rangle\left\langle A\right|}{\left\langle A|B\right\rangle}-\frac{\left|A\right\rangle\left\langle B\right|}{\left\langle B|A\right\rangle}\right]\text{.}

(53)

Eq. (
53
) describes the correct version of the Hamiltonian specifying
the optimal-speed unitary time evolution as originally proposed in Ref.

ali09
. For completeness, observe that for
\mathrm{H}
in Eq.
(
53
), we correctly get
\left\langle A|\mathrm{H|}A\right\rangle/\left\langle A|A\right\rangle=0
and
\Delta E=\left[\left\langle A|\mathrm{H}^{2}|A\right\rangle/\left\langle A|A\right\rangle\right]^{1/2}=E=\Delta E_{\max}
.

III.2
The quantum dynamical trajectory

Given the Hamiltonian
\mathrm{H}
in Eq. (
53
), we shall find the
quantum dynamical trajectory
t\mapsto\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle
with
\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}e^{-\frac{i}{\hslash}\mathrm{H}t}\left|A\right\rangle
connecting initial and final states
\left|A\right\rangle
and
\left|B\right\rangle
, respectively. We shall find
that
\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle
can be written as,

\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle=\left[\cos\left(\frac{E}{\hslash}t\right)-\frac{\cos\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)}\sin\left(\frac{E}{\hslash}t\right)\right]\left|A\right\rangle+\frac{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}{\sin\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)}\sin\left(\frac{E}{\hslash}t\right)\left|B\right\rangle\text{, }

(54)

where
0\leq t\leq T_{AB}^{\min}
with
T_{AB}^{\min}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\hslash\theta_{\mathrm{FS}}/\left(2E\right)
.

First, since we restrict our attention to a traceless Hamiltonian with

E_{2}=-E_{1}=E
), we note that

\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle=e^{-\frac{i}{\hslash}\mathrm{H}t}\left|A\right\rangle=\alpha_{1}e^{\frac{i}{\hslash}Et}\left|E_{1}\right\rangle+\alpha_{2}e^{-\frac{i}{\hslash}Et}\left|E_{2}\right\rangle\text{.}

(55)

Using Eq. (
50
),
\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle
in
Eq. (
55
) becomes

\displaystyle\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}+\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}e^{\frac{i}{\hslash}Et}}{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}+\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}-e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}-\frac{e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}e^{-\frac{i}{\hslash}Et}}{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}+\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}-e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}\right]\left|A\right\rangle+

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}e^{-\frac{i}{\hslash}Et}}{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}+\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}-e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}-\frac{e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}e^{\frac{i}{\hslash}Et}}{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}+\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}-e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}\right]\left|B\right\rangle\text{.}

(56)

After some tedious but straightforward algebra, we note that

\frac{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}+\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}+\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}-e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}=-\frac{1}{2i}\frac{e^{-i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}{\sin\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)}\text{,}

(57)

and, in addition,

-\frac{e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}+\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}-e^{i\varphi_{\alpha}}e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}=\frac{1}{2i}\frac{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}{\sin\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)}\text{.}

(58)

Therefore, making use of Eqs. (
58
) and (
57
), Eq.
(
56
) yields

\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle=\operatorname{Re}\left[-i\frac{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}{\sin\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)}e^{-\frac{i}{\hslash}Et}\right]\left|A\right\rangle+\frac{e^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}}{\sin\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)}\sin\left(\frac{E}{\hslash}t\right)\left|B\right\rangle\text{.}

(59)

To simplify Eq. (
59
), we observe that

\operatorname{Re}\left[-ie^{i\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}}e^{-\frac{i}{\hslash}Et}\right]=\cos\left(\frac{E}{\hslash}t\right)\sin\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)-\sin\left(\frac{E}{\hslash}t\right)\cos\left(\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}-\varphi_{\beta}}{2}\right)\text{.}

(60)

V.1
Geodesics as unit geometric efficiency curves

V.2
Geodesics as null phase curves

In this second subsection, we check that the curve in ray space is a null
phase curve
karol
;
mukunda93
;
mittal22
). We shall check this condition by
showing that the total phase along the horizontal lift of a geodesic in the
projective Hilbert space is zero. This is a necessary (but not sufficient;
there are null phase curves that are not necessarily geodesic curves)
condition to be satisfied by a geodesic curve in ray space as recently
stressed in Refs.
mittal22
. Before presenting the simple check, we
provide some basic mathematical background along with some relevant historical
remarks on the concept of geometric phase.

Basic background
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\backslash\left\{0\right\}
denote the Hilbert space described by an
\left(N+1\right)
-dimensional
complex vector space of normalized state vectors
\left\{\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle\right\}
. In quantum mechanics, a
physical state is not represented by a normalized state vector
\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}\backslash\left\{0\right\}
. Instead, physical states are represented by a ray. A ray is the
one-dimensional subspace
\left\{e^{i\phi\left(t\right)}\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle:e^{i\phi\left(t\right)}\in U(1)\right\}
of
\mathcal{H}\backslash\left\{0\right\}
to which this
vector
\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle
belongs. Two state
vectors
\left|\psi_{1}\left(t\right)\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{2}\left(t\right)\right\rangle
that belong to the same ray are
equivalent,
\left|\psi_{1}\left(t\right)\right\rangle\sim\left|\psi_{2}\left(t\right)\right\rangle
, if
\left|\psi_{1}\left(t\right)\right\rangle=e^{i\phi_{12}\left(t\right)}\left|\psi_{2}\left(t\right)\right\rangle
for some
\phi_{12}\left(t\right)\in\mathbb{R}
. The equivalence relation “
\sim
” gives
rise to equivalence classes on the
\left(2N+1\right)
-dimensional sphere

S^{2N+1}
. The set of all equivalence classes
S^{2N+1}/U\left(1\right)

determines the space of rays (that is, the space of physical states). In
general,
S^{2N+1}/U\left(1\right)
is called the projective Hilbert space

\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
.

This relation between state vectors in Hilbert space and rays in projective
Hilbert space mediated by phase factors can be nicely described in terms of
the fiber bundle formalism
karol
;
nakahara
. Roughly speaking, the main
ingredients of a fiber bundle are a total space
E
, a base space
M
, a fiber
space
\mathrm{F}
, a group
G
acting on the fibers, and a projection map

\pi
that projects the fibers above to points in
M
. In quantum mechanics,

\mathcal{H}\backslash\left\{0\right\}
plays the role of
E
,

\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
plays the part of
M
,
U\left(1\right)
plays the role of
G
, fibers in
F
are represented by all unit
vectors from the same ray and, finally, the projection map
\pi
given by

\pi:\mathcal{H}\backslash\left\{0\right\}\ni\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle\mapsto\pi\left(\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\left(t\right)\right|\in\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)\text{,}

(82)

plays the part of the projection in the fiber bundle construction. For more
details on the fiber bundle formalism, we refer to Refs.

nakahara
;
eguchi80

bohm91

P_{\parallel}^{2}=P_{\parallel}

is the null operator. Using

samuel88

For

Remarks on the geometric phase
. In June of 1983, considering an
adiabatic (i.e., slow varying parameters) and cyclic quantum evolution of a
quantum state
\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle
as an
eigenstate of the Hamiltonian H
\left(t\right)
of the system in
a time interval
T
, Michael Berry discovered that the quantum state gains a
geometrical phase factor in addition to the usual dynamical phase factor

berry84
. The original state
\left|\psi\left(0\right)\right\rangle
returns to itself up to a phase factor,

\left|\psi\left(T\right)\right\rangle=e^{i\phi_{\mathrm{tot}}\left(T\right)}\left|\psi\left(0\right)\right\rangle\text{.}

(85)

The total phase
\phi_{\mathrm{tot}}\left(T\right)
in Eq. (
85
) is
the sum of the dynamical phase
\phi_{\mathrm{dynamical}}\left(T\right)

and the geometric phase
\phi_{\mathrm{geometric}}\left(T\right)
,

\phi_{\mathrm{tot}}\left(T\right)=\phi_{\mathrm{dynamical}}\left(T\right)+\phi_{\mathrm{geometric}}\left(T\right)\text{,}

(86)

with
\phi_{\mathrm{dynamical}}\left(T\right)
defined as

\phi_{\mathrm{dynamical}}\left(T\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}-\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\left\langle\psi\left(t\right)|\mathrm{H}\left(t\right)|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle}{\left\langle\psi\left(t\right)|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle}dt\text{.}

(87)

In the fiber bundle description of quantum mechanics
eguchi80
, one can
regard the space of normalized states as a fiber bundle over the space of
rays, with the bundle having a natural connection that allows to compare the
phases on two neighboring states. In October of 1983, using this fiber bundle
formalism, Barry Simon interpreted in
simon83
the geometric phase

\phi_{\mathrm{geometric}}\left(T\right)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\phi_{\mathrm{tot}}\left(T\right)-\phi_{\mathrm{dynamical}}\left(T\right)
as the line integral of the Abelian connection one-form
A
(for
the geometrical phase on
\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
) over a
closed path
l
in the projective Hilbert space
\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
,

\phi_{\mathrm{geometric}}\left(T\right)={\displaystyle\oint\limits_{l}}\mathcal{A}=\int_{\Sigma}\mathcal{F}\text{.}

(88)

The second equality in Eq. (
88
) is a consequence of Stokes’ theorem
(i.e., recasting a line integral as a surface integral), with
\mathcal{F}\overset{\text{def}}{=}D\mathcal{A}
denoting the Abelian curvature two-form
and
\Sigma
being any surface bounded by
l
in
\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
. The geometrical nature of this phase in Eq.
(
88
) is justified by its dependence solely on the closed path
evolution of the ray in the projective Hilbert space and, moreover, by its
complete independence on any aspect of the Hamiltonian that governs the
dynamical evolution. In 1987, Aharonov and Anandan showed in
aa87
that
the adiabaticity requirement is unnecessary for the emergence of geometric
phases in cyclic quantum evolutions. In 1988, Samuel and Bhandari introduced
in
samuel88
a general setting for Berry’s geometric phase in which
neither unitarity nor cyclicity of the quantum evolution are required. In a
series of works between 1991 and 1995
pati91
;
pati94
;
pati95
, Pati
devoted a serious effort in describing the relation between phases and
distances in quantum evolutions, both cyclic
pati94
and noncyclic

pati95
. In
pati95
, Pati expressed the geometric phase factor

e^{i\phi_{\mathrm{geometric}}\left(T\right)}
in a noncyclic evolution
with
0\leq t\leq T
in terms of the horizontal lift
\left|\bar{\psi}\left(t\right)\right\rangle
of a curve in the projective Hilbert space

\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
as

e^{i\phi_{\mathrm{geometric}}\left(T\right)}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\left\langle\bar{\psi}\left(0\right)|\bar{\psi}\left(T\right)\right\rangle}{\left|\left\langle\bar{\psi}\left(0\right)|\bar{\psi}\left(T\right)\right\rangle\right|}\text{. }

(89)

The horizontal lift
\left|\bar{\psi}\left(t\right)\right\rangle
is
defined in terms of the state
\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle
that satisfies
i\hslash\partial_{t}\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle=\mathrm{H}\left(t\right)\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle
as
pati95
,

\left|\bar{\psi}\left(t\right)\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}e^{\frac{i}{\hslash}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\psi\left(t^{\prime}\right)|\mathrm{H}\left(t^{\prime}\right)|\psi\left(t^{\prime}\right)\right\rangle dt^{\prime}}\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle\text{,}

(90)

with
\left\langle\bar{\psi}\left(t\right)|\partial_{t}\bar{\psi}\left(t\right)\right\rangle=0
. Interestingly, observe that the phase factor in
the horizontal lift

\left|\bar{\psi}\left(t\right)\right\rangle

in Eq. (
90
) can be

expressed
in terms of the connection

\mathcal{A}\left(t\right)
.

Indeed, since

i\hslash\partial_{t}\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle=\mathrm{H}\left(t\right)\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle

and

\left\langle\psi\left(t\right)\left|\dot{\psi}\left(t\right)\right.\right\rangle=i\mathcal{A}\left(t\right)
,
we have

i\left\langle\psi\left(t\right)\left|\mathrm{H}\right|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle=-i\hslash\mathcal{A}\left(t\right)
.
Therefore, Eq.
(
90
) can be recast as

\left|\bar{\psi}\left(t\right)\right\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}e^{-\frac{i}{\hslash}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{A}\left(t^{\prime}\right)dt^{\prime}}\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right\rangle\text{.}

(91)

In 1993, Mukunda and Simon provided in
mukunda93
a very general setting
for the geometric phase for any smooth open curve of unit vectors in Hilbert
space by employing the kinematics of the Hilbert space of states of a general
quantum system along with a properly defined gauge transformation group. In
particular, they showed that the total phase along the horizontal lift of a
geodesic curve in
\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
is zero.
Therefore, since the dynamical phase along an horizontal lift is zero, they
concluded that geodesics are null (geometric) phase curves. In the geodesic
curve scenario,
\left\langle\bar{\psi}\left(0\right)|\bar{\psi}\left(T\right)\right\rangle
is real and positive. Therefore,
\left\langle\bar{\psi}\left(0\right)|\bar{\psi}\left(T\right)\right\rangle=\left|\left\langle\bar{\psi}\left(0\right)|\bar{\psi}\left(T\right)\right\rangle\right|
in Eq. (
89
),
e^{i\phi_{\mathrm{geometric}}\left(T\right)}=1
and, finally,
\phi_{\mathrm{geometric}}\left(T\right)=0
. In conclusion,

\phi_{\mathrm{geometric}}^{\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{geodesic}}\right)}\left(T\right)=0

(92)

is the necessary but not sufficient condition for geodesic behavior of a curve

\gamma_{\mathrm{geodesic}}
in
\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
.
Before presenting this verification, we present for completeness a quick
remark. As pointed out earlier, we stated there exist curves connecting two
pure state that are not necessarily the shortest curves for which, however,
the gained geometric phase is zero. These curves generalize the concept of
geodesic curve and are known in the literature as null phase curves (NPCs,

rabei99
;
mukunda03
;
chaturvedi13
). A geodesic is a NPC. The converse, in
general, is false. The generalization involved in transitioning from geodesics
to NPCs emerges especially when the dimensionality of the complex Hilbert
space is greater than or equal to three. Indeed, in a two-dimensional Hilbert
space, the ray space is the Poincaré sphere and NPCs are great circles
arcs or geodesics on the sphere
\mathcal{S}^{2}
. A NPC connecting any two
nonantipodal points on
\mathcal{S}^{2}
is either the corresponding geodesic,
or it may navigate some extended region of the corresponding great circle.
When
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{H}>2
, NPCs are more numerous than geodesics and there are
infinitely many NPCs connecting any two nonorthogonal points in ray space
(against a single geodesic). Examples of NPCs that are not (free) geodesics
can be found in the framework of the so-called constrained geodesics

rabei99
. Unlike free geodesics, constrained geodesics are paths of
minimum length connecting pairs of points on a smooth submanifold of the
complete ray space that specifies the physical system under consideration. Two
illustrative physical examples of such curves are constrained geodesics on the
submanifolds of single mode coherent states and normalized Gaussian pure
states. In both cases, the constrained geodesics differ from the free
geodesics. In the Gaussian case, for instance, constrained geodesic paths are
assumed to be traversing solely centered normalized Gaussian wave functions
and not even superpositions of Gaussians are to be considered. However, in
both examples, it happens that constrained geodesics are also null phase
curves. For more details, we refer to Ref.
rabei99
. For a formal
definition of a NPC in terms of a real and positive Bargmann invariant of
third order, we refer to Ref.
mukunda03
. We now return to the verification.

VI
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we presented an explicit geodesic analysis of the dynamical
trajectories that emerge from the quantum evolution of a single-qubit quantum
state. The evolution is governed by an Hermitian Hamiltonian operator that
leads to the fastest possible unitary evolution between given initial and
final states. To achieve maximum clarity, we proceeded in a step-by-step
fashion. First, we reviewed preliminary material on the geometry of pure
quantum states. In particular, we emphasized the notions of quantum lines
(Eqs. (
24
) and (
28
)) and quantum geodesic lines (Eq.
(
43
)) on a curved manifold of pure states equipped with the
Fubini-Study metric (Eq. (
13
)). Second, we introduced an optimal-speed
Hamiltonian evolution (Eq. (
53
)) and calculated the corresponding
shortest time quantum dynamical trajectory traced by the evolved quantum state
(Eq. (
54
)). Finally, combining facts and results discussed in the
first two steps, we explicitly checked in the final third step the geodesicity
of the quantum dynamical trajectory emerging from the chosen optimal-speed
Hamiltonian evolution via the geodesicity condition in Eq. (
43
). The
key observation in this derivation was the clever sequential change of
parametrizations (Eqs. (
54
), (
63
), (
71
) and
Figure 1 along with Table I) that leads, finally, to the geodesic path on the
Bloch sphere. For completeness, we also studied the geodesic behavior of the
quantum trajectory in terms of the concepts of geometric efficiency in Eq.
(
79
) and of Berry’s geometric phase in Eq. (
88
).
Working out in detail the optimal-speed Hamiltonian evolution of a two-level
quantum system specified in Eq. (
53
), we provided three alternative
perspectives on the geodesic behavior of a curve
\gamma_{A\rightarrow B}\left(t\right)
with
0\leq t\leq T
in ray space
\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
(with
\mathcal{H=H}_{2}^{1}
and
\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{1}\right)=\mathbb{C}P^{1}\cong S^{2}
):

[i]

Geodesics can be viewed as minimal length curves (Eq.
(
44
)),

\mathrm{Length}\left(\gamma_{A\rightarrow B}^{\left(\mathrm{geodesic}\right)}\right)\leq\mathrm{Length}\left(\gamma_{A\rightarrow B}^{\left(\mathrm{non}\text{-}\mathrm{geodesic}\right)}\right)\text{.}

(93)

[ii]

Geodesics can be regarded as unit geometric efficiency curves
(Eq. (
80
)),

\eta_{\mathrm{geometric}}^{\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{geodesic}}\right)}=1\text{.}

(94)

[iii]

Geodesics can be considered as null phase curves (Eq.
(
92
)),

\phi_{\mathrm{geometric}}^{\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{geodesic}}\right)}\left(T\right)=0\text{.}

(95)

As pointed out in the paper, the relations in Eqs. (
93
) and (
94
)
provide necessary and sufficient geodesicity conditions. The relation in Eq.
(
95
), instead, is only a necessary condition.

From a quantum mechanics standpoint, we focused in this paper on the study of
the time-optimal evolution of closed two-level quantum systems specified by
pure states driven by the Schrödinger equation. From a geometry viewpoint,
we used the Fubini-Study distance measure since it is the only natural choice
for a measure that defines “random states”.
Specifically, the distance between density matrices for both pure and mixed
states must decrease under coarse-graining (i.e., randomization), if the
distance expresses statistical distinguishability
petz96a
;
petz99
. In
this respect, the Fubini-Study metric is the only monotone Riemannian metric
on the space of pure quantum states. However, a more realistic scenario is the
case of open system dynamics in mixed quantum states

taddei13
;
adolfo13
;
deffner13
. In this case, a number of new challenges
are expected to emerge. First, one needs to consider general nonunitary
quantum evolutions where the dynamics is described by a master equation in the
Lindblad form. Finding exact analytical expressions for the actual dynamical
trajectories traced by an open quantum system in a mixed quantum state can be
rather complicated
carlini08
;
brody19
. For an explicit discussion on
some conceptual and computational difficulties in finding the time-optimal
quantum evolution of mixed states governed by a master equation, we refer to
Ref.
carlini08
. Indeed, even limiting the attention to the unitary
evolution of closed physical systems, optimal-time evolutions of mixed (pure)
states are typically generated by time-varying (constant) Hamiltonians

hornedal22
. Second, from a geometric perspective, there are infinitely
many monotone Riemannian metrics on the space of mixed quantum states as
specified by the Morozova-Cencov-Petz theorem
petz96a
;
petz99
.

Therefore, there is the freedom to choose a variety of distance measures
between mixed states. There is the need to study several measures, each of
them with specific physical motivations, convenience, disadvantage. Arguably,
the main hurdle when investigating open systems in mixed quantum states is
this nonuniqueness of the metric. However, even assuming to have chosen the
metric and having ready to use the actual dynamical trajectory of the system,
it is generally not straightforward finding closed form expressions of
geodesic paths on arbitrary manifolds of mixed quantum states equipped with
Riemann metrics of statistical relevance. In a few cases, however, this task
can be successfully

accomplished
weis13
. For example, geodesic
paths connecting two mixed states are known for some metrics, including the
Quantum Fisher information (QFI) metric
uhlmann95
, the Wigner-Yanase
metric
gibilisco03
, and the metric based on the trace distance

cai17
. In particular, in the case of the Bures metric

bures69
;
uhlmann76
;
hubner92
(or, alternatively, QFI metric with

g_{\mu\nu}^{\left(\mathrm{QFI}\right)}=4g_{\mu\nu}^{\left(\mathrm{Bures}\right)}
),
formulas for geodesic paths can be
presented in terms of projections of large circles on a sphere in a purifying
space
dittman95
. Furthermore, in the Wigner-Yanase metric case

wigner63
;
luo03
, it is possible to provide explicit expressions for the
geodesic path, geodesic distance, and, finally, sectional and scalar
curvatures. These relations were originally determined by Gibilisco and Isola
in Ref.
gibilisco03
by mimicking from a quantum-mechanical perspective
the classical pull-back approach to the Fisher information.

Being aware of the above mentioned challenges, our analysis presented in this
paper could be potentially extended in several ways. First, keeping the
dimensionality of the Hilbert space fixed at two, we could build on our
findings to pursue a comparative analysis of geodesic paths of qubits in mixed
quantum states inside the Bloch sphere equipped with different Riemannian
metrics
erik20
;
cafaroprd22
. Second, deviating from the condition of
optimal-speed quantum Hamiltonian evolutions
carlo19A
, we could rely on
our work to characterize nearly-optimal quantum evolutions in

geometric terms by focusing on variations from geodesics and, moreover, on
curvature effects between nearby geodesic paths

cafaro07
;
cafaro11
;
laba17
. Third, as the dimension of the Hilbert space
increases, we expect the geometry of the state spaces to become richer with a
very intricate structure. New features can arise in higher-dimensional Hilbert
spaces, including multipartite quantum entanglement. A first step in this
higher-dimensional setting could be that of considering the geometry of the
Bloch vector

for

d
-
level quantum systems

(i.e., qudits) with

d>2

bertlmann08
. In particular, it
would seem reasonable to begin by studying the geometry of a qutrit (i.e., a
three-level quantum system) specified by

d=3

kurzynski16
;
goyal16
.

In conclusion, despite its limited focus on dimension
d=2
, we hope our work
will stimulate other researchers and open the way toward further explicit
investigations on the interplay between quantum mechanics and geometry. For
the time being, we leave a more in-depth quantitative discussion on these
potential geometric extensions of our analytical findings, including
generalizations to mixed state geometry and quantum evolutions, to future
scientific efforts.
