The weak Lefschetz property for Standard graded, Artinian Gorenstein algebras of embedding dimension four and socle degree three Thanks: We made extensive use of Macaulay2 [ 15 ] . We are very appreciative of this computer algebra system.
Andrew R. Kustin

Abstract
. Let 𝒌 {\boldsymbol{k}} be an arbitrary field and A A be a standard graded Artinian Gorenstein 𝒌 {\boldsymbol{k}} -algebra of embedding dimension four and socle degree three. Then, except for exactly one exception, A A has the weak Lefschetz property. Furthermore, the exception occurs only in characteristic two.

1.
Introduction.

The Lefschetz property is a ring-theoretic abstraction of the Hard Lefschetz
Theorem for compact Kähler manifolds.
Let
{\boldsymbol{k}}
be a field. A graded
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-algebra
A
, equal to
\bigoplus_{i=0}^{s}A_{i}
, has the weak Lefschetz property if there is a linear form
\ell\in A_{1}
so that multiplication by
\ell
from
A_{i}
to
A_{i+1}
has maximal rank for each
i
.
(Similarly,
A

has the strong Lefschetz property if multiplication by
\ell^{s}
has maximal
rank in each degree for every positive integer
s
for some
\ell\in A_{1}
.) Stanley introduced the concept in
[
27
]
where he proved that if the characteristic of
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is zero,
P={\boldsymbol{k}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, and
A=P/(x_{1}^{a_{1}},\dots,x_{n}^{a_{n}})
, then
A
has the strong Lefschetz property. Other early proofs of Stanley’s theorem were also given by
[
25
]
and
[
28
]
.

Our interest in the weak Lefschetz property for fields of arbitrary characteristic is a consequence of our desire to record
the minimal resolution of
A
by free
P
-modules in terms of the coefficients of the Macaulay inverse system which determines
A
. See
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
, and especially
[
10
]
,
where this project is carried out for
quotient rings
A
with Castelnuovo regularity two as a
P
-module, and
[
11
]
for quotient rings
A
of
regularity three
in three variables.
(We also have results along these lines pertaining to ideals of
regularity three in four and five variables.)
We are able to carry out this project
provided
A
has the weak Lefschetz property, independent of the characteristic of
{\boldsymbol{k}}
.
The absence of the weak Lefschetz property is an obstruction to this project; but positive characteristic, in and of itself, does not cause any problem.

The weak Lefschetz property is very sensitive to change in characteristic.
In positive characteristic
p
,
Brenner and
Kaid
[
4
]

gave an explicit description of those
d
and
p
for which

P/(x^{d}_{1},x^{d}_{2},\dots,x^{d}_{n})
has the weak Lefschetz property
when
P
equals
{\boldsymbol{k}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is a field of characteristic
p
, and
n=3
.
The paper
[
21
]
is devoted to the analogous project for
4\leq n
.

Artinian rings with socle degree three are somewhat mysterious.
Bøgvad’s
[
3
]
examples of Artinian Gorenstein rings with transcendental Poincaré series have socle degree three. Rossi and Şega
[
26
]
prove that if
R
is a compressed Artinian Gorenstein local ring with
socle degree not equal to three, then the Poincaré series of all finitely generated
R
-modules are rational, sharing a common
denominator. Similarly, it is shown in
[
20
]
that if
R
is a compressed local Artinian ring
with odd top socle degree at least five, then the Poincaré
series of all finitely generated
R
-modules are rational, sharing a common denominator.
The same conclusion does not hold when the top socle degree is three.

Theorem
1.1
is the main result in the paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{k}}
be a field
and
A
be a standard graded Artinian Gorenstein
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-algebra of embedding dimension four and socle degree three.
If the characteristic of
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is different than two, then
A
has the weak Lefschetz property.
If the characteristic of
A
is equal to two, then

A
has the weak Lefschetz property
if and only if
A
is not isomorphic to

(1.1.1)

\frac{{\boldsymbol{k}}[x,y,z,w]}{(xy,xz,xw,y^{2},z^{2},w^{2},x^{3}+yzw)}.

We first demonstrate that the
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-algebra
A
of (
1.1.1
) does not satisfy the weak Lefschetz property. Indeed, let
\ell=ax+by+cz+dw
be an arbitrary nonzero linear form in
A
. If at least one of the parameters
b
,
c
, or
d
is nonzero, then let
\ell^{\prime}
be the nonzero linear form
\ell^{\prime}=by+cz+dw\in A_{1}
. Observe that

\ell\ell^{\prime}=ax(by+cz+dw)+b^{2}y^{2}+c^{2}z^{2}+d^{2}w^{2}=0\in A.

If
b=c=d=0
, then let
\ell^{\prime}
be the nonzero linear form
\ell^{\prime}=y
of
A_{1}
.
Observe that

\ell\ell^{\prime}=axy=0
in
A
.
In either case, the arbitrary nonzero linear form
\ell
is zero divisor on
A
.

In the rest of the paper we show that if
A
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, but is not isomorphic to the ring of (
1.1.1
), then
A
has the weak Lefschetz property. We use a Macaulay inverse system for
A
.

Let
U
be the vector space
A_{1}
,
P
be the polynomial ring
P=\operatorname{Sym}_{\bullet}U
,
I
be a homogeneous ideal of
P
with
A
isomorphic to
P/I
,
U^{*}
be the dual space
\operatorname{Hom}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}(U,{\boldsymbol{k}})
of
U
, and
D_{\bullet}U^{*}
be the divided power
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-algebra
\bigoplus_{0\leq i}D_{i}U^{*}
, with

D_{i}U^{*}=\operatorname{Hom}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}(\operatorname{Sym}_{i}U,{\boldsymbol{k}}).

The rules for a divided power algebra are recorded in
[
16
, Section 7]
or
[
7
, Appendix 2]
. (In practice these rules say that
w^{(n)}
behaves like
w^{n}/(n!)
would behave if
n!
were a unit in
R
.)

Macaulay duality guarantees that

\operatorname{ann}_{D_{\bullet}U^{*}}I

is a cyclic
P
-submodule of
D_{\bullet}U^{*}
generated by an element
in
D_{3}U^{*}
. (Any generator

\operatorname{ann}_{D_{\bullet}U^{*}}I

in
D_{3}U^{*}

is called
a Macaulay inverse system for
A
.) Furthermore, if
\phi_{3}
is a Macaulay inverse system for
A
, then

I=\operatorname{ann}_{P}\phi_{3}.

The hypothesis that
A
has embedding dimension four ensures that

(1.1.2)

\ell\phi_{3}\neq 0\quad\text{for any nonzero $\ell$ in $U$.}

We observe that the Macaulay inverse system for the
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-algebra
A
of (
1.1.1
) is

(1.1.3)

\phi_{3}=x^{*(3)}+y^{*}z^{*}w^{*}.

In section
3
we put the Macaulay inverse system into the form

(1.1.4)

\displaystyle\alpha\phi_{3}={}

\displaystyle x^{*(3)}+x^{*}\phi_{2,0}+\phi_{3,0}

or

(1.1.5)

\displaystyle\alpha\phi_{3}={}

\displaystyle x^{*(2)}y^{*}+x^{*}\phi_{2,0}+\phi_{3,0},

for some unit
\alpha
,
with
\phi_{i,0}\in D_{i}({\boldsymbol{k}}y^{*}\oplus{\boldsymbol{k}}z^{*}\oplus{\boldsymbol{k}}w^{*})
.
We study various cases depending on whether
\phi_{3}
has form (
1.1.4
) or (
1.1.5
) and also depending on how complicated
\phi_{2,0}
is. We treat the four significant cases in Sections
4
,
5
,
6
, and
7
.
All of our calculations employ the homomorphism

\textstyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}:D_{d}U\otimes\bigwedge^{d}U\to\bigwedge^{d}U^{*}

which is introduced in section
2.B
. The connection between
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}
and the weak Lefschetz property is explained in Lemma
2.9
. Roughly speaking,

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(\ell^{(d)}\otimes x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{d})=\ell x_{1}\phi_{3}\wedge\ldots\wedge\ell x_{d}\phi_{3}\in\textstyle\bigwedge^{d}U^{*},

for
\ell,x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\in U
and
\phi_{3}\in U^{*}
. It is shown in Lemma
2.9
that if
\phi_{3}
is a Macaulay inverse system for
A
and
\ell\phi_{3}
is nonzero for all nonzero
\ell
in
U
, then

\text{$A$ has the weak Lefschetz property}\iff\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}\text{ is not identically zero.}

The hypothesis that
\ell\phi_{3}
is nonzero whenever
\ell
in
U
is nonzero is innocuous. It merely says that the embedding dimension of
A
is equal to the number of variables of
P
. If this hypothesis is not satisfied, then one can view
A
as a quotient of a polynomial ring with one fewer variable than
P
has.

Traditionally, the
Lefschetz properties are studied in a graded
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-algebra where

{\boldsymbol{k}}
is a field
of characteristic zero.
In particular, for example, Gondim and Zappala
[
13
, Cor. 5.5]
have proven that a standard graded Gorenstein
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-algebra of small codimension, with socle degree three, and presented by quadrics, has the weak Lefschetz property, provided the field
{\boldsymbol{k}}
has characteristic zero.
Duality is obtained using the algebra of differential operators
Q={\boldsymbol{k}}[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}]
.
Every graded Artinian Gorenstein algebra
A
has a
presentation of the form

(1.1.6)

A\cong Q/\operatorname{ann}_{Q}F,

for some
n
and some homogeneous polynomial
F\in{\boldsymbol{k}}[x_{1},...,x_{n}]
, where

\operatorname{ann}_{Q}F=\{\phi\in Q\mid\phi(F(x))=0\}.

The Hessian
of the form
F
is the determinant of the square matrix
(\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}})
of second order partial derivatives of
F
.
When
{\boldsymbol{k}}
has characteristic zero, Watanabe (see
[
29
,
22
]
) has shown that
(
1.1.6
) fails to have the Strong Lefschetz Property if and only if one of the
non-trivial higher Hessians of
F
vanishes.
This result has been generalized to the weak Lefschetz property using “mixed
Hessians”, see
[
13
]
.

The study of when the Hessian of a homogeneous form vanishes has a very long history and continues to be active. For the time being keep
{\boldsymbol{k}}
a field of characteristic zero and let
F
be a homogeneous form in
{\boldsymbol{k}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
Hesse
[
17
,
18
]
believed that the Hessian of

F
vanishes if and only if the projective variety
X
, defined by
F
,
is a cone.
However, Gordan and Noether
[
14
]

proved that while Hesse’s claim is true when the degree of
F
is
2
or
n\leq 4
, it is false for

5\leq n
and for forms of degree at least three.
Gordan and Noether realized that
X
being a cone
is equivalent to the condition that the partial derivatives of
F
are
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-linearly dependent,
while
F
has vanishing Hessian if and only if the partial derivatives of
F

are
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-algebraically dependent.
A class of forms
F
, discussed both in
[
14
]

and by Perazzo
[
24
]
,
with vanishing Hessian and for which
V(F)

is not a cone, are the Perazzo forms

F=x_{1}p_{1}+\dots+x_{a}p_{a}+G\in{\boldsymbol{k}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{a},u_{1},\dots,u_{b}],

where
p_{1},\dots,p_{a}
and
G
are in
{\boldsymbol{k}}[u_{1},\dots,u_{b}]
, and
p_{1},\dots,p_{a}
are
linearly independent, but algebraically dependent. In particular, for

a+b=5
, all non-cones defined by a form with vanishing Hessian
are defined by a
Perazzo form.
(See

[
14
]
or
[
30
, Theorem 7.3]
.)
The Lefschetz properties of rings defined by Perazzo forms in characteristic zero are investigated in the recent papers
[
12
,
1
]
.

J. Watanabe and M. de Bondt
[
30
]
have written a detailed modern argument for the Gordan-Noether Theorem. The paper
[
5
]
uses geometric
techniques to give a new proof of the Gordan-Noether Theorem. In both of these papers the field
{\boldsymbol{k}}
has characteristic zero.

We work in arbitrary characteristic; so we do not take literal partial derivatives. Instead we use the divided power algebra
D_{\bullet}U^{*}
which is associated to the polynomial ring
P=\operatorname{Sym}_{\bullet}U
for the vector space
U
over the field
{\boldsymbol{k}}
. We replace the “Hessian of
F
” with the homomorphism “
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}
” of Section
2.B
.

In Section
8
we state and prove the three variable version of the Main Theorem (Theorem
1.1
). Our precise formulation of the three variable version (see Lemma
8.2
) is used in the inductive part of the proof of Theorem
1.1
. (See the case
r=0
in Lemma
5.3
.)
Furthermore, we prove the three variable version using the same argument as we use for the four variable version; except there are fewer cases and each calculation is more straightforward. The reader might want to read Section
8
as a preparation for reading the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Conventions 2.1
.

(a)

The graded algebra

is a

-algebra

is finitely generated as an

-module and

(b)

Let
{\boldsymbol{k}}
be a field and

be a standard graded
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-algebra. Then
A
has the

{\boldsymbol{k}}
-module homomorphism

\mu_{\ell}:A_{i}\to A_{i+1}

.

\bigwedge_{\boldsymbol{k}}

(e)

[

]

[

.

(f)

If

\Delta:D_{i}U\to T_{i}U

\displaystyle D_{i}U

\displaystyle{}\subseteq D_{\bullet}U\xrightarrow{D_{\bullet}(d)}D_{\bullet}(\underbrace{U\oplus\dots\oplus U}_{i\text{ times}})=\underbrace{D_{\bullet}U\otimes\dots\otimes D_{\bullet}U}_{i\text{ times}}

(\{a_{1},a_{1},a_{3}\},\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4},b_{5}\})

to be

(\{2,3,4\},\{1,3,4,7,8\}),\ \ (\{1,2,4\},\{1,3,4,6,7\}),\ \ \text{and}\ \ (\{1,2,3\},\{1,3,4,5,7\})

to obtain
(
7.1.4
).

8.
The three variables Theorem.

In this section we state and prove the three variable version of the Main Theorem (Theorem
1.1
). Our precise formulation of the three variable version (see Lemma
8.2
) is used in the inductive part of the proof of Theorem
1.1
. (See the case
r=0
in Lemma
5.3
.)
Furthermore, we prove the three variable version using the same argument as we use for the four variable version; except there are fewer cases and each calculation is more straightforward. The reader might want to read the present section as a preparation for reading the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{k}}
be a field
and
A
be a standard graded Artinian Gorenstein
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-algebra of embedding dimension three and socle degree three.
If the characteristic of
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is different than two, then
A
has the weak Lefschetz property.
If the characteristic of
A
is equal to two, then

A
has the weak Lefschetz property
if and only if
A
is not isomorphic to

(8.1.1)

\frac{{\boldsymbol{k}}[x,y,z]}{(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})}.

Proof.

It is clear that the ring
A
of (
8.1.1
) does not satisfy the weak Lefschetz property. Indeed, if
\ell
is a nonzero linear form of
A
, then
\ell^{2}=0
in
A
; so, in particular,
\ell:A_{1}\to A_{2}
is not injective.
The Macaulay inverse system for
A
is
\phi_{3}=x^{*}y^{*}z^{*}
.

To complete the proof of Theorem
8.1
, we adopt Data
2.3
, with
d=3
, and use the method of the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Let
U=A_{1}
and
\phi_{3}\in D_{3}U^{*}
be a Macaulay inverse system for
A
. Assume that
\ell\phi_{3}
is nonzero for all
\ell
in
U
. Assume also that either the characteristic of
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is not two or that the characteristic of
{\boldsymbol{k}}
equals two but there does not equal a basis
x^{*},y^{*},z^{*}
for
U^{*}
with
\phi_{3}=x^{*}y^{*}z^{*}
. In Lemma
8.2
, we prove that
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}\neq 0
. Apply
Lemma
2.9
to complete the proof.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{k}}
be a field,
U
be a three-dimensional vector space over
{\boldsymbol{k}}
, and
\phi_{3}
be an element of
D_{3}U^{*}
. Assume

(a)

either the characteristic of
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is not two, or the characteristic of
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is equal to two but
\phi_{3}\neq x^{*}y^{*}z^{*}
for any basis
x^{*}
,
y^{*}
,
z^{*}
of
U^{*}
; and

(b)

\ell\phi_{3}
is nonzero for every nonzero
\ell
in
U
.

Then
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}
is not identically zero.

Proof.

The proof is by contradiction. We assume that
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}
is identically zero.
There are two cases. Either
\phi_{3}
has the form of (
a
) or (
b
) from Lemma
3.1
.

We first assume that
\phi_{3}
has the form of Lemma
3.1
.(
a
).
In other words, there is a basis
x^{*},y^{*},z^{*}
for
U^{*}
so that
\phi_{3}=ax^{*(3)}+x^{*}\phi_{2,0}+\phi_{3,0}
with
a\in{\boldsymbol{k}}
,
a\neq 0
, and
\phi_{i,0}\in D_{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}(y^{*},z^{*})
. Thus,
there are parameters
a,b,\dots,j
, in
{\boldsymbol{k}}
, with
a\neq 0
, such that

(8.2.1)

\phi_{3}=\begin{cases}\phantom{+}ax^{*(3)}+dx^{*}y^{*(2)}+ex^{*}y^{*}z^{*}+fx^{*}z^{*(2)}\\
+gy^{*(3)}+hy^{*(2)}z^{*}+iy^{*}z^{*(2)}+jz^{*(3)}.\end{cases}

Let
x,y,z
be the basis for
U
which is dual to the basis
x^{*},y^{*},z^{*}
for
U^{*}
. Let
\omega_{U}
and
\omega_{U^{*}}
be the bases
x\wedge y\wedge z
and
x^{*}\wedge y^{*}\wedge z^{*}
for
\bigwedge^{3}U
and
\bigwedge^{3}U^{*}
, respectively.

Observe that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(3)}\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle x^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge xy\phi_{3}\wedge xz\phi_{3}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle ax^{2}\wedge(dy^{*}+ez^{*})\wedge(ey^{*}+fz^{*})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle a(df-e^{2})\omega_{U^{*}}.

The assumption that
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}
is identically zero, combined with the hypothesis that
a\neq 0
yields that
df-e^{2}=0
.

Apply Lemma
3.3
and change the basis for
{\boldsymbol{k}}y^{*}\oplus{\boldsymbol{k}}z^{*}
in order to
write

dx^{*}y^{*(2)}+ex^{*}y^{*}z^{*}+fx^{*}z^{*(2)}

in the form
dx^{*}Y^{*(2)}
with
e
and
f
equal to zero for some new basis
Y^{*},Z^{*}
for
{\boldsymbol{k}}y^{*}\oplus{\boldsymbol{k}}z^{*}
.

In Lemma
8.3
we show that when
\phi_{3}
is given in (
8.2.1
) with
e=f=0
, then

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(y^{(3)}\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle-d^{2}i\omega_{U^{*}},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}z\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle adj\omega_{U^{*}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xy^{(2)}\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle a(gi-h^{2})\omega_{U^{*}}.

Recall our assumption that
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}
is identically zero and our local hypothesis that
a\neq 0
. If
d\neq 0
, then
i=j=h=0
,

\phi_{3}=ax^{*(3)}+dx^{*}y^{*(2)}+gy^{*(3)},

(because
e
and
f
continue to be zero), and

z\phi_{3}=0
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
d=0
.

In Lemma
8.3
, we show that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xy^{(2)}\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle a(gi-h^{2})\omega_{U^{*}},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xz^{(2)}\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle a(hj-i^{2})\omega_{U^{*}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xyz\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle a(gj-hi)\omega_{U^{*}}.

Continue to assume that
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}=0
,

a\neq 0
, but
d=e=f=0
. It follows that

h^{2}=gi
,
hi=gj
,
hj=i^{2}
, and

\begin{bmatrix}g&h&i\\
h&i&j\end{bmatrix}

has rank at most one.
Hence some nonzero linear combination of

y\phi_{3}=gy^{*(2)}+hy^{*}z^{*}+iz^{*(2)}\quad\text{and}\quad z\phi_{3}=hy^{*(2)}+iy^{*}z^{*}+jz^{*(2)}

is zero, which is a contradiction. This contradiction establishes Lemma
8.2
for
\phi_{3}
described in Lemma
3.1
.(
a
).

Now we assume that
\phi_{3}
has the form of Lemma
3.1
.(
b
). In other words, there is a basis
x^{*}
,
y^{*}
,
z^{*}
for
U^{*}
so that

\phi_{3}=bx^{*(2)}y^{*}+x\phi_{2,0}+\phi_{3,0}
with
\phi_{i,0}
in
D_{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}(y^{*},z^{*})
,
b\in{\boldsymbol{k}}
, and
b\neq 0
. Thus, there are parameters
a,b,\dots,j
, with
b\neq 0
, such that

(8.2.2)

\phi_{3}=\begin{cases}\phantom{+}bx^{*(2)}y^{*}+dx^{*}y^{*(2)}+ex^{*}y^{*}z^{*}+fx^{*}z^{*(2)}\\
+gy^{*(3)}+hy^{*(2)}z^{*}+iy^{*}z^{*(2)}+jz^{*(3)}.\end{cases}

It is shown in Lemma
8.4

that

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(3)}\otimes\omega_{U})=-b^{2}f\omega_{U^{*}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}z\otimes\omega_{U})=-b^{2}j\omega_{U^{*}}.

Combine the assumption that
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}
is identically zero with the local hypothesis that
b\neq 0
in order to conclude that
f=j=0
.
It is shown in Lemma
8.5

that when
f=0
, then

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}y\otimes\omega_{U})=b(e^{2}-bi)\omega_{U^{*}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(y^{(3)}\otimes\omega_{U})=(\det M)\,\omega_{U^{*}},

where

(8.2.3)

M=\begin{bmatrix}b&d&e\\
d&g&h\\
e&h&i\end{bmatrix}.

The assumption that
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}
is identically zero, together with the local hypothesis that
b\neq 0
, yields that

e^{2}-bi=0
and
\det M=0
.
It follows that all three maximal minors of

M
with row two deleted are zero. This conclusion follows from the technique of (
7.3.1
) applied to the matrix

\begin{bmatrix}b&d&e&0&0&1\\
d&g&h&0&1&0\\
e&h&i&1&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

Take columns
\{2,3\}
to be
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{r-1}\}
and columns
\{1,3,5,6\}

to be
\{b_{1},\dots,\allowbreak b_{r+1}\}
to see that

\det\begin{bmatrix}d&e\\
h&i\end{bmatrix}\det\begin{bmatrix}d&h\\
e&i\end{bmatrix}+i\det M-\det\begin{bmatrix}g&h\\
h&i\end{bmatrix}\det\begin{bmatrix}b&e\\
e&i\end{bmatrix}=0.

The expressions
e^{2}-bi
and
\det M
are zero; hence,
di-he
is also zero. Take

\{1,2\}
to be
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{r-1}\}
and columns
\{1,3,4,5\}

to be
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{r+1}\}
to see that

\det\begin{bmatrix}b&d\\
e&h\end{bmatrix}\det\begin{bmatrix}b&e\\
d&h\end{bmatrix}+b\det M-\det\begin{bmatrix}b&d\\
d&g\end{bmatrix}\det\begin{bmatrix}b&e\\
e&i\end{bmatrix}=0;

hence
bh-de=0
.
At any rate, the matrix

\begin{bmatrix}b&d&e\\
e&h&i\end{bmatrix}

has rank at most one; hence

\displaystyle x\phi_{3}={}

\displaystyle bx^{*}y^{*}+dy^{*(2)}+ey^{*}z^{*}\text{ and}

\displaystyle z\phi_{3}={}

\displaystyle ex^{*}y^{*}+hy^{*(2)}+iy^{*}z^{*}

are linearly dependent. This contradicts the hypothesis that
\ell\phi_{3}
is nonzero for all nonzero
\ell
in
U
.
∎

Lemma 8.3
.

If
\phi_{3}
is given by

(
8.2.1
)
,
with
e=f=0
, then

(a)

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(y^{(3)}\otimes\omega_{U})=-d^{2}i\omega_{U^{*}}
,

(b)

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}z\otimes\omega_{U})=adj\omega_{U^{*}}
,

(c)

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xy^{(2)}\otimes\omega_{U})=a(gi-h^{2})\omega_{U^{*}}
,

(d)

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xz^{(2)}\otimes\omega_{U})=a(hj-i^{2})\omega_{U^{*}}
, and

(e)

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xyz\otimes\omega_{U})=a(gj-hi)\omega_{U^{*}}
.

Proof.

(
a
) We compute

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(y^{(3)}\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle xy\phi_{3}\wedge w^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge yz\phi_{3}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle dy^{*}\wedge(dx^{*}+gy^{*}+hz^{*})\wedge(hy^{*}+iz^{*})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle-d^{2}i\omega_{U^{*}}.

(
b
)
The expansion of
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}z\otimes\omega_{U})
has three summands; however two of the summands have a factor of
xz\phi_{3}=0
; hence

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}z\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle x^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge xy\phi_{3}\wedge z^{2}\phi_{3}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle ax^{*}\wedge dy^{*}\wedge(iy^{*}+jz^{*})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle adj\omega_{U^{*}}.

(
c
)
The expansion of
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xy^{(2)}\otimes\omega_{U})
has three summands; however two of the summands have a factor of
xz\phi_{3}=0
or
xy\phi_{3}\wedge xy\phi_{3}=0
; hence

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xy^{(2)}\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle x^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge y^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge yz\phi_{3}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle ax^{*}\wedge(dx^{*}+gy^{*}+hz^{*})\wedge(hy^{*}+iz^{*})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle a(gi-h^{2})\omega_{U^{*}}.

(
d
)
The expansion of
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xz^{(2)}\otimes\omega_{U})
has three summands; however two of the summands have a factor of
xz\phi_{3}=0
; hence

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xz^{(2)}\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle x^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge yz\phi_{3}\wedge z^{2}\phi_{3}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle ax^{*}\wedge(hy^{*}+iz^{*})\wedge(iy^{*}+jz^{*})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle a(hj-i^{2})\omega_{U^{*}}.

(
e
)
The expansion of
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xyz\otimes\omega_{U})
has six summands; however five of the summands have a factor of
xz\phi_{3}=0
,
yz\phi_{3}\wedge yz\phi_{3}=0
, or
xy\phi_{3}\wedge xy\phi_{3}=0
; hence

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(xyz\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle x^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge y^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge z^{2}\phi_{3}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle ax^{*}\wedge(dx^{*}+gy^{*}+hz^{*})\wedge(iy^{*}+jz^{*})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle a(gj-hi)\omega_{U^{*}}.

∎

Lemma 8.4
.

If
\phi_{3}
has the form of
(
8.2.2
)
,
then

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(3)}\otimes\omega_{U})=-b^{2}f\omega_{U^{*}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}z\otimes\omega_{U})=-b^{2}j\omega_{U^{*}}.

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(3)}\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle x^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge xy\phi_{3}\wedge xz\phi_{3}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle by^{*}\wedge(bx^{*}+dy^{*}+ez^{*})\wedge(ey^{*}+fz^{*})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle-b^{2}f\omega_{U^{*}}.

The expansion of
\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}z\otimes\omega_{U})

has three summands; however one of the summands has a factor of
xz\phi_{3}\wedge xz\phi_{3}=0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}z\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle x^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge yz\phi_{3}\wedge xz\phi_{3}+x^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge xy\phi_{3}\wedge z^{2}\phi_{3}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\phantom{+}by^{*}\wedge(ex^{*}+hy^{*}+iz^{*})\wedge(ey^{*}+fz^{*})\\
+by^{*}\wedge(bx^{*}+dy^{*}+ez^{*})\wedge(fx^{*}+iy^{*}+jz^{*})\end{cases}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle-bef\omega_{U^{*}}-b(bj-ef)\omega_{U^{*}}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle-b^{2}j\omega_{U^{*}}.

∎

Lemma 8.5
.

If
\phi_{3}
has the form of
(
8.2.2
)
, with
f=0
,
then

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}y\otimes\omega_{U})=b(e^{2}-bi)\omega_{U^{*}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(y^{(3)}\otimes\omega_{U})=(\det M)\,\omega_{U^{*}},

where
M
is the matrix of
(
8.2.3
)
.

Proof.

The expansion of

\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}y\otimes\omega_{U})
has three summands. Two of the summands have a factor of
xy\phi_{3}\wedge xy\phi_{3}=0
or
x^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge xz\phi_{3}\in\bigwedge^{2}{\boldsymbol{k}}y^{*}=0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(x^{(2)}y\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle x^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge xy\phi_{3}\wedge yz\phi_{3}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle by^{*}\wedge(bx^{*}+dy^{*}+ez^{*})\wedge(ex^{*}+hy^{*}+iz^{*})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle b(e^{2}-bi)\omega_{U^{*}}.

We compute

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi_{3}}(y^{(3)}\otimes\omega_{U})={}

\displaystyle xy\phi_{3}\wedge y^{2}\phi_{3}\wedge yz\phi_{3}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle(bx^{*}+dy^{*}+ez^{*})\wedge(dx^{*}+gy^{*}+hz^{*})\wedge(ex^{*}+hy^{*}+iz^{*})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\left|\begin{matrix}b&d&e\\
d&g&h\\
e&h&i\end{matrix}\right|\omega_{U^{*}}.

∎
