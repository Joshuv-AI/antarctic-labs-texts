Graph signal processing with categorical perspective
Feng Ji, Xingchao Jian, Wee Peng Tay

Abstract




1
Introduction

Graph signal processing (GSP)
[
5
]
has emerged as a powerful framework for analyzing graph-structured data, where the central concept is the vector space of graph signals. For a graph
G
of size
n
, the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of graph signals has enabled the development of a variety of signal processing tools, which have been successfully applied in various domains, such as social network analysis, the study of sensor networks, and transportation network analysis. However, uncertainty is omnipresent in practice, and using a vector to model a real signal can be erroneous in some situations.

To address this challenge, we propose (cf.
[
3
]
) to use Wasserstein space
[
6
]
as a replacement for the vector space of graph signals, to account for signal stochasticity. The Wasserstein space is strictly more general than the classical graph signal space and provides a more flexible and realistic framework for modeling uncertain signals. An element in the Wasserstein space is called a distributional graph signal in
[
3
]
.

On the other hand, signal processing for a probability space of graphs and operators has also been proposed in
[
4
]
. It is integrated with the notion of distributional graph signals in
[
3
]
, and the unified framework that encompasses existing theories regarding graph uncertainty and provides a more comprehensive approach to analyzing graph-structured data.

The approach is concrete and follows the observation that distributional graph signal transformation can be built up from two types of measurable functions
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\mathcal{G}_{n}
the space of simple weighted graphs on
n
vertices. The function
p
is the projection and
f
factors as
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M_{n}(\mathbb{R}),(r,G)\mapsto(r,h(G))
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times M_{n}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{n},(r,M)=Mr
, for some measurable function
h:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. The setup is still restrictive. For example, the size
n
is used throughout, and hence the change of graph such as graph augmentation is not included as a special case.

In this paper, we propose a vast generalization for graph signal processing with a categorical perspective. Recall that the category theory
[
2
]
deals with the abstract study of structures and relationships between objects. It provides a framework for organizing mathematical concepts and objects. In our case, we shall use categorical language to formalize the notion of signal adaptive graph structures (SAGS) and associated filters introduced in
[
3
]
, as morphisms of the category of correspondences
\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{c}}
in
Section

3
. The aim of the paper is to construct subcategories of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{c}}
that have important signal processing significance. We can use the framework to give an abstract and unified perspective of many concrete GSP ideas.

2
Abstract graph signals

As we have mentioned in
Section

1
, we want to unify the two directions of generalization of the traditional GSP. Recall that if
X
is a metric space, define
\mathcal{P}(X)
to be the space of probability measures on
X
with finite mean and variance, the
2
-Wasserstein space
on
X

[
6
]
. In traditional GSP, the signal space is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It embeds isometrically in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
via
r\mapsto\delta_{r}
, where
\delta_{r}
is the delta distribution at
r
. On the other hand,
[
4
]
considers a distribution
\mu
of graph shift operators. It can be interpreted (trivially) as for each graph signal
r
, a distribution of
\mu_{r}=\mu
of operators is associated with
r
. To generalize, we consider fiberwise measure as follows.

Definition 1
.

For measurable spaces
X,Y
, suppose
f:Y\to X
is
surjective and measurable
. The map
f
is said to be equipped with
fiberwise probability measure
if for each
x\in X
, there is a probability measure on
\mu_{x}
on the fiber
f^{-1}(x)
. For convenience, we may say such an
f
is an
fpm
. We call
Y
the
total space
and
X
the
base space
.

If
f
has fiberwise probability measure, then for each probability measure
\mu
on
X
, there is a pullback measure
f^{*}(\mu)
on
Y
defined by

\displaystyle f^{*}(\mu)(U)=\int_{x\in X}\mu_{x}\Big(U\cap f^{-1}(x)\Big)d\mu,

for measurable subset
U
of
Y
. We shall use
f
with fiberwise probability measure for base space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to model joint information of graphs and signals. For example, if
\Gamma_{n}
is the (discrete) space of unweighted graphs on
n
vertice, then an fpm
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Gamma_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},(r,G)\mapsto r
associates a distribution of graphs
\mu_{r}
(on
\Gamma_{n}
) for each signal
r
.

As a side remark, in the reverse direction, for measurable
g:X\to Y
and a probability
\mu
on
X
, there is a pushforward (or induced) measure
g_{*}(\mu)
on
Y
. It is defined by
g_{*}(\mu)(U)=\mu(g^{-1}(U))
for any measurable subset
U
of
Y
.

Suppose we are given an fpm
f:Y\to X
. A measurable function
g:Y\to Y
induces a
self-equivalence
of the fpm
f
if the following holds:

1.

f=f\circ g
.

2.

For each
x\in X
, let
g_{x}:f^{-1}(x)\to f^{-1}(x)
be the restriction of
g
to the fiber of
x
. Then
{g_{x}}_{*}(\mu_{x})=\mu_{x}
, i.e.,
\mu_{x}(U)=\mu_{x}(g_{x}^{-1}(U))
for any measurable
U\subset f^{-1}(x)
.

3.

If
U
is measurable in
f^{-1}(x)
, then
g_{x}(U)
is also measurable and
\mu_{x}(U)=\mu_{x}(g(U))
.

We want to use the above notion of self-equivalence to define equivalences of fpms.

Definition 2
.

Two fpms
f:Y\to X
and
f^{\prime}:Y^{\prime}\to X
are
fiberwise equivalent
if there are measurable functions
g:Y\to Y^{\prime}
and
h:Y^{\prime}\to Y
such that
h\circ g
and
g\circ h
are self-equivalences of
f
and
f^{\prime}
respectively. The fiberwise equivalence is denoted by
(g,h)
.

The intuition is given by the example that
Y\subset Y^{\prime}
such that
\bar{Y}=Y^{\prime}\backslash Y
is measurable and
\mu^{\prime}_{x}(f^{\prime-1}(x)\cap\bar{Y})=0
for any
x\in X
. In this case, we view
f
and
f^{\prime}
are giving the exact same statistical information and should not distinguish them.

We now describe an important construction, the fiber product, which will be used in subsequently in subsequent sections.

Suppose we have fpms
f:Y\to X
and
f^{\prime}:Y^{\prime}\to X
with fiberwise probability measures
\{\mu_{x}\}_{x\in X}
and
\{\mu_{x}^{\prime}\}_{x\in X}
respectively. Inspired by the fiber product in algebraic topology
[
1
]
, the
fiber product
of
f
and
f^{\prime}
is defined by

\displaystyle Y\times_{X}Y^{\prime}=\{(y,y^{\prime})\in Y\times Y^{\prime}\mid f(y)=f(y^{\prime})\}.

As a subspace of
Y\times Y^{\prime}
, the fiber product
Y\times_{X}Y^{\prime}
carries the subspace
\sigma
-algebra of the product
\sigma
-algebra on
Y\times Y^{\prime}
. The associated surjective measurable map is
h:Y\times_{X}Y^{\prime}\to X,(y,y^{\prime})\mapsto f(y)(=f(y^{\prime}))
. For
x\in X
, its fiber
h^{-1}(x)
is
f^{-1}(x)\times{f^{\prime}}^{-1}(x)
and we equip it with measure
\mu_{x}\times\mu_{x}^{\prime}
. The fiber product can be depicted by the following diagram:

In the diagram, the maps from

.

)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y^{\prime}}

1.

{g^{\prime}}^{-1}(y)\cong{f^{\prime}}^{-1}(f(y))
and

.

2.

The surjection

has fiberwise measure

The diagram is distributional commutative w.r.t.

and

for any probability measure

For each

,
g^{\prime-1}(y)=\{(y,y^{\prime})\mid f(y)=f^{\prime}(y^{\prime})\}
. Therefore, the condition for

holds if and only if

. As the first component of
g^{\prime-1}(y)
is fixed, therefore

g^{-1}(y^{\prime})\cong f^{-1}(f^{\prime}(y^{\prime}))

Notice that as
f:Y\to X
is surjective, so does the map
g:Y\times_{X}Y^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}

\mu_{x}\times\mu_{x}^{\prime}
. For
y^{\prime}\in Y^{\prime}
, its fiber is
f^{-1}(f^{\prime}(y^{\prime}))\times\{y^{\prime}\}
. Its measure is the marginal of
\mu_{f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}\times\mu_{f^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}^{\prime}
at
y^{\prime}
, which is
\mu_{f^{\prime}(y^{\prime})}
.

To show
f^{*}\circ f^{\prime}_{*}(\mu)={g^{\prime}_{*}}\circ g^{*}(\mu)
, consider any test function

on
Y
. We have

\displaystyle\big(f^{*}\circ f^{\prime}_{*}(\mu)\big)(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in Y}\alpha(y)d\big(f^{*}\circ f^{\prime}_{*}\big)(\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in X}\int_{y\in f^{-1}(x)}\alpha(y)d\mu_{x}df^{\prime}_{*}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{y^{\prime}\in Y^{\prime}}\int_{y\in f^{-1}(f^{\prime}(y^{\prime}))}\alpha(y)d\mu_{f^{-1}(f^{\prime}(y^{\prime}))}d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{y^{\prime}\in Y^{\prime}}\int_{y\in f^{-1}(f^{\prime}(y^{\prime}))}\alpha(y)d\mu_{g^{-1}(y^{\prime})}d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{(y,y^{\prime})\in Y\times_{X}Y^{\prime}}\alpha(y)dg^{*}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in Y}\alpha(y)d\big(g^{\prime}_{*}\circ g^{*}\big)(\mu)=(g^{\prime}_{*}\circ g^{*}\big)(\mu)(\alpha).

The result follows.

3
The category of correspondences and its subcategories

As we have pointed out in the previous section, we use the notion of fpm to model graph and signal information. In this section, we demonstrate how to fit the concept to form a category
\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{c}}
, the
category of correspondences
, in which the morphisms correspond to filters in traditional GSP.

The objects of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{c}}
are measurable spaces. A
correspondence

\mathfrak{c}=(f,f^{\prime})
from an object
X
to another
X^{\prime}
is given by the following data: an fpm
f:Y\to X
and a measurable function
f^{\prime}:Y\to X^{\prime}
such that for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}(\mu):=f^{\prime}_{*}\circ f^{*}(\mu)\in\mathcal{P}(X^{\prime}).

We denote it by
(\mathfrak{c},f,f^{\prime})
or simply
\mathfrak{c}
if
f
and
f^{\prime}
are clear from the context. A correspondence is essentially depicted by the diagram
X\xleftarrow{f}Y\xrightarrow{f^{\prime}}X^{\prime}
. We call the space
Y
the
total space
of the correspondence
\mathfrak{c}
.

For measurable spaces
X,X^{\prime}
, two correspondences
\mathfrak{c}=(f,f^{\prime})
and
\mathfrak{d}=(g,g^{\prime})
(with total spaces
Y
and
Z
respectively) are equivalent if there is a fiberwise equivalence
(h,h^{\prime})
with
h:Y\to Z,h^{\prime}:Z\to Y
(cf.
2
) such that
f^{\prime}=g^{\prime}\circ h
and
g^{\prime}=f^{\prime}\circ h^{\prime}
. The condition can be summarized by the following commutative diagram.

4
Linear filters

In this section, we define what we mean by linearity in the categorical setup. The objects we shall consider are finite dimensional vector spaces
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The most intuitive form of linear filters are correspondences of the form
\mathfrak{c}=(p,\times)
where
p:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times M_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
where
p
is the projection to the first component, and
\times:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times M_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the multiplication
(r,M)\mapsto Mr
. However, these correspondences are insufficient to form a category as they do not respect compositions, and we need to enlarge the morphism set.

Definition 4
.

A correspondence between from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
linear filter
if it is equivalent to
\mathfrak{c}=(f,f^{\prime}):\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
with total space
X
of the form:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}

5
Conditional expectation

In this section, we discuss the relationship between our framework and condition expectation.

Suppose we are given
\mathfrak{c}=(f,f^{\prime})
with
f:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and
f^{\prime}:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
. The former is an fpm with fiberwise measures
\{\mu_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}
. It induces a map
\mathfrak{c}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})
. We want to find a map
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
that is a good approximation of
\mathfrak{c}
.

To construct
e_{\mathfrak{c}}
, for
r\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
, define

\displaystyle e_{\mathfrak{c}}(r)=\int_{y\in f^{-1}(r)}f^{\prime}(y)\mathrm{d}\mu_{r}=\int_{r^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}r^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{c}(\delta_{r}).

It is related to conditional expectation as follows. Recall that for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}})
, it pulls back to a distribution
f^{*}(\mu)
on
Y
. In this respect, both
f
and
f^{\prime}
can be viewed as random variables on the sample space
Y
. It is well known that there is a condition expectation
e_{f^{\prime}}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
such that
e_{f^{\prime}}(r)=e_{\mathfrak{c}}(r)
up to a set with
\mu
measure
0
. Due to this fact, the promised approximation property of
e_{\mathfrak{c}}
reads as follows.

Theorem 2
.

For the fpm
\mathfrak{c}=(f,f^{\prime})
, the function
e_{\mathfrak{c}}
is measurable and
e_{\mathfrak{c},*}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})
is well-defined. Moreover, for any measurable
g:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
and subset
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\sup_{\text{supp}(\mu)\subset S}W\big(e_{\mathfrak{c},*}(\mu),\mathfrak{c}(\mu)\big)\leq\sup_{\text{supp}(\nu)\subset S}W\big(g_{*}(\nu),\mathfrak{c}(\nu)\big),

where
W
is the
2
-Wasserstein metric and the supreme is taken over
\mu
(resp.
\nu
) in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}})
supported in
S
.

Proof.

We first show that
e_{\mathfrak{c}}
is measurable. Let
C
be any compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and
\mu_{C}
be the uniform distribution on
C
. It induces the pullback measure
f^{*}(\mu_{C})
on
Y
. Moreover, it is easy to verify that
f^{*}f_{*}(\mu_{C})=\mu_{C}
.

We view
Y
as the sample space with probability distribution
f^{*}(\mu_{C})
and
f
and
f^{\prime}
as random variables. Let
e_{C}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
be the associated conditional expectation. By the construction, we have
e_{C}=e_{\mathfrak{c}}
on
C
and
e_{C}=0
on the complement
\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash C
. Moreover,
e_{C}
is measurable w.r.t. the measure
\mu_{C}
. However, as
\mu_{C}
is uniform,
e_{C}
is also measurable w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure.

Let
C_{1}\subset C_{2}\subset\ldots\subset C_{i}\subset\ldots
be a sequence of compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
such that
\cup_{i\geq 1}C_{i}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
. Then
(e_{C_{i}})_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence of measurable functions whose pointwise limit is
e_{\mathfrak{c}}
. Therefore,
e_{\mathfrak{c}}
is also measurable.

As a consequence, given any distribution
\mu
on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}})
, pushforward induces
e_{\mathfrak{c},*}(\mu)
. We need to show that
e_{\mathfrak{c},*}(\mu)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})
in order to claim that
e_{\mathfrak{c},*}
is well defined as a map
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})
. For the finiteness of the mean, it suffices to use linearity. While for the finiteness of the
2
nd moment, consider the Jensen inequality:

\displaystyle{\left\lVert{\mathbb{E}_{x\in f^{-1}(r)\sim\mu_{r}}f^{\prime}(x)}\right\rVert}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\in f^{-1}(r)\sim\mu_{r}}{\left\lVert{f^{\prime}(x)}\right\rVert}^{2},r\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}.

(1)

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}})
, to show that
e_{\mathfrak{c},*}(\mu)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})
, it suffices to check that

\displaystyle\int_{r\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}{\left\lVert{\mathbb{E}_{x\in f^{-1}(r)\sim\mu_{r}}f^{\prime}(x)}\right\rVert}^{2}d\mu<\infty.

However, by (
1
), the left-hand side is bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{r\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}}\mathbb{E}_{x\in f^{-1}(r)\sim\mu_{r}}{\left\lVert{f^{\prime}(x)}\right\rVert}^{2}d\mu<\infty,

due to the assumption that
\mathfrak{c}(\mu)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})
.

To show the claimed inequality, let
S
be a subset
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}})
be supported on
S
. Consider
\phi
the pushforward measure of
f^{*}(\mu)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
via the map:
Y\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}},y\mapsto(e_{\mathfrak{c}}\circ f(y),f^{\prime}(y))
. The marginals of
\phi
are

\displaystyle(e_{\mathfrak{c}}\circ f)_{*}(f^{*}(\mu))=e_{\mathfrak{c},*}(\mu)\text{ and }{f^{\prime}}_{*}(f^{*}(\mu))=\mathfrak{c}(\mu)

respectively. Therefore,

\displaystyle W(e_{\mathfrak{c},*}(\mu),\mathfrak{c}(\mu))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{(r_{1},r_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}{\left\lVert{r_{1}-r_{2}}\right\rVert}^{2}\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{y\in Y}{\left\lVert{e_{\mathfrak{c}}\circ f(y)-f^{\prime}(y)}\right\rVert}^{2}\mathrm{d}f^{*}(\mu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim f^{*}(\mu)}{\left\lVert{e_{\mathfrak{c}}\circ f(y)-f^{\prime}(y)}\right\rVert}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim f^{*}(\mu)}{\left\lVert{g\circ f(y)-f^{\prime}(y)}\right\rVert}^{2},

The last inequality holds as
e_{\mathfrak{c}}
is the conditional expectation (up to a set of measure
0
) w.r.t.
f^{*}(\mu)
on
Y
.

To estimate the right-hand-side, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim f^{*}(\mu)}{\left\lVert{g\circ f(y)-f^{\prime}(y)}\right\rVert}^{2}\leq\sup_{r\in S}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim f^{*}(\delta_{r})}{\left\lVert{g\circ f(y)-f^{\prime}(y)}\right\rVert}^{2}.

As for any
y\in f^{-1}
,
g\circ f(y)
is the constant
g(r)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim f^{*}(\delta_{r})}{\left\lVert{g\circ f(y)-f^{\prime}(y)}\right\rVert}^{2}=W\big(g_{*}(\delta_{r}),\mathfrak{c}(\delta_{r})\big)^{2}\leq\sup_{\text{supp}(\nu)\subset S}W\big(g_{*}(\nu),\mathfrak{c}(\nu)\big)^{2}.

The result follows.
∎

The result essentially claims that
e_{\mathfrak{c}}
is the best function approximation of
\mathfrak{c}
in the
\infty
-norm. The map
e_{\mathfrak{c}}
also enjoys some interesting algebraic properties.

Lemma 4
.

We have
e_{\mathfrak{c}+\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}=e_{\mathfrak{c}}+e_{\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}
and
e_{\mathfrak{c}\circ\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}(r_{1})=\mathbb{E}_{r_{2}\sim\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}(\delta_{r_{1}})}e_{\mathfrak{c}}(r_{2})
, for morphisms
\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{c^{\prime}}
in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{l}}
whenever the respective binary operation is well-defined.

Proof.

To verify, assume both
\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}
are morphisms from
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
, we compute

\displaystyle e_{\mathfrak{c}+\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}(r)=\int_{r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}r_{2}\mathrm{d}(\mathfrak{c}+\mathfrak{c}^{\prime})(\delta_{r_{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}\int_{r_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}r_{2}+r_{2}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}(\delta_{r_{1}})\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{c}(\delta_{r_{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}r_{2}+e_{\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}(r_{1})\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{c}(\delta_{r_{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}(r_{1})+\int_{r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}r_{2}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{c}(\delta_{r_{1}})=e_{\mathfrak{c}}(r_{1})+e_{\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}(r_{1}).

For the composition, consider
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}\in\text{Mor}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{l}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}},\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}})
and
\mathfrak{c}\in\text{Mor}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{l}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}},\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}})
, we have

\displaystyle e_{\mathfrak{c}\circ\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}(r_{1})=\int_{r_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}}r_{3}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{c}\circ\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}(\delta_{r_{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}\int_{r_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}}r_{3}\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{c}(\delta_{r_{2}})\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}(\delta_{r_{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{r_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}}e_{\mathfrak{c}}(r_{2})\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}(\delta_{r_{1}})=\mathbb{E}_{r_{2}\sim\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}(\delta_{r_{1}})}e_{\mathfrak{c}}(r_{2}).

∎

Corollary 4
.

The assignment
\iota^{*}:\mathfrak{c}=(f,f^{\prime})\mapsto e_{\mathfrak{c}}
induces a functor from
\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{t}}
to
\text{Vect}(\mathbb{R})
as a right inverse to the embedding
\iota
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\iota^{*}(\mathfrak{c})\circ\iota^{*}(\mathfrak{c}^{\prime})=\iota^{*}(\mathfrak{c}\circ\mathfrak{c}^{\prime})
for composable morphisms
\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{t}}
. This requires that
e_{\mathfrak{c}\circ\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}=e_{\mathfrak{c}}\circ e_{\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}
. As
\mathfrak{c}
is a morphism in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathfrak{t}}
,
e_{\mathfrak{c}}
is a linear transformation. Therefore by
4
, we have

\displaystyle e_{\mathfrak{c}\circ\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}(r_{1})=\mathbb{E}_{r_{2}\sim\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}(\delta_{r_{1}})}e_{\mathfrak{c}}(r_{2})=e_{\mathfrak{c}}(\mathbb{E}_{r_{2}\sim\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}(\delta_{r_{1}})}r_{2})=e_{\mathfrak{c}}\circ e_{\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}}(r_{1}).

The result follows.
∎
