Large-Parameter Asymptotics of Generalized Hasting-McLeod Functions
Kurt Schmidt, Robert Buckingham

Abstract
. The generalized Hastings-McLeod solutions to the inhomogeneous Painlevé-II equation arise in multi-critical unitary random matrix ensembles, the chiral two-matrix model for rectangular matrices, non-intersecting squared Bessel paths, and non-intersecting Brownian motions on the circle. We establish the leading-order asymptotic behavior of the generalized Hastings-McLeod functions as the inhomogeneous parameter approaches infinity using the Deift-Zhou nonlinear steepest-descent method for Riemann-Hilbert problems. This analysis is done in both the pole-free region and pole region. The asymptotic formulae show excellent agreement with numerically computed solutions in both regions.



.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

We consider the inhomogeneous Painlevé-II equation

(1)

u^{\prime\prime}(y)=2u(y)^{3}+yu(y)-\alpha,

where
\alpha
is the inhomogeneity parameter. The well-known Hastings-McLeod function,
u_{\text{HM}}
, solves (
1
) when
\alpha=0
with boundary conditions
u_{\text{HM}}(y)\sim\sqrt{-y/2}
as
y\to-\infty
and
u_{\text{HM}}(y)\sim\Ai(y)
as
y\to+\infty
(where
\Ai
is the Airy function)
[
17
]
. It arises in the Tracy-Widom distribution describing a wide range of random phenomena, including, but not limited to, the largest eigenvalue of a Gaussian Unitary Ensemble random matrix
[
20
]
and the longest increasing subsequence of a permutation
[
1
]
.

In 2008, Claeys, Kuijlaars, and Vanlessen found that if one perturbs the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble so that the limiting mean density function of eigenvalues vanishes quadratically at the origin, then perturbations of the Hastings-McLeod function arise in a double-scaling limit
[
10
]
. These perturbations solve (
1
) for
\alpha>-1/2
with boundary conditions

(2)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle u_{\text{HM}}^{(\alpha)}(y)=\sqrt{-\frac{y}{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(1/(-y)^{3/2}\right)\right)&\text{ as }y\to-\infty\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle u_{\text{HM}}^{(\alpha)}(y)=\frac{\alpha}{y}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(1/y^{3}\right)\right)&\text{ as }y\to+\infty\hskip 14.22636pt(\alpha\not=0).\end{cases}

This family of solutions to (
2
) is named the generalized Hastings-McLeod functions. Recently, these functions have been observed in interacting particle systems
[
5
,
12
]
and in a different random matrix model
[
13
]
.
We establish asymptotic formulae for generalized Hastings-McLeod functions,
u_{\text{HM}}^{(\alpha)}
, for large, positive half-integers
\alpha
.

The starting point of our analysis is a Riemann-Hilbert representation for generalized Hastings-McLeod functions discovered by Buckingham and Liechty in 2018 while investigating Dyson Brownian bridges
[
5
]
. We apply the Deift-Zhou method of nonlinear steepest descent developed in
[
11
]
. This involves nontrivial transformations that force the jump discontinuities to be exponentially small as
\alpha\to\infty
except at certain points or arcs. Ignoring these small jumps results in a Riemann-Hilbert problem that is explicitly solvable and is called a
model problem
. This provides a rigorous approximation, with controlled error, to the solution of the original Riemann-Hilbert problem for the generalized Hastings-McLeod functions.

For large
\alpha
, under the scaling

(3)

x:=-\frac{2^{1/3}}{\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2/3}}y,

u^{(\alpha)}_{\text{HM}}
has two distinct behaviors in the
x
-plane. On one hand, the leading-order asymptotic formula is an analytic function in a certain region. We call this section of the
x
-plane
the pole-free region
. On the other hand, in the complement of the closure of the pole-free region, the leading-order asymptotic formula is not analytic and has poles at specific points (depending on
\alpha
). We call this section of the
x
-plane
the pole region
. (See Proposition
3.3
for a precise description of the pole-free region.) Notice, for
\alpha
large enough the scaled generalized Hastings-McLeod functions are analytic in the pole-free region and meromorphic in the pole region. Our Riemann-Hilbert analysis detects this bifurcation. Indeed, in the pole-free region the model problem is solved in terms of elementary functions, while in the pole region, it is solved in terms of Riemann
\Theta
-functions.

is a homology basis, we see that any map defined via path integration on this genus-one surface will be well-defined modulo the periods defined by integrating along
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
. In particular, in order for the Abel map,

(103)

\mathcal{A}(z;x)\equiv\mathcal{A}(z):=\frac{2\pi i}{\ointctrclockwise_{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\text{d}w}{R(w)}}\int_{A}^{z}\frac{\text{d}w}{R(w)},

to be well defined we mod out the value obtained from integrating along
\mathfrak{a}
,

(104)

\frac{2\pi i}{\ointctrclockwise_{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\text{d}w}{R(w)}}\ointctrclockwise_{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\text{d}w}{R(w)}=2\pi i,

and the value obtained from integrating along
\mathfrak{b}
,

(105)

\mathcal{B}(x)\equiv\mathcal{B}:=\frac{2\pi i}{\ointctrclockwise_{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\text{d}w}{R(w)}}\ointclockwise_{\mathfrak{b}}\frac{\text{d}w}{R(w)}.

We now list the basic properties of
\mathcal{A}(z)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Given the lattice
\mathcal{L}:=\left\{j_{1}2\pi i+j_{2}\mathcal{B}:j_{1},j_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
, the boundary values taken by the Abel map,
\mathcal{A}(z)
, on the cuts on the plus sheet are related by the jump conditions

(106)

\mathcal{A}_{+}\left(z^{+}\right)-\mathcal{A}_{-}\left(z^{+}\right)=0\mod\mathcal{L}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{for }z^{+}\in\Sigma_{1},

(107)

\mathcal{A}_{+}\left(z^{+}\right)-\mathcal{A}_{-}\left(z^{+}\right)=-2\pi i\mod\mathcal{L}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{for }z^{+}\in\Gamma,

and

(108)

\mathcal{A}_{+}\left(z^{+}\right)-\mathcal{A}_{-}\left(z^{+}\right)=-\mathcal{B}\mod\mathcal{L}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{for }z^{+}\in\Sigma_{2}.

Also, as
z\to\infty
on the plus sheet,

(109)

\mathcal{A}(z)=\mathcal{A}(\infty)+\frac{\mathcal{A}_{-1}}{z}+\mathcal{O}\left(1/z^{2}\right),\hskip 28.45274pt\text{where }\mathcal{A}_{-1}(x)\equiv\mathcal{A}_{-1}:=-\frac{2\pi i}{\oint_{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\text{d}w}{R(w)}}.

Next, we use
\mathcal{A}(z)
to construct a function (the Baker-Akhiezer function), defined on
\mathbb{T}
, that is path independent without modding. To do this, we employ Riemann
\Theta
-functions. Recall, given
M\in\mathbb{C}
, with
\Real(M)<0
, then for each
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

(110)

\Theta(z;M)\equiv\Theta(z):=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left(kz+\frac{1}{2}Mk^{2}\right).

It is well known (see
[
18
]
) that
\Theta(z;M)
satisfies the following properties:

•

Periodicity:

\Theta(z+2\pi i)=\Theta(z)
.

•

Quasiperiodicity:

\Theta(z+M;M)=e^{-\frac{M}{2}}e^{-z}\Theta(z;M)
. More generally, for
n\in\mathbb{Z}

\Theta(z+nM;M)=e^{-Mn^{2}/2}e^{-nz}\Theta(z;M)
.

•

Evenness:

\Theta(-z)=\Theta(z)
.

•

All of the zeros of
\Theta
are simple, and
\Theta(z_{0})=0
if and only if there exist integers
j_{1},j_{2}
such that

(111)

z_{0}=\left(\pi i+M/2\right)+j_{1}2\pi i+j_{2}M.

We see that the
\Theta
-function gives us a handle on our goal of constructing a well-defined function on
\mathbb{T}
from
\mathcal{A}(z)
. Naturally, we consider
\Theta(z;\mathcal{B})
. Notice by using the
2\pi i
-periodicity of the
\Theta
-function,
\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(z)\right)
is independent of the number of
\mathfrak{a}
-cycles used in the path that connects
A
to
z
(or any cycle in the same homology class as
\mathfrak{a}
). We now develop the machinery that allows us to use the
\mathcal{B}
-quasiperiodicity of
\Theta
to construct a function that is also independent of the number of
\mathfrak{b}
-cycles used in the path that connects
A
to
z
. We start with the observation that the
\mathcal{B}
-quasiperiodicity of
\Theta
allows us to construct a function whose
\mathfrak{b}
-cycle dependence is multiplicative, not additive. Indeed, for complex constants
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, notice

(112)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(z)+C_{1}+C_{2}+n\mathcal{B}\right)}{\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(z)+C_{1}+n\mathcal{B}\right)}&=\frac{\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(z)+C_{1}+C_{2}\right)}{\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(z)+C_{1}\right)}e^{-nC_{2}}.\end{split}

We see that the above quotient “counts” (in the negative direction in the exponent with weight
C_{2}
) the number of
\mathfrak{b}
-cycles used to connect
A
to
z
. So, if we multiply it with a function that “counts” in the positive direction, then the product will be independent of the number of
\mathfrak{b}
-cycles.

We define the differentials

(113)

\Phi:=\frac{2\pi i}{\ointctrclockwise_{\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\text{d}w}{R(w)}}\frac{\text{d}w}{R(w)},\hskip 18.77892pt\Upsilon_{0}:=\frac{w^{2}}{R(w)}\text{d}w,\hskip 18.77892pt\text{ and }\hskip 18.77892pt\Upsilon:=\Upsilon_{0}-\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\ointctrclockwise_{\mathfrak{a}}\Upsilon_{0}\right)\Phi.

Notice that
\Upsilon
vanishes when it is integrated against an
\mathfrak{a}
-cycle. We set the constant

(114)

U(x)\equiv U:=\ointclockwise_{\mathfrak{b}}\Upsilon.

We now list some of the basic properties of the differential
\Upsilon
.

Proposition 4.3
.

The boundary values taken by the map

(115)

z\mapsto\int_{A}^{z}\Upsilon

on
\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}
in the plus-sheet are related by the jump conditions

(116)

\left(\int_{A}^{z^{+}}\Upsilon\right)_{+}+\left(\int_{A}^{z^{+}}\Upsilon\right)_{-}=0\mod U\mathbb{Z}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{for }z^{+}\in\Sigma_{1}

and

(117)

\left(\int_{A}^{z^{+}}\Upsilon\right)_{+}+\left(\int_{A}^{z^{+}}\Upsilon\right)_{-}=-U\mod U\mathbb{Z}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{for }z^{+}\in\Sigma_{2}.

Also, for some constants
\Upsilon_{0},\Upsilon_{-1}\in\mathbb{C}
, as
z\to\infty

(118)

z-\int_{A}^{z}\Upsilon=\Upsilon_{0}+\frac{\Upsilon_{-1}}{z}+\mathcal{O}\left(1/z^{2}\right).

Let
P
be a path that connects
A
to
z
using exactly
n

\mathfrak{b}
-cycles, for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Notice

(119)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\exp\left(kF_{1}\int_{P}\Upsilon\right)&=\exp\left(kF_{1}n\ointclockwise_{\mathfrak{b}}\Upsilon\right)\exp\left(kF_{1}\int_{A}^{z}\Upsilon\right)\\
&=\exp(nkF_{1}U)\exp\left(kF_{1}\int_{A}^{z}\Upsilon\right).\end{split}

Looking at (
112
) and (
119
), we see that if we choose
C_{2}=kF_{1}U
and if the paths in the Abel map and the differential are consistent, then their product is path independent on
\mathbb{T}
.

We also need to specify the constant
C_{1}
. As we are trying to solve a Riemann-Hilbert problem with no poles, we need to control where the denominator of (
112
) vanishes. Thus, we set

(120)

\mathcal{K}(x)\equiv\mathcal{K}:=i\pi+\frac{\mathcal{B}}{2}

to be the
Riemann constant
and

(121)

C_{1}:=-\mathcal{A}(Q)-\mathcal{K},

where
Q\in\mathbb{C}
is specified in (
128
). Now, the Baker-Akhiezer function is given as

(122)

q(z;x,k)\equiv q(z):=\frac{\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(z)-\mathcal{A}(Q)-\mathcal{K}-kF_{1}U\right)}{\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(z)-\mathcal{A}(Q)-\mathcal{K}\right)}e^{-kF_{1}\int_{A}^{z}\Upsilon}.

Notice
q
has a simple pole at
z=Q
.

4.4.3.
The Solution to the Model Riemann-Hilbert Problem

We start with the observation that by permuting the signs of
C_{1}
and
F_{1}U
in
q(z)
we can construct a matrix-valued function that is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2})
with a simple jump discontinuity. Define

(123)

\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}(z;x,k)\equiv\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}(z):=\\
\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{\Theta(\mathcal{A}(z)+\mathcal{A}(Q)+\mathcal{K}-kF_{1}U)}{\Theta(\mathcal{A}(z)+\mathcal{A}(Q)+\mathcal{K})}e^{-kF_{1}\int_{A}^{z}\Upsilon}&\displaystyle\frac{\Theta(\mathcal{A}(z)-\mathcal{A}(Q)-\mathcal{K}+kF_{1}U)}{\Theta(\mathcal{A}(z)-\mathcal{A}(Q)-\mathcal{K})}e^{kF_{1}\int_{A}^{z}\Upsilon}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\Theta(\mathcal{A}(z)-\mathcal{A}(Q)-\mathcal{K}-kF_{1}U)}{\Theta(\mathcal{A}(z)-\mathcal{A}(Q)-\mathcal{K})}e^{-kF_{1}\int_{A}^{z}\Upsilon}&\displaystyle\frac{\Theta(\mathcal{A}(z)+\mathcal{A}(Q)+\mathcal{K}+kF_{1}U)}{\Theta(\mathcal{A}(z)+\mathcal{A}(Q)+\mathcal{K})}e^{kF_{1}\int_{A}^{z}\Upsilon}\end{bmatrix}.

The jump conditions in Propositions
4.2
and
4.3
imply

(124)

\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}_{+}(z)=\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}_{-}(z)\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ for }z\in\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}.

We will construct
\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}(z)
out of
\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)
. Our first step is to introduce a sign change into the jump of
\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}
. In order to do so, we define

(125)

\gamma(z):=\left(\frac{(z-A)(z-C)}{(z-B)(z-D)}\right)^{1/4},

where
\gamma
is cut on
\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}
and as
z\to\infty
,
\gamma(z)=1+\mathcal{O}(1/z)
. We also define

(126)

f^{\text{D}}(z):=\frac{\gamma(z)+\gamma^{-1}(z)}{2}\hskip 49.79231pt\text{ and }\hskip 49.79231ptf^{OD}(z):=\frac{\gamma(z)-\gamma^{-1}(z)}{2i}.

A direct calculation shows that
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}e^{-i\pi/2}
on
\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}
. Hence,

(127)

f^{D}_{+}(z)=f^{OD}_{-}(z)\hskip 28.45274pt\text{and}\hskip 28.45274ptf^{OD}_{+}(z)=-f^{D}_{-}(z)\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ for }z\in\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}.

Also, a straightforward computation shows that

(128)

Q(x)\equiv Q:=\frac{BD-AC}{B+D-A-C}

is the unique solution to the equation
f^{\text{D}}(z)f^{\text{OD}}(z)=0
. Further, it is a simple zero and either
f^{\text{D}}(Q)=0
or
f^{\text{OD}}(Q)=0
. It has been shown in
[
3
]
that while the construction of the parametrix does depend on whether
Q
is a zero of
f^{\text{D}}
or
f^{\text{OD}}
, the final answer (i.e. the formula we extract from
\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\text{(out)}}(z)
; see (
10
)) is the same regardless of which function vanishes at
Q
. Numerically, we see that
f^{\text{OD}}(Q)=0
for various
x
-values in the pole region. Thus, we assume that
Q
is the unique simple zero of
f^{\text{OD}}(z)
, and we proceed accordingly.

We now modify
\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)
by setting

(129)

\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}(z;x,k)\equiv\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}C_{11}&0\\
0&C_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}f^{\text{D}}(z)[\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)]_{11}&f^{\text{OD}}(z)[\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)]_{12}\\
-f^{\text{OD}}(z)[\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)]_{21}&f^{\text{D}}(z)[\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)]_{22}\end{bmatrix},

where
C_{11}
and
C_{22}
are normalizing constants specified in (
130
) and (
131
). Now,
\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}(z)
satisfies the jump condition
\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{+}(z)=\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{-}(z)\boldsymbol{T}(1)
for
z\in z\in\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}
.

It is well known that the Abel map
\mathcal{A}(z)
with domain
\mathbb{T}
is injective
(see for example the discussion in Section 4.5.2 in
[
6
]
). Since
\mathcal{A}(Q^{-})+\mathcal{A}(Q)=0
(here
Q
is on the plus sheet and
Q^{-}
is the equivalent point on the minus sheet), it follows that for each
z
on the plus sheet,
\mathcal{A}(z)+\mathcal{A}(Q)\neq 0
, otherwise
\mathcal{A}(z)
would not be injective.
Hence, the diagonal entries of
\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)
are analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2})
. Further,
Q
was selected so that in the off-diagonal entries of
\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}(z)
the simple pole
z=Q
is canceled by the simple zero of
f^{\text{OD}}(z)
. Therefore, the off-diagonal entries of
\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}(z)
are analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2})
.

Finally, to deduce the values of
C_{11},C_{22}
, we notice as
z\to\infty
,
f^{\text{D}}(z)=1+\mathcal{O}(1/z)
and
f^{\text{OD}}(z)=\mathcal{O}(1/z)
, thus we find the limits of
[\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}]_{11}
and
[\dot{\boldsymbol{R}}]_{22}
as
z\to\infty
and take the reciprocal to find

(130)

C_{11}(x;k)\equiv C_{11}:=\frac{\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(\infty)+\mathcal{A}(Q)+\mathcal{K}\right)}{\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(\infty)+\mathcal{A}(Q)+\mathcal{K}-kF_{1}U\right)}e^{-kF_{1}\Upsilon_{0}}

and

(131)

C_{22}(x;k)\equiv C_{22}:=\frac{\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(\infty)+\mathcal{A}(Q)+\mathcal{K}\right)}{\Theta\left(\mathcal{A}(\infty)+\mathcal{A}(Q)+\mathcal{K}+kF_{1}U\right)}e^{kF_{1}\Upsilon_{0}}.

Here,
C_{11}
and
C_{22}
are not guaranteed to exist. Indeed, their denominators can vanish. In other words,
C_{11}
and
C_{22}
do not exist if and only if

(132)

\mathcal{A}(\infty)+\mathcal{A}(Q)+\mathcal{K}=\pm kF_{-1}U\mod\left\{\mathcal{K}+j_{1}2\pi i+j_{2}\mathcal{B}:j_{1},j_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

When
k
is large enough, if the latter equality holds for some
x
in the pole region, then the left-hand side of (
1.1
) has a pole near
-k^{2/3}2^{1/3}x
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathscr{P}_{k}
denote all of the solutions in the
x
-plane of (
132
). Notice,
\mathscr{P}_{k}
is the set of exceptional points for which Riemann-Hilbert Problem
4.4.1
does not admit a solution. Now, let
\delta>0
be a fixed small number. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
let

(133)

\mathscr{S}_{k}:=\left\{x\text{ in the pole region }:\text{dist}(x,\mathscr{P}_{k})>\frac{\delta}{k^{2/3}}\right\}.

That is,
\mathscr{S}_{k}
is the result of excising a neighborhood of each exceptional point from the pole region.

We have completed the construction of
\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}(z)
, and thus
\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\text{(out)}}(z)
with the help of (
101
).

5.
Numerical Approximation

We use numerical methods to obtain approximations of the exact generalized Hastings-McLeod functions. However, since
u_{\text{HM}}^{(\alpha)}
has poles in the pole region of the plane, we will employ two distinct solvers: a spectral collocation boundary condition solver to handle the smooth, pole-free region, and a pole-vaulting method for the pole region developed by Fornberg and Weideman
[
15
]
. This numerical scheme has been used to survey the pole fields of various Painlevé transcendents; see
[
14
,
16
,
19
]
. In this section we will give a brief description of each method.

5.1.
Spectral Collocation Method

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the interpolation nodes

(166)

t_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{(k-1)\pi}{N-1}\right),\hskip 28.45274ptk=1,\dots,N,

which are Chebyshev points of the second kind, i.e. the extreme points on
[-1,1]
of
T_{N-1}(t)
, the Chebyshev polynomial of degree
N-1
. For a smooth function
f
on
[-1,1]
we set the interpolant
P_{N-1}(t)
to be a linear combination of Lagrangian interpolating polynomials. So,

(167)

f(t)\approx P_{N-1}(t)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{j}(t)f_{j},

where

(168)

f_{j}:=f(t_{j})\hskip 18.77892pt\text{and}\hskip 18.77892pt\varphi_{j}(t):=\prod\limits_{k=1,k\neq j}^{N}\frac{(t-t_{k})}{(t_{j}-t_{k})}.

In fact, since
t_{2},\dots,t_{N-1}
are the critical points of
T_{N-1}(t)
,

(169)

\varphi_{j}(t)=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{c_{j}}\frac{1-t^{2}}{(N-1)^{2}}\frac{T_{N-1}^{\prime}(t)}{t-t_{j}},

where
c_{1}=c_{N}=2
and
c_{2},\dots,c_{N-1}=1
.
Notice, on the set of interpolation nodes
\{t_{1},\dots,t_{N}\}
,
\varphi_{j}(t_{k})
acts as a Kronecker delta function of
j
and
k
. That is,

(170)

\varphi_{j}\left(t_{k}\right)=\delta_{jk}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }j\neq k,\\
1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We now define a collocation derivative operator,
\boldsymbol{D}
, on the interpolant
P_{N-1}(t)
by

(171)

\boldsymbol{D}p_{N-1}(t):=\left[P^{\prime}_{N-1}\left(t_{1}\right),\dots,P^{\prime}_{N-1}\left(t_{N}\right)\right]^{T}.

It was shown in
[
9
, p. 69]
that

(172)

[\boldsymbol{D}]_{k,j}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{c_{k}(-1)^{j+k}}{c_{j}(t_{k}-t_{j})},&j\neq k,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{2(N-1)^{2}+1}{6},&j=k=1,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{2(N-1)^{2}+1}{6},&j=k=N,\\
\displaystyle\frac{-t_{k}}{2(1-t_{k}^{2})},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}.

Furthermore, in our context, taking
k
derivatives of
P_{N-1}(t)
and evaluating at the nodes
\{t_{1},\dots,t_{N}\}
can be accomplished by the operator
\boldsymbol{D}^{(k)}
where

(173)

\boldsymbol{D}^{(k)}:=\left(\boldsymbol{D}\right)^{k}.

5.1.1.
Collocation Method for Generalized Hastings-McLeod

We now turn our attention to applying this method to the generalized Hastings-McLeod function for fixed
\alpha>-1/2
. First, we fix complex numbers
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
in the
y
-plane with
\Imag\left(y_{1}\right)=\Imag\left(y_{2}\right)
,
\Real\left(y_{1}\right)\ll 0\ll\Real\left(y_{2}\right)
, so that the asymptotics (
2
) are good approximations for both
u(y_{1})
and
u(y_{2})
. We also require that, after the appropriate scaling, the horizontal line segment connecting
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
(which we will denote as
[x_{1},x_{2}]
) is contained in the pole-free region of the
x
-plane. Next, we transform the ODE (
1
) from
[y_{1},y_{2}]
, to
[-1,1]
. In particular, we use the linear transformation

(174)

f(t):=\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}+\left(y_{2}-y_{1}\right)t}{2}.

Then, if
u
satisfies (
1
), then
v:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{C}
defined by
v(t):=u(f(t))
satisfies

(175)

\frac{\text{d}^{2}v}{\text{d}t^{2}}(t)=\frac{\left(y_{2}-y_{1}\right)^{2}}{4}\left(2v(t)^{3}+f(t)v(t)-\alpha\right).

We interpolate
v(t)
as in (
167
). That is, for
N
unknown values
v_{1},\dots,v_{N}
we set

(176)

P_{N-1}(t):=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{j}(t)v_{j}.

Our aim is for
v_{1},\dots,v_{N}
to approximate the values
v(t_{1}),\dots,v(t_{N})
respectively. Then,
v(t)\approx P_{N-1}(t)
. Now, we apply the discrete derivative operator (
173
) on the left-hand side of (
176
) and plug in
P_{N-1}
for
v
in the right-hand side of (
175
). As a result, we obtain an
N\times N
system of equations. We can enforce the boundary conditions of the generalized Hastings-McLeod solutions (
2
) by setting

(177)

v_{N}:=f\left(\sqrt{-a/2}\right),

since
x_{1}
was chosen so that (
2
) holds. Hence,
f\left(\sqrt{-a/2}\right)\approx f\left(u^{(\alpha)}_{\text{HM}}\left(x_{1}\right)\right)\approx v_{N}
. Similarly, we set
v_{1}:=f\left(\alpha/2\right)
. Finally, we use a Newton solver on the remaining
N-2
equations and variables.

5.2.
Padé Approximant Method

The collocation method is a boundary condition solver and hence assumes the function is smooth between the two boundary conditions. However, this is not always the case for the generalized Hastings-McLeod functions. Indeed, we know they blow up in the pole region of the
x
-plane. Therefore, we use a Padé approximant method due to Fornberg and Weideman, which is an initial-value problem solver, to handle this case. We now give a brief overview of Padé approximants and apply this method to the generalized Hastings-McLeod functions.

5.2.1.
Padé Expansion

Let
U
be an open set in
\mathbb{C}
. Say
f
is a meromorphic function in
U
and, for some fixed
x_{0}\in U
,
f
is analytic at
x_{0}
. As such,
f
has the following Taylor expansion about
x_{0}
:

(178)

f(x_{0}+h)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}h^{k}+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{n+1}\right).

However, this approximation fails in a neighborhood of a pole. To obtain a more robust approximation that allows for some poles, we convert the Taylor expansion into a Padé expansion. That is, instead of approximating
f
as a polynomial, we approximate
f
as a nontrivial rational function. So, we need to find complex coefficients
a_{0},\dots,a_{\nu_{1}},b_{1},\dots b_{\nu_{2}}
such that

(179)

f(x_{0}+h)=\frac{a_{0}+a_{1}h+\cdots+a_{\nu_{1}}h^{\nu_{1}}}{1+b_{1}h+\cdots+b_{\nu_{2}}h^{\nu_{2}}}+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{n+1}\right),

where
\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}=n
. In our problem we take
n
to be even and set
\nu=\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}:=n/2
. The neighborhood in which the Padé approximation (
179
) is decent is larger than the corresponding neighborhood of the Taylor approximation (
178
) because if
x_{*}
is a pole, then the denominator of (
179
) can vanish near
x_{*}
. As the denominator has
\nu_{2}
zeros, we see that the closure of the domain of (
179
) can contain
\nu_{2}
poles before we lose control of the error term.

5.2.2.
Padé Approximant Method for Generalized Hastings-McLeod.

Fix
\alpha>-1/2
and let
u(y)
be the generalized Hastings-McLeod solution to (
1
). In order to obtain an approximation of
u
in the form of (
179
) we will need a pair of initial values
u(y_{0})
and
u^{\prime}(y_{0})
. First, we use the forward Euler numerical scheme to obtain a partial Taylor series approximation of
u
about
y_{0}
for some even integer
n
(in our problem we set
n=24
). That is, we set
c_{0}=u(y_{0})
and
c_{1}=u^{\prime}\left(y_{0}\right)
and write

(180)

u(y_{0}+h)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}h^{k}+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{n+1}\right)

for
n-1
unknown complex values
c_{2},\dots,c_{n}
. To solve for
c_{2},\dots,c_{n}
we plug (
180
) into the ODE (
1
) and find that

\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n}k(k-1)c_{k}h^{k-2}+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{n-1}\right)=2\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}h^{k}\right)^{3}+(y_{0}+h)\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}h^{k}\right)-\alpha+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{n-1}\right).

So, we can compare the coefficients of
1,h,\dots,h^{n-2}
and obtain an
(n-1)\times(n-1)
system of equations (that can be solved recursively) for the variables
c_{2},\dots,c_{n}
.
To convert (
180
) into a Padé approximant, with
n+1
unknown complex values
a_{0},\dots,a_{\nu},b_{1},\dots b_{\nu}
we write

(181)

\frac{a_{0}+\cdots+a_{\nu}h^{\nu}}{1+b_{1}h+\cdots+b_{\nu}h^{\nu}}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}h^{k}+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{n+1}\right).

Now, by multiplying both sides of (
181
) by
1+b_{1}h+\cdots+b_{\nu}h^{\nu}
, we can compare the coefficients of the
n+1
monomials
1,h,\dots,h^{n}
and obtain an
(n+1)\times(n+1)
system of equations that once again can be solved recursively. Hence, we find that

(182)

u(y_{0}+h)=\frac{a_{0}+\cdots+a_{\nu}h^{\nu}}{1+b_{1}h+\cdots+b_{\nu}h^{\nu}}+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{n+1}\right)

in a neighborhood of
y_{0}
for which
\nu
poles have been excised.

Notice, in order to obtain an approximation resilient to
\nu
poles, we just needed the initial values
u(y_{0})
and
u^{\prime}(y_{0})
. Further, (
182
) can generate decent approximations of
u
(and hence
u^{\prime}
) at points that are not
y_{0}
. Thus, by choosing a point
y_{1}
that is close enough to
y_{0}
such that (
182
) is still a good approximation of
u(y_{1})
, we are able to extend the region of the
y
-plane where we can approximate
u
. As such, given
U
, a “pole filled” region of the complex
y
-plane and
y_{0}\in U
such that the values
u(y_{0})
and
u^{\prime}(y_{0})
are known, we create a coarse grid of node points filling out
U
, with the aim of finding a Padé approximant near each node point. Fornberg and Weideman first implemented such an algorithm in
[
15
]
.

Their idea is to start at
y_{0}
and to generate integration paths that branch out so that each node point is near a path. Further, to preserve accuracy by minimizing numerical cancellations, we want to avoid integrating near a pole. Therefore, we want to choose paths along which the solution remains small in magnitude. This is accomplished by running the following routine.

(1)

Uniformly sample a target node from the aforementioned grid of node points.

(2)

Find the nearest point with a recorded Padé approximation. (Since we start with initial values we know such a point will exist.)

(3)

Take a step of length
h
(in our case
h=1/2
) towards the target. We consider five candidate directions: one aiming directly at the target and the remaining ones veering off by
22.5^{\circ}
and
45^{\circ}
to either side. Evaluate the known Padé approximant at each of the five candidate points, and pick the direction whose candidate had the smallest norm evaluation.

(4)

Apply the forward Euler scheme and Padé approximation method with initial data
u
and
u^{\prime}
corresponding to the chosen candidate.

(5)

Add the candidate point to the list of points with Padé approximants, record the Padé coefficients, and remove all node points within distance
1/2
of the Padé approximant.

(6)

Repeat steps
(3)
through
(5)
until we are within distance
h
away from our target.

Finally, we repeat the above routine until the target points are empty. Now given
y\in U
, we approximate

Appendix B

when

In this appendix we provide proofs of statements (
33
) and (
34
). In both statements
x
is a fixed, real number (and hence, in the pole-free region). The first statement is when
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then

(185)

\Real\left(2h\left(-\overline{z};x\right)+\lambda(x)\right)=\Real\left(2h(z;x)+\lambda(x)\right).

We prove the equivalent statement that

(186)

\Real\left(h(-\overline{z}:x)\right)=\Real\left(h(z;x)\right).

Proof.

Consider the function
T(x):=iS(x)
. Then,
T(x)
is one of the solutions to the cubic equation

(187)

T^{3}-xT-2=0.

The discriminant of (
187
) is
4x^{3}-108
, which vanishes at
x=3
. Indeed, at
x=3
, (
187
) has a double root at
T=-1
and a simple root at
T=2
. Since
S(x)
is continuous at
x=3
, it follows that
T(3)=2
. Further,
4x^{3}-108
does not vanish along the real line (except at
x=3
), so when
x
is real
T(x)
is the largest real root of (
187
). So, when
x<3
,
T(x)
will be the only real root of (
187
); on the other hand, when
x>3
,
T(x)
will be the largest root among the three distinct real roots of (
187
). Notice, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
the cubic polynomial
p(T):=T^{3}-xT-2
is negative at
T=0
. Indeed,
P(0)=-2
. However, since
P(T)\to+\infty
as
T\to+\infty
,
P(T)
must have at least one positive root. Therefore,
T(x)
, being the largest real root of
p
, must be positive, which implies that
S(x)
is negative-imaginary for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Since the square root in (
22
) is principal, it follows that
\Delta(x)
is on the positive real axis and

(188)

a(x)=-\overline{b(x)}.

Further, (
188
) (along with basic trigonometric identities) implies when
x
is real, the square-root function
r(z;x)
(defined in (
23
)) exhibits the symmetry

(189)

r\left(-\overline{z};x\right)=-\overline{r(z;x)}.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the bivariate function of real variables
u
and
v
defined by

(190)

j(u,v;x)\equiv j(u,v):=h(u+iv)-h(-u+iv)=h(z)-h\left(-\overline{z}\right)

(here
z=u+iv
and
j(u,v)
is well defined whenever
h(z)
is). Notice, (
33
) holds if and only if the real part of
j(u,v)
is identically zero. Moreover, since
j(0,0)=0
, it suffices to show that the real part of the partials of
j
vanish. Using the Cauchy-Riemann equations one can show this is equivalent to
\Real(h^{\prime}(z)+h^{\prime}(-\overline{z}))=0
and
\Imag(h^{\prime}(z)-h^{\prime}(-\overline{z}))=0

Looking at (
28
) and (
24
) we see that
h^{\prime}(z)=i(S+2z)r(z)
. Find
t\in\mathbb{R}
so that
S(x)=it
. We compute

(191)

\displaystyle\begin{split}h^{\prime}(z)\pm h^{\prime}(-\overline{z})&=i(it+2z)r(z)\pm i(it-2\overline{z})r(-\overline{z})\\
&=i(it+2z)r(z)\pm i(-it+2\overline{z})\overline{r(z)}\\
&=i\big((it+2z)r(z)\pm\overline{(it+2z)r(z)}\big).\end{split}

Thus, we have that
h^{\prime}(z)+h^{\prime}(-\overline{z})
is purely imaginary and
h^{\prime}(z)-h^{\prime}(-\overline{z})
is purely real, our desired results.
∎

We now prove when
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\Real(2h(z;x)+\lambda(x))
can at most vanish two times on the real line (in the
z
-plane).

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and consider the function
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

(192)

\mathcal{F}(u):=\Real(2h(u;x)+\lambda(x)).

Rolle’s theorem tells us for any two zeros of
\mathcal{F}
, there exists a point between them such that
\Real(h^{\prime})
vanishes at that point. In particular, if
\mathcal{F}
has three or more zeros, then
\Real(h^{\prime})
would vanish at least two times on
\mathbb{R}
. Thus, in order to show that
\mathcal{F}
can have at most two zeros on
\mathbb{R}
it suffices to show that
\Real(h^{\prime})
only vanishes once on
\mathbb{R}
, namely at
0
. Recall

(193)

h^{\prime}(z)=i(S+2z)r(z).

Using (
188
) we see that
h^{\prime}(0)=-S|b|
. Seeing as
S
is purely imaginary, we have that
\Real(h^{\prime}(0))=0
. Since
h^{\prime}
only vanishes at
z=a
,

(none of which are real numbers), it follows

. Moreover, the symmetry (

\arg(h^{\prime}(-u))=\pi-\arg(h(u)).

Hence, it suffices to show that for

For the upper bound we see that, since

\arg(h^{\prime}(u))=\arg(i)+\arg(S+2u)+\arg(r(u))<\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\arg(-a)+\arg(\overline{a})}{2}=\pi.
