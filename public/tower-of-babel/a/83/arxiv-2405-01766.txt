Abstract
. This paper describes infinite sets of linear and polynomial equations in infinitely many variables with the property that the existence of a solution or even an approximate solution for every finite subset of the equations implies the existence of a solution for the infinite set of equations.

1.
Finitely many implies infinite many

In mathematics there are theorems asserting that, for certain classes of equations,
if every finite subset of an infinite set of the equations has a solution,
then the infinite set of equations has a solution.
For example, if every finite subset of an infinite set of linear equations in
n
variables
(that is, equations of the form
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}x_{j}=b_{i}
)
has a solution,
then the infinite set of linear equations in
n
variables has a solution.
For infinite sets of linear equations in infinitely many variables, in which each equation
contains only finitely many variables
(that is, equations of the form
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{j}=b_{i}
such that, for each
i
,
we have
a_{i,j}\neq 0
for only finitely many
j
),
a “finitely solvable implies infinitely solvable” theorem is also true.
For a survey of related results, see Nathanson
[
4
]
.

There are analogous results for linear equations that contain infinitely many variables.
The pair
(p,q)
is
Hölder conjugate

or, simply,
conjugate
if
p>1
and
q>1
are real numbers such that

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1

or if
p=1
and
q=\infty
.
For
1\leq p<\infty
, let

\ell^{p}=\left\{\mathbf{a}=\left(a_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}:\|\mathbf{a}\|_{p}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|a_{j}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}<\infty\right\}

and, for
p=\infty
, let

\ell^{\infty}=\left\{\mathbf{a}=\left(a_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}:\|\mathbf{a}\|_{\infty}=\sup\{|a_{j}|:j=1,2,3,\ldots\}<\infty\right\}

be the classical Lebesque spaces of
infinite sequences of real or complex numbers.

Recall Hölder’s inequality: If
(p,q)
is a conjugate pair and

\mathbf{a}=\left(a_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}\qqand\mathbf{x}=\left(x_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}

then

\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}=\left(a_{j}x_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{1}

and

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|a_{j}x_{j}|=\left\|\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|_{p}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|_{q}.

Thus, the infinite series

(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}x_{j}

converges absolutely and
it makes sense to ask if there is a solution
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}
of the linear equation
in infinitely many variables

(1)

L(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}x_{j}=b.

We retain the usual ambiguity between sequences of numbers and sequences of variables.
An
exact solution
or, simply, a
solution
of equation (
1
)
is a sequence

\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}

such that
L(\mathbf{x})=b
.
Equation (
1
) has an
approximate solution
if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\mathbf{x}_{\varepsilon}\in\ell^{q}
such that

\left|L(\mathbf{x}_{\varepsilon})-b\right|<\varepsilon.

Let
\mathbf{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
be the set of positive integers
and
\mathbf{N}^{k}
the set of
k
-tuples of positive integers.
Extending a classical result of F. Riesz
[
5
, pp. 61–62]

(see also Banach
[
2
, p. 47]
),
Abian and Eslami
[
1
]
proved the following beautiful theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(p,q)
be a conjugate pair with
p>1
and
q>1

and let
\mathbf{a}_{i}=\left(a_{i,j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}

for all
i\in\mathbf{N}
. Consider the linear equation in infinitely many variables

L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{j}=b_{i}.

Let
M>0
.
If, for every finite subset
S
of
\mathbf{N}
, there is a sequence
\mathbf{x}_{S}\in\ell^{q}

such that
\|\mathbf{x}_{S}\|_{q}\leq M
and
L_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{S})=b_{i}
for all
i\in S
,
then there is a sequence
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}

such that
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq M
and
L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=b_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbf{N}
.

Abian and Eslami proved their theorem only for
p>1
and for
countably many equations
with real coefficients and real solutions, but the result also holds for
(p,q)=(1,\infty)
and
for uncountably many equations with complex coefficients and complex solutions.
In Appendix
A
we prove Riesz’s theorem and derive
Theorem
1
from it.

It is important to observe that Theorem
1

is not true without the condition
that the norm of
\|\mathbf{x}_{S}\|_{q}
is uniformly bounded by
M
for all finite sets
S
.
The following example is essentially due to Helly
[
3
]
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}
with
a_{j}\neq 0
for all
j\in\mathbf{N}
.
For all
i\in\mathbf{N}
, define the sequence
\mathbf{a}_{i}=(a_{i,j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}
as follows:

a_{i,j}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $j<i$}\\
a_{i}&\text{if $j\geq i$.}\end{cases}

For all
i\in\mathbf{N}
, consider the linear equation in infinitely many variables

L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{j}=\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}a_{j}x_{j}=1.

We obtain the “triangular” system of equations

\begin{array}[]{rl}a_{1}x_{1}+a_{2}x_{2}+a_{3}x_{3}+\cdots+a_{i}x_{i}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}+\cdots&=1\\
a_{2}x_{2}+a_{3}x_{3}+\cdots+a_{i}x_{i}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}+\cdots&=1\\
a_{3}x_{3}+\cdots+a_{i}x_{i}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}+\cdots&=1\\
\vdots&\\
a_{i}x_{i}+a_{i+1}x_{i+1}+\cdots&=1\\
a_{i+1}x_{i+1}+\cdots&=1\\
\end{array}

For all
i\geq 1
, if
L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=L_{i+1}(\mathbf{x})=1
, then
a_{i}x_{i}=0
and so
x_{i}=0

(because
a_{i}\neq 0
).
It follows that the infinite set of equations has no solution.
However, every finite subset of these equations is solvable.

Here is one solution of the triangular system.
For all
r\in\mathbf{N}
, the sequence
\mathbf{x}_{r}=(x_{r,j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
defined by

x_{r,j}=\begin{cases}1/a_{r}&\text{if $j=r$}\\
0&\text{if $j\neq r$}\end{cases}

is a solution of the finite set of equations
\{L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=1:i=1,\ldots,r\}
.
We have

\|\mathbf{x}_{r}\|_{q}=\frac{1}{|a_{r}|}\qqand\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\|\mathbf{x}_{r}\|_{q}=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{|a_{r}|}=\infty.

Thus, the sequence of solutions
(\mathbf{x}_{r})_{r=1}^{\infty}
is not uniformly bounded in
\ell^{q}
.

More generally, if
\mathbf{x}_{r}
is any solution of the first
r
equations of the triangular system, then

1=\sum_{j=r}^{\infty}a_{j}x_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{r,j}x_{j}\leq\|\mathbf{a}_{r}\mathbf{x}_{r}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{a}_{r}\|_{p}\|\mathbf{x}_{r}\|_{q}

and so

\|\mathbf{x}_{r}\|_{q}\geq\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{a}_{r}\|_{p}}.

Because
\mathbf{a}\in\ell^{p}
, we have
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\|\mathbf{a}_{r}\|_{p}=0

and so the sequence of solutions
(\mathbf{x}_{r})_{r=1}^{\infty}
is not uniformly bounded in
\ell^{q}
.

The following result extends Theorem
1

to approximately solvable systems of linear equations.

Theorem 2
.

Let
(p,q)
be a conjugate pair.
Let
I
be an infinite set. For all
i\in I
, let

\mathbf{a}_{i}=\left(a_{i,j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}

and consider the linear equation in infinitely many variables

L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{j}=b_{i}.

Let
M>0
.
If, for every finite subset
S
of I and every
\varepsilon>0
,
the finite set of linear inequalities

\left\{|L_{i}(\mathbf{x})-b_{i}|\leq\varepsilon:i\in S\right\}

has a solution
\mathbf{x}_{S,\varepsilon}\in\ell^{q}

with
\|\mathbf{x}_{S,\varepsilon}\|_{q}\leq M
,
then the infinite set of linear equations

\left\{L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=b_{i}:i\in I\right\}

has an exact solution
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}
with
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq M
.

In this paper we generalize Theorem
2
to certain infinite
sets of polynomial equations in infinitely many variables and prove
that the existence of norm-bounded or even sequentially bounded
approximate solutions to all finite subsets of the set of polynomial equations is sufficient
to guarantee the existence of an exact solution to the infinite set (Theorems
4
and
5
).
The theorem on linear equations is a special case of the polynomial results.

Our theorems apply to polynomials with coefficients and solutions
in the field of real numbers and also in the field of complex numbers.
However, the “finitely many implies infinitely many” paradigm is not true
for all subfields of the complex numbers.
For example, the paradigm fails for the field
\mathbf{Q}
of rational numbers.
Let
(b_{i})_{i\in I}
be an infinite sequence of irrational numbers and consider
the infinite set of linear equations
\{x_{i}=b_{i}:i\in I\}
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, every finite subset of the equations and, indeed, the infinite set
of equations have approximate solutions in
\mathbf{Q}
, but neither the infinite set
nor any nonempty finite subset of the equations has an exact solution in
\mathbf{Q}
.

2.
Polynomials in infinitely many variables

Let
D
be a positive integer, let
d\in\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
, and let

D<q\leq\infty.

If
q<\infty
, then

\frac{1}{q/(q-d)}+\frac{1}{q/d}=1

and so the real numbers
q/(q-d)>1
and
q/d>1
form a conjugate pair.
For
q=\infty
, we define

\frac{q}{(q-d)}=1\qqand\frac{q}{d}=\infty.

If

\mathbf{a}=\left(a_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q/(q-d)}

and

\mathbf{x}=\left(x_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}

then

\mathbf{x}^{d}=\left(x_{j}^{d}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q/d}.

From Hölder’s inequality we obtain

\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}^{d}=\left(a_{j}x_{j}^{d}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{1}

and

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|a_{j}x_{j}^{d}\right|=\left\|\mathbf{a}\mathbf{x}^{d}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|_{q/(q-d)}\left\|\mathbf{x}^{d}\right\|_{q/d}<\infty.

Thus, the infinite series

\left(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}^{d}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}x_{j}^{d}

converges absolutely.

For all
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{k}
be the set of all
k
-tuples of positive integers
whose sum is at most
D
, that is,

(2)

\mathcal{D}_{k}=\left\{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}:d_{1}+d_{2}+\cdots+d_{k}\leq D\right\}.

This is a finite set of cardinality
\binom{D}{k}
.
The set
\bigcup_{k=1}^{D}\mathcal{D}_{k}
has cardinality
\sum_{k=1}^{D}\binom{D}{k}=2^{D}-1
.
For all

\Delta=(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}

and

J=(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}

we define the monomial

x_{J}^{\Delta}=x_{j_{1}}^{d_{1}}x_{j_{2}}^{d_{2}}\cdots x_{j_{k}}^{d_{k}}

of degree

|\Delta|=d_{1}+d_{2}+\cdots+d_{k}\leq D.

For example, let
D=3
and
k\in\{1,2,3\}
.
There are
7=2^{3}-1
sets of monomials.
For
k=1
and
J=(j_{1})\in\mathbf{N}^{1}
, we have

\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{1}=\{(1),(2),(3)\}\qand x_{J}^{\Delta}\in\left\{x_{j_{1}},\ x_{j_{1}}^{2},\ x_{j_{1}}^{3}\right\}.

For
k=2
and
J=(j_{1},j_{2})\in\mathbf{N}^{2}
, we have

\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{2}=\{(1,1),(1,2),(2,1)\}\qand x_{J}^{\Delta}\in\left\{x_{j_{1}}x_{j_{2}},\ x_{j_{1}}x_{j_{2}}^{2},\ x_{j_{1}}^{2}x_{j_{2}}\right\}.

For
k=3
and
J=(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})\in\mathbf{N}^{3}
, we have

\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{3}=\{(1,1,1)\}\qand x_{J}^{\Delta}=x_{j_{1}}x_{j_{2}}x_{j_{3}}.

Monomials do not have a unique representation in the form
x_{J}^{\Delta}
.
For example, with
D=3
, let
k=2
and
J=(1,1)\in\mathbf{N}^{2}
.
If
\Delta=(1,2)\in\mathcal{D}_{2}
, then
x_{J}^{\Delta}=x_{1}x_{1}^{2}=x_{1}^{3}
.
If
\Delta=(2,1)\in\mathcal{D}_{2}
, then
x_{J}^{\Delta}=x_{1}^{2}x_{1}=x_{1}^{3}
.
If
D=3
,
k=1
,
J=(1)\in\mathbf{N}^{1}
, and
\Delta=(3)\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
, then

x_{J}^{\Delta}=x_{1}^{3}
.
Note that we do not need unique representation of monomials,
but we would have unique representation if we considered only
k
-tuples

(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}
such that
j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{k}
.)

We consider only polynomials with zero constant term. In this paper, “polynomial”
means polynomial with zero constant term.
By moving constants from one side of an equation to the other side,
it is always possible to transform an equation that includes polynomials with nonzero constant terms
to an equivalent equation in which no polynomial has a nonzero constant term.

A
polynomial of degree at most
D
in infinitely many variables

\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}

is a formal power series of the form

(3)

P(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\sum_{J\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}a_{\Delta,J}\ x_{J}^{\Delta}

with real or complex coefficients
a_{\Delta,J}
.
For example, a polynomial of degree at most 3 in infinitely many variables is of the form

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{\infty}a_{(1),(j_{1})}x_{j_{1}}+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{\infty}a_{(2),(j_{1})}x_{j_{1}}^{2}+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{\infty}a_{(3),(j_{1})}x_{j_{1}}^{3}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{\infty}a_{(1,1),(j_{1},j_{2})}x_{j_{1}}x_{j_{2}}+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{\infty}a_{(1,2),(j_{1},j_{2})}x_{j_{1}}x_{j_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{\infty}a_{(2,1),(j_{1},j_{2})}x_{j_{1}}^{2}x_{j_{2}}+\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j_{3}=1}^{\infty}a_{(1,1,1),(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})}x_{j_{1}}x_{j_{2}}x_{j_{3}}.

3.
Multiplicative polynomials

Let
D
be a positive integer and let
1\leq D<q\leq\infty
.
Let

P(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\sum_{J\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}a_{\Delta,J}\ x_{J}^{\Delta}

be a polynomial of degree at most
D
in infinitely many variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}

with coefficients
a_{\Delta,J}
.
We consider polynomials whose coefficients
a_{\Delta,J}
are
multiplicative

in the following sense: For all
d\in\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
there is a sequence

\mathbf{a}_{d}=\left(a_{d,j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q/(q-d)}

such that,
if

\Delta=(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\qqand J=(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}

then

a_{\Delta,J}=a_{(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k}),(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})}=a_{d_{1},j_{1}}a_{d_{2},j_{2}}\cdots a_{d_{k},j_{k}}.

This gives the monomial factorization

\displaystyle a_{\Delta,J}x_{J}^{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\left(a_{d_{1},j_{1}}a_{d_{2},j_{2}}\cdots a_{d_{k},j_{k}}\right)\left(x_{j_{1}}^{d_{1}}x_{j_{2}}^{d_{2}}\cdots x_{j_{k}}^{d_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(a_{d_{1},j_{1}}x_{j_{1}}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(a_{d_{2},j_{2}}x_{j_{2}}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(a_{d_{k},j_{k}}x_{j_{k}}^{d_{k}}\right)

and so

\displaystyle\sum_{J\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}a_{\Delta,J}\ x_{J}^{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{j})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}\left(a_{d_{1},j_{1}}x_{j_{1}}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(a_{d_{2},j_{2}}x_{j_{2}}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(a_{d_{k},j_{k}}x_{j_{k}}^{d_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{\infty}a_{d_{1},j_{1}}x_{j_{1}}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{\infty}a_{d_{2},j_{2}}x_{j_{2}}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{j_{k}=1}^{\infty}a_{d_{k},j_{k}}x_{j_{k}}^{d_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{1}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{2}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{k}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{k}}\right).

For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
, the rearrangement is justified
by the absolute convergence of the
k
infinite series

\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{1}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{1}}\right),\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{2}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{2}}\right),\ldots,\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{k}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{k}}\right)
.
We obtain

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\sum_{J\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}a_{\Delta,J}\ x_{J}^{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left(\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{\infty}a_{d_{1},j_{1}}x_{j_{1}}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{\infty}a_{d_{2},j_{2}}x_{j_{2}}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{j_{k}=1}^{\infty}a_{d_{k},j_{k}}x_{j_{k}}^{d_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{1}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{2}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{k}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{k}}\right).

This is a finite sum, and so the series
P(\mathbf{x})
converges absolutely
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}
.

Let
D
be a positive integer and let
1\leq D<q\leq\infty
.
A
q
-multiplicative polynomial of degree at most
D

or, simply, a
multiplicative polynomial
,
is a polynomial of degree at most
D
in infinitely many variables of the form

P(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{1}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{2}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{k}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{k}}\right)

where

\mathbf{a}_{d}=\left(a_{d,j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q/(q-d)}

for all
d\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
. The infinite series converges for all
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}
.

A
multiplicative polynomial equation
is an equation of the form

P(\mathbf{x})=b

where
P(\mathbf{x})
is a multiplicative polynomial.
We ask if this equation has a solution
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}
.

The analogue of Theorem
1

for linear equations is the following “finitely many implies infinitely many” solvability result
for multiplicative polynomial equations.

Theorem 3
.

Let
D
be a positive integer and let
1\leq D<q\leq\infty
.
Let
I
be an infinite set.
For all
i\in I
and
d\in\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
, let

\mathbf{a}_{i,d}=\left(a_{i,d,j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q/(q-d)}.

For
\Delta=(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
and
J=(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}
,
let

a_{i,\Delta,J}=a_{i,d_{1},j_{1}}a_{i,d_{2},j_{2}}\cdots a_{i,d_{k},j_{k}}.

For all
i\in I
, consider the multiplicative polynomial equation

\displaystyle P_{i}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\sum_{J\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}a_{i,\Delta,J}\ x_{J}^{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left(\mathbf{a}_{i,d_{1}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(\mathbf{a}_{i,d_{2}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(\mathbf{a}_{i,d_{k}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=b_{i}.

Let
M>0
.
If, for every finite subset
S
of I, the finite set of polynomial equations

\{P_{i}(\mathbf{x})=b_{i}:i\in S\}

has a solution
\mathbf{x}_{S}\in\ell^{q}
with
\|\mathbf{x}_{S}\|_{q}\leq M
,
then the infinite set of polynomial equations
\{P_{i}(\mathbf{x})=b_{i}:i\in I\}

has a solution
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}
with
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq M
.

4.
Approximate finite implies exact infinite

The set
\{P_{i}(\mathbf{x})=b_{i}:i\in S\}
of polynomial equations in infinitely many
variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
has an
approximate solution
if,
for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a sequence
\mathbf{x}_{\varepsilon}
such that

|P_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\varepsilon})-b_{i}|\leq\varepsilon
for all
i\in S
.
Theorem
4
states that an infinite set
of multiplicative polynomial equations has an exact solution
if every finite subset of the equations has a
norm-bounded approximate solution.
This is the main result of this paper and immediately implies
Theorems
1
,
2
,
and
3
.

The theorem and the lemmas in this section
are valid in both the real and complex fields.

Theorem 4
.

Let
D
be a positive integer and let

1\leq D<q\leq\infty
. For all
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
, let

\mathcal{D}_{k}=\left\{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}:d_{1}+d_{2}+\cdots+d_{k}\leq D\right\}.

Let
I
be an infinite set and let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
.
For all
i\in I
and
d\in\{1,2,\ldots,D\}
, let

\mathbf{a}_{i,d}=\left(a_{i,d,j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q/(q-d)}

and

\left(\mathbf{a}_{i,d},\mathbf{x}^{d}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,d,j}x_{j}^{d}.

For
\Delta=(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
and
J=(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}
,
define the multiplicative coefficient

a_{i,\Delta,J}=a_{i,d_{1},j_{1}}a_{i,d_{2},j_{2}}\cdots a_{i,d_{k},j_{k}}.

For all
i\in I
, the finite set of sequences
\{\mathbf{a}_{i,d}\}_{d=1}^{D}

determines the multiplicative polynomial

\displaystyle P_{i}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\sum_{J\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}a_{i,\Delta,J}\ x_{J}^{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left(\mathbf{a}_{i,d_{1}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(\mathbf{a}_{i,d_{2}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(\mathbf{a}_{i,d_{k}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{k}}\right).

Let
M>0
and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{i})_{i\in I}
.
If, for every
\varepsilon>0
and every finite subset
S
of
I
,
the finite set of polynomial inequalities

\left\{|P_{i}(\mathbf{x})-b_{i}|\leq\varepsilon:i\in S\right\}

has a solution
\mathbf{x}_{S,\varepsilon}\in\ell^{q}
with
\|\mathbf{x}_{S,\varepsilon}\|_{q}\leq M
,
then the infinite set of polynomial equations

\left\{P_{i}(\mathbf{x})=b_{i}:i\in I\right\}

has an exact solution
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}
with
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq M
.

We begin with two results whose statements and
proofs are valid in both the real and complex cases.

For
M>0
, the closed interval
[-M,M]=\{x\in\mathbf{R}:|x|\leq M\}

and the closed ball
B_{M}=\{x\in\mathbf{C}:|x|\leq M\}
are compact.
For polynomial equations in
\mathbf{R}
we use the compact topological space

\Omega=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}[-M,M]

and for polynomial equations in
\mathbf{C}
we use the compact topological space

\Omega=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}B_{M}.

Let

X_{q,M}=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}:\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq M\right\}.

Lemma 1
.

The set
X_{q,M}
is a compact subset of the topological space
\Omega
.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
and
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq M
, then
|x_{j}|\leq M
for all

j\in\mathbf{N}
and so
X_{q,M}\subseteq\Omega
.
Because
\Omega
is compact, it suffices to prove that
X_{q,M}
is closed
in the product topology on
\Omega
, or equivalently, that the complement of
X_{q,M}

is open.
The complement of
X_{q,M}
is the set

\displaystyle Y_{q,M}

\displaystyle=\Omega\setminus X_{q,M}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\Omega:\mathbf{y}\notin\ell^{q}\right\}\bigcup\left\{\mathbf{y}\in\Omega:\mathbf{y}\in\ell^{q}\text{ and }\|\mathbf{y}\|_{q}>M\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}:\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|y_{j}|^{q}>M^{q}\right\}.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in Y_{q,M}
.
There exist
\varepsilon>0
and
N\in\mathbf{N}
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{N}|y_{j}|^{q}>M^{q}+\varepsilon.

Because the function
f(t)=|t|^{q}
is continuous, there exists
\delta>0
such
that
|t-y_{j}|<\delta
implies

|t|^{q}>|y_{j}|^{q}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2N}

for all
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
.
The set

U=\left\{\mathbf{z}=(z_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\Omega:|z_{j}-y_{j}|<\delta\text{ for all }j\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\right\}

is an open neighborhood of
\mathbf{y}
in
\Omega
.
For all
\mathbf{z}=(z_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in U
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|z_{j}|^{q}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{N}|z_{j}|^{q}\ >\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(|y_{j}|^{q}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2N}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}|y_{j}|^{q}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}>M^{q}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

\displaystyle>M^{q}

and so
\mathbf{z}\in Y_{q,M}
.
It follows that
U\subseteq Y_{q,M}
and so

Y_{q,M}
is an open subset of
\Omega
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
d
be a positive integer,
d<q\leq\infty
, and let
p=q/(q-d)
.
Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}
.
Let
M>0
.
The function
f_{d}
on
X_{q,M}
defined by

f_{d}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}^{d})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}x_{j}^{d}

is continuous with respect to the product topology on
X_{q,M}

as a subspace of
\Omega
.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{x}\in X_{q,M}
, then
\mathbf{x}^{d}\in\ell^{q/d}
.
Because
(p,q/d)
is a conjugate pair,
the infinite series
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}x_{j}^{d}
converges absolutely and the
function
f_{d}(\mathbf{x})
is well-defined.

Let
U
be an open subset of
\mathbf{R}
or
\mathbf{C}
.
We shall prove
f_{d}^{-1}(U)
is open in
X_{q,M}

as a subspace of
\Omega
.

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in f_{d}^{-1}(U)
.
Because
f_{d}(\mathbf{x})\in U
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
U

contains the open set

\left\{t:|t-f_{d}(\mathbf{x})|<\varepsilon\right\}.

Because
\mathbf{a}\in\ell^{p}
, the series

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|a_{j}|^{p}
converges and there is an integer
N_{\mathbf{a}}
such that

\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}|a_{j}|^{p}<\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{3M}\right)^{p}

for all
N\geq N_{\mathbf{a}}
.
Because
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}
, the series

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|x_{j}\right|^{q}
converges
and there is an integer
N_{\mathbf{x}}
such that

\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}|x_{j}|^{q}<\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{3M}\right)^{q}

for all
N\geq N_{\mathbf{x}}
.
Choose
N\geq\max(N_{\mathbf{a}},N_{\mathbf{x}})
and let
\delta>0
satisfy

\delta\sum_{j=1}^{N}|a_{j}|<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Choose
\delta^{\prime}>0
such that
|y_{j}-x_{j}|<\delta^{\prime}
implies

|y_{j}^{d}-x_{j}^{d}|<\delta
for all
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
.
The set

V^{\prime}=\left\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\Omega:|y_{j}-x_{j}|<\delta^{\prime}\text{ for all }j\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\right\}

is an open neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}
in the topological space
\Omega
and so

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}\cap X_{q,M}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{y}=(y_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in X_{q,M}:|y_{j}-x_{j}|<\delta^{\prime}\text{ for all }j\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}\right\}

is an open neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}
in
X_{q,M}
.
We shall prove that
V\subseteq f_{d}^{-1}(U)
.

Let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in V
. We define

\tilde{x_{j}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $j\leq N$}\\
x_{j}&\text{if $j\geq N+1$}\end{cases}

and

\tilde{y_{j}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $j\leq N$}\\
y_{j}&\text{if $j\geq N+1$.}\end{cases}

Then

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=(\tilde{x_{j}})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}\qqand\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=(\tilde{y_{j}})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}

and

\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_{q}\leq\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq M\qqand\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_{q}\leq\|\mathbf{y}\|_{q}\leq M.

Thus,
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in X_{q,M}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\in X_{q,M}
.
We define

\tilde{a_{j}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $j\leq N$}\\
a_{j}&\text{if $j\geq N+1$.}\end{cases}

Then

\tilde{\mathbf{a}}=(\tilde{a_{j}})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}

and

\|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\|_{p}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3M}.

We have

\displaystyle f_{d}(\mathbf{y})-f_{d}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y}^{d})-(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}^{d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}y_{j}^{d}-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}x_{j}^{d}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\left(y_{j}^{d}-x_{j}^{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}(y_{j}^{d}-x_{j}^{d})+\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}a_{j}(y_{j}^{d}-x_{j}^{d}).

Applying the triangle, Hölder, and Minkowski inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|f_{d}(\mathbf{y})-f_{d}(\mathbf{x})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}|a_{j}|\ |y_{j}^{d}-x_{j}^{d}|+\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}|a_{j}(y_{j}^{d}-x_{j}^{d})|

\displaystyle\leq\delta\sum_{j=1}^{N}|a_{j}|+\left\|\left(\tilde{\mathbf{a}},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\right\|_{p}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\right\|_{q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}\right\|_{p}\left(\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\right\|_{q}+\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right\|_{q}\right)

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3M}(M+M)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon.

Therefore,
f_{d}(\mathbf{y})\in U
and
V\subseteq f_{d}^{-1}(U)
.
Thus, the set
f_{d}^{-1}(U)
is open in
X_{q,M}
and the function
f_{d}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}^{d})

is continuous on
X_{q,M}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

We can now prove Theorem

.

Proof.

For all
i\in I
we have the multiplicative polynomial

P_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\left(\mathbf{a}_{i,d_{1}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(\mathbf{a}_{i,d_{2}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(\mathbf{a}_{i,d_{k}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{k}}\right).

5.
An application to Dirichlet series

Here is an example of a multiplicative polynomial. Let
D
be a positive integer and let

D<q\leq\infty
. For
d\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
,
let
{s_{d}}\in\mathbf{C}
with
\Re({s_{d}})>(q-d)/q
.
Then

\Re\left(\frac{qs_{d}}{q-d}\right)>1

and so the Riemann zeta function

\zeta\left(\frac{q{s_{d}}}{q-d}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{q{s_{d}}/(q-d)}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{j^{s_{d}}}\right)^{q/(q-d)}

converges absolutely. Consider the sequence

\mathbf{a}_{d}=\left(a_{d,j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}=\left(\frac{1}{j^{s_{d}}}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q/(q-d)}

for all
d\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
, where

a_{d,j}=\frac{1}{j^{s_{d}}}

and the polynomial

(\mathbf{a}_{d},\mathbf{x}^{d})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{d,j}x_{j}^{d}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{j}^{d}}{j^{s_{d}}}.

Let
k\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
. For all

\Delta=(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\qqand J=(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}

we define the multiplicative coefficient

\displaystyle a_{\Delta,J}

\displaystyle=a_{(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k}),(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})}=a_{d_{1},j_{1}}a_{d_{2},j_{2}}\cdots a_{d_{k},j_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{j_{1}^{s_{d_{1}}}\cdots j_{k}^{s_{d_{k}}}}

.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{J\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}a_{\Delta,J}\ x_{J}^{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{1}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{1}}\right)\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{2}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(\mathbf{a}_{d_{k}},\mathbf{x}^{d_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}\frac{x_{j_{1}}^{d_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{k}}^{d_{k}}}{j_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots j_{k}^{s_{k}}}.

We obtain the
q
-multiplicative polynomial of degree at most
D

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=P(\mathbf{x},s_{1},\ldots,s_{D})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\sum_{J\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}a_{\Delta,J}\ x_{J}^{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\sum_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})\in\mathbf{N}^{k}}\frac{x_{j_{1}}^{d_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{k}}^{d_{k}}}{j_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots j_{k}^{s_{k}}}.

This series converges absolutely for all
\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
.

For
D=1
and
q=2
and for complex numbers
s
with
\Re(s)>1/2
,
the multiplicative polynomial is the Dirichlet series

L(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n^{s}}.

This series converges absolutely for all
\mathbf{x}=(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{2}
.
The corresponding linear equation in infinitely many variables is simply

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n^{s}}=b.

Applying Theorem
2
,
we obtain the following result, which asserts the existence of a Dirichlet series
taking prescribed values at a given infinite set of points in the half-plane
\Re(s)>1/2
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
I
be an infinite set and let
(b_{i})_{i\in I}
and
(s_{i})_{i\in I}

be sequences of complex numbers with
\Re(s_{i})>1/2
for all
i\in I
.
Consider the linear equation in infinitely many variables

L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{n^{s_{i}}}=b_{i}.

Let
M>0
.
If, for every finite subset
S
of I and every

6.
Open problems

.
The results in this paper suggest several questions.

(1)

Are Theorems
3
,
4
, and
5
true for
infinite sets of polynomial equations in which the
polynomials are not multiplicative?

(2)

Are there classes
\mathcal{C}
of infinite sets of polynomial equations in infinitely many variables
for which there is an integer
S=S(\mathcal{C})
such that, if every finite set of at most
S
equations
has a solution or an approximate solution, then the infinite set of equations has a solution?

(3)

Let
E
be a subfield of the complex numbers.
Let
\{P_{i}(\mathbf{x})=b_{i}:i\in I\}
be an infinite set of polynomial equations
in which every finite subset of the equations has an exact solution
or an approximate solution in
E
.

(a)

For what subfields
E
of the complex numbers is it true that
the infinite set of equations have an exact solution in
E
?

(b)

For what sets of polynomial equations might this be true?

(4)

The “finitely many implies infinitely many” paradigm applies to linear equations
and multiplicative polynomial equations with bounded or sequentially bounded norms
(Theorems
3
,
4
,
and
5
). For equations of this special type,
countably many implies finitely many. But there may be other classes of equations
for which this paradigm does not hold, but a stronger condition (“countably many
implies uncountably many”) is true.
The problem is to determine if such “new” classes of equations exist.

(5)

Is there an uncountably infinite set of equations such that every countably infinite subset
of the equations has an exact or an approximate solution, but the
uncountably infinite set of equations has no exact solution?

Appendix A
The theorems of F. Riesz and Abian-Eslami

Theorem 7
.

Let
(p,q)
be a conjugate pair and let
M>0
.
Let
\mathbf{a}_{i}=(a_{i,j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}
for all
i\in\mathbf{N}

and let
(b_{i})_{i\in\mathbf{N}}
be a sequence of numbers.
The following are equivalent.

(a)

For all
r\in\mathbf{N}
,
there exists
\mathbf{x}_{r}=(x_{r,j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
such that

\|\mathbf{x}_{r}\|_{q}\leq M\qqand\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{r,j}=b_{i}\qquad\text{for all $i\in\{1,2,3,\ldots,r\}$.}

(b)

For all
r\in\mathbf{N}
and
h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}\in\mathbf{R}
,

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}b_{i}\right|\leq M\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

(c)

There exists
\mathbf{x}=(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
such that

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{q}\leq M\qqand\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{j}=b_{i}\qquad\text{for all $i\in\mathbf{N}$.}

F. Riesz
[
5
]
proved the equivalence of (b) and (c)
and Abian-Eslami
[
1
]
proved (a) implies (c).

Proof.

First we prove that (a) implies (b).
For all
h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}(a_{i,j})_{j=1}^{\infty}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{p}

and

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\right\|_{p}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

We have
\mathbf{x}_{r}=(x_{r,j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{q}
and so,
by Hölder’s inequality,

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\right)\mathbf{x}_{r}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}x_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{1}

and

\left\|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\right)\mathbf{x}_{r}\right\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}x_{j}\right|<\infty.

For every positive integer
N
, let

g(N)=\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}x_{j}\right|.

We have

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}g(N)=0

and

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}b_{i}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{r,j}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{i,j}x_{r,j}\right|+\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{r,j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}x_{r,j}\right|+\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}x_{r,j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}x_{r,j}\right|+g(N)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}x_{r,j}\right|+g(N)

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\right)\mathbf{x}_{r}\right\|_{1}+g(N)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\right\|_{p}\|\mathbf{x}_{r}\|_{q}+g(N)

\displaystyle\leq M\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}+g(N).

This inequality is valid for all
N
and
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}g(N)=0
.
Therefore,

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}b_{i}\right|\leq M\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}a_{i,j}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

Thus, (a) implies (b).

Next we prove that (b) implies (c).
Let
W
be the vector subspace of
\ell^{p}
spanned by the set
\{\mathbf{a}_{i}:i\in\mathbf{N}\}
.
Let
h_{1},\ldots,h_{r},h^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,h^{\prime}_{r}
be scalars such that

\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h^{\prime}_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}.

Then

\sum_{i=1}^{r}(h_{i}-h^{\prime}_{i})\mathbf{a}_{i}=\mathbf{0}

and

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}b_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}h^{\prime}_{i}b_{i}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}(h_{i}-h^{\prime}_{i})b_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq M\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}(h_{i}-h^{\prime}_{i})a_{i,j}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=M\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{r}(h_{i}-h^{\prime}_{i})\mathbf{a}_{i}\right\|_{p}=0.

It follows that there is a well-defined linear functional
f
on
W
such that

f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}b_{i}.

In particular,
f_{d}(\mathbf{a}_{i})=b_{i}
. Because

\left|f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\right)\right|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}b_{i}\right|\leq M\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\right\|_{p}

for all
\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\mathbf{a}_{i}\in W
, the linear functional
f
has norm
\|f\|\leq M
.
By the Hahn-Banach theorem, there is a bounded linear functional
F
on
\ell^{p}

such that
F(\mathbf{w})=f_{d}(\mathbf{w})
for all
\mathbf{w}\in W
and
\|F\|\leq M
.

For every bounded linear functional
F
on
\ell^{p}
there is a sequence
\mathbf{x}\in\ell^{q}

such that
F(\mathbf{a})=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{a}\in\ell^{p}
.
For all
i\in\mathbf{N}
we have

b_{i}=f_{d}(\mathbf{a}_{i})=F(\mathbf{a}_{i})=(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{i,j}x_{j}.

Thus, (b) implies (c).

The proof that (c) implies (a) is immediate.
∎
