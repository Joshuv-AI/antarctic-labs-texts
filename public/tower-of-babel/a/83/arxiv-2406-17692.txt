From Distributional to Overton Pluralism: Investigating Large Language Model Alignment
Thom Lake, Eunsol Choi, Greg Durrett

Abstract
The alignment process changes several properties of a large language model’s (LLM’s) output distribution. We analyze two aspects of post-alignment distributional shift of LLM responses. First, we re-examine previously reported reductions in response diversity post-alignment. Our analysis suggests that an apparent drop in the diversity of responses is largely explained by quality control and information aggregation. Alignment suppresses irrelevant and unhelpful content while shifting the output distribution toward longer responses that cover information spanning several responses from the base LLM, essentially presenting diverse information in a single response. Finding little evidence that alignment suppresses useful information, it is natural to ask the opposite question: do aligned models surface information that cannot be recovered from base models? Our second investigation shows this is not the case and the behavior of aligned models is recoverable from base models without fine-tuning. A combination of in-context examples and lower-resolution semantic hints about response content can elicit responses from base LLMs that are as similar to alignment-tuned LLM responses as alignment-tuned LLM responses are to each other. Taken together, these results indicate that current alignment techniques capture but do not extend the useful subset of assistant-like base LLM behavior, providing further evidence for the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis. They also show that in-context alignment can go surprisingly far as a strategy for imitating aligned LLMs without fine-tuning. Our code and data is available at this url .

Introduction

After massive scale pre-training over trillions of tokens to learn the next token distribution, large language models (LLMs) go through an alignment step such as
SFT
(
SFT
)
(
Sanh et al., 2022
)
,
RLHF
(
RLHF
)
(
Stiennon et al., 2020
;
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
, or
DPO
(
DPO
)
(
Rafailov et al., 2024
)
which changes the conditional distribution defined by the LLM to favor responses that humans prefer. How does alignment change an LLM’s distribution, beyond the intended consequence of improving the usefulness of outputs? Prior work has observed that it also causes significant reductions in response diversity
(
Khalifa et al., 2020
;
Casper et al., 2023
;
Go et al., 2023
;
Kirk et al., 2024
)
.
Reductions in diversity are concerning, given the broad demand for more personalized models to accommodate diverse human preferences
(
Jang et al., 2023
;
Cheng et al., 2023
)
.

An orthogonal line of work has argued that fine-tuning is largely superficial. The Superficial Alignment Hypothesis conjectures that observed capabilities of aligned LLMs are learned primarily during pre-training and current techniques merely amplify an existing subdistribution corresponding to assistant-like responses
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
.
Combined with observations about diversity, superficial alignment suggests a certain picture of model behavior: there are many types of responses from base models, and fine-tuning boosts the likelihood of returning certain responses without changing their content.

Figure 1:
Comparing outputs from an unaligned (left) and aligned (right) language model pair. A single response from the aligned model contains useful information only surfaced by the unaligned model with repeated sampling while omitting unhelpful content.

This paper provides empirical support for this characterization of current alignment techniques. We demonstrate that the output distribution of an alignment-tuned model corresponds to the useful output
subdistribution
of the corresponding base model in task-oriented settings.
Our argument is divided into two sections which we describe below.

First, we ask whether a loss of diversity after alignment actually corresponds to a loss in diversity of the information content of those responses.
Contrary to prior results
(
Bai et al., 2022
;
Kirk et al., 2024
)
, our experiments suggest the answer is no.
Figure
1
illustrates our findings.
Samples from aligned models are often homogeneous and resemble the one shown here, whereas samples from base models (five shown) exhibit a broad range of semantics.
However, some responses such as the JSON blob in gray (the third response) are not useful, and aligned models failing to cover these is acceptable. The increase in length from the response of the aligned model means that it actually covers many of the useful, distinct responses from the base language model
simultaneously
.
We interpret this finding as a shift from distributional to Overton pluralism in the context of a recently proposed framework
(
Sorensen et al., 2024
)
and characterize this behavior on two datasets of questions with open-ended answers
(
Zhou et al., 2024
;
Wan et al., 2024
)
that arise due to points of scientific debate or differences in opinion.

If the aligned response is a filtered, aggregated view of base model responses, can this behavior be recovered from a base model without fine-tuning?
Prior work has shown in-context learning (ICL) can elicit responses from a base model that are comparable to responses in aligned models on dimensions like helpfulness and factuality
(
Lin et al., 2024
)
. We extend this study, improving in-context example selection and providing lexical hints that nudge models to produce content similar to aligned models.
With our prompt guiding a base model to behave like an aligned model, the base model can produce samples strikingly similar to those from that aligned model, on both Llama 2 and Mistral.
This suggests that the distribution of the base model
can
cover the responses from the aligned model even in the absence of parameter updates.

Together, our study provides an in-depth analysis on how alignment changes responses from LLMs, in terms of response diversity and prompting base models to produce responses that resemble responses from their aligned counterparts. Our success in imitating aligned behavior without fine-tuning suggests possibilities for rapid prototyping and personalization of LLMs.

Background

2.1
Goals of alignment

Alignment
is the general process of shifting the conditional distribution defined by a pre-trained LLM away from the base model’s distribution and toward useful task-oriented responses that align with user expectations and preferences.
Helpfulness, factuality, and safety
(
Zou et al., 2023
;
Andriushchenko et al., 2024
)
are common objectives for the alignment process. Prior work
(
Amodei et al., 2016
;
Bai et al., 2022
)
measures whether such desired properties are achieved through alignment, which differs from our main goal of studying the distribution shifts over the semantics of answers for open-ended questions.

Diversity of responses is typically
not
an explicit goal of alignment. After alignment, model responses should be informative and cover different alternatives, and aligned models should be able to handle prompts corresponding to a range of possible situations. The ability to get diverse answers from an aligned model for the same prompt is present in APIs (e.g., the ability to change decoding parameters like sampling temperature, or the presence of “creative” mode in tools like Bing Chat), but rarely an explicitly evaluated goal.

Alignment methods typically involve parameter updates, such as supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or reinforcement learning on human preferences (RLHF).
In this work we will experiment with
in-context alignment

(
Lin et al., 2024
)
, specifically the URIAL method, which crafts a few-shot prompt enabling a base LLM to generate responses resembling those from their aligned counterparts. The ability to do this without updating parameters supports the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis.

2.2

To measure the semantic diversity of LLMs’ outputs before and after alignment, we chose two datasets containing open-ended questions where a range of valid answers from multiple perspectives exists. We aim to measure if alignment-tuning significantly alters the information surfaced in responses by reducing diversity or suppressing certain viewpoints.

Evaluation criteria

We measure
response quality
along five dimensions using GPT-4
(gpt-4-1106-preview)
and the Just-Eval prompt from URIAL, which was previously found to yield high agreement rates between human preferences in closely related settings
Lin et al. (2024)
. Each response is rated on each aspect from a 1 to 5 scale, corresponding to strong disagreement to strong agreement about whether the response exhibits that aspect. While LLM evaluators have weaknesses and various biases, documented biases
Stureborg et al. (2024)
such as anchoring bias and failure to use the entire score range don’t bias our results, as we are comparing relative rankings of similar systems with a fixed protocol. We additionally performed a small-scale qualitative review of LLM judgments and found we generally agreed with the relative ordering of responses.

We measure
response similarity
between multiple samples using lexical measures
(
Li et al., 2016
;
Zhu et al., 2018
;
Liu et al., 2022
)
which correlate with human judgments of diversity
(
Tevet and Berant, 2020
;
Shaib et al., 2024
)
.
Given a set of sampled responses to a prompt, we measure the per-input similarity of responses by averaging Jaccard similarity over all pairs after converting each response into a set of unique word stems. We refer to this as
Self-Sim
and display the average over for N = 5 samples for each input.

3
Decreased diversity post-alignment

We now investigate how the diversity of output changes during the alignment process. Reducing diversity of responses can be part of improving response quality: for instance, when prompted to solve a math problem, the ideal language model returns the single correct answer and not a “diverse” range of incorrect answers. However, this is not as clearly true on the datasets we investigate.

To ground our discussion, we appeal to a recently proposed framework for the closely related concept of pluralism, which defines several distinct ways for LLMs to be pluralistic, or “capable of representing a diverse set of human values and perspectives”
(
Sorensen et al., 2024
)
.
Their notion of
distributional pluralism
, or the ability of LLM to surface diverse information across samples, can be reflected by the lexical diversity metric we introduced (
Self-Sim
).
On the other hand,
Overton pluralism
, which requires surfacing diverse information within a single response, is not captured this way, but may be captured by length, as capturing diverse information can lead to longer answers.
We observe that aligned model outputs tend to be longer, leading us to question if the alignment process might lead LLMs to exhibit different types of diversity (from pluralistic to Overton).

In this section, we compare the outputs from the base and aligned models to measure how answer semantics change after the alignment process, specifically by looking at information “missing” in the response post-alignment.
Our main hypothesis is that the only information missing from the response is information that is not useful (i.e., low-quality).

3.1
Helpfulness and missingness

The relationship between lexical coverage, semantic coverage, response length, and helpfulness (x-axis) in LIMA-OE.
Cover-LEX and Cover-SEM are computed with respect to Llama 2 Chat, with smaller values corresponding to more content missing from the aligned model response that the base model surfaces. When there is less overlap between the base and aligned model, base response responses are of lower quality. Helpful responses from the base model tend to cover the same content as the reference under both coverage metrics.

Setting

We aim to evaluate the helpfulness of information present in the base model responses,
\mathbf{y}
, that is missing in the aligned model responses,
\mathbf{y}_{r}
.
At a high level, we measure coverage between two responses.
If coverage is
high
, then no information is missing.
We measure this with two metrics:

•

Cover-LEX
computes the fraction of words shared between responses (Jaccard similarity):
|\mathbf{y}\cap\mathbf{y}_{r}|/|\mathbf{y}\cup\mathbf{y}_{r}|
. This metric is identical to the Jaccard similarity from Section
2.3
, but computed between responses from different models on the same input.

•

Cover-SEM
: We measure semantic coverage by prompting GPT-4 to assess if
\mathbf{y_{r}}
covers all information in
\mathbf{y}
. Each output pair is rated on a scale of 1 to 5 where 1 means “
\mathbf{y}
contains completely new information” and a 5 means “
\mathbf{y}
is redundant given
\mathbf{y_{r}}
.”

For both metrics, lower numbers signify there is content in the base model response that is not present in the aligned model response.

We also evaluate the helpfulness of the base response. We use Prompt
C.2
to assign a score on a scale from 1 to 5.

We use Llama 2 Chat as the reference model and Llama 2 Base and Llama 2 ICL as base model responses. As we aim to find useful information missing from reference model responses, we draw five samples from the base model for each reference model response.

Results

Figure
2
presents results showing, for each response pair, the relationship between coverage and helpfulness.
When coverage metrics are low (i.e., the base response contains new information), the quality of the base response also tends to be low.
For instance, in Figure
2
(Cover-SEM), the bottom-left purple cell represents points that are not covered semantically, but which are also not deemed to be helpful.
ICL responses are generally higher quality than base responses, but this also manifests as a comparatively higher amount of coverage.
In conclusion, we find ICL alignment induces increased distributional similarities between models, and the aligned model does not lose much high-quality information.

4
Mimicking aligned models

Our previous analysis showed that alignment emphasizes useful knowledge from base models and unifies more content in a single response. We now ask whether the alignment process is introducing a substantial change over the base model. We argue that if a specific capacity can be consistently evoked using a natural language prompt, it is reasonable to say that the capacity exists in the model.
To investigate this, we explore more sophisticated prompting strategies to see if a base LLM aligned with ICL can match the behavior of alignment-tuned LLMs.
As our goal is to mimic an existing model, we frame our discussion using terminology from the knowledge distillation literature
(
Hinton et al., 2015
)
and refer to the aligned model we wish to imitate as the
teacher
and the prompted base model as the
student
.

The top row depicts Self-Sim (max) for teacher model responses, which are fairly self-similar. Including additional context in the form of teacher responses and in-domain questions increases similarity to the teacher model, finally reaching a point with substantial distributional overlap.

4.1
In-context distillation strategies

Let
f
and
g
be a teacher and student LLM respectively. Given a set of queries on which models will be evaluated,
X=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}

\phi
such that
f(\mathbf{x}_{i})\approx g(\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i}))
. To construct
f

X
(our prompt dataset) as well as a separate input corpus
U=\{\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{m}\}
, a fixed set of example queries that can be used in prompts as well, and the ability to compute outputs of
f
on both
X
and
U
. Examples of our prompts are provided in Appendix
C
.

Static Prompts

These prompts are static, applied globally for all input
\mathbf{x}_{i}

URIAL: Human

\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\newline
[\mathbf{u}_{a};\mathbf{y}_{a};\mathbf{u}_{b};\mathbf{y}_{b};\mathbf{u}_{b};\mathbf{y}_{c};\mathbf{x}_{i}]

Each query
\mathbf{u}_{a},\mathbf{u}_{b},\mathbf{u}_{c}
is manually selected and paired with a human written response
\mathbf{y}_{a},\mathbf{y}_{b},\mathbf{y}_{c}
respectively.

URIAL: Teacher

\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\newline
[\mathbf{u}_{a};f(\mathbf{u}_{a});\mathbf{u}_{b};f(\mathbf{u}_{b});\mathbf{u}_{c};f(\mathbf{u}_{c});\mathbf{x}_{i}]

This is equivalent to URIAL, except that we use responses from the teacher model instead of human-written responses.

Random: Teacher

\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\newline
[\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{1};f(\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{1});\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{2};f(\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{2});\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{3};f(\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{3});\mathbf{x}_{i}]

Each query
\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{j}\neq\mathbf{x}_{i}
is randomly selected from
X
and paired with teacher model responses.

4.2
Evaluation criteria

Our goal is to measure whether a prompted base LLM can mimic an aligned LLM.
In general, we expect even a prompted base LLM to exhibit a wider range of behaviors than an aligned LLM, but our question is whether
some
samples of that base LLM will be close to the aligned model’s behavior.
We therefore compute the maximum similarity between multiple samples from the base model and an aligned output
\mathbf{y}^{*}
.

As we will not reproduce
\mathbf{y}^{*}
exactly, we characterize similarity values based on the
self-
similarity of the aligned model with itself, as defined in Section
2.3
. Our rationale is that multiple aligned responses are likely to be only mildly different in terms of content, so if we can match this self-similarity with our best base model samples, we have evidence in favor of our hypothesis that the base model can get “close enough” to the aligned model’s distribution.

We rely on lexical measures of response similarity.
Let
\mathbf{x}\in X
be a query,
\mathbf{y}\sim f(\mathbf{x})
a teacher response, and
Y=\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{N}\}
,
\mathbf{y}_{i}\sim g(\phi(\mathbf{x}))
, a set of student responses.
We measure the Jaccard similarity between word stems for each
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in Y
for
N=5
student responses and a single teacher response
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}
.
Results are aggregated by taking the max or mean over
N
samples, then averaging similarities over each query
\mathbf{x}\in X
. We refer to this metric as Max-Sim.

4.3
Results

Results are presented in Figure
4

Dynamic prompting shows further increase in maximum similarity to teacher responses. The most similarity between student and teacher models is achieved when students are provided semantic hints about the content of teacher responses in the form of summaries (kNN Prompts and Summary Input). This is nearly equivalent to the average Self-Sim metric of the teacher, Llama 2 Chat, on LIMA-OE (0.33 vs 0.34) and only slightly smaller on
ConflictingQA
(0.31 vs 0.36). Higher lexical similarity to aligned model responses also leads to improvement in quality metrics presented in Section

. We include these results in the Appendix in Table

Related Work

Post-alignment diversity reductions

Several previous works
Florian et al. (2024)
have studied various types of post-alignment diversity reductions, which we highlight here.
From the perspective of content diversity, prior work
Liang et al. (2023)
show aligned LLMs are poorly calibrated in comparison to base LLMs. It has been shown that
SFT
reduces diversity less than
RLHF

(
Kirk et al., 2024
)
.
Prior work
Lahoti et al. (2023)
show models generate biased lists of people and cities post-alignment, but also find prompting can reduce this bias.

Another line of work studies reduced diversity in answers to subjective questions related to identify
(
Santurkar et al., 2023
;
Jiang et al., 2024
;
Sorensen et al., 2024
)
.
This is a different notion of diversity that we do not explore here and it is unclear how such findings would impact the task-oriented settings we study
(
Berglund et al., 2023
)
.
Prior work
Hayati et al. (2023)
show LLMs can generate diverse opinions when prompted to do so and note that
“semantic diversity is not always positively correlated with perspective diversity”
, in line with the conclusions we draw on this topic.

Superficial alignment

Our work is influenced by the conjecture that alignment is largely superficial
(
Zhou et al., 2024
)
.
Empirical evidence for the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis includes the ability to achieve comparable performance to more complex and data-intensive fine-tuning procedures using a small amount of instruction tuning data
(
Zhou et al., 2024
;
Zhao et al., 2024
)
, in-context learning
(
Han, 2023
;
Lin et al., 2024
)
, or proxy-tuning
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
.
In contrast to this prior work, which primarily focuses on showing base LLMs can match the performance of alignment-tuned LLMs according to quantitative measures of response quality, our work provides a detailed analysis of how alignment alters LLM behavior independently of any specific alignment technique.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated the relationship between LLMs before and after alignment.
We revisited previous findings related to post-alignment diversity reduction in the context of open-ended QA and showed that a loss in lexical diversity (distributional pluralism) is compensated for by longer and more comprehensive responses (Overton pluralism). We then investigated to what extent this behavior is already exhibited in base models, showing that with appropriate prompting, base model outputs can be distributionally very similar to outputs from aligned models. We believe these findings shed light on the nature of the alignment process, and they open doors for practitioners to use in-context alignment to explore personalization and rapid prototyping of alignment techniques.

7
Limitations

Our study focuses on two English-language question answering datasets, which do not capture the full range of relevant LLM applications. However, we note that many frequently-used evaluation settings like reasoning datasets (MMLU) do not involve the concepts we investigate like subjectivity or perspectives. In addition, we only evaluate autoregressive Transformer language models with up to 7B parameters; different behavior may be observed at the largest scales, or with larger-scale alignment data. Finally, the analyses we conduct use lexical overlap and prompting GPT to assess similarity. These are imperfect, but we could not find better intermediate semantic representations that could reliably be extracted. Having this could make our findings more precise.

Societal Impact

Our findings indicate that responses from aligned LLMs often cover a range of responses from base models. On the datasets we examined, we did not find evidence of particular viewpoints being systematically suppressed in alignment. However, we stress that our results should not be taken as evidence that LLMs will appropriately handle diverse viewpoints in a wider range of high-stakes settings. For one, our analysis does not cover information missing from base models themselves, which is a crucial source of underrepresentation of cross-cultural perspectives. Our results are meant to provide analytical tools to inform the responsible development of language models, but are not yet suitable for deployment.
