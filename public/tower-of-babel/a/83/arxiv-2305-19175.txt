Witnessing environment dimension through temporal correlations
Lucas B. Vieira, Simon Milz, Giuseppe Vitagliano, Costantino Budroni

Abstract
We introduce a framework to compute upper bounds for temporal correlations achievable in open quantum system dynamics, obtained by repeated measurements on the system. As these correlations arise by virtue of the environment acting as a memory resource, such bounds are witnesses for the minimal dimension of an effective environment compatible with the observed statistics. These witnesses are derived from a hierarchy of semidefinite programs with guaranteed asymptotic convergence. We compute non-trivial bounds for various sequences involving a qubit system and a qubit environment, and compare the results to the best known quantum strategies producing the same outcome sequences. Our results provide a numerically tractable method to determine bounds on multi-time probability distributions in open quantum system dynamics and allow for the witnessing of effective environment dimensions through probing of the system alone.



1
Introduction

Physical systems are never truly isolated, and inevitably interact with their surrounding environment
[
Breuer and Petruccione(2002)
,
Rivas and Huelga(2011)
]
. As a consequence, information within the system leaks away into its surroundings, leading to entanglement between the system and the environment. In many instances, this leaked information may be partially recovered at a later time, leading to non-Markovian dynamics, i.e., non-negligible memory effects and complex correlations in time
[
Rivas et al.(2014)Rivas, Huelga, and Plenio
,
Breuer et al.(2016)Breuer, Laine, Piilo, and Vacchini
,
Milz and Modi(2021)
]
.

Like their spatial counterpart, temporal correlations in quantum mechanics fundamentally differ from those that can be observed in the classical case. Such differences between classical and quantum temporal correlations have been noted since the works of Leggett and Garg
[
Leggett and Garg(1985)
,
Leggett(2008)
,
Emary et al.(2013)Emary, Lambert, and Nori
,
Vitagliano and Budroni(2023)
]
. In the presence of memory, a clear distinction between an underlying process – carrying temporal correlations – and the measurement process – probing said correlations – can be obtained by employing correlation kernels
[
Lindblad(1979)
,
Accardi et al.(1982)Accardi, Frigerio, and
Lewis
]
or higher order quantum maps
[
Pollock et al.(2018)Pollock, Rodríguez-Rosario, Frauenheim,
Paternostro, and Modi
]
for their description.

Broadly speaking, the dimension of a physical system, i.e., the number of perfectly distinguishable states, imposes fundamental constraints over the temporal correlations it is able to produce
[
Budroni et al.(2019)Budroni, Fagundes, and
Kleinmann
]
. In this sense, the physical dimension acts as a memory resource, restricting the amount of information stored about the past that is capable of affecting the future. This memory constraint leads to different behaviors (i.e., different achievable temporal correlations) in classical, quantum, or more general physical theories
[
Fritz(2010)
,
Hoffmann et al.(2018)Hoffmann, Spee, Gühne, and
Budroni
,
Budroni et al.(2019)Budroni, Fagundes, and
Kleinmann
,
Spee et al.(2020)Spee, Budroni, and Gühne
,
Vieira and Budroni(2022)
,
Mao et al.(2022)Mao, Spee, Xu, and Gühne
]
. In particular quantum memories are known to allow for a larger set of correlations than classical ones of the same size
[
Budroni et al.(2019)Budroni, Fagundes, and
Kleinmann
,
Budroni et al.(2021)Budroni, Vitagliano, and
Woods
,
Vieira and Budroni(2022)
]
. However, this advantage only holds for restricted memory size, i.e., if the memory size is
unrestricted
, all correlations compatible with a time-ordered causal structure may be achieved with either classical or quantum memories
[
Fritz(2010)
,
Hoffmann et al.(2018)Hoffmann, Spee, Gühne, and
Budroni
]
. Therefore, understanding the nature of dimensional constraints on observable correlations sheds light on fundamental differences between our descriptions of physical systems, as well as their connection with causality.

These limitations imposed by the dimensionality of physical systems have been exploited for the construction of “dimension witnesses”, inequalities which, when violated, certify the minimum dimension of the system compatible with made observations
[
Gallego et al.(2010)Gallego, Brunner, Hadley, and
Acín
,
Brunner et al.(2013)Brunner, Navascués, and
Vértesi
,
Gühne et al.(2014)Gühne, Budroni, Cabello, Kleinmann, and
Larsson
,
Budroni and Emary(2014)
,
Schild and Emary(2015)
,
Spee et al.(2020)Spee, Budroni, and Gühne
,
Sohbi et al.(2021)Sohbi, Markham, Kim, and Quintino
]
. In a similar spirit, our approach allows for the computation of upper bounds on the temporal correlations achievable in an open system dynamics with an environment of bounded dimension, so that any violations of these bounds certify the minimum dimension of the effective environment. Employing techniques from entanglement detection
[
Doherty et al.(2004)Doherty, Parrilo, and
Spedalieri
,
Navascués et al.(2009)Navascués, Owari, and Plenio
]
, these bounds are obtained by exploiting the inherent symmetries of the problem. In particular, we relax the
a priori
non-linear problem of computing maximum joint probabilities in sequential measurements to a numerically tractable hierarchy of semidefinite programs (SDPs)
[
Boyd and Vandenberghe(2004)
]
. Both the computational accessibility of the final formulation of the problem, as well as the non-triviality of the resulting bounds, are then demonstrated for paradigmatic examples, showing that, indeed, joint probability distributions obtained from probing an open system alone provide viable means to deduce dimensional properties of the a priori experimentally inaccessible environment it is coupled to.

The paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we introduce the temporal sequence protocol, the notion of temporal correlations, and their mathematical description.
Section

3
discusses the application of temporal correlations to the task of characterizing open systems and their environment.
Section

4
describes the semidefinite program we constructed to bound temporal correlations, with
Section

5
presenting the numerical results we obtained in their optimization. In
Section

6
we comment on the various challenges involved in realizing these numerical optimizations, and how we have overcome them. In
Section

7
we comment on several additional technical details involving our work, with
Section

8
covering our conclusions and future outlook.

2
The measurement protocol

Figure 1:
Diagram of the sequential measurement protocol, involving a partially characterized probe system and a non-characterized environment, here separated by the dashed line. The sequence of measurement outcomes
\bm{a}=a_{1}\dots a_{L}
is obtained by repeated preparations of a probe state
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
, fixed and the same at every time step, which is left to interact with the environment, then followed by measurements (semicircles). The environment acts as a
memory
resource, capable of establishing long-term correlations between measurements.

We assume throughout that an experimenter is able to prepare the system in a known state and perform measurements in a certain basis. Its dynamics, on the other hand, are not under experimental control, and are governed by its inevitable interaction with the environment. Typically, this environment is a much larger system, generally inaccessible, and featuring complex dynamics. Together, system and environment undergo closed, i.e., unitary evolution. Their interaction leads to an imprinting of information on the probe system, which can be used to learn something about the environment by means of the probe alone. In fact, this is a common way of making indirect measurement on (typically large many-body) systems that are not fully controlled
[
Braginsky and Khalili(1992)
,
Busch et al.(1996)Busch, Lahti, and Mittelstaedt
]
. For example, a small probe can be used for estimating an unknown parameter of a larger (many-body) environment
[
Degen et al.(2017)Degen, Reinhard, and
Cappellaro
]
, in particular temperature
[
Alipour et al.(2014)Alipour, Mehboudi, and
Rezakhani
,
Sabín et al.(2014)Sabín, White, Hackermuller, and
Fuentes
,
Correa et al.(2015)Correa, Mehboudi, Adesso, and
Sanpera
,
Mehboudi et al.(2019)Mehboudi, Sanpera, and Correa
]
.

We now introduce the details of our scenario and the notation used. By
\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\text{E}}
we denote the finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces of system and environment, with
d_{\text{S}}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}
and
d_{\text{E}}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{\text{E}}
, and their joint space as
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ES}}=\mathcal{H}_{\text{E}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}
, with
d_{\text{ES}}=d_{\text{E}}d_{\text{S}}
.
The experiment involves
L
identical measurements on the probe system, at discrete time steps, each outcome
a_{\ell}\in\mathcal{A}
collected into a sequence
\bm{a}=a_{1}\dots a_{L}
. For simplicity, we consider
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
in the following; the generalization to arbitrary outcomes is straightforward. Measurements on the system are described by a Positive Operator-Valued Measure (POVM)
(E_{S}^{a})_{a}
, i.e.,
E_{S}^{a}\geq 0
and
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}E_{S}^{a}=\mathbbm{1}_{S}
.

The system is initially in the state
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
, interacts with the environment via the global unitary
\mathcal{U}(\cdot)=U\cdot U^{\dagger}
, then it is measured and reprepared in the state
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
. This procedure is repeated a total of
L
times; see
Fig.

1
. The unitary operation is assumed to be the same at each iteration (corresponding to, for example the situation of a time-independent system-environment Hamiltonian and temporally equidistant measurements); see
Section

7.1
for more details.

In the following, we consider a measure-and-prepare operation of the form

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{a}(\rho_{ES})=\operatorname{tr}_{S}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\rho_{ES}\cdot(\mathbbm{1}_{E}\otimes E^{a}_{S})\right]\otimes\rho^{0}_{\text{S}},\end{split}

(1)

with the generalization to arbitrary operations being straightforward. The probability of a sequence of outcomes can then be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}})&=p(a_{1},\dots,a_{L}|d_{\text{E}})\\
&=\operatorname{tr}\left[\mathcal{M}_{a_{L}}\!\!\circ\mathcal{U}\!\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{M}_{a_{1}}\!\!\circ\mathcal{U}(\rho^{0}_{\text{E}}\!\otimes\!\rho^{0}_{\text{S}})\right],\end{split}

(2)

where
p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}})
denotes that it is obtained with an environment of dimension
d_{\text{E}}
. Our task is to establish upper bounds on such probabilities given a finite amount/dimension of “memory”
d_{\text{E}}
. The assumption of identical measurements is essential for capturing this notion of memory, as arbitrary time-dependent operations would impose no non-trivial restriction on the probabilities.

For simplicity, we consider the maximization of the probability of a given sequence
\bm{a}
, namely, to find
\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}
such that
p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}})\leq\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}
, for all possible correlations generated by an environment of dimension
d_{\text{E}}
. The generalization to arbitrary linear functions of the distributions
(p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}}))_{\bm{a}}
is straightforward.

Figure 2:
The protocol with each time step having its own set of input and output Hilbert spaces, denoted by the vertical dotted lines. Note that the input and output spaces of the
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{M}_{a}
are interleaved:
\mathcal{U}
has inputs
\text{I}_{\ell}
and outputs
\text{O}_{\ell}
, but for
\mathcal{M}_{a_{\ell}}
, inputs are
\text{O}_{\ell}
and outputs
\text{I}_{\ell+1}
.

We formulate the problem via the Choi-Jamiołkowski (CJ) isomorphism
[
Jamiołkowski(1972)
,
Choi(1975)
]
, which involves taking multiple copies of the original Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ES}}
to describe its time evolution; see
Fig.

2
. The
\ell
-th unitary acts as
\mathcal{U}:\text{I}_{\ell}\mapsto\text{O}_{\ell}
, while the
\ell
-th measurement, placed between unitaries, acts instead as
\mathcal{M}_{a}:\text{O}_{\ell}\mapsto\text{I}_{\ell+1}
. All spaces are isomorphic, i.e.,
\text{O}\cong\text{I}\cong\mathcal{H}_{\text{ES}}
. For convenience, the
\ell
-th unitary’s input and output spaces are jointly referred to as
\text{A}_{\ell}=\text{I}_{\ell}\otimes\text{O}_{\ell}
. It is important to emphasize that the local input and output spaces of the
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{M}_{a}
are interleaved, in the sense that the outputs of one are the inputs of the other (
Fig.

2
). For clarity, we shall use the cursive
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{O}
to generically refer to each map’s local input and output spaces: for unitaries,
\mathcal{I}=\text{I}_{\ell}
and
\mathcal{O}=\text{O}_{\ell}
, and for measurements,
\mathcal{I}=\text{O}_{\ell}
and
\mathcal{O}=\text{I}_{\ell+1}
.

Given a linear map
\Lambda:\mathcal{I}\mapsto\mathcal{O}
between input and output Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{O}
, with
d_{\mathcal{I}}=\dim\mathcal{I}
and
d_{\mathcal{O}}=\dim\mathcal{O}
, its Choi-Jamiołkowski representation
[
Jamiołkowski(1972)
,
Choi(1975)
]
is given by the matrix

\mathcal{C}(\Lambda):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\mathcal{I}}}\left|i\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle j\right|\otimes\Lambda(\left|i\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle j\right|)=\text{id}_{\mathcal{I}}\otimes\Lambda(\left|\Omega\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\Omega\right|),

(3)

where
\left|\Omega\right\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{\mathcal{I}}-1}\left|ii\right\rangle
is a non-normalized maximally entangled state. The matrix
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)
is typically referred to as a Choi matrix for the map
\Lambda
and, if normalized, also as its Choi state. Note that
\mathcal{C}(\Lambda)
is of size
{d_{\mathcal{I}}d_{\mathcal{O}}\times d_{\mathcal{I}}d_{\mathcal{O}}}
. Since in our case input and output spaces are isomorphic, we have
d_{\mathcal{I}}=d_{\mathcal{O}}=d_{\text{ES}}
. We note that the decomposition of sequential maps in
Eq.

2
is equivalent to the standard formulation of temporal scenarios in the quantum comb
[
Chiribella et al.(2009)Chiribella, D’Ariano, and
Perinotti
]
, process matrix
[
Oreshkov et al.(2012)Oreshkov, Costa, and Brukner
,
Chiribella et al.(2013)Chiribella, D’Ariano, Perinotti, and
Valiron
]
, and process tensor
[
Pollock et al.(2018)Pollock, Rodríguez-Rosario, Frauenheim,
Paternostro, and Modi
]
formalism.

3
Witnesses for open system dynamics

Given a probe system
S
, the first question we may ask is whether or not it is open, i.e., if it is interacting with an environment at all, or equivalently, whether
d_{\text{E}}>1
.
To answer this question, we compute
\omega^{\bm{a}}_{1}:=\max_{\rho^{0}_{\text{S}},(E_{S}^{a})_{a}}\;p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}}=1)
, giving the inequality

p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}}=1)\leq\omega^{\bm{a}}_{1}.

(4)

As an example, the maximum for the sequence
\bm{a}=00101
is
\omega^{\bm{a}}_{1}=(3/5)^{3}(2/5)^{2}=0.03456
, which we explain how to obtain shortly. If we perform an experiment and observe
p(\bm{a}=00101)=0.5
, then we must conclude the system is open. In other words, the inequality
Eq.

4
acts as a witness for open systems.
However, if no violation is observed, the experiment is inconclusive.

To compute this maximum, we first note that, for our choice of
\mathcal{M}_{a}
, since both
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
and
E_{S}^{a}
are the same at each measurement, all outcomes are independent and identically distributed. Writing
q_{a}=\operatorname{tr}\left[\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}E^{a}_{S}\right]
as the probability of outcome
a
, with
\sum_{a}q_{a}=1
, and using
n_{a}
as the number of occurrences of a symbol
a
in
\bm{a}
, we have

p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}}=1)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{a_{\ell}}=\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}q^{n_{a}}_{a},\qquad\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}n_{a}=L.

(5)

The global maximum
\omega^{\bm{a}}_{1}
can be found analytically with standard techniques for optimizing
\{q_{a}\}_{a}
(see
Appendix

C
), and is given by

p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}}=1)\leq\omega^{\bm{a}}_{1}=\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\frac{n_{a}}{L}\right)^{n_{a}},

(6)

with the convention
q^{n_{a}}_{a}=1
for
q_{a}=n_{a}=0
. The maximum is independent of
d_{\text{S}}
and is obtained with
E^{a}_{S}=\tfrac{n_{a}}{L}\mathbbm{1}_{S}
and any
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
.

Similar principles apply when developing witnesses for the case of
d_{\text{E}}>1
, although the calculation of the maximum becomes nontrivial, as the probability no longer factorizes as in
Eq.

5
. Concretely, we want to obtain the tightest bounds of the form

p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}})\leq\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}},

(7)

which hold for all possible realizations of the sequence
\bm{a}
in an experiment. If a violation of
Eq.

7
is observed, we can certify that the dimension is greater than
d_{\text{E}}
.

In the simple case of
d_{\text{E}}=1
, we were able to optimize over
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
and
(E_{S}^{a})_{a}
. In the general case, however, optimizing over all possible protocols (i.e. all possible
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
,
(E_{S}^{a})_{a}
and
U
) is difficult. Nevertheless, some general considerations can be made about how the bounds depend on these parameters; see
Section

7.3
.
In the following, we assume fixed and well characterized preparations and measurements, in agreement with our initial assumptions on the probe system. The optimization we need to perform is, thus,

\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}:=\max_{U}p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}})

(8)

where assumptions about the initial state of the environment can be removed by convexity and symmetry arguments; see
Section

7.2
.
In the following section, we explain how to compute the non-trivial maxima
\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}
via convex optimization methods.

4
A hierarchy of semidefinite programs bounding temporal correlations

The maxima in
Eq.

7
require that the probe state
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
and measurements
(E_{S}^{a})_{a}
are characterized and fixed throughout the experiment, as discussed in the previous section. To obtain upper bounds for the maximum
\omega_{d_{\text{E}}}^{\bm{a}}
, we formulate the problem as a hierarchy of semidefinite programs (SDPs)
[
Boyd and Vandenberghe(2004)
]
. This class of optimization problems are ubiquitous in quantum information theory, offering strict convergence of solutions for many classes of problems, as well as having efficient algorithms widely available for solving them
[
Boyd and Vandenberghe(2004)
,
Wolkowicz et al.(2012)Wolkowicz, Saigal, and
Vandenberghe
]
. For a recent compendium on SDPs in quantum information science, see
[
Skrzypczyk and Cavalcanti(2023)
,
Tavakoli et al.(2023)Tavakoli, Pozas-Kerstjens, Brown, and
Araújo
]
.
In the following, we explain the general steps taken in formulating our problem as an SDP, which can be solved numerically and, importantly, efficiently. A more detailed step-by-step formulation is included in
Appendix

A
.

Under the CJ representation, the repeated applications of the unitary and measurements can each be written as single operators existing in a larger space, encompassing multiple time steps, with their input and output spaces interleaved. This is illustrated in
Fig.

2
. Via
Eq.

3
, we define

\begin{split}C_{U}&:=\tfrac{1}{d_{ES}}\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{U}),\\
M_{a}&:=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{M}_{a}),\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{M}_{a}:=\operatorname{tr}_{\mathcal{O}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[M_{a}\right].\end{split}

(9)

With this, we can write the
L
repeated applications of
\mathcal{U}
as
C_{U}^{\otimes{L}}
, and the measure-and-prepare operations as a single operator

X:=(d_{\text{ES}})^{L}(\rho_{\text{E}}^{0}\otimes\rho^{0}_{\text{S}})\otimes M_{a_{1}}\otimes M_{a_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\hat{M}_{a_{L}},

(10)

where
\hat{M}_{a}
is simply the final measurement without a re-preparation, obtained by partially tracing
M_{a}
over its output space. As the goal is to obtain a maximum, the initial environment state
\rho^{0}_{\text{E}}
can be chosen to be pure by convexity arguments, fixed to be
\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{E}
by unitary invariance, and considered as part of the experimental setup
X
without loss of generality; see
Section

7.2
. We can then express the probability of
\bm{a}
as

\displaystyle p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}})=\operatorname{tr}\left[X^{\text{T}}C_{U}^{\otimes L}\right].

(11)

Note that by
Eq.

3
and
Eq.

9

C_{U}
is positive, rank-1, and normalized, which makes
C_{U}^{\otimes{L}}
a pure symmetric separable quantum state. In addition, each
C_{U}
satisfies
\operatorname{tr}_{\mathcal{O}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[C_{U}\right]=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}}/d_{\text{ES}}
, which arises from the trace-preserving property of each individual unitary.
With all of these observations, we reformulate
Eq.

8
as the equivalent optimization problem:

\begin{split}\textbf{Given:}\quad&\displaystyle d_{\text{E}},\;d_{\text{S}},\;\rho^{0}_{\text{E}},\;\rho^{0}_{\text{S}},\;\{M_{a}\}_{a},\;\bm{a},\\
\text{with}\quad&X:=(d_{\text{ES}})^{L}(\rho^{0}_{\text{E}}\otimes\rho^{0}_{\text{S}})\otimes M_{a_{1}}\otimes\cdots\\
&\cdots\otimes M_{a_{L-1}}\otimes\hat{M}_{a_{L}},\\
\textbf{Find:}\quad&\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}:=\max_{C_{U}}\operatorname{tr}\left[X^{\text{T}}C_{U}^{\otimes L}\right]\\
\textbf{Subject to:}\quad&C_{U}\geq 0,\quad\operatorname{rank}C_{U}=1,\\
&\operatorname{tr}_{\mathcal{O}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[C_{U}\right]=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}}/d_{\text{ES}}.\end{split}

(12)

As
C_{U}
enters in the objective function as a tensor power, and we further require it to be rank-1, the problem is both non-linear and non-convex with respect to
C_{U}
, and thus the above problem cannot be directly solved as an SDP. To reach an SDP
relaxation
of
Eq.

12
, we first transform it into a chain of equivalent problems.

The problem can be made convex by replacing
C_{U}^{\otimes L}
with a separable state on the symmetric subspace

\widetilde{\Phi}_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}:=\sum_{i}p_{i}\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes L},

(13)

with
p_{i}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
.
By convexity, the maximum will be achieved for a rank-1
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}
, therefore this relaxation leaves the optimal value unchanged. Thus, we replace
C_{U}^{\otimes L}
in
Eq.

12
with
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}
satisfying

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\Phi}_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\in\text{SEP}_{L},\qquad&\text{P}^{+}_{L}\widetilde{\Phi}_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}=\widetilde{\Phi}_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}},\end{split}

(14)

and the partial trace constraint of
Eq.

12
, where
\text{SEP}_{L}
is the set of fully separable
L
-partite states, and
\text{P}^{+}_{L}
is the projector onto the symmetric subspace of
L
systems (see the definition in
Eq.

36
in
Section

B.1
). In fact, the constraints in
Eq.

14
are exact, as it can be shown that all separable states in the symmetric subspace are of the form in
Eq.

13
; see
Section

A.1.1
for a proof.

So far, we transformed the original problem into an equivalent one, but due to the condition
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\in\text{SEP}_{L}
it is not yet an SDP. This requirement can be obtained by relaxing the original problem via the quantum de Finetti theorem
[
Caves et al.(2002)Caves, Fuchs, and Schack
]
(see also
[
Christandl et al.(2007)Christandl, König, Mitchison, and
Renner
]
), which tells us that

\widetilde{\Phi}_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}
can be approximated as the reduced state of a larger symmetric (and potentially entangled) state
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
, such that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\approx\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}}[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}]
, where
\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}}
is the trace over systems
L+1,\dots,N
.

While this relaxation
a priori
only provides an upper bound for the original problem, it establishes a hierarchy of approximate solutions, which are known to converge exactly to the separable set as
N
increases
[
Doherty et al.(2004)Doherty, Parrilo, and
Spedalieri
]
. The de Finetti theorem, and analogous results for permutationally invariant (or exchangeable), rather than symmetric, operators have found broad applications in quantum information theory, from entanglement detection
[
Doherty et al.(2002)Doherty, Parrilo, and
Spedalieri
,
Doherty et al.(2004)Doherty, Parrilo, and
Spedalieri
,
Navascués et al.(2009)Navascués, Owari, and Plenio
]
to more general optimization problems, such as evaluating convex roofs of entanglement measures
[
Tóth et al.(2015)Tóth, Moroder, and Gühne
]
, constrained bilinear optimization
[
Berta et al.(2022)Berta, Borderi, Fawzi, and Scholz
]
, dimension-bounded quantum games
[
Jee et al.(2021)Jee, Sparaciari, Fawzi, and Berta
]
, as well as rank-constrained optimization
[
Yu et al.(2022)Yu, Simnacher, Nguyen, and
Gühne
]
.
All the above mentioned results exploit the basic idea of using either the symmetric subspace or permutation invariance to relax nonlinear constraints. In particular, Ref.
[
Berta et al.(2022)Berta, Borderi, Fawzi, and Scholz
]
analyzed the optimization over pairs of quantum channels, basing their construction on permutation invariant operators, and showing the partial trace constraint necessary to translate the usual procedure (e.g., in the entanglement detection approach) from states to channels. Moreover,
[
Yu et al.(2022)Yu, Simnacher, Nguyen, and
Gühne
]
introduced the idea of a rank-constrained optimization based on the symmetric subspace, which is central to impose the rank constraint in
Eq.

12
. Our construction is inspired by these works and could be derived starting from some of these results. However, it is more straightforward to provide a direct construction in terms of the de Finetti theorem; see
Section

A.2
.

Ultimately, we arrive at an SDP relaxation of the original problem:

\begin{split}\textbf{Given:}\quad&\displaystyle d_{\text{E}},\;d_{\text{S}},\;\rho^{0}_{\text{E}},\;\rho^{0}_{\text{S}},\;\{M_{a}\}_{a},\;\bm{a},\;N,\\
\text{with}\quad&X:=(d_{\text{ES}})^{L}(\rho^{0}_{\text{E}}\otimes\rho^{0}_{\text{S}})\otimes M_{a_{1}}\otimes\cdots\\
&\cdots\otimes M_{a_{L-1}}\otimes\hat{M}_{a_{L}},\\
\text{and}\quad&\hat{M}_{a_{L}}:=\operatorname{tr}_{\mathcal{O}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[M_{a_{L}}\right]\\
\textbf{Find:}\quad&\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}:=\max_{\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}}\operatorname{tr}\left[(X^{\text{T}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}})\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right]\\
\textbf{Subject to:}\quad&\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\geq 0,\\
&\text{P}^{+}_{N}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}=\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}},\;\;\operatorname{tr}\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right]=1,\\
&\operatorname{tr}_{\text{O}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right]=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\text{I}_{1}}}{d_{\text{ES}}}\otimes\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right].\end{split}

(15)

Here,
N\geq L
is the size of the symmetric state
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
used in the approximation of the separable
L
-partite state
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}
. The last constraint in
Eq.

15
is required to ensure that
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
represents a sequence of trace-preserving maps, and it can be enforced on a single step due to the symmetry constraint on
{\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}}
; see
Section

A.1.2
. Optionally, one can also add further linear constraints to the SDP to improve its approximation to the separable set, e.g., entanglement witnesses
[
Terhal(2000)
,
Gühne and Tóth(2009)
]
or the positive partial transpose (PPT) criterion
[
Peres(1996)
]

{\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}^{{\text{T}_{\alpha}}}\geq 0,\forall\alpha}
, where
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}^{{\text{T}_{\alpha}}}
is the partial transpose with respect to a bipartition
\alpha
. These conditions are not necessary for convergence, but they may provide better results
[
Navascués et al.(2009)Navascués, Owari, and Plenio
]
at an extra computational cost.

Once approximate solutions
\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}
are obtained from
Eq.

15
, they can be used to establish a convergent hierarchy of upper bounds on the temporal correlations for each sequence, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}\leq\cdots\leq\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N+1}_{d_{\text{E}}}\leq\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}\leq\cdots\leq\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},L}_{d_{\text{E}}},\\
&\text{with}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}=\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}.\end{split}

(16)

Consequently, the above formulates a sequence of SDPs capable of approximating the maxima
\omega_{d_{\text{E}}}^{\bm{a}}
to arbitrary precision. Numerical results obtained from
any
of these SDPs (i.e., for any
N\geq L
) can be used to construct dimensional witnesses, which, when violated, certify the minimum dimension of the effective environment interacting with the system.
A software implementation of the SDP in
Eq.

15
, however, is not straightforward even for small values of
\{d_{\text{S}},d_{\text{E}},L,N\}
, as the memory requirements quickly render the problem computationally intractable. Obtaining the numerical results presented in the next section, thus, required a significant amount of optimization; see
Section

6
for details. A schematic outline of all steps undertaken to formulate the SDP and obtain the numerical results is presented in
Fig.

3
.

. The tractable/intractable labels, on the right, refer to the case

The asymptotic convergence of the hierarchy of bounds in
Eq.

16
for the SDP in
Eq.

, without PPT constraints, is given by

]

|\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}-\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}|\leq\frac{L(L+(d_{\text{ES}})^{2}+1)}{N+d_{\text{ES}}}.

If PPT constraints are included, we only have partial analytical results for the asymptotic error. Numerical evidence and previous results involving PPT constraints

(18)

where
f(L,d_{\text{ES}})
is a function of

and
d_{\text{E}}
. We leave the analysis of these asymptotic error bounds to a future investigation.

6
Implementation

This section outlines the concrete steps we have taken to relax the original problem to a hierarchy of SDPs and to make these SDPs numerically tractable; see
Appendix

B
for more details. As noted previously, a straightforward implementation is not computationally tractable, even for short sequences, due to the large number of variables and constraints involved.

We have addressed these additional numerical challenges by exploiting several properties of the problem. Firstly, the symmetric constraint imposed on
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
can be satisfied automatically by expressing
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
in terms of a basis for the symmetric subspace in the numerical implementation of the SDP. While this provides a significant reduction in the total number of involved variables and constraints, it is by itself insufficient to make the problem tractable.

However, thanks to our specific choice of initial states and measurements, the sparsity of the objective function could be exploited to further reduce the number of variables and constraints, by eliminating all of those that do not affect the objective, either directly or indirectly. This relaxation generally results in a sparse outer approximation of the original problem, i.e., to a value
\Omega^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}\geq\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}
. In our particular case, as we show in
Section

B.2
, this elimination procedure is exact, thus yielding an optimal value
\Omega^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}=\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}
.

6.1
Choice of parameters

Before a concrete implementation, one must first choose the initial environment state
\rho^{0}_{\text{E}}
, as well as fixing the probe state and measurements. As the objective function is convex on
\rho^{0}_{\text{E}}
, and the optimization is over all unitaries, we may – without loss of generality – fix a pure initial state
\rho^{0}_{\text{E}}=\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{E}
and eliminate any assumptions on the environment state; see
Section

7.2
.
On the other hand, as assumptions on
d_{\text{S}}
,
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
and
(E_{S}^{a})_{a}
are experiment-dependent, one must search for “proper” probe states and measurements on a case-by-case basis, but optimal choices, leading to a larger bound, can also be addressed in general terms; see
Section

7.3
. For the choice of sequence, in particular, if it is too simple relative to
d_{\text{E}}
, the maximum attainable probability may be trivial (i.e., equal to one), such that no optimization is required, and no witness can be constructed. Therefore, it is important to choose sequences which have a non-trivial maximum for a given
d_{\text{E}}
. For closed systems, this problem has been solved via the notion of “deterministic complexity”
[
Vieira and Budroni(2022)
]
, which defines the minimum requirements for a sequence to be able to occur deterministically. In the open system case, the conditions for determinism involve not only the available memory, as was the case in the isolated system, but also the dimension of the system. We elaborate on these conditions in
Section

7.3
.

6.2
Symmetric representation

Effectively solving any of the SDPs in the hierarchy requires a significant amount of optimization, as a naive implementation quickly becomes computationally intractable. As a rough example, without any simplifications,
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
is a square matrix of size
(d_{\text{ES}}^{2})^{N}
, which in the simplest non-trivial scenario,
d_{\text{E}}=d_{\text{S}}=2
and
N=L=3
, already results in a
4096\times 4096
matrix, with over 16 million complex scalar variables. The partial trace constraint alone involves over 1 million linear equations between these variables, making the SDP numerically intractable in this naïve formulation.

In practice, several simplifications can be made; see
Appendix

B
for full details.
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
and
X\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}}
may be written directly in terms of an operator basis for the symmetric subspace, thus automatically satisfying the symmetry constraint, which significantly reduces the number of variables. Defining an isometry between the symmetric subspace of
\text{A}_{1}^{N}
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{N}
(with dimension
\dim\mathcal{S}_{N}
), and a canonical basis for
\mathcal{S}_{N}
,

V=\sum_{\bm{t}}\left|\bm{t}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{S}_{N}}\left\langle\text{sym}(\bm{t})\right|_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}},

(21)

we may cast all involved objects directly as
{\dim\mathcal{S}_{N}\times\dim\mathcal{S}_{N}}
matrices:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}&=\sum_{\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}}\phi_{\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}}\left|\text{sym}(\bm{t})\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\text{sym}(\bm{t}^{\prime})\right|_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}},\\
\phi&=V\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}V^{\dagger},\;\text{and}\\
x&=V(X^{\text{T}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}})V^{\dagger},\end{split}

(22)

where
\bm{t}
denotes a “type” for the canonical representation of symmetric states
\left|\text{sym}(\bm{t})\right\rangle_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
; see
[
Harrow(2013)
]
. Under this representation, the objective function becomes
\operatorname{tr}\left[x\phi\right]
, and the constraints may be formulated in terms of
\phi_{\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}}
directly, without resorting to the the full space
\text{A}_{1}^{N}
. Note that
VV^{\dagger}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{N}}
but
V^{\dagger}V\neq\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
(rather,
V^{\dagger}V
is a projector onto
\mathcal{S}_{N}
). As the dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{N}
is
[
Harrow(2013)
]

\dim\mathcal{S}_{N}=\binom{N+d_{\text{ES}}^{2}-1}{N},

(23)

for
d_{\text{ES}}=4
and
N=3
the matrix
\phi
is of size
816\times 816
, with around 600 thousand variables: a reduction to 4% of the original 16 million. With further work, the partial trace constraints can also be efficiently written directly in this representation, leading to
(d_{\text{ES}}\cdot\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}(N-1)+d_{\text{ES}}^{2}-1\\
N-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr))^{2}
equality constraints, resulting in approximately 300 thousand constraints in the
d_{\text{ES}}=4
and
N=3
scenario: a reduction to 28% of the original. This symmetric representation is explained in more detail in
Section

B.1
.

6.3
Sparse implementation

While these are significant improvements, they still prove to be insufficient, as the dense representation of the symmetric problem involves too many variables and constraints to be solvable. As a concrete example, for the simplest case of
\bm{a}=001
,
d_{\text{E}}=2
and
L=N=3
, the SDP solver SCS attempts to allocate a dense array of over 3 TB in size.

To address this, we have developed an algorithm that exploits any sparsity of the objective function, and – whenever possible – constructs a sparse relaxation of the original problem. This is achieved by iteratively selecting which variables of
\phi
and which of its constraints are strictly necessary to solve the problem, due to their direct or indirect influence in the objective function. While generally a relaxation, due to the explicit structure of our problem this sparse implementation is exact, yielding
\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}
. A detailed explanation of our algorithm is available in
Section

B.2
.

7
Discussion

7.1
Repeated unitaries

In the following, we discuss in which cases the condition of repeated unitaries is physically justified. To see this, imagine that the evolution of the system is governed by a time-independent Hamiltonian
H_{\rm{SE}}
. The corresponding unitaries will be of the form
U(t)=e^{-iHt}
. This assumption of time-independence is always possible, as the environment may include any source of time dependence. Ideally, then, we would have the same unitary if we perform the measure-and-prepare operations equally spaced in time, say, by an interval
\Delta t
. One should take into account, however, some uncertainty in the time measurement. Let us then assume that our choice of time for performing a measurement is distributed according to a distribution
q(t)
, centered around
\Delta t
. This means that instead of transforming our system according to the unitary
U(\Delta t)
, we transform it, on average, according to the mapping
\Lambda:=\int\mathcal{U}(t)q(t)dt
, seemingly breaking our assumption of repeated unitaries. Since
\Lambda
is a valid completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) map, it can be dilated by means of a larger environment into a unitary
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
, namely
\Lambda(\rho_{\rm SE})=\operatorname{tr}_{{\rm E}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(\rho_{\rm SE}\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{{\rm E}_{1}})\right]
. To complete the argument that this situation can still be described by repeated unitaries, it is enough to show that the same can be done for multiple copies of it, namely, that the repeated operation
\Lambda^{L}
can be dilated to some unitary
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}^{L}
. To do so, it is enough to provide at each time-step
i
a new environment
{\rm E}_{i}
prepared in the correct initial state
\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{{\rm E}_{i}}
. Let us compute explicitly the case for two time steps, the general case is straightforward. We define
W_{\rm{E}_{1}\rm{E}_{2}}
the swap operator between systems
{\rm E}_{1}
and
{\rm E}_{2}
, and we set
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}:=W_{\rm{E}_{1}\rm{E}_{2}}\circ\,\mathcal{U}_{\rm{SEE}_{1}}^{\prime}
. Let us calculate, for simplicity

\begin{split}\operatorname{tr}_{\rm{E}_{1}\rm{E}_{2}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\mathcal{U}_{\rm{SEE}_{1}}^{\prime}\!\!\circ\!W_{\rm{E}_{1}\rm{E}_{2}}\circ\mathcal{U}_{\rm{SEE}_{1}}^{\prime}(\rho_{\rm{SE}}\!\otimes\!\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{\rm{E}_{1}}\!\otimes\!\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{\rm{E}_{2}})\right]\\
=\operatorname{tr}_{\rm{E}_{1}\rm{E}_{2}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\mathcal{U}_{\rm{SEE}_{1}}^{\prime}\!\!\circ\!W_{\rm{E}_{1}\rm{E}_{2}}(\sigma_{{}_{\rm{SEE}_{1}}}\!\otimes\!\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{\rm{E}_{2}})\right]\\
=\operatorname{tr}_{\rm{E}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\mathcal{U}_{\rm{SEE}_{1}}^{\prime}(\operatorname{tr}_{\rm{E}_{2}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\sigma_{{}_{\rm{SEE}_{2}}}\right]\!\otimes\!\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{\rm{E}_{1}})\right]\\
=\operatorname{tr}_{\rm{E}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\mathcal{U}_{\rm{SEE}_{1}}^{\prime}(\Lambda(\rho_{\rm{SE}})\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{\rm{E}_{1}})\right]=\Lambda^{2}(\rho_{\rm{SE}}),\end{split}

(24)

where
\sigma_{\rm{SEE}_{1}}:=\mathcal{U}_{\rm{SEE}_{1}}^{\prime}(\rho_{\rm{SE}}\otimes\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{\rm{E}_{1}})
. The effect of the final
W_{\rm{E}_{1}\rm{E}_{2}}
operation is to swap the space
\rm{E}_{1}
and
\rm{E}_{2}
that are irrelevant after the operations on
\rm{SE}
have been performed. Evidently, this argument can straightforwardly be extended to more time steps.
In summary, this means that the condition of “the same unitary” is satisfied as long as the time choice is always drawn from the same distribution. In other words, it is sufficient to always “probabilistically repeat” the same operation.

Finally, we remark that even if this procedure may use a very large environment, we are only interested in showing that we can always assume there
is
a unitary dynamics, with the notion of an effective environment, then, taking care of estimating the environment consistent with the observed statistics. Moreover, we also remark that time-independent operations are necessary, as unrestricted time-dependent operations can achieve arbitrarily long temporal correlations even with bounded memory size
[
Mao et al.(2022)Mao, Spee, Xu, and Gühne
]
.

7.2
Effective environment and initial state

The environment of a quantum system, intended as all the physical systems surrounding and possibly interacting with it, is typically a very high-dimensional system, if not directly assumed to be infinite-dimensional. From this perspective, we want to make sense of the notion of
effective
environment. Consider a global transformation of the system and environment. As a first approximation, we can say that the global unitary is of the form
U_{\text{SE}}\otimes U_{\text{E}^{\prime}}
, where
U_{\text{SE}}
is an entangling unitary between the system and the effective environment and
U_{\text{E}^{\prime}}
is acting on the rest of the environment.
At the same time, an evolution of the form
U_{\text{SE}}\otimes U_{\text{E}^{\prime}}
is just an approximation of the full evolution of the environment, as we expect its state to thermalize after some time interval. Nevertheless, from a physical perspective this approximation is still valid if the time required for a single run of the experiment, i.e., the measurement of the temporal sequence, is much shorter than the time needed for the effective environment to thermalize. This is to be expected, as the environment is composed of many particles that may interact with each other with different strengths. Underlying this expectation is the assumption – known as Markovian embedding
[
Budini(2013)
,
Xue et al.(2015)Xue, James, Shabani, Ugrinovskii, and
Petersen
,
Xue et al.(2020)Xue, Nguyen, James, Shabani, Ugrinovskii, and
Petersen
]
and frequently employed in the description and modeling of open quantum system dynamics
[
Tamascelli et al.(2018)Tamascelli, Smirne, Huelga, and
Plenio
,
Luchnikov et al.(2018)Luchnikov, Vintskevich, and
Filippov
,
Luchnikov et al.(2019)Luchnikov, Vintskevich, Ouerdane, and
Filippov
,
Luchnikov et al.(2022)Luchnikov, Kiktenko, Gavreev, Ouerdane,
Filippov, and Fedorov
]
– that the environment can always be split into two parts: a far environment, leading to irretrievable, memoryless information loss, and an effective environment, that can transport memory. Due to the irretrievable information loss, the dynamics of the system and the effective environment is then described by a general (non-unitary) CPTP map, a situation which, as discussed in the previous section, can again be dilated to repeated unitaries.

Reversing this perspective, namely, looking at the problem of characterizing the effective environment from temporal correlation experiments, we may say that this characterization is inherently dependent on the typical time-scales of a single experimental run.
This difference in time scales and the eventual thermalization of the environment, however, are essential for repeating the experiment for collecting statistics, as it is required that the initial state of the environment is always (probabilistically) described by the same state
\rho^{0}_{\text{E}}
in each experimental run. As previously mentioned, this is typically a thermal state, but it does not need to be characterized in our approach. In fact, since the SDP maximizes the temporal correlations, which are linear in the initial state of the environment, we know that the maximum is always achieved with a pure state. Up to local unitaries, we can thus always assume it to be
\rho^{0}_{\text{E}}=\left|0\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle 0\right|_{E}
. The bound calculated for this state is, then, valid for any possible initial state of the environment.

Finally, somewhat independent of the explicit experimental situation, we may see our setup as a question of simulation resources: What is the smallest dimension an environment coupling to a known system must have in order to reproduce observed statistics in a unitary way? Seen in this way, the results we present attribute a “simulation hardness” (in the sense of required environment dimension) to each sequence, that is agnostic with respect to concrete time scales or experimental limitations, but rather inherent to the respective sequence.

7.3
Conditions for deterministic realizations

The maximum probability for a given sequence increases as more memory is available, i.e.,

0\leq\omega_{1}^{\bm{a}}\leq\omega_{2}^{\bm{a}}\leq\cdots\leq\omega_{d_{\text{E}}}^{\bm{a}}\leq 1,

(25)

as larger environments can always simulate the dynamics of smaller ones. Therefore, if the sequence
\bm{a}
is sufficiently simple, its maximum may be trivial for a given
d_{\text{E}}
, i.e.,
\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}=1
.

The maxima
\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}
depend on the sequence, as not all sequences are equally “difficult” to produce with a given amount of memory
d_{\text{E}}
. For example, it is easy to see that the sequence ‘
000
’ can
always
be produced with unit probability, while the sequence ‘
001
’ cannot be produced with unit probability if the environment is not of sufficient size
[
Vieira and Budroni(2022)
]
. This observation suggests a relevant notion of “complexity” of sequences, which offers a more fundamental relationship between
\bm{a}
and
d_{\text{E}}
. Since our goal is to establish non-trivial maxima on the probabilities of sequences, the natural question to ask is how large should
d_{\text{E}}
be such that
\bm{a}
can occur deterministically?

Understanding the conditions where this occurs allows us to pick only scenarios featuring non-trivial maxima. It is useful to recall the following

Definition
.

(Deterministic Complexity
[
Vieira and Budroni(2022)
]
) Let

be a sequence of measurement outcomes, produced by repeated identical measurements on an isolated
d

and it is independent of the model (quantum or classical).

The open system and environment scenario can be equivalently interpreted as the dilation of the scenario involving sequential measurements

First, we establish a correspondence between the single system and open system scenarios. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{a}(\rho_{\text{E}})

Proposition 1

If both

7.4
Choice of sequence

As briefly noted in
Section

7.3
, in order to construct a witness for
d_{\text{E}}>d
, it is vital to choose a sequence
\bm{a}
with sufficient deterministic complexity. If the sequence chosen is too short or too simple (i.e., if
\operatorname{DC}(\bm{a})\leq d
), then the bound
\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d}
is trivial and the dimension
d_{\text{E}}
cannot be tested with such sequence.

However, due to the difficulty in computing these bounds, and the fact longer sequences generally become less probable—and therefore less likely to violate the witness inequality—generally one should choose sequences to be as short as possible while having deterministic complexity of the same size as the minimum environment dimension one wishes to witness.

As a concrete example, if we wish to witness
d_{\text{E}}>3
, we should choose a sequence
\bm{a}
with deterministic complexity
\operatorname{DC}(\bm{a})=4
, e.g.,
\bm{a}=0001
, which is the shortest sequence satisfying this requirement. Then, a violation of the bound
\omega^{0001}_{3}
informs us that, in fact,
d_{\text{E}}\geq 4
.

8
Conclusions and outlook

We presented a method to lower bound the dimension of the environment interacting with a probe system, based only on the statistics of measurements performed on the (partially characterized) system, and without any assumption on the environment or the dynamics. This is achieved via a hierarchy of semidefinite programs that upper bound temporal correlations achievable in various experimental scenarios, under the assumption of finite memory. Such bounds can be applied to the detection of the effective environment size in the dynamics of open systems, as well as a certification of the minimum size of an environment’s dimension compatible with observations.

To keep the discussion simpler, we applied the optimization for a single sequence. It is straightforward to adapt the objective function to arbitrary linear functions of the full probability distribution
(p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}}))_{\bm{a}}
, as those appearing in the temporal inequalities derived in, e.g.,
[
Hoffmann et al.(2018)Hoffmann, Spee, Gühne, and
Budroni
,
Budroni et al.(2019)Budroni, Fagundes, and
Kleinmann
,
Spee et al.(2020)Spee, Budroni, and Gühne
,
Mao et al.(2022)Mao, Spee, Xu, and Gühne
]
. This may, in principle, lead to better witnesses. We leave the numerical explorations of this problem to a future investigation.

We assumed a joint unitary evolution between system and environment, which leads to a CJ representation of these maps given in terms of symmetric states. As explained in
Section

7.1
, this assumption can usually be justified on physical grounds. Nevertheless, a natural question to ask is how do our results change if we consider arbitrary CPTP maps? In that case, the SDP should be modified by replacing the symmetry constraint with permutation invariance, i.e.,
V_{\sigma}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}V^{\dagger}_{\sigma}=\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
for all permutations
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
; see
Eq.

35
. We expect that bounds for CPTP maps may be larger than those for unitary channels of the same dimension, especially for objective functions involving more than a single sequence, but numerical optimization is significantly more costly in this case, as the permutation invariant operator basis is of size
[
Watrous(2018)
, Ch. 7]

\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}N+(d_{\text{ES}})^{4}-1\\
N\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
, in contrast to
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}N+(d_{\text{ES}})^{2}-1\\
N\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)^{2}
in the symmetric case. While it is also possible to write such CPTP maps in terms a dilation of the environment, this approach would likely result in more extraneous variables in the SDP, i.e., the terms in the subspace orthogonal to the dilation ancilla’s initial state, possibly rendering the optimization intractable.

Additionally, the SDP in
Eq.

15
and its related implementation techniques are, in fact, quite general, and can be applied to a wide range of scenarios beyond what we have considered here. As the operator
X
from
Eq.

10
is fixed, any choice of intermediate operations between each unitary could be chosen. E.g., the initial system and environment states could be correlated, and the intermediate maps
\mathcal{M}_{a}
could each be replaced by arbitrary joint operations, even time-dependent ones. Such approaches could then, for example, be used to bound observables for specific types of processes, e.g., quantum processes with only classical memory
[
Giarmatzi and Costa(2021)
]
, by assuming additional entanglement breaking channels on the environment. Therefore, provided the problem is numerically tractable, our techniques are independent of what explicit measurements are chosen.

While the high dimensionality of the current formulation of the SDP quickly renders general numerical implementations intractable, our approach still offers new avenues for the subject of bounding temporal correlations, and their relationship to open-system dynamics. Ultimately, the techniques developed herein should be taken as a proof-of-concept for future developments and improvements. It remains to be seen whether more efficient numerical techniques, or even alternative outer approximations, are better suited for addressing such problems. Further investigation of these avenues is subject to future work.

Nevertheless, the success of our approach highlights the wealth of information contained in temporal correlations and the potential of new techniques for characterizing large complex systems by means of a small probe alone, by exploiting non-trivial properties of the temporal correlations achieved by systems of bounded size.

\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))\cap\text{SEP}_{n}=\left\{\;\sum_{i}p_{i}\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes n}\;\;\middle|\;\;\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{ES}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{ES}},\quad\left\langle\phi_{i}\middle|\phi_{i}\right\rangle=1,\quad p_{i}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{i}p_{i}=1\;\right\},

(37)

which implies
\mathcal{T}\subset\operatorname{Sym}_{L}(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))\cap\text{SEP}_{L}
. In fact, consider
\rho\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))\cap\text{SEP}_{n}
. Since it is separable, we can write it as as a convex mixture of pure product states, i.e.,

\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|,

(38)

with
\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle
a
n
-party pure product state, i.e.,
\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\left|\phi_{i,j}\right\rangle
, with the
\{\left|\phi_{i,j}\right\rangle\}_{j}
not necessarily equal for a given
i
.
However,
\rho\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
implies
\operatorname{ran}(\rho)\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathcal{H})
, and if we prove that
\operatorname{ran}(\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|)\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathcal{H})
for all
i
, then we find that
\{\left|\phi_{i,j}\right\rangle\}_{j}
must be identical for each
i
, otherwise
\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle
fails to be symmetric, namely,
\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|=\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes n}
and, thus,
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes n}
.
It remains to prove that
\operatorname{ran}(\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|)\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathcal{H})
. To show this, we note that by
Eq.

38
and positivity of
\rho
and
\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|
, we have
\operatorname{Ker}(\rho)=\cap_{i}\operatorname{Ker}(\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|)
. By Hermiticity, it follows that
\operatorname{ran}(\rho)=(\operatorname{Ker}(\rho))^{\perp}=\text{span}(\cup_{i}\operatorname{ran}(\left|\Phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\Phi_{i}\right|))
, which concludes the proof.

Importantly,
Eq.

37
, restores the symmetry and rank-1 properties for optimum solutions of
2
when
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}
is restricted to
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))\cap\text{SEP}_{n}
. In light of this, in the following we use
\operatorname{SymSEP}_{n}:=\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))\cap\text{SEP}_{n}
.

A.1.2
The trace-preserving constraint

We now restore the trace-preserving constraint
\operatorname{tr}_{\mathcal{O}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|\right]=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}}/d_{\text{ES}}
for states expressed as in
Eq.

37
. This is established by proving that it is sufficient to satisfy this condition for a single party in the convex mixture.

Proposition 3
.

For any
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\in\operatorname{SymSEP}_{L}
:

\operatorname{tr}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\right]=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}}{d_{\text{ES}}}\otimes\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\right]\quad\iff\quad\operatorname{tr}_{\mathcal{O}_{\ell}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|_{\text{A}_{\ell}}\right]=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}_{\ell}}}{d_{\text{ES}}}\otimes\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes L-1}\quad\forall\ell.

(39)

Proof.

We provide a generalization of the arguments in
[
Yu et al.(2022)Yu, Simnacher, Nguyen, and
Gühne
, App. A]
. From
Eq.

37
, if
\text{P}^{+}_{L}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}=\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}
, we know it admits a decomposition of the form

\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes L},\qquad p_{i}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{i}p_{i}=1.

(40)

We introduce the one-party auxiliary map
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)=\operatorname{tr}_{\mathcal{O}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\cdot\right]-\operatorname{tr}\left[\cdot\right]\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{I}}/d_{\text{ES}}
implementing our partial trace constraint in
\mathcal{T}
, such that
(\mathcal{E}_{\text{A}_{1}}\otimes\text{id}_{\text{A}_{2}}\otimes\text{id}_{\text{A}_{3}^{L}})(\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}})=0

for any
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\in\operatorname{SymSEP}_{L}\cap\,\mathcal{T}
, and, due to symmetry, the same is true if the map had been applied to the second party instead. Defining
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\cdot)=[\mathcal{E}(\cdot)]^{\dagger}
, we may then write
(\mathcal{E}_{\text{A}_{1}}\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{A}_{2}}\otimes\text{id}_{\text{A}_{3}^{L}})(\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}})=0
. As
\mathcal{E}
acts on Hermitian operators and is Hermiticity-preserving, we have
\mathcal{E}=\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
and the explicit distinction is only made for added clarity in the following proof. Applying the map to the convex mixture in
Eq.

40
, we obtain

\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{E}_{\text{A}_{1}}(\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|_{\text{A}_{1}})\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{A}_{1}}(\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|_{\text{A}_{2}})\otimes\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes L-2}=\sum_{i}p_{i}E_{i}\otimes E_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes L-2}=0,

(41)

where
E_{i}=\mathcal{E}(\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|)
. We need this to be true for all
E_{i}
if we want to ensure
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}
obeys our partial trace constraint globally in the convex mixture. To prove this is the case, let
G\in\mathbbm{C}^{d_{\text{ES}}^{2}\times d_{\text{ES}}^{2}}
, so that

\operatorname{tr}\left[(G\otimes G^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{3}^{L}})\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}E_{i}\otimes E_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes L-2}\right)\right]=\sum_{i}p_{i}|\hskip-1.49994pt\operatorname{tr}\left[GE_{i}\right]\hskip-1.49994pt|^{2}\operatorname{tr}\left[\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes L-2}\right]=0,

(42)

which must hold for any
G
. Since
p_{i}\geq 0
,
|\hskip-1.49994pt\operatorname{tr}\left[GE_{i}\right]\hskip-1.49994pt|\geq 0
, and
\operatorname{tr}\left[\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes L-2}\right]=1
, this can only be true for all
G
if all
E_{i}=0
. Furthermore, by the symmetry of each term in the mixture, it is then sufficient to ensure the constraint is satisfied in a single party. From
Eq.

37
, the converse statement follows trivially.
∎

Thus, any state
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\in\operatorname{SymSEP}_{L}
satisfying
\operatorname{tr}_{\text{O}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\right]=(\mathbbm{1}_{\text{I}_{1}}/d_{\text{ES}})\otimes\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\right]
is part of the target set
\mathcal{T}
and vice versa.

A.1.3
The final rank-constrained problem over SEP

Given all of the previous results, we have now established a relation between the sets
\mathcal{T}
and
\text{SEP}_{L}
,

\mathcal{T}=\left\{\;\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\in\text{SEP}_{L}\;\;\middle|\;\;\text{P}^{+}_{L}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}=\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}},\quad\operatorname{tr}_{\text{O}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\right]=(\mathbbm{1}_{\text{I}_{1}}/d_{\text{ES}})\otimes\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\right]\;\right\},

(43)

which allows us to remove the non-linear dependence on the tensor product
(C_{U})^{\otimes L}
, by replacing the search space with
\operatorname{SymSEP}_{L}
, while obeying the linear constraints on the partial trace of one party. We thus obtain:

Optimization Problem 3
.

Rank-1 and symmetric optimum (exact)

\begin{split}\textbf{Given:}\quad&\rho^{0}_{\text{E}},\;\rho^{0}_{\text{S}},\;\{M_{a}\}_{a},\;\bm{a}\\
\textbf{Find:}\quad&\max_{\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\,\in\;\text{SEP}_{L}}\operatorname{tr}\left[X^{\text{T}}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\right]\\
\textbf{Subject to:}\quad&\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\geq 0,\quad\operatorname{tr}\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\right]=1,\quad\text{P}^{+}_{L}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}=\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}},\\
&\operatorname{tr}_{\text{O}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\right]=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\text{I}_{1}}}{d_{\text{ES}}}\otimes\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\right]\end{split}

(44)

However,
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\in\text{SEP}_{L}
is still not a linear constraint and highly non-trivial to be enforced, as a full characterization of the separable set is NP-HARD
[
Gurvits(2003)
]
. Therefore, while a priori appearing more manageable, the above problem is still not in a form that can be tackled numerically. Fortunately, the symmetric constraint can also be exploited to obtain approximations of the symmetric separable states.

A.2
The SEP constraint

A.2.1
Approximation of SEP via the quantum de Finetti theorem

Ensuring
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\in\text{SEP}_{L}
cannot be done exactly, so as it stands the problem is still numerically intractable. However, it is possible to define arbitrarily-precise outer approximations of the separable set through symmetric extensions and the quantum de Finetti theorem
[
Caves et al.(2002)Caves, Fuchs, and Schack
,
Christandl et al.(2007)Christandl, König, Mitchison, and
Renner
]
. Concretely, we can approximate, in principle to any desirable precision, a
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\in\text{SEP}_{L}
by a marginal over a larger symmetric (and not necessarily separable) state
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
, provided
N
is sufficiently large. For any finite
N
, this leads to an outer approximation of
\mathcal{T}
, which establishes a convergent hierarchy of outer approximations, such that for any
N\geq L
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}\leq\cdots\leq\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N+1}_{d_{\text{E}}}\leq\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}\leq\cdots\leq\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},L}_{d_{\text{E}}},\\
&\text{with}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{a},N}_{d_{\text{E}}}=\omega^{\bm{a}}_{d_{\text{E}}}.\end{split}

(45)

More precisely, using the quantum de Finetti theorem we can establish asymptotic error bounds on this approximation
[
Chiribella(2011)
, Cor.1]
. In terms of the trace norm, this asymptotic error bound is given by

\left\|\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}-\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right]\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{2L(L+(d_{\text{ES}})^{2}+1)}{N+(d_{\text{ES}})^{2}}.

(46)

As the symmetry requirement applies to both
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}
and
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
, this application of the quantum de Finetti theorem for approximating
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{L}}\in\text{SEP}_{L}
is straightforward in the SDP in
Eq.

15
, only requiring the use of a larger state
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
as the optimization variable, and a suitable adjustment of the objective function using
(X^{\text{T}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}})
.

A.2.2
Additional separability constraints

To improve the approximation, at an extra computational cost, we may also include additional separability constraints. In our case, we consider the constraints of positive partial transpose (PPT) on
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
:

\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}^{\text{T}_{\alpha}}\geq 0,\quad\forall\;\text{non-equivalent bipartitions}\;\alpha.

(47)

Here, “non-equivalent bipartitions” refers to the fact that it is unnecessary to include all bipartitions, as the state
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
is symmetric, and thus permutation invariant. Therefore, the bipartitions needed are
\alpha_{k}
, where, e.g., we transpose only the first
k
subspaces, for
1\leq k\leq\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor
, with symmetry taking care of the remaining constraints.

The existence of entangled states which have positive partial transpose
[
Horodecki et al.(1998)Horodecki, Horodecki, and
Horodecki
]
means these constraints reduce the feasible convex set to that of PPT states, not the separable states. While entangled PPT states are not suitable solutions to the original problem, they are still adequate approximations and provide upper bounds for the actual optimal values. Inclusion of PPT constraints will perform at least as well as optimizing without them (i.e., the solution can only be improved), but convergence is significantly improved in certain cases
[
Navascués et al.(2009)Navascués, Owari, and Plenio
]
to
O(1/N^{2})
as opposed to
O(1/N)
in the absence of these constraints. As shown in
Table

1
, for the
d_{\text{E}}=1
case PPT constraints were sufficient to provide the exact analytical bounds.

A.2.3
SDP for
SEP
relaxation

By all of the above results, we arrive at the following SDP relaxation of the original problem:

Optimization Problem 4
.

Final outer approximation (SDP).

\begin{split}\textbf{Given:}\quad&\displaystyle d_{\text{E}},\;d_{\text{S}},\;\rho^{0}_{\text{E}},\;\rho^{0}_{\text{S}},\;\{M_{a}\}_{a},\;\bm{a}\\
\textbf{Find:}\quad&\tilde{\omega}_{d_{\text{E}}}^{\bm{a},N}:=\max_{\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}}\operatorname{tr}\left[(X^{\text{T}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}})\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right]\\
\textbf{Subject to:}\quad&\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\geq 0,\quad\operatorname{tr}\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right]=1,\quad\text{P}^{+}_{N}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}=\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}},\\
&\operatorname{tr}_{\text{O}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right]=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\text{I}_{1}}}{d_{\text{ES}}}\otimes\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right]\\
&\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}^{{\text{T}_{\alpha}}}\geq 0,\forall\alpha\in\mathfrak{A},\end{split}

(48)

with
\mathfrak{A}
denoting the set of non-equivalent bipartitions (
Section

A.2.2
). The solutions of this SDP provide upper-bounds for the maxima
\omega_{d_{\text{E}}}^{\bm{a}}
.

Appendix B
Implementation

While in principle numerically accessible, the SDP in
4
still contains too many variables to allow for the computation of upper bounds of
\tilde{\omega}_{d_{\text{E}}}^{\bm{a},N}
, even for low-dimensional cases. This problem can be tackled by exploiting the fact that all appearing objects can be expressed within the symmetric subspace, as well as the (potential) sparsity of the problem.

In our case, a further simplification is possible by first noticing that both
X
and
\text{P}^{+}_{N}
are real-valued. If we additionally consider a real-valued basis for the partial trace, we conclude that, for any feasible
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
, its entry-wise complex conjugate
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}^{*}
is also feasible, while providing the same value for the objective function. Therefore, we may perform the optimization using a real-valued
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
.
Next, we explain how to exploit the symmetry property.

B.1
Symmetric representation

The symmetry constraint can be satisfied automatically by expressing
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
directly in terms of a basis for the symmetric subspace. This allows for a great reduction in the number of variables and linear constraints in the SDP, which is required for its efficient numerical optimization. In the following, we describe how to construct this basis for the symmetric subspace, largely based on
[
Harrow(2013)
]
, and how to adapt the remaining constraints to act directly in this symmetric representation, based on a generalization of results provided in
[
Stockton et al.(2003)Stockton, Geremia, Doherty, and
Mabuchi
]
for the qubit case.

We begin by defining a canonical basis for the symmetric subspace. With the convention
\mathbbm{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
and
\mathbbm{N}_{0}:=\mathbbm{N}\cup\{0\}
, for
d,n\in\mathbbm{N}
, let

{\mathfrak{T}_{d}^{n}}:=\left\{\;(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{d})\;\;\middle|\;\;t_{i}\in\mathbbm{N}_{0},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{d}t_{i}=n\;\right\}

(49)

be the set of weak integer compositions for
n
into exactly
d
parts, possibly of zero size, which we refer to as
types
. These types form a canonical labeling for the basis of the symmetric subspace.
Now, defining
[d]:=\{1,2,\dots,d\}
, let
\bm{u}\in[d]^{n}
, i.e.,
\bm{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
with
u_{\ell}\in[d]
, and
T(\bm{u})=\bm{t}
be the type of the vector
\bm{u}
, where
t_{i}
counts the number of instances where
u_{\ell}=i
holds. As a concrete example, if
d=6
and
n=8
, we might have:

\bm{u}=(1,4,1,2,3,2,2,6)\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad T(\bm{u})=(2,3,1,1,0,1).

(50)

|
In words, types count how many times a number
1,\dots,d
occurs in
\bm{u}
, and therefore are invariant under permutations, i.e.,
T(\bm{u})=T(P_{\sigma}\bm{u})
for any permutation operator
P_{\sigma}
acting on entries of
\bm{u}
. Given a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with
d=\dim\mathcal{H}
, we may now define a basis for
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathcal{H})
in terms of the types
\bm{t}\in{\mathfrak{T}_{d}^{n}}
by constructing the non-normalized and normalized orthogonal basis vectors for the symmetric subspace
[
Harrow(2013)
]
, respectively, as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\text{Sym}(\bm{t})\right\rangle:=\sum_{\bm{u};T(\bm{u})=\bm{t}}\left|u_{1},u_{2},\cdots,u_{n}\right\rangle,\qquad\left|\text{sym}(\bm{t})\right\rangle:=(\bm{t})^{-1/2}\left|\text{Sym}(\bm{t})\right\rangle,\end{split}

(51)

where
(\bm{t})=\frac{(\sum_{i}t_{i})!}{t_{1}!\cdot t_{2}!\cdots t_{d}!}
denotes the multinomial coefficient for the normalization, and, for convenience in notation, we adopt the canonical basis
\{\left|u\right\rangle\}_{u=1}^{d}
for each individual
u_{i}
.
Equation

51
clearly defines a symmetric state, since each
\bm{u}
occurs only once in the sum. Thus, for any permutation operator
V_{\sigma}
, we have
V_{\sigma}\left|\text{sym}(\bm{t})\right\rangle=\left|\text{sym}(\bm{t})\right\rangle
, and similarly for the non-normalized
\left|\text{Sym}(\bm{t})\right\rangle
.

From
Eq.

37
, we know any symmetric separable state can be written as
\sum_{i}p_{i}\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\phi_{i}\right|^{\otimes n}
. Therefore, by expressing
\left|\phi_{i}\right\rangle^{\otimes n}
in terms of the symmetric basis in
Eq.

51
, we may write any symmetric operator by indexing the degrees of freedom of
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
by the types
\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}
. In our current problem, with a local dimension
d=(d_{\text{E}}d_{\text{S}})^{2}=d_{\text{ES}}^{2}
, the symmetric space has total dimension
[
Harrow(2013)
]

D_{\mathcal{S}}=\dim\operatorname{Sym}_{N}(\mathcal{H})=\left(\!\left(\!\begin{smallmatrix}d_{\text{ES}}^{2}\\
N\end{smallmatrix}\!\right)\!\right)=\binom{N+d_{\text{ES}}^{2}-1}{N},

(52)

with
\left(\!\left(\!\begin{smallmatrix}d\\
n\end{smallmatrix}\!\right)\!\right)
the multiset notation, i.e., the number of ways of picking
n
elements out of
d
, with repetitions allowed, which corresponds to the cardinality of
{\mathfrak{T}_{d}^{n}}
. This is a significant reduction from the original
\dim\text{A}_{1}^{N}=(d_{\text{ES}})^{2N}
.

For simplicity in notation, we consider the dependence of
D_{\mathcal{S}}
on
d_{\text{ES}}
and
N
implicit in the following. To concretely exploit this reduction of the number of variables, i.e., in order to write
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
in terms of a smaller matrix, we re-express the problem as follows. Let
\mathcal{S}_{N}:=\mathbbm{C}^{D_{\mathcal{S}}\otimes D_{\mathcal{S}}}
, and
\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
. We specify elements
\phi_{\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}}
, with
\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{T}^{N}_{d_{\text{ES}}^{2}}
, by a canonical orthonormal basis
\{\left|\bm{t}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{S}_{N}}\}_{\bm{t}}
. We define an isometry between the two representations of the symmetric subspace, i.e., the normalized symmetric vectors in the full space
\text{A}_{1}^{N}
and the canonical basis for
\mathcal{S}_{N}
, as follows:

V_{N}=\sum_{\bm{t}}\left|\bm{t}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{S}_{N}}\left\langle\text{sym}(\bm{t})\right|_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}.

(53)

Note that this isometry acts as a projector in
\text{A}_{1}^{N}
, i.e.,
V_{N}{V_{N}}^{\dagger}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{S}_{N}}
but
{V_{N}}^{\dagger}V_{N}\neq\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
. Therefore, if
\text{P}^{+}_{N}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}=\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
, we may write it concisely as:

\begin{split}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}={V_{N}}^{\dagger}\phi{V_{N}}=\sum_{\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}}\phi_{\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}}\left|\text{sym}(\bm{t})\right\rangle\hskip-3.99994pt\left\langle\text{sym}(\bm{t}^{\prime})\right|_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}},\\
\phi=V_{N}\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}{V_{N}}^{\dagger},\quad\text{and}\quad x={V_{N}}(X^{\text{T}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}}){V_{N}}^{\dagger}.\end{split}

(54)

so that the objective function, which acts entirely within the symmetric subspace, can be written as

\operatorname{tr}\left[(X^{\text{T}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}})\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right]=\operatorname{tr}\left[(X^{\text{T}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}}){V_{N}}^{\dagger}\phi{V_{N}}\right]=\operatorname{tr}\left[{V_{N}}(X^{\text{T}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}}){V_{N}}^{\dagger}\phi\right]=\operatorname{tr}\left[x\phi\right].

(55)

Positivity of
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
can be guaranteed by requiring
\phi\geq 0
directly, as
\phi
and
\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}
have the same non-zero eigenvalues. With the chosen normalization of
C_{U}
, we may also write
\operatorname{tr}\left[\phi\right]=1
. In practice, it is sufficient to numerically compute
x
directly by means of
Eq.

54
, as computing the projection of
X^{\text{T}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\text{A}_{L+1}^{N}}
onto the symmetric subspace analytically can be cumbersome, and this computation only needs to be performed once before the numerical optimization.

To rewrite the partial trace constraints of the SDP directly in this symmetric basis, we must find a way to express partial traces in terms of the symmetric representation. In the following, we generalize some results known for qubits
[
Stockton et al.(2003)Stockton, Geremia, Doherty, and
Mabuchi
]
to arbitrary local dimension. But first, recall that the constraint we wish to rewrite is given by

\operatorname{tr}_{\text{O}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right]=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\text{I}_{1}}}{d_{\text{ES}}}\otimes\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\Phi_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}\right].

(56)

Treating types as ordinary vectors, we may define addition between types with the same

\bm{r}\in{\mathfrak{T}_{d}^{n}}
and

[
Stockton et al.(2003)Stockton, Geremia, Doherty, and

]
. Generalizing upon this idea, we may split an
n

k_{i}

\left|\text{Sym}(\bm{t})\right\rangle_{\text{A}_{1}^{N}}=\sum_{\bm{r}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{r}_{m}=\bm{t}}\left|\text{Sym}(\bm{r}_{1})\right\rangle_{\text{B}_{1}}\cdots\left|\text{Sym}(\bm{r}_{m})\right\rangle_{\text{B}_{m}},

(57)

where the sum is over all tuples

\text{B}_{\ell}:=\text{A}_{b_{\ell}+1}^{b_{\ell}+k_{\ell}}

(58)

However, working under such normalization is cumbersome and inefficient due to the several coefficients involved. Instead, we have chosen to normalize directly in terms of the original types
\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}

B.2
Sparse implementation of the SDP

In the following, we describe in detail the algorithm we have developed to obtain a sparse version of the SDP. This is an essential step in rendering the problem numerically tractable. The algorithm works by heuristically exploiting any existing sparsity of the original problem, in particular its objective function, in order to automatically construct a sparse outer approximation. In our particular case, the sparse SDP resulting from our algorithm was not only far simpler, it was in fact an exact sparse representation of the original problem. Below, we also discuss the general conditions required for this to be the case for our algorithm. We highlight that it is potentially applicable to many other SDPs, providing at least an outer approximation, although there are no guarantees that a sparse simplification of the original problem will be obtained.

To see how a problem’s sparsity can be exploited, consider a standard form SDP over
\mathbbm{C}^{n\times n}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{Given:}&\quad F,\;(C^{(k)})_{k},\;(b_{k})_{k}\\
\textbf{Find:}&\quad\max_{X}\;\langle F,X\rangle\\
\textbf{Subject to:}&\quad\langle C^{(k)},X\rangle=b_{k},\quad k=1,...,m\\
&\quad X\geq 0.\end{split}

(65)

where
\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{tr}\left[A^{\dagger}B\right]
. We assume
F
is Hermitian so that
F_{ij}\neq 0
implies
F_{ji}\neq 0
. Our goal is to exploit any sparsity of
F
, which defines the objective function, to simplify this problem by obtaining a suitable sparse version. This is achieved by finding a subset of variables
X_{ij}
which is self-sufficient to solve the problem, and this is done by keeping track of the indices
(i,j)
which can influence the objective function, directly or indirectly.

We begin by defining the initial
base sparsity
of the problem as

\theta_{\text{base}}^{0}:=\left\{\;(i,j)\;\;\middle|\;\;F_{ij}\neq 0\;\right\}\cup\{(i,i)\}_{i=1}^{n},

(66)

i.e., the pairs of indices with non-zero entries in
F
, together with the indices in the diagonal. We include the diagonal indices by default in order to ensure
X\geq 0
can be satisfied properly in the sparse version of the problem, as will be explained later. As
\langle F,X\rangle=\sum_{i,j}F^{*}_{ij}X_{ij}
, the set
\theta_{\text{base}}^{0}
corresponds to the entries of
X
which appear explicitly in the objective function, i.e., the optimization variables
X_{ij}
affecting its value, together with the diagonal entries. However, these are not the only variables relevant to the problem, as they also appear in linear constraints involving other variables not present in the objective, which may affect the objective function indirectly. Our next goal is to collect all of these indirect constraints to the objective function’s variables. Defining the subset of constraints
C^{(k)}
which relate to
\theta_{\text{base}}^{0}
as

\mathcal{K}^{0}:=\bigcup_{(i,j)\in\theta_{\text{base}}^{0}}\left\{\;k\;\;\middle|\;\;C^{(k)}_{ij}\neq 0\;\right\},

(67)

we can define the initial
extended sparsity

\theta_{\text{ext}}^{0}:=\bigcup_{k\in\mathcal{K}^{0}}\left\{\;(i,j)\;\;\middle|\;\;C^{(k)}_{ij}\neq 0\;\right\}.

(68)

In words, this set includes the indices for any variables present in a linear constraint also involving the variables directly affecting the objective function. The extended sparsity
\theta_{\text{ext}}^{0}
specifies an initial guess for the minimum subset of variables
\{X_{ij}\;|\;(i,j)\in\theta_{\text{ext}}^{0}\}
which should be considered in the SDP. The positivity constraint
X\geq 0
could then be specified, in principle, in terms of a block-diagonal matrix which contains the variables specified by
\theta_{\text{ext}}^{0}
within its blocks. Positivity of this block-diagonal matrix, and in turn of
X
, could then be achieved by enforcing positivity for each block separately.

As positivity constraints require significant computational resources, ideally these blocks should be made as small as possible. In order to obtain the minimal block-diagonal form, we first use
\theta_{\text{ext}}^{0}
to construct an adjacency matrix
A^{0}

[A^{0}]_{ij}:=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{if}\;(i,j)\in\theta_{\text{ext}}^{0}\\
0,\quad\text{else}\end{cases},

(69)

with which we construct an undirected graph
G(A^{0})
with
n
vertices, where each edge corresponds to a pair of indices
(i,j)
. We can use this graph to obtain a permutation which leads to the minimal block-diagonal form, as each block corresponds to a disjoint subset of indices which are interrelated. This in turn, corresponds to the notion of
connected components
of a graph, i.e., its disjoint subgraphs, as shown in
Fig.

7
. Let
\{g_{r}\}_{r=1}^{R}
be the
R
connected components of
G(A^{0})
, so that
G(A^{0})=\bigcup_{r=1}^{R}g_{r}
, and let
\nu_{r}
be the set of indices of the vertices in
g_{r}
. We may use the
\nu_{r}
to construct a permutation
\pi=\left(\nu_{1}\;\nu_{2}\;\cdots\;\nu_{R}\right)
, together with its associated permutation matrix
P
, such that
PA^{0}P^{\text{T}}
is block-diagonal, with one block per connected component. E.g., from
Fig.

7
, we could use the permutation
\pi=[(5273)(4)(16)]
, where the ordering of the
\nu_{r}
, or within each
\nu_{r}
, are irrelevant. This permutation leads to the block-diagonal adjacency matrix shown in figure
Fig.

8
, on the left.

Figure 7:
An adjacency matrix
A
and its corresponding graph
G(A)
, with three connected components. Non-zero entries are depicted with the colors (and markers) of the component they belong to. Self-edges, corresponding to
(i,i)
entries, are omitted.

Therefore, if we take
\theta_{\text{ext}}^{0}
to be the sparsity pattern for
X
, we can construct the sparse matrix

\widetilde{X}:=P^{\text{T}}\left(\bigoplus_{r=1}^{R}\widetilde{B}_{r}\right)P,\quad\widetilde{B}_{r}\geq 0\;\forall\,r,

(70)

with sparse blocks of variables given by
\widetilde{B}_{r}
. However, imposing positivity for each block is not always possible in this case, as the blocks themselves may have a sparse structure incompatible with positivity constraints. Concretely, there might be
no

\widetilde{X}\geq 0
matrix with sparsity structure given by
\widetilde{X}_{ij}=0\;\forall\;(i,j)\notin\theta_{\text{ext}}^{0}
.

Figure 8:
(Left) Under the permutation
\pi=[(5273)(4)(16)]
, the matrix
A
of
Fig.

7
becomes block-diagonal, with blocks possibly containing “gaps” indicating that not all pairs of vertices share an edge for that component. (Right) The blocks can be completed by adding the missing entries (i.e., edges).

To address this in general, we instead assume all the blocks are dense. We can then define the initial
completed sparsity

\theta_{\text{comp}}^{0}:=\bigcup^{R}_{r=1}\left\{\;(i,j)\in\nu_{r}\times\nu_{r}\;\right\},

(71)

by including all missing indices
(i,j)
within each block. In graph theoretical terms, we turn each connected component into a complete graph, i.e., every pair of vertices shares an edge, and use the non-zero entries of the resulting adjacency matrix to define
\theta_{\text{comp}}^{0}
; see
Fig.

8
, right panel. Denoting by
B_{r}
the dense block matrices, we can define the first sparse approximation of
X
as

\widetilde{X}^{0}:=P^{\text{T}}\left(\bigoplus_{r=1}^{R}B_{r}\right)P,\quad B_{r}\geq 0\;\forall\,r,

(72)

such that
\widetilde{X}^{0}\geq 0
can be safely imposed through block-positivity, with no risk of rendering the SDP unfeasible.

The next step is to analyze how this sparse structure affects the other constraints in the SDP. These procedures expanded our initial base sparsity
\theta_{\text{base}}^{0}
by including a larger set of indices (and therefore, variables) of the original dense
X
, resulting in
\theta_{\text{comp}}^{0}
. However, these additional variables may themselves appear in other linear constraints, involving even further variables we have not yet taken into account. These inter-dependencies between variables lead to a cascade of new constraints and variables that must be considered, i.e., the additional indices included in
\theta_{\text{comp}}^{0}
specify variables which appear in constraints which were previously ignored, as they did not involve variables specified by
\theta_{\text{base}}^{0}
. Therefore, we repeat the above procedures until a stable and completely self-contained set of indices naturally emerges, i.e., we define a new base sparsity
\theta^{1}_{\text{base}}:=\theta^{0}_{\text{comp}}
, and repeat the process
n+1
times until
\theta^{n+1}_{\text{base}}=\theta^{n}_{\text{base}}
, at which point we call this stable set the
effective sparsity

\theta^{*}
for the problem. In our particular applications, the algorithm converges within 2 to 4 iterations.

Note that, by construction, any variable
X_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in\theta^{*}
appears in the objective function directly, or if not, affects the variables in the objective either directly (through linear constraints) or indirectly (through positivity constraints and, in turn, through further constraints, and so on, recursively).
The goal of the above algorithm is to automatically discover a set of variables and constraints which is self-sufficient, given any objective function and linear constraints for an SDP.

While the algorithm generally provides an outer approximation, it can also lead to an exact sparse version of the original problem if the unused linear constraints are homogeneous, which is the case for the problem we consider. To understand why, we define the set of unused constraints as

\mathcal{K}^{*}_{\perp}:=\left\{\;k\;\;\middle|\;\;C^{(k)}_{ij}=0,\;\forall\;(i,j)\in{\theta^{*}}\;\right\}.

(73)

If the unused constraints are all homogeneous, i.e.,
b_{k}=0
for all
k\in\mathcal{K}^{*}_{\perp}
, then the original dense problem can be solved entirely within
\theta^{*}
, without affecting the objective function, as any sparse solution can be made into a feasible solution to the dense problem. This can be understood as follows. Let
X^{*}
be a solution to the sparse problem, and
X^{*}_{\perp}
a matrix in the complementary space
\theta^{*}_{\perp}
, containing all indices not appearing in
\theta^{*}
. Any constraints discarded by the algorithm (i.e., the indices in
\mathcal{K}^{*}_{\perp}
) will act entirely on
\theta^{*}_{\perp}
. Then, as the objective function contains only variables within
\theta^{*}
, we may write a dense solution
X=X^{*}+X^{*}_{\perp}
, such that

\operatorname{tr}\left[F^{\dagger}X\right]=\operatorname{tr}\left[F^{\dagger}X^{*}\right]+\operatorname{tr}\left[F^{\dagger}X^{*}_{\perp}\right]=\operatorname{tr}\left[F^{\dagger}X^{*}\right],

(74)

with
X^{*},X^{*}_{\perp}\geq 0
, and such that all constraints
\langle C^{(k)},X\rangle=b_{k}
uncouple onto orthogonal spaces. Now, if the linear constraints involving
X^{*}_{\perp}
are all homogeneous (
b_{k}=0
for all
k\in\mathcal{K}^{*}_{\perp}
), we may set
X^{*}_{\perp}=0
directly, while simultaneously satisfying all constraints in the dense problem, and without affecting the objective function. Therefore, any sparse solution
X^{*}
is also a valid solution to the dense problem, with the same value for the objective: the sparse problem is exact. Note that the diagonal indices needed to be included in
\theta_{\text{base}}^{0}
in order to ensure
X\geq 0
is satisfied because of
X^{*}\geq 0
, as otherwise positivity of
X
could always be satisfied for any
X^{*}
by choosing arbitrarily large diagonal entries in
X^{*}_{\perp}
.

Despite the success of this algorithm in giving a sparse solution to our particular problem, in general, there is no guarantee that
\theta^{*}
will be sparse. It may be the case that the algorithm eventually includes all entries of
X
, in which case an alternative approach must be used, e.g., stopping the algorithm before a stable sparsity pattern is reached (generally resulting in an outer approximation), using a more refined completion procedure (e.g., block-wise chordal completions
[
Zheng et al.(2021)Zheng, Fantuzzi, and Papachristodoulou
,
Fukuda et al.(2001)Fukuda, Kojima, Murota, and Nakata
]
), or exploiting additional structures of the original problem. Moreover, the sparsity of the original problem, and thus the efficacy of the above algorithm, relies heavily on the choice of basis in which the problem is represented. Fortunately, for our choice of parameters, the objective function and the resulting
\theta^{*}
were sufficiently sparse for the SDP to be numerically tractable. Furthermore, as the partial trace constraints
\operatorname{tr}_{\text{O}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[{V_{N}}^{\dagger}\phi V_{N}\right]=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\text{I}_{1}}}{d_{\text{ES}}}\otimes\operatorname{tr}_{\text{A}_{1}}\hskip-2.5pt\left[{V_{N}}^{\dagger}\phi V_{N}\right]
are all homogeneous, this approach lead to an exact sparse representation of
Eq.

15
.

Finally, we note that other techniques exist to formulate sparse SDPs
[
Zheng et al.(2021)Zheng, Fantuzzi, and Papachristodoulou
,
Fukuda et al.(2001)Fukuda, Kojima, Murota, and Nakata
]
, typically relying on the problem having an inherent “aggregate sparsity” (the joint sparse structure of
F
and all
C^{(k)}
taken together), or being analytically amenable to a sparser representation by an appropriate change of variables. In our problem, no such structure is a priori apparent: only the objective is inherently sparse, with the linear constraints jointly involving all variables in a non-trivial manner. In such cases, our algorithm is then a suitable technique to obtain an “effective” sparsity focused on the objective function’s inherent sparse structure, with the advantage of being more agnostic to the problem’s overall structure.

Appendix C
Maximum probability for closed systems

Here, we provide a proof for the analytical bounds for the
d_{\text{E}}=1
case mentioned in
Section

3
. To this end, recall that, in the sequential measurement protocol, the probability of a sequence of outcomes can be written as

p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}})=p(a_{1},\dots,a_{L}|d_{\text{E}})=\operatorname{tr}\left[\mathcal{M}_{a_{L}}\!\!\circ\mathcal{U}\!\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{M}_{a_{1}}\!\!\circ\mathcal{U}(\rho^{0}_{\text{E}}\!\otimes\!\rho^{0}_{\text{S}})\right],

(75)

with the measure-and-prepare map
\mathcal{M}_{a}(\rho_{ES})=\operatorname{tr}_{S}\hskip-2.5pt\left[\rho_{ES}\cdot(\mathbbm{1}_{E}\otimes E^{a}_{S})\right]\otimes\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
. If
d_{\text{E}}=1
, the unitary corresponds only to a local rotation on the system, and can be embedded in the state
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
and measurements
E_{S}^{a}
. Thus, the maximum depends only on the state and measurements. To compute it, we first note that since both
\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}
and
(E_{S}^{a})_{a}
are the same at each step and
\mathcal{M}_{a}
is a measure and prepare operation, all outcomes are independent and identically distributed. Writing
q_{a}=\operatorname{tr}\left[\rho^{0}_{\text{S}}E^{a}_{S}\right]
as the probability of outcome
a
, with
\sum_{a}q_{a}=1
, and using
n_{a}
as the number of occurrences of a symbol
a
in
\bm{a}
, we can write the probability as:

p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}}=1)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{a_{\ell}}=\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}q^{n_{a}}_{a},\qquad\text{with}\qquad n_{a}\in\mathbbm{N},\quad\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}n_{a}=L,\qquad q_{a}>0,\;\text{and}\;\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}q_{a}=1.

(76)

For simplicity, we assume that
\bm{a}
contains every symbol of
\mathcal{A}
at least once, such that
n_{a},q_{a}>0
. Otherwise, we could simply assume a smaller
\mathcal{A}
where this is the case. The maximum probability
\omega_{1}^{\bm{a}}
can be found with standard techniques, such as Lagrange multipliers. Using the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}=\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}q^{n_{a}}_{a}-\alpha\left(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}q_{a}-1\right),

(77)

we calculate the partial derivatives for each
q_{a}
, and equate them to zero:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_{a}}=\left(\frac{n_{a}}{q_{a}}\right)p-\alpha=0,\;\forall a.

(78)

Since
q_{a}>0
for all
a
,
p>0
, we may rewrite this as:

\frac{n_{a}}{q_{a}}=\frac{\alpha}{p}=\gamma\quad\to\quad q_{a}=\frac{n_{a}}{\gamma},\;\forall a.

(79)

Summing over
a
and applying the constraints
\sum_{a}q_{a}=1
and
\sum_{a}n_{a}=L
, we obtain:

\sum_{a}q_{a}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{a}n_{a}\quad\to\quad\gamma=L.

(80)

Thus, the maximum value is

\omega_{1}^{\bm{a}}=\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\frac{n_{a}}{L}\right)^{n_{a}},\qquad\text{for}\quad q_{a}=\frac{n_{a}}{L},\;\forall a

(81)

Note that this solution is unique if
q_{a}>0
and
p>0
. The only alternative solutions would require that
q_{a}=0
for some
a
, and thus
p=0<\omega_{1}^{\bm{a}}
. Therefore, this is indeed the global maximum.

We emphasize that this direct calculation of
\omega_{1}^{\bm{a}}
crucially depends on the specific form of the instrument we employ. If the respective outcomes do
not
correspond to a
\mathcal{M}_{a}
of measure-and-prepare form, the joint probabilities
p(\bm{a}|d_{\text{E}})
do not factorize like in
Eq.

75
, and an alternative approach must be used.
