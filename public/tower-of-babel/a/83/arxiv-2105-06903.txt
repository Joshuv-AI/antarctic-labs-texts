Posterior Regularization on Bayesian Hierarchical Mixture Clustering
Weipeng Fuzzy Huang, Tin Lok James Ng, Nishma Laitonjam, Neil J. Hurley

Abstract
Bayesian hierarchical mixture clustering (BHMC) improves traditional Bayesian hierarchical clustering by replacing conventional Gaussian-to-Gaussian kernels with a Hierarchical Dirichlet Process Mixture Model (HDPMM) for parent-to-child diffusion in the generative process. However, BHMC may produce trees with high nodal variance, indicating weak separation between nodes at higher levels. To address this issue, we employ Posterior Regularization, which imposes max-margin constraints on nodes at every level to enhance cluster separation. We illustrate how to apply PR to BHMC and demonstrate its effectiveness in improving the BHMC model.

Introduction

Hierarchical clustering (HC) is a well-known problem that aims to arrange data items into hierarchical structures.
The primary objective of hierarchical cluster analysis is to construct a “meaningful structure” for the chosen items that can either analyze the underlying relationships between the items (e.g., gene analysis) or aid in improving item retrieval (e.g., product catalogs on online retail websites).
In this context, we concentrate on the latter and strive for a structure that clusters similar items together and organizes the clusters hierarchically, allowing items in a child cluster to be viewed as variations of those in a parent cluster.

We study this problem through a Bayesian approach.
Our previous work
(
Huang et al 2021
)
proposes the
Bayesian Hierarchical Mixture Clustering
(BHMC) model, which is in the same framework as other Bayesian generative approaches
(
Williams 2000
;
Adams et al 2010
;
Neal 2003a
;
Knowles and Ghahramani 2015
)
.
The generative model consists of two components: (
i
) a random walk process from a root node to a leaf node; and (
ii
) a parent-to-child random diffusion that connects parameters in a parent node to those in a child node. The common choices for the first component are nested Chinese restaurant process (nCRP)
(
Blei et al 2010
;
Adams et al 2010
;
Huang et al 2021
)
, Dirichlet Diffusion Tree
(
Neal 2003a
)
, and Pitman-Yor Diffusion Tree
(
Knowles and Ghahramani 2015
)
, which are asymptotically equivalent
(
Knowles and Ghahramani 2015
)
.
For the second component, a Gaussian-to-Gaussian (G2G) kernel is commonly applied
(
Adams et al 2010
;
Neal 2003a
;
Knowles and Ghahramani 2015
)
, but the BHMC uses a Hierarchical Dirichlet Process Mixture Model (HDPMM) instead, making each node associated with a mixture model whose components become sparser on descent along a path in the hierarchy.

In this paper, our contribution is the development of a novel approach that incorporates Posterior Regularization (PR)
(
Graça et al 2009
;
Ganchev et al 2010
;
Zhu et al 2011
)
to enhance the separation of sibling clusters at every level of the hierarchy.
We introduce
posterior-regularized BHMC
or RBHMC, which utilizes the PR framework to handle Bayesian models with additional constraints.
The framework is founded on the variational inference (VI)
(
Blei et al 2017
)
of minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between a proposed distribution and the posterior in a constrained space.

A well-structured hierarchy should have clear separation, in particular at higher levels.
In the case of BHMC, this means that mixture components associated with each node should be close together geometrically, relative to their distance from components associated with other nodes on the same level.
However, the inference procedure can get stuck in local modes where high-level components are not sufficiently coherent.
We attribute this to the fact that the data is only generated at the leaf nodes, causing the inference to take a long time to influence higher-level mixture components.
To address this, we regularize the model to focus the optimization on distributions that exhibit the desired separation among nodes at the same level.
PR is employed to impose explicit data dependence on the choice of mixture components at every level, pushing the direct influence of data up to all levels along every path in the hierarchy.
We apply the max-margin property to nodes at every level, ensuring that the decision boundary separating the correct node for each observation is maximally distant from sibling nodes.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section
2
presents the background on Bayesian hierarchical mixture clustering and its limitations. Section
3
presents the posterior regularized solution to address the weaknesses of Bayesian hierarchical mixture clustering, and the statistical inference of the proposed model is presented in Section
4
. We conduct experimental studies to study the behavior of the proposed model.

2.1.1
Weaknesses of the BHMC

The study in
Huang et al 2021
shows that the BHMC performs well compared to other HC algorithms (either Bayesian or Non-Bayesian) based on commonly used evaluation metrics.
However, a weakness of the model is that it allows sibling nodes under a common parent to have shared components, which can result in poor separation and make it challenging to identify the correct path to a target item.
A more human understandable hierarchy can be achieved by ensuring reasonably good separation, resulting in better controlled nodal variance for any level.
The BHMC lacks a mechanism to ensure well-separated sibling nodes in the hierarchy.
An extreme example of a valid hierarchical clustering generated from the BHMC is illustrated in
Figure

4
.

Figure 4:
Hierarchical clustering with shared components.

Taking inspiration from work on posterior regularization (PR)
(
Graça et al 2009
;
Ganchev et al 2010
;
Zhu et al 2014
)
, we aim to overcome the limitation of the BHMC model by using PR to enforce sibling separation during Bayesian learning.
As noted in
Graça et al 2009
, incorporating even minor additional constraints into generative models can be highly challenging for the posterior inference.
However, by utilizing a posterior constraint, we can guide inference towards solutions that exhibit the desired separation without altering the generative process.

3
Posterior Regularized Solution

PR was developed upon VI for learning Bayesian models with constraints
(
Zhu et al 2014
)
.
The objective of VI is to minimize the KL-divergence (denoted by
\KL
) between a proposed distribution and the posterior distribution.
The PR framework includes a penalty function based on a set of feature constraints to the objective.
We use
\mathcal{M}
to denote an arbitrary model and
\E_{q}
to denote the expectation over a distribution
q
.

Definition 1

(RegBayes)
.

Let
q(\mathcal{M})
be a distribution proposed to approximate the posterior
p(\mathcal{M}\mid\mathbf{X})
.
The PR method is to solve

\displaystyle\min_{q(\mathcal{M}),\bm{\xi}}

\displaystyle\penalty\ \KL[q(\mathcal{M})\;\|\;p(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{X})]+U(\bm{\xi})\quad s.t.\penalty\ q(\mathcal{M})\in\mathbb{P}_{cs}(\bm{\xi})

(2)

where
p(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{X})
denotes the joint probability of the model
\mathcal{M}
and the data
\mathbf{X}
, and
U(\bm{\xi})
is a regularization function obtained upon the constraint space
\mathbb{P}_{cs}(\bm{\xi})
.

The constrained space
\mathbb{P}_{cs}(\bm{\xi})
is parameterized by
\bm{\xi}
which itself enters the objective function. In contrast to standard VI which performs unconstrained optimization over
q(\mathcal{M})
, the regularized Bayes solution differs in two ways: (1) the optimization over
q(\mathcal{M})
is performed in the constrained space
\mathbb{P}_{cs}(\bm{\xi})
, and (2) it performs joint optimization over
q(\mathcal{M})
and
\bm{\xi}
.

The conventional approach involves transforming the objective into its dual form.
The model variables are learned using VI, and the dual variables are solved using optimization tools.
However, the VI approach may result in a large number of dual variables and thus intractability in our model.
Fortunately, we can use the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method to infer the proposed model.

3.1
RBHMC

We aim to restrict the search space to solutions where the separation between the siblings is reasonably large.
To this end, we appeal to max-margin, which is used in e.g. support vector machine (SVM) classification to ensure adequate separation between classes.
To clarify its use in HC, it is beneficial to revisit its formulation in this context.

Starting from the simplest SVM binary classification, we consider that a observation
\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbf{X}
could be assigned with a label
\tau_{n}
where
\tau_{n}\in\{-1,1\}
.
Now, we augment it with a latent discriminant variable
\bm{\eta}
so that

\displaystyle\tau_{n}\left(\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\eta_{0}\right)\geq\varepsilon/2\implies\begin{cases}\left(\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\eta_{0}\right)\geq\varepsilon/2&\tau_{n}=1\\
-\left(\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\eta_{0}\right)\geq\varepsilon/2&\tau_{n}=-1\end{cases}\,.

Thus,
\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+\eta_{0}=0
is the hyperplane boundary that separates the two classes, and the data on the two sides are at least
\varepsilon/2
units away from the hyperplane
(
Hastie et al 2009
)
.
However, the data may not be linearly separable, so that no such boundary can be found. Thus, a non-negative slack variable
\xi_{n}\geq 0
is introduced and we write

\displaystyle\tau_{n}\left(\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\eta_{0}\right)\geq\varepsilon/2-\xi_{n}/2\,.

Hence,
\sum_{n}\xi_{n}
is thought of as the sum of the distances by which the data lie on the wrong side of their margin defined by
\varepsilon
.

However, modifications are required for handling multiple classes.
One method is to associate a different
\bm{\eta}
with each cluster.
This
\bm{\eta}
can be employed to form a boundary to separate the data in this cluster from the rest, namely, the one-vs-rest approach
(
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014
)
.
Considering a class denoted with
z
or
z^{\prime}
, where
z
is the node that observation
n
belongs to, the optimization will have the following constraints:

\begin{cases}\left(\bm{\eta}_{z}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\eta_{0}\right)\geq\varepsilon/2-\xi_{n}/2&\mbox{observation $n$ belongs to node $z$}\\
-\left(\bm{\eta}_{z^{\prime}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n}+\eta_{0}\right)\geq\varepsilon/2-\xi_{n}/2&\forall z^{\prime}:z^{\prime}\neq z\end{cases}\\
\implies\mathbb{P}_{cs}^{(n)}=\left\{(\bm{\eta}_{z}-\bm{\eta}_{z^{\prime}})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n}\geq\varepsilon-\xi_{n}\penalty\ \big|\penalty\ \forall z^{\prime}:z^{\prime}\neq z,\xi_{n}\geq 0\right\}

(3)

where
\mathbb{P}_{cs}^{(n)}
denotes the constraint space for observation
n
.
This work extends this idea to a multilevel case to fit the task of HC, where the constraint can be applied to clusters under the same parent.
Given the constraint space, we can apply the PR to restrict the search space for the Bayesian inference.

\displaystyle s.t.\quad

\displaystyle\forall n,\forall\ell,\forall z\in\mathcal{S}(v_{n\ell}):\xi_{n}\geq\varepsilon_{n\ell}^{\Delta}-\E_{q}[\bm{\eta}_{v_{n\ell}}-\bm{\eta}_{z}]^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n},\xi_{n}\geq 0\,.

6
can thus be equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle\min_{q(\mathcal{M})}\penalty\ \KL[q(\mathcal{M})\;\|\;p(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{X})]+2C\sum_{n}\max\left(0,\max_{\ell,z\in\mathcal{S}(v_{n\ell})}\E_{q}\left[\rho_{n\ell z}\right]\right)\,.

(9)

This is the standard technique for SVM to transform the constrained problem to be unconstrained
(
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014
)
.

Solving
Equation

9
is non-trivial due to the regularization term.
An alternative is to minimize an upper bound of the term as a surrogate.
To derive the bound, we first note that the maximum function
\max(\bm{a})
where
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is known to be convex.
In addition, given a convex function
h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
,
\max(const.,h(\bm{a}))
is also convex on
\bm{a}

(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
.
Therefore, we obtain

\max\left(0,\max_{\ell,z\in\mathcal{S}(v_{n\ell})}\E_{q}\left[\rho_{n\ell z}\right]\right)\leq\E_{q}\left[\max\left(0,\max_{\ell,z\in\mathcal{S}(v_{n\ell})}\rho_{n\ell z}\right)\right]\,.

Finally, we solve the following objective,

\displaystyle\min_{q(\mathcal{M})}\penalty\ \KL[q(\mathcal{M})\;\|\;p(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{X})]+2C\sum_{n}\E_{q}\left[\max\left(0,\max_{\ell,z\in\mathcal{S}(v_{n\ell})}\rho_{n\ell z}\right)\right]\,.

(10)

Heuristically, the above regularization minimizes the maximal margin violation between any node in the assigned path from its siblings among all levels.

One can obtain an analytical solution to
q(\mathcal{M})
through employing the variational derivations over the objective.
For instance,
Chen et al 2014
proved that

\displaystyle q^{*}(\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{q(\mathcal{M})}\penalty\ \KL[q(\mathcal{M})\;\|\;p(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{X})]+2C\sum_{n}\E_{q(\mathcal{M})}\left[\max\left(0,\max(\bm{\rho})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\propto p(\mathcal{M}\mid\mathbf{X})\prod_{n}\exp\left\{-2C\max\left(0,\max(\bm{\rho})\right)\right\}\,.

The proof establishes a connection between the objective and the Euler-Lagrange equation, and subsequently derives the updates by setting the derivatives to
0
.

In our case, we specify
\bm{\rho}=\left\{\rho_{n\ell z}\mid\forall n,\forall\ell,\forall z\in\mathcal{S}(v_{n\ell})\right\}
.
The optimal solution of the objective,
q^{*}(\mathcal{M})
, is defined as

\displaystyle q^{*}(\mathcal{M})\propto p(\mathcal{M}\mid\mathbf{X})\prod_{n}\exp\left\{-2C\max\left(0,\max_{\ell,z\in\mathcal{S}(v_{n\ell})}\rho_{n\ell z}\right)\right\}\,.

(11)

Finally, the process to infer
q^{*}(\mathcal{M})
will be derived based upon this formulation.

4
Inference

The RBHMC incorporates both the PR-imposed random variables and the original random variables from the BHMC, which were fully analyzed in
Huang et al 2021
.
Thus, this section primarily focuses on analyzing the PR-imposed variables and their associated sampling steps.
Next, we review the sampling details of the BHMC and connect all components to illustrate the complete inference procedure. Finally, we discuss the criterion for selecting the output hierarchy to conclude this section on algorithm implementation.

4.1
Analyzing the PR-imposed variables

In
Equation

11
, a non-trivial regularization term

\prod_{n}\exp\left\{-2C\max\left(0,\max_{\ell,z\in\mathcal{S}(v_{n\ell})}\rho_{n\ell z}\right)\right\}

is introduced.
One has to decompose the term for sampling the random variables
\bm{\rho}
.
This can be achieved through data augmentation
(
Polson and Scott 2011
;
Chen et al 2014
)
.

Recall the definition of
\bm{\rho}
in
Equation

8
and its usage in
Equation

9
.
We define
s_{n}=(\ell,z)
, a tuple encapsulating the level
\ell
and the sibling
z
that maximizes
\rho_{n\ell z}
, i.e.

s_{n}:=(s_{n1},s_{n2}):=(\ell,z):=\argmax_{\ell,z\in\mathcal{S}(v_{n\ell})}\rho_{n\ell z}

such that
s_{n}
is a deterministic function of the random variables
\bm{v}_{n},\bm{\eta}
and
\mathbf{x}_{n}
.
The corresponding set is written as
\mathbf{s}=\{s_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
.
Accordingly,
\rho_{ns_{n}}
represents the maximum
\rho_{n\ell z}
corresponding to that tuple.

Polson and Scott 2011
demonstrated that, for any arbitrary real
\rho
,

\displaystyle\exp\{-2C\max(0,\rho)\}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(C\rho+\lambda)^{2}}{2\lambda}\right\}d\lambda\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}p(\lambda\mid\rho)d\lambda

where
p(\lambda\mid\rho)
is the density function of the Generalized Inverse Gaussian (GIG) distribution
\gig(\lambda;1/2,1,C^{2}\rho^{2})
, defined as

\displaystyle\gig(x;\kappa,a,b)\propto x^{\kappa-1}\exp\left\{-{\left(ax+{b/x}\right)}/{2}\right\}\,

(12)

where
a>0,b>0
.
The set of augmented variables
\bm{\lambda}=\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
hence follows

\displaystyle p(\lambda_{n}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{v}_{n},\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(C\rho_{ns_{n}}+\lambda_{n})^{2}}{2\lambda_{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{[C(\varepsilon_{0}\mathbbm{1}(s_{n2}\neq v_{ns_{n1}})-(\bm{\eta}_{v_{ns_{n1}}}-\bm{\eta}_{s_{n2}})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n})+\lambda_{n}]^{2}}{2\lambda_{n}}\right\}\,.

(13)

One can further derive that

q^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bm{\eta},\bm{\lambda})\propto p(\mathbf{B},\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta})\\
\prod_{n}p\left(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\bm{v}_{n},c_{n},\mathbf{B},\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta}\right)p(c_{n}\mid\bm{\beta}_{v_{nL}},\bm{v}_{n})p(\lambda_{n}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{v}_{n},\bm{\eta})p(\bm{v}_{n})\,.

(14)

It follows that the target posterior
q^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bm{\eta})
can be approached by sampling from
q^{*}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bm{\eta},\bm{\lambda})
and dropping the augmented variables.

4.2
Sampling the PR-imposed variables

Based on the above decomposition, we specify the corresponding sampling strategy for all variables.
Note that, although sampling paths is similar to that in
Huang et al 2021
, the change of the employed likelihood function leads to sampling deviations at the detailed level.

Sampling
\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{s}

There could be numerous divergence points to new paths.
As a result, sampling the path through a Gibbs step may be impractical, and even a slice sampling scheme
(
Neal 2003b
;
Walker 2007
;
Kalli et al 2011
)
could suffer from intractability, especially when the hyperparameters
L
and
\alpha
jointly create a large initial tree.
The tree data structure is memory-intensive, even for moderately sized datasets, making it difficult to “downsize” the path candidate pool using the posterior slice threshold—a common strategy for slice sampling.
Determining an appropriate initial candidate pool size that ensures expected performance requires independent research, which we leave to future work.
Therefore, we opt to use the Metropolis-Hastings (MH) sampling for the path, similar to the approach detailed in
Huang et al 2021
.

In the RBHMC, the MH scheme proposes a new path
\bm{v}_{n}
using nCRP, along with a deterministic
s_{n}
and new
\bm{\beta}
if the path samples new nodes.
The proposal is accepted with the acceptance probability
\mathcal{A}
.
However, MH can be combined with partially collapsed Gibbs to improve convergence efficiency, as pointed out by
van Dyk and Park 2011
.
Adopting the approach in
Huang et al 2021
, we consider an MH step to sample
\mathbf{V}
with
\mathbf{c}
marginalized, followed immediately by a step sampling
\mathbf{c}
from the complete conditional.
This order adopts
van Dyk and Jiao 2015
’s paradigm to preserve the stationary distribution of the chain in an inference combining MH and partially collapsed Gibbs steps.

Let us consider a joint (regularized) likelihood
\tilde{f}(\cdot)
of
\mathbf{x}_{n}
and
\lambda_{n}
which can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{f}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n},{\lambda}_{n};\bm{v}_{n},\mathbf{B},\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta}\right)=p(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\bm{v}_{n},\bm{\beta}_{v_{nL}},\bm{\theta})p(\lambda_{n}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{v}_{n},\bm{\eta})\,.

(15)

The first term is the marginal likelihood that collapses
\mathbf{c}
, and the second term is given by
Section

4.1
.
A new cluster is associated with the predictive likelihood,
f^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{n})
, implied by the base measure
H
, such that
f^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{n})=\int f(\mathbf{x}_{n};\theta)dp(\theta\mid H)
. Hence, summing over all
K
existing clusters as well as the possibility of generating a new cluster, we obtain

p(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\bm{v}_{n},\bm{\theta},\bm{\beta}_{v_{nL}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{v_{nL}k}f(\mathbf{x}_{n};\theta_{k})+\beta_{v_{nL}}^{*}f^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{n})\,.

Following
Equation

14
, the marginal posterior
q^{*}_{\neg\mathbf{c}}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bm{\eta},\bm{\lambda})
which collapses
\mathbf{c}
, satisfies

q^{*}_{\neg\mathbf{c}}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bm{\eta},\bm{\lambda})\propto p(\mathbf{B},\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta})\prod_{n}p\left(\mathbf{x}_{n}\mid\bm{v}_{n},\mathbf{B},\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta}\right)p(\lambda_{n}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n},\bm{v}_{n},\bm{\eta})p(\bm{v}_{n})\,.

(16)

Now, the acceptance probability of the MH scheme is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\min\left(1,\penalty\ \frac{q^{*}_{\neg\mathbf{c}}(\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\prime},\bm{\eta},\bm{\lambda})\psi(\bm{v}_{n},\mathbf{B},{s}_{n}\mid\bm{v}_{n}^{\prime},\mathbf{B}^{\prime},{s}_{n}^{\prime})}{q^{*}_{\neg\mathbf{c}}(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bm{\eta},\bm{\lambda})\psi(\bm{v}_{n}^{\prime},\mathbf{B}^{\prime},{s}_{n}^{\prime}\mid\bm{v}_{n},\mathbf{B},{s}_{n})}\right)

\displaystyle=\min\left(1,\penalty\ \frac{\tilde{f}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n},\lambda_{n};\bm{v}_{n}^{\prime},\mathbf{B}^{\prime},\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta}\right)}{\tilde{f}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n},\lambda_{n};\bm{v}_{n},\mathbf{B},\bm{\theta},\bm{\eta}\right)}\right)

where the prime denoted variables are those for modification given the new proposal and
\psi(\cdot)
denotes the density of the proposal distribution.
The derivation detail is available in
Section

7
.

Sampling
\eta

With some algebraic manipulation, it may be shown that the discriminant variable
\bm{\eta}_{z}
can be sampled as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}_{z}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{s},\bm{\lambda},\mathcal{M}\setminus\{\bm{\eta}_{z}\}\sim\N\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{z}^{-1}\bm{\nu}_{z},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{z}^{-1}\right)\,,

(17)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{C\lambda_{n}}+C^{2}\varepsilon_{0}

(18a)

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\left\{\sum_{n:v_{n\ell}=z}\left(\tilde{C}_{n}+C^{2}\bm{\eta}_{s_{n2}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n}\right)-\sum_{n:s_{n2}=z}\left(\tilde{C}_{n}-C^{2}\bm{\eta}_{v_{n\ell}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{n}\right)\right\}\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\top}

(18b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Lambda}_{z}

\displaystyle=\nu_{0}^{2}\mathbf{I}+{C^{2}}\sum_{n}\mathbbm{1}(v_{n\ell}=z\penalty\ ||\penalty\ s_{n2}=z)\frac{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\top}}{\lambda_{n}}\,.

(18c)

The derivation steps are provided in
Section

8
.

Sampling
\lambda

Equation

12
facilitates sampling
\lambda_{n}
by
\lambda_{n}\mid\rho_{ns_{n}}\sim\gig(1/2,1,C^{2}\rho_{ns_{n}}^{2})
.
However, rather than sampling
\lambda_{n}
directly, one can sample the reciprocal of
\lambda_{n}
from an Inverse Gamma (IG) distribution:

\lambda_{n}^{-1}\mid\rho_{ns_{n}},s_{n},\bm{v}_{n}\sim\ig\left({\lvert C\rho_{ns_{n}}\rvert}^{-1},1\right)\,

(19)

as
Polson and Scott 2011
showed that,
x\sim\gig(1/2,a,a/b^{2})
is equivalent to
x^{-1}\sim\ig(\lvert b\rvert,a)
.
Notably, RBHMC reverts to BHMC when
C\to 0^{+}
.

We review the sampling steps for the random variables in the BHMC and hence connect the sampling steps for all variables and show a full algorithm.

H
to be a multivariate normal distribution such that
H=\N(\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0})

To apply posterior conjugacy, one could then also set
F

\theta

\bm{\mu}_{k}

.
Furthermore, we write

.

4.4
Output hierarchy

Adams et al 2010
;
Huang et al 2021
propose to output the hierarchies with the highest complete data log likelihood.
For estimation or application, one might prefer presenting the hierarchy with the maximal posterior or likelihood during a finite number of MCMC draws.
However, there are interesting discussions on replacing the maximal posterior with other well-established criteria for clustering, e.g.
Rastelli and Friel 2018
;
Wade and Ghahramani 2018
, etc.
Their research focus is on flat clustering and we leave as a future research challenge to explore a better Bayesian criterion for choosing the output hierarchy.

We follow
Adams et al 2010
;
Huang et al 2021
and still apply the complete data likelihood to select the output hierarchy.
This approach applies the regularized complete data log likelihood (RCDLL) which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{RCDLL}=\sum_{n}\log p(\mathbf{x}_{n},{c}_{n},\bm{v}_{n}\mid\mathcal{M}\setminus\{\mathbf{c},\mathbf{V}\})-2C\max(0,\rho_{ns_{n}})\,.

(20)

For presentation, any node with no siblings will be merged with its parent, and the empty nodes containing no observations will be excluded.

Design of sensitivity analysis

This analysis focuses on the regularization hyperparameters
C
and
\varepsilon_{0}
, while taking into account a hyperprior for the PR imposed variable
\bm{\eta}
.
An overly large value of
C
would dominate the regularization term and result in uniform assignment, whereas a very small value would make the model similar to the original BHMC.
In interpretation,
\varepsilon_{0}
can be interpreted as the largest unnormalized margin between the closest observation and the hyperplane
\bm{\eta}
.
A small value of
\varepsilon_{0}
may not constrain the model effectively, and a large value may penalize certain path assignments unnecessarily.
The analysis aims to explore the sensitivity of the hyperparameters and the approach’s capability to obtain improvements.

Hence, the analysis aims to explore (
i
) the sensitivity of the hyperparameters; (
ii
) the approach’s capability to obtain improvements.
We examine two simple measures, which are

•

Average Inner Distance
(AID):
the average empirical squared
L_{2}
distance within the nodes,

•

Average Outer Distance
(AOD):
the average centroid squared
L_{2}
distance between the siblings.

These scores will be only averaged over the non-empty nodes.
Regarding each hyperparameter configuration, the analysis is repeated
30
times.
We then write
\mathrm{AID}
as the expectation over
\mathrm{AID}_{single}
, and likewise for AOD.
Specifically,

\displaystyle\mathrm{AID}_{single}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|Z_{\mathcal{H}}\setminus\{z_{0}\}|}\sum_{z\neq z_{0}}\frac{2\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{n^{\prime}},\mathbf{x}_{n}\in z}\mathbbm{1}(n>n^{\prime})\lVert\mathbf{x}_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{n^{\prime}}\rVert^{2}_{2}}{N_{z}(N_{z}-1)}\,.

Let
\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{z}
denote the centroid in a node
z
where
\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{z}=\frac{1}{N_{z}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\in z}\mathbf{x}_{n}
.
We write
\mathcal{SP}
to denote a set maintaining the ordered
sibling pairs
under any parent node in the tree.
AOD is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{AOD}_{single}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{SP}|}\sum_{(z,z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{SP}}\lVert\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{z}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{z^{\prime}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

It is possible to generate a random tree with only one child under each parent, known as a singular path, which can cause AOD to fail.
However, any sampling steps that result in this scenario after the burn-in phase will be disregarded.
Due to the
the-rich-get-richer
property of nCRP, once a singular sample is drawn, subsequent samples in the chain are more likely to be singular.
Fortunately, these occurrences are extremely rare.

6
Conclusion

To address the shortcoming of the BHMC model, which may have high nodal variance at higher levels of hierarchy, this paper proposes applying PR to impose max-margin constraints on the hierarchical structure.
The paper details the modeling and inference procedures for an improved Bayesian HC algorithm, called RBHMC. Empirical studies show its advantages over the original BHMC model and its ability to achieve desired improvements.
The authors suggest further investigation and application of this methodology to a wider range of Bayesian tasks.
Additionally, exploring other constraint space constructions and regularized functions would be beneficial.
Finally, extending the approach to handle large-scale problems is essential.

F-measure for Clustering

Let
\{\bm{d}_{1},\dots,\bm{d}_{I}\}
be the set of
I
ground truth classes from the data where
\bm{d}_{i}
stores all data labeled with class
i
.
Apart from that,
\{\bm{\omega}_{1},\dots,\bm{\omega}_{J}\}
denotes the
J
predicted clusters generated by some algorithm where
\bm{\omega}_{j}
stores all data that are assigned to cluster
j
.
Let
N_{ij}
be the number of datum overlapping among the ground truth class
i
and the predicted cluster
j
where
N_{ij}=\lvert\bm{d}_{i}\cap\bm{\omega}_{j}\rvert
.
Also, we denote
N_{i\cdot}=\sum_{j}N_{ij}
and
N_{\cdot j}=\sum_{i}N_{ij}
.
This score is composed of precision (prec) and recall where

\displaystyle\mathrm{prec}(i,j)=\frac{N_{ij}}{N_{\cdot j}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathrm{recall}(i,j)=\frac{N_{ij}}{N_{i\cdot}}\,.

Finally the measure is defined by

\displaystyle\mbox{F-measure}=\sum_{i}\frac{N_{i\cdot}}{n}\max_{j}\frac{2\left[\mathrm{recall}(i,j)\times\mathrm{prec}(i,j)\right]}{\mathrm{recall}(i,j)+\mathrm{prec}(i,j)}\,.
