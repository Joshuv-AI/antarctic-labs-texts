Abstract
. For a given unitary operator U U on a separable complex Hilbert space ℋ \mathcal{H} , we describe the set 𝒞 c ​ ( U ) \mathscr{C}_{c}(U) of all conjugations C C (antilinear, isometric, and involutive maps) on ℋ \mathcal{H} for which C ​ U ​ C = U CUC=U . As this set might be empty, we also show that 𝒞 c ​ ( U ) ≠ ∅ \mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing if and only if U U is unitarily equivalent to U ∗ U^{*} .

1.
Introduction

This is the second in a series of two papers that explore conjugations of unitary operators on separable complex Hilbert spaces. The first paper
[
21
]
in this series explored, for a given unitary operator
U
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, the antilinear, isometric, and involutive maps
C
on
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,
conjugations
, for which
CUC=U^{*}
. An argument with the spectral theorem says there will always be a conjugation
C
with this property. Moreover,
[
21
]
contains several characterizations of the set of
all
such conjugations
C
for which
CUC=U^{*}
. These conjugations are known as the “symmetric conjugations” for
U
.

The purpose of this second paper is to explore, for a given unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, the set

(1.1)

\mathscr{C}_{c}(U):=\{\mbox{$C$ is a conjugation on $\mathcal{H}$}:CUC=U\}.

These are known as the “commuting conjugations” for
U
. The subscript
c
in the definition of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
might initially seem superfluous but we will use it anyway to distinguish this set from
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
(notice the
s
in the subscript), the “symmetric conjugations” mentioned in the previous paragraph.
For an easy example of a commuting conjugation, consider the unitary operator
(Uf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
, the bilateral shift on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
, where
m
is normalized Lebesgue measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. One can check that the map

(Jf)(\xi)=\overline{f(\overline{\xi})}

on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
defines a conjugation which satisfies
JUJ=U
. Moreover (see Example
7.13
), any conjugation
C
on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
for which
CUC=U
takes the form
(Cf)(\xi)=u(\xi)(Jf)(\xi)
, where
u\in L^{\infty}(m,\mathbb{T})
is both unimodular and satisfies

u(\xi)=u(\overline{\xi})
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. An analogous result holds when
(U\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=\xi\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)
on the vector-valued Lebesgue space
\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{H})
(Theorem
6.4
) but not always on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
for a general positive measure on
\mathbb{T}
(see §
5
and the discussion below).

The first issue one needs to resolve is whether, for a given unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, there are
any
conjugations
C
for which
CUC=U
. Indeed, using the known fact from
[
17
]
(see also Proposition
2.8
below) that any unitary operator can be written as a composition of two conjugations, one can fashion a quick argument (see Lemma
2.9
) to see that if
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
, then
U\cong U^{*}
(i.e.,
U
is unitarily equivalent to its adjoint
U^{*}
). One of the main results of this paper (Corollary
8.9
) is the converse.

Theorem 1.2
.

For a unitary operator
U
on a complex separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
;

(b)

U\cong U^{*}
.

Notice how condition (b) in Theorem
1.2
places some restrictions on the class of unitary operators which have commuting conjugations in that, at the very least, the spectrum
\sigma(U)
of
U
must be symmetric with respect to the real axis, i.e.,
\lambda\in\sigma(U)
if and only if
\overline{\lambda}\in\sigma(U)
(since if
U\cong U^{*}
then
\sigma(U)=\sigma(U^{*})=\overline{\sigma(U)}
). Thus, as an example, for the bilateral shift
(Uf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
on
L^{2}(\mu,\mathbb{T})
, where
\mu
is a finite positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
, a standard argument shows that

\sigma(U)=\{\xi\in\mathbb{T}:\mu(I_{\delta}(\xi))>0
for all
\delta>0\}
(
I_{\delta}(\xi)
is the arc of the circle centered at
\xi
of radius
\delta
). Thus, for example, if the measure
\mu
is supported on the top half of
\mathbb{T}
, then there are
no
conjugations
C
on
L^{2}(\mu,\mathbb{T})
for which
CUC=U
. Of course, one could also consider the easy example of a unitary matrix whose eigenvalues are not symmetric with respect to the real axis.

For a unitary operator
U
on a finite dimensional Hilbert space, where we can regard, via a matrix representation with respect to an orthonormal basis,
U
as a unitary matrix, we can use the linear algebra version of the spectral theorem to see that
U\cong U^{*}
if and only if

U=W\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}I_{n_{1}}&\\
&\overline{\xi_{1}}I_{n_{1}}\end{bmatrix}&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&&\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{d}I_{n_{d}}&\\
&\overline{\xi_{d}}I_{n_{d}}\end{bmatrix}\\
&&&&\begin{bmatrix}I_{\ell}&\\
&-I_{k}\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}W^{*},

where
W
is a unitary matrix,
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d}\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{1,-1\}
are distinct eigenvalues of
U
,
I_{m}
detnoes the
m\times m
identity matrix, and the block in the lower right corner might not appear, or might appear as just
I_{\ell}
or just
-I_{k}
, depending on whether
1
or
-1
are eigenvalues of
U
. Of course
n_{j}
,
\ell
, and
k
represent the multiplicities of their respective eigenvalues. As we will prove in Theorem
3.6
, such unitary matrices satisfy
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
and every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
takes the form

C=W\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}&V_{1}\\
V_{1}^{t}&\end{bmatrix}&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&&\begin{bmatrix}&V_{d}\\
V_{d}^{t}&\end{bmatrix}\\
&&&&\begin{bmatrix}Q_{\ell}&\\
&Q_{k}\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}JW^{*},

where each
V_{j}
is an
n_{j}\times n_{j}
unitary matrix,
Q_{\ell}
,
Q_{k}
are
\ell\times\ell
and
k\times k
(respectively) unitary matrices with
Q_{\ell}^{t}=Q_{\ell}
and
Q_{k}^{t}=Q_{k}
(in which only one or perhaps both might not appear depending whether
1
or
-1
are eigenvalues of
U
), and
J
is the conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by
J{\bf x}=\overline{{\bf x}}
(complex conjugating each of the entries of
{\bf x}
).

Another basic type of unitary operator is
(U\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=\xi\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
, where
\mu
is a positive finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space (see §
5
for the precise definitions). As discussed earlier, there might not be any commuting conjugations for
U
. In §
5
we discuss the restrictions one must place on
\mu
so that
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
and, when these conditions are satisfied, describe
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
. Since the operators
\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)\mapsto\xi\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)
on these
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
spaces are the building blocks for any unitary operator on a general Hilbert space, via the spectral theorem, we describe the commuting conjugations (when they exist) for a general unitary operator in §
8
(where we also prove Theorem
1.2
).

A particularly interesting class of unitary operators are the multiplication operators
M_{\psi}f=\psi f
on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
where
\psi
is an inner function. Here one has the added connection to the theory of model spaces
H^{2}\cap(\psi H^{2})^{\perp}

[
11
]
. As discussed in
[
22
]
, these multiplication operators serve as models for general bilateral shifts. In §
7
we show that
M_{\psi}
is unitarily equivalent to its adjoint (and hence
\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\psi})\not=\varnothing
via Theorem
1.2
) and proceed to give a concrete description of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\psi})
(Theorem
7.10
).

In the last section of this paper, we work out a concrete description of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(\mathcal{F})
for the classical Fourier–Plancherel transform
\mathscr{F}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(Example
9.3
) and a description of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(\mathscr{H})
for the classical Hilbert transform
\mathscr{H}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(Example
9.5
).

2.
Basics facts about conjugations

All Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
in this paper are separable and complex.
Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the space of all bounded linear transformations on
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the space of all
bounded antilinear transformations
on
\mathcal{H}
. By this we mean that

C\in\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
when

C({\bf x}+\alpha{\bf y})=C{\bf x}+\overline{\alpha}C{\bf y}
for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
(
C
is antilinear)
and
\sup\{\|C{\bf x}\|:\|{\bf x}\|=1\}
is finite (
C
is bounded).
We say that
C\in\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a
conjugation
if it satisfies the additional conditions that
\|C{\bf x}\|=\|{\bf x}\|
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
(
C
is isometric) and
C^{2}=I
(
C
is involutive).
By the polarization identity, a conjugation also satisfies

(2.1)

\langle C{\bf x},C{\bf y}\rangle=\langle{\bf y},{\bf x}\rangle\;\mbox{for all ${\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}.$}

Conjugations play an important role in operator theory and were initially studied in
[
9
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. More recently, conjugations were explored in
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
8
,
20
,
24
]
.

Example 2.2
.

Many types of conjugations were outlined in
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Below are a few basic ones that are relevant to this paper.

(a)

The mapping
Cf=\bar{f}
defines a conjugation on a standard Lebesgue space
L^{2}(\mu,X)
. In particular, the mapping

C[x_{1}\;x_{2}\;\cdots\;x_{n}]^{t}=[\overline{x_{1}}\;\overline{x_{2}}\;\cdots\;\overline{x_{n}}]^{t}

defines a conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Throughout this paper we will use the symbol
t
to represent the transpose of a matrix. In addition, vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
will be viewed as column vectors since, for an
n\times n
matrix
A
of complex numbers, we will often consider linear transformations on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by
{\bf x}\mapsto A{\bf x}
.

(b)

The mapping
(Cf)(\xi)=\overline{f(\overline{\xi})}
defines a conjugation on
L^{2}(\mu,\mathbb{T})
for any finite positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
.

(c)

On
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
one can consider the two conjugations

(Cf)(t)=\overline{f(t)}
and
(Cf)(t)=\overline{f(-t)}
.
These were used in
[
1
,
2
]
to study symmetric operators and their connections to physics.

This next lemma enables us to transfer a conjugation on one Hilbert space to a conjugation on another. The (easy) proof is left to the reader.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
are Hilbert spaces and
V:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}
is a unitary operator. If
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
then
VCV^{*}
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{K}
.

Example 2.4
.

We have already discussed the how the mapping
(Cf)(\xi)=\overline{f(\overline{\xi})}
on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
is a conjugation that commutes with the bilateral shift
(Uf)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
. Here are a few other examples.

(a)

The conjugation
(Cf)(x)=\overline{f(x)}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
commutes with the unitary operator
(Uf)(x)=f(x-1)
. This conjugation also commutes with the Hilbert transform.

(b)

The conjugation
(Cf)(x)=\overline{f(-x)}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
commutes with the Fourier–Plancherel transform.

Recalling the definition of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
from (
1.1
), let us make a few elementary observations. One can argue from (
2.1
) that

(2.5)

\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)=\mathscr{C}_{c}(U^{*}).

Next we comment that the commuting conjugations are stable under unitary equivalence.

Proposition 2.6
.

Suppose
U,V,W
are unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
such that
WUW^{*}=V
. Then
W\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)W^{*}=\mathscr{C}_{c}(V)
.

If
U
is unitary and
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
, then
UC\in\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and is isometric. This next result has a straightforward proof and determines when
UC
is involutive and hence a conjugation.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator and
C
be a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
. Then
UC
is a conjugation if and only if
CUC=U^{*}
.

We recall the following result from
[
17
]
(also see the proof of Proposition 2.5 from
[
21
]
) which shows that any unitary operator can be built from conjugations.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
. Then there are conjugations
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
U=J_{1}J_{2}.

Moreover,
J_{1}UJ_{1}=U^{*}
and
J_{2}UJ_{2}=U^{*}
.

In the introduction we showed that although every unitary operator
U
satisfies
CUC=U^{*}
with respect to some conjugation
C
, it is possible for
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
(the commuting conjugations for
U
) to be the empty set. Below we begin to determine when this happens (and bring this discussion to fruition in Corollary
8.9
).

Lemma 2.9
.

If
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
, then
U\cong U^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
J_{1}
be as in Proposition
2.8
,
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
, and define
V=J_{1}C
. Clearly
V
is unitary (since it is linear, isometric, and onto) and

VU=J_{1}CU=J_{1}UC=U^{*}J_{1}C=U^{*}V
. Thus,
U\cong U^{*}
.
∎

3.
Commuting conjugations of unitary matrices

For an
n\times n
unitary matrix
U
, the condition as to when
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
is nonempty, along with the description of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
, is straightforward and so we work it out in this separate section. We begin with the following result from
[
16
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

A mapping
C
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a conjugation if and only if
C=VJ
, where
V
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix with
V^{t}=V
and
J
is the conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by

(3.2)

J[x_{1}\;x_{2}\;\cdots\;x_{n}]^{t}=[\overline{x_{1}}\;\overline{x_{2}}\;\cdots\;\overline{x_{n}}]^{t},

i.e.,

C[x_{1}\;x_{2}\;\cdots\;x_{n}]^{t}=V[\overline{x_{1}}\;\overline{x_{2}}\;\cdots\;\overline{x_{n}}]^{t}.

We now establish when
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
for an
n\times n
unitary matrix
U
. This is a special case of Theorem
1.2
.

Proposition 3.3
.

For an
n\times n
unitary matrix
U
the following are equivalent.

(a)

\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
;

(b)

U\cong U^{*}
.

Proof.

The implication
(a)\Longrightarrow(b)
is from Lemma
2.9
. For the implication
(b)\Longrightarrow(a)
, suppose that
U\cong U^{*}
. As mentioned in the introduction, the spectral theorem for unitary matrices implies that
U
is unitarily equivalent to

(3.4)

U^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}I_{n_{1}}&\\
&\overline{\xi_{1}}I_{n_{1}}\end{bmatrix}&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&&\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{d}I_{n_{d}}&\\
&\overline{\xi_{d}}I_{n_{d}}\end{bmatrix}\\
&&&&\begin{bmatrix}I_{\ell}&\\
&-I_{k}\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix},

where
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d}\in\mathbb{T}\setminus\{1,-1\}
are distinct eigenvalues of
U
,
I_{m}
denotes the
m\times m
identity matrix, and the block in the lower right corner might not appear or might appear as just
I_{\ell}
or just
-I_{k}
, depending on whether
1
or
-1
are eigenvalues of
U
. Of course
n_{j}
,
\ell
, and
k
represent the multiplicities of the respective eigenvalues and
2n_{1}+\cdots+2n_{d}+\ell+k=n
.

Now consider the mapping

(3.5)

C^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}&I_{n_{1}}\\
I_{n_{1}}&\end{bmatrix}&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&&\begin{bmatrix}&I_{n_{d}}\\
I_{n_{d}}&\end{bmatrix}\\
&&&&\begin{bmatrix}I_{\ell}&\\
&I_{k}\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}J,

where
J
is the conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
from (
3.2
). Proposition
3.1
says that
C^{\prime}
is a conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and block multiplication will show that
C^{\prime}U^{\prime}C^{\prime}=U^{\prime}
. If
W
is the unitary matrix such that
WU^{\prime}W^{*}=U
, Lemma
2.3
says that
C=WC^{\prime}W^{*}
is a conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and Proposition
2.6
shows that
CUC=U
. Thus,
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose that
U
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix with
U\cong U^{*}
and
W
is a unitary matrix such that
WUW^{*}=U^{\prime}
, where
U^{\prime}
is the matrix from (
3.4
). Then every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
takes the form

(3.7)

C=W\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}&V_{1}\\
V_{1}^{t}&\end{bmatrix}&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&&\begin{bmatrix}&V_{d}\\
V_{d}^{t}&\end{bmatrix}\\
&&&&\begin{bmatrix}Q_{\ell}&\\
&Q_{k}\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}JW^{*},

where each
V_{j}
is an
n_{j}\times n_{j}
unitary matrix,
Q_{\ell}
,
Q_{k}
are
\ell\times\ell
and
k\times k
(respectively) unitary matrices with
Q_{\ell}^{t}=Q_{\ell}
and
Q_{k}^{t}=Q_{k}
(in which only one or perhaps both might not appear depending whether
1
or
-1
are eigenvalues of
U
), and
J
is the conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
from (
3.2
).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
and Proposition
3.2
, the mapping
C
from (
3.7
) defines a conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. As argued in the proof of Proposition
3.3
(replacing the conjugation
C
from (
3.7
) with the conjugation
C^{\prime}
from (
3.5
)), one can see that each conjugation
C
from (
3.7
) belongs to
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
.

Now suppose that
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
. Then
C^{\prime}:=W^{*}CW\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U^{\prime})
, where
U^{\prime}
is the matrix from (
3.4
). From Proposition
3.1
,
C^{\prime}=VJ
, where
V
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix with
V^{t}=V
. Now observe that
JU^{\prime}J=\overline{U^{\prime}}
(the matrix
U^{\prime}
with all the entries conjugated) and
JVJ=\overline{V}=\overline{V^{t}}=V^{*}
and thus

U^{\prime}=C^{\prime}U^{\prime}C^{\prime}=(VJ)U^{\prime}(VJ)=V(JU^{\prime}J)(JVJ)=V\overline{U^{\prime}}V^{*}.

This yields the identity
U^{\prime}V=V\overline{U^{\prime}}
. A computation with block multiplication of matrices and the fact that
V=V^{t}
(along with the facts that
U^{\prime}
is block diagonal with distinct multiples of the identity matrix along its diagonal) will show that

V=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}&V_{1}\\
V_{1}^{t}&\end{bmatrix}&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&&\begin{bmatrix}&V_{d}\\
V_{d}^{t}&\end{bmatrix}\\
&&&&\begin{bmatrix}Q_{\ell}&\\
&Q_{k}\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix},

where
V_{j}
are
n_{j}\times n_{j}
unitary matrices and
Q_{\ell}
and
Q_{k}
are
\ell\times\ell
and
k\times k
unitary matrices with
Q_{\ell}^{t}=Q_{\ell}
,
Q_{k}^{t}=Q_{k}
.
Thus,
C=WC^{\prime}W^{*}
has the desired form in (
3.7
).
∎

4.
Conjugations and spectral measures

A version of the spectral theorem for unitary operators
[
6
, Ch. IX, Thm. 2.2]
(see also
[
18
]
) says that if
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
, then there is a unique spectral measure
E(\cdot)
on
\mathbb{T}
such that

(4.1)

U=\int\xi dE(\xi).

Moreover, for any spectral measure
E(\cdot)
on
\mathbb{T}
(i.e.,
E(\cdot)
is a projection-valued function on the Borel subsets of
\mathbb{T}
such that
E(\mathbb{T})=I
and
E(\cdot)
is countably additive), there is a unique unitary operator
U
associated with
E(\cdot)
via (
4.1
).

For a spectral measure
E(\cdot)
and
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
, the function

\mu_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}(\cdot):=\langle E(\cdot){\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle

defines a finite complex Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
and, in particular, for each
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
,

(4.2)

\mu_{{\bf x}}:=\mu_{{\bf x},{\bf x}}

defines a finite positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
, sometimes called an
elementary measure
.

For a complex-valued Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{T}
, define a new complex Borel measure
\mu^{c}
on the Borel subsets
\Omega
of
\mathbb{T}
by

(4.3)

\mu^{c}(\Omega):=\mu(\Omega^{*}),\;\;\mbox{where}\;\;\Omega^{*}:=\{\overline{\xi}:\xi\in\Omega\}

One can argue that
(\mu^{c})^{c}=\mu
. For a spectral measure
E(\cdot)
on
\mathbb{T}
, we have the family of measures

\{\mu_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}^{c}:{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}\}
defined via (
4.3
).

For the rest of this paper, we use
M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
to denote the set of all finite positive Borel measures on
\mathbb{T}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
and
\mu^{c}\ll\mu
. Then the following hold.

(a)

\mu\ll\mu^{c}
;

(b)

The Radon–Nikodym derivatives satisfy

(4.5)

\frac{d\mu^{c}}{d\mu}(\xi)\cdot\frac{d\mu^{c}}{d\mu}(\bar{\xi})=1\;\;\mbox{for $\mu$-almost every $\xi\in\mathbb{T}$.}

Proof.

Let
h=d\mu^{c}/d\mu
.
Observe that
\mu=(\mu^{c})^{c}\ll\mu^{c}
and

d\mu(\xi)=d\mu^{c}(\bar{\xi})=h(\bar{\xi})d\mu(\bar{\xi})=h(\bar{\xi})d\mu^{c}(\xi)=h(\bar{\xi})h(\xi)d\mu(\xi)

for
\mu
-almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
.
Therefore,

(4.6)

d\mu(\xi)=h(\bar{\xi})d\mu^{c}(\xi)\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;h(\bar{\xi})h(\xi)=1

for
\mu
-almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
.
∎

The following proposition, originally explored in
[
17
]
for symmetric conjugations, relates a
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
with the associated spectral measure
E(\cdot)
for
U
. Define
E^{c}(\cdot)
on Borel subsets
\Omega
of
\mathbb{T}
by

E^{c}(\Omega):=E(\Omega^{*}).

From this definition it follows that

\langle E^{c}(\Omega){\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\mu_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}^{c}(\Omega)\;\mbox{for all ${\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}$.}

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
C
be a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
and
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
with associated spectral measure
E(\cdot)
.
Then we have the following.

(a)

E^{c}(\cdot)
is the associated spectral measure for
U^{*}
.

(b)

CE(\cdot)C
is the spectral measure for
CU^{*}C
.

(c)

CUC=U^{*}
if and only if
CE(\Omega)C=E(\Omega)
for all Borel subsets
\Omega
of
\mathbb{T}
.

(d)

CUC=U
if and only if
CE(\Omega)C=E^{c}(\Omega)
for all Borel subsets
\Omega
of
\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

If
E(\cdot)
is a spectral measure, one can check that
E^{c}(\cdot)
and
CE(\cdot)C
are also spectral measures. Since, for each pair
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
,

\langle U^{*}{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\int\bar{\xi}\,d\langle E(\xi){\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\int\xi\,d\langle E^{c}(\xi){\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle,

the uniqueness of the spectral measure for a unitary operator gives (a).
In a similar way, (b) is a consequence of the computation

\displaystyle\langle CU^{*}C{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle C{\bf y},U^{*}C{\bf x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle UC{\bf y},C{\bf x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\xi\,d\langle E(\xi)C{\bf y},C{\bf x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\xi\,d\langle{\bf x},CE(\xi)C{\bf y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\xi\,d\langle CE(\xi)C{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle.

Note the use of (
2.1
) in the above calculation. To see (c), note that
CU^{*}C=U
if and only if their spectral measures
CE(\cdot)C
and
E(\cdot)
coincide. Symmetrically in (d),
CU^{*}C
equals to
U^{*}
if and only if the spectral measures
CE(\cdot)C
and
E^{c}(\cdot)
coincide.
∎

As we will see in subsequent sections, the set
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
is quite large and so an important step in understanding it is to decompose each
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
into more manageable pieces. This decomposition will involve various types of invariant subspaces. Recall that a (closed) subspace
\mathcal{M}
of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is
invariant
for an
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
if
A\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
;
reducing
if both
A\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
and
A^{*}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
; and
hyperinvariant
if
T\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for every
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
that commutes with
A
. We begin with a simple lemma whose proof follows from (
2.1
) and the fact that
C^{2}=I
.

Lemma 4.8
.

If
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{M}
is a subspace of
\mathcal{H}
such that
C\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, then
C\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}
and
C\mathcal{M}^{\perp}=\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a unitary operator with associated spectral measure
E(\cdot)
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{T}
be a Borel set.

(a)

If
{\Omega}^{*}=\Omega
then for any
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
, we have

C(E(\Omega)\mathcal{H})=E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
.

(b)

If
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
and
E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
is invariant for
C
, then
E(\Omega\setminus\Omega^{*})=0
.

Proof.

For the proof of (a), let
{\bf x}\in E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
and
{\bf y}\in(E(\Omega)\mathcal{H})^{\perp}
. By Proposition
4.7
(d) we have

\langle C{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\langle CE(\Omega){\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\langle E(\Omega^{*})C{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle=\langle C{\bf x},E(\Omega){\bf y}\rangle=\langle C{\bf x},{\bf 0}\rangle=0

and thus
C{\bf x}\in E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
. Now apply Lemma
4.8
.

For the proof of (b) let
{\bf x}\in E(\Omega\setminus\Omega^{*})\mathcal{H}
. From
E(\Omega)=E(\Omega^{*})\oplus E(\Omega\setminus\Omega^{*})
, we can use Proposition
4.7
(d) to see that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\|E(\Omega^{*}){\bf x}\|=\|CE(\Omega)C{\bf x}\|=\|E(\Omega)C{\bf x}\|=\|C{\bf x}\|=\|{\bf x}\|.\qed

For a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
with associated spectral measure
E(\cdot)
and the associated family of elementary measures
\mu_{{\bf x}},{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
from (
4.2
), one can show, as was done in
[
21
]
, that for any
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
the set

\mathcal{H}_{\mu}:=\{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}:\mu_{{\bf x}}\ll\mu\}

is a reducing subspace of
U
. The space
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
was discussed in
[
18
, §65]
as part of a general discussion of the spectral multiplicity theory for unitary operators.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
,
E(\cdot)
its associated spectral measure,
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, and
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
. Then we have the following.

(a)

C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu^{c}}
and
C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu^{c}}^{\perp}
, and thus

(b)

C=C_{\mu,\mu^{c}}\oplus C^{{}^{\prime}}_{\mu,\mu^{c}}
, where
C_{\mu,\mu^{c}}=C|_{{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}}:\mathcal{H}_{\mu}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mu^{c}}
and
C^{{}^{\prime}}_{\mu,\mu^{c}}=C|_{{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}}}:\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mu^{c}}^{\perp}
are antilinear, onto, isometries.

Proof.

Let
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
. By Proposition
4.7
(d),
CE(\cdot)C=E^{c}(\cdot)
and thus

\displaystyle\langle E(\cdot)C{\bf x},C{\bf x}\rangle=\langle{\bf x},CE(\cdot)C{\bf x}\rangle=\langle{\bf x},E^{c}(\cdot){\bf x}\rangle=\langle E^{c}(\cdot){\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle.

Since
\langle E(\cdot){\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle\ll\mu
, it follows that
\langle E(\cdot)C{\bf x},C{\bf x}\rangle\ll\mu^{c}
and thus
C{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu^{c}}
. Similarly,
C\mathcal{H}_{\mu^{c}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
, thus
C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu^{c}}
and
C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu^{c}}^{\perp}
(Lemma
4.8
).
∎

Recall
[
18
, §48]
the standard Boolean operations
\wedge
and
\vee
for
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
defined on Borel subsets
\Omega
of
\mathbb{T}
by

(\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})(\Omega):=\mu_{1}(\Omega)+\mu_{2}(\Omega);

(\mu_{1}\wedge\mu_{2})(\Omega):=\inf\{\mu_{1}(\Omega\cap A)+\mu_{2}(\Omega\setminus A):\mbox{$A$ is a Borel set}\}.

For a unitary operator
U
, there exists a
scalar spectral measure

\nu
, meaning that
\nu(\Delta)=0
if and only if
E(\Delta)=0
, where
E(\cdot)
is the spectral measure for
U

[
6
, p. 293]
(also see the discussion in Theorem 3.8 in
[

]
). For

it was shown in

, Prop. 3.10]
that

if and only if

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
\nu
be any scalar spectral measure for
U
. Suppose that
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
satisfies
\mu^{c}\wedge\nu\ll\mu\wedge\nu
. If
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
, we have the following.

(a)

C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
and
C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}
.

(b)

C=C_{\mu}\oplus C_{\mu}^{\perp}
, where
C_{\mu}:=C|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}
and
C_{\mu}^{\perp}=C|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}}
.

(c)

C_{\mu}\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}})
and
C_{\mu}^{\perp}\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}})
.

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
\nu
be any scalar spectral measure for
U
. Fix a
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
.
If
C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
for some

then
\mu^{c}\wedge\nu\ll\mu\wedge\nu
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.10
we have
C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu^{c}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
. Thus, by
[

, Prop. 3.11]
, we obtain

.
∎

Since a unitary operator is normal, we see that
\ker(U-\alpha I)=\ker(U^{*}-\bar{\alpha}I)
i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{\delta_{\alpha}}=\mathcal{H}_{{\delta_{\alpha}^{c}}}
, where
\delta_{\xi}
denotes an atomic measure with atom at

. This gives us the following corollary.

Corollary 4.13
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
.
Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
be an eigenvalue for

.
Then

C=C_{\delta_{\alpha}}\oplus C_{\delta_{\alpha}}^{\perp},

where

C_{\delta_{\alpha}}=C|_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta_{\alpha}}}
and
C_{\delta_{\alpha}}^{\perp}=C|_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta_{\alpha}}^{\perp}}
are conjugations on
\ker(U-\alpha I)
and
\ker(U-\alpha I)^{\perp}
, respectively.

In the above discussion, we often have the hypothesis that

. As mentioned earlier, this is not always the case (e.g., if

is not unitarily equivalent to its adjoint – Lemma

).

5.
Natural conjugations on vector valued
L^{2}
spaces

This section provides a model for conjugations on vector valued Lebesgue spaces and will be useful in our description of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
in Theorem
8.5
. This notation also sets up our discussion of models for bilateral shifts in the next section.

For a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
and a
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, consider the set
\mathscr{L}^{0}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
of
\mathcal{H}
-valued
\mu
-measurable functions
f
on
\mathbb{T}
and the set

\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H}):=\Big\{\emph{\textbf{f}}\in\mathscr{L}^{0}(\mu,\mathcal{H}):\|\emph{\textbf{f}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu.\mathcal{H})}:=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\|\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}d\mu(\xi)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty\Big\}.

This is often described using tensor notation as
L^{2}(\mu)\otimes\mathcal{H}
.

Also consider
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
, the
\mu
-essentially bounded
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
-valued functions
U
on
\mathbb{T}
. For
\emph{\textbf{U}}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
, define the multiplication operator
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
by

(5.1)

(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)

for
\emph{\textbf{f}}\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
and
\mu
-almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
.
Clearly
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H}))
. If we use the notation
\emph{\textbf{U}}^{*}(\xi)=\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)^{*}
, one can verify that

(5.2)

\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}^{*}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{*}}.

We will use
L^{\infty}(\mu):=\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathbb{C})
to denote the scalar valued
\mu
-essentially bounded functions on
\mathbb{T}
. For ease of notation, we will write
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\varphi}
, when
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
, instead of the more cumbersome
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\varphi\emph{\textbf{I}}_{\mathcal{H}}}
, that is,

(5.3)

(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\varphi}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\varphi\emph{\textbf{I}}_{\mathcal{H}}}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=\varphi(\xi)\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)

for
\emph{\textbf{f}}\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
and
\mu
-almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
. The case when
\varphi(\xi)=\xi
will play an prominent role in this paper in which case we have the vector-valued bilateral shift
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
.

Recall from §
2
that
\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the space of all bounded antilinear operators on
\mathcal{H}
. We define
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
to be the space of all
\mu
-essentially bounded and
\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
-valued Borel functions on
\mathbb{T}
. Similarly as above, for
\emph{\textbf{C}}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
, define

(\emph{\textbf{A}}_{\emph{\textbf{C}}}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=\emph{\textbf{C}}(\xi)\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)

for
\emph{\textbf{f}}\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
and
\mu
-almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
.
One can check that
\emph{\textbf{A}}_{\emph{\textbf{C}}}\in\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H}))
.

For any conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}
, define the conjugation
J
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
by

(5.4)

(\emph{\textbf{J}}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)),\;\;\emph{\textbf{f}}\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H}).

Notice that

\emph{\textbf{J}}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{J}}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\bar{\xi}}

[
21
]
.

We now focus our attention on the scalar valued
L^{2}(\mu)
space and the set
\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\xi})
. This next result shows that when
\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\xi})\not=\varnothing
, there must be some restrictions on
\mu
. The set
\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\xi})
was explored in
[
5
]
when
\mu=m
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
and
C
be a conjugation on
L^{2}(\mu)
such that
CM_{\xi}=M_{\xi}C
. Then
\mu^{c}\ll\mu
(and hence
\mu\ll\mu^{c}
by Proposition
4.4
).

Proof.

From (
2.5
), the identity
CM_{\xi}C=M_{\xi}
implies that
CM_{\bar{\xi}}C=M_{\bar{\xi}}
. For any trigonometric polynomial
p(\xi)
define

p^{\#}(\xi):=\overline{p(\overline{\xi})}.

The above (and the antilinearity of
C
) shows that

CM_{p}C=M_{p^{\#}}.

Therefore, by the weak-
*
density of the trigonometric polynomials in
L^{\infty}(\mu)
, we obtain

(5.6)

CM_{\varphi}C=M_{{\varphi}^{\#}}\;\text{for any $\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mu)$,}

where

(5.7)

{\varphi}^{\#}(\xi)=\overline{\varphi(\overline{\xi})}.

If
\mu^{c}
were not absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
, then there would be a Borel set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
\mu(\Omega)\not=0
but
\mu^{c}(\Omega)=0
. However, (
5.6
) leads to contradiction with
\varphi=\chi_{\Omega}
, since
M_{\chi_{\Omega^{*}}}=0
but
CM_{\chi_{\Omega}}C
is not.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

One can adapt the proof of the above proposition to the vector–valued space
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
.

Now let us focus on the situation when
\mu^{c}\ll\mu
. In this case we also have that
\mu\ll\mu^{c}
(Proposition
4.4
). For
\emph{\textbf{f}}\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})

\emph{\textbf{f}}(\bar{\xi})
or

and define

(5.9)

\emph{\textbf{U}}^{\#}(\xi):=\emph{\textbf{U}}^{*}(\bar{\xi})=\emph{\textbf{U}}(\bar{\xi})^{*}.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
such that
\mu^{c}\ll\mu
and let

.
For a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, a conjugation

on
\mathcal{H}

(5.11)

(\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=(h(\xi))^{\frac{1}{2}}J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\bar{\xi}))

(a)

\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}

\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})

(b)

\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}

As discussed in Proposition
4.4
,
\mu\ll\mu^{c}
and

4.6

\displaystyle(\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)

\displaystyle=h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J((\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\bar{\xi}))

\displaystyle=h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J\Big((h(\bar{\xi}))^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi))\Big)

\displaystyle=(h(\xi)h(\bar{\xi})^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J(J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)))

\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}

\displaystyle=\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)

\displaystyle=h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J((\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\bar{\xi}))

\displaystyle=h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J(\bar{\xi}\emph{\textbf{f}}(\bar{\xi}))

\displaystyle=\xi h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\bar{\xi}))

\displaystyle=\xi(\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)

\displaystyle=(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#})\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi).\qed

\displaystyle(\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)

\displaystyle=h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}J\big((\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\bar{\xi})\big)

\displaystyle=h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J\big(\emph{\textbf{U}}(\bar{\xi})\big(\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{f}}\big)(\bar{\xi})\big)

\displaystyle=h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J\big(\emph{\textbf{U}}(\bar{\xi})h(\bar{\xi})^{\frac{1}{2}}J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)\big))

\displaystyle=(h(\xi)h(\bar{\xi}))^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J(\emph{\textbf{U}}(\bar{\xi})J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)))

\displaystyle=J\emph{\textbf{U}}(\bar{\xi})J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi))

\displaystyle=J((\emph{\textbf{U}}^{\#}(\xi))^{*}J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)))

\displaystyle=(\emph{\textbf{J}}(\emph{\textbf{U}}^{\#})^{*}\emph{\textbf{J}}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi).

Note the use of (
5.9
) above. This proves (a).

Note that

\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
is antilinear and isometric on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
. To prove
that
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
is a conjugation (and thus complete the proof of (b)), Lemma
2.7
says that we just need to check the identity

\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}^{*}.

By (a) this is equivalent to
J\emph{\textbf{U}}(\bar{\xi})J=\emph{\textbf{U}}^{*}(\xi)
since, by (
5.2
),
(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}^{*}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=\emph{\textbf{U}}^{*}(\xi)\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)
.

Statement (c) follows from the fact that
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
is a conjugation on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
, and so
(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#})(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#})=I
, along with the fact
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{*}}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{*}}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}=I
(since
\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)
is unitary for
\mu
-almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
).

To see (d), observe that for any
\emph{\textbf{f}}\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
,

\displaystyle(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)

\displaystyle=\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)

\displaystyle=\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J((\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\bar{\xi}))

\displaystyle=h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)J(\bar{\xi}\emph{\textbf{f}}(\bar{\xi}))

\displaystyle=\xi h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\bar{\xi}))

while

\displaystyle(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)

\displaystyle=\xi(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)

\displaystyle=\xi\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)(\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)

\displaystyle=\xi\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\bar{\xi}))

\displaystyle=\xi h(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)J(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\bar{\xi})),

which completes the proof of (d).
∎

6.
Conjugations and bilateral shifts

Many interesting, and naturally occurring, unitary operators are bilateral shifts. Examples include (i) the translation operator
(Uf)(x)=f(x-1)
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
; (ii) the dilation operator
(Uf)(x)=\sqrt{2}f(2x)
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, (iii) the Fourier transform on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, (iv) the Hilbert transform on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and (v) the special class of multiplication operators
Uf=\psi f
on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
, where
\psi
is an inner function. We refer the reader to
[
22
, Example 6.3]
to see the bilateral nature of each of these operators is worked out carefully. This section gives an initial description of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
for this class of operators, along with the important fact that
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
. Another, more concrete, description will be discussed in the next section. Let us begin with a precise definition of the term “bilateral shift”.

Definition 6.1
.

A unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
is a
bilateral shift
if there is a subspace
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
for which

(a)

U^{n}\mathcal{M}\perp\mathcal{M}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
;

(b)

{\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}U^{n}\mathcal{M}}
.

In the above, note that
U^{-1}=U^{*}
. The subspace
\mathcal{M}
is called an
associated wandering subspace
for the bilateral shift
U
. Of course there is
the
bilateral shift
M_{\xi}
on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
discussed earlier where a wandering subspace
\mathcal{M}
can be taken to be the constant functions.

Though the wandering subspace
\mathcal{M}
in Definition
6.1
is not unique, its dimension is
[
19
]
. The term “bilateral shift ” comes from the fact that since

(6.2)

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\,U^{n}\mathcal{M},

every
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
can be uniquely represented as

{\bf x}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}U^{n}{\bf x}_{n},\;\mbox{where ${\bf x}_{n}\in\mathcal{M}$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$}.

This allows us to define a natural unitary operator

(6.3)

W:\mathcal{H}\to\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M}),\quad W\Big(\bigoplus_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}U^{n}{\bf x}_{n}\Big)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{n}\xi^{n}.

Moreover, thanks to (
6.2
) and (
6.3
),

WUW^{*}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi},

where

\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
is the bilateral shift from (
5.3
) defined on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M})
by

\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)=\xi\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi),\;\mbox{where}\;\;\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{n}\xi^{n}\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M}).

For a bilateral shift
U
on
\mathcal{H}
we wish to describe
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
.
Since
W^{*}UW=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M})
, we know that for any
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
the mapping

\mathfrak{C}=W^{*}CW

is a conjugation on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M})
such that
\mathfrak{C}\mathbf{M}_{\xi}\mathfrak{C}=\mathbf{M}_{\xi}
.
The following result from
[
5
, Theorem 4.3]
describes
\mathfrak{C}
.

Theorem 6.4
.

For
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M})
we have the following.

(a)

\mathscr{C}_{c}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi})\not=\varnothing
.

(b)

Fix a conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{M}
. For a conjugation
\mathfrak{C}
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M})
,
the following are equivalent.

(i)

\mathfrak{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi})
;

(ii)

There is
\mathbf{U}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(m,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{M}))
such that
\mathbf{U}(\xi)
is unitary for almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
,
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}
is
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
–symmetric, and
\mathfrak{C}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\mathbf{U}}{\emph{\textbf{J}}}^{\#}={\emph{\textbf{J}}}^{\#}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\mathbf{U}^{*}}
.

Note that
m=m^{c}
and the definition of
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
from (
5.11
) coincides with the one appearing in
[
5
]
.

Theorem
6.4
yields a description of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
when
U
is a bilateral shift.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
U
be a unitary bilateral shift on
\mathcal{H}
with an associated wandering subspace
\mathcal{M}
. The we have the following.

(a)

\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
.

(b)

For a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
;

(ii)

\mathfrak{C}=WCW^{*}
is a conjugation on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M})
that satisfies any of the equivalent conditions of Theorem
6.4
.

Perhaps one might be a bit unsatisfied with the somewhat vague nature of our current description of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
for a bilateral shift
U
. The next section will give a much more concrete characterization.

7.
Unitary multiplication operators on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})

As discussed in
[
22
, Example 5.16]
there is model for any bilateral shift
U
on
\mathcal{H}
(recall Definition
6.1
) as the multiplication operator
M_{\psi}
on
L^{2}=L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
, where
\psi
is an inner function whose degree is that of the dimension of any wandering subspace for
U
. In this section, we give a concrete description of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\psi})
. If
J^{\#}
is the conjugation on
L^{2}
defined by

(7.1)

(J^{\#}f)(\xi)=f^{\#}(\xi)=\overline{f(\bar{\xi})},

and
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\psi})
, then
CJ^{\#}
is a unitary operator on
L^{2}
for which

(CJ^{\#})M_{\psi}=M_{\psi}(CJ^{\#}).

This trick was used in several places
[
4
,
5
,
8
]
. The bounded operators on
L^{2}
which commute with
M_{\psi}
, i.e., the
commutant
of
M_{\psi}
, were described in
[
21
, Theorem 7.3]
.

Recall the known fact (see for example
[
22
, Proposition 5.17]
) that for an inner
function
\psi
we have the following orthogonal decomposition for
L^{2}
, namely,

(7.2)

L^{2}=\bigoplus_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^{n}\mathcal{K}_{\psi},

where
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}:=H^{2}\cap(\psi H^{2})^{\perp}
is the model space associated with
\psi
(see
[
11
]
for a review of model spaces). In other words,
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is a wandering subspace (as in Definition
6.1
) for the multiplication operator
M_{\psi}
.

Let us set up some notation to be used below. For an inner function
\psi
, let

N:=\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}

and

\{h_{j}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}
be a fixed orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
. There are several “natural” orthonormal bases one can choose
[
11
, Ch. 5]
. Observe that
N
is finite if and only if
\psi
is a finite Blaschke product with
N

[
11
, Prop. 5.19]
. Also define

\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}=L^{2}\oplus L^{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus L^{2}.

The norm of an
\emph{\textbf{f}}=[f_{j}]_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}^{t}
of
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}
is

\|\emph{\textbf{f}}\|:=\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\|f_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

When
N=\infty
, we need to assume that the sum defining
\|\emph{\textbf{f}}\|
above is finite.
Furthermore, the operator
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}
(called the
inflation
of the bilateral shift
M_{\xi}
on
L^{2}
) is given by

\Big(\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}\Big)\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)=\xi\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)=[\xi f_{j}(\xi)]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}.

We also define

\ell^{2}_{N}:=\Big\{{\bf x}=[x_{j}]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N},x_{j}\in\mathbb{C}:\|{\bf x}\|_{\ell^{2}_{N}}=\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}|x_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty\Big\}.

When
N=\infty
, this is the familiar sequence space
\ell^{2}
. Finally, observe that

(7.3)

\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}\cong\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}|_{\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\ell^{2}_{N})}.

As a consequence, using the discussion from §
5
, note that

(7.4)

\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\psi})\not=\varnothing.

We will actually describe
\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\psi})
below.

From
[
21
]
we have the unitary operator

(7.5)

W\colon L^{2}\to\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2},\quad Wf=[f_{j}]_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}^{t},

where

f=\sum\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot(f_{j}\circ\psi)

is a unique decomposition given by
[
21
, Lemma 7.3]
. Note that

(7.6)

f_{j}=\sum\limits_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{mj}\xi^{m}

and the coefficients
a_{mj}
arise from the decomposition from (
7.2
) which yields the unique decomposition

(7.7)

f=\sum\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\sum\limits_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{mj}\psi^{m}.

Also recall from
[
21
, Thm. 7.3]
that

W^{*}[k_{j}]_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}^{t}=\sum\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot(k_{j}\circ\psi).

Let
J
and
J^{\#}
denote the standard conjugations on
L^{2}
defined by

Jf(z)=\overline{f(\xi)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad(J^{\#}f)(\xi)=f^{\#}(\xi):=\overline{f(\bar{\xi})}.

For our inner function
\psi
, observe that
\psi^{\#}=J^{\#}\psi
is also inner.

Proposition 7.8
.

For an inner function
\psi
we have the following.

(a)

J^{\#}\mathcal{K}_{\psi}=\mathcal{K}_{\psi^{\#}}
.

(b)

If
\{h_{j}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
then
\{h_{j}^{\#}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{\psi^{\#}}
.

Proof.

Part (a) was shown in
[
4
, Lemma 4.4]
while part (b) is a consequence of the facts that conjugations preserve orthonormality (recall (
2.1
)).
∎

Let
W_{\#}
be the unitary operator from (
7.5
), where the inner function
\psi
is replaced by
\psi^{\#}
and orthonormal basis and the orthonormal basis
\{h_{j}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}
is replaced by the orthonormal basis
\{h_{j}^{\#}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}
, i.e.,

W_{\#}g=[g_{j}]_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}^{t},\;\mbox{where}\;g=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}^{\#}\cdot(g_{j}\circ\psi^{\#}).

There are the two natural conjugations
J
and
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
on
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}
defined for each
{\bf F}\in\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}
,
{\bf F}=[f_{j}]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}
, by

\displaystyle\emph{\textbf{J}}{\bf F}

\displaystyle=[\bar{f}_{j}]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}=:\bar{{{\bf F}}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}{\bf F}=[f_{j}^{\#}]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}=:{{\bf F}}^{\#}.

Proposition 7.9
.

Let
\psi
be an inner function and
\{h_{j}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
.
Then we have the following.

(a)

If
{\displaystyle f=\sum\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot(f_{j}\circ\psi)}
then
{\displaystyle f^{\#}=J^{\#}f=\sum\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}^{\#}\cdot(f_{j}^{\#}\circ\psi^{\#})}
.

(b)

W_{\#}J^{\#}W^{*}=\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{2}
and observe from (
7.6
) and (
7.7
) that

J^{\#}f=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{j}^{\#}\sum\overline{a_{mj}}(\psi^{\#})^{m}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{j}^{\#}=\sum\limits_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\overline{a_{mj}}\xi^{m}.

Hence

J^{\#}f=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}h_{j}^{\#}\cdot(f_{j}^{\#}\circ\psi^{\#}),

which proves (a). The above also yields

W_{\#}J^{\#}W^{*}[f_{j}]_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}^{t}=W_{\#}J^{\#}f=[f_{j}^{\#}]_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}^{t}=\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}[f_{j}]_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}^{t},

which proves (b).
∎

Theorem 7.10
.

Suppose that
\psi
is inner and
\{h_{j}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
. Then we have the following,

(a)

\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\psi})\not=\varnothing
.

(b)

C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\psi})
if and only if there is a
\Phi=[\varphi_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(m,\ell^{2}_{N})
such that

(7.11)

\Phi(\xi)=\Phi(\bar{\xi})\;\mbox{and}\;\Phi^{*}(\xi)\Phi(\xi)=I

almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
and

(7.12)

Cf=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}(f^{\#}_{j}\circ\psi)\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}h_{k}\cdot(\varphi_{k,j}\circ\psi),

for all
{\displaystyle f=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot(f_{j}\circ\psi)\in L^{2}}
.

Proof.

Statement (a) follows from (
7.4
). To prove (b), observe that since
W
is a unitary operator, then
\widetilde{C}:=WCW^{*}
is a conjugation on
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}
(Lemma

). If
CM_{\psi}=M_{{\psi}}C
, it follows from

[

, Theorem 7.2(c)]
that

\widetilde{C}\Big(\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}\Big)=\Big(\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}\Big)\widetilde{C}.

Since the operator
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}
on
\bigoplus_{j\geqslant 1}L^{2}
is unitary equivalent to
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\ell^{2}_{N})
(recall (
7.3

says there is a

\Phi(\xi)
is unitary for almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
,

\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}
is
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
–symmetric, and

\widetilde{C}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}.

The unitary property gives
\Phi^{*}(\xi)\Phi(\xi)=I
and the
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}

almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. So far, we have shown that if

is a conjugation which commutes with

, then
WCW^{*}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
, where

satisfies the two properties from (

.

Applying Proposition
7.9

we have

\displaystyle Cf

\displaystyle=W^{*}{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}Wf

\displaystyle=W^{*}{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\Phi}W_{\#}J^{\#}\Big(\sum\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot(f_{j}\circ\psi)\Big)

\displaystyle=W^{*}{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\Phi}W_{\#}\Big(\sum\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}^{\#}\cdot(f_{j}^{\#}\circ\psi^{\#})\Big)

\displaystyle=W^{*}[\varphi_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}[f^{\#}_{j}]_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}^{t}

\displaystyle=(f^{\#}_{1}\circ\psi)\cdot\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}h_{j}(\varphi_{j1}\circ\psi\Big)+(f^{\#}_{2}\circ\psi)\cdot\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}h_{j}(\varphi_{j2}\circ\psi\Big)+\cdots

and this completes the proof.

|\varphi_{12}(\xi)|^{2}+|\varphi_{22}(\xi)|^{2}=1,

\overline{\varphi_{11}(\xi)}\varphi_{12}(\xi)+\overline{\varphi_{21}(\xi)}\varphi_{22}(\xi)=0.

Fix the convention that

t=\operatorname{Arg}(\xi)\in(-\pi,\pi]
and that
s(t),\alpha(t),\beta(t),\gamma(t),\delta(t)
are any
2\pi
–periodic real-valued bounded measurable functions. Considering the moduli of the functions above, we obtain

0\leqslant s(t)\leqslant 1,

\varphi_{11}(\xi)=e^{i\alpha(t)}s(t),

\varphi_{12}=e^{i\beta(t)}\sqrt{1-s^{2}(t)},

\varphi_{21}(\xi)=e^{i\gamma(t)}\sqrt{1-s^{2}(t)},

\varphi_{22}(\xi)=e^{i\delta(t)}s(t).

As to the arguments of the functions above, we obtain

\delta(t)=\beta(t))+\gamma(t)-\alpha(t)-\pi.

Incorporating the conditions
\varphi_{ij}(\bar{\xi})=\varphi_{ij}(\xi)
,
i,j=1,2
, we obtain

(7.16)

\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{11}(\xi)&\varphi_{12}(\xi)\\
\varphi_{21}(\xi)&\varphi_{22}(\xi)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{i\alpha(|t|)}s(|t|)&e^{i\beta(|t|)}\sqrt{1-s^{2}(|t|)}\\
e^{i\gamma(|t|)}\sqrt{1-s^{2}(|t|)}&-e^{i(\beta(|t|)+\gamma(|t|)-\alpha(|t|))}s(|t|)\end{bmatrix}.

Finally, every conjugation
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(M_{\xi^{2}})
must take the form

(Cf)(\xi)={f^{\#}_{1}(\xi^{2})}\Big(e^{i\alpha(2|t|)}s(2|t|)+\xi e^{{i}(\gamma(2|t|))}\sqrt{1-s^{2}(2|t|)}\Big)\\
+{f^{\#}_{2}(\xi^{2})}\Big(e^{{i}\beta(2|t|)}\sqrt{1-s^{2}(2|t|)}-\xi e^{i(\beta(2|t|)+\gamma(2|t|)-\alpha(2|t|))}s(2|t|)\Big),

where
t=\operatorname{Arg}(\xi)\in(-\pi,\pi]
and
s(t),\alpha(t),\beta(t),\gamma(t)
are any
2\pi
–periodic real bounded measurable functions.

Example 7.17
.

As a specific nontrivial example of a
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\xi^{2}})
we can take

(Cf)(\xi)={f^{\#}_{1}(\xi^{2})}\big(\sin(2|t|)+\xi\cos(2t)\big)+{f^{\#}_{2}(\xi^{2})}\big(\cos(2t)-\xi\sin(2|t|)\big),

where
t=\operatorname{Arg}(\xi)
and
s(t)=\sin(t),\alpha(t)\equiv 0,\beta(t)\equiv 0,\gamma(t)\equiv 0
.
For another nontrivial example of a

to get

where

.

8.
Conjugations via the spectral theorem

In this section we use the multiplicity theory for unitary operators
[
6
,
18
]
to describe
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
. We also prove that
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
if and only if
U\cong U^{*}
(thus establishing the converse to Lemma
2.9
). We begin with a statement of the spectral multiplicity theory from
[
6
, p. 307, Ch. IX, Theorem 10.20]
.

Theorem 8.1

(Spectral Theorem)
.

For a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, there are mutually singular measures

\mu_{\infty},\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, along with Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\infty},\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2},\ldots
each with corresponding
\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{H}_{k}=k
,
k=\infty,1,2,3,\dots
, along with an isometric isomorphism

\mathcal{I}:\mathcal{H}\to\emph{\textbf{L}}^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}:=\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{\infty},\mathcal{H}_{\infty})\oplus\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})\oplus\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{2},\mathcal{H}_{2})\oplus\cdots

such that

\mathcal{I}U\mathcal{I}^{*}
is equal to the unitary operator

(8.2)

\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(\infty)}\oplus\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(1)}\oplus\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(2)}\oplus\cdots,

where for
i=\infty,1,2,3,\ldots
,

\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(i)}:\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i})\to\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i}),\quad(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(i)}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=\xi\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi).

Remark 8.3
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator with a spectral measure
E(\cdot)
. As previously observed in Proposition
4.7
(a),
E^{c}(\cdot)
is a spectral measure for
U^{*}
. In
[
21
, Theorem 8.1]
, the measures
\mu_{\infty},\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots
from Theorem
8.1
were constructed using the spectral measure
E(\cdot)
. Therefore, the appropriate measures for operator
U^{*}
are
\mu^{c}_{\infty},\mu^{c}_{1},\mu^{c}_{2},\dots
.

We now consider unitary operators with commuting conjugations. Recall from Proposition
5.5
that not all unitary operators have commuting conjugations. In the next result, we re-emphasize this observation in terms of the multiplicity theory from Theorem
8.1
.

Theorem 8.4
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
with the multiplicity representation of
U
given by the mutually singular measures
\mu_{\infty},\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots
as in Theorem
8.1
. If
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
, then
\mu_{i}^{c}\ll\mu_{i}
for all
i=\infty,1,2,\dots
.

Proof.

Lemma
2.9
says that if
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
, then
U\cong U^{*}
. Hence, by Remark
8.3
and
[
6
, p. 305, Theorem IX 10.16]
, the measures
\mu_{i}
and
\mu^{c}_{i}
are mutually absolutely continuous for all
i=\infty,1,2,\dots
.
∎

We now arrive at the description of
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
in terms of the parameters of the spectral theorem.

Theorem 8.5
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator and
C
be a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
. With the notation as in Theorem
8.1
, assuming that
\mu_{i}^{c}\ll\mu_{i}
for
i=\infty,1,2,\dots
, the following are equivalent

(a)

C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
;

(b)

For each
i=\infty,1,2,\dots
, there are conjugations
\emph{\textbf{C}}^{i}\in\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}((\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i}))
such that
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(i)}
is
\emph{\textbf{C}}^{i}
–commuting and

(8.6)

C={\mathcal{I}}^{*}\Big(\bigoplus\emph{\textbf{C}}^{i}\Big)\mathcal{I};

(c)

For each
i=\infty,1,2,\dots
and any conjugation
J^{(i)}
on
\mathcal{H}_{i}
, there is a unitary operator valued function
\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{i}))
such that

J^{(i)}\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}(\xi)J^{(i)}=\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}(\xi)^{\#}

for
\mu_{i}
almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
and

(8.7)

C={\mathcal{I}}^{*}\Big(\bigoplus\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}{\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}\Big)\mathcal{I}={\mathcal{I}}^{*}\Big(\bigoplus\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}\Big)\Big(\bigoplus{\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}\Big)\mathcal{I}.

Proof.

To show (a)
\Longrightarrow
(c),
let

\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}:=\mathcal{I}V\mathcal{I}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(\emph{\textbf{L}}^{2}_{\mathcal{H}})

and define the conjugation
\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{C}}}=\mathcal{I}C\mathcal{I}^{*}
(note the use of Lemma
2.3
). Then

(8.8)

\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{C}}}\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}\,\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{C}}}=\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}.

Let
J^{(i)}
be any a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}_{i}
. Since
\mu^{c}_{i}\ll\mu_{i}
for
i=\infty,1,2,\dots
, let

h_{i}=\frac{d\mu^{c}_{i}}{d\mu_{i}}

and define the map
{\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i})
by

({\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}\emph{\textbf{f}}_{i})(\xi)=h_{i}(\xi)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,J^{(i)}(\emph{\textbf{f}}_{i}(\bar{\xi}))

for
\mu_{i}
almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
and
\emph{\textbf{f}}_{i}\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i})
. By Proposition
5.10
, each of the above maps defines a conjugation on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i})
which satisfies

{\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(i)}{\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(i)}.

Use these conjugations to define the conjugation
\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}\,}^{\#}=\bigoplus{\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}
on
\emph{\textbf{L}}^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

and observe that

\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}\,}^{\#}\,\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}\,\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}\,}^{\#}=\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}.

Moreover,

\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}\ \widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{C}}}\ \widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}\,}^{\#}=\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{C}}}\ \widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}\ \widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}\,}^{\#}=\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{C}}}\ \widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}\,}^{\#}\ \widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}.

The spectral theorem applied to
\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}}
also yields the commutant
[
6
, p. 307, Theorem 10.20]
, namely
there are

\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{i})),\quad i=\infty,1,2,\dots,

such that

\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{C}}}\,\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}\,}^{\#}=\bigoplus\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}}=\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(\infty)}}\oplus\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(1)}}\oplus\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(2)}}\oplus\cdots.

Since
\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{C}}}\,\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}\,}^{\#}
is unitary, it follows that
\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}}
is also unitary and consequently
\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}
is a operator valued operator function such that
\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}(\xi)
is unitary for
\mu_{i}
almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
. Therefore,

\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{C}}}=\Big(\bigoplus\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}}\Big)\Big(\bigoplus{\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}\Big)=\bigoplus\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}}{\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}.

Since
\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{C}}}|_{\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i})}
is a conjugation, it follows that

J^{(i)}\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}(\xi)J^{(i)}=(\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}(\xi))^{\#}

for
\mu_{i}
almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
(Proposition
5.15
). This completes the proof of (a)
\Longrightarrow
(c) .

To prove (c)
\Longrightarrow
(b), it is enough to take
\emph{\textbf{C}}^{(i)}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}}{\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}
. The remaining implication (b)
\Longrightarrow
(a) is trivial.
∎

The following yields the converse of Lemma
2.9
and thus completes the criterion as to when
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)\not=\varnothing
.

Corollary 8.9
.

If
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
such that
U\cong U^{*}
, then there is conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
CUC=U
.

Proof.

If
U\cong U^{*}
, then, as in the proof of Theorem
8.4
, the measures
\mu_{i}
and
\mu^{c}_{i}
are mutually absolutely continuous for all
i=\infty,1,2,\dots
. Now invoke Theorem
8.5
with any conjugation
J^{(i)}
on
\mathcal{H}_{j}
(and
\emph{\textbf{U}}^{(i)}=I_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}
) and observe that the conjugation

C=\mathcal{I}^{*}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}^{*}\bigoplus{\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}}^{(i)}\mathcal{I}

commutes with
U
.
∎

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
\sigma(\mathscr{H})=\{i,-i\}
then

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=\mathscr{E}_{i}\bigoplus\mathscr{E}_{-i}
and

\mathscr{E}_{i}
has orthonormal basis
\mathscr{B}_{i}=\{f_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}

f_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{(x+i)^{n-1}}{(x-i)^{n}}

and
\mathscr{E}_{-i}
has orthonormal basis
\mathscr{B}_{-i}=\{g_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}

g_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{(x-i)^{n-1}}{(x+i)^{n}}.

See
[
15
, Ch. 12]
for details. In this example we will describe
\mathscr{C}_{c}(\mathscr{H})
. Note first that
\mathscr{C}_{c}(\mathscr{H})\not=\varnothing
(recall Example
2.4
and thus
\mathscr{H}\cong\mathscr{H}^{*}
).

Similarly as in Example
9.3
we can identify
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\ell^{2})
, where
\mu=\delta_{i}+\delta_{-i}
.
Then, the conjugation
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
given by equality (
5.11
) is an antilinear extension of operator
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}f_{n}=g_{n},\ \emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}g_{n}=f_{n},\ n\geqslant 1
.
Putting this in matrix form

\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}=\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
J&0\end{bmatrix},

where
J
is a conjugation on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
which fixes all elements of
\mathscr{B}_{i}
and
\mathscr{B}_{-i}
.

Moreover, by Theorem
8.5
, any conjugation
C
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
C\mathscr{H}C=\mathscr{H}^{*}
must take the (block) form

C=\begin{bmatrix}U_{i}&0\\
0&U_{i}^{\#}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&J\\
J&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&U_{i}J\\
U_{i}^{\#}J&0\end{bmatrix},

where
U_{i}
is arbitrary unitary operator on
\mathscr{E}_{i}
and
U_{i}^{\#}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{E}_{-i})
defined as
\langle U_{i}^{\#}g_{m},g_{n}\rangle=\overline{\langle U_{i}^{*}f_{m},f_{n}\rangle}
similar to the previous example.

10.
A remark about invariant subspaces

The first paper in this series
[
21
]
classified, for a fixed unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, the subspaces
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{H}
for which
C\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for every
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
(the symmetric conjugations for
U
). These turned out to be the hyperinvariant subspaces for
U
. What are the subspaces
\mathcal{M}
for which
C\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
(the commuting conjugations for
U
)? We have some partial results in this paper (see for example Proposition
4.9
and Corollary
4.11
). However, we do not have a complete and concise characterization. These subspaces seem complicated to describe in the general abstract situation. However, we do have a characterization in the special case where
U=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
. Recall the notation from Proposition
5.10
.

Theorem 10.1
.

Suppose
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
such that
\mu^{c}\ll\mu
and
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space. For a subspace
\mathcal{K}
of
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
the following are equivalent.

(a)

\mathfrak{C}\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
for every
\mathfrak{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi})
;

(b)

For a fixed conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}
and
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
defined as in (
5.11
),
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
and every
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\mathbf{F}}
, where
\mathbf{F}
belongs to

\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{c}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})):=\{\mathbf{F}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})):J\mathbf{F}(\xi)J=\mathbf{F}(\xi)^{\#}\mbox{ for $\mu$-a.e. $\xi\in\mathbb{T}$}\}.

The proof of this theorem requires a decomposition theorem from
[
25
, proof of Corollary 3.19]
. We include a proof for completeness and since the form of the decomposition is important for the proof of Theorem
10.1
.

Lemma 10.2
.

Any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
can be expressed as a positive constant times the sum of four unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Define

H=\frac{1}{2\|A\|}(A+A^{*})\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;K=\frac{1}{2i\|A\|}(A-A^{*})

and notice that
H
and
K
are selfadjoint contractions and thus
I-H
and
I-K
are positive and hence have unique positive square roots.
Thus,

U_{1,2}=H\pm i(I-H^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;U_{3,4}=iK\pm(I-K^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}

are four unitary operators which satisfy

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{\|A\|}{2}(U_{1}+U_{2}+U_{3}+U_{4}).\qed

Proof of Theorem
10.1
.

The proof of (b)
\Longrightarrow
(a) follows from a special case of Theorem
8.5
. For the proof of (a)
\Longrightarrow
(b), we begin with the fact that since
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi})
then
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for
\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
. Moreover, by Theorem
8.5
any
\mathfrak{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi})
can be written as
\mathfrak{C}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
for some
\emph{\textbf{U}}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
that is unitary valued
\mu
-almost everywhere and also satisfies
J\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)J=\emph{\textbf{U}}(\xi)^{\#}
for
\mu
-almost every
\xi
. Thus,
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}\emph{\textbf{J}}^{\#}
and thus
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\emph{\textbf{U}}}
. Now apply Lemma
10.2
to any
\mathbf{F}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{c}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
and conclude that
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for every
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\mathbf{F}}
where
\mathbf{F}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{c}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
.
∎

Remark 10.3
.

In the scalar case
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}
, note that

\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}))=\{v\in L^{\infty}(\mu):v(\xi)=v(\overline{\xi})\mbox{ for $\mu$-a.e. $\xi\in\mathbb{T}$}\}.

Example 10.4
.

For
the
bilateral shift
M_{\xi}
on
L^{2}=L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
, we know that every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\xi})
takes the form
M_{u}J
, where
(Jf)(\xi)=f(\xi)
and
u\in L^{\infty}
such that
u(\xi)
is unimodular and
u(\xi)=u(\overline{\xi})
almost everywhere. One can check that examples of subspaces that are invariant for every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\xi})
include

(a)

\{g\in L^{2}:g(e^{it})=0,|t|\geqslant\tfrac{\pi}{2},g(e^{it})=g(e^{-it}),|t|<\tfrac{\pi}{2}\};

(b)

\{g\in L^{2}:g(e^{it})=0,|t|\geqslant\tfrac{\pi}{2},g(e^{it})=-g(e^{-it}),|t|<\tfrac{\pi}{2}\};

(c)

\{g\in L^{2}:g(e^{it})=0,|t|\geqslant\tfrac{\pi}{2},g(e^{it})=g(e^{-it}),\tfrac{\pi}{4}<|t|<\tfrac{\pi}{2},g(e^{it})=-g(e^{-it}),|t|<\tfrac{\pi}{4}\}.

The variety of these spaces convinces us that a concise description of the
C
-invariant subspaces for every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(M_{\xi})
seems difficult.
