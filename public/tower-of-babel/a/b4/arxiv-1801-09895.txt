One of the Odd Zeta Values from 𝜻 ⁡ ( 𝟓 ) \boldsymbol{\zeta(5)} to 𝜻 ⁡ ( 𝟐𝟓 ) \boldsymbol{\zeta(25)} Is Irrational. By Elementary Means This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Orthogonal Polynomials, Special Functions and Applications (OPSFA14). The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/OPSFA2017.html
Wadim ZUDILIN

Abstract
Available proofs of result of the type ‘at least one of the odd zeta values ζ ⁡ ( 5 ) , ζ ⁡ ( 7 ) , … , ζ ⁡ ( s ) \zeta(5),\zeta(7),\dots,\zeta(s) is irrational’ make use of the saddle-point method or of linear independence criteria, or both. These two remarkable techniques are however counted as highly non-elementary, therefore leaving the partial irrationality result inaccessible to general mathematics audience in all its glory. Here we modify the original construction of linear forms in odd zeta values to produce, for the first time, an elementary proof of such a result — a proof whose technical ingredients are limited to the prime number theorem and Stirling’s approximation formula for the factorial.

Introduction

Without touching deeply a history of the question (see
[
3
]
for an excellent account of this), we notice that the irrationality of the
zeta values
— values of Riemann’s zeta function

\displaystyle\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}

at integers
s=2,3,\dots
, is known for even
s
and also for
s=3
, while there are only partial results in this direction for odd
s\geq 5
.
A starting point here has been set in the work
[
1
]
with further development, particularly focusing on
\zeta(5)
, in
[
7
]
and
[
10
]
.

We fix an odd integer
s\geq 7
. Our strategy is constructing two sequences of linear forms
r_{n}
and
\hat{r}_{n}
living in the
\mathbb{Q}
-space
\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Q}\zeta(3)+\mathbb{Q}\zeta(5)+\dots+\mathbb{Q}\zeta(s)
, for which we have a control of the common denominators
\lambda_{n}
of rational coefficients and an elementary access to their asymptotic behaviour as
n\to\infty
; more importantly, the two coefficients of
\zeta(3)
in these forms are proportional (with factor
7
), so that
7r_{n}-\hat{r}_{n}
belongs to the space
\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Q}\zeta(5)+\dots+\mathbb{Q}\zeta(s)
. Finally, using
7r_{n}-\hat{r}_{n}>0
and the asymptotics
\lambda_{n}(7r_{n}-\hat{r}_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
of the linear forms

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(7r_{n}-\hat{r}_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\zeta(5)+\mathbb{Z}\zeta(7)+\dots+\mathbb{Z}\zeta(s)

when
s=25
, we conclude that it cannot happen that all the quantities
\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\dots,\allowbreak\zeta(25)
are rational.

The original idea of using the so-called well-poised hypergeometric series to construct linear forms in zeta values of a given parity is due
to Ball and Rivoal
[
1
]
; our new ingredient here is using simultaneously such series and their ‘twists by half’, for an appropriate choice of the parameters. More precisely, our hypergeometric series assume the form

\displaystyle r_{n}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}R_{n}(\nu)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{r}_{n}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}R_{n}\big(\nu-\tfrac{1}{2}\big),

(1)

where the rational-function summand
R_{n}(t)
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle R(t)=R_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{n!^{s-5}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t-j)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t+n+j)\cdot 2^{6n}\prod_{j=1}^{3n}\big(t-n-\frac{1}{2}+j\big)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(t+j)^{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{6n}n!^{s-5}\prod_{j=0}^{6n}\big(t-n+\frac{1}{2}j\big)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(t+j)^{s+1}}.

(2)

The following Sections
2
and
3
discuss, respectively, the arithmetic and analysis of the forms (
1
). In Section
4

we use this information to conclude with the proof of the claimed result and make some relevant comments.

2
Arithmetic ingredients

The notation
d_{n}
will be used for the least common multiple of
1,2,\dots,n
. Recall that the prime number theorem is equivalent to the asymptotics

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{n}^{1/n}=e.

(3)

A rational function
S(t)
of the form

\displaystyle S(t)=\frac{P(t)}{(t-t_{1})^{s_{1}}(t-t_{2})^{s_{2}}\dotsb(t-t_{q})^{s_{q}}},

whose denominator has degree larger than its numerator, possesses a unique partial-fraction decomposition

\displaystyle S(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sum_{i=1}^{s_{j}}\frac{b_{i,j}}{(t-t_{j})^{i}}.

The coefficients here can be computed on the basis of explicit formula

\displaystyle b_{i,j}=\frac{1}{(s_{j}-i)!}\bigl(S(t)(t-t_{j})^{s_{j}}\bigr)^{(s_{j}-i)}\Big|_{t=t_{j}}

for all
i
,
j
in question. This procedure can be illustrated on the following examples, in which all the exponents
s_{j}
are equal to 1:

\displaystyle\frac{n!}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(t+j)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}}{t+k},

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t-j)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(t+j)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{n+k}\binom{n+k}{n}\binom{n}{k}}{t+k},

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t+n+j)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(t+j)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}\binom{2n-k}{n}\binom{n}{k}}{t+k},

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t+\frac{1}{2}-j)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(t+j)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{n+k}\binom{2n+2k}{2n}\binom{2n}{n+k}}{t+k},

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t-\frac{1}{2}+j)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(t+j)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2n-2k}{n-k}}{t+k},

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(t+n-\frac{1}{2}+j)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(t+j)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}\binom{4n-2k}{2n}\binom{2n}{k}}{t+k}.

It also means that the function
R(t)
in (
2
) can be written as

\displaystyle R(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{i,k}}{(t+k)^{i}}

(4)

with the recipe to compute the coefficients
a_{i,k}
in its partial-fraction decomposition. At the same time, the function
R(t)
is a product of ‘simpler’ rational functions given above, with all coefficients of their partial fractions being integral.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
k_{1},\dots,k_{q}
be pairwise distinct numbers from the set
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
and
s_{1},\dots,s_{q}
positive integers. Then the coefficients in the expansion

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{q}(t+k_{j})^{s_{j}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sum_{i=1}^{s_{j}}\frac{b_{i,j}}{(t+k_{j})^{i}}

satisfy

\displaystyle d_{n}^{s-i}b_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\text{where}\quad i=1,\dots,s_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad j=1,\dots,q,

(5)

where
s=s_{1}+\dots+s_{q}
.

In particular,

\displaystyle d_{n}^{s-i}a_{i,k}\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\text{where}\quad i=1,\dots,s\quad\text{and}\quad k=0,1,\dots,n,

(6)

for the coefficients in (
4
).

Proof 2.2
.

Denote the rational function in question by
S(t)
. The statement is trivially true when
q=1
, therefore we assume that
q\geq 2
. In view of the symmetry of the data, it is sufficient to demonstrate the inclusions (
5
) for
j=1
. Differentiating a related product
m
times, for any
m\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}\bigl(S(t)(t+k_{1})^{s_{1}}\bigr)^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m!}\left(\prod_{j=2}^{q}(t+k_{j})^{-s_{j}}\right)^{(m)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{q}\geq 0\\
\ell_{2}+\dots+\ell_{q}=m\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=2}^{q}\frac{1}{\ell_{j}!}\bigl((t+k_{j})^{-s_{j}}\bigr)^{(\ell_{j})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{q}\geq 0\\
\ell_{2}+\dots+\ell_{q}=m\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=2}^{q}(-1)^{\ell_{j}}\binom{s_{j}+\ell_{j}-1}{\ell_{j}}(t+k_{j})^{-(s_{j}+\ell_{j})}.

This implies that

\displaystyle b_{i,1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{q}\geq 0\\
\ell_{2}+\dots+\ell_{q}=s_{1}-i\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=2}^{q}(-1)^{\ell_{j}}\binom{s_{j}+\ell_{j}-1}{\ell_{j}}\frac{1}{(k_{j}-k_{1})^{s_{j}+\ell_{j}}}

for
i=1,\dots,s_{1}
. Using
d_{n}/(k_{j}-k_{1})\in\mathbb{Z}
for
j=2,\dots,q
and
\sum_{j=2}^{q}(s_{j}+\ell_{j})=s-i
for each individual summand, we deduce the desired inclusion in (
5
) for
j=1
, hence for any
j
.

The second claim in the lemma follows from considering
R(t)
as a product of the ‘simpler’ rational functions.

Lemma 2.3
.

For the coefficients
a_{i,k}
in (
4
), we have

\displaystyle a_{i,k}=(-1)^{i-1}a_{i,n-k}\qquad\text{for}\quad k=0,1,\dots,n\quad\text{and}\quad i=1,\dots,s,

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,k}=0\qquad\text{for}\ i\ \text{even}.

Proof 2.4
.

Since
s
is odd, the function (
2
) possesses the following (well-poised) symmetry:
R(-t-n)=-R(t)
. Substitution of the relation into (
4
) results in

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{i,k}}{(t+k)^{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{i,k}}{(-t-n+k)^{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}(-1)^{i}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{i,k}}{(t+n-k)^{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}(-1)^{i}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{i,n-k}}{(t+k)^{i}},

and the identities in the lemma follow from the uniqueness of decomposition into partial fractions. The second statement follows from

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,k}=(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,n-k}=(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,k}.

∎

Lemma 2.5
.

For each
n
,

\displaystyle r_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=2\\
i\;\text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{s}a_{i}\zeta(i)+a_{0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{r}_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=2\\
i\;\text{odd}\end{subarray}}^{s}a_{i}\big(2^{i}-1\big)\zeta(i)+\hat{a}_{0},

with the following inclusions available
:

\displaystyle d_{n}^{s-i}a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\qquad\text{for}\quad i=3,5,\dots,s,\quad\text{and}\quad d_{n}^{s}a_{0},d_{n}^{s}\hat{a}_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Notice that

\displaystyle\big(2^{i}-1\big)\zeta(i)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\big(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}

for
i\geq 2
.

Proof 2.6
.

Our strategy here is to write the series in (
1
) using the partial-fraction decomposition (
4
) of
R(t)
. To treat the first sum
r_{n}
we additionally introduce an auxiliary parameter
z>0
, which we later specialise to
z=1
:

\displaystyle r_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}R_{n}(\nu)z^{\nu}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{i,k}z^{\nu}}{(\nu+k)^{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,k}z^{-k}\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{\nu+k}}{(\nu+k)^{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,k}z^{-k}\left(\operatorname{Li}_{i}(z)-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{z^{\ell}}{\ell^{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\operatorname{Li}_{i}(z)\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,k}z^{-k}-\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{a_{i,k}z^{-(k-\ell)}}{\ell^{i}},

where

\displaystyle\operatorname{Li}_{i}(z)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{\ell}}{\ell^{i}}

for
i=1,\dots,s
are the polylogarithmic functions. The latter are well defined at
z=1
for
i\geq 2
, where
\operatorname{Li}_{i}(1)=\zeta(i)
, while
\operatorname{Li}_{1}(z)=-\log(1-z)
does not have a limit as
z\to 1^{-}
. By taking the limit as
z\to 1^{-}
in the above derivation and using
R_{n}(\nu)=O\big(\nu^{-2}\big)
as
\nu\to\infty
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{1,k}=\lim_{z\to 1^{-}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{1,k}z^{-k}=0,

and

\displaystyle r_{n}=\sum_{i=2}^{s}\zeta(i)\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,k}-\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\ell^{i}}.

(7)

We proceed similarly for
\hat{r}_{n}
, omitting introduction of the auxiliary parameter
z
. Since
R(t)
in (
2
) vanishes at
t=-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{3}{2},\dots,-n+\frac{1}{2}
, we can shift the starting point of summation for
\hat{r}_{n}
to
t=-m-\frac{1}{2}
, where
m=\big\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\big\rfloor
, so that

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu=-m}^{\infty}R_{n}\big(\nu-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)=\sum_{\nu=-m}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{a_{i,k}}{\big(\nu+k-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,k}\sum_{\nu=-m}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\big(\nu+k-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{i,k}\sum_{\nu=-m}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\big(\nu+k-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}a_{i,k}\sum_{\nu=-m}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\big(\nu+k-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{i,k}\left(\sum_{\ell=k-m}^{0}\frac{1}{\big(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\big(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad{}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}a_{i,k}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\big(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-m-1}\frac{1}{\big(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{s}(2^{i}-1)\zeta(i)\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{i,k}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{i,k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-k}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{\big(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}

\displaystyle\quad{}-\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}a_{i,k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-m-1}\frac{1}{\big(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}.

(8)

Now the statement of the lemma follows from the representations in (
7
) and (
8
), Lemma
2.3
, the inclusions (
6
) of Lemma
2.1
and

\displaystyle d_{n}^{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\ell^{i}}\in\mathbb{Z}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad 0\leq k\leq n\quad\text{and}\quad i\geq 1,

\displaystyle d_{n}^{i}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-k}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(\ell+\frac{1}{2})^{i}}\in\mathbb{Z}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad 0\leq k\leq m\quad\text{and}\quad i\geq 1,

\displaystyle d_{n-1}^{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-m-1}\frac{1}{\big(\ell-\frac{1}{2}\big)^{i}}\in\mathbb{Z}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad m+1\leq k\leq n\quad\text{and}\quad i\geq 1.

∎

3
Asymptotic behaviour

In this section we make frequent use of Stirling’s asymptotic formula

\displaystyle n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\qquad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty,

and its corollary

\displaystyle\binom{2n}{n}\sim\frac{2^{2n}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\qquad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty

for the central binomial coefficients. (One may also use somewhat weaker but ‘more elementary’ lower and upper bounds

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{n}\log x\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\log(n!)\leq\int_{2}^{n+1}\log x\,{\mathrm{d}}x

for the factorial coming out from estimating integral sums of the logarithm function, with a nemesis of running into more sophisticated versions for the asymptotics and inequalities below.)

Because the rational function
R_{n}(t)
in (
2
) vanishes at
1,2,\dots,n
and at
\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2},\dots,\allowbreak n-\penalty\frac{1}{2}
,
the hypergeometric series (
1
) can be alternatively written as

\displaystyle r_{n}=\sum_{\nu=n+1}^{\infty}R_{n}(\nu)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{r}_{n}=\sum_{\nu=n+1}^{\infty}R_{n}(\nu-\tfrac{1}{2})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{c}_{k},

with the involved summands

\displaystyle c_{k}=R_{n}(n+1+k)=\frac{2^{6n}n!^{s-5}\prod_{j=0}^{6n}\big(k+1+\frac{1}{2}j\big)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(n+k+1+j)^{s+1}}=\frac{n!^{s-5}(6n+2k+2)!(n+k)!^{s+1}}{2(2k+1)!(2n+k+1)!^{s+1}}

(9)

and

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{k}=R_{n}\big(n+\tfrac{1}{2}+k\big)=\frac{2^{6n}n!^{s-5}\prod_{j=0}^{6n}\big(k+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}j\big)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}\big(n+k+\frac{1}{2}+j\big)^{s+1}}

strictly
positive
. Observe that

\displaystyle\frac{c_{k}}{\hat{c}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{6n}(2k+2+j)}{\prod_{j=0}^{6n}(2k+1+j)}\cdot\left(\prod_{j=0}^{n}\frac{n+k+\frac{1}{2}+j}{n+k+1+j}\right)^{s+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{6n+2k+2}{2k+1}\cdot\left(2^{-2(n+1)}\frac{\binom{4n+2k+2}{2n+k+1}}{\binom{2n+2k}{n+k}}\right)^{s+1}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{6n+2k+2}{2k+1}\left(\frac{n+k}{2n+k+1}\right)^{(s+1)/2}\qquad\text{as}\quad n+k\to\infty.

(10)

Lemma 3.1
.

For
s\geq 7
odd,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}^{1/n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{r}_{n}^{1/n}=g(x_{0})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r_{n}}{\hat{r}_{n}}=1

where

\displaystyle g(x)=\frac{2^{6}(x+3)^{6}(x+1)^{s+1}}{(x+2)^{2(s+1)}}

and
x_{0}
is the unique positive zero of the polynomial

\displaystyle x(x+2)^{(s+1)/2}-(x+3)(x+1)^{(s+1)/2}.

Proof 3.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_{k}}=\frac{\big(k+3n+\frac{3}{2}\big)(k+3n+2)}{(k+1)\big(k+\frac{3}{2}\big)}\left(\frac{k+n+1}{k+2n+2}\right)^{s+1}\sim f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{2}\qquad\text{as}\quad n+k\to\infty,

(11)

where

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{x+3}{x}\left(\frac{x+1}{x+2}\right)^{(s+1)/2}.

For an ease of notation write
q=(s+1)/2\geq 4
. Since

\displaystyle\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}=\frac{1}{x+3}-\frac{1}{x}+q\left(\frac{1}{x+1}-\frac{1}{x+2}\right)=\frac{(q-3)x^{2}+3(q-3)x-6}{x(x+1)(x+2)(x+3)}

and the quadratic polynomial in the latter numerator has a unique positive zero
x_{1}
, the function
f(x)
monotone decreases from
+\infty
to
f(x_{1})
when
x
ranges from
0
to
x_{1}
and then monotone increases from
f(x_{1})
to
f(+\infty)=1
(not attaining the value!) when
x
ranges from
x_{1}
to
+\infty
. In particular, there is exactly one positive solution
x_{0}
to
f(x)=1
. Notice that
0<x_{0}<1
, because
f(1)=4\cdot(2/3)^{q}<1
.

The information gained and asymptotics in (
11
) imply that
c_{k+1}/c_{k}>1
for the indices
k<x_{0}n-\gamma\sqrt{n}
and
c_{k+1}/c_{k}<1

for
k>x_{0}n+\gamma\sqrt{n}
for an appropriate choice of
\gamma>0
dictated by application of Stirling’s formula to the factorials defining
c_{k}
in (
9
) (see
[
2
, Section 3.4]
as well as the second proof of Lemma 3 in
[
1
]
). This means that the asymptotic behaviour of the sum
r_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}
is determined by the asymptotics of
c_{k_{0}}
and its neighbours
c_{k}
, where
k_{0}=k_{0}(n)\sim x_{0}n
and
|k-k_{0}|\leq\gamma\sqrt{n}
, so that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}^{1/n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{k_{0}(n)}^{1/n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Biggl(\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{(s-5)n}\left(\frac{6n+2k_{0}+2}{e}\right)^{6n+2k_{0}+2}\left(\frac{e}{2k_{0}+1}\right)^{2k_{0}+1}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\left(\frac{n+k_{0}}{e}\right)^{(s+1)(n+k_{0})}\left(\frac{e}{2n+k_{0}+1}\right)^{(s+1)(2n+k_{0}+1)}\Biggr)^{1/n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2x_{0}+6)^{2x_{0}+6}(x_{0}+1)^{(s+1)(x_{0}+1)}}{(2x_{0})^{2x_{0}}(x_{0}+2)^{(s+1)(x_{0}+2)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{6}(x_{0}+3)^{6}(x_{0}+1)^{s+1}}{(x_{0}+2)^{2(s+1)}}\cdot f(x_{0})^{2x_{0}}=g(x_{0}).

It now follows from (
10
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{c}_{k+1}}{\hat{c}_{k}}\sim\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_{k}}\qquad\text{as}\quad n+k\to\infty,

(12)

so that the above analysis applies to the sum
\hat{r}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{c}_{k}
as well, and its asymptotic behaviour is determined by the asymptotics of
\hat{c}_{k_{0}}
and its neighbours
\hat{c}_{k}
, where
k_{0}=k_{0}(n)\sim x_{0}n
and
|k-k_{0}|\leq\hat{\gamma}\sqrt{n}
. From (
12
) we deduce that the limits of
\hat{c}_{k_{0}(n)}^{1/n}
and
c_{k_{0}(n)}^{1/n}
as
n\to\infty
coincide, hence
\hat{r}_{n}^{1/n}\to g(x_{0})
as
n\to\infty
. In addition to this, we also get

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r_{n}}{\hat{r}_{n}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{c_{k_{0}(n)}}{\hat{c}_{k_{0}(n)}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{6n+2k_{0}+2}{2k_{0}+1}\left(\frac{n+k_{0}}{2n+k_{0}+1}\right)^{(s+1)/2}=f(x_{0}),

which leads to the remaining limiting relation.

4
Conclusion

We choose
s=25
and apply Lemma
3.1
to find out that
7r_{n}-\hat{r}_{n}>0
for
n
sufficiently large, and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(7r_{n}-\hat{r}_{n})^{1/n}=g(x_{0})=\exp(-25.292363\dots),

where
x_{0}=0.00036713\dots
. Assuming that the odd zeta values from
\zeta(5)
to
\zeta(25)
are all rational and denoting by
a
their common denominator, we use Lemma
2.5
and the asymptotics (
3
) to conclude that the sequence of
positive integers

\displaystyle ad_{n}^{25}(7r_{n}-\hat{r}_{n})

tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
; contradiction. Thus, at least one of the numbers
\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\dots,\allowbreak\zeta(25)
is irrational.

Those who count the prime number theorem as insufficiently elementary may use weaker versions of (
3
), for example,
d_{n}<3^{n}
from
[
5
]
and the choice
s=33
instead, to arrive at the same conclusion (for the larger value of
s
, of course).

\zeta(3)

to significantly reduce the size of

s

\zeta(5)
,
\zeta(7)
,
\zeta(9)

is irrational’ unchanged, we do not discuss this generalisation in greater details. We point out, however, that there are other applications of the hypergeometric ‘twist-by-half’ idea, some discussed in the joint papers
