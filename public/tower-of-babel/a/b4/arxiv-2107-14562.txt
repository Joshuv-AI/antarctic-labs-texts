Time-Slicing Path-integral in Curved Space
Mingnan Ding 1, Xiangjun Xing 1,2,3

Abstract
Path integrals constitute powerful representations for both quantum and stochastic dynamics. Yet despite many decades of intensive studies, there is no consensus on how to formulate them for dynamics in curved space, or how to make them covariant with respect to nonlinear transform of variables (NTV). In this work, we construct a rigorous and covariant formulation of time-slicing path integrals for dynamics in curved space. We first establish a rigorous criterion for equivalence of time-slice Green’s function (TSGF) in the continuum limit (Lemma 1). This implies the existence of infinitely many equivalent representations for time-slicing path integral. We then show that, for any dynamics with second order generator, all time-slice actions are equivalent to a Gaussian (Lemma 2). We further construct a continuous family of equivalent path-integral actions parameterized by an interpolation parameter α ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \alpha\in[0,1] (Lemma 3). We then use these lemmas to develop time-slicing path integral representations both for quantum mechanics and for classical Markov processes in curved space. The action generically contains a term linear in Δ ​ 𝒙 \Delta\boldsymbol{x} , whose concrete form depends on α \alpha . Finally we also establish the covariance of our path-integral formalism, by demonstrating how the action transforms under NTV. The α = 0 \alpha=0 representation of time-slice action is particularly convenient because it is Gaussian and transforms as a scalar, as long as Δ ​ 𝒙 \Delta\boldsymbol{x} transforms according to Ito’s formula .

There are however many dynamic processes, either quantum or classical, happening in curved spaces. There are also processes happening in Euclidean space but with curved boundary, and hence are better formulated using curvilinear coordinates. There are also stochastic processes with multiplicative noises, which resemble very much dynamics in curved spaces. For these processes, it has been known for long time that the above time-slicing prescription of path integral breaks down. In this work, we shall develop a detailed and systematic construction of time-slicing path integral in curved space, and resolve all the above-mentioned confusing issues. Using asymptotic analysis, we shall first establish three lemmas with reasonable degree of mathematical rigor. These lemmas constitute the foundations of all later results. With Lemma 1, we explains why there are so many seemingly different but equivalent representations of time-slicing path integrals, and establish the criterion of equivalence. With Lemma 2, we prove there is a Gaussian representation of time-slicing path integrals. With Lemma 3, we explicitly construct a one-parameter family of equivalent representations for the time-slice Green’s function (TSGF), parameterized by
\alpha\in[0,1]
. The time-slicing path integral, whether it is for the quantum or for the classical stochastic process, can then be obtained by straightforward application of these three lemmas.

For classical Markov processes, we shall further study the connection between time-slicing path integral representation and Langevin dynamics representation. As is well-known, there are infinite number of equivalent representations for a given Langevin dynamics, each parameterized by a continuous parameter
\bar{\alpha}
, which specifies where the noise amplitudes are evaluated. The case of Ito representation (
\bar{\alpha}=0
) is particularly simple, because it implies a linear relation between rate of slow variables and noises, conditioned on the slow variables at earlier time. Exploiting the equivalence between different representations of Langevin dynamics, we shall derive another two-parameter family of equivalent representations for time-slicing path integral of classical Markov processes.

The last major issue we shall address is the covariance property. We first clarify how to construct the new time-slice action when a nonlinear transformation of variable is carried out. We further show that our time-slice action does not transform as a scalar under usual transformation rules of tensor algebra, mainly due to the non-differentiable nature of typical paths that dominate path integrals. Finally, we explicitly show that the
\alpha=0
time-slice action can be made invariant if we demand that
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
transform according to Ito’s formula.

The remaining of this work is organized as follows. In Section II, we establish three lemmas, which constitute the base of all later results. In Section III, we apply these lemmas to obtain a continuous family of equivalent representations for time-slicing path integral. We also revisit the problem of Edwards and Gulyaev and discuss the geometric origin of spurious drift. In Section IV, we study the connection between classical nonlinear Langevin dynamics and time-slicing path integral, and obtain a more general family of equivalent path integral representations for classical Markov processes. In Section V, we discuss the covariance property of time-slicing path integral. Finally, in Section VI, we draw the concluding remark and outline future research directions. In Appendix A, we supply a detailed proof of Lemma 3.

Three Lemmas about TSGF

Consider a Riemannian manifold with coordinate system
\boldsymbol{x}=\{x^{1},x^{2},\cdots,x^{d}\}
and metric tensor
g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})
, and
g(\boldsymbol{x})=\det(g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}))
. We define the volume measure
d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})
and the invariant volume measure
dv(\boldsymbol{x})
as

\displaystyle d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle dx^{1}dx^{2}\cdots dx^{d},

(16a)

\displaystyle dv(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x})}\,d\mu(\boldsymbol{x}).

(16b)

We also define the covariant Dirac delta function as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\delta(x^{i}-x_{0}^{i})=\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}),

(17)

where
\delta(x^{i}-x_{0}^{i})
is the usual 1d Dirac delta function. For an arbitrary function
f(\boldsymbol{x})
on the manifold, we have

\int f(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})dv(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}).

(18)

We consider a dynamics generated by a second order partial differential operator:

\displaystyle\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x})}}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x})}D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})

(19)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x})}}\partial_{i}\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x})}F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x})-\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}),

where
D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})=D^{ji}(\boldsymbol{x})
is symmetric. For now we assume that
D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})
is non-singular, and that
D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}),F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x}),\Phi(\boldsymbol{x})
do not depend on time. Generalization to the case where these functions are time-dependent is straightforward. The case of singular
D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})
is more complicated, and shall not be discussed in this work. It is clear that
\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{x})
as defined in Eq. (
19
) contains as special cases both the quantum Hamiltonian Eq. (
9
) and the classical Fokker-Planck operator Eq. (
14
).

The Green’s function for Eq. (
19

G(\boldsymbol{x},t|\boldsymbol{x}_{0},0)=e^{\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{x})t}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}),

G(\boldsymbol{x},t|\boldsymbol{x}_{0},0)
is also known as

(quantum mechanics), or

As illustrated in Fig.

[0,t]

N

\Delta t=t/N
, and introduce TSGF:

G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)=e^{\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{x})\Delta t}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}),

(21)

with
\Delta t=t/N
. Equation (

) can be rewritten into a product of
N-1

\displaystyle G(\boldsymbol{x},t|\boldsymbol{x}_{0},0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dv_{N-1}\cdots dv_{1}\,G(\boldsymbol{x}_{N}|\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1};\Delta t)

(22)

\displaystyle\,\,\cdots G(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|\boldsymbol{x}_{1};\Delta t)G(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t),\quad

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}=\boldsymbol{x}
, and

. The discretized path
(\boldsymbol{x}_{N},\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
forms

[
19

, which can be used to construct rigorously the sigma algebra of the path space.

To construct path integral representation of Green’s function, we approximate each TSGF in the RHS of Eq. (
22
) by an exponential form. The approximation should be such that in the
continuum limit

\Delta t\rightarrow 0
, the exact Green’s function is recovered. More concretely, all moments of the approximated Green’s function, i.e., the averages of powers of
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
calculated using the approximated version of RHS of Eq. (
22
), must give the exact answers in the limit
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
. Any such approximation of TSGF shall be called
a representation
. Now recall that the number
N
of steps is related to the duration
\Delta t
of each step via
\Delta t=t/N
. If the error in each step is much smaller than
\Delta t
, then the total error in the entire interval is much smaller than
N\times\Delta t=t
, and hence becomes negligible in the continuum limit. Likewise, if two approximations of
G(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{k};\Delta t)
yield the same moments up to order of
\Delta t
, they become indistinguishable in continuum limit, and we shall call these two approximations
equivalent
. It then follows that all representations of TSGF are equivalent to each other. Hence we obtain the first lemma:

Lemma 1

An approximation of TSGF is a representation if and only if it yields correct moments up to
\Delta t
. Two approximations of TSGF are equivalent if they yield the same moments up to
\Delta t
.

As we will demonstrate, there are infinitely many seemingly different but equivalent representations of TSGF, each of them can be used to construct the time-slicing path integral representation of Green’s function. Lemma 1 gives a concrete criterion for testing of the correctness of various approximations, and of the equivalence between different representations of TSGF. Whilst the content of Lemma 1 was recognized or used implicitly by many authors previously, it is important to make it explicit, as it will play an essential role in the analyses below.

\Delta\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}

\Delta x^{i}

\displaystyle\langle\Delta x^{i_{1}}\cdots\Delta x^{i_{M}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\int\!\!dv(\boldsymbol{x})\Delta x^{i_{1}}\cdots\Delta x^{i_{M}}G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t),

\displaystyle\langle\Delta x^{i_{1}}\cdots\Delta x^{i_{M}}\rangle

\displaystyle\!\!\int\!\!dv(\boldsymbol{x})\Delta x^{i_{1}}\cdots\Delta x^{i_{M}}\!\left[1+\Delta t\,\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{x})+\cdots\right]\!\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x},{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}).

), and (
17
), and integrating by parts, we see that the lowest three moments are respectively:

\displaystyle\langle 1\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\Delta t+O(\Delta t^{2}),

\displaystyle\langle\Delta x^{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\Delta t+O(\Delta t^{2}),

(23c)

\displaystyle\langle\Delta x^{i}\Delta x^{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\Delta t+O(\Delta t^{2}).

For all
M\geq 3
, the integral in Eq. (
23b
) vanishes identically (up to
\Delta t
) because of the Dirac delta function. Hence we arrive at the second lemma:

Lemma 2

For all
M\geq 3
,
M
-th order moments of TSGF Eq. (
20
) are at least of order
\Delta t^{2}
, and hence makes no contribution in the continuum limit.

Now consider the following Gaussian distribution:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})\,e^{-(\Delta x^{i}-F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\Delta t)\frac{D^{-1}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}{4\Delta t}(\Delta x^{i}-F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\Delta t)-\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\Delta t}}{\sqrt{(4\pi\Delta t)^{d}\det D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}},

(24)

where
D^{-1}_{ij}
is the inverse matrix of
D^{ij}
, and
\det D^{ij}
is the determinant of the matrix
D^{ij}
. Note that all functions are evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
, and we are attaching the volume element in Eq. (
24
). It is clear that the three lowest order moments of Eq. (
24
) are those given in Eq. (
23c
), and all higher order moments are at least of order
\Delta t^{2}
. Then according to Lemma 1, Eq. (
24
) is a representation of TSGF, and hence can be used to construct the time-slicing path integral. We shall call Eq. (
24
) the
Gaussian representation
of TSGF.

Whilst lemmas 1 and 2 are easy to establish, their importance can hardly be overrated. They constitute a starting point for a systematic construction of equivalent representations of time-slicing path integrals. In particular, to verify that certain approximation of TSGF is a representation, i.e., it can be used to construct time-slicing path integral, we only need to show that (i) its lowest three moments are the same as those of Eq. (
24
), and (ii) all higher moments are smaller than
\Delta t
.
For a discussion of Gaussian representation of TSGF from the viewpoint of renormalization group, see Ref.
[
61
]
.

Using these results, we can construct a continuous family of equivalent representations of TSGF that is parameterized by an interpolation parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
. This is the content of Lemma 3:

Lemma 3

Let
D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})=D^{ji}(\boldsymbol{x})
be symmetric and positive,
F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
a vector, and
\Phi(\boldsymbol{x})
a scalar, all functions of
\boldsymbol{x}
. Let
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\alpha\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
with
\alpha\in[0,1]
. The following one-parameter family of distributions are equivalent to each other, in the sense that their moments are all equal up to order
\Delta t
:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\frac{d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})}{\sqrt{(4\pi\Delta t)^{d}\det D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})}}\exp\bigg\{\!-\!\Big(\Delta x^{i}-F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})\Delta t+2\alpha\partial_{k}D^{ik}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})\Delta t\Big)\frac{D_{ij}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})}{4\Delta t}\Big(\Delta x^{j}-F^{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})\Delta t+2\alpha\partial_{l}D^{jl}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})\Delta t\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 122.34685pt-\,\alpha\,\partial_{i}F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})\Delta t+\alpha^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})\Delta t-\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})\,\Delta t\bigg\}.

(25)

Note that all functions are evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha}
.

There is an extension of Lemma 3, which provides more flexibility in implementation of time-slicing. Using similar methods, it can be proved that the following two-parameter family of equivalent representations, where
D^{ij}
is evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}}=\boldsymbol{x}+\alpha_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
, and whereas
F^{i}
is evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{2}}=\boldsymbol{x}+\alpha_{2}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
, whilst
\Phi
can be evaluated at arbitrary place:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})}{\sqrt{(4\pi\Delta t)^{d}\det D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}})}}\exp\bigg\{

(26)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\bigg(\Delta x^{i}-F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{2}})\Delta t+2\alpha_{1}\partial_{k}D^{ik}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}})\Delta t\bigg)\frac{D^{-1}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}})}{4\Delta t}\bigg(\Delta x^{j}-F^{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{2}})\Delta t+2\alpha_{1}\partial_{l}D^{jl}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}})\Delta t\bigg)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\partial_{i}F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{2}})\Delta t+\alpha_{1}^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}D^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}})\Delta t-\Phi\Delta t\bigg\}.

The proof of Lemma 3 is technically very complicated, and is presented in Appendix
A
. Historically, this equivalent class was obtained by Wissel in 1979
[
59
]
. Wissel’s work however has not received much attention, most likely due to its lack of mathematical rigor. Our proof of Eq. (
25
) is systematic and rigorous. For 1d case, Eq. (
25
) reduces to the result of Haken
[
58
]
.

Several important comments are in order. Firstly note that the
\alpha=0
version of Eq. (
25
) is precisely the Gaussian representation Eq. (
24
). Secondly for
\alpha\neq 0
, Eq. (
25
) is
not
Gaussian in
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
, due to the hidden dependence of various functions on
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
. Thirdly, there are (infinitely many) other representations of TSGF that do not assume the form of Eq. (
25
). For example, the factor
\det D^{ij}
in front of the exponential may be evaluated at a point different from
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha}
. This leads to further revision of the time-slice action. We shall not explore this issue further.

3
The
\alpha
-Representation

We will now study time-slicing path integral representation in curved space, first for quantum mechanics, and then for classical Markov processes. We shall follow the procedure outlined in the preceding section, first derive a Gaussian representation, then construct arbitrary
\alpha
-representation with
\alpha\in[0,1]
. These representations are equivalent to each other, as guaranteed by Lemma 3.

3.1
Quantum mechanics in curved space

The imaginary time Green’s function is already defined in Eqs. (
10
). The TSGF can be written as

G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)=e^{-\Delta t\hat{H}(\boldsymbol{x})/\hbar}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}),

(27)

where
\hat{H}
is given in Eq. (
9
). The moments of
G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)
are calculated up to the order of
\Delta t
, by expanding the exponential and using integration by parts:

\displaystyle\langle 1\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\int d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})\,\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x})}\,\,e^{-\Delta t\hat{H}(\boldsymbol{x})/\hbar}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=1-\frac{\Delta t}{\hbar}V(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})+O(\Delta t^{2}),

(28a)

\displaystyle\langle\Delta x^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\int d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})\,\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x})}\Delta x^{k}e^{-\Delta t\hat{H}(\boldsymbol{x})/\hbar}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{2m}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{j}\left(\sqrt{g}g^{jk}\right)\right)_{\!\!0}+O(\Delta t^{2}),

(28b)

\displaystyle\langle\Delta x^{k}\Delta x^{l}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\int d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})\,\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x})}\Delta x^{k}\Delta x^{l}e^{-\Delta t\hat{H}(\boldsymbol{x})/\hbar}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{m}\,g^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})+O(\Delta t^{2}),

(28c)

where
(\cdots)_{0}
means that all functions inside the bracket are evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
.

Using these moments, we can construct a Gaussian expression for the TSGF:

\displaystyle dv(\boldsymbol{x})\,G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)=\frac{\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}\,d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})}{(2\pi\hbar\Delta t/m)^{d/2}}e^{-A^{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)},

(29a)

where the
time-slice action

A^{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)
is given by

\displaystyle A^{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\Delta x^{i}-\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{2m}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\,\partial_{k}\sqrt{g}g^{ki}\right)_{\!\!0}\,\right]\frac{g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}{2\hbar\Delta t/m}\left[\Delta x^{j}-\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{2m}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\,\partial_{l}\sqrt{g}\,g^{jl}\right)_{\!\!0}\,\right]+\frac{\Delta t}{\hbar}V(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}),

(29b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\Delta x^{i}+\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{2m}g^{kl}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\Gamma^{i}_{kl}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\right]\frac{g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}{2\hbar\Delta t/m}\left[\Delta x^{j}+\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{2m}g^{mn}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\Gamma^{j}_{mn}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\right]+\frac{\Delta t}{\hbar}V(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}),

(29c)

where all functions are evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
, and
\Gamma^{j}_{mn}
is the Christoffel symbol, constructed from the metric tensor:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{k}_{ij}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,g^{kl}(\partial_{i}g_{jl}+\partial_{j}g_{il}-\partial_{l}g_{ij}),\vskip-5.69054pt

(30a)

and we have used the contracting relations:

\displaystyle g^{kl}\Gamma^{i}_{kl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{k}(\sqrt{g}g^{ik}).

(30b)

The distribution Eq. (
29a
) is Gaussian, because the action
A^{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)
is quadratic in
\boldsymbol{x}
, and the prefactor
\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}\,d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})
is independent of
\boldsymbol{x}
. One interesting feature about Eq. (
29b
) is that the average of
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
is non-vanishing if the metric tensor is not constant. This is what we call the
quantum spurious drift
, which has been missed by many previous studies on path integral representation of quantum mechanics in curved space.

Now let us compare Eqs. (
29b
) with (
24
), and make the identification
F^{i}=({\hbar}/{(2m\sqrt{g}}))\partial_{k}(\sqrt{g}g^{ki})
,
D^{ij}=\hbar\,g^{ij}/(2m)
, and
\Phi=V/\hbar
. Applying Lemma 3, we find the following one-parameter family of representations for TSGF, all of which equivalent to Eqs. (
29
):

\displaystyle dv(\boldsymbol{x})\,G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{g(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})}\,d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})}{(2\pi\hbar\Delta t/m)^{d/2}}e^{-A^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)},

(31a)

\displaystyle A^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\Delta x^{i}-\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{m}\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{g}}\partial_{k}\sqrt{g}g^{ik}-\alpha\partial_{k}g^{ik}\right)_{\!\!\alpha}\right]\frac{g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})}{2\hbar\Delta t/m}\left[\Delta x^{j}-\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{m}\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{g}}\partial_{l}\sqrt{g}g^{jl}-\alpha\partial_{l}g^{il}\right)_{\!\!\alpha}\right]

(31b)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar\alpha}{2m}\Delta t\bigg(\partial_{i}\sqrt{{1}/{g}}\,\partial_{k}\sqrt{g}g^{ki}\bigg)_{\!\!\alpha}-\frac{\hbar\alpha^{2}}{2m}\Delta t(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}g^{ij})_{\alpha}+\frac{\Delta t}{\hbar}V(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha}).

where
(\cdots)_{\alpha}
means that all functions are evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha}
.
It is seen from Eq. (
31b
) that whilst the detailed form of quantum spurious drift depends on the choice of
\alpha
, it is always non-vanishing for a generic multi-dimensional model. The lesson we learn here is that a typical quantum trajectory in curved space or in curvilinear coordinates behaves as a biased random walk. For the special case
\alpha=1/2
, the quantum spurious drift reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{m}\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{g}}\partial_{k}\sqrt{g}g^{ik}-\alpha\partial_{k}g^{ik}\right)\rightarrow\frac{\hbar}{2m}g^{ik}\partial_{k}\log\sqrt{g}=\frac{\hbar}{2m}g^{ik}\Gamma^{j}_{kj}.

(32)

3.2
Classical Markov processes

Let us now apply the same procedure to the classical case, whose TSGF is

G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)=e^{-\Delta t\hat{L}_{\rm FP}(\boldsymbol{x})}\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}),

(33)

where the Fokker-Planck operator
\hat{L}_{\rm FP}(\boldsymbol{x})
is given in Eq. (
15
). The first three moments can be calculated straightforwardly. Ignoring higher order terms, we have

\displaystyle\langle 1\rangle\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+O(\Delta t^{2}),

(34a)

\displaystyle\langle\Delta x^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{j}\left(\sqrt{g}L^{kj}\right)-L^{kj}\partial_{j}U\right)_{\!\!0}+O(\Delta t^{2}),

(34b)

\displaystyle\langle\Delta x^{k}\Delta x^{l}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Delta t\,B^{kl}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})+O(\Delta t^{2}),

(34c)

where
(\cdots)_{0}
means that all functions inside the bracket are evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
. The matrix
B
is the symmetric part of
L
, i.e.,
2B^{ij}=L^{ij}+L^{ji}
. For classical Markov processes,
G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)
is interpreted as the transition probability. Hence Eq. (
34a
) can be understood as normalization of total probability.

Using these moments, we obtain a Gaussian representation for TSGF:

\displaystyle dv(\boldsymbol{x})\,G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)=\frac{d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})\,e^{-A^{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)}}{\sqrt{(4\pi\Delta t)^{d}\det B^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}},\quad

(35)

where the time-slice action
A^{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)
is:

\displaystyle A^{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)=\bigg[\Delta x^{i}-\Delta t\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{k}\left(\sqrt{g}L^{ik}\right)-L^{ik}\partial_{k}U\right)_{\!\!0}\bigg]\frac{B^{-1}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}{4\Delta t}\bigg[\Delta x^{j}-\Delta t\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{l}\left(\sqrt{g}L^{jl}\right)-L^{jl}\partial_{l}U\right)_{\!\!0}\bigg],\quad\quad\quad

(36)

where
(B^{-1})_{ij}
is the inverse matrix of
B^{ij}
, and all functions are evaluated at the initial point
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
.

Invoking Lemma 3, we obtain a one-parameter family of equivalent representations:

\displaystyle dv(\boldsymbol{x})G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})\,e^{-A^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)}}{\sqrt{(4\pi\Delta t)^{d}\det B^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})}},

(37a)

\displaystyle A^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg[\Delta x^{i}-\Delta t\left(F^{i}-2\alpha\partial_{k}B^{ik}\right)_{\!\alpha}\bigg]\frac{B^{-1}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha})}{4\Delta t}\bigg[\Delta x^{j}-\Delta t\left(F^{j}-2\alpha\partial_{l}B^{jl}\right)_{\alpha}\bigg]

(37b)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\alpha\,(\partial_{i}F^{i})_{\alpha}\Delta t-\alpha^{2}(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}B^{ij})_{\alpha}\Delta t,

where all functions are evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{x}+\alpha\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
, and
F^{i}
is

\displaystyle F^{i}=\Delta t{\sqrt{1/g}}\,\left(\partial_{k}\sqrt{g}L^{ik}\right)-\Delta t\,L^{ik}\partial_{k}U.

(38)

More generally, we can apply the extension of Lemma 3, and obtain a two-parameter family of equivalent representations of TSGF:

\displaystyle dv(\boldsymbol{x})\,G(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)=\frac{d\mu(\boldsymbol{x})\,e^{-A^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)}}{\sqrt{(4\pi\Delta t)^{d}\det B^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}})}},

(39)

\displaystyle A^{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\Delta t)=\alpha_{2}\partial_{i}F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{2}})\Delta t-\alpha_{1}^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}B^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}})\Delta t

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\bigg(\Delta x^{i}-F^{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{2}})\Delta t+2\alpha_{1}\partial_{l}B^{il}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}})\Delta t\bigg)\frac{B^{-1}_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}})}{4\Delta t}\bigg(\Delta x^{j}-F^{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{2}})\Delta t+2\alpha_{1}\partial_{m}B^{jm}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}})\Delta t\bigg),

where
B^{ij}
is evaluated at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{1}}=\boldsymbol{x}+\alpha_{1}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
, and
F^{i}
at
\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha_{2}}=\boldsymbol{x}+\alpha_{2}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
.

3.3
Two Examples

3.3.1
The problem of Edwards-Gulyaev

We consider the problem studied by Edwards and Gulyaev
[
34
]
: a free particle moves in a flat plane using polar coordinates. The quantum Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}r\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{\phi}^{2}\right),

where we have set
\hbar=m=1
. The classical action is

\displaystyle S_{\rm cl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int dt(\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2})

(41a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int dt(\dot{r}^{2}+r^{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}),

(41b)

which transforms as a scalar under NTV. In Cartesian coordinates, the parameter
\alpha
is irrelevant. The TSGF is

\displaystyle dxdy\,G(x,y|x_{0},y_{0};\Delta t)=\frac{dxdy\,e^{-({\Delta x^{2}+\Delta y^{2}})/(2\Delta t)}}{2\pi\Delta t},

(42)

where
\Delta x=x-x_{0},\Delta y=y-y_{0}
. The time-slice action then reads

A(\Delta x,\Delta y;\Delta t)=\frac{\Delta x^{2}+\Delta y^{2}}{2\Delta t}.

(43)

Using polar coordinates:

\displaystyle r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}},

\displaystyle\phi=\arctan(y/x),

(44)

\displaystyle r_{0}=\sqrt{x_{0}^{2}+y_{0}^{2}},

\displaystyle\phi_{0}=\arctan(y_{0}/x_{0}),

(45)

Edwards and Gulyaev transform Eq. (
42
) into

\displaystyle dv(r,\phi)\,G(r,\phi|r_{0},\phi_{0};\Delta t)

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{dv(r,\phi)\,e^{-[{r^{2}+r_{0}^{2}-2rr_{0}\cos(\phi-\phi_{0})}]/(2\Delta t)}}{2\pi\Delta t},

where
dv(r,\phi)=rdrd\phi=dxdy
. Defining
\Delta r=r-r_{0},\Delta\phi=\phi-\phi_{0}
, and realizing that
\Delta r,\Delta\phi\sim\sqrt{\Delta t}
, the negative exponent of Eq. (
46
) can be expanded up to order of
\Delta t
:

\displaystyle\frac{r^{2}+r_{0}^{2}-2rr_{0}\cos(\phi-\phi_{0})}{2\Delta t}=

(47)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\left[\Delta r^{2}+r_{0}^{2}\Delta\phi^{2}+r_{0}\Delta r\Delta\phi^{2}-\frac{r_{0}^{2}\Delta\phi^{4}}{12}\right]+O(\Delta t^{\frac{3}{2}}).

If we only keep the first two terms in the square bracket in RHS of Eq. (
47
), we would obtain
\left(\Delta r^{2}+r_{0}^{2}\Delta\phi^{2}\right)/{2\Delta t}
, which is the counterpart of the classical action (
41b
) of a small step
\Delta t
. However, because
\Delta r,\Delta\phi\sim\sqrt{\Delta t}
, the third and forth terms in the square bracket scale respectively as
\Delta t^{1/2}
and
\Delta t
(taking into account the factor
1/\Delta t
outside the bracket), and hence can not be ignored according to Lemma 1. Edwards and Gulyaev noticed
[
34
]
the importance of the fourth term, but missed the third term.

This problem can be easily solved using our method. We can treat it as either a quantum case or a classical case. As a quantum case, we note that the polar coordinate version of Eq. (
40
) can be written as Eq. (
9
) with
\hbar=m=1
and
V=0
, and

g_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&r^{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad g^{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&r^{-2}\end{pmatrix},\quad g(r)=r^{2}.

(48)

Substituting these back into Eqs. (
29a
) and (
29b
), we find the TSGF:

\displaystyle dv(r,\phi)\,G(r,\phi|r_{0},\phi_{0};\Delta t)=\frac{r_{0}\,drd\phi}{2\pi\Delta t}e^{-A^{0}(\Delta r,\Delta\phi,r_{0},\phi_{0};\Delta t)},

(49)

where the time-slice action
A^{0}(\Delta r,\Delta\phi,r_{0},\phi_{0};\Delta t)
is

A^{0}(\Delta r,\Delta\phi,r_{0},\phi_{0};\Delta t)=\frac{\left(\Delta r-\frac{\Delta t}{2r_{0}}\right)^{2}+r_{0}^{2}\Delta\phi^{2}}{2\Delta t}.

(50)

Note that Eq. (
49
) looks very different from Eq. (
46
). Eq. (
49
) is Gaussian in
\Delta r
and
\Delta\phi
, whereas by contrast, Eq. (
46
) is clearly not Gaussian. However Lemmas 1 and 2 guarantee that these two distributions are equivalent in the sense that they share the same moments up to order
\Delta t
, which are given by

\displaystyle\langle 1\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,

\displaystyle\langle\Delta r\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{2r_{0}},\quad\langle\Delta\phi\rangle=0,

(51)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\Delta r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta t,\quad\sigma^{2}_{\Delta\phi}=\frac{\Delta t}{r_{0}^{2}}.

The most salient feature of Eqs. (
51
) is that the average of
\Delta r
is non-vanishing, and inversely proportional to
r_{0}
and hence diminishes with increasing
r_{0}
. A non-vanishing average of
\Delta r
is an inevitable consequence of the curvilinear nature of the polar coordinates. To see this, consider a particle starting from
(r_{0},\phi_{0})
, and diffuse isotropically. As illustrated in Fig.
2
, the probability of
r
increasing is larger that of
r
decreasing, simply because there are more space with larger radius.

Figure 2:
Diffusion in plane with polar coordinates: Because of the curved nature of the coordinate lines, there is more space with larger radius than with smaller radius. Hence the probability of
dr>0
is larger than that of
dr<0
.

The Hamiltonian Eq. (
40
) can also be understood as negative of Fokker-Planck operator Eq. (
15
) with
U=0
and
L^{ij}=B^{ij}=\frac{1}{2}g^{ij}
. We can then calculate TSGF using Eqs. (
36
), and obtain the same result Eq. (
49
).

Finally, using Lemma 3 we also have the
\alpha
-representation of TSGF:

\displaystyle dv(r,\phi)G(r,\phi|r_{0},\phi_{0};\Delta)

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r_{\alpha}drd\phi}{2\pi\Delta t}\exp{\!\left[-\frac{(\Delta r-\frac{\Delta t}{2r_{\alpha}})^{2}+r_{\alpha}^{2}\Delta\phi^{2}}{2\Delta t}+\frac{\alpha\Delta t}{2r_{\alpha}^{2}}\right]}.

where
r_{\alpha}=\alpha r+(1-\alpha)r_{0}
. Compared to Eq. (
49
), the action in Eq. (
52
) has an additional term proportional to
\Delta t
which is needed to ensure normalization. The spurious drift is independent of
\alpha
since
\partial_{i}g^{ij}=0
in this case.

3.3.2
Free particle on a unit sphere

Consider a quantum particle moving on a 2-sphere with unit radius. The Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{H}=-\frac{1}{2\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\sin\theta\partial_{\theta}-\frac{1}{2\sin^{2}\theta}\partial^{2}_{\phi},

(53)

which is in the form of Eq. (
9
) with
\boldsymbol{x}=(\theta,\phi)
,
g(\boldsymbol{x})=\sin^{2}\theta
, and

g_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sin^{2}\theta\end{pmatrix},\quad g^{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\sin^{-2}\theta\end{pmatrix}.

(54)

The TSGF can then be obtained using Eqs. (
29

\displaystyle G(\theta,\phi|\theta_{0},\phi_{0};\Delta t)dv(\theta,\phi)

(55)

\displaystyle=

Path-integral from Langevin equation

It is well known that classical Markov processes can be represented by either a Fokker-Planck equation, or a Langevin equation, or a path-integral representation. It is also known that in the presence of multiplicative noises, there are infinite versions of equivalent Langevin equations, each corresponding to a particular scheme of stochastic integration. 5
Covariance

The principle of covariance dictates that all basic equations of a physics theory must be represented in tensor forms, which transform according to tensor algebra under nonlinear transformation of variables (NTV). As such, these equations have the same forms in different coordinate systems, and their validity is independent of choice of coordinate systems. The principle of covariance has served as a cornerstone for the major parts of modern theoretical physics, including general relativity and gauge theories of elementary interactions. The fundamental assumption underlying this principle is that laws of physics are objective, whereas choices of coordinate system are subjective. Change of coordinate system does not change physical laws, but only leads to equivalent representations of the same laws.

In the setting of time-slicing path integral, the issue of covariance turns out to be more subtle than that in general relativity. This is because the objects
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
, which appears ubiquitously in TSGF, are not infinitesimal vectors in conventional sense. It is important to note that even though a large number of works
[
65
,
66
,
57
,
64
,
67
,
68
,
35
,
27
,
52
,
40
]
were published on the topic of path integral in curved space, most of these works do not address explicitly how action transform under general NTV. Edwards and Gulyaev
[
34
]
argued that the usual chain rule of calculus is not applicable in coordinate transformation of path integral, but did not supply detail. Deininghaus and Graham
[
60
]
developed path integral in curved space using normal coordinate systems, which was also discussed and developed by Langouche
et. al.

[
27
]
. More recently, Cugliandolo
et. al.

[
55
,
56
,
57
]
made one dimensional path integral covariant under usual calculus rules by adjusting the interpolation parameter
\alpha
. It remains to be shown whether this approach works for higher dimensions.

We first note that we have formulated both the quantum Hamiltonian Eq. (
9
) and the classical Fokker-Planck operator Eq. (
15
) in terms of tensors and transform as scalars under NTV. For the quantum case, the tensor objects are covariant metric tensor
g_{ij}
and scalar potential
V
. For the classical case, the tensor objects are the contra-variant tensor
L^{ij}
, the covariant metric tensor
g_{ij}
, and the scalar
U(\boldsymbol{x})
. Our path integral representations for TSGT, Eqs. (
29
) for the quantum case and Eqs. (
39
) for the classical case, are also formulated in terms of these tensor objects. When one makes a NTV, these tensor objects transform in the following way:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=p(\boldsymbol{x}),

(77a)

\displaystyle V(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=V(\boldsymbol{x}),

(77b)

\displaystyle U(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle U^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=U(\boldsymbol{x}),

(77c)

\displaystyle g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{ab}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\frac{\partial x^{i}}{\partial x^{\prime a}}g_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})\frac{\partial x^{j}}{\partial x^{\prime b}},

(77d)

\displaystyle b^{i\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle b^{\prime a\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{i}}b^{i\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(77e)

\displaystyle L^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle L^{\prime ab}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{i}}L^{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})\frac{\partial x^{\prime b}}{\partial x^{j}}.

(77f)

We can now use the transformed tensors listed in Eqs. (

\alpha

\displaystyle dv(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,G^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{0};\Delta t)=\frac{\sqrt{g^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{0})}\,d\mu(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}{(2\pi\hbar\Delta t)^{d/2}}e^{-A^{\prime 0}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{0};\Delta t)},

(78a)

But one may also attempt to apply NTV directly to the action

\Delta x^{\prime a}=\left(\partial x^{\prime a}/\partial x^{i}\right)\Delta x^{i}

\alpha

\alpha
-representation of TSGF.

Recall that the

5.2
\alpha=0
Representation of Quantum Path Integral

We can discuss the covariance of quantum time-slicing path integral by making analogy with the classical case. Firstly, we can read off the first moments of
\Delta x^{\prime a}
and
\Delta x^{i}
from Eqs. (
78
) and (
29
):

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta x^{\prime a}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{2m\sqrt{g^{\prime}}}\,\partial^{\prime}_{b}\sqrt{g^{\prime}}\,g^{\prime ab},

(89a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta x^{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{2m\sqrt{g}}\,\partial_{k}\sqrt{g}g^{ik}.

(89b)

Alternatively, we can also treat
\Delta x^{\prime a}
as a function of
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
, expand it in terms of
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
up to the second order:

\displaystyle\Delta x^{\prime a}=\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{i}}\Delta x^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{j}}\Delta x^{i}\Delta x^{j}+\cdots.

(90)

Taking average of this, and using Eqs. (
28
) we find

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta x^{\prime a}\right\rangle\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{i}}\left\langle\Delta x^{i}\right\rangle+\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2}x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{j}}g^{ij}.

(91)

This relation indicates that the average of
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
transforms inhomogeneously under NTV.

Combining Eqs. (
89
) and (
91
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{g^{\prime}}}\,\partial^{\prime}_{b}\sqrt{g^{\prime}}\,g^{\prime ab}=\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{i}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\,\partial_{k}\sqrt{g}g^{ik}+\frac{\partial^{2}x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{j}}g^{ij},

(92)

which can actually be proved independently, just like Eq. (
86
).

Now comes the most interesting observation. If we impose the following transformation law on
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
:

\Delta x^{\prime a}=\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{i}}\Delta x^{i}+\frac{\hbar}{2m}\,g^{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^{i}\partial x^{j}}\Delta t,

(93)

the linear combination

\Delta x^{i}-\frac{\hbar\Delta t}{2m\sqrt{g}}\,\partial_{k}\sqrt{g}g^{ki}

transforms as a contra-variant vector, and consequently the
\alpha=0
time-slice action Eq. (
29b
) transforms as a scalar, i.e., it remains invariant under NTV. We call Eq. (
93
) the
quantum Ito’s formula
because it involves the Planck constant
\hbar
, and is closely related to Ito’s formula (
88
) in the classical case.

6
Conclusion and Outlook

Let us summarize the main results we have achieved in this work: (1) With Lemma 1 we have established a rigorous criterion for equivalence between different representations of time-slicing path integrals. (2) With Lemma 2 we have demonstrated the existence of a Gaussian representation for the time-slice Green’s function. (3) With Lemma 3 we constructed a continuous family of equivalent time-slicing path-integral actions, parameterized by an interpolation parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
. (4) We have established the connection between Langevin dynamics and time-slicing path integral, and have clarified the origin and transformation rules of spurious drift. (5) We have explained why in general path integral actions do not transform as scalars under usual rules of tensor calculus. We have also explicitly demonstrated that the
\alpha=0
representation of path integral action transforms as a scalar if
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}
transform according to Ito’s formula, while all other ingredients transform according to the usual rules of tensor calculus. Our results resolve several major confusions regarding time-slicing path integral in curved space, and provide a rigorous, practical, and manifestly covariant calculation scheme.

In the future, we shall apply the path-integral method developed here to study concrete quantum and classical stochastic dynamics in curved space. We shall also try to develop a similar method for field theory in curved space, as well as for non-abelian gauge theory and nonlinear sigma field theory.
