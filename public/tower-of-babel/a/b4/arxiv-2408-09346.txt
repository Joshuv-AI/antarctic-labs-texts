Rotation index, Milnor–Munkres–Novikov pairing, and group actions on manifolds
Mauricio Bustamante and Bena Tshishiku

Abstract
. We introduce an invariant of a pair of commuting invertible matrices that we call the rotation index. We apply this invariant, together with the Milnor–Munkres–Novikov pairing, to the study of some questions about group actions of ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} , specifically the Nielsen realization problem, higher-rank Anosov actions, and extending actions from the sphere S d − 1 S^{d-1} to the disk D d D^{d} .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Rotation index

We begin by introducing the invariant whose study and application is the focus of this paper. Given a pair
A,B\in\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, we define the
rotation index
as

(1)

\rho(A,B)=\sum_{\lambda,\mu}\dim\big[E_{\lambda}(A)\cap E_{\mu}(B)\big]\pmod{2},

where the sum ranges over pairs
\lambda,\mu
of negative real eigenvalues of
A,B
, respectively, and
E_{\lambda}(A)=\ker[(A-\lambda I)^{m}]
denotes the generalized
\lambda
-eigenspace for
A
.

While
\rho(A,B)
is defined for any pair
A,B
, it is most interesting as an invariant of commuting pairs. The space of commuting pairs is defined as the space
\Hom\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\big)
of group homomorphisms from
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
to
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, topologized as a subspace of
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.

Theorem A
.

For all
d\geq 2
, the function
\rho:\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
is continuous when restricted to the subspace of commuting pairs.

By Theorem
A
,
\rho
is an invariant of path components of
\Hom\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\big)
. It is known by special cases of work of Pettet–Souto
[
PS13
]
and Higuera Rojo
[
HR14
]
that
\Hom\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\big)
has two components when
d\geq 3
. We give an independent proof of this using the rotation index.

Theorem B
.

For all
d\geq 3
, the rotation index defines a bijection

\pi_{0}\big[\Hom\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\big)\big]\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.

The rotation index is related to the second Stiefel–Whitney class
w_{2}(\phi)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
of a representation
\phi:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
,
d\geq 3
, which is the obstruction to lifting
\phi
to the universal cover
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})}\to\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Identifying
H^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2};\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{2}(T^{2};\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
, we may evaluate on a fundamental class
[T^{2}]\in H_{2}(T^{2};\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
to obtain
\langle w_{2}(\phi),[T^{2}]\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.
Since path components of
\Hom\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\big)
correspond to values of the Stiefel–Whitney class, and hence to values of
\rho
, Theorem
B
implies the following corollary.

Corollary 1.1
.

Fix
d\geq 3
. For any representation
\phi:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\langle w_{2}(\phi),[T^{2}]\rangle=\rho(\phi).

Here we write
\rho(\phi)
to mean
\rho(A,B)
, where
(A,B)=(\phi(1,0),\phi(0,1))
is a commuting pair.

In the remainder of the introduction we illustrate how to apply the rotation index
\rho
to study group actions. For each of our applications, we will also use the Milnor–Munkres–Novikov pairing (see
[
BKKT24
, Section 1.3.2]
and references therein)

\pi_{1}^{s}\times\Theta_{d}\to\Theta_{d+1}

where
\Theta_{d}
denotes the Kervaire–Milnor group of homotopy
d
-spheres
[
KM63
]
and
\pi_{1}^{s}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
is the first stable homotopy group of spheres. Below we write
\eta\in\pi_{1}^{s}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
for the generator, and denote the pairing with
\eta
as a map

\begin{array}[]{rccl}\eta:&\Theta_{d}&\to&\Theta_{d+1}\\[5.69054pt]
&\Sigma&\mapsto&\eta\cdot\Sigma\end{array}

An important role will be played by the function

\delta(\Sigma)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }2\mid\eta\cdot\Sigma\\
1&\text{ if }2\nmid\eta\cdot\Sigma.\end{cases}

for
\Sigma\in\Theta_{d}
. The invariant
\delta
takes each of the values
0
and
1
infinitely often (since
\Theta_{d}
is finite for each
d
, necessarily this requires taking
d
to infinity). See
[
BKKT24
, Table 1 and Rmk. 1.10]
.

1.2.
Homotopy Anosov actions on exotic tori

Recall that an exotic
d
-torus is a closed smooth manifold that is homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to the standard torus
T^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For example,
T^{d}\#\Sigma^{d}
and
(T^{d-1}\#\Sigma^{d-1})\times S^{1}
are exotic tori when
\Sigma
is an exotic sphere.

It is known that some exotic tori admit Anosov diffeomorphisms
[
FJ78
,
FG12
]
(i.e. Anosov actions of
\mathbb{Z}
), but there is no known example of an Anosov action of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
on an exotic torus
T^{d}\#\Sigma
. An Anosov action of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
on
T^{d}\#\Sigma
determines (via the induced action on
\pi_{1}
) an Anosov action on the standard torus
T^{d}
, which is called its linearization. Thus in the study of Anosov actions on exotic tori, a basic problem is to determine when an Anosov action on
T^{d}
is the linearization of an Anosov action on
T^{d}\#\Sigma
. For example, by work of Rodriguez-Hertz–Wang, an Anosov action on
T^{d}
that is
without rank-1 factor
(c.f.
[
RHW14
, Defn. 2.8]
) is not the linearization of an Anosov
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
actions on any exotic torus
[
RHW14
, Cor. 1.2]
. Using our Theorem
A
, we give examples with a stronger conclusion, where “Anosov action” is replaced by “smooth action”.

Theorem C
.

For each exotic sphere
\Sigma\in\Theta_{d}
such that
\delta(\Sigma)=1
, there exists an Anosov action
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\curvearrowright T^{d}
without rank-
1
factor that is not the linearization of any smooth
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
action on
T^{d}\#\Sigma
. In fact, there are infinitely many conjugacy classes of such actions.

For example, Theorem
C
applies for
d=8
to the unique exotic torus of the form
T^{8}\#\Sigma
(it is unique because
\Theta_{8}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
).
We prove Theorem
C
in Section
4
.

1.3.
Nielsen realization and Borel’s theme

En route to Theorem
C
we prove the following stronger result.

Theorem D
.

Fix
d\geq 7
, and fix an exotic sphere
\Sigma\in\Theta_{d}
.
Let
G=\langle A,B\rangle<\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
be a subgroup isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. If
\delta(\Sigma)=1
, then the natural homomorphism

\ell:\Diff^{+}(T^{d}\#\Sigma)\to\Out^{+}\big(\pi_{1}(T^{d}\#\Sigma)\big)\cong\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})

splits over
G
if and only if
\rho(A,B)=0
.

Theorem
D
is a result about Nielsen realization, which asks when a group of symmetries of
\pi_{1}(M)
can be realized by a group of symmetries of
M
. For aspherical manifolds, this question fits within “Borel’s theme” (c.f. Weinberger
[
Wei23
]
) that asks to what extent geometric or topological properties of an aspherical manifold
M
can be determined by the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(M)
. Indeed, in the setting of Theorem
D
one can determine if
\mathbb{Z}^{2}<\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
is realized by a group of diffeomorphisms of
T^{d}\#\Sigma
just using the rotation index.

Remark 1.2
.

In Theorem
D
, the assumption
\delta(\Sigma)=1
implies that the surjection
\ell:\Diff^{+}(T^{d}\#\Sigma)\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
does not split by work of Krannich–Kupers and the authors
[
BKKT24
, Thm. A]
.
Whether
\ell
splits over a given subgroup
\mathbb{Z}^{2}<\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
is a more subtle question, which is answered by Theorem
D
.

1.4.
Bordism of group actions

We give one more (somewhat different) application of the rotation index and Milnor–Munkres–Novikov pairing.

Theorem E
.

Fix
d
such that there exists
\Sigma\in\Theta_{d}
with
\delta(\Sigma)=1
. Then there exists a smooth action of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
on the sphere
S^{d-1}
by diffeomorphisms isotopic to the identity such that the action does not extend to an action of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
on
D^{d}
.

Theorem
E
fits within the study of bordism of group actions; see
[
MN20
]
and references therein. The assumption in Theorem
E
that the diffeomorphisms are isotopic to the identity means that each individual diffeomorphism extends from
S^{d-1}
to
D^{d}
, and yet for the examples in Theorem
E
, there is no way to extend the group action.

The construction of the examples
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\curvearrowright S^{d-1}
in Theorem
E
is explicit. It would be interesting to determine whether these actions can extend to any
d
-manifold with boundary
S^{d-1}
.

Proof techniques

Our proofs of Theorems
A
and
B
rely on perturbation theory of linear operators. In Theorem
D
, the obstruction to lifting is detected on the mapping class group; here we use computations from
[
BKKT24
]
and an Euler class argument; computing this Euler class relates to the rotation index. Theorem
C
is deduced from Theorem
D
, together with a simple number-theoretic construction to produce commuting hyperbolic matrices in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
with certain properties. Finally, Theorem
E
is proved by identifying the Milnor-Munkres-Novikov pairing with the obstruction to split a certain central group extension.

2.
The rotation index

In this section we prove some basic properties about the rotation index, including Theorem
A
. We also use this point-of-view to give a new proof that
\Hom(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R}))
has two components for
d\geq 3
. One of the tools we use to prove Theorem
A
is perturbation theory for linear operators, which we recall in §
2.1
along with some other auxiliary results.

2.1.
Perturbation theory and eigenspaces

Generalized eigenspaces do not generally vary continuously on
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
(e.g. because the dimensions may change). Perturbation theory for linear operators gives some information about how the eigenspaces vary and gives a weaker form of continuity, which is helpful for our purposes. We recall this below. Our reference is
[
Kat95
, Ch. 2, §1]
.

Fix
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
\epsilon>0
be a small real number, and denote by
T^{1}_{I}\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
the subspace of unit vectors in the tangent space of
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
at the identity
I
, which we identify with the space of traceless real
d\times d
matrices. We consider perturbations of
A
of the form

A(t,V)=A\cdot\exp(tV),

where
t\in[0,\epsilon)
,
V\in T^{1}_{I}\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, and
\exp(-)
denotes the matrix exponential. Observe that
A(t,V)
is a continuous function, satisfies
A(0,V)=A
, and lies in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
for all
(t,V)\in[0,\epsilon)\times T^{1}_{I}\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.

Recall that the
resolvent

R(\zeta,t,V)=(A(t,V)-\zeta)^{-1}

of
A(t,V)
is defined for all complex numbers
\zeta
which are not eigenvalues of
A(t,V)
.
The
resolvent set
of
A
is the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\spec(A)
, where
\spec(A)
is the set of eigenvalues of
A
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and

\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{C}\times[0,\varepsilon)\times T^{1}_{I}\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})

be an open set with the property that for every
(\zeta,t,V)\in\mathcal{U}
the number
\zeta
is not an eigenvalue of
A(t,V)
. Then the resolvent

R(\zeta,t,V)=(A(t,V)-\zeta)^{-1}

is continuous on
\mathcal{U}
. Moreover
R(\zeta,t,V)
tends to
(A-\zeta)^{-1}
as
t\to 0
uniformly for
\zeta
ranging over compact subsets of the resolvent set of
A
.

Proof.

Continuity on
\mathcal{U}
follows from the fact that matrix inversion in
\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is continuous.

For the second claim, fix a compact subset
K
of the resolvent set of
A
. For
t
sufficiently small,
R(\zeta,t,V)
is defined for all
\zeta\in K
and
V\in T^{1}_{I}\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Uniform continuity of
R(\zeta,t,V)
then follows from compactness.
∎

Now let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
be an eigenvalue of
A
of algebraic multiplicity
m
. Let
\Gamma
be a simple closed curve in
\mathbb{C}
enclosing
\lambda
but no other eigenvalues of
A
. Set

\delta=\min\{||(A-\zeta)^{-1}||^{-1}\ |\ \zeta\in\Gamma\}.

Note that
\delta>0
and
R(\zeta,t,V)
exists for all
\zeta\in\Gamma
if
||A(t,V)-A||<\delta
. Thus the following operator on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
can be defined:

P(t,V):=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}R(\zeta,t,V)\,d\zeta.

Lemma
2.1
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

For
(t,V)
as above, the operator
P(t,V)
is continuous.

The operator
P(t,V)
is the projection to the eigenspaces of
A(t,V)
with eigenvalues lying inside the curve
\Gamma
. In particular, the image of
P(t,V)
is the sum
\bigoplus E_{\lambda^{\prime}}(A(t,V))
of the generalized eigenspaces
E_{\lambda^{\prime}}(A(t,V))
for the eigenvalues
\lambda^{\prime}
of
A(t,V)
lying in
\Gamma

[
Kat95
, I. Problem 5.9]
.

We write
\widehat{\lambda}=\widehat{\lambda}(t,V)
for the eigenvalues of
A(t,V)
lying inside of
\Gamma
, and we refer to these eigenvalues as the
\lambda
-group. The sum of generalized eigenspaces ranging over all
\lambda^{\prime}
in the
\lambda
-group will be abbreviated as

E_{\widehat{\lambda}}(A(t,V)):=\bigoplus E_{\lambda^{\prime}}(A(t,V)).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(A,B)\in\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
be a commuting pair. There exists an open neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
of
(A,B)
such that for every pair
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{U}
with
A^{\prime}B^{\prime}=B^{\prime}A^{\prime}
and for every
\lambda\in\spec(A)
and
\mu\in\spec(B)
we have

\dim\big(E_{\lambda}(A)\cap E_{\mu}(B)\big)=\dim\big(E_{\widehat{\lambda}}(A^{\prime})\cap E_{\widehat{\mu}}(B^{\prime})\big),

where
\widehat{\lambda}
(resp.
\widehat{\mu}
) denotes the
\lambda
-group for
A^{\prime}
(resp. the
\mu
-group for
B^{\prime}
) determined by a fixed isolating contour around
\lambda
(resp.
\mu
).

Proof.

Fix
\lambda\in\spec(A)
and
\mu\in\spec(B)
. Choose simple closed curves
\Gamma_{\lambda}
and
\Gamma_{\mu}
in
\mathbb{C}
such that
\Gamma_{\lambda}
encloses
\lambda
and no other eigenvalue of
A
, and
\Gamma_{\mu}
encloses
\mu
and no other eigenvalue of
B
. Define the spectral projections

P:=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}(A-\zeta)^{-1}\,d\zeta,\qquad Q:=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{\mu}}(B-\eta)^{-1}\,d\eta.

As mentioned above,
P
is the projection onto the generalized eigenspace
E_{\lambda}(A)
and
Q
is the projection onto
E_{\mu}(B)
. In particular
P
and
Q
are idempotent linear operators of finite rank.

Since
A
and
B
commute, so do
P
and
Q
. Therefore
PQ
is again a projection and its image satisfies

\operatorname{im}(PQ)=\operatorname{im}P\cap\operatorname{im}Q=E_{\lambda}(A)\cap E_{\mu}(B).

Thus

\dim\big(E_{\lambda}(A)\cap E_{\mu}(B)\big)=\operatorname{rank}(PQ).

Set

\delta_{A}:=\min_{\zeta\in\Gamma_{\lambda}}||(A-\zeta)^{-1}||^{-1}>0,\qquad\delta_{B}:=\min_{\eta\in\Gamma_{\mu}}||(B-\eta)^{-1}||^{-1}>0.

By Corollary
2.2
, whenever
||A^{\prime}-A||<\delta_{A}
for
A^{\prime}
of the form
A^{\prime}=A\cdot\exp(tV)
, the resolvent
(A^{\prime}-\zeta)^{-1}
exists for every
\zeta\in\Gamma_{\lambda}
and depends continuously on
A^{\prime}
, and similarly whenever
||B^{\prime}-B||<\delta_{B}
the resolvent
(B^{\prime}-\eta)^{-1}
exists for every
\eta\in\Gamma_{\mu}
and depends continuously on
B^{\prime}=B\cdot\exp(tW)
. Hence any subset
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
in the image of the product map

[0,\delta_{A})\times T^{1}_{I}\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times[0,\delta_{B})\times T^{1}_{I}\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\xrightarrow{A\cdot\exp\times B\cdot\exp}\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})

is an open neighborhood of
(A,B)
on which the contour integrals defining spectral projections with respect to
\Gamma_{\lambda}
and
\Gamma_{\mu}
are well-defined.

For
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}
define

P^{\prime}:=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{\lambda}}(A^{\prime}-\zeta)^{-1}\,d\zeta,\qquad Q^{\prime}:=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{\mu}}(B^{\prime}-\eta)^{-1}\,d\eta.

These are continuous functions of
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{V}
. For such
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
the images satisfy

\operatorname{im}(P^{\prime})=E_{\widehat{\lambda}}(A^{\prime})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{im}(Q^{\prime})=E_{\widehat{\mu}}(B^{\prime}),

where
\widehat{\lambda},\widehat{\mu}
denote the
\lambda
- and
\mu
- groups of eigenvalues of
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
inside the chosen contours.

If, in addition

(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{V}\cap\Hom(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R}))

(i.e. the pair is commuting), then
P^{\prime}Q^{\prime}=Q^{\prime}P^{\prime}
, and the same argument as before yields

\dim\big(E_{\widehat{\lambda}}(A^{\prime})\cap E_{\widehat{\mu}}(B^{\prime})\big)=\operatorname{rank}(P^{\prime}Q^{\prime}).

In finite dimensions the rank of a projection equals its trace, and the trace is a continuous function of matrix entries; hence the map
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(P^{\prime}Q^{\prime})

is continuous and takes values in
\mathbb{Z}
, so it is constant on a possibly smaller neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
.

Therefore, for every commuting pair
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{U}
we have

\dim\big(E_{\lambda}(A)\cap E_{\mu}(B)\big)=\operatorname{rank}(PQ)=\operatorname{rank}(P^{\prime}Q^{\prime})=\dim\big(E_{\widehat{\lambda}}(A^{\prime})\cap E_{\widehat{\mu}}(B^{\prime})\big).

This proves the lemma.
∎

The following fact about commuting matrices will be useful in proving Theorem
A
.
Write
\psi:\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathrm{GL}_{2n}(\mathbb{R})
for the homomorphism induced by the ring homomorphism
\mathbb{C}\to\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
given by
x+iy\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x&y\\
-y&x\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. If
B\in\mathrm{SL}_{2n}(\mathbb{R})
commutes with
\psi(\lambda I_{n})
, then
B
is also in the image of
\psi
.

Proof.

Writing
\lambda=x+iy
, we can write
\psi(\lambda I_{n})=xI_{2n}+yJ_{2n}
, where
J_{2n}
is the
(2n)\times(2n)
block diagonal matrix with blocks
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Note that
y\neq 0
by our assumption that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. Using this expression for
\psi(\lambda I_{n})
, we observe that if
B
commutes with
\psi(\lambda I_{n})
, then
B
also commutes with
J_{2n}
. Since
J_{2n}
defines the given complex structure on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, the image of
\psi
is precisely the centralizer of
J_{2n}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
A

We want to show that the rotation index is continuous on commuting pairs. Recall that
\rho(A,B)=\sum\dim\big[E_{\lambda}(A)\cap E_{\mu}(B)\big]\pmod{2}
,
where the sum ranges over
\lambda\in\spec(A)
and
\mu\in\spec(B)
with
\lambda,\mu<0
. Fix such a pair
\lambda,\mu
.

For
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
sufficiently close to
(A,B)
, we know from Lemma
2.3
that

\dim E_{\widehat{\lambda}}(A^{\prime})\cap E_{\widehat{\mu}}(B^{\prime})=\dim E_{\lambda}(A)\cap E_{\mu}(B).

Ultimately, we want to compare
\dim E_{\lambda}(A)\cap E_{\mu}(B)
to the dimension of the subspace of
E_{\widehat{\lambda}}(A^{\prime})\cap E_{\widehat{\mu}}(B^{\prime})
where
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}
both act with negative real eigenvalues (in the sense of generalized eigenvalues).

The action of
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
on
E_{\widehat{\lambda}}(A^{\prime})\cap E_{\widehat{\mu}}(B^{\prime})
is simultaneously upper triangular over
\mathbb{C}
(because
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}
commute). Write
d_{\lambda\mu}
for the dimension of
E_{\widehat{\lambda}}(A^{\prime})\cap E_{\widehat{\mu}}(B^{\prime})
. Fixing a basis so that
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}
are upper triangular, we write
\{1,\ldots,d_{\lambda\mu}\}=N_{A}\sqcup C_{A}
for the partition corresponding to which of the diagonal entries of
A^{\prime}
are negative or non-real complex numbers (positive eigenvalues do not occur because the eigenvalues of
A
on this subspace are in a neighborhood of
\lambda<0
). Similarly, decompose
\{1,\ldots,d_{\lambda\mu}\}=N_{B}\sqcup C_{B}
.

With this notation, we want to show

d_{\lambda\mu}\equiv|N_{A}\cap N_{B}|\pmod{2}.

Since
d_{\lambda\mu}=|N_{A}\cap N_{B}|+|N_{A}\cap C_{B}|+|C_{A}\cap N_{B}|+|C_{A}\cap C_{B}|
, it suffices to show that the sum of the last three terms is even. In fact each of these terms is even. Clearly
|N_{A}\cap C_{B}|+|C_{A}\cap C_{B}|=|C_{B}|
are even since nonreal eigenvalues occur in complex conjugate pairs. Furthermore, both of
|N_{A}\cap C_{B}|
and
|C_{A}\cap N_{B}|
are even by Lemma
2.4
. This completes the proof.
∎

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
B
. It is worth mentioning that the fact that

\pi_{0}\big[\Hom\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\big)\big]\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}

for
d\geq 3
follows from work of Pettet–Souto and Higuera-Rojo
[
PS13
,
HR14
]
(Pettet–Souto show
\Hom\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\big)
deformation retracts
\Hom\big(\mathbb{Z}^{2},\SO(d)\big)
, and Higuera-Rojo computes components of the latter space). For our applications it will be important that the path components correspond to the values of the rotation index.

Proof of Theorem
B

The map is well-defined by Theorem
A
. To see that it’s surjective, observe that
\rho(I,I)=0
and
\rho(D_{1},D_{2})=1
, where
D_{1},D_{2}
are the diagonal matrices
D_{1}=(-1,-1,1,\ldots,1)
and
D_{2}=(-1,1,-1,1,\ldots,1)
. It remains then to show injectivity, and for this we will show that any commuting pair
(A,B)
can be connected by a path of commuting pairs in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
to either
(I,I)
or
(D_{1},D_{2})
. We do this in several steps.

For notational convenience we now write
(A_{1},A_{2})
instead of
(A,B)
for the commuting pair.

Path
\#1
(to semisimple matrices).

Any
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
can be written in the form
A=A^{s}A^{u}
where
A^{s}
is a semisimple matrix and
A^{u}
is a unipotent matrix (multiplicative Jordan decomposition). Similar to
[
PS13
, Prop. 8.4 and its proof]
, for
t\in[0,1]
and
i=1,2
, we construct paths

A_{i}(t)=A_{i}^{s}\,e^{(1-t)\log A_{i}^{u}}

where, for any square matrix
X
and unipotent matrix
Y
, the exponential and logarithm are matrices defined by

e^{X}=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}X^{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\log Y=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k!}(Y-I)^{k}.

For every
t\in[0,1]
the matrices
A_{1}(t)
and
A_{2}(t)
commute. Indeed,
A_{i}^{s}
and
A_{i}^{u}
are polynomials in
A_{i}

[
Bor91
, Ch. 1, Prop 4.2]
, so since
A_{1},A_{2}
commute, each of pair of
A_{1},A_{2},A_{1}^{s},A_{1}^{u},A_{2}^{s},A_{2}^{u}
commute, and similarly,
A_{i}^{s}
and
e^{(1-t)\log A_{j}^{u}}
commute for each pair of
i,j
because the latter can be expressed as a power series in
A_{j}^{u}
.

At time
t=1
of the paths
A_{1}(t),A_{2}(t)
, the matrices obtained are the semisimple parts
A_{1}^{s},A_{2}^{s}
of
A_{1},A_{2}
, respectively.

Path
\#2
(to block diagonal matrices).

We construct commuting paths from
A_{1}^{s},A_{2}^{s}
to block diagonal matrices.

The matrix
A_{1}^{s}
is semisimple, so by the rational canonical form, there exists
P\in\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
such that

(2)

PA_{1}^{s}P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}I_{m_{1}}&&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&\lambda_{r}I_{m_{r}}&\\
&&&M_{1}\otimes I_{n_{1}}\\
&&&&\ddots\\
&&&&&M_{s}\otimes I_{n_{s}}\end{pmatrix}

where
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
if

and
M_{i}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is the “realification” of a nonreal matrix in
\mathrm{GL}_{1}(\mathbb{C})
, i.e. each
M_{i}
has the form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{i}&b_{i}\\
-b_{i}&a_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
where
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
b_{i}\neq 0
.

Since
A_{2}^{s}
commutes with
A_{1}^{s}
, it preserves the eigenspaces corresponding to each real eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
and also preserves the

-dimensional subspace on which

acts by the matrix

(the latter subspace is the kernel of an irreducible quadratic factor of the characteristic polynomial of

). Consequently, we can write

(3)

PA_{2}^{s}P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{1}&&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&F_{r}&\\
&&&H_{1}\\
&&&&\ddots\\
&&&&&H_{s}\\
\end{pmatrix}

where

and

.

Fix a path
P(t)
in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
,
t\in[0,1]
, between the identity and
P
, and consider the path

P(t)\,A_{i}^{s}\,P(t)^{-1}.

For notational convenience, we write
B_{i}=PA_{i}^{s}P^{-1}
, which is the time

endpoint of our second path.

Path
\#3
(to diagonal matrices).

We construct commuting paths of block diagonal matrices from
PA_{1}^{s}P^{-1},PA_{2}^{s}P^{-1}
to matrices that are the identity on the
2n_{i}\times 2n_{i}
blocks of both
PA_{1}^{s}P^{-1}
and
PA_{2}^{s}P^{-1}

2

3

By Lemma
2.4
, the matrices

Let

Consider a path

, commute and take

and

to the identity in

and

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}I_{m_{1}}&&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&\lambda_{r}I_{m_{r}}&\\

\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{pmatrix}F_{1}&&&\\

&&F_{r}&\\
&&&I_{2n}\\
\end{pmatrix}

Path
\#4
(to diagonal matrices in

The sign of each

Path
\#5
(to diagonal matrices in

Next we construct paths

We end this section with the following lemma, which is helpful in deducing Theorem
D
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For a commuting pair
A,B\in\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, the rotation index
\rho(A,B)
depends only on the subgroup generated by
A,B
.

Proof.

We want to show that if
\langle A^{\prime},B^{\prime}\rangle=\langle A,B\rangle
, then
\rho(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=\rho(A,B)
.

First assume that the group
\langle A,B\rangle
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Any two bases for
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
differ by an element of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. The group
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is generated by
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, so it suffices to show

\rho(A,AB)=\rho(A,B)\qquad\rho(AB,B)=\rho(A,B)\qquad\rho(B,A)=\rho(A,B).

The last equality holds because
\rho
is symmetric by definition. The proof of the first two are similar to each other. For definiteness, we show
\rho(A,AB)=\rho(A,B)
.

To show
\rho(A,AB)=\rho(A,B)
, first recall that since
A,B
commute, they are simultaneously upper triangular over
\mathbb{C}
. Assuming
A,B
are upper-triangular, write
\{1,\ldots,d\}=P_{A}\sqcup N_{A}\sqcup C_{A}
for the partition corresponding to which of the diagonal entries of
A
are positive, negative, or non-real complex numbers. We do the same for
B
and for
AB
.

We want to show
|N_{A}\cap N_{B}|\equiv|N_{A}\cap N_{AB}|\pmod{2}
. Observe that

N_{A}\cap N_{AB}=N_{A}\cap\big[(P_{A}\cap N_{B})\cup(N_{A}\cap P_{B})\big]=N_{A}\cap P_{B},

so we want to show that
|N_{A}\cap N_{B}|+|N_{A}\cap P_{B}|\equiv 0\pmod{2}
. Next observe that

|N_{A}\cap N_{B}|+|N_{A}\cap P_{B}|+|N_{A}\cap C_{B}|=|N_{A}|\equiv 0\pmod{2}.

The first equality holds because
P_{B}\cup N_{B}\cup C_{B}=\{1,\ldots,d\}
. The second equality holds because
\det(A)=1
, so the number of its real negative eigenvalues is even. It remains to explain why
|N_{A}\cap C_{B}|
is even. This is because
A
and
B
are real and commute, so the action of
A
on the generalized
(\lambda,\bar{\lambda})
-eigenspace for a complex conjugate pair of eigenvalues of
B
is by the realification of a complex matrix, so real eigenvalues appear with even multiplicity.

The other possibilities for
\langle A,B\rangle
are
\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}
. These cases are easier because the automorphism groups are similarly generated but smaller, so the proof in the
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
case applies the same.
∎

3.
Nielsen realization (Proof of Theorem
D
)

In this section we fix
\Sigma\in\Theta_{d}
with
\delta(\Sigma)=1
and we study the lifting problem

\ell:\Diff^{+}(T^{d}\#\Sigma)\to\Out^{+}\big(\pi_{1}(T^{d}\#\Sigma)\big)\cong\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})

for subgroups of
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
of the form
G=\langle A,B\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. To prove Theorem
D
, we consider the cases
\rho(A,B)=1
and
\rho(A,B)=0
separately.

3.1.
Obstruction to lifting when
\rho\neq 0

For notational convenience we write
(A_{1},A_{2})
instead of
(A,B)
.
Assuming
\rho(A_{1},A_{2})=1
, we will show there does not exist a commuting pair
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\Diff(M)
that induce
A_{1},A_{2}
on
\pi_{1}(M)
.

Lie group reduction

Since
\Diff(T^{d}\#\Sigma)\to\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
factors through the mapping class group
\Mod(T^{d}\#\Sigma)\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
, it suffices to show that there is no commuting pair
[\phi_{1}],[\phi_{2}]
in
\Mod(T^{d}\#\Sigma)
that lifts
A_{1},A_{2}
. This problem reduces to the problem of lifting
A_{1},A_{2}
to a commuting pair in the universal cover

(5)

1\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\to\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})}\to\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to 1

as we now explain.
Let

1\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\to\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})\to\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})\to 1

be the short exact sequence obtained by pullback of (
5
) along the inclusion
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. By
[
BKKT24
, Thm. D]
, when
\delta(\Sigma)=1
, there is an isomorphism
\Mod(T^{d}\#\Sigma)\cong K\rtimes\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
(where
K
is a group whose precise form is not important here), and there is a commutative diagram

\textstyle{K\rtimes\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})\cong\Mod(T^{d}\#\Sigma)}
\textstyle{\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})}
\textstyle{\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})}

This implies that if the commuting pair
A_{1},A_{2}
has a commuting pair of lifts to
\Mod(M)
, then there is also a commuting pair of lifts to
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})<\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})}
.

Thus to prove Theorem
D
, it suffices to show that for every pair of lifts
\widetilde{A}_{1},\widetilde{A}_{2}\in\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})}
, the commutator
[\widetilde{A}_{1},\widetilde{A}_{2}]
is nontrivial. Since the extension (
5
) is central, it suffices to show this for a single pair of lifts.

To proceed, we use the definition of the universal cover as a set of paths, so choosing lifts amounts to choosing paths from
A_{i}
to the identity in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. With this viewpoint, the commutator
[\widetilde{A}_{1},\widetilde{A}_{2}]
defines loop in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
based at the identity, whose homotopy class is an element of the kernel group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\cong\pi_{1}\big(\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\big)
, and we want to show this homotopy class is nontrivial.

By Theorem
B
and its proof, if
\rho(A_{1},A_{2})=1
, then
(A_{1},A_{2})
is connected by a path of commuting pairs in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
to
(D_{1},D_{2})
as defined in
4
. Therefore, it suffices to compute the obstruction for
(D_{1},D_{2})
.

Computing the obstruction

Consider the paths
\eta_{i}(t)
,
0\leq t\leq 1
, in
\SO(3)
defined by

\eta_{1}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\pi t)&-\sin(\pi t)&0\\
\sin(\pi t)&\cos(\pi t)&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\text{\ \ \ and\ \ \ }\eta_{2}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos(\pi t)&-\sin(\pi t)\\
0&\sin(\pi t)&\cos(\pi t)\\
\end{pmatrix}.

These define paths from
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, respectively, to the identity. Note that these are
not
paths of commuting matrices.

Now we compute the homotopy class of the commutator
[\eta_{1},\eta_{2}]
. Recall that the multiplication in
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})}
of two paths
\lambda(t),\mu(t)
in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
based at the identity is the pointwise product path
t\mapsto\lambda(t)\cdot\mu(t)
(this holds in any Lie group).
It is helpful to recall that the pointwise product of paths
\lambda,\mu
is homotopic to the concatenation
\lambda*(\lambda(1)\cdot\eta)
of
\lambda
with the path
t\mapsto\lambda(1)\cdot\eta(t)
(again this holds in any Lie group). Consequently, the path
t\mapsto\eta_{1}(t)\cdot\eta_{2}(t)\cdot\eta_{1}(t)^{-1}\cdot\eta_{2}(t)^{-1}
is homotopic to the concatenation of paths

\eta_{1}*(D_{1}\cdot\eta_{2})*(D_{1}D_{2}\cdot\eta_{1}^{-1})*(D_{1}D_{2}D_{1}^{-1}\cdot\eta_{2}^{-1}).

Note that
D_{1}D_{2}D_{1}^{-1}=D_{2}
. One can compute directly that this loop represents a generator of
\pi_{1}\big(\SO(3)\big)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. A picture of this path is given in Figure

.

3.2.
Constructing lifts when
\rho=0

Assuming
\rho(A_{1},A_{2})=0
, we construct a splitting of
\ell:\Diff^{+}(T^{d}\#\Sigma)\to\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
over
G=\langle A_{1},A_{2}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, i.e. we construct an action of
G
on
T^{d}\#\Sigma
that induces the desired splitting.

The standard action of
G<\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
on
T^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
fixes the origin

. By blowing up, we obtain a smooth action of
G
on a manifold
T^{\prime}
diffeomorphic to
T^{d}\setminus\text{int}(D^{d})
such that the action on the boundary
\partial T^{\prime}\cong S^{d-1}
agrees with the action of
G
on the projectivized tangent space
\mathbb{P}^{+}(T_{o}T^{d})\cong S^{d-1}
. In particular, up to this identification, the action of
G
on
\partial T^{\prime}
factors through the standard action of
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
on
S^{d-1}
(viewed as rays through the origin).

Now we use the paths constructed in the proof of Theorem
B
. In the case
\rho(A_{1},A_{2})=0
, the concatenation of paths
\#1
through
\#5
is a path of commuting matrices starting at
(A_{1},A_{2})
and ending at the identity matrix. Viewing the path as an isotopy of commuting diffeomorphisms of
S^{d-1}
, we attach a collar to
\partial T^{\prime}
on which we perform this isotopy (we can arrange that the path is smooth and locally constant near the boundary, so that the resulting action is smooth). Now we have an action of
G
on a manifold
T^{\prime\prime}
that is diffeomorphic to
T^{\prime}
, such that
G
acts trivially near
\partial T^{\prime\prime}
. We then glue
T^{\prime\prime}
and
D^{d}
along their boundary using a diffeomorphism representing
\Sigma\in\Theta_{d}\cong\pi_{0}\Diff^{+}(S^{d-1})
. Extending the action on
T^{\prime\prime}
to

by the identity on

, defines the desired action of
G
on

.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
D
.∎

4.
Homotopy Anosov actions (Proof of Theorem
C
)

To prove Theorem
C
, fix
d
such that there exists
\Sigma\in\Theta_{d}
satisfying
\delta(\Sigma)=1
.

First we construct (infinitely many conjugacy classes of pairs of) commuting hyperbolic matrices
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
\rho(A_{1},A_{2})=1
.
(Recall that a matrix in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
is called hyperbolic if it has no eigenvalues on the unit circle.)
Write
d=n+3
and define
A_{i}
to be a block diagonal matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}B_{i}&\\
&C_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z})
and
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
are commuting pairs of hyperbolic matrices as defined in the following paragraphs.

Choose
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z})
that are conjugate in
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
to diagonal matrices of the form

(6)

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}&0\\
0&0&\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\text{\ \ \ and\ \ \ }\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}}&0&0\\
0&\mu_{1}&0\\
0&0&\mu_{2}\end{pmatrix}

respectively, where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
are all negative and different from
-1
. As an explicit example, consider the polynomial
\xi=x^{3}+x^{2}-2x-1
. The totally real cubic field
K=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(\xi)
has discriminant 49 (the smallest possible among totally real cubic fields). Fixing a root
\alpha
of
\xi
in
K
, the group of units
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
, modulo its torsion subgroup (which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, generated by
-1
), is freely generated by
\epsilon_{1}:=\alpha^{2}+\alpha-1
and
\epsilon_{2}:=-\alpha^{2}+2
. The action of the units
-\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}
on the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
with the basis
\mathcal{O}_{K}\cong\mathbb{Z}\{1,\alpha,\alpha^{2}\}
gives matrices as in (
6
). For the claims about this number field, compare with
[
Coh93
, §B.4]
.

To choose
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, we recall that for each
n\geq 3
, there exists a subgroup
\mathbb{Z}^{2}<\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
generated by hyperbolic matrices
C_{1},C_{2}
such that all eigenvalues of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are real and positive. Indeed, let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a degree
n
totally real number field. Choose linearly independent units
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
, and let
C_{i}
be the matrix for multiplication by
\alpha_{i}
on
\mathcal{O}_{K}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(with respect to any basis). Since the Galois conjugates of the
\alpha_{i}
are real and not equal to
\pm 1
, they do not lie on the unit circle, so the matrices
C_{i}
are hyperbolic. Furthermore, after replacing
\alpha_{i}
by
\alpha_{i}^{2}
, we can ensure that the eigenvalues of
C_{i}
are positive.

Now define
G=\langle A_{1},A_{2}\rangle
and consider the Anosov action of
G
on
T^{d}
. This action is not the linearization of any smooth action on
T^{d}\#\Sigma
because if it were, then
\Diff^{+}(T^{d}\#\Sigma)\to\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
would split over
G
, contradicting the Theorem
D
. Here we use the fact that
\delta(\Sigma)=1
by assumption and
\rho(A_{1},A_{2})=1
by construction. This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Bordism of group actions (Theorem
E
)

Fix a commuting pair
A,B
in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and fix
f\in\Diff^{+}(S^{d-1})
. We view
A,B
as diffeomorphisms of
S^{d-1}
, via the action on rays in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; since
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
is path-connected, these diffeomorphisms are isotopic to the identity. Consider the subgroup

G=G(f,A,B)
of
\Diff_{0}(S^{d-1})
generated by
fAf^{-1}
and
fBf^{-1}
. Here
\Diff_{0}
denotes diffeomorphisms isotopic to the identity.

The following statement is a more precise form of Theorem
E
. We identify
\pi_{0}\Diff^{+}(S^{d-1})
with
\Theta_{d}
(via the clutching construction). In particular, for each isotopy class
[f]\in\pi_{0}\Diff^{+}(S^{d-1})
, there is an associated element
\eta\cdot[f]\in\Theta_{d+1}
given by the Milnor–Munkres–Novikov pairing.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
A,B\in\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
a pair of commuting matrices,
f\in\Diff^{+}(S^{d-1})
, and
G=G(A,B,f)<\Diff_{0}(S^{d-1})
as above. If
\rho(A,B)=1
and
\eta\cdot[f]\in\Theta_{d+1}
is nonzero, then there is no action of
G
on
D^{d}
that extends the given action of
G
on
S^{d-1}
.

In terms of examples, we could take
(A,B)=(D_{1},D_{2})
from (
4
) to get examples with
G\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. We can also easily find
A,B
so that
G\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
; for example

A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-1&\\
&-1\\
&&1\\
&&&1&1\\
&&&&1\\
&&&&&1\\
&&&&&&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\>\>\text{ and }B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-1&\\
&1\\
&&-1\\
&&&1\\
&&&&1\\
&&&&&1&1\\
&&&&&&1\end{smallmatrix}\right).

We also recall that the function
\eta:\Theta_{d}\to\Theta_{d+1}
is nontrivial for infinitely many values of
d
; see
[
BKKT24
]
.

Before proving Theorem
5.1
, we give two explicit descriptions of the Milnor–Munkres–Novikov pairing
\eta\cdot[f]
for
[f]\in\pi_{0}\Diff^{+}(S^{d-1})
that will be used in the proof. For reference, see
[
Mun70
, §2]
and
[
Lev70
, §7]
.

MMN pairing: first version

Let
d\geq 3
. Write
r_{t}
for a loop in
\SO(d)
based at the identity that generates
\pi_{1}(\SO(d))\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. Given a diffeomorphism
f\in\Diff_{\partial}(D^{d-1})
which is the identity near the boundary, define a diffeomorphism
g
of
D^{d}=[0,1]\times D^{d-1}
rel boundary by

g_{f}(t,x)=\big(t,[r_{t},f](x)\big).

Note that we can write
g_{f}
as the commutator of diffeomorphisms
R,F
where
R(t,x)=(t,r_{t}(x))
and
F(t,x)=(t,f(x))
.

MMN pairing: second version

As a variation of the previous construction, take
r_{t}
as above, fix
f\in\Diff^{+}(S^{d-1})
, and consider the diffeomorphism of
[0,1]\times S^{d-1}
given by
FR^{-1}F^{-1}
, i.e. the map
(t,x)\mapsto\big(t,(f\circ r_{t}^{-1}\circ f^{-1})(x)\big)
. This diffeomorphism is the identity on the boundary, so fixing a (standard) embedding of
[0,1]\times S^{d-1}
in
D^{d}\subset S^{d}
, we can extend by the identity to get a diffeomorphism that represents the same isotopy class as
g_{f}
. To see that the isotopy class is the same, it helps to observe that the diffeomorphism
R
of
[0,1]\times S^{d-1}
extends to
[0,1]\times D^{d}
, and using this, we can conclude that once we extend
R
by the identity to
S^{d}
, we get a diffeomorphism that extends to
D^{d+1}
and hence is (pseudo-)isotopically trivial. Then
FR^{-1}F^{-1}
and
RFR^{-1}F^{-1}
are isotopic.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Extending the given action of
G
on
S^{d-1}
to a smooth action on
D^{d}
amounts to solving the following lifting problem.

\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Diff_{\partial}(D^{d})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Diff_{0}(D^{d})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Diff_{0}(S^{d-1})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{1}
\textstyle{G\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Pulling back this sequence along
G\hookrightarrow\Diff_{0}(S^{d-1})
and taking path components, we obtain an extension

(7)

1\to\Theta_{d+1}\to E\to G\to 1,

where we use the isomorphisms
\Theta_{d+1}\cong\pi_{0}\Diff^{+}(S^{d}\big)\cong\pi_{0}\Diff_{\partial}(D^{d})
.

Observe that the obstruction to splitting the extension (
7
) gives an obstruction to the original lifting problem. The extension (
7
) is central because
\Theta_{d+1}
is abelian and because any
g\in G<\Diff^{+}(S^{d-1})
has a lift to
\Diff_{0}(D^{d})
that is supported near
\partial D^{d}
, while every element of
\Diff_{\partial}(D^{d})
can be isotoped to have support away from
\partial D^{d}
.
Because the extension (
7
) is central, the obstruction to splitting is the commutator of the lifts of the generators of
G

[
Bro94
, §IV.3. Exercise 8]
. The diffeomorphisms
fAf^{-1}
and
fBf^{-1}
of
S^{d-1}
can be extended radially using isotopies
A_{t}
and
B_{t}
from
A
and
B
to the identity in
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Using these isotopies, we extend
fAf^{-1}
and
fBf^{-1}
radially on a collar neighborhood of
\partial D^{d}
. This defines elements
a,b\in\Diff_{0}(\partial D^{d})
and we write
\alpha,\beta
for the corresponding elements of
E
; these are lifts of
fAf^{-1},fBf^{-1}\in G
, respectively. Our goal is to show
[\alpha,\beta]\neq 0
, or equivalently that
[a,b]\in\Diff_{\partial}(D^{d})
is not isotopic to the identity.

The commutator
[a,b]
is supported on a collar
[0,1]\times S^{d-1}
, where it is given by the formula

(t,x)\mapsto\left(t,\big(f\left[A_{t},B_{t}\right]f^{-1}\big)(x)\right).

To show that
[a,b]
is not isotopic to the identity,
recall from the proof of Theorem
D
that if
\rho(A,B)=1
, then the path
r_{t}:=[A_{t},B_{t}]
defines a nontrivial loop in
\pi_{1}\big(\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\big)
. Now by the preceding discussion of the MMN pairing, the diffeomorphism
(t,x)\mapsto\big(t,(f\circ r_{t}^{-1}\circ f^{-1})(x)\big)
represents the isotopy class
\eta\cdot[f]\in\Theta_{d+1}
. Since by assumption
\eta\cdot[f]\neq 0
, it follows that the isotopy class of
[a,b]
is nontrivial, as desired. Consequently, the extension (
7
) does not split.
∎
