Residual-Based a Posteriori Error Estimators for Algebraic Stabilizations
Abhinav Jha

Abstract
In this note, we extend the analysis for the residual-based a posteriori error estimators in the energy norm defined for the algebraic flux correction (AFC) schemes [ 6 ] to the newly proposed algebraic stabilization schemes [ 9 , 10 ] . Numerical simulations on adaptively refined grids are performed in two dimensions showing the higher efficiency of an algebraic stabilization with similar accuracy compared with an AFC scheme.



keyword
steady-state convection diffusion reaction equations; algebraically stabilized finite element methods; a posteriori estimator; adaptive grid refinement

1
Introduction

The convection diffusion reaction equation represents an important mathematical model that defines a wide range of physical phenomena, such as species concentration. The partial differential equation for this model is given by

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\Delta u+{\boldsymbol{b}}\cdot\nabla u+cu

\displaystyle=f\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{in}\ \ \Omega

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=u_{{\mathrm{D}}}\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{on}\ \ \Gamma_{{\mathrm{D}}},

\displaystyle\varepsilon\nabla u\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=g\qquad

\displaystyle\mathrm{on}\ \ \Gamma_{{\mathrm{N}}},

(1)

where
\varepsilon>0
is the constant diffusion coeffecient,
{\boldsymbol{b}}
is the convective field,
c
is the reaction coeffecient,
f
is the source/sink term,
u_{{\mathrm{D}}}
is the Dirichlet boundary condition,
g
is the Neumann boundary condition,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

d\geq 2
is a polygonal domain having boundary
\Gamma
such that
\Gamma=\Gamma_{{\mathrm{D}}}\cup\Gamma_{{\mathrm{N}}}
(
\Gamma_{{\mathrm{D}}}\cap\Gamma_{{\mathrm{N}}}=\emptyset
),
\Gamma_{{\mathrm{D}}}
being the Dirichlet boundary component and
\Gamma_{{\mathrm{N}}}
is the Neumann boundary component.

Whenever convection dominates diffusion, i.e.,
L\|{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{\infty,\Omega}\gg\varepsilon
(where
L
is the characteristic length of the problem), we have the presence of layers on the boundary or the interior of the domain. Layers are narrow regions where the solution has a steep gradient. Another important property of Eq. (
1
) is that the solution satisfies maximum principles. Hence, one would like the numerical solution to satisfy its discrete counterpart, the Discrete Maximum Principle (DMP). Standard numerical techniques such as the central finite difference or Galerkin finite element methods fail on both levels, i.e., they do not approximate the layers and fail to satisfy DMP, and hence we get solutions with under and overshoots.

The linear stabilization schemes such as the Streamline Upwind Petrov Galerkin (SUPG)
[
5
]
approximate the layers but fail to satisfy the DMP, and hence we have the presence of under and overshoots near the layers
[
8
]
. The
Algebraic Flux Correction
(AFC) scheme
[
11
]
belongs to a small class of nonlinear stabilization schemes that satisfy both properties. The numerical analysis of these schemes has been developed in
[
1
]
. Recently, a more generalized approach has been presented in
[
9
,
10
]
called
Algebraic Stabilizations
. We refer to
[
3
]
for a detailed review of these methods.

Another way of resolving the layers is by using adaptive grid refinement which in turn requires the development of a posteriori error estimators. In
[
6
]
, a residual-based posteriori estimator was developed in the energy norm for the AFC schemes, which was independent of the choice of limiters. In this work, we extend the analysis of the residual-based a posteriori estimator for the AFC schemes to the algebraically stabilized methods. Numerical studies are two dimensions are performed for the method developed using
\mathbb{P}_{1}
finite elements.

2
Algebraic Stabilization Schemes

In this paper we use standard notions for Sobolev spaces and their norms. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
be a measurable set, then the
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}
-inner product is defined by
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
and its duality pairing by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. The norm (semi-norm) on
{\mathrm{W}}^{m,p}(\Omega)
is denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{m,p,\Omega}(|\cdot|_{m,p,\Omega})
with the convention
{\mathrm{W}}^{m,2}(\Omega)={\mathrm{H}}^{m}(\Omega)
and
\|\cdot\|_{m,\Omega}=\|\cdot\|_{m,2,\Omega}
(similarly
|\cdot|_{m,\Omega}=|\cdot|_{m,2,\Omega}
).

Under the assumption

c(x)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{b}}(x)\geq\sigma>0,

(2)

it is well known that Eq. (
1
) possesses a unique weak solution
u\in{\mathrm{H}}_{{\mathrm{D}}}^{1}(\Omega)
that satisfies

a(u,v)=\left\langle f,v\right\rangle+\left\langle g,v\right\rangle_{\Gamma_{{\mathrm{N}}}}\qquad\forall v\in{\mathrm{H}}_{0,{\mathrm{D}}}^{1}(\Omega),

with

\displaystyle a(u,v)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left(\nabla u,\nabla v\right)+\left({\boldsymbol{b}}\cdot\nabla u,v\right)+(cu,v),

\displaystyle{\mathrm{H}}_{{\mathrm{D}}}^{1}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega):v|_{\Gamma_{{\mathrm{D}}}}=u_{{\mathrm{D}}}\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathrm{H}}_{0,{\mathrm{D}}}^{1}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega):v|_{\Gamma_{{\mathrm{D}}}}=0\right\},

and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Gamma_{{\mathrm{N}}}}
is the duality pairing restricted to the Neumann boundary, e.g., see
[
12
, Sec. III.1.1]
.

The algebraic stabilizations schemes for Eq. (
1
) reads as (see
[
9
]
): Find
u_{h}\in W_{h}\left(\subset\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Omega})\cap{\mathrm{H}}_{{\mathrm{D}}}^{1}(\Omega)\right)
such that

a_{{\mathrm{AS}}}(u_{h};u_{h},v_{h})=\left\langle f,v_{h}\right\rangle+\left\langle g,v_{h}\right\rangle_{\Gamma_{{\mathrm{N}}}}\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h},

(3)

with
a_{{\mathrm{AS}}}(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot):{\mathrm{H}}_{{\mathrm{D}}}^{1}(\Omega)\times{\mathrm{H}}_{{\mathrm{D}}}^{1}(\Omega)\times{\mathrm{H}}_{0,{\mathrm{D}}}^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

a_{{\mathrm{AS}}}(u;v,w)=a(v,w)+b_{h}(u;v,w),

where
W_{h},V_{h}
are linear finite-dimensional subspaces of
\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Omega})\cap{\mathrm{H}}_{{\mathrm{D}}}^{1}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{C}\left(\overline{\Omega}\right)\cap{\mathrm{H}}_{0,{\mathrm{D}}}^{1}(\Omega)
, respectively and
b_{h}(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
is the nonlinear stabilization term.

For the algebraic stabilization scheme proposed in
[
9
,
10
]
the stabilization term is given by

b(u;v,w)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}b_{ij}(u)v_{j}w_{i}\qquad\forall u,v,w\in\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Omega}),

(4)

with

\displaystyle b_{ij}(u)

\displaystyle=-\max\left\{\left(1-\alpha_{ij}(u)\right)a_{ij},0,\left(1-\alpha_{ji}(u)\right)a_{ji}\right\},\quad i\neq j

\displaystyle b_{ii}(u)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\neq j}b_{ij}(u),

where
N
is the total number of degree of freedoms,
\alpha_{ij}(u)\in[0,1]
is the not necessarily
symmetric limiters,
a_{ij}
is the stiffness matrix entries corresponding to Eq. (
1
), and
v_{j}
(similarly
w_{i}
) denote
v(x_{j})
. In this paper we will concentrate on the Symmetric Monotone Upwind-type Algebraically Stabilized (SMUAS) method proposed in
[
10
]
. This analysis is also applicable to the Monotone Upwind-type Algebraically Stabilized (MUAS) method proposed in
[
9
]
as the SMUAS is a generalization of the MUAS method. The SMUAS is an upwind type linearity-preserving method which satisfies the DMP on arbitrary simplicial meshes. The limiters are computed as follows:

1.

Compute

\displaystyle P_{i}^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in S_{i}}|d_{ij}|\left\{(u_{i}-u_{j})^{+}+(u_{i}-u_{ij})^{+}\right\},

\displaystyle P_{i}^{-}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in S_{i}}|d_{ij}|\left\{(u_{i}-u_{j})^{-}+(u_{i}-u_{ij})^{-}\right\},

where
S_{i}=\{j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\,:\,a_{ij}\neq 0\}
.

2.

Compute

Q_{i}^{+}=\sum_{j\in S_{i}}s_{ij}\left\{(u_{j}-u_{i})^{+}+(u_{ij}-u_{i})^{+}\right\},\ \ Q_{i}^{-}=\sum_{j\in S_{i}}s_{ij}\left\{(u_{j}-u_{i})^{-}+(u_{ij}-u_{i})^{-}\right\}.

3.

Compute

R_{i}^{+}=\min\left\{1,\frac{Q_{i}^{+}}{P_{i}^{+}}\right\},\quad R_{i}^{-}=\min\left\{1,\frac{Q_{i}^{-}}{P_{i}^{-}}\right\},\qquad i=1,\dots,M,

where
M
is the number of non-Dirichlet nodes. If
P_{i}^{+}
or
P_{i}^{-}
is zero, one sets
R_{i}^{+}=1
or
R_{i}^{-}=1
, respectively. The values of
R_{i}^{+}
and
R_{i}^{-}
are set to
1
for Dirichlet nodes as well.

4.

Define

\alpha_{ij}=\begin{cases}R_{i}^{+}&\mbox{ if }u_{i}>u_{j},\\
1&\mbox{ if }u_{i}=u_{j},\\
R_{i}^{-}&\mbox{ if }u_{i}<u_{j},\end{cases}\quad i,j=1,\dots,N.

In the above definition
s_{ij}=\max\{|a_{ij}|,a_{ji}\}
and
u_{ij}=u_{i}+\nabla u_{h}|_{K_{i}^{j}}\cdot(x_{i}-x_{j})
for all
j\in S_{i}
. Here
K_{i}^{j}
is a mesh cell containing
x_{i}
that is intersected by the half line
\{x_{i}+\theta(x_{i}-x_{j}):\theta>0\}
. In
[
10
]
, optimal convergence rates were observed numerically, whereas the algebraic flux correction schemes and the MUAS method did not have the optimal rates on non-Delaunay meshes.

3
A Posteriori Error Estimators

The starting point of the analysis is the natural norm of Eq. (
3
) given by

\|u\|_{{\mathrm{AS}}}^{2}=\|u\|_{a}^{2}+b_{h}(u;u,u),

where
\|\cdot\|_{a}
is the energy norm given by
\|u\|_{a}^{2}=\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\sigma\|u\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

Now, the error in the
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathrm{AS}}}
norm can be written as

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}\|_{{\mathrm{AS}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{{\mathrm{AS}}}(u_{h};u-u_{h},u-u_{h})

\displaystyle=\langle f,u-I_{h}u\rangle+\langle g,u-I_{h}u\rangle_{\Gamma_{{\mathrm{N}}}}-a(u_{h},u-I_{h}u)

\displaystyle\quad+b_{h}\left(u_{h};u-u_{h},u-u_{h}-I_{h}(u-u_{h})\right)+b_{h}(u_{h};u,I_{h}u-I_{h}u_{h}),

(5)

where we have used Galerkin orthogonality arguments and
I_{h}
denotes the Scott-Zhang interpolation operator,
[
4
, Sec. 4.8]
(see
[
6
, Sec. 3.1.1]
for a detailed explanation). Now the first two terms in Eq. (
5
) can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}\|_{{\mathrm{AS}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(R_{K}(u_{h}),u-I_{h}u\right)_{K}+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}}\left\langle R_{F}(u_{h}),u-I_{h}u\right\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle\quad+b_{h}(u_{h};u,I_{h}u-u_{h})+b_{h}(u_{h};u-u_{h},u-u_{h}-I_{h}(u-u_{h})),

(6)

with
R_{K}(u_{h}):=f+\varepsilon\Delta u_{u}-{\boldsymbol{b}}\cdot\nabla u_{u}-cu_{h}|_{K}
, and

R_{F}(u_{h}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\varepsilon\left[\![\nabla u_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F}]\!\right]_{F}&\mathrm{if}\ \ F\in\mathcal{F}_{h,\Omega},\\
g-\varepsilon(\nabla u_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{F})&\mathrm{if}\ \ F\in\mathcal{F}_{h,{\mathrm{N}}},\\
0&\mathrm{if}\ \ F\in\mathcal{F}_{h,{\mathrm{D}}},\end{array}\right.

where
\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h>0}
denotes the triangulation of
\Omega
,
\mathcal{F}_{h}\left(:=\mathcal{F}_{h,\Omega}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h,{\mathrm{N}}}\cup\mathcal{F}_{h,{\mathrm{D}}}\right)
denotes the set of faces (with
\mathcal{F}_{h,\Omega}
,
\mathcal{F}_{h,{\mathrm{N}}}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{h,{\mathrm{D}}}
denoting the interior, Neumann, and Dirichlet faces respectively),
\left[\![\cdot]\!\right]_{F}
denotes the jump across the face
F
, and
\boldsymbol{n}_{F}
denotes the outward pointing normal on face
F
.

The first two terms in Eq. (
6
) can be approximated in a standard way using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, Young’s inequality, interpolation estimates and trace estimates. Using these arguments we get the global upper bound,

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}\|_{a}^{2}+\frac{C_{Y}}{C_{Y}-1}b_{h}(u_{h};u-u_{h},u-u_{h})

(7)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C^{2}_{Y}}{2(C_{Y}-1)}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\mathrm{min}\left\{\frac{C_{I}^{2}}{\sigma},\ \frac{C_{I}^{2}h_{K}^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\}\|R_{K}(u_{h})\|_{0,K}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{C^{2}_{Y}}{2(C_{Y}-1)}\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}\min\left\{\frac{C_{F}^{2}h_{F}}{\varepsilon},\frac{C_{F}^{2}}{\sigma^{1/2}\varepsilon^{1/2}}\right\}\|R_{F}(u_{h})\|_{0,F}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{C_{Y}}{C_{Y}-1}b_{h}(u_{h};u,I_{h}u-u_{h})+\frac{C_{Y}}{C_{Y}-1}b_{h}\left(u_{h};u-u_{h},u-u_{h}-I_{h}(u-u_{h})\right),

where
C_{I}
denotes the interpolation constant
[
4
, Corollary 4.8.15]
,
C_{F}
is the trace estimate constant
[
13
, Sec. 3.3]
, and
C_{Y}
is the generalized Young’s inequality constant which will be chosen later.

Using the linearity of
b_{h}(\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
in the second and the third argument, the last two terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (
7
) can be reduced to

\displaystyle b_{h}(u_{h};u-u_{h},u-u_{h}-I_{h}(u-u_{h}))+b_{h}(u_{h};u,I_{h}(u-u_{h}))

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{h}(u_{h};u-u_{h},u-u_{h})+b_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},I_{h}(u-u_{h})).

Inserting Eq. (
8
) in Eq. (
7
) we notice that the stabilization term on the left and the right cancels. Till here we have followed the same procedure as of
[
6
]
. Now we approximate the second term in Eq. (
8
). We notice that

\displaystyle b_{h}(u_{h};u_{h},I_{h}u-u_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}b_{ij}(u_{h})u_{h,j}(I_{h}u_{i}-u_{h,i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}b_{ij}(u_{h})u_{h,j}\left(I_{h}u_{i}-u_{h,i}\right)+b_{ii}(u_{h})u_{h,i}\left(I_{h}u_{i}-u_{h,i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\neq j}b_{ij}(u_{h})\left(u_{h,j}-u_{h,i}\right)\left(I_{h}u_{i}-u_{h,i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{h}}|b_{E}(u_{h})|h_{E}\left(\nabla u_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{E},\nabla\left(I_{h}u-u_{h}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{E}\right)_{E},

where
\mathcal{E}_{h}
denotes the set of all edges,
h_{E}
corresponds to the edge
E
having end points
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
,
\boldsymbol{t}_{E}
is the tangential derivative along
E
, and we have used the edge formulation from
[
3
]
.

This term is similar to
[
6
, Eq. (35)]
and hence we can use the same bound but by replacing
(1-\alpha_{E})d_{E}
by
|b_{E}|
and hence choosing the optimal value of
C_{Y}=4
, we get the global upper bound as

\|u-u_{h}\|_{a}^{2}\leq\eta^{2}=\eta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}+\eta_{3}^{2},

(9)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\mathrm{min}\left\{\frac{4C_{I}^{2}}{\sigma},\ \frac{4C_{I}^{2}h_{K}^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\}\|R_{K}(u_{h})\|_{0,K}^{2},

\displaystyle\eta_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{h}}\min\left\{\frac{4C_{F}^{2}h_{F}}{\varepsilon},\frac{4C_{F}^{2}}{\sigma^{1/2}\varepsilon^{1/2}}\right\}\|R_{F}(u_{h})\|_{0,F}^{2},

\displaystyle\eta_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{h}}\min\Bigg\{\frac{4\kappa_{1}h_{E}^{2}}{\varepsilon},\frac{4\kappa_{2}}{\sigma}\Bigg\}|b_{E}|^{2}h_{E}^{1-d}\|\nabla u_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}_{E}\|_{0,E}^{2}.

The constants
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are given by

\kappa_{1}=CC_{\mathrm{edge,max}}\left(1+\left(1+C_{I}\right)^{2}\right),\quad\kappa_{2}=CC_{\mathrm{inv}}^{2}C_{\mathrm{edge,max}}\left(1+\left(1+C_{I}\right)^{2}\right),

where
C
is a general constant independent of
h
,
C_{\mathrm{inv}}
is an
inverse inequality constant
[
4
, Lemma 4.5.3]
, and
C_{\mathrm{edge,max}}
is a computable
constant given by
[
6
, Remark 9]
.

Remark 1

The local lower bound for the residual-based estimator was derived in
[
6
, Theorem 2]
. We would like to note that the lower bound was a formal local lower bound as the edge estimates (
\eta_{3}
) were approximated. We can use the same formal local lower bound here as

|b_{E}|\leq C\left(\varepsilon+\|{\boldsymbol{b}}\|_{\infty,\Omega}h+\|c\|_{\infty,\Omega}h^{2}\right)h_{E}^{d-2},

see
[
9
, Eq. (65)]
.

4
Numerical Studies

This section presents numerical studies for the a posteriori error estimator developed in this paper. We compare the algebraic stabilization schemes introduced in Sec.
2
to the AFC scheme with the BJK limiter
[
2
]
. In
[
6
]
, it was shown that the BJK limiter was the most accurate method, although not the most efficient.

The general strategy of solving a posteriori error problem includes

\mbox{{SOLVE}}\rightarrow\mbox{{ESTIMATE}}\rightarrow\mbox{{MARK}}\rightarrow\mbox{{REFINE}}.

In this paper, we will use red-green refinements
[
13
]
to refine the grids, and for the marking of the mesh cells, we use the maximum marking strategy
[
13
]
. We use a fixed-point technique referred to as
fixed point rhs
in
[
7
]
along with a dynamic damping parameter to solve the system of nonlinear equations. The stopping criteria is set to
10^{4}
nonlinear loops or when the residual is less than
10^{-8}\sqrt{\#\mathrm{dofs}}
, where
\#\mathrm{dofs}
stands for the number of degrees of freedom.

The constants arising in Eq. (
9
) are set to unity during the simulations, like in
[
6
]
. For the adaptive loop the stopping criteria is if
\eta\leq 10^{-3}
or when
\#\mathrm{dofs}\geq 10^{5}
. The example considered in this paper is defined on the unit square, i.e.,
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
, and the initial grid is obtained by joining the line
(0,0)
and
(1,1)
. We start the simulation by uniformly refining the grid so that
\#\mathrm{dofs}=25
, followed by two steps of uniform refinement and then the adaptive process. All the simulations used
\mathbb{P}_{1}
finite elements. We would like to note that this is the first simulation with SMUAS on adaptively refined grids.

5
Summary

This paper extended the analysis of
[
6
]
to the class of new algebraic stabilization schemes, which encompasses the MUAS
[
9
]
and the SMUAS
[
10
]
method. Numerical tests were performed to compare the SMUAS method with the AFC scheme using the BJK limiter. It was observed that both the methods are accurate, and the errors are comparable, but the SMUAS method is much more efficient compared to the BJK limiter. In the future, we would like to perform a deeper numerical study of the different algebraic stabilization schemes on adaptively refined grids.
