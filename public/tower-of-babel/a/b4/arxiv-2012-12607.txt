Abstract
We study polynomial-time approximation schemes (PTASes) for constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) such as Maximum Independent Set or Minimum Vertex Cover on sparse graph classes. Baker’s approach gives a PTAS on planar graphs, excluded-minor classes, and beyond. For Max-CSPs, and even more generally, maximisation finite-valued CSPs (where constraints are arbitrary non-negative functions), Romero, Wrochna, and Živný [SODA’21] showed that the Sherali-Adams LP relaxation gives a simple PTAS for all fractionally-treewidth-fragile classes, which is the most general “sparsity” condition for which a PTAS is known. We extend these results to general-valued CSPs, which include “crisp” (or “strict”) constraints that have to be satisfied by every feasible assignment. The only condition on the crisp constraints is that their domain contains an element which is at least as feasible as all the others (but possibly less valuable). For minimisation general-valued CSPs with crisp constraints, we present a PTAS for all Baker graph classes — a definition by Dvořák [SODA’20] which encompasses all classes where Baker’s technique is known to work, except for fractionally-treewidth-fragile classes. While this is standard for problems satisfying a certain monotonicity condition on crisp constraints, we show this can be relaxed to diagonalisability — a property of relational structures connected to logics, statistical physics, and random CSPs.

Introduction

Min-Ones and Max-Ones, studied by Khanna and Motwani (under the names of TMIN and
TMAX, respectively)
[
31
]
and by Khanna, Sudan, Trevisan, and
Williamson
[
32
]
, are Boolean CSPs in which one seeks a
feasible solution (a 0–1 assignment satisfying all constraints) minimising/maximising the number of variables assigned the
label
1
. Classical examples are the Minimum Vertex Cover and the Maximum
Independent Set problem, respectively. A natural generalisation to larger
alphabets is the problem in which one seeks a solution to a CSP instance while
minimising/maximising a sum of unary functions. With injective unary functions, such problems have
been studied under the name of
Strict-CSP
by plumar, Manokaran, Tulsiani, and
Vishnoi
[
36
]
, and Min/Max-Solution by Jonsson, Kuivinen, and
Nordh
[
29
]
. With arbitrary unary functions, such problems
have been studied under the name of
Min-Cost-Hom
by Gutin, Hell, Rafiey, and
Yeo
[
23
]
, Takhanov
[
46
]
, and
others
[
25
,
24
,
40
]
.
In this paper we consider the still more general setting of
general-valued
CSPs, where constraints are functions which give values to every possible assignment on a tuple of variables;
we allow
\infty
or
-\infty
values to express
crisp
(also known as
strict
) constraints,
which have to be satisfied by every feasible (finite-valued) assignment.
While a lot of research is devoted to exact algorithms or optimal approximation
ratios in APX-hard cases (see
[
30
,
27
,
37
]
for surveys),
we seek the most general conditions that allow to obtain a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS).

Baker
[
1
]
gave an elegant method (sometimes known as the
shifting
or
layering technique
) for constructing polynomial-time approximation schemes (PTASes) which applies to many such problems, with the condition that the input instance’s graph (the Gaifman graph) is “sparse”.
This was initially presented for planar graphs, but it is known that similar structural properties are exhibited by all proper minor-closed graph classes
[
20
,
12
,
11
]
and beyond: e.g. graphs embeddable in a fixed surface with few intersections per edge
[
38
,
19
]
, or sparse unit ball intersection graphs in few dimensions
[
26
]

(but not e.g. 3-regular expanders: bounded degree is not sufficient to get a
PTAS even for Independent Set
[
2
]
).
Dvořák
[
15
]
defined
fractionally-treewidth-fragile
classes
— a natural generalisation of earlier sparsity conditions — which encompasses all these examples.
A class of graphs is fractionally-treewidth-fragile if one can remove vertices in a randomised way so that each vertex is removed with arbitrarily small probability
\varepsilon
, but the treewidth after removal is always bounded, the bound depending on
\varepsilon
only.
He showed that if this notion of sparsity can be efficiently certified in a class of graphs, then this suffices to guarantee a PTAS, at least for a few problems such as Weighted Maximum Independent Set.
On the other hand it is not known whether this suffices for Minimum Vertex Cover, for example.

To remedy this, Dvořák
[
16
]
later defined
Baker classes
and proved that (an effective version of) this condition suffices to provide a PTAS
to all monotone optimisation problems expressible in first-order logic (including of course Vertex Cover).
Very roughly, a class of graphs is
Baker
if one can reduce each graph in it to the empty graph by a bounded number of the following steps: either remove a single vertex, or select a breadth-first-search layering and recurse into all subgraphs that can be induced by a few consecutive layers.
Dvořák proved that the family of Baker classes still includes all the examples discussed above;
on the other hand, it is strictly included in the family of fractionally-treewidth-fragile classes (and hence less general)
[
14
]
.
It is worth mentioning that proper minor-closed graph classes can be shown to be
Baker (and fractionally-treewidth-fragile) relatively easily, without using the
Graph Minor Structure theorem, in contrast to the earlier, less general
definitions (see
[
16
]
for details).

In order to provide a PTAS for a class of general-valued CSPs,
a sparsity
condition is not enough
:
we also need to restrict what types of constraints can be used in an instance.
Otherwise, even if the values to be optimised are trivial, either 0 or infinity,
one could use the crisp constraints to express 3-Colouring, which is NP-hard
even on planar graphs of bounded degree
[
10
]
. In fact, as long as all
crisp constraints are available, for any possible restriction on Gaifman graphs,
either the restriction implies bounded treewidth, making the problem exactly
solvable, or it is hard to decide whether the optimum is zero or infinite, by a
result of Grohe, Schwentick, and Segoufin
[
22
]
.
We will hence require a condition which ensures that one can easily decide whether a feasible solution (of finite value) exists.
This usually takes the form of a monotonicity condition.

On the other hand,
some sparsity condition is also necessary
: on
general Gaifman graphs, there is no restriction of constraint types that
would result in a general-valued CSP that admits a PTAS but is not solvable exactly in
polynomial time.

1.1
Related work

The exact solvability of general-valued CSPs has
been characterised for left-hand side restrictions
[
8
]
(tractable cases are precisely classes that have bounded treewidth, up to a certain notion of homomorphic equivalence) and right-hand side
restrictions
[
34
]
(tractable cases are precisely
delineated by certain algebraic properties); both results include the case where infinite values are allowed.

As discussed above, there are no PTASes for general-valued CSPs with only left-hand side or only right-hand side restrictions, beyond exactly solvable cases.
In fact Khanna et al.
[
32
]
, in their work on Min-Ones and Max-Ones with right-hand side restriction, remark that “Our framework lacks such phenomena as PTAS” and discuss left-hand side restrictions as an interesting avenue for future work for that reason.
Similarly
[
29
]
and
[
30
]
ask in the context of
right-hand side restricted Min-Solution and Max-Solution problems: “Under which restrictions on variable scopes does Max Sol admit a PTAS?”.

Very recently, PTASes for left-hand side restricted Max-CSP without crisp constraints, such as Max-Cut, have been studied by Romero, Wrochna, and Živný
[
44
]
.
More generally, they consider so-called finite-valued CSPs, where the only right-hand side restriction is having finite, non-negative values.
They showed a PTAS is possible for every fractionally-treewidth-fragile class of Gaifman graphs.
In fact the algorithm is simply the Sherali-Adams linear programming relaxation (with a growing number of levels giving a better and better approximation),
which is oblivious to the graph structure and does not require it to be efficiently certified in any way.

As for constant-factor approximations, Raghavendra’s celebrated result gave the
best approximation ratio, assuming the Unique Games Conjecture of Khot
[
33
]
, for all
right-hand side restricted Max-CSPs (and also finite-valued
CSPs)
[
41
]
.
Analogous results for monotone Strict-CSPs were obtained by Kumar et
al.
[
36
]
. Constant-factor approximation algorithms have been
established for right-hand side restricted
Min-Cost-Hom
on special graphs by
Hell, Mastrolilli, Nevisi, and Rafiey
[
24
]
, and for
all graphs and some digraphs by Rafiey, Rafiey, and Santos
[
40
]
.

1.2
Our results

As in most research on constraint satisfaction, our main motivation is
to understand the mathematical structure that governs efficient computation and,
if at all possible, to obtain complexity classifications of large fragments of
CSPs. In this paper, the goal was to push the tractability frontier of
general-valued CSPs that admit polynomial-time approximation schemes.
In particular, we try to understand what right-hand side restrictions make
Baker’s technique possible, tentatively answering the aforementioned questions
from
[
32
,
29
,
30
]
. We show that the most
general results known for Vertex Cover and Independent Set (PTASes on all Baker
or fractionally-treewidth-fragile classes, respectively) can be extended to any
to general-valued CSPs with a certain monotonicity restriction, and even further.

To clearly separate left-hand side and right-hand side restriction,
it is convenient to phrase a general-valued CSP (VCSP) as the problem of optimising the
value of a function between two valued structures.
Precise definitions are given in
Section

2
.
Briefly, a
valued structure

\mathbb{A}
consists of a (finite) domain
A
and a collection of functions
f^{\mathbb{A}}\colon A^{n}\to\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
, indexed by symbols
f
belonging to a set of symbols
\sigma
called a signature.
For two (finite) structures
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C}
, the
value
of an assignment
h\colon A\to C

is an expression of the form
\sum f^{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x})f^{\mathbb{C}}(h(\mathbf{x}))
.
We will be seeking to find either the minimum or maximum value over all assignments,
denoted
\minval(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
and
\maxval(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
respectively.

Feasible
assignments are those of finite value.
The reader should think of the left-hand side structure
\mathbb{A}
as of a set of variables
A
together with weighted constraint scopes: for
\mathbf{x}\in A^{n}
,
f^{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x})=w\neq 0
means that the instance applies the constraint “
f
” to variables in
\mathbf{x}
with weight
w
.
The right-hand side structure
\mathbb{C}
encodes the alphabet
C
(to which an assignment
h
maps each variable) and the collection of available constraints, which could be arbitrary
\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
-valued cost functions in general.
An instance of the VCSP is a pair
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
; its Gaifman graph, denoted
by
\Gaifman(\mathbb{A})
, is a graph whose vertex set is the domain
A
with edges between two vertices that occur together in a constraint of non-zero weight.

Minimum Solution

For minimisation, we first consider
(\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\})
-valued right-hand side structures
\mathbb{C}
, in which the sets of zero-valued tuples and finite-valued tuples are anti-monotone, in the following sense.
There is a total order
\leq_{\top}
on
C
, and for all tuples
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in C^{n}
with
\mathbf{x}\leq_{\top}\mathbf{y}
(coordinate-wise) we have that for all non-unary function symbols
f
of
\mathbb{C}
:

•

f^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{x})<\infty
implies
f^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{y})<\infty
, and

•

f^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{x})=0
implies
f^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{y})=0
.

Intuitively, larger tuples are more feasible.
We call valued structures
\mathbb{C}
satisfying this condition
Min-Sol
structures.
We define
\mbox{Min-Sol}_{\mathcal{G}}
to be the general-valued CSP restricted to instances

(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
where
\mathbb{A}
is a
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
-valued structured with
\Gaifman(\mathbb{A})\in\mathcal{G}
and
\mathbb{C}
is a
Min-Sol
structure.

For example, Weighted Minimum Vertex Cover is equivalent to the
Min-Sol
case where

\mathbb{C}
is the structure with domain
\{0,1\}
and
0\leq 1
and
with a 2-ary cost function
f^{\mathbb{C}}(0,0)=\infty
,
f^{\mathbb{C}}(1,0)=f^{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)=f^{\mathbb{C}}(1,1)=0
,
and a unary cost function
u^{\mathbb{C}}(0)=0
,
u^{\mathbb{C}}(1)=1
.

We
show that
\mbox{Min-Sol}_{\mathcal{G}}
admits a PTAS for all graph classes
\mathcal{G}
that are efficiently Baker.
(Dvořák’s definition is somewhat involved but we give an exposition in
Section

4
).
As discussed above, this captures essentially all graph classes where a version of Baker’s technique is known to apply (including excluded-minor classes and more),
except for fractionally-treewidth-fragile classes.
We remark that already the very special case of Minimum Vertex Cover is not known to admit a PTAS on fractionally-treewidth-fragile classes.

Simultaneously, our results are less restrictive on the right-hand side,
as unlike in earlier work such as the framework of
Strict-CSP
of
[
36
]
,
we allow arbitrary values strictly between
0
and
\infty
(not only on unary constraints).
Once we realise this is possible, however, the algorithm turns out to be a rather standard application of Baker’s technique: the only difference is that we increase the number of layers to account for the maximum ratio between finite, positive values (which is a constant depending on values of
\mathbb{C}
only).

The main novelty in our work is establishing the existence of a
PTAS under a weaker assumption on the right-hand side structure
\mathbb{C}
– we only
require that
\mathbb{C}
should be a diagonalisable structure. (As we will
show in
Lemma

3.5
, all

Min-Sol
structures are diagonalisable and thus our result establishes a PTAS
for
Min-Sol
structures as a special case.)
Diagonalisability is a notion derived from the work of Brightwell and
Winkler
[
6
]
in the case of graphs and Briceño, Bulatov,
Dalmau, and Larose
[
5
]
in the case of
relational structures (which are more general than graphs).
The precise definition of diagonalisability is technical and can be found
in
Section

3.1
.
For relational structures, one characterisation is that a structure
\mathbb{C}
is diagonalisable if and only if
the two projection homomorphisms
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\colon\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
(defined as
\pi_{i}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{i}
)
are connected by some sequence of homomorphisms
\psi\colon\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
such that consecutive homomorphisms in the sequence differ at only one vertex, and all the homomorphisms in the sequence are idempotent (meaning
\psi(x,x)=x
).
This turns out to be equivalent to saying that for all structures
\mathbb{A}
, the set of all homomorphisms from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{C}
is connected in a similar sense.
A few other characterisations connect diagonalisability to statistical physics via “mixing” properties.
Diagonalisability is also equivalent to finite duality (the existence of finitely many obstructions to having a homomorphism into
\mathbb{C}
),
a notion important to the study of CSPs via logic
[
7
]
.
For these and many other equivalent definitions of diagonalisability, cf.
[
5
, Corollary 6.3 and Theorem 3.6]
with
J=V(H)
.

Our main result for minimisation
(proved in
Section

4
)
is an approximation scheme for instances
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
where
\mathbb{A}
comes from a Baker class and
\mathbb{C}
is diagonalisable.
One should think of the functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
as polynomials depending on the size of the graph
\Gaifman(\mathbb{A})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an
(f_{1},f_{2})
-efficiently Baker class.
Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
and any instance
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
of general-valued CSP
where
\mathbb{A}
is a
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
-valued structured with
\Gaifman(\mathbb{A})\in\mathcal{G}
and
\mathbb{C}
is diagonalisable, we can find a solution of value
at most
(1+\varepsilon)\minval(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
in time
f_{1}(\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert)+f_{2}(c\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert)\cdot c^{1/\varepsilon}

where
c
depends on
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathcal{G}
only.

Here the constant
c
depends polynomially on
\lvert\mathbb{C}\rvert
and exponentially on the maximum ratio between certain finite positive values of
\mathbb{C}
.
Since every class of graphs that excludes a minor is

(\mathcal{O}\left\lparen n^{2}\right\rparen,\mathcal{O}\left\lparen n\right\rparen)
-efficiently Baker
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
,
Theorem

1.1
in fact gives an EPTAS on such classes for any fixed diagonalisable structure
\mathbb{C}
.

Intuitively, diagonalisability allows to interpolate between any two homomorphisms, and we show this gives a natural way to combine partial solutions in the way needed in Baker’s technique (generalising the simple combination used for Vertex Cover: taking the set-theoretic sum of solutions).
This proof (Theorem
3.7
), which is an entirely new connection between diagonalisability and approximation, is our main contribution.

Maximum Solution

For maximisation, we extend the results of
[
44
]
,
which restricted the right-hand side
\mathbb{C}
to be
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
-valued.
We additionally allow
-\infty
values,
but the set of tuples
\mathbf{y}\in C^{n}
with
f^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{y})=-\infty

is restricted to be monotone in the following very weak sense.
There is an element
c_{\bot}\in C
such that
whenever
\mathbf{y}
is feasible (
f^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{y})\neq-\infty
)
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
is a tuple obtained from
\mathbf{y}
by replacing some of its elements with
c_{\bot}
,
then
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
is still feasible (
f^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\neq-\infty
).

We call structures
\mathbb{C}
satisfying this condition
Max-Sol
structures
and we define
\mbox{Max-Sol}_{\mathcal{G}}
to be the general-valued CSP restricted to instances

(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
where
\mathbb{A}
is a
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
-valued structured with
\Gaifman(\mathbb{A})\in\mathcal{G}
and
\mathbb{C}
is a
Max-Sol
structure.

For example, Weighted Maximum Independent Set is equivalent to the
Max-Sol
case where

\mathbb{C}
is the structure with domain
\{0,1\}
,
with a 2-ary function
f^{\mathbb{C}}(1,1)=-\infty
,
f^{\mathbb{C}}(0,0)=f^{\mathbb{C}}(1,0)=f^{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)=0
,
and a unary function
u^{\mathbb{C}}(0)=0
,
u^{\mathbb{C}}(1)=1
(so
c_{\bot}=0
).

Our main result for maximisation
(proved in
Section

5
) is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a class of graphs that is fractionally-treewidth-fragile.
Then
\mbox{Max-Sol}_{\mathcal{G}}
admits a PTAS.

More precisely, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there is an algorithm that given
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
,
outputs a value between
\maxval(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
and
(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\maxval(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})

in time
{(|\mathbb{A}|+|\mathbb{C}|)}^{k(\varepsilon)}
, where
k(\varepsilon)
is a function depending on
\mathcal{G}
only.
2
2

2

If
\mathcal{G}
is fractionally-
\tw
-fragile with rate
k(\varepsilon)
(Definition
5.16
),
then the exponent in the running time is
\mathcal{O}\left\lparen k(\Omega(\varepsilon))\right\rparen
.
See
[
17
]
for a fragility rates of various graph classes.

The algorithm in
Theorem

1.2
does nothing more than solve a

\Theta(k(\varepsilon))
-th level of the Sherali-Adams linear programming relaxation.
This allows the algorithm to be oblivious to the graph structure, i.e. we do not assume that the fractional-treewidth-fragility of
\mathcal{G}
can be efficiently certified.
Thus the left-hand side restriction on Gaifman graphs is the most general for which a PTAS is known;
as discussed earlier, it includes excluded-minor classes and more.
In fact similarly to
[
44
]
, we conjecture that
\mbox{Max-Sol}_{\mathcal{G}}
does not admit a PTAS for any
\mathcal{G}
that is not fractionally-treewidth-fragile.
Since
\mbox{Max-Sol}_{\mathcal{G}}
is strictly more general (by allowing negative infinite values), this conjecture might be easier to prove than the one in
[
44
]
.

On the other hand, this approach does not give an EPTAS even when
\mathbb{C}
is fixed (i.e. the exponent of
|\mathbb{A}|
increases with
\varepsilon
), and it does not construct an assignment — it only approximates the optimum value.
In contrast, given a class of graphs
\mathcal{G}
for which fractional-treewidth-fragility can be efficiently certified (which includes essentially all known examples),
it is straightforward to construct solutions to
\mbox{Max-Sol}_{\mathcal{G}}
of value at least
(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\maxval(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
in time
\lvert\mathbb{A}\rvert\cdot{\lvert\mathbb{C}\rvert}^{k(\varepsilon)}
.

Our main contribution in proving
Theorem

1.2
is finding the right analogues of the definitions from
[
44
]
– a notion of “closeness” of structures, a dual notion that certifies this closeness with concrete mappings (a distribution of “partial homomorphisms”, see Section

In particular, while the name “partial homomorphism” may sound deceptively simple, we found that pin-pointing their definition (in the context of MaxSol) proved to be a surprisingly intricate balancing act.

We complement
Theorem

1.2
with simple constructions which show that it is impossible to extend other results of
[
44
]
from the setting of purely optimisation Max-CSPs to the setting of general-valued CSPs, which include crisp constraints.
In
[
44
]
the notion of
pliability
is defined (for Max-CSPs), which is a left-hand side restriction that takes the whole structure
\mathbb{A}
into account, not only its Gaifman graph, as done in this introduction so far;
this allowed the authors of
[
44
]
to show that the same framework applies not only to sparse, fractionally-treewidth-fragile instances of Max-CSPS, but also to dense structures.
We define an analogous notion of
strong pliability
and show in

, similarly
to
[

]
, the existence of a PTAS (for general-valued CSPs) under the strong pliability
assumption on the left-hand side structure, which takes the whole structure

\mathbb{A}
into account, not only its Gaifman graph. (Thus this is a more general
tractability result than
Theorem

1.2

However, in
Appendix

we show that even the simplest class of dense structures, namely the class of

\{0,1\}
-valued cliques, does not satisfy strong pliability.
In fact, it is easy to show (cf.
Appendix

) that the
Max-Sol
problem is hard to approximate even when the left-hand side structures are restricted to cliques.

h(\mathbf{x})=(h(x_{1}),\ldots,h(x_{n}))
.

General-valued CSPs

A
signature
is a finite set
\sigma
of (function) symbols such as
f
, each with
a specified arity
\ar(f)
.
For a set of values
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}
,
an
\Omega
-valued structure

\mathbb{A}
over a signature
\sigma
(or

\sigma
-structure, for short) is a finite domain
A
together with
a function
f^{\mathbb{A}}:A^{\ar(f)}\to\Omega
for each symbol
f\in\sigma
.
We denote by
A,B,C,\dots
the domains of structures
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B},\mathbb{C},\dots
.

We define
\tup(\mathbb{A})
to be the set of all pairs
(f,\mathbf{x})
such that
f\in\sigma
and
\mathbf{x}\in A^{\ar(f)}
;
and
\tup_{>0}(\mathbb{A})
to be the set of all pairs
(f,\mathbf{x})\in\tup(\mathbb{A})
with
f^{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x})>0
.

We assume a straightforward
table encoding
of structures: the interpretation
f^{\mathbb{A}}
of a symbol
f
in a structure
\mathbb{A}
is encoded as a collection of triples
\{(f,\mathbf{x},f^{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x}))\mid(f,\mathbf{x})\in\tup(\mathbb{A})\}
. Thus, the size of a
\sigma
-structure
\mathbb{A}
is roughly

|\mathbb{A}|=|\sigma|+|A|+\sum_{(f,\mathbf{x})\in\tup(\mathbb{A})}\log{|\sigma|}+\ar(f)\log{|A|}+|enc(f^{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x}))|

where
enc(\cdot)
denotes a reasonable encoding for elements of
\mathbb{Q}
.

We consider the following computational problem.

Definition 2.1
.

An instance of the
general-valued CSP
(VCSP) consists of an ordered pair of
\sigma
-structures
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
.
For a mapping
h\colon A\to C
, we define the value of
h
to be

\displaystyle\val(h)=\sum_{(f,\mathbf{x})\in\tup(\mathbb{A})}f^{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x})f^{\mathbb{C}}(h(\mathbf{x})).

The goal is to find the minimum or maximum value over all possible mappings

h\colon A\to C
,
denoted
\minval(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
or
\maxval(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
, respectively.

On the left-hand side we will only use
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
-valued structures, with letters
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
;
on the right-hand side we will only use
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
or
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\cup\{-\infty\}
-valued structures, respectively, for minimisation and maximisation, with letters
\mathbb{C},\mathbb{D}
.

For
\lambda\geq 0
we write
\lambda\mathbb{A}
for the
rescaled

\sigma
-structure with domain
A
and
f^{\lambda\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x}):=\lambda f^{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x})
, for
(f,\mathbf{x})\in\tup(\mathbb{A})
.
For a
\sigma
-structure
\mathbb{A}
and subset of the domain
X\subseteq A
, we
define
\mathbb{A}[X]
to be the restriction of
\mathbb{A}
to
X
. That is,
\mathbb{A}[X]
is a

\sigma
-structure over the domain
X
, and
f^{\mathbb{A}[X]}(\mathbf{x})=f^{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x})
for each

f\in\sigma
and
\mathbf{x}\in X^{\ar(f)}
.

Following the influential work on decision CSPs by Grohe, Schwentick, and
Segoufin
[
22
]
, and Grohe
[
21
]
, we will
focus on fragments of the VCSP parametrised by the class of
left-hand side structures (or their underlying class of
graphs). Given a
\sigma
-structure
\mathbb{A}
, the
Gaifman graph
(or

primal graph
), denoted by
\Gaifman(\mathbb{A})
, is the graph whose vertex set
is the domain
A
, and whose edges are the pairs
\{u,v\}
for which there is a
tuple
\mathbf{x}
and a symbol
f\in\sigma
such that
u,v
appear in

\mathbf{x}
and
f^{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x})>0
.

For a graph parameter
\p
and a structure
\mathbb{A}
, we define

\p(\mathbb{A})\coloneqq\p(\Gaifman(\mathbb{A}))
to be the parameter of the Gaifman graph of

\mathbb{A}
. In particular, the
treewidth
of
\mathbb{A}
is defined as

\tw(\mathbb{A})\coloneqq\tw(\Gaifman(\mathbb{A}))
. (We will only use treewidth and excluded
minors as black-boxes and thus will not need their definitions. The reader is referred to Diestel’s
textbook for details
[
13
]
.)

Relational structures

A
relational
\sigma
-structure

\mathbb{C}
includes for each symbol

f\in\sigma
a relation
f^{\mathbb{C}}\subseteq C^{\ar(f)}
. We will view relational
structures as
\{0,\infty\}
-valued structures by associating each function

f^{\mathbb{C}}:C^{\ar(f)}\to\{0,\infty\}
to the relation given by the zero-valued
tuples
\{\mathbf{x}\mid f^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{x})=0\}
.
A
homomorphism
from a relational
\sigma
-structure
\mathbb{C}
to a relational

\sigma
-structure
\mathbb{D}
is a map
\psi:C\to D
that satisfies, for every

f\in\sigma
and every
\mathbf{x}\in C^{\ar(f)}
,
f^{\mathbb{D}}(\psi(\mathbf{x}))\leq f^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{x})
.

For an
n
-ary function
f
, we denote by
\Feas(f)
and

\Opt(f)
the
n
-ary relations defined by
\Feas(f)=\{\mathbf{x}\mid f(\mathbf{x})<\infty\}

and
\Opt(f)=\{\mathbf{x}\mid f(\mathbf{x})=0\}
, respectively.
Let
\mathbb{C}
be a
\sigma
-structure. The relational
\sigma
-structure
\Feas(\mathbb{C})
contains, for each
f\in\sigma
, the relation
\Feas(f^{\mathbb{C}})
; similarly, the relational

\sigma
-structure
\Opt(\mathbb{C})
contains, for every
f\in\sigma
, the relation

\Opt(f^{\mathbb{C}})
.

Our results will be concerned with two particular types of right-hand side structures.

Maximum Solution

For the following definition, recall the example of Weighted Maximum

in this context as not selecting a vertex in an independent set; the partial
order on

then says that a subset of an independent set is also independent.

Definition 2.2

(
\sqsubseteq_{c}
)
.

For an element
c
of a set

\sqsubseteq_{c}
the partial ordering on
C

defined by

and

for all

.
This induces a partial ordering on
C^{n}
coordinate-wise:
we write
\mathbf{x}\sqsubseteq_{c}\mathbf{y}
for

if we can obtain
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{y}
by changing some (possibly none or all) of its coordinates to

.

Definition 2.3

(
Max-Sol
)
.

Let
\sigma
be a finite signature.
A
\sigma
-structure
\mathbb{C}
is called a
Max-Sol
structure

if it is
(\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\cup\{-\infty\})
-valued and
there is an element
c_{\bot}\in C
such
that for all

, the following holds:
whenever

, we have
f^{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0
, for all
\mathbf{x}\sqsubseteq_{c_{\bot}}\mathbf{y}
in
C^{\ar(f)}
.
Equivalently, if a tuple
\mathbf{y}
has non-negative value (not

),
then changing some of its coordinates to

still gives a non-negative
value.
To avoid clutter, we write
\sqsubseteq_{{}_{\bot}}
in place of
\sqsubseteq_{c_{\bot}}
,
with the choice of

implicit.

We denote by
\mbox{Max-Sol}_{\mathcal{G}}
the restriction of the VCSP to instances
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})

where
\mathbb{A}
is a
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
-valued structure with
\Gaifman(\mathbb{A})\in\mathcal{G}

Observe that every

-valued structure is a

thus

-valued

44
]
.

Example 2.4

3.1
Diagonalisability

Briceño, Bulatov, Dalmau, and Larose defined the concepts of product
structure, dismantlability, adjacency, and
link graph for relational structures
[
5
]
. In this section, we
will extend these concepts to valued structures in a natural way. In particular, our
definitions (for structures) coincide with the definitions
in
[
5
]
(for relational structures) when viewed as
\{0,\infty\}
-valued structures.

Informally, we shall consider any two positive finite values to be basically equivalent, because we will be able to bound differences between them by a constant factor; so we shall consider an increase in value significant only if it increases from zero to positive or from finite to infinite.
For a structure
\mathbb{C}
, we say an element
a\in C
is
dominated
by an element
b\in C
if we can always replace
a
with
b
: for any mapping
h\colon A\to C
(from any structure
\mathbb{A}
), assignments to
a
can be changed to assignments to
b
without increasing
\val(h)
significantly (from zero to positive or from finite to infinite).
A structure
\mathbb{C}
is
diagonalisable
if in the product
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
(defined below), one can remove dominated elements one by one until only the diagonal
\{(c,c)\mid c\in C\}
is left.
We will later see how this allows to “blend in” two different mappings
h_{1},h_{2}
from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{C}
by considering them together as a mapping to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

We now proceed with formal definitions.
Given two (valued)
\sigma

\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{D}

from
\mathbb{C}

\mathbb{D}

\psi
is a
homomorphism from
\Feas(\mathbb{C})
to
\Feas(\mathbb{D})
and from
\Opt(\mathbb{C})
to
\Opt(\mathbb{D})
(in other words, finite-valued tuples map to finite-valued tuples and zero-valued tuples map to zero-valued tuples).
It will be more convenient to consider both the
\Feas(\mathbb{C})
and
\Opt(\mathbb{C})

simultaneously. Thus with every structure
\mathbb{C}
we will associate a relational

4.1
Definition of Baker classes

A
layering
of a graph
G
is a function
\lambda\colon V(G)\to\mathbb{Z}
such that
|\lambda(u)-\lambda(v)|\leq 1
for adjacent vertices
u,v
in
G
.
That is, vertices of
G
are partitioned into
layers

\lambda^{-1}(i)
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and edges only go within one layer or between two consecutive layers.

Baker’s technique
[
1
]
relies on a layering of planar graphs such
that the subgraph induced by
\lambda^{-1}(I)
, for any interval
I
(a set of a
few consecutive integers), has bounded treewidth (the bound depending only on

|I|
), as formally stated in
Lemma

3.6
.
As one might imagine, this can be iterated: it would suffice that the subgraph induced by
\lambda^{-1}(I)
itself has such a ‘‘bounded treewidth layering’’.
Consider now the class of graphs obtained from planar graphs by adding a single
vertex, adjacent to all the others; then a layering can only have three
non-empty layers;
3
3

3

There can be a non-empty layer before the universal
vertex, a layer containing the universal vertex, and a layer after.

nevertheless, an algorithm can easily circumvent this by guessing the assignment to the single new vertex (i.e. iterating through all possibilities).

Dvořák
[
16
]
defined a
Baker class
as any class of graphs that can be dealt with in the above ways.
Informally, a class of graphs is Baker if any graph in the class can be reduced to an empty graph by a bounded number of operations: either removing a single vertex, or selecting some layering
\lambda
and continuing separately with every interval in that layering (each subgraph induced by
\lambda^{-1}(I)
for intervals
I
of at most some size).
It turns out the notion of treewidth is not necessary here, as graphs of bounded treewidth also form a Baker class.

Before we state the definition formally, let us make a few remarks.
The idea of iteratively going through “some layering” and then into “every interval” is conveniently phrased as a strategy winning a game in a
bounded
number of rounds (this will be particularly useful when we will want to state an assumption that the choices, including layerings, can be constructed efficiently).
Dvořák’s definition considers graphs with a total ordering of their vertex set. Roughly speaking, this is to restrict the definition to “monotone” strategies, where the single vertices to be deleted are decided upfront — this restriction won’t be important for us, but we will state it as in
[
16
]
(let us also remark that layerings are not restricted by the ordering).
Finally, the definition is made a bit complicated by the fact that the number of consecutive layers we may need to include in an interval may depend on how deep we go (how many iterations of the game are done).
This dependency is formalised as a function
r
below: for a fixed problem and approximation ratio the reader should think of some arbitrarily quickly increasing function
r\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
.
We now proceed with the formal definition.

Let

be the algorithm certifying that

be the integer that is guaranteed to exists for

Our algorithm starts by using

to compute the vertex ordering of

We then proceed with a recursive procedure.
The input of the procedure consists of an instance

is equal to the Gaifman graph of

\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\setminus\dom(\rho)
.
We describe the procedure and claim inductively that it computes a solution of value at most

times the optimum (among all total assignments that agree with
\rho

\dom(\rho)
).
Moreover, we claim the procedure finishes in time at most
{(2|C|\cdot M\cdot\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rceil\cdot 2^{t_{\max}})}^{t_{\max}}\cdot f_{2}(\lvert G^{\prime}\rvert\cdot 2^{t_{\max}})

Starting the recursive procedure with the instance

Let

\operatorname{Set}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\dom(\rho)\end{subarray}}f^{\mathbb{A}}(\mathbf{x})f^{\mathbb{C}}(\rho(\mathbf{x})).

Since all the assignment we consider will agree with

will be a common part of all of them.
We will not include it in the approximation we inductively claim to get;
that is, we claim the recursive procedure will output an assignment

to

where

\val(h)-\val(\rho)
and

is an optimal solution, among solutions that agree with
\rho
on
\dom(\rho)
.

In state
(\emptyset,t)
for any

, our algorithm outputs the assignment

\rho
, which is a total assignment since
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}\setminus\dom(\rho)
is empty.
It is trivially optimal (among assignments that agree with
\rho
).

In state
(G^{\prime},t)
, if
\mathtt{Algo}
selects to delete the minimum vertex
v
of

, we consider each possible assignment to
v
in
C

(G^{\prime}-v,t+1)
, the instance
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
, and the assignment
\rho
extended to
v
.
We output the best solution found this way: since we consider all possible assignments, we get a solution as close to optimum as guaranteed inductively for
t+1
.
In the final running time, the branching over all assignments to
v
in
C
will contribute at most a factor of
|C|^{t_{\max}}
.

If
\mathtt{Algo}
selects a layering
\lambda
of

, we consider each possible shift index
i\in[k]
, where
k\coloneqq(2M-1)\cdot\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rceil\cdot 2^{t}
.
This branching will contribute to the final running time a factor of at most
{(2M\cdot\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rceil\cdot 2^{t_{\max}})}^{t_{\max}}
.
For

, let

so

are intervals of length
\ell

\mathbb{Z}
;

B^{i}_{j}\coloneqq L_{jk-i}\cup\dots\cup L_{jk-i+k},

so

consists of

such intervals, of which the last one overlaps with

;

so

are disjoint for different

.
Note that we chose

to satisfy
|B_{j}|=(k+1)\ell\leq r(t)
.

For a subset of integers

(such as

or

) we henceforth abuse notation and write
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}[I]
as a shorthand for
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}[\lambda^{-1}(I)\cup\dom(\rho)]
and
{\left.\kern-1.2pth\right|_{I}}
as a shorthand for
{\left.\kern-1.2pth\right|_{\lambda^{-1}(I)\cup\dom(\rho)}}

Let

be an optimal solution to

that agrees with
\rho
.
Since

are disjoint for different

, so are the constraints of

(i.e. those contained in

(and accounted for in

).
This implies there exists a shift index

such that

\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{\prime}[O^{i^{*}}]}({\left.\kern-1.2pth^{*}\right|_{O^{i^{*}}}})\ \leq\ \frac{1}{k}\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{\prime}}(h^{*}).

We henceforth only consider the recursion branch where this holds and skip the superscript
{i^{*}}
, and write
B_{j}=B_{j}^{i^{*}}
and
O=O^{i^{*}}
.

Let
\mathbb{A}^{+}
be a
\sigma
-structure with the same domain as
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
, defined by

f^{\mathbb{A}^{+}}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}M\cdot f^{\mathbb{A}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})&\text{if $\operatorname{Set}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\lambda^{-1}(O)\cup\dom(\rho)$}\\
f^{\mathbb{A}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

so that tuples which lie within

are amplified by a factor of
M
.

For each
j
, we recurse into state

computing solutions

to

which agree with

and which by inductive assumption are almost optimal:

\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{+}[B_{j}]}(h_{j})\leq e^{\varepsilon/2^{t+1}}\cdot\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{+}[B_{j}]}({\left.\kern-1.2pth^{*}\right|_{B_{j}}}).

Therefore, summing over

, or contained in

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{+}[B_{j}]}(h_{j})

\displaystyle=e^{\varepsilon/2^{t+1}}\cdot\left(\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{\prime}}(h^{*})+(2M-1)\cdot\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{\prime}[O]}({\left.\kern-1.2pth^{*}\right|_{O}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon/2^{t+1}}\cdot(1+\frac{2M-1}{k})\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{\prime}}(h^{*})

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon/2^{t}}\cdot\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{\prime}}(h^{*}).

(the last inequality holds because we chose

).

Observe that for each

either

there is a unique

for which
\lambda(x)\in B_{j}
,
or

for which

.
In the latter case we let

denote the unique

for which

.
Let

be defined as follows

h^{\prime}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\rho(x)&\qquad\text{if $x\in\dom(\rho)$}\\
h_{j}(x)&\qquad\text{if $\lambda(x)\in B_{j}$ for a unique $j$}\\
\psi_{s(x)}(h_{j}(x),h_{j+1}(x))&\qquad\text{if $\lambda(x)\in B_{j}\cap B_{j+1}$.}\end{cases}

We claim that

is a solution to
(\mathbb{A}^{\prime},\mathbb{C})
satisfying
\val^{\rho}(h^{\prime})\leq\sum_{j}\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{+}[B_{j}]}(h_{j})
.
This will imply

\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{\prime}}(h^{\prime})\leq\sum_{j}\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{+}[B_{j}]}(h_{j})\leq e^{\varepsilon/2^{t}}\val(h^{*}),

concluding that

it is the solution we seek.

Let
(f,\mathbf{x})\in\tup_{>0}(\mathbb{A}^{\prime})
.
Note that since
\lambda
is a layering of

, there are two consecutive levels which contain all
x_{i}\in\operatorname{Set}(\mathbf{x})\setminus\dom(\rho)
.
Consider the three cases.

1.

If
\operatorname{Set}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\dom(\rho)
, then for all

,

f^{\mathbb{C}}(h^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}))=f^{\mathbb{C}}(\rho(\mathbf{x}))=f^{\mathbb{C}}(h_{j}(\mathbf{x})).

2.

Otherwise, if
\operatorname{Set}(\mathbf{x})\not\subseteq\lambda^{-1}(O)\cup\dom(\rho)
, then there is a unique
j
such that

\operatorname{Set}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\lambda^{-1}(B_{j})\cup\dom(\rho)
, and so

for each

(as some

might be in the first or the last layer of an overlap, but for those layers we have

and
\psi_{\ell}=\pi_{2}
).
Thus

3.

Else, if
\operatorname{Set}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\lambda^{-1}(O)\cup\dom(\rho)
(but
\operatorname{Set}(\mathbf{x})\not\subseteq\dom(\rho)
), then there is a unique
j
such that

\operatorname{Set}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\lambda^{-1}(B_{j}\cap B_{j+1})\cup\dom(\rho)

and there is some

Thus

h^{\prime}(x_{i})\in\{\psi_{s}(h_{j}(x_{i}),h_{j+1}(x_{i})),\,\psi_{s+1}(h_{j}(x_{i}),h_{j+1}(x_{i}))\}

for each
x_{i}\in\operatorname{Set}(\mathbf{x})\setminus\dom(\rho)
.
Moreover, since
\psi_{s}
is idempotent, we can also write for

that

h^{\prime}(x_{i})=\rho(x_{i})=\psi_{s}(\rho(x_{i}),\rho(x_{i}))=\psi_{s}(h_{j}(x_{i}),h_{j+1}(x_{i})).

Therefore, as
\psi_{t}
and
\psi_{t+1}
are adjacent, we have by definition of adjacency that

f^{\mathbb{C}}(h^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}))\leq M\cdot f^{\mathbb{C}^{2}}(h_{j}(\mathbf{x}),h_{j+1}(\mathbf{x}))=M\cdot(f^{\mathbb{C}}(h_{j}(\mathbf{x}))+f^{\mathbb{C}}(h_{j+1}(\mathbf{x}))).

This concludes the proof that
\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{\prime}}(h^{\prime})\leq\sum_{j}\val^{\rho}_{\mathbb{A}^{+}[B_{j}]}(h_{j})
and hence
h^{\prime}
is the solution we seek.

To check the running time, observe that every vertex of
G^{\prime}
is contained in
\lambda^{-1}(B_{j})
for at most two

.
Hence the total size of graphs

is at most

.
Since
f_{2}
is sub-additive, the total time required to consider those graphs in this recursive call and all sub-calls contributes a factor of at most
f_{2}(2^{t_{\max}}\lvert G^{\prime}\rvert)
.

5
Maximisation

To present our algorithm for maximisation, we first define what it means for two left-hand side structures
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
to be “close”, in a sense relevant to approximately solving
Max-Sol
.
We then show that there is a dual view which allows to certify “closeness” by a fairly concrete mapping: a distribution of partial homomorphisms.
This is then used to show that values given by Sherali-Adams linear programming relaxations of
Max-Sol
instances on
\mathbb{A}
and on
\mathbb{B}
are also close.
Since the level-
k
Sherali-Adams relaxation solves the problem exactly on instances of treewidth
\mathcal{O}\left\lparen k\right\rparen
,
it gives a PTAS for classes of structures that are “close” to bounded treewidth, as formalised by the notion of “strong pliability” below.
The proofs are similar to those in
[
44
]
; the main new contribution is finding a suitable “dual” definition (a distribution of partial homomorphisms) that makes the proofs work in the
Max-Sol
setting.
We remark we were unable to find an analogue for the
Min-Sol
setting.

Definition 5.5

In
[
44

, the PTAS results for Max-CSPs, with non-negative rational-valued

This is because the class of cliques (as a

In our case, because we consider more general right-hand side structures,

It turns out even the simplest class of dense structures, the class of cliques, is not strongly
\tw
-pliable.

Proposition C.1
.

Let
\sigma
be the signature with one symbol
f
of arity 2.
Let
\mathcal{A}=\{\mathbb{K}_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be the class of cliques, as
\sigma
-structures with
f^{\mathbb{K}_{n}}(x,y)=1
if
x\neq y
and
0
otherwise, for
x,y\in[n]
.
Then,
\mathcal{A}
is not strongly tw-pliable.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is strongly tw-pliable.
Let
\varepsilon=1/10
. There exists some
k
such that for all
n
there exists a structure
\mathbb{B}
with
\tw(\mathbb{B})\leq k-1
and

\dist_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbb{K}_{n},\mathbb{B})\leq\varepsilon
.

Let

and
\mathbb{B}
be such that
\tw(\mathbb{B})\leq k
and
\dist_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathbb{K}_{n},\mathbb{B})\leq\varepsilon
.
It is an easy exercise that for any graph
G
,
\chi(G)\leq\tw(G)+1
, and therefore

\chi(\Gaifman(\mathbb{B}))\leq k

\chi(G)
denotes the chromatic number of

Consider the following class of
Max-Sol
structures: for each
i

\mathbb{C}_{i}
be a
\sigma
-structure over the domain
[i]\cup\{c_{\bot}\}
, and let

0&\qquad\text{if $x=y=c_{\bot}$,}\\
1&\qquad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In other words, for a structure
\mathbb{A}
the instance

asks to colour the vertices of
\mathbb{A}
with

.

\maxval(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{C}_{i})=\maxval(\mathbb{B},\mathbb{C}_{\chi(\Gaifman(\mathbb{B}))})

In view of
Proposition

C.1
, one may ask whether it
is possible to obtain a PTAS on cliques via different means, not relying on our notion of
strong tw-pliability. It turns ous that this is not possible.
This follows from an easy reduction from the Maximum Clique problem.

Lemma C.2

(
[
47

)
.

It is NP-hard to approximate Maximum Clique within a factor
\mathrm{opt}^{1-\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0

That is, unless P=NP, for any
\varepsilon>0
there is no polynomial-time algorithm taking a graph
G
and an integer

as input, that can distinguish between the following cases:

•

G
has a clique of size at least
r
,

•

G
has no clique of size
r^{\varepsilon}
.

Proposition C.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{\mathbb{K}_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be the class of cliques (as defined in
Proposition

C.1
).
Then VCSP restricted to instances
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C})
where
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{A}
and
\mathbb{C}
is a
Max-Sol

Proof.

We reduce an instance
G
of Maximum Clique to a suitable VCSP instance.
Given a graph
G
on
n

\sigma
-structure
\mathbb{C}
over the domain

\displaystyle f^{\mathbb{C}}(x,y)=\begin{cases}-\infty&\qquad\text{if $x,y\in V(G)$ and $xy\not\in E(G)$, or $x=y=\star$}\\

It is clear that
