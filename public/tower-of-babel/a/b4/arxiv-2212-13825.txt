The effective Equation of State in Palatini f ⁡ ( ℛ ) f({\cal R}) cosmology
S.Camera, S.Capozziello, L.Fatibene, A.Orizzonte

Abstract
We investigate how the cosmological Equation of State can be used for scrutinizing extended theories of gravity, in particular, the Palatini f ⁡ ( ℛ ) f(\cal{R}) gravity. Specifically, the approach consists, at first, in investigating the effective Equation of State produced by a given model. Then, the inverse problem can also be considered in view of determining which models are compatible with a given effective Equation of State. We consider and solve some cases and show that, for example, power-law models are (the only models) capable of transforming barotropic Equations of State into effective barotropic ones. Moreover, the form of Equation of State is preserved (only) for f ⁡ ( ℛ ) = ℛ f({\cal R})={\cal R} , as expected. In this perspective, modified Equations of State are a feature capable of distinguishing Extended Gravity with respect to General Relativity. We investigate also quadratic and non-homogeneous effective Equations of State showing, in particular, that they contain the Starobinsky model and other ones.

2
Palatini
f({\cal R})
gravity

Let us assume a spacetime
\cal M
of dimension
\dim({\cal M})=4
.
We start from the fundamental fields
(g_{\mu\nu},\tilde{\Gamma}^{\alpha}_{\beta\mu})
, namely a Lorentzian metric and an independent, possibly non-metric, connection on spacetime
M
.

An important remark is necessary at this point.
In EPS formalism,
g_{\mu\nu}
is a Lorentzian metric on spacetime. It comes from light rays and it defines protocols to measure distances and time lapses.
The connection
\smash{\tilde{\Gamma}^{\alpha}_{\beta\mu}}
is independent of
g_{\mu\nu}
. It is here assumed to be torsionless describing the free fall of material points.
As such, we cannot really distinguish between two connections with the same geodesic trajectories just parameterized differently.
Traditionally, such two connections are called
projectively equivalent
.
This is just because parameterizations of geodesics in spacetime are not endowed with a direct physical meaning, and we are looking for trajectories in spacetime from the very beginning.

This is also a good framework to state the Equivalence Principle in its weakest form: there exists a single projective structure represented by a single connection describing the free fall of all forms of matter.

Different formulations of the Equivalence Principle need to be discussed: in general, we still can choose coordinates so that, at an event
x
, the metric
g
is constant and the connection
\tilde{\Gamma}
vanishes, though not along a whole worldline, as Fermi coordinates show to be possible in the standard GR
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
45
,
34
]
.

As said, the connection
\tilde{\Gamma}^{\alpha}_{\beta\mu}
is not assumed to be metric
a priori
. It defines curvature tensors,
\smash{\tilde{R}^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\mu\nu}}
and its Ricci contraction
\smash{\tilde{R}_{\beta\nu}=\tilde{R}^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\alpha\nu}}
, which depend on the connection only. We can also define

{\cal R}=g^{\alpha\beta}\tilde{R}_{\alpha\beta}

(1)

which depends on both he connection and the metric. It is the scalar curvature.

The dynamics is given by the Lagrangian

L=\sqrt{g}f({\cal R})+L_{m}[g,\Phi]

(2)

where
f({\cal R})
is a regular function
and the matter Lagrangian depends on the metric, the matter fields
\Phi
up to their first derivatives.
It is worth noticing that
the case
f({\cal R})=\frac{1}{2\kappa}{\cal R}
is the standard GR in Palatini formulation.

The field equations are obtained by independent variation with respect to the fields
g
and
\Gamma

\begin{cases}f^{\prime}({\cal R})\tilde{R}_{(\mu\nu)}-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}f({\cal R})g_{\mu\nu}=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}T_{\mu\nu}\cr\tilde{\nabla}_{\alpha}\left(\sqrt{g}f^{\prime}({\cal R})g^{\mu\nu}\right)=0\;,\end{cases}

(3)

where
T_{\mu\nu}
is the global stress-energy tensor and
T=g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}
is its trace. We can contract the first equation with
g^{\mu\nu}
to obtain the
master equation

f^{\prime}({\cal R}){\cal R}-2f({\cal R})=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}T\,.

(4)

We can introduce a new conformal metric
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=\varphi\>g_{\mu\nu}
to solve the second equation.
By fixing the conformal factor
\varphi=\chi\>f^{\prime}({\cal R})
for any constant
\chi
, the second equation becomes

\tilde{\nabla}_{\alpha}\left(\sqrt{\tilde{g}}\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}\right)=0\quad\iff\quad\tilde{\Gamma}^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}=\{\tilde{g}\}^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}\,.

(5)

The function
f({\cal R})
can be chosen so that the master equation has a discrete set of zeros. For example, any analytic function, different from
f({\cal R})=c{\cal R}^{2}
, will do the job, although one can also consider less regular cases, still having a discrete set of zeros.
Whenever the master equation has a discrete set of zeros, one can express
{\cal R}={\cal R}(T)
as a function of matter content, in a discrete set of branches and the theory can be solved on each branch.
This allows, in field equations, solutions for curvature or matter, depending on what we want to represent.

In vacuum, for
T=0
,
{\cal R}
is constant and the function
f({\cal R})
can be traded with a cosmological term in field equations. This is the core of the universality theorem, saying that all Palatini
f({\cal R})
models, in vacuum, behave as the standard Einstein theory with a cosmological constant with a discrete spectrum dictated by the zeros
{\cal R}={\cal R}_{i}(0)
of the master equation
[
46
]
.

In view of the second field equation, the connection
\tilde{\Gamma}^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}
is then metric on-shell, namely along solutions. Since the two metrics are conformal, we have

\displaystyle\{\tilde{g}\}^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}=

\displaystyle\{g\}^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\left(g^{\alpha\epsilon}g_{\mu\nu}-2\delta^{\alpha}_{(\mu}\delta^{\epsilon}_{\nu)}\right){}^{\ast}\nabla_{\epsilon}\ln\varphi=:\{g\}^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}+K^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}

(6)

\displaystyle\tilde{R}^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\mu\nu}=

\displaystyle R^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\mu\nu}+\nabla_{\mu}K^{\alpha}_{\beta\nu}-\nabla_{\nu}K^{\alpha}_{\beta\mu}+K^{\alpha}_{\epsilon\mu}K^{\epsilon}_{\beta\nu}-K^{\alpha}_{\epsilon\nu}K^{\epsilon}_{\beta\mu}

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{\beta\nu}=

\displaystyle R_{\beta\nu}-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}g_{\beta\nu}\Box\ln\varphi-\nabla_{\beta\nu}\ln\varphi-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}g_{\beta\nu}{}^{\ast}\nabla_{\alpha}\ln\varphi{}^{\ast}\nabla^{\alpha}\ln\varphi+

\displaystyle+\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}{}^{\ast}\nabla_{\beta}\ln\varphi{}^{\ast}\nabla_{\nu}\ln\varphi

\displaystyle{\cal R}=

\displaystyle\varphi\>\tilde{R}=R-3\Box\ln\varphi-\hbox{$\frac{3}{2}$}{}^{\ast}\nabla_{\alpha}\ln\varphi{}^{\ast}\nabla^{\alpha}\ln\varphi

where
{}^{\ast}\nabla_{\epsilon}
denotes the covariant derivative in the cases where it does not depend on any connection.
Here, covariant derivatives
\nabla_{\alpha}
are with respect to the metric
g
, the indices in

{}^{\ast}\nabla^{\alpha}\ln\varphi=g^{\alpha\beta}\,{}^{\ast}\nabla_{\beta}\ln\varphi

are raised by the metric
g
. The conformal factor is assumed to be positive within the region of interest
U\subset{\cal M}
(which sets some constraint on how to fix the constant
\chi
),
\nabla_{\beta\nu}\ln\varphi=\nabla_{\beta}\nabla_{\nu}\ln\varphi
is the second covariant derivative, which is symmetric on scalar 0-forms as
\ln\varphi
, and
\Box\ln\varphi=g^{\beta\nu}\,\nabla_{\beta\nu}\ln\varphi
is the d’Alembert operator.

Let us stress that, as for any field equation linear in the Ricci tensor, the first field equation can be recast as follows, once one takes into account the solution of the second equation,

\tilde{R}_{\mu\nu}-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\tilde{R}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{f^{\prime}({\cal R})}\left(T_{\mu\nu}+\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}f({\cal R})g_{\mu\nu}\right)-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}{\cal R}g_{\mu\nu}=:\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}\;.

(7)

The relation above is the Einstein tensor equation with respect to the metric
\tilde{g}
with an
effective stress-energy tensor

\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}
depending on the real
stress-energy tensor

T_{\mu\nu}
and the conformal factor
\varphi
.

An important remark is in order at this point.
As a matter of fact, the physical core of Palatini
f({\cal R})
gravity, in the EPS perspective, is the possible conformal mismatch between the metric
g
, describing distances, and the metric
\tilde{g}
, describing free fall. In Palatini
f({\cal R})
gravity, one does not assume they are the same metric as one does in standard GR, but it is left to dynamics to determine their mutual relation
(which means that also in standard GR, one does not need to assume it but it is enforced by the choice of the Hilbert-Einstein action).

Let us comment that protocols to measure times and distances are today set in a quantum framework, which, by the way, is coherent with Special Relativity, while it is yet not known if it is coherent with general covariance principle.
On the other hand, the free fall of a material point is a genuinely GR issue, and nobody can check if it is coherent with a quantum framework yet.
However, there are some checks which tell us that the conformal factor does not change much within the Solar System. These tests are much harder to support experimentally at galactic or cosmological scales.

Of course, we cannot exclude that, eventually, it will turn out that standard GR is the right theory from the beginning and the conformal factor will actually be constant, that is why standard GR is regarded as a special Palatini
f({\cal R})
gravity, just for a linear
f({\cal R})=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2\kappa}$}{\cal R}+\Lambda
.

Since the field equation (
7
) is in the Einstein form, then the left-hand side obeys the contracted Bianchi identities. Thus,
\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}
is conserved with respect to
\tilde{g}
, namely
\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}\tilde{T}^{\mu\nu}=0
, where
\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}
denotes the covariant derivative with respect to
\tilde{g}
and, of course, indices of
\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}
are raised by
\tilde{g}
.

Because of this form of the field equations, using the metric
\tilde{g}
is called the
Einstein frame(work)
, while using the metric
g
is called the
Jordan frame
.
Endless discussions have been spent to discuss whether the true physical metric on
\cal M
is,
g
or
\tilde{g}
, regarding the other one as a mathematical trick
[
38
]
.

It is worth noticing that,
in EPS formulation, there is no reason to believe that one should do everything with one metric only. As a matter of fact, EPS point of view is that quantum mechanics and the local non-gravitational metric
g
may be conformal but not equal to the metric
\tilde{g}
, which describe the free fall of macroscopic material points.

Of course, none of these interpretations is relevant to the formulae we write down here; they just explain the names we use to denotes objects.
When we call
\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}
the
effective stress-energy tensor
, we exactly mean that it encodes gravitational effects, not quantum effects.
If we set up quantum experiments to test matter composition, we expect to see
T_{\mu\nu}
, not
\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}
. If it can sound strange that we never realized the conformal mismatch between the two frameworks, actually this is precisely the case since we are not able to resolve GR at quantum level, yet. In fact, only Newtonian gravity can be successfully tested on quantum systems
[
53
]
. This goals, at the moment, is quite far to be achieved for GR and the other relativistic theories of gravity
[
54
]
.

If it is there, the discrepancy will sooner or later appear clearly. Nowadays one can reasonably expect to see effects at galactic or cosmological scales, which are, by the way, exactly the scales at which dark sources become relevant while they are not yet detected at laboratory scales.

By the way, arguing that Einstein frame is characterized by Einstein-like field equations is just wrong. Of course, field equations can be
also
recast as Einstein equations for
g
; namely as

\displaystyle R_{\beta\nu}-

\displaystyle\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}Rg_{\beta\nu}=\frac{1}{2f^{\prime}({\cal R})}\left(T_{\beta\nu}+f({\cal R})g_{\beta\nu}\right)+\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}g_{\beta\nu}\Box\ln\varphi+\nabla_{\beta\nu}\ln\varphi+

(8)

\displaystyle+\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}g_{\beta\nu}\nabla_{\alpha}\ln\varphi\nabla^{\alpha}\ln\varphi-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\nabla_{\beta}\ln\varphi\nabla_{\nu}\ln\varphi-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}Rg_{\beta\nu}=:\hat{T}_{\beta\nu}\;.

Also, in this case,
\hat{T}_{\beta\nu}
is called the
Jordan stress-energy tensor
, it is conserved with respect to the metric
g
, namely
\nabla_{\beta}\hat{T}^{\beta\nu}=0
(indices raised by
g
, here).
We shall not use this below, but it just shows that it is not true that field equations in Jordan frame are not in Einstein form. Distinguishing Jordan and Einstein frames needs to be done at the level of action functionals, not at the level of field equations
[
55
,
56
]
.

One can finally show that the real stress-energy tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
is conserved with respect to
g
, namely
\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0
since it comes as a variational derivative of a covariant matter Lagrangian
L_{m}[g,\Phi]
.

Although one can show this directly
[
57
]
, we can also have a direct hint by the fact that
T_{\mu\nu}
does not depend on the gravitational Lagrangian we couple to it. Clearly
T_{\mu\nu}
is conserved in standard GR.

3
Palatini
f({\cal R})
cosmology

After the above discussion on Palatini
f({\cal R})
gravity, we want to apply it to cosmology, namely to spatially flat, homogeneous and isotropic Friedman-Le maître-Robertson Walker (FLRW) cosmology.
Let us define

g=-c^{2}dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)\,\left(\frac{dr^{2}}{1-k\,r^{2}}+r^{2}\,\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}\right)\right)\;,

(9)

where
g
is the FLRW metric, defined after the line element
ds^{2}=g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}
.
We choose to normalize the
scale factor

a_{o}=a(t_{o})=1
to unity today at time
t_{o}
.
In this normalization, the scale factor
a(t)
is assumed dimensionless with
[k]=L^{-2}
, and
[r]=[ct]=L
.
It is worth saying that here we have two metrics and, in principle, we should declare which one is required to be spatially homogeneous and isotropic.

In view of the master equation, we can see that the conformal factor
\varphi
is a function of time
t
only.
Suppose
g
is in FLRW form in coordinates
(t,r,\theta,\phi)
, then we have

\displaystyle\tilde{g}=\varphi\>g=

\displaystyle-c^{2}\varphi\>dt^{2}+\varphi\>a^{2}(t)\left(\frac{dr^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}+r^{2}\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\theta)d\phi^{2}\right)\right)=

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c^{2}d\tilde{t}^{2}+\tilde{a}^{2}(\tilde{t})\left(\frac{dr^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}+r^{2}\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\theta)d\phi^{2}\right)\right)

where we set
d\tilde{t}=\sqrt{\varphi(t)}\>dt
and
\tilde{a}(\tilde{t})=\sqrt{\varphi(t)}\>a(t)
.
Hence
\tilde{g}
is in FLRW form in coordinates
(\tilde{t},r,\theta,\phi)
, therefore it is spatially homogeneous and isotropic.

If we restrict to a conformal factor such that
\varphi(t_{o})=1
, the conformal transformation preserves the normalization of the scale factor, namely also
\tilde{a}(\>\tilde{t}_{o})=1
.

The second step is that being the metric in FLRW form, it means that the corresponding Einstein tensor, as well as the Jordan tensor

J_{\mu\nu}=f^{\prime}({\cal R})\tilde{R}_{\mu\nu}-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}f({\cal R})g_{\mu\nu},

(11)

is a
perfect fluid stress-energy tensor
. In other words, there exist two functions of time,

and

, and a future-directed timelike unit-vector

where

and

are called the

and

of the fluid.
On the other hand,
\tilde{\rho}
and
\tilde{p}
are the

energy-density and pressure.
Immediately we have

and
\tilde{u}_{\mu}=\sqrt{\varphi}\>u_{\mu}
, so that
g(u,u)=-1=\tilde{g}(\tilde{u},\tilde{u})
.

In view of the relation (
7
) between stress-energy tensors, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}=

\displaystyle c^{2}\varphi\>(\tilde{\rho}+\tilde{p})\>u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+\varphi\>\tilde{p}\>g_{\mu\nu}=

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbox{$\frac{c^{2}}{f^{\prime}}$}(\rho+p)\>u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+\left(\hbox{$\frac{p}{f^{\prime}}$}+\hbox{$\frac{f}{2f^{\prime}}$}-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}{\cal R}\right)g_{\mu\nu}

from which, we get the relations between real and effective sources, namely

\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\>\tilde{p}=p+\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\left(f-f^{\prime}{\cal R}\right)\qquad\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\tilde{\rho}=\rho-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\left(f-f^{\prime}{\cal R}\right)

On each branch of the theory, we have

and

, which expresses also

and

as a function of

and

In other words we have

As we supply the EoS,

, for effective matter.

Let us stress that the change of EoS, when changing from Jordan to Einstein frame, can be considered as the only effect introduced by the Palatini
f({\cal R})
cosmology. It is worth discussing how this change can occur and how it can put constraints on related models to be matched with observations.

4
Selecting Palatini
f({\cal R})
cosmologies from EoS

Suppose we measure
\tilde{H}(\tilde{z})
that is equivalent, according to standard cosmology, to know the (total) effective density
\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{a})
.
Then, by conservation, we have
\tilde{p}=-\left(\tilde{a}\>\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}+\hbox{$\frac{1}{3}$}\tilde{\rho}\right)
.
Once we have
\left(\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{a}),\tilde{p}(\tilde{a})\right)
, that is equivalent to have the effective EoS in the implicit form
\tilde{F}(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{p})=0
,
we can investigate which
f({\cal R})
models reproduce the observed effective behaviour. See also
[
51
]
for a general discussion.

For example, it is possible to show that deviations from the barotropic EoS are signals of dynamics departing from power-law models. On the other hand, if we have a quadratic EoS, this is strong constraint on possible dynamics one can consider. In other words, measuring
\tilde{H}(\tilde{z})
gives a lot of information on the sources related to the EoS.

Let us start by reviewing some particular models. After we shall consider the inverse problem, i.e. how to constrain
f({\cal R})
cosmologies by real and effective EoS.

The Starobinsky model

Let us start from the Starobinsky model, namely
f({\cal R})=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2\kappa}$}\left({\cal R}+\epsilon{\cal R}^{2}\right)
.
The master equation in this case is

-{\cal R}={\cal R}-2{\cal R}=\kappa T=\kappa(3p-\rho)\quad\Rightarrow{\cal R}=\kappa(\rho-3p)

(16)

and consequently

f=\hbox{$\frac{\kappa(\rho-3p)}{2\kappa}$}\left(1+\kappa\epsilon(\rho-3p)\right)\qquad f^{\prime}=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2\kappa}$}\left(1+2\kappa\epsilon(\rho-3p)\right)

(17)

If the real matter is barotropic, namely the real EoS is
p=w\rho
, we have the effective sources as

\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\>\tilde{p}=w\rho-\hbox{$\frac{\kappa\epsilon}{4}$}(1-3w)^{2}\rho^{2}\qquad\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\tilde{\rho}=\rho+\hbox{$\frac{\kappa\epsilon}{4}$}(1-3w)^{2}\rho^{2}

(18)

These are already effective EoS in a parameterized form, with
\rho
as a parameter.
This already shows that effective matter is not even barotropic, in fact we have

\tilde{p}=\frac{4w-\kappa\epsilon(1-3w)^{2}\rho}{4+\kappa\epsilon(1-3w)^{2}\rho}\>\tilde{\rho}=\tilde{w}(\rho)\>\tilde{\rho}

(19)

We see that the effective EoS is not barotropic since
\tilde{w}
is not constant (unless one expands around a configuration or
w=\hbox{$\frac{1}{3}$}
). Albeit, in a sense, this is an exact EoS to the extent in which the real EoS is assumed to be exact. From an observational point of view, the dark energy EoS
w_{\mathrm{DE}}(z)
, which is aimed to be measured by several cosmological surveys, cannot be exactly barotropic if we want to describe the evolution of cosmic fluid where the global EoS is not always strictly constant.

We can also eliminate the parameter
\rho
to have the effective EoS in implicit form as
\tilde{F}(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{p})=0
. In fact, starting from (
18
), we can divide and solve for
\rho

\tilde{p}=\frac{4w-\kappa\epsilon(1-3w)^{2}\rho}{4+\kappa\epsilon(1-3w)^{2}\rho}\>\tilde{\rho}\qquad\Rightarrow\rho=\frac{4}{\kappa\epsilon(1-3w)^{2}}\frac{w\tilde{\rho}-\tilde{p}}{\tilde{p}+\tilde{\rho}}\;.

(20)

Summing and eliminating
\rho
, we have

\left((1+5w)\tilde{\rho}-(7+3w)\tilde{p}\right)^{2}=\hbox{$\frac{16\kappa}{\chi\epsilon}$}(1+w)(w\tilde{\rho}-\tilde{p})\;,

(21)

which is the effective EoS.

Power-law models

Let us discuss now power-law models,
f({\cal R})=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2\kappa}$}{\cal R}^{n}
, with
n\not=2
.
Also in this case, the master equation is particularly simple

(n-2){\cal R}^{n}=n{\cal R}^{n}-2{\cal R}^{n}=\kappa T=\kappa(3p-\rho)\;,\quad\Rightarrow{\cal R}^{n}=\hbox{$\frac{\kappa}{2-n}$}(\rho-3p)

(22)

and consequently

f=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2(2-n)}$}(\rho-3p)\,,\qquad\qquad f^{\prime}=\hbox{$\frac{n}{2\kappa}$}\left(\hbox{$\frac{\kappa}{2-n}$}\right)^{\hbox{$\frac{n-1}{n}$}}\left(\rho-3p\right)^{\hbox{$\frac{n-1}{n}$}}\;.

(23)

If the real matter is barotropic, namely the real EoS is
p=w\rho
, we have effective sources as

\displaystyle\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\>\tilde{p}=

\displaystyle\hbox{$\frac{5w+1-wn-n}{8-4n}$}\rho\qquad\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\tilde{\rho}=\hbox{$\frac{9-3n-3wn-3w}{8-4n}$}\rho

(24)

\displaystyle\tilde{p}=\frac{5w+1-wn-n}{9-3n-3wn-3w}\>\tilde{\rho}=\tilde{w}\>\tilde{\rho}\;.

This means that, in the case of power-law models, a barotropic EoS is mapped in a barotropic one and vice versa.

The inverse problem

Suppose we want to go the other way around, that is we measure an EoS and we want to reconstruct the related
f({\cal R})
cosmological model.

For the sake of simplicity, let us first consider a barotropic real EoS,
p=w\>\rho
, and a different barotropic effective EoS,
\tilde{p}=\tilde{w}\>\tilde{\rho}
.
By specializing to this case we have the relation between real and effective sources as well as the master equation

\begin{cases}\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\>\tilde{w}\>\tilde{\rho}=w\>\rho+\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\left(f-f^{\prime}{\cal R}\right)\cr\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\tilde{\rho}=\rho-\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\left(f-f^{\prime}{\cal R}\right)\cr f^{\prime}{\cal R}-2f=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(3w-1)\rho\end{cases}\quad\Rightarrow\frac{(3\tilde{w}-1)(w+1)}{3w\tilde{w}-5w+7\tilde{w}-1}\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}=\frac{1}{{\cal R}}

(25)

which integrates to

f({\cal R})=c{\cal R}^{\hbox{$\frac{3w\tilde{w}-5w+7\tilde{w}-1}{(3\tilde{w}-1)(w+1)}$}}=:c{\cal R}^{n}\;.

(26)

This result shows that models with
f({\cal R})=c{\cal R}^{n}
send real barotropic sources in (different) effective barotropic sources.
If one wants to preserve the barotropic index, namely having
\tilde{w}=w
that is possible if and only if

\hbox{$\frac{3w^{2}+2w-1}{3w^{2}+2w-1}$}=n=1\;.

(27)

Therefore, standard GR is the only model in which effective and real EoS coincide.

Furthermore, it is worth noticing that
w=\hbox{$\frac{1}{3}$}=\tilde{w}
, as well as
w=-1=\tilde{w}
, are solutions for any
n
so that, given both EoS, the function
f({\cal R})
is not, in general, uniquely determined by a single correspondence of EoS, but rather by the map which transforms any real EoS in the corresponding effective EoS.

If we had a reason to believe in a specific model, the direct problem would be fine.
It makes predictions about the gravitational sector which may be confirmed or disproved.
But currently we do not.
If we do not consider dark sources, we have no (or very little) reasons to go beyond the standard GR.
Also considering dark sources, one can go for modifying the matter or the gravitational sector.
Preferring to modify the gravitational sector, the motivation remains purely phenomenological: one should set up a model capable of reproducing an observed phenomenology.

Solving the equation for
f({\cal R})
, in general, is not easy but it gives us a different information about the model rather than the direct problem. However, there are more cases in which it can be done.

The linear effective EoS

Let us consider an effective EoS like

\alpha\>\tilde{p}+\beta\>\tilde{\rho}=\gamma

(28)

which is defined up to a factor that we can fix without changing the EoS itself.
Thus with no loss of generality, we can set
\gamma=1
to parameterized all non-barotropic cases.
This is the simplest modification of barotropic EoS and it allows non-zero pressure in vacuum.
We also assume barotropic real EoS, i.e.
p=w\rho
.
Then we have

\begin{cases}f^{\prime}{\cal R}-2f=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(3w-1)\rho\quad\Rightarrow\rho=\hbox{$\frac{2}{3w-1}$}f^{\prime}{\cal R}-\hbox{$\frac{4}{3w-1}$}f\cr\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\alpha\>\tilde{p}=\hbox{$\frac{\alpha(w+1)}{2(3w-1)}$}f^{\prime}{\cal R}-\hbox{$\frac{\alpha(5w+1)}{2(3w-1)}$}f\cr\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\beta\>\tilde{\rho}=\hbox{$\frac{3\beta(w+1)}{2(3w-1)}$}f^{\prime}{\cal R}-\hbox{$\frac{\beta(3w+7)}{2(3w-1)}$}f\end{cases}

(29)

from which we obtain

2\chi(3w-1)(f^{\prime})^{2}=\left(\alpha+3\beta\right)(w+1)f^{\prime}{\cal R}-\left(\alpha(5w+1)+\beta(3w+7)\right)f\,.

(30)

We can now perform the transformation
f({\cal R})=\Lambda+\alpha{\cal R}+{\cal R}^{2}F({\cal R})
(hence
f^{\prime}=\alpha+2{\cal R}F+{\cal R}^{2}F^{\prime}
) so that the condition becomes

\displaystyle 2\chi(3w-1)(F^{\prime})^{2}{\cal R}^{4}+\left(8\chi(3w-1)F-(w+1)(\alpha+3\beta)\right)F^{\prime}{\cal R}^{3}+

(31)

\displaystyle+\left(\chi(8F^{2}+4cF^{\prime})+(\alpha-\beta)F\right)(3w-1){\cal R}^{2}+

\displaystyle+\left(8\chi(3w-1)F+4\alpha w+4\beta\right)c{\cal R}+

\displaystyle+\left((5\alpha+3\beta)w+\alpha+7\beta)\Lambda+2\chi c^{2}(3w-1)\right)=0

Assuming the analytic function
F({\cal R})=a_{0}+a_{1}{\cal R}+\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}a_{2}{\cal R}^{2}+\dots
and for generic values of the parameters, we get

\Lambda=\frac{-2\chi c^{2}(3w-1)}{(5w+1)\alpha+(3w+7)\beta}\qquad a_{0}=-\frac{\alpha w+\beta}{2\chi(3w-1)}\qquad\dots

(32)

giving rise to a recurrence formula to compute the following coefficients from the previous ones.
Whether this formal series truncates to a polynomial depends on specific values of
\alpha,\beta,w
.

The quadratic effective EoS

Let us now consider an effective EoS like

(\alpha\>\tilde{p}+\beta\>\tilde{\rho})^{2}=\lambda\tilde{p}+\gamma\>\tilde{\rho}\,.

(33)

As a special case, we get the Starobinsky model for

\alpha=-3w-7\qquad\beta=5w+1\qquad\lambda=-\frac{16\kappa(1+w)}{\chi\epsilon}\qquad\gamma=\frac{16\kappa(1+w)w}{\chi\epsilon}\,.

(34)

Let us change coordinates
(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{p})\mapsto(\tilde{e},\tilde{p})
by setting
\tilde{e}=\alpha\>\tilde{p}+\beta\>\tilde{\rho}

(hence
\tilde{\rho}=\hbox{$\frac{1}{\beta}$}\tilde{e}-\hbox{$\frac{\alpha}{\beta}$}\>\tilde{p}
)
so that the effective EoS becomes

\beta\tilde{e}^{2}=\beta\lambda\tilde{p}+\gamma\tilde{e}-\alpha\gamma\>\tilde{p}\quad\Rightarrow(\beta\lambda-\gamma\alpha)\>\tilde{p}=\beta\tilde{e}^{2}-\gamma\tilde{e}\;.

(35)

Then we can consider the equations for the new pressure and density together the master equation and use these to write a differential equation for the function
f({\cal R})
alone.
We proceed as above and, from the master equation, we get

f^{\prime}{\cal R}-2f=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(3w-1)\rho\quad\Rightarrow\rho=\hbox{$\frac{2}{3w-1}$}f^{\prime}{\cal R}-\hbox{$\frac{4}{3w-1}$}f

(36)

Then we make a linear combination of the equations for the effective sources obtaining

\displaystyle\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\>\tilde{e}=

\displaystyle\left(\alpha w+\beta\right)\rho+\hbox{$\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}$}\left(f-f^{\prime}{\cal R}\right)=

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbox{$\frac{(\alpha+3\beta)(w+1)}{2(3w-1)}$}f^{\prime}{\cal R}-\hbox{$\frac{\alpha(5w+1)+\beta(3w+7)}{2(3w-1)}$}f

while from the equation for the effective pressure, using the effective EoS in the form (
35
), we have

\displaystyle\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}(\beta\tilde{e}^{2}-\gamma\tilde{e})=

\displaystyle\hbox{$\frac{2w(\beta\lambda-\gamma\alpha)}{3w-1}$}\left(f^{\prime}{\cal R}-2f\right)+\hbox{$\frac{\beta\lambda-\gamma\alpha}{2}$}\left(f-f^{\prime}{\cal R}\right)=

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbox{$\frac{(\beta\lambda-\gamma\alpha)(w+1)}{2(3w-1)}$}f^{\prime}{\cal R}+\hbox{$\frac{(\gamma\alpha-\beta\lambda)(5w+1)}{2(3w-1)}$}f\,,

from which we get

\displaystyle\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\tilde{e}^{2}=\hbox{$\frac{(3\gamma+\lambda)(w+1)}{2(3w-1)}$}f^{\prime}{\cal R}-\hbox{$\frac{\lambda(5w+1)+\gamma(3w+7)}{2(3w-1)}$}f\,,

(39)

and then

\displaystyle\left(\hbox{$\frac{(\alpha+3\beta)(w+1)}{2(3w-1)}$}f^{\prime}{\cal R}-\hbox{$\frac{\alpha(5w+1)+\beta(3w+7)}{2(3w-1)}$}f\right)^{2}=

(40)

\displaystyle=\chi(f^{\prime})^{2}\>\left(\hbox{$\frac{(3\gamma+\lambda)(w+1)}{2(3w-1)}$}f^{\prime}{\cal R}-\hbox{$\frac{\lambda(5w+1)+\gamma(3w+7)}{2(3w-1)}$}f\right)\,.

The last equation is non-linear and already quite difficult to study, although the effective EoS we selected is not very complicated.
However, it contains the information we are looking for, being a differential equation for the function
f({\cal R})
.
It provides us with all possible Palatini
f({\cal R})
models reproducing the effective EoS we started from.
It is no surprising that among solutions we find
f({\cal R})=a_{1}{\cal R}+a_{2}{\cal R}^{2}
. In general, if we expand the equation as a polynomial in
{\cal R}
, it is possible to get equations to link
(a_{1},a_{2},\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\lambda,w)
.

What we would like to do now is providing EoS parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\lambda,w
and computing
a_{1},a_{2}
to determine
f
.
However, in this case, we already see that we have a 4-degree polynomial (with no constant term), thus we get 4 equations.
With 2 unknowns, we have little hope to determine them uniquely.
In fact we can define
\epsilon=\epsilon(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\lambda,w)
but we are left with 3 equations from which we cannot solve for
\kappa
, which is left undetermined, i.e. they are constraints for
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\lambda,w
.

However, in this case we can try and solve for
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\lambda
and we obtain

\alpha=-(3w+7)c\quad\beta=(5w+1)c\quad\gamma=\hbox{$\frac{16\kappa}{\chi\epsilon}$}w(w+1)c^{2}\quad\lambda=-\hbox{$\frac{16\kappa}{\chi\epsilon}$}(w+1)c^{2}

(41)

where we set
a_{1}=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2\kappa}$}
and
a_{2}=\epsilon
.
That is exactly the Starobinsky model for any value of
c
.

However, this shows precisely that Starobinsky models account for a 1-parameter family of models with effective EoS as

(\alpha\>\tilde{p}+\beta\>\tilde{\rho})^{2}=\lambda\tilde{p}+\gamma\>\tilde{\rho}

(42)

which, however, are a 3-parameters family. Thus we expect other models producing the same type of effective EoS.

In fact, Starobinsky model is not the only model producing effective EoS in the form (
33
).
As a matter of fact, if we assume a function

f({\cal R})=\frac{1}{2\kappa}\frac{({\cal R}+3\Lambda)^{3}}{27\Lambda^{2}}

(43)

and we repeat the above argument, we see that the
effective EoS is in the form (
33
) with

\alpha=-3c\qquad\beta=c\qquad\gamma=-\frac{8\kappa(w-1)\Lambda}{3\chi(w+1)}c^{2}\qquad\lambda=-\frac{8\kappa(3w+1)\Lambda}{3\chi(w+1)}c^{2}

(44)

Then we do not get more up to a polynomial of degree 10.

Finally, we also have

f({\cal R})=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2\kappa}$}\left(\Lambda+{\cal R}\right)

(45)

with effective EoS

\alpha=c\qquad\beta=-wc\qquad\gamma=-\frac{\kappa w(w+1)\Lambda}{\chi}c^{2}\qquad\lambda=\frac{\kappa(w+1)\Lambda}{\chi}c^{2}\,.

(46)

However, even if this is in the form (
33
), one can simplify it to a linear effective EoS as

\tilde{p}-w\tilde{\rho}=\frac{\kappa(w+1)\Lambda}{\chi}

(47)

although generically not homogeneous as a barotropic law.
The same happens for the model with

f({\cal R})=\hbox{$\frac{1}{2\kappa}$}\left(2\Lambda+{\cal R}\right)^{2}\,,

(48)

which is clearly a generalisation of the Starobinsky one.

5
Conclusions and perspectives

Gravitational theories have a notoriously tricky relation with observations. It is well known that, by defining observables, as generally invariant quantities
purely metric covariant theories, in vacuum, has no observables at all
[
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
,
62
]
.
Gravitational observables arise only when matter is adopted to probe theories. Specifically,
in cosmology, we need to understand how observable quantities relate to the theoretical framework, like, for example, that given by the
Cosmological Principle defining some preferred observers. Certainly, in cosmology, we have many constraints on what we
can
measure, making it a coarse grained approach.

In this perspective, surveys like
Euclid
can provide information about the (effective) EoS of dark sources.
Thus one should investigate what information we can achieve within theories of gravity like Extended Gravity or Modified Gravity.

Here, we showed that, with the exception of standard GR, any Palatini
f({\cal R})
model comes with a non-trivial map between real and effective EoS,
in the sense that standard GR is the only theory which preserves EoS.

If we assume barotopic real EoS, power-law models are the only models in which effective EoS are also barotropic, with the exception of
f({\cal R})=c{\cal R}^{2}
which is degenerate
since, for it, the master equation is identically satisfied.

When we take non-barotropic effective EoS, we can also study which models are allowed.
This is done by starting from the definition of effective pressure and density together with the master equation and turn this into a (non-linear) differential equation for the model function
f({\cal R})
.
This function is not easy to be solved even in the simplest cases.
However, if we expand in series of
f({\cal R})
, we can compute approximations of general solutions.

Generically, we can solve the conditions for
f^{\prime}
.
For example, we can solve Eq.(
30
) on two different branches to obtain
f^{\prime}=A({\cal R},f)
which, together with the trivial equation
{\cal R}^{\prime}=1
,
can be considered as a solution of the dynamical system defined by the vector field

X=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\cal R}}+A({\cal R},f)\frac{\partial}{\partial f}

(49)

which shows that, at least locally, one has solutions for
f({\cal R})
.

Here we considered simple toy models with a single real barotropic fluid and still we obtained effective EoS which are non-trivial.
Future investigations have to be devoted to extend to general real EoS and mixtures.
Having non-linear EoS is not really difficult to be dealt with. See, for example,
[
20
,
21
]
.
After all, EoS are themselves an approximation of matter equations, which are certainly valid in a regime and extending it out of this domain is problematic.
For example, ordinary matter is described as dust today since one can neglect non-gravitational interactions among far away galaxies,
but of course such an assumption is at least questionable in the early universe.

Moreover, we already showed that when one allows mixtures, the cosmic evolution is determined by the
total abundance

\Omega(a)
.
However, there are different mixtures producing the same function
\Omega(a)
.
Still we need to define results which hold for any
f({\cal R})
to realize if Palatini approach can be physically sound.

Let us consider the conjecture stating that given an EoS, one can find
f({\cal R})
which produce it.
We already proved that different models can sends a single real EoS to a single effective EoS.
We still have no evidences that two correspondences between real and effective EoS cannot be reproduced by different models.
If this is the case, namely, we can have different models which cannot be distinguished in cosmology, which is very interesting.

If there are specific effective EoS that cannot be produced at all would be also very interesting since it would allow to disprove all Palatini
f({\cal R})
models at a time.
Finally, as we said, if there is a one-to-one correspondence between
f({\cal R})
and a map between real and effective EoS
would be a proof which could be tested, in principle, by observations.
Clearly the approach can work also for other theories of gravity like teleparallel or symmetric teleparallel ones
[
18
,
63
]
. This will the the topic of forthcoming studies.
