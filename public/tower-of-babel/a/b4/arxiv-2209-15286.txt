A refined first-order expansion formula in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} : Application to interpolation and finite element error estimates
Joël Chaskalovic, Franck Assous

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to derive a refined first-order expansion formula in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , the goal being to get an optimal reduced remainder, compared to the one obtained by usual Taylor’s formula. For a given function, the formula we derived is obtained by introducing a linear combination of the first derivatives, computed at n + 1 n+1 equally spaced points. We show how this formula can be applied to two important applications: the interpolation error and the finite elements error estimates. In both cases, we illustrate under which conditions a significant improvement of the errors can be obtained, namely how the use of the refined expansion can reduce the upper bound of error estimates.



keywords
: Taylor’s theorem, interpolation error estimates, finite element, approximation error estimates.

Introduction

Evaluating and improving the accuracy of approximation are very difficult problems in numerical analysis. In this article, we are interested in describing a new point of view on approaching the topic. In particular, we are concerned with the difficulty of accurately determining the error estimate of numerical methods applied to partial differential equations.

From a mathematical point of view, the origin of this problem
[
2
]
can be found in Rolle’s theorem, and therefore, in Lagrange and Taylor’s theorems
[
3
]
: this comes from the existence of a non unique unknown point which appears in the remainder of Taylor’s expansion, as the heritage of Rolle’s theorem, leading to a kind of “uncertainty”.

Now, let us consider more specifically finite element problems. Basically because of this uncertainty, most of the results focus on the asymptotic behavior of the error estimates which strongly depends on the interpolation error (see for example
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
). Indeed, error estimates generally consider, for a given norm, the asymptotic behavior of the difference between the exact and the approximate solution, as the mesh size
h
tends to zero (cf.
[
24
]
).

However, several approaches have been proposed to investigate how to improve the accuracy of approximation. For example, in
the framework of numerical integration, we refer the reader to
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
or
[
20
]
, and references therein. From another point of view, due to the lack of information, heuristic methods were considered, basically based on a probabilistic approach, see for instance
[
1
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
or
[
9
]
and
[
11
]
. This allows to compare different numerical methods, and more precisely finite element, for a given fixed mesh size, see
[
12
]
-
[
13
]
.

Nevertheless, Taylor’s formula introduces an unknown point preventing to precisely determine the interpolation error, and consequently the approximation error of a given numerical method. Hence, the question still remains whether the error upper bounds are as small as possible. Therefore, we focus in this article on the values of the numerical constants which appear in such estimates, aiming to reduce them as much as possible.

To this end, we propose to refine the first order Taylor approximation formula of a function
f
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, at a given point
a
, using
f(a)
and its derivative. To do this, we consider more known values of the first order derivative on intermediary equidistant points between
a
and another given point
b
. These values of the first order derivative are conveniently weighted in order to diminish the associated reminder. Then, we study the resulting properties in the interpolation error estimates and in Lagrange finite element error estimates.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the main result of this paper which deals with a new refined first-order
expansion formula in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Section
3
investigates the consequences in interpolation errors. The case of dimension one and dimension
n>1
are separately investigated. Application to finite elements errors estimates is studied in section
4
. Some remarks are proposed. Concluding remarks follow.

2
A new first order expansion formula in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

We consider a non-empty bounded and simply connected open domain
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},(n\in\mathbb{N}^{*})
, and a given point
a\in U
. We also consider a function
f
which is twice differentiable on
U
.

We recall that the second order differential
D^{2}\!f(x)
at a given point
x\in U
belongs to the vector space of linear and continuous forms
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}))

which can be identified to the space of bilinear, symmetric and continuous form
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
.

In other words, we have

\forall\,(h,h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:\hskip 2.84544ptD^{2}\!f(x).(h,h^{\prime})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}h_{i}h^{\prime}_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(x)\,.

Above, and in the rest of this article, we adopt the following writing convention: For any linear form
L
, we denote by
L.(h)
or
L(h)
the action of
L
on a given vector
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and by

b.(h,h^{\prime})
or
b(h,h^{\prime})
the action of a bilinear form
b
on a given couple of vectors
(h,h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see for example A. Avez
[
4
]
).

Moreover, we assume that there exist two constants
(m_{2},M_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

\forall\,x\in U,\forall(h,h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:m_{2}\|h\|\|h^{\prime}\|\hskip 2.84544pt\leq\hskip 2.84544ptD^{2}\!f(x).(h,h^{\prime})\leq M_{2}\|h\|\|h^{\prime}\|,

(1)

where
\|.\|
denotes a given norm defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Remark that condition (
1
) can be interpreted by using the natural norm
|||.|||
of a bilinear and continuous form
b
, defined by:

\displaystyle\forall\,b\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}):|||b|||\equiv\sup_{(h,h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{*}}\!\!\times\mathbb{R}^{n^{*}}}\frac{|b(h,h^{\prime})|}{\|h\|.\|h^{\prime}\|}.

(2)

Then, applying this definition to the bilinear form
b=D^{2}\!f(x)
, inequality (
1
) can be written as:

\exists\mathcal{M}_{2}>0,\forall\,x\in U:|||D^{2}\!f(x)|||\leq\mathcal{M}_{2},(\mathcal{M}_{2}=\max(|m_{2}|,|M_{2}|)).

We first begin with a straight consequence of the classical first-order Taylor formula.

Proposition 2.1

Let
f
be a twice-differentiable function defined on a non-empty bounded and simply connected open set
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle f(a+h)=f(a)+D\!f(a).(h)+\|h\|\epsilon_{a,1}(h),

(3)

where
\|.\|
denotes a given norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the remainder
\epsilon_{a,1}(h)
satisfiying:

\frac{\|h\|}{2}m_{2}\leq\epsilon_{a,1}(h)\leq\frac{\|h\|}{2}M_{2}.

(4)

Proof :

For a given twice-differentiable function
f
, we have the classical Taylor’s expansion (see for example
[
21
]
):

\displaystyle\exists\,\xi_{a,h}\in\,]a,a+h[\hskip 2.84544pt\mbox{ such that}\hskip 2.84544ptf(a+h)=f(a)+D\!f(a).(h)+\frac{1}{2}\,D^{2}\!f(\xi_{a,h}).(h,h),

where
]a,a+h[
denotes the open line segment bounded by the two points
a
and
a+h
. For

sufficiently small,
]a,a+h[\in U
.
Then, by comparing this expression with (
3
), we have

\|h\|\epsilon_{a,1}(h)=\frac{1}{2}\,D^{2}\!f(\xi_{a,h}).(h,h).

(5)

So, using (
1
) with

, we get (
4
) from (
5
).

Now, to derive the main result, let us first introduce the function
\phi
defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{r c c c l}\phi&:&[0,1]&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&&t&\longmapsto&D\!f(a+th).(h)\,.\end{array}

(6)

First, we remark that
\phi(0)=D\!f(a).(h)
and that
\phi(1)=D\!f(a+h).(h)
.
Moreover, the remainder
\epsilon_{a,1}(h)
introduced in (
3
) satisfies the following result:

Proposition 2.2

Let
\epsilon_{a,1}(h)
defined by the first order Taylor expansion (
3
). Then, we have

\|h\|\epsilon_{a,1}(h)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\phi^{\prime}(t)\,dt.

(7)

Proof :

Using the first-order Taylor’s formula with the integral form of the remainder gives:

f(a+h)=f(a)+D\!f(a).(h)+\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)D^{2}\!f(a+th).(h,h)\,dt.

(8)

Now, deriving function
\phi
defined by (
6
) with respect to
t
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\biggl[D\!f(a+th).(h)\biggr]=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(a+th)h_{i},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(a_{1}+th_{1},\dots,a_{n}+th_{n})h_{i}.

Using standard rule of derivation
[
21
]
to derive a function of
n
variables, each depending on
t
, we get:

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\biggl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(a_{1}+th_{1},\dots,a_{n}+th_{n})h_{i}h_{j}\biggr],

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{2}\!f(a+th).(h,h),

and finally, the first order Taylor’s formula (
8
) leads to:

f(a+h)=f(a)+D\!f(a).(h)+\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\phi^{\prime}(t)dt.

As consequence, the remainder
\epsilon_{a,1}(h)
in (
3
) satisfies (
7
).

Consider now a given
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, for which we define
\epsilon_{a,m+1}(h)
by

f(a+h)=f(a)+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m}\omega_{k}(m)D\!f\!\left(a+\frac{kh}{m}\right)\!\!.(h)+\|h\|\epsilon_{a,m+1}(h),

(9)

where
\omega_{k}(m)
,
0\leq k\leq m
, denote real weights we want to determine to get a corresponding remainder
\epsilon_{a,m+1}(h)

as small as possible. Since we assumed that the domain
U
is open and simply connected, the set of uniformly distributed points
(x_{k})_{k=1,m}
defined by
\displaystyle x_{k}=a+\frac{kh}{m}
belongs to the segment
[a,a+h]
.

The following theorem constitues our main result, and is devoted to the refined first-order expansion in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 2.3

Let
f
be a twice differentiable real mapping defined on a non-empty bounded and simply connected open
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, such that (
1
) holds, and let
a\in U
be a given point.

If the weights
\omega_{k}(m),(k=0,m),
satisfy:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m}\omega_{k}(m)=1,

then, the following refined first-order expansion formula holds:

f(a+h)=f(a)+\left(\frac{D\!f(a)+D\!f(a+h)}{2m}+\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m-1}D\!f\!\left(a+\frac{kh}{m}\right)\!\!\right)\!.(h)+\|h\|\epsilon_{a,m+1}(h),

(10)

where the remainder
\epsilon_{a,m+1}(h)
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\|h\|}{8m}(m_{2}-M_{2})\leq\epsilon_{a,m+1}(h)\leq\frac{\|h\|}{8m}(M_{2}-m_{2}).

(11)

Moreover, for a uniform distribution of points, this result is optimal in the sense that the weights
\omega_{k}(m)
in (
10
) guarantee the remainder
\epsilon_{a,m+1}(h)
to be minimal.

Remark 1

\frac{}{}

1.

In the theorem above, we use the convention that, when

, the sum

2.

3
Application to the interpolation error

3.1
The case of dimension one

In this subsection, we consider the refined first-order expansion formula (

) with two points, (i.e.

) in the one-dimensional case, namely with

\displaystyle f(a+h)=f(a)+\left(\frac{Df(a)+Df(a+h)}{2}\right)\!\!.(h)+\|h\|\epsilon_{a,2}(h),

\displaystyle f(a+h)=f(a)+\left(\frac{f^{\prime}(a)+f^{\prime}(a+h)}{2}\right)\!\!.h+|h|\epsilon_{a,2}(h),

\frac{|h|}{8}(m_{2}-M_{2})\leqslant\epsilon_{a,2}(h)\leqslant\frac{|h|}{8}(M_{2}-m_{2}).

Now, for

\forall x\in[a,b]:\Pi_{[a,b]}(f)(x)=\frac{x-b}{a-b}\hskip 2.84544ptf(a)+\frac{x-a}{b-a}\hskip 2.84544ptf(b).

e(.)
defined by

\forall x\in[a,b]:e(x)=\Pi_{[a,b]}(f)(x)-f(x),

Lemma 3.1

Let
f
be a function of

. The following interpolation error estimate holds:

\forall x\in[a,b]:|\Pi_{[a,b]}(f)(x)-f(x)|\leq\frac{(b-a)}{4}\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{16}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty},

(30)

where
\|.\|_{\infty}
denotes the classical norm on

.

Proof :

We showed in
[
15
]
(see Formula (56)) that the usual interpolation polynomial
\Pi_{[a,b]}(f)
introduced above can be written, using (
29
), as:

\displaystyle\Pi_{[a,b]}(f)(x)=f(x)+\biggl(\frac{f^{\prime}(b)-f^{\prime}(a)}{2(b-a)}\biggr)(b-x)(x-a)+\frac{(b-x)(x-a)}{(b-a)}\left[\frac{}{}\!\epsilon_{x,2}(b)-\epsilon_{x,2}(a)\right],

(31)

where
\epsilon_{x,2}(b)
and
\epsilon_{x,2}(a)
are the remainders of (
29
) for
a
and
b
respectively.

Consider now these two remainders written in the integral form. Due to (
17
), for
m=1
, we have

\displaystyle\epsilon_{x,2}(a)=\int_{0}^{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}-t\right)\!(a-x)f^{\prime\prime}\!\bigl(x+t(a-x)\bigr)\,dt,

and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{x,2}(b)=\int_{0}^{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}-t\right)\!(b-x)f^{\prime\prime}\!\bigl(x+t(b-x)\bigr)\,dt.

As a consequence, using that
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{1}{2}-t\right|dt=\frac{1}{4}
, these two remainders are bounded by

\displaystyle|\epsilon_{x,2}(a)|\leq\frac{(x-a)}{4}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\,\mbox{ and }\,|\epsilon_{x,2}(b)|\leq\frac{(b-x)}{4}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\,.

Now, using that
\displaystyle\sup_{a\leq x\leq b}(x-a)(b-x)=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{4}
, together with (
31
) yields

\displaystyle|\Pi_{[a,b]}(f)(x)-f(x)|\leq\frac{(b-a)\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{4}+\frac{(b-a)^{2}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{16}\,.

(32)

Considering now the usual Taylor’s formula, classically, the interpolation error
e(.)
is bounded by (see for example
[
19
]
,
[
17
]
)

\displaystyle\forall x\in[a,b]:|f(x)-\Pi_{[a,b]}(f)(x)|\leq\frac{1}{2}(x-a)(x-b)\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}.

So, using again that
\displaystyle\sup_{a\leq x\leq b}(x-a)(b-x)=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{4}
, we get from this error bound that

\displaystyle\forall x\in[a,b]:|f(x)-\Pi_{[a,b]}(f)(x)|\leq\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{8}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}.

(33)

Now, we aim at evaluating the improvement obtained in the upper bound involved in (
30
), compared to the one deduced from the usual interpolation error estimate (
33
). Hence, we are looking for functions
f
and for a positif number
\beta<1
such that

\frac{(b-a)\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}}{4}+\frac{(b-a)^{2}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{16}\leq\beta\,\frac{(b-a)^{2}\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{8},

(34)

that can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{\Lambda},\quad\mbox{where }\Lambda=\frac{4}{(2\beta-1)(b-a)}\,.

(35)

Hence, our goal is to identify values of
\beta
and class of functions

such that (

) does not hold for
0\leq\beta\leq 1/2
. In addition, since we are interested by illustrating (
35

solutions to:

\displaystyle\forall x\in[a,b]:|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(x)}{\Lambda},

(36)

which implies that, in the sequel, we will only consider convex functions, i.e. functions such that
f^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0,\forall x\in[a,b]
, since it is clear that solutions of (
36
) also satisfy (
35
).

The next lemma determines a necessary condition for inequality (
36
) to be verified:

Lemma 3.2

Let
f
be a convex function of
C^{2}([a,b])
, which satisfies inequality (

). Then, we have

\displaystyle\forall x\in[a,b],f(x)\geq\max\biggl(f(a)+\frac{f^{\prime}(a)}{\Lambda}\bigl(e^{\Lambda(x-a)}-1\bigr),f(a)-\frac{f^{\prime}(a)}{\Lambda}\bigl(e^{-\Lambda(x-a)}-1\bigr)\biggr)\,.

Proof :

From differential inequality (
36
), we get the two following second order differential inequalities, for all
x\in[a,b]
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}f^{\prime\prime}(x)-\Lambda f^{\prime}(x)&\geq&\hskip 2.84544pt0,\\[5.69046pt]
f^{\prime\prime}(x)+\Lambda f^{\prime}(x)&\geq&\hskip 2.84544pt0,\end{array}\right.

that can be rewritten, setting
F(x)=f^{\prime}(x)
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}F^{\prime}(x)-\Lambda F(x)&\geq&\hskip 2.84544pt0,\\[5.69046pt]
F^{\prime}(x)+\Lambda F(x)&\geq&\hskip 2.84544pt0,\end{array}\right.

Now, multiplying the first inequality by the integrating factor
e^{-\Lambda(x-a)}
and the second one by the integrating factor

e^{\Lambda(x-a)}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\biggl[F(x)e^{-\Lambda(x-a)}\biggr]\geq 0\,,\hskip 2.84544pt\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{d}{dx}\biggl[F(x)e^{\Lambda(x-a)}\biggr]\geq 0\,.

This means that both functions
F(x)e^{-\Lambda(x-a)}
and
F(x)e^{\Lambda(x-a)}
are increasing on
[a,b]
, or in other words, returning to function
f^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\forall x\in[a,b]:f^{\prime}(x)\geq e^{\Lambda(x-a)}f^{\prime}(a)\mbox{ and }f^{\prime}(x)\geq e^{-\Lambda(x-a)}f^{\prime}(a).

It suffices to integrate thes two differential inequalities on the interval
[a,x],(x\in[a,b])
to obtain the inequality of Lemma
3.2
.

The next lemma enables us to determine sufficient conditions to determine a class of functions
f
satisfying condition (
36
), and consequently condition (
34
).

Lemma 3.3

Let
\delta>0
be a real number and let

be the solution to the problem
(P)
defined by, for all

\displaystyle\hskip 2.84544ptf^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle-\Lambda f^{\prime}(x)=\hskip 2.84544pt\delta,

(37a)

\displaystyle\hskip 2.84544ptf^{\prime}(a)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\delta}{\Lambda}.

(37b)

Then, we have

\forall\beta\in\mbox{$]\frac{1}{2},1]$}:f(x)=f(a)+\frac{f^{\prime}(a)}{\Lambda}\bigl(e^{\Lambda(x-a)}-1\bigr)+\frac{\delta}{\Lambda}\biggl[\frac{e^{\Lambda(x-a)}-1}{\Lambda}-(x-a)\biggr].

In particular,

given by (

).

Proof :

Two successive integrations lead to the solution

given by (

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\biggl(\!f^{\prime}(a)+\frac{\delta}{\Lambda}\biggr)\Lambda e^{\Lambda(x-a)}

(P)
satisfies condition (

).

Furthermore, remark also that, due to condition (

Hence, for all

\displaystyle e^{\Lambda(x-a)}\geq\frac{\delta}{2\biggl(f^{\prime}(a)+\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\Lambda}\biggr)\Lambda},\forall x\in[a,b],

which leads to

Finally, we obtained that solutions

With Lemma

be the corresponding approximate solution to (

Then, we have the following error estimate:

\left\|u-u^{(1)}_{{\sf h}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{C}{\alpha}\left\|u-\pi_{{\sf h}}(u)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\displaystyle\frac{C}{\alpha}\min\left(\frac{\|D^{2}(u)\|_{\infty}}{2}{\sf h}^{2},\frac{\|D(u)\|_{\infty}}{2}{\sf h}+\frac{\|D^{2}(u)\|_{\infty}}{4}{\sf h}^{2}\right)\sqrt{\mu(\Omega)}\,.

(59)

Proof :

Due to Céa’s lemma , we have

\forall v_{{\sf h}}\in V^{(1)}_{{\sf h}}:\left\|u-u^{(1)}_{{\sf h}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\hskip 2.84544pt\leq\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{C}{\alpha}\left\|u-v_{{\sf h}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

So, we choose as the particular element
v^{*}_{{\sf h}}
, the

interpolation function
\pi_{{\sf h}}(u)
defined by (

V^{(1)}_{{\sf h}}

as follows:

\|u-\pi_{{\sf h}}(u)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{{\sf h}}}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}|u-\pi_{{\sf h}}(u)|^{2}d\Omega=\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{{\sf h}}}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}\left|\frac{}{}\!u_{|_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}}-\pi_{{}_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}}(u_{|_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}})\right|^{2}d\Omega,

(60)

where we used the property that,
\forall\,\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{{\sf h}}
,
(\pi_{{\sf h}}\,u)\bigl|_{{\mathcal{S}_{k}}}=\pi_{{}_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}}(u_{|_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}})
.
Moreover, due to the interpolation error (
54

\left|\frac{}{}\!u_{|_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}}-\pi_{{}_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}}(u_{|_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}})\right|\leq\min\left(\frac{\|D^{2}(u)\|_{\infty}}{2}\hskip 2.84544pt,\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{\|D(u)\|_{\infty}}{2}+\frac{\|D^{2}(u)\|_{\infty}}{4}\right).

(61)

As a consequence, inequality (

\|u-\pi_{{\sf h}}(u)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{{\sf h}}}\!\!\min\biggl(\frac{\|D^{2}(u)\|_{\infty}}{2}\,diam(S_{k})^{2},\frac{\|D(u)\|_{\infty}}{2}\,diam(S_{k})+\frac{\|D^{2}(u)\|_{\infty}}{4}\,diam(S_{k})^{2}\biggr)^{\!2}\!\mu(\mathcal{S}_{k})\,.

where

denotes the measure of

Finally, from (

\left\|u-u^{(1)}_{{\sf h}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\hskip 2.84544pt\leq\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{C}{\alpha}\left\|u-\pi_{{\sf h}}(u)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{C}{\alpha}\min\left(\frac{\|D^{2}(u)\|_{\infty}}{2}{\sf h}^{2},\frac{\|D(u)\|_{\infty}}{2}{\sf h}+\frac{\|D^{2}(u)\|_{\infty}}{4}{\sf h}^{2}\right)\!\sqrt{\mu(\Omega)}\,.

Also in that case, this error estimate allows us to get a noticeable improvement of the upper bound of the approximation error (around 50 percents smaller),
as soon as the minimum involved in this inequality is equal to

5
Conclusions and perspectives

In this paper we derived a refined first-order expansion formula in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to minimize the unknown remainder which appears in the classical Taylor’s formula. For a given function, this new formula is composed by a linear combination of its first derivatives, computed at
m+1
equally spaced points. We showed that the corresponding remainder can be minimized for a suitable choice of the weights involved in this linear combination. In particular, we proved that the new remainder is
4m
smaller than the one which appears in the classical first Taylor’s formula.

Afterwards, we considered two important applications: the interpolation error and the finite elements error estimates. In both cases, we showed that we can gain a significant improvement of the error estimate upper bounds. For example, in the one-dimensional case, when
m=1
(with two points involved in the refined formula), we showed that the upper bound of these errors is four times smaller than the usual ones estimated by the classical Taylor formula.

Concerning the finite elements error estimates, for linear second elliptic PDE’s, since the approximation error is bounded by the interpolation error, we proved that, by the help of the
corrected
interpolation polynomial introduced in subsection (
4.2
), we obtained for the interpolation error, a lower upper bound than the usual one.

Several other applications can also be concerned by this new refined first-order expansion formula. For example, the approximation error involved in ODE’s approximation where Taylor’s formula is basically used to derive numerical schemes.
