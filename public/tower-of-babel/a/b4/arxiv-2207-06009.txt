Distributed Safe Resource Allocation using Barrier Functions footnoteinfo
Xuyang Wu, Sindri Magnússon, Mikael Johansson

Abstract
Resource allocation plays a central role in many networked systems such as smart grids, communication networks and urban transportation systems. In these systems, many constraints have physical meaning and having feasible allocation is often vital to avoid system breakdown. Hence, algorithms with asymptotic feasibility guarantees are often insufficient since it is impractical to run algorithms for an infinite number of rounds. This paper proposes a distributed feasible method (DFM) for safe resource allocation based on barrier functions. In DFM, every iterate is feasible and thus safe to implement. We prove that under mild conditions, DFM converges to an arbitrarily small neighbourhood of the optimal solution. Numerical experiments demonstrate the competitive performance of DFM.

safe resource allocation, feasible method, distributed optimization, barrier function, safe optimization.

Introduction

Resource allocation among cooperative agents in networked systems is a central problem in water distribution networks, urban transportation systems, and power networks. In these systems, an allocation is safe only when it is feasible, and violation of physical constraints may cause system breakdown. Consequently, it is important that resource allocation mechanisms always generate feasible allocations.
To make these challenges more concrete, we consider three resource allocation problems from power networks and communications that currently lack distributed solutions.

Economic dispatch
[
20
]
: In a smart grid where some users can generate power and some users have demands, the users wish to cooperatively find the optimal power allocations:

\displaystyle\underset{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},i\in\mathcal{V}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}f_{i}(x)

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}x_{i}=c,

\displaystyle x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i},~~i\in\mathcal{V}

where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the power injection of node
i\in\mathcal{V}
(negative injection
x_{i}<0
means that node
i
consumes power from the grid). The cost function
f_{i}
is the generation cost or the disutility related to shifting power demands for node
i
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}
is the power injection not accounted for by the nodes in
\mathcal{V}
. The coupling constraint ensures that the supply meets the demand, which is a hard physical constraint in power systems.
The local constraints
\mathcal{X}_{i}
are important, as they specify hard constraints on devices or user preferences.

Multiple resources
[
5
]
: In smart grids, users might get power from different sources, e.g., renewable or coal, with different preference and the goal is to minimize user’s total disutility. To model such scenarios, we need to consider multiple coupling constraints, e.g.,

\displaystyle\underset{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},i\in\mathcal{V}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}f_{i}(x_{i}^{\text{renew}},x_{i}^{\text{coal}})

(2)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}x_{i}^{\text{renew}}=0,~~\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}x_{i}^{\text{coal}}=0,

\displaystyle x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i},~i\in\mathcal{V}.

Here, the local objective functions encode user preferences for different energy sources. This problem is similar to economic dispatch, but is more challenging to solve. In fact, as we will review in Section
1.1
, most existing feasible methods can only handle a single coupling constraint.

Rate control in networks
[
8
]
: Consider a network with several transmitters where each user
i
transmits along a single route. The goal is to find the end-to-end transmission rates
x_{i}
that maximize the total user utility

\begin{split}\underset{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},~i\in\mathcal{V}}{\operatorname{maximize}}~~&~\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}U_{i}(x_{i})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&~\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}}x_{i}\leq c_{\ell},\quad\forall\ell\in\mathcal{L},\\
&~x_{i}\geq 0,~\forall i\in\mathcal{V}.\end{split}

(3)

Here,
\mathcal{V}
is the set of transmitters (users),
x_{i}
and
U_{i}
are the transmission rate and the utility function of user
i\in\mathcal{V}
, respectively,
\mathcal{L}
is the set of communication links, and
c_{\ell}
is the capacity of link
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{T}_{\ell}
is the set of transmitters whose data flow is routed across link
\ell
.

1.2
Contribution

Motivated by the lack of distributed feasible methods for solving resource allocation problems with local constraints on general networks, we propose a class of distributed feasible methods (DFM) based on barrier functions. We also propose a reachability condition, which is necessary for a general class of distributed feasible algorithms including DFM and those in
[
19
,
12
,
6
,
7
]
to converge to the optimum from an arbitrary initial allocation. Compared to existing feasible methods
[
7
,
10
,
11
]
that allow for local constraints, DFM can handle a broader range of problems and networks. Specifically, DFM allows for

1.

multi-dimensional variables, multiple coupling constraints, and general undirected networks, while
[
7
,
10
,
11
]
only consider scalar variables and one coupling constraints, and
[
10
,
11
]
rely on star networks.

2.

non-convex objective functions, while the objective functions are required to be convex in
[
7
,
10
]
and strongly convex in
[

.

With the above features, DFM can solve all the three motivating examples in Section

A preliminary conference version of this work can be found in

, which also transforms the problem using barrier functions. However, both the algorithm and the convergence results in

[

Problem, Challenges, and Solution

2.1
Problem Formulation

Consider a network of
n
nodes described by an undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is the set of vertices (representing nodes) and
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
is the set of edges (representing links). Each node
i\in\mathcal{V}
observes a local constraint set
\mathcal{X}_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
, a local cost function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and a local constraint matrix
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_{i}}
. The nodes aim at cooperatively finding the optimal resource allocation:

\begin{split}\underset{x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i},i\in\mathcal{V}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}f_{i}(x_{i})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}A_{i}x_{i}=c.\end{split}

(4)

Note that (
4
) also allows for coupling inequality constraints in the form of
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}A_{i}x_{i}\leq c
. These are simply handled by introducing local variables
y_{i}
and local constraints on the form
A_{i}x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all nodes
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, in combination with the global constraint

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}y_{i}=c
.
For example, the coupling constraint in (
3
) can be re-written as

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle\leq y_{i\ell},\quad\forall\ell\in\mathcal{L},\forall i\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell},

(5)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}}y_{i\ell}

\displaystyle=c_{\ell},\quad\;\forall\ell\in\mathcal{L}.

(6)

For the theoretical analysis, we impose the following assumptions on problem (
4
).

Assumption 1

The following conditions hold:

(a)

Each
f_{i}
is
L_{i}
-smooth for some
L_{i}>0
.

(b)

Each
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}:g_{i}^{j}(x)\leq 0,~j=1,\ldots,q_{i}\}
for some convex and
\beta
-smooth functions
g_{i}^{j}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, each
g_{i}^{j}
is
\beta_{1}
-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{X}_{i}
.

(c)

There exists
\{\tilde{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}
such that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}A_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}=c
and
g_{i}^{j}(\tilde{x}_{i})<0
for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
and
j=1,\ldots,q_{i}
.

(d)

The optimal value
f^{\star}
of problem (
4
) is bounded from below, i.e.,
f^{\star}>-\infty
.

Assumptions
1
(a) and (d) are standard in optimization, especially when each
f_{i}
is non-convex. Assumptions
1
(b)–(c) enable the use of barrier functions for the problem transformation in Section
2.3.2
.

2.2
Challenges

Solving (
4
) with non-asymptotic feasibility guarantee in a distributed way is challenging and primal-dual methods generally can only ensure asymptotic feasibility. Existing distributed primal feasible methods for solving (
4
) include the weighted gradient method in
[
7
,
19
]
, the multi-step weighted gradient method in
[
6
]
, and the coordinate-wise weighted gradient method in
[
12
]
. However, none of them can handle general
A_{i}
’s or general convex
\mathcal{X}_{i}
’s. Specifically,
[
7
,
19
,
6
]
assume each
A_{i}=I
,
[
12
]
requires each
A_{i}
to be a non-zero scalar,
[
7
]
needs each
\mathcal{X}_{i}
to be the set of non-negative scalars, and
[
19
,
6
,
12
]
do not allow for local constraint sets. In this subsection, we will show by simple examples that a straightforward extension of the methods in
[
7
,
19
,
6
]
to handling general
A_{i}
’s or local constraint sets may fail even if
\mathcal{G}
is connected. The multi-step weighted gradient method
[
6
]
is more complicated and is not analysed here for simplicity.

Extension to handle general
A_{i}
’s
: When
A_{i}=1
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, the methods in
[
7
,
19
,
12
]
can be described as follows. Start from a feasible solution
\{x_{i}^{0}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}
. At each iteration, for an edge set
\mathcal{E}^{k}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, find
p_{ij}^{k}

\forall\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}^{k}
by solving

\begin{split}\operatorname{minimize}~~&~f_{i}^{k}(x_{i}^{k}+p_{ji})+f_{j}^{k}(x_{j}^{k}+p_{ij})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&~p_{ji}+p_{ij}=0,\end{split}

(7)

where
f_{i}^{k}(x_{i})=f_{i}(x_{i}^{k})+\langle\nabla f_{i}(x_{i}^{k}),x_{i}-x_{i}^{k}\rangle+\frac{L_{i}}{2}\|x_{i}-x_{i}^{k}\|^{2}
is a quadratic surrogate function for
f_{i}
around iterate
x_{i}^{k}
. The decision variable
p_{ij}
describes the optimal re-allocation of resources from node
i
to node
j
(by the optimality conditions for (
7
), this quantity is proportional to the difference in the gradients of the loss functions of the nodes at their current iterates). For
\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{E}^{k}
,
p_{ij}^{k}=\mathbf{0}
. Then, the resource allocation is updated as

x_{i}^{k+1}=x_{i}^{k}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}w_{ij}p_{ij}^{k},~\forall i\in\mathcal{V}.

(8)

where
w_{ij}=w_{ji}>0
are scalar weights. In the weighted gradient descent method
[
7
,
19
]
,
\mathcal{E}^{k}=\mathcal{E}
and in the coordinate-wise weighted gradient method
[
12
]
,
\mathcal{E}^{k}
is randomly sampled from
\mathcal{E}
. Note that by (
8
),

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}x_{i}^{k+1}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}x_{i}^{k}=\!\sum_{\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}}\!\!\!w_{ij}(p_{ij}^{k}+p_{ji}^{k})=\mathbf{0}.

(9)

Hence, if
\{x_{i}^{0}\}_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}
satisfies the coupling constraint, then so will
\{x_{i}^{k}\}_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}
for all future iterations
k\geq 0
.

For general
A_{i}
’s, it is natural to find a feasible resource-reallocation by replacing (
7
) by

\begin{split}\operatorname{minimize}~~&~f_{i}^{k}(x_{i}^{k}+p_{ji})+f_{j}^{k}(x_{j}^{k}+p_{ij})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&~A_{i}p_{ji}+A_{j}p_{ij}=\mathbf{0}.\end{split}

(10)

Using a similar argument as above, the resource updates
p_{ij}
and
p_{ji}
ensure that future iterates
\{x_{i}^{k}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}
satisfy the global coupling constraint by (
9
). However, as the next example shows, this algorithm could fail to converge to the optimum.

Example 1

Consider the line graph with four nodes:

The problem is

\begin{split}\underset{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},~i=1,\ldots,4}{\operatorname{minimize}}~&~\sum_{i=1}^{4}f_{i}(x_{i})=\frac{1}{2}(x_{i}-\theta_{i})^{2}\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&~x_{1}+x_{4}=1,\end{split}

(11)

where
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\theta_{4})=(1,0,0,1)
. This is an example of (
4
) without local constraints. For this example, the point
(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3}^{\prime},x_{4}^{\prime})=(0,0,0,1)
is a fixed point of the update (
8
) with
p_{ij}^{k}
,
\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}^{k}
computing from (
10
). To see this, note that by letting
x_{i}^{k}=x_{i}^{\prime}

\forall i\in\mathcal{V}
, the solution to (
10
) is
p_{ij}^{k}=p_{ji}^{k}=\mathbf{0}
for any

In Section

Extension to handle general local constraint sets
: A straightforward extension of the above method to (

) with general local constraint sets is to add the constraints
x_{i}^{k}+p_{ji}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}
and
x_{j}^{k}+p_{ij}\in\mathcal{X}_{j}
to problem (

), leading to

\begin{split}\operatorname{minimize}~~&~f_{i}^{k}(x_{i}^{k}+p_{ji})+f_{j}^{k}(x_{j}^{k}+p_{ij})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&~A_{i}p_{ji}+A_{j}p_{ij}=\mathbf{0},\\
&~x_{i}^{k}+p_{ji}\in\mathcal{X}_{i},~x_{j}^{k}+p_{ij}\in\mathcal{X}_{j}.\end{split}

(12)

The iterates
p_{ij}^{k}

\forall\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}^{k}
are obtained by solving the above problem and the remaining parts are the same as earlier, i.e.,
p_{ij}^{k}=p_{ji}^{k}=\mathbf{0}
for
\{i,j\}\in\mathcal{E}\setminus\mathcal{E}^{k}
and

is updated by (

). We additionally require
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}w_{ij}\leq 1
for all

. Then,

is a convex combination of

and

,
\forall j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
, which all belong to
\mathcal{X}_{i}
. Hence,

\forall i\in\mathcal{V}
. By similar derivation in (
9
),
\{x_{i}^{k+1}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}
is also feasible to the coupling constraint. Therefore, all iterates are feasible to (
4
).

A_{i}=1
which satisfies the conditions in
[

,

,

2.3
Solution to the Challenges

2.3.1
Reachability of a general class of feasible methods

This subsection proposes a reachability condition. It enables a general class of distributed feasible methods to explore the whole feasible set, which is often necessary for guaranteeing optimality. The condition is also helpful for understanding and circumventing the failure in Example
1
. For convenience, let
\mathbf{x}^{k}=[(x_{1}^{k})^{T},\ldots,(x_{n}^{k})^{T}]^{T}
for all
k\geq 0
and
A=[A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}]
.

General update scheme
: Start from a feasible iterate
\mathbf{x}^{0}
of (
4
). For any
k\geq 0
, update the iterate vector according to

\mathbf{x}^{k+1}=\mathbf{x}^{k}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k},

(13)

where
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}=[(u_{i1}^{k})^{T},\ldots,(u_{in}^{k})^{T}]^{T}
and
u_{ij}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
is the change that node
i
makes on
x_{j}^{k}
,
j\in\mathcal{V}
. We assume that

\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}=\{\mathbf{u}_{i}:u_{ij}=\mathbf{0}~\forall j\notin\bar{\mathcal{N}}_{i},~\sum_{j\in\bar{\mathcal{N}}_{i}}A_{j}u_{ij}=\mathbf{0}\},

i.e., each node can only affect its own iterate and the iterates of its neighbors. When nodes only have information of themselves and their neighbors, this is a natural way to preserve feasibility of the coupling constraint. The extended method in Section
2.2
for handling general
A_{i}
’s and the feasible methods in
[
7
,
19
,
6
,
12
]
all belong to this scheme.

Below we introduce the reachability condition. Note that by (
13
) and
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
,
\mathbf{x}^{k+1}-\mathbf{x}^{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}+\ldots+\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{S}_{i}\subseteq\operatorname{Null}(A)
for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
. Hence, with the update (
13
),
the optimal solution
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
is reachable from any
\mathbf{x}^{0}\in\operatorname{Null}(A)
only if the following assumption holds
.

Assumption 2
(Reachability)

It holds that

\mathcal{S}_{1}+\ldots+\mathcal{S}_{n}=\operatorname{Null}(A).

(14)

This assumption involves both the network
\mathcal{G}
and the constraint matrix
A
. Under Assumption
2
, the update (
13
) can explore the whole set
\{\mathbf{x}:A\mathbf{x}=c\}
while preserving feasibility of all iterates to the coupling constraint. However, if Assumption
2
fails to hold, the scheme is unable to reach
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
from any initial allocation
\mathbf{x}^{0}
that satisfies
\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\mathbf{x}^{0}\in\operatorname{Null}(A)\setminus(\mathcal{S}_{1}+\ldots+\mathcal{S}_{n})
.

It can be verified that Example
1
fails to satisfy Assumption
2
. However, by simply adding the link
\{1,4\}
to
\mathcal{G}
, Assumption
2
holds. Moreover, by the following Lemma, Assumption
2
is satisfied by the coupling constraint in problems (
1
)-(
2
) and the coupling constraint (
6
), which correspond to the three motivating examples in Section
1
.

Lemma 1

If all the
A_{i}
’s have full row rank and
\mathcal{G}
is connected, then Assumption
2
holds. Moreover, if we define transmitters who share the same transmission link as neighbors in problem (
3
), i.e.,
\mathcal{E}=\{\{i,j\}:~i,j\in\mathcal{T}_{\ell}\text{ for some }\ell\in\mathcal{L}\}
, then (
6
) satisfies Assumption
2
.

Proof 2.1
.

See Appendix
7.1
.

In problems (
1
)-(
2
), each
A_{i}=I
which has full row rank. Then by Lemma
1
, Assumption
2
holds when
\mathcal{G}
is connected.

We also discuss Assumption

on the consensus constraint.

Example 2.2

(consensus constraint)
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is connected, then the constraint that every iterate should be in consensus, i.e.
x_{1}=x_{2}=\ldots=x_{n}
,
can be rewritten as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{G}}]_{i}x_{i}=\mathbf{0}
where
[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{G}}]_{i}
is the

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{G}}
of
\mathcal{G}
. Hence, we can include this consensus constraint in problem (

contains a star network, i.e., if

2.3.2
Problem Transformation using Barrier Function

As illustrated in Example
2
, distributed feasible methods for solving (
4

) using barrier functions:

\begin{split}\underset{x_{i}\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i},i\in\mathcal{V}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~~&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}f_{i}(x_{i})+\rho B_{i}(x_{i})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}A_{i}x_{i}=c,\end{split}

(15)

where each
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i}=\{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}:~g_{i}^{j}(x_{i})<0,~\forall j=1,\ldots,q_{i}\}
is the interior of
\mathcal{X}_{i}
and each
B_{i}(x_{i})=-\sum_{j=1}^{q_{i}}\frac{1}{g_{i}^{j}(x_{i})}
is the inverse barrier function of the constraints
g_{i}^{j}(x_{i})\leq 0
,
\forall j=1,\ldots,q_{i}
. Problem (

in Section

4
).

For simplicity, we rewrite (

) in the compact form:

\begin{split}\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}}}{\operatorname{minimize}}~~~&F(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}f_{i}(x_{i})+\rho B_{i}(x_{i})\\
\operatorname{subject~to}~&A\mathbf{x}=c,\end{split}

(16)

where
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1}^{T},\ldots,x_{n}^{T}]^{T}
,
\mathcal{\tilde{X}}=\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{1}\times\ldots\times\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
N=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}d_{i}
,

, and
B(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}B_{i}(x_{i})
.

2.

6
Conclusion

Our ability to allocate resources in a safe and efficient manner is critical to the operation of many physical network systems. We have proposed a distributed feasible method (DFM) to solve resource allocation problems while guaranteeing that all iterates are feasible and thus safe to implement. DFM is designed based on barrier functions and can handle more general problems (multiple coupling constraints, multi-dimensional variables, and non-convex objective functions) and network topologies than the previous state-of-the-art.

Strongly convex

(56)

Since

(57)

\mathbf{a}=(WW^{\dagger})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{k})

\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{x}^{k}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\star}

\begin{split}\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle+\frac{\sigma}{2}\|\mathbf{b}\|^{2}&=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{\mathbf{a}}{\sqrt{\sigma}}+\sqrt{\sigma}\mathbf{b}\right\|^{2}-\frac{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}{2\sigma}\geq-\frac{\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}}{2\sigma},\end{split}

Combining the above equation with (

which further yields the result.

Proof of Lemma
4.5

\mathbf{x}^{\alpha}

16
). Hence,

f

(59)

In addition, by the definition of
