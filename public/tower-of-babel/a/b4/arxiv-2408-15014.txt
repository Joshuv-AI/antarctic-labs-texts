Simple Models of Randomization and Preservation Theorems
Karim Khanaki, Massoud Pourmahdian

Abstract
The main purpose of this paper is to provide new, more uniform model-theoretic and combinatorial proofs of the preservation theorems established in [ 5 ] and [ 8 ] . More precisely, we show that randomization preserves both N ​ I ​ P NIP and stability: if a first-order theory T T has N ​ I ​ P NIP , then so does T R T^{R} , and if T T is stable, then so is T R T^{R} . Our proofs rely on the systematic use of simple models of T R T^{R} and quantitative combinatorial methods. In the N ​ I ​ P NIP case, we use indiscernible arrays and Rademacher mean width, whereas in the stable case, we use a Ramsey lemma for cross-blocks and sequential Rademacher mean width. Using simple models of T R T^{R} has the advantage of allowing us to re-prove these theorems in a simpler and quantitative manner. We finally turn our attention to N ​ S ​ O ​ P NSOP in randomization. We show that based on the definition of N ​ S ​ O ​ P NSOP given in [ 14 ] , T R T^{R} is stable if and only if it is N ​ I ​ P NIP and N ​ S ​ O ​ P NSOP .

Introduction

The main concern of the current paper is the general question of whether certain model-theoretic properties are preserved under the randomization construction.
Following the seminal paper of Keisler,
[
13
]
, the randomization
of a first-order theory
T
, denoted by

, was introduced in

[
8
]
as a complete continuous theory.
Several papers, including
[

[
5
]
,
[

[

[

Among these preservation results, the case of

under randomization, in the general continuous-logic setting, was established by Ben Yaacov in
[
5

, using substantial combinatorial and analytic machinery, including continuous VC theory, the correspondence between random types and Keisler measures, and estimates involving Gaussian mean width. The general framework for the randomization of continuous theories was subsequently developed in
[
6

This raises the question of whether
NIP
preservation can be proved more directly, using methods closer to standard model-theoretic practice. We answer this question by working directly with simple models of randomization, rather than relying on the correspondence between random types and Keisler measures. This approach gives a more concrete proof and also yields quantitative bounds.

More precisely, our argument does not require a separate finite-VC-dimension result for the class of expectation functions associated with a measurable family of function classes of uniformly bounded VC dimension. Instead, we work directly with simple models of randomization and use the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem to control the VC dimension along the relevant increasing chain of function classes.

The preservation of stability under randomization was originally proved by Ben Yaacov and Keisler (in
[
8
]
, for classical case) using an adaptation of Shelah’s ranks and the definability of types. Our proof follows a different route, while retaining the guiding idea of working directly with simple models of randomization. The stable case requires a different combinatorial analysis from the NIP case: we use a Ramsey-theoretic lemma for cross-blocks and sequential Rademacher mean-width estimates in place of the indiscernible-array argument and the ordinary Rademacher mean-width estimates used for
NIP
. Thus, the two preservation proofs share a common model-theoretic framework but exploit distinct combinatorial mechanisms suited to the respective properties.

In addition to the preservation theorems, we study
NIP
and stability in configurations of independent random variables. The corresponding results, Theorems
3.5
and
3.22
, are proved by Ramsey-theoretic arguments and do not rely on Rademacher complexity or learning-theoretic machinery. These theorems therefore provide a complementary part of our approach: whereas mean-width estimates are useful in the general preservation arguments, the independent-variable setting admits a more explicitly combinatorial treatment.

We further raise some issues concerning the
NSOP
property. Without a universally accepted definition of
NSOP
for continuous logic,
we show that, in the context of randomization, the definition of
NSOP

given in
[
14
]
is the weakest condition among the known concepts
that satisfy Shelah’s theorem. That is,
T^{R}
is stable if and only if it is
NIP
and
NSOP
.

The choice of
NSOP
is relevant because the form of Shelah’s theorem used here holds for randomization theories under this notion. By contrast,
[
15
]
discusses continuous theories outside the randomization setting for which the corresponding form of Shelah’s theorem fails under the notion of SOP considered there.

The motivation for this paper is not merely to provide alternative proofs of known preservation theorems, but to identify a more direct and flexible model-theoretic framework for studying which structural properties survive randomization. The original proofs of
NIP
and stability preservation, while fundamental, rely on substantially different techniques: the former draws on VC-theoretic and analytic machinery, whereas the latter uses an adaptation of Shelah’s ranks and the definability of types. We show that simple models of randomization provide a common setting in which both preservation results can be approached through concrete combinatorial configurations. In the
NIP
case, this leads to a proof based on indiscernible arrays and Rademacher mean width, together with quantitative bounds; in the stable case, an analogous strategy uses a Ramsey lemma for cross-blocks and sequential Rademacher mean width. Moreover, the preservation results for independent random variables (i.e., Theorems
3.5
and
3.22
) are established by Ramsey-theoretic arguments that do not depend on Rademacher complexity or learning-theoretic machinery. These complementary methods reveal which parts of the preservation phenomena are genuinely model-theoretic and which admit quantitative combinatorial formulations. The discussion of
NSOP
further places the preservation of stability in a broader classification-theoretic context, using the notion of
NSOP
adopted in this paper. Finally, the method’s applicability to incomplete theories, and its potential extension to continuous theories, indicate that simple models are not merely a technical convenience: they offer a systematic framework for investigating preservation under randomization and for seeking analogous results for other model-theoretic properties.

and

in

In Section 4, we finally turn our attention to the randomization of continuous theories and present a proof sketch showing how one could extend the ideas from Section 3 showing that the
NIP

of a continuous theory

2.2
Simple Models of Randomization

Although we assume some familiarity with continuous logic, randomizations and Keisler measures (cf.
[
8
]
and
[
18
]
), to make this article self-contained, we will briefly sketch some basic materials of the randomization of first-order theory
T
, denoted by
T^{R}
, as a continuous first-order theory.
Recall from
[
8
]

T^{R}
, is a complete continuous theory whose elements of models are random elements of
M
for some model
M
of
T
.
3
3

3

Note that for an arbitrary (not necessarily complete) theory,
T^{R}
can also be defined and will not necessarily be a complete theory. We only deal with this case in Theorem
3.19
.

We consider a randomization of a model
M
of
T
as a two-sorted continuous structure. While the elements in the first sort
\bf K
are random elements of
M
, the elements in the second sort
\bf B
, on the other hand, are events in an underlying probability space. So, the randomization language
L^{R}
is a two-sorted continuous language with sorts
\bf K
and
\bf B
, an
n
-ary function symbol
\llbracket\phi(\bar{x})\rrbracket
of sort
{\bf K}^{n}\to{\bf B}
for each first-order formula
\phi(\bar{x})\in L
with
|\bar{x}|=n
, a
[0,1]
-valued unary predicate symbol
\mathbb{P}
of sort
\bf B
for probability, and the Boolean operations
\cap,\cup,^{c},\perp,\top
of sort
\bf B
. The language
L^{R}
also has distance predicates
d_{\bf B}
of sort
\bf B
and
d_{\bf K}
of sort
\bf K
.

A pre-structure for
L^{R}
is a pair
({\cal K},{\cal B})
where
\cal K
is an interpretation of sort
\bf K
and
\cal B
is an interpretation of sort
\bf B
.
For a model
M\models T
and an atomless finitely additive probability space
\Omega=(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{B}_{0},\mu_{0})
denote

M_{0}^{\Omega}:=\big\{f\colon\Omega_{0}\to M:f\text{ has finite image, and for each }a\in M,f^{-1}(\{a\})\in\mathcal{B}_{0}\big\}.

Then
(M_{0}^{\Omega},{\cal B}_{0})
is
a simple randomization
of
M
and an
L^{R}
-pre-structure in which the sort
\bf K
is interpreted as
M_{0}^{\Omega}
and the sort
\bf B
as
{\cal B}_{0}
respectively. Furthermore, the measure
{\mathbb{P}}
is given by
{\mathbb{P}}(B)=\mu_{0}(B)
for each
B\in{\cal B}_{0}
, and
\llbracket\phi(\bar{x})\rrbracket
functions are
\llbracket\phi(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\rrbracket=\mu_{0}(\{t\in\Omega_{0}:M\models\phi(f_{1}(t),\dots,f_{n}(t))\})
.
Its distance predicates are
d_{\bf K}(f,g)=\mu_{0}(\{t\in\Omega_{0}:f(t)\neq g(t)\})
and
d_{\bf B}(B,C)=\mu_{0}(B\triangle C)
where
\triangle
is the symmetric difference operation.
By Proposition 2.2 of
[
8
]
, there is a unique complete theory
T^{R}
for
L^{R}
, called the
randomization of
T
, such that for each model
N
of
T
,
(M_{0}^{\Omega},{\cal B}_{0})
is a pre-model of
T^{R}
. The simple randomizations of models of

are called

T^{R}
.

Every simple pre-model
(M_{0}^{\Omega},{\cal B}_{0})
leads to a model
M^{\Omega}
of
T^{R}
, through the identification of distance zero elements in both sorts as well as completing both sorts with respect to their metrics. These structures are called
simple models
of
T^{R}
.

By Theorem 2.9 of
[
8
]
,
T^{R}
is a complete continuous theory and enjoys the elimination of quantifiers in the language
L^{R}
. So any
L^{R}
-definable predicate is a uniform limit of boolean combinations of
L^{R}
-formulas of the form
\mathbb{P}\llbracket\phi(x_{1},...,x_{n})\rrbracket
where
\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in L
.

Suppose that
f,g
are elements of the simple randomization

(M_{0}^{\Omega},{\cal B}_{0})
. Then there are finite measurable partitions
{\cal A}_{1},\ldots,{\cal A}_{k}
and
{\cal B}_{1},\ldots,{\cal B}_{m}

of
\Omega_{0}
and elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\in M
such that for any
i\leq k
and all
t\in{\cal A}_{i}
,
f(t)=a_{i}
, and
for any
j\leq m
and all
t\in{\cal B}_{j}
,
g(t)=b_{j}
. We say that
f
and
g
are
independent

if for every
i\leq k
,
j\leq m
, we have
\mu_{0}({\cal A}_{i}\cap{\cal B}_{j})=\mu_{0}({\cal A}_{i})\cdot\mu_{0}({\cal B}_{j})
.

The significance of simple pre-structures is explained in the following proposition:

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(M_{0}^{\Omega},{\cal B}_{0})
be a simple randomization of
M
. Suppose that
f,g\in M_{0}^{\Omega}
are independent, and
\phi(x;y)\in L
with
|x|=|y|=1
. Then, there are
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\in M
, and
r_{i},s_{j}\in[0,1]
with
\sum_{i\leq k}r_{i}=\sum_{j\leq m}s_{j}=1

{\mathbb{P}}\llbracket\phi(f;g)\rrbracket=\sum_{j\leq m}\sum_{i\leq k}s_{j}\cdot r_{i}\cdot\phi(a_{i};b_{j})
.

Proof.

As
f\in M_{0}^{\Omega}
, there are a finite measurable partition
{\cal A}_{1},\ldots,{\cal A}_{k}
of
\Omega_{0}
and elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in M
such that for any
i\leq k
and all
t\in{\cal A}_{i}
,
f(t)=a_{i}
. This suggests that,
we consider

as a formal sum
\sum_{i\leq k}a_{i}^{{\cal A}_{i}}
.
Similarly, we assume that

is of the form
\sum_{j\leq m}{b_{j}}^{{\cal B}_{j}}
where
b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\in M
and
{\cal B}_{1},\ldots,{\cal B}_{m}
is a measurable partition of
\Omega_{0}

{\mathbb{P}}\llbracket\phi(f;g)\rrbracket={\mathbb{P}}\llbracket\phi(\sum_{i\leq k}a_{i}^{{\cal A}_{i}};\sum_{j\leq m}b_{j}^{{\cal B}_{j}})\rrbracket=\sum_{j\leq m}\sum_{i\leq k}\mu_{0}({\cal B}_{j}\cap{\cal A}_{i})\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\llbracket\phi(a_{i};b_{j})\rrbracket

\overset{\text{*}}{=}\sum_{j\leq m}\sum_{i\leq k}\mu_{0}({\cal B}_{j})\cdot\mu_{0}({\cal A}_{i})\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\llbracket\phi(a_{i};b_{j})\rrbracket

=\sum_{j\leq m}\sum_{i\leq k}\mu_{0}({\cal B}_{j})\cdot\mu_{0}({\cal A}_{i})\cdot\phi(a_{i};b_{j}).

The equality
\overset{\text{*}}{=}
follows from the independence of

and

.
(To be more precise here, in the penultimate line,

from

to
\{a_{i}\}
. Similarly for
b_{j}
.)

2.3
The

-Theorem

We recall the classical VC theorem, which will be used
in the proofs of our main results.
The reader is referred to
[
18
]
and
[
5
]
for extensive accounts of

Definition 2.8
.

A Keisler measure
\mu(x)
over a model

is called generically stable if for every formula
\phi(x;y)
and

, there are

\sup_{b\in N}\big|\mu(\phi(x;b))-{\rm Ave}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n};\phi(x;b))\big|\leq\epsilon.

Clearly, every generically stable measure is definable over and finitely satisfiable in a small set/model (cf.
[
18

). On the other hand, notice that the measures of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}\cdot\phi(a_{i};y)
(with
a_{i}\in M
and

) are generically stable.

By the
VC
-theorem (cf.
[
18
]
, Theorem 6.6 and Lemma 7.24), the number

in Definition

depends only on
\phi(x;y)
and
\epsilon
:

Fact 2.9

(
[
19
]
, Fact 1.2)
.

Let
\phi(x;y)
be a
NIP
formula and
\epsilon>0
. Then there is an integer

, depending only on
\phi
and
\epsilon
, such that for any generically stable measure
\mu(x)
on
M
, there are
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in M
such that

\sup_{b\in M}|\mu(\phi(x;b))-{\rm Ave}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n};\phi(x;b))|\leq\epsilon.

(In this case, sometimes for brevity, we write
|\mu(\phi)-{\rm Ave}(\bar{a})(\phi)|\leq\epsilon
.)

Definition 3.3
.

Fix

.

Proposition 3.4
.

(Assuming

) Let

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose
T
is an
NIP
theory and
M
a model of
T
.
Then for each formula
\phi(x;y)
and
r<s
, there is a natural number
N
such that there is
no
sequence
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N})
of average measures of elements in
M
which is shattered by average elements in
M
for
\phi(x;y)
and
r<s
.

Proof.

Suppose on the contrary that there are a formula
\phi(x;y)
and
r<s
such that for any large number
N
there is a sequence
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N})
of average measures of elements in
M
which is shattered by average elements in
M
for
\phi
and
r<s
.
That is, for any
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
there are
b_{1}^{I},\ldots,b_{t}^{I}\in M
such that

\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mu_{i}(\phi(x;b_{j}^{I}))\leq r
if
i\in I
, and
\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mu_{i}(\phi(x;b_{j}^{I}))\geq s
otherwise.

Without loss of generality, using Fact
2.9
, we can assume that there exists a fixed
k
such that for every
i
the measure
\mu_{i}(\phi(x;y))
is of the form
{\rm Ave}(\bar{a}_{i};\phi(x;y))
where
\bar{a}_{i}=(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,k})\in M^{k}
. Indeed, set
\epsilon=\frac{1}{8}|s-r|
, and using Fact
2.9
, for
\phi(x;y)
and a fixed
k
, consider a sequence
(\nu_{i}:i\leq N)
of average measures of the forms
\nu_{i}={\rm Ave}(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,k};\phi(x;y))
such that
|\mu_{i}(\phi)-\nu_{i}(\phi)|<\epsilon
. Set
r^{\prime}=r+2\epsilon
and
s^{\prime}=s-2\epsilon
. Then, the sequence
(\nu_{i}:i\leq N)
is shattered by elements in
M_{0}^{\Omega}
for
\phi
and
r^{\prime}<s^{\prime}
. Thus, for consistency in notation, we continue to use
\mu_{i}
and
r,s
.

Fix an even number
m\geq 2n
. Let
d=\operatorname{VC}(\varphi(x;y))
and choose
n=d+1
. By Fact
2.3
, for sufficiently large
N
, there is a
\varphi
-
k
-
n
-indiscernible sub-array

(\bar{a}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\bar{a}_{i_{m}})

of
(\bar{a}_{1},\ldots,\bar{a}_{N})
, where
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}
.

By the assumption, there are
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\in M
such that

\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mu_{i_{\ell}}\bigl(\varphi(x;b_{j})\bigr)\leq r\quad\text{if $\ell$ is even},

and

\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mu_{i_{\ell}}\bigl(\varphi(x;b_{j})\bigr)\geq s\quad\text{if $\ell$ is odd}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle(m-1)(s-r)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\left|\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mu_{i_{\ell+1}}(\varphi(x;b_{j}))-\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mu_{i_{\ell}}(\varphi(x;b_{j}))\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\left|\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\Bigl(\mu_{i_{\ell+1}}(\varphi(x;b_{j}))-\mu_{i_{\ell}}(\varphi(x;b_{j}))\Bigr)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\Bigl|\mu_{i_{\ell+1}}(\varphi(x;b_{j}))-\mu_{i_{\ell}}(\varphi(x;b_{j}))\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\Bigl|\mu_{i_{\ell+1}}(\varphi(x;b_{j}))-\mu_{i_{\ell}}(\varphi(x;b_{j}))\Bigr|.

Since
n=d+1
is larger than the VC-dimension of
\varphi(x;y)
, Lemma
2.4
gives a constant
K=2n-2=2d
, independent of
m,t,j,h
, such that

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\left|\varphi(a_{i_{\ell+1},h};b_{j})-\varphi(a_{i_{\ell},h};b_{j})\right|\leq K.

It follows that

(m-1)(s-r)\leq\frac{1}{tk}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\sum_{h=1}^{k}K=K.

Since
m
can be chosen arbitrarily large, this contradicts
s-r>0
. This proves the theorem.
∎

The reader should note that the preceding argument proves
the result only for formulas
\phi(x;y)
with
|y|=1
.
However, the argument can be extended to tuples
y
of
arbitrary finite length.

Remark 3.6
.

With the restriction that one of the variables
x
or
y
is interpreted only by elements in classical models such as
M
,
the argument presented in Theorem
3.5

gives a quantitative bound for
VC
-dimension of
{\mathbb{P}}\llbracket\phi(x;y)\rrbracket
in terms of
VC
-dimension of
\phi(x;y)
, the value
\epsilon=s-r
and the Ramsey number (of

where
m,n,k
are given in Fact

).
Furthermore, our proof can be formulated in a purely combinatorial language without any reference to specific model-theoretic language.

From now on, we aim to show that the previous theorem can be translated into the randomized setting, noting that the independence of the
f_{i}
’s and
g_{I}
’s in Proposition
3.4
imposes a limitation.
Therefore, in order to resolve this issue, we introduce a new concept (namely, Rademacher complexity), and by combining well-known facts about this complexity with Theorem 3.5, we arrive at the desired result.

Rademacher Mean Width and VC-Dimension

The following definitions and most of the material after them are standard in learning theory; nevertheless, we present them fairly precisely. Our main goal is to arrive at Proposition
3.15
using Theorem
3.5
and these facts from learning theory.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\sigma
be a randomly chosen vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which
\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}^{n}
. We call
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\sup_{a\in A}\sigma\cdot a]
the
Rademacher mean width
, denoted
w_{R}(A)
.

This definition will be used for a class of functions.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
Q\subseteq[0,1]^{X}
be a function class, and let
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
. Let
Q(\bar{x})=\{(q(x_{1}),\dots,q(x_{n})):q\in Q\}
.
Define the Rademacher mean width
r_{Q}(n)
to be
\sup_{\bar{x}\in X^{n}}w_{R}(Q(\bar{x}))
.

The following fact is a well-known classical result in learning theory, which shows the relationship between
r_{Q}
and the VC-dimension, and fat-shattering dimension.

For a function class
Q\subseteq[0,1]^{X}
, we use the
scale-sensitive VC-dimension
vc_{\epsilon}(Q)
and the
fat-shattering dimension
fs_{\epsilon}(Q)
in the sense of
Anderson
[
1
]
. More precisely,
vc_{\epsilon}(Q)
is
the supremum, over
0\leq r\leq 1-\epsilon
, of the VC-dimension
of the corresponding
(r,r+\epsilon)
-threshold class, while

fs_{\epsilon}(Q)
is the largest cardinality of a finite subset of

X
that is
\epsilon
-fat-shattered by
Q
. We use these
standard scale-sensitive notions throughout; in particular, the
quantitative relations between
vc_{\epsilon}(Q)
,

fs_{\epsilon}(Q)
, and the covering numbers of
Q
are those
given in
[
1
, Definition 2.16 and Facts 2.17–2.18]
. In the following, we call a class
Q
a VC class if
vc_{\epsilon}
is finite for every
\epsilon
.

Fact 3.9

(Lemma 2.11,
[
1
]
)
.

Let
X
be a set, and
Q\subseteq[0,1]^{X}
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

Q
is a VC class.

(ii)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{r_{Q}(n)}{n}=0

Moreover, the implication

\mathrm{VC}(Q)<\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{r_{Q}(n)}{n}\longrightarrow 0

is quantitative: for every
0\leq r<s\leq 1
, an upper bound on the
\mathrm{VC}
-dimension of
Q
together with
s-r
gives a uniform bound on
\frac{r_{Q}(n)}{n}
, tending to
0
as
n\to\infty
.

The following remark demonstrates precisely the “moreover” part of the previous fact.
Indeed, all the material preceding Proposition
3.15
, with the possible exception of Lemmas
3.12
,
3.14
consists of standard facts from learning theory, and readers familiar with these results may skip this
material.

Remark 3.10

(Uniform quantitative form of (i)
\Longrightarrow
(ii) of Fact 3.9)
.

Moreover, the convergence in Fact 3.9 is uniform for classes with
uniform bounds on their scale-sensitive VC-dimensions. More precisely,
suppose that for every
\epsilon>0
there is
d(\epsilon)<\infty
such
that

vc_{\epsilon}(Q)\leq d(\epsilon).

Then, for every
0<\eta<1
, there is

N=N(\eta,d(\cdot))

such that

\frac{r_{Q}(n)}{n}<\eta\qquad\text{for all }n\geq N

and for every class
Q
satisfying the above bounds.

Indeed, fix
0<\delta<1
. By Definition 2.16 and
Facts 2.17–2.18 of
[
1
]
, a uniform bound on

vc_{\delta/4}(Q)
yields a uniform bound on the covering numbers
of
Q
. More precisely, since

vc_{\delta/4}(Q)\leq d(\delta/4),

Fact 2.17 gives

fs_{\delta/4}(Q)\leq D_{\delta},\qquad D_{\delta}:=\left(2\left\lceil\frac{4}{\delta}\right\rceil-1\right)d(\delta/4).

Hence, by Fact 2.18, there is a constant

C_{\delta,d(\delta/4)}<\infty,

depending only on
\delta
and
d(\delta/4)
, such that

\log N_{Q,\delta}(n)\leq C_{\delta,d(\delta/4)}(\log n)^{2}

for all
n\geq 2
.

Fix a sample
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
, and let
G
be a

\delta
-net for
Q(\bar{x})
with respect to the
l_{\infty}
-metric.
Then

|G|\leq N_{Q,\delta}(n).

For every
q\in Q
, choose
g\in G
such that

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|q(x_{i})-g(x_{i})|\leq\delta.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\sup_{q\in Q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}q(x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq n\delta+\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\max_{g\in G}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}g(x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq n\delta+\sqrt{2n\log|G|}.

Taking the supremum over
\bar{x}\in X^{n}
, we obtain

r_{Q}(n)\leq n\delta+\sqrt{2n\log N_{Q,\delta}(n)}.

Hence

\frac{r_{Q}(n)}{n}\leq\delta+\sqrt{\frac{2\log N_{Q,\delta}(n)}{n}}\leq\delta+\sqrt{2C_{\delta,d(\delta/4)}}\,\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}.

Now fix
0<\eta<1
and put
\delta=\eta/2
. Since

\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\longrightarrow 0,

there is an integer

N=N(\eta,d(\cdot))

such that, for every
n\geq N
,

\sqrt{2C_{\eta/2,d(\eta/8)}}\,\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}<\frac{\eta}{2}.

Consequently,

\frac{r_{Q}(n)}{n}<\eta

for every
n\geq N
and every class
Q
satisfying

vc_{\epsilon}(Q)\leq d(\epsilon)\qquad\text{for all }\epsilon>0.

The following lemma quantitatively expresses the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) of Fact
3.9
.

Lemma 3.11

(Shattering implies a lower bound for the Rademacher mean width)
.

Let
Q\subseteq[0,1]^{X}
, and let
0\leq r<s\leq 1
.
If
Q

(r,s)
-shatters

x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in X
, then

r_{Q}(m)\geq\frac{s-r}{2}\,m.

Equivalently, if

\frac{r_{Q}(m)}{m}<\frac{s-r}{2},

then
Q
does not
(r,s)
-shatter
m
points.

Proof.

Put

t:=\frac{r+s}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta:=\frac{s-r}{2}.

Since
Q

(r,s)
-shatters

x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
, for every

\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})\in\{-1,+1\}^{m}

there is
q_{\sigma}\in Q
such that

\sigma_{i}\bigl(q_{\sigma}(x_{i})-t\bigr)\geq\delta\qquad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,m.

Hence

\displaystyle\sup_{q\in Q}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}q(x_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}q_{\sigma}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=t\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\bigl(q_{\sigma}(x_{i})-t\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq t\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}+\delta m.

Taking expectation with respect to the independent Rademacher
variables
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup_{q\in Q}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}q(x_{i})\right]\geq\delta m,

because

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\right]=0.

By the definition of
r_{Q}(m)
,

r_{Q}(m)\geq\delta m=\frac{s-r}{2}\,m.

This proves the lemma.
∎

We recall that Remark
3.10
and Lemma
3.11
above are not necessary for complete theories and are used only to prove the preservation of NIP for arbitrary theories.

In the following, we connect the notion of the Rademacher mean width with our main question.

Fix a formula
\varphi(x;y)
. Set
X=M_{0}^{\Omega}
and

Q=\{\phi_{a}:a\in M\},

where

\phi_{a}(f)=\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f;a^{\Omega})\rrbracket.

Fix a family of equal-measure partitions

\mathcal{B}_{k}=(B_{k,1},\ldots,B_{k,k})\qquad(k\geq 1)

of
\Omega
, with

\mathbb{P}(B_{k,j})=\frac{1}{k},

such that whenever
k\mid\ell
, the partition

\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
refines
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.
Such a family can be chosen on an atomless probability space.

For
a\in M
and an event
B
, put

\phi_{a,B}(f)=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\llbracket\varphi(f;a^{\Omega})\rrbracket\cap B\bigr).

For each
k\geq 1
, define

Q_{k}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\phi_{a_{j},B_{k,j}}:\ a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in M\right\}.

Put

\widehat{Q}=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}Q_{k!}.

Lemma 3.12
.

Under the above assumptions and notation,

r_{Q_{k}}(n)\leq r_{Q}(n)
for all
n
.

Proof.

For simplicity, and without loss of generality, we may assume
k=2
.
(Anywhere in the proof where
2
appears, it can be replaced by
k
to obtain the general case.) Assume that
\mu(B)=\mu(B^{c})=1/2
. Note that the following relation holds for every
a
and
f
:
\phi_{a}(f)=\phi_{a,B}(f)+\phi_{a,B^{c}}(f)
.
We also observe that:

\sup_{c,d}(\phi_{c,B}(f)+\phi_{d,B^{c}}(f))=\sup_{c}\phi_{c,B}(f)+\sup_{d}\phi_{d,B^{c}}(f)
.
Therefore, by the linearity of
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}
, we have:

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\sup_{c}\sum_{1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{c,B}(f_{i})+\sup_{d}\sum_{1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{d,B^{c}}(f_{i})]=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\sup_{c}\sum_{1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{c,B}(f_{i})]+\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\sup_{d}\sum_{1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\phi_{d,B^{c}}(f_{i})]

for any
\bar{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\in M_{0}^{\Omega}
.
Therefore,
r_{Q^{\prime}}(n)\leqslant r_{Q_{1}}(n)+r_{Q_{2}}(n)
for all
n
.

We will show that
r_{Q_{1}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}r_{Q}
and similarly
r_{Q_{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}r_{Q}
. To summarise,
r_{Q^{\prime}}\leqslant r_{Q}
.

For this, define
g_{a,f_{i}}(\omega):=1_{\{\phi(f_{i}(\omega),a)\text{ is true}\}}
.
Define the measure
\mu^{\prime}
via
\mu^{\prime}(A):=2\mu(A\cap B)
for any event
A
. Then,
\phi_{a,B}(f_{i})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}g_{a,f_{i}}d\mu^{\prime}
, and by linearity

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\sup_{a}\sum_{1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(\frac{1}{2}\int g_{a,f_{i}}d\mu^{\prime})]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\sup_{a}\sum_{1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(\int g_{a,f_{i}}d\mu^{\prime})]

for any
\bar{f}
.

Claim
.

For each
i\leq n
, there exists
f_{i}^{\prime}\in M_{0}^{\Omega}
such that for every
a\in M
,

\int_{\Omega}g_{a,f_{i}}\,d\mu=\int_{\Omega}g_{a,f_{i}^{\prime}}\,d\mu^{\prime}.

Proof of Claim.

Recall that
\mu^{\prime}(A)=2\mu(A\cap B)
for every event
A
, where
B\subseteq\Omega
is a measurable set with
\mu(B)=\frac{1}{2}
. For
i\leq n
, write the simple random element
f_{i}
in the form

f_{i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}c_{i,\ell}^{A_{i,\ell}},

where
A_{i,1},\ldots,A_{i,m_{i}}
is a measurable partition of
\Omega
and
c_{i,1},\ldots,c_{i,m_{i}}\in M
. Since
\mu^{\prime}
is atomless and

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu(A_{i,\ell})=1,

there is a measurable partition

C_{i,1},\ldots,C_{i,m_{i}}

of
\Omega
such that

\mu^{\prime}(C_{i,\ell})=\mu(A_{i,\ell})\qquad(\ell=1,\ldots,m_{i}).

Define

f_{i}^{\prime}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}c_{i,\ell}^{C_{i,\ell}}\in M_{0}^{\Omega}.

Then, for every
a\in M
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}g_{a,f_{i}}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu(A_{i,\ell})\,\mathbf{1}_{\{M\models\varphi(c_{i,\ell},a)\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m_{i}}\mu^{\prime}(C_{i,\ell})\,\mathbf{1}_{\{M\models\varphi(c_{i,\ell},a)\}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}g_{a,f_{i}^{\prime}}\,d\mu^{\prime}.

Thus the desired equality holds.
∎

To summarise, since the functions
f_{i}
are simple, constructing the corresponding
f_{i}^{\prime}
from the
f_{i}
is straightforward, and conversely, one can recover the
f_{i}
from the
f_{i}^{\prime}
. Therefore,

r_{Q_{1}}(n)=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\bar{f}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\sup_{a}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(\int g_{a,f_{i}^{\prime}}d\mu^{\prime})]=

\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\bar{f}}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\sup_{a}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}(\int g_{a,f_{i}}d\mu)]=\frac{1}{2}r_{Q}(n).

A similar argument holds for
Q_{2}
. Therefore, this suffices for the proof.
∎

Note that in the above lemma, the given bound is independent of
k
and hence holds uniformly for every
k\geq 2
. Therefore, by Theorem
3.5
, assuming
T
is
NIP
, since
Q
is a VC class, it follows that
Q^{\prime}
is also a VC class.

We now only need the following simple lemma:

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
Q_{1}\subseteq Q_{2}\subseteq\dots
be an increasing sequence of function classes from
X
to
[0,1]
, and let
\hat{Q}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}Q_{n}
. Then for any sample size
k\geq 1
,

r_{\hat{Q}}(k)=\sup_{n\geq 1}r_{Q_{n}}(k).

Proof.

Fix a sample

\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in X^{k}.

For each
n\geq 1
, define

Z_{n}(\sigma):=\sup_{f\in Q_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}f(x_{i}),\qquad\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{k}.

Since
Q_{n}\subseteq Q_{n+1}
, we have

Z_{n}(\sigma)\leq Z_{n+1}(\sigma)

for every
\sigma
. Moreover,

-k\leq Z_{n}(\sigma)\leq k,

because
f(x_{i})\in[0,1]
for every
f\in Q_{n}
.

Since

\widehat{Q}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}Q_{n},

we have, for every
\sigma
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}Z_{n}(\sigma)=\sup_{f\in\widehat{Q}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}f(x_{i}).

Thus, by the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle w_{R}(\widehat{Q}(\bar{x}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left[\sup_{f\in\widehat{Q}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}f(x_{i})\right]

Lemma 3.14

(Simultaneous approximation by nested equal-measure partitions)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be an atomless probability
space whose probability algebra is separable with respect to
the metric

Let

be a simple randomization over this probability
space.

There exists a fixed sequence of measurable partitions

\mathcal{B}_{2^{k}}=(B_{2^{k},1},\ldots,B_{2^{k},2^{k}}),\qquad k\geq 0,

such that

\mathbb{P}(B_{2^{k},j})=2^{-k}\qquad(1\leq j\leq 2^{k}),

each
\mathcal{B}_{2^{k+1}}
refines
\mathcal{B}_{2^{k}}
, and the
algebra generated by these partitions is dense in the probability
algebra with respect to
d_{\mathbb{P}}
.

Moreover, for every finite family

g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in M_{0}^{\Omega}
and every
\epsilon>0
,
there exist
k\geq 0
and

g_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,g_{m}^{\prime}\in M_{0}^{\Omega}
such that each
g_{i}^{\prime}
is
constant on every cell of
\mathcal{B}_{2^{k}}
and

where

In particular, the same partition
\mathcal{B}_{2^{k}}
works
simultaneously for all
g_{i}^{\prime}
.

Furthermore, for every
L
-formula
\varphi(x;y)
and every

,

\left|\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f;g_{i})\rrbracket-\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f;g_{i}^{\prime})\rrbracket\right|\leq d_{K}(g_{i},g_{i}^{\prime})<\epsilon.

Since the probability algebra is atomless and separable, it is
isomorphic, as a probability algebra, to the Lebesgue probability
algebra on
[0,1]
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{2^{k}}
be the inverse images,
under such an isomorphism, of the standard dyadic partitions
of

into

These partitions are nested, and the algebra generated by them
is dense in the probability algebra with respect to

d_{\mathbb{P}}
.

Fix

and
\epsilon>0
.
Since the

are simple random variables, there is a finite
measurable partition

of
\Omega
such that every

is constant on every

.
Write

By the density established above, choose

in the algebra generated by the dyadic

Replace the

by the disjoint sets

and

D_{\ell}=\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{j<\ell}D_{j}.

Then
D_{1},\ldots,D_{\ell}
form a measurable partition, and
each
D_{j}
belongs to the algebra generated by the dyadic
partitions.

Put

Outside

agrees with membership
in

. Since

is a partition, outside

occurs, and the corresponding

has the same index. Consequently, the original tuple

(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
agrees with the tuple defined by assigning

on

.

Since there are finitely many

and the dyadic algebras
are increasing, there is a single

Define

Each

is constant on every cell of
\mathcal{B}_{2^{k}}
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle d_{K}(g_{i},g_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(E)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}(A_{j}\mathbin{\triangle}C_{j})

\displaystyle<\epsilon

Finally, the events

\llbracket\varphi(f;g_{i})\rrbracket
and

\llbracket\varphi(f;g_{i}^{\prime})\rrbracket

and

differ. Hence

\left|\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f;g_{i})\rrbracket-\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f;g_{i}^{\prime})\rrbracket\right|\leq d_{K}(g_{i},g_{i}^{\prime})<\epsilon.

(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})
-shattered.

Hence every predicate

\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(x;y)\rrbracket

is NIP. By strong quantifier elimination
for randomizations
[
8
, Theorem 2.9]
,
event coding, and preservation of NIP
under continuous connectives and
uniform limits,
T^{R}
is NIP.
∎

3.2
Preservation of Stability

The reader might reasonably expect that the method presented above could also be applied in proving the preservation of stability.
In fact, the above proof can be adapted to yield a proof for the stable case as well, but using the
notions of Littlestone dimension from learning theory—which correspond to the Shelah
2
-rank
in model theory—and sequential Rademacher mean width
instead of VC-dimension and Rademacher mean width used in the NIP case.

Next, we recall the notion of stability in continuous logic.

Definition 3.20
.

Let
T_{c}
be a complete continuous theory and
\varphi(x;y)
a partitioned formula.

(i) We say that
\varphi(x;y)
has the
OP
(order property) if there are
r<s\leq 1
and sequences
(a_{i}:i<\omega)
and
(b_{j}:j<\omega)
of parameters (in the monster model) such that
\varphi(a_{i};b_{j})\leq r
if
i<j
and
\varphi(a_{i};b_{j})\geq s
otherwise. We say that
\varphi(x;y)
is stable if it has no
OP
.

(ii) We say that
T_{c}
has the
OP
(or is unstable) if there is some formula
\varphi(x;y)
with
OP
.
T_{c}
is stable if it has no
OP
.

Notice that by setting
r=0
and
s=1
in Definition
3.20
, we obtain exactly the definition of stable/unstable in classical logic.

Remark 3.21
.

By compactness,
T_{c}
has
OP
if there are a formula
\varphi(x;y)
and
r<s\leq 1
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and each (pre-)model
M
, there are
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in M
such that
\varphi(a_{i};b_{j})\leq r
if
i<j
, and
\varphi(a_{i},b_{j})\geq s
otherwise.

Let
\mu(x)={\rm Ave}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
and
\nu(y)={\rm Ave}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})
be average measures. In the following, we write

\mu\otimes\nu(\phi(x;y)):=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\leq k}\mu(\phi(x;b_{j}))=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\leq k}\nu(\phi(a_{i};y))=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\leq k}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\leq k}\phi(a_{i};b_{j}).

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
T
be a stable theory and
M\models T
.
For every
L
-formula
\phi(x;y)
and every

0\leq r<s\leq 1
, there is a natural number
N

such that there are no sequences

(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N})\quad\text{and}\quad(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N})

of average measures of elements in
M
satisfying

\mu_{i}\otimes\nu_{j}(\phi(x;y))\leq r\quad\text{if }i<j,

and

\mu_{i}\otimes\nu_{j}(\phi(x;y))\geq s\quad\text{if }i\geq j.

Proof.

Suppose, towards a contradiction, that the conclusion fails.
Then for arbitrarily large
N
there are average measures

\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N}

such that

\mu_{i}\otimes\nu_{j}(\phi(x;y))\leq r\quad\text{if }i<j,

and

\mu_{i}\otimes\nu_{j}(\phi(x;y))\geq s\quad\text{if }i\geq j.

Put

\delta:=\frac{s-r}{8}.

Since
T
is stable, it is NIP. Applying
Fact
2.9
to
\phi(x;y)
and to the
opposite formula
\phi^{\mathrm{opp}}(y;x)
, we may
choose fixed lengths for approximating both families of
measures, independently of
N
. By repeating entries
in the approximating tuples, we may assume that the two
lengths are the same integer
k
.

Thus there are average measures

\widehat{\mu}_{i}=\operatorname{Ave}(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,k}),\qquad\widehat{\nu}_{j}=\operatorname{Ave}(b_{j,1},\ldots,b_{j,k})

such that

\sup_{b\in M}\left|\mu_{i}(\phi(x;b))-\widehat{\mu}_{i}(\phi(x;b))\right|\leq\delta

and

\sup_{a\in M}\left|\nu_{j}(\phi(a;y))-\widehat{\nu}_{j}(\phi(a;y))\right|\leq\delta.

Replacing the first measure changes the product average
by at most
\delta
, and replacing the second changes
it by at most another
\delta
. Hence

\left|\mu_{i}\otimes\nu_{j}(\phi(x;y))-\widehat{\mu}_{i}\otimes\widehat{\nu}_{j}(\phi(x;y))\right|\leq 2\delta.

Consequently,

\widehat{\mu}_{i}\otimes\widehat{\nu}_{j}(\phi(x;y))\leq r+2\delta\quad\text{if }i<j,

whereas

\widehat{\mu}_{i}\otimes\widehat{\nu}_{j}(\phi(x;y))\geq s-2\delta\quad\text{if }i\geq j.

Our choice of
\delta
ensures that

r+2\delta<s-2\delta.

Let
m\geq 1
be arbitrary. Since the assumed
configurations exist for arbitrarily large
N
, we may
choose
N
sufficiently large to apply
Fact
2.5
to the blocks

\bar{a}_{i}=(a_{i,p})_{p=1}^{k},\qquad\bar{b}_{i}=(b_{i,q})_{q=1}^{k},

with target length
2m
.

We obtain indices

i_{1}<\cdots<i_{2m}

and matrices
U,V\in\{0,1\}^{k\times k}
such that,
for every
u<v
,
p\leq k
, and
q\leq k
,

\phi(a_{i_{u},p};b_{i_{v},q})=U_{pq},\qquad\phi(a_{i_{v},p};b_{i_{u},q})=V_{pq}.

By the definition of the average measures and the
homogeneity of the cross-block patterns,

\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{p,q=1}^{k}U_{pq}=\widehat{\mu}_{i_{u}}\otimes\widehat{\nu}_{i_{v}}(\phi(x;y))\leq r+2\delta

for every
u<v
, while

\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{p,q=1}^{k}V_{pq}=\widehat{\mu}_{i_{v}}\otimes\widehat{\nu}_{i_{u}}(\phi(x;y))\geq s-2\delta.

It follows that

\sum_{p,q=1}^{k}U_{pq}<\sum_{p,q=1}^{k}V_{pq}.

Since all entries of
U
and
V
belong to

\{0,1\}
, there are
p,q\leq k
such that

U_{pq}=0,\qquad V_{pq}=1.

Define

c_{t}:=a_{i_{2t},p},\qquad d_{u}:=b_{i_{2u-1},q}\qquad(1\leq t,u\leq m).

If
t<u
, then
2t<2u-1
, so the forward
cross-block pattern gives

\neg\phi(c_{t};d_{u}).

If
t\geq u
, then
2t>2u-1
, so the reverse
cross-block pattern gives

\phi(c_{t};d_{u}).

Therefore,

\phi(c_{t};d_{u})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t\geq u.

Since
m
was arbitrary,
\phi(x;y)
has the order
property. This contradicts the stability of
T
.
Hence the required natural number
N
exists.
∎

Remark 3.23
.

Similar to the
NIP
case (cf. Remark
3.6
),
with the condition that at least one of the variables
x
or
y
is interpreted only by elements of the classical models,
Theorem
3.22
provides a quantitative bound for not having order property for formula
{\mathbb{P}}\llbracket\phi(x;y)\rrbracket
.

Sequential Rademacher Mean Width and Littlestone Dimension

The preservation of stability under randomization was established for classical theories in Theorem 5.14 of
[
8
]
and for continuous theories in Theorem 4.9 of
[
6
]
. In particular, if
T
is stable, then so is its randomization
T^{R}
.
Using ideas from the present paper,
this follows from Theorem
3.22
(instead of Theorem
3.5
), a stable version of Proposition
3.15
, Proposition
2.6
and elimination of quantifiers, similar to the proof of Corollary
3.16
.
Of course, to prove a proposition analogous to Proposition
3.15
in the stable case, we need the
notion of Littlestone dimension (which corresponds to Shelah
2
-rank) and Sequential Rademacher mean width instead of VC-dimension
and Rademacher mean width used in the NIP case, which we address in this paper.

Definition 3.24

(Sequential Rademacher mean width)
.

Let
X
be a set and let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq[0,1]^{X}
.
For
n\geq 1
, an
X
-valued tree of depth
n
is a family

\mathbf{x}=\bigl(x_{t}(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{t-1})\bigr)_{t=1}^{n},

where

x_{t}:\{-1,+1\}^{t-1}\longrightarrow X.

Define the sequential Rademacher mean width of
\mathcal{F}
by

\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F})=\sup_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\epsilon_{t}f\bigl(x_{t}(\epsilon_{<t})\bigr)\right],

where
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}
are independent
Rademacher random variables and

\epsilon_{<t}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{t-1})
.

We use the following qualitative consequence of
[
17
, Theorem 1]
.

Fact 3.25
.

If
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{X}
has finite Littlestone dimension, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{H})}{n}=0.

Proof.

For every
0<\epsilon\leq 1/2
, the binary-valued
nature of
\mathcal{H}
implies

\operatorname{sfat}_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{H})\leq\operatorname{Ldim}(\mathcal{H})<\infty.

Hence
\mathcal{H}
has finite sequential
fat-shattering dimension at every positive scale.
The asserted convergence now follows from

[
17
, Theorem 1]
.
∎

Fix an
L
-formula
\varphi(x;y)
, a model
M\models T
,
and a simple randomization
M_{0}^{\Omega}
of
M
. Put

X=M_{0}^{\Omega}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q=\{\varphi_{b}:b\in M^{|y|}\},

where

\varphi_{b}(f)=\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f;b^{\Omega})\rrbracket.

We first show that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(Q)}{n}=0.

Fix
\eta>0
. For each
f\in M_{0}^{\Omega}
, let

\mu_{f}
be the finitely supported Keisler measure defined by

\mu_{f}(\varphi(x;b))=\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f;b^{\Omega})\rrbracket.

Since
\mu_{f}
is a finite convex combination of realized types,
it is generically stable. By Fact
2.9
, there is
an integer
k
, independent of
f
, such that for every

f\in M_{0}^{\Omega}
there is a tuple

\bar{a}_{f}=(a_{f,1},\ldots,a_{f,k})\in M^{k}
satisfying

\sup_{b\in M^{|y|}}\left|\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f;b^{\Omega})\rrbracket-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}_{\varphi(a_{f,i};b)}\right|\leq\eta.

For this fixed
k
, let
X_{k}=M^{k}
and define

F_{b}(\bar{a})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}_{\varphi(a_{i};b)}\qquad(\bar{a}\in M^{k},\ b\in M^{|y|}).

Write

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\{F_{b}:b\in M^{|y|}\}.

For each
1\leq\ell\leq k
, define the binary class

\mathcal{H}_{k,\ell}=\{h_{b,\ell}:b\in M^{|y|}\},

where

h_{b,\ell}(\bar{a})=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}_{\varphi(a_{i};b)}\geq\ell.

Notice that

F_{b}(\bar{a})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}h_{b,\ell}(\bar{a}).

We claim that every
\mathcal{H}_{k,\ell}
has finite Littlestone
dimension. Indeed, the condition

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}_{\varphi(a_{i};b)}\geq\ell

is expressed by an
L
-formula

\theta_{k,\ell}(\bar{x};y)
. If this formula had arbitrarily
long order patterns, setting

\mu_{i}=\operatorname{Ave}(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,k})\quad\text{and}\quad\nu_{j}=\operatorname{Ave}(b_{j})

would give

\mu_{i}\otimes\nu_{j}(\varphi(x;y))\leq\frac{\ell-1}{k}\quad\text{if }i<j,

and

\mu_{i}\otimes\nu_{j}(\varphi(x;y))\geq\frac{\ell}{k}\quad\text{if }i\geq j.

This contradicts Theorem
3.22
, applied with

r=(\ell-1)/k
and
s=\ell/k
.
Thus
\theta_{k,\ell}
is stable, and its Littlestone dimension
is finite.

By Fact
3.25
, for each

\ell\leq k
,

\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{H}_{k,\ell})=o(n).

Using (
Sequential Rademacher Mean Width and Littlestone Dimension
) and the subadditivity of
sequential Rademacher mean width, we obtain

\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F}_{k})\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{H}_{k,\ell})=o(n).

Now consider an arbitrary
X
-valued tree

\mathbf{f}=(f_{t}(\epsilon_{<t}))_{t=1}^{n}
.
At every node, choose the tuple

\bar{a}_{f_{t}(\epsilon_{<t})}
supplied by
(
Sequential Rademacher Mean Width and Littlestone Dimension
). These tuples form an
X_{k}
-valued tree.
Consequently, (
Sequential Rademacher Mean Width and Littlestone Dimension
) gives

\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(Q)\leq n\eta+\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\mathcal{F}_{k}).

Dividing by
n
, using (
Sequential Rademacher Mean Width and Littlestone Dimension
), and then letting

\eta
tend to zero proves (
Sequential Rademacher Mean Width and Littlestone Dimension
).

We next establish the sequential analogue of the Rademacher
mean-width bound used in the NIP argument. Use the same nested
family of equal-measure partitions

\mathcal{B}_{k}=(B_{k,1},\ldots,B_{k,k})

as in the definition of
Q_{k}
. Recall that

\varphi_{a,B}(f)=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\llbracket\varphi(f;a^{\Omega})\rrbracket\cap B\bigr)

and

Q_{k}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\varphi_{a_{j},B_{k,j}}:a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in M\right\}.

Then

\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(Q_{k})\leq\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(Q).

To see this, fix an
X
-valued tree

\mathbf{f}=(f_{t}(\epsilon_{<t}))_{t=1}^{n}
.
For each cell
B_{k,j}
, let
\mathbb{P}_{j}
be the
normalized restriction of
\mathbb{P}
to
B_{k,j}
.
Since the random variables
f_{t}(\epsilon_{<t})
are simple
and the probability space is atomless, for each node there is
a simple random variable
f^{\prime}_{t,j}(\epsilon_{<t})
on

\Omega
having the same distribution under
\mathbb{P}

as
f_{t}(\epsilon_{<t})
has under
\mathbb{P}_{j}
.
Consequently, for every
a\in M
,

\varphi_{a,B_{k,j}}\bigl(f_{t}(\epsilon_{<t})\bigr)=\frac{1}{k}\varphi_{a}\bigl(f^{\prime}_{t,j}(\epsilon_{<t})\bigr).

The family
(f^{\prime}_{t,j})_{t=1}^{n}
is again an
X
-valued
tree. Since the parameters
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
in the definition
of
Q_{k}
can be optimized independently, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\sup_{q\in Q_{k}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\epsilon_{t}q\bigl(f_{t}(\epsilon_{<t})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{k}\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(Q)=\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(Q).

Taking the supremum over all such trees proves
(
Sequential Rademacher Mean Width and Littlestone Dimension
).

As in the NIP proof, put

\widehat{Q}=\bigcup_{m\geq 1}Q_{m!}.

The classes
Q_{m!}
are increasing. It follows from
(
Sequential Rademacher Mean Width and Littlestone Dimension
) that

\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\widehat{Q})\leq\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(Q).

In particular, by (
Sequential Rademacher Mean Width and Littlestone Dimension
),

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{R}_{n}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\widehat{Q})}{n}=0.

We can now prove preservation of stability.

Theorem 3.26
.

For a complete theory
T
,
if
T
is stable, then
T^{R}
is stable.

Proof.

Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
T^{R}
is not stable.
By quantifier elimination for randomizations and the order-property
criterion for stability, there are an
L
-formula

\varphi(x;y)
and
0\leq r<s\leq 1
such that, for every

N
, there are

f_{1},\ldots,f_{N},\ g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}\in M_{0}^{\Omega}

with

\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f_{i};g_{j})\rrbracket\leq r\quad\text{if }i<j,

and

\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f_{i};g_{j})\rrbracket\geq s\quad\text{if }i\geq j.

Let

\eta=\frac{s-r}{8}.

Fix
N
. By the equal-weight approximation lemma
(Lemma
3.14
), applied to the finite
family
g_{1},\ldots,g_{N}
, we may choose a common equal-measure
partition

\mathcal{B}_{k}=(B_{k,1},\ldots,B_{k,k})

and random variables
g^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,g^{\prime}_{N}
, each constant on
the cells of this partition, such that

\left|\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f_{i};g_{j})\rrbracket-\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f_{i};g^{\prime}_{j})\rrbracket\right|<\eta

for every
i,j\leq N
.

Write

g^{\prime}_{j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}b_{j,\ell}^{B_{k,\ell}},\qquad b_{j,\ell}\in M,

and put

q_{j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\varphi_{b_{j,\ell},B_{k,\ell}}.

Then
q_{j}\in Q_{k}\subseteq\widehat{Q}
, and

q_{j}(f_{i})=\mathbb{P}\llbracket\varphi(f_{i};g^{\prime}_{j})\rrbracket.

It follows from (
Proof.
) and
(
Proof.
) that

q_{j}(f_{i})\leq r+\eta\quad\text{if }i<j,

and

q_{j}(f_{i})\geq s-\eta\quad\text{if }i\geq j.

Set

r^{\prime}=r+\eta,\qquad s^{\prime}=s-\eta.

Then

s^{\prime}-r^{\prime}=(s-r)-2\eta>0.

Let
d\geq 1
. Choose
N\geq 2^{d}
and take the corresponding
configuration above. The ladder pattern
(
Proof.
)–(
Proof.
) yields a binary tree of
depth
d
whose nodes are selected from

f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}
and which is

(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})
-shattered by
\widehat{Q}
.
Indeed, recursively query a median point
f_{i}
among the
remaining indices
j
: by (
Proof.
)–(
Proof.
),
the two outcomes retain respectively the indices
j>i

and
j\leq i
. Since
N\geq 2^{d}
, every path of length
d

has at least one remaining witness
q_{j}
.

Write

c=\frac{r^{\prime}+s^{\prime}}{2},\qquad\alpha=\frac{s^{\prime}-r^{\prime}}{2}.

For every path

\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{d})\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}
,
choose a witness
q_{\epsilon}\in\widehat{Q}
for the tree,
with the convention that
\epsilon_{t}=+1
corresponds to the
upper threshold and
\epsilon_{t}=-1
to the lower threshold.
Then

\epsilon_{t}\bigl(q_{\epsilon}(x_{t}(\epsilon_{<t}))-c\bigr)\geq\alpha\qquad(1\leq t\leq d).

Therefore,

\sup_{q\in\widehat{Q}}\sum_{t=1}^{d}\epsilon_{t}q(x_{t}(\epsilon_{<t}))\geq c\sum_{t=1}^{d}\epsilon_{t}+\alpha d.

Taking expectation over
\epsilon
, and using

\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{d}\epsilon_{t}]=0
, gives

\mathfrak{R}_{d}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\widehat{Q})\geq\alpha d=\frac{s^{\prime}-r^{\prime}}{2}\,d.

By (
Sequential Rademacher Mean Width and Littlestone Dimension
), for all sufficiently large

,

\frac{\mathfrak{R}_{d}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\widehat{Q})}{d}<\frac{s^{\prime}-r^{\prime}}{2}.

This contradicts (
Proof.
). Hence

Remark 3.27
.

Although the proof of Theorem
3.26
works for the preservation of stability for complete theories, similarly to the
NIP
case (i.e. Proposition
3.15
)
one can find quantitative bounds for
sequential Rademacher mean width and the Littlestone dimension

3.3
NSOP
and Shelah’s Theorem for
T^{R}

Before the discussion, we emphasize that in this section
T
, and consequently
T^{R}
, are
complete
theories in classical first-order logic and continuous logic, respectively.
The concept of strict order property (
SOP
) has various generalizations in continuous logic, e.g.
[
14
, Definition 1.1(i)]
. This notion was further studied in
[
15
]
. The key feature of this property is highlighted within the classical model theory, by a theorem of Shelah which states a first-order complete theory is stable if and only if it is
NIP
and
NSOP
. In this part of the paper we show that, in the context of randomization, Definition
[
14
, Definition 1.1(i)]
is the weakest condition amongst its counterpart definitions which satisfies the Shelah’s theorem. That is,
T^{R}
is stable if and only if it is
NIP
and
NSOP
.
We first review the notion of
SOP
of
[
15
]
for continuous logic.

In the following,
\cal U
and
\cal C
denote the monster models of
T
and
T^{R}
, respectively. Recall that in continuous logic
1
is “False” and
0
is “True”, but this is a minor issue, and for consistency in notation, we continue to use
\varphi=1
if
\models\varphi
and
\varphi=0
if
\models\neg\varphi
.

Definition 3.28

(
[
14
]
Definition 1.1(i), and
[
15
]
, Definition 3.3)
.

Let
T_{c}
be a complete continuous theory,
\varphi(x;y)
a partitioned formula and
\epsilon>0
.

(i)
We say that the formula
\varphi(x;y)
has the
\epsilon
-strict order property
(
\epsilon
-
SOP
) if there exists a sequence

(a_{i}b_{i}:i<\omega)
in the monster model
\cal C
of
T_{c}
such that for any
b\in\cal C
and for all
i<j
,

\varphi(b;a_{i})\leqslant\varphi(b;a_{i+1}),\ \mbox{ and }\ \varphi(b_{j};a_{i})+\epsilon\leqslant\varphi(b_{i+1};a_{j}).

(ii) We say that the formula
\varphi(x;y)
has the
strict order property (
SOP
) if it has
\epsilon
-
SOP
for
some
\epsilon>0
.

(iii) We say that
T_{c}
has
SOP
if there is some formula
\varphi(x;y)

with
SOP
. We say that
T_{c}
has the
NSOP
if it does not have the
SOP
.

Proposition 3.29
.

If
T
has
SOP
then so has
T^{R}
.

Proof.

Let
\Omega
be an atomless probability space and
{\cal U}_{0}^{\Omega}
the simple pre-model with underlying probability space
\Omega
(as in Subsection 2.2).
As
T
has
SOP
, there are a formula
\phi(x;y)
and sequences
(a_{i}:i<\omega)
and
(b_{j}:j<\omega)
of elements in
\cal U
such that
\phi(b;a_{i})\leq\phi(b;a_{i+1})
for any
b\in\mathcal{U}
, and
\models\neg\phi(b_{i+1};a_{i})\wedge\phi(b_{i+1};a_{i+1})
for each
i<\omega
. Therefore,

{\mathbb{P}}\llbracket\phi(b;a_{i})\rrbracket\leq{\mathbb{P}}\llbracket\phi(b;a_{i+1})\rrbracket
, for any
b\in\cal U
. (Notice that in the previous sentence
b
is the constant map
f_{b}:\Omega\to\{b\}
defined by
t\mapsto b
. Similarly for the
