Multi-Triplon Excitations of Hubbard Ladders with Site-Dependent Potentials
Nobuya Maeshima 1,2 and Ken-ichi Hino 2,1

Abstract
We study low-lying spin-singlet excitations of two-leg Hubbard ladders with site-dependent potentials. Using general formulas of the charge disproportionation induced by the site-dependent potentials, we derive the contributions of spin degrees of freedom to the spectral functions such as the dynamical charge structure factor N ⁡ ( 𝒌 , ω ) N(\bm{k},\omega) and the optical conductivity σ γ ​ ( ω ) \sigma_{\gamma}(\omega) along the γ ( = x , y ) \gamma(=x,y) -direction of the two-leg ladders. Numerical results obtained by the Lanczos diagonalization method have clarified that the multi-triplon singlet states, including two- and three-triplon excitations, can be detected by observing these quantities. Furthermore, we have found that ladders with random potentials also have non-negligible contributions of these excitations to σ x ​ ( ω ) \sigma_{x}(\omega) .

Introduction

Quantum spin systems have long been regarded an ideal arena for exploring quantum many-body phenomena. In particular,
S=1/2
two-leg spin ladders are notable for their tractability and typical low-dimensional features of quantum systems, such as disordered ground states, finite spin gaps, and non-trivial excitations
[
1
,
2
]
. The elementary excitations in
S=1/2
two-leg ladders are quasiparticles called ”triplons,” each carrying a spin of
S=1

[
3
]
.
The triplon state is regarded as a quasiparticle in a wide class of quantum spin systems with finite spin gaps, and its dynamical properties have been examined in various materials
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. Also, in two-leg ladders, experimental studies using inelastic neutron scattering (INS) have successfully identified the finite energy gap and the single-triplon dispersion
[
9
,
10
]
.

Recent experimental achievements strongly indicate that two-leg spin ladders are excellent platforms for studying two-triplon states; high-resolution analysis of INS experiments has confirmed the existence of a two-triplon continuum and bound states in the spin-triplet (
S=1
) sector of the excited states
[
11
,
12
]
. Besides these two-triplon states with
S=1
, which are detectable with INS, two-triplon singlet (
S=0
) excitations have also been observed using Raman spectroscopy
[
13
,
14
]
and phonon-assisted optical absorption
[
15
]
. In addition, resonant inelastic x-ray scattering (RIXS) has been employed to explore two-triplon
S=0
excitations
[
16
,
17
]
. These experimental results have been compared with theoretical studies on two-triplon states
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
.

Turning our attention to recent studies of other quasiparticles in quantum magnets, we can see various experimental challenges to observe excited states having three or more numbers of quasiparticles, contributing to the spectral properties of these systems in high-energy regions.
The majority of these studies are devoted to bound states formed by strong inter-quasiparticle interactions and are investigated using INS
[
24
,
25
]
, THz spectroscopy
[
26
,
27
]
, and infrared magnetospectroscopy
[
28
]
.
Additionally, multi-quasiparticle continuum states composed of weakly interacting (or free) quasiparticles have also been examined in relation to the high-energy spectral components of INS or RIXS in 1D chains
[
29
,
30
]
, or those of RIXS in a kagome antiferromagnet
[
31
]
.
As for the triplon states, in contrast, it has been only proposed theoretically that three-triplon states of the ladder can appear in the RIXS intensity
[
32
]
. Thus, methods to detect multi-triplon states are still lacking.

We present an alternative framework to investigate multi-triplon excitations of the two-leg ladders in the
S=0
sector. Our approach is based on an analytical treatment for the one-dimensional (1D) ionic Hubbard model, a single-band Hubbard chain with an alternating potential
[
33
]
, where the charge disproportionation at each site is represented in terms of the spin operators of the corresponding Heisenberg model.
In this work, the analytical method is extended to Hubbard models on general lattices and applied to the two-leg Hubbard ladders with site-dependent potentials.
We have analytically proved that the spin-degrees of freedom of the two-leg ladders can have finite contributions to the dynamical charge structure factor
N(\bm{k},\omega)
and the optical conductivity
\sigma_{\gamma}(\omega)
along the
\gamma(=x,y)
-direction of the ladders.

Then, we numerically demonstrate that these spectral functions probe the two and three-triplon excitations of two-leg ladders with periodic potentials and with strong rung coupling, where spin dimers called “rungs” weakly couple along one direction.
The observed excitations can be selected by tuning the periodicity of the site-dependent potential or the wave number perpendicular to the leg.
Obtained results of
N(\bm{k},\omega)
and
\sigma_{\gamma}(\omega)
calculated with the Lanczos diagonalization elucidate that
their spectral peaks lie in the two- and three-triplon continuum and the two-triplon singlet bound state.
Also, analytical results with bond operator method
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
are found to be consistent with our calculated results.
Furthermore, we investigate the optical conductivity
\sigma_{x}(\omega)
with random potential and show that observed spectral signatures result from these multi-triplon excitations.

In Sec.
2
, our theoretical framework for the single chain is extended to general lattices
and then applied to two-leg ladders.
In Sec.
3
, our numerical and analytical results are presented, and Section
4
is devoted to the conclusion.

2
Theory

2.1
General Hubbard models

Here, we explain the method, which is an extension of our previous framework for the 1D ionic Hubbard model
[
33
]
.
We employ the single-band Hubbard model at half-filling with a site-dependent potential.
The Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle{\cal H}=-\sum_{jl\tau}t_{jl}c^{\dagger}_{j\tau}c_{l\tau}+U\sum_{l}n_{l\uparrow}n_{l\downarrow}+\sum_{l}e_{l}(n_{i}-1),

(1)

where
c^{\dagger}_{j\tau}
(
c_{j\tau}
) is the creation (annihilation) operator of an electron with spin
\tau
at site
j
,
n_{j\tau}=c^{\dagger}_{j\tau}c_{j\tau}
, and
n_{j}=n_{j\uparrow}+n_{j\downarrow}
.
The parameter
U
gives the on-site Coulomb interaction, and
t_{jl}
represents the transfer integral between sites
j
and
l
.
In addition,
e_{j}
denotes the site-dependent potential at site
j
.
Here we set
t_{jj}=0
and
e_{j}=\epsilon_{j}+\phi_{j}
at site
j
,
where
\epsilon_{j}
is an intrinsic site-dependent potential and
\phi_{j}
is a virtually introduced scalar field.
For the large-
U
case of this model, we derive the Heisenberg Hamiltonian as
[
37
,
33
]

{\cal H}^{s}=\sum_{jl}K_{jl}\left(\bm{S}_{j}\cdot{\bf\it S}_{l}-\frac{1}{4}\right),

(2)

with the exchange interaction
K_{jl}=2t_{jl}^{2}/(U-e_{j}+e_{l})
.
The charge disproportionation
\delta n_{l}\equiv n_{l}-1
from the half-filling for
\phi_{l}=0
is evaluated as

\displaystyle\delta n^{s}_{l}=\left.\frac{\partial{\cal H}^{s}}{\partial\phi_{l}}\right|_{\phi_{l}=0}=\sum_{j}\eta_{lj}\left(\bm{S}_{l}\cdot\bm{S}_{j}-\frac{1}{4}\right),

(3)

where
\bm{S}_{j}
is the spin operator at site
j
, and the coefficient
\eta_{lj}
associating
\delta n^{s}_{l}
with the spin operators is given by

\displaystyle\eta_{lj}=\frac{8Ut^{2}_{lj}(\epsilon_{l}-\epsilon_{j})}{[U^{2}-(\epsilon_{l}-\epsilon_{j})^{2}]^{2}}.

(4)

Here, the superscript
s
of quantities means the contribution from the spin degrees of freedom.

Next, let us consider the dynamical charge structure factor
N(\bm{k},\omega)
of the Hubbard model (
1
) defined by

N(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}|\langle\alpha|n_{\bm{k}}|0\rangle|^{2}\delta(\omega-E_{\alpha}+E_{0}),

(5)

where
n_{\bm{k}}=\sum_{l}e^{-i{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{R}_{l}}n_{l}
,
{\bm{k}}
denotes the wave number,
N
is the system size, and
\bm{R}_{l}
represents the location of site
l
.
Its counterpart
N^{s}(\bm{k},\omega)
of the spin degrees of freedom is obtained by replacing
n_{\bm{k}}
in Eq. (
5
) with

\delta n^{s}_{\bm{k}}=\sum_{l}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}_{l}}\delta n^{s}_{l}=\sum_{l}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}_{l}}\sum_{j}\eta_{lj}\left(\bm{S}_{l}\cdot\bm{S}_{j}-\frac{1}{4}\right).

(6)

At the same time, the eigenstate
\ket{\alpha}
and the eigenenergy
E_{\alpha}
of the Hamiltonian (
1
)
are also replaced by their counterparts,
|\alpha\rangle^{s}
and
E_{\alpha}^{s}
, of the Heisenberg Hamiltonian (
2
).

Now, we discuss the optical conductivity of the Hubbard model (
1
) given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\gamma}(\omega)=\frac{\pi}{N\omega}\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}|\langle\alpha|J_{\gamma}|0\rangle|^{2}\delta(\omega-E_{\alpha}+E_{0})

(7)

and
\sigma^{s}_{\gamma}(\omega)
in terms of the spin degrees of freedom,
where
J_{\gamma}
is the
\gamma
component of the current operator of the model (
1
).
Following the procedure to derive
N^{s}(\bm{k},\omega)
, we represent
J_{\gamma}
with respect to the operators of spin system (
2
).
To this aim, let us express the electric polarization
[
37
]
for
\gamma
direction by using
\delta n^{s}_{j}
of Eq. (
3
) as

\displaystyle P^{s}_{\gamma}\equiv\sum_{j}(-e)R_{j}^{\gamma}\delta n^{s}_{j}=-e\sum_{ij}\eta_{ij}R^{\gamma}_{i}\left({\bf\it S}_{i}\cdot{\bf\it S}_{j}-\frac{1}{4}\right),

(8)

where
e
is the absolute value of the charge of an electron.
Then the current operator is derived as
[
38
]

\displaystyle J^{s}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{dP^{s}_{\gamma}}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[P^{s}_{\gamma},{\cal H}^{s}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{\hbar}\sum_{ijl}\eta_{ij}\overline{K}_{il}(R^{\gamma}_{i}-R^{\gamma}_{j})\ {\bf\it S}_{i}\cdot({\bf\it S}_{j}\times{\bf\it S}_{l}),

(9)

where

\displaystyle\overline{K}_{il}=\frac{4U(t_{il})^{2}}{U^{2}-(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{l})^{2}},

(10)

and the optical conductivity is defined as

\displaystyle\sigma^{s}_{\gamma}(\omega)=\frac{\pi}{N\omega}\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}|\,^{s\,}\!\langle\alpha|J^{s}_{\gamma}|0\rangle^{s}|^{2}\delta(\omega-E^{s}_{\alpha}+E^{s}_{0}),

(11)

As
n^{s}_{\bm{k}}
and
J^{s}_{\gamma}
commute with the total spin
{\bf\it S}_{\rm tot}=\sum_{l}{\bf\it S}_{l}
,
the spectral functions
N^{s}(\bm{k},\omega)
and
\sigma^{s}_{\gamma}(\omega)
can detect the singlet excitation
[
37
,
38
]
.
Also, it is noted that these formulas can be applied to the 1D ionic Hubbard model by setting

\displaystyle t_{jj+1}=t,\ \epsilon_{j}=-(-1)^{j}\frac{\Delta}{2},\ {\rm and}\ R_{j}=aj,

(12)

with the lattice constant
a
and the strength of the potential
\Delta
. Then, it can be confirmed that the same results of Refs.
\citen
katsura,yokoi,komaki are obtained.

We here stress the importance of the quantity
\eta_{lj}
in our framework.
The point is that
\eta_{lj}
appears both in
N^{s}(\bm{k},\omega)
and
\sigma^{s}_{\gamma}(\omega)
and that
\eta_{lj}
is proportional to the energy difference
(\epsilon_{l}-\epsilon_{j})
between the neighboring sites
l
and
j
. Therefore, the finite site-dependence of the potential
\epsilon_{l}
is essential for observing these spectral functions.

(13)

\displaystyle\eta_{{\bf\it R}{\bf\it R}\pm{\bf\it a}_{\gamma}}({\bf\it Q})=-e^{-i\bm{Q}\cdot\bm{R}}\eta_{\gamma}({\bf\it Q}),

\displaystyle\eta_{\gamma}({\bf\it Q})\equiv\frac{8Ut^{2}_{\gamma}\Delta_{\gamma}({\bf\it Q})}{[U^{2}-\Delta^{2}_{\gamma}({\bf\it Q})]^{2}}.

In the same manner,

of our study does not detect the three-triplon bound state.
This is because the ladder treated here has a weaker

than
\overline{K}_{y}
, where the three-triplon bound states are not clearly visible in the spectral functions
[
32
]
.
Therefore,

would show some signature of the bound state for

35

36

We use the procedure of Ref.
\citen
gopalan for the two-leg ladder in order to evaluate

of the ladder with
{\bf\it Q}=(\pi,0)
, and the results are shown in Fig.

The analytical form of

is shown in Appendix C.
We can see that the spectral weight of

In particular, the dominant part exists in the vicinity of the lower boundary.
It can also be seen that there is no signal of the bound state below the continuum, and a non-negligible amount of the weight is found near the upper boundary of the continuum.
This is because the bond operator theory of Ref.
\citen

[
33

The result of

Equation (

4
Summary

In this work, we have studied the low-energy spectral properties of the two-leg Hubbard ladders with site-dependent potentials.
Our approach combining the analytical treatment for the Hubbard models with the numerical diagonalization demonstrated that the spectral functions
N^{s}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\omega)
and
\sigma^{s}_{\gamma}(\omega)
, the contributions of the spin degrees of freedom to the dynamical charge structure factor and the optical conductivity along the
\gamma=x,y
direction, detect the two- and three-triplon excitations of the two-leg spin ladders.
The key ingredient to detect these multi-triplon excitations is the finite site-dependent potential of the ladder.
In our framework, this point is expressed in terms of the quantity
\eta_{lj}
, which appears in the definitions of
N^{s}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\omega)
and
\sigma^{s}_{\gamma}(\omega)
.
Numerical results obtained using the Lanczos diagonalization are consistent with the results of bond operator theory.
We have also examined that, by tuning the periodicity of the potential,
\sigma^{s}_{\gamma}(\omega)
can select detectable excited states;
for example,
\sigma^{s}_{x}(\omega)
probes either the two-triplon bound state or the three-triplon continuum.
Furthermore, we confirmed that the two-leg ladder with the random potential also has finite spectral weights in
\sigma^{s}_{x}(\omega)
stemming from the two- and three-triplon states, which suggest that ladder materials with intrinsic or acquired randomness and strong intra-rung couplings are good candidates for experimentally observing and identifying these multi-triplon states.

.
Here, we follow the procedure of Ref.
\citen
gopalan and pick up only essential points to reproduce our calculations for the convenience of the reader.

Let us introduce the bond operators. The
\alpha
-component of the spin operator of the site
{\bf\it R}=(l,n)
on the leg
n=0
or
1
is represented as

\displaystyle S^{\alpha}_{0l}

\displaystyle\equiv S^{\alpha}_{{\bf\it R}=(l,0)}=\frac{1}{2}(s^{\dagger}_{l}t_{l\alpha}+t^{\dagger}_{l\alpha}s_{l}-\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}t^{\dagger}_{l\beta}t_{l\gamma})

(45)

and

\displaystyle S^{\alpha}_{1l}

\displaystyle\equiv S^{\alpha}_{{\bf\it R}=(l,1)}=\frac{1}{2}(-s^{\dagger}_{l}t_{l\alpha}-t^{\dagger}_{l\alpha}s_{l}-\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}t^{\dagger}_{l\beta}t_{l\gamma}),

(46)

where
s_{l}
and
t_{l\alpha}(\alpha=x,y,z)
are bond operators for the singlet and triplet states of a rung labeled by
l
.
Here, we use the summation convention for repeated indices and the totally antisymmetric tensor
\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
.
These operators have bosonic statistics to satisfy the algebra of the spin operator
S^{\alpha}_{{\bf\it R}}
.
In addition, they follow the hard-core condition

\displaystyle s^{\dagger}_{l}s_{l}+t^{\dagger}_{l\alpha}t_{l\alpha}=1

(47)

for each rung.

Using the bond operators, we rewrite the Hamiltonian of the two-leg ladder (
20
) as

\displaystyle{\cal H}^{s}=H_{0}+\lambda H_{1},

(48)

where

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}\overline{K}_{y}\left(-\frac{3}{4}s^{\dagger}_{l}s_{l}+\frac{1}{4}t^{\dagger}_{l\alpha}t_{l\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{l}\mu_{l}(s^{\dagger}_{l}s_{l}+t^{\dagger}_{l\alpha}t_{l\alpha}-1)

(49)

and

\displaystyle H_{1}=\frac{\overline{K}_{y}}{2}\sum_{l}\left(t^{\dagger}_{l\alpha}t_{l+1\alpha}s^{\dagger}_{l+1}s_{l}+t^{\dagger}_{l\alpha}t^{\dagger}_{l+1\alpha}s_{l}s_{l+1}+\text{H.c.}\right).

(50)

Here we note that the chemical potential
\mu_{l}
is introduced to impose the hard-core condition (
47
)
and the quartic term with respect to
t^{(\dagger)}_{l}
(
H_{2}
in Ref.
\citen
gopalan ) is omitted here following the theoretical treatment
[
35
]
.

Now, the replacement
s^{(\dagger)}_{l}\to\langle s^{(\dagger)}_{l}\rangle=\bar{s}
using the mean field
\bar{s}
is introduced to diagonalize the Hamiltonian (
48
).
In addition, the chemical potential
\mu_{l}
is replaced by the site-independent one
\mu
because of the translational invariance of the ladder system.
Applying the Fourier transformation of the operations
t_{l\alpha}=N_{x}^{-1}\sum_{k}t_{k\alpha}e^{ikl}
,
we obtain the mean-field Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{m}(\mu,\bar{s})=

\displaystyle N_{x}\left(-\frac{3}{4}\overline{K}_{y}\bar{s}^{2}-\mu\bar{s}^{2}+\mu\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k}[\Lambda_{k}t^{\dagger}_{k\alpha}t_{k\alpha}+\Delta_{k}(t^{\dagger}_{k\alpha}t^{\dagger}_{-k\alpha}+t_{k\alpha}t_{-k\alpha})]

(51)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{4}-\mu+\lambda\overline{K}_{y}\bar{s}^{2}\cos k,\quad\Delta_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\overline{K}_{y}\bar{s}^{2}\cos k,

(52)

and
N_{x}
is the number of rungs.
Using the Bogoliubov transformation

\displaystyle\gamma_{k\alpha}=\cosh\theta_{k}t_{k\alpha}+\sinh\theta_{k}t^{\dagger}_{-k\alpha}

(53)

with

\displaystyle\cosh\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda_{k}}{\omega_{k}}+1}

(54)

and

\displaystyle\sinh\theta_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\text{sgn}(\Delta_{k})\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda_{k}}{\omega_{k}}-1},

(55)

we diagonalize the mean-field Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle H_{m}(\mu,\bar{s})=

\displaystyle N_{x}\left(-\frac{3}{4}\overline{K}_{y}\bar{s}^{2}-\mu\bar{s}^{2}+\mu\right)-\frac{N_{x}}{2}\left(\frac{\overline{K}_{y}}{4}-\mu\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}\left(\gamma^{\dagger}_{k\alpha}\gamma_{k\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(56)

Here, the quantity

\displaystyle\omega_{k}=\sqrt{\Lambda^{2}_{k}-(2\Delta_{k})^{2}}

(57)

is the dispersion of the Bogoliubov boson, which corresponds to the triplon of the two-leg ladder.

The next step is to obtain physical quantities within this mean-field theory.
Following the method in the original paper
[
35
]
, we calculate the mean fields
\bar{s}
and
\mu
at the ground state by numerically solving the saddle-point equations

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial H_{m}}{\partial\mu}\right\rangle=0\ \text{and}\ \left\langle\frac{\partial H_{m}}{\partial\bar{s}}\right\rangle=0.

(58)

Then, we evaluate the dynamical charge structure factor
N^{s}(\bm{k}^{\prime},\omega)
for
{\bf\it Q}=(\pi,0)
, where the charge disproportionation
\delta n^{s}_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}
of Eq. (
21
) is given by

\displaystyle\delta n^{s}_{{\bf\it k}}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}_{x}({\bm{k}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\eta_{x}\sum_{\bm{R}}e^{-i{\bf\it k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf\it R}}\sum_{\mathbf{r}=\pm{\bf\it a}_{x}}{\bf\it S}_{{\bf\it R}}\cdot{\bf\it S}_{{\bf\it R}+{\bf\it r}}.

(59)

For this purpose, we represent

\displaystyle\delta n^{s}_{{{\bf\it k}}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\delta n^{0}_{k_{x}^{\prime}}\quad\text{for}\quad\bm{k}^{\prime}=(k_{x}^{\prime},0)

(60)

\displaystyle=\delta n^{\pi}_{k_{x}^{\prime}}\quad\text{for}\quad\bm{k}^{\prime}=(k_{x}^{\prime},\pi),

(61)

and then express these quantities with the boson operators.

First, we deal with

\displaystyle\delta n^{0}_{k_{x}^{\prime}}=-\eta_{x}\sum_{\bm{l}}e^{-ik_{x}^{\prime}l}\sum_{r=\pm 1}({\bf\it S}_{0l}\cdot{\bf\it S}_{0l+r}+{\bf\it S}_{1l}\cdot{\bf\it S}_{1l+r}),

(62)

which is necessary to calculate
N^{s}(k^{\prime}_{x},k^{\prime}_{y}=0,\omega)
.
Here, we use the boson representation

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{0l}\cdot\bm{S}_{0j}+\bm{S}_{1l}\cdot\bm{S}_{1j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{s}^{2}}{2}\left(t^{\dagger}_{l\alpha}t_{j\alpha}+t^{\dagger}_{l\alpha}t^{\dagger}_{j\alpha}+t^{\dagger}_{j\alpha}t_{l\alpha}+t_{j\alpha}t_{l\alpha}\right),

(63)

where the replacement
s^{(\dagger)}_{i}\to\bar{s}
is introduced, and only the two-body terms of the boson operators are left.
Using the Fourier transformation and the Bogoliubov boson
\gamma^{(\dagger)}_{k\alpha}
,
\delta n^{0}_{k_{x}^{\prime}}
is expressed as

\displaystyle\delta n^{0}_{k_{x}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{x}\bar{s}^{2}\sum_{p}\left\{-[\cos p+\cos(p-k_x')]\cosh\theta_{p-k_{x}^{\prime}}\sinh\theta_{p}\right.

\displaystyle\times\gamma^{\dagger}_{p-k_{x}^{\prime}\alpha}\gamma^{\dagger}_{-p\alpha}+\cos p\cosh\theta_{p+k_{x}^{\prime}}\cosh\theta_{p}\gamma^{\dagger}_{-p-k_{x}^{\prime}\alpha}\gamma^{\dagger}_{p\alpha}

\displaystyle+\cos(p-k_x')\sinh\theta_{p-k_{x}^{\prime}}\sinh\theta_{p}\gamma^{\dagger}_{p-k_{x}^{\prime}\alpha}\gamma^{\dagger}_{-p\alpha}

\displaystyle-[\cos p+\cos(p-k_x')]\sinh\theta_{p-k_{x}^{\prime}}\cosh\theta_{p}\gamma_{-p+k_{x}^{\prime}\alpha}\gamma_{p\alpha}

\displaystyle+\cos p\sinh\theta_{p+k_{x}^{\prime}}\sinh\theta_{p}\gamma_{p+k_{x}^{\prime}\alpha}\gamma_{-p\alpha}

\displaystyle+\left.\cos(p-k_x')\cosh\theta_{p-k_{x}^{\prime}}\cosh\theta_{p}\gamma_{-p+k_{x}^{\prime}\alpha}\gamma_{p\alpha}\right\},

(64)

where the two-boson creation or annihilation terms are left to treat the two-triplon continuum contributing to
N^{s}(k^{\prime}_{x},k^{\prime}_{y}=0,\omega)
.
Then
N^{s}(k^{\prime}_{x},k^{\prime}_{y}=0,\omega)
is represented as

\displaystyle N^{s}(k^{\prime}_{x},k^{\prime}_{y}=0,\omega)=\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}|\langle\alpha|\delta n^{0}_{k^{\prime}_{x}}|0\rangle|^{2}\delta(\omega-E_{\alpha}+E_{0})

\displaystyle=6\eta_{x}^{2}\bar{s}^{4}\sum_{p}\left[\cos\left(p+\frac{k^{\prime}_{x}}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{k^{\prime}_{x}}{2}\right)\sinh(\theta_p + \theta_{p+k'_x})\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\cos p\cosh(\theta_p + \theta_{p+k'_x} )\right]

\displaystyle\times\left[\cos\left(p+\frac{k^{\prime}_{x}}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{k^{\prime}_{x}}{2}\right)\sinh(\theta_p + \theta_{p+k'_x})\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\cos\left(p+\frac{k^{\prime}_{x}}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{k^{\prime}_{x}}{2}\right)\cosh(\theta_p + \theta_{p+k'_x} )\right]\delta(\omega-\omega_{p}-\omega_{p+k^{\prime}_{x}}).

(65)

As for

\displaystyle\delta n^{\pi}_{k_{x}^{\prime}}=-\eta_{x}\sum_{\bm{l}}e^{-ik_{x}^{\prime}l}\sum_{r=\pm 1}({\bf\it S}_{0l}\cdot{\bf\it S}_{0l+r}-{\bf\it S}_{1l}\cdot{\bf\it S}_{1l+r}),

(66)

we use the same notation as

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{0l}\cdot\bm{S}_{0j}-\bm{S}_{1l}\cdot\bm{S}_{1j}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\bar{s}}{2}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\delta}\left(t^{\dagger}_{l\alpha}t^{\dagger}_{j\beta}t_{j\delta}+t_{l\alpha}t^{\dagger}_{j\beta}t_{j\delta}+t^{\dagger}_{j\alpha}t^{\dagger}_{l\beta}t_{l\delta}+t_{j\alpha}t^{\dagger}_{l\beta}t_{l\delta}\right),

(67)

and then obtain

\displaystyle\delta n^{\pi}_{k_{x}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\bar{s}}{2}\eta_{x}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\delta}(1+e^{-ik_{x}^{\prime}})\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{x}}}\sum_{pq}\left[e^{i(k_{x}^{\prime}+p)}+e^{-p}\right]

\displaystyle\times(\cosh\theta_{p}-\sin\theta_{p})(\cosh\theta_{q}\sinh\theta_{k_{x}^{\prime}+p+q}\gamma^{\dagger}_{p\alpha}\gamma^{\dagger}_{q\beta}\gamma^{\dagger}_{-k_{x}^{\prime}-p-q\delta}

\displaystyle+\sinh\theta_{q}\cosh\theta_{k_{x}^{\prime}+p+q}\gamma_{-p\alpha}\gamma_{-q\beta}\gamma_{k_{x}^{\prime}+p+q\delta}).

(68)

It should be noted that the three-boson creation or annihilation terms are left in Eq. (
68
).
Finally,
N^{s}(k^{\prime}_{x},k^{\prime}_{y}=\pi,\omega)
is expressed as

\displaystyle N^{s}(k^{\prime}_{x},k^{\prime}_{y}=\pi,\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}|\langle\alpha|\delta n^{\pi}_{k^{\prime}_{x}}|0\rangle|^{2}\delta(\omega-E_{n}+E_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{12\eta_{x}^{2}\bar{s}^{2}}{N_{x}}(1+\cos k^{\prime}_{x})\sum_{pq}\cos\left(p+\frac{k^{\prime}_{x}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times\cosh\theta_{q}\sinh\theta_{p+q+k^{\prime}_{x}}(\cosh\theta_{p}-\sinh\theta_{p})

\displaystyle\times\left[\cos\left(p+\frac{k^{\prime}_{x}}{2}\right)(\cosh\theta_{p}-\sinh\theta_{p})\sinh(\theta_{p+q+k'_x+} - \theta_{q} )\right.

\displaystyle+\cos\left(p+\frac{k^{\prime}_{x}}{2}\right)(\cosh\theta_{q}-\sinh\theta_{q})\sinh(\theta_{q} - \theta_{p+q+k'_x+} )

\displaystyle+\left.\cos\left(p+q+\frac{k^{\prime}_{x}}{2}\right)(\cosh\theta_{p+q+k^{\prime}_{x}}-\sinh\theta_{p+q+k^{\prime}_{x}})\sinh(\theta_q - \theta_p)\right]

\displaystyle\times\delta(\omega-\omega_{p}-\omega_{q}-\omega_{p+q+k^{\prime}_{x}}).

(69)
