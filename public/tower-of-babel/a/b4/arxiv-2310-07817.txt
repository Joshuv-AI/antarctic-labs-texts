Nonlinear global Fréchet regression for random objects via weak conditional expectation


Abstract
Random objects are complex non-Euclidean data taking value in general metric space, possibly devoid of any underlying vector space structure. Such data are getting increasingly abundant with the rapid advancement in technology. Examples include probability distributions, positive semi-definite matrices, and data on Riemannian manifolds. However, except for regression for object-valued response with Euclidean predictors and distribution-on-distribution regression, there has been limited development of a general framework for object-valued response with object-valued predictors in the literature. To fill this gap, we introduce the notion of a weak conditional Fréchet mean based on Carleman operators and then propose a global nonlinear Fréchet regression model through the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) embedding. Furthermore, we establish the relationships between the conditional Fréchet mean and the weak conditional Fréchet mean for both Euclidean and object-valued data. We also show that the state-of-the-art global Fréchet regression recently developed by Petersen and Müller, 2019 emerges as a special case of our method by choosing a linear kernel. We require that the metric space for the predictor admits a reproducing kernel, while the intrinsic geometry of the metric space for the response is utilized to study the asymptotic properties of the proposed estimates. Numerical studies, including extensive simulations and a real application, are conducted to investigate the performance of our estimator in a finite sample.

1
Introduction

Encountering complex non-Euclidean data, taking values in a general metric space that may defy any inherent linear structure, has become increasingly common in areas such as biological or social sciences with the rapid advancement of technology.
Examples of such “
random object
” data, recorded in the form of images, shapes, networks, or life tables
(
Marron and Alonso, 2014
)

include distributional data in Wasserstein space
(
Delicado and Vieu, 2017
;
Le Gouic and Loubes, 2017
)
,
symmetric positive definite matrix objects
(
Dryden et al., 2009
)
,
data on the surface of the sphere
(
Di Marzio et al., 2014
)
,
phylogenetic trees
(
Billera et al., 2001
)
, and finite-dimensional Riemannian manifolds objects
(
Afsari, 2011
;
Bhattacharya and Patrangenaru, 2003
;
Bhattacharya and Patrangenaru, 2005
;
Pennec, 2018
;
Afsari, 2011
;
Huckemann, 2015
)
,
among others. Since the data are metric space valued, many classical notions of statistics, such as the definition of sample or population mean as an average or expected value, do not apply anymore and need to be replaced by barycenters or Fréchet means
(
Fréchet, 1948
)
. In the regression context, the conditional Fréchet mean for random object response
Y
, residing in a metric space
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}},d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}})
, given a Euclidean predictor
X\in\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}
, is defined as
(
Hein, 2009
;
Petersen and Müller, 2019
)

\displaystyle E_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(Y|X=x)=m_{\oplus}(x):=\mathrm{argmin}_{y\in\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}}E[d^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}(Y,y)|X=x].

(1)

The Fréchet regression proposed by
Petersen and Müller, 2019
generalizes the globally linear least squares method
and the nonparametric local linear regression to fit the conditional Fréchet mean. They aim for direct modeling of the joint distribution of the response and the predictor by viewing the regression function as an alternative target of weighted Fréchet means, with weights that change globally linearly (or locally) with the predictors and are derived from those of the corresponding standard multiple linear regression (or local linear kernel regression) with Euclidean responses.
The globally linear approach, in particular, targets an alternative formulation than (
1
) given by

\displaystyle\tilde{m}_{\oplus}(x)=\mathrm{argmin}_{y\in\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}}E[s(X,x)d^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}(Y,y)],

(2)

where the weight function
s(X,x)=1+(x-\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}\Sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(X-\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})
varies globally and linearly with the output points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}
, hence the nomenclature;
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
being the expectation and covariance matrix for the predictors
X
.

Model (
2
) coincides with model (
1
) in the special case of multiple linear regression with Euclidean responses and predictors.
However, for a general metric space-valued response
Y\in\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}},
the above two targets are different, thus making the regression relationship for general metric-valued data quite restrictive.
Although the local regression, which indeed targets (
1
) with an asymptotically negligible bias, is more flexible, it is effective only when the dimension of the predictor is relatively low. As this dimension gets higher, its accuracy drops significantly
–

a phenomenon known as the curse of dimensionality.
Recently
Bhattacharjee and Müller, 2021
developed a single index Fréchet regression that projects the multivariate predictors onto a desired direction parameter vector to form a single index, thus facilitating inference for Fréchet regression. However, the model assumptions are still somewhat restrictive, and in general, the Fréchet regression framework can only accommodate Euclidean predictors.

In this work, we propose a non-linear global object regression framework that strikes a balance between the fully linear approach and the fully local approach. By mapping the predictor metric space into an RKHS, the new regression method offers the flexibility to accommodate a spectrum of model complexities such as the linear model, the polynomial model, and a family of functions that is dense in the
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
space. This flexibility is made possible via a novel probabilistic machinery that we call
the weak conditional Fréchet mean
, which is developed from the concept of weak conditional mean introduced by
Li and Song, 2022
in the context of sufficient dimension reduction for functional data. It is important to note that there is no concept of linearity in an abstract metric space where the statistical objects reside–the model proposed in
Petersen and Müller, 2019
is called linear because of the linear form of the weight function through which the dependence of the response on the predictor is characterized in (
2
). We develop the notion of a weak conditional Fréchet mean utilizing the smoothness in the predictor space and the intrinsic geometry implied by the metric in the response space, and introduce a novel nonlinear object regression approach as a generalization of nonlinear regression in metric spaces.

In addition to this flexibility, our method also allows both the response and the predictor to be metric-space-valued random objects.
Studying the relation between two arbitrary random objects is also increasingly important. Unfortunately, not much exists in the literature in this regard, barring special cases of distribution-on-distribution regression
(
Chen et al., 2019
;
Chen et al., 2023
;
Ghodrati and Panaretos, 2022
)
. Our proposed method accommodates more general predictors, such as random vectors, functions, or even object-valued predictors, as long as the predictor space admits an RKHS embedding. We discuss the details of constructing appropriate kernels to generate such RKHSs and study the relevant operators generated to achieve this goal. Interestingly, in a special case, where the kernel for the RKHS is taken to be the linear kernel on a Euclidean space, our nonlinear global Fréchet regression reduces to the (linear) global regression proposed by citepete:19.

Along with–and also as a preparation for–our development of the nonlinear global Fréchet regression, we also give an in-depth development toward a coherent and comprehensive theoretical foundation for weak conditional mean and weak Fréchet conditional mean, as we perceive they will play an increasingly important role in regression for functional data and metric-space-valued data. These serve as a bridge by which we can bring many tools available in classical regression to the new regression problems where the regression variables are random functions or random objects. In particular, we discuss the transparent and highly interpretable interrelations among four types of conditional means–the conditional mean, the weak conditional mean, the conditional Fréchet mean, and the weak conditional Fréchet mean (see Figure
1
).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 defines the preliminary setup of the problem and focuses on the construction of the weak conditional mean for the classical/ Euclidean paradigm in detail. It is important to note that Section 2 by itself is a key contribution to the state-of-the-art literature for the Hilbert space-valued functional data. Section 3 defines the weak condition moments for object responses and predictors, establishes the global non-linear object regression model, and studies its connections to the global linear object regression framework. In Section 4, we propose a suitable estimator for the weak conditional Fréchet mean from the observed data. In this vein, the construction of the underlying RKHS is discussed, and an M-estimation setting is devised. Section 5 establishes the asymptotic convergence rates of the proposed methods. Simulation results are presented in Section 6 to show the numerical performances of the proposed methods. Section 7 analyzes a real application of the proposed method for the mortality-vs-fertility distributions. All proofs are presented in Section S.1. of the Supplementary Material.

2
Weak conditional mean and further development

In this section, we first introduce the notations with a focus on the construction of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space on the space where the predictor objects lie. Next, we outline the basic idea underlying the construction of the weak conditional expectation in
Li and Song, 2022
. We will also derive some new properties of weak conditional expectation and give a more general theory about the weak conditional expectation that is needed in later development.

2.1
Random objects and reproducing kernels

Let
(\Omega,{\cal{F}},P)
be a probability space. Let
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}},d_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})
and
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}},d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}})
be metric spaces, where
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
are set and
d_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
are the metrics. Let
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
be the Borel
\sigma
-fields in
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
corresponding to the open sets determined by
d_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
. Let
X:\Omega\to\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
Y:\Omega\to\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
be random elements that are measurable, respectively, with respect to
{\cal{F}}/{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
{\cal{F}}/{\cal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
. Such random elements are called
statistical objects
. Let

P_{\scriptscriptstyle{XY}}=P\mbox{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}$}}(X,Y)^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}
,
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}=P\mbox{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}$}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}
and
P_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}=P\mbox{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\circ}$}}Y^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}
be the distributions of
(X,Y)
,
X
and
Y
, respectively.

We will assume that there exists a positive definite kernel
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}:\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\times\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
. While there are sufficient conditions for a metric space to possess such kernels, we make this requirement our general assumption.

Assumption 1

There is a positive definite kernel
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}:\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\times\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
.

For example, if
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is of negative type, then the metric-induced kernel is positive definite
(
Sejdinovic et al., 2013
)
. Furthermore,
Zhang et al., 2021
showed that, if
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is complete and separable, and there is a continuous injection from
\rho:\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\to{\cal{H}}
for some separable Hilbert space
{\cal{H}}
, then, for any analytic function
F(t)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle{i=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\infty}}a_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}t^{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}
with
a_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}>0
, the function
\kappa:\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\times\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form
F(\langle\rho(x_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}),\rho(x_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}}})
is a cc-universal kernel
(
Micchelli et al., 2006
)
.

Let
\kappa_{G}(x,x^{\prime})=\exp(-\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}d_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(x,x^{\prime}))
and
\kappa_{L}(x,x^{\prime})=\exp(-\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}d_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(x,x^{\prime}))
denote the Gaussian and Laplacian kernels, respectively.
Zhang et al., 2021
showed that both
\kappa_{G}
and
\kappa_{L}
on a complete and separable metric space
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
are positive definite and universal, and the RKHS
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
generated by such kernels is dense in
L^{2}(P_{X})
.

Note that we do not impose the above assumption on
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
.

2.2
Weak conditional mean via uncentered regression operator

We first define the extended Carleman operator, which is a slight extension of the definition in
Weidmann, 2012
.

Definition 1
(Carleman operator)

Let
{\cal{G}}
be a set,
{\cal{M}}
a Hilbert space of real-valued functions on
{\cal{G}}
,
{\cal{H}}
another Hilbert space, and
A:{\cal{H}}\to{\cal{M}}
a linear operator. If, for each
x\in{\cal{G}}
, the linear functional

A_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}:{\cal{H}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ f\mapsto(Af)(x)

is bounded, then we call
A
an extended Carleman operator. The Riesz representation
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{A}}(x)
of
A_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}
is called the inducing function of
A
.

In the rest of the paper,
{\cal{G}}
is the metric space
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
,
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is the RKHS generated by
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
,
{\cal{H}}
is the real line
\mathbb{R}
, and
A:\mathbb{R}\to{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is the regression operator.

We next introduce the regression operator. Let
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
be a generic Hilbert space, and let
U:\Omega\to{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
be a random element. We make the following assumption.

Assumption 2

{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
are separable.

These conditions are mild: for example, by Theorem 2.7.5 of
Hsing and Eubank, 2015
, if
\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is separable and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is continuous, then
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is separable. Since
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
will be taken to be
\mathbb{R}
for the rest of the paper, it is separable. Consider the tensor products

\displaystyle\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)\otimes\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X),\quad\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)\otimes U.

The above quantities are members of the tensor product spaces
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\otimes{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
, respectively. By simple calculation,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\,\|\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)\otimes\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\otimes{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}}}=\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(X,X),\\
&\,\|\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)\otimes U\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}=\sqrt{\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(X,X)}\|U\|.\end{split}

(3)

We make the following assumption.

Assumption 3

E\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(X,X)<\infty
,
E(\sqrt{\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(X,X)}\|U\|)<\infty
.

Since
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
are separable,
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\otimes{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
are separable. Furthermore, by Assumption
3
and relations in (
3
), we have

\displaystyle E(\|\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)\otimes\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\otimes{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}}})<\infty,\quad E(\|\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)\otimes U\|_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\otimes{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}})<\infty.

By Theorem 2.6.5 of
Hsing and Eubank, 2015
, the following Bochner integrals

\displaystyle\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Omega}}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)\otimes\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)dP,\quad\int_{\scriptscriptstyle{\Omega}}\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X)\otimes UdP

are defined. They will be denoted by
M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}
and
M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}
, respectively, and will be called the covariance operator of
X
and the cross-covariance operator from
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
to
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
. It can be shown that, for any
f,g\in{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
h\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle f,M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}}}=E[f(X)g(X)],\quad\langle f,M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}h\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}}}=E[f(X)\langle U,h\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}].

(4)

Henceforth, for a linear operator
A:{\cal{H}}\to{\cal{H}}
, let
\mathrm{ran}(A)
denote the range of
A
and
\mathrm{ker}(A)
denote the kernel of
A
; that is,
\mathrm{ran}(A)=\{Af:f\in{\cal{H}}\}
and
\mathrm{ker}(A)=\{f\in{\cal{H}},Af=0\}
.
Furthermore, let
\overline{\mathrm{ran}}(A)
denote the closure of
\mathrm{ran}(A)
. We make the following assumption.

Assumption 4

\mathrm{ker}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})=\{0\}
and
\mathrm{ran}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}})\subseteq\mathrm{ran}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})
.

This assumption is very mild. By (
4
),
M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}f=0
implies
E[f^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(X)]=0
, which implies that
f(X)=0
almost surely. If
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is continuous, then
f(X)=0
everywhere. Hence, if
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is continuous, then
\mathrm{ker}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})=\{0\}
. As argued in
Li, 2018
, the assumption
\mathrm{ran}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}})\subseteq\mathrm{ran}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})
is a smoothness assumption about the relation between
U
and
X
. Under
\mathrm{ker}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})=\{0\}
,
M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}:{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\to\mathrm{ran}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})
is an injective function. Thus the inverse function
M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}:\mathrm{ran}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})\to{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is defined. By
\mathrm{ran}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}})\subseteq\mathrm{ran}(M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})
, the operator

\displaystyle R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}=M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}

is well-defined and is called the regression operator
(
Lee et al., 2016
)
. Note, however, that since
M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}
is a trace class operator,
M_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}
is an unbounded operator. Nevertheless, as argued by
Li, 2018
, it is entirely reasonable to assume
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}
to be a bounded or even compact operator, which imposes a type of smoothness again on the relation between
U
and
X
.

Assumption 5

R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}:{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}\to{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is a bounded operator.

As shown below, this assumption implies that
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}
is an extended Carleman operator.

Proposition 1

If
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}
is a bounded operator, then it is an extended Carleman operator.

The next theorem is the key property of the regression operator. Since it is more general than those given in
Lee et al., 2016
and
Li and Song, 2022
, we provide a proof here.

Theorem 1

If Assumptions
1
through
5
are satisfied and, for any
\alpha\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
,
E(\langle\alpha,Y\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}|X)
is in the
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(P_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})
-closure of
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
, then

1.

E(\langle\alpha,Y\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}|X)\in\mathrm{ran}(R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}})
almost surely;

2.

for any
\alpha\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
,
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}(\alpha)(X)=E[\langle\alpha,U\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}|X]
almost surely.

As a special case, when
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is dense in
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(P_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})
, the conclusion of the theorem holds because in that case
E[\langle\alpha,U\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}|X]
is always in the
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(P_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})
-closure of
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
. This was the result proved in
Li and Song, 2022
.
The weak conditional mean is defined as the inducing function of the linear operator
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}
.

Definition 2

If Assumptions
1
through
5
are satisfied, then the random element

\displaystyle\omega\mapsto\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}}}(X(\omega)),\quad\Omega\to{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}

is the weak conditional expectation of
U
given
X
; that is
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}}}(X)=E(U\mbox{\raisebox{-0.51114pt}{$\,\overset{\overset{\rule[0.0pt]{0.29439pt}{1.10413pt}}{\rule[0.0pt]{0.32771pt}{1.22911pt}}}{\rule[0.0pt]{0.39993pt}{1.49994pt}}\,$}}X)
.

It follows easily from Theorem
1
that the weak conditional expectation reduces to the true conditional expectation under assumptions therein.

Corollary 1

Under the assumptions in Theorem
1
, we have

\displaystyle E(U\mbox{\raisebox{-0.51114pt}{$\,\overset{\overset{\rule[0.0pt]{0.29439pt}{1.10413pt}}{\rule[0.0pt]{0.32771pt}{1.22911pt}}}{\rule[0.0pt]{0.39993pt}{1.49994pt}}\,$}}X)=E(U|X).

2.3
Weak conditional mean via centered regression operator

An alternative definition of the regression operator, as given in
Lee et al., 2016
, is the centered version of
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}
. Let

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}=E[(\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,x)-\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})\otimes(\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,x)-\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})],\quad\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}=E[(\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,x)-\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})\otimes(U-\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}})].

These operators are defined under Assumption
3
. We make a similar range assumption as Assumption
4
.

Assumption 6

\mathrm{ran}(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}})\subseteq\mathrm{ran}(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})
.

In general,
\mathrm{ker}(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})\neq\{0\}
, and so function
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}:{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}\to{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is not invertible. However, the restricted operator

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\overline{\mathrm{ran}}(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})}}
is an invertible function. We call its inverse
[\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}|_{\scriptscriptstyle{\overline{\mathrm{ran}}(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})}}]^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}
the Moore-Penrose inverse, and denote it by
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\dagger}}
. Note that this is a mapping from
\mathrm{ran}(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})
to
\overline{\mathrm{ran}}(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}})
. Under Assumption
6
,
the operator

\displaystyle R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}:=\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\dagger}}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}

is well defined, and, to distinguish it from
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}
above, we denote it by
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}
and call it the centered regression operator.

Assumption 7

R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}
is a bounded operator.

We now give the alternative definition of the weak conditional expectation using
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}
. It turns out that this alternative definition deals with the constant function better than the uncentered version.

Definition 3

Suppose
R_{XU}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}
is defined and is a Carleman operator. Then the following random element

\displaystyle E(U)+\lambda_{R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}}(X)-E[\lambda_{R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}}(X)]

is called the weak conditional expectation of
U
given
X
with respect to
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
.

The next proposition is a parallel result of Theorem
1
for the centered regression operator. We will say that a function
f
belongs to a subset of
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(P_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})
modulo constant if there is a constant
c
such that
f+c
belongs to that subset.

Proposition 2

If Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
, and
7
are satisfied and, for any
\alpha\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
,
E(\langle\alpha,Y\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}|X)
belongs to the
L_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(P_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})
-closure of
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
modulo constant, then

1.

E(\langle\alpha,Y\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}|X)\in\mathrm{ran}(R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}})
modulo constant almost surely;

2.

for any
\alpha\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
,

\displaystyle E[\langle\alpha,U\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}|X]=\langle\alpha,E(U)\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}}}+R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}(\alpha)(X)-E[R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}(\alpha)(X)].

(5)

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
1
and is omitted.
The advantage of Definition
3

is that the former does not require the function

to be a member of

, while the latter usually does, as shown in the next corollary. In the following,

stands for the function

.

Corollary 2

Suppose

1.

both
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}
and
R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}
are defined and bounded;

2.

for any
\alpha\in{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
,

-closure of

E(U)-E[\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}}}(X)]\neq 0
.

Then
\mathbbm{1}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
belongs

almost surely.

The next simple example illustrates the advantage of
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}+\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}}}(X)-E[\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}}}}(X)]

\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{R_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU}}}}(X)
as the definition of weak conditional expectation.

Example 1

Suppose
U
and
X
are random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{q}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}
, respectively. Assume that

\displaystyle E(U|X)=a+B^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}X.

where
a
is a nonzero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}
, and
B
is a matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p\times q}}
. Under this model, it can be easily shown that

\displaystyle E(U|X)=E(U)+[\mathrm{cov}(U,X)][\mathrm{var}(X)]^{\scriptscriptstyle{\scriptscriptstyle-1}}(X-E(X)).

(6)

Let
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{U}}
be the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{q}}
and
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is the Hilbert space consisting of functions of the form

3.3

For existence, by the extreme value theorem, if the function

E_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(Y\mbox{\raisebox{-0.50003pt}{$\,\overset{\overset{\rule[0.0pt]{0.29439pt}{1.10413pt}}{\rule[0.0pt]{0.32771pt}{1.22911pt}}}{\rule[0.0pt]{0.39993pt}{1.49994pt}}\,$}}X)

Definition 5

4
Estimation

In the last section, we have described the solution to the nonlinear object regression framework at the population level. In the following, we implement the regression at the sample level. The key steps involve the construction of the sample estimate for the regression function as an M-estimator based on
i.i.d.
paired observations
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
. In order to quantify the sample objective function minimized by the regression estimator, we need to express the underlying RKHS
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and the relevant auto covariance and cross-covariance operators with a coordinate representation system (see, e.g.,
Horn and Johnson, 2012
;
Li, 2018
).

4.1
Coordinate representation

Suppose that
{\cal{L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
is a finite dimensional linear space with basis
{\cal{B}}=\{\xi_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\xi_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},\dots,\xi_{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}\}.
Then for any
\xi\in{\cal{L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},
there is a unique vector
(a_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},a_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}},\dots,a_{\scriptscriptstyle{p}})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\intercal}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}

The vector

with respect to
{\cal{B}}
, and denoted by

. Throughout this section, we will use this notation to describe coordinate representation. Next, we introduce the coordinate representation of a linear operator between two (finite-dimensional) linear spaces. Suppose
{\cal{L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
is another linear space with basis

and

is a linear operator from
{\cal{L}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}

. Then for any

, we have

\displaystyle A\xi

\displaystyle=A\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left([\xi]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{B}}}}\right)_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}\xi_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left([\xi]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{B}}}}\right)_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}(A\xi_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left([\xi]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{B}}}}\right)_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left([A\xi_{i}]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{C}}}}\right)_{j}\eta_{j}\ =\sum_{j=1}^{q}\left\{\left({}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{C}}}}[A]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{B}}}}\right)\left([\xi]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{B}}}}\right)\right\}_{j}\eta_{j},

where
{}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{C}}}}[A]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{B}}}}
is
the
q\times p
matrix with
(i,j)
th entry
([A\xi_{j}]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{C}}}})_{i}
.
The above equation implies that
[A\xi]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{C}}}}=(_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{C}}}}[A]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{B}}}})([\xi]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{B}}}}).
Therefore we call the matrix
{}_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{C}}}}[A]_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{B}}}}
the coordinate representation of the linear operator
A
with respect to the bases
{\cal{B}}
and
{\cal{C}}
. Similarly, for two Hilbert spaces

and
{\cal{H}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}

and
{\cal{B}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
, and a linear operator

to represent the coordinate representation of

relative to spanning systems

and

.

Let

Further, since the evaluation functional of the objective functions, the weak conditional Fréchet mean minimizes depend on

Similarly define

At the sample level, we estimate

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x)

\displaystyle=\underset{y\in\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}}{\mathrm{argmin}\ }J_{n}(y),\text{ where }J_{n}(y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}(y)+\langle\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,x)-\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}},\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\dagger}}\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{XU(y)}}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}}}.

4.3
Tuning parameter selection

We use the general cross-validation criterion
(

)
to determine the tuning constant

involved in the Tikhonov-regularization of the inverse auto-covariance operator

\displaystyle\text{GCV}(\epsilon_{X})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(Y_{i},\hat{Y}_{i})}{\left(1-\mathrm{tr}[QG_{X}(G_{X}+\epsilon_{X}I_{n})^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}+1_{n}1_{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}/n]/n\right)^{2}},

where

are respectively the observed and predicted responses for the

The numerator of this criterion quantifies the prediction error, while the denominator controls the degree of overfitting. We minimize the criterion over a grid
\{10^{-6},\dots,10^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}\}

5
Convergence results

In this section, we develop the asymptotic convergence results for the proposed Fréchet regression method. In particular, the convergence of the covariance operators with a suitable rate is established, which is used in turn to show the convergence of the regression estimate using the M-estimation theory.

Simulation studies

In this section, we evaluate the numerical performances of the proposed nonlinear object-on-object regression method under different simulation settings for commonly observed random objects.

In all of the following simulation scenarios, we consider the Gaussian radial basis kernel
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{G}}(y,y^{\prime})=\exp(-\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}d^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(y,y^{\prime}))

as a candidate to construct the underlying RKHS
{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
in the predictor space. We choose the parameters
\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
as the fixed
quantities

\displaystyle\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}=\frac{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}}{2\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}},\ \sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{G}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}=\binom{n}{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}\sum_{i<j}d_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}(X_{i},X_{j}),\ \rho_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}=1.

The same choices of tuning parameters were used in
Lee et al., 2013
;
Li and Song, 2017
;
Zhang et al., 2022
. The metrics
d_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
and
d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
for the predictor and response metric spaces, respectively, are chosen appropriately to enhance the interpretability of the results in each of the following scenarios considered.

Scenario 1: Univariate distribution-on-object regression

We consider univariate distributional objects as responses coupled with various types of statistical objects as predictors. Let
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}},d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}})
be the metric space of univariate distributions endowed with Wasserstein metric
d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}=d_{\scriptscriptstyle{W}}
, as described in (
10
) Section
3.3
. A sample of distributional object response,
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
is taken in equivalent forms of either CDF, quantile functions, or densities. However, the distributions
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are usually not fully observed in practice, and the latent curves need to be recovered from the discrete observations
\{Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}\}_{j=1}^{m}
for the
i^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{th}}}
sample;
i=1,\dots,n
, that one encounters in reality. For this, we employ nonparametric smoothing with a suitable bandwidth choice implemented by the
CreateDensity()
function in the
frechet
R package
(
Chen et al., 2020
)
. While considering distributional predictors, the trajectories
X_{i}
are recovered from the discrete observations
\{X_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}\}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{m}}
;
i=1\dots,n
in a similar manner.

The random distributional response
Y
is generated conditional on
X
by adding noise to the quantile functions, which are demonstrated in the following simulation settings for various types of predictor objects. Generally, we let
Y=N(\zeta(x),\eta^{2}(x)),
where the mean and variance of the response distribution are dependent on
X
. To this end, the auxiliary distribution parameters
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
and
\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
, given
X
, are independently sampled such that
E(\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}|X=x)=\zeta(x)
and
E(\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}^{2}|X=x)=\eta^{2}(x)
, and the corresponding distributional response in its quantile representation is constructed as
Q_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}(\cdot)=\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}+\sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}\Phi^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}(\cdot)
.

To obtain the global nonlinear Fréchet regression estimator, one needs to solve the minimization problem in (
13
). We consider quantile function representation of the distributional responses. If
Q_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y_{i}}}
is the quantile function corresponding to
Y_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n;
and
\hat{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(\cdot;x)
is the quantile function corresponding to the distribution
\hat{f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x)
in (
13
), using similar logic as the proof of Proposition 4,

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(\cdot;x)=\mathrm{argmin}_{\scriptscriptstyle{q\in Q(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}})}}\lVert q-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle{i=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}w_{\scriptscriptstyle{in}}(x)Q_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y_{i}}}\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle{L^{2}[0,1]}}.

The existence and uniqueness of the solution of the above, and therefore of (
13
), is guaranteed
\hat{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(\cdot;x)
corresponds to the orthogonal projection of
g_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle{i=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}w_{\scriptscriptstyle{in}}(x)Q_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y_{i}}}
as an element of the Hilbert space
L^{2}([0,1])
on the closed and convex set
Q(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}})
, where
Q(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}})
is the space of quantile functions corresponding to distributions in
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}},d_{\scriptscriptstyle{W}})
, as shown in Proposition 4. Here
w_{\scriptscriptstyle{in}}(x)=1+\langle\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,x)-\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}},\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle{XX}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\dagger}}(\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(\cdot,X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}})-\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle{{\cal{M}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}}}
is the nonlinear weight assigned to an observation at location
x
.

Taking an equidistant grid
\{u_{j}\}_{\scriptscriptstyle{j=1}}^{M}
on
[0,1]
and evaluating
g_{j}:=g_{\scriptscriptstyle{x}}(u_{j})
, a discretized version,
\hat{Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\ast}}
, of the approximation of
\hat{Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(\cdot;x)
is computed by solving the constrained quadratic program problem
\hat{Q}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\ast}}=\mathrm{argmin}_{\scriptscriptstyle{q\in\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{M}}}}\lVert g-q\rVert_{\scriptscriptstyle{E}}
such that
q_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\leq q_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\dots\leq q_{\scriptscriptstyle{M}}.
We employ an OSQP solver to implement this in practice.

We set the sample size
n=200
and
400
, and the number of discrete observations per sample
m=50
and
100
and generate the samples
(X_{i},\{Y_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}\}_{j=1}^{m})_{i=1}^{n}
. We use half of the samples to train the predictors via the proposed object regression method and then evaluate the prediction error as the discrepancy between the estimated and true responses using the rest of the data set by computing the Wasserstein distance metric (
10
) between the two distributions. The tuning parameter for the Tikonov regularization is determined by the method described in Section
4.3
. The experiment is repeated
B=100
times, and averages of the prediction error are computed as

\displaystyle\text{MPE}:=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}d_{\scriptscriptstyle{W}}(Y^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{test}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{b}},\hat{Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{test}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{b}}),

(17)

where
Y^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{test}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{b}}
and
\hat{Y}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{test}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{b}}
are the observed and predicted responses in the test set, respectively, for the
b
-th replicate,
b=1\dots,B.
The standard errors are also computed and will be reported in parentheses.

Scenario 2: Multivariate distribution-on-object regression

We now consider the scenario where both
X
and
Y
are bivariate random Gaussian distributional objects.
The construction of the kernel
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}
is done using the sliced
2
-Wasserstein distance, which is obtained by computing the average Wasserstein distance of the projected univariate distributions along
randomly picked directions. To define formally,

Definition 6
(Sliced Wasserstein metric)

let
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
be two measures in
{\cal{P}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}(M)
, the set of Borel probability measures on
(M,{\cal{B}}(M))
that have finite
p-
th moment and
is dominated by the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
, with
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
,
d>1
.
Let
S^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}
be the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
. For
\theta\in S^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}
, let
T_{\scriptscriptstyle{\theta}}:\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
be the linear transformation
x\mapsto\langle\theta,x\rangle.
Further, let
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\circ T_{\scriptscriptstyle{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}
and
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\circ T_{\scriptscriptstyle{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}

be the push-forward measures by the mapping
T_{\scriptscriptstyle{\theta}}
. The sliced
p-
Wasserstein distance between
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}
and
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}
is then defined
by

\displaystyle SW_{p}(\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})=\left(\int_{S^{\scriptscriptstyle{d-1}}}W_{\scriptscriptstyle{p}}^{p}(\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}}\circ T_{\scriptscriptstyle{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}},\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\circ T_{\scriptscriptstyle{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}})d\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(19)

For
p=2
,
Kolouri et al., 2016
show that the square of sliced Wasserstein distance is conditionally negative definite and hence that the Gaussian RBF kernel defined as
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}(x,x^{\prime})=\exp(-\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}}SW_{2}^{2}(x,x^{\prime}))
is a positive definite kernel.

We generate discrete observations for the predictor distributions
X_{i};\ i=1,\dots,n,
given by
\{X_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}\}_{j=1}^{m}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}N(a_{i}(1,1)^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}},b_{i}I_{2}),
where
a_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}N(0.5,0.5^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})
and
b_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}Beta(2,3).

To compute the Gram matrix associated with the multivariate predictor distribution supported on
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{d}}
,
d>1
, the sliced Wasserstein distance is estimated using a Monte Carlo method:

\displaystyle SW_{2}(\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}}},\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X_{\scriptscriptstyle{k}}}})\approx\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}}}\circ T_{\scriptscriptstyle{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}},\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X_{\scriptscriptstyle{k}}}}\circ T_{\scriptscriptstyle{\theta}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}})\right)^{\scriptscriptstyle{1/2}},

where
\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle{X_{\scriptscriptstyle{i}}}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{X_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}}}
is the empirical measure for the
i-
th sample,
i=1,\dots,n,

\{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{l}}\}_{\scriptscriptstyle{l=1}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}
are
i.i.d.
samples drawn from the uniform distribution on

The random responses

-1/\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix},

\Lambda=\mbox{diag}(\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{1}},\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle{2}})

Model III.1 (Euclidean predictors)

The real-valued predictors

\mu_{j}(x)=b_{j}-2(x-c_{j})^{2}
,

. Here

and

, and

are sampled independently of

\Sigma(x)

A

Scenario 4: Network object-on-object regression

Model IV.1 (Euclidean predictors)
: Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple (with no self-loops), weighted, undirected network with a set of nodes
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\}
and a set of edge weights
E=\{w_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}:w_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}\geq 0,\ i,j=1,\dots,r\}
, where
w_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}=0
indicates
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
are not connected and
w_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}>0
otherwise, with
w_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}<M
for some
M>0.
A network can be uniquely represented by its graph Laplacian
L=(l_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}),
where
l_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}=-w_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}\text{ if }i\neq j
and
l_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}=\sum_{k\neq i}w_{ik}\text{ if }i=j,

for
i,j=1,\dots,r
.
The space of graph Laplacians is given by
\mathcal{L}_{r}=\{L=(l_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}):L=L^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}},\ L1_{r}=0_{r},\ -W\leq l_{\scriptscriptstyle{ij}}\leq 0\ \text{ for some W }\geq 0\text{ and }i\neq j\},
where
1_{r}
and
0_{r}
are the
r
-vectors of ones and zeroes, respectively.
Note that
\mathcal{L}_{r}
is not a linear space, but a bounded, closed, and convex subset in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{r^{2}}}
of dimension
r(r-1)/2
. Owing to the fact that
x^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}Lx\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle{r}}
and
L\in\mathcal{L}_{r}
, it can be seen as a metric space of positive-semidefinite matrix objects, equipped with a suitable choice of metric such as the Frobenius or power metric.

To assess the performance of our proposed methods, we consider the space
(\mathcal{L}_{r},d_{\scriptscriptstyle{F}})
, where
d_{\scriptscriptstyle{F}}
is the Frobenius metric as per (
11
). The data generation mechanism, as follows, is similar to that in
Zhou and Müller, 2022
. Denote the half vectorization
excluding the diagonal of a symmetric and centered matrix by
vech
, with inverse operation
vech^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}
. By the symmetry and centrality, every graph Laplacian
L
is fully known by its upper (or lower) triangular part, which can then be vectorized into
vech(L)
, a vector of length
d=r(r-1)/2
. We construct the conditional distributions
F_{L|X}
by assigning an independent beta distribution to each element of
vech(L)
. Specifically, a random
sample
(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
is generated using beta distributions whose parameters depend on the scalar predictor
X
and vary under different simulation scenarios. The random response
L

is then generated conditional on
X
through an inverse half vectorization
vech^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}
applied to
(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
. The the true
regression function
m(x)
is defined as
m(x)=vech^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}(-x,\dots,-x)
,
L=vech^{\scriptscriptstyle{-1}}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\top}}
, where
\beta_{j}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}Beta(X,1-X).

To ensure that the random response
L
generated in simulations resides in
\mathcal{L}_{r}
, the off-diagonal entries
-\beta_{j}

j=1,\dots,d,
need to be nonpositive and bounded below. Thus we choose
\beta_{j}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}Beta(X,1-X)
. The scalar predictor
X_{i}
are randomly sampled from a
Unif(0,1)
distribution to obtain the samples of pairs
(X_{i},L_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,n
, setting
r=5,20
, and following the above procedure.
The prediction error w.r.t the Frobenius metric is shown in the rightmost column of Table
6
. The method performs better for higher
n
and lower
r.

The fit of the model is further demonstrated by computing the estimation error by virtue of the residual map for the
i^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{th}}}
subject given by
T_{i}:\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}\to\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
, which is defined as the optimal transport map
T_{i}=\nu_{i}\#\hat{\nu}_{i},
that pushes forward the observed response
\nu_{i}
to the fitted value
\hat{\nu}_{i}
. Using the theory of optimal transport for univariate distributions
(

)
, this map can be explicitly computed as
T_{i}=Q_{\hat{\nu}_{i}}\circ F_{\nu_{i}}
, where
Q_{\hat{\nu}_{i}}
and
F_{\nu_{i}}
are, respectively, the quantile function and the CDF of the distributions
\hat{\nu}_{i}
and
\nu_{i}
.
Using these residual maps, one can obtain an analog of the “residual plot” in the classical regression case, compared to the identity map. Looking at the deviation from the identity map, one can see in which parts of the support of the distributions the model provides a good fit and where less so, and the departure from the identity can serve as a diagnostic tool for the validity of the model. Note
that, contrary to classical regression, where the residuals add up to zero by construction, the residual
maps are not constrained to have a mean equal to the identity.

As a measure of the efficacy of the fitted model, we compute the mean prediction error as the Wasserstein discrepancy between the observed and the predicted distributions in the test set. We repeat the process
100

0.693
with a standard error of
0.151)
, supporting the efficacy of the model.

8
Discussion

In this contribution, we proposed a nonlinear global object-on-object regression method based on the intrinsic geometry of the metric space where the responses reside coupled with suitable linear operators defined via the reproducing kernel Hilbert space on the predictor space. This contribution is one of the first to model the regression relationship between metric-valued object pairs beyond scalar-or-vector-valued predictors. Further, we bridge the gap between the conditional Fréchet mean, and the globally linear Fréchet means proposed by
Petersen and Müller, 2019
by introducing the notion of a more general weak conditional Fréchet mean. This provides a way to link random object data analysis to non-linear global reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) regression models, allowing for arbitrary non-linear functions beyond linear or polynomial regression.
In the process of defining the weak conditional Fréchet mean, the weak conditional moments for the classical Hilbertian objects are discussed, and the relevant properties are proved, which is an important construct on its own and makes a separate contribution to the literature.

The concept of weak Fréchet moments can be extended to Fréchet median or as a minimizer of Huber loss by substituting
E[d^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}(Y,\cdot)\mbox{\raisebox{-0.50003pt}{$\,\overset{\overset{\rule[0.0pt]{0.29439pt}{1.10413pt}}{\rule[0.0pt]{0.32771pt}{1.22911pt}}}{\rule[0.0pt]{0.39993pt}{1.49994pt}}\,$}}X]
by
E[\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}(Y,\cdot)\mbox{\raisebox{-0.50003pt}{$\,\overset{\overset{\rule[0.0pt]{0.29439pt}{1.10413pt}}{\rule[0.0pt]{0.32771pt}{1.22911pt}}}{\rule[0.0pt]{0.39993pt}{1.49994pt}}\,$}}X]
, for any appropriate convex loss function
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
in the metric space
(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}},d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}})
, depending on the context and interpretation of the problem. This calls for potential future research. The selection of a suitable metric in the response or predictor space is also an open problem.

Further, the rate of convergence of the proposed estimator is derived as
\approx n^{-1/4},
which entails from the work of
Li and Song, 2017
. This rate can be further improved using a suitable rate carried out from the RKHS regression literature.

Appendix A
Technical assumptions for M-estimators

Assumption 11

The weak conditional Fréchet means
f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x)
and
\hat{f}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x)
exist and are unique, the latter almost surely. Further, the minimizer at the population level is well separated. i.e.,
for any
\epsilon>0,

\underset{d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}(y,f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x))>\epsilon}{\inf\ }J(y,x)-J(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x),x)>0.

Assumption 12

Let
B_{\delta}(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x))\subset\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}
be the ball of radius
\delta
, centered at
f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x)
and
N(\epsilon,B_{\delta}(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x)),d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}})
be its covering number using balls of radius
\epsilon
. Then the entropy integral is computed from the covering number given by

J=J(\delta):=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1+\log N(\delta\epsilon,B_{\delta}(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x)),d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}})}d\epsilon=O(1)\text{ as }\delta\to 0.

Assumption 13

There exist constants
\eta>0
,
C>0
, and
\beta>1
,
possibly depending on
x\in(\Omega_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}},d_{\scriptscriptstyle{X}})
, such that

J(y,x)-J(f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x),x)\geq Cd_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\beta}}(y,f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x)),

for any small neighborhood
d_{\scriptscriptstyle{Y}}(y,f_{\scriptscriptstyle{\oplus}}(x))<\eta.

Assumption
11
is commonly used to establish the consistency of an M-estimator; see Chapter 3.2 in
Van der Vaart and Wellner, 2000
. In particular, it ensures that weak convergence of the empirical process
\tilde{J}_{n}
to the population process
J
, which in turn implies convergence of their minimizers. The conditions on the covering number in Assumption
12
and curvature in Assumption
13
arise from empirical process theory and control the behavior of
\tilde{J}_{n}-J
near the minimum, which is necessary to obtain rates of convergence.
These assumptions are again satisfied for many random objects of interest, the common examples of random objects such as distributions, covariance matrices, networks, and so on (see Propositions 1-3 of
Petersen and Müller, 2019
).
