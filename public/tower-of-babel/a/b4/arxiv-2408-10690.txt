Spectral Function Space Learning and Numerical Linear Algebra Networks for Solving Linear Inverse Problems
Andrea Aspri, Leon Frischauf, Otmar Scherzer

Abstract
We consider solving a probably ill-conditioned linear operator equation, where the operator is not modeled by physical laws but is specified via training pairs (consisting of images and data) of the input-output relation of the operator. We derive a stable method for computing the operator, which consists of first a Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization of images and a principal component analysis of the data. This two-step algorithm provides a spectral decomposition of the linear operator. Moreover, we show that both Gram-Schmidt and principal component analysis can be written as a deep neural network, which relates this procedure to de-and encoder networks. Therefore, we call the two-step algorithm a linear algebra network. Finally, we provide numerical simulations showing the strategy is feasible for reconstructing spectral functions and for solving operator equations without explicitly exploiting the physical model.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

In the following, we analyze under which assumptions the PCA is stable. Starting point of this discussion is
Seidman’s veto

[
26
]
, which states that regularization by projection is in general not a regularization method. This means that by projecting onto
X_{S}
inversion of
F
on the range of
F
of
X_{S}
is not stable. On the other hand if
F
is inverted on
Y_{S}
, then it is in fact stable (see again
[
26
]
). However, in the context of machine learning this requires to collect training data of
F^{*}{\bf{y}}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,S
, which is in general not available. As a conclusion from
[
26
]
we find that
F
can be stably inverted by projection if
X_{S}
is the space of the singular vectors corresponding to the largest
S
singular values (see for instance
[
12
]
). We emphasize again, that here stability refers to inversion of the truncated SVD after orthonormalization. Now, we show how this singular values can be calculated with machine learning techniques:

Theorem 2.3
(Spectral theory: See Theorem 2.5.2 in
[
15
]
).

Let the operator
F:\mathds{R}^{\underline{m}}\to\mathds{R}^{\overline{m}}
be linear. Then for every
{\bf{x}}\in\mathds{R}^{\underline{m}}

F{\bf{x}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\min\left\{\underline{m},\overline{m}\right\}}\gamma^{j}\left<{\bf{x}},{\bf{u}}^{j}\right>{\bf{v}}^{j}\text{ and }F^{T}{\bf{v}}^{j}=\gamma^{j}{\bf{u}}^{j},F{\bf{u}}^{j}=\gamma^{j}{\bf{v}}^{j},

(2.5)

where
{\bf{u}}^{j}\in\mathds{R}^{\underline{m}}
,
j=1,\ldots,\underline{m}
and
{\bf{v}}^{j}\in\mathds{R}^{\overline{m}}
,
j=1,\ldots,\overline{m}
are orthonormal, respectively, and

0\leq\gamma^{1}\leq\gamma^{2}\leq\cdots\leq\gamma^{\min\left\{\underline{m},\overline{m}\right\}}.

In matrix form, this identity becomes more compact:

F={\bf V}D{\bf U}^{T}\text{ with }{\bf U}\in\mathds{R}^{{\underline{m}\times\underline{m}}},{\bf V}\in\mathds{R}^{{\overline{m}\times\overline{m}}},

(2.6)

where
{\bf U}
and
{\bf V}
are orthonormal and

\displaystyle D=\text{diag}(\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2},\cdots,\gamma^{\min\left\{\underline{m},\overline{m}\right\}})\in\mathds{R}^{\overline{m}\times\underline{m}}.

In the following, we group the spectral values:

Definition 2.4
.

Let

be linear with trivial nullspace with spectral decomposition as in

. We denote by

\Gamma:=\left\{\gamma^{j}:j=1,\ldots,\min\left\{\underline{m},\overline{m}\right\}\right\}=\left\{\hat{\gamma}^{k}:k=1,\ldots,\hat{m}\right\}

(2.7)

the set of distinct singular values. Because

is assumed to have trivial nullspace
\gamma^{j}>0
for all
j=1,\ldots,\min\left\{\underline{m},\overline{m}\right\}
. Moreover, we associate to each multiple singular value the associated singular vectors:
For every

let

E^{k}:=\text{span}\left\{{\bf{v}}^{j}:\gamma^{j}=\hat{\gamma}^{k},j=1,\ldots,\min\left\{\underline{m},\overline{m}\right\}\right\}.

(2.8)

Now, we apply the spectral theory to show that the proposed decoding algorithm is stable.

Definition 2.5

With this result, we can state the main result of this paper:

Theorem 2.6

Let

{{\overline{{\mathbf{W}}}}}

Remark 2.7
.

Theorem 2.6
tells us

(i)

that orthonormalization of the training images is the basis of stable decoding. Problematic is, however, that orthonormalization may not provide us with an ordering of the singular vectors. For instance, there is no guarantee
that singular vectors can be recovered according to low indices, which usually carry most of the information.

(ii)

Orthonormalization is unstable. Thus,

2.3. PCA expressed as a deep network

This subsection shows that spectral value decomposition can be expressed as a customized deep network. Thereby, we make use of the fact that

Let us denote the column vectors of

Then it follows (see

(2.12)

Writing this in matrix notation reads as follows:

A=QR\text{ with }Q=({\overline{\bf a}}_{1},\ldots,{\overline{\bf a}}_{n})\text{ and }R_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\left<{\bf a}_{j},{\overline{\bf a}}_{i}\right>&\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,j-1\\

In fact, it follows from

:

{\overline{{\bf a}}}_{j}=\frac{1}{\left\|\rho({\bf a}_{j})\right\|}\left({\bf a}_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\left<{\bf a}_{j},{\overline{\bf a}}_{i}\right>{\overline{\bf a}}_{i}\right)=\sigma(\rho({\bf{a}}_{j})),\quad j=1,\ldots,\overline{m},

(2.14)

Figure 3
with

3. Image representation and decoder

We consider the decoder as represented in

(3.1)

From

We denote the dimension of

From

In other words

\psi_{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{m(l)}\nu_{j}{\bf{v}}^{l(j)}.

(3.3)

Applying
Equation 2.5
and
Equation 2.8
then implies that

\psi_{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{m(l)}\nu_{j}{\bf{v}}^{l(j)}=\frac{1}{\hat{\gamma}^{k(l)}}F\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m(l)}\nu_{j}{\bf{u}}^{l(j)}\right).

We summarize the calculations now in the following lemma:

Lemma 3.1
(Decoder).

The minimum norm solution of
Equation 3.2
is
given by

\displaystyle x_{ls}=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\left<{\bf{y}},\psi_{l}\right>\left(\frac{1}{\hat{\gamma}^{k(l)}}\sum_{j=1}^{m(l)}\nu_{j}{\bf{u}}^{l(j)}\right)=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\frac{\left<{\bf{y}},\psi_{l}\right>}{(\hat{\gamma}^{k(l)})^{2}}F^{*}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m(l)}\nu_{j}{\bf{v}}^{l(j)}\right)=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\frac{\left<{\bf{y}},\psi_{l}\right>}{(\hat{\gamma}^{k(l)})^{2}}F^{*}\psi_{l},

(3.4)

and therefore is a

.

We take as a prototype example for
F
the Radon transform in two dimensions (see
Definition 4.2
below). The singular value decomposition of the Radon transform has been computed in
[
9
]
(see also
[
22
]
) for the general case of images of
n
variables. In particular for
n=2
we consider the Radon transform as an operator from
L^{2}(\mathcal{B}(0,1))
into
L^{2}(Z,w^{-1})
, where
\mathcal{B}(0,1)
is the unit disk in
\mathds{R}^{2}
centered at the origin,
Z=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[-1,1]
, and
w(s)=(1-s^{2})^{1/2}
is a weight function. This means that
L^{2}(Z,w^{-1})
is the weighted
L^{2}
-space with norm

\|g\|_{L^{2}(Z,w^{-1})}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\int_{-1}^{1}|g(\omega,s)|^{2}w(s)^{-1}\ ds\,\mathrm{d}\omega\;.

for
g\in L^{2}(Z,w^{-1})
.
Before diving into the numerical details, we provide the necessary notational clarifications and preliminary information to facilitate the analysis.

Convention 4.1
.

For
\vec{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
we use the angular representation
\phi=\tan^{-1}(\omega_{2}/\omega_{1})\in[0,2\pi)
, which therefore satisfies

\omega_{1}=\cos(\phi)\text{ and }\omega_{2}=\sin(\phi).

(4.1)

Moreover, the orthogonal vector related to
\vec{\omega}
is given by

\vec{\omega}^{\bot}=(-\sin(\phi),\cos(\phi))^{T}
.

For the reader’s convenience we recall the definition of the Radon transform for functions on the unit disk in
\mathds{R}^{2}
:

Definition 4.2
(Radon transform on the unit disk).

Let
Z:=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[-1,1]
. We define the Radon transform as

\displaystyle R:L^{2}(\mathcal{B}(0,1))

\displaystyle\to L^{2}({Z},w^{-1}).

(4.2)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto R[f](\vec{\omega},s)=\int_{-\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}^{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}f\left(s\vec{\omega}+t\vec{\omega}^{\bot}\right)dt

One can show that this operator is continuous and satisfies (see
[
9
]
):

\|R[f]\|_{L^{2}({Z},w^{-1})}\leq\sqrt{4\pi}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{B}(0,1))}.

In the following, we recall the expression of the adjoint operator of the Radon-transform
R
defined on the unit disk. This is the operator
R^{*}:L^{2}(Z,w^{-1})\to L^{2}(\mathcal{B}(0,1))
, which satisfies

\left<R[f],g\right>_{L^{2}({Z},w^{-1})}=\left<f,R^{*}[g]\right>_{L^{2}(\mathcal{B}(0,1))}.

Definition 4.3
(Adjoint).

For every
g\in L^{2}(Z,w^{-1})
and almost all
\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})^{T}\in\mathds{R}^{2}
the adjoint of the Radon-transform
\mathds{R}^{*}
is given by

R^{*}[g](\vec{x})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{g(\vec{\omega},\vec{x}\cdot\vec{\omega})}{w(\vec{x}\cdot\vec{\omega})}\,d\vec{\omega}.

In the following we give a survey on the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the Radon transform for functions on the unit disk. The results are essential taken from
[
22
]
with the main difference that the we consider the adjoint
R^{*}
restricted to the range of
R
(in contrast to
Definition 4.3
).

Before recalling the spectral decomposition of the Radon-transform, we review the general definition of a spectral decomposition:

Definition 4.4
(Spectral decomposition,
[
12
]
).

Let
K:X\to Y
be a compact linear operator. A
singular system

(u_{k},v_{k};\gamma_{k})
is defined as follows:

(i)

\gamma_{k}^{2}
,
k\in\mathds{N}_{0}
are the non-zero eigenvalues of the selfadjoint operator
K^{*}K
(and also
KK^{*}
) written in decreasing order. We always take
\gamma_{k}>0
.

(ii)

u_{k}
,
k\in\mathds{N}_{0}
are a complete orthonormal system of eigenvectors of
K^{*}K
(on the space
\overline{\mathcal{R}(K^{*})}=\overline{\mathcal{R}(K^{*}K)}
.

(iii)

v_{k}:=\frac{1}{\left\|Ku_{k}\right\|}Ku_{k}
,
k\in\mathds{N}_{0}
.

The set
\left\{v_{k}:k\in\mathds{N}_{0}\right\}
are a complete orthonormal system of eigenvectors of
KK^{*}
which span
\overline{\mathcal{R}(K)}=\overline{\mathcal{R}(KK^{*})}
. Moreover, the following formulas hold:

\displaystyle Ku_{k}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{k}v_{k},\quad K^{*}u_{k}=\gamma_{k}v_{k},

\displaystyle Kx

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\left<x,u_{k}\right>v_{k}\text{ for all }x\in X,\quad K^{*}y

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\left<y,v_{k}\right>u_{k}\text{ for all }y\in Y.

Theorem 4.5
.

[
22
, p 99]

The spectral decomposition of the Radon transform is given by
\left\{(u_{k,l},v_{k,l};\gamma_{k,l}):(k,l)\in\mathcal{I}\right\}

where

(i)

\mathcal{I}=\left\{(k,l):k\in\mathds{N}_{0},l\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,k\right\},\text{ satisfying }l+k\text{ is even}\right\}.

(ii)

\gamma_{k}^{2}=\gamma_{k,l}^{2}=\frac{4\pi}{k+1}>0
is independent of
l
.

(iii)

Let
Y_{s}
be the spherical harmonics in
\mathds{R}^{2}
and let

s\mapsto C_{k}(s)=\frac{\sin\left((k+1)\arccos{(s)}\right)}{\sin(\arccos{(s)})}

denote the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind. Moreover, let

\frac{1}{c(k)}=\left\|wC_{k}Y_{k-2l}\right\|_{L^{2}(Z,w^{-1})}=\left\|\sqrt{w}C_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}.

Then the normalized eigenfunctions of
RR^{*}
on the orthogonal complement of the nullspace of
R^{*}
,
\mathcal{N}^{\bot}
are given by

(s,\omega)\to v_{k,l}(s,\omega):=c(k)w(s)C_{k}(s)Y_{k-2l}(\omega)\text{ and }u_{k,l}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}R^{*}[v_{kl}]\;.

(4.3)

The calculation of the singular value decomposition of the Radon transform has been computed in several work (see for instance
[

,
21
,
22
]
and
[
23
, Theorem 6.4]
to mention but a few).

Remark 4.6
.

(i)

The existence of a singular value decomposition with
\gamma_{k}\to 0
in particular shows that the Radon-transform is compact.

(ii)

From
Theorem 4.5
,
item (i)
and
item (ii)
we see that, in general,
\gamma_{k}
has multiplicity higher than one. The sets
E^{k}
associated to a spectral value
\gamma_{k}
are spanned by the spectral functions
v_{kl}
with
l
such that
(k,l)\in\mathcal{I}
. For example, taking
k=0,\ldots,7
, and
l=0,\ldots,k
, with
k+l
even (meaning that
(k,l)\in\mathcal{I}
), we have

\displaystyle E^{1}

\displaystyle=\textrm{span}\left\{v_{0,0}\right\},\quad E^{2}=\textrm{span}\left\{v_{1,1}\right\},\quad E^{3}=\textrm{span}\left\{v_{2,0},v_{2,2}\right\},\quad E^{4}=\textrm{span}\left\{v_{3,1},v_{3,3}\right\},

(4.4)

\displaystyle E^{5}

\displaystyle=\textrm{span}\left\{v_{4,0},v_{4,2},v_{4,4}\right\},\quad E^{6}=\textrm{span}\left\{v_{5,1},v_{5,3},v_{5,5}\right\},\quad E^{7}=\textrm{span}\left\{v_{6,0},v_{6,2},v_{6,4},v_{6,6}\right\},

\displaystyle E^{8}

\displaystyle=\textrm{span}\left\{v_{7,1},v_{7,3},v_{7,5},v_{7,7}\right\}.

It is an interesting fact stated in
[

]
that the adjoint of the Radon-transform

has a nullspace. In fact the nullspace of
\mathds{R}^{*}
is given by

\mathcal{N}=\left\{v_{k,l}:k\in\mathds{N}_{0},l\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,k\right\},\text{ satisfying }l+k\text{ is odd}\right\}.

In fact also

Conclusion

In this paper we have shown that a double orthonormalization strategy, consisting first of orthonormalization of training images and second of a principal component analysis of the training data theoretically provides us with the singular value decomposition of a linear operator (without making use of an explicit physical model). Two further interesting aspects are shown: First: Orthonormalization, like Gram-Schmidt, can be expressed as a deep neural network, and this opens up exploiting synergies with de-and encoder strategies. Secondly, not only the singular value decomposition can be implemented purely data driven but also the decoding, meaning that for arbitrary data the minimum-norm solution can be computed data driven.
