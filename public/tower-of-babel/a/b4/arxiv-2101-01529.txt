Abstract
. If Z Z is an open subscheme of Spec ⁡ ℤ \operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z} , X X is a sufficiently nice Z Z -model of a smooth curve over ℚ \mathbb{Q} , and p p is a closed point of Z Z , the Chabauty-Kim method leads to the construction of locally analytic functions on X ⁡ ( ℤ p ) X({\mathbb{Z}_{p}}) which vanish on X ⁡ ( Z ) X(Z) ; we call such functions “Kim functions”. At least in broad outline, the method generalizes readily to higher dimensions. In fact, in some sense, the surface M 0 , 5 M_{0,5} should be easier than the previously studied curve M 0 , 4 = ℙ 1 ∖ { 0 , 1 , ∞ } M_{0,4}=\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{0,1,\infty\} since its points are closely related to those of M 0 , 4 M_{0,4} , yet they face a further condition to integrality. This is mirrored by a certain weight advantage we encounter, because of which, M 0 , 5 M_{0,5} possesses new Kim functions not coming from M 0 , 4 M_{0,4} . Here we focus on the case “ ℤ ⁡ [ 1 / 6 ] \mathbb{Z}[1/6] in half-weight 4”, where we provide a first nontrivial example of a Kim function on a surface. Central to our approach to Chabauty-Kim theory (as developed in works by S. Wewers, D. Corwin, and the first author) is the possibility of separating the geometric part of the computation from its arithmetic context. However, we find that in this case the geometric step grows beyond the bounds of standard algorithms running on current computers. Therefore, some ingenuity is needed to solve this seemingly straightforward problem, and our new Kim function is huge.



1.
Introduction

The Chabauty-Kim method, introduced in
[
Kim1
,
Kim2
]
, extends the classical Chabauty method in two (related) directions. By going to higher quotients of the fundamental group (where the Chabauty method stops at the abelianization) it produces
p
-adic analytic functions (“Kim functions”) which vanish on integral points, beyond the Chabauty bound. Thus, it can be applied in cases where the Chabauty method does not apply. However, even in cases where the Chabauty method does apply to produce a
p
-adic analytic function which can be used to bound the set of integral points, it rarely produces a sharp bound. As one climbs up the tower of unipotent quotients, however, the Chabauty-Kim method produces more functions. Together, these may be used to give a sharp bound. Indeed, according to Kim’s conjecture
[
BDCKW
]
, this should be the case for (suitable integral models) of all hyperbolic curves.

Going exactly one step beyond the abelian quotient leads to the so-called
quadratic Chabauty method
. In a growing number of cases
[
BD2
,
BDCKW
,
BD1
,
BBM2
,
BBM1
,
BB
,
BKK
,
Kim3
]
this has been worked out to produce numerical results, and those results have been used to provide numerical evidence for the conjecture. Of particular note is the work
[
BDM+
]
whose final point-count (apart from verifying another case of the conjecture) solved an old and sought-after problem in arithmetic.

The methods of Dan-Cohen–Wewers
[
DCW1
,
DCW2
,
DC
]
and Corwin–Dan-Cohen
[
CDC1
,
CDC2
]
, while so far limited to the simplest of all cases (

), have been particularly successful in going beyond the quadratic level. These articles incorporate the methods of mixed Tate motives and motivic iterated integrals (see, for instance,
[
DG
,

,
Bro1
,

]
). A key point is the possibility of extracting the geometric aspects of the computation from their natural arithmetic surroundings. The result is an algorithm which includes among its subalgorithms a
geometric step
and an
arithmetic step
. The arithmetic step involves a search for enough motivic iterated integrals to generate suitable portions of the mixed Tate Hopf algebra, and its halting is conditional on conjectures of Goncharov, refined somewhat with respect to ramification. Before embarking on the present work, we regarded the geometric step as being comparatively simple, as it may, in principle, be solved by standard algorithms.

Kim’s method generalizes naturally to higher dimensions. The connection with the section conjecture suggests that a suitable generalization of Kim’s conjecture may hold for anabelian varieties. In this article we take a conservative step in this direction. Kim’s conjecture for

over

implies Kim’s conjecture for

to

not coming from

(see §

Our first opportunity to take advantage of this weight advantage occurs for

in half-weight

Our conclusions (so far) are somewhat mixed. Much of the work for

With a careful (but elementary) analysis of the geometric step (and a certain method involving resultants) we are nevertheless able to produce a new Kim function, which turns out to be huge.

Speaking of
order
, a word is in order concerning the order of multiplication in fundamental groups. For many reasons, it seems to us far more natural to let
\alpha\beta
denote first
\beta
, then
\alpha
. For instance, this is the notation used in
category theory
, which is why it is sometimes referred to as the “functorial order”. The reverse “lexicographic” order, it seems to us, leads to the systematic reversal of a vast swath of mathematics. However, for reasons we do not understand, there appears to be quite a tradition of using lexicographic order, and the authors of
[
CDC1
]
, in particular, chose to follow this tradition. Thus, we’re forced to resolve this conflict within the body of the article by using both orderings and spelling out where and how we transition between them. For this purpose, we prefer not to think of the question of ordering as being merely a matter of
notation
. Rather, given a Tannakian category, we have a
functorial fundamental groupoid
and a
lexicographic fundamental groupoid
, and the one is the opposite of the other. To prevent any confusion, we systematically denote objects stemming from the lexicographic fundamental groupoid with a superscript ‘
\times

lexicographic

Y=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}[z_{1},z_{2},z_{1}^{-1},z_{2}^{-1},(1-z_{1})^{-1},(1-z_{2})^{-1},(1-z_{1}z_{2})^{-1}].

The modular interpretations of these functions are determined by the formulas

x(\mathbb{P}^{1},0,1,\infty,a)=a,

\hskip 10.00002ptz_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1},0,1,\infty,c,d)=\frac{c}{d},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002ptz_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{1},0,1,\infty,c,d)=d.

In terms of our presentations, the map

f:(\mathbb{P}^{1},0,1,\infty,c,d)\mapsto(\mathbb{P}^{1},0,1,\infty,d)

corresponds to the second projection

Y\to X

which extends to

{\mathbb{G}_{m}}\times{\mathbb{G}_{m}}\to{\mathbb{G}_{m}}

and respects our chosen tangential base-points. This means that it induces a
{\mathbb{G}_{m}}
-equivariant map of polylogarithmic quotients

\pi^{\mathrm{PL}}(Y)\to\pi^{\mathrm{PL}}(X).

Thus, for any
Z\subset\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in Z
, if
F
is a Kim function on
X({\mathbb{Z}_{p}})
associated to
Z
and to
\pi^{\mathrm{PL}}_{\geq-n}(X)
, then
f^{\sharp}F
is a Kim function on
Y({\mathbb{Z}_{p}})
associated to
Z
and to
\pi^{\mathrm{PL}}_{\geq-n}(Y)
.

But the function of Theorem
1.1
is not of this form. Indeed, a simple dimension count shows that (regardless of
p
) there are no Kim functions for
X
over
Z=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}[1/6]
in half-weight
n=4
.
