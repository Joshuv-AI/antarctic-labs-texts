Boolean Functions on S n S_{n} Which Are Nearly Linear
Yuval Filmus

Abstract
We show that if f : S n → { 0 , 1 } f\colon S_{n}\to\{0,1\} is ϵ \epsilon -close to linear in L 2 L_{2} and 𝔼 [ f ] ≤ 1 / 2 \E[f]\leq 1/2 then f f is O ⁡ ( ϵ ) O(\epsilon) -close to a union of “mostly disjoint” cosets, and moreover this is sharp: any such union is close to linear. This constitutes a sharp Friedgut–Kalai–Naor theorem for the symmetric group. Using similar techniques, we show that if f : S n → ℝ f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R} is linear, Pr [ f ∉ { 0 , 1 } ] ≤ ϵ \Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]\leq\epsilon , and Pr [ f = 1 ] ≤ 1 / 2 \Pr[f=1]\leq 1/2 , then f f is O ⁡ ( ϵ ) O(\epsilon) -close to a union of mostly disjoint cosets, and this is also sharp; and that if f : S n → ℝ f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R} is linear and ϵ \epsilon -close to { 0 , 1 } \{0,1\} in L ∞ L_{\infty} then f f is O ⁡ ( ϵ ) O(\epsilon) -close in L ∞ L_{\infty} to a union of disjoint cosets.

Introduction

Consider a Boolean function
f\colon S_{n}\to\{0,1\}
, where
S_{n}
is the symmetric group on
n
elements, consisting of all permutations of
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We say that
f
is
linear
if it can be written as

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i,j}x_{i,j},

where
x_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
is the indicator for the input permutation sending
i
to
j
. Ellis, Friedgut and Pilpel
[
7
]
showed that if
f
is a Boolean linear function on
S_{n}
then
f
must be a
dictator
: either
f(\pi)
depends only on
\pi(i)
for some
i\in[n]
, or it depends only on
\pi^{-1}(j)
for some
j\in[n]
(see
[
2
]
for an alternative proof).

In this paper, we answer several relaxed versions of the same question:

1.

What is the structure of a Boolean function
f\colon S_{n}\to\{0,1\}
which is
\epsilon
-close to linear, in the sense that there exists a linear function
g\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\E[(f-g)^{2}]\leq\epsilon
?

2.

What is the structure of a linear function
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
which is
\epsilon
-close to Boolean, in the sense that
\E[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]\leq\epsilon
? (Here
\dist(f,\{0,1\})=\min(|f-0|,|f-1|)
.)

3.

What is the structure of a linear function
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
which is
\epsilon
-close to Boolean, in the sense that
\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]\leq\epsilon
?

4.

What is the structure of a linear function
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
which is
\epsilon
-close to Boolean, in the sense that
\dist(f(\pi),\{0,1\})\leq\epsilon
for all
\pi\in S_{n}
?

The first two questions measure distance in the
L_{2}
metric, the third one measures distance in the
L_{0}
metric, and the fourth one measures distance in the
L_{\infty}
metric. Below, all notions of distance are
L_{2}
unless explicitly stated.

The answer to the first three questions is similar, and involves the notion of an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets. A
coset

(i,j)
consists of all permutations sending
i
to
j
, for some
i,j\in[n]
.
Two cosets
(i,j),(k,\ell)
are
disjoint
if the corresponding sets of permutations are disjoint; this happens when either
i=k
or
j=\ell
(but not both).
An
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets
is a collection
\mathcal{C}
of
O(n)
cosets such that the number of pairs
(i,j),(k,\ell)\in\mathcal{C}
which are not disjoint is
O(\epsilon n^{2})
.

We can now state our main structure theorems, answering all of the questions above. In the first three theorems, we assume that either
\E[f]\leq 1/2
or
\Pr[f=1]\leq 1/2
. To obtain a theorem covering all possible
f
, we simply need to consider
1-f
when
\E[f]>1/2
or
\Pr[f=1]>1/2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
f\colon S_{n}\to\{0,1\}
is
\epsilon
-close to linear and
\E[f]\leq 1/2
then there exists an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets
\mathcal{C}
such that
f
is
\epsilon
-close to
g=\max_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
, that is,
\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\epsilon)
.

Conversely, if
\mathcal{C}
is an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets then
g=\max_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
is
O(\epsilon)
-close to linear.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is linear,
\epsilon
-close to Boolean, and satisfies
\E[f]\leq 1/2
then there exists an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets
\mathcal{C}
such that
f
is
O(\epsilon)
-close to
g=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
, that is,
\E[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
.

Conversely, if
\mathcal{C}
is an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets then
g=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
is
O(\epsilon)
-close to Boolean.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is linear,
\epsilon
-close to Boolean in
L_{0}
, and satisfies
\Pr[f=1]\leq 1/2
then there exists an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets
\mathcal{C}
such that
f
is
O(\epsilon)
-close to
g=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
, that is,
\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\epsilon)
.

Conversely, if
\mathcal{C}
is an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets then
g=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
is
O(\epsilon)
-close to Boolean in
L_{0}
.

Answering the fourth question, we can guarantee that the cosets in
\mathcal{C}
are completely disjoint.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists a constant
\epsilon_{0}>0
for which the following holds.

If
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is linear and
\epsilon
-close to Boolean in
L_{\infty}
, where
\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}
, then
g(\pi)=\round(f(\pi),\{0,1\})
is a dictator.

Since
x_{i,j}
is the indicator function of the coset
(i,j)
, the function
g
in
Theorem

1.1
is the indicator function of the union of the cosets in
\mathcal{C}
.

If
f
is not very close to a constant, then we can approximate
f
by a dictator in all settings.

Theorem 1.5
.

The following holds for some constant
K>0
.
If
f\colon S_{n}\to\{0,1\}
is
\epsilon
-close to linear and
\delta\leq\E[f]\leq 1-\delta
, where
\delta\geq K\sqrt{\epsilon}
, then
f
is
O(\epsilon/\delta)
-close to a dictator.

Theorem 1.6
.

The following holds for some constant
K>0
.
If
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is linear,
\epsilon
-close to Boolean, and
\delta\leq\E[f]\leq 1-\delta
, where
\delta\geq K\sqrt{\epsilon}
, then
\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\epsilon/\delta)
for some dictator
g
.

Theorem 1.7
.

The following holds for some constant
K>0
.
If
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is linear,
\epsilon
-close to Boolean in
L_{0}
, and
\delta\leq\Pr[f=1]\leq 1-\delta
, where
\delta\geq K\sqrt{\epsilon}
, then
\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\epsilon/\delta)
for some dictator
g
.

The tradeoff between
\delta
and
\epsilon/\delta
is tight, as the following example shows:

f=\sum_{j=1}^{\delta n}x_{1j}+\sum_{j=1}^{(\epsilon/\delta)n}x_{2j}.

This function is roughly
(\delta+\epsilon/\delta)
-far from constant and
\min(\delta,\epsilon/\delta)
-far from a dictator.

Theorems

1.1
,
1.2
,
1.5
and
1.6
improve on prior work of the author together with Ellis and Friedgut
[
5
,
6
]
, on which we shall have more to say below. (We are not aware of prior work considering the
L_{0}
or
L_{\infty}
versions.)

Background

The
n
-dimensional
Boolean cube
is the set
\{0,1\}^{n}
. A function
f
on the Boolean cube is
linear
if it can be written as

f=c+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i},

where
x_{i}
is the value of the
i
’th input coordinate. It is not hard to see that a Boolean linear function on the Boolean cube is either constant or of the form
x_{i}
or
1-x_{i}
.

The classical Friedgut–Kalai–Naor (FKN) theorem
[
12
]
states that if
f
is a Boolean function which is
\epsilon
-close to linear, then
f
is
O(\epsilon)
-close to some Boolean linear function, and the same holds if we assume that
f
is a linear function which is
\epsilon
-close to Boolean. (The
L_{0}
version is less interesting, since if
f
is a linear function then either
f
is Boolean or
\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]\geq 1/4
; and the
L_{\infty}
version is easy.)

The FKN theorem is a standard result in an area of study known as
Boolean function analysis

[
20
]
. Traditionally, Boolean function analysis concerns itself with functions on the Boolean cube (and sometimes, Gaussian space). In the past decade, Boolean function analysis has been expanded to many other domains, due to applications in combinatorics and theoretical computer science.

One of the first domains to be explored in this way is the symmetric group. As part of their proof of the Deza–Frankl conjecture in Erdős–Ko–Rado theory, Ellis, Friedgut and Pilpel
[
7
]
showed that a Boolean linear function on
S_{n}
is a dictator; we call this an
exact
FKN theorem. Motivated by this application, Ellis, Friedgut and the author studied the structure of Boolean functions on
S_{n}
which are close to linear, in a sequence of two papers
[
5
,
6
]
. The first paper shows that if
f\colon S_{n}\to\{0,1\}
is sparse, that is
\E[f]=c/n
, and
\epsilon c/n
-close to linear, then
f
is
O(c^{2}(\sqrt{\epsilon}+1/n)/n))
-close to a maximum of
\round(c)
cosets, and moreover
c
is close to an integer. The second paper shows that if
f\colon S_{n}\to\{0,1\}
is balanced, that is
\eta\leq\E[f]\leq 1-\eta
, and
\epsilon
-close to linear, then
f
is
O(\eta^{-1}(\epsilon^{1/7}+1/n^{1/3}))
-close to a dictator. Both of these results are improved by our main theorems.

Exact and general FKN theorems have been proved for many other domains, such as product domains
[
21
,
13
,
18
]
, the slice
[
8
]
, the multislice
[
9
]
, and simplicial complexes
[
3
]
. An exact FKN theorem was proved for several classical association schemes
[
11
]
, but general FKN theorems are not known in these domains.

The higher-dimensional analog of the exact FKN theorem states that a degree
d
Boolean function on the cube depends on
O(2^{d})
coordinates
[
19
,
1
,
22
]
, and can be expressed as a decision tree of depth
O(d^{3})

[
19
,
17
]
. The corresponding FKN theorem is due to Kindler and Safra
[
15
,
16
]
, and states that if a Boolean function is
\epsilon
-close to degree
d
then it is
O(\epsilon)
-close to a Boolean degree
d
function. Together with Ihringer, we extended the former result to the slice
[
10
]
, and the latter result was extended to the slice by Keller and Klein
[
14
]
. Another extension of the Kindler–Safra theorem, to the biased Boolean cube, is due to Dinur, Harsha and the author
[
4
]
; this domain shares some of the confounding features of the symmetric group.

A function on the symmetric group has degree
d
if it can be written as a degree
d
polynomial in the variables
x_{i,j}
. When
d\geq 2
, a Boolean degree
d
function no longer depends on a constant number of “coordinates”, but it can be written as a decision tree of depth
O(d^{8})

[
2
]
, whose internal nodes contain queries of the form “
\pi(i)=?
” or “
\pi^{-1}(j)=?
”.

It is not clear what the extension of the Kindler–Safra theorem to the slice should look like; some ideas can be gleaned from
[
4
]
. Nevertheless, in the balanced case it is natural to conjecture the following.

Conjecture 1.8
.

If
f\colon S_{n}\to\{0,1\}
is
\epsilon
-close to degree
d
and
1/3\leq\E[f]\leq 2/3
then
f
is
O(\epsilon)
-close to a Boolean degree
d
function.

\E[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]=\epsilon

Section

, we show that

can be approximated by a function of the form

g=e+\sum_{i,j}e_{i,j}x_{i,j},

where

where
r
is an integer, the
r_{i,j}
are bounded integers,
O(n)
many of the
r_{i,j}
are non-zero, and furthermore, with probability
\Omega(1)
, a random permutation
\pi

for all

Given this, we deduce in

1-g
is close to a function of the form

h=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j},

where
\mathcal{C}
is an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets. This proves
Theorem

1.2
up to the possibility that
h
approximates
1-g
rather than
g
.

We prove a version of
Theorem

1.6
for
h
in

, and use it to derive

1.2

2.1
FKN theorem on the cube

We start by stating the classical Friedgut–Kalai–Naor theorem on the Boolean cube.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be a Boolean function satisfying
\E[(f^{>1})^{2}]=\epsilon
. Then
\E[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
for some function
g\in\{0,1,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},1-x_{1},\ldots,1-x_{n}\}
.

This formulation of the FKN theorem states that a Boolean function which is close to linear is close to a Boolean function depending on at most one coordinate. Another formulation states that a linear function which is close to Boolean is close to a Boolean function depending on at most one coordinate.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=c+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}.

If
\E[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]=\epsilon
then there exist
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}-d_{i})^{2}=O(\epsilon).

Furthermore, at most one of the
d_{i}
is non-zero.

Proof.

Let
F=\round(f,\{0,1\})
. Then
\E[(F^{>1})^{2}]\leq\E[(F-f)^{2}]=\E[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]=\epsilon
. According to
Theorem

2.1
,
\E[(F-g)^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
for some function
g
which is of one of the forms
0,1,x_{i},1-x_{i}
, where
i\in[n]
. Thus
\E[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\E[(f-F)^{2}]+\E[(F-g)^{2}])=O(\epsilon)
.

The next step is to consider the Fourier expansions of
f
and
g
, and to apply Parseval’s identity
\|f-g\|^{2}=\sum_{S}(\hat{f}(S)-\hat{g}(S))^{2}
. The Fourier expansion of
f
is

f=c+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(-1)^{x_{i}}.

The Fourier expansion of
g
is one of the following:

0,1,\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{x_{j}},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{x_{j}}.

In all cases, we can write

g=d-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}(-1)^{x_{i}},

where
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
, and at most one
d_{i}
is non-zero. Parseval’s identity implies that

\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}-d_{i})^{2}=\sum_{|S|=1}(\hat{f}(S)-\hat{g}(S))^{2}\leq\E[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\epsilon),

from which the corollary immediately follows.
∎

2.2
Reduction to the cube

The starting point of the proof is a reduction to the Boolean cube. Recall that our initial goal is proving
Theorems

1.2
and
1.6
. Accordingly, we are given a function
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i,j}x_{i,j}

such that
\E[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]=\epsilon
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
be two permutations of
[n]
, and let
S_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}\subset S_{n}
consist of all permutations which send
\{a_{2t-1},a_{2t}\}
to
\{b_{2t-1},b_{2t}\}
for
t\in[\lfloor n/2\rfloor]
; if
n
is odd, then these permutations necessarily send
a_{n}
to
b_{n}
. Let
f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
be the restriction of
f
to
S_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
, and let
\epsilon_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}=\E[\dist(f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}},\{0,1\})^{2}]
.

We identify
S_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
with an
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
-dimensional cube
\{0,1\}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
in the following way. Given
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})\in\{0,1\}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
, if
x_{t}=1
then the permutation sends
a_{2t-1}
to
b_{2t-1}
and
a_{2t}
to
b_{2t}
, and if
x_{t}=0
then the permutation sends
a_{2t-1}
to
b_{2t}
and
a_{2t}
to
b_{2t-1}
. Thus

f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})=c+\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(c_{a_{2t-1},b_{2t-1}}+c_{a_{2t},b_{2t}}-c_{a_{2t-1},b_{2t}}-c_{a_{2t},b_{2t-1}})x_{t},

where
c
is given by slightly different formulas according to the parity of
n
: when
n
is even,

c=\sum_{t=1}^{n/2}(c_{a_{2t-1},b_{2t}}+c_{a_{2t},b_{2t-1}}),

and when
n
is odd,

c=\sum_{t=1}^{(n-1)/2}(c_{a_{2t-1},b_{2t}}+c_{a_{2t},b_{2t-1}})+c_{a_{n},b_{n}}.

If we choose
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
uniformly at random and then a random permutation in
S_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
, then we obtain a uniformly random permutation. This shows that

\E_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}[\epsilon_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}]=\epsilon.

In the following subsection we use this, together with
Corollary

2.2
, to approximate
f
by a sparse weighted sum of
x_{i,j}
’s.

2.3
Sparse representation

Our goal in this subsection is to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a linear function satisfying
\E[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]=\epsilon
, where
\epsilon\leq 1
. Then there is an integer
e
and integers
e_{i,j}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
such that the function

g=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{i,j}x_{i,j}

satisfies
\E[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
. Moreover, only
O(n)
many of the
e_{i,j}
are non-zero.

Furthermore,
\Pr[g\notin\{0,1\}]=O(\epsilon)
.

Since
f
is linear, it can be written as

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i,j}x_{i,j}.

If
n=1
then
f=c_{1,1}
is constant, where
\dist(c_{1,1},\{0,1\})^{2}=\epsilon
. If
|c_{1,1}|^{2}=\epsilon
then we can take
g=0
, and if
|c_{1,1}-1|^{2}=\epsilon
then we can take
g=1
. From now on, we assume that
n\geq 2
.

Let
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in S_{n}
. Applying
Corollary

2.2
to
f_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
shows that for some
d_{1},\ldots,d_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
, we have

\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(c_{a_{2t-1},b_{2t-1}}+c_{a_{2t},b_{2t}}-c_{a_{2t-1},b_{2t}}-c_{a_{2t},b_{2t-1}}-d_{t})^{2}=O(\epsilon_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}).

Moreover, at most one of
d_{1},\ldots,d_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
is non-zero. In order to capitalize on that, define

e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}=\round(c_{i_{1},j_{1}}+c_{i_{2},j_{2}}-c_{i_{1},j_{2}}-c_{i_{2},j_{1}},\{0,\pm 1\}).

Clearly

\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(c_{a_{2t-1},b_{2t-1}}+c_{a_{2t},b_{2t}}-c_{a_{2t-1},b_{2t}}-c_{a_{2t},b_{2t-1}}-e_{a_{2t-1},a_{2t},b_{2t-1},b_{2t}})^{2}\leq\\
\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(c_{a_{2t-1},b_{2t-1}}+c_{a_{2t},b_{2t}}-c_{a_{2t-1},b_{2t}}-c_{a_{2t},b_{2t-1}}-d_{t})^{2}=O(\epsilon_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}),

(1)

which implies that

\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(e_{a_{2t-1},a_{2t},b_{2t-1},b_{2t}}-d_{t})^{2}=O(\epsilon_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}).

Since at most one of the
d_{t}
is non-zero, this implies that

\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}[e_{a_{2t-1},a_{2t},b_{2t-1},b_{2t}}\neq 0]\leq 1+O(\epsilon_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}).

(2)

Choosing
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
uniformly at random and taking expectation of (
1
), we obtain

\lfloor n/2\rfloor\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}\in[n]\\
i_{1}\neq i_{2},j_{1}\neq j_{2}\end{subarray}}[(c_{i_{1},j_{1}}+c_{i_{2},j_{2}}-c_{i_{1},j_{2}}-c_{i_{2},j_{1}}-e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}})^{2}]=O\bigl(\E[\epsilon_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}]\bigr)=O(\epsilon).

Doing the same for (
2
), we obtain

\lfloor n/2\rfloor\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}\in[n]\\
i_{1}\neq i_{2},j_{1}\neq j_{2}\end{subarray}}[e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}\neq 0]\leq 1+O(\epsilon)=O(1).

Markov’s inequality shows that each of the following holds with probability at least
2/3
over a random choice of
i_{1},j_{1}\in[n]
:

\displaystyle\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{2},j_{2}\in[n]\\
i_{2}\neq i_{1},j_{2}\neq j_{1}\end{subarray}}[(c_{i_{1},j_{1}}+c_{i_{2},j_{2}}-c_{i_{1},j_{2}}-c_{i_{2},j_{1}}-e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}})^{2}]=O(\epsilon/n),

(3)

\displaystyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{2},j_{2}\in[n]\\
i_{2}\neq i_{1},j_{2}\neq j_{1}\end{subarray}}[e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}\neq 0]=O(1/n).

(4)

Hence there exists a choice of
i_{1},j_{1}\in[n]
for which both of these statements hold simultaneously.

Let us take stock of our situation. We have shown that on average,

d_{i_{2},j_{2}}:=c_{i_{1},j_{1}}+c_{i_{2},j_{2}}-c_{i_{1},j_{2}}-c_{i_{2},j_{1}}\approx e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}},

where
e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
, and only
O(n)
of these coefficients are non-zero.
This suggests finding a different representation of
f
which involves the left-hand sides. Indeed,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{i,j}x_{i,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},j_{1}}x_{i,j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},j}x_{i,j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i,j_{1}}x_{i,j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i,j}x_{i,j}=\\
nc_{i_{1},j_{1}}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i,j_{1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i,j}x_{i,j},

which implies that for an appropriate
d
,

f=d+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{i,j}x_{i,j}.

Furthermore, defining
e_{i,j}:=e_{i_{1},i,j_{1},j}
, (
3
) implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(d_{i,j}-e_{i,j})^{2}=O(n\epsilon),

since
d_{i,j}=e_{i,j}=0
if
i=i_{1}
or
j=j_{1}
. Moreover, (
4
) shows that only
O(n)
of the coefficients
e_{i,j}
are non-zero.

We would now like to say that if we replace the coefficients
d_{i,j}
with the coefficients
e_{i,j}
then the resulting function is close to
f
. While this is correct, we have to be careful when doing the switch, replacing
x_{i,j}
with the corresponding centered term
x_{i,j}-1/n
. For an appropriate
d^{\prime}
,

f=d^{\prime}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{i,j}(x_{i,j}-1/n).

Define

g=d^{\prime}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{i,j}(x_{i,j}-1/n),

which for an appropriate
e
can also be written as

g=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{i,j}x_{i,j}.

Below, we will show that
\E[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
. In order to complete the proof of
Lemma

2.3
, we show that we can slightly modify
g
so that
e
becomes an integer.

Since
\E[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]=\epsilon
and
\E[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
, we have
\E[\dist(g,\{0,1\})^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
. Since all
e_{i,j}
are integers, this implies that
\dist(e,\mathbb{Z})^{2}=O(\epsilon)
. Let
e^{\prime}
be the integer closest to
e
, and define

g^{\prime}=e^{\prime}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{i,j}x_{i,j}.

Then
\E[(g-g^{\prime})^{2}]=(e-e^{\prime})^{2}=O(\epsilon)
and so
\E[(f-g^{\prime})^{2}]=\epsilon
.

The function
g^{\prime}
satisfies
\E[\dist(g^{\prime},\{0,1\})^{2}]=O(\E[\dist(f,\{0,1\})^{2}]+\E[(f-g^{\prime})^{2}])=O(\epsilon)
. Since
g^{\prime}
is integer-valued, this implies furthermore that
\Pr[g^{\prime}\notin\{0,1\}]=O(\epsilon)
.

In the rest of this subsection, we show that
\E[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
. If we define
\delta_{i,j}=d_{i,j}-e_{i,j}
then on the one hand

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{i,j}^{2}=O(\epsilon),

(5)

and on the other hand

f-g=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{i,j}(x_{i,j}-1/n).

Calculation shows that

\E[(x_{i_{1},j_{1}}-1/n)(x_{i_{2},j_{2}}-1/n)]=\E[x_{i_{1},j_{1}}x_{i_{2},j_{2}}]-\frac{1}{n^{2}}=\begin{cases}\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}&\text{if }i_{1}=j_{1}\text{ and }i_{2}=j_{2},\\
-\frac{1}{n^{2}}&\text{if }i_{1}=j_{1}\text{ or }i_{2}=j_{2}\text{ but not both},\\
\frac{1}{n^{2}(n-1)}&\text{if }i_{1}\neq j_{1}\text{ and }i_{2}\neq j_{2}.\end{cases}

It follows that

\E[(f-g)^{2}]=\underbrace{\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{i,j}^{2}}_{A}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2}\in[n]\\
j_{1}\neq j_{2}\end{subarray}}\delta_{i,j_{1}}\delta_{i,j_{2}}}_{B}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2}\in[n]\\
i_{1}\neq i_{2}\end{subarray}}\delta_{i_{1},j}\delta_{i_{2},j}}_{C}+\\
\underbrace{\frac{1}{n^{2}(n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2}\in[n]\\
i_{1}\neq i_{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2}\in[n]\\
j_{1}\neq j_{2}\end{subarray}}\delta_{i_{1},j_{1}}\delta_{i_{2},j_{2}}}_{D}.

Equation

5
immediately implies that
A=O(\epsilon)
. As for
B
, using
|\delta_{1}\delta_{2}|\leq(\delta_{1}^{2}+\delta_{2}^{2})/2
, we can bound

|B|\leq\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{i,j}^{2}=O(\epsilon).

The same bound holds for
C
. Finally, we can bound
D
in the same way:

|D|\leq\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{i,j}^{2}=O(\epsilon).

Altogether, this shows that
\E[(f-g)^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
, as wanted.

2.4
Sporadic representation

Lemma

2.3
constructs a function
g
which is almost Boolean in an
L_{0}
sense. Furthermore,
g
has a representation
g=e+\sum_{i,j}e_{i,j}x_{i,j}
which is sparse in the sense that the
support

\{(i,j):e_{i,j}\neq 0\}
has size
O(n)
. In order to make further progress, we need to find an alternative representation of
g
whose support satisfies the stronger property that with constant probability, a random permutation hits
no
coset in the support. We will have to pay for this by allowing larger coefficients in the sum.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
g\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}
be a function of the form

g=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{i,j}x_{i,j},

where
e\in\mathbb{Z}
;
e_{i,j}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
for all
i,j\in[n]
; at most
Cn
of the
e_{i,j}
are non-zero; and
\Pr[g\notin\{0,1\}]=\epsilon
. There exist constants
b,N\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\gamma>0
(possibly depending on
C
) such that if
n\geq N
then
g
has an alternative representation

g=r+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{i,j}x_{i,j},

where
r\in\mathbb{Z}
;
r_{i,j}\in\{-b,\ldots,b\}
for all
i,j\in[n]
; at most
O_{C}(n)
of the
r_{i,j}
are non-zero; and

\Pr_{\pi\in S_{n}}[r_{i,\pi(i)}=0\text{ for all }i\in[n]]\geq\gamma.

The first step in the proof is constructing the new representation. An obstacle for the “hitting” property is a
line
(row or column) in which all or almost all of the
e_{i,j}
are non-zero. Accordingly, let
\alpha_{i}\in\{-1,0,1\}
be a most common value of
e_{i,1},\ldots,e_{i,n}
, let
\beta_{j}\in\{-1,0,1\}
be the most common value of
e_{1,j},\ldots,e_{n,j}
, and define
r_{i,j}=e_{i,j}-\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j}
, so that

g=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(r_{i,j}+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j})x_{i,j}=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{i,j}x_{i,j}.

Note that
r=e+\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
r_{i,j}\in\{0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\pm 3\}
.

We claim that most of the coefficients
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}
are equal to zero. Indeed, if
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
then among
e_{i,1},\ldots,e_{i,n}
, at least
(2/3)n
are non-zero. Therefore at most
Cn/(2/3)n=(3/2)C
of the
\alpha_{i}
are non-zero. Similarly, at most
(3/2)C
of the
\beta_{j}
are non-zero.
If
r_{i,j}\neq 0
then at least one of
e_{i,j},\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}
is non-zero. This shows that the number of non-zero
r_{i,j}
is at most
Cn+(3/2)Cn+(3/2)Cn=4Cn
.

It remains to prove the hitting property: if
R=\{(i,j)\in[n]^{2}:r_{i,j}\neq 0\}
, then with probability
\Omega(1)
, a random permutation
\pi
“avoids”
R
, in the sense that
(i,\pi(i))\notin R
for all
i\in[n]
. We will prove this using two properties of
R
. First, as noted above,
|R|\leq 4Cn
. Second, we can bound the intersection of
R
with any row or column.
Given
i\in[n]
, notice that by construction,
e_{i,1}-\alpha_{i},\ldots,e_{i,n}-\alpha_{i}
contains at most
(2/3)n
many non-zero entries (since a most common value of
e_{i,1},\ldots,e_{i,n}
must be represented by at least
n/3
entries). Since at most
(3/2)C
of the
\beta_{j}
are non-zero, we conclude that
R
contains at most
(2/3)n+(3/2)C
entries on row
i
(that is, of the form
(i,\cdot)
), which is at most
(3/4)n
for an appropriate
N
. A similar property holds for columns.

We now show that if we sample a random permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, then with probability
\Omega(1)
it avoids
R
. We sample
\pi
in two stages. The first stage consists of sampling
n/5
carefully chosen entries of
\pi
, and in the second stage we sample the remaining
4n/5
entries of
\pi
. We assume for simplicity that
n
is divisible by
10
.

The first stage consists of
n/5
iterations, labelled by
t\in\{0,\ldots,n/5-1\}
, which alternate between row iterations (when
t
is even) and column iterations (when
t
is odd). At the
t
’th iteration,
t
values of
\pi
have been sampled so far. Let
I_{t}
consist of those indices
i\in[n]
on which
\pi(i)
is undefined, let
J_{t}
consist of those indices
j\in[n]
on which
\pi^{-1}(j)
is undefined, and let
R_{t}=R\cap(I_{t}\times J_{t})
. If
t
is even, let
i\notin I_{t}
be a choice which maximizes the number of entries in the
i
’th row of
R_{t}
. Denote this number by
m_{t}
, and sample
\pi(i)
uniformly among
J_{t}
. If
t
is odd, let
j\notin J_{t}
be a choice which maximizes the number of entries in the
j
’th column of
R_{t}
. Denote this number by
m_{t}
, and sample
\pi^{-1}(j)
uniformly among
I_{t}
.

The first stage is
successful
if the defined values of
\pi
avoid
R
. This happens with probability

p=\prod_{t=0}^{n/5-1}\left(1-\frac{m_{t}}{n-t}\right).

Recall that
R
contains at most
(3/4)n
entries on each row and column. Thus
m_{t}\leq(3/4)n
, and so
m_{t}/(n-t)\leq(3/4)n/(4/5)n=15/16
. Since
\log(1-x)/-x
is increasing, it is not hard to check that
1-m_{t}/(n-t)\geq e^{-3m_{t}/(n-t)}\geq e^{-4m_{t}/n}
, and consequently

p\geq e^{-\sum_{t=0}^{n/5-1}4m_{t}/n}\geq e^{-16C},

since the sum of
m_{t}
is at most
|R|\leq 4Cn
.

Let
I^{\prime}=I_{n/5}
,
J^{\prime}=J_{n/5}
, and
R^{\prime}=R_{n/5}
. Thus after the first stage, in order to complete the sampling process we need to sample
\pi(i)
for each
i\in I^{\prime}
, the allowed values being
J^{\prime}
; and the newly sampled values must avoid
R^{\prime}
, in which case we say that the second stage is
successful
. Furthermore,
n^{\prime}:=|I^{\prime}|=|J^{\prime}|=4n/5
.

We claim that
R^{\prime}
contains very few entries on each row or column. Indeed, suppose that some row of
R^{\prime}
contains
s
entries. This means that
m_{t}\geq s
for the
n/10
even
t
in the range
0,\ldots,n/5-1
, since otherwise this row would have been chosen in one of the row iterations. Consequently,
|R|\geq(n/10)s
. Since
|R|\leq 4Cn
, we conclude that
s\leq 40C
. Similarly, every column of
R^{\prime}
contains at most
40C
entries. Moreover,
|R^{\prime}|\leq 40Cn^{\prime}
.

If
|R^{\prime}|\leq n^{\prime}/2
then we can lower-bound the probability that the second stage is successful using the union bound: denoting by
\pi^{\prime}
the part of
\pi
left undefined after the first stage,

\Pr[\pi^{\prime}\text{ hits }R^{\prime}]\leq\sum_{(i,j)\in R}\Pr[\pi^{\prime}(i)=j]\leq\frac{|R^{\prime}|}{n^{\prime}}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

In order to lower-bound the probability that the second stage is successful in general (when
|R^{\prime}|\geq n^{\prime}/2
), we use truncated inclusion-exclusion (also known as the Bonferroni inequality). Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be a parameter to be chosen later. Then

\Pr[\pi^{\prime}\text{ hits }R^{\prime}]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{2d}(-1)^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{(i_{1},j_{1}),\ldots,(i_{k},j_{k})\}\subset R^{\prime}\\
\text{all different}\end{subarray}}\Pr[\pi^{\prime}(i_{1})=j_{1},\ldots,\pi^{\prime}(i_{k})=j_{k}].

We stress that the sum is over
unordered

k
-tuples of entries of
R^{\prime}
.
Let us say that a
k
-tuple of entries is
consistent
if all of
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
are distinct and all of
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}
are distinct. If a
k
-tuple is consistent then the probability that
\pi^{\prime}(i_{s})=j_{s}
for all
s\in[k]
is
1/n^{\prime\underline{k}}:=1/n^{\prime}(n^{\prime}-1)\cdots(n^{\prime}-k+1)
, and otherwise the probability is
0
. Hence if
N_{k}
is the number of unordered consistent
k
-tuples of entries in
R^{\prime}
,

\Pr[\pi^{\prime}\text{ hits }R^{\prime}]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{2d}(-1)^{k}\frac{N_{k}}{n^{\prime\underline{k}}}.

(6)

Let
M_{k}
be the number of
ordered
consistent
k
-tuples of entries in
R^{\prime}
. Then
N_{k}=M_{k}/k!
. We proceed to give upper and lower bounds on both
M_{k}
and
n^{\prime\underline{k}}
. Clearly
n^{\prime\underline{k}}\leq n^{\prime k}
, and on the other hand,

n^{\underline{k}}=n^{k}\prod_{s=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac{s}{n}\right)\geq\left(1-\frac{k(k-1)}{2n^{\prime}}\right)n^{k}.

As for
M_{k}
, clearly
M_{k}\leq|R^{\prime}|^{k}
. On the other hand, we can lower bound
M_{k}
as follows. There are
|R^{\prime}|
choices for the first entry. Since each row and column of
R^{\prime}
contains at most
40C
entries, there are at least
|R^{\prime}|-80C
choices for the second entry,
|R^{\prime}|-160C
choices for the third entry, and so on. In total,

M_{k}\geq\prod_{s=0}^{k-1}(|R^{\prime}|-40Cs)=|R^{\prime}|^{k}\prod_{s=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\frac{40Cs}{|R^{\prime}|}\right)\geq\left(1-\frac{O(Ck^{2})}{|R^{\prime}|}\right)|R^{\prime}|^{k}\geq\left(1-\frac{O(Ck^{2})}{n^{\prime}}\right)|R^{\prime}|^{k},

using the assumption
|R^{\prime}|\geq n^{\prime}/2
.

Putting both estimates together, we get that for even
k
,

\frac{N_{k}}{n^{\prime\underline{k}}}\leq\left(1-\frac{O(k^{2})}{n}\right)^{-1}\frac{|R^{\prime}|^{k}}{k!n^{\prime k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ast)}}{{\leq}}\left(1+\frac{O(k^{2})}{n}\right)\frac{(|R^{\prime}|/n^{\prime})^{k}}{k!}\leq\frac{(|R^{\prime}|/n^{\prime})^{k}}{k!}+\frac{O(k^{2}(40C)^{k})}{k!n},

where
(\ast)
holds whenever
n
is large enough as a function of
k
.
Similarly, for odd
k
,

\frac{N_{k}}{n^{\prime\underline{k}}}\geq\left(1-\frac{O(Ck^{2})}{n}\right)\frac{(|R^{\prime}|/n^{\prime})^{k}}{k!}\geq\frac{(|R^{\prime}|/n^{\prime})^{k}}{k!}-\frac{O(Ck^{2}(40C)^{k})}{k!n}.

Substituting these estimates in (
6
), we obtain that if
n
is large enough as a function of
d
then

\Pr[\pi^{\prime}\text{ hits }R^{\prime}]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{2d}(-1)^{k}\frac{(|R^{\prime}|/n^{\prime})^{k}}{k!}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{2d}\frac{(1+C)k^{2}(40C)^{k}}{k!}.

We can bound the second term by
O_{C}(1/n)
by extending the sum to infinity and noting that the series converges. As for the first term, it is a truncation of the Taylor series of
e^{-x}
at
x=|R^{\prime}|/n^{\prime}
. Since the Taylor series is an alternating sum, we can bound

e^{-|R^{\prime}|/n^{\prime}}\geq\sum_{k=0}^{2d}(-1)^{k}\frac{(|R^{\prime}|/n^{\prime})^{k}}{k!}-\frac{(|R^{\prime}|/n^{\prime})^{2d+1}}{(2d+1)!}.

Altogether, using
1/2\leq|R^{\prime}|/n^{\prime}\leq 40C
we obtain

\Pr[\pi^{\prime}\text{ hits }R^{\prime}]\leq e^{-1/2}+\frac{(40C)^{2d+1}}{(2d+1)!}+O_{C}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

Let
e^{-1/2}=1-\delta
.
We choose
d
so that the second term is at most
\delta/3
, and we choose
N
(the lower bound on
n
) so that
n
is large enough as a function of
d
for the estimates above to hold, and furthermore the third term is at most
\delta/3
. We conclude that
\pi^{\prime}
avoids
R^{\prime}
with probability at least
\delta/3
, and so the second stage is successful with probability at least
\min(1/2,\delta/3)=\delta/3
. Altogether,
\pi
avoids
R
with probability at least
e^{-16C}(\delta/3)
.

2.5
Sum of cosets

Applying
Lemma

2.3
and
Lemma

2.4
in sequence, we approximate
f
with a function
g=r+\sum_{i,j}r_{i,j}x_{i,j}
, such that with constant probability, a random permutation avoids all non-zero
r_{i,j}
’s. This implies that
g=r
with constant probability, and so
r\in\{0,1\}
(for small enough
\epsilon
). In order to say something similar about the coefficients
r_{i,j}
, we need a similar hitting property that holds relative to a coset
(i,j)
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
R\subseteq[n]^{2}
, and suppose that
|R|\leq Cn
and that with probability at least
\gamma>0
, a random permutation
\pi
avoids
R
, that is
(i,\pi(i))\notin R
for all
i\in[n]
. If
n\geq 8C/\gamma+2
then for all
i,j\in[n]
,

\Pr_{\pi\in S_{n}}[\pi\text{ avoids }R\setminus\{(i,j)\}\mid\pi(i)=j]\geq\gamma/2.

Similarly, if
n\geq 32C/\gamma+3
then for all
i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}\in[n]
such that
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
and
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
,

\Pr_{\pi\in S_{n}}[\pi\text{ avoids }R\setminus\{(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})\}\mid\pi(i_{1})=j_{1}\text{ and }\pi(i_{2})=j_{2}]\geq\gamma/4.

Proof.

Let
\pi^{\prime}
be a uniformly random permutation. We define a permutation
\pi
such that
\pi(i)=j
as follows. If
\pi^{\prime}(i)=j
, then we simply define
\pi=\pi^{\prime}
. Otherwise, let
\pi^{\prime}(i)=j^{\prime}
and
\pi^{\prime}(i^{\prime})=j
. Then
\pi
is obtained from
\pi^{\prime}
by setting
\pi(i)=j
and
\pi(i^{\prime})=j^{\prime}
. By symmetry,
\pi
is a uniformly random permutation such that
\pi(i)=j
, and furthermore, in the second case,
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
is chosen uniformly over all pairs such that
i^{\prime}\neq i
and
j^{\prime}\neq j
.

If the permutation
\pi
hits
R\setminus\{(i,j)\}
then either
\pi^{\prime}
hits
R
or
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in R
. The first event happens with probability at most
1-\gamma
, and a union bound shows that the second event happens with probability at most
|R^{\prime}|/(n-1)^{2}\leq Cn/(n-1)^{2}\leq 4C/n\leq\gamma/2
. Hence
\pi
avoids
R\setminus\{(i,j)\}
with probability at least
\gamma-\gamma/2\geq\gamma/2
. This concludes the proof of the first part of the lemma.

To prove the second part of the lemma, note first that since
n\geq 8C/\gamma+2
,

\Pr_{\pi\in S_{n}}[\pi\text{ avoids }R\setminus\{(i_{1},j_{1})\}\mid\pi(i_{1})=j_{1}]\geq\gamma/2.

Since
n\geq 2
, we have
|R\setminus\{(i_{1},j_{1})\}|\leq Cn\leq 2C(n-1)
. Thinking of the coset
(i_{1},j_{1})
as a copy of
S_{n-1}
, we apply the lemma again, with
n\leftarrow n-1
,
R\leftarrow R\cap\overline{\{i_{1}\}}\times\overline{\{j_{1}\}}
,
C\leftarrow 2C
, and
\gamma\leftarrow\gamma/2
, to deduce the second part of the lemma.
∎

Using this property, we can uncover the structure of functions satisfying the premises of
Lemma

2.4
. We state the following lemma in terms of
g
rather than
f
to facilitate its reuse in
Section

3
.

Recall that an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets

\mathcal{C}\subseteq[n]^{2}
consists of
O(n)
many cosets such that the number of pairs of cosets in
\mathcal{C}
which are not disjoint is
O(\epsilon n^{2})
. In the “direct” part of
Lemma

2.6
, the hidden big O constants depends only on
C
, and in the “converse” part, they can be arbitrary.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
g\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}
be a function satisfying the premises of
Lemma

2.4
. There exist constants
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\epsilon_{0}>0
, possibly depending on
C
, such that the following holds whenever
n\geq N
and
\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}
.

There is an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets
\mathcal{C}
, and a choice
G\in\{g,1-g\}
, such that the function
h=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
satisfies
\Pr[G\neq h]=O(\epsilon)
and
\E[(G-h)^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
.

Conversely, if
\mathcal{C}
is an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets then
h=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
satisfies
\Pr[h\notin\{0,1\}]=O(\epsilon)
and
\E[\dist(h,\{0,1\})^{2}]=O(\epsilon)
.

Proof.

Let
b,N_{1},\gamma
be the constants promised by
Lemma

2.4
, let
N_{2}=32C/\gamma+3
, and choose
N=\max(N_{1},N_{2})
.
Since
n\geq N_{1}
,
Lemma

2.4
applies, and gives us a representation

g=r+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{i,j}x_{i,j}

with the stated properties. In particular,
\Pr[g=r]\geq\gamma
, and so if
\epsilon<\gamma
, we conclude that
r\in\{0,1\}
. If
r=0
then we take
G=g
, and otherwise we take
G=1-g
. In the sequel, we assume for simplicity that
r=0
; an identical argument works when
r=1
.

Since
n\geq N_{2}
, we can apply
Lemma

2.5
to
R=\{(i,j):r_{i,j}\neq 0\}
. Applying the lemma to any
(i,j)\in R
, we obtain

\Pr_{\pi\in S_{n}}[\pi(i)=j\text{ and }\pi\text{ avoids }R\setminus\{(i,j)\}]\geq\frac{\gamma/2}{n}.

When this event happens,
g=r+r_{i,j}=r_{i,j}
. The events for different
(i,j)\in R
are disjoint, and so if we denote
B=\{(i,j)\in R:r_{i,j}\neq 1\}
then

\epsilon\geq\sum_{(i,j)\in B}\Pr_{\pi\in S_{n}}[\pi(i)=j\text{ and }\pi\text{ avoids }R\setminus\{(i,j)\}]\geq\frac{\gamma/2}{n}|B|,

implying that
|B|\leq(2\epsilon/\gamma)n=O(\epsilon n)
.

We define
\mathcal{C}=R\setminus B
. Since
\Pr[\pi(i)=j]=1/n
for each
(i,j)\in B
, the union bound shows that
\Pr[g\neq h]=O(\epsilon)
. Moreover,

\E[(g-h)^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(i,j)\in B}r_{i,j}^{2}+\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})\in B}|r_{i_{1},j_{1}}r_{i_{2},j_{2}}|\leq\frac{b^{2}|B|}{n}+\frac{b^{2}|B|^{2}}{n(n-1)}=O(\epsilon).

To complete the proof of the first part of the lemma, it remains to show that
\mathcal{C}
is
\epsilon
-disjoint. Clearly
|\mathcal{C}|\leq|R|\leq Cn
. For every unordered pair
(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})\in\mathcal{C}
of distinct non-disjoint cosets, since
n\geq N_{2}
we can apply
Lemma

2.5
to obtain

\Pr_{\pi\in S_{n}}[\pi(i_{1})=j_{1}\text{ and }\pi(i_{2})=j_{2}\text{ and }\pi\text{ avoids }\mathcal{C}\setminus\{(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})\}]\geq\frac{\gamma/4}{n(n-1)}.

When this event happens,
g=2
. These events are disjoint for different unordered pairs, and so if we denote by
P
the number of such unordered pairs then

\Pr[h=2]\geq\frac{\gamma/4}{n(n-1)}P.

On the other hand,
\Pr[h=2]\leq\Pr[g=2]+\Pr[g\neq h]=O(\epsilon)
, and so
P\leq O(\epsilon n^{2})
. This shows that
\mathcal{C}
is
\epsilon
-disjoint.

Let us now turn to the converse part. Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
is an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets, and let
h=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
. Again denoting by
P
the number of unordered pairs of non-disjoint cosets in
\mathcal{C}
, the union bound shows that

\Pr[h\notin\{0,1\}]=\Pr[h\geq 2]\leq\frac{P}{n(n-1)}=O(\epsilon).

In order to bound
\E[\dist(h,\{0,1\})^{2}]
, we first note that if
z\in\mathbb{N}
then
\dist(z,\{0,1\})^{2}=\Theta(z(z-1))
. Hence it suffices to bound
\E[h(h-1)]
:

\E[h(h-1)]=\E[h^{2}]-\E[h]=\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{n}+\frac{2P}{n(n-1)}-\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{n}=O(\epsilon).\qed

forms the bulk of the proof of
Theorems

and

2.6
Constant or dictator

In order to prove
Theorems

1.5
and
1.6
, we will analyze the function
h
constructed in
Lemma

.

Recall that a
dictator
is a function of the form
\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
where the cosets in
\mathcal{C}
are disjoint, that is, all of them are on the same row, or all of them are on the same column.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq[n]^{2}
be an
\epsilon
-disjoint family of cosets, and let
h=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}}x_{i,j}
.

For every
\delta\geq\sqrt{\epsilon}
, either
\Pr[h\neq 0]=O(\delta)
and
\E[h^{2}]=O(\delta)
, or there exists a dictator
H
such that
\Pr[h\neq H]=O(\epsilon/\delta)
and
\E[(h-H)^{2}]=O(\epsilon/\delta)
.

Proof.

Let us first notice that

\E[h^{2}]\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{n}+\frac{|\mathcal{C}|(|\mathcal{C}|-1)}{n(n-1)}=O(1).

This completes the proof when
\delta\geq 1
, since
\Pr[h\neq 0]\leq 1\leq\delta
and
\E[h^{2}]=O(1)=O(\delta)
. From now on, we assume that
\delta<1
. The lemma also trivially holds when
n=1
, so we can assume that
n\geq 2
.

By definition of
\epsilon
-disjoint family, there exists a constant
L>0
such that the number of unordered pairs of non-disjoint cosets in
\mathcal{C}
is at most
L\epsilon n^{2}
.
Since
\delta\geq\sqrt{\epsilon}
, if
\delta<1/\sqrt{L}n
then
L\epsilon n^{2}\leq L\delta^{2}n^{2}<1
, and so
h
is a dictator, completing the proof. From now on, we assume that
\delta\geq 1/\sqrt{L}n
.

Let
K>0
be a constant to be determined. We say that a line (row or column) of
\mathcal{C}
is
heavy
if it contains at least
K\delta n
entries.

Suppose first that there are no heavy lines. Then every row and column contains at most
K\delta n
cosets in
\mathcal{C}
. Each coset is thus disjoint from at most
2K\delta n
many cosets in
\mathcal{C}
, and so the number of unordered pairs of non-disjoint cosets in
\mathcal{C}
is at least
|\mathcal{C}|(|\mathcal{C}|-2K\delta n)/2
. If
|\mathcal{C}|\geq 4K\delta n
then
|\mathcal{C}|(|\mathcal{C}|-2K\delta n)/2\geq|\mathcal{C}|^{2}/4\geq 4K^{2}\delta^{2}n\geq 4K^{2}\epsilon n
, which contradicts the upper bound
L\epsilon n^{2}
for
K>\sqrt{L}/2
. Hence
|\mathcal{C}|\leq 4K\delta n
.

We claim that in this case,
\Pr[h\neq 0]=O(\delta)
and
\E[h^{2}]=O(\delta)
. Indeed, the union bound shows that
\Pr[h\neq 0]\leq 4K\delta=O(\delta)
, and

\E[h^{2}]\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{n}+\frac{L\epsilon n^{2}}{n(n-1)}=O(\delta+\epsilon)=O(\delta),

since
\epsilon\leq\delta^{2}<\delta
.

Suppose next that
\mathcal{C}
contains some heavy line, say
\mathcal{L}
. Every entry in
\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{L}
is disjoint from at most one entry of
\mathcal{L}
, and so the number of unordered pairs of non-disjoint cosets in
\mathcal{C}
is at least
|\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{L}|(|\mathcal{L}|-1)
. Since
\delta\geq 1/\sqrt{L}n
, we have

|\mathcal{L}|-1\geq K\delta n\left(1-\frac{1}{K\delta n}\right)\geq K\delta n\left(1-\frac{1}{K/\sqrt{L}}\right),

which is at least
K\delta n/2
for
K\geq 2\sqrt{L}
. In total, the number of unordered pairs of non-disjoint cosets is at least
|\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{L}|K\delta n/2
.
Since this number is at most
L\epsilon n^{2}
, we conclude that
|\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{L}|\leq 2(L/K)(\epsilon/\delta)n
.

The required dictator is
H=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{L}}x_{i,j}
. The union bound shows that
\Pr[h\neq H]\leq 2(L/K)(\epsilon/\delta)=O(\epsilon/\delta)
. Moreover,

\E[(h-H)^{2}]\leq\frac{|\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{L}|}{n}+\frac{L\epsilon n^{2}}{n(n-1)}=O(\epsilon/\delta+\epsilon)=O(\epsilon/\delta),

since
\delta<1
.
∎

3.1
FKN theorem on the cube

We start by proving an
L_{0}
analog of the Friedgut–Kalai–Naor theorem on the Boolean cube.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=c+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}.

If
\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]<1/4
then either
f\in\{0,1\}
or
f\in\{x_{i},1-x_{i}\}
for some
i\in[n]
.

In particular,
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
, and at most one of
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
is non-zero.

Proof.

We start by showing that
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
. If
c_{i}\notin\{0,\pm 1\}
, then for each assignment to all variables other than
x_{i}
, there is at least one assignment to
x_{i}
such that
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\notin\{0,1\}
, and so
\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]\geq 1/2
, contradicting the assumption.

Similarly, we claim that at most one of
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
can be non-zero. If
c_{i},c_{j}\neq 0
, then
c_{i}x_{i}+c_{j}x_{j}
attains at least three different values as
x_{i},x_{j}\in\{0,1\}
.
Therefore for each assignment to all variables other than
x_{i},x_{j}
, there is at least one assignment to
x_{i},x_{j}
such that
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\notin\{0,1\}
, and so
\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]\geq 1/4
, contradicting the assumption.

We are left with three options:
f=c
,
f=c+x_{i}
,
f=c-x_{i}
. In the first case, necessarily
c\in\{0,1\}
. In the second case, necessarily
c=0
, since otherwise
\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]\geq 1/2
. Similarly, in the third case, necessarily
c=1
.
∎

Corollary

2.2
has the useful feature that if
N\geq 2
of
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
are far from zero (more than
1/2
in absolute value) then
\epsilon=\Omega(N-1)
. In contrast, even if all of
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
are non-zero then we cannot improve on
\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]\leq 1
(the bound
1
should be compared to the bound
\Omega(N-1)
). This makes it harder to approximate
f
by a sparse sum in the
L_{0}
setting.

a_{1}\neq a_{2}
, and
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
, a
square
. A
singleton
is a set of the form
\{a\}\times\{b\}
, where
a,b\in[n]
. Two squares
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{b_{1},b_{2}\},\{a_{3},a_{4}\}\times\{b_{3},b_{4}\}
are
compatible
if
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\cap\{a_{3},a_{4}\}=\emptyset
and
\{b_{1},b_{2}\}\cap\{b_{3},b_{4}\}=\emptyset
. Similarly, the square
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
is compatible with the singleton
\{a_{3}\}\times\{b_{3}\}
if
a_{3}\neq a_{1},a_{2}
and
b_{3}\neq b_{1},b_{2}
.

We associate permutations
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in S_{n}
with the set
\bigl\{\{\mathbf{a}_{2t-1},\mathbf{a}_{2t}\}\times\{\mathbf{b}_{2t-1},\mathbf{b}_{2t}\}:t\in[\lfloor n/2\rfloor]\bigr\}
of
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
compatible squares, accompanied by the compatible singleton
\{\mathbf{a}_{n}\}\times\{\mathbf{b}_{n}\}
when
n
is odd. This set of
\lceil n/2\rceil
compatible squares and (zero or one) singletons completely specifies
S_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
. We call such a set a
square system
(although when
n
is odd, it also contains a singleton). If we choose
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
at random, then the resulting square system is a
(uniformly) random square system
.

A permutation
\pi

hits
a square
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
if
\pi(\{a_{1},a_{2}\})=\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
. If
\pi
does not hit a square
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
, then whenever
\pi\in S_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
, the corresponding square system does not contain the square
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
.

We denote by
S_{\Sigma}
the set of permutations hitting all squares in the square system
\Sigma
. If
\Sigma
is a random square system and we choose a random element of
S_{\Sigma}
, then the result is a random element of
S_{n}
.

We move on to the analog of
Lemma

2.5
. We are given a set of bad squares. In our applications this set will consist of squares
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
such that
c_{a_{1},b_{1}}+c_{a_{2},b_{2}}-c_{a_{1},b_{2}}-c_{a_{2},b_{1}}\notin\{0,\pm 1\}
(in one application) or
c_{a_{1},b_{1}}+c_{a_{2},b_{2}}-c_{a_{1},b_{2}}-c_{a_{2},b_{1}}\neq 0
(in another), where
f=\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}x_{i,j}
.

We are given that a random square system typically contains a small number of bad squares (in our applications, zero or one). We want to say that this still holds even conditioned on the square system containing one or two given squares.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
n\geq 2
, let
R\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}\times\binom{[n]}{2}
be a collection of squares of density
\rho=|R|/\binom{n}{2}^{2}
, and suppose that for some
C\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Pr_{\Sigma}[|\Sigma\cap R|>C]=\delta,

where
\Sigma
is a random square system.

For any square
\sigma
,

\Pr_{\Sigma}[|\Sigma\cap(R\setminus\{\sigma\})|>C\mid\sigma\in\Sigma]\leq\delta+O(\rho).

For any two compatible squares
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
,

\Pr_{\Sigma}[|\Sigma\cap(R\setminus\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\})|>C\mid\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma]\leq\delta+O(\rho).

Proof.

Let
\Sigma^{\prime}
be a random square system. We wish to modify it so that it contains
\sigma=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
. We do this in a number of steps.

First, suppose that
n
is odd. If the singleton of
\Sigma^{\prime}
is
\{a_{1}\}\times\{B\}
then we switch
a_{1}
with a random element in
[n]\setminus\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
. We handle singletons of the forms
\{a_{2}\}\times\{B\},\{A\}\times\{b_{1}\},\{A\}\times\{b_{2}\}
in an analogous way (some singletons could require two fixing steps).

At this point, regardless of the parity of
n
, we know that
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}
all participate in squares. If
a_{1},a_{2}
are not part of the same square, then they are part of two different squares
\{a_{1},A_{2}\}\times\{B_{1},B_{2}\}
and
\{A_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
. We replace these squares with the squares
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{B_{1},B_{2}\}
and
\{A_{1},A_{2}\}\times\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
. We act similarly if
b_{1},b_{2}
are not part of the same square.

Finally, if
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
and
\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
are not part of the same square, then they are part of two different squares
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{B_{1},B_{2}\}
and
\{A_{1},A_{2}\}\times\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
. We replace these squares with the squares
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}\times\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
and
\{A_{1},A_{2}\}\times\{B_{1},B_{2}\}
.

Denote the resulting random square system by
\Sigma
. This is a uniformly random square system containing the square
\sigma
. By construction,
|\Sigma\setminus\Sigma^{\prime}|=O(1)
. By symmetry, if we choose
\Sigma^{\prime}
uniformly at random and a random square in
\Sigma\setminus\Sigma^{\prime}
, we either get
\sigma
or a uniformly random square compatible with
\sigma
. The number of squares compatible with
\sigma
is
\binom{n-2}{2}^{2}
, which is
\Omega(\binom{n}{2}^{2})
assuming
n\geq 4
(otherwise the lemma trivially holds). Therefore a union bound shows that the probability that
\Sigma\setminus\Sigma^{\prime}
contains any square in
R\setminus\{\sigma\}
is
O(\rho)
. By assumption,
|\Sigma^{\prime}\cap(R\setminus\{\sigma\})|>C
with probability at most
\delta
, and so overall, the probability that
|\Sigma\cap(R\setminus\{\sigma\})|>C
is at most
\delta+O(\rho)
.
This proves the first part of the lemma.

Now suppose that instead of a single square
\sigma
we are given two compatible squares
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
. We can assume that
n\geq 4
, since otherwise the lemma trivially holds. Applying the first part of the lemma to
\sigma_{1}
, we obtain

\Pr_{\Sigma}[|\Sigma\cap(R\setminus\{\sigma_{1}\})|>C\mid\sigma_{1}\in\Sigma]\leq\delta+O(\rho).

If we remove
\sigma_{1}
from
\Sigma
, we obtain a random square system in a copy of
S_{n-2}
. The density of
R\setminus\{\sigma_{1}\}
inside this copy is
\rho^{\prime}=|R\setminus\{\sigma_{1}\}|/\binom{n-2}{2}^{2}=O(\rho)
. Applying the first part of the lemma to
\sigma_{2}
, we obtain

\Pr_{\Sigma}[|\Sigma\cap(R\setminus\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\})|>C\mid\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma]\leq\delta+O(\rho).\qed

3.3
Sparse representation

Our goal in this subsection is to prove the following analog of
Lemma

2.3
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a linear function satisfying
\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]=\epsilon
. Then there is an integer
e
and integers
e_{i,j}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
such that the function

g=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{i,j}x_{i,j}

satisfies
\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\epsilon)
, and so
\Pr[g\notin\{0,1\}]=O(\epsilon)
. Moreover, only
O(n)
many of the
e_{i,j}
are non-zero.

In the proof, we can assume that
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
for an appropriate constant
\epsilon_{0}>0
. Indeed, if
\epsilon\geq\epsilon_{0}
then we can satisfy the lemma by choosing
g=0
.

Similarly, we can assume that
n>N
for an appropriate constant
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Indeed, if
n\leq N
then either
\epsilon\geq 1/N!
, in which case we are done by choosing
\epsilon_{0}
small enough, or
\epsilon<1/N!
, in which case
f
is Boolean. In the latter case, we can take
g=f
and apply
Theorem

2.8
to represent
f
in the required form.

Since
f
is a linear function, we can write

f=\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}x_{i,j}.

For
i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}\in[n]
, define
d_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}=c_{i_{1},j_{1}}+c_{i_{2},j_{2}}-c_{i_{1},j_{2}}-c_{i_{2},j_{1}}
. If we switch
i_{1},i_{2}
or
j_{1},j_{2}
, then this expression changes sign. This ensures that the following sets are well-defined:
R_{0}
is the set of squares
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\times\{j_{1},j_{2}\}
such that
d_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}\neq 0
, and
R_{1}
is the set of squares
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\times\{j_{1},j_{2}\}
such that
d_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}\notin\{0,\pm 1\}
. Note that if
i_{1}=i_{2}
or
j_{1}=j_{2}
then
d_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}=0
.

Let
\Sigma
be a random square system, let
f_{\Sigma}=f|_{S_{\Sigma}}
, and let
\epsilon_{\Sigma}=\Pr[f_{\Sigma}\notin\{0,1\}]
. Thus
\E[\epsilon_{\Sigma}]=\epsilon
, and so
\Pr[\epsilon_{\Sigma}\geq 1/4]\leq 4\epsilon
.

Whenever
\epsilon_{\Sigma}<1/4
,
Lemma

3.1
shows that
\Sigma\cap R_{1}=\emptyset
, and so
\Pr[|\Sigma\cap R_{1}|>0]\leq 4\epsilon
. Moreover, if we choose
\Sigma
at random, then a random square of
\Sigma
belongs to
R_{1}
with probability at most
4\epsilon
. Since a random square of
\Sigma
is just a random square, this shows that
\rho_{1}=|R_{1}|/\binom{n}{2}^{2}\leq 4\epsilon
.
Applying
Lemma

3.2
with
C=0
for any
\sigma\in R_{1}
, we deduce that

\Pr_{\Sigma}[|\Sigma\cap R_{1}|=\{\sigma\}]=\frac{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}{\binom{n}{2}^{2}}\left(1-\Pr_{\Sigma}[|\Sigma\cap(R_{1}\setminus\{\sigma\})|>0\mid\sigma\in\Sigma]\right)=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right)(1-O(\epsilon))=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right),

assuming
\epsilon_{0}
is small enough.
These events are disjoint for different
\sigma\in R_{1}
, and so

\Pr_{\Sigma}[\Sigma\cap R_{1}\neq\emptyset]=\Omega\left(\frac{|R_{1}|}{n^{3}}\right).

Since this probability is at most
4\epsilon
, we conclude that
|R_{1}|=O(\epsilon n^{3})
, and so a random square belongs to
R_{1}
with probability
O(\epsilon/n)
.

Lemma

3.1
shows that if
\epsilon_{\Sigma}<1/4
then not only
\Sigma\cap R_{1}=\emptyset
, but moreover
|\Sigma\cap R_{0}|\leq 1
. Therefore if we choose a random square system
\Sigma
and a random square in
\Sigma
, then it belongs to
R_{0}
with probability at most
\rho_{0}=4\epsilon+1/\lfloor n/2\rfloor=O(\epsilon+1/n)
.
Applying
Lemma

3.2
with
C=1
for any compatible
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in R_{0}
, we deduce that

\Pr_{\Sigma}[|\Sigma\cap(R_{0}\setminus\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\})|\leq 1]=\frac{\lfloor n/2\rfloor(\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1)}{\binom{n}{2}^{2}\binom{n-2}{2}^{2}}\left(1-\Pr_{\Sigma}[|\Sigma\cap(R_{0}\setminus\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\})|>1\mid\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma]\right)=\\
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n^{6}}\right)\bigl(1-O(\epsilon+1/n)\bigr)=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n^{6}}\right),

assuming
\epsilon_{0}
is small enough and
N
is large enough. If any of these events happens then
2\leq|\Sigma\cap R_{0}|\leq 3
, and so each
\Sigma
can belong to at most three of these events. Therefore, if we denote by
P
the number of ordered pairs of compatible squares in
R_{0}
then

\Pr[|\Sigma\cap R_{0}|\geq 2]=\Omega\left(\frac{P}{n^{6}}\right).

Since this probability is at most
O(\epsilon)
, we conclude that
P=O(\epsilon n^{6})
.
On the other hand, since a given square is incompatible with at most
4n^{3}
other squares,

P\geq|R_{0}|(|R_{0}|-4n^{3}).

Hence either
|R_{0}|\leq 8n^{3}
or
P\geq|R_{0}|^{2}/2
, which implies that
|R_{0}|=O(\sqrt{\epsilon}n^{3})=O(n^{3})
. In both cases,
|R_{0}|=O(n^{3})
, and so a random square belongs to
R_{0}
with probability
O(1/n)
.

Summarizing our work so far, we have shown that a random square belongs to
R_{1}
with probability
O(\epsilon/n)
and to
R_{0}
with probability
O(1/n)
. We can sample a random square
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\times\{j_{1},j_{2}\}
by first sampling
i_{1},j_{1}\in[n]
and then sampling
i_{2},j_{2}\in[n]
subject to
i_{2}\neq i_{1}
and
j_{2}\neq j_{1}
. Therefore there exist
i_{1},j_{1}\in[n]
such that the
d_{i,j}:=d_{i_{1},i,j_{1},j}
satisfy

\displaystyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq i_{1}\\
j\neq j_{1}\end{subarray}}[d_{i,j}\notin\{0,\pm 1\}]=O(\epsilon/n),

\displaystyle\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq i_{1}\\
j\neq j_{1}\end{subarray}}[d_{i,j}\neq 0]=O(1/n).

When
i=i_{1}
or
j=j_{1}
, we have
d_{i,j}=0
, and so the bounds above hold even if we sample
i,j\in[n]
in an unrestricted fashion.

As in the proof of
Lemma

2.3
, we have

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(d_{i,j}-c_{i_{1},j_{1}}+c_{i_{1},j}+c_{i,j_{1}})x_{i,j}=-nc_{i_{1},j_{1}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i,j_{1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{i,j}x_{i,j},

and so for an appropriate
e
,

f=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{i,j}x_{i,j}.

We let
e_{i,j}=d_{i,j}
if
d_{i,j}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
, and
e_{i,j}=0
otherwise. This defines a function

g=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{i,j}x_{i,j}

which differs from
f
only when the input permutation
\pi
satisfies
d_{i,\pi(i)}\notin\{0,\pm 1\}
for some
i\in[n]
. Applying the union bound, we obtain

\Pr[f\neq g]\leq\frac{\bigl|\bigl\{(i,j)\in[n]^{2}:d_{i,j}\notin\{0,\pm 1\}\bigr\}\bigr|}{n}=O(\epsilon).

This implies that
\Pr[g\notin\{0,1\}]\leq\Pr[f\notin\{0,1\}]+\Pr[f\neq g]=O(\epsilon)
, which is strictly smaller than
1
for small enough
\epsilon_{0}
. Therefore
g(\pi)\in\{0,1\}
for some
\pi\in S_{n}
. Since
e_{i,j}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
, this implies that
e
is an integer. Finally, by construction the number of non-zero
e_{i,j}
is at most the number of non-zero
d_{i,j}
, which is
O(n)
.

4.1
FKN theorem on the cube

For reference, here is an
L_{\infty}
analog of the Friedgut–Kalai–Naor theorem on the Boolean cube.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=c+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}.

If
\dist(f(x),\{0,1\})\leq\epsilon
for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\epsilon<1/4
then the function
g(x)=\round(f(x),\{0,1\})
satisfies
g\in\{0,1,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},1-x_{1},\ldots,1-x_{n}\}
.

Proof.

Since
f(0,\ldots,0)=c
, we see that
\dist(c,\{0,1\})\leq\epsilon
. Suppose that
|c|\leq\epsilon
; otherwise we can consider
1-f
. Let
e^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
have a unique
1
-coordinate at position
i
. Since
f(e^{(i)})-f(0,\ldots,0)=c_{i}
, we see that
\dist(c_{i},\{0,1\})\leq 2\epsilon
. Since
f(e^{(i)}+e^{(j)})-f(0,\ldots,0)=c_{i}+c_{j}
, also
\dist(c_{i}+c_{j},\{0,1\})\leq 2\epsilon
.

Let
d_{i}=\round(c_{i},\{0,1\})
. If
d_{i}=d_{j}=1
then
c_{i}+c_{j}\geq 2-2\epsilon>1+2\epsilon
, which is impossible. Therefore at most one
d_{i}
is non-zero. If
d_{j}=0
then
|c_{j}|\leq 2\epsilon
, and so if
x,y
differ only in the
j
’th coordinate then
|f(x)-f(y)|=|c_{j}|\leq 2\epsilon
, and so
g(x)=g(y)
, since otherwise
|f(x)-f(y)\geq 1-2\epsilon>2\epsilon
. Consequently,
g
depends on at most one coordinate.
∎

Sparse representation

Lemma 4.2

Let

Let
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in S_{n}
be any two permutations, and consider
S_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
. For every
t\in[\lfloor n/2\rfloor]
, we can find two permutations
\alpha,\beta\in S_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
such that
f(\alpha)-f(\beta)=d_{\mathbf{a}_{2t-1},\mathbf{a}_{2t},\mathbf{b}_{2t-1},\mathbf{b}_{2t}}
. Since
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
are arbitrary, this shows that
\dist(d_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}},\{0,\pm 1\})\leq 2\epsilon
for all
i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}\in[n]
(this trivially holds if
i_{1}=i_{2}
or
j_{1}=j_{2}
).

Similarly, for every distinct
s,t\in[\lfloor n/2\rfloor]
we can find two permutations
\alpha,\beta\in S_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
such that
f(\alpha)-f(\beta)=d_{\mathbf{a}_{2s-1},\mathbf{a}_{2s},\mathbf{b}_{2s-1},\mathbf{b}_{2s}}\pm d_{\mathbf{a}_{2t-1},\mathbf{a}_{2t},\mathbf{b}_{2t-1},\mathbf{b}_{2t}}
, for our choice of sign. This shows that
\round(d_{\mathbf{a}_{2t-1},\mathbf{a}_{2t},\mathbf{b}_{2t-1},\mathbf{b}_{2t}},\{0,\pm 1\})\neq 0
for at most one
t\in[\lfloor n/2\rfloor]
(here we are using
\epsilon<1/6
).
Therefore a random rectangle
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\times\{j_{1},j_{2}\}
satisfies
d_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}\neq 0
with probability at most
1/\lfloor n/2\rfloor=O(1/n)
.

We can fix values
i_{1},j_{1}
so that the number of
d_{i,j}:=d_{i_{1},i,j_{1},j}
which are not
2\epsilon
-close to
0
is at most

(n-1)^{2}\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq i_{1}\\
j\neq j_{1}\end{subarray}}[d_{i_{1},i,j_{1},j}\neq 0]=O(n).

As in the proofs of
Lemmas

2.3
and
3.3
, we have

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(d_{i,j}-c_{i_{1},j_{1}}+c_{i_{1},j}+c_{i,j_{1}})x_{i,j}=-nc_{i_{1},j_{1}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i,j_{1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{i,j}x_{i,j},

and so for an appropriate
d
,

f=d+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{i,j}x_{i,j}.

4.3
Sporadic representation

Next, we prove an analog of
Lemma

2.4
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a linear function satisfying
\dist(f(\pi),\{0,1\})\leq\epsilon
for all
\pi\in S_{n}
, where
\epsilon<1/4
and
n\geq N
for an appropriate constant
N\in\mathbb{N}
. We can write

f=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{i,j}x_{i,j}

in such a way that each
e_{i,j}
is
2\epsilon
-close to an integer; at most
O(n)
of the
e_{i,j}
are
2\epsilon
-close to non-zero integers; and each row or column of the
n\times n
matrix formed by the
e_{i,j}
contains at least
n/4
entries which are
2\epsilon
-close to
0
.

The proof uses the first part of the argument in the proof of
Lemma

2.4
. Our starting point is the representation

f=d+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{i,j}x_{i,j}

promised by
Lemma

4.2
, in which at most
Cn
of the
d_{i,j}
are
2\epsilon
-close to
0
.

Let
D_{i,j}=\round(d_{i,j},\{0,\pm 1\})
,
let
\alpha_{i}\in\{-1,0,1\}
be a most common value of
D_{i,1},\ldots,D_{i,n}
, let
\beta_{j}\in\{-1,0,1\}
be the most common value of
D_{1,j},\ldots,D_{n,j}
, and define
e_{i,j}=d_{i,j}-\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j}
, so that

g=d+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(e_{i,j}+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j})x_{i,j}=d+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{i,j}x_{i,j}.

We claim that most of the coefficients
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}
are equal to zero. Indeed, if
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
then among
d_{i,1},\ldots,d_{i,n}
, at least
(2/3)n
are not
2\epsilon
-close to
0
. Therefore at most
Cn/(2/3)n=(3/2)C
of the
\alpha_{i}
are non-zero. Similarly, at most
(3/2)C
of the
\beta_{j}
are non-zero.
If
E_{i,j}:=\round(e_{i,j},\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
then at least one of
D_{i,j},\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}
is non-zero. This shows that the number of non-zero
E_{i,j}
is at most
Cn+(3/2)Cn+(3/2)Cn=4Cn
.

Given
i\in[n]
, notice that by construction,
D_{i,1}-\alpha_{i},\ldots,D_{i,n}-\alpha_{i}
contains at most
(2/3)n
many non-zero entries. Since at most
(3/2)C
of the
\beta_{j}
are non-zero, we conclude that the
n\times n
matrix formed by the
E_{i,j}
contains at most
(2/3)n+(3/2)C
non-zero entries on row
i
, which is at most
(3/4)n
for an appropriate
N
. A similar property holds for columns.

4.4
Main theorem

We are now ready to uncover the structure of
f
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
f\colon S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a linear function satisfying
\dist(f(\pi),\{0,1\})\leq\epsilon
for all
\pi\in S_{n}
, where
\epsilon<1/40
and
n\geq N
for an appropriate constant
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
g(\pi)=\round(f(\pi),\{0,1\})
is a dictator.

Proof.

Apply
Lemma

4.3
to get a representation

f=e+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{i,j}x_{i,j}.

with the properties stated by the lemma, and let
E_{i,j}=\round(e_{i,j},\mathbb{Z})
. According to the lemma,
|e_{i,j}-E_{i,j}|\leq 2\epsilon
, and at most
O(n)
of the
E_{i,j}
are non-zero.

For a rectangle
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\times\{j_{1},j_{2}\}
, let
e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}=e_{i_{1},j_{1}}+e_{i_{2},j_{2}}-e_{i_{1},j_{2}}-e_{i_{2},j_{1}}
, and let
E_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}=E_{i_{1},j_{1}}+E_{i_{2},j_{2}}-E_{i_{1},j_{2}}-E_{i_{2},j_{1}}
. We can find two permutations
\alpha,\beta
such that
f(\alpha)-f(\beta)=e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}
, and so
\dist(e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}},\{0,\pm 1\})\leq 2\epsilon
. Since
|e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}-E_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}|\leq 8\epsilon
, this shows that
\dist(E_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}},\{0,\pm 1\})\leq 10\epsilon
, and so
E_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
.

If
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\times\{j_{1},j_{2}\}
and
\{i_{3},i_{4}\}\times\{j_{3},j_{4}\}
are two compatible squares, then we can find two permutations
\alpha,\beta
such that
f(\alpha)-f(\beta)=e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}\pm e_{i_{3},i_{4},j_{3},j_{4}}
, for a sign of our choice. This implies that
\dist(E_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}\pm E_{i_{3},i_{4},j_{3},j_{4}},\{0,\pm 1\})\leq 18\epsilon
, and so
E_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}},E_{i_{3},i_{4},j_{3},j_{4}}
cannot both be non-zero.

Suppose that
E_{i_{1},j_{1}}\neq 0
. There are at least
n/4
many
i_{2}
such that
E_{i_{2},j_{1}}=0
, and at least
n/4
many
j_{2}
such that
E_{i_{2},j_{2}}=0
. Since at most
O(n)
of the
E_{i,j}
are non-zero, for large enough
N
there must be a choice of
i_{2}
and
j_{2}
out of these
(n/4)^{2}
options such that furthermore
E_{i_{2},j_{2}}=0
, and so
E_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}=E_{i_{1},j_{1}}
. This shows that
E_{i,j}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
for all
i,j\in[n]
.

We now run the same argument on pairs. Suppose that
E_{i_{1},j_{1}},E_{i_{3},j_{3}}\neq 0
, where
i_{1}\neq i_{3}
and
j_{1}\neq j_{3}
. There are
\Omega(n^{2})
many choices for
i_{2},i_{4}
such that
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4}
are all distinct and
E_{i_{2},j_{1}}=E_{i_{4},j_{3}}=0
(assuming
N\geq 4
). Similarly, there are
\Omega(n^{2})
many choices for
j_{2},j_{4}
such that
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}
are all distinct and
E_{i_{1},j_{2}}=E_{i_{3},j_{4}}=0
. Since at most
O(n)
of the
E_{i,j}
are non-zero, at most
O(n^{2})
out of these
\Omega(n^{4})
choices satisfy
E_{i_{2},j_{2}}\neq 0
or
E_{i_{4},j_{4}}\neq 0
, and so for large enough
N
, there must be some choice of
i_{2},i_{4},j_{2},j_{4}
such that the squares
\{i_{1},i_{2}\}\times\{j_{1},j_{2}\}
and
\{i_{3},i_{4}\}\times\{j_{3},j_{4}\}
are compatible,
E_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}=E_{i_{1},j_{1}}
, and
E_{i_{3},i_{4},j_{3},j_{4}}=E_{i_{3},j_{3}}
. This shows that all non-zero
E_{i,j}
must line on the same row or column.

Suppose that all non-zero
E_{i,j}
lie on row
I
(the other case is similar). If
E_{I,j_{1}}=1
and
E_{I,j_{2}}=-1
then for any
i\in I
we have
E_{I,i,j_{1},j_{2}}=2
, which impossible. By possibly replacing
f
with
1-f
, we can assume that
E_{I,j}\in\{0,1\}
for all
j\in[n]
.

Recall that
g(\pi)=\round(f(\pi),\{0,1\})
.
If
\alpha,\beta\in S_{n}
are two permutations differing on a transposition and satisfying
\alpha(I)=\beta(I)
then
f(\alpha)-f(\beta)=e_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}
for some
i_{1},i_{2}\neq I
. Since
E_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}=0
, this implies that
|g(\alpha)-g(\beta)|\leq 10\epsilon
, and so
g(\alpha)=g(\beta)
.

Assuming
N\geq 3
, any two permutations agreeing on the image of
I
can be obtained from one another by applying a sequence of transpositions. Therefore
g(\pi)
depends only on
\pi(I)
, and so it is a dictator.
∎

To deduce
Theorem

1.4
, it remains to handle the case in which
n
is small.

See
1.4

Proof.

Let
N
be the constant from
Lemma

4.4
. If
n\geq N
then we are done, so assume that
n<N
. Apply
Lemma

4.2
to obtain a representation

f=d+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{i,j}x_{i,j}

such that
\dist(d_{i,j},\{0,\pm 1\})\leq 2\epsilon
for all
i,j
. Denoting by
\mathrm{id}
the identity permutation, we have

f(\mathrm{id})=d+\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i,i},

and so
f(\mathrm{id})
is
(2n+1)\epsilon
-close to an integer.

Let
D=\round(d,\mathbb{Z})
and
D_{i,j}=\round(d_{i,j},\{0,\pm 1\})
. Then the function

h=D+\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i,j}x_{i,j}

satisfies
|f(\pi)-h(\pi)|\leq(4n+1)\epsilon
for all
\pi\in S_{n}
, and so
\dist(h(\pi),\{0,1\})\leq(4n+2)\epsilon
for all
\pi\in S_{n}
. Choosing
\epsilon_{0}<1/(4N+2)
, this guarantees that
h
is Boolean, and so a dictator by
Theorem

2.8
. Choosing
\epsilon_{0}<1/(8N+2)
, we see that
h(\pi)=\round(f(\pi),\{0,1\})=g(\pi)
for all
\pi\in S_{n}
, completing the proof.
∎
