Nonlocal Effective Electromagnetic Wave Characteristics of Composite Media: Beyond the Quasistatic Regime
Salvatore Torquato, Jaeuk Kim

Abstract
We derive exact nonlocal homogenized constitutive relations for the effective electromagnetic wave properties of disordered two-phase composites and metamaterials from first principles. This exact formalism enables us to extend the long-wavelength limitations of conventional homogenization estimates of the effective dynamic dielectric constant tensor 𝜺 e ​ ( 𝐤 I , ω ) \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{I},\omega) for arbitrary microstructures so that it can capture spatial dispersion well beyond the quasistatic regime (where ω \omega and 𝐤 I {\bf k}_{I} are the frequency and wave vector of the incident radiation). We accomplish this task by deriving nonlocal strong-contrast expansions that exactly account for complete microstructural information (infinite set of n n -point correlation functions) and hence multiple scattering to all orders for the range of wave numbers for which our extended homogenization theory applies, i.e., 0 ≤ | 𝐤 I | ​ ℓ ≲ 1 0\leq|{\bf k}_{I}|\ell\lesssim 1 (where ℓ \ell is a characteristic heterogeneity length scale). Because of the fast-convergence properties of such expansions, their lower-order truncations yield accurate closed-form approximate formulas for 𝜺 e ​ ( 𝐤 I , ω ) \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{I},\omega) that apply for a wide class of microstructures. These nonlocal formulas are resummed representations of the strong-contrast expansions that still accurately capture multiple scattering to all orders via the microstructural information embodied in the spectral density, which is easy to compute for any composite. The accuracy of these microstructure-dependent approximations is validated by comparison to full-waveform simulation computations for both 2D and 3D ordered and disordered models of composite media. Thus, our closed-form formulas enable one to predict accurately and efficiently the effective wave characteristics well beyond the quasistatic regime for a wide class of composite microstructures without having to perform full-blown simulations. We find that disordered hyperuniform media are generally less lossy than their nonhyperuniform counterparts. We also show that certain disordered hyperuniform particulate composites exhibit novel wave characteristics, including the capacity to act as low-pass filters that transmit waves “isotropically” up to a selected wave number and refractive indices that abruptly change over a narrow range of wave numbers. Our results demonstrate that one can design the effective wave characteristics of a disordered composite by engineering the microstructure to possess tailored spatial correlations at prescribed length scales. Thus, our findings can accelerate the discovery of novel electromagnetic composites.

II.1
n

A two-phase random medium is a domain of space

\mathcal{I}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1,&\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{V}_{i},\\
&0,&\mathbf{x}\notin\mathcal{V}_{i}.\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

The
n
-point correlation function

i

;
Torquato 2002

\displaystyle S_{n}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})=\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{n}{\cal I}^{(i)}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right\rangle,

(3)

where angular brackets denote an ensemble average over realizations. The function

\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}

i

For statistically homogeneous media,

II.2
Two-point statistics

III
Exact Strong-Contrast Expansions and Nonlocality

The original strong-contrast expansions for the effective dynamic dielectric constant obtained by Rechtsman and Torquato
Rechtsman and Torquato 2008
were derived from homogenized constitutive relations that are local in space and hence are strictly valid only in the quasistatic regime. In the present work, we follow the general strong-contrast formalism of Torquato
Torquato 2002
that was devised for the purely static problem
Torquato 2002
and show that it naturally leads to exact homogenized constitutive relations for the averaged fields that are nonlocal in space.
The crucial consequences of this development are exact expressions for the effective dynamic dielectric constant tensor
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{I})
for a macroscopically anisotropic medium of arbitrary microstructure into which a plane wave of wave vector
{\bf k}_{I}
is incident.
These expressions for
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{I})
are valid from the infinite-wavelength limit down to wavelengths (
\lambda=2\pi/\absolutevalue{\vect{k}_I}
) on the order of the heterogeneity length scale
\ell
.
We then briefly explain how our theory departs substantially from standard multiple-scattering theory
Frisch 1968
;
Sheng 1995
;
Tsang 2001
;
Cazé and Schotland 2015
.
We explicitly show they necessarily require complete microstructural information, as embodied in the infinite set of
n
-point correlation functions (Sec.
II.1
) of the composite.
We also describe the fast-convergence properties of strong-contrast expansions and their consequences for extracting accurate approximations for
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{I})
.

III.1
Strong-contrast expansions

Consider a macroscopically large ellipsoidal two-phase statistically homogeneous but anisotropic composite specimen in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
embedded inside an infinitely large reference phase
I
with a dielectric constant tensor
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{I}
.
The microstructure is perfectly general, and it is assumed that a characteristic heterogeneity length scale
\ell
is much smaller than the specimen size, i.e.,
\ell\ll L
.
The shape of this specimen is purposely chosen to be nonspherical since any rigorously correct expression for the effective property must ultimately be independent of the shape of the composite specimen in the infinite-volume limit.
It is assumed that the applied or incident electric field
\mathinner{{\bf E}_{0}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}
is a plane wave of an angular frequency
\omega
and wave vector
{\bf k}_{I}
in the reference phase, i.e.,

\mathinner{{\bf E}_{0}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}=\tilde{{\bf E}}_{0}\mathinner{\exp\mathopen{\left(i({\bf k}_{I}\cdot{\bf x}-\omega t)\right)}}.

(19)

Our interest is the exact expression for the effective dynamic dielectric constant tensor
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{I},\omega)
.
Without loss of generality, we assume a linear dispersion relation in the reference phase, i.e.,
k_{I}\equiv\absolutevalue{\vect{k}_I}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{I}}\omega/c
,
where
c
is the speed of light in vacuum, and thus we henceforth do not explicitly indicate the dependence of functions on
\omega
.
The composite is assumed to be nonmagnetic, implying that the phase magnetic permeabilities are identical, i.e.,
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\mu_{0}
, where
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permeability of the vacuum.
For simplicity, we assume real-valued, frequency-independent isotropic phase dielectric constants
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
.
Nonetheless, the composite can be generally lossy (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}
is complex-valued) due to scattering from the inhomogeneities in the local dielectric constant.
It is noteworthy that our results can be straightforwardly extended to phase dielectric constants that are complex-valued (dissipative media), but this is not done in the present work.

Here we present a compact derivation of strong-contrast expansions. It follows the general formalism of Torquato
Torquato 2002
closely but departs from it at certain key steps when establishing the nonlocality of the homogenized constitutive relation.
(A detailed derivation is given in the Supplementary Material
SM
.)
For simplicity, we take the reference phase
I
to be phase
q
(equal to 1 or 2).
Under the aforementioned assumptions, the local electric field
\mathinner{{\bf E}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}
solves the time-harmonic Maxwell equation
Rechtsman and Torquato 2008
:

\curl{\curl{\fn{\vect{E}}{\vect{x}}}}-{k_{q}}^{2}\mathinner{{\bf E}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}=\quantity(\frac{\omega}{c})^{2}\mathinner{{\bf P}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}},

(20)

where
\mathinner{{\bf P}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}
is the
polarization field
given by

\mathinner{{\bf P}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}\equiv\quantity[\fn{{\varepsilon}}{\vect{x}} - {\varepsilon}_q]\mathinner{{\bf E}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}

(21)

and

\varepsilon({\bf x})=(\varepsilon_{p}-\varepsilon_{q}){\cal I}^{(p)}({\bf x})+\varepsilon_{q}

(22)

is the local dielectric constant, and
{\cal I}^{(p)}({\bf x})
is the indicator function for phase
p
[cf. Eq. (
2

The vector
\mathinner{{\bf P}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}
is the induced flux field relative to reference phase
q
due to the presence of phase
p
, and hence is zero in the reference phase
q
and nonzero in the “polarized” phase
p
(
p\neq q
).

Using the Green’s function formalism, the local electric field can be expressed in terms of the following
integral equation

;
Torquato 2002

\displaystyle\mathinner{{\bf E}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}=

\displaystyle\mathinner{{\bf E}_{0}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}+\int\mathinner{\boldsymbol{G}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}}\right)}}\cdot\mathinner{{\bf P}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}^{\prime}\right)}}\differential{\vect{x}'},

(23)

where the second-rank tensor Green’s function
\mathinner{\boldsymbol{G}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}

2
2

2

The Green’s function in this work (
24

\mathinner{\boldsymbol{G}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}=-\boldsymbol{D}^{(q)}\mathinner{\delta\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}+\mathinner{\boldsymbol{H}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}},

(24)

is a constant second-rank tensor that arises when one excludes an infinitesimal region around the position of the singularity
{\bf x}^{\prime}={\bf x}
in the Green’s function, and
\mathinner{\boldsymbol{H}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}
represents the contribution outside of the “exclusion” region:

\displaystyle\mathinner{H^{(q)}_{ij}\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}=

\displaystyle i\frac{\pi}{2\varepsilon_{q}}\quantity(\frac{k_q}{2\pi r})^{d/2}\big\{\quantity[k_q r \Hankel{d/2-1}{k_qr} -\Hankel{d/2}{k_qr}]\delta_{ij}

\mathinner{\mathcal{H}_{\nu}^{(1)}\mathopen{\left(x\right)}}

The Fourier transform of Eq. (

\mathinner{\tilde{G}^{(q)}_{ij}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}\right)}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{q}}\frac{{k_{q}}^{2}\delta_{ij}-k_{i}k_{j}}{k^{2}-{k_{q}}^{2}}.

(26)

The use of Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathinner{{\bf F}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}=

\displaystyle\mathinner{{\bf E}_{0}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}+\int_{\epsilon}\mathinner{\boldsymbol{H}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}}\right)}}\cdot\mathinner{{\bf P}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}^{\prime}\right)}}\differential{\vect{x}'},

(27)

where the integral subscript

indicates that the integral is to be carried out by omitting the exclusion region and then allowing it to uniformly shrink to zero.
Here
\mathinner{{\bf F}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}

\mathinner{{\bf E}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}
via

\mathinner{{\bf F}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}=\quantity{\tens{I} + \tens{D}^{(q)}[\fn{\varepsilon}{\vect{x}} - \varepsilon_q]}\cdot{\bf E}({\bf x}).

(28)

Using the definitions (

\mathinner{{\bf P}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}

\mathinner{{\bf F}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}

\mathinner{{\bf P}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}=\mathinner{\boldsymbol{L}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}\cdot\mathinner{{\bf F}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}},

\mathinner{\boldsymbol{L}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}=[\mathinner{\varepsilon\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}-\varepsilon_{q}]\cdot\quantity{\tens{I} + \tens{D}^{(q)}[\fn{\varepsilon}{\vect{x}} - \varepsilon_q]}^{-1}.

(30)

It is noteworthy that one is free to choose any convenient exclusion-region shape, provided that its boundary is sufficiently smooth.
The choice of the exclusion-region shape is crucially important because it determines the type of
modified
electric field that results as well as the corresponding expansion parameter in the series expansion for the effective dynamic dielectric constant tensor.
For example, when a spheroidal-shaped exclusion region at a position
{\bf x}
in

\boldsymbol{D}^{(q)}=\frac{A^{*}}{\varepsilon_{q}}\boldsymbol{I},

where
\boldsymbol{I}
is the second-rank identity tensor and

In the special cases of a sphere, disk-like limit, and needle-like limit,

A^{*}=1/d,1,0
, respectively.
Thus, for these three cases, Eq. (
30
) yields the following shape-dependent tensor:

\displaystyle\mathinner{\boldsymbol{L}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf x};A^{*}\right)}}=\mathinner{\mathcal{I}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}\boldsymbol{I}

\displaystyle\times\begin{cases}d\varepsilon_{q}\beta_{pq},&A^{*}=1/d~(\text{spherical})\\
\varepsilon_{q}\quantity(1 - \varepsilon_q/\varepsilon_p),&A^{*}=1~(\text{disk-like})\\
\varepsilon_{q}\quantity(\varepsilon_p/\varepsilon_q - 1),&A^{*}=0~(\text{needle-like})\end{cases},

(32)

where
\beta_{pq}
is the dielectric

defined by

\beta_{pq}\equiv\frac{\varepsilon_{p}-\varepsilon_{q}}{\varepsilon_{p}+(d-1)\varepsilon_{q}}.

(33)

Moreover, in these three cases, the generalized cavity intensity field
\mathinner{{\bf F}\mathopen{\left({\bf x};A^{*}\right)}}
reduces to

\mathinner{{\bf F}\mathopen{\left({\bf x};A^{*}\right)}}\to\begin{cases}\mathinner{{\bf E}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}+\frac{\displaystyle\mathinner{{\bf P}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}}{\displaystyle d\varepsilon_{q}},&A^{*}=1/d~(\text{spherical})\\
\frac{\displaystyle\mathinner{{\bf D}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}}{\displaystyle\varepsilon_{q}},&A^{*}=1~(\text{disk-like})\\
\mathinner{{\bf E}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}},&A^{*}=0~(\text{needle-like}),\end{cases}

(34)

respectively, where
\mathinner{{\bf D}\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}
is the displacement field.
Importantly, among these three cases, the original and modified strong-contrast expansions arise only when the exclusion region is taken to be a sphere, as we elaborate below.

We now show that our formalism yields an exact relation between the polarization field
{\bf P}({\bf x})
and the applied field
{\bf E}_{0}({\bf x})
that is nonlocal in space.
It is more convenient at this stage to utilize a compact linear operator notation, which enables us to express the integral equation (
27
) as

\displaystyle{\bf F}={\bf E}_{0}+\boldsymbol{H}{\bf P},

(35)

where we temporarily drop the superscript
q
.
A combination of this equation with Eq. (
29
) yields the following integral equation for the polarization field

{\bf P}=\boldsymbol{L}{\bf E}_{0}+\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{H}{\bf P}.

(36)

The desired nonlocal relation is obtained from Eq. (
36
) by successive substitutions:

\displaystyle{\bf P}=\boldsymbol{S}{\bf E}_{0}.

(37)

where

\boldsymbol{S}=[\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{H}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{L},

(38)

is a
generalized scattering operator
that has superior mathematical properties compared to the
scattering operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
that arises in standard
multiple-scattering theory
Frisch 1968
;
Sheng 1995
;
Tsang 2001
, as we elaborate below in Remark
viii.
.
More explicitly, the nonlocal relation (
37
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathinner{{\bf P}\mathopen{\left({\bf 1}\right)}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}\differential{\vect{2}}\mathinner{\boldsymbol{S}\mathopen{\left({\bf 1},{\bf 2}\right)}}\cdot\mathinner{{\bf E}_{0}\mathopen{\left({\bf 2}\right)}},

(39)

where boldface numbers
{\bf 1},{\bf 2}
are shorthand notations for position vectors
{\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2}
.
Ensemble averaging Eq. (
39
) and invoking statistical homogeneity yields the convolution relation

\mathinner{\left\langle{\bf P}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf 1}\right)}}=\int_{\epsilon}\differential{\vect{2}}\mathinner{\left\langle\boldsymbol{S}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf 1}-{\bf 2}\right)}}\cdot\mathinner{{\bf E}_{0}\mathopen{\left({\bf 2}\right)}},

(40)

where the operator
\left\langle\boldsymbol{S}\right\rangle
depends on relative positions, i.e.,
\mathinner{\left\langle\boldsymbol{S}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf 1},{\bf 2}\right)}}=\mathinner{\left\langle\boldsymbol{S}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf 1}-{\bf 2}\right)}}
, and angular brackets denote an ensemble average.
Formally, the nonlocal relation (
40
) is the same as the one given in Torquato
Torquato 2002
for the static problem, but nonlocality was not explicitly invoked there.
Taking the Fourier transform of (
40
) yields a compact Fourier representation of this nonlocal relation, namely,

\mathinner{\widetilde{\left\langle{\bf P}\right\rangle}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}\right)}}=\mathinner{\widetilde{\left\langle\boldsymbol{S}\right\rangle}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}\right)}}\cdot\mathinner{\tilde{{\bf E}}_{0}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}\right)}},

(41)

where
\mathinner{\widetilde{\left\langle f\right\rangle}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}\right)}}\equiv\int\mathinner{\left\langle f\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf x}\right)}}\mathinner{\exp\mathopen{\left(-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}\right)}}\differential{\vect{x}}
.
From Eq. (
19

\mathinner{\tilde{{\bf E}}_{0}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}\right)}}=\tilde{{\bf E}}_{0}\mathinner{\delta\mathopen{\left({\bf k}-{\bf k}_{q}\right)}}
, implying that the wave vector
{\bf k}
in Eq. (

{\bf k}_{q}
.

As in the static case
Sen and Torquato 1989
;
Torquato 2002
and quasistatic regime
Rechtsman and Torquato 2008
, the ensemble-averaged operator
\mathinner{\left\langle\boldsymbol{S}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}
, which is given explicitly in the Supplementary Material

in terms of the
n
-point correlation functions and products of the tensor
\boldsymbol{H}({\bf r})
, depends on the shape of the macroscopic ellipsoidal composite specimen (see Fig.

).
This shape-dependence arises from because
\boldsymbol{H}
decays like

for large

, and hence,
\mathinner{\left\langle\boldsymbol{S}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}
involves conditionally convergent integrals
Torquato 2002
.
To avoid such conditional convergence issues, we follow previous strong-contrast formulations by seeking to eliminate
the applied field
{\bf E}_{0}
in Eq. (
40
) in favor of the average cavity field
\mathinner{\left\langle{\bf F}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}
in order to get a corresponding nonlocal homogenized constitutive relation between
\mathinner{\left\langle{\bf P}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}
and
\mathinner{\left\langle{\bf F}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}

Thus, solving for
{\bf E}_{0}
in Eq. (

\langle{\bf F}\rangle=[\left\langle\boldsymbol{S}\right\rangle^{-1}+\boldsymbol{H}]\left\langle{\bf P}\right\rangle.

\mathinner{\left\langle{\bf P}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf 1}\right)}}=\int\differential{\vect{2}}\boldsymbol{L}_{e}^{(q)}({\bf 1}-{\bf 2})\cdot\mathinner{\left\langle{\bf F}\right\rangle\mathopen{\left({\bf 2}\right)}},

\displaystyle\mathinner{\boldsymbol{L}_{e}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}\equiv

\displaystyle\int\differential{\vect{r}'}\quantity[\fn{\tens{\varepsilon}_e}{\vect{r}'} - \varepsilon_q \tens{I}\fn{\delta}{\vect{r}'}]\cdot\Big[\boldsymbol{I}\mathinner{\delta\mathopen{\left({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}\right)}}

\mathinner{\Delta_{n}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf r}_{1},\cdots,{\bf r}_{n}\right)}}=\begin{vmatrix}\mathinner{S_{2}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2}\right)}}&\mathinner{S_{1}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf r}_{1}\right)}}&\cdots&0\\

(53)

For macroscopically isotropic media, the effective dielectric tensor is isotropic, i.e.,
\mathinner{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}=\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}\boldsymbol{I}

The corresponding strong-contrast expansion for
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}

\displaystyle{\phi_{p}}^{2}\beta^{2}_{pq}[\varepsilon_{e}({\bf k}_{q})+(d-1)\varepsilon_{q}][\varepsilon_{e}({\bf k}_{q})-\varepsilon_{q}]^{-1}

\displaystyle\phi_{p}\beta_{pq}-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathinner{A_{n}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}{\beta_{pq}}^{n},

(54)

\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}=\Tr[\mathinner{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}]/d

\mathinner{A_{n}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}=\Tr[\mathinner{\boldsymbol{A}_{n}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}]/d

n\geq 2
and
\Tr[\;]
denotes the trace operation.
Furthermore, for statistically isotropic media, the effective dielectric constant becomes independent of the direction of the wave vector, i.e.,
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}=\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}
.

Remarks:

i.

Importantly, the strong-contrast expansion (
49
) is a series representation of a linear fractional transformation of
the variable
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})
(left-hand
side of the equation), rather than the effective dielectric constant
tensor itself.
The series expansion in powers of the polarizability
\beta_{pq}
of this particular rational function of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})
has important consequences for the predictive power of approximations
derived from the expansion, as detailed
in Sec.
III.2
.

ii.

The fact that the exact expansion (
49
), extracted from our nonlocal relation (
46
), is explicitly given in terms of integrals over products of the relevant Green’s functions and the
n
-point correlation functions to infinite order implies that multiple-scattering to all orders is exactly treated for the range of wave numbers for which our extended homogenization theory applies, i.e.,
0\leq|{\bf k}_{q}|\ell\lesssim 1
.

iii.

Note that Eq. (
49
) represents two different series
expansions: one for
q=1
and
p=2
and the other for
q=2
and
p=1
.

iv.

The exact expansions represented by (
49
) are
independent of the reference phase
q
and hence independent of the wave vector
{\bf k}_{q}
.

v.

For
d=2
, the strong-contrast expansion applies for TE polarization only.
This implies that the electric field and wave vector are parallel to the plane or transverse to an axis of symmetry in a 3D system whose cross sections are identical.

vi.

Formally, the original strong-contrast expansions that apply in the quasistatic regime
Rechtsman and Torquato 2008
can be obtained from the nonlocal strong-contrast expansions (
49
) by simply replacing the exponential functions that appear in the expressions for the second-rank tensors

\mathinner{\boldsymbol{A}_{n}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}
, defined by Eq. (
52
), by unity.

vii.

For statistically isotropic media, the effective phase speed
\mathinner{c_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}
and attenuation coefficient
\mathinner{\gamma_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}
are determined by the scalar effective dielectric constant

\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}
:

\displaystyle\mathinner{c_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}/c=

\displaystyle{n_{e}(k_{q})}^{-1}=\real\quantity[1/\sqrt{\fn{\varepsilon_e}{k_q}}],

(55)

\displaystyle\mathinner{\gamma_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}/c=

\displaystyle{\kappa_{e}(k_{q})}^{-1}=\imaginary\quantity[1/\sqrt{\fn{\varepsilon_e}{k_q}}],

(56)

where
{n_{e}}(k_{q})
and
\mathinner{\kappa_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}
are the effective refractive and extinction indices, respectively.
The quantity
\mathinner{\exp\mathopen{\left(-2\pi\gamma_{e}/c_{e}\right)}}
is the factor by which the incident-wave amplitude is attenuated for a period
2\pi/\omega
.

viii.

The strong-contrast formalism is a significant departure from perturbative expansions obtained from standard
multiple-scattering theory
Frisch 1968
;
Sheng 1995
;
Tsang 2001
;
Cazé and Schotland 2015
. The operator
\boldsymbol{S}
defined in Eq. (
38
) is a generalization of the standard scattering operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}=[\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{G}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}

Frisch 1968
;
Sheng 1995
;
Tsang 2001
,
where
\boldsymbol{V}\equiv\quantity[\fn{\varepsilon}{\vect{r}}-\varepsilon_q]\boldsymbol{I}
is the
scattering potential
.
Thus, the perturbation series resulting from
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
is a weak-contrast expansion with the inherent
limitations that it converges only for small contrast ratios (see also Sec.
III.2
). By contrast, due to the
different possible choices for the exclusion regions and reference phases,
there is an infinite variety of series expansions that result from the strong-contrast formalism with generally fast-convergence properties. The particular strong-contrast expansion can be designed for different classes of microstructures (see Appendixes
A
and D).
The corresponding strong-contrast “
self-energy
”
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is a linear fractional transform of
\boldsymbol{L}_{e}
[see Eq. (
48
)],
namely,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\boldsymbol{L}_{e}}^{-1}\quantity[\tens{I} -\tens{D} {\tens{L}_e}^{-1}]^{-1}
, which is a
generalization of the self-energy in standard multiple-scattering theory
Frisch 1968
;
Sheng 1995
;
Tsang 2001
;
Cazé and Schotland 2015
.
Thus, it is highly nontrivial to relate diagrammatic expansions of the strong-contrast
formalism to those of multiple-scattering theory.
An elaboration of how strong-contrast expansion generalize those from multiple-scattering theory is presented in the Supplementary Material
SM
.

III.2
Convergence properties and accuracy of truncated series

The form of the strong-contrast expansion parameter
\beta_{pq}
in Eq. (
49
) is a direct consequence of the choice of a spherical region excluded from the volume integrals in Eq. (
49
) due to singularities in the Green’s functions
Rechtsman and Torquato 2008
.
It is bounded by

-\frac{1}{d-1}\leq\beta_{pq}\equiv\frac{\varepsilon_{p}-\varepsilon_{q}}{\varepsilon_{p}+(d-1)\varepsilon_{q}}\leq 1,

(57)

which implies that the strong-contrast expansion (
49
) can converge rapidly, even for
infinite contrast ratio
\varepsilon_{p}/\varepsilon_{q}\to\infty
.
Other choices for the shape of the exclusion region will lead to different
expansion parameters that will generally be bounded but can lead to
expansions with significantly different convergence properties
Torquato 2002
.
In Appendix
A
, we present the corresponding expansions for disk-like and needle-like exclusion regions, which
are exceptional cases that lead to slowly converging
weak-contrast
expansions with expansion parameter
(\varepsilon_{p}-\varepsilon_{q})/\varepsilon_{q}
, and thus are unbounded when
\varepsilon_{p}/\varepsilon_{q}\to\infty
.

Figure 3:
Schematic of the optimal multiscale “coated-spheres” model that realizes the isotropic Hashin-Shtrikman bounds on
\varepsilon_{e}

Hashin and Shtrikman 1963
.
Each composite sphere is composed of a spherical inclusion of one phase (dispersed phase) that is surrounded by a concentric spherical shell of the other phase such that the fraction of space occupied by the dispersed phase is equal to its overall phase volume fraction. The composite spheres fill all space, implying that their sizes range down to the infinitesimally small.
When phase 2 is the disconnected inclusion (dispersed) phase, this two-phase medium minimizes and maximizes the effective static dielectric constant
\varepsilon_{e}
for prescribed volume fraction and contrast ratio, when
\varepsilon_{2}/\varepsilon_{1}>1
and
\varepsilon_{2}/\varepsilon_{1}<1
, respectively.
It has recently been proved that these highly degenerate optimal Hashin-Shtrikman multiscale distributions of spheres are hyperuniform
Kim and Torquato 2019a
;
Kim and Torquato 2019b
.

Importantly, in the purely static case, the expansion (
49
) becomes identical to one derived by Sen and Torquato
Sen and Torquato 1989
and its truncation after second-order terms [i.e., setting
\boldsymbol{A}_{n}^{(p)}=0
for all
n\geq 3

Hashin and Shtrikman 1963
derived by Willis
Willis 1981
that are optimal since
they are realized by certain statistically anisotropic composites in which there is a disconnected, dispersed phase in a connected matrix phase
Milton 1981
.
In the case of an isotropic effective dielectric constant
\varepsilon_{e}
, the optimal Hashin-Shtrikman upper and lower bounds for any phase-contrast ratio
\varepsilon_{2}/\varepsilon_{1}
are exactly realized by the multiscale “coated-spheres” model, which is depicted in Fig.
3
in two dimensions. Affine transformations of the coated spheres in the
d
orthogonal directions lead to oriented coated ellipsoids that are optimal for the
macroscopically anisotropic case.
The lower bound corresponds to the case when the high-dielectric-constant phase is the dispersed, disconnected phase and the upper bound corresponds to the instance in which the high-dielectric-constant phase is the connected matrix.
Thus, Torquato
Torquato 1985
;
Torquato 2002
observed that the strong-contrast expansions (
49
) in the static limit can be regarded as ones that perturb around such optimal composites, implying that the first few terms of the expansion can yield accurate approximations of the effective property for a class of particulate composites as well as more general microstructures, depending on whether the high-dielectric phase percolates or not.
For example, even when
\varepsilon_{2}/\varepsilon_{1}\gg 1
, the dispersed phase 2 can consist of identical or polydisperse particles of general shape (ellipsoids, cubes, cylinders, polyhedra) with
prescribed orientations

that may or not
overlap
, provided that the particles are prevented from forming large clusters
compared to the specimen size. Moreover, when
\varepsilon_{2}/\varepsilon_{1}\ll 1
, the matrix phase
can be a cellular network
Torquato and Chen 2018
. Finally, for moderate values of the contrast ratio
\varepsilon_{2}/\varepsilon_{1}
,
even more general microstructures (e.g., those without well-defined inclusions) can be accurately treated.
Importantly, we show that for the dynamic problem under consideration, the first few terms of the expansion (
49
) yield accurate approximations of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})
for a similar wide class of two-phase media (see Sec.
VIII
). Analogous approximations were derived and applied for the quasistatic regime
Rechtsman and Torquato 2008
;
Chen and Torquato 2018
.

IV
Strong-Contrast Approximation Formulas

Here we describe lower-order truncations of the strong-contrast expansions that are expected to yield accurate closed-form formulas for
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})
that apply
over a broad range of wavelengths (
k_{q}\ell\lesssim 1
), volume fractions and contrast ratios for a wide class of microstructures.

IV.1
Macroscopically anisotropic media

For the ensuing treatment, it is convenient to rewrite the expansions (
49
), valid for macroscopically anisotropic media in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, in the following manner:

\displaystyle{\phi_{p}}^{2}\beta^{2}_{pq}\frac{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})+(d-1)\varepsilon_{q}\boldsymbol{I}}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})-\varepsilon_{q}\boldsymbol{I}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{p}\beta_{pq}\boldsymbol{I}-\sum_{n=2}^{M}\mathinner{\boldsymbol{A}_{n}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}{\beta_{pq}}^{n}+\mathinner{\mathcal{R}_{M}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}},

(63)

where the
M
th-order remainder term is defined as

\mathinner{\mathcal{R}_{M}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}\equiv\sum_{n=M+1}^{\infty}\mathinner{\boldsymbol{A}_{n}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}{\beta_{pq}}^{n}.

(64)

Truncating the exact nonlocal expansion (
63
) at the two- and three-point levels, i.e., setting
\mathinner{\mathcal{R}_{2}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}=0
and
\mathinner{\mathcal{R}_{3}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}=0
, respectively, yields

\displaystyle{\phi_{p}}^{2}\beta^{2}_{pq}\frac{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})+(d-1)\varepsilon_{q}\boldsymbol{I}}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})-\varepsilon_{q}\boldsymbol{I}}=

\displaystyle\phi_{p}\beta_{pq}\boldsymbol{I}-\mathinner{\boldsymbol{A}_{2}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}{\beta_{pq}}^{2},

(65)

\displaystyle{\phi_{p}}^{2}\beta^{2}_{pq}\frac{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})+(d-1)\varepsilon_{q}\boldsymbol{I}}{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})-\varepsilon_{q}\boldsymbol{I}}=

\displaystyle\phi_{p}\beta_{pq}\boldsymbol{I}-\Big[\mathinner{\boldsymbol{A}_{2}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}{\beta_{pq}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathinner{\boldsymbol{A}_{3}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}{\beta_{pq}}^{3}\Big],

(66)

Compared to the quasistatic approximation
Rechtsman and Torquato 2008
, these nonlocal approximations substantially extend the range of applicable wave number, namely,
0\leq\absolutevalue{\vect{k}_q}\ell\lesssim 1
.

IV.2
Macroscopically isotropic media

All of the applications considered in this paper, will focus on the case of macroscopically isotropic media, i.e., they are described by the scalar effective dielectric constant
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}=\mbox{Tr}\,[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})]/d
but depend on the direction of the wave vector
{\bf k}_{q}
.

IV.2.1
Strong-contrast approximation at the two-point level

Solving Eq. (
54
) for the effective dielectric constant
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}
yields the strong-contrast approximation for macroscopically isotropic media:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}}{\varepsilon_{q}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{d{\beta_{pq}}{\phi_{p}}^{2}}{\phi_{p}(1-\beta_{pq}\phi_{p})-\beta_{pq}\mathinner{A_{2}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{d{\beta_{pq}}{\phi_{p}}^{2}}{\phi_{p}(1-\beta_{pq}\phi_{p})+\frac{(d-1)\pi\beta_{pq}}{2^{d/2}\mathinner{\Gamma\mathopen{\left(d/2\right)}}}\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}},

(67)

where
\beta_{pq}
is defined in Eq. (
33
),
\mathinner{A_{2}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}\equiv\Tr[\mathinner{\boldsymbol{A}_{2}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}]/d
, and
\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left({\bf Q}\right)}}
is what we call the
nonlocal attenuation function
of a composite for reasons we describe below.
The direct- and Fourier-space representations of
\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left({\bf Q}\right)}}
are given as

\displaystyle\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left({\bf Q}\right)}}

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{2^{d/2}\mathinner{\Gamma\mathopen{\left(d/2\right)}}}{\pi}{Q}^{2}\int_{\epsilon}\frac{i}{4}\quantity(\frac{Q}{2\pi r})^{d/2-1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\mathinner{\mathcal{H}_{d/2-1}^{(1)}\mathopen{\left(Qr\right)}}e^{-i{\bf Q}\cdot{\bf r}}\mathinner{\chi_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left({\bf r}\right)}}\differential{\vect{r}}

(68)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathinner{\Gamma\mathopen{\left(d/2\right)}}}{2^{d/2}\pi^{d+1}}{Q}^{2}\int\frac{\mathinner{\tilde{\chi}_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left({\bf q}\right)}}}{\absolutevalue{\vect{q}+\vect{Q}}^{2}-Q^{2}}\differential{\vect{q}}.

(69)

The exponential
\mathinner{\exp\mathopen{\left(-i{\bf Q}\cdot{\bf r}\right)}}
in Eq. (
68
) arises from the phase difference associated with the incident waves at positions separated by
{\bf r}
. In the quasistatic regime, this phase factor is negligible, and Eq. (
68
) reduces to the local attenuation function
\mathinner{\mathcal{F}\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}
(derived in Ref.
Rechtsman and Torquato 2008
and summarized in the Supplementary Material

) because it is barely different from unity over the correlation length associated with the autocovariance function
\mathinner{\chi_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left(r\right)}}
.
The strong-contrast approximation (
67
) was postulated in Ref.
Kim and Torquato 2020
on physical grounds. By contrast, the present work derives it as a consequence of our exact nonlocal formalism (Sec.
III
).

For statistically isotropic media, the effective dielectric constant as well as the attenuation function are independent of the direction of the incident wave vector
{\bf k}_{q}
, and thus, they can be considered as functions of the wave number, i.e.,
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}=\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}
and
\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}=\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left({\bf k}_{q}\right)}}
.
Then, the real and imaginary parts of Eq. (
68
) can be simplified as

\displaystyle\imaginary[\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}]=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\frac{Q^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\mathinner{\tilde{\chi}_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left(2Q\cos\phi\right)}}\differential{\phi},&d=2\\
-\frac{Q}{2(2\pi)^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{2Q}q\mathinner{\tilde{\chi}_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left(q\right)}}\differential{q},&d=3\end{cases}

(70)

\displaystyle\real[\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}]=

\displaystyle-\frac{2Q^{2}}{\pi}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}\frac{1}{q(Q^{2}-q^{2})}\imaginary[\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(q\right)}}],

(71)

where Eq. (
71
) is valid for
d=2,3
, and
\mathrm{p.v.}
stands for the Cauchy principal value.
Following conventional usage, we say that a composite attenuates waves at a given wave number if the imaginary part of the effective dielectric constant is positive. Recall that attenuation in the present study occurs only because of multiple-scattering effects (not absorption).
While it is the imaginary part of
F(Q)
that determines directly the degree of attenuation or, equivalently,
\imaginary[\varepsilon_{e}]
, we see from Eq. (
71
) that the real part of
F(Q)
is directly related to its imaginary part.
It is for this reason that we refer to the complex function
F(Q)
as the (nonlocal) attenuation function.

IV.2.2
Modified strong-contrast approximation at the two-point level

Here we extend the validity of the strong-contrast approximation (
67
) so that it is accurate at larger wave numbers and hence better captures spatial dispersion.
This is done by an appropriate rescaling of the wave number in the reference phase,
k_{q}
, which we show is tantamount to approximately accounting for higher-order contributions in the remainder term
\mathinner{\mathcal{R}_{2}\mathopen{\left({k}_{q}\right)}}
.
Given that the strong-contrast expansion for isotropic media perturbs around the Hashin-Shtrikman structures (see Fig.
3
) in the static limit, it is natural to use the scaling
\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}/\varepsilon_{q}}k_{q}
, where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}
is the Hashin-Shtrikman estimate, i.e.,

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}\equiv\varepsilon_{q}\quantity[1+\frac{d\phi_p\beta_{pq}}{1-\phi_p \beta_{pq}}],

(72)

which gives the Hashin-Shtrikman lower bound and upper bound if
\varepsilon_{p}>\varepsilon_{q}
and
\varepsilon_{p}<\varepsilon_{q}
, respectively.
This scaling yields the following
scaled
strong-contrast approximation for statistically isotropic media:

\frac{\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}}{\varepsilon_{q}}=1+\frac{d{\beta_{pq}}{\phi_{p}}^{2}}{\phi_{p}(1-\beta_{pq}\phi_{p})+\frac{(d-1)\pi\beta_{pq}}{2^{d/2}\mathinner{\Gamma\mathopen{\left(d/2\right)}}}\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}}{\varepsilon_{q}}}k_{q}\right)}}}.

(73)

We now show that the scaled approximation (
73
) indeed provides good estimates of leading-order corrections of
\mathinner{\mathcal{R}_{2}\mathopen{\left({k}_{q}\right)}}
in powers of
k_{q}
.
To do so, we employ the concept of the averaged (effective) Green’s function
\left\langle\mathinner{\boldsymbol{G}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf q}\right)}}\right\rangle
of an inhomogeneous medium which in principle accounts for the all multiple-scattering events

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathinner{\boldsymbol{G}^{(q)}\mathopen{\left({\bf q}\right)}}\right\rangle=\quantity(\frac{\omega}{c})^{2}\quantity{\qty[q^2-{\fn{k_e}{\omega}}^2]\tens{I} -\vect{q}\vect{q}}^{-1},

(74)

where
{\mathinner{k_{e}\mathopen{\left(\omega\right)}}}\equiv\sqrt{\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(\omega\right)}}}\omega/c=\sqrt{\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(\omega\right)}}/\varepsilon_{q}}k_{q}
is the effective wave number at a frequency
\omega
,
and
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(\omega\right)}}
is the exact effective dynamic dielectric constant, assuming a well-defined homogenization description
Sheng 1995
;
Carminati and Sáenz 2009
.
Since exact complete microstructural information is, in principle, accounted for with the effective Green’s function (
74
), the exact strong-contrast expansion can be approximately equated to the one truncated at the two-point level with an attenuation function given in terms of the effective Green’s function, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\phi_{p}}^{2}\beta^{2}_{pq}\frac{\varepsilon_{e}(k_{q})+(d-1)\varepsilon_{q}}{\varepsilon_{e}(k_{q})-\varepsilon_{q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{p}\beta_{pq}-\mathinner{A_{2}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}{\beta_{pq}}^{2}+\mathinner{\mathcal{R}_{2}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}},

(75)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\phi_{p}\beta_{pq}-\mathinner{A_{2}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left(\mathinner{k_{e}\mathopen{\left(\omega\right)}}\right)}}{\beta_{pq}}^{2}.

(76)

When the functional form of
\mathinner{A_{2}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}
or, equivalently,
\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}
is available, it is possible to solve Eq. (
76
) for
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}
in a self-consistent manner. Instead, we show that by assuming
k_{e}(\omega)\approx\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}/\varepsilon_{q}}k_{q}
, which results in Eq. (
73
), we obtain good estimates of the leading-order corrections of
\mathinner{\mathcal{R}_{2}\mathopen{\left({k}_{q}\right)}}
in powers of
k_{q}
.
Thus, the scaled approximation (
73
) provides better estimates of the higher-order three-point approximation (given in Appendix
C
) than the unmodified strong-contrast approximation (
60
).
This can be easily confirmed in the quasistatic regime from the small-
k_{q}
expansions of
\mathinner{A_{2}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}
and
\mathinner{A_{3}^{(p)}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}
given in Ref.
Rechtsman and Torquato 2008
.
We also confirm the improved predictive capacity of the scaled strong-contrast approximation to better capture dispersive characteristics for ordered and disordered models via comparison to finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations; see Figs.
8
and
9
, and Sec. VIII in the Supplementary Material
SM
.

We consider four models of 2D and 3D disordered media

The particles of dielectric constant
\varepsilon_{2}

\varepsilon_{1}

V.1
Overlapping spheres

Overlapping spheres (also called the fully-penetrable-sphere model) refer to
an uncorrelated (Poisson) distribution of spheres of radius
a
throughout a matrix
Torquato 2002
.
For such nonhyperuniform models at number density
\rho
in
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the autocovariance function is known analytically
Torquato 2002
:

\mathinner{\chi_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left(r\right)}}=\exp\quantity(-\rho v_2(r;a))-{\phi_{1}}^{2},

(77)

where
\phi_{1}=\exp(-\rho\fn{v_1}{a} )
is the volume fraction of the matrix phase (phase 1),
v_{1}(a)
is given by (
13
),
and
\mathinner{v_{2}\mathopen{\left(r;a\right)}}
represents the union volume of two spheres whose centers are separated by a distance
r
.
In two and three dimensions, the latter quantity is explicitly given, respectively, by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathinner{v_{2}\mathopen{\left(r;a\right)}}}{\mathinner{v_{1}\mathopen{\left(a\right)}}}=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} 2\mathinner{\Theta\mathopen{\left(x-1\right)}}&+\frac{2}{\pi}\Big[\pi+x\quantity(1-x^2)^{1/2}\\
&-\mathinner{\cos^{-1}\mathopen{\left(x\right)}}\Big]\mathinner{\Theta\mathopen{\left(1-x\right)}}\end{aligned},&d=2\\
2\mathinner{\Theta\mathopen{\left(x-1\right)}}+\quantity(1+\frac{3x}{2}-\frac{x^3}{2})\mathinner{\Theta\mathopen{\left(1-x\right)}},&d=3\end{cases}

where
x\equiv r/2a
, and
\Theta(x)
(equal to 1 for
x>0
and zero otherwise) is the Heaviside step function.
For
d=2
and
d=3
, the particle phase (phase 2) percolates when
\phi_{2}\approx 0.68
(Ref.
Quintanilla et al. 2000
) and
\phi_{2}\approx 0.29

(Ref.
Rintoul and Torquato 1997
), respectively. The corresponding spectral densities are easily found numerically by performing the Fourier transforms
indicated in Eq. (
7
). In this work, we apply this model
for
\phi_{2}
’s well below the percolation thresholds.

V.2
Equilibrium packings

Another disordered nonhyperuniform model we treat is distributions of equilibrium (Gibbs) of identical hard spheres of radius
a

along the stable fluid branch
Hansen and McDonald 2013
;
Torquato 2002
. The structure factors of such packings are well approximated by the Percus-Yevick solution
Hansen and McDonald 2013
;
Torquato 2002
,
which is analytically solvable for odd values of
d
. For
d=3
, the Percus-Yevick solution gives the following expression for the structure factor
\mathinner{S\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}

Torquato 2002
:

\displaystyle\mathinner{S\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}=

\displaystyle\Big(1-\rho\frac{16\pi a^{3}}{q^{6}}\Big\{\big[24a_{1}\phi_{2}-12(a_{1}+2a_{2})\phi_{2}q^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+(12a_{2}\phi_{2}+2a_{1}+a_{2}\phi_{2})q^{4}]\cos(q)

\displaystyle+[24a_{1}\phi_{2}q-2(a_{1}+2a_{1}\phi_{2}+12a_{2}\phi_{2})q^{3}\big]\sin(q)

\displaystyle-24\phi_{2}(a_{1}-a_{2}q^{2})\Big\}\Big)^{-1},

(78)

where
q=2Qa
,
a_{1}=(1+2\phi_{2})^{2}/(1-\phi_{2})^{4}
, and
a_{2}=-(1+0.5\phi_{2})^{2}/(1-\phi_{2})^{4}
.
Using this solution in conjunction with Eq. (
11
) yields the corresponding spectral density
{\tilde{\chi}}_{{}_{V}}(Q)
.
For
d=2
, we obtain the spectral density from Monte Carlo generated disk packings
Torquato 2002
.

V.3
Hyperuniform polydisperse packings

Class I hyperuniform packings of spheres with a polydispersity in size can be constructed from nonhyperuniform progenitor point patterns via a tessellation-based procedure
Kim and Torquato 2019a
;
Kim and Torquato 2019b
.
Specifically, we employ the centers of 2D and 3D configurations of identical hard spheres in equilibrium at a packing fraction
0.45
and particle number
N=1000

as the progenitor point patterns.
One begins with the Voronoi tessellation
Torquato 2002
of these progenitor point patterns.
We then rescale the particle in the
j
th Voronoi cell
C_{j}
without changing its center such that the packing fraction inside this cell is identical to a prescribed value
\phi_{2}<1
.
The same process is repeated over all cells.
The final packing fraction is
\phi_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathinner{v_{1}\mathopen{\left(a_{j}\right)}}/V_{\mathfrak{F}}=\rho\mathinner{v_{1}\mathopen{\left(a\right)}}
, where
\rho
is the number density of particle centers
and
a
represents the mean sphere radius.
In the small-
\absolutevalue{\vect{Q}}
regime, the spectral densities of the resulting particulate composites exhibit a power-law scaling
\mathinner{\tilde{\chi}_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left({\bf Q}\right)}}\sim\absolutevalue{\vect{Q}}^{4}
,
which are of class I.

V.4
Stealthy hyperuniform packings

Stealthy hyperuniform particle systems, which are also class I, are defined by the spectral density vanishing around the origin, i.e.,
\mathinner{\tilde{\chi}_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left({\bf Q}\right)}}=0
for
0<\absolutevalue{\vect{Q}}\leq Q_{\mathrm{U}}
;
see Eq.(
14
).
We obtain the spectral density from realizations of disordered stealthy hyperuniform packings for
d=2,3
that are numerically generated via the following two-step procedure.
Specifically, we first generate such point configurations consisting of
N
particles in a fundamental cell
\mathfrak{F}
under periodic boundary conditions via the collective-coordinate optimization technique
Uche et al. 2004
;
Batten et al. 2008
;
Zhang et al. 2015
, which amounts to finding numerically the ground-state configurations for the following potential energy;

\mathinner{\Phi\mathopen{\left({\bf r}^{N}\right)}}=\frac{1}{V_{\mathfrak{F}}}\sum_{{\bf Q}}\mathinner{\tilde{v}\mathopen{\left({\bf Q}\right)}}\mathinner{S\mathopen{\left({\bf Q}\right)}}+\sum_{i<j}\mathinner{u\mathopen{\left(r_{ij}\right)}},

(79)

where

\mathinner{\tilde{v}\mathopen{\left({\bf Q}\right)}}=\begin{cases}1,&Q_{\text{L}}<\absolutevalue{\vect{Q}}\leq Q_{\text{U}},\\
0,&\mathrm{otherwise},\end{cases}

(80)

and a soft-core repulsive term
Zhang et al. 2017

\mathinner{u\mathopen{\left(r\right)}}=\begin{cases}(1-r/\sigma)^{2},&r<\sigma,\\
0,&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(81)

In contrast to the usual collective-coordinate procedure
Uche et al. 2004
;
Batten et al. 2008
;
Zhang et al. 2015
, the interaction (
79
) used here also includes a soft-core repulsive energy (
81
), as done in Ref.
Zhang et al. 2017
.
Thus, the associated ground-state configurations are still disordered, stealthy and hyperuniform, and their nearest-neighbor distances are larger than the length scale
\sigma
due to the soft-core repulsion
\mathinner{u\mathopen{\left(r\right)}}
.
Finally, to create packings, we follow Ref.
Zhang et al. 2016b
by circumscribing the points by identical spheres of radius
a<\sigma/2
under the constraint that they cannot overlap
(See the Supplementary Material
SM
for certain results concerning stealthy “nonhyperuniform” packings in which
Q_{\text{L}}>0
.)
The parameters used to generate these disordered stealthy packings are summarized in the Supplementary Material
SM
.

VI
Results for the Nonlocal Attenuation Function

We report some general behaviors of the nonlocal attenuation function
\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left({\bf Q}\right)}}
[cf. Eqs. (
68
) or (
69
)]
for nonhyperuniform and hyperuniform media for long and intermediate wavelengths.
We also provide plots of both the real and imaginary parts of
\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}

for the four models of disordered two-phase media considered in this work, which depends on wave number
Q
.

The function
\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}
depends on the microstructure via the spectral density
\mathinner{\tilde{\chi}_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}
.
Thus, assuming that the latter quantity has the power-law scaling
\mathinner{\tilde{\chi}_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}\sim Q^{\alpha}
as
Q\to 0
,
the asymptotic behavior of
\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}
in the long-wavelength limit (
Q\to 0
) follows as

\displaystyle\imaginary[\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}]

\displaystyle\sim\begin{cases}Q^{d},&\text{nonhyperuniform}\\
Q^{d+\alpha},&\text{hyperuniform}\end{cases},

\displaystyle\text{as~}Q\to 0,

(82)

\displaystyle\real[\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}]

\displaystyle\sim Q^{2},

\displaystyle~~\text{as~}Q\to 0,

(83)

where we use Eqs. (
68
) and (
70
). Recall that the exponent
\alpha
lies in the open interval
(0,\infty)
for hyperuniform systems (see Sec.
II.4
). For nonhyperuniform systems studied here, we take
\alpha=0
.
The reader is referred to the Supplementary Material
SM
for derivations of Eqs. (
82
) and (
83
).
Importantly, in the quasistatic regime, the imaginary parts of the effective dielectric constant for
both strong-contrast approximations, (
67
) and
(
73
), are determined by the asymptotic behaviors
\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}
indicated in Eq. (
82
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\imaginary[\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}]\sim\imaginary[\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}]

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{k_{q}}^{d},&\text{nonhyperuniform}\\
{k_{q}}^{d+\alpha},&\text{hyperuniform}\end{cases},\quad\text{as~}k_{q}\to 0.

(84)

Thus, hyperuniform media are less lossy than their nonhyperuniform counterparts in the quasistatic regime.

In the case of stealthy hyperuniform media [i.e.,
\mathinner{\tilde{\chi}_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}=0
for
0\leq Q<Q_{\mathrm{U}}
],
the imaginary part of
\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}
defined by Eq. (
70
) is identically zero (transparent or lossless) for any
space dimension
d
for a range of wave numbers; specifically,

\displaystyle\imaginary[\mathinner{F\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}]=0,~~\text{for }0\leq Q<Q_{\mathrm{U}}/2.

(85)

(The Supplementary Material
SM
describes how the local attenuation function
\mathinner{\mathcal{F}\mathopen{\left(Q\right)}}
derived in Ref.
Rechtsman and Torquato 2008

generally differs from its nonlocal counterpart.)
The transparency interval in which
\imaginary[\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}]=0
predicted by the two strong-contrast approximations [Eqs. (
67
) and (
73
)] is thus given by

\displaystyle\imaginary[\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}]=0,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for~}\begin{cases}0\leq k_{q}<\frac{Q_{\mathrm{U}}}{2},&\text{[from Eq. \eqref{eq:modified-strong-contrast-approximation_2pt}],}\\
0\leq k_{q}<\frac{Q_{\mathrm{U}}}{2(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}/\varepsilon_{q})^{1/2}},&\text{[from Eq. \eqref{eq:scaled-2pt-approx}}],\end{cases}

(86)

where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}
is given in Eq. (
72
).
When
\varepsilon_{p}>\varepsilon_{q}
, since
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}>\varepsilon_{q}
, the scaled approximation
accurately predicts a narrower transparency interval than the unscaled variant, as verified in Sec.
VIII
.
Interestingly, the transparency interval obtained from the less
accurate formula (
67

simulation results for stealthy hyperuniform “point” scatterers
Leseur et al. 2016

for the four distinct models of disordered particulate media in

VII
Simulation Procedure to Compute Effective Dynamic Dielectric Constant

In Ref.
Kim and Torquato 2020
, we established preliminary comparisons of strong-contrast approximation (
67
) and numerical simulations via the extended version of the fast-Fourier-Transform-based technique
Moulinec and Suquet 1998
;
Eyre and Milton 1999
.
Because of convergence issues, however,
in this paper, we employ a more reliable numerical technique, i.e., the FDTD method
Taflove et al. 2013
, using an open-source software package
Oskooi et al. 2010
.
We focus here on particulate media and take the matrix to be the reference medium (phase 1) and the particles to be the polarized phase (phase 2).

Figure 7:

Schematic of the general simulation setup for either (a) periodic or (b) nonperiodic composites consisting of
N
spheres of radius
a

in a matrix.
In both cases, Gaussian pulses of electric fields propagate from the planar sources (shown in red lines) to the packings (shown in black circles).
The wave number (spectrum) of the pulses spans between
\min[k_{1}]
and
\max[k_{1}]
.
Periodic boundary conditions are applied along all directions, except for the propagation direction
\hat{{\bf x}}
.
The perfectly matched layers (PML, shown in blue) of thickness
L_{\mathrm{PML}}
are placed at both ends of the simulation box to absorb any reflected and transmitted waves. To estimate the effective dielectric constant as described in step 2 below, we consider the subregion
V
(shown in gray)
that excludes from the composite within the simulation box two relatively thin slabs of thickness
L_{\mathrm{boundary}}
along the propagation direction.

The general simulation setup is schematically illustrated in Fig.
7
.
The simulation procedure for macroscopically isotropic media is carried out in three steps:

1.

For a given medium, we obtain the steady-state spatial distributions of electric field
\mathinner{E_{y}\mathopen{\left({\bf r},\omega\right)}}
and electric displacement field
\mathinner{D_{y}\mathopen{\left({\bf r},\omega\right)}}
at a given frequency
\omega
(or the corresponding wave number
k_{1}
).
Specifically, the planar source generates Gaussian pulses of electric fields that propagate along the
\hat{{\bf x}}
direction
with wave number
k_{1}
that spans between
\min[k_{1}]
and
\max[k_{1}]
.
Using the aforementioned MEEP package
Oskooi et al. 2010
, we compute time evolution of electric field
\mathinner{E_{y}\mathopen{\left({\bf r},t\right)}}
and electric displacement field
\mathinner{D_{y}\mathopen{\left({\bf r},t\right)}}
for a period of time
6\pi\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}}/\quantity{c \min[k_1]}
, where
c
is the speed of light in vacuum.
We then compute the temporal Fourier transforms of these fields inside packings.
The values of the simulation parameters (indicated in Fig.
7
) for the
2D and 3D ordered and disordered models studied in this article are summarized in the Supplementary Material
SM
.

2.

At each value of
k_{1}
, we postprocess
\mathinner{E_{y}\mathopen{\left({\bf r},\omega\right)}}
and
\mathinner{D_{y}\mathopen{\left({\bf r},\omega\right)}}
to estimate the effective dielectric constant
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{*}\mathopen{\left(k_{1},\omega\right)}}
of a single configuration by solving the following self-consistent equation:

\mathinner{\varepsilon_{*}\mathopen{\left(k_{1}\right)}}=\mathinner{\overline{\varepsilon_{*}}\mathopen{\left(k_{1}\right)}},

(87)

where

\displaystyle\mathinner{\varepsilon_{*}\mathopen{\left(k_{1}\right)}}\equiv\frac{\mathinner{\tilde{D}_{y}\mathopen{\left(k_{e},\omega\right)}}}{\mathinner{\tilde{E}_{y}\mathopen{\left(k_{e},\omega\right)}}},\quad\mathinner{\overline{\varepsilon_{*}}\mathopen{\left(k_{1}\right)}}\equiv\quantity(\frac{k_e}{\omega/c})^{2},

k_{e}
is a complex-valued effective wave number, and

\displaystyle\mathinner{\tilde{D}_{y}\mathopen{\left(q,\omega\right)}}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{V}}\int_{V}\mathinner{D_{y}\mathopen{\left({\bf r},\omega\right)}}e^{-iq\hat{{\bf x}}\cdot{\bf r}}\differential{\vect{r}},

\displaystyle\mathinner{\tilde{E}_{y}\mathopen{\left(q,\omega\right)}}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{V}}\int_{V}\mathinner{E_{y}\mathopen{\left({\bf r},\omega\right)}}e^{-iq\hat{{\bf x}}\cdot{\bf r}}\differential{\vect{r}},

where
V
is a rectangular parallelepiped subregion within the composite (shown in gray in Fig. 6) that is slightly smaller
than the simulation box and is used to reduce undesired boundary effects.
The homogenization task is carried out by numerically finding the minimizer of
\absolutevalue{\varepsilon_* - \overline{\varepsilon_*}}^{2}
with an initial guess
\overline{\varepsilon_{*}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}
via the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) nonlinear optimization algorithm
Liu and Nocedal 1989
, where
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}
is the Hashin-Shtrikman estimate given by Eq. (
72
). Details of step 2 are provided in the Supplementary Material
SM
.

3.

Steps 1-2 are repeated for a sufficient number of configurations for disordered media.
Then, we compute the effective dielectric constant at a given
k_{1}
by ensemble averaging
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{*}\mathopen{\left(k_{1}\right)}}
, i.e.,
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{1}\right)}}=\left\langle\mathinner{\varepsilon_{*}\mathopen{\left(k_{1}\right)}}\right\rangle
.

It is important to note that we ensure that the run times employed in step 1 are sufficiently long
such that the computed effective dielectric constants achieve stable and accurate steady-state values.
We emphasize that the result
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{*}\mathopen{\left(k_{1}\right)}}
obtained from Eq. (
87
) in step 2 is nonlocal in space because it is calculated from the nonlocal constitutive relation
\mathinner{\tilde{D}_{y}\mathopen{\left(k_{e},\omega\right)}}=\mathinner{\varepsilon_{*}\mathopen{\left(k_{1}\right)}}\mathinner{\tilde{E}_{y}\mathopen{\left(k_{e},\omega\right)}}
.
For periodic media, step 3 is unnecessary because all configurations are identical.

VIII

Discussion

All previous closed-form homogenization estimates of the effective dynamic dielectric constant apply only at long wavelengths (quasistatic regime) and for very special
macroscopically isotropic
disordered composite microstructures, namely, nonoverlapping spheres or spheroids in a matrix.
In this work, we lay the theoretical foundation that enables us to substantially extend previous
work in both its generality and applicability.
First, we derive exact homogenized constitutive relations for the effective dynamic dielectric constant tensor
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})
that are
nonlocal in space
from first principles.
Second, our strong-contrast representation of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})
exactly accounts for complete microstructural information (infinite set of
n
-point correlation functions) for
arbitrary microstructures
and hence multiple scattering
to all orders for the range of wave numbers for which our extended homogenization theory applies, i.e.,
0\leq|{\bf k}_{q}|\ell\lesssim 1

(where
\ell
is a characteristic heterogeneity length scale).
Third, we extract from the exact expansions accurate nonlocal closed-form approximate formulas for
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{e}({\bf k}_{q})
, relations (
67
) and (
73
), which are resummed representations
of the exact expansions that incorporate microstructural information through the spectral density
\mathinner{\tilde{\chi}_{{}_{V}}\mathopen{\left({\bf Q}\right)}}
, which is easily ascertained for general microstructures either
theoretically, computationally, or via scattering experiments. Depending on whether the high-dielectric phase percolates or not, the wide
class of microstructures that we can treat includes
particulate media consisting of identical or polydisperse particles of general shape (ellipsoids, cubes, cylinders, polyhedra)
with prescribed orientations that may or not
overlap
, and cellular networks as well as media without well-defined inclusions (Sec.
III.2
).
Our approximations account for multiple scattering across a range of wave numbers.
Fourth, we carry out precise full-waveform simulations for various 2D and 3D models of ordered and disordered media
to validate the accuracy of our nonlocal microstructure-dependent approximations for wave numbers well beyond the quasistatic regime.

Having established the accuracy of the scaled strong-contrast approximation (
73
), we then apply it to four models of 2D and 3D disordered media (both nonhyperuniform and hyperuniform)
to investigate the effect of microstructure on the effective wave characteristics. Among other findings, we show that disordered hyperuniform media are generally less lossy than their nonhyperuniform counterparts. We also find that our
scaled
formula (
73
)
accurately predicts that disordered stealthy hyperuniform media possess a transparency wave number interval

[0,0.5\,Q_{\mathrm{U}}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{HS}}/\varepsilon_{q})^{-1/2})
[cf. (
86
)], where most
nonhyperuniform disordered media are opaque.
Note that, using multiple-scattering simulations, Leseur
et al.

Leseur et al. 2016
were the first to show that stealthy hyperuniform systems should exhibit a transparency interval, but for “point” scatterers, not the finite-sized
scatterers considered here. Interestingly, their transparency-interval prediction coincides
with the one predicted by our less accurate strong-contrast formula [cf. Eq. (
86
)].

The accuracy of our nonlocal closed-form formulas has important practical implications, since one can now use them to accurately and efficiently predict the effective wave characteristics well beyond the quasistatic regime of a wide class of composite microstructures without having to perform computationally expensive full-blown simulations.
Thus, our nonlocal formulas can be used to accelerate the discovery of novel electromagnetic composites by appropriate tailoring of the spectral densities
and then constructing the corresponding microstructures by using the Fourier-space inverse methods
Chen and Torquato 2018
.
For example, from our findings in the present study, it is clear that stealthy disordered particulate media can be employed as low-pass filters that transmit waves
“isotropically” up to a selected wave number. Moreover, using the spectral densities
of the type found by Chen and Torquato
Chen and Torquato 2018
for stealthy hyperuniform packings
(characterized by a peak value at
Q=Q_{\text{U}}
with intensities that rapidly decay to zero for larger wave numbers)
and formula (
73
),
one can design materials with refractive indices that abruptly change over a narrow range
of wave numbers. Of course, one could also explore the design space of effective
wave properties of nonhyperuniform
disordered composite media for potential applications.

Previously, disordered media were often described using cluster expansions
Frisch 1968
;
Sheng 1995
;
Tsang 2001
, while ordered media were often studied through dispersion relations and band-structures calculations.
Accordingly, it has been of primary importance to bridge the gap between the treatments of ordered and disordered to better understand the optical properties of correlated media.
Thus, our work represents an initial step toward a unified theory to describe the effective optical properties of both ordered and disordered microstructures over a wide range of incident wavelengths.

There are a variety of directions for future research.
First, our formalism can be straightforwardly
extended to heterogeneous materials composed of more than two phases or continuous media.
Second, it is also of interest to extend our formalism to applications and phenomena (e.g., magnetic effects) relevant to the smaller wavelengths noted for metamaterials
Linden et al. 2004
;
Smith et al. 2004
;
Wang et al. 2012
.

Appendix B
Kramers-Kronig Relations

Kramers-Kronig relations connect the real and imaginary parts of any complex function that is analytic in the upper half-plane and meets
mild conditions
Jackson 1990
;
Milton et al. 1997
.
Since causality in a dielectric response function of a homogeneous material implies such analyticity properties, the Kramers-Kronig
relations enable one to directly link the real part of a response function
to its imaginary part or vice versa, even if the real or imaginary parts are
only available in a finite frequency range
Jackson 1990
;
Milton et al. 1997
.
Thus, when a heterogeneous
material can be treated as a homogeneous material with a dynamic effective dielectric constant
\varepsilon_{e}(k_{q})
,
Kramers-Kronig relations immediately apply to the exact strong-contrast
expansion (
63
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\real[\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}]=

\displaystyle\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(\infty\right)}}+\frac{2}{\pi}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}\frac{q\imaginary[\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(q\right)}}]}{q^{2}-{k_{q}}^{2}},

(90)

\displaystyle\imaginary[\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}]=

\displaystyle-\frac{2k_{q}}{\pi}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{q}\frac{\real[\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(q\right)}}]-\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(\infty\right)}}}{q^{2}-{k_{q}}^{2}},

(91)

where we assume a linear dispersion relation in the reference phase (i.e.,
k_{q}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{q}}\omega/c
) and

\lim_{\omega\to\infty}\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(\omega\right)}}=\varepsilon_{q}
is real-valued.
The Kramers-Kronig relations may or may not
be obeyed when the strong-contrast expansion is truncated at the two-point level, yielding Eq. (
67
).
Here we analytically show that the effective dielectric constant
\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}
for isotropic media obtained
from either the unscaled or scaled strong-contrast formulas [see Eqs.(
67
) and (

satisfies the Kramers-Kronig relations.

We begin by rewriting either

\mathinner{\varepsilon_{e}\mathopen{\left(k_{q}\right)}}\approx\varepsilon_{q}+\quantity[a+b\fn{F}{k_q}]^{-1}
, where

The general analytic properties of the nonlocal attenuation
