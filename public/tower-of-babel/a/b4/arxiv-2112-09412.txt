Phase diagram and Topological Expansion in the complex Quartic Random Matrix Model


Abstract
. We use the Riemann-Hilbert approach, together with string and Toda equations, to study the topological expansion in the quartic random matrix model. The coefficients of the topological expansion are generating functions for the numbers 𝒩 j ​ ( g ) \mathscr{N}_{j}(g) of 4 4 -valent connected graphs with j j vertices on a compact Riemann surface of genus g g . We explicitly evaluate these numbers for Riemann surfaces of genus 0 , 1 , 2 , 0,1,2, and 3 3 . Also, for a Riemann surface of an arbitrary genus g g , we calculate the leading term in the asymptotics of 𝒩 j ​ ( g ) \mathscr{N}_{j}(g) as the number of vertices tends to infinity. Using the theory of quadratic differentials, we characterize the critical contours in the complex parameter plane where phase transitions in the quartic model take place, thereby proving a result of David [ Dav91 ] . These phase transitions are of the following four types: a) one-cut to two-cut through the splitting of the cut at the origin, b) two-cut to three-cut through the birth of a new cut at the origin, c) one-cut to three-cut through the splitting of the cut at two symmetric points, and d) one-cut to three-cut through the birth of two symmetric cuts.

1.
Introduction and Main Results

Our starting point is
the unitary ensemble of
n\times n
Hermitian random matrices,

(1.1)

\textrm{d}\mu_{nN}(M;u)=\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}(u)}e^{-N\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathscr{V}(M;u)}\textrm{d}M,

with the quartic potential

(1.2)

\mathscr{V}(z;u)=\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\frac{uz^{4}}{4},

where
u>0
and
N>0
are
parameters
of the model, and

(1.3)

\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}(u)=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}e^{-N\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathscr{V}(M;u)}\textrm{d}M.

is the
partition function
.

As well known (see, e.g.,
[
BL14
]
), the ensemble of eigenvalues of
M
,

Me_{k}=z_{k}e_{k},\;k=1,\ldots,n,

is given by the probability distribution

(1.4)

\displaystyle\textrm{d}\mu_{nN}(z;u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{nN}(u)}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}\prod^{n}_{j=1}\exp\left[-N\left(\frac{z^{2}_{j}}{2}+\frac{uz^{4}_{j}}{4}\right)\right]\textrm{d}z_{1}\cdots\textrm{d}z_{n},

\displaystyle z=\left\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\right\},

where

(1.5)

\mathcal{Z}_{nN}(u)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\cdots\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}\prod^{n}_{j=1}\exp\left[-N\left(\frac{z^{2}_{j}}{2}+\frac{uz^{4}_{j}}{4}\right)\right]\textrm{d}z_{1}\cdots\textrm{d}z_{n},

is the
eigenvalue partition function
. The partition functions

\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}(u)
and

\mathcal{Z}_{nN}(u)
are related by the formula,

(1.6)

\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{nN}(u)}{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}(u)}=\frac{1}{\pi^{n(n-1)/2}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}k!

(see, e.g.,
[
BL14
]
).

We define the
free energy
of the unitary ensemble of
n\times n
Hermitian random matrices as

(1.7)

\mathscr{F}_{nN}(u)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\ln\,\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}(u)}{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}(0)}\,.

Observe that by (
1.6
),

(1.8)

\mathscr{F}_{nN}(u)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\ln\,\frac{{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}(u)}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}(0)}\,.

The quantity

(1.9)

{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}(0)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\cdots\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}\prod^{n}_{j=1}\exp\left(\frac{-Nz^{2}_{j}}{2}\right)\textrm{d}z_{1}\cdots\textrm{d}z_{n}

is the partition function of the Gaussian unitary ensemble (GUE), and
it is equal to

(1.10)

{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}(0)={\mathcal{Z}}^{\rm GUE}_{nN}=\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^{n^{2}}\frac{(2\pi)^{n/2}}{(2n)^{n^{2}/2}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}k!.

We will be especially interested in the free energy in the case when
n=N
.
The free energy

\mathscr{F}_{NN}(u)
admits the asymptotic expansion,

(1.11)

\mathscr{F}_{NN}(u)\sim\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{f}_{2g}(u)}{N^{2g}}\,,

in the sense that for any integer
M>0
, as
N\to\infty
,

(1.12)

\mathscr{F}_{NN}(u)=\sum_{g=0}^{M}\frac{\mathcal{f}_{2g}(u)}{N^{2g}}+O\left(N^{-2(M+1)}\right)\,.

In addition, the coefficients
\mathcal{f}_{2g}(u)
are analytic functions of
u
in a neighborhood of the origin independent of
g
. This was proven by Bleher and Its in
[
BI05
]
, for any
u>0
, and for general real 1-cut potentials
V
in
[
EM03
]
. More recently, probabilistic arguments have been used to study partition functions for generalized
\beta
ensembles (again with real 1-cut potentials) in
[
BG13
]
. Moreover, the asymptotics of the partition function for the real Gaussian-type, Laguerre-type, and Jacobi-type 1-cut potentials
V
were found using Riemann-Hilbert analysis in
[
Cha18
]
, and
[
CG21
]
.

Asymptotic expansion (
1.11
) is called the
topological expansion
, and its study was initiated in the classical work of Bessis, Itzykson, and Zuber
[
BIZ80
]
. As shown in
[
BIZ80
]
, the functions

\mathcal{f}_{2g}(u)
are generating functions for the number of topologically different 4-valent graphs with
j
vertices,
\mathscr{N}_{g}(j)
, on a closed Riemannian surface of genus
g
. We will discuss this remarkable fact later.

To evaluate the asymptotics of the Taylor coefficients of the functions

\mathcal{f}_{2g}(u)
, we will study an
analytic continuation

of the partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{NN}(u)
to the complex plane in
u
and singularities of the analytic continuation. Observe that integral (
1.5
) defining the eigenvalue partition
\mathcal{Z}_{NN}(u)

converges for
\Re u>0
, and we will prove that topological expansion (
1.11
) is valid for any
u
with
\Re u>0
. Also,
we will prove that all the functions

\mathcal{f}_{2g}(u)
are
analytic
in the half-plane
\Re u>0
.

To extend the partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{nN}(u)
to

\Re u\leq 0
, we will use a
regularization
of

\mathcal{Z}_{nN}(u)
. Assume first that
u>0
, and
let us make the change of variables

(1.13)

z_{j}=\sigma^{1/2}\zeta_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad u=\sigma^{-2}\,,\quad\sigma>0,

in the integral in (
1.5
):

(1.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{nN}(u)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\cdots\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}\prod^{n}_{j=1}\exp\left[-N\left(\frac{z^{2}_{j}}{2}+\frac{uz^{4}_{j}}{4}\right)\right]\textrm{d}z_{1}\cdots\textrm{d}z_{n}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\cdots\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{k})^{2}\prod^{n}_{j=1}\exp\left[-N\left(\frac{\sigma\zeta^{2}_{j}}{2}+\frac{\zeta^{4}_{j}}{4}\right)\right]\textrm{d}\zeta_{1}\cdots\textrm{d}\zeta_{n}.

Define now the quartic polynomial

(1.15)

V(\zeta;\sigma):=\mathscr{V}(\sigma^{1/2}\zeta;\sigma^{-2})=\frac{\sigma\zeta^{2}}{2}+\frac{\zeta^{4}}{4},

The corresponding partition function of eigenvalues is given by

(1.16)

Z_{nN}(\sigma)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\cdots\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(\zeta_{j}-\zeta_{k})^{2}\prod^{n}_{j=1}\exp\left[-N\left(\frac{\sigma\zeta^{2}_{j}}{2}+\frac{\zeta^{4}_{j}}{4}\right)\right]\textrm{d}\zeta_{1}\cdots\textrm{d}\zeta_{n},

which
converges for all

\sigma\in{\mathbb{C}}
and defines
Z_{nN}(\sigma)

as an entire function on the complex plane. Note that

(1.17)

\mathcal{Z}_{nN}(u)=\sigma^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}}Z_{nN}(\sigma),\quad\sigma=u^{-1/2}.

This formula gives an analytic continuation of the partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{nN}(u)
to the two-sheet covering of the complex plane.

Similar to (
1.8
) we define the free energy for the quartic polynomial (
1.15
) as

(1.18)

F_{nN}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\ln\,\frac{Z_{nN}(\sigma)}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}^{\rm GUE}}\,,

where the value of
{\mathcal{Z}}_{nN}^{\rm GUE}
is given in formula (
1.10
). From formulae (
1.17
) and (
1.8
) we obtain the relation between the free energies
{\mathscr{F}}_{nN}(u)
and

F_{nN}(\sigma)
:

(1.19)

\mathscr{F}_{nN}(u)=\frac{\ln\sigma}{2}+F_{nN}(\sigma),\quad\sigma=u^{-1/2}.

In this work our goal will be

(1)

to find and calculate critical curves of the matrix model with the quartic polynomial

V(z;\sigma)
on the complex plane
\sigma\in{\mathbb{C}}
, and

(2)

to prove the topological expansion of the free energy

F_{NN}(\sigma)
in the one-cut region on the complex plane
\sigma
and calculate the coefficients of the topological expansion.

1.2.
Topological Expansion of the Free Energy and Combinatorics of Four-valent Graphs

Our third main result in this paper concerns the topological expansion of the free energy,

(1.30)

F_{NN}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\ln\frac{Z_{NN}(\sigma)}{{\mathcal{Z}}_{NN}^{\rm GUE}}\,.

The existence of the
\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}
expansion of the free energy for general real potentials

\mathscr{V}(z)=\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\sum^{\nu}_{k=1}u_{k}z^{k},

is proven in
[
EM03
]
, where
u_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
are such that the corresponding partition function exists. The analogous result for the complex cubic potential
\mathscr{V}(z)=\frac{z^{2}}{2}+uz^{3}
was proven in
[
BD12
]
, and in this work we extend this result for the complex quartic potential (
1.2
), or equivalently for (
1.15
).

Theorem 1.4
.

For all
\sigma
in the one-cut region
\mathcal{O}_{1}
, the free energy
F_{NN}(\sigma)
admits the topological expansion,

(1.31)

F_{NN}(\sigma)\sim\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}\frac{f_{2g}(\sigma)}{N^{2g}}\,,

and the functions
f_{2g}(\sigma)
are analytic in
\sigma
for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{1}
.

Remark 1.5
.

Due to (
1.19
), we also have

(1.32)

\mathscr{F}_{NN}(u)\sim\sum_{g=0}^{M}\frac{\mathcal{f}_{2g}(u)}{N^{2g}}\,.

In §
6

\mathcal{f}_{2g}(u)
are analytic functions of
u
in a neighborhood of the origin independent of

\frac{1}{12}
.

As mentioned before, (
1.32
) is referred to as the topological expansion of the partition function. Roughly, the quest for models of quantum gravity led to the 2-dimensional reduction in which large but finite collections of different geometries on Riemann surfaces are considered, and one seeks a natural probability measure on these geometries. F. David
[
Dav85
]
and V. Kazakov
[
Kaz85
]
first introduced such random surfaces discretized using polygons to define models of two-dimensional quantum gravity, making use of the connection between graphical enumeration and integrals over large matrices discovered by t’Hooft
[
tH74
]
.
To understand the probability measure, one needs to know how many of these geometries there are, and the problem in enumerative geometry that emerges is to count the number of graphs that can be embedded into a Riemann surface, according to the genus of the surface and the number of vertices of different valences. As discovered in the subsequent works
[
BIPZ78
]
,

[
Bes79
]
and
[
BIZ80
]
, the topological expansion above should be an expansion of generating functions, in which
\mathcal{f}_{2g}(u)
is a combinatorial generating function whose
j
-th coefficient yields the number of labelled graphs with
j
vertices of valence 4, that can be embedded in a Riemann surface of genus
g
. Yet another connection was discovered by Witten
[
Wit91
]
, to the intersection theory of the moduli space of Riemann surfaces, where intersection numbers can also be computed using matrix integrals.

Since the emergence of rigorous mathematical analysis of the partition function by Riemann-Hilbert methods in
[
EM03
]
and in
[
BI05
]
, there have followed works aimed at extracting explicit information about the generating functions and about the important combinatorial coefficients. For example, in
[
EMP08
]
the authors initiated an investigation of the generating functions in the topological expansion in the case that all vertices were of a fixed, even, valence. They made use of both the Toda equations and the string equations, and provided a description of structural properties of the generating functions in terms of inversion of certain differential operators. They extracted some explicit information for enumeration of maps on surfaces of genus
0
,
1
, and
2
, along with recursive definitions for higher genus. (Explicit representation of the generating function means a complete solution of the combinatorial problem for each genus and maps of a fixed valence type.) Later, Ercolani
[
Erc11
,
Erc14
]
continued this research, analyzing a hierarchy of partial differential equations coming from the Toda lattice equations (and the asymptotic expansion of the partition function) and derived semi-explicit characterizations of the
\mathcal{f}_{2g}(u)
as rational functions of other auxiliary functions.

As already mentioned above, for the three-valent case (the cubic matrix model), the Riemann-Hilbert analysis and topological expansion were established in
[
BD12
]
and
[
BDY17
]
, where in particular the authors explicitly evaluated the combinatorial coefficients explicitly for genus
0
and
1
. Characterization results for the generating functions for other odd valences have recently been obtained by Ercolani and Waters
[
EW21
]
.

An interesting difference in approach between the present work and the works
[
Erc11
,
Erc14
,
EMP08
,
EW21
]
is the following: we exploit the string equation and a (possibly new) explicit equation for the first derivative of the free energy (
6.2
) to obtain recursive relations, whereas in the works of Ercolani and collaborators, they use the string equation and a hierarchy of partial differential equations derived from the Toda lattice system of ordinary differential equations. At present our equation for the first derivative of the free energy is only known for the quartic model, while the analysis of Ercolani and collaborators works for more general single valence settings.

In §
6
we establish a number of results concerning these generating functions. We provide recursive relations in
g
, as well as explicit representations for
g=0,1,2,
and
3
. The representations (
6.140
), (
6.141
), and (
6.142
) respectively for

agree with the classical paper of Bessis, Itzykson, and Zuber

, while we believe the result for

is new. As with other representations, the recursive algorithm does yield explicit representations for any genus, but requires more effort as the genus increases. To that end, let us highlight the following result regarding enumeration of graphs.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\mathscr{N}_{j}(g)
be the number of connected labeled 4-valent graphs with
j
vertices which are realizable on a closed Riemann surface of genus
g
, but not realizable on Riemann surfaces of lower genus. For the Riemann surface of genus three we have

(1.33)

\begin{split}\mathscr{N}_{j+4}(3)&=\frac{16\cdot 48^{j}\left(j+3\right)!}{3(j)!}\times\\
&\left(\frac{2741}{10}(j+5)!-\frac{291}{10}j(j+4)!-\frac{2741}{1260}\frac{(2j+9)!}{4^{j}(j+4)!}-\frac{292j(2j+7)!}{315\cdot 4^{j}(j+3)!}\right),\quad j\in{\mathbb{N}},\end{split}

where
\mathscr{N}_{1}(3)=\mathscr{N}_{2}(3)=\mathscr{N}_{3}(3)=\mathscr{N}_{4}(3)=0

or

We also highlight a result that describes the asymptotic behavior of the number of four-valent graphs on a Riemann surface of arbitrary genus

Theorem 1.7

The asymptotics of the number of connected labeled
4
-valent graphs on a Riemann surface of genus

\mathscr{N}_{j}(g)=\mathcal{K}_{g}48^{j}j!j^{\frac{5g-7}{2}}\left(1+O(j^{-1/2})\right),\qquad j\to\infty,

\mathcal{K}_{g}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{12^{\frac{5g-1}{2}}}{\left(\frac{5g-5}{2}\right)!}\frac{1}{5g-3}\mathcal{C}_{2g},&g=2k+1,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{12^{\frac{5g-1}{2}}2^{5g-4}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\left(\frac{5g-4}{2}\right)!}{\left(5g-3\right)!}\mathcal{C}_{2g},&g=2k,\end{cases}\qquad k\in{\mathbb{N}},

while

Remark 1.8

Remark 1.10

A very interesting direction of research is to explore the precise connection between the asymptotics of the labeled graphs embedded on a Riemann surface of genus

2.
Equilibrium Measure

In this section we first discuss the equilibrium measure for a general complex polynomial
V(z)
, and then we will specify it to
the equilibrium measure of the quartic complex polynomial
(
1.15
).

2.1.
Equilibrium Measure for a General Complex Polynomial.

Let

(2.1)

V(z)=\frac{z^{2p}}{2p}+\sum_{j=1}^{2p-1}\frac{v_{j}z^{j}}{j}

be a polynomial of even degree
2p
with the leading coefficient
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2p}
and complex coefficients

\displaystyle\frac{v_{j}}{j}\,,\;j=1,\ldots,2p-1
.
We follow the work of Kuijlaars and Silva
[
KS15
]
, also
see the works
[
Rak12
]
,
[
MFR11
]
,
[
Ber11
]
.

For a given
\varepsilon
, such that

\frac{\pi}{4p}>\varepsilon>0,

consider the sectors

(2.2)

\displaystyle S^{+}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;\Big|\;|\arg z|\leq\frac{\pi}{4p}-\varepsilon\right\},

\displaystyle S^{-}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\;\Big|\;|\arg z-\pi|\leq\frac{\pi}{4p}-\varepsilon\right\}.

Observe that in these sectors,

(2.3)

\lim_{z\to\infty}\Re V(z)=\infty.

Let us define a class
\mathcal{T}
of
admissible contours
on the complex plane. By a contour we mean a
continuous curve

z=z(t)
,
-\infty<t<\infty
, without self-intersections. We say that a contour
\Gamma
is admissible if

(1)

The contour
\Gamma
is a finite union of
C^{1}
Jordan arcs.

(2)

There exists
\varepsilon>0
and
r_{0}>0
, such that
\Gamma
goes from
S^{-}_{\varepsilon}
to
S^{+}_{\varepsilon}
in the sense that
\forall\,r>r_{0},

\exists\,t_{0}<t_{1}
such that

z(t)\in S^{-}_{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{r}\quad\forall\,t<t_{0};\quad z(t)\in S^{+}_{\varepsilon}\setminus D_{r}\quad\forall\,t>t_{1},

where
D_{r}
is the disk centered at the origin with radius
r
. We will assume that the contour
\Gamma
is oriented from
(-\infty)

to
(+\infty)
, where
(-\infty)
lies in the sector
S^{-}_{\varepsilon}
and

(+\infty)
in the sector
S^{+}_{\varepsilon}
. The orientation defines an order
on the contour
\Gamma
.

An example of an admissible contour is the real line.

Let
\Gamma\in\mathcal{T}
be an admissible contour and
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
the space of probability measures
\nu
on
\Gamma
such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\underset{\Gamma}{\int}

\displaystyle|\Re V(s)|\,\textrm{d}\nu(s)<\infty.

Consider the following real-valued functional on
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
:

(2.5)

I_{V,\Gamma}(\nu):=\underset{\Gamma\,\times\,\Gamma}{\iint}\log\frac{1}{|z-s|}\,\textrm{d}\nu(z)\textrm{d}\nu(s)+\int_{\Gamma}\Re V(s)\,\textrm{d}\nu(s).

Then there exists a unique minimizer
\nu_{V,\Gamma}
of the functional

I_{V,\Gamma}(\nu)
, so that

(2.6)

\min_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)}I_{V,\Gamma}(\nu)=I_{V,\Gamma}(\nu_{V,\Gamma}).

See the work
[
ST97
]
.

The probability measure
\nu_{V,\Gamma}
is called the

equilibrium measure
of the functional
I_{V,\Gamma}(\nu)
.
The support of
\nu_{V,\Gamma}
is a compact set
J_{V,\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
.
An important fact is that the equilibrium measure is uniquely determined by the
Euler–Lagrange variational conditions
. Namely,

\nu_{V,\Gamma}
is the unique probability measure
\nu
on
\Gamma
such that
there exists a constant
l
, a Lagrange multiplier, such that

(2.7)

\displaystyle U^{\nu}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\,\Re V(z)=\ell,\quad z\in\textrm{supp}\,\nu,

\displaystyle U^{\nu}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\,\Re V(z)\geq\ell,\quad z\in\Gamma\setminus\textrm{supp}\,\nu,

where

(2.8)

U^{\nu}(z)=\int_{\Gamma}\log\frac{1}{|z-s|}\,\textrm{d}\nu(s)

is the
logarithmic potential
of the measure
\nu

[
ST97
]
.

Now we maximize
I_{V}(\nu_{V,\Gamma})
over
\Gamma\in\mathcal{T}
. The main result of the work of Kuijlaars and Silva concerns the existence and properties of the maximizing contour
\Gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{T}
.
They prove that the maximizing contour
\Gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{T}
exists, and
the equilibrium measure

\nu_{\rm eq}=\nu_{V,\Gamma_{0}}

on
\Gamma_{0}
is supported by a set
J\subset\Gamma_{0}
which is a finite union
of
analytic arcs

\Gamma_{0}[a_{k},b_{k}]\subset\Gamma_{0},\;k=1,\ldots,q
,

J=\bigcup_{k=1}^{q}\Gamma_{0}[a_{k},b_{k}],\quad a_{1}<b_{1}\leq a_{2}<b_{2}\leq\ldots\leq a_{q}<b_{q},

that are
critical trajectories

of a quadratic differential
Q(z)\,\textrm{d}z^{2}
(see the beginning of §
3
for a review of definitions and basic facts about quadratic differentials), where
Q(z)
is a polynomial of degree

(2.9)

\deg Q(z)=2\deg V(z)-2=4p-2.

Furthermore, Kuijlaars and Silva prove that the polynomial
Q(z)
is equal to

(2.10)

Q(z)=\left(-\omega(z)+\frac{V^{\prime}(z)}{2}\right)^{2},

where

(2.11)

\omega(z)=\int_{J}\frac{\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(s)}{z-s}

is the resolvent of the measure
\nu_{0}
. Expanding

\frac{1}{z-s}=\frac{1}{z}+\frac{s}{z^{2}}+\frac{s^{2}}{z^{3}}+\ldots,

we obtain that as
z\to\infty
,

(2.12)

\omega(z)=\frac{1}{z}+\frac{m_{1}}{z^{2}}+\ldots,\quad m_{k}=\int_{J}s^{k}\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(s).

In addition, the equilibrium measure
\nu_{\rm eq}
is absolutely continuous
with respect to the arc length and

(2.13)

\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(s)=\frac{1}{\pi\textrm{i}}\,Q_{+}(s)^{1/2}\textrm{d}s,

where
Q_{+}(s)^{1/2}
is the limiting value of the function

(2.14)

Q(z)^{1/2}=-\int_{J}\frac{\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(s)}{z-s}+\frac{V^{\prime}(z)}{2},

as
z\to s\in J
from the left-hand side of
J
with respect to the orientation of the contour
\Gamma_{0}
from
(-\infty)
to
\infty
.
Observe that as
z\to\infty
,

(2.15)

Q(z)^{1/2}=-\left(\frac{1}{z}+\frac{m_{1}}{z^{2}}+\ldots\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(z^{2p-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{2p-1}v_{j}z^{j-1}\right).

A very important result of Kuijlaars and Silva is that
the equilibrium measure
\nu_{\rm eq}
is
unique as the max-min measure
. On the other hand, the contour
\Gamma_{0}
is not unique because it can be deformed outside of the support
J
of
\nu_{\rm eq}
.

2.2.
The
g
-function.

We define the
g
-function as

(2.16)

g(z)=\int_{J}\log(z-s)\,\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(s),

where for a fixed
s\in J
, we consider a cut of
\log(z-s)
on the part of the curve

\Gamma_{0}
where
z<s
with respect to the ordering on
\Gamma_{0}
. Observe that by (
2.11
),

(2.17)

g^{\prime}(z)=\int_{J}\frac{\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(s)}{z-s}=\omega(z),

In addition, by (
2.8
), the logarithmic potential
U^{\nu_{\rm eq}}(z)
is equal to

(2.18)

U^{\nu_{\rm eq}}(z)=\int_{J}\log\frac{1}{|z-s|}\,\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(s)=-\Re g(z)

hence the Euler–Lagrange variational conditions (
2.7
) can be written as

(2.19)

\displaystyle-\Re g(z)+\frac{1}{2}\,\Re V(z)=\ell,\quad z\in J,

\displaystyle-\Re g(z)+\frac{1}{2}\,\Re V(z)\geq\ell,\quad z\in\Gamma_{0}\setminus J.

2.3.
Regular and Singular Equilibrium Measures

An equilibrium measure
\nu_{\rm eq}
is called
regular
if the following three conditions hold:

(1)

The arcs
\Gamma_{0}[a_{k},b_{k}],\;k=1,\ldots,q,
of the support of
\nu_{\rm eq}
are disjoint.

(2)

The end-points
\{a_{k},b_{k},\;k=1,\ldots,q\}
are simple zeros of the polynomial
Q(s)
.

(3)

There is a contour
\Gamma_{0}
containing the support
J
of
\nu_{\rm eq}

such that

(2.20)

U^{\nu}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\,\Re V(z)>\ell,\quad z\in\Gamma_{0}\setminus J.

An equilibrium measure
\nu_{\rm eq}
is called
singular
(or

critical
) if it is not regular.

2.3.1.
Regular Equilibrium Measures

Suppose that
an equilibrium measure
\nu_{\rm eq}
is regular.
Since the resolvent

(2.21)

\omega(z)=\int_{J}\frac{\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(s)}{z-s}

is analytic on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus J
,
it follows from equation (
2.10
) that if the equilibrium measure
\nu_{0}
is regular then all the zeros of the
polynomial
Q(z)
different from the end-points
\{a_{k},b_{k},\;k=1,\ldots,q\}
are of even degree, hence

Q(z)
can be written as

(2.22)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{4}\,h(z)^{2}R(z),

where
h(z)
is a polynomial,

(2.23)

h(z)=\prod_{j=0}^{r}(z-z_{j}),

with zeroes
z_{0},\ldots,z_{r}
different from the end-points
\{a_{k},b_{k},\;k=1,\ldots,q\}
,
and

(2.24)

R(z)=\prod_{k=1}^{q}(z-a_{k})(z-b_{k}).

Thus,

(2.25)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{4}\,h(z)^{2}R(z)=\frac{1}{4}\prod_{j=0}^{r}(z-z_{j})^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{q}(z-a_{k})(z-b_{k}).

By taking the square root with the plus sign, we obtain that

(2.26)

Q(z)^{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\,h(z)R(z)^{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{j=0}^{r}(z-z_{j})\left[\prod_{k=1}^{q}(z-a_{k})(z-b_{k})\right]^{1/2},

Correspondingly, equation (
2.13
) can be rewritten as

(2.27)

\displaystyle\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\textrm{i}}\,h(z)R_{+}(z)^{1/2}\textrm{d}z=\frac{1}{2\pi\textrm{i}}\,\prod_{j=0}^{r}(z-z_{j})\left[\prod_{k=1}^{q}(z-a_{k})(z-b_{k})\right]_{+}^{1/2}\textrm{d}z.

Now we will apply the above results for the equilibrium measure of a general complex polynomial
V(z)
to the quartic polynomial

V(z)=V(z;\sigma)=\frac{z^{4}}{4}+\frac{\sigma z^{2}}{2}\,,\quad\sigma\in{\mathbb{C}}.

2.4.
Equilibrium Measures for the Quartic Polynomials

V(z;\sigma)

For the quartic polynomial in hand, equation (
2.10
) for the
polynomial
Q(z)
reads

(2.28)

Q(z)=\left(-\omega(z)+\frac{z^{3}+\sigma z}{2}\right)^{2},\quad\omega(z)=\int_{J}\frac{\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(s)}{z-s}\,.

Since the polynomial
V(z)
is even, the uniqueness of the
equilibrium measure
\nu_{\rm eq}
implies that

(1)

\nu_{\rm eq}
is even,
\nu_{\rm eq}(-s)=\nu_{\rm eq}(s)
.

(2)

The resolvent
\omega(z)
is odd,
\omega(-z)=-\omega(z)
, and

(3)

The polynomial
Q(z)
is even,
Q(-z)=Q(z)
.

Considering
z\to\infty
, we obtain that

(2.29)

Q(z)=\left(\frac{z^{3}+\sigma z}{2}-\frac{1}{z}-\frac{m_{2}}{z^{3}}-\ldots\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\big[z^{6}+2\sigma z^{4}+(\sigma^{2}-4)z^{2}-4(\sigma+m_{2})\big].

Since
Q(z)
is a polynomial of degree 6, the possible number of cuts

q
in formula (
2.25
) can be
q=1,2,
and
3
. Let us consider them in more detail.

2.4.1.
One-Cut Equilibrium Measure

When
q=1
, formula (
2.25
) gives that

(2.30)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{4}\,(z-z_{0})^{2}(z-z_{1})^{2}(z-a_{1})(z-b_{1}).

Since the equilibrium measure
\nu_{0}
is even and the polynomial
Q(z)
is even, we have that

(2.31)

-a_{1}=b_{1},\quad-z_{1}=z_{0},

hence

(2.32)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{4}\left(z^{2}-z_{0}^{2}\right)^{2}(z^{2}-b_{1}^{2}).

Equating this expression to the one (
2.29
), we obtain that

(2.33)

(z^{2}-c^{2})^{2}(z^{2}-b^{2})=z^{6}+2\sigma z^{4}+(\sigma^{2}-4)z^{2}-4(\sigma+m_{2}).

Comparing the coefficients at
z^{4}
and
z^{2}
, we obtain the system of equations,

(2.34)

\left\{\;\begin{aligned} &b_{1}^{2}+2z_{0}^{2}=-2\sigma,\\
&2b_{1}^{2}z_{0}^{2}+z_{0}^{4}=\sigma^{2}-4.\end{aligned}\right.

From the first equation we have that

\sigma=-\frac{b_{1}^{2}}{2}-z_{0}^{2}.

Substituting this expression into the second equation and simplifying we obtain that

b_{1}^{2}(b_{1}^{2}-4z_{0}^{2})=16.

Thus, we have the system of equations,

(2.35)

\left\{\;\begin{aligned} &b_{1}^{2}+2z_{0}^{2}=-2\sigma,\\
&b_{1}^{2}(b_{1}^{2}-4z_{0}^{2})=16.\end{aligned}\right.

Solving it we obtain that

(2.36)

b_{1}^{2}=\frac{2}{3}\left(-\sigma\pm\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad z_{0}^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\left(-2\sigma\mp\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\right).

As shown by Bleher and Its (see
[
BI99
,
BI03
,
BI05
]
), for real
\sigma>-2
, the one-cut equilibrium measure persists with a real
b_{1}>0
. This determines the sign
in the latter formulae,

(2.37)

b_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\left(-\sigma+\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\right)}\quad\text{and}\quad z_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}\left(-2\sigma-\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\right)}.

Observe that for
\sigma=-2
, we have
b_{1}=2
and
z_{0}=0
. Theorem
1.1
tells us that the point
\sigma=-2
corresponds to a split of the cut
[-2,2]
at
z_{0}=0
, when
\sigma
is decreasing from
\sigma>-2
to
\sigma<-2
. As shown in
[
BI03
]
, the critical behavior
of the quartic model at
\sigma=-2
is governed by
the Hastings-McLeod solution to the second Painlevé equation PII.
In what follows we will show that formulae (
2.37
) are analytically extended from the real half-line
\sigma>-2
to the whole one-cut region
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{1}
on the complex plane.

Remark 2.1
.

Notice that the branch cuts for
z_{0}(\sigma)
and
b_{1}(\sigma)
are different. Indeed for
b_{1}(\sigma)
, since
-\sigma+\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\neq 0
for all
\sigma
, there are only two branch cuts
L_{\pm}
emanating from
\pm\textrm{i}\sqrt{12}
. However when we consider
z_{0}(\sigma)
, we notice that
-2\sigma-\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}
does vanish for
\sigma=-2
. So, for
z_{0}(\sigma)
, apart from the two branch cuts emanating from
\pm\textrm{i}\sqrt{12}
(which we chose to be
L_{\pm}
: the same as the ones for
b_{1}(\sigma)
) there is one more branch cut
L
which emanates from
-2
. In this work we choose
L_{\pm}=\pm\textrm{i}\sqrt{12}-t
and
L=-2-t
,
t>0
. We choose the branches so that for
\sigma>-2
:

z_{0}(\sigma)=\textrm{i}y_{0}\quad\mbox{with}\quad y_{0}>0,\qquad\quad\text{and}\quad\qquad b_{1}(\sigma)>0.

Since
z^{2}_{0}(\sigma)\in(0,\infty)
for
\sigma\in(-\infty,-2)
, the branch cut in the
z_{0}^{2}
-plane is the positive real axis and we fix the branch of
z_{0}
by fixing
0\leq\arg(z^{2}_{0})<2\pi
.

In the one-cut regime the
g
function can be explicitly computed. To this end, using (
2.10
), (
2.17
), and (
2.32
) we can write

(2.38)

g(z;\sigma)=\frac{V(z)+\ell^{(1)}_{*}}{2}+\frac{\eta_{1}(z;\sigma)}{2},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,b_{1}],

where

(2.39)

\eta_{1}(z;\sigma):=-\int^{z}_{b_{1}}(s^{2}-z^{2}_{0})\sqrt{s^{2}-b_{1}^{2}}\textrm{d}s,\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,b_{1}],

in which the path of integration does not cross
\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,b_{1}(\sigma)]
. Notice that

(2.40)

\eta_{1,+}(z)=-\eta_{1,-}(z),\qquad z\in J_{\sigma},

and

(2.41)

\eta_{1,+}(z)-\eta_{1,-}(z)=4\pi\textrm{i},\qquad z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,-b_{1}).

Using several integration by parts and trigonometric substitutions we find

(2.42)

\eta_{1}(z;\sigma)=\frac{z}{8}(b_{1}^{2}+4z^{2}_{0}-2z^{2})\sqrt{z^{2}-b_{1}^{2}}+2\log\left(\frac{z+\sqrt{z^{2}-b_{1}^{2}}}{b_{1}}\right),

where we have used (
2.35
) in simplifying the expression. Therefore we have the following explicit form of the
g
-function in the one-cut regime

(2.43)

g(z;\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma z^{2}}{2}+\frac{z^{4}}{4}+\ell^{(1)}_{*}\right)-\frac{z}{16}(b_{1}^{2}+4\sigma+2z^{2})\sqrt{z^{2}-b_{1}^{2}}+\log\left(\frac{z+\sqrt{z^{2}-b_{1}^{2}}}{b_{1}}\right),

where we have also used (
2.35
). Here the branches must be chosen to ensure that the branch cut for
g
is
\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,b_{1}]
. Also the constant
\ell_{*}^{(1)}
can be found using the requirement that

g(z;\sigma)=\log z+\mathcal{O}(z^{-1})

as
z\to\infty
. Indeed,

(2.44)

\ell^{(1)}_{*}=\frac{1}{12}\left(\sigma^{2}-\sigma\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\right)+\log\left(-\sigma+\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}-\log 6.

In what follows we use the notations

(2.45)

\mathscr{G}^{(j)}_{1}(z;\sigma):=g_{+}(z;\sigma)-g_{-}(z;\sigma),

and

(2.46)

\mathscr{G}^{(j)}_{2}(z;\sigma):=g_{+}(z;\sigma)+g_{-}(z;\sigma)-V(z;\sigma)-\ell^{(j)}_{*},

for
j=1,2
, and
3
, respectively associated with the one-cut, two-cut, and three-cut regimes. We have

(2.47)

\mathscr{G}^{(1)}_{1}(z;\sigma)=\begin{cases}0,&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}(b_{1},\infty),\\
\eta_{1,+}(z;\sigma),&z\in J_{\sigma},\\
2\pi\textrm{i},&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,-b_{1}),\end{cases}

(2.48)

\mathscr{G}^{(1)}_{2}(z;\sigma)=\begin{cases}\eta_{1}(z;\sigma),&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}(b_{1},\infty)\\
0,&z\in J_{\sigma},\\
\eta_{1,\pm}(z;\sigma)\mp 2\pi\textrm{i},&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,-b_{1}).\end{cases}

Note that for
z\in J_{\sigma}
, in particular we have

(2.49)

\Re(g_{+}(z;\sigma))+\Re(g_{-}(z;\sigma))-\Re V(z;\sigma)-\Re(\ell^{(1)}_{*})=0.

Also, since
\rho_{V}(s;\sigma)\textrm{d}s
is a probability measure and thus real-valued on
J_{\sigma}
, we have

(2.50)

\Re(g_{+}(z;\sigma))+\Re(g_{-}(z;\sigma))=2\int_{J_{\sigma}}\log|z-s|\textrm{d}\nu_{\rm eq}(s).

Comparing the last two equations with (
2.8
) and the first member of (
2.7
) implies that the Euler-Lagrange constant

is given by

(2.51)

\ell\equiv\ell^{(1)}=-\frac{\Re\ell^{(1)}_{*}}{2},

where
\ell_{*}^{(1)}
is explicitly given by (

).

2.4.2.
Two-Cut Equilibrium Measure

Consider now a regular equilibrium measure
with two cuts,

(2.52)

J=\Gamma[a_{1},b_{1}]\cup\Gamma[a_{2},b_{2}].

When
q=2
, formula (
2.25
) gives that

(2.53)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{4}\,(z-c)^{2}(z-a_{1})(z-b_{1})(z-a_{2})(z-b_{2}).

Since the polynomial
Q(z)
is even, we have that
c=0
and, in general, we have the two cases for the end-points

a_{1}<b_{1}<a_{2}<b_{2}
:

(1)

Either

(2.54)

-a_{1}=b_{2},\quad-b_{1}=a_{2},

or

(2)

(2.55)

-a_{1}=b_{1},\quad-a_{2}=b_{2},

but we will see that the latter case is impossible, hence
Q(z)

has the form

(2.56)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{4}\,z^{2}(z^{2}-a_{2}^{2})(z^{2}-b_{2}^{2}).

Matching this expression to (
2.29
), we obtain that

(2.57)

z^{2}(z^{2}-a_{2}^{2})(z^{2}-b_{2}^{2})=z^{6}+2\sigma z^{4}+(\sigma^{2}-4)z^{2}-4(\sigma+m_{2}),

and equating the coefficients at
z^{4}
and
z^{2}
on the left and right, we obtain the system of equations,

(2.58)

a_{2}^{2}+b_{2}^{2}+2\sigma=0,\quad(a_{2}^{2}-b_{2}^{2})^{2}=16.

Solving it, we obtain that

(2.59)

a_{2}^{2}=\mp 2-\sigma,\quad b_{2}^{2}=\pm 2-\sigma.

For any real
\sigma<-2
we have that

(2.60)

a_{2}=\sqrt{-2-\sigma},\quad b_{2}=\sqrt{2-\sigma}

(see
[
BI99
]
). We will see below that the latter equations hold in the whole two-cut region
\sigma\in\mathcal{O}_{2}
on the complex plane. Similar to the one-cut regime, in the two-cut regime the
g
function can also be explicitly computed. Using (
2.10
), (
2.17
), and (
2.56
) we can write

(2.61)

g(z;\sigma)=\frac{V(z)+\ell^{(2)}_{*}}{2}+\frac{\eta_{2}(z;\sigma)}{2},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,b_{2}],

where

(2.62)

\eta_{2}(z;\sigma):=-\int^{z}_{b_{2}}s\sqrt{(s^{2}-b_{2}^{2})(s^{2}-a_{2}^{2})}\textrm{d}s,

in which the path of integration does not cross
\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,b_{2}]
. The latter integral can be evaluated explicitly

(2.63)

\begin{split}\eta_{2}(z;\sigma)&=-\frac{1}{4}(z^{2}-\frac{b_{2}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}}{2})\sqrt{(z^{2}-b_{2}^{2})(z^{2}-a_{2}^{2})}\\
&+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{b_{2}^{2}-a_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}\log\left[\frac{2z^{2}-b_{2}^{2}-a_{2}^{2}+2\sqrt{(z^{2}-b_{2}^{2})(z^{2}-a_{2}^{2})}}{b_{2}^{2}-a_{2}^{2}}\right].\end{split}

In view of (
2.60
) this can be simplified as

(2.64)

\begin{split}\eta_{2}(z;\sigma)&=-\frac{1}{4}(z^{2}+\sigma)\sqrt{(z^{2}+\sigma-2)(z^{2}+\sigma+2)}\\
&+\log\left[\frac{z^{2}+\sigma+\sqrt{(z^{2}+\sigma-2)(z^{2}+\sigma+2)}}{2}\right].\end{split}

So we have the following explicit form of the
g
-function in the two-cut regime

(2.65)

\begin{split}g(z;\sigma)&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma z^{2}}{2}+\frac{z^{4}}{4}+\ell^{(2)}_{*}\right)-\frac{1}{8}(z^{2}+\sigma)\sqrt{(z^{2}+\sigma-2)(z^{2}+\sigma+2)}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\log\left[\frac{z^{2}+\sigma+\sqrt{(z^{2}+\sigma-2)(z^{2}+\sigma+2)}}{2}\right].\end{split}

The constant
\ell_{*}^{(2)}
can be found using the requirement that

g(z;\sigma)=\log z+\mathcal{O}(z^{-1})

as
z\to\infty
. In this way we obtain that

(2.66)

\ell^{(2)}_{*}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{2}.

Recalling (
2.45
) and (
2.46
) and straightforward calculations we have

(2.67)

\mathscr{G}^{(2)}_{1}(z;\sigma)=\begin{cases}0,&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[b_{2},\infty),\\
\eta_{2,+}(z;\sigma),&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[a_{2},b_{2}],\\
\pi\textrm{i},&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[-a_{2},a_{2}],\\
\eta_{2,+}(z;\sigma),&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[-b_{2},-a_{2}],\\
2\pi\textrm{i},&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,-b_{2}],\end{cases}

and

(2.68)

\mathscr{G}^{(2)}_{2}(z;\sigma)=\begin{cases}\eta_{2}(z;\sigma),&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[b_{2},\infty),\\
0&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[a_{2},b_{2}],\\
\eta_{2,\pm}(z;\sigma)\mp\pi\textrm{i},&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[-a_{2},a_{2}],\\
0,&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[-b_{2},-a_{2}],\\
\eta_{2,\pm}(z;\sigma)\mp 2\pi\textrm{i},&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,-b_{2}],\\
\end{cases}

where we have used the fact that

\int_{-a_{2}}^{a_{2}}s\sqrt{(s^{2}-b_{2}^{2})(s^{2}-a_{2}^{2})}=0.

Notice that on the support we have

(2.69)

g_{+}(z;\sigma)+g_{-}(z;\sigma)-V(z;\sigma)-\ell^{(2)}_{*}=0.

Taking the real part of this equation and comparing with (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) we find that the two-cut Euler-Lagrange constant
\ell
is given by

(2.70)

\ell\equiv\ell^{(2)}=-\frac{\Re\ell^{(2)}_{*}}{2},

where
\ell_{*}^{(2)}
is given by (
2.66
).

2.4.3.
Three-Cut Equilibrium Measure

Consider now a regular equilibrium measure with three cuts, when

(2.71)

J=\Gamma[a_{1},b_{1}]\cup\Gamma[a_{2},b_{2}]\cup\Gamma[a_{3},b_{3}],\qquad a_{1}<b_{1}<a_{2}<b_{2}<a_{3}<b_{3}.

In this case formula (
2.25
) gives that

(2.72)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{4}R(z)=\frac{1}{4}\,(z-a_{1})(z-b_{1})(z-a_{2})(z-b_{2})(z-a_{3})(z-b_{3}).

The evenness of
Q(z)
implies that

(2.73)

-a_{1}=b_{3}\equiv c_{3},\quad-b_{1}=a_{3}\equiv b_{3},\quad-a_{2}=b_{2}\equiv a_{3},

hence

(2.74)

Q(z)=\frac{1}{4}\,(z^{2}-a_{3}^{2})(z^{2}-b_{3}^{2})(z^{2}-c_{3}^{2}).

Matching this equation to (
2.29
), we obtain that

(2.75)

(z^{2}-b_{2}^{2})(z^{2}-a_{3}^{2})(z^{2}-b_{3}^{2})=z^{6}+2\sigma z^{4}+(\sigma^{2}-4)z^{2}-4(\sigma+m_{2}),

and equating the coefficients at
z^{4}
and
z^{2}
, we obtain
the system of two algebraic equations with three unknowns,

(2.76)

\displaystyle a_{3}^{2}+b_{3}^{2}+c_{3}^{2}+2\sigma=0,

\displaystyle a_{3}^{4}+b_{3}^{4}+c_{3}^{4}-2a_{3}^{2}b_{3}^{2}-2b_{3}^{2}c_{3}^{2}-2a_{3}^{2}c_{3}^{2}=16.

The above equations provide four real conditions to determine the six real unknowns
\Re a_{3},\Im a_{3},\Re b_{3},\Im b_{3},\Re c_{3},\Im c_{3}
. Below we justify that the remaining two real conditions for determining the end points are given by

(2.77)

\Re\left(\displaystyle\int^{b_{3}}_{a_{3}}\sqrt{R(s)}\textrm{d}s\right)=0,

(2.78)

\Re\left(\displaystyle\int^{c_{3}}_{b_{3}}\left(\sqrt{R(s)}\right)_{+}\textrm{d}s\right)=0.

To that end, we use (
2.10
), (
2.17
), and (
2.74
) to write the
g
-function as

(2.79)

g(z;\sigma)=\frac{V(z)+\ell^{(3)}_{*}}{2}+\frac{\eta_{3}(z;\sigma)}{2},\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,c_{3}],

where

(2.80)

\eta_{3}(z;\sigma):=-\int^{z}_{c_{3}}\sqrt{R(s)}\textrm{d}s=-\int^{z}_{c_{3}}\sqrt{(s^{2}-a_{3}^{2})(s^{2}-b_{3}^{2})(s^{2}-c_{3}^{2})}\textrm{d}s,

in which the path of integration does not cross
\Gamma_{\sigma}(-\infty,c_{3}]
. On the support we have

(2.81)

g_{+}(z;\sigma)+g_{-}(z;\sigma)-V(z)-\ell^{(3)}_{*}=\begin{cases}0,&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[b_{3},c_{3}]\\
\displaystyle\int^{b_{3}}_{a_{3}}\sqrt{R(s)}\textrm{d}s,&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[-a_{3},a_{3}]\\
\displaystyle\int^{b_{3}}_{a_{3}}\sqrt{R(s)}\textrm{d}s+\displaystyle\int^{-a_{3}}_{-b_{3}}\sqrt{R(s)}\textrm{d}s,&z\in\Gamma_{\sigma}[-c_{3},-b_{3}]\\
\end{cases}

Taking the real part of this equation and comparing with (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) yields

(2.82)

\ell\equiv\ell^{(3)}=-\frac{\Re\ell^{(3)}_{*}}{2},

(2.83)

\Re\left(\displaystyle\int^{b_{3}}_{a_{3}}\sqrt{R(s)}\textrm{d}s\right)=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\Re\left(\displaystyle\int^{-a_{3}}_{-b_{3}}\sqrt{R(s)}\textrm{d}s\right)=0.

Equations in (
2.83
) are the three-cut
gap conditions
. Note that, due to the symmetry of
R
, if one of the above gap conditions hold, the other one holds automatically as well, so the requirement (
2.77
) is justified. Since the equilibrium measure (
2.13
) is positive along the support, we have an immediate justification of the requirement (
2.78
).

3.
Critical graphs in the
z
-plane

This section is devoted to characterization of the boundaries between the one-cut, two-cut and the three-cut regimes in the
\sigma
-plane using the theory of quadratic differentials.

Here we briefly recall some definitions and basic facts about quadratic differentials from
[
Str84
]
. The critical points of a quadratic differential
Q(z)\textrm{d}z^{2}
are the zeroes and poles of
Q(z)
, while all other points are called regular points of
Q(z)\textrm{d}z^{2}
. For any fixed
0\leq\theta<2\pi
the
\theta
-arc of a quadratic differential
Q(z)\textrm{d}z^{2}
is defined as the smooth curve
L_{\theta}
along which

(3.1)

\arg Q(z)\textrm{d}z^{2}=\theta,

and thus a
\theta
-arc can only contain regular points of
Q
, because at the singular points the argument is not defined. Through each regular point of a meromorphic quadratic differential passes exactly one
\theta
-arc. A maximal
\theta
-arc is called a
\theta
-trajectory. We will refer to a
\pi
-trajectory ( resp.
0
-trajectory) which is incident with a critical point as a
critical trajectory
(resp.
critical orthogonal trajectory
). If
b
is a critical point of
Q(z)\textrm{d}z^{2}
, then the totality of the solutions to

(3.2)

\Re\left(\int^{z}_{b}\sqrt{Q(s)}\textrm{d}s\right)=0,

is referred to as the
critical graph
of
\int^{z}_{b}\sqrt{Q(s)}\textrm{d}s
(see §5 of
[
Str84
]
). A critical (orthogonal) trajectory is called
short
if it is incident only with finite critical points. A simple closed
geodesic polygon
with respect to a meromorphic quadratic differential
Q(z)\textrm{d}z^{2}
(also referred to as a
Q
-polygon) is a Jordan curve
\Sigma
composed of open
\theta
-arcs and their endpoints. The endpoints may be regular or critical points of
Q(z)\textrm{d}z^{2}
, which form the vertices of the
Q
-polygon. By a
loop
we mean a geodesic polygon whose single vertex is a singular point of the associated quadratic differential. If at least one of the end points of
\Sigma
is a singular point, we call it a
singular geodesic polygon
. Let
\Sigma
by a
Q
-polygon, and let
\texttt{V}_{\Sigma}
and
\texttt{Int}\Sigma
denote its set of vertices and interior respectively. The
Teichmüller’s lemma
states that

(3.3)

\#\texttt{V}_{\Sigma}-2=\sum_{z\in\texttt{V}_{\Sigma}}(\texttt{ord}(z)+2)\frac{\theta(z)}{2\pi}+\sum_{z\in\texttt{Int}\Sigma}\texttt{ord}(z),

where
\theta(z)
is the interior angle of
\Sigma
at
z
, and
\texttt{ord}(z)
is the order of the point
z
with respect to the quadratic differential: it is zero for a regular point, it is
n
(
-n
) if
z
is a zero (pole) of order
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
of the quadratic differential.

3.1.
The One-cut Regime

Let us recall the definition of the function
\eta
introduced in §
2.4
:

(3.4)

\eta_{1}(z;\sigma):=-\int^{z}_{b_{1}(\sigma)}\left(s^{2}-z^{2}_{0}(\sigma)\right)\sqrt{s^{2}-b^{2}_{1}(\sigma)}\textrm{d}s.

We sometimes need to choose the starting point of integration to be
\pm z_{0}(\sigma)

Lemma 3.3
.

The set
\mathscr{J}^{(1)}_{\sigma}

Proof.

In view of the first part of Definition

\eta_{1}(-z;\sigma)=\eta_{1}(z;\sigma)\pm 2\pi\textrm{i}.

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

[Theorem 3 of
[
BBG+22
]
]

The critical graph
\mathscr{J}^{(1)}_{\sigma}
deforms continuously with respect to
\sigma
.

Consider the one-cut quadratic differential

Lemma 3.5
.

There are no singular finite geodesic polygons with one or two vertices associated to the quadratic differential (
3.8
).

Proof.

Suppose that such a singular finite
Q_{1}
-polygon exists. For this geodesic polygon, the left hand side of (
3.3
) is either
-1
or zero, while the right hand side of (
3.3
) is certainly a positive integer. This is because such a polygon can not enclose a pole as the quadratic differential (
3.8
) has no finite poles, and because
\texttt{ord}(\pm b_{1})=1

\texttt{ord}(\pm z_{0})=2

\theta(\pm b_{1})\in\{\frac{2\pi}{3},\frac{4\pi}{3}\}

and the more singular points

encloses, the larger the right hand side gets. Therefore (
3.3

∎

Similar to the approach taken in

4.1.
One-cut to Three-cut Transition.

In this subsection we search for an analytic description for the values of
\sigma
such that
z_{0}(\sigma)\in\mathscr{J}^{(1)}_{\sigma}
, that is
\Re[\Psi(\sigma)]=0
where

(4.1)

\begin{split}\Psi(\sigma)&:=\eta_{1}(z_{0}(\sigma);\sigma)=-\frac{\sigma}{4}\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}\left(-2\sigma-\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\right)}\sqrt{-\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}}\\
&+2\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}\left(-2\sigma-\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\right)}+\sqrt{-\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}}}{\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\left(-\sigma+\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\right)}}\right).\end{split}

If we compute

(4.2)

\left[\frac{\textrm{d}\Psi}{\textrm{d}\sigma}\right]^{2}=\frac{1}{12}\left(12+\sigma^{2}+2\sigma\sqrt{12+\sigma^{2}}\right),

we do not obtain a meromorphic quadratic differential, which is the preferred object to deal with (as opposed to what we had in (
4.11

\sigma
and
z_{0}(\sigma)

\Xi(b_{1}):=\left[\frac{\textrm{d}\Psi}{\textrm{d}b_{1}}\right]^{2}=\frac{(16-b_{1}^{4})(16+3b_{1}^{4})^{3}}{256b_{1}^{10}}.

We can make things a bit simpler, as in the variable
\beta:=b_{1}^{2}

(4.4)

\Xi(\beta)=\left[\frac{\textrm{d}\Psi}{\textrm{d}\beta}\right]^{2}=\frac{(16-\beta^{2})(16+3\beta^{2})^{3}}{1024\beta^{6}}.

Thus we can express
\Psi
as

(4.5)

\Psi(\beta)=\int^{\beta}_{-\frac{4\textrm{i}}{\sqrt{3}}}\sqrt{\Xi(s)}\textrm{d}s.

The initial point of integration is chosen to be

(and

\sigma\mapsto\beta

not

4.2.

Due to the symmetry with respect to the origin, the transition from the two-cut regime to the three-cut regime could only occur through birth of a cut at the origin. Define

, and

are respectively given by (

Note that for

In particular, let

For this choice of

Note that the orthogonal polynomials (and hence their norms) with respect to

The Riemann-Hilbert analysis is standard and we do not provide the details here. For one-cut real potentials see e.g.

Proof.

,

6.
Topological Expansion of the Free Energy

Proposition 6.1
.

For
\sigma
in the one-cut regime we have

(6.1)

\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial\sigma^{2}}=\frac{N^{2}}{4n^{2}}R_{n}(R_{n-1}+R_{n+1}),

and

(6.2)

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{N^{2}}{2n^{2}}R_{n}(\frac{n}{N}+R_{n-1}R_{n+1}).

Proof.

By the Heine’s identity for Hankel determinants and noting that
h_{k}=D_{k+1}/D_{k}
, where
D_{k}
is the
k\times k
Hankel determinant generated by the weight
e^{-NV(z;\sigma)}
, we have

(6.3)

Z_{nN}(\sigma)=n!\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}h_{k},

hence

(6.4)

F\equiv F_{nN}(\sigma)=\frac{\ln n!}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\ln h_{k}.

Differentiating equation (
5.1
) with
j=k
, we obtain that

(6.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h_{k}}{\partial\sigma}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N}{2}\int_{\Gamma}z^{2}P_{k}^{2}(z)e^{-NV(z)}\,\textrm{d}z

\displaystyle=-\frac{N}{2}\int_{\Gamma}[P_{k+1}(z)+\gamma_{k}^{2}P_{k-1}(z)]^{2}e^{-NV(z)}\,\textrm{d}z

\displaystyle=-\frac{N}{2}\,\left(h_{k+1}+\gamma_{k}^{4}h_{k-1}\right)=-\frac{Nh_{k}}{2}\,\left(\gamma^{2}_{k+1}+\gamma_{k}^{2}\right),

hence

(6.6)

\frac{\partial\ln h_{k}}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{N}{2}\,\left(\gamma^{2}_{k+1}+\gamma_{k}^{2}\right),

and by (
6.4
),

(6.7)

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{N}{2n^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\gamma^{2}_{k+1}+\gamma_{k}^{2}\right).

It is convenient to introduce also the
\psi
-functions,

(6.8)

\psi_{k}(z):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h_{k}}}P_{k}(z)e^{-NV(z)/2}.

They satisfy the orthogonality conditions,

(6.9)

\int_{\Gamma}\psi_{j}(z)\psi_{k}(z)\,dz=\delta_{jk},

and the recurrence relation,

(6.10)

z\psi_{k}(z)=\gamma_{k+1}\psi_{k+1}(z)+\gamma_{k}\psi_{k-1}(z).

Define the vector function

(6.11)

\vec{\Psi}_{k}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{k}(z)\\
\psi_{k-1}(z)\end{pmatrix},\quad k\geq 1.

Then
\vec{\Psi}_{k}(z)
satisfies the ODE

(6.12)

\vec{\Psi}_{k}^{\prime}(z)=NA_{k}(z)\vec{\Psi}_{k}(z),

where

(6.13)

A_{k}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle-\frac{V^{\prime}(z)}{2}-\gamma_{k}u_{k}(z)&\gamma_{k}v_{k}(z)\\
-\gamma_{k}v_{k-1}(z)&\displaystyle\frac{V^{\prime}(z)}{2}+\gamma_{k}u_{k}(z)\end{pmatrix},

and

(6.14)

u_{k}(z)=[W(\mathcal{Q},z)]_{k,k-1},\quad v_{k}(z)=[W(\mathcal{Q},z)]_{kk},

where

(6.15)

W(\mathcal{Q},z)=\frac{V^{\prime}(\mathcal{Q})-V^{\prime}(z)}{\mathcal{Q}-z}\,.

(see equation (1.5.2) in
[
BL14
]
)
For the quartic polynomial (
1.15
), we obtain from (
6.15
) that

(6.16)

W(\mathcal{Q},z)=\mathcal{Q}^{2}+\mathcal{Q}z+z^{2}+\sigma,

hence

(6.17)

u_{k}(z)=\gamma_{k}z,\quad v_{k}(z)=\gamma_{k}^{2}+\gamma_{k+1}^{2}+z^{2}+\sigma.

Substituting these formulae in (
6.13
), we obtain that

(6.18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\,\psi_{k}^{\prime}(z)=-\left(\frac{z^{3}+\sigma z}{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}z\right)\psi_{k}(z)+\gamma_{k}(\gamma_{k}^{2}+\gamma_{k+1}^{2}+z^{2}+\sigma)\psi_{k-1}(z),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\,\psi_{k-1}^{\prime}(z)=-\gamma_{k}(\gamma_{k-1}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}+z^{2}+\sigma)\psi_{k}(z)+\left(\frac{z^{3}+\sigma z}{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}z\right)\psi_{k-1}(z).

Differentiating (
5.5
) with respect to
\sigma
, and using (
6.6
) yields

(6.19)

\frac{\partial\gamma_{k}}{\partial\sigma}=\frac{N\gamma_{k}}{4}\,(\gamma_{k-1}^{2}-\gamma_{k+1}^{2}),

hence

(6.20)

\frac{\partial\gamma_{k}^{2}}{\partial\sigma}=\frac{N\gamma_{k}^{2}}{2}\,(\gamma_{k-1}^{2}-\gamma_{k+1}^{2}).

Differentiating equation (
6.7
), we obtain that

(6.21)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N^{2}}{2n^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{\partial\gamma_{k+1}^{2}}{\partial\sigma}+\frac{\partial\gamma_{k}^{2}}{\partial\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{N^{2}}{4n^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left[\gamma_{k+1}^{2}(\gamma_{k}^{2}-\gamma_{k+2}^{2})+\gamma_{k}^{2}(\gamma_{k-1}^{2}-\gamma_{k+1}^{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{4n^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(I_{k+1}-I_{k}),

where

(6.22)

I_{k}=\gamma_{k}^{2}(\gamma_{k-1}^{2}+\gamma_{k+1}^{2}),\quad I_{0}=0.

Observe that the last sum in (
6.21
) is telescopic and
I_{0}=0
, hence

(6.23)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial\sigma^{2}}=\frac{N^{2}}{4n^{2}}\,\gamma_{n}^{2}(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2}),

(cf. equation (1.4.21) in
[
BL14
]
) which is exactly (
6.1
) recalling the notation introduced in (
5.16
).

From equation (
1.18
) we have that

(6.24)

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\sigma}=\frac{1}{n^{2}Z}\,\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\sigma}\,,

and from (
1.16

(6.25)

\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{N}{2}\int_{\Gamma}\cdots\int_{\Gamma}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}^{2}\right)\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{-NV(z_{k})}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}z_{n},

hence

(6.26)

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{N}{2n^{2}}\,\big\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}^{2}\big\rangle\,,

where

(6.27)

\displaystyle\big\langle f(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\big\rangle=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\cdots\int_{\Gamma}f(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{-NV(z_{k})}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}z_{n},}{\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\cdots\int_{\Gamma}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{-NV(z_{k})}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}z_{n},}\,.

By the permutation symmetry,

(6.28)

\big\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}z_{k}^{2}\big\rangle=n\big\langle z_{1}^{2}\big\rangle=n\int_{\Gamma}z^{2}p(z)dz,

where

(6.29)

p(z)=\frac{1}{Z}\,\int_{\Gamma}\cdots\int_{\Gamma}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{-NV(z_{k})}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\cdots\mathrm{d}z_{n}\bigg|_{z_{1}=z},

is the one-point density function. Thus, from (
6.26
) we obtain that

(6.30)

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{N}{2n}\,\int_{\Gamma}z^{2}p(z)dz\,.

The one-point density function
p(z)
can be expressed in terms of

as

(6.31)

p(z)=\frac{1}{n}\,\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi_{k}(z)^{2},

where
\psi_{k}(z)
are defined in (

)
(see, e.g., formula (1.2.24) in
[

]
). By the Christoffel-Darboux formula, equation (

(cf. formula (1.3.5) in
[
BL14
]
). By equations (

),

(6.33)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\,\psi_{n}^{\prime}(z)=-\left(\frac{z^{3}+\sigma z}{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}z\right)\psi_{n}(z)+\gamma_{n}(\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2}+z^{2}+\sigma)\psi_{n-1}(z),

Substituting these formulae into (

Substituting this furthermore in (

\displaystyle-\int_{\Gamma}\gamma_{n}(z^{3}+\sigma z+2\gamma_{n}^{2}z)z^{2}\psi_{n-1}(z)\psi_{n}(z)\,dz

Iterating three term recurrence relation (

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n+1}\gamma_{n+2}\psi_{n+2}(z)+(\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2})\psi_{n}(z)+\gamma_{n-1}\gamma_{n}\psi_{n-2}(z),

\displaystyle z^{2}\psi_{n-1}(z)

\displaystyle z^{3}\psi_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n+1}\gamma_{n+2}\gamma_{n+3}\psi_{n+3}(z)+\gamma_{n+1}(\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2}+\gamma_{n+2}^{2})\psi_{n+1}(z)

\displaystyle+\gamma_{n}(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2})\psi_{n-1}(z)+\gamma_{n-2}\gamma_{n-1}\gamma_{n}\psi_{n-3}(z),

hence

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}z^{2}\,\psi_{n}^{2}(z)\,dz=\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2},

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}z^{4}\,\psi_{n}^{2}(z)\,dz=\gamma_{n+1}^{2}\gamma_{n+2}^{2}+(\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2})^{2}+\gamma_{n-1}^{2}\gamma_{n}^{2},

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}z^{5}\psi_{n-1}(z)\psi_{n}(z)\,dz=\gamma_{n}\gamma_{n+1}^{2}(\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2}+\gamma_{n+2}^{2})

\displaystyle+\gamma_{n}(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2})(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2})+\gamma_{n-2}^{2}\gamma_{n-1}^{2}\gamma_{n}\,.

(6.38)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma}\gamma_{n}^{2}(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}+z^{2}+\sigma)z^{2}\psi_{n}^{2}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}^{2}(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}+\sigma)(\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma}\gamma_{n}(z^{3}+\sigma z+2\gamma_{n}^{2}z)z^{2}\psi_{n}(z)\psi_{n-1}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}^{2}\gamma_{n+1}^{2}(\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2}+\gamma_{n+2}^{2})

\displaystyle+\gamma_{n}^{2}(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2})(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2})+\gamma_{n-2}^{2}\gamma_{n-1}^{2}\gamma_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle+(\sigma+2\gamma_{n}^{2})\gamma_{n}^{2}(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2}),

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma}\gamma_{n}^{2}(\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2}+z^{2}+\sigma)z^{2}\psi^{2}_{n-1}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}^{2}(\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2}+\sigma)(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2})

\displaystyle+\gamma_{n}^{2}\big[\gamma_{n}^{2}\gamma_{n+1}^{2}+(\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2})^{2}+\gamma_{n-2}^{2}\gamma_{n-1}^{2}\big],

and by (
6.35
),

(6.39)

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{N^{2}}{2n^{2}}\,(A-B+C).

The expression
(A-B+C)
turns out to be remarkably simple:

(6.40)

A-B+C=\gamma_{n}^{2}\big[\gamma_{n}^{2}(\sigma+\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2})+\gamma_{n-1}^{2}\gamma_{n+1}^{2}\big].

By string equation (

),

(6.41)

\gamma_{n}^{2}(\sigma+\gamma_{n-1}^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}+\gamma_{n+1}^{2})=\frac{n}{N}\,,

hence

and

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{N^{2}}{2n^{2}}\,\gamma_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{n}{N}+\gamma_{n-1}^{2}\gamma_{n+1}^{2}\right).

Recalling the notation introduced in (

).
∎

A straightforward calculation shows that
