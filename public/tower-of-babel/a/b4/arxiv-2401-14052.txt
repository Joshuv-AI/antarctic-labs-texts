Testing Alpha in High Dimensional Linear Factor Pricing Models with Dependent Observations
Huifang Ma

Abstract
In this study, we introduce three distinct testing methods for testing alpha in high dimensional linear factor pricing model that deals with dependent data. The first method is a sum-type test procedure, which exhibits high performance when dealing with dense alternatives. The second method is a max-type test procedure, which is particularly effective for sparse alternatives. For a broader range of alternatives, we suggest a Cauchy combination test procedure. This is predicated on the asymptotic independence of the sum-type and max-type test statistics. Both simulation studies and practical data application demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed methods when handling dependent observations. Keywords: asymptotic independence, Cauchy combination test, high-dimensional linear factor model, time series.



1
Introduction

This study focuses on the detection of alpha in Linear Factor Pricing Models (LFPM), particularly when the quantity of securities significantly outweighs the temporal dimension of the return series. LFPM is a cornerstone in the field of finance. Inspired by the Arbitrage Pricing Theory
(
Ross 1976
)
, LFPM elucidates the relationship between security returns and market factors. LFPM manifests in various forms, such as the renowned single-factor model, namely the Sharpe-Lintner Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)
(
Sharpe 1964
;
Lintner 1965
)
, the Fama-French three-factor model
(
Fama and French 1993
)
, and the Fama-French five-factor model
(
Fama and French 2015
)
. Each factor in these models typically carries substantial economic significance and pricing capability.

When the number of securities
N
is fixed,
Gibbons et al. 1989
introduced an exact multivariate F-test under the assumption of joint normality. This classic GRS test procedure was later expanded by
MacKinlay and
Richardson 1991
,
Zhou 1993
, and
Beaulieu et al. 2007
. In today’s financial markets, the number of securities often exceeds a thousand, which may surpass the length of the considered time period. Therefore, it’s not appropriate to assume a fixed number of securities. To address this, recent advancements have focused on devising tests that accommodate situations where the number of securities
N
exceeds the time periods
T
. For instance,
Pesaran and Yamagata 2012
;
Pesaran and Yamagata 2017
;
Pesaran and Yamagata 2023
introduced a sum-type test statistic that replaces the sample covariance matrix with an identity matrix in the traditional F-test, performing well under dense alternatives.
Lan et al. 2018
proposed a random projection procedure which admits the covariance matrix of the idiosyncratic term to be nonsparse. For sparse alternatives,
Gungor and Luger 2013
and
Feng et al. 2022b
proposed max-type test statistic.
Fan et al. 2015
proposed a power enhancement procedure to strengthen the test power under the sparse alternatives.
Yu et al. 2023
utilized the thresholding covariance estimator of
Fan et al. 2013
to propose a novel Wald-type test and proposed a new Cauchy combination test which combinates the Wald-type test and max-type test.
Xia and Zhang 2023
consider
L
-statistics for handling the sparse alternatives. For heavy-tailed distributions,
Liu et al. 2023
proposed a robust spatial sign-based nonparametric test procedure.
Zhao et al. 2022
extended
Liu et al. 2023
’s results by considering a class of weighted spatial-sign tests and choosing an optimal test INST.

In addition, Some literatures considered some more general LFPMs. For example,
Gagliardini
et al. 2016
and
Ma et al. 2020
established two sum-type testing procedures which allow the existence of time-variation coefficients.
Ma et al. 2023
introduce a maximum-type test that performs well in scenarios where the alternative hypothesis is sparse and an adaptive test by combining the maximum-type test and
Ma et al. 2020
’s sum-type test.
Zhao 2023
also proposed a robust spatial-sign-based nonparametric test procedure for the time-varying factor pricing model. In addressing observed non-traded factors, a substantial body of literature exists that estimates the risk premiums associated with these factors using a two-pass regression methodology. This approach, pioneered by
Fama and MacBeth 1973
, is also featured in recent works such as those by
Pesaran and Smith 2021b
and
Pesaran and Smith 2021a
. When it comes to unobserved factors, numerous methods have been developed to estimate these factors and their corresponding loadings, primarily through principal component analysis. For example,
Giglio et al. 2021
devised a multiple testing procedure that operates on the residuals remaining after the influence of the estimated latent factors and loadings has been removed.

Most existing studies do not take into account the dependence structure among observations, instead requiring the assumption of identical and independent distribution for random errors. Only the works of
Ma et al. 2020
and
Ma et al. 2023
have extended their testing procedures under the assumption of martingale difference. In the financial market, observations often exhibit some form of dependence structure, particularly in daily and weekly data. As demonstrated in real data applications, the residuals of the traditional linear factor pricing model are time-dependent, making the assumption of identical and independent distribution unsuitable for random error. In high-dimensional settings,
Ayyala et al. 2017
proposed a sum-type test procedure for the
M-
dependent stationary Gaussian process, with corrections to the proofs of
Ayyala et al. 2017
’s main theorems provided by
Cho et al. 2019
. For the max-type test procedure, significant efforts have been made towards Gaussian approximation for high-dimensional time series, as seen in the works of
Zhang and Wu 2017
,
Zhang and Cheng 2018
, and
Chang et al. 2024
. An overview of these efforts can be found in
Chernozhukov et al. 2023
. To the best of our knowledge, no literature exists that considers the high-dimensional alpha test of linear factor pricing models with dependent observations.

In this study, we initially introduce a sum-type test statistic, which is derived by simply summing the squares of the estimated alphas for each security. We compute the expectation and asymptotic variance of this sum-type test statistic and affirm its asymptotic normality under the assumption of an
MA(\infty)
process. Notably, we relax the assumptions of normal distribution and
M
-dependence as presented in
Ayyala et al. 2017
. Subsequently, we provide a consistent estimator of the expectation and asymptotic variance and propose a sum-type test procedure that performs exceptionally well under dense alternatives, i.e., when many alphas of the securities are non-zero. In cases where only a few alphas of the securities are non-zero, we propose a max-type test statistic, which is the maximum of the square of estimated alphas divided by its corresponding long-run variances. For dependent observations, we demonstrate that the limit null distribution of the proposed max-type test statistic also follows a Gumbel distribution and establish its consistency under sparse alternatives.

It’s important to note that the underlying truth in real applications is typically unknown, as the density or sparsity is contingent on the properties of the securities involved. Recent literature has demonstrated that the maximum-type test statistic is asymptotically independent of the sum-type test statistic, leading to the proposal of a corresponding combination test in numerous high-dimensional problems. These include high-dimensional mean testing problems by
Xu et al. 2016
,
He et al. 2021
and
Feng et al. 2023
, cross-sectional independence test in high-dimensional panel data models by
Feng et al. 2022a
, testing of high-dimensional covariance matrix by
Yu et al. 2022
, high-dimensional change point inference by
Wang and Feng 2023
and testing alpha of high dimensional time-varying factor model by
Ma et al. 2023
. All the aforementioned papers affirm the asymptotic independence between the sum-type test statistic and the max-type test statistic, based on the assumption of identical and independent distribution. However, this assumption may not be directly applicable in our context. In this study, we are the first to establish the asymptotic independence between the sum-type test statistic and the max-type test statistic under the assumption of time-dependence. We then introduce a Cauchy combination test procedure, which is capable of handling both dense and sparse alternatives simultaneously. Furthermore, we provide a power analysis of the proposed Cauchy combination test procedure. The advantages of our proposed tests over existing methods are demonstrated through extensive Monte Carlo experiments and an empirical application.

In summary, there are three main contributions of this paper:

1.

We have relaxed the assumption of Gaussian distribution and
M
-dependence as presented in
Ayyala et al. 2017
, and have established the asymptotic normality of the sum-type test statistic in the presence of dependent observations.

2.

For the max-type test, we have also established the limit null distribution and its consistency under sparse alternatives, assuming time-dependent observations.

3.

We are the first to establish the asymptotic independence between the sum-type test statistic and the max-type test statistic under the assumption of time-dependence. We have proposed a Cauchy combination test procedure, which performs very well under neither dense nor sparse alternatives.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Sections 2, we present the sum-type test, the max-type test procedures and the Cauchy combination test procedure. Section 3 showcases some simulation studies. An empirical application is provided in Section 4. Section 5 contains some discussions. All technical details are included in the Appendix.

2
Test Procedures

2.1
Sum-type test

Assume that there are a total of
N
securities and each security has
T
observations of its excess return that are regularly collected from different time periods.
Let
y_{it}
be the excess return of security
i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
at time
t\in\{1,\cdots,T\}
. Let
{\bm{f}}_{t}=(f_{t1},\cdots,f_{tp})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{p}
denote the
p
-vector of observed factors at time
t
,
which represents the excess return over the excess return on the
p
observed factors. In this paper, we assume that the number of factors
p
is fixed, which depends on the specific multi-factor model studied.

Assume that security returns are governed by the following multi-factor model:

\displaystyle y_{it}=\alpha_{i}+\bm{\beta}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{f}}_{t}+\varepsilon_{it},~i\in\{1,\cdots,N\},~t\in\{1,\cdots,T\},

(1)

where
\alpha_{i}
is a scalar representing the security specific intercept,
\bm{\beta}_{i}=(\beta_{i1},\cdots,\beta_{ip})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{p}

is a vector of multiple regression betas of security
i
with respect to the
p
factors, and the
\varepsilon_{it}
’s are the corresponding idiosyncratic error terms. Note that for the classic CAPM
(
Sharpe 1964
;
Lintner 1965
)
, the single common factor is the market risk, while for the Fama and French three-factor model
(
Fama and French 1993
)
, the common factors are the market risk, SMB and HML, where SMB and HML measure the historic excess returns of small-cap stocks over big-cap stocks and of value stocks over growth stocks, respectively.

Stacking by cross-sectional observations, we can write the above multi-factor model as

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{t}=\bm{\alpha}+{\bf B}{\bm{f}}_{t}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{t},~t\in\{1,\cdots,T\},

(2)

where
{\bf y}_{t}=(y_{1t},\cdots,y_{Nt})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{N}
,
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{N})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{N}
,

{\bf B}=(\bm{\beta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\beta}_{N})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{N\times p}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}=(\varepsilon_{1t},\cdots,\varepsilon_{Nt})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{N}
. In addition, there are some notation to be used in later sections:
{\bf y}_{i\cdot}=(y_{i1},\cdots,y_{iT})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{T}
,

\bm{\varepsilon}_{i\cdot}=(\varepsilon_{i1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{iT})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{T}
, for each
i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
;

{\bf Y}=({\bf y}_{1},\cdots,{\bf y}_{N})\in\mathcal{R}^{T\times N}
,
\bm{\varepsilon}=(\bm{\varepsilon}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\varepsilon}_{N})\in\mathcal{R}^{T\times N}
,

{\bf F}=({\bm{f}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{f}}_{T})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{T\times p}
.

To evaluate the marginal return associated with an additional strategy that is not explained by existing factors, many researchers employ the specification test for a factor model
by testing

\displaystyle H_{0}:\bm{\alpha}=\mathbf{0}~~\text{versus}~~H_{1}:\bm{\alpha}\not=\mathbf{0}.

(3)

This test has a special explanation in the seminal single-factor model CAPM. Specifically, if a single factor or a particular portfolio is mean-variance efficient,
i.e. it minimizes variance for a given level of expected return, then
E(y_{it})=\bm{\beta}_{i}^{\top}E({\bm{f}}_{t})
must be satisfied
with
\bm{\beta}_{i}=\mathrm{cov}(y_{it},{\bm{f}}_{t})\mathrm{var}^{-1}({\bm{f}}_{t})\in\mathcal{R}^{p}
standing for the systematic risk on security
i
, which
is equivalent to
\bm{\alpha}=\mathbf{0}
under the distributional assumption given by (
1
).
Thus, in CAPM the above test in (
3
) is actually a test of the mean-variance efficiency.

Let
\bm{1}_{T}=(1,\cdots,1)^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{T}
,
{\bf I}_{T}
be the
T\times T
identity matrix and
{\bf M}_{{\bf F}}={\bf I}_{T}-{\bf F}({\bf F}^{\top}{\bf F})^{-1}{\bf F}^{\top}
;

\hat{\bm{\alpha}}=(\hat{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\alpha}_{N})^{\top}
be the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimator of, where

\hat{\alpha}_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{i\cdot}^{\top}\left(\frac{{\bf M}_{{\bf F}}\bm{1}_{T}}{\bm{1}_{T}^{\top}{\bf M}_{{\bf F}}\bm{1}_{T}}\right).

And
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t}^{\top}
with
\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t}=(\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{1t},\cdots,\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{Nt})^{\top}
,
where
\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{it}
is the OLS residual from the regression of
y_{it}
on an intercept and
{\bm{f}}_{t}
,
i.e.

\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{i\cdot}=(\hat{\varepsilon}_{i1},\cdots,\hat{\varepsilon}_{iT})^{\top}={\bf M}_{{\bf F}}(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\hat{\alpha}_{i}).

To handle dense alternatives, we propose a sum-type test, named the SUM test, with the test statistic constructed as

\displaystyle T_{{\text{Sum}}}=\hat{\bm{\alpha}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\alpha}}.

(4)

Note that, if the error term is identical and independent distributed,
Pesaran and Yamagata 2023
also construct a similar sum-type test statistic

\displaystyle T_{{\rm PY}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{t_{i}^{2}-v/(v-2)\}}{v/(v-2)\sqrt{2(v-1)/(v-4)\{1+(N-1)\tilde{\rho}_{MT}^{2}\}}},

\displaystyle t_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{2}(\bm{1}_{T}^{\top}{\bf M}_{{\bf F}}\bm{1}_{T})}{v^{-1}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{i\cdot}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{i\cdot}},

where
v=T-p-1
and

\tilde{\rho}_{MT}=2/\{N(N-1)\}\sum_{i=2}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{ij}

is the corresponding correlation estimator of

\rho^{2}=2/\{N(N-1)\}\sum_{i=2}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\rho^{2}_{ij}

with
\tilde{\rho}_{ij}
denoting the multiple testing estimator of
the correlation

\rho_{ij}=\sigma_{ij}/(\sigma_{ii}^{1/2}\sigma_{jj}^{1/2})

(
Bailey et al. 2019
)
.
However, when the errors
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
are time-dependent, a bias term that cannot be ignored appears in
T_{{\rm PY}}
. That is, under the null hypothesis, the expectation of
T_{{\rm PY}}
is not zero, i.e.,
E(T_{{\rm PY}})\not=0
. Furthermore, the asymptotic variance of
T_{{\rm PY}}
will no longer be one. Therefore,
T_{{\rm PY}}
may not perform well with dependent observations.

In our proposed test, we allow both
N
and
T
to be large, and allow
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
’s be dependent linear processes. We need the conditions for the error terms and factors given below.

(C1)

(i)
\{{\bm{f}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{f}}_{T}\}
is an strictly stationary. (ii)

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{f}}}\equiv\mathrm{cov}({\bm{f}}_{t})
is strictly positive definite. (iii)
{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{f}}_{t}
is bounded for all

.

Under Condition (C1), we have
T^{-1}\bm{1}_{T}^{\top}{\bf M}_{{\bf F}}\bm{1}_{T}\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{p}\omega
,
T^{-1}{\bf F}^{\top}\bm{1}_{T}\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{p}E({\bm{f}}_{t})=:{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{f}}}
,
T^{-1}{\bf F}^{\top}{\bf F}\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{p}\mu_{{\bm{f}}}\mu_{{\bm{f}}}^{\top}+{\bm{\Sigma}}_{{\bm{f}}}=:{\bf\Lambda}_{{\bm{f}}}
. Define
e_{t}:=\frac{1}{\omega}(1-{\bm{f}}_{t}^{\top}{\bf\Lambda}_{{\bm{f}}}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}_{{\bm{f}}})
. Because
{\bm{f}}_{t}
is strictly stationary, so
e_{t}
is also strictly stationary.
Define
{\bm{X}}_{t}:=\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}e_{t}
. We consider the following dependent model for
{\bm{X}}_{t}
:

(C2)

{\bm{X}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{X}}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
follow an
MA(\infty)
process of the form
(
Zhang et al. 2018
)

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}_{t}=\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}{\bm{z}}_{t-k}.

(5)

(i)
\{{\bm{z}}_{t}\}
is a sequence of
N

{\bm{z}}_{t}
are
\left\{z_{it}\right\},
then the two dimensional array
\left\{z_{it}:1\leq i\leq N,t\geq 1\right\}
of variables are i.i.d. satisfying the moment conditions
\mathbb{E}z_{it}=0,\mathbb{E}\left|z_{it}\right|^{2}=1
and
\mathbb{E}\left|z_{it}\right|^{4}=\nu_{4}<\infty
.

(ii)
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|b_{k}|<\infty
,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}=s\neq 0
and
b_{k}=o(T^{-5})
, i.e.
\lim_{T\to\infty}T^{5}b_{T}=0
.

Unlike the
M-
dependent Gaussian stationary assumption in
Ayyala et al. 2017
, we allow the error term can follows a
MA(\infty)
process and
z_{it}
could be not normal distributed.
Under Condition (C2),
{\bm{X}}_{t}
has mean zero and auto-covariance structure given by
\Gamma_{h},h\in\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.
\mathbb{E}({\bm{X}}_{t}{\bm{X}}_{t+h}^{\top})=:\Gamma_{h}=a_{h}\bm{\Sigma}
for
h=0,1,2,\cdots
and
\Gamma_{h}=\Gamma_{-h}^{\top}
with
a_{h}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}b_{k+h}
. Next, we give some conditions of structure of the auto-covariance matrix.

(C3)

There exist two positive constants
M_{0}
and
M_{1}
such that
\|\bm{\Sigma}\|_{2}\leq M_{0}
and
\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma})/N\geq M_{1}
, and
\mathrm{tr}^{2}(\bm{\Sigma})/(T\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2}))=o(1)
. Here
\|\cdot\|_{2}
stands for either the spectral norm of a matrix or the Euclidean norm of a vector.

(C4)

{\bf\Omega}_{T}:=T\mathrm{cov}(\bar{{\bm{X}}}_{T})=\Gamma_{0}+2\sum_{h=1}^{T}(1-\frac{h}{T})\Gamma_{h}\to\Gamma_{0}
, where
\Gamma_{0}
is the spectral matrix evaluated at the zero frequency.

(C5)

N=O(T)
, and
N\to\infty
as
T\to\infty
.

Condition (C3) reflects Assumption (A3) as stated in
Zhang et al. 2018
, suggesting that the correlation among these variables should not be overly high. Condition (C4) is a standard assumption made for multivariate time series. In this study, we permit the number of securities
N
to be comparable to the length of the time period
T
, which is generally sufficient for most empirical studies.

We propose to use a standardized version of
T_{{\text{Sum}}}
as the test statistic. From simple mathematical derivation, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{\alpha}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\eta_{t}

with
\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{t},\cdots,\eta_{T})^{\top}=\frac{{\bf M}_{{\bf F}}\bm{1}_{T}}{T^{-1}\bm{1}_{T}^{\top}{\bf M}_{{\bf F}}\bm{1}_{T}}
. Define

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{{\text{Sum}}}=\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}~~\text{with}~~\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{\alpha}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}e_{t}.

(6)

In the appendix, we will show the difference between
T_{{\text{Sum}}}
and
\tilde{T}_{{\text{Sum}}}
are negligible.
So, we only need to consider the mean and variance of
\tilde{T}_{{\text{Sum}}}
under
H_{0}
, given below:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\tilde{T}_{{\text{Sum}}})=\mathbb{E}(\bar{{\bm{X}}}_{T}^{\top}\bar{{\bm{X}}}_{T})=\frac{1}{T}\left\{\mathrm{tr}(\Gamma_{0})+2\sum_{h=1}^{T}\left(1-\frac{h}{T}\right)\mathrm{tr}(\Gamma_{h})\right\}=:\mu_{T},

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(\tilde{T}_{{\text{Sum}}})\approx\frac{2}{T^{2}}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T}^{2})=:\sigma_{T}^{2},

We use
\mu_{T}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{T}\{\mathrm{tr}(\Gamma_{0})+2\sum_{h=1}^{M}(1-\frac{h}{T})\mathrm{tr}(\Gamma_{h})\}
replace
\mu_{T}
, where
M=\lceil\min(N,T)^{1/8}\rceil
. The reasons for such substitution will be explained later.

First, we give the asymptotic normality of the test statistic
T_{{\text{Sum}}}
.

Theorem 1

Under Conditions (C1)-(C5) hold and assuming
M=\lceil\min(N,T)^{1/8}\rceil
. Under
H_{0}
, we have
(T_{{\text{Sum}}}-\mu_{T}^{\prime})/\sigma_{T}\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{d}N(0,1)
as
\min(N,T)\to\infty
.

Next, we consider the following alternative hypothesis:

(C6)

\bm{\alpha}^{\top}(\Gamma_{h}\Gamma_{-h})^{1/2}\bm{\alpha}=o\{(M+1)^{-1}T^{-1}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T}^{2})\}
,
h=0,\cdots,M
.

Theorem 2

Under Conditions (C1)-(C6) hold and assuming
M=\lceil\min(N,T)^{1/8}\rceil
, we have
(T_{{\text{Sum}}}-\mu_{T}^{\prime})/\sigma_{T}\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{d}N\left(\frac{\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}}{\sqrt{2T^{-2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T}^{2})}},1\right)
as
\min(N,T)\to\infty
.

It is worth noting that
(T_{{\text{Sum}}}-\mu_{T}^{\prime})/\sigma_{T}
is usually unknown since it involves population parameters. Thus it cannot be used as a test statistic in practice. We therefore need to find consistent estimators of
\mu_{T}^{\prime}
and
\sigma_{T}^{2}
. Since
\hat{\varepsilon}_{it}\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{p}\varepsilon_{it}
and
\eta_{t}-e_{t}=o_{p}(1)
, the following formula gives an estimator of the auto-covariance matrix
\Gamma_{h}

\displaystyle\hat{\Gamma}_{h}=\frac{1}{T-h}\sum_{t=1}^{T-h}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t+h}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t}^{\top}\eta_{t+h}\eta_{t}.

Then, we consider the plug-in estimator of
\mu_{T}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{T}=\frac{1}{T-p-1}\left\{\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\Gamma}_{0})+2\sum_{h=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{h}{T-p-1}\right)\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\Gamma}_{h})\right\}.

We next find a ratio consistent estimator of
\sigma_{T}^{2}
. For testing unit root,
Zhang et al. 2018
proposed an estimator for
a_{0}^{2}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})
and prove that the estimator is suitable for some special correlation structure. Their main idea was to construct a special set of subscripts such that only pairwise correlations exist between the four samples in each component of the estimator. Similarly, we consider the following estimator for
a_{h_{1}}a_{h_{2}}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{2})=\mathrm{tr}(\Gamma_{h_{1}}\Gamma_{h_{2}})
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{tr}(\Gamma_{h_{1}}\Gamma_{h_{2}})}\equiv S_{h_{1},h_{2}}=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{[T/2]-h_{2}}\sum_{s=t+[T/2]}^{T-h_{2}}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{s}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{t+h_{1}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{s+h_{2}}\eta_{t}\eta_{s}\eta_{t+h_{1}}\eta_{s+h_{2}}}{(T-h_{2}/2-\frac{3}{2}[T/2]+1/2)([T/2]-h_{2})}.

Accordingly,

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{T}^{2}=\frac{2}{T^{2}}\left(S_{0,0}+2\sum_{r=1}^{M}S_{0,r}+2\sum_{r=1}^{M}S_{r,0}+4\sum_{r=1}^{M}\sum_{s=1}^{M}S_{r,s}\right).

Next, we show that the above two estimators are consistent.

Theorem 3

Assume the conditions in Theorems 1 and 2 hold. Then as
\min(N,T)\to\infty
,
\hat{\mu}_{T}-\mu_{T}^{\prime}=o_{p}(\sigma_{T})
and
\hat{\sigma}_{T}^{2}/\sigma_{T}^{2}\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{p}1
.

According to Theorems
1
-
3
, the
p
-value associated with
T_{{\text{Sum}}}
is

p_{{\text{Sum}}}=1-\Phi\left(\frac{T_{{\text{Sum}}}-\hat{\mu}_{T}}{\hat{\sigma}_{T}}\right),

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
{N}(0,1)
. If the
p
-value is below some pre-specified significant level, say
\gamma\in(0,1)
, then we rejected the null hypothesis.

According to Theorem
1
and
2
, the power function of
T_{{\text{Sum}}}
is

\displaystyle\beta_{{\text{Sum}}}(\bm{\alpha})=\Phi\left(-z_{\gamma}+\frac{T\bm{\alpha}^{\top}\bm{\alpha}}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T}^{2})}}\right)

(7)

where
z_{\gamma}
is the upper
\gamma
quantile of
N(0,1)
. Note that the power function
\beta_{{\text{Sum}}}(\bm{\alpha})
has the similar form of the power function of
Chen and Qin 2010
’s test for the high dimensional two sample mean problem. If the error term
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
are identical and independent distributed and the variances of each
\varepsilon_{it},i=1,\cdots,N
are equal,
\beta_{{\text{Sum}}}
are the same as the power function of
T_{{\rm PY}}
.

2.2
Max-type test

The power function (
7
) indicates that the sum-type test
T_{{\text{Sum}}}
can achieve strong performance when a significant number of alphas are nonzero. Conversely, if only a limited number of alphas are nonzero,
T_{{\text{Sum}}}
may not exhibit sufficient power. In scenarios involving sparse alternatives, numerous studies have shown that the max-type test procedure is highly effective. For instance,
Cai et al. 2014
demonstrated its utility in high-dimensional two-sample problems,
Feng et al. 2022b
applied it to high-dimensional alpha testing in linear factor pricing models, and
Ma et al. 2023
used it for high-dimensional alpha testing in time-varying factor pricing models.

Consequently, we proposed the following max-type test

\displaystyle T_{{\text{Max}}}=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{T\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{i}},

where
\hat{\sigma}_{i}=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{M}}\omega(h/M)\hat{\phi}_{i,h}
,
\hat{\phi}_{i,h}=\frac{1}{T-h}\sum_{t=h+1}^{T}\hat{\varepsilon}_{it}\hat{\varepsilon}_{i,t-h}\eta_{t}\eta_{t-h}
and
\mathcal{M}=\{0,\pm 1,\dots,\pm M\}
. Here
\omega(\cdot)
is a weighted function. For simplicity, we use the plain estimate in the article, i.e.
\omega(\cdot)=1
. Here
\hat{\sigma}_{i}
is the long-run variance estimator.

Next, we will demonstrate that when
(N,T)\to\infty
,
T_{{\text{Max}}}-2\log(N)+\log\{\log(N)\}
has a type I extreme value distribution. To proceed, we first introduce some conditions.

Let
{\bf D}_{0}\equiv\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{0},\dots,\sigma_{N}^{0})
be the diagonal matrix of the long-run covariance of
{\bm{X}}_{t}
,
{\bf\Omega}:=\Gamma_{0}+2\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\Gamma_{h}
, and
\bm{R}_{0}:={\bf D}_{0}^{-1/2}{\bf\Omega}{\bf D}_{0}^{-1/2}
. Let
[{\bf A}]_{ij}
denote the
(i,j)
-th element of matrix
{\bf A}
.

(C7)

\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}[\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}]_{ij}^{4}<C
for some positive constant
C
.

(C8)

\max_{1\leq i<j\leq N}[\bm{R}_{0}]_{ij}\leq r<1
and
\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}[\bm{R}_{0}]_{ij}^{2}\leq c
for some constants
r,c>0
.

(C9)

M^{1+a}T^{1/q-1/2}\log^{2}(N)\log^{3/2}(NT)=o(1)
and
M^{qa^{2}+(q+7)a+2}T^{-qa/2}\log^{2+5qa/2}(N)\log^{7a}(NT)=o(1)
for some
q\geq 4
and
a>1/2-1/q
.

Condition (C7) and (C8) are similar to the conditions in Lemma 6 in
Cai et al. 2014
, which assume the correlations between those variables are not very large. In the special case with
M\asymp T^{1/8}
, Condition (C9) becomes
\log(N)=o(T^{\tau})
where
\tau=\frac{2}{5}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1+a}{8})\wedge\frac{-qa^{2}+(3q-7)a-2}{16+(56+20q)a}
with
q\geq 4
and
a>1/2-1/q
.

Theorem 4

Under Conditions (C1)-(C2) and (C7)-(C9), and assuming
M=\lceil\min(N,T)^{1/8}\rceil
, we have as
\min(N,T)\to\infty
,

\displaystyle P_{H_{0}}\left(T_{{\text{Max}}}-2\log(N)+\log\{\log(N)\}\leq x\right)\to\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{x}{2}\right)\right\}\equiv F(x).

Here,
\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}
denotes the probability measure under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
. According to the limiting null distribution derived in Theorem
4
, we can easily obtain the
p
-value associated with
T_{{\text{Max}}}
, namely,

p_{{\text{Max}}}=1-F(T_{{\text{Max}}}-2\log(N)+\log\{\log(N)\}).

Again, small values of
p_{{\text{Max}}}
indicate rejecting the null hypothesis. Next, we turn to the power analysis of the proposed max-type test.

Theorem 5

Under Conditions (C1)-(C2) and (C7)-(C9), and assuming
M=\lceil\min(N,T)^{1/8}\rceil
, we have as
\min(T,N)\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}(c)}P(\Phi_{\gamma}=1)\to 1,

where
\Phi_{\gamma}\equiv I[T_{{\text{Max}}}-2\log(N)+\log\{\log(N)\}\geq q_{\gamma}]
,
q_{\gamma}\equiv-\log(\pi)-2\log\left\{\log(1-\gamma)^{-1}\right\}
,
c=4
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(c)\equiv\left\{\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,\alpha^{\prime}_{N})\in\mathcal{R}^{N}:\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\alpha_{i}/\sqrt{\sigma_{i}^{0}}\geq c\sqrt{\log(N)/{T}}\right\}.

(8)

Theorem
5
demonstrates that if at least one alpha is significantly large, the suggested max-type test maintains consistency. As showed in Theorem 3 in
Feng et al. 2022b
, the rate
\sqrt{\log(N)/T}
is minimax rate optimal, which means any
\gamma
-level test is unable to detect the alternative hypothesis in probability uniformly over

\left\{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{R}^{N}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}I\left(\alpha_{i}\neq 0\right)=k_{N}\right\}\bigcap\{\bm{\alpha}\in\left.\mathcal{R}^{N}:\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\left|\alpha_{i}\right|>c_{4}\sqrt{\log(N)/T}\right\}

where
k_{N}=O(N^{r}),r<1/4
. Hence the order of the lower bound
\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\alpha_{i}/\sqrt{\sigma_{i}^{0}}\geq c\sqrt{\log(N)/{T}}
cannot be improved.

2.3
Cauchy combination test

In real-world scenarios, it’s often unclear whether the intercept vector is sparse or dense. To accommodate different alternative behaviors, we integrate the Sum-test and Max-test. The crucial point is that these two test statistics are asymptotically independent under certain mild conditions and the null hypothesis.

Theorem 6

Under Conditions (C1)-(C9) and assuming
M=\lceil\min(N,T)^{1/8}\rceil
, under
H_{0}
, we have as
\min(N,T)\to\infty
,

\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}\left(\frac{T_{{\text{Sum}}}-\hat{\mu}_{T}}{\hat{\sigma}_{T}}\leq x,T_{{\text{Max}}}-2\log(N)+\log\{\log(N)\}\leq y\right)\to\Phi(x)F(y).

According to Theorem
6
, we suggest combining the corresponding
p
-values by using Cauchy Combination Method
(
Liu and Xie 2020
)
, to wit,

p_{CC}=1-G[0.5\tan\{(0.5-p_{{\text{Max}}})\pi\}+0.5\tan\{(0.5-p_{{\text{Sum}}})\pi\}],

where
G(\cdot)
is the CDF of the the standard Cauchy distribution. If the final
p
-value is less than some pre-specified significant level
\gamma\in(0,1)
, then we reject
H_{0}
.

Next, we analyze the power of the adaptive testing procedure. We consider the following sequence of alternative hypotheses, to wit,

H_{1,NT}:||\bm{\alpha}||_{0}=o[N/\log^{2}\{\log(N)\}]~~\text{and}~~||\bm{\alpha}||_{2}=O\{T^{-1/2}\mathrm{tr}^{1/4}({\bf\Omega}_{T}^{2})\}.

(9)

In fact, the asymptotic independence between the maximum-type and sum-type statistics still hold under the hypotheses given in (
9
).

Theorem 7

Under the same condition as Theorem
6
, we have as
(N,T)\to\infty
, under
H_{1,NT}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{T_{{\text{Sum}}}-\hat{\mu}_{T}}{\hat{\sigma}_{T}}\leq x,T_{{\text{Max}}}-2\log(N)+\log\{\log(N)\}\leq y\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\to\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{T_{{\text{Sum}}}-\hat{\mu}_{T}}{\hat{\sigma}_{T}}\leq x\right)\mathbb{P}\left(T_{{\text{Max}}}-2\log(N)+\log\{\log(N)\}\leq y\right).

Long et al. 2023
show that the power of Cauchy combination-based test would be powerful than that of the test based on
\min\{p_{{\text{Max}}},p_{{\text{Sum}}}\}
(referred to as the minimal
p
-value combination), say
\beta_{M\wedge S,\gamma}=P(\min\{{p}_{{\text{Max}}},{p}_{{\text{Sum}}}\}\leq 1-\sqrt{1-\gamma})
. Obviously,

\displaystyle\beta_{CC,\gamma}\geq\beta_{M\wedge S,\gamma}

\displaystyle\geq P(\min\{{p}_{{\text{Max}}},{p}_{{\text{Sum}}}\}\leq\gamma/2)

\displaystyle=\beta_{{{\text{Max}}},\gamma/2}+\beta_{{{\text{Sum}}},\gamma/2}-P({p}_{{\text{Max}}}\leq\gamma/2,{p}_{{\text{Sum}}}\leq\gamma/2)

\displaystyle\geq\max\{\beta_{{{\text{Max}}},\gamma/2},\beta_{{{\text{Sum}}},\gamma/2}\}.

(10)

On the other hand, under
H_{1}
in (
9

\displaystyle\beta_{CC,\gamma}\geq\beta_{{{\text{Max}}}\wedge S,\gamma}\geq\beta_{{{\text{Max}}},\gamma/2}+\beta_{{{\text{Sum}}},\gamma/2}-\beta_{{{\text{Max}}},\gamma/2}\beta_{{{\text{Sum}}},\gamma/2}+o(1),

(11)

due to the asymptotic independence entailed by Theorem
7

\gamma

\beta_{{{\text{Max}}},\gamma}
and
\beta_{{{\text{Max}}},\gamma/2}
should be small, and the same fact applies to
\beta_{S,\gamma}

2.3
)–(
11

In this section, we compare our proposed methods with three test procedures for the independent case: the sum-type test procedure proposed by
Pesaran and Yamagata 2017
(abbreviated as PY), the max-type test procedure proposed by
Feng et al. 2022b
(abbreviated as FLM) and the combination test procedure proposed by
Feng et al. 2022b
(abbreviated as COM).

This simulation is designed to mimic the commonly used Fama-French three-factor model,
where the factors
{\bm{f}}_{t}
have strong serial correlation and heterogeneous variance.
Specifically, we consider the example studied
in
Feng et al. 2022b
. The response
Y_{it}

1

p=3

\displaystyle Y_{it}=\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{ij}f_{tj}+\varepsilon_{it},

and

We generate each factor from an autoregressive conditional

Specifically

\displaystyle f_{t1}=

\displaystyle 0.53+0.06f_{t-1,1}+h_{t1}^{1/2}\zeta_{t1},~\mbox{Market factor},

\displaystyle f_{t3}=

\displaystyle 0.19+0.19f_{t-1,2}+h_{t2}^{1/2}\zeta_{t2},~\mbox{SMB factor},

\displaystyle f_{t3}=

\displaystyle 0.19+0.05f_{t-1,3}+h_{t3}^{1/2}\zeta_{t3},~\mbox{HML factor},

\displaystyle=0.62+0.74h_{t-1,2}+0.19\zeta_{t-1,2}^{2},~\mbox{SMB},

Real Data Application

In this study, we focus on the stocks listed in the S&P 500 index, a globally recognized benchmark for U.S. equities. We have compiled weekly returns for all the securities in the S&P 500 index from January 14, 2005, to November 24, 2023, excluding any missing values. This gives us a total of
T=888
observations over this extensive period. As the composition of the index changes over time, we have limited our analysis to
N=393
securities that were consistently part of the S&P 500 index throughout the entire period. The time series data for the risk-free rate of return and other factors were sourced from Ken French’s data library web page.
We have chosen the one-month U.S. treasury bill rate as the risk-free rate, denoted as
r_{ft}
. As a proxy for the market return
r_{mt}
, we used the value-weighted return on all stocks listed on the NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ, as provided by CRSP. We calculated the Small Minus Big (SMB) factor,
\text{SMB}_{t}
, as the average return on three small portfolios minus the average return on three big portfolios. Similarly, the High Minus Low (HML) factor,
\text{HML}_{t}
, is calculated as the average return on two value portfolios minus the average return on two growth portfolios. Both these factors are based on the stocks listed on the NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ.

We use the Fama-French three-factor model to describe the above panel data.
Recall that the Fama-French three-factor model is

\displaystyle Y_{it}=r_{it}-r_{ft}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i1}(r_{mt}-r_{ft})+\beta_{i2}SMB_{t}+\beta_{i3}HML_{t}+\epsilon_{it},

(12)

for each
i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
and

t\in\{\tau,\cdots,\tau+T^{\prime}-1\}
, where
r_{mt}-r_{ft}
is also referred
to as the market factor. We are interested in testing

\displaystyle H_{0}:\alpha_{1}=\dots\alpha_{N}=0,\text{versus}~H_{1}:\exists~i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}\text{ s.t. }\alpha_{i}\neq 0.

(13)

Initially, we examine the presence of time series dependence in the residual sequences of the dataset. We apply the Box-Pierce test, a conventional autocorrelation test, to the residuals of each security under the Fama-French three-factor model with the total sample sizes. The histogram of p-values from the Box-Pierce test for the U.S. datasets is shown in Figure
7
. We notice a significant number of
p
-values below 0.1, leading us to believe that some residual sequences may exhibit autocorrelation. Furthermore, we employ the max-type high-dimensional white noise test procedure, as described in
Feng et al. 2022c
, to verify if the residuals are white noise. The
p
-value of this test is 2e-11, indicating some time-dependence among
\epsilon_{it}
. Consequently, test procedures based on the i.i.d assumption may not perform well and could yield inaccurate results. To analyze this dataset, we apply three test procedures proposed in this paper - SUM, MAX, and CC, which are designed to handle such time-dependence.

We next examine the hypothesis (
13
) using a rolling window procedure with a window length of
T=260
(equivalent to 5 years). The p-values for each alpha test procedure over the time period are depicted in Figure

. Our observations reveal that for the initial seven years and the final six years, nearly all tests do not reject the null hypothesis. This suggests that the three observed factors can effectively account for the stock returns. However, during the five-year period in the middle, the CC test frequently rejects the null hypothesis, leading us to infer that the markets were inefficient during these periods. Interestingly, there are instances over the long time period where the MAX or SUM test does not reject the null hypothesis. This demonstrates that our CC test procedure is more robust and efficient under various sparsity alternatives.

5
Conclusion

Initially, we introduced the sum-type and max-type tests for a high-dimensional linear pricing model with dependent observations. These tests perform well against both dense and sparse alternatives. To broaden the application, we introduced a Cauchy combination test that combines the sum-type and max-type tests. Both simulation studies and real data applications have shown the necessity of considering the time-dependence of the error term. Ignoring this could lead to significant size distortion in methods based on the i.i.d assumption.

Furthermore, given the importance of allowing for time-variation in the risk-exposure coefficients under LFPMs, our future work will focus on developing sum-type, max-type, and combination test procedures that allow for such time-variation. We plan to use a more complex and powerful technical framework to establish the asymptotic theory, as opposed to the current one.

Lemma 1

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}f(W(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{q_{T}}))-\mathbb{E}f(W(\bm{\xi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{q_{T}}))|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{q_{T}}|\mathbb{E}f(W_{k})-\mathbb{E}f(W_{k+1})|.

By Taylor’s expansion, we have

\displaystyle f(W_{k})-f(W_{k,0})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{i!}f^{(i)}(W_{k,0})(W_{k}-W_{k,0})^{i}+O(|W_{k}-W_{k,0}|^{3}),~~\text{and}

\displaystyle f(W_{k+1})-f(W_{k,0})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{i!}f^{(i)}(W_{k,0})(W_{k+1}-W_{k,0})^{i}+O(|W_{k+1}-W_{k,0}|^{3}).

According to the properties of
u(\bm{\xi}_{t},\bm{\zeta}_{s})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(W_{k}-W_{k,0}|\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k-1},\bm{\xi}_{k+1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{q_{T}})=\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}(u(\bm{\zeta}_{t},\bm{\xi}_{k})|\bm{\zeta}_{t})+\sum_{s=k+1}^{q_{T}}\mathbb{E}(u(\bm{\xi}_{k},\bm{\xi}_{s})|\bm{\xi}_{s})=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(W_{k+1}-W_{k,0}|\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k-1},\bm{\xi}_{k+1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{q_{T}})=\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}(u(\bm{\zeta}_{t},\bm{\zeta}_{k})|\bm{\zeta}_{t})+\sum_{s=k+1}^{q_{T}}\mathbb{E}(u(\bm{\zeta}_{k},\bm{\xi}_{s})|\bm{\xi}_{s})=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(W_{k}-W_{k,0})^{2}|\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k-1},\bm{\xi}_{k+1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{q_{T}}]=\mathbb{E}[(W_{k+1}-W_{k,0})^{2}|\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{k-1},\bm{\xi}_{k+1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{q_{T}}].

(A.5)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{i!}f^{(i)}(W_{k,0})(W_{k}-W_{k,0})^{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{i!}f^{(i)}(W_{k,0})(W_{k+1}-W_{k,0})^{i}\right].

Thus, there exists a positive constant
C
such that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}f(W(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{q_{T}}))-\mathbb{E}f(W(\bm{\xi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{q_{T}}))|\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{k=1}^{q_{T}}(\mathbb{E}|W_{k}-W_{k,0}|^{3}+\mathbb{E}|W_{k+1}-W_{k,0}|^{3})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{k=1}^{q_{T}}[(\mathbb{E}|W_{k}-W_{k,0}|^{4})^{3/4}+(\mathbb{E}|W_{k+1}-W_{k,0}|^{4})^{3/4}].

Next, we derive the upper bounds for
\mathbb{E}|W_{k}-W_{k,0}|^{4}
and
\mathbb{E}|W_{k+1}-W_{k,0}|^{4}
. According to the properties of
u(\bm{\xi}_{t},\bm{\zeta}_{s})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|W_{k}-W_{k,0}|^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}(u(\bm{\zeta}_{t},\bm{\xi}_{k})^{4})+\sum_{s=k+1}^{q_{T}}\mathbb{E}(u(\bm{\xi}_{k},\bm{\xi}_{s})^{4})+6\sum_{t_{1}=1}^{k-1}\sum_{t_{2}=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}(u(\bm{\zeta}_{t_{1}},\bm{\xi}_{k})^{2}u(\bm{\zeta}_{t_{2}},\bm{\xi}_{k})^{2})

\displaystyle+6\sum_{s_{1}=k+1}^{q_{T}}\sum_{s_{2}=k+1}^{q_{T}}\mathbb{E}(u(\bm{\xi}_{k},\bm{\xi}_{s_{1}})^{2}u(\bm{\xi}_{k},\bm{\xi}_{s_{2}})^{2})+6\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\sum_{s=k+1}^{q_{T}}\mathbb{E}(u(\bm{\zeta}_{t},\bm{\xi}_{k})^{2}u(\bm{\xi}_{k},\bm{\xi}_{s})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}c_{tk}^{4}+\sum_{s=k+1}^{q_{T}}c_{ks}^{4}+6\sum_{t_{1}=1}^{k-1}\sum_{t_{2}=1}^{k-1}c_{t_{1},k}^{2}c_{t_{2},k}^{2}+6\sum_{s_{1}=k+1}^{q_{T}}\sum_{s_{2}=k+1}^{q_{T}}c_{k,s_{1}}^{2}c_{k,s_{2}}^{2}+6\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\sum_{s=k+1}^{q_{T}}c_{tk}^{2}c_{ks}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3\rho_{0}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}c_{tk}^{2}+\sum_{s=k+1}^{q_{T}}c_{ks}^{2}\right)^{2}.

(A.6)

Similarly, we get
\mathbb{E}|W_{k+1}-W_{k,0}|^{4}\leq 3\rho_{0}(\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}c_{tk}^{2}+\sum_{s=k+1}^{q_{T}}c_{ks}^{2})^{2}
. Thus,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}f(W(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{q_{T}}))-\mathbb{E}f(W(\bm{\xi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{q_{T}}))|=O\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{q_{T}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}c_{tk}^{2}+\sum_{s=k+1}^{q_{T}}c_{ks}^{2}\right)^{3/2}\right\}.

Since
\{\bm{\xi}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{q_{T}}
has the same first two moment as
\{\bm{\zeta}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{q_{T}}
, we have

\displaystyle c_{ts}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{(w_{T}-M)^{2}}{2T^{2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T,M}^{2})}\mathbb{E}[(\bm{\xi}_{t}^{\top}\bm{\xi}_{s})^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2T^{2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T,M}^{2})}\sum_{k_{1}=(t-1)w_{T}+1}^{tw_{T}-M}\sum_{k_{2}=(s-1)w_{T}+1}^{sw_{T}-M}\sum_{k_{3}=(t-1)w_{T}+1}^{tw_{T}-M}\sum_{k_{4}=(s-1)w_{T}+1}^{sw_{T}-M}\mathbb{E}(\bm{\gamma}_{k_{1}}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{k_{2}}\bm{\gamma}_{k_{3}}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{k_{4}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2T^{2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T,M}^{2})}\mathrm{tr}\left\{\left(\sum_{k_{1}=(t-1)w_{T}+1}^{tw_{T}-M}\sum_{k_{2}=(t-1)w_{T}+1}^{tw_{T}-M}\Gamma_{k_{1}-k_{2},M}\right)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(w_{T}-M)^{2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{w_{T}-M}^{2})}{2T^{2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T,M}^{2})}\{1+o(1)\}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}f(W(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\zeta}_{q_{T}}))-\mathbb{E}f(W(\bm{\xi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\xi}_{q_{T}}))|=O\left[q_{T}\left\{q_{T}\frac{(w_{T}-M)^{2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{w_{T}-M}^{2})}{2T^{2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T,M}^{2})}\right\}^{3/2}\right]=o(1).

In summary, (
6.1
) is proved.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\varphi^{*}_{1}/\sqrt{2T^{-2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T,M}^{2})}|<\epsilon+x\right)\mathbb{P}\left(B_{i_{1}}...B_{i_{d}}\right)+N^{-c}

\displaystyle\left\{\mathbb{P}\left(|\varphi^{*}_{1}/\sqrt{2T^{-2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T,M}^{2})}|<\epsilon+x,|\varphi^{*}_{2}/\sqrt{2T^{-2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf\Omega}_{T,M}^{2})}|<\epsilon\right)+N^{-c}\right\}\mathbb{P}\left(B_{i_{1}}...B_{i_{d}}\right)+N^{-c}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{N}(x+2\epsilon)\right)\mathbb{P}\left(B_{i_{1}}...B_{i_{d}}\right)+2N^{-c}.

Likewise,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{N}(x)B_{i_{1}}...B_{i_{d}}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(A_{N}(x-2\epsilon)\right)\mathbb{P}\left(B_{i_{1}}...B_{i_{d}}\right)-2N^{-c}.

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}\left(A_{N}(x)B_{i_{1}}...B_{i_{d}}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(A_{N}(x)\right)\mathbb{P}\left(B_{i_{1}}...B_{i_{d}}\right)\right|\leq\Delta_{N,\epsilon}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(B_{i_{1}}...B_{i_{d}}\right)+2N^{-c},
