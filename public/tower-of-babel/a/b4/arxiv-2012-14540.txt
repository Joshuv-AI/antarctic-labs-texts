Source Identification for Mixtures of Product Distributions
Spencer L. Gordon, Bijan Mazaheri, Yuval Rabani, Leonard J. Schulman

Abstract
We give an algorithm for source identification of a mixture of k k product distributions on n n bits. This is a fundamental problem in machine learning with many applications. Our algorithm identifies the source parameters of an identifiable mixture, given, as input, approximate values of multilinear moments (derived, for instance, from a sufficiently large sample), using 2 O ⁡ ( k 2 ) ​ n O ⁡ ( k ) 2^{O(k^{2})}n^{O(k)} arithmetic operations. Our result is the first explicit bound on the computational complexity of source identification of such mixtures. The running time improves previous results by Feldman, O’Donnell, and Servedio (FOCS 2005) and Chen and Moitra (STOC 2019) that guaranteed only learning the mixture (without parametric identification of the source). Our analysis gives a quantitative version of a qualitative characterization of identifiable sources that is due to Tahmasebi, Motahari, and Maddah-Ali (ISIT 2018).

“Learning” means computing any hypothetical model that generates observable statistics

Clearly, source identification is a more challenging goal that implies also learning.
Source identification is desirable for a variety of reasons. A key reason is the danger

Preliminaries and main theorem

Notation

There is a hidden variable
H
ranging in
[k]
, and
n
binary observable variables
X_{i}
; we write

{\mathbf{m}}_{ij}:=\Pr[X_{i}=1\mid H=j]
.

Vectors are row vectors unless otherwise indicated. For
S\subseteq[n]
define the random variable

X_{S}=\prod_{i\in S}X_{i}
.
We make extensive use of
Hadamard product
for

u=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
,
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})

\displaystyle\odot:\mdmathbb R^{k}\times\mdmathbb R^{k}

\displaystyle\to\mdmathbb R^{k}

\displaystyle u\odot v

\displaystyle=(u_{1}v_{1},\ldots,u_{k}v_{k})

The identity for this product is the all-ones vector
{\mdmathbb{1}}
. We associate with vector

operator
u_{\odot}=\diag(u)
, a

diagonal matrix, so that

v\cdot u_{\odot}=u\odot v.

Let
{\mathbf{m}}_{i}
be the row vector
({\mathbf{m}}_{i1},\ldots,{\mathbf{m}}_{ik})
.
Let
{\mathbf{M}}\in\mdmathbb{R}^{2^{[n]}\times k}
be the matrix with rows indexed by subsets

, with rows

{\mathbf{M}}_{S}={\mathbf{m}}_{i_{1}}\odot{\mathbf{m}}_{i_{2}}\odot\dotsb\odot{\mathbf{m}}_{i_{s}}.

In particular,
{\mathbf{M}}_{\emptyset}={\mdmathbb{1}}
and
{\mathbf{M}}_{\left\{i\right\}}={\mathbf{m}}_{i}
for all

.

Observe that source identification is not possible if
{\mathbf{M}}
has less than full column rank, i.e.,
\operatorname{rank}{\mathbf{M}}<k
, as then the mixing weights cannot be unique.

For a collection of subsets
\mathcal{S}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
, let
{\mathbf{M}}[\mathcal{S}]
denote the restriction of
{\mathbf{M}}
to the rows
{\mathbf{M}}_{S},S\in\mathcal{S}
. E.g.,
{\mathbf{M}}={\mathbf{M}}[2^{[n]}]

The empirical multi-linear moments

For a finite sample drawn from the model, we let
\emom(S)
be the empirical estimate of
\operatorname{\mdmathbb{E}}[X_{S}]
, i.e., the fraction of samples for which
\prod_{i\in S}X_{i}=1
. These
\emom(S)
for
S\subseteq[n]
are the complete list of “observables” of the model. Each converges, in the infinite-sample limit, to the value
\mom(S):=\operatorname{\mdmathbb{E}}[X_{S}]
,

\mom(S)={\mathbf{M}}_{S}\pi^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathbf{M}}_{S}\pi_{\odot}{\mdmathbb{1}}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Comparison to the iid case

The fact that we have access
only
to multi-linear moments is the key constraint of the problem.
Compare with the “power” case, i.e., when we know in advance that all rows
{\mathbf{m}}_{i}
are identical. (That is, the
X_{i}
are iid conditional on
H
.) Then observing bit
X_{2}
alone tells us nothing about
X_{1}
that we do not learn from observing bit
X_{1}
. But observing the product
X_{1}X_{2}
does tell us something new: in fact it is distributed as would be a binary observable whose row had entries
({\mathbf{m}}_{11}^{2},\ldots,{\mathbf{m}}_{1k}^{2})
. With
2k
rows, we obtain the first
2k
moments of the
k
-sparse distribution corresponding to
{\mathbf{m}}_{1}
(i.e., the real-valued,
k
-sparse distribution which places atomic probability
\pi_{j}
at
{\mathbf{m}}_{1j}\in\mdmathbb R
, also called a “
k
-spike” distribution). From this point on, one may apply the time-honored method of Prony to identify the source. For details, a runtime analysis, and further references, see
[
GMRS20
]
.

Much of the interest of the present problem, by contrast, is due precisely to the fact that we
cannot
read out higher moments of the distributions corresponding to any of the bits
X_{i}
, because the various rows do not have any assumed relationship. How to nonetheless obtain higher moments of individual rows, is the challenge our algorithm tackles.

Main theorem

In what follows

.

(

) Given access to the joint statistics of

(

(

Further definitions

Let
V_{(i)}
be the subspace of
\mdmathbb R^{k}
spanned by the
i
’th standard basis vector,
and
P_{(i)}
the projection onto
V_{(i)}
w.r.t. usual inner product (i.e., as a matrix, all

).
For a separated vector
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})

define the Lagrange interpolation polynomials

by
p_{v,i}(v_{j})=\delta_{ij}
. We then have the matrix equation

p_{v,i}(v_{\odot})=P_{(i)}.

Write
p_{v,i}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}p_{v,i,j}x^{j}

\|p_{v,i}\|_{c}=\sum_{0}^{k-1}j|p_{v,i,j}|

It will be very useful to put our observables in matrix form.
Let
S,T\subseteq[n]
be disjoint sets and take any
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{S},\mathcal{B}\subseteq 2^{T}
. Then the matrix

\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}}
is observable (meaning every entry of it is a function of the joint statistics of the observable random variables

), where

\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq{\mathbf{M}}[\mathcal{B}]\pi_{\odot}{\mathbf{M}}[\mathcal{A}]^{\top}.

(1)

Let
{\tilde{\mathbf{C}}}_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\left[\emom(B\cup A)\right]_{B\in\mathcal{B},A\in\mathcal{A}}

We’ll use
\left\|\cdot\right\|
to denote the operator norm for matrices (with respect to the Euclidean norm in the domain and range).

Strategy

If
{\mathbf{m}}
possesses
2k-1
or
3k-3
(depending on the version of the algorithm; this affects only runtime)
\zeta
-separated rows of
{\mathbf{m}}

\zeta

\zeta

The algorithm has the following structure: range over all

\zeta

Any two such runs which do terminate successfully, must result in very close parameter reconstructions.

Solving the power distribution problem

Once we’ve computed

Lemma 25

u\in U
and let
v_{i}=P_{(i)}u
and
u_{i}={\mathcal{P}_{U}}v_{i}={\mathcal{P}_{U}}P_{(i)}u
. Then
since
\sum P_{(i)}=I
,
\sum u_{i}={\mathcal{P}_{U}}\sum P_{(i)}u=u
.

Taking
u={\mdmathbb{1}}/\sqrt{k}\in U
, we find
\left\|v_{i}\right\|^{2}\geq 1/k
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. (On the other hand of course
\left\|v_{i}\right\|\leq 1
.) By assumption
{\varepsilon}\geq\left\|{\mathcal{P}^{\perp}_{U}}P_{(i)}{\mdmathbb{1}}/\sqrt{k}\right\|=\left\|{\mathcal{P}^{\perp}_{U}}v_{i}\right\|=\left\|{\mathcal{P}_{U}}v_{i}-v_{i}\right\|
. So
\left\|{\mathcal{P}_{U}}v_{i}\right\|\geq 1/\sqrt{k}-{\varepsilon}
. For
i\neq j
,

v_{i}^{*}v_{j}=0
, so
\left|({\mathcal{P}_{U}}v_{i})^{*}({\mathcal{P}_{U}}v_{j})\right|=\left|(({\mathcal{P}_{U}}v_{i}-v_{i})+v_{i})^{*}(({\mathcal{P}_{U}}v_{j}-v_{j})+v_{j})\right|\leq\left|v_{i}^{*}v_{j}\right|+2{\varepsilon}+{\varepsilon}^{2}=2{\varepsilon}+{\varepsilon}^{2}
. So we have
k
vectors
{\mathcal{P}_{U}}v_{i}
in
U
, each of length
\geq 1/\sqrt{k}-{\varepsilon}
, with inner products
\leq 2{\varepsilon}+{\varepsilon}^{2}
in absolute value. Their Gram matrix has diagonal entries
\geq(1/\sqrt{k}-{\varepsilon})^{2}\geq 1/k-2{\varepsilon}+{\varepsilon}^{2}
and off-diagonal entries
\leq 2{\varepsilon}+{\varepsilon}^{2}
in absolute value. Since
{\varepsilon}=1/3k^{2}
, this matrix is strictly diagonally dominant and therefore nonsingular, contradicting the assumption that
U
has dimension
<k
.
∎

Lemma 28
.

Let
{\mdmathbb{1}}\in U\subset\mdmathbb R^{k}
. Then

\|{\mathcal{P}^{\perp}_{U}}v_{\odot}\|_{U}>\beta
.

Proof.

By Lemma
27
, there is an
i
s.t.
\left\|{\mathcal{P}^{\perp}_{U}}P_{(i)}\right\|_{U}>1/3k^{2}
. Applying Lemma
26
to this
i
, we have that

\left\|{\mathcal{P}^{\perp}_{U}}v_{\odot}\right\|_{U}\geq\frac{1}{3k^{2}\left\|p_{v,i}\right\|_{c}}
.
Now we need an upper bound on
\left\|p_{v,i}\right\|_{c}
. Recall
p_{v,i}(x)=\frac{\prod_{j\neq i}(x-\lambda_{j})}{\prod_{j\neq i}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})}
. Simply by upper-bounding all
\lambda_{j}
by
1
and lower-bounding all separations by
\zeta
,
we have the bound

\left\|p_{v,i}\right\|_{c}\leq\zeta^{1-k}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}\ell^{\prime}\binom{k-1}{\ell^{\prime}}\leq(k-1)(2/\zeta)^{k-1}
.
∎

Lemma 29
.

Fix
\zeta
-separated vectors
{\mathbf{m}}_{1},\dotsc,{\mathbf{m}}_{k-1}
. Then there exists a
k\times k
matrix
V
, with rows
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{k}
, such that:

1.

The row
v_{1}={\mdmathbb{1}}/\sqrt{k}
, and for
\ell\geq 1
,
v_{\ell+1}\coloneqq{\mathbf{m}}_{\ell}\odot u
, where
u
is a unit vector in
U_{\ell}\coloneqq\Span\left\{v_{1},\dotsc,v_{\ell}\right\}
.

2.

Any unit vector in
U_{\ell}
can be formed as a linear combination of the rows in
[{\mathbf{m}}_{R}]_{R\subseteq[\ell]}
with coefficients bounded in maximum magnitude by
\beta^{-\ell}
.

Proof.

We’ll use induction over
\ell
. For
\ell>1
, we’ll apply Lemma
28
to find a unit vector
u^{*}\in U_{\ell-1}
such that
\left\|{\mathcal{P}^{\perp}_{U}}({\mathbf{m}}_{\ell}\odot u^{*})\right\|>\beta
. We’ll set
v_{\ell}\coloneqq{\mathbf{m}}_{\ell}\odot u^{*}
. Then
U_{\ell}=\Span(U_{\ell-1},{\mathcal{P}^{\perp}_{U}}({\mathbf{m}}_{\ell}\odot u^{*}))
.
Now
\left\|{\mathbf{m}}_{\ell}\odot u^{*}\right\|\leq 1
. Any vector
u\in U_{\ell}
can be written as
c_{1}u^{(\ell-1)}+c_{2}({\mathbf{m}}_{\ell}\odot u^{*})
for
u^{(\ell-1)}\in U_{\ell-1}
a unit vector and where
\left|c_{2}\right|\leq\beta^{-1}
and

\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}&\beta\end{bmatrix}

By the induction hypothesis, we can expand
u^{(\ell-1)}
and
u^{*}
in terms of
{{\mathbf{m}}_{S}}_{R\subseteq[\ell-1]}
as follows:

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=c_{1}u^{(\ell-1)}+c_{2}({\mathbf{m}}_{\ell}\odot u^{*})

\displaystyle=c_{1}\sum_{R\subseteq[\ell-1]}\alpha_{R}{\mathbf{m}}_{R}+c_{2}{\mathbf{m}}_{\ell}\sum_{R\subseteq[\ell-1]}\alpha^{\prime}_{R}{\mathbf{m}}_{R}

\displaystyle=\sum_{R\subseteq[\ell-1]}\left(c_{1}\alpha_{R}{\mathbf{m}}_{R}+c_{2}\alpha^{\prime}_{R}{\mathbf{m}}_{R\cup\left\{\ell\right\}}\right)

where
\left\|\alpha\right\|_{\infty},\left\|\alpha^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\beta^{\ell-1}

∎

Corollary 30
.

For any unit column vector

satisfies
\left\|{\mathbf{M}}[2^{S}]z\right\|_{\infty}\geq\beta^{k}2^{-k}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
29
, we know that we can write
z^{\mathsf{T}}=\lambda^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbf{M}}
where
\lambda\in\mdmathbb{R}^{2^{k}}
and
\left\|\lambda\right\|_{\infty}\leq\beta^{-k}
. Thus,
1=\left\|z\right\|^{2}=\sum_{R\subseteq S}\lambda_{R}{\mathbf{m}}_{R}z
. There must be some
R\subseteq S
for which
\left|\lambda_{R}{\mathbf{m}}_{R}z\right|\geq 1/2^{k}
. Since
\left|\lambda_{R}\right|\leq\beta^{-k}
we immediately get that
\left\|{\mathbf{M}}z\right\|_{\infty}\geq\left|{\mathbf{m}}_{R}z\right|\geq\beta^{k}2^{-k}
.
∎

Appendix A

Lemma 33
.

For an invertible
n\times n
matrix
M
and a perturbed matrix
\tilde{M}
, if
\left\|\tilde{M}-M\right\|=\varepsilon\leq\sigma_{n}(M)/2
, then

\left\|\tilde{M}^{-1}-M^{-1}\right\|\leq 2\left\|M^{-1}\right\|^{2}\varepsilon,\quad\text{ and }\left\|\tilde{M}^{-1}\right\|\leq 2\left\|M^{-1}\right\|.

Proof.

First, we observe that

\left\|\tilde{M}^{-1}\right\|=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}(\tilde{M})}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}(M)-\sigma_{n}(M)/2}\leq 2\left\|M^{-1}\right\|.

We use the identity
\tilde{M}^{-1}-M^{-1}=\tilde{M}^{-1}\left(M-\tilde{M}\right)M^{-1}
.

\left\|\tilde{M}^{-1}-M^{-1}\right\|=\left\|\tilde{M}^{-1}\left(M-\tilde{M}\right)M^{-1}\right\|\leq 2\left\|M^{-1}\right\|^{2}\left\|M-\tilde{M}\right\|.

∎
