High-Dimensional Sparse Single-Index Regression Via Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion
Runxiong Wu, Chang Deng, Xin Chen

Abstract
Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) has recently been used in the field of single-index models to estimate the directions. Compared with some other well-established methods, it requires relatively weaker conditions. However, its performance has not yet been studied in the high-dimensional scenario, where the number of covariates is much larger than the sample size. In this article, we propose a new efficient sparse estimate in HSIC based single-index model. This new method estimates the subspace spanned by the linear combinations of the covariates directly and performs variable selection simultaneously. Due to the non-convexity of the objective function, we use a majorize-minimize approach together with the linearized alternating direction method of multipliers algorithm to solve the optimization problem. The algorithm does not involve the inverse of the covariance matrix and therefore can handle the large p p small n n scenario naturally. Through extensive simulation studies and a real data analysis, we show our proposal is efficient and effective in the high-dimensional setting. The 𝙼𝚊𝚝𝚕𝚊𝚋 \verb|Matlab| codes for this method are available online.

1
Introduction

Let
Y\in\mathbb{R}
be an univariate response and
{\bf X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a
p\times 1
predictor, the single-index model as a practically useful generalization of the classical linear regression model considers the following problem

Y=g({\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf X},\epsilon),

(1.1)

where
{\bm{\beta}}
is a
p\times 1
vector,
\epsilon
is an unknown random error independent of
{\bf X}
, and
g
is a link function. Let
\mbox{span}({\bm{\beta}})
denote the subspace spanned by
{\bm{\beta}}
, the goal of the single-index model is to estimate
\mbox{span}({\bm{\beta}})
without specifying or estimating the link function
g
. To our best knowledge,
Li and Duan 1989
firstly studied this problem and proposed to estimate the
\mbox{span}({\bm{\beta}})
under the linear condition that
E({\bf X}|{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf X})
is a linear function of
{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf X}
. This linear condition applies to the marginal distribution of
{\bf X}
and is common in regression modeling.

Later,
Cook 1994
;
Cook 1998
introduced sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) expanding the concept of the single-index model. It aims to find the minimal subspace
{\cal S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
Y\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}{\bf X}|P_{{\cal S}}{\bf X}
, where
\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}
stands for independence and
P_{{\cal S}}
stands for the projection operator to the subspace
{\cal S}
. Under mild conditions
(
Cook 1996
;
Yin et al. 2008
)
, such a subspace exists and is unique. We call it the central subspace, denote it by
{\cal S}_{Y|{\bf X}}
and call its dimension
d=\mbox{dim}({\cal S}_{Y|{\bf X}})
, which is often far less than
p
. When the central subspace is one dimensional or in other words
d=1
, the caused regression problem is the single-index model (
1.1
). There are many methods proposed to estimate the central subspace
(
Li 1991
;
Cook and Weisberg 1991
;
Xia et al. 2002
;
Cook and Ni 2005
;
Zhu and Zeng 2006
;
Li and Wang 2007
;
Wang and Xia 2008
;
Cook and Forzani 2009
;
Zeng and Zhu 2010
;
Yin and Li 2011
;
Ma and Zhu 2012
)
. For a comprehensive list of references about SDR methods, please refer to
Ma and Zhu 2013
.

Unfortunately, one drawback of the dimension reduction methods mentioned above is that the estimated linear combinations still contain all the original predictors, which often makes it difficult to interpret the extracted components. To improve interpretability, numerous attempts have been made to perform variable selection and dimension reduction simultaneously, including
Cook 2004
;
Ni et al. 2005
;
Li et al. 2005
;
Li 2007
;
Li and Yin 2008
;
Chen et al. 2010
. These methods perform well when the number of covariates
p
is less than the sample size denoted by
n
, but don’t work under the scenario
p>n
. To tackle the difficulty,
Yin and Hilafu 2015
proposed sequential procedures in SDR and
Lin et al. 2018
proposed high-dimensional sparse sliced inverse regression (SIR). Moreover,
Wang et al. 2018
introduced a reduced-rank regression method for estimating the sparse directions, and
Tan et al. 2018b
proposed a convex formulation for fitting sparse SIR in high dimensions. Other recent high-dimensional SDR methods can be seen in
Qian et al. 2019
and
Tan et al. 2020
.

In this article, following the work of
Zhang and Yin 2015
and
Tan et al. 2018b
, we develop a new approach using Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) for single-index models. The proposed method can handle the scenario
p>n
and require the weakest conditions among the existing high-dimensional sparse SDR methods. The key idea is to formulate the HSIC based single-index model in a form of estimating the orthogonal projection
{\bm{\beta}}{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}
onto the subspace span(
{\bm{\beta}}
) rather than span(
{\bm{\beta}}
), with the constraints of the nuclear norm and the operator norm to relax the normalization constraint. Moreover, our proposal uses a lasso penalty on the orthogonal projection
{\bm{\beta}}{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}
to encourage the estimated solution to be sparse. To sum up, the main contributions of our work are as follows. First, our method extends the HSIC-based single-index regression
(
Zhang and Yin 2015
)
to a sufficient variable selection method. Since it does not involve the inversion of the sample covariance matrix, it can naturally handle a large
p
small
n
situation. Second, motivated by the majorization-minimization principle, we design a fast and efficient algorithm to solve the problem. The objective function of our method is non-linear, so the algorithm in this article is more complicated and tricky than the algorithm in
Tan et al. 2018b
. Third,
Tan et al. 2018b
proposed a cross-validation scheme based on the idea of
Cook and Forzani 2008
to select the tuning parameters. Their method requires that the distribution of
{\bf X}|Y
follows normal distribution, while our method apply a kernel method to estimate the link function which perfectly avoid this assumption. Last but not least, we can easily extend our method to situations where the response is multivariate.

The article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the background of HSIC-based single-index method and Section 3 details our proposed method. In Section 4, we conduct extensive simulation studies and a real data analysis. A short conclusion and some technical proofs are provided in Section 5 and Appendix.

) relative to the inner product

and

, where

is the identity matrix. The trace of a matrix

is tr(

) and the Euclidean inner product of two matrices

, is

2
Overview of HSIC-based Single-Index Regression

Gretton et al. 2005a
;
Gretton et al. 2007
;
Gretton et al. 2009
proposed an independence criterion termed the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion to detect statistically significant dependence between two random variables. For univariate
X
and
Y
, HSIC denoted by
H(X,Y)
has a population expression

\begin{split}H(X,Y)=&E\left[K(X-X^{\prime})L(Y-Y^{\prime})\right]+E\left[K(X-X^{\prime})\right]E\left[L(Y-Y^{\prime})\right]\\
&-2E\left\{E\left[K(X-X^{\prime})|X\right]E\left[L(Y-Y^{\prime})|Y\right]\right\},\end{split}

(2.1)

where
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
denote independent copies of
X
and
Y
, and
K(\cdot)
and
L(\cdot)
are
positive definite kernel functions. The definition of HSIC exists when the various expectations over the kernels are finite, which is true as long as the kernels
K(\cdot)
and
L(\cdot)
are bounded. One often used kernel is a Gaussian kernel
(
Kankainen 1995
, see)
, i.e.,

K:=\exp\left(\frac{-\|X-X^{\prime}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{X}^{2}}\right)\mbox{ and }L:=\exp\left(\frac{-\|Y-Y^{\prime}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\right).

Moreover,
Feuerverger 1993
showed that the statistic is equivalent to the characteristic function-based statistic when the Gaussian kernel choice is adopted.
Throughout the article, we present our method using the Gaussian kernel, however, our method can be extended to other kernel choices without much issue.

According to
Gretton et al. 2005b
, HSIC equals
0
if and only if two random variables are independent, which makes it possible for its application in the field of SDR. Indeed, under a mild condition,
Zhang and Yin 2015
showed that solving
(\ref{eqn2.2})
with respect to a
p\times 1
vector
{\bm{\beta}}
would yield a basis of
{\cal S}_{Y|{\bf X}}
, or in other words, the single-index direction:

\underset{{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf\Sigma}{\bm{\beta}}=1}{\max}\,H({\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf X},Y),

(2.2)

where
{{\bf\Sigma}}
denotes the covariance matrix of
{\bf X}
. Note that solving
(\ref{eqn2.2})
may not have a unique solution in terms of
{\bm{\beta}}
, but we are interested only in span(
{\bm{\beta}}
), which is unique as shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that the support of
{\bf X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a compact set, and that
{\bm{\eta}}
spans the central subspace such that
{\bm{\eta}}^{\top}{\bf\Sigma}{\bm{\eta}}=1
. If
P^{\top}_{{\bm{\eta}}({\bf\Sigma})}{\bf X}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Q^{\top}_{{\bm{\eta}}({\bf\Sigma})}{\bf X}
, then any result
{\bm{\beta}}
of solving
(\ref{eqn2.2})
satisfies span(
{\bm{\beta}}
)=span(
{\bm{\eta}}
).

Let
\left({\bf X},{\bf Y}\right)=\left\{({\bf X}_{i},Y_{i}):i=1,\ldots,n\right\}
be a random sample of
n
i.i.d. random vectors
({\bf X},Y)
, and
\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{Y}
be the sample covariance matrix and sample variance of
{\bf X}
and
Y
, respectively. The corresponding sample version of
H({\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf X},Y)
, denoted by
H_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf X},{\bf Y})
, is a sum of three U-statistics
(
Serfling 1980
;
Gretton et al. 2007
, see)
:

H_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf X},{\bf Y})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}K_{ij}({\bm{\beta}})L_{ij}-\frac{2}{n^{3}}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n}K_{ij}({\bm{\beta}})L_{ik}+\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{i,j,k,l=1}^{n}K_{ij}({\bm{\beta}})L_{kl},

(2.3)

where

K_{ij}({\bm{\beta}}):=\exp\left(\frac{-({\bm{\beta}}^{\top}({\bf X}_{i}-{\bf X}_{j}))^{2}}{2{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}{\bm{\beta}}}\right)\mbox{ and }L_{kl}:=\exp\left(\frac{-\|Y_{k}-Y_{l}\|^{2}}{2\hat{\sigma}_{Y}^{2}}\right).

In later sections, we will utilize the equivalent form
(
Gretton et al. 2007
;
Wu and Chen 2021
, see)
, obtained by replacing the U-statistics with V-statistics

H_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf X},{\bf Y})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mbox{tr}({\bf K}{\bf H}{\bf L}{\bf H})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}K_{ij}({\bm{\beta}})\tilde{L}_{ij}

(2.4)

rather than Equation
(\ref{eqn2.3})
, where
{\bf K}
and
{\bf L}
are the
n\times n
matrix with entries
K_{ij}({\bm{\beta}})
and
L_{ij}
respectively,
\displaystyle{\bf H}={\bf I}-\frac{1}{n}{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}
, and
{\bf 1}
is a
n\times 1
vector of ones. Here,
\tilde{L}_{ij}
denotes the
(i,j)
-th entry of the product matrix
{\bf H}{\bf L}{\bf H}
. The estimator of a basis for the central subspace
{\cal S}_{Y|{\bf X}}
is

{\bm{\eta}}_{n}=\underset{{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}{\bm{\beta}}=1}{\arg\max}\,H_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf X},{\bf Y}).

(2.5)

Then, the central subspace is estimated as span(
{\bm{\eta}}_{n}
) and the sufficient dimension reduced variable is
{\bm{\eta}}_{n}^{\top}{\bf X}
. The following proposition characterizes the asymptotic properties of the estimator
{\bm{\eta}}_{n}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Under the assumptions in Proposition 1, if
{\bm{\eta}}_{n}=\underset{{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}{\bm{\beta}}=1}{\arg\max}\,H_{n}({\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf X},{\bf Y})
, then
{\bm{\eta}}_{n}
converges in probability to
c{\bm{\eta}}
as
n\to\infty
, where
c=1
or
c=-1
. Furthermore, under some other regularity conditions,
\sqrt{n}({\bm{\eta}}_{n}-c{\bm{\eta}})\to N(0,{\bf V}_{11})
, where
{\bf V}_{11}
is a covariance matrix.

For details about the regularity conditions and the specific form of
{\bf V}_{11}
, please refer to
Zhang and Yin 2015
and its online supplementary material.

3
Method

3.1
Problem Formulation

Let
{\bf\Pi}={\bm{\beta}}{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}
, the HSIC-based single-index regression procedure (
2.5
) can be rewritten as the following minimization problem:

\begin{split}&\underset{{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal M}}{\rm min}\,-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\langle{\bf\Pi},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\right)\tilde{L}_{ij},\\
&\mbox{ s.t. }\quad\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}\in{\cal B},\end{split}

(3.1)

where
{\bf Z}_{ij}=({\bf X}_{i}-{\bf X}_{j})({\bf X}_{i}-{\bf X}_{j})^{\top}
,
{\cal B}=\left\{\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}:{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}{\bm{\beta}}=1\right\}
, and
{\cal M}
is the set of
p\times p
symmetric semi-definite positive matrices. In this new formulation, our focus is changed to directly estimate the orthogonal projection
{\bf\Pi}
onto the subspace instead of estimating the basis
{\bm{\beta}}
.

In high dimensional SDR, it is often true that only a few elements of
{\bf X}
are informative and we would like to select these variables only. To achieve this goal,
Tan et al. 2018b
introduces the notion of subspace sparsity and imposes a lasso penalty on all elements of
{\bf\Pi}
to encourage such sparsity. Moreover, they utilize the nuclear norm and the spectral norm to relax the constraint. Following the work of them, we propose the sparse estimate by solving

\begin{split}&\underset{{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal M}}{\rm min}\,-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\langle{\bf\Pi},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\right)\tilde{L}_{ij}+\lambda\|{\bf\Pi}\|_{1},\\
&\mbox{ s.t. }\quad{\rm tr}(\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2})\leq 1,\end{split}

(3.2)

where
\lambda
is a tunning parameter. Note that we only consider the dimension of the central subspace to be 1, so there is no need to impose spectral norm constraint. More similar work can be seen in sparse principal component analysis, canonical correlation analysis, and sliced inverse regression
(
Vu et al. 2013
;
Gao et al. 2017
;
Tan et al. 2018a
;
Tan et al. 2018b
;
Tan et al. 2020
)
. In addition, when the kernel is the product kernel, we can naturally extend the method to settings where the response is multivariate. That is, for a
q
-dimensional response
{\bf Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{q})^{\top}
, we use the product kernel:

L:=\prod_{i=1}^{q}\exp\left(\frac{-|Y_{i}-Y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}|^{2}}{2\sigma_{Y_{i}}^{2}}\right),

where
{\bf Y}^{\prime}=(Y_{1}^{{}^{\prime}},\ldots,Y_{q}^{{}^{\prime}})^{\top}
is an independent copy of
{\bf Y}
.

3.2
Computation

In this subsection, we propose an efficient optimization algorithm for solving the problem
(\ref{eqn3.2})
. Let
f({\bf\Pi})
denote the objective function of the problems
(\ref{eqn3.1})
. Although
f({\bf\Pi})
is not convex, it is differentiable and has Lipschitz continuous gradient over the bounded convex set. We state properties about the objective function
f({\bf\Pi})
in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

f({\bf\Pi})
is differentiable and its derivative function is

\nabla f({\bf\Pi})=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\langle{\bf\Pi},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\right)\tilde{L}_{ij}{\bf Z}_{ij},

(3.3)

or equivalently,

\nabla f({\bf\Pi})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\bf X}^{\top}\left({\rm diag}({\bf C}{\bm{1}}_{n})-{\bf C}\right){\bf X},

(3.4)

where
{\bf C}
is a
n\times n
matrix with the entry
\displaystyle c_{ij}=\exp\left(-\frac{\langle{\bf\Pi},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\right)\tilde{L}_{ij}
and
{\bf X}=[{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}]^{\top}
. Moreover,
\nabla f({\bf\Pi})
is Lipschitz over the set
{\cal D}=\left\{{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal M},{\rm tr}(\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2})\leq 1\right\}
.

We prove the Proposition
3.1
in the Appendix.

Remark 1
.

It is worth noting that we would like to use the expression form
(\ref{eqn3.4})
instead of
(\ref{eqn3.3})
when actually calculating the derivative function
\nabla f({\bf\Pi})
. Plus, the Lipschitz continuity property of
f({\bf\Pi})

motivates us to design a method for performing the optimization in this work from the viewpoint of the majorization-minimization principle
(
Lange et al. 2000
;
Hunter and Lange 2004
)
.

Since the objective function
f({\bf\Pi})
has a Lipschitz continuous gradient over the bounded set
{\cal D}
, there exists a positive constant
L<\infty
such that

f({\bf\Pi})\leq f(\tilde{{\bf\Pi}})+\langle{\bf\Pi}-\tilde{{\bf\Pi}},\nabla f(\tilde{{\bf\Pi}})\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|{\bf\Pi}-\tilde{{\bf\Pi}}\|_{\rm F}^{2},

(3.5)

for all
{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal D}
and
\tilde{{\bf\Pi}}\in{\cal D}
. Thus, the right hand side of (
3.5
) is a majorizing
function of
f({\bf\Pi})
at
{\bf\Pi}
(i.e., the right hand side of (
3.5
) is greater than or equal to
f({\bf\Pi})
for all
{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal D}
with equality when
{\bf\Pi}=\tilde{{\bf\Pi}}
). This suggests the following majorize-minimize (MM) iteration to solve the problem (
3.2
):

\displaystyle{\bf\Pi}^{(r+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal D}}{\arg\min}\,\left\{f({\bf\Pi}^{(r)})+\langle{\bf\Pi}-{\bf\Pi}^{(r)},\nabla f({\bf\Pi}^{(r)})\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|{\bf\Pi}-{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf\Pi}\|_{1}\right\},

(3.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal D}}{\arg\min}\,\frac{L}{2}\Big\|{\bf\Pi}-\left[{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f({\bf\Pi}^{(r)})\right]\Big\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf\Pi}\|_{1},

where
{\bf\Pi}^{(r+1)}
and
{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}
are the
(r+1)
-th and
r
-th iterates of the optimization variable corresponding to
{\bf\Pi}
, respectively. By the property
(\ref{eqn3.5})
, we can easily obtain

f({\bf\Pi}^{(r+1)})+\lambda\|{\bf\Pi}^{(r+1)}\|_{1}\leq f({\bf\Pi}^{(r)})+\lambda\|{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}\|_{1}\mbox{ for all }r,

which means that iterates generated from the algorithm are guaranteed to monotonically decrease the objective function value.

Hunter and Lange 2004
showed the sequence
\left\{{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}\right\}_{r\geq 0}
obtained by the iterative formula
(\ref{eqn3.6})
converges to a critical point of the problem
(\ref{eqn3.2})
. The MM algorithm is a well-applicable and simple algorithmic framework for solving such problems. The key challenge in making the proposed algorithm efficient numerically lies in solving the subproblem
(\ref{eqn3.6})
.

The subproblem
(\ref{eqn3.6})
is a quadratic problem with the convex constraint, so any local minimum can be guaranteed to be a global minimum. We employ the linearized alternating direction method of multipliers algorithm
(
Zhang et al. 2011
;
Wang and Yuan 2012
;
Yang and Yuan 2013
, L-ADMM,)
to solve it. This algorithm can allow us to tackle the difficult caused by the interaction between the penalty term and the constraints. We give the derivation details of solving the subproblem
(\ref{eqn3.6})
through this algorithm in the Appendix. In practice, we find that this algorithm can solve the subproblem quite efficiently.

3.3
Tuning Parameter Selection

The tuning parameter
\lambda
in our proposed method determines the sparsity level of the estimate.
Tan et al. 2018b
proposed a cross-validation approach based on the framework of principal fitted components
(
Cook and Forzani 2008
, PFC,)
to select the corresponding sparsity tuning parameter. However, the PFC method requires that the distribution of
{\bf X}|Y
should be normally distributed, which may not be suitable in the real application. To avoid the assumption, we use the Nadaraya-Watson kernel method to estimate the conditional expectation
E(Y|{\bf X})
. Let
\hat{{\bf\Pi}}
be an estimate of the orthogonal projection
{\bf\Pi}
, the sufficient dimension direction estimator
\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}
is estimated by the top eigenvector of
\hat{{\bf\Pi}}
. Given a new data
{\bf X}^{*}
, the Nadaraya-Watson kernel estimator of conditional mean
E(Y|{\bf X}={\bf X}^{*})
is

\hat{E}(Y|{\bf X}={\bf X}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{K_{h}(\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}^{\top}({\bf X}^{*}-{\bf X}_{i}))}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{h}(\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}^{\top}({\bf X}^{*}-{\bf X}_{j}))}Y_{i},

(3.7)

where
K_{h}
is a kernel with a bandwidth
h
. In this article, we use a Gaussian kernel and take the leave-one-out estimate for bandwidth selection. Note that there is a trick to compute the cross-validation function with a single fit. This trick vastly reduces the computational complexity, at the price of the increasing memory consumption. For specific details, please refer to
Fan and Gijbels 1996
.

We then construct an M-fold cross-validation procedure based on
(\ref{eqn3.7})
to select the tuning parameter
\lambda
. Suppose
C_{1},\ldots,C_{M}
are
M
equally sized and mutually disjoint subsamples of the whole dataset. The cross-validation procedure utilizes each single subsample be the test data, and the remaining
M-1
subsamples be the training data. For each fixed tuning parameter
\lambda
, the corresponding overall prediction error is computed as
\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i\in C_{m}}\left\{Y_{i}-\hat{E}(Y|{\bf X}={\bf X}_{i})\right\}^{2}/(M|C_{m}|)
where
|C_{m}|
denotes the cardinality of the set
C_{m}
. Finally, we choose the tuning parameter which minimizes the prediction error.

5
Conclusion

In this article, we extend the HSIC based SDR method of
Zhang and Yin 2015
to handle a large
p
and small
n
scenario by borrowing the idea from
Tan et al. 2018b
. The proposed method estimates the basis of the central subspace and performs sufficient variable selection simultaneously. Compared with other high-dimensional sparse SDR methods, our proposed method requires the weakest conditions so far. It enjoys a model free property and requires very mild conditions on
{\bf X}
and no particular assumption on
Y|{\bf X}
, or
{\bf X}|Y
. The simulation studies showed that our method is highly efficient and stable in both
n>p
and
n<p
scenarios.

There are several possible prospects for future research. It may be of interest to extend this idea to multiple-index models, which is not trivial since it needs a new algorithm design. Moreover, the current computational bottleneck for our proposed method is on solving the majorization step, which has a computational complexity of
O(p^{3})
per iteration. Thus, it will be also interesting to redesign a highly efficient algorithm such that our proposed method is scalable to accommodate large-scale data. Finally, the asymptotic properties for our method are deserved to discuss in the future which are not covered in this article.

Appendix A
Some technical derivations

A.1
Proof of Proposition
3.1
:

We first compute the gradient function
\nabla f({\bf\Pi})
.
Recalling the definition of
f({\bf\Pi})
, we directly have

\nabla f({\bf\Pi})=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\langle{\bf\Pi},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\right)\tilde{L}_{ij}{\bf Z}_{ij}.

Let us define a matrix
{\bf C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\displaystyle c_{ij}=\exp\left(-\frac{\langle{\bf\Pi},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\right)\tilde{L}_{ij}
and
{\bf X}=[{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{\bf X}_{n}]^{\top}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla f({\bf\Pi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{ij}{\bf Z}_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{ij}({\bf X}_{i}-{\bf X}_{j})({\bf X}_{i}-{\bf X}_{j})^{\top}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{ij}\left({\bf X}_{i}{\bf X}_{i}^{\top}+{\bf X}_{j}{\bf X}_{j}^{\top}-{\bf X}_{i}{\bf X}_{j}^{\top}-{\bf X}_{j}{\bf X}_{i}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{ij}\left({\bf X}_{i}{\bf X}_{i}^{\top}-{\bf X}_{i}{\bf X}_{j}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}{\bf X}^{\top}\left(\mbox{diag}({\bf C}{\bm{1}}_{n})-{\bf C}\right){\bf X},

which establishes the first part of Proposition
3.1
. Next, we prove the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f({\bf\Pi})
over the bounded set
{\cal D}=\left\{{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal M},{\rm tr}(\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2})\leq 1\right\}
. For any
{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal D}
and
\tilde{{\bf\Pi}}\in{\cal D}
, by the triangle inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla f({\bf\Pi})-\nabla f(\tilde{{\bf\Pi}})\|_{\rm F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\langle{\bf\Pi},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\right)\tilde{L}_{ij}{\bf Z}_{ij}-\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\langle\tilde{{\bf\Pi}},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\right)\tilde{L}_{ij}{\bf Z}_{ij}\|_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|\tilde{L}_{ij}|\|{\bf Z}_{ij}\|_{\rm F}\Big|\exp\left(-\frac{\langle{\bf\Pi},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{\langle\tilde{{\bf\Pi}},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|\tilde{L}_{ij}|\|{\bf Z}_{ij}\|_{\rm F}\Big|\frac{\langle{\bf\Pi}-\tilde{{\bf\Pi}},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle}{2}\Big|,

where the last inequality holds since
|e^{x}-e^{y}|\leq|x-y|
, for any
y\leq x\leq 0
. Further by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we know
|\langle{\bf\Pi}-\tilde{{\bf\Pi}},{\bf Z}_{ij}\rangle|\leq\|{\bf\Pi}-\tilde{{\bf\Pi}}\|_{\rm F}\|{\bf Z}_{ij}\|_{\rm F}
. Thus, we finally get

\displaystyle\|\nabla f({\bf\Pi})-\nabla f(\tilde{{\bf\Pi}})\|_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|\tilde{L}_{ij}|\|{\bf Z}_{ij}\|_{\rm F}^{2}\|{\bf\Pi}-\tilde{{\bf\Pi}}\|_{\rm F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|\tilde{L}_{ij}|\|{\bf Z}_{ij}\|_{\rm F}^{2}}{4n^{2}}\|{\bf\Pi}-\tilde{{\bf\Pi}}\|_{\rm F},

where
\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|\tilde{L}_{ij}|\|{\bf Z}_{ij}\|_{\rm F}^{2}}{4n^{2}}
is constant which verifies the claim.

A.2
Linearized Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers Algorithm for Solving
(\ref{eqn3.6})

To implement the linearized alternating direction method of multipliers algorithm, we rewrite the subproblem in formula
(\ref{eqn3.6})
as

\begin{split}&\underset{{\bf\Pi},{\bf H}\in{\cal M}}{\min}\;\frac{L}{2}\Big\|{\bf\Pi}-\left[{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f({\bf\Pi}^{(r)})\right]\Big\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf\Pi}\|_{1}+\infty\cdot{\mathbb{I}}_{({\rm tr}({\bf H})>1)},\\
&\mbox{ s.t. }\quad\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}={\bf H}.\end{split}

This is also equivalent to minimize the following scaled augmented Lagrangian function,

\begin{split}{\cal L}_{\rho}({\bf\Pi},{\bf H},{\bf\Gamma})=&\frac{L}{2}\Big\|{\bf\Pi}-\left[{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f({\bf\Pi}^{(r)})\right]\Big\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf\Pi}\|_{1}+\infty\cdot{\mathbb{I}}_{({\rm tr}({\bf H})>1)}\\
&+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}-{\bf H}+{\bf\Gamma}\|_{\rm F}^{2},\end{split}

where
\rho
is a small constant and
{\bf\Gamma}
is the dual variable. The L-ADMM minimizes the augmented Lagrangian function by alternatively solving one block of variables at a time. In particular, to update
{\bf\Pi}
at the
j
-th iteration, we need to minimize

\frac{L}{2}\Big\|{\bf\Pi}-\left[{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f({\bf\Pi}^{(r)})\right]\Big\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf\Pi}\|_{1}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}-{\bf H}_{j}+{\bf\Gamma}_{j}\|_{\rm F}^{2},

where
{\bf H}_{j}
and
{\bf\Gamma}_{j}
are the
j
-th estimates of
{\bf H}
and
{\bf\Gamma}
respectively. However, there is no closed-form solution for the above minimization problem. To tackle the difficulty,
Fang et al. 2015
proposed to linearize the quadratic term in the above problem by applying a second-order Taylor Expansion. Following the work of them, we obtain the update for
{\bf\Pi}
:

\begin{split}{\bf\Pi}_{j+1}=\underset{{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal M}}{\arg\min}\;&\frac{L}{2}\Big\|{\bf\Pi}-\left[{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f({\bf\Pi}^{(r)})\right]\Big\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf\Pi}\|_{1}\\
&+\rho\langle{\bf\Pi}-{\bf\Pi}_{j},\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}{\bf\Pi}_{j}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}-\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}({\bf H}_{j}-{\bf\Gamma}_{j})\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}\rangle+\frac{\tau}{2}\|{\bf\Pi}-{\bf\Pi}_{j}\|_{\rm F}^{2}.\end{split}

As suggested by
Fang et al. 2015
, we pick
\tau\geq 4\rho\lambda_{\rm max}^{2}(\hat{{\bf\Sigma}})
to ensure the convergence of the linearized alternating direction method of multipliers algorithm. The above iterate can be written in the more familiar notation:

\begin{split}{\bf\Pi}_{j+1}=\underset{{\bf\Pi}\in{\cal M}}{\arg\min}\;&\frac{L+\tau}{2}\Big\|{\bf\Pi}-\Big(\frac{\tau}{L+\tau}\left[{\bf\Pi}_{j}-\frac{\rho}{\tau}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}{\bf\Pi}_{j}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}+\frac{\rho}{\tau}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}({\bf H}_{j}-{\bf\Gamma}_{j})\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}\right]\\
&+\frac{L}{L+\tau}\left[{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}-\frac{\nabla f({\bf\Pi}^{(r)})}{L}\right]\Big)\Big\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\lambda\|{\bf\Pi}\|_{1}\end{split}

which has the closed-form solution

{\bf\Pi}_{j+1}={\rm Soft}\left(\frac{\tau}{L+\tau}\left[{\bf\Pi}_{j}-\frac{\rho}{\tau}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}{\bf\Pi}_{j}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}+\frac{\rho}{\tau}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}({\bf H}_{j}-{\bf\Gamma}_{j})\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}\right]+\frac{L}{L+\tau}\left[{\bf\Pi}^{(r)}-\frac{\nabla f({\bf\Pi}^{(r)})}{L}\right],\frac{\lambda}{L+\tau}\right),

where Soft is the element-wise soft-thresholding to a matrix:
{\rm Soft}(A_{ij},b)={\rm sign}(A_{ij})\max(|A_{ij}|-b,0)
. Next, the update of
{\bf H}
can be obtained as

{\bf H}_{j+1}=\underset{{\bf H}\in{\cal M},{\rm tr}({\bf H})\leq 1}{\arg\min}\;\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf H}-(\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}_{j+1}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}+{\bf\Gamma}_{j})\|_{\rm F}^{2},

which has a closed-form solution according to the following proposition.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
{\cal F}=\left\{{\bf H}\in{\cal M}:{\rm tr}({\bf H})\leq 1\right\}
and
\displaystyle P_{{\cal F}}({\bf W})=\underset{{\bf H}\in{\cal F}}{\arg\min}\;\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf H}-{\bf W}\|_{\rm F}^{2}.
If
{\bf W}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\omega_{i}\bf{u}_{i}\bf{u}_{i}^{\top}
is a spectral decomposition of
\bf{W}
, then
P_{{\cal F}}({\bf W})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\omega_{i}-\theta^{*})_{+}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}
, where
(\omega_{i}-\theta)_{+}=\max(\omega_{i}-\theta,0)
and
\theta^{*}
is the minimum value satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{p}(\omega_{i}-\theta)_{+}\leq 1.

The above proposition follows directly from Lemma 4.1 in
Vu et al. 2013
, Proposition 10.2 in
Gao et al. 2017
, and Proposition 1 in the Appendix of
Tan et al. 2018b
. Thus, by Proposition
A.1
, we have

{\bf H}_{j+1}=P_{{\cal F}}(\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}_{j+1}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}+{\bf\Gamma}_{j}).

Finally, we update the dual variable by

{\bf\Gamma}_{j+1}={\bf\Gamma}_{j}+\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}{\bf\Pi}_{j+1}\hat{{\bf\Sigma}}^{1/2}-{\bf H}_{j+1}.
