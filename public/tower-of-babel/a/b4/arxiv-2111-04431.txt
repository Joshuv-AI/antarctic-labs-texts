Inverting the discrete curl operator: a novel graph algorithm to find a vector potential of a given vector field Journal: Journal of L a T e X Templates
Silvano Pitassi, Riccardo Ghiloni, Ruben Specogna

Abstract
We provide a novel framework to compute a discrete vector potential of a given discrete vector field on arbitrary polyhedral meshes. The framework exploits the concept of acyclic matching, a combinatorial tool at the core of discrete Morse theory. We introduce the new concept of complete acyclic matchings and we show that they give the same end result of Gaussian elimination. Basically, instead of doing costly row and column operations on a sparse matrix, we compute equivalent cheap combinatorial operations that preserve the underlying sparsity structure. Currently, the most efficient algorithms proposed in literature to find discrete vector potentials make use of tree-cotree techniques. We show that they compute a special type of complete acyclic matchings. Moreover, we show that the problem of computing them is equivalent to the problem of deciding whether a given mesh has a topological property called collapsibility . This fact gives a topological characterization of well-known termination problems of tree-cotree techniques. We propose a new recursive algorithm to compute discrete vector potentials. It works directly on basis elements of 1 1 - and 2 2 -chains by performing elementary Gaussian operations on them associated with acyclic matchings. However, the main novelty is that it can be applied recursively. Indeed, the recursion process allows us to sidetrack termination problems of the standard tree-cotree techniques. We tested the algorithm on pathological triangulations with known topological obstructions. In all tested problems we observe linear computational complexity as a function of mesh size. Moreover, the algorithm is purely graph-based so it is straightforward to implement and does not require specialized external procedures. We believe that our framework could offer new perspectives to sparse matrix computations.



1
Introduction

In this paper we present a novel framework to solve the discrete version of the following potential problem: determine a vector field with specified curl, i.e. a vector potential of a given vector field.
To do so, an “inverse curl” operator is required, necessarily including the specification of additional conditions (a gauge condition in physical parlance) to uniquely define a resulting solution.
Let us now formally introduce the problem by first considering the continuous case and then moving towards its discrete version, where we define discrete counterparts of vector fields and differential operators acting on them.

Let us consider a bounded domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where a vector field
\bm{J}
is defined.
Assume that
\Omega
is topologically trivial, i.e., it is homeomorphic to a closed
3
-ball (or, equivalently, to a cube).
We first determine necessary and sufficient conditions for assuring that
\bm{J}
is the curl of a vector field
\bm{H}
, i.e.
\bm{H}
is a vector potential of
\bm{J}
.
The answer is well-known, being a classical problem in vector analysis.
A vector field
\bm{J}
is the curl of a vector field if and only if its divergence is zero and its flux is vanishing across all the (but one) connected components of
\partial\Omega
.
In our case,
\partial\Omega
is connected, so the flux condition can be omitted since it is automatically verified.

Let us consider a
mimetic discretization
of our continuous problem.
Mimetic discretization methods like the Mimetic Finite Difference method (MFD)
[
1
]
, Discrete Geometric Approach (DGA)
[
2
]
, Finite Integration Technique (FIT), Discrete de Rham (DDR) methods
[
3
]
include the structure of exterior calculus, thus retaining fundamental properties of the continuous theory.

We recall the main concepts of mimetic discretization in
Section

2
.
We cover the domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with a polyhedral mesh
\mathcal{K}
, namely a regular CW cell complex having cells being polyhedra.
Continuous vector fields and differential operators are replaced by their discrete counterparts.
We define a discrete vector field as a collection of
degrees of freedom
(DoFs).
We introduce the discrete vector field
\bm{h}
, an array of DoFs with values on each edge of
\mathcal{K}
; next, the discrete vector field
\bm{i}
, an array of DoFs with values on each face of
\mathcal{K}
.
The central problem of this paper is to find a discrete vector potential
\bm{h}
such that

\mathbb{C}\bm{h}=\bm{i},

(1)

where
\mathbb{C}
is the usual incidence matrix between the faces and the edges of the mesh.
In intimate analogy with the continuous case, a necessary and sufficient condition to have a consistent discrete vector potential
\bm{h}
is that array
\bm{i}
represents a
discrete solenoidal
vector field, i.e. it verifies
\mathbb{D}\bm{i}=\bm{0}
, where
\mathbb{D}
is the incidence matrix between cells and faces.

Our main motivation to solve problem
Eq.

1
stems from the fact that this algorithmic primitive is an enabling technology for solving many problems arising in computational physics, from electromagnetism to elasticity and fluid mechanics
[
4
]
. First, it can be used to solve the vector laplacian in nearly linear time
[
5
]
. The idea is that, instead formulating the vector laplacian by using a vector potential, the scalar potential
[
6
]
or the mixed-hybrid
[
2
]
formulations could be used instead, which produce linear systems that can be solved in nearly linear time by using algebraic multigrid methods. Second, inverse discrete curl is at the root of efficient algorithms to compute a cohomology basis and source fields for solving magnetostatics and eddy current problems by mimetic or finite element methods
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
We think
\bm{h}
as a discrete magnetic field and
\bm{i}
as a discrete current; then
Eq.

1
expresses the so-called discrete Ampère’s law.
In the electromagnetic literature, discrete fields
\bm{h}
satisfying
Eq.

1
are often called
source fields
.
A different application in computational electromagnetics is to find a magnetic vector potential from a magnetic induction field
[
11
]
.

There are two analogous discrete potential problems: the problem of determining a scalar potential with assigned gradient and a vector field with assigned divergence.
However, as we will see in our discourse, and as been already pointed out in literature
[
12
]
, these two problems are less challenging than problem
Eq.

1
, since they are easily solved in linear worst-case complexity using standard spanning tree constructions.

Many different algorithms have been proposed in literature to solve linear system
Eq.

1
.

A naive solution to the problem would be to solve
Eq.

1
by a linear system solver.
However, these techniques are not feasible from the practical point of view since they show cubic worst-case computational complexity.
Instead, the most efficient methods to solve linear system
Eq.

1
are based on the so-called
tree-cotree decomposition
.
Tree-cotree decomposition arises from graph theory and consists in partitioning the edges of a graph into a
spanning tree
and its complement, referred to as the cotree.
The basic idea, rooted in the works
[
6
,
7
]
, goes as follows.
First, the values of array
\bm{h}
relative to a
spanning tree
on the vertices and edges of
\mathcal{K}
are set to zero.
Next, discrete Ampère’s law is iteratively enforced on each face of the mesh.
This principle is the core of the algorithms proposed in
[
13
,
12
]
.

Tree-cotree techniques are frequently claimed to be general in the literature.
However, proofs about their termination are not discussed at all
[
6
,
7
]
.
In fact, such techniques are not guaranteed to converge.
A careful analysis of the termination properties can be found in
[
13
]
, where it is shown that they strongly depend on the choice of the spanning tree.
In particular, there exist situations involving a topologically trivial complex (for example, certain meshes of a cube or a 3-ball) and a spanning tree on which such techniques do not terminate.

To overcome termination issues of tree-cotree techniques, different approaches have been proposed.
The approach in
[
14
]
is based on the idea of symbolic computations.
Although being general, this approach is slower than previous approaches.
Moreover, it is difficult to implement in practice since it requires specialized data structures to manage the symbolic computations; for instance, it requires object oriented programming languages to implement the symbolic computations.
In
[
12
]
, termination issues are solved using an explicit formula based on a double integral computation.
However, these double integral computations require specialized algorithms that are time consuming.

The aim of this paper is to describe an efficient and easy to implement algorithm to solve linear system
Eq.

1
.
Our novel algorithm is based on concepts of
discrete Morse theory

[
15
]
.
This theory employs a construction called
acyclic matching

[
16
]
, which collects combinatorial operations analogous to topological operations in the continuum.
In
Section

3
we review basic concepts at the core of discrete Morse theory as well as our specialized definitions.

Starting from acyclic matchings of discrete Morse theory, we derive three main contributions.

Firstly, we provide a unified framework based on discrete Morse theory for the solution of linear system
Eq.

1
and we present in
Section

4
.
We introduce the novel concept of
complete acyclic matching
.
The crucial fact of our framework is the following: complete acyclic matchings give the same end result of Gaussian elimination.
What the whole procedure boils down to is avoiding costly matrix algebra operations by performing equivalent cheap combinatorial operations.
By this procedure, the sparsity of linear system
Eq.

1
is retained, whereas this would not be
the case in the standard Gaussian elimination.
In fact, sparse systems become dense in intermediate steps due to an inconvenient choice of the backward elimination algorithm.

Secondly, using the newly introduced framework, we show that tree-cotree decomposition techniques are algorithms to compute specialized complete acyclic Morse matchings.
Although they use a different language to describe the same actions, they provide the same end results.
We show that the question of finding this kind of specialized complete acyclic matchings is equivalent to the following
collapsibility problem
: decide whether a 3-dimensional simplicial complex embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with trivial topology and with Lipschitz boundary is
collapsible
.
This fact establish the topological nature of termination problems of tree-cotree techniques and shows why they arise in practical applications.
Indeed, there exist examples of triangulations of 3-balls that are not collapsible
[
17
]
.
As a result, for certain triangulations, avoiding termination problems by a careful choice of the input spanning tree of tree-cotree techniques is an impossible task and thus we need to resort to new approximation strategies.

Thirdly, we provide a new recursive algorithm to compute discrete vector potentials we describe in
Section

5
.
It consists of a new greedy heuristics to construct acyclic matchings together with a recursive construction, which has no analogous in classical discrete Morse theory.
Indeed, we do not define a new chain complex, the so-called
Morse complex
, but instead we employ acyclic matchings to express new basis elements during Gaussian elimination in terms of the original basis elements of the vector space of 1-chains and the vector space of 2-chains.
Fundamentally, we do not tackle the problem of computing specialized complete acyclic matchings like in tree-cotree techniques, which, as discussed above, suffers from well-known topological termination problems.
Instead, we introduce a recursive approach whose basic outline is as follows.
We first try to find a complete acyclic matching on linear system
Eq.

1
.
If we do not succeed, we transform linear system
Eq.

1
into a new smaller linear system, by considering a suitable subset of the newly computed basis during Gaussian elimination.
We show that if we can solve this new smaller linear system, then we can get a solution of linear system in
Eq.

1
.
The crucial fact is that we can recursively apply the algorithm on the new smaller linear system.
More specifically, we try to find a complete acyclic matching on the corresponding new smaller linear system.
Again, acyclic matchings provides a new basis during Gaussian elimination so that the whole approach can be applied recursively.
The fundamental computational advantage of our recursive approach is that all the costly algebraic operations are replaced by elementary cheap combinatorial operations on significantly smaller instances of the original linear system
Eq.

1
.

We tested our recursive algorithm on challenging benchmark problems.
These include also pathological meshes with known topological obstructions.
Our algorithm exhibits linear computational complexity for all tested problems.
Moreover, the algorithm is also purely graph-theoretic, so straightforward to implement and do not require any additional specialized procedure.
We collect simulation results in
Section

6
.

While obtaining a theoretical linear worst-case complexity bound is hard, our algorithm solves all issues that are typically found in practice.
Moreover, the whole framework proves to be general and offers new perspectives to sparse matrix computations.
These observations are summarized in
Section

7
.

Notation

The domain of interest of this paper is a closed and bounded polyhedral domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with Lipschitz boundary. We assume that
\Omega
has trivial topology, i.e., it is homeomorphic to a closed
3
-dimensional ball or, equivalently,
\Omega
is simply connected and its boundary
\partial\Omega
is connected (
\partial\Omega
is homeomorphic to a
2
-sphere indeed); see
[
18
]
(Section 6) and
[
19
]
(Section 3).
We consider a
polyhedral cell complex
(or
polyhedral mesh
) subdivision
\mathcal{K}
of
\Omega
.
Elements of
\mathcal{K}
are called
cells
.
A
k
-cell
\sigma
is a
k
-dimensional subset in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
homeomorphic to a closed
k
-dimensional ball.
A
0
-cell is a point of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We equip each
k
-cell with an inner orientation.
We denote by
\dim{\sigma}
the dimension of the cell
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
and we write
\mathcal{K}_{k}
the subcollection of all
k
-cells in
\mathcal{K}
.
We focus on the 3-dimensional case, thus we have 3-cells (or volumes) in
\mathcal{K}_{3}
, 2-cells (or faces) in
\mathcal{K}_{2}
, 1-cells (or edges) in
\mathcal{K}_{1}
and 0-cells (or vertices) in
\mathcal{K}_{0}
.
We denote by
c
a generic volume, by
f
a face, by
e
an edge and by
v
a vertex.
We denote by
\mathfrak{v},\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{c}
the cardinality of
\mathcal{K}_{0},\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{3}
, respectively.
A polyhedral cell complex
\mathcal{K}
is
simplicial
if all its cells are simplicies and the boundary of each cell has the natural simplicial decomposition, see
[
20
]
.
If
\mathcal{K}
is simplicial,
\mathcal{K}
is also called a triangulation of
\Omega
.

The mesh
\mathcal{K}
has the structure of a
(regular) cell complex
, namely, the following three conditions hold
[
20
]
.
First, for each
k
-cell
\sigma
in
\mathcal{K}
its
boundary

\partial\sigma
is a union of
(k-1)
-cells in
\mathcal{K}
for
k\in\{1,2,3\}

[
20
]
.
Second, given distinct
k
-cells
\sigma,\tau
, their intersection
\sigma\cap\tau
is either empty or is a union of lower dimensional cells in
\mathcal{K}
.
Third, given a
l
-cell
\sigma
and
k
-cell
\tau
with
l\leq k
,
\sigma\neq\tau
and
\sigma\cap\tau\neq\emptyset
, we have
\sigma\cap\tau\subset\partial\tau
.

We can now define a new object, called a real
k
-chain.
A
k
-chain
a
of
\mathcal{K}
is a formal linear combination of
k
-cells
a=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\sigma_{i}
, where
\sigma_{i}
are
k
-cells in
\mathcal{K}
and
a_{i}
are real coefficients.
The number
r
denotes the cardinality of the collection of
k
-cells in
\mathcal{K}
and is any number among
\mathfrak{v},\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{f}
or
\mathfrak{c}
.
The set of
k
-chains, equipped with the natural addition and scalar multiplication, provides a real vector space.
We denote it by
C_{k}(\mathcal{K})
.
Note that each
k
-cell is also a
k
-chain.
If
\sigma
is a
k
-cell, by
-\sigma
we denote the cell
\sigma
but with opposite orientation.
The set of all
k
-cells form a basis for
C_{k}(\mathcal{K})
, which we call
canonical basis
for
C_{k}(\mathcal{K})
.
We identify the boundary of each
k
-cell with the linear combination of the
(k-1)
-cells in
\partial\sigma
defined by setting

\partial\sigma\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}\,\rho_{i},

(2)

where
w_{i}
is different from zero if and only if
\rho_{i}\subset\partial\sigma
and in this case,
w_{i}
is equal to
+1

\rho_{i}
has the orientation induced by that of
\sigma
by using the right-hand rule and
-1
otherwise
[
21
]
.

The real vector space of
k
-chains and the real vector space of
(k-1)
-chains are connected by a linear map called

\partial_{k}:C_{k}(\mathcal{K})\to C_{k-1}(\mathcal{K})
.
We define the boundary operator by linearity on the space of chains by setting

\partial_{k}a\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\,\partial\sigma_{i},

(3)

where
a=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\sigma_{i}
as above.
Note that
Eq.

3

\mathcal{K}
is a cell complex.
Since
\mathcal{K}
is a cell complex, it can verified that
\partial_{k-1}\circ\partial_{k}=0
for

; see, for example
[
20
]
.

Let us now consider the concept of a
k
-cochain
.
A
k
-cochain
b
acts on a
k
-chain to produce a real number and therefore

-cochains are elements of the dual space of
C_{k}(\mathcal{K})
.
We define the vector space of
k
-cochains

to be the dual space of linear functionals
b:C_{k}(\mathcal{K})\to\mathbb{R}
.
We denote the value of a
k

k

\langle{b}\,,{a}\rangle\coloneqq b(a)
.
Let us consider the canonical basis
\{\sigma_{i}\in C_{k}(\mathcal{K})\mid i=1,\dots,r\}
of the vector space of
k
-chains
C_{k}(\mathcal{K})
.
From basic linear algebra, there exist unique linear functionals
\{\sigma^{i}\in C^{k}(\mathcal{K})\mid i=1,\dots,r\}
such that

\langle{\sigma^{i}}\,,{\sigma_{j}}\rangle=\delta_{ij},

(4)

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta.
The set
\{\sigma^{i}\in C^{k}(\mathcal{K})\mid i=1,\dots,r\}
defined by

k

, which is called

.
We have established a one-to-one correspondence between chains and cochains.
This chain-cochain natural duality yields the real linear isomorphism
\phi_{k}:C_{k}(\mathcal{K})\to C^{k}(\mathcal{K})
sending each
\sigma_{i}
to
\sigma^{i}
.
We will write a generic
k
-cochain

as a sum
b=\sum_{i=1}^{r}b_{i}\sigma^{i}

.

For
k
-cochains, in intimate analogy with chains, we can define a

\delta^{k}:C^{k}(\mathcal{K})\to C^{k+1}(\mathcal{K})
as the
dual of the boundary operator
, i.e., it is defined by requiring that, for every
b\in C^{k}(\mathcal{K})
and

\langle{\delta^{k}b}\,,{a}\rangle=\langle{b}\,,{\partial_{k+1}a}\rangle.

(5)

In mimetic methods the coboundary operator
\delta^{k}
acts as a discrete counterpart of the continuous differential operators
[
22
,
21
]
.
Specifically,
\delta^{0}
acts as the discrete gradient,
\delta^{1}
as the discrete curl and
\delta^{2}
as the discrete divergence.

A straightforward calculation using
Eq.

5
shows that
\delta^{k}\circ\delta^{k-1}=0
for
k\in\{1,2\}
.
These relations mimic the structure of continuous differential operators
[
22
,
21
]
.
In particular, discrete differential operators form a chain complex

C^{*}=\cdots\xrightarrow{\delta^{k-1}}C^{k}(\mathcal{K})\xrightarrow{\delta^{k}}C^{k+1}(\mathcal{K})\xrightarrow{\delta^{k+1}}\cdots,

(6)

where
C^{k}(\mathcal{K})=0
if
k<0
or

.
Since the domain
\Omega
is topologically trivial the sequence is

for

for

.

In the case of
k
-chains, there is a natural choice of a basis given by the canonical basis.
Using the isomorphism
\phi_{k}:C_{k}(\mathcal{K})\to C^{k}(\mathcal{K})
, we have also fixed a canonical dual basis for

.
Since the coboundary operator

is a linear map between

Thus, to represent the coboundary operator as a matrix, we must always explicitly state which bases are chosen and, in fact, we will soon see the benefits of
changing the bases.

Let us now consider an arbitrary basis for the vector space of

-chains

We denote it by

Using the isomorphism

In what follows, we take this process of choosing a basis for

When this is done, we say that we have chosen a basis

for the entire chain complex

, i.e. a basis for each

We write

to denote a chain complex with a basis.
We define the

Having chosen bases in

We introduce a more common notation from mimetic methods

to

We represent

-chains and

As explained in the introduction, the aim of this paper is to devise an efficient algorithm for the solution of the following problem: find array
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{e}}
such that

\mathbb{C}\bm{h}=\bm{i},

(7)

where
\bm{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{f}}

In what follows, the concept of a partition of a given index set will play an important role.

A representation of the vector

\bm{v}=({{\bm{v}}|_{I_{l}}})_{l\in\{1,\dots,p\}}.

(10)

Let us consider a product of index sets

and

.
We will need a corresponding notion of

9

.
Let us consider partitions of

\{I_{1},\dots,I_{p}\}

We have a corresponding partition of

The

{{\mathbb{A}}|_{I_{l}\times J_{m}}}\coloneqq(\mathbb{A}_{i,j})_{i\in I_{l},j\in J_{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{l}\times J_{m}}.

(11)

A representation of the matrix
\mathbb{A}

3
Discrete Morse Theory

The underlying principle of our construction follows an ad hoc reformulation of Forman’s Discrete Morse theory
[
15
]
given by Kozlov
[
16
]
, where the basic tool is a combinatorial object called acyclic matching.
Several special cases of our construction have already appeared in literature.
We present a formulation of discrete Morse theory adapted to our purposes, along with smaller, more illustrative instances, which will provide insights on the structure of our algorithm.

3.1
Informal introduction to discrete Morse theory

The first concept is that of
elementary collapse
.
One may view discrete Morse theory as a generalization of the theory of simplicial collapses.
The concept of collapse, originated in Whitehead’s work
[
23
]
, provides a combinatorial operation that is analogous to the continuous operation called deformation retraction, i.e., the operation of continuously shrinking a topological space to a subset.
More specifically, let
(\sigma,\tau)
be a pair of cells such that
\sigma\subset\tau
and
\dim\sigma=\dim\tau-1
.
For this pair, to induce an elementary collapse, we require
\tau
to be a cell of maximal dimension in
\mathcal{K}
and the only one cell of
\mathcal{K}
containing
\sigma
; we refer to this as saying that the pair
(\sigma,\tau)
is
free
in
\mathcal{K}
.
Equivalently, we also say that
\sigma
is free in
\mathcal{K}
; see
Fig.

1
(a).
We say that
\mathcal{K}

collapses to

\mathcal{L}
if one could get from
\mathcal{K}
to
\mathcal{L}
in a finite sequence of elementary collapses.
If
\mathcal{K}
is equivalent to a single vertex, then we say that
\mathcal{K}
is
collapsible
; in this case there exists a sequence of elementary collapses leaving a single vertex.

Dropping the uniqueness condition on
\tau
, we obtain what we refer to as an
internal collapse
, see
Fig.

1
(b).

Geometrically, in both cases, we obtain a collapse of the pair
(\sigma,\tau)
by contracting the whole cell
\tau
onto
\partial\tau\setminus\sigma
.

Figure 1:
(a) Elementary collapse of free pair
(\sigma,\tau)
. (b) Internal collapse of pair
(\sigma,\tau)
; the resulting cell complex is not more simplicial.

In intimate analogy with elementary collapses, we may combine many internal collapses to form a sequence of internal collapses, again without affecting the homotopy type.

We thus have a family of pairs
\{(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}),\dots,(\sigma_{n},\tau_{n})\}
to be collapsed, in this order.
One may view the set of all such pairs as a
matching
on
\mathcal{K}
.
Accordingly, we refer to cells contained in some pair as
matched
and other cells as
unmatched
or
critical
(with respect to the matching).

Let
\mathcal{K}^{\,(i)}
be the resulting cell complex after the first
i
collapses.
For the pairs to form a sequence of elementary collapses, we require that each new pair
(\sigma_{i},\tau_{i})
is free in
\mathcal{K}^{\,(i-1)}
.
For generic collapses we apply the same requirement, except that we restrict our attention to the family of matched cells.
Specifically, we do not require
(\sigma_{i},\tau_{i})
to be free in
\mathcal{K}^{\,(i-1)}
, but
\tau_{i}
must be the only matched cell of
\mathcal{K}^{\,(i-1)}
containing
\sigma_{i}
.
Equivalently, for each
i
, we should have that
\sigma_{i}
is not contained in
\tau_{i+1},\dots,\tau_{n}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
.
We refer to a matching on
\mathcal{K}
admitting an ordering with this property as
acyclic
.
We formalize all this concepts in
Section

3.2
.

The main theorem of discrete Morse theory states that an acyclic matching induces a homotopy equivalence between
\mathcal{K}
and the so-called Morse complex, a cell complex formed by critical cells only
[
16
]
(Theorem 11.13 (b)).

3.2
Acyclic matchings

We start our exposition by examining acyclic matchings from a purely combinatorial point of view without any reference to topology.
Indeed, our interest is in using discrete Morse theory to develop a fast algorithm for the solution of linear system
Eq.

7
to be applied to cell complexes arising from experimental or numerical meshes of real case problems.
We give a specific version of combinatorial discrete Morse theory by Kozlov
[

]
that is adapted to our purposes.

For any
\sigma,\tau\in\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
, define

1

\sigma=\tau
and

otherwise.
Extend, by linearity,

Let us consider the chain complex

We define a relation

Given distinct basis elements

We introduce the

respectively.

Let

If the cardinality of

.
In this case, there exists a unique basis element

is free.
If the cardinality of

\sigma

.
In this case, if

Definition 3.1

(Matching, acyclic matching)
.

A

such that:

1.

(\sigma,\tau)\in\mathcal{M}

.

2.

each
\sigma\in\mathcal{B}
is the first component of at most one pair
(\sigma,\tau)
in
\mathcal{M}
.

A matching

is called

if there does not exist a cycle

\tau_{1}\succ\sigma_{1}\prec\tau_{2}\succ\cdots\prec\tau_{h}\succ\sigma_{h}\prec\tau_{1},

with

and all

A matching
\mathcal{M}_{k}
of

then

The following result is a reformulation of Theorem 11.2 in
[
16
]
by Kozlov.
It describes the crucial combinatorial property that characterizes acyclic matchings.
Its proof can be obtained by a suitable adaption of the mentioned Theorem 11.2, see pages 181-182 of
[
16
]
.

Theorem 1
.

A matching
\mathcal{M}
on
\mathcal{B}
is acyclic if and only if there exists a total order of pairs of
\mathcal{M}
as
\{(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}),\dots,(\sigma_{n},\tau_{n})\}
such that, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
,
\sigma_{i}
is not incident to any
\tau_{i+1},\dots,\tau_{n}
.

In what follows, we will write an acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}
as a sequence
\{(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}),\dots,(\sigma_{n},\tau_{n})\}
, where it is understood that the total order is chosen according to
Theorem

1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a matching on
\mathcal{B}
.
We say that a basis element in
\mathcal{B}
is
matched
(with respect to
\mathcal{M}
) if it is contained in some pair in
\mathcal{M}
(both as first or second component of the pair), otherwise, we say that it is
unmatched
or
critical
(with respect to
\mathcal{M}
).

We denote by
\mathcal{U}_{k}
the set of all
\tau\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
such that
\tau
is matched with some
\sigma\in\mathcal{B}_{k-1}
.
Similarly, we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{k}
the set of all
\sigma\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
such that
\sigma
is matched with some
\tau\in\mathcal{B}_{k+1}
.
Given
\mathcal{D}_{k}
and
\mathcal{U}_{k}
, there is a corresponding set of
critical

k
-chains

\mathcal{C}_{k}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}_{k}\setminus(\mathcal{D}_{k}\cup\mathcal{U}_{k}).

(17)

Finally, we set
\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{U}_{k}
,
\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{D}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
It easy to see that the sets
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C}
provide a partition of
\mathcal{B}

\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{C}.

(18)

Our definition of a matching is related to the presentation of the combinatorial Morse theory of Forman
[
15
]
and in particular the more recent formulation given by Kozlov
[
16
]
.
In earlier presentations, elements in
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{D}
are not explicitly introduced since what is important is only the bijective pairing between their elements.
Instead, in our setting they will play a fundamental role since we use discrete Morse theory from a purely combinatorial point of view and elements in
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{D}
will be used to select suitable submatrices.
The set of critical elements is present also in classical discrete Morse theory.
The set of critical elements will play a fundamental role in
Algorithm

3
, where critical elements become the new input for subsequent iterations.
In
Algorithm

3
, it is also essential to be able to express new basis elements as a function of the previous ones.
Hence, we need to keep track of basis structure at each iteration.

An important difference with classical discrete Morse theory is that in Forman
[
15
]
a new chain complex, the so-called Morse complex, is constructed.
Our version of discrete Morse theory operates directly on basis elements by performing elementary operations on it.
This is needed to describe how incidence matrices transform in the new bases due to our combinatorial operations.

3.3
Basis transformations associated with an acyclic matching

We begin by considering simple examples to develop geometric intuition behind the general definitions.
When simplifying a cell complex, the effect of a collapse is that of changing the structure of the basis

, by performing elementary operations on it.
There are three elementary operations to obtain a new basis from a previous one.
If

1.

Exchanging elements
\xi_{i}
and
\xi_{j}

2.

Multiplying

by

3.

Replacing

Let us consider a prototype example of a simplicial complex

The set of all 1-chains is generated by the canonical basis

Depending on whether a collapse is elementary or internal we have corresponding elementary operations on

Example 3

(Interior collapse)

Let us now consider the collapse of the pair

We now give a recursive definition of the

4
Acyclic matchings and Gaussian elimination

We shall now head towards an algorithm to reduce the matrix
\mathbb{C}
to a row echelon form by means of elementary operations on the basis
\mathcal{B}
.
The whole procedure boils down to a standard train of thought used in basic linear algebra.
When the coboundary operator
\delta^{1}
is given as a finite matrix
\mathbb{C}
, the bases and orders are already determined.
However, we can get any other bases by applying elementary row and column operations on the matrix
\mathbb{C}
.
Our algorithm will produce a change of bases in such a way that the matrix
\mathbb{C}
in the new bases has an invertible upper triangular submatrix (i.e. a matrix with non-zeros only in its upper triangle and main diagonal) and thus it can be transformed in row echelon form.
Hence, we can fast solve the system by processing the unknown variables in reverse order, a standard process known as back substitution.

4.1
Acyclic matchings and Gaussian elimination

The crucial observation is the following general novel result.

Lemma 1
.

Denote by
\mathbb{D}_{k}
one among the matrices
\mathbb{G}
,
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{D}
for

{{\mathbb{D}_{k}}|_{\mathcal{U}_{k+1}\times\mathcal{D}_{k}}}

Proof.

Since the matching

is acyclic, for every

is not incident to any basis element

Thus, all the non-zero entries in each column are above the diagonal as the rows and columns are arranged in the total order induced by the matching.
∎

By

However,

In fact, in this case,

containing it.
Then, add the pair
(v,e)
to
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and remove
v,e
from
T
.
Since
v
is a leaf, the obtained graph is again a tree.
By iteratively repeating this process, we define a complete acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
The matching
\mathcal{M}_{0}
is well-defined: if a vertex
v
is matched during the process, it is removed from
T
and thus cannot appear in any other pair; it is acyclic since every tree is.
The matching
\mathcal{M}_{0}
is complete, since
\mathcal{K}
is connected,
T
is spanning, namely all vertices of
\mathcal{K}
are in
T
and they are eventually added to
\mathcal{M}_{0}
in the above process, except the last one.

Case
k=2
.
Before considering the case
k=1
, we show the similarity of the construction for
k=2
with the case
k=0
.
We need to construct a complete acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{2}
of 2-chains on basis
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
.
By definition, the number of matched elements in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
has to equal to the rank of
\mathbb{D}
, which is
\mathfrak{c}
.
Note that the cell complex
\mathcal{K}
defines a manifold with boundary.
It implies that if
c,c^{\prime}
are distinct volumes and
f
is a face such that
f\subset\partial c\cap\partial c^{\prime}
, then
c,c^{\prime}
are the only volumes that contain
f
.
Thanks to the manifold condition, it is well-defined the so-called
complete dual graph
of
\mathcal{K}
.
The dual graph
\tilde{G}=(\tilde{V}\cup\tilde{V}_{\infty},\tilde{E}\cup\tilde{E}_{\infty})
of
\mathcal{K}
is a graph with set of vertices
\tilde{V}
given by volumes of
\mathcal{K}
and
\{c,c^{\prime}\}\in\tilde{E}
if
\dim(c\cap c^{\prime})=2
.
There is an additional vertex
\tilde{v}_{\infty}\in\tilde{V}_{\infty}
and there are additional edges
\tilde{E}_{\infty}
in
\tilde{G}
.
Each edge in
\tilde{E}_{\infty}
corresponds to a volume
c
whose boundary
\partial c
contains a boundary face
f
; thus, if
f
is a boundary face and
c
is the unique volume incident to it, then
\{\tilde{v}_{\infty},c\}\in\tilde{E}_{\infty}
.
Let us consider a spanning tree
\tilde{T}
on
\tilde{G}
.
By repeating the same construction detailed for
k=0
on
\tilde{T}
, we obtain a finite sequence
S
of pairs of the form
(c,\tilde{e})
, where
c
is a vertex in
\tilde{V}\cup\tilde{V}_{\infty}
(i.e. a volume of
\mathcal{K}
or
\tilde{v}_{\infty}
) and
\tilde{e}=\{c,c^{\prime}\}
is an edge in
\tilde{E}\cup\tilde{E}_{\infty}
.
For each pair
(c,\tilde{e})
, we choose a face
f
of
\mathcal{K}
such that
f\subset\partial c\cap\partial c^{\prime}
if
\tilde{v}_{\infty}\notin\tilde{e}=\{c,c^{\prime}\}
,
f\subset\partial c
if
c^{\prime}=\tilde{v}_{\infty}
and
f\subset\partial c^{\prime}
if
c=\tilde{v}_{\infty}
.
Replace in
S
each pair
(c,\tilde{e})
with either
(f,c)
if
c\neq\tilde{v}_{\infty}
or
(f,c^{\prime})
if
c=\tilde{v}_{\infty}
.
In this way, we obtain a complete acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{2}
of 2-chains of
\mathcal{K}
.
Thus, all vertices of the dual graph are eventually added to
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, except the vertex
\tilde{v}_{\infty}
, and all volumes in
\mathcal{K}
are matched.

By combining
Theorem

4
with the above results, we state the following theorem which solves the discrete potential problem for
k\in\{0,2\}
.

Theorem 5
.

For
k\in\{0,2\}
, we can find in linear time a discrete potential
\bm{v}\in C^{k}(\mathcal{K})
of
\bm{w}\in C^{k+1}(\mathcal{K})
with
\mathbb{D}_{k+1}\bm{w}=\bm{0}
, namely a solution of
\mathbb{D}_{k}\bm{v}=\bm{w}
.

Case
k=1
.
With no surprise, it turns out to be the most challenging case.
To begin with, we show the following result.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a 3-dimensional topologically trivial simplicial complex embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with Lipschitz boundary as in
Section

2
.
Then,
\mathcal{K}
is collapsible if and only if there exists a complete acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{1}
of 1-chains on the canonical basis
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
.

Proof.

Let us consider a sequence of elementary collapses
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{K}
leading from
\mathcal{K}
to a vertex.
We define an acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{1}
of 1-chains (on
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
) by selecting, according to the total order of
\mathcal{M}
, all pairs made by matched edges and faces in
\mathcal{M}
.
Note that
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is necessarily acyclic since
\mathcal{M}
is.
Next, since
\mathcal{K}
collapses to a vertex, all faces in
\mathcal{K}
are matched in
\mathcal{M}
.
In particular, all volumes in
\mathcal{K}
are matched in
\mathcal{M}
and there are
\mathfrak{c}
corresponding matched faces.
Thus, there are
\mathfrak{f}-\mathfrak{c}
matched faces in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
It follows that
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is complete since the rank of
\mathbb{C}
is exactly
\mathfrak{f}-\mathfrak{c}
.

To prove the converse result, let us consider a complete acyclic matching of 1-chains
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.

We need the following construction.
First let us consider the complete dual graph
\tilde{G}=(\tilde{V}\cup\tilde{V}_{\infty},\tilde{E}\cup\tilde{E}_{\infty})
of
\mathcal{K}
as defined above for the case
k=2
.
Consider the subgraph
\tilde{G}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}=(\tilde{V}\cup\tilde{V}_{\infty},\tilde{E}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}})
of
\tilde{G}
which has the same vertices of
\tilde{G}
and
\tilde{E}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
includes an edge
\{c,c^{\prime}\}\in\tilde{E}
of
\tilde{G}
if the unique face
f
of
\mathcal{K}
such that

is critical with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
We now show that
\tilde{G}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
has
\mathfrak{c}
edges (i.e, the number of vertices of
\tilde{G}
minus one) and is connected.
First, since
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is a complete acyclic matching the number of critical faces, which is equal to the number of edges in
\tilde{E}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
, is
\mathfrak{f}-(\mathfrak{f}-\mathfrak{c})=\mathfrak{c}
.
Second, assume that, for the sake of contradiction,
\tilde{G}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
is disconnected.
Let
\tilde{V}^{*}
be the set of nodes in a fixed connected component of
\tilde{G}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
.
Let
\tilde{E}_{\tilde{V}^{*}}\subset\tilde{E}\cup\tilde{E}_{\infty}
be the subset of
cut edges
, namely edges of
\tilde{G}
with one vertex in
\tilde{V}^{*}
and one vertex in its complement
(\tilde{V}\cup\tilde{V}_{\infty})\setminus\tilde{V}^{*}
.
Note that since
\tilde{G}
is connected,
\tilde{E}_{\tilde{V}^{*}}
is not empty.
Moreover, by definition of
\tilde{G}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
, to each
\tilde{e}\in\tilde{E}_{V^{*}}
corresponds a matched face
f
of
\mathcal{K}
with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{1}
forming the pair
(e,\tilde{e})=(e,f)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
for a unique edge

of
\mathcal{K}
.

Now we construct a cycle of the form
\tilde{e}_{1}\succ e_{1}\prec\tilde{e}_{2}\succ\dots\prec\tilde{e}_{h}\succ e_{h}\prec\tilde{e}_{1}
with
h\geq 2
,
(e_{i},\tilde{e}_{i})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,h\}
and all
\tilde{e}_{i}\in\tilde{E}_{V^{*}}
being distinct.
Start with a cut edge
\tilde{e}_{1}
and consider the unique edge
e_{1}
of
\mathcal{K}
forming the matched pair
(e_{1},\tilde{e}_{1})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
Then,
e_{1}
is contained in at least one other cut edge

, otherwise

cannot be a cut edge.
By iteratively repeating this process, we obtain a sequence
S
of pairs
(e_{1},\tilde{e}_{1}),\dots,(e_{i},\tilde{e}_{i})
such that
\tilde{e}_{1}\succ e_{1}\prec\tilde{e}_{2}\succ\dots\prec\tilde{e}_{i-1}\succ e_{i}\prec\tilde{e}_{i}
.
Since we have finite graphs, eventually we will run out of those cut edges which do not appear as the second component of a pair in
S
.
Thus, at some step
i
of the above process, we must get a cut edge that is the second component of a pair in
S
and we get a cycle of the form
Eq.

16
, which is a contradiction since
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is acyclic.
We have proved that
\tilde{G}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
is a connected subgraph of
\tilde{G}
with
\mathfrak{c}
edges and thus it is a spanning tree of
\tilde{G}
.
We define a complete acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{2}
of 2-chains by using the spanning tree construction detailed above for
k=2
on
\tilde{G}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
.

Now,
\mathcal{M}_{2}
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}
define a sequence of elementary collapses leading from
\mathcal{K}
to a single vertex as follows.
Since every volume of
\mathcal{K}
is matched with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, the set of critical cells of
\mathcal{K}
with respect to

form a 2-dimensional subcomplex
\mathcal{K}^{\,(1)}
of

.
By applying Theorem 11.13 (a) in
[

to
\mathcal{K}^{\,(1)}
.
Next, observe that, by construction, the set of matched faces in

with respect to

is exactly the set of critical faces of

with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
Thus, the set of critical cells of
\mathcal{K}^{\,(1)}
with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{1}
form a 1-dimensional subcomplex
\mathcal{K}^{\,(2)}
of
\mathcal{K}^{\,(1)}
.
By applying Theorem 11.13 (a) in
[
16
]
there exists a sequence of elementary collapses leading from
\mathcal{K}^{\,(1)}
to
\mathcal{K}^{\,(2)}
.

\mathcal{K}^{\,(2)}
is a topologically trivial 1-dimensional subcomplex, thus it is a spanning tree on

.
Thus

collapses to a single vertex.
∎

Now we focus on the
Spanning Tree Technique
(STT) algorithm introduced in
[
13
]
and implicitly used in many works
[
7
,
8
,
12
,
6
]
.
The algorithm, given as input a spanning tree
T
on
\mathcal{K}
, computes a discrete vector potential
\bm{h}
as follows.
To start with, STT sets the value
\bm{h}_{e}=0
if
e\in T
, otherwise the value
\bm{h}_{e}
at this stage is unknown.
Next, all faces
f
of
\mathcal{K}
are loaded into a list
L
.
The main loop of STT works until there are no more faces in
L
.
In each iteration, we randomly search for a face
f
with two boundary edges
e_{1},e_{2}\subset\partial f
such that the values
\bm{h}_{e_{1}},\bm{h}_{e_{2}}
are known. Then, the value of
\bm{h}_{e}
of the remaining boundary edge
e\subset\partial f
is determined by

\bm{h}_{e}=(\mathbb{C}_{f,e})^{-1}(\bm{i}_{f}-\mathbb{C}_{f,e_{1}}\bm{h}_{e_{1}}-\mathbb{C}_{f,e_{2}}\bm{h}_{e_{2}})

(26)

and face
f
is removed from the list
L
.
In the case when
L
is non-empty and there is no available face
f
satisfying the above property, then STT does not terminate since it stalls in a infinite loop.
As shown in
[
13
]
, STT termination depends on the choice of the input spanning tree
T
.

We now show that the STT algorithm boils down to a procedure to construct complete acyclic matchings of 1-chains on the canonical basis
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
.
This result completes the picture of the equivalence between tree-cotree techniques and the problem of finding complete acyclic matching of
k
-chains.
The idea behind this observation has it root on the fact that tree-cotree techniques describe the same actions of acyclic matchings although using a different language.

To formally state the next theorem we introduce the following notations.
Given a spanning tree
T
on
\mathcal{K}
, we say that STT
terminates
(with input
T
) if it does not stall in an infinite loop.
We say that STT
uses the pair
(e,f)
if STT determines the value of edge
e\subset\partial f
via
Eq.

26
during its main loop execution.
Finally, we say that STT
uses edge
e
if STT uses the pair
(e,f)
for some face
f\in L
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a simplicial complex as in
Theorem

6
.
There exists a spanning tree on
\mathcal{K}
for which STT terminates if and only if there exists a complete acyclic matching of 1-chains on the canonical basis
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
.

Proof.

Let
T
be a spanning tree such that STT terminates.
We now define an acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{T}
of 1-chains on
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
such that every
cotree edge
of
T
, namely an edge
e\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
which is not an edge of
T
, is matched in
\mathcal{M}_{T}
.

\mathcal{M}_{T}
is constructed during STT execution as follows.
We initialize
\mathcal{M}_{1}=\emptyset
.
Next, if STT uses a pair
(e,f)
during its execution then we add
(e,f)
to
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
In this way we get a total order of pairs in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
where a pair
(e_{i},f_{i})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
comes before than a pair
(e_{j},f_{j})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
in this total order with
i<j
if STT uses
(e_{i},f_{i})
before than
(e_{j},f_{j})
.
The set
\mathcal{M}_{T}
of all such pairs is a complete acyclic matching.
First, we see that
\mathcal{M}_{T}
is a matching since if STT uses the pair
(e,f)
, then the value of
\bm{h}_{e}
set by STT can never be reassigned.
Second, the matching
\mathcal{M}_{T}
is acyclic since if STT uses the pair
(e_{i},f_{i})
, then
e_{i}
is not contained in the boundary of any matched face
f_{j}
in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
for
1\leq j<i
.
Third, since STT terminates, every cotree edge
e
of
T
is matched in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
Using Euler’s formula, the fact that
\mathcal{K}
is topologically trivial and that
T
is a spanning tree on
\mathcal{K}
we get
\mathfrak{e}-(\mathfrak{v}-1)=\mathfrak{f}-\mathfrak{c}
.
Thus
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is complete.

Conversely, let
\mathcal{M}_{1}
be a complete acyclic matching of 1-chains on
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
.
We need the following construction, which mimics the one described in
Theorem

6
.
Consider the subgraph
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}=(V,E_{\mathcal{M}_{1}})
of
\mathcal{K}
with set of vertices given by vertices of
\mathcal{K}
and
E_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
includes an edge
e\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
if
e
is critical with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
We now show that
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
has
\mathfrak{v}-1
edges and is connected.
First, since
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is a complete acyclic matching the number of critical edges, which is equal to the number of edges in
E_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
, is
\mathfrak{e}-(\mathfrak{f}-\mathfrak{c})=\mathfrak{v}-1
where we have used Euler’s formula
\mathfrak{v}-\mathfrak{e}+\mathfrak{f}-\mathfrak{c}=1
and the fact that
\mathcal{K}
is topologically trivial.
Second, assume that, for the sake of contradiction,
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
is disconnected.
Let
V^{*}
be the set of nodes in a fixed connected component of
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
.
Let
E_{V^{*}}\subset\mathcal{K}_{1}
be the subset of
cut edges
, namely edges of
G
with one vertex in
V^{*}
and one vertex in its complement
V\setminus V^{*}
.
Note that since
\mathcal{K}
is connected,
E_{V^{*}}
is not empty.
Moreover, by definition of
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
, to each
e\in E_{V^{*}}
corresponds a matched edge
e
of
\mathcal{K}
with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{1}
forming the pair
(e,f)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
for a unique face
f
of
\mathcal{K}
.

Now we construct a cycle of the form
f_{1}\succ e_{1}\prec f_{2}\succ\dots\prec f_{h}\succ e_{h}\prec f_{1}
with
h\geq 2
,
(e_{i},f_{i})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,h\}
and all
e_{i}\in E_{V^{*}}
being distinct.
Start with a cut edge
e_{1}
and consider the unique face
f_{1}
of
\mathcal{K}
forming the matched pair
(e_{1},f_{1})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
Then,
f_{1}
contains at least one other cut edge
e_{2}\in E_{V^{*}}
, otherwise
e_{1}
cannot be a cut edge.
By iteratively repeating this process, we obtain a sequence
S
of pairs
(e_{1},f_{1}),\dots,(e_{i},f_{i})
such that
f_{1}\succ e_{1}\prec f_{2}\succ\dots\prec f_{i-1}\succ e_{i}\prec f_{i}
.
Since we have finite graphs, eventually we will run out of those cut edges which do not appear as the first component of a pair in
S
.
Thus, at some step
i
of the above process, we must get a cut edge that is the first component of a pair in
S
and we get a cycle of the form
Eq.

16
, which is a contradiction since
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is acyclic.
We have proved that
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
is a connected subgraph of
\mathcal{K}
with
\mathfrak{v}-1
edges and thus it is a spanning tree on
\mathcal{K}
.

Now we prove that STT with input
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
terminates.
Note that, by definition of
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
, if
(e,f)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
then
e
is a
cotree edge
of
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
, namely
e
is not an edge of
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
.
Denote by
C
the set of all cotree edges of
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
.
Moreover, since
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is complete, reasoning as above using Euler’s formula, we get that every cotree edge of
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
is matched with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
Since
\mathcal{M}_{1}=\{(e_{1},f_{1}),\dots,(e_{n},f_{n})\}
is acyclic, we can order pairs in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
in such a way that, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
,
f_{i}
is not incident to any
e_{i+1},\dots,e_{n}
.
Let
E_{\text{STT}}
be the set of cotree edges used by STT during its execution.
Note that
E_{\text{STT}}
is not empty since STT can use at least the pair
(e_{1},f_{1})
, thanks to the total order chosen on
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
Suppose that STT does not terminate.
This means that, during its execution, it does not use any cotree edge and
C\setminus E_{\text{STT}}\neq\emptyset
.
We will show that this is impossible.
Let
i
be the minimum integer such that the cotree edge
e_{i}
belongs to
C\setminus E_{\text{STT}}
.
Since pairs in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
are ordered as described above,
i>1
and
f_{i}
can be only incident to cotree edges
e_{j}
with
j\leq i
.
Moreover, by definition of
i
, there is no
j
with
j<i
such that the cotree edge
e_{j}
belongs to
C\setminus E_{\text{STT}}
.
Hence, STT should have at least used the pair
(e_{i},f_{i})
, i.e.
e_{i}
belongs to
E_{\text{STT}}
as well.
This gives the desired contradiction and completes the proof.
∎

The proof of
Theorem

7
shows that, if the STT algorithm terminates for a given spanning tree
T
, starting from
T
we can construct a complete acylclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{T}
of 1-chains on
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
.
But also the other way around, namely, if we have a complete acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{1}
of 1-chains on
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
, starting from
\mathcal{M}_{1}
we can construct a spanning tree
G_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
for which STT terminates.

By combining
Theorem

6
and
Theorem

7
we now state the following result which gives a topological characterization of termination problems of tree-cotree techniques.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a simplicial complex as in
Theorem

6
.
Then, there exists a spanning tree on
\mathcal{K}
for which STT terminates if and only if
\mathcal{K}
is collapsible.

There are known examples of triangulations of 3-balls which are not collapsible
[
17
]
.
Hence, using
Theorem

8
, there are triangulations
\mathcal{K}
of 3-balls such that, for every possible spanning tree of
\mathcal{K}
given as input, STT does not terminate.

We remark that it is NP-complete to decide whether a given 3-dimensional simplicial complex (embedded or not) is collapsible
[
27
]
.
However, to the authors knowledge, the related question for the case of 3-dimensional simplicial complexes embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and with Lipschitz boundary is still an open problem.
Yet, numerical evidence shows that this problem is very difficult in general although good heuristics exists
[
28
]
.

These results are the cause of the well-known termination problems of tree-cotree techniques
[
13
]
; see also the discussion in
Section

6
.

4.3
The case where we cannot find a complete acyclic matching

As shown by
Theorem

6
and the discussion at the end of
Section

4.2
, for cell complex
\mathcal{K}
given as input, we cannot find in general a complete acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{1}
of 1-chains on the canonical basis
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
.
Consequently,
Theorem

4
cannot be applied in general for
\mathcal{B}=\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
.

Let us now assume that acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{1}
is not complete.
We shall now show that, after solving another linear system, we can still get a discrete vector potential solution
\bm{h}
solution of
Eq.

7
by exploiting back substitution.

As shown in
Section

3.3
, an acyclic matching acts on a basis
\mathcal{B}
by performing elementary operations on it.
Let us consider the new basis
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\mathcal{B}\cdot\mathcal{M}_{1}=\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{C}
.
There is a corresponding block partition of linear system
Eq.

7
as

\begin{pmatrix}{{\mathbb{C}}|_{\mathcal{U}_{2}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}}}&{{\mathbb{C}}|_{\mathcal{U}_{2}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}}}\\
{{\mathbb{C}}|_{\mathcal{C}_{2}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}}}&{{\mathbb{C}}|_{\mathcal{C}_{2}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{{\bm{h}}|_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}}\\
{{\bm{h}}|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{{\bm{i}}|_{\mathcal{U}_{2}}}\\
{{\bm{i}}|_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}}\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

The crucial fact turns out to be that
{{\mathbb{C}}|_{\mathcal{C}_{2}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}}}
is a zero matrix.
Thus, we can determine a discrete vector potential
\bm{h}=({{\bm{h}}|_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}},{{\bm{h}}|_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}})^{T}
solution of
Eq.

Algorithm description

The goal of this section is a recursive algorithm that reduces matrix

into a row echelon form by means of elementary operations on the basis
\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{k}\mathcal{B}_{k}
and we present in
Section

We first describe in

5.1
Greedy approach to construct acyclic matchings

To minimize computational effort of change of basis in
Eq.

28
, we have to carefully choose how to construct the acyclic matching
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
It is visible from
Section

3.3
that constructing acyclic matchings by internal collapses can get more complicated than by elemetary collapses since changes of basis of type 2 and 3 are involved.
In this case, we have to express the new basis elements as a linear combination of the previous ones and hence they cannot be removed from the data structure after each collapse.

The above discussion motivates the concept of
degree
of a basis element
\sigma\in\mathcal{B}
.
We define
\mathrm{deg}(\sigma)
as the cardinality of the coboundary of
\sigma
, i.e. the set
\mathrm{cobd}_{\mathcal{B}}{(\sigma)}
defined in
Eq.

15
.
Note that if
\mathrm{deg}(\sigma)
is 1 then
\sigma
is free.
In our approach, also the case
\mathrm{deg}(\sigma)=2
will play a fundamental role.
We define
\sigma\in\mathcal{B}
to be
flat
if
\mathrm{deg}(\sigma)=2
.

To reduce the amount of computation during Gaussian elimination, it is wise to first search for basis elements with lowest degree.
Indeed, if
\mathrm{deg}(\sigma)
is 1, then
\sigma
is free and the new basis is a selection of the previous one and no algebraic operations are needed.
In this case, it should be possible to avoid all the matrix algebra computation and efficiently organize the basis so that matrices are in triangular form as in
Lemma

1
.

Keeping in mind the above heuristics, we search for basis elements having smallest degree.
Specifically, we do not strictly choose basis elements with minimum degree but instead we proceed in a sequential manner.
First, we search for all free basis elements until exhaustion.
Next, we search for all flat basis elements until exhaustion.
We have pursued this method because been motivated by its practical implementation and performance on test problems, rather than by following the best theoretical greedy approach; see the discussion in
Section

6
.

We construct acyclic matchings using a standard elementary collapse greedy procedure, where we search for collapsing sequences of free basis elements in a monotone-like fashion
[
28
]
.
That is, we proceed in sequential order with respect to the dimension of the basis elements by first collapsing 2-chains and then 1-chains.

5.2
Recursive algorithm

We now present a recursive construction of acyclic matchings.

Normally one aims at finding a complete acyclic matching, namely a matching that reaches the needed number of matched pairs so that we can apply

However, as proved in

In

\mathcal{M}_{1}
is not complete.

Let us consider the case where
\mathcal{M}_{1}

After applying basis transformations associated with
\mathcal{M}_{1}

\mathcal{B}

As pointed out in

The novel idea is that we may call the whole routine recursively, where the output set of critical basis elements obtained from the previous iteration becomes the input basis for the next iteration.
Fundamentally, we think linear system

We consider 3-balls of

In

Bing’s construction works as follows.
One starts with a triangulated 3-ball

A knotted spanning arc is an arc as in

We dig a knot-shaped tubular hole inside
\Omega

\Omega

In this way we obtain a 3-ball

Conclusions

The novel algorithm presented in this paper was proved to be general, straightforward to implement and outperforms competing state-of-art algorithms in the class of admissible meshes while showing linear average complexity with respect to the input mesh size.
By applying discrete Morse theory, we have shown that for the important class of
simplicial triangulations we achieve linear computational complexity for all considered test problems.
These include, besides real case triangulations having simple topological properties, also pathological triangulations.
A challenging test case made of one hundred trefoil knots have been considered and yet the proposed algorithm succeeds in computing a discrete vector potential.
Thus, we expect that our algorithm works for every practical mesh that one encounters in practical applications.

Worst-case complexity analysis can be misleading in the analysis of our
Algorithm

3
.
Worst-case complexity analysis of our algorithm assumes that at certain iteration no new matched pairs are possible.
In this case we need to employ a sparse linear system solver, which leads to a cubical worst-case complexity with respect to mesh size parameters.
Yet, the average complexity is linear in all tested problems.
Our point of view is that the reason we see linear computational complexity is because our examples restrict to 3-dimensional cell complexes decomposing bounded domains of

with sufficiently regular boundaries.
