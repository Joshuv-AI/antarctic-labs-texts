Brownian Motion in the 𝒑 \bm{p} -Adic Integers is a Limit of Discrete Time Random Walks
Tyler Pierce ∗ and David Weisbart ∗

Abstract
. Vladimirov defined an operator on balls in ℚ p \mathds{Q}_{p} , the p p -adic numbers, that is analogous to the Laplace operator in the real setting. Kochubei later provided a probabilistic interpretation of the operator. This Vladimirov-Kochubei operator generates a real-time diffusion process in the ring of p p -adic integers, a Brownian motion in ℤ p \mathds{Z}_{p} . The current work shows that this process is a limit of discrete time random walks. It motivates the construction of the Vladimirov-Kochubei operator, provides further intuition about the properties of ultrametric diffusion, and gives an example of the weak convergence of stochastic processes in a profinite group.



1.
Introduction

For any prime
p
and any positive real number
b
, the Vladimirov operator
\Delta_{b}
(see Section
2
) is an example of one type of pseudodifferential operator on the field of
p
-adic numbers,
\mathds{Q}_{p}
. The Vladimirov operator is the
p
-adic analog of the fractional Laplacian in the real setting. Take
\rho
to be the fundamental solution to the parabolic pseudodifferential equation

(1)

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\rho(t,x)=-\Delta_{b}\rho(t,x).

The indexed collection of functions
(\rho(t,\cdot))_{t>0}
forms a convolution semigroup of probability density functions that gives rise to a probability measure
{\rm P}
on
D([0,\infty)\colon\mathds{Q}_{p})
, the Skorohod space of
\mathds{Q}_{p}
-valued càdlàg functions on
[0,\infty)
endowed with the Skorohod metric
[
20
]
. The measure
{\rm P}
is a
p
-adic analog of the Wiener measure on the space of continuous, real-valued paths. Take
Y
to be the function on
[0,\infty)\times D([0,\infty)\colon\mathds{Q}_{p})
that is defined on any pair
(t,\omega)
by

(2)

Y(t,\omega)=\omega(t).

Curry variables to obtain for each
t
in
[0,\infty)
the random variable
Y_{t}
on
D([0,\infty)\colon\mathds{Q}_{p})
that acts on any
\omega
in
D([0,\infty)\colon\mathds{Q}_{p})
by

(3)

Y_{t}(\omega)=\omega(t).

Because of the close formal analogy between the Vladimirov operator and the Laplace operator, the current work refers to the stochastic process
(D([0,\infty)\colon\mathds{Q}_{p}),{\rm P},Y)
as a
p
-adic Brownian motion.

Authors traditionally refer to diffusion processes of the type that the current paper studies as ultrametric diffusion processes
[
1
,
2
]
, but such processes may potentially be more general. In some respects, a true analog of real Brownian motion in the
p
-adic setting is the process that Bikulov and Volovich introduced
[
6
]
. This is a very different stochastic process, one that is parameterized by a
p
-adic time variable.

Motivated by the recent works of Khrennikov, Oleschko, and Correa Lopez on
p
-adic models of a porous medium
[
11
,
12
]
, Kochubei recently studied both linear and nonlinear heat equations on balls in
\mathds{Q}_{p}

[
13
]
. These works motivate the current investigation. There is a challenge even in the real setting, as Kochubei pointed out, of interpreting the meaning of a non-local operator on a bounded domain
[
7
]
. Vladimirov defined a version of the operator
\Delta_{b,N}
on the ball
{\mathds{B}}(N)
, the ball of radius
p^{N}
in
\mathds{Q}_{p}
centered at
0

[
21
]
. Kochubei gave a probabilistic interpretation of the operator
\Delta_{b,N}

[
14
]
, and explicitly gave the heat kernel for this operator
[
14
, Theorem 4.9]
.

The study of Brownian motion in non-Archimedean settings is not only of intrinsic mathematical interest but also appears to have substantial physical applications. For example, Parisi’s early work on spin glasses
[
15
,
16
]
implicitly involved ultrametric structures. Later, Parisi and Sourlas
[
17
]
and Avetisov, Bikulov, and Kozyrev
[
3
]
independently proposed
p
-adic models to describe replica symmetry breaking. Significant applications of
p
-adic diffusion in physics which have received experimental confirmation are found in the works of Avetisov, Bikulov, and Zubarev
[
4
]
, and Bikulov and Zubarev
[
8
]
, who utilized the Vladimirov equation to describe protein molecule dynamics. Since Brownian motion in the real setting is a scaling limit, a general question arises: In what sense can sequences of discrete time random walks approximate Brownian motion in non-Archimedean settings?

Earlier works demonstrate that Brownian motion in several non-Archimedean settings are scaling limits
[
5
,
23
,
18
]
. The first of these works
[
5
]
shows that
p
-adic Brownian motion is a limit of discrete-time random walks. The second work
[
23
]
simplified the approach and improved the results of the first by showing that
p
-adic Brownian motion is a scaling limit of a single discrete-time process valued in a discrete group. This extended the results to a wider class of Vladimirov operators and demonstrated the weak convergence of the processes on unbounded time intervals, rather than just compact time intervals. The latest work
[
18
]
extended the second to the setting of Brownian motion in any finite-dimensional vector space over any non-Archimedean local field of any characteristic. The current work provides what appears to be the first example of such results in the setting of a profinite group by demonstrating that any Brownian motion with paths in the ring of
p
-adic integers is a limit of discrete-time random walks.

Section
2
reviews the basic theory of harmonic analysis on
\mathds{Q}_{p}
and its unit ball
\mathds{Z}_{p}
, and presents in a restricted setting the operator that Vladimirov and Kochubei developed and studied, the
Vladimirov-Kochubei operator
on
\mathds{Z}_{p}
. It also reviews some properties of the Brownian motion associated with the Vladimirov-Kochubei operator. Section
3
develops for each natural number
m
a discrete time random walk on
\mathds{Z}_{p}
by first constructing a random walk on each finite group
\mathds{Z}_{p}/\penalty p^{m}\mathds{Z}_{p}
, and then by embedding the finite groups into
\mathds{Z}_{p}
to obtain a sequence of embedded processes. Section
4
establishes moment estimates for the processes in the finite groups. Given an appropriate time scale for each embedded process, Section
5
establishes a uniform moment estimate for the sequence of measures
({\rm P}^{m})
on
D([0,\infty)\colon\mathds{Z}_{p})
that are associated with the embedded processes. These moment estimates show that the sequence of measures is uniformly tight
[
10
]
. Section
5
also establishes the convergence of the finite dimensional distributions of the approximating measures to the finite dimensional distributions of the measure
{\rm P}
for the process that Kochubei constructed. The convergence of the finite dimensional distributions together with the uniform tightness of the sequence of approximating measures implies Theorem
1.1
, the main result of the current work.

Theorem 1.1
.

The sequence of measures
({\rm P}^{m})
converges weakly to
{\rm P}
.

It is in this precise sense that Brownian motion in
\mathds{Z}_{p}
is a limit of discrete time random walks.

2.
The limiting processes

2.1.
Path Spaces

The current work involves the construction and convergence of probability measures on a space of paths
\Omega(I)
with state space
\mathcal{S}
equal to either a finite Abelian group,
\mathds{Q}_{p}
, or a ball in
\mathds{Q}_{p}
, and domain
I
equal to
[0,\infty)
or
\mathds{N}_{0}
, the natural numbers with
0
. There is a general procedure for constructing such measures. In the case that
I
is the interval
[0,\infty)
and
\mathcal{S}
is
\mathds{Q}_{p}
or a ball in
\mathds{Q}_{p}
, the fundamental solution
\rho
to an analogue of the heat equation in
\mathcal{S}
gives for each
t
in
[0,\infty)
a probability density function
\rho(t,\cdot)
. The indexed collection
(\rho(t,\cdot))_{t>0}
is a convolution semigroup of probability density functions.

Any finite sequence
h
of pairs of the form

h=((t_{0},U_{0}),(t_{1},U_{1}),\dots,(t_{n},U_{n})),

where the sequence
(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
is a strictly increasing finite sequence in
[0,\infty)
,
t_{0}
is equal to
0
, and each
U_{i}
is a Borel subset of
\mathcal{S}
, is a
history
for paths in
\mathcal{S}
. The sequence
(0,t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
is the
epoch
for
h
and the sequence
(U_{i})_{i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}}
is the
route
for
h
. Take
\ell(h)
to be
n
, the
length of the history
. For each
i
in
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, associate an epoch
e_{h}
and a route
U_{h}
with a given history
h
by writing

e_{h}(i)=t_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{h}(i)=U_{i}.

Take
H(I,\mathcal{S})
to be the set of all histories for
\mathcal{S}
-valued paths with domain
I
. On specifying a path space
\Omega(I)
, take
\mathscr{C}
to be defined for any
h
in
H(I,\mathcal{S})
by

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(h)

\displaystyle=\left\{\omega\in\Omega(I)\colon\forall i\in\mathds{N}_{0}\cap[0,\ell(h)],\;\omega(e_{h}(i))\in U_{h}(i)\right\}.

For any history
h
,
\mathscr{C}(h)
is a
simple cylinder set
. The set
\mathscr{C}(H(I,\mathcal{S}))
is the
set of all simple cylinder sets of
\Omega(I)
and is a
\pi
-system, but not an algebra. The
set of cylinder sets
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathscr{C}(H(I,\mathcal{S}))
—its elements are the
cylinder sets
.

The Kolmogorov extension theorem guarantees that there is a measure
{\rm P}
on the cylinder sets of
F([0,\infty)\colon\mathcal{S})
, the space of all paths from
[0,\infty)
to
\mathcal{S}
, so that for any history
h
, if
U_{h}(0)
contains
0
, then

(4)

\displaystyle{\rm P}(\mathscr{C}(h))=\int_{U_{h}(1)}\cdots\int_{U_{h}(\ell(h))}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(h)}\rho(e_{h}(i)-e_{h}(i-1),x_{i}-x_{i-1})\,{\rm d}\mu(x_{1})\cdots{\rm d}\mu(x_{\ell(h)}),

and if
U_{h}(0)
does not contain
0
, then
{\rm P}(\mathscr{C}(h))
is equal to
0
. In all current settings, certain moment estimates for
{\rm P}
will guarantee that there is a version of the stochastic process
(F([0,\infty)\colon\mathcal{S}),{\rm P},Y)
with sample paths in
D([0,\infty)\colon\mathcal{S})

[
9
,
10
]
. In the case when
I
is
\mathds{N}_{0}
and
\mathcal{S}
is a discrete space, the measure
\mu
will be a counting measure, or weighted counting measure, and the integrals will be sums.

The probabilities that are given by (
4
) are the
finite dimensional distributions
for the stochastic process
(D([0,\infty)\colon\mathcal{S}),{\rm P},Y)
. It is frequently useful to specify the finite dimensional distributions for a stochastic process by using the properties of stochastic processes before knowing that there is, indeed, a stochastic process with the given finite dimensional distributions. Refer to the construction of the finite dimensional distributions for a stochastic process as the construction of the
abstract stochastic process

\tilde{Y}
and the construction of the law for a random variable without actually specifying its domain as the construction of an
abstract random variable

\tilde{X}
. A model for the abstract random variable
\tilde{X}
is a random variable with the same law as
\tilde{X}
. A model for the abstract stochastic process
\tilde{Y}
is a stochastic process with the same finite dimensional distributions as
\tilde{Y}
. Distinguish probabilities associated with abstract random variables and stochastic processes from those associated with their models by writing
{\rm Prob}(\tilde{X}\in A)
for the probability that
\tilde{X}
takes a value in some Borel set
A
. Use an analogous notation for
\tilde{Y}
.

2.2.
Brownian motion in
\mathds{Q}_{p}

The field of
p
-adic numbers
(\mathds{Q}_{p},+,\times)
is the completion of the rational numbers with respect to the
p
-adic absolute value,
|\cdot|
. For each integer
k
, denote by
{\mathds{B}}(k)
and
{\mathds{S}}(k)
the sets

{\mathds{B}}(k)=\{x\in\mathds{Q}_{p}\colon|x|_{p}\leq p^{k}\}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathds{S}}(k)=\{x\in\mathds{Q}_{p}\colon|x|_{p}=p^{k}\},

and by
\mathds{Z}_{p}
the
ring of integers
, the set

. Take
\mu
to be the unique Haar measure on the locally compact Abelian group
(\mathds{Q}_{p},+)
that gives
\mathds{Z}_{p}
unit measure.

For any
x
in
\mathds{Q}_{p}
, there is a unique function
a_{x}
with

(5)

a_{x}\colon\mathds{Z}\to\{0,1,2,\dots,p-1\}\quad\text{and}\quad x=\sum_{k\in\mathds{Z}}a_{x}(k)p^{k}.

Denote by
\{x\}
the
fractional part of
x
, where

\{x\}=\sum_{k<0}a_{x}(k)p^{k},

and by
\chi
the additive rank 0 character on
\mathds{Q}_{p}
that is given by

\chi(x)=e^{2\pi{\sqrt{-1}}\{x\}}.

The Pontryagin dual of
\mathds{Q}_{p}
is again
\mathds{Q}_{p}
, and every character on
\mathds{Q}_{p}
can be written as
\chi(z\,\cdot\,)
for some
z
in
\mathds{Q}_{p}
, where for any
y
in
\mathds{Q}_{p}
,

\chi(z\,\cdot\,)(y)=\chi(zy).

The Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
and inverse Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
are unitary operators on

that are given for any
f
in

(\mathcal{F}f)(x)=\int_{\mathds{Q}_{p}}\chi(-xy)f(y)\,{\rm d}\mu\!\left(y\right)\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathcal{F}^{-1}f)(y)=\int_{\mathds{Q}_{p}}\chi(xy)f(x)\,{\rm d}\mu(x).

Denote by
SB(\mathds{Q}_{p})
the Schwartz-Bruhat space of compactly supported, locally constant,
\mathds{C}
-valued functions. Take
{\mathcal{M}}
to be the multiplication operator that acts on
SB(\mathds{Q}_{p})
by

({\mathcal{M}}f)(x)=|x|^{b}f(x).

Denote by
\Delta^{\prime}_{b}
the unique self-adjoint extension of the essentially self-adjoint operator that acts on any
f
in
SB(\mathds{Q}_{p})
by

(\Delta^{\prime}_{b}f)(x)=\big(\mathcal{F}^{-1}{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{F}f\big)\!(x).

For any
\mathds{C}
-valued function
g
with domain
(0,\infty)\times\mathds{Q}_{p}
and any
t
in
(0,\infty)
, take
g_{t}
to be the function that is defined for every
x
in
\mathds{Q}_{p}
by

g_{t}(x)=g(t,x).

Denote by
\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{b})
the set of all such
g
so that for all
t
in
(0,\infty)
,
g_{t}
is in the domain of
\Delta^{\prime}_{b}
. Take
\Delta_{b}
to be the
Vladimirov operator with exponent
b
that acts on any
g
in
\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{b})
by

(\Delta_{b}g)(t,x)=(\Delta_{b}g_{t})(x).

Similarly extend the Fourier transform to act on
\mathds{C}
-valued functions on
(0,\infty)\times\mathds{Q}_{p}
that are square integrable for each positive
t
and the operator
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}
to act on
\mathds{C}
-valued functions on
(0,\infty)\times\mathds{Q}_{p}
that for any
x
in
\mathds{Q}_{p}
are differentiable in the
t
variable.

Take
D
, the diffusion coefficient, to be any positive real number. Denote by
\rho
the fundamental solution to (
1
), which for any
(t,x)
in
(0,\infty)\times\mathds{Q}_{p}
is given by

(6)

\rho(t,x)=\left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}{\rm e}^{-Dt|\cdot|^{b}}\right)\!(x).

For any positive
t
, the Fourier transform of
\rho(t,\cdot)
is the function
\phi(t,\cdot)
that is given by

(7)

\phi(t,x)={\rm e}^{-Dt|x|^{b}},

and so
\rho
is given by

(8)

\rho(t,x)=\sum_{k\in\mathds{Z}}p^{k}\left({\rm e}^{-Dtp^{kb}}-{\rm e}^{-Dtp^{(k+1)b}}\right)\mathds{1}_{{\mathds{B}}(-k)}(x).

Follow Varadarajan
[
20
]
to see that there is a measure
{\rm P}
on the space

that gives rise to the finite dimensional distributions given by (

-adic Brownian motion.

Take

The

The Pontryagin dual of

Define the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{0}

to

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{0}f)(y)=\int_{\mathds{Z}_{p}}\langle-x,\langle y\rangle\rangle f(x)\,{\rm d}\mu(x)\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathcal{F}_{0}^{-1}\tilde{f})(x)=\int_{\mathds{Q}_{p}/\penalty\mathds{Z}_{p}}\langle x,\langle y\rangle\rangle\tilde{f}(\langle y\rangle)\,{\rm d}\mu_{0}(\langle y\rangle).

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Vol({\mathds{B}}_{m}(k))=p^{k-m}&\text{}\\

p^{-ir}=\left(x_{i}+\frac{1}{p^{mb}-1}\right)^{-\frac{r}{b}}p^{-rm}\beta^{\frac{r}{b}}<x_{i}^{-\frac{r}{b}}p^{-rm}\beta^{\frac{r}{b}},

which implies together with (

(29)

\displaystyle I(m,n)

\displaystyle\leq Kp^{-rm}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}nx_{i}^{-\frac{r}{b}}\left(1-x_{i}\right)^{n-1}\Delta(i),

where

K=\frac{c(m)p^{r}(p-1)}{p^{b}(p^{b}-1)(p^{r+1}-1)}\beta^{\frac{r-b}{b}}.

The sum in (
29
) is a lower Riemann sum approximation of the integral

\displaystyle\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{m-1}}nx^{-\frac{r}{b}}(1-x)^{n-1}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle<\int_{0}^{1}nx^{-\frac{r}{b}}(1-x)^{n-1}\,{\rm d}x.

Follow the earlier arguments in the
\mathds{Q}_{p}
and local field settings
[

,
18
]
to obtain the inequality

(30)

\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}nx_{i}^{-\frac{r}{b}}\left(1-x_{i}\right)^{n-1}\Delta(i-1)\leq n^{-\frac{b-r}{b}}\Big(\frac{n+\frac{b-r}{b}}{n}\Big)^{\frac{r}{b}},

which together with (
29
) implies that

(31)

I(m,n)\leq Kp^{-rm}n^{\frac{r}{b}}\Big(\frac{n+\frac{b-r}{b}}{n}\Big)^{\frac{r}{b}}.

Take
C
to be equal to
K\Big(\frac{n+\frac{b-r}{b}}{n}\Big)^{\frac{r}{b}}
to obtain the inequality

(32)

I(m,n)\leq Cp^{-rm}n^{\frac{r}{b}}.

The inequality (
20
) gives an upper bound for the right hand term in (

\displaystyle\Big(1-\phi(1)^{n}\Big)\left(p-1\right)p^{r}\left\{\frac{1-p^{-m(r+1)}}{p^{r+1}-1}\right\}\leq\left(1-\left(1-\dfrac{\beta p^{b}\big(1+\frac{1}{p^{mb}}\big)}{p^{mb}}\right)^{n}\right)\frac{p^{r}(p-1)}{p^{r+1}-1}.

∎

5.1.
The embedded processes

Given any null sequence
(\tau_{m})
, a sequence of
time scales
, take
(\iota_{m})
to be a sequence of functions so that for each

\iota_{m}\colon\mathds{N}_{0}\times G_{m}\to[0,\infty)\times\mathds{Z}_{p}\quad\text{by}\quad\iota(n,[x])=(\tau_{m}n,\Gamma_{m}([x])).

For typographical efficiency, take
\lambda_{m}
to be the reciprocal of
\tau_{m}
. The function
\iota_{m}
acts on the stochastic process
\big(F(\mathds{N}_{0}\colon G_{m}),{\rm P}^{\ast}_{m},S\big)
to produce a continuous time process in the following way. For any real number

{\rm P}_{m}
on any simple cylinder set

(33)

{\rm P}_{m}(C(h))={\rm P}^{\ast}_{m}\left(\bigcap_{i\in\{0,1,\dots,\ell(h)\}}\left(S_{\lfloor e_{h}(i)\lambda\rfloor}\right)^{-1}(\Gamma^{-1}_{m}(U(i))\right).

Take
\sigma
to be any positive real number, and specialize
(\tau_{m})
so that

\lambda_{m}=\sigma p^{mb}.

Take
{\mathds{E}}_{m}
to be the expected value with respect to the measure
{\rm P}_{m}
. Denote by

the quantity

A straightforward calculation shows that if

\beta p^{b}\Big(1+\frac{1}{p^{mb}}\Big)<p^{mb}.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any natural number

that is greater than

and any real number

in
(0,1)
, there is a constant

so that for any

in

,

\displaystyle\left(1-\left(1-\dfrac{\beta p^{b}\big(1+\frac{1}{p^{mb}}\big)}{p^{mb}}\right)^{t\sigma p^{mb}}\right)\leq Kt^{s}.

Proof.

For any natural number

and any

in
[0,n]
,

(34)

1-x\leq\Big(1-\frac{x}{n}\Big)^{n}\quad\text{and so}\quad x\geq 1-\Big(1-\frac{x}{n}\Big)^{n}.

Change variables and take
m
to be large enough so that

\beta p^{b}\Big(1+\frac{1}{p^{mb}}\Big)\leq p^{mb}

to see that (

) implies that

2p^{b}\beta\sigma t\geq\left(1-\left(1-\dfrac{\beta p^{b}\big(1+\frac{1}{p^{mb}}\big)}{p^{mb}}\right)^{t\sigma p^{mb}}\right).

The quantity on the right hand side of (

) is bounded above by

, and for any
t
in
[0,1]
,
t^{s}
is greater than or equal to

, so

(36)

\max(2p^{b}\beta\sigma,1)t^{s}\geq\left(1-\left(1-\dfrac{\beta p^{b}\big(1+\frac{1}{p^{mb}}\big)}{p^{mb}}\right)^{t\sigma p^{mb}}\right).

∎

Denote by
N(p,b)
the maximum of
M(p,b)
and

.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any natural number
m
that is greater than

and any positive real number

, there is a constant

that is independent of
t
so that for any

in
(0,b)

{\mathds{E}}_{m}[|Y_{t}|^{r}]\leq Ct^{\frac{r}{b}}.

Proof.

For any positive real number

, there is an increasing sequence
(t_{m})
so that

t_{m}\lambda_{m}=\lfloor t\lambda_{m}\rfloor\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{m\to\infty}t_{m}=t.

The equality

{\mathds{E}}_{m}[|Y_{t}|^{r}]={\mathds{E}}_{m}^{\ast}[|S_{t_{m}\lambda_{m}}|^{r}]

together with Theorem
4.1
, with

replaced by

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}(t_{m}\lambda_{m})^{\frac{r}{b}}p^{-mr}+\left(1-\left(1-\dfrac{\beta p^{b}\big(1+\frac{1}{p^{mb}}\big)}{p^{mb}}\right)^{t_{m}\sigma p^{mb}}\right)\frac{p^{r}(p-1)}{p^{r+1}-1}

\displaystyle\leq Kt_{m}^{\frac{r}{b}}\sigma^{\frac{r}{b}}\leq Kt^{\frac{r}{b}}\sigma^{\frac{r}{b}},

since

is bounded above by

.

∎

Henceforth, take

to be greater than or equal to

.

Proposition 5.2
.

Each stochastic process
