Escape from compact sets of normal curves in Carnot groups
Enrico Le Donne, Nicola Paddeu

Abstract
. In the setting of subFinsler Carnot groups, we consider curves that satisfy the normal equation coming from the Pontryagin Maximum Principle. We show that, unless it is constant, each such a curve leaves every compact set, quantitatively. Namely, the distance between the points at time 0 and time t t grows at least of the order of t 1 / s t^{1/s} , where s s denotes the step of the Carnot group. In particular, in subFinsler Carnot groups there are no periodic normal geodesics.



1.
Introduction

This paper originates from the following question:
can subRiemannian manifolds have arbitrarily short geodesic loops?
Important subRiemannian manifolds are Carnot groups, which have dilation structures and therefore are self-similar. Hence, for these spaces, the question rephrases as:
do geodesic loops exist in a Carnot group?
We remark that not only geodesic loops do not exist in the Euclidean spaces, but also do not exist in normed vector spaces with strictly convex norm. Finite-dimensional normed vector spaces are exactly the Carnot groups of nilpotency step 1, equipped with geodesic distances.
We refer to
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
for an introduction to Carnot groups and the fact that when equipped with their Carnot-Carathéodory distances they are exactly the metric spaces that are homogeneous, locally compact, geodesic, and admit dilations.

Some difficulties in Carnot groups are that the distance function may not be convex, that at every scale there exist pairs of points joined by more than one geodesic, and geodesics may not be globally length-minimizing.
However, the biggest difficulty, as in other subRiemannian problems, is the presence of geodesics that are singular points of the endpoint map, which we call the
abnormal geodesics
.
By the Pontryagin Maximum Principle we know that if a geodesic is not abnormal, then it satisfies a geodesic equation called the
normal equation
. The curves satisfying the normal equation, called
normal curves
(or
normal trajectories
), are more manageable. In this paper we focus on normal curves and we prove that, except for the constant ones, they cannot form loops and actually they leave every compact set in a quantitative way.
The main result of this paper is the following statement.

Theorem 1.1

(Normal curves leave compact sets)
.

Let
G
be a subFinsler Carnot group of step
s
.
Fix a norm
N
on the space of right-invariant co-vectors. Then, there exists a constant
\epsilon>0
such that for every normal curve
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow G
parametrized by arc-length there holds

(1)

d(\gamma(t),\gamma(t^{\prime}))\geq\frac{\epsilon}{N(\lambda)^{\frac{1}{s}}}|t-t^{\prime}|^{\frac{1}{s}}-1,\ \ \forall t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},

for every co-vector
\lambda
associated to
\gamma
, see Definition
33
.

As a consequence we rule out the presence of some geodesic loops.

Corollary 1.2
.

In subFinsler Carnot groups normal loops are constant.

We stress that the above
corollary has been know to be true in step 2 Carnot groups (by the complete integration of geodesics) and in jet spaces by the work of Bravo-Doddoli
[
5
]
.
Moreover, a similar question arose in geometric group theory of the large-scale geometry of nilpotent groups. In fact, because of the work of Hoda
[
11
]
, it would be important to prove that Carnot groups cannot have isometric copies of the standard unit circle. The latter result is known for finite-dimensional normed spaces and was proven by Creutz, see
[
8
, Lemma 1.7]
.

1.1.
The basic case of loops in subRiemannian Carnot groups

We give here a simpler proof of the non-existence of normal loops in subRiemannian Carnot groups. The same idea will be pushed to prove Corollary
1.2
and to show the quantitative result of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Corollary
1.2
for subRiemannian Carnot groups.

Let
G
be a subRiemannian Carnot group with left-invariant scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
Let
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow G
be
a normal geodesic that makes a loop.
Without loss of generality we assume

\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=1_{G}
.

Since
\gamma
is normal, by definition its control

u
satisfies the
normal equation
: for some

\lambda\in(T_{\gamma(1)}G)^{*}
and all controls
v
we have

(2)

\lambda(\mathrm{d}\End_{u}v)=\langle u,v\rangle,

where
\End
is the end-point map from
1_{G}
, see for example
[
1
, Corollary 8.8]
. When
v=u
we get

(
2
bis)

\lambda(\mathrm{d}\End_{u}u)=||u||_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Now the idea is to consider the curve dilated by the Carnot dilations: for
\tau>0
let
\delta_{\tau}:G\to G
be the dilation of factor
\tau
.
On the one hand, we obviously have

(\delta_{\tau}\circ\gamma)(1)=1_{G}
.
On the other hand, the control of the curve
\delta_{\tau}\circ\gamma
is
\tau u
.
Hence
we have that

(3)

\End(\tau u)=\delta_{\tau}(\End(u))=1_{G},\ \ \forall\tau>0.

Equation (
3
) implies that

(4)

\mathrm{d}\End_{u}u=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\End((1+\epsilon)u)\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}=0.

Consequently, equations (
4
) and (
bis
) imply that
||u||_{L^{2}}=0
and therefore
\gamma
is the constant curve. ∎

1.2.
The strategy of the proof

We present here the key ideas that we shall use in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We work in the context of subFinsler spaces, smooth manifolds equipped with a distribution, in which the distance between two points is given by the infimum of the lengths of paths tangent to the distribution joining the two points. The length is measured with respect to a continuously varying norm.

We start by writing the normal equation coming from the Pontryagin Maximum Principle in terms of the sub-differentials of the energy, see Proposition
2.13
. We use this formulation of the normal equation to get the analog of (
bis
), which expresses the energy of a normal control
u
in terms of some co-vector
\lambda
applied to
\mathrm{d}\End_{u}u
.
We then use the properties of the one parameter subgroup of dilations
\delta
to define the vector field

\vec{\delta}(g):=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta_{t}(g)\right|_{t=1},\forall g\in G.

The first key point is that we have
\vec{\delta}(\End(u))=\mathrm{d}\End_{u}u
, see (
44
). In particular we shall get

(5)

||u||^{2}_{L^{2}}=\lambda(\vec{\delta}(\End(u)).

We show that
g\mapsto\lambda(\vec{\delta}(g))
is a finite sum of homogeneous functions of degree of homogeneity smaller than the step
s
, and therefore it can be bounded by
Cd(1,g)^{s}
when
d(1,g)>1
, for some constant
C>0
. This bound together with (
5
) will give (
1
).

1.3.
Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows: we dedicate Section 2 to a brief presentation of the notions of subFinsler geometry that we need in the paper. We recall the definitions of subFinsler manifolds, Carnot groups, and self-similar spaces; we define normal curves and we characterize them in terms of sub-differentials of the energy. In Section 3, we prove the main results of the paper. We also prove a slight generalization of Corollary
1.2
to self-similar spaces. Section 4 contains some examples: we show that length-
1
normal geodesics can stay arbitrarily close to the starting point and that the exponent
\frac{1}{s}
in Theorem
1.1
is the biggest possible.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
SubFinsler geometry and Carnot groups

We start recalling some basic definitions and facts from subFinsler geometry following
[
12
]
and
[
3
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold. A
distribution
on
M
is a sub-bundle of the tangent bundle. We call a distribution
bracket-generating
if, in some neighborhood of every point, the Lie algebra generated by vector fields tangent to the distribution contains a frame for the tangent bundle on that neighborhood. A
subFinsler manifold
is a smooth manifold
M
, with a bracket-generating distrubution
\Delta
and a continuously varying norm
||\cdot||
, equipped with the Carnot-Carathéodory distance

(6)

\begin{split}d_{cc}(x,y):=\inf\{\int_{0}^{1}||\dot{\gamma}(t)||\mathrm{d}t\ \big|\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M\text{ absolutely continuous; }\\
\dot{\gamma}(t)\in\Delta_{\gamma(t)},\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,1];\gamma(0)=x;\gamma(1)=y\}.\end{split}

The fact that
d_{cc}
is a distance is guaranteed by Chow’s Theorem (see for example
[
9
, Section 1]
).
In this paper we focus mainly on Carnot groups: particular Lie groups with left-invariant distributions and norms.

Definition 2.2
.

A
stratification
of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
as a direct sum
\mathfrak{g}=V_{1}\oplus...\oplus V_{s}
with

(7)

[V_{1},V_{j}]=V_{j+1},\ \ \forall j\in\{1,...,s\},

where
V_{s+1}:=\{0\}
. The sub-spaces
V_{i}
are called
strata
of the stratification. A Lie algebra equipped with a stratification is called
stratified
.
A
Carnot group
is a simply connected Lie group with stratified Lie algebra, with the left-invariant extension of the first stratum as distribution and a left-invariant norm, equipped with the Carnot-Carathéodory distance.

Carnot groups are self-similar, in the sense that
there are natural automorphisms that act as homotheties.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
G
be Carnot group with stratified Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=V_{1}\oplus...\oplus V_{s}
. The
dilation

\delta_{\lambda}:G\rightarrow G
of factor
\lambda
, with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, is the Lie group automorphism defined setting

(8)

(\delta_{\lambda})_{*}(v)=\lambda^{i}v,\ \ ,\forall i\in\{1,...,s\},\ \forall v\in V_{i}.

The presence of dilations will be crucial in our proof of the non-existence of periodic normal geodesics, see (
61
).

2.2.
Homogeneous spaces and self-similar distances

In differential geometry, the term
homogeneous space
is referred to the quotient space of a Lie group modulo a closed subgroup, in order to still have a transitive action of the Lie group. However, in metric geometry and, more generally in analysis on metric spaces, the term homogeneous is referred to functions that gets multiplied by some dilations of the space.
Every metric space admitting a dilation, also called homothety, is said to be self-similar.

In subFinsler geometry, self-similar spaces are well characterized. As differentiable manifold they have a homogeneous structure of a quotient space of a Carnot group modulo the action of a dilation-invariant subgroup via left-multiplication. However, the well-defined action on the right is not by isometries. Hence they are not isometrically homogeneous spaces. They are still called homogeneous because they admit dilations. To avoid this double use of the word homogeneity, we shall call them self-similar (subFinsler) spaces. By the work of Bellaïche (
[
4
]
,
[
3
]
) we know that the metric tangents of (constant-rank) subFinsler manifolds are (constant-rank) self-similar spaces.

.

A
self-similar subFinsler space
is a subFinsler manifold obtained as the quotient space of a (left-invariant) subFinsler Carnot group with respect to the left-action of a dilation-invariant subgroup, and it is equipped with the quotient distribution. Namely,
assume
G
is a Carnot group with distribution
\Delta
and left-invariant norm
||\cdot||
and

is a closed dilation-invariant subgroup, for which

. On the quotient manifold
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{G}$}}}:=\{Hg:g\in G\}
we define a subFinsler structure that makes the projection

2.3.
Pontryagin Maximum Principle and normal curves

In the remaining part of this section we define the end-point map and we state the Pontryagin Maximum Principle. Afterwards, we present normal curves and we characterize them using sub-differentials of the energy.

Definition 2.5
.

(Controls)

Let
M
be a subFinsler manifold with distribution
\Delta
, choose
X_{1},...,X_{n}\in\Vecc(M)
such that
\Span(\{X_{1}(p),...,X_{n}(p)\})=\Delta_{p}
for every
p\in M
. The elements of
\Omega:=L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{n})
are called controls.

Consider a subFinsler manifold
M
and fix
X_{1},...,X_{n}\in\Vecc(M)
such that the distribution at every
p\in M
coincides with
\Span(\{X_{1}(p),...,X_{n}(p)\})
. Let
\Omega
be
L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
To each control
u\in\Omega
and each point
p\in M
we associate the unique curve
\gamma_{u}:[0,1]\rightarrow M
solving

(10)

\begin{cases}\gamma_{u}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}(t)X_{i}(\gamma_{u}(t)),\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,1];\\
\gamma_{u}(0)=p.\end{cases}

The existence and uniqueness of the solution of (
10
) is guaranteed by Carathéodory Existence and Uniqueness Theorem (
[
15
, Theorem 3.4]
,
[
7
, Theorem 1.3]
).
We shall say that
\gamma_{u}
is a/the
curve with control
u
, or that
u
is a
control
of
\gamma_{u}
. Two controls could give the same curve, because the vector fields
X_{j}
’s might not be linearly independent at some point. Moreover, every curve of finite length admits a reparametrization with control in
\Omega
.
We can use Equation (
10
) to define the flow along a control. This will be useful for the computation of the differential of the end-point map.

Definition 2.6
.

(Flow along a control)

Let
M
be a subFinsler manifold with distribution
\Delta
, let
X_{1},...,X_{n}\in\Vecc(M)
be such that
\Span(\{X_{1}(p),...,X_{n}(p)\})=\Delta_{p}
for every
p\in M
and define
\Omega:=L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The
flow along a control

u\in\Omega
is the map
\phi:[0,1]\times M\rightarrow M
defined as

(11)

\phi^{t}(p):=\gamma_{u}(t),\ \forall t\in[0,1],

where
\gamma_{u}
is the curve solving (
10
). For fixed
s,t\in[0,1]
, with
s<t
, we will write
\phi_{s}^{t}:M\rightarrow M
for the flow from
s
to
t
:

(12)

\phi_{s}^{t}:=\phi^{t-s}\circ(\phi^{s})^{-1}.

Example 2.7
.

Let
G
be a subFinsler Carnot group with first stratum
V_{1}
. Let
X_{1},...,X_{n}
be a left-invariant frame of the first stratum. Using the frame
X_{1},...,X_{n}
identify
L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
L^{2}([0,1],V_{1})
. For all
p\in G
and
u\in L^{2}([0,1],V_{1})
, equation (
10
) rewrites as

(13)

\begin{cases}\gamma_{u}^{\prime}(t)=\mathrm{d}L_{\gamma_{u}(t)}u(t),\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,1];\\
\gamma_{u}(0)=p.\end{cases}

In particular, if
\phi:[0,1]\times G\rightarrow G
is the flow along a control
u\in L^{2}([0,1],V_{1})
, see (
11
), then it is trivial to check that for all

the curve
t\mapsto L_{p}(\phi^{t}(1_{G}))
solves (

(14)

\phi^{t}(p)=R_{\phi^{t}(1_{G})}(p),\ \forall p\in G,\forall t\in[0,1].

Definition 2.8
.

(End-point map)

Let
M
be a subFinsler manifold with distribution
\Delta
. Choose
X_{1},....,X_{n}\in\Vecc(M)
such that
\Span(\{X_{1}(p),...,X_{n}(p)\})=\Delta_{p}
. Denote
\Omega:=L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{n})
and fix a point

. The

\End:\Omega\rightarrow M
associated to

and

(15)

\End(u):=\gamma_{u}(1),

where
\gamma_{u}
solves (

In a Carnot group we always assume the point

to be a left-invariant frame of the first stratum.

Proposition 2.9
.

(Differential of the end-point map,
[
1
, Proposition 8.5]
).

Let
M
be a subFinsler manifold with distribution
\Delta
, let
X_{1},...,X_{m}\in\Vecc(M)
such that
\Delta_{p}=\Span(\{X_{1}(p),...,X_{m}(p)\})
for every
p\in M
. Fix

. Set
\Omega:=L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{m})
and denote with
\End:\Omega\rightarrow M
the end-point map associated to

and

. Then
\End
is smooth on

and for every

(16)

\mathrm{d}\End_{u}v=\int_{0}^{1}(\phi_{t}^{1})_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}(t)X_{i}(\gamma(t))\mathrm{d}t;

where
\phi^{t}
is the flow associated to the control

and
\gamma(t):=\phi^{t}(q)
, see (
11
).

Definition 2.10
.

(Energy)

Let
M
be a subFinsler manifold with continuously varying norm
||\cdot||:\Delta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The
energy
at a point
p\in M
is the function
\E_{p}:\Delta_{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

\E_{p}:=\frac{1}{2}||\cdot||_{p}^{2}.

For an absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M
with
\dot{\gamma}\in\Delta
we define its
energy
as
\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}||\dot{\gamma}(t)||^{2}\mathrm{d}t
.

The curves realizing the infimum in (
6
) are called
length-minimizing
. It is well-known that the same infimum is realized by minimizers of the energy, considering length-minimizing curves re-parametrized by constant speed.

We are now ready to state the well known Pontryagin Maximum Principle, which gives first-order necessary conditions for curves to be energy-minimizing.

Theorem 2.11
.

(PMP,
[
2
, Theorem 12.10]
).

Let
M
be a subFinsler manifold with distribution
\Delta
, let
X_{1},...,X_{m}\in\Vecc(M)
such that
\Delta_{p}=\Span\{X_{1}(p),...,X_{m}(p)\}
for every
p\in M
. Let
\Omega
be
L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{m})
.

For every
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
and
v=(v_{1},...,v_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
; define
h_{v,\nu}:T^{*}M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

(17)

h_{v,\nu}(\eta):=\left\langle\eta,\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}X_{i}(\pi(\eta))\right\rangle+\nu\E_{\pi(\eta)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}X_{i}(\pi(\eta))\right),\ \ \forall\eta\in T^{*}M,

where
\pi:T^{*}M\rightarrow M
is the canonical projection.
If a curve
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M
with control
u
is energy-minimizing, then there exists
\nu\in\{-1,0\}
and an absolutely continuous curve
\eta:[0,1]\rightarrow T^{*}M
such that
\eta(0)\in T^{*}_{\gamma(0)}M
and

(18)

\dot{\eta}(t)=\vec{h}_{u(t),\nu}(\eta(t)),\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,1];

(19)

h_{u(t),\nu}(\eta(t))\geq h_{v,\nu}(\eta(t)),\ \ \forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,1],

where
\vec{h}_{u(t),\nu}(\eta(t))

In this paper we will use a reformulation of Proposition
2.11
. We first need to recall the definition of sub-differential.

Definition 2.12
.

(Sub-differentials)

Let
V
be a vector space and
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. A
sub-differential
of
f
at
v\in V
is a linear function
a:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

(20)

a(u-v)\leq f(u)-f(v),\quad\forall u\in V.

We will use
\partial_{v}f
to denote the

of
f
at
v
.

It is an easy exercise to check that the set of sub-differentials of a convex function is always a non-empty closed convex set.

Proposition 2.13
.

(subFinsler PMP revised)

Let
M
be a subFinsler manifold with distribution
\Delta
, let

such that
\Delta_{p}=\Span(\{X_{1}(p),...,X_{m}(p)\})

. If a curve
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M
with control

\left(\phi_{t}^{1}\right)^{*}\lambda\in\partial_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}\E_{\gamma(t)},\ \ \text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,1]

or

\lambda\left(\left(\phi_{t}^{1}\right)_{*}X\right)=0,\ \ \forall t\in[0,1],\ \forall X\in\Delta_{\gamma(t)},

the function

denoting the flow along the control

Proof.

By Proposition
2.11
if a curve
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow M
with control

is energy-minimizing then there exist
\nu\in\{-1,0\}
and an absolutely continuous curve
\eta:[0,1]\rightarrow T^{*}M
such that

and equations (

) and (

It is well know (see for example
[

, Section 12.2]
) that Equation (

) rewrites for all

\begin{cases}\pi(\eta(t))=\gamma(t);\\

Setting

, by (

) and the definition of

∎

Definition 2.15

Let

The following remark ensures that the restriction of a normal curve to every sub-interval of its domain is a normal curve and therefore that Definition

Remark 2.16

Let

3.
Proof of main results

3.1.
The differential of the dilations

This sub-section is devoted to a computation of the differential of the one-parameter family
\delta
of dilations in Carnot groups. The aim is to write the vector field

(34)

\vec{\delta}(g):=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\delta_{\tau}(g)\bigg|_{\tau=1}

as a linear combination of right-invariant vector-fields. Then, using the particular form of the coefficients of this combination, we will get an estimate of
\lambda(\vec{\delta}(g))
in terms of the distance
d(1,g)
for every right-invariant co-vector
\lambda
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a Carnot group with stratified Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=V_{1}\oplus...\oplus V_{s}
. An ordered basis
X_{1},...,X_{n}
of
\mathfrak{g}
is
adapted to the stratification
if

X_{i}\in V_{j},\ \ \forall j\in{1,...,s},\forall i\in\{1,...,n\}\text{ s.t. }\dim(V_{j-1})<i\leq\dim(V_{j}),

where we are setting
V_{0}:=\{0\}
.

We say that a vector
X\in\mathfrak{g}
has
degree of homogeneity

j\in\{1,...,s\}
if
X\in V_{j}
.
We say that a function
f:G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
homogeneous
of degree
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
if
f\circ\delta_{\tau}=\tau^{\alpha}f
for all
\tau\geq 0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a Carnot group, fix a basis
X_{1},...,X_{m}
adapted to the stratification and let
\vec{\delta}
be as in (
34
). Then

(35)

\vec{\delta}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}P_{i}X_{i}^{\dagger},

where, for each
i\in\{1,...,m\}
,
we denote by
X_{i}^{\dagger}
the right-invariant extension of
X_{i}
and
P_{i}:G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are homogeneous functions of degree
d_{i}
,
the integer
d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
being the degree of homogeneity of
X_{i}
.

Proof.

Fix
\tau>0
. For all
g\in G
we have

(36)

\left(\left(\delta_{\tau}\right)_{*}\vec{\delta}\right)(g)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\delta_{\tau}\circ\delta_{1+\epsilon}(\delta_{\tau}^{-1}(g))\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\delta_{1+\epsilon}(g)\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}=\vec{\delta}(g).

Moreover, for all
i\in\{1,...,m\}
there holds

(37)

\left(\delta_{\tau}\right)_{*}X_{i}^{\dagger}=\tau^{d_{i}}X_{i}^{\dagger},

since
\left(\delta_{\tau}\right)_{*}X_{i}^{\dagger}(1_{G})=\tau^{d_{i}}X_{i}^{\dagger}(1_{G})
and
\left(\delta_{\tau}\right)_{*}X_{i}^{\dagger}
is a right-invariant vector field, being
\delta_{\tau}
a group homomorphism and
X_{i}^{\dagger}
right-invariant.

Since
\vec{\delta}
is a smooth vector field there exist smooth functions
P_{1},...,P_{m}:G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that (
35
) holds. To conclude the proof of the lemma we have to show that

(38)

P_{i}\circ\delta_{\tau}=\tau^{d_{i}}P_{i},\ \ \forall i\in\{1,...,m\}.

For all
i\in\{1,...,m\}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}P_{i}X_{i}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e differenzialedelta}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\vec{\delta}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e pushforwarddelta}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{\tau}\right)_{*}\vec{\delta}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e differenzialedelta}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{\tau}\right)_{*}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}P_{i}X_{i}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e pushforwardXi}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau^{d_{i}}(P_{i}\circ\delta_{\tau}^{-1})X_{i}^{\dagger},

and consequently (
38
) holds.
∎

The identity found in Lemma
3.2
allows us to give an estimate of the value that we get applying a right-invariant co-vector to the vector
\vec{\delta}(g)
defined in (
34
). Up to constants, the bound we find depends only on the co-vector and on the distance of
g
from the origin. To get a quantitative estimate we fix a norm on the Lie algebra.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and
\lambda
a right-invariant co-vector. Choose a basis
\{X_{1},...,X_{n}\}
of
\,T_{1}G
and define

(39)

N(\lambda):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda(X_{i})|.

Up to a multiplicative constant
the norm
N
is equivalent to every other norm on the space of right-invariant co-vectors.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
G
be a Carnot group. Define
\vec{\delta}
as in (
34
). There exists a constant
C>0
such that for every right-invariant co-vector
\lambda
we have

(40)

\lambda(\vec{\delta}(g))\leq CN(\lambda)\max(d(1,g),d(1,g)^{s}),\qquad\forall g\in G,

where
N(\lambda)
is defined as in Definition
3.3
.

Proof.

Choose a basis
X_{1},...,X_{n}
adapted to the stratification; call
\{X_{i}^{\dagger}\}_{i\in\{1,...,n\}}
the right-invariant extension of this basis. Let
d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
be the degree of homogeneity of
X_{i}
. From Lemma
3.2
we have that (
35
) holds. For all
i\in\{1,...,n\}
, being the function
P_{i}
homogeneous of degree
d_{i}
, there exist a constant
C_{i}>0
such that

(41)

P_{i}(g)\leq C_{i}d(1,g)^{d_{i}},\ \ \forall g\in G.

Setting
C=\max_{1,...,n}C_{i}
, for every right-invariant co-vector
\lambda
, by definition of
N(\lambda)
, we have

(42)

\lambda(\vec{\delta}(g))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e differenzialedelta}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}(g)\lambda(X_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e normalambda},\eqref{e inequalitihomogeneity}}}{{\leq}}CN(\lambda)\max(d(1,g),d(1,g)^{s}),\ \ \forall g\in G.

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

3.2.

We are now ready to prove the main result of this paper.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow G

,

is defined by (

Applying Lemma
3.4
we have that there exists a constant

such that

\lambda(\vec{\delta}(\End(u_{t})))\leq CN(\lambda)\max(d(1,\End(u_{t})),d(1,\End(u_{t}))^{s}),\ \ \forall t>0.

) and (

\lambda(\mathrm{d}\End_{u_{t}}u_{t})\leq CN(\lambda)\max(d(1,\gamma(t)),d(1,\gamma(t))^{s}),\ \ \forall t>0.

(47)

t\lambda(\mathrm{d}\End_{u_{t}}u_{t})=\int_{0}^{1}||t\dot{\gamma}(tx)||^{2}\mathrm{d}x=t^{2},\ \ \forall t>0.

From equations (

) and (

(48)

\tilde{\lambda}(\ker(\mathrm{d}\pi_{\tilde{\gamma}(1)}))=\{0\}.

Denote with

.
We claim that for a.e.

\begin{split}\frac{||\dot{\gamma}(t)||_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{G}$}}}}^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{||\mathrm{d}\pi_{q}(w)||^{2}_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{G}$}}}}}{2}-a(\mathrm{d}\pi_{q}(w)-\dot{\gamma}(t)),\forall w\in T_{q}G.\end{split}

Consequently, by (

(54)

\frac{||u(t)\cdot X(q)||_{G}^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{||\mathrm{d}\pi_{q}(w)||^{2}_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{G}$}}}}}{2}-a(\mathrm{d}\pi_{q}(w)-\dot{\gamma}(t)),\forall w\in T_{q}G.

By definition of
||\cdot||_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{G}$}}}}
we have
||\mathrm{d}\pi_{q}(w)||^{2}_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{G}$}}}}\leq||w||_{G}^{2}

(55)

\frac{||u(t)\cdot X(q)||_{G}^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{||w||_{G}^{2}}{2}-(a\circ\mathrm{d}\pi_{q})(w-u(t)\cdot X(q)),\forall w\in T_{q}G,

which proves the claim (
52
).

If we call
\tilde{\phi}^{t}
the flow along

in
G
and

the flow along

in
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{G}$}}}
, we have that

(56)

\pi\circ\tilde{\phi}^{t}=\phi^{t}\circ\pi,\ \ \forall t\in[0,1],

since for every

and

solve the differential equation

\begin{cases}\sigma^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}(t)\pi_{*}X_{i}(\sigma(t));\\

In particular, for all

, the curve

Being

\left(\phi_{t}^{1}\right)^{*}\lambda\in\partial_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}\left(\frac{\left(||\cdot||_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\displaystyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\textstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.67416pt}{$\scriptstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.19583pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{G}$}}}}\right)_{\gamma(t)}^{2}}{2}\right),\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,1].

We prove that
\tilde{\gamma}
is normal showing that for

\left(\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{1}\right)^{*}\tilde{\lambda}\in\partial_{\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}(t)}\left(\frac{\left(||\cdot||_{G}\right)_{\tilde{\gamma}(t)}^{2}}{2}\right),\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,1].

\left(\left(\phi_{t}^{1}\right)^{*}\lambda\right)\circ\pi_{*}\in\partial_{\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}(t)}\left(\frac{\left(||\cdot||_{G}\right)_{\tilde{\gamma}(t)}^{2}}{2}\right),\text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,1],

and by (

(60)

\left(\tilde{\phi}_{t}^{1}\right)^{*}\tilde{\lambda}=\left(\left(\phi_{t}^{1}\right)^{*}\lambda\right)\circ\pi_{*},\ \ \forall t\in[0,1].

Finally, Equation (
50
) come trivially from the definition of
\tilde{\lambda}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove that in self-similar spaces normal loops starting from the origin are constant.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Let
G
be a Carnot group and
H<G
be a subgroup invariant under the action of dilations, with
\Lie(H)\cap V_{1}=\{0\}
. Denote with
\pi:G\rightarrow{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{G}$}}}
the canonical projection. Let
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{G}$}}{\raisebox{-1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{H}$}\mkern-4.0mu\diagdown\mkern-5.0mu\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{G}$}}}
be a normal curve such that
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=\pi(1_{G})
. From Lemma
50
we know there exists exists a normal curve
\tilde{\gamma}:[0,1]\rightarrow G
with control
u
such that
\pi\circ\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}(0)=1_{G}
. In particular
\tilde{\gamma}(1)\in H
and thus

(61)

\mathrm{d}\End_{u}u=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\delta_{\tau}(\tilde{\gamma}(1))\bigg|_{\tau=1}\in T_{\tilde{\gamma}(1)}H,

being

50
) we know that
\tilde{\gamma}
solves Equation (
31
) with respect to a covector
\tilde{\lambda}\in T^{*}_{\tilde{\gamma}(1)}G
such that
\tilde{\lambda}(T_{\tilde{\gamma}(1)}H)=\{0\}

(62)

||u||_{L^{2}}=\tilde{\lambda}(\mathrm{d}\End_{u}u)=0.

We proved that
\tilde{\gamma}
is constant and thus
\gamma
is constant. Since
\gamma
was an arbitrary normal loop starting from the origin, we concluded the proof of the theorem.
∎

4.
Some examples

4.1.
Curves with end-point arbitrarely close to the origin

Even if by Theorem
3.5
we know that in Carnot groups non-constant normal loops don’t exists, we can find normal curves of length
1
with end-point arbitrarily close to the origin. We present a simple example in the Heisemberg group.

Definition 4.1
.

The
3-dimensional Heisemberg group
is the Carnot group
(\mathbb{R}^{3},\cdot)
with product law

(x,y,z)\cdot(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}):=(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}(x^{\prime}y-xy^{\prime})),\forall x,y,z,x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}.

In the Heisemberg group we choose as orthonormal left-invariant frame of the first stratum the two vector fields
\{X,Y\}
defined for all
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle X(x,y,z):=\partial_{x}-\frac{y}{2}\partial_{z};

\displaystyle Y(x,y,z):=\partial_{y}+\frac{x}{2}\partial_{z}.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
G:=(\mathbb{R}^{3},\cdot)
be the subRiemannian 3-dimensional Heisemberg group. For every
\epsilon>0
there exists a normal curve
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, parametrized by arclength, such that
\gamma(0)=1_{G}
and
d(\gamma(1),1_{G})<\epsilon
.

Proof.

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, in the coordinates of Definition
4.1
, consider the curve
\gamma_{N}:[0,1]\rightarrow G
defined as
\gamma_{N}(t):=(x_{N}(t),y_{N}(t),z_{N}(t))
with

x_{N}(t):=\frac{\cos(2\pi Nt)-1}{2\pi N};

y_{N}(t):=\frac{\sin(2\pi Nt)}{2\pi N};

z_{N}(t):=\frac{2\pi Nt-\sin(2\pi Nt)}{8(\pi N)^{2}}.

The curve
\gamma_{N}
is the horizontal lift of a circle of radius
\frac{1}{2\pi N}
travelled
N
times and is a normal geodesic parametrized by arc-length (see for example
[
12
, Section 1.4.1]
for a characterization of geodesics in the Heisemberg group). We have
\gamma_{N}(0)=1_{G}
and
\gamma_{N}(1)=(0,0,\frac{1}{4\pi N})
. Therefore, for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
N>0
such that
\gamma_{N}(1)\in B(1_{G},\epsilon)
. We showed that for every
\epsilon>0
there exists a normal curve of length
1
having end-point in
B(1_{G},\epsilon)
, thus the proof of the proposition is concluded.
∎

4.2.
Optimality of the exponent in Theorem
1.1

In an arbitrary Carnot group we cannot obtain an estimate as the one in Theorem
1.1
with an exponent smaller than the reciprocal of the step. Indeed, for every
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we provide an example of a normal curve
\gamma
, in a filiform group of step
s
, for which the distance of
\gamma(t)
from the origin is bounded above by a constant times
t^{\frac{1}{s}}
.

Definition 4.3
.

The
subRiemannian filiform group of first type
is the Carnot group with stratified Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=V_{1}\oplus...\oplus V_{s}
with basis
X_{1},Y_{1},...,Y_{s}
,

\{X_{1},Y_{1}\}
being an orthonormal basis of
V_{1}
,
V_{j}=\Span(Y_{j})
for
j=2,...,s
and only non-trivial bracket relations
[X_{1},Y_{i}]=Y_{i+1}
, for every
i=1,...,s-1
.

We recall that in the subRiemannian filiform groups of first type all length-minimizing curves are normal (see for example
[
6
, Proposition 4.1]
).

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
G
be the sub-Riemmanian filiform group of first type of step
s
. Let
Y_{n}
be a vector spanning the
s
-th stratum and
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow G
be a normal curve with
\gamma(0)=1_{G}
,
\gamma(1)=\exp(Y_{n})
and
\gamma|_{[0,1]}
length-minimizing. Then there exists
C>0
such that for every
t>1
there holds

(63)

d(1,\gamma(t))<Ct^{\frac{1}{s}}.

Proof.

Let
\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
be an orthonormal frame of the first stratum. Let
\lambda
be a co-vector associated to
\gamma
, see Definition
33
. Since the energy at the origin is differentiable with differential

\mathrm{d}(\E_{1})_{w}v=\langle w,v\rangle,\ \ \forall v,w\in T_{1}G,

equation (
33
) rewrites as

(64)

\lambda(\Ad_{\gamma(t)}X_{i})=\langle u(t),X_{i}\rangle,\ \ \forall t\in\mathbb{R},\forall i\in\{1,2\}.

We claim that

(65)

\gamma(m+s)=\exp(mY_{n})\gamma(s),\ \ \forall m\in\mathbb{N},\forall s\in\mathbb{R}.

Indeed, the curve
\exp(mY_{n})\gamma(s)
solves the normal Equation (
64
) with respect to the covector
\lambda
:

(66)

\begin{split}(\exp(mY_{n})\gamma(s))^{\prime}=\mathrm{d}L_{\exp(mY_{n})}\gamma(s)^{\prime}=\\
=\mathrm{d}L_{\exp(mY_{n})}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda(\Ad_{\gamma(s)}X_{i})X_{i}(\gamma(s))=\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda(\Ad_{\exp(mY_{n})\gamma(s)}X_{i})X_{i}(\exp(mY_{n})\gamma(s)),\end{split}

where in the second equality we used that
\gamma
solves (
64
) and the last equality comes from the fact that
\exp(mY_{n})
is in the center.

We have

(67)

d(1,\exp(mY_{n}))=d(1,\delta_{m^{\frac{1}{s}}}(\exp(Y_{n})))=m^{\frac{1}{s}}d(1,\exp(Y_{n})),\ \ \forall m\in\mathbb{N}.

Equation (
67
) together with (
65
) and the triangle inequality give us

(68)

d(1,\gamma(m+s))\leq m^{\frac{1}{s}}d(1,\exp(Y_{n}))+d(1,\gamma(s)),\ \ \forall m\in\mathbb{N},\forall s\in\mathbb{R}.

In particular, being
\gamma|_{[0,1]}
length minimizing and
\gamma(1)=\exp(Y_{n})
, we have

(69)

\begin{split}d(1,\gamma(m+s))\leq m^{\frac{1}{s}}d(1,\exp(Y_{n}))+d(1,\exp(Y_{n}))\leq\\
\leq d(1,\exp(Y_{n}))(m^{\frac{1}{s}}+1),\ \ \forall m\in\mathbb{N},\forall s\in[0,1].\end{split}

Consequently, setting
C=2d(1,\exp(Y_{n}))
, we have for
m>1
that

(70)

d(1,\gamma(m+s))<Cm^{\frac{1}{s}}<C(m+s)^{\frac{1}{s}},\ \ \forall m\in\mathbb{N},\forall s\in[0,1].

This concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎
