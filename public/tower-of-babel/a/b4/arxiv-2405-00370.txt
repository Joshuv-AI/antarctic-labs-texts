Simple recursions displaying interesting evolutions
Francesco Calogero

Abstract




1
Introduction

In this paper firstly the
very simple nonlinear recursion
is
introduced:

y_{n+1}=\left(y_{n}-1\right)^{2}~,~~~n=0,1,2,...;

(1)

and—on the basis of rather elementary considerations—the main
qualitative
features of its evolution are analyzed, firstly in the context
of
real
numbers (including the subcase of
rational

numbers) and then in the more general context of
complex
numbers.
Then—via a
simple but nonlinear
change of dependent
variables—the following,
related
, class of recursions, is
introduced:

x_{n+1}=\frac{f_{2}\left(x_{n}\right)^{2}+f_{1}x_{n}+f_{0}}{g_{2}\left(x_{n}\right)^{2}+g_{1}x_{n}+g_{0}}~,~~~n=0,1,2,...;

(2)

it features the
6

a priori arbitrary coefficients

f_{j}
and
g_{j}
(
j=2,1,0
), which however turn out to be expressed—by
explicit
formulas—in terms of only
3

arbitrary parameters

a,b,c
; implying that the
6

a priori arbitrary coefficients

f_{j}
and
g_{j}
must satisfy—as it were,
a posteriori
—
3

constraints
, which are also reported below. The recursion thus
obtained—featuring of course only the
3

arbitrary parameters
a,b,c
—is also reported below; as well as a rather detailed
qualitative
description of the behavior of its solutions.

The recursion (
1
) is discussed in
Section 2
, and the
recursion (
2
) (in fact, mainly the subcase of it mentioned just above)
in
Section 3
. Terse concluding remarks, including a
hint

at why these findings might be of broader scientific interest than just to
elementary number players, are in
Section 4
.

Notation
: above and hereafter
n
is a
non-negative
integer
, playing the role of
independent variable
:
n=0,1,2,3,...
; the index
j
takes the
3
values
2,1,0
;
y_{n}
and
x_{n}
are the

dependent variables
, and they may be
real
numbers, or
simpler or more complicated entities, as explained below. Below the
”approximate equality symbol”
\cong
is occasionally used to indicate that
an equation involving
real
numbers has been replaced by an
approximate rational
approximation to it (for instance
\sqrt{2}\cong 7/5=1.4
: we of course always employ the
decimal
system…).
\blacksquare

2
The first recursion

The
nonlinear recursion
investigated firstly is defined by the
following
very simple
rule (see (
1
)):

y_{n+1}=\left(y_{n}-1\right)^{2}~.

(3)

For any assignment of the
initial value

y_{0}
, the subsequent
values
y_{n}
for
n=1,2,3
,…are clearly
all
well defined: how
shall they evolve as
n
increases? Note that this recursion implies that,
if the
initial
value
y_{0}
is an
integer
or a
rational
number,
all
the subsequent numbers
y_{n}
with
n=1,2,3,...
, shall also be
integer
or
rational
numbers.
Likewise if
y_{0}
were a
complex number
, all the subsequent
numbers
y_{n}
would be
complex numbers
: indeed an analogous
phenomenon would of course also characterize the evolution determined by the
recursion (
3
) even in
more general
cases, for instance if
the symbol
y_{0}
were a
square matrix
of
integer (
or of

real
, or of
complex
)
numbers
,
all
the
subsequent quantities
y_{n\text{ }}
yielded by the recursion (
3
)
would also have the
same
nature as
y_{0}
.

2.1
The case of
real
numbers

An elementary observation is that, in this case, whichever is the initial
value
y_{0}
, the subsequent numbers
y_{n}
with
n=1,2,3,...
shall

all
be
positive numbers
. Hence the analysis can be in this
case be restricted to the case of
positive real numbers.

Next we note that clearly, if the
initial
value is
y_{0}=0
, the
recursion (
3
) yields an extremely simple
periodic
outcome
:y_{m}=1
if
m
is a
positive odd integer,

y_{m}=0
if
m
is a

positive even integer
. And clearly an
analogous
outcome
obtains if the
initial
value is
y_{0}=1
: then
y_{m}=0
if
m

is a
positive odd integer,

y_{m}=1
if
m
is a
positive
even integer
.

But if the
initial value
is a
real
number inside the
interval
0<y_{0}<1
or in its neighborhood, then the outcome is less
trivial. Indeed, if
y_{0}=\varepsilon
with
0<\varepsilon<1
, then
y_{1}=\left(1-\varepsilon\right)^{2},

y_{2}=\varepsilon^{2}\left(2-\varepsilon\right)^{2};
etc. . It is therefore plain that, if
\varepsilon
is a
very small
number
very close
to
0
,
the numbers produced by the recursion (
3
) shall, at
every

step, jump from one side to the other side of the interval
0<y<1
,
getting at every consecutive step closer to its borders, but never actually
reaching them; except, so to say, in the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty

(but this is of course not a proper limiting value of the sequence
y_{n}
,
as the value of
y_{n}
gets, at every step of this sequence, alternatively
closer to the number
0
or to the number
1
). And obviously this is also
true if the
initial
value
y_{0}
is close to the right end of the
interval
0<y_{0}<1
. But how close must the initial value
y_{0}
be to one
or the other end of the interval
0<y_{0}<1
in order for this kind of
behavior to prevail?

Let us (as it were, in a
very naive
mood) investigate firstly what
happens when we start from the
very middle
of this interval, namely
if we start from the value
y_{0}=1/2
. We then get the following sequence:

\displaystyle y_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1/2\right)^{2}~=1/4~=0.25~,

(4a)

\displaystyle y_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(3/4\right)^{2}~=9/16=0.5625~,

(4b)

\displaystyle y_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(7/16\right)^{2}=49/256=0.191406~,

(4c)

\displaystyle y_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(207/256\right)^{2}=0.6538238525390625~,...

(4d)

These
rational numbers
seem to, alternatively, approach from above
and from below the
2
ends of the interval from
0
to
1
. So
one

might
bet
that also for
all initial

real numbers

in the interval from
0
to
1
that behavior shall characterize the
sequence (
3
). But
he
would loose that
bet (
maybe

she
would not be so
naive
?).

Indeed, let us search next for the
equilibria
of the recursion (
3
). Such values
y_{n}
of the dependent variable
y_{n}
must of
course satisfy the
algebraic
equation

y=\left(y-1\right)^{2}~;

(5a)

they are therefore the
2
roots of the
second-degree

algebraic equation

\left(y\right)^{2}-3y+1=0~;

(5b)

hence they are the
2

real
(
positive nonrational
)
numbers

\bar{y}_{\pm}=\left(3\pm\sqrt{5}\right)/2~~,

(6a)

\bar{y}_{-}\cong 0.381966011250~,~~~\bar{y}_{+}\cong 2.618033988749~.

(6b)

And
one
of these
2
numbers happens to fall
inside
the
interval from
0
to
1
, while the other falls
outside
it.

So we must proceed with our investigation. Let us begin by
investigating—in the context of
real numbers
in the interval from

0
to
1
—whether the
equilibrium point

\bar{y}_{-}
of the
recursion (
3
) is a
stable
or
unstable
equilibrium
point. To this end we introduce the change of variables from the set of

real numbers

y_{n}
to the set of
real numbers
\eta_{n}
,

y_{n}=\bar{y}_{-}+\varepsilon\eta_{n}~;

(7a)

here and hereafter we assume the parameter
\varepsilon
to be
infinitesimally small
and
positive,
\varepsilon>0
; this implies
that the
real numbers

\eta_{n}
shall evolve according to the
following
linear
recursion

\eta_{n+1}=2\left(\bar{y}_{-}-1\right)\eta_{n}~,

(7b)

which implies

\eta_{n}=\eta_{0}\left[2\left(\bar{y}_{-}-1\right)\right]^{n}\cong\eta_{0}\left(-1.23606797748\right)^{n}~,

(7c)

(with
\eta_{0}
an
arbitrary real nonvanishing number
); and this
clearly entails that
\bar{y}_{-}
is an
unstable
—
repulsive
—
equilibrium point
, i. e. such that any sequence of

real numbers
yielded by the recursion (
3
) which starts

sufficiently close
to the
equilibrium point

\bar{y}_{-}

shall get
away
from it, by becoming at every consecutive step a bit
farther from it (first to one side, next time to the other, and so on); and
so on for a while, until the values of
y_{n}
get
sufficiently close
to the boundary values of the interval from
0
to
1
, for the regime
described above taking over, characterized by the fact that the values of
y_{n}
get closer and closer to the boundaries of the interval from
0
to
1
, but always jumping from one side of that interval to the other at every
step.

We have thus completely described the peculiar behavior of the solutions of
the recursion (
3
) in the
real numbers
context, provided
0\leq y_{0}\leq 1
; and clearly that behavior would be analogous in the more
restricted context of
rational numbers
, with the
simplification
due to the fact that,
in that context
, the
unstable equilibrium
value
\bar{y}_{-}
inside that interval does
not
exist. Therefore,
in that context
, the
bet
mentioned
above would be
won
rather than
lost
! Related
question
: would an Artificial Intelligence (AI) be able to identify
this difference
among the sequences yielded by the recursion (
3
)
in the
2
contexts of
real
and
rational
numbers?
Empirical finding
: it depends on
how
you pose the question to
it/her/him; and also on how
advanced
is the AI to whom you have
access.

To complete our analysis of the behavior of the solutions of the recursion (
3
) in the
real numbers
context, there remains to consider
the possibility that the initial value
y_{0}
be larger than unity:
y_{0}>1
. Then clearly, if
1<y_{0}<2,
the next value
y_{1}
ends up

inside
the interval
0<y<1
, and we already know what happens next.
(And we also trivially know what happens if
y_{0}=2
). But we also know
that, a little beyond the value
y=2
, there is the
second

equilibrium point
\bar{y}_{+}
, see (
6
). Is this a
stable

or an
unstable
equilibrium point? To find out we make now the
following change of variables from the set of
real numbers

y_{n}

to the set of
real numbers
\zeta_{n}
, by setting

y_{n}=\bar{y}_{+}+\varepsilon\zeta_{n}~,

(8a)

where we again assume the parameter
\varepsilon
to be
infinitesimal
and
positive
: this implies that now the
real
numbers

\zeta_{n}
shall evolve according to the following
linear

recursion

\zeta_{n+1}=2\left(\bar{y}_{+}-1\right)\zeta_{n}\cong\left(3.6236067977498\right)\zeta_{n}~,

(8b)

which implies

\zeta_{n}=\zeta_{0}\left[2\left(\bar{y}_{+}-1\right)\right]^{n}\cong\zeta_{0}\left(3.6236067977498\right)^{n}~.

(8c)

We therefore conclude that this is also an
unstable
(i. e.,
repelling
) equilibrium point; hence if the
initial value
is any

real number

larger
than
\bar{y}_{+},

y_{0}>\bar{y}_{+}

(implying
\zeta_{0}>0
), then the sequence of
positive real numbers

y_{n}
shall indefinitely grow faster and faster, tending to
+\infty

as the index
n
tends itself to
+\infty
; while if the
initial
value
y_{0}
is any
positive

real number

smaller

than
\bar{y}_{+},

2<y_{0}<\bar{y}_{+}
(implying
\zeta_{0}<0
), then
the sequence of numbers
y_{n}
shall initially decrease in absolute value
until it approaches to value
2
. But when it is sufficiently
close

to
2
(namely, just a bit
smaller
than
1+\sqrt{2}
), at the next
step it shall end inside the interval from
0
to
2
, and at the following
step it shall surely fall inside the interval
0<y<1
; and its next
evolution has been described above.

The qualitative description of the somewhat remarkable behavior of this
recursion is thus completely ascertained, in the context of the quantities
y_{n}
being
real numbers
.

Let us therefore
summarize
our findings of the behavior of the
sequence of numbers yielded by the recursion (
3
) in the context in
which the variables
y_{n}
are
real numbers
(the rest of this
paragraph is formulated so as to be understandable even to someone who has
not followed the details of the above analysis). If the
initial
value
y_{0}
is an
arbitrary negative real number
,
y_{0}<0
, the
number
y_{1}
shall be a
positive

number
,
y_{1}>0
,
hence this case is essentially included in the case in which the
initial value

y_{0}
is itself
positive
,
y_{0}>0
, see below;
but before considering that case, let us recall the
special
case
when the
initial value

y_{0}

vanishes
,
y_{0}=0
, hence
the recursion (
3
) yields clearly the following
periodic

sequence of numbers:
y_{n}=1
whenever the
index

n
(which is by
definition a
nonnegative integer number
) is an
odd integer
, while
y_{n}=0
when the
index

n
is an
even integer
;
and let us also immediately note that the analogous
periodic

sequence obtains if the
initial value
is instead
y_{0}=1
, except
that then
y_{n}=1
when the
index

n
is an
even integer
,

y_{n}=0
whenever the
index

n
is an
odd integer
. Next
we consider the cases in which
y_{0}
, hence
all
the following
numbers

y_{n}
are
positive numbers
,
y_{n}>0
. We begin
by recalling what happens if the initial value
y_{0}
lies
inside

the interval from
0
to
1
,
0<y_{0}<1
: then there exists a
single unstable equilibrium point
of the sequence (
3
),
characterized by the
irrational real value
\bar{y}_{-}
(see (
6
)), such that if
y_{0}=\bar{y}_{-}
then
y_{n}=\bar{y}_{-}
for

all
values of the index
n
(
n=1,2,3,...
). Let us consider next
the case in which the
initial value
is a
real number

positive but less then unity,
0<y_{0}<1
, but different from
\bar{y}_{-}

; then the numbers
y_{n}
shall jump at
each
step of the sequence
from being
smaller
to being
larger
than
\bar{y}_{-}
, but
always remaining
inside
the interval
0<y<1
: jumping at every step
from being closer to one and then the other end of that interval, but never
reaching those extremal values
0
and
1
. The last case to be considered
in this summary is the case in which the initial value
y_{0}
is a
positive real
number larger than unity,
y_{0}>1
: there are then
3

possibilities: if
y_{0}=\bar{y}_{+}
(see (
6
)), then for
all
values of the index
n
,
y_{n}=y_{0}=\bar{y}_{+}
(
equilibrium
); if
1<y_{0}<\bar{y}_{+}
, then as the index
n
increases,
the numbers
y_{n}
shall eventually approach the value
1
from above and
then enter the interval
0<y<1,
and then behave as described above;
finally, if
y_{0}>\bar{y}_{+}
, as the index
n
increases, the
corresponding numbers
y_{n}
shall become
larger and larger
,
increasing with jumps of
increasing
lengths: being of course always

positive

finite real numbers
, albeit characterized by the
property

y_{n+2}-y_{n+1}>y_{n+1}-y_{n}>0~.

(9)

This is the end of our
complete
description of the
qualitative
behavior of the sequence (
3
) in the
real
numbers
context.

Skeptical readers not convinced by the arguments presented up to here are of
course welcome to check by
numerical computations
—easily
performable with the help of a PC—whether this description is indeed
correct…

2.2
The case of
complex
numbers

The next interesting case is that in which the recurring numbers are instead

complex numbers
, for which we adopt hereafter the notation

z_{n}=y_{n}+\mathbf{i}u_{n}

(10)

where hereafter
\mathbf{i}
is the
imaginary
unit (so that
\mathbf{i}^{2}=-1
) and the
2
numbers
y_{n}
and
u_{n}
are
real
numbers
(of course the
real numbers

y_{n}
should not be confused
with the
real numbers

y_{n}
discussed above; except in the
special case in which
u_{0}=0
hence
u_{n}=0
, when the
complex

numbers
z_{n}
discussed hereafter reduce to the
real
numbers
discussed above; a case we hereafter
exclude
from consideration).

Then the recursion (
3
) can be reformulated as follows:

z_{n+1}=\left(z_{n}-1\right)^{2}~,

(11a)

implying (via (
10
))

y_{n+1}=\left(y_{n}-1\right)^{2}-\left(u_{n}\right)^{2}~,

(11b)

u_{n+1}=2u_{n}\left(y_{n}-1\right)~.

(11c)

The last formula written above implies that

y_{n}=1+u_{n+1}/\left(2u_{n}\right)~,

(12)

and via this formula we then obtain, for the dependent variable
u_{n}
, the
recursion

u_{n+2}=u_{n+1}\left\{-2+\left(u_{n+1}\right)^{2}/\left[2\left(u_{n}\right)^{2}\right]-2\left(u_{n}\right)^{2}\right\}~.

(13)

This formula suggests introducing the new dependent variable

w_{n}=\left(u_{n}\right)^{2}~,

(14a)

which shall then satisfy the recursion

w_{n+2}=w_{n+1}\left[w_{n+1}-4w_{n}\left(1+w_{n}\right)\right]^{2}/\left(2w_{n}\right)^{2}~.

(14b)

The
initial-values
problem of this
second-order recursion
(
14b
) requires now the assignment of the
2

initial data
w_{0}
and
w_{1}
.

Clearly if, for any
arbitrary
(nonvanishing!) value of
w_{0}\neq 0
,
w_{1}=0
, then
w_{n}=0
also for
n=2,3,
…: this special
pseudoequilibrium
solution of the recursion in the context of
complex numbers
z_{n}
correspond to the
periodic
solution in
the context of
real
numbers
y_{n}
discussed above, when
y_{n}

jumps at every step from the value
0
to the value
1
and then from the
value
1
to the value
0
and so on and on indefinitely. We also get an

analogous pseudoequilibrium
outcome (
w_{n}=0
for
n=2,3,
…) if
w_{0}
is again any
arbitrary
(
nonvanishing
!)
real

number and

w_{1}=4w_{0}\left(1+w_{0}\right)~.

(15)

But in both cases, we would have just returned to the previously treated
case of
real
numbers…

The next question to investigate in order to arrive at a
qualitative

understanding of the behavior of the sequence of
complex numbers
z_{n}
produced by the recursion (
11
) is to investigate whether
this recursion possesses a genuine
equilibrium
solution, i. e. such
that

y_{n}=\bar{y}~,~~~u_{n}=\bar{u}~,

(16)

where
\bar{y}
is a
real number
and
\bar{u}
a
nonvanishing real number
. It is then obvious that the
third
of the

3
eqs. (
11
) implies
\bar{y}=3/2
, and then the
second
of the
3
eqs. (
11
) implies
\left(\bar{u}\right)^{2}=-5/4
, which is of course
incompatible
with the requirement
that
\bar{u}
be a
real number
. We thus conclude that the
recursion (
11
) does
not have any equilibrium solution

(of course in the context of
complex numbers
which are
not
real
).

Finally, it is rather easy to convince oneself that therefore the
qualitative
evolution of the sequence produced by the recursion (
11
) shall always be, in some sense,
dominated
by the
behavior of its
imaginary
component
u_{n}
; hence that
eventually
—whatever the
complex
initial datum
z_{0}=y_{0}+\mathbf{i}u_{0}
(with
y_{0}
and
u_{0}

arbitrary real numbers
,
except for the requirement
u_{0}\neq 0
)—then
\left|z_{n}\right|\
shall always become
very large
, evolving

asymptotically
(as the index
n
becomes larger and larger:
n\rightarrow\infty
)
approximately
as follows:

z_{n+1}\simeq\left(z_{n}\right)^{2}~,~~~y_{n+1}\simeq-\left(y_{n}\right)^{2}~,~\ ~u_{n+1}\simeq-2y_{n}u_{n}~,

(17)

implying that
\left|u_{n}\right|>>\left|y_{n}\right|
, that both
\left|y_{n}\right|
and
\left|u_{n}\right|

increase steadily
by steps each larger than the preceding one (
\left|y_{n+2}\right|-\left|y_{n+1}\right|>>\left|y_{n+1}\right|-\left|y_{n}\right|>>0,~\left|u_{n+2}\right|-\left|u_{n+1}\right|>>\left|u_{n+1}\right|-\left|u_{n}\right|>0
), and that the numbers
y_{n}~
become eventually
all negative
(
y_{n}<0
), while the signs
of the numbers
u_{n}\
change at every step (so that
u_{n+1}u_{n}<0
).

This provides a rather detailed description of the
qualitative

behavior of the sequence of
complex numbers

z_{n}
produced by the
recursion (
11
) for any
arbitrary
(complex!)
initial value

z_{0}
, when the index
n
becomes
sufficiently large
; or even for
all
values of the index
n
if the
initial
value

z_{0}
is itself
sufficiently large
(for instance the
requirement
\left|z_{0}\right|>1
would be enough; of course
always provided
u_{0}
does
not
vanish!).

Let us finally emphasize that what we described here is the
qualitative
behavior of the sequence of numbers produced by the recursions (
3
) and (
11
). Of course it is nowadays easy to
investigate
numerically
—by computer—such very simple
recursions. But let us also emphasize that some of the
qualitative

findings reported above are
rigorous mathematical statements
; while

no
finding
only
based on
numerical experiments

has
any

rigorous mathematical validity
beyond the
validity
of the
numerical findings themselves
.

3
The second recursion

In the
first
subsection of this
Section 3
it is shown
that, via a
simple
yet
nonlinear
change of dependent
variables, the recursion (
2
) may be related to the recursion (
1
), and the restrictions on the
3+3=6

coefficients

f_{j}
and
g_{j}
of the recursion (
2
) that are implied by this development are
ascertained. Then, in the
second
subsection of this
Section
3
, the
qualitative
behavior of the solutions of the
subclass
of the recursion (
2
) identified in the
first

subsection is outlined, and an interesting
subcase
is also treated.

3.1
A simple nonlinear change of dependent variables

In this subsection the
simple
yet
nonlinear
change of
dependent variables is introduced, from the variables
y_{n}
satisfying the
recursion (
1
) to the variable
x_{n}
satisfying the recursion (
2
):

y_{n}=\left(ax_{n}+b\right)/\left(x_{n}+c\right)~.

(18a)

It involves the
3

a priori arbitrary parameters
a,~b,~c
.

This relation can be immediately inverted:

x_{n}=-\left(cy_{n}-b\right)/\left(y_{n}-a\right)~.

(18b)

And by inserting these relations in the recursion (
1
) satisfied by the
dependent variable
y_{n}
there obtains the recursion (
2
