Second-order homogenization of periodic Schrödinger operators with highly oscillating potentials
Éric Cancès, Louis Garrigue, David Gontier

Abstract
. We consider the homogenization at second-order in ε \varepsilon of 𝕃 \mathbb{L} -periodic Schrödinger operators with rapidly oscillating potentials of the form H ε = − Δ + ε − 1 ​ v ​ ( x , ε − 1 ​ x ) + W ⁡ ( x ) H^{\varepsilon}=-\Delta+\varepsilon^{-1}v(x,\varepsilon^{-1}x)+W(x) on L 2 ​ ( ℝ d ) L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) , where 𝕃 \mathbb{L} is a Bravais lattice of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , v v is ( 𝕃 × 𝕃 ) (\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}) -periodic, W W is 𝕃 \mathbb{L} -periodic, and ε ∈ ℕ − 1 \varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}^{-1} . We treat both the linear equation with fixed right-hand side and the eigenvalue problem, as well as the case of physical observables such as the integrated density of states. We illustrate numerically that these corrections to the homogenized solution can significantly improve the first-order ones, even when ε \varepsilon is not small.



1.
Introduction

The homogenization of Schrödinger operators of the form

H^{\varepsilon}=-\Delta+\varepsilon^{-1}v\left(x,\varepsilon^{-1}x\right)+W(x)\quad\mbox{on }L^{2}(\mathcal{D}),

(1)

where
\mathcal{D}
is a domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
W\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D};\mathbb{R})
,
\varepsilon>0
, and the potential
v\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathcal{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
is periodic with zero mean in its second variable, has an already long history. To our knowledge, such operators have been first studied in the reference textbook
[
PBL78
, Section 12]
in the case when
\mathcal{D}
is a bounded domain and
H^{\varepsilon}
has form domain
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
(Dirichlet boundary conditions).
The authors identified a homogenized Schrödinger operator of the form

H^{0}:=-\Delta+V_{0}+W\quad\text{on}\quad L^{2}(\mathcal{D}),

and a corrector
\chi_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, and showed that, for
\mu<\min(\sigma(H^{0}))
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\,{\left\|u^{\varepsilon}_{\mu,f}-\left(1+\varepsilon\chi_{1}\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot\right)\right)u^{0}_{\mu,f}\right\|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{D})}=0},

where the functions
u_{\mu,f}^{\varepsilon}
and
u_{\mu,f}^{0}
are respectively the solutions to the equations
(H^{\varepsilon}-\mu)u_{\mu,f}^{\varepsilon}=f
and
(H^{0}-\mu)u_{\mu,f}^{0}=f
with Dirichlet boundary conditions.
They also proved error bounds on the ground-state eigenvalue:
{\left|E_{1}^{\varepsilon}-E_{1}^{0}\right|\leqslant c\varepsilon}
, where
E_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}
and
E_{\ell}^{0}
denote the lowest
\ell^{\rm th}
eigenvalue of
H^{\varepsilon}
and
H^{0}
, respectively, counting multiplicities.
Then in
[
Zha21
]
, using techniques of
[
KLS13
]
, Zhang proved that there is a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
independent of
\varepsilon
such that

\left\|u^{\varepsilon}_{\mu,f}-\left(1+\varepsilon\chi_{1}\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot\right)\right)u^{0}_{\mu,f}\right\|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{D})}\leqslant c\varepsilon,\quad\quad\left|E_{\ell}^{\varepsilon}-E_{\ell}^{0}\right|\leqslant c\left(E^{0}_{\ell}-\mu\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\varepsilon.

The present article is a contribution to the study of Schrödinger operators of the form (
1
), whose originality is threefold. First, we consider a fully periodic setting, that is
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
v
is
\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
-periodic,
W
is
\mathbb{L}
-periodic, and
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}^{-1}
, where
\mathbb{L}
is a given Bravais lattice of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our results can be extended to some point to rational values of
\varepsilon
(see Remark
2.2
and Section
3
); on the other hand, the irrational case corresponding to incommensurate potentials remains out of the scope of this study. As will be clarified in a future work, Schrödinger operators similar to these ones are encountered in the study of the electronic structure of emerging moiré materials
[
AEJ+21
]
. Possible applications to cold-atom lattices or optical lattices could also be considered. Second, we provide
H^{1}
-approximations of eigenmodes of order
\varepsilon^{2}
, and approximations of eigenvalues up to order
\varepsilon^{4}
. Third, we use a slightly different strategy of proof based on resolvent estimates.

Let us briefly explain our strategy in the simple case of first-order approximations. When
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}^{-1}
, both operators
H^{\varepsilon}
and
H^{0}
commute with
\mathbb{L}
-translations, and one can consider their Bloch transforms
[
RS78
, Chapter XIII]
. For
k
in the first Brillouin zone
\Omega^{*}
, we denote their respective Bloch fibers by
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and
H^{0}_{k}
. First, we identify a first order corrector
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(1)}
so that, for all
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}^{-1}
, and all
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

\left\|\left(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}-z\right)\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(1)}-\left(H^{0}_{k}-z\right)\right\|_{H^{2}_{{\rm per}}\to H^{-1}_{\rm per}}\leqslant C(1+|z|)\varepsilon,

for a constant
C\geqslant 0
independent of
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
k\in\Omega^{*}
and
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}^{-1}
(see Lemma
5.3
). Note that the operators are seen as bounded operators from the
H^{2}_{\rm per}
to the
H^{-1}_{\rm per}
Sobolev spaces, instead of the
usual

H^{2}_{\rm per}
and
L^{2}_{\rm per}
ones (in some sense, we loose one derivative because of the highly-oscillatory potential). Next, we transform the previous inequality into a resolvent estimate, of the form

\left\|\left(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}-\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(1)}\left(H^{0}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}\right\|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}\rightarrow H^{1}_{\rm per}}\leqslant C(k,z)\varepsilon,

see Theorem
2.3
. From this estimate and usual functional calculus, we derive
a priori
estimates on the Bloch functions of
H^{\varepsilon}
(Section
2.4
) as well as on several quantities of interest (QoI) such as the kinetic and potential energies of the fermionic ground states, and the integrated density of states (IDOS) of the operator
H^{\varepsilon}
(Section
2.5
).

We believe that our technique could be used to address other cases. Let us mention for instance the analysis of the scattering and interface properties of Schrödinger operators with highly-oscillatory potentials
[
DW11
]
, or the homogenization of operators in divergence form
-\div A(x,\frac{x}{\varepsilon})\nabla
, which have been thoroughly studied in
[
MV97
,
Zhu20
,
Kes79
,
SV93
]
for first order corrections, and in
[
AA99
,
COV08
,
BFFO17
]
for second order ones (see also
[
AH13
,
HA21
]
for other models).

The article is organized as follows. The main theoretical results are presented in Section
2
, and are illustrated numerically in Section
3
. We show in particular that our second-order corrector greatly improve the first-order one, even in a regime where
\varepsilon
is not so small.
Interestingly, the Ansatz for second-order homogenization given by the formal two-scale expansion (Section
4
) turns out to be unsuitable for some applications. We propose a variant of it, better suited to e.g. compute spectral projectors and related physical properties, or deal with degenerate eigenvalues.
All the proofs are postponed until Section
5
.

2.
Main results

We introduce some notation in Section
2.1
, and define first- and second-order corrector operators in Section
2.2
. We then state in Section
2.3
estimates on the error between the resolvent
(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}-z)^{-1}
of the Bloch fiber
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
of
H^{\varepsilon}
and computable first and second-order approximations of it given by homogenization theory. These estimates provide error bounds on the solution to the linear equation
(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}-z)u^{\varepsilon}=f
. They also pave the way to the derivation of error estimates on eigenvalues and eigenfunctions (Section
2.4
) via Cauchy’s residue formula. Error bounds on physical quantities of interest are given in Section
2.5
.

2.1.
Notation and mathematical framework

We consider a Bravais lattice
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}e_{1}+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}e_{d}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with unit cell

\Omega=(0,1)e_{1}+\cdots+(0,1)e_{d}
, and denote by
\mathbb{L}^{*}
the dual lattice of
\mathbb{L}
and
\Omega^{*}
the first Brillouin zone.
We also denote by

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad L^{p}_{\rm per}(\Omega):=\{f\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\;|\;f\mbox{ $\mathbb{L}$-per}\},

\displaystyle W^{s,p}_{\rm per}(\Omega):=\{f\in W^{s,p}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\;|\;f\mbox{ $\mathbb{L}$-per}\},\qquad H^{s}_{\rm per}(\Omega):=W^{s,2}_{\rm per}(\Omega),

the
\mathbb{L}
-periodic Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces for all
1\leqslant p\leqslant\infty
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
. We endow
H_{\rm per}^{s}(\Omega)
with its natural inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{H^{s}_{\rm per}}:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{L}^{*}}\left(1+|n|^{2}\right)^{s}\overline{c_{n}(f)}c_{n}(g),

where, for
n\in\mathbb{L}^{*}
,
c_{n}(f):=|\Omega|^{-1/2}\int_{\Omega}f(x)e^{-in\cdot x}{\rm d}x
is the
n
-th Fourier coefficient of the
\mathbb{L}
-periodic distribution
f
.

We also introduce the following Banach spaces of
\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
-periodic functions: for all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\rm per}(\Omega))

\displaystyle:=\left\{f\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\;\big|\;f\mbox{ $\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}$-periodic}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{m}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{n}_{\rm per}(\Omega))

\displaystyle:=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\rm per}(\Omega))\;\big|\;

\displaystyle\forall\alpha,

\displaystyle\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\;\mbox{s.t.}\;|\alpha|\leqslant m,\;|\beta|\leqslant n,\;\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}g}{\partial x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}y}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\bigg\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{m}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{n}_{{\rm per},0}(\Omega))

\displaystyle:=\left\{f\in\mathcal{C}^{m}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{n}_{\rm per}(\Omega))\;\big|\;\fint_{\Omega}f(\cdot,y)\,dy=0\right\},

where
\fint_{\Omega}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}
. We denote the two variables of
f\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\rm per}(\Omega))
by
x
and
y
respectively, and call
x
the macroscopic variable and
y
the microscopic one. We endow
\mathcal{C}^{m}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{n}_{\rm per}(\Omega))
with the norm

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\mathcal{C}^{m}_{\rm per}(\mathcal{C}^{n}_{\rm per})}:=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\\
|\alpha|\leqslant m\end{subarray}}{\text{{max}}}\quad\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\\
|\alpha|\leqslant n\end{subarray}}{\text{{max}}}\quad\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\end{subarray}}{\text{{max}}}\quad\left|\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}g}{\partial x^{\alpha}\partial y^{\beta}}(x,y)\right|.

We are interested in studying the Schrödinger operator

\boxed{H^{\varepsilon}=-\Delta+\varepsilon^{-1}v\left(x,\varepsilon^{-1}x\right)+W(x)\quad\mbox{on }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),}

(2)

where
\varepsilon>0
and

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega,\mathcal{C}^{0}_{{\rm per},0}(\Omega)),\qquad W\in W^{1,\infty}_{\rm per}\left(\Omega\right).

(3)

These smoothness conditions may probably be weakened, but we do not explore this question. Note that some smoothness properties are required for the function
v
so that
x\mapsto\varepsilon^{-1}v(x,\varepsilon^{-1}x)
is a well-defined function (this may not hold for instance if
v
is only defined
almost everywhere
).

We will repeatedly use the next Lemma, which is a straightforward consequence of the continuous embeddings
\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\rm per}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{p}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
and
W^{2,p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
for all
1\leqslant p<\infty
, together with elliptic regularity.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{C}^{m}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\rm per}(\Omega))
, and set
M_{g}(x):=\fint_{\Omega}g(x,y)\,dy
. The function
g-M_{g}
is in
\mathcal{C}^{m}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{0}_{{\rm per},0}(\Omega))
, and there is a unique solution
h_{g}
in
\mathcal{C}^{m}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\rm per}(\Omega))
to the family of elliptic problems

(-\Delta_{y}h_{g})(x,y)=(g-M_{g})(x,y).

In addition, there is
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
independent of
g
so that

\|h_{g}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{m}_{\rm per}(\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\rm per})}\leqslant C_{m}\|g\|_{\mathcal{C}^{m}_{\rm per}(\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\rm per})}.

(4)

2.2.
Definition of the correctors

From now on, we use the notation

\displaystyle\boxed{f_{\varepsilon}(x):=f\left(x,\varepsilon^{-1}x\right).}

The study of the operator
H^{\varepsilon}
in (
1
) is numerically difficult, due to the large and fast oscillating potential
\varepsilon^{-1}v_{\varepsilon}
. In order to define the homogenized operator, we introduce the function
\chi_{1}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{1}_{{\rm per},0}(\Omega))
solution to

\Delta_{y}\chi_{1}(x,y)=v(x,y).

(5)

The function
\chi_{1}
is involved in the definition of the first-order corrector of the homogenized problem and will play a key role in the sequel. We also define the homogenized (macroscopic) potential
V\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
by

V(x):=\fint_{\Omega}v(x,y)\chi_{1}(x,y)\,{\rm d}y=-\fint_{\Omega}|\nabla_{y}\chi_{1}(x,y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}y.

(6)

The above formulae for
V_{0}
and
\chi_{1}
result from a formal two-scale expansion presented in Section
4.1
. As will be shown later, and as expected from the results in
[
PBL78
,
Zha21
]
, the (first-order) homogenized Schrödinger operator is given by

\boxed{H^{0}:=-\Delta+V_{0}(x)\quad\text{on }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\mbox{with}\quad V_{0}(x):=V(x)+W(x)}

(7)

Since
V_{0}
and
W
are
\mathbb{L}
-periodic,
H^{0}
is decomposed by the Bloch transform (see e.g.
[
RS78
, Chapter XIII]
): we denote by
\nabla_{k}:=\nabla+ik
, and

H_{k}^{0}:=(-i\nabla_{k})^{2}+V_{0}=-\nabla_{k}^{2}+V_{0}=-\Delta-2ik\cdot\nabla+|k|^{2}+V_{0},

(8)

the Bloch fiber of
H^{0}
at quasi-momentum
k\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The operator
H^{0}_{k}
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
with domain
H^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
. It is bounded from below and has compact resolvent.

In general, the operator
H^{\varepsilon}
is not
\mathbb{L}
-periodic. However, it is
\mathbb{L}
-periodic if
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}^{-1}
, in which case we can also define its Bloch decomposition:

H^{\varepsilon}_{k}:=-\nabla_{k}^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}v_{\varepsilon}+W,\quad\mbox{ for }\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}^{-1}.

(9)

Remark 2.2

(Supercells)
.

When
\varepsilon=p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
is rational, the operators
H^{0}
and
H^{\varepsilon}
are both
p\mathbb{L}
-periodic. The results below allow one to study the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
for a family of rational numbers with uniformly bounded numerators. The constants appearing in our estimates depend a priori on
p
, the numerical simulations in Section
3
suggest that they do not however.

The operator
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
is difficult to handle numerically for small values of
\varepsilon
by brute force approaches because of the highly-oscillatory, large magnitude, potential
\varepsilon^{-1}v_{\varepsilon}
. Our goal is to approach the eigenmodes of
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
by post-treatment of the ones of the homogenized
H^{0}_{k}
(or of a perturbation of
H^{0}_{k}

at the macroscopic scale
, see below). The corresponding expressions are first inferred formally by a two-scale expansion, and then justified by rigorous error bounds.

We already defined the function
\chi_{1}
in (
5
).
We now introduce the first-order corrector as the multiplication operator
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(1)}
on
L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
defined by

\boxed{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(1)}:=1+\varepsilon(\chi_{1})_{\varepsilon}.}

(10)

In order to define our second order correctors, we first define the macroscopic potential
V_{1}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
by

V_{1}(x):=2\fint_{\Omega}\left(\nabla_{x}\chi_{1}(x,y)\cdot\nabla_{y}\chi_{1}(x,y)-\chi_{1}(x,y)|\nabla_{y}\chi_{1}(x,y)|^{2}\right)\,{\rm d}y.

(11)

We also define the two-scale vector field
A_{2}\in\left(\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{1}_{{\rm per},0}(\Omega))\right)^{d}
solution to

\Delta_{y}A_{2}(x,y):=-2\left(\nabla_{y}\chi_{1}\right)(x,y),\quad\mbox{for a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(12)

and the second-order corrector
\chi_{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{1}_{{\rm per},0}(\Omega))
as the solution to

\Delta_{y}\chi_{2}(x,y):=\left[\chi_{1}(x,y)v(x,y)-V(x)\right]-2[(\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{y})\chi_{1}](x,y).

(13)

Whereas there is only one natural way to define a homogenized operator and an associated corrector for first-order homogenization, there are (at least) two natural ways to do that at second order. The first way is to use the pair
(H^{0}_{k},\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{z,k})
suggested by the formal two-scale expansion (see Section
4.1
); the second one is to use the pair
(H^{0}_{k}+\varepsilon V_{1},\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{k})
where
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{k}
is defined as follows:

\boxed{\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{k}:=1+\varepsilon(\chi_{1})_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{2}\left(\left(\chi_{2}\right)_{\varepsilon}+\left(A_{2}\right)_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{k}\right).}

(14)

The operators
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{k}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{z,k}
are related by the formula

\boxed{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{z,k}=\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{k}-\varepsilon\left(1+\varepsilon(\chi_{1})_{\varepsilon}\right)\left(H^{0}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}_{\perp}V_{1},}

(15)

where
(H^{0}_{k}-z)^{-1}_{\perp}
denotes the pseudo-inverse of
(H^{0}_{k}-z)
(i.e.
(H^{0}_{k}-z)^{-1}_{\perp}
coincides with the inverse of
(H^{0}_{k}-z)
on
(\Ker(H^{0}_{k}-z))^{\perp}
and is identically equal to
0
on
\Ker(H^{0}_{k}-z)
). The respective merits of these two approaches are discussed in Remark
2.5
below.

2.3.
Approximations of the resolvent and solutions to the linear equation

The following theorem is key for deriving all the results in this article.

Theorem 2.3

(Approximations of the resolvent)
.

Let
v
and
W
satisfying (
3
),
\omega^{*}
a compact subset of
\overline{\Omega^{*}}
, and
\mathscr{C}
a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
such that

\dist\left(\mathscr{C},\overline{\bigcup_{k\in\omega^{*}}\sigma(H^{0}_{k})}\right)>0.

Then, there exist
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]\cap\mathbb{N}^{-1}
,
k\in\omega^{*}
and
z\in\mathscr{C}
, we have
z\notin\sigma(H^{\varepsilon}_{k})\cup\sigma(H^{0}_{k}+\varepsilon V_{1})
and

\displaystyle\left\|\left(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}-\left(H^{0}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}\right\|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}\to L^{2}_{\rm per}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\varepsilon,

(16)

\displaystyle\left\|\left(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}-\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(1)}\left(H^{0}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}\right\|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}\rightarrow H^{1}_{\rm per}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\varepsilon,

(17)

\displaystyle\left\|\left(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}-\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{k}\left((H^{0}_{k}+\varepsilon V_{1})-z\right)^{-1}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}\rightarrow H^{1}_{\rm per}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2},

(18)

\displaystyle\left\|\left(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}-\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{z,k}\left(H^{0}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}\rightarrow H^{1}_{\rm per}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2}.

(19)

The inequality (
16
) implies in particular that the spectrum of
H_{k}^{\varepsilon}
converges to the one of
H_{k}^{0}
. In particular, the operator
H_{k}^{\varepsilon}
is bounded from below with a bound independent of
\varepsilon
.

The following corollary is a straightforward reformulation of Theorem
2.3
. We state it as an independent result because of its importance for practical applications, and skip its proof for the sake of brevity. For
f
in
L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
, we define by
u^{\varepsilon}_{k,z,f}
,
u^{0}_{k,z,f}
and
u^{0,\varepsilon}_{k,z,f}
the solutions, when they exist, to

(H_{k}^{\varepsilon}-z)u=f,\quad(H_{k}^{0}-z)u=f,\quad\text{and}\quad(H_{k}^{0}+\varepsilon V_{1}-z)u=f.

Corollary 2.4

(First and second-order homogenization of the linear equation)
.

Let
v
and
W
satisfying (
3
),
\omega^{*}
a compact subset of
\overline{\Omega^{*}}
, and
\mathscr{C}
a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
such that

\dist\left(\mathscr{C},\overline{\bigcup_{k\in\omega^{*}}\sigma(H^{0}_{k})}\right)>0.

Then, there exists
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
k\in\omega^{*}
,
z\in\mathscr{C}
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]\cap\mathbb{N}^{-1}
,
z
is in the resolvent sets of both
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and
H^{0}_{k}+\varepsilon V_{1}
, and

•

for all
f\in L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
,

\left\|u^{\varepsilon}_{k,z,f}-u^{0}_{k,z,f}\right\|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}+\left\|u^{\varepsilon}_{k,z,f}-\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(1)}u^{0}_{k,z,f}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}}\leqslant C\varepsilon\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}};

•

if in addition
f\in H^{1}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
, then

\displaystyle\left\|u^{\varepsilon}_{k,z,f}-\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{z,k}u^{0}_{k,z,f}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2}\left\|f\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}},

(20)

\displaystyle\left\|u^{\varepsilon}_{k,z,f}-\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{k}u^{0,\varepsilon}_{k,z,f}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2}\left\|f\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}}.

(21)

Remark 2.5
.

There are two ways to construct second-order corrections, given by (
20
) and (
21
). In view of the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{z,k}
in (
15
), to compute the approximation
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{z,k}u^{0}_{k,z,f}
in (
20
), one needs to solve two macro-scale equations, independent of
\varepsilon>0
, namely

(H^{0}_{k}-z)u^{0}_{k,z,f}=f\quad\text{and}\quad(H^{0}_{k}-z)v^{1}_{k,z,f}=V_{1}u^{0}_{k,z,f}.

(note that
(H^{0}_{k}-z)_{\perp}^{-1}=(H^{0}_{k}-z)
since
z\notin\sigma(H^{0}_{k})
). Instead, for the computation of
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{k}u^{1,\varepsilon}_{k,z,f}
, one only needs to solve one macro-scale equation, which depends on
\varepsilon>0
, namely

(H^{0}_{k}+\varepsilon V_{1}-z)u^{0,\varepsilon}_{k,z,f}=f.

Using (
20
) is computationally more efficient if we are interested in computing the solution for many values of
\varepsilon
since only two macroscopic elliptic PDEs independent of
\varepsilon
have to be solved. On the other hand, if one is interested in the solution for a single value of
\varepsilon
, it is better to use (
21
) since only one macroscopic PDE (depending on
\varepsilon)
has to be solved.

2.4.
Homogenization of the eigenvalue problem

We now consider the approximation of the eigenmodes of

Remark 2.8

Theorem 2.9

(Homogenization of degenerate eigenmodes)
.

Assume that (
3
) holds true and that, for some
k\in\Omega^{*}
,
E^{0}_{\ell,k}=\cdots=E^{0}_{\ell+m-1,k}
is a degenerate eigenvalue of
H^{0}_{k}
of multiplicity
{m\geqslant 1}
.
Then, there exists
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]\cap\mathbb{N}^{-1}
,
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and
(H^{0}_{k}+\varepsilon V_{1})
have exactly
m
eigenvalues
E^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant E^{\varepsilon}_{\ell+m-1,k}
and
E^{0,\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant E^{0,\varepsilon}_{\ell+m-1,k}
(counted with their multiplicities) in the interval
I_{\delta}:=[E^{0}_{\ell,k}-\delta,E^{0}_{\ell,k}+\delta]
, and, setting

P^{0}:=\mathds{1}_{I_{\delta}}(H^{0}_{k})=\mathds{1}_{\{E^{0}_{\ell,k}\}}(H^{0}_{k}),\quad P^{\varepsilon}:=\mathds{1}_{I_{\delta}}(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}),\quad P^{0,\varepsilon}:=\mathds{1}_{I_{\delta}}(H^{0}_{k}+\varepsilon V_{1}),

we have the convergences

\displaystyle\left\|P^{\varepsilon}-P^{0}\right\|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}\to L^{2}_{\rm per}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\,\varepsilon,

(31)

\displaystyle\left\|P^{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(1)}P^{0}\right\|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}\to H^{1}_{\rm per}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\,\varepsilon,

(32)

\displaystyle\left\|P^{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{k}P^{0,\varepsilon}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}\to H^{1}_{\rm per}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\,\varepsilon^{2},

(33)

\displaystyle\left\|\left(P^{\varepsilon}-\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{E^{0}_{\ell,k},k}P^{0}\right)P^{0}\right\|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}\to H^{1}_{\rm per}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\,\varepsilon^{2}.

(34)

and

\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\left|E^{\varepsilon}_{\ell+j,k}-E^{0,\varepsilon}_{\ell+j,k}\right|\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2},\quad\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\left|E^{\varepsilon}_{\ell+j,k}-E^{0}_{\ell+j,k}-\varepsilon\eta_{j}\right|\leqslant C\varepsilon^{2},

(35)

where
\eta_{0}\leqslant\eta_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\eta_{m-1}
are the eigenvalues of the
m\times m
hermitian matrix
\left(\langle\psi_{\ell+i,k}^{0},V_{1}\psi_{\ell+j,k}^{0}\rangle\right)_{0\leqslant i,j\leqslant m-1}
.

An interpretation of (
35
) is that the degeneracy can be lifted at order
\varepsilon
by the macroscopic perturbation potential
V_{1}
. To obtain more accurate approximations of the eigenvalues of
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
, we can proceed as follows. The families of functions
(\psi^{0}_{\ell+j,k})_{0\leqslant j\leqslant m-1}
and
(\psi^{0,\varepsilon}_{\ell+j,k})_{0\leqslant j\leqslant m-1}
form
L^{2}_{\rm per}
-orthonormal bases of the ranges of the orthogonal projectors
P^{0}
and
P^{0,\varepsilon}
. Applying respectively the operators
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{E^{0}_{\ell,k},k}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{k}
to these functions, we deduce from (
33
)-(
34
) that the so-obtained two families of functions form second-order
H^{1}_{\rm per}
-approximations of
L^{2}_{\rm per}
-orthogonal bases of
P^{\varepsilon}
. Solving the variational approximation of the eigenvalue problem
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}\psi=E\psi
in one of these two families, we can obtain approximations of the
m
eigenvalues of
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
close to

with
O(\varepsilon^{4})
accuracy.

2.5.
Homogenization of physical quantities

Let us now explain how to use the above estimates to compute approximations of a few physical quantities of interest (QoI). We first consider the kinetic and potential contributions

T^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}:=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{k}\psi^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}|^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad V^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}:=\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon^{-1}v_{\varepsilon}+W)|\psi^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}|^{2}

(36)

to the total energy
E^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}
. We set

\displaystyle T^{0}_{\ell,k}:=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{k}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}|^{2},\quad V_{\ell,k}:=\int_{\Omega}V|\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}|^{2},

\displaystyle W_{\ell,k}:=\int_{\Omega}W|\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}|^{2},\quad V^{0}_{\ell,k}=V_{\ell,k}+W_{\ell,k}.

and introduce the macroscopic potentials
F
and
G
defined as

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}\fint_{\Omega}v(x,y)\nabla_{x}A_{2}(x,y)\,{\rm d}y=-\fint_{\Omega}\nabla_{x}\chi_{1}(x,y)\cdot\nabla_{y}\chi_{1}(x,y)\,{\rm d}y,

(37)

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle:=\fint_{\Omega}v(x,y)|\chi_{1}(x,y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}y=-2\fint_{\Omega}\chi_{1}(x,y)|\nabla_{y}\chi_{1}(x,y)|^{2}\,{\rm d}y,

(38)

which are related to
V_{1}
through the equality

V_{1}=G-2F.

(39)

Theorem 2.10

(Kinetic and potential energies)
.

Assume the
v
and
w
satisfy (
3
). Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
k\in\Omega^{*}
be such that
E^{0}_{\ell,k}
is a non-degenerate eigenvalue of
H^{0}_{k}
. Then for
\varepsilon>0
small enough,
E^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}
is a non-degenerate eigenvalue of
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and the kinetic and potential components of the energy
E^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}
defined in (
36
) satisfy

\displaystyle T^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}

\displaystyle=T^{0}_{\ell,k}-V_{\ell,k}+\varepsilon\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(2F-2G\right)\left|\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}\right|^{2}-2\int_{\Omega}(2V+W){\rm Re}(\overline{\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}}\phi^{1}_{\ell,k})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

\displaystyle V^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}

\displaystyle=V^{0}_{\ell,k}+V_{\ell,k}+\varepsilon\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(3G-4F\right)\left|\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}\right|^{2}+2\int_{\Omega}(2V+W){\rm Re}(\overline{\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}}\phi^{1}_{\ell,k})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

where
\phi^{1}_{\ell,k}:=-(H^{0}_{k}-E^{0}_{\ell,k})_{\perp}^{-1}(V_{1}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k})
.

In particular, the homogenization process leads to a non-negative energy transfer from kinetic to potential energy of magnitude
\delta E_{\ell,k}=-V_{\ell,k}\geqslant 0
(recall that
V\leqslant 0
, see (
6
)):

T^{0}_{\ell,k}=\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}T^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}\right)-\delta E_{\ell,k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad V^{0}_{\ell,k}=\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}V^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}\right)+\delta E_{\ell,k}.

(40)

This is due to the fact that
\psi^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}
oscillates at scale
\varepsilon
and therefore has a higher kinetic energy than
\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}
. Note also that in view of (
39
),

\displaystyle E^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}

\displaystyle=T^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}+V^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}=T^{0}_{\ell,k}+V^{0}_{\ell,k}+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\left(G-2F\right)\left|\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}\right|^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=E^{0}_{\ell,k}+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}V_{1}|\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}|^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

(41)

in agreement with (
30
). We also have (see Section
5.5.1
)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\Delta\psi^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}\right|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}{\rm d}x\left|\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)\right|^{2}\fint_{\Omega}v(x,y)^{2}{\rm d}y+O(\varepsilon),

(42)

so that
\varepsilon\|\psi^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}\|_{H^{2}_{\rm per}}
is bounded uniformly in
\varepsilon
.

The second QoI is the integrated density of states
\mathcal{I}^{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}(E):=\fint_{\Omega^{*}}\tr\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,E]}\left(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}\right){\rm d}k=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\fint_{\Omega^{*}}\mathds{1}\left(E^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}\leqslant E\right){\rm d}k,

which we want to compare to the homogenized integrated density of states

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{0}(E):=\fint_{\Omega^{*}}\tr\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,E]}\left(H^{0}_{k}\right){\rm d}k=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\fint_{\Omega^{*}}\mathds{1}\left(E^{0}_{\ell,k}\leqslant E\right){\rm d}k.

The arguments we use are similar to the ones in
[
CEG+20
]
. Consider an
\mathbb{L}
-periodic Schrödinger operator
H
, with Bloch eigenvalues
E_{\ell,k}
. For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the level set

\displaystyle S^{\mu}_{\ell}:=\left\{k\in\Omega^{*}\,\bigr|\,E_{\ell,k}=\mu\right\}.

We say that
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
is a non-degenerate point of the spectrum of
H
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(C1):

S^{\mu}:=\bigcup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}S_{\ell}^{\mu}\neq\emptyset
(
\mu\in\sigma(H)
),

(C2):

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,
S_{\ell}^{\mu}\cap S_{\ell+1}^{\mu}=\emptyset
(no surface crossing at energy level
\mu
),

(C3):

\partial_{k}E_{\ell,k}\neq 0
for all
k\in S_{\ell}^{\mu}
(no van Hove singularities at energy level
\mu
).

Here and below
\partial_{k}E_{\ell,k}
denotes the gradient of the function
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni k\mapsto E_{\ell,k}\in\mathbb{R}
, which is well-defined by Kato’s perturbation theory on
S_{\ell}^{\mu}
in view of the non-degeneracy condition (C2).

Theorem 2.11

(Integrated density of states)
.

Assume
v
and
W
satisfy (

(1)

If

, there exists

such that for all

\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}(E)=\mathcal{N}^{0}(E)
;

(2)

If
E
is a non-degenerate point of the spectrum of
H^{0}
, then we have, using the notation of Theorem
2.7
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}(E)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}^{0}(E)+O(\varepsilon)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\fint_{\Omega^{*}}\mathds{1}\left(E^{0,\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}\leqslant E\right)\,{\rm d}k+O(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\fint_{\Omega^{*}}\mathds{1}\left(E^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{\ell,k}\leqslant E\right)\,{\rm d}k+O(\varepsilon^{4})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\fint_{\Omega^{*}}\mathds{1}\left(\widetilde{E}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{\ell,k}\leqslant E\right)\,{\rm d}k+O(\varepsilon^{4}).

4.
Formal two-scale expansions

In this section, we perform the formal two-scale expansions for both the eigenvalue problem and the linear equation.

4.1.
Two-scale expansion of the eigenvalue problem

We start with the eigenvalue problem

H^{\varepsilon}_{k}\psi^{\varepsilon}=E^{\varepsilon}\psi^{\varepsilon}.

(45)

Our goal is to approximate the eigenmode
(E^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k},\psi^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k})
with simpler quantities, computed from macroscopic objects. Without loss of generality, we may impose the intermediate normalization condition

\langle\psi^{0}_{\ell,k},\psi^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}=1.

(46)

We consider the formal expansions

\psi^{\varepsilon}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}\varepsilon^{i}\psi_{i}\left(x,\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad E^{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}\varepsilon^{i}E_{i}.

(47)

where
\psi_{i}(x,y)
is
\mathbb{L}
-periodic with respect to both
x
and
y
, and
insert these expansions in (
45
)-(
46
). In what follows, we write
f(x)
if
f
depends only on the variable
x
, and
f
if it depends on the two variable
(x,y)
. Regrouping the terms in
\varepsilon^{i}
and considering
x
and
y=\frac{x}{\varepsilon}
as independent variables, we obtain

\varepsilon^{-2}\left(-\Delta_{y}\psi_{0}\right)+\varepsilon^{-1}\left[-\Delta_{y}\psi_{1}+(v-2\nabla_{x,k}\cdot\nabla_{y})\psi_{0}\right]\\
+\sum_{i\geqslant 0}\varepsilon^{i}\bigg(-\Delta_{y}\psi_{i+2}+\left(v-2\nabla_{x,k}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)\psi_{i+1}\\
+\left(-\nabla_{x,k}^{2}+W(x)\right)\psi_{i}-\left(E*\psi\right)_{i}\bigg)=0,

where we used the relations

\displaystyle\nabla_{k}\left(fg\right)=f\nabla_{k}g+g\nabla f,\qquad\nabla_{k}^{2}\left(fg\right)=g\nabla_{k}^{2}f+2\nabla_{k}f\cdot\nabla g+f\Delta g.

At the formal level, we interpret the normalization condition (
46
) as

\fint_{\Omega}\langle\psi_{\ell,k}^{0},\psi_{0}(\cdot,y)\rangle_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}{\rm d}y=1,\quad\text{and}\quad\forall i\geqslant 1,\quad\fint_{\Omega}\langle\psi_{\ell,k}^{0},\psi_{i}(\cdot,y)\rangle_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}{\rm d}y=0.

Terms in
\varepsilon^{-2}
.

At leading order in
\varepsilon
, the equation becomes
\Delta_{y}\psi_{0}(x,y)=0
, from which we infer that
\psi_{0}
is independent of
y
. We write
\psi_{0}(x,y)=\psi_{0}(x)
to simplify the notation. The normalization condition simply reads
\langle\psi^{0}_{\ell,k},\psi_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}=1
.

Terms in
\varepsilon^{-1}
.

Since
\psi_{0}
is independent of
y
, the next order simply reads

\Delta_{y}\psi_{1}(x,y)=v(x,y)\psi_{0}(x).

Integrating in
y
on
\Omega
, we obtain the compatibility condition

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(x)\fint_{\Omega}v(x,y)\,{\rm d}y=0

for all
x
, which justifies the assumption
\fint_{\Omega}v(\cdot,y)\,{\rm d}y=0
. We will see later that
\psi_{0}
is a normalized eigenfunction of a Schrödinger operator, and therefore does not vanish on a set of positive measure by the unique continuation principle. This also motivates the introduction of the corrector
\chi_{1}
defined in (
5
), and satisfies
\Delta_{y}\chi_{1}=v
. We obtain

\psi_{1}(x,y)=\chi_{1}(x,y)\psi_{0}(x)+U_{1}(x),

(48)

where the function
U_{1}
only depends on the macroscopic variable
x
. Since
\fint_{\Omega}\chi_{1}(x,y){\rm d}y=0
, the normalization condition reads

\langle\psi^{0}_{\ell,k},U_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}=0.

(49)

Terms in
\varepsilon^{0}
.

We have

\displaystyle-\Delta_{y}\psi_{2}+\left(v-2\nabla_{x,k}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)\psi_{1}+\left(-\nabla_{x,k}^{2}+W(x)-E_{0}\right)\psi_{0}=0.

Inserting the formula for
\psi_{1}
in (
48
) and reordering the terms, we get

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\left[\left(-\nabla_{k}^{2}+W-E_{0}\right)\psi_{0}\right](x)+v\chi_{1}\psi_{0}(x)-2\left(\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{y}\chi_{1}\right)\psi_{0}(x)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{y}\psi_{2}-2\nabla_{y}\chi_{1}\cdot\nabla_{k}\psi_{0}(x)+vU_{1}(x).

(50)

Averaging with respect to
y
on the periodic
\Omega
, the last four terms of the right-hand side vanish and we get

\left(-\nabla_{k}^{2}+W(x)-E_{0}\right)\psi_{0}(x)+\underbrace{\left(\fint_{\Omega}v(x,y)\chi_{1}(x,y)\,{\rm d}y\right)}_{=V(x)}\psi_{0}(x)=0,

which justifies the definition (
6
) of the homogenized potential
V
. The pair
(E_{0},\psi_{0})
therefore is an eigenmode of the homogenized operator
H^{0}_{k}=-\nabla_{k}^{2}+V+W
such that
\langle\psi^{0}_{\ell,k},\psi_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}=1
. Assuming for simplicity that
E^{0}_{\ell,k}
is a non-degenerate eigenvalue of
H^{0}_{k}
, we obtain that

E_{0}=E^{0}_{\ell,k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\psi_{0}=\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}.

(51)

Going back to (
50
) and recalling that
V_{0}=V+W
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}\psi_{2}

\displaystyle=\left[v\chi_{1}-V_{0}(x)-2\left(\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)\chi_{1}\right]\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}-2\nabla_{y}\chi_{1}\cdot\nabla_{k}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)+vU_{1}(x).

All quantities have null average in
y\in\Omega
. Recalling the definition of
\chi_{2}
and
A_{2}
in (
13
)-(
12
), we integrate this expression to obtain

\displaystyle\psi_{2}=\chi_{2}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)+A_{2}\cdot\nabla_{k}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)+\chi_{1}U_{1}(x)+U_{2}(x),

(52)

where the function
U_{2}
only depends on the macroscopic variable
x
. In order to have an approximation of
\psi^{\varepsilon}_{\ell,k}
up to order
2
, we need to identify the functions
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
appearing in the expression of
\psi_{2}
. It turns out that only the expression of
U_{1}
matters (the term
U_{2}
will give a higher order contribution). To find
U_{1}
, we consider the next order.

Terms in
\varepsilon^{1}
.

Using (
51
), the term in
\varepsilon^{1}
reads

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}\psi_{3}=\left(v-2\nabla_{x,k}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)\psi_{2}+\left(-\nabla_{x,k}^{2}+W(x)-E_{0}\right)\psi_{1}-E_{1}\psi^{0}(x).

Inserting the expressions found for
E_{0}
,
\psi_{0}
,
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
, this is also

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}\psi_{3}=

\displaystyle\left(v-2\nabla_{x,k}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)\left[\chi_{2}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)+A_{2}\cdot\nabla_{k}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)+\chi_{1}U_{1}(x)+U_{2}(x)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\left(-\nabla_{x,k}^{2}+W(x)-E^{0}_{\ell,k}\right)\left[\chi_{1}\psi_{0}(x)+U_{1}(x)\right]-E_{1}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x).

(53)

We first average in
y
. Recall that
V_{1}
was defined in (
11
) by
V_{1}(x):=\fint_{\Omega}v(x,y)\chi_{2}(x,y){\rm d}y
,
and notice that

\displaystyle\fint_{\Omega}v(x,y)A_{2}(x,y){\rm d}y\hskip-1.42271pt=\fint_{\Omega}\chi_{1}\left(\Delta_{y}A_{2}\right)\hskip-1.42271pt=-2\fint_{\Omega}\chi_{1}\left(\nabla_{y}\chi_{1}\right)\hskip-1.42271pt=-\fint_{\Omega}\nabla_{y}|\chi_{1}|^{2}=0.

(54)

We obtain

\displaystyle E_{1}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)

\displaystyle=V_{1}(x)\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)+V(x)U_{1}(x)+\left(-\nabla_{k}^{2}+W(x)-E^{0}_{\ell,k}\right)U_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(H^{0}_{k}-E^{0}_{\ell,k}\right)U_{1}(x)+V_{1}(x)\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x).

Taking the inner product with
\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}
yields

\displaystyle E_{1}=\int_{\Omega}V_{1}\left|\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}\right|^{2},

(55)

and hence

\displaystyle(H^{0}_{k}-E^{0}_{\ell,k})U_{1}=(E_{1}-V_{1})\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}\in(\psi^{0}_{\ell,k})^{\perp}\quad\mbox{(in $L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)$)}.

(56)

Still assuming that
E_{k}^{0}
is non-degenerate, and recalling that
\langle\psi^{0}_{\ell,k},U_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}=0
, see (
49
), we finally get

U_{1}=-\left(H^{0}_{k}-E_{k}^{0}\right)_{\perp}^{-1}(V_{1}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}).

(57)

Note that
U_{1}
coincides with the function
\phi^{1}_{\ell,k}
introduced in Theorem
2.10
.
One can continue the computations, and find explicit expressions for
U_{2}(x)
,
\psi_{3}(x,y)
, and so on (see Appendix
B
). For our purpose however, we may stop here.

Conclusion.

Collecting the results in (
48
), (
51
), (
52
), (
57
), we obtain

\displaystyle\psi^{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)+\varepsilon\left[(\chi_{1})_{\varepsilon}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)-\left(H^{0}_{k}-E_{k}^{0}\right)_{\perp}^{-1}(V_{1}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon^{2}\left[(\chi_{2})_{\varepsilon}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}(x)+(A_{2})_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{k}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}-\left(\chi_{1}\right)_{\varepsilon}\left(H^{0}_{k}-E_{k}^{0}\right)_{\perp}^{-1}(V_{1}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon^{2}U_{2}(x)+O(\varepsilon^{3})\qquad\mbox{(formal two-scale expansion)}.

At this point, we may expect that the remainder is actually
O(\varepsilon^{3})
for the
L^{2}_{\rm per}
-norm, but certainly not for the
H^{1}_{\rm per}
-norm because the following term in the expansion, namely
\varepsilon^{3}\psi_{3}\left(x,\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)
, is highly oscillatory. On the other hand, we may expect that the remainder is
O(\varepsilon^{2})
for the
H^{1}_{\rm per}
-norm since the latter only involves first derivatives of quantities oscillating at scale
\varepsilon
. As our goal is to obtain first-order approximations of eigenfunctions of
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
for the
H^{1}_{\rm per}
-norm, we may get rid of the non-oscillatory term
\varepsilon^{2}U_{2}(x)
. We finally end up with

\displaystyle\psi^{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle\left(1+\varepsilon(\chi_{1})_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{2}\left[\lx@nobreakspace(\chi_{2})_{\varepsilon}+(A_{2})_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{k}\right]\right)\psi^{0}_{\ell,k}

\displaystyle\quad-\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon(\chi_{1})_{\varepsilon})\left(H^{0}_{k}-E_{k}^{0}\right)_{\perp}^{-1}(V_{1}\psi^{0}_{\ell,k})+O_{H^{1}_{\rm per}}(\varepsilon^{2})\qquad\mbox{(expected)}.

This motivates the introduction of the operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{z,k}
defined in (
15
).

4.2.
Two-scale expansion of the linear equation

We now perform similar computations to solve the linear equation

\displaystyle\left(H^{\varepsilon}_{k}-z\right)u^{\varepsilon}=f.

In what follows, we assume that
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H^{0}_{k})
, and we denote by
u^{0}_{k,z,f}:=(H^{0}_{k}-z)^{-1}f
the solution of the homogenized equation. We make the Ansatz

u^{\varepsilon}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty}\varepsilon^{i}u_{i}\left(x,\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right).

We obtain the new set of equations

\sum_{i\geqslant-2}\varepsilon^{i}\bigg(-\Delta_{y}u_{i+2}+\delta_{i\geqslant-1}\left(v-2\nabla_{x,k}\cdot\nabla_{y}\right)u_{i+1}\\
+\delta_{i\geqslant 0}\left(\left(-\nabla_{x,k}^{2}+W(x)\right)u_{i}-f\right)\bigg)=0.

Repeating the same arguments as in the previous section (this time,
(H_{k}^{0}-z)
is invertible), we get (recall that we note
f
for
f(x,y)
)

\displaystyle u_{0}=

\displaystyle u^{0}_{k,z,f}(x)

\displaystyle u_{1}=

\displaystyle\chi_{1}u^{0}_{k,z,f}(x)-[\left(H^{0}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}(V_{1}u^{0}_{k,z,f})](x)

\displaystyle u_{2}=

\displaystyle\left(\chi_{2}+A_{2}\cdot\nabla_{k}\right)u^{0}_{k,z,f}(x)-\chi_{1}[\left(H^{0}_{k}-z\right)^{-1}(V_{1}u^{0}_{k,z,f})](x)+U_{2}(x),

which suggests that

u^{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon,(2)}_{z,k}u^{0}_{k,z,f}+O_{H^{1}_{\rm per}}(\varepsilon^{2})\qquad\mbox{(expected)}.

and

(2)

For any

in the sense of quadratic forms.

(4)

(63)

Proof.

5.2.
Operator expansions

We will make repeated use of the following relations. First, for
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
, we have

\begin{cases}\nabla(f_{\varepsilon})=(\nabla_{x}f)_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{-1}(\nabla_{y}f)_{\varepsilon},\\
\Delta(f_{\varepsilon})=(\Delta_{x}f)_{\varepsilon}+2\varepsilon^{-1}(\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{y}f)_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{-2}(\Delta_{y}f)_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

(65)

Second, if
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
and
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
have suitable regularities, we have in the sense of linear operators on
L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{k}^{2}\left(f_{\varepsilon}\bullet\right)=

\displaystyle(\Delta_{x}f)_{\varepsilon}+2\varepsilon^{-1}((\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{y})f)_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{-2}(\Delta_{y}f)_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle+2\left[(\nabla_{x}f)_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{-1}(\nabla_{y}f)_{\varepsilon}\right]\cdot\nabla_{k}+f_{\varepsilon}\nabla_{k}^{2},

(66)

\displaystyle\nabla_{k}^{2}(A_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{k}\bullet)=

\displaystyle\left[(\Delta_{x}A)_{\varepsilon}+2\varepsilon^{-1}\left((\nabla_{x}\cdot\nabla_{y})A\right)_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{-2}(\Delta_{y}A)_{\varepsilon}\right]\cdot\nabla_{k}

\displaystyle+2\left[({\rm d}_{x}A)_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{-1}({\rm d}_{y}A)_{\varepsilon}\right]:\nabla_{k}^{\otimes 2}+(A_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{k})\nabla_{k}^{2},

(67)

where we used the following notation:

•

if
\psi\in H^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
,
\nabla_{k}^{\otimes 2}\psi
is the matrix-valued function of
L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}^{d\times d})
with entries
[\nabla_{k}^{\otimes 2}\psi]_{ij}=(\partial_{x_{i}}+ik_{i})(\partial_{x_{j}}+ik_{j})\psi
. Note that
[\nabla_{k}^{2}\psi](x)
is the trace of the matrix
[\nabla_{k}^{\otimes 2}\psi](x)\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
;

•

the derivatives
{\rm d}_{x}A
and
{\rm d}_{y}A
of the two-scale vector field
A
are the two-scale matrix fields

{\rm d}_{x}A(x,y)=\left[\frac{\partial A_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(x,y)\right]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\rm d}_{y}A(x,y)=\left[\frac{\partial A_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}(x,y)\right]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d};

•

the doubly contracted product
P:Q
is defined as
P:Q=\tr(P^{T}Q)
.

5.2.1.
Expansion of

Lemma 5.5

(Approximations of eigenvalues)
.

Let

and form domain

in the sense of quadratic forms, for some constants

(101)

This shows that if we have an

, then

(i.e. the Rayleigh quotient since

is normalized) provides an approximation of

of order

.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle 2\re\left<\psi,\psi-\varphi\right>_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}=2-2\re\left<\psi,\varphi\right>_{L^{2}_{\rm per}}=\left\|\psi-\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}_{\textup{per}}}^{2},

hence

\displaystyle\langle\varphi|H|\varphi\rangle-E

\displaystyle=\langle\psi-\varphi|H|\psi-\varphi\rangle-2\re\langle\psi|H|\psi-\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi-\varphi|H|\psi-\varphi\rangle-E\left\|\psi-\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}_{\textup{per}}}^{2}

and similarly the remaining two estimates of
