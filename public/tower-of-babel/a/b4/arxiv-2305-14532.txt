Berry Curvature Spectroscopy from Bloch Oscillations
Christophe De Beule, E. J. Mele

Abstract
Artificial crystals such as moiré superlattices can have a real-space periodicity much larger than the underlying atomic scale. This facilitates the presence of Bloch oscillations in the presence of a static electric field. We demonstrate that the optical response of such a system, when dressed with a static field, becomes resonant at the frequencies of Bloch oscillations, which are in the THz regime when the lattice constant is of the order of 10 ​ nm 10\;\text{nm} . In particular, we show within a semiclassical band-projected theory that resonances in the dressed Hall conductivity are proportional to the lattice Fourier components of the Berry curvature. We illustrate our results with a low-energy model on an effective honeycomb lattice.

\varepsilon_{\bm{R}}

\omega_{B}\tau\gg 1

\tau

These results can thus potentially be used to map out the distribution of the Berry curvature in systems with time-reversal symmetry by measuring the resonances in the dressed optical Hall conductivity in the nonperturbative regime where
\omega_{B}\tau\gg 1

First-shell approximation.

In conclusion, we developed a band-projected semiclassical theory for the optical response of an artificial crystal, such as a moiré material, that is dressed by a uniform static field. When the static field is sufficiently strong, achieved for field strengths of order
10\;\text{kV/cm}
for a lattice constant of order
10\;\text{nm}
, the dressed system becomes resonant at the Bloch frequencies which are in the
10\;\text{THz}
regime. We quantified this effect by defining a dressed optical conductivity whose imaginary part displays resonant peaks, while the real part vanishes at resonance. In particular, the height of the resonances in the optical Hall conductivity probe the lattice Fourier components of the Berry curvature and are independent of the relaxation time. One thus obtains an intrinsic probe of the quantum geometry of the band by resonantly coupling light to Bloch oscillations. The dressed optical conductivity can for example be obtained from THz Faraday rotation and ellipticity spectroscopy measurements
Spielman et al. 1994
;
Shimano et al. 2011
. In contrast to probes of the Berry curvature multipoles, such at the rectified second-order response involving the Berry curvature dipole
Sodemann and Fu 2015
, our proposal works at linear order in the optical response, and works best for a smooth Berry curvature dominated by the first coordination shell whose lowest multipoles are zero or small. Moreover, by changing the in-plane direction of the static field, one can tune contributions from different lattice vectors. This work thus provides a novel route to probe the Berry curvature in time-reversal symmetric moiré and other artificial crystals which have a large real-space periodicity.

Semiclassical model of electron dynamics

The semiclassical equations of motion for an electron in a two-dimensional (2D) crystal, occupying an energy band with dispersion
\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}
subjected to a uniform electric field
\bm{E}(t)
are given by
Chang and Niu 1995
;
Sundaram and Niu 1999

\displaystyle\hbar\dot{\bm{r}}_{n\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{k}}\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}-\hbar\dot{\bm{k}}\times\bm{\Omega}_{n\bm{k}},

(S1a)

\displaystyle\hbar\dot{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=-e\bm{E}(t),

(S1b)

where the dot stands for the time derivative
d/dt
. Here
e>0
is the elementary charge,
n
is the band index, and
\bm{\Omega}_{n\bm{k}}=\Omega_{n\bm{k}}\hat{z}
is the Berry curvature. The latter is defined as

\Omega_{n\bm{k}}=i\left(\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{x}}\bigg\rvert\frac{\partial u_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{y}}\right\rangle_{\text{cell}}-\left\langle\frac{\partial u_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{y}}\bigg\rvert\frac{\partial u_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial k_{x}}\right\rangle_{\text{cell}}\right),

(S2)

where
u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
are cell-periodic Bloch functions in periodic gauge,
u_{n,\bm{k}+\bm{G}}(\bm{r})=e^{-i\bm{G}\cdot\bm{r}}u_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
with
\bm{G}
a reciprocal lattice vector, and
\langle u_{n\bm{k}}|u_{m\bm{k}}\rangle_{\text{cell}}=\delta_{nm}
.

In the following, we consider a single band and omit the band index
n
.
The band dispersion and Berry curvature can be expanded as

\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}=\sum_{\bm{R}}\varepsilon_{\bm{R}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}},\qquad\Omega_{\bm{k}}=\sum_{\bm{R}}\Omega_{\bm{R}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}},

(S3)

where

\varepsilon_{\bm{R}}=V_{c}\int_{\bm{k}}\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}},\qquad\Omega_{\bm{R}}=V_{c}\int_{\bm{k}}\Omega_{\bm{k}}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}},\qquad\int_{\bm{k}}=\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}.

(S4)

II
Boltzmann transport equation

The Boltzmann equation for the distribution function
f(\bm{k},\bm{r},t)
in the relaxation-time approximation, is given by

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\frac{d\bm{k}}{dt}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}f+\frac{d\bm{r}}{dt}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{r}}f=\frac{f^{0}-f}{\tau},

(S5)

where
f^{0}(\bm{k})
is the equilibrium distribution function, i.e.,
f^{0}(\bm{k})=n_{F}(\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu)
with
n_{F}(z)=1/(e^{z/k_{B}T}+1)
the Fermi function, with
\mu
the chemical potential and
T
the temperature.

Let us consider a general uniform time-dependent electric field
\bm{E}(t)
. We are interested in the steady-state solutions (not necessarily static) of

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}-\frac{e}{\hbar}\bm{E}(t)\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}f=\frac{f^{0}-f}{\tau}.

(S6)

In a translational-invariant system,

f(\bm{k},t)=\sum_{\bm{R}}f_{\bm{R}}(t)\,e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}},

(S7)

where
\bm{R}
are lattice vectors, and similarly for
f^{0}(\bm{k})
. We then obtain an ordinary differential equation for each Fourier component
f_{\bm{R}}(t)
,

\frac{df_{\bm{R}}}{dt}+\left(\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{ie}{\hbar}\bm{E}(t)\cdot\bm{R}\right)f_{\bm{R}}(t)=\frac{f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{\tau},

(S8)

whose general solution is given by

f_{\bm{R}}(t)=\frac{f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{\tau}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,e^{-\frac{t-t^{\prime}}{\tau}}\exp\left[\frac{ie}{\hbar}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}du\,\bm{E}(u)\cdot\bm{R}\right],

(S9)

with
t_{0}
an integration constant.
In the static limit, i.e., for a time-independent electric field, we have

\lim_{\bm{E}(t)\rightarrow\bm{E}}f_{\bm{R}}(t)=\frac{f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{\tau}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,e^{-(t-t^{\prime})\left(\frac{1}{\tau}-ie\bm{E}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar\right)}=f^{0}_{\bm{R}}\,\frac{1-e^{-\frac{t-t_{0}}{\tau}\left(1-ie\tau\bm{E}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar\right)}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}.

(S10)

The steady-state solution is thus given by

\displaystyle f_{\bm{R}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{\tau}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,e^{-\frac{t-t^{\prime}}{\tau}}\exp\left[\frac{ie}{\hbar}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}du\,\bm{E}(u)\cdot\bm{R}\right]

(S11)

\displaystyle=f^{0}_{\bm{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-s}\exp\left[\frac{ie}{\hbar}\int_{t-s\tau}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\bm{E}(t^{\prime})\cdot\bm{R}\right],

(S12)

where
s=(t-t^{\prime})/\tau
. The exponential factor can be interpreted as the integrated momentum shift between two scattering events at
t-s\tau
and
t
. Going back to momentum space, we have
Mikhailov 2017

f(\bm{k},t)=\sum_{\bm{R}}f_{\bm{R}}(t)e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-s}f^{0}\left(\bm{k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\int_{t-s\tau}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\bm{E}(t^{\prime})\right).

(S13)

We now consider the following driving field:

\bm{E}(t)=\bm{E}_{0}+\bm{E}_{1}(t),\qquad\bm{E}_{1}(t)=\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}e^{i\omega t}+\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{*}e^{-i\omega t}=2\,\text{Re}\left(\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}e^{i\omega t}\right),

(S14)

where
E_{0}=|\bm{E}_{0}|
is large compared to
E_{1}=|\bm{E}_{1}|
. In this case, the Fourier components of the distribution function become

\displaystyle f_{\bm{R}}(t)

\displaystyle=f^{0}_{\bm{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-\left(1-\frac{ie\tau}{\hbar}\,\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}\right)s}\exp\left[\frac{ie}{\hbar}\int_{t-s\tau}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\bm{E}_{1}(t^{\prime})\cdot\bm{R}\right]

(S15)

\displaystyle=f^{0}_{\bm{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-\left(1-\frac{ie\tau}{\hbar}\,\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}\right)s}\exp\left[\frac{ie}{\hbar}\left(\frac{\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}}{i\omega}\,e^{i\omega t}\left(1-e^{-i\omega s\tau}\right)+\text{c.c.}\right)\right].

(S16)

Up to first order in
E_{1}
, we can expand this as

\displaystyle f_{\bm{R}}(t)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}+\frac{ief^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{2\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-\left(1-\frac{ie\tau}{\hbar}\,\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}\right)s}\left[\frac{\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}}{i\omega}\,e^{i\omega t}\left(1-e^{-i\omega s\tau}\right)+\text{c.c.}\right]

(S17)

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}\left[1+\frac{e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}{\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}}\,e^{i\omega t}-\frac{e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{*}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}{\omega+e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar+\frac{i}{\tau}}\,e^{-i\omega t}\right].

(S18)

Defining the frequency-space Fourier components as

f_{\bm{R}}(t)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\bm{R},m}\,e^{im\omega t},\qquad f_{\bm{R},m}(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi/\omega}dt\,f_{\bm{R}}(t)\,e^{-im\omega t},

(S19)

with
f_{\bm{R},-m}=f_{-\bm{R},m}^{*}
, we have, for example,

\displaystyle f_{\bm{R},0}

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}\left[1-\frac{2|e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar|^{2}}{\left(\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}\right)\left(\omega+e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar+\frac{i}{\tau}\right)}+\mathcal{O}(E_{1}^{4})\right],

(S20)

\displaystyle f_{\bm{R},1}

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}\left[\frac{e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}{\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}}+\mathcal{O}(E_{1}^{3})\right],

(S21)

\displaystyle f_{\bm{R},2}

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}\left[\frac{e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}{\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}}\frac{e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}{2\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}}+\mathcal{O}(E_{1}^{4})\right].

(S22)

III
Dressed optical conductivity

The steady-state current is given by
\bm{j}(t)=\bm{j}_{\text{Bloch}}(t)+\bm{j}_{\text{geom}}(t)
with

\displaystyle\bm{j}_{\text{Bloch}}(t)

\displaystyle=-e\int_{\bm{k}}f_{\bm{k}}(t)\nabla_{\bm{k}}\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}=\frac{ie}{\hbar V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\bm{R}\,\varepsilon_{-\bm{R}}f_{\bm{R}}(t),

(S23)

\displaystyle\bm{j}_{\text{geom}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\left[\bm{E}(t)\times\hat{z}\right]\int_{\bm{k}}f_{\bm{k}}(t)\Omega_{\bm{k}}=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar V_{c}}\,\hat{z}\times\sum_{\bm{R}}\Omega_{-\bm{R}}\bm{E}(t)f_{\bm{R}}(t),

(S24)

with

V_{c}\int_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}=\delta_{\bm{R},\bm{0}},\qquad\sum_{\bm{R}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}=\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{V_{c}}\,\delta(\bm{k}).

(S25)

The frequency components of the currents are thus given by

\displaystyle\bm{j}_{\text{Bloch}}^{(m)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{ie}{\hbar V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\bm{R}\,\varepsilon_{-\bm{R}}f_{\bm{R},m},

(S26)

\displaystyle\bm{j}_{\text{geom}}^{(m)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar V_{c}}\,\hat{z}\times\sum_{\bm{R}}\Omega_{-\bm{R}}\left(\bm{E}_{0}f_{\bm{R},m}+\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\,f_{\bm{R},m-1}+\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{*}\,f_{\bm{R},m+1}\right),

(S27)

with
\bm{j}^{(-m)}=\left(\bm{j}^{(m)}\right)^{*}
. For example, the DC component of the geometric current becomes

\displaystyle\bm{j}_{\text{geom}}^{(0)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar V_{c}}\,\hat{z}\times\sum_{\bm{R}}\frac{\Omega_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}\Bigg\{\left[1-\frac{|e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar|^{2}}{\left(\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}\right)\left(\omega+e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar+\frac{i}{\tau}\right)}\right]\bm{E}_{0}

(S28)

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{\left(e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar\right)\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{*}}{\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}}-\frac{\left(e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{*}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar\right)\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}}{\omega+e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar+\frac{i}{\tau}}\right]+\mathcal{O}(E_{1}^{4})\Bigg\}.

(S29)

In lowest order of
|e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar\omega|
, the first harmonics are given by

\displaystyle\bm{j}_{\text{Bloch}}^{(1)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{-ie}{\hbar V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\frac{\bm{R}\varepsilon_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}\frac{e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}{\omega+e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar+\frac{i}{\tau}},

(S30)

\displaystyle\bm{j}_{\text{geom}}^{(1)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar V_{c}}\,\hat{z}\times\sum_{\bm{R}}\frac{\Omega_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}\left[\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}+\frac{\left(e\bm{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar\right)\bm{E}_{0}}{\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}}\right].

(S31)

We now define the
dressed
optical conductivity
\sigma_{ij}(\omega,\bm{E}_{0})
through
j_{i}^{(1)}=\sigma_{ij}\mathcal{E}_{1j}
. The current can thus be written as

j_{i}(t)=j_{i}^{(0)}+2\,\text{Re}\left[\sigma_{ij}(\omega,\bm{E}_{0})\mathcal{E}_{1j}e^{i\omega t}\right]+\mathcal{O}(E_{1}^{2}).

(S32)

Making use of
(\bm{a}\times\bm{b})_{i}=\epsilon_{ijk}a^{j}b^{k}
with
\epsilon_{i3k}=-\epsilon_{ik}
the permutation symbol, we find

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}(\omega,\bm{E}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{ie^{2}}{\hbar^{2}V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\frac{R_{i}R_{j}\varepsilon_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{\left(1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar\right)\left(\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}\right)}

(S33)

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon_{ik}e^{2}}{\hbar V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\frac{\Omega_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}\left[\delta_{kj}+\frac{eE_{0k}R_{j}/\hbar}{\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}}\right],

(S34)

with
\sigma_{ij}(\omega,\bm{E}_{0})=\sigma_{ij}(-\omega,\bm{E}_{0})^{*}
. As a check, we undress the conductivity:

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}(\omega,\bm{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{ie^{2}}{\hbar^{2}V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\frac{R_{i}R_{j}\varepsilon_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{\omega-\frac{i}{\tau}}-\epsilon_{ij}\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\frac{1}{V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\Omega_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}

(S35)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{\bm{k}}f^{0}_{\bm{k}}\,\frac{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}}{i\omega+\frac{1}{\tau}}-\epsilon_{ij}\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\int_{\bm{k}}f^{0}_{\bm{k}}\Omega_{\bm{k}},

(S36)

with
\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial k_{i}
. This is a well-known result for the conductivity: the first term is the Drude contribution and the second term is the anomalous Hall conductivity.

Let us now consider the dressed longitudinal conductivity
\sigma_{L}=\left(\sigma_{xx}+\sigma_{yy}\right)/2
which is a scalar and the dressed Hall conductivity,
\sigma_{H}=\left(\sigma_{xy}-\sigma_{yx}\right)/2
which is a pseudoscalar. We find

\displaystyle\sigma_{L}(\omega,\bm{E}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{ie^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\frac{R^{2}\varepsilon_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{\left(1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar\right)\left(\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}\right)},

(S37)

\displaystyle\sigma_{H}(\omega,\bm{E}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ij}\sigma_{ij}=-\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\frac{1}{V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\frac{\Omega_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{1-ie\tau\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar}{\omega-e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar-\frac{i}{\tau}}\right),

(S38)

where we used
\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{ik}=\delta_{jk}
. When the static field is strong, i.e, for
\omega_{B}\tau\gg 1
, where
\omega_{B}=eE_{0}L/\hbar
is the Bloch frequency with
L
the lattice constant,
\sigma_{L}
and
\sigma_{H}
simplify to

\displaystyle\sigma_{L}(\omega,\bm{E}_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{2}}{h}\frac{\pi}{\tau\hbar V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\frac{R^{2}\varepsilon_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{\omega_{\bm{R}}\left(\omega-\omega_{\bm{R}}-\frac{i}{\tau}\right)}=\frac{e^{2}}{h}\frac{\pi}{V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\left(\frac{-R^{2}\varepsilon_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{\hbar\omega_{\bm{R}}}\right)\frac{\left(\omega-\omega_{\bm{R}}\right)\tau+i}{\left(\omega-\omega_{\bm{R}}\right)^{2}\tau^{2}+1},

(S39)

\displaystyle\sigma_{H}(\omega,\bm{E}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{h}\frac{\pi}{i\tau V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\frac{\Omega_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}}{\omega-\omega_{\bm{R}}-\frac{i}{\tau}}=\frac{e^{2}}{h}\frac{\pi}{V_{c}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\left(-i\Omega_{-\bm{R}}f^{0}_{\bm{R}}\right)\frac{\left(\omega-\omega_{\bm{R}}\right)\tau+i}{\left(\omega-\omega_{\bm{R}}\right)^{2}\tau^{2}+1},

(S40)

with
\omega_{\bm{R}}=e\bm{E}_{0}\cdot\bm{R}/\hbar
. As mentioned in the main text, we see that at resonance,
\sigma_{L}
is purely reactive, while
\sigma_{H}
is purely dissipative (since
\Omega_{\bm{R}}
is imaginary for a system with time-reversal symmetry). Indeed, the dissipated power from the oscillating field over one period can be written as

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi/\omega}dt\,j_{i}(t)E_{1i}(t)

(S41)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi/\omega}dt\,j_{i}^{(m)}(\omega)e^{-im\omega t}\left(\mathcal{E}_{1i}e^{-i\omega t}+\mathcal{E}_{1i}^{*}e^{i\omega t}\right)

(S42)

\displaystyle=j_{i}^{(-1)}(\omega)\mathcal{E}_{1i}+j_{i}^{(1)}(\omega)\mathcal{E}_{1i}^{*}

(S43)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{ij}(\omega)+\sigma_{ji}^{*}(\omega)}{2}\,2\mathcal{E}_{1i}^{*}\mathcal{E}_{1j}

(S44)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{ij}(\omega)+\sigma_{ji}^{*}(\omega)}{2}\,\mathcal{E}_{1i}^{*}\mathcal{E}_{1j}+\frac{\sigma_{ij}(-\omega)+\sigma_{ji}^{*}(-\omega)}{2}\,\mathcal{E}_{1i}\mathcal{E}_{1j}^{*}

(S45)

Hence the absorpative part of the conductivity tensor is given by the Hermitian part:

\sigma^{\text{abs}}_{ij}=\text{Re}\,\frac{\sigma_{ij}+\sigma_{ji}}{2}+i\,\text{Im}\,\frac{\sigma_{ij}-\sigma_{ji}}{2}=\text{Re}\,\frac{\sigma_{ij}+\sigma_{ji}}{2}+i\epsilon_{ij}\,\text{Im}\,\sigma_{H}.

(S46)

Similarly, the reactive part of the conductivity tensor is given by the anti-Hermitian part. For linearly polarized light, we can take
\mathcal{E}_{1}
real and
\text{Im}\,\sigma_{H}
does not contribute to dissipation. However, this term does give rise to dissipation for circularly polarized light. In this case, the imaginary part of the optical Hall conductivity gives a transverse response that lags by a quarter cycle. Hence for circular polarization
\mathcal{E}_{1x}=\pm i\mathcal{E}_{1y}
, the current response due to
\text{Im}\,\sigma_{H}
actually lies parallel to the field and contributes to dissipation.
