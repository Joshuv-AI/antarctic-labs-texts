Global existence and convergence near equilibrium for the moving interface problem between Navier-Stokes and the linear wave equation
Daniel Coutand

Abstract




Abstract.

We first establish existence for all positive time near equilibrium for the moving interface problem between the Navier-Stokes equations for the evolving fluid phase (moved by the fluid velocity) and an elastic body modelled by the linear wave equation. This problem has an infinite number of simple solutions with a flat interface (with zero velocity in the fluid, and zero horizontal velocity in the solid), that we call flat interface solutions. We then show that if the initial data is close enough to the canonical equilibrium, the solution converges towards a flat interface solution in large time, showing that these flat interface solutions capture the long time behaviour of this fluid-structure problem near the canonical equilibrium. This result is established with gravity (which can be set to zero or not).

Introduction

Fluid-structure interaction problems are omnipresent in nature, and involve at the mathematical level a coupling between a fluid phase (most often modelled by the Navier-Stokes equations) and a solid phase (most often modelled by an hyperbolic PDE), with a time dependent interface moving with the velocity of the fluid.

Global existence (near equilibrium) for this type of moving interface problems coupling a Navier-Stokes fluid phase with a second order hyperbolic equation in the solid phase, was still completely open until the present paper. The results previously obtained so far in this direction have considered models with damping being added either in the solid phase or on the interface, see for instance

The addition of damping to the linear wave equation in the solid phase provides directly some dissipative terms in this phase, although some delicate analysis is needed to obtain the remaining relevant quantities. Without this addition, there is no obvious way to identify dissipative terms in the solid phase, which renders the issue of global existence substantially more challenging than the already complex cases previously treated in the literature.

The aim of the present paper is first to establish global in (positive) time existence for the moving interface problem between the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations and the linear wave equation modelling the elastic phase (without any damping being added), when the initial data is close to an equilibrium.

We then show convergence in large time toward a particular solution of the problem with a flat interface (there is an infinite number of such particular solutions). This is done with the gravity constant
g
which is either strictly positive or null. The paper establishes these results when the volume of the elastic phase is close enough to the volume of its reference configuration (where the linear wave equation is set).

We now briefly outline the paper.

In Section
2
, we define our notations. In particular, we take the viewpoint of defining fractional Sobolev norms on
\Gamma

In Section

We call these special solutions

In Section
4
, we introduce our Arbitrary Lagrangian representation of the problem via a Stokes extension problem (and not the standard Lagrangian coordinates in the fluid), in order to obtain global in time existence. The fundamental interest of this choice of representation is explained in Section
6
. We then introduce our functional framework, with in particular a dissipative norm (associated with the viscosity in the fluid). We finally state our main Theorems and write down the variational problems respectively associated with the system (
11
) and its first and second time differentiated versions.

In Section
5
, we gather needed estimates for the pressure that will be used later on.

In the crucial Section

{\Omega_{0}^{s}}

In Section
7

In Section
8

In Section
9

We conclude the proof of Theorem
1

For a solution satisfying the assumptions of Theorem

4
Arbitrary Lagrangian formulation of the problem and statement of our results

The main difficulty that the standard Lagrangian formulation of the problem would introduce is that the obvious identity

\eta(x,t)=\eta(x,0)+\int_{0}^{t}u(X(x,s),s)ds\,,

would yield by Cauchy-Schwarz a multiplicative constant of order
\sqrt{t}
when estimating the cofactor matrix for the Lagrangian variables, which is unsuitable for large time.

This is why we use an Arbitrary Lagrangian formulation of the problem, where the fluid domain is described not by using the Lagrangian coordinates, but instead by using the Stokes extension in
{\Omega_{0}^{f}}
of the trace of
\eta
on
\Gamma
taken from
{\Omega_{0}^{s}}
. This then obviously avoids the issue outlined above, as
\nabla^{3}\eta\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\Omega_{0}^{s}}))
from our framework introduced later in this Section.

Now, this does not avoid all difficulties. Once this formulation is adopted, a formidable difficulty arises when estimating terms such as

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\Omega_{0}^{f}}}q\bar{\partial}^{2}\tilde{a}_{i}^{j}\bar{\partial}^{2}v,_{j}^{i}dxdt\,

since
q
does not behave like a dissipative term. Although, as we will see,
\bar{\partial}q
is indeed dissipative, the fact
q
is linked to
\eta,_{3}
on
\Gamma
unfortunately does not allow any conclusion on making
q
behave like
\bar{\partial}q
.

Instead, we will show the rather surprising property that the Stokes extension of
\bar{\partial}\eta
in
{\Omega_{0}^{f}}
is actually a dissipative term, making the integral term above possible to estimate in a good way, independently of time.

4.1
An extension of
\eta
(coming from the solid phase) into
{\Omega_{0}^{f}}

4.1.1
Definition of our Arbitrary Lagrangian map

We denote by
\tilde{\eta}
the (periodic in the horizontal directions) extension of
\eta
(defined in
{\Omega_{0}^{s}}
) into
{\Omega_{0}^{f}}
defined by the linear Stokes problem:

\displaystyle-\Delta\tilde{\eta}+\nabla f

\displaystyle=0\ \ \

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \ {\Omega_{0}^{f}}\,,

(19a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{|{\Omega_{0}^{f}}|}\int_{\Gamma}\eta^{3}dx_{h}\ \ \

\displaystyle\text{in}\ \ {\Omega_{0}^{f}}\,,

(19b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=\eta\ \

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \ \Gamma\,,

(19c)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=0\ \

\displaystyle\text{on}\ \ \Gamma_{top}\,,

(19d)

which we then use in the formulation (
11
).

Remark 12
.

The compatibility condition (
19b
) ensures that the linear Stokes problem (

\int_{\Omega_{0}^{f}}\operatorname{div}\tilde{\eta}dx=\int_{\partial{\Omega_{0}^{f}}}\tilde{\eta}\cdot N^{f}dx_{h}\,.

Remark 13
.

The reason we use this Stokes extension and not an harmonic extension is for (
65
) established after, as it allows for
\bar{\partial}\operatorname{div}\tilde{\eta}=0
, whereas an harmonic extension would not provide this.

We then see from the Dirichlet boundary condition on
\Gamma
in (

\eta

(\tilde{\eta}+\text{Id})(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{h_{s},h\},t)=(\eta+\text{Id})(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{h_{s},h\},t)\,,

and so
\tilde{\eta}+\text{Id}
describes the motion of the fluid domain at each time.

Remark 14
.

Note that for the basic individual cell
{\Omega_{0}^{f}}
, although we may not have
(\tilde{\eta}+\text{Id})({\Omega_{0}^{f}},t)=(\eta+\text{Id})({\Omega_{0}^{f}},t)
, the thing that matters is that
(\tilde{\eta}+\text{Id})(\Gamma,t)=(\eta+\text{Id})(\Gamma,t)

4.1.2
Properties enabled by elliptic regularity for the linear extension operator

The first property needed later on is that for any first order horizontal derivative

\displaystyle-\Delta(\bar{\partial}\tilde{\eta})+\nabla(\bar{\partial}f)

\displaystyle=0\ \ \ \text{in}\ \ {\Omega_{0}^{f}}\,,

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\bar{\partial}\tilde{\eta})

\displaystyle=0\ \ \ \text{in}\ \ {\Omega_{0}^{f}}\,,

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=\bar{\partial}\eta^{s}\ \ \text{on}\ \ \Gamma\,,

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ \Gamma_{top}\,,

(we remind the top of the domain is horizontal for the Dirichlet condition on

), we have by standard elliptic regularity

\|\bar{\partial}\tilde{\eta}\|_{H^{2}({\Omega_{0}^{f}})}\lesssim\|\bar{\partial}\eta^{s}\|_{H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma)}=\|\bar{\partial}(\eta^{s}-\eta_{e})\|_{H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma)}\,,

(20)

where we used
\bar{\partial}\eta_{e}=0
.

Standard regularity for the first, second and third time-differentiated versions of (

\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}_{H^{3}({\Omega_{0}^{f}})}\lesssim\|v^{f}\|^{2}_{H^{\frac{5}{2}}(\Gamma)}\lesssim D(t)^{2}\,,

(21)

\|\tilde{v}_{t}\|^{2}_{H^{2}({\Omega_{0}^{f}})}\lesssim\|v^{f}_{t}\|^{2}_{H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Gamma)}\lesssim D(t)^{2}\,.

(22)

\|\tilde{v}_{tt}\|^{2}_{H^{1}({\Omega_{0}^{f}})}\lesssim\|v^{f}_{tt}\|^{2}_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)}\lesssim D(t)^{2}\,.

(23)

where the dissipative norm
D(t)
is defined in (
26

) and (

), we take the viewpoint the trace is taken from the fluid phase
{\Omega_{0}^{f}}
, whereas for (
20

{\Omega_{0}^{s}}
.

This is why
\tilde{v}
,
\tilde{v}_{t}
and
\tilde{v}_{tt}
have their norms controlled in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}({\Omega_{0}^{f}}))
,

does not seem to have this kind of control. As we will see later on,

is also controlled in

We will also need the estimate for
\tilde{\eta}
when no horizontal derivative is applied.
We first notice that elliptic regularity applied to (

\|\tilde{\eta}\|_{H^{3}({\Omega_{0}^{f}})}\lesssim\left|\int_{\Gamma}\eta^{3}dx_{h}\right|+\|\eta\|_{H^{\frac{5}{2}}(\Gamma)}\lesssim\|\eta-\eta_{e}\|_{H^{\frac{5}{2}}(\Gamma)}\,,

where we used
\eta_{e}=0
on
\Gamma

\|\tilde{\eta}\|_{H^{3}({\Omega_{0}^{f}})}\lesssim N(t)\,.

(24)

4.2
Local in time existence

We now define the norm
N(t)
which is finite initially, and remains finite (so long as the solution exists):

\displaystyle N(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{2}\|\partial_{t}^{k}v(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{2-k}(\Omega_{0}^{f})}+\sum_{k=0}^{2}\|\partial_{t}^{k}v(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{2-k}(\Omega_{0}^{s})}+\|\eta(\cdot,t)-\eta_{e}\|_{H^{3}({\Omega_{0}^{s}})}\,,

(25)

where
\eta_{e}
is defined in (
12a
),
as well as the dissipative norm

(associated to the viscous phase) which is square integrable in time (so long as the solution exists):

\displaystyle D(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{2}\|\partial_{t}^{k}v(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{3-k}({\Omega_{0}^{f}})}\,.

(26)

This dissipative norm has a particularily important part to play in establishing global in time existence.

The total energy
E(t)
is then defined as

E(t)=\sup_{[0,t]}N(t)^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}D(s)^{2}ds\,.

(27)

Due to the good elliptic properties of subsection

, the local in time existence of (

Remark 15
.

Of course,

and

are functions of the initial data
\eta(\cdot,0)
and

, which means that

implies that

is smoother than

or

. See for instance the property (

(although this property is not really needed in this paper). What matters is that the finite norm

is the framework giving existence of a solution to the problem which keeps this property propagating in (positive) time.

4.3
Small data assumption

Therefore, given an initial data
(v_{0}(x),\eta_{0}(x))
satisfying the appropriate compatibility conditions and regularity, we have that the problem (
11
) has a local in time solution on
[0,T]
,
(v,\eta)
such that

E(T)<\infty\,.

(28)

In this paper, we are interested in solutions close enough to equilibrium initially, namely such that

E(0)<\epsilon\,,

for
\epsilon>0
small. We know from the local in time theory that for a large time
T(\epsilon)>1
the solution will still exist at

and that

.

Due to (

into

\forall t\in[0,T(\epsilon)]\,,\ \tilde{J}\in[\frac{99}{100},\frac{101}{100}]\,,\ \|\tilde{A}-\text{Id}\|_{L^{\infty}({\Omega_{0}^{f}})}\leq\frac{1}{100}\,.

for
\epsilon>0

It is important to keep in mind these estimates, as they allow for a faster treatment of the global in time proof.

We will prove that for
\epsilon>0
small enough, the solution is defined on
[0,\infty)
, its energy

remains small, and the solution converges in large time towards a flat interface solution, as defined in

.

4.4
Statement of the Theorems

Theorem 1

(Global in time existence)
.

Let us assume that

1.

Our initial data satisfies the required compatibility conditions for local existence of a smooth solution (with finite
N(t)
).

2.

The elastic coefficient is large enough relative to gravity:

\displaystyle\lambda\geq\max(1,cg^{2})

(30)

for

large enough.

3.

The initial energy is small enough:
E(0)=N(0)^{2}\leq\epsilon_{0}
,

for
\epsilon_{0}>0
small enough. Then, the local in time solution exists for all positive time and
E(t)
remains small, of order
\epsilon_{0}
.

Remark 16
.

In the absence of gravity,

, and the condition (

reduces to

. This condition can be relaxed for

\lambda>0
, but for the sake of simplicity in the proofs, it is assumed this way.

Remark 17
.

Due to the last term in the energy (

requires

(average height of the elastic phase) to be close to
h_{s}
(height of the reference domain for the elastic phase, where the wave equation is set).

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic convergence)
.

Let us assume that
the assumptions of Theorem
1
are satisfied. Then, the interface

converges towards the flat interface

(defined in (

, while the velocity in the fluid phase converges to zero as

, and the vertical component of the displacement converges to the solution of a one dimensional wave equation in the solid phase. To be more specific, the norms in which these convergences hold are:

2.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|\eta-(0,0,h_{e}-h_{s})\|_{H^{\frac{5}{2}}(\Gamma)}=0\,,

3.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|v^{h}\|_{H^{1}({\Omega_{0}^{s}})}=0=\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|\eta^{h}\|_{H^{2}({\Omega_{0}^{s}})}
, where

There exists

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|v^{3}-\alpha_{t}\|_{L^{2}({\Omega_{0}^{s}})}=0=\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|\eta^{3}-\alpha\|_{H^{1}({\Omega_{0}^{s}})}\,.

6.1
L^{2}
in time estimate on
\Gamma
for the wave equation

Our starting point is to take horizontal derivatives of the linear wave equation, take the scalar product with two horizontal derivatives and one vertical derivative of
\eta
and integrate in space-time. As we seek to obtain fractional derivative space regularity on

From our reminder in Section

, since by horizontal periodicity

\int_{[0,L]^{2}}\bar{\partial}\eta(x_{h},x_{3},t)dx_{h}=0\,,

we have expanding
\bar{\partial}\eta(\cdot,x_{3},t)
(where

Since
\bar{\partial}\eta
is solution of the wave equation (without gravity term), we have for each mode (using

Multiplying (

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{h_{s}}x^{n}_{tt}x,_{3}^{n}+\lambda\lambda_{n}x^{n}x,_{3}^{n}-\lambda x,_{33}^{n}x,_{3}^{n}dx_{3}dt

Integrating by parts in space then yields

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(-|x^{n}_{t}|^{2}+\lambda\lambda_{n}|x^{n}|^{2}-\lambda|x,_{3}^{n}|^{2})(h_{s},\cdot)dt-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(-|x^{n}_{t}|^{2}+\lambda\lambda_{n}|x^{n}|^{2}-\lambda|x,_{3}^{n}|^{2})(0,\cdot)dt

Step 3. Convergence of

\displaystyle\|(\Lambda_{1}v_{3}-v_{3}),_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\|v_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\,.

By

Step 5. Study of the difference between

To shorten notations, we denote

\displaystyle\|m(\Lambda_{1}v^{3}-v_{3})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,h_{s})}(n)+\lambda\|m((\Lambda_{0}\eta^{3}-\eta_{3}),_{3})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,h_{s})}(n)

The basic energy estimate on the system (note the gravity terms cancel each other when forming the difference):

\displaystyle(\alpha^{n}-m(\eta^{3}))_{tt}-\lambda(\alpha^{n}-m(\eta^{3})),_{33}
