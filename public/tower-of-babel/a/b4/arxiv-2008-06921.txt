Slice-torus link invariants, combinatorial invariants, and positivity conditions.
Carlo Collari

Abstract
. We prove some necessary conditions for a link to be either concordant to a quasi-positive link, quasi-positive, positive, or the closure of a positive braid. The main applications of our results are a characterisation of positive links with unlinking number 1 1 and 2 2 , and a combinatorial criterion to test if a positive link is the closure of a positive braid. Finally, we compile a table of all positive and positive-braid prime links with less than 8 8 crossings.



1.
Introduction

In this note we study slice-torus and combinatorial invariants of multi-component links – as opposed to knots – in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
satisfying various positivity conditions. The aim of this note is dual; the first goal is to provide a necessary condition for a link to be concordant to a quasi-positive link, and compare it to other known obstructions. The second goal is to investigate certain combinatorial invariants of quasi-positive, positive and positive-braid links, providing obstructions for a link to fall in one of the aforementioned classes. As applications we give a characterisation of positive links with unlinking number
1
and
2
, and a combinatorial criterion to test if a positive link is the closure of a positive braid. Finally, we compile a table of all positive and positive-braid prime links with less than
8
crossings.

In what follows all manifolds and sub-manifolds are smooth and oriented, and boundaries are endowed with the induced orientation.

1.1.
Concordance to quasi-positive links

A
link
is a
1
-dimensional closed sub-manifold of the
3
-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, and a
knot
is a connected link. The letter
L
will be reserved for links, whereas the letter
K
will be reserved for knots. Two links are
equivalent
up to orientation-preserving ambient isotopy. As customary in the literature, we shall abuse the notation and make no difference between a link and its equivalence class.

Quasi-positive links are a special family of links which sits in the intersection between knot theory and the theory of complex curves in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. More precisely, quasi-positive link are those links obtained as the transverse intersection of a complex curve in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with
\mathbb{S}^{3}=\{(w,z)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\,|\,|w|^{2}+|z|^{2}=1\}
– see
[
4
,
37
]
.
This hints to the fact that quasi-positive links should have special properties with respect to concordance.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
be two
\ell
-component links. Then,
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
are
concordant
if there exists properly embedded disjoint annuli
A_{1}\sqcup...\sqcup A_{\ell}\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}\times[0,1]
, such that both
A_{i}\cap\mathbb{S}^{3}\times\{0\}
and
A_{i}\cap\mathbb{S}^{3}\times\{1\}
are non-empty for each
i
, and

\left(A_{1}\cup...\cup A_{\ell}\right)\cap\mathbb{S}^{3}\times\{0\}=L_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad\left(A_{1}\cup...\cup A_{\ell}\right)\cap\mathbb{S}^{3}\times\{1\}=rL_{1}^{*},

where
rL
denotes the link
L
with its orientation reversed, and
L^{*}
denotes the image of
L
under an orientation reversing diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.
A link concordant to an unlink is called (strongly)
slice
.

If one considers topologically locally flat embeddings instead of smooth embeddings, quasi-positive links do not present any special properties; in fact, every link is topologically locally flat concordant to a (strongly) quasi-positive link
[
8
]
. It follows that all concordance invariants which are also invariant under topological locally flat concordance cannot detect quasi-positive links. In particular, the linking matrix and, more generally, all classical concordance invariants obtained from the Seifert form, such as the Levine-Tristram signatures, fail to distinguish these links – cf.
[
38
,
39
]
.

The situation is different if one looks from the smooth perspective.
First, we recall that slice-torus link invariants are a special class of
\mathbb{R}
-valued concordance invariants of links, which vanish on unlinks – see
[
13
]
for the precise definition. Prominent examples of slice-torus link invariants are the Ozsváth-Szabó-Rasmussen
\tau
-invariant
[
11
,
33
,
36
]
, and (a re-scaling of) the Rasmussen
s
-invariant
[
5
,
35
]
. It is known that the slice-torus link invariants provide an obstruction to being concordant to a quasi-positive link; indeed, it follows from
[
13
, Theorem 3.2]
(see
[
10
,
27
]
, and references therein, for knots, and special slice-torus link invariants) that: if
L
is an
\ell
-component link concordant to a quasi-positive link, and
\nu
is a slice-torus invariant, then

(1.1)

2\nu(L)-\ell=-\chi_{4}(L),

where
\chi_{4}
is the (smooth)
slice Euler characteristic
, that is

\chi_{4}(L)=\max\{\chi(\Sigma)\,|\,\Sigma\subset\mathbb{D}^{4}\ \text{compact surface with no closed components},\,\partial\Sigma=\Sigma\cap\partial\mathbb{D}^{4}=L\}.

An immediate consequence of (
1.1
) is that there are links which are not concordant to any quasi-positive link, eg. the figure-eight knot
4_{1}
is such that
\nu(4_{1})=0
, and
-\chi_{4}(4_{1})=1

[
25
]
.

While there are some results for special classes of links including knots, to the best of the author’s knowledge (
1.1
) is the only known general obstruction to being concordant to a quasi-positive link.

Remark 1.2
.

To be fair, there is another general obstruction to being concordant to a quasi-positive link. This obstruction is stated in terms of the Hom-Wu invariant
\nu^{+}

[
12
,
23
]
– which is not a slice-torus link invariant. Concretely, it follows from
[
12
, Proposition 1.6]
(cf.
[
23
, Proposition 3]
for knots) that if
L
is concordant to a quasi-positive link, then

2\nu^{+}(L)-\ell=-\chi_{4}(L)

However, this obstruction is strictly weaker than Equation (
1.1
) specialised to the case
\nu=\tau

[
12
,
23
]
.

In this note we provide a new obstruction to being concordant to a quasi-positive link based on the slice-torus link invariants and linking numbers.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
L
be a link, and let
\nu
be a slice-torus link invariant. If
L
is concordant to a quasi-positive link, then

(1.2)

\nu(L)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu(L_{i})\leq\sum_{i<j}\ell k(L_{i},L_{j}),

for each partition of
L
into sub-links
L_{1},...,L_{k}
.

Note that Theorem
1.3
poses constraints on the linking matrix of a quasi-positive link in terms of its sub-links. This is slightly surprising for two reasons; first, as we mentioned above, all linking matrices can be realised by quasi-positive links. Second, all links arise as sub-links of a quasi-positive link by adding a single unknotted component. Since
we were unable to find a proof of this fact, which is well-known two experts, we included one in Appendix
A
.

Going back to our result, we note that the obstructions provided by Theorem
1.3
and by (
1.1
) are independent. First, we give an example where (
1.1
) provides a stronger obstruction than Theorem
1.3
.
Recall that
L
is the
split union
of
L_{1},...,L_{k}
, we write
L=L_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup L_{k}
, if
L=L_{1}\cup....\cup L_{k}
and the
L_{i}
’s are separated by embedded copies of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Example 1.4
.

Slice-torus link invariants are additive under split union
[

. Thus, the split union of a figure-eight knot and an unlink does not satisfy (

).

On the other hand, we use Theorem

to prove the following result.

We conclude the first part of the paper by studying the concordance self-linking number
sl_{c}
. We shall be more precise in Section
2
, but we briefly recall the definitions and some properties of
sl_{max}
and
sl_{c}
.
Each link can be represented non-uniquely as a closed braid
[
2
]
, and to each braid
\beta
is associated its self-linking number
sl(\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}
. However, this number depends on the braid used to represent the given link. To obtain a link invariant and a concordance invariant, respectively, one can define

sl_{max}(L)=\max\{sl(\beta)\,|\,\text{the closure of }\beta\text{ is }L\},

and

sl_{c}(L)=\max\{sl(\beta)\,|\,\text{the closure of }\beta\text{ is concordant to }L\}.

Rudolph’s
slice-Bennequin inequality

[
41
]
tells us that, for each braid
\beta
representing a link
L
, we have

sl(\beta)\leq-\chi_{4}(L).

It follows that both
sl_{max}
and
sl_{c}
are finite numbers.
Furthermore, the slice-Bennequin inequality is sharp for quasi-positive braids
[
41
, Section 3]
.
Hence, if
L
is either a quasi-positive link, or is concordant to a quasi-positive link, then
sl_{max}(L)=-\chi_{4}(L)
, or
sl_{c}(L)=-\chi_{4}(L)
, respectively.

The slice-Bennequin inequality can be refined to a
slice-torus Bennequin inequality
; that is, for each
\ell
-component link
L
, and each slice-torus link invariant
\nu
, we have

sl_{max}(L)\leq sl_{c}(L)\leq 2\nu(L)-\ell\leq-\chi_{4}(L).

The first inequality is trivial. The second inequality is well-known to experts, and can be inferred in this general setting from the combinatorial bound
[
13
, Theorem 1.4]
applied to the closure of a braid (see Corollary
2.5
). Finally, the third inequality follows from
[
13
, Proposition 2.11]
.
In particular, the equality
sl_{c}(L)=-\chi_{4}(L)
gives a strictly stronger obstruction to being concordant to a quasi-positive link than Equation (
1.1
) – cf. Remark
1.6
.

Unfortunately, the concordance self-linking number is incredibly difficult to compute even in simple cases. Which is the reason why, to the best of the author’s knowledge, this quantity has never been considered before. In this paper we shall compute
sl_{c}
for links which are closures of pure braids (Proposition
3.5
). Moreover, we completely characterise the closures of alternating pure braids which are concordant to a quasi-positive link (Proposition
3.6
).

Remark 1.6
.

Despite how difficult is to compute
sl_{c}
, sometimes it can be estimated and used effectively as an obstruction.
For instance,
[
15
, Proposition 1.6]
uses the maximal self-linking number
sl_{max}
to show that the link
L(k)
in Figure
1
is not quasi-positive. A similar reasoning, where
sl_{max}
is replaced by
sl_{c}
, can be used to (re-)prove Theorem
1.5
– see Proposition
3.2
and Example
3.3
.

Remark 1.7
.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
uses the equality
sl_{max}=2\nu-\ell
, rather than quasi-positivity itself. In particular, Theorem
1.3
still holds if we replace the hypothesis "
L
is concordant to a quasi-positive link" with “
L
such that
sl_{c}(L)=2\nu(L)-\ell
”. Thus the condition
sl_{c}(L)=-\chi_{4}(L)
provides a stronger, if less computable, obstruction than Theorem
1.3
.

1.2.
Obstructions to positivity conditions

In the second part of this paper we deal with equivalence rather than concordance. Our aim is to provide necessary conditions for a link to be quasi-positive, positive, or the closure of a positive braid, and investigate the consequences of these conditions. Our first result is the following necessary condition for certain links to be quasi-positive.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
L
be a quasi-positive link. If there exists a partition of
L
into sub-links
L_{1},...,L_{k}
such that

(1.3)

\nu(L)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu(L_{i})=\sum_{i<j}\ell k(L_{i},L_{j}),

then

2\nu(L_{i})-\ell_{i}=sl_{max}(L_{i}),\quad\text{for all }i\in\{1,...,k\}.

Exactly as in the case of Theorem
1.3
, see Remark
1.7
, the proof of Theorem
1.8
uses the condition
sl_{max}=2\nu-\ell
rather than quasi-positivity itself.
Therefore, Theorem
1.8
provides a weaker obstruction than the conditions
sl_{max}=2\nu-\ell
and
sl_{max}=-\chi_{4}
.
Nonetheless, Theorem
1.8
is independent from the condition
2\nu-\ell=-\chi_{4}
(Example
3.1
).

Now, we restrict our attention to positive links, which are special quasi-positive links
[
32
,
40
]
. More precisely, we use combinatorial link invariants to establish some conditions for a link to be positive.

Before stating our result, we need to establish some terminology. Recall that link is
split
if it is the split union of two links, and is
completely split
if it is the split union of knots.
The
splitting number

\wsp
of a link
L
is the minimum number of crossing changes needed to obtain a completely split link from
L
. The splitting number must not be confused with what we call the
strong splitting number

\ssp
, which has a similar definition but the only crossing changes allowed are those between distinct components. We remark that sometimes, in the literature, what we call strong splitting number is referred to as splitting number, and what we call splitting number is sometimes called weak splitting number. Finally,
unlinking number

u(L)
is the minimal number of crossing changes to obtain an unlink from
L
. The unlinking number of a knot is also called
unknotting number
.
Now, we are ready to state our result.

Theorem 1.9
.

If
L=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{\ell}
is a positive link, then we have

(1.4)

\ell k(L)=\wsp(L)=\ssp(L)=u(L)-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}u(K_{i}),

where
\ell k(L)
is
the total linking number
, that is the sum of
\ell k(K_{i},K_{j})
, for all
i<j
.

Before going to the applications of Theorem
1.9
, we pause to comment on our result. First, we compare our result with other known criteria: the non-negativity of the linking matrix, the positivity of sub-links, and the negativity of the signature
\sigma

[
16
,
34
,
45
]
.

Let us start by proving that Equation (
1.4
) implies the non-negativity of the linking matrix. Denote by
|\ell k|(L)
the sum of all the absolute values of the linking numbers between different components. Lemma
2.7
implies that

\ell k(L)\leq|\ell k|(L)\leq\ssp(L).

Therefore, if
\ell k(L)=\ssp(L)
then
|\ell k|(L)=\ell k(L)
. Hence all linking numbers must equal their absolute value, and thus they must be non-negative.
Moreover, it is also known that if the link is positive and is not completely split, then there must be at least a non-zero linking number. This is also implied by Equation (
1.4
); in fact, it follows directly from the definitions that a link is completely split if, and only if,
\wsp(L)=0
. Therefore, if a link is positive and is not completely split, then (
1.4
) implies
\ell k(L)=\wsp(L)>0
. So at least one among the linking numbers must be positive.
In the next proposition we show that Theorem
1.9
provides a strictly stronger obstruction than the linking matrix, and that it is independent from the positivity of sub-links, and the sign of the signature.

Proposition 1.10
.

There exists a family of
2
-component links
\{L^{\prime}(n)\}_{n\geq 2}
with positive components, negative signature, and positive linking matrix, such that
\wsp(L^{\prime}(n))\neq\ssp(L^{\prime}(n))
for all
n
.
Furthermore, there are infinitely many
2
-component links with positive signature, and a negative component, which satisfy (
1.4
).

Finally, we test the efficiency of Theorem
1.9
on prime links with less than
8
crossings. The result is contained in Table

. In this table, which is of independent interest, we listed all prime links with less than
8
crossings, their positivity, their braid-positivity, and whether or not our criteria (ie. Theorems
1.9
and
1.13
) can be used to effectively obstruct these properties.

Now, let us turn to the applications of Theorem
1.9
. Nakamura in
[
32
, Theorem 5.1]
proved that if
K
is a positive knot, then
u(K)=1
if, and only if,
K
is a (non-trivial) twist knot. As an application of Theorem
1.9
, we extend Nakamura’s result to multi-component links.

Proposition 1.11
.

Let
L
be a positive link. Then,
u(L)=1
if, and only if,
L
is the split union of a (possibly empty) unlink with either the positive Hopf link, or a (non-trivial) twist knot.

Moreover, we use Theorem
1.9
to obtain a characterisation of all links with unknotting number
2
.

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
L
be a positive link. Then,
u(L)=2
if, and only if,
L
is the split union of a (possibly empty) unlink and one of the following

(1)

a positive link with total linking number
2
and unknotted components;

(2)

a connected sum of a positive Hopf link and a positive twist knot;

(3)

the split union of a positive Hopf link and a positive twist knot;

(4)

the split union of two non-trivial positive twist knots;

(5)

a positive knot with unknotting number
2
.

Furthermore, each of the above families contains infinitely-many links.

Finally, we focus on links which are obtained as the closure of positive braids, which we call
positive-braid links
. In this case we can strengthen the statement of Theorem
1.9
.

Theorem 1.13
.

If
L=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{\ell}
is a positive-braid link, then

\nu(L)-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\nu(K_{i})=\ell k(L)=\wsp(L)=\ssp(L),

for any slice-torus link invariant
\nu
. Furthermore, given any partition of
L
into sub-links, say
L_{1},...,L_{k}
, we have

\nu(L)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu(L_{i})=u(L)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}u(L_{i})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\ell k(L_{i},L_{j}).

As consequences of Theorem
1.13
we obtain two results; the first result is a formula for the unlinking number of positive links with positive-braid components (Proposition

Let

Background material and notation

The main scope of this section is to fix the notation and review some results needed in the proofs of the main theorems. The section is organised as follows; we start with some facts concerning braids. Then, we pass to the definition of self-linking number, and the description of some of its properties. 2.1.
Braids

The
braid group
on
n

B_{n}
, is the group generated by
\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n-1}
(called
Artin generators
), and subject to the relations

\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i},\ \text{if }|i-j|>1,\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1},\ \forall i,j.

Alternatively, one can define the braid group diagrammatically, the reader can consult
[
19
, Chapter 10]
for details, and refer to Figure
2
for a schematic description.
A
braid
is a word in the Artin generators and their inverses. In the rest of the paper no difference shall be made between the algebraic and the diagrammatic interpretations of the braid group.

By joining the start and end points of each strand of a braid, as illustrated on the right side of Figure

[

Definition 2.1

A braid is
quasi-positive
if it is the product of conjugates of Artin generators, and it is
positive
if it is the product of Artin generators.
A link is called
quasi-positive
(resp.

Lemma 2.2
.

A braid is non-split and alternating if, and only if, it satisfies following properties

(i)

all
\sigma_{i}
appear (possibly with negative exponents),

(ii)

each
\sigma_{i}
appears with exponents of a fixed sign (that is all positive or all negative),

(iii)

\sigma_{i}

always appear with exponents of opposite signs.

Proof.

It is a straightforward verification that a braid satisfying (ii) and (iii) is alternating, and that it is not alternating otherwise. It is also immediate that if a braid does not satisfy (i) then it is split, since there are no crossing between a pair of adjacent strands. The only non-trivial point is that if a braid is alternating and satisfies (i) then it is non-split – which follows from
[

2.4.
Unlinking and splitting numbers

In this subsection we collected two basic facts concerning unlinking and splitting numbers. Let us by recalling some basic terminology. If a crossing change features a single component it is called
self-crossing change
, otherwise it is called
mixed crossing change
.
A
splitting sequence
(resp.
unlinking sequence
) for a link
L
is a finite sequence of links
L=L^{(0)},L^{\prime},...,L^{(k)}
such that:
L^{(i)}
is obtained from
L^{(i+1)}
via a single crossing change, and
L^{(k)}
is the split union of knots (resp. an unlink). A splitting sequence featuring only mixed crossing changes is called
strong splitting sequence
. The unlinking, splitting, and strong splitting numbers are the minimal lengths of the eponymous sequences.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
14
, Lemma
2.1
]
)
.

Let
L
be a link. If there exists a splitting sequence with
m
mixed crossing changes, then

|\ell k|(L)\leq m,

where
|\ell k|
denotes the
absolute linking number
, that is the sum of the absolute value of the linking numbers between the components of
L
– not to be confused with
|\ell k(L)|
.

If the absolute linking number equals the strong splitting number, one can say something about the unlinking number, and splitting number
\wsp
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
L=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{\ell}
be a link. If
\ssp(L)=|\ell k|(L)
, then

(i)

\wsp(L)=|\ell k|(L)
;

(ii)

u(L)-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}u(K_{i})=|\ell k|(L)
.

Proof.

The equality in (i) has been proved in
[
14
, Remark 2.3]
. Consider a minimal unlinking sequence for
L
with
m
mixed crossing changes and
s
self crossing changes. Since an unlinking sequence is also a splitting sequence, Lemma
2.7
implies
|\ell k|(L)\leq m
. Note that mixed crossing change do not change the isotopy class of the components. Thus, the sum of the unknotting numbers of the
K_{i}
is less than, or equal to,
s
. It follows that

|\ell k|(L)+\sum_{i}u(K_{i})\leq m+s=u(L).

The opposite inequality, and hence the equality, follows from a simple observation; a particular unlinking sequence is given by a minimal strong splitting sequence followed by a minimal unknotting sequence for each component. ∎

which are twist knots (case d.), or there is a single

What is left to show is that there are infinitely many knot in each case. It is known that there are infinitely-many twist knots – eg.

9

, hence it is sufficient to show that the following families are infinite;

(1)

positive links with linking number two and trivial components;

(2)

positive knots with unknotting number

.

On the right of Figure

. Note that
\ell k(L^{\prime\prime}(k))=2

are unknotted. It is known that two alternating, non-split, and reduced diagrams of the same link must have the same number of crossings

]

are alternating, non-split, and reduced, we have

. Hence family (i) is infinite.

Now, we shall argue that
u(K\#K^{\prime})=2
when

and
K^{\prime}
are positive twist knots.
Then, family (ii) must be infinite since there are infinitely-many twist knots. The unlinking number is sub-additive, thus
u(K\#K^{\prime})\leq u(K)+u(K^{\prime})=2
. The sum of two positive knots is a positive knot. Hence

if, and only if,

is a twist knot by

. However, twist knots are prime, and therefore it must be

.
∎

Appendix A
Any link is a sub-link of a quasi-positive link

In this short appendix we prove that any link arises as sub-link of a quasi-positive link.

Theorem A.1
.

Any link
L
is a sub-link of a quasi-positive link
L^{\prime}
. Furthermore,
L^{\prime}
can be obtained from
L
by adding a single unknotted component.

Proof.

Let
\beta=\sigma_{i_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdot\cdots\cdot\sigma_{i_{k}}^{\varepsilon_{k}}\in B_{n}
be a braid representative for
L
, where
\varepsilon_{1},...,\varepsilon_{k}\in\{\pm 1\}
.
Our aim is to define a new braid
\beta^{\prime}\in B_{n+1}
which is quasi-positive, and whose closure contains
L
as a sub-link.
First, define the following braid

w_{r}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{n+1}\cdot...\cdot\sigma_{i_{r}}\cdot\sigma_{i_{r}}\cdot...\cdot\sigma_{n+1}&\text{if }\varepsilon_{r}=-1\text{}\\
1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad\text{for each $r\in\{1,...,k\}$ }

Notice that the permutation associated to each
w_{r}
is trivial.
Now, define

\beta^{\prime}=(\sigma_{i_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}w_{1})\cdot\cdots\cdot(\sigma_{i_{k}}^{\varepsilon_{k}}w_{k})\in B_{n+1}.

Clearly, the permutation associated to
\beta^{\prime}
fixes
n+1
, and behaves as the permutation associated to
\beta
on
\{1,...,n\}
. This fact implies that the closure of
\beta^{\prime}
has an unknotted component
U
which starts and ends on the
(n+1)
-th strand.
The braid
\beta^{\prime}
is quasi-positive since it is a product of quasi-positive braids. Indeed, we have

\sigma_{i_{r}}^{\varepsilon_{i_{r}}}w_{r}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{i_{r}}^{-1}(\sigma_{n+1}...\sigma_{i_{r}+1})\sigma_{i_{r}}(\sigma_{i_{r}}...\sigma_{n+1})&\text{if }\varepsilon_{r}=-1\text{,}\\
\sigma_{i_{r}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Finally, we have to show that the link
L
is a sub-link of the closure
L^{\prime}
of
\beta^{\prime}
. We remark that the unknotted component
U
has no self-crossings in
\beta^{\prime}
, and the
w_{r}
are precisely the crossings between
U
and that the rest of the components. Therefore, the braid associated to
L^{\prime}\setminus U
is obtained removing all the
w_{r}
from
\beta^{\prime}
thus re-obtaining
\beta
.
∎
