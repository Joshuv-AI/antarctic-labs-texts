Localizing Unsynchronized Sensors with Unknown Sources
Dalia El Badawy, Viktor Larsson, Marc Pollefeys, Ivan Dokmanić

Abstract
We propose a method for sensor array self-localization using a set of sources at unknown locations. The sources produce signals whose times of arrival are registered at the sensors. We look at the general case where neither the emission times of the sources nor the reference time frames of the receivers are known. Unlike previous work, our method directly recovers the array geometry, instead of first estimating the timing information. The key component is a new loss function which is insensitive to the unknown timings. We cast the problem as a minimization of a non-convex functional of the Euclidean distance matrix of microphones and sources subject to certain non-convex constraints. After convexification, we obtain a semidefinite relaxation which gives an approximate solution; subsequent refinement on the proposed loss via the Levenberg-Marquardt scheme gives the final locations. Our method achieves state-of-the-art performance in terms of reconstruction accuracy, speed, and the ability to work with a small number of sources and receivers. It can also handle missing measurements and exploit prior geometric and temporal knowledge, for example if either the receiver offsets or the emission times are known, or if the array contains compact subarrays with known geometry.

I-C

Contributions

Our work is motivated by the fact that the two-stage procedure is suboptimal. The (valid) reason to adopt sequential estimation are poor local minima in the loss function which involves both the times and the positions. From a statistical point of view, however, joint estimation is preferred. Further, timing estimation can fail or require many random restarts; the same holds for the non-convex optimization to recover the relative configurations from
\bar{\bm{\mathit{R}}}^{T}\bar{\bm{\mathit{S}}}
. The two-stage procedure also makes it challenging to exploit prior geometric information such us known distances or distance bounds.

In light of related work and the above discussions, we summarize our contributions as follows:

•

What we are usually interested in are the sensor positions. We thus formulate a new self-localization loss which is invariant to offsets and delays. The proposed loss uses non-squared distances as opposed to the usual squared; we prove that minimizing this objective yields the maximum likelihood estimate of the positions under Gaussian noise. It enables us to recover the geometry without worrying about the times and without random restarts.

•

We formulate a non-convex semidefinite optimization problem in terms of this new loss. We work with the full point set
\bm{\mathit{X}}=[\bm{\mathit{R}};\bm{\mathit{S}}]
and the corresponding Gram matrix. The Gram matrix and its constraints intrinsically
glue
together the sources and the receivers and thus obviate the need for the non-convex optimization step of Crocco et al.
[
22
]
(see Section
III-A
);

•

Although our method does not require synchronization, it can leverage synchronization among the receivers or the sources if it is fully or partially present
[
39
,
47
,
48
]
. It can further leverage geometric side information such as when the receivers contain subarrays with known relative geometry or some sources and receivers are collocated. Finally, it can handle missing measurements.

The proposed method achieves state-of-the art results, not only in terms of localization accuracy, runtime, or exploiting side information, but also in the ability to solve near-minimal problems with few nodes. Finally, we note that as suggested by Ono
[
30
]
, our method can also be interpreted as a virtual synchronization method.

II
Problem Statement

We wish to localize a set of
M
receivers with unknown positions
\bm{\mathit{r}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathit{r}}_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
using a set of
K
sources with unknown positions
\bm{\mathit{s}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathit{s}}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume that the sources emit pulses whose times of arrival (TOA) can be measured at the receivers.
3
3

3

Note that these measurements can be obtained without physically emitting pulses. In audio, for example, one often emits long chirps which is followed by pulse compression.

Since we do not assume synchronization between the sources and the receivers, the absolute emission time
\tau_{k}
of the
k
th source is unknown. Since the sources are not necessarily synchronized among themselves, the differences
\tau_{k}-\tau_{k^{\prime}}
are also unknown. Similarly, since we do not assume synchronization among receivers (nor knowing their internal delays), the temporal frames of reference of each receiver are shifted by an unknown
\sigma_{m}
with respect to some reference clock. The times of arrival of the
k
th source signal at the
m
th receiver thus become

t_{mk}=v^{-1}\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m}-\bm{\mathit{s}}_{k}\right\rVert+\sigma_{m}+\tau_{k},

(1)

where for simplicity we let both
\sigma_{m}
and
\tau_{k}
have arbitrary sign. We assume the transmission speed
v
to be known and w.l.o.g. let
v=1
for the remainder of the paper.

Note that if we instead use the time-difference-of-arrival (TDOA), then with the first sensor as the reference, the TDOA is

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{mk}

\displaystyle=t_{mk}-t_{1k}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m}-\bm{\mathit{s}}_{k}\|+(\sigma_{m}-\sigma_{1})-\|\bm{\mathit{r}}_{1}-\bm{\mathit{s}}_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m}-\bm{\mathit{s}}_{k}\|+\tilde{\sigma}_{m}+\tilde{\tau}_{k}.

And so we can still think of the TDOA
\tilde{t}_{mk}
as a TOA but with modified emission times and offsets.

II-A

Minimal Number of Sources and Receivers

Common sensor localization scenarios are either in the horizontal plane
(d=2)
or in 3D space (
d=3
). The number of the degrees of freedom in the positions of sources and receivers is then
d(M+K)
. However, since rigid motions of the entire setup cannot be distinguished from TOA data, we can choose the associated
d(d+1)/2
degrees of freedom freely (
d
translational and
\binom{d}{2}
rotational). Each source and receiver come with an additional unknown time, which gives another
M+K
degrees of freedom. However, we can choose a global time offset arbitrarily, which is another gauge of invariance and subtracts one degree of freedom. The total number of the degrees of freedom is thus

\#(\text{DOF})=(d+1)(M+K-\tfrac{d}{2})-1

As measurements we get
MK
TOAs. In order to get a dimension-zero solution set (a solution set that is a finite or countable set of points, but not necessarily unique), this number should at least match the number of the degrees of freedom. Solving for the minimal number of sources as a function of the number of receivers, we obtain the following relation

K\geq\frac{(d+1)M-d(d+1)/2-1}{M-(d+1)}.

For
d=3
this gives
K\geq(4M-7)/(M-4)
implying that the smallest number of receivers that can be localized is
M=5
for which at least
K=13
sources are required to get a dimension-zero solution. Some other minimal cases are:
(M=7,K=7),(M=13,K=5)
.

As we demonstrate with real and numerical experiments in Section
VI
, our algorithms can operate in the vicinity of this minimal regime, where previous methods either fail or perform poorly.

III
A Loss Insensitive to Offsets and Delays

Instead of proceeding sequentially by first estimating the unknown times as in the case of the state-of-the-art methods
[
39
,
16
]
, we note that the primary task is usually position estimation rather than timing estimation. Besides, when the positions are known, estimating the timings
\left\{\sigma_{m}\right\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\left\{\tau_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
boils down to a simple algebraic exercise (see Section
III-D
). We thus devise a data fidelity metric which is insensitive to the unknown timings, yet is only minimized with the correct positions.

We begin by writing (
1
) in matrix form as

\bm{\mathit{T}}=\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}+\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T}+\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\tau}}^{T},

(2)

where
\bm{\mathit{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times K}
,
\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}=(\delta_{mk})
and
\delta_{mk}=\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m}-\bm{\mathit{s}}_{k}\right\rVert
,
\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}=[\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{M}]^{T}
and
\bm{\mathit{\tau}}=[\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{K}]^{T}
. Now we make the key observation: multiplying (
2
) on both sides by a matrix which has an all-ones vector in the nullspace will annihilate the two terms that depend on the unknown times.

Given an integer
L
, let
\bm{\mathit{J}}_{L}
be a geometric centering matrix of size
L
,
4
4

4

We often indicate matrix and vector sizes in subscripts. While making the notation a bit clunkier, it helps keep track of dimensions.

\bm{\mathit{J}}_{L}:=\bm{\mathit{I}}_{L}-\tfrac{1}{L}\mathbf{1}_{L}\mathbf{1}_{L}^{T}.

(3)

It is easy to verify that
\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}=\bm{\mathit{0}}_{M}
and
\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}=\bm{\mathit{0}}_{K}^{T}
so that the matrix

\bm{\mathit{P}}=\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{T}}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}=\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}

Proposition 1
.

We have
\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{T}}_{1}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}=\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{T}}_{2}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}
if and only if there exist
\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}^{\prime}
and
\bm{\mathit{\tau}}^{\prime}
such that
\bm{\mathit{T}}_{1}=\bm{\mathit{T}}_{2}+\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}^{\prime}\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T}+\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\tau}}^{\prime T}
. In particular, for
\bm{\mathit{T}}
as in (
2
), we have
\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{T}}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}=\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}
(
4
).

Remark:
If
\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}
happened to be a Euclidean (squared) distance matrix (EDM) of a point set
\bm{\mathit{X}}
, for example
\bm{\mathit{D}}=(d_{ij}^{2})
,
d_{ij}=\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{x}}_{i}-\bm{\mathit{x}}_{j}\right\rVert
, then
-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{N}\bm{\mathit{D}}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{N}
would equal the Gram matrix of the (centered)
\bm{\mathit{X}}
. No similar statement can be made here simply because
\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}
holds non-squared distances, and because it only corresponds to one off-diagonal block of
\bm{\mathit{D}}
.

Proof.

The nullspace of the linear operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}:\mathbb{R}^{M\times K}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{M\times K}

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{A}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{J}}}{{\mapsto}}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{A}}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}

is spanned by

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{J})=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{K}^{T},\bm{\mathit{e}}_{1}\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T},\ldots\bm{\mathit{e}}_{M}\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T}\}.

Note that not all of those

correspond to adding a constant offset to either a source or a receiver. As a consequence, any nullspace vector can be written as a linear combination of source / receiver offsets which proves the proposition.
∎

1

\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}
is sufficient to determine the positions in the sense that the only locations such that this transformation matches the measurements are the true ones (if the original problem is solvable).

We can then propose the following timing-invariant objective for localization

\underset{\bm{\mathit{R}},\bm{\mathit{S}}}{\textrm{min}}\;\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}(\bm{\mathit{R}},\bm{\mathit{S}})-\bm{\mathit{T}})\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}\right\rVert_{F}^{2},\\

(5)

where

denotes the Frobenius norm,
\bm{\mathit{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
are the receiver positions, and
\bm{\mathit{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
are the source positions. This objective is justified by the following direct corollary of Proposition
1
.

Corollary 1
.

If the unsynchronized localization problem has a unique solution (up to a rigid transformation), then vanishing loss in (
5
) implies correct localization.

Further, the loss (
5
) is in fact equivalent to the ML loss
given the offsets

\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}
and
\bm{\mathit{\tau}}
. Namely, assuming i.i.d. Gaussian noise on the TOA measurements we have

f_{ML}(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}},\bm{\mathit{\sigma}},\bm{\mathit{\tau}})=\|\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}+\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T}+\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\tau}}^{T}-\bm{\mathit{T}}\|_{F}^{2},

and the loss in (
5

f(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}})=\min_{\bm{\mathit{\sigma}},\bm{\mathit{\tau}}}f_{ML}(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}},\bm{\mathit{\sigma}},\bm{\mathit{\tau}}).

To see this note that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}}f_{ML}=2(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}+\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T}+\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\tau}}^{T}-\bm{\mathit{T}})\mathbf{1}_{K}=0

(6)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}^{\star}=-\frac{1}{\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{K}}(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}+\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\tau}}^{T}-\bm{\mathit{T}})\mathbf{1}_{K}.

(7)

Inserting into the original cost we get

f_{ML}(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}},\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}^{\star},\bm{\mathit{\tau}})=\|(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}+\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\tau}}^{T}-T)\underbrace{(I_{K}-\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1}_{K}\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T})}_{J_{K}}\|_{F}^{2}.

Solving for

\bm{\mathit{Q}}
we have that
\bm{\mathit{H}}=\widehat{\bm{\mathit{R}}}^{T}\widehat{\bm{\mathit{S}}}
, with
\widehat{\bm{\mathit{R}}}=\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{R}}
,
\widehat{\bm{\mathit{S}}}=\bm{\mathit{Q}}^{-1}\bm{\mathit{S}}
. Finding the right
\bm{\mathit{Q}}
(which can be assumed to be upper-triangular which fixes the rotational gauge freedom) and the (one) translation vector is then achieved via direct non-convex optimization, though over a lower dimensional space than the original problem.

We sidestep this problem of having to determine
\bm{\mathit{Q}}
in order to
stitch
together
\bm{\mathit{R}}
and
\bm{\mathit{S}}
by writing the measurements in terms of the full point set

\bm{\mathit{X}}:=[\bm{\mathit{R}},\ \bm{\mathit{S}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N},

where
N=M+K
, and always working with the entire
\bm{\mathit{X}}
. We denote the corresponding full Gram matrix by
\bm{\mathit{G}}=\bm{\mathit{X}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
. The off-diagonal blocks of the Gram matrix
\bm{\mathit{G}}
encode the relative geometry between
\bm{\mathit{R}}
and
\bm{\mathit{S}}
—by recovering
\bm{\mathit{G}}
, we know that relative geometry. Note that this only resolves the relative ambiguity between
\bm{\mathit{R}}
and
\bm{\mathit{S}}
which is
an artifact of the previous methods. The inherent invariance of localization from distances to Euclidean motions remains.

The matrix of squared distances (the EDM) between the column vectors in
\bm{\mathit{X}}
can be written as a linear function of the Gram matrix. Letting
\bm{\mathit{D}}=(\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{x}}_{n}-\bm{\mathit{x}}_{n^{\prime}}\right\rVert^{2})_{n,n^{\prime}=1}^{N}
, one has

\bm{\mathit{D}}=\mathcal{D}(\bm{\mathit{G}}):=\mathop{\mathsf{diag}}(\bm{\mathit{G}})\mathbf{1}_{N}^{T}-2\bm{\mathit{G}}+\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathop{\mathsf{diag}}(\bm{\mathit{G}}),

(8)

with
\mathop{\mathsf{diag}}(\bm{\mathit{G}})=[g_{11},\ldots,g_{NN}]^{T}
. This means that the matrix of distances
\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}
between sources and receivers can be written as an entrywise square root of a linear function of
\bm{\mathit{G}}
, since

\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}^{\bullet 2}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathit{G}})\overset{\text{def}}{=}\bm{\mathit{S}}_{\text{row}}\,\mathcal{D}(\bm{\mathit{G}})\,\bm{\mathit{S}}_{\text{col}},

(9)

where
\bm{\mathit{S}}_{\text{row}}:=[\bm{\mathit{I}}_{M},\ \bm{\mathit{0}}_{M\times K}]
and
\bm{\mathit{S}}_{\text{col}}:=[\bm{\mathit{0}}_{K\times M},\ \bm{\mathit{I}}_{K}]^{T}
are the appropriate row and column selection matrices, and
(\,\cdot\,)^{\bullet 2}
denotes an entrywise square. In view of Proposition
1
, this leads us to the first reformulation of the original problem:

\displaystyle\!\min_{\bm{\mathit{G}}}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}(\sqrt[\bullet]{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathit{G}})}-\bm{\mathit{T}})\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

(10a)

subject to

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{G}}\succeq 0,

(10b)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{G}}\mathbf{1}_{N}=\bm{\mathit{0}},

(10c)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}(\bm{\mathit{G}})=d.

(10d)

The constraint (
10c
) resolves the translation ambiguity; it is equivalent to the point set being centered around the origin
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\bm{\mathit{x}}_{n}=0
.

Note that now the receivers and sources are being translated together, unlike in earlier work where they were split. Further, the estimated locations are directly read out from a factorization of
\bm{\mathit{G}}
, without the need for stitching.

III-B

Semidefinite Relaxation

The formulation (
10a
) remains nonconvex. Thus, direct approaches such as randomly-initialized first-order methods (e.g., projected gradient descent) are likely to get stuck in a local minimum. We thus propose to instead solve a convex relaxation of the problem.

There are two sources of nonconvexity: the entrywise square root in the objective, and the rank constraint on the Gram matrix. We begin by replacing the entrywise square root
\sqrt[\bullet]{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathit{G}})}
by a new matrix variable
\bm{\mathit{B}}
, and adding the constraint that the entrywise square of
\bm{\mathit{B}}
must equal
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathit{G}})
.

Concretely, we write the objective as

\min_{\bm{\mathit{G}},\bm{\mathit{B}}}\ \left\lVert\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}(\bm{\mathit{B}}-\bm{\mathit{T}})\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}\right\rVert_{F}^{2},

and ask that
\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\bullet 2}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathit{G}})
to obtain an equivalent formulation. We now proceed by reformulating the added constraint as a rank constraint on some semidefinite matrix (or matrices). Since the entrywise square does not mix the entries of
\bm{\mathit{B}}
, we can add such constraints on a per-entry basis. This is computationally more efficient (and empirically works better) than working with the entire vectorization of
\bm{\mathit{B}}
at once.

We can equivalently write the constraint
b_{mk}^{2}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathit{G}})_{mn}
as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{L}}_{mk}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathit{G}})_{mk}&b_{mk}\\
b_{mk}&1\end{bmatrix}\succeq\bm{\mathit{0}}

(11)

\displaystyle b_{mk}\geq 0

(12)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}(\bm{\mathit{L}}_{mk})=1.

(13)

All the non-convexity is now lumped into the rank constraints,
\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}(\bm{\mathit{G}})=d
and
\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}(\bm{\mathit{L}}_{mk})=1
for
1\leq m,k\leq M,K
. The final step is to relax all of the rank constraints to get

\displaystyle\!\min_{\bm{\mathit{G}},\bm{\mathit{B}}}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}(\bm{\mathit{B}}-\bm{\mathit{T}})\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

(14a)

subject to

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{G}}\succeq 0,

(14b)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{G}}\mathbf{1}_{N}=\bm{\mathit{0}},

(14c)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{L}}_{mk}\succeq\bm{\mathit{0}},~\text{for all}~1\leq m,n\leq M,K,

(14d)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{B}}\geq 0.

(14e)

We can finally reconstruct the point set from the recovered
\bm{\mathit{G}}
using singular value decomposition. We have
\bm{\mathit{G}}=\bm{\mathit{U}}\Lambda\bm{\mathit{V}}^{\top}
, where
\Lambda=\mathop{\mathsf{diag}}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{N})
with the singular values
\sigma_{i}
assumed to be sorted in decreasing order. We reconstruct the point set using
\hat{\bm{\mathit{X}}}=[\mathop{\mathsf{diag}}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d}),\bm{\mathit{0}}_{d\times(N-d)}]\bm{\mathit{V}}^{\top}
. If the rank of the estimated
\bm{\mathit{G}}
is truly
d
, as it ideally should be since it describes a
d
-dimensional point set, then the trailing singular values would be zero anyway.

III-C

Refinement by Levenberg-Marquardt

In general, solving the above semidefinite program gives point configurations which are good coarse approximations to the true geometry, although they are not good enough for most applications. The reason can be tracked down to the fact that after relaxing the rank constraints, the estimated matrices have higher rank than desired. Still, the positive semidefinite constraints constrain geometry sufficiently to get decent estimates in many situations. One strategy would be to employ various rank minimization strategies. Informally, we tried this and it works decently but it is very slow.

Instead, we propose to refine the output of the SDR using the Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) algorithm. It is fast, accurate, and it works better. We have also empirically observed that simple gradient descent is
much
slower requiring more iterations to converge.

To derive the LM updates, we need to compute the Jacobian of the loss in order to linearize it. The loss

\mathcal{J}(\bm{\mathit{R}},\bm{\mathit{S}})=\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}(\bm{\mathit{R}},\bm{\mathit{S}})-\bm{\mathit{T}})\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}\right\rVert_{F}^{2},

can be written as

\mathcal{J}(\bm{\mathit{R}},\bm{\mathit{S}})=\left\lVert f(\bm{\mathit{R}},\bm{\mathit{S}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

where
f(\bm{\mathit{R}},\bm{\mathit{S}})=\bm{\mathit{A}}\bm{\mathit{\delta}}(\bm{\mathit{R}},\bm{\mathit{S}})-\bm{\mathit{t}}
,

\bm{\mathit{A}}=\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}^{T}\otimes\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M},\quad\bm{\mathit{t}}=\bm{\mathit{A}}\,\mathsf{vec}(\bm{\mathit{T}}),

and
\bm{\mathit{\delta}}=\mathsf{vec}(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}})
with
\otimes
denoting the Kronecker product.
Since
\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times(M+K)}\to\mathbb{R}^{M\times K}
, the Jacobian is a tensor in
\mathbb{R}^{M\times K\times d\times(M+K)}
. We can compute

[\mathsf{D}\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}]_{m,k,:,\ell}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m}-\bm{\mathit{s}}_{k}}{\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m}-\bm{\mathit{s}}_{k}\right\rVert}&\ell=m,\\
-\dfrac{\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m}-\bm{\mathit{s}}_{k}}{\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m}-\bm{\mathit{s}}_{k}\right\rVert}&\ell=M+k,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

with the understanding that the first
M
slices in the last index of
\mathsf{D}
correspond to the receivers and the last
K
to the sources. Note that the Jacobian is not well defined at
\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m}=\bm{\mathit{s}}_{k}
. In that case, it would be possible to remove the corresponding variables from the optimization and adjust the Jacobian accordingly.

To make things easier to compute with, we rewrite
\bm{\mathit{T}}
as a function of a single vector

\bm{\mathit{x}}=\bm{\mathit{x}}(\bm{\mathit{R}},\bm{\mathit{S}})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{vec}(\bm{\mathit{R}})\\
\mathsf{vec}(\bm{\mathit{S}})\end{bmatrix},

and rearrange the output into a vector so that
\bm{\mathit{\delta}}(\bm{\mathit{x}})=\mathsf{vec}(\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}(\bm{\mathit{R}},\bm{\mathit{S}}))
. The Jacobian
\mathsf{D}\bm{\mathit{\delta}}
is obtained from
\mathsf{D}\bm{\mathit{T}}
by collapsing the first two dimensions and the last two dimensions so that
\mathsf{D}\bm{\mathit{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{MK\times d(M+K)}
. Finally the Jacobian of
f
is computed as

\mathsf{D}f=\bm{\mathit{A}}\ \mathsf{D}\bm{\mathit{\delta}},

and we have an affine approximation of
f
as (with some abuse of notation)

f(\bm{\mathit{x}};\bm{\mathit{x}}_{0})=f(\bm{\mathit{x}}_{0})+\mathsf{D}f(\bm{\mathit{x}})(\bm{\mathit{x}}-\bm{\mathit{x}}_{0}).

This leads to the LM update,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{x}}^{(k+1)}=\argmin_{\bm{\mathit{z}}}\ \left\lVert f(\bm{\mathit{z}};\bm{\mathit{x}}^{(k)})\right\rVert^{2}+\lambda\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{z}}-\bm{\mathit{x}}^{(k)}\right\rVert^{2}

which is solved by

\bm{\mathit{x}}^{(k+1)}=\bm{\mathit{x}}^{(k)}-(\mathsf{D}f(\bm{\mathit{x}}^{k})^{T}\mathsf{D}f(\bm{\mathit{x}}^{k})+\lambda\bm{\mathit{I}})^{-1}\mathsf{D}f(\bm{\mathit{x}}^{k})^{T}f(\bm{\mathit{x}}^{(k)}).

The full localization algorithm is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

and

using SDR (

[

]
for the optimization problem (

). It has a
O(\sqrt{n}\log{\frac{1}{\epsilon}})

is the number of variables. The LM refinement is

per iteration.

III-D

Estimating Offsets and Delays

Once an estimate
\widehat{\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}}
of
\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}
is computed, we can also compute the unknown offsets
\bm{\mathit{\tau}}
and delays
\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}
. We start by noting from (
2
) that

\bm{\mathit{T}}-\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}=\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T}+\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\tau}}^{T},

where the right hand side is linear in
(\bm{\mathit{\sigma}},\bm{\mathit{\tau}})
. We exploit it by vectorizing both sides:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathit{e}}:=\mathsf{vec}(\bm{\mathit{T}}-\bm{\mathit{\Delta}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{K}\otimes\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}+\tau\otimes\mathbf{1}_{M}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{V}}\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}+\bm{\mathit{W}}\bm{\mathit{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathit{V}}&\bm{\mathit{W}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}\\
\bm{\mathit{\tau}}\end{bmatrix},

with
\bm{\mathit{V}}:=\mathbf{1}_{K}\otimes\bm{\mathit{I}}_{M}
,
\bm{\mathit{W}}:=\bm{\mathit{I}}_{K}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{M}
. From here, estimating the times vector amounts to solving an overdetermined system of linear equations. Note that the system matrix has a nullspace of dimension one (spanned by
\alpha\mathbf{1}
) due to the global offset ambiguity. To eliminate the offset ambiguity we arbitrarily set
\sigma_{1}=0
, and let
\bm{\mathit{V}}^{\prime}
be
\bm{\mathit{V}}
with the first column removed. The least-squares estimate of the unknown timings is then given as

\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}}^{\prime}\\
\widehat{\bm{\mathit{\tau}}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathit{V}}^{\prime}&\bm{\mathit{W}}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{e}},

(15)

with
\widehat{\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}}^{\prime}=[\widehat{\sigma}_{2},\ldots,\widehat{\sigma}_{M}]^{T}
and
(\,\cdot\,)^{\dagger}
denoting the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.

IV
Variants

In many cases, we have some prior information about the distances or offset times. The proposed method makes it straightforward to leverage this prior knowledge. In the following, we explain how by describing several typical scenarios.

IV-A

Localization of Subarrays

Suppose that some distances are known. It is often the case that the set of receivers consists of several compact subarrays with known geometry (voice-based assistants, smart phones). These compact subarrays are then distributed in the space of interest at unknown positions and with random orientations, together with discrete microphones.

Since the EDM is a linear function of the Gram matrix, this prior knowledge corresponds to simple linear constraints on
\bm{\mathit{G}}
. We thus augment the original program with a set of
known
inter-sensor distances for microphone pairs

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\big\{

\displaystyle(m_{1},m_{2})\ :\ m_{1}<m_{2},

\displaystyle\text{distance}~d_{m_{1}m_{2}}~\text{between}~\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m_{1}}~\text{and}~\bm{\mathit{r}}_{m_{2}}~\text{is known}\big\},

Then we add the following set of constraints to our optimization:

\mathcal{D}(\bm{\mathit{G}})_{m_{1}m_{2}}=d_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{2},\ \text{for all}\ (m_{1},m_{2})\in\mathcal{M},m_{1}\neq m_{2}.

(16)

If the set of receivers is partitioned as

\bm{\mathit{R}}=[\bm{\mathit{R}}_{1},\bm{\mathit{R}}_{2},\ldots,\bm{\mathit{R}}_{J}],

with the distances within the
j
th subarray
\bm{\mathit{R}}_{j}
known, these constraints correspond to knowing the diagonal subblocks of the upper-left block of
\mathcal{D}
.

We can again proceed with an LM refinement step. In this case, we consider the following objective

\displaystyle\!\min_{\theta}

\displaystyle\left\lVert f(\theta)\right\rVert^{2}

(17a)

subject to

\displaystyle g(\theta)=0,

(17b)

where
g:\mathbb{R}^{d(M+K)}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{M}|}
is the known-distances residual defined as

g_{m_{1}m_{2}}(\bm{\mathit{X}})=\textrm{edm}(\bm{\mathit{X}},\bm{\mathit{X}})_{m_{1}m_{2}}-d_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{2}

with
\textrm{edm}(\bm{\mathit{X}},\bm{\mathit{X}})_{m_{1}m_{2}}=\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{x}}_{m_{1}}-\bm{\mathit{x}}_{m_{2}}\right\rVert^{2}
being the entries of the EDM of the point set
\bm{\mathit{X}}
. We solve (
17
) using the Augmented Lagrangian algorithm
[
50
]
. The corresponding update is given in Appendix
A
.

In some cases the distances may not be known exactly, but we might have access to good bounds. We once more leverage the linearity of
\mathcal{D}
in the Gramian by adding the following linear constraints to the program,

\underline{d}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{2}\leq\mathcal{D}(\bm{\mathit{G}})_{m_{1}m_{2}}\leq\bar{d}_{m_{1}m_{2}}^{2}.

(18)

where
\bar{d}_{m_{1}m_{2}}
and
\underline{d}_{m_{1},m_{2}}
are upper and lower bounds on the distance
d_{m_{1},m_{2}}
.

Finally, we note that priors might be available not only for inter-receiver distances but also for inter-source distances and the distances between the sources and the receivers (we often have a coarse idea about how far the source events are from the microphones). These can be added to the constraints completely analogously.

IV-B

When One Set of Times is Known

When one set of timings is known (either the source emission times or the receiver offsets), it would still be possible to use the general formulation with the derived loss (
10a
) designed for the case when both sets of times are unknown (
2
). Though that would require more measurements than necessary given such prior knowledge as we show next.

Both cases (unknown emission times or unknown offsets) are captured by the simplified measurement model

\bm{\mathit{T}}=\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}+\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\tau}}^{T},

(19)

up to a transposition as necessary (exchanging the role of sources and receivers). From here, it follows that the influence of the timings can be eliminated simply by one left multiplication by
\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}
so that

\bm{\mathit{P}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{T}}=\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\Delta}}

does not depend on
\bm{\mathit{\tau}}
. We can thus replace the loss (
10a
) by

\left\lVert\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}(\bm{\mathit{B}}-\bm{\mathit{T}})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

(20)

To see how this reduces the minimal required number of measurements (sources or sensors), recall the degrees-of-freedom count from Section
II-A
. Concretely,

\#(\text{DOF})=d(M+K-\tfrac{d+1}{2})+M

gives

K\geq\frac{(d+1)(2M-d)}{2(M-d)}.

For
d=3
, this becomes

K\geq\frac{4M-6}{M-3},

so that the minimal number of receivers is now
M=4
and the minimal cases for the dimension-zero solution are
M=4,K=10
,
M=5,K=7
,
M=6,K=6
, and
M=9,K=5
. Another way to see this is by noting that the operator
\bm{\mathit{A}}\mapsto\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{A}}
has a smaller nullspace than
\bm{\mathit{A}}\mapsto\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}\bm{\mathit{A}}\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}
.

IV-C

Sources with Known (Constant) Offset

As a last example of prior information that is easily handled, we mention the practically relevant case where the source is for example a smartphone emitting periodic pulses. More generally, the source is emitting signals at known (not necessarily regular) intervals. The phone is not synchronized with the receivers, so the emitting times are strictly speaking unknown, but only up to one unknown offset—the start time of the emissions. This case has been studied in previous work
[
39
,
46
]
.

We show how to incorporate this using our proposed method. We have that

\tau_{k}=\tau_{o}+\delta_{k},

with
\delta_{k}
is the known offset of the
k
th emission with respect to the unknown time
\tau_{o}
. When the emissions are periodic, we have
\delta_{k}=k\cdot\delta
, but for simplicity we keep the notation more general.

Note that in general, the vectors
\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}
and
\bm{\mathit{\tau}}
can never be recovered uniquely, unless one of the times is known. The reason for this is that the global time reference can be decided arbitrarily (we used this fact when counting the degrees of freedom in Section
II-A
).

It then follows that the case of sources with known offsets is mathematically equivalent to the case when only the receivers’ offsets are unknown. Concretely, first write

\bm{\mathit{\tau}}=\tau_{o}\mathbf{1}_{K}+\bm{\mathit{\delta}},

where
\bm{\mathit{\delta}}
is a known vector. Then note that

\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T}+\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\tau}}^{T}=(\bm{\mathit{\sigma}}+\tau_{o}\mathbf{1}_{M})\mathbf{1}_{K}^{T}+\mathbf{1}_{M}\bm{\mathit{\delta}}^{\top}.

Since
\mathbf{1}\bm{\mathit{\delta}}^{\top}
is known it can be absorbed in the measurements; the one unknown offset
\tau_{o}
can then be absorbed in receiver offsets.

IV-D

Missing Measurements

One upshot of our formulation is that it allows localization even when some sensor–receiver measurements are unavailable. We denote the set of missing entries by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\big\{(m,k):{}

\displaystyle\text{distance between}~m^{\textrm{th}}~\text{receiver and }

\displaystyle k^{\textrm{th}}~\text{source is missing}\big\}.

We then replace the objective (
14a
) with

\min_{\bm{\mathit{G}},\bm{\mathit{B}},\left\{\alpha_{mk}\right\}}\ \bigg\|\bm{\mathit{J}}_{M}(\bm{\mathit{B}}-\bm{\mathit{T}}+\sum_{\mathclap{(m,k)\in\mathcal{M}}}\alpha_{mk}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{m}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{k}^{\top})\bm{\mathit{J}}_{K}\bigg\|_{F}^{2},

(21)

where
\bm{\mathit{e}}_{n}
denotes the canonical basis, and the coefficients
\alpha_{mk}
account for the missing entries.

The LM refinement then proceeds with
\theta=\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{vec}(\bm{\mathit{R}})\\
\mathsf{vec}(\bm{\mathit{S}})\\
\mathsf{vec}(\bm{\alpha})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(M+K)+|\mathcal{M}|}
. The Jacobian now includes the derivative with respect to
\bm{\alpha}
as well

[\mathsf{D}_{\bm{\alpha}}f]_{mk,ij}=\begin{cases}1&(m,k)\in\mathcal{M},\;i=m,j=k,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(22)

Note that this approach can be used in the presence of outliers by first estimating them using RANSAC for instance. Then those outliers can be treated as missing entries.

VII
Conclusion

We formulated a timing-invariant objective that allows us to localize sensors and sources in an unsynchronized setting. Based on this objective, we proposed an SDP relaxation using the full Gram matrix of the point set and then a subsequent refinement step using the LM algorithm. We have thus eliminated the need for having to first estimate the unknown timings as well as avoided the multiple initializations required when solving non-convex problems. We also proposed an approach to handle missing data and showed how to seamlessly incorporate additional information such as knowledge of some distances or partial synchronization.

Using numerical simulations, we demonstrated the feasibility of the approach in different scenarios and in near-minimal configurations. We compared our approach to a two-stage state-of-the-art method
[
16
]
. Not only did our approach outperform the two-stage algorithm in terms of localization error, but also in terms of runtime. We also tested our algorithms on real times of arrival measured in an office environment. We were able to localize the 12 microphones to within 0.04 m average error.

Future work could focus on dealing with outliers which arise in the presence of multipath, for example. The method could be similar to our missing data approach except that the positions of the outliers are not known and need to be determined.
