Power Measurement Enabled Channel Autocorrelation Matrix Estimation for IRS-Assisted Wireless Communication
Ge Yan, Lipeng Zhu, Rui Zhang

Abstract
By reconfiguring wireless channels via passive signal reflection, intelligent reflecting surface (IRS) can bring significant performance enhancement for wireless communication systems. However, such performance improvement generally relies on the knowledge of channel state information (CSI) for IRS-involved links. Prior works on IRS CSI acquisition mainly estimate IRS-cascaded channels based on the extra pilot signals received at the users/base station (BS) with time-varying IRS reflections, which, however, needs to modify the existing channel training/estimation protocols of wireless systems. To address this issue, we propose in this paper a new channel estimation scheme for IRS-assisted communication systems based on the received signal power measured at the user terminal, which is practically attainable without the need of changing the current protocol. Due to the lack of signal phase information in measured power, the autocorrelation matrix of the BS-IRS-user cascaded channel is estimated by solving an equivalent rank-minimization problem. To this end, a low-rank-approaching (LRA) algorithm is proposed by employing the fractional programming and alternating optimization techniques. To reduce computational complexity, an approximate LRA (ALRA) algorithm is also developed. Furthermore, these two algorithms are extended to be robust against the receiver noise and quantization error in power measurement. Simulation results are provided to verify the effectiveness of the proposed channel estimation algorithms as well as the IRS passive reflection design based on the estimated channel autocorrelation matrix.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, intelligent reflecting surface (IRS) has received great attention due to its appealing capability of reconfiguring wireless channels.
By applying tunable phase shifts to incident wireless signals, IRS can effectively control their propagation channels and thereby significantly enhance the wireless communication performance, such as spectral/energy efficiency and transmission reliability
[

,

Given such benefits as well as its high deployment flexibility, low hardware cost, and low power consumption, IRS has been identified as a key enabling technology for future wireless networks such as 6G
[

3

.
However, to reap the high performance gain by IRS, it is essential to acquire the channel state information (CSI) for the IRS channels with its assisting base station (BS) and users, which is practically difficult due to the following reasons.
On one hand, the passive IRS is not equipped with wireless transceivers at its reflecting elements, making it impossible to estimate the BS-IRS and IRS-user channels separately.
Instead, only the cascaded BS-IRS-user/user-IRS-BS channel can be estimated at the user/BS
[
6

.
On the other hand, to compensate for the significant product-distance path loss of the IRS-cascaded link, the number of IRS reflecting elements needs to be sufficiently large in practice, e.g., several tens or even hundreds
[

This results in high-dimensional IRS channel vectors/matrices that incur prohibitive overhead and computational complexity to estimate.

To tackle the above challenges, extensive studies have been devoted to the cascaded channel estimation for IRS-assisted wireless communication systems, while these works mainly adopted conventional pilot-based channel estimation approaches by sending additional pilot signals with concurrent time-varying IRS reflections

In the aforementioned works, IRS-involved CSI is estimated based on the received complex-valued pilot signals at the users/BS.
However, in the protocol of existing wireless communication systems, such as 4G/5G
[

]
, the pilot signals are dedicated to estimating the BS-user direct channels only.
As such, substantially additional pilots are required to estimate the new IRS-cascaded CSI, which thus requires significant modifications of the existing channel estimation/training protocols.
To address this issue, IRS reflection designs based on the received signal power measurement at the users have been proposed
[
24
,
25

As user power measurement is commonly adopted and easy to obtain in existing wireless systems, such as reference signal received power (RSRP)
[
26
]
, this approach can be practically implemented without any change of the current protocols.
For example, for the conditional sample mean (CSM) method proposed in
[
24
]
, the received signal power at the user was modelled as a random variable and a large number of IRS reflection patterns are randomly generated for user power measurement.
After the user measures the received signal power values for all IRS reflection patterns, each IRS reflecting element sets the reflection coefficient as the one that achieves the maximum received power expectation conditioned on its value.
The majority voting algorithm proposed in
[
25
]
employed a similar idea but the received signal strength indicator (RSSI) was used instead of RSRP.
Both methods in
[
24
,
25
]
directly design the IRS reflection coefficients based on the power measurement without estimating the channel.
However, to obtain the optimal IRS reflection performance, an excessively large number of IRS training reflections/power measurement are generally required (in the quadratic order of the number of IRS reflecting elements).
Therefore, these methods may still be time-consuming for practical implementation.

It is worth noting that the methods in
[
25
,
24
]
did not fully exploit the power measurement to obtain explicit CSI of IRS-cascaded channels, thus resulting in their high overhead and low efficiency.
To improve the existing IRS channel estimation/reflection designs based on user power measurement, this paper proposes a new channel autocorrelation matrix estimation scheme.
Specifically, since there is no signal phase information in received power measurement, the IRS-cascaded channel vector cannot be completely recovered, while its autocorrelation matrix can be (uniquely) estimated.
By equivalently transforming the channel autocorrelation estimation problem into a rank-minimization problem, a low-rank-approaching (LRA) algorithm is proposed to recover the channel autocorrelation matrix given the received signal power measurement with time-varying random IRS reflections. In particular, the LRA algorithm converts the rank-minimization problem into a fractional programming problem and alternating optimization is applied to obtain its solution.
To reduce the computational complexity of the LRA algorithm, an approximate LRA (ALRA) algorithm is also proposed, where the fractional programming is approximated by low-complexity quadratic programming.
Furthermore, the robust extensions of both LRA and ALRA algorithms are designed by considering the effects of receiver noise and quantization error in power measurement.
The convergence and estimation accuracy of the proposed algorithms are verified via simulations.
Besides, it is validated that with the IRS reflection designs based on the estimated channel autocorrelation matrix, significantly higher passive reflection gains can be achieved compared to other benchmark schemes, which demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed channel estimation methods for IRS-assisted wireless communication systems based on user power measurement.

III
Channel Autocorrelation Matrix Estimation

We assume that the user is quasi-static and its channels (or predominant deterministic channel components) with the BS and IRS do not change within a sufficiently long channel coherence time, during which the IRS first changes its reflection coefficients
T_{p}
times in total.
In the meanwhile, the user measures the corresponding received signal power values and feed them back to a central processing unit (e.g., BS or IRS controller) that can design accordingly and then set the IRS reflection coefficients for data transmission.
Specifically, for the

With all the received signals’ power obtained, the channel autocorrelation matrix is estimated based on

(

IV
LRA Algorithm

In this section, the channel autocorrelation matrix estimation problems (

Instead of directly finding a rank-one/two matrix solution, the proposed LRA algorithm iteratively approaches a low-rank matrix solution via alternating optimization.

Solution for Problem (

For

\displaystyle~\mathrm{s.t.}\ p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{V}_{t})=p_{t},\ t=1,\ldots,T_{p},

\displaystyle~~~~~~{\bm{H}}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{N}.

(6b)

The equivalence between problems (

As the channel autocorrelation matrix

V
Approximate LRA Algorithm

The proposed LRA algorithms can approach a low-rank matrix solution via alternating maximization of the eigenvalue-ratio functions, but their computational complexity may be high in practice because SDP is involved for each iteration.
In this section, proper approximations are implemented in the optimization of
\bm{H}
and
\bm{H}_{r}
for
b\geq 2
and
b=1
, respectively, and the ALRA algorithm is proposed to reduce the computational complexity.
The basic principle of the LRA algorithms is retained, while the eigenvalue-ratio functions are replaced by quadratic functions.
By exploiting the vector representations of hermitian matrices, closed-form solutions are derived for the ALRA algorithm during the iterations and thus the computational complexity is significantly reduced.

V-A

Distance-Minimization Approximation

For each iteration of Algorithm
1
with
b\geq 2
, a normalized eigenvector
\bm{x}
is obtained and then
\bm{H}
is optimized via problem (
9
).
The objective of problem (
9
) can be written as

\mathop{\max_{\bm{H}}}\frac{\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{X})}{\text{tr}(\bm{H})}=\text{tr}(\bm{X})\mathop{\max_{\bm{H}}}\frac{\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{X})}{\text{tr}(\bm{H})\text{tr}(\bm{X})},

(19)

where
\bm{X}=\bm{x}\bm{x}^{H}
.
Note that
\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{X})=\text{vec}(\bm{H})^{H}\text{vec}(\bm{X})
and for
\bm{H}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{N}
, we have
\text{tr}(\bm{H})\geq\sqrt{\text{tr}(\bm{H}^{H}\bm{H})}=\|\text{vec}(\bm{H})\|_{2}
.
Thus, the objective function on the right-hand side of (
19
) is upper-bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{X})}{\text{tr}(\bm{H})\text{tr}(\bm{X})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{X})}{\sqrt{\text{tr}(\bm{H}^{H}\bm{H})}\sqrt{\text{tr}(\bm{X}^{H}\bm{X})}}

(20a)

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{vec}(\bm{H})^{H}\text{vec}(\bm{X})}{\|\text{vec}(\bm{H})\|_{2}\|\text{vec}(\bm{X})\|_{2}},

(20b)

which is the inner product of two normalized vectors
\text{vec}(\bm{H})/\|\text{vec}(\bm{H})\|_{2}
and
\text{vec}(\bm{X})/\|\text{vec}(\bm{X})\|_{2}
.
As a result, the optimization of
\bm{H}
can be approximately seen as aligning the direction of
\text{vec}(\bm{H})
to that of
\text{vec}(\bm{X})
, i.e., minimizing the cosine distance between them, which is defined as

d_{c}(\text{vec}(\bm{H}),\text{vec}(\bm{X}))=1-\frac{\text{vec}(\bm{H})^{H}\text{vec}(\bm{X})}{\|\text{vec}(\bm{H})\|_{2}\|\text{vec}(\bm{X})\|_{2}}.

(21)

To reduce the computational complexity, we consider to minimize the Euclidean distance between
\text{vec}(\bm{H})
and
\mu\text{vec}(\bm{X})
, where
\mu
is a scaling factor to be determined.
Specifically, the distance-minimization problem is given by

\displaystyle\mathop{\min_{\bm{H},\mu}}\ d_{e}(\bm{H},\mu\bm{X})

(
22
)

\displaystyle~\mathrm{s.t.}\ p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{V}_{t})=p_{t},\ t=1,\ldots,T_{p},

(22a)

\displaystyle~~~~~~\bm{H}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{N},

(22b)

where
d_{e}(\bm{H},\mu\bm{X})
denotes the Euclidean distance between vectors
\text{vec}(\bm{H})
and
\text{vec}(\mu\bm{X})
and it is defined as

d_{e}(\bm{H},\mu\bm{X})=\|\text{vec}(\bm{H})-\text{vec}(\mu\bm{X})\|_{2}=\|\bm{H}-\mu\bm{X}\|_{F}.

(23)

To further simplify the optimization of
\bm{H}
, we relax the semidefinite constraint (
22b
) to a hermitian constraint and solve the following problem:

\displaystyle\mathop{\min_{\bm{H},\mu}}\ \|\bm{H}-\mu\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}

(
24
)

\displaystyle~\mathrm{s.t.}\ p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{V}_{t})=p_{t},\ t=1,\ldots,T_{p},

(24a)

\displaystyle~~~~~~\bm{H}^{H}=\bm{H}.

(24b)

Similarly, for Algorithm
2
with
b=1

\bm{H}_{r}
in problem (
18

For
b=1

\bm{X}=\bm{x}_{1}\bm{x}_{1}^{T}+\bm{x}_{2}\bm{x}_{2}^{T}

To improve the recovery accuracy, we consider a vector

\displaystyle\mathop{\min_{\bm{H}_{r},\bm{\mu}}}\ \|\bm{H}_{r}-\left(\mu_{1}\bm{X}_{1}+\mu_{2}\bm{X}_{2}\right)\|_{F}^{2}

(

(25b)

Solution for Problem (

According to
[
29
]
, all hermitian matrices of size
N\times N
form a linear space of dimension
N^{2}
with real combination coefficients.
In particular, each
N\times N
hermitian matrix
\bm{A}
can be represented by an
N^{2}
-dimensional real vector
\bm{w}_{a}
, which is the coordinate of
\bm{A}
in the hermitian matrix space.
Following the definition in
[
29
]
, denote the bijective mapping from
\bm{A}
to
\bm{w}_{a}
as function
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}\times 1}
, such that
\bm{w}_{a}=\mathcal{M}(\bm{A})
and
\bm{A}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\bm{w}_{a})
.
It has been shown in
[
29
]
that for any hermitian matrices
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
, we have
\textup{tr}(\bm{A}\bm{B})=\bm{w}_{a}^{T}\bm{w}_{b}
, where
\bm{w}_{a}=\mathcal{M}(\bm{A})
and
\bm{w}_{b}=\mathcal{M}(\bm{B})
.
Based on this property, problem (
24
) can be equivalently written as a quadratic optimization problem with vector variables.
Denote
\bm{w}=\mathcal{M}(\bm{H})
,
\bm{w}_{X}=\mathcal{M}(\bm{X})
, and
\bm{w}_{t}=\mathcal{M}(\bm{V}_{t})
,
t=1,\ldots,T_{p}
.
Then, we have
p_{t}=p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{V}_{t})=p_{0}\bm{w}^{T}\bm{w}_{t}
,
\forall t
, and

\displaystyle\|\bm{H}-\mu\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left((\bm{H}-\mu\bm{X})^{H}(\bm{H}-\mu\bm{X})\right)

(26)

\displaystyle=\|\bm{w}-\mu\bm{w}_{X}\|_{2}^{2}.

Define
\bm{C}=p_{0}[\bm{w}_{1},\ldots,\bm{w}_{T_{p}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}\times T_{p}}
, and then the power constraints
p_{t}=p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{V}_{t})
can be expressed as
\bm{C}^{T}\bm{w}=\bm{p}
.
Thus, problem (
24
) can be equivalently written as

\mathop{\min_{\bm{w},\mu}}\ \|\bm{w}-\mu\bm{w}_{X}\|_{2}^{2},~~\mathrm{s.t.}\ \bm{C}^{T}\bm{w}=\bm{p},

(27)

where the hermitian constraint (
22b
) is dropped because it is guaranteed by the transformation from
\bm{H}
to
\bm{w}
.
For any given
\mu

where
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{p}\times 1}
is the Lagrange multiplier.
Then,

and

can be solved via the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bm{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}-\bm{C}^{T}\bm{w}=\bm{0}_{T_{p}\times 1},

(29a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\bm{w}}

\displaystyle=2\bm{w}-2\mu\bm{w}_{X}-\bm{C}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{0}_{N^{2}\times 1}.

(29b)

By assuming that

and
\text{rank}(\bm{C})=T_{p}
hold
3
3

3

In practical systems, the number of power measurement

.
.

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}

\displaystyle=2(\bm{C}^{T}\bm{C})^{-1}(\bm{p}-\mu\bm{C}^{T}\bm{w}_{X}),

(30a)

\displaystyle\bm{w}

\displaystyle=\bm{D}\bm{p}+\mu(\bm{I}_{N^{2}}-\bm{D}\bm{C}^{T})\bm{w}_{X},

(30b)

where
\bm{D}=\bm{C}(\bm{C}^{T}\bm{C})^{-1}
.
Thus, the optimal

If

\bm{H}^{(i-1)}
, i.e.,

\lambda_{1}^{(i-1)}\bm{X}^{(i)}=\mathop{\arg\min_{\text{rank}(\bm{A})\leq 1}}{\|\bm{H}^{(i-1)}-\bm{A}\|_{F}^{2}}.

(36)

On the other hand,
\bm{H}^{(i)}
and
\mu^{(i)}
are jointly optimized to minimize
\|\bm{H}-\mu\bm{X}^{(i)}\|_{F}^{2}
under constraints (
24a
) and (
24b
).
Therefore, for
i\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{H}^{(i)}-\mu^{(i)}\bm{X}^{(i)}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\bm{H}^{(i-1)}-\lambda_{1}^{(i-1)}\bm{X}^{(i)}\right\|_{F}^{2}

(37a)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\bm{H}^{(i-1)}-\mu^{(i-1)}\bm{X}^{(i-1)}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(37b)

which guarantees the convergence of the ALRA algorithm for
b\geq 2
.
Similarly, the ALRA algorithm for
b=1
converges with a non-increasing sequence
\|\bm{H}_{r}^{(i)}-(\mu_{1}^{(i)}\bm{X}_{1}^{(i)}+\mu_{2}^{(i)}\bm{X}_{2}^{(i)})\|_{F}
, indicating that the estimated channel autocorrelation matrix approaches a rank-two matrix iteratively.

VI
Robust Estimation with Practical Power measurement

In the above sections, the channel autocorrelation matrix was estimated based on the received signal power.
However, the exact value of the received signal power cannot be obtained in practical communication systems.
This is because the received signals are corrupted by noise.
Besides, the power measurement at the user can only be fed back to the central processing unit approximately after quantization, which thus incurs quantization error.
In this section, the robust extensions of the proposed LRA and ALRA algorithms are designed based on practical power measurement, where the effects of receiver noise and quantization error are considered.

VI-A

Power Measurement

According to (
1
), the noisy signal power received at the user is given by

p_{r}=\left|\left(\bm{v}^{H}\bar{\bm{h}}\right)x+z\right|^{2}=p_{0}\text{tr}(\bar{\bm{H}}\bm{V})+Z,

(38)

where
Z=|z|^{2}+2\text{Re}((\bm{v}^{H}\bar{\bm{h}})xz^{*})
is the noise power satisfying
\mathbb{E}[Z]=\sigma^{2}
.
By averaging
N_{0}
noisy power values, each practical power measurement is given by

q=\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{0}}{\left|\left(\bm{v}^{H}\bar{\bm{h}}\right)x+z_{i}\right|^{2}}=p_{0}\text{tr}(\bar{\bm{H}}\bm{V})+\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{0}}{Z_{i}},

(39)

where
z_{i}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2})
,
i=1,\ldots,N_{0}
, are i.i.d. random noise variables and
Z_{i}=|z_{i}|^{2}+2\text{Re}((\bm{v}^{H}\bar{\bm{h}})xz_{i}^{*})
.
Define
\bar{Z}=\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{0}}{Z_{i}}
as the average noise power in each power measurement.
If
N_{0}
is sufficiently large,
\bar{Z}
converges to a Gaussian random variable with expectation
\mathbb{E}[\bar{Z}]=\sigma^{2}
, according to the central limit theorem.

Despite the above model of practical power measurement, its value cannot be exactly acquired because of the quantization.
For example, in the current protocol
[
33
]
, the RSRP value is mapped into equally quantized levels within the range from
-156
dBm to
-44
dBm.
Thus, to investigate the effect of power quantization mapping on the performance of channel autocorrelation matrix estimation, we split the range
[-156,-44]
dBm into
M
equal levels and define
D=112/M
dB as the interval for each level.
As such, the set of quantization power levels is given by
\Omega_{D}=\{-44-(l+1/2)D~
dBm
,l=0,\ldots,M-1\}
.
For the
l
-th level, the index
l
is reported to the central processing unit if the power value
q
lies between
-44-(l+1)D
dBm and
-44-lD
dBm.

VI-B

Reformulation of Channel Estimation Problem

Denote the actual power value for the
t
-th measurement as
q_{t}=p_{0}\text{tr}(\bar{\bm{H}}\bm{V}_{t})+\bar{Z}_{t}
, where
\bar{Z}_{t}
is the noise power.
Given the quantized power level index
l_{t}
, we have
\zeta_{t}\leq q_{t}\leq\xi_{t}
, where
\zeta_{t}=10^{-7.4-(l_{t}+1)D/10}
and
\xi_{t}=10^{-7.4-l_{t}D/10}
are the lower and upper bounds of the
l_{t}
-th power level.
For
b\geq 2
, the channel autocorrelation matrix estimation problem is reformulated as

\displaystyle\mathop{\min_{\bm{H},\bm{\delta}\geq\bm{0}_{T_{p}\times 1}}}\ {\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}}

(

)

\displaystyle~\mathrm{s.t.}\ \zeta_{t}-\delta_{t}\leq p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{V}_{t})+\sigma^{2}\leq\xi_{t}+\delta_{t},\forall t,

(40a)

\displaystyle~~~~~~\text{rank}(\bm{H})=1,~\bm{H}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{N},

(40b)

where
\bm{\delta}=[\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{T_{p}\times 1}]\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{p}\times 1}
is a slack vector which is introduced to tackle the uncertainty of noise power
\bar{Z}_{t}
.
For
b=1
, the problem is given by

\displaystyle\mathop{\min_{\bm{H}_{r},\bm{\delta}\geq\bm{0}_{T_{p}\times 1}}}\ {\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}}

(
41
)

\displaystyle~\mathrm{s.t.}\ \zeta_{t}-\delta_{t}\leq p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}_{r}\bm{V}_{t})+\sigma^{2}\leq\xi_{t}+\delta_{t},\forall t,

(41a)

\displaystyle~~~~~~\text{rank}(\bm{H}_{r})\leq 2,~\bm{H}_{r}\in\mathbb{M}_{+}^{N}.

(41b)

It should be noticed that problems (
40
) and (
41
) are an non-convex optimization problems because of the rank constraints, whose optimal solutions cannot be solved by existing optimization tools in polynomial time.
In the following, we generalize the proposed LRA and ALRA algorithms to solve problems (

) and (

Robust LRA Algorithm

In this subsection, the robust extension of the LRA algorithm is proposed.

VI-D

Robust ALRA Algorithm

In this subsection, the robust ALRA algorithm is proposed with a lower computational complexity.
Similar to that in Section
V
, the Euclidean distance functions are employed as approximations of the eigenvalue-ratio functions of the robust LRA algorithm.
Specifically, given
\bm{X}
for
b\geq 2
, the optimization of
\bm{H}
(i.e., problem (
43
)) is approximated by

\displaystyle\mathop{\min_{\bm{H},\bm{\delta}\geq\bm{0}_{T_{p}\times 1},\mu}}\ \|\bm{H}-\mu\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}+\rho\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}

(
50
)

\displaystyle~\mathrm{s.t.}\ \zeta_{t}-\delta_{t}\leq p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{V}_{t})+\sigma^{2}\leq\xi_{t}+\delta_{t},\forall t,

(50a)

\displaystyle~~~~~~\bm{H}^{H}=\bm{H}.

(50b)

Note that problem (
50
) is a quadratic minimization problem with inequality constraints, which is convex but the optimal solution cannot be obtained in closed form.
To derive an explicit solution, we define
\hat{q}_{t}=(\zeta_{t}+\xi_{t})/2
, and approximate constraint (
50a
) with
\hat{q}_{t}-\delta_{t}\leq p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{V}_{t})+\sigma^{2}\leq\hat{q}_{t}+\delta_{t}
,
\forall t
.
Then, problem (

) can be further approximated by

\displaystyle\mathop{\min_{\bm{H},\mu}}\ \|\bm{H}-\mu\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}+\rho\sum_{t=1}^{T_{p}}{\left|p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}\bm{V}_{t})+\sigma^{2}-\hat{q}_{t}\right|^{2}}

51
)

\displaystyle~\mathrm{s.t.}\ \bm{H}^{H}=\bm{H},

(51a)

which is a quadratic minimization problem with linear constraints.
By leveraging the transformation
\mathcal{M}
, problem (
51
) can be equivalently written as

\mathop{\min_{\bm{w},\mu}}\ \|\bm{w}-\mu\bm{w}_{X}\|_{2}^{2}+\rho\|\bm{C}^{T}\bm{w}-(\hat{\bm{q}}-\sigma^{2}\bm{1}_{T_{p}})\|_{2}^{2},

(52)

with
\hat{\bm{q}}=[\hat{q}_{1},\ldots,\hat{q}_{T_{p}}]^{T}
.
By taking the first derivative of the objective function, the optimal solutions can be obtained in closed form as

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{\|\bm{\psi}\|_{2}^{2}}\bm{\psi}^{T}\bm{\Upsilon}\bm{C}(\hat{\bm{q}}-\sigma^{2}\bm{1}_{T_{p}}),

(53a)

(53b)

\displaystyle\bm{\psi}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{I}_{N^{2}}-\bm{\Upsilon}\right)\bm{w}_{X},

(53c)

\displaystyle\bm{\Upsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{I}_{N^{2}}+\rho\bm{C}\bm{C}^{T}\right)^{-1},

(53d)

and thus the optimal solution for problem (
51
) is given by

.

b\geq 2
can be implemented by replacing line
4
in Algorithm
3

51
) given
\bm{X}
from the previous iteration, i.e.,
\bm{H}^{\star}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\bm{w}^{\star})
, where
\bm{w}^{\star}
is given by (

).

\bm{H}^{\star}
for problem (

b=1
, the robust estimation of

&~~~~~~~~~~+\rho\sum_{t=1}^{T_{p}}{\left|p_{0}\text{tr}(\bm{H}_{r}\bm{V}_{t})+\sigma^{2}-\hat{q}_{t}\right|^{2}}\end{aligned}

(

\displaystyle~\mathrm{s.t.}\ \bm{H}_{r}^{T}=\bm{H}_{r}.

(54a)

According to the derivations for solving problem (

{\bm{w}}_{r}^{\star}

\bm{\mu}^{\star}

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\rho\left({\bm{\Psi}}^{T}{\bm{\Psi}}\right)^{\dagger}{\bm{\Psi}}^{T}{\bm{\Upsilon}}{\bm{C}}(\hat{\bm{q}}-\sigma^{2}\bm{1}_{T_{p}}),

(56a)

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{r}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\rho{\bm{\Upsilon}}{\bm{C}}(\hat{\bm{q}}-\sigma^{2}\bm{1}_{T_{p}})+{\bm{\Upsilon}}{\bm{W}}_{X}\bm{\mu}^{\star},

\displaystyle{\bm{\Psi}}

(56c)

\displaystyle{\bm{\Upsilon}}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{I}_{N^{2}}+\rho{\bm{C}}{\bm{C}}^{T}\right)^{-1}.

(56d)

Thus, the robust ALRA algorithm for

4
in Algorithm

with the optimal solution for problem (

\bm{X}_{2}

\displaystyle\bm{\Upsilon}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{I}_{N^{2}}+\rho\bm{C}\bm{C}^{T}\right)^{-1}

[

.

Next, the convergence of the proposed robust ALRA algorithm is analyzed.
For
b\geq 2

and can be seen as a penaltized version of the squared distance function

In the

-th iteration for

\bm{x}^{(i)}
is the eigenvector of
\bm{H}^{(i-1)}
corresponding to the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}^{(i-1)}

\lambda_{1}^{(i-1)}\bm{X}^{(i)}
is the rank-one matrix closest to
\bm{H}^{(i-1)}

Moreover,
\bm{H}^{(i)}
and
\mu^{(i)}
are solved optimally from problem (

\bm{X}^{(i)}

Thus, it is easy to verify that

\displaystyle\varphi(\bm{H}^{(i)},\mu^{(i)},\bm{X}^{(i)})

VIII
Conclusion

This paper studied the IRS-cascaded channel estimation problem based on received signal power measurement at the user.
Since the signal phase information is not available in power measurement, the channel autocorrelation matrix was estimated by solving a rank-minimization problem.
Based on the analysis of existence and uniqueness of the solution, two algorithms of different computational complexity were proposed to solve the channel autocorrelation estimation problem.
By relaxing the non-convex low-rank constrained problem to a fractional programming problem, the proposed LRA algorithm applied the alternating optimization method to iteratively approach a low-rank matrix solution.
To reduce computational complexity, the ALRA algroithm was also developed to obtain an approximate solution in closed-form during each iteration.
Moreover, robust extensions of the LRA and ALRA algorithms were proposed based on practical power measurement, where the effects of receiver noise and quantization error in power measurement were considered.
The convergence and efficiency of the proposed algorithms were validated via simulations, demonstrating that small estimation errors of the channel autocorrelation matrix can be achieved.
It was also shown that by applying the IRS reflection designs based on the channel autocorrelation matrices estimated by the proposed LRA and ALRA algorithms, significant improvement in the effective channel gain between BS and user can be achieved compared to other benchmark schemes.
Furthermore, the performance of the robust LRA and robust ALRA algorithms was evaluated using practical power measurement with noise and quantization errors, which demonstrated their robustness in improving the effective channel gain for IRS-assisted communication systems, even with imperfect power measurement.

.
Then, we have
\hat{\bm{H}}_{r}=\text{Re}(\hat{\bm{H}})=\bar{\bm{H}}_{r}
, which is uniquely determined.

Appendix D
Complementary Proof of Lemma
1
in Appendix
A

In this section, the detailed proof for the dimension of space
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)
defined in Appendix
A
is given to complete the proof of Lemma
1
, where it is claimed that
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))=\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
.
The function
\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
is defined in Lemma
1
as

\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{N^{2}-N}{2}+1,~~~b=1\\
&N^{2}-N+1,~~~~b\geq 2\end{aligned}\right..

(63)

To prove
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))=\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
, we first denote the
N^{2}
-dimensional hermitian matrices space as
\mathcal{S}_{H}(N)
and explicitly define an orthonormal basis for it.
Then, we show that

can be spanned by
\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}

D-A

Orthonormal Basis of Space
\mathcal{S}_{H}(N)

Consider vector
\bm{b}_{1}=\bm{1}_{N}/\sqrt{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
, and denote
\bm{b}_{2},\ldots,\bm{b}_{N}
as the vectors that form an orthonormal basis along with
\bm{b}_{1}
for the
N
-dimensional real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
.
Then, an orthonormal basis for space
\mathcal{S}_{H}(N)
defined in
[
29
]
can be given by the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Matrices
\{\bm{B}_{1},\ldots,\bm{B}_{N};\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)},\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)},1\leq n<l\leq N\}
form an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{S}_{H}(N)
defined as

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{n}

\displaystyle=\textup{diag}(\bm{b}_{n}),~1\leq n\leq N,

(64a)

\displaystyle\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\bm{e}_{n}\bm{e}_{l}^{T}+\bm{e}_{l}\bm{e}_{n}^{T}\right),~1\leq n<l\leq N,

(64b)

\displaystyle\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)}

\displaystyle=\frac{j}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\bm{e}_{n}\bm{e}_{l}^{T}-\bm{e}_{l}\bm{e}_{n}^{T}\right),~1\leq n<l\leq N,

(64c)

where
\bm{e}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
has a
1
for the
n
-th element and all other elements equal to zero.

Proof
:
.

It is easy to verify that these matrices are hermitian and orthonormal to each other under the Frobenius product
\text{tr}(\bm{A}^{H}\bm{B})
for arbitrary matrices
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
.
Meanwhile, on one hand, for any
N\times N
hermitian matrix
\bm{A}
, its main diagonal entries form an
N
-dimensional real vector
\bm{d}_{\bm{A}}=\text{diag}(\bm{A})
.
Thus, there exists a real vector
\bm{c}_{\bm{A}}=[c_{\bm{A},1},\ldots,c_{\bm{A},N}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}
such that
\bm{d}_{\bm{A}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{c_{\bm{A},n}\bm{b}_{n}}
, i.e.,

\text{diag}(\bm{d}_{\bm{A}})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{c_{\bm{A},n}\bm{B}_{n}}.

(65)

On the other hand, entries of matrices
\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)}
and
\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)}
are zero except for two on the
n
-th row and
l
-th column, and the
l
-th row and
n
-th column,
\forall n<l
.
Thus, it can be verified that
\bm{A}
can be written as

\bm{A}=\sqrt{2}\sum_{n<l}{\text{Re}(A_{nl})\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)}+\text{Im}(A_{nl})\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)}}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}{c_{\bm{A},n}\bm{B}_{n}},

(66)

which is the linear combination of
N(N-1)+N=N^{2}
matrices with
N^{2}
real coefficients.
Therefore, the
N^{2}
matrices
\{\bm{B}_{1},\ldots,\bm{B}_{N};\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)},\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)}
,
1\leq n<l\leq N\}
form an orthonormal basis for the space of hermitian matrices.
∎

D-B

Dimension of Space
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)

To show
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))=\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
, we provide a two-fold proof, where we first prove
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))\leq\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
and then
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))\geq\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
.

For the first part, we show
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))\leq\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
.
Since
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)
is spanned by
\mathcal{S}_{V}^{b}(N)
with real coefficients, we have
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)\in\mathcal{S}_{H}(N)
and thus
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))\leq N^{2}
.
Due to the unit-amplitude of the IRS reflecting coefficients, however, the diagonal entries in any matrix
\bm{V}\in\mathcal{S}_{V}^{b}(N)
are always equal to 1, i.e.,
\text{diag}(\bm{V})=\bm{1}_{N}=\sqrt{N}\bm{b}_{1}
.
Then, it is easy to verify that
\bm{V}
is orthogonal to
\bm{B}_{2},\ldots,\bm{B}_{N}
for any values of
b
, i.e.,

\text{tr}(\bm{V}^{H}\bm{B}_{n})=\sqrt{N}\bm{b}_{1}^{T}\bm{b}_{n}=0,~2\leq n\leq N.

(67)

Moreover, for
b=1
,
\bm{V}
is always a real matrix and thus we have
\text{tr}(\bm{V}^{H}\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)})=\text{Im}(V_{nl})=0
,
\forall n\neq l
.
Thus, for
b\geq 2
,
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)
should be a subspace of the space spanned by
\{\bm{B}_{1};\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)},\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)},1\leq n<l\leq N\}
, leading to
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))\leq N^{2}-N+1=\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
, while for
b=1
,
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{1}(N)
should be a subspace of the space spanned by
\{\bm{B}_{1};\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)},1\leq n<l\leq N\}
, leading to
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{1}(N))\leq N(N-1)/2+1=\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(1)}
.
Therefore,
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))\leq\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
holds for any
b
.

For the second part of the proof, we prove by induction.
To prove
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))\geq\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
, it suffices to show that there exist at least
\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{V}^{b}(N)
such that they are linearly independent with real coefficients.
For the following, we first prove the case of
b=1
, and then consider the case of
b\geq 2
.

For
b=1
, we show that there always exists
\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(1)}=(N^{2}-N)/2+1
matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{V}^{1}(N)
that are linearly independent with real coefficients.
It is easy to verify that this is true when
N\leq 3
.
Assume that this is true for
N=L\geq 3
, and let
K_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(1)}
for simplicity, then we have to show that this is true for
N=L+1
.
Suppose
\bm{V}_{k}=\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{H}
,
k=1,\ldots,{K_{1}}
are linearly independent matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{V}^{1}(L)
, where
\bm{v}_{k}\in{\{\pm 1\}}^{L}
,
k=1,\ldots,K_{1}
.
Define matrix
\bm{W}=[\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{K_{1}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times{K_{1}}}
, which has full row rank
L
according to the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

The matrix
\bm{W}
has full row rank
L
for
L\geq 3
.

Proof
:
.

As
K_{1}>L
for
L\geq 3
, we have
\text{rank}(\bm{W})\leq L<{K_{1}}
.
If
\text{rank}(\bm{W})<L
, then the space spanned by the columns of
\bm{W}
, denoted as
\text{span}(\bm{W})
, has a dimension lower than
L
.
Since all vectors in
\{\pm 1\}^{L}
span the
L
-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{L}
, there exists a vector
\bm{x}\in\{\pm 1\}^{L}
such that
\bm{x}\notin\text{span}(\bm{W})
.
Let
\bm{x}=\bm{v}_{x}+\bm{y}
, where
\bm{v}_{x}\in\text{span}(\bm{W})
while
\bm{y}\perp\text{span}(\bm{W})
.
Note that
\bm{X}=\bm{x}\bm{x}^{H}\in\mathcal{S}_{V}^{1}(N)
and thus matrices
\{\bm{V}_{1},\ldots,\bm{V}_{K_{1}},\bm{X}\}
must be linearly dependent with real coefficients according to the first part of the proof, because there are
{K_{1}}+1>\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(1)}
hermitian matrices from
\mathcal{S}_{V}^{1}(N)
in total.
Hence, there exists
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{{K_{1}}\times 1}
and
\mu_{x}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that they are not all zeros and

\mu_{x}\bm{X}+\sum_{k=1}^{{K_{1}}}{\mu_{k}\bm{V}_{k}}=\bm{0}_{L\times L}.

(68)

Consider the matrix
\bm{Y}=\bm{y}\bm{y}^{H}
and we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left(\bm{Y}^{H}\left(\mu_{x}\bm{X}+\sum_{k=1}^{{K_{1}}}{\mu_{k}\bm{V}_{k}}\right)\right)

(69a)

\displaystyle=\mu_{x}\text{tr}(\bm{Y}^{H}\bm{X})+\sum_{k=1}^{{K_{1}}}{\mu_{k}\text{tr}(\bm{Y}^{H}\bm{V}_{k})}.

(69b)

Note that
\text{tr}(\bm{Y}^{H}\bm{X})=|\bm{y}^{H}(\bm{v}_{x}+\bm{y})|^{2}=\|\bm{y}\|_{2}^{4}
and
\text{tr}(\bm{Y}^{H}\bm{V}_{k})=|\bm{y}^{H}\bm{v}_{k}|^{2}=0
,
\forall k
.
Thus, equation (
69
) is equivalent to
\mu_{x}\|\bm{y}\|_{2}^{4}=0
, which leads to
\mu_{x}=0
as
\bm{y}
is nonzero.
Then, equation (
68
) becomes
\sum_{k=1}^{{K_{1}}}{\mu_{k}\bm{V}_{k}}=\bm{0}
for some
\bm{\mu}\neq\bm{0}
.
This is contradictory to the assumption that
\{\bm{V}_{k},k=1,\ldots,{K_{1}}\}
are linearly independent with real coefficients.
Therefore,
\bm{W}
should have full row rank
L
.
∎

Given Lemma
3
, we implicitly construct
\mathcal{D}_{L+1}^{(1)}
linearly independent matrices in
S_{V}^{1}(L+1)
for the case of
N=L+1
.
Define
(L+1)
-dimensional vectors

\bm{u}_{k}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{v}_{k}\\
1\end{array}\right],~\bm{y}_{k}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{v}_{k}\\
-1\end{array}\right],~k=1,\ldots,{K_{1}},

(70)

and matrices
\bm{U}_{k}=\bm{u}_{k}\bm{u}_{k}^{H}
and
\bm{Y}_{k}=\bm{y}_{k}\bm{y}_{k}^{H}
,
\forall k
.
Obviously,
\bm{U}_{k},\bm{Y}_{k}\in S_{V}^{1}(L+1)
,
\forall k
, and there are
2{K_{1}}=2\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(1)}
matrices in total.
Note that for
L\geq 3
, we have
2\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(1)}>\mathcal{D}_{L+1}^{(1)}
, so matrices
\{\bm{U}_{k},\bm{Y}_{k},k=1,\ldots,{K_{1}}\}
are linearly dependent with real coefficients according to the first part of the proof, i.e., there exists real vectors
\bm{\alpha}=[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K_{1}}]^{T},\bm{\beta}=[\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{K_{1}}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{{K_{1}}\times 1}

\displaystyle\bm{0}_{(L+1)\times(L+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{{K_{1}}}{\alpha_{k}\bm{U}_{k}+\beta_{k}\bm{Y}_{k}}

(71a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{{K_{1}}}{\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}(\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k})\bm{V}_{k}&(\alpha_{k}-\beta_{k})\bm{v}_{k}\\
(\alpha_{k}-\beta_{k})\bm{v}_{k}^{H}&\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k}\end{array}\right]}.

\sum_{k=1}^{{K_{1}}}{(\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k})\bm{V}_{k}}=\bm{0}
.
However,
\{\bm{V}_{k},k=1,\ldots,{K_{1}}\}
are linearly independent with real coefficients by assumption, which leads to
\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}=\bm{0}
.
Then,
\sum_{k=1}^{{K_{1}}}(\alpha_{k}-\beta_{k})\bm{v}_{k}=2\sum_{k=1}^{{K_{1}}}{\alpha_{k}\bm{v}_{k}}=2\bm{W}\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0}
.
Reversely, it is easy to verify that (
71
) holds if
\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{W}\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0}
.
Thus, (
71

\bm{F}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\alpha}\\
\bm{\beta}\end{array}\right]=\bm{0}_{(K_{1}+L)\times 1},~\bm{F}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\bm{I}_{K_{1}}&\bm{I}_{K_{1}}\\
\bm{W}&\bm{0}_{L\times K_{1}}\end{array}\right].

(72)

Therefore, the maximum number of linearly independent matrices among
\{\bm{U}_{k},\bm{Y}_{k},k=1,\ldots,{K_{1}}\}
with real coefficients, denoted as

, should be equal to the number of linearly independent columns of matrix

.
Since
{K_{1}}=\mathcal{D}_{L}^{(1)}=(L^{2}-L)/2+1

.
Thus, there exists
\mathcal{D}_{L+1}^{(1)}
linearly independent matrices with real coefficients in the set
S_{V}^{1}(L+1)
, which completes the proof of
\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{1}(N))\geq\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(1)}

For

.

For

.

If

2L
.

, consider
(L+1)
-dimensional vectors

\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))\geq\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}

\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))=\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
, as stated in Appendix
A
.
Additionally, for
b\geq 2
, it has been shown that
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)
is a subspace of the space spanned by
\{\bm{B}_{1};\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)},\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)},1\leq n<l\leq N\}
with real coefficients, whose dimension is
N^{2}-N+1
, which is the same as the dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)
given above.
Hence,
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)
equals to this spanned space and
\{\bm{B}_{1};\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)},\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)},1\leq n<l\leq N\}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)
for
b\geq 2
.
Similarly, an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)
for
b=1
is given by
\{\bm{B}_{1};\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)},1\leq n<l\leq N\}
.

Appendix E
Complementary Proof of Proposition
1
in Appendix
B

In this section, the uniqueness of the solution for problem (
4
) demonstrated in Proposition
1
is proved in details.
First we consider the case of
b\geq 2
and then
b=1
.

For
b\geq 2
, suppose there exists a matrix
\hat{\bm{H}}\neq\bar{\bm{H}}
that is also a solution to Problem (
4
).
Then,
\hat{\bm{H}}
is a rank-one hermitian matrix and we have
\text{tr}(\hat{\bm{H}}\bm{V}_{t})=p_{t}=\text{tr}(\bar{\bm{H}}\bm{V}_{t})
,
t=1,\ldots,T_{p}
.
By defining
\hat{\bm{H}}=\hat{\bm{h}}\hat{\bm{h}}^{H}
and
\mathcal{\bm{E}}=\bar{\bm{H}}-\hat{\bm{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, we have
\text{rank}(\mathcal{\bm{E}})\leq 2
and
\text{tr}(\mathcal{\bm{E}}\bm{V}_{t})=0
for
\forall t
.
If
D_{V}=\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}=\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N))
, the matrix
\mathcal{\bm{E}}
is perpendicular to the space
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)
, which results in
\text{tr}(\mathcal{\bm{E}}\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)})=\text{tr}(\mathcal{\bm{E}}\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)})=0
,
1\leq n<l\leq N
, and also
\text{tr}(\mathcal{\bm{E}}\bm{B}_{1})=\text{tr}(\mathcal{\bm{E}})/\sqrt{N}=0
.
According to the definitions of matrices
\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)},\bm{E}_{nl}^{(I)},1\leq n<l\leq N
, all non-diagonal entries of
\mathcal{\bm{E}}
are zero and thus we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bm{E}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathcal{E}_{11}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&\mathcal{E}_{22}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&0&\mathcal{E}_{NN}\end{array}\right],

(80)

where
\mathcal{E}_{nn}=|\bar{h}_{n}|^{2}-|\hat{h}_{n}|^{2}
,
\forall n
.
Due to
\text{rank}(\mathcal{\bm{E}})\leq 2
, we assume
\mathcal{E}_{nn}=0
for
n\geq 3
without loss of generality, and thus
|\bar{h}_{n}|=|\hat{h}_{n}|
for
n\geq 3
.
By assuming
N\geq 3
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{n1}=\bar{h}_{n}\bar{h}_{1}^{*}-\hat{h}_{n}\hat{h}_{1}^{*}=0
,
n=3,\ldots,N
, leading to
|\bar{h}_{n}\bar{h}_{1}|=|\hat{h}_{n}\hat{h}_{1}|
for
\forall n\geq 3
.
Since
\bar{h}_{n}\neq 0
holds with probability
1
for any
n
, we find that
|\bar{h}_{1}|=|\hat{h}_{1}|
.
Similarly,
|\bar{h}_{2}|=|\hat{h}_{2}|
can be obtained.
Then,
\mathcal{E}_{11}=\mathcal{E}_{22}=0
and thus
\mathcal{\bm{E}}=\bm{0}
, which indicates
\bar{\bm{H}}=\hat{\bm{H}}
.
Hence,
\bar{\bm{H}}
is the unique solution to Problem (
4
) with probability
1
for
b\geq 2
when
N\geq 3
and
D_{V}=\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(b)}
.

For
b=1
, the IRS reflection vectors are all real vectors and it is easy to verify that
\bar{\bm{H}}
and
\bar{\bm{H}}^{*}
are both solutions for problem (
4
).
Suppose there exists a matrix
\hat{\bm{H}}
that does not equal to
\bar{\bm{H}}
or
\bar{\bm{H}}^{*}
but is also a solution for problem (
4
), and its real part is denoted as
\hat{\bm{H}}_{r}=\text{Re}(\hat{\bm{H}})
.
Note that the ranks of both
\bar{\bm{H}}_{r}=\text{Re}(\bar{\bm{H}})
and
\hat{\bm{H}}_{r}
are no more than two and
\text{tr}(\bar{\bm{H}}_{r}\bm{V}_{t})=\text{tr}(\bar{\bm{H}}\bm{V}_{t})=p_{t}=\text{tr}(\hat{\bm{H}}\bm{V}_{t})=\text{tr}(\hat{\bm{H}}_{r}\bm{V}_{t})

Similar to the case where
b\geq 2
, we define
\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r}=\bar{\bm{H}}_{r}-\hat{\bm{H}}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, which is a real symmetric matrix satisfying
\text{rank}(\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r})\leq 4
and
\text{tr}(\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r}\bm{V}_{t})=0

t=1,\ldots,T_{p}
.
When
D_{V}=\mathcal{D}_{N}^{(1)}=\text{dim}(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{1}(N))
, the matrix
\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r}
is perpendicular to the space
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{b}(N)
and thus
\text{tr}(\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r}\bm{E}_{nl}^{(R)})=0
,
1\leq n<l\leq N
.
As a result,
\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathcal{E}_{r,11}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&\mathcal{E}_{r,22}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&0&\mathcal{E}_{r,NN}\end{array}\right],

(81)

where
\mathcal{E}_{r,nn}
is the
n
-th diagonal element of
\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r}
.
Without loss of generality, we have
\mathcal{E}_{r,nn}=0
for
n\geq 5
due to
\text{rank}(\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r})\leq 4
.
Since
\bar{\bm{H}}=\bar{\bm{h}}\bar{\bm{h}}^{H}
, it is easy to verify that
\bar{\bm{H}}_{r}=\bar{\bm{h}}_{r}\bar{\bm{h}}_{r}^{T}+\bar{\bm{h}}_{m}\bar{\bm{h}}_{m}^{T}

\bar{\bm{h}}_{r}=\text{Re}(\bar{\bm{h}})

\bar{\bm{h}}_{m}=\text{Im}(\bar{\bm{h}})
.
Similarly, by defining
\hat{\bm{H}}=\hat{\bm{h}}\hat{\bm{h}}^{H}
, we have
\hat{\bm{H}}_{r}=\hat{\bm{h}}_{r}\hat{\bm{h}}_{r}^{T}+\hat{\bm{h}}_{m}\hat{\bm{h}}_{m}^{T}
, where
\hat{\bm{h}}_{r}=\text{Re}(\hat{\bm{h}})
and
\hat{\bm{h}}_{m}=\text{Im}(\hat{\bm{h}})
.
Consider real matrices

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{A}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{h}_{r,1}&\bar{h}_{m,1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\bar{h}_{r,4}&\bar{h}_{m,4}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{A}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{h}_{r,1}&\hat{h}_{m,1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\hat{h}_{r,4}&\hat{h}_{m,4}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{B}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{h}_{r,5}&\bar{h}_{m,5}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\bar{h}_{r,N}&\bar{h}_{m,N}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{B}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{h}_{r,5}&\hat{h}_{m,5}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\hat{h}_{r,N}&\hat{h}_{m,N}\end{array}\right],

where
\bar{h}_{r,n}
,
\bar{h}_{m,n}
,
\hat{h}_{r,n}
and
\hat{h}_{m,n}
are the
n
-th elements of vectors
\bar{\bm{h}}_{r}
,
\bar{\bm{h}}_{m}
,
\hat{\bm{h}}_{r}
and
\hat{\bm{h}}_{m}
, respectively.
Then, the matrix
\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r}
can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bm{E}}_{r}

\displaystyle=\bar{\bm{H}}_{r}-\hat{\bm{H}}_{r}

(83a)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{\bm{A}}\\
\bar{\bm{B}}\end{array}\right]\left[\bar{\bm{A}}^{T},\bar{\bm{B}}^{T}\right]-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\bm{A}}\\
\hat{\bm{B}}\end{array}\right]\left[\hat{\bm{A}}^{T},\hat{\bm{B}}^{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{\bm{A}}\bar{\bm{A}}^{T}-\hat{\bm{A}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{T}&\bar{\bm{A}}\bar{\bm{B}}^{T}-\hat{\bm{A}}\hat{\bm{B}}^{T}\\
\bar{\bm{B}}\bar{\bm{A}}^{T}-\hat{\bm{B}}\hat{\bm{A}}^{T}&\bar{\bm{B}}\bar{\bm{B}}^{T}-\hat{\bm{B}}\hat{\bm{B}}^{T}\end{array}\right],

Since

which means that
