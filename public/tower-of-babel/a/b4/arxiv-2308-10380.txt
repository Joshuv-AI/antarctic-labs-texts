A Human-on-the-Loop Optimization Autoformalism Approach for Sustainability
Ming Jin, Bilgehan Sel, Fnu Hardeep, Wotao Yin

Abstract
This paper outlines a natural conversational approach to solving personalized energy-related problems using large language models (LLMs). We focus on customizable optimization problems that necessitate repeated solving with slight variations in modeling and are user-specific, hence posing a challenge to devising a one-size-fits-all model. We put forward a strategy that augments an LLM with an optimization solver, enhancing its proficiency in understanding and responding to user specifications and preferences while providing nonlinear reasoning capabilities. Our approach pioneers the novel concept of human-guided optimization autoformalism , translating a natural language task specification automatically into an optimization instance. This enables LLMs to analyze, explain, and tackle a variety of instance-specific energy-related problems, pushing beyond the limits of current prompt-based techniques. Our research encompasses various commonplace tasks in the energy sector, from electric vehicle charging and Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning (HVAC) control to long-term planning problems such as cost-benefit evaluations for installing rooftop solar photovoltaics (PVs) or heat pumps. This pilot study marks an essential stride towards the context-based formulation of optimization using LLMs , with the potential to democratize optimization processes. As a result, stakeholders are empowered to optimize their energy consumption, promoting sustainable energy practices customized to personal needs and preferences.

Related Work

Our research resides at the confluence of large language models, autoformalization, and optimization.

Autoformalization.
The recent success of LLMs has inspired numerous studies utilizing them in the field of mathematical autoformalization. This direction is concerned with the conversion of natural language into formal languages

In a manner analogous to formal languages in mathematics, convex solvers accept problems in a so-called standard form
[

Mirroring the scenario in mathematical autoformalization in Isabelle, we observe that despite the scarcity of CVXPY examples online, large language models exhibit a surprisingly adept understanding of it, though they occasionally make syntactic errors. We correct these errors in our debugging process, leveraging feedback sourced directly from Python.

Energy Concierge Framework

Design principles.
To ensure that conversational AI can effectively promote greater energy efficiency and sustainable energy practices, we propose a set of design principles. These include a modular pipeline, consisting of several components such as an LLM, code compiler, and user interface that can be used for a wide range of energy-related problems. Interactivity is also a key aspect, enabling users to engage with the system in a natural and conversational manner. Personalization is crucial, tailoring the system to each user’s needs, preferences, and constraints. Real-time feedback is provided to users, offering timely information and allowing them to adjust their behavior accordingly. Lastly, explainability plays a significant role, involving the provision of clear explanations of the system’s behavior, rationale, and limitations in order to improve user trust and acceptance of the system.

If the query necessitates optimization, the system’s Prompt Engineered input requests the LLM to identify specific input parameters needed to solve the problem (

Upon receiving the user’s responses (

4.

The program interface then receives the generated code and executes it to solve the optimization problem (

After obtaining the solution, the LLM explains the results, such as the optimal charging schedule and associated cost savings (

7a, 7b).

3.1
Optimization autoformalism

A general optimization problem can be written as:

\displaystyle\text{minimize}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}

\displaystyle f(x;\theta)

(
P(\theta)
)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle g_{i}(x;\theta)\leq 0,\quad i=1,2,\dots,m

\displaystyle h_{j}(x;\theta)=0,\quad j=1,2,\dots,n,

where we seek to minimize the objective function
f(x;\theta)
subject to a set of inequality constraints

x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the decision variable and
\theta

P(\theta)

\theta

This not only conserves time but also facilitates the optimization of intricate energy systems and boosts user engagement by providing real-time feedback and insights.

The human-guided autoformalism proposed in this study is a novel approach for automatically translating natural language task specifications to optimization instances. However, this approach grapples with issues such as ambiguity, incompleteness, and user-specific preferences. 3.1.1
Optimization formulation

The first step in the optimization process is to identify the key components of the problem, such as the objective function, decision variables, and constraints, after the user provides a task description. Let [T] denote the task provided by the user (e.g., “help to optimize my EV charging schedule to minimize cost”). We have experimented with two approaches:

•

Approach 1: We inquire, “What is the [A] in [T] formulated as an optimization problem?”, where [A] can be “objective function”, “decision variable”, or “constraints”. Once these elements are identified, we request users to specify parameters in these elements, thereby translating the query into an optimization instance.

•

Approach 2: We initially prompt the LLM with “[T]. Identify the most important 5 parameters.”. Subsequently, we present the task [T] along with user inputs to the LLM to formulate an optimization instance.

Interestingly, the first approach tends to underperform, while the second approach typically results in the LLM accurately identifying the most relevant parameters. This discrepancy can be ascribed to the training methodology and information processing mechanisms of LLMs like GPT-4. LLMs are trained on a broad spectrum of data and not exclusively on optimization problems. Thus, when directly prompted to formulate specific components of an optimization problem, they might not yield the most accurate or helpful responses due to their generalist nature. The second approach, however, breaks down the task into smaller, digestible parts (identifying the top 5 parameters), which corresponds well with the operational mode of LLMs. LLMs excel when the tasks are simplified, and they can focus on singular concepts at a time. Moreover, the initial identification of crucial parameters provides a context to the LLM. With a grasp on the vital factors, the LLM can use this context to generate more accurate and pertinent responses while formulating the optimization problem. In contrast, without this contextual framework (in the first approach), the LLM might struggle to pinpoint and focus on the pivotal aspects of the problem. This is in line with recent approaches
[
WWS+22b
,
YYZ+23
]
. Furthermore, a direct request to formulate an optimization problem component might leave room for ambiguity and misinterpretation, owing to the complexity of such tasks. By identifying key parameters initially, this ambiguity can be significantly reduced or even eliminated.

Upon pinpointing the essential parameters of the optimization problem, the subsequent step involves transforming the natural language query into a computational instance using the prompt: ”Write a Python code using [lib] to solve this optimization problem,” where [lib] could be either CVXPY or SCIPY. This transformation translates the query into code that formats the optimization problem in a standard format interpretable by existing libraries such as CVXPY
[
DB16
]
or SCIPY
[
VGO+20
]
. To discern the superior library, we assessed each package based on the number of accurate, error-free codes generated. Generally, we found that translating into CVXPY code yields a success rate ranging from 51% to 80%, whereas SCIPY showcases an impressive 71% to 100% success rate (refer to Fig.
2
for more details). We attribute this performance disparity to SCIPY’s broader acceptance and longer existence in the Python community compared to CVXPY, which implies a greater availability of SCIPY-related content online that the model could have leveraged during training. Consequently, the LLM might have encountered more optimization problem instances solved using SCIPY than with CVXPY in its training data.

3.1.2
Solving an optimization and debugging

To address the limitations of LLMs in nonlinear reasoning
[
PBG21
,
RBC+21
]
, we have enabled the model to interact with an external Python program execution to solve optimization tasks. A vital step in interfacing with the solver involves extracting code from the LLM’s output. To accomplish this, we deploy regular expressions to isolate the relevant code block within the text. Regular expressions, as a pattern-matching language, facilitate the search for specific patterns within the text. We prompt the LLM to encapsulate the code block with a unique delimiter (like triple backticks or a phrase like “the code starts/ends here”), which we can then search for, extracting the enclosed text. The extracted text undergoes further processing to ensure its validity and compatibility with the optimization solver. This method, we found, can extract code reliably, without necessitating a large dataset of labeled examples, unlike when training a machine learning model to identify code blocks
[
IKCZ16
]
.

During our development process, we encountered an important issue: the code generated by the LLM occasionally triggers errors during execution. We have classified these errors into two categories: erroneous translation into an optimization problem and syntactic bugs in an otherwise correctly translated optimization problem.
3
3

3

Errors can also arise due to the limitations of the optimization solvers employed to solve the generated optimization problem. Regardless of the syntactical correctness of the generated code, the solver might fail to find a feasible or optimal solution due to the problem’s complexity or other constraints. However, since most energy problems involve linear or quadratic convex optimization, we presume that such errors are comparatively infrequent. We do not classify infeasibility as an error.
The first category of errors may result from ambiguity, incomplete information, or inaccurate identification of hyperparameters. The second category can often be traced back to the limitations of LLM’s natural language processing capabilities in accurately capturing the specific syntax required by the solver.

Figure 4
:
Illustration of the process of solving the optimization problem and debugging the code generated by the LLM. The flow adheres to the

To rectify the first type of error, which arises from inaccurate translations, our primary reliance is on user interaction and clarification of the formulated optimization (i.e., optimization auto-informalism, as detailed in the following subsection). For the second type of error, we resort to an automated process. Initially, we pinpoint the error message or unanticipated behavior identified by the solver, and this information is fed into the LLM to isolate relevant code snippets or whole functions. The LLM is then employed to generate potential remedies for the identified issue. Subsequently, we assess the proposed solutions in a new iteration to determine if they ameliorate the problem. If they fail, we persist with the process until a suitable solution emerges. This procedure is graphically represented in Fig.
4
. Our findings indicate that taking more than one sample before commencing the debugging process often results in fewer queries to the LLM (as seen in the multiple code sample blocks in Fig.
6
). Moreover, potential optimization codes can be tested concurrently to expedite the solution-finding process. In instances where all code results in execution errors, we can revert to the debugging process, again running it in parallel. By adhering to this procedure, we’ve been successful in resolving the majority of the errors encountered during our experiments.

3.1.3
Optimization auto-informalism

Upon discovering the optimal solution, it is the responsibility of the LLM to articulate the results in a comprehensible, natural language format. This system is designed to provide detailed explanations of the optimal solution, alongside any constraints or preferences factored in during the optimization process (refer to Steps 7a and 7b in Fig.
3
). This approach, aptly termed auto-informalism, dovetails with autoformalism by offering intuitive insight into the optimization outcomes.

In order to generate an explanation of the optimization code, we prompt the LLM with the generated and revised code using a simple request: “Please explain the following code.” We then delve deeper, seeking clarification on the decision variables, objective function, and constraints implicated in the optimization problem.
