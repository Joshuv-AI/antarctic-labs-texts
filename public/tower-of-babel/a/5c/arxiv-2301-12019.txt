A Greedy Sensor Selection Algorithm for Hyperparameterized Linear Bayesian Inverse Problems
Nicole Aretz, Peng Chen, Denise D. Degen, Karen Veroy

Abstract
We consider optimal sensor placement for a family of linear Bayesian inverse problems characterized by a deterministic hyper-parameter. The hyper-parameter describes distinct configurations in which measurements can be taken of the observed physical system. To optimally reduce the uncertainty in the system’s model with a single set of sensors, the initial sensor placement needs to account for the non-linear state changes of all admissible configurations. We address this requirement through an observability coefficient which links the posteriors’ uncertainties directly to the choice of sensors. We propose a greedy sensor selection algorithm to iteratively improve the observability coefficient for all configurations through orthogonal matching pursuit. The algorithm allows explicitly correlated noise models even for large sets of candidate sensors, and remains computationally efficient for high-dimensional forward models through model order reduction. We demonstrate our approach on a large-scale geophysical model of the Perth Basin, and provide numerical studies regarding optimality and scalability with regard to classic optimal experimental design utility functions.

In this paper, we consider inverse problem settings, in which a deterministic hyper-parameter describes anticipated system configurations such as material properties or loading conditions.
Each configuration changes the model non-linearly, so we obtain a
family
of possible posterior distributions for any measurement data.
Supposing data can only be obtained with a single set of sensors regardless of the system’s configuration, the optimal experimental design task becomes to reduce the uncertainty in each posterior uniformly over all hyper-parameters.
This task is challenging for high-dimensional models since 1) each configuration requires its own computationally expensive model solve, and 2) for large sets of admissible measurements, the comparison between sensors requires the inversion of the associated, possibly dense noise covariance matrix.
By building upon
[
6
]
, this paper addresses both challenges and proposes in detail a sensor selection algorithm that remains efficient even for correlated noise models.

The main contributions are as follows:
First, we identify an observability coefficient as a link between the sensor choice and the maximum eigenvalue of each posterior distribution. We also provide an analysis of its sensitivity to model approximations.
Second, we decompose the noise covariance matrix for any observation operator to allow fast computation of the observability gain under expansion with additional sensors.
Third, we propose a sensor selection algorithm that iteratively constructs an observation operator from a large set of sensors to increase the observability coefficient over all hyper-parameters.
The algorithm is applicable to correlated noise models, and requires, through the efficient use of model order reduction techniques, only a
single
full-order model evaluation per selected sensor.

While the main idea and derivation of the observability coefficient are similar to

Moreover, the proposed method is tested using a large-scale geophysical model of the Perth Basin.

We consider the problem of identifying unknown states

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}(\theta)\approx\left[\ell_{1}(x_{\rm{true}}(\theta)),\dots,\ell_{K}(x_{\rm{true}}(\theta))\right]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}.

The measurements are obtained by a set of

Hyper-parameterized forward model

We consider the unknown state

\displaystyle\forall~\theta\in\mathcal{P}.

(1)

Following a probabilistic approach to inverse problems, we express the initial uncertainty in
\mathbf{u}_{\rm{true}}=\mathbf{u}_{\rm{true}}(\theta)
of any

in configuration
\theta

with Gaussian prior

\mathbf{u}_{\rm{pr}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

The latter defines the inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}
and its induced norm

\displaystyle\left<\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right>_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}:=\mathbf{u}^{T}\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{u}},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}^{2}:=\left<\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\right>_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}},

\displaystyle\forall~\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}.

(2)

With these definitions, the probability density function for

is

\displaystyle\pi_{\rm{pr}}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{M}\det\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{\rm{pr}}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}^{2}\right).

For simplicity, we assume

to be independent realizations of

such that we may consider the same prior for all

without accounting for a possible history of measurements at different configurations.

Sensor library and noise model

For taking measurements of the unknown states
\{x_{\rm{true}}(\theta)\}_{\theta}
, we call any linear functional
\ell\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
a
sensor
, and its application to a state

\ell(x)\in\mathbb{R}
.
We model experimental measurements

where
\varepsilon_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\text{{cov}}(\varepsilon_{\ell},\varepsilon_{\ell}))

We permit noise in different sensor measurements to be correlated with a known covariance function

In a slight overload of notation, we write
\text{{cov}}:\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\text{{cov}}(\ell_{i},\ell_{j}):=\text{{cov}}(\varepsilon_{\ell_{i}},\varepsilon_{\ell_{j}})
as a symmetric bilinear form over the sensor library.
Any ordered subset

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle:=\left[\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{K}\right]^{T}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K},

\displaystyle Lx:=\left[\ell_{1}(x),\dots,\ell_{K}(x)\right]^{T}.

The experimental measurements of

have the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}=\left[\ell_{1}(x_{\rm{true}})+\varepsilon_{\ell_{1}},\dots,\ell_{K}(x_{\rm{true}})+\varepsilon_{\ell_{K}}\right]^{T}=Lx_{\rm{true}}+\varepsilon\qquad\text{with}\qquad\varepsilon=\left[\varepsilon_{\ell_{1}},\dots,\varepsilon_{\ell_{K}}\right]^{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{L}),

(3)

where
\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{L}
is the noise covariance matrix defined through

\displaystyle\Sigma_{L}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K},\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\left[\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{L}\right]_{i,j}:=\text{{cov}}(\ell_{j},\ell_{i})=\text{{cov}}(\varepsilon_{\ell_{j}},\varepsilon_{\ell_{i}})

(4)

with an auxiliary scaling parameter
3
3

3

We introduce

\displaystyle\forall~\mathbf{d},\tilde{\mathbf{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}.

(5)

Measured with respect to this norm, the largest observation of any (normalized) state is thus

\displaystyle\gamma_{L}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\left\|x\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}=1}\left\|Lx\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\left\|Lx\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}}{\left\|x\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}}.

We show in Section

We also define the

\displaystyle G_{L,\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K},\quad\text{such that}\quad G_{L,\theta}\left(\mathbf{u}\right):=Lx_{\theta}(\mathbf{u}).

With the assumptions above – in particular the linearity and uniform continuity (

Posterior distribution

Once noisy measurement data
\mathbf{d}\approx Lx_{\rm{true}}(\theta)
is available, Bayes’ theorem yields the posterior probability density function as

\pi_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}(\mathbf{u}~|~\mathbf{d})=\frac{1}{Z(\theta)}\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\|G_{L,\theta}\left(\mathbf{u}\right)-\mathbf{d}\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{\rm{pr}}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}^{2}\right)\propto\pi_{\rm{pr}}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\Phi_{L}\left(\mathbf{d}~\big|~\mathbf{u},\theta\right),

(8)

with normalization constant

Z(\theta):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\|G_{L,\theta}\left(\mathbf{u}\right)-\mathbf{d}\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}^{2}\right)~d\mu_{\rm{pr}}.

Due to the linearity of the parameter-to-observable map, the posterior measure
\mu_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
is a Gaussian

\displaystyle\mu_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{u}_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}(\mathbf{d}),\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta})

with known (c.f.
[

) mean and covariance matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}(\mathbf{d})=\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}\left(\textstyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{G}_{L,\theta}^{T}\Sigma_{L}^{-1}\mathbf{d}+\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{\rm{pr}}\right)

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

(9)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}=\left(\textstyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{G}_{L,\theta}^{T}\Sigma_{L}^{-1}\mathbf{G}_{L,\theta}+\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}.

(10)

The posterior
\mu_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
thus depends not only on the choice of sensors, but also on the configuration
\theta
under which their measurements were obtained.
Therefore, to decrease the uncertainty in all possible posteriors with a single,
\theta

, the construction of

should account for all admissible configurations

under which

may be observed.

The linearity of

However, in combination with the hyper-parameter

The Observability Coefficient

In this section, we characterize how the choice of sensors in the observation operator
L
and its associated noise covariance matrix
\Sigma_{L}
influence the uncertainty in the posteriors
\mu_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
,
\theta\in\mathcal{P}
.
We identify an observability coefficient that bounds the eigenvalues of the posterior covariance matrices
\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
,
\theta\in\mathcal{P}
with respect to
L
, and facilitates the sensor selection algorithm presented in Section
4

3.1
Eigenvalues of the Posterior Covariance Matrix

The uncertainty in the posterior
\pi_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
for any configuration
\theta\in\mathcal{P}
is uniquely characterized by the posterior covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
, which is in turn connected to the observation operator
L
through the parameter-to-observable map
G_{L,\theta}
and the noise covariance matrix
\Sigma_{L}
.
To measure the uncertainty in
\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
, the optimal experimental design literature suggests a variety of different utility functions to be minimized over
L
in order to optimize the sensor choice.
Many of these utility functions can be expressed in terms of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{L}^{\theta,1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{L}^{\theta,M}>0
of
\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
, e.g.,

A-OED:

\displaystyle\text{trace}(\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_{L}^{\theta,m}

(mean variance)

D-OED:

\displaystyle\det(\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta})=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_{L}^{\theta,m}

(volume)

E-OED:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta})=\lambda_{L}^{\theta,1}

(spectral radius).

In practice, the choice of the utility function is dictated by the application.
In E-optimal experimental design, for instance, posteriors whose uncertainty ellipsoids stretch out into any one direction are avoided, whereas D-OED minimizes the overall volume of the uncertainty ellipsoid regardless of the uncertainty in any one parameter direction.
We refer to
[
3
]
for a detailed introduction and other optimal experimental design criteria.

Considering the hyper-parameterized setting where each configuration
\theta
influences the posterior uncertainty, we seek to choose a single observation operator
L
such that the selected utility function remains small for
all
configurations
\theta\in\mathcal{P}
, e.g., for E-OED, minimizing

\displaystyle\min_{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{K}\in\mathcal{L}}~\max_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}}~\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta})\quad\text{such that}\quad L=\left[\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{K}\right]^{T}

guarantees that the longest axis of each posterior covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
for any
\theta\in\mathcal{P}
has the same guaranteed upper bound.
The difficulty here is that the minimization over
\mathcal{P}
necessitates repeated, cost-intensive model evaluations to compute the utility function for many different configurations
\theta
.
In the following, we therefore introduce an upper bound to the posterior eigenvalues that can be optimized through an observability criterion with far fewer model solves.
The bound’s optimization indirectly reduces the different utility functions through the posterior eigenvalues.

Recalling that
\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
is symmetric positive definite, let
\{\psi_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
be an orthonormal eigenvector basis of
\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}
, i.e.
\psi_{m}^{T}\psi_{n}=\delta_{m,n}
and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}\psi_{m}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{L}^{\theta,m}\psi_{m}

\displaystyle m=1,\dots,M.

(11)

Using the representation (
10
), any eigenvalue
\lambda_{L}^{\theta,m}
can be written in the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{L}^{\theta,m}}=\psi_{m}^{T}\left[\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}\right]^{-1}\psi_{m}=\psi_{m}^{T}\left[\textstyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{G}_{L,\theta}^{T}\Sigma_{L}^{-1}\mathbf{G}_{L,\theta}+\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}\right]\psi_{m}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left\|G_{L,\theta}\left(\psi_{m}\right)\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}^{2}+\|\psi_{m}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}^{2}.

(12)

Since
\psi_{m}
depends implicitly on
L
and
\theta
through
(
11
)
, we cannot use this representation directly to optimize over
L
.
To take out the dependency on
\psi_{m}
, we bound
\|\psi_{m}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm{pr}}^{\max}}
in terms of the maximum eigenvalue of the prior covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}
.
Likewise, we define

\displaystyle\beta_{G}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\frac{\left\|G_{L,\theta}\left(\mathbf{u}\right)\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}}=\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\frac{\left\|Lx_{\theta}(\mathbf{u})\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}},

(13)

as the minimum ratio between an observation for a parameter
\mathbf{u}
relative to the prior’s covariance norm.
From (
12
) and (
13
) we obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle\lambda_{L}^{\theta,m}=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{\left\|G_{L,\theta}\left(\psi_{m}\right)\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}^{2}}{\|\psi_{m}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}^{2}}+1\right)^{-1}\|\psi_{m}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}^{-2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\beta_{G}(\theta)^{2}+1\right)^{-1}\lambda_{\rm{pr}}^{\max}.

Geometrically, this bound means that the radius
\lambda_{L}^{\theta,1}
of the outer ball around the posterior uncertainty ellipsoid is smaller than that of the prior uncertainty ellipsoid by at least the factor
\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\beta_{G}(\theta)^{2}+1\right)^{-1}
.
By choosing
L
to maximize
\min_{\theta}\beta_{G}(\theta)
, we therefore minimize this outer ball containing all uncertainty ellipsoids (i.e., for any
\theta\in\mathcal{P}
).
As expected, the influence of
L
is strongest when the measurement noise is small such that data can be trusted (
\sigma^{2}\ll 1
), and diminishes with increasing noise levels (
\sigma^{2}\gg 1
).

3.2
Parameter Restriction

An essential property of
\beta_{G}(\theta)
is that
\beta_{G}(\theta)=0
if
K<M
, i.e., the number of sensors in
L
is smaller than the number of parameter dimensions.
In this case,
\beta_{G}(\theta)
cannot distinguish between sensors during the first
M-1
steps of an iterative algorithm, or in general when less than a total of
M
sensors are supposed to be chosen.
For medium-dimensional parameter spaces (
M\in\mathcal{O}(10)
), we mitigate this issue by restricting
\mathbf{u}
to the subspace
\text{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{\min\{K,M\}}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
spanned by the first
\min\{K,M\}
eigenvectors of
\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}
corresponding to its largest eigenvalues, i.e., the subspace with the largest prior uncertainty.
For high-dimensional parameter spaces or when the model
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
has a non-trivial null-space, we bound
\beta_{G}(\theta)
further

\displaystyle\beta_{G}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\frac{\left\|Lx_{\theta}(\mathbf{u})\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}}{\left\|x_{\theta}(\mathbf{u})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}}\frac{\left\|x_{\theta}(\mathbf{u})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}}\geq\inf_{x\in\mathcal{W}_{\theta}}\frac{\left\|Lx\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}}{\left\|x\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}}\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\frac{\left\|x_{\theta}(\mathbf{u})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}}=\beta_{L|\mathcal{W}}(\theta)~\underline{\eta}(\theta)

(14)

where we define the linear space
\mathcal{W}_{\theta}
of all achievable states

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\theta}:=\{x_{\theta}(\mathbf{u})\in\mathcal{X}:~\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\}

and the coefficients

\displaystyle\beta_{L|\mathcal{W}}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{W}_{\theta}}\frac{\left\|Lx\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}}{\left\|x\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}},

\displaystyle\underline{\eta}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\frac{\left\|x_{\theta}(\mathbf{u})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}}.

(15)

The value of
\underline{\eta}(\theta)
describes the minimal state change that a parameter
\mathbf{u}
can achieve relative to its prior-induced norm
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\Sigma_{\rm{pr}}^{-1}}
.
It can filter out parameter directions that have little influence on the states
x_{\theta}(\mathbf{u})

In contrast, the observability coefficient

4.1
Cholesky decomposition

The covariance function
cov
connects an observation operator
L
to its observability coefficients
\beta_{G}(\theta)
and
\beta_{L|\mathcal{W}}(\theta)
through the noise covariance matrix
\Sigma_{L}
.
Its inverse enters the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\Sigma_{L}^{-1}}
and the posterior covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\rm{post}}^{L,\theta}

The inversion poses a challenge when the noise is correlated, i.e., when
\Sigma_{L}
is not diagonal, as even the expansion of
L
with a single sensor
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
changes each entry of
\Sigma_{L}^{-1}

In naive computations of the observability coefficients and the posterior covariance matrix, this leads to
M

each time the observation operator is expanded.
In the following, we therefore expound on how
\Sigma_{L}^{-1}
changes under expansion of
L
to exploit its structure when comparing potential sensor choices.

Suppose

to

\displaystyle:=\left[\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{K},\ell\right]^{T}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K+1}.

), the noise covariance matrix

of the expanded operator

has the form

\displaystyle\Sigma_{[L,\ell]}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{L}&\mathbf{v}_{L,\ell}\\

for the original observation operator

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{C}_{L}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{L,\ell},

\displaystyle v_{\ell,\ell}

\displaystyle c_{\ell,\ell}

Remark 7
.

Specifically, we follow a greedy approach where, in iteration

Remark 8
.

Since the computation of
\tilde{\beta}_{G}(\theta)
requires as many reduced-order model solves as needed for the posterior covariance matrix over the surrogate model, it is possible to directly target an (approximated) optimal experimental design utility function in the greedy step in place of

without major concessions in the computational efficiency.
The orthogonal matching pursuit step can then still be performed for the “worst-case" parameter with only one full-order model solve, though its benefit for the utility function should be evaluated carefully.

Runtime

Assuming the dominating computational restriction is the model evaluation to solve for
x_{\theta}(\mathbf{u})
– as is usually the case for partial differential equation models – then the runtime of each iteration in Algorithm
4
is determined by one full-order model evaluation, and
K_{\mathcal{L}}

Compared to computed the posterior covariance matrix for the chosen configuration, the orthogonal matching pursuit step saves
N-1

The other main factor in the runtime of Algorithm

K_{\mathcal{L}}

The parameter dimension

not only enters as a scaling factor, but also affects the cost of the reduced-order model itself since larger values of

16

In turn, the computational cost of the reduced-order model indicates how large
\Xi_{\rm{train}}
may be chosen for a given computational budget.
While some cost can be saved through adaptive training sets and models, overall, this connection to

stresses the need for an adequate initial parameter reduction as discussed in Section

They share many structural similarities:

1.

Depth:

Despite the availability of 5 measurement depths, sensors have only been chosen on the lowest and the upmost layers with 5 sensors each.
The lower sensors were chosen first (with one exception, sensor 3 in
\theta_{\rm{ref}}
-training), presumably because the lower layer is closer to the uncertain Neumann boundary condition and therefore yields larger measurement values.

2.

Pairing

Each sensor on the lowest layer has a counterpart on the upmost layer that has almost the same position on the horizontal plane.
This pairing targets noise sensitivity: With the prescribed error covariance function, the noise in two measurements is increasingly correlated the closer the measurements lie horizontally, independent of their depth coordinate.
Choosing a reference measurement near the zero-Dirichlet boundary at the surface helps filter out noise terms in the lower measurement.

3.

Organization

On each layer, the sensors are spread out evenly and approximately aligned in 3 rows and 3 columns.
The alignment helps distinguish between the constant, linear, and quadratic parts of the uncertain Neumann flux function in north-south and east-west directions.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we analyzed the connection between the observation operator and the eigenvalues of the posterior covariance matrix in the inference of an uncertain parameter via Bayesian inversion for a linear, hyper-parameterized forward model.
We identified an observability coefficient whose maximization decreases the uncertainty in the posterior probability distribution for all hyper-parameters.
To this end, we proposed a sensor selection algorithm that expands an observation operator iteratively to guarantee a uniformly large observability coefficient for all hyper-parameters.
Computational feasibility is retained through a reduced-order model in the greedy step and an orthogonal matching pursuit search for the next sensor that only requires a single full-order model evaluation.
The validity of the approach was demonstrated on a large-scale heat conduction problem over a section of the Perth Basin in Western Australia.
Future extensions of this work are planned to address 1) high-dimensional parameter spaces through parameter reduction techniques, 2) the combination with the parameterized-background data-weak
inf-sup
-criterion to inform sensors by functionalanalytic means in addition to the noise covariance, and 3) the expansion to non-linear models through a Laplace approximation.
