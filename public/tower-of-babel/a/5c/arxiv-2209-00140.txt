Abstract
. An essential cover of the vertices of the n n -cube { 0 , 1 } n \{0,1\}^{n} by hyperplanes is a minimal covering where no hyperplane is redundant and every variable appears in the equation of at least one hyperplane. Linial and Radhakrishnan gave a construction of an essential cover with ⌈ n 2 ⌉ + 1 \lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+1 hyperplanes and showed that Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(\sqrt{n}) hyperplanes are required. Recently, Yehuda and Yehudayoff improved the lower bound by showing that any essential cover of the n n -cube contains at least Ω ⁡ ( n 0.52 ) \Omega(n^{0.52}) hyperplanes. In this paper, building on the method of Yehuda and Yehudayoff, we prove that Ω ⁡ ( n 5 / 9 ( log ⁡ n ) 4 / 9 ) \Omega\left(\frac{n^{5/9}}{(\log n)^{4/9}}\right) hyperplanes are needed.



1.
Introduction

An
essential cover
of the vertices of the
n
-cube
\{0,1\}^{n}
by hyperplanes is a minimal covering where every variable appears in the equation of at least one hyperplane and no hyperplane is redundant.
Linial and Radhakrishnan
[
7
]
introduced this notion in 2005, which can be formally defined as follows.
Throughout this paper,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the scalar product and
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
A collection of
k
hyperplanes with equations
\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
, where
x,v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i\in[k]
, forms an
essential cover
of the
n
-cube
\{0,1\}^{n}
if

(E1)

For every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
for some
i\in[k]
;

(E2)

For every
j\in[n]
, there exists
i\in[k]
such that
v_{i}
satisfies
v_{ij}\neq 0
;

(E3)

For every
i\in[k]
, there is a vertex
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
but
\langle v_{j},x\rangle\neq\mu_{j}
for all
j\neq i
.

In the same paper, Linial and Radhakrishnan showed that any essential cover
has
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
hyperplanes. However, this bound is far from the best-known constructions, which were also obtained in
[
7
]
.
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, they constructed essential covers of size
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+1
. When
n
is even, one of the covers is given by the hyperplanes defined by the equations
x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}=n/2
and
x_{2i-1}-x_{2i}=0
, for
i\in[n/2]
.
This construction can also be adapted for odd
n
.
Even though no explicit conjecture on the size of the smallest essential cover was made by Linial and Radhakrishnan,
\lceil n/2\rceil+1
hyperplanes are likely to be needed. In 2013, Saxton
[
9
]
formulated the following conjecture concerning permanents of matrices that, if true, would imply that every essential cover has at least
\lceil n/2\rceil+1
hyperplanes.

Conjecture 1

(Saxton
[
9
]
)
.

If
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a matrix such that for every
J\subset[m]
we have
\left|\bigcup_{j\in J}\{i\in[n]:B_{ij}\neq 0\}\right|\geq 2|J|-1
, then
B
contains an
m\times m
submatrix with non-zero permanent.

The methods employed by Linial and Radhakrishnan
[
7
]
to show the
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
bound are purely algebraic. They applied a result of Alon and Füredi
[
1
]
to show that
if the hyperplanes with equations
\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
, for
i\in[k]
, form an essential cover of the
n
-cube then
|\textup{supp}(v_{i})|<2k
for every
i\in[k]
. Here, for a vector
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by
\textup{supp}(v_{i}):=\{j\in[n]:v_{ij}\neq 0\}
the
support
of
v_{i}
, i.e, the indices of the non-zero entries of
v_{i}
.
The
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
bound immediately follows from this result combined with property (E2), which is equivalent to
|\cup_{i}\textup{supp}(v_{i})|\geq n
.
In
[
7
]
, Linial and Radhakrishnan also applied the Littlewood–Offord Lemma
[
8
]
to obtain an alternative proof for a weaker result that any essential cover has
\Omega(n^{1/3})
hyperplanes.
Although applying the Littlewood–Offord Lemma crudely gives a weaker bound, it gives some insight into the role of the support of the vectors in an essential cover.

The Littlewood–Offord Lemma states that a hyperplane with equation
\langle v,x\rangle=\mu
contains at most
2^{n}/\sqrt{|\textup{supp}(v)|}
points of the
n
-cube
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Thus, when the support is large, the number of vertices covered by the hyperplane is small.
Let us briefly explain the idea of how this is used to show that every essential cover has
\Omega(n^{1/3})
hyperplanes.
Suppose that the hyperplanes with equations
\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
, for
i\in[k]
, form an essential cover of the
n
-cube.
As every variable appears in the equation of at least one hyperplane,
|\textup{supp}(v_{i})|
is at least
n/k
on average.
Thus, on average, every hyperplane covers at most
(k/n)^{1/2}
proportion of the
n
-cube.
If
(k/n)^{1/2}k<1
, then there is an uncovered vertex, and hence we must have
k\geq n^{1/3}
.

Recently, a new approach was introduced by Yehuda and Yehudayoff
[
10
]
to show that any essential cover of the
n
-cube contains
\Omega(n^{0.52})
hyperplanes.
This improves the previous bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
by Linial and Radhakrishnan
[
7
]
.
Their proof applies a lemma of Bang
[
3
]
, which was used to solve Tarski’s plank problem in Euclidean spaces.
Roughly speaking, Bang’s lemma states that for a
k\times k
symmetric matrix
M
with positive diagonal entries and a vector
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, there exists
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{k}
which is far from the hyperplanes
\langle M_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
, for
i\in[k]
.
The idea is to apply Bang’s lemma to
VV^{T}
, where
V
is a properly normalized matrix, whose rows are associated with the vectors of an essential cover.
Then, one would hope to show that the vector
V^{T}\varepsilon
is sufficiently close to some vertex
u\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, but it is far enough from any of the hyperplanes
\langle V_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
.
This implies that
u
itself is far from all hyperplanes
\langle V_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
, for all
i\in[k]
.
When making this argument precise, some issues come along the way. For example, one technical difficulty is that we need to control the column norm of
V
to show that
V^{T}\varepsilon
is close to some vertex of
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

The main contribution of this paper is an improvement on the lower bound on the number of hyperplanes needed in an essential cover of the
n
-cube.
The main idea behind the proof is to combine the proof strategy of Yehuda and Yehudayoff
[
10
]
with a stronger result regarding covering systems whose associated matrix has small column norm.

Theorem 2
.

An essential cover of the
n
-cube has
\Omega\left(\frac{n^{5/9}}{(\log n)^{4/9}}\right)
hyperplanes.

Remark.
Yehuda and Yehudayoff’s proof
[
10
]
gives a lower bound of
n^{12/23-o(1)}\approx n^{0.5217-o(1)}
, while Theorem
2
gives the lower bound of
n^{5/9-o(1)}\geq n^{0.5555}
.

For an essential cover with hyperplane equations
\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
, for
i\in[k]
, denote by
V
the
k\times n
matrix whose
i
-th row is given by
v_{i}
.
We call
V
an
essential
matrix and
Vx=\mu
an
essential covering system
.
To prove Theorem
2
, we suppose for contradiction that there exists an essential covering system
Vx=\mu
, where the number of rows in
V
is
O\left(\frac{n^{5/9}}{(\log n)^{4/9}}\right)
.
Then, we show that there exists a point
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
which is not covered by any of the equations
\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
.
The core of the argument is using the following facts.

•

Rows with large support cannot cover many vertices (c.f. Lemma
3
).

•

Rows corresponding to vectors with
many scales
cannot cover many vertices (c.f. Definition
4
and Lemma
7
).

•

If a set of rows have small column norm, then we can find a vertex far from all hyperplanes (c.f. Lemma
9
). Finding this sparse submatrix is possible because the size of the support of each row is bounded by
2k
(c.f. Lemma
13
).

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we state the Littlewood–Offord anti-concentration inequality and introduce the notion of a vector with many scales (c.f. Definition
4
). In the same section, we also state and show a more refined anti-concentration inequality for vectors with many scales (c.f. Lemma
7
).
In Section
3
, we show that rows with small column norm cannot cover many vertices (c.f. Proposition
11
).
In Section
4
, we prove the structural lemma for the matrix
V
(c.f. Lemma
15
) that will allow us to explore the core idea mentioned above. Finally, in Section
5
, we prove Theorem
2
.
We highlight that the main novelty from the proof in
[
10
]
is Proposition
11
, where we obtain better upper bounds for the probabilities that a randomly selected vertex lies in a hyperplane.

2.
Anti-concentration for vectors

In this section, we introduce two anti-concentration inequalities that are used in the proof of Theorem
2
.
The first is the classical Littlewood–Offord Lemma
[
8
]
, which was proved by Erdős
[
5
]
using Sperner’s Theorem.
The second is an exponential anti-concentration bound of Yehuda–Yehudayoff
[
11
]
for vectors with ‘nearly exponential decay’.
These are called vectors with
many scales
.

For
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote
\textup{supp}(v):=\{i\in[n]:v_{i}\neq 0\}
and

\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}
the probability space generated by taking
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
uniformly at random.

Lemma 3

(Littlewood–Offord)
.

For every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}\left(\langle x,v\rangle=a\right)\leq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{|\textup{supp}(v)|}}.

The Littlewood–Offord Lemma is tight up to a multiplicative constant. If
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, then the event

\langle x,v\rangle=\mathbb{E}(\langle x,v\rangle)\pm 4\sqrt{|\textup{supp}(v)|}

occurs with constant probability. By the pigeonhole principle, it follows that there exists some
a\in\mathbb{Z}
for which
\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}\left(\langle x,v\rangle=a\right)=\Omega(|\textup{supp}(v)|^{-\frac{1}{2}})
.
However, when
v
has ‘nearly exponential decay’, this bound can be considerably improved.
For example, when
v=(1,2,\ldots,2^{n-1})
, then
\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}\left(\langle x,v\rangle=a\right)\leq 2^{-n}
.
More generally, when the coordinates of
v
decay nearly exponentially, then we expect an exponential-type anti-concentration bound.
Following
[
11
]
, we introduce the notion of a vector with
many scales
, which formalizes this notion of nearly exponential decay between the coordinates of a vector.

For
A\subseteq[n]
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denote by
v^{A}
the vector
v
restricted to the set
A
.
For
p\geq 1
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\|v\|_{p}:=(\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}^{p})^{1/p}
be the
\ell_{p}
-norm of
v
.

Definition 4
.

The vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
S
scales if
there exists a partition
[n]=P_{1}\cup\ldots\cup P_{S}
for which
v^{(s)}=v^{P_{s}}
satisfies

\|v^{(s)}\|_{2}\geq C_{1}\|v^{(s+1)}\|_{2}

for every
s<S
,
where
C_{1}=4C_{0}^{2}
and
C_{0}=4.706
.
The smallest scale of
v
with respect to the partition
P_{1}\cup\ldots\cup P_{S}
is defined to be
v^{(S)}
and the size of the smallest scale is
\|v^{(S)}\|_{2}
.

Note that the definition of having
S
scales depends on the constant
C_{1}=4\cdot(4.706)^{2}
.
Let us briefly explain the motivation behind this choice.
For a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\ell_{2}
-norm 1, let

z_{r}=z_{r}(x)=\langle x^{(r)},v^{(r)}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i\in P_{r}}v_{i},

where
x^{(r)}=x^{P_{r}}
.
Using the Payley–Zygmund inequality (c.f. Lemma
5
) and the second moment method, Yehuda and Yehudayoff
[
11
]
showed that the event

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\|v^{(r)}\|_{2}}{C_{0}}\leq|z_{r}|\leq C_{0}\|v^{(r)}\|_{2}

occurs with probability at least
1/C_{0}
for each
r\in[S]
, see Claim
6
.
Let
E
be the set of indices
r
for which (
1
) holds.
Using Chernoff’s inequality, we can show that with high probability
|E|=\Omega(S)
.
Moreover, if
v
is a vector with
S
scales, then for
r,s\in E
and
r<s
we have

\displaystyle|z_{r}|\geq\frac{\|v^{(r)}\|_{2}}{C_{0}}\geq 4C_{0}\|v^{(s)}\|_{2}\geq 4|z_{s}|.

Then
C_{1}
is chosen to be
4C_{0}^{2}
since it implies that the sequence
(|z_{r}|)_{r\in E}
decay exponentially.
Once we have this property, we can show that the anti-concentration bound is
\exp(-\Omega(S))
.

Before stating the anti-concentration bound of Yehuda and Yehudayoff
[
11
]
for vectors with
S
scales,
we state the Payley–Zygmund inequality.
For self-completeness, we include its proof.

Lemma 5

(Payley–Zygmund inequality)
.

Let
\theta\in[0,1]
and
Z
be a non-negative random variable with finite variance. Then,

\mathbb{P}\big(Z>\theta\E(Z)\big)\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\frac{\E(Z)^{2}}{\E(Z^{2})}.

Proof.

The expectation of
Z
can be written as

\E(Z)=\E\big(Z\cdot 1_{\{Z\leq\theta\E(Z)\}}\big)+\E\big(Z\cdot 1_{\{Z>\theta\E(Z)\}}\big).

The first term of this sum is bounded by

\E\big(Z\cdot 1_{\{Z\leq\theta\E(Z)\}}\big)\leq\theta\E(Z).

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the second term is bounded by

\E\big(Z\cdot 1_{\{Z>\theta\E(Z)\}}\big)\leq\left(\E(Z^{2})\right)^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(Z>\theta\E(Z)\big)^{1/2}.

Combining these bounds, we conclude that

\E(Z)\leq\theta\E(Z)+\left(\E(Z^{2})\right)^{1/2}\mathbb{P}\big(Z>\theta\E(Z)\big)^{1/2}
. This implies

\mathbb{P}\big(Z>\theta\E(Z)\big)\geq(1-\theta)^{2}\frac{\E(Z)^{2}}{\E(Z^{2})}.\qed

Let
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be chosen uniformly at random.
The next claim states that if
\|v\|_{2}=1
, then
\langle x,v\rangle
is close to
\E(\langle x,v\rangle)
with probability bounded away from
0
.
As we do not have any assumption on
\textup{supp}(v)
, observe that we cannot hope to have a probability close to 1.

Claim 6

(Yehuda–Yehudayoff
[
11
]
)
.

If
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
\ell_{2}
-norm
\|v\|_{2}=1
, then for every
C_{0}\geq 4.706
we have that

\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{C_{0}}\leq\Big|\langle x,v\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\Big|\leq C_{0}\right)\geq\frac{1}{C_{0}}.

Proof.

Define
Z=\big(\langle x,v\rangle-\E(\langle x,v\rangle)\big)^{2}
.
Let us calculate the expected value of
4Z
.
Note that

4Z=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\left(2x_{i}-1\right)\right)^{2}.

As
\E(2x_{i}-1)=0
and
\E\big((2x_{i}-1)^{2}\big)=1
, we have

\E(4Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{2}=1.

Now let us calculate the second moment of
4Z
.
As
\E(2x_{i}-1)=\E\big((2x_{i}-1)^{3}\big)=0
and
\E\big((2x_{i}-1)^{2}\big)=\E\big((2x_{i}-1)^{4}\big)=1
, we have

\E\big((4Z)^{2}\big)=\E\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}(2x_{i}-1)\right)^{4}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{4}+6\sum_{i\neq j}v_{i}^{2}v_{j}^{2}\leq 3\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}=3.

Let
C\geq 4.706
. As
\E(4Z)=1
, by Markov’s inequality we have

\mathbb{P}\left(4Z\geq 4C^{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{4C^{2}}.

As
\E(4Z)=1
and
\E\big((4Z)^{2}\big)\leq 3
, by the Payley-Zygmond inequality (c.f. Lemma
5
) we have

\mathbb{P}\left(4Z>\dfrac{4}{C^{2}}\right)\geq\dfrac{1}{3}\left(1-\dfrac{4}{C^{2}}\right)^{2}.

Combining the previous inequalities, it follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(\dfrac{1}{C^{2}}<Z<C^{2}\right)\geq\dfrac{1}{3}\left(1-\dfrac{4}{C^{2}}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{4C^{2}}.

The last expression is at least
C^{-1}
whenever
C\geq 4.706
.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove the anti-concentration bound of Yehuda and Yehudayoff
[
11
]
for vectors with
S
scales.
Throughout this paper, logarithms are in base

Lemma 7

(Yehuda–Yehudayoff
[
11
]
)
.

There is a constant
C_{2}>1
such that the following holds.
If
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
S
scales and the size of the smallest scale is
\delta>0
,
then for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\geq 2
we have

\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}\big(|\langle x,v\rangle-a|<b\delta\big)<C_{2}\exp\left(-\tfrac{S}{C_{2}}+C_{2}\log(b)\right).

Proof.

Let
[n]=P_{1}\cup\ldots\cup P_{S}
be the partition of
[n]
associated to the scales of
v
.
For
r\in[S]
, define

z_{r}=\langle x^{(r)},v^{(r)}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i\in P_{r}}v_{i},

where
x^{(r)}=x^{P_{r}}
.
Let
E
be the set of indices
r\in[S]
for which the event

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\|v^{(r)}\|_{2}}{C_{0}}\leq|z_{r}|\leq C_{0}\|v^{(r)}\|_{2}

We claim that
(|z_{r}|)_{r\in E}
decreases exponentially and that
|z_{r}|>3b\delta
whenever
r\in E
and
r<S-\log(3b)
.
In fact, note that if
r\in E
and
s>r
(

(3)

|z_{r}|\geq\frac{\|v^{(r)}\|_{2}}{C_{0}}\geq 4C_{0}\|v^{(s)}\|_{2},

This implies that

|z_{r}|\geq 4|z_{s}|\qquad\text{and}\qquad|z_{r}|\geq 4C_{0}\cdot(4C_{0}^{2})^{S-(r+1)}\cdot\delta

.

The second inequality follows from (

.
Now we show that

is large with high probability.
Observe that the indicators
(1_{\{r\in E\}})_{r\in[S]}
are independent from each other.
By Claim

\mathbb{P}\left(r\in E\right)\geq C_{0}^{-1}
, and

Claim 8
.

The probability that (

Proof of Claim

.
Suppose for contradiction that there exist
\varepsilon\neq\varepsilon^{\prime}
, both satisfying (

\varepsilon_{r_{0}}\neq\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r_{0}}
, so
|\varepsilon_{r_{0}}-\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r_{0}}|=2

(6)

\displaystyle 2b\delta>\Big|\sum_{r\in R}(\varepsilon_{r}-\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r})z_{r}\Big|\geq 2|z_{r_{0}}|-\Big|\sum_{r\in R:r>r_{0}}(\varepsilon_{r}-\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r})z_{r}\Big|.

However, since
|z_{r}|\geq 4|z_{s}|
for every
r,s\in R
with
r<s
, we have

(7)

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{r\in R:r>r_{0}}(\varepsilon_{r}-\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r})z_{r}\Big|\leq 2|z_{r_{0}}|\cdot\sum_{i\geq 1}4^{-i}=\frac{2|z_{r_{0}}|}{3}.

By (
6
) and (
7
), we conclude that

2b\delta>\Big|\sum_{r\in R}(\varepsilon_{r}-\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r})z_{r}\Big|\geq\frac{4|z_{r_{0}}|}{3}>4b\delta,

a contradiction.
The last inequality follows from the fact
r_{0}\in R
.
∎

3.
A lemma from convex geometry

In this section, we introduce Bang’s Lemma, which is the main tool of the proof.
This lemma was obtained by Bang
[
3
]
in his proof of the symmetric case of Tarski’s plank problem, as observed by Ball
[
2
]
.
Roughly speaking, it states that for every
k\times k
symmetric matrix
M
with positive diagonal entries and a vector
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, there exists a vector
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
for which
(M\varepsilon)_{t}
is far from
\zeta_{t}
, for all
t\in[k]
.
That is,
\varepsilon
is far from the hyperplanes
\langle M_{i},x\rangle=\zeta_{i}
, where
M_{i}
denotes the
i
-th row of
M
.

Lemma 9

(Bang
[
2
,
3
]
)
.

Let
M
be a
k\times k
symmetric matrix such that
M_{tt}\geq 0
for every
t\in[k]
.
For
\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k}
, there exists
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
such that

(8)

\displaystyle\big|\big(M(\theta\varepsilon)\big)_{t}-\zeta_{t}\big|\geq M_{tt}\theta_{t}

for every
t\in[k]
, where
\theta\varepsilon:=(\theta_{1}\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\varepsilon_{k})
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
be a vector that maximizes the expression

\langle M(\theta\varepsilon),\theta\varepsilon\rangle-2\langle\theta\varepsilon,\zeta\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}M_{ij}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}-2\sum_{i=1}^{k}\theta_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\zeta_{i}.

Let
t\in[k]
and
\varepsilon^{(t)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
be the vector which differs from
\varepsilon
only in the
t
-th coordinate. By the maximality of
\varepsilon
, we have that

(\varepsilon_{t}-\varepsilon^{(t)}_{t})\left[\sum_{i\neq t}M_{it}\theta_{i}\theta_{t}\varepsilon_{i}+\sum_{i\neq t}M_{ti}\theta_{i}\theta_{t}\varepsilon_{i}-2\theta_{t}\zeta_{t}\right]\geq 0.

This is equivalent to

(9)

4\theta_{t}\varepsilon_{t}\left[\sum_{i\neq t}M_{it}\theta_{i}\varepsilon_{i}-\zeta_{t}\right]=4\theta_{t}\varepsilon_{t}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{it}\theta_{i}\varepsilon_{i}-\zeta_{t}-M_{tt}\theta_{t}\varepsilon_{t}\right]\geq 0.

Simplifying the left-hand side of (
8
), we conclude that

\big|\big(M(\theta\varepsilon)\big)_{t}-\zeta_{t}\big|=\left|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}M_{it}\theta_{i}\varepsilon_{i}-\zeta_{t}\right|\geq\varepsilon_{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{it}\theta_{i}\varepsilon_{i}-\zeta_{t}\right)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eq:lem12}}}}{{\geq}}\ M_{tt}\theta_{t}\varepsilon_{t}^{2}=M_{tt}\theta_{t},

for every
t\in[k]
.
∎

Lemma
9
above tells us that we can find a vector from
\{\pm 1\}^{k}
that is far from each of the hyperplanes
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\sum_{j=1}^{k}M_{ij}\theta_{j}x_{j}=\zeta_{i}\right\}
for
i\in[k]
. We will apply Lemma
9
to find a vector
y\in[0,1]^{n}
that is far away from each of the hyperplanes
\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
, for
i\in[k]
. Note that this vector
y
is not necessarily a vertex of the cube
\{0,1\}^{n}
. We define a probability distribution over the
n
-cube
\{0,1\}^{n}
based on the vector
y
which will make it unlikely for a randomly sampled vector to lie in any of the hyperplanes. We use the probabilistic method to conclude, similar to Lemma
3
, that a collection of vectors with a certain structure cannot cover many vertices. This intuition is formalized in Proposition
11
below. For its proof, we make use of the following version of Hoeffding’s inequality (Lemma
10
). We highlight that this stronger inequality allows us to improve the logarithmic term on the number of hyperplanes in an essential cover. The slightly weaker lower bound
\Omega\left(\frac{n^{5/9}}{(\log n)^{2/3}}\right)
could be obtained by applying Bernstein’s inequality
[
4
]
instead.

Lemma 10

(Hoeffding’s inequality
[
6
]
)
.

Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell}
be independent zero mean random variables such that, for every
j\in[\ell]
,
a_{j}\leq z_{j}\leq b_{j}
almost surely. Then, for every
t>0
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j}z_{j}\geq t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{j}(b_{j}-a_{j})^{2}}\right).

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and
V
be an
\ell\times m
matrix whose rows have
\ell_{2}
-norm 1.
Roughly speaking, the proposition says that if the
\ell_{2}
-norm and the support of each column of
V
are small, then the rows of
Vx=\mu
do not cover the entire
m
-cube
\{0,1\}^{m}
.
The idea of the proof of Theorem
2
is to apply Proposition
11
.
For every essential matrix, we find a large submatrix whose norm and support of each column are small.
We then apply Proposition
11
to such submatrix and show that there must be a vertex of the
n
-cube which is not covered.
Below,
v_{*j}
denotes the vector corresponding to the
j
-th column of the matrix
V
.

Proposition 11
.

Let
V
be an
\ell\times m
matrix such that
\|v_{i}\|_{2}=1
for all
i\in[\ell]
, where
v_{i}
is the
i
-th row of
V
.
Suppose that
\alpha=\max\limits_{j\in[m]}|\textup{supp}(v_{*j})|
and

\beta=\max\limits_{j\in[m]}\|v_{*j}\|_{2}^{2}
satisfy

2\alpha\beta\log(4\ell)\leq 1.

Then, for every
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, the hyperplanes given by the rows of the system
Vx=\mu
do not cover the entire
m
-cube
\{0,1\}^{m}
.

Proof.

Let
\theta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
be given by

\theta\coloneqq(2\log(4\ell))^{1/2}
and

\zeta:=2\mu-V\cdot\bar{1}
, where
\bar{1}
denotes the vector with all coordinates equal to 1.
By Bang’s Lemma (c.f. Lemma
9
) for
M:=VV^{T}
and
\theta_{i}=\theta
for
i\in[\ell]
, there exists a vector
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{\ell}
such that

(10)

\displaystyle|(VV^{T}\theta\varepsilon)_{i}-\zeta_{i}|\geq\theta

for every
i\in[\ell]
.
In particular, the vector
y^{\prime}:=\theta V^{T}\varepsilon
satisfies
|\langle v_{i},y^{\prime}\rangle-\zeta_{i}|\geq\theta
for every
i\in[\ell]
.
By the Cauchy–Schwartz inequality, we have

\|y^{\prime}\|_{\infty}=\|\theta V^{T}\varepsilon\|_{\infty}=\max_{j\in[n]}\left|\theta\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}v_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\right|\leq\theta\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\leq 1.

We would like to find a vector of the
m
-cube (i.e. in
\{0,1\}^{m}
) that satisfies no row from the system of equations
Vx=\mu
.
First, we use
y^{\prime}
to find a vector
y\in[0,1]^{m}
which is far from the hyperplanes
\langle v_{i},x\rangle=\mu_{i}
, for
i\in[\ell]
.
For this, we set
y:=\frac{y^{\prime}+\bar{1}}{2}
.
As
\|y^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, note that
y\in[0,1]^{m}
.
By (
10
), we have

(11)

\displaystyle|(V(2y-\bar{1}))_{i}-\zeta_{i}|=|2(Vy)_{i}-2\mu_{i}|\geq\theta.

Define the random vector
w:=y+\delta
, where
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a random vector with independent entries and distribution given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\delta_{i}=1-y_{i}\right)=y_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}\left(\delta_{i}=-y_{i}\right)=1-y_{i}.

Observe that the random variables
\delta_{i}
are chosen so that we have
w\in\{0,1\}^{m}
and
\mathbb{E}(\delta)=0
.
To finish the proof of Proposition
11
, it suffices to show that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}(\langle w,v_{i}\rangle=\mu_{i})<1
.
To bound each of the probabilities
\mathbb{P}(\langle w,v_{i}\rangle=\mu_{i})
, we first note that

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\langle w,v_{i}\rangle=\mu_{i})\leq\mathbb{P}\big(|\langle\delta,v_{i}\rangle|=|\mu_{i}-\langle y,v_{i}\rangle|\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|\langle\delta,v_{i}\rangle|\geq\frac{\theta}{2}\right),

where we used (
11
) for the last inequality.

The variables
(\delta_{j}v_{ij})_{j\in[m]}
are independent, bounded by
-v_{ij}\leq\delta_{j}v_{ij}\leq v_{ij}
and centered (i.e.
\E[\delta_{j}v_{ij}]=0
) for every
j\in[\ell]
.
By (
12
) and Hoeffding’s inequality (c.f. Lemma
10
), we have

\mathbb{P}(\langle w,v_{i}\rangle=\mu_{i})\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|\langle\delta,v_{i}\rangle|\geq\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\leq 2\cdot\exp\left(-\dfrac{2(\theta/2)^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{ij}^{2}}\right)=2\exp\left(\frac{-\theta^{2}}{2}\right).

As
\theta=(2\log(4\ell))^{1/2}
, we obtain

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbb{P}\big(\langle w,v_{i}\rangle=\mu_{i}\big)\leq 2\ell\exp\left(\frac{-\theta^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}.\qed

Proposition
11
above is the main difference in the proof of Theorem
2
compared to the proof from
[
10
]
. Roughly speaking, the better dependence on parameters of the condition
2\alpha\beta\log(4\ell)\leq 1
allows us to substantially improve the lower bound on the number of hyperplanes in any essential cover of the cube.

4.
The structure of essential covers

In this section, we prove structural statements for essential matrices.
The goal is to decompose an essential matrix in such a way that we find a suitable submatrix where we can apply Proposition
11
.
The decomposition will be done in two steps.
In the first decomposition, we divide the essential matrix into four blocks, see Subsection
4.1
.
In one of the blocks, we will have small column norm and row norm either
0
or
1
.
As every row needs to have norm 1 to apply Proposition
11
, we decompose this block even further to deal with the rows of norm
0
.
This is the second decomposition (see Subsection
4.2
), which is the main result of this section.

The proofs of the decomposition lemmas are done algorithmically and have the same flavor as the proof for the lower bound of
n^{1/3}
given by Linial and Radhakrishnan
[
7
]
.
For the reader to be familiarized with the high-level techniques used in the decompositions, we first state and prove this result.

Lemma 12

(Linial–Radhakrishnan
[
7
]
.)
.

An essential cover of the
n
-cube has at least
n^{1/3}
hyperplanes.

Proof.

Let
V
be a
k\times n
essential matrix.
As usual, denote its rows by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
.
For
\ell>0
(to be chosen later to be
n^{2/3}
), we run the following algorithm.

Initiate

L_{1}\leftarrow\emptyset
,
L_{2}\leftarrow[k]
,
M_{1}\leftarrow[n]
and
M_{2}\leftarrow\emptyset
.

While
there is
i\in L_{2}
such that
|\textup{supp}(v_{i})\setminus M_{2}|<\ell
,

L_{1}\leftarrow L_{1}\cup\{i\}
and
L_{2}\leftarrow L_{2}-\{i\}
;

M_{2}\leftarrow M_{2}\cup\textup{supp}(v_{i})
and
M_{1}\leftarrow M_{1}-\textup{supp}(v_{i})
;

Output:

L_{1}
,
L_{2}
,
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
.

end.

The algorithm outputs a partition of the rows

L_{1}\cup L_{2}=[k]
and a partition of the columns
M_{1}\cup M_{2}=[n]
with the following properties.
If
L_{1},M_{1}\neq\emptyset
, then the submatrix
V(L_{1}\times M_{1})
is identically
0
. If
L_{2}\neq\emptyset
,
then
M_{1}\neq\emptyset
and each row in
V(L_{2}\times M_{1})
has support of size at least
\ell
.
Moreover, by relabeling the rows in
L_{1}
and the columns in
M_{2}
if necessary, the vectors in
L_{1}
have the following property.
If
L_{1}\neq\emptyset
, then we have
M_{2}\neq\emptyset
,

\textup{supp}(v_{i})\subseteq M_{2}
and

|\textup{supp}(v_{i})\setminus\bigcup_{j<i}\textup{supp}(v_{j})|<\ell
for all
i\leq|L_{1}|
.
Below, Figure
1
represents a partition given by the algorithm.

Figure 1.
The decomposition of
V
in the proof of Lemma
12
.

We are now ready to bound the size of the essential cover.
If
L_{1}=[k]
, then we have
M_{2}=[n]
. As
\bigcup_{i}\textup{supp}(v_{i})=[n]
, this implies that
k\cdot\ell\geq n
.
If
L_{1}\neq[k]
, then
L_{2}\neq\emptyset
and hence there exists
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
not covered by the hyperplanes in
L_{1}
. Let
P
be the
|M_{1}|
-dimensional cube agreeing with
x
on
M_{2}
. Fix the
M_{2}
-coordinates of
x
. Now, the hyperplanes corresponding to the rows in
L_{2}
form a covering of
P
. By Lemma
3
, each hyperplane in
L_{2}
covers at most
2^{|M_{1}|}/\sqrt{\ell}
vertices of
P
, and hence
L_{2}
needs to have size at least
\sqrt{\ell}
for the
|M_{1}|
-cube to be covered.
That is, we have
k\geq|L_{2}|\geq\sqrt{\ell}.

We conclude that

k\geq\min\left\{\frac{n}{\ell},\sqrt{\ell}\right\}
. Choosing
\ell=n^{2/3}
, we obtain
k\geq n^{1/3}
.
∎

Note that the main idea of the proof is to explore the facts that hyperplanes with big support cannot cover many vertices, and that only few hyperplanes can have relatively small support.
We shall make use of these properties in the first and second decompositions (c.f. Lemmas
14
and
15
).
The next lemma is a key ingredient for the proof of such decompositions.
It bounds the support of every row in an essential matrix.

Lemma 13

(Linial–Radhakrishnan
[
7
]
)
.

Let
V
be a
k\times n
essential matrix. Then, we have

|\textup{supp}(v_{i})|\leq 2k
for all
i\in[k]
.

Using Lemma
13
, we can easily show that every essential cover of the
n
-cube has
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
hyperplanes.
Indeed, by Lemma
13
, every
k\times n
essential matrix
V
has at most
2k^{2}
non-zero entries.
On the other hand, every column of
V
has a non-zero entry, and hence we have at least
n
non-zero entries in total.
This implies that
2k^{2}\geq n
.
In
[
7
]
, Linial and Radhakrishnan were able to obtain a slightly stronger lower bound (by a multiplicative constant) by showing that in every column we have at least two non-zero entries.

4.1.
First decomposition

Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be vectors with
\ell_{2}
-norm 1 given by an essential cover of the
n
-cube.
We would like to apply Proposition
11
to show that
k
cannot be small, as otherwise, there will be a vertex not covered by the hyperplanes.
To apply this proposition, we need to bound the support and the
\ell_{2}
-norm of each column of
V
.
However, we might not have good bounds for these quantities: the support of each column can be as large as
k
and the norm can be as large as
\Omega(\sqrt{k})
.
Luckily, we expect a random large submatrix of
V
to be much more well-behaved.

In the first decomposition (c.f. Lemma
14
), we rescale
V
and find a large submatrix
V[L_{1}\times M_{1}]
with the following properties.
The rows of
V[L_{1}\times M_{1}]
have
\ell_{2}
-norm either
0
or
1
; the columns have support roughly bounded by
2k^{2}/n
;
the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the column vectors is bounded by
W^{-1/2}
, where
W=W(n)>0
is an arbitrary function;
and the size of the set
M_{2}:=M_{1}^{c}
is roughly bounded by
kW
.
In the proof of Theorem
2
, the function
W=(n\log n)^{1/9}
is chosen so that, even if we apply the first decomposition multiple times inside the matrix,
the union of the sets
M_{2}
will have size at most
n/8
.
This will help us to find a large submatrix whose rows have
\ell_{2}
-norm
1
and whose columns have small norm.

Ideally, to apply Proposition
11
, we wish that all the rows in
V[L_{1}\times M_{1}]
had norm
1
.
However, this cannot be guaranteed in this step, as the first decomposition (c.f. Lemma
14
) holds for every matrix, not necessarily coming from an essential cover.
In Lemma
15
, which is the core of the proof of Theorem
2
together with Proposition
11
, we refine this decomposition to control the rows with many zeros.
Once we find a submatrix
V[L_{1}\times M_{1}]
whose rows have norm either 0 or 1 and whose columns have small support,
we still need to deal with the rest of the matrix
V
.
However, the decomposition is done in such a way that if
i\notin L_{1}
, then the coordinates of
v_{i}
have approximately exponential decay.

For a vector
v
and a set
A
, denote by
{\left.\kern-1.2ptv\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{A}}
the subvector of
v
restricted to the coordinates in
A
.
We show that there exists
a\in\{0,1\}^{n-|M_{1}|}
such that

\langle{\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{i}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M_{1}}},u\rangle\neq\mu_{i}-\langle{\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{i}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M_{1}^{c}}},a\rangle

for every
i\notin L_{1}
and
u\in\{0,1\}^{M_{1}}
.
Thus, the strategy is reduced to restrict ourselves to the subcube
\{0,1\}^{M_{1}}\times a
and show that
V[L_{1}\times M_{1}]
is not a covering system for this subcube.

Let
V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
be a matrix with rows
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
and
V^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
be a matrix with rows
v^{\prime}_{1},\dots,v^{\prime}_{k}
. We say that
V^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
is a
rescaling
of a matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
if there are real numbers
(\phi_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
such that
v^{\prime}_{i}=\phi_{i}v_{i}
for
i\in[k]
.
The first decomposition lemma, obtaining a matrix decomposition as in Figure
2
, is as follows.

Lemma 14
.

There exists
C_{3}>0
such that the following holds. For every
V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
,
S\in\mathbb{N}
and
W>0
, there are partitions
[k]=L_{1}\cup L_{2}
and
[n]=M_{1}\cup M_{2}
, with
|M_{2}|\leq C_{3}kSW
, such that for some rescaling
V^{\prime}
of
V
we have the following.

(i)

Every row in
V^{\prime}[L_{1}\times M_{1}]
has
\ell_{2}
-norm either
0
or
1
;

(ii)

Every column in
V^{\prime}[L_{1}\times M_{1}]
has
\ell_{2}
-norm strictly less than
W^{-1/2}
;

(iii)

Every row
i\in L_{2}
of
V^{\prime}
has
S
scales, and the position of its smallest scale contains the
M_{1}
columns.

Figure 2.
The decomposition of a rescaling of
V
as in Lemma
14
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that each column of
V
has
\ell_{2}
-norm equal to
1
.
The strategy to obtain the desired partitions
[k]=L_{1}\cup L_{2}
and
[n]=M_{1}\cup M_{2}
is the following algorithm. Start with
L_{1}=[k]
and
M_{1}=[n]
and move columns from
M_{1}
to
M_{2}
until every column in
M_{1}
has small norm. While moving those columns, if a row has a certain fraction of its norm moved to
M_{2}
, then this creates a scale. If we create
S
scales for a row, then we move that row to
L_{2}
.

Formally, the algorithm is as follows. Let
\tau>0
be so that
\frac{1-\tau}{\tau}=C_{1}^{2}
, where
C_{1}
is the constant from Definition
4
.

Initiate

L_{1}\leftarrow[k]
,
L_{2}\leftarrow\emptyset
,
M_{1}\leftarrow[n]
and
M_{2}\leftarrow\emptyset
.

While
there exists a column
j\in M_{1}
with
\sum_{i\in L_{1}}{v_{ij}}^{2}\geq\tau W^{-1}
, do:

1. Set
M_{1}\leftarrow M_{1}\setminus\{j\}
and
M_{2}\leftarrow M_{2}\cup\{j\}
. If there is more than one such column
j
,

then we choose only one of them arbitrarily.

2. For
i\in L_{1}
such that
\|{\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{i}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M_{1}}}\|_{2}^{2}\in(0,\tau]
,
multiply the row
i
by
\|{\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{i}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M_{1}}}\|_{2}^{-2}
.
That is, set

v_{i}\leftarrow v_{i}\cdot\|{\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{i}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M_{1}}}\|_{2}^{-2}.

3. If Step 2 is executed
S
times for a row
i
, then set
L_{1}\leftarrow L_{1}\setminus\{i\}
and
L_{2}\leftarrow L_{2}\cup\{i\}
.

M_{2}\leftarrow M_{2}\cup\textup{supp}(v_{i})
and
M_{1}\leftarrow M_{1}\setminus\textup{supp}(v_{i})
;

For

i\in L_{1}
such that
\|{\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{i}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M_{1}}}\|_{2}\neq 0
, multiply the row
i
by
\|{\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{i}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M_{1}}}\|_{2}^{-2}
.
That is, set

v_{i}\leftarrow v_{i}\cdot\|{\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{i}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M_{1}}}\|_{2}^{-1}.

Output:

V
,
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
,
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
.

end.

We now make some simple observations about the algorithm.
At Step 3 of the
While
loop, if
\|{\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{i}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M_{1}}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\tau
for some
i\in L_{1}
, then
multiplying
v_{i}
by
\|{\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{i}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{M_{1}}}\|_{2}^{-2}
renormalizes the vector
v_{i}
so that its
\ell_{2}
-norm restricted to the set
M_{1}
is 1.
For each
i\in[k]
, denote by
v_{i}(t)
and
M_{1}(t)
the vector
v_{i}
and the set
M_{1}
after the
t
-th iteration of the
While
loop, respectively.
Let
t_{1}<t_{2}
be two steps corresponding to two consecutive renormalizations of a row
v_{i}
.
Before executing Step 3 in iteration
t_{2}
, we have the following mass distribution in
v_{i}(t_{2}-1)

if we renormalize it

has
S
