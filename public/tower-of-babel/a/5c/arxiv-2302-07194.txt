Score Approximation, Estimation and Distribution Recovery of Diffusion Models on Low-Dimensional Data
Minshuo Chen, Kaixuan Huang, Tuo Zhao, Mengdi Wang

Abstract
Diffusion models achieve state-of-the-art performance in various generation tasks. However, their theoretical foundations fall far behind. This paper studies score approximation, estimation, and distribution recovery of diffusion models, when data are supported on an unknown low-dimensional linear subspace. Our result provides sample complexity bounds for distribution estimation using diffusion models. We show that with a properly chosen neural network architecture, the score function can be both accurately approximated and efficiently estimated. Furthermore, the generated distribution based on the estimated score function captures the data geometric structures and converges to a close vicinity of the data distribution. The convergence rate depends on the subspace dimension, indicating that diffusion models can circumvent the curse of data ambient dimensionality.



1
Introduction

Diffusion models achieve state-of-the-art performance in image and audio generating tasks
(
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Dathathri et al. 2019
;
Song et al. 2020b
;
Ho et al. 2020
)
and are one of the fundamental building blocks of the more advanced image synthesis system, e.g., DALL-E-2
(
Ramesh et al. 2022
)
and stable diffusion
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
.

A standard diffusion model
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2020
)
consists of a forward process and a backward process: In the forward process, a data point is sequentially corrupted by Gaussian random noises and in the limit the data distribution is transformed into white noise; In the backward process, a denoising neural network is trained to sequentially remove the added noise in the data and restore the clean data point. Using the trained denoising network for the backward process, one can generate diverse and high fidelity samples by first sampling from the standard Gaussian distribution and then progressively removing noises.

The distinctive denoising objective separates diffusion models from other deep generative models such as GANs
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
)
, and Normalizing Flows
(
Rezende and Mohamed 2015
)
. As shown by
Vincent 2011
, the training of denoising network essentially learns the so-called “score function”, i.e., the gradient of log probability density function. Therefore, diffusion models fall into the category of Score-based Generative Models (SGMs).

Despite the empirical success of diffusion models, the theory is still in its embryo. Here we are interested in answering two fundamental questions:

Q1
.
Can neural networks well approximate and learn score functions, especially when data have intrinsic geometric structures? If so, how should one choose the neural network architectures, and what is the sample complexity of learning?

Q2
.
Can diffusion models estimate the data distribution using the learned score functions? If so, how are the data intrinsic geometric structures being captured and how do they affect the sample complexity?

Both
Q1
and
Q2
raise a practical concern about the real world data, such as high resolution images. These data, though having high ambient dimensions, often exhibit low-dimensional structures
(
Pope et al. 2021
)
, due to symmetries, repetitive patterns, and local regularities
(
Tenenbaum et al. 2000
;
Roweis and Saul 2000
)
. Deep neural networks have been known for capturing certain low-dimensional data geometric structures
(
Schmidt-Hieber 2017
;
Suzuki 2018
;
Nakada and Imaizumi 2020
;
Shen et al. 2022
)
. However, whether such abilities hold for diffusion models remains unclear.

Some recent works skipped
Q1
and attempted to study
Q2
, by directly assuming that the score function is accurately learned up to a small error under certain metric, e.g.,
L^{2}
/
L^{\infty}
norm
(
De Bortoli 2022
;
Lee et al. 2022a
;
Chen et al. 2022b
;
Lee et al. 2022b
)
.
De Bortoli 2022
in particular studied low-dimensional manifold data. These progresses unveil important theoretical insights about the sampling properties of the backward process of diffusion models, however, leaving
Q1
largely untouched. As a result, a full theoretical picture of diffusion models is lacking.

To bridge the gap between theory and practice, we make a first step towards an integrated analysis to answer both
Q1
and
Q2
for diffusion models. The combined result provides sample complexity bounds of diffusion models for learning data distributions supported on low-dimensional linear subspaces. Specifically, we consider data point
\mathbf{x}=A\mathbf{z}
, where
\mathbf{z}
is referred to as the latent variable, columns of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d<D
. We refer to
d
as the intrinsic dimension and
D
as the ambient dimension.

Based on such a low-dimensional linear subspace assumption, we can decompose the score function of the linear subspace data into on-support and orthogonal components (Lemma
1
). We then characterize their distinct behaviors of the two components, where on-support component carries latent distribution information and orthogonal component forces the subspace recovery.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

\bullet
We specify an encoder-decoder neural architecture with skip-layer connections and establish its approximation guarantees with respect to the score functions under the
L^{2}
norm (Theorem
1
). Specifically, given an approximation error
\epsilon
, we show that the network size needs to be exponential in
1/\epsilon
with the exponent depending on the data intrinsic dimension
d
.

\bullet
We establish statistical guarantees of score estimation using our properly chosen encoder-decoder neural network. We show that such a neural score estimator converges to the ground truth score under the
L^{2}
norm at a rate of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{0}}}n^{-\frac{1}{d+5}})
, where
n
is the sample size and
t_{0}
is an early stopping time (Theorem
2
). This result indicates that the neural score estimator does not suffer from the curse of the data ambient dimensionality in score estimation, when the data exhibit intrinsic geometric structures.

\bullet
We establish distribution estimation guarantees using the learned neural score estimator. By simulating a discretized backward process, the generated data distribution of diffusion models converges to a close vicinity of the data distribution (Theorem
3
). Specifically, for the on-support direction, generated distribution enjoys a
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-\frac{1}{2(d+5)}})
rate of convergence in Total Variation distance. For the orthogonal direction, the generated distribution vanishes in magnitude, and the support of the data is approximated recovered. Our analysis demonstrates that diffusion models are free of the curse of data ambient dimensionality.

1.1
Related work

Several recent works study diffusion models from the sampling perspective.
De Bortoli et al. 2021
study the convergence of diffusion Schrödinger bridges by assuming the score estimator is accurate under the
L^{\infty}
norm.
Lee et al. 2022a
provide polynomial convergence guarantees of SGMs, under the assumption that the score estimator is accurate under the
L^{2}
norm. In addition,
Lee et al. 2022a
require the data distribution satisfying a log-Sobolev inequality. Concurrent works
Chen et al. 2022b
and
Lee et al. 2022b
improve previous results by extending to distributions with bounded moments. Their analyses still assume access to an accurate score estimator under the
L^{2}
norm. It is worth mentioning that
Lee et al. 2022b
allow the error of the score estimator under the
L^{2}
norm to scale with time.

Moreover,
De Bortoli 2022
made an interesting attempt to analyze diffusion models for learning low-dimensional manifold data. Assuming the score estimator is accurate under the
L^{\infty}
norm (extension to the
L^{2}
norm is also provided),
De Bortoli 2022
provide distribution estimation guarantees of diffusion models in terms of the Wasserstein distance. The obtained convergence rate has an exponential dependence on the diameter of manifold.

As stated, aforementioned works hardly touch
Q1
and provide partial understandings of diffusion models. To the best of our knowledge,
Block et al. 2020
is the only work in existing literature, which provides score estimation guarantees under the
L^{2}
norm. Yet the error bound depends on some unknown Rademacher complexity of certain concept class. In comparison, our work is explicit on the choice of a neural network concept class and score estimation error bound. Note that
Block et al. 2020
also provide sampling convergence guarantees under the assumption of access to an accurate score estimator under the
L^{2}
norm. We are also aware of
Song et al. 2020a
and
Liu et al. 2022
studying score estimation and distribution estimation from an asymptotic statistics point of view.

2
Preliminaries

We briefly review diffusion models and score matching using neural networks.

Forward and backward SDEs

The forward process in diffusion models progressively adds noise to original data. Here we consider the Ornstein-Ulhenbeck process, which is described by the following SDE,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}g(t)\mathbf{X}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{g(t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}~~~\text{for}~~g(t)>0,

(1)

where initial
\mathbf{X}_{0}\sim P_{\rm data}
follows the data distribution,
(\mathbf{W}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Wiener process, and
g(t)
is a nondecreasing weighting function. We denote the marginal distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
at time
t
as
P_{t}
. Roughly speaking, after an infinitesimal time, (
1
) shrinks the magnitude of data and corrupts data by Gaussian white noise.
More precisely, given
\mathbf{X}_{0}
, the conditional distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathbf{X}_{0}
is Gaussian
{\sf N}(\alpha(t)\mathbf{X}_{0},h(t)I_{D})
, where
\alpha(t)=\exp(-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}g(s)ds)
and
h(t)=1-\alpha^{2}(t)
. Consequently, under mild conditions, (
1
) transforms initial distribution
P_{\rm data}
to
P_{\infty}={\sf N}(\bm{0},I_{D})
. Therefore, (
1
) is also known as the variance preserving forward SDE
(
Song et al. 2020b
)
.

In practice, the forward process (
1
) will terminate at a sufficiently large time horizon
T>0
, where the corrupted marginal distribution
P_{T}
is expected to be close to the standard Gaussian distribution.

Diffusion models generate fake data by reversing the time of (
1
), which leads to the following backward SDE,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{2}g(T-t)\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t}+g(T-t)\nabla\log p_{T-t}(\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{g(T-t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{t},

(2)

where
\nabla\log p_{t}(\cdot)
is the score function, i.e., the gradient of log probability density function of
P_{t}
, and
\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{t}
is a reversed Wiener process. Under mild conditions, when initialized at
\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{0}\sim P_{T}
, the backward process
(\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
has the same distribution as the time-reversed version of the forward process
(\mathbf{X}_{T-t})_{0\leq t\leq T}

(
Anderson 1982
;
Haussmann and Pardoux 1986
)
.

Working with (
2
), however, leads to difficulties, as both the score function
\nabla\log p_{t}
and initial distribution
P_{T}
are unknown. In practice, several surrogates are deployed. Firstly, we replace
P_{T}
by the standard Gaussian distribution. Secondly, we use a score estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}
instead of ground truth score
\nabla\log p_{t}
. The estimated score
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}
is often parameterized by a neural network. With these substitutions, we obtain the following practical backward SDE,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\leftarrow}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{2}g(T-t){\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\leftarrow}}_{t}+g(T-t)\widehat{\mathbf{s}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\leftarrow}_{t},T-t)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{g(T-t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{t},\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\leftarrow}_{0}\sim{\sf N}(\bm{0},I_{D}).

(3)

Diffusion models then generate data by simulating a discretization of (
3
) with
\eta>0
being the discretization step size:

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\Leftarrow}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{2}g(T-t){\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\Leftarrow}}_{k\eta}+g(T-t)\widehat{\mathbf{s}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\Leftarrow}_{k\eta},T-k\eta)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{g(T-t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{t},\text{ for}~~t\in[k\eta,(k+1)\eta],

(4)

Throughout the paper, we take
g(t)=1
for simplicity.

Score matching

To estimate the score function, a conceptual way is to minimize a weighted quadratic loss:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{s}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{0}^{T}w(t)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{t}\sim P_{t}}\left[\left\lVert\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{X}_{t})-\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{X}_{t},t)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

where
w(t)
is a weighting function and
{\mathcal{S}}
is a concept class (often neural networks). However, such an objective function is intractable, as
\nabla\log p_{t}
is unknown. As shown by
Vincent 2011
, rather than minimizing the integral above, we can minimize an equivalent objective,

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{s}\in{\mathcal{S}}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}w(t)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}\sim P_{\rm data}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathbf{X}_{0}}\Big[\big\|\nabla_{\mathbf{X}_{t}}\log\phi_{t}(\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathbf{X}_{0})-\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{X}_{t},t)\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\Big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(5)

Here
\phi_{t}(\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathbf{X}_{0})
denotes the transition kernel of the forward process, which is Gaussian. Hence, we have an analytical form

\nabla_{\mathbf{X}_{t}}\log\phi_{t}(\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathbf{X}_{0})=-\frac{\mathbf{X}_{t}-\alpha(t)\mathbf{X}_{0}}{h(t)}.

Note that
\nabla_{\mathbf{X}_{t}}\log\phi_{t}(\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathbf{X}_{0})
is the noise added to
\mathbf{X}_{0}
at time
t
. Therefore, (
5
) is known as denoising score matching.

In practice, we approximate (
5
) by its empirical version. Specifically, given
n
i.i.d. data points
\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim P_{\rm data}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, we sample
\mathbf{X}_{t}
given
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{i}
from
{\sf N}(\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}_{i},h(t)I_{D})
. We also sample time
t
uniformly from interval
[t_{0},T]
for some small
t_{0}>0
. (In Section
5
, we will choose
t_{0}
based on sample size
n
.) The reason behind avoiding
[0,t_{0}]
is to prevent score from blowing up and stabilize training
(
Vahdat et al. 2021
;
Song and Ermon 2020
)
. To this end, the empirical score matching objective is

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{s}\in{\mathcal{S}}}~\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{s}),

(6)

where the loss function
\ell(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{s})
is defined as

\ell(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathbf{s})=\frac{1}{T-t_{0}}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{i}}[\left\lVert\nabla_{\mathbf{X}_{t}}\log\phi_{t}(\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathbf{X}_{0})-\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{X}_{t},t)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Note that we have already taken
w(t)=1/(T-t_{0})
for simplicity and assumed sufficient sampling of
\mathbf{X}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
t
, as they are cheap to generate. For notational convenience, we denote population loss
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\rm data}}[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot)]
.

3
Score decomposition

In this section, we show that for a low-dimensional data distribution, the score function can be decomposed – each component of the score function has distinct properties. Exploiting these properties enables an efficient approximation and estimation of the score function; see Section
4
.

We consider data
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
supported on a
d
-dimensional unknown linear subspace with
d\ll D
.

Assumption 1
.

Data point
\mathbf{x}
can be written as
\mathbf{x}=A\mathbf{z}
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}
is an unknown matrix with orthonormal columns. The latent variable
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
follows some distribution
P_{z}
with a density function
p_{z}
.

Given such a low dimensional structure of the data, we can show that the ground-truth score function has the following decomposition.

Lemma 1
.

Let data
\mathbf{x}=A\mathbf{z}
follows Assumption
1
. The score function
\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
decomposes as

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\underbrace{A\nabla\log p_{t}^{\sf LD}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x})}_{\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x},t)\text{:~on-support~score}}\underbrace{-\frac{1}{h(t)}\left(I_{D}-AA^{\top}\right)\mathbf{x}}_{\mathbf{s}_{\perp}(\mathbf{x},t)\text{:~ortho.~score}},

where

p_{t}^{\sf LD}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime})=\int\phi_{t}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}

with
\phi_{t}(\cdot|\mathbf{z})
being the Gaussian density function of
{\sf N}(\alpha(t)\mathbf{z},h(t)I_{d})
for
\alpha(t)=e^{-t/2}
and
h(t)=1-e^{-t}
.

The proof follows from algebraic manipulation, which is deferred to Appendix
A.1
. Here
p_{t}^{\sf LD}
denotes a density function on the latent space (superscript stands for “latent distribution”). The on-support score
\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}
belongs to the column span of
A
, depends on the projected data
A^{\top}\mathbf{x}
, and is orthogonal to
\mathbf{s}_{\perp}
. When
t\to 0
, we can check that
\mathbf{s}_{\perp}
will blow up since
h(t)\to 0
. This observation is consistent with the score blowup phenomenon for manifold data
(
Song and Ermon 2020
;
Kim et al. 2021
;
Pidstrigach 2022
;
De Bortoli 2022
)
, as our linear subspace is a special type of manifolds.

The decomposition of
\nabla\log p_{t}
also suggests a decomposition of the backward process. Specifically, we denote
\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\parallel}=AA^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\perp}=(I_{D}-AA^{\top})\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t}
. Then the dynamic in (
2
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\parallel}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\parallel}+\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\parallel},T-t)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+AA^{\top}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{t},

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\perp}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{h(T-t)}\right]\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\perp}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+(I_{D}-AA^{\top})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{t}.

A graphical illustration is provided in Figure
1
. The dynamics of
\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\parallel}
incorporates information from the latent distribution
P_{z}
, while the dynamics of
\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\perp}
is linear and much simpler. The interesting part is that the coefficient in the drift term of
\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\perp}
is always negative, indicating that
\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t,\perp}
will vanish eventually and the data support will be perfectly recovered.

Figure 1:
Demonstration of score decomposition induces two backward processes.

For better interpretation, we analyze a Gaussian example. Detailed computation is provided in Appendix
A.2
.

Example 1
.

We take latent distribution
P_{z}={\sf N}(\bm{0},\Sigma)
with
\Sigma={\rm diag}(\lambda^{2}_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}^{2})\succ 0
, a
d
-dimensional Gaussian distribution. The score function can be computed as

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\underbrace{-A\Sigma_{t}^{-1}A^{\top}\mathbf{x}}_{\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}}\underbrace{-\frac{1}{h(t)}(I_{D}-AA^{\top})\mathbf{x}}_{\mathbf{s}_{\perp}},

where
\Sigma_{t}={\rm diag}(\dots,\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{k}^{2}+h(t),\dots)
.

One can verify that
\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}
now is linear in
\mathbf{x}
, whereas
\mathbf{s}_{\perp}
blows up when
t
approaches
0
. Moreover, only the on-support score
\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}
carries the covariance information of the latent distribution and will guide the distribution recovery.

A closer evaluation further reveals
\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}
is Lipschitz continuous, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{z}_{1},t)-\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{z}_{2},t)\right\rVert_{2}\leq\max\{\lambda_{d}^{-2},1\}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\right\rVert_{2}

for any
t\in[0,T]
and
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2}
, and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{z},t_{1})-\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{z},t_{2})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\max\{\lambda_{d}^{-2},1\}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}|t_{1}-t_{2}|.

for any
\mathbf{z}
and
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T]
. Such properties are essential to develop score approximation and estimation results.

4
Score approximation and estimation

In practice, score functions are approximated by neural networks. To ensure an effective learning, the network class should be expressive enough to approximate the score function. This section first establishes a score approximation theory. Built upon the approximation theory, we next provide statistical guarantees of the score matching.

4.1
Score approximation

We rearrange terms of
\nabla\log p_{t}
in Lemma
1
as

\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{h(t)}A\big(h(t)\nabla\log p_{t}^{\sf LD}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x})+A^{\top}\mathbf{x}\big)-\frac{1}{h(t)}\mathbf{x}.

Accordingly, we consider score networks in the form of

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}=\bigg\{\mathbf{s}_{V,\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{1}{h(t)}V{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(V^{\top}\mathbf{x},t)-\frac{1}{h(t)}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle:~V\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}~\text{with~orthonormal~columns},

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[t_{0},T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}~\text{a~ReLU~network}\bigg\}.

Figure 2:
Network architecture of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
.

Remark 1
.

The network family
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
resembles commonly used architectures of score networks, e.g., U-Net
(
Ronneberger et al. 2015
)
: (1)
-\frac{1}{h(t)}\mathbf{x}
contributes as a shortcut connection; (2)
V\mathbf{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(V^{\top}\mathbf{x},t)
retains an encoder-decoder structure, where
V
,
V^{\top}
are the linear decoder and encoder, respectively. See Figure
2
for an illustration of the network architecture. We will show later that through score matching,
V
indeed recovers the unknown data subspace.

We configure the ReLU network
\mathbf{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
in
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
by hyperparameters. Specifically,
\mathbf{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\in{\rm NN}(L,M,J,K,\kappa,\gamma,\gamma_{t})
with

\displaystyle{\rm NN}(L,M,J,K,\kappa,\gamma,\gamma_{t})=

\displaystyle\Big\{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},t)=W_{L}\sigma(\dots\sigma(W_{1}[\mathbf{z}^{\top},t]^{\top}+\mathbf{b}_{1})\dots)+\mathbf{b}_{L}:

\displaystyle\quad\text{network~width~bounded~by~}M,~\sup_{\mathbf{z},t}\left\lVert\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},t)\right\rVert_{2}\leq K,

\displaystyle~~~\max\{\left\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}\right\rVert_{\infty},\left\lVert W_{i}\right\rVert_{\infty}\}\leq\kappa~\text{for}~i=1,\dots,L,

\displaystyle~~~\sum_{i=1}^{L}\big(\left\lVert W_{i}\right\rVert_{0}+\left\lVert\mathbf{b}_{i}\right\rVert_{0}\big)\leq J,

\displaystyle~~~\left\lVert\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z}_{1},t)-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z}_{2},t)\right\rVert_{2}\leq\gamma\left\lVert\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\right\rVert_{2}~\text{for~any~}t\in[0,T],

\displaystyle~~~\left\lVert\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},t_{1})-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},t_{2})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\gamma_{t}|t_{1}-t_{2}|~\text{for~any~}\mathbf{z}\Big\},

where the network width refers to the maximum dimensions of the weight matrices,
\sigma
is the ReLU activation, and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{0}
denote the maximum magnitude of entries and the number of nonzero entries, respectively. In the sequel, we write
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}(L,M,J,K,\kappa,\gamma,\gamma_{t})
to reflect the configuration of
\mathbf{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
. To establish our score approximation theory, we impose an assumption on the latent distribution
P_{z}
.

Assumption 2
.

The density function
p_{z}>0
is twice continuously differentiable. Moreover, there exist positive constants
B,C_{1},C_{2}
such that when
\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}\geq B
, the density function
p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\leq(2\pi)^{-d/2}C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2)
.

Assumption
2
describes the tail behavior of
P_{z}
being sub-Gaussian, which is commonly adopted in high-dimensional statistics literature
(
Vershynin 2018
;
Wainwright 2019
)
. We also need the following regularity assumption on the score function.

Assumption 3
.

The on-support score function
\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{z},t)
is
\beta
-Lipschitz in
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any
t\in[0,T]
.

Lipschitz score functions are a standard assumption in existing literature
(
Block et al. 2020
;
Lee et al. 2022a
;
Chen et al. 2022b
)
. Yet Assumption
3
only requires the Lipschitz continuity of the on-support score. As an example, the Gaussian data in Example
1
verifies Assumption
3
. We remark that
\nabla\log p_{t}
itself is
(\beta+\frac{1}{h(t)})
-Lipschitz, which matches the weaker assumption of
Lee et al. 2022b
. When
t
goes to zero, the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla\log p_{t}
goes to infinity.

The following theorem presents an approximation theory using
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
for score functions.

Theorem 1
.

Given an approximation error
\epsilon>0
, we choose
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
with

\displaystyle L=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}+d\right),~K=\mathcal{O}\left(2d^{2}\log\left(\frac{d}{t_{0}\epsilon}\right)\right),

\displaystyle M=\mathcal{O}\left((1+\beta)^{d}T\tau d^{d/2+1}\epsilon^{-(d+1)}\log^{d/2}\left(\frac{d}{t_{0}\epsilon}\right)\right),

\displaystyle J=\mathcal{O}\left((1+\beta)^{d}T\tau d^{d/2+1}\epsilon^{-(d+1)}\log^{d/2}\left(\frac{d}{t_{0}\epsilon}\right)\left(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}+d\right)\right),

\displaystyle\kappa=\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{2(1+\beta)\sqrt{d\log\left(\frac{d}{t_{0}\epsilon}\right)},T\tau\right\}\right),

\displaystyle\gamma=10d(1+\beta),~\gamma_{t}=10\tau,

where
\tau=\sup_{t\in[t_{0},T]}\sup_{\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{d\log\frac{d}{t_{0}\epsilon}}}\left\lVert\frac{\partial}{\partial t}[h(t)\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{z},t)]\right\rVert_{2}
. Then for any data distribution
P_{\rm data}
satisfying Assumptions

1
–
3
, there exists an
\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
such that for any
t\in[t_{0},T]
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,t)-\nabla\log p_{t}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(P_{t})}\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}+1}{h(t)}\epsilon.

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.1
. Theorem
1
confirms the universal approximation ability of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
for score functions. A few remarks are in order.

Universal approximation under the
L^{2}
norm

Many existing universal approximation theory of neural networks focus on approximating target functions on a compact domain under the
L^{\infty}
norm
(
Yarotsky 2017
;
Schmidt-Hieber 2017
;
Chen et al. 2019a
;
Gühring et al. 2020
)
. Instead, we provide an
L^{2}
-approximation error bound over the unbounded input domain
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, where we tackle the unboundedness through a truncation argument. In addition, thanks to the encoder-decoder architecture, the network size only depends on the intrinsic dimension
d
of data.

Lipschitz score network

Conventional universal approximation theory of neural networks hardly provide network Lipschitz continuity guarantees
(
Cybenko 1989
;
Barron 1993
;
Yarotsky 2017
)
. By our construction, the Lipschitz constraints
\gamma
and
\gamma_{t}
do not undermine the approximation power of score networks. In practice, such a Lipschitz regularity is often enforced during training, e.g., adding regularization
(
Virmaux and Scaman 2018
;
Pauli et al. 2021
;
Gouk et al. 2021
)
. Further, from a theoretical perspective, the Lipschitz property of the estimated score is essential to bounding the distribution recovery error, as we demonstrate in Section
5
.

Time as an additional input dimension

We take time
t
as an additional input dimension to the score network. The network size depends on the Lipschitz constant
\tau
. We show a very coarse upper bound of
\tau
in Appendix
B.1
. However,
\tau
depends on the latent distribution
P_{z}
and is highly instance specific. In Example
1
, we have
\tau=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d\log\left(d/(t_{0}\epsilon)\right)})
, much smaller than its coarse upper bound. More interestingly, in practice, time
t
is embedded using sinusoidal positional encoding scheme
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
and the processed embedding is added to the input data. Such a dimensional lift of time opens research directions, however, the analysis is beyond the scope of this paper.

4.2
Score estimation theory

In this subsection, we provide sample complexity for score estimation using
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
. As we have parameterized the score function using deep neural networks, we can rewrite the score matching objective in (
6
) as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\mathbf{s}_{V,\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{s}_{V,\bm{\theta}}),

where
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}
is defined in (
6
). The following theorem establishes the
L^{2}
convergence of
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}}
to
\nabla\log p_{t}
when the sample size
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
3
hold. We choose
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
as in Theorem
1
with
\epsilon=n^{-\frac{1-\delta(n)}{d+5}}
for
\delta(n)=\frac{d\log\log n}{\log n}
. Then with probability
1-\frac{1}{n}
, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-t_{0}}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,t)-\nabla\log p_{t}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(P_{t})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{t_{0}}\left(n^{-\frac{2-2\delta(n)}{d+5}}+Dn^{-\frac{d+3}{d+5}}\right)\log^{3}n\right),

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
hides factors depending on
\beta
,
\log D
,
d
,
\log t_{0}
and
\tau
defined in Theorem
1
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.2
. To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
2
is the first explicit sample complexity bound for score matching. The rate of convergence only depends on intrinsic dimension
d
. When
n
is sufficiently large,
\delta(n)
is negligible and the squared
L^{2}
estimation error converges at a rate of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{t_{0}}n^{-\frac{2}{d+5}})
. (We hide other factors depending on
d
in the bound to highlight the fast convergence in terms of sample size
n
.
As
d
is often much smaller than
D
and
n
is large for diffusion models, those factors on
d
do not undermine the convergence guarantee.)

Theorem
2
becomes vacuous if
t_{0}\to 0
when
n
is fixed. This is a consequence of the blowup of score function
\nabla\log p_{t}
as
t_{0}\to 0
. Although larger
t_{0}
leads to a better estimation error bound, following the backward process until a large time
t_{0}
gives poor distribution recovery. In the following section, we will show a tradeoff on
t_{0}
.

5
Distribution estimation

This section establishes distribution estimation guarantees using the estimated score functions. Recall that in reality, diffusion models generate data using the discretized backward process (
4
) with step size
\eta
. Given an estimated score function
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}}
as in Theorem
2
, we denote the generated distribution by
\widehat{P}^{\sf dis}_{t_{0}}
.

We focus on three major criteria to assess the quality of
\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}^{\sf dis}
: 1). How accurate is the subspace
A
recovered; 2). What is the estimation error of
\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}^{\sf dis}
to the on-support latent distribution
P_{z}
; 3). What is the behavior of
\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}^{\sf dis}
in the orthogonal space.

Recall from Lemma
1
, we denote on-support latent distribution as
P^{\sf LD}_{t}
with density function
p^{\sf LD}_{t}
. Since we early-stop at time
t_{0}
, we compare the estimated distribution with
P_{t_{0}}^{\sf LD}
. Now we summarize our results in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Given the estimated score
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
in Theorem
2
, we choose
T=\Theta(\log n),t_{0}={\mathcal{O}}(\min\{c_{0},1/\beta\})
, where
c_{0}=\sigma_{\min}(\mathbb{E}_{P_{z}}[\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^{\top}])
is the minimum eigenvalue. Then the following items hold with probability
1-\frac{1}{n}
.

1)
. The unknown data subspace is recovered as

\displaystyle\left\lVert VV^{\top}-AA^{\top}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{c_{0}}n^{-\frac{2-2\delta(n)}{d+5}}\log^{7/2}n\right),

2)
. Under the condition
{\sf KL}(P_{z}||{\sf N}(\bm{0},I_{d}))<\infty
, we choose the step size
\eta\leq\frac{t_{0}^{2}}{d}n^{-\frac{2-2\delta(n)}{d+5}}
. Recall
(VU)^{\top}_{\sharp}\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}^{\sf dis}
denotes the pushforward distribution. Then there exists an orthogonal matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that the total variation distance

\displaystyle{\sf TV}(P_{t_{0}}^{\sf LD},(VU)^{\top}_{\sharp}\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}^{\sf dis})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{c_{0}t_{0}}}n^{-\frac{1-\delta(n)}{d+5}}\log^{2}n\right).

Moreover, the Wasserstein-2 distance between
P_{t_{0}}^{\sf LD}
and
P_{z}
satisfies

\displaystyle{\sf W}_{2}(P_{t_{0}}^{\sf LD},P_{z})=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{dt_{0}}\right).

3)
. The orthogonal pushforward
(I-VV^{\top})_{\sharp}\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}^{\sf dis}
of the continuous-time generated data distribution is
{\sf N}(\bm{0},\Sigma)
, with
\Sigma\preceq ct_{0}I
for a constant
c>0
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
C
. Theorem
3
has the following interpretations.

Subspace recovery error

Item 1 of Theorem
3
confirms that the subspace is accurately learned, in that the column span of matrix
V
closely matches that of
A
. The error is proportional to the score estimation error and depends on the minimum eigenvalue of the covariance of
P_{z}
. The intuition behind is that we need
P_{z}
to span every direction of column span of
A
for estimation.

Meanwhile, item 1 does not translate to
\|A-V\|_{\rm F}
being small, since the column span is invariant under orthogonal transformation, i.e., column spans of
A
and
AU
for an orthogonal
U
are identical. Therefore, we need such an orthogonal transformation in item 2.

Tradeoff on
t_{0}

From item 2, we observe that the latent distribution error
{\sf TV}(P_{t_{0}}^{\sf LD},(VU)^{\top}_{\sharp}\widehat{P}^{\sf dis}_{t_{0}})
increases as
t_{0}
decreases, because the error of score estimation amplifies. On the other hand, the bias
{\sf W}_{2}(P_{t_{0}}^{\sf LD},P_{z})=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{t_{0}d}\right)
shrinks as
t_{0}
decreases. This reveals a tradeoff concerning recovery of data distribution
P_{z}
. Although we cannot directly translate total variation distance to Wasserstein-2 distance and vice versa, we can make them in the same order, which implies setting
t_{0}=n^{-\frac{1-\delta(n)}{d+5}}
. We thus obtain

\displaystyle{\sf TV}(P_{t_{0}}^{\sf LD},(VU)^{\top}_{\sharp}\widehat{P}^{\sf dis}_{t_{0}})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n^{-\frac{1-\delta(n)}{2(d+5)}}\log^{2}n\right)\quad\text{and}\quad{\sf W}_{2}(P_{t_{0}}^{\sf LD},P_{z})=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n^{-\frac{1-\delta(n)}{2(d+5)}}\right).

Vanishing in the orthogonal space

The behavior of
\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}^{\sf dis}
matches our discussion in the score decomposition. In particular,
(I-VV^{\top})_{\sharp}\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}^{\sf dis}
degenerates to a point mass at origin when
t_{0}\to 0
. Due to item 1,
(I-AA^{\top})_{\sharp}\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}^{\sf dis}
is also approximately vanishing.

Proof sketch of main results

This section is devoted to proving Theorems
1
–
3
. Due to space limit, we only describe key steps.

6.1
Proof sketch of Theorem
1

Theorem
1
is established by construction. A significant difference from the existing universal approximation theories is that the input domain of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
is unbounded. We manipulate the tail behavior of
P_{z}
for developing a truncation argument.

In the construction, we choose
V=A
and the approximation of the score boils down to that of
\mathbf{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{z},t)
to
h(t)\nabla\log p_{t}^{\sf LD}(\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{z}
for
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We denote
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{z},t)=h(t)\nabla\log p_{t}^{\sf LD}(\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{z}
. By Assumption
3
,
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{z},t)
is
(\beta+1)
-Lipschitz in
\mathbf{z}
.

Figure 3:
Construction of
\bar{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{z},t)
for approximating
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{z},t)
. For a fixed
t
, inside
[-R,R]^{d}
, we uniformly partition the hypercube into smaller hypercubes. On each of the small hypercube, we locally approximate
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{z},t)
by its value on the center. To detect the local region, we construct a trapezoid function
\psi
on each coordinate.

Let
R>B
be a truncation radius. On the hypercube
[-R,R]^{d}\times[t_{0},T]
, we construct
\bar{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}
as a piecewise linear function for approximating
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{z},t)
. Outside of the hypercube, we simply set
\bar{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{0}
. See Figure
3
for an illustration.

The
L^{2}
approximation error is evaluated as

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bar{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,t)-\mathbf{g}(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(P_{t}^{\sf LD})}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left\lVert\left(\bar{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,t)-\mathbf{g}(\cdot,t)\right)\mathds{1}\{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}\leq R\}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(P_{t}^{\sf LD})}}_{(A)}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left\lVert\left(\bar{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,t)-\mathbf{g}(\cdot,t)\right)\mathds{1}\{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}>R\}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(P_{t}^{\sf LD})}}_{(B)}.

Term
(A)
is directly bounded by the approximation error of
\bar{\mathbf{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}
on the hypercube. Term
(B)
utilizes the tail behavior of
P_{t}
. In particular, since
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{z},t)
is Lipschitz in
\mathbf{z}
, for sufficiently large
R
,
\left\lVert\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{z},t)\right\rVert_{2}
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}\right)
whenever
\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}>R
. Consequently, term
(B)
is bounded by

\displaystyle(B)=\mathcal{O}\left(\int_{\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}>R}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}p_{t}(\mathbf{z})d\mathbf{z}\right).

Note that Assumption
2
implies that
P_{t}
has a sub-Gaussian tail. Therefore,
(B)
can be bounded (by Lemma
2
), which leads to a choice of
R=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{d\log\frac{d}{t_{0}}+\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}}\right)
. The Lipschitzness of the constructed network is analyzed by adapting
Chen et al. 2020
.

6.2
Proof of Theorem
2

We first focus on the equivalent objective
\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})
and then translate to the desired score matching error.

We begin with an oracle inequality for bounding
\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathcal{L}^{\rm trunc}(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})-(1+a)\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm trunc}(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})}_{(A)}+\underbrace{\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})-\mathcal{L}^{\rm trunc}(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})}_{(B)}

\displaystyle\quad+(1+a)\underbrace{\inf_{\mathbf{s}_{V,\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{s}_{V,\bm{\theta}})}_{(C)},

where
a>0
is arbitrary, and
\mathcal{L}^{\rm trunc}(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})
is a truncated loss defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\rm trunc}(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim P_{\rm data}}\left[\ell(\mathbf{x};\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})\mathds{1}\{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\leq R\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right]

for some radius
R>0
to be determined, and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm trunc}
is the empirical counterpart of
\mathcal{L}^{\rm trunc}
. We truncate on
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}
to achieve an uniform upper bound on the loss
\mathcal{L}
for concentration. Here term
(A)
is the statistical error due to finite samples, term
(B)
is the truncation error, term
(C)
reflects the approximation error of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
. We bound these error terms separately.

\bullet

Bounding term
(A)
. Suppose we choose
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
as in Theorem
1
with
\epsilon
to be determined. For term
(A)
, we apply a Bernstein-type concentration inequality (Lemma
15
) to obtain with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle(A)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{R^{2}+K^{2}}{t_{0}an}\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(1/(t_{0}n),{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)}{\delta}+\frac{1}{n}\right).

Here
\mathcal{N}(\tau,{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
is the covering number of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN}
under a properly defined norm. (The choice of norm is involved; see details in Appendix
B.2
.)

\bullet

Bounding term
(B)
. Term
(B)
is relatively simple and share the same high-level idea of bounding the truncation error in Theorem
1
. As a result, we have

\displaystyle(B)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{t_{0}}K^{2}R^{d}\exp(-C_{2}R^{2}/2)\right).

\bullet

Bounding term
(C)
. Denote
\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}}
as the approximator constructed in Theorem
1
, and we further decompose
(C)
into two terms,

\displaystyle(C)\leq\underbrace{\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})-(1+a)\mathcal{L}^{\rm trunc}(\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})}_{(C_{1})}+(1+a)\underbrace{\mathcal{L}^{\rm trunc}(\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})}_{(C_{2})}.

Term
(C_{1})
is very similar to term
(A)
. Recall
P_{z}
has a light tail. Thus, with high probability, we have
(C_{1})=\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm trunc}(\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})-(1+a)\mathcal{L}^{\rm trunc}(\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}})
, which allows us to apply Bernstein-type concentration. Since
\bar{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}}
is independent of data,
(C_{1})
converges rather fast. Term
(C_{2})
is the approximation error and we bound it by

\displaystyle(C_{2})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d}{t_{0}(T-t_{0})}\epsilon^{2}\right)+E,

where
E
is the gap between equivalent losses
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{s}_{V,\bm{\theta}})
and
\frac{1}{T-t_{0}}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{V,\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,t)-\nabla\log p_{t}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(P_{t})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
. Such a gap is independent of
\mathbf{s}_{V,\bm{\theta}}
(see derivation in
Chen et al. 2022b
).

\bullet

Putting
(A)
,
(B)
,
(C)
together
. We choose
a=\epsilon^{2}
and
R=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{d\log d+\log K+\log\frac{n}{\delta}}\right)
. Summing up
(A)
,
(B)
and
(C)
gives rise to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-t_{0}}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{V,\bm{\theta}}(\cdot,t)-\nabla\log p_{t}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(P_{t})}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{-d-3-2\delta(n)}}{t_{0}n}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{t_{0}}+\frac{1}{n}\right).

We have plugged in an upper bound on the covering number
\mathcal{N}(1/(t_{0}n),{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm NN},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
and omitted a
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
term. Optimally choosing
\epsilon=n^{-\frac{1-\delta(n)}{d+5}}
yields the desired result.

6.3
Proof of Theorem
3

We will be succinct on how to prove items 1 and 3, and focus on the proof of item 2. The intuition behind item 1 is that the mismatch between the column span of
A
and
V
will be significantly amplified due to the blowup of the orthogonal score. Therefore, an accurate neural score estimator forces
A
and
V
to match. Item 3 can be obtained by analytically solving the orthogonal backward process.

\bullet

Proof of item 2
. We consider the continuous-time generated distribution
\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}
for an exposure of the main idea. The discrete result is obtained by adding discretization error (Lemma
4
).

For the ground-truth backward process, we consider the corresponding latent backward process
\mathbf{Z}^{\leftarrow}_{t}=A^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\leftarrow}_{t}
, which satisfies the following SDE

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Z}^{\leftarrow}_{t}=\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}^{\leftarrow}_{t}+\nabla\log p^{\sf LD}_{T-t}(\mathbf{Z}^{\leftarrow}_{t})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{\mathbf{W}}^{\sf LD}_{t},

where
\overline{\mathbf{W}}_{t}^{\sf LD}
is a standard Wiener process in the latent space.

For the learned process, similarly we consider
{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\leftarrow,r}}_{t}=U^{\top}V^{\top}{\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\leftarrow}}_{t}
. We first show that
({\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\leftarrow,r}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the following SDE

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\leftarrow,r}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{2}{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\leftarrow,r}}_{t}+\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}^{\sf LD}_{\theta,U}({\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\leftarrow,r}}_{t},T-t)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{\mathbf{W}}^{\sf LD}_{t},

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{U,\bm{\theta}}^{\sf LD}(\mathbf{z},t)=\frac{1}{h(t)}[U^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(U\mathbf{z},t)-\mathbf{z}]
is the latent score estimator.

Observe that
P_{t_{0}}^{\sf LD}
is the marginal distribution of
\mathbf{Z}^{\leftarrow}_{T-t_{0}}
, and
(VU)_{\sharp}^{\top}\widehat{P}_{t_{0}}
is the marginal distribution of
{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}^{\leftarrow,r}}_{T-t_{0}}
.
To this end, it suffices to bound the divergence between the two stochastic processes above. In the proof, we first convert the score matching error bound to the latent score matching error between
\nabla\log p^{\sf LD}_{t}(\mathbf{z})
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{U,\bm{\theta}}^{\sf LD}(\mathbf{z},t)
. Then, similar to
Chen et al. 2022b
, we adopt Girsanov’s Theorem and bound the difference of the KL divergence of the two process via the error bound of their drift terms.

7
Conclusion and discussion

This paper studies distribution estimation of diffusion models for low-dimensional linear subspace data. We show that with a properly chosen neural network, the score function can be accurately approximated and estimated. The estimation error converges at a rate depending on the data intrinsic dimension. We further show data distribution can be efficiently learned using the estimated score function. The convergence rate is also free of the curse of ambient dimensionality.

Linear subspace assumption

Diffusion models are very new in the field of machine learning theory. The theoretical analysis has been very challenging, especially when taking the intrinsic geometric structures of the data into consideration. Although we make a linear subspace assumption, characterizing the behavior of diffusion models in the on-support and orthogonal subspaces has already required highly non-trivial analysis. We expect to stimulate more sophisticated followup works, which analyze diffusion models under more general assumptions such as manifold data.

End-to-end distribution learning

Given our linear subspace assumption, one may advocate PCA-like methods, which first reduce the data dimension by estimating the subspace structure, and then estimate the
data distribution on a projected subspace. However, such a two-step method is rarely used in practice, and does not necessarily help us understand the empirical success of diffusion models. On the contrary, our results consider a more realistic end-to-end learning scheme, and show that the learned diffusion model can capture the unknown linear structure and the data distribution, and enjoy fast distribution estimation guarantees with a proper score network.

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\nabla\int\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}{\int\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}=\frac{\int\nabla\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}{\int\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}},

(7)

where the last equality holds since
\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})
is continuously differentiable in
\mathbf{x}
. Substituting
\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})
into (
7
) gives rise to

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{-D/2}h^{-D/2}(t)}{\int\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}\int\frac{1}{h(t)}\left(\alpha(t)A\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\lVert\alpha(t)A\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{-D/2}h^{-D/2}(t)}{\int\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}\int\frac{1}{h(t)}\left(\alpha(t)A\mathbf{z}-AA^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\lVert\alpha(t)A\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{(2\pi)^{-D/2}h^{-D/2}(t)}{\int\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}\int\frac{1}{h(t)}\left(I_{D}-AA^{\top}\right)\mathbf{x}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\lVert\alpha(t)A\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\int\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}\int\frac{1}{h(t)}\left(\alpha(t)A\mathbf{z}-AA^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}}\underbrace{-\frac{1}{h(t)}\left(I_{D}-AA^{\top}\right)\mathbf{x}}_{\mathbf{s}_{\perp}}.

We can further simplify
\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}
. We decompose
\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})
as

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-D/2}h^{-D/2}(t)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\lVert\alpha(t)A\mathbf{z}-AA^{\top}\mathbf{x}+\left(I_{D}-AA^{\top}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-D/2}h^{-D/2}(t)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left(\left\lVert\alpha(t)A\mathbf{z}-AA^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\left(I_{D}-AA^{\top}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}h^{-d/2}(t)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\lVert\alpha(t)\mathbf{z}-A^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times(2\pi)^{-(D-d)/2}h^{-(D-d)/2}(t)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\lVert\left(I_{D}-AA^{\top}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right).

We denote

\displaystyle\phi_{t}\left(A^{\top}\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z}\right)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}h^{-d/2}(t)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\lVert\alpha(t)\mathbf{z}-A^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\phi_{t}\left((I_{D}-AA^{\top})\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-(D-d)/2}h^{-(D-d)/2}(t)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\lVert\left(I_{D}-AA^{\top}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)

\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x}|A\mathbf{z})=\phi_{t}\left(A^{\top}\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z}\right)\phi_{t}\left((I_{D}-AA^{\top})\mathbf{x}\right)
into

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{1}{\int\phi_{t}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}\int\frac{1}{h(t)}\left(\alpha(t)A\mathbf{z}-AA^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)\phi_{t}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}.

As can be seen,
\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}
only depends on the projected data
A^{\top}\mathbf{x}
. Therefore, it is legitimate to overload
\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{x},t)
by
\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x},t)
. The benefit is that the first input of
\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x},t)
now has the intrinsic dimension

\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=A^{\top}\mathbf{x}

\frac{1}{h(t)}(\alpha(t)\mathbf{z}-A^{\top}\mathbf{x})\phi_{t}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}\phi_{t}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z})

\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}\phi_{t}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})}{\int\phi_{t}(\mathbf{z}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}=A\nabla\log p_{t}^{\rm ld}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x})
. The proof is complete.
∎

We find the marginal distribution
P_{t}
of the forward process is still Gaussian. Density function
p_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\int\phi_{t}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}
. We check

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\lVert A^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\alpha(t)\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\mathbf{z}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{z}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}-\alpha(t)\left(\alpha^{2}(t)I_{d}+h(t)\Sigma^{-1}\right)^{-1}A^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{\left(\alpha^{2}(t)I_{d}+h(t)\Sigma^{-1}\right)^{-1}}^{2}\right),

where
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{A}=\mathbf{x}^{\top}A\mathbf{x}
. Therefore,
\phi_{t}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})p_{z}(\mathbf{z})
corresponds to a Gaussian distribution with mean vector
\alpha(t)\left(\alpha^{2}(t)I_{d}+h(t)\Sigma^{-1}\right)^{-1}A^{\top}\mathbf{x}
. To this end, Lemma
1
leads to

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(A^{\top}\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h(t)}\left(\alpha^{2}(t)A\left(\alpha^{2}(t)I_{d}+h(t)\Sigma^{-1}\right)^{-1}A^{\top}\mathbf{x}-AA^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h(t)}A\left({\rm diag}\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}(t)}{\alpha^{2}(t)+h(t)\lambda_{1}^{-2}},\dots,\frac{\alpha^{2}(t)}{\alpha^{2}(t)+h(t)\lambda_{d}^{-2}}\right)-I_{d}\right)A^{\top}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=A~{\rm diag}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}^{-2}}{\alpha^{2}(t)+h(t)\lambda_{1}^{-2}},\dots,\frac{\lambda^{-2}_{d}}{\alpha^{2}(t)+h(t)\lambda_{1}^{-2}}\right)A^{\top}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=A~{\rm diag}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{1}^{2}+h(t)},\dots,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{d}^{2}+h(t)}\right)A^{\top}\mathbf{x}.

Lastly, we check

is Lipschitz continuous. We need to upper bound

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\rm diag}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{1}^{2}+h(t)},\dots,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{d}^{2}+h(t)}\right)\right\rVert_{\rm op}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{d}^{2}+h(t)}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{d}^{2}+(1-\lambda_{d}^{2})h(t)}.

We discuss two cases. If

\frac{1}{\lambda_{d}^{2}+(1-\lambda_{d}^{2})h(t)}\leq 1
; if
\lambda_{d}\leq 1

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\rm diag}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{1}^{2}+h(t)},\dots,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{d}^{2}+h(t)}\right)\right\rVert_{\rm op}\leq\max\{\lambda_{d}^{-2},1\}.

For the Lipschitzness with respect to
t
, we take time derivative of

:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\rm diag}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{1}^{2}+h(t)},\dots,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{d}^{2}+h(t)}\right)

\displaystyle={\rm diag}\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}(t)(\lambda_{1}^{2}-1)}{(\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{1}^{2}+h(t))^{2}},\dots,\frac{\alpha^{2}(t)(\lambda_{d}^{2}-1)}{(\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{d}^{2}+h(t))^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\preceq{\rm diag}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{1}^{2}+h(t)},\dots,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{d}^{2}+h(t)}\right).

Therefore, for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T]
and
\mathbf{z}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{z},t_{1})-\mathbf{s}_{\parallel}(\mathbf{z},t_{2})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert{\rm diag}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{1}^{2}+h(t)},\dots,\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}(t)\lambda_{d}^{2}+h(t)}\right)\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}|t_{1}-t_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\lambda_{d}^{-2},1\}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}|t_{1}-t_{2}|.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\psi^{2}(-\eta)+\int_{0}^{(K_{T}-1)\eta}\psi^{2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{\psi^{2}((K_{T}-1)\eta)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+(e^{T}-1)^{2}[1/(e^{T}-e^{T-t_{0}-\eta})-1/(e^{T}-1)]}{\psi^{2}(T-t_{0}-\eta)}

(
\psi^{2}(-\eta)\leq 1
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(e^{T}-1)^{2}(e^{T}-e^{T-t_{0}-\eta})}{\psi^{2}(T-t_{0}-\eta)}

(when
T\geq 1
)

\displaystyle\leq e^{t_{0}+\eta}-1

\displaystyle\leq e(t_{0}+\eta).

(when
t_{0}+\eta\leq 1
)

∎

Bernstein-type concentration inequality

The following concentration bound is useful in the proof of Theorem
2
.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a bounded function class, i.e., there exists a constant
B
such that any
g\in\mathcal{G}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto[0,B]
. Let
\mathbf{z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be i.i.d. random variables. For any
\delta\in(0,1)
,
a\leq 1
, and
\tau>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]>\frac{(1+3/a)B}{3n}\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}{\delta}+(2+a)\tau\right)\leq\delta\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]-\frac{1+a}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})>\frac{(1+6/a)B}{3n}\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}{\delta}+(2+a)\tau\right)\leq\delta.

Proof.

The proof utilizes Bernstein-type inequalities. Consider the deviation
\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]
first. Let
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}
be a discretization of
\mathcal{G}
, where
\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})
is the covering number with respect to the function
L_{\infty}
norm. Then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]\leq\max_{k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{k}(\mathbf{z}_{i})-2\mathbb{E}[g_{k}(\mathbf{z})]+(2+a)\tau,

as for any
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we can find some
g_{k^{\star}}
such that
\left\lVert g-g_{k^{\star}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau
. Therefore, it is enough to show

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{k}(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g_{k}(\mathbf{z})]>\frac{(1+3/a)B}{3n}\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}{\delta}\right)\leq\delta.

By union bound, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{k}(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g_{k}(\mathbf{z})]>\frac{(1+3/a)B}{3n}\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 72.26999pt\leq\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g_{1}(\mathbf{z})]>\frac{(1+3/a)B}{3n}\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}{\delta}\right).

Therefore, it further suffices to provide an upper bound on

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]>\frac{(1+3/a)B}{3n}\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}{\delta}\right),

where
g\in\mathcal{G}
is any fixed function. Let
\lambda>0
be some parameter to be chosen later. Chernoff bound yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]>\frac{(1+3/a)B}{3n}\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 122.85876pt\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]\right)\right)\right]}{\exp\left(\frac{(1+3/a)\lambda B}{3n}\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}{\delta}\right)}.

(13)

It remains to find
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]\right)\right)\right]
. We rewrite

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-a\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]-\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]-\frac{a}{B}\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(\mathbf{z})].

Introducing independent ghost samples
\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{1},\dots,\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]-\frac{a}{B}\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(\mathbf{z})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{i})\right]-\frac{a}{B}\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(\mathbf{z})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-g(\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{i})\right]-\frac{a}{2B}\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(\mathbf{z})+g^{2}(\bar{\mathbf{z}})]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-g(\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{i})\right]-\frac{a}{2B}\Var\left[g(\mathbf{z})-g(\bar{\mathbf{z}})\right],

where inequality
(i)
invokes identity
\Var\left[g(\mathbf{z})-g(\bar{\mathbf{z}})\right]=\mathbb{E}[(g(\mathbf{z})-g(\bar{\mathbf{z}}))^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(\mathbf{z})+g^{2}(\bar{\mathbf{z}})]
. For convenience, we denote
h_{i}=g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-g(\bar{\mathbf{z}}_{i})
. For
0<\lambda<3n/B
, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\lambda}{n}h_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[1+\frac{\lambda}{n}h_{i}+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda/n)^{j}h_{i}^{j}}{j!}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}1+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda/n)^{j}B^{j-2}}{2\cdot 3^{j-2}}h_{i}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2n^{2}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{\lambda B}{3n}}\mathbb{E}[h_{i}^{2}]

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\leq}\exp\left(\frac{3\lambda^{2}}{6n^{2}-2\lambda Bn}\Var(h_{i})\right),

where inequality
(i)
follows from
\mathbb{E}[h_{i}]=0
and
|h_{i}|\leq B
, and inequality
(ii)
invokes
1+x\leq\exp(x)
for
x\geq 0
. To this end, we derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-2\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}-\frac{\lambda a}{2Bn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var[h_{i}]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{3\lambda^{2}}{6n^{2}-2\lambda Bn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var[h_{i}]-\frac{\lambda a}{2Bn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Var[h_{i}]\right),

where
(i)
follows from Jensen’s inequality.
We choose
\lambda=\frac{3n}{(1+3/a)B}
, which satisfies
\frac{3\lambda^{2}}{6n^{2}-2\lambda Bn}=\frac{\lambda a}{2Bn}
and
\lambda<3n/B
. Substituting into (
E
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-(1+a)\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]>\frac{(1+3/a)B}{3n}\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{G},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty})}.

Therefore, the first inequality is proved. The second inequality can be proved in the exact same argument, by observing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]-\frac{1+a}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})

\displaystyle=2\left(\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-\frac{a}{2}\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{z})]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(\mathbf{z}_{i})-\frac{a}{2B}\mathbb{E}[g^{2}(\mathbf{z})]\right).

The proof is complete.
∎

Tail bound of Gaussian integral

Tail bounds of Gaussian integrals appear frequently in score approximation and estimation theories. We show the following results.

Lemma 16
.

Consider a probability density function
p(\mathbf{x})=\exp\left(-C\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2\right)
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and constant
C>0
. Let
R>0
be a fixed radius. Then it holds

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}>R}p(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2d\pi^{d/2}}{C\Gamma(d/2+1)}R^{d-2}\exp(-CR^{2}/2),

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}>R}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}p(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2d\pi^{d/2}}{C\Gamma(d/2+1)}R^{d}\exp(-CR^{2}/2).

Proof.

We apply change of variable using polar coordinate systems. For the first integral, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}>R}p(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}>R}\exp(-C\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{R}^{\infty}\int_{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1}}r^{d-1}\exp\left(-Cr^{2}/2\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin^{d-j-1}(\theta_{j})~\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{d-1}

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\frac{d\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}\int_{R}^{\infty}r^{d-1}\exp\left(-Cr^{2}/2\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{=}\frac{d(2\pi)^{d/2}}{2C^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2+1)}\int_{CR^{2}/2}^{\infty}u^{d/2-1}\exp(-u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{d/2}}{C^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2+1)}\int_{(CR^{2}/2)^{d/2}}^{\infty}\exp\left(-v^{2/d}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\overset{(iii)}{\leq}\frac{2d\pi^{d/2}}{C\Gamma(d/2+1)}R^{d-2}\exp(-CR^{2}/2).

In
(i)
, we invoke the identity
\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1}}r^{d-1}\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin^{d-j-1}(\theta_{j})~\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{d-1}=\int_{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}
being the volume of a unit
d
-ball. To obtain
(ii)
, we change variable by letting
u=Cr^{2}/2
. Inequality
(iii)
bounds the upper tail of incomplete gamma function
(
Qi and Mei 1999
, Inequality (10) with
\alpha=2/d,A=-d
)
.

A similar argument can be applied to the second integral:

\displaystyle\int_{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}>R}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}p(\mathbf{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}>R}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\exp(-C\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{R}^{\infty}\int_{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1}}r^{d+1}\exp\left(-Cr^{2}/2\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin^{d-j-1}(\theta_{j})~\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{d-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{d\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}\int_{R}^{\infty}r^{d+1}\exp\left(-Cr^{2}/2\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\frac{d\pi^{d/2}}{(d+2)\Gamma(d/2+1)}\left(\frac{2}{C}\right)^{d/2+1}\int_{(CR^{2}/2)^{d/2+1}}^{\infty}\exp\left(-v^{2/(d+2)}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2d\pi^{d/2}}{C\Gamma(d/2+1)}R^{d}\exp(-CR^{2}/2).

The proof is complete.
∎

Matrix norm inequalities

The following lemma deals with matrices with orthonormal columns, whose linear span is approximately equal. These are useful results in deriving score estimation error bounds in Theorem
3
.

Lemma 17
.

Let
A,V\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}
with
d<D
be two matrices with orthonormal columns, i.e.,
A^{\top}A=V^{\top}V=I_{d}
. Given any
\epsilon>0
, if
\|(I_{D}-VV^{\top})A\|^{2}_{\rm F}\leq\epsilon
, then the following holds

(a).

\displaystyle\left\|(I_{D}-AA^{\top})V\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon,

\displaystyle\left\|VV^{\top}-AA^{\top}\right\|^{2}_{\rm F}

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon,

\displaystyle\left\|V^{\top}AA^{\top}V-I_{d}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon.

(b).

There exists an orthogonal matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that

\left\|U-V^{\top}A\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}\leq 2\epsilon.

Proof of Lemma
17
.

The first set of results in item (a) follows from some algebraic manipulation. Consider
\left\lVert\left(I_{D}-AA^{\top}\right)V\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}
first. We have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(I_{D}-AA^{\top}\right)V\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left(\left(V-AA^{\top}V\right)\left(V-AA^{\top}V\right)^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(VV^{\top}-AA^{\top}VV^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(VV^{\top}-AA^{\top}VV^{\top}-VV^{\top}AA^{\top}+AA^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(\left(AA^{\top}-VV^{\top}\right)\left(AA^{\top}-VV^{\top}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert AA^{\top}-VV^{\top}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2},

where
(i)
follows from
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(VV^{\top})=d=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(AA^{\top})
. Similarly, we have

\left\lVert\left(I_{D}-VV^{\top}\right)A\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert AA^{\top}-VV^{\top}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}.

Next we consider
\|V^{\top}AA^{\top}V-I_{d}\|_{\rm F}^{2}
. We have

\displaystyle\left\|V^{\top}AA^{\top}V-I_{d}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(V^{\top}AA^{\top}VV^{\top}AA^{\top}V-2V^{\top}AA^{\top}V+I_{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(VV^{\top}AA^{\top}(VV^{\top}-I_{D})AA^{\top}+(I_{D}-VV^{\top})AA^{\top}-AA^{\top}+I_{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(VV^{\top}AA^{\top}(VV^{\top}-I_{D})AA^{\top}+(I_{D}-VV^{\top})AA^{\top}\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(AA^{\top}-I_{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left((VV^{\top}AA^{\top}-I_{D})(VV^{\top}-I_{D})AA^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left((VV^{\top}AA^{\top}-VV^{\top})(VV^{\top}-I_{D})AA^{\top}\right)+\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left((VV^{\top}-I_{D})(VV^{\top}-I_{D})AA^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|VV^{\top}(AA^{\top}-I_{D})\right\|_{\rm F}\cdot\left\|(VV^{\top}-I_{D})AA^{\top}\right\|_{\rm F}+\left\|(VV^{\top}-I_{D})A\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\epsilon=2\epsilon.

For item (b), we consider the SVD decomposition of
V^{\top}A
. Let
V^{\top}A=W_{1}^{\top}\Sigma W_{2}
, where
W_{1},W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are orthogonal matrices, and
\Sigma={\rm diag}(s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{d})
are diagonal matrix with
s_{1},\dots,s_{d}
being the singular values of
V^{\top}A
. Then we have

\left\|V^{\top}AA^{\top}V-I_{d}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(s_{i}^{2}-1)^{2}.

Let
U=W_{1}^{\top}W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Then we know that
U
is orthonormal. We have

\displaystyle\left\|U-V^{\top}A\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(s_{i}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}(s_{i}-1)^{2}(s_{i}+1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(s_{i}^{2}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|V^{\top}AA^{\top}V-I_{d}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}.

The proof is complete.
∎
