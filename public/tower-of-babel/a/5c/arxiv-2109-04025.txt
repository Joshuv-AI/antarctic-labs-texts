Introduction

Lattices are geometric objects that look like regular orderings of points in real space. More formally, a lattice
\mathcal{L}
is the set of all integer linear combinations of some linearly independent vectors
\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
The matrix
B=(\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{n})
whose columns are these vectors is called a
basis
of
\mathcal{L}
, and we denote the lattice it generates by
\mathcal{L}(B)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(B):=\{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\boldsymbol{b}_{i}:a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}}\}
. The number of vectors
n
in a basis is an invariant of
\mathcal{L}
, and is called its
rank
.

In recent years, lattices have played a central role in both cryptanalysis and the design of secure cryptosystems. One very attractive quality of many lattice-based cryptosystems (e.g.,
[
Ajt98
,
AD97
,
MR07
,
Reg09
,
GPV08
]
) is that they are secure assuming that certain key lattice problems are sufficiently hard to approximate in the
worst case
.
Motivated by this and myriad other applications of lattices in computer science, many works have studied the
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness of both exact and approximate lattice problems (e.g.,
[
vEB81
,
ABSS97
,
Ajt98
,
Mic00
,
Mic01
,
Kho06
,
Kho05
,
LLM06
,
HR12
,
Mic12
]
).
More recently, motivated especially by the need to understand the
concrete
security of lattice-based cryptosystems, a number of works
[
BGS17
,
AS18
,
BP20
,
AC21
,
ABGS21
]
have studied the
fine-grained
complexity of lattice problems. That is, for a meaningful real-world security bound, it is not enough to say merely that there is no
polynomial-time
algorithm for a suitable lattice problem. Rather, a key goal is to show
2^{\Omega(n)}
-hardness, or even
2^{Cn}
-hardness for some explicit
C>0
, of a problem under general-purpose complexity-theoretic assumptions, like variants of the (Strong) Exponential Time Hypothesis.

In this work, we extend the latter line of research by showing improved fine-grained complexity results for two key lattice problems,
the Bounded Distance Decoding Problem (
\mathrm{BDD}
) and the Shortest Vector Problem (
\mathrm{SVP}
). To define these problems, we first recall some notation.
Let
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert
denote the
minimum distance
of
\mathcal{L}
, i.e., the length of a shortest non-zero vector in
\mathcal{L}
, and let
\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}}\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{v}\rVert
denote the distance between a target vector
\boldsymbol{t}
and
\mathcal{L}
. When using the
\ell_{p}
norm, we denote these quantities by
\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})
and
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})
, respectively.

BDD and SVP.

The Bounded Distance Decoding Problem in the
\ell_{p}
norm for relative distance
\alpha
, denoted
\mathrm{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
, is the search promise problem defined as follows: given a basis
B
of a lattice
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(B)
and a target vector
\boldsymbol{t}
satisfying
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})\leq\alpha\cdot\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})
as input, the goal is to find a closest lattice vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
to the target vector
\boldsymbol{t}
such that
\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{p}=\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})
. (We note that
\boldsymbol{v}
is guaranteed to be unique when
\alpha<1/2
, but that
\mathrm{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
is well-defined for any
\alpha=\alpha(n)>0
.)
The
\gamma
-approximate Shortest Vector Problem in the
\ell_{p}
norm, denoted
\mathrm{SVP}_{p,\gamma}
, is the decision promise problem defined as follows: given a basis
B
of a lattice
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(B)
and a distance threshold
r>0
as input, the goal is to decide whether
\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})\leq r
(i.e., the input is a YES instance) or
\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})>\gamma r
(i.e., the input is a NO instance), with the promise that one of the two cases holds.
1
1

1

In other literature, this decision problem is often called
\mathrm{GapSVP}_{p,\gamma}
, whereas
\mathrm{SVP}_{p,\gamma}
usually denotes the corresponding
search
problem (of finding a nonzero lattice vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
for which
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert\leq\gamma\cdot\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})
, given an arbitrary lattice
\mathcal{L}
.) There is a trivial reduction from the decision problem to the search problem, so any hardness of the former implies identical hardness of the latter.

Although it seems far out of reach using known techniques, proving that
\mathrm{SVP}_{p,\gamma}
is hard for a sufficiently large polynomial approximation factor
\gamma=\gamma(n)
, or that
\mathrm{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
is hard for sufficiently small inverse-polynomial relative distance
\alpha=\alpha(n)
, would imply the provable security of lattice-based cryptography.
2
2

2

We note that the relative distance
\alpha
in
\mathrm{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
is not an approximation factor
per se
, but it is analogous to one in a precise sense. Namely, for
p=2
there is a rank-preserving reduction from
\mathrm{BDD}_{2,\alpha}
to
\mathrm{SVP}_{2,\gamma}
with
\gamma=O(1/\alpha)

[
LM09
,
BSW16
]
, so sufficiently strong (fine-grained) hardness of the former problem translates to corresponding hardness for the latter problem. A similar reduction holds in reverse, but with a larger loss:
\alpha=\Omega(\sqrt{n/\log n}/\gamma)
.

On the other hand, most concrete security estimates for lattice-based cryptosystems
are based on the runtimes of the fastest known (possibly heuristic) algorithms for exact or near-exact
\mathrm{SVP}
. So, apart from its inherent theoretical interest, understanding the fine-grained complexity of (near-)exact
\mathrm{SVP}
and
\mathrm{BDD}
sheds light on questions of great practical importance.

Our results assume (randomized or non-uniform) “gap” variants of the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) and Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH). Recall that “plain” ETH asserts that solving the

1.2
Our Techniques

1.2.1
Locally Dense Lattices

As in nearly all prior work on the complexity of
\mathrm{BDD}
and
\mathrm{SVP}
(e.g.,
[
Mic01
,
Kho05
,
LLM06
,
AS18
,
BP20
]
), a key component of our results is the construction of a family of “locally dense” lattices, which are specified by a lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
and corresponding target vector
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
. For our purposes, a locally dense lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
is one with few “short” vectors, many vectors “close” to
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
, but few vectors “too close” to
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
.
(Other works such as
[
Mic14
]
define locally dense lattices in a closely related but different way, e.g., without the requirement of few “too close” vectors.)

For a discrete set
S
, which we will take to be a lattice or a subset of a lattice, define

\displaystyle N_{p}(S,r,\boldsymbol{t})

\displaystyle:=\left|{\{{\boldsymbol{x}\in S:\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{p}\leq r}\}}\right|\ \text{,}

\displaystyle N_{p}^{o}(S,r,\boldsymbol{t})

\displaystyle:=\left|{\{{\boldsymbol{x}\in S:\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{p}<r}\}}\right|\ \text{.}

Somewhat more formally, we define a locally dense lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
,
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
with relative distance
\alpha_{G}
in the
\ell_{p}
norm to be one for which

N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},\alpha_{G},\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger})\geq\nu^{n}\cdot N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},1,\boldsymbol{0})

(1)

for some
\nu>1
. That is,
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
,
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
is such that the number
G
of “close vectors” (within distance
\alpha_{G}
of
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
) is an exponential factor larger than the number of short vectors (of norm at most one). Similarly, we require there to be an exponential factor more close vectors than “too close” vectors, along with some other technical conditions. We defer discussing these issues until the main body of the paper, and for the remainder of the introduction focus on the constraint in
Equation

1
.

A crux in showing hardness of

\mathrm{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
and
\mathrm{SVP}_{p,\gamma}
is constructing good locally dense lattices, and their parameters govern the precise hardness results that we can obtain. A family of locally dense lattices with smaller relative distance
\alpha_{G}

and larger
\nu
leads to stronger hardness results.
To obtain ETH-type hardness results, we simply need
\nu
to be a constant greater than
1
, and then we can show
2^{\Omega(n)}
-hardness of
\mathrm{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
for any constant
\alpha>\alpha_{G}
.
For SETH-type hardness results, we get
2^{n/C}
-hardness of
\mathrm{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
whenever our reduction to
\mathrm{BDD}
has a multiplicative rank-increase factor of
C
.
The value of
C
depends on the gap factor
\nu
in
Equation

1
, so to show such hardness for explicit
C>0
we need an explicit lower bound on
\nu
.
Our reductions also give a tradeoff between
C
and
\alpha
, as shown in the right plot in
Figure

1
.
The full situation is actually a bit more complicated when taking “too close” vectors into account, but we again defer discussing this for now.
The situation for
\mathrm{SVP}
is similar to the situation for
\mathrm{BDD}
.

1.2.2
Sparsification

An important technique in our work is randomized lattice sparsification, an efficient algorithm that essentially does the following.
Given (a basis of) a lattice
\mathcal{L}
and an index
q\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
as input, the algorithm randomly samples a sublattice
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
such that for any fixed, finite set of lattice points
S\subseteq\mathcal{L}
satisfying some mild conditions,
\left|{S\cap\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\right|\approx\left|{S}\right|/q
with probability near
1
.
A variant of this algorithm, additionally given
\boldsymbol{t}\in\lspan(\mathcal{L})
as input, randomly samples
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
and
\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
such that for any fixed, finite set of points
S\subseteq\mathcal{L}-\boldsymbol{t}
satisfying some mild conditions,
\left|{S\cap(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})}\right|\approx\left|{S}\right|/q
with probability near
1
.

Intuitively, some mild caveats aside, sparsification says that a lattice with few short vectors (and few “too close” vectors) is just as good as a lattice with
no
short non-zero vectors (and no “too close” vectors), since the latter can be efficiently obtained from the former.
Indeed, sparsifying with index
q\approx N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{0})
allows us to turn a lattice
\mathcal{L}
and target
\boldsymbol{t}
satisfying, say,
N_{p}(\mathcal{L},\alpha r,\boldsymbol{t})\geq 100\cdot N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{0})
into a lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
and target
\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
with
N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\alpha r,\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})\geq 1
and
N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\},r,\boldsymbol{0})=0
, so
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\mathcal{L})\leq\alpha r
and
\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})\geq r
.
That is, the output
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
satisfies the
\mathrm{BDD}
promise
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\mathcal{L})\leq\alpha\cdot\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})
.
See
Section

2.2
for a formal description of sparsification.

1.2.3
A Transformation Using Locally Dense Lattices

Define

\mathrm{CVP}^{\prime}_{p,\gamma}
to be the following variant of the decision version of the
\gamma
-approximate Closest Vector Problem: given a basis
B
of a rank-
n
lattice
\mathcal{L}
and a target vector
\boldsymbol{t}
as input, decide whether there exists
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{{0,1}\}^{n}
such that
\lVert B\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{t}\rVert_{p}\leq 1
(i.e., the input is a YES instance), or whether
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L})>\gamma
(i.e., the input is a NO instance), under the promise that one of the two cases holds. In other words,
\mathrm{CVP}^{\prime}
is the variant of
\mathrm{CVP}
that asks whether there is a
binary
combination of basis vectors close to the target. Much is known about the (fine-grained) complexity of
\mathrm{CVP}^{\prime}
,
which will be useful for us (see
Theorems

2.17
and
2.18
).

Our reductions from
\mathrm{CVP}^{\prime}
to
\mathrm{BDD}
and to
\mathrm{SVP}
have the same basic form. Given a rank-
n^{\prime}
instance
B^{\prime},\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
of
\mathrm{CVP}^{\prime}_{p,\gamma}
for some
\gamma>1
, we apply the following transformation with some scaling factors
s,\ell>0
and some locally dense lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{L}(B^{\dagger})
of rank
n-n^{\prime}
with target
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
satisfying
Equation

1
:

B:=\begin{pmatrix}sB^{\prime}&0\\
I_{n^{\prime}}&0\\
0&\ell B^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\ \text{,}\qquad\boldsymbol{t}:=\begin{pmatrix}s\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\\
\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{n^{\prime}}\\
\ell\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\ \text{.}

(2)

Essentially the same transformation appears in both
[
AS18
]
and
[
BP20
]
, and similar ideas appear in a number of works before that. Our work differs in its constructions of locally dense lattice gadgets
(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
,
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger})
, its more general reductions, and its improved analysis.

Here we give a rough analysis of the transformation using two observations.
First, we observe that appending
I_{n^{\prime}}
to
B^{\prime}
allows us to upper bound the number of short lattice vectors
in
\mathcal{L}(B)
by

N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L}(B),r^{\prime},\boldsymbol{0})\leq N_{p}^{o}(\mathbb{Z}^{n^{\prime}}\oplus\mathcal{L}(\ell B^{\dagger}),r^{\prime},\boldsymbol{0})

(3)

for any
r^{\prime}>0
.
Second, suppose that
B^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
is a YES instance of
\mathrm{CVP}^{\prime}
.
Then there exists
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{{0,1}\}^{n^{\prime}}
such that
\lVert B^{\prime}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\rVert\leq 1
, and hence for each
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-n^{\prime}}
with
\lVert B^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}\rVert_{p}\leq\alpha_{G}
we get that
\lVert B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})-\boldsymbol{t}\rVert_{p}\leq r
, where
r:=(s^{p}+n^{\prime}/2^{p}+(\alpha_{G}\cdot\ell)^{p})^{1/p}
.
So,

N_{p}(\mathcal{L}(B),r,\boldsymbol{t})\geq N_{p}(\mathcal{L}(B^{\dagger}),\alpha_{G},\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger})\ \text{.}

(4)

To transform a YES instance of
\mathrm{CVP}^{\prime}_{p,\gamma}
to a valid instance of
\mathrm{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
, it essentially suffices to set the parameters
r,s,\ell
and use suitable
B^{\dagger}
,
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
so that, say,

N_{p}(\mathcal{L}(B^{\dagger}),\alpha_{G},\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger})\geq 100\cdot N_{p}^{o}(\mathbb{Z}^{n^{\prime}}\oplus\mathcal{L}(\ell B^{\dagger}),r/\alpha,\boldsymbol{0})\ \text{.}

(5)

Indeed, if
Equation

5
holds, then by
Equations

3
and
4
,
N_{p}(\mathcal{L}(B),r,\boldsymbol{t})\geq 100\cdot N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L}(B),r/\alpha,\boldsymbol{0})
.
We can then sparsify
\mathcal{L}(B)
to obtain a lattice with no non-zero vectors of norm less than
r/\alpha
, and at least one vector within distance
r
of
\boldsymbol{t}
, as needed.

We recall that by assumption,
N_{p}(\mathcal{L}(B^{\dagger}),\alpha_{G},\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger})\geq\nu^{n-n^{\prime}}
, which is important for satisfying
Equation

5
since
N_{p}^{o}(\mathbb{Z}^{n^{\prime}}\oplus\mathcal{L}(\ell B^{\dagger}),r/\alpha,\boldsymbol{0})
will typically be exponentially large in
n^{\prime}
.
We also need that if the input
\mathrm{CVP}^{\prime}
instance is a NO instance, then there will be few vectors in
\mathcal{L}(B)
that are close to
\boldsymbol{t}
, which depends on
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
having few vectors “too close” to
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
, but again we defer discussing this. See
Lemma

3.2
for a precise description of the useful properties of the transformation given in
Equation

2
.

When reducing to
\mathrm{SVP}_{p,\gamma^{\prime}}
instead of
\mathrm{BDD}_{p,\alpha}
we apply a further transformation to
B,\boldsymbol{t}
before sparsifying. Namely, we apply Kannan’s embedding, which appends the vector
(\boldsymbol{t},u)
, for some value
u>0
, to
B
to obtain a new basis:

B,\boldsymbol{t},u\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}B&\boldsymbol{t}\\
0&u\end{pmatrix}\ \text{.}

The analysis in this case is a bit more subtle as well—we need to upper bound quantities of the form
N_{p}(\mathcal{L}(B),(r^{p}-(wu)^{p})^{1/p},w\cdot(\boldsymbol{t},u))
not just for
w=0,1
(corresponding to short and “too close” vectors in the
\mathrm{BDD}
case, respectively) but for all integers
w\geq 2
too—but the idea is similar.
In fact, we use a result from
[
AS18
]
(presented in
Theorem

4.2
) that analyzes the combination of Kannan’s embedding and sparsification already, essentially reducing our task to bounding the quantities
N_{p}(\mathcal{L}(B),(r^{p}-(wu)^{p})^{1/p},w\cdot(\boldsymbol{t},u))
.

1.2.4
Specific Locally Dense Lattices

We conclude this summary of techniques by describing the specific locally dense lattices
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}

that we use to instantiate
Equation

2
.
We use two main families of locally dense lattices for our results.

Exponential kissing number lattices.

The first family of locally dense lattices is derived from a family of “exponential kissing number” lattices
\{{\mathcal{L}_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. Here,
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is of rank
n
and has exponential Euclidean kissing number, i.e.,
N_{2}(\mathcal{L}_{n},\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}),\boldsymbol{0})=2^{\Omega(n)}
.
Previously,
[
AS18
]
showed how to use the existence of such a family to prove
2^{\Omega(n)}
-hardness of
\mathrm{SVP}_{p,\gamma}
for all
p\geq 1
and some
\gamma>1
(and in particular, for
1\leq p\leq 2
, for which the result was not already known from other techniques), assuming non-uniform Gap-ETH. However, no such family of lattices was known at the time, and in fact proving the existence of such a family was a longstanding open question.

In seminal work appearing shortly after the publication of
[
AS18
]
, Vlăduţ
[
Vlă19
]
succeeded in constructing such a family of lattices.
Moreover, he proved the existence of such a family with an explicit exponential lower bound.
More specifically, he showed the existence of
\{{\mathcal{L}_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with
N_{2}(\mathcal{L}_{n},\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{n}),\boldsymbol{0})\geq 2^{c_{\mathsf{kn}}n-o(n)}
where
c_{\mathsf{kn}}\geq 0.02194
(see
Theorem

3.6
).

The proofs of
Theorems

1.1
and
1.3
both use these “Vlăduţ lattices” to construct locally dense lattices, but in different ways.
The proof of
Theorem

1.1
constructs a locally dense lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
,
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
with relative distance
\alpha\approx 2^{-c_{\mathsf{kn}}}\approx 0.98491
, but with a non-explicit lower bound on the gap factor
\nu
in
Equation

1
. The proof of
Theorem

1.3
constructs a locally dense lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
,
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
with relative distance
\alpha\approx 1
but with an explicit lower bound on
\nu
—essentially
\nu\geq 2^{c_{\mathsf{kn}}}
. The values
\alpha^{\dagger}_{p,C}
in
Theorem

1.3
are defined to be a certain quantity relating the maximum possible kissing number in a lattice of rank
(C-1)n^{\prime}
, roughly
2^{c_{\mathsf{kn}}\cdot(C-1)n^{\prime}}
, and the number of vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{n^{\prime}}
of norm at most
r
for some
r>0
.

The proof of
Theorem

1.3
is actually somewhat simpler than that of
Theorem

1.1
, so we first give a bit more detail on it. We note that taking
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}:=\mathcal{L}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}:=\boldsymbol{0}
for a Vlăduţ lattice
\mathcal{L}_{n}

almost
yields a locally dense lattice family with
\nu^{n-o(n)}
many close vectors for
\nu\geq 2^{c_{\mathsf{kn}}}
and relative distance
\alpha=1
, but there are two issues:
(1) Vlăduţ lattices have exponential kissing number with respect to the
\ell_{2}
norm rather than general
\ell_{p}
norms, and
(2) the origin is itself a “too close” vector.

We handle issue (1) by applying norm embeddings
[
RR06
]
with distortion
(1+\varepsilon)
to
\{{\mathcal{L}_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
to obtain a family of lattices
\{{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with exponentially high “handshake number” in the
\ell_{p}
norm:

N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{n},(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{n}),\boldsymbol{0})\geq 2^{c_{\mathsf{kn}}n-o(n)}\ \text{,}

where applying the norm embedding is efficient for any
\varepsilon\geq 1/\poly(n)
.
We handle issue (2) by “sparsifying away the origin.”
Specifically, for all sufficiently large
n
we show how to sample a
sublattice
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
satisfying

N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}_{n},(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}_{n}),\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime\prime})\geq N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{n},(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}_{n}),\boldsymbol{0})/4\geq 2^{c_{\mathsf{kn}}n-o(n)}

with positive probability. In particular, this shows that such lattices exist.

For the proof of
Theorem

1.1
, we take
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
to be
\mathcal{L}_{n}
scaled so that
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}_{n})=1
, and take
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
to be a uniformly random vector of norm
\delta
for some appropriately chosen constant
0<\delta<1
. We then analyze
N_{2}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}),\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger})
for some appropriately chosen constant
0<\varepsilon<\delta
. Intuitively, there is a tradeoff between choosing smaller
\delta
, which makes
N_{2}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}),\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger})
larger but requires
\varepsilon
to be smaller, and larger
\delta
, which makes
N_{2}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}),\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger})
smaller but allows for
\varepsilon
to be larger. The relative distance
\alpha
that our reduction achieves is essentially the smallest
\alpha=1-\varepsilon
for which we can ensure that
N_{2}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger},(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}),\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger})\geq 2^{\Omega(n)}
.
To translate these results to general
\ell_{p}
norms, we again use norm embeddings. We also use additional techniques for dealing with “too close” vectors.

We note that the construction of locally dense lattices from lattices with exponential kissing number in
[
AS18
]
does not give explicit bounds on the relative distance
\alpha
or gap factor
\nu
achieved; the reduction there essentially just needs
\nu>1
and
\alpha=1-\varepsilon
for non-explicit
\varepsilon>0
.
On the other hand, the construction in
Theorem

1.1
gives an explicit bound on
\alpha
but not on
\nu
, and the construction in
Theorem

1.3
gives an explicit bound on
\nu
but with
\alpha>1
. Therefore,
Theorems

1.1
and
1.3
can be seen as different refinements of the corresponding analysis in
[
AS18
]
.
A very interesting question is whether it’s possible to get a construction that simultaneously achieves explicit
\nu
and relative distance
\alpha=1-\varepsilon
; our current techniques do not seem to be able to achieve this. Such a construction would lead to new (Gap-)SETH-hardness results for
\mathrm{SVP}
.

The integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

The second family of locally dense lattices that we consider, used to prove
Theorems

1.2
and
1.4
, simply takes
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
to be the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}
to be the all-
t
s vector for some constant
t
(without loss of generality,
t\in[0,1/2]
):

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}:=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\ \text{,}\qquad\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}:=t\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\ \text{.}

This family was also used in
[
AS18
]
and
[
BP20
]
.

We will be especially interested in the case where
t=1/2
. In this case
N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathrm{dist}_{p}(1/2\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{n},\mathbb{Z}^{n}),1/2\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{n})=2^{n}
, where
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(1/2\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{n},\mathbb{Z}^{n})=n^{1/p}/2
. So, for our analysis it essentially suffices to upper bound
N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},r,\boldsymbol{0})
for some
r=n^{1/p}/(2\alpha)
.
We have good techniques for doing this; see
Section

2.3
.
(We note that the “
p_{0}
barrier” mentioned earlier comes from
p_{0}
being the smallest value of
p
satisfying
N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},n^{1/p}/2,\boldsymbol{0})\leq 2^{n}
.)

The SETH-hardness result for
\mathrm{SVP}_{1,p}
for
p>p_{0}
in
[
AS18
]
, the hardness results for
\mathrm{BDD}
in
[
BP20
]
, and the Gap-SETH-hardness result for
\mathrm{SVP}_{\gamma,p}
in
Theorem

1.4
all take
\boldsymbol{t}^{\dagger}=1/2\cdot\boldsymbol{1}
, and analyze
N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},n^{1/p}/2,1/2\cdot\boldsymbol{1})/N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},n^{1/p}/(2\alpha),\boldsymbol{0})=2^{n}/N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},n^{1/p}/(2\alpha),\boldsymbol{0})
for some
\alpha>0
.
That is, they essentially just need to upper bound
N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},n^{1/p}/(2\alpha),\boldsymbol{0})
for some
\alpha
(with
\alpha=1
for the
\mathrm{SVP}
hardness results).
As alluded to in the discussion after the statement of
Theorem

1.2
, the Gap-ETH-hardness result in
[
AS18
]
for
\mathrm{SVP}_{p,\gamma}
essentially works by proving that for every
p>2
there exist
t\in(0,1/2]
and

(not necessarily
t=1/2
or
r=n^{1/p}/2
) such that

\frac{N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},r,t\cdot\boldsymbol{1})}{N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},r/\alpha,\boldsymbol{0})}\geq 2^{\Omega(n)}

(6)

for some non-explicit
\alpha<1
.

We do something similar, but study the more refined question of what the
minimum
value of
\alpha
is such that
Equation

6
holds for some
t
and
r
.
This minimum value of
\alpha
is essentially how we define the quantities
\alpha^{\ddagger}_{p}
used in
Theorem

1.2
; see
Equation

20
for a precise definition.
We note that, interestingly, in experiments this minimum value of
\alpha
is always achieved by simply taking
t=1/2
. That is, empirically it seems that we do not lose anything by fixing
t=1/2
and only varying
r
.
5
5

5

This is especially interesting since
[
EOR91
]
notes that
N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},r,t\cdot\boldsymbol{1})
is
not
maximized by

for some
p>2
, including
p=3
, and some
fixed

. For
1\leq p\leq 2
,

[

]
and
[

]
note that for any fixed

,

(up to a subexponential error term) by taking

.

1.3
Open Questions

One of the most interesting aspects of this and other work on the complexity of lattice problems is the interplay between geometric objects—here, lattices with exponential kissing number and locally dense lattices generally—and hardness results.
Proving a better lower bound on
c_{\mathsf{kn}}
would immediately translate into an improved bound on the values of
\alpha_{\mathsf{kn}}
and
\alpha^{\dagger}_{p,C}
, and more generally proving the existence of some family of gadgets with smaller relative distance
\alpha
and at least
2^{\Omega(n)}
close vectors would translate into a hardness result improving on both
Theorems

1.1
and
1.2
.

There is also the question of
constructing
locally dense lattices.
The difference between existence and efficient randomized construction of locally dense lattices roughly corresponds to needing “non-uniform” versus “randomized” hardness assumptions. It is also an interesting question whether randomness is needed at all for showing hardness of
\mathrm{BDD}
or
\mathrm{SVP}
. In this work we crucially use randomness for sparsification in addition to using it to construct locally dense lattices. Indeed, derandomizing hardness reductions for

\mathrm{SVP}
(and similarly,
\mathrm{BDD}
) is a notorious, decades-old open problem.

instead of
\boldsymbol{w}=(\boldsymbol{u}^{\top},\boldsymbol{v}^{\top})^{\top}
.
We use
\boldsymbol{0}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{1}_{n}
to denote the all-
0
s and all-
1
s vectors of dimension
n
, respectively. We sometimes omit the subscript
n
when it is clear from context.
Finally, we occasionally abuse notation by mixing elements of a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
(for prime
q

2.1
Lattices and Point Counting

A
lattice

\mathcal{L}
is a discrete additive subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
;
concretely, a lattice is the set of all integer linear combinations of a set of linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
This set of vectors is called a
basis
of the lattice. Its cardinality
n
is defined to be the
rank
of the lattice (which turns out to be independent of the choice of basis), and the dimension
m
of the basis vectors is defined to be the
dimension
of the lattice.
A basis is often represented by a matrix
B
whose columns are the vectors in the basis, and the lattice generated by
B
is denoted
\mathcal{L}(B)
.
Using this representation, a rank-
n
lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
with basis
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
can be written as
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(B)=B\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
We denote the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of a matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
by
B^{+}:=(B^{\top}B)^{-1}B^{\top}
. When
B
is a basis and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}(B)
is a lattice vector,
B^{+}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is its coefficient vector.

We recall that for
p\in[1,\infty)
the
\ell_{p}
norm of a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is defined as
\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{p}:=\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|{x_{i}}\right|^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}
,
and for
p=\infty
it is defined as
\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\infty}:=\max_{i=1}^{m}\left|{x_{i}}\right|
.
The
minimum distance

\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})
of a lattice
\mathcal{L}
in the
\ell_{p}
norm is defined to be the minimum length of nonzero vectors in the lattice, i.e.,

\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{p}\ \text{.}

Equivalently, as the name suggests, it is the minimum distance between any two distinct vectors in the lattice.
For
p=2
, we simply write
\lambda_{1}
for
\lambda^{(2)}_{1}
.
We denote the distance between a vector
\boldsymbol{t}
and a lattice
\mathcal{L}
in the
\ell_{p}
norm by

\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{L}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{L}}\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{p}\ \text{.}

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, a discrete set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, a target
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and a distance
r\geq 0
, we define the following two point-counting functions:

\displaystyle N_{p}(S,r,\boldsymbol{t})

\displaystyle:=\left|{\{{\boldsymbol{x}\in S:\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{p}\leq r}\}}\right|\ \text{,}

\displaystyle N_{p}^{o}(S,r,\boldsymbol{t})

\displaystyle:=\left|{\{{\boldsymbol{x}\in S:\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{p}<r}\}}\right|\ \text{.}

We will typically use a lattice or a subset of a lattice as the discrete set
S
.

In the the following claim we observe two simple bounds on point counts in lattices.

Claim 2.1
.

For any lattice
\mathcal{L}
, target
\boldsymbol{t}\in\lspan(\mathcal{L})
, and
r\geq 0
:

1.

N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{0})\geq 2r/\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})-1
;

2.

N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{t})\leq N_{p}(\mathcal{L},2r,\boldsymbol{0})
.

Proof.

For
Item

1
, the inequality is trivial when
r=0
. For
r>0
, we have

N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{0})\geq N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{v}),r,\boldsymbol{0})=1+2\cdot\Bigl\lceil\frac{r}{\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})}-1\Bigr\rceil\geq\frac{2r}{\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})}-1\ \text{,}

where
\boldsymbol{v}
is an arbitrary shortest vector in
\mathcal{L}
with
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{p}=\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})
.

For
Item

2
, if
N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{t})=0
then the inequality is trivial. Otherwise fix
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
to be such that
\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{p}\leq r
. Then for every
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{L}
satisfying
\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{p}\leq r
, we have
\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}
and
\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{p}\leq 2r
by the triangle inequality.
Moreover, for
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\neq\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{u}_{2}-\boldsymbol{v}
.
Hence we know
N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{t})\leq N_{p}(\mathcal{L},2r,\boldsymbol{0})
, as desired.
∎

Given lattice-target pairs
(\mathcal{L}_{1},\boldsymbol{t}_{1})
and
(\mathcal{L}_{2},\boldsymbol{t}_{2})
, the following claim gives an upper bound on the number of close vectors in the “direct sum lattice”
\mathcal{L}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{L}_{2}
to
(\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\boldsymbol{t}_{2})
in terms of the product of the numbers of close vectors in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
to
\boldsymbol{t}_{1}
and in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
to
\boldsymbol{t}_{2}
.

Claim 2.2
.

For lattices
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}
, targets
\boldsymbol{t}_{1}\in\lspan(\mathcal{L}_{1}),\boldsymbol{t}_{2}\in\lspan(\mathcal{L}_{2})
, and
r\geq\max\{{\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1}),\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})}\}
,

\displaystyle N_{p}(\mathcal{L}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{L}_{2},r,(\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\boldsymbol{t}_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq N_{p}(\mathcal{L}_{1},(r^{p}-\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})^{p})^{1/p},\boldsymbol{t}_{1})\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L}_{2},(r^{p}-\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})^{p})^{1/p},\boldsymbol{t}_{2})\ \text{.}

Additionally, if
r<\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})
or
r<\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})
,
N_{p}(\mathcal{L}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{L}_{2},r,(\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\boldsymbol{t}_{2}))=0
.
Moreover, these results hold with
N^{o}_{p}
in place of
N_{p}
.

Proof.

For every lattice vector
(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\in\mathcal{L}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{L}_{2}
,
\mathrm{dist}_{p}((\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\boldsymbol{t}_{2}),(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}))\leq r
(respectively
<r
) holds only if
\lVert\boldsymbol{t}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\rVert_{p}^{p}+\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})^{p}\leq r^{p}
(resp.
<r
) and
\lVert\boldsymbol{t}_{2}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rVert_{p}^{p}+\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})^{p}\leq r^{p}
(resp.
<r
). Hence the desired inequalities follow.
∎

2.2
Sparsification

The following lemma shows that the number of vectors in a collection of
m
pairwise linearly independent (respectively, distinct) vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
that satisfy a random linear constraint (respectively, random affine constraint) is relatively concentrated around its expectation
m/q
.
Similar results appear in
[
Kho05
]
and
[
Ste16
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\delta>0
, let
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, let
q
be a prime, and let
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

1.

If
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\neq s\boldsymbol{a}_{j}
for all
i\neq j
and
s\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, then

\Pr_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}[\left|{\{{i\in[m]:\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=0}\}}\right|\leq(1-\delta)m/q]\leq\frac{q}{\delta^{2}m}\ \text{.}

2.

If
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{a}_{j}
for all
i\neq j
, then

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},\\
y\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}\end{subarray}}[\left|{\{{i\in[m]:\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=y}\}}\right|\leq(1-\delta)m/q]\leq\frac{q}{\delta^{2}m}\ \text{.}

In particular, for
\delta=1
, the probabilities are upper bounded by
q/m
.

Proof.

We start by proving
Item

1
.
Let
X_{i}
be an indicator random variable for the event
\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=0
, where
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
It holds that
\E[X_{i}]=1/q
, that
\Var[X_{i}]=1/q\cdot(1-1/q)\leq 1/q
, and that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
are pairwise independent.
For the last point, because
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{j}
for
i\neq j
are linearly independent, the linear map
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto(\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle,\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{j},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle)
has a kernel of dimension
n-2
. It follows that
\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{j},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=0
for a
q^{n-2}/q^{n}=1/q^{2}
fraction of vectors
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and hence that
X_{i},X_{j}
are pairwise independent.
The result then follows by applying Chebyshev’s inequality to
\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}
.

We next show that
Item

1
implies
Item

2
.
For each
i\in[m]
, let
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\prime}:=(\boldsymbol{a}_{i},-1)
, and note that if
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{a}_{j}
then
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\prime}\neq s\boldsymbol{a}_{j}^{\prime}
for all
s\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Moreover, for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=y
if and only if
\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\rangle=0
, where
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{x},y)
.
Therefore by
Item

1
,

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},\\
y\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}\end{subarray}}[\left|{\{{i:\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=y}\}}\right|\leq(1-\delta)m/q]=\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}\end{subarray}}[\lvert\{{i:\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\rangle=0}\}\rvert\leq(1-\delta)m/q]\leq\frac{q}{\delta^{2}m}\ \text{.}\qed

The following lemma shows that when
q\gg m
, it is probable that no vector in the collection satisfies a random affine constraint.
This is similar to
[
BP20
, Lemma 2.8]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, let
q
be a prime, and let
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. Then:

1.

It holds that

\Pr_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\\
y\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}\end{subarray}}[\exists\ i\in[m]\text{ such that }\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=y]\leq\frac{m}{q}\ \text{.}

2.

If
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{m}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, then for fixed
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,

\Pr_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}[\exists\ i\in[m]\text{ such that }\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=y]\leq\frac{m}{q}\ \text{.}

Proof.

We have that
\Pr_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},y\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}}[\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=y]=1/q
for each
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
.
Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\neq 0
then
\Pr_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}[\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=y]=1/q
for any fixed
y
.
The claims then follow by union bound.
∎

We next show how to sparsify a lattice and argue about vector counts in the resulting sparsified lattice.
In particular, we use
Lemmas

2.3
and
2.4
to lower bound the minimum distance, lower bound the number of close vectors (and hence also upper bound the close distance), and lower bound the too-close distance in the resulting sparsified lattice.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
, let
\mathcal{L}
be a lattice of rank
n
with basis
B
, let
\boldsymbol{t}\in\lspan(\mathcal{L})
, let
q
be a prime, and let
r\geq 0
. Let

\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be sampled uniformly at random,
and define

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}:=\{{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}:\langle B^{+}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)}\}\ \text{,}\quad\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}:=\boldsymbol{t}-B\boldsymbol{z}\ \text{.}

Then the following hold:

1.

(Minimum distance.)
If
r\leq q\cdot\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})
, then

\Pr[\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})<r]\leq N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\},r,\boldsymbol{0})/q
.

2.

(Close vector count and distance.)
If
r<q\cdot\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})/2
, then
for any
\delta>0
,

\Pr[N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},r,\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})\leq(1-\delta)N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{t})/q]\leq\frac{q}{\delta^{2}\cdot N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{t})}+\frac{1}{q^{n}}\ \text{.}

In particular, for
\delta=1
,
\Pr[\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})>r]\leq q/N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{t})+1/q^{n}
.

3.

(Too-close distance.)

\Pr[\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})<r]\leq N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{t})/q+1/q^{n}
.

Proof.

For
Item

1
, let
m:=N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\},r,\boldsymbol{0})
, let
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\in\mathcal{L}
be the
m
distinct non-zero lattice vectors satisfying
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rVert_{p}<r
, and let
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}:=B^{+}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
for
i\in[m]
.
Because
r\leq q\cdot\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})
, we know that
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\notin q\mathcal{L}
and thus
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\not\equiv\boldsymbol{0}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)
.
Therefore by
Lemma

2.4
,
Item

2
with
y=0
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime})<r]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\exists\ i\in[m]\text{ such that }\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\exists\ i\in[m]\text{ such that }\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)]

\displaystyle\leq m/q\ \text{.}

For
Items

2
and
3
we will use the fact that the statistical distance between
(\boldsymbol{x},\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle)
and
(\boldsymbol{x},y)
with
y\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}
sampled uniformly at random is
(q-1)/q^{n+1}<1/q^{n}
. This follows by a direct computation and noting that
(\boldsymbol{x},y)
and
(\boldsymbol{x},\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle)
are identically distributed conditioned on
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
. Additionally, we note that

\displaystyle N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},r,\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})=N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}+B\boldsymbol{z},r,\boldsymbol{t})=N_{p}(\{{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}:\langle B^{+}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\equiv\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)}\},r,\boldsymbol{t})\ .

(7)

For
Item

2
, let
m:=N_{p}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{t})
, let
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\in\mathcal{L}
be the
m
distinct lattice vectors satisfying
\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rVert_{p}\leq r
, and let
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}:=B^{+}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
for
i\in[m]
.
By triangle inequality,
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{i}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\rVert_{p}\leq 2r
for all
i,j\in[m]
.
Then, because
2r<q\cdot\lambda^{(p)}_{1}(\mathcal{L})
, for
i\neq j
we know that
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}-\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\notin q\mathcal{L}
and thus
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\not\equiv\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)
.
Let
y\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}
be sampled uniformly at random.
Then by
Equation

7
and
Lemma

2.3
,
Item

2
,

\displaystyle\Pr[N_{p}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},r,\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})\leq(1-\delta)m/q]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\left|{\{{i\in[m]:\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\equiv\langle\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)}\}}\right|\leq(1-\delta)m/q]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[\left|{\{{i\in[m]:\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\equiv y\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)}\}}\right|\leq(1-\delta)m/q]+\frac{1}{q^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q}{\delta^{2}m}+\frac{1}{q^{n}}\ \text{,}

For
Item

3
, let
m:=N_{p}^{o}(\mathcal{L},r,\boldsymbol{t})
, let
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\in\mathcal{L}
be the
m
distinct lattice vectors satisfying
\lVert\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\rVert_{p}<r
, and let
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}:=B^{+}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
for
i\in[m]
.
Let
y\sim\mathbb{F}_{q}
be sampled uniformly at random.
Then by
Equation

7
and
Lemma

2.4
,
Item

1
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime})<r]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\exists i\in[m]\text{ such that }\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i}-\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[\exists i\in[m]\text{ such that }\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\equiv y\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)]+\frac{1}{q^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{n}}\ \text{.}\qed

2.3
Point Counting via the Theta Function

Here we present results related to counting points in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
using theta functions
\Theta_{p}
(which are the
\ell_{p}
norm analogs of the Gaussian function).
The main result presented in this section,
Theorem

2.8
, was originally proved in
[
MO90
]
and
[
EOR91
]
. Here we follow the nomenclature used in
[
AS18
]
.
For
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\tau>0
, and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, define

\Theta_{p}(\tau,t):=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(-\tau\left|{z-t}\right|^{p})\ \text{.}

We note that without loss of generality we may take
t\in[0,1/2]
.
For
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, extend this to

\Theta_{p}(\tau,\boldsymbol{t}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Theta_{p}(\tau,t_{i})=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\exp(-\tau\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{t}\rVert_{p}^{p})\ \text{.}

For
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\tau>0
, and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, define

\mu_{p}(\tau,t):=\E_{X\sim D_{p}(\tau,t)}[\left|{X}\right|^{p}]=\frac{1}{\Theta_{p}(\tau,t)}\cdot\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|{z-t}\right|^{p}\cdot\exp(-\tau\left|{z-t}\right|^{p})\ \text{.}

where
D_{p}(\tau,t)
is the probability distribution over
\mathbb{Z}-t
that assigns probability
\exp(-\tau\left|{x}\right|^{p})/\Theta_{p}(\tau,t)
to
x\in\mathbb{Z}-t
.
For
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, extend this to

\mu_{p}(\tau,\boldsymbol{t}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{p}(\tau,t_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E_{X\sim D_{p}(\tau,t_{i})}[\left|{X}\right|^{p}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\Theta_{p}(\tau,t_{i})}\cdot\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|{z-t_{i}}\right|^{p}\cdot\exp(-\tau\left|{z-t_{i}}\right|^{p})\ \text{.}

The following fact about
\mu_{p}
is claimed but unproven in
[
AS18
]
.

Claim 2.6
.

For any
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r>\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, there exists a unique
\tau>0
such that
r^{p}=\mu_{p}(\tau,\boldsymbol{t})
.

Proof.

By calculation (see also
[
AS18
, Lemma 6.2]
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\Theta_{p}(\tau,t_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}-\left|{z-t_{i}}\right|^{p}\cdot\exp(-\tau\left|{z-t_{i}}\right|^{p})

\displaystyle=-\Theta_{p}(\tau,t_{i})\cdot\mu_{p}(\tau,t_{i})\ \text{,}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\mu_{p}(\tau,t_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Theta_{p}(\tau,t_{i})}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}-\left|{z-t_{i}}\right|^{2p}\cdot\exp(-\tau\left|{z-t_{i}}\right|^{p})+\mu_{p}(\tau,t_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=-\operatorname*{Var}_{X\sim D_{p}(\tau,t_{i})}[\left|{X}\right|^{p}]<0\ \text{.}

Moreover, recalling that
\mu_{p}(\tau,t_{i})=\E_{X\sim D_{p}(\tau,t_{i})}[\left|{X}\right|^{p}]
, we have
\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\mu_{p}(\tau,t_{i})=\min_{x\in\mathbb{Z}-t_{i}}[\left|{x}\right|^{p}]=\mathrm{dist}_{p}(t_{i},\mathbb{Z})^{p}
and
\lim_{\tau\to 0}\mu_{p}(\tau,t_{i})=\infty
(see
2.10
).
Then
\mu_{p}(\tau,\boldsymbol{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{p}(\tau,t_{i})
is strictly decreasing in
\tau
for
\tau\in(0,\infty)
, and
\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\mu_{p}(\tau,\boldsymbol{t})=\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathbb{Z}^{n})^{p}
,
\lim_{\tau\to 0}\mu_{p}(\tau,\boldsymbol{t})=\infty
.
Hence for every
r>\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, there exists a unique
\tau>0
such that
\mu_{p}(\tau,\boldsymbol{t})=r^{p}
, as desired.
∎

The following defines functions
\beta_{p,t}(a)
that, as we will see, are equal to or closely approximate
N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},an^{1/p},t\cdot\boldsymbol{1})
.

Definition 2.7
.

For
p\in[1,\infty)
,
t\in[0,1/2]
, and
a\geq 0
, we define
\beta_{p,t}(a)
as follows.

1.

For
a<t
, define
\beta_{p,t}(a):=0
.

2.

For
a=t
, define
\beta_{p,1/2}(1/2):=2
and for
t\neq 1/2
define
\beta_{p,t}(t):=1
.

3.

For
a>t
, define

\beta_{p,t}(a):=\exp(\tau^{*}a^{p})\cdot\Theta_{p}(\tau^{*},t)\ \text{,}

where
\tau^{*}>0
is the unique solution to
\mu_{p}(\tau^{*},t)=a^{p}
.

We note that
\beta_{p,t}(a)
is well-defined in the
a>t
case by
2.6
.
We will also work with the inverse function
\beta_{p,t}^{-1}(\nu)
, which we show is well-defined in
2.9
for
\nu\geq\beta_{p,t}(t)
.

The following theorem says that
\beta_{p,t}(a)^{n}
is equal to the number of integer points in a
an^{1/p}
-scaled,
(t\cdot\boldsymbol{1})
-centered
\ell_{p}
ball in
n
dimensions up to a subexponential error term.
This also implies that
a=\beta_{p,t}^{-1}(\nu)
corresponds to the radius
an^{1/p}
at which
N_{p}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},an^{1/p},t\cdot\boldsymbol{1})\approx\nu^{n}
, where the approximation again holds up to a subexponential error term.
This theorem will be very important for our analysis.
We state the result using the notation of
[
AS18
, Theorem 6.1]
, but again note that it was originally proven for
p=2
in
[
MO90
]
and for general
p
in
[
EOR91
]
.

Theorem 2.8
.

For any constants
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\tau>0
, there is another constant
C^{*}>0

Lemma 3.2

For any

For

Definition 3.5

The approach uses three observations:
