Abstract
We consider generalizations of the k k -Center problem in graphs of low doubling and highway dimension. For the Capacitated k k -Supplier with Outliers (CkSwO) problem, we show an efficient parameterized approximation scheme (EPAS) when the parameters are k k , the number of outliers and the doubling dimension of the supplier set. On the other hand, we show that for the Capacitated k k -Center problem, which is a special case of CkSwO , obtaining a parameterized approximation scheme (PAS) is W ⁡ [ 1 ] \mathrm{W[1]} -hard when the parameters are k k , and the highway dimension. This is the first known example of a problem for which it is hard to obtain a PAS for highway dimension, while simultaneously admitting an EPAS for doubling dimension.

Introduction

The well-known
k
-Center
problem and its generalizations has plenty of
applications, for example
selecting suitable locations for building hospitals to serve households of a
municipality (see
[
3
]
for a survey of healthcare
facility location in practice).
In this setting, the number of hospitals we can actually build is limited,
e.g. by budgetary constraints.
We want to choose the locations so that the quality of the provided
service is optimal, and
a societally responsible way of measuring the quality of service is to ensure
some minimal availability of healthcare to every household.
We can quantify this by measuring the distance of a household to its nearest
hospital, and then minimize this distance over all households.
This strategy, however, does not account for the reality that healthcare
providers have (possibly different) limits on the number of patients they can
serve, and thus we introduce capacity constraints.
Furthermore, as the instances are given by transportation networks,
we model them by the titular doubling dimension and highway dimension, which we define later.

We formalize the problem as follows.
In the
Capacitated
k
-Supplier

{G=(V,E)}

{V_{S}\subseteq V}

A

{\lvert\phi(V_{C})\rvert\leq k}

{\lvert\phi^{-1}(u)\rvert\leq L(u)}

For a pair of vertices

For a subset of vertices

We omit the subscript

The

\cost(\phi)=\max_{u\in V_{C}}\mathrm{dist}(u,\phi(u))
and we want to find a feasible solution of minimum cost.
Let us mention the following special cases of

If
{V=V_{S}=V_{C}}

k
-Center

(CkC)

If
{L(u)=\infty}

It is known that

\mathrm{NP}

Two popular approaches of dealing with

Given an instance

The rationale behind parameterized algorithms is to capture the “difficulty” of the instance by the parameter

In this work, we focus on the superpolynomial part of the running time of

as
\mathcal{O}^{*}(f(q))

Another parameter we consider is

Definition 2

The

The doubling dimension of a graph is the doubling dimension of its shortest

Folklore results show that every metric for which the distance function is given by the
\ell_{q}
-norm in
D

\bbbr^{D}
has doubling dimension
\mathcal{O}(D)
.
As a transportation network is embedded on a large sphere (namely the Earth), a reasonable model is to assume that the shortest-path metric abides to the Euclidean
\ell_{2}
-norm.
Buildings in cities form city blocks, which form a grid of streets.
Therefore it is reasonable to assume that the distances in cities are given by the Manhattan
\ell_{1}
-norm.
Road maps can be thought of as a mapping of a transportation network into
\bbbr^{2}
.
It is then sensible to assume that transportation networks have constant doubling dimension.

Edge lengths.

, in a solution

We prove that

Lemma 1

For any
\lambda\geq 2\gamma
, where

Proof

Consider a scale

Let
{H=\mathcal{S}\cup Z}

From the way we constructed

Every edge of length

Now consider the case

We have already seen that every edge of length

Every edge of length

From the construction of the graph, it then must be the case that

Let

For every edge of

In the first case, a shortest path between any pair of vertices of

Any shortest path from a vertex of

In the second case, a vertex

The neighbor

This means that
{\lvert B_{v}(\gamma r)\cap H\rvert=1}

∎

Now we prove that

.

Lemma 2
(

If

To prove the backward implication, we start need to show that a solution of cost

Lemma 3

Let

of cost

.
Then for each

and for each

we have and
\lvert\overline{E}_{i,j}\cap D\rvert=1

Proof

We prove this statement by contradiction.
Suppose that there exists

\lvert\overline{V}_{i}\cap D\rvert\neq 1
.
We first consider the case when
\lvert\overline{V}_{i}\cap D\rvert=0
.
Vertices of
S_{i}
have to be covered by vertices at distance at most
\lambda+1
from them excluding those belonging to
\overline{V}_{i}
.
Let

be the set of vertices originating from subdivided edges of arrows between
\overline{V}_{i}
and

.
Then it must be the case that

as
(\cup_{u\in S_{i}}B_{u}(\lambda+1))\setminus\overline{V_{i}}=S_{i}\cup W
.
The distance between a pair of vertices of

is

, which is strictly greater than

since

.
Hence for a vertex

For a vertex

, and so

.
In total, we would have to pick at least

to cover

.
We have
\lvert Z\rvert>\overline{k}-\frac{\overline{k}+1}{2}
and

must contain

.
As

and thus

.

In the case that there exist

and

We have shown that every

and every

contains at least one vertex of

.
By calculating

, which is equal to the number of color classes plus the number of edge sets, it follows that each of them must contain exactly

∎

Lemma 4

If
\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda}

Proof

Let

.
For

be the vertex of

and for
{i\in\langle k-1\rangle},j\in\langle i+1,k\rangle

be the vertex of
\overline{E}_{i,j}\cap D
.
These vertices are well-defined by Lemma

.
To prove that these vertices encode a

and

\overline{u}_{i}

.
We will only present the proof of incidence for

and

.
Before we prove that

First suppose

Then

as for every two distinct vertices

and

and

Consider the set

\mathcal{T}=(N(y_{i,j})\cup N(r_{i,j})\cup N(p_{i,j}))\setminus\phi^{-1}(\{x_{i},u_{i},e_{i,j},x_{i,j}\}).

(1)

It follows that in a solution of cost

,

or

or

.
We have

However,

\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{T}\rvert

\displaystyle\geq 2N^{4}+2MN^{3}+4N^{3}+3(\overline{k}+1)-((2N^{3}-2N^{2})+(N-1)+(M-1))

\displaystyle>2N^{4}+2MN^{3}+2N^{3}+3(\overline{k}+1),

.

.

Proof
(of Theorem
1.1
)

Let
\gamma\geq 4
be the universal constant in Definition
1
, and
\lambda=2\gamma
.
For contradiction, suppose that there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with an approximation ratio
c=\left((1+\frac{1}{\gamma})-\varepsilon\right)
for some fixed
\varepsilon>0
parameterized by
k
, treewidth and highway dimension.

Given a
Multicolored Clique
instance
\mathcal{I}=(G,k)
, we produce a
CkC
instance
\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{\lambda}=(\overline{G},\overline{k},L)
with edge lengths
d_{\lambda}
using the reduction from Section
2
.
We run algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on the instance
\mathcal{\overline{I}}
, which takes time
\mathcal{O}^{*}(f(\overline{k},\mathrm{tw}(\overline{G}),h(\overline{G})))
for some computable function
f
.
We have
\overline{k}=7k(k-1)+2k
.
By the observation of Dom et al.
[
17
]
, graph
\overline{G}
has treewidth
\mathcal{O}(k^{4})
.
By Lemma
1
, graph
\overline{G}
has highway dimension
\mathcal{O}(k^{4})
.
Thus there exists a computable function

such that

.
If
\mathcal{I}
is a YES-instance, then
\mathcal{\overline{I}}
has a solution of cost
\lambda
by Lemma
2
.
On the other hand, if
\mathcal{I}
is a NO-instance, then any solution of
\mathcal{\overline{I}}
has to have cost at least

, which follows from Lemma

.
Algorithm

is able to distinguish between these two cases in time

, since

.
This is an
\mathrm{FPT}
algorithm for a
\mathrm{W[1]}
-hard problem, which contradicts
\mathrm{FPT}\neq\mathrm{W[1]}
.
∎

3
EPAS on graphs of bounded doubling dimension

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
, i.e. we show the existence of an EPAS for
CkSwO
on instances where the supplier set has bounded doubling dimension.
To be more precise, we develop a decision algorithm which, given a cost
\varrho\in\bbbr^{+}
, and
\varepsilon>0
, computes a solution of cost
(1+\varepsilon)\varrho
in
\mathrm{FPT}
time with parameters
k
,
p
, doubling dimension and
\varepsilon
.
Formally, the result is the following lemma.

Lemma 5

Let
\mathcal{I}=(G,k,p,L)
be a
CkSwO
instance.
Moreover, let
(V_{S},\mathrm{dist})
be the shortest-path metric induced by
V_{S}
and
\Delta
be its doubling dimension.
There exists an algorithm which, given a cost
\varrho\in\bbbr^{+}
and
\varepsilon>0
, either

•

computes a feasible solution of cost
(1+\varepsilon)\varrho
if
(1+\varepsilon)\varrho\geq\mathrm{OPT}(\mathcal{I})
, or

•

correctly decides that
\mathcal{I}
has no solution of cost at most
\varrho
,

running in time
\mathcal{O}^{*}\left((k+p)^{k}\varepsilon^{-\mathcal{O}(k\Delta)}\right)
.

Using Lemma
5
, we can obtain the algorithm of Theorem
1.2
as follows.
We can first assume without loss of generality, that
V_{C}\cup V_{S}=V(G)
.
Suppose that we can guess the optimum cost
\mathrm{OPT}
of any
CkSwO
instance.
By using
\mathrm{OPT}
as
\varrho
in Lemma
5
, we can output a solution of cost
(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}
.
To guess the optimum cost
\mathrm{OPT}
, observe that
\mathrm{OPT}
must be one of the inter-vertex distances.
Hence the minimum inter-vertex distance
\varrho
for which the algorithm outputs a solution has the property that
\varrho\leq\mathrm{OPT}
and consequently
(1+\varepsilon)\varrho\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}
.

The main ingredient of the algorithm is the notion of a
\delta
-net.
For a metric
(X,\mathrm{dist})
, a subset
Y\subseteq X
is called a
\delta
-cover
if for every
u\in X
there exists a
v\in Y
such that
\mathrm{dist}(u,v)\leq\delta
.
If a
\delta
-cover
Y
has an additional property that for every two distinct
u,v\in Y
we have
\mathrm{dist}(u,v)>\delta
, then we say that
Y
is a
\delta
-net
.
Observe that a
\delta
-net can be computed greedily in polynomial time.

Let us give the main idea behind the algorithm.
Given an instance of the problem and
\varepsilon>0
, let
\phi^{*}
be an optimum solution of cost
\mathrm{OPT}
,
V_{C}^{*}
be clients that are not outliers according to
\phi^{*}
, i.e.
V_{C}^{*}=\{u\in V_{C}\mid\phi^{*}(u)\neq\bot\}
, and
Y
be an
(\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{OPT})
-net of the metric
(V_{S},\mathrm{dist})
.
Consider an assignment function
\phi
constructed as follows.
For each client
u\in V_{C}^{*}
we set
\phi(u)
to the nearest point of
Y
to
\phi^{*}(u)
, and for the remaining clients we set the value of
\phi
to
\bot
.
If for every selected supplier
s\in(\phi(V_{C})\setminus\{\bot\})
we have
\lvert\phi^{-1}(s)\rvert\leq L(s)
, then
\phi
is a feasible solution.
Since
Y
is an
(\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{OPT})
-net, the cost of
\phi
is at most
(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}
.

The main obstacle to implementing an algorithm from this idea is that we do not know the optimum solution
\phi^{*}
.
However, by the definition of the net
Y
, we know that each selected supplier
\phi^{*}(V_{C}^{*})
is near some point of
Y
.
If
Y
was not too large, we could guess which
k
of its points are near to every supplier of
\phi^{*}(V_{C}^{*})
.
Later, we will also show how to ensure that the solution we create respects capacities of suppliers we pick.

We now show how to bound the size of the net.
Let
(X,\mathrm{dist})
be a metric of doubling dimension
\Delta
, by the
aspect ratio
of a set
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
, we mean the diameter of
X^{\prime}
divided by the minimum distance between any two distinct points of
X^{\prime}
, that is
\frac{\max_{u,v\in X^{\prime}}\mathrm{dist}(u,v)}{\min_{u,v\in X^{\prime},u\neq v}\mathrm{dist}(u,v)}.
The following lemma by Gupta et al.
[
26
]
shows that the cardinality of a subset
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
can be bounded by its aspect ratio and
\Delta
.

Lemma 6
(
[
26
]
)

Let
(X,\mathrm{dist})
be a metric and
\Delta
its doubling dimension.
Consider a subset
{X^{\prime}\subseteq X}
of aspect ratio
\alpha
and doubling dimension
\Delta^{\prime}
.
Then it holds that
\Delta^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta)
and
\lvert X^{\prime}\rvert\leq 2^{\mathcal{O}(\Delta\lceil\log_{2}\alpha\rceil)}
.

Using Lemma
6
, we bound the size of
Y
.

Lemma 7

Let
\mathcal{I}=(G,k,p,L)
be an instance of the
CkSwO
problem,
{\varepsilon>0}
, and
{\varrho\in\bbbr^{+}}
a cost.
Moreover let
(V_{S},\mathrm{dist})
be the shortest-path metric induced by
V_{S}
and
\Delta
its doubling dimension.
Assume that for each supplier
s\in V_{S}
there exists a client
c\in V_{C}
such that
\mathrm{dist}(s,c)\leq\varrho
.
If
\mathcal{I}
has a feasible solution
\phi
with
\cost(\phi)\leq\varrho
, then an
(\varepsilon\varrho)
-net
Y
of
V_{S}
has size at most
(k+p)\varepsilon^{-\mathcal{O}(\Delta)}
.

Before we prove the lemma, let us make a few comments the statement of the lemma.
We do not know the cost of the optimum solution and we are merely guessing it.
Hence we need to also consider the case when our guess on the cost
\varrho
is wrong, i.e. it is less than the cost of the optimum solution.
The requirement that every supplier has a client nearby is a natural one:
if we assume that our solution has cost
\varrho
and a supplier
s
has
\mathrm{dist}(s,V_{C})>\varrho
, then it will never be picked in a solution.
Thus we can without loss of generality remove all such suppliers from the input.

Proof
(of Lemma
7
)

Let
V_{C}^{\prime}=\{u\in V_{C}\mid\phi(u)\neq\bot\}
and
V_{S}^{\prime}=\phi(V_{C}^{\prime})
.
Since
\phi
is a solution of cost
\varrho
, all clients of
V_{C}^{\prime}
can be covered by balls of radius
\varrho
around vertices of
V_{S}^{\prime}
and there are
k
such balls.
To cover the outliers
V_{C}\setminus V_{C}^{\prime}
, we use the fact that there are at most
p
of them.
Thus we place a ball of radius
\varrho
centered at each outlier.
Since every supplier is at distance at most
\varrho
from some client and every client lies in one of the balls of radius
\varrho
we have placed, by increasing the radius of every placed ball to
2\varrho
we cover all suppliers as well.

In total,
V_{C}\cup V_{S}
can be covered by
k+p
balls of diameter at most
4\varrho
.
For each such ball, let us consider the aspect ratio of points of the net
Y
which lie in it.
Since the diameter is at most
4\varrho
and the distance of every two points of a net is more than
\varepsilon\varrho
, the aspect ratio is at most
\frac{4}{\varepsilon}
.
By Lemma
6
each of the balls contains at most
\varepsilon^{-\mathcal{O}(\Delta)}
points of
Y
.
Thus
\lvert Y\rvert\leq(k+p)\varepsilon^{-\mathcal{O}(\Delta)}
.
∎

When we gave the intuition behind the algorithm, we assumed that the derived solution
\phi
, which replaces every optimum supplier of
\phi^{*}(V_{C})\setminus\{\bot\}
by its nearest net point, does not violate the capacity of any selected net point, i.e. for every
s\in\phi(V_{C})\setminus\{\bot\}
we have
\lvert\phi^{-1}(s)\rvert\leq L(s)
.
This does not have to be the case, so instead of replacing every optimum supplier by its nearest net point, we need to select the replacement net point in a more sophisticated manner, in particular to avoid violating the capacity of the replacement net point.

Let
V_{S}^{*}
be the optimum supplier set corresponding to the optimum assignment function
\phi^{*}
.
Suppose that we are able to guess a subset
S^{*}\subseteq Y
of size
k
such that for every supplier
u\in V_{S}^{*}
we have
\mathrm{dist}(u,S^{*})\leq\varepsilon\varrho
.
Let
A\colon V_{S}^{*}\to S^{*}
map each optimum supplier to its nearest net point.
As we have discussed, we cannot just replace each supplier
u\in V_{S}^{*}
by
A(u)
since it may happen that
{\lvert(\phi^{*})^{-1}(u)\rvert>L(A(u))}
.
However, there is a supplier in the ball
B_{A(u)}(\varepsilon\varrho)
which is guaranteed to have capacity at least
L(u)
since
u\in B_{A(u)}(\varepsilon\varrho)

Thus we can implement the “replacement step” by replacing each optimum supplier by the supplier of highest capacity in
B_{A(u)}(\varepsilon\varrho)
and this increases the cost of the optimum solution by at most

, i.e. the diameter of the ball.

We must also consider the case when
\lvert A^{-1}(v)\rvert>1
for some net point
v\in S^{*}
.
Generalizing the previous idea, we replace suppliers
A^{-1}(v)
by
\lvert A^{-1}(v)\rvert
suppliers of
B_{v}(\varepsilon\varrho)
with the highest capacities.
As we do not know the optimum solution, we do not know
\lvert A^{-1}(v)\rvert
either.
Nevertheless, we know that
\lvert S^{*}\rvert\leq k
, and so we can afford to guess these values after guessing the set
S^{*}
.

The final ingredient we need is the ability to verify our guesses.
That is, given a set of at most
k
suppliers, we need to check if there exists a feasible solution of a given cost which assigns clients to a prescribed set of suppliers.
We do so by a standard reduction to network flows.

Lemma 8

Given a
CkSwO

\mathcal{I}=(G,k,p,L)

\varrho\in\bbbr^{+}

\phi\colon V_{C}\to S

\lvert\phi^{-1}(\bot)\rvert\leq p

Proof

We build a network

is the source,

is the sink, and

.
Refer to Fig.

For each vertex of

we are going to create a unique vertex in
V^{\prime}
, we denote this bijection by

For a subset

we denote

.
For each client

we add a vertex

to

, and add an edge from

with capacity

For each supplier

to

and assign

.
We add edges from each vertex of

with capacity
1
.
We add an edge from

to

with capacity

.
Finally we add a vertex

to

to

with capacity

We claim that the desired assignment

exists if and only if the maximum flow of

is
\lvert V_{C}\rvert
.

To prove the forward implication, let
\phi
be a feasible solution of the instance

.
For each client

As

units flowing through the edge
(m(s),b)
.
For each outlier

we send a unit of flow on the path

The solution

creates at most

is at most

(o,b)
.
There is one unit of flow going through each client, hence the flow has value
\lvert V_{C}\rvert
.
All edges leaving the source

To prove the backward implication, let the maximum flow of the network

be
\lvert V_{C}\rvert
and let

be the corresponding flow.
Assume that

assigns an integral flow to each edge, this is without loss of generality since we can compute such a flow using the Edmonds–Karp
[
18
]
algorithm, and it is a well known fact that if the capacities are integral, then the flow through each edge will be integral as well.
To simplify the exposition, let

, that is we remove all edges through which there is no flow.
From the construction of network

and the preceding assumptions, each vertex of

has one outgoing edge and this edge has its other endpoint in

.
We construct a feasible solution
\phi
to the instance

as follows.
If the outgoing edge from

of a client

Otherwise the outgoing edge from

.
Since the capacity of the edge

is

From the construction of the network, the distance between a client and its assigned supplier is at most

.
Finally, the capacity of the edge

Clearly, the construction of the network

The aforementioned Edmonds–Karp algorithm

Note that we have also described how to compute a feasible solution to

Lemma 9

Let
\mathcal{I}=(G,k,p,L)
be a

(\varepsilon\varrho)

Proof

Let

For an optimum supplier

To circumvent this problem, we fix a linear order

Formally, we define for a net point

•

P(v)=\{v^{\prime}\in Y\mid v^{\prime}\prec v\}

•

•

For a net point

The sets

We guess a subset

For each

from it.

The running time of our algorithm is dominated by the time required to guess

From

Since

is

In total, the running time of the algorithm is
\mathcal{O}^{*}\left(\binom{\lvert Y\rvert}{k}k^{k}\right)

∎

4
EPAS for
kSwO
in Low Highway Dimension Graphs

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
, i.e. we show an EPAS for
k
-Supplier with Outliers
in low highway dimension graphs.
We assume without loss of generality that edge lengths are integral.
In our algorithm, we will require the constant
\gamma
in Definition
1
to be strictly greater than
4
.

In contrast to
CkSwO
, we can simply specify the solution of
kSwO
for a given cost
\varrho
by a subset of suppliers
S\subseteq V_{S}
.
Then a client
c
can be assigned to any supplier
s
such that
\mathrm{dist}(c,s)\leq\varrho
; if such a supplier does not exist, then
c
is an outlier.
For such a solution
S
, we denote the set of outliers by
S^{\bot}
.

Let us first give an overview of our algorithm.
We use a framework by Becker et al.
[
7
]
for embedding low highway dimension graphs into low treewidth graphs.
An

of an (undirected)
guest graph

G
into a
host graph

H
is a distance-preserving injective mapping
\psi\colon V(G)\to V(H)
.
For the purpose of this overview, suppose that for every two vertices
u,v\in V(G)
we have
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq\mathrm{dist}_{H}(\psi(u),\psi(v))\leq c\cdot\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)
for some constant
c\in\bbbr^{+}
.
Ideally, we would like to solve the problem optimally in the host graph and then translate the solution in the host graph back into the input guest graph.
The resulting solution in the guest graph would be a
c
-approximate solution of the input instance.

Our situation is complicated by the fact that optimally solving
k
-Center
, which is a special case of
kSwO
, is already
\mathrm{W[1]}
-hard in low treewidth graphs, as was shown by Katsikarelis et al.
[
30
]
.
On the bright side, the authors of the same paper show that it is possible to design an EPAS for
k
-Center
in low treewidth graphs.
We generalize their algorithm to
kSwO
.
The following theorem summarizes the properties of the algorithm we obtain.

Theorem 4.1

Let
{\mathcal{I}=(G,k,p)}
be a
kSwO
instance,
\varrho\in\bbbn
a cost, and
{\varepsilon>0}
.
Suppose that we receive a nice tree decomposition
5
5

5

See Section
5
for a formal definition.
of
G
of width
\mathrm{tw}(G)
on input.
There exists an algorithm which either

•

returns a solution of cost
(1+\varepsilon)\varrho
if
(1+\varepsilon)\varrho\geq\mathrm{OPT}(\mathcal{I})
, or

•

correctly decides that
\mathcal{I}
does not have a feasible solution of cost
\varrho
,

running in time
\mathcal{O}^{*}\left((\mathrm{tw}(G)/\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{tw}(G))}\right)
.

To ease the presentation, we defer the full proof of Theorem
4.1
to Section
5
, however, let us at least give the idea behind the algorithm.
We use the standard approach of dynamic programming on nice tree decompositions.
We use a technique of Lampis
[
31
]
for designing approximation schemes for problems hard in low treewidth graphs.
The idea behind his technique is that if the cause of the hardness of a problem is due to the necessity of storing large numbers in the dynamic programming table, which in our case are integers from 0 to
\varrho
, then instead of storing exact values we store powers of
(1+\delta)
for an appropriately selected
\delta
.
This decreases the size of the dynamic programming table from
\varrho^{\mathrm{tw}}
to roughly
(\log\varrho)^{\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{tw})}
, which is sufficient for us.
This approach is not cost-free though, since storing rounded approximate values may create errors which can accumulate during the execution of the algorithm.
We will show how to bound this error in the height of the given tree decomposition.
Together with a result of Chatterjee et al.
[
12
]
, which rebalances a given tree decomposition into a nice tree decomposition with a logarithmic height and while only increasing the width by a constant factor, we will obtain an EPAS parameterized by treewidth of the input graph.

The main result by Becker et al.
[
7
]
that we use is the following theorem.
Note that this result requires that the universal constant
\gamma
in Definition

is strictly greater than
\gamma

Theorem 4.2
(
[
7

There is a function

such that, for every

, graph

\begin{split}\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)&\leq\mathrm{dist}_{H}(\psi(u),\psi(v))\\
&\leq(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon))\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)+\varepsilon\cdot\min\{\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,U),\mathrm{dist}_{G}(v,U)\}.\end{split}

(3)

Our algorithm starts by computing a constant factor approximation solution

We select

Since

We apply the algorithm of Theorem

.
For a subset of suppliers

let

in graph

Let
\mathrm{OPT}_{G}
and

We apply the algorithm of Theorem

.
As

Our goal is to bound the cost of the obtained solution in the input graph

Lemma 10

Let

(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution in the host graph

Let

For a client

By definition of

From (

\cost_{H}(S^{*})\leq\\
\max_{u\in V_{C}^{*}}\{(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon))\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,S^{*}(u))+\varepsilon\cdot\min\{\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,U),\mathrm{dist}_{G}(S^{*}(u),U)\}\}.

(4)

By maximizing over each term of (

The following definition of treewidth is given in
[
14
]
.
A
tree decomposition
of a graph
G
is a pair
\mathcal{T}=(T,\{X_{t}\}_{t\in V(T)})
, where
T
is a tree whose every node
t
is assigned a vertex subset
X_{t}\subseteq V(G)
, called a
bag
, such that the following three properties hold.

Property 1

Every vertex of
G
is in at least one bag, i.e.
\cup_{t\in V(T)}X_{t}=V(G)
.

Property 2

For every edge
e\in E(G)
, there exists a node
t\in T
such that the corresponding bag
X_{t}
contains both endpoints of
e
.

Property 3

For every
v\in V(G)
, the set
T_{u}=\{t\in V(T)\mid u\in X_{t}\}
, that is the set of nodes whose corresponding bags contain
u
, induces a connected subtree of
T
.

To improve comprehensibility, we shall refer to vertices of the underlying tree as
nodes
.
It follows from Property
3
that for all
i,j,k\in V(T)
, if
j
is on the path from
i
to
k
in
T
, then
X_{i}\cap X_{k}\subseteq X_{j}
.
The
width
of a tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\{X_{t}\}_{t\in V(T)})
equals
\max_{t\in V(T)}(\lvert X_{t}\rvert-1)
, that is, the maximum size of its bag minus
1
.
The
treewidth
of a graph
G
, denoted by
\mathrm{tw}(G)
, is the minimum possible width of a tree decomposition of
G
.

For algorithmic purposes, it is often more convenient to work with nice tree decompositions:
a rooted tree decomposition
(T,\{X_{t}\}_{t\in V(T)})
with root
r\in V(T)
is a
nice tree decomposition
if each of its leaves
\ell\in V(T)
contains an empty bag (that is
X_{\ell}=\emptyset
) and inner nodes are one of the following three types:

•

Introduce node:
a node
t
with exactly one child
t^{\prime}
where
X_{t}=X_{t^{\prime}}\cup\{u\}
for some vertex
u\not\in X_{t^{\prime}}
. We say that
u
is
introduced
at
t
.

•

Forget node:
a node
t
with exactly one child
t^{\prime}
where
X_{t}=X_{t^{\prime}}\setminus\{v\}
for some vertex
v\in X_{t^{\prime}}
. We say that
v
is
forgotten
at
t
.

•

Join node:
a node
t
with exactly two children
t_{1},t_{2}
where
X_{t}=X_{t_{1}}=X_{t_{2}}
.

It is known that if graph
G
admits a tree decomposition of width at most
k
, then it also admits a nice tree decomposition of width at most
k
.
Furthermore, given a tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,\{X_{t}\}_{t\in V(T)})
of
G
of width at most
k
, one can in
\mathcal{O}(k^{2}\cdot\max(\lvert V(T)\rvert,\lvert V(G)\rvert))
time compute a nice tree decomposition of
G
of width at most
k
that has at most
\mathcal{O}(k\cdot\lvert V(G)\rvert)
nodes, for more details, see
[
14
, Lemma 7.4]
.
For this reason we shall always assume without loss of generality that input tree decompositions of our algorithms are nice.
By
V_{t}
we denote vertices which appear in bags in the subtree rooted at the vertex corresponding to
X_{t}
and by
G[X_{t}]
and
G[V_{t}]
we denote the subgraph induced by vertices in bag
X_{t}
and vertices
V_{t}
respectively.

Lemma 11

A
kSwO
instance
\mathcal{I}=(G,k,p)
admits a feasible solution of cost
\varrho
if and only if it admits a valid distance labelling function
\mathrm{dl}\colon V\to\{0,\ldots,\varrho\}\cup\{\infty\}
of cost
k
and penalty
p
.

Proof

Let
S\subseteq V_{S}
be a solution of
\mathcal{I}
of cost
\varrho
.
We construct the required distance labelling function
\mathrm{dl}
as follows:

1.

for each vertex
u\in V
with
\mathrm{dist}(u,S)\leq\varrho
we set
\mathrm{dl}(u)=\mathrm{dist}(u,S)
,

2.

we set the labels of the remaining vertices to
\infty
.

It is immediate that the cost of
\mathrm{dl}
is at most
k
.
From the definition of
kSwO
it is also clear that outliers are clients whose distance from
S
exceeds
\varrho
, therefore the penalty of
\mathrm{dl}
is at most
p
.
Consider a vertex
u\in V
and a shortest path
\pi
of length at most
\varrho
between
u
and its nearest opened supplier
s\in S
.
Let
v
be the neighbor of
u
on
\pi
.
We have
\mathrm{dist}(u,s)=d(u,v)+\mathrm{dist}(v,s)
and
\mathrm{dl}(u)=\mathrm{dist}(u,s)
and
\mathrm{dl}(v)=\mathrm{dist}(v,s)
, this shows that every vertex and particularly every client with a finite label is satisfied.

For the opposite direction, let
\mathrm{dl}
be a distance labelling function with properties given by the lemma statement.
We select vertices with label
0
as the solution
S
.
By definition of a distance labelling function these can only be suppliers.
It suffices to show that the distance of vertices with finite labels from
S
is at most the value of their label.
This is the case because a valid distance labelling function assigns finite labels to all but (up to)
p
vertices and the maximum finite label is
\varrho
.
We prove this claim by induction on label values.
The base case is immediate since vertices with label
0
are precisely the solution
S
.
In the induction step consider a vertex
u
with a finite label
\ell
.
Since
u
is a satisfied vertex, there exists a neighbor
v\in N(u)
such that
\mathrm{dl}(u)\geq\mathrm{dl}(v)+d(u,v)
.
As edge lengths are positive, we have
\mathrm{dl}(u)>\mathrm{dl}(v)
and using triangle inequality we obtain

\mathrm{dist}(u,S)\leq\mathrm{dist}(u,v)+\mathrm{dist}(v,S)\leq\mathrm{dist}(u,v)+\mathrm{dl}(v)\leq\mathrm{dl}(u)

(8)

where the second inequality follows from the induction hypothesis
\mathrm{dist}(v,S)\leq\mathrm{dl}(v)
.
This shows that clients with finite labels are covered by the solution

and since the penalty of
\mathrm{dl}
is at most
p
, there are at most
p

Also the cost of
\mathrm{dl}
is at most
k
thus we also have
\lvert S\rvert\leq k
.
∎

According to the proven equivalence, we may refer to a client with label
\infty
as an outlier and a supplier with label

as an opened supplier.

For the opposite direction, let

be an entry set to
1

From the description of the algorithm, this means that there exist entries

We can prove the remaining required properties, i.e.

Running time.

The size of the table at each node is at most

.

(

This is a generalization of the approximate distance labelling used in the original algorithm

Let

be a

\delta>0

Lemma 12

Let

be an instance of the

If there exists a valid
\delta
-labelling function of

.

Proof

We select vertices with label
0
as the solution

.
The number of opened suppliers is the cost of the function, hence we open at most
k

We will show by induction on

that if

, then
\mathrm{dist}(u,S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)
.
In the base case let

be an
\varepsilon
-satisfied vertex with
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)=(1+\delta)
.
Then there exists a neighbor
v\in N(u)
with
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)\geq\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(v)+\frac{d(u,v)}{1+\varepsilon}
.
Since

and the only possible
\delta

is

Then we have

For the induction step, let

be an
\varepsilon
-satisfied vertex with
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)=(1+\delta)^{i+1}

There exists a neighbor

\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)\geq\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(v)+\frac{d(u,v)}{1+\varepsilon}
.
As edge lengths are positive, we have

By triangle inequality we have

Since all satisfied clients have a
\delta
-label at most

, all satisfied clients are at distance at most

from

.
The number of outliers is at most

as the penalty of

is at most

.

V_{i}
and similarly let
S^{\prime}\subseteq X_{j}
be the set of satisfied vertices in
V_{j}
.
We claim that at least one of the following must be true:

Case 1

\mathrm{dl}(u)=0
.

Case 2

S=S^{\prime}\cup\{u\}
.

Case 3

u\not\in S
and
\mathrm{dl}(u)<\infty
.

Case 4

\mathrm{dl}(u)=\infty
.

Suppose for contradiction that
\mathrm{dl}(u)\in\{1,\ldots,\varrho\}
and
S\supseteq S^{\prime}\cup\{u,v_{1}\}
where
v_{1}\in X_{i}\setminus S^{\prime}
.
If
v_{1}
is satisfied in
X_{i}
but not in
X_{j}
, then the sole cause of this fact is that
v_{1}
is satisfied by
u
as the labels of its neighbors aside from
u
remain the same between
X_{i}\cap N(v_{1})
and
X_{j}\cap N(v_{1})
.
Thus we have
\mathrm{dl}(v_{1})\geq\mathrm{dl}(u)+d(v_{1},u)
.
Since
u
is a satisfied vertex and
\mathrm{dl}(u)\in\{1,\ldots,\varrho\}
, there exists a vertex
v_{2}\in X_{j}
such that
\mathrm{dl}(u)\geq\mathrm{dl}(v_{2})+d(u,v_{2})
.
Together we have
\mathrm{dl}(v_{1})\geq\mathrm{dl}(v_{2})+d(v_{2},u)+d(u,v_{1})\geq\mathrm{dl}(v_{2})+d(v_{1},v_{2})
where the last inequality holds from the preprocessing using Lemma
13
.
However, the last inequality shows that
v_{1}\in S^{\prime}
which is a contradiction.

We therefore need to establish that for each of the four cases above, the algorithm produces an entry
D^{\delta}_{i}[\mathrm{dl}_{\delta},S_{\delta},k_{i},p_{i}]
with
S\subseteq S_{\delta}
,
k_{i}\leq\lvert\mathrm{dl}^{-1}(0)\cap V_{i}\rvert
,
p_{i}\leq\lvert\mathrm{dl}^{-1}(\infty)\cap V_{i}\cap V_{C}\rvert
, and
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)
which is at most a factor
(1+\delta)^{h}
apart from
\mathrm{dl}(u)
.
Assume by the induction hypothesis that there exists an entry
D^{\delta}_{j}[\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}^{\prime},S_{\delta}^{\prime},k_{j},p_{j}]
with value
1
for some
S_{\delta}^{\prime}\supseteq S^{\prime}
,
k_{j}\leq\lvert\mathrm{dl}^{-1}(0)\cap V_{j}\rvert
,
p_{j}\leq\lvert\mathrm{dl}^{-1}(\infty)\cap V_{j}\cap V_{C}\rvert
and
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}^{\prime}
which has
(\forall v\in X_{j})\left(\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}^{\prime}(v)\in\left[\frac{\mathrm{dl}(v)}{(1+\delta)^{h-1}},(1+\delta)^{h-1}\mathrm{dl}(v)\right]\right)
.

Case
1

If
\mathrm{dl}(u)=0
, the algorithm considers a
\delta
-labelling function
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}
which agrees with
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}^{\prime}
on
X_{j}
and sets
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)=0
.
Since the entry
D^{\delta}_{j}[\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}^{\prime},S_{\delta}^{\prime},k_{j},p_{j}]
has value
1
, the algorithm sets the entry
D^{\delta}_{i}[\mathrm{dl}_{\delta},S_{\delta},k_{j}+1,p_{j}]
to
1
for some
S_{\delta}
.
We claim that
S\subseteq S_{\delta}
.
To see this, let
v\in S\setminus S^{\prime}
.
Then
v
must be satisfied by
u
and we have
\mathrm{dl}(v)\geq\mathrm{dl}(u)+d(u,v)
.
From the induction hypothesis,
\mathrm{dl}(u)=0
, and
(1+\delta)^{h-1}\leq 1+\varepsilon
, we have
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(v)\geq\frac{\mathrm{dl}(v)}{(1+\delta)^{h-1}}\geq\frac{d(u,v)}{1+\varepsilon}
.
This shows that every vertex
v\in S\setminus S^{\prime}
is satisfied in
X_{i}
.

Case
2

Assume that
\mathrm{dl}(u)\not\in\{0,\infty\}
and
u\in S
.
Then there must exist a vertex
v
which satisfies
u
, that is
\mathrm{dl}(u)\geq\mathrm{dl}(v)+d(u,v)
.
Let
r=(1+\delta)^{h-1}\mathrm{dl}(u)
.
The algorithm considers a
\delta
-labelling function
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}
which agrees with
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}^{\prime}
on
X_{j}
and sets
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)=(1+\delta)^{\lceil\log_{1+\delta}r\rceil}
.
Using
(1+\delta)^{h-1}\leq 1+\varepsilon
we have
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)\geq(1+\delta)^{h-1}\mathrm{dl}(u)\geq(1+\delta)^{h-1}(\mathrm{dl}(v)+d(u,v))\geq\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(v)+\frac{d(u,v)}{1+\varepsilon}
.
Hence the algorithm correctly adds
u
to
S_{\delta}^{\prime}
to obtain
S_{\delta}\supseteq S
.
Moreover we have the required upper bound as well since

\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)=(1+\delta)^{\lceil\log_{1+\delta}r\rceil}\leq(1+\delta)^{\log_{1+\delta}((1+\delta)^{h-1}\mathrm{dl}(u))+1}\leq(1+\delta)^{h}\mathrm{dl}(u).

(17)

Case
3

Consider the case when
S\setminus S^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
, otherwise there is nothing to prove.
Let
v\in S\setminus S^{\prime}
, then
v
must be satisfied by
u
, that is
\mathrm{dl}(v)\geq\mathrm{dl}(u)+d(u,v)
.
Let
r=\frac{\mathrm{dl}(u)}{(1+\delta)^{h}}
.
The algorithm considers a
\delta
-labelling function
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}
which agrees with
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}^{\prime}
on
X_{j}
and sets
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)=(1+\delta)^{\lceil\log_{1+\delta}r\rceil}
.
Using the induction hypothesis and
(1+\delta)^{h-1}\leq 1+\varepsilon
, we have

\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(v)\geq\frac{\mathrm{dl}(v)}{(1+\delta)^{h-1}}\geq\frac{\mathrm{dl}(u)}{(1+\delta)^{h-1}}+\frac{d(u,v)}{1+\varepsilon}\geq\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)+\frac{d(u,v)}{1+\varepsilon}.

(18)

Hence the algorithm extends
S_{\delta}^{\prime}
by adding all elements of
S\setminus S^{\prime}
to create the set
S_{\delta}
.
Moreover we have the required upper bound as well since

\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)=(1+\delta)^{\lceil\log_{1+\delta}r\rceil}\leq(1+\delta)^{\log_{1+\delta}\frac{\mathrm{dl}(u)}{(1+\delta)^{h}}+1}\leq\frac{\mathrm{dl}(u)}{(1+\delta)^{h-1}}.

(19)

Case
4

The algorithm considers a
\delta
-labelling function
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}
which agrees with
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}^{\prime}
on
X_{j}
and sets
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)=\infty
.
Since a vertex with label
\infty
cannot satisfy a neighbor by definition, we have
S_{\delta}=S_{\delta}^{\prime}
.
The desired bound on
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)
for
u\in X_{i}
follows trivially.

We conclude that whenever a feasible solution of cost
\varrho
exists to the input instance, we are able to recover from the root bag of the dynamic programming table a solution of cost
(1+\varepsilon)^{2}\varrho
with at most
k
centers and at most
p
outliers.
In particular, there exists an entry in the dynamic programming table of the root bag
D_{r}[\mathrm{dl}_{\delta},X_{r}\cap V_{C},k_{r},p_{r}]
where
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}
is a
\delta
-labelling of
X_{r}
where for all
u\in X_{r}
we have
\mathrm{dl}_{\delta}(u)\leq(1+\delta)^{H}\mathrm{dl}(u)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\varrho
,
k_{r}\leq k
and
p_{r}\leq p
.

It remains to bound the running time of the algorithm.
We have
\lvert\Sigma_{\varrho}\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\log_{1+\delta}\varrho)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\varrho}{\log(1+\delta)}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\varrho}{\delta}\right)
where we use the fact that
\ln(1+\delta)\approx\delta
for sufficiently small
\delta
(that is, sufficiently large
n
).
By setting
\delta=\Omega\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{tw}\cdot\log n}\right)
and assuming
k,p\leq\lvert V(G)\rvert
, we get a running time
\mathcal{O}^{*}\left((\mathrm{tw}\cdot\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon})^{\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{tw})}\right)
.
Using Lemma
14
, this is an FPT algorithm with running time
\mathcal{O}^{*}\left((\mathrm{tw}/\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{tw})}\right)
.
∎

6
Open Problems

We conclude with the following open problems.
The algorithms given by Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
have the number of outliers in the base of the exponent.
Is it possible to remove the outliers from the set of parameters?
An improvement of Theorem
1.1
would be to show that the hardness is preserved in the case of planar graphs.
It may be of interest that
Planar Capacitated Dominating Set
is
\mathrm{W[1]}
-hard when parameterized by solution size
[
9
]
.
Goyal and Jaiswal
[
25
]
have shown that it is possible to 2-approximate
CkC
when the parameter is only
k
, and that this result is tight.
An improvement of Theorem
1.1
would be to show that this lower bound is tight in low highway dimension graphs.
Finally, we ask whether there exists a problem which admits an EPAS in low highway dimension graphs but we cannot approximate in low doubling dimension graphs, i.e. the converse of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.
