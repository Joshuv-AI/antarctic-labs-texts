1
Introduction

Concept-based learning is an interpretability paradigm that leverages human-understandable concepts to explain a deep learning model’s predictions
(
Kim et al. 2018
;
Koh et al. 2020
;
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
;
Yuksekgonul, Wang, and Zou 2022
)
.
Within this paradigm, Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs)
(
Koh et al. 2020
)
are a family of models that first predict concepts from an input (via a
concept predictor
), then use these concepts to predict a downstream label (via a
label predictor
).
This design allows CBMs to (1) provide concept-based explanations via their predicted concepts, and (2) improve their test performance when deployed with experts via
concept interventions

(
Chauhan et al. 2022
;
Shin et al. 2023
;
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2023b
)
.

Concept predictors are the key to interpretability in CBMs since they construct explanations for CBM predictions.
For example, we can understand that a CBM predicts an image to be a “
dove
”, because it detected “
white feathers
” and a “
white head
”.
However, such explanations rely on the faithfulness of the underlying concept predictors; for poorly constructed concept predictors, “
white head
” could be predicted due to the presence of “
white feathers
”, thereby relying on correlations between concepts rather than the presence of concepts.
Such issues compromise the interpretability of CBMs and have been studied from the perspectives of concept-task leakage
(
Mahinpei et al. 2021
;
Havasi, Parbhoo, and Doshi-Velez 2022a
)

and misaligned explanations
(
Margeloiu et al. 2021
)
.

Figure 1:

We investigate whether concept-based models properly capture known concept “
localities
”.
For example, we train a CBM to predict whether an image has a fruit (left panel A), with concepts such as “
Has Bowl
” and “
Has Apple
”.
Here, concepts are localised to image subregions (middle panel B).
We study whether CBMs learn these localities by analysing how changes to input features, both within and outside a concept’s locality, impact a CBM’s predictions (right panel C).

We analyse concept predictors through the lens of
locality
, defined as the idea that concept predictors should only leverage relevant features when making predictions (Figure
1
).
Concept predictors respecting locality are more interpretable, as they make predictions based only on concept-specific features and not spurious correlations.
This allows for better generalisation across data points and greater transferability, as the patterns that these predictors rely upon are more robust.
Surprisingly, we find that concept predictors may fail to respect locality, as perturbations to irrelevant features impact their predictions.
We complement our empirical findings by proving that concept correlations may lead to accurate models that fail to respect locality.
Our findings cast doubt on the interpretability of CBMs: without respecting locality, it is unclear whether a concept is predicted from spurious correlations or from features relevant to the concept itself.

Our main contributions are: (1) we analyse the predictions made by concept predictors through the lens of
locality
to understand if these predictions reflect the presence of a concept. We demonstrate that popular architectures may fail to capture locality, especially with complex models or datasets; (2) we show that a variety of known mitigation methods designed to improve robustness and generalisation fail to address this problem, suggesting that this issue is a general symptom of concept-based models; and (3) we theoretically demonstrate that increasing the rate of concept correlations in a dataset can lead to models with high accuracy but which fail to respect locality.
Our results encourage scepticism when interpreting concept predictions as they can be easily manipulated.

Related Work

Concept Learning

Concept learning
(
Kim et al. 2018
;
Goyal et al. 2019
;
Ghorbani et al. 2019
;
Kazhdan et al. 2020
;
Yeh et al. 2020
;
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
;
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2023b
)
is a subfield of eXplainable AI (XAI) where a deep learning model’s predictions are explained via high-level units of information (i.e., “concepts”).
Methods vary from fully concept-supervised
(
Koh et al. 2020
;
Chen, Bei, and Rudin 2020
;
Yuksekgonul, Wang, and Zou 2022
;
Kim et al. 2023a
)
, where one has access to task and concept labels, to concept-unsupervised
(
Ghorbani et al. 2019
;
Yeh et al. 2020
;
Oikarinen et al. 2023
;
Pham et al. 2024
)
, where only task labels are available. In this paper, we explore CBMs
(
Koh et al. 2020
)
due to their ubiquity as building blocks for state-of-the-art methods
(
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
;
Yuksekgonul, Wang, and Zou 2022
;
Kim et al. 2023a
;
Oikarinen et al. 2023
;
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2023c
)
.

Robustness of Concept-Based Models

Since
Margeloiu et al. 2021
showed, via saliency maps
(
Baehrens et al. 2010
)
, that CBMs may construct concept representations by exploiting spurious correlations, several works have studied this and related phenomena
(
Havasi, Parbhoo, and Doshi-Velez 2022b
;
Sinha et al. 2023
;
Heidemann, Monnet, and Roscher 2023
;
Sheth and Ebrahimi Kahou 2024
;
Raman, Zarlenga, and Jamnik 2024
)
.
Among these,
Mahinpei et al. 2021
showed how CBMs tend to encode undesired information even when concepts are independent (known as “concept leakage”).
Follow-up works used this to study how leakage may be (1) detected at inference
(
Marconato, Passerini, and Teso 2022
)
, (2) measured at test-time
(
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2023a
;
Marconato et al. 2024
)
, (3) affected by inter-concept correlations
(
Heidemann, Monnet, and Roscher 2023
)
, and (4) exploited for adversarial attacks
(
Sinha et al. 2023
)
.

Our work differentiates itself from these by being the first to study a CBM concept predictor’s robustness when changing features outside of those relevant to the concept.
We build on the work by
Margeloiu et al. 2021
and
Furby et al. 2023
by developing a quantitative method to measure locality and analyse the presence of this phenomenon across situations.
Moreover, we differentiate ourselves from
Mahinpei et al. 2021
and
Ghorbani, Abid, and Zou 2019
as follows: while Mahinpei et al. study whether concept predictors leak information, and Ghorbani et al. study the fragility of explanation methods in a post-hoc manner, we study the types of features leveraged by concept predictors.
We finally differentiate ourselves from
Furby et al. 2024
by focusing on the impact of architectural and dataset choices upon locality rather than on the impact of inaccurate data labelling.

3
Introducing Locality

Concept Learning Setup

In concept-based learning, each training sample
\mathbf{x}
has an associated set of
k
binary concepts
\mathbf{c}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
. This set serves as a low-dimensional human-understandable representation for each sample containing information relevant to a downstream task.
For example, when predicting fruits in an image, concepts can include the fruit’s
colour
and
shape
.
Formally, we are given a training set of features
X=\{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, task labels
Y=\{y^{(i)}\in\{1,\cdots,L\}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and concepts
C=\{\mathbf{c}^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
For each data point,
\mathbf{c}^{(i)}
is a binary
k
-length vector where
\mathbf{c}^{(i)}_{j}
indicates the presence of the
j
-th concept (e.g., “
red fruit
”) in
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
.

Concept learning can be used to develop interpretable architectures where predictions use a set of high-level concepts rather than raw input features.
This is typically done through a Concept Bottleneck Model (CBM)
(
Koh et al. 2020
)
, a two-stage architecture consisting of a concept predictor
g:\mathbf{x}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf{c}}
and a subsequent label predictor
f:\hat{\mathbf{c}}\mapsto\hat{y}
.
The concept predictor predicts probabilities for the presence of each concept, which can be rounded to get 0-1 concept predictions.

Locality in Concept Predictors

The key to interpretability in CBMs is trustworthy predictions from their concept predictors
g
.
Concept predictors play two roles: (1) they assist with downstream tasks by providing a representation from which a task can be solved and (2) they serve as the medium for CBMs to explain their predictions, as task predictions are understood through concepts.
While metrics such as accuracy allow us to measure the former objective, quantifying the latter requires analysing concept predictors to understand if their predictions are faithful.

To understand whether concept predictors truly reflect the presence of each concept, we study concept predictors through the lens of “locality.”
We define locality as a property of a dataset where concepts are uniquely defined by a subset of input features.
For example, in image-based datasets, some features are naturally restricted to certain spatial regions, while in biological datasets, concepts might be restricted to groups of interacting genes
(
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2023c
)
.
CBMs should respect these conditions, as predicting whether a bird has “
white feathers
” based on its head would be incorrect.
By using the proper features for concept prediction, we allow for concept predictors to be more robust to noise, and therefore, more transferable across tasks.
While not all datasets exhibit locality (e.g., abstract concepts such as text sentiment), we claim that
datasets with locality should have this property captured by CBMs
.

Defining Locality

CBMs respect locality if each concept is predicted only from its relevant features, which we call a concept’s “local region”.
For each concept
j
, we define its local region for a data point,
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
, as
\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\subseteq\{1\cdots m\}
, where
m
is the number of features in
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
. This corresponds to the minimum set of features that can completely predict the corresponding concept value.
That is,
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{c}_{j}^{(i)}|\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})})=0
, where
\mathcal{H}
is the entropy function, and
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{a}
is the
a^{\mathrm{th}}
feature for data point
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
; this implies that the features within a concept’s locality are sufficient to fully predict the underlying concept.
For example, if concept
j
corresponds to the “
red tail colour
” concept, then the set of features,
\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})
would be all pixels corresponding to the tail in a bird image.

We note that respecting locality does not preclude a CBM from taking advantage of contextual information or using global (rather than local) concepts.
Instead, locality simply ensures that predictions are made based only on
relevant
contextual information. Hence, for concepts that are a function of global input properties, all features are relevant, and, therefore, these predictors respect locality by default.

In this work, we argue that properly capturing a dataset’s locality ensures that concept predictors avoid exploiting potentially spurious correlations across concepts. This enables the same concept predictor to be used across distinct downstream tasks and facilitates the understanding of a concept predictor’s outputs, as they guarantee to leverage only salient features for their predictions rather than spurious features.
Nevertheless, we note that accurate models can still fail to respect locality: models can be accurate whilst predicting concepts based on spurious correlations, making the model uninterpretable (we prove this in Section
5
).

Measuring Locality

We measure whether a CBM captures locality by determining if predictions from the CBM’s concept predictor,
g
, can be arbitrarily modified through changes to input features outside a concept’s local region.
Formally, for an input
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
and concept
j
,
we perturb features outside of
\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})
, that is, features in
\{1\cdots m\}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})
, to see if we can modify
g
’s prediction of
\mathbf{c}_{j}
.
We operationalise this through three metrics, each exploring a different perspective on perturbation.

Locality Leakage

Locality leakage measures a CBM’s response when a concept’s irrelevant features are perturbed.
We aim to find a similar sample,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, so that relevant features are not perturbed,
\forall a\in\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}),\;\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{a}=\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{a}
while the difference in predicted concept,
|g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})_{j}-g(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})_{j}|
, is maximised.
Formally, we define locality leakage as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Bigg(\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\text{ s.t. }\\
\forall a\in\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}),\;\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{a}=\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{a}\end{subarray}}|g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})_{j}-g(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})_{j}|\Bigg)

We construct
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
for each
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
by maximising
|g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})_{j}-g(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})_{j}|
via projected gradient ascent.
This score captures how easy it is to change a concept’s prediction by modifying irrelevant features.
A large locality leakage implies that modifications to irrelevant features significantly impact a concept’s prediction.
For ease of evaluation, we do not constrain the magnitude of change outside a concept’s locality.
Nevertheless, since this may lead to significantly altered samples, we demonstrate in Appendix
E
that constrained perturbations of a concept’s out-of-locality features can still lead to changes in its predicted value.
Our approach is similar to that of adversarial attacks as both perturb inputs to reduce model performance.
However, our metric is used to understand the impact of features outside of a concept’s local region, while adversarial attacks focus on the presence of nearly identical examples which diverge in model predictions.

Locality Masking

To understand if concept predictors heavily exploit spurious correlations to make predictions, we develop a second metric that restricts the type of perturbations to be spatially continuous
masks of features
.
Such a metric allows us to understand which features are important for predicting each concept.
We investigate two notions of masking: (i)
locality relevant masking
to measure whether masking relevant features leads to significant changes in concept predictions, and (ii)
locality irrelevant masking
to measure whether masking irrelevant features impacts concept predictions. For a concept-locality-obeying model, we expect a maximal change in prediction for the former, and minimal change for the latter.
Formally, we let
locality relevant masking
be the mean change in concept prediction
|g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})_{j}-g(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})_{j}|
across all concepts
j
, where
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
is produced from
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
by setting all features in
\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathbf{x}(i))
to some fixed value
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
(e.g., zero).
We similarly define
locality irrelevant masking
, but instead mask features for a concept with a different local region,
c_{j^{\prime}}
, to measure if masking
c_{j^{\prime}}
impacts predictions for
c_{j}
, averaged across all
j^{\prime}
.

Locality Intervention

To understand if CBM predictions are reliant upon spurious inter-concept correlations, we introduce the locality intervention metric. Locality intervention captures how
in-distribution
modifications to concept
c_{j^{\prime}}
impact a semantically unrelated concept
c_{j}
. We measure this via the maximum change in prediction for
c_{j}
while varying other concepts for sample
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
:
\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}^{(l)}|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{(i)}=\mathbf{c}_{j}^{(l)}\end{subarray}}(|g(\mathbf{x}^{(l)})_{j}-g(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})_{j}|
).
We measure the maximum of this value across all concepts
j
: smaller values indicate more robust concept predictors.

4
Evaluating Concept Predictors and Locality

Datasets

We study CBM localities in three datasets: a synthetic dataset, the Caltech-UC Davis Birds (CUB) dataset
(
Wah et al. 2011
)
, and the Common Objects in Context (COCO) dataset
(
Lin et al. 2014
)
.
The synthetic dataset allows us to analyse the relationship between concept predictors and factors such as model structure and dataset complexity.
The CUB and COCO datasets allow us to understand CBMs in commonly used concept-learning datasets with known localities
(
Koh et al. 2020
;
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
)
:

1.

Synthetic
consists of images of independent objects (either triangles or squares). Similar to prior work, we annotate each sample specifying whether at least one of its objects is a square, similar to labels constructed by prior work
(
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
)
.
We vary the number of objects in each sample to test for the effect of data complexity on locality (see Appendix
B
).

2.

CUB

(
Wah et al. 2011
)
consists of bird images annotated with their species (i.e., downstream task) and visual properties such as “
beak colour
” and “
tail length
” (i.e., concepts).

3.

COCO

(
Lin et al. 2014
)

consists of images of everyday objects (e.g., “
forks
”, “
broccoli
”) whose presences are used as concepts. Similar to prior work, we construct a binary task label that indicates the presence of any object in a predetermined subset
(
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
)
.

Unless specified otherwise, we train CBMs jointly (concept and label predictors together), use an InceptionV3
(
Szegedy et al. 2016a
)
model for the concept predictor, and a 2-layer MLP for the label predictor, all of which is similar to prior work
(
Koh et al. 2020
)
(see Appendix
C
).

We apply locality leakage and intervention to understand locality in the synthetic dataset, while we apply the locality masking metric to understand locality in both real-world datasets.
We do this because not all metrics are appropriate for use in real-world datasets; locality leakage significantly alters images, which might result in out-of-distribution examples, while locality intervention requires counterfactual examples, which are hard to find in real-world datasets.

Locality Leakage

To understand whether perturbations outside of a concept predictor’s local region impact predictions, we measure the locality leakage of CBMs trained on our synthetic dataset.
We construct concept predictors using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with varying capacities and evaluate these on synthetic datasets with varying numbers of concepts (details in Appendix
C
).
Additionally, we analyse the impact of using independent CBM training in Appendix
G
.

Across all models, irrelevant features can be perturbed so that concept predictions can be changed in at least 40% of cases, revealing a weakness of CBMs.
While neural networks are susceptible to adversarial attacks, the susceptibility of neural networks to locality leakage is surprising due to the simplicity of the dataset.
In the training dataset, perturbations outside of the local region have no impact on the concept label, so ideally, CBMs should have no incentive to leverage features outside of the local region.

We show that such results hold across different constructions of CBMs.

Prior work argues that larger models are prone to memorising spurious correlations
(
Sagawa et al. 2020

, which may explain why larger models are worse at capturing locality.
Higher-capacity concept predictors may learn to depend on irrelevant but correlated out-of-locality features to predict concepts.

This casts
a serious concern about what sorts of relationships and correlations a CBM’s concept predictor exploits when predicting concepts.

Dataset Complexity

To understand the impact of dataset complexity on locality leakage, we compare models trained on the synthetic dataset with varying numbers of objects.

Similar to the trends with model size, we observe that more complex datasets lead to higher locality leakage (Figure
2
b), with the effect being most pronounced when the concept predictor has fewer layers.
Increasing the number of concepts results in more variation outside of a concept’s local region, potentially causing models to memorise those irrelevant features.
We find that this effect is diminished in larger models, where they always exhibit high locality leakage, even when the number of concepts is small (Figure
2
b).
As deployment-level CBMs commonly contain many parameters and are trained on datasets with several concepts, these results highlight the need to further study and address locality leakage in high-capacity and high-data-complexity regimes.

CBM Variants

Recent work has developed new variants of CBMs, including Concept Embedding Models (CEMs), which increase the representational power of label predictors
(
Espinosa Zarlenga et al. 2022
)
, and Probabilistic CBMs (ProbCBMs), which incorporate uncertainty
(
Kim et al. 2023b
)
.
These models improve upon vanilla CBMs, as CEMs improve concept intervention accuracy, while ProbCBMs allow for uncertainty quantification.
We investigate whether these improvements cause CBMs to respect localities by evaluating CEMs and ProbCBMs according to the locality leakage metric.
We perform evaluation using the 1, 2, and 4-object synthetic datasets and measure locality leakage.

We find that ProbCBMs exhibit lower locality leakage compared to CEMs, both models being comparable to a 4-layer CBM (Figure
2
c).
For both, locality leakage is constant across datasets, potentially due to the size of these models.
Nevertheless, their high leakage scores suggest that recent improvements to CBMs fail to address locality leakage, hinting at a fundamental issue.

Locality Masking

Locality Masking in CUB and COCO

In this section, we use the locality masking metric to understand if CBMs trained on real-world datasets capture localities.
We do this by exploiting the fact that concepts in CUB are clustered by body parts.
To evaluate the impact of masking, say, the concept “
red head
”, we construct a circular mask of radius
\epsilon=0.3l
(we evaluate the impact of varying
\epsilon
in Appendix
K
) around the centre for the bird’s
head
, where
l
is the image side length.
To avoid masking unrelated features, we mask only features closer to the centre of the masked concept than the centre of any other concept.
We note that each concept in CUB is part of a concept group representing co-located concepts (e.g., “
beak red colour
” and “
beak blue colour
” are in the concept group
beak
).
Therefore we measure, for example, the impact of masking
beak
on concept predictions for “
beak blue colour
” and “
beak straight shaped
”.

For each concept group
G\subset\{1\cdots k\}
, we measure if masking impacts concept predictions for concepts within
G
:
\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{j\in G}|g(\mathbf{x^{\prime}})_{j}-g(\mathbf{x^{(i)}})_{j}|
, where
\mathbf{x^{\prime}}
masks the concept group
G
from
\mathbf{x^{(i)}}
.
We evaluate locality masking within a concept group, because concepts within a group have the same local region.
This allows us to understand how masking a concept’s local region impacts concept predictions, both for concepts within and outside a group.
For COCO, we mask all features within each concept’s bounding box and measure the change in predictions.
We use a zero-mask (i.e.,
\eta=0
)
and explore other masks (e.g., mean masks) in Appendix
M
.

Locality Intervention

To understand if concept predictors leverage spurious inter-concept correlations, we evaluate CBMs using the locality intervention metric.
This measures a concept predictor’s response to structured in-distribution perturbations.
Specifically, we vary the number of concept combinations seen during training to study the impact of training concept diversity on locality intervention.
We expect that more diverse training data leads to more robust concept predictors (i.e., learning to identify a concept without depending on irrelevant concepts).
We study this in our synthetic
4
-object dataset as it offers a relatively large number of concept combinations, allowing fine-grained control of its diversity.

Figure
3
b shows that models achieve
25\%
concept prediction error when seeing
50\%
of concept combinations.
We find that predictions from concept predictors can be modified (i.e., non-zero locality intervention metric) until the training dataset contains
75\%
of concept combinations.
Before that, a CBM’s concept predictions seem sensitive to changes in semantically unrelated concepts, implying that models leverage features beyond the local region to predict concepts.

Conversely, leveraging features beyond the local region should not assist with concept prediction, as these features are irrelevant to concept prediction.
Since all models perfectly learn concept combinations found in the training set, this reduction in performance indicates that increasing the number of data points would not increase diversity.
Instead, developing robust concept models requires truly diverse datasets in terms of the
number of concept combinations
, so that models can properly learn for each concept.
However, in many real-world scenarios, only a small set of combinations is available (e.g., birds with “
white tails
” and “
blue feathers
” might be rare). In these settings, training such models is challenging: concept predictors are less likely to pay attention to the relevant features; instead, they pick up on spurious correlations.

5
Mitigating Locality in CBMs

We investigate whether robustness techniques allow CBMs to capture localities.
In addition to the methods here, we demonstrate in the appendices that
\ell_{2}
regularisation (Appendix
I
), adversarial training (Appendix
J
), and residual connections (Appendix
P
) fail to help CBMs capture localities.

Failures of Existing Solutions

First, we investigate whether existing methods that address leakage-related issues in CBMs can improve localities captured by CBMs.
We explore two methods: the first modifies the learning rate
(
Sinha et al. 2023
)
, and the second changes the training loss
(
Heidemann, Monnet, and Roscher 2023
)
.
We select these two as they both aim to improve the robustness of CBMs through train-time modifications.
The first method, which we call “
two-step training
”, first trains concept predictors in isolation for several epochs, then jointly trains CBMs using a learning rate decayed by a factor
L

(
Sinha et al. 2023
)
, which prevents concept-task leakage.
The second method, which we call “
correlation training
”, weights the loss higher for concept pairs where only one concept is present, as this enables learning concept
j
without interference from concept
j^{\prime}
.
We scale losses by a factor
S
whenever only one concept is present in any of the top-
R
correlated concept pairs.
We run ablation studies to select values for
L
and
S
in Appendix
L
.

We find these methods are unable to improve CBM locality (Figure
4
).

Using Vision Language Models

Recent work combines vision language models (VLMs) with concept predictions, such as label-free CBMs
(

, which allows for CBMs to be trained without labels.
Such models could potentially improve CBM locality because they leverage VLMs trained across large, diverse datasets, which could reduce spurious correlations.
We evaluate label-free CBMs on CUB using the locality masking metric and compare using only concept groups that are present in both CUB and the Label-Free concept list.

In Figure

Masking relevant features has little impact on concept predictions, implying that these models leverage features outside of their local region for concept prediction.
The use of such features implies that even CBM variants based on VLMs such as Label-Free CBMs fail to respect the underlying localities in a dataset.

Successes and Failures of Pruning

If model size is partially responsible for locality leakage, then pruning may offer a solution to improve it.
We prune a constant percentage of weights based on their norm
(
Tishby, Pereira, and Bialek 2000
)
and analyse the impact of pruning rate and retraining upon model accuracy and leakage (we detail hyperparameter choices in Appendix
R
).

We find that pruning improves locality leakage only in simple scenarios (Figure
5
a left).
For large models, accuracy degrades whenever locality leakage (Figure
5
a right) or locality masking (Figure
5
b) decreases.
When datasets contain few concepts, pruning can help.
However, as the number of concepts or the model’s capacity increases, the effect of pruning is muted.
We note, however, that as locality leakage seems to arise from various factors, the impact of pruning is limited to only situations where models are both small and the dominant reason for locality leakage.
These results suggest that existing robust training pipelines for CBMs are not fit to develop locality-preserving concept predictors.

Impossibility of good concept predictors

The interpretability of CBMs relies on effective concept predictors.
However, our empirical findings cast doubt on whether the constructed explanations genuinely represent the presence of training concepts.
We theoretically substantiate this concern by illustrating that, when concepts are correlated, we can create concept predictors that exclusively utilise concepts learned by other predictors, even when such concepts are semantically independent.
In other words, correlated concepts make it difficult to learn concept predictors which only rely on relevant concepts.
Our theorem reveals that models can be trained to predict concepts accurately while inadequately reflecting the presence of these concepts, highlighting a fundamental limitation of CBMs (proof in Appendix
S
).

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose out of
k
total concepts, we know
m
concepts,
\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\cdots\gamma_{m}\}\subset\{1,2,\cdots k\}
, with perfect accuracy.
Let
M_{j,q,i,r}=\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{j}=q|\mathbf{c}_{i}=r]
be a correlation matrix, where
q,r\in\{0,1\}
.
For any concept
j\notin\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{m}\}
whose value we do not know, if for
s
different triplets
(q,i,r)
, with
i\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{m}\}
, we have: i)
M_{j,q,i,r}\geq 1-\alpha
and ii)
\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{i}=r]\geq\beta
,
then there exists a concept predictor that achieves error
\epsilon=\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{\hat{c}}_{j}\neq\mathbf{c}_{j}]\leq\alpha+(1-\beta)^{s}
using only information about the concepts
\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{m}\}
.

To prove this, we construct an algorithm which achieves the desired error rate while leveraging correlated
concepts.

This result aggregates some of the main factors which we empirically found to impact locality leakage.
We find that high rates of correlation, captured by
\alpha
, and high dataset complexity, captured by
s
, can lead to accurate models which fail to reflect concept locality.
More complex algorithms can lead to better bounds for
\epsilon
, which corresponds to our results on model size (Section
4
).
These results corroborate our empirical findings, demonstrating that concept-based models can have good accuracy yet fail to be useful for interpretability.

6
Discussion and Conclusion

Developing Locality-Respecting Concept-Based Models

Our experiments reveal several weaknesses in the concept predictors that underlie concept-based models, as concept predictors with seemingly high accuracy can misrepresent the reasons behind their predictions.
Developing locality-respecting concept-based models requires locality-respecting concept predictors, yet as we showed, overcoming their limitations is difficult.
These experiments can assist future work by providing insight into what types of solutions might be able to alleviate this problem.
One potential way to improve trustworthiness is to leverage concept location annotations to enforce that concept predictors only leverage relevant features.
For example, this could be done through the use of bounding boxes so that concept predictors only make predictions using features from those bounding boxes.
Another method is to leverage human uncertainty information so that concept confidences are well-calibrated, such as in situations where concepts are occluded
(
Collins et al. 2023
)
.

Limitations

We propose several metrics to understand concept predictors, yet our metrics are not exhaustive.
For example, models which respect localities might still collapse in the presence of distortions due to the out-of-distribution nature of these new data points.
To get around this, we leverage the locality intervention metric, but in real-world datasets, this is difficult to measure due to the lack of counterfactual concept labels.
One potential avenue for addressing this limitation is to explore generative approaches (e.g., GANS
(
Goodfellow et al. 2020
)
) to enrich a training set’s concept diversity.

Conclusion

We explored whether CBMs respect and capture concept
localities
, the minimum set of features that define a concept’s value.
To understand if CBMs properly identify such localities, we developed three metrics and found that across datasets, CBMs fail to capture localities.
This deficiency poses a threat to the interpretability of CBMs and raises scepticism regarding their predictions.
Potential solutions to improve the robustness of CBMs fail to improve locality, and we show that this problem is inherent to datasets with correlated concepts.
We hope our results may act as a call for the development of locality-aware CBMs.

Appendix G
Impact of Independent Models

Typically, CBMs are trained in a joint fashion, where concept and task predictors are trained simultaneously, allowing for higher performance
(
Koh et al. 2020
)
.
However, prior work has demonstrated that such models exhibit concept-task leakage
(
Mahinpei et al. 2021
)
, where information about the task is leaked into the concept predictor.
This leads to the concept predictor exploiting leaked task-relevant information, which jeopardises the independence of the concept predictors.
To counter this, we evaluate whether independent training, where the concept predictor is trained with no task knowledge, allows predictors to properly capture a dataset’s localities.
We train
7
-layer concept predictors on the
2
-object synthetic dataset, selecting this model because it is the deepest and exhibits the highest locality leakage (thereby presenting the best opportunity to reduce locality leakage).

We find that both independent and joint models exhibit a locality leakage of
1.00\pm 0.00
while achieving perfect concept accuracy.
This suggests that independent CBM training has no impact on locality leakage in concept predictors.
These results hint at a fundamental issue with using multi-label neural networks as concept predictors,
and suggest the need to explore alternative architectures or training paradigms to improve a CBM’s understanding of locality.

We study whether MLP models capture locality, as they present a scenario where the parameter size, along with depth and width, can be varied arbitrarily.
We try out concept predictors with MLPs, varying the width between
\{5,10,15\}
and varying the depth between
\{1,2,3\}
so that the number of parameters for concept predictors is the same magnitude as those consisting of CNNs.

For MLP-based concept predictors, we see that when using a single hidden layer, one observes an increase in locality leakage as the width of the hidden layer is increased (Table
1
).
This trend is similar to the one exhibited in CNN concept predictors, where larger models lead to more locality leakage.
However, unexpectedly, we also find that when the model width is fixed, and the number of hidden layers is increased, the amount of locality leakage does not necessarily increase.
The reason for this is unclear; it might be due to the small hidden layer sizes creating an information bottleneck
(
Tishby, Pereira, and Bialek 2000
)
, where information gets compressed when travelling between layers.
From these experiments, we see that increasing the number of parameters generally leads to increasing locality leakage, across both CNN and MLP models, showing that locality leakage might be endemic to CBMs rather than a feature of CNN models.

In Figure
17
, we evaluate the hidden neurons themselves in a 1-depth model to understand the types of patterns they pick up on as the number of model parameters increases.
We see that some neurons pick up on relevant patterns useful for concept prediction, while others pick up purely on noise.
As the number of hidden layers increases, while the ratio of noisy neurons to total neurons stays constant, the number of such neurons increases.
The increase in the number of noisy neurons might be partially responsible for the increase in locality leakage; these “noisy neurons” implicitly pick up on patterns outside of the concept’s local region, which is then leveraged by the locality leakage metric.

Pruning Details

We consider pruning rates in
\{25\%,50\%,75\%,95\%\}
and consider either retraining for five epochs or no retraining, to see if that assists with accuracy and locality.
We select such values to capture a spectrum of pruning rates, and consider retraining for five epochs because we found that most models tend to stop improving significantly after accuracy (without also declining in locality leakage) after this.
We do not freeze concept predictors during retraining as we found similar results regardless of freezing.

Appendix S
Concept Correlation Theory

This section presents our proof for Theorem
5.1
discussed in Section
5
. For us to present our proof, we begin by showing two lemmas that will become useful later on. The first lemma, shown below, introduces a simple identity on sets of real numbers in
[0,1]
:

Lemma S.1
.

Consider a set of
n
real numbers
\{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\}
, where
\forall i,\;0\leq p_{i}\leq 1
. Then, the following identity must hold:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})=1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-p_{i})

Proof.

To simplify this proof, we will show the equivalent but more accessible statement that

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-p_{i})=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})

We proceed to do this by induction on the number of real numbers
n
. Our base case
n=1
, trivially follows by noting that both sides of the equation above resolve to
1-p_{1}
when
n=1
.

Now, assume that the claim above holds for all positive integers
n
up to some value
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Consider what happens when
n=k+1
:

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}(1-

\displaystyle p_{i})=(1-p_{k+1})\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-p_{i})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-p_{i})-p_{k+1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-p_{i})

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})\Big)-p_{k+1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-p_{i})

\displaystyle=1-\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})+p_{k+1}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-p_{j})\Bigg)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}p_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-p_{j})

where the inductive hypothesis was applied in our second step. This shows that our statement holds for
n=k+1
and therefore holds for all positive integers
n
.
∎

The second lemma directly builds on the identity introduced in Lemma
S.1
to produce a simple upper bound for a sum of products involving the same list of reals in
[0,1]
:

Lemma S.2
.

Consider a set of
n
real numbers
\{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\}
, where
\forall i,\;0\leq p_{i}\leq 1
.
Then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})\leq 1

Proof.

We begin by noticing that, because all
\{p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\}
are reals such that
\forall i,\;0\leq p_{i}\leq 1
, each of the terms in the product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-p_{i})
must be non-negative. This trivially implies that the product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-p_{i})
itself must be non-negative. Therefore, we can conclude that:

\displaystyle 0\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-p_{i})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow 1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-p_{i})\leq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-p_{j})\leq 1

(by Lemma
S.1
)

which is exactly what we wanted to show.

∎

Equipped with these two lemmas, we now present a proof for the key theoretical result introduced in Section
5
:

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose out of
k
total concepts, we know
m
,
\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\cdots\gamma_{m}\}\subset\{1,2\cdots k\}
, with perfect accuracy.
Let
M_{j,q,i,r}=\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{j}=q|\mathbf{c}_{i}=r]
be a correlation matrix, where
q,r\in\{0,1\}
.
For any concept
j\notin\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{m}\}
whose value we do not know, if for
s
different triplets
(q,i,r)
, with
i\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{m}\}
, we have i)
M_{j,q,i,r}\geq 1-\alpha
and ii)
\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{i}=r]\geq\beta
,
then there exists a concept predictor that achieves error
\epsilon=\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{\hat{c}}_{j}\neq\mathbf{c}_{j}]\leq\alpha+(1-\beta)^{s}
using only information about the concepts
\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{m}\}
.

Proof.

We propose an algorithm which achieves error rate
\epsilon=\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{j}=r]\leq\alpha+(1-\beta)^{s}
.
To predict
\mathbf{c}_{j}
, we leverage the correlation between
\mathbf{c}_{j}
and an
s
different values of
M_{j,q,i,r}
, where
i\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{m}\}
.
By leveraging this, we’re able to accurately predict the presence of
\mathbf{c}_{j}

For this, let the
s
triplets satisfying (i) and (ii) be
\mathcal{B}:=\{B_{l}\>|\;B_{l}=(q_{l},i_{l},r_{l})\}_{l=1}^{s}
.
Furthermore, for any triplet
B_{l}=(q_{l},i_{l},r_{l})
in this set, let
p_{l}
be
\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{i_{l}}=r_{l}]
.

Here, we propose an algorithm that takes as an input the set of triplets
\mathcal{B}
, along with their associated probabilities
\{p_{l}\}_{l=1}^{s}
, and returns a label
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{j}
for an unknown concept
j
.
Our algorithm proceeds as follows: We will iterate with an index variable
u
starting with
u=1
and finishing with
u=s
. At each iteration, we select the triplet
B_{u}=(q_{u},i_{u},r_{u})
.
As stated above, we know
i_{u}\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{m}\}
, and therefore we have perfect knowledge of
\mathbf{c}_{i_{u}}

Therefore, we can check if
\mathbf{c}_{i_{u}}=r_{u}
. If that is the case, then our algorithm predicts
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{j}=q_{u}
. Otherwise, we continue to the next iteration of the loop.
If we reach the end of the loop, then we simply guess that
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{j}=0
.

We note that our algorithm will terminate at step
u
with probability
p_{u}\prod_{l=1}^{u-1}(1-p_{l})
.
This is because to terminate at step
u
, we must encounter the event
\mathbf{c}_{i_{u}}=r_{u}
, which occurs with probability
\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{i_{u}}=r_{u}]=p_{u}
.
Additionally, we must not terminate in any previous steps
l<u
, each event happening with probability
\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{i_{l}}\neq r_{l}]=1-\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{i_{l}}=r_{l}]=1-p_{l}
.
If we terminate at step
u
, then we know that
\mathbf{c}_{i_{u}}=r_{u}
and we predict
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{j}=q_{u}
.
This prediction fails with probability
\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{j}\neq q_{u}|\mathbf{c}_{i_{u}}=r_{u}]=1-\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{j}=q_{u}|\mathbf{c}_{i_{u}}=r_{u}]=1-M_{j,q_{u},i_{u},r_{u}}
.
For the sake of simplifying notation, and because
j
remains constant and implicitly known throughout the algorithm, we use
M_{B_{u}}
to represent
M_{j,q_{u},i_{u},r_{u}}
for triplet
B_{u}=(q_{u},i_{u},r_{u})
.

The error rate
\epsilon=\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{\hat{c}}_{j}\neq\mathbf{c}_{j}]
of this algorithm is then given by

\displaystyle\epsilon=\sum_{u=1}^{s}(1-M_{B_{u}})p_{u}\prod_{l=1}^{u-1}(1-p_{l})+\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{j}\neq 0]\prod_{u=1}^{s}(1-p_{u})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u=1}^{s}(1-M_{B_{u}})p_{u}\prod_{l=1}^{u-1}(1-p_{l})+\prod_{u=1}^{s}(1-p_{u})

as
\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{c}_{j}\neq 0]\leq 1

Then, we note that
p_{u}\geq\beta
, implying that
\prod_{u=1}^{s}(1-p_{u})\leq(1-\beta)^{s}
.
Finally, we note that
1-M_{B_{u}}\leq\alpha
, so
\sum_{u=1}^{s}M_{B_{u}}p_{u}\prod_{l=1}^{u-1}(1-p_{l})\leq\alpha\sum_{u=1}^{s}p_{u}\prod_{l=1}^{u-1}(1-p_{l})\leq\alpha
by Lemma
S.2
.
Combining all of the above gives us an error
\epsilon\leq\alpha+(1-\beta)^{s}
.

∎
