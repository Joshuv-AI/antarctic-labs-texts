A Theoretical Case Study of the Generalisation of Machine-learned Potentials
Yangshuai Wang, Shashwat Patel, Christoph Ortner

Abstract
Machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) are typically trained on datasets that encompass a restricted subset of possible input structures, which presents a potential challenge for their generalization to a broader range of systems outside the training set. Nevertheless, MLIPs have demonstrated impressive accuracy in predicting forces and energies in simulations involving intricate and complex structures. In this paper we aim to take steps towards rigorously explaining the excellent observed generalisation properties of MLIPs. Specifically, we offer a comprehensive theoretical and numerical investigation of the generalization of MLIPs in the context of dislocation simulations. We quantify precisely how the accuracy of such simulations is directly determined by a few key factors: the size of the training structures, the choice of training observations (e.g., energies, forces, virials), and the level of accuracy achieved in the fitting process. Notably, our study reveals the crucial role of fitting virials in ensuring the consistency of MLIPs for dislocation simulations. Our series of careful numerical experiments encompassing screw, edge, and mixed dislocations, supports existing best practices in the MLIPs literature but also provides new insights into the design of data sets and loss functions.

In the present work we study the generalization of MLIPs for atomistic materials simulations. In this case, MLIPs are typically trained on limited data-sets that cover only a very small section of the full space of possible input structures containing at most hundreds of atoms. Predictions during simulations are on much larger domains often containing hundreds of thousand or even millions of atoms. As a paradigm case study, we selected the MLIPs simulation of dislocations in crystalline solids. This setting is sufficiently well understood that it can be studied by rigorous analytical tools, while still being of significant interest for materials modelling.

The investigation of dislocations has a long history in materials modelling due to the substantial influence that dislocations exert on the mechanical, electronic, and thermal material properties

By undertaking a comprehensive study of dislocation generation, motion, and interaction, one can attain a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of the mechanical response and failure modes exhibited by materials. Employing MLIPs in simulating dislocations opens up new opportunities for improved quantitative understanding how they influence material behaviour

In a recent work

As opposed to the commonly used statistical method to study the uncertainty quantification and error propagation

While

While it is clear that an extension to more complex scenarios is conceptually possible, the intricacies involved in such an extension require a thorough investigation.

Our primary contribution in the present work lies in providing such an extension for dislocation simulations. By leveraging known properties of their associated cores and elastic fields we will provide rigorous qualitative estimates of MLIPs generalisation errors. In contrast to point defects studied in

The slower decay of the elastic far-field associated with dislocations results in a fundamental distinction: training solely on energies and forces is insufficient to achieve a

Aside from the intrinsic value of a rigorous theoretical analysis, our study results in interesting practical considerations for optimizing the training of MLIPs, which we will highlight throughout this work and summarize in the Conclusion.

Background

2.1
Dislocation far fields

Our theoretical results will utilize known properties of equilibrium dislocation configurations, which we now review. These results will
not
form part of the computational schemes, but only used to analyze model errors.

We consider a model for straight dislocation lines
following the setup of
[
27
]
. Let
\mathsf{B}\mathbb{Z}^{3}
denote a 3D Bravais lattice oriented in such a way that the dislocation direction can be chosen parallel to
e_{3}
and the Burgers vector can be chosen as
\mathsf{b}=(\mathsf{b}_{1},0,\mathsf{b}_{3})\in\mathsf{B}\mathbb{Z}^{3}
. We further assume, without loss of generality, that the displacement fields are independent of the
x_{3}
-direction and thus only functions of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. We denote the resulting (projected) 2D reference lattice by

\Lambda={\sf A}\mathbb{Z}^{2}:=\{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2}):\ell\in\mathsf{B}\mathbb{Z}^{3}\}.

We restrict our analysis to single-species Bravais lattices. While there are no conceptual obstacles to generalising our analysis to multi-lattices, the notational details become more involved. Our numerical exploration in Section
4
will also include tests in a multi-lattice setting.

Let
u^{\rm CLE}(\cdot;\mathsf{b}):\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote a
far-field predictor
for single straight dislocation with Burgers vector
\mathsf{b}
, solving the associated continuum linearised elasticity (CLE) equation
[
29
]
. The derivation of
u^{\rm CLE}
is reviewed in the
B
.

A general deformed configuration of the infinite lattice
\Lambda
, with single straight dislocation configuration, is a map
y:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, decomposed into

\displaystyle y(\ell)=\ell+u^{\rm CLE}(\ell)+u(\ell)=y^{\rm CLE}(\ell)+u(\ell),

(2.1)

where the
relative displacement
field
u:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
is called the
core corrector
and accounts for discreteness and nonlinearity in atomistic models.

Let
\bar{y}^{\Lambda}
be the equilibrium state (under a suitable atomistic interaction law, to be specified later) of the single straight dislocation configuration of the infinite lattice
\Lambda
and
\bar{u}^{\Lambda}:=\bar{y}^{\Lambda}-y^{\rm CLE}
. Under mild and general conditions on the lattice and interaction law, it was rigorously shown
[
26
,
27
]
that the equilibrium core corrector has a generic decay,

\displaystyle\big|D\bar{u}^{\Lambda}(\ell)\big|\leq C|\ell|^{-2}\log(|\ell|),

(2.2)

where
D\bar{u}^{\Lambda}(\ell)
is a a finite difference gradient of
\bar{u}^{\Lambda}
centered at
\ell\in\Lambda
. The rigorous definition of
Du(\ell)
is given in (
1.17
).
A consequence of the decay (
2.2
) is that one can define a truncation operator
\Pi_{R}
such that
\Pi_{R}u(\ell)=0
for all
\ell\in\Lambda\setminus B_{R}
with approximation error

\|D\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\Lambda}-D\bar{u}^{\Lambda}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq CR^{-1}\log({R}).

(2.3)

The construction of
\Pi_{R}
is subtle
[
27
, Section 7.2]
and is therefore reviewed in (
1.29
).

2.2
Supercell simulations

When simulating dislocations using electronic structure models like density functional theory (DFT), periodic boundary conditions are commonly used. In the case of periodic domains (supercells), it is necessary to consider a periodic array of dislocations with alternating signs.
We therefore now
formally
extend the predictor-corrector framework reviewed in the previous section to multiple dislocations in a supercell (instead of an infinite domain) and will then use this setting in the remainder of the paper to study training and generalisation of MLIPs.
For the sake of simplicity of presentation, we will skip over some technical details but fill these gaps in the
A.1
.

We specify the
simulation
domain as a continuous cell
\Omega_{N}:=\mathsf{P}(-N/2,N/2]^{2}
, where
\mathsf{P}=(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is invertible and
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathsf{A}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. For a sufficiently large
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\Lambda_{N}:=\Lambda\cap\Omega_{N}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\Lambda_{N}^{{\rm per}}:=\bigcup_{\alpha\in N\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(\mathsf{P}\alpha+\Lambda_{N}),

where
\Lambda_{N}
is the supercell and
\Lambda_{N}^{{\rm per}}
is the resulting periodically repeated infinite defective lattice. We denote the space of periodic displacements by

\mathscr{U}^{{\rm per}}_{N}:=\{u:\Lambda_{N}^{{\rm per}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}~|~u(\ell+\mathsf{P}\alpha)=u(\ell)~\textrm{for}~\alpha\in N\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}.

We define a
dislocation configuration
to be a set
\mathcal{D}
of
n_{\mathcal{D}}
pairs
(x^{\rm core}_{i},\mathsf{b}_{i})\in\Omega_{N}\times\mathsf{B}\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, where
x^{\rm core}_{i}
is the core position for the
i
-th dislocation with accompanying Burgers vector
\mathsf{b}_{i}
. For compatibility with periodic boundary condition, we require that
n_{\mathcal{D}}
is even and the net-Burgers vector vanishes, i.e.,
\sum_{i}\mathsf{b}_{i}={\bm{0}}
. An illustration of a quadrupole screw dislocation configuration in W is shown in Figure
1
. We define the minimum separation distance of
\mathcal{D}
by

L_{\mathcal{D}}:=\min_{i\neq j}|x^{\rm core}_{i}-x^{\rm core}_{j}|.

Figure 1:
Illustration of a configuration of four screw dislocations in W, colored via Common Neighbor Analysis in Ovito
[
30
]
.

Analogous to
u^{\rm CLE}
,
we denote the CLE predictor for a periodic dislocation configuration
\mathcal{D}
by

u^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}(\cdot;\mathcal{D}):\Lambda_{N}^{\rm per}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
; for the details of its construction see
[
15
]
. As in (
2.1
) we can decompose a periodic configuration into
y(\ell)=\ell+u^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}(\ell)+u(\ell)
, where
u
is now the corrector field for multiple cores. We will now use the knowledge about core structure in the infinite-domain decomposition (
2.1
) to further refine the construction in the periodic case.

More specifically, let
\mathcal{E}(y)
be a potential energy functional defined acting on a periodic configuration
y
. An equilibrium defect geometry is obtained by solving

\displaystyle\bar{y}\in\arg\min\big\{\mathcal{E}(y),~y-y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}\in\mathscr{U}_{N}^{{\rm per}}\big\},

(2.4)

where
y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}(\ell):=\ell+u^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}(\ell)
.
Motivated by the theory for a single straight dislocation (
2.2
), we now make an intuitive and mild assumption on the existence, stability and regularity of equilibrium configurations for (
2.4
).

Assumption 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a dislocation core configuration with sufficiently large core separation distance
L_{\mathcal{D}}\geq L_{0}
.
Then we assume that there exists a strongly stable equilibrium
\bar{y}
of (
2.4
) satisfying

\exists~c_{0}>0\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})v,v\rangle\geq c_{0}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})},

with a stencil norm
\|\cdot\|_{\ell^{2}}
defined by (
1.17
). Moreover, we assume that the equilibrium
\bar{y}
can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\bar{y}(\ell)=y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}(\ell)+\sum_{(x^{\rm core}_{i},\mathsf{b}_{i})\in\mathcal{D}}\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\Lambda}(\ell-x^{\rm core}_{i};\mathsf{b}_{i})+\omega(\ell),\qquad\forall\ell\in\Lambda_{N},

(2.5)

where the defect core truncation operator
\Pi_{R}
is defined by (
1.29
) with radius
R=L_{\mathcal{D}}/3
, and the reminder term
\omega
satisfies

\displaystyle\|D\omega\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}\leq C\,\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\,L_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}\log(L_{\mathcal{D}}),

(2.6)

with a constant
C
dependent on the interaction law, on the stability constant
c_{0}
, but independent of the dislocation configuration
\mathcal{D}
(except, possibly, implicitly through
c_{0}
), the separation distance
L_{\mathcal{D}}
, or the domain size
N
.

Assumption
2.1
not only gives the existence of the equilibrium of the multiple dislocations in a periodic domain, but also establishes its structure: The equilibrium can be decomposed into two parts, a truncated defect core centered at each point defect and a remainder term. Our generalisation analysis in the next section heavily relies on this result.
The condition that
L_{\mathcal{D}}
is sufficiently large entails that defect cores do not overlap too strongly and hence the truncated infinite-lattice core corrector provides a good estimate for the core structure of interacting dislocations. The scaling of
L_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}\log(L_{\mathcal{D}})
of the remainder
\omega
is directly related to the truncation of the core at radius
R\propto L_{\mathcal{D}}
; cf. (
2.3
).
The scaling
\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}
of the remainder is simply due to accounting for the number of cores being truncated in a 2-norm.

Remark 2.1
.

We believe that Assumption
2.1
can be proven rigorously, possibly requiring some additional assumptions to avoid edge cases about the distribution of dislocation cores. Similar results for point defects are proven in
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
and for dislocation with different boundary conditions and simplified interaction law in
[
28
]
. To give further evidence for Assumption
2.1
we give a sketch of a proof in
A.2
. This proof assumes a uniform bound on the possible number of dislocation cores but is otherwise general. Removing that bound would be the main technical hurdle to a fully rigorous proof.

Since the focus of the current work is on a model error analysis for machine learned interatomic potentials, a rigorous proof of Assumption
2.1
goes well outside the scope of the current paper. Even if Assumption
2.1
were only valid under additional assumptions, this would not change the relevance of our following main results.

2.3
Machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs)

Small-scale single-defect simulations, requiring only few evaluations (e.g. geometry optimisation) can be routinely carried out using an electronic structure model such as density functional theory. However, due to the significant computational expense associated with electronic structure models, large-scale multi-defect simulations and long-time evolution are normally undertaken using interatomic potentials. While those were mostly empirical models in the past, it is now possible to construct machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs)
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
]
, fitted to an electronic structure model, and closely matching its predictions. MLIPs are becoming part of the standard toolbox of computational materials science. Our general analysis is agnostic to the choice of MLIP architecture, hence we give only a brief and generic introduction.

Virtually all modern MLIPs for materials model the total energy
\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}
as a sum of site energies,

\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}(y;\boldsymbol{c})=\sum_{i}\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\rm ML}(y;\boldsymbol{c}),

where
\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}_{i}
describes the local energy contribution from the
i
-th atomic site. The site energy
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\rm ML}(y;\boldsymbol{c})
is parameterized, and optimization of its parameters
\boldsymbol{c}
is achieved through the minimization of a loss function.

Given a training set
\mathfrak{R}
containing atomic configurations
y_{R}
, together with corresponding observations: total energies
\mathcal{E}(y_{R})
, forces
-\nabla\mathcal{E}(y_{R})
, and possibly other quantities such as virials, hessians, and so forth. A common choice in materials modelling is a quadratic cost function penalizing errors in energy, forces is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{c}):=\sum_{y_{R}\in\mathfrak{R}}\Big(W_{\rm E}\big|\mathcal{E}(y_{R})-\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}(y_{R};\boldsymbol{c})\big|^{2}+W_{\rm F}\big|\nabla\mathcal{E}(y_{R})-\nabla\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}(y_{R};\boldsymbol{c})\big|^{2}\Big),

(2.7)

where
W_{\rm E},W_{\rm F}
are weights that may depend on the configurations as well as the observations.

We will loosely think of (
2.4
) as the “high-fidelity” model, too expensive to solve in practice.
Given an MLIP
\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}
fitted to
\mathcal{E}
, we can now instead compute the equilibrium geometry with the new potential energy model,

\displaystyle\bar{y}^{\rm ML}\in

\displaystyle\arg\min\big\{\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}(y),~y-y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}\in\mathscr{U}_{N}^{{\rm per}}\big\}.

(2.8)

The geometry and energy errors committed in the approximate problem (
2.8
) are, respectively,

\|D\bar{y}-D\bar{y}^{\rm ML}\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\big|\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})-\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y}^{\rm ML})\big|.

The selection of training data, loss functions, and weight parameters leading to (
2.7
) plays a pivotal role in achieving accurate MLIPs (i.e. with the ability to make accurate predictions). The primary focus of this paper is to provide an analytical framework dedicated to elucidating these critical choices within the context of dislocation simulations.

3
Generalization and Error estimates

In this section we develop a theoretical framework to assess the error between the exact and approximate equilibrium geometries
\bar{y},\bar{y}^{\rm ML}
and energies
\mathcal{E}(\bar{y}),\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y}^{\rm ML})
in terms of the selected training data and in terms of the accuracy of the fit.
Since MLIPs are fitted to actual
ab initio
data, the training domains must be chosen small (order 10s to 100s or atoms). We think of the ability to train on small domains but predict on large and complex structures as a form of generalisation, which we can study rigorously within our framework.
To that end, we first introduce the training domains, the
matching conditions
between the (
ab initio
) reference and the MLIPs, and then give a rigorous error estimate for predictions on large simulation domains with (potentially) large and complex dislocation configurations.

3.1
Training domains and matching conditions

In the setting of Assumption
2.1
(dislocation cores cannot get too close) it is intuitive that each training domain should only contain a single dislocation. However, in contrast to our approach for point defects as presented in
[
21
]
, a dipole configuration needs to be taken into consideration to enable the use of periodic boundary conditions on the training domain.

Illustration of simulation domain
\Omega_{N}
(left) and training domain
\Omega_{L}
(lower right) for screw dislocations in W.

To be more precise, given
L\leq L_{\mathcal{D}}\ll N
, we call
\Omega_{L}:=\mathsf{P}(-L,L]^{2}
the
training domain
while
\Omega_{N}
is called the
simulation domain
. The defected lattice
\Lambda_{L}:=\Lambda\cap\Omega_{L}
incorporates a dipole configuration to facilitate the application of periodic boundary conditions (see e.g.
[
20
]
) with distance of order
O(L)
to prevent interaction.
See Figure
2
for an illustration of screw dislocations in W. Similarly as in the previous section, let
\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L}
be the corresponding space of periodic displacements. We equip
\mathscr{U}_{L}^{\rm per}
with the norm
\|v\|_{\mathscr{U}_{L}^{\rm per}}:=\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{L})}
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{L}(y)
and
\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}_{L}(y)
be the energy functionals defined on
\Lambda_{L}
, and the equilibrium of the corresponding variational problem with
\mathcal{E}_{L}(y)
is denoted by
\bar{y}_{L}
.
To train an MLIP capable of accurately simulating dislocations we ensure that the training structures in the loss (
2.7
) contains the training cell minimizer
\bar{y}_{L}
, as well as perturbed states for example obtained through sub-sampling an MD trajectory, or simply rattling the atom positions.

We now introduce
matching conditions
between the reference model and MLIPs that are directly motivated by such a loss function and training set but are technically more stringent, which makes a rigorous error analysis tractable.
Let
\delta>0
and
B_{\delta}(\bar{y}_{L})\subset\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L}
all periodic atomic displacements at (energy-norm) distance at most
\delta
from
\bar{u}_{L}
.
Then the matching condition for energy and forces are, respectively, defined by

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm E}

\displaystyle:=\max_{y_{L}\in B_{\delta}(\bar{y}_{L})}\big|\mathcal{E}_{L}(y_{L})-\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}_{L}(y_{L})\big|,\qquad\text{and}

(3.9)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm F}

\displaystyle:=\max_{y_{L}\in B_{\delta}(\bar{y}_{L})}\big\|-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{L}(y_{L})+\nabla\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}_{L}(y_{L})\big\|_{(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L})^{*}},

(3.10)

where
\|\cdot\|_{(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L})^{*}}
is the dual norm of
\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L}
.

Mathematically, to guarantee convergence of MLIP equilibria, one needs to ensure stability and this requires accuracy of the MLIP hessian, hence we also introduce a hessian matching condition

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm H}

\displaystyle:=\big\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}_{L}(\bar{y}_{L})-\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}_{L}(\bar{y}_{L})\big\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L},(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L})^{*})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\big\|\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}_{L}^{\rm hom}(x_{0})-\nabla^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML,hom}_{L}(x_{0})\big\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L},(\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{L})^{*})},

(3.11)

where the second term represents the force constant error on the homogeneous lattice with the identical mapping
x_{0}(\ell)=\ell
for
\ell\in\Lambda_{L}
. The rationale behind establishing this matching condition stems from our stability analysis (cf. (
1.41
)), which necessitates control of the Hessian at defect cores as well as at the far field (homogeneous lattice).

Finally, it turns out that to accurately model the elastic fields between dislocation cores one should provide some measure of accuracy of the Cauchy–Born continuum linear elastic response. This can be understood in terms of accuracy of the stress (or, alternatively, the virial): Let
W_{\rm cb}(\mathsf{F})
(resp.
W^{\rm ML}_{\rm cb}
) denote the unit cell energy per unit volume under deformation
\mathsf{F}
, for the reference model (resp. MLIP model) detailed in (
1.22
), then
\partial_{\mathsf{F}}W_{\rm cb}
is the stress. It is common in MLIP fitting to add stresses to the loss function. For the purpose of our analysis it is convenient to introduce the matching conditions

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm V}_{j}:=\big|\partial^{j+1}_{\mathsf{F}}W_{\rm cb}(\mathsf{I})-\partial^{j+1}_{\mathsf{F}}W_{\rm cb}^{\rm ML}(\mathsf{I})\big|,\qquad\text{for }j=1,2,

(3.12)

where
\mathsf{I}
is the identity matrix.
The matching condition
\varepsilon^{\rm V}_{1}
represents the error in the continuum linear elastic response, while the the matching condition
\varepsilon^{\rm V}_{2}
measures a leading order error in the nonlinear elastic response.

3.2
Error estimates

We are now in a position to quantify the geometry and energy errors committed during MLIP simulations of dislocations in terms of the fit accuracy. The proof of the following theorem is given in the

.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
\bar{u}
is a strongly stable equilibrium of (

2.1

Then, for
\varepsilon^{\rm F},\varepsilon^{\rm H},\varepsilon^{\rm V}_{1}
sufficiently small, there exists an equilibrium
\bar{y}^{\rm ML}
of (

\displaystyle\|D\bar{y}-D\bar{y}^{\rm ML}\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}

\displaystyle\leq~C^{\rm G}\cdot\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot\big(\varepsilon^{\rm F}+\log^{1/2}(L_{\mathcal{D}})\cdot\varepsilon^{\rm V}_{1}+L^{-1}\cdot\varepsilon^{\rm V}_{2}+L^{-2}\big),

4
Numerical Tests

The generalization analysis of the previous section gives insights into the construction of accurate MLIPs. It directs the selection of training data and the assignment of weights. We now use these results to guide a concrete MLIP implementation and test that implementation on a range of carefully designed numerical experiments to illustrate the theoretical predictions.

First, we need to choose a parameterisation of MLIPs from an abundance of available options

Since the choice of MLIP architecture is non-essential and since the ACE model has been described in-depth in other references, we only briefly review the most salient details: Given a cutoff radius

Moreover, they are naturally body-ordered which gives a physically interpretable approximation parameter to converge the fit accuracy. A detailed review of the ACE model and its approximation parameters is provided in the

4.1.2
Training sets and loss

Following the generalisation analysis, our aim is to construct ACE models that match a reference model in the sense of making

and

The matching conditions on virial stress (

We first introduce the training set,

5
Conclusion

We presented a theoretical and numerical study of the generalization capability of MLIPs for dislocation simulations in a crystalline solid. We investigated the error propagation from fitting MLIPs on a small training domain to making predictions on a large simulation domain. Our analysis identifies what observations one should acquire from the reference model to obtain accurate predictions in this case.
Our theoretical results partially justify existing best practices in the MLIPs literature, but also provide more fine-grained qualitative information about how prediction accuracy depends on the choice of training data.
This approach also suggests a new perspective on how to approach the collection of training data, augmenting e.g. the emerging active learning approaches, and in particular also on the design of loss functions.

The present work intends to highlight the potential of our approach in a relatively simple setting where a detailed and rigorous study is possible, but as a result is limited in scope. A large number of possible generalisations are possible, for example to more complex material and defect geometries, or how our analytic approach to error estimation might be combined with more statistical approached.

As discussed in Section

We introduce the finite difference stencil to represent the local atomistic environment. For
\ell\in\Lambda_{N}^{\rm per}
and
\rho\in\Lambda_{N}^{\rm per}-\ell

D_{\rho}v(\ell):=v(\ell+\rho)-v(\ell)
. For a subset
\mathcal{R}\subset\Lambda_{N}^{\rm per}-\ell

define

(1.17)

where the nearest neighbours set

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\ell):=

(1.18)

Next, we introduce the concept of site potential, a crucial factor that provides insight into the local energy contributions from specific atomic sites. This element holds significant importance in defining the fundamental physical model utilized in our simulations. Although quantum mechanical models often rely on total energies, there are situations where it is feasible and relevant to construct site energies

The reference site potential is a mapping

Regularity and locality:

For all

possesses partial derivatives up to

\mathfrak{n}\geq 3

for all

The energy-difference functional is then defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(y):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda_{N}}V_{\ell}\big(Dy(\ell)\big).

In order to derive the

For

More precisely, the reference and MLIPs site potentials are approximated by the Cauchy-Born elastic energy density functional
W_{\rm cb},~W^{\rm ML}_{\rm cb}:\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
respectively, with

\displaystyle W_{\rm cb}(\mathsf{F}):=V(\mathsf{F}\cdot\Lambda_{*})\qquad\text{and}\qquad W^{\rm ML}_{\rm cb}(\mathsf{F}):=V^{\rm ML}(\mathsf{F}\cdot\Lambda_{*}),

(1.22)

where
\Lambda_{*}:=\Lambda\setminus 0
. The derivative (virial stress) and even higher order derivatives with respect to the deformation
\mathsf{F}
can be obtained by direct calculations,

\displaystyle\partial^{j}_{\mathsf{F}}W_{\rm cb}(\mathsf{F}_{0}):=\partial^{j}_{\mathsf{F}}W_{\rm cb}(\mathsf{F})\big|_{\mathsf{F}=\mathsf{F}_{0}}=-\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\cdots,\rho_{j})\in(\Lambda_{*})^{j}}V_{,\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\mathsf{F}_{0}\cdot\Lambda_{*})\underbrace{\otimes{\rho}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\rho}_{j}}_{=:~\otimes\boldsymbol{\rho}},

(1.23)

where
\otimes
denotes the standard Kronecker product.

We then introduce the following accuracy measure of the virial stress:

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\rm V}_{j}:=\big|\partial^{j+1}_{\mathsf{F}}W_{\rm cb}(\mathsf{I})-\partial^{j+1}_{\mathsf{F}}W_{\rm cb}^{\rm ML}(\mathsf{I})\big|:=\Bigg|\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in(\Lambda_{*})^{j+1}}\big(V_{,\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\mathsf{I})-V^{\rm ML}_{,\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\mathsf{I})\big)\otimes{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\Bigg|,

(1.24)

for
j=1,\cdots,\mathfrak{n}-1
, where
\mathsf{I}
is the identity matrix.

For the purpose of error analysis, we require the equilibrium of the single straight dislocation in infinite lattice. Following the results in
[
26
, Theorem 2.1]
, the corresponding energy-difference functional reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\Lambda}(y):=\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}V_{\ell}\big(Dy(\ell)\big),

(1.25)

where
V_{\ell}({\bf g}):=V\big({\bf g}\big)-V\big(Dy^{\rm CLE}(\ell)\big)
.

The corresponding variational problem for the equilibrium state is

\bar{y}^{\Lambda}\in\arg\min\big\{\mathcal{E}^{\Lambda}(y),~y-y^{\rm CLE}\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda)\big\},

(1.26)

where “
\arg\min
” is understood as the set of local minimizers and the discrete energy space for infinite lattice

\displaystyle{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda):=\big\{u:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}~\big\lvert~\|Du\|_{\ell^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}(\Lambda)}<\infty\big\}.

(1.27)

We will need a strong stability condition
[
26
,
27
]
as well as qualitative information about the equilibrium, that is,

\displaystyle\exists~\bar{c}>0~{\rm s.t.}~\big\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\Lambda}(\bar{y}^{\Lambda})v,v\big\rangle\geq\bar{c}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}}\qquad\forall~v\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda).

(1.28)

Before introducing the equilibrium of multiple dislocation configurations in
\Lambda_{N}
, we define a family of defect core truncation operators
\{\Pi_{R}^{i}\}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}_{i=1}
. Let
\eta\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2};[0,1])
be a cut-off function satisfying
\eta(x)=1
for
|x|\leq 4/6
and
\eta(x)=0
for
|x|\geq 5/6
. We denote
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
as the
canonical triangulation
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose nodes are the reference sites
\Lambda
(cf.
[
27
, Section 2.1]
). Let
Iu
be the piecewise affine interpolant of
u
with respect to
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
, and
A_{R}:=B_{5R/6}\setminus B_{4R/6}
be an annulus, then we can define the truncation operator by

\displaystyle\Pi^{i}_{R}u(\ell):=\eta\Big(\frac{\ell-x^{\rm core}_{i}}{R}\Big)\big(u(\ell)-a^{i}_{R}\big),\quad\textrm{where}\quad a^{i}_{R}:={\mathchoice{{-\mkern-19.0mu\int}}{{-\mkern-16.0mu\int}}{{-\mkern-16.0mu\int}}{{-\mkern-16.0mu\int}}}_{x^{\rm core}_{i}+A_{R}}Iu(x)\,{\rm d}x.

(1.29)

In particular, we denote
\Pi_{R}
as the case that the core is placed at the origin.

A.2
Proof of Assumption
2.1

As discussed in Remark
2.1
, Assumption
2.1
is a conceptually straightforward but technically involved extension of our previous work
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
from point defects to dislocations. Our method of proof would lead to a constant
L_{0}
that may depend on the number of defect cores
n_{\mathcal{D}}
, which is the most difficult gap to fill towards a rigorous proof of Assumption
2.1
. In this section, we aim to present a sketch of the proof, focusing on the scenario where the number of cores is bounded.

We first define an approximated solution (predictor) to the variational problem (
2.4
) for dislocation configuration
\mathcal{D}
with truncation radius
R=L_{\mathcal{D}}/3
to be

\displaystyle z(\ell)=y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}(\ell)+\sum_{(x^{\rm core}_{i},\mathsf{b}_{i})\in\mathcal{D}}\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\Lambda}(\ell-x^{\rm core}_{i};\mathsf{b}_{i}),\qquad\forall\ell\in\Lambda_{N},

(1.30)

where
\Pi_{R}
is defined by (
1.29
) with the core positioned at the origin. We then give an estimate on the residual of such an approximated solution in terms of
L_{\mathcal{D}}
. To be more precise, we want to prove that there exists a constant
L_{0}>0
such that, for
L_{\mathcal{D}}>L_{0}
,

\displaystyle\big|\langle\delta\mathcal{E}(z),v\rangle\big|\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot L_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})},

(1.31)

where the constant
C
is independent of
N,n_{\mathcal{D}},L_{\mathcal{D}}
.

As a matter of fact, for any
v\in\mathscr{U}^{{\rm per}}_{N}
, we define

\displaystyle v_{i}:=\Pi^{i}_{r}v\quad\textrm{for}~i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}},\quad\textrm{and}\quad v_{0}:=v-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}v_{i},

(1.32)

where
r:=R+1=L_{\mathcal{D}}/3+1
and the defect core truncation operator
\Pi^{i}_{r}
is defined by (
1.29
).

We then decompose the residual into three parts

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mathcal{E}(z),v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\langle\delta\mathcal{E}(z),v_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta\mathcal{E}(z),v_{0}\rangle+\sum^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}_{i=1}\langle\delta\mathcal{E}(z)-\delta\mathcal{E}\big(y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+T_{N}^{\rm per}\bar{u}^{\Lambda}(\cdot-\ell_{i})\big),v_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt+\sum^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}_{i=1}\langle\delta\mathcal{E}\big(y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+T_{N}^{\rm per}\bar{u}^{\Lambda}(\cdot-\ell_{i})\big),v_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=:T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3},

(1.33)

where the operator
T_{N}^{\rm per}
maps the displacements from
\mathscr{U}^{1,2}(\Lambda)
to
\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{N}(\Lambda_{N})
. The construction of
T_{N}^{\rm per}
follows a similar procedure to that of training domains discussed in Section
3.1
.

Note that
r:=R+1=L_{\mathcal{D}}/3+1
. The term
T_{1}
can be estimated by analyzing the residual of the linear elasticity predictor
u^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}
. Following the analysis of
[
28
, Lemma 4.3]
and adapting it to the periodic setting, we can obtain that
|T_{1}|\leq Cr^{-2}\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}
. For the last two terms, following the proof of
[
21
, Lemma 6.3]
, we have
|T_{2}|\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot L_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}\log(L_{\mathcal{D}})\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}
and
|T_{3}|\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot R^{-1}\log(R)\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}
. Hence, taking into account these estimates with (
A.2
), we can obtain the following consistency

\big|\langle\delta\mathcal{E}(z),v\rangle\big|\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot L_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}\log(L_{\mathcal{D}})\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}.

To apply the inverse function theorem
[
21
, Lemma A.1]
, we then proceed to prove that
\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z)
is positive. This result employs the ideas similar to those used in the proofs of
[
27
, Theorem 7.7]
and
[
28
, Lemma 5.2]
, modified here to an periodic setting and extended to cover the case of multiple straight dislocations.

We consider the following decomposition

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z)v,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z)v_{i},v_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z)v_{0},v_{0}\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z)v_{i},v_{i}\rangle+2\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z)v_{0},v_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=:S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3},

(1.34)

where the first one is related to the stability of homogeneous lattice (phonon stability)
[
21
, Proposition 6.1]
, the second one and the third one can be estimated by following the analysis in
[
28
]

as well as the strongly stable of single dislocation shown in (
1.28
).

Hence, we can prove that, there exists a constant
L_{0}>0
such that, for
L_{\mathcal{D}}>L_{0}
, there exists
\bar{c}_{0}\geq\bar{c}/2
so that

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z)v,v\rangle\geq\bar{c}_{L_{\mathcal{D}}}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}\qquad\forall~v\in\mathscr{U}^{{\rm per}}_{N}.

(1.35)

We are aware of no argument that allows us to give a quantitative or even qualitative estimate on the magnitude of
L_{0}
.

Applying the inverse function theorem
[
21
, Lemma A.1]
, we can obtain that there exist
L_{0}>0
, where
\mathcal{D}
satisfies
L_{\mathcal{D}}\geq L_{0}
, and
z
is an approximated solution defined by (
1.30
) corresponding to
\mathcal{D}
. It follows that for any
v\in\mathscr{U}_{N}^{{\rm per}}
, there exists
\omega\in\mathscr{U}_{N}^{{\rm per}}
with

\displaystyle\|D\omega\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot L_{\mathcal{D}}^{-1}\log(L_{\mathcal{D}}),

(1.36)

such that

\displaystyle\langle\delta\mathcal{E}(z+\omega),v\rangle=0,\quad\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(z+\omega)v,v\rangle\geq\frac{\bar{c}_{L_{\mathcal{D}}}}{2}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}}.

Writing
\bar{y}:=z+\omega
yields the stated result of the Assumption
2.1
.

A.3
Proof of the generalisation analysis

We are ready to give the detailed proof of the generalisation analysis (Theorem
3.1
), which is the main result in this paper.

Proof.

Applying the framework of the
a priori
error estimates in
[
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
]
, we divide the proof into several steps in order to apply the inverse function theorem
[
21
, Lemma A.1]
.

1. Stability:
For any
v\in\mathscr{U}^{{\rm per}}_{N}
, we consider the stability of

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})v,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})v,v\rangle+\big(\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})v,v\rangle-\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})v,v\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=:S_{1}+S_{2}.

(1.37)

From the Assumption
2.1
, we can obtain that

S_{1}:=\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})v,v\rangle\geq c_{0}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}}.

We split the test function
v
. For
L\leq L_{\mathcal{D}}
, let
r:=L/3+1
, we define

\displaystyle v_{i}:=\Pi^{i}_{r}v\quad\textrm{for}~i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}},\quad\textrm{and}\quad v_{0}:=v-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}v_{i}.

(1.38)

It is shown in
[
21
, Lemma A.2]
that
\|Dv_{i}\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}\leq C\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}
for
i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
.

The term
S_{2}
can be further split into three parts

\displaystyle\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})\big)v,v\big\rangle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i,j=0}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})\big)v_{i},v_{j}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})\big)v_{0},v_{0}\big\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+2\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})\big)v_{0},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle~S_{21}+S_{22}+S_{23},

(1.39)

where we have ensured that supp
(v_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
only overlaps with supp
(v_{0})
by the choice of
r
and therefore all other cross-terms vanish.

To simply the notation, we denote
x_{0}(\ell):=\ell
,
\tilde{y}:=y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega
and
\tilde{u}:=u^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega
. For the term
S_{21}
, for
L
sufficiently large and
t\in[0,1]
, we can Taylor expand both
\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}
and
\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}
at the reference configuration

\displaystyle\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\tilde{y})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\tilde{y})\big)v_{0},v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(x_{0})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(x_{0})\big)v_{0},v_{0}\big\rangle+\big\langle\big(\delta^{3}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(x_{0}+t\tilde{u})-\delta^{3}\mathcal{E}(x_{0}+t\tilde{u})\big)\tilde{u}v_{0},v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle~S^{\rm(a)}_{21}+S^{\rm(b)}_{21}.

(1.40)

To estimate the first term
S^{\rm(a)}_{21}
, for
L
sufficiently large, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle|S^{\rm(a)}_{21}|\leq C\varepsilon^{\rm H}\cdot\|Dv_{0}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}\leq C\varepsilon^{\rm H}\cdot\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})},

(1.41)

where
\varepsilon^{\rm H}
is defined by (
3.1
) and the last inequality follows from the fact that
\|Dv_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}\leq C\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}
shown in
[
21
, Lemma A.2]
.

For the term
S^{\rm(b)}_{21}
, from the definition given by (
1.20
), similarly we can obtain

\displaystyle|S^{\rm(b)}_{21}|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{\ell\in\Lambda_{N}}\big|Du^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}(\ell)+D\omega(\ell)\big|_{\mathcal{N}}\cdot\big|Dv_{0}(\ell)\big|^{2}_{\mathcal{N}}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|Du^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\textrm{supp}(v_{0}))}+\|D\omega\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\textrm{supp}(v_{0}))}\big)\cdot\|Dv_{0}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\textrm{supp}(v_{0}))}

\displaystyle\leq CL^{-1}\cdot\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})},

(1.42)

where the last inequality follows from the estimates (
1.36
) and (
2.65
).

Let
\Pi_{R}\bar{y}^{\Lambda}:=y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\Lambda}
. To estimate
S_{22}
, recall the definition of the predictor by (
1.30
) and the construction of
v_{i}
, for each
i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
and
L
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\Pi_{R}\bar{y}^{\Lambda}(\cdot-x^{\rm core}_{i}))-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{R}\bar{y}^{\Lambda}(\cdot-x^{\rm core}_{i}))\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\Pi_{R}\bar{y}^{\Lambda}(\cdot-x^{\rm core}_{i}))-\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y}_{L}(\cdot-x^{\rm core}_{i}))\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y}_{L}(\cdot-x^{\rm core}_{i}))-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y}_{L}(\cdot-x^{\rm core}_{i}))\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y}_{L}(\cdot-x^{\rm core}_{i}))-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\Pi_{R}\bar{y}^{\Lambda}(\cdot-x^{\rm core}_{i}))\big)v_{i},v_{i}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~C(\varepsilon^{\rm H}+L^{-1}+\|D\bar{y}^{\Lambda}-D\bar{y}_{L}\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{L})})\cdot\|Dv_{i}\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\textrm{supp}(v_{i}))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~C(\varepsilon^{\rm H}+L^{-1})\cdot\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})},

(1.43)

where the last inequality follows from the results in
[
27
]
.

Noting that
\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(y)v_{0},v_{i}\rangle=\langle\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(y)(v-v_{i}),v_{i}\rangle
, the term
S_{23}
can be estimated similarly by

\displaystyle\big\langle\big(\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta^{2}\mathcal{E}(\bar{y})\big)v_{0},v_{i}\big\rangle\leq C(\varepsilon^{\rm H}+L^{-1})\cdot\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}.

(1.44)

Hence, combining the estimates from (
A.3
) to (
1.44
), for
L
sufficiently large and the matching condition
\varepsilon^{\rm H}
sufficiently small, we have

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})v,v\rangle\geq\frac{c_{0}}{2}\|Dv\|^{2}_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}.

(1.45)

2. Consistency:

We estimate the consistency error, for any
v\in\mathscr{U}_{N}^{\rm per}
, by

\displaystyle\langle\delta{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y}),v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta\mathcal{E}(\bar{y}),v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}_{i=1}\langle\delta{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta\mathcal{E}(\bar{y}),v_{i}\rangle+\langle\delta{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta\mathcal{E}(\bar{y}),v_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=:T_{1}+T_{2},

(1.46)

where
v_{i},i=0,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
, are constructed by (
1.38
).

Let
\Pi_{R}\bar{y}^{\Lambda}:=y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\Pi_{R}\bar{u}^{\Lambda}
. To estimate
T_{1}
, for each
i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathcal{D}}
, we denote

\displaystyle T^{\rm(i)}_{1}:=

\displaystyle\langle\delta{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}(\bar{y})-\delta\mathcal{E}(\bar{y}),v_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\langle\delta{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm ML}\big(\Pi_{R}\bar{y}^{\Lambda}(\cdot-x^{\rm core}_{i})\big)-\delta\mathcal{E}\big(\Pi_{R}\bar{y}^{\Lambda}(\cdot-x^{\rm core}_{i})\big),v_{i}\big\rangle.

(1.47)

Given
\delta>0
, for
L
sufficiently large, we have
\Pi_{R}\bar{y}^{\Lambda}\in B_{\delta}(\bar{y}_{L})
. Recalling the definition of
\varepsilon^{\rm F}
by (
3.10
), we can obtain

\displaystyle|T_{1}|\leq\sum^{n_{\mathcal{D}}}_{i=1}|T^{\rm(i)}_{1}|\leq C\sqrt{n_{\mathcal{D}}}\cdot\varepsilon^{\rm F}\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}(\Lambda_{N})}.

(1.48)

In the next steps of our analysis, we draw from techniques employed in the study of the Cauchy–Born continuum model
[
48
,
31
]
and blended atomistic-to-continuum methods
[
25
,
53
,
54
,
55
,
56
]
. For
v\in\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{N}
, we introduce two smooth interpolant operators. The first operator
\mathcal{I}:\mathscr{U}^{\rm per}_{N}\rightarrow C^{2,1}
, is employed to establish the regularity of
v

[
53
, Section 2.2.1]
while the second one
\mathcal{J}:\mathscr{U}_{N}^{\rm per}\rightarrow\dot{H}^{1}_{\rm per}
, is utilized for the construction of the so-called
smeared bond integrals

[
53
, Section 5.1]
. The dual operator of
\mathcal{J}
is then represented as
\mathcal{J}^{*}:\dot{H}^{-1}_{\rm per}\rightarrow(\mathscr{U}_{N}^{\rm per})^{*}
. It is shown in
[
53
, Lemma 5.1]
that
\|\nabla(\mathcal{J}v)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}}
.

Recalling the Cauchy-Born elastic energy density functional defined by (
1.22
), we denote the corresponding Cauchy-Born energy as
\mathcal{E}_{\rm cb}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\rm ML}_{\rm cb}
. To further simplify the notation, we denote
\mathcal{F}:=\delta\mathcal{E}
, and similarly we define
\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb}
,
\mathcal{F}^{\rm ML}
, and
\mathcal{F}^{\rm ML}_{\rm cb}
. Note that
\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb},\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb}^{\rm ML}\in\dot{H}^{-1}_{\rm per}
.
The functionals
\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb}
(and analogously
\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb}^{\rm ML}
) are
defined
via the identity:

\big\langle\mathcal{J}^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb}(y),v\big\rangle=\big\langle\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb}(y),\mathcal{J}v\big\rangle=\int_{\Omega_{N}}\partial_{\rm F}W_{\rm cb}\big(\nabla(\mathcal{I}y)\big):\nabla(\mathcal{J}v)\,{\rm d}x,\qquad\forall v\in\mathscr{U}_{N}^{\rm per}.

We first split
T_{2}
into three parts:

\displaystyle T_{2}=

\displaystyle~\big\langle\mathcal{F}(y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega)-\mathcal{F}^{\rm ML}(y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega),v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\big\langle\mathcal{F}(y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega)-\mathcal{J}^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb}(y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega),v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+\big\langle\mathcal{J}^{*}\mathcal{F}^{\rm ML}_{\rm cb}(y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega)-\mathcal{F}^{\rm ML}(y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega),v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+\big\langle\mathcal{J}^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb}(y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega)-\mathcal{J}^{*}\mathcal{F}^{\rm ML}_{\rm cb}(y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega),v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle~T_{21}+T_{22}+T_{23}.

(1.49)

Let
\tilde{y}:=y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega
and
\tilde{u}:=u^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per}+\omega
. The first two terms,
T_{21}
and
T_{22}
, can be estimated by the Cauchy-Born (continuum) modeling error
[
48
, Lemma 4.5]
:

\displaystyle|T_{21}|+|T_{22}|

\displaystyle=\Big|\big\langle\mathcal{F}(\tilde{y})-\mathcal{J}^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb}(\tilde{y}),v_{0}\big\rangle\Big|+\Big|\big\langle\mathcal{J}^{*}\mathcal{F}^{\rm ML}_{\rm cb}(\tilde{y})-\mathcal{F}^{\rm ML}(\tilde{y}),v_{0}\big\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\nabla^{3}(\mathcal{I}\tilde{y})\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla^{2}(\mathcal{I}\tilde{y})\|^{2}_{L^{4}}\big)\cdot\|\nabla(\mathcal{J}v_{0})\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq CL^{-2}\cdot\|Dv_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq CL^{-2}\cdot\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}},

(1.50)

where the last line follows from the fact that
|\nabla(\mathcal{I}y^{\rm CLE}_{\rm per})|\leq C|x|^{-1}
, the estimate
\|\nabla(\mathcal{J}v_{0})\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|Dv_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}
by
[
53
, Lemma 5.1]
and
\|Dv_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq C\|Dv\|_{\ell^{2}}
shown in
[
21
, Lemma A.2]
.

To estimate the last term
T_{23}
, we have

\displaystyle~\big\langle\mathcal{J}^{*}\mathcal{F}^{\rm ML}_{\rm cb}(\tilde{y})-\mathcal{J}^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\rm cb}(\tilde{y}),v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

Appendix B
Predictors

In this part, we briefly recall the predictor of a single dislocation on infinite lattice
\Lambda
, which requires a small modification to the standard CLE solution. Let
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the position of dislocation core and
\Gamma:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}~|~x_{2}=\hat{x_{2}},x_{1}\geq\hat{x_{1}}\}
be the “branch-cut", with
\hat{x}
chosen such that
\Gamma\cap\Lambda=\emptyset
. We define the far-field predictor
u_{0}
by

\displaystyle u_{0}(x):=u^{\rm lin}(\xi^{-1}(x)),

(2.58)

where
u^{\rm lin}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma;\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the solution of continuum linear elasticity (CLE)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{j\beta}_{i\alpha}\frac{\partial^{2}u^{\rm lin}_{i}}{\partial x_{\alpha}\partial x_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{in}~~\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma,

\displaystyle u^{\rm lin}(x+)-u^{\rm lin}(x-)

\displaystyle=-{\sf b}\qquad\text{for}~~x\in\Gamma\setminus\{\hat{x}\},

(2.59)

\displaystyle\nabla_{e_{2}}u^{\rm lin}(x+)-\nabla_{e_{2}}u^{\rm lin}(x-)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{for}~~x\in\Gamma\setminus\{\hat{x}\},

where the forth-order tensor
\mathbb{C}
is the linearised Cauchy-Born tensor (derived from the potential
V^{\rm h}
, see
[
27
, § 7]
for more detail),

\displaystyle\xi(x)=x-{\sf b}_{12}\frac{1}{2\pi}\eta\left(\frac{|x-\hat{x}|}{\hat{r}}\right)\arg(x-\hat{x}),

(2.60)

with
\arg(x)
denoting the angle in
(0,2\pi)
between
x
and

{\sf b}_{12}=({\sf b}_{1},{\sf b}_{2})=({\sf b}_{1},0)
, and

\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\eta=0
in
(-\infty,0]
and
\eta=1
in

[1,\infty)
which removes the singularity. It is widely recognized that the gradient of the displacement field
u_{0}
follows
r^{-1}
with respect to the distance from
\hat{x}
.

In order to model dislocations, the homogeneous site potential
V
must be invariant under lattice slip. Following
[
27
]
, we define the slip operator
S_{0}
acting on the displacements
w:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, by (
\mathsf{b}_{12}
represents the projection of the Burger’s vector to the
(x_{1},x_{2})
plane)

S_{0}w(x):=\begin{cases}~~w(x)\qquad\qquad\qquad&x_{2}>\hat{x}_{2}\\
~~w(x-\mathsf{b}_{12})-\mathsf{b}\qquad&x_{2}<\hat{x}_{2}\end{cases}.

We may then formulate the slip invariance condition by defining a mapping
S
, where
S
is an
\ell^{2}
-orthogonal operator with dual
S^{*}=S^{-1}
by

Su(\ell):=\begin{cases}~~u(\ell)\qquad&\ell_{2}>\hat{x}_{2}\\
~~u(\ell-\mathsf{b}_{12})\qquad&\ell_{2}<\hat{x}_{2}\end{cases},\qquad S^{*}u(\ell)=\begin{cases}~~u(\ell)\qquad&\ell_{2}>\hat{x}_{2}\\
~~u(\ell+\mathsf{b}_{12})\qquad&\ell_{2}<\hat{x}_{2}\end{cases}.

The slip invariance condition can now be expressed as

V\big(D(u_{0}+u)(\ell)\big)=V\big(S^{*}DS_{0}(u_{0}+u)(\ell)\big),\qquad\forall~\ell\in\Lambda,u\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda),

(2.61)

where
u_{0}
is defined by (
2.58
).

In our analysis we require that applying the slip operator to the predictor map
u_{0}
yields a smooth function in the half-space
\Omega_{\Gamma}=\{x_{1}\geq\hat{x}_{1}+\hat{r}+\mathsf{b}_{1}\}
.
It is therefore natural to define (likewise to
[
27
]
) the elastic strains

e(\ell):=\big(e_{\rho}(\ell)\big)_{\rho\in\Lambda-\ell},\qquad e_{\rho}(\ell)=\begin{cases}~~S^{*}D_{\rho}S_{0}u_{0}(\ell)\qquad&\ell\in\Omega_{\Gamma}\\
~~D_{\rho}u_{0}(\ell)\qquad&\ell\notin\Omega_{\Gamma}\end{cases},

(2.62)

and the analogous definition for corrector
u

Du(\ell):=\big(D_{\rho}u(\ell)\big)_{\rho\in\Lambda-\ell},\qquad D_{\rho}u(\ell)=\begin{cases}~~S^{*}D_{\rho}Su(\ell)\qquad&\ell\in\Omega_{\Gamma}\\
~~D_{\rho}u(\ell)\qquad&\ell\notin\Omega_{\Gamma}\end{cases}.

(2.63)

Using this notation, the slip invariance condition (
2.61
) may be written as, for
u\in{\mathscr{U}}^{1,2}(\Lambda)
,

V\big(D(u_{0}+u)(\ell)\big)=V\big(e(\ell)+Du(\ell)\big).

(2.64)

The following lemma, proven in
[
26
]
, is a straightforward extension of
[
27

.

Lemma 2.1

If the predictor
u_{0}
is defined by (

Appendix C
The Atomic Cluster Expansion

We briefly review the construction of the ACE potential, but refer to
[
1
,
5
,
34
,
35
]
for further details.
Given a
correlation order

\mathcal{N}\in\mathbb{N}
, we first write the ACE site potential in the form of an
atomic body-order expansion
,
\displaystyle V^{\rm ACE}\big(\{\boldsymbol{g_{j}}\}\big)=\sum_{N=0}^{\mathcal{N}}\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{j_{1}\neq\cdots\neq j_{N}}V_{N}(\boldsymbol{g}_{j_{1}},\cdots,\boldsymbol{g}_{j_{N}})
, where the
N
-body potential
V_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{dN}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
can be approximated by using a tensor product basis
[
1
, Proposition 1]
,

\displaystyle\phi_{\boldsymbol{n\ell m}}\big(\{\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\big):=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\phi_{n_{j}\ell_{j}m_{j}}(\boldsymbol{g}_{j})\quad

\displaystyle{\rm with}\quad\phi_{n\ell m}(\boldsymbol{r}):=P_{n}(r)Y^{m}_{\ell}(\hat{r}),~~\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~r=|\boldsymbol{r}|,~\hat{r}=\boldsymbol{r}/r,

where
P_{n},~n=0,1,2,\cdots
are radial basis functions (for example, Jacobi polynomials), and
Y_{\ell}^{m},~\ell=0,1,2,\cdots,~m=-\ell,\cdots,\ell
are the complex spherical harmonics.
The basis functions are further symmetrised to a permutation invariant form,

\displaystyle\sum_{(\boldsymbol{n,\ell,m})~{\rm ordered}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{n\ell m}}\circ\sigma,

where
S_{N}
is the collection of all permutations, and by
\sum_{(\boldsymbol{n,\ell,m})~{\rm ordered}}
we mean that the sum is over all lexicographically ordered tuples
\big((n_{j},\ell_{j},m_{j})\big)_{j=1}^{N}
.
The next step is to incorporate the invariance under point reflections and rotations

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{n\ell}i}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{m}i}^{\boldsymbol{n\ell}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{n\ell m}}\circ\sigma\quad{\rm with}\quad\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=\big\{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}~|~-\ell_{\alpha}\leq\mu_{\alpha}\leq\ell_{\alpha}\big\},

where the coefficients
\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{m}i}^{\boldsymbol{nl}}
are given in
[
1
, Lemma 2 and Eq. (3.12)]
.
It was shown in
[
1
]
that the basis defined above is explicit but computational inefficient. The so-called “density trick" technique used in
[
2
,
5
,
6
]
can transform this basis into one that is computational efficient. The alternative basis is

\displaystyle B_{\boldsymbol{n\ell}i}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{m}i}^{\boldsymbol{n\ell}}A_{\boldsymbol{nlm}}\quad{\rm with~the~correlations}\quad A_{\boldsymbol{nlm}}:=\prod_{\alpha=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\phi_{n_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}m_{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{g}_{j}),

which avoids both the
N!
cost for symmetrising the basis as well as the
C_{J}^{N}
cost of summation over all order
N
clusters within an atomic neighbourhood.
The resulting basis set is then defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{N}:=\big\{B_{\boldsymbol{n\ell}i}~|~(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{2N}~{\rm ordered},~\sum_{\alpha}\ell_{\alpha}~{\rm even},~i=1,\cdots,\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{n\ell}}\big\},

(3.66)

where
\boldsymbol{n}_{\boldsymbol{n\ell}}
is the number of basis functions for the selected
(\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{l})
channels; see
[
1
, Proposition 7 and Eq. (3.12)]
.

Once the finite symmetric polynomial basis set
\boldsymbol{B}\subset\bigcup^{\mathcal{N}}_{N=1}\boldsymbol{B}_{N}
is constructed, the ACE site potential can be expressed as

\displaystyle V^{\rm ACE}(\boldsymbol{g};\{c_{B}\}_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}})=\sum_{B\in\boldsymbol{B}}c_{B}B(\boldsymbol{g})

(3.67)

with the coefficients
c_{B}
. The corresponding force of this potential is denoted by
\mathcal{F}^{\rm ACE}
.

The family of potentials are systematically improvable (see
[
1
, Section 6.2]
): by increasing the body-order, cutoff radius and polynomial degree they are in principle capable of representing an arbitrary many-body potential energy surface to within arbitrary accuracy
[
1
,
35
]
.
