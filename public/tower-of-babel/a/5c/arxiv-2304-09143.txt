The Casimir effect for stack of graphenes
Natalia Emelianova, Rashid Kashapov, Nail Khusnutdinov

Abstract
We consider a stack of parallel sheets composed of conducting planes with tensorial conductivities. Using the scattering matrix approach, we derive explicit formulas for the Casimir energy of two, three, and four planes, as well as a recurrence relation for arbitrary planes. Specifically, for a stack of graphene, we solve the recurrence relations and obtain formulas for the Casimir energy and force acting on the planes within the stack. Moreover, we calculate the binding energy in the graphene stack with graphite interplane separation, which amounts to E i ​ b = 9.9 E_{ib}=9.9 meV/atom. Notably, the Casimir force on graphene sheets decreases rapidly for planes beyond the first one. In particular, for the second graphene layer in the stack, the force is 35 35 times smaller than that experienced by the first layer.

I
Introduction

The Casimir effect
Casimir 1948
, which was originally considered for two perfect slabs, now plays an important role in various phenomena in physics, chemistry, and biology. For instance, books
Bordag et al. 2009a
;
*Milton:2001:CEPMZE
;
*Parsegian:2006:VdWFHBCEP
;
*Milonni:1994:QVItQE
and recent reviews
Woods et al. 2016
have highlighted its significance in these fields. The Casimir force, which can have different values and signs depending on the types of materials used, such as graphene
Bordag et al. 2009b
, topological insulators
Grushin and Cortijo 2011
;
*Lu:2018:cdWtfbatis
, chiral metamaterials
Zhao et al. 2009
, Weyl semimetals
Wilson et al. 2015
;
*RodriguezLopez:2020:socoriciotiaiws
, the shape of material boundaries, and the external conditions including temperature, chemical potential, and magnetic field, has demonstrated its importance in chemistry
Sheehan 2009
, biophysics
Machta et al. 2012
;
*Pawlowski:2013:qcemufobscm
, and in layered systems such as graphite.

Since the 1970s, van der Waals/Casimir energy and force have been studied for multilayered periodic systems, including a periodic stack of dielectric materials
Ninham and Parsegian 1970a
;
*Ninham:1970:vdWFaTF
. The Lifshitz formula has been generalized for layered dielectric systems using nonstandard recursion relations for Fresnel coefficients and the fluctuation-dissipative theorem
Tomaš 2002
;
*Tomas:2012:cealm
. The Casimir force acting on a plane in a stack perpendicular to the
z
-axis was calculated as the difference in the
(z,z)
component of the regularized stress tensor of neighbouring planes. In Ref.
Teo 2010
, a plane in a stack was considered as a piston for a five-plane system. The Casimir force for a five-layered magnetodielectric planar system was discussed in Ref.
Ellingsen and Brevik 2007
as an ideal system for detection of the temperature dependence of the Casimir force, and a path-integral approach was used in Ref.
Kheirandish et al. 2011
;
*Amooghorban:2011:cfmmbgl
to calculate the force for a magnetodielectric layered planar system. Different geometries of multilayered systems were considered in Ref.
Sernelius 2014
in the framework of normal-mode techniques, and a modal approach was developed for layered materials in Ref.
Davids et al. 2010
. Finally, Ref.
Abbas et al. 2017
explored the thermal and electrostatic manipulation of the Casimir force in graphene-dielectric multilayers and demonstrated the possibility of consistent modulation of the Casimir pressure by changing the number of graphene sheets in the stack.

The study presented herein deals with the calculation of Casimir energy for a layered system with a different shape, i.e., a set of cylindrical concentric shells, which has been previously discussed in Ref.
Tatur and Woods 2008
. In Ref.
Khusnutdinov and Kashapov 2015
;
*Khusnutdinov:2015:cefasocp
;
*Khusnutdinov:2016:cpefasocp
;
*Kashapov:2016:tcefpls
;
*Khusnutdinov:2018:tcaciinp2dm
;
*Khusnutdinov:2019:cei2dmss
, the layered system of conductive planes was analysed in details. Two models of conductivity were taken into consideration, namely the constant conductivity and the Drude–Lorentz 7-oscillator model of graphene conductivity, based on the conductivity of graphite
Djurišić and Li 1999
.

It is important to note that, in all of the aforementioned references, the scalar type of materials was considered. Conversely, the conductivity of graphene has a tensorial form
Bordag et al. 2009b
;
Fialkovsky et al. 2011
;
*Bordag:2016:ECefdg
. For two planes with matrix Fresnel reflection coefficients, the formula for the Casimir energy, including some variations and a correct derivation, can be found in Ref.
Bordag 1995
;
*Reynaud:2010:sacf
;
*Ingold:2015:cesa
and Ref.
Fialkovsky et al. 2018
, respectively. In a system consisting of two planes without virtual photon production, this formula can be represented as an integral over imaginary frequency
\xi=-\mathrm{i}\omega
,

E_{2}=\Re\iintop\nolimits\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\xi\ln\det\left[\mathbf{I}-e^{-2dk_{E}}\bm{r}^{\prime}_{1}\bm{r}_{2}\right],

(1)

where
\bm{r}^{\prime}_{1}
and
\bm{r}_{2}
are the Fresnel reflection matrices for the first and second planes,
d
represents the interplane distance, and
k_{E}=\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\bm{k}^{2}}
. The generalization of this expression for more plains is not obvious and requires careful derivation, which is the main focus of this paper.

In Sec.
II
, the scattering problem for a layered system containing
\mathsf{n}
parallel conductive planes with tensorial conductivity is formulated. A general expression of Casimir’s energy is obtained, and it is calculated for
\mathsf{n}=2,3
, and
4
planes with different conductivities and interplane distances.

In Sec.
II
, recurrent relations for the energy are derived, and a relation for energies for
\mathsf{n},\mathsf{n}-1,\mathsf{n}-2
planes is obtained. In the case of a stack of graphenes with equal interplane distance, this recurrent relation can be solved in manifest form. By using eigenvalues of the reflection Fresnel matrix, the matrix form of solutions is represented as a sum of scalar forms over eigenvalues. Analogous to Casimir’s energy, the Casimir force acting on a specific plane in the stack is determined.

Finally, Sec.
IV
is dedicated to a particular case of a stack of graphenes, where the Casimir energy and force are numerically calculated at zero temperature, taking into account the conductivity tensor determined within the framework of 3D QED
Bordag et al. 2009b
.

Throughout the paper, the units where
\hbar=c=1
are used.

II
Scattering problem

Let us consider the scattering problem for the system of
\mathsf{n}
conductive planes, which are perpendicular to the axis
z
in the points
z_{1}<z_{2}<\cdots<z_{\mathsf{n}}
. This system divides all space on
\mathsf{n}+1
domains,
(D_{1}|z_{1}|D_{2}|z_{2}|\ldots,|z_{\mathsf{n}}|D_{\mathsf{n}+1})
. For a plane
j
at position
z=z_{j}
(see Fig.
1
) we have the following scattering problem

\begin{pmatrix}\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{j}\\
\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{j+1}\end{pmatrix}=\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{j}\begin{pmatrix}\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{j}\\
\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{j+1}\end{pmatrix},

(2)

where

\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{r}_{j}&\bm{t}^{\prime}_{j}\\
\bm{t}_{j}&\bm{r}^{\prime}_{j}\end{pmatrix},

(3)

is the scattering
4\times 4
matrix on the plain
j
(
j=1,\ldots,\mathsf{n}
). Here,
\bm{r}_{j}
and
\bm{t}_{j}
are the
2\times 2
reflection and transmission coefficients of plane
j
, correspondingly. The
\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{j}(\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{j})
is 2 component vector
(E_{x},E_{y})^{T}
in the domain
D_{j}
. The movement to the right
\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{j}
(left
\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{j}
), is defined by factor
e^{+\mathrm{i}k_{z}z}(e^{-\mathrm{i}k_{z}z})
,

Figure 1:
The scattering on the plane
j
is shown. The waves moving from the left (right) to the right (left) are defined by factor
e^{+\mathrm{i}k_{z}z}(e^{-\mathrm{i}k_{z}z})
.

To simplify notation, we do not write out the factors with a position of planes. They may be restored by replacements

\bm{r}_{j}\to\bm{r}_{j}e^{2\mathrm{i}k_{z}z_{j}},\ \bm{r}^{\prime}_{j}\to\bm{r}^{\prime}_{j}e^{-2\mathrm{i}k_{z}z_{j}},\ \bm{t}_{j}\to\bm{t}_{j},\ \bm{t}^{\prime}_{j}\to\bm{t}^{\prime}_{j}.

(4)

The scattering matrix of total system,
\bm{\mathcal{S}}
, is defined by relation

\begin{pmatrix}\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{1}\\
\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\end{pmatrix}=\bm{\mathcal{S}}\begin{pmatrix}\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{1}\\
\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{T}^{\prime}\\
\mathbf{T}&\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{1}\\
\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

To obtain matrix
\bm{\mathcal{S}}
, we represent the set of connected scattering equations (
2
) into form
\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{E}=0
, where

\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{1}&\mathbf{C}_{1}&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&\mathbf{A}_{2}&\mathbf{C}_{2}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&\mathbf{A}_{3}&\mathbf{C}_{3}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{n}+1}&\mathbf{C}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf{E}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{E}_{1}\\
\mathbf{E}_{2}\\
\mathbf{E}_{3}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{E}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\end{pmatrix},

(6)

and

\mathbf{A}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{r}_{i}&-\mathbf{I}\\
\bm{t}_{i}&0\end{pmatrix},\mathbf{C}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{t}^{\prime}_{i}\\
-\mathbf{I}&\bm{r}^{\prime}_{i}\end{pmatrix},\mathbf{E}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{i}\\
\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{i}\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

Here,
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix
2\times 2
. To obtain a relation between
\mathbf{E}_{1}
and
\mathbf{E}_{\mathsf{n}+1}
, we make elementary transformations of lines in the matrix
\mathbf{M}
to the form where the last line has the first and last elements, only. To do this, we sequentially will make zeros blocks, starting with the third line.

Let us illustrate these transformations by using an example with three planes. We multiply the second line of
\mathbf{M}
on the left on
-\mathbf{A}_{3}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}
and add it to third line of the matrix:

\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{1}&\mathbf{C}_{1}&0&0\\
0&\mathbf{A}_{2}&\mathbf{C}_{2}&0\\
0&-\mathbf{A}_{3}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{2}&0&\mathbf{C}_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

Next, we multiply on the left the first line on
\mathbf{A}_{3}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{2}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}
and add it to the third line and then multiply the third line on
\mathbf{C}_{3}^{-1}
:

\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{1}&\mathbf{C}_{1}&0&0\\
0&\mathbf{A}_{2}&\mathbf{C}_{2}&0\\
\mathbf{C}_{3}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{3}\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{2}\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{1}&0&0&\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix}.

(9)

The last line corresponds to equations

\mathbf{K}_{3}\begin{pmatrix}\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{1}\\
\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{4}\\
\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{4}\end{pmatrix},

(10)

where

\mathbf{K}_{3}=(\mathbf{C}_{3}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{3})(\mathbf{C}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{2})(\mathbf{C}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{1})=\mathbf{B}_{3}\mathbf{B}_{2}\mathbf{B}_{1},

(11)

and
\mathbf{B}_{i}=\mathbf{C}_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{i}
(the general structure of these matrices may be found in Appendix
A
). For a system with
\mathsf{n}
planes we obtain

(-1)^{\mathsf{n}+1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathsf{n}}\begin{pmatrix}\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{1}\\
\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\stackrel{{{}_{\rightarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\\
\stackrel{{{}_{\leftarrow}}}{{\mathbf{E}}}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\end{pmatrix},

(12)

where

\mathbf{K}_{\mathsf{n}}=\mathbf{B}_{\mathsf{n}}\mathbf{B}_{\mathsf{n}-1}\cdots\mathbf{B}_{1},

(13)

are the matrices
4\times 4
. For
\mathsf{n}=0
, there is no scattering at a whole, and therefore
\mathbf{K}_{0}=\mathbf{I}_{4}
, the identity matrix
4\times 4
. By using simple algebra, we can represent this system in the form (
5
) and obtain the scattering matrix of the total system in terms of the matrix
\mathbf{K}
(we omit index
\mathsf{n}
in
\mathbf{K}_{\mathsf{n}}
for simplicity)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{K}_{22}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{21},\ \mathbf{R}^{\prime}=\mathbf{K}_{12}\mathbf{K}_{22}^{-1},\ \mathbf{T}^{\prime}=(-1)^{\mathsf{n}+1}\mathbf{K}_{22}^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mathsf{n}+1}\left(\mathbf{K}_{11}-\mathbf{K}_{12}\mathbf{K}_{22}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{21}\right).

(14)

For
\mathsf{n}=0
we obtain
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}^{\prime}=0
and
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}^{\prime}=\mathbf{I}
as should be the case. The determinant of this block-matrix reads

\det\mathbf{K}=\det(\mathbf{K}_{22})\det(\mathbf{K}_{11}-\mathbf{K}_{12}\mathbf{K}_{22}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{21})=\frac{\det\mathbf{T}}{\det\mathbf{T}^{\prime}}.

(15)

As noted in Ref.
Fialkovsky et al. 2018
, due to symmetry, the scattering in opposite directions has to be taken into account. All quantities in the opposite direction we will mark by asterisk over
\accentset{*}{g}=g(-k_{z})
. In Ref.
Fialkovsky et al. 2018
, it was shown that

\displaystyle\accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}-\mathbf{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{T}^{\prime})^{-1},\ \accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}}=(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{T}^{\prime}\mathbf{R}^{\prime-1}\mathbf{T})^{-1},

\displaystyle\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{T}-\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\mathbf{T}^{\prime-1}\mathbf{R})^{-1},\ \accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}=(\mathbf{T}^{\prime}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{\prime})^{-1},

(16)

where the right hand side is calculated for
+k_{z}
. Changing
k_{z}\to-k_{z}
we obtain the inverse formulas

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}-\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}\accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime})^{-1},\ \mathbf{R}=(\accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}}-\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}\accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime-1}\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}})^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}-\accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime-1}\accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}})^{-1},\ \mathbf{T}=(\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}-\accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}}\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}\accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime})^{-1}.

(17)

These relations valid for a scattering matrix of each plane, too.

The Casimir energy is defined by the logarithm of the determinant of the scattering matrix, and it may be represented in the following form
Fialkovsky et al. 2018

\det\bm{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{\det\mathbf{R}}{\det\accentset{*}{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}}=\frac{\det\mathbf{T}^{\prime}}{\det\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}}.

(18)

By using relation (
14
) we obtain the following formula (see Appendix
A
)

\det\bm{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{\det\mathbf{T}^{\prime}}{\det\accentset{*}{\mathbf{T}}^{\prime}}=\frac{\det\accentset{*}{\mathbf{K}}_{22}}{\det\mathbf{K}_{22}}.

(19)

The contribution to the Casimir energy,

\ln\det\bm{\mathcal{S}}=\ln\det\accentset{*}{\mathbf{K}}_{22}-\ln\det\mathbf{K}_{22},

(20)

is the difference of scatterings in opposite directions.

Without changing the Casimir energy, we can multiply the matrix
\mathbf{K}_{\mathsf{n}}
on the arbitrary non-degenerate matrix
\mathbf{W}
, which has no dependence on the positions of planes. We use this freedom and define the matrix
\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{n}=\mathbf{K}_{22}\mathbf{W}
in that way that
\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathsf{n}}=\mathbf{I}+\ldots
. Then,

\det\bm{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{\det\accentset{*}{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{\mathsf{n}}}{\det\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathsf{n}}},

(21)

and the Casimir energy reads

E_{\mathsf{n}}=\Re\iintop\nolimits\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\xi\ln\det\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathsf{n}},

(22)

where
\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathsf{n}}
is calculated at the imaginary axis
\omega=\mathrm{i}\xi
.

The special cases with
\mathsf{n}=1,2,3,4
planes are considered in Appendix
B
. They are

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(21)}=\mathbf{I}-\bm{r}_{2}\bm{r}^{\prime}_{1},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{3}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(32)}\bm{t}^{\prime-1}_{2}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(21)}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{2}-\bm{r}_{3}\bm{t}_{2}\bm{r}^{\prime}_{1}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{2},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{4}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(43)}\bm{t}^{\prime-1}_{3}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(32)}\bm{t}^{\prime-1}_{2}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(21)}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{2}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{3}-\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(43)}\bm{t}^{\prime-1}_{3}\bm{r}_{3}\bm{t}_{2}\bm{r}^{\prime}_{1}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{2}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{3}

\displaystyle-\bm{r}_{4}\bm{t}_{3}\bm{r}^{\prime}_{2}\bm{t}^{\prime-1}_{2}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(21)}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{2}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{3}-\bm{r}_{4}\bm{t}_{3}\bm{t}_{2}\bm{r}^{\prime}_{1}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{2}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{3}.

(23)

The positions of planes are restored by replacement (
4
). Here
\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(il)}=\mathbf{I}-\bm{r}_{i}\bm{r}^{\prime}_{l}
is the matrix for two planes
i
and
l
(numeration of planes starts from one for the first plane and up to
\mathsf{n}
for the last one). We observe in the above expressions that the matrices
\bm{t}
appear for internal planes, only. The first terms are additive contributions to the energy; the rest terms describe the non-additivity of the Casimir energy. For example, the first term for
\mathsf{n}=3
planes gives the following contribution

\ln\det(\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(32)}\bm{t}^{\prime-1}_{2}\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(21)}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{2})=\ln\det\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(32)}+\ln\det\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{2}^{(21)},

(24)

which is the sum of the contribution of pairs of neighboring planes – first with the middle and the middle with the last one. The second term in
\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{3}
contains reflection matrices of the first and last planes and transmission matrix of the middle plane. It looks like a contribution due to the interaction of the first and last terms through the middle.

III
Recurrent relations

To obtain a recurrent relation for
\bm{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathsf{n}}
we use Eq. (
13
). The matrix
\mathbf{K}_{\mathsf{n}}
has the following form

\mathbf{K}_{\mathsf{n}+1}=\mathbf{B}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathsf{n}}.

(25)

By using this relation for index
\mathsf{n}
,
\mathsf{n}+1
and the manifest form of matrix
\mathbf{B}_{\mathsf{n}}
(
78
) we obtain the recurrent relation for block
(2,2)
the matrix

where

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{\mathsf{n}}

\displaystyle=-\bm{t}^{\prime-1}_{\mathsf{n}+2}\left[\mathbf{I}-\bm{r}_{\mathsf{n}+2}\left(\bm{r}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{n}+1}-\bm{t}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\bm{r}^{-1}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\bm{t}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\right)\right],

\displaystyle=-\bm{t}^{\prime-1}_{\mathsf{n}+2}\bm{r}_{\mathsf{n}+2}\bm{t}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\bm{r}^{-1}_{\mathsf{n}+1}.

In terms of these matrices, we obtain the relation we need

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}-\bm{r}_{\mathsf{n}+2}\bm{r}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{n}+1})\bm{t}^{\prime-1}_{\mathsf{n}+1}+\bm{r}_{\mathsf{n}+2}\bm{t}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\bm{r}^{-1}_{\mathsf{n}+1},

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}_{\mathsf{n}}

\displaystyle=-\bm{r}_{\mathsf{n}+2}\bm{t}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\bm{r}^{-1}_{\mathsf{n}+1}\bm{t}^{-1}_{\mathsf{n}}.

V
Conclusion

We considered here the Casimir energy for a stack of conductive planes and a force acting on the plane on this stack. To calculate the Casimir force, the scattering theory is used. Starting from the scattering on the single plane, we found the scattering matrix for the set of planes and found recurrent relation (
29
) for the Casimir energy (
22
). In the particular case of a stack of graphene with equal interplane distance, the recurrent relation (
36
) may be solved in the manifest form (
47
). The reflection Fresnel matrix may be diagonalized with eigenvalues corresponding to the TE and TM electromagnetic modes. The resulting expression for the Casimir energy is the sum of contributions of these modes (
63
). The function
D_{\mathsf{n}}
has the same form (
62
) which was found in Ref.
Khusnutdinov et al. 2015
for the constant conductivity case. We have to use, only the corresponding reflection coefficients for graphene (
54
).

For small interplane separations
md\ll 1
, the Casimir energy is divergent as
1/d^{3}
(without mass gap parameter
m
). In the opposite limit of large distances, the energy falls as
1/(m^{2}d^{5})
in accordance to Ref.
Bordag et al. 2009b
. The binding energy of infinite stack graphene with interplane separation of graphite,
d_{c}
, is
E_{ib}=9.9
meV/atom, which is by
10
% smaller than for constant conductivity case considered in Ref.
Khusnutdinov et al. 2015
. The obtained value of binding energy depends only weakly on the mass gap parameter because the case of graphite interplane separation corresponds to the case
md_{c}\ll 1
(see the insert in Fig.
3
).

The Casimir force acting on the graphene
s
in the stack of
\mathsf{n}
graphene is a sum of two contributions (
73
), where function
G_{s,\mathsf{n}}
has the same form (
74
) as for constant conductivity case found in Ref.
Khusnutdinov et al. 2015
with corresponding reflections coefficients for TE and TM modes. The force acting on graphene falls very quickly – for the second plane it is
35
times smaller than for the first graphene (see Fig.
5
). The extremes graphenes squeezes the stack. The force acting on graphene in a stack is quick,
\mathsf{n}\sim 10
(see Fig.
6
), becomes the same as for an infinite stack.

The influence of non-zero temperature and chemical potential can be taken into account by employing the corresponding conductivity obtained in Ref.
Fialkovsky et al. 2011
;
*Bordag:2016:ECefdg
. We will analyze this influence in forthcoming papers.

Some properties:

\mathbf{B}_{i}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-\bm{t}^{-1}_{i}&\bm{t}^{-1}_{i}\bm{r}^{\prime}_{i}\\
