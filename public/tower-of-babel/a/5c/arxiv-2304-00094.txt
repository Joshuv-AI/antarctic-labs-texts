Optimal density compensation factors for the reconstruction of the Fourier transform of bandlimited functions
Melanie Kircheis, Daniel Potts

Abstract
An inverse nonequispaced fast Fourier transform (iNFFT) is a fast algorithm to compute the Fourier coefficients of a trigonometric polynomial from nonequispaced sampling data. However, various applications such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are concerned with the analogous problem for bandlimited functions, i. e., the reconstruction of point evaluations of the Fourier transform from given measurements of the bandlimited function. In this paper, we review an approach yielding exact reconstruction for trigonometric polynomials up to a certain degree, and extend this technique to the setting of bandlimited functions. Here we especially focus on methods computing a diagonal matrix of weights needed for sampling density compensation. Key words : inverse nonequispaced fast Fourier transform, nonuniform fast Fourier transform, direct inversion, density compensation, bandlimited functions, iNFFT, NFFT, NUFFT AMS Subject Classifications : 65Txx, 65T50, 65F05.



1
Introduction

The nonequispaced fast Fourier transform (NFFT) is a fast algorithm to evaluate a trigonometric polynomial

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}}\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{x}}

(1.1)

with given Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
,
at nonequispaced points
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
, with
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
where for
\boldsymbol{M}\coloneqq(M,\dots,M)^{T}
,
M\in 2\mathbb{N}
, we define the multi-index set

\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\left[-\tfrac{M}{2},\tfrac{M}{2}\right)^{d}

with cardinality
|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|=M^{d}
.
For more information see
[
8
, pp. 377-381]
and references therein.
In case of equispaced points
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
and
|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|=N
, this evaluation can be realized by means of the well-known fast Fourier transform (FFT); an algorithm that is invertible.
However, various applications need to perform an inverse nonequispaced fast
Fourier transform (iNFFT), i. e., compute the Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
from given function evaluations
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
of the trigonometric polynomial (
1.1
).
Hence, we are interested in an inversion also for nonequispaced data.
In contrast to iterated procedures, where multiple applications of the NFFT are needed to compute a solution, we consider so-called direct methods,
where for a fixed set of points
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
, the reconstruction can be realized with the same number of arithmetic operations as a single application of an adjoint NFFT.
To achieve this, a certain precomputational step is compulsory, since the adjoint NFFT does not yield an inversion of the NFFT by itself, see
[
4
, Sec. 3]
.
Although this precomputations might be rather costly, they need to be done only once for a given set of points

\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
,
while the actual reconstruction step is very efficient.
Thus, direct methods are especially beneficial in case of multiple applications with fixed points.

For the setting of trigonometric polynomials several approaches are known,
while in this paper we focus on the analogous problem for bandlimited functions with

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})=\int\limits_{\left[-\frac{M}{2},\frac{M}{2}\right)^{d}}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1.2)

In various applications such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), cf.
[
1
]
, the aim is the reconstruction of point evaluations
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})\in\mathbb{C}
,
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
, of an object
\hat{f}
from given measurements
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
,
j=1,\dots,N
, of the form (
1.2
).
To this end, known approaches for the direct inversion of the NFFT shall be extended to this setting.
In particular, we investigate methods computing a diagonal matrix of weights
w_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
,
j=1,\dots,N
,
which are needed for sampling density compensation due to the nonequispaced sampling
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
.

Therefore, this paper is organized as follows.
Firstly, in Section
2
we review the approach in
[
4
, Sec. 3.1]
, that leads to an exact reconstruction for all trigonometric polynomials (
1.1
) of degree
\boldsymbol{M}
.
Subsequently, in Section
3
we aim for an analogous approach for bandlimited functions
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as well.
Finally, in Section
4
we show some numerical examples to investigate the accuracy of our approaches.

2
Trigonometric polynomials

Let
\mathbb{T}^{d}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathbb{Z}^{d}
denote the
d
-dimensional torus with
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
The inner product of two vectors shall be defined as usual as

\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{x}\coloneqq k_{1}x_{1}+\dots+k_{d}x_{d}
.
For the reconstruction of the Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
,
from function evaluations
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
,
j=1,\dots,N
,
of trigonometric polynomials
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
of degree
\boldsymbol{M}
, see (
1.1
),
the following result is known, cf.
[
4
, Cor. 3.4]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|\leq N
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
j=1,\dots,N
, be given.
Then the density compensation factors
w_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}=\delta_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{k}},\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}},

(2.1)

with the Kronecker delta
\delta_{\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{k}}
, are optimal,
since for all trigonometric polynomials of degree
\boldsymbol{M}
, see (
1.1
),
an exact reconstruction of the Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=h_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\mathrm{w}}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\,f(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}},\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}.

(2.2)

Proof.

It is known that
\{\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{x}}\colon\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
forms an orthonormal basis
of the Hilbert space
L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
of all
1
-periodic, complex-valued functions, see
[
8
, p. 161]
.
To achieve an exact reconstruction for all trigonometric polynomials (
1.1
)
with maximum degree
\boldsymbol{M}
it suffices to consider the set of basis functions with
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
.
For each of these basis functions with fixed
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
we have

\displaystyle h_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\mathrm{w}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell}-\boldsymbol{k})\boldsymbol{x}_{j}},\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}},

and

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=c_{\boldsymbol{k}}(f)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell}-\boldsymbol{k})\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\delta_{\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{k}},\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}},

where
\delta_{\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{k}}
denotes the Kronecker delta.
Thus, to obtain (
2.2
) we need to assure that the weights satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(\boldsymbol{\ell}-\boldsymbol{k})\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}=\delta_{\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{k}},\quad\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}.

(2.3)

Since for
\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
we have
(\boldsymbol{\ell}-\boldsymbol{k})\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}
, the property (
2.3
) is fulfilled by (
2.1
).
In other words, by defining the nonequispaced Fourier matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|}\coloneqq\left(\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}\right)_{j=1,\,\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{N}\ \in\mathbb{C}^{N\times|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|}

(2.4)

as well as
\boldsymbol{w}\coloneqq(w_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
, an exact solution to the linear system of equations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|}^{T}\,\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{0}}\coloneqq\left(\delta_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{k}}\right)_{\boldsymbol{k}\in{\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}}}

(2.5)

yields an exact reconstruction for all trigonometric polynomials (
1.1
) of degree
\boldsymbol{M}
.
As already mentioned in
[
3
, Sec. 3.1]
an exact solution to (
2.5
) can only be found
if (
2.5
) is an underdetermined system with
|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|\leq N
.
∎

Thus, in case
|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|\leq N
and the condition number of
\boldsymbol{A}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|}
is sufficiently small, the optimal density compensation factors
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
can be found as solution to
the linear system of equations (
2.5
).
As mentioned in
[
4
, Sec. 3.1.1]
the unique solution is then given by the normal equations of second kind

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|}^{T}\overline{\boldsymbol{A}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|}}\,\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{0}},\quad\overline{\boldsymbol{A}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|}}\,\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{w},

(2.6)

and can efficiently be computed by an iteration procedure combining the CG algorithm and the NFFT.

However, when
|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|>N
there is no theoretical guaranty.
In addition, the numerics in
[
4
]
have shown that the least squares solution satisfying the normal equations of first kind

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{A}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|}}\,\boldsymbol{A}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|}^{T}\,\boldsymbol{w}=\overline{\boldsymbol{A}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{2M}}|}}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{0}},

(2.7)

is not a good approximation.
This is why, we recommend another computation scheme in this setting.
To this end, note that (
2.5
) implies by (
2.3
) that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{I}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|}

(2.8)

with the weight matrix
\boldsymbol{W}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(w_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
.
Hence, in case a solution to (
2.5
) cannot be found using (
2.6
),
one may try to find suitable weights utilizing (
2.8
) by minimizing the Frobenius norm

\big\|\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{I}_{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|}\big\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
.
As shown in
[
4
, Sec. 3.4.2]
this minimizer can be obtained by solving
\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{b}
,
where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Big(\left|\left[\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\right]_{j,s}\right|^{2}\Big)_{j,s=1}^{N}\quad\text{ and }\quad\boldsymbol{b}=|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{N}.

(2.9)

Note that by means of the definition (
2.4
) as well as

\boldsymbol{f}\coloneqq\left(f(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\right)_{j=1}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{f}}\coloneqq(\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}}
,
the reconstruction (
2.2
) can be denoted as
\boldsymbol{\hat{f}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{f}
.
For further details see
[
4
]
.

3
Bandlimited functions

Since various applications are concerned with bandlimited functions instead of trigonometric polynomials,
we now aim to extend the method from Section
2
to bandlimited functions
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with maximum bandwidth
\boldsymbol{M}
, i. e., functions whose (continuous) Fourier transform

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},\quad\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(3.1)

is supported on
\left[-\frac{M}{2},\frac{M}{2}\right)^{d}
.
Thus, their inverse Fourier transform

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

can be written as in (
1.2
).

3.1
Reconsideration as trigonometric polynomials

To find a suitable reconstruction technique for a bandlimited function
f
, we consider its 1-periodized version

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{r}).

Note that
\tilde{f}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is uniquely representable in form of its absolute convergent Fourier series

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\tilde{f})\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{x}},

(3.2)

see
[
8
, Thm. 4.5]
, where the Fourier coefficients are given by

\displaystyle c_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\tilde{f})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}),\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(3.3)

cf. (
3.1
).
Moreover, it is known that
f
is a bandlimited function with bandwidth
\boldsymbol{M}
, i. e., we have
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})=0
,
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
.
Therefore, the periodic function (
3.2
) in fact is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
\boldsymbol{M}
as in (
1.1
), which makes it reasonable to utilize the result from Section
2
to reconstruct the Fourier coefficients (
3.3
).
Namely, an exact solution to the linear system (
2.5
) yields an exact reconstruction

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})=c_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\tilde{f})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\,\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}},\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}.

(3.4)

Note that using the nonequispaced Fourier matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|}
in (
2.4
), the weight matrix
\boldsymbol{W}=\mathrm{diag}(w_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
as well as the vectors

\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}\coloneqq(\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}))_{j=1}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{f}}\coloneqq(\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}))_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}}
,
the reconstruction (
3.4
) can be denoted as
\boldsymbol{\hat{f}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}
.
In addition, by (
3.2
) we have
\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\hat{f}}
, such that (
2.8
) is fulfilled.

However, in practical applications, such as MRI, this is only a hypothetical case, since the periodization
\tilde{f}
cannot be sampled.
Due to a limited coverage of space by the acquisition, the function
f
is typically only on a bounded domain, w.l.o.g. for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\left[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{d}
, cf.
[
1
]
.
Thus, we need to assume that
f
is small outside the interval
\left[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{d}
, such that
\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\approx f(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
,
and we have to deal with the approximation

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})\approx\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\,f(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}},\quad\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}.

(3.5)

This is to say, by (
3.4
) the error in the approximation (
3.5
) solely occurs because
f
is not known on whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Note that the reconstruction (
3.5
) can also be denoted as
\boldsymbol{\hat{f}}\approx\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{f}
with
\boldsymbol{W}=\mathrm{diag}(w_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
from (
2.5
).

Remark 3.1
.

As known by
[
6
, Ex. 1.22]
, the periodization of the function

g(\boldsymbol{t})=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{x}}
,
\boldsymbol{t}\in\left[-\frac{M}{2},\frac{M}{2}\right)^{d}
,
with
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
fixed, possesses the absolutely and uniformly convergent Fourier series

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{x}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\,\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\big),\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{d},

(3.6)

with the
d
-variate
\mathrm{sinc}
function
\mathrm{sinc}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\prod_{t=1}^{d}\mathrm{sinc}(x_{t})

with

\displaystyle\mathrm{sinc}(x)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\sin x}{x}&\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\,,\\[4.30554pt]
1&\quad x=0\,,\end{array}\right.

and equispaced points

\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\odot{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=\left(M^{-1}\ell_{1},\dots,M^{-1}\ell_{d}\right)^{T}
,

\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Since the Fourier coefficients
\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\big)
in (
3.6
) are
\ell_{2}
-summable, we may introduce the
\mathrm{sinc}
operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\bigg(\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\big)\bigg)_{j=1,\,\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}^{N}.

(3.7)

By additionally defining the one-sided infinite Fourier matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\left(\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\right)_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\,\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}},

the matrix product
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\mathcal{F}=\Bigg(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\,\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\big)\Bigg)_{j=1,\,\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{N}=\boldsymbol{A}

(3.8)

with the nonequispaced Fourier matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|}
in (
2.4
),
since the components of (
3.8
) coincide with point evaluations of (
3.6
)
at
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
, and
\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
.

3.2
Connection to previous work

Analogous to
[
4
, Thm. 3.8]
we aim to extend the approximation (
3.4
) onto the whole interval, i. e., we consider

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{v})\approx\tilde{h}(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\,\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}},\quad\boldsymbol{v}\in\left[-\tfrac{M}{2},\tfrac{M}{2}\right)^{d}.

Inserting this into the inverse Fourier transform (
1.2
) yields

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\left[-\frac{M}{2},\frac{M}{2}\right)^{d}}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}\approx\int\limits_{\left[-\frac{M}{2},\frac{M}{2}\right)^{d}}\tilde{h}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\,\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\int\limits_{\left[-\frac{M}{2},\frac{M}{2}\right)^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\,\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\cdot|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\,\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x})\big),\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Especially, by evaluation at
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{s}
,
s=1,\dots,N
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x}_{s})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}\,\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\cdot|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\,\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{s})\big).

Therefore, one could also aim to choose suitable weights
w_{j}
based on this equation.
More precisely, by defining the nonequispaced
\mathrm{sinc}
matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}\coloneqq\bigg(\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{s})\big)\bigg)_{j,s=1}^{N}\ \in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},

(3.9)

as well as

\boldsymbol{f}=\left(f(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\right)_{j=1}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}=(\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}))_{j=1}^{N}
,
one may try to find a weight matrix
\boldsymbol{W}=\mathrm{diag}(w_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
such that the approximation

\boldsymbol{f}\approx|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}

is best as possible.
Note that still
\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}
is unknown, such that we have to deal with the overall approximation

\boldsymbol{f}\approx|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{f}
,
i. e., one would ideally aim for

\boldsymbol{I}_{N}=|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}\boldsymbol{W}
.
We remark that on the main diagonal
|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}\boldsymbol{W}=\boldsymbol{I}_{N}
reads as

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|}=w_{j}\,\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\big)=w_{j}\,\mathrm{sinc}(0)=w_{j},\quad j=1,\dots,N.

However, for all other entries with
j\neq s
we would need

\mathrm{sinc}(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{s}))=0
, which is only true for
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
equispaced.
In other words, for arbitrary points
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
equality in

|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}\boldsymbol{W}=\boldsymbol{I}_{N}

cannot be fulfilled for any weights.

Hence, one can only look for an approximate solution, e. g. by considering the least squares problem

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{W}=\mathrm{diag}(w_{j})_{j=1}^{N}}{\text{Minimize }}\ \||\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}\boldsymbol{W}-\boldsymbol{I}_{N}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

When denoting the
j
-th column of
\boldsymbol{W}
,
\boldsymbol{I}_{N}
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}

as
\boldsymbol{w}_{j}
,
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
and
[\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}]_{j}
, respectively,
we may rewrite the Frobenius norm by only considering the nonzero entries via

\displaystyle\||\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}\boldsymbol{W}-\boldsymbol{I}_{N}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\||\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\cdot\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\||\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\cdot[\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}]_{j}\,{w_{j}}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus, as stated in
[
2
]
(without proof) the least squares solution to the minimization problem is given by

\displaystyle{w_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|}\,[\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}]_{j}^{\dagger}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{j}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|}\left([\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}]_{j}^{*}\,[\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}]_{j}\right)^{-1}[\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}]_{j}^{*}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{j}

Remark 3.2
.

Note that in
[
2
]
it was claimed that this approach coincides with the one in
[
7
]
only considering finite sections of (
3.7
) with
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
.
However, we remark that this claim only holds asymptotically for
|\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}|\to\infty
.

By the classical sampling theorem of Shannon-Whittaker-Kotelnikov, see
[
10
,
9
,
5
]
,
any bandlimited function
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with maximum bandwidth
\boldsymbol{M}
can be recovered from its uniform samples
f(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})
,
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and we have

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\,\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\big),\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3.11)

Now we apply the sampling theorem of Shannon-Whittaker-Kotelnikov to the shifted
\mathrm{sinc}
function

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathrm{sinc}(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}))\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
j
fixed.
By evaluation at
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{s}
,
s=1,\dots,N
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{sinc}\big(

\displaystyle M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{s})\big)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\big)\,\mathrm{sinc}\big(M\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{s}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\big).

(3.12)

Using the
\mathrm{sinc}
operator
\mathcal{C}
in (
3.7
) and the nonequispaced
\mathrm{sinc}
matrix in (
3.9
), this can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}=\mathcal{C}\mathcal{C}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}.

(3.13)

Hence, a restriction to finitely many
\boldsymbol{\ell}
in (
3.12
) corresponds to uniform truncation of a Shannon series (
3.11
), which is known as a poor approximation due to the slow convergence of the
\mathrm{sinc}
function.
Thus, equality in (
3.13
) is only satisfied for the operator
\mathcal{C}
in (
3.7
), while considering finite sections of (
3.7
) with
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{M}}
implies a poor approximation of
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathrm{n}}
.
