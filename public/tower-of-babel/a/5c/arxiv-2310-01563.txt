Local algorithms and the failure of log-depth quantum advantage on sparse random CSPs
Antares Chen, Neng Huang, Kunal Marwaha

Abstract
We construct and analyze a message-passing algorithm for random constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) at large clause density, generalizing work of El Alaoui, Montanari, and Sellke for Maximum Cut [ AMS21 ] through a connection between random CSPs and mean-field Ising spin glasses [ AMS20 , JMSS22 ] . For CSPs with even predicates, the algorithm asymptotically solves a stochastic optimal control problem dual to an extended Parisi variational principle. This gives an optimal fraction of satisfied constraints among algorithms obstructed by the branching overlap gap property of Huang and Sellke [ HS22 ] , notably including the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm and all quantum circuits on a bounded-degree architecture of up to ϵ ⋅ log ⁡ n \epsilon\cdot\log n depth [ CLSS22 ] .



1
Introduction

Today’s quantum computers are starting to show signs of advantage over classical machines.
Many people are seeking to use this technology in a practical application as soon as possible.
One such area is combinatorial optimization: even though the problems are
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard, optimization has incredible value to the contemporary world.
This industry is also eager to experiment with quantum computers, perhaps because rigorous worst-case analysis is not always relevant.

The most popular quantum protocol for classical optimization is the
Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm
(QAOA), proposed in
[
FGG14
]
.
Given an optimization problem, the protocol associates a qubit to each variable, and evolves a quantum state to a near-ground state of the problem, depending on choice of hyperparameters.
The QAOA has also sparked the interest of theorists, characterizing the protocol’s performance on canonical problems, and debating whether it is any more powerful than existing classical algorithms (e.g.
[
BMO+15
,
Has19
,
BFM+21
]
).

One key property of the QAOA is its
locality
: at each layer of the algorithm, a qubit interacts only with qubits associated with adjacent variables in the optimization problem. Locality is known to limit the QAOA up to logarithmic circuit depth when the optimization problem is
sparse

[
FGG20a
,
FGG20b
]
.
This limitation is due to a phenomenon known as the
overlap gap property
(OGP), a type of clustering behavior of near-optimal solutions
[
GS14
]
.
Notably, this is an
average-case
property of many random optimization problems. Recent formulations of the OGP have led to quantitative obstructions for broad families of algorithms, both quantum and classical
[
CGPR19
,
CLSS22
,
HS22
]
.

In this work, we show that
with high probability
over a large family of sparse optimization problems, there is an
optimal
algorithm among those obstructed by the OGP. Furthermore, this algorithm is
classical
, so there can be no quantum advantage by the QAOA up to logarithmic depth.
In fact, this no-go result extends to logarithmic-depth quantum circuits on fixed, bounded-degree architectures, such as the
brickwork
circuits
[
BFK09
]
used in random circuit sampling
[
BFNV18
]
.
This is the strongest
average-case
result known to the authors regarding quantum advantage in classical optimization.

Our algorithm is related to the theory of
message-passing
used in random inference problems
[
BM11
]
, and inspired by rich connections to statistical physics models such as the mean-field spin glass
[
SK75
]
.
The parameters of the algorithm are chosen by solving a stochastic control problem dual to a problem determining the quantitative barrier of the OGP, as pioneered in
[
AMS20
,
HS22
]
.
In particular, our algorithm extends work of
[
AMS21
]
, and uses a sparse-to-dense connection observed in e.g.
[
DMS17
,
JMSS22
]
. The performance of the algorithm, like that of
[
Mon19
,
AMS20
,
AMS21
]
, is dependent on a technical condition (
1
) which we explain in the text.

Related work

Approximate Message Passing

Approximate Message Passing (AMP) is an algorithmic framework used to design locally computable, iterative methods for a variety of high-dimensional statistical inference and recovery tasks
[
LKZ17
,
RGV17
,
MV21
,
MLLK22
,
BJF23
]
.
Many of the ideas motivating AMP originate from the study of
belief propagation
, an iterative method rediscovered multiple times in different scientific contexts; see
[
MM09
,
KF09
]
for a broader history.
AMP was first introduced in the context of compressed sensing and signal recovery
[
DMM09
,
Kab03
]
, partly inspired by the use of structured priors in decoding low-density parity check codes
[
RU08
,
MEK12
]
.
In spin glass theory, AMP is synonymous with computing the fixed points of a certain Thouless–Anderson–Palmer (TAP) free energy functional
[

,
KMS+12
,
ZK16
,
CP18
,
CPS23
]
.
In fact, our algorithm is inspired by similar connections between the TAP equations and belief propagation
[
MPV87
,
MM09
]
.

A beneficial feature of AMP is that one can rigorously analyze the asymptotic behavior of statistics in the framework. This is typically done through the use of
state evolution
statements
[
Bol14
,
BM11
]
: laws of large numbers which dictate the behavior of such statistics when the dimension in the problem approaches infinity.
This has been subsequently used to compute approximate ground states of both Ising and spherical mean-field spin glasses
[

, as well as Max-Cut on random regular graphs of large degree
[

]

Recent demonstrations of quantum advantage (e.g.

ZWD+20

Earlier suggestions of quantum advantage with the QAOA

Recently,

B.2

Here, we show that the conditional distribution

by linearity. By

and

,

are independent for different

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{b\in\partial i\setminus a}\widetilde{R}_{i,b,\ell}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{(d-1)^{2}}\sum_{b\in\partial i\setminus a}\E\left[\widetilde{R}_{i,b,\ell}^{2}\right]=O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)\,,

where the last equality follows by
Lemma

B.1
.

Now we inspect

, we may express it with any variable

\displaystyle\nu_{\ell+1}=\frac{\xi^{\prime}\left((\ell+1)\delta\right)-\xi^{\prime}(\ell\delta)}{r}=\frac{1}{d}\cdot\sum_{b\in\partial i}\sum_{S\subseteq\partial b,i\in S}\hat{f}(S)^{2}\cdot\left(\left((\ell\delta+\delta\right)^{|S|-1}-\left(\ell\delta\right)^{|S|-1}\right)\,.

Similarly, we may simplify

as

\displaystyle\phi_{i}^{\ell}(S,S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\varnothing\subsetneq\widetilde{T}\subseteq S\setminus i}\left(\prod_{j\in(S\setminus i)\setminus\widetilde{T}}\left(z_{j\to b}^{\ell-1}\right)^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{j\in\widetilde{T}}\left(A_{j\to b}^{\ell-1}\cdot\tau_{j\to b}^{\ell-1}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\left(\phi_{i}^{\ell}(S,S)-\rho_{i}^{\ell}(S,S)\right)^{2}

where the inequality follows by Cauchy-Schwarz. By

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\phi_{i}^{\ell}(S,S)-\rho_{i}^{\ell}(S,S)\right)^{2}\right]=O_{d}\left(\sum_{\varnothing\subsetneq\widetilde{T}\subseteq S\setminus i}\E\left[\left(\prod_{j\in\widetilde{T}}\left(\tau_{j\to b}^{\ell-1}\right)^{2}-\prod_{j\in\widetilde{T}}\nu_{\ell}\right)^{2}\right]\right)\,.

Each term in the sum above is

by the inductive hypothesis and

B.8
, so the total value is

.

Now we compare

with
\left(\left((\ell\delta+\delta\right)^{|S|-1}-\left(\ell\delta\right)^{|S|-1}\right)

around factor

\displaystyle\E[\rho_{i}^{\ell}(S,S)]

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in S\setminus i}\left(\E\left[\left(z_{j\to b}^{\ell-1}\right)^{2}\right]+\E\left[\left(A_{j\to b}^{\ell-1}\right)^{2}\cdot\nu_{\ell}\right]\right)-\prod_{j\in S\setminus i}\E\left[\left(z_{j\to b}^{\ell-1}\right)^{2}\right]

the last equality is by

We now put these facts together. First, since

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\left(\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{b\in\partial i\setminus a}R_{i,b,\ell}\right)-\nu_{\ell+1}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{b\in\partial i\setminus a}\sum_{S\subseteq\partial a,i\in S}\hat{f}(S)^{2}\cdot\left(\phi_{i}^{\ell}(S,S)-\left((\ell\delta+\delta\right)^{|S|-1}+\left(\ell\delta\right)^{|S|-1}\right)\right)^{2}\right]+O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)

\displaystyle 2\E\left[\left(\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{b\in\partial i\setminus a}\sum_{S\subseteq\partial b,i\in S}\hat{f}(S)^{2}\cdot\left(\rho_{i}^{\ell}(S,S)-\left((\ell\delta+\delta\right)^{|S|-1}+\left(\ell\delta\right)^{|S|-1}\right)\right)^{2}\right]+O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)\,.

\displaystyle O_{d}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1}\subseteq\partial b_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\partial b_{2}\\
i\in S_{1},S_{2}\end{subarray}}\left(\E\left[\rho_{i}^{\ell}(S_{1},S_{1})\right]-\left((\ell\delta+\delta\right)^{|S|-1}+\left(\ell\delta\right)^{|S|-1}\right)\left(\E\left[\rho_{i}^{\ell}(S_{2},S_{2})\right]-\left((\ell\delta+\delta\right)^{|S|-1}+\left(\ell\delta\right)^{|S|-1}\right)\right)\,,

where the last equality follows the inductive hypothesis,

3.5
, and

B.7

The remaining expectation is

B.10

Choose any variable

, factor

, and

. We have

\plim_{d\to\infty}\,\E\Big[\big(u_{i\to a}^{\ell}\big)^{2}\;\Big|\;{\cal G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\Big]=\nu_{\ell}\,.

Follows from

We now compute the higher-order moments of
u_{i\to a}^{\ell}\;\big|\;{\cal G}_{i}^{\ell-1}
; they approach that of a Gaussian as

.

B.11
.

Assume
2
.
Choose any variable
i
, and
0\leq\ell\leq L-1
. Then for any
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\plim_{d\to\infty}\,\E\left[\left(u_{i\to a}^{\ell+1}\right)^{2p}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]=(2p-1)!!\cdot\left(\nu_{\ell+1}\right)^{p}\,,

\displaystyle\plim_{d\to\infty}\,\E\left[\left(u_{i\to a}^{\ell+1}\right)^{2p+1}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]=0\,.

Proof.

By definition, we have that

\displaystyle u^{\ell+1}_{i\to a}=w_{i\to a}^{\ell+1}-\mathbbm{1}_{[\ell\geq 1]}w_{i\to a}^{\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{b\in\partial i\setminus a}\left(\mathsf{D}_{i;b}f\big(\bm{z}_{\partial b\rightarrow b}^{\ell}\big)-\mathbbm{1}_{[\ell\geq 1]}\cdot\mathsf{D}_{i;b}f\big(\bm{z}_{\partial b\rightarrow b}^{\ell-1}\big)\right)\,.

So then for any
s\geq 2
, we may write the
s^{\text{th}}
moment as

\displaystyle\E\left[\left(u_{i\to a}^{\ell+1}\right)^{s}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(d-1)^{s/2}}\cdot\E\left[\bigg(\sum_{b\in\partial i\setminus a}\left(\mathsf{D}_{i;b}f\left(\bm{z}_{\partial b\to b}^{\ell}\right)-\mathbbm{1}_{[\ell\geq 1]}\cdot\mathsf{D}_{i;b}f\left(\bm{z}_{\partial b\to b}^{\ell-1}\right)\right)\bigg)^{s}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(d-1)^{s/2}}\cdot\sum_{b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\in\partial i\setminus a}\E\left[\prod_{\iota=1}^{s}\left(\mathsf{D}_{i;b_{\iota}}f\left(\bm{z}_{\partial b_{\iota}\to b_{\iota}}^{\ell}\right)-\mathbbm{1}_{[\ell\geq 1]}\cdot\mathsf{D}_{i;b_{\iota}}f\left(\bm{z}_{\partial b_{\iota}\to b_{\iota}}^{\ell-1}\right)\right)\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]\,.

Note that each term has mean zero by
Corollary

3.6
, and is independent for different
b\in\partial i\setminus a
by
Remark

2.5
and
Lemma

3.1
.
So, if any factor
b
appears exactly
once
in
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{s}\}
, then the expectation of the corresponding product is zero. Let
c
be the number of distinct factors in
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{s}\}
; then surviving terms must satisfy
c\leq\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\rfloor
.

How large are the terms with
c
distinct factors? The distinct factors can be chosen from
\partial i\setminus a
in
O_{d}(d^{c})
ways, and the degeneracy from permuting the set of factors is at most
s!
.
By
Lemma

B.1
, each term in the sum is bounded, so the terms with
c
distinct factors altogether contribute
\frac{1}{(d-1)^{s/2}}\cdot s!\cdot O_{d}(d^{c})=O_{d}(d^{c-s/2})
to the sum. Note that when
c<s/2
, this value is
O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
, so the only contribution larger than this is when
c=\frac{s}{2}
.

•

Suppose
s
is
odd
. Then
c
never equals
\frac{s}{2}
, since
c\leq\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\rfloor=\frac{s-1}{2}
. So the
s^{\text{th}}
moment has order
O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)
, and thereby
\plim_{d\to\infty}\,\E\left[\left(u_{i\to a}^{\ell+1}\right)^{s}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]=0
.

•

Suppose
s
is
even
. When
c=\frac{s}{2}
, each distinct factor appears exactly twice in
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\}
. As a result, we may write the
s^{\text{th}}
moment
\E\left[\left(u_{i\to a}^{\ell+1}\right)^{s}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]
as

\displaystyle\frac{(s-1)!!}{(d-1)^{s/2}}\cdot\sum_{b_{1},\dots,b_{s/2}\in\partial i\setminus a}\prod_{\iota=1}^{s/2}\E\left[\left(\mathsf{D}_{i;b_{\iota}}f\left(\bm{z}_{\partial b_{\iota}\to b_{\iota}}^{\ell}\right)-\mathbbm{1}_{[\ell\geq 1]}\cdot\mathsf{D}_{i;b_{\iota}}f\left(\bm{z}_{\partial b_{\iota}\to b_{\iota}}^{\ell-1}\right)\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]+O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{(s-1)!!}{(d-1)^{s/2}}\cdot\left(\sum_{b\in\partial i\setminus a}\E\left[\left(\mathsf{D}_{i;b}f\left(\bm{z}_{\partial b\to b}^{\ell}\right)-\mathbbm{1}_{[\ell\geq 1]}\cdot\mathsf{D}_{i;b}f\left(\bm{z}_{\partial b\to b}^{\ell-1}\right)\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]\right)^{s/2}+O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{(s-1)!!}{(d-1)^{s/2}}\cdot\left(\E\left[\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}\in\partial i\setminus a}\prod_{\iota\in\{1,2\}}\left(\mathsf{D}_{i;b_{\iota}}f\left(\bm{z}_{\partial b_{\iota}\to b_{\iota}}^{\ell}\right)-\mathbbm{1}_{[\ell\geq 1]}\cdot\mathsf{D}_{i;b_{\iota}}f\left(\bm{z}_{\partial b_{\iota}\to b_{\iota}}^{\ell-1}\right)\right)\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]\right)^{s/2}+O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle(s-1)!!\cdot\left(\left(\tau_{i\to a}^{\ell+1}\right)^{2}\right)^{s/2}+O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\,,

where the second equality follows because the terms where
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
have zero mean (i.e. see proof of
Lemma

B.7
).
By
Lemma

B.9
(and
2
), we have
\plim_{d\to\infty}\,\E\left[\left(u_{i\to a}^{\ell+1}\right)^{s}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell}\right]=(s-1)!!\cdot\left(\nu_{\ell+1}\right)^{s/2}
.∎

B.2.3
Wasserstein distance to Gaussian goes to zero

We now show that the Wasserstein distance of
u^{\ell}_{i\to a}\;\big|\;\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}
and
{\cal N}\big(0,\nu_{\ell}\big)
converges in probability to zero in the large degree limit.
We describe the intuition here. Let
X_{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}u^{\ell}_{i\to a}\;\big|\;\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}
.
Because the moments converge in probability
to that of a Gaussian random variable with distribution
{\cal N}\big(0,(\tau^{\ell}_{i\to a})^{2}\big)
,
and the moments of a Gaussian determine its distribution (e.g.,
[
Bil95
, Example 30.1]
),
X_{d}
must converge in probability in distribution to some
X\sim{\cal N}\big(0,(\tau^{\ell}_{i\to a})^{2}\big)
.
Furthermore, for each even
p>0
, because
X_{d}\to X
and the
p^{\text{th}}
moment converges in probability, the Wasserstein distance of
X_{d}
and
X
converges in probability to zero.

Let us formalize this reasoning. Denote
{\cal P}(\mathbb{R})
by the set of all real probability measures. We recall the definition of a sequence of measures converging in Wasserstein distance:

B.12
Convergence in
p
-Wasserstein distance.
p_{p_{p_{p}}}

Given
p>0
, let
\mu
be a measure in
{\cal P}(\mathbb{R})
, and
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
a sequence of measures in
{\cal P}(\mathbb{R})
. We say that
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges to
\mu
in
p
-Wasserstein distance if

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert\mu_{n}-\mu\rVert_{W_{p}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\Big(\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu_{n},\mu)}\,\E_{x,y\sim\gamma}\,\left[\lvert x-y\rvert^{p}\right]\Big)^{1/p}=0\,,

(B.2)

where
\Gamma(\mu_{n},\mu)
is the set of all real measures with marginals
\mu_{n}
and
\mu
.

If a distribution is uniquely determined by its moments, convergence of moments implies convergence in distribution:

B.13
e.g.,
[
Bil95
, Theorem 30.2]
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure in
{\cal P}(\mathbb{R})
uniquely determined by its moments, and let
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of probability measures in
{\cal P}(\mathbb{R})
. If

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{p}\,d\mu_{n}(t)\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{p}\,d\mu(t)\,,

then
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges weakly to
\mu
.

From here, convergence in
p
-Wasserstein distance follows by convergence in distribution and convergence in the
p^{\text{th}}
absolute moment around
0
:

B.14
e.g.,
[
Vil09
, Definition 6.8(i) and Theorem 6.9]
.

Fix
p>0
. Let
\mu
be a measure in
{\cal P}(\mathbb{R})
, and
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of measures in
{\cal P}(\mathbb{R})
that converges weakly to
\mu
, and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert t\rvert^{p}\,d\mu_{n}(t)\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert t\rvert^{p}\,d\mu(t)\,.

Then
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges to
\mu
in
p
-Wasserstein distance.

B.15
e.g.,
[
Bil95
, Theorem 20.5(ii)]
.

Choose a sequence of random variables
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and a random variable
X
. Then
\plim_{n\to\infty}X_{n}=X
if and only if every subsequence
(n_{i})_{i\in I}
has a sub-subsequence
(n_{j})_{j\in J}
(
J\subseteq I
) such that
(X_{n_{j}})_{j\in J}
converges almost surely to
X
.

Altogether, this implies the following:

B.16
.

Assume
2
.
Fix any variable
i\in V
, and integer
1\leq\ell\leq L
. Then there exists a random variable
U\sim{\cal N}\left(0,\nu_{\ell}\right)
independent from
{\cal G}_{i}^{\ell-1}
such that for any
p>0
,

\displaystyle\plim_{d\to\infty}\E\left[\big\lvert u_{i\to a}^{\ell}-U\big\rvert^{p}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\right]=0

Proof.

Consider the sequence of random variables
\left(u^{\ell}_{i\to a}\;\big|\;\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\right)_{d\geq 1}
.
By
Lemma

3.5
,
Corollary

B.10
, and
Lemma

B.11
(and
2
),
each finite moment of this sequence converges in probability as
d\to\infty
to that of a Gaussian
U\sim{\cal N}\left(0,\nu_{\ell}\right)
.

Now consider any subsequence
\left(u^{\ell}_{i\to a}\;\big|\;\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\right)_{d\in I}
.
By repeated application of
Lemma

B.15
, there exists a sub-subsequence
\left(u^{\ell}_{i\to a}\;\big|\;\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\right)_{d\in J}
(
J\subseteq I
) such that
for every

k
, the
k^{\text{th}}
moment converges
almost surely
to the
k^{\text{th}}
moment of
{\cal N}\left(0,\nu_{\ell}\right)
.
Because a Gaussian distribution is uniquely determined by its moments (
[
Bil95
, Example 30.1]
),
Lemmas

B.13
and
B.14
imply
that the probability measures associated with
\left(u^{\ell}_{i\to a}\;\big|\;\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\right)_{d\in J}
converge almost surely in
p
-Wasserstein distance to that of
{\cal N}\left(0,\nu_{\ell}\right)
, for all

. Thus, for any
I
, there exists a

\left(\E\left[\big\lvert u_{i\to a}^{\ell}-U\big\rvert^{p}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\right]\right)_{d\in J}

0
.
The statement follows by
Lemma

B.15
.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of state evolution

Here, we prove
Theorem

4.3
.
First, we show that the average of a pseudo-Lipschitz function is not affected by replacing an input message
u_{i\to a}^{\ell}
with a Gaussian
U\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\nu_{\ell}\right)
.
We use this inductively to complete the proof.

C.1
.

Assume
2
.
Let
1\leq\ell\leq L
, and
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a pseudo-Lipschitz function.
For any factor
a
and variable
i\in\partial a
, we have

\displaystyle\E\left[\left|\psi\left(u_{i\to a}^{1},\dots,u_{i\to a}^{\ell-1},u_{i\to a}^{\ell}\right)-\psi\left(u_{i\to a}^{1},\dots,u_{i\to a}^{\ell-1},U\right)\right|\right]=o_{d}(1),

where
U\sim{\cal N}\left(0,\nu_{\ell}\right)
is a Gaussian random variable independent of
{\cal G}_{i}^{\ell-1}
.

Proof.

By
Definition

4.1
, there exists an absolute constant
C\geq 0
such that for any
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
,

\displaystyle\E\big[\lvert\psi(\bm{x})-\psi(\bm{y})\rvert\big]

\displaystyle\leq\E\big[C\cdot\big(1+\lVert\bm{x}\rVert+\lVert\bm{y}\rVert\big)\cdot\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{y}\rVert\big]\,.

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz to each term, we see

\displaystyle\E\big[\lvert\psi(\bm{x})-\psi(\bm{y})\rvert\big]\leq C\cdot\left(1+\sqrt{\E\big[\lVert\bm{x}\rVert^{2}\big]}+\sqrt{\E\big[\lVert\bm{y}\rVert^{2}\big]}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\E\big[\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{y}\rVert^{2}\big]}\,.

(C.1)

We fix any
1\leq\ell\leq L
. Given
\mathcal{G}^{\ell-1}_{i}
, let
U\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\nu_{\ell})
be the Gaussian random variable associated with
u_{i\to a}^{\ell}
in
Theorem

B.16
. We define
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
as

\bm{x}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{i\rightarrow a}^{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{i\rightarrow a}^{\ell-1}\\
u_{i\rightarrow a}^{\ell}\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\qquad\bm{y}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{i\rightarrow a}^{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{i\rightarrow a}^{\ell-1}\\
U\end{pmatrix}\,.

Note that by
Lemma

B.7
,
\Delta_{x}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\E\left[\|\bm{x}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\right]=\left(\tau_{i\to a}^{\ell}\right)^{2}+\sum_{s=1}^{\ell-1}\left(u_{i\to a}^{s}\right)^{2}
, and
\Delta_{y}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\E\left[\|\bm{y}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\right]=\nu_{\ell}+\sum_{s=1}^{\ell-1}\left(u_{i\to a}^{s}\right)^{2}
.
Applying
Equation

C.1
, we have

\displaystyle\E\big[\lvert\psi(\bm{x})-\psi(\bm{y})\rvert\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\big]

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\left(1+\sqrt{\E\big[\lVert\bm{x}\rVert^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\big]}+\sqrt{\E\big[\lVert\bm{y}\rVert^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\big]}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\E\big[\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{y}\rVert^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\big]}

\displaystyle=C\cdot\left(1+\Delta_{x}+\Delta_{y}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\E\left[\left(u_{i\to a}^{\ell}-U\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\right]}\,.

Note that by
Theorem

B.16
, the right-most expectation converges in probability to zero. Taking the total expectation,

\displaystyle\E\left[\left|\psi(\bm{x})-\psi(\bm{y})\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\E\left[\left|\psi(\bm{x})-\psi(\bm{y})\right|\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E\left[C\left(1+\Delta_{x}+\Delta_{y}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\E\big[\left(u_{i\to a}^{\ell}-U\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{i}^{\ell-1}\big]}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\E\left[C^{2}\left(1+\Delta_{x}+\Delta_{y}\right)^{2}\right]}\cdot\sqrt{\E\big[\left(u_{i\to a}^{\ell}-U\right)^{2}\big]}\,.

By definition and
Proposition

B.8
,
\E[\Delta_{x}^{2}]
and
\E[\Delta_{y}^{2}]
are bounded independent of
d
, so the first expectation is bounded independent of
d
. The second expectation is
o_{d}(1)
by
Theorem

B.16
. The statement follows.
∎

Proof.

Note that
Equation

4.2
follows from
Equation

4.1
by

B.3
and pseudo-Lipschitzness of
\psi
.
So we focus on
Equation

4.1
, proceeding via induction.
The base case
\ell=1
follows directly from
Lemma

C.1
(using our assumption of
2
).

For the inductive step, assume that the statement holds up to
\ell
.
Define the function
\tilde{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\tilde{\psi}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle def}}}{{=}}\E_{U\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\nu_{\ell+1})}\big[\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell},U)\big]\,.

\displaystyle\E\big[\psi\big(U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell+1}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\E_{U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}}\big[\tilde{\psi}\big(U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}\big)\big]

\displaystyle\E\left[\tilde{\psi}\big(u_{i\to a}^{1},\ldots,u_{i\to a}^{\ell}\big)\right]=\E\big[\tilde{\psi}\big(U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell}\big)\big]+o_{d}(1)=\E\big[\psi\big(U_{1},\ldots,U_{\ell+1}\big)\big]+o_{d}(1)\,.
