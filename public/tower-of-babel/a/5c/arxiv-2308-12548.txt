Abstract
In this paper, we present a generalized Japan Standard Time algorithm (JST-algo) for higher-order atomic clock ensembles and mathematically clarify the relations of the (generalized) JST-algo and the conventional Kalman filtering algorithm (CKF-algo) in the averaged atomic time and the clock residuals for time scale generation. In particular, we reveal the fact that the averaged atomic time of the generalized JST-algo does not depend on the observation noise even though the measurement signal is not filtered in the algorithm. Furthermore, the prediction error of CKF-algo is rigorously shown by using the prediction error regarding an observable state space. It is mathematically shown that when the covariance matrices of system noises are identical for all atomic clocks, considering equal averaging weights for the clocks is a necessary and sufficient condition to ensure equivalence between the generalized JST-algo and CKF-algo in averaged atomic time. In such homogeneous systems, a necessary and sufficient condition for observation noises is presented to determine which algorithm can generate the clock residuals with smaller variances. A couple of numerical examples comparing the generalized JST-algo and CKF-algo are provided to illustrate the efficacy of the results.



I
Introduction

An
atomic clock ensemble is a collection of highly accurate atomic clocks that work together to achieve a precise and stable timekeeping system. Atomic clocks are devices that measure time based on the vibrations of atoms with constant resonance frequencies, e.g., cesium and rubidium atoms.
Even though individual atomic clocks can be accurate, they still have some tiny variations due to the environmental factors like temperature fluctuations, external electromagnetic fields, and quantum mechanical effects.
By combining the measurements from multiple atomic clocks within an ensemble, the national metrology institutes (NMIs) all over the world can reduce these variations and create a more reliable and robust timekeeping system
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
The advancements in atomic clock technology and the development of accurate time scales based on these ensembles offer numerous benefits and applications that are crucial for the future smart society, e.g.,
satellite navigation
[
5
]
,
financial networks with high-frequency trading and
time-sensitive transactions
[
6
]
,
telecommunications
[
7
]
, etc.

The variations in tick rates of atomic clocks are referred to as time deviations from the ideal clock behavior, which can be modeled as stochastic processes.
To improve the accuracy of time scales, the researchers obtained experimental evidence for modeling the behavior of atomic clocks and the time deviations the clocks experience as a series of linear stochastic differential equations
[
8
]
.
Based on this, in the task of time generation, how to properly deal with the prediction problem for time deviations is the main issue to guarantee excellent performance of the generated time
[
2
]
.
The algorithm that deals with such a prediction problem of time deviations is referred to as the algorithm of averaged atomic time.

The Kalman filter is a mathematical method used for state estimation/prediction in control theory and signal processing to estimate the state of a dynamic system based on a series of noisy measurements.
It is well known that the key advantage of the Kalman filter is its ability to provide an optimal estimate by dynamically balancing the trade-off between the model predictions and the actual measurements, effectively reducing the impact of observation noises
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.
In time and frequency community, the Kalman filter has been constructed as the algorithm of averaged atomic time using the difference of clock reading between two clocks as measurement signals
[

,

,

.
However,
it is reported that one may face the numerical instability problem of the Kalman filter in the atomic clock ensembles

]
.
This is because practical implementations often ignore the fact that the dynamic system of atomic clock ensembles is undetectable

,
whereas detectability is a necessary condition ensuring asymptotic convergence of error covariances

20
]
.
In atomic clock ensembles, since the detectability condition is broken,
the computational errors in error covariances of the Kalman filter grow unboundedly and hence lead to numerical instability problem

]

Except for the CKF-algo,
the JST-algo
[
21
]
, is specific to Japan’s timekeeping system governed by the National Institute of Information and Communications Technology (NICT),
while Japan is known for its advanced technology and precision timekeeping capabilities.
The detailed algorithm can be found in

(and the reference therein) and is applicable for the atomic clock ensembles with second-order clocks.
But there is no literature discussing the relation between the JST-algo and the CKF-algo.
It is interesting to compare the two methods and ask when they are equal and which method is superior to the other.

In this paper, we mathematically clarify the relation among the algorithms of averaged atomic time by the CKF-algo and JST-algo.
Different from the preliminary version
[
1

, we generalize the results for higher-order atomic clock ensembles.
Specifically, we generalize the existing JST-algo to a generalized form for the atomic clock ensembles with higher-order models, where the proposed generalized JST-algo is reduced to the existing JST-algo for second-order clocks.
By theoretically analyzing the generalized JST-algo in state-space model, we reveal the fact that the averaged atomic time of JST-algo does not depend on the observation noise even though the measurement signal is not filtered in the algorithm.
Furthermore, using observable Kalman canonical decomposition, the prediction error of CKF-algo is derived.
We show the fact that when the covariance matrix of the system noises is identical for all atomic clocks, considering equal averaging weights for the clocks is a sufficient and necessary condition guaranteeing equivalence between the generalized JST-algo and CKF-algo in averaged atomic time.
In addition, different from the preliminary version

,
i=1,\ldots,n

n
independent one-dimensional Gaussian random noises with the variance given by
\sigma_{i}^{j}\geq 0
,
i=1,\ldots,n

For example, it is often assumed that the order

II-B

Task of Time Generation

Consider a discrete-time sequence

the accuracy of the generated time (

[

]
)

\displaystyle{\rm TA}(t):=\hat{h}_{0}(t)-h_{0}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{i}[\Delta h^{i}(t)-\Delta\hat{h}^{i}(t)],

{\rm TA}[k]=C\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}[k],\quad k=0,1,\ldots,T,

(27)

does not depend on the observation noise

\boldsymbol{w}[\cdot]

for any weight
\beta

satisfying
\beta_{1}+\ldots+\beta_{m}=1
.
In addition, the ensemble prediction error

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}

is given by

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}[k+1]=A[k]\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}[k]+\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)\boldsymbol{v}[k]

(28)

Proof:

For the following analysis, we let

P:=\mathds{1}_{m}\beta^{{\sf T}},\quad\overline{P}:=I_{m}-\mathds{1}_{m}\beta^{{\sf T}}

which are projection matrices satisfying

\overline{P}=\overline{V}^{\dagger}\overline{V}-\mathds{1}_{m}(\beta-\tfrac{1}{m}\mathds{1}_{m})^{{\sf T}},\quad\overline{P}e_{1:m-1}\overline{V}=\overline{P}.

Now letting
\overline{V}^{{\ddagger}}:=\overline{V}^{\dagger}-\mathds{1}_{m}(\beta-\tfrac{1}{m}\mathds{1}_{m})^{{\sf T}}e_{1:m-1}
,
the predicted time deviations of the JST-algo are written as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}[k+1]=

\displaystyle\bigl(C{A}[k]\otimes P\bigr)\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}[k]+\overline{P}e_{1:m-1}\boldsymbol{y}[k+1]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl(C{A}[k]\otimes P\bigr)\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}[k]+\overline{P}e_{1:m-1}\bigl\{\Bigl(CA[k]\!\otimes\!\overline{V}\bigr)\boldsymbol{x}[k]

\displaystyle\qquad+\bigl(C\otimes\overline{V}\bigr)\boldsymbol{v}[k]+\boldsymbol{w}[k+1]\bigr\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl(C{A}[k]\otimes P\bigr)\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}[k]+\bigl(CA[k]\!\otimes\!\overline{P}\bigr)\boldsymbol{x}[k]

\displaystyle+\overline{P}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}[k]+\overline{V}^{{\ddagger}}\boldsymbol{w}[k+1],

(29)

Thus the prediction error
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[\cdot]:=\boldsymbol{x}[\cdot]-\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}[\cdot]
of JST-algo follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1}[k+1]

\displaystyle=\bigl(C{A}[k]\otimes P\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]+P\boldsymbol{v}_{1}[k]-\overline{V}^{{\ddagger}}\boldsymbol{w}[k+1]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{2:n}[k+1]

\displaystyle=\bigl(A_{2:n}[k]\otimes I_{m}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{2:n}[k]+\boldsymbol{v}_{2:n}[k].

(30)

where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1}
is understood as the clock residuals
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}
of time deviations.
Equivalently,
we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k+1]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left[\;\begin{matrix}&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!C{A}[k]\otimes P\\
0&A_{2:n}[k]\otimes I_{m}\end{matrix}\>\right]}_{F^{{\ddagger}}\bigl(A[k]\otimes I_{m}\bigr)}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]-\underbrace{\left[\;\begin{matrix}\overline{V}^{{\ddagger}}\\
0\end{matrix}\>\right]}_{\overline{F}}\boldsymbol{w}[k+1]

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left[\;\begin{matrix}&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!C\otimes P\\
0&I_{n-1}\otimes I_{m}\end{matrix}\>\right]}_{F^{{\ddagger}}}\boldsymbol{v}[k].

(31)

Here, note that
\overline{F}
,
F^{{\ddagger}}

satisfy

\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)\overline{F}=0
,

\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)F^{{\ddagger}}=I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}

due to
\beta^{{\sf T}}\overline{V}^{{\ddagger}}=0
,
\beta^{{\sf T}}P=\beta^{{\sf T}}

and hence we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}[k+1]

\displaystyle=\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)\bigl\{F^{{\ddagger}}\bigl(A[k]\otimes I_{m}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]

\displaystyle\qquad+F^{{\ddagger}}\boldsymbol{v}[k]-\overline{F}\boldsymbol{w}[k+1]\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)\Bigl(A[k]\otimes I_{m}\Bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]+\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)\boldsymbol{v}[k]

\displaystyle=A[k]\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]+\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)\boldsymbol{v}[k],

(32)

which completes proof.
∎

Remark 1

The result of Theorem
1
can contribute to theoretically explaining why NMIs all over the world often require more atomic clocks to generate more accurate time scales.
Specifically, consider a homogeneous ensemble with a constant sampling interval
\tau_{k}=\tau
for
k=0,1,\ldots,T
,
where the covariance of state noise
\boldsymbol{v}[k]
is written as
Q\otimes I_{m}
for some
Q
.
In this case, supposing that the initial error
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[0]=\hat{\mu}_{0}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}
for some
\hat{\mu}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the initial covariance of prediction error is
\boldsymbol{P}_{0}
,
it can be shown from (
27
) that the expected value of the averaged atomic time

\mathbb{E}[{\rm TA}[k]]=CA^{k}(\tau)\hat{\mu}_{0}

does not depend on the number
m
of the atomic clocks,
but the covariance

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}({\rm TA}[k])=

\displaystyle C\Big\{A^{k}(\tau)\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)\boldsymbol{P}_{0}\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta\bigr)A^{k}(\tau)^{{\sf T}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum\nolimits_{i=0}^{k-1}A^{i}(\tau)\beta^{{\sf T}}\beta QA^{i}(\tau)^{{\sf T}}\Big\}C^{{\sf T}}

(33)

is diminished by increasing the number
m
of the atomic clocks when the weights of the clocks are set to all the same (since
\beta^{{\sf T}}\beta=\tfrac{1}{m}
under
\beta=\tfrac{1}{m}\mathds{1}_{m}
).
In other words, better prediction performance of the averaged atomic time can be achieved for the atomic clock ensemble with larger number
m
of the clocks.

Now, we begin to make a theoretical analysis for CKF-algo.
It is well known that the CKF-algo may result in numerical instability in the real implementation of time generations.
This is because the computation error accumulates in the Kalman gains due to the divergence of error covariance under undetectability of the state space model.
Two covariance reduction methods are developed in
[
16
]
and
[
24
]

to suppress the numerical instability in the real implementation.
However, neither of them can absolutely avoid the appearance of numerical instability especially for the case when the initial covariance
\boldsymbol{P}_{0}
is large, and the theoretical analysis of CKF-algo for the ideal case without computation errors is still unclear.

To reveal the theoretical expression of averaged atomic time
{\rm TA}[k]
of the CKF-algo,
we note that the state profile
\boldsymbol{x}
of the ensemble can be decomposed by observable Kalman canonical decomposition as

\boldsymbol{x}=\left[\;\begin{matrix}I_{n}\otimes\overline{V}^{\dagger}&I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}\end{matrix}\>\right]\left[\;\begin{matrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm o}\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm\bar{o}}\end{matrix}\>\right]

(34)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm o}:=\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{nm-n}
,

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm\bar{o}}:=\tfrac{1}{m}\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

denote the observable and unobservable state, respectively.
Using this fact and letting

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm o}[k]:={\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\rm o}[k]-\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\rm o}[k]=\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k],

(35)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]:={\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]-\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]=\tfrac{1}{m}\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k],

(36)

the ensemble prediction error

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}
is transformed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}[k]

\displaystyle=(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}})\left[\;\begin{matrix}I_{n}\otimes\overline{V}^{\dagger}&I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}\end{matrix}\>\right]\left[\;\begin{matrix}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm o}[k]\\
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]

\displaystyle=\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\overline{V}^{\dagger}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm o}[k]+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k].

(37)

That is to say, once we derive the dynamics of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm o}[k]
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]
,
the dynamics of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}[k]
can be accordingly derived.

In terms of CKF-algo,
it can be theoretically shown that the predicted observable state
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\rm o}
under CKF-algo follows

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}=\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}{\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}^{\sf T}\bigr(\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}^{\sf T}+\hat{R}\bigr)^{-1}}

(38)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{F}_{\rm o}[k](\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k})\boldsymbol{F}_{\rm o}[k]^{\sf T}+W_{\rm o}

(39)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\rm o}[k+1]=\boldsymbol{F}_{\rm o}[k]\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\rm o}[k]+\hat{\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}[k]-\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\rm o}[k])

(40)

where
\boldsymbol{F}_{\rm o}[k]:=A[k]\otimes I_{m-1}
,
\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}:=C\otimes I_{m-1}
,
W_{\rm o}:=(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})\hat{W}(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})^{{\sf T}}
, are the system matrix, measurement matrix, and system noise covariance for the observable subspace.
Note that
\hat{\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}:=(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})\boldsymbol{K}_{k}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}:=(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})\boldsymbol{P}_{k}(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})^{{\sf T}}

are understood as the Kalman gain and the error covariance of CKF-algo in observable state space, respectively.
The detailed derivation of (
40
) is attached in Appendix below for reference.

The next result shows that the averaged atomic time
{\rm TA}[k]
of JST-algo and CKF-algo are equivalent to each other if we adopt some specific guesses of the noise covariance and choose equal weights for the clocks.

Theorem 2

Consider the system model (
4
) for an
m
-clock ensemble.
For a given initial guess
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}[0]
,
if the guess
\hat{W}
of the system noise covariance is given by
\hat{W}=Q\otimes I_{m}

for some
Q\geq 0
,
then the averaged atomic time
{\rm TA}[k]
of the CKF-algo is given by (
27
)
with the ensemble prediction error

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}[k+1]=

\displaystyle A[k]\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}[k]+(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{v}[k]

\displaystyle-(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\overline{V}^{\dagger})\hat{\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}(\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm o}[k]+\boldsymbol{w}[k])

(41)

where the prediction error of observable state
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm o}
follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm o}[k+1]=

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{F}_{\rm o}[k]-\hat{\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm o}[k]-\hat{\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}[k]

\displaystyle+(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})\boldsymbol{v}[k].

(42)

Furthermore, with such a
\hat{W}
,
the generalized JST-algo and CKF-algo generate
the same averaged atomic time
{\rm TA}[k]
for
k=0,1,\ldots,T

if and only if the weights of the atomic clocks are all equal, i.e.,

\beta=\tfrac{1}{m}\mathds{1}_{m}
.

Proof:

First, using

\boldsymbol{y}[k]=\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{x}[k]+\boldsymbol{w}[k]=\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm o}[k]+\boldsymbol{w}[k]
,
it follows that the derivation (
42
) of the prediction error in the observable state is immediate from (
40
) since
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm o}
follows

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm o}[k+1]=\boldsymbol{F}_{\rm o}[k]\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\rm o}[k]+(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})\boldsymbol{v}[k].

In terms of the prediction error in the unobservable state,
since the prediction error of Kalman filter is

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k+1]=(\boldsymbol{F}[k]-\boldsymbol{K}_{k}\boldsymbol{H})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]-\boldsymbol{K}_{k}\boldsymbol{w}[k]+\boldsymbol{v}[k],

we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k+1]=

\displaystyle A[k]\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]+\tfrac{1}{m}\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{v}[k]

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{m}\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]+\boldsymbol{w}[k])

(43)

Note that the condition
\hat{W}=Q\otimes I_{m}
indicates

(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{K}_{k}=(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{P}_{k}{{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}\bigr(\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{P}_{k}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}+\hat{R}\bigr)^{-1}}=0
for
k=0,1,\ldots,T
, because
(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{P}_{k}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}=0
,
k=0,1,\ldots,T
.
In particular, since
\boldsymbol{P}_{0}=pI_{nm}
, it follows that

(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{P}_{0}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}=0
holds.
Furthermore, let
(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{P}_{k}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}:=\boldsymbol{U}_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{1}=

\displaystyle(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\left(\boldsymbol{F}[0](\boldsymbol{P}_{0}-\boldsymbol{K}_{0}{\boldsymbol{H}}\boldsymbol{P}_{0})\boldsymbol{F}[0]^{\sf T}+\hat{W}\right){\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle pA[0]A[0]^{\sf T}(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}}){\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}+(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\hat{W}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}=0,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{2}=

\displaystyle(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\left(\boldsymbol{F}[1](\boldsymbol{P}_{1}-\boldsymbol{K}_{1}{\boldsymbol{H}}\boldsymbol{P}_{1})\boldsymbol{F}[1]^{\sf T}+\hat{W}\right){\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A[1](I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\left(\boldsymbol{F}[0](\boldsymbol{P}_{0}-\boldsymbol{K}_{0}{\boldsymbol{H}}\boldsymbol{P}_{0})\boldsymbol{F}[0]^{\sf T}+\hat{W}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\boldsymbol{F}[1]^{\sf T}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}+(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\hat{W}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle pA[1]A[0]A[0]^{\sf T}A[1]^{\sf T}(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}}){\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}=0,

whereas the proof for
\boldsymbol{U}_{k}=0
,
k>2
can be similarly handled.

Thus, the prediction error of unobservable state is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k+1]=

\displaystyle A[k]\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]+\tfrac{1}{m}\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{v}[k]

(44)

Now, noting that

\beta^{{\sf T}}\overline{V}^{\dagger}\overline{V}=\beta^{{\sf T}}-\tfrac{1}{m}\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}}=\beta^{{\sf T}}_{\rm df}
,
it follows from

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}[k\!+\!1]=

\displaystyle\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\overline{V}^{\dagger}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm o}[k+1]+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k+1]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\overline{V}^{\dagger}\bigr)\Big(\boldsymbol{F}_{\rm o}[k]\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm o}[k]+(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})\boldsymbol{v}[k]\Big)

\displaystyle+A[k]\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]+\tfrac{1}{m}\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{v}[k]

\displaystyle-\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\overline{V}^{\dagger}\bigr)\hat{\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}\Big(\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm o}[k]+\boldsymbol{w}[k])\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A[k]\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]+\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\bigr)\boldsymbol{v}[k]

\displaystyle-\bigl(I_{n}\!\otimes\!\beta^{{\sf T}}\overline{V}^{\dagger}\bigr)\hat{\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}\Big(\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm o}[k]\!+\!\boldsymbol{w}[k])\Big)

(45)

that (
41
) holds.
Then it can be seen
that CKF-algo and JST-algo generate the same averaged atomic time
{\rm TA}[k]
if and only if

\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\overline{V}^{\dagger}\bigr)\hat{\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}=\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\beta^{{\sf T}}\overline{V}^{\dagger}\overline{V}\bigr){\boldsymbol{K}}_{k}=(I_{n}\otimes\beta_{\rm df}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{K}_{k}=0
, i.e.,

(I_{n}\otimes\beta_{\rm df}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{P}_{k}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}=0
,
k=0,1,\ldots,T
.

Recalling
\boldsymbol{P}_{0}=pI_{nm}
and
\beta_{\rm df}
satisfies
\beta_{\rm df}^{{\sf T}}\mathds{1}_{m}=0
, it follows that

(I_{n}\otimes\beta_{\rm df}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{P}_{0}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}=0

(I_{n}\otimes\beta_{\rm df}^{{\sf T}})\boldsymbol{P}_{1}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{\sf T}=0

,

,

Remark 2

The equivalence result in Theorem
2
indicates that more precise averaged atomic time can be achieved by the clock ensemble with larger number
m
of the clocks for both JST-algo and CKF-algo in a homogeneous ensemble (see Remark
1
).
However, it is worth noting that the computation cost of the CKF-algo may be larger than the generalized JST-algo when the number
m
of the clocks is too big because CKF-algo requires more matrix computation than the generalized JST-algo.
A discussion in terms of the runtime of CKF-algo and the generalize JST-algo will be given in Section
IV
later.

III-C

Relation Between The Generalized JST-algo and CKF-algo in Clock Residual

Except the comparison of accuracy with the averaged atomic time
{\rm TA}[k]
, it is important to compare

Theorem 3

Consider the system model (
4
) for an
m
-clock ensemble.
For a given initial guess
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}[0]
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{1}[k]

(47)

for

, where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm o}[k]
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]
are given by (
42
) and (
44
), respectively.

Furthermore,
if the sampling interval is constant, i.e.,
\tau_{k}=\tau
for

,
then the mean of clock residual
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1}

(48)

If, in addition, there is no observation noise, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{w}[k]=0
,

,
then the covariance of clock residual
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1}
satisfy

Proof:

First, similar to the decomposition of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm ens}
in (
37
),
the clock residual
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1}
can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1}[k]

\displaystyle=(C\otimes I_{m})\left[\;\begin{matrix}I_{n}\otimes\overline{V}^{\dagger}&I_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}\end{matrix}\>\right]\left[\;\begin{matrix}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm o}[k]\\
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]\end{matrix}\>\right]

\displaystyle=\bigl(C\otimes\overline{V}^{\dagger}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm o}[k]+\bigl(C\otimes\mathds{1}_{m}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]

\displaystyle=\overline{V}^{\dagger}\bigl(\underbrace{C\otimes I_{m-1}}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm o}[k]+C\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]\mathds{1}_{m}.

(50)

which directly proves (
47
) for CKF-algo.
Now it follows from the error dynamics (
31
) of the generalized JST-algo that
the prediction error
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm JST}_{\rm o}=\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]
of the generalized JST-algo follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm JST}_{\rm o}[k+1]=\bigl(I_{n}\otimes\overline{V})\bigl\{F^{{\ddagger}}\bigl(A[k]\otimes I_{m}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+F^{{\ddagger}}\boldsymbol{v}[k]-\overline{F}\boldsymbol{w}[k+1]\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\bigl(A_{2:n}^{0}[k]\otimes\overline{V}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}[k]+\bigl(I^{0}_{n-1}\otimes\overline{V}\bigr)\boldsymbol{v}[k]\!-\!\left[\;\begin{matrix}\!I_{m-1}\!\\
0\end{matrix}\>\right]\boldsymbol{w}[k+1]

\displaystyle=\bigl(A_{2:n}^{0}[k]\otimes I_{m-1}\bigr)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm JST}_{\rm o}[k]+\bigl(I^{0}_{n-1}\otimes\overline{V}\bigr)\boldsymbol{v}[k]

\displaystyle\quad-\left[\;\begin{matrix}I_{m-1}\\
0\end{matrix}\>\right]\boldsymbol{w}[k+1]

(51)

which is diverging since the eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A_{2:n}^{0}
is given by
\lambda\in\{0,1,\ldots,1\}
.

) indicates

\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm JST}_{\rm o}[k+1]=-\boldsymbol{w}[k+1].

(52)

2
that
(
46
) is immediate since
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm JST}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm\bar{o}}[k]
under
\beta=\tfrac{1}{m}\mathds{1}_{m}
.
Next, under the condition
\tau_{k}=\tau
, note that the mean of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\rm CKF}_{\rm o}
in (

) is converging to zero

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1}^{\rm JST}[k]-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1}^{\rm CKF}[k]\Big]

\displaystyle=-\overline{V}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}[k]\boldsymbol{H}_{\rm o}^{{\sf T}}\bigl(\overline{V}^{\dagger}\bigr)^{{\sf T}}\!\leq 0

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the comparison between two time scale generation algorithms using atomic clock ensembles, that are, CKF-algo and JST-algo.
We presented a generalized JST-algo via the state-space model of the higher-order atomic clock ensemble, where the proposed generalized JST-algo is reduced to the existing JST-algo for second-order clocks.
By revealing the theoretical expressions of the averaged atomic times, we discussed the relation between the generalized JST-algo and CKF-algo.
It is found that even though the measurement signal is not filtered,
JST-algo can yield the averaged atomic times independent to the observation noise.
The prediction error of Kalman filtering algorithm was rigorously shown by using the prediction error regarding an observble state space.
We proved that the generalized JST-algo is equivalent to CKF-algo in the sense of generating averaged atomic time if and only if equal averaging weights are considered for the atomic clocks when the covariance matrices of system noises are identical for all the clocks.

In such a homogeneous clock ensemble, we further revealed the theoretical relation between the generalized JST-algo and CKF-algo in the individual clock residuals and presented the sufficient and necessary condition for observation noises to determine which algorithm can generate the clock residuals with smaller variances.
We discussed the relation between the runtime of the algorithms versus the number of clocks in one of the numerical examples.
It is found that if the observation noises are tiny enough by some sophisticated measuring equipment, one is suggested to imply the generalized JST-algo (instead of CKF-algo) for time scale generation since the generalized JST-algo can generate smaller clock residuals and the calculation cost is lower than CKF-algo if the number of the atomic clocks is large.
