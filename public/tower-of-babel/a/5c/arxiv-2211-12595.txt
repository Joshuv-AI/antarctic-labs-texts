Posterior Contraction and Testing for Multivariate Isotonic Regression Thanks: The research was supported in part by NSF Grant number DMS-1916419.
Kang Wang, Subhashis Ghosal

Abstract
We consider the nonparametric regression problem with multiple predictors and an additive error, where the regression function is assumed to be coordinatewise nondecreasing. We propose a Bayesian approach to make an inference on the multivariate monotone regression function, obtain the posterior contraction rate, and construct a universally consistent Bayesian testing procedure for multivariate monotonicity. To facilitate posterior analysis, we set aside the shape restrictions temporarily, and endow a prior on blockwise constant regression functions with heights independently normally distributed. The unknown variance of the error term is either estimated by the marginal maximum likelihood estimate, or is equipped with an inverse-gamma prior. Then the unrestricted block-heights are a posteriori also independently normally distributed given the error variance, by conjugacy. To comply with the shape restrictions, we project samples from the unrestricted posterior onto the class of multivariate monotone functions, inducing the “projection-posterior distribution”, to be used for making an inference. Under an 𝕃 1 \mathbb{L}_{1} -metric, we show that the projection-posterior based on n n independent samples contracts around the true monotone regression function at the optimal rate n − 1 / ( 2 + d ) n^{-1/(2+d)} . Then we construct a Bayesian test for multivariate monotonicity based on the posterior probability of a shrinking neighborhood of the class of multivariate monotone functions. We show that the test is universally consistent, that is, the level of the Bayesian test goes to zero, and the power at any fixed alternative goes to one. Moreover, we show that for a smooth alternative function, power goes to one as long as its distance to the class of multivariate monotone functions is at least of the order of the estimation error for a smooth function. To the best of our knowledge, there is no other test for multivariate monotonicity available in the Bayesian or the frequentist literature. Keywords: Isotonic regression; Multivariate isotonic regression; Bayesian tests for multivariate monotonicity; Contraction rate.

Introduction

Shape restricted inference is an important nonparametric statistical technique with a long history. Functions with qualitative shape restrictions, like monotonic functional relationship between variables, are quite common in natural sciences, sociology, economics and many other areas. Shape restrictions on the function space can also serve as the relaxation to restricted parametric models, such as log-concave density estimation. The shape constraints themselves yield function estimators with good statistical properties without resorting to subjective selection of the smoothness level, such as in kernel or spline smoothing. Starting from early works on statistical inference under order restrictions, problems with monotonicity constraints on parameters of interest, regression functions and probability densities were extensively studied. For the univariate monotone function estimation problem, the least squares estimator for an isotonic regression function and the maximum likelihood estimator for a decreasing density function have interesting geometrical representations respectively as the slope of the greatest convex minorant and the least concave majorant of a cumulative sum diagram. Bayesian approaches to isotonic regression are also available in the literature. Most of these approaches deal with a univariate isotonic regression function. Neelon and Dunson
[

[

Chipman et al.

Bayesian testing procedure of the monotonicity in the univariate case has also been proposed by a few authors. Salomond
[

6

8

[

For testing the monotonicity, they considered the Bayes factor and converted the monotonicity hypothesis to a condition on the minimum of the derivative functions. To the best of our knowledge, no test for multivariate monotonicity, Bayesian or frequentist, is yet available in the literature.

In this paper, we consider a Bayesian approach to multivariate monotone regression, using the projection technique. We show that the resulting induced posterior supported on block-wise constant multivariate monotone function contracts at the optimal rate with respect to an
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-metric. The basis of the result is a new
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-approximation result for multivariate monotone functions by piecewise constant functions. We then construct a test for multivariate monotonicity based on the posterior probability of a slight enlargement of the set of multivariate monotone functions. We show that the resulting Bayesian test is universally consistent in that the size of the test goes to zero, and the power goes to one at any fixed alternative, as the sample size increases to infinity.
We further show that, even for alternatives approaching the null region, the power can go to one, provided that the alternative maintains a distance at least a sufficiently large multiple of the posterior contraction rate determined by its smoothness. These results generalize the testing results of Chakraborty and Ghosal
[
6
]
to the multidimensional predictors.

\mathbb{R}
stands for the real line,

for the set of integers,

. For

with respect to a measure

-norm of

. For a distance

Definition 1
.

A function

, where

f(\bm{x}_{1})\leq f(\bm{x}_{2})

\bm{x}_{1}\preceq\bm{x}_{2}

The space of all multivariate monotone functions on

\mathcal{M}
.

We consider the nonparametric multivariate regression model

\displaystyle Y=f(\bm{X})+\varepsilon,

(2.1)

where
\bm{X}
is the

-dimensional predictor and

is an error term with zero mean and finite variance, independent of

. We shall assume, essentially without loss of generality, that the domain of

. Instead of a traditional smoothness assumption on the regression function

We observe the data

independently from the model. The predictor variable

may be deterministic, or may be obtained independently from a fixed distribution

, independent of the random error variable

To make inference on

, we adopt a Bayesian approach by putting an appropriate prior distribution on

The main objective of this paper is to study the contraction rate of the posterior distribution, and construct a Bayesian test for multivariate monotonicity with some desirable large sample frequentist properties. To facilitate Bayesian inference, we construct a likelihood based on the working model assumption that
\varepsilon_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
, although the actual distribution may be non-normal. For a given

, let
p_{f,\sigma}(y|x)=(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)^{-1}\exp[-(y-f(x))^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})]
stand for the conditional density of

given

.

Let
G_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\bm{X}_{i}}
denote the empirical distribution of
\bm{X}
. For a deterministic predictor variable
\bm{X}
, this is a sequence of deterministic distributions, while for a random

stand for the true value of the regression function

The usual approach to Bayesian inference for model (
2.1

A compliant prior will have to maintain the order restriction on the step-heights, which makes the posterior computation more challenging. More importantly, frequentist analyses such as posterior contraction rates and limiting coverage of credible regions are extremely hard. The projection-posterior approach provides a simple tool to “correct” a non-compliant posterior distribution by projecting posterior samples on the relevant parameter space and uses the resulting induced distribution to make inference, as in Lin and Dunson
[
27
]
and Chakraborty and Ghosal
[
6

8
]

[
42

To obtain posterior contraction rate in terms of a global metric like an

6

Let
\mathcal{F}_{J}=\{f:f=\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\theta_{\bm{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{I_{\bm{j}}},\theta_{\bm{j}}\in\mathbb{R}\}

were unrestricted, a conjugate prior for the model (

\displaystyle\theta_{\bm{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{ind}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{N}(\zeta_{\bm{j}},\sigma^{2}\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{2}),\qquad\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}],

(2.2)

\displaystyle\theta_{\bm{j}}|(\mathbb{D}_{n},\sigma^{2},J)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{ind}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{N}((N_{\bm{j}}\bar{Y}|_{I_{\bm{j}}}+\zeta_{\bm{j}}\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2})/(N_{\bm{j}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}),\sigma^{2}/(N_{\bm{j}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2})),

where

The resulting posterior for

To study the asymptotic properties of the posterior distribution of

in the setting of a deterministic predictor, we consider the

-distance, while for a random predictor arising from a distribution

It will be seen that the projection posterior inherits the convergence properties of the original posterior if the same metric is used to obtain the projection, and hence it will be sufficient to study the unrestricted posterior, which can be done using traditional tools like moment bounding or by applying the general theory of posterior contraction (cf., Ghosal and van der Vaart

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(Y_{i}-\sum_{\bm{j}:\bm{X}_{i}\in I_{\bm{j}}}\zeta_{\bm{j}}\big)^{2}-\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\frac{N_{\bm{j}}^{2}(\bar{Y}|_{I_{\bm{j}}}-\zeta_{\bm{j}})^{2}}{N_{\bm{j}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}}\right].

primarily used in this paper, the minimization in (

) reduces to

\displaystyle\min_{\theta^{\ast}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}N_{\bm{j}}|\theta_{\bm{j}}^{\ast}-\theta_{\bm{j}}|^{p},

(2.10)

while the use of the Lebesgue measure leads to the unweighted isotonization problem of the minimization of
\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}|\theta_{\bm{j}}^{\ast}-\theta_{\bm{j}}|^{p}
subject to the restriction that
\bm{\theta_{\bm{j}}}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{C}
.

3
Main results

Let a sample from the projection-posterior defined by the minimization of an
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-distance, be denoted by
f^{\ast}=\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\theta_{\bm{j}}^{\ast}\mathbbm{1}_{\bm{j}}
. The first part of the following theorem under abstract conditions gives the projection-posterior contraction rates with respect to a variety of
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-metrics. In the second part of the theorem, the conclusion is specialized to the empirical
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-metric or the
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-metric with respect to the distribution of the predictor under easily verifiable conditions.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
J
be deterministic, Assumptions
2
–
3
hold and
let
G^{\ast}
be a distribution on
[0,1]^{d}
possibly depending on
n
and
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n}
satisfying the conditions that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\big[\max_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}G^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})\big]\lesssim J^{-d},\qquad\mathrm{E}\big[\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}G^{*}(I_{\bm{j}})(N_{\bm{j}}+1)^{-1}\big]\lesssim J^{d}/n.

(3.1)

Let
f^{\ast}
be the
\mathbb{L}_{1}(G^{\ast})
-projection of
f
sampled from the unrestricted posterior on
\mathcal{F}_{J}
. Assume further that either
\sigma
is known, or a consistent estimator is plugged-in, or its posterior distribution is consistent. Then for
\epsilon_{n}=\max\{\sqrt{J^{d}/n},J^{-1}\}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{0}\Pi(\|f^{\ast}-f_{0}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}>M_{n}\epsilon_{n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\to 0\mbox{ for any }M_{n}\to\infty.

(3.2)

The optimal
\mathbb{L}_{1}(G^{*})
-rate
n^{-1/(2+d)}
is obtained above by choosing
J\asymp n^{1/(2d+1)}
.

Further, let Assumption
1
hold, and if the predictor is random, assume
J^{d}(\log n)/n\to 0
.
Then the assertion (
3.2
) holds for
G^{*}
the empirical distribution
G_{n}
for both deterministic and random predictor, and also for
G^{*}=G
if the predictor is random with distribution
G
.

The optimal rate above reduces to the
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-optimal rate
n^{-1/3}
in the univariate case obtained by Chakraborty and Ghosal
[
6
]
.
We may also like to study the posterior contraction rate with respect to the
\mathbb{L}_{p}
-metric. However, for
p>1
, the
\mathbb{L}_{p}
-approximation rate by the step function
f_{J}
is weaker, only
J^{-1/p}
, at monotone functions with jumps; see Remark
A.1
. Hence the
\mathbb{L}_{p}
-contraction rate of the corresponding procedure will be suboptimal.

The distribution of a random predictor
\bm{X}
is often unknown, but we can compute the
\mathbb{L}_{1}(G_{n})
-projection. The following corollary asserts that for random predictors with density bounded and bounded away from
0
, the

\mathbb{L}_{1}(G_{n})
-projection posterior achieves
the same posterior contraction rate with respect to the
\mathbb{L}_{1}(\lambda)
-metric (and hence also under the
\mathbb{L}_{1}(G)
-metric, which is equivalent under the assumed condition).

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n}
be i.i.d. with distribution
G
admitting a density function
g
bounded and bounded away from
0
. Let
J
be deterministic,
J\to\infty
and
J^{d}(\log n)/n\to 0
.
Then under Assumptions
2
and
3
, for
\epsilon_{n}=\max\{\sqrt{J^{d}/n},J^{-1}\}
and any
M_{n}\to\infty
,

\mathrm{E}_{0}\Pi(\|f^{\ast}-f_{0}\|_{1,\lambda}>M_{n}\epsilon_{n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\to 0
where
f^{\ast}
is the
\mathbb{L}_{1}(G_{n})
-projection of
f
sampled from the unrestricted posterior.

Next, we shall construct a Bayesian test for the multivariate coordinatewise monotonicity. A natural Bayesian test is based on the posterior probability of the region under the null hypothesis, that is, reject the hypothesis if
\Pi(f\in\mathcal{M}|\mathbb{D}_{n})
is less than
0.5
. However, such a test cannot be consistent since, non-monotone functions will also lie in any neighborhood of a monotone function, so posterior consistency does not imply that the test will be consistent. In numerical experiments, we observe that the Lebesgue
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-distance between a sample from the unrestricted posterior and the set
\mathcal{M}
is often positive for sample size up to
1000
. To avoid a false
rejection of the null hypothesis, we enlarge the class of monotone functions to include functions separated by a distance at most
\delta_{n}
, where
\delta_{n}
decreases with
n
appropriately. Then we consider the posterior probability of the enlarged set,
\Pi(\rho(f,\mathcal{M})\leq\delta_{n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})
, where
\rho
is a suitable metric, usually an
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-distance. This idea was also pursued in
Salomond
[
34
]
and Chakraborty and Ghosal
[
6
]
for Bayesian tests for monotonicity in the univariate case, respectively using the
\mathbb{L}_{\infty}
- and an
\mathbb{L}_{1}
-distance. Below, we consider random predictors obtained from a fixed distribution
G
independently.
The following result shows that the resulting test is consistent at the null and at all fixed alternatives, and the power goes to one at an alternative belonging to a Hölder smooth class
\mathcal{H}(\alpha,L)
(see Definition C.4 of Ghosal and van der Vaart
[
18
]
) even if the alternative approaches the null, provided that happens sufficiently slowly.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
3
hold for a random predictor with distribution
G
, and let
\rho
stand for the
\mathbb{L}_{1}(G)
-distance.
Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
M_{n}\to\infty
be predetermined and
J\asymp n^{1/(2+d)}
. Then for the test
\phi_{n}=\mathbbm{1}\{\Pi(\rho(f,\mathcal{M}_{J})\leq M_{n}n^{-1/(d+2)}|\mathbb{D}_{n})<\gamma\}
, we have

(i)

\mathrm{E}_{0}\phi_{n}\to 0
for any fixed
f_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
;

(ii)

\mathrm{E}_{0}(1-\phi_{n})\to 0
for any fixed integrable
f_{0}\notin\overline{\mathcal{M}}
, where
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
is the
\mathbb{L}_{1}(G)
-closure of
\mathcal{M}
;

(iii)

\sup\{\mathrm{E}_{0}(1-\phi_{n}):f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(\alpha,L),\rho(f_{0},\mathcal{M})>\tau_{n}(\alpha)\}\to 0
, where

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}Cn^{-\alpha/(2+d)},&\text{ for some $C>0$ if $\alpha<1$},\\
CM_{n}n^{-1/(2+d)},&\text{ for any $C>1$ if $\alpha=1$.}\end{cases}

The separation rate
n^{-\alpha/(2+d)}
appearing above for consistency at smooth alternatives is weaker than the corresponding rate
n^{-\alpha/(2\alpha+d)}
for estimation. This is because the value of
J\asymp n^{1/(2+d)}
is optimal for estimating monotone functions, but is suboptimal for estimating
\alpha
-smooth functions. The problem can be avoided simultaneously for all
\alpha\leq 1
by putting a prior on
J
and using a larger enlargement in terms of the weaker Hellinger distance on the density

\displaystyle p_{f,\sigma}(\bm{x},y)=(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})^{-1}\exp[-(y-f(\bm{x}))^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})]

(3.3)

of
(\bm{X},Y)
(with respect to the product of
G
and the Lebesgue measure) with size dependent on the random
J
drawn from its posterior distribution. In this case, the posterior sampling is more involved as the posterior probabilities of each value of
J
also need to be obtained, which involves computations of a large matrix and its determinant, and a stronger separation is needed in terms of the weaker Hellinger metric.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\sigma
be known, Assumptions
1
–
3
hold for a random predictor with distribution
G
,
and Lebesgue density
g
bounded away from zero. Assume
\varepsilon
is sub-Gaussian.
Let
\rho
stand for the Hellinger metric on the density of
(\bm{X},Y)
induced on the regression function, that is,

\displaystyle\rho^{2}(f_{1},f_{2})=2\big\{1-(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-1/2}\int\exp[-(f_{1}(\bm{x})-f_{2}(\bm{x}))^{2}/(8\sigma^{2})]dG(\bm{x})\big\}.

(3.4)

Let
J
be given a prior satisfying (
2.4
). Consider the test

\phi_{n}=\mathbbm{1}\{\Pi(\rho(f,\mathcal{M}_{J})\leq M_{0}\sqrt{(J^{d}\log n)/n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})<\gamma\},

for a predetermined
\gamma\in(0,1)
and a sufficiently large

. Assume that
f_{0}
is bounded. Then

(i)

for any fixed

,

;

(ii)

for any fixed
f_{0}
integrable on
[0,1]^{d}
, and
f_{0}\notin\overline{\mathcal{M}}
,
\mathrm{E}_{0}(1-\phi_{n})\to 0
, where
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
is the
\mathbb{L}_{1}(G)
-closure of
\mathcal{M}
;

(iii)

for alternatives in the Hölder function class, we have for a sufficiently large constant

\displaystyle\sup\{\mathrm{E}_{0}(1-\phi_{n}):f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}(\alpha,L),\rho(f_{0},\mathcal{M})>C(n/\log n)^{-\alpha/(1+2\alpha)}\}\to 0.

.

In both results on testing, we can allow deterministic predictors with

-distance to derive properties (i) and (iii). This follows from a similar proof by obtaining posterior contraction with respect to the

Remark 3.2
.

As the distribution

and

(3.5)

Simulation for Bayesian monotonicity testing

d

\log(dn^{1/4})

\displaystyle H_{0}:\beta_{1}\geq 0\text{ and }\beta_{2}\geq 0,\text{ against }H_{1}:\beta_{1}<0\text{ or }\beta_{2}<0.

Given the significance level
\eta=0.05

\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}

Proof of Proposition

.

For a given

\bar{h}=\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\lambda(I_{\bm{j}})^{-1}\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}h\mathrm{d}\lambda\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{I_{\bm{j}}}
.
Clearly,
\bar{h}\in\mathcal{M}
if
h\in\mathcal{M}
.
Since
f
is constant on
I_{\bm{j}}
, for every
\bm{x}\in I_{\bm{j}}
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}h\mathrm{d}\lambda}{\lambda(I_{\bm{j}})}-f(\bm{x})\bigg|^{p}=\frac{\big|\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}\left(h-f\right)\mathrm{d}\lambda\big|^{p}}{\lambda(I_{\bm{j}})^{p}}\leq\frac{\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}|h-f|^{p}\mathrm{d}\lambda}{\lambda(I_{\bm{j}})},

(5.1)

by Jensen’s inequality. Taking integrals on both sides of (
5.1
) over
I_{\bm{j}}
, it follows that

\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}\left|\bar{h}-f\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}\lambda\leq\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}\left|h-f\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}\lambda
.
Hence the monotone projection of
f\in\mathcal{F}_{J}
onto
\mathcal{M}

also belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{J}
.
The existence of
f^{\ast}
is ensured by the convexity and the closedness of
\mathcal{C}
and the convexity of
\mathbb{L}_{p}
-losses.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Since the posterior for
\sigma
is consistent, it is sufficient to condition on the value of
\sigma
lying in a small neighborhood of
\sigma_{0}
, unless
\sigma
is known.
Let
f_{0,J}=\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}f_{0}(\bm{j}/\bm{J})\mathbbm{1}_{I_{\bm{j}}}
. Then
f_{0,J}\in\mathcal{M}_{J}
.
As
f^{\ast}
is the
\mathbb{L}_{1}(G^{\ast})
-projection of
f
onto
\mathcal{M}_{J}
and
f_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle\|f^{\ast}-f_{0}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{\ast}-f\|_{1,G^{\ast}}+\|f-f_{0,J}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}+\|f_{0,J}-f_{0}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f-f_{0,J}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}+\|f_{0,J}-f_{0}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}.

(5.2)

By Lemma
A.2
,
\|f_{0,J}-f_{0}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}\lesssim J^{-1}
as
G^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})\lesssim J^{-1}
is assumed.
Hence it suffices to bound
\|f-f_{0,J}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
N_{\bm{j}}>0
for all
\bm{j}
.
Let
\bar{f}_{0,J}=\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\theta_{0,\bm{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{I_{\bm{j}}}
, where

\theta_{0,\bm{j}}=N_{\bm{j}}^{-1}\sum_{i:\bm{X}_{i}\in I_{\bm{j}}}f_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})
.
Then Lemma
A.2
applied twice and the triangle inequality give
\|{f}_{0,J}-\bar{f}_{0,J}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}\lesssim J^{-1}
.
Therefore it suffices to show that

\mathrm{E}_{0}\Pi(\|f-\bar{f}_{0,J}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}>M_{n}\sqrt{J^{d}/n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\to 0
.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality first and then Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\Pi(\|f-\bar{f}_{0,J}\|_{1,G^{\ast}}>M_{n}\sqrt{J^{d}/n}|\mathbb{D}_{n},\sigma)

(5.3)

\displaystyle=\Pi(\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}G^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})\left|\theta_{\bm{j}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}}\right|>M_{n}\sqrt{J^{d}/n}|\mathbb{D}_{n},\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq\Pi(\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}G^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})\left|\theta_{\bm{j}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}}\right|^{2}>M_{n}^{2}{J^{d}}/n|\mathbb{D}_{n},\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq M_{n}^{-2}J^{-d}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}nG^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})\mathrm{E}[(\theta_{\bm{j}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}|\mathbb{D}_{n},\sigma].

We decompose

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[(\theta_{\bm{j}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}|\mathbb{D}_{n},\sigma]=\text{Var}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})+(\mathrm{E}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}.

(5.4)

We observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}nG^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})\text{Var}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n},\sigma)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\min\{1,\min_{\bm{j}}\{\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}\}\}}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\frac{nG^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})}{N_{\bm{j}}+1}.

(5.5)

From (
2.3
), we know

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}nG^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})(\mathrm{E}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}nG^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})\left(\frac{N_{\bm{j}}\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}\zeta_{\bm{j}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}}\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}}{N_{\bm{j}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\frac{nG^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})N_{\bm{j}}^{2}(\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}})^{2}}{(N_{\bm{j}}+1)^{2}}+\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\frac{nG^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})}{(N_{\bm{j}}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\frac{nG^{\ast}(I_{\bm{j}})}{N_{\bm{j}}+1}

by noting that
\mathrm{E}[(\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}})^{2}|\bm{X},\sigma]=\sigma^{2}/N_{\bm{j}}
.
Hence, the expectations of the expressions in (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) are bounded by a constant multiple of
J^{d}
in view of (
3.1
). Combining these with (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\Pi(\|f-\bar{f}_{0,J}\|_{2,G^{\ast}}>M_{n}\sqrt{J^{d}/n}|\mathbb{D}_{n},\sigma)\lesssim M_{n}^{-2},

(5.7)

and hence the first part of the theorem is established.

If
\max\{N_{\bm{j}}:\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]\}\lesssim n/J^{d}
, then Lemma
A.3
ensures that the estimator and the posterior for
\sigma
are consistent. For
G^{\ast}=G_{n}
, the condition (
3.1
) holds because
nG_{n}(I_{\bm{j}})(N_{\bm{j}}+1)^{-1}\leq 1
. If
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n}
are i.i.d. with a bounded density
g
, then
\max\{N_{\bm{j}}:\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]\}\lesssim n/J^{d}
by Lemma
A.1
, provided that
J^{d}(\log n)/n\to 0
. If
G^{\ast}=G_{n}
for either random or deterministic predictors
\bm{X}_{i}
, (
5.5
) is bounded by
J^{d}
up to some positive constant. If
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n}
are i.i.d.
G
, then owing to
N_{\bm{j}}\sim\text{Bin}(n,G(I_{\bm{j}}))
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{0}[(N_{\bm{j}}+1)^{-1}]=\begin{cases}\frac{1-(1-G(I_{\bm{j}}))^{n+1}}{(n+1)G(I_{\bm{j}})},&\text{ if }G(I_{\bm{j}})>0;\\
1,&\text{ if }G(I_{\bm{j}})=0,\end{cases}

(5.8)

so that
nG(I_{\bm{j}})\mathrm{E}[(N_{\bm{j}}+1)^{-1}]\leq 1
, implying that (
3.1
) holds for
G^{*}=G
. This completes the proof of the second part of the theorem.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

With
p_{f,\sigma}
defined by (
3.3
), the Hellinger distance between
p_{f_{1},\sigma}
and
p_{f_{2},\sigma}
is
\rho(f_{1},f_{2})
and the Kullback-Leibler divergences are given by

\displaystyle K(p_{f_{0},\sigma};p_{f,\sigma})=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|f-f_{0}\|_{2,G}^{2},\qquad V(p_{f_{0},\sigma};p_{f,\sigma})=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\|f-f_{0}\|_{2,G}^{2}.

Thus the Kullback-Leibler ball
\{f:K(p_{f_{0},\sigma};p_{f,\sigma})\leq\epsilon^{2},V(p_{f_{0},\sigma};p_{f,\sigma^{2}})\leq\epsilon^{2}\}
contains the
\mathbb{L}_{2}(G)
-ball
\{f:\|f-f_{0}\|_{2,G}\leq C\epsilon\}
for some
C>0
, and hence to study posterior contraction at a true
f_{0}
, it suffices to lower bound the prior probability of the latter. Since
\|f_{0}-f_{0,J}\|^{2}_{2,G}\leq(f_{0}(\bm{1})-f_{0}(\bm{0}))\|f_{0}-f_{0,J}\|_{1,G}
, to keep
\|f_{0}-f_{0,J}\|_{2,G}
within a targeted
\epsilon
(which may or may not depend on
n
),
J
should be sufficiently large to make
\|f_{0}-f_{0,J}\|_{1,G}\leq c\epsilon^{2}
for some sufficiently small
c>0
. If a value
\bar{J}
, possibly depending on
n
, achieves this, then using (
2.4
), we can lower bound the required
\mathbb{L}_{2}(G)
-prior concentration by

\displaystyle\Pi(\bar{J})\Pi(\|f-f_{0,\bar{J}}\|_{2,G}\leq C\epsilon|J=\bar{J})

\displaystyle\geq\Pi(\bar{J})\Pi(\cap_{j=1}^{\bar{J}}\{|\theta_{\bm{j}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}}|\leq C_{1}\epsilon^{2}\})

\displaystyle\gtrsim\exp\{-b_{2}\bar{J}^{d}\log\bar{J}-C_{2}\bar{J}^{d}\log(1/\epsilon)\}

for some constant
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. Let
J_{n}
stand for a sufficiently large multiple of
(n\epsilon^{2})^{1/d}
. There are two situations to be considered. If
\epsilon>0
is fixed at an arbitrarily small number, then
\bar{J}
may be chosen as a sufficiently large constant. Then the lower bound for prior concentration in
\epsilon
-neighborhood is a fixed positive number. Hence it follows that
\Pi(J\geq J_{n})/\Pi(\|f-f_{0}\|_{2,G}\leq C\epsilon)=o(e^{-2n\epsilon^{2}})
, and hence by Theorem 8.20 of Ghosal and van der Vaart
[
18
]
,
\Pi(J>J_{n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\to_{P_{0}}0
. If
\epsilon=\epsilon_{n}\to 0
is chosen so that

and the corresponding
\bar{J}=\bar{J}_{n}
satisfies
\log\bar{J}_{n}\lesssim\log n
, and it holds that
\log(1/\epsilon_{n})\lesssim\log n
and
\bar{J}_{n}^{d}\log n\lesssim n\epsilon_{n}^{2}
, then for the choice
J_{n}=L(n\epsilon_{n}^{2}/\log n)^{1/d}
for some sufficiently large constant

, it again follows that
\Pi(J\geq J_{n})/\Pi(\|f-f_{0}\|_{2,G}\leq C\epsilon_{n})=o(e^{-2n\epsilon_{n}^{2}})
, and hence again by Theorem 8.20 of Ghosal and van der Vaart
[
18
]
,
\Pi(J>J_{n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})\to_{P_{0}}0
.

First we establish an auxiliary estimate essential to prove the assertions (i), (ii) and (iii). We claim that for any bounded measurable
f_{0}
(not necessarily monotone or smooth) and a given

, if

\log J_{n}\lesssim\log n
,
there exists a sufficiently large constant
M_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{0}\Pi(\|f-f_{0,J}\|_{2,G}\geq M_{0}\sqrt{J^{d}(\log n)/n},J\leq J_{n}|\mathbb{D}_{n})<\delta,

(5.15)

when
n
large enough. The posterior probability in the expectation of the last display can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{J=1}^{J_{n}}\Pi(J|\mathbb{D}_{n})\Pi\big(\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}(\theta_{\bm{j}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}G(I_{\bm{j}})\geq M_{0}^{2}J^{d}(\log n)/n\big|\mathbb{D}_{n}\big).

(5.16)

By Markov’s inequality and Assumption
3
,

\max_{J\leq J_{n}}\Pi\big(\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}_{n}]}(\theta_{\bm{j}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}G(I_{\bm{j}})\geq M_{0}^{2}J^{d}(\log n)/n\big|\mathbb{D}_{n}\big)\\
\leq\max_{J\leq J_{n}}\frac{n}{M_{0}^{2}J^{d}\log n}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}_{n}]}G(I_{\bm{j}})\big[\text{Var}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})+(\mathrm{E}(\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathbb{D}_{n})-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}\big]

which is bounded in probability by a constant multiple of

\displaystyle\max_{J\leq J_{n}}\frac{n}{M_{0}^{2}J^{d}\log n}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}G(I_{\bm{j}})[(N_{\bm{j}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2})^{-1}+(\bar{Y}|_{I_{\bm{j}}}-\theta_{0,\bm{j}})^{2}]

(5.17)

It is clear that
G(I_{\bm{j}})\asymp J^{-d}
.
By Lemma
A.1
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{0}(\bigcap_{J=1}^{J_{n}}\{C_{1}n/J^{d}\leq\min_{\bm{j}}N_{\bm{j}}\leq\max_{\bm{j}}N_{\bm{j}}\leq C_{2}n/J^{d}\})\to 1,

provided

, for two constant
C_{1}
and

.
Then

uniformly for all
\bm{j}\leq\bm{J}
and
J
.
By the union bound of sub-Gaussian variables (see van der Vaart and Wellner
[
40

(\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}})^{2}\lesssim(J^{d}\log n)/n
with arbitrarily high probability, provided
\log J_{n}\lesssim\log n
.
As
f_{0}
is bounded, we have

|N_{\bm{j}}^{-1}\sum_{i:\bm{X}_{i}\in I_{\bm{j}}}f_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-\theta_{0,\bm{j}}|^{2}\lesssim J^{d}(\log n)/n
uniformly for all
\bm{j}
and
J
with high probability.

).

To prove (i), we observe that the

Lemma A.2
.

Let
G^{*}
be a probability measure on
[0,1]^{d}
such that
\max\{G^{*}(I_{\bm{j}}):\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]\}\lesssim J^{-d}

f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

J

f_{J}:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
f_{J}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\theta_{\bm{j}}\mathbbm{1}\{\bm{x}\in I_{\bm{j}}\}
,
\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}
, where
\theta_{\bm{j}}
is any value between
f((\bm{j}-\bm{1})/J)
and

\|f-f_{J}\|_{p,G^{*}}\lesssim J^{-1/p}

Moreover, for some appropriate choices of

\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]

Proof.

For
\theta_{\bm{j}}

\displaystyle\|f-f_{J}\|_{1,G^{*}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{j}}\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}|f-\theta_{\bm{j}}|\mathrm{d}G^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\bm{j}}(f(\bm{j}/J)-f((\bm{j}-\bm{1})/J))G^{*}(I_{\bm{j}})

\displaystyle\lesssim J^{-d}\sum_{\bm{j}}(f(\bm{j}/J)-f((\bm{j}-\bm{1})/J)).

To get the upper bound of the summation in the last inequality, we first decompose the index set

\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]

be the largest possible subset of

\{\ldots,\bm{j}-2\cdot\bm{1},\bm{j}-\bm{1},\bm{j},\bm{j}+\bm{1},\bm{j}+2\cdot\bm{1},\ldots\}

A_{\bm{j}}
. Note that
A_{\bm{j}}
can be identified by its minimal element. The minimal element of
A_{\bm{j}}
should satisfy that at least one of its coordinates is
1
, otherwise we can subtract this element by
\bm{1}
while the smaller element is still in
[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]
, thus should be in
A_{\bm{j}}
, contradicting the fact of the minimal element. The number of different minimal elements is no larger than
dJ^{d-1}
, by choosing a coordinate equal to
1
among all
d
coordinates and setting the rest ones free in
\{1,\ldots,J\}
. The construction of
A_{\bm{j}}
gives
\sum_{\bm{l}\in A_{\bm{j}}}(f(\bm{l}/J)-f((\bm{l}-\bm{1})/J))\leq f(\bm{1})-f(\bm{0})
. Then we have
\|f-f_{J}\|_{1,G^{*}}\lesssim J^{-d}(dJ^{d-1}(f(\bm{1})-f(\bm{0})))\lesssim J^{-1}
.

The monotonicity constraint will be maintained by choosing
\theta_{\bm{j}}=\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}f(\bm{x})d\bm{x}/G(I_{\bm{j}})
for
\bm{j}\in[\bm{1},\bm{J}]
, or
\theta_{\bm{j}}=f((\bm{j}-\bm{1})/J)
, for instance.

For
p>1
, note that
\|f-f_{J}\|_{p,G^{*}}^{p}\leq(f(\bm{1})-f(\bm{0}))^{p-1}\|f-f_{J}\|_{1,G^{*}}
. Then the conclusion follows.
∎

Remark A.1
.

For
p>1
, the
\mathbb{L}_{p}
approximation rate in Lemma
A.2
may not be improved.
To see this, consider
f=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}\{j:x_{j}>c_{j}\}
, where
\bm{c}
is a fixed vector with irrational coordinates in
[0,1]
. Note that
\bm{c}
is never on the boundary of any hypercube used for partitioning. Clearly,
f
is a multivariate monotone function with discontinuity at any
\bm{x}
that shares a coordinate with
\bm{c}
. Let
\bm{j}^{*}
be the index such that
\bm{c}\in I_{\bm{j}^{*}}
. and generally for a given
J
, for
k=1,\ldots,d
,
\min\{c_{j^{*}_{k}}-(j_{k}^{*}-1)/J,j_{k}^{*}/J-c_{j^{*}_{k}}\}\gtrsim 1/J
. For any hypercube
I_{\bm{j}}
used in the partition such that
j_{k}=j^{*}_{k}
for some
k=1,\ldots,d
, there is a jump of size at least
1
within
I_{\bm{j}}
. Hence, no matter how
\bm{\theta}
is chosen,
\int_{I_{\bm{j}}}|f-f_{J}\|_{p}^{p}\gtrsim J^{-d}
for all such hypercubes. The number of hypercubes with this property is of the order
J^{d-1}
, and hence it follows that
\int|f-f_{J}\|_{p}^{p}\gtrsim J^{-1}
. This shows that the approximation order cannot improve using equispaced knot points to form the hypercubes for the piecewise constant approximation.

Remark A.2
.

In view of Lemma
A.1
, if
J^{d}(\log n)/n\to 0
, then the empirical distribution satisfies the condition
\max\{G_{n}(I_{\bm{j}}):\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]\}\lesssim J^{-d}
in probability, and hence
\|f-f_{J}\|_{1,G_{n}}\lesssim J^{-d}
, and the implicit constant of proportionality in
\lesssim
does not depend on
f
.

Lemma A.3
.

Suppose
J
is deterministic and satisfies
J\to\infty
and
J^{d}/n\to 0
. For
\bm{X}
either deterministic or random,
under Assumptions
1
-
3
, we have

(i)

\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}
converges in probability to
\sigma_{0}^{2}
at the rate of
\max\{n^{-1/2},J^{d}/n,J^{-1}\}
.

(ii)

If we endow
\sigma^{2}

\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
) for some
\beta_{1}>0,\beta_{2}>0
,

By (

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon^{2}_{i}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-\theta_{0,\left\lceil\bm{X}_{i}J\right\rceil})^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}N_{\bm{j}}(\theta_{0,\bm{j}}-\zeta_{\bm{j}})^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(f_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-\theta_{0,\left\lceil\bm{X}_{i}J\right\rceil})+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}N_{\bm{j}}\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}}(\theta_{0,\bm{j}}-\zeta_{\bm{j}})

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-\theta_{0,\left\lceil\bm{X}_{i}J\right\rceil})(\theta_{0,\left\lceil\bm{X}_{i}J\right\rceil}-\zeta_{\left\lceil\bm{X}_{i}J\right\rceil})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\frac{N_{\bm{j}}^{2}(\theta_{0,\bm{j}}-\zeta_{\bm{j}})^{2}+N_{\bm{j}}^{2}(\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}})^{2}+2N_{\bm{j}}^{2}\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}}(\theta_{0,\bm{j}}-\zeta_{\bm{j}})}{N_{\bm{j}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-\theta_{0,\left\lceil\bm{X}_{i}J\right\rceil})(\theta_{0,\left\lceil\bm{X}_{i}J\right\rceil}-\zeta_{\left\lceil\bm{X}_{i}J\right\rceil})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\frac{\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}N_{\bm{j}}(\theta_{0,\bm{j}}-\zeta_{\bm{j}})^{2}}{N_{\bm{j}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\frac{\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}N_{\bm{j}}\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}}(\theta_{0,\bm{j}}-\zeta_{\bm{j}})}{N_{\bm{j}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\frac{N_{\bm{j}}^{2}(\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}})^{2}}{N_{\bm{j}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}}.

Note that
\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}
,
\zeta_{\bm{j}}
and
f_{0}
are all bounded.
Then we can bound

|\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}-\sigma^{2}_{0}|
up to a constant by

\displaystyle\big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon^{2}_{i}-\sigma^{2}_{0}\big|+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-\theta_{0,\left\lceil\bm{X}_{i}J\right\rceil}|+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}(\theta_{0,\bm{j}}-\zeta_{\bm{j}})^{2}

(A.1)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\big|\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}\frac{N_{\bm{j}}\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}}(\theta_{0,\bm{j}}-\zeta_{\bm{j}})}{N_{\bm{j}}+\lambda_{\bm{j}}^{-2}}\big|+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}{N_{\bm{j}}(\bar{\varepsilon}|_{I_{\bm{j}}})^{2}}.

The first term of (
A.1
) is
O_{\mathrm{P}_{0}}(n^{-1/2})
.
By the monotonicity of
f_{0}
, the second term is bounded by
n^{-1}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[\bm{1}:\bm{J}]}N_{\bm{j}}(f_{0}(\bm{j}/\bm{J})-f_{0}((\bm{j}-\bm{1})/\bm{J}))

By Remark
A.2
, following the same argument of the proof of Lemma
A.2
, we have the second term is

and

for deterministic

under Assumption
1
.
The third term is bounded by a constant multiple of

since the hyperparameters

Noting that

For the fourth term, by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have
