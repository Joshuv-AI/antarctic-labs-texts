Abstract
We provide the first fully polynomial-time randomized approximation scheme for the following two counting problems: 1. Given a Context-Free Grammar G G over alphabet Σ \Sigma , count the number of words of length exactly n n generated by G G 2. Given a circuit φ \varphi in Decomposable Negation Normal Form (DNNF) over the set of Boolean variables X X , compute the number of assignments to X X such that φ \varphi evaluates to 1. Finding polynomial time algorithms for the aforementioned problems has been a longstanding open problem. Prior work could either only obtain a quasi-polynomial runtime or a polynomial-time randomized approximation scheme for non-deterministic finite automata and non-deterministic tree automata.

Introduction.

In this paper, we focus on the following computational problems:

Problem 1

(#CFG)
.

Given a Context-Free Grammar (CFG)
G
over alphabet
\Sigma
and an integer
n
in unary, compute the number of words of length exactly
n
generated by
G
. Notationally, we are interested in computing
|L_{n}(G)|
, where
L_{n}(G)=L(G)\cap\Sigma^{n}
and

is the language generated by

Problem 2

(#DNNF)
.

Given a circuit

n

\varphi
evaluates to
1
. Notationally, we are interested in computing

Since counting and sampling are inter-reducible for self-reducible problems, we are equally interested in the design of uniform samplers for CFG and DNNF circuits.

Context-free grammars and decomposable negation normal form circuits are fundamental objects in computer science. Context-free grammars are central in programming languages, and form the basis for almost every modern programming language
[
1
]
. Decomposable Negation Normal Form (DNNF) circuits
were introduced by
[
4
]
, as a representation for Boolean functions that support many interesting problems in polynomial-time, such as satisfiability, clausal entailment or existential variable quantification. Accordingly, DNNF circuits have found applications in planning
[
12
]
, diagnosis
[
4
]
, databases
[
6
]
, design automation
[
13
]
, and the like.

Both #CFG and #DNNF are #P-complete via simple reductions from counting models of a formula in disjunctive normal form, and consequently, there has been an interest in determining whether there exists a polynomial-time randomization scheme to approximately compute the desired quantity within specified tolerance and confidence.
The best-known randomized approximation scheme
[
5
,
7
]
for #CFG and #DNNF has a
quasi-polynomial
runtime, i.e., running time
\exp(\mathrm{polylog}(|G|,n))
for #CFG and
\exp(\mathrm{polylog}(|\varphi|))
for #DNNF. Recent work
[
2
,
3
,
8
,
9
]
has demonstrated the existence of a fully polynomial-runtime approximation scheme (FPRAS) for subsets of #CFG and #DNNF, but the problem of whether there exists a fully polynomial runtime approximation scheme for #CFG and #DNNF remained open.

As highlighted by
[
5
]
, these problems have proven resistant to numerous approaches:

“The main and obvious open question is whether a truly polynomial-time
algorithm exists for sampling from the n-slice of a context-free language, or for
estimating its size
\ldots
A superficially appealing approach is
\ldots
using dynamic
programming. We have not been able to make this approach work nor have we been successful with other approaches such as proving rapid mixing of a suitably defined Markov chain, or finding unbiased estimators with small standard deviations”

The primary contribution of our work is to present the first FPRAS for #CFG and #DNNF, formalized in the form of the following theorems.

Theorem 1
.

There is an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that takes a CFG
G
and
n
(in unary) as input and returns an estimate
\mathsf{est}
such that

1.1.1
\Delta

We will use notations introduced in

of primitive elements (also referred to as inputs/variables) and operators
O
. A
\Delta

\mathcal{P}=(q_{i}\in\Omega:0\leq i\leq|\mathcal{P}|-1)
(

represents the size of

) such that for all

or
q_{i}=q_{j}\circ q_{k}
where

and

. We refer to each

q_{j}
and

of a node is its distance to an input node. Input nodes have height 0; if

, then the height of

is one plus the maximum of the height of

and

. The depth of

is the maximum height of any

. We say that

.

1.1.2
Context-Free Grammars.

A
context-free grammar
(CFG) is a tuple
G=(V,\Sigma,R,S)
where:

•

V
is a finite set of nonterminal symbols,

•

\Sigma
is a finite set of terminal symbols (the alphabet), with
V\cap\Sigma=\emptyset
,

•

is a finite set of production rules,

•

S\in V
is the start symbol.

We write production rules
(A,\alpha)\in R
as
A\to\alpha
. A grammar is in
Chomsky Normal Form
if every production rule is of the form
A\to BC
or
A\to a
, where
A,B,C\in V
and
a\in\Sigma
.

For a grammar
G
and integer
n\geq 0
, we define
L_{n}(G)
as the language of all strings derivable from the start symbol
S
in exactly
n
derivation steps. The following proposition, due to
[
5
]
, shows that we can represent
L_{n}(G)
using a
(\cup,\cdot)
program over the algebra
(2^{\Sigma^{*}},\Sigma,\{\cup,\cdot\})
, where
2^{\Sigma^{*}}
is the set of all languages over
\Sigma
, and
\cup
and
\cdot
denote union and concatenation of languages. In addition, the
(\cup,\cdot)
program is

, that is, every node represents a language of fixed-length words. We state the proposition and provide a formal proof in Appendix for completneess.

Proposition 3
.

For any context-free grammar
G=(V,\Sigma,R,S)
in Chomsky Normal Form, there exists a
(\cup,\cdot)
homogeneous program
\mathcal{P}
over the algebra
(2^{\Sigma^{*}},\Sigma,\{\cup,\cdot\})
such that
\mathcal{P}
represents
L_{n}(G)
for any

.

1.1.3
DNNF Circuits.

We now turn our attention to DNNF circuits, which are refinements of circuits in Negation Normal Form (NNF). Given a set of Boolean variables
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
, let
\hat{X}
be the set of literals (variables and their negations) of
X
, i.e.,
\hat{X}=\{x_{1},\neg x_{1},x_{2},\neg x_{2},\ldots,x_{n},\neg x_{n}\}
. An NNF circuit can be viewed as a
(\vee,\wedge)
program defined over the algebra
(2^{\hat{X}},\hat{X},(\vee,\wedge))
. Let
\mathit{var}(q)
be the set of variables appearing
below

q
(defined recursively), wherein
\mathit{var}(x_{i})=\mathit{var}(\neg x_{i})=\{x_{i}\}
. An NNF circuit is decomposable (DNNF) if it holds that for every
\wedge
node
q_{i}=q_{j}\wedge q_{k}
, we have
\mathit{var}(q_{j})\cap\mathit{var}(q_{k})=\emptyset
. A DNNF circuit is smooth if it holds that for every
\vee
node
q_{i}=q_{j}\vee q_{k}
, we have
\mathit{var}(q_{j})=\mathit{var}(q_{k})
. Every DNNF circuit can be transformed into a smooth DNNF circuit in polynomial time, and therefore, we will assume DNNF circuits are smooth. Given a DNNF circuit, let
\mathit{models}(\varphi)
represent the set of satisfying assignments of
\varphi
, i.e., assignments to variables for which
\varphi
evaluates to
1
.

1.3
Technical Overview.

The high-level idea of our algorithm is conceptually simple. Let
n=\deg(\mathcal{P})
. The nodes of
\mathcal{P}
are visited in bottom-up order. For every node
q
, we compute or construct several objects:

•

A rational number
p(q)
that estimates
\frac{16n}{|\mathit{supp}(q)|}
.

•

Sets of samples
S^{1}(q),\dots,S^{m}(q)
, where
m
is fixed. The number
m
of sample sets per node is a product
m=n_{s}\cdot n_{t}
, with
n_{s}
and
n_{t}
polynomial in the input size (see Section
3
for exact values). Each
S^{r}(q)
is a subset of
\mathit{supp}(q)
. Ideally, the
m
sets would be stochastically independent, but that is not the case here for technical reasons. The number
m
of sample sets per node is predefined, but the size of each sample set is not. One of the main challenges is to keep these sets small to ensure polynomial running time.

Nodes of
\mathcal{P}
with effective height
0
have a small
\mathit{supp}(q)
, and, therefore, for these nodes, the algorithm finds
\mathit{supp}(q)
, sets
p(q)
to its target value (i.e.,
\frac{16n}{|\mathit{supp}(q)|}
), and independently constructs the sets
\{S^{r}(q)\}_{r=1}^{m}

by sampling directly from
\mathit{supp}(q)
. For nodes of higher effective height,
p(q)
and
S^{r}(q)
are computed in a randomized procedure that uses the values of
p(\cdot)
and
S^{r}(\cdot)
already obtained for the children of
q
. In particular, the samples for
q
are constructed directly from its children’s samples. Once all nodes of
\mathcal{P}
have been processed, the algorithm outputs
\frac{16n}{p(o)}
, where
o
is the output node of
\mathcal{P}
. Informally, if
p(o)
is a good estimate of
\frac{16n}{|\mathit{supp}(o)|}
, then the algorithm outputs a good estimate of
|\mathit{supp}(o)|
, which equals
|\mathit{supp}(\mathcal{P})|
.

Unsurprisingly, the primary challenge lies in analyzing this approach. For analysis, we would ideally want
S^{r}(q)
to be such that every element of
\mathit{supp}(q)
is in
S^{r}(q)
independently with identical probability. However, the desideratum of i.i.d. samples is rather too expensive, which led to quasi-polynomial runtime in prior work
[
5
]
2
2

2

As observed by
[
5
]
, their techniques lends itself to FPRAS for constant depth circuits or for the case when
(+,\times)
-program was a formula instead of circuit
. In recent works on FPRAS for Non-deterministic Finite Automaton (NFA)
[
2
]
and Non-deterministic Tree Automaton (NTA)
[
3
]
, independence could be achieved by exploiting

3
3

3

Algorithm 1

\mathsf{reduce}({\cal Z},t)

We assume

has effective height at least

If

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{r}(q)=S^{r}(q_{1})\otimes S^{r}(q_{2})\quad\qquad p(q)=\frac{p(q_{1})p(q_{2})}{16n}\quad\qquad S^{r}(q)=\mathsf{reduce}\left(\hat{S}^{r}(q),\frac{p(q)}{p(q_{1})p(q_{2})}\right)

1.3.2
Further Technical Difficulties.

A careful reader will immediately notice the situation is more delicate than what has been portrayed above. In particular, two concerns warrant immediate attention:

1.

The first concern is about expressions of the form
\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)
and
\alpha^{\prime}\in S^{r}(q)]\leq\frac{(p(q))^{2}}{\prod_{\hat{q}\in\tau}p(\hat{q})}
These state an inequality between a probability, so a fixed number, and
p(q)
which itself is a random variable.

2.

The second concern is about the independence of the sample sets
S^{r}(\cdot)
and
S^{l}(\cdot)
for
r\neq l
as we seek to rely on median of means estimation to establish
p(q)
is a good approximation of
16n|\mathit{supp}(q)|^{-1}
in the context of
+
nodes. Although samples in
S^{r}(\cdot)
can only construct samples in other
S^{r}(\cdot)
sets,
the sampling probabilities of
S^{r}(q)
and
S^{l}(q)
for the same
q
are identical, i.e.,
p(q)
. Consequently, the random variables corresponding to the two sets are not independent.

While the high-level intuition remains the same, the technical analysis circumvents these issues through a carefully defined random process, as outlined in Section
4.2.2
. This approach enables us to work with variables that are
better behaved
than
S^{r}(\cdot)
and
p(\cdot)
for analyzing the algorithm. An interesting direction for future work would be to simplify the analysis, potentially avoiding reliance on the random process.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: We present, in Section
2
, the notion of derivation trees, which is crucial for our analysis. We present the algorithm in Section
3
and analyze the correctness and runtime complexity of the algorithm in Section
4
.

Derivation Trees

Let
q
be a node in a
(+,\times)
program and let
\alpha\in\mathit{supp}(q)
. Recall that the nodes of the program are totally ordered by
\prec
. We associate the pair
(\alpha,q)
with a unique derivation tree rooted at q and denoted by
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)
. The tree is defined recursively as follows:

•

If
q
is an input node, then
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)=q
.

•

If
q
is a
+

q^{\prime}
be the first child of
q
with respect to
\prec
such that
\alpha\in\mathit{supp}(q^{\prime})
. Then,
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)=(q,\{\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q^{\prime})\})

•

If
q
is a
\times
node
q_{1}\times q_{2}
then we have
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
such that
\alpha_{1}\in\mathit{supp}(q_{1})
and
\alpha_{2}\in\mathit{supp}(q_{2})
. Then,
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)=(q,\{\mathit{tree}(\alpha_{1},q_{1}),\mathit{tree}(\alpha_{2},q_{2})\})

Each derivation tree
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)
in a degree
n
multilinear program has
|\alpha|
leaves, so at most
n
leaves. We claim that
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)
has at most
4n
nodes in total.
\times
nodes have fan-in of
2
in
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)
and
+
nodes have fan-in of
1
in
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)
so, replacing
+
nodes by edges in
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)
yields a binary tree
T
with at most
n
leaves and whose internal nodes are the
\times
nodes. So at most
n
nodes of the derivation tree are
\times
nodes. By assumption on
\mathcal{P}
, there can only be one
+
node between two
\times
nodes or between a
\times
node and a leaf. So the number of
+
nodes in
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)
is at most the number of edges in
T
, so at most
2n
. Thus,
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)
has at most
4n
nodes in total.

It is worth observing that
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)\sqcap\mathit{tree}(\alpha^{\prime},q^{\prime})
forms an antichain, i.e., it does not contain two nodes such that one is an ancestor of the other. Let

program. We define

Lemma 4
.

For every
\alpha\in\mathit{supp}(q)
and antichain
\tau
over nodes of
\mathit{tree}(\alpha,q)
,
|I(\alpha,q,\tau)|\leq\frac{|\mathit{supp}(q)|}{\prod_{\hat{q}\in\tau}|\mathit{supp}(\hat{q})|}
, where
\prod_{\hat{q}\in\tau}|\mathit{supp}(\hat{q})|=1
when
\tau=\emptyset
.

Proof.

The lemma is clear when
\tau=\emptyset
. Let
\mathit{var}(\tau)=\bigcup_{\hat{q}\in\tau}\mathit{var}(\hat{q})
. In a derivation trees, if two nodes
\hat{q}
and
\hat{q}^{\prime}
are such that none is not the ancestor of the other, then
\mathit{var}(\hat{q})\cap\mathit{var}(\hat{q}^{\prime})=\emptyset
. So, since
\tau
is an antichain over a derivation trees, it holds that
\mathit{var}(\hat{q})\cap\mathit{var}(\hat{q}^{\prime})=\emptyset
for every
\hat{q}^{\prime}\neq\hat{q}
in
\tau
.

Let
I(\alpha,q,\tau)=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots\}
. For every node
\hat{q}\in\tau
let
\alpha_{\hat{q}}
be the restriction of
\alpha
to
\mathit{var}(\hat{q})
, let
\alpha_{i,\hat{q}}
be the restriction of
\alpha_{i}
to
\mathit{var}(\hat{q})
. Let also
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}
be the restriction of
\alpha_{i}
to
\mathit{var}(q)\setminus\mathit{var}(\tau)
. Note that
\alpha_{i}=\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\prod_{\hat{q}\in\tau}\alpha_{i,\hat{q}}
. From the definition of
I(\alpha,q,\tau)
, we have that, for every
\hat{q}\in\tau
and every
i
,
\alpha_{\hat{q},i}=\alpha_{\hat{q}}
. Thus, for every
i\neq j
, we have
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\neq\alpha^{\prime}_{j}
.

Now, for any
\hat{q}\in\tau
and any
\beta_{\hat{q}}\in\mathit{supp}(\hat{q})
, if we replace
\alpha_{\hat{q}}
by
\beta_{\hat{q}}
in
\alpha_{i}
, then we still have a monomial in
\mathit{supp}(q)
. This can be done for all
\hat{q}
, so each
\alpha_{i}
is used to create monomials of the form
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\prod_{\hat{q}\in\tau}\beta_{\hat{q}}
(
\alpha_{i}
itself is of that form). If two monomials
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\prod_{\hat{q}\in\tau}\beta_{\hat{q}}
and
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\prod_{\hat{q}\in\tau}\beta^{\prime}_{\hat{q}}
are such that

for some

then the two monomials are distinct since their restrictions to
\mathit{var}(\hat{q})
are precisely

and
\beta^{\prime}_{\hat{q}}
, respectively. So we create

different monomials from

, all in

.

For

, the monomials

and

are distinct since their restrictions to

are
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}
and

, respectively. So in total can create

. Hence
|I(\alpha,q,\tau)|\leq|\mathit{supp}(q)|/\prod_{\hat{q}\in\tau}|\mathit{supp}(\hat{q})|
.

Algorithm.

Our goal is to compute a good approximation of
|\mathit{supp}(\mathcal{P})|
. Let
n
be the degree of
\mathcal{P}
. Our algorithm constructs in a bottom-up manner
p(q)
, which seeks to estimate
16n/|\mathit{supp}(q)|
, and samples from
\mathit{supp}(q)
for every node
q
of
\mathcal{P}
. In fact, for every
q
we sample several sample sets
S^{1}(q),\dots,S^{n_{s}n_{t}}(q)
, all subsets of
\mathit{supp}(q)
, where the numbers
n_{s}
and
n_{t}
are given in algorithm
counter
.
S^{r}(q)
and
p(q)
are computed using
\mathsf{reduce}
, thus they are random variables. We estimate
16n/|\mathit{supp}(q)|
rather than
1/|\mathit{supp}(q)|
for technical reasons. The sets
S^{r}(q)
are sampled in such a way that, ideally,
\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)]=p(q)
holds for every
\alpha\in\mathit{supp}(q)
once the value of
p(q)
is known. Thus, if
p(q)
is a good estimate of
16n/|\mathit{supp}(q)|
, then each set
S^{r}(q)
should contain around
16n
monomials in expectation.

The main part of the algorithm is
countCore
.
countCore
has access to all estimates
p(q)
and all sample sets
S^{r}(q)
and processes the nodes of
\mathcal{P}
in height-first order (so a node is processed after its children). Processing a node
q
means computing a value for
p(q)
and constructing all sets
S^{1}(q),\dots,S^{n_{s}n_{t}}(q)
.

The first nodes verify
|\mathit{supp}(q)|\leq 16n|\mathcal{P}|^{2}
, i.e., their effective height is
0
. They are handled in a particular way. The algorithm starts by listing a polynomial amount of monomials of
\mathit{supp}(q)
. If the enumeration lists fewer than
16n|\mathcal{P}|^{2}+1
monomials, then we know that
q\in Q^{0}
and we have found
\mathit{supp}(q)
. So we set
p(q)
to
\min(1,16n/|\mathit{supp}(q)|)
and
S^{r}(q)
to
\mathsf{reduce}(\mathit{supp}(q),p(q))
; this ensures that
\Pr[\alpha\in S^{r}(q)]=p(q)
(lines
5
to
8
in

|\mathit{supp}(q)|<16n

as both an estimate of
16n/|\mathit{supp}(q)|
and a probability. Hence our choice to set
p(q)=\min(1,16n/|\mathit{supp}(q)|)
.

When the enumeration of the support finds that

\textup{{countCore}}^{*}

A.1
Proof of Proposition

See

Proof.

The core idea is to construct a program that directly mirrors the structure of derivations in a Chomsky Normal Form (CNF) grammar. The construction is a dynamic programming algorithm, building up the language of derivable strings of length
i
from the languages of strings of lengths less than
i
.

We construct a program node, let’s call it
q_{A,i}
, for each non-terminal
A\in V
and each length
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. The goal is for each node
q_{A,i}
to represent the language
L_{i}(A)
, which is the set of all words of length exactly
i
that can be derived from
A
.

The construction proceeds by mapping the two types of CNF production rules to the operations of the
(\cup,\cdot)
algebra:

1.

Terminal Productions (
A\to a
):
A string of length 1 can be derived from a non-terminal
A
if there is a rule of the form
A\to a
. Since there may be multiple such rules for a given
A
(e.g.,
A\to a
and
A\to b
), the total set of length-1 strings derivable from
A
is the
union
of the outcomes of all its applicable terminal productions. We therefore define the node
q_{A,1}
as the union of all terminals
a
for which the rule
A\to a
exists.

2.

Non-terminal Productions (
A\to BC
):
Deriving a string of length
i>1
from
A
involves both concatenation and union operations, stemming from different sources of choice in the derivation.

•

Concatenation:
Any single rule
A\to BC
implies that a word derivable from
A
is formed by the
concatenation
of a word from
B
and a word from
C
. This maps directly to the program’s concatenation (
\cdot
) operation.

•

Union:
The union operation (
\cup
) arises from two distinct choices in the derivation process:

(a)

Choice of Length Split:
For a single rule
A\to BC
, a word of length
i
can be formed by concatenating a word of length
k
from
B
and a word of length
i-k
from
C
. Since this split can occur in multiple ways (i.e., for any
k
from
1
to
i-1
), the total language generated by this single rule is the
union
over all possible length splits.

(b)

Choice of Production Rule:
A given non-terminal
A
can typically be the left-hand side of multiple production rules (e.g.,
A\to B_{1}C_{1}
,
A\to B_{2}C_{2}
, etc.). The complete language
L_{i}(A)
is the set of words derivable from
any
of these rules. Therefore, we must take the
union
of the languages generated by each individual rule.

Combining these observations, our program constructs each node
q_{A,i}
(for
i>1
) by implementing a nested union: an
outer
union over all production rules with
A
on the left-hand side, and an
inner
union over all possible length splits for each rule’s concatenated sub-problems.

Since this bottom-up construction perfectly mimics the definition of a derivation in CNF for a fixed length, the node
q_{A,i}
will represent the language
L_{i}(A)
. The final output of the program is the node
q_{S,n}
, which represents the set of words of length
n
derivable from the start symbol
S
, which is exactly
L_{n}(G)
.

(The special case for
n=0
is handled separately: if
S\to\varepsilon
is a rule,
L_{0}(G)=\{\varepsilon\}
; otherwise, it is
\emptyset
. The program can represent these trivial languages directly.)
∎

Note that the premises of
\alpha\in\mathsf{reduce}(\mathit{supp}(q),p)
occur with probability one as we assume that
\alpha
is in
\mathit{supp}(q)
. Since all contain-events are reduce-events, the
premises
of a contain-event are well-defined. For
E
a contain-event for
q
,
\mathit{premises}(E)
is the set of its premises.
E
occurs only if its premises occur; formally,

\Pr[E]=\Pr[E\mid\mathit{premises}(E)]\Pr[\mathit{premises}(E)].

Next, let
\mathit{premises}^{*}(E)
, the
extended premises of
E
, be defined as
\mathit{premises}^{*}(E)=\mathit{premises}(E)
when
q\in Q^{0}
, and as
\mathit{premises}^{*}(E)=\mathit{premises}(E)\cup\bigcup_{E^{\prime}\in\mathit{premises}(E)}\mathit{premises}^{*}(E^{\prime})
otherwise. In words,
\mathit{premises}^{*}(E)
contains the premises of
E
, and the premises of its premises, and so on. We have that

\Pr[\mathit{premises}(E)]=\Pr[\mathit{premises}^{*}(E)].

Let
\mathit{premises}^{*}[E]=\{E\}\cup\mathit{premises}^{*}(E)
. Finally, for
\mathcal{E}
a set of contain-events, let
\mathit{premises}^{*}(\mathcal{E})=\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\mathit{premises}^{*}(\mathcal{E})
and
\mathit{premises}^{*}[\mathcal{E}]=\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\mathit{premises}^{*}[E]
.

Say
E\,:\,\alpha\in\mathfrak{S}_{h,t}(q)=\mathsf{reduce}(\mathcal{Z},p)
for some (random) set
\mathcal{Z}
and some probability
p
. If the premises of
E
are known to hold then
\alpha\in\mathcal{Z}
is known and the occurence of
E
boils down to a probability-
p
coin toss to determine whether
\alpha
is reduced away. No other contain-event can influence that particular coin toss except those whose (extended) premises force the result of that coin toss. Formally, let
\mathcal{E}
be a set of contain-events such that
E\not\in\mathit{premises}^{*}(E^{\prime})
for any
E^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}
, then
E
is independent of
\mathcal{E}
when conditioned on
\mathit{premises}(E)
:

\Pr[E\text{ and }\mathcal{E}\mid\mathit{premises}(E)]=\Pr[E\mid\mathit{premises}(E)]\Pr[\mathcal{E}\mid\mathit{premises}(E)]

As a consequence, the event
\alpha\in\mathfrak{S}_{h,t}(q)
is decomposable in a chain-rule manner as a product of probabilities of reduce-events conditioned on their premises, with one event per node of the derivation tree
\mathit{tree}^{*}(\alpha,q)
. Informally, the following lemma says that
\alpha\in\mathfrak{S}_{h,t}(q)
occurs if and only if all independent coin-tosses in the
\mathsf{reduce}
procedures required to propagate the samples that compose
\alpha

\mathit{tree}^{*}(\alpha,q)
are successful.

Lemma 25
.

For
\mathcal{E}
a set of contain-events we have

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]=\prod\limits_{E\in\mathit{premises}^{*}[\mathcal{E}]}\Pr[E\mid\mathit{premises}(E)].

Proof.

Let
maxHeight(\mathcal{E})
be the largest effective height of nodes targeted by contain-events in
\mathcal{E}
.
We proceed by induction on
maxHeight(\mathcal{E})
. If
maxHeight(\mathcal{E})=0
then
\mathcal{E}
has only events of the form
\alpha\in\mathsf{reduce}(\mathit{supp}(q),p)
that are mutually independent and whose premises
\alpha\in\mathit{supp}(q)
occur with probability
1
. Formally,
\Pr[\alpha\in\mathsf{reduce}(\mathit{supp}(q),p)]=\Pr[\alpha\in\mathsf{reduce}(\mathit{supp}(q),p)\mid\alpha\in\mathit{supp}(q)]\Pr[\alpha\in\mathit{supp}(q)]=\Pr[\alpha\in\mathsf{reduce}(\mathit{supp}(q),p)\mid\alpha\in\mathit{supp}(q)]
and

\Pr[\mathcal{E}]=\prod_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\Pr[E\mid\mathit{premises}(E)]

and the claim follows from
\mathit{premises}^{*}[E]=\{E\}
for contain-events of effective height
0
. Now suppose the claim holds for all sets
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
with
maxHeight(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})<i
(with
i>0
) and suppose
maxHeight(\mathcal{E})=i
. Let
E_{1},\dots,E_{k}
be the events of effective height
i
in
\mathcal{E}
.
E_{1}
occurs only if its premises occur:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\mathcal{E}\cup\mathit{premises}(E_{1})]=\Pr[E_{1},\,\mathcal{E}\setminus\{E_{1}\}\mid\mathit{premises}(E_{1})]\cdot\Pr[\mathit{premises}(E_{1})]

Since
E_{1}
has maximum effective height in
\mathcal{E}
, it cannot be an extended premises of any event in
\mathcal{E}\setminus\{E_{1}\}
, hence

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle=\Pr[E_{1}\mid\mathit{premises}(E_{1})]\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{E}\setminus\{E_{1}\}\mid\mathit{premises}(E_{1})]\Pr[\mathit{premises}(E_{1})]

\displaystyle=\Pr[E_{1}\mid\mathit{premises}(E_{1})]\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{E}\setminus\{E_{1}\}\,\mathit{premises}(E_{1})].

Repeating this argument with
E_{2},E_{3},\dots,E_{k}
gives

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{E}]

\displaystyle=\Pr[E_{1}\mid\mathit{premises}(E_{1})]\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{E}\setminus\{E_{1}\},\,\mathit{premises}(E_{1})]

\displaystyle=\Pr[E_{1}\mid\mathit{premises}(E_{1})]\Pr[E_{2}\mid\mathit{premises}(E_{2})]\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{E}\setminus\{E_{1},\,E_{2}\},\,\mathit{premises}(E_{1}),\,\mathit{premises}(E_{2})]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\mathcal{E}\setminus\{E_{1},\dots,E_{k}\},\,\mathit{premises}(E_{1}),\dots,\mathit{premises}(E_{k})]\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Pr[E_{j}\mid\mathit{premises}(E_{j})]

Let
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\mathcal{E}\setminus\{E_{1},\dots,E_{k}\}\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\mathit{premises}(E_{j})
. By induction,

\Pr[\mathcal{E}^{\prime}]=\prod_{E\in\mathit{premises}^{*}[\mathcal{E}^{\prime}]}\Pr[E\mid\mathit{premises}(E)]
. The proof is finished by observing that

\displaystyle\mathit{premises}^{*}[\mathcal{E}^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\mathit{premises}^{*}[\mathcal{E}\setminus\{E_{1},\dots,E_{k}\}]\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\mathit{premises}^{*}[\mathit{premises}(E_{j})]

\displaystyle=\mathit{premises}^{*}[\mathcal{E}\setminus\{E_{1},\dots,E_{k}\}]\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\mathit{premises}^{*}[E_{j}]\setminus\{E_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\mathit{premises}^{*}[\mathcal{E}]\setminus\{E_{1},\dots,E_{k}\}
