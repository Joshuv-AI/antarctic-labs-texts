1.
Introduction

A natural generalization of conjugacy is twisted conjugacy and the study of this can be dated back to
1939
by the work of Gantmacher (see
[
Gan39
]
).
Let
G
be a group and
\varphi
an automorphism of
G
. Two elements
x,y\in G
are said to be
\varphi
-twisted conjugate, denoted by
x\sim_{\varphi}y
, if
y=gx\varphi(g)^{-1}
for some
g\in G
. Clearly,
\sim_{\varphi}
is an equivalence relation on
G
. The equivalence classes with respect to this relation are called the
\varphi
-twisted conjugacy classes
or
the Reidemeister classes
of
\varphi
. If
\varphi=\mathrm{Id}
, then the
\varphi
-twisted conjugacy classes are the (ordinary) conjugacy classes. The
\varphi
-twisted conjugacy class containing
x\in G
is denoted by
[x]_{\varphi}
or simply
[x]
, when
\varphi
is clear from the context. The Reidemeister number of
\varphi
, denoted by
R(\varphi)
, is the number of all
\varphi
-twisted conjugacy classes.
This number is either a natural number or
\infty
.
A group
G
is said to have the
R_{\infty}
-property
if
R(\varphi)
is infinite for every automorphism
\varphi
of
G
.

The Reidemeister number is closely related to the Nielsen number of a self-map of a manifold. More precisely, let
M
be a closed connected manifold of dimension at least
3
and
f:M\to M
a continuous map, the minimal number of fixed points among all continuous maps homotopic to
f
is the Nielsen number
N(f)
. Note that the Nielsen number
N(f)
is always finite and is homotopy invariant. The computation of
N(f)
is difficult and a central object to study in the Nielsen-Reidemeister fixed point theory (cf.
[
Fel10
]
). This number is bounded above by the Reidemeister number
R(f_{\#})
, where
f_{\#}
is the induced map on
\pi_{1}(M)
. If
M
is a Jiang-type space, then either
N(f)=0
or
N(f)=R(f_{\#})
(we refer the reader to
[
Jia83
, p. 37, Theorem 5.6]
for details). Thus, in this scenario, if

R(f_{\#})=\infty
then
N(f)=0
which in turn implies that one can deform
f
, up to homotopy, to a fixed-point free map.

The class of groups which have the
R_{\infty}
-property is a robust and active area of research begun by Fel’shtyn and Hill in
[
FH94
]
. The problem has a long history of references, for example, see
[
Fel10
,
FT15
,
DLGO21
,
DS21
]
and the references therein.
In the context of algebraic groups, Steinberg proved that
for a connected linear algebraic group
G
over an algebraically closed field
k
and a surjective endomorphism
\varphi
of
G, if the fixed-point subgroup
G^{\varphi}
is finite then
R(\varphi)=1
, and hence
G
does not satisfy the
R_{\infty}
-property (see
[
Ste68
, Theorem 10.1]
). The above result is classically known as the
Lang-Steinberg theorem
. Recently, Bose and the first author of this paper proved that a connected linear algebraic group
G
has the (algebraic)
R_{\infty}
-property if and only if the fixed-point subgroup
G^{\varphi}
is infinite for every automorphism
\varphi
of
G
(cf.
[
BB20
, Theorem 17]
,
[
BB21
, Theorem 2.4]
).
In the realm of geometric group theory, in
[
LL00
,
Fel01
]
the authors proved that the non-elementary (Gromov) hyperbolic groups have the
R_{\infty}
-property. Further, non-elementary relatively hyperbolic groups were shown to have this property by Fel’shtyn (see
[
Fel10
, Theorem 3.3 (2)]
).

The motivation of the present work comes from the results of Fel’shtyn and Gonçalves
[
FG10
]
.
The
mapping class group

\mathrm{MCG}(S)
of a surface
S
is the group of isotopy classes of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of
S
.
They proved that the mapping class group
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
of an orientable closed surface
S
has the
R_{\infty}
-property if and only if
S
is not
\mathbb{S}^{2}
(see
[
FG10
, Theorem 4.3]
). In the Appendix of that paper, Dahmani et al. proved that, except for a few low complexity cases, the mapping class group of a compact surface has the
R_{\infty}
-property, using geometric group theoretic methods.
Recently, there has been a surge of activities on infinite-type surfaces and corresponding groups, namely the big mapping class groups (see
[
AV20
]
).

The goal of this paper is to exhibit a similarity between the finite-type and infinite-type settings.
Now, let
S
be an infinite-type surface (i.e.
S
is a connected, second countable, oriented
2
-manifold without boundary such that the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(S)
is not finitely generated) and
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
denote the big mapping class group of
S
.
As opposed to the above mentioned results (mostly algebraic in nature) our approach will be significantly more geometric. The technique from the Appendix of
[
FG10
]
can be modified suitably in our set-up to show that the big mapping class group
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
does satisfy the
R_{\infty}
-property under some restrictions.
A finite-type connected subsurface
\Sigma\subset S
is called
non-displaceable
if
f(\Sigma)\cap\Sigma\neq\emptyset
for all
f\in\mathrm{Homeo}(S)
. One of the main results of this paper is the following:

Theorem

(Theorem
3.4
)
.

If
S
is a connected orientable infinite-type surface without boundary containing a non-displaceable subsurface, then
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
has the
R_{\infty}
-property.

Using the Birman exact sequence, we prove that if
S
is an infinite-type surface with an isolated puncture, then the big mapping class group
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
possesses the
R_{\infty}
-property (see Corollary
3.7
). For details, see Section
2.2
and Section
3
.
We also prove the following.

Theorem

(Theorem
3.11
)
.

Let
S
be an infinite-type surface such that the space of ends
\mathrm{Ends}(S)
is a Cantor set
C
with the following: either (a)
\mathrm{genus}(S)<\infty
or (b)
\mathrm{Ends}(S)=\mathrm{Ends}^{np}(S)
.
Then
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
possesses the
R_{\infty}
-property.

As an immediate application of the above results, we characterize the
S_{\infty}
-property of the mapping class groups. For necessary definitions and results, we refer to Section
4
. In particular, we prove the following result in this direction.

Theorem

(Theorem
4.3
)
.

Let
S
be a connected orientable surface without boundary. Then
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
has the
R_{\infty}
-property if and only if
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
has the
S_{\infty}
-property.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we fix some notations and terminologies and collect some basic results which will be used throughout this paper.

2.1.
Infinite-type surfaces

Let
S
be a connected, orientable surface (
2
-dimensional manifold) without boundary. Then

S
is said to be of
finite-type
if its fundamental group is finitely generated; otherwise,
S
is said to be of
infinite-type
.
Note that whenever the surface
S
is of infinite-type the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(S)
of
S
is a free group of countable infinite rank (see, for instance,
[
Sti93
, p. 142, Theorem 4.2.2]
). Finite-type surfaces (without boundary) are classified by the genus and number of punctures. We need more information to classify infinite-type surfaces, which we will discuss here. Let
S
be an infinite-type surface.
An
exiting sequence
is a collection

U_{1}\supseteq U_{2}\supseteq\cdots
of connected open subsets of
S
such that

(1)

For any
n
,
U_{n}
is not relatively compact.

(2)

The boundary of
U_{n}
is compact for all
n
.

(3)

Every relatively compact subset of
S
is disjoint from
U_{n}
for all but
finitely many
n
.

Two exiting sequences are
equivalent
if every element of the
first sequence is contained in some element of the second, and vice-versa.
An
end of
S
is an equivalence class of exiting sequences, and we
write
\mathrm{Ends}(S)
for the space of ends of
S
.
Note that
\mathrm{Ends}(S)
is a
totally disconnected, separable and compact space. In particular, it is a subset of a Cantor set. We refer to
[
AV20
, Section 2.1]
for the topology of the space of ends.
An end of
S
is called
planar
if it has a representative exiting sequence
whose elements are eventually planar; otherwise it is said to be
non-planar
(or
accumulated by genus
).
We denote by
\mathrm{Ends}^{p}(S)
and
\mathrm{Ends}^{np}(S)
the subspaces of planar
and non-planar ends of
S
, respectively. Clearly,
\mathrm{Ends}(S)=\mathrm{Ends}^{p}(S)\sqcup\mathrm{Ends}^{np}(S)
.
Infinite-type surfaces without boundary have been classified by the following (due to Kerékjártó
[
Ker23
]
and Richards
[
Ric63
]
):
two connected orientable surfaces
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are homeomorphic if and only if they have the same genus,
and there exists a homeomorphism
h:\mathrm{Ends}(S_{1})\rightarrow\mathrm{Ends}(S_{2})
whose restriction to
\mathrm{Ends}^{np}(S_{1})
defines a homeomorphism
between
\mathrm{Ends}^{np}(S_{1})
and
\mathrm{Ends}^{np}(S_{2})
. Note that
\text{genus}(S)<\infty
if and only if
\mathrm{Ends}^{np}(S)=\emptyset
.

2.2.
Mapping class groups

For a surface
S
, let
\mathrm{Homeo}(S)
denote the group of homeomorphisms of
S
. The subgroup of
\mathrm{Homeo}(S)
consisting of homeomorphisms which are isotopic to the identity is denoted by
\mathrm{Homeo}_{0}(S)
(this is also the connected component of the identity with respect to the compact-open topology). Let
\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}(S)
(resp.
\mathrm{Homeo}^{-}(S)
) denote the set of all orientation-preserving (resp. orientation-reversing) homeomorphisms of
S
.
The (extended)
mapping class group
of
S
, denoted by
\mathrm{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
, is the group of isotopy classes of homeomorphisms of
S
, i.e.
\mathrm{MCG}^{\pm}(S)=\mathrm{Homeo}(S)/\mathrm{Homeo}_{0}(S)
. Equivalently, it is defined as
\pi_{0}(\mathrm{Homeo}(S))
with respect to the compact open topology. Now
\mathrm{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
acts naturally by homeomorphisms on the space of ends
\mathrm{Ends}(S)
, and the kernel is called the pure mapping class group, denoted by
\mathrm{PMCG}^{\pm}(S)
. Both of these groups have subgroups of index
2
, denoted by
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
and
\mathrm{PMCG}(S)
respectively, consisting of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms.
Notice that
\mathrm{MCG}(S)=\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}(S)/\mathrm{Homeo}_{0}(S).
When the surface is of infinite-type, the mapping class group is homeomorphic to the Baire space, i.e.
\mathrm{MCG}(S)\cong\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
. We call the mapping class group as
big mapping class group
if the surface is of infinite-type. We define a particular type of element in
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
which will be used later. Let
A=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]
be the annulus. Define the twist map
T:A\rightarrow A
given by
T(\theta,t)=(\theta+2\pi t,t)
for all
(\theta,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]
. Let
\alpha
be an essential simple closed curve in
S
(i.e.
\alpha
is an embedding of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, not homotopic to a point or a puncture). Fix a regular neighbourhood
N
of
\alpha
and an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
\psi:A\rightarrow N
. Now define
T_{\alpha}:S\rightarrow S
by

T_{\alpha}(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi\circ T\circ\psi^{-1}(s)&\text{if}\;s\in N\\
s&\text{if}\;s\in S\setminus N.\end{array}\right.

The map
T_{\alpha}
is called a
Dehn twist about
\alpha
. Throughout the paper, we will use left Dehn twist. Note that a Dehn twist neither depends on
N
nor on
\alpha
(up to isotopy). It is an infinite order element of
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
. Now we have the following basic result. For details, we refer the reader to
[
FM12
, Chapter 3]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
FM12
, Section 3.3]
Let
T_{\alpha}
be a Dehn twist about
\alpha
and
f\in\mathrm{MCG}(S)
, and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the following hold:

(1)

fT_{\alpha}f^{-1}=T_{f(\alpha)}
.

(2)

T_{\alpha}^{n}=T_{\beta}^{m}
if and only if
\alpha=\beta
and
m=n
.

2.3.
Some basic results

To determine the
R_{\infty}
-property we have to understand the automorphism group of
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
(resp.
\mathrm{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
). Following the work of Bavard et. al.
[
BDR20
]
we will briefly describe the automorphism group of big mapping class groups.

Automorphisms:

For a group
G
, let
\textup{Aut}(G)
denote the group of all automorphisms of
G
, and
\mathrm{Inn}(G):=\{i_{g}\mid i_{g}(x)=gxg^{-1}\;\forall x\in G\}
, the normal subgroup of
\textup{Aut}(G)
consisting of only the inner automorphisms of
G
. The outer automorphism group of
G
, is denoted by
\mathrm{Out}(G):=\textup{Aut}(G)/\mathrm{Inn}(G)
. A subgroup
H
of
G
is called
characteristic
if
\varphi(H)=H
for all
\varphi\in\textup{Aut}(G)
.
By virtue of
[
BDR20
, Theorem 1.1]
, for every
\varphi\in\textup{Aut}(\mathrm{MCG}^{\pm}(S))
there exists
h\in\mathrm{Homeo}(S)
such that
\varphi(f)=hfh^{-1}
, for all
f\in\mathrm{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
. That is every automorphism of
\mathrm{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
is inner.
Now let
\varphi\in\textup{Aut}(\mathrm{MCG}(S))
. Since
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
is an index two subgroup of
\mathrm{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
again by
[
BDR20
, Theorem 1.1]
, there exists
h\in\mathrm{Homeo}(S)
such that
\varphi(f)=hfh^{-1}
. Now there are two possibilities for
h
: (a)
h\in\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}(S)
, or (b)
h\in\mathrm{Homeo}^{-}(S)
.

(a) If
h\in\mathrm{Homeo}^{+}(S)
, then
\varphi(f)=hfh^{-1}=i_{h}(f)
, i.e.
\varphi
is an inner automorphism of
\mathrm{MCG}(S)
.

(b) If
h\in\mathrm{Homeo}^{-}(S)
, then

\varphi^{2}(f)=\varphi(hfh^{-1})=h^{2}fh^{-2}=i_{h^{2}}(f)
. Thus
\varphi^{2}=i_{h^{2}}\in\mathrm{Inn}(\mathrm{MCG}(S))
.

Therefore
\overline{\varphi}
is an element of order
1
or
2
in
\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{MCG}(S))
, where
\textup{Aut}(\mathrm{MCG}(S))\rightarrow\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{MCG}(S))
given by
\varphi\mapsto\overline{\varphi}
. Since
\varphi
is an arbitrary automorphism, we have
\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{MCG}(S))\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\cong\langle\overline{\psi}\mid\psi:f\mapsto hfh^{-1},h\in\mathrm{Homeo}^{-}(S)\rangle
. We summarize this as follows:

Lemma 2.2
.

[
BDR20
, Theorem 1.1, Corollary 1.2]

If
S
is an infinite-type surface then the following hold:

(1)

\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{MCG}^{\pm}(S))=\{e\}
.

(2)

\mathrm{Out}(\mathrm{MCG}(S))\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.

(3)

If
G<\mathrm{MCG}(S)
is a finite index subgroup then
\mathrm{Out}(G)
is finite.

(4)

\mathrm{MCG}(S),\mathrm{PMCG}(S)
and
\mathrm{PMCG}^{\pm}(S)
are characteristic subgroups in
\mathrm{MCG}^{\pm}(S)
.

Suppose that
1\rightarrow N\overset{i}{\rightarrow}G\overset{\pi}{\rightarrow}Q\rightarrow 1
is an exact sequence of arbitrary groups. If
Q
is finite and
N
has infinitely many conjugacy classes, then we
claim
that
G
also has infinitely many conjugacy classes.
If
G
has finitely many conjugacy classes then we will arrive at a contradiction. Since
N
has infinitely many conjugacy classes there exist infinitely many elements
n_{r}\in N
(
r\geq 0
) such that
n_{r}
and
n_{s}
are not conjugate in
N
(
r\neq s
) but
n_{0}
and
n_{r}
are conjugate in
G
for all
r\geq 0
. So for all
r\geq 1
there exists
g_{r}\in G
such that
n_{r}=g_{r}n_{0}g_{r}^{-1}
.
Since
Q(\cong G/N)
is finite there exist distinct
r,s\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g_{s}g_{r}^{-1}\in N
. Therefore
n_{s}=g_{s}n_{0}g_{s}^{-1}=g_{s}g_{r}^{-1}n_{r}g_{r}g_{s}^{-1}=(g_{s}g_{r}^{-1})n_{r}(g_{s}g_{r}^{-1})^{-1}
. This is a contradiction to the assumption that
n_{r}
and
n_{s}
are not conjugate in
N
. We record this elementary observation as follows:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
1\rightarrow N\rightarrow G\rightarrow Q\rightarrow 1
be an exact sequence of groups. If
Q
is finite and
N
has infinitely many conjugacy classes then so does
G
.

The above lemma holds in more generality (see,
[
MS14
, Lemma 2.2(ii)]
). Next, we collect the following two important results:

Lemma 2.4
.

[
MS14
, Lemma 2.1]

Let
1\rightarrow N\rightarrow G\rightarrow Q\rightarrow 1
be an exact sequence of groups. Suppose that
N
is a characteristic subgroup of
G
and
Q
has the
R_{\infty}
-property. Then
G
also has the
R_{\infty}
-property.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
FLT08
, Corollary 3.2]

Let
\varphi\in\textup{Aut}(G)
and
i_{g}\in\mathrm{Inn}(G)
. Then
R(\varphi\circ i_{g})=R(\varphi)
. In particular,
R(i_{g})=R(\mathrm{Id})
, i.e. the number of inner twisted conjugacy classes in
G
is equal to the number of conjugacy classes in
G
.

Some geometric results:

Let
G
be a group acting on a metric space
(X,d)
by isometries. Let
x\in X
and
g\in G
. We
claim
that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(x,g^{n}x)}{n}
exists. Suppose that
f:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
is a function given by
f(n)=d(x,g^{n}x)
. Clearly,
f(n+m)=d(x,g^{m+n}x)\leq d(x,g^{m}x)+d(x,g^{n}x)=f(m)+f(n)
, for any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,
f
is subadditive.
Now, let
M=\inf\frac{f(n)}{n}
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
n>0
such that
\frac{f(n)}{n}<M+\epsilon
. So, for every
m>n
, we have
m=kn+r
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq r<n
. So,
\frac{f(m)}{m}=\frac{f(kn+r)}{m}\leq\frac{f(kn)}{m}+\frac{f(r)}{m}\leq\frac{kn(M+\epsilon)}{m}+\frac{f(r)}{m}
.
Therefore
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(x,g^{n}x)}{n}=M
. This proves the claim. For instance, see
[
BH99
, Chapter II.6, Exercise 6.6(1)]
.

The
asymptotic translation length of

g\in G
is given by
\tau(g):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(x,g^{n}x)}{n}
. This in itself is of independent interest in geometric group theory. We have the following basic result:

Lemma 2.6
.

(1)

\tau(g)
does not depend on the choice of
x
.

(2)

\tau(g)=\tau(h^{-1}gh)
for all
g,h\in G
.

(3)

\tau(g^{k})=|k|\tau(g)
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
y\in X
be a point distinct from
x
. Then, we have

d(x,g^{n}x)-d(x,y)-d(g^{n}x,g^{n}y)\leq d(y,g^{n}y)\leq d(y,x)+d(x,g^{n}x)+d(g^{n}x,g^{n}y)
.

This implies
\frac{d(x,g^{n}x)}{n}-2\frac{d(x,y)}{n}\leq\frac{d(y,g^{n}y)}{n}\leq\frac{d(x,g^{n}x)}{n}+2\frac{d(x,y)}{n}
.

Thus, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(x,g^{n}x)}{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(y,g^{n}y)}{n}
.

(2)

For any
g,h\in G
, we have

\tau(h^{-1}gh)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(x,(h^{-1}gh)^{n}x)}{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(x,h^{-1}g^{n}hx)}{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(hx,g^{n}(hx))}{n}.

Then by
(1)
,
\tau(h^{-1}gh)=\tau(g)
.

(3)

Assume that
k>0
, then

\tau(g^{k})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(x,(g^{k})^{n}x)}{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{kd(x,g^{nk}x)}{nk}=k\lim_{nk\to\infty}\frac{d(x,g^{nk}x)}{nk}=k\tau(g).

Similarly one can check this for
k<0
. Hence we have the result.

∎

The above result immediately provides a sufficient condition for a group to have infinitely many conjugacy classes (cf.
[
Gro87
, Corollary 8.2.G]
). Recall that an isometry
g
is called
loxodromic
if
\tau(g)>0
.

Corollary 2.7
.

If a metric space
(X,d)
admits an isometric
G
-action such that
\tau(g)>0
, then the number of conjugacy classes of
G
is infinite.

Proof.

First, note that the order of
g
is infinite because
\tau(g)>0
. Now look at the infinite sequence
\{g^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of distinct elements of
G
. By Lemma
2.6

g^{i}
is not conjugate to
g^{j}
, for
i\neq j
. Thus,
G
has infinitely many conjugacy classes.
∎

In order to describe the next set of results we fix some notations.
Let
\varphi\in\textup{Aut}(G)
and
\mathbb{Z}=\langle t\rangle
. Define the following extension of
G
containing
G
as a normal subgroup:

G_{\varphi}:=\langle G,t\mid tgt^{-1}=\varphi(g)\;\forall g\rangle=G\rtimes_{\varphi}\mathbb{Z}=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}G\cdot t^{n}.

Now, if
x
and
y
are
\varphi
-twisted conjugate in
G
then
y=gx\varphi(g^{-1})
for some
g\in G
. Therefore
y=gxtg^{-1}t^{-1}
in
G_{\varphi}
which implies that
yt=g(xt)g^{-1}
, i.e.
xt
and
yt
are conjugate in
G_{\varphi}
.
Conversely, suppose that
xt
and
yt
are conjugate in
G_{\varphi}
. Then there exists an element
gt^{n}
in
G_{\varphi}
such that
yt=(gt^{n})(xt)(gt^{n})^{-1}=g(t^{n}xt^{-n})tg^{-1}=g\varphi^{n}(x)tg^{-1}.
Thus

Lemma 3.6

The fundamental group

Proof.

As Equation (

\mathrm{ker}(\mathcal{F})=\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{P})=\pi_{1}(S,x)

2.2
there exists an

\varphi(f)=hfh^{-1}

.
Let

\mathcal{F}(f)=\mathrm{Id}
.

Claim:

\varphi(f)\in\mathrm{ker}(\mathcal{F})
.

Let

is homotopic to identity, i.e.

\varphi(f)\in\mathrm{ker}(\mathcal{F})

Isogredience classes

Most of what is contained in this section are known and can mainly be found in
[
FT15
]
,
[
BDR21
]
and the references therein. Still, it seems worthwhile to combine several results in a fashion that suits our purpose and make the paper more self-contained.
Suppose
\Phi\in\mathrm{Out}(G):=\mathrm{Inn}(G)\backslash\textup{Aut}(G)
. Two elements
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
are said to be
isogredient
(or similar) if
\beta=i_{h}\circ\alpha\circ i_{h^{-1}}
for some
h\in G
, where
i_{h}(g)=hgh^{-1}
for all
g\in G
. Observe that this is an equivalence relation on
\Phi
. Fix a representative
\gamma\in\Phi
. Then
\alpha=i_{a}\circ\gamma
and
\beta=i_{b}\circ\gamma
for some
a,b\in G
. Therefore

\displaystyle i_{b}\circ\gamma

\displaystyle=\beta=i_{h}\circ\alpha\circ i_{h^{-1}}=i_{h}\circ i_{a}\circ\gamma\circ i_{h^{-1}}

\displaystyle i_{b}

\displaystyle=i_{h}\circ i_{a}\circ(\gamma\circ i_{h^{-1}}\circ\gamma^{-1})

\displaystyle i_{b}

\displaystyle=i_{h}\circ i_{a}\circ i_{\gamma(h^{-1})}

\displaystyle i_{b}

\displaystyle=i_{ha\gamma(h^{-1})}.

Thus,
\alpha
and
\beta
are isogredient if and only if
b=ha\gamma(h^{-1})c
for some
c\in Z(G)
, the center of
G
.
Let
S(\Phi)
denote the number of isogredience classes of
\Phi
. If
\Phi=\overline{\textup{Id}}
, then
S(\overline{\textup{Id}})
is the number of ordinary conjugacy classes of
G/Z(G)
. A group
G
has the
S_{\infty}
-property
if
S(\Phi)=\infty
for all
\Phi\in\mathrm{Out}(G)
. Observe that if a group
G
has the
S_{\infty}
-property then
G
has the
R_{\infty}
-property. We summarize this discussion as follows:

Lemma 4.1
.

[

, cf. Theorem 3.4]

(1)

If

has the

-property then

has the
R_{\infty}
-property.

(2)

If
G
is centerless and

has the

-property then

has the
S_{\infty}
-property.

Levitt and Lustig
[

proved that any non-elementary hyperbolic group has the

-property. A torsion-free non-elementary hyperbolic group is centerless, therefore it satisfies the

-property.
