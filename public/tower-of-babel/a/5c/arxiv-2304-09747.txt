Complementation of Subquandles
K.J. Amsberry J.A. Bergquist T.A. Horstkamp M.H. Lee D.N. Yetter

Abstract
Saki and Kiani proved that the subrack lattice of a rack R R is necessarily complemented if R R is finite but not necessarily complemented if R R is infinite. In this paper, we investigate further avenues related to the complementation of subquandles. Saki and Kiani’s example of an infinite rack without complements is a quandle, which is neither ind-finite nor profinite. We provide an example of an ind-finite quandle whose subobject lattice is not complemented, and conjecture that profinite quandles have complemented subobject lattices. Additionally, we provide a complete classification of subquandles whose set-theoretic complement is also a subquandle, which we call strongly complemented , and provide a partial transitivity criterion for the complementation in chains of strongly complemented subquandles. One technical lemma used in establishing this is of independent interest: the inner automorphism group of a subquandle is always a subquotient of the inner automorphism group of the ambient quandle.



1
Introduction

Quandles were first introduced as a complete classical knot invariant up to orientation reversal by Joyce
[
1
]
in 1982. In recent years, quandles and racks have found numerous applications in knot theory and algebraic topology. Aside from these applications, quandles are also interesting as an algebraic structure. Indeed, quandles are an abstraction of groups, as any group is a quandle under the operation of right conjugation. Quandles can also be constructed from abelian groups using the Takasaki operation
[
6
]
.

Like other algebraic structures, quandles have subobjects. These subobjects, or subquandles, are difficult to study because quandles lack constants such as an identity element, so that there is no notion of a kernel for a quandle homomorphism, and thus no analogue of normal subgroups. Nonetheless, some progress has been made towards understanding interesting properties of subquandles. Recently Saki and Kiani
[
4
]
showed that the lattice of subquandles of a finite quandle is necessarily complemented, but that infinite quandles may have non-complemented subquandles. The present work investigates several problems related to subquandle lattices, subquandle inner automorphism groups, and subquandles whose set-theoretic complements are also subquandles.

3
Subquandle Lattices

For a quandle
Q
and
A\subseteq Q
, we write
\langle\langle A\rangle\rangle
to denote the subquandle of
Q
generated by
A
. As shown by Saki and Kiani in
[
4
]
, the set of subquandles of any quandle
Q
under inclusion forms a lattice, which we denote by
\mathcal{L}(Q)
. In particular, the two lattice operations
meet
and
join
, which will be described momentarily, are well-defined. Given two subquandles
Q_{1},Q_{2}\preccurlyeq Q
, their meet is
Q_{1}\wedge Q_{2}=Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}
and their join is
Q_{1}\vee Q_{2}=\langle\langle Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}\rangle\rangle
. Note that
Q_{1}\wedge Q_{2}
is the largest subquandle of in
Q
contained in
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, while
Q_{1}\vee Q_{2}
is the smallest subquandle containing
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
.

It is also worth noting that
Q_{1}\vee Q_{2}
is not necessarily
Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}
, as
Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}
may not be a subquandle of
Q
. For example, if
Q
is the Tait quandle on
\{1,2,3\}
,
\{1\}\preccurlyeq Q
and
\{2\}\preccurlyeq Q
but
\{1\}\vee\{2\}=Q
.

Definition 3.1
.

The subquandle lattice
\mathcal{L}(Q)
of a quandle
Q
is
complemented
if for any
Q_{1}\preccurlyeq Q
, there exists some
Q_{2}\preccurlyeq Q
such that
Q_{1}\vee Q_{2}=Q
and
Q_{1}\wedge Q_{2}=\emptyset
.

In
[
4
]
, Saki and Kiani proved the following results.

.

[
4
]

The subrack lattice of a finite rack is complemented.

3.1
Strongly Complemented Subquandles

In this section, we investigate a special class of complemented subquandles: subquandles whose set-theoretic complement is also a subquandle. As we will show, these subracks are particularly interesting in light of
[
3
]
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
Q
be a quandle and
Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
. Then
Q^{\prime}
is
strongly complemented
in
Q
if
Q\setminus Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
.

To motivate this definition, let us consider three examples.

Example 3.4
.

1.

Consider the integers
\mathbb{Z}
under the dihedral operation
\rhd_{\mathrm{dih}}
as described in Example
2.5
. Here, the even integers form a subquandle, as do the odd integers. Thus, both the even integers and the odd integers are strongly complemented subquandles of
\mathbb{Z}
.

2.

Let
G
be a group, and let
\{C_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
be any collection of conjugacy classes of
G
. Because the conjugacy class of
G
form a partition of
G
,
\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Lambda}C_{\alpha}\preccurlyeq\mathrm{Conj}(G)
is a strongly complemented subquandle of
G
.

3.

For a quandle
Q
, any union of orbits under the action of the inner automorphism group
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
is strongly complemented in
Q
.

We will prove that strongly complemented subquandles of a quandle
Q
can also be described in terms of actions of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
— this description will be made explicit in Theorem
3.7
. Before proceeding, we describe the two different actions of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
which will be used throughout this paper. We shall make extensive use of both of these actions, often together, so it is essential to have an efficient way of distinguishing between them.

The first action is the usual right action
\cdot:Q\times\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\to Q
given by
q\cdot\sigma=\sigma(q).
As described previously, the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
creates an orbit for each
q\in Q
, which we denote by
q\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
. In a similar way, for
Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
, we use the notation
Q^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
to refer to the union of the orbits of elements of
Q^{\prime}
, i.e.,
Q^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)=\bigcup_{q\in Q^{\prime}}q\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q).
We refer to this as the
orbit closure
of
Q^{\prime}
. There is more to this name than simply the fact that the orbit closure is closed under the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
. Recall:

Definition 3.5
.

A
closure operation
on a partially ordered set
(X,\leq)
is a function
\mathrm{cl}:X\rightarrow X
which is inflationary, that is
x\leq\mathrm{cl}(x)
, and idempotent, that is
\mathrm{cl}(\mathrm{cl}(x))=\mathrm{cl}(x)
.

We then have:

Proposition 3.6
.

Orbit closure is a closure operation on the lattice of subquandles.

For the second action, suppose
Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
. The second action is the right action
\cdot:\mathcal{L}(Q)\times\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\to\mathcal{L}(Q)
given by
Q^{\prime}\cdot\sigma=\sigma[Q^{\prime}]
, or the image of
Q^{\prime}
under
\sigma
. Thus, we use the notation
[Q^{\prime}]\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
to refer to the set of images of
Q^{\prime}
under elements of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
, i.e.,
[Q^{\prime}]\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)=\{Q^{\prime}\cdot\sigma:q\in Q^{\prime}\}.
We use this notation to capture the action on
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
on the subquandle lattice
\mathcal{L}(Q).

Finally, for ease of notation, we will write
q\sigma
instead of
q\cdot\sigma
and
Q^{\prime}\sigma
instead of
Q^{\prime}\cdot\sigma
, where
q\in Q
and
Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
. With our two actions defined, we state our classification of strongly complemented subquandles:

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
Q
be a quandle, and let
Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
. Denote the subquandle lattice of
Q
by
\mathcal{L}(Q)
. The following are equivalent:

1.

Q\setminus Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
,

2.

Q^{\prime}
is a union of orbits under the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
on
Q
,

3.

Q^{\prime}
is a fixed point of the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
on
\mathcal{L}(Q)
,

4.

Q=\#(Q^{\prime},Q\setminus Q^{\prime},M)
for a mesh
M
.

Proof.

First, suppose that
Q^{\prime}=Q\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
, so
Q^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i}
for some indexing set
\mathcal{I}
and each
Q_{i}
an orbit of the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
on
Q
. Now, pick any
x\in Q^{\prime}
and any
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
. Then,
x\in Q_{i}
for some
i\in\mathcal{I}
, and
x\sigma\in Q_{i}\subseteq Q^{\prime}
because
Q_{i}
is an orbit.
Since
x\in Q^{\prime}
was arbitrary,
Q^{\prime}\sigma\subseteq Q^{\prime}
. Also, since
\sigma
was an arbitrary inner automorphism,
Q^{\prime}\sigma^{-1}\subseteq Q^{\prime}
, which implies
x\sigma^{-1}\in Q^{\prime}
. Also,
x=(x\sigma^{-1})\sigma
, so
Q^{\prime}\subseteq Q^{\prime}\sigma
. Thus, since
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
was arbitrary,
[Q^{\prime}]\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)=\{Q^{\prime}\sigma:q\in Q^{\prime}\}=\{Q^{\prime}\}
, as desired.

Now suppose that
[Q^{\prime}]\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)=\{Q^{\prime}\}
, and pick any
x\in Q^{\prime}
. Because
x\sigma\in Q^{\prime}
for any
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
,
x\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)=\{x\sigma:\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\}\subseteq Q^{\prime}
. Since
x\in Q^{\prime}
was arbitrary,
Q^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\subseteq Q^{\prime}.

Moreover,
Q^{\prime}\subseteq Q\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q),
as each
x=x\cdot\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{Inn}(Q)}\in x\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
. Thus,
Q^{\prime}=Q\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
, as desired.

Before proceeding, we establish a useful lemma:

Lemma 3.8
.

If
Q^{\prime}
is not a fixed point under the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
on
\mathcal{L}(Q)
, then there exists some
x\in Q\setminus Q^{\prime}
such that
Q^{\prime}\cdot S_{x}\neq Q^{\prime}
. In other words, there exists some
x\in Q\setminus Q^{\prime}
and some
y\in Q^{\prime}
such that
y\rhd x\not\in Q^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
[Q^{\prime}]\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\neq\{Q^{\prime}\}
, by definition, there exists some
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
such that
Q^{\prime}\cdot\sigma\neq Q^{\prime}
. Now, assume for the sake of contradiction that all of the symmetries of elements in
Q
fix
Q^{\prime}
. Then the composition of any number of symmetries and symmetry inverses must also fix
Q^{\prime}
. Hence, all elements of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
must fix
Q^{\prime}
, a contradiction. So, there must exist some
x
whose symmetry does not fix
Q^{\prime}
. Accordingly, there exists some
y\in Q^{\prime}
such that
y\rhd x\not\in Q^{\prime}
. Clearly,
x
cannot be in
Q^{\prime}
as
Q^{\prime}
is a subquandle and is closed under
\rhd
. Thus,
x\in Q\setminus Q^{\prime}
, as desired.
∎

(2)
\Leftrightarrow
(1): If
Q^{\prime}
is a union of orbits, so is
Q\setminus Q^{\prime}
. Since unions of orbits are subquandles,
Q^{\prime}
is strongly complemented. For the converse, assume for the sake of contradiction that
Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
is strongly complemented but not a union of orbits. Because
Q^{\prime}
is not a union of orbits,
Q^{\prime}
is not a fixed point under the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
on
\mathcal{L}(Q)
. Hence, by the previous lemma, there exists some
q\in Q^{\prime}
and some
y\in Q\setminus Q^{\prime}
such that
q\rhd y\in Q\setminus Q^{\prime}
. Because
Q\setminus Q^{\prime}
is a subquandle, it is closed under
\rhd^{-1}
, so
q=(q\rhd y)\rhd^{-1}y\in Q\setminus Q^{\prime}
. Hence,
q\in Q^{\prime}\cap(Q\setminus Q^{\prime}),
a contradiction.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(4): If
Q^{\prime}
is strongly complemented,
Q^{\prime}
is a union of orbits. Because Theorem
2.15
also holds for semidisjoint unions of unions of orbits, we obtain
Q=\#(Q^{\prime},Q\setminus Q^{\prime},M)
for some mesh
M
. The converse follows directly from the definition of the semidisjoint union.
∎

As an immediate corollary, we have that the only strongly complemented subquandles of a connected quandle
Q
are
Q
and the empty quandle, as
Q
is only orbit of the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
on
Q
. Another easy corollary is that the the sublattice consisting of strongly complemented subquandles is boolean algebra.

3.2
Chains of Strongly Complemented Subquandles

We now consider chains of strongly complemented quandles. Theorem 4.9 below shows that any subquandle nested between a strongly complemented subquandle and its ambient quandle is also strongly complemented. We also also provide a transitivity criterion for strong complementedness in Theorem 4.13. First, we establish a useful lemma of independent interest:

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
Q
be a quandle and suppose
Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
. Then, there exists some subgroup
S_{Q^{\prime}}\leq\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
and some normal subgroup
K_{Q^{\prime}}\trianglelefteq S_{Q^{\prime}}
such that
\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})\cong S_{Q^{\prime}}/K_{Q^{\prime}}
.

In other words, the inner automorphism group of a subquandle is a subquotient of the inner automorphism group of the ambient quandle.

Proof.

Define
S_{Q^{\prime}}:=\langle\{S_{q}:q\in Q^{\prime}\}\rangle\leq\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
, where each
S_{q}\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q).
Consider the restriction map
\tau:S_{Q^{\prime}}\to\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
defined by
\tau(f)=f|_{Q^{\prime}}.

Claim 3.10
.

\tau
is well-defined.

Proof.

For
z\in Q
and
\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}
, we write
S_{z}^{\epsilon}
to mean the symmetry
(x\mapsto x\rhd^{\epsilon}z)\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q).
We need two fairly obvious properties of bijections which preserve a given subset:
First, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
Q^{\prime}\subseteq Q
be sets. For each
1\leq i\leq n
, let
f_{i}:Q\to Q
satisfy
f_{i}[Q^{\prime}]\subseteq Q^{\prime}.
Then,

(f_{n}\circ f_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1})|_{Q^{\prime}}=(f_{n}|_{Q^{\prime}})\circ(f_{n-1}|_{Q^{\prime}})\cdots\circ(f_{1}|_{Q^{\prime}}).

Note that each restriction
f_{i}|_{Q^{\prime}}
is well-defined by the assumption
f_{i}[Q^{\prime}]\subseteq Q^{\prime}.

Second, let
Q^{\prime}\subseteq Q
be sets, and suppose
f:Q\to Q
is a bijection such that
f[Q^{\prime}]=Q^{\prime}
. Then
(f|_{Q^{\prime}})^{-1}=f^{-1}|_{Q^{\prime}}.

The first fact allows us to freely move restrictions to
Q^{\prime}
in and out of compositions of symmetries of
Q
. The second fact allows us to freely move inverses of symmetries of
Q
in and out of restrictions to
Q^{\prime}
. Now, to show
\tau
maps every element of
S_{Q^{\prime}}
into
\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
, suppose
f\in S_{Q^{\prime}}
has a decomposition
f=S_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}\circ S_{x_{n-1}}^{\epsilon_{n-1}}\cdots\circ S_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and each
x_{i}\in Q^{\prime}
,
\epsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\tau(f)

\displaystyle=(S_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}\circ S_{x_{n-1}}^{\epsilon_{n-1}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}})|_{Q^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(S_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}|_{Q^{\prime}})\circ\cdots\circ(S_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}|_{Q^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=(S_{x_{n}}|_{Q^{\prime}})^{\epsilon_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ(S_{x_{1}}|_{Q^{\prime}})^{\epsilon_{1}}\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime}).

Hence,
\tau[S_{Q^{\prime}}]\subseteq\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
.
∎

Claim 3.11
.

\tau
is surjective.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
, and suppose
\alpha=T_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ T_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}
, where each
T_{x_{i}}^{\epsilon_{i}}\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
is defined via
T_{x_{i}}^{\epsilon_{i}}(x):=x\rhd^{\epsilon_{i}}x_{i}
. Note that
S_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}
is an element of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q).
Moreover,

\displaystyle\tau(S_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}})

\displaystyle=(S_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}})|_{Q^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=S_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}|_{Q^{\prime}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}|_{Q^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(S_{x_{n}}|_{Q^{\prime}})^{\epsilon_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ(S_{x_{1}}|_{Q^{\prime}})^{\epsilon_{1}}

\displaystyle=T_{x_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ T_{x_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}=\alpha.

∎

Claim 3.12
.

\tau
is a group homomorphism.

Proof.

Let
f,g\in S_{Q^{\prime}}
be arbitrary, and suppose
f=S_{x_{n}}^{e_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{x_{1}}^{e_{1}}
and
g=S_{y_{m}}^{f_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ S_{y_{1}}^{f_{1}},
such that
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and each
x_{i},y_{j}\in Q^{\prime}
,
e_{i},f_{j}\in\{-1,1\}
. Since each symmetry fixes
Q^{\prime}
,
f
and
g
must also fix
Q^{\prime}
, as they are compositions of symmetries and inverse symmetries. Hence, by Fact 1,
\tau(f\circ g)=(f\circ g)|_{Q^{\prime}}=f|_{Q^{\prime}}\circ g|_{Q^{\prime}}=\tau(f)\circ\tau(g)
.
∎

Therefore,
\tau
is a surjective group homomorphism. By the First Isomorphism Theorem,

S_{Q^{\prime}}/\ker(\tau)\cong\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime}),
as desired.
∎

Theorem 3.13
.

Suppose
Q^{\prime\prime}\preccurlyeq Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
and that
Q^{\prime\prime}
is strongly complemented within
Q
. Then,
Q^{\prime\prime}
is strongly complemented in
Q^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.7
, it suffices to show that
Q^{\prime\prime}
is a fixed point under the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
on
\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime})
. To that end, fix arbitrary
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
. Consider
S_{Q^{\prime}}:=\langle S(Q^{\prime})\rangle\leq\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
, where
S(Q^{\prime})
denotes the image of
Q^{\prime}
under the function which maps elements to their symmetries. Since the restriction map
\tau:S_{Q^{\prime}}\to\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
from Lemma
3.9
is surjective, there exists some
\sigma^{\prime}\in S_{Q^{\prime}}
extending
\sigma.
Then, because
Q^{\prime\prime}
is strongly complemented in
Q
, by Theorem
3.7
, it is a fixed point of the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
on
\mathcal{L}(Q).
In particular,
Q^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{\prime}=Q^{\prime\prime}
. But, since
\sigma^{\prime}
extends
\sigma:Q^{\prime}\to Q^{\prime}
and since
Q^{\prime\prime}\subseteq Q^{\prime}
, we must also have
Q^{\prime\prime}\sigma=Q^{\prime\prime}
. Thus, since
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
was arbitrary,
Q^{\prime\prime}
is indeed a fixed point under the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
on
\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime})
, as desired.
∎

To establish our transitivity criterion, we will need several technical lemmas.

Lemma 3.14
.

Suppose
Q^{\prime\prime}\preccurlyeq Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
such that
Q^{\prime\prime}
is strongly complemented within
Q^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
is strongly complemented within
Q
. Then,
Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\subseteq Q^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By the definition of
Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q),
it suffices to show that
q\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\subseteq Q^{\prime}
for any
q\in Q^{\prime\prime}.
To that end, fix arbitrary
q\in Q^{\prime\prime}
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
. Since
Q^{\prime}
is strongly complemented within
Q
, by Theorem
3.7
,
[Q^{\prime}]\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)=\{Q^{\prime}\},
so
Q^{\prime}f=Q^{\prime}
for any
f\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
. In particular, since
q\in Q^{\prime}
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
,
q\sigma\in Q^{\prime}
. But since
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
was arbitrary, it follows that
q\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\subseteq Q^{\prime}
, as desired.
∎

It turns out that
Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))
will complement
Q^{\prime\prime}
in
\mathcal{L}(Q)
, which the above lemma will help show.

Lemma 3.15
.

Suppose
Q^{\prime\prime}\preccurlyeq Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
such that
Q^{\prime\prime}
is strongly complemented within
Q^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
is strongly complemented within
Q
. Then, for any
\gamma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
,
Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma
is strongly complemented within
Q^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
Q^{\prime\prime}
is strongly complemented in
Q^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
is strongly complemented in
Q
, by Theorem
3.7
, we have
Q^{\prime}\setminus Q^{\prime\prime}\preccurlyeq Q^{\prime}
and
[Q^{\prime}]\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)=\{Q^{\prime}\}
. Fix arbitrary
\gamma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
. Since the images of subquandles of
Q
under inner automorphisms of
Q
are still subquandles of
Q
,
(Q^{\prime}\setminus Q^{\prime\prime})\gamma\preccurlyeq Q
. Also, because
Q^{\prime}\setminus Q^{\prime\prime}\subset Q^{\prime}
and
\gamma:Q^{\prime}\to Q^{\prime},

(Q^{\prime}\setminus Q^{\prime\prime})\gamma\subset Q^{\prime}
. But, since we showed
(Q^{\prime}\setminus Q^{\prime\prime})\gamma
is a quandle,
(Q^{\prime}\setminus Q^{\prime\prime})\gamma\preccurlyeq Q^{\prime}
. We also know
Q^{\prime}\gamma=Q^{\prime}
because
[Q^{\prime}]\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)=\{Q^{\prime}\}
. Also, since
\gamma
is a bijection,
(Q^{\prime}\setminus Q^{\prime\prime})\gamma=Q^{\prime}\gamma\setminus Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma
. Combining the previous two statements, we have
(Q^{\prime}\setminus Q^{\prime\prime})\gamma=Q^{\prime}\setminus Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma\preccurlyeq Q^{\prime}
. Thus, since its set-theoretic complement is a subquandle of
Q^{\prime}
,
Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma
is strongly complemented within
Q^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 3.16
.

Suppose
Q^{\prime\prime}\preccurlyeq Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
such that
Q^{\prime\prime}
is strongly complemented within
Q^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
is strongly complemented within
Q
. Let
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
. Then there exists some
\sigma^{\prime}\in\langle\{S_{x}:x\in Q\setminus Q^{\prime}\}\rangle\leq\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
such that
Q^{\prime\prime}\sigma=Q^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
, we may fix a representation
\sigma=S_{x_{1}}S_{x_{2}}\cdots S_{x_{n}}
of
\sigma
as a string of symmetries, where each
x_{i}\in Q
and
\epsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
. Our strategy will be to show that by removing all instances of symmetries at elements in
Q^{\prime}
which appear in
S_{x_{1}}S_{x_{2}}\cdots S_{x_{n}}
, we obtain another element
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
which acts the same as
\sigma
upon
Q^{\prime\prime}
in
\mathcal{L}(Q)
.

A systematic way to construct
\sigma^{\prime}
from the string
S_{x_{1}}S_{x_{2}}\cdots S_{x_{n}}
is by checking if
x_{1}
is in
Q^{\prime}
and removing
S_{x_{1}}
iff it is, then similarly checking
x_{2}
and possibly removing
S_{x_{2}}
, and so on. Assume for the sake of contradiction that in doing this process, there is an index
k
such that
x_{k}\in Q^{\prime}
and that when
S_{x_{k}}
is removed from the string
S_{x_{1}}S_{x_{2}}\cdots S_{x_{n}}
, the resulting inner automorphism acts differently than
\sigma
does upon
Q^{\prime\prime}
. Let
i
be the first such index
k
, so for all
1\leq j<i
such that
x_{j}\in Q^{\prime}
, removing
S_{x_{j}}
doesn’t change the resulting action on
Q^{\prime\prime}
, but removing
S_{x_{i}}
does.

Then, let
\gamma=S_{x_{1}}\cdots S_{x_{i-1}}
and
\gamma^{\prime}=S_{x_{i+1}}\cdots S_{x_{n}}
, so we have

\sigma=\gamma S_{x_{i}}\gamma^{\prime}
. By the associativity of group action, we have

Q^{\prime\prime}\sigma=((Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma)S_{x_{i}})\gamma^{\prime}
. Moreover, by the previous lemma,
Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma
is strongly complemented within
Q^{\prime}
. Hence,
Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma
is a fixed point of the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
on
\mathcal{L}(Q^{\prime})
, as follows from Theorem
3.7
. Additionally, since
x_{i}\in Q^{\prime}
, we know that
S_{x_{i}}|_{Q^{\prime}}
is an element of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q^{\prime})
, and moreover, we have
xS_{x_{i}}|_{Q^{\prime}}=xS_{x_{i}}
for any
x\in Q^{\prime}
. From this and the previous statement, it follows that
(Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma)S_{x_{i}}=(Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma)S_{x_{i}}|_{Q^{\prime}}=Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma
. Now, substituting, we have the identity

Q^{\prime\prime}\sigma=((Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma)S_{x_{i}})\gamma^{\prime}=(Q^{\prime\prime}\gamma)\gamma^{\prime}=Q^{\prime\prime}(\gamma\gamma^{\prime}).

Hence, removing
S_{x_{i}}
doesn’t change the action of the resulting string (namely
\gamma\gamma^{\prime}
) on
Q^{\prime\prime}
, contradicting our assumption that it did. So, no such index
i
can exist, and hence removing all symmetries at elements in
Q^{\prime}
from
S_{x_{1}}S_{x_{2}}\cdots S_{x_{n}}
also doesn’t resulting string’s, namely
\sigma^{\prime}
’s, action on
Q^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
Q^{\prime\prime}\preccurlyeq Q^{\prime}\preccurlyeq Q
such that
Q^{\prime\prime}
is strongly complemented within
Q^{\prime}
, while
Q^{\prime}
is strongly complemented within
Q
. Then
Q^{\prime\prime}
is complemented within
Q
.

Proof.

Since
Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
is a union of orbits by construction, by Theorem
3.7
, it follows that
Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))\preccurlyeq Q
. And since
Q^{\prime\prime}\subseteq Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
,
Q^{\prime\prime}\cap Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))=\emptyset
. Thus, to show
Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))
is a complement for
Q^{\prime\prime}
in
\mathcal{L}(Q)
, it remains to show that
Q^{\prime\prime}\vee Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))=Q
. To do this, it suffices to show that
Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\subseteq Q^{\prime\prime}\vee Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))
— if this holds, we will have

\underbrace{(Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)))\cup(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))}_{Q}\subseteq Q^{\prime\prime}\vee Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)),

which implies
Q=Q^{\prime\prime}\vee Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)).

To that end, choose any
x\in Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
. Then, there is some
\sigma\in\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
such that
x\in Q^{\prime\prime}\sigma
. By Lemma
3.16
, there exists some
\sigma^{\prime}\in\langle\{S_{x}:x\in Q\setminus Q^{\prime}\}\rangle
such that
Q^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{\prime}=Q^{\prime\prime}\sigma
. Fix
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\in Q\setminus Q^{\prime}
and
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}\in\{-1,1\}
such that
\sigma^{\prime}=S_{q_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}\dots S_{q_{n}}^{\epsilon_{n}}
. By the definition of the action of
\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
,
Q^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{\prime}=\{q\rhd^{\epsilon_{1}}q_{1}\dots\rhd^{\epsilon_{n}}q_{n}:q\in Q^{\prime\prime}\}
. Thus,
x=q\rhd^{\epsilon_{1}}q_{1}\dots\rhd^{\epsilon_{n}}q_{n}
for some
q\in Q^{\prime\prime}
. In particular,
x\in\langle\langle Q^{\prime\prime}\cup(Q\setminus Q^{\prime})\rangle\rangle
. Additionally, we know
Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\subseteq Q^{\prime}
by Lemma
3.14
, so
Q\setminus Q^{\prime}\subseteq Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))
. Therefore,

\displaystyle x\in\langle\langle Q^{\prime\prime}\cup(Q\setminus Q^{\prime})\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle\subseteq\langle\langle Q^{\prime\prime}\cup(Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)))\rangle\rangle=Q^{\prime\prime}\vee Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))

by the definition of join. But, since
x\in Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
was arbitrary, it follows that
Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)\subseteq Q^{\prime\prime}\vee Q\setminus(Q^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))
, as desired.
∎

This theorem not only shows that
Q^{\prime\prime}
is complemented when it lies two links down in a finite chain of strongly complemented subquandles of
Q
— it also provides an explicit complement for
Q^{\prime\prime}
, namely
Q\setminus(Q\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))
. Moreover, since this complement of
Q^{\prime\prime}
is a union of orbits, it is strongly complemented within
Q
, namely by
Q\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q)
.

An natural question to consider is whether complementedness is carried down further links in a finite chain of strongly complemented subquandles. More explicitly, suppose we have a chain of strongly complemented subquandles,
Q_{3}\preccurlyeq Q_{2}\preccurlyeq Q_{1}\preccurlyeq Q
. A natural attempt would be to consider
Q\setminus(Q_{3}\cdot\mathrm{Inn}(Q))
as a possible complement of
Q_{3}
in
\mathcal{L}(Q)
. However, the join requirement for this to be a complement is not nearly as straightforward to verify. It is our hope that future research will prove that complementedness is transitive across chains of strongly complemented of arbitrary finite length, or else find a counterexample.

3.3
Infinite Quandles

The question of whether the subquandle lattice of a finite quandle is complemented has been settled by previous research. More explicitly, since quandles are racks, the following theorem shows that the subquandle lattices of finite quandles are necessarily complemented.

Theorem 3.18
.

[
4
]

Let
R
be a finite rack. Then the following hold:

1.

The intersection of all the maximal subracks of
R
is empty.

2.

Suppose that
R_{1},R_{2}\preccurlyeq R
. Then
R_{1}
is complemented in
\mathcal{R}(R_{1}\vee R_{2})
by some subset of
R_{2}
.

Corollary 3.19
.

[
4
]

If
R
is a finite rack, then
\mathcal{R}(R)
is complemented.

The natural follow-up to this result is whether infinite racks, and hence infinite quandles, have necessarily complemented subobject lattices. Some infinite racks, such as trivial racks, possess complemented subobject lattices. However, the following theorem demonstrates that this is not always the case:

Theorem 3.20
.

[
4
]

The rack
(\mathbb{Q},\rhd_{\mathrm{dih}})
does not have a complemented subrack lattice. In particular, the singleton
\{0\}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{Q}
lacks a complement.

To understand the proof of Theorem
3.20
, we first need the following definition.

Definition 3.21
.

We say
M\prec Q
is
maximal
if there is no
N\prec Q
such that
M\prec N
. Equivalently,
Q
is the only proper extension of
M
in
\mathcal{L}(Q).

Observe that since singletons are always subquandles, any maximal subquandle
M
of a quandle
Q
must be complemented by
\{x\}
for any
x\in Q\setminus M
. However, this is not true for racks, as racks may not necessarily have singleton subracks.

The proof of Theorem
3.20
relies the containment of all subracks of a finite rack
R
in some maximal subrack of
R

[
4
]
. In particular, the “top layer” of the subrack lattice of
R
is composed of a set of pairwise-disjoint maximal subracks under which every other proper subrack of
R
lies. However, this strategy is no longer useful for infinite quandles, as the following result shows that maximal subquandles may not even exist.

Proposition 3.22
.

(\mathbb{Q},\rhd_{\mathrm{dih}})
has no maximal subquandles.

Proof.

Fix arbitrary
M\prec\mathbb{Q}
. We use the fact that maximal subquandles must be complemented by every singleton in their set-theoretic complement and show that this cannot hold for
M
. We will also use an analagous fact for groups:

Lemma 3.23
.

Let
G
be a group. Then,
M\lneq G
is maximal iff
\langle M\cup\{x\}\rangle=G
for each
x\in G\setminus M
.

Proof.

The forward direction is clear. Now suppose
\langle M\cup\{x\}\rangle=G
for each
x\in G\setminus M
. Fix arbitrary
N\leq G
properly extending
M
. Then, there is some nonempty
S\subseteq G\setminus M
such that
N=\langle M\cup S\rangle.
Fixing some
s\in S
, we see

G=\langle M\cup\{s\}\rangle\subseteq\langle M\cup S\rangle=N,

so
N=G
. Hence, the only subgroup of
G
properly extending
M
is
G
itself, so
M
is maximal.
∎

By Theorem
3.20
,
\{0\}
has no complement in
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{Q})
, so
\{0\}
is not a complement of
M
. Thus, if
0\notin M
,
M
cannot be maximal. Now suppose
0\in M.
By Lemma 5.1 in
[
4
]
,
M
is a subgroup of
(\mathbb{Q},+).
Since
\mathbb{Q}
has no maximal subgroups, by Lemma
3.23
, there is some
q\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus M
such that
\langle M\cup\{q\}\rangle
is a proper subgroup of
\mathbb{Q}.
Hence,
\langle\langle M\cup\{q\}\rangle\rangle\subseteq\langle M\cup\{q\}\rangle\subset Q
, so
\{q\}
is not a complement of
M
in
\mathcal{L}(Q)
. Therefore,
M
cannot be maximal.
∎

3.4
Ind-finite Quandles

Although
\mathbb{Q}
is an infinite quandle with a non-complemented sublattice,
\mathbb{Q}
does not have any clear relationship to finite quandles, which do have complemented sublattices. In this section, we capture one type of relationship an infinite quandle can have with finite quandles by examining ind-finite quandles. We also provide an example which shows that the subquandle lattices of ind-finite quandles are not necessarily complemented.

Definition 3.24
.

A set
(A,\leq)
is
directed
if for all
a,b\in A
, there exists some
c\in A
such that
a\leq c
and
b\leq c
. In other words, every pair of elements in
A
has an upper bound.

Definition 3.25
.

A nonempty quandle
Q
is
ind-finite
if there is a family of finite quandles
\{Q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
indexed by a directed set
(\mathcal{I},\leq)
such that for all
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
,

1.

Q_{i}\preccurlyeq Q
,

2.

|Q_{i}|<\infty
,

3.

i\leq j
implies
Q_{i}\preccurlyeq Q_{j}
, and

4.

Q=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i}.

There is an equivalent formulation of ind-finiteness which will soon be useful. This formulation requires the Axiom of Dependent Choice.

Lemma 3.26
.

Suppose
Q
is a nonempty quandle. Then,
Q
is ind-finite if and only if every finitely generated subquandle of
Q
is finite.

Proof.

Suppose that
Q
is ind-finite with
Q=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i}
,
and fix an arbitrary finitely generated subquandle
Q^{\prime}
of
Q
. Let
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}
be a generating set for
Q^{\prime}
. Then, for each
1\leq j\leq n
, since
g_{j}\in Q
, there exists some
i(j)\in\mathcal{I}
such that
g_{j}\in Q_{i(j)}
. Clearly,

Q^{\prime}=\langle\langle\{g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n}\}\rangle\rangle\subseteq\langle\langle Q_{i(1)},\dots,Q_{i(n)}\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle\cup_{1\leq j\leq n}Q_{i(j)}\rangle\rangle.

But, since
\preccurlyeq
is a total order on
\{Q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
,
\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq n}Q_{i(j)}=Q_{m}
,
where
m=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}i(j).
Thus,
\langle\langle Q_{i(1)},\dots,Q_{i(n)}\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle Q_{m}\rangle\rangle=Q_{m}
. Since
Q_{m}
is finite by assumption and
Q^{\prime}\subseteq Q_{m}
,
Q^{\prime}
must also be finite.

Now suppose that every finitely generated subquandle of
Q
is finite. Let
x\in Q
be arbitrary, and define
Q_{0}=\langle\langle x\rangle\rangle
and
\mathcal{I}=|Q|
(which is a cardinal that isn’t necessarily finite). For each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, while
\bigcup_{k\leq i}Q_{k}\neq Q,
let
Q_{i+1}=\langle\langle Q_{i},y\rangle\rangle
for some
y\in Q\setminus\bigcup_{k\leq i}Q_{k}.
Since each
Q_{i}
is finitely generated subquandle of
Q
by construction, each
Q_{i}
must also be finite. Moreover,
\bigcup_{i\in|Q|}Q_{i}=Q
, since every element of
Q
can be reached by this construction, and
Q_{i}\prec Q_{i+1}
for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
. By definition,
Q
is ind-finite.
∎

As a corollary, we find that the infinite quandle
\mathbb{Q}
cannot be ind-finite.

Corollary 3.27
.

(\mathbb{Q},\rhd_{\mathrm{dih}})
is not ind-finite.

Proof.

Fix any
x,y\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
x<y
. By part (5) of Theorem 3.3 in
[
5
]
,

\langle\langle x,y\rangle\rangle=\{r_{0}x+r_{1}y\},

where
r_{0},r_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
, exactly one of
r_{0},r_{1}
is odd, and
r_{0}+r_{1}=1
. In particular,
\{y+j(y-x):j\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\langle\langle x,y\rangle\rangle.
Hence,
\langle\langle x,y\rangle\rangle
is finitely-generated and infinite subquandle of
\mathbb{Q}
, so by the previous lemma,
\mathbb{Q}
cannot be ind-finite.
∎

But, it turns out that we can transform
\mathbb{Q}
quite easily to create an ind-finite quandle. We will also see that this transformed quandle maintains the properties of having a non-complemented subquandle lattice and no maximal subquandles, thus proving that the subquandle lattices of ind-finite quandles are not necessarily complemented.

Proposition 3.28
.

(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z},\rhd_{\mathrm{dih}})
is ind-finite.

Proof.

It is easy to see that when an abelian group is made into a quandle using the Takasaki operation, any subgroup is a subquandle. Therefore,
\langle\frac{1}{n!}+\mathbb{Z}\rangle
is a (finite) subquandle of
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}.
Observe then that
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
is the ascending union of the subgroups generated by the cosets of the reciprocals of factorials, i.e.,
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\langle\frac{1}{n!}+\mathbb{Z}\rangle
, and thus is easily seen to be ind-finite.
∎

Proposition 3.29
.

(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z},\rhd_{\mathrm{dih}})
does not have a complemented subquandle lattice and has no maximal subquandles.

Proof.

Note that
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}=\{[\frac{m}{n}]:\frac{m}{n}\in\mathbb{Q}\},
where
[\frac{m}{n}]=\frac{m}{n}+\mathbb{Z}.
We are assuming
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
is equipped with the (well-defined) operation
[x]\rhd_{\mathrm{dih}}[y]=[2x-y]
, where
x,y\in\mathbb{Q}
. Thus, in the proofs of Lemma 5.1 and Theorem 5.2 in
[
4
]
, we may replace the elements
x\in\mathbb{Q}
with their corresponding cosets
[x]\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
to obtain the following analogous results for
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
:

1.

The nonempty subquandles of
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
are exactly the cosets of the subgroups of
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}.

2.

\mathcal{L}((\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z},\rhd_{\mathrm{dih}}))
is not complemented. In particular,
\{\mathbb{Z}\}
has no complement.

To show
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
has no maximal subquandles, fix an arbitrary
M\prec\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
— we show that
M
cannot be maximal. As in Proposition
3.22
, if
\mathbb{Z}\notin M
,
M
cannot be maximal. Additionally, if
\mathbb{Z}\in M
, note that
(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z},+)
has no maximal subgroups, so the same argument from Proposition
3.22
shows that
M
cannot be maximal.
∎

3.5
Profinite Quandles

Profinite quandles are the dual to ind-finite quandles — just as ind-finite quandles are the direct limit of a directed system of finite quandles, profinite quandles are the inverse limit of an inverse system of finite quandles. In this section, we formalize the notion of a profinite quandle and provide several examples of infinite profinite quandles. We leave the complementedness of profinite quandle lattices as a question for future research.

Definition 3.30
.

Let
(\mathcal{I},\leq)
be a directed set, and let
\{Q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be a family of quandles indexed by
\mathcal{I}
. Suppose we have a family of quandle homomorphisms
\{f_{ij}:Q_{j}\to Q_{i}:i,j\in\mathcal{I},\ i\leq j\}
such that for all
i,j,k\in\mathcal{I}
,

1.

f_{ii}=\mathrm{id}_{Q_{i}}
, and

2.

If
i\leq j\leq k
,
f_{ik}=f_{ij}\circ f_{jk}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q_{k}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q_{j}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q_{i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{ik}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{jk}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{ij}}

Then, the pair
(\{Q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{f_{ij}\}_{i\leq j\in\mathcal{I}})
is called an
inverse system
of quandles and morphisms over
\mathcal{I}.

Definition 3.31
.

Given an inverse system
(\{Q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{f_{ij}\}_{i\leq j\in\mathcal{I}})
, we define the
inverse limit
of the system as follows:

Q=\lim_{\overleftarrow{i\in\mathcal{I}}}Q_{i}=\left\{\vec{q}\in\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}Q_{i}:q_{i}=f_{ij}\left(q_{j}\right)\text{ for all }i\leq j\text{ in }\mathcal{I}\right\}.

We say a quandle
Q
is
profinite
it is the inverse limit of the inverse system composed of a family of finite quandles and their morphisms. Indeed, this construction always yields a quandle:

Proposition 3.32
.

Profinite quandles are quandles under coordinatewise operations.

Proof.

Suppose that
Q=\displaystyle\lim_{\overleftarrow{i\in\mathcal{I}}}Q_{i},
where
(\{Q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{f_{ij}\}_{i\leq j\in\mathcal{I}})

is an inverse system of finite quandles. Also suppose the associated quandle operation for each
Q_{i}
is
\rhd_{i}.
Because each
Q_{i}
is a quandle under
\rhd_{i}
, axioms Q1, Q2, and Q3 hold for
Q
since they hold in each of its coordinates. But, since
Q
may be infinite, we must also verify that
Q
is closed under its operation
\rhd
. Without loss of generality, let
\mathcal{I}=\mathbb{N}
. Also let
(q_{1},q_{2},\dots),(r_{1},r_{2},\dots)\in Q
be arbitrary. Then,

\displaystyle(q_{1},q_{2},\dots)\rhd(r_{1},r_{2},\dots)

\displaystyle=(q_{1}\rhd_{1}r_{1},q_{2}\rhd_{2}r_{2},\dots),

so, by the definition of
Q
, it suffices to show that for arbitrary
i,j\in\mathcal{I}
,
f_{ij}(q_{j}\rhd_{j}r_{j})=q_{i}\rhd_{i}r_{i}
. But, because
f_{ij}
is a quandle homomorphism,
f_{ij}(q_{j}\rhd_{j}r_{j})=f_{ij}(q_{j})\rhd_{i}f_{ij}(r_{j})=q_{i}\rhd_{i}r_{i}
. Hence,
Q
is closed under
\rhd
, as desired.
∎

Next, we provide a canonical way to construct a profinite quandle from a profinite abelian group: consider the group as a Takasaki kei, as defined in Example
2.5
.

Proposition 3.33
.

Suppose
(\{Q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{f_{ij}\}_{i\leq j\in\mathcal{I}})
is an inverse system of finite abelian groups and their morphisms. Then, its inverse limit
Q
is a profinite Takasaki kei under coordinatewise operations.

Proof.

Since each
Q_{i}
is an abelian group, it is a Takasaki kei. Additionally, each group homomorphism
f_{ij}
is a quandle homomorphism by following lemma:

Lemma 3.34
.

Suppose
(Q_{1},\rhd_{\mathrm{dih}})
and
(Q_{2},\rhd_{\mathrm{dih}})
are Takasaki quandles, and suppose
(Q_{1},+)
and
(Q_{2},+)
are the corresponding groups. Then, any group homomorphism
f:Q_{1}\to Q_{2}
is also a quandle homomorphism.

This lemma proves that regarding an abelian group as a Takasaki quandle is a functor into
\mathbf{Kei}
, the full subcategory of
\mathbf{Quand}
whose objects are keis. We shall give it a name.

Definition 3.35
.

The

Takasaki functor
, denoted
\mathrm{Tak}:\mathbf{Ab}\rightarrow\mathbf{Kei}
, assigns to each abelian group the Takesaki kei.

Thus,
(\{Q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{f_{ij}\}_{i\leq j\in\mathcal{I}})
is also an inverse system of finite Takasaki quandles. To show that the inverse limit of this inverse system is a profinite quandle, it suffices to show that the functor
\mathrm{Tak}
has a left adjoint, as this will show it preserves all limits. We shall construct this left adjoint explicitly.

Definition 3.36
.

Let
K
be a kei. Then we define
\mathrm{AdTak}(K)
to be the quotient group

\mathrm{AdTak}(K)=\langle K\rangle_{Ab}/\langle x\rhd y-2y+x:x,y\in K\rangle_{Ab}

It is easy to see that
\mathrm{AdTak}
is a left adjoint to
\mathrm{Tak}
.

Proposition 3.37
.

Let
\eta
be the inclusion
x\mapsto\overline{x}
. Also let
A
be an abelian group, and let
\varphi
be a morphism of keis
\varphi:K\to\mathrm{Tak}(A)
. Then
\varphi
factors uniquely through
\mathrm{Tak}(\varphi^{\prime})
for a unique morphism of abelian groups
\varphi^{\prime}
, as in the commutative diagram below.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{Tak}(\mathrm{AdTak}(K))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{Tak}(A)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Tak}(\varphi^{\prime})}

Proof.

The map
\eta
can be thought of as the composition (in sets) of the inclusion
K\hookrightarrow\mathrm{AdTak}(K)
along with
p:\langle K\rangle_{Ab}\to\langle K\rangle_{Ab}/\langle x\rhd y-2y+x:x,y\in K\rangle_{Ab}
(regarded as a function between underlying sets). By the universal property of free abelian groups, the function
\varphi
induces a unique group homomorphism
\varphi^{\prime}:\langle K\rangle_{Ab}\to A
. By definition of a Takasaki kei, for any elements
x,y\in K
, we have
\varphi(x\rhd y)=\varphi(x)\rhd\varphi(y)=2\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)
. Hence
\varphi(x\rhd y)-2\varphi(y)+\varphi(x)=\varphi(x\rhd y-2y+x)=0
. Therefore,

\langle x\rhd y-2y+x:x,y\in K\rangle_{Ab}\subseteq\ker(\varphi^{\prime})
, which implies
\langle x\rhd y-2y+x:x,y\in K\rangle_{Ab}\trianglelefteq\ker(\varphi^{\prime})
. By the universal property of quotients in abelian groups,
\varphi^{\prime}
induces yet another (unique) homomorphism
\mathrm{Tak}(\varphi^{\prime}):\mathrm{AdTak}(K)\to A
of abelian groups.
∎

Therefore, by Proposition
3.33
, the inverse limit of
(\{Q_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},\{f_{ij}\}_{i\leq j\in\mathcal{I}})
is a profinite quandle under coordinatewise operations.
∎

Because
\mathrm{Tak}
admits a left adjoint, it preserves all limits, in particular the limits giving a profinite abelian group as the limit of the diagram of all its finite quotient groups. This observation gives rise to a large class of profinite keis:

Example 3.38
.

1.

Suppose
p\in\mathbb{N}
is a prime. Then, the
p
-adic integers
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
form a profinite Takasaki quandle.

2.

The additive group of the ring of profinite integers
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}=\prod_{p}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
forms a profinite Takasaki quandle.

The same argument applied to the functor
\mathrm{Conj}
gives:

Example 3.39
.

If
G
is a profinite group, then
\mathrm{Conj}(G)
is a profinite quandle.

Clearly some pro-finite quandles have complemented subquandle lattices. For example, besides finite quandles
[
4
]
, trivial quandles with an infinite profinite set (that is an infinite totally disconnected compact Hausdorff space) as an underlying set are profinite quandles, being the inverse limit of a diagram of finite trivial quandles. Moreover, these quandles, as do all trivial quandles, have the (necessarily complemented) Boolean algebra of all subsets equipped with trivial quandle structures as a subquandle lattice.

However, in general, if
Q
is infinite, it is hard to determine if
\mathcal{L}(Q)
is complemented, as even the lattice of subquandles of
\mathcal{L}(Q)
is difficult to examine because the underlying sets of either class of examples above are uncountable.

Nonetheless, we conjecture that the subquandle lattice of a profinite quandle is complemented.
