Consistency of Fractional Graph-Laplacian Regularization in Semi-Supervised Learning with Finite Labels
Adrien Weihs, Matthew Thorpe

Abstract
Laplace learning is a popular machine learning algorithm for finding missing labels from a small number of labelled feature vectors using the geometry of a graph. More precisely, Laplace learning is based on minimising a graph-Dirichlet energy, equivalently a discrete Sobolev W 2 , 1 \mathrm{W}^{2,1} semi-norm, constrained to taking the values of known labels on a given subset. The variational problem is asymptotically ill-posed as the number of unlabeled feature vectors goes to infinity for finite given labels due to a lack of regularity in minimisers of the continuum Dirichlet energy in any dimension higher than one. In particular, continuum minimisers are not continuous. One solution is to consider higher-order regularisation, which is the analogue of minimising Sobolev W s , 2 \mathrm{W}^{s,2} semi-norms. In this paper we consider the asymptotics of minimising a graph variant of the Sobolev W s , 2 \mathrm{W}^{s,2} semi-norm with pointwise constraints. We show that, as expected, one needs s > d / 2 s>d/2 where d d is the dimension of the data manifold. We also show that there must be an upper bound on the connectivity of the graph; that is, highly connected graphs lead to degenerate behaviour of the minimiser even when s > d / 2 s>d/2 .



Keywords and phrases.
fractional Laplacian, non-parametric regression, semi-supervised learning, asymptotic consistency, PDEs on graphs, nonlocal variational problems

Introduction

A typical question in machine learning is the one of asymptotic consistency.
Suppose that a machine learning algorithm is formulated as a variational problem depending on a parameter
\varepsilon
, on a set of
n
feature vectors
\Omega_{n}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
where we assume that
x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
: the algorithm aims to minimize a discrete objective
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}(u_{n})
over
u_{n}:\Omega_{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
It is a natural problem to consider the asymptotics of the minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}(\cdot)
: in what sense and to what do the latter converge as
n\to\infty
and
\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon\to 0
?
Furthermore, by taking the appropriate limit of the objectives
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}(\cdot)
, we obtain a limiting continuum objective
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(\cdot)
which is defined for functions
u:\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{m}
: it is also relevant to ask how the minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(\cdot)
relate to the limit of the minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}(\cdot)
. Answering these questions allows one to gain rigorous insights in the design of machine learning algorithms.

The specific problem we consider throughout the paper is the regression problem associated to a higher-order variant of Laplace learning. In order to develop intuition, we start by introducing Laplace learning
[
63
]
.
Given labels
\{\ell_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\{0,1\}
and feature vectors
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, semi-supervised algorithms aim to find the missing labels for feature vectors
\{x_{i}\}_{i=N+1}^{n}
.
On an undirected graph
G_{n,\varepsilon}=(\Omega_{n},W_{n,\varepsilon})
with vertices
\Omega_{n}
and edge-weight matrix
W_{n,\varepsilon}=(w_{\varepsilon,ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
, the Laplace learning method computes the missing labels by solving the variational problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}&u_{n}\in\argmin_{u_{n}:\Omega_{n}\to\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}(u_{n})\quad\text{such that }u_{n}(x_{i})=\ell_{i}\text{ for }i\leq N\\
&\text{where }\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}(u_{n})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}w_{\varepsilon,ij}\left(u_{n}(x_{i})-u_{n}(x_{j})\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(1)

The motivation behind this formulation is that vertices
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
that are close in the graph — i.e.
w_{\varepsilon,ij}
is large — should have similar labels: we impose a discrete regularity requirement on
u_{n}
.
From the latter equation we note that
u_{n}(x_{i})
will take values in
\mathbb{R}
whereas, for our purposes of binary classification, we have labels
\ell_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.
Therefore, in order to classify our vertices, the proposed classification rule for
N<i\leq n
is:

\hat{\ell}_{i}=\begin{cases}0\quad\text{if }u_{n}(x_{i})<0.5\\
1\quad\text{else.}\end{cases}

(2)

Laplace learning is defined, via (
1
) and (
2
), as a variational problem given the graph
G_{n,\varepsilon}
.
In some applications, rather than being given a graph, one has to define the edge-weight matrix
W_{n,\varepsilon}
.
Since our aim will be to study asymptotic consistency with
n\to\infty
, we need to define
W_{n,\varepsilon}
in a scalable way.
A natural construction is to use the feature vectors and define
w_{\varepsilon,ij}
as follows:

w_{\varepsilon,ij}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\eta\left(\frac{|x_{i}-x_{j}|}{\varepsilon}\right)

(3)

for a non-decreasing function
\eta:[0,\infty)\mapsto[0,\infty)
. A typical choice of
\eta
would be
\eta=\mathds{1}_{[0,1]}
, the indicator function of the set
[0,1]
: this ensures that whenever
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are further apart than
\varepsilon
, we have
w_{\varepsilon,ij}=0
.
Defining the edge-weights by (
3
) has two advantages.

On the geometrical side, this allows us to link the intrinsic geometry defined in the graph with the extrinsic Euclidean geometry from the ambient space.
In particular, this will allow for closeness in the graph to be linked to closeness in Euclidean distance and thus, the weighted finite differences
w_{\varepsilon,ij}|u(x_{i})-u(x_{j})|^{2}
can be approximated by
|\nabla u(x_{i})|^{2}
when
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is sufficiently smooth.

On the asymptotic side, as we let
n
tend to infinity, which means that we increase the number of vertices in the graph, it is then natural to let
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}
tend to 0 as there is increasingly more local information available at each point which allows one to resolve the geometry in the graph at finer scales. Since the numerical cost often correlates with the number of neighbours (or the density of the matrix
W_{n,\varepsilon}
), scaling
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
also has the advantage of decreasing computation time.
From an analysis point-of-view scaling
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
allows us to replace the discrete objective
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}
based on finite differences with a continuum objective
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
based on derivatives. For example, up to appropriate scaling which is detailed in (
6
), the continuum energy associated to Laplace learning using weights defined in (
3
) is a
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}
semi-norm. We recall that for
1\leq k\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\geq 1
,
\alpha
a multi-index and
u\in\mathrm{W}^{k,p}(\Omega)
, the norm and semi-norm of
\mathrm{W}^{k,p}(\Omega)
can be defined as
\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{k,p}}=\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}+\sum_{1\leq|\alpha|\leq k}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}
and
\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}
respectively.

While we will rigorously fix notations in Section
3.1
, we now introduce the objective functions that we will be considering.
Given the graph Laplacian
\Delta_{n,\varepsilon}
defined in (
9
) and the discrete
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{n})
inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{n})}
defined in (
10
), we define the fractional Laplacian energy
[
22
]
for
u_{n}:\Omega_{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
s>0
:

\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}^{(s)}(u_{n})=\langle u_{n},\Delta_{n,\varepsilon}^{s}u_{n}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{n})}.

The associated continuum energy is an approximate
\mathrm{W}^{s,2}
semi-norm (as
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the fractional Sobolev semi-norms are defined differently to the above and we refer to Remarks
3.5
and
3.8
for further discussions) for
u\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\Omega)
where
\mathcal{H}^{s}
is defined in (
17
):

\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{\infty}(u)=\langle u,\Delta_{\rho}^{s}u\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

and
\Delta_{\rho}
is the continuum weighted Laplacian defined in (
14
). For
s=1
, we recover the discrete and continuum energies of Laplace learning.

The underlying question when studying asymptotic consistency is the one of the appropriate notion of convergence.
For a function
u:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we define
u\lfloor_{\Omega_{n}}
to be the restriction of
u
onto the sample points
\Omega_{n}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
.
The first type of convergence that has been analysed in the literature is concerned with the notion of
pointwise
convergence of energies: for
u:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{m}
, one is interested if
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}(u\lfloor_{\Omega_{n}})\to\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(u)
as
n\to\infty
and
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
at an appropriate rate.
This has for example been studied in
[
3
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
35
]
,
[
40
]
,
[
39
]
,
[
51
]
and
[
57
]
.
Another convergence type is
spectral
convergence, where one analyses the convergence of the eigenpairs of the discrete operator to the continuum one.
Convergence of eigenvalues has for example been studied in
[
3
]
,
[
28
]
and
[
12
]
.
Convergence of eigenvectors is more subtle and we return to this below.

Usually one really wants to establish convergence of minimisers; that is, if
u_{n}
is the constrained minimiser of
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}
and
u_{\infty}
is the constrained minimiser of
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{(s)}
then does
u_{n}
converge to
u_{\infty}
?
Since
u_{n}
is a discrete function defined on
\Omega_{n}
and
u_{\infty}
is a continuum function defined on
\Omega
we need to define a notion of convergence that allows one to compare functions on different domains.
There are two natural ways to do this.
In the first we restrict
u_{\infty}
to the domain
\Omega_{n}
and compare to
u_{n}
, i.e. we treat
u_{\infty}\lfloor_{\Omega_{n}}-u_{n}
which is a function from
\Omega_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
However, note that we must have sufficient regularity of
u_{\infty}
in order to define it’s restriction to
\Omega_{n}
.
There are certain settings where one has enough regularity of the continuum minimiser, e.g. for
p
-Laplacian regularisation, in order to be able to define pointwise evaluation, see for example
[
53
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
12
]
, and
[
27
]
.

When one doesn’t have enough regularity to define
u_{\infty}\lfloor_{\Omega_{n}}
, we need to consider another approach. The second notion of convergence, rather than restricting
u_{\infty}
to
\Omega_{n}
, extends
u_{n}
to
\Omega
.
One way to define this extension is via an optimal transport map
T_{n}:\Omega\to\Omega_{n}
between the empirical measure
\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
and the continuum measure
\mu
.
This defines a partitioning of the state space
\Omega
. The extension of
u_{n}
to
\Omega
can then be defined by
\tilde{u}_{n}=u_{n}\circ T_{n}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
One can then compare
\tilde{u}_{n}-u_{\infty}
.
If one uses an
\mathrm{L}^{p}
norm on
\tilde{u}_{n}-u_{\infty}
then this defines the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
distance, introduced by
[
32
]
and reviewed in Section
2.1
, which can be viewed as a metric in an appropriate space.
In this work it is the latter notion of convergence, i.e.
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
, that we consider.

We point out that, since eigenvectors of
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}^{(s)}
are discrete functions and eigenvectors of
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{(s)}
are continuum functions, the convergence of eigenvectors uses one of the two notions of convergence described above.
In particular, uniform convergence (which falls into the first type of convergence) was studied in
[
60
]
,
[
46
]
,
[
52
]
,
[
61
]
and
[
12
]
, and
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
(or related) convergence was studied in
[
32
]
and
[
28
]
.

Having described the numerous types of convergence at our disposal, we now come back to our question of interest: when do constrained minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(\cdot)
converge to the constrained minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{(s)}(\cdot)
?
Our answer is given in Theorem
3.2
and is broken into two cases: the
ill-posed
case where we provide conditions on the scaling
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
(depending on
s
and
\dim(\Omega)
) for constrained minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}
to converge to constants, and the
well-posed
case where we provide conditions on the scaling of
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
for constrained minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}
to converge to constrained minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{(s)}
.
Conditions for the ill-posed case in a closely related setting can be found in
[
22
]
.
Our main contribution is to add conditions for the well-posed case.

The paper is set out as follows.
In the next section we present some background material, including a description of the metric space
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
in which we work, an overview of
\Gamma
-convergence, and a brief description of related works.
The main results are presented in Section

along with our assumptions and notation.
The proofs are given in Section
4
.
There is a small gap in the rate at which
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
that is not covered by our theoretical results, and so we provide some numerical experiments in Section
5
to give insights into what can be expected in this regime.

2.1
The
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
Space

Let
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
be the set of probability measures on
\Omega
and
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
be the set of probability measures on
\Omega
with finite
p
th-moment.
The set of functions
u
that are measurable with respect to
\mu
and such that
\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<+\infty
is denoted by
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mu)
.
The pushforward of a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
by a map
T:\Omega\to\mathcal{Z}
is the measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
defined by

\nu(A)=T_{\#}\mu(A):=\mu(T^{-1}(A))=\mu\left(\left\{x\,|\,T(x)\in A\right\}\right)\qquad\text{for all measurable sets }A.

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega)
we denote by
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
the set of all probability measures on
\Omega\times\Omega
such that the first marginal is
\mu
and the second marginal is
\nu
, i.e.
(P_{X})_{\#}\pi=\mu
and
(P_{Y})_{\#}\pi=\nu
where
P_{X}:\Omega\times\Omega\ni(x,y)\mapsto x\in\Omega
and
P_{Y}:\Omega\times\Omega\ni(x,y)\mapsto y\in\Omega
.
We start by recalling the definition of the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
space and metric from
[
31
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

For an underlying domain
\Omega
, define the set

\mathrm{TL}^{p}=\left\{(\mu,u)\,|\,\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Omega),u\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mu)\right\}.

For
(\mu,u),(\nu,v)\in\mathrm{TL}^{p}
, we define the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
distance
d_{\mathrm{TL}^{p}}
as follows:

d_{\mathrm{TL}^{p}}((\mu,u),(\nu,v))=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}|x-y|^{p}+|u(x)-v(y)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y).

The key property of the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
distance is that
d_{\mathrm{TL}^{p}}((\mu,u),(\nu,v))
is equal to the
p
-Wasserstein distance between the measures
\mu
and
\nu
raised to the graphs of
u
and
v
, i.e.
d_{\mathrm{TL}^{p}}((\mu,u),(\nu,v))=d_{\mathrm{W}^{p}}((\mathrm{Id}\times u)_{\#}\mu,(\mathrm{Id}\times v)_{\#}\nu)
where
d_{\mathrm{W}^{p}}
is the
p
-Wasserstein distance, see
[
31
]
.
This is useful, since it allows one to leverage the properties of the well-studied Wasserstein distances.
We refer to
[
59
,
49
]
for a treatment of optimal transport and Wasserstein distances.

We will say that a sequence
\{(\mu_{n},u_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathrm{TL}^{p}
converges in
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
to some
(\mu,u)\in\mathrm{TL}^{p}
if

d_{\mathrm{TL}^{p}}((\mu_{n},u_{n}),(\mu,u))\to 0.

As shown in
[
31
]
, by leveraging the connection between the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
distance and the
p
-Wasserstein distance we have the following equivalent notions of convergence. A version of the following proposition can also be stated for non-absolutely continuous measures.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(\mu,u)\in\mathrm{TL}^{p}
where
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure and let
\{(\mu_{n},u_{n})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence in
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
. The following are equivalent:

1.

(\mu_{n},u_{n})
converges to
(\mu,u)
in
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
;

2.

\mu_{n}
converges weakly to
\mu
and there exists a sequence of transport maps
\{T_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with
(T_{n})_{\#}\mu=\mu_{n}
and
\int_{\Omega}|x-T_{n}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\to 0
such that

\int_{\Omega}|u(x)-u(T_{n}(x))|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\to 0;

3.

\mu_{n}
converges weakly to
\mu
and for any sequence of transport maps
\{T_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with
(T_{n})_{\#}\mu=\mu_{n}
and
\int_{\Omega}|x-T_{n}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\to 0
, we have

\int_{\Omega}|u(x)-u(T_{n}(x))|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\to 0.

In our work,
\mu_{n}
will denote the empirical measures of our samples
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mu
will be the measure from which the points are sampled.
Furthermore,
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
u
will respectively be the minimizers of our discrete objectives
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}(\cdot)
and continuum objective
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(\cdot)
.
Since (in our setting with probability one)
\mu_{n}\weakstarto\mu
then with a small abuse of notation we say that
u_{n}
converges to
u
in
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
if
(\mu_{n},u_{n})
converges to
(\mu,u)
in
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
.

We recall the following result, the proof of which is a simple consequence of
[
28
, Theorem 2]
(in the special case where
\Omega
is a torus) with the Borel–Cantelli Lemma, for use in our analysis.

Theorem 2.3
.

Existence of transport maps.
Assume that
\Omega
is a torus,
x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
where
\mu
has a density that is bounded above and below by positive constants.
Then, there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., there exists a sequence of transport maps
\{T_{n}:\Omega\mapsto\Omega_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
from
\mu
to
\mu_{n}
such that:

\begin{cases}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^{1/2}\|\mathrm{Id}-T_{n}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}{\log(n)^{3/4}}\leq C&\text{if }d=2;\\
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^{1/d}\|\mathrm{Id}-T_{n}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}}{\log(n)^{1/d}}\leq C&\text{if }d\geq 3.\end{cases}

(4)

In terms of the assumptions we introduce later, the conditions in the above theorem are given by
S.1
,
M.1
,
M.2
and

2.2
\Gamma
-Convergence

The appropriate framework to describe the convergence of variational problems is
\Gamma
-convergence from the calculus of variations.
We refer to
[
6
]
for a detailed treatment of the subject and only recall the key properties we later need here.
We begin with the definition of
\Gamma
-convergence.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(Z,d_{Z})
be a metric space and
E_{n}:Z\to\mathbb{R}
a sequence of functionals.
We say that
E_{n}

\Gamma
-converges to
E
with respect to
d_{Z}
if:

1.

For every
z\in Z
and every sequence
\{z_{n}\}
with
d_{Z}(z_{n},z)\to 0
:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}E_{n}(z_{n})\geq E(z);

2.

For every
z\in Z
, there exists a sequence
\{z_{n}\}
with
d_{Z}(z_{n},z)\to 0
and

\limsup_{n\to\infty}E_{n}(z_{n})\leq E(z).

The notion of
\Gamma
-convergence allows one to derive the convergence of minimizers from compactness.

Definition 2.5
.

We say that a sequence of functionals
E_{n}:Z\to\mathbb{R}
has the compactness property if the following holds: if
\{n_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an increasing sequence of integers and
\{z_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a bounded sequence in
Z
for which
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}E_{n_{k}}(z_{k})<\infty
, then the closure of
\{z_{k}\}
has a convergent subsequence.

Proposition 2.6
.

Convergence of minimizers.
Let
E_{n}:Z\mapsto[0,\infty]
be a sequence of functionals which are not identically equal to
\infty
.
Suppose that the functionals satisfy the compactness property and that they
\Gamma
-converge to
E:Z\mapsto[0,\infty]
.
Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{z\in Z}E_{n}(z)=\min_{z\in Z}E(z).

Furthermore, the closure of every bounded sequence
\{z_{n}\}
for which

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(E_{n}(z_{n})-\inf_{z\in Z}E_{n}(z)\right)=0

(5)

has a convergent subsequence and each of its cluster points is a minimizer of
E
.
In particular, if
E
has a unique minimizer, then any sequence satisfying (
5
) converges to the unique minimizer of
E
.

In our work, the setup will be as follows: we will show that our discrete objective
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}

\Gamma
-converges, with respect to the
\mathrm{TL}^{2}
topology, to the continuum objective
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
.
Then, we will show that the sequence of minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}
,
\{u_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, are precompact in
\mathrm{TL}^{2}
— its closure has a convergent subsequence.
Using (
5
), we will obtain that the minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}
converge to the minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
.

and one finds the same technical tools applied in
[
8
]
too.

The authors in
[
32
]
extended the spectral convergence results cited in Section
1
to include convergence of eigenvectors using the
\mathrm{TL}^{p}
topology.
They show that eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian converge to eigenfunctions of a weighted continuum Laplacian.
A consequence of this result is that minimizers of

\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}(u_{n})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}_{n}n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}w_{\varepsilon_{n},ij}(u_{n}(x_{i})-u_{n}(x_{j}))^{2}

converge to the minimizers of

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(u)=\begin{cases}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u(x)|^{2}\rho(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x&\text{if }u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}\\
+\infty&\text{if }u\in\mathrm{L}^{2}\setminus\mathrm{W}^{1,2}.\end{cases}

While the authors in
[
32
]
were interested in the machine learning problem of spectral clustering and its consistency, the semi-supervised problem requires pointwise constraints as in (
1
). This has led to poor performance of Laplace learning (see
[
44
]
and
[
23
]
). We briefly elaborate on the intuition stemming from Sobolev embeddings which motivates the use of higher-order variations of Laplace learning to overcome these issues. As was shown in
[
32
]
and noted above, minimizers of Laplace learning converge to minimizers of a
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}
semi-norm. If we now constrain our minimizer
u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}
to take certain values
\ell_{i}
at
x_{i}
for
i\leq N
, we need
u
to be at least continuous which is satisfied only for
d=1
. In most applications, we have
d>1
and consequently, with a large data set — which theoretically resembles the large data limit setting — the minimizer of Laplace learning will not be regular and, in fact, be an almost constant function developing spikes at the imposed labels (see for example
[
10
, Figure 1]
). Since our use of the graph structure relies on the idea that connected points in the graph should have similar labels, it is clear that an irregular function cannot be used for the purpose of labelling the rest of the dataset and justifies the need for other learning algorithms. In particular, one approach to solve this problem is to consider methods for which the continuum limit describes a higher-order Sobolev semi-norm since the general Sobolev embeddings guarantee that
u\in\mathrm{W}^{s,p}
is continuous whenever
sp>d
allowing for greater flexibility than Laplace learning.

Fixing
s=1
and considering
p>1
leads to
p
-Laplacian regularization defined as

\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}(u_{n})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p}_{n}n^{2}}\sum_{i=1,j=1}^{n}w_{\varepsilon_{n},ij}|u_{n}(x_{i})-u_{n}(x_{j})|^{p}\quad\text{subject to }u_{n}(x_{i})=\ell_{i}\text{ for }i\leq N.

(6)

In
[
53
]
, it is shown that minimizers of (
6
) converge to the minimizers of either

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}(u)=\begin{cases}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\rho^{2}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&\text{if }u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,p},\\
+\infty&\text{else}\end{cases}

(7)

or

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}^{(\mathrm{con})}(u)=\begin{cases}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\rho^{2}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&\text{if }u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,p}\text{ and }u(x_{i})=\ell_{i}\text{ for }i\leq N,\\
+\infty&\text{else.}\end{cases}

(8)

The parameter that controls the convergence to either functional is
\varepsilon_{n}
(intuitively one might expect that
p>d
is enough for the constrained minimizers of (
6
) to converge to minimizers of (
8
) and whilst this is necessary it turns out not to be sufficient
[
53
]
): indeed, as we will present later on,
\varepsilon_{n}
has to tend to
0
but the rate at which it does so is crucial in the analysis.

Taking
p\to\infty
in
p
-Laplacian regularization, one can consider the
\infty
-Laplacian and the associated Lipschitz learning problem
[
42
]
. Various convergence results related to the latter are presented in
[
9
]
,
[
48
]
and
[
7
]
. It is also worth noting that if one decides to fix a constant localization parameter
\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon
in
p
-Laplace regularization, then we are in the setting of non-local
p
-Laplacian regularization and convergence results related to the limit of (
6
) are discussed in
[
37
]
,
[
38
]
and
[
24
]
.

Another higher-order variant of Laplace learning that has been introduced in
[
62
]
and studied in
[
22
]
is fractional Laplacian regularization (see (
11
) for the discrete energy and (
13
) for the associated semi-supervised learning problem). In this case, the idea is to fix
p=2
and to let
s
vary. This leads to the continuum problem introduced in (
15
) which can be shown to be related to a
\mathrm{W}^{s,2}
semi-norm
[
22
, Lemma 17]
. Similar to the convergence result in
[
32
]
for Laplace learning, it is shown in
[
22
]
that minimizers of (
11
) converge to minimizers of (
15
). It is precisely this setting that we aim to extend to semi-supervised learning by adding pointwise constraints. The results of our analysis are stated in Theorem
3.2
.

The advantage of fractional Laplace regularization over
p
-Laplacian regularization comes from the formulation of the discrete energy (
11
) in terms of the eigenpair decomposition of the graph Laplacian as detailed in (
12
). The latter allows one to use Lagrange multipliers in order to find an exact discrete minimizer to the semi-supervised problem while one has to rely on gradient descent to compute an approximate solution to
p
-Laplacian regularization as described in
[
53
]
. We refer to Remark
3.8
for a further discussion on higher-order variations of Laplace learning.

Lastly, we point out other approaches that have been proposed to deal with the regularity problems of Laplace learning in semi-supervised learning. In particular, we refer to game theoretic
p
-Laplacian regularization
[
8
]
and Poisson learning
[
10
]
.
One can also study estimators which are required to lie in a space spanned by a finite number of eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian as is done in
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
or properly re-weight the Laplacian as in
[
50
]
and
[
14
]
.

3
Main Results

In this section we present our main results.
We start by defining the notation we will use, then we state our assumptions followed by the main results.
The proofs of the main results are in the following section.

3.1
Notation and Setting

We now want to formalize the notation used throughout the paper and describe our precise setting.

3.1.1
Graph Setting

Given a space
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a measure
\mu
on
\Omega
that has density
\rho
, iid samples
\Omega_{n}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from
\mu
, a weight function
\eta
and a length-scale
\varepsilon
, we will define a random geometric graph as follows.
For
\varepsilon>0
, we write
\eta_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)=\varepsilon^{-d}\eta(\cdot/\varepsilon)
.
We define a graph
G_{n,\varepsilon}=(\Omega_{n},W_{n,\varepsilon})
where
\Omega_{n}
are the vertices and
W_{n,\varepsilon}=(w_{\varepsilon,ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
is the edge weight matrix with entries
w_{\varepsilon,ij}=\eta_{\varepsilon}(|x_{i}-x_{j}|)
.

Let
D_{n,\varepsilon}
be the diagonal matrix with entries
d_{n,\varepsilon,ii}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{\varepsilon,ij}
and define

\sigma_{\eta}=\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta(|h|)|h|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}h<\infty.

The graph Laplacian is defined as

\Delta_{n,\varepsilon}:=\frac{2}{\sigma_{\eta}n\varepsilon^{2}}(D_{n,\varepsilon}-W_{n,\varepsilon}).

(9)

With a small abuse of notation we can interpret
\Delta_{n,\varepsilon}
as a matrix
\Delta_{n,\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
or as an operator
\Delta_{n,\varepsilon}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{n})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{n})
where
\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
is the empirical measure.

3.1.2
Variational Problems

Given functions
u_{n},v_{n}:\Omega_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{n})
inner product:

\langle u_{n},v_{n}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{n})}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{n}(x_{i})v_{n}(x_{i}).

(10)

Such functions can be considered vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and with an abuse of notation we will understand
u_{n}
as both a function
u_{n}:\Omega_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and a vector
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We denote the eigenpairs of
\Delta_{n,\varepsilon}
by
\{(\lambda_{n,\varepsilon,k},\psi_{n,\varepsilon,k})\}_{k=1}^{n}
where
\lambda_{n,\varepsilon,k}
are in increasing order,
0=\lambda_{n,\varepsilon,1}<\lambda_{n,\varepsilon,2}\leq\lambda_{n,\varepsilon,3}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n,\varepsilon,n}
, (where strict inequality between
\lambda_{n,\varepsilon,1}
and
\lambda_{n,\varepsilon,2}
follows when the graph
G_{n,\varepsilon}
is connected) and note that
\{\psi_{n,\varepsilon,k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
form for a basis of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{n})
.

For
s>0
, we will be considering the following energies on the graph:

\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}^{(s)}(u_{n})=\langle u_{n},\Delta_{n,\varepsilon}^{s}u_{n}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{n})}.

(11)

Note that with the eigenfunction decomposition, we can write

\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}^{(s)}(u_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{n,\varepsilon,k}^{s}\langle u_{n},\psi_{n,\varepsilon,k}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{n})}^{2}.

(12)

Suppose now that we are given
\{\ell_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
N<n
labels for the first
N
samples
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
The problem we consider is to find a function
u_{n}
defined as:

u_{n}\in\argmin_{v_{n}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{n})}\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(v_{n})\quad\text{such that }v_{n}(x_{i})=\ell_{i}\text{ for }i\leq N.

(13)

Using Lagrange multipliers, one can show the well-posedness of (
13
).

This discrete problem has a continuum analogue.
Namely, let
\Delta_{\rho}
be the continuum weighted Laplacian operator defined by

\Delta_{\rho}u(x)=-\frac{1}{\rho(x)}\mathrm{div}(\rho^{2}\nabla u)(x),\,x\in\Omega\quad\quad\quad\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}=0,\,x\in\partial\Omega.

(14)

and let
\{(\lambda_{k},\psi_{k})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be its associated eigenpairs where
\lambda_{1}=0<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\ldots
and note that
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
form a basis of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu)
.
The continuum energy is then defined as

\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{\infty}(u)=\langle u,\Delta^{s}_{\rho}u\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu)}

or, using the eigenfunction decomposition,

\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{\infty}(u)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{s}\langle u,\psi_{k}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu)}^{2}.

(15)

We look at the associated problem, namely to find
u
:

u\in\argmin_{v\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{n})}\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{\infty}(v)\quad\text{such that }v(x_{i})=\ell_{i}\text{ for }i\leq N.

(16)

Well-posedness of (
16
) is derived from Theorem
3.2
and the convexity of
\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{\infty}(\cdot)
.

We lastly define the energies for which we will prove
\Gamma
-convergence.
We consider the set

\mathcal{H}^{s}(\Omega)=\{h\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu)\,|\,\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{\infty}(h)<+\infty\}.

(17)

As pointed out in Section
2.3
,
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\Omega)
is closely related to the Sobolev space
\mathrm{W}^{s,2}(\Omega)
.

Consider for
(\nu,v)\in\mathrm{TL}^{2}
:

\mathcal{F}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}((\nu,v))=\begin{cases}\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(v)&\text{if }\nu=\mu_{n}\text{ and for }i\leq N,v(x_{i})=\ell_{i}\\
+\infty&\text{else.}\end{cases}

(18)

The continuum-limit of this energy is either

\mathcal{F}((\nu,v))=\begin{cases}\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{\infty}(v)&\text{if }\nu=\mu,v\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\Omega)\text{ and for }i\leq N,v(x_{i})=\ell_{i}\\
+\infty&\text{else}\end{cases}

(19)

or

\mathcal{G}((\nu,v))=\begin{cases}\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{\infty}(v)&\text{if }\nu=\mu\text{ and }v\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\Omega),\\
+\infty&\text{else}\end{cases}

depending on the asymptotic behaviour of
\varepsilon_{n}
which we detail in Theorem
3.2
.

3.1.3
Probability setting

In the sequel, some of our results will depend on the data set
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
. For example, given
n
, a length-scale
\varepsilon_{n}
and a weight construction as in (
3
), as we will point out in Section
3.2
, the graph is only connected for some subset of all sequences
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Since we have the modeling assumption
x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mu
, we can characterize how large this subset of sequences is in a probabilistic manner. In particular, we can create a probability space
(\Psi,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
in which each element is a sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and our aim will be to show that there exists
\Psi^{\prime}\subseteq\Psi
with
\mathbb{P}(\Psi^{\prime})=1
such that some property holds for all sequences in
\Psi^{\prime}
.

We now detail the construction of
(\Psi,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Consider the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{B}(\Omega),\mu)
where
\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
. Let
\Psi=\Omega^{\mathbb{N}}
.
For any set
J\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
0<|J|<\infty
, we define the coordinate maps
X_{J}:\Psi\mapsto\Omega^{|J|}
by

X_{J}(Y)=(\psi_{J_{1}},\ldots,\psi_{J_{|J|}})

where
J=\{J_{1},\ldots,J_{|J|}\}
and
\psi\in\Psi
.

By classical measure theoretical arguments, for any set
J\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
|J|<\infty
and an appropriate product
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{B}^{|J|}(\Omega)
– the smallest
\sigma
-field that makes the coordinate maps
\{X_{\{j\}}\}_{j\in J}
measurable, we can define a probability space
(\Omega^{|J|},\mathcal{B}^{|J|}(\Omega),\mathbb{P}^{J})
where
\mathbb{P}^{J}
is the product measure satisfying
\mathbb{P}^{J}(A_{J_{1}}\times\ldots\times A_{J_{|J|}})=\mu(A_{J_{1}})\times\ldots\times\mu(A_{J_{|J|}})
for
A_{J_{i}}\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
.

By an application of the Daniell–Kolmogorov Theorem
[
55
, Theorem 2.4.3]
(and because
(\Omega,\mathbb{B}(\Omega))
is Polish), there exists a measure space
(\Psi,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
such that

\mathbb{P}(X_{J}^{-1}(A))=\mathbb{P}^{J}(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}^{|J|}(\Omega)
. It is also straight-forward to show that the coordinate maps
\{X_{\{i\}}:\Psi\mapsto\Omega\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
are independent and have distribution
\mu
. For any
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, there exists
\psi\in\Psi
such that
x_{i}=X_{\{i\}}(\psi)
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.2
Assumptions

We start by listing the assumptions on the space
\Omega
.

Assumptions 1
.

We make the following assumption on the space.

S.1

The feature vector space
\Omega
is the unit torus
\nicefrac{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}{{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}}
. We write
d_{\mathrm{man}}
for the manifold distance.

The assumption that
\Omega
is the torus greatly simplifies our analysis in several places.
Firstly, we don’t have to analyse the boundary as we would in a bounded Euclidean domain (the pointwise rates of convergence in the graph-to-continuum Laplacian are different close to the boundary compared with the interior).
And secondly, we don’t have to take into account curvature, as we would on a manifold. However, we do not expect significant changes in the proofs if the latter were to be considered.

We make the following assumptions on the measure.

Assumptions 2
.

Assumptions on the measure.

M.1

The measure
\mu
is a probability measure on
\Omega
.

M.2

There is a strictly positive Lipschitz continuous Lebesgue density
\rho
of
\mu
.

Since the density
\rho
is strictly positive and continuous on a compact domain then we can infer that
\rho
is bounded from above and below by strictly positive constants, i.e.

0<\min_{x\in\Omega}\rho(x)\leq\max_{x\in\Omega}\rho(x)<+\infty.

We also have that
\mu
has finite
p
th moment.

The data consists of feature vectors
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and labels
\{\ell_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and we make the following assumptions.

Assumptions 3
.

Assumptions on the data.

D.1

Feature vectors
\Omega_{n}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are iid samples from a measure
\mu
satisfying
M.1
.
We denote by
\mu_{n}
the empirical measure associated to our samples.

D.2

There are
N
labels
\{\ell_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}
corresponding to the first
N
feature vectors
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

It is straightforward to generalise to the case where labels are multidimensional, i.e.
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with only an additional notational burden on the presentation.

The weight function
\eta
is assumed to satisfy the following assumptions.

Assumptions 4
.

Assumptions on the weight function or kernel.

W.1

The function
\eta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is decreasing, with compact support, Lipschitz continuous on
[0,1]
, and has
\eta(0.5)>0.5
and
\eta(1)=0
.

W.2

The function
\eta
integrates to unity over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta(|x|)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
.

The assumption that
\eta
is Lipschitz continuous on
[0,1]
,
\eta(1/2)>0
, and integrates to unity (Assumption

) are not important and could be replaced by the assumption that
\eta
is Lipschitz continuous on

for some

and

.
Fixing

We also note that Assumption

The assumption that

[
28
]
.

We finally have the assumptions on the length scale
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}
which we scale with the number of feature vectors.

Assumptions 5

Assumptions on the length-scale.

L.1

The length scale
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}
is positive and converges to 0, i.e.
0<\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
.

L.2

\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}
satisfies either the lower bound (in the ill-posed case)

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(n)}{n\varepsilon_{n}^{d}}&=0\qquad\text{if }d\geq 3\\
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(\log(n))^{3/2}}{n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}&=0\qquad\text{if }d=2\end{split}

(
.W
.I)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(n)}{n\varepsilon_{n}^{d+4}}=0.

(
.W
.W)

Assumption
.W

N_{1}
such that for all

the graph
G_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}=(\Omega_{n},W_{n,\varepsilon_{n}})
is connected (see
[
36

or
[

3.3
Main Results

The condition on
\varepsilon_{n}
stated in
.W
is a lower bound on
\varepsilon_{n}
.
Our results are mostly concerned with finding an upper bound on
\varepsilon_{n}
, just as is found in
[

, Theorem 2.1]
for the
p
-Laplacian.
Indeed, it can be shown that if
\varepsilon_{n}
goes to

too slowly, the minimizers of (

) converge to a minimizer of (
7
).
Minimizers of (

) are simply the constant functions and, in particular, do not consider the information from the labels
\{\ell_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
We can therefore consider this regime as degenerate or asymptotically ill-posed.
Conversely, if we have that
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
sufficiently quickly (which for the

), then the minimizers of (

Remark 3.1
.

Lower bounds on

.
Suppose that we are able to find an upper bound on
\varepsilon_{n}
of the form
n\varepsilon_{n}^{h(s)}\leq C
for some

and

. Combined with a lower bound of the form
\lim_{n\to\infty}\log(n)n^{-1}\varepsilon_{n}^{-g(d)}=0

(from (

Remark 3.3
.

Ill-posed case.
We want to comment on the results of Theorem

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|u_{n}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\leq C

Regarding the assumption that

. Indeed, by (

Remark 3.5
.

Sobolev embeddings.
Suppose that we had
\alpha=0
in the statement of Theorem

(equivalently suppose that the discrete eigenfunctions are uniformly bounded) then we would require
s>\max\{d/2+2,2d+9\}
. The condition
s>d/2+2
can also be considered from the point of view of Sobolev embeddings. By
[
22
, Lemma 4]
, we know that this implies
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathrm{C}^{0,\gamma}(\Omega)
for some
\gamma>0
. This is a natural consequence of being in the well-posed regime as we set pointwise constraints in our continuum problem (
19
), hence requiring the minimizer of the latter in
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\Omega)
to be at least continuous. However, we note that the same embedding would apply with the tighter condition
s>d/2
. We therefore conjecture that the extra +2 term in our result is an artifact of our proof of Theorem

.

Remark 3.6
.

The relationship between the asymptotics of
\varepsilon_{n}
and Sobolev embeddings.
Assuming the conjecture in Remark
3.5
, one is able to deduce what the optimal bounds on
\varepsilon_{n}
should be. Indeed, using the equivalence discussed in Remark
3.1
, we expect

\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{1/d}\ll\varepsilon_{n}\ll\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{1/2s}.

This implies the following two things about our results for the well-posed regime: firstly, we should be able to only impose the sharper lower bound (
.I
) which is related only to the connectivity of the graph; secondly, our requirement that
n\varepsilon^{s/2-1/2}
is bounded is not sharp and should be replaced with
n\varepsilon_{n}^{2s}
being bounded. On the other hand, the condition
n\varepsilon_{n}^{2s}\to\infty
is sharp for the ill-posed regime. The above considerations imply the existence of a gap in our analysis, namely the regime when

\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{1/(s/2-1/2)}\ll\varepsilon_{n}\ll\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{1/2s}.

While we postulate that this still corresponds to the well-posed regime, we investigate this hypothesis in Section
5
.

Remark 3.7
.

Lower bound gap.
As was pointed out in Remark
3.6
, replacing the lower bound

with

amounts to using (

) instead of (

and Theorem

Remark 3.8
.

Approximating Sobolev semi-norms and numerical schemes.
As succinctly mentioned in Section

allows one to transition from finite differences to derivatives. Informally (see

4.1

4.1.1
Weyl’s Law

Weyl’s law is well known in Euclidean and manifold settings with uniform density.
It states that eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of the unweighted Laplacian, i.e. in Euclidean domains
\Delta:=\Delta_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}
and on manifolds

is the Laplace-Beltrami operator, scale as
k^{2/d}
.
The version of Weyl’s law proven below is analogous to
[
22
, Lemma 28]
but adapted to our setting.

Proposition 4.1
.

Weyl’s law.
Let Assumptions
S.1
and
M.2
hold.
Define
\Delta_{\rho}
by (

be the eigenvalues of

arranged in increasing order.
Then, there exists constants

c_{W}k^{2/d}\leq\lambda_{k}\leq C_{W}k^{2/d}.

Proof.

Define
c=\min_{x\in\Omega}\rho(x)>0
and
C=\max_{x\in\Omega}\rho(x)<+\infty
.
Define

\mathcal{V}_{k-1}=\{V\subset\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(\Omega)\,|\,\dim(V)=k-1\}

and let

.
For

\frac{c^{2}}{C}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}\leq\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\rho^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\rho\,\mathrm{d}x}\leq\frac{C^{2}}{c}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}.

From the latter, we can deduce that:

\displaystyle\frac{c^{2}}{C}\sup_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{k-1}}\inf_{u\in(\mathrm{W}^{1,2}\cap V^{\perp})\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{k-1}}\inf_{u\in(\mathrm{W}^{1,2}\cap V^{\perp})\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\rho^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\rho\,\mathrm{d}x}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{2}}{c}\sup_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{k-1}}\inf_{u\in(\mathrm{W}^{1,2}\cap V^{\perp})\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x}.

Since

holds then the unweighted Laplacian,

where

is the

and hence, injecting (

for some constants

∎

Remark 4.10
.

Tail of the eigenvalues.
From the proof of Theorem
4.8
, it is apparent that it is the lack of control on the tail of the eigenvalues, i.e. on
\{\lambda_{n,k}\}_{k\geq\lfloor K_{n}\rfloor+1}
, that induces the term

C\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(u_{n})}n\varepsilon_{n}^{s/2-1/2}d_{\mathrm{man}}(x_{i},x_{j})

in (
54
). In turn, it is the latter that will imply the upper bound on
\varepsilon_{n}
as we will require
n\varepsilon_{n}^{s/2-1/2}
to be bounded for Proposition
4.13
.

In order to circumvent this, one could imagine only considering functions in the set
\mathrm{Tr}(n)=\{u:\Omega_{n}\to\mathbb{R}\,|\,\langle u,\psi_{n,k}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{n})}=0\text{ for $k\geq\lfloor K_{n}\rfloor+1$}\}
and substitute the truncated energy
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n},\text{trunc}}^{(s)}(u_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor K_{n}\rfloor}\lambda_{n,k}^{s}\langle u,\psi_{n,k}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{n})}^{2}
for
\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(\cdot)
. The authors believe the analysis to be analogous to the one presented here besides a few changes in Proposition
4.21
. The particularly nice feature of this truncated problem is that
\varepsilon_{n}
would no longer have an upper bound: we expect to be in the well-posed regime whenever
s>2\alpha+2+d/2
(with the sharp bound still being
s>d/2
as explained in Remark
3.6
) and in the ill-posed regime when
s<d/2
.

4.2

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|v(x_{i})-\ell_{i}|^{2}\mu(\{x\,|\,T_{n}(x)=x_{i}\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|v(x_{i})-\ell_{i}|^{2}\mu_{n}(\{x_{i}\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|v(x_{i})-\ell_{i}|^{2}}{n}

from which we deduce that

in
\mathrm{TL}^{2}
.

Let

be large enough so that (

) holds. We now show that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}(v_{n},\mu_{n})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(v_{n})\leq\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{\infty}(v).

To this purpose, we note that

v_{n}=\bar{v}_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}(\ell_{i}-\bar{v}_{n}(x_{i}))

and applying Lemma
4.15
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon}^{(s)}{}(v_{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(\bar{v}_{n})}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\ell_{i}-\bar{v}_{n}(x_{i})|\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(\delta_{x_{i}})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(\bar{v}_{n})}+C\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(\delta_{x_{i}})}

(84)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{n,\varepsilon_{n}}^{(s)}(\bar{v}_{n})}+C\left(\frac{1}{n\varepsilon_{n}^{2s}}\right)^{1/2}

(85)

where we used the fact that

is bounded on

for (

) (and

since by assumption

Having shown (

5.1
Critical boundary between well-posed and ill-posed regimes

In this section, we will investigate the gap in the upper bound alluded to in Remark
3.6
. In particular, we will rely on the same methodology as in
[

.

To test the gap between the well-posed and ill-posed regime, i.e.
n^{-\frac{2}{s-1}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{n}\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2s}}
(by Corollary
3.4
we know that
\varepsilon_{n}\lesssim n^{-\frac{2}{s-1}}
implies asymptotic well-posedness and
\varepsilon_{n}\gg n^{-\frac{1}{2s}}
implies asymptotic ill-posedness)
we will consider the following setting: we choose the uniform measure on
[0,1]^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions, the kernel function
\eta(t)=1
if
t\leq 1
and
\eta(t)=0
else and
s=16
. This choice of
s
satisfies the constraint
s>2d+9=13
from Remark
3.1
. The training set consists of the points
(0.1,0.1)
and
(0.9,0.9)
labelled
0
and
1
respectively.

We will vary the number of points in our graph from
n=100
to
n=5000
. For each
n
, we will also consider a wide range of
\varepsilon_{n}
ranging from roughly the connectivity radius to above
n^{-1/2s}
. For each combination of
(n,\varepsilon_{n})
, we then compute the discrete minimizer
u_{n}
using Lagrange multipliers and compare it to the continuum solution
u
. The continuum solution is computed using a finite-difference scheme on a regular grid of 10000 points on
[0,1]^{2}
.
The error considered is

\text{err}(n,\varepsilon_{n},u_{n})=\|u_{n}-u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mu_{n})}

(86)

where, in order to evalutate
u
on the graph, we use spline interpolation.
Finally, by re-sampling the points for each
n
, we repeat the experiments one hundred times and average the error.

We are interested in finding the value of
\varepsilon_{n}
where fractional Laplacian learning switches from the well-posed to the ill-posed regime. In order to compute the latter, we start by smoothing the function
\varepsilon_{n}\mapsto\text{err}(n,\varepsilon_{n},u_{n})
and compute the maximizer of its first derivative and the minimizer of its second derivative which we denote by
\hat{\varepsilon}_{n}
and
\varepsilon_{n}^{*}
respectively.
We choose
\hat{\varepsilon}_{n}
and
\varepsilon_{n}^{*}

greater than the minimizer of
\text{err}(n,\cdot,u_{n})
.
Both
\hat{\varepsilon}_{n}
and
\varepsilon_{n}^{*}
could be taken as reasonable definitions of the transition point between the well-posed and ill-posed regime and, it is therefore interesting to understand how they scale with
n
. Using the five largest values of
n
, the best linear fits between
\log(\hat{\varepsilon}_{n})
,
\log(\varepsilon_{n}^{*})
and
\log(n)
yield that

\hat{\varepsilon}_{n}\approx\frac{0.6541}{n^{0.05}}\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon_{n}^{*}\approx\frac{0.7312}{n^{0.06}}.

For
s=16

1/(2s)=0.03125
and
2/(s-1)\approx 0.134

\hat{\varepsilon}_{n}
and
\varepsilon_{n}^{*}
seem to scale accurately with powers that are different to

. In fact, we note that

. On one hand, this indicates that we should be able to extend the well-posed regime of Theorem
3.2
to

\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2/(s-1)}\ll\varepsilon_{n}\ll\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{1/s}

and, by Remark
3.1

3.7

On the other hand, we are not able to fully confirm the conjecture made in Remark
