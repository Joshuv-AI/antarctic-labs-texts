A Fueter operator for 3/2-spinors
Ahmad Reza Haj Saeedi Sadegh, Minh Lam Nguyen, Jesus Sanchez Jr

Abstract
. We show the non-compactness of the moduli space of solutions with a uniform bound on the curvatures of the monopole equations for 3 / 2 3/2 -spinors on a closed 3-manifold is equivalent to the existence of ‘3/2-Fueter sections’ that are solutions of a non-linear differential equation. These are sections of a fiber bundle whose fiber is a special 4-dimensional submanifold of the hyperkähler manifold of center-framed charged one S ​ U ​ ( 3 ) SU(3) -instantons on ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} . This fiber bundle does not inherit a hyperkähler structure. Our results provide a version of the Haydys correspondence when working with 3 / 2 3/2 -spinors. Furthermore, although the 3 / 2 3/2 monopole equations are nonlinear elliptic, the corresponding Fueter operator describing points within the compactification of the 3 / 2 3/2 -monopole equations lacks nonlinear ellipticity. We conclude with a generalization of the notion of Rarita-Schwinger-type operator which provides a framework for understanding the linearization of the 3 / 2 3/2 -Fueter operator and discuss non-ellipticity from this perspective. Keywords: Rarita-Schwinger operator, Seiberg-Witten equations, gauge theory, Fueter sections

Introduction

There is a generalization of the Seiberg-Witten equations (in dimension three or four) where a spinor bundle is replaced by some fiber bundle whose fibers are hyperkähler manifolds admitting certain
\text{Sp}(1)\times_{\mathbb{Z}/2}\text{U}(1)
-symmetry (e.g.,
[
21
,
19
,
4
,
12
,
10
]
). In Physics, such a geometric PDE defined on a base manifold (
source manifold
) associated with such a fiber bundle (
target manifold
) is called a gauged-sigma model. The generalized Seiberg-Witten (GSW) equations can be thought of as an analog of the symplectic vortex equations
[
5
]
, and the moduli space of solutions of certain GSW equations is conjectured to carry some important information about manifolds with special holonomy groups (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
6
]
). Unlike the classical Seiberg-Witten equations, the moduli space is not expected to be compact. Thus, understanding the boundary of the moduli space of GSW equations is an active research problem in mathematical gauge theory (e.g., see
[
16
,
17
,
20
]
). Moreover, any invariant derived from studying a gauged-sigma model is expected to carry both information about the target manifold and the source manifold. From a low-dimensional topology viewpoint, invariants obtained from GSW may shed some new information about the topology of 3- or 4-dimensional manifolds.

(Non-linear) Dirac (or Dirac-type) operators are among the key ingredients in defining various (generalized) SW equations. In
[
15
,
9
]
, we propose a different generalization of the Seiberg-Witten equations where we replace the Dirac operator with a non-Dirac type operator called the Rarita-Schwinger operator (a definition is given below). Our motivation is to introduce a program defining a topological (or geometrical) invariant of 3- and 4-manifolds using the Rarita-Schwinger operator in the context of Seiberg-Witten-type gauge theory. The Rarita-Schwinger operator was originally introduced to study the dynamics of
3/2
-spin particles; it is one of the few meaningful geometric first-order elliptic differential operators acting on a vector bundle which is not required to be
a priori
a Clifford module (e.g., see
[
23
,
2
]
).

The RS-SW equations share many similar features with generalized Seiberg-Witten equations (specifically, multiple-spinor Seiberg-Witten equations). However, there is already evidence that studying the Rarita-Schwinger Seiberg-Witten (RS-SW)-type equations is interesting. Firstly, in dimension 4, the non-compactness of the moduli space of solutions of RS-SW equations is directly tied with only topological information of the manifold (see
[
15
]
for more details). Secondly, in dimension 3, under a uniform
L^{6}
-boundedness of the curvature of the connection involved in the definition of the RS operator, there is a sequence of solutions up to gauge transformations converges weakly to a limiting (twisted) spinor and connection solving degenerate RS-SW equations (see
[
9
]
, also see below for more details). Thirdly, which is the main point of this paper, we extend a Haydys correspondence in this setting, we show that the limiting solutions of the degenerate RS-SW equations correspond to solutions of a non-linear Rarita-Schwinger operator
\mathfrak{Q}
.

Main result

Let
Y
be a closed, oriented smooth Riemannian
3
-manifold. Suppose
P_{spin^{c}}\to Y
is a
spin^{c}
structure on
Y
and
\not{S}:=P_{spin^{c}}\times_{Spin^{c}(3)}\mathbb{H}
is denoted by the associated spinor bundle over
Y
.
\not{S}
is a Clifford module; and thus there is a Dirac operator
D_{A}
on it.
D_{A}
is determined by

•

a Clifford multiplication induced by the Riemannian metric on
Y
,

•

the covariant derivative
\nabla_{A}
induced by the Levi-Civita connection on
Y
and a
U(1)
-connection
A\in\mathcal{A}(\det P_{spin^{c}})
on
\det P_{spin^{c}}=P_{spin^{c}}/Spin(3)
.

The Dirac operator
D_{A}
acts on sections of
\not{S}
, which are referred to as
1/2
-spinors in Physics. There is also a cousin to the Dirac operator called the
Rarita-Schwinger (RS) operator

[
18
,
14
,
2
]
. Associated to a
spin^{c}
structure on
Y
, a RS operator is also defined based on a choice of a
U(1)
-connection on the determinant line bundle of the
spin^{c}
structure. Very briefly, a RS operator
Q_{A}
is defined by

Q_{A}=\pi_{3/2}\circ D^{TY}_{A}|_{\Gamma(X,ker\,c)},

where

•

ker\,c
denotes the sub-bundle of
T^{*}Y\otimes\not{S}
that is the kernel of the Clifford multiplication
c:T^{*}Y\otimes\not{S}\to\not{S}
,

•

D^{TY}_{A}
is a Dirac operator on
T^{*}Y\otimes\not{S}
,

•

\pi_{3/2}
is the orthogonal projection of
T^{*}Y\otimes\not{S}\to ker\,c
.

The operator
Q_{A}
acts on sections of
ker\,c
, which are referred to as
3/2
-spinors
.
Q_{A}
is the first example of many higher-spin Dirac operators associated with a
spin^{c}
structure on
Y
. Having set up the terminologies, the Rarita-Schwinger-Seiberg-Witten (RS-SW) equations are a system of geometric PDEs on
Y
that look for unknowns
(A,\psi)\in\mathcal{A}(\det P_{spin^{c}})\times\Gamma(Y,ker\,c)
satisfying

(0.1)

\begin{cases}Q_{A}\psi=0,\\
\star_{3}F_{A}-\mu(\psi)=0.\end{cases}

where
\mu:ker\,c\subset\Gamma(Y,T^{*}Y\otimes\not{S})\to\Gamma\left(i\mathfrak{su}(\not{S})\right)
is a quadratic map that maps each
3/2
-spinor
\psi
to the traceless part of the endomorphism
\psi\psi^{*}
on
\not{S}
.

By blowing-up along the locus of the reducible solutions
\psi\equiv 0
of (
0.1
), we obtain the equations with an extra unknown
\epsilon\in(0,\infty)

(0.2)

\begin{cases}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{L^{4}}=1},\\
Q_{A}\psi=0,\\
\epsilon^{2}\star_{3}F_{A}-\mu(\psi)=0.\end{cases}

The reason for blowing-up using
L^{4}
-norm is given in Remark
0.1
below.

The equation (
0.1
) has an abelian gauge symmetry given by
\mathcal{G}=Maps(Y,S^{1})
. The moduli space is defined to be the solution space of (
0.1
) mod
\mathcal{G}
. Note that the solutions to (
0.1
) can also be thought of as critical points of a certain modified Chern-Simons-Dirac functional
\mathcal{L}^{RS}
, where (
0.1
) is interpreted as the minimizing condition for
\mathcal{L}^{RS}
(see
[
9
]
for more details).

In dimension four, the RS-SW equations were first introduced by the second-named author in
[
15
]
. The moduli space of the
4
-D RS-SW equations is known to be
not
compact in general–a feature that is different from the classical Seiberg-Witten theory. There is a topological obstruction in terms of an inequality relating the second Betti number
b_{2}
and the signature of the four manifolds that guarantees the non-compactness of the moduli space. Thus, on those four manifolds that satisfy such topological conditions, we are guaranteed to have non-trivial solutions of the RS-SW equations.

The story is a bit different in dimension three. In
[
9
]
, we studied the behavior of the convergence of solutions of (
0.1
) after the blow-up procedure. In particular, we proved that if
\{(A_{n},\psi_{n},\epsilon_{n})\}
is a sequence of solutions of (
0.2
) such that
F_{A_{n}}
are uniformly bounded in
L^{6}
and
\epsilon_{n}
gets arbitrarily small, then away from a closed nowhere-dense subset
Z\subset Y
, after passing through a subsequence and up to gauge transformations,
A_{n}
converges weakly to
A
in
L^{2}_{1,loc}
,
\psi_{n}
converges weakly to
\psi
in
L^{2}_{2,loc}
, and
(A,\psi)
must satisfy the following degenerate situation

(0.3)

Q_{A}\psi=0,\quad\mu(\psi)=0.

Remark 0.1
.

We give a brief comment on why we blow up (
0.1
) using the
L^{4}
-norm as opposed to the
L^{2}
-norm that usually appears in ordinary generalized Seiberg-Witten theory. Unlike the Wietzenböck formula of the Dirac operator,
Q_{A}^{2}
contains a second-order term
P_{A}P^{*}_{A}
, where
P_{A}:=\pi_{3/2}\circ\nabla_{A}
. A common strategy to prove such a convergence result stated above is starting with Green’s integration by parts formula, where one has to control
\left\lVert P^{*}_{A}\psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
. Essentially, the divergence of
\psi
can be controlled by the curvature
F_{A}
via gauge-fixing if
\psi
is assumed to have
L^{4}
-unit norm by a standard elliptic estimate applied to the Rarita-Schwinger operator in dimension 3 (see Section 3 of
[
9
]
for more details).

The limiting solutions of the moduli space are called
3/2
-Fueter sections
. Formally, they solve (
0.2
) by setting
\epsilon=0
. Then one of the consequences of the above statement is that the existence of
3/2
-Fueter sections is an obstruction to the compactness of the moduli space of (
0.1
). Indirectly, if one can show that (
0.2
) with
\epsilon=0
has no solution in certain situations, then potentially the moduli space of (
0.1
) would be compact!

In this paper, we study the degenerate situation of (
0.1
). Viewing the Clifford module
T^{*}Y\otimes\not{S}
as a (linear) hyperkähler fiber bundle over
Y
and
\mu
as an associated hyperkähler moment
map
, the hyperkähler reduction
\mu^{-1}(0)/S^{1}
in turn is another hyperkähler fiber bundle over
Y

[
13
]
. We consider a subbundle of
\mu^{-1}(0)/S^{1}
by intersecting the entire total space with
ker\,c
. Denote such a subbundle by
\mathscr{W}_{0}

whose fiber dimension is
4
(ref. Proposition
2.1
)
. Note that
\mathscr{W}_{0}
no longer necessarily inherits a hyperkähler structure. However, there is a one-to-one correspondence between solutions of (
0.3
) and solutions of a certain non-linear differential operator
\mathfrak{Q}
defined on
\mathscr{W}_{0}
. In particular, the main result of this paper shows that

Theorem
.

For any gauge equivalence class of solutions
[(A,\psi)]
of (
0.3
), there is a solution

\Psi\in\Gamma(\mathscr{W}_{0})
of the
3/2
-Fueter equation

(0.4)

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathfrak{Q}\Psi=0.

Conversely, for any solution
\Psi
of (
0.4
), there exists a
\text{Spin}^{c}(3)
-structure
P_{\text{Spin}^{c}}\to Y
with a connection
A

on its determinant line bundle, a section
\psi\in\Gamma(\mathscr{W})
where
\mathscr{W}:=P_{\text{Spin}^{c}}\times_{\text{Spin}^{c}(3)}W
such that the pair
(A,\psi)
satisfies the equation (
0.3
). Moreover, any two solutions
\left(A_{1},\psi_{1}\right)
and
\left(A_{2},\psi_{2}\right)
of (
0.3
) which correspond to
\Psi
must be gauge equivalent. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{Q}
is a non-linear operator that is not elliptic.

We call
\mathfrak{Q}
a
3/2-Fueter operator
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time such an operator has been defined in the literature. Its construction mirrors that of the Fueter operator, which can be thought of as a generalized (non-linear) Dirac operator defined on a hyperkähler fiber bundle over
Y
associated with a
\text{Spin}^{c}
structure (see
[
12
,
10
,
21
]
). Whenever there is a Dirac operator, there is also an RS operator. Following this philosophy, it is not unexpected to have a counterpart of the Fueter operator for the
3/2
-spinors. Our 3/2-Fueter operator
\mathfrak{Q}
fulfills exactly this role. In other words,
\mathfrak{Q}
is the gauged-sigma model for the RS operator.

One of the main technical difficulties one faces when working with
\mathscr{W}_{0}
is that it is defined using both Clifford contraction
c
and the quadratic map
\mu
, and these maps do not interact cleanly with one another. This creates several problems when one wants to ascertain basic properties of the objects at play e.g.

(I)

smoothness of
\mathscr{W}_{0}
(cf. Proposition
2.1
);

(II)

analytic properties of the linearization of
\mathfrak{Q}
(cf. Theorem
3.2
).

In the hyperkähler framework, the quaternionic structure does most of the work. In our framework we needed to utilize a different structure, namely that there is a background
Spin^{c}\left(3\right)
action which preserves the quadratic map and the bundle
\ker c\rightarrow Y
. Perhaps the result which is most exotic in our main theorem is the fact that the linearization of
\mathfrak{Q}
is
not
a nonlinear elliptic operator. Following the usual expectation, one may have conjectured that for a general nonlinear elliptic PDE, the compactification of its solution space would consist of adding in solutions to an appropriate limiting nonlinear PDE which maintained ellipticity. In fact, prior to the writing of this paper, we believed that such would be the case. This has been the case for many nonlinear elliptic PDEs’ within the literature
[
22
,
6
,
11
,
10
,
8
]
; but does not seem to be so for the
3/2
-monopole equations on a
3
-manifold.

Remark 0.2
.

One should think of the main theorem above as a so-called
Haydys correspondence
for 3/2-spinors (see
[
12
,
10
]
).

Remark 0.3
.

We have a rather explicit local description of the fibers of
\mathscr{W}_{0}
(ref. Proposition
2.1
). The fibers are
4
-dimensional manifolds and can be viewed as a variety, which means that the total space
\mathscr{W}_{0}
is
7
-dimensional. It would be interesting to know more about the topology and geometry of
\mathscr{W}_{0}
.

Remark 0.4
.

In this article, the construction of the fiber bundle
\mathscr{W}_{0}
and the 3/2-Fueter operator is obtained from applying the fiberwise hyperkähler reduction to the subbundle of
3/2
-spinors associated with a
\text{Spin}^{c}
-structure on
Y
. One could generalize from the
\text{Spin}^{c}
-structure to a more general
Clifford module
, that is a complex (hermitian) vector bundle
\mathscr{E}\to Y
that carries a fiberwise Clifford action of the tangent bundle
TY
, i.e., a bundle map
c:TY\otimes\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{E}
with a fixed Clifford connection. In this situation, one can consider the subbundle
\mathscr{W}:=\ker(c)
which carries a similar contraction of the Dirac operator, a generalized Rarita-Schwinger operator
Q:\Gamma(\mathscr{W})\to\Gamma(\mathscr{W})
. Assume there is a fiberwise moment map
\mu:\mathscr{E}\to i\mathfrak{su}(2)
that is invariant under the
U(1)
-action (induced from the complex structure). If
0\in i\mathfrak{su}(2)
is a regular value for both
\mu
and
\mu|_{\mathscr{W}}
then one can apply the hyperkähler reductions to fibers of
\mathscr{W}
to obtain a fiber bundles
\mathscr{E}_{0}\to Y
and
\mathscr{W}_{0}\to Y
. The bundle
\mathscr{E}_{0}
is a hyperkähler bundle which carries a Fueter operator. It would be interesting to see if one can mimic the method in this article to obtain a 3/2-Fueter operator
\mathfrak{Q}
on
\mathscr{W}_{0}
.

The RS-SW equations can be of interest to high-energy Physics. From a physical perspective, the equations describe a minimally coupled
U(1)
gauge field with spin
3/2
-charged matter. In Physics, spin
3/2
-fields are usually considered to be a part of the supergravity multiplet, but those would not be charged under
U(1)
gauge symmetry. Thus, solutions of (
0.1
) should be referred to as spin
3/2
-charged matter. However, it is possible to swap out
U(1)
with some other compact Lie group
G
to account for the twisted supergravity phenomenon and derive a similar behavior of convergence result as in
[
9
]
and a version of Haydys correspondence analogous to the main result of this paper. This circle of ideas will be addressed elsewhere.

As a concluding remark for this subsection, we state the main motivation for us to study (
0.1
). Our larger goal is to derive a new
3
-manifold invariant using RS operator. The equations we study fall largely within a framework of Lagrangian QFTs. Thus, one could either define a numerical invariant via Rozansky-Witten’s approach by taking a formal path integral of
\exp(\text{const}\cdot\mathcal{L}^{RS})
against the moduli space of (
0.1
), or define a homological invariant via a certain Floer theory approach that should categorify the previously mentioned numerical invariant. The results in our previous paper
[
9
]
and in this current one serve as a first step toward this program.

Organization of the paper

In Section
1
of the paper, we discuss background materials. We begin by recalling the notions of connection and covariant derivative on fiber bundles, 3/2-spinors, and hyperkähler structure and its reduction. We then discuss the notion of hyperkähler bundles and their associated generalized Dirac operators.

In Section
2
, we introduce an 8-dimensional hyperkähler manifold with an action of
\text{Spin}^{c}\left(3\right)
and a special invariant 4-dimensional submanifold
W_{0}
. We study this 4-manifold in more detail in the subsequent subsections. We show the existence of a canonical normal bundle over this manifold. We then introduce a nonlinear differential operator on this bundle that is called the
3/2-Fueter operator
.

In Subsections
3.1
and
3.2
, we prove the paper’s main result by dividing it into two parts. In the first part, we prove that the existence of solutions to the degeneracy equations (
0.3
) is equivalent to the existence of solutions to the 3/2-Fueter operator. In the second part, we prove that the 3/2-Fueter equation is a non-elliptic equation.

In Section
4
, we give a definition of a generalized Rarita-Schwinger-type operator, which is a general formalism of the linearized
3/2
-Fueter operator. There, we also discuss a general (non)ellipticity result regarding these operators. Finally, we give an alternative proof of the non-ellipticity of the linearization of
\mathfrak{Q}
based on the general (non)ellipticity result developed there.

1.
Preliminaries

1.1.
Connections on fiber bundles

We first recall some basic facts about
Ehresmann
connections on a fiber bundle. Consider a fiber
bundle

\pi:\mathscr{M}\to Y
. The
vertical bundle

\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}
is defined as the kernel of the differential map
d\pi:T\mathscr{M}\to TY
. A choice of (
Ehresmann
) connection on
\mathscr{M}\to Y
determines a bundle decomposition
T\mathscr{M}=\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\oplus\mathcal{H}\mathscr{M}
where
we call

\mathcal{H}\mathscr{M}
the horizontal bundle whose fibers naturally identify with tangent spaces of
Y
, i.e., for all
p\in\mathscr{M}
:

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{H}\mathscr{M}|_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\simeq}\limits^{d\pi}}T_{\pi(p)}Y.

The covariant derivative (associated with the connection) of a section of the fiber bundle is defined as follows. Let
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathscr{M})
be a section, i.e., a map
\phi:Y\to\mathscr{M}
such that
\pi\circ\phi=\textup{id}_{Y}.
Taking a differential gives a bundle map
d\phi:TY\to T\mathscr{M}.
Composing with the projection map
T\mathscr{M}\to\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}
gives the covariant derivative of the section:

\nabla\phi:TY\to\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}

and equivalently, a
\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}
-valued one-form

(1.1)

\nabla\phi\in\Omega^{1}(Y,\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}).

Remark 1.1
.

When
\pi:\mathscr{M}\to Y
is a vector bundle, one has a natural isomorphism
\mathscr{M}\simeq\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}
and hence the covariant derivative above is an
\mathscr{M}
-valued one-form.

When
P\to Y
is a principal
G
-bundle, by a connection, we mean an equivariant decomposition
TP=\mathcal{V}P\oplus\mathcal{H}P
. Fixing a connection on the principal bundle
P\to Y

gives a covariant derivative on any associated bundle
\mathscr{M}=P\times_{G}M

where
M
is a
manifold with an action of
G
; any section
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathscr{M})
can be considered as an equivariant function
\phi:P\to M
. The restriction of the differential
d\phi:TP\to TM
to the horizontal subbundle
\mathcal{H}P
gives
a
G
-equivariant
bundle map

d\phi|_{\mathcal{H}P}:\mathcal{H}P\to TM.

which is equivalent to a map
\nabla\phi:TY\to P\times_{G}TM
. Now using the canonical isomorphism
\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\simeq P\times_{G}TM
we have obtained the bundle map

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla\phi:TY\to\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M},

and equivalently, a vector bundle valued one-form

\nabla\phi\in\Omega^{1}(Y,\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}).

1.2.
3/2-spinors

In this article,
\mathbb{H}
denotes the division algebra of quaternions with its natural inner product, and
\text{Im}\mathbb{H}
denotes its subspace of pure imaginary elements.
Let
\mathbb{H}^{\pm}
be the positive and negative half-spin representations of the Clifford algebra
\text{C}\ell(4)
with their natural identification with quaternions
\mathbb{H}
. Both
\mathbb{H}^{\pm}
give the two spin representations of the Clifford algebra
\text{C}\ell(3)
; however, they are equivalent as the representations of the spin group
\text{Spin}(3)\subset\text{C}\ell(3)
.

The Clifford representation of
\text{C}\ell(3)=\text{C}\ell(\text{Im}\mathbb{H})
on
\mathbb{H}
is induced from the standard left action of the imaginary quaternions on the quaternions. It is easy to see that this representation is complex.

The Clifford action on the spinors
\mathbb{H}
then gives a linear map
(identifying
\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{3}
)

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{c}:\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle\left(z\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}q\right)^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}w

\displaystyle\mapsto z\cdot q\cdot w.

where
\left(z\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}q\right)^{*}
denotes the Hermitian dual. The kernel of the above map is what we call the space of
3/2-spinors
:

W:=\ker(\widehat{c}).

Observe that
\text{Im}\mathbb{H}
admits an action by
\text{SU}\left(2\right)\simeq\mathbb{S}^{3}
via the adjoint representation, and
\mathbb{H}
admits the standard left action of
\text{SU}\left(2\right)
. Thus,
\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H}\right)
also admits an action of
SU(2)
. The standard hermitian structure on
\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H}\right)
given by
\langle\phi,\psi\rangle:=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left(\phi\psi^{*}\right)
is
\text{SU}\left(2\right)
-invariant. This hermitian structure coincides with the tensor hermitian structure on
(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H})^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{H}\simeq\textup{Hom}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})
. One can check that in this case that the map
\widehat{c}
is
\text{SU}\left(2\right)
-equivariant. Thus
W
is also acted on by
\text{SU}\left(2\right)
with weight
m=3/2
and
the orthogonal complement of
W
in
\textup{Hom}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})
is a copy of spinor space
\mathbb{H}
. Indeed, consider the map

(1.2)

\displaystyle\iota:\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle\to\textup{Hom}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}q^{*}\otimes qsdV\left(q\right)

The map
\iota
is
\text{SU}\left(2\right)
-equivariant and provides
the
\text{SU}\left(2\right)
-invariant orthogonal decomposition

\textup{Hom}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})=\iota(\mathbb{H})\oplus W.

We also put a Riemannian structure on
M^{+}:=\textup{Hom}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})
, by putting the inner product on the tangent spaces
T_{\phi}M^{+}\simeq M^{+}
given by

(A,B):=\frac{1}{2}\textup{Re}(\textup{tr}(AB^{*})).

1.3.
Hyperkähler
manifolds

Let
(M,g,I,J,K)
be a hyperkähler manifold that is a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with three integrable almost complex structures on the tangent bundle that are
g
-
Kähler
and satisfy the quaternionic relations:

I^{2}=J^{2}=K^{2}=IJK=-1.

One has three Kähler forms

\omega_{I}(u,v):=g(Iu,v),\ \ \omega_{J}(u,v):=g(Ju,v),\ \ \omega_{K}(u,v):=g(Ku,v).

Proposition 1.2
.

[
13
]

With respect to the complex structure
I
, one has the holomorphic symplectic form

\Omega=\omega_{J}+i\omega_{K}.

Conversely, any Kähler manifold with a holomorphic symplectic form is hyperkähler.

The 2-sphere of complex structures

(1.3)

\mathfrak{b}(M,g)=\{aI+bJ+cK:a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}=1\}

consists of integrable almost complex structures on
M
that are Kähler with respect to the metric
g
. When the Riemannian metric is fixed we drop the reference to the metric for brevity of the notation. Hence for any oriented orthonormal basis
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the triple
(I^{\prime},J^{\prime},K^{\prime})
with

I^{\prime}:=u_{1}\cdot(I,J,K),\ J^{\prime}:=u_{2}\cdot(I,J,K),\ K^{\prime}=u_{3}\cdot(I,J,K),

give a hyperkähler structure on
(M,g)
equivalent to the original one (obtained by a rotation).

The most basic examples of a hyperkähler manifolds are quaternionic vector spaces, including
\mathbb{H}
or
\textup{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(E,\mathbb{H})
where
E
is a complex hermitian vector space. One obtains more such examples via hyperkähler quotients.

1.4.
Hyperkähler reduction

Consider a hyperkähler manifold
(M,g,I,J,K)
carrying an action of a compact Lie group
G

preserving
the symplectic forms
\omega_{I},\omega_{J},\omega_{K}
. Such an action of
G
on
M
is called a
hyperkähler action
.

For any complex structure
S\in\{I,J,K\}
and any
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, by Cartan’s magic formula we have

0=L_{X^{M}_{\xi}}\omega_{S}=d(\iota_{X^{M}_{\xi}}\omega_{S})+\iota_{X^{M}_{\xi}}(d\omega_{S}),

where
\iota_{v}
denotes the usual contraction operator of a differential form by a vector field on
M
, and
X^{M}_{\xi}
denotes the
Killing
vector field on
M
associated with
\xi
. Since
\omega_{S}
is a symplectic form, the above identity tells us that
\iota_{X^{M}_{\xi}}\omega_{S}
is a closed
1
-form. Furthermore, if we assume that
H^{1}_{dR}(M;\mathbb{R})
is trivial, then every closed
1
-form is exact. Thus, there exists a function

\mu^{S}_{X^{M}_{\xi}}:M\to\mathbb{R},\quad d\mu^{S}_{X^{M}_{\xi}}=\iota_{X^{M}_{\xi}}\omega_{S}.

For this, we may define (
since
G
is compact we may assume that there is an
Ad
-invariant metric on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
G
) a map
\mu^{S}:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
where

\langle\mu^{S}(m),\xi\rangle=\mu^{S}_{X^{M}_{\xi}}(m).

We can combine each of the moment maps defined above as

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mu=\mu^{I}i+\mu^{J}j+\mu^{K}k:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H}\cong\mathfrak{g}^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{su}(2).

Here,
\{i,j,k\}
denotes the standard bases of the purely imaginary quaternions
\text{Im}\mathbb{H}
. Note that by construction, such a map
\mu
is
G
-equivariant. This prompts the following definition.

Definition 1.3
.

A
hyperkähler moment map
of a hyperkähler action
G\curvearrowright M
is a map
\mu:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{su}(2)
such that

(1)

\mu
is
G
-equivariant,

(2)

d\langle\mu,\xi\rangle=\iota_{X^{M}_{\xi}}\omega
, for all
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\omega
is the hyperkähler form associated with the hyperkähler structure on
M
. Here we view
\omega
as the triple of hyperkähler forms previously defined, now interpreted as an
\mathfrak{su}(2)
-valued form.

If a hyperkähler
G
-action possesses a hyperkähler moment map
\mu
, we say that
G\curvearrowright M
is a
tri-Hamiltonian action
.

Remark 1.4
.

The notation
\mu
used to describe a hyperkähler moment map on
G
-hyperkähler manifold
M
is standard. However, in the situation where
M:=\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(E,\mathbb{H})
and
G=U(1)
, where
E
is some
n
-dimensional hermitian vector space, we consider a particular moment map that coincides with the quadratic map that appears in (generalized) Seiberg-Witten theory (cf. Example
1.7
). Thus, with abuse of notation, we use the same label
\mu
for such a moment map on
\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(E,\mathbb{H})
. Of course, this is a very specific phenomenon that happens for certain
linear
hyperkähler manifolds, and not all hyperkähler
U(1)
-manifolds will have a moment map arise in this manner.

To get a new hyperkähler manifold from an old one, we suppose that there is a tri-Hamiltonian action of
G
which is free and consider the associated hyperkähler moment map

\mu:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{su}(2).

Theorem 1.5
.

[
13
]

If

is an invariant regular value, then the quotient
\mu^{-1}(c)/G
is a hyperkähler manifold of dimension
\dim(M)-4\dim(G).

When the choice of the invariant regular value

is clear, we simply denote by
M\mathbin{/\mkern-5.0mu/\mkern-5.0mu/}G
the hyperkähler reduction
\mu^{-1}(c)/G
. Denote by
\mathcal{L}\subset TM
the subbundle spanned by the Killing vector field of the action of
G
. So, the fibers of
\mathcal{L}
are isomorphic to the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. We will need the following lemma in the subsequent sections:

Lemma 1.6
.

[
13
]

One has the following
G
-equivariant orthogonal decomposition:

TM|_{\mu^{-1}(c)}=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{L}\oplus I\mathcal{L}\oplus J\mathcal{L}\oplus K\mathcal{L}

where
\mathcal{H}
is a vector bundle whose fiber is isometrically isomorphic to the tangent spaces of
M\mathbin{/\mkern-5.0mu/\mkern-5.0mu/}G
under the quotient map
\tau:M\to M\mathbin{/\mkern-5.0mu/\mkern-5.0mu/}G
and one also has

T\mu^{-1}(c)=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{L}.

Example 1.7

Here, we view

1.5.
Hyperkähler bundles

The hyperkähler structure gives a parallel action of
\text{Im}\mathbb{H}
on the tangent bundle
M
:

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma:M\times\text{Im}\mathbb{H}\to\textup{End}(TM)

(x,ai+bj+ck)\mapsto aI_{x}+bJ_{x}+cK_{x}

which satisfies the Clifford relations:

\gamma(v)^{2}+|v|^{2}=0.

(Note that
\gamma
can be equivalently given by an isometry between the unit sphere
\textup{S}(\text{Im}\mathbb{H})
and
the fibers of
\mathfrak{b}(M)
, the bundle of 2-spheres of complex structures on the tangent spaces.
) Hence, one obtains the Clifford action

(1.6)

\gamma:M\times\text{C}\ell(3)\to\textup{End}(TM)

that is parallel with respect to the Riemannian metric.
Conversely, any parallel Clifford action (
1.6
) is given by an oriented isometry

\gamma:M\times\textup{S}(\text{Im}\mathbb{H})\to\mathfrak{b}(M).

This observation extends to hyperkähler bundles over 3-manifolds due to Taubes
[
21
]
. Fix an oriented Riemannian 3-manifold
(Y,g_{Y})
which is always parallelizable. We then can write

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}TY\simeq T^{*}Y\simeq P_{\text{SO}}\times_{\text{SO}(3)}\text{Im}\mathbb{H}.

Here
P_{\text{SO}}
is the bundle of oriented orthonormal frames of
Y
.

Definition 1.8
.

By a
hyperkähler bundle
over
Y
we mean a fiber bundle
\pi:\mathscr{M}\to Y
with fiber a hyperkähler manifold
(M,g,I,J,K)
with an isometry of bundles
\gamma:\textup{S}(T^{*}Y)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\to}\limits^{\simeq}}\mathfrak{b}(\mathscr{M})
. Here
\textup{S}(T^{*}Y)
is the unit tangent bundle and
\mathfrak{b}(\mathscr{M})
is defined fiberwise as in (
1.3
). By the discussion above, we then obtain an action

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma:\pi^{*}T^{*}Y\to\textup{End}(\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M})

that extends to a bundle map
\gamma:\pi^{*}\text{C}\ell(T^{*}Y)\to\textup{End}(\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M})
,
where
\text{C}\ell(T^{*}Y)
is the bundle of Clifford algebras associated with the Riemannian structure.
Equivalently, one obtains a section of the tensor bundle

(1.7)

\gamma\in\Gamma(\pi^{*}TY\otimes\textup{Hom}(\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}))

which is parallel along the fibers of
\mathscr{M}
.

In this paper, the main examples of hyperkähler bundles are associated bundles. For some compact Lie group
\mathscr{K}
, a principal
\mathscr{K}
-bundle
P
, and a hyperkähler manifold
M
with a
\mathscr{K}
-action, we form the hyperkähler bundle
\mathscr{M}:=P\times_{\mathscr{K}}M
. A special case treated here is for

M=\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{3},\mathbb{H})\backslash\{0\}\ \textup{or}\ \Big(\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{3},\mathbb{H})\backslash\{0\}\Big)\mathbin{/\mkern-5.0mu/\mkern-5.0mu/}U(1),

\mathscr{K}=\text{Spin}^{c}(3),

and
P
is a principal
\text{Spin}^{c}(3)
-bundle.

1.6.
The Fueter operator

Let
\mathscr{M}\to Y
be a hyperkähler bundle and fix
an Ehresmann connection on it.
For a section
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathscr{M})
, we can pull back the Clifford section (
1.7
) to obtain:

(1.8)

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\phi^{*}\gamma\in\Gamma(Y,TY\otimes\phi^{*}\textup{End}(\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M})).

Then we may compose this pullback section with the covariant derivative
\nabla\phi\in\Omega^{1}(Y,\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M})
to obtain the
Fueter operator

\mathfrak{F}
:

(1.9)

\mathfrak{F}\phi:=\phi^{*}\gamma\circ\nabla\phi\in\Gamma(Y,\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}).

The Fueter operator is a nonlinear generalization of Dirac operators first introduced in
[
21
]
. When
N
is a representation of the Clifford algebra
\text{C}\ell(3)
, one obtains a Clifford module
\mathscr{N}:=P_{\text{Spin}^{c}}\times_{\text{Spin}^{c}(3)}N
. Since
\mathscr{N}
is a vector bundle, for any section
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathscr{N})
, one has the natural identification
\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{N}\simeq\mathscr{N}
, and hence the Fueter operator is the usual Dirac operator

\mathfrak{F}\phi\in\Gamma(\mathscr{N}).

We can say a bit about the linearization of the Fueter operator. First observe that the Fueter operator defines a vector field on the infinite-dimensional manifold of global sections
\Gamma\left(Y,\mathscr{M}\right)
, where the tangent space at a section
\phi\in\Gamma\left(Y,\mathscr{M}\right)
is given by
T_{\phi}\Gamma\left(Y,\mathscr{M}\right)\simeq\Gamma\left(Y,\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\right)
. Thus, if we want to linearize the Fueter operator, we will need some notion of connection for the tangent bundle
T\Gamma\left(Y,\mathscr{M}\right)
. For this, we use the fact that
\mathscr{M}\rightarrow Y
is a bundle of Riemannian manifolds which implies that the fibers have a Levi-Civita connection. In this case, we can define the parallel transport along a smooth one-parameter family of sections
\phi_{t}:[a,b]\rightarrow\Gamma\left(Y,\mathscr{M}\right)
as follows: for
V\in\Gamma\left(\phi_{t}^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\right)
and
y\in Y
, and
s\in[a,b]
we let

\displaystyle P^{T\Gamma\left(Y,\mathscr{M}\right)}_{t\rightarrow s}:\Gamma\left(\phi^{*}_{t}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Gamma\left(Y,\phi^{*}_{s}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\right)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(y\rightarrow P^{\mathscr{M}_{y},LC}_{\phi_{t}(y)\rightarrow\phi_{s}(y)}V_{y}\right)

Now observe that the pullback of the vertical bundle gives a
Clifford module

(1.10)

\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\to Y.

To see why this bundle is a Clifford module, first note that the action of quaternions on
\mathcal{V}\mathbb{M}
can be interpreted as an action of the tangent bundle
TY\simeq Y\times\text{Im}\mathbb{H}
. Hence, one obtains a linear Dirac operator
D^{\phi}
on the Clifford module (
1.10
).

The following proposition is well-known. However, we could not pinpoint an exact reference to a proof of it in the literature. Below, we give a detailed proof of the statement.

Proposition 1.9
.

The linearization
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{F}_{\phi}
of the Fueter operator (
1.9
) at
\phi
is given by
D^{\phi}
. In particular, the linearization is elliptic.

Proof.

We first describe the induced connection on the Clifford module
\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}
. We may identify the tangent space
T_{\phi}\Gamma(Y,\mathscr{M})
with
\Gamma(Y,\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M})
. Hence the linearization of the connection
\nabla
at
\phi
gives a map

\nabla^{\phi}:=L_{\phi}\nabla:\Gamma(Y,\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M})\to\Gamma(Y,TY^{*}\otimes\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M})

\psi\mapsto\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}P_{\phi_{t}}\nabla\phi_{t}

where
\phi_{t}\in\Gamma(T,\mathscr{M})
is a curve of sections
with
\phi_{0}=\phi
such that
\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}\phi_{t}=\psi
and
P_{\phi_{t}}:\phi_{t}^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\to\phi_{0}^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}
is the bundle isomorphism given, for every
y\in Y
, by the parallel transport along the curve
s\mapsto\phi_{s}(y)
in
\mathscr{M}_{y}
via the Levi-Civita connection on the fiber
\mathscr{M}_{y}
.

The linearization
\nabla^{\phi}
gives a connection on the vector bundle
\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\to Y
. To see why this is a connection, take
\psi\in\Gamma(Y,\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M})
which can be represented by a curve of sections
\phi_{t}\in\Gamma(Y,\mathscr{M})
, i.e.,
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi_{t}|_{t=0}=\psi
.
We need to show
\nabla^{\phi}f\cdot\psi=f\nabla^{\phi}\psi+df\cdot\psi
for
f\in C^{\infty}(Y)
. First, note the “tangent vector”
f\cdot\psi
corresponds to the curve
\phi_{f\cdot t}

given by
t\mapsto\phi_{f(y)t}(y)
for each fixed
y\in Y
. So, we may write

\nabla^{\phi}f\cdot\psi=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}P_{\phi_{f.t}}\nabla\phi_{f\cdot t}=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}(P_{\phi_{f.t}}{\pi^{v}d(\phi_{f\cdot t}}))

=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}(P_{\phi_{f.t}}\pi^{v}(d\phi)|_{f\cdot t}+t\cdot df\cdot P_{\phi_{f.t}}\pi^{v}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}|_{s=f\cdot t}\phi_{s})=f\nabla^{\phi}\psi+df\cdot\psi.

Here
\pi^{v}:T\mathscr{M}\to\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}
is the projection along the horizontal bundle associated with the connection onto the vertical bundle,
\nabla=\pi^{v}\circ d
, and
d\phi|_{f.t}
is given by
t\mapsto d\phi_{s}(y)|_{s=f.t}
for each fixed
y\in Y
. The equality from the first to the second line follows from the equality
d(\phi_{f.t})=d\phi|_{f.t}+t.df.\frac{\partial}{\partial s}|_{s=f.t}\phi_{s}
.

To see why
L_{\phi}(\mathfrak{F})=D^{\phi}
, first note that the Dirac operator
D^{\phi}
on
\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}
is defined by

D^{\phi}=\phi^{*}\gamma\circ\nabla^{\phi}:\Gamma(Y,\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M})\to\Gamma(Y,\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}).

Since
\gamma
is parallel along fibers of
\mathscr{M}
, we have
P_{\phi_{t}}\phi_{t}^{*}\gamma P_{\phi_{t}}^{-1}=\phi_{0}^{*}\gamma
. So, we may write

L_{\phi}(\mathfrak{F})\psi=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}P_{\phi_{t}}\mathfrak{F}\phi_{t}=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}P_{\phi_{t}}\phi_{t}^{*}\gamma\circ\nabla\phi_{t}=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}\big(P_{\phi_{t}}\phi_{t}^{*}\gamma P_{\phi_{t}}^{-1}\circ P_{\phi_{t}}\nabla\phi_{t}\big)

=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}\big(\phi_{0}^{*}\gamma\circ P_{\phi_{t}}\nabla\phi_{t}\big)=\phi^{*}\gamma\circ L_{\phi}\nabla\psi=D^{\phi}\psi,

as claimed.
∎

Remark 1.10
.

The connection
\nabla^{\phi}
on
\phi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\to Y
does not need to be a Clifford connection
[
3
]
, so the Dirac operator
D^{\phi}
does not share many of the geometric properties of the classical Dirac operators (such as the Lichnerowicz-Weitzenböck formula, etc.). However, its symbol is still given by the Clifford multiplication by the corresponding covectors.

2.
An aquaternionic moduli space

In this section, we fix the following notations:

{M}:=\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})\backslash\{0\},

which is hyperkähler manifold with a
U(1)
-equivariant moment map

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{M}_{0}:=M^{\mu}/U(1).}

is no longer a regular value.

2.1.
\textup{Spin}\left(3\right)
action

The
set-up in (

)
was crucial in the description of the noncompactness theorem for the multi-spinor Seiberg-Witten equation
[

]
in terms of nonlinear harmonic spinors.
We have an action of
\textup{Spin}(3)
on
\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H}
where
\textup{Spin}(3)
acts on the
\text{Im}\mathbb{H}

Also,
\textup{Spin}(3)
acts on
\mathbb{H}
by restricting the action of the Clifford algebra
\text{C}\ell(3)
to the
\textup{Spin}(3)
group. Therefore, we have an action of
\textup{Spin}(3)
on
\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})

carries an action of

\textup{Spin}\left(3\right)
.

2.2.
An aquaternionic quotient

As in Section
1.2
, we have an orthogonal decomposition

\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}\otimes\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})=\iota(\mathbb{H})\oplus W

where
\iota:\mathbb{H}\to\textup{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})
is an
SU(2)
-equivariant embedding and
W
is the vector space of
3/2
-spinors given as the kernel of the Clifford action. This decomposition is

preserved by the diagonal action of
\text{Spin}\left(3\right)
. Denote the image of
W^{\mu}:=W\cap\Big(\mu^{-1}(0)\backslash\{0\}\Big)
under the projection
M\to M_{0}
by
W_{0}
which carries an action of
\text{Spin}(3)
. Note that if we add in right multiplication by elements in
U(1)
, then the action by
\text{Spin}\left(3\right)
is enlarged to action of
\text{Spin}^{c}\left(3\right)\simeq U\left(2\right)
. We call
W_{0}
the
aquaternionic
moduli space of
\text{SU}(3)
-instantons, and we will show this is a 4-dimensional manifold. The reason for calling this space ‘aquaternionic’ is that it is formed by applying the hyperkähler quotient to the non-hyperkähler submanifold

, which, as a result,
W_{0}
does not carry a hyperkähler structure.

The proposition below provides a local description of
W_{0}
, but in the future, we would like to understand more about the global properties of this moduli space. In general, the subspace
W^{\mu}
of
W
will be a quadratic variety and certainly has a conical singularity at the origin. Our concern is whether
W^{\mu}\setminus\{0\}
is
smooth
. Being a quadratic variety, a priori there could be other singularities within
W^{\mu}
away from the origin. The key problem in checking smoothness is that the map
\mu
does not interact cleanly with the defining condition of
W
, which leads to difficulty when checking that
\mu
has rank
3
along
W^{\mu}
. We use the background

symmetry to ascertain smoothness.

Proposition 2.1
.

The moduli space
W_{0}
is a 4-dimensional submanifold of
M_{0}
.

Proof.

If we can prove that the quadratic variety
W^{\mu}\setminus\{0\}
is smooth, then because the action of
U\left(1\right)
on
W^{\mu}\setminus\{0\}
is free, this would imply the smoothness of
W_{0}
.
Firstly, note that as a complex representation of
\text{Spin}\left(3\right)\simeq\text{SU}\left(2\right)
, the action on
\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H}\right)
is equivalent to
\mathfrak{sl}\left(2,\mathbb{C}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}^{2}
with the adjoint action on the
\mathfrak{sl}\left(2,\mathbb{C}\right)
component and the standard action on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. If

X=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad Y=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad H=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix},\quad\widehat{H}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}H

is the standard basis for
\mathfrak{sl}\left(2,\mathbb{C}\right)
, then for
\vec{z},\vec{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
one has

\displaystyle\mu\left(\left(z_{1}X+z_{2}Y+z_{3}\widehat{H}\right)\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+\left(w_{1}X+w_{2}Y+w_{3}\widehat{H}\right)\otimes\vec{e}_{2}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{|\vec{z}|^{2}-|\vec{w}|^{2}}{2}&\vec{w}^{*}\vec{z}\\
\vec{z}^{*}\vec{w}&\frac{|\vec{w}|^{2}-|\vec{z}|^{2}}{2}\end{bmatrix}.

We also obtain that

W=\mathbb{C}\cdot\left\{Y\otimes\vec{e}_{2},X\otimes\vec{e}_{1},Y\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+H\otimes\vec{e}_{2},H\otimes\vec{e}_{1}-X\otimes\vec{e}_{2}\right\}

which implies that a generic element of
W
will take the form

aY\otimes\vec{e}_{2}+bX\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+c\left(Y\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+H\otimes\vec{e}_{2}\right)+d\left(H\otimes\vec{e}_{1}-X\otimes\vec{e}_{2}\right),\quad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}

which we can re-express the above as

\left(\sqrt{2}d\widehat{H}+bX+cY\right)\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+\left(\sqrt{2}c\widehat{H}-dX+aY\right)\otimes\vec{e}_{2}.

The above element will lie in
\mu^{-1}\left(0\right)
when the system of equations is satisfied

2|d|^{2}+|b|^{2}+|c|^{2}=2|c|^{2}+|d|^{2}+|a|^{2},\quad 2\overline{d}c-\overline{b}d+\overline{c}a=0

which, by rearranging, is equivalent to the system

|d|^{2}+|b|^{2}=|c|^{2}+|a|^{2},\quad 2\overline{d}c-\overline{b}d+\overline{c}a=0,\quad\left(\star\right)

We will analyze the above system, but first a word from our sponsor: group theory!

As we have discussed above, the space
W
is naturally acted on by
\textup{SU}\left(2\right)
and has weight
m=3/2
. We can enlarge this action to an action by
U(2)
by adding in the complex multiplication action on

by
U(1)
. However, there is
alternative action
of
U(2)
on
W
given as follows: As an
\text{SU}(2)
representation,
W
is isomorphic to the action of
\text{SU}(2)
on
\text{Sym}^{3}\left(\mathbb{C}^{2}\right)
by pullback and this action naturally lifts to an action of
U(2)
by pullback. However the action given above by
U(2)
and the action by pullback are
not
the same action. For the covering group homomorphism

\displaystyle D_{3}:SU(2)\times U(1)

\displaystyle\rightarrow SU(2)\times U(1)

\displaystyle\left(U,z\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(U,z^{3}\right)

this descends to a degree three covering group homomorphism

D_{3}:U(2)\rightarrow U(2).

If we let
U(2)\circlearrowright_{P}W
denote the action by pullback, and
U(2)\circlearrowright_{C}W
denote the canonical action given above, we have that

PD_{3}=C.

The importance of the above statement is the following: for computing the size of

-orbits within

, we are free to interchange between the

C
action.
The

-action is simplest to compute with, thus suppose that some element

has an isotropy group with dimension
\geq 1
. Then, after conjugation, there is some
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{2}
, for which

\begin{bmatrix}e^{i\theta_{1}t}&0\\
0&e^{i\theta_{2}t}\end{bmatrix}\cdot w=w,\quad\text{for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$}

where the
\cdot
denotes the usual action of
U(2)
on
\text{Sym}^{3}\left(\mathbb{C}^{2}\right)
by pullback. If we express

w=az_{1}^{3}+bz_{1}^{2}z_{2}+cz_{1}z_{2}^{2}+dz_{2}^{3}

then a case-by-case analysis will reveal that if
w\neq 0
is left fixed by the action of the above element of
U(2)
, then all but one of the coefficients above

must vanish. But this means that up to a constant

(and up to the action of some element

correspond to the matrices tensored with vectors above. We are now interested in whether non-zero points with positive-dimensional isotropy lie within

for any

. Thus, if

has positive-dimensional isotropy, so does

for any

is a non-zero multiple of one of the matrix-vector tensor products above. We rewrite each of the above matrix-vector tensor products using the usual Pauli-spin matrix basis for

E_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}i&0\\

giving us the four possibilities (up to scale) for

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{2}-iE_{3}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix},\quad\frac{1}{2}\left(E_{2}-iE_{3}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}-iE_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\begin{bmatrix}0\\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(iE_{3}+E_{2}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}-iE_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\

and none of the above elements lie within

. Thus, all

aY\otimes\vec{e}_{2}+bX\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+c\left(Y\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+H\otimes\vec{e}_{2}\right)+d\left(H\otimes\vec{e}_{1}-X\otimes\vec{e}_{2}\right)

First, note that the first equations in

along with the constraint of lying on

|d|^{2}+|b|^{2}=|c|^{2}+|a|^{2},\quad 0\leq|d|,|c|\leq\frac{1}{2}.

We now use the

to the non-negative real axis

If

, then we have that
\overline{c}a=0
which implies that either

with no further constraints on

or

(thus we obtain a

However, letting

. Letting

, we also obtain a circle in the complex plane centered at

. Thus we are interested in counting the number of intersection points between two circles in the complex plane. Observe that when
c=0
, we are forced to put

,

and

can take any value satisfying

(thus we obtain a circle in the

-plane). So we will assume that

and thus these two circles have distinct centers which implies their size of intersection is bounded above by
2
. All of this to say the following: If we let

be the projection to the

2

and along the codimension

2
-torus. Thus

has topological dimension at most
4
. Because
U(2)
-orbits are closed, connected,

must be a full
U(2)
-orbit, hence smooth.

∎

p_{0}:\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}_{0}|_{\mathscr{W}_{0}}\to\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0}.

Definition 2.5
.

Fix the Levi-Civita connection on
P_{\text{SO}}
. Any section of the bundle
\mathscr{W}_{0}
can be considered as a section of the bundle
\mathscr{M}_{0}
. Let then
\Psi\in\Gamma(\mathscr{W}_{0})
be a section, and apply the Fueter operator on
\mathscr{M}_{0}
to obtain a section
\mathfrak{F}\Psi\in\Gamma(Y,\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}_{0})
. Applying the projection map (
2.4
), we obtain the
3/2-Fueter operator

\mathfrak{Q}
by

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathfrak{Q}\Psi:=p_{0}\circ\mathfrak{F}\Psi\in\Gamma(Y,\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0}).

3.
A Haydys correspondence for
3/2
-spinors and non-ellipticity

3.1.
Haydys correspondence

In this section, we prove the first part of the paper’s main result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\left(Y,g_{Y}\right)
be a Riemannian three manifold with
\text{Spin}^{c}\left(3\right)
-structure
P_{\text{Spin}^{c}}\rightarrow Y
and bundle of
3/2
-spinors
\mathscr{W}\rightarrow Y
.
Then any solution
\left(A,\psi\right)
of the degenerate Rarita-Schwinger Seiberg-Witten equations

Q_{A}\psi=0,\quad\mu\left(\psi\right)=0

gives rise to a solution
\Psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)
of the
3/2
-Fueter equation

\mathfrak{Q}\Psi=0.

Conversely, for any solution
\Psi
of the
3/2
-Fueter equation, there exists a
\text{Spin}^{c}\left(3\right)
-structure
P_{\text{Spin}^{c}}\rightarrow Y
with a connection
A
on its determinant line bundle along with a section
\psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}\right)
such that the pair
\left(A,\psi\right)
satisfies the degenerate Rarita-Schwinger Seiberg-Witten equations.

Proof.

We are interested in a reformulation of the degenerate PDE

\begin{cases}Q_{A}\psi=0\\
\mu\left(\psi\right)=0\end{cases}

where
\psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}\right)
and
A
is a hermitian compatible connection for
\text{det}P_{\text{Spin}^{c}\left(3\right)}
, and
\psi
is non-vanishing along
Y
. As we have seen above, we know that for each fixed
y\in Y
, the subset

\mathscr{M}^{+,\mu}_{y}:=\mu^{-1}\left(0\right)_{y}

is a quadratic variety in
\mathscr{M}^{+}_{y}=T^{*}_{y}Y\otimes\not{S}_{y}
which is smooth away from the origin with smooth component
\mathscr{M}^{\mu}=\mathscr{M}\cap\mu^{-1}\left(0\right)
. Moreover, the quadratic subvariety

\mathscr{W}^{+,\mu}_{y}:=\mathscr{M}^{+,\mu}_{y}\cap\mathscr{W}_{y}

is also smooth away from the origin, with smooth component
\mathscr{W}_{y}=\mathscr{M}^{\mu}_{y}\cap\mathscr{W}_{y}
. Both
\mathscr{M}^{\mu}
and
\mathscr{W}^{\mu}
are acted on freely by
U(1)
and away from the origin give a smooth quotient
\mathscr{M}_{0}:=\mathscr{M}^{\mu}/U(1)
and
\mathscr{W}_{0}:=\mathscr{W}^{\mu}/U(1)
. If
\psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}\right)
satisfies the degenerate PDE above, then we have that at
y\in Y

\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}:T_{y}Y\rightarrow T_{y}\mathscr{W}^{\mu}.

We can split the vertical distributions of
\mathscr{M}^{\mu}
and
\mathscr{W}^{\mu}
into a Hopf component, given by the vertical distribution of the fibration
p:\mathscr{M}^{\mu}\circlearrowleft U(1)\rightarrow\mathscr{M}_{0}
, and its orthogonal component, which we simply denote by

\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}^{\mu}=\mathcal{L}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{M},\quad\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}^{\mu}=\mathcal{L}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{W}.

This splitting of
\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}^{\mu}
gives us a method for splitting the differential of
\psi

\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}=\nabla_{A}\psi^{\mathcal{L}}_{y}+\nabla_{A}\psi^{\mathcal{H}^{W}}_{y}.

Recall that for a fixed
y\in Y
, the entire discussion is taking place within the
C\ell\left(T^{*}Y,g_{y}\right)
-module
\mathscr{M}_{y}^{+}
. Moreover, the bundle
\mathcal{H}^{M}
is
closed
under Clifford multiplication. The Hopf line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathscr{M}^{\mu}
gets mapped to
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}\mathscr{M}^{\mu}
under Clifford multiplication, where the orthogonal complement is computed within
\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}
. Thus, upon applying Clifford contraction to the differential of
\psi
, we obtain the decomposition

c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}=\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}\right)^{\perp}+\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{M}}.

Applying the projection on the
3/2
component gives the evaluation of the Rarita-Schwinger operator

Q_{A}\psi_{y}=\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}\right)^{\perp}+\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{M}}

This orthogonal projection could ruin the fact that
\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\perp}
and
\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{M}}
are linearly independent. Let us see what we can say for sure about the linear dependence of the projections above. For starters, recall that subbundle
\mathcal{N}\leqslant\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}^{\mu}\big|_{\mathscr{W}^{\mu}}
has real rank
2
and when we restrict to
\mathcal{W}^{\mu}
, we obtain a splitting

\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}^{\mu}\big|_{\mathscr{W}^{\mu}}=\mathcal{H}^{W}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{\parallel}\oplus\mathcal{N}\oplus\mathcal{L}.

where
\mathcal{H}^{\parallel}
is a real rank
2
line bundle over
\mathscr{W}^{\mu}
. We also have that

\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}\big|_{\mathscr{W}^{\mu}}=\mathcal{H}^{W}\oplus\mathcal{L}\oplus\mathcal{V}^{\perp}\mathscr{W}^{\mu}

where the perpendicular complement is computed relative to the inner product in
\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}
. In this case we can further split the differential as

\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{M}}=\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{W}}+\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{\parallel}}+\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}\right)^{\mathcal{N}}.

By definition one has that
\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi_{y}\right)^{\mathcal{N}}=0
. Moreover, note that if
a\in\mathcal{H}^{\parallel}
then
\pi_{3/2}a
must be perpendicular to
\mathcal{H}^{W}
, for otherwise we could write

a=\pi_{3/2}a+a^{W^{\perp}}

and this element would have non-trivial inner product with
\mathcal{H}^{W}
, contradicting the fact that
a
is perpendicular to
\mathcal{H}^{W}
. Similarly, we must have that for any
m\in\mathcal{V}^{\perp}\mathscr{M}^{\mu}
, that
\pi_{3/2}m
must be perpendicular to
\mathcal{H}^{W}
. What this entails is the following: if
Q_{A}\psi=0
, then we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=Q_{A}\psi

\displaystyle=\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{W}}+\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{\parallel}}+\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{N}}+\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{W}}+\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{\parallel}}+\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\perp}

By what we have just said above, we can assert that we must have

0=\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{W}},\quad 0=\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{\parallel}}+\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\perp}

For the section
\Psi=p\psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)
, the above identity proves

\mathfrak{Q}\Psi=0.

Thus the first direction of Haydy’s correspondence has been established.

To obtain the reverse direction of Haydy’s correspondence, suppose that we have a section
\Psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)
which satisfies

\mathfrak{Q}\Psi=0.

For the initial
P_{\text{Spin}^{c}\left(3\right)}
-structure on
Y
, we obtain a principal
U\left(1\right)
-bundle

p:\mathscr{W}^{\mu}\circlearrowleft U\left(1\right)\rightarrow\mathscr{W}_{0}.

We can pull the principal bundle back along
\Psi
to obtain a principal
U\left(1\right)
-bundle on
Y
,
\Psi^{*}\mathscr{W}^{\mu}\circlearrowleft U\left(1\right)\rightarrow Y
. The condition for the bundle to be trivial is equivalent to the existence of a section
\psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}^{\mu}\right)
such that
p\psi=\Psi
. If no such section exists, let
L=\Psi^{*}\mathscr{W}^{\mu}\times_{U\left(1\right)}\mathbb{C}\rightarrow Y
be the induced hermitian line bundle. Then for the twisted
\text{Spin}^{c}\left(3\right)
-structure given by
P_{\text{Spin}^{c}\left(3\right)}\times_{U\left(1\right)}\times\Psi^{*}\mathscr{W}^{\mu}\rightarrow Y
, we obtain a twisted bundle of
3/2
-spinors
\mathscr{W}^{L}\rightarrow
, and under the diffeomorphism
\mathscr{W}^{L}_{0}\simeq\mathscr{W}_{0}
, the section
\Psi
of
\mathscr{W}^{L}_{0}
will admits a lifting section
\psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}^{L,\mu}\right)
. All this to say, for a given section
\Psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)
, there always exists a
\text{Spin}^{c}
-structure
P_{\text{Spin}^{c}}\rightarrow Y
for which the
\Psi
admits a lifting section
\psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}^{\mu}\right)
.

If
\psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}^{\mu}\right)
is a lifting section of
\Psi
, then we have that for
any
connection
A
of
\text{det}P_{\text{Spin}^{c}}
the identity

p_{*}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{M}}=c\circ\nabla^{\mathscr{W}_{0}}\Psi

where
\nabla^{\mathscr{W}_{0}}
is the Ehresmann connection of
\mathscr{W}_{0}\rightarrow Y
. In this case, if we began with a connection
A_{0}
for
L
, note that for any
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}\left(Y,\mathbb{R}\right)
one has

\left(c\circ\nabla_{A_{0}+i\alpha}\psi\right)^{\perp}=\left(c\circ\nabla_{A_{0}}\psi\right)^{\perp}+c\left(\alpha\right)i\psi.

The assumption that
\mathfrak{Q}\Psi=0
only grants us that
\left(c\circ\nabla_{A}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{W}}=0
. To see how to annihilate that
\mathcal{H}^{\parallel}
and
\perp
components, we apply
\pi_{3/2}
to the above relation and note then that we are searching for an
\alpha\in\Omega\left(Y,\mathbb{R}\right)
satisfying

\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A_{0}}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{\parallel}}+\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A_{0}}\psi\right)^{\perp}=-\pi_{3/2}\left(c\left(\alpha\right)i\psi\right)

By Proposition 2.3 (cite correctly), the restriction of
\pi_{3/2}
to
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}\mathscr{M}^{\mu}
is injective and thus surjective onto its image
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}\mathscr{W}^{\mu}
. Moreover, for any
\psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}^{\mu}\right)
, the bundle map induced from the following
C^{\infty}\left(Y\right)
-linear map of sections

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(T^{*}Y\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Gamma\left(\psi^{*}\mathcal{V}^{\perp}\mathscr{M}^{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\rightarrow c\left(\alpha\right)i\psi

is an isomorphism. These observations imply that one can indeed find a
unique

\alpha\in\Omega^{1}\left(Y,\mathbb{R}\right)
such that

\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A_{0}}\psi\right)^{\mathcal{H}^{\parallel}}+\pi_{3/2}\left(c\circ\nabla_{A_{0}}\psi\right)^{\perp}=-\pi_{3/2}\left(c\left(\alpha\right)i\psi\right).

This implies that for the connection
A=A_{0}+i\alpha
, the section
\psi
satisfies the degenerate Rarita-Schwinger Seiberg-Witten equations

Q_{A}\psi=0,\quad\mu\left(\psi\right)=0.

Note that we have shown for each
\psi
covering
\Psi
, there is a
unique
connection
A
for
\text{det}P_{Spin^{c}(3)}
for which
\left(\psi,A\right)
satisfy the degenerate Rarita-Schwinger Seiberg-Witten equations. This implies that if we have two solutions
\left(\psi_{1},A_{1}\right)
and
\left(\psi_{2},A_{2}\right)
to the degenerate Rarita-Schwinger Seiberg-Witten equations that cover the same section
\Psi
, they must be gauge equivalent, because
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
will be related by a gauge transformation. This finishes the reverse direction of the Haydys correspondence.
∎

3.2.
Non-ellipticity for
3/2
-Fueter operator

We now turn our attention to the question of nonlinear ellipticity of the Rarita-Schwinger Fueter operator
\mathfrak{Q}
which leads us to study its linearization at a section
\Psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}:\Gamma\left(\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)\rightarrow\Gamma\left(\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0}\right).

We first make a few remarks regarding the operator above which will simplify the analysis. Firstly, recall the global formula for
\mathfrak{Q}
, given by

\mathfrak{Q}{\Psi}=p_{0}\mathfrak{F}\Psi\in\Gamma\left(Y,\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0}\right).

Recall that for each
y\in Y
one has
\mathscr{W}_{y}^{\mu}=\mathscr{M}_{y}^{\mu}\cap W_{y}
, however this intersection is not orthogonal, i.e. one does not have that
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}\mathscr{M}^{\mu}
is contained in
\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}^{\mu}
. This implies that the orthogonal projection
p_{0}
from
\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}\big|_{\mathscr{W}_{0,y}}
onto
\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0,y}
will have a non-trivial covariant derivative to the Levi-Civita connection of
\mathscr{M}_{y}
, which we denote by
\nabla^{\mathcal{V}}p_{0}
. Thus, if
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{F}
denotes the covariant derivative of
\mathfrak{F}
, we have that for
\Psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)
and
\Phi\in\Gamma\left(\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)
that

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}\Phi=p_{0}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{F}_{\Psi}\Phi+\left(\nabla_{\Phi}^{\mathcal{V}}p_{0}\right)\mathfrak{F}_{\Psi}.

Using our formula for
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{F}
, we can simplify the above expression to

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}\Phi=p_{0}D^{\Psi}\Phi+\left(\nabla^{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}p_{0}\right)\mathfrak{F}_{\Psi}.

Note that the term involving the covariant derivative of
p_{0}
is
C^{\infty}\left(Y\right)
-
linear
in
\Phi
and thus represents a zeroth-order term in the differential operator
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any section
\Psi\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)
, the linearization

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}:\Gamma\left(\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)\rightarrow\Gamma\left(\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)

is a real non-elliptic differential operator of order
1
.

Proof.

Fix
\Psi\in\Gamma\left(Y,\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)
and recall that within the formula

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}\Phi=p_{0}D^{\Psi}\Phi+\left(\nabla^{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}p_{0}\right)\mathfrak{F}_{\Psi}

where
D^{\Psi}:\Gamma\left(Y,\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}_{0}\right)\rightarrow\Gamma\left(Y,\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}_{0}\right)
is the Dirac operator associated with the Clifford module
\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}_{0}
and
p_{0}
is the orthogonal projection from
\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}_{0}
onto
\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0}
. This implies that the symbol of
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}
at
y\in Y
and
\xi\in T^{*}Y
will be

\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}\right)_{y}\left(\xi\right)=i\left(\Psi^{*}p_{0}\right)c^{\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}_{0}}\left(\xi\right).

Our task is to show that for any choice of
\Psi\in\Gamma\left(Y,\mathscr{W}_{0}\right)
, there is
\xi\neq 0
such that the above operator is not invertible.

As in the proof of Haydys’ correspondence, we are free to assume that there is a section
\psi\in\Gamma\left(Y,\mathscr{W}^{\mu}\right)
which covers
\Psi
. In this case the Clifford module
\Psi^{*}\mathcal{V}\mathscr{M}_{0}=\mathcal{H}^{M}
is a subbmodule of the Clifford module
\psi^{*}T^{*}Y\otimes\not{S}\simeq T^{*}Y\otimes\not{S}
. It is here that we need to utilize the background
U(2)
-symmetry. Namely, keep in mind that when verifying ellipticity, it is a pointwise condition one checks at each
y\in Y
. Thus we can assume that coordinates have been fixed and we have identified
\mathscr{M}^{+}_{y}\simeq\mathfrak{sl}\left(2,\mathbb{C}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
T^{*}_{y}Y\simeq\mathfrak{su}\left(2\right)
. Furthermore the subbundle
\mathcal{H}^{W}\rightarrow\mathscr{W}^{\mu}
is
U(2)
-equivariant and is identified with
\mathcal{V}\mathscr{W}_{0}
under the projection
p:\mathscr{W}^{\mu}\rightarrow\mathscr{W}_{0}
. In this case we note that the Clifford multiplication is
U(2)
-equivariant, i.e. for any
U\in U(2)
with image
U_{SO}\in SO(3)
under the natural representation
U(2)\simeq Spin^{c}\left(3\right)\rightarrow SO(3)
, one has

U\cdot c\left(\xi\right)w=c\left(U_{SO}\xi\right)U\cdot w,\quad w\in\mathfrak{sl}\left(2,\mathbb{C}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}^{2},

where
w\in\mathcal{H}^{W}_{\psi\left(y\right)}
and
U\cdot w\in\mathcal{H}^{W}_{U\cdot\psi\left(y\right)}
. Therefore, we are free to move the point
\psi\left(y\right)
to a well-chosen point in
\mathscr{W}^{\mu}_{y}
which will make the computations feasible. With this in mind, as before, we pick
y\in Y
so that

\psi_{0}=\psi\left(y\right)=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},0,0,0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},0\right).

At
y
, the Clifford algebra
C\ell\left(T^{*}_{y}Y,g_{y}\right)
is generated by
\{1\otimes E_{1},1\otimes E_{2},1\otimes E_{3}\}
and a dimension count will show that the submodule
\mathcal{H}^{M}
will be rank
2
and thus will be generated by two well-chosen vectors. Recall that at our chosen point, one has that

\text{Ker}D\mu=\mathbb{R}\cdot\left\{e_{3},e_{4},e_{6},e_{2}-e_{7},e_{1}+e_{8},e_{9},e_{5}+e_{10},e_{11},e_{12}\right\}

and the Hopf line
\mathcal{L}
is spanned by
e_{4}+e_{11}
. Thus, if we pick
e_{6}=H\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}
and
e_{5}+e_{10}=Y\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}
, these will both lie in
\mathcal{H}^{M}=\text{Ker}D\mu-\mathcal{L}
. Furthermore, if we study the
C\ell\left(T^{*}Y,g_{Y}\right)
-span of these two vectors we obtain two independent submodules of
\mathcal{H}^{M}
, thus these will be our two well-chosen generators of
\mathcal{H}^{M}
. We further note that

H\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}=-iE_{1}\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}=E_{1}\otimes\vec{e}_{1}

thus we see that the submodule spanned by
H\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}
is really the submodule spanned by
E_{1}\otimes\vec{e}_{1}
. The latter generator
Y\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}
cannot be written as
v\otimes s
for some vector
v\in T_{y}Y
and
s\in\mathbb{H}
and in fact no nonzero vectors in this submodule can be expressed in this way. We find then the following four vectors give a
\mathbb{R}
-spanning set for
\text{Ker}m

\displaystyle Y\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}-X\otimes i\vec{e}_{1},\quad Y\otimes\vec{e}_{2}+X\otimes\vec{e}_{1},

\displaystyle Y\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}-H\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}+H\otimes i\vec{e}_{2},\quad-Y\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes\vec{e}_{2}-H\otimes\vec{e}_{1}-H\otimes\vec{e}_{2}.

and note that although the above vectors are orthogonal to one another, their lengths are given by
1
,
1
,
\sqrt{3}
, and
\sqrt{3}
, respectively (recall that we are dividing the trace norm on
\mathfrak{sl}\left(2,\mathbb{C}\right)
by
2
). We use
\mathscr{B}
to denote the orthonormal basis given by the vectors above divided by their lengths. For a number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\beta=\beta_{2}+i\beta_{3}\in\mathbb{C}
we consider elements in the Clifford algebra of the form

\alpha E_{1}+\beta_{2}E_{2}+\beta_{3}E_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}i\alpha&-\overline{\beta}\\
\beta&-i\alpha\end{bmatrix}

Thus, we compute

\displaystyle\left(\alpha E_{1}+\beta_{2}E_{2}+\beta_{3}E_{3}\right)

\displaystyle\left(Y\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}-X\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}\right)=Y\otimes\left(-i\overline{\beta}\vec{e}_{1}+\alpha\vec{e}_{2}\right)-X\otimes\left(-\alpha\vec{e}_{1}+i\beta\vec{e}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(Y\otimes\vec{e}_{2}+X\otimes\vec{e}_{1}\right)-\beta_{2}\left(Y\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}\right)+\beta_{3}\left(-Y\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes\vec{e}_{2}\right)

and

\displaystyle\left(\alpha E_{1}+\beta_{2}E_{2}+\beta_{3}E_{3}\right)

\displaystyle\left(Y\otimes\vec{e}_{2}+X\otimes\vec{e}_{1}\right)=Y\otimes\left(-\overline{\beta}\vec{e}_{1}-i\alpha\vec{e}_{2}\right)+X\otimes\left(i\alpha\vec{e}_{1}+\beta\vec{e}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\left(Y\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}-X\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}\right)+\beta_{2}\left(-Y\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes\vec{e}_{2}\right)+\beta_{3}\left(Y\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}\right)

and

\displaystyle\left(\alpha E_{1}+\beta_{2}E_{2}+\beta_{3}E_{3}\right)\left(Y\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}-H\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}+H\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=Y\otimes\left(-\alpha\vec{e}_{1}+i\beta\vec{e}_{2}\right)+X\otimes\left(-i\overline{\beta}\vec{e}_{1}+\alpha\vec{e}_{2}\right)-H\otimes\left(-\alpha\vec{e}_{1}+i\beta\vec{e}_{2}\right)+H\otimes\left(-i\overline{\beta}\vec{e}_{1}+\alpha\vec{e}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta_{2}\left(Y\otimes i\vec{e}_{2}-X\otimes i\vec{e}_{1}\right)-\beta_{3}\left(Y\otimes\vec{e}_{2}+X\otimes\vec{e}_{1}\right)+\alpha\left(-Y\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes\vec{e}_{2}+H\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+H\otimes\vec{e}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 216.81pt-H\otimes i\beta\vec{e}_{2}-H\otimes i\overline{\beta}\vec{e}_{1}

We find that the matrix of the symbol of the linearization of the
3/2
-Fueter operator at our well-chosen point is given by

M\left(\alpha E_{1}+\beta_{2}E_{2}+\beta_{3}E_{3},\mathscr{B}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\alpha&\frac{\beta_{2}}{\sqrt{3}}&-\frac{\beta_{3}}{\sqrt{3}}\\
\alpha&0&-\frac{\beta_{3}}{\sqrt{3}}&-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\sqrt{3}}\\
-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\sqrt{3}}&\frac{\beta_{3}}{\sqrt{3}}&0&\frac{\alpha}{3}\\
\frac{\beta_{3}}{\sqrt{3}}&\frac{\beta_{2}}{\sqrt{3}}&-\frac{\alpha}{3}&0\end{bmatrix}

To compute the last column we used the fact that Clifford multiplication is skew-symmetric on the euclidean vector space
\mathcal{H}^{M}
and composition with an orthogonal projection preserves the skew-symmetry. If we compute the Pfaffian of the skew-symmetric matrix above, which we give the formula below for the reader’s convenience

\text{Pf}\left(A\right)=a_{12}a_{34}-a_{13}a_{24}+a_{14}a_{23}

we get

\text{Pf}\left(M\left(\alpha E_{1}+\beta_{2}E_{2}+\beta_{3}E_{3},\mathscr{B}\right)\right)=\frac{-\alpha^{2}+\beta_{2}^{2}+\beta_{3}^{2}}{3}

which vanishes when
|\alpha|=\sqrt{\beta_{2}^{2}+\beta_{3}^{2}}
. Recalling that the Pfaffian squares to the determinant, we see that the symbol will not be an isomorphism when
|\alpha|=\sqrt{\beta_{2}^{2}+\beta_{3}^{2}}
.

∎

4.
A generalized Rarita-Schwinger-typer operator

The definition of
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}
defined above resembles the definition of a Rarita-Schwinger operator. Thus, borrowing Dirac operator terminology, we incline to call
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}
a
Rarita-Schwinger-type operator
. In fact, there are different ways one can construct a Rarita-Schwinger-type operator and
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q_{\psi}}
is a particular instance of a general formalism, which we describe below.

4.1.
Definition and ellipticity

Let
X
be a Riemannian manifold. Suppose
\mathscr{E},\mathscr{S}
are Clifford bundles over
X
, equipped with their own hermitian inner products. Denote by
D^{\mathscr{E}}:\Gamma(\mathscr{E})\to\Gamma(\mathscr{E})
the Dirac operator on
\mathscr{E}
, and
c_{\mathscr{S}}:T^{*}X\otimes\mathscr{S}\to\mathscr{S}
be the Clifford multiplication on
\mathscr{S}
. Let
h:\mathscr{E}\to T^{*}X\otimes\mathscr{S}
be a bundle map. We consider

m:=c_{\mathscr{S}}\circ h:\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{S},

and define
P_{m}:\mathscr{E}\to\ker\;m
to be the orthogonal projection of
\mathscr{E}
onto
\ker\;m
.

Definition 4.1
.

Consider
\mathscr{Q}:=P_{m}\circ D^{\mathscr{E}}
to be the differential operator on
\Gamma(\ker\;m)
. We call
\mathscr{Q}
a
Rarita-Schwinger-type operator
.

For our context, we are mainly interested in the (non)ellipticity of the Rarita-Schwinger-type operators. It turns out that not all such operators are elliptic. Hence, it is natural to ask what some sufficient conditions are to ensure that
\mathscr{Q}:=P_{m}\circ D^{\mathscr{E}}
is an elliptic operator.

Note that
\mathscr{Q}
is elliptic if and only if its principal symbol
\sigma_{\mathscr{Q}}(x,\xi)=P_{m}(x)\circ c(\xi)|_{\ker\;m_{x}}
is injective. Here,
c
denotes the Clifford multiplication on
\mathscr{E}
,
x\in X
and
\xi\in T^{*}_{x}X
. This means that, if
v\in\ker\;m_{x}
and
P_{m}(x)\circ c(\xi)v=0
, then
v=0
. Equivalently, if
v\in\ker\;m_{x}
and

c(\xi)v\in(\ker\;m_{x})^{\perp}=Im\;m^{*}_{x}=Im\;h^{*}\circ c^{*}_{\mathscr{S}},

then
v
has to be trivial. Here are some sufficient conditions to ensure that
\mathscr{Q}
is elliptic. In the theorem below, if
0\neq\xi\in T^{*}X
, we have the contraction map
\iota_{\xi}:T^{*}_{x}X\otimes\mathscr{S}\rightarrow\mathscr{S}
given by taking the inner product of the
T^{*}_{x}X
coefficients with
\xi
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\widehat{c}_{\mathscr{S}}
denote the Clifford multiplication on the Clifford module
T^{*}X\otimes\mathscr{S}
induced by
1\otimes c_{\mathscr{S}}
. Suppose the following holds:

(a)

For all
\xi\in T^{*}X
and
v\in\mathscr{E}
, we have
h(c(\xi)v)=\widehat{c}_{\mathscr{S}}(\xi)h(v)
,

(b)

mm^{*}
is an isomorphism,

(c)

For each
\xi\in T^{*}X
and
|\xi|=1
, then

\mathbf{1}_{\mathscr{E}}+2c(\xi)^{-1}m^{*}(mm^{*})^{-1}\iota_{\xi}h

is injective.

Then
\mathscr{Q}=P_{m}\circ D^{\mathscr{E}}
is an elliptic operator.

Proof.

Firstly, if
w\in\ker\;c_{\mathscr{S}}
, then for each
\xi\in T^{*}X
such that
|\xi|=1
, we must have

(4.1)

c_{\mathscr{S}}(\widehat{c}_{\mathscr{S}}(\xi)(w))=-2\iota_{\xi}w.

Let
v\in\ker\;m
. Then this means that
h(v)\in\ker\;c_{\mathscr{S}}
. As a result, by (
4.1
), we have

(4.2)

c_{\mathscr{S}}(\widehat{c}_{\mathscr{S}}(\xi)h(v))=-2\iota_{\xi}h(v).

Additionally, suppose that
c(\xi)v\in Im\;m^{*}
. This means that there is an
s\in\mathscr{S}
such that

(4.3)

c(\xi)v=m^{*}s.

By applying
m=c_{\mathscr{S}}\circ h
to both sides of the above equation, using condition (a) and (
4.2
), we have

\displaystyle mm^{*}(s)

\displaystyle=c_{\mathscr{S}}(h\circ c(\xi)v)=c_{\mathscr{S}}(\widehat{c}_{\mathscr{S}}(\xi)h(v))=-2\iota_{\xi}h(v)

Because of (b),
(mm^{*})^{-1}
exists and we can find what
s
is in terms of
v
. In particular,

(4.4)

s=-2(mm^{*})^{-1}\iota_{\xi}h(v).

Substitute (
4.4
) for
s
in (
4.3
) to yield

0=(\mathbf{1}_{\mathscr{E}}-(-2)c(\xi)^{-1}m^{*}(mm^{*})^{-1}\iota_{\xi}h)(v).

From (c), we see that
\mathbf{1}_{E}+2c(\xi)^{-1}m^{*}(mm^{*})^{-1}\iota_{\xi}h
is invertible. As a result,
v=0
. Therefore,
\mathscr{Q}=P_{m}\circ D^{\mathscr{E}}
is elliptic as claimed.
∎

For example, in the actual Rarita-Schwinger operator setting (
\mathscr{E}:=T^{*}X\otimes\mathscr{S}
, and
h
is the identity map), one can check that all three conditions above are satisfied, roughly, because of the fact that all eigenvalues of
(mm^{*})^{-1}
turn out to be
1/3
, which is different from
1/2
.

Another instance where one can attain ellipticity (without solving an eigenvalue problem) is if one knows that

(4.5)

\left\lVert-2c(\xi)^{-1}m^{*}(mm^{*})^{-1}\iota_{\xi}h\right\rVert<1.

Such a situation where (
4.5
) holds is the following: let
\mathcal{W}\subset T^{*}X
be any subbundle of
X
so that
d:=dim\;\mathcal{W}>4
(of course, this requires
dim\;X
to be appropriately large). Let

\mathscr{E}=\mathcal{W}\otimes\mathscr{S},

Note that
\mathscr{E}
is a Clifford bundle over
X
. Let
D^{\mathscr{E}}
be the corresponding Dirac operator on
\mathscr{E}
. Define
m=c_{\mathscr{S}}\circ i
, where
i:\mathscr{E}=\mathcal{W}\otimes\mathscr{S}\hookrightarrow T^{*}X\otimes\mathscr{S}
is the inclusion map. Let
P_{m}
be the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{W}\otimes\mathscr{S}=\mathscr{E}
onto
\ker\;m
. Then

\mathscr{Q}^{\mathcal{W}}=P_{m}\circ D^{\mathscr{E}}

is elliptic.

We shall see why the condition
d>4
is relevant for (
4.5
). Indeed, firstly, note that since the Clifford multiplication on
\mathscr{E}
is exactly the one on
T^{*}X\otimes\mathscr{S}
, which is induced by the Clifford multiplication on
\mathscr{S}
,
i
satisfies (a). Next, to check for
(b)
, it suffices to check that
m:=c_{\mathscr{S}}\circ i:\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{S}
has full rank. Equivalently, for an
s\in\mathscr{S}
, fix an
w_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
that is non-trivial, we ask if there is an
s^{\prime}\in S
such that
c_{\mathfrak{s}}(i(w_{0}\otimes s^{\prime}))=s
. Note that

\displaystyle c_{\mathscr{S}}(i(w_{0}\otimes s^{\prime}))=c_{\mathscr{S}}(w_{0}\otimes s^{\prime})=c_{\mathscr{S}}(w_{0})s^{\prime}.

Thus,
s^{\prime}
can be found explicitly by setting
s^{\prime}=-c_{\mathscr{S}}(w_{0})s/||w_{0}||^{2}
. Lastly, we consider condition (c). Note that
m^{*}=i^{*}\circ c^{*}_{\mathscr{S}}:\mathscr{S}\to\mathscr{E}
. Suppose
\mathcal{W}
(locally) is spanned by an orthonormal basis
\{w_{1},\cdots,w_{d}\}
. Then

m^{*}(s)=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}\otimes c_{\mathscr{S}}(w_{j})s.

The above implies that

(4.6)

\left\lVert m^{*}(s)\right\rVert^{2}=d\left\lVert s\right\rVert^{2}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle mm^{*}(s)

\displaystyle=m\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{j}\otimes c_{\mathscr{S}}(w_{j})s\right)=-\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{\mathscr{S}}(w_{j})^{2}s=d\cdot s.

This implies that

(4.7)

(mm^{*})^{-1}(s)=\dfrac{1}{d}s.

Now, by (
4.7
),

\displaystyle(mm^{*})^{-1}\iota_{\xi}i(v)=\dfrac{1}{d}\iota_{\xi}i(v)=\dfrac{1}{d}\iota_{\xi}(v).

Thus,

\displaystyle m^{*}(mm^{*})^{-1}\iota_{\xi}i(v)=\dfrac{1}{d}m^{*}\iota_{\xi}(v).

As a result, using (
4.6
) and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for
|\xi|=1
, we must have

\displaystyle\left\lVert-2c(\xi)^{-1}m^{*}(mm^{*})^{-1}\iota_{\xi}i(v)\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2}{d}\left\lVert c(\xi)^{-1}m^{*}\iota_{\xi}(v)\right\rVert=\dfrac{2}{d}\left\lVert m^{*}\iota_{\xi}(v)\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2}{d}\cdot\sqrt{d}\cdot\left\lVert\iota_{\xi}(v)\right\rVert\leq\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\left\lVert v\right\rVert.

Hence, for (
4.5
) to be satisfied, we need
2/\sqrt{d}<1
; equivalently,
d>4
. If that is the case, then (c) is achieved. Therefore,
\mathscr{Q}^{\mathcal{W}}
is elliptic as claimed.

Conditions (a) and (b) in Proposition
4.2
are geometric enough that we expect them to hold for many generic situations. However, condition (c) imposes a strong algebraic constraint. Thus, the verification of (c) potentially varies a lot depending on the situation. Furthermore, we find that the algebraic constraint given by (c) is a feature in the sense that one can always check it by direct computation. Hence, it is of independent interest to see in what situation where (c) is also a necessary condition for ellipticity of Rarita-Schwinger-type operators. The corollary below provide such an instance.

Corollary 4.3
.

With the same setting as in Proposition
4.2
but now,
\mathscr{E}
is a sub-Clifford module of
T^{*}X\otimes\mathscr{S}
. Let
h=\iota
be the inclusion of
\mathscr{E}\hookrightarrow T^{*}X\otimes\mathscr{S}
. Suppose conditions (a)–(b) hold. Assume that

m:\operatorname{im}(c(\xi)|_{\ker\;m})\to\mathscr{S}

is injective for each
\xi\neq 0
. Then
\mathscr{Q}=P_{m}\circ D^{\mathscr{E}}
is elliptic if and only if (c) holds.

Proof.

As mentioned above, ellipticity of
\mathscr{Q}
is equivalent to the fact that if
\xi\in T^{*}X
and non-trivial, then
c(\xi)v\in\operatorname{im}m^{*}
for
v\in\ker m
implies that
v=0
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
\xi
has unit length. If
m:\operatorname{im}(c(\xi)|_{\ker\;m})\to\mathscr{S}
is injective, then

c(\xi)v=m^{*}s\Leftrightarrow m(c(\xi)v)=mm^{*}(s),

which, by computation in the proof of Proposition
4.2
, the above is equivalent to

0=(\mathbf{1}_{\mathscr{E}}-(-2)c(\xi)^{-1}m^{*}(mm^{*})^{-1}\iota_{\xi}h)(v),

Therefore, ellipticity of
\mathscr{Q}
is equivalent to the above equation only has trivial solution as claimed.
∎

Unfortunately, although the previously discussed example is general and not too computationally heavy (in the sense that one does not have to explicitly calculate the eigenvalues), it is not applicable to our situation of
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}
(one of the obvious reasons is a dimensional one). It turns out that one has to explicitly calculate the eigenvalues of
mm^{*}
to prove non-ellipticity. By Proposition
4.2
and Corollary
4.3
, it is enough to verify that condition (c) fails to obtain non-ellipticity of
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}
.

To end this subsection, we give an alternative proof of Theorem
3.2
using some of the general formalism developed here.

Another proof of Theorem
3.2
.

We show that
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}

is non-invertible. With the formula
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}=p_{0}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{F}

above, we have that, up to zeroeth order terms, at

where

Recall that, similar to the proof of Haydys’ correspondence, without loss of generality, we suppose there a section

that covers
\Psi
. And the Clifford module

is the kernel of this map. So,

is a Rarita-Schwinger-type operator. Thus, by Corollary
4.3

4.2
.

For part (a), this is trivial. For parts (b)–(c), like in the proof of Theorem
3.2
in the previous section, we utilize the
U(2)

Since the subbundle

Now observe that if

where

Reusing the identity

c\left(\xi\right)w=-2P_{\mathcal{H}^{M}}\widehat{c}^{*}\left(\widehat{c}P_{\mathcal{H}^{M}}\widehat{c}^{*}\right)^{-1}\iota_{\xi}w.

Equivalently, this means that

(4.8)

w=-2c(\xi)^{-1}P_{\mathcal{H}^{M}}\widehat{c}^{*}\left(\widehat{c}P_{\mathcal{H}^{M}}\widehat{c}^{*}\right)^{-1}\iota_{\xi}w.

Therefore, by Corollary
4.3
, to prove the non-ellipticity of
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\mathfrak{Q}_{\Psi}

\displaystyle\left(-Y\otimes\vec{e}_{1}+X\otimes\vec{e}_{2}+H\otimes(-\vec{e}_{1}-\vec{e}_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=m\left(Y\otimes(-i\alpha_{0}\vec{e}_{1}-\beta\vec{e}_{2})+X\otimes(-\overline{\beta}\vec{e}_{1}-i\alpha_{0}\vec{e}_{2})+H\otimes((-i\alpha_{0}+\overline{\beta})\vec{e}_{1}+(i\alpha_{0}-\beta)\vec{e}_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-2i\alpha_{0}+\overline{\beta}\\
-2i\alpha_{0}+\beta\end{bmatrix}.

As a result, the matrix that represents
m:\operatorname{im}(c(\xi)|_{\ker m})\to\not{S}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
with respect to the chosen basis elements above is given by

\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{3}&\beta_{2}&2\alpha_{0}-\beta_{3}&\beta_{2}\\
-\beta_{3}&-\beta_{2}&-2\alpha_{0}-\beta_{3}&\beta_{2}\\
-\beta_{2}&\beta_{3}&-\beta_{2}&-2\alpha_{0}-\beta_{3}\\
-\beta_{2}&\beta_{3}&\beta_{2}&-2\alpha_{0}+\beta_{3}\end{bmatrix}

The above matrix has the determinant given by

4\beta_{2}^{2}+4\beta_{3}^{2}+8\beta_{2}^{2}\beta_{3}^{2}=4(\beta_{2}^{2}+\beta_{3}^{2})^{2},

which is always positive because
\beta_{2},\beta_{3}
cannot be both zero by the hypothesis. The proof is complete.
