Abstract
(Deutsch) Im Hauptteil dieser Dissertation betrachten wir einen periodisch realisierten mikroskopischen Supraleiter, der durch die BCS-Theorie beschrieben wird und externen elektromagnetischen Feldern ausgesetzt ist. Wir zeigen, dass der Supraleiter im Limes makroskopischer und schwacher Magnetfelder korrekt durch die Ginzburg–Landau-Theorie beschrieben wird. Die wichtigste Neuerung unserer Ergebnisse besteht darin, dass wir einen nicht verschwindenden magnetischen Fluss durch die Einheitszelle des Periodengitters zulassen. Diese Hauptresultate werden durch verschiedene unveröffentlichte Arbeiten auf dem Gebiet der BCS-Theorie ergänzt. Außerdem stellen wir der Präsentation dieser Ergebnisse eine umfassende Einführung in die BCS-Theorie voran, die sich für Masterstudent*innen und Doktorand*innen eignet. Damit hoffen wir, einen Beitrag zur Schließung der Lücke der fehlenden Einführungsliteratur auf diesem Gebiet zu leisten. Die Dissertation umfasst ein zweites Thema, in dem wir Ideen für den Aufbau von Quantengittersystemen liefern, um exponentielle Abschätzungen für den Adiabatensatz zu beweisen. Diese Notizen sind das Ergebnis eines Forschungsaufenthalts an der University of British Columbia (UBC) in Vancouver, Kanada.

Zusammenfassung

Diese Arbeit ist auf dem Gebiet der Vielteilchen-Quantensysteme angesiedelt und umfasst zwei Themen:

(i)

BCS-Theorie der Supraleitung im Regime der schwachen magnetischen Felder,

(ii)

Adiabatische Theorie im Kontext von Quantengittersystemen.

Das Thema (i) bildet den Hauptteil dieser Arbeit. In diesem Teil untersuchen wir periodisch ausgedehnte Supraleiter, die schwachen und makroskopischen externen elektrischen und magnetischen Feldern ausgesetzt sind. Diese Felder sind so beschaffen, dass sie einen nicht verschwindenden magnetischen Fluss durch die Einheitszelle induzieren, was die hauptsächliche Herausforderung an der Aufgabenstellung dieser Arbeit darstellt. Der Supraleiter wird mathematisch im Rahmen der BCS-Theorie der Supraleitung beschrieben. Diese Theorie ist eine effektive Zweiteilchentheorie mit einem Paarungsmechanismus, die nach den drei Physikern John Bardeen, Leon Neil Cooper und John Robert Schrieffer benannt ist, die 1957 eine mit dem Nobelpreis ausgezeichnete attraktive Wechselwirkung im Supraleiter postulierten. Ihr berühmter Ansatz ist Inspiration für unzählige Arbeiten und seine rigorose Verifikation aus der Perspektive der Vielteilchenquantenmechanik fordert die Mathematische Physik bis heute heraus.

Unser Modell wird variationell in Form eines freien Energiefunktionals, des sogenannten BCS-Funktionals, beschrieben und wir untersuchen die Fluktuation dessen Minimums, der sogenannten BCS-Energie, sowie die Verschiebung der kritischen Temperatur des Systems, die durch die makroskopischen externen Felder im Grenzbereich schwacher Feldstärken verursacht werden. Wir zeigen, dass diese Verschiebungen in diesem Regime durch die Ginzburg–Landau-Theorie beschrieben wird und leiten geeignete asymptotische Beschreibungen her.

Unser Hauptbeitrag sind signifikante konzeptionelle Vereinfachungen in der Analysis des Funktionals auf Testzuständen im Vergleich zu den Arbeiten von Frank, Hainzl, Seiringer und Solovej aus dem Jahr 2012 bzw. 2016 sowie ein neues Zerlegungsresultat für die Cooper-Paar-Wellenfunktion, das den konstanten Anteil des Magnetfeldes einbezieht. Dieses Zerlegungsresultat ermöglicht es uns, die genannten Resultate für Systeme mit einem solchen konstanten Feldanteil zu beweisen. Letzterer ist für den nicht verschwindenden magnetischen Fluss verantwortlich und stellt die mathematische Behandlung des Problems vor erhebliche Schwierigkeiten. Der wichtigste mathematische Grund ist, dass die Komponenten des magnetischen Impulsoperators nicht kommutieren, was die Anwendung von Fourieranalysis unmöglich macht. Die Präsentation der Resultate ist in zwei Veröffentlichungen aufgeteilt, die in dieser Arbeit enthalten sind.

Darüber hinaus liefern wir mehrere Ergebnisse, die helfen können, das Projekt in Zukunft voranzubringen. Das erste Ergebnis ist ein Zerlegungsresultat für magnetische Potentiale, die im Zusammenhang mit periodischen Magnetfeldern auftreten. Diese Ergebnisse werden verwendet, um zu argumentieren, dass die magnetischen Potentiale, die in den Arbeiten in den Kapiteln
und
behandelt werden, erschöpfend sind. Wir liefern auch eine spektrale Zerlegung des periodischen Landau-Hamiltonians. Unser letztes Ergebnis ist eine Störungstheorie des tiefliegenden Spektrums des Operators
K_{T,\mathbf{A}}-V
, der in der BCS-Theorie eine wichtige Rolle spielt. Dies geschieht mit Hilfe einer Combes–Thomas-Abschätzung für den Resolventenkern von
K_{T}-V
, die von unabhängigem Interesse sein könnte.

Ein Problem in der BCS-Theorie war schon immer das Fehlen von Einsteigerliteratur. Wir versuchen, diese Lücke zu schließen, indem wir mit einer umfassenden Einführung in das Thema beginnen, die für Leser*innen geschrieben ist, die zum ersten Mal mit der BCS-Theorie in Kontakt kommen.

Im Rahmen von Punkt (ii) skizzieren wir den Beweis von exponentellen Abschätzungen für den Adiabatensatz in Quantengittersystemen. Zwar ist die Arbeit noch nicht abgeschlossen, dennoch präsentieren wir hier den aktuellen Zustand des Projekts und die Grundlagen, die wir entwickeln mussten, um dieses Problem in Zukunft lösen zu können. Summary

This thesis is located in the field of many-body quantum systems and covers two topics:

(i)

BCS theory in the weak magnetic field regime,

(ii)

Adiabatic theory for quantum lattice systems.

The topic (i) constitutes the major part of this thesis. In this part, we investigate periodically extended superconductors that are subject to weak and macroscopic external electric and magnetic fields. These fields are such that the magnetic flux through the unit cell is non-vanishing, which opens up the main challenge for the contributions of this thesis. The superconductor is mathematically described in the framework of BCS theory of superconductivity. This theory is an effective two-particle pairing theory, which is named after the three physicists John Bardeen, Leon Neil Cooper, and John Robert Schrieffer, who postulated a Nobel prize awarded attractive interaction inside the superconductor in 1957. Their famous ansatz is inspiration for countless works and its verification from first principle quantum mechanics continues to challenge mathematical physics until the present day.

Our model is described in a variational manner in terms of a free energy functional, the so-called BCS functional, and we investigate the fluctuation of its minimum, the so-called BCS energy, and the critical temperature shift of the system, which are caused by the external fields in the weak-field limit. We show that in this regime the superconductor is described by Ginzburg–Landau theory and derive appropriate asymptotic descriptions.

We mainly contribute with significant conceptual simplifications in the trial state analysis compared to the works of Frank, Hainzl, Seiringer, and Solovej from 2012 and 2016 as well as a new decomposition result for the Cooper pair wave function which encompasses the constant magnetic field contribution. This decomposition result enables us to prove the aforementioned results for systems with such a constant magnetic field contribution. The latter is responsible for the non-vanishing magnetic flux and imposes significant difficulties to the mathematical treatment of the problem. The main mathematical reason is that the components of the magnetic momentum operator do not commute, which makes it impossible to use Fourier analysis. The work is splitted into two papers, which are included in this thesis.

In addition, we provide several results that may be helpful in continuing the project in the future. The first result is a decomposition result for magnetic potentials that arise in the context of periodic magnetic fields. These results are used to argue that the magnetic potentials covered in the works of Chapters
and
are exhaustive. We also provide a spectral decomposition of the periodic Landau Hamiltonian. Our last result is an asymptotic analysis of the low-lying spectrum of the operator
K_{T,\mathbf{A}}-V
, which plays a prominent role in BCS theory. This is done with the help of a Combes–Thomas estimate for the resolvent kernel of
K_{T}-V
, which might be of independent interest.

A problem in BCS theory has always been the lack of introductory literature. We attempt to fill this gap by beginning with a comprehensive introduction to the subject, written for readers coming into contact with BCS theory for the first time.

In point (ii), we outline the proof of exponential estimates for the adiabatic theorem in extended quantum lattice systems. While the work is not yet complete, we present here the current state of the project and the new locality setup we needed to develop for such systems in order to solve this problem in the future. This work was started during my research stay at the University of British Columbia in Vancouver, Canada, in 2019.

Proof.

Set

\displaystyle f(t)\leqslant\eta f(u)+(1-\eta)f(s)

so that

\displaystyle f(t)-f(s)\leqslant\eta f(u)-\eta f(s)

which, in turn, is

\displaystyle\frac{f(t)-f(s)}{t-s}\leqslant\frac{f(u)-f(s)}{u-s}.

In the same manner, we obtain

\displaystyle f(u)-f(t)

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-\eta)f(u)-(1-\eta)f(s)=\frac{u-t}{u-s}[f(u)-f(s)].\qed

(a)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\mathrm{d}\nu:=\int_{M}F\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{A_{n}}2^{-n}\mu(A_{n})^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mu=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-n}=1.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}:=\int_{M}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\nu
. Then, the map
U\colon\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
,
Ug=F^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}g

\displaystyle\int_{M}\|Ug(m)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\int_{M}|F^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|^{2}\cdot\|g(m)\|^{2}\cdot F\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\|g\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

and

. Hence, we may suppose without loss that
\int_{M}\mathrm{d}\mu=1

\{\eta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
for
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
and let
F_{k}
be the element of
\mathcal{H}
with

for almost all
m\in M
. The

. Moreover, any
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
has an expansion

with
f_{k}\in L^{2}(M,\mu;\mathbb{C})
and
\|\psi\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|f_{k}\|^{2}
(see Lemma
1.B.4
(a)). Define functions

by
AF_{k}(m)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}a_{k\ell}(m)F_{\ell}
for almost all
m\in M
. Choose a countable dense subset
\mathcal{D}
in
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
of vectors
\varphi

\varphi=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}\eta_{k}
and define

(whence
\|\Phi\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}

\mathfrak{A}(m)\varphi:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}a_{k\ell}(m)\eta_{\ell}
, we obtain

\displaystyle A(f\Phi)(m)=f(A\Phi)(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}f(m)\alpha_{k}AF_{k}(m)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}f(m)\alpha_{k}a_{k\ell}(m)F_{\ell}(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{N}f(m)\alpha_{k}a_{k\ell}(m)\eta_{\ell}=f(m)\mathfrak{A}(m)\varphi

\displaystyle=f(m)\mathfrak{A}(m)\Phi(m),

since
f\mathbbs 1_{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathcal{A}
. Moreover, we get

\displaystyle\|A(f\varphi)(m)\|^{2}=\Bigl\|\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}a_{k\ell}(m)\eta_{\ell}\Bigr\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}|f(m)|^{2}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\Bigl|\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}a_{k\ell}(m)\Bigr|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(m)

\displaystyle\leqslant\|A\|^{2}\int_{M}|f(m)|^{2}\cdot\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(m)

\displaystyle=\|A\|^{2}\int_{M}|f(m)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(m)\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\alpha_{k}|^{2}.

Hence, by duality, using
\|\Phi\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\alpha_{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{A}(m)\varphi\|\leqslant\|A\|\cdot\|\Phi\|=\|A\|\cdot\|\varphi\|

for almost all
m\in M

\mathfrak{A}(m)
may be extended to a bounded operator on
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
for almost every

\mathfrak{A}\in L^{\infty}(M,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
. Then, let
B
be the corresponding decomposable operator and let
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
have the form
\psi=\sum_{k=1}^{N}f_{k}F_{k}
with

\displaystyle(A\psi)(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}f_{k}(m)(AF_{k})(m)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}f_{k}(m)\mathfrak{A}(m)\eta_{k}=\mathfrak{A}(m)\sum_{k=1}^{N}f_{k}(m)\eta_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{A}(m)\psi(m)=(B\psi)(m).

Since such
\psi

. To see density, let
\psi\in\mathcal{H}

\psi(m)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(m)\eta_{k}
for certain

and a.e.

\langle\eta_{\ell},\psi(m)\rangle=f_{\ell}(m)

\displaystyle\|f_{\ell}\|^{2}=\int_{M}|f_{\ell}(m)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{M}|\langle\eta_{\ell},\psi(m)\rangle|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leqslant\int_{M}\|\psi(m)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\|\psi\|^{2}.

Thus,

for all

(monotone convergence and Parseval). Finally, for

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\psi-\sum_{k=1}^{N}f_{k}F_{k}\Bigr\|^{2}=\int_{M}\Bigl\|\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}f_{k}(m)F_{k}(m)\Bigr\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Here, the integrand goes pointwise to

and is bounded by
\|\psi(m)\|^{2}
which is integrable. We conclude by dominated convergence.∎

Definition 1.B.12
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sa}}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime}):=\{A\colon\mathcal{D}(A)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\prime}:A=A^{*}\}
denote the set of self-adjoint operators in
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. A function
\mathfrak{A}\colon M\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sa}}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
is called measurable if and only if the function
m\mapsto(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}
is measurable. Given such a function, we define an operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}=\int_{M}^{\oplus}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\mu
with the domain

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A)=\Bigl\{\psi\in\mathcal{H}:\psi(m)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}(m))\text{ a.e. \& }\int_{M}\|\mathfrak{A}(m)\psi(m)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty\Bigr\}.

Remark 1.B.13
.

The foregoing definition makes the following sense. If
\mathfrak{A}\colon M\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sa}}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
is measurable and
\psi(m)\in\ran((\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1})
for almost every
m\in M
, so that there is an
\eta(m)\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
with
(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta(m)=\psi(m)
a.e., then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}(m)\psi(m)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{A}(m)(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta(m)

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta(m)-\mathrm{i}(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta(m)

\displaystyle=\eta(m)+\mathrm{i}(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta(m)

is measurable. Thus, the definition of
\mathcal{D}(A)
makes sense.

Theorem 1.B.14
(
[
RS78n
, Theorem XIII.85 (b)]
)
.

A self-adjoint operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
has the form
\int_{M}^{\oplus}\mathfrak{A}(m)\,\mathrm{d}\mu
if and only if
(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}
is a bounded decomposable operator.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
)

By assumption, there is a function
\mathfrak{A}\colon M\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sa}}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
such that
g(m):=(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}
is measurable. We may define

\displaystyle G:=\int_{M}^{\oplus}g(m)\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

To show is that
G=(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}
. Let
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
, then
(G\psi)(m)=(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\psi(m)
i.e.
(G\psi)(m)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}(m))
a.e. Furthermore, since

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}(m)(G\psi)(m)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{A}(m)(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\psi(m)=\psi(m)-\mathrm{i}(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\psi(m)

\displaystyle=\psi(m)-\mathrm{i}(G\psi)(m),

we have that
\mathfrak{A}(m)(G\psi)(m)
is square integrable (since
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
and
G
is bounded (by 1)) so that
G\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
. Since
A=\int_{M}^{\oplus}\mathfrak{A}(m)\,\mathrm{d}\mu
, we obtain

\displaystyle((A+\mathrm{i})G\psi)(m)=(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\psi(m)=\psi(m).

Thus,
G
is a right-sided inverse of
A+\mathrm{i}
. Furthermore, let
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
and compute

\displaystyle(G(A+\mathrm{i})\psi)(m)=(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})^{-1}(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})\psi(m)=\psi(m).

Thus,
G
is also a left-sided inverse and hence the inverse of
A+\mathrm{i}
.

(
\Leftarrow
)

By assumption, there is a measurable function
g\in L^{\infty}(M,\mu;\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime}))
such that

\displaystyle(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}=\int_{M}^{\oplus}g(m)\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

First, we note that
(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}=\int_{M}^{\oplus}g(m)^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mu
. This follows from a standard computation. We claim that
g(m)
has dense range and is injective almost everywhere. To see this, let
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
with
\varphi(m)\in\ker(g(m))
a.e. Then
0=g(m)\varphi(m)=((A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\varphi)(m)
so that
(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\varphi=0
which means
\varphi=0
since
(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}
is injective. Hence,
g(m)
is injective almost everywhere. If
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
with
\varphi(m)\in\ran(g(m))^{\perp}
almost everywhere, then, for all
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle 0=\langle\varphi(m),g(m)\psi(m)\rangle=\langle g(m)^{*}\varphi(m),\psi(m)\rangle=\langle(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}\varphi,\psi\rangle(m)

so that
\varphi\in\ker((A-\mathrm{i})^{-1})
. This implies
\varphi=0
so that
\varphi(m)=0
almost everywhere. Hence
g(m)
has dense range in
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. Hence, define
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}(m)):=\ran(g(m))
and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}(m):=g(m)^{-1}-\mathrm{i}

on
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}(m))
. Then
\mathfrak{A}(m)
is densely defined. We claim that
\mathfrak{A}(m)
is self-adjoint for almost every
m\in M
. To see this, first note that
\mathfrak{A}(m)
is symmetric: For a.e.
m\in M
pick
\varphi(m),\psi(m)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}(m))=\ran(g(m))
so there is some
\eta(m)\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
with
\psi(m)=g(m)\eta(m)=((A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta)(m)
. This implies that
\psi\in\ran(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}=\mathcal{D}(A)
. Analogously,
\varphi(m)=g(m)\xi(m)
for some
\xi(m)\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\langle\varphi(m),\mathfrak{A}(m)\psi(m)\rangle\hskip-70.0pt

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0pt=\langle\xi(m),g^{*}(m)[g(m)^{-1}-\mathrm{i}]g(m)\eta(m)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\xi(m),g^{*}(m)[\mathbbs 1-\mathrm{i}g(m)]\eta(m)\rangle=\langle\xi,(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}[\mathbbs 1-\mathrm{i}(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}]\eta\rangle(m)

\displaystyle=\langle\xi,(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}[A+\mathrm{i}-\mathrm{i}](A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta\rangle(m)

\displaystyle=\langle\xi,(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}[A-\mathrm{i}+\mathrm{i}](A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta\rangle(m)

\displaystyle=\langle\xi,[\mathbbs 1+\mathrm{i}(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}](A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta\rangle(m)=\langle\xi(m),[\mathbbs 1+\mathrm{i}g^{*}(m)]\psi(m)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle[\mathbbs 1-\mathrm{i}g(m)]\xi(m),\psi(m)\rangle=\langle[g(m)^{-1}-\mathrm{i}]\varphi(m),\psi(m)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathfrak{A}(m)\varphi(m),\psi(m)\rangle.

To show that
\mathfrak{A}(m)
is even self-adjoint for a.e.
m\in M
, let
\eta(m)\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
and set
\psi(m):=g(m)\eta(m)
. Per definition,
\psi(m)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}(m))
and

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})\psi(m)=(g(m)^{-1}-\mathrm{i}+\mathrm{i})g(m)\eta(m)=\eta(m).

Hence,
\ran(\mathfrak{A}(m)+\mathrm{i})=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. For
\ran(\mathfrak{A}(m)-\mathrm{i})
, we proceed analogously, using that

\displaystyle(A+\mathrm{i})(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}=(A-\mathrm{i}+2\mathrm{i})(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}=\mathbbs 1+2\mathrm{i}(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}

implies that

\displaystyle g(m)^{*}=g(m)[\mathbbs 1+2\mathrm{i}g(m)^{*}].

(1.B.4)

If
\psi(m)\in\ran(g(m))
so that
\psi(m)=g(m)\eta(m)
, we have that

\displaystyle g(m)^{*}[\mathbbs 1-2\mathrm{i}g(m)]\eta(m)=g(m)[\mathbbs 1+2\mathrm{i}g(m)^{*}][1-2\mathrm{i}g(m)]\eta(m)=\psi(m).

(1.B.5)

The last equality follows from an easy computation involving the first resolvent equation showing

\displaystyle[\mathbbs 1-2\mathrm{i}g(m)][\mathbbs 1+2\mathrm{i}g(m)^{*}]=[\mathbbs 1-2\mathrm{i}(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}][\mathbbs 1+2\mathrm{i}(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}](m)=\mathbbs 1(m).

Hence,
\psi(m)\in\ran(g(m)^{*})
. Thus, interchanging the roles of
\mathrm{i}
and
-\mathrm{i}
and the stars, we obtain
\ran(g(m))=\ran(g(m)^{*})
. Hence, if
\xi(m)\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
and
\varphi(m):=g(m)^{*}\xi(m)
, we have
\varphi(m)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}(m))
and

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{A}(m)-\mathrm{i})\varphi(m)

\displaystyle=[g(m)^{-1}-2\mathrm{i}]g(m)^{*}\xi(m)

\displaystyle=[g(m)^{-1}-2\mathrm{i}]g(m)[\mathbbs 1+2\mathrm{i}g(m)^{*}]\xi(m)

\displaystyle=[\mathbbs 1-2\mathrm{i}g(m)][\mathbbs 1+2\mathrm{i}g(m)]\xi(m)=\xi(m).

Thus,
\mathfrak{A}(m)
is self-adjoint for almost all
m\in M
. This now enables us to define
\widetilde{A}:=\int_{M}^{\oplus}\mathfrak{A}(m)\,\mathrm{d}\mu
. Let us show that
\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{A})=\mathcal{D}(A)
. To see this let
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{A})
. Then
\psi(m)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}(m))=\ran(g(m))
a.e. so
\psi(m)=g(m)\eta(m)
for a certain
\eta(m)\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. We obtain that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}(m)\psi(m)=[g(m)^{-1}-\mathrm{i}]g(m)\eta(m)=\eta(m)-\mathrm{i}\psi(m).

(1.B.6)

Since
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
and
\int_{M}\|\mathfrak{A}(m)\psi(m)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty
by definition of
\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{A})
, we infer that
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
. Hence,
\psi=(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta\in\mathcal{D}(A)
. Reversely, let
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(A)
so that
\psi=(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta
for some
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
. Then
\psi(m)=g(m)\eta(m)
so that
\psi(m)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{A}(m))
for almost all
m\in M
. Furthermore, (
1.B.6
) says that
\mathfrak{A}(m)\psi(m)
is square integrable over
M
. Hence,
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{A})
. Finally, we have that

\displaystyle(A\psi)(m)

\displaystyle=(A(A+\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta)(m)=(\eta-\mathrm{i}(A-\mathrm{i})^{-1}\eta)(m)

\displaystyle=\eta(m)-\mathrm{i}g(m)\eta(m)=g(m)^{-1}\psi(m)-\mathrm{i}\psi(m)=\mathfrak{A}(m)\psi(m)

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{A}\psi)(m).\qed

Lemma 2.A.1

(Vitali’s Covering lemma,
[
S13e
]
)
.

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}>0
and let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
E\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})
. Then, there is a disjoint subfamily
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, i.e.
B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})\cap B_{r_{j}}(x_{j})=\emptyset
for all
i,j\in I
,
i\neq j
and we have

\displaystyle E\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{3r_{i}}(x_{i})

Remark 2.A.2
.

One can prove this in more generality, but the constant “
3
” does not remain true. Often it is proved with
5
instead but possibly anything bigger than
3
would do.

Proof.

Without loss assume that
r_{1}\geqslant r_{2}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant r_{n}\geqslant 0
. Choose
D_{1}:=B_{r_{1}}(x_{1})
. Assume recursively that
D_{1},\ldots,D_{k-1}\in\{B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are already chosen. If

\displaystyle I_{k}:=\Bigl\{1\leqslant j\leqslant n:B_{r_{j}}(x_{j})\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{k-1}D_{i}=\emptyset\Bigr\}=\emptyset

then set
m=k-1
and terminate. Otherwise, let
\ell_{k}:=\min I_{k}
and choose
D_{k}:=B_{r_{\ell_{k}}}(x_{\ell_{k}})
. If
B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})\notin\{D_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then, by definition, there is
j<i
such that
B_{r_{j}}(x_{j})\cap B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})\neq\emptyset
(if not, then
i
would be the minimum of
I_{k}
for some
k
and
B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})
would have been chosen). Since
r_{j}\geqslant r_{i}
, we get
B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})\subseteq B_{3r_{j}}(x_{j})
because for
y\in B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})
and
z\in B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})\cap B_{r_{j}}(x_{j})
arbitrary, we have

\displaystyle|y-x_{j}|\leqslant|y-x_{i}|+|x_{i}-z|+|z-x_{j}|<r_{i}+r_{i}+r_{j}\leqslant 3r_{j}

Hence, we get
D_{k}\cap D_{\ell}=\emptyset
for
1\leqslant k,\ell\leqslant m
with
k\neq\ell
by construction and

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}B_{3r_{\ell_{j}}}(x_{\ell_{j}})\qed

Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and define the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function by

\displaystyle(Mf)(x):=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{|B_{r}(x)|}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y

Here,
|B|
denotes the Lebesgue measure of
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 2.A.3
.

If
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
Mf
is lower semi-continuous and thus measurable.

Proof.

We want to prove that for
0<t<\infty
; the set

\displaystyle E_{t}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:(Mf)(x)>t\}=(Mf)^{-1}((t,\infty))

is open. For
t\leqslant 0
,
E_{t}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
so the claim there is clear. Let
x\in E_{t}
. Then, by definition of
Mf
, there is
r>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{r}(x)|}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y>t

Choose
r^{\prime}>r
such that still

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{r^{\prime}}(x)|}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y>t

Let

with

. Then

since for each

, we have

\displaystyle|y-x^{\prime}|\leqslant|y-x|+|x^{\prime}-x|<r+r^{\prime}-r=r^{\prime}

Hence, by the translational invariance of the Lebesgue measure, we get

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{|B_{r^{\prime}}(x)|}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{|B_{r^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})|}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{|B_{r^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})|}\int_{B_{r^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})}|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y\leqslant(Mf)(x^{\prime})

Hence,
x^{\prime}\in E_{t}
and
B_{r^{\prime}-r}(x)\subseteq E_{t}
.
∎

(Weak type maximal inequality)

, and

\displaystyle|\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(Mf)(x)>t\}|\leqslant\frac{3^{n}}{t}\cdot\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

Let

be compact and consider the trivial cover

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{r}(x)|}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y>t

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\subseteq E_{t}\\

Theorem 2.A.5

(Lebesgue’s Differentiation Theorem)
.

Let
1\leqslant p<\infty
and
f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{p}(U)
, where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is open. Then, for almost all
x\in U
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\searrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}y=0

Proof.

Let
g\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
g
is uniformly continuous. Hence, given
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\delta>0
such that
|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}
whenever
|x-y|<\delta
. Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
r<\delta
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{r}(x)|}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}y<\frac{1}{|B_{r}(x)|}\int_{B_{r}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}y\cdot\varepsilon=\varepsilon

Hence,

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\searrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}y=0

(2.A.1)

Let
f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{p}(U)
. By extending to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
0
, we may assume that
f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Without loss, assume that
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. If not, then for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the function
f_{k}=f\mathbbs 1_{B_{k}(0)}
is in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Assume the result holds for
f_{k}
except for a null set
E_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, it holds for
f
on the complement of the null set
\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}E_{k}
. For, if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}E_{k}
, and
r>0
is small, then there is
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
with
B_{r}(x)\subseteq B_{k_{0}}(0)
. Then, since
x\notin E_{k_{0}}
, apply the result to
f_{k_{0}}
, since
f_{k_{0}}|_{B_{r}(x)}=f|_{B_{r}(x)}
. Now, for an arbitrary
g\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, use (
2.A.1
) and convexity of
t\mapsto t^{p}
to get

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\searrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{p-1}\limsup_{r\searrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|g(y)-g(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt+4^{p-1}\limsup_{r\searrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)-g(y)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt+4^{p-1}|f(x)-g(x)|^{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant 4^{p-1}\sup_{r>0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)-g(y)|^{p}+4^{p-1}|f(x)-g(x)|^{p}

\displaystyle=4^{p-1}\bigl[M(|f-g|^{p})(x)+|f(x)-g(x)|^{p}\bigr]

Hence, if, for given
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\searrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}y>\varepsilon

Then,
M(|f-g|^{p})(x)>2^{2p-3}\varepsilon
or
|f(x)-g(x)|>2^{2p-3}\varepsilon
. Now, using the Tshebyshev inequality, we get

\displaystyle|X_{1}^{\varepsilon}|:=\bigl|\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|f(x)-g(x)|^{p}>2^{2p-3}\varepsilon\bigr\}\bigr|\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{2p-3}\varepsilon}\cdot\|f-g\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{p}

and using the maximal inequality
2.A.4
we obtain

\displaystyle|X_{2}^{\varepsilon}|:=\bigl|\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:M(|f-g|^{p})(x)>2^{2p-3}\varepsilon\bigr\}\bigr|\leqslant\frac{3^{d}}{2^{2p-3}\varepsilon}\cdot\|f-g\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{p}

Putting everything together, we see that

\displaystyle|A_{\varepsilon}|:=\Bigl|\Bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\limsup_{r\searrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}y>\varepsilon\Bigr\}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leqslant|X_{1}^{\varepsilon}|+|X_{2}^{\varepsilon}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{3^{d}+1}{2^{2p-3}\varepsilon}\cdot\|f-g\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{p}

By density, we may choose a sequence
(g_{n})_{n}\subseteq C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
g_{n}\to f
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as
n\to\infty
. It follows that
A_{\varepsilon}
has zero measure for every
\varepsilon>0
. Hence,

\displaystyle 0=\Bigl|\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}A_{\frac{1}{k}}\Bigr|=\Bigl|\Bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\limsup_{r\searrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}y>0\Bigr\}\Bigr|

so that the claim holds for almost each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
∎

Proposition 2.C.2

(Product rule for
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

Let
f\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
f\cdot g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with weak derivative

\displaystyle\nabla(fg)=(\nabla f)\cdot g+f\cdot(\nabla g).

Proof.

Since
f
is weakly differentiable, for every
v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}g\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\nabla g)\cdot v\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.C.1)

By density, this extends to all
v\in H_{0}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Now, since
f\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
f
is almost everywhere classically differentiable and we have
\nabla(fv)=\nabla f\cdot v+f\nabla v
almost everywhere. Since
v
has compact support and
f,\nabla f
are bounded, we conclude that
fv\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Thus,
fv
is a valid test function in (
2.C.1
). Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\nabla g)\cdot fv\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g\cdot\nabla(fv)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g\cdot(\nabla f)\cdot v\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g\cdot f\cdot(\nabla v)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}fg\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(f\cdot\nabla g+\nabla f\cdot g)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Hence,
fg
is weakly differentiable with
\nabla(fg)=f\nabla g+\nabla f\cdot g
. Furthermore, since
f,\nabla f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g,\nabla g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have that
fg,\nabla(fg)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Thus,
fg\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

Proposition 2.C.3

(Chain rule for
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

Let
f\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
g^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Then
g\circ f\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with weak derivative

\displaystyle\nabla(g\circ f)=(g^{\prime}\circ f)\cdot\nabla f.

Proof.

Since
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle g(t)=g(0)+\int_{0}^{t}g^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Denoting
F:=g\circ f
, it follows that for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle|F(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|g(0)|+\Bigl|\int_{0}^{f(x)}g^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Bigr|\leqslant|g(0)|+\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\cdot|f(x)|\leqslant|g(0)|+\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|f\|_{\infty}

Hence,
F\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Since
f
is classically differentiable almost everywhere, we obtain
\nabla F(x)=g^{\prime}(f(x))\cdot\nabla f(x)
for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by the classical chain rule. Then
\nabla F
coincides with the weak derivative of
F
a.e. Furthermore,
|\nabla F(x)|\leqslant\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}<\infty
. Hence,
F\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎
