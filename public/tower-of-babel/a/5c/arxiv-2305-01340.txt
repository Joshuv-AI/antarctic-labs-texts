A-posteriori error estimates for systems of hyperbolic conservation laws Thanks: Submitted to the editors August 8, 2026.
Jan Giesselmann, Aleksey Sikstel

Abstract
We provide rigorous and computable a-posteriori error estimates for first order finite-volume approximations of nonlinear systems of hyperbolic conservation laws in one spatial dimension. Our estimators rely on recent stability results by Bressan, Chiri and Shen and a novel method to compute negative order norms of residuals. Numerical experiments show that the error estimator converges with the rate predicted by a-priori error estimates.

Introduction

We are concerned with the error analysis of numerical approximations of solutions to Cauchy-problems for one-dimensional systems of hyperbolic conservation laws defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}(t,x)+\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u}(t,x))_{x}&=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\\
\boldsymbol{u}(0,x)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(x).\end{split}

(1)

Here
\boldsymbol{u}\,\colon\,[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
denotes the conserved quantity,
\boldsymbol{f}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{R}^{m})
the flux function and
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{D})
is the initial condition. We assume that the system is strictly hyperbolic, i.e. the Jacobian of the flux
\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{f}
has
m
distinct real eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{u})<\cdots<\lambda_{m}(\boldsymbol{u})
and the set of the corresponding eigenvectors forms an orthonormal basis. Furthermore, we assume that each characteristic field is either genuinely nonlinear or linear degenerate. Finally, we assume that the system is endowed with a strictly convex entropy
e
and corresponding entropy flux
q
, i.e. a pair of functions
e\,\colon\,\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and
q\,\colon\,\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\mathrm{D}e\cdot\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{f}=\mathrm{D}q
, see classical textbooks
[
4
]
and
[
1
]
.

Our goal is to provide rigorous a-posteriori error estimates for numerical schemes and for technical simplicity, to outline the ideas, we restrict ourselves to first order finite-volume schemes. Extending our method to higher order schemes is subject of future research. In general, a-posteriori error estimates require uniqueness of solutions and rely on a suitable stability theory of the underlying PDEs.
Indeed, it is well known that for one dimensional hyperbolic systems as specified above and data that have small total variation a Lipschitz continuous semi-group of entropy solutions exist. To be more precise, we rely on stability results provided by Bressan, Chiri and Shen
[
2
]
. In
[
2
]
these results are used to prove convergence of a quite general class of numerical schemes provided numerical solutions satisfy an a-posteriori verifiable criterion, i.e. bounded total variation and bounded oscillation. In contrast, we aim at providing a fully a-posteriori error estimate since this leads to sharper bounds and will serve as a basis for adaptive numerical schemes.

In this work, we consider solutions to the Cauchy-problem (
1
) that are constant outside of some space-time cylinder
\Omega_{T}:=[0,T]\times\Omega
where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
is some non-empty interval. In order to define the finite-volume scheme, let
\Omega_{T}
be discretized by grid points
(t^{n},x_{j-\frac{1}{2}})
where
n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
and
j\in\{0,\ldots,J\}
. We denote the corresponding time-steps by
\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}:=t^{n+1}-t^{n}
and cell widths by
\Delta x_{j}:=x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}
. Finite-volume schemes approximate the solution in each cell by spatial averages

\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j}\approx\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\int_{x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}}^{x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}}\boldsymbol{u}(t^{n},x)\,dx,

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{0}
consists of piece-wise averages of the initial condition
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
and the discrete evolution of these spatial averages is given by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n+1}_{j}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j}-\frac{\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j+1}\right)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j-1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j}\right)\right).

(2)

The numerical flux
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\,\colon\,\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
approximates
\boldsymbol{f}
at the cell boundary
x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}
. Since we consider explicit time-integration, the CFL-condition
restricting the timestep is necessary for the stability of the scheme.
For details on finite-volume schemes we refer to established literature, e.g.
[
10
]
,
[
23
]
.

Finite volume solutions are interpreted as functions in
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})
by setting

\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,x)=\boldsymbol{u}_{j}^{n}\,\text{ if }\,(t,x)\in K_{j}^{n}:=[t^{n},t^{n+1})\times(x_{j-\frac{1}{2}},x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}),

i.e. piecewise constant on rectangular space-time cells. We define the space of such piecewise constant functions by

\mathbf{P}_{0}(\Omega_{T}):=\Bigg\{v\,\colon\,[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\,\colon\,v\Big|_{K_{j}^{n}}\equiv const\,\,\forall n\in\{0,\ldots,N\},\,j\in\{0,\ldots,J\},\\
v\Big|_{[0,T]\times\left(-\infty,\,x_{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)}\equiv const,\,\,v\Big|_{[0,T]\times\left(x_{J+\frac{1}{2}},\,\infty\right)}\equiv const\Bigg\}.

(3)

In
[
2
]
, estimates of the
L^{\infty}([0,T),L^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
-error for solutions
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{P}_{0}{(\Omega_{T})}^{m}
produced by first order finite-volume schemes for Cauchy-problems are presented.
These estimates hinge on suitable consistency and stability results.
The error estimates presented in
[
2
]
depend on a rather abstract consistency criterion, equations (
4
) and (
5
) below. It is proven in
[
2
]
that certain numerical schemes produce solutions satisfying this criterion with
\varepsilon
going to zero under mesh refinement.

Indeed, error estimates are provided in
[
2
]
if, firstly, there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that the weak residual of the numerical solution can be bounded, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t^{1},x)\varphi(t^{1},x)\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t^{2},x)\varphi(t^{2},x)\,dx+\int_{t^{1}}^{t^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\varphi_{t}+\boldsymbol{f}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})\varphi_{x}\right)\,dx\,dt\right|\\
&\quad\leq C\varepsilon\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}(t^{2}-t^{1})\sup_{t\in[t^{1},t^{2}]}TV[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,\,\cdot\,)]\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad\forall\,0\leq t^{1}<t^{2}\leq T,\end{split}

(4)

where
C>0
is a constant,
\varphi
denotes a
scalar
test function with compact support, and
t^{1}<t^{2}
with
t^{1},t^{2}\in[0,T]
(cf. condition
(\mathrm{P}_{\varepsilon})
in
[
2
]
). In addition, the same
\varepsilon>0
needs to bound the violation of the entropy inequality, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}e\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t^{2},x)\right)\varphi(t^{2},x)\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}e\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t^{1},x)\right)\varphi(t^{1},x)\,dx\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\int_{t^{1}}^{t^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(e\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)\varphi_{t}+q(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})\varphi_{x}\right)\,dx\,dt\\
&\leq C\varepsilon\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}(t^{2}-t^{1})\sup_{t\in[t^{1},t^{2}]}TV[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,\,\cdot\,)]\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\,\varphi\geq 0,\,\,\forall\,0\leq t^{1}<t^{2}\leq T.\end{split}

(5)

These consistency results are combined in
[
2
]
with stability estimates that are obtained by comparing
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
with the solution of a linearized PDE in regions where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
is smooth, whereas in regions of large jumps
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
is compared to solutions of Riemann problems. The key condition a numerical solution needs to satisfy so that the results from
[
2
]
are applicable is uniformly bounded total variation and uniformly bounded oscillations. Both these properties are a-posteriori verifiable. The conceptual difference between the work at hand and
[
2
]
is that we aim at actually computing values for
\varepsilon
from the numerical solution instead of proving a-priori convergence rates. This leads to smaller values of
\varepsilon
.

The starting point of our work is the observation that the estimates (
4
) and (
5
) measure residuals in the
W^{-1,1}
-norm. Here, “residual” refers to the quantity that is obtained when the numerical solution is inserted into the weak form of the PDE and of the entropy inequality, respectively.
Let us note that by the very definition of residual, i.e. the left hand sides of (
4
) and (
5
), this approach can only provide error estimates for numerical solutions such that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,x)\in\mathbb{D}
for almost all
(t,x)
.

The consistency estimates in
[
2
]
are already local in time but in order to obtain results that can serve as a basis for mesh-adaptation we also need to localize them in space. Moreover, using an infinite-dimensional space of test functions is not practical and we need to show that this can be replaced by a finite-dimensional space (that is in fact three-dimensional).

Let us compare our results with a-posteriori error estimates for hyperbolic conservation laws that are available in the literature.
Early results concerning wave-front tracking and the Glimm-scheme are due to
[
18
,
14
,
19
]
. For scalar problems, strong stability results are available:
L^{1}
-contraction based on Kružhkov doubling of variables
[
17
]
was used for a-posteriori estimates in
[
15
]
. Results for scalar problems in 1D provided in
[
13
]
, based on stability results in
[
21
]
, are conceptually somewhat similar to our results since they measure residuals in dual norms. However, we are able to provide rigorous upper bounds for these dual norms by introducing a suitable projection.

Error estimates for systems of hyperbolic conservation laws based on relative entropy were obtained in
[
12
,
9
,
6
]
. They do not provide informative bounds if the exact solution is discontinuous, since the classical relative entropy does not provide stability for discontinuous solutions.
In one spatial dimension, the limitations of the relative entropy method can be mitigated by the theories of shifts and
a
-contractions, see
[
16
]
and
[
3
]
.
This opens the door for improved relative entropy based error estimators, see the forthcoming paper
[
8
]
.

Finally, goal-oriented error estimates were considered by
[
11
]
but require the solution of adjoint problems for which well-posedness in case of systems is unclear, even in one space dimension, if the primal solution is discontinuous.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In the second section we explain how the bounds for the residual can be localized and be made computable. In Section 3 we discuss how oscillation bounds that enter the error estimates can be computed efficiently. We also explain how the bounds for residuals and oscillations can be combined to provide a-posteriori error estimates. In the final section we present numerical experiments showing that the error bounds scale as predicted by the a-priori analysis in
[
2
]
.

2.2
Computable bounds for local weak residuals

Computing bounds in (
4
) and (
5
) seems to require testing with an infinite set of test functions which is not practical. We devise a suitable projection operator mapping Lipschitz continuous test functions into a finite dimensional space that allows us to bound the
W^{-1,1}
-norms of weak residuals by their operator norms on this finite dimensional space. We give the details of this procedure for (
4
); (
5
) can be treated analogously.

Equation (
9
) shows that the value of
\mathcal{B}^{n}_{j}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\varphi)
depends on a small number of scalar values and motivates the search for a low dimensional test space whose elements are characterized by averages on the edges in
\mathbf{E}^{\prime}\left(K_{j}^{n}\right):=\left\{S_{j-\frac{1}{2}},\,S^{n+1},\,S_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
, i.e. the edges of
K_{j}^{n}
that enter (
9
).
Such a space is provided by affine linear functions on
K_{j}^{n}
, i.e.,

\mathbf{V}(K^{n}_{j}):=\biggl\{v\,\colon\,K^{n}_{j}\to\mathbb{R},\,v=\alpha_{1}+\frac{(t^{n+1}-t)}{\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\alpha_{2}+\frac{(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-x)}{\Delta x_{j}}\alpha_{3},\,\\
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\,i\in\{1,2,3\}\biggr\},

(10)

which we equip with the
W^{1,\infty}
norm. We define a projection from the test functions to
\mathbf{V}(K^{n}_{j})
that preserves the averages on
\mathbf{E}^{\prime}\left(K_{j}^{n}\right)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{P}^{n}_{j}\,\colon\,W^{1,\infty}(K^{n}_{j})\to\mathbf{V}(K^{n}_{j}),\\
&\varphi\mapsto\mathcal{P}^{n}_{j}\varphi\text{ with }\fint_{S}{\mathcal{P}^{n}_{j}\varphi}\,dS=\fint_{S}{\varphi}\,dS,\,S\in\mathbf{E}^{\prime}\left(K_{j}^{n}\right),\end{split}

(11)

This allows us to efficiently compute an estimate for
\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(W^{1,\infty}(K^{n}_{j}),\,\mathbb{R}^{m})}
, given some piecewise-constant function
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{P}_{0}(\Omega_{T})^{m}
.

Proposition 3
.

The operator
\mathcal{P}^{n}_{j}
is well-defined and linear and for any
\varphi\!\in\!W^{1,\infty}(K^{n}_{j})

\|\mathcal{P}^{n}_{j}\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(K^{n}_{j})}\leq\max\left\{3,\,\sqrt{8+8(c_{j}^{n})^{2}}\right\}\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(K^{n}_{j})},

(12)

where
c_{j}^{n}:=\frac{\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{\Delta x_{j}}
.

Remark 4
.

Note that in practice one would want to use (
2
) with a CFL-conditions which means that
c_{j}^{n}>0
is bounded from above by the maximal wave speed.

Proof.

Since the cell
K_{j}^{n}
is a domain with Lipschitz-boundary, the trace of any
\varphi\in W^{1,\infty}(K_{j}^{n})

is well defined on each edge.

Given the averages of the test function
\boldsymbol{\overline{\varphi}}=\left(\overline{\varphi}_{j-\frac{1}{2}},\,\overline{\varphi}^{n+1},\,\overline{\varphi}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{T}
, the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})^{T}
of
\mathcal{P}^{n}_{j}\varphi\in\mathbf{V}(K^{n}_{j})
are determined by the solution of the linear system

\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2&-1/2\\
1&0&0\\
1&1/2&1/2\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\overline{\varphi}}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&-2&1\\
-1&0&1\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\overline{\varphi}}.

(13)

The matrix describing the mapping from the averages to the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, as defined in (
13
), is invertible. Hence, prescribing the averages along the edges yields a well-defined operator
\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}
.

The
W^{1,\infty}
-norm of
\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi
can be estimated as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(K_{j}^{n})}=&\max\left\{\|\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{j}^{n})},\|\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi)\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{j}^{n})}\right\}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle~\eqref{eq:hat-fcts-subspace-t}}}{{=}}&\left\{\max\{(\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi)(\nu)\,\colon\,\nu\in\mathbf{N}(K_{j}^{n})\},\,\max\left\{\frac{|\alpha_{2}|}{\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}},\,\frac{|\alpha_{3}|}{\Delta x_{j}}\right\}\right\}.\end{split}

(14)

We first estimate
\|\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{j}^{n})}
:

\max\{(\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi)(\nu)\,\colon\,\nu\in\mathbf{N}(K_{j}^{n})\}\\
=\max\left\{|\alpha_{1}|,\,|\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}|,|\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{3}|,|\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}|\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle~(\ref{eq:system-P-coeff})}}{{\leq}}3\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}.

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\left|\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\varphi(t,x_{j+\frac{1}{2}})-\varphi(t,x_{j-\frac{1}{2}})\,dt\right|\leq\Delta x_{j}\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}.

(16)

Since,
\|\varphi\|_{W^{1.\infty}}
is the lipschitz constant of
\varphi

\left|\varphi(\xi_{1})-\varphi(\xi_{2})-(\varphi(\xi_{3})-\varphi(\xi_{2}))\right|\leq\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}(\left|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}\right|+\left|\xi_{3}-\xi_{2}\right|)\\
\leq 2\sqrt{2}\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\left|\left(\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}},\,\Delta x_{j}\right)^{T}\right|.

(17)

Thus, using the relation
\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=c_{j}^{n}\Delta x_{j}
, we have
|\alpha_{2}|\leq\sqrt{8+8(c_{j}^{n})^{2}}\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}
.

Summarizing the above results, we obtain

\|\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(K_{j}^{n})}\leq\max\left\{3,\,\sqrt{8+8(c_{j}^{n})^{2}}\right\}\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(K_{j}^{n})}

and remark that the estimate
does not depend on the grid size if the CFL ratio
\frac{\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{\Delta x_{j}}
is uniformly bounded in each timestep
.

Let us recall that
\mathcal{P}
preserves averages, i.e.

{\left(\overline{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi}\right)}_{j\pm\frac{1}{2}}=\overline{\varphi}_{j\pm\frac{1}{2}}\text{ and }{\left(\overline{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n,\pm}\varphi}\right)}^{n+1}=\overline{\varphi}^{n+1}

(18)

and, thus,
\mathcal{B}^{n}_{j}
is invariant when
\mathcal{P}^{n}_{j}
is applied to the test function.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbf{P}_{0}(\Omega_{T})}^{m}
be a piecewise-constant function and
\varphi\in W^{1,\infty}(K^{n}_{j})
on a space-time cell
K^{n}_{j}\subset\Omega_{T}
. Then

\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\varphi)=\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi).

(19)

Proposition
5
implies that for any
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbf{P}_{0}(\Omega_{T})}^{m}
the operator norm of
\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]
on the set of all test functions can be estimated by the operator norm of
\left.\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\right|_{\mathbf{V}}
in the following manner.

Proposition 6
.

For any piecewise-constant function
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbf{P}_{0}(\Omega_{T})}^{m}

\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(W^{1,\infty}(K^{j}_{n}),\mathbb{R}^{m})}\leq\max\left\{3,\,\sqrt{8+8(c_{j}^{n})^{2}}\right\}\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V}(K^{j}_{n}),\mathbb{R}^{m})},

(20)

with
c_{j}^{n}
as in Proposition
3
.

Proof.

Using the definition of the norm of a linear operator and applying previous results yields:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(W^{1,\infty}(K^{j}_{n}),\mathbb{R}^{m})}&=\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}\left(K^{n}_{j}\right)}\neq 0}\frac{\left|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\varphi)\right|}{\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}\left(K^{n}_{j}\right)}}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:bjn-invariant-under-P}}}{{=}}\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}\left(K^{n}_{j}\right)}\neq 0}\frac{\left|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi)\right|}{\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}\left(K^{n}_{j}\right)}}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:P-is-cont}}}{{\leq}}\max\left\{3,\,\sqrt{8+8(c_{j}^{n})^{2}}\right\}\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}\left(K^{n}_{j}\right)}\neq 0}\frac{\left|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi)\right|}{\|\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi\|_{\mathbf{V}\left(K^{n}_{j}\right)}}\\
&=\max\left\{3,\,\sqrt{8+8(c_{j}^{n})^{2}}\right\}\sup_{\phi\in\mathbf{V}(K^{n}_{j}),\,\|\phi\|_{W^{1,\infty}\left(K^{n}_{j}\right)}=1}\left|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\phi)\right|\\
&=\max\left\{3,\,\sqrt{8+8(c_{j}^{n})^{2}}\right\}\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V}(K^{j}_{n}),\mathbb{R}^{m})}.\end{split}

(21)

In the next step, we show that the weak residual on
[t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega
, can be estimated by the sum of the local residua. To this end, we define

\mathcal{B}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\,\colon\,W^{1,\infty}([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad\mathcal{B}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\varphi)\!=\!\sum_{j=0}^{J}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\left(\left.\varphi\right|_{K^{n}_{j}}\right),

(22)

where the numerical fluxes on the boundary of
\Omega
are obtained by means of some constant (in time and space) outer states
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{-1}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{J+1}
. The local spaces of affine linear functions are gathered to define the space of piecewise linear functions
\mathbf{V}([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega):=\bigoplus_{j}\mathbf{V}(K^{n}_{j})
with the corresponding norm:
v\in\mathbf{V}([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega),\,\|v\|_{\mathbf{V}}:=\max_{j}\left\{\left\|v\big|_{K_{j}^{n}}\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(K_{j}^{n})}\right\}
.

Recall that the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}
in (
9
) corresponds to the weak residual, i.e. the left-hand side of the inequality (
4
), on
K_{j}^{n}
except that we added numerical fluxes. It is a tedious but straight-forward calculation to show that the numerical fluxes cancel upon summation and
\mathcal{B}^{n}
coincides with the weak residual.

As a consequence, the operator norm of
\mathcal{B}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]
can be bounded by summing up the local contributions of the operator norms of
\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]
.

Proposition 7
.

For any piecewise-constant function
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbf{P}_{0}(\Omega_{T})}^{m}

\|\mathcal{B}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(W^{1,\infty}([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega),\mathbb{R}^{m})}\leq C^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\left\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V}(K_{j}^{n})\mathbb{R}^{m})}

(23)

holds, where
C^{n}:=\max\left\{3,\,\max_{j=1,\ldots,J}\sqrt{8+8(c_{j}^{n})^{2}}\right\}
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\mathcal{B}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(W^{1,\infty}([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega),\mathbb{R}^{m})}&=\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}\left([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega\right)}=1}\left|\mathcal{B}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\varphi)\right|\\
&=\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}\left([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega\right)}=1}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{J}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\left(\left.\varphi\right|_{K_{j}^{n}}\right)\right|\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta\text{-ineq.},~\eqref{eq:bjn-invariant-under-P}}}{{\leq}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,\infty}\left([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega\right)}=1}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\left|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\left(\left.\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\varphi\right|_{K_{j}^{n}}\right)\right|\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle~(\ref{eq:estimate-B})}}{{\leq}}C\sum_{j=0}^{J}\sup_{\|\phi\|_{\mathbf{V}\left(K_{j}^{n}\right)}=1}\left|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\phi)\right|.\end{split}

Since the space
\mathbf{V}([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega)
is finite-dimensional, the bound (
23
), together with the total variation of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
, allows to compute
\varepsilon
satisfying the weak residual inequality (
4
). In the following we proceed with localizing the weak entropy dissipation residual (
5
) similarly. To this end, we first rewrite the left-hand side of
(\ref{eq:P_eps_entropy})
as already done for the weak residual, cf. equation (
9
).

Definition 8
.

Let
\psi\in W^{1,\infty}(K_{j}^{n})
be a non-negative test function,
\widehat{q}
a numerical entropy flux and let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{P}_{0}(\Omega_{T})^{m}
be a piecewise-constant function. We define the linear local weak entropy dissipation operator as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\,\colon\,W^{1,\infty}(K_{j}^{n})\to\mathbb{R},\\
&\psi\mapsto\Delta x_{j}\left(e\left({\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,^{n}_{j}\right)-e\left({\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,^{n+1}_{j}\right)\right){\overline{\psi}}\,^{n+1}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad+\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\widehat{q}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j-1}^{n},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}^{n})\overline{\psi}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}-\widehat{q}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}^{n},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j+1}^{n})\overline{\psi}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad+\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}q\left({\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,^{n}_{j}\right)\left(\overline{\psi}_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-\overline{\psi}_{j-\frac{1}{2}}\right),\end{split}

(24)

and the global weak entropy dissipation as
\mathcal{E}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\,\colon\,W^{1,\infty}([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
in the same way as the weak residual on
\Omega_{T}
is defined in equation (
22
).

Similarly to Proposition
5
we obtain that

\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\psi)=\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\mathcal{P}_{j}^{n}\psi).

(25)

For ease of notation we define the cone of affine linear functions with non-negative edge averages as
\mathbf{V}^{+}(K_{j}^{n}):=\{v\in\mathbf{V}(K_{j}^{n})\,\colon\,\fint_{S}{v}\,dS\geq 0\quad\forall S\in\mathbf{E}\left(K_{j}^{n}\right)\}
. In the following we only need to consider
\phi\in\mathbf{V}^{+}(K_{j}^{n})
such that
\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\phi)<0
. We obtain the following bounds, with the same notation as in Propositions
6
and
7
, applying the same technique we have used for the weak residual:

\inf_{\psi\in W^{1,\infty}(K_{j}^{n}),\,\psi\geq 0}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\psi)}{\|\psi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(K_{j}^{n})}}\geq C^{n}\inf_{\phi\in\mathbf{V}^{+}(K_{j}^{n})}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\phi)}{\|\phi\|_{\mathbf{V}(K_{j}^{n})}},

(26)

with
C^{n}
as in Proposition
7
, and its global version

\inf_{\|\psi\|_{W^{1,\infty}([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega)}=1,\,\psi\geq 0}\mathcal{E}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\psi)\geq C^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\inf_{\phi\in\mathbf{V}^{+}(K_{j}^{n}),\,\|\phi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(K_{j}^{n})}=1}\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\phi).

(27)

Next, we present an example in order gain intuition for the scaling of the weak residuals:

Remark 9
(Local scaling of the weak residual).

Firstly, we consider a specific stationary approximation of a stationary shock in the (scalar) Burgers equations, i.e.,
f(u)=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}
,

\widehat{u}_{j}^{n}:=\begin{cases}1&,\quad j<0,\\
0&,\quad j=0,\\
-1&,\quad j>0.\end{cases}

This numerical solution is stationary if the Engquist-Osher flux or the Godunov flux is used.
If the numerical flux from the scheme is also used in the definition of the weak residual the latter satisfies:

\left\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{u}]\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V}(K_{j}^{n}),\mathbb{R})}=\begin{cases}0&,\quad j<0,\\
\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Delta x_{0}&,\quad j=0,\\
0&,\quad j>0.\end{cases}

The residual is concentrated in a single cell and the residual on a time-slab (
23
) satisfies
\|\mathcal{B}^{n}[\widehat{u}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(W^{1,\infty}([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega),\,\mathbb{R}^{m})}=O(\Delta x_{0}\cdot\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})
.
This, in turn, implies that the so-obtained
\varepsilon
in the inequality (
4
), scales as
\varepsilon=O(\Delta x_{0})
.
This seems reasonable, since, arguably, one cannot expect the residual to converge to zero faster than the approximation error.

It is worthwhile to note that the numerical approximation

\widehat{u}_{j}^{n}:=\begin{cases}1&,\quad j\leq 0,\\
-1&,\quad j>0,\end{cases}

which is also stationary for the Godunov flux and the Engquist-Osher flux, satisfies
\|\mathcal{B}^{n}[\widehat{u}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(W^{1,\infty}([t^{n},t^{n+1}]\times\Omega),\,\mathbb{R}^{m})}=0
for all
n
.

Secondly, for any numerical solution that is obtained by a Lipschitz continuous and consistent numerical flux with mesh size
\Delta x:=\max\{\Delta x_{j-1},\Delta x_{j},\Delta x_{j+1}\}
one can show that

\left\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{u}]\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V}(K_{j}^{n}),\mathbb{R})}\leq L\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\Delta x\right)\left(\left|\widehat{u}^{n}_{j-1}-\widehat{u}^{n}_{j}\right|+\left|\widehat{u}^{n}_{j}-\widehat{u}^{n}_{j+1}\right|\right),

(28)

where
L
is the Lipschitz-constant of the numerical flux so that

\sum_{j}\left\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{u}]\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V}(K_{j}^{n}),\mathbb{R})}\leq 2L\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\Delta x\right)TV[\widehat{u}].

In this case we obtain that
\varepsilon
scales as
\varepsilon=O(\Delta x)
, i.e. with order one in mesh size, same as the numerical scheme. It can be seen from (
28
) that weak-residuals are concentrated in cells adjacent to significant jumps in
\widehat{u}
.

Now, we can compute a bound for
\varepsilon
satisfying both inequalities in
[0,t]\times\Omega
where
t\in(0,T]
as follows. First, taking the
\ell^{\infty}\ell^{1}
-norm of
(\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|)_{(j,n)}
over all cells
K_{j}^{n}
such that
K_{j}^{n}\cap([0,t]\times\Omega)\neq\emptyset
and
(\{t\}\times\Omega)\cap\overline{K_{j}^{N^{\prime}}}\neq\emptyset
(i.e.
N^{\prime}
is the largest time-layer of cells in
[0,t]\times\Omega
), yields estimates of the weak residual and the entropy dissipation residual in
[0,t]\times\Omega
denoted by

\displaystyle\beta:=\max_{n\in\{1,\ldots,N^{\prime}\}}\frac{1}{\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V}(K_{j}^{n}),\mathbb{R}^{m})},

(29)

\displaystyle\eta:=\max_{n\in\{1,\ldots,N^{\prime}\}}\frac{1}{\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\left|\inf_{\phi\in\mathbf{V}^{+}(K_{j}^{n}),\,\|\phi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}=1}\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\phi)\right|

(30)

where we use the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Then, we can determine
\varepsilon
satisfying (
4
) and (
5
) as follows

\varepsilon:=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\equiv const,\\
\dfrac{C\max\left\{\beta,\eta\right\}}{\sup_{s\in[0,t]}TV[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,\,\cdot\,)]}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(31)

where
C=\max\left\{3,\,\max_{j\in\{0,\ldots,J\},n\in\{0,\ldots,N^{\prime}\}}\sqrt{8+8(c_{j}^{n})^{2}}\right\}
.

Note that the total variation in (
31
) is zero whenever
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,\,\cdot\,)\equiv const
. However, in this case both inequalities (
4
) and (
5
) are satisfied trivially, since the weak residual
\beta
is zero as well and the entropy is constant.

2.3
An algorithm for computing bounds on local weak residuals

In this section, we present an algorithm for the computation of the previously presented upper bound
(
23
) for the weak residual and the weak entropy dissipation residual.

We observe that if the flux used in the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}
is the same as the one used in the finite volume scheme, (
2
), then
\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]((t,x)\mapsto c)=0
for any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, in order to estimate the operator norm
\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V}(K^{j}_{n}),\mathbb{R}^{m})}
we normalize test functions
\phi\in\mathbf{V}(K_{j}^{n})
,
defined in (
10
) as
\phi(t,x)=\alpha_{1}+\frac{(t^{n+1}-t)}{\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\alpha_{2}+\frac{(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-x)}{\Delta x_{j}}\alpha_{3}
, by setting
\alpha_{1}=0
. Note that
\|\mathrm{D}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
implies
|\alpha_{2}|\leq\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
and
|\alpha_{3}|\leq\Delta x_{j}
. Since
\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]
is linear, its norm can be estimated as follows

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V}(K^{j}_{n}),\mathbb{R}^{m})}&=\sup_{\|\phi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}=1}\left|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}](\phi)\right|\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\|\mathrm{D}(\phi)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1}}{{\leq}}\left|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]((t,x)\mapsto t)\right|+\left|\mathcal{B}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]((t,x)\mapsto x)\right|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\big(\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big)^{2}\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j-1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j}\right)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j+1}\right)\right|\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\Delta x_{j}\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j-1},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j}\right)+\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}_{j+1}\right)-2\boldsymbol{f}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}^{n})\right|.\end{split}

(32)

We perform a similar estimate for the entropy dissipation residual operator
\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}
keeping in mind that the test functions are in
\mathbf{V}^{+}(K_{j}^{n})
, i.e. their averages are non-negative. We define

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{1}&:=\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\left((t,x)\mapsto 1\right)\\
&=\Delta x_{j}\left(e\left({\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,^{n}_{j}\right)-e\left({\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,^{n+1}_{j}\right)\right)+\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\widehat{q}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j-1}^{n},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}^{n})-\widehat{q}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}^{n},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j+1}^{n})\right),\\
\mathcal{E}_{2}&:=\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\left((t,x)\mapsto t^{n+1}-t\right)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big)^{2}\left(\widehat{q}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j-1}^{n},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}^{n})-\widehat{q}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}^{n},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j+1}^{n})\right),\\
\mathcal{E}_{3}&:=\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]\left((t,x)\mapsto x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-x\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\big(\Delta x_{j}\big)^{2}\left(e\left({\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,^{n}_{j}\right)-e\left({\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}}\,^{n+1}_{j}\right)\right)+\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Delta x_{j}\left(\widehat{q}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j-1}^{n},\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}^{n})-q(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}^{n})\right).\end{split}

(33)

Due to linearity of
\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]
we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\inf_{\phi\in\mathbf{V}^{+}(K_{j}^{n}),\,\|\phi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}=1}\mathcal{E}_{j}^{n}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}]&=\inf_{\phi\in\mathbf{V}^{+}(K_{j}^{n}),\,\|\phi\|_{W^{1,\infty}}=1}\biggl(\alpha_{1}\mathcal{E}_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\mathcal{E}_{2}+\frac{\alpha_{3}}{\Delta x_{j}}\mathcal{E}_{3}\!\!\biggl).\\
&\geq\min\{0,\mathcal{E}_{1}\}+\min\{0,\mathcal{E}_{2}\}+\min\{0,\mathcal{E}_{3}\}.\end{split}

(34)

The last estimate holds due to the fact that
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}=1
implies
|\alpha_{1}|\leq 1
.

These estimates provide the final ingredients for Algorithm
1
that computes
\varepsilon
.

3
L^{\infty}L^{1}
error estimation

In the first part of this section, we explain how the
L^{\infty}L^{1}
-error of piecewise-constant approximate solution can be bounded in terms of the computable upper bounds for weak residual and entropy dissipation residual derived in Section
2
. This is based on stability estimates from
[
2
]
that depend on certain quantities related to the oscillation of the numerical solution.
We present an algorithm for the computation of these quantities and the overall a-posteriori error estimator in the second part of this section.

3.1
Computing oscillation bounds

The statements in
[
2
, Thm. 3.1 and Thm. 4.1]
provide error estimates for regions where the solution is either smooth or has isolated discontinuities, respectively.
To use Bressan’s stability results it is required to compute oscillations in
meso-timeslabs

[0,\tau]\times\Omega
. We can choose the length of the meso timeslab and arguments from
[
2
]
show that
\tau\approx\Delta t^{\frac{1}{3}}
will lead to good error estimates. In particular, we expect that
\tau\gg\Delta t
.

As discussed in
[
2
]
, our task is to partition the strip
[0,\tau]\times\Omega
into trapezoids
S_{k}
, that enclose isolated discontinuities of certain strength and trapezoids
G_{k}
, covering the remaining parts of the meso-timeslab, where the solution is mainly “smooth”. In the following, we distinguish between discontinuities in the numerical solution that are due to the fact that we use the finite-volume approximation but approximate a smooth function and
significant
discontinuities that we believe to approximate discontinuities in the exact solution.

The idea behind this partitioning is comparing
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
to solutions of linearized hyperbolic PDEs in “smooth” regions whereas in regions near significant discontinuities
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
is compared to solutions of Riemann problems. The latter can only be done for sufficiently isolated significant discontinuities. Since the aforesaid isolated significant discontinuities might correspond to different wave types (shocks or contact discontinuities) we shall refer to them as
surges
. The fact that surges are required to be sufficiently isolated, i.e. the distance between surges has to be large enough such that no wave interactions occur in the meso-timeslab implies that some significant discontinuities of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
might end up in the “smooth” trapezoids. If this happens it reduces the accuracy of the estimate, however, this does not affect the scaling of the estimator, see Remark
15
for details.

In order to distinguish surges from “smooth” regions, e.g. piecewise-constant approximations of rarefaction waves, a lower threshold
\sigma>0
on the surge strength, i.e. on the maximal absolute difference of the conservative variables, needs to be prescribed. Following the construction in
[
2
, Section 4]
, a surge is enclosed in a narrow strip
\{(t,x)\,\colon\,t\in[0,\tau],\,|x-\gamma(t)|<\delta\}
around the approximate surge curve
\gamma(t)=x_{0}+\lambda t
originating at some footpoint
x_{0}\in\Omega
. We choose the strip-width
\delta
of order
\delta=O(\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}})
which will ensure correct asymptotic behavior later on. The narrow strip is contained in a trapezoid of width at least
2\left(\delta+\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}\right)
. Suppose we have localized
J_{S}
surges in a meso-timeslab (details on this will be given in Algorithm
2
). Then we define the trapezoid surrounding the
k
-th surge originating at
x_{0,k}
as a convex hull

\displaystyle\begin{split}&S_{k}:=\text{conv}\left\{(0,a^{\prime}_{k}),\,(0,b^{\prime}_{k}),\,(\tau,a_{k}),\,(\tau,b_{k})\right\},\text{ where: }\\
&a_{k}=x_{0,k}+\lambda^{-}\tau-\delta_{k}-\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}},\quad b_{k}=x_{0,k}+\lambda^{+}\tau+\delta_{k}+\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}\\
&a^{\prime}_{k}=a_{k}-\lambda^{+}\tau,\quad b^{\prime}_{k}=b_{k}-\lambda^{-}\tau,\end{split}

(35)

for
k\in\{0,\ldots,J_{S}\}
. Let
\lambda^{+}
and
\lambda^{-}
denote the maximal and minimal speed of the characteristics in
[0,\tau]\times\Omega
, respectively.
The remaining parts of the meso-timeslab,
[0,\tau]\times\Omega\,\,\backslash\,\,\bigcup_{k=0}^{J_{S}}S_{k}
, are covered, in accordance with
[
2
, Eqs. (3.1)–(3.5)]
, by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&G_{k}:=\text{conv}\left\{(0,a^{\prime}_{k}),\,(0,b^{\prime}_{k}),\,(\tau,\,a^{\prime}_{k}+\tau\lambda^{+}+\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}),\,(\tau,\,b^{\prime}_{k}+\tau\lambda^{-}-\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}})\right\},\end{split}

(36)

where
a^{\prime}_{k}<a^{\prime}_{k+1}<b^{\prime}_{k}<b^{\prime}_{k+1}
for all
k\in\{0,\ldots,J_{G}\}
, are chosen such that
b^{\prime}_{k}-a^{\prime}_{k}>2\tau(\lambda^{+}-\lambda^{-})
. The last condition ensures that each point in the meso-timeslab is contained in at most two smooth trapezoids, and, if there are only finitely many surges in the exact solution, the number of surge trapezoids should be bounded uniformly in
\Delta x
(by the number of surges in the exact solution). The slopes of the trapezoids
G_{k}
are given by the maximal and minimal characteristic speeds adjusted by
\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}/\tau\approx\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}
and we have
[0,\tau]\times\Omega=\bigcup_{k=0}^{J_{S}}S_{k}\,\cup\,\bigcup_{k=0}^{J_{G}}G_{k}
.

In the trapezoids covering “smooth” regions, the supremum of oscillations is defined as

\boldsymbol{\kappa}:=\max_{k\in\{0,\ldots,J_{G}\}}\sup_{(t,x),\,(s,y)\in G_{k}}\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,x)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,y)\right|.

(37)

The final ingredient required for the
L^{\infty}L^{1}
-error estimators are the oscillations of the solution in the vicinity of a surge. For surge-trapezoids,
S_{k}
, it is expected that oscillations outside of the narrow strip of width
\delta_{k}
surrounding the
k
-th surge curve are of the same order of magnitude as the oscillations in “smooth” regions. Thus, we define the left and right sub-trapezoids of
S_{k}
excluding the strip around the
k
-th surge as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&S_{k}^{l}:=\left\{(t,x)\in S_{k}\,\colon\,a^{\prime}_{k}+\lambda^{+}t\leq x\leq x_{0,k}-\delta_{k}+\lambda t\right\},\\
&S_{k}^{r}:=\left\{(t,x)\in S_{k}\,\colon\,x_{0,k}+\delta_{k}+\lambda t\leq x\leq b^{\prime}_{k}+\lambda^{-}t\right\},\\
\end{split}

(38)

cf.
[
2
, Eqs. (4.5)-(4.6)]
, and the oscillations in
S_{k}
as

\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{k}^{\prime}:=\max\left\{\sup_{(t,x),\,(s,y)\in S_{k}^{l}}\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,x)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,y)\right|,\sup_{(t,x),\,(s,y)\in S_{k}^{r}}\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,x)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,y)\right|\right\}.

(39)

Remark 10
.

Note that we do not require precise surge curves. Indeed, we exclude strips of width
\delta_{k}=O(\varepsilon^{2/3})
around approximate surge curves
\gamma_{k}
when computing oscillations in surge trapezoids
S_{k}
. This allows us to handle uncertainty of surge positions due to numerical dissipation as well as the fact that, in general, surge curves do not have constant speed. In the following subsection we present algorithms for surge detection and determination of widths of strips around surges
\delta_{k}
.

The above definitions allow us to state the following local in time a-posteriori error estimate, summarizing
[
2
, Thm. 3.1]
and
[
2
, Thm. 4.1]
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\,\colon\,[0,\infty)\times\Xi\to\Xi
define a Lipschitz-semigroup of entropy weak solutions of the hyperbolic conservation law (
1
) in the domain of the semigroup
\Xi\subset L^{1}(\mathbb{R};\,\mathbb{R}^{m})
of functions with small total variation. Given a threshold on the surge strength,
\sigma_{0}>0
we define

C_{0}:=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\min_{k\in\{0,\ldots,J_{S}\}}\left(\varepsilon/\delta_{k}+\delta_{k}/\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}+2\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}_{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}}.

(40)

Let the meso-timeslab
[0,\tau]\times\Omega
be partitioned into trapezoids according to (
35
) and (
36
). Then, there exist constants
C_{S}
and
C_{G}
depending on
C_{0}
such that the error at time
\tau
of the solution is estimated as

\max_{0\leq t\leq\tau}\left\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,\,\cdot\,)-\mathcal{S}_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{S}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{S}+C_{G}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{G}

(41)

where

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{S}:=\tau\sum_{k=0}^{J_{S}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta_{k}}+\frac{\delta_{k}}{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}}+2\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}_{k}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{G}:=\left(\tau+\boldsymbol{\kappa}\right)\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}TV[\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,\,\cdot\,)]\,\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(42)

This theorem provides a basis for constructing adaptive mesh refinement methods for time-marching schemes and, more importantly, serves as the basis for the error estimate over the whole space-time domain
\Omega_{T}
presented right after a few remarks.

Remark 12
.

1.

The discussion in
[
2
, Rem. 5.1]
sheds light on the expected asymptotic behavior of the error bound (
42
). The variety of choices in the construction of the stability estimates implies that a rigorous analysis of the asymptotics cannot be performed easily. However, the same variety allows tuning the estimates in a heuristic manner in hope of achieving sharper bounds. This considerations led to the choices of the parameters that are incorporated in the statement of Theorem
11
and are expected to provide error bounds approaching zero with
\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}|\log\varepsilon|
. In the following section we will perform numerical experiments to investigate the error bound asymptotics.

2.

Many choices in the computation of the error estimator, e.g. the precise choice of the trapezoidal domain decomposition or the precise values of
\delta_{k}
, influence how sharp the estimate in Theorem
11
is. However, as long as the scaling of these choices is appropriate, e.g. as long as
\delta_{k}
scales with
\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}
, these choices do not affect the overall asymptotics.

3.

Prescribing a threshold
\sigma
on the surge strength allows us to select the sharper error bound in
[
2
, Thm. 4.1]
.

4.

The estimate given in Theorem
11
is local in time in the sense that it quantifies errors on a meso-timeslab. Implicitly, there is a stronger locality of the error bound in the sense that diminishing local residual contributions of a particular space-time cell (e.g. by refining it in an adaptive mesh setting) reduces the overall error bound. However, due to the nature of non-linear hyperbolic conservation laws, in particular the lack of coercivity, obtaining error bounds for specific cells or showing efficiency of a-posteriori error estimates seems out of reach.

We summarize the analytic results of this work with an estimate of the
L^{\infty}L^{1}
-error of a finite volume solution on the whole space-time domain
\Omega_{T}
. To this end, we define
\varepsilon_{T}
as in (
31
), however incorporating all cells in
\Omega_{T}
. We partition the domain into timeslabs of size
\tau\approx\varepsilon_{T}^{\frac{1}{3}}
and denote quantities in the
\mu
-th timeslab by the superscript
μ
. Thus, we denote the “smooth” oscillations in the
\mu
-th timeslab as
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\mu}
and the oscillations in the
k
-th surge-trapezoid by
(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{k}^{\prime})^{\mu}
, as in (
37
) and (
39
), respectively. Furthermore, the maximal oscillation of all surge-trapezoids is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}_{\max}:=\max_{k,\mu}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{k}^{\prime})^{\mu}
and similarly the maximal width of a strip around a surge by
\delta_{\max}:=\max_{k,\mu}(\delta_{k})^{\mu}
. Summing up the estimates
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{S}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{G}
, as well as pulling
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}_{\max}
and
\delta_{\max}
out of the sum yields the main theorem.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
define a Lipschitz-semigroup of entropy weak solutions of the hyperbolic conservation law (
1
) and
\sigma>0
a lower threshold on the surge strength. Let the space-time domain
\Omega_{T}
be uniformly partitioned into
M+1
timeslabs of size
\tau\approx\varepsilon_{T}^{\frac{1}{3}}
, i.e.
[0,T]=\bigcup_{\mu=0}^{M}[\mu\tau,(\mu+1)\tau]
. Let the
\mu
-th timeslab contain
J^{\mu}_{S}
surges and be partitioned into trapezoids
S^{\mu}_{k}
and
G^{\mu}_{k}
as defined in (
35
) and (
36
), respectively. Then, there exist constants
C_{S}
and
C_{G}
depending on
C_{0}
, cf. (
40
), such that the
L^{\infty}L^{1}
-error at time
T
of the piecewise-constant solution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbf{P}_{0}(\Omega_{T})
is estimated as

\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(t,\,\cdot\,)-\mathcal{S}_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{S}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{S}^{T}+C_{G}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}^{T}_{G}

(43)

where

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{S}^{T}:=\left(\varepsilon_{T}^{\frac{1}{3}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}_{\max}+\delta_{\max}\right)\sum_{\mu=0}^{M}J_{S}^{\mu},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{G}^{T}:=\varepsilon_{T}^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(T+\sum_{\mu=0}^{M}\kappa^{\mu}\right).

(44)

Remark 14
.

Unfortunately, it is not feasible to compute either of the constants
C_{S}
and
C_{G}
since they depend, among other quantities, on the Lipschitz constant of the semigroup
\mathcal{S}
and on constants that emerge from the implicit function theorem used to estimate the jump strength.

Remark 15
(Scaling of the error estimator).

1.

The arguments in
[
2
, Rem. 5.1]
assert that, if the numerical solution approximates an exact solution that is piecewise Lipschitz with finitely many discontinuities, then the oscillations in trapezoids containing surges fulfill
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}_{k}=C^{\prime}\tau
for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
. This is due to the fact that
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}_{k}
measures oscillations excluding the surge curve, where the solution is assumed to be Lipschitz continuous. If no wave interactions are present, then
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{G}
behaves as
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}|\log\varepsilon|
, as mentioned in Remark
12
, and, since we assumed
\delta_{k}=O(\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}})
, the heuristic expectation of the overall behavior of the error estimator is

C_{S}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{S}^{T}+C_{G}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}^{T}_{G}=O(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}|\log\varepsilon|=O(1)\cdot(\max_{n}\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{3}}\left|\log\max_{n}\Delta t^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right|.

(45)

2.

If a numerical solution features a surge that is smeared out to the extent that it remains undetected, then its contribution to the oscillation in a “smooth” trapezoid will be small (similar to the oscillations of other smooth trapezoids). Thus, this will increase the amount by which the error estimator overestimates the error but it will not reduce the convergence order of the error estimator.

3.

The proposed trapezoid partition of the domain enforces a distance of
O(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}})
between surges and, thus, points in the space-time domain where surges interact are necessarily covered by smooth trapezoids
G_{k}
. This implies that the oscillations in these
G_{k}
will be large. The discussion in
[
2
, Rem. 5.1]
shows that if the exact solution has only finitely many surges then the sum of length of time-slabs with undetected surge interactions is

O(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}\cdot[\text{total nr.~of surge interactions}]=O(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}).

Thus, the undetected surge-interactions do not affect the asymptotics of the right-hand side of (
43
).

3.2
Algorithm for computing suitable trapezoids

In order to make the error estimator (
43
) fully computable, it remains to formulate an algorithm for the decomposition of meso-timeslabs into trapezoids containing “smooth” parts and trapezoids containing surges.

In the algorithms presented in this section we employ the following data structures. A linked
\mathtt{list}
is initialized by a set-like notation or as an empty list if no arguments are provided. The
k
-th element of a list
\mathtt{L}
is denoted by the subscript notation
\mathtt{L}_{k}
. Trapezoids are represented via the
\mathtt{trpz}(I_{1},I_{2})
-structure where
I_{1}=[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
and
I_{2}=[a,b]
are the bottom and the upper interval of the trapezoid, respectively. Corners of a
\mathtt{trpz}
-object
\mathtt{T}
are denoted by
\mathtt{T}.a^{\prime}
,
\mathtt{T}.b^{\prime}
,
\mathtt{T}.a
and
\mathtt{T}.b
. Trapezoids containing strong discontinuities are stored via
\mathtt{surgetrpz}(S^{l},\,S^{r})
, where
S^{l}
and
S^{r}
are the parts of the trapezoid away from the surge, as defined in (
38
). These two trapezoidal regions are sufficient to define a trapezoid enclosing a surge and, moreover, we are interested in the oscillations in
S^{l}
and
S^{r}
only. Furthermore, we define a function
\mathtt{inb}(x)
that projects
x
to the domain
\Omega
that we employ to avoid trapezoids leaving the domain.

The function
surge-trpzs
searches for sufficiently isolated surge-footpoints in the lowest timelevel of each meso-timeslab. Subsequently, the intersection of the cone of extreme characteristic speeds emanating from each potential surge-footpoint with the highest timelevel of the meso-timeslab is scanned for corresponding surges.

In order to reliably tag cells containing jumps the function
detect-jumps
implements multiresolution analysis techniques based on wavelets
[
5
,
20
]
. In principle, any appropriate discontinuity indicator could be employed, for instance the entropy-based indicator introduced in
[
22
]
.

In order to identify the trapezoidal decomposition of the domain
\Omega_{T}
, that is required in Theorem
13
, we proceed by iterating through meso-timeslabs of size
\tau\approx\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}
. In each timeslab, strips of width
\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}
containing surges are tagged. If we find no or more than one jump regions in this cone at the highest time level no surge curve is created. This may mean that certain significant discontinuities are not tagged as surges causing some mild overestimation that has been discussed in the second and third point of Remark
15
.

Since the exact positions of surge-footpoints,
x_{0,k}
, are unknown, we set
x_{0,k}
to the center of the found surge-region in the lowest time-level of the meso-timeslab. Thus, if a surge is found the next step is determining the width of the strip around the
k
-th surge
\delta_{k}
. To this end, we divide the strip of width
\delta_{k}
in two parts: one to the left and one to the right of the surge with widths
\delta_{k}^{l}
and
\delta_{k}^{r}
, respectively. Each initial width
\delta_{k}^{l,r}
is set to a slightly smaller value than the width of the sub-trapezoids
S_{k}^{l}
and
S^{r}_{k}
, i.e.
\delta^{l,r}_{k}=\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{3}}-\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}
. We save the maximal and minimal value of the solution in the (thin) trapezoids
S_{k}^{l,r}
. Subsequently, we reduce each
\delta_{k}^{l,r}
by
\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}
(and by this increase the area of
S_{k}^{l,r}
) until either the oscillations
S_{k}^{l,r}
computed by (
39
) reach a value larger or equal
\tau
or
\delta_{k}
reaches
\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{3}}
. Here, we
include oscillations of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
in all cells that have non-empty intersection with the trapezoid. In each step, it is required to calculate the maximal and minimal values only in the added area of
S_{k}^{l,r}
. Hence, the cost of this procedure is the same as the cost of calculating the oscillations in
S_{k}^{l,r}
. This procedure, described in detail in Algorithm
2
, ensures that large oscillations concentrate in a smallest possible strip of width
\delta_{k}
keeping oscillations in
S_{k}^{l,r}
small.

C=1
and
\gamma=\frac{7}{5}
. We consider two Riemann problems: one where the solution consists of two rarefaction waves and one where we have a rarefaction and a shock. For the third test case we consider a solution to the Burgers equation

containing a single shock whose speed is rapidly changing. For each case we study
\varepsilon

\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{E}}_{S}

from (

In all three test cases we employ the local Lax-Friedrich numerical flux, i.e.

\widehat{f}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{L},\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{R})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{f}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{L})+\boldsymbol{f}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{R})\right)-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\max}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{R}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{L}\right)

with
\lambda_{\max}=\max\{\|\lambda(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{L})\|_{\infty},\,\|\lambda(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{R})\|_{\infty}\}
,
where
\lambda(\boldsymbol{u})
denotes the set of eigenvalues of
D\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})
, and the corresponding numerical entropy flux

\widehat{q}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{L},\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{R})=\frac{1}{2}\left(q(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{L})+q(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{R})\right)-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\max}\left(e\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{R}\right)-e\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{L}\right)\right).

The CFL-number is set to 0.9 and the spatial grid consists of
2\cdot 2^{L}
cells uniformly discretizing
\Omega=(-5,5)
. Here,
L
denotes the level of refinement and, thus, the coarsest possible grid, for
L=0
, contains two cells. The boundary ghost cells for all cases are set to constant continuations of the values in their respective neighbors.
We monitor the scaling of several quantities with respect to the level
L

with empirical orders of convergence
\text{EoC}:=-\log_{2}(e_{L+1}/e_{L})
.

For the shock detection we employ the Daubechies wavelets and set the minimal surge strength to
\sigma=0.1
. The error estimator is implemented in the

-framework
