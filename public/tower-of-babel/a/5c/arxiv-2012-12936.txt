Newton acceleration on manifolds identified by proximal gradient methods This work is partly funded by the ANR JCJC project STROLL (ANR-19-CE23-0008).
Gilles Bareilles, Franck Iutzeler, Jérôme Malick

Abstract
Proximal methods are known to identify the underlying substructure of nonsmooth optimization problems. Even more, in many interesting situations, the output of a proximity operator comes with its structure at no additional cost, and convergence is improved once it matches the structure of a minimizer. However, it is impossible in general to know whether the current structure is final or not; such highly valuable information has to be exploited adaptively. To do so, we place ourselves in the case where a proximal gradient method can identify manifolds of differentiability of the nonsmooth objective. Leveraging this manifold identification, we show that Riemannian Newton-like methods can be intertwined with the proximal gradient steps to drastically boost the convergence. We prove the superlinear convergence of the algorithm when solving some nondegenerated nonsmooth nonconvex optimization problems. We provide numerical illustrations on optimization problems regularized by ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm or trace-norm.

Introduction

Nonsmoothness naturally appears in various applications of optimization, e.g. in decomposition methods in operations research

In these applications,

Exploiting the underlying smooth substructure of objective functions to develop second-order methods has been a subject of fruitful research in nonsmooth optimization, pioneered by the developments around

-Newton algorithms

Interestingly, these Newton-type methods for nonsmooth optimization are connected to the standard Newton methods of nonlinear programming (SQP) and to the Newton methods of Riemannian optimization; see

In this paper, we focus on a special situation where the smooth substructure
can be exploited numerically.
We consider the nonsmooth optimization problem

In the ideal case where we know that the iterates are on the optimal manifold, one could switch to a more sophisticated method, e.g. updating parameters of first-order methods as in

Our algorithm uses the same basic ingredients that work behind the scenes for existing nonsmooth second-order algorithms (e.g.

Along the way, our study reveals results that have some interest on their own, in particular: we refine the smoothness properties of the proximal gradient operator around structured critical points; we formalize complementary properties on line searches in Riemannian optimization; we also bring a careful attention to the technical details induced by nonconvexity.

Submanifolds.

A subset

A

A basic tool to investigate approximations on manifolds is notion of the

at

T_{x}\mathcal{M}\triangleq\left\{c^{\prime}(0)~|~c:I\to\mathcal{M}\text{ is a smooth curve around $0$ and }c(0)=x\right\}.

The tangent space is a
p
-dimensional space containing

. Each tangent space
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
is equipped with a scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{x}:T_{x}\mathcal{M}\times T_{x}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}

T\mathcal{B}\triangleq\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}(x,T_{x}\mathcal{M}).

Note also that both tangent and normal spaces at

T_{x}\mathcal{M}=\text{Im}~\operatorname{D}_{\varphi}(0)=\text{Ker}~\operatorname{D}_{\Phi}(x)\qquad N_{x}\mathcal{M}=\text{Ker}~\operatorname{D}_{\varphi}(0)^{*}=\text{Im}~\operatorname{D}_{\Phi}(x)^{*}

A

{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y)=\inf_{c\in C_{x,y}}\int_{0}^{1}\|c^{\prime}(t)\|_{c(t)}\mathrm{d}t

Riemannian Gradients and Hessian.

Let
F:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, the

of

at

\operatorname{D}F(x):T_{x}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}

\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]\triangleq\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}F\circ c(t)\right|_{t=0}

If

Notions of derivation for vector fields and of acceleration for curves are used to define second-order objects. Let a curve

and a smooth vector field

on

of

\frac{\operatorname{D}}{\mathrm{d}t}Z(t)\triangleq\mathrm{proj}_{c(t)}Z^{\prime}(t)

The

Equivalently, we have

A second order Taylor development can now be formulated. Let

Algorithms on manifolds: retractions and convergence rates.

Iterative Riemannian methods require a way to produce curves on

given a point

\eta

as a smooth map
\operatorname{R}:T\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{M}
such that

\operatorname{R}_{x}(0)=x\quad
and
\quad\operatorname{D}\operatorname{R}_{x}(0):T_{x}\mathcal{M}\to T_{x}\mathcal{M}
is the identity map:
D\operatorname{R}_{x}(0)[v]=v

where, for each

is defined as the restriction of

at
x

\operatorname{R}_{x}(v)=\operatorname{R}(x,v)

is a retraction

such that, for all

, the curve

has zero acceleration at 0:
c^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
.
Thus

at

(2.3)

Finally, the convergence rates on manifolds are defined as follows. A sequence of points
(x_{k})

converges (Q-)linearly
to some point
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{M}
if there exist an integer
K>0
and a constant

such that, for all

{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{k+1},\bar{x})\leq q~{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{k},\bar{x}).

The sequence
converges with order at least

if there exists an integer

and a constant

such that, for all

{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{k+1},\bar{x})\leq q~{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{k},\bar{x})^{p}.

The convergence is

when

2.2
Recalls on nonsmooth optimization

We review the basic notions of variational analysis used in this paper, following the monograph
rockafellar2009variational
.
For this section,
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a proper function.

Subgradients.

Consider a point
\bar{x}
with
g(\bar{x})
finite. The set of
regular subgradients

\widehat{\partial}g(\bar{x})\triangleq\left\{v:g(x)\geq g(\bar{x})+\langle v,x-\bar{x}\rangle+o(\|x-\bar{x}\|)\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}

is closed and convex, but the subdifferential mapping
\widehat{\partial}g(\cdot)
may not be outer semi-continuous
(
rockafellar2009variational
, Th. 8.6, Prop. 8.7)
. To overcome this problem, the set of
(general or limiting) subgradients
is defined as

\partial g(\bar{x})\triangleq\left\{\lim_{r}v_{r}:v_{r}\in\widehat{\partial}g(x_{r}),x_{r}\to\bar{x},g(x_{r})\to g(\bar{x})\right\}.

The limiting subdifferential is by design outer semi-continuous:

\limsup_{x\to\bar{x}}\partial g(x)=\{u:\exists x_{r}\to\bar{x},\exists u_{r}\to u\text{ with }u_{r}\in\partial g(x_{r})\}~\subset~\partial g(\bar{x}),

which is an attractive property to study the properties of sequences of points whose subgradients converge.
We say that a function is
(Clarke) regular
at
\bar{x}
if the regular and limiting subdifferentials at
\bar{x}
coincide
(
rockafellar2009variational
, Def. 7.25, Cor. 8.11)
. This is notably the case for convex functions where the two above definitions coincide with the convex subdifferential
(
rockafellar2009variational
, Prop. 8.12)
.

Optimality conditions and critical points.

The subdifferential allows to derive optimality conditions: for a local minimizer

of

A point satisfying these conditions is called a critical point. The analysis of the algorithms of this paper will provide convergence guarantees towards critical points.

In this section, we leverage this ability to an algorithmic advantage by reducing our working space to the identified structure. “Smooth” structures (involving smooth submanifolds and smooth restrictions on it) are of special interest and open the way to Newton acceleration.

Let us start by specifying

Smoothness and localization of the proximal gradient

The results of this section are built on

Definition 3.1

(partial smoothness)
.

A function

\mathcal{C}^{2}
-)

relative to a set

(smoothness) the restriction of

to

\mathcal{C}^{2}

•

(regularity)

\bar{x}

•

(sharpness) the affine span of
\partial g(\bar{x})
is a translate of

(sub-continuity) the set-valued mapping

restricted to
\mathcal{M}
is continuous at

Under this assumption, we show in the next theorem that the proximal gradient smoothly locates active manifolds: if some input

is mapped onto
\mathcal{M}
, then the proximal gradient is
\mathcal{M}
-valued and
\mathcal{C}^{1}
around
\bar{y}
. This result is based on the sensitivity analysis of partly smooth functions
(

(

and
(

Theorem 3.1

(Proximal gradient points smoothly locate manifolds)

Let
f
be a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
g
a lower semi-continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose that
g
is both
r
-prox-regular at
\bar{x}
and partly-smooth relative to
\mathcal{M}
at
\bar{x}
.

Take
\gamma
,
\bar{\gamma}
such that
0<\gamma<\bar{\gamma}\leq 1/r
and
\bar{x}=\mathbf{prox}_{\bar{\gamma}g}(\bar{y}-\bar{\gamma}\nabla f(\bar{y}))
. If

i)

\frac{1}{\gamma}(\bar{y}-\bar{x})-\nabla f(\bar{y})\in\ri\partial g(\bar{x})
(the relative interior of the subdifferential at
\bar{x}
);

ii)

either a)
\gamma
is sufficiently close to
\bar{\gamma}
, or b)
\bar{y}
is sufficiently close to
\bar{x}
;

then, the proximal gradient
y\mapsto\mathbf{prox}_{\gamma g}(y-\gamma\nabla f(y))
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and
\mathcal{M}
-valued near
\bar{y}
.

Proof.

Adopting the same reasoning as in
(
lewis2002active
, Sec. 5)
and
(
daniilidis2006geometrical
, Sec. 4.1)
, we consider the function

\begin{array}[]{rl}\rho:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}&\to\mathbb{R}\\
(x,y)&\mapsto g(x)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|x-y+\gamma\nabla f(y)\|^{2},\end{array}

and denote by
\rho_{y}=\rho(\cdot,y)
. Computing the proximal gradient
\mathbf{prox}_{\gamma g}(y-\gamma\nabla f(y))
can then be seen as minimizing the parametrized function
\rho_{y}
.

Step 1.
As a first step, we study the minimizers of
\rho_{y}
restricted to
\mathcal{M}
, for
y
near
\bar{y}
. We consider the parametric manifold optimization problem, for
y
near
\bar{y}
:

\min_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\rho_{y}(x).

(
P_{\mathcal{M}}(y)
)

Since
g
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-partly-smooth relative to
\mathcal{M}
and
f
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\rho_{y}
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathcal{M}
. Moreover, the
r
-prox-regularity gives easily (see
Lemma

A.2
) that
\rho_{\bar{y}}
is lower-bounded by
(\frac{1}{\gamma}-r)\|\cdot-\bar{x}\|^{2}/2
on a neighborhood of
\bar{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and, a fortiori, in
\mathcal{M}
. From usual rationale (see e.g.

(
boumal2022intromanifolds
, Chap 4.2, 6.1)
), this implies

\operatorname{grad}\rho_{\bar{y}}(\bar{x})=0\qquad\operatorname{Hess}\rho_{\bar{y}}(\bar{x})\succeq\Big(\frac{1}{\gamma}-r\Big)I\succ 0,

which are the conditions to apply the implicit functions theorem, as follows.

We consider the equation
\Phi(x,y)=0
, for
x,y
near
\bar{x},\bar{y}
, where
\Phi:\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to T\mathcal{B}
is defined as

. This function is continuously differentiable on a neighborhood of
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, and its differential relative to
\bar{x}
at that point,
\operatorname{Hess}\rho_{\bar{y}}(\bar{x})
, is invertible. The implicit function theorem thus grants the existence of neighborhoods
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{x}}
,
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{y}}
of
\bar{x}
,
\bar{y}
in
\mathcal{M}

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a continuously differentiable function

such that, for any
y
in
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{y}}
,

. Actually,
\hat{x}(y)
is a strong minimizer of
\rho_{y}
on
\mathcal{M}
for
y
close enough to
\bar{y}
. Indeed, the mapping
\hat{x}
is continuous on

.

Step 2.
As a second step, we turn to show that the minimizer
\hat{x}(y)
of
\rho_{y}
on

is actually a strong critical point of
\rho_{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(
lewis2002active
, Def. 5.3)
, and thus the proximal gradient of point
y
. More precisely, we claim that, for
y
near
\bar{y}
and

, there holds
0\in\ri\partial\rho_{y}(x)
, that is

\frac{1}{\gamma}(y-x)-\nabla f(y)\in\ri\partial g(x).

This property holds at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
by assumption. By contradiction, assume there exist sequences of points
(y_{r})
with limit
\bar{y}
,
(x_{r})=(\hat{x}(y_{r}))
with limit
\bar{x}=\hat{x}(\bar{y})
and
(h_{r})
of unit norm
\|h_{r}\|=1
such that for all

separates 0 from

\inf_{h\in\partial\rho_{y_{r}}(x_{r})}\langle h_{r},h\rangle\geq 0.

Since
(h_{r})
is bounded, a converging subsequence can be extracted from it, let
\bar{h}
denote its limit. At the cost of renaming iterates, we assume that
\lim_{r\to\infty}h_{r}=\bar{h}
. The above property still holds at the limit

. Since

(x,y)\in\mathcal{N}_{\bar{x}}\times\mathcal{N}_{\bar{y}}\mapsto\partial\rho_{y}(x)=\partial g(x)+\frac{1}{\gamma}(x-y)

such that

and

. We have for all

\langle u_{r},h_{r}\rangle\geq 0
, which yields at the limit

separates

Conclusion.
We thus have a continuously differentiable function

defined on a neighborhood of

such that i)

\hat{x}(y)
is a strong minimizer of

on

This last point tells us that

. The characterization of proximity by the optimality condition (

) gives
that

\bar{y}
.

The following example shows that in the nonconvex setting, ii) and iii) do not necessarily imply i). Take

4.1
Ensuring functional descent while preserving local rates: line search

We use in the following convergence proofs three properties of
\mathrm{ManAcc}_{\mathcal{M}}
: it should produce an update that lives on
\mathcal{M}
, enjoy a superlinear local convergence rate, and not degrade function value. For this last point, we consider a simple line search and we prove that, under mild assumptions,
it helps to find a point which decreases function value, and retains the favorable local properties. Surprisingly, this result does not appear in the standard references on Riemannian optimization. We provide here the necessary developments inspired from the classical monograph
dennis1996numerical
.

Standing at point
x\in\mathcal{M}
with a proposed direction
\eta\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}
, a stepsize
\alpha>0
is
acceptable
if it satisfies the following
Armijo

F(\operatorname{R}_{x}(\alpha\eta))\leq F(x)+m_{1}\alpha\langle\operatorname{grad}F(x),\eta\rangle,\qquad\text{for $0<m_{1}<1/2$.}

(4.1)

The line search employs a second-order retraction
\operatorname{R}_{x}
, e.g. the exponential map, a projection retraction
Absil2012ProjectionlikeRO
, or any other second-order retraction
boumal2022intromanifolds
.

Indeed, in many applications of Riemannian optimization, computing geodesics and the exponential map can be costly and then retractions provide an efficient alternative. For this reason, we consider here second-order retractions

;
boumal2022intromanifolds
.
The conditions under which stepsizes satisfying the Armijo rule exist are discussed in
(
dennis1996numerical
, Sec 6.3)
, the following lemma can then be derived.

Lemma 4.1

Let
1

\mathcal{M}

Proof.

We adapt a part of the proof of

Since

is bounded below, there exists a smallest

∎

In addition, a line search performed near a minimizer with a Newton direction
should accept the unit stepsize, so that a full step may be taken. This is the case when the Riemannian Hessian around this minimizer is positive definite as stated by the next lemma, which is a direct corollary of

Let

This theorem states that alternating proximal gradient steps and Riemannian Newton steps converges quadratically to structured points with virtually the same assumptions the Euclidean Newton method.
However, the two standard issues of Newton’s method
still hold in our setting: at each iteration, a linear system has to be solved to produce the Newton direction; and this direction does not always provide descent (without positive definiteness of the Hessian).
We show in the next section that truncated versions overcome these issues also in our framework.

6
Concluding remarks

This paper proposes and studies a nonsmooth optimization algorithm
exploiting the underlying smooth geometry revealed by the proximal operator.
The method alternates between a proximal gradient step providing identification and a Riemann Newton acceleration providing superlinear convergence.
This algorithm has two special features: (
i
) it does not rely on prior knowledge of the final manifold, and (
ii
) its convergence is guaranteed in the (structured) nonconvex case.

Several extensions of this algorithm are possible; specifically, both building blocks can be refined: other Newton accelerations could be considered (e.g. trust-region
absil2007trust
, cubic regularization
agarwal2018adaptive
)
as well as other proximal algorithms (e.g. prox-Newton
lee2014proximal
, fast proximal gradient
beck2009fast
).
We focused here on the simplest Newton acceleration to highlight the ideas and the working horses of our approach.

r
prox-regular at
\bar{x}
for subgradient
\bar{v}\triangleq(\bar{y}-\bar{x})/\bar{\gamma}
.

Then, for any
\gamma\in(0,\min(1/r,\bar{\gamma}))
, there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{y}}
of
\bar{y}
over which
\mathbf{prox}_{\gamma g}
is single-valued and
(1-\gamma r)^{-1}
-Lipschitz continuous. Furthermore, there holds

x=\mathbf{prox}_{\gamma g}(y)\Leftrightarrow(y-x)/\gamma\in\partial g(x)

for
y\in\mathcal{N}_{\bar{y}}
and
x
near
\bar{x}
in the sense
\|x-\bar{x}\|<\varepsilon
,
|g(x)-g(\bar{x})|<\varepsilon
and
\|(y-x)/\gamma-\bar{v}\|<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

One can easily check that prox-regularity of
g
at
\bar{x}
for subgradient
\bar{v}
is equivalent to prox-regularity of function
\tilde{g}
around
0
for subgradient
0
, with
\tilde{g}=g(\cdot+\bar{x})-\langle\bar{v},\cdot\rangle-g(\bar{x})
and a change of variable

. Similarly,
\bar{x}=\mathbf{prox}_{\bar{\gamma}g}(\bar{y})
is characterized by its global optimality condition

g(x)+\frac{1}{2\bar{\gamma}}\|x-\bar{y}\|>g(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{2\bar{\gamma}}\|\bar{x}-\bar{y}\|^{2}\qquad\text{ for all }x\neq\bar{x},

which we may write as

g(x)>g(\bar{x})+\langle\bar{v},x-\bar{x}\rangle-\frac{1}{2\bar{\gamma}}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}\qquad\text{ for all }x\neq\bar{x}.

Under that same change of variables, since
\tilde{g}(0)=0
, this optimality condition rewrites as

\tilde{g}(\tilde{x})>-\frac{1}{2\bar{\gamma}}\|\tilde{x}\|^{2}\qquad\text{ for all }\tilde{x}\neq 0.

We may thus apply Theorem 4.4 from
poliquin1996prox
to get the claimed result on

, which transfers back to
g
as our change of function and variable is bijective. We thus obtain that for
\gamma\in(0,\min(1/r,\bar{\gamma}))
, on a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{y}}
of
\bar{y}
,

is single-valued,
(1-\gamma r)^{-1}
-Lipschitz continuous and
\mathbf{prox}_{\gamma g}(y)=[I+\gamma T]^{-1}(y)
, where
T
denotes the
g
-attentive
\varepsilon
-localization of
\partial g
at
\bar{x}
. Taking
y
near
\bar{y}
and
x
near
\bar{x}
such that
\|x-\bar{x}\|<\varepsilon
,
|g(x)-g(\bar{x})|<\varepsilon
and
\|(y-x)/\gamma-\bar{v}\|<\varepsilon
allows to identify the localization of
\partial g(x)
with
\partial g(x)
, so that

\frac{y-x}{\gamma}\in\partial g(x)\Leftrightarrow\frac{y-x}{\gamma}\in T(x)\Leftrightarrow(I+\gamma T)(x)=y\Leftrightarrow x=\mathbf{prox}_{\gamma g}(y).

Note that the proof of
(
poliquin1996prox
, Th. 4.4)
includes a minor error relative to the Lipschitz constant computation, we report here a corrected value.
∎

Now, we show that critical points of prox-regular functions are strong local minimizers; this result appears more or less explicitly in some articles, including

Lemma A.2

Let
f
and
g
denote two functions and
\bar{x}
,
\bar{y}
two points such that
f
is differentiable at
\bar{y}
and
g
is
r
-prox-regular at
\bar{x}
for subgradient
\frac{1}{\gamma}(\bar{y}-\bar{x})-\nabla f(\bar{y})\in\partial g(\bar{x})
with
\gamma\in(0,1/r)
.
Then, the function
\rho_{\bar{y}}:x\mapsto g(x)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\bar{y}-\gamma\nabla f(\bar{y})-x\|^{2}
satisfies

\rho_{\bar{y}}(x)\geq\rho_{\bar{y}}(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}-r\right)\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2},\qquad\text{for all $x$ near $\bar{x}$.}

Proof.

Prox-regularity of
g
at
\bar{x}
with subgradient
\frac{1}{\gamma}(\bar{y}-\gamma\nabla f(\bar{y})-\bar{x})\in\partial g(\bar{x})
writes

g(x)\geq g(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{\gamma}\langle\bar{y}-\gamma\nabla f(\bar{y})-\bar{x},x-\bar{x}\rangle-\frac{r}{2}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}.

The identity
2\langle b-a,c-a\rangle=\|b-a\|^{2}+\|c-a\|^{2}-\|b-c\|^{2}
applied to the previous scalar product yields:

g(x)\geq g(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\bar{y}-\gamma\nabla f(\bar{y})-\bar{x}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\bar{y}-\gamma\nabla f(\bar{y})-x\|^{2}-\frac{r}{2}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2},

which rewrites

\underbrace{g(x)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\bar{y}-\gamma\nabla f(\bar{y})-x\|^{2}}_{=\rho_{\bar{y}}(x)}\geq\underbrace{g(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\bar{y}-\gamma\nabla f(\bar{y})-\bar{x}\|^{2}}_{=\rho_{\bar{y}}(\bar{x})}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}-r\right)\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2},

which is the claimed inequality.
∎

B.1
Euclidean spaces and manifolds, back and forth

We establish here a connection between the Riemannian and the Euclidean distances.

Lemma B.1

Consider a point
\bar{x}
of a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
, equipped with a retraction
\operatorname{R}
such that
\operatorname{R}_{\bar{x}}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
\bar{x}
in
\mathcal{M}
such that

(1-\varepsilon){\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,\bar{x})\leq\|\operatorname{R}^{-1}_{\bar{x}}(x)\|\leq(1+\varepsilon){\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,\bar{x})\qquad\text{ for all $x\in\mathcal{U}$.}

where
\operatorname{R}^{-1}_{\bar{x}}:\mathcal{M}\to T_{\bar{x}}\mathcal{M}
is the smooth inverse of
\operatorname{R}_{\bar{x}}
defined locally around
\bar{x}
.

Proof.

The retraction at
\bar{x}
can be inverted locally around
0
. Indeed, as
\operatorname{D}\operatorname{R}_{\bar{x}}(0_{T_{\bar{x}}\mathcal{M}})=I
is invertible and
\operatorname{R}_{\bar{x}}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
, the implicit function theorem provides the existence of a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
inverse function
\operatorname{R}^{-1}_{\bar{x}}:\mathcal{M}\to T_{\bar{x}}\mathcal{M}
defined locally around
\bar{x}
. Furthermore, one shows by differentiating the relation
\operatorname{R}_{\bar{x}}\circ\operatorname{R}_{\bar{x}}^{-1}
that the differential of
\operatorname{R}^{-1}_{\bar{x}}
at
\bar{x}
is the identity.

We consider the function
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
f(x)=\|\log_{\bar{x}}(x)\|-\|\operatorname{R}_{\bar{x}}^{-1}(x)\|
. Clearly
f(\bar{x})=0
, and
\operatorname{D}f(\bar{x})=0
as the differentials of both
\operatorname{R}_{\bar{x}}^{-1}
and logarithm at
\bar{x}
are the identity. In local coordinates
\hat{x}=\log_{\bar{x}}{x}
around
\bar{x}
,
f
is represented by the function
\hat{f}=f\circ\exp_{\bar{x}}:T_{\bar{x}}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
. As
\hat{f}(\hat{\bar{x}})=0
,
\operatorname{D}\hat{f}(\hat{\bar{x}})=0
and
\hat{f}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
, there exists some
C>0
such that

-C\|\hat{x}-\hat{\bar{x}}\|^{2}\leq\hat{f}(\hat{x})\leq C\|\hat{x}-\hat{\bar{x}}\|^{2}\qquad\text{in a neighborhood $\hat{\mathcal{U}}$ of $\hat{\bar{x}}$,}.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, by taking a small enough neighborhood
\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\subset\hat{\mathcal{U}}
, there holds

-\varepsilon\|\hat{x}-\hat{\bar{x}}\|\leq\hat{f}(\hat{x})\leq\varepsilon\|\hat{x}-\hat{\bar{x}}\|.

Thus for all
x
in
\mathcal{U}=\operatorname{R}_{\bar{x}}(\hat{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime})
,

-\varepsilon\|\log_{\bar{x}}(x)\|\leq\|\log_{\bar{x}}(x)\|-\|\operatorname{R}_{\bar{x}}^{-1}(x)\|\leq\varepsilon\|\log_{\bar{x}}(x)\|,

as
\hat{x}=\log_{\bar{x}}(x)
,
\hat{\bar{x}}=0
. We conclude with
{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,\bar{x})=\|\hat{x}-\hat{\bar{x}}\|=\|\log_{\bar{x}}(x)\|
.
∎

We recall a slightly specialized version of
(
miller2005newton
, Th. 2.2)
, which is essentially the application of the implicit function theorem around a point of a manifold.

Proposition B.1

Consider a
p
-dimensional
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-submanifold
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
around a point
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{M}
. The mapping
\operatorname{R}:T\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{M}
, defined for
(x,\eta)\in T\mathcal{B}
near
(\bar{x},0)
by
\mathrm{proj}_{x}(\operatorname{R}(x,\eta))=\eta
defines a second-order retraction near
(\bar{x},0)
. The point-wise retraction, defined as
\operatorname{R}_{x}=\operatorname{R}(x,\cdot)
, is locally invertible with inverse
\operatorname{R}_{x}^{-1}=\mathrm{proj}_{x}
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-p}
denote a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
function defining
\mathcal{M}
around
\bar{x}
: for all
x
close enough to
\bar{x}
, there holds
x\in\mathcal{M}\Leftrightarrow\Psi(x)=0
, and
\operatorname{D}\Psi(x)
is surjective. Consider the equation
\Phi(x,\eta_{t},\eta_{n})=0
around
(\bar{x},0,0)
, with

\begin{array}[]{rl}\Phi:\left\{x,\eta_{t},\eta_{n}:x\in\mathcal{M},\eta_{t}\in T_{x}\mathcal{M},\eta_{n}\in N_{x}\mathcal{M}\right\}&\to\mathbb{R}\\
x,\eta_{t},\eta_{n}&\mapsto\Psi(x+\eta_{t}+\eta_{n}).\end{array}

The partial differential
\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{n}}\Phi(\bar{x},0,0)
is, for
\xi_{n}\in N_{\bar{x}}\mathcal{M}
,

\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{n}}\Phi(\bar{x},0,0)[\xi_{n}]=\operatorname{D}\Psi(\bar{x})[\xi_{n}].

Since
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{M}
,
\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{n}}\Phi(\bar{x},0,0)
is surjective from
N_{\bar{x}}\mathcal{M}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n-p}
so its a bijection. The implicit function theorem provides the existence of neighborhoods
\mathcal{N}_{\bar{x}}^{1}\subset\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}\subset\cup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}T_{x}\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}_{0}^{3}\subset\cup_{x\in\mathcal{M}}N_{x}\mathcal{M}
and a unique
\mathcal{C}^{k}
function
\eta_{n}:\mathcal{N}_{\bar{x}}^{1}\times\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}\to\mathcal{N}_{0}^{3}
such that, for all
x\in\mathcal{N}_{\bar{x}}^{1}
,
\eta_{t}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}
and
\eta_{n}\in\mathcal{N}_{0}^{3}
,
\eta_{n}(\bar{x},0)=0
and

\Phi(x,\eta_{t},\eta_{n}(x,\eta_{t}))=0\Leftrightarrow x+\eta_{t}+\eta_{n}(x,\eta_{t})\in\mathcal{M}.

It also provides an expression for the partial derivative of
\eta_{n}
at
(x,0)
along
\eta_{t}
: for
\xi_{t}\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}
,

\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{t}}\eta_{n}(x,0)[\xi_{t}]=-\left[\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{n}}\Phi(x,0,0)\right]^{-1}\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{t}}\Phi(x,0,0)[\xi_{t}].

As noted before,
\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{n}}\Phi(x,0,0)
is bijective since
x\in\mathcal{M}
. Besides,
\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{t}}\Phi(x,0,0)=\operatorname{D}\Phi(x)[\xi_{t}]=0
since
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
identifies as the kernel of
\operatorname{D}\Phi(x)
. Thus
\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{t}}\eta_{n}(x,0)=0
.

Now, define a map
\operatorname{R}:\mathcal{N}_{\bar{x}}^{1}\times\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}\to\mathcal{M}
by
\operatorname{R}(x,\eta_{t})=x+\eta_{t}+\eta_{n}(x,\eta_{t})
. This map has degree of smoothness
\mathcal{C}^{k}
since
\eta_{n}
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
, satisfies
\operatorname{R}(x,0)=x
since
\eta_{n}(x,0)=0
and satisfies
\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{t}}\eta_{n}(x,0)=I+\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{t}}(x,0)=I
. Thus
\operatorname{R}
defines a retraction on a neighborhood of
(\bar{x},0)
.

We turn to show the second-order property of
\operatorname{R}
. Consider the smooth curve
c
defined as
c(t)=\operatorname{R}(x,t\eta)
for some
x\in\mathcal{N}_{\bar{x}}^{1}
,
\eta_{t}\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}\cap\mathcal{N}_{0}^{2}
. It’s first derivative writes

c^{\prime}(t)=\eta+\operatorname{D}_{\eta_{t}}\eta_{n}(x,t\eta)[\eta]=\eta.

The acceleration of the curve
c
is obtained by computing the derivative of
c^{\prime}(\cdot)
in the ambient space and then projecting onto
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
. Thus
c^{\prime\prime}(t)=0
and in particular,
c^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
which makes
\operatorname{R}
a second-order retraction.
∎

Theorem B.1

(Soundness of the Riemannian line search)

Consider a manifold
\mathcal{M}
equipped with a retraction
\operatorname{R}
and a twice differentiable function
F:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
that admits a strong local minimizer
x^{\star}
, that is, a point such that
\operatorname{Hess}F(x^{\star})
is positive definite. If
x
is close to
x^{\star}
,
\eta
brings a superlinear improvement towards
x^{\star}
, that is
{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(\operatorname{R}_{x}(\eta),x^{\star})=o({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star}))
as
x\to x^{\star}
, and
0<m_{1}<1/2
, then
\eta
is acceptable by the Armijo rule (
4.1
) with unit stepsize
\alpha=1
.

Proof.

Let
x,\eta\in T\mathcal{B}
denote a pair such that
x
is close to
x^{\star}
and
{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(\operatorname{R}_{x}(\eta),x^{\star})=o({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star}))
. For convenience, let
x_{+}=\operatorname{R}_{x}(\eta)
denote the next point.

Following
absil2009optimization
(see e.g. the proof of Th. 6.3.2), we work in local coordinates around
x^{\star}
, representing any point
x\in\mathcal{M}
by
\widehat{x}=\log_{x^{\star}}(x)
and any tangent vector
\eta\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}
by
\widehat{\eta}_{x}=\operatorname{D}\log_{x^{\star}}(x)[\eta]
. The function
F
is represented by
\widehat{F}=F\circ\exp_{x^{\star}}:T_{x^{\star}}\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
. Defining the coordinates via the logarithm grants the useful property that the Riemannian distance of any two points
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
matches the euclidean distance between their representatives:
{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y)=\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{y}\|
. Besides, there holds

\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]=\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})[\widehat{\eta}]\quad\text{ and }\quad\operatorname{Hess}F(x)[\eta,\eta]=\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})[\widehat{\eta},\widehat{\eta}].

(B.1)

Indeed,
\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]=(F\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(0)
and
\operatorname{Hess}F(x)[\eta,\eta]=(F\circ\gamma)^{\prime\prime}(0)
, where
\gamma
denotes the geodesic curve defined by
\widehat{\gamma}(t)=\widehat{x}+t\widehat{\eta}
. Using
F\circ\gamma=\widehat{F}\circ\widehat{\gamma}
, one obtains the result.

Step 1.
We derive an approximation of
\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]=\langle\operatorname{grad}F(x),\eta\rangle
in terms of
\operatorname{D}^{2}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})[\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]^{2}
. To do so, we go through the intermediate quantity
\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})[\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x}]
, and handle precisely the
o(\cdot)
terms. By smoothness of
\widehat{F}
and since
\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})=0
, Taylor’s formula for
\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}
writes

\displaystyle\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})[\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{D}^{2}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})[\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x},\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]+o(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{D}^{2}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})[\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]^{2}+\operatorname{D}^{2}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})[\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x^{\star}},\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]+o(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{D}^{2}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})[\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]^{2}+o(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|^{2}),

where, in the last step, we used that
\|\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|=o(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|)
to get that
\|\operatorname{D}^{2}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})[\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x^{\star}},\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]\|=\|\operatorname{D}^{2}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})\|\|\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|=o(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|^{2})
.
We now turn to show that
\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})[\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x}]
behaves as
\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]
up to
o(\|\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x}\|^{2})
. Since
\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]=\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})[\widehat{\eta}]
by (
B.1
), there holds:

\|\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]-\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})[\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x}]\|=\|\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})[\widehat{\eta}-(\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x})]\|\leq\|\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})\|\|\widehat{\eta}-(\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x})\|.

As
F
is twice differentiable and
\exp
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
,
\widehat{F}
is twice differentiable as well. In particular its derivative is locally Lipschitz continuous, so that for
\widehat{x}
near
\widehat{x^{\star}}
, we obtain a first estimate:

\|\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})\|=\|\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})-\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})\|=\mathcal{O}(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|).

Besides, the following estimate holds
\|\widehat{\eta}-(\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x})\|=o(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|)
. Indeed, as the function
\log_{x^{\star}}\circ\operatorname{R}_{x}:T_{x}\mathcal{M}\to T_{x^{\star}}\mathcal{M}
is differentiable, there holds for
\eta\in T_{x}\mathcal{M}
small,

\log_{x^{\star}}(\operatorname{R}_{x}(\eta))=\log_{x^{\star}}(\operatorname{R}_{x}(0))+\operatorname{D}\log_{x^{\star}}(\operatorname{R}_{x}(0))[\operatorname{D}\operatorname{R}_{x}(0)[\eta]]+o(\|\eta\|),

which simplifies to
\widehat{x_{+}}=\widehat{x}+\widehat{\eta}+o(\|\eta\|)
.
Lemma

B.1
allows to write
\|\eta\|=\|\operatorname{R}_{x}^{-1}(x_{+})\|=\mathcal{O}({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x_{+}))
. Using the triangular inequality and the assumption that
{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{+},x^{\star})=o({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{*}))
we get

{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x_{+})\leq{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})+{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x^{\star},x_{+})=\mathcal{O}({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{*}))=\mathcal{O}(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|),

so that the second estimate holds.

Combining the two above estimates allows to conclude that

\|\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]-\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})[\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x}]\|=o(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|^{2}),

so that overall,

\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]=\operatorname{D}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x})[\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x}]+o(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|^{2})=-\operatorname{D}^{2}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})[\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]^{2}+o(\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|^{2}).

Using that
\|\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}\|={\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})
and
\operatorname{D}^{2}\widehat{F}(\widehat{x^{\star}})=\operatorname{Hess}F(x^{\star})
(
B.1
), we obtain

\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]=-\operatorname{Hess}F(x^{\star})[\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]^{2}+o({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})^{2}).

(B.2)

Step 2.
The function
F
admits a second-order development around
x^{\star}
: applying
Eq.

2.3
with the exponential map
\exp_{x^{\star}}
as a second-order retraction yields

F(x)=F(x^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Hess}F(x^{\star})[\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]^{2}+o({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})^{2}),

(B.3)

where we used that
{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})=\|\log_{x^{\star}}(x)-\log_{x^{\star}}(x^{\star})\|
. Denote
0<l\leq L
the lower and upper eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Hess}F(x^{\star})
. The combination
\eqref{eq:proofunitstepsize_fdev}+m_{1}\eqref{eq:proofunitstepsize_gradhess}
writes

\displaystyle F(x)+m_{1}\operatorname{D}F(x)[\eta]

\displaystyle=F(x^{\star})+(\frac{1}{2}-m_{1})\operatorname{Hess}F(x^{\star})[\widehat{x}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]^{2}+o({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})^{2})

\displaystyle\geq F(x^{\star})+(\frac{1}{2}-m_{1})l{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})^{2}+o({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})^{2}),

Let
\varepsilon>0
such that

. As

, for
x
close enough to
x^{\star}
there holds
{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{+},x^{\star})\leq\varepsilon{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})
. Combining this with the second-order development of

at
x_{+}
, there holds:

\displaystyle F(x_{+})

\displaystyle=F(x^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Hess}F(x^{\star})[\widehat{x_{+}}-\widehat{x^{\star}}]^{2}+o({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{+},x^{\star})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq F(x^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}L{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{+},x^{\star})^{2}+o({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{+},x^{\star})^{2})

\displaystyle\leq F(x^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}L\varepsilon^{2}{\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})^{2}+o({\dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x^{\star})^{2}).

Subtracting the two estimates yields

which ensures that the Armijo condition is satisfied.

∎
