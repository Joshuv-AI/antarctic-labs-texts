Statistical learning on randomized data to verify quantum state k k -designs
Lorenzo Versini, Karim K. Alaa El-Din, Florian Mintert, Rick Mukherjee

Abstract
Random ensembles of pure states have proven to be extremely important in various aspects of quantum physics such as benchmarking the performance of quantum circuits, testing for quantum advantage, providing novel insights for many-body thermalization and studying the black hole information paradox. Although generating a fully random ensemble is experimentally challenging, approximations of it are just as useful and are known to emerge naturally in a variety of physical models, including Rydberg setups. These are referred to as approximate quantum state designs, and verifying their degree of randomness can be an expensive task, similar to performing full quantum state tomography on many-body systems. In this theoretical work, we efficiently validate the character of approximate quantum designs with respect to data size acquisition when compared to the conventional frequentist approach. This is achieved by translating the information residing in the complex many-body state into a succinct representation of classical data using a random projective measurement basis, which is then processed using methods of statistical inference such as maximum likelihood estimation and neural networks and benchmarked against the predictions of shadow tomography. Our scheme of combining machine learning methods for postprocessing the data obtained from randomized measurements for efficient characterisation of (approximate) quantum state k k designs is applicable to any noisy quantum platform that can generate quantum designs.

2
Theory

2.1
Constructing approximate quantum state
k
-design using projected ensembles

A uniform distribution of all pure states in a Hilbert space is defined by the Haar ensemble
[
16
]
. Averaging arbitrary functions across the ensemble is impossible in practicality, due to the finite number of samples which are collected. Instead, it is possible to use quantum state designs that reproduce the universal properties of randomness of the Haar ensemble up to a certain degree. However, even these are usually constructed approximately using random unitary quantum circuits
[
30
,
45
,
32
]
, a task which requires sufficient depth of the quantum circuit and which scales polynomially with the number of qubits and adds up to the infidelity of the operation.

Another approach to generating approximate state designs involves making local measurements on many-body states that evolve under a time-dependent chaotic Hamiltonian
[
29
,
28
]
. This approach of constructing approximate
k
-designs, which is also referred to as the
projected ensemble method
, has the benefit of being generic in its implementation, in the sense that it can be realized in experiments that simulate Ising models
[
28
]
. The type of system that we consider here consists of
N
spins, which are partitioned into a subsystem with size
N_{A}
, and the remaining
N_{B}=N-N_{A}
spins are treated as bath such that
N_{A}\ll N_{B}
. As long as the size of A is smaller than that of B, a reasonably low number of spins can effectively generate approximate quantum state
k
-designs. In this work, we use this approach to generate the approximate quantum state
k
-designs which will later be characterized using statistical learning methods.

Using the projective measurement approach, an ensemble of pure states is obtained by performing local measurements on the bath (B) and considering the states projected in the complimentary subsystem A conditional on the measured outcome in B. The naive brute-force approach to characterise the ensemble of states would be to collect measurements in all directions (
X,Y,Z
), both for
A
and
B
, in order to reconstruct the full many-body state which contains the information about the entire system. However, this procedure is expensive, especially for larger systems. Alternatively, one may measure both the system and the bath, solely in the computational or
Z
basis as was done in the original experiment
[
28
]
. Measuring the entire system in the computational basis results in the loss of useful information (such as phase), which could have been used for characterizing the ensemble of pure states. Instead, we propose to measure the state of the subsystem
A
in either of the
X
,
Y
or
Z
basis, which is chosen randomly for each spin, and the state of the bath
B
in the
Z
basis. This measurement scheme results in bitstrings
r_{A}
and
z_{B}
for subsystem
A
and bath
B
respectively. Each bitstring contains a string of bits 0 or 1 representing the state of the individual spin when measured in the chosen basis. More details about the measurement scheme are provided in Methods. The scheme of measurements used in A is referred to as
random basis measurement
as depicted in Fig.
7
. The many-body state of the full system is denoted by
|\Psi\rangle
and the state of sub-system
A
conditioned that a particular bitstring
z_{B}
has been measured is given by

|\Psi_{A}(z_{B})\rangle=(\mathbb{1}_{A}\otimes\langle z_{B}|)|\Psi\rangle/\sqrt{p(z_{B})},

(1)

where
p(z_{B})=\langle\Psi|(\mathbb{1}_{A}\otimes|z_{B}\rangle\langle z_{B}|)|\Psi\rangle
is the probability to measure a specific bitstring
z_{B}
. The marginal probability of measuring the sub-system
A
in a particular state
r_{A}
for a given choice of measuring basis is given by

\displaystyle p(r_{A})

\displaystyle=\sum_{z_{B}}p(z_{B})p(r_{A}|z_{B})

\displaystyle=\sum_{z_{B}}p(z_{B})|\langle r_{A}|\Psi_{A}(z_{B})\rangle|^{2}

(2)

where
p(r_{A}|z_{B})
is the conditional probability distribution. Thus, the ensemble of states is a collection of all such conditional states of sub-system
A
along with its corresponding probabilities to measure a given bitstring
z_{B}
and is written as

\mathcal{E}=\{(|\Psi_{A}(z_{B})\rangle,p(z_{B}))\}\qquad\forall\ z_{B}\in[0,2^{N_{B}}).

(3)

The
k^{\text{th}}
moment of the ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is defined as follows

\rho^{(k)}_{\mathcal{E}}=\sum_{z_{B}}p(z_{B})\ (|\Psi_{A}(z_{B})\rangle\langle\Psi_{A}(z_{B})|)^{\otimes k},

(4)

while the
k^{\text{th}}
moment of the Haar ensemble for a Hilbert space of dimensionality
d
is defined as
\rho^{(k)}_{Haar}=\int_{\psi\sim Haar(d)}d\psi(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)^{\otimes k}
, which has a closed analytical form as detailed in
A
. The degree of closeness between
\mathcal{E}
and the Haar ensemble is quantified in the trace distance between the two ensembles which is defined as

\delta_{(k)}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\left(\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{(k)}-\rho_{Haar}^{(k)}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{(k)}-\rho_{Haar}^{(k)}\right)}\right).

(5)

Thus, for sufficiently small
\delta_{(k)}
, using the many-body state
|\Psi\rangle
, an ensemble of pure states on the subsystem is obtained whose probability distribution is indistinguishable from the Haar ensemble up to
k
-th moment, thereby forming an approximate quantum state
k
-design. The efficient and accurate characterization of (approximate) quantum state
k
-designs which is related to estimating the value of
\delta_{(k)}
depends on the type of information encoded in the ensemble of states
\mathcal{E}
. This in turn relies on the actual details of the measurement protocol. In this work, we use four different protocols to characterise quantum designs: (i) frequentist method (ii) shadow tomography (iii) complex-restricted Boltzmann machine (cRBM) and (iv) maximum likelihood (maxLK) estimation of which the latter two prove to be efficient with respect to data acquisition. The underlying theory of these three protocols and their implementation for this work are described in
C
.

2.2
Generating random ensemble states with Rydberg simulator

Rydberg atoms are neutral atoms with their valence electron placed in a highly excited state possessing large dipole moments. The characteristic strong dipole-dipole interaction between neighbouring Rydberg atoms has been exploited for a wide variety of applications that include many-body physics
[
46
]
, quantum simulation
[
47
]
and quantum computing
[
48
]
. In this work, a linear chain of trapped alkaline-earth atoms is considered, which are initialized to a state with all atoms in their ground state
|g\rangle
. The laser with detuning
\Delta
and Rabi frequency
\Omega
excites the individual atoms to their Rydberg state, denoted by
|e\rangle
. The system evolves according to the time-independent Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}-\hbar\Delta\sum^{N}_{i=1}\hat{n}_{i}+\frac{C_{6}}{a^{6}}\sum_{j>i}\frac{\hat{n}_{i}\hat{n}_{j}}{|i-j|^{6}},

(6)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}=|g\rangle_{i}\langle e|_{i}+|e\rangle_{i}\langle g|_{i}
and
\hat{n}_{i}=|e\rangle_{i}\langle e|_{i}
. Two atoms in their Rydberg states interact with each other via the van der Waals interaction, whose strength is given by the van der Waal coefficient
C_{6}
, which is the intrinsic property of the atom. Simultaneous excitations of nearest neighbouring atoms to their Rydberg states are suppressed due to the Rydberg blockade mechanism. This effect allows us to work with the reduced Hilbert space, which makes the numerical simulations more tractable.

For the numerical simulation, the entire system with
N
atoms is partitioned into subsystem
A
and bath
B
with sizes
N_{A}
and
N_{B}=N-N_{A}
respectively, while maintaining
N_{A}\ll N_{B}
as shown in Fig.
2
.

3
Results

An exemplary many-body system that undergoes chaotic dynamics and generates approximate quantum
k
-designs is a linear chain of interacting Rydberg atoms
[
28
]
. A numerical simulation of the Rydberg Hamiltonian dynamics provides the final many-body state on which projective measurements are performed to construct the ensemble of states. Results presented here are for system size
N=10
with sub-system size
N_{A}=2
and have been verified for different subsystem sizes.

A system of interacting Rydberg atoms can naturally evolve from a highly ordered initial state consisting of all atoms in their ground state to a chaotic many-body state at later times. Whether this final chaotic state can provide a projective ensemble of states that statistically represent a quantum state
k
-design is addressed in two different ways. Firstly, a random ensemble of states is known to possess certain universal statistical properties such as the steady state behaviour of the marginal probability distributions
p(r_{A}=z_{A})
(see Eq.
2.1
but now in
Z
basis) at long times
[
29
]
. This is ascertained in Fig.
3
which shows the expected convergence of higher moments of the conditional probability distributions, which are defined as
\sum_{z_{B}}p(z_{B})p(z_{A}=00|z_{B})^{k}
to values
k!/(D_{A}\times(D_{A}+1)\ldots(D_{A}+k-1))
at long times.
D_{A}
is the dimensionality of the system Hilbert space taken into consideration. For a sub-system of two qubits, the blockade interaction prohibits Rydberg excitation next to each other, giving
D_{A}=3
. Thus, in Fig.
3
(a), we find that the mean (which is also the first moment) of the conditional probability
p(z_{A}=00|z_{B})
approaches the value
1/3
. In Fig.
3
(b), the higher order moments are shown, which approach their respective values of
k!
when re-scaled by their respective factor (
D_{A}\times(D_{A}+1)\ldots(D_{A}+k-1)
). Although Figs
3
(a-b) were done for measurements in
Z
basis, the saturation of conditional probabilities at long times holds true independent of the basis chosen for projective measurements. The saturation of conditional probabilities with projective measurements in the
Z
basis has been verified in the recent experiment
[
28
]
and is the first signature that the ensemble of states is approaching a Haar-random distribution.

However, a more direct approach for verifying that a certain ensemble state is an approximate quantum
k
-design is to show the (almost) vanishing value of the trace distance
\delta_{(k)}
which is defined in Eq.

Following this up with efficient post-processing of the classical data with methods such as maximum likelihood optimization or RBM can potentially allow future experiments to estimate trace distances between the fitted many-body wave function and Haar-random distribution. The results of our approach are compared against the predictions of shadow tomography.

4
Discussion

The complexity of large quantum systems makes them difficult to characterize accurately and full state tomography is inefficient as it requires an exponentially large number of experimental runs which also amounts to exponentially large amount of data to process. Improving the efficiency of quantum state tomography, in particular for large systems is a constant challenge and an active area of research
[
37
,
53
]
. Characterising the randomness of an ensemble of states is no exception to this plight. Full characterization of a quantum state density matrix in a Hilbert space of dimensionality
D
necessitates at least the preparation of
O(D^{2})
independent copies of the system
[
43
,
54
]
. However, the Hilbert space of
N
interacting spins such as that of the Rydberg setup has dimensionality
D=2^{N}
, in which case the full characterization of a quantum state density matrix
\rho
will require
O(D^{2})=O(2^{2N})
independent copies of the system, which is still an exhaustively large number of copies.

Often a far less complete description of the state is adequate in certain cases such that the number of experimental runs and the amount of data needed is drastically reduced. The key idea is to translate the information about the many-body state of a system into classical data by performing measurements in the random bases. At the expense of losing certain information, the hope is that the relevant features of the quantum state can still be estimated. In this regard, classical shadow tomography has a relatively reduced scaling to
O(\log^{4}(M)\log(D))

[
55
]
where
M
are the two-outcome measurements
[
55
]
. Randomized basis measurements can reduce this scaling further to
O(\log(M))
independent copies
[
40
,
56
]
and is at the heart of the modern classical shadow methods
[
40
]
. However, depending on the many-body quantum state that is being characterised, relevant information can be lost leading to an inaccurate description of the quantum state. In such cases, statistical learning methods can be used to make accurate and, in certain cases, more efficient predictions of the quantum state, as was found in this work on characterisation of emergent random ensembles of pure states.

This work uniquely accomplishes the efficient characterization of ensembles of random states by combining the benefits of random basis state measurements and statistical learning methods. The ability to identify information in reduced data sets that would otherwise be missed by naive observations is one of the appealing features of this kind of learning
[
57

. In contrast to tomographic methods, our scheme requires a significantly lower number of measurements and it is applicable to a broad class of quantum simulators that realize spin models. While both methods, maxLK and cRBM do make accurate estimations, the current implementations of cRBM are computationally more time-consuming by orders of magnitude when compared to the maxLK approach. This may change in the future with better and more flexible versions of cRBM routines. Future works can involve noise modelling that takes into account imperfections in measurements, perhaps using Bayesian methods
[

60

and could make comparisons between the approach used in this work with other similar methods such as shadow estimation
[

Appendix B
Simulating measurement data

Repeated measurements are taken to obtain statistics by randomly sampling the measurement directions between the three mutually orthogonal directions. For spin systems, and in particular for Rydberg systems, this can be experimentally implemented by randomly rotating each spin using a unitary operator followed by a state measurement in the z basis
[
24
]
. The data set
\mathcal{D}
of size
||\mathcal{D}||
is constructed by measuring the bath
B
in the
z
basis and the system
A
in all bases. It is subsequently split into subsets that share the same outcome
z_{B}
of the bath measurement, which we label as
\mathcal{D}(z_{B})
. Each
\mathcal{D}(z_{B})
contains multiple simulated measurements of subsystem
A
for a particular
z_{B}
as shown in Fig.
7
. The
i
th measurement in
A
is denoted by
|r_{A}^{(i)}(z_{B})\rangle_{\mathbf{b}}=|b_{A,1}^{(i)}\rangle\otimes\ldots\otimes|b_{A,N_{A}}^{(i)}\rangle
where
|b_{A,j}^{(i)}\rangle\in|\{0,1\}\rangle_{\{X,Y,Z\}}
are the eigenstates of the
\sigma_{b_{j}}
Pauli operator with eigenvalue
\pm 1
. It should be noted that the amount of data available to train the state
|\Psi(z_{B})\rangle
for a particular
z_{B}
fluctuates for each run of numerically simulated measurements. In general,
z_{B}
bitstrings with a higher probability
p(z_{b})
are more likely to be sampled and the size of the corresponding dataset
\mathcal{D}(z_{B})
will be on average larger. This also implies that subsets
\mathcal{D}(z_{B})
with low
p(z_{b})
may have measurements for
A
that are missing in certain directions. For example, in the illustrative set of measurements provided in Fig.
7
, the dataset
\mathcal{D}(10010101)
contains measurements of sub-system

in the basis
\{ZX,YZ,YX,ZY\}
for a given outcome
z_{B}
. Here the first spin for
A
was never measured in the
X
basis which may then affect the quality of the training. However, the states with particularly low
p(z_{B})
do not contribute significantly to the
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{(k)}
thereby reducing the impact of inaccurate state characterisation due to the limited data.

Appendix C
Estimators for random ensemble state

For every outcome
z_{B}
in the full dataset, we use
\mathcal{D}(z_{B})
to fit a state
|\Psi_{A}(z_{B})\rangle
for sub-system
A
which is achieved by optimizing the likelihood of the collected samples. We do this by using three different ansatzes for the state
|\Psi_{A}(z_{B})\rangle
: a state function parametrised with a complex restricted Boltzmann machine, a maximum likelihood approach where the coefficients of
|\Psi(z_{B})\rangle
are directly optimised and a density operator reconstructed with shadow tomography. Using a suitable estimator of our choice, we then estimate the state for
A
for each such outcome
z_{B}
. Since an estimator is used for each outcome individually, an
ensemble
of estimators is applied to reconstruct
\mathcal{E}
. This is better than using one estimator to predict the full state in one run, whose data representation has a much higher complexity than the individual outcome. Thus, the methods used for estimation are referred to as “ensemble estimators”.

is measured only in the

C.1
Complex restricted Boltzmann machines

A restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) is a type of bipartite neural network with two layers which are referred to as visible and hidden layers, as shown in Fig.
9
. It is used to learn the probability distribution over binary data in an unsupervised approach. The network shows no intra-layer connections but just interlayer ones, thus motivating the name
restricted
. The nodes in the visible and hidden layers have bias vectors
\mathbf{b}
and
\mathbf{c}
associated to them, whereas a matrix
\mathbf{W}
provides weights for the connections. The nodes
z_{i}
and
h_{j}
can only take binary values 0 or 1 and are thus used to represent a probability distribution
p(\mathbf{z})
, where
\mathbf{z}
is a vector of
N
binary entries
\{0,1\}
i.e.
\mathbf{z}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
. The state of the RBM is completely defined using the two vectors:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=(z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{V})\qquad

\displaystyle z_{i}\in\{0,1\}

(13a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}

\displaystyle=(h_{1},h_{2},...,h_{H})\qquad

\displaystyle h_{j}\in\{0,1\}

(13b)

where
V
and
H
are the total numbers of nodes in the visible and hidden layers respectively. Each state of the RBM has an associated energy given by

E_{\theta}(\bf{z},\bf{h})=-\bf{b}^{T}\bf{z}-\bf{c}^{T}\bf{h}-\bf{z}^{T}{W\bf h},

(14)

which is parametrized by the weights and biases of the model
\theta=\{\bf b,\bf c,W\}
. The probability distribution
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{h})
(chosen to be a Boltzmann distribution) and the partition function
Z_{\theta}(\bf z,\bf h)
associated to the state energy are given as

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{h})=\frac{1}{Z_{\theta}}e^{-E_{\theta}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{h})},

(15)

\displaystyle Z_{\theta}=\sum_{\mathbf{z}}\sum_{\bf h}e^{-E_{\theta}(\bf z,\bf h)}.

(16)

In our work, we are interested in the probability
p_{\theta}(\bf z)
, which is the marginal of
p_{\theta}(\bf z,\bf h)
and has the following analytical expression,

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\bf{z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bf h^{\prime}}p_{\theta}(\bf z,\bf h^{\prime})=\frac{1}{Z_{\theta}}e^{-\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(\bf z)},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(\bf{z})

\displaystyle=-\bf{b}^{T}\bf{z}-\sum_{j=1}^{H}\ln\left[1+\exp\left(c_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{V}W_{ij}z_{i}\right)\right].

(18)

Using this formalism, a particular state
\bm{z}={010...1}
is associated with a probability
p_{\theta}(z)
that is parametrized by
\theta=\{\bf b,\bf c,W\}
. The goal is to find the optimal parameters
\theta
that give the approximation
p_{\theta}(\bf z)\approx q(\bf z)
given some data

\mathcal{D}=[z^{(1)},z^{(2)},\dots,z^{(D)}],\qquad\mathrm{where}\ z^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}^{V}

(19)

sampled according to the target distribution
q(\bf z)
which the data are sampled from. This is referred to as training of the RBM with respect to the data.

Figure 9:
Network diagram of RBM. The network is divided into a hidden and visible layer, each one consisting of nodes
h
and
z
that take binary values 0 and 1. RBM is parametrised by
\theta
which groups the weights associated to the interlayer connections and biases associated to the hidden and visible nodes. An energy
E_{\theta}(\bf\,\bf h)
can be calculated for each network configuration. The energy allows defining a probability
p_{\theta}(\bf\,\bf h)
that is inspired by the Boltzmann distribution.
Z_{\theta}
is the normalizing partition function.

Training the RBM with data:
The commonly used cost function that needs to be minimized while training the RBM is the Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
63
,
64
]
which is defined as

C_{\theta}=\sum_{z}q(z)\log{\frac{q(z)}{p_{\theta}(z)}}=\sum_{z}q(z)\log{q(z)}-q(z)\log{p_{\theta}}(z),

(20)

where the term
\sum_{z}q(z)\log{p_{\theta}(z)}=\langle\log{p_{\theta}}\rangle_{q}
is the averaged value of
\log{p_{\theta}(z)}
with respect to
q(z)
. Given the function
C_{\theta}
, the parameters are updated to minimise this using the following rule:

\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla_{\theta}C_{\theta}

(21)

Here
\eta
is the
learning rate
, and it affects the step size of the parameter update. The dependence of the cost function on the parameters is captured by the following expression
[
63
,
64
]
,

\nabla_{\theta}C_{\theta}\approx\langle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}-\langle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\rangle_{p_{\theta}}.

(22)

The first term is often called the
positive phase
, and it represents the average of the gradient of
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
(cfr.
17
) evaluated at the values of
z
in the dataset
\mathcal{D}
. The second term is the
negative phase
, and it represents the average of the gradient of
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
taken over the learned probability distribution
p_{\theta}(z)
. This last term can be computationally expensive to evaluate during training. Indeed, the expression for
p_{\theta}(z)
involves the calculation of the partition function
Z_{\theta}
in
16
and is a task that scales like
\mathcal{O}(2^{H}\times 2^{V})
, which becomes intractable for large systems. The standard approach to calculate the value
\langle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{E}_{\theta}\rangle_{p_{\theta}}
is using an algorithm called
contrastive divergence
. This algorithm allows to sample
z
bitstrings according to
p_{\theta}
without calculating the partition function of the system explicitly. A detailed explanation of the optimization algorithm is out of the scope of this work, but it can be found in
[
63
,
64
]
. For a system size of
2
qubits, the wavefunction is described as

|\Psi\rangle=\psi_{00}|00\rangle+\psi_{01}|01\rangle+\psi_{10}|10\rangle+\psi_{11}|11\rangle

(23)

and following Born’s rule, the probability distribution is given by
p(z)=|\psi_{z}|^{2}
. This provides the data that will be used to train the RBM, which in turn implies optimizing the parameters
\theta
such that the probability
p_{\theta}(z)\approx p(z)
. Wavefunctions can be reconstructed using positive real-valued coefficients
\psi_{z,\theta}:=\sqrt{p_{\theta}(z)}
, but this approach has the disadvantage that phase information is discarded. A more general approach suggested by Torlai and Melko
[
65
]
is to consider two RBMs, one that describes the amplitude of the coefficients
\psi_{z}
and the other that describes the phase
\phi(z)
of the coefficients
\psi_{z}
. The two RBMs are parametrized with their own weights and biases separately, which are labelled as
\theta
and
\mu
. They both have associated effective energies
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(z)
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(z)
described by Eq. (
17
). The coefficients of the wavefunction are now parametrized as:

\displaystyle\psi_{z,\theta\mu}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{\theta}(z)}\ e^{i\phi_{\mu}(z)}=Z_{\theta}^{-1/2}\ e^{-\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(z)/2}\ e^{-i\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(z)/2}

\displaystyle=Z_{\theta}^{-1/2}\ e^{-(\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(z)+i\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(z))/2}.

(24)

One can reconstruct the phase
\phi_{z}
of the quantum state by measuring along different directions. In this manner, a complex quantum wavefunction can be represented by an RBM and its parameters
(\theta,\mu)
can be trained to learn the coefficients
\psi_{z,\theta\mu}\approx\psi_{z}
.

C.2
Maximum likelihood method

Another way to efficiently estimate
|\Psi_{A}(z_{B})\rangle
from the data set is to apply a maximum likelihood fitting (maxLK). Similar to the previous approach, the coefficients in Eq.
23
are fitted in a manner that maximizes the
likelihood
of the dataset
\mathcal{D}(z_{B})
. This is achieved by minimizing the following cost function
[
65
]
,

\mathcal{C}_{\bm{\psi}}^{\mathcal{D}(z_{B})}=-\frac{1}{\|\mathcal{D}(z_{B})\|}\sum_{i=1}^{||\mathcal{D}(z_{B})||}\log\left|\langle\Psi_{A}(z_{B};\bm{\psi})|r^{(i)}_{A}(z_{B})\rangle\right|^{2}.

(25)

over all the possible vectors of complex coefficients
\bm{\psi}
in Eq. (
23
). The optimisation is numerically implemented using the Python library NumPy
[
66
,
67
]
. Interestingly, if the measurements performed on
A
are restricted to the
Z
basis, then minimizing the above cost function is equivalent to implementing the frequentist method which uses the following ansatz:

|\Psi_{A}(z_{B})\rangle=\sum_{z^{\prime}_{A}}\sqrt{p(z^{\prime}_{A}|z_{B})}|z^{\prime}_{A}\rangle=\sum_{z^{\prime}_{A}}\sqrt{\frac{p(z^{\prime}_{A},z_{B})}{p(z_{B})}}|z^{\prime}_{A}\rangle.

(26)

The joint probability
p(z_{A},z_{B})
is estimated from the outcome frequencies in the data sets. The frequentist analysis is the approach used in the Rydberg experiment
[
28
]
, and served as a benchmark for the two methods proposed in this work.

C.3
Equivalence between Maximum-likelihood and Frequentist approaches

In this subsection, we mathematically show that the frequentist approach is just a particular case of the max-likelihood when considering a positive coefficient wavefunction. There are
M=4
possible outcomes for a two-qubit wavefunction
|\Psi\rangle
that is measured only in the
Z
basis which are
(00,01,10,11)
. The probability of the
m^{th}
outcome is
p_{m}=|\psi_{m}|^{2}
, where
\psi_{m}=\langle m|\Psi\rangle
is the coefficient of the wavefunction in the computational basis. Consider a case where the measurement has been repeated
N
times, giving the following outcomes
[00,01,00,\ldots]
. The number of times the
m^{th}
outcome occurs in the dataset is labelled with
n_{m}
where trivially
\sum_{m}n_{m}=N
. The likelihood function defined in Eq. (8) for our set of measurements of the two qubit wavefunction is re-written as

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log{\left|\langle m^{(i)}|\Psi\rangle\right|^{2}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_{m^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}(n_{00}\log p_{00}+n_{01}\log p_{01}+n_{10}\log p_{10}+n_{11}\log p_{11})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\prod_{m}\ p_{m}\ ^{n_{m}}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\log\mathcal{L}

(27)

where
m^{(i)}
is the outcome of the
i^{th}
measurement in the set of
N
measurements. Using the technique of Lagrange multipliers,
L
can be maximized under the constraint
\sum_{m}p_{m}=1
which stated below,

\frac{\partial(\mathcal{L}-\alpha\sum_{m}p_{m})}{\partial p_{m^{\prime}}}=\frac{n_{m^{\prime}}}{p_{m^{\prime}}}\mathcal{L}-\alpha=0\qquad\forall\ m^{\prime}.

(28)

The factor
{\mathcal{L}}/{\alpha}
is a constant that can be fixed by enforcing the condition
\sum_{m}p_{m}=1
giving

p_{m}=\frac{n_{m}}{N}.

(29)

Thus
L
is maximized when
p_{m}=n_{m}/N
implying that the maximum likelihood approach reduces to the frequentist method when measurements only along one direction are considered.

C.4
Shadow Tomography

The shadow tomography on the ensemble of states is performed similarly to the procedure described in
[
43
,
40
,
44
]
. For every simulated measurement, the bath is measured in the
Z
basis, whereas each qubit A is measured in a random basis uniformly sampled from the set
\{X,Y,Z\}
. Each qubit in subsystem A is collapsed into some state
a_{i}
, where
i
indexes the measured qubit, which depends both on the chosen basis and the measurement outcome. The datasets
\mathcal{D}(z_{B})
contains measurements of the state
|\Psi(z_{B})\rangle
in different basis. We label with
|\bm{a}^{(n)}\rangle
the state that subsystem
A
is collapsed into upon measurement and with
|a_{i}^{(n)}\rangle
the collapsed state of qubit
i
. Then, the contribution to the shadow tomography protocol from that measurement is:

\rho_{A}^{(n)}(z_{B})=\bigotimes_{i}\left(3|a_{i}^{(n)}\rangle\langle a_{i}^{(n)}|-1\right),

(30)

where the tensor product is over each qubit in subsystem A. The density matrix is then reconstructed by averaging over all the measurements in a dataset:

\rho_{A}{(z_{B})}=\mathbb{E}\left[\rho^{(n)}_{A}(z_{B})\right]

(31)

The operator
\rho_{A}{(z_{B})}
can be used in the definition of the ensemble
\mathcal{E}=\left\{(p(z_{B}),\rho_{A}(z_{B}))\right\}
to calculate its
k^{th}
moment and compare it with the Haar Ensemble though the trace distance metric.
