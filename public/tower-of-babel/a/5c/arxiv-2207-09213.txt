Algebraic classes in mixed characteristic and André’s p p -adic periods
Giuseppe Ancona, Dragoş Frăţilă

Abstract
. Motivated by the study of algebraic classes in mixed characteristic, we define a countable subalgebra of ℚ ¯ p {\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p} which we call the algebra of André’s p p -adic periods. The classical tannakian formalism cannot be used to study these new periods. Instead, inspired by ideas of Drinfel’d on the Plücker embedding and further developed by Haines, we produce an adapted tannakian setting which allows us to bound the transcendence degree of André’s p p -adic periods and to formulate the p p -adic analog of the Grothendieck period conjecture. We exhibit several examples where special values of classical p p -adic functions appear as André’s p p -adic periods and we relate these new conjectures to some classical problems on algebraic classes.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

As Tate himself pointed out, the Tate conjecture predicts the existence of algebraic classes but does not predict which cohomological classes should be algebraic
[
Mil07
, Aside 6.5]
.
More precisely, let
X
be a smooth projective variety over a field of finite type. Denote by
\operatorname{CH}(X)
the Chow group of
X
and by
\mathrm{H}_{\ell}(X)
the
\ell
-adic cohomology of
X
.
Then we have the cycle class map

\mathsf{cl}_{X}\colon\operatorname{CH}(X)\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}_{\ell}(X),

and the prediction is that the

\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
-vector space
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
should coincide with the Galois invariant part of the
\ell
-adic cohomology
\mathrm{H}_{\ell}(X)
.
However, it does not describe the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\subset\mathrm{H}_{\ell}(X)
and actually the inclusion

\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\subset\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})

is very mysterious.

For example, in the proof of the fact that when
A
is an abelian variety over a finite field, the natural injection

\End(A)\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}\hookrightarrow\End_{\Gal}(\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\ell}(A))

is also surjective
[
Tat66
]
, the crucial elements in
\End(A)\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
that Tate constructed are actually
\ell
-adic limits of endomorphisms that do not belong to
\End(A)\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. In fact, even though Tate’s theorem says that abelian varieties over finite fields have plenty of endomorphisms, it is in general very hard to construct explicit ones besides the Frobenius.

Another example of the subtle difference between
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
and
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
appears also in the study of the Kuga–Satake construction for the proof of the Tate conjecture for K3 surfaces over finite fields. There, one would like to construct sufficiently many divisors on the K3 surface
S
by reducing modulo
p
the divisors on several lifitings
\tilde{S}
. To do so, the key point is to understand the position, inside the cohomology of
S
, of the Hodge filtration induced by a given lifting with respect to
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
, where
X
is the Kuga–Satake variety associated with
S
.

As a very concrete instance of this subtlety, notice that one can find in
\ell
-adic (or crystalline) cohomology plenty of Galois-invariant lines without fundamental classes of subvarieties. For example, consider a surface
S
over a finite field which admits two line bundles
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
on
S
such that their classes
\mathsf{cl}_{S}(L_{1})
and
\mathsf{cl}_{S}(L_{2})
in
\ell
-adic cohomology are linearly independent and whose intersection product is non zero (for example,
S
is an abelian surface). Take a non rational scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
. Then the
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
-line generated by
\mathsf{cl}_{S}(L_{1})+\alpha\cdot\mathsf{cl}_{S}(L_{2})
is Galois-invariant but it cannot contain the class of a line bundle as the intersection product between classes of line bundles is a rational number.

In characteristic zero there is a clear candidate for the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector subspace
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
.
Namely, the Artin comparison theorem gives an isomorphism between
\ell
-adic cohomology and singular cohomology, and
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
is described by the Hodge conjecture. Again in
[
Mil07
, Aside 6.5]
, Tate suggests that the absence of a conjectural description of
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
in positive characteristic is one reason for which algebraic classes are less understood there, even in codimension one (in contrast to the characteristic zero situation where we have the Lefschetz
(1,1)
theorem). This paper is intended as a first step towards such a description.
The setup that we study is the case where the variety has a lifting to characteristic zero and the cohomology is the crystalline one (the
\ell
-adic setting is discussed in
Section

10
but our investigation is less satisfying).

1.2.
From algebraic classes to periods

For simplicity in this introduction, let
X_{0}
be a smooth projective variety over
\mathbb{Q}
and let
p
be a prime number of good reduction.
Fix a model
X
of
X_{0}
over some localization of
\Spec(\mathbb{Z})
and let
X_{p}
be the fiber of
X
over the prime
p
which we assume moreover to be smooth.
In order to describe the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector subspace
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\subset\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{crys}}(X_{p},\mathbb{Q}_{p})
, one can use the
Berthelot comparison theorem
[
Ber74
, Theorem V.2.3.2]
between the de Rham cohomology of
X_{0}
and the crystalline cohomology of
X_{p}
:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/\mathbb{Q})\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}\simeq\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{crys}}(X_{p},\mathbb{Q}_{p})

and deduce a natural map

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/\mathbb{Q})\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}.

This map relates two different
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces: algebraic classes in characteristic
p
with rational coefficients and de Rham cohomology in characteristic zero. Our original task to describe the position of
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
inside
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{crys}}(X_{p},\mathbb{Q}_{p})
is equivalent, in this mixed characteristic setting, to describing the position of
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
with respect to
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/\mathbb{Q})
.
For example, one can fix a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/\mathbb{Q})
and try to write the coordinates of the elements of
\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q})
with respect to this basis.
Note that these coordinates are numbers in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and if we change the fixed basis of
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/\mathbb{Q})
, the
\mathbb{Q}
-span of these coordinates in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
does not change.

Our investigations led us to think that these coordinates behave like periods, both for their transcendental properties and for their relations to special values.
Our evidence is the following:

a)

Special values of the
p
-adic logarithm, products of special values of the
p
-adic Gamma function and the
p
-adic hypergeometric functions appear as such coordinates (see
Examples

9.6
,
5.13
and
5.16
).

b)

The transcendence degree of these coordinates can be bounded in terms of motivic Galois groups (
Section

6
) and it is possible to state an analog of the Grothendieck period conjecture (
Section

8
), which for elliptic curves appears to be related to a conjecture of André (
Remark

7.3
).

These coordinates for all possible
X
span a countable
\mathbb{Q}
-subalgebra of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
which, in light of the above properties, seems to be a
p
-adic analogue of classical periods.
This algebra is unrelated to Fontaine’s ring of
p
-adic periods, which has several spectacular cohomological properties but for which the naive Grothendieck period conjecture
[
And90
]
does not hold. More precisely, it is shown in loc. cit. that its veracity depends on the choice of an embedding
{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\hookrightarrow{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p}
.
However, more recently André has proved (see
[
And23
, Theorem 3.5]
) that for a generic such embedding, the surjectivity in the Tate conjecture implies the
p
-adic Grothendieck conjecture (for Fontaine’s periods).
As we will explain in §
1.5
, other attempts to construct such a subalgebra of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of
p
-adic periods appeared in the literature.
Our approach not only includes and generalizes the previous ones but, more importantly, it is the first one providing a sharp bound of the transcendence degree and which allows a formulation of a
p
-adic analog of the Grothendieck period conjecture.

1.3.
Classical periods

In the classical setting, periods are complex numbers which can be written as integrals of algebraic differential forms on simplices of algebraic varieties defined over a number field. The cohomological interpretation is the following. Following de Rham and Grothendieck, for a variety
X
over
\mathbb{Q}
we have that integration gives a functorial isomorphism

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X/\mathbb{Q})\otimes\mathbb{C}\simeq\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{Betti}}(X,\mathbb{Q})\otimes\mathbb{C}

and the coordinates of one
\mathbb{Q}
-structure with respect to the other are precisely the periods
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)
associated with
X
.

Many interesting complex numbers appear as periods, such as
2\pi i
, special values of the logarithm, products of special values of the Gamma function, and one would like to be able to control their transcendence degree. To do so, the idea of Grothendieck was to consider all possible functorial isomorphisms

\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X/\mathbb{Q})\otimes R\simeq\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{Betti}}(X,\mathbb{Q})\otimes R,

for all commutative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebras
R
, which are moreover compatible with the Künneth formula.
The general tannakian formalism will ensure that there is a variety
\mathcal{T}_{X}
over
\mathbb{Q}
representing all such comparison isomorphisms.
Notice that the isomorphism (
1.3
) corresponds to a
\mathbb{C}
-point of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
with residue field equal to the field generated by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)
, hence the transcendence degree of the algebra generated by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)
is at most the dimension of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
. Grothendieck has conjectured that they should be equal, i.e., that such a bound is optimal.
It turns out that this bound, together with all the tannakian interpretation that we sketched above, is at the heart of several recent advances in the arithmetic study of multizeta-values, as for example in
[
Bro12
]
.

In order to make the transcendence bound useful, one needs to compute the dimension of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
. To do so, Grothendieck has considered all possible functorial automorphisms

\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X/\mathbb{Q})\otimes R\simeq\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X/\mathbb{Q})\otimes R,

for all commutative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebras
R
, which are compatible with the Künneth formula.
This turns out to be representable by an algebraic group
G(X)
, called the motivic Galois group of
X
, whose natural action on
\mathcal{T}_{X}
by right composition makes
\mathcal{T}_{X}
a
G(X)
-torsor.
In particular,
\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
G(X)
have the same dimension. Moreover, the group
G(X)
can be explicitly calculated in numerous cases providing thus explicit bounds for the transcendence degree of the algebra generated by the classical periods
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)
.

1.4.
Main results

In our setting we would like to study

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/\mathbb{Q})\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}

induced by the Berthelot comparison isomorphism (
1.2
). Our
p
-adic periods
\mathcal{P}_{p}(X)
are defined as the coordinates of the left
\mathbb{Q}
-structure with respect to the right one. In order to study their transcendence properties one would like to imitate Grothendieck’s construction we sketched above, with the isomorphism (
1.3
) replaced by the injection (
1.4
). The fact that this injection is not in general an isomorphism does not allow one to use the same tannakian constructions and let us think for a longtime that such a tannakian framework adapted to
p
-adic periods could not exist. This is probably also the reason why all the previous approaches to
p
-adic periods in the literature have considered only settings where (
1.4
) is an isomorphism (see §
1.5
).

The tannakian interpretation of the Plücker embedding and a generalization of it due to Drinfel’d (see
[
Hai
]
for a detailed account), inspired us to think that such a construction might also apply in our setting, namely considering injections instead of isomorphisms.
We believe that this psychological step, as natural as it can appear afterwards, is a real contribution of our work.
More precisely, consider all possible functorial injections

\Image(\mathsf{cl}_{X_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\otimes R\hookrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/\mathbb{Q})\otimes R

for all commutative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebras
R
, which are compatible with the tensor structures (see
Definition

6.1
for a precise definition) and show that there is a variety
\mathcal{H}_{X}
over
\mathbb{Q}
representing all such injections (
Theorem

6.3
).
As in the classical case, the map (
1.4
) is a
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-point of
\mathcal{H}_{X}
and one deduces the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

The transcendence degree of the algebra generated by
p
-adic periods satisfies the bound

(1.5)

\displaystyle\trdeg_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{P}_{p}(X))\leq\dim\mathcal{H}_{X}.

A second difference with the classical case comes from the fact that the algebra generated by the
p
-adic periods of
X
does not contain, in general, all the
p
-adic periods of all powers of
X
.
This comes from the fact that the Künneth formula holds for cohomology groups but does not hold for algebraic classes (as in general powers of
X
have more algebraic classes than just products of algebraic classes on
X
).
For this reason one should also consider the larger algebra
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\langle X\rangle)
generated by the
p
-adic periods of all powers
X^{n}
and the tannakian constructions we described above actually give the following inequalities

\trdeg_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{P}_{p}(X))\leq\trdeg_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{P}_{p}(\langle X\rangle))\leq\dim\mathcal{H}_{X}.

We also construct a finer tannakian object which gives a sharper bound for the
p
-adic periods of
X
; see

Definition

6.11
for details.
All these bounds improve the previous ones and give conceptual explanations to several known relations on
p
-adic special values; see
Section

1.5
.

As for classical periods
Section

1.3
, one needs to be able to compute the dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{X}
in order for the bound to be useful.
To do so we consider the motivic Galois group
G(X_{0})
introduced in §
1.3
. It has again a natural action on
\mathcal{H}_{X}
by right composition. Now there is the third difference with respect to the classical case:
\mathcal{H}_{X}
is not in the general a
G(X_{0})
-torsor but we show that it is always a
G(X_{0})
-homogeneous space.

In order to compute
\dim\mathcal{H}_{X}
, one has to describe a stabilizer. We compute the stabilizer precisely of the
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-point induced by (
1.4
). It turns out that such a
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-algebraic subgroup is the motivic Galois group
G(X_{p})
associated with crystalline cohomology
1
1

1

Note that
G(X_{p})
is indeed a subgroup of
G(X_{0})\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, through the isomorphism (
1.1
).
, namely the
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-algebraic group representing all functorial, tensor automorphisms
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{crys}}(X_{p},\mathbb{Q}_{p})\otimes R\simeq\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{crys}}(X_{p},\mathbb{Q}_{p})\otimes R
, for all
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-algebras
R
.
To sum up we obtain

(1.6)

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{H}_{X}=\dim G(X_{0})-\dim G(X_{p}).

The
p
-adic analog of the Grothendieck period conjecture predicts that the inequality
\trdeg(\mathcal{P}_{p}(\langle X\rangle))\leq\dim\mathcal{H}_{X}
should actually be an equality (
8.3
).
An analogous conjecture is formulated for the subalgebra
\mathcal{P}_{p}(X)\subset\mathcal{P}_{p}(\langle X\rangle)
(see
Proposition

6.15
and
8.1
). To be precise, these conjectures are formulated only under a minor condition, called ramification condition, which is briefly discussed in §
1.5
and detailed in
Section

7
.
As already mentioned, our setting is the first where the Grothendieck period conjecture has a
p
-adic analog; see §
1.5
.

As particular cases, these conjectures predict the precise transcendence degree of some special values (logarithm, hypergeometric, Gamma function, …) which will hopefully be useful to number theorists. As pointed out in §
1.3
, tannakian techniques have been at the heart of several recent advances on rationality questions on special values (for example multiple zeta values as in
[
Bro12
]
) and we hope that the basis built in this paper might play the same role in the study of
p
-adic special values.

1.5.
Related work

This paper suggests that there should be a bridge between
algebraic classes in characteristic
p

and a
p
-adic analog of classical periods.

During our work we discovered that a similar bridge was crossed by André in the opposite direction: the motivation and the conclusion are reversed
[
And95
]
.
His aim was to find a canonical
\mathbb{Q}
-structure of
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{crys}}(X_{p},\mathbb{Q}_{p})
and he realized that, when
X_{p}
is a supersingular abelian variety, this is possible using algebraic classes.
He made a conjecture in this setting which is implied by our analog of the Grothendieck period conjecture (it is a special case of the weak version of our conjecture, as presented in
Section

3
).
He realized afterwards that in order for this conjecture to be reasonable one needs to impose some ramification condition above
p

[
And03
, I.5.3]
.
We propose a generalization of it and show that for a given variety
X_{0}/\mathbb{Q}
this condition is satisfied for all but finitely many primes
p
(
Section

7
).
As it appears clear, André’s work became a great source of inspiration for us and for this reason we would like to call the periods we introduce in this paper André’s
p
-adic periods.

In the setting of mixed Tate motives analogous constructions already exist
[
Fur07
,
Bro17
]
. These works considered the action of the Frobenius on the crystalline realization and defined the
p
-adic periods of the motive as the coefficients of this action with respect to a rational basis induced by (
1.1
).
It turns out that André’s
p
-adic periods for mixed Tate motives come indeed from those coefficients. However, for general motives, the coefficients of the Frobenius matrix are only special examples of André’s
p
-adic periods; see
Remarks

5.17
and
9.7
. Moreover, even just for the coefficients of the Frobenius matrix our definition of
p
-adic periods seems to be the right one for several reasons; see
Remark

9.8
for details.
Probably the most important one is the following: the coefficients of the Frobenius matrix satisfy some algebraic relations due to the fact that the characteristic polynomial of the Frobenius has integral coefficients.
Those relations have a motivic interpretation in our setting (
Proposition

9.9
) whereas they do not have one in the setting of
[
Fur07
,
Bro17
]
; see
Remark

9.10
for more details. In particular, there is no reasonable
p
-adic Grothendieck period conjecture in their setting.

After a conversation between Joseph Ayoub and the authors, the three of us think that the algebra of motivic
p
-adic periods we construct in this paper should be related to the algebra of abstract
p
-adic periods constructed in
[
Ayo20
]
. In that article Ayoub conjectured that his algebra should be the algebra of functions on a homogeneous motivic space but not on a motivic torsor. It is exactly what happens in our constructions. A precise relation between these two algebras will be studied in the future. This would be the
p
-adic analogue of
[
Ayo15
, Fait 1.4]
, namely a comparison between the algebra of motivic periods coming from the tannakian setting and the algebra of motivic periods constructed in the triangulated setting.

1.6.
Organization of the paper

The weak version of the classical Grothendieck period conjecture allows one to formulate concrete conditions predicting the existence of algebraic classes. We start the paper with its
p
-adic analog and discuss the relation between this new conjecture and some classical ones (
Section

3
).

For the strong version of the conjecture we need to introduce the motivic framework (
Section

4
).
In this setting we can construct the algebra of André’s
p
-adic periods associated to a motive (
Section

5
).
In
Section

6
we construct the homogeneous space
\mathcal{H}_{X}
and show that it is representable.

As already mentioned, a ramification condition is needed in order to have a reasonable Grothendieck period conjecture.
This is discussed in
Section

7
.
The ramification condition and the construction of the torsor space allow us to define the
p
-adic analog of the Grothendieck period conjecture (
Section

8
).

The paper is written in the language of pure motives but most of the work applies also to the mixed setting. In
Section

9
, we briefly outline which changes are needed in this context.
Finally, in
Section

10
we discuss the
\ell
-adic version of these questions, where (
1.1
) is replaced by Artin’s comparison theorem between singular and
\ell
-adic cohomology.
In this case, despite some positive results, the general picture is less satisfying.

2.
Conventions

Throughout the paper we will work with the following notation and conventions.

(1)

Base fields.
We fix a prime number
p
and an algebraic closure
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
. Let
\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\subset\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
be the ring of algebraic
p
-adic integers.
Define
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\subset\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
to be the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{Q}
in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
.
We write
\mathcal{O}
for the ring of algebraic integers.
The inclusion
\mathcal{O}\subset\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
induces
a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{p}
of
\mathcal{O}
above the ideal
(p)
of
\mathbb{Z}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}
the localization of
\mathcal{O}
at
\mathfrak{p}
and by
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
the residue field.

(2)

Crystalline cohomology.
Fix a smooth projective variety
X_{p}
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.
The usual crystalline cohomology of
X_{p}
has coefficients in (the ring of integers of)
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}^{ur}_{p}
the completion of
\mathbb{Q}^{ur}_{p}
, the maximal unramified extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
in
{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p}
.
We will work instead with crystalline cohomology with
{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p}
coefficients, which is defined as follows. First, consider the limit of the crystalline cohomology of all models of
X_{p}
defined over finite fields.
Such a limit defines a Weil cohomology with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}^{ur}_{p}
. Then, one extends the coefficients from
\mathbb{Q}^{ur}_{p}
to
{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p}
and obtains a
Weil cohomology with coefficients in
{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p}
.
(The construction of such a Weil cohomology is analogous to
[
D’A23
, §2.2, §3]
and is implicit in
[
And03
, I.5.3.5]
.)

Assume moreover that
X_{p}
is the reduction modulo
p
of a smooth projective scheme
X
over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}
with generic fiber
X_{0}
. Then one has a canonical comparison

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}})\otimes{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p}\simeq\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{crys}}(X_{p},{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p})

which is obtained from
[
Ber74
, Theorem V.2.3.2]

through the analogous limit process.

(3)

Motives.
We will work with homological motives defined over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}
and their restriction to
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and to
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.
More precisely, for a base
S=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}},\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, or
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
and a field of coefficients of characteristic zero
L
, we let
{\mathcal{M}ot}(S)_{L}
denote the category of homological motives over
S
with coefficients in
L
with respect to a Weil cohomology with coefficients containing
L
. For generalities see
[
And04
, IV]
.

The category
{\mathcal{M}ot}(S)_{L}
depends a priori on the chosen Weil cohomology.
In characteristic zero all classical Weil cohomologies (singular, de Rham,
\ell
-adic) give rise to the same category because of the existence of comparison theorems.
In positive characteristic we will always work with one given cohomology, which will be crystalline (except in
Section

10
).

In order to have realization functors with values in usual vector spaces (not super-vector spaces with the Koszul sign commutativity) we will work only with motives verifying the sign Künneth conjecture (see
[
And04
, Section 6]
for details).
We abuse notation and still denote by
{\mathcal{M}ot}(S)_{L}
the category of motives with the commutativity constraint modified.

In this paper we will work with
L={\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
up to
Section

6
and with

L=\mathbb{Q}
from
Section

7
on.
When
L={\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
we drop it from the notation and we simply write

{\mathcal{M}ot}(S)={\mathcal{M}ot}(S)_{{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}}.

(4)

Cycle class map.

For a motive
M
, we think of the vector space

\Hom_{{\mathcal{M}ot}(S)}(\mathbbm{1},M)

as the space of algebraic classes on
M
.
Indeed, if
F
are the coefficients of the cohomology and
R
is the associated realization functor any map
f\in\Hom_{{\mathcal{M}ot}(S)}(\mathbbm{1},M)
is characterized by the image of
1\in R(\mathbbm{1})=F
in
R(M)
.
When
M

The map

3.
Some questions on algebraic classes in mixed characteristic

In this section we would like to raise some new questions on algebraic classes in mixed characteristic and relate them to classical conjectures.

We let
X
be a smooth projective scheme over an open subset of the ring of integers of some number field.
We denote the generic fiber by
X_{0}
(it is a smooth projective variety over a number field
K
) and by
X_{p}
the fiber above a fixed place
\mathfrak{p}
(it is a smooth projective variety over a finite field).

Berthelot
[
Ber74
, Theorem V.2.3.2]
has defined a natural isomorphism between the de Rham cohomology of
X_{0}
and the crystalline cohomology of
X_{p}
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/K)\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}\simeq\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{crys}}(X_{p},K_{\mathfrak{p}})

where
K_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the
\mathfrak{p}
-adic completion of
K
.
The left hand side has a
K
-structure, namely
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/K)
.
The right hand side contains a natural
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{alg}}(X_{p})
the algebraic classes (with rational coefficients) on
X_{p}
.
These do not, in general, span the whole crystalline cohomology.

Question 3.1
.

(pGPCw)
With the above assumptions, let
\gamma\in\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{alg}}(X_{p})
be an algebraic class in the crystalline cohomology of
X_{p}
living in the subspace
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/K)\subset\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X_{0}/K)\otimes K_{\mathfrak{p}}
under Berthelot’s isomorphism (
3.1
).
Does then
\gamma
lift to an algebraic class in characteristic zero? Namely, is it true that
\gamma
is in the image of the specialization map
\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{alg}}(X_{0})\to\mathrm{H}^{*}_{\mathrm{alg}}(X_{p})
?

Remark 3.2
.

Let us compare now
3.1
, which will be denoted (
p
GPCw), with three classical conjectures which we recall here in an informal way (see
[
And04
, VII]
for more details).
These three conjectures are the Hodge conjecture (HC), the weak form of the Grothendieck period conjecture (GPCw) and the Fontaine–Messing
p
-adic variational Hodge conjecture (
p
HC).

(HC)

A rational class in singular cohomology is algebraic if and only if it is in the right step of the de Rham filtration.

(GPCw)

A rational class in de Rham cohomology is algebraic if and only if it is rational for singular cohomology.

(
p
HC)

An algebraic class in crystalline cohomology lifts to characteristic zero if and only if it is in the right step of the de Rham filtration.

The above three conjectures together with
3.1
naturally fit into a table

(
p
GPCw)

(GPCw)

(
p
HC)

(HC)

The right part of the table is the characteristic zero part, the left part is the mixed characteristic one, the bottom part is the filtration part and the top part is the rationality part.

The conjecture (GPCw) is implied by the strong form of the Grothendieck period conjecture (concerning transcendence of periods); see
[
And04
, Proposition 7.5.2.2]
. (Notice that
loc. cit.
applies under our hypothesis; see §
2
(
6
).)
Its
p
-adic counterpart is discussed in
Section

8
.

Using the motivic language
one can reformulate
3.1
as follows:

Question 3.3
.

Does an algebraic correspondence defined over a finite field lift to characteristic zero as soon as its action on de Rham cohomology is rational?

The following is a special case.

Question 3.4
.

Does an endomorphism of an abelian variety over a finite field lift (up to isogeny) to characteristic zero as soon as its action on de Rham cohomology is rational?

Here is a little evidence.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
E
be a non CM elliptic curve defined over a quadratic number field
K
. Consider a place of good reduction over an inert prime
\mathfrak{p}
and suppose that the reduction is a supersingular elliptic curve
E_{p}
over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
.
Then there is at least one endomorphism of
E_{p}
whose action on the de Rham cohomology is not rational.

Proof.

Let us argue by contradiction and suppose that the algebra
\End{E_{p}}
acts on
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(E/K)
. We will construct a non trivial endomorphism of
E
and thus contradict the non CM property.

First notice that since
E_{p}
is supersingular, the algebra
\End{E_{p}}\otimes K
has dimension four over
K
and this forces it to be equal to
\End(H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(E/K))\simeq M_{2}(K)
.
In this matrix algebra the elements that respect the de Rham filtration form a
K
-subalgebra
A
of codimension one.
Let
\alpha
be a
K
-linear form on
\End E_{p}\otimes K
such that
\ker(\alpha)=A
.

Second, consider the
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra
B:=A\cap\End{E_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
and let us show that it is of dimension at least two.
Indeed, as
K
is quadratic, the restriction of
\alpha
to
\End{E_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
is equivalent to the datum of two
\mathbb{Q}
-linear forms on
\End{E_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. Their common kernel is precisely
B
and therefore the dimension of
B
is at least
4-2=2
.

In conclusion there is an endomorphism
f\in\End{E_{p}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
which is not a multiple of the identity and whose action respects the de Rham filtration.
By
[
BO83
, Theorem 3.15]
such an
f
lifts to characteristic zero.
This gives a contradiction as we supposed that
E
was not CM.
∎

Definition 4.2

A lax-monoidal functor between two monoidal categories is the data of a functor
\mathcal{F}\colon(\mathcal{C},\otimes)\to(\mathcal{D},\otimes)
together with morphisms

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle\colon\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}}\to\mathcal{F}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle\mu_{X,Y}

\displaystyle\colon\mathcal{F}(X)\otimes\mathcal{F}(Y)\to\mathcal{F}(X\otimes Y),\text{ for all objects }X,Y\text{ of }\mathcal{C}

On the other hand,

Remark 5.7
.

Contrary to the classical situation of complex periods, it is not true that the
p
-adic periods of

contain the

-adic periods of every object

For singular and de Rham cohomology, all classes on

are polynomials on classes on

For instance, it could happen that

But even if a motive has algebraic classes, it is not true in general that they generate all the algebraic classes of objects in

(

Fix a (non canonical) factorisation of

Theorem 6.5

(

, Proposition 2.3, Claim 2.4, Lemma 5.2]

[

]
)

Let

The space of lax-monoidal natural transformations

On the other hand, the linear group

Definition 6.8

(1) The map

Proposition 6.10

Let

Remark 6.18
.

We briefly interrupt the series of examples to highlight a particular point about matrix coefficients which might clarify the difference between the algebras
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{mot}}_{p}(M)
and
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{mot}}_{p}(\langle M\rangle)
.

Let
G
be a reductive group over
k
, an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero.
For any representation
V
of
G
we have the matrix coefficients map

\displaystyle m_{V}\colon

\displaystyle V\boxtimes V^{\vee}\to\mathcal{O}(G)

\displaystyle v\boxtimes\xi\mapsto(g\mapsto\xi(gv)).

The Peter–Weyl theorem gives an isomorphism of
G\times G
-modules

(6.4)

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{V\text{ irr}}V\boxtimes V^{\vee}\simeq\mathcal{O}(G)

where
V
runs over all irreducible representations of
G
.
Moreover, for any faithful irreducible representation
V
, the summand
V\boxtimes V^{\vee}
generates
\mathcal{O}(G)
as an algebra (see
[
Wat12
, §3.5 Theorem]
).

Now let
H\leq G
be a closed subgroup. The map (
6.4
) restricts to an isomorphism of
G
-modules

\bigoplus_{V\text{ irr}}V^{H}\boxtimes V^{\vee}\simeq\mathcal{O}(G/H).

However, now a summand
V^{H}\boxtimes V^{\vee}
might not generate the algebra
\mathcal{O}(G/H)
.
An extreme example is for irreducible representations
V
of
G
such that
V^{H}=\{0\}
. This is the case for
\mathbb{G}_{m}\leq\operatorname{SL}(2)
and any even dimensional irreducible representation. A less extreme example is mentioned below for
\mathbb{G}_{m}\leq\operatorname{PGL}(2)
and

.
We do not know for which irreducible representations

the matrix coefficients of

generate the algebra
\mathcal{O}(G/H)
.

In the motivic setting, the algebra
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{mot}}_{p}(\langle M\rangle)
is of the form
\mathcal{O}(G/H)
whereas
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{mot}}_{p}(M)
corresponds to the subalgebra generated by

where

viewed as a

-module (see

).

This phenomenon does not happen in the classical case of complex periods because
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathrm{mot}}(\langle M\rangle)
is of the form

.

7.
The ramification condition

We start this section by recalling an example (see
Example

7.1
), due to André, which in particular shows that the Berthelot point

\mathfrak{c}_{\mathrm{Bert}}\in\mathcal{H}_{M}({\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p})
from
Definition

6.8
is not always a generic point of the space
\mathcal{H}_{M}
introduced in
Definition

6.1
.

Following again André, who studied similar questions for the
H^{1}
of abelian varieties, one can hope that this kind of examples is sporadic; see
Remark

7.3
.
He formulated a ramification condition for the
H^{1}
which allows one to avoid this pathological example.

The goal of this section is to find a generalization of this ramification condition which makes sense for any motive (
Definition

7.6
).
We show that such a condition is generically satisfied (
Corollary

7.9
).

In order to formulate this ramification condition we need to work with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}
(contrary to the previous sections, where the field of coefficients was
{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
).

Example 7.1

(
[
And03
, I.4.6.4]
)
.

Let
E
be a CM elliptic curve over
{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
with
\End(E)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{-3}]
. Let
p
be a place above
3
.
Then the reduction modulo
p
of
E
is supersingular.
Consider the motive
M:=\cEnd(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(E))=\mathfrak{h}^{1}(E)\otimes\mathfrak{h}^{1}(E)^{\vee}
and its reduction modulo
p
.
In
loc. cit.
, André shows that all the
p
-adic periods of
M
are algebraic numbers (see the ramified case of
Example

5.13
for more details). On the other hand, the homogeneous space
\mathcal{H}_{M}
has dimension one (
Example

6.16
). This means that the transcendence degree of the algebra generated by the periods is strictly smaller than the dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{M}
(equivalently, the Berthelot point does not live on the generic point of
\mathcal{H}_{M}
).

In the above example the prime number
3
ramifies in the number field
{\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{-3}]=\End(E)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}}
.
André conjectures that this ramification condition should be the only situation where all the periods of the motive
M:=\cEnd(\mathfrak{h}^{1}(E))
are algebraic (see
Remark

7.3
for more details).

Remark 7.2
.

(Relation to Gamma functions.)
In the classical complex setting, periods of abelian CM motives are related to products of special values of the Gamma function (the Gross–Deligne conjecture
[
G78
, p.209]
).
There are explicit relations between such special values
[
Del79
, Appendix]

which are conjectured to be the only ones.
Such a prediction is coherent with the Grothendieck period conjecture.

In the
p
-adic context the situation is more subtle as
Example

7.1
shows. There seems to be a discrepancy between the ramified and the unramified case, namely in the ramified situation the algebricity of the
p
-adic periods is not fully explained by motives.
The reason seems to boil down to the fact that the
p
-adic periods have different expressions in terms of special values of the
p
-adic gamma function in the two cases (see
Example

5.13
for the two expressions).

Remark 7.3
.

(André’s Betti lattices and ramification.) In
[
And95
]
(and later
[
And03
, I.5.3]
),
André investigated the possibility of constructing a second

-structure to crystalline cohomology – especially for the

{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
-structure given by the de Rham cohomology

Such a canonical structure should be called "Betti", and denoted

It should come with two properties: first the endomorphisms of

and second any algebraic classes on any power of

He proposed a construction in two cases:

(1)

If

[

. In this case one can take the de Rham cohomology of the Serre–Tate lifting.
(This is interesting if one started with a non CM lift

– in particular not the Serre–Tate lifting itself).

(2)

If
A
is supersingular. In this case he shows that there exists a unique Betti structure

with the property that the endomorphisms of
A

[
And03

.

The proposed
{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
-structure

is related to the one we are studying
in an indirect way; see
Example

5.14
for the supersingular reduction case and
Example

5.16

In

On the other hand, as he afterwards noticed, those special values of the Gamma function might turn out to be algebraic by the Gross–Koblitz formula

Definition 7.4
.

A motive
N\in{\mathcal{M}ot}({\overline{\mathbb{Q}}})_{\mathbb{Q}}
is called CM if
\End(N)
is a number field of degree equal to the rank of

.

The name CM is justified by the remark below. Those motives are needed in order to generalize André’s ramification condition from abelian varieties to general motives; see the definition which follows.

Remark 7.5
.

(Not needed in the sequel.) The standard conjectures predict that if the motive

is a CM motive with the above definition, then

has a positive involution, hence it should indeed be a CM number field.

Definition 7.6
.

Let
M\in{\mathcal{M}ot}({\overline{\mathbb{Q}}})_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a motive, let
\langle M\rangle
be the tannakian category it generates and let

be a prime number.
We say that

ramifies in

such that the prime

ramifies in the number field
\End(N)
.
If

does not ramify in

we say that the motive

is unramified above

.

Remark 7.7
.

In the
Examples

5.14
and
5.16
the prime

is not ramified in
\langle M\rangle
. The reason is that the tannakian groups are
\operatorname{PGL}(2)
and

and so the only CM objects in

are the Tate twists (see for example the proof of the next proposition). For Tate twists the ramification condition is empty as

Proposition 7.8
.

Let
M\in{\mathcal{M}ot}({\overline{\mathbb{Q}}})_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a motive. Then there exists a number field
L_{M}
such that for all CM objects
N\in\langle M\rangle
we have
\End(N)\hookrightarrow L_{M}
.

Proof.

We consider the Betti realization
R_{B}
(
4.1
) restricted to
\langle M\rangle
and denote the corresponding tannakian group by
G_{B}(M)
.
It is an algebraic group over
\mathbb{Q}
and the analog of
Theorem

4.4
says that
R_{B}
induces an equivalence of tensor categories
R_{B}\colon\langle M\rangle\to\operatorname{Rep}_{\mathbb{Q}}(G_{B}(M))
.
Therefore we can place ourselves in the representation category
\operatorname{Rep}_{\mathbb{Q}}(G_{B}(M))
and, to ease the notation, we will write
G
instead of
G_{B}(M)
.

Let
V\in\operatorname{Rep}_{\mathbb{Q}}(G)
be a CM object, i.e.,
K:=\End_{G}(V)
is a number field of degree equal to
\dim(V)
.
This turns
V
into a
K
-vector space of dimension 1 and moreover the action of
G
on
V
commutes with
K
.
Since
\End_{K}(V)
is abelian, we deduce that the action of
G
on
V
factorizes through its abelianization
G/[G,G]
.
We can therefore assume from now on that
G
is an abelian algebraic group.

One can write
G
as a direct product
U\times S
where
U
is unipotent (abelian) and
S
is a diagonalizable group, possibly disconnected if
G
is. (For pure motives,
G
is reductive and hence
U=\{1\}
. We do not assume it here so it applies also in the context of mixed motives in
Section

9
.)

Since
U
is abelian it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{r}
for some
r\geq 0
.
Let
L
be a finite extension of
\mathbb{Q}
such that
S
splits over it, i.e.,
S_{L}\simeq\mathbb{G}_{m}^{s}\times\Gamma
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
and some finite group
\Gamma
.
Extend the group
G
to
L
and notice that all its irreducible representations on
L
-vector spaces are of dimension 1.

Put
V_{L}:=V\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}L
and note that
V_{L}
is semisimple as a representation of

(see
[
CR81
, Theorem 7.5]
).
All simple representations of
G_{L}
are of dimension one over
L
, therefore
V_{L}
splits as a direct sum of representations of dimension one.
Using the equalities
\End_{G_{L}}(V_{L})=\End_{G}(V)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}L=K\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}L
we deduce that
\End_{G_{L}}(V_{L})
is commutative, hence isomorphic to

for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that
\End_{G}(V)
embeds in the field

and this concludes the proof. ∎

Corollary 7.9
.

For
M\in{\mathcal{M}ot}({\overline{\mathbb{Q}}})_{\mathbb{Q}}
there exists a field

such that if

does not ramify in

, then it does not ramify in
\langle M\rangle

\langle M\rangle
.

Conjecture 8.3

Proposition 8.4
.

Let

(1)

The following are equivalent:

holds for

(b)

(c)

(2)

holds for

(b)

We only prove the equivalence between

Corollary 8.5

8.3

Proof.

We use the description (

Conjecture 8.6
.

Suppose that
M\in{{\mathcal{M}ot}({\overline{\mathbb{Q}}})_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\textrm{gr}}}
is unramified above
p
.
Then we conjecture that the inclusion of

-vector spaces

Z_{0}(M)_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\subset R_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\cap Z_{p}(\spe(M))_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}

is in fact an equality.

In

we were interested in the above equality with coefficients in

. Here we give a simple argument which allows one to go from

to
\mathbb{Q}

Proposition 8.7
.

Fix a motive
M\in{{\mathcal{M}ot}({\overline{\mathbb{Q}}})_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\textrm{gr}}}
that is unramified over

and that satisfies

.

Then the inclusion of
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces

Z_{0}(M)_{\mathbb{Q}}\subset R_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\cap Z_{p}(\spe(M))_{\mathbb{Q}}

is an equality.

Proof.

Consider the commutative diagram

\begin{matrix}Z_{0}(M)_{\mathbb{Q}}\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}&\subset&(R_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\cap Z_{p}(\spe(M))_{\mathbb{Q}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\\
\downarrow&&\downarrow\\
Z_{0}(M)_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}&=&R_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\cap Z_{p}(\spe(M))_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\end{matrix}

The first vertical arrow is an isomorphism because extending coefficients commutes with numerical equivalence
[
And04
, Proposition 3.2.7.1]
or alternatively it commutes with homological equivalence for classical cohomologies in characteristic zero (one reduces to singular cohomology through comparison theorems).

The second vertical arrow is injective because it is even injective when one extends to
{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p}
-coefficients.
This comes from the Berthelot comparison isomorphism (
3.1
) and again the fact that extending coefficients commutes with numerical equivalence.

Hence, by passing through the first vertical arrow we deduce that the diagonal composition is an isomorphism, whereas by passing through the second vertical map we have that the diagonal is a composition of injective maps. We deduce that all arrows are isomorphisms and therefore the inclusion is an equality.
∎

Let us make a tannakian translation of the problem, as in the proof of

Let

We consider the diagram of categories

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Rep}_{k}(G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Vect}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Rep}_{k}(H)}

Consider the functor

(Recall that

In the case we are interested in we want to apply this abstract setting to

With this tannakian translation, the contents of

(1)

(2)

Remark 8.10
.

As for classical periods, there is no converse of the above proposition.
Namely, in the abstract tannakian setting of the proof, (2) does not imply (1) in general.

For an example, consider the trivial subgroup

.
Notice that
\mathcal{H}=G
.
Consider

and consider the matrix

\alpha:=\begin{pmatrix}a&1\\

We claim that for all representations

we have

\alpha(V)\cap V=\{0\}\text{ inside }V\otimes_{k}K.

Notice first that it is enough to check it on irreducible representations.
For

they are all of the form

.
A non enlightening computation shows that for such representations the claim is true.
Thus (2) holds in this example.

However,
\alpha
is not generic in

as its Zariski closure has dimension two, hence (1) does not hold.

9.
Mixed case

In this section we sketch how to adapt the constructions of the paper in the setting of mixed motives as developped by Voevodsky et al.
[
Voe00
,
Ayo07
,
CD19
]
.

There are two subtleties of different level.
The first is that for a non proper scheme over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}}
the Berthelot comparison theorem (
3.1
) does not hold in general (e.g., the
special fiber by
X_{p}
can be empty).
This problem is solved considering the subcategory of motives verifying this property, which contains for instance the extensions of motives of smooth proper varieties.

The second issue is more serious; we need to work with a subcategory of Voevodsky’s motives
\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}})
which is tannakian and for which the realization functors are fiber functors.
But in the mixed context, the only case where this is known is for mixed Tate motives over the ring of integers of a number field.

On the other hand, the case of mixed motives seems to be a setting where plenty of interesting periods should appear.
This comes from the fact that over
{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
there are lots of non trivial extensions of motives, whereas over
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}}_{p}
all extensions conjecturally split, giving rise to non liftable maps and, in particular, to non liftable algebraic classes (see
Remark

9.7
).

Remark 9.1
.

There exists a triangulated tensor subcategory
\mathrm{DM}^{B}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}})
of

containing the motives of smooth proper schemes and for which the Berthelot comparison theorem (
3.1
) holds. Indeed by
[
Vez22
, Remark 4.5]
there is a natural monoidal transformation (denoted by
\alpha
in
loc. cit.
) between the crystalline and de Rham realization functors. In particular the subcategory of
\mathrm{DM}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{p}})
on which the transformation is invertible is indeed a tensor triangulated subcategory. The fact that it contains motives of smooth proper schemes is again in
[
Vez22
, Remark 4.5]
.

Notation 9.2
.

Consider from now on

a sub-triangulated category endowed with a

-structure for which the realization functors are

-exact.
Write

for the heart and suppose that the realization functors are fiber functors making

a tannakian category.
Suppose moreover that the restriction of
\mathcal{A}
to the special fiber forms a semisimple abelian category.

Remark 9.3
.

An example of

as in

[

]
.
It is conjectured that the whole

Example 9.6

(Based on

and
[

, Examples 2 and 3]
)
Let

be a rational number and

be the Kummer motive
K_{a}=H^{1}(\mathbb{G}_{m},\{1,a\})
.
When
a\neq 0,1,-1
, it fits in an exact sequence

which is not split as these extensions are classified by
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
.

This motive has an algebraic class whereas its dual
K_{a}^{\vee}
does not have algebraic classes (otherwise the exact sequence would split).
For this reason one expects the
p
-adic periods of

to be interesting numbers.
In what follows we compute them and we make a comparison with the classical ones
4
4

4

Here we will write the Betti cohomology with respect to the de Rham cohomology (and not the inverse).
This seems the best analog to
Definition

5.1
.
Notice that this is the convention of Deligne and it is the inverse of the one of André
[
And04
, Remark 23.1.1.1]
.
.

The matrix of complex periods is

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{C}}(K^{\vee}_{a}):=\begin{pmatrix}2\pi i&\log(a)\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

where we chose bases for Betti and de Rham realizations adapted to the weight and the Hodge filtrations.

Let us now compute the
p
-adic periods.
(To have a prime of good reduction,
a
must be invertible modulo
p

First, the specialization
\spe(K^{\vee}_{a})
splits canonically

\spe(K^{\vee}_{a})=\mathbbm{1}(1)\oplus\mathbbm{1}

hence the space of algebraic classes

Z_{p}(\spe(K^{\vee}_{a}))=\Hom(\mathbbm{1},\spe(K^{\vee}_{a}))=\Hom(\spe(K_{a}),\mathbbm{1})

has dimension one and has a canonical generator given by

(0,\id)
. This corresponds to the unique map
f:\spe(K_{a})\rightarrow\mathbbm{1}
that is Frobenius equivariant and is the identity when restricted to
\mathbbm{1}
.

On the other hand, the action of the absolute Frobenius on crystalline cohomology can be computed.
Its matrix (with respect to the same basis of the de Rham cohomology used above) is

\varphi(K_{a}):=\begin{pmatrix}1&\log(a^{1-p})\\
0&p\end{pmatrix}.

This means that the unique map
f
written in the same basis is

f=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\log(a^{1-p})}{1-p}\end{pmatrix}.

By dualizing back (and reordering the basis with respect to the weight filtration) one gets the
p
-adic periods

\mathcal{P}_{p}(K^{\vee}_{a})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\log(a^{1-p})}{1-p}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

It is amusing to notice that the
p
-adic logarithm is not defined at

in general (except if

is
1
modulo

) so that the

-adic number
\frac{\log(a^{1-p})}{1-p}
is the best analog of the complex number
\log(a)
; i.e., passing from
\varphi(K_{a})
to
\mathcal{P}_{p}(K^{\vee}_{a})
corrects the entries and gives the "right"

-adic period. (The correction from
\log(a^{1-p})
to
\frac{\log(a^{1-p})}{1-p}
might also be obtained by an ad hoc change of basis, but recall that the above computations are made with respect to a canonical basis.)

Finally, the groups

and

are respectively
\mathbb{G}_{a}\rtimes\mathbb{G}_{m}
and
\mathbb{G}_{m}
. In particular the
p
-adic Grothendieck period conjecture predicts that the transcendence degree of the
p
-adic periods of

is

, i.e., that

is transcendental. This is the only non trivial case that we are aware of where the conjecture is actually known as it is follows from a theorem of Mahler

(see also Bertrand

Remark 9.7
.

(Pure vs mixed periods.)
In the conjectural abelian category of mixed motives, any motive is expected to have a weight filtration which should split over finite fields.
In particular, there should be plenty of non trivial extensions

0\rightarrow N\rightarrow M\rightarrow\mathbbm{1}\rightarrow 0

(with
N
being a motive of negative weight) that split when reduced modulo
p
.
This leads to algebraic classes modulo
p
that are not liftable, just as the example above, hence they provide a source of non trivial
p
-adic periods.
In the same spirit as the Kummer example, they are related to the coefficients of the matrix of the absolute Frobenius acting on crystalline cohomology.

Let us give another concrete example along these lines. Let
E
be an elliptic curve over
\mathbb{Q}
,
O
be its origin and
P
be a rational non torsion point. The localization triangle in the triangulated category of motives gives a non trivial extension that should correspond in the abelian category of motives to a short exact sequence

0\rightarrow\mathbbm{1}\rightarrow H^{1}(E,\{O,P\})\rightarrow H^{1}(E)\rightarrow 0,

which we dualize

0\rightarrow H^{1}(E)^{\vee}\rightarrow H^{1}(E,\{O,P\})^{\vee}\rightarrow\mathbbm{1}\rightarrow 0,

to have the suited form as above (with
N=H^{1}(E)^{\vee}
and
M=H^{1}(E,\{O,P\})^{\vee}).

The Frobenius matrix is of the shape

\varphi(H^{1}(E,\{O,P\})^{\vee})=\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\
d&e&f\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

and the
p
-adic period matrix is a vector of the shape

\mathcal{P}_{p}(H^{1}(E,\{O,P\})^{\vee})=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

The relation between the two is given by

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
d&e\par\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\par\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}c\\
f\par\end{pmatrix}.

The general picture would be the following. Let
M
be a motive and, for all integers
n
, let
M_{n}
be its associated
n
-th graded piece for the weight filtration.
As all extensions split modulo
p
, one has

\spe(M)=\bigoplus_{n}\spe(M_{n}).

Moreover, for weight reasons, one has
Z_{p}(\spe(M))=Z_{p}(\spe(M_{0}))
.

Fix a basis for each
R_{\mathrm{dR}}(M_{n})
and a splitting of the weight filtration of
R_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)
and consider the induced basis of
R_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)
.
For a given cycle
\gamma\in Z_{p}(\spe(M))
, its coordinates with respect to this basis have the form

\mathcal{P}_{p}(\gamma)=\begin{pmatrix}\vdots\\
v_{n}\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}

where
v_{n}
is a vector living in weight
n
.

Notice that the coordinates of
v_{0}
are the pure
p
-adic periods of
\gamma
seen as an element in
Z_{p}(\spe(M_{0}))
and the total vector

\mathcal{P}_{p}(\gamma)

is the only Frobenius invariant vector whose zeroth coordinate is precisely
v_{0}
.

In particular, mixed
p
-adic periods are generated by pure
p
-adic periods and the coefficients of the matrix of the Frobenius. (Recall also that there are more pure
p
-adic periods than just the coefficients of the Frobenius matrix; see
Remarks

5.17
and
5.16
.)

Remark 9.8
.

(Frobenius matrix vs
p
-adic periods.)
As the above remarks and examples explained, the coefficients of the Frobenius matrix are intimately related to André’s
p
-adic periods. Some authors, especially in the context of mixed Tate motives, usually call
p
-adic periods those coefficients
[
Fur07
,
Bro17
]
. Let us explain why André’s
p
-adic periods are actually better
p
-adic analogues of classical complex periods.

First,
Example

9.6
shows that the
p
-adic numbers appearing as André’s
p
-adic periods have a more striking analogy with the corresponding complex ones than the coefficients of the Frobenius have.

Second, recall that for motives other than mixed Tate motives there are more André
p
-adic periods than just the coefficients of the Frobenius matrix (
Remark

5.17
).

Third, and more important, the motivic interpretation of André’s
p
-adic periods seems to behave as in the complex case, contrary to the coefficients of the Frobenius. Indeed we expect that the evalutation map

\mathrm{eval}_{B}\colon\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{M})\to{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p}
is injective; see Proposition
8.4
. This is the analogous of the fact that the classical evalutation map for complex periods

\mathcal{O}(\Isom(R_{\mathrm{dR}},R_{B}))\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is expected to be injective. On the other hand the evaluation map

\mathcal{O}(\Aut(R_{\mathrm{dR}}))\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}_{p}
considered by
[
Fur07
,
Bro17
]

is well known to be non injective at least because the characteristic polynomial of the Frobenius has integral coefficients and this gives relations between the entries of the Frobenius that cannot be seen in
\Aut(R_{\mathrm{dR}})
. For example if one takes a non CM elliptic curve, the group
\Aut(R_{\mathrm{dR}})
has dimension
4
whereas the four coefficients of the Frobenius are related at least by trace and determinant; see
Example

5.14
. In our setting we do not know if
\mathrm{eval}_{B}\colon\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{M})\to{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}_{p}
is injective but we do know at least that the relations induced by the characteristic polynomial have motivic origins as shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 9.9
.

Let
M
be a motive and

be an endomorphism of its specialization. We will see

as an algebraic class in positive characteristic, more precisely as an element of

Suppose that the realization of

-adic periods

By definition of

Remark 9.10
.

(Continuing
Remark

9.8
.)
In the same vein as the above proposition and remark notice that our constructions give a much better bound to the transcendence degree of the coefficients of the Frobenius matrix than the classical ones. If
M
is a motive and

is the transcendence degree of the algebra generated by those coefficients, one gets

from the constructions in

whereas one gets

d_{M}\leq\dim G_{\mathrm{dR}}(M\otimes M^{\vee})-\dim G_{\mathrm{crys}}(M\otimes M^{\vee})\leq\\
\leq\dim G_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)-\dim G_{\mathrm{crys}}(M)\leq\dim G_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)

All the above inequalities are, in general, strict.

The last one is an equality when

f^{2}=b\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
and
h^{2}=a\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}\setminus\mathbb{Q}^{\times 2}
with the relation
fh=-hf
.

Consider the action of
D
on
T_{\ell}(E)
.
The minimal polynomial of
h
is
T^{2}-a
. We know that for half of the primes
\ell
this polynomial splits over
\mathbb{F}_{\ell}
and hence over
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
.
Let
\ell
be such a prime and let
v\in T_{\ell}(E)
be an eigenvector for
h
.
Due to the relation
fh=-hf
, the vector
f(v)

is also an eigenvector for
h
but for the other eigenvalue.
In particular
v,f(v)
is a
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
basis of
T_{\ell}(E)_{\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}}
.
In this basis, the action of
D
on
T_{\ell}(E)_{\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}}
is through the following matrices:

(10.2)

\displaystyle f\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&b\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle h\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&-\alpha\end{pmatrix}

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
is a square root of
a
, hence an algebraic number.

We now claim that there exists
\sigma
(and a suitable
\ell
) such that
v
lives inside the lattice of singular cohomology
\Lambda
(and hence
f(v)
does as well).
This will conclude the proof as the algebra
D
will act on
T_{\ell}(E)
with respect to a basis of
\Lambda
through algebraic numbers (more precisely, numbers in
\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{a}])
.

To show the claim, we argue as in the proof of
Proposition

10.10
.
Fix a
\sigma
and consider the identification
T_{\ell}(E)=\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
.
We want to modify this
\sigma
to obtain the one with the desired property using the group
G=\Gal(K)
and the relation (
10.1
).

Recall that
T_{\ell}(E)
is a free module of rank one over
F\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
and that, moreover,
for all but finitely many
\ell
, the group
G=\Gal(K)
acts on
T_{\ell}(E)
through
(F\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{\ell})^{\times}
(this is due to Deuring; see
[
Ser72
, §4.5]
and the references therein).
For such an
\ell
, there will be an element of
G
that sends the vector
v
to an element of
\Lambda
.
∎

Remark 10.12
.

In conclusion, despite some positive results, it does not seem reasonable to expect the existence of an
\ell
-adic analogue of the Grothendieck period conjecture, or at least one independent of the embedding
\sigma:{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
as in Questions
10.2
and
10.3
. Following the spirit of
[
And90
, Proposition 2]
, it seems reasonable to expect that for a generic choice of
\sigma
these questions should have positive anwser and that they should be consequences of the Mumford–Tate conjecture.
