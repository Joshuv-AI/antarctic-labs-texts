Conditional-mean Multiplicative Operator Models for Count Time Series
Christian H. Weiß, Fukang Zhu

Abstract
Multiplicative error models (MEMs) are commonly used for real-valued time series, but they cannot be applied to discrete-valued count time series as the involved multiplication would not preserve the integer nature of the data. Thus, the concept of a multiplicative operator for counts is proposed (as well as several specific instances thereof), which are then used to develop a kind of MEMs for count time series (CMEMs). If equipped with a linear conditional mean, the resulting CMEMs are closely related to the class of so-called integer-valued generalized autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (INGARCH) models and might be used as a semi-parametric extension thereof. Important stochastic properties of different types of INGARCH-CMEM as well as relevant estimation approaches are derived, namely types of quasi-maximum likelihood and weighted least squares estimation. The performance and application are demonstrated with simulations as well as with two real-world data examples. Key words: Count time series; INGARCH models; multiplicative error model; multiplicative operator; semi-parametric estimation.



1
Introduction

Since their introduction by
Engle 2002
, the
multiplicative error models
(MEMs) received considerable research interest in the literature, see
Brownlees et al. 2012
,
Cipollini and Gallo 2023
, and the references therein. MEMs are defined for a positively real-valued process
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots\}}
; let
\mathcal{F}_{s}
denote the information being available up to time
s
. Then,
(Y_{t})
follows a MEM if it satisfies the equation

Y_{t}\ =\ \mu_{t}\cdot\varepsilon_{t},

(1.1)

where
\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is a deterministic, truly positive number, and where
\varepsilon_{t}
is a positively real-valued random variable that is generated independently of
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
with (conditional) mean
E[\varepsilon_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=E[\varepsilon_{t}]=1
and variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}:=V[\varepsilon_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=V[\varepsilon_{t}]\in(0,\infty)
. The observations
(Y_{t})
of the MEM satisfy

E[Y_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ =\ \mu_{t},\qquad V[Y_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\sigma_{t}^{2}\,\mu_{t}^{2}.

(1.2)

If
(\varepsilon_{t})
are i. i. d. with mean 1 and variance
\sigma^{2}
, and if the conditional means
(\mu_{t})
satisfy the equation

\mu_{t}\ =a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\,Y_{t-i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\,\mu_{t-j},

(1.3)

then the resulting MEM is said to be an autoregressive conditional duration (ACD) model of order
(p,q)
, see
Engle & Russell 1998
. Here, the constraints
a_{0}>0
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{p},b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}\geq 0
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}<1
have to be satisfied to ensure the existence of a truly positive conditional mean.
The construction (
1.1
) is also closely related to that of the generalized autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (GARCH) models
(
Bollerslev 1986
)
.

The conditional-mean equation (
1.3
) of the ACD model gave rise to
Heinen 2003
to define an integer-valued counterpart for a count process
(X_{t})
, i. e., where the
X_{t}
are (unbounded) count random variables with range
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
. With
M_{t}=E[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
denoting the conditional mean (thus, now,
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{(X_{t-1},M_{t-1}),(X_{t-2},M_{t-2}),\ldots\}
),
Heinen 2003
considered the model equation

M_{t}\ =\ a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\,X_{t-i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\,M_{t-j},

(1.4)

where
a_{0}>0,a_{1},\ldots,a_{p}\geq 0,b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}\geq 0
, and

X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is generated by a conditional count distribution having mean
M_{t}
, such as the Poisson (Poi) distribution
\textup{Poi}(M_{t})
.
Heinen 2003
referred to this model as the autoregressive conditional Poisson (ACP) model, while
Ferland et al. 2006
used the term integer-valued GARCH (INGARCH) model. Although there is still a debate about the appropriate terminology for models of the type (
1.4
), see Remark 4.1.2 in
Weiß 2018
, it seems that the name INGARCH model is most often used in the literature. Thus, in the sequel, we shall also refer to count models satisfying (
1.4
) as INGARCH-type models.

In this article, our aim is to define MEM-type models for count time series, but not starting at (
1.3
) as done by
Heinen 2003
, but one step earlier right with the original definition (
1.1
) of a MEM. But since the multiplication
\alpha\cdot\epsilon
of a count random variable
\epsilon
by a positive real number
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
does usually not lead to a count value anymore
(“multiplication problem”, see
Weiß 2018
, p. 16)
, an integer-valued substitute of the multiplication is required. This multiplication problem is well known from the adaption of the autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) model to the count-data case. The so-called integer-valued ARMA (INARMA) model modifies the ordinary ARMA recursion by substituting its multiplications by so-called “thinning operators” (plus several further assumptions, see
Weiß 2018
for details).
During the last decades, a huge number of different types of thinning operators have been proposed in the literature, see
Weiß 2008
and
Scotto et al. 2015
for surveys. Generally, a thinning operator “
thinn
” is a random operator from
\mathbb{N}_{0}
to
\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
whose conditional mean satisfies
E[\textup{thinn}(\epsilon)\ |\ \epsilon]\leq\epsilon
. Many common thinning operators are defined in an additive way and fall within the family of
generalized thinning
operators proposed by
Latour 1998
:

\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon\ :=\ \sum_{j=1}^{\epsilon}\ Z_{j},

(1.5)

where the
Z_{j}
are i. i. d. count random variables (
counting series
, generated independently of
\epsilon
) with mean
\alpha\in(0,1)
and variance
\nu>0
. As a result,

E[\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon\ |\ \epsilon]=\alpha\cdot\epsilon\ \leq\epsilon
, which justifies its use as an integer-valued substitute of the multiplication.

1.1 Example

Popular examples of generalized thinning are

(i)

binomial thinning
dating back to
Steutel & van Harn 1979
(and commonly denoted by “
\alpha\circ
”), where the
Z_{j}
are Bernoulli random variables,
Z_{j}\sim\textup{Bin}(1,\alpha)
(thus
\nu=\nu(\alpha)=\alpha(1-\alpha)
), such that
\alpha\circ\epsilon|\epsilon\ \sim\textup{Bin}(\epsilon,\alpha)
with range
\{0,\ldots,\epsilon\}
;

(ii)

negative-binomial (NB) thinning
proposed by
Ristić et al. 2009
(and commonly denoted by “
\alpha*
”), where the
Z_{j}
are geometrically distributed with parameter
\pi:=1/(1+\alpha)
,
Z_{j}\sim\textup{NB}(1,\pi)
(hence
E[Z_{j}]=\alpha
and
\nu(\alpha)=\alpha(1+\alpha)
), such that
\alpha*\epsilon|\epsilon\ \sim\textup{NB}\big(\epsilon,1/(1+\alpha)\big)
with range
\mathbb{N}_{0}
;

(iii)

Poisson thinning

(
Scotto et al. 2015
)
with
Z_{j}\sim\textup{Poi}(\alpha)
(thus
\nu(\alpha)=\alpha
) such that
\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon|\epsilon\ \sim\textup{Poi}(\epsilon\cdot\alpha)
with range
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

In this article, we propose multiplicative operators for counts, which arise, for example, by generalizing operator (
1.5
). Section
2
introduces new multiplicative operators and discusses their basic properties.
Using such multiplicative operator, we adapt the MEM (
1.1
) to count time series, leading to a
count MEM
(CMEM). In particular, we propose CMEMs under the framework of INGARCH model, referred to as INGARCH-CMEMs.
Two specific cases are studied in Section
3
, namely CMEMs based on the compounding operator and the binomial multiplicative operator, respectively. Stationarity and ergodicity of the new models are established, and some probabilistic properties are also discussed. Note that our novel INGARCH-CMEMs differ from the thinning-based MEM adaption recently proposed by
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
, see Remark
3.1.1
for details.
In Section
4
, two kinds of semi-parametric estimation methods are considered: quasi-maximum likelihood estimation (QMLE) and two-stage weighted least squares estimation (2SWLSE).
The finite-sample performance of these methods is investigated by simulations in Section
5
.
Section
6
then illustrates the application of our novel CMEMs to two real-world data examples, namely to count time series referring to infectious diseases and financial transactions, respectively.
Finally, Section
7
concludes the article and outlines topics for future research.

2
Multiplicative Operators for Counts

For being able to adapt (
1.1
) in full generality, the restriction
E[\textup{thinn}(\epsilon)\ |\ \epsilon]\leq\epsilon
being inherent to any thinning operator is too limiting, also the case “
>\epsilon
” (i. e., also a “thickening”) should be permitted.
Thus, any operator “
\alpha\odot:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
” satisfying

\textstyle E[\alpha\odot\epsilon\ |\ \epsilon]\ =\ \alpha\cdot\epsilon\qquad\text{for all }\alpha>0

(2.1)

is referred to as an integer-valued
multiplicative operator
.
Note that the multiplicative property refers to the conditional mean of
\alpha\odot\epsilon
, but to keep it simple, we use the terminology “multiplicative operator” for “
\alpha\odot
”.
By (
2.1
), also
E[\alpha\odot\epsilon]=\alpha\,E[\epsilon]
is satisfied. To compute the (conditional) variance of
\alpha\odot\epsilon
, further assumptions on “
\alpha\odot
” are necessary. In any of the subsequent special cases, also the conditional variance of
\alpha\odot\epsilon|\epsilon
is proportional to
\epsilon
, with the constant of proportionality given by some
\nu=\nu(\alpha)>0
. In this special case, we immediately obtain

V[\alpha\odot\epsilon\ |\ \epsilon]\ =\ \nu\cdot\epsilon,\qquad V[\alpha\odot\epsilon]\ =\ \nu\,E[\epsilon]+\alpha^{2}\,V[\epsilon].

(2.2)

While moment calculations can be done solely based on (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), likelihood calculations or the simulation of “
\alpha\odot
” require to fully specify the conditional distribution of
\alpha\odot\epsilon|\epsilon
. This is done in the subsequent special cases.

The requirements imposed by (
2.1
) are easily achieved with an operator of the form (
1.5
), namely by requiring that
\alpha
can take any value in
(0,\infty)
. To not confuse the terminology, we refer to

\begin{array}[]{@{}l}\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon\ :=\ \sum_{j=1}^{\epsilon}\ Z_{j}\qquad\text{with i.\,i.\,d.\ counting series }(Z_{j}),\\[4.30554pt]
\text{which has arbitrary mean~$\alpha>0$ and variance~$\nu>0$},\end{array}

(2.3)

as a
compounding operator
.
Important (conditional) moments of “
\alpha\bullet_{\nu}
” are

\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\qquad}l}E[\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon\ |\ \epsilon]\ =\ \alpha\cdot\epsilon,&V[\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon\ |\ \epsilon]\ =\ \nu\cdot\epsilon,\qquad E\big[(\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon)^{2}\ \big|\ \epsilon\big]\ =\ \nu\,\epsilon+\alpha^{2}\,\epsilon^{2},\\[4.30554pt]
E[\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon]\ =\ \alpha\,E[\epsilon],&V[\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon]\ =\ \nu\,E[\epsilon]+\alpha^{2}\,V[\epsilon],\end{array}

(2.4)

see (
2.2
). Also the probability generating function (pgf) of (
2.3
) is easily expressed. Denoting the pgf of the counting series by
\textup{pgf}_{Z}(u):=E[u^{Z}]
, and that of
\epsilon
by
\textup{pgf}_{\epsilon}(u)
, it holds that

\textup{pgf}_{\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon}(u)\ =\ \textup{pgf}_{\epsilon}\big(\textup{pgf}_{Z}(u)\big).

(2.5)

Thus, the distribution of
\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon
can be expressed as the convolution

P(\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon=k)\ =\ \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}P(\epsilon=l)\cdot P(\alpha\bullet_{\nu}l=k).

(2.6)

Obviously, a compounding operator with Poisson or geometric counting series (recall Example
1.1
) is still well-defined, with

\alpha\bullet_{\nu}l\ \sim\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\textup{Poi}(l\,\alpha)&\text{if }Z_{j}\sim\textup{Poi}(\alpha),\\[2.15277pt]
\textup{NB}\big(l,1/(1+\alpha)\big)&\text{if }Z_{j}\sim\textup{NB}\big(1,1/(1+\alpha)\big).\\
\end{array}\right.

However, a Bernoulli counting series with
\alpha>1
does not exist, and the extension of the binomial thinning operator “
\alpha\circ
” to a full multiplicative operator is not obvious. In what follows, we propose a generalized definition of the “
\alpha\circ
” operator, which contains both thinning (if
\alpha<1
) and thickening (if
\alpha>1
):

\alpha\otimes\epsilon=\ \lfloor\alpha\rfloor\cdot\epsilon\ +\ \textup{Bin}\big(\epsilon,\ \alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\big).

(2.7)

Here,
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
denotes the greatest integer less than or equal to
\alpha
,
\textup{Bin}\big(\epsilon,\ \alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\big)
expresses a count random variable being generated according to this (conditional) distribution, and the two terms in the above addition are conditionally independent.
The operator
\otimes
in (
2.7
) is referred to as the
binomial multiplicative operator
, and it differs from the (purely deterministic and non-multiplicative) rounding operator of
Kachour and Yao 2009
.
Note that if
\alpha>1
, it is guaranteed that
\alpha\otimes\epsilon
gives a value
\geq\epsilon
, which differs from the compounding operator
\alpha\bullet_{\nu}
with
\alpha>1
.
\alpha=1
corresponds to a (non-random) preservation of
\epsilon
.
Further stochastic properties of the binomial multiplicative operator are as follows.

2.1 Proposition

Operator (
2.7
) has the conditional range
\big\{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\,\epsilon,\ldots,(\lfloor\alpha\rfloor+1)\,\epsilon\big\}
, the conditional moments

E[\alpha\otimes\epsilon\ |\ \epsilon]\ =\ \alpha\cdot\epsilon,\qquad V[\alpha\otimes\epsilon\ |\ \epsilon]\ =\ (\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)(1-\alpha+\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)\,\epsilon\ =:\nu(\alpha)\,\epsilon,

the unconditional moments

E[\alpha\otimes\epsilon]\ =\ \alpha\cdot E[\epsilon],\qquad V[\alpha\otimes\epsilon]\ =\ \alpha^{2}\cdot V[\epsilon]\ +\ (\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)(1-\alpha+\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)\cdot E[\epsilon],

and the pgf

\textup{pgf}_{\alpha\otimes\epsilon}(u)\ =\ \textup{pgf}_{\epsilon}\Big(u^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}\,\big(1-(\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)+(\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)\,u\big)\Big).

Proof.

The conditional mean of the operator is

\displaystyle E[\alpha\otimes\epsilon\ |\ \epsilon]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\big[\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\,\epsilon\ |\ \epsilon]\ +\ E\big[\textup{Bin}\big(\epsilon,\ \alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\big)\ |\ \epsilon\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\,\epsilon\ +\ (\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)\,\epsilon\ =\ \alpha\cdot\epsilon.

The conditional variance is

\displaystyle V[\alpha\otimes\epsilon\ |\ \epsilon]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V\big[\underbrace{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\,\epsilon}_{\text{(*)}}\ +\ \textup{Bin}\big(\epsilon,\ \alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\big)\ |\ \epsilon\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V\big[\textup{Bin}\big(\epsilon,\ \alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\big)\ |\ \epsilon\big]\ =\ (\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)(1-\alpha+\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)\,\epsilon,

where at (*), we used that
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\,\epsilon\big|\epsilon
is conditionally constant. So the unconditional moments immediately follow from (
2.2
).
The pgf is

\displaystyle\textup{pgf}_{\alpha\otimes\epsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\big[u^{\alpha\otimes\epsilon}\big]\ =\ E\Big[E\big[u^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\cdot\epsilon\ +\ \textup{Bin}(\epsilon,\ \alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)}\ \big|\ \epsilon\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\Big[u^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\cdot\epsilon}\cdot E\big[u^{\textup{Bin}(\epsilon,\ \alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)}\ \big|\ \epsilon\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\Big[u^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor\cdot\epsilon}\cdot\big(1-(\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)+(\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)\,u\big)^{\epsilon}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textup{pgf}_{\epsilon}\Big(u^{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}\,\big(1-(\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)+(\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)\,u\big)\Big),

which completes the proof of Proposition
2.1
.
∎

While our main interest is in CMEMs based on the compounding operator (
2.3
) and on the binomial multiplicative operator (
2.7
), respectively, let us conclude Section
2
by pointing out that multiplicative operators do not need to be of such an additive structure. Actually, any count distribution with closed-form mean might give rise to a multiplicative operator. For illustration, let us take the zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) distribution
\textup{ZIP}(\lambda,\omega)
with
\lambda>0
and
\omega\in(0,1)
as the starting point. Its mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
are given by the relations
\mu=(1-\omega)\,\lambda
and
\sigma^{2}/\mu=\lambda-\mu+1
. Thus, a ZIP-multiplicative operator “
\alpha\varoast_{\kappa}
” with
\alpha>0
and
\kappa>1
might be defined by requiring that
\alpha\varoast_{\kappa}\epsilon|\epsilon
is conditionally ZIP-distributed with mean
E[\alpha\varoast_{\kappa}\epsilon\,|\,\epsilon]=\alpha\,\epsilon
and variance
V[\alpha\varoast_{\kappa}\epsilon\,|\,\epsilon]=\kappa\cdot E[\alpha\varoast_{\kappa}\epsilon\,|\,\epsilon]
, i. e., with conditional ZIP-parameters
\lambda=\alpha\,\epsilon+\kappa-1
and
\omega=(\kappa-1)/(\alpha\,\epsilon+\kappa-1)
. Note that (
2.2
) also applies to “
\alpha\varoast_{\kappa}
”.

3
MEMs for Count Time Series

Inspired by Equation (
1.1
), a CMEM for a process
(X_{t})
consisting of unbounded counts is defined by

X_{t}\ =\ M_{t}\odot\epsilon_{t},

(3.1)

where
M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is a deterministic truly positive number.
(\epsilon_{t})
is i. i. d., and
\epsilon_{t}
is a count random variable that is generated independently of
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
with mean
E[\epsilon_{t}]=1
and variance
V[\epsilon_{t}]=\sigma^{2}
. The multiplicative operator “
\odot
” defined in (
2.1
) is executed independently of
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
and
\epsilon_{t}
.

With these assumptions, the conditional mean of (
3.1
) satisfies

\begin{array}[]{@{}rl}E[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ =&E\big[E[M_{t}\odot\epsilon_{t}\ |\ \epsilon_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\\[4.30554pt]
=&E[M_{t}\cdot\epsilon_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ =\ M_{t}\cdot 1\ =\ M_{t}.\end{array}

(3.2)

Thus, if the conditional mean
M_{t}
follows a recursive scheme of the form

M_{t}\ =\ f(X_{t-1},\ldots,X_{t-p},\ M_{t-1},\ldots,M_{t-q})\quad\text{with }p\geq 1,\ q\geq 0,

(3.3)

then the CMEM becomes an INGARCH-type model of order
(p,q)
, denoted as
INGARCH
(p,q)
-CMEM
.
The most well-known special case of (
3.3
) is the linear specification (
1.4
).
But also non-linear mean specifications are possible for
M_{t}
, e. g., by adapting the log-linear structure discussed by
Fokianos & Tjøstheim 2011
or the approximately linear softplus structure proposed by
Weiß et al. 2022
, both allowing for negative model parameters (also see Section
5
).

3.1 Remark

One of the formal advantages of the proposed model is that it is defined through a stochastic equation and not by a conditional distribution. In particular, the standard Poi-INGARCH model of
Ferland et al. 2006
is nested in this model, see Section
3.1
for details, and is thus defined by an equation instead of a conditional distribution.

3.2 Remark

We have already mentioned some specific operators before. An important class of operators defined by (2.1) is given by a hierarchical mixture of INGARCHs. We already indicated that the negative binomial INGARCH is a specific case of our model. Indeed, the mixed Poisson autoregression
\{Y_{t},t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
given by

Y_{t}=N_{t}(\varepsilon_{t}M_{t}),~~M_{t}=h(Y_{t-1},M_{t-1}),~~t\in\mathbb{Z},

is a specific case of (
3.1
), where
(N_{t})
is an independent sequence of homogeneous Poisson processes with unit intensity. More generally, any hierarchical mixture process given by

\displaystyle Y_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\varepsilon_{t}

\displaystyle\sim F_{M_{t},\varepsilon_{t}},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{t}

\displaystyle\sim F_{\varepsilon_{t}},

(
F_{M_{t},\varepsilon_{t}}
and
F_{\varepsilon_{t}}
being given distributions) is a particular instance of model (2.1). So

f_{Y_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(y)=\sum_{\varepsilon}f_{M_{t},\varepsilon_{t}}(y,\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon_{t}}(\varepsilon)

is a finite or infinite mixture of distributions over
\varepsilon_{t}
depending on whether the range of
\varepsilon_{t}
is finite or infinite. In this sense, the proposed model (
3.1
) is deeply related to a mixture of INGARCH representations, see
Zhu et al. 2010
,
Mao et al. 2020
,
Doukhan et al. 2021
, among others.

In the special case, where the multiplicative operator “
\odot
” also satisfies
V[\alpha\odot\epsilon\,|\,\epsilon]=\nu(\alpha)\cdot\epsilon
, we conclude from (
2.2
) that

\begin{array}[]{@{}rl}V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ =&E\big[V[M_{t}\odot\epsilon_{t}\ |\ \epsilon_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]+V\big[E[M_{t}\odot\epsilon_{t}\ |\ \epsilon_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\\[4.30554pt]
=&E[\nu(M_{t})\cdot\epsilon_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]+V[M_{t}\cdot\epsilon_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ =\ \nu(M_{t})+\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2}.\end{array}

(3.4)

So the conditional variance is caused by two sources: the variance of the operator “
\odot
” and the one of the innovation term
\epsilon_{t}
.

To establish the existence of an INGARCH
(p,q)
-CMEM, one can benefit from the existing INGARCH literature. To apply Theorem 2.1 of
Doukhan and Neumann 2019
, for example, one has to ensure that on the one hand, assumptions (A
1^{\prime}
) and (A2) of
Doukhan and Neumann 2019
hold for the given INGARCH specification (
3.3
), and on the other hand, certain distributional requirements for the multiplicative operator “
\odot
” have to be satisfied. More precisely, if also the conditional distribution of
X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}=M_{t}\odot\epsilon_{t}|M_{t}=:Q(M_{t})
satisfies (A3) of
Doukhan and Neumann 2019
, then their Theorem 2.1 can be used to conclude on the existence of a stationary solution to (
3.1
) and (
3.3
), as well as on mixing properties.
This, certainly, requires to first fix a specific choice of the multiplicative operator “
\odot
”.
In what follows, we discuss two kinds of CMEMs (
3.1
) with the linear specification (
1.4
), namely based on the compounding operator (
2.3
) and on the binomial multiplicative operator (
2.7
), respectively.

3.1
CMEMs Using a Compounding Operator

In this section, let us consider the special case where the CMEM (
3.1
) uses the compounding operator (
2.3
)
\alpha\bullet_{\nu}
with arbitrary
\alpha,\nu>0
as the multiplicative operator, i. e.,

X_{t}\ =\ M_{t}\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon_{t},

(3.5)

where
M_{t}
is given in (
1.4
) and
\epsilon_{t}
is the same one defined in (
3.1
).
Note that
X_{t}
can be written as
\sum_{j=1}^{\epsilon_{t}}Z_{tj}
, where
(Z_{tj})
are count random variables with
E[Z_{tj}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=M_{t}
, and
(\epsilon_{t})
are independent of
(Z_{tj})
. Given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1},Z_{sj}
are conditionally independent for
s\geq t
and different
j
. Furthermore, if
t
is fixed, then the
Z_{tj}
are conditionally i. i. d. with some
Z_{t}
for different
j
, and more discussions about
Z_{t}
will be given later in the context of Theorem
3.1.6
.

The formulae (
2.4
)–(
2.5
) are now used to derive properties of the compounding CMEM (
3.5
). While still
E[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=M_{t}
holds, we get

\begin{array}[]{@{}rl}E[X_{t}^{2}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ =&E\big[E[(M_{t}\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon_{t})^{2}\ |\ \epsilon_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\\[4.30554pt]
\overset{\eqref{CompoundingCMom}}{=}&E\big[\nu(M_{t})\,\epsilon_{t}+M_{t}^{2}\,\epsilon_{t}^{2}\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\ =\ \nu(M_{t})+(\sigma^{2}+1)M_{t}^{2},\end{array}

where
\nu=\nu(M_{t})
. Thus,

V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ =\ \nu(M_{t})+\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2},

(3.6)

also see (
3.4
).
In the special case of a Poi-counting series, we have
\nu(M_{t})=M_{t}
, whereas a NB-counting series leads to
\nu(M_{t})=M_{t}(1+M_{t})
, recall Example
1.1
.
Compared with an ordinary INGARCH model with conditional mean
M_{t}
, the main difference in conditional mean and variance between the CMEM (
3.5
) and the INGARCH model is as follows: while having the same conditional mean
M_{t}
, there is an additional term
\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2}
in the conditional variance of the CMEM, which makes the CMEM more flexible for applications in practice. Only in the boundary case where
\epsilon_{t}=1
deterministically (thus
\sigma^{2}=0
), the CMEM specification reduces to the ordinary INGARCH approach.
In particular, the special case
\epsilon_{t}\equiv 1
and Poi-counting series just agrees with the ordinary Poi-INGARCH model of
Ferland et al. 2006
.

3.1.1 Remark

Aknouche and Scotto 2023
proposed a multiplicative thinning-based INGARCH model defined by

X_{t}=\lambda_{t}\cdot\epsilon_{t},~~~\lambda_{t}=1+\omega\circ m+\sum_{i=1}^{q}\alpha_{i}\circ Y_{t-i}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{j}\circ\lambda_{t-j},

(3.7)

where
\epsilon_{t}
is given like in (
3.1
), and where
\circ
is the binomial thinning operator discussed in Example
1.1
(i). Model (
3.7
) has a similar mean structure as model (
3.5
), and the inclusion of “
\omega\circ
” makes model (
3.7
) analogous to (
3.5
).
But there are also substantial differences between both approaches.
First, the positive integer
m
in (
3.7
) is not treated as a parameter (for practice,
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
suggest to choose
m
as the integer part of the sample mean).
Second, the conditional variance of model (
3.7
) is a function of two recursive items, see equation (2.11) in
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
, while the conditional variance of our model (
3.1
) is just a function of
M_{t}
, see (
3.6
). The complexity of the conditional variance also affects the estimation method discussed in Section
4
.

The conditional pgf of the compounding CMEM (
3.5
) satisfies

\textup{pgf}_{X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(u)\ =\ E[u^{M_{t}\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon_{t}}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ \overset{\eqref{CompoundingPgf}}{=}\ \textup{pgf}_{\epsilon}\big(\textup{pgf}_{Z|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(u)\big),

(3.8)

where
Z|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
refers to the counting series of the operator “
M_{t}\bullet_{\nu}
” given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, recall (
3.5
).

3.1.2 Example

In the special case of Poisson-distributed innovations,
\epsilon_{t}\sim\textup{Poi}(1)
, (
3.8
) implies a conditional compound-Poisson (CP) distribution, namely
\textup{pgf}_{X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(u)=\exp\big(\textup{pgf}_{Z|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(u)-1\big)
. Thus, if
M_{t}
also satisfies (
1.4
), we get a type of CP-INGARCH model as discussed by
Gonçalves et al. 2015a
.
If, in addition, we have a Poisson-counting series
Z|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\sim\textup{Poi}(M_{t})
with
\textup{pgf}_{X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(u)=\exp\big(M_{t}\,(u-1)\big)
, (
3.8
) implies
\textup{pgf}_{X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(u)=\exp\big(\exp\big(M_{t}\,(u-1)\big)-1\big)
, i. e., we get a Neyman type-A (NTA) INGARCH model as discussed by
Gonçalves et al. 2015b
.

Another special case is
\epsilon_{t}\sim\textup{ZIP}(\frac{1}{1-\omega},\omega)
with
\omega\in(0,1)
, i. e., a ZIP distribution with mean 1 and
\textup{pgf}_{\epsilon}(u)=\omega+(1-\omega)\,\exp(\frac{u-1}{1-\omega})
, which leads to a zero-inflated CP-INGARCH model as discussed by
Gonçalves et al. 2016
. Generally, there are two possible reasons for the compounding operator
\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon=\sum_{j=1}^{\epsilon}Z_{j}
from (
2.3
) to become zero: either
\epsilon=0
, or
\epsilon>0
but all
Z_{1}=\ldots=Z_{\epsilon}=0
, i. e.,

\textstyle P(\alpha\bullet_{\nu}\epsilon=0)\ =\ P(\epsilon=0)\ +\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(\epsilon=i)\,P(Z=0)^{i}.

Thus, if
\epsilon_{t}\sim\textup{ZIP}(\frac{1}{1-\omega},\omega)
, we get

\begin{array}[]{@{}rl}P(X_{t}=0\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1})\ =&\omega\ +\ (1-\omega)\,{\rm e}^{-1/(1-\omega)}\,\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\omega)^{-i}}{i!}\,\textup{pgf}_{Z|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(0)^{i}\\[3.87498pt]
\ =&\omega\ +\ (1-\omega)\,\exp\Big(\frac{\textup{pgf}_{Z|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(0)-1}{1-\omega}\Big),\end{array}

in accordance with the expression for
\textup{pgf}_{X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(0)
.

The following proposition gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the first-order stationarity of the
model introduced in (
3.5
), which is analogous to the corresponding property of
Ferland et al. 2006
. The proof of Proposition
3.1.3
is done with the same arguments used in Proposition 1 of
Ferland et al. 2006
, so we omit the details.

3.1.3 Proposition

The process
(X_{t})
defined in (
3.5
) is first-order stationary if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}<1
.

As the next step, we discuss the second-order stationarity. We make the following assumption

\frac{V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]}{E[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]}=v_{0}+v_{1}M_{t},

(3.9)

with
v_{0}\geq 0
and
v_{1}\geq 0
not being simultaneously zero. It is obvious that the Poisson- and NB-counting series satisfy the above assumption, recall (
3.6
), leading to
v_{1}=\sigma^{2}
and
v_{1}=1+\sigma^{2}
, respectively.
Then, the results of Section 3.2 in
Gonçalves et al. 2015a
hold for the model (
3.5
). In particular, we have the following proposition.

3.1.4 Proposition

Consider a first-order stationary INGARCH
(1,1)
-CMEM defined according to (
3.5
), which also satisfies (
3.9
). A necessary and
sufficient condition for second-order stationarity is
(a_{1}+b_{1})^{2}+v_{1}a_{1}^{2}<1
.

For second-order stationarity of the INGARCH
(p,q)
-CMEM model with general order
(p,q)
, we refer to Theorem 4 in
Gonçalves et al. 2015a
, which will be not repeated here.

If
\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}<1
, we obtain the unconditional mean of
X_{t}
as

\mu:=E[X_{t}]=\frac{a_{0}}{1-\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}}.

(3.10)

Next, we derive the unconditional variance of
X_{t}
. From Theorem 1 in
Weiß 2009
, we know that
the autocovariances
\gamma_{X}(k):={\rm Cov}[X_{t},X_{t-k}]
and
\gamma_{M}(k):={\rm Cov}[M_{t},M_{t-k}]

satisfy the linear equations

\begin{array}[]{@{}l}\displaystyle\gamma_{X}(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\gamma_{X}(|k-i|)+\sum_{j=1}^{\min(k-1,q)}b_{j}\gamma_{X}(k-j)+\sum_{j=k}^{q}b_{j}\gamma_{M}(j-k),\\
\\[-8.61108pt]
\displaystyle\gamma_{M}(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{\min(k,p)}a_{i}\gamma_{M}(|k-i|)+\sum_{i=k+1}^{p}a_{i}\gamma_{X}(i-k)+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\gamma_{M}(|k-j|),\end{array}

(3.11)

for
k\geq 1
and
k\geq 0
, respectively. Then, using (
3.6
), the variance can be obtained from

V[X_{t}]=E[\nu(M_{t})+\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2}]+V[M_{t}]\ =\ E[\nu(M_{t})]+\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}+(\sigma^{2}+1)\,V[M_{t}],

(3.12)

where
V[M_{t}]=\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\gamma_{X}(i)+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\gamma_{M}(j)
.
For the Poisson-counting case, we have
\nu(M_{t})=M_{t}
, thus

V[X_{t}]=\mu+\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}+(\sigma^{2}+1)\,V[M_{t}],

(3.13)

while for the NB-counting case with
\nu(M_{t})=M_{t}(1+M_{t})
, we have

V[X_{t}]=E[M_{t}+M_{t}^{2}]+\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}+(\sigma^{2}+1)\,V[M_{t}]=\mu+\mu^{2}(\sigma^{2}+1)+(\sigma^{2}+2)\,V[M_{t}].

(3.14)

Let us illustrate the required computations for the case
p=q=1
, which is most common in applications.

3.1.5 Example

If
p=q=1
, we get from (
3.11
), after few calculations, that

\big(1-(a_{1}+b_{1})^{2}+a_{1}^{2}\big)\,V[M_{t}]=a_{1}^{2}\,V[X_{t}].

This can be used to obtain
V[X_{t}]
after substituting
V[M_{t}]
into (
3.13
) or (
3.14
), respectively. The autocorrelation function (ACF)
\rho(k)=Corr[X_{t},X_{t-k}]
takes the usual form
(
Weiß 2009
, see)

\rho(k)=(a_{1}+b_{1})^{k-1}\,\frac{a_{1}\,(1-b_{1}(a_{1}+b_{1}))}{1-(a_{1}+b_{1})^{2}+a_{1}^{2}}.

Together with (
3.10
), it can be utilized for deriving method-of-moment estimators.

In practice, equations (
3.11
) can be used together with the sample ACF for the identification of the model order
(p,q)
as well as for getting initial parameter estimates (method of moments). This is discussed in some more details in Sections
4.3
and
6
.

Before discussing the stationary properties of
(X_{t})
, we first recall the definition of the CP-INGARCH model given in
Gonçalves et al. 2015a
.
The count process
(Z_{t})
is said to satisfy a CP-INGARCH
(p,q)
model if the conditional characteristic function of
Z_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is
\Phi_{Z_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(u)=\exp\{{\rm i}M_{t}^{*}[\varphi_{t}(u)-1]/\varphi^{\prime}_{t}(0)\}
with
u\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, and if
M_{t}^{*}
is defined like in (
1.4
), i. e.,
M_{t}^{*}=a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}Z_{t-i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}M_{t-j}^{*}
.
Here,
(\varphi_{t})
is a family of characteristic functions on
\mathbb{R}
, and “i” denotes the imaginary unit.

The following theorem establishes the stationarity and ergodicity of the compounding CMEM
(X_{t})
if the counting series arises from the CP-family. Recall from (
3.5
) that
X_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{\epsilon_{t}}Z_{tj}
, where
(Z_{tj})
is supposed to be identically distributed with some
(Z_{t})
that satisfies
E[Z_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=E[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=M_{t}
.

3.1.6 Theorem

Assume that
(Z_{t})
satisfies a CP-INGARCH process.
If
\varphi_{t}
is deterministic and independent of
t
, and if
\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}<1
holds,
then the process
(X_{t})
defined according to the compounding CMEM (
3.5
) is stationary and ergodic.

Proof.

Using arguments in
Gonçalves et al. 2015a
, we know that
M_{t}
can be written as
M_{t}=a_{0}/\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\psi_{k}X_{t-k}
, where the coefficient
\psi_{k}
is given by the power expansion of a rational function in the neighbourhood of 0.
Based on the construction method for proving stationarity and ergodicity in
Ferland et al. 2006
and
Gonçalves et al. 2015a
,
let
M_{t}^{(n)}=a_{0}/\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\psi_{k}X_{t-k}
,
M_{t}^{\ast(n)}=a_{0}/\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\psi_{k}Z_{t-k}
,
r^{(n)}=E\big|(M_{t}^{(n)}-M_{t}^{\ast(n)})-(M_{t}^{(n-1)}-M_{t}^{\ast(n-1)})\big|
.
Note that
0\leq r^{(n)}=|\psi_{n}|\,E|X_{t-n}-Z_{t-n}|=|\psi_{n}|\,\big(E[(X_{t-n}-Z_{t-n})\,\mathds{1}(A_{n})]+E[(Z_{t-n}-X_{t-n})\,\mathds{1}(A_{n}^{c})]\big)\leq|\psi_{n}|\,\big(E(X_{t-n}-Z_{t-n})+E(Z_{t-n}-X_{t-n})\big)=0
,
where
A_{n}=\{X_{t-n}-Z_{t-n}>0\}
and
\mathds{1}(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function.
Thus,
r^{(n)}=0
. Therefore,
(M_{t}^{(n)}-M_{t}^{\ast(n)})
is a Cauchy sequence and thus
(M_{t}^{(n)}-M_{t}^{\ast(n)})
converges in
L^{1}
and a.s., i. e.,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(M_{t}^{(n)}-M_{t}^{\ast(n)})=0
a.s.
Using Theorem 5 in
Gonçalves et al. 2015a
, we know that
(Z_{t})
is stationary and ergodic.

Notice that
X_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{\epsilon_{t}}Z_{tj}
, and recall that
(Z_{tj})
is supposed to be identically distributed with
(Z_{t})
. Then, there exists a real function
\xi
, which does not depend on
t
, such that

X_{t}=\xi\big((Z_{tj},\epsilon_{t}),t\in\mathbb{Z},j\in\mathbb{N}\big)
.
Note that
\epsilon_{t}
is i. i. d. and independent of
\{Z_{tj}\}
, so the result follows by applying Theorem 36.4 in
Billingsley 1995
.
∎

We conclude Section
3.1
by pointing out that if the distribution of
\epsilon_{t}
is not further specified,
then the resulting INGARCH-CMEM can be understood as a kind of semi-parametric INGARCH model, in analogy to the semi-parametric INAR models proposed by
Drost et al. 2009
and
Liu et al. 2021
. Then, the conditional distribution of
X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is a convolution between the distribution of counting series and the one of
\epsilon_{t}
, recall (
2.6
). Numerically exact computations are possible if the range of
\epsilon_{t}
is assumed to be bounded, because then, (
2.6
) becomes a finite sum. Generally, denoting
p_{\epsilon,l}=P(\epsilon_{t}=l)
, the distribution of
\epsilon_{t}
is completely determined by
p_{\epsilon,2},p_{\epsilon,3},\ldots
with the constraint
\sum_{l\geq 2}l\cdot p_{\epsilon,l}<1
, where
p_{\epsilon,0}=\sum_{l\geq 2}(l-1)\cdot p_{\epsilon,l}
and
p_{\epsilon,1}=1-p_{\epsilon,0}-\sum_{l\geq 2}p_{\epsilon,l}
because of the requirement
E[\epsilon_{t}]=1
.

3.2
CMEMs Using a Binomial Multiplicative Operator

In this section, we consider the special case where the CMEM (
3.1
) uses the binomial multiplicative operator
\otimes
defined in (
2.7
) as the multiplicative operator, i. e.,

X_{t}\ =\ M_{t}\otimes\epsilon_{t},

(3.15)

where
M_{t}
is given in (
1.4
), and where
\epsilon_{t}
is defined like in (
3.1
).
Note that
X_{t}
can be expressed as
\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor\cdot\epsilon_{t}\ +\ \textup{Bin}\big(\epsilon_{t},\ M_{t}-\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor\big)
. As pointed out in Section
2
, the two terms in the above addition are independent.

Let us derive properties of the CMEM (
3.15
).
First, we note that the first-order stationarity given in Proposition
3.1.3
still holds here.
Note that
E[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=M_{t}
, and that the variance function
\nu(\alpha)=(\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)(1-\alpha+\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)
according to Proposition
2.1
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle E[X_{t}^{2}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\ =

\displaystyle\ E\Big[E\big[\big(\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor\cdot\epsilon_{t}\ +\ \textup{Bin}(\epsilon_{t},\ M_{t}-\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)\big)^{2}\ |\ \epsilon_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ E\Big[(M_{t}-\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)(1-\ M_{t}+\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)\epsilon_{t}+M_{t}^{2}\,\epsilon_{t}^{2}\ \big|\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\Big]

\displaystyle\ =

\displaystyle\ (M_{t}-\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)(1-\ M_{t}+\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)+(\sigma^{2}+1)M_{t}^{2},

\displaystyle V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=

\displaystyle\ (M_{t}-\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)(1-\ M_{t}+\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)+\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2},

(3.16)

also see (
3.4
).
It is easy to see that
\nu(\alpha)\in[0,0.25]
, so
\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2}\leq V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 0.25+\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2}
holds.
Similar to arguments in Section
3.1
,
if
\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}<1
, we obtain the unconditional mean of
X_{t}
as

\mu:=E[X_{t}]=\frac{a_{0}}{1-\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}}.

Then, the variance is given by

\displaystyle V[X_{t}]

\displaystyle=E\Big[(M_{t}-\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)(1-\ M_{t}+\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)+\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2}\Big]+V[M_{t}]

\displaystyle=E\big[(M_{t}-\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)(1-\ M_{t}+\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)\big]+\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}+(\sigma^{2}+1)\,V[M_{t}],

where
V[M_{t}]=\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\gamma_{X}(i)+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\gamma_{M}(j)
.
If
p=q=1
, for example, then like in Section
3.1
, we can insert
(1-(a_{1}+b_{1})^{2}+a_{1}^{2})\,V[M_{t}]=a_{1}^{2}\,V[X_{t}]
. However, as there is a floor operation in
V[X_{t}]
, we cannot obtain a closed-form expression for
V[X_{t}]
this time. But we have

\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}+(\sigma^{2}+1)V[M_{t}]\leq V[X_{t}]\leq 0.25+\mu^{2}\sigma^{2}+(\sigma^{2}+1)V[M_{t}]
, so we can approximate
V[X_{t}]
by using these bounds.

The following theorem establishes the stationarity and ergodicity of
(X_{t})
, which can be proven using the technique similar to that in
Agosto et al. 2016
.
Note that because of
E[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=M_{t}
, also
E[X_{t}]=E[M_{t}]
holds.

3.2.1 Theorem

If
\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}<1
holds, there exists a weakly dependent stationary and ergodic solution to (
3.15
).

Proof.

We first verify that the process
(X_{t})
satisfies the conditions of Theorem 3.1 in
Doukhan and Wintenberger 2008
from which the theorem will follow. To simplify the notation, assume without loss of generality
p=q
in the following. With
\beta(z)=1-\sum_{i=1}^{q}b_{i}z^{i},z\in\mathbb{C}
, the assumption
\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}<1
implies that
\beta(z)^{-1}=\psi(z):=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\psi_{i}z^{i}
is well-defined for
|z|\leq 1+\eta
with some
\eta>0
, where
\psi
is exponentially decreasing and defined recursively by
\psi_{0}=1
and
\psi_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\psi_{n-i}\geq 0
for
n\geq 1
. Next, with
\alpha(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{q}a_{i}z^{i-1}
, note that
M_{t}
defined in (
1.4
) can be rewritten in terms of the backshift operator
B
as
\beta(B)M_{t}=a_{0}+\alpha(B)X_{t-1}
. Then,

M_{t}=a_{0}/\beta(1)+\psi(B)\alpha(B)X_{t-1}=a_{0}/\beta(1)+\theta(B)X_{t-1},

where
\theta(z):=\psi(z)\alpha(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\theta_{i}z^{i}
with
\theta_{i}\geq 0
. Thus,
X_{t}
satisfies
X_{t}=F(X_{t-1},X_{t-2},\ldots;\eta_{t})
, where

\displaystyle F(X_{t-1},X_{t-2},\ldots;\eta_{t})

\displaystyle=\lfloor a_{0}/\beta(1)+\theta(B)X_{t-1}\rfloor\cdot\epsilon_{t}\

\displaystyle~~~+\ \textup{Bin}_{t}\big(\epsilon_{t},\ a_{0}/\beta(1)+\theta(B)X_{t-1}-\lfloor a_{0}/\beta(1)+\theta(B)X_{t-1}\rfloor\big).

Here,
\eta_{t}=(\epsilon_{t},\textup{Bin}_{t})^{\prime}
is an i. i. d. sequence with
\textup{Bin}_{t}
denoting a binomial random variable being executed at time
t
.

From
Klenke and Mattner 2010
, we know that the stochastic ordering
\textup{Bin}(n,p_{1})\leq_{\rm st}\textup{Bin}(n,p_{2})
holds if and only if
p_{1}\leq p_{2}
.
Denote
F_{m}
as the cumulative distribution function (cdf) of
\lfloor m\rfloor\cdot\epsilon\ +\ \textup{Bin}\big(\epsilon,\ m-\lfloor m\rfloor\big)
, with
m
being its expectation.
Let
F_{m}^{-1}(u):=\inf\{t\geq 0,F_{m}(t)\geq u\}
for
u\in[0,1]
. Let
U
be a uniform random variable on
(0,1)
, and define
Z_{i}=F_{m_{i}}^{-1}(U),i=1,2
. Then, using arguments similar to those in Lemma 4 of
Chen et al. 2022
, we have
E|Z_{1}-Z_{2}|=|m_{1}-m_{2}|
.

For any two deterministic sequences
(x_{t-i})_{i\geq 1}
and
(\tilde{x}_{t-i})_{i\geq 1}
, we can define
m_{t}
and
\tilde{m}_{t}
accordingly. Then, we obtain

\displaystyle E\big|F(x_{t-1},x_{t-2},\ldots;\eta_{t})-F(\tilde{x}_{t-1},\tilde{x}_{t-2},\ldots;\eta_{t})\big|

\displaystyle=E\Big|\lfloor m_{t}\rfloor\cdot\epsilon_{t}\ +\ \textup{Bin}(\epsilon_{t},\ m_{t}-\lfloor m_{t}\rfloor)-\big(\lfloor\tilde{m}_{t}\rfloor\cdot\epsilon_{t}\ +\ \textup{Bin}(\epsilon_{t},\ \tilde{m}_{t}-\lfloor\tilde{m}_{t}\rfloor)\big)\Big|

\displaystyle=|m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t}|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\theta_{i}|x_{t-1-i}-\tilde{x}_{t-1-i}|=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\theta_{i-1}|x_{t-i}-\tilde{x}_{t-i}|,

where we have used that
\theta_{i}\geq 0
. Observe that by the identity
\alpha(z)=\beta(z)\theta(z)
, it follows that
\theta(1)<1
if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}<1
.
This verifies the conditions of Theorem 3.1 in
Doukhan and Wintenberger 2008
, which, in turn, implies that
(X_{t})
is weakly dependent.
∎

4
Parameter Estimation and Model Diagnostics

In this section, we consider two kinds of estimation methods for the INGARCH-CMEMs discussed in Section
3
: QMLE and 2SWLSE, where the former has been discussed
by
Christou and Fokianos 2014
,
Ahmad and Francq 2016
and
Aknouche et al. 2018
, and the latter by
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
.
To implement these estimation approaches, the INGARCH structure as well as the multiplicative operator have to be specified, but not the distribution of
\epsilon_{t}
. Thus, QMLE and 2SWLSE approaches can be understood as semi-parametric estimation approaches.

Throughout this section, let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be observations from models (
3.5
) or (
3.15
), respectively, with true parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}:=(a_{00},a_{01},\ldots,a_{0p},b_{01},\ldots,b_{0q})^{\prime}
and
\sigma_{0}^{2}
. We assume
that
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}\in\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}\subset(0,\infty)\times[0,1)^{p+q}
and
\sigma_{0}^{2}\in\Delta\subset(0,\infty)
, where the latter condition ensures the non-degeneracy of
\epsilon_{t}
.

4.1
Quasi-Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Because models (
3.5
) and (
3.15
) have the same mean, the QMLE approach will yield the same estimate of the regression parameters, although observations come from different models.

4.1.1
QMLE of Regression Parameters

Christou and Fokianos 2014
considered Poisson QMLE for negative-binomial INGARCH models, and
Ahmad and Francq 2016
studied the asymptotic distribution of the estimator when the parameter belongs to the interior of the parameter space and when it lies at the boundary.

For any generic
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}:=(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{p},b_{1},\ldots,b_{q})^{\prime}\in\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}
, define

M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\ =\ a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\,X_{t-i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\,M_{t-j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}),~~~t\in\mathbb{Z},

(4.1)

where
M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})=M_{t}
.
Let
l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=X_{t}\log M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})-M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
, then the Poisson QMLE (PQ) of
\theta_{0}
is defined as

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{PQ}}=\arg\max_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}\in\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\Theta$}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}).

(4.2)

Under some regularity conditions, using techniques similar to those in Theorem 2 of
Christou and Fokianos 2014
,
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{PQ}}
can be proven to be consistent and asymptotically normal, i. e.,

\begin{array}[]{@{}l}\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{PQ}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}\textup{N}\big(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\ \mbox{{{G}}}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\mbox{{{G}}}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})\mbox{{{G}}}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\big),\quad\text{where}\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle\mbox{{{G}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=-E\left[\frac{\partial^{2}l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}}\right]=E\left[\frac{1}{M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}}\right],\\
\\[-8.61108pt]
\displaystyle\mbox{{{G}}}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\sigma^{2})=E\left[V\left[\frac{\partial l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\right]=E\left[\frac{v_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\sigma^{2})}{M_{t}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}}\right],\end{array}

where
v_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\sigma^{2}):=V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
.
In practice, maximization of (
4.2
) is implemented by assuming some (fixed or random) positive starting values
\tilde{M}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}),\ldots,\tilde{M}_{1-q}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}),X_{0},\ldots,X_{1-p}
. Let

\tilde{M}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
be an observable counterpart of
M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
, given by the recursion

\tilde{M}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}X_{t-i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\tilde{M}_{t-j}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}),~~~t\geq 1.

(4.3)

Then, we have an observable counterpart of
l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
as
\tilde{l}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=X_{t}\log\tilde{M}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})-\tilde{M}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
, and we can approximate
l_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
by
\tilde{l}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
.

Besides the Poisson QMLE, there are other versions of QMLE as well.
Aknouche et al. 2018
and
Aknouche and Francq 2021
discussed the negative-binomial QMLE and the exponential QMLE, respectively. As noted by
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
, the asymptotic efficiency of the QMLE depends on the model’s variance-to-mean relationship
V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=g(M_{t})
, where
g
is a positive real-valued function.
Thus, each one of the Poisson, negative-binomial, and exponential QMLE is asymptotically optimal whenever
g(x)=c_{1}x
,
g(x)=c_{2}x(1+c_{3}x)
, and
g(x)=c_{4}x^{2}
, respectively, for any
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}>0
.
From (
3.6
) and (
3.2
), we may conclude that negative-binomial and exponential QMLEs are asymptotically efficient for models (
3.5
) and (
3.15
), respectively. We should point out that they are not universally (asymptotically) efficient but only within the class of all QMLEs in the linear exponential family (see also
Wooldridge 1999
and the references therein).

For model (
3.5
), the negative-binomial QMLE (NQ) is defined as

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{NQ}}=\arg\max_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}\in\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\Theta$}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\Big\{X_{t}\log\tilde{M}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})-(r+X_{t})\log(r+\tilde{M}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}))\Big\}

for some fixed
r>0
(in their simulations,
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
used
r=1
throughout). Note that
Aknouche et al. 2018
also proposed another estimation approach, which consists of four stages estimating both conditional mean and dispersion parameters.

For model (
3.15
), the exponential QMLE (EQ) is defined as

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{EQ}}=\arg\max_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}\in\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\Theta$}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\Big\{-\log\tilde{M}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})-X_{t}/\tilde{M}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\Big\}.

Large-sample properties of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{NQ}}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{EQ}}
are discussed by
Aknouche et al. 2018
and
Aknouche and Francq 2021
, respectively, which will not be repeated here. From a practical point of view, the crucial difference are modified expressions for the matrices
\mbox{{{G}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
and
\mbox{{{G}}}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\sigma^{2})
involved in the asymptotic normal distribution, namely

\textstyle\mbox{{{G}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=E\left[\frac{1}{M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})(r+M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}))}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}}\right],\ \mbox{{{G}}}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\sigma^{2})=E\left[\frac{v_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$},\sigma^{2})}{M_{t}^{2}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})(r+M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}))^{2}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}}\right]\ \text{for }\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{NQ}},

and

\textstyle\mbox{{{G}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=E\left[\frac{1}{M_{t}^{2}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}}\right],\ \mbox{{{G}}}_{1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\sigma^{2})=E\left[\frac{v_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$},\sigma^{2})}{M_{t}^{4}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$})}{\partial\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}}\right]\ \text{for }\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{EQ}}.

For any of the three QMLE approaches, the estimates are determined by numerical optimization. To initialize the optimization routine, a moment estimate of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
might be used, which is easily obtained from sample mean and ACF by solving the moment properties provided by Section
3
.

4.1.2
Estimation of Parameter
\sigma^{2}

It remains to estimate the parameter value
\sigma_{0}^{2}
, which corresponds to the variance of
\epsilon_{t}
. For model (
3.5
) with Poisson-counting series, the conditional variance is given by
V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=M_{t}+\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2}
, recall (
3.6
). Thus, let
e_{t}=(X_{t}-M_{t})^{2}-(M_{t}+\sigma^{2}\,M_{t}^{2})
. Then, we have
(X_{t}-M_{t})^{2}=M_{t}+\sigma^{2}\,M_{t}^{2}+e_{t}
, or equivalently,

\frac{(X_{t}-M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}))^{2}-M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}{M_{t}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})}=\sigma^{2}+\tau_{t},

(4.4)

where
\tau_{t}=e_{t}/M_{t}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
is a term of martingale difference. Replacing
M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
by
\hat{M}_{t}:=\tilde{M}_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{PQ}})
or
\tilde{M}_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{NQ}})
or
\tilde{M}_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{EQ}})
, respectively (one might also use the moment estimate of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
), we obtain the least-squares estimate of
\sigma_{0}^{2}
as

\hat{\sigma}_{\textup{Poi}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{(X_{t}-\hat{M}_{t})^{2}-\hat{M}_{t}}{\hat{M}_{t}^{2}}.

Under some regularity conditions, using techniques similar to those in Theorem 3.2 of
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
,
\hat{\sigma}_{\textup{Poi}}^{2}
can be proven to be consistent and asymptotically normal, i. e.,

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\sigma}_{\textup{Poi}}^{2}-\sigma_{0}^{2})\xrightarrow{d}\textup{N}(0,\Lambda),\quad\text{where}\quad\Lambda=E\left[\left(\frac{(X_{t}-M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}))^{2}-(M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})+\sigma^{2}_{0}\,M_{t}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}))}{M_{t}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})}\right)^{2}\right].

In the case of an NB-counting series, we have
V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=M_{t}(1+M_{t})+\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2}
according to (
3.6
), so we would define the least-squares estimate of
\sigma_{0}^{2}
as

\hat{\sigma}_{\textup{NB}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{(X_{t}-\hat{M}_{t})^{2}-\hat{M}_{t}(1+\hat{M}_{t})}{\hat{M}_{t}^{2}}\ =\ \hat{\sigma}_{\textup{Poi}}^{2}-1.

But as can be seen by the last equality, we just end up with the estimator of the Poi-case reduced by one. This seems plausible as now a larger part of the variation is explained by the counting series.

Finally, for model (
3.15
), the conditional variance is given by
V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=(M_{t}-\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)(1-\ M_{t}+\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)+\sigma^{2}M_{t}^{2}
, recall (
3.2
). Thus, let
e_{t}=(X_{t}-M_{t})^{2}-\big((M_{t}-\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)(1-\ M_{t}+\lfloor M_{t}\rfloor)+\sigma^{2}\,M_{t}^{2}\big)
. Then, we have

\hat{\sigma}_{\textup{Bin}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{(X_{t}-\hat{M}_{t})^{2}-(\hat{M}_{t}-\lfloor\hat{M}_{t}\rfloor)(1-\hat{M}_{t}+\lfloor\hat{M}_{t}\rfloor)}{\hat{M}_{t}^{2}},

and
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\sigma}_{\textup{Bin}}^{2}-\sigma_{0}^{2})\xrightarrow{d}\textup{N}(0,\Lambda)
with

\Lambda=E\left[\left(\frac{(X_{t}-M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}))^{2}-\big((M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})-\lfloor M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\rfloor)(1-\ M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})+\lfloor M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\rfloor)+\sigma^{2}_{0}\,M_{t}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\big)}{M_{t}^{2}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})}\right)^{2}\right].

4.2
Two-Stage Weighted Least-Squares Estimation

As an alternative to the above QMLEs,
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
proposed a 2SWLSE approach.
The 2SWLSE is not asymptotically efficient in the sense that its asymptotic variance
reaches the Cramer–Rao bound, but is only “never asymptotically less efficient” than any QMLE
in the linear exponential family. When the conditional distribution of the model is known and is
a member of the exponential family, then the 2SWLS estimate is asymptotically efficient (in the
sense of the Cramer–Rao bound). See
Aknouche 2012
and
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
for more
details and other models.
We shall describe the estimation procedure as follows.
For convenience, we again denote
v_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\sigma^{2}):=V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
.
For an arbitrary fixed weighting point, say
(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}_{\ast},\sigma^{2}_{\ast})^{\prime}\in\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}\times\Delta
(in practice, the aforementioned moment estimates of
(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})
might be used), a first-stage weighted least-squares estimator (1WLSE, briefly “1W”) is defined as

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{1W}}=\arg\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}\in\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\Theta$}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{(X_{t}-\tilde{M}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}))^{2}}{v_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{\ast},\sigma^{2}_{\ast})}.

(4.5)

We next estimate
\sigma^{2}
like in Section
4.1.2
, and we denote the estimator as
\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
. But here, we replace
M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
by
\hat{M}_{t}=\tilde{M}_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{1W}})
.
After having consistent estimates of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
and
\sigma_{0}^{2}
, the optimal weight is estimated by
v_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{1W}},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n})
, and a second-stage weighted least-squares estimator (2WLSE, briefly “2W”) of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
has the form

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{2W}}=\arg\min_{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\theta$}\in\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\Theta$}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{(X_{t}-\tilde{M}_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}))^{2}}{v_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{1W}},\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n})}.

(4.6)

Under some regularity conditions, using techniques similar to those in Theorem 3.3 in
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
,
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{2W}}
can be proven to be strongly consistent and asymptotically normal, i. e.,
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{2W}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}\textup{N}(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\mbox{{{J}}}^{-1}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})),

where

\mbox{{{J}}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})=E\left[\frac{1}{v_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\prime}}\right].

Aknouche and Scotto 2023
stated that
\hat{\theta}_{\textup{2W}}
is asymptotically more efficient than
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{1W}}
and the QMLEs.
Note that if
b_{0j}=0
for
1\leq j\leq q
, then
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{1W}}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{\textup{2W}}
have a closed form, which is omitted here.

4.3
Approaches for Model Diagnostics

To check the adequacy of the fitted INGARCH-CMEMs (or to compare the performance of different candidate models), several approaches are possible, see Section 2.4 in
Weiß 2018
for a general survey. It should be noted, however, that we solely considered semi-parametric estimation approaches, i. e., we did not specify the full distribution of the considered CMEM. Thus, mainly moment-related diagnostic checks are relevant here.

The first option is to compare the fitted model’s mean, variance, and ACF (which can be computed from the results derived in Section
3
) to the corresponding sample moments.

Second, to check the adequacy of conditional moments, it is common to consider the standardized Pearson residuals
(R_{t})
, which are given by

R_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}},\hat{\sigma}^{2})\ =\ \frac{X_{t}-E[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1};\ \hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}},\hat{\sigma}^{2}]}{\sqrt{V[X_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1};\ \hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}},\hat{\sigma}^{2}]}}\overset{\eqref{CountMEMcvar}}{=}\frac{X_{t}-M_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})}{\sqrt{\nu\big(M_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})\big)+M_{t}^{2}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})\cdot\hat{\sigma}^{2}}}

(4.7)

for the models considered here. For an adequate model, the Pearson residuals have mean zero, variance one, and are uncorrelated.
In particular,
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
used the mean squared Pearson residual,
\textup{MSPR}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}R_{t}^{2}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}},\hat{\sigma}^{2})
,
for checking the adequacy of the dispersion structure, also see
Aleksandrov and Weiß 2020
for a more general analysis of this statistic. For an andequate model, the MSPR should be close to one.

Third, for INGARCH-type models, also another type of residuals is commonly used, where the
X_{t}
(or powers thereof) are scaled by
M_{t}
, see
Weiß et al. 2017
for a general discussion.
For INGARCH-type and multiplicative models such as the one proposed by
Zhu and Wang 2010
and
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
and the CMEMs considered here, especially the scaled residuals
S_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})=X_{t}/M_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})
are reasonable, and
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
proposed to compute the sample mean and variance thereof (MSR and VSR, respectively). Note that if
(X_{t})
stems from the CMEM (
3.5
) satisfying (
3.6
), then

\begin{array}[]{@{}rl}E[S_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})]\ =&E\big[E[X_{t}/M_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\ =\ 1,\\[4.30554pt]
V[S_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})]\ =&0\ +\ E\big[V[X_{t}/M_{t}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]\ =\ \sigma^{2}+E\big[\nu(M_{t})/M_{t}^{2}\big].\end{array}

The last term,
E\big[\nu(M_{t})/M_{t}^{2}\big]
, depends on the specific type of CMEM. For the compounding CMEM with Poisson counting series, we have
V[S_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})]=\sigma^{2}+E[1/M_{t}]
, while
V[S_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})]=\sigma^{2}+1+E[1/M_{t}]
for the NB-counting series. The latter result also shows that an NB-counting series is possible only if the scaled residuals’ variance is larger than one. Generally, if
\nu
is differentiable, we can approximate
E\big[\nu(M_{t})/M_{t}^{2}\big]
by a second-order Taylor expansion around the marginal mean
\mu=E[X_{t}]
as

E\left[\frac{\nu(M_{t})}{M_{t}^{2}}\right]\ \approx\ \frac{\nu(\mu)}{\mu^{2}}+\frac{\mu^{2}\,\nu^{\prime\prime}(\mu)-4\,\mu\,\nu^{\prime}(\mu)+6\,\nu(\mu)}{2\,\mu^{4}}\,V[X_{t}],\quad E\left[\frac{1}{M_{t}}\right]\ \approx\ \frac{1}{\mu}+\frac{1}{\mu^{3}}\,V[X_{t}].

For the binomial CMEM (
3.15
), the function
\nu(\alpha)=(\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)(1-\alpha+\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)
is not differentiable, but it is bounded by
\nu(\alpha)\in[0,0.25]
. Thus,
V[S_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})]\leq\sigma^{2}+E[0.25/M_{t}^{2}]
, i. e.,
V[S_{t}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})]
often takes a value close to
\sigma^{2}
.

As a fourth type of diagnostic check,
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
proposed to compute the mean absolute residual (MAR) defined as
\textup{MAR}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}|X_{t}-M_{t}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})|
, where smaller values indicate a better model fit.

3.1.5
). As the DGP, we considered twelve types of INGARCH
(1,1)
-CMEM, namely:

•

and
\sigma^{2}=1
or
\sigma^{2}=0.4
,

similar to the simulation scenario in Section 4 of
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
;

•

(a_{0},a_{1},b_{1})=(3,0.35,0.5)
and
\sigma^{2}=1
or
\sigma^{2}=0.1

;

•

;

and these parametrizations are combined with either the compounding CMEM (

(1,1)

The considered values of
\sigma^{2}
are realized by an appropriate choice of the innovations’ distribution. Recall that the standard INGARCH model corresponds to a compounding INGARCH-CMEM with deterministic
\epsilon_{t}=1
(thus
\sigma^{2}=0

for such examples.

1
also demonstrates one of the general patterns in Tables S.1–S.6, namely that the estimates of the AR-parameter
a_{1}
and the variance
\sigma^{2}
are only mildly affected by bias, whereas the estimates of the feedback parameter
b_{1}
and especially of the intercept parameter
a_{0}
might be biased also for larger sample sizes. From Tables S.1–S.6, it gets clear that this bias is mainly caused by the extent of
b_{1}
: the larger
b_{1}
, the stronger the bias (especially of
a_{0}
). The problems in estimating the intercept term of INGARCH-type models are well known in the literature, see
Fokianos et al. 2009
for example.

Further details can be recognized from Tables S.1–S.6. If
\sigma^{2}=1
(so a large
\sigma^{2}
, always see the left part of Tables S.1–S.6), the PQ-method often causes larger bias and SE than NQ, EQ (especially for
a_{0}
), whereas we often see the opposite ranking if
\sigma^{2}<1
(right part of Tables S.1–S.6). This goes along with the discussion of asymptotic optimality in
Aknouche and Scotto 2023
, recall Section
4.1.1
, because for small
\sigma^{2}
, the conditional variance of (
3.5
) is close to a linear function in
M_{t}
. The 2W-method often has bias and SE between the ones of PQ and NQ, EQ. Comparing Tables S.1 vs. S.2, S.3 vs. S.4, and S.5 vs. S.6, i. e., the case of a Poi-counting series to a binomial one, we do not see big differences in performance, i. e., the estimation approaches generally perform similar for both classes of DGP. An exception is the bias of
a_{0}
, which is somewhat larger for the Bin-counting series if
b_{1}\geq 0.5
. Finally, the values of SSE and ASE are often close to each other. If there are visible differences between SSE and ASE, these mainly happen for
n=300
, and the strongest absolute discrepancies are observed for the intercept term
a_{0}
. The relative deviation, however, is sometimes stronger for
\sigma^{2}
. In particular, the 2W-method sometimes leads to visibly larger SSE than ASE (e. g., lower left block in Table S.6), which is caused by a few extreme values of the estimates. The QMLE have less tendency to produce extreme estimates. Overall, the approximate SE seems to give reasonable insight into the true SE of the estimator in most cases.

We conclude this section with some simulation experiments on the effects of a possible model misspecification. Here, we focus on the DGPs with parametrization
(a_{0},a_{1},b_{1})=(2.8,0.4,0.2)
and
\sigma^{2}=1
or
\sigma^{2}=0.4
(see Tables S.1 and S.2). In our first experiment, we fitted an INGARCH
(1,1)
-CMEM to the generated time series by assuming the wrong multiplicative operator:

•

for the DGP with Poi-counting series from Table S.1, the misspecified model assumes a Bin-counting series, leading to the simulation results of Table S.7;

•

for the DGP with Bin-counting series from Table S.2, the misspecified model assumes a Poi-counting series, leading to the simulation results of Table S.8.

As the estimation approaches PQ, NQ, and EQ for
(a_{0},a_{1},b_{1})
do not depend on the specific structure of the multiplicative operator, the corresponding simulated means and SEs in Tables S.7 and S.8 are identical to those in Tables S.1 and S.2, respectively. The 2W-method, by contrast, depends on the choice of the multiplicative operator, but as can be seen by comparing Table S.7 to S.1 and Table S.8 to S.2, the effect of the misspecified operator is negligible. So altogether, the estimation of
(a_{0},a_{1},b_{1})
is robust against such model misspecification. The approximate SEs, in turn, are always affected by the misspecified operator, but the difference between correct and misspecified ASE is again small for
(a_{0},a_{1},b_{1})
.

A clear difference is only observed for the estimation of
\sigma^{2}
(and its corresponding ASE), which is plausible in view of Section
4.1.2
, where tailor-made estimation methods are presented for each type of counting series. However, in view of George Box’ famous words “all models are wrong but some are useful” (i. e., real-world data will never follow exactly one of the considered models, but some of these models might provide a good approximation to the true DGP), the practitioner is probably more interested in the performance of the fitted models. In Table S.9, the mean MAR values are provided if estimation is done based on the correct vs. the misspecified model. For the estimation approaches PQ, NQ, and EQ, the MAR values are identical as the estimates for
(a_{0},a_{1},b_{1})
do not depend on the specific multiplicative operator. Also for the 2W-method, there is an at most slight difference in the mean MARs, so the MAR performance is very robust against such misspecification. Analogous results but concerning the MSPR performance are shown in Table S.10. If the Poi-counting series is misspecified as a Bin-counting series (upper part of Table S.10), there is again an at most tiny difference in the resulting MSPRs. A somewhat stronger effect (although still quite small) is observed in the opposite scenario, where the Bin-counting series is misspecified as a Poi-counting series. So it seems that the CMEM with Bin-counting series can better adapt to the given DGP than the Poi-one.

As a final experiment on the effects of model misspecification, we introduce non-linearity into the DGP while model fitting still assumes the conditional linearity in (
1.4
). More precisely, we modify (
1.4
) by adding the softplus response function
s_{c}(x)=c\,\ln\big(1+\exp(x/c)\big)
with adjustment parameter
c>0
, in analogy to the class of softplus INGARCH models proposed by
Weiß et al. 2022
:

\textstyle M_{t}\ =\ s_{c}\Big(a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}a_{i}\,X_{t-i}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}\,M_{t-j}\Big).

(5.1)

Increasing
c
increases the extent of non-linearity, whereas
\lim_{c\to 0}s_{c}(x)=\max\{0,x\}
is piecewise linear. We focus on the illustrative choice
c=2
(for the default choice
c=1
, we hardly observed any effect on estimates and diagnostics), where the results are summarized in Tables S.11–S.14. Comparing Table S.11 to S.1 and Table S.12 to S.2, we note a slight effect of the model misspecification on the estimates for all parameters, and with the strongest effect again on
\sigma^{2}
. For practice, however, the main question is the achieved model performance. In Tables S.13 and S.14, the MAR and MSPR performance, respectively, is analyzed in comparison to that of the truly linear DGPs. For the MSPR values in Table S.14, there is an at most tiny effect if DGPs with Poi-counting series are considered, whereas the increase in MSPR is slightly more pronounced in the Bin-case. The main difference, however, is observed in the MAR performance, see Table S.13, namely clearly increased MAR values under neglected non-linearity. This is plausible as the softplus response function directly affects the conditional mean
M_{t}
, which, in turn, is the crucial quantity when computing the MAR, see Section
4.3
.

7
Conclusions and Future Research

In this article, we proposed a novel framework for transferring the well-established MEMs for real-valued time series to the case of count time series. The proposed CMEMs make use of an appropriate multiplicative operator for counts. After discussing such operators and the corresponding CMEMs in general, we focused on two special cases, namely a compounding operator using, e. g., a Poi-counting series, and a binomial multiplicative operator. We established the existence of stationary and ergodic solutions to such INGARCH-CMEMs and derived relevant moment properties. Afterwards, we developed two approaches for semi-parametric model estimation, namely QMLE and 2WLSE. The finite-sample performance of these estimation approaches was analyzed in a simulation study, and the applicability of our novel CMEMs was demonstrated with two real-world data examples.

There are several directions for future research. First, CMEMs using another multiplicative operator (such as the ZIP-multiplicative operator mentioned in the end of
Section
2
) or a non-linear mean specification might be analyzed. Second, the semi-parametric INGARCH model sketched in the end of
Section
3.1
deserves further investigation, e. g., with respect to the question if a semi-parametric maximum likelihood (ML) approach, analogous to
Drost et al. 2009
and
Liu et al. 2021
,
is possible for INGARCH models. Such an ML-fitted CMEM would be attractive for applications as it would allow, for example, for density forecasts like in
Aknouche et al. 2022
.
Third, related to the previous research direction, it could also be studied if a combination of semi-parametric ML estimation with the smoothing method discussed
by
Faymonville et al. 2023
could be beneficial for practice.
Finally,
\mathbb{Z}
-valued GARCH models also get attention, see
Cui et al. 2021
and
Xu and Zhu 2022
among others, so how to generalize the CMEM framework to the
\mathbb{Z}
-valued case is interesting.
