Moment Intermittency in the PAM with Asymptotically Singular Noise
Pierre Yves Gaudreau Lamarre, Promit Ghosal, and Yuchen Liao

Abstract
. Let ξ \xi be a singular Gaussian noise on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} that is either white, fractional, or with the Riesz covariance kernel; in particular, there exists a scaling parameter ω > 0 \omega>0 such that c ω / 2 ξ ( c ⋅ ) c^{\omega/2}\xi(c\cdot) is equal in distribution to ξ \xi for all c > 0 c>0 . Let ( ξ ε ) ε > 0 (\xi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0} be a sequence of smooth mollifications such that ξ ε → ξ \xi_{\varepsilon}\to\xi as ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 . We study the asymptotics of the moments of the parabolic Anderson model (PAM) with noise ξ ε \xi_{\varepsilon} as ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 , both for large (i.e., t → ∞ t\to\infty ) and fixed times t t . This approach makes it possible to study the moments of the PAM with regular and singular noises in a unified fashion, as well as interpolate between the two settings. As corollaries of our main results, we obtain the following: (1) When ξ \xi is subcritical (i.e., 0 < ω < 2 0<\omega<2 ), our results extend the known large-time moment and tail asymptotics for the Stratonovich PAM with noise ξ \xi . Our method of proof clarifies the role of the maximizers of the variational problems (known as Hartree ground states) that appear in these moment asymptotics in describing the geometry of intermittency. We take this opportunity to prove the existence and study the properties of the Hartree ground state with a fractional kernel, which we believe is of independent interest. (2) When ξ \xi is critical or supercritical (i.e., ω = 2 \omega=2 or ω > 2 \omega>2 ), our results provide a new interpretation of the moment blowup phenomenon observed in the Stratonovich PAM with noise ξ \xi . That is, we uncover that the latter is related to an intermittency effect that occurs in the PAM with noise ξ ε \xi_{\varepsilon} as ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 for fixed finite times t > 0 t>0 .



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in the continuous parabolic Anderson model (PAM)
with a time-independent Gaussian noise. That is, the partial differential equation with random coefficients

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\big(\kappa\Delta+\xi(x)\big)u(t,x)\\
u(0,x)=1\end{cases},\qquad t\geq 0,~x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\kappa>0
are fixed,
\Delta
is the Laplacian operator,
and
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a centered stationary Gaussian process. A
fundamental problem involving the PAM consists of understanding the
occurrence of intermittency at large times. Informally, the latter refers to the observation
that the random field
x\mapsto u(t,x)
tends to develop tall and narrow peak-like formations
as
t\to\infty
. We refer to
[
30
, Chapter 1]
and references therein
for a recent overview.

As explained in
[
39
]
,
arguably the most elementary rigorous indication that intermittency occurs in
(
1.1
) is a growth property of its moments: More specifically,
suppose that there exists a scale function
A
such that
A(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty

and such that the limits

\ell_{p}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\langle u(t,x)^{p}\rangle}{A(t)},

which are called Lyapunov exponents,
exist and are finite for some values of
p>0
.

Remark 1.1
.

Here, as is common in a subset of the PAM literature, we use
\langle\cdot\rangle

to denote the expectation with respect to
\xi
.
Also, since
x\mapsto\xi(x)
is stationary, so is
x\mapsto u(t,x)
;
hence
\ell_{p}
does not depend on the choice of
x
.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that (
1.1
) is intermittent
if there exists a subset of
(0,\infty)
—which contains at least two points—on which
the function
p\mapsto\ell_{p}/p
is strictly increasing.

We refer to
[
39
, Pages 210–212]
for an explanation of
the geometric significance of this condition.
The classical theory of the continuous PAM concerns the case where

\xi
is a regular random process (e.g., Hölder continuous).
In this setting, the intermittency phenomenon is very well understood,
as shown in the works of Carmona, Gärtner, König, and Molchanov
[
3
,
20
,
21
]
.
In contrast to that, in recent years there has been a growing interest in extending this theory to
singular noises. In this paper, our aim is to contribute to the understanding of intermittency in the PAM with a
singular Gaussian noise—where the product
\xi\cdot u(t,\cdot)
is interpreted in the Stratonovich sense—via a study of the moments.
We discuss our approach and state our main results in Section
2
.

In the remainder of this section,
we introduce the singular noises that we are interested in (Section
1.1
),
we briefly recall the current state of the art concerning the moments
of the regular and singular PAM (Section
1.2
),
we provide some motivation for our investigations and a brief overview of our results (Section
1.3
),
we discuss open questions that arise from our results (Section
1.4
),
and we give an outline of the organization of this paper (Section
1.5
).

1.1.
Stratonovich PAM with Singular Noise

We make the following assumption on
\xi
:

Assumption 1.3
.

Informally, we view
\xi
as a centered Gaussian process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with covariance

\langle\xi(x)\xi(y)\rangle=\gamma(x-y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\gamma
is one of the following three kernels:
Letting
\sigma>0
be a fixed constant,

(1)

(
White
)
\gamma(x)=\sigma^{2}\delta_{0}(x)
, where
\delta_{0}
denotes the Dirac delta distribution.

(2)

(
Riesz
)
\gamma(x)=\sigma^{2}|x|^{-\omega}
for some
0<\omega<d
.

(3)

(
Fractional
)
\gamma(x)=\sigma^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}
for some
\omega_{i}\in(0,1)
.

Since
\gamma
is either a Schwartz distribution or has a singularity at zero,

\xi
must be defined rigorously as a random Schwartz distribution. More specifically,

\xi
is a centered Gaussian process on
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(the smooth and compactly
supported functions) with covariance

\langle\xi(f)\xi(g)\rangle=\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}f(x)\gamma(x-y)g(y)~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,\qquad f,g\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Remark 1.4
.

The three covariance kernels listed in Assumption
1.3
all satisfy

\gamma(cx)=c^{-\omega}\gamma(x),\qquad c>0,~x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

for some scaling exponent
\omega>0

(
\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\omega_{i}
in the case of fractional noise and
\omega=d
in the case of white noise).

\omega
can be viewed as a parameter that quantifies the extent to which

\xi
is singular, i.e., a larger
\omega
corresponds to a more singular covariance/noise.

The main difficulty in dealing with the PAM with singular noise is that
\xi
is not
actually a function that can be evaluated pointwise. Thus, extending the results on the
intermittency of the PAM with regular noise to the singular setting poses serious technical challenges. In fact, the
very definition of the singular PAM is nontrivial, as the pointwise product
\xi(x)u(t,x)

in (
1.1
) requires a careful justification.
One way to get around this issue,
which leads to the Stratonovich solution,
is to proceed as follows:

Definition 1.5
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, denote the Gaussian kernel with variance
\varepsilon^{2}
as

p_{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-|x|^{2}/2\varepsilon^{2}}}{(2\pi\varepsilon^{2})^{d/2}},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

noting that
p_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-d}p_{1}(x/\varepsilon)
. We define the mollified noise

\xi_{\varepsilon}:=\xi*p_{\varepsilon/\sqrt{2}}
, where
*
denotes the convolution. In particular,

\xi_{\varepsilon}
is a centered Gaussian process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with covariance

\langle\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)\xi_{\varepsilon}(y)\rangle=\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x-y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\gamma*p_{\varepsilon}
.

With this in hand, for every
\varepsilon>0
, we may now consider the smoothed PAM

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\big(\kappa\Delta+\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)\big)u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\\
u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=1\end{cases},\qquad t\geq 0,~x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Since
\xi_{\varepsilon}
has smooth sample paths, the solution of (
1.2
) is classically well defined.
Then, one hopes that the solution of (
1.1
) can be constructed by
taking the limit

(1.3)

\displaystyle u(t,x):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x).

On the one hand, if the noise is not too singular—that is, if the condition
0<\omega<2
holds—then the limit
(
1.3
) is nontrivial
and gives rise to a meaningful notion of solution
(e.g.,
[
29
, Section 5]
). On the other hand, when
\omega\geq 2
,
the limit (
1.3
) blows up. In some such
cases, the divergence of
u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)
can be compensated by subtracting diverging
renormalization constants from the noise,
thus allowing to salvage a meaningful notion of nontrivial solution
[
10
,
14
,
24
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
However, in some cases, the noise is so singular that it is not clear that any meaningful notion of
Stratonovich solution can be salvaged (e.g., the white noise with

\omega=d\geq 4
; see
[
26
, Section 1.1, (PAM)]
).

Remark 1.6
.

The procedure described in the above paragraph works for more general
mollifiers than the Gaussian kernel used in Definition
1.5
.
For simplicity and definiteness, in this paper we use the Gaussian kernel.

Remark 1.7
.

There are alternate ways of defining a solution of the singular PAM, such as the
Skorokhod solution (e.g.,
[
8
,
11
]
and
[
29
, Section 3]
).
The present paper deals exclusively with the Stratonovich solution.

1.2.
Moments of the PAM - Known Results

In order to motivate our investigations, we review the known results regarding
the moments of the continuous PAM with Gaussian noise.

1.2.1.
Regular PAM

Consider
u_{1}(t,\cdot)
(i.e., (
1.2
) in the case
\varepsilon=1
),
which is the PAM with smooth Gaussian noise
\xi_{1}
.
In this case,
[
21
, Theorem 1 and Section 4.1]
states that

(1.4)

\displaystyle\log\langle u_{1}(t,x)^{p}\rangle=\left(\frac{p^{2}\gamma_{1}(0)}{2}\right)t^{2}-p^{3/2}\chi\,t^{3/2}\big(1+o(1)\big)\qquad\text{as }t\to\infty

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p\geq 1
.
In (
1.4
), we define

(1.5)

\displaystyle\chi:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
\|f\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\left(\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f(x)|_{2}^{2}~\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{4}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}f(x)^{2}\big((x-y)^{\top}\Sigma(x-y)\big)f(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\right),

where
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the order 1 Sobolev Hilbert
space,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
denotes the Lebesgue
L^{p}
norm,
and
-\Sigma
is the Hessian matrix of the covariance kernel
\gamma_{1}(x)
evaluated at
x=0
.
In particular, intermittency in the sense of Definition
1.2

occurs with scale function
A(t)=t^{2}
and Lyapunov exponents
\ell_{p}=\frac{p^{2}\gamma_{1}(0)}{2}

for all
p\geq 1
.

The first order in the asymptotic (
1.4
) (i.e., the term
\ell_{p}t^{2}
)
was proved by Carmona and Molchanov
[
3
]
for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
Later, Gärtner and König
[
21
]
extended the result to every
p\geq 1
and
also provided the second-order asymptotic
p^{3/2}\chi\,t^{3/2}
.
As explained in

[
21
, Section 0.4.3]
, the derivation of this second order—and more specifically the minimizers of
\chi
—provide a precise description of the geometry of intermittent peaks in
u_{1}(t,\cdot)
for large
t
.

Remark 1.8
.

The setting considered in
[
3
,
21
]
is more general
than stationary Gaussian noises with a covariance of the form
\gamma_{1}=\gamma*p_{1}
for a

\gamma
satisfying Assumption
1.3
. Since the latter is the
setting that is of interest to us in this paper, we do not state the moment asymptotics
of the PAM with regular noise on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in its full generality.

1.2.2.
Singular PAM - Subcritical Regime

If
\xi
is as in Assumption
1.3

with scaling parameter
0<\omega<2
, then we say that it is subcritical.
In this regime, it is known that intermittency in the sense of Definition
1.2
occurs.
More specifically, define the variational constant

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}:=\sup_{f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),~\|f\|_{2}=1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}f(x)^{2}\gamma(x-y)f(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y-\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f(x)|_{2}^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\right).

Arguing as in
[
13
, Lemma A.2 with
\alpha_{0}=0
]
,
it can be shown that
\mathbf{M}\in(0,\infty)
.
In the case of Riesz and Fractional noise,

[
12
, Theorem 1.1,
\beta_{0}=\rho=0

and
\alpha=2
]
states that

(1.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\langle u(t,x)^{p}\rangle}{t^{(4-\omega)/(2-\omega)}}=p^{(4-\omega)/(2-\omega)}\mathbf{M}=\ell_{p}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p\in\{1\}\cup[2,\infty)
.
For white noise with
d=\omega=1
,
[
37
, (2)]

proves (
1.7
) for
p=1
, and
[
29
, Theorem 6.9 (ii)]

shows that there exists constants
0<C<C^{\prime}
such that

(1.8)

\displaystyle C(pt)^{(4-\omega)/(2-\omega)}\leq\log\langle u(t,x)^{p}\rangle\leq C^{\prime}(pt)^{(4-\omega)/(2-\omega)}

for
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
t>0
.

Remark 1.9
.

While we did not find such a statement in the literature,
we expect that the general methodology developed in
[
12
,
13
]
could be suitably adapted
to prove (
1.7
) for
p\geq 2
in the case of one-dimensional white noise.
That being said, if
p\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
, then (
1.7
) appears to be out of reach of
the currently-known techniques for all three noises considered in Assumption
1.3
;
see
[
12
, (6.2)]
.

1.2.3.
Singular PAM - Critical Regime

If
\xi
is as in Assumption
1.3

with
\omega=2
, then we say that it is critical.
In this regime, it is expected that there exist moment blowup thresholds
t_{0}(p)\in(0,\infty)
such that

(1.9)

\displaystyle\langle u(t,x)^{p}\rangle\begin{cases}<\infty&\text{if }t<t_{0}(p)\\
=\infty&\text{if }t>t_{0}(p)\end{cases}

for all
p>0
; see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem 1.7]
,

[
11
,
23
]
,
[
14
, (1.26)]
,
and
[
38
, Corollary 1.2]
.
In particular, if there is intermittency in this regime, then it does not follow from Definition
1.2
; the Lyapunov exponents do not exist when the moments blow up in finite time.

We refer to
[
11
, Contribution 1 and Remarks 1.1 and 1.2]

for a related result concerning the Skorokhod PAM when
p\in\{1\}\cup[2,\infty)
,
and to
[
11
, Page 5]

for the mention of a conjecture regarding the value of
t_{0}(p)
in the Stratonovich setting considered in this paper.
In both cases, it is known/conjectured that
t_{0}(p)
is related to the best constant

(1.10)

\mathcal{G}:=\inf\bigg\{C>0:\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}f(x)^{2}\gamma(x-y)f(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f(x)|_{2}^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\text{ s.t. }\|f\|_{2}=1\bigg\};

see
[
11
, (1.9), (3.21), Notation 3.12, and Theorem 3.14]
.

Remark 1.10
.

The fact that
\mathcal{G}
is finite for the covariance kernels in
Assumption
1.3
when
\omega=2
is established in

[
4
, (C.1) for
p=d=2
]
for white noise,
Proposition
7.1
below for Riesz noise,
and
[
11
, Remark 3.11]
for fractional noise.

1.2.4.
Singular PAM - Supercritical Regime

If
\xi
is as in Assumption
1.3
with

\omega>2
, then we say that it is supercritical.
In this regime, it is expected that
\langle u(t,x)^{p}\rangle=\infty
for all
p,t>0
;
see, e.g.,
[
23
, Remark 1.7]
and
[
32
, Theorem 2 for
d=3
]
.
Thus, intermittency cannot be established by studying Lyapunov exponents in this regime either.

1.3.
Motivational Problems and Outline of Results

The results discussed above
raise a number of interesting problems. For instance:

Problems 1.11
.

In the subcritical regime:

•

Can the maximizers of the variational problem
\mathbf{M}
in (
1.6
)
be argued to describe the geometry of intermittent peaks in the singular PAM, in similar fashion to the
minimizers of
\chi
in (
1.4
) for the smooth PAM (e.g., the heuristic derivation in
[
21
, Section 0.4.3]
)?

•

If the answer to the above is affirmative, then how/why does the contribution of the geometry of peaks
(i.e., the variational constant) undergo a transition from the second- to the first-order
asymptotic when going from a regular to a singular noise?

•

Can the exact asymptotic (
1.7
) be established for every
p>0

and every noise in Assumption
1.3

(in particular, including
p\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
)?

Problems 1.12
.

In the critical and supercritical regimes:

•

What is the geometric significance (if any) of the blowup of the moments
(either in finite time
in the critical regime or for all times in the supercritical regime)?

•

What is the geometric significance (if any) of the
variational constant
\mathcal{G}
in (
1.10
)
that is expected to characterize the moment blowup thresholds
t_{0}(p)
in (
1.9
)?

Our main results, which we state in full in Section
2
,
shed new light on all of these problems. More specifically, our
results fit into three main categories:

Firstly, the main theorems proved in this paper are

Theorems
2.5
,
2.17
,
and
2.22
. The latter provide moment asymptotics for the PAM with a noise that becomes
increasing singular (i.e.,
\xi_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
) in the subcritical, critical, and supercritical regimes respectively.
The setting that we consider in this paper,
which is explained in details in Section
2.1
, allows us to treat the
PAM with smooth and singular noises in a unified
way, as well as interpolate between the two settings.
In particular,
our method of proof is mostly inspired by the methodology developed in

[
21
]
for the smooth setting, and thus
provides the same geometric interpretation of intermittency (see Sections
3.2
and
3.3
for the details).

Secondly, we have three corollaries of our main theorems, which improve known
results and provide new insights on the behavior of the moments of the PAM with singular noise:

•

As a corollary of Theorem
2.5
,
in
Theorem
2.6
we extend
(
1.7
) and (
1.8
)
by establishing precise moment asymptotics
in the subcritical regime
for all
p>0
,
which also allow us to obtain precise tail asymptotics for the solution of the PAM.
Combining this with the fact that
our moment asymptotics in Theorem
2.5

interpolate between the classical and singular asymptotics in
(
1.4
) and
(
1.7
)/(
1.8
), as well as the
geometric interpretation of our asymptotics in Sections

3.2
and
3.3
,
this provides a complete answer to Problems
1.11
(see Section
2.2

for more details).

•

As corollaries of Theorems
2.17
and
2.22
,
in
Theorems
2.19
and
2.23

we make progress on Problems
1.12
by uncovering that
the moment blowup phenomenon discussed in Sections
1.2.3

and
1.2.4
is related
to a
finite time
intermittency effect, which
we can detect in the moment asymptotics of
u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
with
t>0
fixed.
Moreover, we explain how the best constant
\mathcal{G}
in (
1.10
) characterizes
the occurrence of this intermittency phenomenon in the critical regime.
To the best of our knowledge, these are the first results that establish the existence of an intermittency
phenomenon in the PAM with an asymptotically singular noise and a fixed finite time.

Thirdly, in
Theorem
2.11
,
we prove the existence and study the properties of the maximizers of the variational problem

\mathbf{M}
in (
1.6
) in the case of the fractional kernel
\gamma(x)=\sigma^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}
; the relevance of these maximizers to intermittency is discussed in Section
3.3
.
Maximizers of variational problems
of the form
\mathbf{M}
for general
\gamma
are known in the mathematical physics literature as Hartree ground states
and are important objects in quantum mechanics; see, e.g.,
[
19
]
and references therein. (In
that interpretation, the kernel
\gamma
represents an interaction potential between distinct particles.)
Thus, the existence and properties of these maximizers have been studied for various choices of

\gamma
, including the white and Riesz noises (see Theorems
2.7
and
2.9
).
To the best of our knowledge, the standard results in this theory concern the case where

\gamma
is symmetric decreasing and has integrable singularities (i.e., the measure of
level sets
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\gamma(x)>t\}
is finite); see, e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 2]
.
Neither of these properties hold for the fractional covariance kernel. Thus, we believe that Theorem
2.11

may be of independent interest, as it studies the Hartree ground state problem in a situation where
there is less symmetry and integrability than the usual setting.

1.4.
Discussion

1.4.1.
Extensions

Many of the moment asymptotics stated in
Sections
1.2.2
–
1.2.4

admit a generalization if

•

\xi
is replaced by a possibly time-dependent noise of the form

\langle\xi(s,x)\xi(t,y)\rangle=|s-t|^{\omega_{0}}\gamma(x-y)

for some
\omega_{0}\in[0,1)
; and/or

•

the term
\xi(x)\cdot u(t,x)
or
\xi(t,x)\cdot u(t,x)
in the definition
of the PAM is interpreted as a Wick product, which leads to the Skorokhod solution; and/or

•

the Laplacian operator
\Delta
is replaced by
a fractional Laplacian of the form
-(-\Delta)^{s}
for some
0<s<1
.

See, for instance,
[
6
,
7
,
9
,
11
,
12
,
14
]
. It is thus natural to ask the following:

Problems 1.13
.

Is it possible to prove analogues of Theorems
2.5
,

2.17
, and
2.22

for the PAM with a time-independent noise and/or Skorokhod solution and/or fractional Laplacian?

Among other things, an affirmative answer to these problems could improve the known precise moment asymptotics in
[
12
, Theorem 1.1]
to all
p>0
in the subcritical case,
and it could show that the moment blowup phenomenon proved in
[
11
, Contribution 1]
for the
critical Skorokhod/time-dependent case is also related to an intermittency effect
that occurs in the mollified PAM when
\varepsilon\to 0
for fixed times.

However, many specifics of the techniques employed in this paper cannot be directly extended to the
time-independent noise, Skorokhod, or fractional Laplacian settings. Most notably, our frequent
reliance on the spectral expansion (
3.3
), which is exclusive to the
Stratonovich and time-independent settings. Thus, we expect that solving
Problems
1.13
would require a number of new ideas (such as replacing spectral
expansions with the hypercontractivity trick developed in
[
33
]
), which we leave open for future works.

1.4.2.
Intermittency in the Critical and Supercritical Regimes

While Theorems
2.19
and

2.23
shed new light on
the relationship between intermittency and the moment blowup phenomenon
in the critical and supercritical regimes, they do not appear to say
anything definitive about the occurrence (or not) of intermittency
in the usual large-time setting.
At most, these results suggest that
if
intemittency occurs
in the critical or supercritical PAM at large times, then it is likely explained by
a different mechanism than the PAM with smooth or subcritical noise.

More specifically, the phase transition in
Theorem
2.5

shows that the large-time intermittency in the subcritical PAM comes
from the same mechanism as the PAM with smooth noise:
As illustrated in the transition from Figure

1
to Figure
3
,
as we make
\varepsilon
increasingly small compared to
t
, the geometry of peaks in
the noise and the PAM at large times undergoes a transition from the classical asymptotics,
and eventually stabilizes to a size and shape independent of
\varepsilon
.

In contrast, Theorems
2.19
and
2.23

show that the intermittency in the PAM with smooth noise
cannot transform into an intermittency effect that occurs at large times
in the critical or supercritical PAM. Indeed, if we define the renormalized subcritical/critical PAM as

u(t,x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\mathrm{e}^{-tc_{\varepsilon}}

(for an appropriate choice of diverging renormalization constants
c_{\varepsilon}
), then the tall and narrow
peaks that generate the moment asymptotics in Theorem
2.19
and
2.23
disappear once we take the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit, and thus have no
contribution in the geometry of
u(t,x)
as
t\to\infty
.

As a final remark, we note the work of König, Perkowski, and Van Zuijlen
[
31
]
,
who studied the almost-sure asymptotics of the critical Stratonovich PAM in the case of white noise.
In particular,
[
31
, Theorem 1.1 (b)]
states that, from the point of view of
almost-sure logarithmic asymptotics of the PAM, an intermittency effect cannot
be observed (at least on boxes near the origin of side-length
\sim t^{a}
for
0<a<1
). This is in sharp contrast to the case of smooth noise, as explained in

[
20
, Section 1.6]
. Therefore, it appears that the occurrence
of an intermittency phenomenon at large times (as well as its exact nature if it does occur) in the critical and supercritical
PAM remains open.

2.1.
Our Approach: Asymptotically Singular Noise

Instead of studying

as

(i.e., (

))
or studying

by first sending

and then

(i.e.,

1.2.4
),
we consider the setting where both

\varepsilon
and

Definition 2.1
.

We introduce the master parameter

\mathsf{m}\in[0,\infty).

We assume that
\mathsf{e}=\mathsf{e}(\mathsf{m})
and
\mathsf{t}=\mathsf{t}(\mathsf{m})

are positive functions of this parameter
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathsf{m}\geq 0}\mathsf{e}\leq 1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\inf_{\mathsf{m}\geq 0}\mathsf{t}>0.

The function
\mathsf{e}
represents the dependence of
\varepsilon
on the master parameter
\mathsf{m}
,
and
\mathsf{t}
represents the dependence of
t
on
\mathsf{m}
.

Remark 2.2
.

As we have done in (
2.1
),
in order to improve readability, throughout this paper we mostly keep the
dependence of
\mathsf{e}
and
\mathsf{t}
(and various other functions) on the master parameter
implicit. For instance, if we state an asymptotic of the form

\log\langle u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle=F(\mathsf{e},\mathsf{t})+o(1)\qquad\text{as }\mathsf{m}\to\infty

for some functional

, then it should be understood as

\log\big\langle u_{\mathsf{e}(\mathsf{m})}\big(\mathsf{t}(\mathsf{m}),x\big)^{p}\big\rangle=F\big(\mathsf{e}(\mathsf{m}),\mathsf{t}(\mathsf{m})\big)+o(1)\qquad\text{as }\mathsf{m}\to\infty.

Limits of the form
\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}F(\mathsf{e},\mathsf{t})=l
should be interpreted in the same way.

We are mainly interested in taking
\mathsf{e}\to 0
as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
.
In doing so, our hope is twofold:

(1)

Since
u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)
has a smooth noise for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
some of the techniques used for the case

\varepsilon=1
in
[
21
]
that are not applicable to the singular case remain available.

(2)

If
\varepsilon
is small, then
u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)
’s geometry is similar to that of

u(t,\cdot)
(possibly up to a renormalization).
Thus, if
\mathsf{e}\to 0
at a fast enough
rate as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
, then the asymptotics

u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t},\cdot)=u_{\mathsf{e}(\mathsf{m})}\big(\mathsf{t}(\mathsf{m}),\cdot\big)

might shed new light on the intermittency of the PAM with singular noise.

Since intermittency is a phenomenon that typically occurs at large times,
we will in many cases consider the large-
\mathsf{m}
asymptotics of the moments
\langle u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle

under the assumption that both
\mathsf{e}\to 0
and
\mathsf{t}\to\infty
as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
.
However, as we explain in the coming sections, one of the main insights
of this paper is that it is also interesting to consider the case where
\mathsf{t}
remains bounded
(see Theorems
2.19
and
2.23
).

Remark 2.3
.

A similar approach was implemented in
[
22
]
, wherein the main focus was to
establish a phase transition in the
almost-sure asymptotics of the PAM with asymptotically singular noise
(see Theorems 1.7 and 1.11 therein).
The moments in the case of asymptotically singular white noise
were studied in
[
22
, Theorem 1.17]
. However, the latter is
weaker and less general than the results in this paper,
it is proved using different techniques,
and it does not provide
any insight on Problems
1.11
and
1.12
(which are the main focus of this paper).

We now proceed to an exposition of our results.

2.2.
Main Results Part 1 - Subcritical Regime

Consider the subcritical regime where
0<\omega<2
, which we henceforth abbreviate as Sub.

Assumption 2.4
.

In the Sub regime, in addition to (
2.1
),
we always assume that

\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\mathsf{t}=\infty.

We consider three cases of the Sub regime, depending on the behavior of
\mathsf{e}
relative
to

\bullet

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\mathsf{e}\,\mathsf{t}^{1/(2-\omega)}=\infty

\bullet

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\mathsf{e}\,\mathsf{t}^{1/(2-\omega)}=\mathfrak{c}

\bullet
Case Sub-
3:

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\mathsf{e}\,\mathsf{t}^{1/(2-\omega)}=0

Our first main result is as follows:

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.4
holds.
Let
p>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be fixed.

•

In case Sub-1, as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty

(2.2)

\displaystyle\log\langle u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle=\left(\frac{p^{2}\gamma_{1}(0)}{2}\right)\mathsf{e}^{-\omega}\mathsf{t}^{2}-p^{3/2}\chi\,\mathsf{e}^{-(2+\omega)/2}\mathsf{t}^{3/2}\big(1+o(1)\big),

where we recall that
\chi
is defined in (
1.5
).

•

In case Sub-2,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\frac{\log\langle u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle}{\mathsf{t}^{(4-\omega)/(2-\omega)}}=p^{(4-\omega)/(2-\omega)}\mathbf{M}_{\mathfrak{c},p},

where we define the variational constant

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\mathfrak{c},p}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
\|f\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}f(x)^{2}\gamma_{p^{1/(2-\omega)}\mathfrak{c}}(x-y)f(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y-\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f(x)|_{2}^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\right).

•

In case Sub-3,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\frac{\log\langle u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle}{\mathsf{t}^{(4-\omega)/(2-\omega)}}=p^{(4-\omega)/(2-\omega)}\mathbf{M},

where we recall that
\mathbf{M}
is defined in (
1.6
).

2.2.1.
Interpretation of Theorem
2.5

In case Sub-1,
\mathsf{t}
blows up quickly relative to
\mathsf{e}
’s size.
Thus, it is natural to expect that
u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t},\cdot)
should behave similarly to
u_{1}(\mathsf{t},\cdot)
as

The asymptotic (
2.2

Apart from the additional factors of

and

in the first- and second-order terms respectively,
this is the same as the classical result (
1.4

(In fact, in the case where

recover exactly (

that we also prove moment asymptotics for

Next, by carefully examining (

),
it is to be expected that a transition occurs when

is so small
that the first- and second-order terms are the same size. This occurs

and

In cases Sub-2 and Sub-3, we show that this transition does indeed

stated earlier in (
1.7
).

Referring back to Problems

,
we note that Theorem

provides an
affirmative answer to the first two items raised therein:
Since our proof of Theorem

mostly follows the general methodology developed in

We refer to Sections

and

interpretation of the following from the point of view of intermittency:

(1)

The maximizers of the variational problems in (

) and (

(2)

the coalescence phenomenon whereby the first- and second-order terms

Precise Asymptotics for all

Regarding the third item raised in Problems

Let

2.2.3.
Geometry of Maximizers

The significance of the variational constants
\mathbf{M}_{\mathfrak{c},p}
and
\mathbf{M}
in describing
the geometry of intermittency (as explained in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
)
motivates studying the existence and properties of its maximizers.

For the white noise,
0<\omega<2
implies that
d=1
. In this case, we have the following
well-known result (e.g.,
[
18
, Lemma 3.1]
and
[

):

Theorem 2.7

(
[
18
,
37
]
)
.

Suppose that
d=1
and
\gamma=\sigma^{2}\delta_{0}
.
The set of maximizers of
\mathbf{M}
consists of all functions

of the form

f_{\star}(x)=\pm\frac{\sigma}{2^{3/2}\kappa^{1/2}}\mathrm{sech}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\kappa}(x-z)\right),

where
z\in\mathbb{R}
can be any real number.

Remark 2.8
.

The importance of the function
\mathrm{sech}=1/\mathrm{cosh}
in the description of
the geometry of intermittency of the PAM with one-dimensional white noise
was also pointed out in
[
16
]
, in the context of the localization of the
associated Schrödinger operator on a large interval.

For the Riesz noise, the minimizers are not explicitly known, but their existence and
basic properties are nevertheless well-understood and classical
(e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 2]
):

Theorem 2.9

(
[
19
]
)
.

Suppose that
\gamma(x)=\sigma^{2}|x|^{-\omega}
for some
0<\omega<\min\{2,d\}
.
There exists maximizers of
\mathbf{M}
. Suppose that
f_{\star}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is such a maximizer.

(1)

f_{\star}
is either positive or negative.

(2)

f_{\star}
is smooth and decays exponentially at infinity; that is,
there exists some constants
a,b>0
such that
|f_{\star}(x)|\leq a\mathrm{e}^{-b|x|}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(3)

|f_{\star}|
is symmetric decreasing about a point; that is, there
exists some
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a nonincreasing function
\rho:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)

such that
|f_{\star}(x-z)|=\rho(|x|)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark 2.10
.

To the best of our knowledge, the uniqueness of the maximizer
f_{\star}

in Theorem
2.9

(up to a
\pm
sign and/or a shift
f_{\star}(\cdot-z)
)
is only known when
d=3
and
\omega=1

[
34
]
. Otherwise, the uniqueness
appears to be a hard open problem (e.g.
[
19
, Problem 3]
).

Next, the existence
and basic properties of the maximizers of
\mathbf{M}
in the case of the fractional
noise do not appear to be known. One of the contributions of this paper is to prove the existence and study the geometry of such maximizers:

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
\gamma(x)=\sigma^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}
, where
\omega_{i}\in(0,1)
and
\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\omega_{i}\in(0,2)
.
There exists maximizers of
\mathbf{M}
. Suppose that
f_{\star}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is such a maximizer.

(1)

f_{\star}
is either positive or negative.

(2)

f_{\star}
is smooth and decays exponentially at infinity.

(3)

|f_{\star}|
is symmetric decreasing along every axis about a point; that is, there exists some
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that for every fixed
1\leq i\leq d
and
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
(for all

),
there exists a nonincreasing function
\rho:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that

\big|f_{\star}\big((x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},r,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{d})-z\big)\big|=\rho(|r|)\qquad\text{for every }r\in\mathbb{R}.

Remark 2.12
.

The fractional covariance kernel
\gamma(x)=\sigma^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}
is symmetric decreasing
along every axis about the point
z=0
. Thus, much like the white and Riesz noises,
the maximizers of
\mathbf{M}
for fractional noise inherit some of the same symmetries (up to a translation) as the
associated covariance kernel.

Remark 2.13
.

We do not attempt to settle the question of the
uniqueness (up to change of sign or translations) of the maximizers in Theorem
2.11
,
which we also expect to be a hard problem (see Remark

).

Finally, in Section
3.3
we showcase
how the maximizers of

describe

) to the singular ones in (

).
This can be made more formal with the following statement:

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
0<\omega<2
.
For every

,
the constant
\mathbf{M}_{\mathfrak{c},p}
is finite and positive,
and it has maximizers.
For every fixed

,
we have the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathfrak{c}\to 0}\mathbf{M}_{\mathfrak{c},p}=\mathbf{M}.

Moreover, if

is a sequence such that

is a maximizer of

for all

then every vanishing sequence of

’s has a

along which

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{\star}^{(\mathfrak{c}_{n})}(\cdot-z_{n})-f_{\star}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=0

The limit

in (

2.3.
Main Results Part 2 - Critical Regime

Consider the critical regime
\omega=2
, which we henceforth abbreviate as Crt.

Assumption 2.16
.

For Crt, in addition to (
2.1
),
we assume that one of the following two cases holds:

\bullet
Case Crt-
1:

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\mathsf{t}=\infty
.

\bullet
Case Crt-
2:

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\mathsf{e}=0
and
\displaystyle\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\mathsf{t}=t

for some fixed time
t\in(0,\infty)
.

In particular, in this regime
\mathsf{t}
does not necessarily blow up as

\mathsf{m}\to\infty
.

Our second main result is as follows:

Theorem 2.17
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.16
holds.
Let
p>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be fixed.

•

In case Crt-1, as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
, one has

(2.9)

\displaystyle\log\langle u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle=\left(\frac{p^{2}\gamma_{1}(0)}{2}\right)\mathsf{e}^{-2}\mathsf{t}^{2}-p^{3/2}\chi\,\mathsf{e}^{-2}\mathsf{t}^{3/2}\big(1+o(1)\big),

where we recall that
\chi
is defined in (
1.5
).

•

In case Crt-2,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\frac{\log\langle u_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle}{\mathsf{e}^{-2}}=pt\,\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p},

where we define the variational constant

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
\|f\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{pt}{2}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}f(x)^{2}\gamma_{1}(x-y)f(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y-\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f(x)|_{2}^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\right).

2.3.1.
Interpretation of Theorem
2.17

In (
2.9
),
the first order term
\mathsf{e}^{-2}\mathsf{t}^{2}
always dominates the second order term

\mathsf{e}^{-2}\mathsf{t}^{3/2}
when
\mathsf{t}\to\infty
. Thus, no matter how quickly
\mathsf{e}
vanishes
compared to
\mathsf{t}
’s size, we do not observe
a phase transition in the large-time asymptotics similar to Theorem
2.5
in the Crt regime.

Instead, in order for the
the first- and second-order terms in (
2.9
) to be of the same
size and coalesce,
\mathsf{t}
must remain bounded, in which case both
terms grow on the order of
\mathsf{e}^{-2}
.
Case Crt-2 describes one aspect of this situation—when
\mathsf{t}

converges to a constant time
t
—which yields the asymptotic
(
2.10
). As it turns out, the latter result provides
unexpected insights into Problems
1.12
. In order to understand
why that is, we state some properties of the variational constant therein:

Proposition 2.18
.

The constant
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}
is finite for every
p>0
and
t>0
.
Moreover, letting
\mathcal{G}
be defined as in (
1.10
), we have that

(1)

if
p<\frac{2\kappa}{t\mathcal{G}}
(equivalently
t<\frac{2\kappa}{p\mathcal{G}}
),
then
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}=0
; and

(2)

if
p>\frac{2\kappa}{t\mathcal{G}}
(equivalently
t>\frac{2\kappa}{p\mathcal{G}}
) then
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}
is
positive and strictly increasing in
p
.

In particular, (
2.10
) and Proposition
2.18
(2) imply the following surprising result:

Theorem 2.19
.

Let
\xi
be one of the noises in Assumption
1.3

in the critical regime
\omega=2
.
For every fixed
t>0
, the random field

x\mapsto u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
is intermittent
as
\varepsilon\to 0
with rate function
A(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{-2}
, in the sense that the Lyapunov exponents

Given the first main result of

regarding the moment blowup threshold for the critical Skorokhod PAM,
and the blowup threshold

, it is natural
to conjecture the following:

.

in (

)

for all

If true, then combining this conjecture with

answers parts of Problems

.

2.4.

Consider the critical regime

Assumption 2.21

In the Sup regime, in addition to (

.

Suppose that Assumption
2.21
holds.
Let

and

As

(2.12)

2.4.1.
Interpretation of Theorem

Our proposed interpretation of Theorem

.

Let

in the supercritical regime

3.1.
Semigroup Theory Notations

The main tool used in the proof of our moment asymptotics is the Feynman-Kac
formula. For this, we use the following:

•

Given any
r>0
, we denote the centered open box with side-length
2r
as
Q_{r}:=(-r,r)^{d}
.

•

We use
\mathbbm{1}
to denote the function equal to one everywhere, and given a set

K
, we use
\mathbbm{1}_{K}
to denote the indicator function of
K
.

•

We use
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to denote a Brownian motion with generator
\kappa\Delta
.
We use
\mathbb{E}_{x}
and
\mathbb{P}_{x}
to denote the expectation
and probability law of
W
conditioned on the starting point
W_{0}=x
.
(
Remark.
We always assume that
W
is independent of the noise
\xi
.)

•

We use
(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to denote
W
’s normalized occupation measures; that is,

L_{t}(K):=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{\{W_{s}\in K\}}~\mathrm{d}s\qquad\text{for any Borel set $K\subset\mathbb{R}$}.

•

For every closed set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we use
\mathscr{P}(K)
to denote the set of probability measures supported in
K
.

•

We use
(\cdot,\cdot)
to denote both the
L^{2}
inner product and integration against a measure.
That is, for two functions
f,g
and a measure
\mu
, one has

(f,g):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)g(x)~\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{and}\qquad(f,\mu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)~\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

•

We denote the Dirichlet form of
\kappa\Delta
as

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}(f):=\kappa\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}~\mathrm{d}x,\qquad f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Henceforth, given a smooth function
V
(possibly random), we use
u^{V}(t,x)
to denote the solution of

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u^{V}(t,x)=\big(\kappa\Delta+V(x)\big)u^{V}(t,x)\\
u^{V}(0,\cdot)=\mathbbm{1}\end{cases},\qquad t\geq 0,~x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In particular, with the notation introduced in previous sections, we have that

u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}(t,x).

For every smooth function
V
and
r>0
we use
u^{V}_{r}(t,x)
to denote the solution of

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{r}^{V}(t,x)=\big(\kappa\Delta+V(x)\big)u_{r}^{V}(t,x)\\
u_{r}^{V}(0,\cdot)=\mathbbm{1}_{Q_{r}}\\
u^{V}_{r}(t,x)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus Q_{r}}(x)=0\end{cases},\qquad t\geq 0,~x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

By the Feynman-Kac formula,

(3.2)

\displaystyle u^{V}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{t(V,L_{t})}\right]\qquad\text{and}\qquad u^{V}_{r}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{e}^{t(V,L_{t})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{L_{t}\in\mathscr{P}(Q_{r})\}}\right].

We let
\lambda^{V}_{k}(Q_{r})
(
k\in\mathbb{N}
) denote the eigenvalues of the operator
\kappa\Delta+V
on
Q_{r}
with Dirichlet
boundary conditions in decreasing order, and we let
e^{V}_{k}(Q_{r})
denote the corresponding
orthonormal eigenfunctions. In particular, we have the spectral expansion

(3.3)

\displaystyle u^{V}_{r}(t,\cdot)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda^{V}_{k}(Q_{r})}\big(e^{V}_{k}(Q_{r}),\mathbbm{1}\big)e^{V}_{k}(Q_{r}).

Though the eigenfunctions
e^{V}_{k}(Q_{r})
are defined on
Q_{r}
,
we extend their domain to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by setting their value to be zero outside of
Q_{r}
.

3.2.
Heuristic Derivation of Theorems
2.5
,
2.17
,
and
2.22

If intermittency occurs in
u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}(t,\cdot)
,
then we expect that there exists sparse localized regions of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
inside which
u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}(t,\cdot)
takes unusually large values.
In particular, the main contribution to
\langle u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}(t,x)^{p}\rangle
comes from the event where a large
peak in the noise is present near

,
thus inducing a large peak in
u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}(t,\cdot)
near
x
as well.
Since
u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}(t,\cdot)
is stationary, there is no loss of generality in
studying this phenomenon at
x=0

In this case, we expect that we can approximate

\displaystyle\langle u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}(t,0)^{p}\rangle\approx\big\langle u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}_{R\alpha_{\varepsilon}(pt)}(t,0)^{p}\big\rangle=\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathrm{e}^{t(\xi_{\varepsilon},L_{t})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{L_{t}\in\mathscr{P}(Q_{R\alpha_{\varepsilon}(pt)})\}}\right]^{p}\right\rangle,

where

and

, and

4.1.1.
Three Remarks

Before proving Lemmas
4.2

and
4.3
, we comment on the differences
between the latter and the statement of Theorem
3.4
:
Indeed, instead of concerning the asymptotics of the moments
\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle,

Lemmas
4.2

and
4.3
concern the asymptotics of

\Big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}),\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)\Big\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{p\mathsf{t}\lambda^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})}\right\rangle

for an appropriate choice of functions

Firstly, since Propositions
4.4
and
4.5

rely on the
weak
large deviation principle for Brownian occupation measures
(e.g.,
[
15
, Section 4.2]
), it is necessary to restrict our asymptotics
to a compact domain. In Sections
5
and
6

we rigorously establish that up to an error of order
\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}

we can replace
\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle

by
\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle
, provided
\mathsf{R}
is not
too small.

Secondly, while Propositions
4.4
and
4.5
can
easily be used to obtain asymptotics for the first moment
\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},x)\rangle

(for an appropriate choice of
\mathsf{R}
), the same is not true for
\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle

when

the solution of the PAM makes it more difficult to integrate the noise in the Feynman-Kac
formula and apply the large deviation principle, as done informally in (

).
In order to get around this issue, a crucial aspect of the strategy developed in
[

]

is to show that, up to an error of order
\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}
,
we can replace
\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle
by
\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(p\mathsf{t},x)\rangle
.
Heuristically, this can be justified by using a spectral expansion and only keeping the leading
eigenvalue, as in (

However, given that the integral of the eigenfunctions

is more convenient to
control than its value

at a single point

, a formal version of
this heuristic is easier to achieve if we instead look at the integral of the solution

\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{p}.

In Sections
5
and
6
,
we rigorously prove that up to an error of order
\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}
,
one has

(4.6)

\displaystyle\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\rangle\approx\Big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{p}\Big\rangle\approx\Big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(p\mathsf{t},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)\Big\rangle.

Thirdly, the replacement of

by a truncated time

in Lemma

amounts to a technical

it is in many cases necessary to “integrate out” the contribution of the PAM’s solution on the

and

for the details.

The remainder of Section

devoted to the proof of Lemmas

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\beta_{\varepsilon}(pt)\alpha_{\varepsilon}(pt)^{4}\varepsilon^{-\omega}}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}\gamma_{1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{\varepsilon}(pt)(x-y)}{\varepsilon}\right)~\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\bigg)\bigg).

From this point on, we argue on a case-by-case basis:

Case Sub-1:
For sake of readability, let us denote

\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{h}(\mathsf{m}):=\mathsf{e}^{(\omega-2)/4}(p\mathsf{t})^{-1/4}=\big(\mathsf{e}(p\mathsf{t})^{1/(2-\omega)}\big)^{-(2-\omega)/4}.

By definition of case Sub-1 (recall Assumption
2.4
), we note that
\mathsf{h}\to 0
as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
.
By definition of
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
,
\beta_{\varepsilon}
, and
H_{\varepsilon}
in case Sub-1
(recall Definition
3.3
), we also have that

\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})^{2}H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})}{p\mathsf{t}}=\mathsf{h}^{-2}\frac{\gamma_{1}(0)}{2},\quad\frac{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})^{4}\mathsf{e}^{-\omega}}{2}=\frac{\mathsf{h}^{-2}}{2},\quad\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})}{\mathsf{e}}=\mathsf{h}.

Applying this to (
4.2
), and using (
4.2
) to
replace
F_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))=F_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})\big(1+o(1)\big)

for
F=\alpha,\beta,H
, we get that for Sub-1,

\frac{1}{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))}\log\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))(\Xi_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}}^{p},\mu)}\right\rangle\\
=\frac{\mathsf{h}^{-2}}{2}(1+o(1))\left(-\gamma_{1}(0)+\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}\gamma_{1}\big(\mathsf{h}(1+o(1))\,(x-y)\big)~\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right).

Since
\gamma_{1}(x)
is smooth and has a strict maximum at zero,
we can approximate its values for small
x
using a Taylor expansion of order 2
(e.g.,
[
25
, Theorem 1.23]
), which yields

\gamma_{1}(hx)=\gamma_{1}(0)-h^{2}\frac{x^{\top}\Sigma x}{2}+h^{3}O\big(|x|^{3}\big),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~h>0.

Therefore, given that

\sup_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}(Q_{R})}\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}O(|x|^{3})~\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)<\infty

for every
R>0
, we conclude that (
4.11
) holds for case Sub-1.

Case Sub-2:
Let us denote

\mathsf{c}=\mathsf{c}(\mathsf{m}):=\mathsf{e}(p\mathsf{t})^{1/(2-\omega)}.

Since we are in case Sub-2, we note that
\mathsf{c}\to p^{1/(2-\omega)}\mathfrak{c}

as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
; moreover,
according to Definition
3.3
in that same case, we have that

\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})^{2}H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})}{p\mathsf{t}}=0,\qquad\frac{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})^{4}\mathsf{e}^{-\omega}}{2}=\frac{\mathsf{c}^{-\omega}}{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})}{\mathsf{e}}=\frac{1}{\mathsf{c}}.

Thus, by (
4.2
) and (
4.2
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))}\log\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))(\Xi_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}}^{p},\mu)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1+o(1)}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}\gamma_{\mathsf{c}(1+o(1))}(x-y)~\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

(4.16)

\displaystyle=-J_{p^{1/(2-\omega)}\mathfrak{c}}(\mu)+o(1)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}\Big(\gamma_{\mathsf{c}(1+o(1))}(x-y)-\gamma_{p^{1/(2-\omega)}\mathfrak{c}}(x-y)\Big)~\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

As
\gamma_{1}
is smooth and bounded and
\gamma_{c}(x)=c^{-\omega}\gamma_{1}(x/c)
, one has
\gamma_{\mathsf{c}(1+o(1))}\to\gamma_{p^{1/(2-\omega)}\mathfrak{c}}
uniformly on compacts
as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
.
Thus, the integral in (
4.16
) is uniformly small
over
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(Q_{R})
as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
, concluding the proof of (
4.11
) in this case.

Case Crt-1:
In this case, according to Definition
3.3
, we have that

\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})^{2}H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})}{p\mathsf{t}}=(p\mathsf{t})^{1/2}\frac{\gamma_{1}(0)}{2},\qquad\frac{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})^{4}\mathsf{e}^{-\omega}}{2}=\frac{(p\mathsf{t})^{1/2}}{2},\qquad\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})}{\mathsf{e}}=(p\mathsf{t})^{-1/4}.

The proof then follows from the same argument as for case Sub-1,
with the only difference that we replace the function denoted
\mathsf{h}
therein by
(p\mathsf{t})^{-1/4}
.

Case Crt-2:
In this case, according to Definition
3.3
, we have that

\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})^{2}H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})}{p\mathsf{t}}=0,\qquad\frac{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})^{4}\mathsf{e}^{-\omega}}{2}=\frac{p\mathsf{t}}{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})}{\mathsf{e}}=1.

Thus, by (
4.2
) and (
4.2
), we have

\frac{1}{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))}\log\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))(\Xi_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}}^{p},\mu)}\right\rangle=\frac{-p\mathsf{t}}{2}J_{1+o(1)}(\mu)\big(1+o(1)\big).

The result then follows from the facts that
\mathsf{t}\to t
as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty

(by definition of case Crt-2; see Assumption
2.16
)
and, arguing as (
4.16
),

\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\sup_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}(Q_{R})}\left|J_{1+o(1)}(\mu)-J_{1}(\mu)\right|=0.

Case Sup:
This follows from the same argument
as Sub-1 with

\mathsf{h}=\mathsf{h}(\mathsf{m}):=\mathsf{e}^{(\omega-2)/4}(p\mathsf{t})^{-1/4}=\big(\mathsf{e}(p\mathsf{t})^{-1/(\omega-2)}\big)^{(\omega-2)/4},

which vanishes as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
thanks to Assumption
2.21
.
With this case in hand, the proof of (
4.11
), and thus Proposition
4.4
, is now complete.
∎

In Case Sub-3, the same calculation as in (
4.16
)
implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))}\log\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))(\Xi_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}}^{p},L_{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))})}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{2}}\gamma_{\mathsf{e}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))^{1/(2-\omega)}}(x-y)~\mathrm{d}L_{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))}(x)\mathrm{d}L_{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))}(y)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{-J_{\mathsf{e}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))^{1/(2-\omega)}}(L_{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))})}{2}.

By definition of case Sub-3 and (
4.2
),
\mathsf{e}(p(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d}))^{1/(2-\omega)}\to 0
as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
;
thus the proof of (
4.12
) is simply a matter of noting that
for every
\varepsilon>\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
and
t>0
,
one has

(4.17)

\displaystyle-J_{\varepsilon}(L_{t})\leq-J_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(L_{t})\leq-J_{0}(L_{t}).

Letting
\widehat{\cdot}
denote the Fourier transform, we have by definition of
L_{t}
, the Parseval formula,
and the convolution theorem that

(4.18)

\displaystyle-J_{\varepsilon}(L_{t})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}(W_{s},x)}~\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\widehat{\gamma}(x)\widehat{p_{\varepsilon}}(x)~\mathrm{d}x.

Since
\gamma
is a covariance function,
\widehat{\gamma}\geq 0
.
Computing explicitly that

\widehat{p_{\varepsilon}}(x)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi^{2}|x|^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}&\varepsilon>0\\
1&\varepsilon=0\end{cases},

and noting that this function is decreasing in
\varepsilon
for all
x
,
we therefore conclude (
4.17
).

We now prove (
4.13
). Once again using Fourier transforms,
we note that

(4.19)

\displaystyle-J_{\varepsilon}(f^{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f^{2}}(x)|^{2}\widehat{\gamma}(x)\widehat{p_{\varepsilon}}(x)~\mathrm{d}x.

Consequently,
J_{\varepsilon}(f^{2})\geq J_{0}(f^{2})

for every function
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\|f\|_{2}=1
and
\varepsilon>0
. With this in hand, in order
to prove (
4.13
), it suffices to show that for every
such
f
, one has

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}J_{\varepsilon}(f^{2})=J_{0}(f^{2}).

This follows from (
4.19
) by monotone convergence.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.3

We begin with (

).
Let us define

.
By applying the same scaling identities used to obtain (

\Big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(p\mathsf{t},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)\Big\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})+o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}\Big\langle\big(u^{\Xi_{\mathsf{e},\mathsf{t}}^{p}}_{\tilde{\mathsf{R}}}(p\mathsf{t},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)\Big\rangle.

We then obtain (

)

.

5.5.
Proof of Lemma

Note that
\boldsymbol{\chi}^{p}_{R}\geq\boldsymbol{\chi}^{p}_{R^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathsf{m}\to\infty}\frac{\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)^{p}\big\rangle}{\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\rangle}=0.

On the one hand, for
p\geq 1
, as remarked in
[
21
, (3.16)]
, combining the triangle inequality with (
6.5
)
and the fact that
u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}(t,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{distr.}}}{{=}}u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}(t,0)\geq u^{\xi_{\varepsilon}}_{R}(t,0)\geq 0

implies that

0\leq\big\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\big\rangle^{1/p}-\big\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\big\rangle^{1/p}\leq\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)^{p}\big\rangle^{1/p}=o\big(\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\rangle^{1/p}\big).

If we divide all terms in the above inequality by
\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\rangle^{1/p}
, then we obtain (
6.2
).
On the other hand, for
0<p<1
we have that

(x+y)^{p}\leq x^{p}+y^{p}
for all
x,y\geq 0
, hence

0\leq\big\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},x)^{p}\big\rangle-\big\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\big\rangle\leq\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)^{p}\big\rangle=o\big(\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\rangle\big);

this also implies (
6.2
).

In order to establish (
6.5
), we use two tools:
Let
\tau_{r}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:W_{t}\not\in Q_{r}\}

denote the first hitting time of
Q_{r}
’s boundary by
W
.
By the Feynman-Kac formula, one has

(6.6)

\displaystyle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)=\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{\mathsf{R}}\leq\mathsf{t}\}}\right],

and by the reflection principle,

(6.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}[\tau_{\mathsf{R}}\leq\mathsf{t}]\leq\mathrm{e}^{-C\mathsf{R}^{2}/\mathsf{t}}

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\mathsf{m}
.
The details of the proof of (
6.5
)
using these depend on which case we are considering,
and thus we split the remainder of the proof into two steps.

6.2.1.
Sub-1, Crt-1, and Sup

Suppose first that
p\geq 1
. In that case,
Thanks to (
6.6
) and
[
21
, (3.17)]

(note that, in the notation of
[
21
]
,
R(pt)
corresponds to
\mathsf{R}
in our setting),
we have that for
p\geq 1
and large
\mathsf{m}
,

(6.8)

\displaystyle\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)^{p}\big\rangle\leq\mathrm{e}^{H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})-C\mathsf{R}^{2}/\mathsf{t}}.

If we combine this with the fact that

(6.9)

\displaystyle\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\rangle\geq\mathrm{e}^{-H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})+\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})\boldsymbol{\chi}^{p}(1+o(1))}\qquad\text{as }\mathsf{m}\to\infty

thanks to (
5.1
), we have that

\frac{\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)^{p}\big\rangle}{\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\rangle}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})\boldsymbol{\chi}^{p}(1+o(1))-C\mathsf{R}^{2}/\mathsf{t}}\qquad\text{as }\mathsf{m}\to\infty.

When
0<p<1
, we use Jensen’s inequality and (
6.8
)
in the case
p=1
to get

\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)^{p}\big\rangle\leq\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)\big\rangle^{p}\leq\mathrm{e}^{pH_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t})-pC\mathsf{R}^{2}/\mathsf{t}}.

Once again combining this with (
5.1
) yields

\frac{\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)^{p}\big\rangle}{\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\rangle}\leq\mathrm{e}^{pH_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t})-H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})+\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})\boldsymbol{\chi}^{p}(1+o(1))-pC\mathsf{R}^{2}/\mathsf{t}}\qquad\text{as }\mathsf{m}\to\infty.

Since
\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})=o(H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))=o(H_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t}))
in cases Sub-1, Crt-1, and Sup,
in order to prove (
6.5
)
it suffices to find a function
\mathsf{R}
that satisfies
\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})=o(\mathsf{R})
,

\mathsf{R}=\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}
, and
H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})=o(\mathsf{R}^{2}/\mathsf{t})
.
It is easily seen to be the case that
\mathsf{R}=H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})
satisfies all three of these conditions.

6.2.2.
Sub-2, Sub-3, and Crt-2

If
p\geq 1
, then by Jensen’s inequality and (
6.6
), we have that

\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)^{p}\big\rangle\leq\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}p\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{\mathsf{R}}\leq\mathsf{t}\}}\right]\right\rangle.

If we then apply Hölder’s inequality and (
6.7
), we get that

\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)^{p}\big\rangle\leq\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}2p\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]\right\rangle^{1/2}\mathrm{e}^{-C\mathsf{R}^{2}/2\mathsf{t}}

as
\mathsf{m}\to\infty
.
Similarly, when
0<p<1
we have that

\big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)-u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t},0)\big)^{p}\big\rangle\leq\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}2\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]\right\rangle^{p/2}\mathrm{e}^{-pC\mathsf{R}^{2}/2\mathsf{t}}.

In cases Sub-2, Sub-3, and Crt-2, it is always the case that

\alpha_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})=o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))
,

\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})=\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}
, and

\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})=o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})^{2}/\mathsf{t})
.
Moreover, since
H_{\varepsilon}=0
in those cases, we know from
(
5.1
)
that
\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)^{p}\rangle^{-1}=\mathrm{e}^{O(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}
.
Thus, by choosing
\mathsf{R}=\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})
, in order to prove (
6.5
), it is enough to show that

\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]\right\rangle\leq\mathrm{e}^{O(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}

for every
\theta>0
.
On the one hand, in cases Sub-1 and Sub-2, this follows from the fact that

\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]\right\rangle\leq\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\theta\xi(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]\right\rangle\leq\mathrm{e}^{O(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))},

where the first inequality follows from the same argument as in the proof of (
4.17
),
and the second inequality follows from (
1.7
) (except that we replace
\gamma
by
\theta^{2}\gamma

in the variational problem
\mathbf{M}
).
On the other hand, in case Crt-2, we simply note that

\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]\right\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{[0,\mathsf{t}]^{2}}\theta^{2}\gamma_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{u}-W_{v})~\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v\right)\right]\leq\mathrm{e}^{\theta^{2}\mathsf{t}^{2}\mathsf{e}^{-2}\|\gamma_{1}\|_{\infty}},

recalling that
\mathsf{e}^{-2}=O(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))
and that
\mathsf{t}
is bounded in that case.
With this, the proof of Lemma
6.1
is now complete.

6.3.
Proof of Lemma
6.2

The proof of this result is very similar
to that of Lemma
5.4
.
We begin with the following general bound:

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
p>0
,
\theta\geq 1
and
\mathsf{R}=\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}
.
For any function
\mathsf{d}
such that
0\leq\mathsf{d}<\mathsf{t}
,

\Big\langle\big(u^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{p}\Big\rangle\leq\begin{cases}\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}\left(\Big\langle\big(u^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(p\mathsf{t},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)\Big\rangle+1\right)&\text{if }p\geq 1\\
\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{p\mathsf{t}\lambda^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})}+1\right)&\text{if }0<p<1\end{cases}.

By arguing as in
[
21
, (3.37)]
,
for every
p>0
, we have that

(6.10)

\displaystyle\big(u^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{p}\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathsf{t}\lambda^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})}\big(e^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}}),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{2}+\big(2\mathsf{R}\big)^{2d}\right)^{p}.

Suppose first that
p\geq 1
. Since

f(k):=\frac{\big(e^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}}),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{2}}{(2\mathsf{R})^{2d}},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}

is a probability measure on
\mathbb{N}
, an application of Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathsf{t}\lambda^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})}\big(e^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}}),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{2}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq(2\mathsf{R})^{2d(p-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{p\mathsf{t}\lambda^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})}\big(e^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}}),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=(2\mathsf{R})^{2d(p-1)}\big(u^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(p\mathsf{t},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big).

If we combine this with

\mathsf{R}=\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}
, the inequality
(x+y)^{p}\leq 2^{p}(x^{p}+y^{p})

for all
x,y>0
and
p\geq 1
, and (
6.10
),
then we obtain the claimed result for
p\geq 1
.

Now suppose that
0<p<1
.
Since
(\sum_{k}x_{k})^{p}\leq\sum_{k}x_{k}^{p}
for every
0<p<1
and
x_{k}\geq 0
, we get
from (
6.10
) that

\big(u^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{p}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{p\mathsf{t}\lambda^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})}\big(e^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}}),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{2p}+\big(2\mathsf{R}\big)^{2dp}.

Given that
\mathsf{R}=\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}
and

\big(e^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{k}(Q_{\mathsf{R}}),\mathbbm{1}\big)\leq(2\mathsf{R})^{d}
, the result follows.
∎With this in hand, we may now prove (
6.3
) and (
6.4
):

6.3.1.
Proof of (
6.3
)

Let
A_{\mathsf{d},\mathsf{t}}(Q_{r})
be as in (
5.8
),
and let
\mathsf{d}
,
\mathsf{c}
, and
\mathsf{h}
be as in (
5.9
).
By the Feynman-Kac formula,

u_{\mathsf{R}}^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}(\mathsf{t},0)\leq\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{A_{\mathsf{d},\mathsf{t}}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})\}}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\int_{0}^{\mathsf{d}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\leq\mathsf{c}\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\int_{0}^{\mathsf{d}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s>\mathsf{c}\}})\right]\\
\leq\mathrm{e}^{\mathsf{c}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{\mathsf{d}}^{\mathsf{t}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{A_{\mathsf{d},\mathsf{t}}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\int_{0}^{\mathsf{d}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s>\mathsf{c}\}}\right].

Since
\mathsf{c}=o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))
and
(x+y)^{p}\leq\zeta(p)(x^{p}+y^{p})

for some constant
\zeta(p)>0

whenever
x,y\geq 0
, thanks to Proposition
6.3
,
(
6.3
) will be proved if we show that,
for large enough
\mathsf{m}
, one has

(6.11)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{\mathsf{d}}^{\mathsf{t}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{A_{\mathsf{d},\mathsf{t}}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})\}}\right]^{p}\right\rangle\leq\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}\Big\langle\big(u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{p}\Big\rangle

and

(6.12)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\int_{0}^{\mathsf{d}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s>\mathsf{c}\}}\right]^{p}\right\rangle\leq\begin{cases}\mathrm{e}^{-p\mathsf{h}\mathsf{c}+4H_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t})}&\text{if }p\geq 1\\
\mathrm{e}^{-p\mathsf{h}\mathsf{c}+4pH_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t})}&\text{if }0<p<1\end{cases}.

(
6.11
) can be proved using essentially the same argument as (
5.10
), except that this
time we use the upper bound
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Pi_{\mathsf{d}}(x)=O(\mathsf{d}^{-d/2})=\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}
.
As for (
6.12
), essentially the same argument used
to arrive at (
5.12
) together with Jensen’s inequality yields

\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\int_{0}^{\mathsf{d}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s>\mathsf{c}\}}\right]^{p}\right\rangle\\
\leq\mathrm{e}^{-p\mathsf{h}\mathsf{c}}\left\langle\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\frac{1+\mathsf{h}\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq s\leq\mathsf{d}\}}}{\mathsf{t}+\mathsf{h}\mathsf{d}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\mathrm{e}^{(\mathsf{t}+\mathsf{h}\mathsf{d})\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})}\right]~\mathrm{d}s\right)^{p}\right\rangle=\begin{cases}\mathrm{e}^{-p\mathsf{h}\mathsf{c}+H_{\mathsf{e}}(p(\mathsf{t}+\mathsf{h}\mathsf{d}))}&\text{if }p\geq 1\\
\mathrm{e}^{-p\mathsf{h}\mathsf{c}+pH_{\mathsf{e}}(\mathsf{t}+\mathsf{h}\mathsf{d})}&\text{if }0<p<1\end{cases}.

Then, we obtain (
6.12
) by
noting that since
\mathsf{h}\mathsf{d}\leq\mathsf{t}
for large enough
\mathsf{m}
, we have the inequality

(\mathsf{t}+\mathsf{h}\mathsf{d})^{2}\leq 2(\mathsf{t}^{2}+(\mathsf{h}\mathsf{d})^{2})\leq 4\mathsf{t}^{2}
for large enough
\mathsf{m}
;
hence
H_{\mathsf{e}}(q(\mathsf{t}+\mathsf{h}\mathsf{d}))\leq\mathsf{4}H_{\mathsf{e}}(q\mathsf{t})
for all
q>0
.

6.3.2.
Proof of (
6.4
)

Let
\theta>1
be arbitrary, and let
\theta^{\prime}>1
be such that
1/\theta^{\prime}+1/\theta=1
.
For every function
\mathsf{d}=\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{m})
such that
0\leq\mathsf{d}<\mathsf{t}
,
the Feynman-Kac formula followed by two applications of Hölder’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\big\langle u^{\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(t,x)\big\rangle^{p}=\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{t}}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{L_{\mathsf{t}}\in\mathscr{P}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})\}}\right]^{p}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{d}}\theta^{\prime}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]^{p/\theta^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{\mathsf{d}}^{\mathsf{t}}\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{A_{\mathsf{d},\mathsf{t}}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})\}}\right]^{p/\theta}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{d}}\theta^{\prime}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]^{p}\right\rangle^{1/\theta^{\prime}}\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{\mathsf{d}}^{\mathsf{t}}\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{A_{\mathsf{d},\mathsf{t}}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})\}}\right]^{p}\right\rangle^{1/\theta}.

By the Markov property,

\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{\mathsf{d}}^{\mathsf{t}}\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{A_{\mathsf{d},\mathsf{t}}(Q_{\mathsf{R}})\}}\right]^{p}\leq O(\mathsf{d}^{-d/2})\big(u^{\theta\xi_{\mathsf{e}}}_{\mathsf{R}}(\mathsf{t}-\mathsf{d},\cdot),\mathbbm{1}\big)^{p}.

Therefore, by Proposition
6.3
, it suffices to
make sure that it is possible to choose
\mathsf{d}
such that
\mathsf{d}=\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}

and

\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\mathsf{d}}\theta^{\prime}\xi_{\mathsf{e}}(W_{s})~\mathrm{d}s\right)\right]^{p}\right\rangle\leq\mathrm{e}^{o(\beta_{\mathsf{e}}(p\mathsf{t}))}.

This can be proved using the
same argument as in (
5.13
)
(up to applying Jensen’s inequality to move
p
inside of
\mathbb{E}_{0}
or outside of
\langle\cdot\rangle
,
depending on whether
p\geq 1
or
0<p<1
).

With this, the proof of Lemma
6.2
—and
therefore also (
6.1
)—is now complete.
In particular, combining this with (
5.1
), this
concludes the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

7.1.
The Constant
\mathcal{G}
for Riesz Noise

We begin with the following statement:

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
d>2
and
\sigma>0
, and
take
\gamma(x)=\sigma^{2}|x|^{-2}
.
There exists a finite constant
C>0
such that
for every
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|f\|_{2}=1
, one has

\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)\leq C\mathscr{S}(f)
.

While we did not find a proof for this exact statement in the literature, it follows
from standard scaling arguments (e.g.,
[
5
, Lemma A.4]
) and the
fact that

\sup_{f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),~\|f\|_{2}=1}\left(\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)^{1/2}-\mathscr{S}(f)\right)<\infty

(e.g., apply a trivial change of variables to
[
2
, (1.19)]
).

7.2.
Proof of Proposition
2.18

The fact that
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}<\infty
follows from
its definition in (
2.11
), and noting that if
\|f\|_{2}=1
, then

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}(f)\leq\frac{\|\gamma_{1}\|_{\infty}\|f\|_{2}^{4}}{2}=\frac{\|\gamma_{1}\|_{\infty}}{2}<\infty.

Next, we show that
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}
is positive if
p>\frac{2\kappa}{t\mathcal{G}}
.
The proof is based on scaling arguments.
Given
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|f\|_{2}=1
, we denote

f_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{d/2}f(\varepsilon x)
.
By definition of
\gamma_{1}
, and since
\omega=2
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(\varepsilon^{-1}x)=\gamma*p_{1}(\varepsilon^{-1}x)=\varepsilon^{2}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x).

Consequently, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|f\|_{2}=1
, one has (by a change
of variables)

(7.2)

\displaystyle pt\mathcal{J}_{1}(f_{\varepsilon})-\mathscr{S}(f_{\varepsilon})=pt\varepsilon^{2d}\mathcal{J}_{1}\big(f(\varepsilon\cdot)\big)-\varepsilon^{2}\mathscr{S}(f)=\varepsilon^{2}\big(pt\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(f)-\mathscr{S}(f)\big).

Thus, an application of monotone convergence (using Fourier transforms as in (
4.19
))
yields

(7.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{-2}\big(pt\mathcal{J}_{1}(f_{\varepsilon})-\mathscr{S}(f_{\varepsilon})\big)=pt\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)-\mathscr{S}(f).

This relates the critical variational problem (
2.11
) to the following new variational problem:

(7.4)

\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}:=\sup_{f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),~\|f\|_{2}=1}\big(pt\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)-\mathscr{S}(f)\big).

Indeed, if
p>\frac{2\kappa}{t\mathcal{G}}
, then by definition of
\mathcal{G}

we have that
\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}>0
(in fact, we actually have that
\overline{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}=\infty
by a similar scaling argument as above). Therefore there exists
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|f\|_{2}=1
such that

\displaystyle pt\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)-\mathscr{S}(f)>0.

Hence by (
7.3
) we have for
\varepsilon>0
small enough

\displaystyle pt\mathcal{J}_{1}(f_{\varepsilon})-\mathscr{S}(f_{\varepsilon})>0,

which implies that
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}
is positive since
f_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

with
\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{2}=1
for every
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|f\|_{2}=1
.

We now argue that if
p<\frac{2\kappa}{t\mathcal{G}}
, then
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}=0
.
In this case by definition of
\mathcal{G}
, for any
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|f\|_{2}=1
we have

pt\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)-\mathscr{S}(f)\leq 0.

Since

\mathcal{J}_{1}(f)\leq\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)

(this is easily checked using a Fourier transform as in (
4.19
)),
this implies that
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}\leq 0
.
The fact that
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}=0
then immediately follows from
(
7.3
).

We now finish the proof of Proposition
2.18
by showing that
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}
is strictly increasing in
p

whenever
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}>0
.
Take
p>q>\frac{2\kappa}{t\mathcal{G}}
.
For every
\delta>0
, we can find some
g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\|g\|_{2}=1
and

qt\mathcal{J}_{1}(g)-\mathscr{S}(g)\geq\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,q}-\delta.

Since
\mathscr{S}(g)\geq 0
, this also implies that

qt\mathcal{J}_{1}(g)\geq\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,q}-\delta.

In particular,

\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}-\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,q}\geq pt\mathcal{J}_{1}(g)-\mathscr{S}(g)-\big(qt\mathcal{J}_{1}(g)-\mathscr{S}(g)+\delta\big)\\
=(p-q)t\mathcal{J}_{1}(g)-\delta\geq\frac{(p-q)(\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,q}-\delta)}{q}-\delta.

If we take
\delta\to 0
in the above, this yields

\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,p}-\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,q}\geq\frac{p-q}{q}\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{crt}}_{t,q}>0,

concluding the proof.

7.4.
Proof of Lemma
7.2

The finiteness of
\mathbf{M}_{\mathfrak{c},p}
follows from the same argument as in
(
7.1
). As for positivity,
let
f
be such that
\|f\|_{2}=1
, and let
f_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{d/2}f(\varepsilon x)
.
Arguing as in (
7.2
) and (
7.3
),
except that in the subcritical case one has

\gamma_{p^{1/(2-\omega)}\mathfrak{c}}(\varepsilon^{-1}x)=\varepsilon^{\omega}\gamma_{\varepsilon p^{1/(2-\omega)}\mathfrak{c}}(\varepsilon^{-1}x),

we see that as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}(f_{\varepsilon})-\mathscr{S}(f_{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon^{2}\Big(\varepsilon^{\omega-2}\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon p^{1/(2-\omega)}\mathfrak{c}}(f)-\mathscr{S}(f)\Big)=\varepsilon^{2}\Big(\varepsilon^{\omega-2}\big(1+o(1)\big)\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)-\mathscr{S}(f)\Big).

Since
\varepsilon^{\omega-2}\to\infty
when

, the positivity of
\mathbf{M}_{\mathfrak{c},p}

follows from the existence of a function

such that

\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)>0
, which is trivial.

7.5.
Proof of Lemma
7.3

Since
\gamma
is a covariance function, the bilinear map

(7.7)

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\gamma}:=\frac{1}{2}\iint f(x)\gamma(x-y)g(y)~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

is a semi-inner product. Thus, its induced semi-norm satisfies the (reverse) triangle inequality, whence
for every functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
(noting that
\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)=\langle f^{2},f^{2}\rangle_{\gamma}
), one has

(7.8)

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{1})}-\sqrt{\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{2})}\right|\leq\sqrt{\langle f_{1}^{2}-f_{2}^{2},f_{1}^{2}-f_{2}^{2}\rangle_{\gamma}}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left|\langle f_{1}^{2}-f_{2}^{2},f_{i}^{2}\rangle_{\gamma}\right|}.

The claimed result then follows from the fact that in the subcritical regime, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one has

(7.9)

\displaystyle\int\gamma(x-y)f(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y\leq C\|f\|_{2}^{2-\omega}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{\omega}.

Indeed,
if we combine (
7.9
) with (
7.8
),
we get that (
7.8
) is bounded above by

(7.10)

\sqrt{C\left(\int|f_{1}(x)^{2}-f_{2}(x)^{2}|~\mathrm{d}x\right)\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|f_{i}\|_{2}^{2-\omega}\|\nabla f_{i}\|_{2}^{\omega}}\\
=\sqrt{C\left(\int|f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x)||f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)|~\mathrm{d}x\right)\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|f_{i}\|_{2}^{2-\omega}\|\nabla f_{i}\|_{2}^{\omega}},

If we now apply Cauchy-Schwarz in the
\mathrm{d}x
integral in (
7.10
),
then we get that (
7.8
) is bounded above by

(7.11)

\displaystyle\sqrt{C\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{2}\|f_{1}+f_{2}\|_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|f_{i}\|_{2}^{2-\omega}\|\nabla f_{i}\|_{2}^{\omega}}

which immediately implies the result.
To conclude, we note that
the inequality (
7.9
) in the case of Riesz and fractional noise is proved in
[
5
, (A.17) and (A.29)]
.
In the case of one-dimensional white noise, we remark that every
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})

is uniformly Hölder-
1/2
(since
|f(y)-f(x)|\leq\left|\int_{x}^{y}f^{\prime}(z)~\mathrm{d}z\right|\leq\|f^{\prime}\|_{2}\sqrt{y-x}

for all
y>x
).
Therefore, since
f
is square-integrable,
f(x)\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
, which then implies by Cauchy-Schwarz that

\int\delta_{0}(x-y)f(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y=f(x)^{2}=-\int_{x}^{\infty}(f(y)^{2})^{\prime}=-2\int_{x}^{\infty}f(y)f^{\prime}(y)~\mathrm{d}y\leq 2\|f\|_{2}\|f^{\prime}\|_{2}.

7.6.
Proof of Lemma
7.4

As mentioned in Section
7.3.1
,
we only need to prove the result in the case of subcritical fractional
noise; we thus henceforth assume that
\gamma(x)=\sigma^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}

Our proof relies on the version of the concentration-compactness

, which is as follows:

Lemma 7.7

(
[

]
)
.

Let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that
f_{n}\geq 0
and
\|f_{n}\|_{2}=1

for every

. There exists a subsequence
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

that satisfies one of the following three possibilities:

(1)

(
Compactness
) There exists a sequence
(z_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{\{|x|<R\}}f_{n_{k}}(x-z_{k})^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\geq 1-\varepsilon,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(2)

(
Vanishing
) For every

(7.13)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|x|<R\}}f_{n_{k}}(x-z)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x=0.

(3)

(

) There exists

such that for every
\varepsilon>0
,
there is some

and nonnegative

•

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|f_{k}^{(i)}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty

•

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\inf\left\{|x-y|:x\in\mathrm{supp}(f_{k}^{(1)})\text{ and }y\in\mathrm{supp}(f_{k}^{(2)})\right\}=\infty

•

for every

,
\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int|\nabla f_{n_{k}}(x)|^{2}-|\nabla f_{k}^{(1)}(x)|^{2}-|\nabla f_{k}^{(2)}(x)|^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\geq 0
.

(
Remark.
Though this is not explicit in the notation,

k_{0}
and

depend on

Our objective is to show that any sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that satisfies
(
7.5
) must also satisfy the compactness
condition stated in (
7.12
). Indeed, if such is
the case, then a direct application of the argument contained in the two
paragraphs following
[
36

yields (
7.6
).
Thanks to Lemma
7.7
, for this it suffices to prove that

.

7.6.1.
Vanishing

We begin by proving that vanishing does not occur.
Suppose that the sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies (
7.5
), and
that we can find a subsequence such that (
7.13
) occurs.
We claim that this implies that
\mathbf{M}\leq 0
, which is a contradiction.
Thanks to (
7.5
), in order to prove
\mathbf{M}\leq 0
,
it is enough to show that the vanishing condition (
7.13
)
implies that

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{n_{k}})=0.

For this purpose, for every
\theta>0
, this limsup is bounded above by

(7.14)

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\iint_{\{|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq\theta~\forall 1\leq i\leq d\}}f_{n_{k}}(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

(7.15)

\displaystyle\qquad+\limsup_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\iint_{\{|x_{j}-y_{j}|>\theta\}}f_{n_{k}}(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

We now prove that (
7.14
) vanishes for all
\theta>0
,
and that (
7.15
) can be made arbitrarily small by taking
\theta\to\infty
.

We begin with the claim regarding (
7.14
).
By a straightforward change of variables and the fact that
\|f_{n_{k}}\|_{2}=1
, we have that

\iint_{\{|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq\theta~\forall 1\leq i\leq d\}}f_{n_{k}}(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|y_{i}|\leq\theta~\forall 1\leq i\leq d\}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(x+y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y.

For every
\theta>0
, we can find a smooth and compactly supported function
g_{\theta}
and
a large enough
R_{\theta}>0
such that

\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y_{i}|\leq\theta~\forall 1\leq i\leq d\}}\leq g_{\theta}(y)\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|<R_{\theta}\}},\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In particular, an application of (
7.9
) with the function

f(y)^{2}=f_{n_{k}}(x+y)^{2}g_{\theta}(y)\leq f_{n_{k}}(x+y)^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|<R_{\theta}\}}

yields

(7.16)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|y_{i}|\leq\theta~\forall 1\leq i\leq d\}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(x+y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq C\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|f_{n_{k}}(x+\cdot)\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\cdot|<R_{\theta}\}}\|_{2}^{2-\omega}\cdot\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\|\nabla\big(f_{n_{k}}(x+\cdot)\sqrt{g_{\theta}}\big)\Big\|_{2}^{\omega}.

The first supremum on the right-hand side of (
7.16
)
goes to zero as
k\to\infty
for every
R_{\theta}>0
thanks to the vanishing condition (
7.13
).
Thus, in order to show that the lim sup in (
7.14
) is zero, it suffices to show
that the second supremum on the right-hand side of (
7.16
) remains
bounded as
k\to\infty
. For this purpose, we apply the product rule and
(x+y)^{2}\leq 2(x^{2}+y^{2})
, which yields

\displaystyle\sup_{k\geq 0}\Big\|\nabla\big(f_{n_{k}}(x+\cdot)\sqrt{g_{\theta}}\big)\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{k\geq 0}2\int\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(x+y)\right)^{2}g_{\theta}(y)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}\sqrt{g_{\theta}(y)}\right)^{2}f_{n_{k}}(x+y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{k\geq 0}2\|\nabla f_{n_{k}}\|_{2}^{2}+2d\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left\|\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}\sqrt{g_{\theta}}\right)^{2}\right\|_{\infty}.

By combining (
7.9
) with
\mathbf{M}>0
and
\omega<2
,
the fact that
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies (
7.5
) implies that

(7.17)

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\|\nabla f_{n}\|_{2}<\infty,

as desired.

We now conclude the proof that vanishing does not occur by
showing that (
7.15
) can be made arbitrarily small by taking
\theta\to\infty
.
Note that, for any
\theta>0
, one has

(7.18)

\iint_{\{|x_{1}-y_{1}|>\theta\}}f_{n_{k}}(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq\theta^{-\omega_{1}}\iint f_{n_{k}}(x)^{2}\prod_{i=2}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Let us denote
\tilde{x}:=(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})
and similarly for
\tilde{y}
, as well as
\tilde{\omega}:=\sum_{i=2}^{d}\omega_{i}
.
If we apply (
7.9
) with
\gamma(x)=\prod_{i=2}^{d}|x_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}
, then we get that for every fixed
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}

(7.19)

\displaystyle\sup_{\tilde{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\int f_{n_{k}}(x_{1},\tilde{x})^{2}\prod_{i=2}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}~\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}\leq C\|f_{n_{k}}(x_{1},\cdot)\|_{2}^{2-\tilde{\omega}}\|\nabla f_{n_{k}}(x_{1},\cdot)\|_{2}^{\tilde{\omega}}.

for some constant
C>0
independent of
x_{1}
,
k
, and
\theta
.
In particular,
the right-hand side of (
7.18
) is bounded above by

C\theta^{-\omega_{1}}\iint\|f_{n_{k}}(x_{1},\cdot)\|_{2}^{2-\tilde{\omega}}\|\nabla f_{n_{k}}(x_{1},\cdot)\|_{2}^{\tilde{\omega}}f_{n_{k}}(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}y.

Now integrate out
\mathrm{d}y
from the above display, which yields

C\theta^{-\omega_{1}}\int\|f_{n_{k}}(x_{1},\cdot)\|_{2}^{2-\tilde{\omega}}\|\nabla f_{n_{k}}(x_{1},\cdot)\|_{2}^{\tilde{\omega}}~\mathrm{d}x_{1}.

Next, applying Hölder’s inequality with
p=\frac{1}{1-\tilde{\omega}/2}
and
q=\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}/2}
gives the upper bound

C\theta^{-\omega_{1}}\int\|f_{n_{k}}(x_{1},\cdot)\|_{2}^{2-\tilde{\omega}}\|\nabla f_{n_{k}}(x_{1},\cdot)\|_{2}^{\tilde{\omega}}~\mathrm{d}x_{1}\leq C\theta^{-\omega_{1}}\|\nabla f_{n_{k}}\|_{2}^{\tilde{\omega}}.

Combining this with (
7.17
) and (
7.18
), we conclude that

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\iint_{\{|x_{1}-y_{1}|>\theta\}}f_{n_{k}}(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=O(\theta^{-\omega_{1}}).

Using the same argument (but interchanging the roles of
x_{1}
and
y_{1}
with
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
), one has

(7.20)

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\iint_{\{|x_{j}-y_{j}|>\theta\}}f_{n_{k}}(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=O(\theta^{-\omega_{j}})

for all
2\leq j\leq d
.
With this, we obtain that

\lim_{\theta\searrow 0}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\iint_{\{|x_{j}-y_{j}|>\theta\}}f_{n_{k}}(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f_{n_{k}}(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=0,

thus concluding the proof that vanishing cannot occur at the same time as (
7.5
).

7.6.2.
Dichotomy

We now conclude the proof of Lemma
7.4

by showing that the dichotomy condition stated in Lemma
7.7

cannot occur at the same time as (
7.5
).
Suppose then that
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies both (
7.5
)
and the dichotomy condition. The contradiction that we obtain from this assumption
is based on the following quantities: For every
0<a<1
, we define

\mathbf{M}(a):=\sup_{f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),~\|f\|_{2}^{2}=a}\big(\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)-\mathscr{S}(f)\big).

By the straightforward change of variables
f\mapsto f/\sqrt{a}
, we note that for any
a<1
, one has

\mathbf{M}(a):=\sup_{f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),~\|f\|_{2}=1}a\big(a\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)-\mathscr{S}(f)\big)<a\mathbf{M}.

Therefore,

(7.21)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(a)+\mathbf{M}(1-a)<a\mathbf{M}+(1-a)\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}\qquad\text{for every }0<a<1.

We now prove that the assumption that
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies both
(
7.5
)
and the dichotomy condition
contradicts (
7.21
).

Let
0<a<1
be as in the dichotomy statement in Lemma
7.7
,
and for every
\varepsilon>0
, recall the definitions of
f^{(i)}_{k}
and
\delta_{p}(\varepsilon)

in the same statement.
By (
7.5
) and the definition of the dichotomy condition, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\Big(\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{n_{k}})-\mathscr{S}(f_{n_{k}})\Big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\Big(\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(i)})-\mathscr{S}(f_{k}^{(i)})\Big)+\Big(\mathscr{S}(f_{k}^{(1)})+\mathscr{S}(f_{k}^{(2)})-\mathscr{S}(f_{n_{k}})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big(\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{n_{k}})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(1)})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(2)})\Big)\Bigg)

(7.22)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{M}(a)+\mathbf{M}(1-a)+\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\Big(\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{n_{k}})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(1)})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(2)})\Big).

Thus, to get a contradiction, it suffices to prove that the remaining limit in (
7.22
) is zero.

Recall the bilinear map notation introduced in (
7.7
).
For any
\varepsilon>0
and
k\geq 0
, we can write

(7.23)

\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{n_{k}})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(1)})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(2)})=\Big(\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{n_{k}})-\Big\langle(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2},(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2}\Big\rangle_{\gamma}\Big)\\
+\Big(\Big\langle(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2},(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2}\Big\rangle_{\gamma}-\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(1)})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(2)})\Big).

We begin by controlling the first term on the right-hand side of (
7.23
).
If we combine (
7.8
) and (
7.11
)
with the fact that
\|f_{n_{k}}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
and
\|f_{k}^{(i)}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
are uniformly
bounded in
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
k\geq 0
, then we get that

\Big|\sqrt{\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{n_{k}})}-\sqrt{\Big\langle(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2},(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2}\Big\rangle_{\gamma}}\Big|\leq C\|f_{n_{k}}-(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})\|_{2}

for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
k\geq 0
, where the constant
C>0
is independent of
k
and
\varepsilon
.
By definition of the dichotomy condition, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is some
k_{0}\geq 0
such that

\|f_{n_{k}}-(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})\|_{2}\leq\delta_{2}(\varepsilon)

whenever
k\geq k_{0}
.
Recalling that
\delta_{2}(\varepsilon)\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we therefore conclude that

(7.24)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\Big(\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{n_{k}})-\Big\langle(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2},(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2}\Big\rangle_{\gamma}\Big)=0.

It now only remains to control the second term on the right-hand side of (
7.23
).
For every
\varepsilon>0
, we have that

(7.25)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\inf\left\{|x-y|:x\in\mathrm{supp}(f_{k}^{(1)})\text{ and }y\in\mathrm{supp}(f_{k}^{(2)})\right\}=\infty.

In particular, if
k
is large enough, then
f_{k}^{(1)}f_{k}^{(2)}=0
;
hence
(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2}=[f_{k}^{(1)}]^{2}+[f_{k}^{(2)}]^{2}
.
Therefore, since
\mathcal{J}_{0}(f)=\langle f^{2},f^{2}\rangle_{\gamma}
, we can expand

\Big\langle(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2},(f_{k}^{(1)}+f_{k}^{(2)})^{2}\Big\rangle_{\gamma}-\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(1)})-\mathcal{J}_{0}(f_{k}^{(2)})=2\Big\langle[f_{k}^{(1)}]^{2},[f_{k}^{(1)}]^{2}\Big\rangle_{\gamma}.

Since the
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{\infty}
norms are equivalent on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, (
7.25
)
implies that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a sequence of indices
1\leq i(k)\leq d
and numbers
d_{k}>0
such that

•

|x_{i(k)}-y_{i(k)}|>d_{k}
for every
x\in\mathrm{supp}(f_{k}^{(1)})
and
y\in\mathrm{supp}(f_{k}^{(2)})
; and

•

d_{k}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
.

In particular, we can write

\Big\langle[f_{k}^{(1)}]^{2},[f_{k}^{(1)}]^{2}\Big\rangle_{\gamma}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\iint_{\{|x_{i(k)}-y_{i(k)}|>d_{k}\}}[f_{k}^{(1)}(x)]^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}[f_{k}^{(2)}(y)]^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

As
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|f_{k}^{(i)}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty
for
i=1,2
, by replicating the argument leading up to
(
7.20
), we conclude that
\Big\langle[f_{k}^{(1)}]^{2},[f_{k}^{(1)}]^{2}\Big\rangle_{\gamma}\leq Cd_{k}^{-\omega_{i(k)}}

for some constant
C>0
independent of
k
. Therefore, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\Big\langle[f_{k}^{(1)}]^{2},[f_{k}^{(1)}]^{2}\Big\rangle_{\gamma}=0.

If we combine this with (
7.22
), (
7.23
), and (
7.24
),
then we finally obtain that dichotomy cannot occur simultaneously with (
7.5
),
thus concluding the proof of Lemma
7.4
.

7.7.
Proof of Lemma
7.5

For every fixed
x_{2},\ldots,x_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
and nonnegative measurable function
g
,
we note that the one-dimensional function
x_{1}\mapsto S_{e_{1}}(g)(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})
is the symmetric
decreasing rearrangement of the function
x_{1}\mapsto g(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})

(see, e.g.,
[
35
, Page 80]
). Therefore, by the Pólya-Szegő inequality (e.g.,
[
35
, Lemma 7.17]
),

\int\left(\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\geq\int\left(\frac{\partial S_{e_{1}}(f)(x)}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x,

and since the symmetric decreasing rearrangement preserves the
L^{p}
-norm
(
[
35
, Page 81]
),

\int\sum_{i=2}^{d}\left(\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x=\int\sum_{i=2}^{d}\left(\frac{\partial S_{e_{1}}(f)(x)}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x.

In particular,

-\mathscr{S}(f)\leq-\kappa\int|\nabla S_{e_{1}}(f)(x)|_{2}^{2}~\mathrm{d}x.

Next, by Fubini’s theorem, we can write

\iint f(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\
=\iint\prod_{i=2}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}\left(\int f(x_{1},\tilde{x})^{2}|x_{1}-y_{1}|^{-\omega_{1}}f(y_{1},\tilde{y})^{2}~\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}y_{1}\right)~\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}\mathrm{d}\tilde{y},

where we denote
\tilde{x}:=(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})
and similarly for
\tilde{y}
.
Let
\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
be fixed.
By the one-dimensional Riesz rearrangement inequality (e.g.,
[
35
, Lemma 3.6]
),
we have that

\int f(x_{1},\tilde{x})^{2}|x_{1}-y_{1}|^{-\omega_{1}}f(y_{1},\tilde{y})^{2}~\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}y_{1}\\
\leq\int[S_{e_{1}}(f)(x_{1}-z_{1},\tilde{x})]^{2}|x_{1}-y_{1}|^{-\omega_{1}}[S_{e_{1}}(f)(y_{1}-z_{1},\tilde{y})]^{2}~\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}y_{1}.

for any
z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, since
x_{1}\mapsto|x_{1}|^{-\omega_{1}}
is strictly decreasing in
|x_{1}|
,
it follows from
[
35
, Theorem 3.9]
that the above inequality is an equality only if
there exists
z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f(x_{1},\tilde{x})=S_{e_{1}}(f)(x_{1}-z_{1},\tilde{x})\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(y_{1},\tilde{y})=S_{e_{1}}(f)(y_{1}-z_{1},\tilde{y})

for almost every
x_{1},y_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
.
If we choose the same
z_{1}
for all

\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{y}
, this implies that

\iint f(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}f(y)^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq\iint[S_{e_{1}}(f)(x_{1}-z_{1},\tilde{x})]^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}[S_{e_{2}}(f)(y_{1}-z_{1},\tilde{y})]^{2}~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,

for any
z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, and if there is no
z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f=S_{e_{1}}(f)\big(\cdot-(z_{1},0,\ldots,0)\big)
almost everywhere, then the inequality is strict.

If we then iterate the above argument with the Steiner symmetrizations with respect to the axes
e_{2},\ldots,e_{d}
,
we conclude the proof of Lemma
7.5
.

7.8.
Proof of Lemma
7.6

By definition,

S_{\mathrm{coord}}(f)(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{S_{e_{d}}(S_{e_{d-1}}(\cdots(S_{e_{1}}(\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:f(y)>\ell\}))))}(x)~\mathrm{d}\ell.

Thus, the lemma follows from the fact that for any measurable set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
, the set

S_{e_{d}}(S_{e_{d-1}}(\cdots(S_{e_{1}}(A))))

is symmetric with respect to every coordinate axis
(e.g.,
[
17
, Claim #1 in Theorem 2.4]
).
