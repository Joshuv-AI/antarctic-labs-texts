Robust transitionless quantum driving: Concatenated approach
Zhi-Cheng Shi, Cheng Zhang, Li-Tuo Shen, Jie Song, Yan Xia, X. X. Yi

Abstract
We propose a concatenated approach for implementing transitionless quantum driving regardless of adiabatic conditions while being robustness with respect to all kinds of systematic errors induced by pulse duration, pulse amplitude, detunings, and Stark shift, etc. The current approach is particularly efficient for all time-dependent pulses with arbitrary shape, and only the phase differences between pulses is required to properly modulate. The simple physical implementation without the help of pulse shaping techniques or extra pulses makes this approach quite universal and provides a different avenue for robust quantum control by the time-dependent Hamiltonian.



Introduction.
The adiabatic passage (AP)
Allen and Eberly 1975
;
Oreg et al. 1984
;
Král et al. 2007
plays an important role in quantum optics
Scully and Zubairy 1997
and quantum computation
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
;
Vandersypen and Chuang 2005
;
Albash and Lidar 2018
, due to the insensitivity to various errors in physical parameters.
In the adiabatic process,
the system always evolves along an eigenstate connecting the initial state with the target state.
One typical example of AP is the stimulated Raman adiabatic passage (STIRAP)
Kuklinski et al. 1989
;
Shore et al. 1992
;
Vitanov et al. 2017
;
Fedoseev et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Vitanov 2020
, a popular way for population inversion in three-level systems.
Regardless of its robustness,
the common characteristic of AP are incompleteness of population transfer
and slow change of parameters over time.

To overcome these shortcomings,
a technique called shortcuts to adiabaticity (STA)
Demirplak and Rice 2003
;
Demirplak and Rice 2005
;
Berry 2009
;
Chen et al. 2010a
;
Chen et al. 2010b
;
Del Campo 2013
;
Ibáñez et al. 2012
;
Vitanov and Drewsen 2019
;
Kölbl et al. 2019
;
Mostafavi et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2021

has emerged that can directly cancel nonadiabatic transitions by introducing extra counterdiabatic fields.
This technique allows the system to perfectly evolve along the eigenstate of the original Hamiltonian at a very fast rate but suffers a great loss on the robustness of AP since it requires exactly knowing the system parameters in advance. Recently, several works are devoted to the robustness of STA
Ruschhaupt et al. 2012
;
Martikyan et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Liu and Wang 2023
.
Another essential requirement in STA is the pulse shaping technique to tailor the original waveform, and some counterdiabatic fields sometimes are prohibited from a physical point of view so its realization may be challenging.

As another alternative, the composite adiabatic passage (CAP) technique
Torosov et al. 2011
,
a combination of the best of both composite pulses
Levitt and Freeman 1979
;
Wimperis 1994
;
Cummins et al. 2003
;
Genov et al. 2014
;
Ichikawa et al. 2011
;
Bando et al. 2013
;
Wang et al. 2014
;
Calderon-Vargas and Kestner 2017
;
Morton et al. 2005
;
Dridi et al. 2020
;
Ivanov et al. 2022
;
Torosov et al. 2020
;
Gevorgyan and Vitanov 2021
;
Shi et al. 2022
;
Torosov and Vitanov 2022
and adiabatic passage
Zlatanov and Vitanov 2017
;
Zlatanov and Vitanov 2020
, has been proposed to achieve complete population inversion in two-level systems,
while its extension version named composite STIRAP
Torosov and Vitanov 2013
;
Bruns et al. 2018
achieves the same objective in three-level systems.
The main advantage of CAP over STA is the retained robustness of AP and the simplicity of implementation,
because one just properly modulates the relative phases between different pulses.
Nevertheless, some fundamental issues still need to be addressed in CAP, e.g., the unaccessible of obtaining universal rotation operations (or arbitrary superposition states) and the requirement of longer duration compared with traditional adiabatic passages.

In this work, through carefully designing the phase differences,
we propose a different dynamical mechanism for achieving perfect transitionless quantum driving in a robust manner, while simultaneously performing high precision quantum operations even
without
knowing the magnitudes of various systematic errors.
The current approach is constructed by multiply concatenating the Hamiltonians with the same arbitrary pulse shape but different well-designed constant phases.
In particular, it is unnecessary to satisfy the adiabatic condition, and thus the total duration does not have to be long.

Gauge invariance.
Consider a quantum system with near-neighbor interactions, and the general form of the time-dependent Hamiltonian is (
\hbar=1
)

\displaystyle H(t)=\sum\lambda_{j,j}(t)|j\rangle\langle j|+\lambda_{j,j+1}(t)|j\rangle\langle j\!+\!1|+\mathrm{H.c.},~

(1)

where
\lambda_{j,j}(t)
denote level energies, and
\lambda_{j,j+1}(t)
are the coupling strengths of near-neighbor levels.
When introducing extra constant phases to the coupling strengths, the Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle H(t,\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\lambda_{j,j}(t)|j\rangle\langle j|+\lambda_{j,j+1}(t)e^{i\theta_{j}}|j\rangle\langle j\!+\!1|+\mathrm{H.c.}

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\lambda_{j,j}(t)|\tilde{j}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}|+\lambda_{j,j+1}(t)|\tilde{j}\rangle\langle\widetilde{j\!+\!1}|+\mathrm{H.c.},

(3)

where
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots)
represents a vector parametrizing different constant phases in external fields.
Through making appropriate transformations to the original basis:
|\tilde{j}\rangle=\exp(-{i\sum_{k=1}^{k=j-1}\theta_{k}})|j\rangle
, the form of the Hamiltonian with different constant phases is the same as the previous one.
Namely, the addition of constant phases simply means that we are choosing a set of new basis and the expression of the propagator remains unchanged, i.e.,
U_{1}(t)=\tilde{U}_{1}(t,\bm{\theta})
, where
U_{1}(t)
and
\tilde{U}_{1}(t,\bm{\theta})
represent the propagators in the basis
|j\rangle
and
|\tilde{j}\rangle
, respectively. Therefore, the physical property of the Hamiltonian (
2
) is still trivial after introducing arbitrary constant phases.
Actually, this trivial property originates from the gauge invariance of the system
Wang and Plenio 2016
.

Nontriviality of phase differences
. Even though the trivial property do not change in any way for arbitrary constant phases,

phase differences
are of nontrivial physical significance and can be used for implementing perfect transitionless quantum driving in a robust manner.
To make it more clear,
we demonstrate the detailed design workflow in Fig.
1
, where the propagator corresponding to the Hamiltonian
H(t,\bm{\theta}_{n})
reads
U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{n})=\mathbf{T}\exp{\left[-i\int H(t,\bm{\theta}_{n})t\right]}
with
\mathbf{T}
being a time-ordering operator.
As shown in the blue box,
we initially concatenate
N_{1}
Hamiltonians
H(t,\bm{\theta}_{n})
with the same time-varying shape but different
\bm{\theta}_{n}
to create a composite propagator
U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\prime})=U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{N_{1}})\cdots U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})
, where
the phase differences
\bm{\theta}_{mn}=\bm{\theta}_{m}-\bm{\theta}_{n}
are properly modulated to achieve perfect transitionless quantum driving.

When the quantum system exhibits various errors, transitionless quantum driving becomes imperfect and thus produces nonadiabatic transitions.
To solve it,
we continue to concatenating
{N_{2}}
propagators
U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{n}^{\prime})
to produce a new one
U_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\prime\prime})=U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{N_{2}}^{\prime})\cdots U_{2}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{1})
, where
U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{n}^{\prime})
are generated by adding different constant phase shifts
\bm{\vartheta}_{n}
to
N_{1}
Hamiltonians
H(t,\bm{\theta}_{n})
.
Through altering the phase differences
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{mn}=\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{m}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{n}
, the propagator
U_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\prime\prime})
would sharply suppress the nonadiabatic transition induced by various errors.

Note that the propagator
U_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\prime\prime})
becomes approximately diagonalized after the first two concatenations, but there still exists phase errors on diagonal elements and those errors would reduce the precision of quantum operations. Thus, we require to further concatenate
{N_{3}}
propagators
U_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{n}^{\prime\prime})
to obtain the new one
U_{4}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime})=U_{3}(\bm{\theta}_{N_{3}}^{\prime\prime})\cdots U_{3}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{1})
.
Similarly, the phase differences
\bm{\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{mn}=\bm{\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{m}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{n}
are finely tuned to promote the accuracy of quantum operations.
Indeed, the propagators
U_{4}(\bm{\theta}_{n}^{\prime\prime\prime})
can be also concatenated for more other uses if necessary.
Through this concatenated approach, we obtain a sequence for implementing robust quantum operations under transitionless quantum driving.

Transitionless quantum driving.

To illustrate this, let us consider a three-level
\Lambda

The form of the Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}(t)

\displaystyle\!=\!

\displaystyle\Delta(t)\sigma_{ee}\!+\!\left[\Omega_{p}(t)e^{i\theta_{p}}\sigma_{ge}\!+\!\Omega_{s}(t)e^{i\theta_{s}}\sigma_{fe}\!+\!\mathrm{H.c.}\right]\!,~~~

Robustness.

In reality, there are many factors that prevent us from exactly acquiring the full information of a quantum system.
For example, in the context of atoms driven by external fields, the external fields are usually assumed to be monochromatic while they actually have a certain linewidth. Due to their interactions, the energy level of atoms may also shift, i.e., the so-called Stark shift
Scully and Zubairy 1997
. Furthermore, the inhomogeneous distribution of external fields as well as tiny vibrations of atoms at equilibrium, can create small uncertainties in the interactions of these systems. All these uncertainties can be regarded as errors in various physical parameters, such as pulse duration, pulse amplitude, and detuning.
These errors mainly lead to two unfavorable effects: the generation of nonadiabatic transitions and the imprecision of quantum control.
Actually,
both effects can be largely avoided by further concatenating the propagators with well-designed constant phases.
Next, we elaborate on this point.

the system evolution is strictly restricted in the subspace
\{|g\rangle,|f\rangle\}
,
and the rotation operation becomes remarkable error tolerant.

Discussion.

The concatenated approach may be applicable to various kinds of quantum systems as long as phase modulation is accessible, although we just take the typical three-level system to illustrate this issue. Actually, the
phases only have to satisfy some constraints rather than arbitrary values when the Hamiltonian has long-ranged interactions
sup
.
On the other hand, there are several basic requirements in the current STA (e.g, see a recent review
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2019
and the references therein), such as multiparameter regulation, pulse shaping, and specific operation time. Also, the STA technique makes it difficult to make the system simultaneously inhibit multiple systematic errors.
Nevertheless, none of them are required in the concatenated approach, since the dynamical procedure is completely out of line with the
original framework of STA.
In particular, the design of phase differences does not rely on the certain type of systematic errors, because they are derived from the propagator instead of the Hamiltonian. Therefore, this approach is quite universal for resisting any errors.

Conclusion.

We have developed a concatenated approach of constructing a dynamical mechanism for both achieving perfect transitionless quantum driving and improving the robustness with respect to various systematic errors.
It is particularly significant that the pulse shape can be arbitrary while the quantum system does not have to satisfy the adiabatic condition.
By properly designing the phase differences between different Hamiltonians, the unwanted nonadiabatic transitions
are sharply suppressed even in the presence of all kinds of errors. Meanwhile, quantum operations with very high fidelity are naturally obtained. Of course, this concatenated procedure can still be carried on for other uses.
The simplicity, flexibility, and versatility of the concatenated approach opens a promising avenue for high precision control in quantum information processing.

\Lambda

The form of the Hamiltonian reads (
\hbar=1

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta(t)|e\rangle\langle e|+\Omega_{p}(t)e^{i\theta_{p}}|g\rangle\langle e|+\Omega_{s}(t)e^{i\theta_{s}}|f\rangle\langle e|+\mathrm{H.c.},

(S1)

where the transition
|g\rangle(|f\rangle)\leftrightarrow|e\rangle
is driven by the pump (Stokes) pulse with the coupling strength
\Omega_{p}(t)
[
\Omega_{s}(t)
], the phase
\theta_{p}
(
\theta_{s}
), and the detuning
\Delta(t)
.
For this Hamiltonian, we easily obtain the eigenenergies

\displaystyle E_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(S2a)

\displaystyle E_{+}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta(t)}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\Delta(t)^{2}}{4}+\Omega(t)^{2}}=\Omega(t)\cot\varphi(t),

(S2b)

\displaystyle E_{-}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta(t)}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\Delta(t)^{2}}{4}+\Omega(t)^{2}}=-\Omega(t)\tan\varphi(t),

(S2c)

and the corresponding eigenstates

\displaystyle|E_{0}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|d(t)\rangle=\cos\phi(t)e^{-i\theta_{sp}}|g\rangle-\sin\phi(t)|f\rangle,

(S3a)

\displaystyle|E_{+}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin\phi(t)\sin\varphi(t)e^{i\theta_{p}}|g\rangle+\cos\phi(t)\sin\varphi(t)e^{i\theta_{s}}|f\rangle+\cos\varphi(t)|e\rangle=\sin\varphi(t)e^{i\theta_{s}}|b(t)\rangle+\cos\varphi(t)|e\rangle,~~~~~

(S3b)

\displaystyle|E_{-}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\sin\phi(t)\cos\varphi(t)e^{i\theta_{p}}|g\rangle+\cos\phi(t)\cos\varphi(t)e^{i\theta_{s}}|f\rangle-\sin\varphi(t)|e\rangle=\cos\varphi(t)e^{i\theta_{s}}|b(t)\rangle-\sin\varphi(t)|e\rangle,

where
\Omega(t)=\sqrt{\Omega_{p}(t)^{2}\!+\!\Omega_{s}(t)^{2}}
,

\tan 2\varphi(t)=2\Omega(t)/\Delta(t)
,

\tan\phi(t)=\Omega_{p}(t)/\Omega_{s}(t)
,
\theta_{sp}=\theta_{s}\!-\!\theta_{p}

The transition mechanism of the dark state is very easily revealed in the adiabatic representation spanned by Eqs. (
S3a
)–(
S3c

The matrix form of the Hamiltonian in the adiabatic basis
\{|E_{0}(t)\rangle,|E_{+}(t)\rangle,|E_{-}(t)\rangle\}
reads

\displaystyle H_{a}(t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-i\dot{\phi}(t)\sin{\varphi(t)}&-i\dot{\phi}(t)\cos{\varphi(t)}\\
i\dot{\phi}(t)\sin{\varphi(t)}&\Omega(t)\cot{\varphi(t)}&i\dot{\varphi}(t)\\
i\dot{\phi}(t)\cos{\varphi(t)}&-i\dot{\varphi}(t)&-\Omega(t)\tan{\varphi(t)}\end{array}\right],

where the overdot represents a time derivative.
Obviously,
to make the dark state
|d(t)\rangle
decouple to other adiabatic states,
we have to set
\dot{\phi}(t)=0
,
and the solution is

\displaystyle{\phi}(t)=\mathrm{constant}.

(S8)

This means the SP pulses must be synchronized.
In fact,
this synchronization between the SP pulses over time is an essential condition to make one state decouple to other states.
In the following, we give a brief proof for it.

Consider a general unitary transform
R
on this system, and the matrix form of
R
in the basis
\{|g\rangle,|f\rangle,|e\rangle\}
is

\displaystyle R(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\cos{\phi(t)}e^{-i\vartheta}&\sin{\phi(t)}&0\\[4.30554pt]
-\sin{\phi(t)}&\cos{\phi(t)}e^{i\vartheta}&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&0&1\cr\end{array}\right].

We now study the system dynamics in a rotation frame with respect to
R
, where the basis are
\{|r_{1}(t)\rangle,|r_{2}(t)\rangle,|e\rangle\}
with
|r_{1}(t)\rangle=\cos{\phi(t)}e^{-i\vartheta}|g\rangle-\sin{\phi(t)}|f\rangle
and

|r_{2}(t)\rangle=\sin{\phi(t)}|g\rangle+\cos{\phi(t)}e^{i\vartheta}|f\rangle
.
Here, the undetermined parameters
\phi(t)
and
\vartheta
are used to ensure that one basis is decoupled from other two bases in this rotation frame.
We first calculate the expression of the Hamiltonian in the basis
\{|r_{1}(t)\rangle,|r_{2}(t)\rangle,|e\rangle\}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle H_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{\dagger}(t)H(t)R(t)-iR^{\dagger}(t)\dot{R}(t),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&h_{12}&h_{13}\\[4.30554pt]
h_{21}&0&h_{23}\\[4.30554pt]
h_{31}&h_{32}&h_{33}\cr\end{array}\right]

where

\displaystyle h_{12}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{21}^{*}=-i\dot{\phi}(t)e^{-i\vartheta},

(S17a)

\displaystyle h_{13}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{31}^{*}=\Omega_{p}(t)\cos\phi(t)e^{i(\theta_{p}+\vartheta)}-\Omega_{s}(t)\sin\phi(t)e^{i\theta_{s}},

(S17b)

\displaystyle h_{23}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{32}^{*}=\Omega_{p}(t)\sin\phi(t)e^{i\theta_{p}}+\Omega_{s}(t)\cos\phi(t)e^{i(\theta_{s}-\vartheta)},

(S17c)

\displaystyle h_{33}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta(t).

(S17d)

It can be found that
it is required
h_{12}=h_{13}=0
to decouple
|r_{1}(t)\rangle
from
|r_{2}(t)\rangle
and
|e\rangle
,
or
h_{12}=h_{32}=0
to decouple
|r_{2}(t)\rangle
from
|r_{1}(t)\rangle
and
|e\rangle
.
For
h_{12}=0
, we have
\dot{\phi}(t)=0
, and the solution is

\displaystyle{\phi}(t)=\mathrm{constant}.

(S18)

On the other hand,
the equation
h_{13}=0
can be simplified as

\displaystyle\tan\phi(t)=\frac{\Omega_{p}(t)}{\Omega_{s}(t)}e^{i(\theta_{p}-\theta_{s}+\vartheta)},

(S19)

while the equation
h_{23}=0
is simplified as

\displaystyle\cot\phi(t)=-\frac{\Omega_{p}(t)}{\Omega_{s}(t)}e^{i(\theta_{p}-\theta_{s}+\vartheta)}.

(S20)

As a result, we acquire that
\vartheta=\theta_{s}-\theta_{p}=\theta_{sp}
and the Stokes and pump pulses must be synchronized, since

is real constant.

S2
Perfect transitionless quantum driving by two SP pulse pairs

In the section, we demonstrate that perfect transitionless quantum driving is obtained by merely concatenating two SP pulse pairs with a well-designed phase difference.

Due to the decoupling between the dark state
|b(t)\rangle
and the states
|b(t)\rangle
and
|e\rangle
,
the general expression of the propagator for the single SP pulse pair in the basis
\{|d(t)\rangle,|b(t)\rangle,|e\rangle\}
is written as

\displaystyle U_{1}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\[2.15277pt]
0&se^{i\alpha}&re^{-i\theta_{s}}\\[2.15277pt]
0&-r^{*}e^{i\theta_{s}}&se^{-i\alpha}\end{array}\right],

where
r
and
\alpha
are determined by the parameters of the SP pulse pair and
s=\sqrt{1-|r|^{2}}
.
Thus, the propagator for two SP pulse pairs is given by

\displaystyle U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\prime})=U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{2})U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&{U}_{2,bb}&{U}_{2,be}\\[4.30554pt]
0&{U}_{2,eb}&{U}_{2,ee}\end{array}\right],\!

where

\displaystyle{U}_{2,bb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{U}^{*}_{2,ee}=-|r|^{2}e^{i(\theta_{s,1}-\theta_{s,2})}+s^{2}e^{i2\alpha},

(S29a)

\displaystyle{U}_{2,eb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{U}^{*}_{2,be}=-r^{*}s\left[e^{-i(\alpha-\theta_{s,1})}+e^{i(\alpha+\theta_{s,2})}\right],

(S29b)

and
\theta_{s,1}
(
\theta_{s,2}
) are the phase of the first (second) Stokes pulse.
To perfectly implement transitionless quantum driving,
we need to nullify the matrix element
{U}_{2,eb}
, and thus the equation becomes

\displaystyle e^{-i(\alpha-\theta_{s,1})}+e^{i(\alpha+\theta_{s,2})}=0.

(S30)

It is easily to obtain the solution of Eq. (
S30
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\theta_{s,2}-\theta_{s,1}=\pi-2\alpha.

(S31)

This is actually Eq. (5) in the main text.
As a result, the propagator given by Eq. (
S2
) becomes diagonalized, i.e.,

\displaystyle U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\prime})=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\[2.15277pt]
0&e^{i2\alpha}&0\\[2.15277pt]
0&0&e^{-i2\alpha}\end{array}\right].\!~~~

Therefore, we can perform perfect transitionless quantum driving by only two SP pulse pairs regardless of whether it exists the nonadiabatic transition in the single SP pulse pair.

S3
Robustness against nonadiabatic transition induced by various systematic errors

In this section, we demonstrate how to concatenate
N_{2}
propagators
U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{n_{2}}^{\prime})
with different constant phases to achieve robust transitionless quantum driving in the presence of systematic errors, i.e., the second concatenation in the green box of Fig. 1 in the main text.

When the quantum system exhibits various errors (e.g., pulse amplitude error, pulse duration error, and detuning error etc.), it leads to the deviation of two quantities
r
and
\alpha
in the propagator
U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{n_{2}}^{\prime})
.
In the following, let us study this issue separately.

(1)
The deviation of the quantities

r
(
s
):
r\rightarrow(1+\delta_{r})r
[
s\rightarrow(1+\delta_{s})s
].
The propagator
U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})
for the single SP pulse pair becomes

\displaystyle U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\[2.15277pt]
0&(1+\delta_{s})se^{i\alpha}&(1+\delta_{r})re^{-i\theta_{s,1}}\\[2.15277pt]
0&-(1+\delta_{r})r^{*}e^{i\theta_{s,1}}&(1+\delta_{s})se^{-i\alpha}\end{array}\right],

Then, the propagator for two SP pulse pairs is given by

\displaystyle U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\prime})=U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{2})U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&{U}_{2,bb}&{U}_{2,be}\\[4.30554pt]
0&{U}_{2,eb}&{U}_{2,ee}\end{array}\right],

where

\displaystyle{U}_{2,bb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{U}^{*}_{2,ee}=-(1+\delta_{r})^{2}|r|^{2}e^{i(\theta_{s,1}-\theta_{s,2})}+(1+\delta_{s})^{2}s^{2}e^{i2\alpha},

(S44a)

\displaystyle{U}_{2,eb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{U}^{*}_{2,be}=-(1+\delta_{r})(1+\delta_{s})r^{*}s\left[e^{-i(\alpha-\theta_{s,1})}+e^{i(\alpha+\theta_{s,2})}\right].

(S44b)

Obviously, the solution of Eq. (
S44b
) is

\displaystyle\theta_{s,2}-\theta_{s,1}=\pi-2\alpha,

(S45)

which is independent of the deviation in the quantity
r
(
s
).
Therefore, once the phase difference satisfy Eq. (
S45
) in the two SP pulse pairs, one can completely eliminate the effect of the deviation in the quantity
r
(
s
) no matter how large it is.

(2)
The deviation of the quantity
\alpha
:
\alpha\rightarrow(1+\delta_{\alpha})\alpha
.
In this case,
the propagator
U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})
becomes

\displaystyle U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&se^{i(1+\delta_{\alpha})\alpha}&re^{-i\theta_{s,1}}\\[4.30554pt]
0&-r^{*}e^{i\theta_{s,1}}&se^{-i(1+\delta_{\alpha})\alpha}\end{array}\right].

As a result, the propagator
U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\prime})
is

\displaystyle U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\prime})=U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{2})U_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&e^{2i\alpha}(|r|^{2}+e^{2i\alpha\delta_{\alpha}}s^{2})&2irs\sin(\alpha\delta_{\alpha})e^{i(\alpha-\theta_{s,1})}\\[4.30554pt]
0&2ir^{*}s\sin(\alpha\delta_{\alpha})e^{i(\theta_{s,1}-\alpha)}&e^{-2i\alpha}(|r|^{2}+e^{-2i\alpha\delta_{\alpha}}s^{2})\end{array}\right].

Obviously, Eq. (
S3
) demonstrates that the nonadiabatic transition is induced by the presence of the deviation
\delta_{\alpha}
.

It is worth mentioning that the unfavorable effect caused by the deviation
\delta_{\alpha}
can only be sharply reduced rather than completely canceled in this situation.
Next, we elaborate the implementation of robust transitionless quantum driving by designing the phase differences in a train of
N_{2}
propagators
U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{n_{2}}^{\prime})
,
where the recombination propagator
U_{3}({\theta}_{1}^{\prime\prime})
reads

\displaystyle U_{3}({\theta}_{1}^{\prime\prime})=U_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{N_{2}}^{\prime})\cdots U_{2}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{1})=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&{U}_{3,bb}&{U}_{3,be}\\[4.30554pt]
0&{U}_{3,eb}&{U}_{3,ee}\end{array}\right],

with
\bm{\theta}_{n_{2}}^{\prime}=(\theta_{s,{n_{2}}},\theta_{p,{n_{2}}})
,
n_{2}=1,\dots,N_{2}
.
Through the Tayor-Maclaurin expansion for the propagator (
S3
) with respect to
\delta_{\alpha}
, we can obtain the series of the element
{U}_{3,eb}
.
Then, to achieve robustness against the deviation
\delta_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\partial^{k}_{\delta_{\alpha}}\!U_{3,eb}=0,~~~k=0,1,2,3,\dots

(S58)

where

When the number of the propagators

For a longer sequence, the solution can be given by concatenating the obtained solutions of short sequences.

S4
High precision quantum operations

After the second concatenation of SP pulse pairs, the nonadiabatic transition induced by
various errors is dramatically suppressed, and thus the propagator
U_{3}({\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{1})
can be approximately written in a diagonalized form,

\displaystyle U_{3}({\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{1})\approx|d(t)\rangle\langle d(t)|+\exp[{i2\beta(1+\delta_{\beta})}]|b(t)\rangle\langle b(t)|+\exp[{-i2\beta^{\prime}(1+\delta_{\beta^{\prime}})}]|e\rangle\langle e|.

(S74)

Actually, the deviation
\delta_{\beta}
in Eq. (
S74
) would decline the precision of quantum operations.
To show it more clearly, we transform the propagator
U_{3}({\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{1})
back to the original representation spanned by
\{|g\rangle,|f\rangle,|e\rangle\}
.
In the basis
\{|g\rangle,|f\rangle\}
, the form of the propagator
U_{3}({\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{1})
is given by

\displaystyle U_{3}({\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{1})=\exp\left[{-\frac{i}{2}\beta(1+\delta_{\beta})\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\sigma}}\right],

(S75)

where
\bm{n}=(-\sin\phi(t)\cos\theta_{sp},\sin\phi(t)\sin\theta_{sp},\cos\phi(t))
.
It is easily observed from Eq. (
S75
) that the rotation angle would produce errors in the Bloch sphere due to the presence of the deviation
\delta_{\beta}
.

To further restrain the influence of the deviation
\delta_{\beta}
on the precision of quantum operations, we need to a third
concatenation of SP pulse pairs.
That is, we concatenate
{N_{3}}
propagators
U_{3}({\theta}_{n_{3}}^{\prime\prime})
to form a new one,

\displaystyle U_{4}({\theta}_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime})=U_{3}({\theta}_{N_{3}}^{\prime\prime})\cdots U_{3}({\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{1})=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}{U}_{4,gg}&{U}_{4,gf}&0\\[4.30554pt]
{U}_{4,fg}&{U}_{4,ff}&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&0&1\end{array}\right],

where
{\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{n_{3}}
is the adjustable phases in this situation,
n_{3}=1,\dots,N_{3}
.
Next,
we design the phases
{\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{n_{3}}
by using two different ways.
The first one is to compensate for the deviation only in the population of the target state,
called as the population-compensation (PC) sequence.
The second one is to compensate for the deviation in the both population and phase of the target state,
called as fidelity-compensation (FC) sequence hereafter.

The construction of the PC sequence is as follows.
Taking similar steps in Sec.
S3
, we modulate the phases
{\theta}_{n_{3}}^{\prime\prime}
to nullify the coefficients
\partial^{k}_{\delta_{\beta}}\!|U_{4,fg}|^{2}
in a increasing order as many as possible, where
\partial^{k}_{\delta_{\beta}}\!|U_{4,fg}|^{2}
represents the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
derivative of
|U_{4,fg}|^{2}
with respect to
\delta_{\beta}
,
k=1,2,\dots

It is worth mentioning that the zero-order coefficient
\partial^{0}_{\delta_{\beta}}\!|U_{4,fg}|^{2}
is used to control the population of the state
|f\rangle
.
In the following, we first take the case
N_{3}=3
as the example to demonstrate the design of the phases
{\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{n_{3}}
.
The form of the total propagator reads

\displaystyle U_{4}({\theta}_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime})=U_{3}(\theta_{3}^{\prime\prime})U_{3}(\theta_{2}^{\prime\prime})U_{3}(\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}),

(S80)

and the equations to be solved are

\displaystyle\partial^{0}_{\delta_{\beta}}\!|U_{4,fg}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left[2+\cos(\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}+\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime})-\cos(\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}-\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime})\right]=P_{f},

(S81a)

\displaystyle\partial^{1}_{\delta_{\beta}}\!|U_{4,fg}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi}{4}\left[2\cos\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime}\cos^{2}\frac{\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}}{2}+\cos\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}\right]=0,

(S81b)

where
P_{f}
represents the population of the state
|f\rangle
.
It readily obtains the solution of Eq. (
S81
), which are

\displaystyle\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\arctan\pm\left(\sqrt{1-P_{f}^{2}}\pm\sqrt{2P_{f}-P_{f}^{2}}\right),

(S82a)

\displaystyle\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\arctan\pm\left(\sqrt{1-P_{f}^{2}}\mp\sqrt{2P_{f}-P_{f}^{2}}\right).

(S82b)

When
{N_{3}}
is large, it is convenient to adopts numerical methods, and a group of numerical solutions for different
N_{3}
are presented in Table
SII
, while the corresponding excitation profiles of the state
|f\rangle
are plotted in Fig.
S2
(a).
Obviously,
the excitation profile for the single propagator
U_{3}({\theta}_{1}^{\prime\prime})
cannot keep the target value in the presence of the deviation
\delta_{\beta}
,
while the excitation profile for the case
N_{3}=3
shows a primary flat platform around
\delta_{\beta}=0
.
To achieve better robustness,
we can continue to concatenate more propagators
U_{3}({\theta}_{n_{3}}^{\prime\prime})
to generate a longer sequence.
Of course, the long sequence can also be concatenated by short sequences.
In this way,
more free phases
{\theta}^{\prime\prime}_{n_{3}}
are introduced to nullify more high-order coefficients
\partial^{k}_{\delta_{\beta}}\!|U_{4,fg}|^{2}
.
As expected,
the flat central platform of the excitation profile around
\delta_{\beta}=0
becomes wider with the increase of
N_{3}
; see the solid yellow and violet curves in Fig.

(a).

To robustly obtain an arbitrary superposition state
|\psi_{T}\rangle=\cos{\alpha}|g\rangle+\sin{\alpha}e^{i\chi}|f\rangle
,
it is not enough to achieve high-precision population transfer because different relative phases
\chi
correspond to distinct states even if they have the same
\alpha
.
Therefore,
if the objective is to implement a superposition state carrying an specific relative phase,
it is inadequate to merely exploit the Taylor expansion of the amplitude
|U_{4,fg}|^{2}
.
Next,
we describe how to design such pulse sequence that takes the relative phase into account by using
the fidelity
F
instead, whose definition is given by

\displaystyle F=|\langle\psi_{T}|\Psi(N^{\prime}_{3}T)\rangle|^{2},

(S83)

where
|\Psi(N^{\prime}_{3}T)\rangle
represents the final state after concatenating
N^{\prime}_{3}
pulses.
Here, to make it distinct to the PC sequence, we replace the pulse number
N_{3}
by
N^{\prime}_{3}
.

Again, starting from the propagator (
S4
),
when the initial state of the system is
|g\rangle
,
the final state becomes

\displaystyle|\psi_{T}\rangle=U_{4,gg}|g\rangle+U_{4,fg}|f\rangle.

(S84)

As a result,
the expression of the fidelity
F
in Eq. (
S83
) reads

\displaystyle F=|U_{4,gg}|^{2}\cos^{2}{\alpha}+|U_{4,fg}|^{2}\sin^{2}{\alpha}+\dfrac{1}{2}\sin{2\alpha}\left(U_{4,gg}U^{*}_{4,fg}e^{i\chi}+U_{4,fg}U^{*}_{4,gg}e^{-i\chi}\right).

(S85)

In the presence of the deviation,
the Taylor expansion of the fidelity can be written as

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{0}+f_{1}\delta_{\beta}+f_{2}\delta_{\beta}^{2}+\cdots,

(S86)

where
f_{k^{\prime}}=(k^{\prime}!)^{-1}\partial^{k^{\prime}}_{\delta_{\beta}}F|_{\delta_{\beta}=0}
is the coefficient of the
k^{\prime\mathrm{th}}
-order term.
At this time,
in order to obtain a superposition state with high fidelity while maintaining robust against the deviation
\delta_{\beta}
,
it is necessary to vanish as more low-order terms in Eq. (
S86
) as possible.

We begin with exemplifying this pulse design by using
N^{\prime}_{3}=3
, where only the zero-order and first-order terms need to be concerned. The expressions of these two coefficients are given by

\displaystyle f_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16}\Big\{8+\cos{(\chi\!+\!\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!-\!2\alpha)}-\cos{(\chi\!+\!\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!2\alpha)}-\cos{(\chi\!+\!\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!-\!2\alpha\!+\!2\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime})}+8\cos{2\alpha}\sin{\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}}\sin{\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime}}

(S87a)

\displaystyle-\cos{(\chi\!+\!\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!2\alpha\!+\!2\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime})}-4\sin{2\alpha}\big[\cos{(\chi\!+\!\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!2\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime})}+2\cos{\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}}\sin{(\chi\!+\!\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime})}\big]\Big\},

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{16}\big\{2\sin{2\alpha}\left[\sin{(\chi\!+\!\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime})}\!-\!\sin{(\chi\!+\!\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!2\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime})}\!+\!\sin{(\chi\!+\!\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!2\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime})}\right]\!+\!2\cos{(2\alpha+\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime})}

(S87b)

\displaystyle+2\cos{(2\alpha\!-\!\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime})}+\cos{(\chi+\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!2\alpha)}-\cos{(\chi\!+\!\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}\!-\!2\alpha)}+8\cos{2\alpha}\cos^{2}{\frac{\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}}{2}}\cos{\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime}}\big\},

where the phase differences are
\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}=\theta^{\prime\prime}_{2}-\theta^{\prime\prime}_{1}
and
\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime}=\theta^{\prime\prime}_{3}-\theta^{\prime\prime}_{2}
.
Hence,
the equations to be solved are

\displaystyle f_{0}=1,

(S88a)

\displaystyle f_{1}=0,

(S88b)

where Eq. (
S88a
) ensures to perfectly obtain the target state in the absence of the deviation
\delta_{\beta}
,
and Eq. (
S88b
) means the nullification of the first-order term.
Generally,
obtaining analytical solutions for Eqs. (
S88
) is quite difficult except for some special circumstances.
For instance,
when
\alpha=\pi/4
,
i.e.,
the target state being the maximum superposition state, Eqs. (
S88
) can be reduced into

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\Big[4\!+\!\sin{\zeta}\!-\!\sin{(2\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}\!+\!\zeta)}-2\sin(\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime})\cos(\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}+2\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime}+\zeta)-4\cos(\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime})\sin(\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}+\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime}+\zeta)\Big]=1,

(S89a)

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}\cos{\frac{\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}}{2}}\sin{\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime}}\cos{(\frac{\theta_{21}^{\prime\prime}}{2}+\theta_{32}^{\prime\prime}+\zeta)}=0,

(S89b)

where
\zeta=\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}+\chi
.
Obviously, it is possible to attain the analytical solutions of Eq. (
S89
), which are given in the Table

f_{2}
and find out the ones that result in the smallest value of
f_{2}
.

S6
Gauge invariance for all Hamiltonians

In the main text, we briefly demonstrate the property of the Hamiltonian with near-neighbor interactions is trivial when the constant phases is arbitrary.
In fact, this can be suitable for
all
Hamiltonians, up to some constraints on the phases. Next, we explain this issue.

The general form of the time-dependent Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,j}(t)|j\rangle\langle j|+\sum_{m<j}\lambda_{j,m}(t)|j\rangle\langle m|+\mathrm{H.c.},

(S90)

where the real number
\lambda_{j,j}(t)
denotes level energies, and the complex number
\lambda_{j,m}(t)
is the coupling strength between
|j\rangle
and
|m\rangle
. Then, the expression of the propagator can be written as

\displaystyle U(t)=\sum_{j,m=1}^{J}U_{jm}|j\rangle\langle m|,

(S91)

where the complex number
U_{jm}
characterizes the transition between
|j\rangle
and
|m\rangle
.

When introducing extra constant phases to the coupling strengths, the Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle H(t,\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j,j}(t)|j\rangle\langle j|+\sum_{m<j}\lambda_{j,m}(t)e^{i\theta_{jm}}|j\rangle\langle m|+\mathrm{H.c.}

(S92)

where
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{12},\dots,\theta_{1J},\theta_{23},\theta_{24},\dots)
parameterizes different constant phases in external fields. In this situation, the total number of arbitrarily adjustable phases in external fields is

\displaystyle(J-1)+(J-2)+\cdots+1=J(J-1)/2,

(S93)

Next, we rewrite the Hamiltonian (
S92
) in a set of new basis
|\tilde{j}\rangle
, which is connected to the original basis according to the following relations:
|\tilde{j}\rangle=e^{\theta_{j}}|j\rangle
, and the form reads

\displaystyle H(t,\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\lambda_{j,j}(t)|\tilde{j}\rangle\langle\tilde{j}|+\sum_{m<j}\lambda_{j,m}(t)e^{i(\theta_{jm}+\theta_{j}-\theta_{m})}|\tilde{j}\rangle\langle\tilde{m}|+\mathrm{H.c.}

(S94)

To make the form of Eq. (
S94
) be the same as Eq. (
S90
), we demand

\displaystyle\theta_{jm}+\theta_{j}-\theta_{m}=0.

(S95)

Note that the number of
\theta_{jm}
is
J(J-1)/2
, while it is only
J
for
\theta_{j}
.
As a result, the number of the phases
\theta_{jm}
that can take arbitrary value is not more than
J
, and the rest needs to satisfy Eq. (
S95
).
For the Hamiltonian with near-neighbor interactions, as shown in the main text, there are exactly
J
phases in external fields. Therefore, all of them can be chosen arbitrary.

When the phases
\theta_{jm}
in external fields satisfy Eq. (
S95
), the expression of the propagator also reads

\displaystyle U(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,m=1}^{J}U_{jm}|\tilde{j}\rangle\langle\tilde{m}|,

(S96)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j,m=1}^{J}U_{jm}e^{i(\theta_{j}-\theta_{n})}|j\rangle\langle m|,

(S97)

where
U_{jm}
exactly comes from Eq. (
S91
).
This means that after introducing extra phases in external fields, the transition amplitude is still left unchange but different phases.
