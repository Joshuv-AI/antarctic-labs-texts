Abstract
In formal epistemology, group knowledge is often modelled as the knowledge that the group would have, if the agents shared all their individual knowledge. However, this interpretation does not account for relations between agents. In this work, we propose the notion of synergistic knowledge which makes it possible to model those relationships. We interpret synergistic knowledge on simplicial models that are based on semi-simplicial sets. As examples, we investigate the use of consensus objects and the problem of the dining cryptographers. Furthermore, we introduce the axiom system 𝖲𝗒𝗇 \mathsf{Syn} for synergistic knowledge and show that it is sound and complete with respect to the presented simplicial models.

Introduction

The successful application of combinatorial topology to distributed systems has sparked the interest of modal logicians. A topological model interprets the different configurations of a distributed system as an abstract structure called a simplicial complex. Within a simplicial complex, one can express which global states are indistinguishable to processes. If a process cannot tell two configurations apart, then, for any distributed protocol, it must behave the same in both states. Naturally, this approach, exemplified by Herlihy, Kozlov, and Rajsbaum
[
15
]
, has parallels with formal epistemology. Hence, studying simplicial interpretations of modal logic is a novel and promising subject.

Due to its application to well known problems such as agreement (cf. Halpern and Moses
[
13
]
), modal logic has been proven to be vital for the formal analysis of distributed Algorithms. Models for modal logic are usually based on a possible worlds approach, where the modal operator
\square
is evaluated on Kripke frames. A Kripke frame is a graph whose vertices represent worlds, i.e., global states, and whose edges depict an indistinguishability relation. In a world
w
, a formula
\phi
is known, denoted by
\square\phi
, if and only if
\phi
is true in each world indistinguishable from
w
. These frames can be extended to multi-agent systems by introducing an indistinguishability relation for each agent. A formula
\phi
is distributed knowledge of a group, first introduced by Halpern and Moses
[
13
]
, if and only if
\phi
is true in all worlds that cannot be distinguished by any member of the group.

Some recent publications in logic have explored simplicial complexes as models for modal logics with multiple agents. In its simplest form, pure simplicial complexes describe settings in which agents never die and impure complexes are capable of capturing an agent’s death. Goubault, Ledent, and Rajsbaum
[
11
]
pioneered the analysis of simplicial interpretations and laid the foundation for pure complexes as epistemic models. Work regarding impure complexes was conducted independently by van Ditmarsch, Kuznets, and Randrianomentsoa
[
7
]
and by Goubault, Ledent, and Rajsbaum
[
12
]
. The latter work shows the equivalence between their simplicial models and Kripke models for the logic
\mathsf{KB4}_{n}
, whereas van Ditmarsch, Kuznets, and Randrianomentsoa
[
7
]
propose a three-valued logic in which formulas that reference dead agents might be undefined. Van Ditmarsch, Kuzents, and Randrianomentsoa
[
6
]
further compare two-valued with three-valued semantics and categorise simplicial models accordingly. Lastly, Goubault et al.
[
10
]
generalise impure complexes based on their containment of sub-worlds, i.e., worlds whose set of alive agents is contained in another indistinguishable world’s set of alive agents.

One example of a slightly relaxed version of a simplicial complex is a semi-simplicial set. Simply put, semi-simplicial sets allow us to model global states that consist of the same local states, but differ in their meaning. The local state of a process is represented by a vertex that is labelled accordingly and global states correspond to edges. The processes cannot distinguish between the global states because their local states can be part of both. That is,

In this paper, we present a semantics for epistemic reasoning on simplicial models that are based on semi-simplicial sets and introduce the synergistic knowledge operator
[G]
. Our notion of synergistic knowledge is capable of describing scenarios in which a group of agents can know more than just the consequences of their pooled knowledge. Different from the higher-order interpretation of distributed knowledge by Goubault et al.
[
9
]
, which analyses the knowledge of a set of agents, we unfold relations within a group and analyse its knowledge with respect to the internal structure of the group. With this interpretation, the knowledge of two seemingly equal groups can differ due to internal relations between agents. Limiting oneself to sets of agents cannot model this without introducing hybrid agents that represent relations between agents.

Given a finite set of agents
\mathsf{Ag}
, we introduce a new modality
[G]
, where the set
G\subseteq\mathsf{Pow}(\mathsf{Ag})\setminus\{\emptyset\}
is called an agent pattern. Agent patterns are best understood representing relations within a group. For example, if
~{G=\{\{a,b,c\}\}}
, then, the agents
a,b,
and
c
mutually benefit from each other and
[G]
reasons about the synergy of the three agents; whereas if
G=\{\{a\},\{b\},\{c\}\}
, the modality
[G]
describes traditional distributed knowledge. When interpreting the language of synergistic knowledge on simplicial models based on semi-simplicial sets,

is true in a simplex
S
if and only if

in
G

Another contribution of this work is the axiom system
\mathsf{Syn}
. We show that
\mathsf{Syn}
is sound and complete with respect to our simplicial models. Our proof employs a variant of the unravelling method used to establish completeness for traditional distributed knowledge (cf. Fagin et al.

). However, our logic has new axioms that close knowledge under sub-simplices. For example, we cannot only rely on the usual monotonicity axiom that states
[G]\varphi\rightarrow[H]\varphi

. Instead, we need that

[\{B\}]\phi\rightarrow[G]\phi\quad\text{if there exists $A\in G$ and $B\subseteq A$.}

(1)

[G\cup\{B\}]\phi\rightarrow[G]\phi\quad\text{if there exists $A\in G$ and $B\subseteq A$.}

After introducing the syntax and semantics of
\mathsf{Syn}
, we proceed by proving soundness and completeness of
\mathsf{Syn}
with respect to simplicial models. Soundness can be proven right away, but completeness is more involved. To prove completeness, we first introduce different types of Kripke models and show that a weaker version of

\mathsf{Syn}

\mathsf{Syn}

We first introduce
\kappa

\kappa

\mathsf{Syn}

\mathsf{Syn}
with respect to simplicial models, we develop a novel construction that takes a proper

\mathsf{Syn}
with respect to our simplicial models. The constructive nature of the translation intuitively demonstrates the relation between the two semantic approaches.

Example 1

A

we have that
\sim_{H}\subseteq\sim_{G}
, i.e.,
w\sim_{H}v
implies
w\sim_{G}v
. Thus,
\mathcal{M},v\Vdash\phi
whenever
w\sim_{H}v
. Therefore, it follows that
\mathcal{M},w\Vdash[H]\phi
.

6.

(Equiv): Assume that for an arbitrary
G
, there exists
A\in G
with
B\subseteq A
. Further, let
w\in W
be arbitrary such that
\mathcal{M},w\Vdash[G\cup\{B\}]\phi
. Therefore, for all
v\in W
,
w\sim_{G\cup\{B\}}v
implies
\mathcal{M},v\Vdash\phi
by assumption. By
\mathsf{K}4
we have that
\sim_{G}\subseteq\sim_{G\cup\{B\}}
and thus
w\sim_{G}v
implies
w\sim_{G\cup\{B\}}v
. Hence,
\mathcal{M},v\Vdash\phi
whenever
w\sim_{G}v
and thus
\mathcal{M},w\Vdash[G]\phi
.

Lastly, we show (
G
-Nec). Let
A\in\mathcal{L}
and assume
\Vdash_{\kappa}A
. We need to show that
[G]A
is
\kappa
-valid. Let
\mathcal{M}=(W,\sim,V)
be an arbitrary
\kappa
-model. By assumption,
\mathcal{M},w\Vdash A
for all
w\in W
. Thus, for any
v\in W
with
w\sim_{G}v
, it holds that
\mathcal{M},v\Vdash A
. By the definition of truth,
\mathcal{M},w\Vdash[G]A
, and since
w\in W
was arbitrary,
\mathcal{M}\Vdash[G]A
. Moreover, due to
\mathcal{M}
being arbitrary,
[G]A
is
\kappa
-valid.
∎

Definition 10
.

Let
G
be an agent pattern and let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}
, we define

\Gamma\setminus[G]:=\{\phi\mid[G]\phi\in\Gamma\}.

Definition 11

.

A set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is consistent if and only if
\Gamma\not\vdash\bot
.
\Gamma
is maximal consistent if none of its proper supersets is consistent.

Definition 12

(Canonical Model)
.

The canonical model
\mathcal{M}^{c}=(W^{c},\sim^{c},V^{c})
for
\mathcal{L}
is defined as

1.

W^{c}:=\{\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}\mid\Gamma\text{ is a maximal consistent set}\}

2.

3.

V^{c}(p):=\{\Gamma\in W^{c}\mid p\in\Gamma\}.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{c}=(W^{c},\sim^{c},V^{c})
be the canonical model,
G
be an agent pattern, and
\Gamma\in W^{c}
, then

\Gamma\in\mathsf{Alive}(G)_{\mathcal{M}^{c}}\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathsf{alive}(G)\in\Gamma.

Proof.

We first show that

\Gamma\in\mathsf{Alive}(G)_{\mathcal{M}^{c}}\quad\text{implies}\quad\mathsf{alive}(G)\in\Gamma.

Assume
\Gamma\in\mathsf{Alive}(G)_{\mathcal{M}^{c}}
. By Definition
8
,
\Gamma\sim^{c}_{G}\Gamma
. Towards a contradiction, assume that
\mathsf{alive}(G)\not\in\Gamma
, i.e.,
[G]\bot\in\Gamma
by the maximal consistency of
\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma\sim^{c}_{G}\Gamma
, i.e.,
\Gamma\setminus[G]\subseteq\Gamma
, this yields
\bot\in\Gamma
, which contradicts the consistency of
\Gamma
. Thus
\mathsf{alive}(G)\in\Gamma
.
We now show that

\mathsf{alive}(G)\in\Gamma\quad\text{implies}\quad\Gamma\in\mathsf{Alive}(G)_{\mathcal{M}^{c}}.

Assume
\mathsf{alive}(G)\in\Gamma
. We need to show
\Gamma\sim^{c}_{G}\Gamma
. Let
\phi\in\Gamma\setminus[G]
, i.e.,
[G]\phi\in\Gamma
. Since

\mathsf{alive}(G)\to([G]\phi\to\phi)

is an axiom of
\mathsf{Syn^{\text{-}}}
and by the maximal consistency of
\Gamma
, it follows that
~{\phi\in\Gamma}
, i.e.,
\Gamma\sim^{c}_{G}\Gamma
. By Definition
8
, we obtain
\Gamma\in\mathsf{Alive}(G)_{\mathcal{M}^{c}}
.
∎

Lemma 12
.

\mathcal{M}^{c}
is a
\kappa
-model.

Proof.

We show that
\mathcal{M}^{c}
satisfies all properties of
\kappa
-models.

1.

Symmetry: Assume
\Gamma\setminus[G]\subseteq\Delta
. We need to show
\Delta\setminus[G]\subseteq\Gamma
. Let
\phi\in\Delta\setminus[G]
, i.e.,
[G]\phi\in\Delta
, and assume towards a contradiction that
\phi\not\in\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is a maximal consistent set and (B) is an axiom of
\mathsf{Syn^{\text{-}}}
, we have
\neg\phi\in\Gamma
as well as
[G]\neg[G]\neg\phi\in\Gamma
by the maximal consistency of
\Gamma
. Therefore,
\neg[G]\neg\phi\in\Gamma\setminus[G]
and thus
\neg[G]\neg\phi\in\Delta
. This is a contradiction because we assumed
[G]\phi\in\Delta
. Hence, we conclude
\phi\in\Gamma
which shows that
\Delta\setminus[G]\subseteq\Gamma
.

2.

Transitivity: Assume
\Gamma\sim^{c}_{G}\Delta
and
\Delta\sim^{c}_{G}\Phi
. We need to show
\Gamma\sim^{c}_{G}\Phi
. By assumption, we have
\Gamma\setminus[G]\subseteq\Delta
and
\Delta\setminus[G]\subseteq\Phi
. Let
\phi\in\Gamma\setminus[G]
, i.e.,
[G]\phi\in\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is maximally consistent and because (4) is an axiom of
\mathsf{Syn^{\text{-}}}
, we have
[G][G]\phi\in\Gamma
, and thus
[G]\phi\in\Delta
. Further, since
\Delta\sim^{c}_{G}\Phi
, we have
\phi\in\Phi
and hence
\Gamma\setminus[G]\subseteq\Phi
.

3.

\mathsf{K}1
: Assume
\Gamma\in\mathsf{Alive}(G)
and
\Gamma\in\mathsf{Alive}(H)
. By Lemma
11
it follows that
\mathsf{alive}(G)\in\Gamma
and
\mathsf{alive}(H)\in\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is a maximal consistent set and (Union) is an axiom of
\mathsf{Syn^{\text{-}}}
it follows that
\mathsf{alive}(G\cup H)\in\Gamma
. By Lemma
11
we have
\Gamma\in\mathsf{Alive}(G\cup H)
.

4.

\mathsf{K}2
: Assume
A,B\in G
and let
\Gamma\in\mathsf{Alive}(G)
. By Lemma
11
it follows that
\mathsf{alive}(G)\in\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is a maximal consistent set and (Clo) is an axiom of
\mathsf{Syn^{\text{-}}}
it follows that
\mathsf{alive}(\{A\cup B\})\in\Gamma
by the maximal consistency of
\Gamma
. By Lemma
11
we have
\Gamma\in\mathsf{Alive}(\{A\cup B\})
.

5.

\mathsf{K}3
: Assume
(\Gamma,\Delta)\in\sim^{c}_{H}
, we need to show that
(\Gamma,\Delta)\in\sim^{c}_{G}
. Let
~{\phi\in\Gamma\setminus[G]}
, i.e.,
[G]\phi\in\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is maximally consistent and (Mono) is an axiom of

we have that
[H]\phi\in\Gamma
. Since we assumed

we have
~{\phi\in\Delta}
.

6.

\mathsf{K}4
: Assume
(\Gamma,\Delta)\in\sim^{c}_{G}
, we need to show that
(\Gamma,\Delta)\in\sim^{c}_{G\cup\{B\}}
. Let
\phi\in\Gamma\setminus[G\cup\{B\}]
, i.e.,
[G\cup\{B\}]\phi\in\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is maximally consistent and (Equiv) is an axiom of
\mathsf{Syn^{\text{-}}}
we have that
[G]\phi\in\Gamma
. Since we assumed
\Gamma\setminus[G]\subseteq\Delta
we have
\phi\in\Delta
.

7.

\mathsf{NE}
: Let
\Gamma\in W^{c}
be arbitrary. Since
\Gamma
is maximally consistent and (NE) is an axiom of
\mathsf{Syn^{\text{-}}}
, there exists an agent pattern

such that
\mathsf{alive}(G)\in\Gamma
. By Lemma

,
\Gamma\sim^{c}_{G}\Gamma
.
∎

The truth lemma is standard and completeness follows as usual.

Lemma 13

be the canonical model. For each world

\mathcal{M}^{c},\Gamma\Vdash\phi\text{ iff }\phi\in\Gamma.

\mathsf{Syn^{\text{-}}}

(

A pre-model

Definition 14

(History)
.

Let
F=(W,\sim)
be a frame. A history

is a non-empty and finite sequence of triples

where

1.

w\sim_{G}v
and
G
is maximal under set inclusion. That means, there does not exist an agent pattern
G^{\prime}
with
G\subsetneq G^{\prime}
and
w\sim_{G^{\prime}}v
;

2.

(w^{\prime},G^{\prime},v^{\prime})
is the successor of
(w,G,v)
, then

.

We write

to denote the last world of a history

(w,G,v)
is the last element of

\ell(h)=v
. Furthermore,
h\parallel(\ell(h),G,v)
denotes the extension of

with
(\ell(h),G,v)
. The set of all histories over a frame

is denoted by

.

Lemma 14

(Downwards closure)

Let
F=(W,\sim)
be a
\kappa
-frame and consider a history

and let

be an element of

. If there exists

with

, then

.

Proof.

Towards a contradiction, suppose that

. Consider the agent pattern

. Clearly

and

because of

. This contradicts

being maximal under set inclusion and thus,

.

(

Lemma 15

Let
F=(W,\sim)
be a
\kappa
-frame and consider

with
~{h\rightarrow_{G}h^{\prime}}
for some agent pattern

2.

By assumption,

∎

Theorem 4
.

Let
F=(W,\sim)
be a
\kappa
-frame. Then
U(F)
is a
\kappa
-frame.

Proof.

Observe that
\approx_{G}=(\rightarrow_{G}\cup\rightarrow_{G}^{-1})^{*}
is transitive and symmetric, because the transitive closure of a symmetric relation is transitive and symmetric. Moreover,
\mathsf{NE}
follows because histories are not empty.

•

\mathsf{K}1
: Assume
(h,h)\in\approx_{G}
and
(h,h)\in\approx_{H}
. By Remark
6
,
\ell(h)\sim_{G}\ell(h)
as well as
\ell(h)\sim_{H}\ell(h)
. Since
F
satisfies
\mathsf{K}1
, it holds that
\ell(h)\sim_{G\cup H}\ell(h)
. Hence,

•

\mathsf{K}2
: Assume

,
\ell(h)\sim_{G}\ell(h)
. Because

\mathsf{K}2
, we have
\ell(h)\sim_{\{A\cup B\}}\ell(h)
. Thus

with

is a valid history which implies that

by symmetry and transitivity.

•

\mathsf{K}3
: Assume
(h,h^{\prime})\in\approx_{H}
and
G\subseteq H
. By Corollary
4
, there must exist a
\rightarrow_{H}
-path
\tau
from
h
to
h^{\prime}
. Let
(s,s^{\prime})\in\tau
be arbitrary. Since

\mathsf{K}3
, Lemma

implies that

. Therefore,
\tau
is a
\rightarrow_{G}
-path and

by Corollary

.

•

\mathsf{K}4
: Assume
(h,h^{\prime})\in\approx_{G}
and that there exists

with

. By Corollary
4
there exists a
\rightarrow_{G}
-path
\tau
from

to

. Let
(s,s^{\prime})\in\tau
be arbitrary. Since

satisfies
\mathsf{K}4

implies that

is a

We call a

. Further, we often write

. If

in a path instead of

-path has a

(respectively

Lemma 16
.

Let
F=(W,\sim)
be a
\kappa
-frame and consider the unravelled frame
U(F)
. If
(h_{1},h_{n})\in\approx_{G}
, then
h_{1}
and
h_{n}
have a common prefix.

Proof.

If one history is a prefix of the other, the claim follows trivially. Hence, we assume that neither of them is a prefix of the other.
Corollary
4
ensures that there exists a
\rightarrow_{G}
-path
\tau=(h_{1},h_{2}),\cdots,(h_{n-1},h_{n})
, which we can assume to be non-redundant. Moreover, due to our first assumption,
\tau
must have at least one change of direction. In order to show that a common prefix of
h_{1}
and
h_{n}
exists, we show that
\tau
has exactly one change of direction, and that change is of the form
h_{i-1}\leftarrow h_{i}\rightarrow h_{i+1}
with
i>1
.

Let
h_{i}
be the history at which the first change of direction occurs. Notice, that this implies that
i>1
. First, we observe that this change of direction cannot be of the form
h_{i-1}\rightarrow h_{i}\leftarrow h_{i+1}
, because this would imply
h_{i-1}=h_{i+1}
and contradict
\tau
being non-redundant. Therefore, the first change of direction must be of the form
h_{i-1}\leftarrow h_{i}\rightarrow h_{i+1}
. A subsequent change of direction would be of the form
h_{i+k-1}\rightarrow_{G}h_{i+k}\leftarrow_{G}h_{i+k+1}
for
k\geq 1
which contradicts that
\tau
is non-redundant. Therefore,
h_{i}
is a common prefix.
∎

Lemma 17
.

Let
F=(W,\sim)
be a
\kappa
-frame and consider the unravelled frame
U(F)
. For any two agent patterns

and

(h,h^{\prime})\in\approx_{G}\text{ and }(h,h^{\prime})\in\approx_{H}\text{ implies }(h,h^{\prime})\in\approx_{G\cup H}.

Assume

and

•

h^{\prime\prime}_{G}\rightarrow_{G}\cdots\rightarrow_{G}h

•

h^{\prime\prime}_{G}\rightarrow_{G}\cdots\rightarrow_{G}h^{\prime}

•

h^{\prime\prime}_{H}\rightarrow_{H}\cdots\rightarrow_{H}h

•

h^{\prime\prime}_{H}\rightarrow_{H}\cdots\rightarrow_{H}h^{\prime}

Observe that either
h^{\prime\prime}_{G}=h^{\prime\prime}_{H}
or one of the histories is a proper prefix of the other. If
h^{\prime\prime}_{G}\neq h^{\prime\prime}_{H}

be the longer history. If they are of the same length, fix either
h^{\prime\prime}=h^{\prime\prime}_{G}
or
h^{\prime\prime}=h^{\prime\prime}_{H}

By Definition

and

for all

6
Conclusion

This work presented the normal modal logic
\mathsf{Syn}
, a logic in which we can reason about what a group of agents knows beyond their pooled individual knowledge. The new modality
[G]
allows us to express the knowledge of a group while also accounting for relations between its members. Hence, the knowledge of two seemingly equal groups may differ due to different relationships among their agents. Moreover, we provided simplicial semantics for
\mathsf{Syn}
based on semi-simplicial sets and demonstrated its use by analysing consensus objects and the dining cryptographers problem. To round off our work on synergistic knowledge, we showed that
\mathsf{Syn}
is sound and complete with respect to our simplicial models.

Semi-simplicial sets were initially studied in order to represent improper Kripke models and to explore new types of group knowledge (cf. van Ditmarsch et al.
[
5
]
). While synergistic knowledge definitely is a new form of distributed knowledge, we still must require that
\mathsf{Syn}
contains the axiom (P) because for any simplex

,

is a valid agent pattern. Thus,
\mathsf{Syn^{\text{-}}}
can describe improper Kripke models. For example, the

Future Work

In this work, we presented a candidate for an indistinguishability relation. However, there may be other reasonable options. For example, another possible notion of indistinguishability is one that takes the connectivity of an agent pattern into account. It is an intuitive option for settings where adding an unrelated agent to a pattern must not result in new knowledge. For example, adding an agent without any communication links to a network does not strengthen the network’s reasoning power. In other words, an agent pattern is only as strong as its weakest connected component. Unfortunately, the concept of indistinguishability so far cannot capture this.
