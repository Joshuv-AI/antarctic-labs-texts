Abstract
For some spatially nonlocal diffusion models with a finite range of nonlocal interactions measured by a positive parameter δ \delta , we review their formulation defined on a bounded domain subject to various conditions that correspond to some inhomogeneous data. We consider their consistency to similar inhomogeneous boundary value problems of classical partial differential equation (PDE) models as the nonlocal interaction kernel gets localized in the local δ → 0 \delta\to 0 limit, and at the same time, for rescaled fractional type kernels, to corresponding inhomogeneous nonlocal boundary value problems of fractional equations in the global δ → ∞ \delta\to\infty limit. Such discussions help to delineate issues related to nonlocal problems defined on a bounded domain with inhomogeneous data.

Introduction

[

,

,

•

They can serve as bridges to build connections between continuum PDEs, discrete algebraic, graph and network models, metric geometry, and fractional differential equations

[
30

While the related issues have been subject to earlier investigations, see e.g.

,

2
Local, nonlocal and fractional models

For illustration, we consider problems defined on
a bounded, open domain
{\Omega}\subset{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
with a Lipshitz boundary
{\partial{\Omega}}
.
We adopt a set of notation that is largely similar to those used in
[
20
]
but with necessary modifications in some cases.

Let the closure of
{\Omega}
be denoted by
{\overline{{\Omega}}}={\Omega}\cup\partial{\Omega}
.
The complements are denoted by

{{\Omega}_{c}}={{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\overline{{\Omega}}}
and
{{\overline{{\Omega}}}_{c}}={{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\Omega}
respectively. Note that
{{\overline{{\Omega}}}_{c}}=\overline{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus{\Omega}}=\overline{{{\Omega}_{c}}}
.

Given the domain
{\Omega}
and a positive parameter
\delta>0
, we define an
interaction layer of width
\delta
by

{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}:=\{{\bm{y}}\in{{\Omega}_{c}}\;\mid\;\text{dist}({\bm{y}},{\Omega})<\delta\,\}.

(1)

We let
\overline{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}
denote the closure of

{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
.

{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}={\Omega}\cup{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}

and
{{\overline{{\Omega}}}_{{\delta}}}=\overline{{\Omega}\cup{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}={\overline{{\Omega}}}\cup\overline{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}
.
In this work, we are interested in some special cases and limiting regimes

\delta=0,\quad 0\leftarrow{\delta}\ll\text{diam\,}{\Omega},\quad{\delta}\approx\text{diam\,}{\Omega},\quad\text{diam\,}{\Omega}\ll{\delta}\to\infty,\quad{\delta}=\infty.

(2)

Note that

{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{0}}}=\emptyset
and
\overline{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{0}}}}={\partial{\Omega}}
, while
{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}={{\Omega}_{c}}
and
\overline{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}}={{\overline{{\Omega}}}_{c}}
.

We also define some domains for any pair of points
{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}
. These include
{\Omega}^{2}={\Omega}\times{\Omega}
, which denotes the tensor product of any domain
{\Omega}
with itself and

{\widetilde{{\Omega}}_{\delta}}={{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega}^{2}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\widetilde{{\Omega}}_{\infty}}=({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})^{2}\setminus{\Omega}_{c}^{2}.

2.1
Local diffusion models

We first consider a classical Poisson problem
for an unknown solution
u\colon\Omega\subset{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\begin{cases}-{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}u({\bm{x}}):=-(\Delta u)({\bm{x}})=F({\bm{x}},u({\bm{x}})),&\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega},\\
{{\mathcal{B}}_{0}}u({\bm{x}})=g_{0}({\bm{x}}),&\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in\overline{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{0}}}}={\partial{\Omega}},\end{cases}

(3)

where
F({\bm{x}},u)

and
g_{0}({\bm{x}})
denote given functions defined on
{\Omega}\times{\mathbb{R}}
and
\partial{\Omega}
, respectively. Here, we elect to work with the Laplacian
\Delta
rather than more general diffusion operators.
A special case is that
F({\bm{x}},u)
is independent of
u
, thus leading to a standard linear PDE on
{\Omega}
.
For the boundary conditions operator
{{\mathcal{B}}_{0}}
, we have the choices

{{\mathcal{B}}_{0}}u({\bm{x}})=\begin{cases}u({\bm{x}}),&{\bm{x}}\in\partial{\Omega}^{D}\;\mbox{(Dirichlet BC)},\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}({\bm{x}})&{\bm{x}}\in\partial{\Omega}^{N}\;\mbox{(Neumann BC)},\end{cases}

(4)

where
{\partial{\Omega}^{D}}
and
{\partial{\Omega}^{N}}
are subset of
{\partial{\Omega}}
such that

{\partial{\Omega}^{D}}\cup{\partial{\Omega}^{N}}={\partial{\Omega}}
with
{\partial{\Omega}^{D}}\cap{\partial{\Omega}^{N}}
having zero
d-1
dimensional measure. Note that

{{\mathcal{B}}_{0}}
can also take on other forms, (e.g.,

{{\mathcal{B}}_{0}}u({\bm{x}})=\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{n}}({\bm{x}})-\alpha u({\bm{x}}),\quad\forall{\bm{x}}\in\partial{\Omega}^{N}

(5)

for a positive constant
\alpha>0
)
to yield other (e.g., Robin type) boundary conditions.
In the case
{\partial{\Omega}^{D}}={\partial{\Omega}}
, we have a pure Dirichlet problem while
{\partial{\Omega}^{N}}={\partial{\Omega}}
leads to a pure Neumann problem. In the latter case, compatibility conditions may be needed on the data, and an additional constraint (e.g., having a zero mean over
{\Omega}
) on the solutions may be imposed to ensure uniqueness.

Remark 2.1
.

Problem (
3
), which is named the local diffusion problem here, is a well-studied local PDE model used for steady-state normal diffusion.

2.2
Fractional diffusion models

Fractional PDEs are PDEs with fractional-order partial derivatives of unknown solutions. They are nonlocal integral models with a global or infinite range of interactions.
Here, we consider a nonlocal fractional diffusion model associated with the
integral fractional Laplacian

{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}=-(-\Delta)^{s}
, which, for
u\colon{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
and
s\in(0,1)
, is given by

\displaystyle-{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}u({\bm{x}})=(-\Delta)^{s}u({{\bm{x}}})

\displaystyle=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}(u({{\bm{x}}})-u({{\bm{y}}})){{\gamma}_{s}}{(|{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}|)}{d{\bm{y}}},\quad\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}

(6)

where the kernel function is defined by

{{\gamma}_{s}}(|{\bm{z}}|)=\displaystyle\frac{{C_{{d},s}}}{|{\bm{z}}|^{{d}+2s}},\quad\text{with}\quad{C_{{d},s}}=\displaystyle\frac{2^{2s}s\Gamma(s+{{{d}}/{2}})}{\pi^{{d}/2}\Gamma(1-s)},

(7)

where
\Gamma
denotes the gamma function. Note that the singular integral in (
6
) should be interpreted in the principal value sense.

The
fractional diffusion problem
considered here is then given by

\begin{cases}-{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}u({\bm{x}})=F({\bm{x}},u({\bm{x}})),&\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}{,}\\
{{\mathcal{B}}_{\infty}^{s}}u({\bm{x}})=g_{\infty}({\bm{x}}),&\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}}={{\Omega}_{c}},\end{cases}

(8)

where
F({\bm{x}},u)
is as before and
g_{\infty}({\bm{x}})
is a given data defined for
{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}
. The operator
{{\mathcal{B}}_{\infty}^{s}}
is defined by

{{\mathcal{B}}_{\infty}^{s}}u({\bm{x}})=\begin{cases}u({\bm{x}}),&{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}\;\mbox{(Dirichlet VC)},\\
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}^{s}}u({\bm{x}}),&{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}\;\mbox{(Neumann VC)},\end{cases}

(9)

where
{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}
and
{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}
are two disjoint subdomains of

{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}
such that
\overline{{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}\cup{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}}={{\overline{{\Omega}}}_{c}}
.

{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}^{s}}
is a suitably defined nonlocal operator, for example, given by
[
26
]
,

{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}^{s}}u({{\bm{x}}})=-\int_{{\Omega}}(u({{\bm{y}}})-u({{\bm{x}}})){{\gamma}_{s}}{(|{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}|)}{{d{\bm{y}}}},\quad\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}={{\Omega}_{c}}}.

(10)

In the above definition,
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}^{s}}
is defined as an integral over the domain
{\Omega}
only, instead of the whole space
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the constraint
{{\mathcal{B}}_{\infty}^{s}}u=g_{\infty}
is applied on a domain having a nonzero volume in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, thus representing a nonlocal boundary condition which is also called a volume constraint (VC)
[
30
]
.

The integral fractional Laplacian is sometimes called the whole space fractional Laplacian as the integral ranges the whole space
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.
It is a special case of the nonlocal Laplacian defined later (see (
19
) and related discussions in section
2.3
). It is also equivalent to the Fourier representation
[
76
]

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|^{2s}(u,\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\bm{x}}})\,\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}{d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|^{2s}\mathcal{F}\{u\}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}))({\bm{x}}),

(11)

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform. Formally if we let
s\to 1^{-}
in the above, it recovers the Fourier spectral representation of the classical local Laplacian
-\Delta
. Indeed, it is well known that
(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)\rightarrow-\Delta u(x)
as
s\to 1^{-}
for any function
u
with sufficient regularity; see
e.g.

[
68
, Theorems 3 and 4]
and
[
25
, Proposition 4.4]
.

Remark 2.3
.

The fractional Laplacian (
6
) is often used to model superdiffusion. In probabilistic terms, in contrast to the local Laplacian that corresponds to the normal diffusion described by Brownian motion, the fractional Laplacian can be connected to superdiffusion associated with Lévy flights in which the length of particle jumps follows a heavy-tailed power law distribution, reflecting the long-range interactions between particles, see e.g.
[
26
,
56
,
66
]
.

The problem (
8
) is called a fractional diffusion model here to distinguish it from other diffusion models. It is given in the distribution sense so that the equations are only specified over an open domain
{\Omega}
.
More rigorous descriptions of (
8
) will be discussed later.

Other variants of the operators
{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}
and
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}^{s}}
are discussed in section
2.4
. For notation simplicity, we drop the superscript
s
in
{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}
,

{{\mathcal{B}}_{\infty}^{s}}
and
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}^{s}}
whenever there is no ambiguity, that is

{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}={{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}}
,

{{\mathcal{B}}_{\infty}^{s}}={{\mathcal{B}}_{\infty}}
and
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}^{s}}={{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}}
.
Meanwhile,
similar to the local case, one can also consider other volume constraints
such as

{{\mathcal{B}}_{\infty}}u({\bm{x}})={{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}}u({\bm{x}})-\alpha u({\bm{x}})
for
{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}_{c}}
to mimic a Robin type constraint.

2.3
Nonlocal diffusion models

To distinguish from the local and fractional counterparts, we consider a class of special nonlocal diffusion models here associated with a finite range of interactions, measured by the so-called horizon parameter (interaction radius)
\delta>0
.

For
{\delta}>0
, we consider the following nonlocal problem for a scalar-valued unknown function
u({\bm{x}})
defined on
{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}
,

\begin{cases}-{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}u=F({\bm{x}},u({\bm{x}})),&\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega},\\
{{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta}}u=g_{\delta}({\bm{x}}),&\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}},\end{cases}

(12)

with
F
and
g_{\delta}
given and a
nonlocal diffusion operator
defined by

{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}u{({\bm{x}})}:=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}},\quad\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},

(13)

where the kernel function
{\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}
is symmetric,

i.e.
,

{\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}={\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}})},\quad\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},

non-negative, and with a compact support in
{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}
in
B_{\delta}({\bf 0})
,
the
\delta
-ball of the origin.
that is,

{\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}=0\text{ whenever }|{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}|>{\delta}.

Again, the integral in (
13
) (and the one in (
19
), which will be introduced shortly) should be understood in the sense of principal value throughout the paper. Moreover, due to the compact support of
{\gamma_{\delta}}
, for
{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}
, we can rewrite (
13
) as

{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}u({\bm{x}}):=\int_{{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}}(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}},\quad\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}.

(14)

Analogous to (
4
), we have the choice of volume constraints

{{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta}}u({\bm{x}})=\begin{cases}u({\bm{x}})&{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}\;\mbox{(Dirichlet VC)},\\
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\delta}}u({\bm{x}}),&{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}\;\mbox{(Neumann VC)},\end{cases}

(15)

where
{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
and
{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
are two disjoint subdomains of

{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
such that
\overline{{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}\cup{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}=\overline{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}
.
The operator
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\delta}}
can be defined, for example, analogously to (
10
) by

{{\mathcal{N}}_{\delta}}u({\bm{x}}):=-\int_{\Omega}(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}},\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}.

(16)

Note that generally,
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\delta}}u
only needs to be defined and applied on

{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
. Its definition on
{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
is given for the convenience of notation only.

Rather than considering kernels in the general form, we focus on
those translation-invariant (
i.e.
, functions of
{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}
) and radial symmetric (
i.e.
, depending only on
{|{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}|}
).
In particular, we consider a more specific re-scaled form given by

{\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}=C_{d}({\delta})\gamma\biggl(\displaystyle\frac{|{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}|}{{\delta}}\biggr)

(17)

for a kernel
\gamma({|\cdot|})
that satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\gamma(s)\geq 0,\text{ and non-increasing, }\forall s\in[0,1),\;\;\gamma(s)=0,\forall s\geq 1,\\
\widehat{\gamma}(|{\bm{z}}|)=|{\bm{z}}|^{2}\gamma(|{\bm{z}}|)\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}).\end{array}\right.

(18)

For such kernels, we see that
{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}
can also be written as

{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}u{({\bm{x}})}:=C_{d}({\delta})\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}})){\gamma}\left(\frac{{|{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}|}}{\delta}\right){d{\bm{y}}},\quad\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

(19)

The scaling factor
C_{d}({\delta})
is of special interest in this work and is defined differently depending on the regimes that we study. We give two examples below.

•

For
0<{\delta}\ll\text{diam\,}({\Omega})
,

C_{d}({\delta})
is chosen to satisfy

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}{|{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}|}^{2}{\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}d{\bm{y}}={{\delta}^{d+2}}C_{d}({\delta})\int_{{B_{1}({\bf 0})}}|\bm{\xi}|^{2}\gamma(|\bm{\xi}|){d}\bm{\xi}=2d.

(20)

so that as
{\delta}\to 0
, the local limit of
{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}
becomes the standard Laplacian
{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}=\Delta

[
30
,
28
,
54
,
55
]
.

•

For

{\delta}\gg\text{diam\,}({\Omega})
, the factor and kernels are chosen to satisfy

C_{d}({\delta})=\frac{C_{d,s}}{{\delta}^{d+2s}}\quad\text{for}\quad{\gamma}(t)=t^{-{d}-2s}\mathbbm{1}_{(0,1)},

(21)

so that
C_{d}({\delta}){\gamma}(t/{\delta})=C_{d,s}t^{-d-2s}\mathbbm{1}_{(0,{\delta})}
,
that is,
{\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}
is a truncation of the
fractional kernel
{{\gamma}_{s}}
defined in (
7
)

{\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}=\begin{cases}{{\gamma}_{s}}{(|{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}|)}=\displaystyle\frac{{C_{{d},s}}}{{|{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}|}^{{d}+2s}},&\quad\forall\>{{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}}\in B_{\delta}({\bf 0}),\\
0,&\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(22)

Consequently, we have
{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}\to{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}=-(-\Delta)^{s}
as
{\delta}\to\infty

[
22
,
12
,
75
]
.

Remark 2.4
.

Although
{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}u({\bm{x}})
is defined for any
{\bm{x}}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, independent of any spatial domain, for problem (

{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}u{({\bm{x}})}

(23)

Remark 2.7
.

Concerning the operator
{{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta}}
in (
15
),
one may consider more general forms. For example,
with
{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}={{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
, we may have

{{\mathcal{B}}_{\delta}}u({\bm{x}})={{\mathcal{N}}_{\delta}}u({\bm{x}})-{{{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}}u({\bm{x}}),\;\forall{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}

(24)

where the linear operator

{{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}
is given by

{{{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}}u({\bm{x}})=\int_{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}u({\bm{y}})\sigma_{\delta}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})d{\bm{y}},\;\forall{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}={{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}

(25)

where the

\sigma_{\delta}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
can be a bounded and symmetric kernel
such that as

,

\int_{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}{{{\mathcal{D}}_{\delta}}}u({\bm{x}})v({\bm{x}})d{\bm{x}}\to\int_{\partial{\Omega}}u({\bm{x}})v({\bm{x}})d{\bm{x}},

(26)

for any pair of smoothly defined functions
u
and

on
{\Omega}
. In this case, we get a problem analogous to the local Robin boundary value problem.

Remark 2.8
.

While we focus on nonlocal diffusion operators

) with a symmetric kernel

,

Note that for the pure Neumann case,

,

,

over

and the equations in (

) are modified to

\begin{cases}-{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}u=F({\bm{x}},u({\bm{x}}))-\lambda_{N},&\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega},\\
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\delta}}u={g_{N}^{\delta}}({\bm{x}})-\lambda_{N},&\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}},\end{cases}

where

where

2.4
Regional fractional and nonlocal Laplacians

An operator related to the fractional Laplacian, but defined with respect to the domain
{\Omega}
, is the
regional fractional Laplacian
given by
[
7
,
14
]

(-\Delta)_{\Omega}^{s}u({{\bm{x}}})=\int_{{\Omega}}(u({{\bm{x}}})-u({{\bm{y}}})){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}}{,}\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}.

(29)

It can be used to define a problem given by

(-\Delta)_{\Omega}^{s}u({{\bm{x}}})=F({\bm{x}},u({\bm{x}})),\quad\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega},

(30)

which is another approach to generalize the local Poisson Neumann problem to the nonlocal fractional case
[

, Section 7]
. Note that in comparison with the fractional Laplacian
{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}
defined in (
6
),
the only difference is that the regional operator in (

) involves an integral over
{\Omega}
instead of
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.

Similarly, a nonlocal problem on
{\Omega}
related to the regional fractional Laplacian is

-{{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},\Omega}}u({\bm{x}})=F({\bm{x}},u({\bm{x}})),\quad\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega},

(31)

where the
regional nonlocal diffusion operator

{{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},\Omega}}
is given by

{{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},\Omega}}u({\bm{x}})=\int_{{\Omega}}(u({{\bm{y}}})-u({{\bm{x}}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}}{,}\quad\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}.

(32)

Then (
30
) is a special case of (
31
) by choosing special fractional kernels as in (

)
and setting
\delta=\infty
. Hence, for kernels of the form
(
21
), we may see
{{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},\Omega}}
as a “regional” nonlocal Laplacian
with respect to the domain
{\Omega}
.

Indeed, let
{\Omega}={\Omega}_{1}\cup{\Omega}_{2}
with
{\Omega}_{1}\cap{\Omega}_{2}=\emptyset
,
the problem (

), by a simple change of notations, can be equivalently written as the nonlocal problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-{{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},\Omega}}u({\bm{x}})=F({\bm{x}},u({\bm{x}}))&\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}_{1},\\
{{\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}}u({\bm{x}})=G({\bm{x}},u({\bm{x}}))&\quad\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}_{2}.\end{array}\right.

(33)

Here, a nonlocal operator
{{\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}}
is introduced, which
is defined by

{{\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}}u({\bm{x}}):=-{{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},\Omega}}u({\bm{x}})=\int_{{\Omega}}(u({\bm{x}})-u({\bm{y}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}},\quad\forall{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}_{2},

(34)

and
G({\bm{x}},u({\bm{x}}))=F({\bm{x}},u({\bm{x}}))
on

{\Omega}_{2}
.

{{\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}}
and

only carry symbolic meanings in this case and the equivalence of
(

) and (

) holds independently of how

{\Omega}
is decomposed into
{\Omega}_{1}
and
{\Omega}_{2}
. For example,
a special choice of the domain
{\Omega}_{2}
, for
\delta
sufficiently small, is the interior
{\delta}
-layer
of
{\Omega}
given by

\displaystyle{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}^{0}_{\delta}}}=\{{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}\>\mid\>\text{dist}({\bm{x}},{\partial{\Omega}})<{\delta}\,\}.

(35)

Remark 2.9
.

Note that the definition (

) of

has been adopted in discussions in

-{{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},\Omega}}

-{{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},\Omega}}u({\bm{x}})
is applied on

{{\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}}u({\bm{x}})
inherits the definition on

{\Omega}_{2}
.

Remark 2.10
.

We note that the reformulation (
33
) of
(

) resembles more closely, in appearance, the conventional way of presenting the problem as an equation coupled with a boundary condition (or say volume constraint). Yet, the equivalence of the formulations also highlights the fact that for nonlocal problems, the notion of boundary conditions can be superfluous. A more general way to understand the formulation is that a nonlocal model is largely described by the law of nonlocal interactions that could be altered due to the presence of a boundary.

Remark 2.11
.

We also note that the nonlocal diffusion operator
{{\mathcal{L}}_{\delta}}
reformulated in (

{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}
instead of

for compactly supported kernels of an interaction range

defined on the combined domain
{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}
. However, this association can only be meaningful in the case where the equation (

{\Omega}
, is extended to all
{\bm{x}}\in{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}

Let us examine some limiting cases formally. First, with the kernel

properly scaled with respect to the horizon parameter
\delta
as in (

), thus (

to a Neumann problem of the local diffusion operator with the corresponding boundary condition determined by the limiting behavior of

on the interior
{\delta}
-layer

{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}^{0}_{\delta}}}
defined in (

).

Likewise, with

chosen as in (

exactly the regional fractional Laplacian Neumann problem (

. We thus see the regional nonlocal Laplacian again provides a bridge between
the local Laplacian and the regional fractional Laplacian.

We note that spectral decomposition is another

Generically, such spectrally represented operators might not yield operators with a finite range of interactions spatially. In many cases, the resulting nonlocal kernels for their spatial integral formulations might not have simple analytical representations in real space. We thus do not consider further in this direction, interested readers can find additional discussions in

2.5
Nonlocal flux and Nonlocal Green’s identities

In
[
31
,
20
]
, the operator
{{\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}}
is also used to provide a nonlocal Green’s formula that shares more symbolic
resemblance to the classical Green’s formula for the local Laplacian and for defining a nonlocal flux. We provide a more rigorous statement and derivation here for clarity.

Proposition 2.12
.

Given the nonlocal operators defined by a rescaled kernel
(
17
) with the conditions
(
18
) and
(
20
) satisfied,
for functions
u
and
v
in
C^{2}(\overline{{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}})
, we have the nonlocal Green’s first identity given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{({\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime})^{2}}(v({\bm{y}})-v({\bm{x}}))(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}=-\int_{{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}}v({\bm{x}}){\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}}u({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{{\Omega}}v({\bm{x}}){\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}}u({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}+\int_{{\Omega}^{\prime}}v({\bm{x}}){\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}}u({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}.

(36)

This also readily implies the also the nonlocal Green’s second identity

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}}v({\bm{x}}){\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}}u({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}-\int_{{\Omega}}u({\bm{x}}){\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}}v({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{{\Omega}^{\prime}}u({\bm{x}}){\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}}v({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}+\int_{{\Omega}^{\prime}}v({\bm{x}}){\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}}u({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}.

(37)

Proof.

To prove the Proposition
2.12
, the key is to show that the integrals are well-defined so that the classical Fubini theorem can be applied to get (
2.12
). For

u\in C^{2}(\overline{{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}})
, we now check that

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}}u\in L^{1}({\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime})
, which is a sufficient condition to achieve this. To simplify the notation, we use
{\Omega}
to replace
{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}
so that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}u({\bm{x}})=\int_{\Omega}(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}d{\bm{y}}.

Then it suffices to show that
{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}u\in L^{1}({\Omega})
for all
u\in C^{2}(\overline{{\Omega}})
.

By the regularity of
u
, we have for
{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}
,

\begin{split}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}u({\bm{x}})&=\int_{{\Omega}\backslash B_{{\rm{dist}}({\bm{x}},\partial{\Omega})}({\bm{x}})}(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}d{\bm{y}}\\
&\qquad+\int_{B_{{\rm{dist}}({\bm{x}},\partial{\Omega})}({\bm{x}})}(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}})){\gamma_{\delta}}(|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}|)d{\bm{y}}\\
&\leq C\int_{{\Omega}\backslash B_{{\rm{dist}}({\bm{x}},\partial{\Omega})}({\bm{x}})}|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}|{\gamma_{\delta}}(|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}|)d{\bm{y}}\\
&\qquad+C\int_{B_{{\rm{dist}}({\bm{x}},\partial{\Omega})}({\bm{x}})}|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}|^{2}{\gamma_{\delta}}(|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}|)d{\bm{y}}\\
&\leq C\left(\int_{{\rm{dist}}({\bm{x}},\partial{\Omega})<|{\bm{z}}|<{\delta}}|{\bm{z}}|{\gamma_{\delta}}(|{\bm{z}}|)d{\bm{z}}+1\right)\end{split}

where the constant
C
depends on
\|u\|_{C^{2}(\overline{{\Omega}})}
. Thus, the task remains is to show that

w({\bm{x}}):=\int_{{\rm{dist}}({\bm{x}},\partial{\Omega})<|{\bm{z}}|<{\delta}}|{\bm{z}}|{\gamma_{\delta}}(|{\bm{z}}|)d{\bm{z}}\in L^{1}({\Omega})\,.

Let
{\Omega}^{\epsilon}
denote an inner layer of width
\epsilon>0
surrounding
\partial{\Omega}
where
\epsilon>0
is a small fixed number.
Since it is obvious that

w\in L^{1}({\Omega}\backslash{\Omega}^{\epsilon})
, we only need to show

w\in L^{1}({\Omega}^{\epsilon})
. Define for
t\in(0,\epsilon)
,

\partial{\Omega}_{t}:=\{{\bm{x}}\in{\Omega}~|~{\rm{dist}}({\bm{x}},\partial{\Omega})=t\}.

Then, there exists a general constant
C>0
,

\begin{split}\int_{{\Omega}^{\epsilon}}\int_{{\rm{dist}}({\bm{x}},\partial{\Omega})<|{\bm{z}}|<{\delta}}|{\bm{z}}|{\gamma_{\delta}}(|{\bm{z}}|)d{\bm{z}}&\leq C\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\int_{\partial{\Omega}_{t}}\int_{t<r<{\delta}}r^{d}{\gamma_{\delta}}(r)drd{\bm{x}}dt\\
&\leq C\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\int_{t<r<{\delta}}r^{d}{\gamma_{\delta}}(r)drdt\\
&=C\int_{0}^{\delta}\int_{0}^{\min(r,\epsilon)}r^{d}{\gamma_{\delta}}(r)dtdr\\
&\leq C\int_{0}^{\delta}r^{d+1}{\gamma_{\delta}}(r)dr<\infty.\end{split}

Thus, we get the desired result that

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}u\in L^{1}({\Omega})
.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

Note that by density argument, one can show that the above identities hold in the closure of
C^{2}(\overline{{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}})
with respect to suitable norms.

Remark 2.14
.

In
[

]
and
[

, the identity
(

for

\displaystyle\int_{{\Omega}}v({\bm{x}}){{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},\Omega}}u({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}=\int_{{\Omega}}u({\bm{x}}){{\mathcal{L}}_{{\delta},\Omega}}v({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}.

(38)

This relation, as a consequence of Fubini’s theorem, can be seen as
a nonlocal version of integration by parts.

It is worth pointing out that a similar role as

{{\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}}
can be played by the operator
{{\mathcal{N}}_{\delta}}
defined earlier in (

). In fact, corresponding to the function
u
defined on a union
{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime}
of domains
{\Omega}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}({\Omega}_{1},{\Omega}^{\prime})+\mathcal{F}({\Omega}_{2},{\Omega}^{\prime})=\mathcal{F}({\Omega},{\Omega}^{\prime})\quad\text{for }\;{\Omega}={\Omega}_{1}\cup{\Omega}_{2},\;{\Omega}_{1}\cap{\Omega}_{2}=\emptyset.

Remark 2.15
.

With

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}({\Omega}_{1},{\Omega}_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}({\Omega}_{1},{\Omega}_{2})+\mathcal{F}({\Omega}_{2},{\Omega}_{2})=\mathcal{F}({\Omega}_{1}\cup{\Omega}_{2},{\Omega}_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\Omega}_{2}}\int_{{\Omega}}(u({\bm{x}})-u({\bm{y}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}\,d{\bm{y}}\,d{\bm{x}}=\int_{{\Omega}_{2}}{{\mathcal{N}}_{{\delta},{\Omega}}}u({\bm{x}})\,d{\bm{x}}.

Next, let us state

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{({\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{\prime})^{2}\setminus{\Omega}^{\prime 2}}(v({\bm{y}})-v({\bm{x}}))(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}

(40)

where

3
Well-posed inhomogeneous nonlocal variational problems

We use linear problems where
F({\bm{x}},u)=f({\bm{x}})
as illustrations for well-posed nonlocal models with a horizon parameter
{\delta}
and inhomogeneous nonlocal constraints.
We consider weak formulations corresponding to (
12
) and make connections to a minimization principle. We pay particular attention to the results that are independent of
{\delta}
so that the local and fractional cases can then become special cases, which enforces the generality of the nonlocal models.

3.1
Function spaces and norms

Given a generic domain
{\Omega}
, a parameter
\delta>0
and the extended domain
{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}
,
let us first define an
‘energy’ space

{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}=\{v({\bm{x}})\in L^{2}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})\colon\|v\|_{{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}}<\infty\},

(47)

where
\|\cdot\|_{{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}}
denotes an
‘energy’ norm
induced by the inner product

{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}}}=(\cdot,\cdot)_{{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}}+(\cdot,\cdot)_{{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}}

with

(\cdot,\cdot)_{{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}}
being defined as:

\displaystyle(u,v)_{{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\widetilde{{\Omega}}_{\delta}}}(v({\bm{y}})-v({\bm{x}}))(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}\,{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}\,,

(48)

for any
u,v\in{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}
. Here
{\widetilde{{\Omega}}_{\delta}}={{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}^{2}\setminus{\Omega}^{2}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
.

Remark 3.1
.

A different choice of the nonlocal space and its norm, defined over a domain
{\widehat{{\Omega}}}
, corresponds to
the inner product

\displaystyle{\langle u,v\rangle}_{{V({\widehat{{\Omega}}})}}=(u,v)_{{V({\widehat{{\Omega}}})}}+(u,v)_{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}}

(49)

where

\displaystyle(u,v)_{{V({\widehat{{\Omega}}})}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}}\int_{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}}(u({\bm{y}})-u({\bm{x}}))(v({\bm{y}})-v({\bm{x}})){\gamma_{\delta}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}

(50)

Such space and norm can be associated with the regional version of the nonlocal and fractional versions of diffusion discussed in Section
2.4
.
When
{\widehat{{\Omega}}}
is replaced by
{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}
, the main difference with the norm of
{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}
induced from (
48
)
is the inclusion of additional nonlocal interactions in
{{\Omega}^{2}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}

for the norm in
V({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})
. Note that this difference disappears for functions constrained to have zero values on
{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
.

The space
{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}
is by definition a subspace of
L^{2}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})

and
can be shown, for suitably chosen kernels like those specified by (
20
) or (
22
), to be a Hilbert space.

The
constrained energy space
is a closed subspace of
{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}

defined by

{V_{c,{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}=\{v({\bm{x}})\in{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}\colon v({\bm{x}})=0\ \mbox{for ${\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}$}\}.

(51)

Note that if
{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}=\emptyset
, we may pick a nontrivial subset
K
of
{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}

to define the space by
space
{V_{c,{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}
by

{V_{c,{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}=\{v({\bm{x}})\in{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}\>\colon\>\int_{K}v({\bm{x}})d{\bm{x}}=0\;\}.

(52)

Some natural choices include
K={\Omega}
and
K={{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}
.
The dual space of
{V_{c,{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}
is denoted by
{V^{\star}_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}
with
(\cdot,\cdot)_{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}
being the duality pairing which, for functions vanishing on
{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
, is also used to denote the equivalent
L^{2}({\Omega}\cup{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}})

{\delta}_{0}>0
, we assume that for all
{\delta}<{\delta}_{0}
, the function
w_{D}^{\delta}
in (
59
)
and(
60
) is extended to
{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{{\delta}_{0}}
with the extension bounded in
V_{c,{\delta}_{0}}({\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{{\delta}_{0}})\cap\alpha L^{m}({\Omega})
and
w_{D}^{\delta}
converges in
L^{2}({\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{{\delta}_{0}})
to
w_{D}^{0}\in H^{1}({\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{{\delta}_{0}})
as
{\delta}\to 0
, where
w_{D}^{0}|_{{\partial{\Omega}^{D}}}=g_{D}^{0}
.

\bullet

We also assume that the Neumann data
{g_{N}^{\delta}}
, as a function defined on
{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}
, is uniformly bounded in the dual space of the energy space. Moreover,

\displaystyle\lim_{{\delta}\to 0}\int_{{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}{g_{N}^{\delta}}({\bm{x}})v({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}=\int_{{\partial{\Omega}^{N}}}g_{N}^{0}({\bm{x}})v({\bm{x}}){d{\bm{x}}}\,,\quad\forall v\in C^{\infty}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}).

(74)

Remark 4.2
.

Let us note that under the assumption on
m
and the Sobolev imbedding theorem, we have
\alpha w_{D}^{0}\in\alpha L^{m}({\Omega})
.

Remark 4.3
.

Notice the special geometric assumptions on the domains
{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
and
{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
in the above discussions. In particular, we require
\{\overline{{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}\}_{{\delta}>0}
and
\{\overline{{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}\}_{{\delta}>0}
to be sequences of nested sets that decrease monotonically to
{\partial{\Omega}^{D}}
and
{\partial{\Omega}^{N}}
, respectively, as
\delta\to 0
. These assumptions rule out the more complicated situations where
{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
or
{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}
may not be attached to the boundary
\partial{\Omega}
.
Some numerical experiments are provided in cases these geometric assumptions are violated.

With these assumptions, we present the convergence theorem for
\delta\to 0
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
u_{\delta}
and
u_{0}
be the weak solutions to the nonlocal and local boundary value problems, respectively. Assume that
\|{\widehat{f}}\|_{V^{\star}_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}
and
\|{g_{D}^{\delta}}\|_{V_{t}({{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}})}
are bounded uniformly in
{\delta}
, then we have

\|u_{\delta}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\to 0\,,

as
{\delta}\to 0
.

Proof.

From the well-posedness theorem, it is easy to see that
\|u_{\delta}\|_{{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}}
,

\|u_{\delta}-w_{D}^{\delta}\|_{{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}(u_{\delta},u_{\delta})

are uniformly bounded and
(u_{\delta}-w_{D}^{\delta})|_{{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}=0
. Take
{\delta}_{0}>0
and let
{\Omega}_{1}:=\overline{{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}^{D}_{\mathcal{I}_{{\delta}_{0}}}}
. By the assumptions on the boundary sets, for any
{\delta}<{\delta}_{0}
, we can always do a zero extension of the function
u_{\delta}-w_{D}^{\delta}

onto
{\Omega}_{1}
, and

\begin{split}&\int_{{\Omega}_{1}}\int_{{\Omega}_{1}}{\gamma_{\delta}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\left((u_{\delta}-w_{D}^{\delta})({\bm{y}})-(u_{\delta}-w_{D}^{\delta})({\bm{x}})\right)^{2}{d{\bm{y}}}{d{\bm{x}}}\\
\leq&\int_{({\Omega}\cup{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}})^{2}\backslash({{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}})^{2}}{\gamma_{\delta}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\left((u_{\delta}-w_{D}^{\delta})({\bm{y}})-(u_{\delta}-w_{D}^{\delta})({\bm{x}})\right)^{2}{d{\bm{y}}}{d{\bm{x}}}\\
\end{split}

We can then use Lemma
1
to conclude that any
L^{2}({\Omega}_{1})
-limit (up to a subsequence) of
u_{\delta}-w_{D}^{\delta}
as
{\delta}\to 0
belongs to
H^{1}({\Omega}_{1})
. Let
v^{\ast}
denote the limit function and
u^{\ast}:=v^{\ast}+w_{D}^{0}
. By the assumption on the Dirichlet data,
u^{\ast}
is the
L^{2}({\Omega}_{1})
-limit of
u_{\delta}
. We want to show
u^{\ast}=u_{0}
. First, since
(u_{\delta}-w_{D}^{\delta})|_{{\Omega}^{D}_{\mathcal{I}_{{\delta}_{0}}}}=0
for all
{\delta}<{\delta}_{0}
, we then have
v^{\ast}|_{{\Omega}^{D}_{\mathcal{I}_{{\delta}_{0}}}}=0
. This implies that
u^{\ast}\in H^{1}({\Omega})
and
u^{\ast}|_{{\partial{\Omega}^{D}}}=w_{D}^{0}|_{{\partial{\Omega}^{D}}}=g_{D}^{0}
.
Now we only need to show that
u^{\ast}
satisfies the weak form (
73
) for any
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}})
with
v|_{{\partial{\Omega}^{D}}}=0
. Assume that the extension of
v
is made such that
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{{\Omega}\cup{\Omega}_{\mathcal{I}_{{\delta}_{0}}}})
and
v|_{{\Omega}^{D}_{\mathcal{I}_{{\delta}_{0}}}}=0
. Since
u_{\delta}
is the weak solution to the nonlocal problem, we have

{{{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}}}(u_{\delta},v){+{\mathcal{Q}}(u_{\delta},v)}=(f,v)_{{\Omega}}+(g^{\delta}_{N},v)_{{{\Omega}^{N}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}\,.

(75)

The right-hand side of (
75
) converges to the right-hand of (
73
) from the assumption on the Neumann boundary data. Using the same argument in
[
69
, Theorem 3.5]
, one can show that as
{\delta}\to 0
,

\int_{{\Omega}}\int_{{\Omega}}{\gamma_{\delta}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})(u_{\delta}({\bm{y}})-u_{\delta}({\bm{x}}))(v({\bm{y}})-v({\bm{x}})){d{\bm{y}}}{d{\bm{x}}}\to\int_{{\Omega}}\nabla u^{\ast}\cdot\nabla v{d{\bm{x}}}\,.

Note also that

\begin{split}&\left|\iint_{({{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}\times{\Omega})\cup({\Omega}\times{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}})}{\gamma_{\delta}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})(u_{\delta}({\bm{y}})-u_{\delta}({\bm{x}}))(v({\bm{y}})-v({\bm{x}})){d{\bm{y}}}{d{\bm{x}}}\right|\\
\leq&\|u_{\delta}\|^{2}_{{V_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}}\cdot\iint_{({{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}\times{\Omega})\cup({\Omega}\times{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}})}{\gamma_{\delta}}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})(v({\bm{y}})-v({\bm{x}}))^{2}{d{\bm{y}}}{d{\bm{x}}}\\
\leq&C\int_{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}|\nabla v|^{2}{d{\bm{x}}}\to 0\,,\end{split}

as
{\delta}\to 0
. We then have the convergence of
{{{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}}}(u_{\delta},v)
to the integral of
\nabla u^{\ast}\cdot\nabla v
on
{\Omega}
. Meanwhile, by the assumption on
m
, we have
u_{\ast}\in L^{m}({\Omega})
. Moreover, we observe that there is a constant
C>0
only depends on
{\Omega}
and
m
such that

\displaystyle\|u_{\delta}-u^{\ast}\|_{L^{m-1}({\Omega})}^{m-1}

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{\delta}-u^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\|u_{\delta}-u^{\ast}\|_{L^{2(m-2)}({\Omega})}^{m-2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u_{\delta}-u^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\|u_{\delta}-u^{\ast}\|_{L^{m}({\Omega})}^{m-2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u_{\delta}-u^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}(\|u_{\delta}\|_{L^{m}({\Omega})}+\|u^{\ast}\|_{L^{m}({\Omega})})^{m-2},

where we have used
2(m-2)\leq m
by the choice of
m
.
Thus, we get the convergence of
u_{\delta}
to
u^{\ast}
in
L^{m-1}({\Omega})
(and consequently the convergence of
q(u_{\delta})
to
q(u^{\ast})
in
L^{1}({\Omega})
) from the
convergence of
u_{\delta}
to
u^{\ast}
in
L^{2}({\Omega})
.
We thus have the convergence of
{\mathcal{Q}}(u_{\delta},v)
to
{\mathcal{Q}}(u_{\ast},v)
.
Therefore
u^{\ast}
is the weak solution of the local boundary value problem, and this implies
u^{\ast}=u_{0}
.
∎

4.3
Fractional limit

Here, we make more studies on treating fractional PDEs as either specialized nonlocal models or limiting cases.

We can let
\delta=\infty
for kernels of the types in (
22
). We identify some of the spaces in this case:

V_{\infty}({\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\infty})=H^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),\quad V_{c,\infty}({\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\infty})={H}^{s}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}):=\{u\in H^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\colon u=0\ \;\forall\,{\bm{x}}\in{{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}}\}

Remark 4.9
.

We consider the case that
{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}={{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}
. For
s>1/2
, then
{H}^{s}_{c}({\Omega})
coincides with the space
H_{0}^{s}({\Omega})
that is the closure of
C_{0}^{\infty}({\Omega})
with respect to the
H^{s}({\Omega})
-norm, whereas for
s<1/2
,
{H}^{s}_{c}({\Omega})
is isomorphic to
H^{s}({\Omega})
(i.e., functions in

H^{s}({\Omega})
with zero extension to
{\Omega}_{c}
).
In the critical case
s=1/2
,
{H}^{s}_{c}({\Omega})\subsetneq H^{s}_{0}({\Omega})
. See e.g.
[
50
, Chapter 3]
for a detailed discussion.

Let us first consider an example of a pure Dirichlet type problem.
For
{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}={{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}={{\Omega}_{c}}
, the following weak formulation has been given in
[
3
]
:

Find
u({\bm{x}})\in{H}^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
such that
u({\bm{x}})=g_{D}^{\infty}({\bm{x}})
for all
{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}_{c}}
and

{\mathcal{A}}_{s}(u,v)={(f,v)_{\Omega}}{+}(g_{D}^{\infty},v)_{{{\Omega},{{\Omega}_{c}}}}\quad\text{{for all} }v\in{H}^{s}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),

(76)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{s}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\setminus({{\Omega}_{c}}\times{{\Omega}_{c}})}\;(u({{\bm{y}}})-u({{\bm{x}}}))(v({{\bm{y}}})-v({{\bm{x}}})){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{{\Omega}}\int_{{\Omega}}\;(u({{\bm{y}}})-u({{\bm{x}}}))(v({{\bm{y}}})-v({{\bm{x}}})){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{{\Omega},{\Omega}}(u,v)}

\displaystyle\quad\,+\underbrace{\int_{{\Omega}}u({{\bm{x}}})v({{\bm{x}}})\int_{{{{\Omega}_{c}}}}{{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}}_{{\mathcal{A}}_{{\Omega},{{\Omega}_{c}}}(u,v)}

(77)

and

(g_{D}^{\infty},v)_{{\Omega},{{\Omega}_{c}}}=\int_{\Omega}v({\bm{x}})\int_{{\Omega}_{c}}g_{D}^{\infty}({\bm{y}}){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}.

(78)

It is easy to see that due to the Dirichlet constraint on
{{\Omega}^{D}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}={{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\infty}}}={{\Omega}_{c}}
,
u({\bm{x}})=g_{D}^{\infty}({\bm{x}})
and
v({\bm{x}})=0
for
{\bm{x}}\in{{\Omega}_{c}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle(g_{D}^{\infty},v)_{{\Omega},{{\Omega}_{c}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}v({\bm{x}})\int_{{\Omega}_{c}}g_{D}^{\infty}({\bm{y}}){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}v({\bm{x}})\int_{{\Omega}_{c}}u({\bm{y}}){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}

Meanwhile, assuming that Fubini’s theorem holds, then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{{\Omega},{{\Omega}_{c}}}(u,v)-(g_{D}^{\infty},v)_{{\Omega},{{\Omega}_{c}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{{\Omega}_{c}}v({\bm{x}})(u({\bm{x}})-u({\bm{y}})){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{{\Omega}_{c}}(v({\bm{x}})-v({\bm{y}}))(u({\bm{x}})-u({\bm{y}})){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\Omega}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}(v({\bm{x}})-v({\bm{y}}))(u({\bm{x}})-u({\bm{y}})){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{{\Omega}_{c}}(v({\bm{x}})-v({\bm{y}}))(u({\bm{x}})-u({\bm{y}})){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\Omega}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}(v({\bm{x}})-v({\bm{y}}))(u({\bm{x}})-u({\bm{y}})){{\gamma}_{s}}{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{d{\bm{y}}d{\bm{x}}}

Thus, (
76
) is equivalent to that defined (
61
) for
\delta=\infty
with
{\mathcal{Q}}=0
.

Remark 4.10
.

One may also enforce the volume constraints via Lagrange multipliers, see e.g.
[

]
.

One can derive some rigorous results on the limit of (

) as
\delta\to\infty
for kernels satisfying (

), particularly for the case of pure Dirichlet condition. For example, one can extend the result given
[

]
for the case of

{g_{D}^{\delta}}\equiv 0
and

(homogeneous Dirichlet problem) to inhomogeneous data.

Theorem 4.11
.

For kernels satisfying (
22
),
let
u_{\delta}
and
u_{\infty}
be the weak solutions to the nonlocal and fractional Dirichlet boundary value problems associated with
an element
w\in H^{s}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and

{g_{D}^{\delta}}=w\mid_{{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}}
and

g_{D}^{\infty}=w\mid_{{{\Omega}_{c}}}
respectively. Assume that
\|{\widehat{f}}\|_{V^{\star}_{{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}
is bounded uniformly in
{\delta}
, then we have

\|u_{\delta}-u_{\infty}\|_{H^{s}({\Omega})}\to 0\,,

as
{\delta}\to\infty
.

Indeed, one can get the above conclusion by considering the corresponding problems for

and

with homogeneous Dirichlet data and noticing that

{{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta}}(w,v)\to{\mathcal{A}}_{s}(w,v)

5
Numerical experiments and discussion

In this section, we shall present some numerical examples to show the behaviours of the solution to
the nonlocal model (
12
) with inhomogeneous boundary conditions.
Since the Dirichlet boundary conditions have been intensively studied in existing works,
we focus on the cases for
the pure Neumann or mixed type boundary conditions here.

We consider a one-dimensional nonlocal problem in the unit interval
\Omega=(0,1)

with boundary region
{{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{I}}_{\delta}}}=(-\delta,0)\cup(1,1+\delta)
. Throughout the section,
we choose the kernel functions in the fractional type:

\gamma_{\delta}(x,y)=C_{s,\delta}|x-y|^{-1-2s}\chi_{B_{\delta}(0)}(x-y)

Here the normalization constant
C_{s,\delta}(\delta)
is defined by

C_{s,\delta}=\begin{cases}(2-2s)\delta^{-2+2s},\quad&\text{for}~~\delta\leq\delta_{s},\\
\displaystyle\frac{2^{2s}s\Gamma(s+{{1}/{2}})}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma(1-s)},\quad&\text{for}~~\delta\geq\delta_{s},\end{cases}\quad\text{with}\quad\delta_{s}=\Big(\displaystyle\frac{(2-2s)\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma(1-s)}{2^{2s}s\Gamma(s+1/2)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2-2s}}.

In our computation, we use the standard Galerkin finite element method with uniform mesh size
h=1/M
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
h\ll\delta
and
r=\delta/h
is an integer. Define the grid points
\{x_{r}\}_{i=-r}^{M+r}
such that
x_{r}=rh
, we then define
X_{h}
to be the set of functions in
{V_{c,{\delta}}({{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}})}
(see the definition in (
51
)) which are
linear when restricted to the
subintervals
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
,
i=-r,\ldots,M+r
.
We fix a sufficiently small mesh size
h=10^{-4}
.

Example 1. Local limit: pure Neumann boundary conditions.

To begin with, we examine the nonlocal model (
12
) with the inhomogeneous Neumann boundary conditions. We define the source term

f(x)=x(1-x)
for all
x\in\Omega

and the Neumann type volumetric constraint

g_{N}^{\delta}(x)=\begin{cases}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{6\delta},\quad&x\in(-\delta,0),\\
0,\quad&x\in(1,1+\delta).\end{cases}

Note that the source term
f
and boundary data
g_{N}^{\delta}
satisfy the compatibility condition (
67
), which implies the existence of the weak solution.
We shall test two types of Neumann Boundary conditions that correspond to the bilinear forms (
62
)
and (
69

{{\mathcal{N}}_{\delta}}

(81)

With the nonlocal Neumann boundary condition

find
u_{h}\in X_{h}
such that for all
v_{h}\in X_{h}
,

\begin{split}{{\mathcal{A}}_{\delta,{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}}(u_{h},v_{h})=}\frac{1}{2}\int_{{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}\times{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}}&(v_{h}(y)-v_{h}(x))(u_{h}(y)-u_{h}(x))\gamma_{\delta}(x-y)\,dy\,dx\\
&=\int_{\Omega}f(x)v_{h}(x)\,dx+\int_{\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}}g_{N}^{\delta}v_{h}(x)\,dx.\end{split}

(83)

Both finite dimensional problems (
82
) and (
83
) are well-posed by the Lax–Milgram theorem and the nonlocal version of Poincaré inequality in Lemma

Example 3. Fractional limit.

In this example, we shall numerically check the limit of nonlocal diffusion models as
\delta\rightarrow\infty
. To this end, we
consider the boundary value problem of steady fractional diffusion model

\begin{cases}-{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}u_{\infty}(x)+u_{\infty}=f(x),&\quad\forall\,x\in{\Omega}{,}\\
\mathcal{N}_{\infty}^{s}u_{\infty}(x)=g(x),&\quad\forall\,x\in{{\Omega}_{c}},\end{cases}

(84)

where the fractional operator
{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}
and the Neumann operator
\mathcal{N}_{\infty}^{s}
are defined in (
6
) and (
10
), respectively.
Since the closed form of the fractional model is unavailable, we assume that the solution is a Gaussian function:

u_{\infty}(x)=\exp\big(-(x-{{1}/{2}})^{2}\big)\qquad\forall~~x\in\mathbb{R}.

(85)

Then it is easy to compute the source term
f
and the Neumann boundary data
g
numerically, since
u_{\infty}(x)
decays double exponentially as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
.
In our computation, we evaluate the numerical solution of the following
boundary value problem of the steady nonlocal diffusion problem with a finite horizon:

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u_{\delta}(x)+u_{\delta}=f(x),&\quad\forall\,x\in{\Omega}{,}\\
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}u_{\delta}(x)=g(x),&\quad\forall\,x\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}},\end{cases}

(86)

Note that, by adding lower order terms
u_{\infty}
and
u_{\delta}
in (
84
) and (
86
) respectively, we will not need to be concerned with data incompatibility for the corresponding fractional and nonlocal problems.

In Figure
5.5
, we plot the numerical solutions
u_{\delta}
with various
\delta
and
s
. The numerical results clearly show the convergence of
u_{\delta}
to
u_{\infty}
as
\delta\rightarrow\infty
. Besides, for smaller
s
, we observe a slower convergence, which might be due to the slower decay of the kernel function in the case of smaller
s
.

Similar to Example 1, we also consider the boundary value problem with another type of Neumann boundary condition:

\begin{cases}-{{\mathcal{L}}_{\infty}^{s}}u_{\infty}(x)+u_{\infty}=f(x),&\quad\forall\,x\in{\Omega}{,}\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}^{s}u_{\infty}(x)=\widetilde{g}(x),&\quad\forall\,x\in{{\Omega}_{c}},\end{cases}

(87)

where the fractional Neumann operator is defined by

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}^{s}u(x)=-C_{s,\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u(y)-u(x)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,dy,\quad\quad\forall\,x\in{{{\Omega}_{c}}}.

(88)

Let the solution to (
87
) be of the form (
85
). Then we are able to compute the new Neumann boundary data

\widetilde{g}(x)=\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\infty}^{s}u_{\infty}(x)
.
The corresponding nonlocal model reads

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u_{\delta}(x)+u_{\delta}=f(x),&\quad\forall\,x\in{\Omega}{,}\\
{\mathcal{N}}_{\delta,{{\widehat{{\Omega}}}_{\delta}}}u_{\delta}(x)=\widetilde{g}(x),&\quad\forall\,x\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}},\end{cases}

(89)

Again, our numerical results indicate that the functions
u_{\delta}
converge to
u_{\infty}
as
\delta\rightarrow\infty
, and the convergence rate deteriorates as
s\rightarrow 0
, see Figure
5.5
.
For both cases, we observe that the convergence rate is around
O(\delta^{-2s})
(see Table
3
), which still awaits theoretical justification.

Conclusion

The discussion here offers a glimpse into some of the recent studies of nonlocal models with a finite range of interactions defined on a bounded domain involving various types of nonlocal boundary conditions. In particular, we
provide a consistent presentation of such models to classical PDEs, seen as the local limits, and fractional PDEs, seen as the limit with an infinite range of interactions.
Obviously, there are many more known results in the literature and more interesting and open questions to be considered. For example, while the associated nonlocal boundary conditions are illustrated for problems defined in a bounded domain, the
inhomogeneous data are only prescribed in abstract function spaces. It is interesting to give more precise characterizations of these spaces by applying recently developed trace and extension theorems for the nonlocal function spaces

[
38
,
43
]
.
Also, while we stress the consistent local and fractional limits, one can also further examine how the rates of convergence depend on the model and data in these limits
[
41
,
42
,
23
]
. Moreover, the formulations are
only presented in the context of time-independent scalar equations, one can consider the extensions to nonlinear systems and evolution equations, particularly those connected to peridynamics
[
64
,
40
,
36
,
28
]
.
Another interesting topic, as previously mentioned in the discussion, is on the connection of the nonlocal problems to the stochastic process and their limits, from the local diffusion equation and Brownian motion to fractional diffusion and Lévy jump processes
[
26
,
39
]
.

In addition, one may consider the numerical analysis of approximation methods and discuss, among many practical issues, the issue of asymptotic compatible approximation
[
72
]
. Further numerical experiments can also be carried out to provide more comparisons of the nonlocal models and their various limits.
