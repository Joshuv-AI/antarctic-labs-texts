Abstract
. We prove structural results for measure preserving systems, called Furstenberg systems, naturally associated with bounded multiplicative functions. We show that for all pretentious multiplicative functions these systems always have rational discrete spectrum and, as a consequence, zero entropy. We obtain several other refined structural and spectral results, one consequence of which is that the Archimedean characters are the only pretentious multiplicative functions that have Furstenberg systems with trivial rational spectrum, another is that a pretentious multiplicative function has ergodic Furstenberg systems if and only if it pretends to be a Dirichlet character, and a last one is that for any fixed pretentious multiplicative function all its Furstenberg systems are isomorphic. We also study structural properties of Furstenberg systems of a class of multiplicative functions, introduced by Matomäki, Radziwiłł, and Tao, which lie in the intermediate zone between pretentiousness and strong aperiodicity. In a work of the last two authors and Gomilko, several examples of this class with exotic ergodic behavior were identified, and here we complement this study and discover some new unexpected phenomena. Lastly, we prove that Furstenberg systems of general bounded multiplicative functions have divisible spectrum. When these systems are obtained using logarithmic averages, we show that trivial rational spectrum implies a strong dilation invariance property, called strong stationarity, but, quite surprisingly, this property fails when the systems are obtained using Cesàro averages.

Pretentious multiplicative functions.

We first focus on the class of pretentious multiplicative functions (see Definition
3.1
), which already exhibit interesting structural properties.
The simplest examples are the Dirichlet characters, these are the periodic completely multiplicative functions, their non-zero values are always roots of unity and their Furstenberg systems are rotations on finite cyclic groups. A particular example is given by the sequence

\chi_{3,1}(n):={\bf 1}_{3{\mathbb{Z}}+1}(n)-{\bf 1}_{3{\mathbb{Z}}+2}(n),\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}},

with (a unique) Furstenberg system isomorphic to a rotation on
{\mathbb{Z}}/(3{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Other simple examples of pretentious
completely multiplicative functions can be obtained by assigning the value
-1
to finitely many primes and
1
to the remaining primes. This gives rise to pretentious multiplicative functions that can be approximated in density by periodic sequences, and their Furstenberg systems are ergodic procyclic systems, that is, rotations on inverse limits of cyclic groups.
Consider for example the completely multiplicative function defined by

f(p^{k}(pn+j)):=(-1)^{k},\quad j=1,\ldots,p-1,\,k,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},

where
p
is some fixed prime.
Its (unique) Furstenberg system is isomorphic to an ergodic rotation on the inverse limit of the cyclic groups
{\mathbb{Z}}/(p^{s}{\mathbb{Z}})
,
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Things become more complicated when we assign values different than one on infinitely many primes. If we define the completely multiplicative function by

f(p):=-1,\quad\text{ for }p\in{\mathbb{P}}^{\prime},\text{ such that }\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{p}<+\infty,

and
f(p):=1
on
{\mathbb{P}}\setminus{\mathbb{P}}^{\prime}
, then
f
turns out to always be pretentious, but the structural properties of its Furstenberg systems are less clear.
And things become even more interesting when
f
is allowed to take values on the complex unit disc
{\mathbb{U}}
. For non-zero
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, consider for example the pretentious completely multiplicative function (often called an
Archimedean character
), defined by

f(n):=n^{it},\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Then
f
cannot be approximated in density by periodic sequences, it has uncountably many Furstenberg systems for Cesàro averages but only one for logarithmic averages, and they are all isomorphic to the identity transformation on the circle with the Lebesgue measure.
Another interesting example is given by defining
f
on the primes by (for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we let
e(t):=e^{2\pi it}
)

f(p):=e(1/\log\log{p}),\quad p\in{\mathbb{P}},

which leads to a completely multiplicative function that pretends to be
1
but does not have convergent means.
We can also get mixed behavior, with all previous aspects present, while still maintaining our pretentiousness assumption.
For instance, this is the case when we define the completely multiplicative function on the primes by

f(p):=p^{it}\cdot\chi_{3,1}(p)\cdot e(1/\log\log{p}),\quad p\in{\mathbb{P}}.

For more examples, see Section
3.2.1
.
For general pretentious multiplicative functions, it is not at all clear if their Furstenberg systems always have rational discrete spectrum or even zero entropy, and in fact, examples of multiplicative functions in the MRT class (see Definition
2.3
) seem to indicate otherwise. It is these and related, more refined questions, that we answer in this article.

In Theorems
2.7
,
2.8
we show that
all Furstenberg systems of pretentious multiplicative functions
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
, for Cesàro or logarithmic averages, have rational discrete spectrum, and any two Furstenberg systems of a fixed
f
are isomorphic.

This is not a property shared by general bounded multiplicative functions. For example, every element of the MRT class of multiplicative functions that is studied below, has a wide variety of pairwise non-isomorphic Furstenberg systems, the structure of which differs sharply depending on whether we use Cesàro or logarithmic averages. This is well illustrated in Theorems
2.18
,

,
2.20
.
Furthermore, we show that these systems
are ergodic exactly when
the multiplicative function pretends to be a Dirichlet character, in which case the multiplicative function has good subsequential approximations in the Besicovitch norm by periodic sequences.
Our results complement those in

[
2
,
9
]
, where rational discrete spectrum was established for a restricted class of pretentious multiplicative functions, see Theorem

below. Theorem

, together with Theorem

, enable us in several cases to identify the spectrum of all Furstenberg systems of pretentious multiplicative functions, and we show that

is the only one that has trivial rational spectrum. Lastly, in Theorem

, for completely multiplicative functions that pretend to be Dirichlet characters,
we describe explicitly the spectrum of their Furstenberg systems and characterize them up to isomorphism as rotations on procyclic groups. A rather immediate consequence of the previous results is the

non-obvious fact, that if a sequence satisfies the Sarnak or the Chowla-Elliott conjecture, then so does any of its multiples by a pretentious multiplicative function (see Theorems

and
2.16
). Some other consequences, regarding existence and vanishing of correlations of pretentious multiplicative functions, are given in Theorems
2.13
and

.

MRT multiplicative functions.

We study a class of multiplicative functions that lie in the intermediate zone between pretentiousness and strong aperiodicity. They were introduced in
[
38
, Appendix B]
, in order to give examples of non-pretentious multiplicative functions with non-vanishing
2
-point correlations. They are constructed by imitating the function
n^{it}
on long intervals of consecutive primes, but using different values of
t
as the size of the interval grows (the explicit defining properties are given in Definition
2.3
).
This class was studied in
[
24
]
,
where Furstenberg systems with rather exotic and unexpected behavior were identified.
In Theorem
2.17
, we show that all MRT multiplicative functions are aperiodic (this was known only for a certain range of parameters). In Theorems
2.18
-
2.20
, we give structural results for their Furstenberg systems that complement those in
[
24
]
, covering a wider range of subsequential limits. This enables us to show in Theorem
2.19
that in the case of Cesàro averages,
trivial rational spectrum does not always imply a dilation invariance property known as strong stationarity, contrasting a result for logarithmic averages that holds for all bounded multiplicative functions (see Theorem
2.4
).
Moreover, although unipotent systems feature in our structural results both in the case of Cesàro and logarithmic averages, the exact structure in each case is sharply different - in the first case we show in Theorems
2.18
and
2.19
that we get unipotent systems of fixed level, while in the second case we get mixtures of infinitely many unipotent systems with an unbounded number of levels, see Theorem
2.20
.

In the course of proving the previous results, we also establish some structural properties for general bounded multiplicative functions that are of independent interest.

General bounded multiplicative functions.
We study spectral properties of Furstenberg systems of general multiplicative functions with values on the complex unit disc for Cesàro and logarithmic averages. In
[
17
]
, it was shown that for logarithmic averages, the spectrum of these systems is a subset of the rationals, and here we give some additional information. In Theorem
2.1
we show that for completely multiplicative functions the spectrum is a divisible subset of
{\mathbb{T}}
. These properties are used in order to identify the spectrum of pretentious completely multiplicative functions. Finally, in Theorem
2.4
, we show that if a Furstenberg system for logarithmic averages of a bounded multiplicative function has
trivial rational spectrum, then the system is necessarily strongly stationary (see Definition
3.10
), a property that has very strong structural consequences, some of which are recorded in Corollary
2.5
. As stated before, the MRT class provides examples where this property fails for Furstenberg systems of multiplicative functions defined using Cesàro averages.

2.1.
Spectral results for general completely multiplicative functions

We start with some results about Furstenberg systems
of general completely multiplicative functions.
These results are of independent interest, but they will also be used subsequently to deduce results for pretentious multiplicative functions and to contrast results obtained for certain MRT multiplicative functions.

2.1.1.
Divisibility properties of the spectrum

In this subsection, we discuss divisibility properties of the spectrum (see Definition
3.3
) of Furstenberg systems of bounded completely multiplicative functions. Note that in all cases, we get stronger results when the Furstenberg systems are defined using logarithmic averages versus Cesàro averages, and it is not clear if equally strong results can be obtained for Cesàro averages (see a related question in Section
2.5
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be a Furstenberg system of a completely multiplicative function
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
. Let also
\alpha\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
and
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
f(r)\neq 0
.

(i)

(Logarithmic averages) If the Furstenberg system is defined using logarithmic averages, then

(\alpha+k)/r\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
for some
k\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
.

(ii)

(Cesàro averages) If the Furstenberg system is defined using Cesàro averages, then

(\alpha+k)/r\in\spec(X,\mu_{r},T)
for some
k\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
, where
(X,\mu_{r},T)
is another Furstenberg system of
f
for Cesàro averages.
If
(X,\mu,T)
is ergodic, then we can take
\mu_{r}=\mu
.

Remarks
.

\bullet
The result fails if we assume multiplicativity and not complete multiplicativity.
Take for example
f(n):=(-1)^{n+1}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\alpha=1/2
,
r=2
, or
f:={\bf 1}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus 3{\mathbb{Z}}}-{\bf 1}_{3{\mathbb{Z}}}
, and
\alpha=1/3
,
r=3
.
Also, in the completely multiplicative case, the result fails if we allow
f(r)
to take the value
0
, consider for example the case of Dirichlet characters.

\bullet
We caution the reader that if
\alpha=1/p
,
p\in{\mathbb{P}}
, and
f(r)\neq 0
, then the previous result is not going to give us additional values in the spectrum unless
p
divides
r
, and it will give us additional values if
r=p
(see Corollary
2.3
).

\bullet
If
\mu=\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,\delta_{T^{n}f}
, then in part (
ii
)
we can take
\mu_{r}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[rN_{k}^{\prime}]}\,\delta_{T^{n}f}
for any subsequence
N_{k}^{\prime}
of
N_{k}
for which the previous weak-star limit exists, where we think of
f
as a point in
{\mathbb{U}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Theorem
2.1
is proved in Section
4.1
. The key idea is to study the action of the maps
\tau_{r}
, defined in (
10
), on eigenfunctions of the system, and show that in the case of Furstenberg systems of completely multiplicative functions, non-zero functions are mapped to non-zero functions by
\tau_{r}
. This is a consequence of Lemma
4.2
, which we combine with Lemma

in order to prove Theorem
2.1

Definition 2.1
.

A subset
A
of
{\mathbb{T}}
is called
divisible
if for every
\alpha\in A
and
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists
\alpha^{\prime}\in A
such that
\alpha=r\alpha^{\prime}
.

For example, the sets
\{m/2^{k}\colon m=0,\ldots,2^{k}-1,k\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
and
{\mathbb{Q}}\cap[0,1)
are divisible, and any non-trivial divisible subset of
{\mathbb{T}}
has to be infinite.

The following is an immediate consequence of the previous result.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}\setminus\{0\}
be a completely multiplicative function.

(i)

(Logarithmic averages) The spectrum of any Furstenberg system of
f
for logarithmic averages is a divisible subset of
{\mathbb{T}}
.

(ii)

(Cesàro averages) The combined spectrum of all Furstenberg systems of
f
for Cesàro averages is a divisible subset of
{\mathbb{T}}
. The same property holds
for the spectrum of any fixed Furstenberg system of
f
as long as the system is ergodic.

Remark
.

If
f
is completely multiplicative but is allowed to take the value
0
, then the result fails, consider for example
f
to be a non-trivial Dirichlet character. The result also fails if
f
is not allowed to take the value
0
but is only assumed to be multiplicative, see
the examples given in the first remark after Theorem
2.1
.

Note that if
\alpha=0
or
\alpha=m/n
and
(r,n)=1
, then the content of Theorem
2.1
is empty. On the other hand, if
\alpha=1/p
and
r=p^{s}
for some
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then we do get non-trivial consequences as the next result shows.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be a Furstenberg system of a completely multiplicative function
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
and suppose that
1/p\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
for some
p\in{\mathbb{P}}
with
f(p)\neq 0
.

(i)

(Logarithmic averages) If the Furstenberg system is defined using logarithmic averages,
then
q/p^{s}\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
for every
s\in{\mathbb{N}},q\in\{0,\ldots,p^{s}-1\}
.

(ii)

(Cesàro averages) If the Furstenberg system is defined using Cesàro averages, then

q/p^{s}\in\spec(X,\mu_{p,s},T)
for every
s\in{\mathbb{N}},q\in\{0,\ldots,p^{s}-1\},

where
(X,\mu_{p,s},T)
is another Furstenberg system of
f
for Cesàro averages.
If
(X,\mu,T)
is ergodic, then we can take
\mu_{p,s}=\mu
for every
p\in{\mathbb{P}}
and
s\in{\mathbb{N}}

Remark
.

As a consequence, if the rational spectrum of a Furstenberg system of a
completely multiplicative function
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to\{-1,1\}
is non-trivial, then it is infinitely generated. This is not the case for general multiplicative functions
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to\{-1,1\}
,
see the examples in the first remark after Theorem
2.1
- they are given by non-constant periodic sequences, hence their Furstenberg systems have non-trivial finite spectrum (hence non-divisible).

Proof.

We prove the first part.
Let
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Since
f(p)\neq 0

) of Theorem
2.1

for some

The second part follows by arguing as in the first part and applying part (

2.1.2.
Trivial rational spectrum implies strong stationarity

It turns out that for Furstenberg systems of bounded multiplicative functions defined using logarithmic averages, trivial rational spectrum has very strong structural consequences. A notable one is strong stationarity, the dilation invariance property described in Definition
3.10
, which also played a crucial role in the description of Furstenberg systems of general bounded multiplicative functions in
[
17
]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

If
(X,\mu,T)
is a Furstenberg system for logarithmic averages of a multiplicative function
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
, then the following two conditions are equivalent:

(i)

The system has trivial rational spectrum.

(ii)

The system is strongly stationary.

Remarks
.

\bullet
The implication
\eqref{I:sst1}\implies\eqref{I:sst2}
follows from
[
29
]
and holds for general strongly stationary systems. So the more interesting implication is
\eqref{I:sst2}\implies\eqref{I:sst1}
, and this makes use of the fact that
f
is multiplicative.

\bullet
Quite surprisingly, as the examples of Theorem
2.19
show, the result is no longer true when the Furstenberg systems are defined using Cesàro averages.

Theorem
2.4
is proved in Section
4.2
and uses
the ergodic limit formulas stated in Theorem
4.3
and a recent result about correlations of multiplicative functions of Tao-Teräväinen
[
47
]
stated in Theorem
4.5
.

Using the previous result and known results about the structure of strongly stationary systems (see the Main Theorem in
[
15
]
for part (i) and
[
17
, part (ii) of Proposition 3.12]
for part (ii)), we get the following structural result for Furstenberg systems of bounded multiplicative functions that have trivial rational spectrum.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
be a multiplicative function and
(X,\mu,T)
be a Furstenberg system for logarithmic averages
that has trivial rational spectrum. Then:

(i)

The system
(X,\mu,T)
has trivial spectrum, is strongly stationary, and almost every ergodic component is isomorphic to a direct product of a Bernoulli system and an inverse limit of nilsystems.

(ii)

The system
(X,\mu,T)
is disjoint from all ergodic systems with zero entropy.

The bulk of this result can also be deduced from
[
17
, part (ii) of Proposition 3.12]
and
[
18
, Theorem 1.5]
.

The Liouville function is probably the most noteworthy example of a multiplicative function for which
it is not known whether all its Furstenberg systems have trivial rational spectrum (the Chowla conjecture predicts that this is indeed the case), i.e. it is not known whether any rational in
(0,1)
is on the spectrum
of any of its Furstenberg systems for Cesàro or logarithmic averages.

Structural results for pretentious multiplicative functions

Our goal in this section is to give a detailed description of the Furstenberg systems of pretentious multiplicative functions. See Definition

2.2.1.
A known structural result

We start with a known result that gives substantial information for a rich class of

Theorem 2.6

(
[

9
]
)
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}

\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}}\frac{1}{p}(1-f(p)\cdot\overline{\chi(p)})\quad\text{converges}.

Then

it has a unique Furstenberg system with respect to Cesàro averages (hence also for logarithmic), and this unique system is isomorphic to an ergodic procyclic system.

Remarks
.

\bullet
Note that condition (

(see Definition

see Example (

) in Section

\bullet
The reader will find results that extend various aspects of Theorem

When

2.2.2.
New structural results

Our first main result
applies to all pretentious multiplicative functions and shows that their Furstenberg systems have rational discrete spectrum. It extends Theorem

multiplicative functions that satisfy property (

Theorem 2.7
.

All Furstenberg systems of pretentious multiplicative functions for Cesàro or logarithmic averages have rational
discrete spectrum. As a consequence, they have zero entropy and they do not have irrational spectrum.

Remarks
.

\bullet
The collection of Furstenberg systems of a complex valued pretentious multiplicative function may depend on whether we use Cesàro or logarithmic averages. This is the case, for example, when
f(n)=n^{it}
,
t\neq 0
, see the discussion in the Example (

) of Section

. It is a non-trivial fact though, that when
f
pretends to be a Dirichlet character, its Furstenberg systems for Cesàro and logarithmic averages coincide, see part (

) of Theorem

below.

\bullet
Establishing that all Furstenberg systems of pretentious multiplicative functions have zero entropy is a non-trivial task on its own.
In fact, prior to our work it seemed plausible that some MRT functions (see Definition
2.3
) were pretentious, in which case we would get using
[
24
, Corollary 2.10]
,
that some pretentious multiplicative function has a Bernoulli Furstenberg system, hence of positive entropy.

\bullet
It seems likely that this result can also be obtained by studying the formulas for the
2
-point correlations given by Klurman in
[
32
]
. We opted to take a different approach
in order to also get the more refined properties stated in Theorem
2.8
.

\bullet

It is a rather straightforward consequence of results of Klurman in
[
32
]
, that if
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
is a non-trivial aperiodic multiplicative function, then some Furstenberg system of

for logarithmic averages has a Lebesgue component (see Corollary

in the appendix), and as a consequence, does not have rational discrete spectrum. Hence, a multiplicative function that takes values in
{\mathbb{U}}
is pretentious if and only if
all its Furstenberg systems for logarithmic averages have rational discrete spectrum. It follows from
[
34
, Theorem 1.2]
that a similar equivalence also holds for Cesàro averages.

Our main result for pretentious multiplicative functions is stated next and gives more refined structural information about their Furstenberg systems. In what follows, when we write
f\sim g
we mean
{\mathbb{D}}(f,g)<+\infty
(see Definition

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
be a pretentious multiplicative function and
(X,\mu,T)
be a
Furstenberg system of

for Cesàro or logarithmic averages.

(i)

If
f\sim\chi
for some primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
, then

(X,\mu,T)
is an ergodic procyclic system and it is non-trivial if
f\neq 1
.

(ii)

If
f\sim n^{it}\cdot\chi
for some

(X,\mu,T)

is a non-ergodic system with rational discrete spectrum and its spectrum is non-trivial unless
f(n)=n^{it}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, for some
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Furthermore,

(X,\mu,T)
is isomorphic to the direct product of the system
({\mathbb{T}},m_{\mathbb{T}},\id)

and some Furstenberg system of
\tilde{f}:=f\cdot n^{-it}\sim\chi
.

(iii)

In cases (
i
) and (
ii
), any two Furstenberg systems of
f
for Cesàro or logarithmic averages are isomorphic.

(iv)

In case (
i
), a Furstenberg system of
f
for Cesàro averages along
(N_{k})
is well defined if and only if it is well defined for logarithmic averages, and the two Furstenberg systems are equal (i.e. the corresponding

(v)

In case (

), every sequence

has a subsequence
(N_{k}^{\prime})
along which

is Besicovitch rationally almost periodic for Cesàro and logarithmic averages. In case (

along which
f
is Besicovitch rationally almost periodic for Cesàro or logarithmic averages.

Remarks
.

\bullet
Note that a pretentious multiplicative function belongs to exactly one of the classes treated in cases (

) and (
ii
).

\bullet
See Proposition

for additional information regarding a variant of part (

Section

We deduce from Theorem

(ii)

Our second corollary concerns multiplicative functions that are equal to Archimedean characters.

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}

for some

(ii)

At least one Furstenberg system of

(iii)

2.2.3.
Spectral results

We give a result that in conjunction with Theorem
2.1
helps us identify the spectrum of Furstenberg systems of pretentious multiplicative functions.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
be a multiplicative function that
satisfies
f\sim n^{it}\cdot\chi
for some
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
with conductor
q
, and
(X,\mu,T)
be a Furstenberg system of
f
for Cesàro or logarithmic averages. Then for every

the following properties are equivalent:

(i)

1/p\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
.

(ii)

Either
p\mid q
or
f(p^{s})\neq p^{ist}\cdot\chi(p^{s})
for some
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Remarks
.

\bullet
If
f\sim 1
and
s\geq 2
, then

does not always imply
1/p^{s}\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
. Take for example
f(n):={\bf 1}_{{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus 3{\mathbb{Z}}}-{\bf 1}_{3{\mathbb{Z}}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
f(3^{2})\neq 1
and
1/3^{2}\notin\spec(X,\mu,T)
.

\bullet
If
f
is completely multiplicative,
f\sim 1
, and
f(p)\notin\{0,1\}
, then combining this result with Theorem
2.8
and Theorem
2.1
, we get that
q/p^{s}\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
for every
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

f(p)=1
, then
1/p\not\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
.

The implication
\eqref{I:spectrum1}\implies\eqref{I:spectrum2}
of Theorem
2.11
is proved in Section
7.3

and uses the periodic approximation property of Proposition
5.5
, in order to deduce the result for

from its periodic approximants. The implication

\eqref{I:spectrum2}\implies\eqref{I:spectrum1}
is proved in Section
7.4
. It is somewhat more involved and
uses part (

) of Theorem
2.8
and a combination of elementary and ergodic considerations.

Our next result provides more refined spectral information
for multiplicative functions that pretend to be Dirichlet characters and a complete characterization of the spectrum of their Furstenberg systems. We will use it to justify various
claims we make in the examples given in Section
3.2.1
. We assume complete multiplicativity in order to have Theorem
2.1
available for us, and explain in the remarks which consequences carry over to general multiplicative functions.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}\setminus\{0\}
be a completely multiplicative function with
f\sim\chi
for some primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
with conductor
q

and
(X,\mu,T)
be a Furstenberg system for Cesàro or logarithmic averages of
f
. Then

\spec(X,\mu,T)
is equal to the subgroup
\Lambda
of
{\mathbb{T}}
generated by
\{1/p^{s}\colon p\in A,s\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
, where
A:=\{p\in{\mathbb{P}}\colon\text{either }p\mid q\text{ or }f(p)\neq\chi(p)\}
.

Remarks
.

\bullet
Since ergodic discrete spectrum systems are isomorphic if and only if they have the same spectrum, we deduce from part (
i
) of Theorem
2.8
and the previous result, that if
f
is as in the statement, then all Furstenberg systems of
f
are isomorphic to an ergodic rotation on the procyclic group that is the dual group of the subgroup
\Lambda
defined in the statement above.

\bullet
The conclusion fails for non-completely multiplicative functions, even if they take values on
\pm 1
; see the example in the first remark following Theorem
2.11
.
Another example is given by the square of the Möbius function (see Example (
v
) in Section
3.2.1
). The conclusion also fails if
f
is completely multiplicative but we allow it to take the value
0
, consider for example
f:={\bf 1}_{2{\mathbb{Z}}+1}
, which has
1/2
but not
1/4
on the spectrum of its Furstenberg system.

\bullet
See the claim in Section
7.5
for a variant of the inclusion
\spec(X,\mu,T)\subset\Lambda
, which holds for all multiplicative functions that pretend to be Dirichlet characters. Also, our argument gives that even without complete multiplicativity,

\spec(X,\mu,T)
contains the subgroup
\Lambda
of
{\mathbb{T}}
generated by
\{1/p\colon p\in A\}
, where
A:=\{p\in{\mathbb{P}}\colon\text{ either }p\mid q\text{ or }f(p^{s})\neq\chi(p^{s})\text{ for some }s\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
.

\bullet
Using part (
ii
) of Theorem
2.8
and the previous result, we can explicitly identify the structure of the Furstenberg systems of any pretentious completely multiplicative function that avoids the value
0
.

We prove Theorem
2.12
in Section
7.5
.
We essentially use the divisibility properties of the spectrum given in Section
2.1.1
, Proposition

, which asserts that in this case Cesàro and logarithmic averages can be used interchangeably, and
part (
i
) of Theorem

To facilitate discussion, we introduce the following notions and refer the reader to Definition

\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,a(n)\,w(n)=0

2.4.
Structural results about MRT multiplicative functions

We focus here on a class of multiplicative functions defined in
[
38
, Appendix B]
, and which were used to show that the 2-point Elliott conjecture fails for some aperiodic multiplicative functions. It turns out that this class provides a rich playground on which one can construct examples of multiplicative functions with rather exotic statistical behavior, resulting in Furstenberg systems with unexpected structural properties, not to be found within the class of pretentious or strongly aperiodic multiplicative functions (such as those satisfying
[
38
, Equation (1.9)]
).
Our goal here is to give an explicit description of their Furstenberg systems for Cesàro and logarithmic averages
for a wider range of parameters than those dealt in
[
24
]
. As a consequence, we get further unanticipated properties for such Furstenberg systems, only to be found on this class of multiplicative functions with “intermediate randomness” properties.

2.4.1.
The setting

We reproduce here the definition of the MRT class of completely multiplicative functions from

[
24
, Definition 3.1]
.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that the completely multiplicative function
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
belongs to the
MRT class
,
or is an
MRT (multiplicative) function
if the following property is satisfied: There exist strictly increasing sequences of integers
(t_{m})
,
t_{1}:=1
, and
(s_{m})
such that, for each
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the following holds:

(i)

t_{m}<s_{m+1}<s_{m+1}^{2}\leq t_{m+1}
,

(ii)

f(p)=p^{is_{m+1}}
for each prime
p\in(t_{m},t_{m+1}]
,

(iii)

|f(p)-p^{is_{m+1}}|\leq 1/t_{m}^{2}
for each prime
1<p\leq t_{m}
.

It is shown in
[
38
, Appendix B]
that the MRT class is non-empty, and if
s_{m+1}>e^{t_{m}}
, then
f
is an aperiodic multiplicative function. But this is not a growth assumption that we impose in our defining axioms, nor is it known whether it follows from these axioms.
On the other hand, it is shown in
[
24
]
that the axioms (
i
)-(
iii
) imply that, for every
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
s_{m+1}/t^{K}_{m}\to\infty
.

It is a consequence of
[
24
]
that every element of the MRT class has uncountably many different Furstenberg systems, and depending on the choice of our averaging intervals
[N_{m}]
, we may get substantially different structural properties, ranging from the identity system to the Bernoulli system. Our goal here is to give
a complete classification of these Furstenberg systems for Cesàro and logarithmic averages, when roughly speaking,
N_{m}
grows as a fractional power of
s_{m+1}
(i.e.
\lim_{m\to\infty}N_{m}/s_{m+1}^{\beta}=c
for some
\beta,c>0
).

We stress that in the structural results that we give below, we fix an arbitrary MRT function
f
and describe the structure of different Furstenberg systems of this fixed
f
for Cesàro or logarithmic averages, depending on the choice of the sequence
N_{m}\to\infty
that defines the Furstenberg system.

The following is a first non-trivial consequence of our results.

Theorem 2.17
.

All MRT multiplicative functions are aperiodic (non-pretentious).

This follows at once from Theorem
2.18
(or the structural result obtained in
[
24
]
) and Theorem
2.7
, since for every MRT function not all of its Furstenberg systems for Cesàro averages have rational discrete spectrum.

2.4.2.
Furstenberg systems of MRT functions for Cesàro averages

We first state structural results for Furstenberg systems of MRT functions defined using Cesàro averages. In the next subsection, we cover the case of logarithmic averages, which lead to systems with substantially different structural properties.

Definition 2.4
.

For
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, we consider the measure preserving system

(Y_{d},\nu_{d},S_{d}),

where
Y_{d}:={\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}
,

S_{d}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}):=(x_{0},x_{1}+x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}+x_{d-1}),\quad x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}\in{\mathbb{T}}.

We call it the

.

For

S_{0}(x_{0})=x_{0}
on
{\mathbb{T}}
with
m_{\mathbb{T}}
. For

, we have

S_{1}(x_{0},x_{1})=(x_{0},x_{1}+x_{0})
on
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
with
m_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}
, and so on.
We introduce these systems in order to reproduce in an ergodic setting the correlations of MRT functions given in Proposition
8.4
.

Definition 2.5
.

Given
a,b\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{C}}

a(n)\prec b(n)
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}a(n)/b(n)=0

We start with a result previously obtained in
[

]
when

and

is not an integer (the case

is covered by Theorem

). We use somewhat different techniques to cover the more general case below.

Theorem 2.18
.

For
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, let
(X,\mu_{d},T)
be the Furstenberg system for Cesàro averages of the MRT function
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}

[N_{m}]
that satisfies

Theorem

is proved in Section

.

Our method of proof is somewhat different than the one used in
[

, the additional flexibility of our method enables us to give more refined results and also handle subsequent problems concerning logarithmic averages. We continue with a case that exhibits different structural behavior and gives examples of non strongly stationary systems, a feature not present in the case of logarithmic averages.

Definition 2.6

For

This system helps us reproduce the correlations of MRT functions given in Proposition

and leads to the following result.

Theorem 2.19

For
\alpha>0
and

, let
(X,\mu_{\alpha,d},T)

2.5.
Open problems

We refer the reader to Definition
3.9
for the various ergodic notions associated with bounded sequences throughout this subsection. We also remark that although the conjectures below are stated for Cesàro averages, similar conjectures can be made for logarithmic averages and we find them equally interesting.

In Theorem

We believe that this zero-entropy property characterizes pretentiousness within the class of all bounded multiplicative functions.

Conjecture 1

A non-trivial multiplicative function

Conjecture 2

Let

We also state a conjecture regarding a variant of

3.
Background

In this section we gather some notions and basic facts from ergodic theory and number theory used throughout this article.

3.1.
Dirichlet characters

A
Dirichlet character

\chi
is a periodic completely multiplicative function, and is often thought of as a multiplicative function in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{m}
for some
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In this case,

\chi
takes the value
0
on integers that are not coprime to
m
, and
takes values on
\phi(m)
-roots of unity on all other integers, where
\phi
is the Euler totient function. The indicator function of the integers that are relatively prime to
m
is called the
principal character modulo
m
and is denoted by
\chi_{0,m}
. A Dirichlet character
\chi
mod
m
may be determined by a character
\chi^{\prime}
of strictly smaller modulus
m^{\prime}\mid m
by the formula
\chi=\chi^{\prime}\cdot\chi_{0,m}
. If this is not the case, we say that
\chi
is
primitive
and its period
m
is then denoted by
q
and is called the
conductor of
\chi
. Note that
\chi=1
is the only primitive Dirichlet character with conductor
q=1
and also the only Dirichlet character that is primitive and principal. We remark also that for every Dirichlet character
\chi
mod
m
, there exists a primitive Dirichlet character
\chi^{\prime}
such that
\chi=\chi^{\prime}\cdot\chi_{0,m}
.

3.2.
Pretentious multiplicative functions

Following Granville and Soundararajan
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
, we define the notion of pretentiousness
and a related distance between multiplicative functions.

Definition 3.1
.

If
f,g\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
are multiplicative functions, we define the distance between them as

(5)

{\mathbb{D}}^{2}(f,g):=\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}}\frac{1}{p}\big(1-\Re(f(p)\cdot\overline{g(p)})\big).

We say that:

(i)

f
pretends to be
g
and write
f\sim g
if
{\mathbb{D}}(f,g)<+\infty
.

(ii)

f
is
pretentious
if
f\sim n^{it}\cdot\chi
for some
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
.

(iii)

f
is
aperiodic
(or
non-pretentious
) if it is not pretentious.

(iv)

f
is
trivial
if
\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}\,|f(n)|^{2}=0
(in which case
f
is aperiodic).

In case (
ii
), the real number
t
and the primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
(and hence its conductor
q
) are uniquely determined, meaning, if
n^{it}\cdot\chi\sim n^{it^{\prime}}\cdot\chi^{\prime}
for some
t,t^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and primitive Dirichlet characters
\chi,\chi^{\prime}
, then
t=t^{\prime}
and
\chi=\chi^{\prime}
(see for example
[
36
, Proposition 6.2.3]
).
It is also known that, for every non-zero
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have
n^{it}\not\sim\chi
for every Dirichlet character
\chi
(see for example
[
28
, Corollary 11.4]
).
Note that for every Dirichlet character
\chi
there exists a primitive Dirichlet character
\chi^{\prime}
such that
\chi\sim\chi^{\prime}
. So without loss of generality, in our statements we use primitive Dirichlet characters, which in
some cases offers notational advantages.

It follows from
[
9
, Corollary 1]
that a multiplicative function
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
is aperiodic if and only if
its Cesàro averages on every infinite arithmetic progression are zero, that is,

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}\,f(an+b)=0
for every
a\in{\mathbb{N}},b\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.

It can be shown (see
[
27
]
or
[
28
, Section 4.1]
) that
{\mathbb{D}}
satisfies the triangle inequality

{\mathbb{D}}(f,g)\leq{\mathbb{D}}(f,h)+{\mathbb{D}}(h,g)

for all
f,g,h\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
.
If
f
takes values on the unit circle, then we always have
f\sim f
. In general, it may be that
f\not\sim f
, which happens if and only if
\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}\,|f(n)|^{2}=0
, i.e. if
f
is trivial (for a proof, see for example
[
3
, Lemma 2.9]
).

Also, for all
f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
, we have (see

[
26
, Lemma 3.1]
)

{\mathbb{D}}(f_{1}f_{2},g_{1}g_{2})\leq{\mathbb{D}}(f_{1},g_{1})+{\mathbb{D}}(f_{2},g_{2}).

It follows that if
f_{1}\sim g_{1}
and
f_{2}\sim g_{2}
, then
f_{1}f_{2}\sim g_{1}g_{2}
.

3.2.1.
Some examples

In order to get a sense of the variety of measure preserving systems that arise as Furstenberg systems of pretentious multiplicative functions and their spectral properties, we provide a rather extensive assortment of examples.
The terminology we use is explained in Sections

and

. In all cases, the Furstenberg systems can be taken either for Cesàro or logarithmic averages.
The properties
recorded in Examples (

Finally, we remark that Theorem

(i)

f(2):=-1

(ii)

(iii)

(vi)

with the Lebesgue measure. In the case of logarithmic averages, it has a unique Furstenberg system, isomorphic to the identity transformation on

(vii)

f(p):=e(1/p)

(viii)

3.2.2.
Mean values of multiplicative functions

We will need the following notion.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a sequence
A\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is
slowly varying
if for every
c\in(0,1)
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{n\in[N^{c},N]}|A(n)-A(N)|=0.

For example, the sequence
A(n)=\log\log\log{n}
, defined for
n\geq 3
, is slowly varying,
but the sequence
A(n)=\log\log{n}
, defined for
n\geq 2
, is not slowly varying.

We will use the following result that can be found in the form stated below in
[
11
, Theorems 6.2 and 6.3]
. We will only apply this in the case of pretentious multiplicative functions.

Theorem 3.1

(Delange-Wirsing-Halász)
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
be a multiplicative function.

(i)

If
f\not\sim n^{it}
for every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, or
f\sim n^{it}
for some
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
f(2^{s})=-2^{ist}
for every
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}\,f(n)=0.

(ii)

If
f\sim n^{it}
for some
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
f(2^{s})\neq-2^{ist}
for some
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then there exist a non-zero
L\in{\mathbb{C}}
and a slowly varying sequence
A\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that

{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}\,f(n)=L\,N^{it}e(A(N))+o_{N}(1).

Remarks
.

\bullet
If
f
is completely multiplicative, then
L
is always non-zero since we cannot have
f(2^{s})=-2^{ist}
for every
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

\bullet
Part (
i
) also holds for logarithmic averages, this follows using partial summation. Part (
ii
) fails for logarithmic averages, take for example
f(n)=n^{it}
for some
t\neq 0
, then the logarithmic averages vanish but not the Cesàro averages (more generally, the logarithmic averages vanish if
f\sim n^{it}
for some
t\neq 0
).
On the other hand, if
t=0
(equivalently, if
f\sim 1
), then part (
ii
)
continues to hold
if we replace
{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}\,f(n)
with
\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N]}\,f(n)
.
To see this, note that since
\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{n\in[N^{c},N]}|A(n)-A(N)|=0
for every
c>0
, we can use partial summation to deduce the asserted asymptotic for the averages
\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N^{c},N]}\,f(n)
for all
c>0
. Letting
c\to 0^{+}
,
gives the result for the averages
\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N]}\,f(n)
.

3.3.
Measure preserving systems

Throughout the article, we make the typical assumption that all probability spaces
(X,{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
considered are
standard Borel
, meaning,
X
can be endowed with the structure of a complete and separable metric space and
{\mathcal{X}}
is its Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
A
measure preserving system
, or simply
a system
, is a quadruple
(X,{\mathcal{X}},\mu,T)

where
(X,{\mathcal{X}},\mu)
is a probability space and
T\colon X\to X
is an invertible, measurable, measure preserving transformation.
In general, we omit the
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{X}}
and write
(X,\mu,T)
.
The system is
ergodic
if the only
T
-invariant sets in
{\mathcal{X}}
have measure
0
or
1
. If
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, with
T^{n}f
we denote the composition
f\circ T^{n}
, where for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we let
T^{n}:=T\circ\cdots\circ T
(
n
times),
T^{-n}=(T^{-1})^{n}
, and
T^{0}=\id
.

Factors and isomorphisms

A
homomorphism
, also called a
factor map
, from a system
(X,{\mathcal{X}},\mu,T)
onto a
system
(Y,{\mathcal{Y}},\nu,S)
is a measurable map
\Phi\colon X\to Y
,
such that

\mu\circ\Phi^{-1}=\nu
and with
S\circ\Phi=\Phi\circ T
valid for
\mu
almost every
x\in X
.
When we have such a homomorphism, we say that the system
(Y,{\mathcal{Y}},\nu,S)
is a
factor
of the system
(X,{\mathcal{X}},\mu,T)
. If
the factor map
\Phi\colon X\to Y
is injective on a
T
-invariant set of full
\mu
-measure,
we say that
\Phi
is an
isomorphism
and that
the systems
(X,{\mathcal{X}},\mu,T)
and
(Y,{\mathcal{Y}},\nu,S)

are
isomorphic
.

The
pushforward of
\mu
by
\Phi
, is denoted by
\Phi_{*}\mu
or
\mu\circ\Phi^{-1}
, and is defined by

\int F\,d(\Phi_{*}\mu)=\int F\circ\Phi\,d\mu

for every
F\in L^{\infty}(\nu)
.

Spectrum, procyclic, and rational discrete spectrum systems

The notion of the spectrum and the class of systems with rational discrete spectrum play a crucial role in this article and we define these concepts next.

Definition 3.3
.

Given a system
(X,\mu,T)
, we define its
spectrum
, and denote it by
\spec(X,\mu,T)
, to be the set of

\alpha\in[0,1)
for which there exists a non-zero
g\in L^{2}(\mu)
such that
Tg=e(\alpha)\cdot g
.
We call any such
g
an
eigenfunction of the system
.

Hence,
\alpha\in\spec(X,\mu,T)

Definition 3.4

Furstenberg systems of sequences

\bullet

3.5.
Besicovitch rationally almost periodic sequences

We will use the following variant of the classical notion of Besicovitch rational almost periodicity.

Definition 3.11
.

Let

[

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}|a(n)-a_{\varepsilon}(n)|^{2}\leq\varepsilon.

\limsup_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N_{k}]}
with

\limsup_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}

It is easy to verify that if a sequence is Besicovitch rationally almost periodic for Cesàro (or logarithmic) averages, then it has a mean value on every infinite arithmetic progression and its correlations for Cesàro (or logarithmic) averages exist. As
a consequence, it has a unique Furstenberg system for Cesàro (or logarithmic) averages.

Theorem 3.2

(i)

has a unique Furstenberg system

for Cesàro averages along
(N_{k})
.

(ii)

The system

is an ergodic procyclic system.

Furthermore, a similar statement holds for logarithmic averages.

[

is given only for Cesàro averages, but exactly the same argument also works for logarithmic averages.

4.1.
Divisibility property of the spectrum - Proof of Theorem

In this subsection we prove Theorem

Definition 4.1
.

Let

be the shift transformation on the sequence space

(i)

For

(ii)

For

(M_{z}x)(j):=z\cdot x(j),\quad j\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

It satisfies the commutation relation

Lemma 4.1

(Proof of Lemma 2.3 in
[
29
]
)
.

Let
(X,T)
be the sequence space with the shift transformation and
\tau_{r}\colon X\to X
be the dilation map defined in (

\chi\colon X\to{\mathbb{U}}
be a function, such that
for some
\alpha\in[0,1)

\chi(Tx)=e(\alpha)\,\chi(x)
for every

Then

\chi(\tau_{r}x)=\chi_{0}(x)+\cdots+\chi_{r-1}(x),\quad x\in X,

for some
\chi_{0},\ldots,\chi_{r-1}
that are linear combinations of the functions
\chi\circ\tau_{r}\circ T^{j}
,

, and satisfy

\chi_{k}(Tx)=e((\alpha+k)/r)\,\chi_{k}(x),\quad k=0,\ldots,r-1,\,x\in X.

Remark
.

Note that if

is equipped with the product topology and
\chi
is Borel measurable, then also
\chi_{k}
is Borel measurable for
k=0,\ldots,r-1
.

Proof.

For
k=0,\ldots,r-1

\chi_{k}:=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}e(-j(k+\alpha)/r)\,\chi\circ\tau_{r}\circ T^{j}

Then the asserted properties follow by a direct computation using our assumption and the commutation relation (

).
∎

We caution the reader that when we consider a sequence space system
(X,\mu,T)
we may have

(with respect to

\tau_{r}\chi=\tau_{r}\chi_{0}=\cdots=\tau_{r}\chi_{r-1}=0

12

12

For example, let

\chi(x):={\bf 1}_{\{x_{0}\}}(x)-{\bf 1}_{\{x_{1}\}}(x)

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be a Furstenberg system for logarithmic averages of a completely multiplicative function

(M_{f(r)})_{*}\mu\leq r\cdot(\tau_{r})_{*}\mu.

Furthermore, a similar statement holds if a Furstenberg system is defined using Cesàro averages and is ergodic.

Remark
.

For Cesàro averages we get the following more explicit result: If we let

\mu:=\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,\delta_{T^{n}f}
and
\mu_{r}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[rN_{k}]}\,\delta_{T^{n}f}
and we assume that both weak-star limits exist (which can always be arranged by passing to a subsequence of
(N_{k})
), then

(M_{f(r)})_{*}\mu\leq r\cdot(\tau_{r})_{*}\mu_{r}.

Proof.

It suffices to show that for every
F\in C(X)
with
F\geq 0
, we have

(14)

\int M_{f(r)}F\,d\mu\leq r\int\tau_{r}F\,d\mu.

Suppose that the Furstenberg system of

is taken along
(N_{k})
.
Thinking of
f
as the element
(f(k))
of the sequence space
X
, we get that

is the weak-star limit

(15)

\mu=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,\delta_{T^{n}f}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}/r]}\,\delta_{T^{n}f},

where the second identity holds because of the logarithmic averaging.
Note also that for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
M_{z}F,\tau_{r}F\in C(X)
, whenever

It follows from these facts and (

\int M_{f(r)}F\,d\mu=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}/r]}\,(F\circ M_{f(r)})(T^{n}f)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}/r]}\,F(T^{n}M_{f(r)}f).

Since

(16)

(M_{f(r)}f)(n)=f(r)f(n)=f(rn)=(\tau_{r}f)(n).

Hence, using the commutation relation (

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}/r]}\,F(\tau_{r}T^{rn}f)\leq r\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,F(\tau_{r}T^{n}f)=r\int\tau_{r}F\,d\mu,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}/r]}\,a(rn)\leq r\,\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,a(n),

which is valid for every sequence

Suppose now that the Furstenberg system

\mu=\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N_{k}/r]}\,\delta_{T^{n}f}.

Assuming the claim, we use (

In order to prove the claim, after passing to a subsequence, we can assume that the second limit also exists (if the identity fails, then it would also fail on a subsequence along which the second weak-star limits exist). Let

\mu^{\prime}
be the measure by this limit (the limit on the right hand side in (

5.
Preliminary results for pretentious multiplicative functions

The main goal of this section is to do some preparatory work that will be used in the next two sections to prove our main structural results for Furstenberg systems of pretentious multiplicative functions. The main ingredient needed for later use is Proposition
5.5
, which establishes a key subsequential Besicovitch rational almost periodicity property for all multiplicative functions that pretend to be Dirichlet characters. This fact is proved via the decomposition result of Lemma
5.4
, which in turn follows from the estimate in Lemma
5.3
that we prove in the next subsection.

5.1.
Preliminary estimates

Our main goal in this subsection is to establish the estimate in Lemma
5.3
, which we were not able to find in the form needed in the literature. See though
[
32
, Proposition 2.3]
and
[
33
, Lemma 2.5]

for closely related concentration inequalities.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
be a multiplicative function such that
f\sim 1
.
For every
p\in{\mathbb{P}}
we can write
f(p)=r_{p}\,e(\theta_{p})
for some
r_{p}\in[0,1]
and
\theta_{p}\in[-1/2,1/2)
.
Then

\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}}\frac{1-r_{p}}{p}<+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}}\frac{\theta_{p}^{2}}{p}<+\infty.

Proof.

Since
f\sim 1
, we have

\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}}\frac{1-r_{p}\cos(\theta_{p})}{p}<+\infty.

From this we get immediately that the first series converges. If we use this fact and add and subtract
\cos(\theta_{p})
into the numerator of the last series, we deduce that the series

\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}}\frac{1-\cos(\theta_{p})}{p}
converges, from which the convergence of the second series readily follows (we crucially use here that
\theta_{p}
is in
[-1/2,1/2)
and not in
[0,1)
).
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a multiplicative function such that for every
p\in{\mathbb{P}}
we have
f(p^{s}):=e(\theta_{p})
for some
\theta_{p}\in[-1/2,1/2)
and all
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let

(21)

A(N):=\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N]}\frac{\theta_{p}}{p}.

Then, for some universal constant
C>0
, we have

(22)

{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}|f(n)-e(A(N))|^{2}\leq C\,\Big(\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N]}\frac{\theta_{p}^{2}}{p}+\frac{\log\log{N}}{\log{N}}\Big).

Proof.

For convenience, we use probabilistic language. The reader can translate the argument to conventional number theoretic language by replacing
X_{p}
with
\theta_{p}\cdot{\bf 1}_{p{\mathbb{Z}}}

and
{\mathbb{E}}_{N}
with
{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}
throughout.

For a fixed
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we consider the finite probability space that consists of the interval
[N]
together with the probability measure that assigns mass
1/N
to each point in
[N]
. For a prime
p\in[N]
, we define the random variable

X_{p}\colon[N]\to\{0,\theta_{p}\}
by

X_{p}(n):=\begin{cases}\theta_{p}\quad&\text{if }\,p\mid n,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

These random variables are relevant for our problem because if we let

S_{N}:=\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N]}\,X_{p},

then from the definition of
f
on powers of primes and its multiplicativity, we have

f(n)=e(S_{N}(n)),\quad n\in[N].

Note that if

a(N):={\mathbb{E}}_{N}(S_{N}),

then

(23)

{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}|f(n)-e(a(N))|^{2}\leq 4\pi^{2}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}|S_{N}(n)-a(N)|^{2}=4\pi^{2}\cdot\operatorname{Var}_{N}(S_{N}),

where to get the first estimate we used that
|e(x)-e(y)|=2\pi\big|\int_{x}^{y}e(t)\,dt\big|\leq 2\pi|x-y|
.

So, our problem reduces to getting an upper bound for
\operatorname{Var}_{N}(S_{N})
.
We start with some easy computations that give:

(24)

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(X_{p})=\frac{\theta_{p}}{N}\,\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{p}\Big\rfloor,

\operatorname{Var}_{N}(X_{p})={\mathbb{E}}_{N}(X_{p}^{2})-({\mathbb{E}}_{N}(X_{p}))^{2}\leq\frac{\theta_{p}^{2}}{p},

\operatorname{Cov}_{N}(X_{p},X_{q})={\mathbb{E}}_{N}(X_{p}\cdot X_{q})-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(X_{p})\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(X_{q})=\frac{\theta_{p}\,\theta_{q}}{N}\left(\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{pq}\Big\rfloor-\frac{1}{N}\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{p}\Big\rfloor\cdot\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{q}\Big\rfloor\right).

To get the last equality we assume that
p\neq q
and uses that
{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(X_{p}\cdot X_{q})=\frac{\theta_{p}\,\theta_{q}}{N}\,\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{pq}\Big\rfloor
, which follows from
the primality of
p,q
.

Now let us assume first that all
\theta_{p}
’s have the same sign. Then using the inequality

\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{pq}\Big\rfloor-\frac{1}{N}\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{p}\Big\rfloor\cdot\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{q}\Big\rfloor\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q},

we get for all
p\neq q

\operatorname{Cov}_{N}(X_{p},X_{q})\leq\frac{\theta_{p}\,\theta_{q}}{N}\Big(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}\Big).

Using these estimates and the identity

\operatorname{Var}_{N}(S_{N})=\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N]}\operatorname{Var}_{N}(X_{p})+\sum_{p,q\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N],p\neq q}\operatorname{Cov}_{N}(X_{p}\cdot X_{q}),

we deduce that

\operatorname{Var}_{N}(S_{N})\leq\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N]}\frac{\theta_{p}^{2}}{p}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p,q\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N],p\neq q}\theta_{p}\,\theta_{q}\Big(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}\Big).

Since
\theta_{p}\in[-1/2,1/2)
,
p\in{\mathbb{P}}
, we can bound the second sum by
\sum_{p,q\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N],p\neq q}\frac{1}{p}
, which in turn is bounded by
C_{1}\,\frac{N}{\log{N}}\,\log\log{N}
for some universal constant
C_{1}
. Hence,

(25)

\operatorname{Var}_{N}(S_{N})\leq\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N]}\frac{\theta_{p}^{2}}{p}+C_{1}\,\frac{\log\log{N}}{\log{N}}.

The bound (
22
), with
a(N)
in place of
A(N)
, then follows by combining (
23
) and (
25
).
To get (
22
), as stated, note that by (
24
)

a(N)=\sum_{p\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N]}\frac{\theta_{p}}{N}\,\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{p}\Big\rfloor

and

|a(N)-A(N)|\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\log{N}}

for some universal constant
C_{2}>0
.

This completes the proof in the case where all the
\theta_{p}
’s have the same sign.

In the general case, we can decompose
f
as a product
f_{+}\cdot f_{-}
, where
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
are multiplicative functions defined by

f_{+}(p^{s}):=\begin{cases}e(\theta_{p}),\quad&\text{if }\theta_{p}\geq 0\\
1,\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad f_{-}(p^{s}):=\begin{cases}e(\theta_{p}),\quad&\text{if }\theta_{p}<0\\
1,\quad&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for every
p\in{\mathbb{P}}
and every
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Now, let

A_{+}(N):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N],\\
\theta_{p}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\theta_{p}}{p}\quad\text{ and }\quad A_{-}(N):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in{\mathbb{P}}\cap[N],\\
\theta_{p}<0\end{subarray}}\frac{\theta_{p}}{p}.

Then, we have for all
n\in[N]

\displaystyle|f(n)-e(A(N))|

\displaystyle=|f_{+}(n)\cdot f_{-}(n)-e(A_{+}(N))\cdot e(A_{-}(N))|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{+}(n)-e(A_{+}(N))|+|f_{-}(n)-e(A_{-}(N))|.

It follows that

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}|f(n)-e(A(N))|^{2}\leq 2\cdot\left({\mathbb{E}}_{N}|f_{+}(n)-e(A_{+}(N))|^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}_{N}|f_{-}(n)-e(A_{-}(N))|^{2}\right),

and we get the conclusion by applying the preceding analysis to
f_{+}
and
f_{-}
.
∎

The next estimate will be key for us.

Lemma 5.3

Let

Remark
.

Recall that, by Lemma

We first work with Cesàro averages. In this case, the estimate follows immediately from Lemma

6.
Structural results for pretentious multiplicative functions

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
2.8
, which is the main structural result for Furstenberg systems of pretentious multiplicative functions given in Section
2.2.2
.
In Section
6.1
, we do some preparatory work that enables us for multiplicative functions that pretend to be Dirichlet characters to work interchangeably with Furstenberg systems defined using Cesàro or logarithmic averages. In Section

, we show that certain correlation limits of multiplicative functions that pretend to be Dirichlet characters exist, and conclude that all Furstenberg systems of such multiplicative functions for Cesáro or logarithmic averages are isomorphic (a property that we will later extend to all pretentious multiplicative functions). In Section

6.1.

The goal of this subsection is to show in Proposition

7.1.
Necessary conditions for spectrum

The next result gives a necessary condition that often helps us decide when a certain number belongs to the spectrum of some Furstenberg system of a bounded sequence.
Furthermore, it gives an identity that we will use to prove Theorem
2.13
when we happen to know that this number is not in the spectrum.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be the Furstenberg system for Cesàro averages of a sequence
a\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
taken along a sequence

(N_{k})
. If
\alpha\in(0,1)
is such that
\alpha\not\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
, then

(47)

\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,e(-n\alpha)\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}a_{j}(n+n_{j})=0

for all
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}\in\{a,\overline{a}\}
.
A similar statement also holds when we consider logarithmic averages.

Proof.

Arguing by contradiction, suppose that the conclusion fails for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}\in\{a,\overline{a}\}
. Using van der Corput’s lemma (the variant needed follows from
[
35
, Lemma 3.1]
), we get

\lim_{H\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{h\in[H]}\,e(-h\alpha)\Big(\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}a_{j}(n+n_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\overline{a_{j}}(n+h+n_{j})\Big)\neq 0.

(Note that all the limits as
k\to\infty
exist because
a
is assumed to admit correlations along
(N_{k})
.)
Using (
8
) to translate this equation to the Furstenberg system, we get that

\lim_{H\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{h\in[H]}\,e(-h\alpha)\int F\cdot T^{h}\overline{F}\,d\mu\neq 0,

where
F:=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}T^{n_{j}}F_{j}
for some
F_{j}\in\{F_{0},\overline{F_{0}}\}
and
F_{0}
is the
0^{\text{th}}
-coordinate projection. This implies that
\alpha
is on the spectrum of
(X,\mu,T)
, in fact,

G:=\lim_{H\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{h\in[H]}\,e(-h\alpha)\,T^{h}\overline{F},

where the limit is taken in
L^{2}(\mu)
, is a non-zero
e(\alpha)
-eigenfunction. This contradicts our assumption
\alpha\not\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
and completes the proof.

Alternatively, one can argue that if the conclusion fails, then we get a nontrivial joining of the system with the rotation by

Now, if the limit in (

.

∎

The following simpler condition often suffices to deduce that a certain rational number belongs to the spectrum of a Furstenberg system of a bounded sequence (we caution the reader though, that the condition is far from necessary).

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be the Furstenberg system for Cesàro averages of a sequence

taken along

belongs to the spectrum of

for some

\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}

Our assumption is equivalent to

(48)

\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,(1-q\,{\bf 1}_{q{\mathbb{N}}+r}(n))\,a(n)\neq 0.

Upon substituting the identity

in (

(we only need to appeal to the

is in the spectrum of the system.
∎

7.2.
Preparation for the proof of Theorem

. Then, for

, there exist
a_{j},b_{j}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
a_{j}/b_{j}\in G_{j}
,
(a_{j},b_{j})=1
, and such that

1/p=a_{1}/b_{1}+a_{2}/b_{2}.

Since
1/p
does not belong to the subgroup
G_{1}
and we also have

a_{1}/b_{1}\in G_{1}

and
(a_{1},b_{1})=1
, we easily deduce that
p
does not divide
b_{1}
. Similarly, we get that
p
does not divide
b_{2}
. On the other hand, we have
b_{1}b_{2}=p(a_{1}b_{2}+a_{2}b_{1})
, hence
p
divides either
b_{1}
or
b_{2}
,
a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

7.3.
Proof of the implication
\eqref{I:spectrum1}\implies\eqref{I:spectrum2}
of Theorem

By Proposition

, Furstenberg systems for Cesàro and logarithmic averages coincide, so we only explain the argument for Cesàro averages. Suppose that the Furstenberg system

is taken along the sequence

.

Before proceeding further, let us make the following simple observation: Suppose that

7.4.
Proof of the implication
\eqref{I:spectrum2}\implies\eqref{I:spectrum1}
of Theorem
2.11

We assume that
f\sim\chi
for some primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
with conductor
q
and we have either
p\nmid q
or

f(p^{s})\neq\chi(p^{s})
for some

and

. Our goal is to show that
1/p\in\spec(X,\mu,T)
.
We do this in three steps. The bulk of the proof is contained on the first step, where we work under the additional hypothesis
f\sim 1
and
f(2^{s_{0}})\neq-1
for some

f\sim 1
and

7.4.1.
The case
f\sim 1
and
f(2^{s_{0}})\neq-1
for some
s_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}

We assume that

f\sim 1
and
f(2^{s_{0}})\neq-1
for some
s_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, this is in addition to our hypothesis that

f(p^{s})\neq 1
for some
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Note first that since
f\sim 1
, Proposition
6.2
implies that the Furstenberg systems of
f
for Cesàro and logarithmic averages coincide. So, we only have to treat the case of logarithmic averages, and this turns out to offer a substantial advantage since
it enables us to replace
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}/p]}
with

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}
throughout.

So, let
(X,\mu,T)
be a Furstenberg system of
f
for logarithmic averages
taken along the sequence
N_{k}\to\infty
.
Note that then the limit

L:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,f(n)
exists and since
f\sim 1
and
f(2^{s_{0}})\neq-1
for some
s_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we get by Theorem
3.1
that
L\neq 0
(see the remarks following the theorem).

Arguing by contradiction, suppose that
1/p\notin\spec(X,\mu,T)
. By Corollary
7.2
, this implies

(58)

L=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,f(pn+j)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,f(n),\quad\text{for every }\,j\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}.

Using these identities, our goal is to show that
f(p^{s})=1
for every
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
, which would contradict our hypothesis.

For convenience, we use the convention

\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,a(n):=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,a(n)

whenever the limit exists.
In what follows we will use the following basic property of logarithmic averages:
If
a\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
is such that the limit
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{k}]}\,a(n)
exists,
then we also have that the limit
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[pN_{k}]}\,a(n)
also exists and
the two limits are equal. Using this fact to justify the first identity below and
(
58
) to justify the second, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(pn)+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(pn+j)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(pn)+L\cdot\frac{p-1}{p}.

Repeating this process one more time in order to evaluate

\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(pn)
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(pn)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(p^{2}n)+\frac{1}{p^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(p(pn+j))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(p^{2}n)+L\cdot\frac{p-1}{p}\cdot\frac{f(p)}{p},

where to get the second identity we used that
f(p(pn+j))=f(p)\,f(pn+j)
for
j=1,\ldots,p-1
and (
58
).
Combining these two identities gives

\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(n)=\frac{1}{p^{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(p^{2}n)+L\cdot\frac{p-1}{p}\Big(1+\frac{f(p)}{p}\Big).

Repeating this process
M-2
more times, we deduce

{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(n)=\frac{1}{p^{M}}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(p^{M}n)+L\cdot\frac{p-1}{p}\,\sum_{s=0}^{M-1}\frac{f(p^{s})}{p^{s}}.

Letting
M\to\infty
gives

L={\mathbb{E}}_{n\in{\mathbf{N}}}\,f(n)=L\cdot\frac{p-1}{p}\cdot\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{f(p^{s})}{p^{s}}.

Since
L\neq 0
, we deduce that

(59)

\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{f(p^{s})}{p^{s}}=\frac{1}{p-1}.

Since
\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p^{s}}=\frac{1}{p-1}
and
\Re(f(p^{s}))\in[-1,1]
, comparing real parts in (
59
) we get
\Re(f(p^{s}))=1
for every
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Since
f(p^{s})\in{\mathbb{U}}
, we deduce that
f(p^{s})=1
for every
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
, completing the proof in this first case.

We assume that

7.5.
Proof of Theorem
2.12

Since
f\sim\chi

for some primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
with conductor
q
, Proposition
6.2
implies that Furstenberg systems of
f
for Cesàro and logarithmic averages coincide. We
are going to work with logarithmic averages in order to have

i
) of Corollary
2.3
available to us.

The inclusion
\spec(X,\mu,T)\subset\Lambda
follows from the following stronger fact that applies to general multiplicative functions that pretend to be Dirichlet characters.

Claim.

Let
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{U}}
be a multiplicative function such that
f\sim\chi
for some primitive Dirichlet character
\chi
with conductor

. If

and
\Lambda
is the subgroup generated by
\{1/p^{s}\colon p\in A,s\in{\mathbb{N}}\}

then
\spec(X,\mu,T)\subset\Lambda
.

Let us see how we prove the claim. Since by the first part

(X,\mu,T)
is an ergodic procyclic system,
\spec(X,\mu,T)
is a subgroup of the rationals in
{\mathbb{T}}
.
Any such subgroup is generated by elements of the form
1/p^{s}
for
p\in{\mathbb{P}}
and
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
(see, for example,
[
43
]
). By Theorem
2.11
, if
p\not\in A
, then

be the
0^{\text{th}}
-coordinate projection and
F\colon{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be as in Lemma
8.6
.
Combining Proposition
8.2
with Lemma
8.6
, we deduce that

\lim_{m\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[\alpha s_{m+1}^{1/d}]}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}f^{k_{j}}(n+n_{j})=\int\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}S_{\alpha,d}^{n_{j}}F^{k_{j}}\,dm_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}}.

Hence,

(65)

\int\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}T^{n_{j}}F_{0}^{k_{j}}\,d\mu_{\alpha,d}=\int\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}S_{\alpha,d}^{n_{j}}F^{k_{j}}\,dm_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}}

holds for every
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell},n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Next, we define the map
\Phi\colon{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}\to X
by

\Phi(y):=(F(S_{\alpha,d}^{n}y))_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}},\quad y\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}.

We clearly have
\Phi\circ S_{\alpha,d}=T\circ\Phi
. Moreover, a direct computation shows that the map
\Phi
is injective. Lastly, since
F=F_{0}\circ\Phi
, identity (
65
) implies that

\int\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}T^{n_{j}}F_{0}^{k_{j}}\,d\mu_{\alpha,d}=\int\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}T^{n_{j}}F_{0}^{k_{j}}\,d(m_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}}\circ\Phi^{-1}).

Hence,
\mu_{\alpha,d}=m_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}}\circ\Phi^{-1}
, since a linearly dense subset of
C(X)
has the same integral with respect to the two measures (see second remark after Definition
3.5
). This establishes the asserted isomorphism.

Since the system
({\mathbb{T}}^{d+1},m_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}},S_{\alpha,d})
has trivial rational spectrum,
so does the system
(X,\mu_{\alpha,d},T)
.

To prove that the system
(X,\mu_{\alpha,d},T)
is not strongly stationary, it suffices to show that

\int\overline{F_{0}}\cdot TF_{0}\,d\mu\neq\int\overline{F_{0}}\cdot T^{r}F_{0}\,d\mu

for some
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Equivalently, by applying (
65
) (for
\ell=2
,
n_{1}=0
,
n_{2}=r
,
k_{1}=-1
,
k_{2}=1
) and (
64
), it suffices to show that for fixed
\alpha,d
we have

\int_{0}^{1}e(1/(\alpha^{d}x^{d}))\,dx\neq\int_{0}^{1}e(r/(\alpha^{d}x^{d}))\,dx

for some non-zero
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Direct computation shows that this is the case, for example when
\alpha=d=1
and
r=2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.18
.

We combine Proposition
8.4
with Lemma
8.7
, and argue as in the proof of Theorem
2.19
.
∎

9.
Furstenberg systems of MRT functions - Logarithmic averages

In this section we will prove structural results for Furstenberg systems of MRT multiplicative functions (see Definition
2.3
) when these systems are defined using logarithmic averages. In particular, we will prove Theorem
2.20
. In the case of logarithmic averages
the correlations of MRT functions turn out to be different than those for Cesàro averages, and this leads to substantially different Furstenberg systems.

9.1.
Correlations of MRT functions for logarithmic averages

The main goal of this subsection is to give in Proposition
9.1
an explicit description of the correlations for logarithmic averages of MRT functions when we average over sequences of intervals that grow as fractional powers of
s_{m+1}
, a notion that we define next.
Again, using Lemma
8.1
our starting point is the following identity

(66)

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{m}]}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}f^{k_{j}}(n+n_{j})=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{m}]}\,e\big(s_{m+1}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\log(n+n_{j})\big)\big),

which holds for all
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell},n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
As in the case of Cesàro averages, depending on how we choose
N_{m}
in relation with
s_{m+1}
we get different Furstenberg systems, and the next notion will help us identify which sequences
N_{m}
give rise to which systems.

Definition 9.1
.

We define the
fractional degree
of a sequence of positive real numbers
L_{N}\to+\infty
to be

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log{L_{N}}}{\log{N}}

if the limit exists (could be
+\infty
).

Remarks
.

\bullet
Note that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log{L_{N}}}{\log{N}}=\begin{cases}0,\quad&\text{if }\,L_{N}\prec N^{\varepsilon}\text{ for every }\varepsilon>0\\
c\in(0,+\infty),\quad&\text{if }\,N^{c-\varepsilon}\prec L_{N}\prec N^{c+\varepsilon}\text{ for every }\varepsilon>0\\
+\infty,\quad&\text{if }\,N^{d}\prec L_{N}\text{ for every }d\in{\mathbb{N}}.\end{cases}

\bullet
If
c\in(0,+\infty)
, then the sequence
(N^{c})
has fractional degree
c
.
More generally, the same holds for the sequence
(N^{c}(\log{N})^{b}+L^{\prime}_{N})
for every
b\in{\mathbb{R}}
and every
(L^{\prime}_{N})
with
L^{\prime}_{N}\prec N^{c}
.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
N_{m}:=s_{m+1}^{1/c}
for some
c>0
.
For every
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}
,
n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
let
i_{0}
be the minimum
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}

such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}^{i}\neq 0
and
i_{0}:=+\infty
if no such
i
exists.
Then

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N_{m}]}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}f^{k_{j}}(n+n_{j})=\begin{cases}0,&\quad\text{if }\,0\leq i_{0}\leq c\\
1-c/i_{0},&\quad\text{if }\,c<i_{0}<+\infty\\
1,&\quad\text{if }\,i_{0}=+\infty.\end{cases}

Furthermore, the same identity holds if
(L_{N})
is a sequence of positive real numbers with fractional degree
1/c
and
N_{m}:=L_{s_{m+1}}
.

Remark
.

By taking
c:=1/2
(or any other
c<1
) we get the following
non-vanishing property of the
2
-point correlations of
f

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[s_{m+1}^{2}]}\ \overline{f(n)}\cdot f(n+h)=\frac{1}{2}

for every non-zero
h\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proposition
9.1
is an immediate consequence of identity (
66
) and the next result.

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
(L_{N})
be a sequence of positive real numbers with fractional degree
1/c
for some
c\in(0,+\infty)
.
For every
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell},n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
let
i_{0}
be the minimum
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}

such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}^{i}\neq 0
and
i_{0}:=+\infty
if no such
i
exists.
Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,e\big(N\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\log(n+n_{j})\big)\big)=\begin{cases}0,&\quad\text{if }\,0\leq i_{0}\leq c\\
1-c/i_{0},&\quad\text{if }\,c<i_{0}<+\infty\\
1,&\quad\text{if }\,i_{0}=+\infty.\end{cases}

Remarks
.

\bullet
For example, if
A
is the value of the limit on the left hand side, then for
c\in[1,2)
(in which case
L_{N}
has fractional degree in
(1/2,1]
) we have
A=0
if either
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\neq 0
or
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}\neq 0
(since
i_{0}\leq 1\leq c
); we have
A=1-c/2
if

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}=0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}^{2}\ \neq 0
(since
c<i_{0}=2
), and so on.

\bullet
Note that the limit
A
remains the same if we replace
n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}
with

rn_{1},\ldots,rn_{\ell}
, for every
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
n_{1}\ldots,n_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Hence, the Furstenberg system
of the multiplicative function that has such correlations is strongly stationary. This is unlike the case of Cesàro averages, where for
L_{N}:=N^{1/k}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we got that the corresponding correlations given in Lemma
8.6
were not dilation invariant.

Proof.

In order to have a specific example in mind, in the course of the proof the reader may find it convenient to assume that
L_{N}=N^{1/c}
where
c>0
. We denote by
A
the limit of the averages we want to compute.

We shall use the following basic estimate that follows by applying the Taylor-Lagrange theorem for the function

\log{x}
: For every
a\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
M_{N}>0
, we have

(67)

\Big|\log(n+a)-\log n-\sum_{i=1}^{d}(-1)^{i-1}\frac{a^{i}}{n^{i}}\Big|\leq\frac{C_{a}}{M_{N}^{d+1}}\,\text{ for all }n\geq M_{N}.

The utility of this approximation is that it enables us to connect various exponential sums that appear below to those treated in Corollary
A.6
.

Case 1 (
i_{0}\leq c
).
If
i_{0}\leq c
, we claim that
A=0
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
. We also take
\varepsilon<1
.
We apply (
67
) for
d_{\varepsilon}:=[1/\varepsilon]
,
a:=n_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, and use that

\lim_{N\to\infty}(N/M_{N}^{d_{\varepsilon}+1})=0
for
M_{N}:=N^{\varepsilon}
. We get that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\max_{n\in[N^{\varepsilon},L_{N}]}N\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\log(n+n_{j})-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\,\log{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\varepsilon}}(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}^{i}\,n^{-i}\Big|=0.

Using this and the estimate
\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N]}\,{\bf 1}_{[1,N^{\varepsilon}]}(n)\leq\varepsilon
, we get that for

A_{\varepsilon}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,e\big(N\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\,\log{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\varepsilon}}(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}^{i}\,n^{-i}\big)\big),

we have

|A-A_{\varepsilon}|\leq\varepsilon.

So in order to show that
L=0
, it suffices to show that for every
\varepsilon>0
we have
A_{\varepsilon}=0
.
Equivalently, it suffices to show that (for every
\varepsilon>0
)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,e\big(N\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\,\log{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\varepsilon}}(-1)^{i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}^{i}\,n^{-i}\big)\big)=0.

Recall that the defining property of
i_{0}
implies that

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}^{i}=0
for
i=0,\ldots,i_{0}-1
. Since
c\geq i_{0}
and the fractional degree of
L_{N}
is
1/c
(so positive in particular), we have
N^{\gamma}\prec L_{N}\prec N^{\frac{1}{i_{0}}+\varepsilon}
for some
\gamma>0
and every
\varepsilon>0
. Hence, Corollary
A.6
applies and gives
A=0
.

Case 2
(i_{0}>c)
.
We claim that
A=1-c/i_{0}
if
i_{0}
is finite
and
L=1
if
i_{0}=+\infty
. Suppose first that
i_{0}
is finite.

We decompose

A=A_{1}+A_{2},

where

A_{1}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,{\bf 1}_{[1,N^{1/i_{0}}]}(n)\,e\big(N\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\log(n+n_{j})\big)\big)

and

A_{2}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N^{1/c}]}\,{\bf 1}_{[N^{1/i_{0}},L_{N}]}(n)\,e\big(N\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\log(n+n_{j})\big)\big).

(Note that since
L_{N}
has fractional degree
1/c>1/i_{0}
we have
N^{1/i_{0}}\prec L_{N}
and the logarithmic averages over
[L_{N}]
and
[N^{1/c}]
coincide.)

Case 2a (Computation of
A_{1}
).
We claim that
A_{1}=0
. Indeed, we have

A_{1}=c\cdot i_{0}^{-1}\cdot\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N^{1/i_{0}}]}\,e\big(N\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\log(n+n_{j})\big)\big)

and
by Case 1 the last limit is
0
.

Case 2b (Computation of
A_{2}
).

It remains to show that
A_{2}=1-c/i_{0}
.
For
\varepsilon>0
such that
1/i_{0}+\varepsilon<1/c
, let

(68)

A_{2,\varepsilon}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N^{1/c}]}\,{\bf 1}_{[N^{1/i_{0}+\varepsilon},N^{1/c}]}(n)\,e\big(N\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\log(n+n_{j})\big)\big).

Since
A_{2}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}A_{2,\varepsilon}
, it suffices to compute
A_{2,\varepsilon}
for these values of
\varepsilon>0
.

We apply (
67
) for
d:=i_{0}-1
(which is
\geq 0
since
i_{0}\geq 1
),
a=n_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, and
M_{N}:=N^{1/i_{0}+\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon
small enough so that
M_{N}\prec L_{N}
. Note that since

\lim_{N\to\infty}(N/M_{N}^{d+1})=0
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\max_{n\in[N^{1/i_{0}+\varepsilon},L_{N}]}N\Big|\log(n+a)-\log n-\sum_{i=1}^{i_{0}-1}(-1)^{i-1}\frac{a^{i}}{n^{i}}\Big|=0.

(If
i_{0}=1
the sum over
i
is empty.)
For
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, we apply these identities for
a:=n_{j}
, multiply them by
k_{j}
, and add them up. We deduce using the defining property of
i_{0}

(
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}^{i}=0
for
i=0,\ldots,i_{0}-1
), that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\max_{n\in[N^{1/i_{0}+\varepsilon},L_{N}]}N\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\log(n+n_{j})\Big|=0.

Hence,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\max_{n\in[N^{1/i_{0}+\varepsilon},L_{N}]}\Big|e\big(N\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\log(n+n_{j})\big)-1\big|=0.

If we combine this with (
68
) we get (we use here that the fractional degree of
L_{N}
is
1/c
and the fact that we use logarithmic averages)

A_{2,\varepsilon}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,{\bf 1}_{[N^{1/i_{0}+\varepsilon},L_{N}]}(n)=1-c/i_{0}-c\varepsilon.

Hence,
A_{2}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}A_{2,\varepsilon}=1-c/i_{0}
. This completes the proof of the Case 2 when
i_{0}
is finite.

If
i_{0}=+\infty
(in this case
A=A_{2}
), then the previous argument gives for every
i_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
the following identity

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,{\bf 1}_{[N^{1/j_{0}},L_{N}]}(n)\,e\big(N\big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}\log(n+n_{j})\big)\big)=1-c/j_{0}.

Letting
j_{0}\to+\infty
gives that
A=A_{2}=1
, as required.
∎

9.2.
Ergodic models of MRT functions for logarithmic correlations

As was the case in Section
8.2
, our goal is to
identify simple measure preserving systems and functions that reproduce the correlations
in the number theory setting obtained in Proposition
9.1
. With a bit of guesswork we
get that the systems in Definition
2.7
help us do the job; in fact the next lemma motivated their definition.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
c>0
. For
d\geq\lfloor c\rfloor
let
(Y_{d},\nu_{d},S_{d})
be the level
d
unipotent system given in Definition
2.4

and

F_{d}\colon{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}

F_{d}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}):=e(x_{d}).

Let also the system

(Z_{c},\nu^{\prime}_{c},R_{c})

2.7

G_{c}\in L^{\infty}(\nu^{\prime}_{c})

G_{c}:=\sum_{d=\lfloor c\rfloor}^{\infty}{\bf 1}_{Y_{d}}\cdot F_{d}.

Lastly, for every
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell},n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
let
i_{0}
be the minimum
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}

such that
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}n_{j}^{i}\neq 0
and

\int\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}R_{c}^{n_{j}}G_{c}^{k_{j}}\,d\nu^{\prime}_{c}=\begin{cases}0,&\quad\text{if }\,0\leq i_{0}\leq c\\

We have that

\int\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}R_{c}^{n_{j}}G_{c}^{k_{j}}\,d\nu^{\prime}_{c}=\Big(1-\frac{c}{\lceil c\rceil}\Big)C_{\lfloor c\rfloor}+c\sum_{d=\lceil c\rceil}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{d}-\frac{1}{d+1}\Big)\,C_{d},

By Lemma

Appendix A
Exponential sum estimates

In this appendix we gather some simple facts and exponential sum estimates used in the proofs of the results regarding the structure of Furstenberg systems of MRT multiplicative functions.

A.1.
Cesàro averages

The next result is an immediate consequence of
[
25
, Theorem 2.9]
.

Theorem A.1

(Kuzmin-van der Corput)
.

Let
q\geq 2
be an integer and
c\in(0,1)
. Suppose that
C_{1},C_{2}
are constants (depending on
q
and
c
only) and
h\in C^{q}([1,+\infty))
be a function, such that for some
L,M>0
, we have

C_{1}\,M\,L^{-r}\leq\max_{x\in[cL,L]}|h^{(r)}(x)|\leq C_{2}\,M\,L^{-r}

for
r=1,\ldots,q
. Then there exists a positive constant
C_{3}
, depending only on
c
,
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
(and not on
L,M
),
such that

\big|{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[cL,L]}\,e(h(n))\big|\leq C_{3}((M/L^{q})^{1/Q}+1/M),

where
Q:=4\cdot 2^{q-2}-2
.

We are only going to use the following consequence that applies to a special class of functions
g

appearing in our arguments.

Corollary A.2
.

Let
g(x):=c_{0}\,\log x+\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}x^{-i}
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Let also

i_{0}
be the minimum
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
such that
c_{i}\neq 0
.
Then for every
q\geq 2
and
c\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C_{c,q,g}
such that the following holds: If

L>0
is large enough (depending only on
c,q,g
), then
for every
N>0
we have

\big|{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[cL,L]}\,e(N\,g(n))\big|\leq C_{c,q,g}\big((N/L^{q+i_{0}})^{1/Q}+L^{i_{0}}/N\big),

where
Q:=4\cdot 2^{q-2}-2
.

Proof.

Let
N>0
,
c>0
, and
h_{N}(x):=Ng(x)
.
We have

h_{N}^{(r)}(x)=N\big(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\,c_{i}\,c_{r,i}\,x^{-i-r}\big)

for every
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
for some non-zero constants
c_{0,r},\ldots,c_{k,r}
.
Bounding the derivatives of the function inside the parenthesis (which does not depend on
N
), we get that there exist
L_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, depending only on
c,g,r
but not on
N
, such that for all
L\geq L_{0}
we have (we also use the defining property of
i_{0}
here)

C_{1}\,NL^{-i_{0}-r}\leq\max_{x\in[cL,L]}|h_{N}^{(r)}(x)|\leq C_{2}\,NL^{-i_{0}-r}

for
r=1,\ldots,q
.
Using Theorem
A.1
for
h:=h_{N}
and
M:=NL^{-i_{0}}
, we get that the asserted estimate holds for all
L\geq L_{0}
.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
L_{N}\to\infty
be a sequence of positive integers. For
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
let
a_{N}\colon[L_{N}]\to{\mathbb{U}}
be finite sequences such that for every small enough
c>0
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[cL_{N},L_{N}]}\ a_{N}(n)=0.

Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,a_{N}(n)=0.

Remark
.

We crucially use here that
\frac{1}{L_{N}}\sum_{n\in[1,cL_{N}]}|a_{N}(n)|\leq c
and this converges to
0
as
c\to 0^{+}
. An analogous property fails for logarithmic averages, so this convergence criterion cannot be used for logarithmic averages.

Combining the above, we get the following qualitative result that we will use in subsequent sections.

Corollary A.4
.

Let
g(x)=c_{0}\,\log x+\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}x^{-i}
be non-zero
and
i_{0}
be the minimum
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
such that
c_{i}\neq 0
.
Let also
(L_{N})
be a sequence of positive real numbers that satisfies
N^{\gamma}\prec L_{N}\prec N^{1/i_{0}}
for some
\gamma>0
.
Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,e(N\,g(n))=0.

Remark
.

If
i_{0}=0
, then our only growth assumption is
N^{\gamma}\prec L_{N}
for some
\gamma>0
.

Proof.

Let
c\in(0,1)
. Choose
q\geq 2
such that
(q+i_{0})\gamma>1
. Using Corollary
A.2
and our growth assumption on
L_{N}
, we get that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[cL_{N},L_{N}]}\,e(N\,g(n))=0.

We deduce from this using Lemma
A.3
that the asserted convergence to
0
holds.
∎

A.2.
Logarithmic averages

The next lemma will enable us to deduce convergence results for logarithmic averages
of variable sequences from corresponding results for Cesàro averages once we have some additional uniformity.

Lemma A.5
.

For
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
L_{N}
be a sequence of positive integers that satisfies
N^{\gamma}\prec L_{N}
for some
\gamma>0
, and let
a_{N}\colon[L_{N}]\to{\mathbb{U}}
be finite sequences.
Suppose that
for all small enough
c,\delta>0
we have

(69)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{n\in[L_{N}^{\delta},L_{N}^{1-\delta}]}\big|{\mathbb{E}}_{k\in[cn,n]}\,a_{N}(k)\big|=0.

Then

(70)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,a_{N}(n)=0.

Proof.

We first note that our assumption (
69
) implies

(71)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{n\in[L_{N}^{\delta},L_{N}^{1-\delta}]}\big|{\mathbb{E}}_{k\in[n]}\,a_{N}(k)\big|=0.

Indeed, one immediately verifies that the difference between the two expressions is
bounded by a function of
c
that converges to
0
as
c\to 0^{+}
.

Note also that in order to show (
70
), it suffices to show that for all small enough
\delta>0
we have

(72)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}^{\delta},L_{N}^{1-\delta}]}\,a_{N}(n)=0.

Indeed, because of the logarithmic averaging, the difference between this limit and the one in (
70
) is bounded by a function of
\delta
that converges to
0
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
.

Now let
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
be small enough. Equation (
71
) implies that there exists
N_{0}
such that for
N>N_{0}
we have

(73)

\sup_{n\in[L_{N}^{\delta},L_{N}^{1-\delta}]}\big|{\mathbb{E}}_{k\in[n]}\,a_{N}(k)\big|\leq\varepsilon.

Using partial summation, we get for
N\geq N_{0}
that

\Big|\sum_{n\in[L_{N}^{\delta},L_{N}^{1-\delta}]}\frac{a_{N}(n)}{n}\Big|\leq 2+\sum_{n\in[L_{N}^{\delta},L_{N}^{1-\delta}]}\Big|\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,a_{N}(k)\Big|\leq 2+\sum_{n\in[L_{N}^{\delta},L_{N}^{1-\delta}]}\frac{\varepsilon}{n},

where the last estimate follows from (
73
). Note also that since
N^{\gamma}\prec L_{N}
, we have
\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n\in[L_{N}^{\delta},L_{N}^{1-\delta}]}\,\frac{1}{n}=+\infty
. We deduce that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\big|\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}^{\delta},L_{N}^{1-\delta}]}\,a_{N}(n)\big|\leq\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary,
this implies (
72
) and completes the proof.
∎

Corollary A.6
.

Let
g(x):=c_{0}\,\log x+\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}x^{-i}
be non-zero
and
i_{0}
be the minimum
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
such that
c_{i}\neq 0
.
Let also
(L_{N})
be a sequence of positive real numbers that satisfies

N^{\gamma}\prec L_{N}\prec N^{\frac{1}{i_{0}}+\varepsilon}

for some
\gamma>0
and for every
\varepsilon>0
.
Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,e(N\,g(n))=0.

Remarks
.

\bullet

If
i_{0}=0
, then our only growth assumption is
N^{\gamma}\prec L_{N}
for some
\gamma>0
.

\bullet
If
c\in(0,1/i_{0}]
, then the assumptions are satisfied when
L_{N}:=N^{c}
or
L_{N}:=N^{c}\log N
. If
i_{0}=0
, then the assumptions are satisfied when
L_{N}=N^{c}
for all
c\in(0,+\infty)
.
Note that by Corollary
A.4
, if we use Cesàro averages, then a similar result holds for
c\in(0,1/i_{0})
but not for
c=1/i_{0}
.
For example, although
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[N]}\,e(N/n)=0
, we have
\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}\,e(N/n)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let
c\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0
be arbitrary. Let
\gamma>0
be such that
N^{\gamma}\prec L_{N}
. Choose
q\geq 2
such that
(q+i_{0})\gamma\delta>1
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
(1+\varepsilon)(1-\delta)<1
. Using Corollary
A.2
and our growth assumption on
L_{N}
, we get that
there exists a constant
C_{c,q}
such that for all large enough
N
we have

\sup_{L_{N}^{\delta}\leq n\leq L_{N}^{1-\delta}}\big|{\mathbb{E}}_{k\in[cn,n]}\,e(Ng(n))\big|\leq C_{c,q}\big(N/N^{(q+i_{0})\gamma\delta})^{1/Q}+N^{(1+\varepsilon)(1-\delta)}/N\big),

where
Q:=4\cdot 2^{q-2}-2
if
i_{0}\geq 1
, and a similar estimate with
1

in place of
N^{(1+\varepsilon)(1-\delta)}
if
i_{0}=0
.
Since
(q+i_{0})\gamma\delta>1
and
(1+\varepsilon)(1-\delta)<1
,
we get

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{L_{N}^{\delta}\leq n\leq L_{N}^{1-\delta}}\big|{\mathbb{E}}_{k\in[cn,n]}\,e(Ng(n))\big|=0.

Applying Lemma
A.5
, we deduce

\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\mathbb{E}^{\log}_{n\in[L_{N}]}\,e(N\,g(n))=0

completing the proof.
∎

In this appendix we prove an ergodic result that was used in the proof of part (

) of Theorem

Our setting is the following:
We consider two systems

and
(Z,\lambda,\id)

•

•

\lambda

For

(M_{z}y)(k):=(z\cdot y(k)),\quad k\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

.
Using the previous notation and assumptions, we have the following result.

Proposition B.1
.

Suppose that there exists
z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that the measure
\nu
is not invariant by
M_{z}
. Then there exists
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that

(Y,\nu,S)\times(Z,\lambda_{r},\id)

\lambda_{r}
is the pushforward of
\lambda

Proof.

Our plan is as follows: We first define the positive integer

\tilde{y}\in Y
and

. Once this is done, we prove that the map

Definition of
r
.
Let

(77)

G:=\{z\in Z\colon\nu\text{ is invariant by }M_{z}\}.

Since
G

\mathbb{S}^{1}

,
there exists

such that

where
\zeta_{r}
is an order
r
root of unity.

Definition of
\tilde{y}(x)
and
\tilde{z}(x)
.
Using the ergodicity of the measure
\nu
and the fact that the map
M_{z}
preserves
C(Y)
, we deduce that for
\nu
-almost all
y\in Y
and for all

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}\,\delta_{M_{z}S^{n}y}=(M_{z})_{\ast}\nu,

where

denotes the pushforward of
\nu
by
M_{z}
. It follows
from this and (
75
), (
76
),
that for
\mu
almost every

\mu_{x}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}\,\delta_{T^{n}x},

and for

almost every

\mu_{M_{z}(y)}=(M_{z})_{\ast}\nu
.
Next, for
\mu
almost every

, we let

H_{x}:=\{w\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\colon(M_{w^{-1}})_{\ast}\mu_{x}=\nu\}.

Then for
\nu\otimes\lambda
almost every

75
) and (

), we get

This establishes that for

.
Hence, for
\mu
almost every

\tilde{z}(x):=\text{ the unique element of }\ zG\ \text{ that has argument in }\ [0,2\pi/r).

Let us justify that the map

\Phi
.
We define the map

where

,
\tilde{y}(x)\in Y
,
\tilde{z}(x)\in Z

In this last appendix we justify some remarks made immediately after Theorem

The next result

Proposition C.1
