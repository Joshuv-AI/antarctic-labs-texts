Noisy atomic magnetometry with Kalman filtering and measurement-based feedback
Júlia Amorós-Binefa ID, Jan Kołodyński ID

Abstract
Sensing a magnetic field with an atomic magnetometer operated in real time presents significant challenges, primarily due to sensor non-linearity, the presence of noise, and the need for one-shot estimation. To address these challenges, we propose a comprehensive approach that integrates measurement, estimation and control strategies. Specifically, this involves implementing a quantum non-demolition measurement based on continuous light-probing of the atomic ensemble. The resulting photocurrent is then directed into an Extended Kalman Filter to produce instantaneous estimates of the system’s dynamical parameters. These estimates, in turn, are utilised by a Linear Quadratic Regulator, whose output is applied back to the system through a feedback loop. This procedure automatically steers the atomic ensemble into a spin-squeezed state, yielding a quantum enhancement in precision. Furthermore, thanks to the feedback proposed, the atoms exhibit entanglement even when the measurement data is discarded. To prove that our approach constitutes the optimal strategy in realistic scenarios, we derive ultimate bounds on the estimation error applicable in the presence of both local and collective decoherence, and show that these are indeed attained. Additionally, we demonstrate that for large ensembles the EKF not only reliably predicts its own estimation error in real time, but also accurately estimates spin-squeezing at short timescales.

Introduction

Optical magnetometers that rely on atomic ensembles pumped and probed with laser light

constitute ultraprecise magnetic field sensors, achieving sensitivities comparable to the state-of-the-art SQUID-based devices
Clarke and Braginski 2004

;
Pustelny et al. 2013

These tasks, in particular, fall into the category of real-time sensing problems in which the sensor is employed to track a time-varying signal (magnetic field) while continuously acquiring measurement data. Such a scenario may be considered the most demanding, as it requires the sensing procedure to be performed only once, with the sensor being controlled “on the fly”.
Although entanglement—a crucial resource in quantum metrology

;
Giovannetti et al. 2004

;

Apart from substantial experimental challenges, an important hurdle is the proposal and verification of an accurate dynamic model of the atomic noise, which would then allow for the tools of control and statistical inference theory to be used in the design of a future device. This contrasts with the setting of optomechanical sensors operating at cryogenic temperatures

Once pumped, the atoms are continuously monitored by a linearly polarised probe beam directed along the

\displaystyle y(t)dt=2\eta\sqrt{M}\big\langle\hat{J}_{y}(t)\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}dt+\sqrt{\eta}\,dW,

;

An essential feature of the above formalism is the incorporation of measurement back-action exerted onto the atoms

;
van Handel et al. 2005
. In particular, within Eq. (
1
), the mean
\big\langle\hat{J}_{y}(t)\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}(t)\hat{J}_{y}\right\}

conditional

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}(t)\equiv\rho(t|\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})
, i.e. the one most consistent (minimising the mean-square distance

\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}=\{y(\tau)\}_{0\leq\tau\leq t}
. Additionally, to explicitly write the dynamics of

, we must also account for the measurement-based control strategy introduced in Fig.
1
. This strategy assumes that based on the photocurrent record (potentially whole history),
\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}
, estimates of some dynamical parameters for the atomic system are made—denoted by a vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)

1

, are then used to set the control (scalar) field to a specific value

and thus modifying the Larmor frequency at time

\omega\to\omega+u(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})
. As the control field

Bearing this in mind and simplifying notation by denoting the control field as
u(t)\equiv u(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})
, we write the stochastic master equation (SME) that governs the dynamics of the conditional atomic state

as

;
van Handel et al. 2005

\displaystyle d\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}=

\displaystyle-i(\omega+u(t))[\hat{J}_{z},\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}]dt

\displaystyle+\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(j)}]\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}dt\,+\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}\mathcal{D}[\hat{J}_{z}]\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}dt

\displaystyle+M\mathcal{D}[\hat{J}_{y}]\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}dt+\sqrt{\eta M}\mathcal{H}[\hat{J}_{y}]\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}dW,

(2)

where the superoperators

III
Simulating the system dynamics

In this work, we explicitly simulate for the first time the SME (
2
). However, even though we adapt most recent numerical methods
Rossi et al. 2021
to incorporate decoherence and feedback, as discussed below, such a ‘brute-force’ approach is possible only for atomic ensembles of moderate size. That is why, our motivation is still to propose an effective model of dynamics that applies to relevant experiments
Kuzmich et al. 2000
;
Shah et al. 2010
;
Kong et al. 2020
, and can thus be used to design the building blocks of an estimation+control scheme – here, EKF+LQR introduced later in Sec.
V
. Importantly, the numerical solution allows us to validate our approach, so that our results can then be extrapolated to relevant ensemble sizes, beyond the reach of ‘brute-force’ numerics.

First, let us provide, however, the physical motivation and the reason of why our effective model succeeds in describing dynamics of large atomic ensembles. As shown in Fig.
2
, for a highly polarised ensemble, it is common to approximate the Wigner distribution of the total spin on a Bloch sphere with the distribution of a single bosonic mode in a plane (phase space)
Pezzè et al. 2018
, by using the Holstein-Primakoff transformation
Mølmer and Madsen 2004
;
Madsen and Mølmer 2004
. However, such an approximation employed in previous works
Geremia et al. 2003
;
Stockton et al. 2004
;
Mølmer and Madsen 2004
;
Madsen and Mølmer 2004
;
Deutsch and Jessen 2010
is insufficient for our purposes, as it fails to correctly capture the Larmor precession of the spin. Thus, in order not to lose the necessary information about the curvature of the Bloch sphere, we move to a frame rotating at the Larmor frequency, see Fig.
2
, in which the effective bosonic modes co-precess with the spin. A similar approach has been pursued when studying the emergence of a quantum chaotic behaviour in such a system
Muñoz Arias et al. 2020
. Formally, as done below, this corresponds to tracking the evolution of the ensemble spin-operators up to their second-order moments, with higher-order contributions being ignored.

III.1
Exact model: numerical solution

Optically pumped magnetometers operate with atomic numbers in the range of
N\approx 10^{5}\!-\!10^{13}

Kuzmich et al. 2000
;
Wasilewski et al. 2010
;
Shah et al. 2010
;
Koschorreck et al. 2010
;
Sewell et al. 2012
;
Kong et al. 2020

1/2

1/2

0

(\tfrac{1}{2})

O(N^{3})

;
Baragiola et al. 2010
;
Shammah et al. 2018

), e.g. CSS, must evolve within it, further reducing the complexity to
O(N)

Chase and Geremia 2008
;
Baragiola et al. 2010
;
Shammah et al. 2018

In this work, we use the numerical solution of the SME (
2

;
Albarelli et al. 2018

III.2
Approximate model: co-moving Gaussian picture

III.2.1
Linear-Gaussian regime

Still, the exploitation of permutational symmetry is not sufficient to reach experimentally relevant values of

IV
Ultimate Limits on Precision

With an established scalable method for simulating the system, our attention now turns to one of the fundamental questions in atomic magnetometry: how to most accurately infer the true value of the Larmor frequency
\omega
for a particular measurement record
\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}
. With the photocurrent being continuously acquired, employing a Bayesian approach to estimation is apt in this scenario, offering a systematic way of continually updating our knowledge of the parameter as new data becomes available.

Typically, in Bayesian estimation theory we seek an optimal estimator
\tilde{\omega}_{t}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})
of
\omega
that minimises the average mean squared error (aMSE)
Van Trees and Bell 2007
;
Teklu et al. 2009
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t},\omega)}\!\left[\left(\tilde{\omega}_{t}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})-\omega\right)^{2}\right]

(5)

\displaystyle=\int\!\!d\omega\,p(\omega)\!\int\!\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}\;p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)\left(\tilde{\omega}_{t}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})-\omega\right)^{2},

where the averaging
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\cdot}\right]
is performed over all measurement trajectories up to time
t
,
\int\!\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}
, and also over all possible values of the estimated parameter,
\int\!\!d\omega
. The
prior distribution

p(\omega)
in Eq. (
5
) represents our knowledge of
\omega
before collecting any measurement data, while the likelihood
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)
is the probability of observing a measurement record
\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}
given the parameter value
\omega
. The optimal estimator minimising the aMSE is generally given by the mean of the posterior distribution
Van Trees and Bell 2007
;
Teklu et al. 2009
:

\tilde{\omega}_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})=\mathbb{E}_{p(\omega|\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})}\!\left[\omega\right]=\int\!d\omega\;\omega\,p(\omega|\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}).

(6)

Constructing the posterior distribution
p(\omega|\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})
is a hard task. However, in the case of systems with linear dynamics and additive Gaussian noise, the posterior does not have to be explicitly reconstructed since the optimal estimator (
6
) is given by the
Kalman filter
(KF)
Kalman 1960
;
Kalman and Bucy 1961
. For non-linear systems, other methods exist, such as the
Extended Kalman Filter
(EKF)
Simon 2006
or the Unscented Kalman Filter
Julier et al. 1995
;
Julier et al. 2000
, that allow one to efficiently tackle the problem but do not guarantee optimality.

IV.1
Noiseless performance at small time-scales

In the case of our system, the LG regime is exactly the scenario in which the KF provides the optimal estimation strategy. For instance, in the absence of noise, i.e. for
\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}=0
and
\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}=0
in Eq. (
2
), the aMSE of the KF (estimator) follows then the Heisenberg scaling in
N
and a superclassical scaling in time
Geremia et al. 2003
;
Amorós-Binefa and Kołodyński 2021
:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{t}}\right]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\frac{3}{\eta Mt^{3}},\;\;\textrm{for}\;\;t\ll(NM)^{-1}.

(7)

However, both of the aforementioned scalings should be taken with a pinch of salt, as they rely on the assumption of
t
being small enough for the LG regime to be valid. In particular, the scaling in time must eventually become at most quadratic due to the Hamiltonian being bounded in Eq. (
2
)
Kurdziałek et al. 2023
. Furthermore, by ignoring quantum fluctuations in the
\hat{J}_{x}
-direction, we disregard the fact that the optimal time
t
actually decreases with
N
in Eq. (
7
), what puts the Heisenberg scaling into question
Caprotti et al. 2024
.

Nonetheless, the emergence of super-classical scalings
N^{2}
and
t^{3}
in Eq. (
7
) is a manifestation of generating conditional spin-squeezing
Ma et al. 2011
at short timescales and, hence, the ensemble exhibiting then interatomic entanglement
Pezzè et al. 2018
. In particular, as depicted in Fig.
2
, the continuous measurement of
\hat{J}_{y}
(
\hat{X}
-quadrature in the LG-plane) squeezes its variance in detriment of the variance of
\hat{J}_{z}
(
\hat{P}
-quadrature in the LG-plane) as the time evolves. Since our interest is to prepare a state highly sensitive to small variations of
\omega
, we wish for it to have a maximal polarisation along
x
and maximal squeezing along
y
. How closely our state aligns with this particular geometry is quantified by the (Wineland) squeezing parameter
\xi_{y}^{2}

Kitagawa and Ueda 1993
;
Wineland et al. 1994
, which effectively compares any state with a CSS pointing along
x
. Hence, we define its inverse as the relevant
spin-squeezing parameter

Ma et al. 2011
, i.e.:

\xi^{-2}_{y}(t)\coloneqq\left(\!\left.\frac{\mathrm{V}_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}(t)}{\big\langle\hat{J}_{x}(t)\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}^{2}}\right/\frac{\mathrm{V}_{y}^{\textsc{css}}}{\langle\hat{J}_{x}\rangle_{\textsc{css}}^{2}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{\big\langle\hat{J}_{x}(t)\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}^{2}}{N\;\mathrm{V}_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}(t)},

(8)

which guarantees spin-squeezing at time
t
when
\xi^{-2}_{y}(t)>1
, whereas for
\xi^{-2}_{y}(t)\leq 1
any spin-squeezing, and hence any multi-particle entanglement
Pezzè et al. 2018
, cannot be certified.

In experiments,
\xi^{-2}\approx 10
(10dB) and
\xi^{-2}\approx 100
(20dB) have been achieved with magnetically-sensitive
Bohnet et al. 2014
and atomic-clock
Hosten et al. 2016
states, respectively, for an ensemble of
N\approx 10^{5}
rubidium atoms by conducting cavity-enhanced pre-measurements. Recently,
\xi^{-2}\approx 2.8
(4.5dB) was demonstrated for
N\approx 10^{11}
by also taking into account measurements conducted after the sensing phase of the protocol (retrodiction)
Bao et al. 2020
.

IV.2
Noisy bounds

When moving away from the LG regime, the optimality of the estimation method, e.g. of the EKF, cannot be assured. However, the aMSE (
5
) can
always
be lower-bounded by the Bayesian Cramér-Rao Bound
Van Trees and Bell 2007
;
Fritsche et al. 2014
:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{t}}\right]\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}[p(\omega)]+\int\!d\omega\,p(\omega)\,\mathcal{F}[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)]}

(9)

where
\mathcal{F}[\;\cdot\;]
is the Fisher information computed w.r.t. the estimated parameter
\omega
, see App.
D
. The bound is dictated by two distinct contributions: one coming from our prior knowledge about the parameter, and the other associated with the information about the parameter contained within the measured data. Importantly, as the bound (
9
) always applies for a given measurement scheme determining
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)
, it proves the optimality of the estimation strategy considered when saturated.

Nonetheless, both Eqs. (
5
) and (
9
) still depend on a particular choice of the measurement scheme. Hence, in order to construct a benchmark applicable in any scenario, we determine a further lower bound on the aMSE (
5
) that is
independent
of both the estimation method and the measurement strategy. In particular, the presence of decoherence allows us to derive such a bound in App.
D
, which for a Gaussian prior distribution,
p(\omega)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0})
, reads:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{t}}\right]\geq\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\left(\dfrac{\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}}{t}+\dfrac{2\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}}{tN}\right)^{-1}}\underset{\sigma_{0}\to\infty}{\geq}\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}}{t}+\frac{2\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}}{Nt}.

(10)

The bound (
10
)—that we refer to as the
Classical Simulation
(CS)
limit
following
Amorós-Binefa and Kołodyński 2021
;
Matsumoto 2010
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2012
—applies at
any timescale
, consistently vanishing when
\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}=\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}=0
, i.e. in absence of noise. Otherwise, it holds for
any measurement-based feedback strategy
, independently of the initial state of the system, or the form of the measurements (also adaptive) involved, see App.
D
and Ref.
Amorós-Binefa and Kołodyński 2021
.

As a consequence, the CS limit (
10
) directly disproves the possibility of attaining the super-classical scalings of
N^{2}
and
t^{3}
in the presence of decoherence. In particular, the first term in Eq. (
10
) sets an
N
-independent bound dictated by the collective decoherence
Amorós-Binefa and Kołodyński 2021
, while the second one arising from the local noise follows the Standard Quantum Limit (SQL) of
1/Nt
—leaving room only for a constant-factor quantum enhancement
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2012
. The latter observation unfortunately disproves the conjecture about breaching the SQL-like scaling in
N
despite local dephasing, formulated in Ref.
Rossi et al. 2020
based on numerical evidence.

V
Estimation and control

With a universal lower bound established for the aMSE, let us propose the estimation and control strategies that we anticipate to yield the lowest possible estimation error, while remaining feasible for implementation.

A natural choice of an estimator tailored to the non-linear Gaussian dynamical model derived in Eq. (
4
) is the EKF
Crassidis and Junkins 2011
;
Simon 2006
. However, even though the CoG approximation accounts for the co-precession of the LG-plane with the mean angular-momentum vector
\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{J}\hskip 2.84544pt}\hskip-2.84544pt(t)\rangle

y

4b
) is always determined by
\mathrm{V}_{y}
. That is why, the principal aim of the measurement-based feedback that we introduce is to keep
\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{J}\hskip 2.84544pt}\hskip-2.84544pt(t)\rangle
pointing along its initial
x
-direction, so that the measurement may induce squeezing perpendicularly to
\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{J}\hskip 2.84544pt}\hskip-2.84544pt(t)\rangle
at all times, prolonging the LG-regime of Fig.
2
.

For this purpose, we use the
Linear Quadratic Regulator
(LQR) to find the control law, which we expect to be optimal in the LG regime
Stockton et al. 2004
. Within our scheme, the control field
u(t)
provided by the LQR is built from the estimates of the EKF, unlike other measurement-based control strategies that rely on feeding back directly the photocurrent (
1

;
Thomsen et al. 2002
.

V.1
Estimator: Extended Kalman Filter

Within the CoG approximation, the ensemble dynamics is completely described by a vector of dynamical parameters,

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}},u,0,t]+K(y(t)-h[\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}},0,t])

\displaystyle=(F-GSR^{-1}H)\Sigma+\Sigma(F-GSR^{-1}H)^{\mathsf{T}}+

V.2
Controller: Linear Quadratic Regulator

As motivated at the beginning of this section, a naive control strategy—that we refer to as
field compensation

u(t)=-\tilde{\omega}(t)
in all Eqns. (

However, as will become clear below, see e.g. Fig.

\tilde{\omega}(t)\approx\omega

In particular, we focus on the LG regime in which it is sufficient to describe the system by only two (rather than seven in

\boldsymbol{z}(t)=(\langle\hat{J}_{y}\rangle,\omega)^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}(t)=A\,\boldsymbol{z}(t)+B\,u(t)+\sigma(t)\,\boldsymbol{\xi},

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!dt\;\left[\boldsymbol{z}^{\mathsf{T}}(t)P\boldsymbol{z}(t)+u(t)Vu(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!dt\;\left[p_{J}\big\langle\hat{J}_{y}\big\rangle_{\!\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}^{2}+p_{\omega}\,\omega^{2}+\nu\,u^{2}(t)\right],

Conditional v.s. unconditional spin-squeezing

When discussing

results, we can either focus on the spin-squeezing parameter (
8
) evaluated along a specific measurement trajectory (i.e.
conditional
), relevant for real-time magnetometry, or examine the entire feedback loop system as a mechanism for generating an entangled state independent of our observations (i.e.
unconditional
). While the
conditional

In the absence of feedback, the impact that continuously measuring the system has on the unconditional dynamics of

is simply to introduce extra collective decoherence—e.g. in case of the SME (
2
) with
u(t)=0
after taking
\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})}\!\left[\cdot\right]
of both sides, the measurement induces only the extra
M\mathcal{D}[\hat{J}_{y}]
-term
. On the contrary, in the presence of feedback, the effective master equation describing the unconditional evolution cannot be easily deduced from the conditional dynamics, e.g. from Eq. (
2
), unless restrictive assumptions (e.g. Markovianity) are made
Nurdin and Yamamoto 2017
, which are not fulfilled for the LQR-based control strategy described in Sec.
V.2
. However, as such restrictive feedback scenarios are known to
unconditionally
drive the system into a spin-squeezed state
Wiseman 1994
;
Thomsen et al. 2002

In subplots (b) and (d) of Fig.
4

3
(b) for
N=200
, but also yields significant unconditional spin-squeezing (red dots) above the classical limit (horizontal solid black line). Furthermore, as Fig.
4
presents extrapolated results using the CoG model (
4
) for
N=10^{5}
, thanks to dealing with a large atomic ensemble the EKF estimates very accurately both the average conditional spin-squeezing parameter (
8
) and the polarization, i.e. in Fig.

VIII
Conclusions

The quantum dynamics of a (spin-
1/2
) atomic magnetometer is studied; first by exact numerical simulation, and later by introducing and verifying a co-moving Gaussian approximation suitable for large atomic ensembles, while incorporating a measurement-based feedback loop, as well as local and collective decoherence in the form of dephasing along the magnetic-field direction.

Based on the effective Gaussian description, an efficient estimation+control scheme is proposed that comprises an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) and a Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR). In parallel, ultimate limits on real-time sensitivity are derived that apply to
all
schemes involving measurement-based feedback and continuous measurements, given the decoherence considered. By saturating these, the EKF+LQR strategy is proven to be optimal at short timescales of experimental relevance. This is possible thanks to the enhancement of sensitivity by the continuous measurement, which generates inter-atomic entanglement (conditional spin-squeezing) in real time. The entanglement emerges even if an experimenter disregards the recorded measurements—the atomic ensemble is driven by the LQR-control into a state that is entangled also “on average”. Additionally, since for large atomic numbers (
N\gtrsim 10^{5}
) the EKF correctly predicts from measurements not only the magnetic field, but also the means and variances of ensemble spin-operators, its use is sufficient for an experimenter to infer the conditional spin-squeezing and, hence, benefit fully from it in real time.

Our work demonstrates for the first time that practical measurement-based feedback schemes can significantly enhance the operation of atomic sensors by exploiting quantum entanglement despite the noise. As a result, we believe that it paves the way for such schemes to be employed in the state-of-the-art atomic magnetometers.

Appendix C
Discrete-time picture of a measurement-based feedback scheme

Any conditional dynamics of a quantum system which undergoes a continuous measurement and measurement-based feedback, such as the SME (
2
), corresponds to the continuous-time limit (
{\delta t}\to 0
) of a discretised evolution consisting of a sequence of completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) maps,
\Phi_{\delta t}
of duration
{\delta t}
, that are interspersed by weak sequential measurements
Belavkin 1989
.

In particular, the system state at time
t=k\,\delta t
, i.e. after
k
steps of the discretised sequence, generally reads:

\displaystyle\rho[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]\coloneqq\frac{\Phi_{k}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\dots\Phi_{1}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{1}}\rho_{0}\hat{E}_{y_{1}}^{\dagger}\!\right]\!\dots\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\!\right]}{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)},

(26)

where the map acting on the state in between measurements for a time
\delta t
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}\coloneqq\Phi_{{\delta t}}^{(k)}(\omega,\boldsymbol{y}_{k}),

(27)

is determined by the system intermediate evolution, which incorporates feedback and thus depends generally on the whole past measurement record,
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\}
, apart from the estimated parameter,
\omega
.

The denominator in Eq. (
26
) is the
likelihood
, i.e. the probability of observing the measurement trajectory
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
given a particular value of the estimated parameter
\omega
:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\Phi_{k}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\dots\Phi_{1}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{1}}\rho_{0}\hat{E}_{y_{1}}^{\dagger}\!\right]\!\dots\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\!\right]\right\},

(28)

where the measurement operators
\hat{E}_{y_{\ell}}
in Eqs. (
26
) and (
28
) are determined by the continuous measurement being performed and yield a positive operator-valued measure (POVM),
\{\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\}_{y_{k}}
with
\sum_{y_{k}}\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{E}_{y_{k}}=\mathds{1}
. We have set for simplicity the measurements to be the same within a sequence, however, such an assumption is unnecessary in the derivation of precision bounds that follows. In particular, our analysis applies also to schemes in which the type of measurement is adaptively changed over the course of a single measurement trajectory.

Let us also note that the state
\rho[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]
specified in Eq. (
26
) is actually the discretised version of the conditional density matrix introduced in the main text above Eq. (
2
),
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}(t)\equiv\rho(t|\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})
, which is then formally defined in the continuous-time limit as follows:

\rho(t|\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})\coloneqq\lim_{{\delta t}\to 0}\left\{\rho[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad k=\frac{t}{{\delta t}}\right\},

(29)

being now conditioned on a continuous measurement record,
\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}\coloneqq\lim_{{\delta t}\to 0}\{\boldsymbol{y}_{k=t/{\delta t}}\}
.

C.1
Separability of feedback maps in a sequence

As the impact of decoherence and feedback is considered to be time-invariant (Markovian) in between measurements, the dynamical map (
27
) forms a semigroup with a fixed dynamical generator. The form of the generator, however, may vary stochastically from one step to another, as the feedback incorporated by the map is built on measurement outcomes that fluctuate. Nevertheless, because the increments of the measurement stochastic process are independent and stationary, the map (
27
) generally takes the form:

\Phi_{k}=\exp{\left\{{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}^{(k)}+\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Y}}^{(k)}\right\}},

(30)

with the dynamical generator being split into its deterministic and stochastic parts and
\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}
denoting a general (discrete) Lévy process
Applebaum 2009
.

Importantly, the finite increment of the Lévy process
\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}
can always be expressed using the Lévy-Itô decomposition as
Applebaum 2009
:

\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}=\mu_{k}{\delta t}+\sigma_{k}\Delta\!\mathrm{W}_{k}+\!\!\int_{|x|<1}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!x\,\tilde{\mathrm{N}}({\delta t},\mathrm{d}x)+\!\!\int_{|x|\geq 1}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!x\,\mathrm{N}({\delta t},\mathrm{d}x),

(31)

which includes a deterministic drift, a Gaussian diffusion with
\Delta\!\mathrm{W}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\delta t})
, and two jump terms, respectively.

The last term in Eq. (
31
) represents large jumps of magnitude
|x|\geq 1
, and the first integral term is a compensated Poisson process that accounts for small jumps of magnitude
|x|<1
. Here,
x
is the size of a jump,
\mathrm{d}x
is a small interval of jump sizes, and
\mathrm{N}({\delta t},\mathrm{d}x)
is the number of jumps in the time interval
{\delta t}
whose size falls in
\mathrm{d}x
. However, for small jumps, we actually integrate over the
compensated
Poisson measure,
\tilde{\mathrm{N}}({\delta t},\mathrm{d}x)\coloneqq\mathrm{N}({\delta t},\mathrm{d}x)-\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\,{\delta t}
, where
\nu(\mathrm{d}x)
is the Lévy measure, telling us the expected number of jumps of size
\mathrm{d}x
per unit time (i.e.
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\mathrm{N}({\delta t},\mathrm{d}x)}\right]=\nu(\mathrm{d}x){\delta t}
). In the special case where this Lévy measure is finite, i.e.
\int x\nu(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty
, we can further simplify the Lévy-Itô decomposition as

\displaystyle\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}=\mu_{k}^{\prime}{\delta t}+\sigma_{k}\Delta\!\mathrm{W}_{k}+\!\!\int_{|x|\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!x\,\mathrm{N}({\delta t},\mathrm{d}x)

(32)

where
\mu_{k}^{\prime}\coloneqq\mu_{k}-\int_{|x|<1}x\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)
. Crucially, the variance of the Poisson jumps is proportional to
{\delta t}
, i.e.

\mathrm{Var}\!\left[\int_{|x|\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!x\,\mathrm{N}({\delta t},\mathrm{d}x)\right]={\delta t}\int_{|x|\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!x^{2}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x).

(33)

Therefore, it is always valid to expand the dynamical map (
30
) to the lowest order in
{\delta t}
as follows

\displaystyle\!\!\Phi_{\!k}\!=\!\mathds{1}\!+\!{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\!\mathrm{det}}^{(k)}\!+\!\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Y}}^{(k)}\!\!+\!\frac{1}{2}(\!\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}\!)^{2}(\!\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Y}}^{(k)})^{2}\!\!+\!O({\delta t}^{\frac{3}{2}}),

(34)

because all higher-order terms—denoted schematically as
O({\delta t}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}})
—even when containing stochastic increments cannot contribute at the lowest
{\delta t}
-order. By the same arguments, we can rewrite Eq. (
34
) as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\!k}

\displaystyle\!=\!\left(\!\mathds{1}\!+\!{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\!\mathrm{det}}^{(k)}\!+\!O({\delta t}^{2})\!\right)\!\big(\!\mathds{1}\!+\!\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Y}}^{(k)}\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\!(\!\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k})^{2}(\!\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Y}}^{(k)})^{2}

\displaystyle+O({\delta t}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}})\big)=\Xi_{\omega}\circ\mathfrak{F}_{\mathrm{Y}},

(35)

which can be interpreted as the lowest order in the Suzuki-Trotter expansion, consisting of deterministic and stochastic components. Specifically, the deterministic part is given by

\Xi_{\omega}\coloneqq\exp{\{{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}^{(k)}\}},

(36)

whereas the stochastic part reads

\mathfrak{F}_{\mathrm{Y}}\coloneqq\exp{\{\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Y}}^{(k)}\}}.

(37)

Together, these parts describe the sequential action of the map (
30
).

This treatment accounts for the Markovian feedback of
Wiseman 1994
, as well as any Bayesian feedback
Albarelli and Genoni 2024
. In particular, for Markovian feedback, by identifying the stochastic term as
\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Y}}^{(k)}\cdot\equiv-\mathrm{i}\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}[\hat{F},\,\cdot\,]
, one recovers results of Ref.
Wiseman 1994
, as later shown explicitly in Sec.
E.3
. Considering instead a general Bayesian feedback that may utilise the whole measurement record, we identify the deterministic part of the generator in Eq. (
30
) as the one encoding the parameter, i.e.
{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{det}}^{(k)}\equiv{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
, whereas the stochastic part as the one depending on the measurement trajectory
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
via feedback, i.e.
\Delta\!\mathrm{Y}_{\!k}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Y}}^{(k)}\equiv{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathds{1}+{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{k}}+O({\delta t}^{2})\right)\left(\mathds{1}+{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}+O({\delta t}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}})\right)

\displaystyle=\Xi_{\omega}\circ\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}+O({\delta t}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}),

(38)

where we define
\Xi_{\omega}\coloneqq\exp\{{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{k}}\}
and
\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\coloneqq\exp\{{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\}
as the maps responsible for noisy parameter encoding and the application of the feedback, respectively. As the above analysis applies to any measurement-based feedback, we consistently recover the feedback of our scheme in Eq. (
2
) by considering
{\delta t}\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\cdot\equiv-\mathrm{i}{\delta t}\,u(\boldsymbol{y}_{k})[\hat{H},\,\cdot\,]
and its continuous-time limit.

Having established in Eq. (
38
) that any measurement-based feedback and noisy parameter encoding can always be separated in the
{\delta t}\to 0
limit at the level of maps, we may decompose the sequential structure of the conditional state in Eq. (
26
) further. In particular, for small enough
{\delta t}
, we can always write

\displaystyle\;\rho[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]=

(39)

\displaystyle\ =\frac{\Xi_{\omega}\!\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\dots\Xi_{\omega}\!\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{1}}\rho_{0}\hat{E}_{y_{1}}^{\dagger}\!\right]\!\right]\dots\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\!\right]\!\right]}{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)},

with the likelihood defined now as

\displaystyle\;p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)=

(40)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\Xi_{\omega}\!\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\dots\Xi_{\omega}\!\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{1}}\rho_{0}\hat{E}_{y_{1}}^{\dagger}\!\right]\!\right]\dots\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\!\right]\!\right]\right\}.

Appendix D
Classical Simulation bound for any protocol with local and global decoherence

Although the Larmor frequency
\omega
in
\Xi_{\omega}
may be allowed to follow itself a stochastic process
Amoros-Binefa et al. 2025
, here we focus on estimating a constant value by employing a Bayesian strategy. Within the Bayesian approach to estimation theory, the aMSE can be lower-bounded by different classes of Bayesian bounds
Fritsche et al. 2014
. For our purposes, we choose the (marginal unconditional) Bayesian Cramér-Rao Bound (BCRB)
Bobrovsky et al. 1987
;
Fritsche et al. 2014
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{t}}\right]\geq(J_{B})^{-1},

(41)

where
J_{B}
is the Bayesian information (BI)
Van Trees and Bell 2007
,

\displaystyle J_{B}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p(\omega,\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})}\!\left[\left(\partial_{\omega}\ln p(\omega,\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})\right)^{2}\right].

(42)

The BI can be split into two terms,
J_{B}=J_{P}+J_{M}
. The first term,
J_{P}
, represents the contribution of our prior knowledge about
\omega
,

\displaystyle J_{P}=\mathcal{F}[p(\omega)]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p(\omega)}\!\left[\left(\partial_{\omega}\ln p(\omega)\right)^{2}\right],

(43)

corresponding to the Fisher information (FI) of the prior distribution
p(\omega)
evaluated w.r.t. the estimated parameter
\omega
.
As in this work we assume the
a priori
knowledge about
\omega
to be described by a Gaussian distribution of mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
,

\displaystyle\omega\sim p(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(\omega-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

(44)

its FI corresponds to just the inverse of the variance, i.e.:

\displaystyle J_{P}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}.

(45)

The second term, namely the contribution of the measurement record, or
J_{M}
, can be understood as averaging the FI of the likelihood over the prior distribution, i.e.:

\displaystyle J_{M}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p(\omega,\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t})}\!\left[\left(\partial_{\omega}\ln p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)\right)^{2}\right]

(46)

\displaystyle=\int d\omega\;p(\omega)\,\mathcal{F}[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)],

(47)

with

\mathcal{F}[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)}\!\left[\left(\partial_{\omega}\ln p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)\right)^{2}\right]

(48)

being the FI of
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)
w.r.t.
\omega
, i.e. the likelihood of observing a measurement trajectory
\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}
given that the true value of the Larmor frequency is
\omega
.

Now, as
J_{M}
, or equivalently
\mathcal{F}[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)]
, depends on a particular measurement strategy assumed, in what follows we focus on establishing a universal upper bound on
J_{M}
that applies no matter the measurement sequence, incl. measurement-based feedback, and which stems from our previous work
Amorós-Binefa and Kołodyński 2021
.

The proof that follows is fully based on the form of the map
\Xi_{\omega}
responsible for noisy
\omega
-encoding. Moreover, in Eq. (
38
) we have shown that
\Xi_{\omega}
emerges from
\Phi_{k}
by separating it — up to order
{\delta t}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}
— into two terms, one including the
\omega
-encoding, i.e.
\Xi_{\omega}
, and the other one the feedback,
\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}
. Therefore, the bounds we now derive apply to any form of measurement-based feedback, but are specific to the map
\Xi_{\omega}
. In particular, let us further split the internal dynamics given by the map
\Xi_{\omega}
into two additional maps,

\Xi_{\omega}=\Omega\circ\Lambda_{\omega},

(49)

where
\Omega
denotes the non-unitary evolution arising in between measurements due to the collective decoherence (of strength
\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}
), and the channel
\Lambda_{\omega}
encompasses both the unitary frequency-encoding and the non-unitary local decoherence (of strength
\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}
). Note that in the SME (
2
) both generators of the collective and local decoherence commute with one another and the
\omega
-encoding, so must their resulting CPTP maps upon integration, i.e.
[\Omega,\Lambda_{\omega}]=0
.
However, even if the two maps were not to commute, the map
\Xi_{\omega}
could still be split to order
{\delta t}^{2}
, using the Trotter-Suzuki approximation.
As a result, the conditional state
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}[k]\equiv\rho[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]
given by Eq. (
39
), by substituting Eq. (
49
), can then be written as

\displaystyle\;\rho[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]=

(50)

\displaystyle\ =\frac{\!\Omega\!\left[\Lambda_{\omega}\!\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\!\dots\Omega\!\left[\Lambda_{\omega}\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}\!\!\left[\hat{E}_{y_{1}}\rho_{0}\hat{E}_{y_{1}}^{\dagger}\right]\right]\right]\!\dots\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\right]\right]\right]}{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)}.

Although the above decomposition is valid for any type of measurement-based feedback in the
{\delta t}\ll 1
limit, in case of the feedback considered in the SME (
2
), it applies for any
{\delta t}>0
, as the feedback in Eq. (
2
) effectively changes the Larmor frequency and, hence, commutes with both the
\omega
-encoding and the decoherence.

Analogously, the denominator of Eq. (
50
) is now the special case of the general likelihood (
28
), and reads

\displaystyle\;p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)=

(51)

\displaystyle=\!\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\!\Omega\!\left[\Lambda_{\omega}\!\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\!\dots\Omega\!\left[\Lambda_{\omega}\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}\!\!\left[\hat{E}_{y_{1}}\rho_{0}\hat{E}_{y_{1}}^{\dagger}\right]\right]\right]\!\dots\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\right]\right]\right]\!\right\}\!.

D.1
Convex decomposition of the likelihood

Similarly to our previous work
Amorós-Binefa and Kołodyński 2021
, which dealt only with collective decoherence, our motivation is to find convex decomposition of the effective noisy
\omega
-encoding map, i.e.
\Omega\left[\Lambda_{\omega}\left[\;\cdot\;\right]\right]
in Eq. (
50
), so that the discretised likelihood (
51
) can be decomposed as follows:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)=\int\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\;q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)\;p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})

(52)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}=\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}\}
is a sequence of sets, each containing
N
auxiliary frequency-like random variables, e.g.
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{\ell}=\{\zeta^{(1)}_{\ell},\zeta^{(2)}_{\ell},\dots,\zeta^{(N)}_{\ell}\}
indicates that within the
\ell
th step the first probe undergoes the Larmor precession for
{\delta t}
with frequency
\zeta^{(1)}
, the second probe with
\zeta^{(2)}
etc.

While
q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)
represents the mixing distribution that crucially contains all the
\omega
-dependence,
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})
in Eq. (
52
) can be interpreted as a (fictitious) likelihood of obtaining the measurement record
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
, while the discretised measurements are interspersed by CPTP maps within which each probe undergoes frequency encoding with frequencies specified by the sequence
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}
, i.e.:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})=

(53)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\!\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}}\!\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\!\dots\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}}\!\!\left[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{1}}\rho_{0}\hat{E}_{y_{1}}^{\dagger}\right]\right]\!\dots\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\right]\right]\!\right\}\!.

D.1.1
The map
\Omega\circ\Lambda_{\omega}
as a convex mixture of unitaries

We express the overall map
\Omega\left[\Lambda_{\omega}\!\left[\;\cdot\;\right]\right]
as a mixture of unitaries by decomposing separately the collective map
\Omega[\cdot]
that acts on all the probes, and
\Lambda_{\omega}[\cdot]
that exhibits a tensor product structure with local maps acting independently on each probe.

In case of
\Omega
representing the evolution of the atomic state under collective decoherence (dephasing along the magnetic-field direction), we can simply use the results presented by us in Ref.
Amorós-Binefa and Kołodyński 2021
and write

\Omega[\;\cdot\;]=\int d\xi\;p_{\mathrm{coll}}(\xi)\;\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi\hat{J}_{z}{\delta t}}\;\cdot\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi\hat{J}_{z}{\delta t}},

(54)

with a Gaussian distribution
p_{\mathrm{coll}}(\xi)=\mathcal{N}(0,V_{\mathrm{coll}})
of zero mean and variance:

V_{\mathrm{coll}}\coloneqq\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}/{\delta t}.

(55)

On the other hand, the overall map associated with the local decoherence, i.e.:

\Lambda_{\omega}=\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}},

(56)

can be described as the formal solution of the following master equation,

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\,\rho=-\mathrm{i}[\hat{H},\rho(t)]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}{[\hat{L}_{i}]}\rho(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\omega[\hat{J}_{z},\rho(t)]+\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}{[\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(i)}]}\rho(t)

\displaystyle=\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\omega}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}[\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(i)},\;\cdot\;]+\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}{[\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(i)}]}\;\cdot\;\right)\rho(t)

\displaystyle=\left[\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}^{(i)}\right]\rho,

(57)

where
\hat{J}_{z}
is the collective angular momentum in the
z
-direction,
\hat{J}_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(i)}
, with the subscript
(i)
denoting the position of
\hat{\sigma}_{z}
in the tensor-product structure, and
\mathcal{L}^{(i)}=-\mathrm{i}\omega/2[\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(i)},\;\cdot\;]+\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}/2\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(i)}]\;\cdot
.

It then follows that the collective map
\Lambda_{\omega}
can be written as a tensor product of the individual CPTP maps for each atom,
\Lambda_{\omega}^{(i)}
. Namely,

\Lambda_{\omega}=\mathrm{e}^{\bigoplus t\mathcal{L}^{(i)}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}^{(i)}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\Lambda_{\omega}^{(i)},

(58)

with the semigroup map
\Lambda_{\omega}^{(i)}=\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}^{(i)}}
defined by the GKSL generator
\mathcal{L}^{(i)}
representing the unconditional evolution of the
i
th atom, i.e.:

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho_{i}(t)}{dt}=-i\omega[\hat{s}_{z}^{(i)},\rho_{i}(t)]+2\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}\mathcal{D}[\hat{s}_{z}^{(i)}]\rho_{i}(t),

(59)

where
\hat{s}_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}
, and
\rho_{i}=\text{Tr}_{\forall\neq i}{(\rho)}
is the state after tracing out all atoms except the
i
th one. Then, using again results introduced in
Amorós-Binefa and Kołodyński 2021
, the unconditional equation described above can be written as a convex combination of unitary evolutions

\Lambda_{\omega}^{(i)}[\;\cdot\;]=\int d\upsilon^{(i)}\,p_{\mathrm{loc}}(\upsilon^{(i)}|\omega)\;\mathcal{U}_{\upsilon^{(i)}{\delta t}}[\;\cdot\;],

(60)

where
\upsilon^{(i)}
is just a dummy random variable that follows
\upsilon^{(i)}\sim p_{\mathrm{loc}}(\upsilon^{(i)}|\omega)=\mathcal{N}(\omega,V_{\mathrm{loc}})
, a Gaussian with mean
\omega
and variance

V_{\mathrm{loc}}\coloneqq 2\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}/{\delta t}.

(61)

The unitary channel
\mathcal{U}_{\upsilon^{(i)}{\delta t}}[\,\cdot\,]
is also parametrised w.r.t. the auxiliary variable
\upsilon^{(i)}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{U}_{\upsilon^{(i)}{\delta t}}[\;\cdot\;]=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\,\upsilon^{(i)}\hat{s}_{z}^{(i)}{\delta t}}\,\cdot\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,\upsilon^{(i)}\hat{s}_{z}^{(i)}{\delta t}}.

(62)

Hence, it follows that the overall map
\Lambda_{\omega}
in Eq. (
56
) is equivalent to a convex combination of tensor products of unitary maps:

\Lambda_{\omega}[\,\cdot\,]\!=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\!\Lambda_{\omega}^{(i)}[\,\cdot\,]\!=\!\!\int\!\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\upsilon}\,\wp_{\mathrm{loc}}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}|\omega)\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{U}_{\upsilon^{(i)}{\delta t}}[\,\cdot\,],

(63)

where
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}=(\upsilon^{(1)},\dots,\upsilon^{(i)},\dots,\upsilon^{(N)})
,
\wp_{\mathrm{loc}}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}|\omega)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{\mathrm{loc}}(\upsilon^{(i)}|\omega)
and
\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\upsilon}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\upsilon^{(i)}
. Note that since
\exp{(A)}\otimes\exp{(B)}=\exp{(A\oplus B)}
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}[\,\cdot\,]

\displaystyle\equiv\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{U}_{\upsilon^{(i)}{\delta t}}[\,\cdot\,]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\upsilon^{(i)}\hat{\jmath}_{z}^{(i)}}\;\cdot\;\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\upsilon^{(i)}\hat{\jmath}_{z}^{(i)}},

(64)

where
\hat{\jmath}_{z}^{(i)}=\underbrace{\mathds{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathds{1}}_{i-1}\otimes\,\hat{s}_{z}^{(i)}\otimes\underbrace{\mathds{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathds{1}}_{N-i}
,
\hat{s}_{z}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(i)}
.

Finally, combining the maps (
54
) and (
56
), we get

\Omega[\Lambda_{\omega}[\;\cdot\;]]=\int\!\!d\xi\,p_{\mathrm{coll}}(\xi)\int\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\upsilon}\,\wp_{\mathrm{loc}}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}|\omega)\;\mathcal{U}_{\xi,\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}[\;\cdot\;],

(65)

where
\mathcal{U}_{\xi,\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}[\;\cdot\;]=\mathrm{e}^{-i{\delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\xi+\upsilon^{(i)})\hat{\jmath}_{z}^{(i)}}\;\cdot\;\mathrm{e}^{i{\delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\xi+\upsilon^{(i)})\hat{\jmath}_{z}^{(i)}}
. Furthermore, let us for convenience redefine
\upsilon^{(i)}
as
\upsilon^{(i)}=\zeta^{(i)}-\xi
, so that the above decomposition becomes:

\displaystyle\Omega[\Lambda_{\omega}[\;\cdot\;]]=

\displaystyle\quad=\int\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\!\left[\!\int\!d\xi\,p_{\mathrm{coll}}(\xi)\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{\mathrm{loc}}(\zeta^{(i)}-\xi|\omega)\right]\!\mathcal{U}_{\zeta}[\;\cdot\;]

\displaystyle\quad=\int\!\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\!\left[\!c_{1}\!\!\int\!\!d\xi\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{coll}}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\!\!\frac{(\zeta^{(i)}-\xi-\omega)^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}}}\!\right]\!\mathcal{U}_{\zeta}[\;\cdot\;]

\displaystyle\quad=\int\!\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\,c_{2}\,f(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\,g(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}|\omega)\,\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}[\;\cdot\;],

(66)

where the vector
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
represents a collection of each auxiliary frequency acting on each particle, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\zeta^{(1)},\dots,\zeta^{(i)},\dots,\zeta^{(N)})
. The unitary map parametrised by the aforementioned auxiliary frequencies is denoted as
\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}[\;\cdot\;]=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\,{\delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta^{(i)}\hat{\jmath}_{z}^{(i)}}\,\cdot\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,{\delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta^{(i)}\hat{\jmath}_{z}^{(i)}}
, while we define the normalisation constant
c_{2}\coloneqq c_{1}\sqrt{2\pi V_{\mathrm{coll}}V_{\mathrm{loc}}/(NV_{\mathrm{coll}}+V_{\mathrm{loc}})}
for convenience, being proportional to
c_{1}\coloneqq(2\pi V_{\mathrm{coll}})^{-1/2}(2\pi V_{\mathrm{loc}})^{-N/2}
.

The final expression in Eq. (
66
) is a consequence of the following equality:

\int\!\!d\xi\;\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{coll}}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(\zeta^{(i)}-\xi-\omega)^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}}}\!=\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\,f(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\,g(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}|\omega),

(67)

where
\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
is the average of auxiliary frequencies experienced by the
N
atoms, i.e.,
\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta^{(i)}
, whereas

f(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\coloneqq\exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}}\left(\sum_{i}\left(\!\zeta^{(i)}\!\right)^{2}-N\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{2}\right)\right\}

(68)

and

g(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}|\omega)\coloneqq\exp\!\left\{-\frac{(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}-\omega)^{2}}{2V_{Q}}\right\},

(69)

are Gaussian-like functions—the latter exhibiting a new “effective” variance:

V_{Q}\coloneqq V_{\mathrm{coll}}+\frac{V_{\mathrm{loc}}}{N}=\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}+2\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}/N}{{\delta t}}=\frac{\kappa_{Q}}{{\delta t}},

(70)

with
\kappa_{Q}\coloneqq\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}+2\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}/N
.

To prove Eq. (
67
), we first define a new variable
\nu^{(i)}=\zeta^{(i)}-\omega
and expand the exponent

(\zeta^{(i)}-\xi-\omega)^{2}=(\xi-\nu^{(i)})^{2}=\xi^{2}-2\xi\nu^{(i)}+(\nu^{(i)})^{2}
. If we now take the sum and rearrange terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{(\xi-\nu^{(i)})^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}}=\frac{\xi^{2}-2\xi\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\nu^{(i)}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}(\nu^{(i)})^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}/N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi^{2}-2\xi\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}+N(\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}/N}-\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq m}\nu^{(i)}\nu^{(m)}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}/N}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\xi-\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}/N}+\frac{(N-1)\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}/N}-\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq m}\nu^{(i)}\nu^{(m)}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}/N},

(71)

where
\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\nu^{(i)}
and

\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=m}(\nu^{(i)})^{2}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\neq m}\nu^{(i)}\nu^{(m)}.

(72)

Crucially, the last two terms in (
71
) are independent of the frequency
\omega
. This will matter later on, but for now, let us show how they only depend on
\zeta
:

\displaystyle(N-1)\,\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq m}\nu^{(i)}\nu^{(m)}=-\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}(\nu^{(i)})^{2}

\displaystyle=-\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{2}+2\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\omega-\omega^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\left((\zeta^{(i)})^{2}-2\omega\zeta^{(i)}+\omega^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}(\zeta^{(i)})^{2}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{2},

(73)

where we used Eq. (
72
) and that
\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}-\omega
. Hence,

\displaystyle\!\!\exp{\!\left\{\!\!-\!\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\frac{(\zeta^{(i)}\!-\!\xi\!-\!\omega)^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}}\!\right\}}\!=\!f(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\exp{\!\left\{\!-\frac{(\xi-\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}/N}\!\right\}},

(74)

which upon substituting into the l.h.s. of Eq. (
67
) allows us to directly evaluate the Gaussian integral over
\xi
, i.e.:

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\!\int\!d\xi\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{coll}}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(\xi-\overline{\boldsymbol{\nu}})^{2}}{2V_{\mathrm{loc}}/N}}=

\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\sqrt{2\pi\frac{V_{\mathrm{coll}}V_{\mathrm{loc}}}{NV_{\mathrm{coll}}+V_{\mathrm{loc}}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}-\omega)^{2}}{2(V_{\mathrm{coll}}+V_{\mathrm{loc}}/N)}},

(75)

and arrive at the r.h.s. of Eq. (
67
).

D.2
Upper bound on the Fisher Information

Next, by substituting the convex combination of the map
\Omega[\Lambda_{\omega}[\;\cdot\;]]
in terms of unitaries, that is, equation (
66
), into (
51
),

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)=

\displaystyle=\!\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\!\Omega\!\!\left[\!\Lambda_{\omega}\!\!\left[\!\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\!\!\left[\!\,\hat{\!E}_{y_{k}}\dots\Omega\!\!\left[\!\Lambda_{\omega}\!\!\left[\!\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}\!\!\!\left[\!\hat{E}_{y_{1}}\rho_{0}\hat{E}_{y_{1}}^{\dagger}\!\right]\right]\right]\!\!\dots\!\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\!\right]\right]\right]\!\right\}\!

\displaystyle=\!\!\int\!\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\!\!\left(\!\prod_{j}^{k}\!c_{2}f(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j})g(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j}}|\omega)\!\right)\!\!\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{k}}\!\big[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\!\big[\!\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\dots

\displaystyle\dots\mathcal{U}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}}\!\big[\mathfrak{F}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}\!\big[\hat{E}_{y_{1}}\rho_{0}\hat{E}_{y_{1}}^{\dagger}\big]\big]\big]\!\dots\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\big]\big]\!

\displaystyle=\!\!\int\!\!\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\!\!\left(\!\prod_{j}^{k}\!c_{2}f(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j})g(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j}}|\omega)\!\right)\!p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}),

(76)

where we have used (
53
) in the last step. Note that by comparing (
76
) with (
52
), we can easily identify the auxiliary conditional probability
q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)
as a product distribution

q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)\!=\prod_{j=1}^{k}q(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j}|\omega)=c_{2}\prod_{j=1}^{k}f(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j})g(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}|\omega).

(77)

As discussed at length in Ref.
Amorós-Binefa and Kołodyński 2021
, the expression for
q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)
allows us to directly construct an upper bound on the Fisher information evaluated w.r.t. the likelihood,
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)
, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\left[\mathcal{S}\left(q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)\right)\right]\leq\mathcal{F}\left[q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)\right],

(78)

where
\mathcal{S}\!:q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)\rightarrow p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\omega)
is a stochastic map
\mathcal{S}[\;\cdot\;]=\int d\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}\;p(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k})[\;\cdot\;]
, under which the Fisher information can only decrease due to its contractivity.

We compute the Fisher information of
q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)
w.r.t. the parameter
\omega
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left[q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)\right]

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)}\!\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\omega^{2}}\ln q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)\right]

(79)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}-\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)}\!\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\omega^{2}}\ln g(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}|\omega)\right],

(80)

where Eq. (
80
) follows from the fact that for each
j
th timestep only the function
g(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}|\omega)
depends on
\omega
. As a result, after substituting further the Gaussian form (
69
) of
g(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}|\omega)
, we obtain

\mathcal{F}\left[q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{k}|\omega)\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{V_{Q}}=\frac{k}{V_{Q}}=\frac{k{\delta t}}{\kappa_{Q}},

(81)

after substituting also for
V_{Q}
according to Eq. (
70
).

D.2.1
Evaluating the
{\delta t}\to 0
limit

It is then straightforward to see that when taking the limit of
{\delta t}\rightarrow 0
, the Fisher information of
q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}|\omega)
becomes
\mathcal{F}\left[q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}|\omega)\right]=t/\kappa_{Q}
, and therefore,

\displaystyle J_{B}

\displaystyle=J_{P}+J_{M}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\int d\omega\;p(\omega)\,\mathcal{F}[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}|\omega)]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\int d\omega\;p(\omega)\,\mathcal{F}\left[q(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}|\omega)\right]=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{t}{\kappa_{Q}}.

(82)

Hence, for a constant field, we obtain the following lower bound on the estimation aMSE,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{t}}\right]\geq(J_{B})^{-1}\geq V_{t}=\dfrac{1}{\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{\dfrac{\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}}{t}+\dfrac{2\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}}{tN}}}.

(83)

Appendix E
Retrieving the SME for homodyne measurement and field-compensating feedback

Any conditional dynamics involving a
continuous measurement
, such as the SME (
2
), is generally derived as a continuous-time limit (
{\delta t}\to 0
) of a discretised evolution consisting of a sequence of completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) maps,
\Phi_{\delta t}
of duration
{\delta t}
, that are interspersed by weak sequential measurements
Belavkin 1989
. In fact, the continuous measurement record
\boldsymbol{y}_{\leq t}
over time
t=k{\delta t}
corresponds to the limiting case of a sequence of outcomes
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\}
after letting
k\to\infty
as
{\delta t}\to 0
.

Additionally, by focusing on a single
{\delta t}
-step in Eq. (
26
), we can show that consecutive conditional states are related as follows:

\rho[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]=\frac{\Phi_{k}[\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}]}{\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\Phi_{k}[\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}]\right\}}.

(84)

This iterative relationship can be further split to differentiate between the state before and after performing the measurement given by the POVM
\,\hat{\!E}_{y_{k}}
. In other words, we label, analogously to the discrete Kalman filter:

update :

\displaystyle\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]\,=\,\text{state after measurement update $k$}

but before evolution
k\!\shortminus\!1\to k

predict :

\displaystyle\rho[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]\,=\,\text{state after measurement update $k$}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;\,\text{and after evolution $k\!\shortminus\!1\to k$},

such that the state at time
k
is written as:

\rho[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]=\frac{\Phi_{k}[\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]]}{\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\Phi_{k}[\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]]\right\}},

(85)

where
\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]
is the updated state after we have performed the measurement but before evolving the system under the internal map
\Phi_{k}[\;\cdot\;]
, i.e.,

\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]=\frac{\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}}{\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\right\}}.

(86)

Both definitions readily emerge from Eq. (
84

\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}\right\}
.

Let us now focus on deriving an expression for
\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]
and its unnormalized version,

\tilde{\rho}[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]\coloneqq\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{E}_{y_{k}}^{\dagger}.

(87)

E.1
Substituting the form of CM.

Within the continuous measurement framework
Belavkin 1989
, the Kraus operators of the
k
th measurement in Eq. (

\{\hat{E}_{y_{k}}\}_{y_{k}}
, are generally associated with an interaction of the system with a bosonic mode,
\hat{B}_{k}
satisfying

\hat{E}_{y_{k}}=\bra{y_{k}}\hat{U}_{{\delta t}}\ket{0},

(88)

where the bosonic mode is initialised in the vacuum state
\ket{0}
before being projected onto the state
\ket{y_{k}}
associated with a particular outcome
y_{k}
of the quadrature operator

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{k}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}+\hat{B}_{k}),

(89)

while the (weak) interaction is generated by the unitary operation
Belavkin 1989
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Albarelli and Genoni 2024
:

\hat{U}_{{\delta t}}=\exp\!\left\{\sqrt{M{\delta t}}\,\left(\hat{L\,}\!\otimes\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}-\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\otimes\hat{B}_{k}\right)\right\},

(90)

with
M
parametrising the strength of the continuous measurement and
\hat{L\,}\!
denoting
any
system operator that is continuously probed.

Knowing that, we can expand the numerator of Eq. (
86
) as

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]\!=\!\braket{y_{k}|\hat{U}_{\delta t}|0}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\braket{0|\hat{U}_{\delta t}|y_{k}}

(91)

\displaystyle\quad=\!\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{P}}\{\hat{U}_{\delta t}(\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\!\otimes\!\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0})\hat{U}_{\delta t}^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}\!\otimes\!\ket{y_{k}}\!\!\bra{y_{k}})\},

where
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{P}}
denotes the partial trace over the probe subspace. Note that the probe is modelled as a series of discretized uncorrelated modes interacting with the system in a conveyor-belt fashion
Albarelli and Genoni 2024
. In other words, before the system-probe interacting unitary is applied, the state of each probe mode is assumed to be in the vacuum state. Therefore, the joint state of the system and the probe before the interaction occurs is, at each step
\rho[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]\!\otimes\!\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}
.
To now derive a complete expression for the evolution of the reduced state of the atoms under the action of the Kraus operators, we have to first evolve the joint state under the action of the interaction Hamiltonian. Namely,

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\delta t}(\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\!\otimes\!\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0})\hat{U}_{\delta t}^{\dagger}=\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\!\otimes\!\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}

\displaystyle\;+\!M\hat{L\,}\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\!\otimes\!\ket{1}\!\!\bra{1}{\delta t}

\displaystyle\;+\!\left(\hat{L\,}\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\!\otimes\!\ket{1}\!\!\bra{0}\!+\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\!\otimes\!\ket{0}\!\!\bra{1}\right)\!\!\sqrt{\!M{\delta t}}

\displaystyle\;+\!\frac{M}{2}\!\Big(\!\sqrt{2}\hat{L\,}\!^{2}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\!\otimes\!\ket{2}\!\!\bra{0}\!-\!\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\!\hat{L\,}\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\!\otimes\!\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}

\displaystyle\;+\!\sqrt{2}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}](\!\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger})^{2}\!\otimes\!\ket{0}\!\!\bra{2}\!-\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\!\hat{L\,}\!\!\otimes\!\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}\!\Big){\delta t}

\displaystyle\;+\!O({\delta t}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}),

(92)

where we have used the expansion of the unitary operator up to order
{\delta t}
:

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{{\delta t}}=

\displaystyle\mathds{1}\!\otimes\!\mathds{1}+\left(\!\hat{L\,}\!\!\otimes\!\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}\!-\!\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\!\otimes\!\hat{B}_{k}\!\right)\!\sqrt{M{\delta t}}+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\!\hat{L\,}\!^{2}\!\otimes\!(\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger})^{2}

\displaystyle-\!\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\!\hat{L\,}\!\!\otimes\!\hat{B}_{k}\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}\!-\!\hat{L\,}\!\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\!\otimes\!\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{B}_{k}\!+\!(\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger})^{2}\!\otimes\!(\hat{B}_{k})^{2}\!\Big)M{\delta t}

\displaystyle+O({\delta t}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}),

(93)

and the fact that
\hat{B}_{k}
and
\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}
are discretized annihilation and creation operators obeying the standard rules.
Next, we simply project the state of the probe onto
\ket{y_{k}}\!\!\bra{y_{k}}
and trace out the bath (also referred to as probe), yielding,

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]=\braket{y_{k}|\hat{U}_{\delta t}|0}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\braket{0|\hat{U}_{\delta t}|y_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left|\braket{y_{k}|0}\right|^{2}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]

\displaystyle+\Big(\!\braket{y_{k}|1}\!\!\braket{0|y_{k}}\!\!\hat{L\,}\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]

\displaystyle+\braket{y_{k}|0}\!\!\braket{1|y_{k}}\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\!\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\!\Big)\!\sqrt{\!M{\delta t}}

\displaystyle+\frac{M}{2}\!\Big(\!\sqrt{2}\!\braket{y_{k}|2}\!\!\braket{0|y_{k}}\hat{L\,}\!^{2}\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]

\displaystyle-\left|\braket{y_{k}|0}\right|^{2}\!\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\!\hat{L\,}\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}\!\braket{y_{k}|0}\!\!\braket{2|y_{k}}\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}](\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger})^{2}

\displaystyle-\left|\braket{y_{k}|0}\right|^{2}\!\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\!\hat{L\,}\!\!\Big){\delta t}

\displaystyle+M\!\left|\braket{y_{k}|1}\right|^{2}\hat{L\,}\!\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\hat{L\,}\!^{\dagger}\,{\delta t}+O({\delta t}^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}).

(94)

The state

\displaystyle p_{\delta t}(y_{k}|\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1})

Additionally, it is useful to also derive the probability of the measurement of the probe yielding
y_{k}

before

\displaystyle p_{0}(y_{k}|\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1})

With the definition of the operator

\displaystyle\braket{y_{k}|1}

\displaystyle=\braket{y_{k}|\hat{B}_{t}^{\dagger}|0}=\braket{y_{k}|(\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}+\hat{B}_{k})|0}=\sqrt{2}\braket{y_{k}|\hat{y}_{k}|0}

(97)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\braket{y_{k}|2}=(2y_{k}^{2}-1)\braket{y_{k}|0},

E.2
Recovering the SME (
2
) with Bayesian feedback

As discussed in Sec.
C.1
, the internal map
\Phi_{k}
can be decomposed into deterministic and stochastic components. One particular form of this decomposition arises when the evolution between measurements is described by

\Phi_{k}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}{\delta t}}.

(108)

At first glance, this expression might appear purely deterministic. However, while the map itself does not explicitly include a stochastic increment (such as a Wiener process or a jump term), it remains stochastic due to the dependence of the generator
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}
on the random measurement outcomes. Specifically, the generator is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}

\displaystyle=-i(\omega+u(t))[\hat{J}_{z},\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}]

(109)

\displaystyle+\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}_{z}^{(j)}]\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}}\,+\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}\mathcal{D}[\hat{J}_{z}]\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(c)}},

where the feedback term
u(t)\equiv u(\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
introduces the stochasticity indirectly, as it varies with the random measurement record. However, for small time steps, we can still approximate Eq. (
108
) as:

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}[\,\cdot\,]=[\,\cdot\,]+\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}[\,\cdot\,]{\delta t}+O({\delta t}^{2}).

(110)

Consequently, the unnormalized conditional state of Eq. (
E.1
) becomes:

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}[k|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]\!+\!M\mathcal{D}[\hat{L\,}\!]\rho[k\!\shortminus\!1|\boldsymbol{y}_{k\shortminus 1}]{\delta t}

Appendix G
Benchmarking against a classical strategy with a strong measurement

As demonstrated already by the numerical considerations in Sec.
VI.1
, the optimal EKF+LQR strategy not only attains the lowest aMSE in estimating the Larmor frequency
\omega
, but also steers the ensemble into a (conditionally) spin-squeezed and, hence, entangled state. However, as the continuous probing introduces also extra decoherence into the dynamics—note the term
\propto~\!\!M\mathcal{D}[\hat{J}_{y}]
in Eq. (
2
)—one may question whether such an entanglement-enhanced approach is actually beneficial. As an alternative, we consider a
classical strategy
within which the atomic ensemble evolves without being disturbed until a given time
t
, at which a
strong (destructive) measurement
is performed that can in principle provide much more information. As we now show, such an approach is deemed useless, even when considering the most general strong measurements, as it is crucial for the single-shot (Bayesian) estimation scenario for the continuous measurement to constantly gather more and more information about
\omega
over time.

G.0.1
Quantum Bayesian Cramér-Rao Bound

Considering such a classical strategy with atoms just undergoing precession and local (
\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}
) or collective (
\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}
) decoherence after being initialised in a CSS state, we can lower-bound the minimal aMSE—as defined in Eq. (
5
) but with
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
representing now the outcomes of a
single
strong measurement performed at time
t
—by resorting to a straightforward generalisation of the BCRB (
9
), i.e. the
Quantum Bayesian Cramér-Rao Bound
(QBCRB):

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}}\right]\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}[p(\omega)]+\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\rho_{t},\hat{J}_{z}]},

(134)

which now applies to any possible quantum measurement performed at time
t
, with the Fisher information
\mathcal{F}[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\omega)]
appearing in Eq. (
9
) being replaced by the
Quantum Fisher Information
(QFI)
Tóth and Apellaniz 2014
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2015
:

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\rho_{t},\hat{J}_{z}]\coloneqq t^{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\rho_{t}L^{2}\right\}=t^{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left\{\mathrm{i}[\rho_{t},\hat{J}_{z}]L\right\}

(135)

that generally satisfies
\mathcal{F}[p(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\omega)]\leq\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\rho_{t},\hat{J}_{z}]

Helstrom 1976
, but here it is also
\omega
-independent. The
L
-operator in Eq. (
135
) is the solution to
\mathrm{i}[\rho_{t},\hat{J}_{z}]=\frac{1}{2}\{\rho_{t},L\}

Helstrom 1976
.

G.0.2
Local decoherence

When only local decoherence (
\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}>0
,
\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}=0
) is present, since the initial CSS state is a product of
N
single-spin states, i.e.,
\ket{\mathrm{CSS}_{x}}=\ket{+}^{\otimes N}
, we can write the state of the atoms at time
t
as

\rho_{t}=\Lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\otimes N}\left[(|+\rangle\langle+|)^{\otimes N}\right]=\varrho_{t}^{\otimes N},

(136)

where
\varrho_{t}=\Lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\left[|+\rangle\langle+|\right]=\frac{1}{2}(1,\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}t};\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}t},1)
.
Now, as the QFI (
135
) is additive on product states
Tóth and Apellaniz 2014
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2015
, we can explicitly evaluate it for local decoherence as:

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\rho_{t},\hat{J}_{z}]=N\mathcal{F}_{Q}\!\left[\rho_{t},\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right]=Nt^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}t}.

(137)

Hence, assuming the prior distribution of
\omega
to be Gaussian with
p(\omega)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0})

134

\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}}\right]\geq\frac{1}{1/\sigma_{0}^{2}+Nt^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa_{\mathrm{loc}}t}}.

(138)

G.0.3
Collective decoherence

In the case of collective decoherence, we resort to computing the QFI (
135

\displaystyle\rho_{t}=\Lambda_{\mathrm{coll}}\!\left[\ket{\mathrm{CSS}_{x}}\!\bra{\mathrm{CSS}_{x}}\right]=

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{n,m=-J}^{J}\!\!\!b_{J,n}b_{J,m}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa_{\mathrm{coll}}\,t\,(m-n)^{2}/2}|J,n\rangle\langle J,m|,
