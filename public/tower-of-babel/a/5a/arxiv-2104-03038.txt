1.
Introduction

The
topological complexity
of a path connected space
X
was introduced by Farber (cf.
[
6
]
). It is a homotopy invariant measure of the complexity to construct a motion-planning algorithm on the space
X
. Let
I=[0,1]
and
X^{I}
denotes the free path space. Consider the fibration,

(1)

p:X^{I}\rightarrow X\times X,~~\gamma\mapsto(\gamma(0),\gamma(1)).

Then the topological complexity of
X
is defined to be the least positive integer
k
such that there exists an open cover
\{U_{1},\cdots,U_{k}\}
of
X\times X
with continuous section of
p
over each
U_{i}
(i.e. a continuous map
s_{i}:U_{i}\rightarrow X^{I}
satisfying
p\circ s_{i}=\operatorname{Id}_{U_{i}}
for
i=1,2,\cdots,k
). It is denoted by
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(X)
. Generalizing the idea, Rudyak defined higher topological complexity (cf.
[
13
]
). He introduced
n-th topological complexity

\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}(X),\ n\geq 2
such that
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{2}(X)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(X)
. We recall the definition of higher topological complexity in the next section. Farber and Grant introduced symmetric topological complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{S}(X)
of a path connected space
X
with the idea that the motion planning between
(p,q)
and
(q,p)
are inverses of each other (cf.
[
7
]
). They observed that
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{S}(X)
is not a homotopy invariant. To rectify it, Basabe, González, Rudyak, and Tamaki defined another version of symmetric topological complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}(X)
and generalized it for higher version
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(X)
(see
[
2
]
). The
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(X)
is homotopy invariant and naturally defined. In (
[
2
]
) the authors proved that this two invariants differ by at most
1
for
n=2
.

A simplicial approach of topological complexity,
simplicial complexity
, was introduced by González (cf.
[
8
]
).
It is proved there that for a finite simplicial complex
K
, the simplicial complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}(K)
and the topological complexity of its geometric realization
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(|K|)
are equal. Higher analogue of this result was obtained in
[
10
]
. In this paper we introduce
symmetric simplicial complexity

\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}(K)
and its higher version
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(K)
. We show that they are equal to symmetric topological complexity of the geometric realization of
K
. This result is a symmetric version of
[
10
, Theorem 3.5]
.

Theorem A
.

For a finite simplicial complex
K
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(K)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|K|)
, for any
n\geq 2
.

(See Theorem
3.19
)

A combinatorial analogue of topological complexity was introduced by Tanaka for a finite space (or equivalently for a finite poset, see Section
4.1
) (
[
16
]
). It is proved there that the combinatorial complexity
\text{CC}^{\infty}(P)
of a finite space
P
represent the topological complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
, where
|\mathcal{K}(P)|
denote the realization of the order complex of
P
(see Section
3.1
). This was generalized to higher combinatorial complexity in
[
10
]
. Moreover Tanaka introduced symmetric combinatorial complexities
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{S}(P)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}(P)
for a finite space
P
, where the first one is an analogue of
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{S}
and the second one is of
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}
(cf.
[
17
]
). He proved that the symmetric combinatorial complexities of
P
and symmetric topological complexities of
|\mathcal{K}(P)|
are same:
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{S}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{S}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
. Here we introduce
higher symmetric combinatorial complexity

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(P)
for a finite space
P
and generalized the second result. This can also be described as a symmetric analogue of
[
10
, Theorem 5.6]
.

Theorem B
.

For a finite space
P
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(\mathcal{K}(P))=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
,
n\geq 2
.

(See Theorem
4.20
)

1.1
.

Organization.

The organization of the rest of the paper is as follows. In Section 2, we recall basic ideas of topological complexity and symmetric topological complexity. In Section 3, we introduce symmetric simplicial complexity of a simplicial complex
K
and prove Theorem A. In Section 4, define higher symmetric combinatorial complexity of a finite space
P
and prove Theorem B.

2.
Symmetric topological complexity

In this section we first recall the definition of topological complexity and its higher versions. Then we review basic concepts of symmetric topological complexity. For details we refer to
[
6
,
5
,
13
,
7
,
2
]
.

2.1
.

Topological complexity.

Let
q:E\rightarrow B
be a fibration. The
sectional category
of
q
is the minimum integer
k
such that
B
can be cover by
k
open subsets,
U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup\cdots\cup U_{k}=B
and on each
U_{i}
there is a section
s_{i}:U_{i}\to E
of
q
. It is denoted by

\operatorname{secat}(q)
. If no such
k
exists then we say
\operatorname{secat}(q)=\infty
. Then topological complexity of
X
can be described as
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(X)=\operatorname{secat}(p)
, where
p
is the fibration of Equation
1
.

Suppose
I_{n},n\geq 2,
denote the wedge of
n
intervals
[0,1]\times\{j\}
, where
(0,j),j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
are identified. We denote
j
-th interval by
[0,1]_{j}
and the parameter in
[0,1]_{j}
by
(t,j)=t_{j}
. For any two points
t^{1}_{j},t^{2}_{j}\in[0,1]_{j}
we write
t^{2}_{j}\geq t^{1}_{j}
if and only if
t^{2}\geq t^{1}
. Consider the mapping space
X^{I_{n}}
and the fibration

(2)

e_{n}:X^{I_{n}}\rightarrow X^{n},~~~\alpha\mapsto(\alpha(1_{1}),\alpha(1_{2}),\cdots,\alpha(1_{n})).

Then the
n-th topological complexity
of
X
is defined to be
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}(X):=\operatorname{secat}(e_{n})
. It can be defined alternatively as
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}(X)=\operatorname{secat}(e^{\prime}_{n})
, where

(3)

e^{\prime}_{n}:X^{I}\rightarrow X^{n},~~~~e^{\prime}_{n}(\alpha)=(\alpha(0),\alpha(\frac{1}{n-1}),\alpha(\frac{2}{n-1}),\cdots,\alpha(1)).

This is because
e_{n}
and
e_{n}^{\prime}
are both fibrational replacement of the diagonal map
X\to X^{n}
. Clearly
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{2}(X)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(X)
.
Also it is known that
\{\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}(X)\}
is a non-decreasing sequence. If a space
Y
is homotopy equivalent to
X
, then
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}(Y)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}(X)
for any
n\geq 2
. Consequently,
X
is contractible if and only if
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}(X)=1
for any
n\geq 2
.

2.2
.

Symmetric topological complexity.

To define symmetric topological complexity we need the notion of
equivariant sectional category
. Let
G
be a finite group. A topological space
E
with an action of a group
G
is called a
G
-space. A subset
U\subset E
is called
G
-invariant
if
gU\subseteq U
for all
g\in G
. Consider another
G
-space
B
. A fibration
q:E\to B
is called
G
-fibration if
q
is a
G
-map, i.e
q(gx)=gq(x)
for all
x\in E
and
g\in G
. The
equivariant sectional category
of a
G
-fibration
q
is the minimum number
k
such that
B
can be cover by
G
-invariant open subsets,
U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup\cdots\cup U_{k}=B
and on each
U_{i}
there is a
G
-section (a section which is
G
-map) of
q
. It is denoted by
\operatorname{secat}_{G}(q)
. If no such
k
exists then we say
\operatorname{secat}_{G}(q)=\infty
.

Farber and Grant (
[
7
]
) introduced
symmetric topological complexity

\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{S}(X)
for a path connected space
X
. For symmetric motion planning they consider a section
s:X\times X\to X^{I}
(not necessarily continuous) of
p
in Equation (
1
), such a way that
s(x,x)(t)=x
and
s(x,y)(t)=s(y,x)(1-t)
, for all
x,y\in X
and
t\in I
. For this they take the subspaces
P^{\prime}X=\{\alpha\in X^{I};~\alpha(0)\neq\alpha(1)\}
and
F(X;2)=\{(x,y)\in X\times X;~x\neq y\}
and restrict the fibration
p
on
P^{\prime}X
,

p^{\prime}:P^{\prime}X\to F(X;2).

Note that
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-acts freely on both
P^{\prime}X
and
F(X;2)
by
\alpha\to\alpha^{-1}
and
(x,y)\to(y,x)
respectively and
p^{\prime}
is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-map. Also,
P^{\prime}X=p^{-1}(F(X;2))
. So
p^{\prime}
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-fibration. The symmetric topological complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{S}(X)
is defined to be
\operatorname{secat}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(p^{\prime})+1
. The extra one comes to define motion planning on the diagonal of
X\times X
. Farber and Grant noticed that
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{S}(X)
is not homotopy invariant. To overcome this problem, Basabe, González, Rudyak, and Tamaki introduced another version of symmetric topological complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}(X)
of
X
, which is easier to handle and natural (cf.
[
2
]
). They also generalized it to
higher version
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(X)
. In this paper we use Basabe, González, Rudyak, and Tamaki’s definition of symmetric topological complexity. Let us recall the definition.

Consider the symmetric group
\Sigma_{n}
of permutations of
n
symbols. For any
g,g^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{n},j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
we have
g^{\prime}\circ g(j)=g^{\prime}(g(j))
. Take the action (left) of
\Sigma_{n}
on
X^{n}
by permuting elements: that is
g(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots x_{n})=(x_{g(1)},x_{g(2)},\cdots,x_{g(n)})
. Also,
\Sigma_{n}
acts on
{I_{n}}
by
gt_{j}=t_{g(j)}
, for
t\in[0,1]
and
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. This induces an action of
\Sigma_{n}
on
X^{I_{n}}
given by
g\alpha(t_{j})=\alpha(t_{g(j)})
. So
X^{n}
and
X^{I_{n}}
are
\Sigma_{n}
-spaces and the fibration
e_{n}
in Equation
2
is a
\Sigma_{n}
-fibration.

2.3 Definition

(
[
2
]
)
.

The
symmetric topological complexity

\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(X)
of
X
is defined to be:

\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(X):=\operatorname{secat}_{\Sigma_{n}}(e_{n}).

Here a
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant subset of
X^{n}
is called
symmetric subset
and a section on a symmetric subset of
X^{n}
is called a
symmetric section
. From the definition it is clear that
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(X)\geq\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}(X)
. In (
[
2
]
), authors proved that
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(X)
is homotopy invariant, for any
n\geq 2
.

2.4 Lemma
.

Let
X
and
Y
be any two spaces. Then
n
maps
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}:X\to Y
are in same homotopy class if and only if there is a map
H:X\times I_{n}\to Y
such that
H(x,1_{j})=f_{j}(x)
for any
1\leq j\leq n
.

Proof.

Let us assume that the maps
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}:X\to Y
are in same homotopy class. Then there are homotopy maps
H_{j}:X\times I\to Y
such that
H_{j}(x,0)=f_{1}(x)
and
H_{j}(x,1)=f_{j}(x)
for
1\leq j\leq n
. That means
H_{j}
is a homotopy from
f_{1}
to
f_{j}
. Since
H_{j}
assume the value at
(x,0)
for all
j
, so we can define a map
H:X\times I_{n}\to Y
with
H(x,1_{j})=f_{j}(x)
for
1\leq j\leq n
.

Conversely, if we restrict the map
H
on union of
[0,1]_{i}
and
[0,1]_{j}
in
I_{n}
, it gives homotopy between
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
for
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
. ∎

In the above case we call
H
a
homotopy between

f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}
.

2.5 Definition
.

Assume that
\mathbf{X}
is a
\Sigma_{n}
-space and
Y
any topological space. Then maps
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}:\mathbf{X}\to Y
, satisfying
f_{j}(gx)=f_{g(j)}(x)
, are called
symmetrically homotopic
if there is a homotopy
H:\mathbf{X}\times I_{n}\to Y
between them satisfying
H(gx,t_{j})=H(x,t_{g(j)})

g\in\Sigma_{n},~x\in\mathbf{X},~t\in[0,1],~1\leq j\leq n
. In this case we call
H
a
symmetric homotopy
between
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}
. Note that the homotopy satisfies the relation
H(gx,0)=H(x,0),~~x\in\mathbf{X},~g\in\Sigma_{n}
.

Let
X
be a topological space and
A
be a
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant subset of
X^{n}
. Define
p_{j}:A\to X
as the composition
A\hookrightarrow X^{n}\to X
, where the first map is the inclusion and the second map is the projection onto the
j
-th factor. Then clearly
p_{j}(gx)=p_{g(j)}(x),~x\in A,g\in\Sigma_{n}
. We will use the following lemma to define symmetric simplicial complexity.

2.6 Lemma
.

With notations as above, the maps
\{p_{j}:~1\leq j\leq n\}
are symmetrically homotopic if and only if
e_{n}:X^{I_{n}}\to X^{n}
admits a symmetric section on
A
.

Proof.

A symmetric section
s:A\to X^{I_{n}}
of
e_{n}
satisfies,
s(gx)(t_{j})=s(x)(t_{g_{(}j)}).
A symmetric homotopy
H:A\times I_{n}\to X
between
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{n}
satisfies
H(gx,t_{j})=H(x,t_{g(j)}).
Hence if we set

s(x)(t_{j})=H(x,t_{j}),~~x\in A,t_{j}\in I_{n},

then the existence of one of
s
and
H
implies the existence of the other.
∎

2.7 Remark
.

In view of Lemma
2.6
, the symmetric topological complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(X)
can be described as the minimum integer
k
such that
X^{n}
can be covered by
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant open subsets,
U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup\cdots\cup U_{k}=X^{n}
and on each
U_{i}
composition maps
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{n}:U_{i}\hookrightarrow X^{n}\to X
are symmetrically homotopic.

The following proposition is a simple equivariant analogue of
[
10
, Proposition 2.2]
.

2.8 Proposition
.

Let
X
be an ENR. Then
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(X)=k
, where
k
is the minimal integer such that there exist a
\Sigma_{n}
-equivariant section
s:X^{n}\rightarrow X^{I_{n}}
(which is not necessarily continuous) of the fibration
e_{n}
and a splitting
G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup G_{r}=X^{n}
, where each
G_{i}
is locally compact and
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant subset of
X^{n}
and each restriction
s_{|G_{i}}:G_{i}\rightarrow X^{I_{n}}
is continuous for
i=1,2,\cdots,r
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to
[
10
, Proposition 2.2]
, only here the sections are
\Sigma_{n}
-map and the subsets are
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant.
∎

Combining Remark
2.7
and Proposition
2.8
, we have the following.

2.9 Corollary
.

Let
X
be an ENR. Then
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(X)
is the minimal integer
k
such that there exist a splitting
G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup G_{r}=X^{n}
with each
G_{i}
is locally compact and
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant subset of
X^{n}
and on each
G_{i}
the composition of inclusion with the projections maps
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{n}:G_{i}\hookrightarrow X^{n}\to X
are symmetrically homotopic.

3.
Symmetric simplicial complexity

González introduced the notion of simplicial complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}(K)
for simplicial complex
K
(cf.
[
8
]
). This is based on contiguity of simplicial maps. It is proved in that simplicial complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}(K)
is equal to the topological complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(|K|)
of the geometric realization of
K
, for a finite simplicial complex
K
. This has been generalized for higher simplicial complexity (cf.
[
10
]
). In this section we first recall some basic ideas on simplicial complexes and simplicial complexity. After that we introduce symmetric simplicial complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}(K)
and its higher version
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(K)
. Finally we show that
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(K)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|K|)
.

3.1
.

Simplicial complexes.

We begin by recalling some basic ideas of simplicial complexes (
[
3
,
4
,
14
]
). A
simplicial complex

K
consists of of a set
V(K)
, called
vertices
and a set
S(K)
of finite nonempty subsets of of
V(K)
, called
simplexes
such that,

(a)

Singleton subsets of
V(K)
is a simplex.

(b)

Any non empty subset of a simplex is a simplex.

We say
K
is a finite simplicial complex if the set
V(K)
is finite. A set
\sigma\in S(K)
with
q+1
elements is called a
q
-
simplex
and if
\sigma^{\prime}\subset\sigma
then
\sigma^{\prime}
is called a
face
of
\sigma
. A
simplicial map

\phi:K\to L
is a function from the vertices of
K
to the vertices of
L
such that for any simplex
\sigma=\{v_{0},v_{1},\cdots,v_{q}\}
in
K
the image
\phi(\sigma)=\{\phi(v_{0}),\phi(v_{1}),\cdots,\phi(v_{q})\}
is a simplex in
L
(possibly of lower dimension). For a poset
P
the
order complex

\mathcal{K}(P)
is the simplicial complex whose vertex set is
P
and simplexes are totally ordered finite subsets of
P
. The simplex set
S(K)
is naturally a poset with inclusion of faces, called
face poset
and we denote it by
\mathcal{X}(K)
. The order complex of
\mathcal{X}(K)
is called the
barycentric subdivision

\operatorname{sd}(K)
of
K
. Thus the set of vertices of
\operatorname{sd}(K)
is the set
S(K)
and a
q
-simplex of
\operatorname{sd}(K)
is a chain
\sigma_{0}\subsetneq\sigma_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\sigma_{q}
of face inclusions of simplexes of
K
. For any simplicial complex
K
the
geometric realization

|K|
is the set of all functions
\alpha:V(K)\to I=[0,1]
such that: (i) for any
\alpha
, the set
\{v\in V(K);\alpha(v)\neq 0\}\in S(K)
, (ii) for any
\alpha,\sum_{v\in V(K)}\alpha(v)=1
. Then the linear map
|\operatorname{sd}(K)|\to|K|
takes each vertex of
\operatorname{sd}(K)
to the corresponding point of
|K|
which is a homeomorphism. For any vertex
v\in V(K)
the
open star
of
v
denoted by
\operatorname{st}(v)
and defined as
\operatorname{st}(v)=\{\alpha\in|K|;\alpha(v)\neq 0\}
. Recall that a vertex map
\phi:V(K)\to V(L)
is a simplicial approximation of a continuous map
f:|K|\to|L|
if and only if
f(\operatorname{st}(v))\subset\operatorname{st}(\phi(v))
for all
v\in V(K).

The categorical product of simplicial complexes do not possess the desired property that
|K\times L|=|K|\times|L|.
To overcome this we need the notion of ordered simplicial complex (cf.
[
4
,
11
]
).

3.2 Definition
.

An
ordered simplicial complex

K
is a simplicial complex
K
together with a partial order on its set of vertices, restricting to a total order on each of its simplices. The simplexes are denoted by ordered sets
\{v_{0}\leq v_{1}\leq\cdots\leq v_{q}\}.

3.3 Example
.

(a)

Every simplicial complex
K
can be thought as an ordered simplicial complex by selecting a total order on its vertex set.

(b)

The order complex of a poset is an ordered simplicial complex.

(c)

In particular, for any simplicial complex
K
, the subdivision
\operatorname{sd}(K)
is an ordered simplicial complex structure with respect to inclusion of faces ordering.

In general, in an ordered simplicial complex vertices of each simplex is totally ordered but a totally ordered finite subset of vertices may not be a simplex.

3.4 Definition
.

The
cartesian product

K\times L
of two ordered simplicial complexes
K
and
L
is also an ordered simplicial complex whose vertex set is
V(K)\times V(L)
with partial order given by
(u_{1},v_{1})\leq(u_{2},v_{2})
if and only if
u_{1}\leq u_{2}
and
v_{1}\leq v_{2}
. An ordered set
\{(u_{0},v_{0})\leq(u_{1},v_{1})\leq\cdots\leq(u_{q},v_{q})\}
is a
q
-simplex in
K\times L
if
\{u_{0}\leq u_{1}\leq\cdots\leq u_{q}\}
and
\{v_{0}\leq v_{1}\leq\cdots\leq v_{q}\}
are simplexes of
K
and
L
respectively.

Then the projection maps
p_{1}:K\times L\to K
and
p_{2}:K\times L\to L
induces homeomorphism
|p_{1}|\times|p_{2}|:|K\times L|\to|K|\times|L|
. In particular
|K^{n}|=|K|^{n}.

The notion of homotopy of continuous maps is replaced by
contiguity
of simplicial maps (see
[
14
]
). For a positive integer
c
, two simplicial maps
\phi,\phi^{\prime}:K\rightarrow L
are called
c
-
contiguous
if there is a sequence of simplicial maps
\phi=\phi^{0},\phi^{1},\phi^{2}\cdots,\phi^{c}=\phi^{\prime}:K\rightarrow L
, such that
\phi^{i-1}(\sigma)\cup\phi^{i}(\sigma)
is a simplex of
L
for each simplex
\sigma
of
K
and

. We write
\phi\sim\phi^{\prime}
if
\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
are

-contiguous for some positive integer

. This defines an equivalence relation on the set of simplicial maps

and the equivalence classes are called

. If
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\cdots,\phi_{n}:K\to L
are simplicial maps belonging to the same contiguity class then there is a simplicial map
\phi_{*}:K\to L
and a positive integer
c
such that each
\phi_{j}
is
c
-contiguous with
\phi_{*}
. Simplicial approximations of a same continuous map are
1
-contiguous, i.e, they are in same contiguity class. For any simplicial complexes

, if the simplicial maps
\phi,\phi^{\prime}:K\to L
are
c
-contiguous, then for any simplicial maps
\psi:J\to K
,
\theta:L\to M
, the composition simplicial maps
\theta\circ\phi\circ\psi,\theta\circ\phi^{\prime}\circ\psi:J\to K\to L\to M
are also

-contiguous.

3.5
.

Equivariant simplicial complexes.

Let
G
be a finite group. A

G
-complex
is a simplicial complex

with simplicial
G

G

such that for a simplex

is also a simplex (of same dimension). In addition, if

an

G
-complex
. A

between simplicial

is a simplicial map

such that

is
G
-equivariant map on the vertex set (cf.
[

]
,
[

]
).

.

(a)

\Sigma_{n}
-complex.

(b)

Let

3.8 Definition

3.9 Lemma
.

(
[

, Lemma 3.5.2]
,
[

Simplicial maps

\Sigma_{n}
-complex and

Proof.

Assume that
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\cdots,\phi_{n}:K\to L

and a simplicial map
\phi_{*}:K\to L
such that for each

\phi_{*}
is

-contiguous with

via the simplicial maps
\phi_{*}=\phi^{0}_{j},\phi^{1}_{j},\phi^{2}_{j},\cdots,\phi^{c}_{j}=\phi_{j}:K\to L

H(x,t_{j})=c\Big(\frac{l+1}{c}-t\Big)\big(|\phi^{l}_{j}|(x)\big)+c\Big(t-\frac{l}{c}\Big)\big(|\phi^{l+1}_{j}|(x)\big),\ \text{for}\ t_{j}\in\Big[\frac{l}{c},\frac{l+1}{c}\Big]_{j}

where

and

in

j
we define an another map
f_{j}:|L_{i}|\times I\to K
by
f_{j}(x,t)=tp_{j}(x)+(1-t)(|\pi_{j}|(x))
, where
p_{j}
is the composition
|L_{i}|\hookrightarrow|K|^{n}\to K
. Now consider the homotopy
F:|L_{i}|\times I_{n}\to K
is defined by: for each
x
, the path
F(x,t_{j})
is the concatenation
(h_{j}*f_{j})(x,t)
,

(h_{j}*f_{j})(x,t)=\begin{cases}h_{j}(x,2t)&\mbox{ if }~~t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]\\
f_{j}(x,2t-1)&\mbox{if }~~t\in[\frac{1}{2},1].\end{cases}

By assumption
h_{j}(gx,t)=h_{g(j)}(x,t)
and by definition of
f_{j},f_{j}(gx,t)=f_{g(j)}(x,t)
for any
g\in\Sigma_{n}
and
F(x,1_{j})=p_{j}(x)
. So
F
is a symmetric homotopy and
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{n}
are symmetrically homotopic on
|L_{i}|
. Now we set
G_{1}=|L_{1}|
and
G_{i}=|L_{i}|-(|L_{1}|\cup|L_{2}|\cup\cdots\cup|L_{i-1}|)
for
i\geq 2
. Then each
G_{i}
is locally compact,
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant and
|K^{n}|=\sqcup_{i}G_{i}
. The restrictions of
p_{j}
, for
j=1,2,\cdots,n,
on each
G_{i}
are also symmetrically homotopic. From Corollary
2.9
, we have
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|K|)\leq k
. So
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|K|)\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(K)
, for any
r\geq 0
and
n\geq 2
. ∎

As in the case of simplicial complexity, now we show that
\{\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(K)\}_{r}
is a decreasing sequence.

3.17 Lemma
.

For a simplicial complex
K
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(K)\geq\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,r+1}(K)
,
r\geq 0,~~n\geq 2
.

Proof.

Let
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(K)=k
and
\{L_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a symmetric subcomplexes covering
\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})
such that the restrictions
\pi_{j}:L_{i}\rightarrow K

1\leq j\leq n,
are symmetrically contiguous, for each
i
. So there is a contiguity chain
\pi_{*}=\pi^{0}_{j},\pi^{1}_{j},\cdots,\pi^{c}_{j}=\pi_{j}:L_{i}\to K
such that
\pi_{j}^{l}(gv)=\pi_{g(j)}^{l}(v)
and
\pi_{*}(gv)=\pi_{*}(v)

v\in V(L_{i})
,
l\in\{1,2,\cdots,c\}
. Take the subcomplexes
\{\operatorname{sd}(L_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
which are symmetric and covers
\operatorname{sd}^{r+1}(K^{n})
. We choose a
\Sigma_{n}
-approximation
\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}:\operatorname{sd}^{r+1}(K^{n})\rightarrow\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})
of the identity on
|K|^{n}
. Clearly, for each
L_{i}
, the sequence of maps
\pi_{*}\circ\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}=\pi^{0}_{j}\circ\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})},\pi^{1}_{j}\circ\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})},\cdots,\pi^{c}_{j}\circ\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}=\pi_{j}\circ\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}:\operatorname{sd}(L_{i})\to K
for
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
give a contiguity chain. Since
\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}
is a
\Sigma_{n}
-map, for any vertex
v
of
\operatorname{sd}(L_{i})
,
l\in\{1,2,\cdots,c\}
we have,

\pi_{*}\circ\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}(gv)=\pi_{*}(g\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}(v))=\pi_{*}(\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}(v))=\pi_{*}\circ\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}(v),

\pi_{j}^{l}\circ\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}(gv)=\pi_{j}^{l}(g\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}(v))=\pi_{g(j)}^{l}(\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}(v))=\pi_{g(j)}^{l}\circ\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}(v).

This implies that the restrictions
\pi_{j}\circ\iota_{\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})}:\operatorname{sd}(L_{i})\rightarrow K
, for
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
are symmetrically contiguous, for each
i
. So
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,r+1}\leq k
and therefore
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(K)\geq\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,r+1}(K)
.
∎

Above Lemma allows us to make the following definition.

3.18 Definition
.

For a simplicial complex
K
, the
n
-th symmetric simplicial complexity
or simply
symmetric simplicial complexity
is defined as:

\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(K)=\min_{r\geq 0}\{\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(K)\}.

The main theorem of this section is the following.

3.19 Theorem
.

For a finite simplicial complex
K
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(K)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|K|)
,
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.16
, it is clear that
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(K)\geq\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|K|)
. Now we prove the other inequality. Let
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|K|)=k
. Using Remark
2.7
we get an symmetric open cover
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of
|K|^{n}
such that the composition maps
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{n}:U_{i}\hookrightarrow|K|^{n}\to|K|
are symmetrically homotopic for each
i
. Since
K
is finite,
|K|
is compact and so is
|K|^{n}=|K^{n}|
. Therefore by Lebesgue lemma of compact metric spaces, there exists
\delta>0
such that any set of diameter less than
\delta
is contained in one of the open sets
U_{i}
. Since, with increasing subdivision, the diameter of simplices goes to
0
, there is a large integer
r\geq 0
such that realization of each simplex
\sigma
of
\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})
contained one of
U_{i}
. Let
L_{i}
be the subcomplex of
\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})
consisting of those simplices whose realization contained in
U_{i}
. For each
i
, the set is
U_{i}
is
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant and so for each simplex
\sigma
of
L_{i}
,
g|\sigma|
contained in
U_{i}
, for any
g\in\Sigma_{n}
. Therefore
g\sigma
is a simplex of
L_{i}
. Thus each subcomplex
L_{i}
is symmetric. Also
\{L_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
covers
\operatorname{sd}^{r}(K^{n})
. Since the maps
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{n}:U_{i}\hookrightarrow|K|^{n}\to|K|
are symmetrically homotopic, their restriction on
|L_{i}|
,
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{n}:|L_{i}|\hookrightarrow|K|^{n}\to|K|
are also symmetrically homotopic. Now by Proposition
3.10
there is positive integer
r_{0}
and approximations
\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\cdots,\pi_{n}:\operatorname{sd}^{r_{0}}(L_{i})\hookrightarrow\operatorname{sd}^{r+r_{0}}(K^{n})\rightarrow K
of
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{n}
respectively such that they are symmetrically contiguous, for each
i
. Therefore
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(K)\leq k
and hence
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(K)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|K|)
.
∎

4.
Symmetric combinatorial complexity

Tanaka introduced a combinatorial approach of topological complexity (cf.
[
16
]
). The basic idea of Tanaka’s paper is to describe topological complexity by combinatorics of finite posets. He used a combinatorial analogue of the path-space fibration of the Equation
1
to define
combinatorial complexity

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{0}(P)
of a finite poset
P
, where zero means no barycentric subdivision of
P\times P
is involved. It is shown that
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{0}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(P)
but
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{0}(P)
does not capture
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
, where
\mathcal{K}(P)
is the
order complex
of
P
. To describe
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
combinatorially, Tanaka used barycentric subdivision of
P\times P
to define combinatorial complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}(P)
. Finally it is shown that for a large barycentric subdivision of
P\times P
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
. The above idea has been generalized to higher versions and shown that for a finite poset
P
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
(cf.
[
10
]
). Tanaka further defined a combinatorial analog of symmetric topological complexities
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{S}(X)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}(X)
for a finite poset
P
(
[
17
]
). We denote this symmetric combinatorial complexities by
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{S}(P)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}(P)
respectively. Tanaka there also used barycentric subdivision of
P\times P
to define these symmetric combinatorial complexities. For a large barycentric subdivision of
P\times P
the value is stable and he denotes this stable value by
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{S}(P)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}(P)
and proved that
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{S}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{S}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
. In this section we first recall some basic ideas of finite poset and its connection with finite spaces. Then we recall combinatorial complexity and symmetric combinatorial complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}(P)
of a finite poset
P
. Finally we introduce higher symmetric combinatorial complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(P)
and prove
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
.

4.1
.

Finite poset and finite space.

We begin by recalling the relation between finite poset and finite space. We refer reader to
[
15
]
for this. Let
P
be a finite poset. We denote
U_{x}=\{y:~~y\leq x\}
. Then
\{U_{x};x\in P\}
generates a
T_{0}
topology on the finite set
P
. On the other hand, given a finite
T_{0}
topological space
P
, let
U_{x}
denotes the intersection of all open sets containing
x,
where
x\in P
. Then we can consider
P
as a poset, the partial relation on
P
, defined by
x\leq y
if and only if
U_{x}\subseteq U_{y}
. Thus a finite poset is equivalent to a finite
T_{0}
space. We will simply write finite space to mean a finite
T_{0}
space. From now onwards we assume all our finite spaces are connected. A map between finite spaces is continuous if and only if it preserves the partial order. Given two finite spaces
P,Q
, we denote by
Q^{P}
the space of maps
P\to Q
with the compact-open topology. This finite
T_{0}
-space corresponds to the set of order preserving maps
P\to Q
with the pointwise ordering, i.e.
f\leq g
if
f(x)\leq g(x)
for every
x\in P
.

4.2 Example
.

Let
K
be a finite simplicial complex. Recall that the face poset
\mathcal{X}(K)
is the collection of all simplices in
K
with the partial order of face inclusions. For any simplex
\sigma
of
K
we have

U_{\sigma}=\{\sigma^{\prime};~\sigma^{\prime}\leq\sigma\}=\{\sigma^{\prime};~\sigma^{\prime}\text{ is a face of}~\sigma\}=\mathcal{X}(\sigma).

So
\{\mathcal{X}(\sigma);~\sigma\text{ is a simplex of}~K\}
generates a
T_{0}
topology on
\mathcal{X}(K)
.

4.3 Remark
.

For any subcomplex
L
of a finite simplicial complex
K
, we have

\mathcal{X}(L)=\bigcup_{\sigma\in S(L)}\mathcal{X}(\sigma).

So by Example
4.2
we can say that
\mathcal{X}(L)
is open in
\mathcal{X}(K)
.

Let
J_{m}
denotes the finite space consisting of
m+1
points with the zigzag order

0\leq 1\geq 2\leq\cdots\geq(\leq)m
.

This finite space is called the
finite fence
with length
m
. It behaves like an interval in the category of finite spaces. An order preserving map
\gamma:J_{m}\rightarrow P
is called a combinatorial path or simply a path. Thus a combinatorial path is just a zigzag
\gamma(0)\leq\gamma(1)\geq\gamma(2)\leq\cdots\geq(\leq)\gamma(m)
of elements of
P
. If
m
is even, inverse of a path
\gamma:J_{m}\rightarrow P
defined by
\gamma^{-1}:J_{m}\rightarrow P,\gamma^{-1}(i)=\gamma(m-i)
. A connected finite space is always path-connected. Two maps
f,g:P\rightarrow Q
between two finite spaces are called
combinatorially homotopic
if there exist
m\geq 0
and a continuous map (or equivalently order preserving map)
H:P\times J_{m}\rightarrow Q
such that
H(x,0)=f(x)
and
H(x,m)=g(x)
, i.e., there is a fence
f=f_{0}\leq f_{1}\geq f_{2}\leq\cdots f_{n}=g
.

For
n\geq 2
we denote
J_{n,m}
be the finite poset of
nm+1
points

\{0,1_{1},1_{2},\cdots,1_{n},2_{1},2_{2},\cdots,2_{n},\cdots,m_{1},m_{2},\cdots,m_{n}\}.

The partial ordering on
J_{n,m}
consists of
n
finite fences, each of length
m
, as below:

0\leq 1_{1}\geq 2_{1}\leq\cdots\geq(\leq)m_{1},

0\leq 1_{2}\geq 2_{2}\leq\cdots\geq(\leq)m_{2},

\cdots

0\leq 1_{n}\geq 2_{n}\leq\cdots\geq(\leq)m_{n}.

We use the parameter
t_{j}
for the
j
-th fence.

4.4 Definition
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}:P\to Q
be
n
order preserving maps between two finite spaces. Then we say
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}
are
combinatorially homotopic
if there exists
m\geq 0
and an order preserving map
H:P\times J_{n,m}\rightarrow Q
such that
H(x,m_{j})=f_{j}(x)
for
x\in P
and
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. Moreover, if
P
is a
\Sigma_{n}
-space then we called
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}
are
symmetrically combinatorially homotopic
if the maps satisfies
f_{j}(gx)=f_{g(j)}(x)
and the homotopy map satisfy
H(gx,t_{j})=H(x,t_{g(j)})
for any
g\in\Sigma_{n},x\in P
and
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. In this case the homotopy is called
symmetric combinatorial homotopy
.

The following lemma is a generalization of
[
17
, Proposition 2.2]
.

4.5 Lemma
.

Let
P
be a finite
\Sigma_{n}
-space and
Q
be arbitrary finite space. Then any maps
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}:P\to Q
are symmetrically homotopic if and only if they are symmetrically combinatorially homotopic.

Proof.

Assume that
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}:P\to Q
are symmetrically homotopic. Then get a symmetric homotopy
H:P\times I_{n}\to Q
. By homotopy theory of finite spaces, there is a
\Sigma_{n}
-map
h:J_{n,m}\to I_{n}
such that
j
-th fence maps to
j
-th interval and
m_{j}
maps to
1_{j}
, for some
m>0
and
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. Define
H^{\prime}
as the composition map
H^{\prime}:P\times J_{n,m}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{Id}\times h}P\times I_{n}\xrightarrow{H}Q
. Then
H^{\prime}
is a symmetric combinatorial homotopy between
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}
.

Conversely, assume that there is a symmetric combinatorial homotopy
H^{\prime}:P\times J_{n,m}\to Q
between
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}
. We define a
\Sigma_{n}
-map
h^{\prime}:I_{n}\to J_{n,m}
such that each
j
-th interval of
I_{n}
maps to
j
-th fence of
J_{n,m}
given by the equation

h^{\prime}(t)=\begin{cases}2k-1&\mbox{ if }~~t=\frac{2k-1}{m},\\
2k&\mbox{if }~~\frac{2k-1}{m}<t<\frac{2k+1}{m},\end{cases}

for
k=0,1,2,\cdots
.
Then the composition map
H:P\times I_{n}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{Id}\times h^{\prime}}P\times J_{n,m}\xrightarrow{H}Q
gives a symmetric homotopy between
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}
.
∎

Now onwards, we will use symmetrical homotopy to mean any one of the above interpretations. Let us define
barycentric subdivision
of a finite space
P
.

4.6 Definition
.

The
barycentric subdivision
of a finite space
P
is defined as the face poset
\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{K}(P))
of the order complex
\mathcal{K}(P)
(see Section
3.1
). It is denoted by
\operatorname{sd}(P)
.

The following Proposition is a symmetric version for
n
maps of
[
1
, Lemma 4.10 and Proposition 4.11]
.

4.7 Proposition
.

Let
P
be a finite
\Sigma_{n}
-space and
Q
be any finite space. Assume that the maps
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{n}:P\to Q
are symmetrically homotopic. Then the simplicial maps
\mathcal{K}(f_{1}),\mathcal{K}(f_{2}),\cdots,\mathcal{K}(f_{n}):\mathcal{K}(P)\to\mathcal{K}(Q)
are symmetrically contiguous.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that there is symmetric homotopy
F:J_{n,1}\times P\to Q
. We write
f_{j}^{0}(x)=F(x,0_{j})\leq F(x,1_{j})=f_{j}(x)
for all
x\in P
. Then we have
f_{j}^{0}(gx)=f_{g(j)}^{0}(x)
and
f_{j}(gx)=f_{g(j)}(x)
for any
j
and any
x\in P,g\in\Sigma_{n}
. We prove our result in four steps. First we construct sequences of order preserving maps
f_{j}^{0},f_{j}^{1},f_{j}^{2},\cdots,f_{j}^{m}=f_{j}:P\to Q
.

Step I:
Here we define the maps
f_{j}^{1}:P\to Q
. Define
A_{j}=\{x\in P;f_{j}^{0}(x)\neq f_{j}(x)\}
. Note that
A_{g(j)}=g^{-1}A_{j}
. So if one of
A_{j}
’s is empty the all
A_{j}
are empty and in this case
f_{j}^{1}=f_{j}
. Assume that
A_{1}
is non empty. Then we define
f_{j}^{1}:P\to Q
by,

f_{j}^{1}(x)=\begin{cases}f_{j}(x)&\mbox{ if}~~x\in A_{j}\mbox{ and it is a maximal element of }A_{j}\\
f_{j}^{0}(x)&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We claim that the maps
f_{j}^{1}
are order preserving. Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in P
with
x_{2}\geq x_{1}
. If none of them is maximal element of
A_{j}
then
f_{j}^{1}(x_{2})\geq f_{j}^{1}(x_{1})
, since
f_{j}^{0}
is order preserving. If
x_{2}
is maximal element of
A_{j}
and
x_{1}
is any other element of
P
with
x_{2}\geq x_{1}
(so
x_{1}
can not be maximal of
A_{j}
) then
f_{j}^{1}(x_{2})=f_{j}(x_{2})\geq f_{j}(x_{1})\geq f_{j}^{0}(x_{1})=f_{j}^{1}(x_{1})
. Lastly if
x_{1}
is maximal element of
A_{j}
and
x_{2}(\notin A_{j})\ x_{1}
then
f_{j}^{1}(x_{2})=f_{j}^{0}(x_{2})=f_{j}^{1}(x_{2})\geq f_{j}^{1}(x_{1})=f_{j}^{1}(x_{1})
. So the maps
f_{j}^{1}
are order preserving.

Step II:
We now show that
f_{j}^{1}(gx)=f_{g(j)}^{1}(x)
for any
j
and any
x\in P,g\in\Sigma_{n}
. It is clear that
gx
is a maximal element of
A_{j}
if and only if
x
is a maximal element of
g^{-1}A_{j}=A_{g(j)}
. If
gx
is maximal element of
A_{j}
then
f_{j}^{1}(gx)=f_{j}(gx)=f_{g(j)}(x)=f_{g(j)}^{1}(x)
. Also if
gx
is not a maximal element of
A_{j}
then
f_{j}^{1}(gx)=f_{j}^{0}(gx)=f_{g(j)}^{0}(x)=f_{g(j)}^{1}(x)
. So we have
f_{j}^{1}(gx)=f_{g(j)}^{1}(x)
holds for any
j
and any
x\in P,g\in\Sigma_{n}
.

Step III:
Now we repeat this construction. We use
f_{j}^{1}
and
f_{j}
to define
f_{j}^{2}
, and use
f_{j}^{2}
and
f_{j}
to define
f_{j}^{3}
and so on. By finiteness of
P
and
Q
this process will end and we get the order preserving maps
f_{j}^{0},f_{j}^{1},f_{j}^{2},\cdots,f_{j}^{m}=f_{j}:P\to Q
such that
f_{j}^{l}(gx)=f_{g(j)}^{l}(x)
for any
1\leq j\leq n,0\leq l\leq m,g\in\Sigma_{n}
and
x\in P
.

Step IV:
Now we show that the simplicial maps
\mathcal{K}(f_{1}),\mathcal{K}(f_{2}),\cdots,\mathcal{K}(f_{n}):\mathcal{K}(P)\to\mathcal{K}(Q)
are symmetrically contiguous. Let
C=\{x_{0}\leq x_{1}\leq,\cdots,\leq x_{c}\}
be a chain in
P
. Since the set
\{x\in P;f_{j}^{l-1}(x)\neq f_{j}(x)\}
has at most one maximal element in
C
, so by definition of
f_{j}^{l}
we have,
f_{j}^{l-1}
and
f_{j}^{l}
differ by at most one element on
C
(say
x_{p}
). Thus we have

f_{j}^{l-1}(x_{0})=f_{j}^{l}(x_{0})\leq\cdots\leq f_{j}^{l-1}(x_{p})\leq f_{j}^{l}(x_{p})\leq\cdots\leq f_{j}^{l-1}(x_{c})=f_{j}^{l}(x_{c}).

Therefore for any chain
C
in P,
f_{j}^{l-1}(C)\cup f_{j}^{l}(C)
is also a chain in
Q
. In other words for any simplex
\sigma
of
\mathcal{K}(P)
,
\mathcal{K}(f_{j}^{l-1})(\sigma)\cup\mathcal{K}(f_{j}^{l})(\sigma)
is a simplex in
\mathcal{K}(Q)
. So
\mathcal{K}(f_{j}^{l-1})(\sigma)
and
\mathcal{K}(f_{j}^{l})(\sigma)
are one contiguous. Also
f_{j}^{l}(gx)=f_{g(j)}^{l}(x)
implies
\mathcal{K}(f_{j}^{l})(gx)=\mathcal{K}(f_{g(j)}^{l})(x)
holds for any
x\in V(\mathcal{K}(P))\simeq P
. So we can say that the simplicial maps
\mathcal{K}(f_{1}),\mathcal{K}(f_{2}),\cdots,\mathcal{K}(f_{n}):\mathcal{K}(P)\to\mathcal{K}(Q)
are symmetrically contiguous.
∎

To prove our main theorem of this section, we need the following lemma. It is a generalised version of
[
17
, Lemma 2.4]
.

4.8 Lemma
.

Let
K
be a finite simplicial
\Sigma_{n}
-complex and the maps
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\cdots,\phi_{n}:K\to L
are symmetrically contiguous. Then the induced maps
\mathcal{X}(\phi_{j}):\mathcal{X}(K)\to\mathcal{X}(L)
are symmetrically homotopic.

Proof.

Assume that there is a chain of contiguous maps
\phi_{*}=\phi_{j}^{0},\phi_{j}^{1},\cdots,\phi_{j}^{c}=\phi_{j}:K\to L
such that
\phi_{*}(gv)=\phi_{*}(v)
and
\phi_{j}^{l}(gv)=\phi_{g(j)}(v)
for
1\leq l\leq c,1\leq j\leq n,v\in V(K)
and
g\in\Sigma_{n}
. For each
l
and
j
we define

h_{j}^{l}:\mathcal{X}(K)\to\mathcal{X}(L),~~h_{j}^{l}(\sigma)=\phi_{j}^{l-1}(\sigma)\cup\phi_{j}^{l}(\sigma),

for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}(K)
. Since
\mathcal{X}(\phi_{j}^{l-1})(\sigma)=\phi_{j}^{l-1}(\sigma)
and
\mathcal{X}(\phi_{j}^{l})(\sigma)=\phi_{j}^{l}(\sigma)
, so
\mathcal{X}(\phi_{j}^{l-1})\leq h_{j}^{l}\geq\mathcal{X}(\phi_{j}^{l})
. We define a combinatorial homotopy
H:\mathcal{X}(K)\times J_{n,2c}\to\mathcal{X}(L)
by,

H(\sigma,m_{j})=\begin{cases}h_{j}^{\frac{m+1}{2}}(\sigma)&\mbox{ if }~~m\mbox{ is odd,}\\
\mathcal{X}\big(\phi_{j}^{\frac{m}{2}}\big)(\sigma)&\mbox{ if }~~~~m\mbox{ is even}.\end{cases}

Now
\mathcal{X}(\phi_{j}^{l-1})\leq h_{j}^{l}\geq\mathcal{X}(\phi_{j}^{l})
implies
H
is order preserving and
H(\sigma,2c_{j})=\mathcal{X}(\phi_{j}^{c})(\sigma)=\phi_{j}(\sigma)
. Also

\mathcal{X}(\phi_{j}^{l})(g\sigma)=\phi_{j}^{l}(g\sigma)=\phi_{g(j)}^{l}(\sigma)=\mathcal{X}(\phi_{g(j)}^{l})(\sigma),

and

h_{j}^{l}(g\sigma)=\phi_{j}^{l-1}(g\sigma)\cup\phi_{j}^{l}(g\sigma)=\phi_{g(j)}^{l-1}(\sigma)\cup\phi_{g(j)}^{l}(\sigma)=h_{g(j)}^{l}(\sigma).

Hence
H
is a symmetric homotopy between
\mathcal{X}(\phi_{j}),~1\leq j\leq n
.
∎

4.9
.

Combinatorial complexity.

Here we recall the basics of higher combinatorial complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}(P)
as introduced in (
[
10
]
). Consider the mapping space
P^{J{n,m}}
, and the canonical order preserving map

(5)

q_{n,m}:P^{J_{n,m}}\rightarrow P^{n},~~q_{n,m}(\gamma)=(\gamma(m_{1}),\gamma(m_{2}),\cdots,\gamma(m_{n})).

Note that Tanaka considered the following map to define
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{2}(P)
,

q_{m}:P^{J_{m}}\rightarrow P\times P\text{ defined by }q_{m}(\gamma)=(\gamma(0),\gamma(m)),~~m\geq 0.

Let
\tau_{P^{n}}:\operatorname{sd}(P^{n})\rightarrow P^{n}
be the map sending
p_{0}\leq p_{1}\leq\cdots\leq p_{n}
to the last element
p_{n}
. This is a weak homotopy equivalence, and the induced simplicial map
\mathcal{K}(\tau_{P^{n}}):\mathcal{K}(\operatorname{sd}(P^{n}))=\operatorname{sd}(\mathcal{K}(P^{n}))\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(P^{n})
is a simplicial approximation of the identity on
|\mathcal{K}(P^{n})|
(see
[
9
]
). For
r\geq 0
, we define

(6)

\tau_{P^{n}}^{r}:\operatorname{sd}^{r}(P^{n})\rightarrow P^{n}

as the composition

\operatorname{sd}^{r}(P^{n})\xrightarrow{\tau_{\operatorname{sd}^{r-1}(P^{n})}}\operatorname{sd}^{r-1}(P^{n})\xrightarrow{\tau_{\operatorname{sd}^{r-2}(P^{n})}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\tau_{\operatorname{sd}(P^{n})}}\operatorname{sd}(P^{n})\xrightarrow{\tau_{P^{n}}}P^{n}
.

Note that
\tau_{P^{n}}^{0}
is identity map on
P^{n}
. For
r\geq 0
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{r}(P)
is defined to be the smallest non-negative integer
k
such that there exist an open cover
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of
\operatorname{sd}^{r}(P^{n})
and an positive integer
m
, with a map
s_{i}:Q_{i}\rightarrow P^{J_{n,m}}
such that
q_{n,m}\circ s_{i}=\tau_{P^{n}}^{r}
on
Q_{i}
for each
i
. If no such
k
exists, then
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{r}(P)=\infty
. The sequence
\{\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{r}(P)\}
is a decreasing sequence on
r
. So we have the following definition for
n
-th combinatorial complexity
.

4.10 Definition
.

The
n
-th combinatorial complexity

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}(P)
of
P
is defined as:

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}(P)=\min_{r\geq 0}\{\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{r}(P)\}.

We have the following theorem.

4.11 Theorem

(
[
10
]
)
.

For any finite space
P
, we have
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)
,
n\geq 2
.

4.12
.

Symmetric combinatorial complexity.

In (
[
17
]
), Tanaka defined symmetric combinatorial complexity
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}(P)
for a finite space
P
. He considered the mapping space
P^{J_{2m}}
and the canonical map

q_{2m}:P^{J_{2m}}\rightarrow P\times P,~~~q_{2m}(\gamma)=(\gamma(0),\gamma(2m)),~~m\geq 0.

Note that
P^{J_{2m}}
and
P\times P
both are
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-spaces where the non trivial action is given by
\gamma\to\gamma^{-1}
and
(x,y)\to(y,x)
. Also
q_{2m}
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-map. Tanaka defined
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{2,2m}^{\Sigma,r}(P)
as the smallest non-negative integer
k
such that there exist
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-invariant open cover
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of
\operatorname{sd}^{r}(P\times P)
and on each
Q_{i}
there is a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-map
s_{i}:Q_{i}\rightarrow P^{J_{2m}}
with
q_{2m}\circ s_{i}=\tau_{P\times P}^{r}
. If no such
k
exists, then
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{2,2m}^{\Sigma,r}(P)=\infty
. He proved that
\{\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{2,2m}^{\Sigma,r}(P)\}
is a decreasing sequence on
m
and
r
both. He defined the
symmetric combinatorial complexity

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}(P)
as the limit of
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{2,2m}^{\Sigma,r}(P)
as
m\to\infty,r\to\infty
.

Let us define
n
-th symmetric combinatorial complexity. Here we consider the space
P^{J{n,m}}
and the map
q_{n,m}:P^{J_{n,m}}\rightarrow P^{n}
as in Equation
5
. Note that
{J_{n,m}}
is a
\Sigma_{n}
-space, the action given by
g(t_{j})=t_{g(j)},t\in\{1,2,\cdots,m\},j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. This induces an action of
\Sigma_{n}
on
P^{J{n,m}}
given by
g\gamma(t_{j})=\gamma(t_{g(j)})
. Also
P^{n}
is a
\Sigma_{n}
-space, the action is given by
g(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots x_{n})=(x_{g(1)},x_{g(2)},\cdots,x_{g(n)})
and the maps
q_{n,m}
in Equation
5
and
\tau_{P^{n}}^{r}
in Equation

are
\Sigma_{n}
-maps.

4.13 Definition
.

Let
P
be a finite space. We define
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n,m}^{\Sigma,r}(P)
as the minimum number
k
such that there is a
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant open cover
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of
\operatorname{sd}^{r}(P^{n})
and on each open set there is
\Sigma_{n}
-map
s_{i}:Q_{i}\to P^{J_{n,m}}
satisfying
q_{n,m}\circ s_{i}=\tau_{P^{n}}^{r}
. If no such
k
exists, then we define
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n,m}^{\Sigma,r}(P)
to be
\infty
.

4.14 Lemma
.

Let
P
be a finite space. Then
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n,m}^{\Sigma,r}(P)\geq\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n,m+1}^{\Sigma,r}(P)
, for any
m,r\geq 0
and
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

We assume that
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n,m}^{\Sigma,r}(P)=k
and
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant open cover of
\operatorname{sd}^{r}(P^{n})
such that on each open set there is
\Sigma_{n}
-map
s_{i}:Q_{i}\to P^{J_{n,m}}
satisfying
q_{n,m}\circ s_{i}=\tau_{P^{n}}^{r}
. Consider the retraction map
R:J_{n,m+1}\rightarrow J_{n,m}
sending each
(m+1)_{j}
to
m_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq n
. Clearly this is an order preserving map and it induces a
\Sigma_{n}
-map
R^{*}:P^{J_{n,m}}\rightarrow P^{J_{n,m+1}},\gamma\rightarrow\gamma\circ R
. Then we have the following commutative diagram.

\textstyle{P^{J_{n,m}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{R^{*}}
\scriptstyle{q_{n,m}}
\textstyle{P^{J_{n,m+1}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

∎

4.15 Remark
.

Since for any
m
the value
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n,m}^{\Sigma,r}(P)\geq 1
, so by Lemma
4.14
the value is stable for large
m
. We denote the stable value by
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(P)
.

For
r\geq 0
, let
\rho_{j}:\operatorname{sd}^{r}(P^{n})\rightarrow P
denote the composition of
\tau^{r}_{P^{n}}:\operatorname{sd}^{r}(P^{n})\rightarrow P^{n}
and the
j
-th projection
p_{j}:P^{n}\to P
for
j=1,2,\cdots,n
. We have the following lemma which gives an alternative formulation of the definition for
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(P)
.

4.16 Lemma
.

With notations as above,
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(P)
is the minimal number
k
such that there exist a
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant open cover
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of
\operatorname{sd}^{r}(P^{n})
and the maps
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\cdots,\rho_{n}:Q_{i}\rightarrow P
are symmetrically homotopic.

Proof.

The existence of maps
s:Q\to P^{J_{n,m}}
and
H:Q\times J_{n,m}\to P
are equivalent by exponential law. Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\in Q
. We set
s(\mathbf{x})(t_{j})=H(\mathbf{x},t_{j}),
for
t_{j}
is parameter for
j
-th fence and
j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. If
Q
is an
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant set then
s
is an
\Sigma_{n}
-map if and only if
H
satisfy the relation
H(g\mathbf{x},t_{j})=H(\mathbf{x},t_{g(j)})
.
Also we have,

\displaystyle q_{n,m}\circ s_{i}=\tau_{P^{n}}^{k}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow[q_{n,m}\circ s_{i}(\mathbf{x})](j)=[\tau_{P^{n}}^{k}(\mathbf{x})](j)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow s(\mathbf{x})(m_{j})=\rho_{j}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow H(\mathbf{x},m_{j})=\rho_{j}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow H\text{ is a homotopy between }\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\cdots,\rho_{n}.

Hence the Lemma follows.
∎

The value
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(P)
depends on
r
. If we increase
r
the value
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(P)
will decrease. We have the following Proposition for
r=0
. This is a symmetric version of
[
10

.

4.17 Proposition
.

For any finite space
P
, we have
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,0}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}^{\Sigma}(P)
,
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
4.16
we get,
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,0}(P)
is the minimal integer
k
such that there exist a
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant open cover
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
of
P^{n}
and the projection maps
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\cdots,\rho_{n}:Q_{i}\rightarrow P
are symmetrically homotopic if and only if
\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}_{n}^{\Sigma}(P)=k
(using Lemma
4.5
and Remark
2.7
).

∎

4.18 Lemma
.

Let
P
be a finite space, and

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,r}(P)\geq\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}_{n}^{\Sigma}(\mathcal{K}(P))
,

(ii)

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,r}(P)\geq\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,r+1}(P)
.

Proof.

(i) Assume that
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,r}(P)=k
. By Lemma

there exists a symmetric open cover

4.7
we can say that the maps

is symmetric open set implies the subcomplex
\mathcal{K}(Q_{i})
is symmetric and
\{\mathcal{K}(Q_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a cover of

. Also
\mathcal{K}(\rho_{j})=\mathcal{K}(p_{j}\circ\tau^{r}_{P^{n}})=\mathcal{K}(p_{j})\circ\iota^{r}_{\mathcal{K}(P^{n})}=\pi_{j}
, for

and by Lemma
3.7

\iota^{r}_{\mathcal{K}(P^{n})}
is
\Sigma_{n}
-approximation of identity. So,
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(\mathcal{K}(P))\leq k
and therefore
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}_{n}^{\Sigma}(\mathcal{K}(P))\leq k
.

(ii) Let
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,r}(P)=k
. Then we have a
\Sigma_{n}
-invariant open cover

\operatorname{sd}^{r}(P^{n})
such that on each

\Sigma_{n}

for some

. Let us take the open cover

of
\operatorname{sd}^{r+1}(P^{n})
, where

and set

such that the following diagram commutes:

U_{i}

We get
\{\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}_{n}^{\Sigma,r}(P)\}_{r}
is a decreasing sequence bounded below by

. So we make the following definition.

4.19 Definition
.

For a finite space
P
, the
n
-th symmetric combinatorial complexity

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(P)
of
P
is defined as:

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(P)=\min_{r\geq 0}\{\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma,r}_{n}(P)\}.

Let us now prove our main theorem. This can be viewed as a symmetric version of
[

and higher analogue of

4.20 Theorem
.

For a finite space
P
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(P)=\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(\mathcal{K}(P))=\operatorname{\mathsf{T}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(|\mathcal{K}(P)|)

Proof.

In (i) of Lemma

, if we take

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(P)\geq\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(\mathcal{K}(P))

\operatorname{\mathsf{C}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(P)\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(\mathcal{K}(P))
. Let
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}C}^{\Sigma}_{n}(\mathcal{K}(P))=k

\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\cdots,\pi_{n}:L_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(P)

\displaystyle\tau_{P}\circ\mathcal{X}(\pi_{j})

.
∎
