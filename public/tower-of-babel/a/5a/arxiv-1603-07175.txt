Abstract
We consider the problem ϵ 2 Δ u − V ( y ) u + u p = 0 , u > 0 in Ω , ∂ u ∂ ν = 0 on ∂ Ω , \epsilon^{2}\Delta u-V(y)u+u^{p}\,=\,0,~~u>0~~\quad{\rm in}\quad\Omega,~~\quad\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\,=\,0\quad{\rm on}~~~\partial\Omega, where Ω \Omega is a bounded domain in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} with smooth boundary, the exponent p > 1 p>1 , ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 is a small parameter, V V is a uniformly positive, smooth potential on Ω ¯ \bar{\Omega} , and ν \nu denotes the outward normal of ∂ Ω \partial\Omega . Let Γ \Gamma be a curve intersecting orthogonally with ∂ Ω \partial\Omega at exactly two points and dividing Ω \Omega into two parts. Moreover, Γ \Gamma satisfies stationary and non-degeneracy conditions with respect to the functional ∫ Γ V σ \int_{\Gamma}V^{\sigma} , where σ = p + 1 p − 1 − 1 2 \sigma=\frac{p+1}{p-1}-\frac{1}{2} . We prove the existence of a solution u ϵ u_{\epsilon} concentrating along the whole of Γ \Gamma , exponentially small in ϵ \epsilon at any positive distance from it, provided that ϵ \epsilon is small and away from certain critical numbers. In particular, this establishes the validity of the two dimensional case of a conjecture by A. Ambrosetti, A. Malchiodi and W.-M. Ni(p.327, [ 4 ] ).



Keywords:
Ambrosetti-Malchiodi-Ni conjecture, concentration sets, Fermi coordinates

Introduction

We consider the following problem for the existence of concentration phenomena

\epsilon^{2}\Delta u-V(y)u+u^{p}\,=\,0,\;\;\;u>0\quad{\rm in}\quad\Omega,\qquad\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\,=\,0\quad{\rm on}\quad\partial\Omega,

(1.1)

where
\Omega
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbf{d}}
with smooth boundary,
\epsilon>0
is a small parameter,
\nu
denotes the outward normal of
\partial\Omega
and
the exponent
p>1
.

1.1
Case 1:
V\equiv 1

If
V\equiv 1
, problem (
1.1
) takes the form

\epsilon^{2}\Delta u-u+u^{p}\,=\,0,\;\;\;u>0~\quad{\rm in}\quad\Omega,~~~~~\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\,=\,0~~~\;\;{\rm on}~\;\;~\partial\Omega,

(1.2)

which is known as the stationary equation of Keller-Segel system in chemotaxis
[
42

limiting stationary equation of Gierer-Meinhardt system in biological pattern formation
[
30
]
. Problem (

studied extensively in recent years. See
[
51

In the pioneering papers
[

]

[
52
]
-
[

of
\partial\Omega

Case 2:

In this case, we first concern the problem on the whole space, i.e.,

\epsilon>0

\displaystyle{\inf_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbf{d}}}}V(y)>0.

In the pioneering work

V^{\prime}({y}_{0})=0,\,V^{\prime\prime}({y}_{0})\neq 0

being exponentially small in
\epsilon
outside any neighborhood of
{y}_{0}
.
This result has been subsequently extended to higher dimensions to the construction of solutions exhibiting
concentration around one or more points of space under various assumptions on the

We refer the reader for instance to

An important question is whether solutions exhibiting concentration on higher dimensional set exists.
In
[
3
]
, Ambrosetti, Malchiodi and Ni considered the case of

and constructed
radial solutions

exhibiting concentration on a sphere

u_{\epsilon}(r)\sim V^{\frac{1}{p-1}}(r_{0})w\big(V^{\frac{1}{2}}(r_{0})\epsilon^{-1}(r-r_{0})\big),\quad r=|y|

under the assumption that
r_{0}>0
is a non-degenerate critical point of

M(r)=r^{n-1}V^{\sigma}(r),

(1.5)

where

is the unique (even) solution of

w^{\prime\prime}-w+w^{p}=0,\ \ w>0,\ \ w^{\prime}(0)=0,\ \ w(\pm\infty)=0,

(1.6)

and

\sigma=\frac{p+1}{p-1}-\frac{1}{2}.

(1.7)

Based on heuristic arguments, in 2003 A. Ambrosetti, A. Malchiodi and W.-M. Ni raised the following conjecture (p.465,
[
3
]
):

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a non-degenerate
\mathbf{k}
-dimensional stationary manifold of the following functional

\int_{\mathcal{K}}V^{\frac{p+1}{p-1}-\frac{1}{2}({\mathbf{d}}-\mathbf{k})}.

Then there exists a solution to (
1.3
) concentrating near
{\mathcal{K}}
, at least along a subsequence
\epsilon_{j}\to 0
.

For
{\mathbf{d}}=2
,
\mathbf{k}=1
, del Pino, Kowalczyk and Wei
[
23
]
proved the validity of this conjecture under some gap condition.
Recently, Mahmoudi, Malchiodi and Montenegro
[
44
]
established the validity of the above conjecture in the case of
{\mathbf{d}}=3,\mathbf{k}=1
. They also considered the complex solutions of (
1.3
) carrying momentums.
For the concentration phenomena directed along general manifolds of codimension one, see
[
56
]
.
For a more general problem with suitable hypotheses for the potentials, Bartsch and Peng constructed spike layered solution which concentrates on certain
{\mathbf{d}}-1
dimensional spheres in
[
9
,
10
]
.

Let us go back to the problem on smooth bounded domain with homogeneous Neumann boundary condition,
i.e., problem (
1.1

V
is a smooth function and

\displaystyle{\inf_{y\in\bar{\Omega}}}V(y)>0.

(1.8)

Ambrosetti, Malchioci and Ni
[

imposed the following hypotheses on the function

(V1).

V\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
,

(V2).

is bounded and satisfies (

and introduced the auxiliary potential

M(r)\,=\,r^{{\mathbf{d}}-1}V^{\sigma}(r),~~~{\rm where}~\sigma\,=\,\frac{p+1}{p-1}-\frac{1}{2}.

(1.9)

By taking advantage of the boundary effect, they showed the existence of interior concentration for radial symmetric solutions to (
1.1
).
One of their results is as follows:

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathbf{d}}
be the ball
B_{1}
and
p>1
.
Suppose that the function
M
satisfies the condition

M^{\prime}(1)>0.

(1.10)

Then there exists a family of radial solutions

of (

is a local maximum for

1-r_{\epsilon}\sim\epsilon|\log\epsilon|

To the best knowledge of the authors, for (
1.1

and the concentration set).

For problem (
1.1

\Omega

This is the conjecture also by A. Ambrosetti, A. Malchiodi and W.-M. Ni in 2004 (p.327,

Let

\int_{\mathcal{K}}V^{\frac{p+1}{p-1}-\frac{1}{2}({\mathbf{d}}-\mathbf{k})}.

1.1
) concentrating near
{\mathcal{K}}
, at least along a subsequence
\epsilon_{j}\to 0
.

1.3
Main results

In the present paper, to avoid complicated computation, we will consider (
1.1
) and provide an affirmative answer to the last-mentioned conjecture(called
Ambrosetti-Malchiodi-Ni conjecture
) only in the case:
{\mathbf{d}}=2
and
\mathbf{k}=1
.
It should be mentioned that the potential term
V
and the boundary of
\Omega
will play an important role in the sense that they basically determine the location of the concentration set and also bring much more difficulties in our procedure of the constructing solutions due to the intersection between
\partial\Omega
and the concentration set.
The new ingredient is to set up a new local coordinate system such that we can decompose the interaction among the interior concentration layers, the boundary of
\Omega
and the potential term
V
.

More precisely, in the present paper, for the existence of a solution
u_{\epsilon}
concentrating along a curve, say
\Gamma
,
we make the following assumptions on
\Gamma
:

(A1).

\Gamma
is a curve intersecting
\partial\Omega
at exactly two points, saying
P_{1},P_{2}
, and, at these
points
\Gamma\bot\partial\Omega
.
In the small neighborhoods of
P_{1},~P_{2}
, the boundary
\partial\Omega
are two curves, say
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
, which can be represented
by the graphs of two functions respectively

{y}_{2}\,=\,\varphi_{1}({y}_{1})\quad\mbox{with }\ (0,\varphi_{1}(0))\,=\,P_{1},

{y}_{2}\,=\,\varphi_{2}({y}_{1})\quad\mbox{with }\ (0,\varphi_{2}(0))\,=\,P_{2}.

Moreover, after rescaling, we can always assume that
|\Gamma|=1
and then denote
k
the curvature of
\Gamma
, also
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
the signed curvatures of
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
at
P_{1}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
at
P_{2}
.

(A2).

The curve
\Gamma
is a non-degenerate geodesic relative to the weighted arc length
\int_{\Gamma}V^{\sigma}
, where

\sigma
is defined in (
1.9
).
This will be clarified in the next section, see (
2.9
) and (
2.11
).

By recalling
w
given in (
1.6
),
we consider the associated linearized eigenvalue problem,

h{{}^{\prime\prime}}-h+pw^{p-1}h\,=\,\lambda h\quad{\rm in}~\mathbb{R},~\quad h(\pm\infty)\,=\,0.

(1.11)

It is well known that this equation possesses a unique positive eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
with associated eigenfunction
Z
(even and positive) which can be normalized so that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}Z^{2}=1
.
In fact, a simple computation shows that

\lambda_{0}\,=\,\frac{1}{4}(p-1)(p+3),\quad Z\,=\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}w^{p+1}}\,}w^{\frac{p+1}{2}}.

(1.12)

By setting

\lambda_{*}\,=\,\frac{\lambda_{0}\ell^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\ell=\int_{\Gamma}V^{\frac{1}{2}},

(1.13)

we can formulate our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{d}}=2
and
\Gamma
be a non-degenerate, stationary curve for the weighted length functional
\int_{\Gamma}V^{\sigma}
, as described above. For given
c>0
there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0

such that for all
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
satisfying the gap condition

|\epsilon^{2}j^{2}-\lambda_{*}|\geq c\epsilon,~~\forall~j\in\mathbb{N},

(1.14)

problem (
1.1
) has a positive solution
u_{\epsilon}
, which, for
y
given by (
2.4
) in the neighborhood of
\Gamma
, takes the form

u_{\epsilon}(y)\,=\,V(0,\theta)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}w\Big(V(0,\theta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{t}{\epsilon}\Big)(1+o(1)).

(1.15)

For some number
c_{0}>0,~u_{\epsilon}
satisfies globally

u_{\epsilon}(y)\,\leq\,\exp\big(-c_{0}\epsilon^{-1}{\mathrm{dist}}(y,\Gamma)\big).

∎

2.1
Modified Fermi Coordinates

Recall the assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
in Section
1
and the notation therein.
For basic notions of curves, such as the signed curvature, the reader can refer to the book by do Carmo
[
27
]
. The local coordinate system can be given as the following:

Step 1.

Let the natural parameterization of the curve
\Gamma
be as follows:

\gamma_{0}:[0,1]\rightarrow\Gamma\subset\bar{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}.

We can extend
\gamma_{0}
slightly in a smooth way, i.e., for some small positive numbers
\sigma_{0}
, and define the mapping

\gamma:(-\sigma_{0},1+\sigma_{0})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},

such that

\gamma(\tilde{\theta})\,=\,\gamma_{0}(\tilde{\theta}),~~\forall\,\tilde{\theta}\in[0,1].

There holds the Frenet formula

\gamma^{\prime\prime}\,=\,kn,\quad n^{\prime}\,=\,-k\gamma^{\prime},

where
k
,
n
are the curvature and the normal of
\gamma
.
Choosing
\delta_{0}>0
very small, and setting

{\mathfrak{S}}_{1}\,\equiv\,(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})\times(-\sigma_{0},1+\sigma_{0}),

we construct the following mapping

\displaystyle{\mathbb{H}}:{\mathfrak{S}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{H}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{1})\,\equiv\,\Omega_{\delta_{0},\sigma_{0}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mathbb{H}(\tilde{t},\tilde{\theta})\,=\,\gamma(\tilde{\theta})+\tilde{t}\,n(\tilde{\theta}).

Note that
{\mathbb{H}}
is a diffeomorphism (locally) and
{\mathbb{H}}(0,\tilde{\theta})=\gamma(\tilde{\theta})
.

Step 2
.
Denote the preimage

{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}}_{1}\,\equiv\,{\mathbb{H}}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{1}\right),\qquad{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}}_{2}\,\equiv\,{\mathbb{H}}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{2}\right),

which can be parameterized respectively by
\left(\tilde{t},{\tilde{\varphi}}_{1}(\tilde{t})\right)
and
\left(\tilde{t},{\tilde{\varphi}}_{2}(\tilde{t})\right)
for some smooth functions
{\tilde{\varphi}}_{1}(\tilde{t}),\,{\tilde{\varphi}}_{2}(\tilde{t})
,
and then define a mapping

\tilde{{\mathbb{H}}}:\,{\mathfrak{S}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{S}}_{2}\,\equiv\,\tilde{{\mathbb{H}}}({\mathfrak{S}}_{1})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},

in the form

t=\tilde{t},\quad\theta=\frac{\tilde{\theta}-{\tilde{\varphi}}_{1}(\tilde{t})}{{\tilde{\varphi}}_{2}(\tilde{t})-{\tilde{\varphi}}_{1}(\tilde{t})}.

This transformation will straighten up the curves
{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}}_{1}
and
{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}}_{2}
.

It is obvious that

\displaystyle{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}}^{-1}(0,\theta)\,=\,(0,{\theta}),\ \theta\in[0,1].

(2.1)

Moreover, we have

Lemma 2.1
.

There hold

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{1}^{\prime}(0)=0,\quad\tilde{\varphi}_{2}^{\prime}(0)=0.

(2.2)

Then, there exist the relations

\displaystyle k_{1}={\tilde{\varphi}}^{\prime\prime}_{1}(0),\qquad k_{2}={\tilde{\varphi}}^{\prime\prime}_{2}(0).

(2.3)

The proof of this lemma will be given in Appendix
A
.
∎

Step 3
.
We define the
modified Fermi coordinates

y\,=\,F(t,\theta)\,=\,{\mathbb{H}}\circ{\tilde{\mathbb{H}}}^{-1}(t,\theta)\,:\,(-\delta_{0},\delta_{0})\times(-\sigma_{0},1+\sigma_{0})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} F(t,\theta)&\,=\,{\mathbb{H}}\Big(t,\,\,\big({\tilde{\varphi}}_{2}(t)-{\tilde{\varphi}}_{1}(t)\big)\theta+{\tilde{\varphi}}_{1}(t)\Big)\\

For convenience’s sake, in the following, we also denote

\Theta(t,\theta)\,\equiv\,\big({\tilde{\varphi}}_{2}(t)-{\tilde{\varphi}}_{1}(t)\big)\theta\,+\,{\tilde{\varphi}}_{1}(t),

and so that

\displaystyle F(t,\theta)\,=\,\gamma\big(\Theta(t,\theta)\big)\,+\,t\,n\big(\Theta(t,\theta)\big).

(2.5)

Note also that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Theta(0,\theta)\,=\,\theta,\qquad\Theta_{t}(0,\theta)\,=\,0,\qquad\Theta_{\theta}(0,\theta)\,=\,1,\quad\ \\
\Theta_{tt}(0,\theta)\,=\,(k_{2}-k_{1})\theta+k_{1},\qquad\Theta_{tt\theta}(0,\theta)\,=\,k_{2}-k_{1},\end{aligned}

(2.6)

due to (
2.2
) and (
2.3
).
These quantities will play an important role in the further settings.

The asymptotic expressions of this coordinate system
will be given by the following basic facts.

.

in (

) has the following properties:

\,\,F(0,\theta)\,=\,\gamma(\theta)
,

(2).

\,\,\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}t}(0,\theta)\,=\,n(\theta)
,

(3).

\,\,q_{1}(\theta)\,\equiv\,\frac{{\partial}^{2}F}{{\partial}t^{2}}(0,\theta)\,=\,\gamma{\,{}^{\prime}}(\theta)\cdot\Theta_{tt}(0,\theta)\quad\bot\,n(\theta)
,

(4).

\,\,q_{2}(\theta)\,\equiv\,\frac{{\partial}^{3}F}{{\partial}t^{3}}(0,\theta)\,=\,\gamma{\,{}^{\prime}}(\theta)\cdot\Theta_{ttt}(0,\theta)+3n{{}^{\prime}}(\theta)\cdot\Theta_{tt}(0,\theta)\quad\bot\,n(\theta)

Consider the deformation of

\partial\Omega

\Gamma_{\mathfrak{g}}\,:\,\gamma\big(\Theta({\mathfrak{g}}(\theta),\theta)\big)\,+\,{\mathfrak{g}}(\theta)\,n\big(\Theta({\mathfrak{g}}(\theta),\theta)\big).

We write

in the modified Fermi coordinates by setting

The curve
\Gamma
is said to be stationary for the weighted length

if the first variation of

at

is equal to zero.
That is, for any smooth function

\begin{split}0\,=\,{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}(0)[h]\,=\,&\int_{0}^{1}V^{\sigma}(0,\theta)\Theta_{\theta}^{-1}\Big[(-kh)\Theta_{\theta}^{2}\,+\,\Theta_{\theta}(\Theta_{t}h^{\prime}+\Theta_{\theta t}h)\Big]\,{\rm d}\theta\\
&+\int_{0}^{1}\big(V^{\sigma}\big)_{t}\cdot h\cdot\Theta_{\theta}\,{\rm d}\theta,\end{split}

which is equivalent to the relation

\sigma V_{t}(0,\theta)\,=\,k(\theta)V(0,\theta).

(2.9)

We assume the validity of this relation at
\Gamma
and then consider the second variation of

\begin{split}{\mathcal{J}}{{}^{\prime\prime}}(0)[h,f]\,=\,&\,\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\Big|_{s=0}{\mathcal{J}}^{\prime}(0+sf)[h]\\
\,=\,&\,\int_{0}^{1}V^{\sigma}(\Theta_{tt}f+f^{\prime})h^{\prime}\,{\rm d}\theta+\int_{0}^{1}V^{\sigma}\Theta_{tt}\cdot f^{\prime}h\,{\rm d}\theta\\
&+\int_{0}^{1}\Big[(V^{\sigma})_{tt}-2k(V^{\sigma})_{t}+\Theta_{tt\theta}V^{\sigma}\Big]fh\,{\rm d}\theta\\
\,=\,&\,h\cdot V^{\sigma}(\Theta_{tt}f+f^{\prime})\big|_{0}^{1}+\int_{0}^{1}\big(-V^{\sigma}f{{}^{\prime\prime}}-\sigma V^{\sigma-1}V_{\theta}f^{\prime}\big)h\,{\rm d}\theta\\
&+\int_{0}^{1}\Big[-\sigma V^{\sigma-1}V_{\theta}\Theta_{tt}+(V^{\sigma})_{tt}-2k(V^{\sigma})_{t}\Big]fh\,{\rm d}\theta.\end{split}

The curve
\Gamma
is said to be nondegenerate if

(2.10)

then
f\equiv 0
.

2.9

\begin{split}f{{}^{\prime\prime}}+\sigma V^{-1}&V_{\theta}f^{\prime}+\Big[\sigma V^{-1}V_{\theta}\Theta_{tt}-\sigma V^{-1}V_{tt}+(1+\frac{1}{\sigma})k^{2}\Big]f\,=\,0\quad\mbox{in }(0,1),\\
&f^{\prime}(1)+k_{2}f(1)\,=\,0,\qquad f^{\prime}(0)+k_{1}f(0)\,=\,0,\end{split}

(2.11)

has only the trivial solution.

\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}\theta}(t,\theta)\,=\,\gamma^{\prime}(\theta)-k(\theta)t\gamma{\,{}^{\prime}}(\theta)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{1}^{\prime}(\theta)+\frac{t^{3}}{6}q_{2}^{\prime}(\theta)+O(t^{4}).

Specially, there hold

\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}t}(t,0)\,=\,n(0)+tq_{1}(0)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{2}(0)+O(t^{3}),

\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}t}(t,1)\,=\,n(1)+tq_{1}(1)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{2}(1)+O(t^{3}),

\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}\theta}(t,0)\,=\,\gamma^{\prime}(0)-k(0)t\gamma{\,{}^{\prime}}(0)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{1}^{\prime}(0)+\frac{t^{3}}{6}q_{2}^{\prime}(0)+O(t^{4}),

\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}\theta}(t,1)\,=\,\gamma^{\prime}(1)-k(1)t\gamma{\,{}^{\prime}}(1)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{1}^{\prime}(1)+\frac{t^{3}}{6}q_{2}^{\prime}(1)+O(t^{4}).

These formulas will play an important role in the derivation of the local form of (
1.1
), which will be given in Appendix

Appendix B
Local forms of the differential operators in (

In this section, we are devoted to presenting the expressions of the differential operators

We first derive the metric matrix.
Note that

\gamma^{\prime}(\theta)\perp n(\theta),\quad n^{\prime}(\theta)\bot n(\theta),\quad q_{1}(\theta)\bot n(\theta),\quad q_{2}(\theta)\bot n(\theta).

As the first step, here are the computation of the metric matrix:

\begin{split}g_{11}&\,=\,\left<\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}t},\,\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}t}\right>\\
&\,=\,\left<n+tq_{1}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{2}+O(t^{3}),\,\,n+tq_{1}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{2}+O(t^{3})\right>\\
&\,=\,1+t^{2}|q_{1}|^{2}+O(t^{3}),\end{split}

and

\begin{split}g_{12}&\,=\,\left<\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}t},\,\,\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}\theta}\right>\\
&\,=\,\left<n+tq_{1}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{2}+O(t^{3}),\,\,\gamma^{\prime}-kt\gamma{{}^{\prime}}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{1}^{\prime}+O(t^{3})\right>\\
&\,=\,\frac{t^{2}}{2}<n,q_{1}^{\prime}>+t<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>-t^{2}k<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+\frac{t^{2}}{2}<q_{2},\gamma^{\prime}>+O(t^{3})\\
&\,=\,t<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\big(<q_{2},\gamma^{\prime}>-k<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>\big)+O(t^{3}),\end{split}

where we have used the fact

\displaystyle<q_{1}^{\prime},n>-k<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>\,=\,<q_{1}^{\prime},n>+<q_{1},n^{\prime}>\,=\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}<q_{1},n>\,=\,0.

The last element is

\begin{split}g_{22}&\,=\,\left<\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}\theta},\,\,\frac{{\partial}F}{{\partial}\theta}\right>\\
&\,=\,\left<\gamma^{\prime}-kt\gamma{{}^{\prime}}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{1}^{\prime}+O(t^{3}),\,\,\gamma^{\prime}-kt\gamma{{}^{\prime}}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}q_{1}^{\prime}+O(t^{3})\right>\\
&\,=\,1-2kt+t^{2}\left(<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>+k^{2}\right)+O(t^{3}).\end{split}

So the determinant of the metric matrix is

\begin{split}g&\,=\,\mbox{det}(g_{ij})\\
&\,=\,1-2kt+t^{2}\left(<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>+k^{2}\right)+t^{2}|q_{1}|^{2}-t^{2}<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>^{2}+O(t^{3})\\
&\,=\,1-2kt+t^{2}\left(<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>+k^{2}\right)+O(t^{3}),\end{split}

where we have used
|q_{1}|^{2}-<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>^{2}=0
due to the expression of
q_{1}
in Lemma
2.2
.

We turn to the computation of the inverse of the metric matrix.
By

\big[1+at+bt^{2}+O(t^{3})\big]^{-1}\,=\,1-at+(a^{2}-b)t^{2}+O(t^{3}),

we have

\displaystyle g^{-1}\,\,=\,

\displaystyle\,1+2kt+t^{2}\left(3k^{2}-<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>\right)+O(t^{3}).

Whence

\begin{split}g^{11}&\,=\,g_{22}\cdot g^{-1}\\
&\,=\,1-2kt+t^{2}\left(<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>+k^{2}\right)+2kt-4k^{2}t^{2}+t^{2}\left(3k^{2}-<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>\right)+O(t^{3})\\
&\,=\,1+O(t^{3}),\\
\\
\end{split}

\begin{split}-g^{12}&\,=\,g_{12}\cdot g^{-1}\\
&\,=\,t<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+2kt^{2}<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(<q_{2}^{\prime},\gamma{{}^{\prime}}>-k<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>\right)+O(t^{3})\qquad\qquad\qquad\\
&\,=\,t<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\big(3k<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+<q_{2},\gamma^{\prime}>\big)+O(t^{3}),\\
\\
\end{split}

\begin{split}g^{22}&\,=\,g_{11}\cdot g^{-1}\,=\,1+2kt+t^{2}\left(3k^{2}-<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>+|q_{1}|^{2}\right)+O(t^{3}).\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\end{split}

We can get the following formulas

\begin{split}\sqrt{g}&\,=\,1-kt+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>+O(t^{3}),\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\end{split}

\begin{split}(\sqrt{g})^{-1}&\,=\,1+kt+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\left(2k^{2}-<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>\right)+O(t^{3}),\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\end{split}

\begin{split}\sqrt{g^{22}}&\,=\,1+kt+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\Big(3k^{2}-<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>+|q_{1}|^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\cdot 4k^{2}\Big)+O(t^{3})\\
&\,=\,1+kt+\frac{1}{2}\big(2k^{2}-<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>+|q_{1}|^{2}\big)t^{2}+O(t^{3}),\end{split}

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sqrt{g^{22}}}&\,=\,1-kt+\Big(k^{2}-k^{2}+\frac{1}{2}<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>-\frac{1}{2}|q_{1}|^{2}\Big)t^{2}+O(t^{3})\qquad\\
&\,=\,1-kt+\frac{1}{2}\big(<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>-|q_{1}|^{2}\big)t^{2}+O(t^{3}).\end{split}

Now, recall the definition of Laplace-Beltrami operator in the form

\begin{split}\triangle_{t,\theta}u\,=\,&\,g^{11}\frac{{\partial}^{2}u}{{\partial}t^{2}}+2g^{12}\frac{{\partial}^{2}u}{{\partial}t{\partial}\theta}+g^{22}\frac{{\partial}^{2}u}{{\partial}\theta^{2}}+(\sqrt{g})^{-1}\left[\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}(\sqrt{g}g^{11})+\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta}(\sqrt{g}g^{21})\right]\frac{{\partial}u}{{\partial}t}\\
&+(\sqrt{g})^{-1}\left[\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}(\sqrt{g}g^{12})+\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta}(\sqrt{g}g^{22})\right]\frac{{\partial}u}{{\partial}\theta}.\end{split}

(B.1)

The last two coefficients in (
B.1
) can be computed as follows.
Since

\begin{split}\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{11}&\,=\,1-kt+\frac{t^{2}}{2}<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>+O(t^{3}),\end{split}

we can get

\begin{split}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}(\sqrt{g}g^{11})&\,=\,-k+t<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>+O(t^{2}).\end{split}

Since

\begin{split}\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{12}&\,=\,-t<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>-\frac{t^{2}}{2}(3k<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+<\gamma^{\prime},q_{2}>)+kt^{2}<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+O(t^{3})\\

\begin{split}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta}(\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{12})&\,=\,-t<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>^{\prime}-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(k<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+<\gamma^{\prime},q_{2}>\right)^{\prime}+O(t^{3}),\\

\begin{split}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}(\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{11})+\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta}(\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{12})&\,=\,-k+t\left(<q_{1}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}>-<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>^{\prime}\right)+O(t^{2})\\
&\,=\,-k+O(t^{2}),\end{split}

where we have used the relations

We obtain the coefficient of the first bracket in (

On the other hand, by

\begin{split}\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{22}&\,=\,1-kt+\frac{t^{2}}{2}<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+2kt-2k^{2}t^{2}+t^{2}\big(3k^{2}-<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+|q_{1}|^{2}\big)+O(t^{3})\\

we can get

\begin{split}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta}(\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{22})&\,=\,k^{\prime}t+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\big(2k^{2}-<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+2|q_{1}|^{2}\big)^{\prime}+O(t^{3}).\end{split}

This implies that

\begin{split}&\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}(\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{12})+\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta}(\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{22})\,=\,-<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+t(k^{\prime}-k<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>-<q_{2},\gamma{\,{}^{\prime}}>)+O(t^{2}),\end{split}

which gives the coefficient of the second bracket in (

\begin{split}&(\sqrt{g})^{-1}\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}t}(\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{12})+\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\theta}(\sqrt{g}\cdot g^{22})\right)\\
&\,=\,-<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>+t\big(k^{\prime}-2k<q_{1},\gamma^{\prime}>-<q_{2},\gamma^{\prime}>\big)+O(t^{2}).\end{split}

Denote
