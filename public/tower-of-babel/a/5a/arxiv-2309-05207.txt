Insights into the Energy Transfers in Hydrodynamic Turbulence Using Field-theoretic Tools
Mahendra K. Verma 1

Abstract
Turbulent flows exhibit intriguing energy transfers. In this paper, we compute the renormalized viscosities, mode-to-mode energy transfers, energy fluxes, and shell-to-shell energy transfers for the two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) hydrodynamic turbulence (HDT) using field-theoretic methods. We employ Craya-Herring basis that provides separate renormalized viscosities and energy transfers for its two components. In addition, Craya-Herring basis eliminates complex tensor algebra and simplifies the calculations considerably. In the k − 5 / 3 k^{-5/3} spectral regime of 2D HDT, the energy transfers between neighbouring ( local ) wavenumbers are forward, but they are backwards for distant ( nonlocal ) wavenumbers. The individual transfers between the distant wavenumber shells are small, but their cumulative sum is significant and it overcomes the forward local transfer to yield a constant inverse energy cascade. For 3D HDT, the mode-to-mode and shell-to-shell energy transfers reveal forward energy transfers for both local and nonlocal wavenumbers. More importantly, using scale-by-scale energy transfers we show that the cumulative nonlocal and local energy transfers are of the same order, which is contrary to the assumption of local energy transfers in turbulence. For 3D HDT, the renormalized viscosity, ν 2 ​ ( k ) \nu_{2}(k) , computed by us and other authors are in general agreement, and it varies as k − 4 / 3 k^{-4/3} . For 2D HDT, our the renormalized viscosity, ν 1 ​ ( k ) > 0 \nu_{1}(k)>0 , but ν 1 ​ ( k ) \nu_{1}(k) reported in literature shows significant variations including a negative ν 1 ​ ( k ) \nu_{1}(k) . In this paper, we argue that the inconsistencies in ν 1 ​ ( k ) \nu_{1}(k) indicate inadequacy of renormalization group analysis that takes into account only local interactions and excludes nonlocal ones. In 2D HDT, the opposing nature of local and nonlocal energy transfers amplifies the error in ν 1 ​ ( k ) \nu_{1}(k) . Based on the sweeping effect , which is connected to the nonlocal interactions, Kraichnan 1964a too advocated nonapplicability of field theory in the Eulerian framework.

Introduction

Field theory provides powerful tools that have been used to explain complex phenomena in high-energy physics, condensed-matter physics, statistical physics, and turbulence
(
Peskin & Schroeder 1995
;
Lancaster & Blundell 2014
)
. This paper focusses on the application of field theory to the
locality of interactions
and
viscosity renormalization
in two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) hydrodynamic turbulence (HDT). Throughout the paper, we assume the flow to be incompressible.

Thousands of papers have been written on the field-theoretic analysis of turbulence. These works provide valuable insights into the dynamics of HDT, magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) turbulence, scalar turbulence, weak turbulence, rotating turbulence, etc. In particular, we learn about the renormalized parameters, energy fluxes, and correlation functions using field theory. Yet, there are many unresolved issues, e.g., intermittency, higher-order correlations, nature of multiscale energy transfers, application of field theory to anisotropic turbulence, etc. Therefore, researchers still use field theory to solve the above problems. Note that numerical simulations and experiments complement such analytical works.

Kolmogorov, Taylor, Batchelor, Chandrasekhar, Onsager, and others pioneered analytical theories of turbulence, some of which may also classify as field theory. However, to limit the scope of the paper, we skip the works of the above giants and focus on the extension of quantum field theory to turbulence, which was pioneered by Kraichnan. In his first work,
Kraichnan 1959
employed first-order expansion, which he termed as
Direct Interaction Approximation
(DIA), to 3D HDT and derived expressions for the Green’s function and correlation function. Kraichnan employed a wavenumber cutoff for an integral for the Green’s function that led to a prediction of
k^{-3/2}
energy spectrum, which is not observed in experiments. Later,
Kraichnan 1964a
discovered
sweeping effect
, which is a transport of small-scale fluctuations by the large-scale structures; in this work, Kraichnan argued that field theory cannot be applied to HDT in the Eulerian framework because of the sweeping effect.

These negative results prompted Kraichnan to employ Lagrangian-based field theory to turbulence that predicts
k^{-5/3}
energy spectrum
(
Kraichnan 1964b
)
. However, in the Eulerian framework itself,
Kraichnan 1971
computed the
effective viscosity
of the Green’s function and the Kolmogorov constants for 2D and 3D HDT. Here, Kraichnan reported that the effective viscosities for 2D HDT and 3D HDT are positive. However, based on energy fluxes,
Kraichnan 1976
reported
negative eddy viscosity
for 2D HDT, which is opposite to his earlier prediction
(
Kraichnan 1971
)
. These contradictions are due to the complexities of 2D HDT, which will be discussed in this paper. Refer to
Leslie 1973
for a detailed discussion on Kraichnan’s works on turbulence.

The effective viscosity of the Green’s function is computed rigorously using renormalization group (RG) analysis that does not require a wavenumber cutoff. The leading RG calculations for 3D HDT are by
Forster et al. 1977
,
Yakhot & Orszag 1986
,
DeDominicis & Martin 1979
,
Orszag 1973
,
McComb & Shanmugasundaram 1983
,
McComb 2014
,
Zhou et al. 1988
, and
Zhou 2010
who predicted the renormalized viscosity to scale as
k^{-4/3}
. For the RG calculation, many authors employed wavenumber-dependent forcing and noise renormalization
(
Forster et al. 1977
;
Yakhot & Orszag 1986
)
. On the other hand, McComb, Zhou, and coworkers used self-consistent recursive RG scheme for their computation. The other notable field-theoretic works on turbulence are by
Wyld 1961
,
Martin et al. 1973
,
Eyink 1993
,
Moriconi 2004
,
Canet 2022
,
Arad et al. 1999
,
Biferale & Procaccia 2005
,
Bos & Rubinstein 2013
,
Kaneda 2007
, among others.

The works on the RG analysis of 2D HDT is limited (in comparison to 3D HDT). In one of the leading works,
Olla 1991
obtained two different spectral regimes by tuning the noise:
k^{-3}
energy spectrum with a constant enstrophy flux, and
k^{-5/3}
spectrum with a constant energy flux. For the
k^{-5/3}
spectral regime,
Olla 1991
derived the renormalization constant to be 0.642 and the Kolmogorov constant to be 6.45.
Nandy & Bhattacharjee 1995
employed self-consistent mode-coupling scheme and obtained similar constants.

Liang & Diamond 1993
argued that no RG fixed point exists for 2D HDT due to the dual cascade.

There are significant number of works on the field-theoretic computation of energy flux.
Kraichnan 1959
was the first researcher to formulate field-theoretic computation of energy flux using
combined energy transfer
and DIA.
Tools such as Quasi Normal Approximation, Eddy-damped Quasi Normal Markovian Approximation (EDQNM), and Lagrangian-based DIA were developed by
Kraichnan 1959
,
Chandrasekhar 1955
,
Herring 1965
,
Orszag 1973
,
Leslie 1973
, and others. Other notable field-theoretic computations of energy flux are by
Yakhot & Orszag 1986
,
McComb 1990
, and
Zhou et al. 1989
. Refer to
Leslie 1973
,
Lesieur 2008
, and
McComb 1990
for more details.

Later, a new formalism called
mode-to-mode energy transfer
was developed to compute the energy fluxes, as well as shell-to-shell energy transfers
(
Dar et al. 2001
)
. This formalism clearly identifies the
giver
and
receiver
Fourier modes in a triad, thus resolves the ambiguity encountered in the computation of shell-to-shell energy transfers using the combined energy transfer
(
Domaradzki & Rogallo 1990
;
Dar et al. 2001
;
Verma 2004
)
.
Verma 2001
and
Verma 2004
employed field theory to compute the energy fluxes for HDT and MHD turbulence using the mode-to-mode energy transfers.

Starting from
Kolmogorov 1941
, it has been assumed that the energy transfers in HDT is
local
in Fourier space. That is, the most dominant energy transfers occur among the wavenumbers of similar magnitudes.
Domaradzki & Rogallo 1990
,
Verma et al. 2005
,
Domaradzki et al. 2009
, and
Aluie & Eyink 2009
examined this hypothesis by studying the shell-to-shell energy transfers using EDQNM approximation, numerical simulations, and field-theoretic tools. They observed significant nonlocal energy transfers in 2D and 3D HDT. However, they reported that the cumulative local transfers dominate the nonlocal counterpart because the
local triads
outnumber the nonlocal ones. In this paper, we show that the above result is incorrect, and that the cumulative local and nonlocal energy transfers are of the same order.

The field-theoretic tools are very powerful, but they are too complex and daunting involving complicated tensor algebra and singular integrals. Fortunately, we discovered that Craya-Herring basis
(
Craya 1958
;
Herring 1974
;
Sagaut & Cambon 2018
;
Verma 2019
)
simplifies the field-theoretic calculations of turbulent flows dramatically. Another major benefit of this basis is that it allows a separate computation of the renormalized viscosities and energy transfers for each component (one component in 2D, and two components in 3D). As a result, using Craya-Herring basis we not only obtain finer details of turbulence, but also eliminate the complex tensor algebra. We will demonstrate these simplifications in this paper. In addition, we deviate from the conventional
\int dpdq\delta({\bf k-p-q})
integrals to
\int dpd\gamma
, where
\gamma
is the angle between
k
and
p
in a triad
(k,p,q)
. This new scheme simplifies the asymptotic analysis, as well as the evaluation of the singular integrals of energy fluxes.

Now, we brief the results of this paper in relation to the past works. In this paper, we employ perturbative field theory to first order and compute the renormalized viscosities, mode-to-mode energy transfers, energy fluxes, and shell-to-shell energy transfers for 2D and 3D HDT. We perform our calculation in Craya-Herring basis that provides separate contributions from the two components of Craya-Herring basis. For the RG analysis, we employ recursive procedure proposed by McComb, Zhou, and coworkers
(
McComb & Shanmugasundaram 1983
;
Zhou et al. 1989
;
McComb 1990
;
Zhou 2010
)
. For the energy transfer computation, we employ Quasi-normal approximation, as in
Kraichnan 1959
and
Orszag 1973
.

Using the above method, we compute the energy fluxes and the Kolmogorov constants for 2D and 3D HDT in the wavenumber regime where the energy spectra are
k^{-5/3}
. Our calculation, which is in Craya-Herring basis, is much more concise than the previous works. We show that the energy flux is positive for 3D HDT, but reversed for 2D HDT. These results are consistent with earlier works.

Using field theory we compute the mode-to-mode energy transfer rates for 2D and 3D triads. For a triad
(k,p,q)
with
k
as the receiver wavenumber and
p
as giver wavenumber, we show that in 2D HDT, the energy transfer is forward when
p\lessapprox k
(local wavenumbers), but it is backward for distant wavenumbers, i.e., when
p\ll k
. In 3D HDT, the energy transfers are forward for both local and distant wavenumbers. The shell-to-shell energy transfers exhibit similar behaviour. Further, the shell-to-shell energy transfer between distant shells decreases as the shell separation increases; this variation follows
(K/P)^{-4/3}
scaling where
K
and
P
are the wavenumbers of the receiver and giver shells respectively. These results are consistent with those of
Domaradzki 1992
and
Verma et al. 2005
.

In this paper we go a step further and compute the cumulative local and nonlocal transfers by summing up the energy transfers over appropriate wavenumber bands. Using detailed study we show that for 3D HDT, the cumulative local and nonlocal energy transfers are of the same order. For example, the energy transfers
(0.7R,R)\rightarrow(R,R/0.7)
and
(0,0.1R)\rightarrow(10R,\infty)
contribute approximately 17% each to the total energy flux
\Pi(R)
for a wavenumber sphere of radius
R
; here
(k_{1},k_{2})
represents a wavenumber band. This result is contrary to the general belief that the eenergy transfers in turbulence is local in wavenumber space. Our work negates the conclusions of
Verma et al. 2005
and
Aluie & Eyink 2009
who had argued that the local energy transfers dominate the nonlocal ones because the local triads outnumber the nonlocal ones.

In 2D HDT, the energy transfers between neighbouring shells is forward and significant, but the transfer between the distant shells is backward and small. However, a sum of nonlocal energy transfers is large and it overcompensates the forward energy transfer, yielding an inverse energy flux.

We also compute the renormalized viscosity,
\nu_{1}(k)
, for 2D HDT, and find this to be positive and approximately 1/4th of the reported values by
Kraichnan 1971
,
Olla 1991
, and
Nandi et al. 2014
. Researchers have reported negative
\nu_{1}(k)
or absence of stable RG fixed point for 2D HDT
(
Kraichnan 1976
;
Verma et al. 2005
;
Liang & Diamond 1993
)
. In this paper, we argue that the inconsistencies in
\nu_{1}(k)
are due to the inability of the RG analysis to take into account the nonlocal interactions. The coarse-graining process in RG considers the local energy transfers only, which is forward; this is the reason why
\nu_{1}(k)
is positive in our calculation. The eddy viscosity based on the energy flux is negative because the energy flux includes both local and nonlocal interactions
(
Kraichnan 1976
)
.

The velocity field for 3D HDT has two Craya-Herring components. Our 3D RG calculation shows that the renormalized viscosity for the transverse component is positive, and it dominates the renormalized viscosity for the parallel component. In 3D HDT, the RG parameter has no inconsistency (in contrast to 2D HDT) because the local and nonlocal energy transfers are positive for this case.

The outline of the paper is as follows: In Sections 2, we introduce the relevant hydrodynamic equations in Fourier space and in Craya-Herring basis. In Section 3, we describe the renormalization group analysis of 2D and 3D HDT. Sections 4 and 5 contain discussions on the energy transfers in a triad, as well as the energy flux in 2D and 3D HDT. The shell-to-shell energy transfers in 2D and 3D HDT have been discussed in Sections 6 and 7 respectively. We conclude in Section 8.

2
Governing Equations

In this section, we introduce the hydrodynamic equations in Fourier space, as well as in Craya-Herring basis. We also define the correlation functions, energy spectra, and volume integrals for HDT. These quantities play an important role in perturbative field theory of turbulence, which is a theme of this paper.

2.1
Hydrodynamics in Spectral Space

The Navier–Stokes equation describing an incompressible flow is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bf u}({\bf r},t)}{\partial t}+{\bf u}({\bf r},t)\cdot\nabla{\bf u}({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla p({\bf r},t)+\nu\nabla^{2}{\bf u}({\bf r},t)+{\bf F}_{u}({\bf r},t),

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(2)

where
{\bf u}
is the velocity fluctuation with a zero mean,
{\bf F}_{u}
is the external force,
p
is the pressure field, and
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity. Without a loss of generality, we assume the density of the fluid to be unity, which leads to the constraint of Eq. (
2
).

In this paper, we solve the Fourier-version of Eqs. (
1
,
2
) using field theory. In field theory, it is often assumed that the system is of infinite extent, where the transformation from real space to Fourier space and vice versa are as follows
(
Peskin & Schroeder 1995
)
:

\displaystyle{\bf u}({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\bf k}{(2\pi)^{d}}{\bf u}({\bf k},t)\exp(i{\bf k\cdot r}),

(3)

\displaystyle{\bf u}({\bf k},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d{\bf r}[{\bf u}({\bf r},t)\exp(-i{\bf k\cdot r})],

(4)

where
d
is the space dimension, which could be 2 or 3 in this paper. In Fourier space, Navier-Stokes equations are
(
Lesieur 2008
;
Verma 2019
)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\nu k^{2}){\bf u}(\mathbf{k},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\int\frac{d\bf k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\{{\bf k}\cdot{\bf u}({\bf q},t)\}{\bf u}({\bf p},t)-i{\bf k}p(\mathbf{k},t)+{\bf F}_{u}({\bf k},t),

(5)

\displaystyle{\bf k\cdot u}(\mathbf{k},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(6)

where
{\bf k=p+q}

p(\mathbf{k},t)=-\frac{i}{k^{2}}{\bf k}\cdot{\bf F}_{u}({\bf k},t)-\frac{1}{k^{2}}\int\frac{d\bf k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\{{\bf k}\cdot{\bf u}({\bf q},t)\}\{{\bf k}\cdot{\bf u}({\bf p},t)\}\}.

(7)

It is straightforward to derive the following evolution equation for the
modal energy

E({\bf k})=|{\bf u(k)}|^{2}/2

(

Verma 2004
)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+2\nu k^{2})E(\mathbf{k},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bf p}S^{uu}({\bf k|p|q})+\Re[{\bf F}_{u}({\bf k},t)\cdot{\bf u}^{*}({\bf k},t)],

(8)

where

S^{uu}({\bf k|p|q})=\Im\left[\{{\bf k}\cdot{\bf u}({\bf q},t)\}\{{\bf u}({\bf p},t)\cdot{\bf u}^{*}({\bf k},t)\}\}\right]

(9)

is the

from the

{\bf u(p)}
to the

mode
{\bf u(k)}
with the mediation of mode
{\bf u(q)}
. Using

energy flux
emanating from a wavenumber sphere of radius
R
as follows. The energy flux
\Pi(R)
is the net nonlinear energy transfer rate from all the modes residing inside the sphere to the modes outside the sphere. Hence, the ensemble average of
\Pi(R)
is
(
Dar et al. 2001
;
Verma 2004
;
Verma 2019

\left\langle\Pi(R)\right\rangle=\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{d{\bf k^{\prime}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{R}\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\left\langle S^{uu}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle.

(10)

In this paper, we will compute the renormalized viscosity, as well as
\left\langle S^{uu}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\Pi(k_{0})\right\rangle
, using field theory. In general, these calculations are quite complex, but they get simplified in Craya-Herring basis, which will be introduced in the next subsection.

u_{1}({\bf k},t)
and
u_{2}({\bf k},t)
in a triad. For the same, we consider a wavenumber triad
({\bf k^{\prime},p,q})
with

, and choose

\hat{n}=\frac{{\bf q\times p}}{|{\bf q\times p}|}.

(13)

Since
{\bf k=p+q}
, we deduce that
{\bf k^{\prime}=-k}
. The Craya-Herring basis vectors for the interacting wavenumbers are illustrated in Fig.
2
. Note that
\alpha,\beta
,

Verma 2019
derived the equations for

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\nu k^{2}){u}_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\nu k^{2}){u}_{1}({\bf p},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ip\sin(\gamma-\alpha)u_{1}^{*}({\bf q},t)u_{1}^{*}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)+F_{1}({\bf p},t),

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\nu k^{2}){u}_{1}({\bf q},t)

\displaystyle E({\bf k},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(|u_{1}({\bf k},t)|^{2}+|u_{2}({\bf k},t)|^{2}\right)=C_{1}({\bf k},t)+C_{2}({\bf k},t).

(20)

\displaystyle H_{K}({\bf k},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Re[{\bf u}^{*}({\bf k},t)\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}({\bf k},t)]=k\Im[u_{1}^{*}({\bf k},t)u_{2}({\bf k},t)].

where

;

)
,

\displaystyle S^{uu}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})

\displaystyle k^{\prime}\sin\beta\cos\gamma\Im\{u_{1}({\bf q},t)u_{1}({\bf p},t)u_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)\},

\displaystyle-k^{\prime}\sin\beta\Im\{u_{1}({\bf q},t)u_{2}({\bf p},t)u_{2}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)\}.

(24)

u_{1}

2.3
Energy Spectrum and Volume Integrals

In this paper, we will focus on homogeneous and isotropic hydrodynamic turbulence in 2D and 3D. However, it is insightful to construct correlation functions for a
d
-dimensional flow:

\left\langle u_{i}({\bf k},t)u_{j}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)\right\rangle=\left[P_{ij}({\bf k})C_{u}({\bf k})-i\epsilon_{ijk}k_{k}\frac{H_{K}({\bf k})}{k^{2}}\right]\delta({\bf k+k^{\prime}}),

(25)

where
C_{u}({\bf k})
is the equal-time velocity correlation function,
H_{K}({\bf k})
is the kinetic helicity, and
\epsilon_{ijk}
is the Levi-Civita tensor. In this paper, we focus on nonhelical flows where
H_{K}({\bf k})=0
. An isotropic
d
-dimensional divergence-free vector field has
d-1
Craya-Herring components with

\left\langle|u_{1}({\bf k}|^{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle|u_{2}({\bf k}|^{2}\right\rangle=...=\left\langle|u_{d-1}({\bf k}|^{2}\right\rangle=C_{u}({\bf k}).

(26)

The total kinetic energy is

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle u^{2}\right\rangle}{2}=\int E(k)dk=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}(d-1)C_{u}({\bf k})=\frac{1}{2}\frac{S_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}(d-1)\int dkk^{d-1}C_{u}({\bf k}),

(27)

where
E(k)
is the one-dimensional (1D) shell spectrum, and
S_{d}=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)
is the surface area of the
d
-dimensional sphere. Specifically,
S_{3}=4\pi
and
S_{2}=2\pi
. Using Eq. (
25
), we derive the following relationship between the modal energy and 1D energy spectrum
(
Kraichnan 1959
;
Leslie 1973
;
Verma 2004
)
:

E(k)=\frac{(d-1)}{2}C_{u}({\bf k})\frac{S_{d}k^{d-1}}{(2\pi)^{d}}.

(28)

Hence, in 3D,

Field-theoretic computations of turbulence involve volume integrals, which are typically of the form

Refer to Fig.
3

p
and
\gamma

\gamma\rightarrow-\gamma
. Hence, we employ

\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\gamma\rightarrow 2\int_{0}^{\pi}d\gamma.

(31)

On the contrary, for the 3D integral, we vary
p
,
\gamma
, and
\phi
. The azimuthal symmetry yields

\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi=2\pi.

(32)

As illustrated in Eq. (
10

\int d{\bf p}
representing the energy transfers to
{\bf u(k)}
from all
{\bf u(p)}

\int d{\bf k}
representing a sum over all
{\bf u(k)}
’s present outside the sphere. Hence, the integrals for the energy fluxes are of the form

\displaystyle\int d\mathrm{VolET}=\int d{\bf k}\int d{\bf p}=\begin{cases}(2\pi\times 2)\int_{R}^{\infty}kdk\int_{0}^{R}pdp\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}\text{~~~for~$d=2$}\\
(4\pi\times 2\pi)\int_{R}^{\infty}k^{2}dk\int_{0}^{R}p^{2}dp\int_{-1}^{1}dz\text{~~~for~$d=3$}\end{cases}

(33)

where
z=\cos\gamma
, and

For ease of integration of Eq. (

Kraichnan 1959

, we employ the following transformation for a triad

(see Fig.

k=\frac{R}{u};~~~p=\frac{Rv}{u};~~~q=\frac{Rw}{u},

Using the Jacobian

(36)

and

For a 2D flow, the corresponding integral is

\int d{\bf p}d{\bf q}\delta({\bf k-p-q})

The integrals for the RG operations have the following form. The coarse-graining operation during renormalization constrains the
p
and

\int d\mathrm{VolRG}=\int d{\bf p}=2\int_{k_{n}}^{k_{n}b}pdp\int_{\mathrm{lower}}^{\mathrm{upper}}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}},

(39)

whereas in 3D,

\int d\mathrm{VolRG}=\int d{\bf p}=2\pi\int_{k_{n}}^{k_{n}b}p^{2}dp\int_{\mathrm{lower}}^{\mathrm{upper}}dz,

(40)

where
p\in(k_{n},k_{n}b)
. We will provide more details on the integration limits in Section

After the above preliminary discussion on the relevant equations and correlation function, etc., we are ready to perform field-theoretic calculation, namely renormalization group and energy transfer analysis. There are many works on the renormalization group analysis of turbulence, hence, we will cover this topic briefly. However, the energy transfer calculations are detailed with several important and novel results.

3
Renormalization Group Analysis of Hydrodynamic Turbulence

In this section, we derive the renormalized viscosities for 2D HDT and 3D HDT using Craya-Herring basis that simplifies the calculations considerably. We follow the recursive RG method proposed by McComb, Zhou, and coworkers
(
McComb & Shanmugasundaram 1983
;
McComb 1990
;
Zhou et al. 1988
)
. Note that the coupling constant, the coefficient in front of the nonlinear term
{\bf u\cdot\nabla u}

(
Forster et al. 1977
;
McComb 2014
)
. Therefore, vertex renormalization is not required in HDT. In addition, in the recursive RG, the forcing or noise is introduced at large scales so as to produce a steady-state with Kolmogorov spectrum. Hence
noise renormalization
too is avoided in this scheme, and the energy spectrum is taken as
k^{-5/3}
. Note that such a choice for
E(k)
is as arbitrary as the choice of noise that yields Kolmogorov’s spectrum (as is done in noise renormalization). For further details, we refer the reader to books and reviews—
Leslie 1973
,
Lesieur 2008
,
McComb 1990
;
McComb 2014
,
Zhou 2010
;
Zhou 2021
,
Sagaut & Cambon 2018
, and references therein.

First, we compute the renormalized viscosity for 2D HDT.

For 2D HDT, the large variations in the value of
\nu_{1*}
is confusing, yet they indicate certain limitations of the RG procedure in handling the complex dynamics of 2D HDT. The renormalized viscosity
\nu_{1}^{(n)}
is derived by coarse-graining the modes in the wavenumber band
(k_{n},k_{n+1})
, and by ignoring the modes with longer wavenumbers. Hence, the RG analysis implicitly assumes locality of interactions. In contrast, the energy flux calculations include both local and nonlocal interactions. The energy transfers between the local Fourier modes are forward, but those between the nonlocal modes are backward; the latter transfers are responsible for the inverse energy cascade [see
Domaradzki & Rogallo 1990
;
Verma et al. 2005
, and Section
4
]. This is the reason why the
effective
viscosity based on the RG analysis is positive, but the eddy viscosity based on energetics is negative
(
Kraichnan 1971
;
Olla 1991
;
Nandy & Bhattacharjee 1995
;
Kraichnan 1976
)
. We believe that exclusion of the nonlocal interactions is the reason for the inaccurate RG predictions of
\nu_{1*}
. Refer to Appendix
B
for details.

Kraichnan 1964a
showed that in HDT, the large-scale structures
sweep
small-scale fluctuations, a phenomena called
sweeping effect
. Based on these observations, Kraichnan argued that Eulerian framework is not suitable for field-theoretic treatment, and advocated for Lagrangian direct interaction approximation (DIA). Since the sweeping effect is related to the nonlocal interactions, our arguments for the inapplicability of RG to 2D turbulence has similarities with those of
Kraichnan 1964a
.

The resolution of the above issues requires detailed theoretical analysis and numerical simulations.
Verma et al. 2020
and
Verma & Alam 2023
performed direct numerical simulation of 3D HDT and the shell model respectively, and computed
\nu(k)
using the unequal-time correlation functions of Eq. (
53
). A similar analysis for 2D HDT may provide us the renormalized viscosity that will help validate the analytical predictions. At the end of this section, we remark that for ease of notation, the renormalized viscosity
\nu^{(n)}_{1}
is conveniently written as
\nu_{1}(k)
; that is,
\nu_{1}(k)
is the effective viscosity for all the wavenumbers less than
k
.

In the next section, we will compute the renormalized viscosity for 3D HDT.

3.2
Renormalization of the
u_{2}
Component of 3D HDT

In 3D HDT, a velocity Fourier mode has two Craya-Herring components,
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
(see Fig.
1
). In this subsection, we compute the renormalized viscosities for the two components. This is unlike past works, e.g.,
Yakhot & Orszag 1986
,
McComb & Shanmugasundaram 1983
, and
Zhou et al. 1989
, where a single renormalized viscosity has been computed. Hence, Craya-Herring basis provides more details on turbulence dynamics.

At first, we compute
\nu_{2}^{(n)}
, for which we follow the same steps as in Section
3.1
. Note, however, that for 3D HDT, we assume that the flow is forced at large scales to achieve a constant energy flux in the inertial range, where we employ our RG analysis. We start with Eq. (
17
), but with all possible triads. One of the intermediate steps in the derivation of
\nu_{2}^{(n)}
is

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\nu_{2}^{(n+1)}k^{2}){u}^{<}_{2}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ik^{\prime}\int\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\{\sin\gamma u_{1}^{<*}({\bf p},t)u_{2}^{<*}({\bf q},t)-\sin\beta u_{1}^{<*}({\bf q},t)u_{2}^{<*}({\bf p},t)\}

\displaystyle+ik^{\prime}\int\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\{\sin\gamma u_{1}^{>*}({\bf p},t)u_{2}^{>*}({\bf q},t)-\sin\beta u_{1}^{>*}({\bf q},t)u_{2}^{>*}({\bf p},t)\},

where
\nu_{2}^{(n+1)}
is the renormalized viscosity for
u_{2}(k)
with
k\in s(k_{0},k_{n+1})
. In the above equation, the terms of the form
\int d{\bf p}\left\langle u_{1}^{>*}({\bf q},t)u_{2}^{>*}({\bf p},t)\right\rangle
contribute to viscosity renormalization. In this subsection we show that
\nu_{2}^{(n+1)}\neq\nu_{1}^{(n+1)}
, which is expected because the dynamical evolution of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are different. We capture this interesting aspect of viscosity renormalization for the first time.

For 3D isotropic HDT, we employ the following scaling laws:

\displaystyle C_{1}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{2}({\bf k})=(2\pi)^{3}\frac{1}{4\pi k^{2}}E(k),

(74)

\displaystyle E(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{\mathrm{Ko}}\epsilon_{u}^{2/3}k^{-5/3},

(75)

\displaystyle\nu^{(n)}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{2*}\sqrt{K_{\mathrm{Ko}}}\epsilon_{u}^{1/3}k_{n}^{-4/3},

(76)

where
\epsilon_{u}
is the energy flux,
K_{\mathrm{Ko}}
and
\nu_{2*}
are the Kolmogorov and renormalization constants, respectively, for 3D HDT.
Following similar steps as in the
\nu_{1*}
computation, we observe that the second integral of Eq. (
3.2
) contributes to the viscosity renormalization. The associated Feynman diagrams are shown in Fig.
6
, and the corresponding integral is

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Delta}\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{kqC_{1}({\bf p})\sin\gamma\sin\alpha+kpC_{1}({\bf q})\sin\beta\sin\alpha}{\nu(p)p^{2}+\nu(q)q^{2}}u_{2}({\bf k^{\prime}},t),

(77)

In the nondimensional equation,
I_{3}
contributes to viscosity renormalization as follows:

\displaystyle\nu_{2*}^{2}(1-b^{-4/3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{b}p^{\prime 2}dp^{\prime}\int^{p^{\prime}/2}_{(p^{\prime 2}+1-b^{2})/(2p^{\prime})}dzF_{3},

(78)

where the prefactor
1/2=2\pi/(4\pi)
is due to the cancellation of factors in Eqs. (
29
,
40
), and

\displaystyle F_{3}(p^{\prime},z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-z^{2})p^{\prime-11/3}+(1-z^{2})p^{\prime 2}q^{\prime-11/3}.

(79)

Feynman diagrams associated with the renormalization of
\nu_{2}
for the
u_{2}
component.

To evaluate the integral of Eq. (

) we employ Gaussian quadrature and a Romberg scheme, as in the computation of
\nu_{1}(k)
. The numerical computation yields
I=0.1197
, hence

\nu_{2*}=\sqrt{\frac{I}{1-b^{-4/3}}}=0.4859.

(80)

For 3D HDT, we compute
\nu_{1*}
using a modified version of Eq. (
69
), which is

\displaystyle\nu_{1*}^{2}(1-b^{-4/3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{b}p^{\prime 2}dp^{\prime}\int^{p^{\prime}/2}_{(p^{\prime 2}+1-b^{2})/(2p^{\prime})}dz(F_{4}+F_{5})

(81)

with

\displaystyle F_{4}(p^{\prime},z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-z^{2})p^{\prime}(p^{\prime}-2z)(2p^{\prime}z-1)q^{\prime-17/3}}{p^{\prime 2/3}+q^{\prime 2/3}},

(82)

\displaystyle F_{5}(p^{\prime},z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-z^{2})(1-p^{\prime 2})(2p^{\prime}z-1)p^{\prime-11/3}q^{\prime-2}}{p^{\prime 2/3}+q^{\prime 2/3}}.

(83)

A numerical integration of Eq. (
81
) yields

\nu_{1*}=0.09218.

(84)

Since
\nu_{1*}\ll\nu_{2*}
, we conclude that for 3D HDT, the
u_{2}
component contributes much more to the renormalized viscosity (
\nu_{*}
) than the
u_{1}
component. Hence, we compare
\nu_{2*}
with the past predictions.
Kraichnan 1971
predicted that
\nu_{*}=0.315
, whereas
McComb 1990
and
Zhou et al. 1988

reported
\nu_{*}
be approximately 0.40. Other researchers too reported similar values for
\nu_{*}

(
Yakhot & Orszag 1986
;
Nandy & Bhattacharjee 1995
)
.
Zhou et al. 1988
argued that an inclusion of triple nonlinearity in the RG analysis increases
\nu_{*}
to around 0.5, but this topic beyond the scope of this paper. Note that our prediction for
\nu_{2*}
is comparable to the past ones.

Using Eqs. (
65
,
76
), we derive that for both
\nu_{1}^{(n)}
and
\nu_{2}^{(n)}
,

\frac{\nu_{1,2}^{(n)}}{\nu_{1,2}^{(n)}}=\left(\frac{k_{n+1}}{k_{n}}\right)^{-4/3}=b^{-4/3}.

(85)

In quantum field theory, we express the running coupling constant in terms of
b=\exp(l)

(
Peskin & Schroeder 1995
)
. Using
b^{-4/3}\approx 1-4l/3
(for small
l
), we derive that

\frac{d\nu}{dl}\approx-\frac{4}{3}\nu.

(86)

Therefore, the renormalized viscosity
\nu
increases with the decreases of
k_{n}
. This is similar to the
running coupling constant
in quantum chromodynamics. A cautionary remark, however, is in order. For the Navier-Stokes equation, the real coupling constant, which is the coefficient of the nonlinear term, is constant. Here,
\nu
is similar to the mass term of the
\phi^{4}
theory
(
Peskin & Schroeder 1995
)
.

With this we close our discussion on the viscosity renormalization for HDT. We employed Craya-Herring basis for our computation that eliminates complex tensor algebra. We will employ
\nu_{1*}
and
\nu_{2*}
for the computation of energy flux and mode-to-mode energy transfers, which are covered in Sections
4
and
5
.

4
Energy Transfers and Flux for the
u_{1}
Component and 2D HDT

Researchers have computed the energy transfers and fluxes for HDT using various field-theoretic tools. Another related topic of interest is
locality of nonlinear interactions
in turbulence. Note, however, that the energy transfers in 2D HDT has not been studied in detail. This paper attempts to fill this gap by computing
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle
and the corresponding energy flux using Craya-Herring basis. Our calculations are not only compact, but they provide detailed insights into the energy transfers in 2D HDT.

In this section, we compute the energy transfer rates and energy flux in the wavenumber regime with
k^{-5/3}
energy spectrum. In the Appendix
C
, we briefly discuss the mode-to-mode enstrophy transfer and enstrophy flux in this spectral region.

4.1
Mode-to-mode Energy Transfers for the
u_{1}
Component

In this subsection, we will compute the mode-to-mode energy transfers between the
u_{1}
components of Craya-Herring basis. We start with Eq. (
23
) and present the ensemble-averaged
mode-to-mode energy transfer
from
u_{1}({\bf p},t)
to
u_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)
with the mediation of
u_{1}({\bf q},t)
, which is

\displaystyle\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k^{\prime}\sin\beta\cos\gamma\Im\{\left\langle u_{1}({\bf q},t)u_{1}({\bf p},t)u_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)\right\rangle\}

(87)

with
{\bf k^{\prime}+p+q}=0
. We assume the turbulence to be homogeneous, isotropic, and steady, and that it satisfies Eqs. (
63
-
65
). To estimate
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle
, it is customary to assume that the variables
u_{1}({\bf p},t)
,
u_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)
, and
u_{1}({\bf q},t)
are quasi-normal, according to which the three-variable correlation of Eq. (
87
) is nonzero (unlike Gaussian variables). However, the first-order expansion of the triple correlation of Eq. (
87
) leads to a fourth-order correlation, that is expanded as a sum of products of two second-order correlations under the assumption that the variables are Gaussian
(
Kraichnan 1959
;
Orszag 1973
)
. The corresponding Feynman diagrams are given in Fig.
7
.

Figure 7:
Feynman diagrams associated with the energy transfers between the
u_{1}
components. Equation (
89
) illustrates two ways to get the second-order correlation functions, which leads to the factor 2 in all the digrams.

Let us evaluate the integral corresponding to the first Feynman diagram of Fig.
7
. Here,
u_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)
is expanded using the Green’s function as [see Eq. (
14
)]

\displaystyle u_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}G_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t-t^{\prime})k^{\prime}\int\frac{d{\bf r}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sin(\beta-\gamma)[u_{1}^{*}({\bf r},t^{\prime})u_{1}^{*}({\bf s},t^{\prime})]

(88)

with
{\bf k^{\prime}+r+s}=0
. Substitution of the above in Eq. (
87
) leads to a fourth-order correlation, which is expanded as a sum of products of two second-order correlations:

\displaystyle\left\langle u_{1}({\bf q},t)u_{1}({\bf p},t)u_{1}({\bf r},t^{\prime})u_{1}({\bf s},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=\left\langle u_{1}({\bf q},t)u_{1}({\bf p},t)\right\rangle\left\langle u_{1}({\bf r},t^{\prime})u_{1}({\bf s},t^{\prime})\right\rangle+

\displaystyle\left\langle u_{1}({\bf q},t)u_{1}({\bf r},t^{\prime})\right\rangle\left\langle u_{1}({\bf p},t)u_{1}({\bf s},t^{\prime})\right\rangle+\left\langle u_{1}({\bf q},t)u_{1}({\bf s},t^{\prime})\right\rangle\left\langle u_{1}({\bf p},t)u_{1}({\bf r},t^{\prime})\right\rangle.

(89)

Note that
\left\langle u_{1}({\bf q},t)u_{1}({\bf p},t)\right\rangle=\left\langle u_{1}({\bf r},t^{\prime})u_{1}({\bf s},t^{\prime})\right\rangle=0
because
{\bf p}\neq-{\bf q}
and
{\bf r}\neq-{\bf s}
. Using the above correlations and Eq. (
25
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\langle u_{1}({\bf q},t)u_{1}({\bf p},t)u_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)\right\rangle_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}G_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t-t^{\prime})ik^{\prime}\int\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sin(\beta-\gamma)\times

(90)

\displaystyle 2[\bar{C}_{1}({\bf p},t-t^{\prime})\bar{C}_{1}({\bf q},t-t^{\prime})].

Using the properties of temporal relations of Eqs. (
52
,
53
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\langle u_{1}({\bf q},t)u_{1}({\bf p},t)u_{1}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)\right\rangle_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i2k^{\prime}\sin(\beta-\gamma)C({\bf p})C({\bf q})}{\nu(k)k^{2}+\nu(p)p^{2}+\nu(q)q^{2}}.

(91)

We compute the integrals corresponding to the other two Feynman diagrams of Fig.
7
and add them to the above, which yields

\displaystyle\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{numr}_{1}}{\nu(k)k^{2}+\nu(p)p^{2}+\nu(q)q^{2}},

(92)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{numr}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2[k^{\prime}\sin(\beta-\gamma)C({\bf p})C({\bf q})+p\sin(\gamma-\alpha)C({\bf k^{\prime}})C({\bf q})+q\sin(\alpha-\beta)C({\bf k^{\prime}})C({\bf p})]

(93)

\displaystyle\times k^{\prime}\sin\beta\cos\gamma.

The physics in the inertial range is scale invariant. Hence, we employ the transformation of Eq. (
34
) to simplify the integral of Eq. (
92
), which leads to

\displaystyle\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle(v,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{numr}_{2}}{\pi^{2}(1+v^{2/3}+w^{2/3})}=k^{4}\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle,

(94)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{numr}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2w^{-2}[(2v^{2}z^{2}-vz)(vw)^{-8/3}+(v^{3}z-2v^{2}z^{2})w^{-8/3}+(1-v^{2})zv^{-5/3}](1-z^{2}),

and
w^{2}=1+v^{2}-2vz
. We study the properties of mode-to-mode interactions in a triad
(1,v,w)
using Eqs. (
94
,
4.1
). Note that

\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle
has a dimension of
k^{-4}
, whereas
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle
is dimensionless. In Fig.
8
(a) we illustrate the density plot of
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle(v,z)
. We deduce the following properties for the energy transfers in 2D HDT using this plot and Eqs. (
94
,
4.1
).

It is straightfoward to show that
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle=0
for an equilateral triad for which
v=w=1
and
z=1/2
(or
\gamma=\pi/3
). This triad is illustrated as Case A in Fig.
9
. Interestingly,
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle\approx 0
for triads with
v\approx 1
that corresponds to the central region of Fig.
8
(a). The triads with
v\approx w\gg 1
too have negligible energy transfers because

\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle\approx-\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}z^{2}(1-z^{2})v^{-8/3}\rightarrow 0

(96)

in this regime.

In 2D HDT, the triads with
v\approx 1
and
z\approx 1
(Type C and D of Fig.
9
), and the triads with
v\approx 0
(Type E) dominate the energy transfers. For Type C and D triads,
v\approx 1
,
z\approx 1
, and

w\approx 0
. Hence, Eq. (
4.1
) yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{numr}_{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2w^{-14/3}[(2v^{2}z^{2}-vz)v^{-8/3}+v^{3}z-2v^{2}z^{2}](1-z^{2})

(97)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2w^{-14/3}vz[(2vz(v^{-8/3}-1)+v^{2}(1-v^{-14/3})](1-z^{2})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2w^{-14/3}vz[(2vz(-8/3)(v-1)+v^{2}(14/3)(v-1)](1-z^{2})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{8}{3}w^{-14/3}(1-v)(1-z).

Therefore,

\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle\approx\frac{\mathrm{numr}_{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\approx\frac{4}{3\pi^{2}}w^{-14/3}(1-v)(1-z).

(98)

These
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle
’s contribute significantly to the energy flux due to
w^{-14/3}
singularity. Clearly,
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle>0
for
v=1-\epsilon
, and
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle<0
for
v=1+\epsilon
(
\epsilon>0
). Hence, the energy transfers between the neighbouring wavenumbers is forward, i.e., from smaller wavenumber to larger wavenumber. This feature appears to contradict the inverse energy cascade in the
k^{-5/3}
spectral regime, but it will be resolved in the following discussion.

Next, we consider the triads of Type E, for which
v\approx 0
and
w\approx 1
. For this case, using
w^{2}=1+v^{2}-2vz\approx 1-2vz
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{numr}_{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2v^{-5/3}z[(2vz-1)(1+(8/3)vz)+1-v^{2}](1-z^{2})

(99)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2v^{-5/3}z\left[-\frac{2}{3}vz+v^{2}\left(\frac{16}{3}z^{2}-1\right)\right](1-z^{2})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{3}v^{-2/3}z^{2}(1-z^{2}).

Hence,

\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle\approx\frac{\mathrm{numr}_{2}}{2\pi^{2}}\approx-\frac{2}{3\pi^{2}}v^{-2/3}z^{2}(1-z^{2})

(100)

Therefore,
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle<0
for such triads. That is, the modes with small wavenumber receive energy from
u_{1}(k=1)
. These energy transfers lead to an inverse energy transfers in 2D HDT, and they overcompensate the forward local energy transfers due to the triads with
v\approx 1
. Interestingly, for triads of Type E, the mode-to-mode enstrophy transfer has mixed sign that leads to a forward enstrophy flux. Refer to Appendix
C
for details.

In Fig.
8
(b) we plot
\mathrm{VolET}\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle
, which is
v\log(1/v)/\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle
. The two subfigures exhibit similar behaviour, except that the energy transfers in Fig.
8
(b) are amplified for the
z\approx 1
region due to the
1/\sqrt{1-z^{2}}
factor.

4.2
Energy flux for the
u_{1}
Component and 2D HDT

In 2D HDT, the energy flux for a wavenumber sphere of radius
R
is the cumulative energy transfers between the
u_{1}
components, as given below:

\left\langle\Pi(R)\right\rangle=\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{d{\bf k^{\prime}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{R}\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle.

(101)

Using
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle
derived in the previous subsection, we compute the energy flux for 2D HDT in the
k^{-5/3}
spectral regime. We substitute
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle
in Eq. (
101
)), and employ the change of variables from
k,p,q
to
u,v,w
using Eq. (
34
). Consequently, the energy flux equation gets transformed to

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle\Pi(R)\right\rangle}{\epsilon_{u}}=\frac{K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}^{3/2}}{\nu_{1*}}I_{4},

(102)

where

\displaystyle I_{4}=\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}dv[\log(1/v)]v\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}\frac{\mathrm{numr}_{2}}{(1+v^{2/3}+w^{2/3}))},

(103)

with
\mathrm{numr}_{2}
given in Eq. (
4.1
), and the prefactor
4/\pi=4\pi/(\pi^{2})
is due to the cancellation of factors in Eqs. (
30
,
37
).

We compute
I_{4}
numerically. To avoid inacurracies due to singularities in
I_{4}
, we employ the Gauss-Jacobi quadrature for the
dz
integral, and a Romberg iterative scheme for the
dv
integral. Refer to Section
A
for a brief discussion on the integration procedure. This process yields
I_{4}\approx-0.07569
, whose negative sign indicates an inverse cascade of energy. Since
\Pi_{u}(R)
of Eq. (
102
) is independent of
R
, we conclude that the inertial-range energy flux is constant. Since
\left\langle\Pi(R)\right\rangle=-\epsilon_{u}
and
\nu_{1*}=0.1441
, we estimate the Kolmogorov constant for 2D HDT as

K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}=\left(\frac{\nu_{1*}}{I_{4}}\right)^{2/3}\approx 1.536.

(104)

The above
K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}
differs significantly from the previously reported values of
K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}
, which is around 6. For example,
Kraichnan 1971
reported that
K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}=6.69
.
Olla 1991
and
Nandy & Bhattacharjee 1995
reported similar constants in their field-theoretic calculation. These discrepancies may be due to certain limitations of field-theoretic treatment for 2D HDT, as discussed in Section
3.1
.

Interestingly, by changing the lower limit of the integral of Eq. (
103
) from
\int_{0}^{1}dv
to
\int_{0.22}^{1}dv
, our formalism yields
I_{4}\approx-0.009773
and

\bar{K}_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}=6.013,

(105)

which is in a reasonable agreement with the previous results. The integral
I_{4}
is nearly zero for the lower integral limit of 0.265; for this case,
\bar{K}_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}\rightarrow\infty
. That is,
\bar{K}_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}
increases as the lower limit of
v
approaches 0.265. Note that the physical constants depend on integral cutoffs
(
Kraichnan 1959
;
Leslie 1973
;
Peskin & Schroeder 1995
)
, hence the above adjustment is reasonable. In the following discussion, we provide a physical interpretation for the above feature.

(R,R/V)
. See Fig.

We choose
V=s^{-n}
with
s=2^{1/4}
and

\int_{V}^{1}dvT(v)
for these

that exhibits complex energy transfers in 2D HDT. First,
\int_{V}^{1}dvT(v)>0
for
V\gtrapprox 0.265
. That is, any wavenumber band
(RV,R)
with
0.265<V<1
transfers energy in the forward direction. Second,
\int_{0}^{1}dvT(v)=-1
implying that the modes in the band
(0,0.265R)
receive energy via nonlocal interactions and overcompensate the forward energy transfer
\int_{0.265}^{1}dvT(v)
. The latter energy transfers are responsible for the inverse energy cascade.

For small
V
, we can estimate
\int_{0}^{V}dvT(v)
using asymptotic analysis. Since,
v\ll 1
for this case, we employ Eqs. (
100
,
106

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{V}dvT(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}^{3/2}}{\nu_{1*}}\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{V}^{1}dv[\log(1/v)]v\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right)v^{-2/3}z^{2}(1-z^{2})

(107)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3}\frac{K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}^{3/2}}{\nu_{1*}}\int_{0}^{V}dv[\log(1/v)]v^{1/3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\frac{K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}^{3/2}}{\nu_{1*}}V^{4/3}\left(\log(1/V)+\frac{3}{4}\right)

with
\mathrm{Ko2D}=1.536
and
\nu_{1*}=0.1441

\displaystyle\int_{V}^{1}dvT(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}dvT(v)-\int_{0}^{V}dvT(v)

(108)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1+\frac{1}{4}\frac{K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}^{3/2}}{\nu_{1*}}V^{4/3}\left(\log(1/V)+\frac{3}{4}\right),

which is plotted in Fig.

as triangles. Note that the above formula is in good agreement with the numerical results up to
V\approx 0.2
. Overall, the consistent picture exhibited in Fig.

furnish a strong credence to our field-theoretic calculation of energy transfers.

Let us compare our
\int_{V}^{1}dvT(v)
with a similar quantity proposed by
Kraichnan 1971
,
\int_{v}^{1}ds[Q(s)/s]
, which is the fraction of inertial-range energy transfer from triads for whom the ratio of the smallest to the middle wavenumber is greater than
v
. The two functions are very different, as is evident from Fig.
11
and Fig. 2 of
Kraichnan 1971
. The most crucial difference is that
\int_{0}^{1}dvT(v)=-1
, but
\int_{0}^{1}ds[Q(s)/s]=0
. Hence,
Kraichnan 1971
’s formula does not capture the inverse energy cascade well. We believe that the crucial differentiation between
giver
and
receiver
mode in our formalism makes our method more reliable than that of
Kraichnan 1971
.

The fractional energy fluxes discussed above indicate the importance of nonlocal energy transfers in creating the inverse energy cascade in 2D HDT. In comparison, the local energy transfers (from
p\lessapprox k
) are forward. The opposing nature of local and nonlocal energy transfers is a possible reason for the inadequacy of RG analysis in providing a correct renormalized viscosity
\nu(k)
(see Section
3.1
). Note that 3D HDT too has significant local and nonlocal energy transfers, but they are both forward. Hence, there is no apparent contradiction in the RG calculation for 3D HDT (see Section
5
).

Thus, the field-theoretic calculations of energy transfers provide valuable insights into the turbulence dynamics in 2D HDT, in particular, its inverse energy cascade. We also remind the reader about the benefits of Craya-Herring basis and the integral
\int dpdz
. Our integrals and the asymptotic analysis are much more compact than those reported earlier using the cartesian basis and
\int dpdq

(
Kraichnan 1959
;
Orszag 1973
)
.

Verma et al. 2005

Verma et al. 2005

\int dpdq

\int dpdz

,

,

\displaystyle\frac{K_{\mathrm{KoCH2}}^{3/2}}{\nu_{2*}}\frac{9}{40}\left(\frac{k-p}{k}\right)^{4/3}.

for the type C triads of Fig.

, we deduce that

. Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{V}^{1}dvT(v)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{K_{\mathrm{KoCH2}}^{3/2}}{\nu_{2*}}\frac{9}{40}\left(\frac{q}{k}\right)^{4/3}.

,
133

or

. Many authors, e.g.,
Kraichnan 1971
,
Domaradzki & Rogallo 1990
,

Verma et al. 2005
,
Aluie & Eyink 2009
, arrived at similar conclusions. In contrast, using
k^{-3/2}
energy spectrum,
Kraichnan 1959
predicted

, which is incorrect. Note, however, that 3D HDT has significant energy transfers from nonlocal triads, as described above.

It is instructive to study
\int_{V}^{1}dvT(v)
for some sample

\int_{0.7}^{1}dvT(v)\approx 0.18
. Hence, only 18% energy is transferred from the giver modes with

. Thus, only a fraction of energy is transferred from triads with
k\approx p
, in contrast to the general belief in local energy transfers in 3D HDT. Also,

. Consequently, around 17% contribution to the energy flux comes from the nonlocal triads for which the giver wavenumbers have

, consistent with the above discussion.

In summary, our calculations on the mode-to-mode energy transfers and energy flux, as well as their asymptotic limits, show that in 3D HDT, the cumulative local and nonlocal energy transfers are of the same order, and they are forward. The foward nonlocal energy transfers do not cancel the local transfers, which are encountered in the RG analysis. This is possibly the reason why the RG predictions of
\nu^{(n)}_{2}
for 3D HDT are similar. Note, however, that
\nu^{(n)}_{1}
predictions for 2D HDT have significant variations including the sign changes. We attribute these contradictory results to the nonlocal energy transfers that oppose the local transfers in 2D HDT.

The above detailed calculations exhibit the complexity of energy transfers in HDT. In the next two sections we report the shell-to-shell energy transfers in 3D and 2D HDT.

6
Shell-to-shell Energy Transfers in 3D HDT

In comparison to the energy flux, shell-to-shell (S2S) energy transfers provide a more detailed information about the turbulence dynamics. EDQNM calculations and numerical simulations reveal that maximal energy transfers take place among the neighbouring shells, and that the energy transfer decreases rapidly when the distance between the giver and receiver shells increases
(
Domaradzki & Rogallo 1990
;
Verma et al. 2005
;
Aluie & Eyink 2009
)
.
Verma et al. 2005
performed a preliminary calculation of the S2S energy transfers using field theory. The present field-theoretic computations expand the previous work, as well as correct some errors in them.

We divide the wavenumber space logarithmically into shells
(
Domaradzki & Rogallo 1990
;
Dar et al. 2001
;
Lesieur 2008
)
, with the
n
-th shell representing the wavenumbers band
(k_{0}s^{n},k_{0}s^{n+1})
. In this paper, we compute the shell-to-shell energy transfer from the
m
-th shell to the
n
-th shell using

\left\langle T^{m}_{n}\right\rangle=\int_{k_{0}s^{n}}^{k_{0}s^{n+1}}\frac{d{\bf k^{\prime}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{k_{0}s^{m}}^{k_{0}s^{m+1}}\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left\langle S^{u_{2}u_{2}}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle.

(134)

The above formula, proposed by
Dar et al. 2001
and
Verma 2004
, removes the ambiguity between the giver and receiver wavenumbers that plagues the earlier works
(
Domaradzki & Rogallo 1990
)
. In our S2S energy transfer computation for 3D HDT, we ignore the energy transfers via the
u_{1}

8
Conclusions

In this paper, we employ perturbative field theory to incompressible Navier-Stokes equation in 2D and 3D, and compute the renormalized viscosities, energy transfers, energy fluxes, and shell-to-shell energy transfers. The novelty of our approach is an application of Craya-Herring basis that enables separate evaluation of the renormalized viscosities and energy transfers for the two components,
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
. These novel calculations provide many valuable insights into the turbulence dynamics.

For 2D HDT, we successfully compute the mode-to-mode energy transfers, energy flux, and shell-to-shell energy transfers. These computations reveal forward energy transfer for neighbouring wavenumbers or shells, and inverse energy transfer for the distant wavenumbers or shells. That is, in a triad
(k,p,q)
, where
k,p,q
are the receiver, giver, and mediator wavenumbers respectively, the energy flows from
p
to
k
when
p\lessapprox k
(local), but from
k
to
p
when
p\ll k
(nonlocal). In addition, in 2D HDT, individual local transfers are significant, but the nonlocal ones are weak and decrease as
(k/p)^{-4/3}
. However, the cumulative nonlocal energy transfer is significantly large and it overcompensates the forward local energy transfers to yield an inverse energy cascade.

For 2D HDT, the renormalized viscosity
\nu^{(n)}_{1}
computed using the RG analysis is somewhat uncertain. Our
\nu^{(n)}_{1}
is positive, but it is around 4 times smaller than those reported by
Kraichnan 1971
,
Olla 1991
, and
Nandy & Bhattacharjee 1995
. Note, however, that some researchers reported negative
\nu^{(n)}_{1}
, while
Liang & Diamond 1993
argued that the RG fixed point for 2D HDT is unstable. We believe that the inconsistencies in
\nu^{(n)}_{1}
is due to the inability of the RG analysis to take into account the distant or nonlocal interactions. The opposing nature of the local and nonlocal interactions in 2D HDT makes the RG analysis quite complex, and it needs to be refined in future.

Interestingly, the eddy viscosity for 2D HDT is negative because it takes into account both local and nonlocal energy transfers (see Appendix
B
). On the contrary, the positive
\nu^{(n)}_{1}
computed by some authors is due to the forward energy transfers between the local wavenumbers, which goes into the RG calculations. Note that the sweeping effect
(
Kraichnan 1964a
)
is related to the nonlocal interactions between the large-scale structures and small-scale fluctuations. Hence, ours and Kraichnan’s arguments on the inapplicability of field-theoretic tools to HDT in Eulerian framework have certain similarities.

In 3D HDT, each velocity mode has two components in Craya-Herring basis. In this paper, we compute the
renormalized viscosities (
\nu^{(n)}_{1}
and
\nu^{(n)}_{2}
) and energy transfers for both the components. Interestingly,
\nu^{(n)}_{1}\ll\nu^{(n)}_{2}
, and the mode-to-mode energy transfers and the energy flux via
u_{1}
channel is much weaker than those via
u_{2}
channel. Hence, we focus on the latter energy transfers.

For 3D HDT, we compute the shell-to-shell energy transfers using field theory and find both local and nonlocal energy transfers to be forward. The local transfers are strong, but the nonlocal ones are weak and they fall off as
(k/p)^{-4/3}
. These results are consistent with the earlier works, e.g.,
Domaradzki & Rogallo 1990
,
Verma et al. 2005
, and
Aluie & Eyink 2009
. More importantly, we compute the cumulative local and nonlocal energy transfers via fractional energy flux and shell-to-shell energy transfers. We show that
the cumulative local and nonlocal energy transfers are of the same order
. This new result is contrary to earlier predictions by
Domaradzki & Rogallo 1990
,
Verma et al. 2005
, and
Aluie & Eyink 2009
that the cumulative local energy transfers dominate the nonlocal ones. Earlier,
Verma & Donzis 2007
had argued that the origin of the
bottleneck effect
may be the nonlocal energy transfers. There are possibly more consequences of our finding on turbulence modelling.

A typical derivation of
k^{-5/3}
energy spectrum for HDT employs locality of interactions. Does the nonlocal energy transfers discussed in this paper spoil this scaling argument? Fortunately, this is not the case because cumulative energy transfers for both local and nonlocal triads scale as
(k/p)^{-4/3}
[see Eqs. (
129
,
128
)]; this common scaling law for the energy transfers saves the scaling arguments employed in the derivation of
k^{-5/3}
spectrum.

For 3D HDT, our RG predictions of
\nu^{(n)}_{2}
and the Kolmogorov constant are in agreement with the past results by various authors
(
Yakhot & Orszag 1986
;
McComb 1990
;
Zhou 2010
)
. Note that the coarse-graining in RG analysis takes into account the local interactions, not the nonlocal ones. Since the forward nonlocal energy transfers do not counter the local ones in 3D HDT,
\nu^{(n)}_{2}
computed using the RG analysis do not vary significantly. This feature differs from
\nu^{(n)}_{1}
of 2D HDT that exhibits significant variations because of the opposing directions of energy transfers for the local and nonlocal triads.

In summary, our field-theoretic calculations provide many valuable insights into the turbulence dynamics, in particular on the local and nonlocal energy transfers. In this paper we report the renormalized viscosities and the Kolmogorov constants for 2D and 3D HDT. It is utmost important to test these predictions using high-resolution numerical simulations and experiments. The renormalized viscosity can be computed using the two-time correlation functions in the inertial range
(
Verma et al. 2020
;
Verma & Alam 2023
)
, whereas the fractional energy flux and shell-to-shell energy transfers can be computed using Fourier transforms
(
Dar et al. 2001
;
Verma 2019
)
. Note, however, that these computations may require very high-resolution (e.g.,
8192^{3}
grid) and long numerical simulations. We hope that these tests will be performed in near future.

The framework presented in this paper is general that may be applicable to other flows, e.g., scalar turbulence, magnetohydrodynamic turbulence, rotating turbulence, etc. We plan to perform these calculations in future.

Appendix A
Evaluation of the Renormalization and Energy-Flux Integrals

Computation of field-theoretic integrals, which are typically singular, is always tricky
(
Peskin & Schroeder 1995
)
. In this section, we briefly describe how we overcome singularities in our calculations.

For the RG procedure, the integrals of Eqs. (
69
,
78
) are finite because they are performed in the band
1\leq p^{\prime}\leq b
and
1\leq q^{\prime}\leq b
. Here, we employ the Gaussian quadrature for the
dq^{\prime}
integral and a Romberg scheme for the
dp^{\prime}
integral. This procedure yields finite and accurate results. However, the integrals for the energy fluxes are singular and they need special attention.

In 2D HDT, the energy-flux integral is of the following form:

\displaystyle I=\int_{0}^{1}dv[\log(1/v)]v\int_{-1}^{1}dzf(v,z)\sqrt{1-z^{2}},

(148)

where
f(v,z)
involves singularities. For accurate evaluation of
dz
integration, we employ the Gauss-Jacobi quadrature:

\int_{-1}^{1}dzf(v,z)(1-z)^{1/2}(1+z)^{1/2}\approx\sum_{k}f(v,z_{k})w_{k},

(149)

where
z_{k}
is the
k
th root of Jacobi polynomials, and
w_{k}
is the corresponding
weight
. Note that
f(v,z_{k})
is evaluated at
z=z_{k}
. The Gauss-Jacobi quadrature yields finite answer for these singular integrals. We employ a Romberg iterative scheme for the subsequent
dv
integration.

In 3D HDT, the energy-flux integral is of the following form:

\displaystyle I=\int_{0}^{1}dv[\log(1/v)]v^{4}\int_{-1}^{1}dzg(v,z)(1-z^{2}).

(150)

The
dz
integration for the above form is performed using the Gauss-Jacobi quadrature as follow:

\int_{-1}^{1}dzg(v,z)(1-z)^{1}(1+z)^{1}=\sum_{k}g(v,z_{k})w^{\prime}_{k},

(151)

where
\{z_{k}\}
and
\{w^{\prime}_{k}\}
are the roots and weights of the Jacobi polynomials. After this, a Romberg iterative scheme is employed for the
dv
integration.

The integral computations for the shell-to-shell energy transfers are similar to those for the energy fluxes.

Appendix B
Eddy Viscosity and its Relation to the Renormalized Viscosity

Eddy viscosity is an important quantity in atmospheric physics and astrophysics. It represents an enhanced viscosity at large scales due to turbulence.
Kraichnan 1976
and
Leith & Kraichnan 1972
computed the eddy viscosity using EDQNM approximation and field theory.

McComb & Shanmugasundaram 1983
and
Zhou et al. 1989
argued that eddy viscosity is same as the renormalized viscosity. In fact, they use the phrase “renormalized eddy viscosity” for
\nu^{(n)}
. In this Appendix, we relate these two viscosities using the RG equation.

The RG equation for velocity field is
(
Yakhot & Orszag 1986
)

\displaystyle[\partial_{t}+\nu^{(n)}k^{2}]{\bf u}^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\int\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{d}}[{\bf k\cdot u^{<}(q},t)]{\bf u^{<}(p},t)+{\bf F^{<}(k^{\prime}},t),

(152)

where
\nu^{(n)}
is the renormalized viscosity at
k=k_{n}
, the cutoff wavenumber;
{\bf F^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)
is the external force; and
{\bf k^{\prime}+p+q}=0
. In Eq. (
152
), the convolution
\int d{\bf p}
is performed over
0<p\leq k_{n}
(also see Section
3
). Here, we employ cartesian coordinate system that simplifies the derivation of the connection between the renormalized viscosity and eddy viscosity. Using Eq. (
152
), we derive the following equation for the modal energy
E(k^{\prime},t)=|{\bf u}^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)|^{2}/2
:

\displaystyle[\partial_{t}+2\nu^{(n)}k^{2}]E^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\Im\{[{\bf k\cdot u^{<}(q},t)][{\bf u^{<}(p},t)\cdot{\bf u^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)]\}

(153)

\displaystyle+\Re[{\bf F^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)\cdot{\bf u^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)],

where
\Re[.]
and
\Im[.]
represent, respectively, the real and imaginary parts of the argument. Note that

\int\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\Im\{[{\bf k\cdot u^{<}(q},t)][{\bf u^{<}(p},t)\cdot{\bf u^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)]\}=0

(154)

because of the
detailed conservation of energy

(
Kraichnan 1959
;
Verma 2019
)
. The above identity also follows from the fact that

\int d{\bf x}[({\bf u\cdot\nabla u})\cdot{\bf u}]=\int d{\bf x}\nabla\cdot[\frac{u^{2}}{2}{\bf u}]=0,

(155)

where the derivatives are performed at length scale
2\pi/k_{n}
. As a consequence of the above conservation law, Eq. (
154
) gets simplified to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)=-2\nu^{(n)}k^{2}E^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)+\Re[{\bf F^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)\cdot{\bf u^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)].

(156)

Equation (
156
) has the same structure as the dissipative Navier-Stokes equation, except that
\nu
of the original equation is replaced by
\nu^{(n)}
. The renormalized viscosity varies with wavenumber, hence it is called
eddy viscosity
.

We can derive interesting relations based on Eq. (
156
). An integration Eq. (
156
) from
k=0
to
k_{n}
yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{k_{n}}dk^{\prime}E^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)=-\int_{0}^{k_{n}}dk^{\prime}2\nu^{(n)}k^{2}E^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)+\int_{0}^{k_{n}}dk^{\prime}\Re[{\bf F^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)\cdot{\bf u^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)].

(157)

For 3D HDT, under steady state (
\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{k_{n}}[.]=0
). Hence,

\int_{0}^{k_{n}}dk^{\prime}2\nu^{(n)}k^{2}E^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)=\int_{0}^{k_{n}}dk^{\prime}\Re[{\bf F^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)\cdot{\bf u^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)]=\epsilon_{u}.

(158)

That is, the energy injected at large scales leaves the wavenumber sphere as the energy flux. This feature forms a basis for the
Large Eddy Simulations
. Interestingly, with
E(k,t)=K_{\mathrm{Ko}}\epsilon_{u}^{2/3}k^{-5/3}
,
the integral of Eq. (
158
) yields

\int_{0}^{k_{n}}dk^{\prime}2\nu^{(n)}k^{2}E^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)=\frac{3}{2}\nu_{2*}K_{\mathrm{KoCH2}}^{3/2}\epsilon_{u}\approx 1.638\epsilon_{u},

(159)

which is close to
\epsilon_{u}
. This is reasonable considering various approximations made in the calculation, e.g.,
k^{-5/3}
extends to all the wavenumber range.

Equation (
157
) leads to some inconsistencies in 2D HDT that is forced at
k=k_{f}
. For
k<k_{f}
, in the absence of
{\bf F^{<}(k^{\prime}},t)
, Eq. (
157
) predicts that

\partial_{t}\int_{0}^{k_{n}}dk^{\prime}E^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)=-\int_{0}^{k_{n}}dk^{\prime}2\nu^{(n)}k^{2}E^{<}({\bf k^{\prime}},t)<0

(160)

for positive
\nu^{(n)}
. That is, the large-scale energy decays with time in spite of the inverse energy cascade. This is a contradiction that can be resolved only if
\nu^{(n)}<0
. Note that
Kraichnan 1976
predicted eddy viscosity to be negative for 2D HDT based on the energy flux computation. On the contrary, RG analysis yields positive
\nu^{(n)}
due to the forward energy transfers for the local interactions (see Section
3.1
). These observations indicate that we need a closer look at the field-theoretic treatment of 2D HDT, as well as local interaction issues in the RG treatment of turbulence.

Appendix C

In a 2D HDT that is forced at

(
Kraichnan 1967
;
Boffetta & Ecke 2012
)
. In Sections

C.1
Mode-to-mode Enstrophy Transfer

For 2D HDT, the enstrophy transfer rate from
\omega({\bf p})
to
\omega({\bf k})
with the mediation of
{\bf u}({\bf q})
is
(
Verma 2019
)

S^{\omega\omega}({\bf k|p|q})=\Im\left[\{{\bf k}\cdot{\bf u}({\bf q},t)\}\{\omega({\bf p},t)\omega^{*}({\bf k},t)\}\}\right].

(161)

A simplification of Eq. (
161
) yields
(
Verma 2019
)

\displaystyle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf k|p|q})=\frac{kp}{\cos\gamma}S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf k|p|q}).

(162)

In terms of the transformed variables
u,v,w
of Eq. (
34
), we obtain

\displaystyle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})=\frac{v}{z}S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w}).

(163)

For enstrophy transfers in 2D HDT: (a) Density plot of

. (b) Density plot of
[v\log(1/v)/\sqrt{1-z^{2}}]\left\langle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle
.

We have already estimated
S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})
using field theory (see Section
4
). We will use these results to estimate
S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})
. In Fig.
17
, we illustrate the density plots of
S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})
and
\mathrm{VolET}S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})
. It is interesting to contrast
Fig.
8
and Fig.
17
.

Triads of Type A and B of Fig.
9
have small
S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})
due to its proportionality with
S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})
. However, triads of Type C, D, and E exhibit large magnitudes for
S^{\omega\omega}({\bf k|p|q})
, and their asymptotic hehaviour are as follows. For triads with
v\approx 1
and
z\approx 1
(Types C and D), using Eq. (
98
), we derive that

\left\langle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle\approx\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle\approx\frac{4}{3\pi^{2}}w^{-14/3}(1-v)(1-z).

(164)

For the triads with
v\approx 0
(Type E),
\left\langle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle
and

\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle
have very different properties. Following Eq. (
99
), we derive that

\displaystyle\left\langle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v}{z}\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle

(165)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\left[-\frac{2}{3}v^{1/3}z+v^{4/3}\left(\frac{16}{3}z^{2}-1\right)\right](1-z^{2}).

To a leading-order, Eq. (
165
) yields

\left\langle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle\approx-\frac{2}{3\pi^{2}}v^{1/3}z

(166)

that satisfies the following property:

\left\langle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle(v,z)=-\left\langle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle(v,-z).

(167)

Hence,

\left\langle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle=\begin{cases}>0&\mathrm{for}~z<0\\
<0&\mathrm{for}~z>0\end{cases}

(168)

which is unlike
\left\langle S^{u_{1}u_{1}}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle
where the contributions from all of small
z
’s add up. Figures
8
and
17
clearly illustrate the above contrast the small
z
. These contrasting behaviour is responsible for the inverse energy cascade and the forward, but vanishing, enstrophy flux in the
k^{-5/3}
regime of 2D HDT
(
Boffetta & Ecke 2012
)
.

Note, however, that
\int_{-1}^{1}zdz=0
. Hence, the integral of the leading-order term of Eq. (
165
) vanishes. However, the integral of
\left\langle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf 1|v|w})\right\rangle(v,z)
is nonzero due to the higher-order terms of Eq. (
165
).

C.2
Enstrophy Flux

The enstrophy flux for a wavenumber sphere of radius
R
is defined as
(
Lesieur 2008
;
Verma 2019
)

\Pi_{\omega}(R)=\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{d{\bf k^{\prime}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{R}\frac{d{\bf p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left\langle S^{\omega\omega}({\bf k^{\prime}|p|q})\right\rangle.

(169)

Using the relation of Eq. (
162
) and Eqs. (
102
,
103
), we derive a field-theoretic estimate of the enstrophy flux as

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle\Pi_{\omega}(k)\right\rangle}{\epsilon_{u}}=k^{2}\frac{K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}^{3/2}}{\nu_{1*}}4\pi\int_{0.256}^{1}dv[\log(1/v)]v\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}\frac{v}{z}\frac{\mathrm{numr}_{2}}{\pi^{2}(1+v^{2/3}+w^{2/3}))}

(170)

with
\mathrm{Ko2D}=6.013
and
\nu_{1*}=0.1441
. We choose the lower limit for the
dv
integral as 0.22, as in the energy flux computation that yields
\mathrm{Ko2D}=6.395
. We employ the Gauss-Jacobi quadrature for the
dz
integral, and a Romberg scheme for the
dv
integral. Our integral computation yields

\frac{\left\langle\Pi_{\omega}(k)\right\rangle}{\epsilon_{u}}\approx k^{2}\frac{K_{\mathrm{Ko2D}}^{3/2}}{\nu_{1*}}0.02242\approx 3.534k^{2}.

(171)

For 2D HDT with
k_{f}=100

Interestingly, the enstrophy flux is positive, but it decreases as
k\rightarrow 0
. The opposite signs for the enstrophy and energy fluxes are consistent with
Fjørtoft 1953
’s argument that in 2D HDT, the energy and enstrophy cascade along different directions.
Gupta et al. 2019
simulated 2D HDT and computed the enstrophy flux for a forced case. Figure
18
illustrates
\Pi_{\omega}(k)
for one of their runs. The figure illustrates that
\Pi_{\omega}(k)\sim k^{2}
, consistent with the predictions of Eq. (
171
). For 3D HDT,
Verma 2019
employed scaling arguments and showed that
\left\langle\Pi_{\omega}(k)\right\rangle\sim k^{2}
.
Sadhukhan et al. 2019
verified the above results using numerical simulations.

We expect certain complications arising due to wavenumber-dependent (
k^{2}
) enstrophy flux,
\Pi_{\omega}(k)

(
Gupta et al. 2019
;
Verma 2022
)
. We will address this issue in a future communication.
