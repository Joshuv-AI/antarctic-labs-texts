A modular construction of type theories
Frédéric Blanqui ORCID:0000-0001-7438-5554, Gilles Dowek ORCID:0000-0001-6253-935X, Emilie Grienenberger, Gabriel Hondet, François Thiré

Abstract
. The λ ​ Π \lambda\Pi -calculus modulo theory is a logical framework in which many type systems can be expressed as theories. We present such a theory, the theory 𝒰 {\mathcal{U}} , where proofs of several logical systems can be expressed. Moreover, we identify a sub-theory of 𝒰 {\mathcal{U}} corresponding to each of these systems, and prove that, when a proof in 𝒰 {\mathcal{U}} uses only symbols of a sub-theory, then it is a proof in that sub-theory.

Introduction

The
\lambda\Pi\text{-calculus modulo theory}
(

)
[

]

[

]
,
\lambda\text{-Prolog}

[
NadathurMiller
]
, Isabelle
[
Paulson
]
, the

[

]
, also called the

,

[

,
dowek03jsl
]
, Pure type systems

[

,

[

,

,

,
Grienenberger19
]
. It is
thus an extension of Predicate logic that provides the
possibility for all symbols to bind variables, a syntax for
proof-terms, a notion of computation, a notion of proof reduction for
axiomatic theories, and the possibility to express both constructive
and classical proofs.

\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
enables to express all theories that can be expressed in
Predicate logic, such as geometry, arithmetic, and set theory, but
also Simple type theory
[
Church40
]
and the Calculus of
constructions
[
CoquandHuet88
]
, that are less easy to define in
Predicate logic.

We present a theory in
\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
, the theory
{\mathcal{U}}
, where all proofs of
Minimal, Constructive, and Ecumenical predicate logic; Minimal,
Constructive, and Ecumenical simple type theory; Simple type theory
with predicate subtyping, prenex predicative polymorphism, or both;
the Calculus of constructions, and the Calculus of constructions with
prenex predicative polymorphism can be expressed. This theory is
therefore a candidate for a universal theory, where proofs developed
in implementations of Classical predicate logic (such as automated
theorem proving systems, SMT solvers, etc.), Classical simple type
theory (such as HOL 4, Isabelle/HOL, HOL Light, etc.), the Calculus of
constructions (such as Coq, Matita, Lean, etc.), and Simple type
theory with predicate subtyping and prenex polymorphism (such as PVS),
can be expressed.

Moreover, the proofs of the theory
{\mathcal{U}}
can be classified as proofs
in Minimal predicate logic, Constructive predicate logic, etc. just by
identifying the axioms they use, akin to proofs in geometry that can
be classified as proofs in Euclidean, hyperbolic, elliptic, neutral,
etc. geometries. More precisely, we identify sub-theories of the
theory
{\mathcal{U}}
that correspond to each of these theories, and we prove
that when a proof in
{\mathcal{U}}
uses only symbols of a sub-theory,
then it is a proof in that sub-theory.

In Section
2
, we recall the definition of
\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
and of a
theory. In Section
3

{\mathcal{U}}
step by
step without any (other than informal) reference to a known system. In
Section

\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
, and prove that every fragment of
{\mathcal{U}}
,
including
{\mathcal{U}}
itself, is indeed a theory, that is, it is defined by
a confluent and type-preserving rewriting system. Finally, in
Section
, we detail the sub-theories of
{\mathcal{U}}
that
correspond to the above mentioned systems.

This paper is an extended version of
[

]
. It provides more

3

The theory

3.1.
Object-terms

The notions of term, proposition, and proof are not primitive in

\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}

{\mathcal{U}}
introduce these
notions. We first define a notion analogous to the Predicate logic
notion of term, to express the objects the theory speaks about, such
as the natural numbers. As all expressions in
\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
are called
“terms”, we shall call these expressions “object-terms”, to
distinguish them from the other terms.

To build the notion of object-term in
\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
we
declare a constant
{\color{blue}I}
of type
TYPE

{\color{blue}I}:\mbox{\tt TYPE}
(
{\color{blue}I}
-decl)

and constants of type

for the function symbols, for instance a constant
0

of type
{\color{blue}I}
and a constant

of type
{\color{blue}I}\rightarrow{\color{blue}I}

The
object-terms, for instance
(\mbox{{succ}}~(\mbox{{succ}}~0))
and

(\mbox{{succ}}~x)

\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
terms of type
{\color{blue}I}

and, in an object-term, the variables are
\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
variables of
type
{\color{blue}I}
. If we wanted to have object-terms of several sorts, like in

of type

3.4.
Proofs

Predicate logic defines a language for terms and propositions,
but proofs have to be defined in a second step, for instance as
derivations in natural deduction, sequent calculus, etc. These
derivations, like object-terms and propositions, are trees. Therefore,
they can be represented as
\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
terms.

Using the Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov interpretation, a proof of the
proposition
A\mathbin{\color{blue}\Rightarrow}B
should be a
\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
term expressing a function
mapping proofs of
A
to proofs of
B
. Then, using the Curry-de
Bruijn-Howard correspondence, the type of this term should be the
proposition
A\mathbin{\color{blue}\Rightarrow}B
itself. But, this is not possible in the theory

{\mathcal{U}}
yet, as the proposition
A\mathbin{\color{blue}\Rightarrow}B
has the type
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prop}}
, and not
the type
TYPE
.

A strict view on the Curry-de Bruijn-Howard correspondence leads to
identify
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prop}}
and
TYPE
, yielding the conclusion that all types,
including
{\color{blue}I}
, and
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prop}}
itself, are propositions. A more moderate
view leads to the introduction of an embedding
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}
of propositions into
types, mapping each proposition
A
to the type
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}~A
of its
proofs. This view is moderate in two respects. First, the
propositions themselves are not the types of their proofs: if
t
is a
proof of
A
, then it does not have the type
A
, but the type

{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}~A
. Second, this embedding is not surjective. So not all types
are types of proofs, in particular
{\color{blue}I}
and
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prop}}
are not.

So, in the theory
{\mathcal{U}}
, we declare a constant
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}

{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}:{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prop}}\rightarrow\mbox{\tt TYPE}
(
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}
-decl)

When assigning the type
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prop}}\rightarrow\mbox{\tt TYPE}
to the constant
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}
, we
use the fact that
\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
supports dependent types, that is the
possibility to build a family of types
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}~x
parameterized with
a variable
x
of type
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prop}}
, where
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prop}}
is itself of type
TYPE
.

According to the Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov interpretation, a proof of

A\mathbin{\color{blue}\Rightarrow}A
is a
\lambda\Pi/\penalty{\equiv}
term expressing a function mapping proofs of

A
to proofs of
A
, so that it can be both built and used as a
function. In particular, the identity function

According to the Curry-de Bruijn-Howard correspondence, this term
should have the type
{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}~(A\mathbin{\color{blue}\Rightarrow}A)

{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}~A\rightarrow{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}~A

{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}~(A\mathbin{\color{blue}\Rightarrow}A)
and

allows the

{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}~A\rightarrow{\color{blue}\mbox{\it Prf}}~A

interpretation of proofs for implication: a proof of

function mapping proofs of

-red).
