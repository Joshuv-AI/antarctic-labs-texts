Signatures of Kondo-Majorana interplay in ac response
Krzysztof P. Wójcik, Tadeusz Domański, Ireneusz Weymann

Abstract
We analyze dynamical transport properties of a hybrid nanostructure, comprising a correlated quantum dot embedded between the source and drain electrodes, which are subject to an ac voltage, focusing on signatures imprinted on the charge transport by the side-attached Majorana zero-energy mode. The considerations are based on the Kubo formula, for which the relevant correlation functions are determined by using the numerical renormalization group approach, which allows us to consider the correlation effects due to the Coulomb repulsion and their interplay with the Majorana mode in a non-perturbative manner. We point out universal features of the dynamical conductance, showing up in the Kondo-Majorana regime, and differentiate them against the conventional Kondo and Majorana systems. In particular, we predict that the Majorana quasiparticles give rise to universal fractional values of the ac conductance in the well-defined frequency range below the peak at the Kondo scale. We also show this Kondo scale to actually increase with strengthening the coupling to the topological superconducting wire.

II
Theoretical description

The considered nanostructure (Fig.
1
) consists
of a correlated quantum dot placed between two external leads
and additionally coupled to a topological superconducting nanowire,
hosting the Majorana boundary modes described by the operators

\hat{\gamma}_{1}
and
\hat{\gamma}_{2}

External leads are subject to a time-dependent bias voltage,

In what follows, we briefly specify the low-energy microscopic

II.1
Hamiltonian

The system’s Hamiltonian consists of the following terms

\hat{H}=\sum_{j={\rm L,R}}\left(\hat{H}_{j}+\hat{H}_{j{\rm-QD}}\right)+\hat{H}_{\rm QDM}.

(1)

The external electrodes are assumed to be reservoirs of the itinerant electrons

\hat{H}_{j}=\sum_{\sigma}\int[\varepsilon-\mu_{j\sigma}(t)]\;\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j\varepsilon\sigma}\hat{c}_{j\varepsilon\sigma}d\varepsilon,

(2)

where

(

) denote the creation (annihilation)
operators of spin-
\sigma
electrons in
j
-th lead,
which satisfy anticommutation relation
{\{\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j\varepsilon\sigma},\hat{c}_{j^{\prime}\varepsilon^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\}=\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})}
,
and
\mu_{j\sigma}(t)
is the corresponding time-dependent chemical potential.
Electron tunneling between the
j
-th lead and the quantum dot is described by

\hat{H}_{j{\rm-QD}}=\sum_{\sigma}\int\sqrt{\rho_{j}(\varepsilon)}\left(v_{j}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\hat{c}_{j\varepsilon\sigma}+\mathrm{H.c.}\right)d\varepsilon,

(3)

where
v_{j}
is the hopping matrix element and
\rho_{j}(\varepsilon)

is the density of states of
j
-th lead.
Since our considerations refer to a narrow energy region
(of a width
\sim
meV) inside the topological gap of
superconducting nanowire, we assume the density of states to be flat,

\rho_{j}(\varepsilon)=\rho\equiv 1/2D
, where
D
is the band energy cut-off,
used as a convenient energy unit throughout,
D\equiv 1
.
The hybridization effects (
3
) can be taken into account
by introducing the couplings
\Gamma_{j}=\pi|v_{j}|^{2}\rho_{j}
.

The last term of the Hamiltonian (
1
) describes
the quantum dot combined with the Majorana modes of the
TS
Leijnse and Flensberg 2011
;
Liu and Baranger 2011

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\rm QDM}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{d}\sum_{\sigma}\hat{n}_{\sigma}+U\hat{n}_{\uparrow}\hat{n}_{\downarrow}

(4)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle V_{\rm M}(\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}-\hat{d}_{\downarrow})\hat{\gamma}_{1}+i\varepsilon_{\rm M}\hat{\gamma}_{1}\hat{\gamma}_{2},

where
\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}
(
\hat{d}_{\sigma}
) is the creation (annihilation)
operator for spin-
\sigma
electrons on the QD of energy
\varepsilon_{d}

and Coulomb correlations

,

\hat{n}_{\sigma}=\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\hat{d}_{\sigma}
is the corresponding electron number operator,
and
\hat{\gamma}_{1,2}
are the operators of the boundary zero-energy modes.
Hybridization of the quantum dot spin-
\downarrow
electrons
with the left hand-side Majorana quasiparticle is denoted by
V_{\mathrm{M}}
,
while
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{M}}
stands for an overlap between the Majorana modes.
It is useful to represent the Majorana operators
\hat{\gamma}_{i}
in
terms of the conventional fermion operators
\hat{f}_{\rm M}^{(\dagger)}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\hat{f}_{\rm M}+\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{\rm M})/\sqrt{2},

(5a)

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i(\hat{f}_{\rm M}-\hat{f}^{\dagger}_{\rm M})/\sqrt{2},

(5b)

which obey the standard anti-commutation relations.

II.2
AC conductance

The charge current flowing from the
j
-th lead
to the quantum dot in spin channel
\sigma
can be expressed by the operator

{\hat{I}_{j\sigma}(t)=e\,\partial_{t}\hat{N}_{j\sigma}(t)}
,
where
{\hat{N}_{j\sigma}=\int\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j\varepsilon\sigma}\hat{c}_{j\varepsilon\sigma}{\rm d}\varepsilon}

counts total number of spin-
\sigma
electrons in the aforementioned lead,
time argument indicates the Heisenberg picture,
and
e=|e|
stands for the elementary charge.
Assuming the topological superconductor to be grounded,
the applied time-dependent voltage affects the chemical potentials of external leads as

V_{j\sigma}(t)\equiv-\mu_{j\sigma}(t)/e
.
Within the linear-response Kubo formalism
Kubo 1966
,
it is useful to define the Fourier transform

{V_{j\sigma}(t)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int e^{-i\omega t}V_{j\sigma}(\omega){\rm d}\omega}
.
The expectation value of the charge current,
I_{j\sigma}(t)\equiv\langle{\hat{I}_{j\sigma}(t)}\rangle
,
can be then expressed in terms of the Fourier transforms by
Tóth et al. 2007
;
Moca et al. 2010

I_{j\sigma}(\omega)=\sum_{j^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\mathcal{G}_{jj^{\prime}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\omega)V_{j^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}(\omega),

(6)

with the system’s admittance

\mathcal{G}_{jj^{\prime}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\omega)=\frac{i}{\omega}\left(\langle\!\langle\hat{I}_{j\sigma}|\hat{I}_{j^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle^{\rm ret}_{\omega}-\langle\!\langle\hat{I}_{j\sigma}|\hat{I}_{j^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle^{\rm ret}_{\omega=0}\right),

(7)

where
\langle\!\langle\hat{I}_{j\sigma}|\hat{I}_{j^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle^{\rm ret}_{\omega}
is the Fourier-transform
of the retarded Green’s function of the current operator,

{\langle\!\langle\hat{I}_{j\sigma}|\hat{I}_{j^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle^{\rm ret}_{t}=-i\Theta(t)\langle{[\hat{I}_{j\sigma}(t),\hat{I}_{j^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}(0)]}\rangle}

(we set the time units such that
\hslash\equiv 1
throughout the paper).
In what follows, we shall focus on the properties of the frequency-dependent conductance of the system

G_{jj^{\prime}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\omega)=\,\mathrm{Re}\!\;\left\{\mathcal{G}_{jj^{\prime}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\omega)\right\}=-\frac{1}{\omega}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\;\left\{\langle\!\langle\hat{I}_{j\sigma}|\hat{I}_{j^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle^{\rm ret}_{\omega}\right\}.

(8)

The current operator for our setup is given by

\hat{I}_{j\sigma}=ie\left(v_{j}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}\hat{d}_{\sigma}-\mathrm{H.c.}\right)

(9)

with

\hat{\psi}_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}=\int\!\sqrt{\rho_{j}}\;\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{j\varepsilon\sigma}{\rm d}\varepsilon

(10)

being the corresponding field operator for the creation of a spin-
\sigma
electron in the
j
-th lead.
We introduce an effective tunnel-matrix element,

v=\sqrt{v_{L}^{2}+v_{R}^{2}}
, and perform a transformation from the
left-right
to the
even-odd
basis

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}^{\rm e}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v_{L}}{v}\hat{\psi}_{L\sigma}+\frac{v_{R}}{v}\hat{\psi}_{R\sigma},

(11)

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}^{\rm o}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{v_{R}}{v}\hat{\psi}_{L\sigma}+\frac{v_{L}}{v}\hat{\psi}_{R\sigma}.

(12)

Then, for the even (odd) current operator one finds

\hat{I}_{\sigma}^{\rm e(o)}=ive\;\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\sigma}^{\rm e(o)},

(13)

with

\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\sigma}^{\rm e(o)}=\hat{\psi}^{{\rm e(o)}\dagger}_{\sigma}\hat{d}_{\sigma}-\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\hat{\psi}^{{\rm e(o)}}_{\sigma},

(14)

such that
\hat{I}_{j\sigma}
can be expressed as

\hat{I}_{j\sigma}=ie\left[\frac{v_{j}^{2}}{v}\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm e}_{\sigma}+(-1)^{\delta_{j,L}}\frac{v_{L}v_{R}}{v}\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm o}_{\sigma}\right].

(15)

Thanks to
\langle\!\langle\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm e}_{\sigma}|\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm o}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm o}_{\sigma}|\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm o}_{-\sigma}\rangle\!\rangle=0
, one obtains

\displaystyle\frac{G_{jj^{\prime}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\omega)}{G_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\eta_{jj^{\prime}}\frac{2\Gamma_{L}\Gamma_{R}}{\Gamma}\frac{1}{\omega}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left[\langle\!\langle\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm o}_{\sigma}|\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm o\dagger}_{\sigma}\rangle\!\rangle^{\rm ret}_{\omega}\right]

(16)

\displaystyle-\frac{2\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{j^{\prime}}}{\Gamma}\frac{1}{\omega}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left[\langle\!\langle\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm e}_{\sigma}|\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\rm e\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle^{\rm ret}_{\omega}\right],\qquad

where
\Gamma=\Gamma_{L}+\Gamma_{R}
,
G_{0}=2e^{2}/h
,
and
\eta_{jj^{\prime}}=1
if
j=j^{\prime}
and
\eta_{jj^{\prime}}=-1
otherwise.

Note that
\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}^{\rm o}
is not present in the tunneling Hamiltonian,
Eq. (
3
),
i.e.
it is just a free fermionic field whose Green’s functions are known exactly.
Thus, one can relate the odd contribution to the quantum dot spectral function
through the Wick theorem without introducing any approximations.
On the other hand, the current correlation function associated with the even channel
needs to be determined explicitly. Then, the formula for the
frequency-dependent conductance can be
written in a more compact form as

Tóth et al. 2007
;
Moca et al. 2010
;
Płomińska et al. 2017

\frac{G_{jj^{\prime}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\omega)}{G_{0}}=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\eta_{jj^{\prime}}\frac{2\Gamma_{L}\Gamma_{R}}{\Gamma^{2}}g^{\rm o}_{\sigma}(\omega)+\frac{2\Gamma_{j}\Gamma_{j^{\prime}}}{\Gamma^{2}}g^{\rm e}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\omega)

(17)

with the functions

g^{\rm o}_{\sigma}(\omega)\!=\!\frac{\Gamma}{2\omega}\!\int\!\!\,\mathrm{Im}\!\;\langle\!\langle\hat{d}_{\sigma}|\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\rangle\!\rangle^{\rm ret}_{\omega^{\prime}}[f(\omega^{\prime}\!+\omega)\!-\!f(\omega^{\prime}\!-\omega)]{\rm d}\omega^{\prime},

(18)

and

g^{\rm e}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\omega)\!=\!-\frac{\Gamma}{\omega}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\;\langle\!\langle\mathcal{I}^{\rm e}_{\sigma}|\mathcal{I}^{{\rm e}}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle^{\rm ret}_{\omega},\quad\;

(19)

where
f(\omega)=\left[1+\exp{(\omega/T)}\right]^{-1}
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution function
(
k_{B}\equiv 1
).

II.3
Treatment of the correlations

To deal with the correlation effects in the low-temperature regime
where the Kondo effect emerges, we make use of the NRG approach

Wilson 1975
;
Legeza et al.
;
Tóth et al. 2008
in its full-density-matrix implementation
Anders and Schiller 2005
,
which allows for sum-rule conserving determination of the spectral functions

Weichselbaum and von
Delft 2007
relevant for our study.
This method reliably yields the set of low and high energy eigen-states,
and the Green’s functions can be calculated from
the Lehmann representation, imposing a broadening with the log-Gaussian kernel
Weichselbaum and von
Delft 2007
.
We also use the
z
-averaging trick
Oliveira and Oliveira 1994
with
8
different values of
z
,
which allows to avoid over-broadening of spectral functions. This is
particularly important for correct calculation of the even contribution, where

{\langle\!\langle\mathcal{I}^{\rm e}_{\sigma}|\mathcal{I}^{{\rm e}}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\!\rangle^{\rm ret}_{\omega}/\omega}

needs to be obtained at small
\omega
, cf. Eq. (
19
).
For specific computations we have chosen the following model parameters:
the discretization parameter
\Lambda=2
,
the number of states kept per iteration
N_{K}=2048
,
broadening width in the range
0.2<b<0.35
,

U=0.1D
, and
\Gamma=U/10
.
We focus on the zero-temperature case (
T=0
).

III.1
Odd response function

We start the discussion from the

dimensionless

It describes the contribution from the processes that change

These processes are governed mainly by the local spectral

Note that the spin symmetry is broken only by the coupling

Therefore,

V
Conclusions

We have investigated the dynamical charge transport
through the correlated quantum dot side-attached to
the topological superconducting nanowire, focusing
on the strong coupling regime.
We have identified the frequency range where the signatures
of Majorana modes occupying the ends of the wire can be observed.
Our analysis of all the contributions to conductance
between the normal leads and the quantum dot revealed
that in general total conductance is a combination
of two contributions: the odd and even in
exchanging the left and right leads, respectively.
We have proposed different biasing schemes allowing for
addressing each of these contributions
separately, and we also discussed the case of driving
only one of the normal leads when both contributions are relevant.

We have shown that at low frequencies only the even
contribution remains non-zero, leading to generalization
of the zero-bias anomaly known from
dc
studies to the
ac
case.
At higher frequencies clear signatures of the Coulomb
interactions and the Kondo effect are reported.
When properly tuned, the device can be used to generate
the spin current (with much smaller charge current still present)
and fully spin-polarized electric current.
The full frequency dependence uniquely characterizes
the relevant low-temperature Kondo-Majorana interplay,
which we hope will stimulate and foster further
endeavors to observe it experimentally.

Alternative experimental realization

As we have shown in the main text of the paper,
application of the symmetric or antisymmetric voltage bias between
the leads may be used to address the chosen
response function summed over source spins.
However, even if spin-sensitive measurements are
performed, the even contribution is always a sum
of two terms, cf. Eq. (
29
).
Separately addressing one of them would require
application of the bias to only one spin species
in the normal leads, which can pose a considerable experimental challenge.
However, an alternative system might be proposed,
where
g^{\rm e}_{\sigma\bar{\sigma}}(\omega)
would not require
such a sophisticated biasing, at the expense of
adding one more quantum dot to the system,
as depicted in Fig.
9
. The device presented
there has the same low energy structure, when one
assumes that quantum dots are very small
(thus the corresponding
U
is very large) and are placed in a strong magnetic field,
rendering one of the spins (say spin-
\uparrow
) irrelevant
for the low-energy processes.
Proximity of quantum dots enforces also inter-dot Coulomb repulsion
U^{\prime}
,
and we assume that the direct hopping between the dots is suppressed.
In such a scenario, the spin becomes irrelevant,
while the quantum dot index plays the role of an isospin.
Only the second dot is directly coupled to the topological
superconductor, and each quantum dot has its own bath of
free electrons in leads
1
and
2
, correspondingly.
Together they constitute a single effective electrode
possessing the isospin index, and
U^{\prime}
leads to Coulomb
blockade and the (isospin) Kondo effect when the two QDs
are (in total) singly occupied. Crucially, now the bias
applied to only one (iso)spin channel is easily realizable,
simply as a bias applied only to leads connected to the
relevant quantum dot, and the response can also be measured

Appendix B
The case of conventional superconductor

One of the most prominent results of our study is that
the even contribution to the frequency-dependent conductance maintains the
universal value of
G_{0}
over an extended range of frequencies.
Here we would like to stress, that while the existence of non-zero conductance at low
\omega
is
a result of superconductivity, its universal character
is a clear hallmark of a topological protection. Namely,
we have verified through direct calculations that when
the TS is replaced by a conventional superconductor,

G^{\rm e}(\omega\to 0)>0
, but is not universal. Its
magnitude becomes significant only for the quantum dot-superconductor
coupling strength
\Gamma_{\rm S}
exceeding
\sim U/4
.
Therefore, any effect stemming from direct coupling of
the dot to the superconducting shell, covering the nanowire
in typical realizations of TS, can be safely ignored.
