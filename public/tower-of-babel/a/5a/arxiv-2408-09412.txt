A new interpretation of the weighted pseudoinverse and its applications Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Haibo Li

Abstract
Consider the generalized linear least squares (GLS) problem min ⁡ ‖ L ​ x ‖ 2 ​ s . t . ‖ M ⁡ ( A ​ x − b ) ‖ 2 = min \min\|Lx\|_{2}\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \|M(Ax-b)\|_{2}=\min . The weighted pseudoinverse A M ​ L † A_{ML}^{{\dagger}} is the matrix that maps b b to the minimum 2-norm solution of this GLS problem. By introducing a linear operator induced by { A , M , L } \{A,M,L\} between two finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, we show that the minimum 2-norm solution of the GLS problem is equivalent to the minimum norm solution of a linear least squares problem involving this linear operator, and A M ​ L † A_{ML}^{{\dagger}} can be expressed as the composition of the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of this linear operator and an orthogonal projector. With this new interpretation, we establish the generalized Moore-Penrose equations that completely characterize the weighted pseudoinverse, give a closed-form expression of the weighted pseudoinverse using the generalized singular value decomposition (GSVD), and propose a generalized LSQR (gLSQR) algorithm for iteratively solving the GLS problem. We construct several numerical examples to test the proposed iterative algorithm for solving GLS problems. Our results highlight the close connections between GLS, weighted pseudoinverse, GSVD and gLSQR, providing new tools for both analysis and computations.

Introduction

Consider the generalized linear least squares (GLS) problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|Lx\|_{2}\ \ \ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \ \ \|M(Ax-b)\|_{2}=\min

(1.1)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
M\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m}
and
L\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
. In some literature it is also called the weighted linear least squares problem. The GLS problem generalizes the standard least squares (LS) problem
\min\|x\|_{2}\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \|Ax-b\|_{2}=\min
by incorporating weighting matrices
M
and
L
, which introduces additional constraints and objectives tailored to specific data characteristics. For instance,
M
might be used to increase
the relative importance of accurate measurements, while
L
could adjust the regularization or constraint structure to improve stability or enforce certain properties in the solution
[
11
, §6.1]
. Such problems arise in a variety of practical applications, including scatter data approximation
[
35
]
, functional data analysis
[
17
]
, ill-posed inverse problems
[
9
]
, surface fitting problems
[
34
]
and many others.

The GLS problem is relatively simple when
L
has full column rank, where it must have a unique solution. The case that both
L
and
M
have full column rank has been extensively studied in earlier literature; see e.g.
[
31
,
1
]
. For general rectangular matrices
M
and
L
, in
[
24
]
the authors proposed the concept of projection under seminorms to study the existence and structure of the solutions of
eq.

1.1
. A well-known result is that the uniqueness of the solution of
eq.

1.1
is equivalent to
\mathcal{N}(MA)\cap\mathcal{N}(L)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, where
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
is the null space of a matrix. In the case of nonuniqueness, there is a unique minimum 2-norm solution of
eq.

1.1
. The GLS problem was then intensively studied in
[
8
]
, where the author gave the expression of the general solutions. Specifically, the author demonstrated that the minimum 2-norm solution can be written as
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}b
, where
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is the so-called
M,L
weighted pseudoinverse of
A
that shares several properties analogous to the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.

The weighted pseudoinverse is a generalization of the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of a single matrix. Since the concept of the pseudoinverse was first introduced by Moore
[
25
,
26
]
and later by Penrose
[
29
,
30
]
, it has received considerable attention and many applications
[
10
,
22
,
21
,
3
]
, and there have been several various generalizations of the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse, such as the restricted pseudoinverse proposed for linear-constrained LS problems
[
23
,
16
,
2
]
, the product generalized inverse
[
6
]
, and another type of weighted pseudoinverse (different from that in this paper)
[
5
,
33
,
32
]
. Among these generalizations, the weighted pseudoinverse proposed in
[
8
]
has attracted significant attention. For example, in
[
13
]
, the authors proposed an algorithm for computing the GSVD of
\{A,L\}
, where at each iteration
L_{IA}^{{\dagger}}z
needs to be computed for some vector
z
; here
I
is the identity matrix. Moreover, using
L_{IA}^{{\dagger}}
, the general-form Tikhonov regularization problem
\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{\|Ax-b\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|Lx\|_{2}^{2}\}
can be transformed to a standard-form problem, which is much easier for analysis and computations; see e.g.
[
14
,
15
]
.

However, computing the weighted pseudoinverse and solving the GLS problem are both quite challenging. Existing methods primarily depend on matrix factorizations, which are effective only for small-scale problems. For the case that
\mathcal{N}(MA)\cap\mathcal{N}(L)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
with
M=I_{m}
, the identity matrix of order
m
, the author in
[
8
]
gave a closed-form expression of
A_{IL}^{{\dagger}}
using the generalized singular value decomposition (GSVD) of
\{A,L\}
, which can be used to compute the solution of
eq.

1.1
. Furthermore, he proposed a more efficient algorithm for computing
A_{IL}^{{\dagger}}
based on QR factorizations, which avoids the GSVD computation. For large-scale matrices, however, there is a lack of efficient methods for computing
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
or for iteratively computing
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}b
for a given
b
. This may partly be due to an insufficient understanding of the properties of the weighted pseudoinverse. In contrast, the properties of the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
A^{{\dagger}}
are well-established, and a variety of computational methods are available for it. For example, there is a closed-form expression of
A^{{\dagger}}
by using the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
A
, and
A^{{\dagger}}b
is the minimum 2-norm solution of
\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|Ax-b\|_{2}
, which can be computed efficiently by the iterative solver LSQR
[
28
]
. It would be beneficial to gain new insights into the weighted pseudoinverse and to establish deeper analogies with the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. This could enable the development of efficient iterative methods for computing
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
.

In this paper, we provide a new interpretation of the weighted pseudoinverse and use it to design an iterative algorithm for computing
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}b
. To achieve this, we first introduce a linear operator
\mathcal{A}
between two finite dimensional Hilbert spaces, with non-Euclidean inner products induced by the matrices
\{A,M,L\}
. Then we formulate an operator-type LS problem involving
\mathcal{A}
, showing that its minimum norm solution coincides with the minimum 2-norm solution of
eq.

1.1
. This result establishes a connection between
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
and
\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}
, the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\mathcal{A}
. Building on this connection, we derive a set of generalized Moore-Penrose equations that fully characterize the weighted pseudoinverse. Additionally, by using the generalized singular value decomposition (GSVD) of
\{A,L\}
, we give a closed-form expression for
A_{IL}^{{\dagger}}
, which is applicable regardless of whether
\mathcal{N}(A)\cap\mathcal{N}(L)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
or not.
To address the practical computational challenges involving
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
, we extend the classical Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization (GKB) method
[
10
]
and propose a novel iterative algorithm called the
generalized LSQR
(gLSQR). The design of this algorithm leverages the connection between
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
and
\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}
, which can efficiently compute
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}b
by iteratively refining the solutions to the GLS problem
eq.

1.1
without requiring any matrix factorizations. To demonstrate the effectiveness of gLSQR, we construct several numerical examples of GLS problems and show its ability to compute solutions with high accuracy across these diverse scenarios. The results in this paper highlight the close connections between GLS, weighted pseudoinverse, GSVD and gLSQR, providing new tools for both analysis and computations of related applications.

The paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
we review several basic properties of the LS problem and Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. In
Section

3
we analyze the GLS problem from the perspective of an equivalent operator-type LS problem. Building on this perspective, we offer a new interpretation of the weighted pseudoinverse and present several basic properties. In
Section

4
we generalize the GKB method and propose the gLSQR algorithm for iterative computing
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}b
. In
Section

5
we construct several nontrial numerical examples to test the gLSQR algorithm. Finally, we conclude the paper in
Section

6
.

Throughout the paper, we denote by
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
the null space and range space of a matrix or linear operator, respectively, denote by
\mathbf{0}
the zero matrix/vector with orders clear from the context, and denote by
\mathrm{span}\{\cdot\}
the subspace spanned by a group of vectors or columns of a matrix.

2
Linear least squares and pseudoinverse of linear operators

We review several basic properties of the LS problems and the pseudoinverse of linear operators in the context of Hilbert spaces; see e.g.
[
12
,
1
]
for more details. These properties will be used in the subsequent sections.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be two Hilbert spaces, and let
T:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
be a bounded linear operator where its adjoint is denoted by
T^{*}
. Consider the linear operator equation
Tx=y
, which has a solution if and only if
y\in\mathcal{R}(T)
. Otherwise, we consider the least squares solution. An element
x\in\mathcal{X}
is called a least squares solution of
Tx=y
if it satisfies

\|Tx-y\|_{\mathcal{Y}}=\inf\{\|Tz-y\|_{\mathcal{Y}}:z\in\mathcal{X}\}.

(2.1)

If the set of all least squares solutions has an element of minimum
\mathcal{X}
-norm, i.e.

\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}=\inf\{\|z\|_{\mathcal{X}}:z\ \mathrm{is\ a\ least\ squares\ solution\ of\ }Tx=y\},

(2.2)

then we call such an
x
a best-approximate solution of
Tx=y
. The following well-known result describes the existence and uniqueness of the least squares solution and best-approximate solution.

Theorem 1
.

For the linear operator equation
Tx=y
, the following properties holds:

(1)

it has a least squares solution if and only if
y\in\mathcal{R}(T)+\mathcal{R}(T)^{\perp}
;

(2)

if (1) is satisfied, then
x
is a least squares solution if and only if

T^{*}Tx=T^{*}y

(2.3)

holds, which is called the normal equation;

(3)

if (1) is satisfied, then
x
is the unique best-approximate solution if and only if
eq.

2.3
is satisfied and
x\in\mathcal{N}(T)^{\perp}
.

The best-approximate solution is closely related to the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
T
, which is the linear operator mapping
y
to the best-approximate solution of
Tx=y
. Based on
Theorem

1
, the standard definition of the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse is as follows.

Definition 2
.

For the bounded linear operator
T:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, define its restriction as
\widetilde{T}:=T|_{\mathcal{N}(T)^{\perp}}:\mathcal{N}(T)^{\perp}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(T)
. The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
T^{{\dagger}}
of
T
is defined as the unique linear extension of
\widetilde{T}^{-1}
to
\mathcal{D}(T^{{\dagger}}):=\mathcal{R}(T)+\mathcal{R}(T)^{\perp}
such that
\mathcal{N}(T^{{\dagger}})=\mathcal{R}(T)^{\perp}
.

It has been proved that the following four “Moore-Penrose” equations hold:

\begin{cases}TT^{{\dagger}}T=T,\\
T^{{\dagger}}TT^{{\dagger}}=T^{{\dagger}},\\
T^{{\dagger}}T=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(T)^{\perp}},\\
TT^{{\dagger}}=\mathcal{P}_{\overline{R(T)}}|_{\mathcal{D}(T^{{\dagger}})},\end{cases}

(2.4)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
is the orthogonal operator onto a subspace
\mathcal{S}
.
Moreover, the Moore-Penrose equations uniquely characterize
T^{{\dagger}}
, i.e. there exists a unique linear operator
T^{{\dagger}}
that satisfies equations
eq.

2.4
, and the last two conditions can even be relaxed as that
TT^{{\dagger}}
and
T^{{\dagger}}T
are two orthogonal projectors. The following limit property holds:

\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\left(T^{*}T+\delta I\right)^{-1}T^{*}=\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}T^{*}\left(TT^{*}+\delta I\right)^{-1}

(2.5)

where
I:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
is the identity operator. Using the pseudoinverse, the following well-known result describes the structure of the least squares solutions.

Theorem 3
.

Let
y\in\mathcal{D}(T^{{\dagger}})
. Then
x^{{\dagger}}:=T^{{\dagger}}y
is the unique best-approximate solution of
Tx=y
, and the set of all least squares solutions is
x^{{\dagger}}+\mathcal{N}(T)
.

Now we come back to the settings with matrices. By treating
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
as a linear operator between the Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, all the above results directly apply to
A
. Let the SVD of
A
be
U^{\top}AV=\Sigma
,
where
\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{r}&\\
&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
\Sigma_{r}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
,
\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{r}>0
, and
U=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
V=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are orthogonal matrices. The pseudoinverse of
A
has the expression
A^{{\dagger}}=V\Sigma^{{\dagger}}U^{\top}
with
\Sigma^{{\dagger}}=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{r}^{-1}&\\
&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, and the LS problem
\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|Ax-b\|_{2}
has a unique minimum 2-norm solution
x^{{\dagger}}=A^{{\dagger}}b=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{u_{i}^{\top}b}{\sigma_{i}}v_{i}
. We remark that for a compact linear operator
T
, there exists an analogous decomposition to SVD, called the singular value expansion (SVE). Using the SVE of
T
, we can also give a similar expression of
x^{{\dagger}}
for the linear operator equation; see e.g.
[
19
, §15.4]
.

For large-scale matrix
A
, the LSQR algorithm is an efficient iterative approach for the LS problems
\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|Ax-b\|_{2}
. This algorithm is based on the GKB process, where the main computations are matrix-vector products involving
A
and
A^{\top}
. At the
k
-th step, the GKB process of
A
and
b
generates two groups of 2-orthogonal vectors, which form orthonormal bases of the Krylov subspaces
\mathcal{K}_{k+1}(AA^{\top},b)
and
\mathcal{K}_{k}(A^{\top}A,A^{\top}b)
, respectively. Meanwhile, it reduces
A
to a
(k+1)\times k
lower bidiagonal matrix, which is then used in the LSQR algorithm to iterative compute an approximate solution. Besides, the GKB process is often used as a precursor for computing a partial SVD of a large-scale matrix.

To summarize this section, we remark that the LS problem, Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse, SVD, GKB process and LSQR algorithm are all closely related. Each of them plays an important role in matrix computation problems, from providing theoretical analysis tools to enhancing the efficiency of numerical computations. Clearer relationships among these concepts are illustrated in
Figure

4.1
at the end of
Section

4
.

3
Generalized linear least squares and weighted pseudoinverse

First, we present a result that characterizes the solutions of the GLS problem, which allows us to reformulate the GLS problem as an equivalent operator-type LS problem. We then provide a new interpretation of the weighted pseudoinverse and establish several of its basic properties.

3.1
Generalized linear least squares

In the following part, we use
\|x\|_{C}
to denote the seminorm
(x^{\top}Cx)^{1/2}
for a symmetric positive semidefinite
C
. It becomes a norm when
C
is strictly positive definite. The following result provides a criterion for determining a solution of the GLS problem.

Theorem 4
.

For the GLS problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|x\|_{Q}\ \ \ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \ \ \|Ax-b\|_{P}=\mathrm{min},

(3.1)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and
P\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are symmetric positive semidefinite matrices, let
G=A^{\top}PA+Q
. The following properties hold:

(1)

if
x
is a solution of
eq.

3.1
, then
\mathcal{P}_{\cal{R}(G)}x
is also a solution; conversally, if
x\in\mathcal{R}(G)
is a solution, then
x+z
is a solution for any
z\in\mathcal{N}(G)
;

(2)

the vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a solution of
eq.

3.1
if and only if

\begin{cases}A^{\top}P(Ax-b)=\mathbf{0},\\
x^{\top}Gz=0,\ \ \ \forall\ z\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA).\end{cases}

(3.2)

Proof.

First note that
G
is symmetric positive semidefinite. Thus, any vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has the decomposition
x=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}x+\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(G)}x=:x_{1}+x_{2}
and
x_{1}\perp x_{2}
, where
\perp
is the orthogonal relation in Euclidean spaces. Using the relation
\mathcal{N}(G)=\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)\cap\mathcal{N}(Q)
, we can verify that

\|A(x_{1}+x_{2})-b\|_{P}=\|Ax_{1}-b\|_{P},\ \ \ \|x_{1}+x_{2}\|_{Q}=\|x_{1}\|_{Q}.

The first property immediately follows.

To prove the second property, suppose
x
is a solution to
eq.

3.1
. Then it is a solution to the problem
\min_{x}\|Ax-b\|_{P}
. Taking the gradient of it leads to
A^{\top}P(Ax-b)=\mathbf{0}
. Now
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}x
is a solution to
eq.

3.1
. Note that

x^{\top}Gz=(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}x)^{\top}G(G^{{\dagger}}Gz)=(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}x)^{\top}G(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}z),

(3.3)

and
(\mathcal{R}(G),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G})
is a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle_{G}:=x^{\top}Gx^{\prime}
. We only need to prove
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}x\perp_{G}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))
, where
\perp_{G}
is the orthogonal relation in
(\mathcal{R}(G),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G})
. Since
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))
is a closed subspace of
(\mathcal{R}(G),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G})
, we have the decomposition
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}x=\bar{x}_{1}+\bar{x}_{2}
such that
\bar{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))
and
\bar{x}_{2}\perp_{G}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))
. We only need to prove
\bar{x}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
. First we prove
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))\subseteq\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
. To see it, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we use the decomposition
z=z_{1}+z_{2}
such that
z_{1}\in\mathcal{N}(G)
and
z_{2}\in\mathcal{N}(G)^{\perp}=\mathcal{R}(G)
, which indicates
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}z=z_{2}
. Thus, if
z\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
, then
A^{\top}PAz_{2}=A^{\top}PAz-A^{\top}PAz_{1}=A^{\top}PAz=0
since
z_{1}\in\mathcal{N}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
.

Notice that
\bar{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
, which leads to
\|A(\bar{x}_{1}+\bar{x}_{2})-b\|_{P}=\|A\bar{x}_{2}-b\|_{P}
. This indicates that
\bar{x}_{2}
is a solution to
\min_{x}\|Ax-b\|_{P}
. Since

\bar{x}_{2}^{\top}Q\bar{x}_{1}=\bar{x}_{2}^{\top}(A^{\top}PA+Q)\bar{x}_{1}=\bar{x}_{2}^{\top}G\bar{x}_{1}=0,

we have

\|\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}x\|_{Q}^{2}=\|\bar{x}_{1}\|_{Q}^{2}+\bar{x}_{2}^{\top}Q\bar{x}_{1}+\|\bar{x}_{2}\|_{Q}^{2}=\|\bar{x}_{1}\|_{Q}^{2}+\|\bar{x}_{2}\|_{Q}^{2}\geq\|\bar{x}_{2}\|_{Q}^{2}.

Specifically, if the second relation of
eq.

3.2
is not satisfied, which means
\bar{x}_{1}\neq\mathbf{0}
, it must hold
\|\bar{x}_{1}\|_{Q}>0
, which can be proved as follows. If
\|\bar{x}_{1}\|_{Q}=0
, then
\bar{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{N}(Q)
. Combining with
\bar{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))\subseteq\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
, it must hold
\bar{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{N}(Q)\cap\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)=\mathcal{N}(G)
. Using the relation
\bar{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{R}(G)
and
\mathcal{N}(G)\cap\mathcal{R}(G)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, we obtain
\bar{x}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
. Therefore, if
\bar{x}_{1}\neq\mathbf{0}
, then
\|\bar{x}_{2}\|_{Q}<\|\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}x\|_{Q}=\|x\|_{Q}
, contradicting with that
x
is a solution.

Now we prove that
eq.

3.2
is a sufficient condition. Since
eq.

3.1
has at least one solution in
\mathcal{R}(G)
, by the first property, we only need to prove that there is only one solution in
\mathcal{R}(G)
that satisfies
eq.

3.2
. The existence has already been proved. To see the uniqueness, suppose
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are such two solutions. Then is must hold that
x_{1}=x_{2}+z
with
z\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
. From
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{R}(G)
and
x_{1},x_{2}\perp_{G}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))
we obtain
z\in\mathcal{R}(G)
and
z\perp_{G}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(M)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))
. Combining with
z\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
leads to
z\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))
. Therefore, we have
z\perp_{G}z
, leading to
z=\mathbf{0}
. This proves the uniqueness of the solution in
\mathcal{R}(G)
that satisfies
eq.

3.2
.

From
Theorem

4
and its proof, we have the following result.

Corollary 5
.

There exists a unique solution of
eq.

3.1
in
\mathcal{R}(G)
, which is the minimum 2-norm solution of
eq.

3.1
. Denoting this solution by
x^{{\dagger}}
, then the set of all the solutions of
eq.

3.1
is
x^{{\dagger}}+\mathcal{N}(G)
.

Therefore, in order to solve
eq.

3.1
, a key step is to seek the solution
x^{{\dagger}}
in
\mathcal{R}(G)
. To investigate the property of
x^{{\dagger}}
, we introduce the following linear operator:

\mathcal{A}:(\mathcal{R}(G),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G})\rightarrow(\mathcal{R}(P),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{P}),\ \ \ v\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}Av,

(3.4)

where
v
and
Av
are column vectors under the canonical bases of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. It is obvious that
\mathcal{A}
is a bounded linear operator. Let

\mathcal{A}^{*}:(\mathcal{R}(P),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{P})\rightarrow(\mathcal{R}(G),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G}),\ \ \ u\mapsto\mathcal{A}^{*}u

be the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{A}
, which is defined by the relation
\langle\mathcal{A}v,u\rangle_{P}=\langle\mathcal{A}^{*}u,v\rangle_{G}
for any
v\in(\mathcal{R}(G),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G})
and
(\mathcal{R}(P),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{P})
. The following result describes the effect of
\mathcal{A}
on a vector under the canonical bases.

Lemma 6
.

Under the canonical bases of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for any
u\in(\mathcal{R}(P),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{P})
, it holds

\mathcal{A}^{*}u=G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pu.

(3.5)

Proof.

Under the canonical bases, it holds

\langle\mathcal{A}v,u\rangle_{P}=\langle\mathcal{A}^{*}u,v\rangle_{G}\ \Leftrightarrow\ (\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}Av)^{\top}Pu=v^{\top}G(\mathcal{A}^{*}u).

Since
v\in\mathcal{R}(G)
, we have
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v=v
and

(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}Av)^{\top}P=(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}A\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v)^{\top}P=v^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}A^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}P=v^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}A^{\top}P.

Thus, we have

v^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}A^{\top}Pu=v^{\top}G(\mathcal{A}^{*}u)\ \Leftrightarrow\ v^{\top}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}A^{\top}Pu-G(\mathcal{A}^{*}u))=0

for any
v\in\mathcal{R}(G)
. Noticing that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}A^{\top}Pu-G(\mathcal{A}^{*}u)\in\mathcal{R}(G)
, it follows that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}A^{\top}Pu=G(\mathcal{A}^{*}u)
. This equality can also be written as

GG^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pu=G(\mathcal{A}^{*}u)\ \Rightarrow\ G^{{\dagger}}GG^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pu=G^{{\dagger}}G(\mathcal{A}^{*}u)\ \Leftrightarrow\ G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pu=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{A}^{*}u).

Since
\mathcal{A}^{*}u\in\mathcal{R}(G)
, we immediately obtain
\mathcal{A}^{*}u=G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pu
.

Now we can reformulate the minimum 2-norm solution of
eq.

3.1
as the solution of the following equivalent operator-type LS problem.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{X}:=(\mathcal{R}(G),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G})
and
\mathcal{Y}:=(\mathcal{R}(P),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{P})
.
The minimum
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}}
-norm solution of the LS problem

\min_{v\in\mathcal{X}}\|\mathcal{A}v-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b\|_{\mathcal{Y}}

(3.6)

is the unique solution of
eq.

3.1
in
\mathcal{R}(G)

Proof.

First note that
eq.

3.6
has a unique
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}}
-norm solution. In fact,
v
is such a solution if and only if

\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}^{*}(\mathcal{A}v-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b)=\mathbf{0},\\
v\perp_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A}),\end{cases}

(3.7)

where
\perp_{\mathcal{X}}
is the orthogonal relation in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
. We only need to prove the equivalence between
eq.

3.2
and
eq.

3.7
for
x\in\mathcal{R}(G)
. For notational consistency, here we uniformly use
v
instead of
x
. The proof includes the following two steps.

Step 1: prove
A^{\top}P(Av-b)=\mathbf{0}\Leftrightarrow\mathcal{A}^{*}(\mathcal{A}v-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b)=\mathbf{0}
for any
v\in\mathcal{R}(G)
. By

6
, we have

\mathcal{A}^{*}(\mathcal{A}v-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b)=G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}Av-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b)=G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P(Av-b).

Thus, the “
\Rightarrow
” relation is obvious. To get the “
\Leftarrow
” relation, suppose
G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P(Av-b):=G^{{\dagger}}u=\mathbf{0}
. Let the square root decomposition of

be
P=L_{P}^{\top}L_{P}
. Then

u\in\mathcal{N}(G^{{\dagger}})=\mathcal{N}(G)=\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)\cap\mathcal{N}(Q)=\mathcal{N}(L_{P}A)\cap\mathcal{N}(Q)\subseteq\mathcal{N}(L_{P}A),

since

is symmetric and
\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)=\mathcal{N}(L_{P}A)
. On the other hand, we have
u=A^{\top}P(Av-b)\in\mathcal{R}((L_{P}A)^{\top})=\mathcal{N}(L_{P}A)^{\perp}
. Therefore,
u\in\mathcal{N}(L_{P}A)\cap\mathcal{N}(L_{P}A)^{\perp}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, leading to
u=\mathbf{0}
. This proves the “
\Leftarrow
” relation.

Step 2: prove
v\perp_{G}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))\Leftrightarrow v\perp_{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A})
for any
v\in\mathcal{R}(G)
. Since
v
is a vector under the canonical basis, we only need to prove
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A})
. Note that
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A})=\{v\in\mathcal{R}(G):\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}Av=\mathbf{0}\}
. In the proof of
Theorem

4
we have already shown that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))=\mathcal{R}(G)\cap\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
. Thus, for any
v\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))
, we have

A^{\top}PAv=\mathbf{0}\ \Rightarrow\ L_{P}Av=\mathbf{0}\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}Av=P^{{\dagger}}L_{P}^{\top}L_{P}Av=\mathbf{0}.

This implies that
v\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A})
, and then
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A})
. To prove the “
\supseteq
” relation, let
v\in\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A})
. Then
v\in\mathcal{R}(G)
and

. It follows that
A^{\top}PAv=\mathbf{0}
, leading to

. This proves
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))\supseteq\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{A})
. The whole proof is then completed.

Theorem

7
enables us to study the GLS problem by applying the extensive tools available for LS problems, thereby facilitating both analysis and computations. Building on this theorem, we give a new interpretation of the weighted pseudoinverse in the following part.

3.2
Weighted pseudoinverse and its properties

In this part, we come back to the GLS problem of the form

. By setting

and

Theorem 8
(
[
8
]
).

For any

,
M\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m}
and
L\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
, the problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|Lx\|_{2}\ \ \ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \ \ \|M(Ax-b)\|_{2}=\min

(3.8)

has the general solution

x=(I_{n}-(L\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(MA)})^{{\dagger}}L)(MA)^{{\dagger}}Mb+\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(MA)}(I_{n}-(L\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(MA)})^{{\dagger}}L\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(MA)})z,

It is shown that

is
2

\mathcal{N}(MA)\cap\mathcal{N}(L)
, thereby it is the minimum 2-norm solution of

3.8
. To describe the map that takes
b
to
x^{{\dagger}}
,
define the matrix

A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}:=(I_{n}-(L\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(MA)})^{{\dagger}}L)(MA)^{{\dagger}}M,

(3.10)

which is called the
ML
-weighted pseudoinverse of
A
. Note that
x^{{\dagger}}=A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}b
and note from

7
that
x^{{\dagger}}=\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b
for any
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We immediately have the following result.

Theorem 9
.

Following the notations in
Theorem

8
and
Theorem

7
, let
P=M^{\top}M
and
Q=L^{\top}L
. Then under the canonical bases of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, it holds

A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}=\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}.

(3.11)

Theorem

9
establishes a connection between the weighted pseudoinverse and the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. Specifically, if
M
has full column rank, then
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}=I_{m}
, and
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
is essentially the pseudoinverse
\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}
. Using this new interpretation, we derive the following “generalized” Moore-Penrose equations to characterize the weighted pseudoinverse.

Theorem 10
.

For the weighted pseudoinverse
X:=A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
, the following generalized Moore-Penrose equations hold:

\begin{cases}XAX=X,\\
MAXA=MA,\\
(M^{\top}MAX)^{\top}=M^{\top}MAX,\\
(GXAG^{{\dagger}})^{\top}=XA,\\
XM^{{\dagger}}M=X.\end{cases}

(3.12)

Moreover,
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
is the unique solution of the above matrix equations.

Proof.

Using
eq.

3.11
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(P)^{\perp}}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(M)^{\perp}}=M^{{\dagger}}M
, the fifth identity immediately follows. We use the identities
eq.

2.4
to prove the first four identities. Since
\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}=\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}
, for any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
under the canonical basis, it holds

A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}u=\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u=\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u=\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}AA_{ML}^{{\dagger}}u=A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}AA_{ML}^{{\dagger}}u,

which implies the first identity. Notice that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}A\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(G)}v=P^{{\dagger}}PA\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(G)}v=\mathbf{0}
due to
\mathcal{N}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
, implying that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}Av=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}A\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v
for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}Av

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}A\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}A\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}AA_{ML}^{{\dagger}}Av.

This implies
M^{{\dagger}}MA=M^{{\dagger}}MAA_{ML}^{{\dagger}}A
, leading to the second identity.
For the third identity, use the relation

\langle\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u,\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u^{\prime}\rangle_{P}=\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u,(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}})^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u^{\prime}\rangle_{P}

for any
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ (\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}AA_{ML}^{{\dagger}}u)^{\top}P\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u^{\prime}=(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u)^{\top}P(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}})^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\ u^{\top}(AA_{ML}^{{\dagger}})^{\top}Pu^{\prime}=u^{\top}P(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}})^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u^{\prime}.

It follows that
P(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}})^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u^{\prime}=(AA_{ML}^{{\dagger}})^{\top}Pu^{\prime}
for any
u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Using the fourth identity of
eq.

2.4
, which implies
(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}})^{*}=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}
, we have

PAA_{ML}^{{\dagger}}u^{\prime}=P\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}u^{\prime}=(AA_{ML}^{{\dagger}})^{\top}Pu^{\prime},

leading to
(AA_{ML}^{{\dagger}})^{\top}M^{\top}M=M^{\top}MAA_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
, which is just the third identity. For the fourth identity, use the relation

\langle\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v,\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v^{\prime}\rangle_{G}=\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v,(\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A})^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v^{\prime}\rangle_{G}

for any
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ (A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}A\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v)^{\top}G\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v^{\prime}=(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v)^{\top}G(\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A})^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v^{\prime}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\ v^{\top}(A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}A)^{\top}Gv^{\prime}=v^{\top}G(\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A})^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v^{\prime},

where we have used
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}A\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}Av
. It follows that
G(\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A})^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v^{\prime}=(A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}A)^{\top}Gv^{\prime}
for any
v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Using the third identity of
eq.

2.4
, which implies
(\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A})^{*}=\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A}
, we have

GA_{ML}^{{\dagger}}Av^{\prime}=G\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}v^{\prime}=(A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}A)^{\top}Gv^{\prime},

leading to
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}Av^{\prime}=G^{{\dagger}}GA_{ML}^{{\dagger}}Av^{\prime}=G^{{\dagger}}(A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}A)^{\top}Gv^{\prime}
. Thus, it holds
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}A=G^{{\dagger}}(A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}A)^{\top}G
, which is the fourth identity.

To show that
X
is unique, first we prove that if
X
satisfies the first four identities then
X
must satisfy

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}X=X.

(3.13)

Using the first and fourth identities, we get

\displaystyle G^{{\dagger}}GX=G^{{\dagger}}GXAX=G^{{\dagger}}G(G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}X^{\top}G)X=G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}X^{\top}GX=XAX=X.

Suppose
Y
is another matrix satisfying equations
eq.

3.12

Y
also satisfies
eq.

3.13
.
Note that the fifth identity implies
X=X\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}=XP^{{\dagger}}P
.
It follows that

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=(XA)X=(GX\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}AG^{{\dagger}})^{\top}X=(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}AG^{{\dagger}})^{\top}X^{\top}GX

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{=}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}A\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}YAG^{{\dagger}})^{\top}X^{\top}GX=(G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Y^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)})A^{\top}(X\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)})^{\top}GX

\displaystyle\overset{(2)}{=}YAG^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}X^{\top}GX=YA(XA)X=YAX=YAYAX=(YP^{{\dagger}}P)AYAX

\displaystyle=YP^{{\dagger}}(PAY)^{\top}AX=YP^{{\dagger}}Y^{\top}A^{\top}PAX=Y(X^{\top}A^{\top}PAYP^{{\dagger}})^{\top}

\displaystyle=Y(PAXAYP^{{\dagger}})^{\top}=Y(PAYP^{{\dagger}})^{\top}\overset{(3)}{=}Y\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}AY=YAY=Y,

where for “
\overset{(1)}{=}
” we use

3.13

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}A=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}AYA
due to the second identity, for “
\overset{(2)}{=}

due to the fourth identity, and for “
\overset{(3)}{=}
” we use
(PAYP^{{\dagger}})^{\top}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}AY
due to the third identity. This proves the uniqueness of

.

, the author shows that if
\mathcal{N}(A)\cap\mathcal{N}(L)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, then
A_{IL}^{{\dagger}}=X\Sigma_{A}^{{\dagger}}U_{A}^{\top}
. In the following result, we give a similar expression for
A_{IL}^{{\dagger}}
, which is applicable regardless of whether
\mathcal{N}(A)\cap\mathcal{N}(L)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
or not.

Theorem 13
.

For any two matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
L\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
with the GSVD as described in
Theorem

, let
G=A^{\top}A+L^{\top}L
. Then

A_{IL}^{{\dagger}}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}X\Sigma_{A}^{{\dagger}}U_{A}^{\top}.

(3.18)

Proof.

First we prove that

x:=X\Sigma_{A}^{{\dagger}}U_{A}^{\top}b
is a solution of
eq.

3.8
. We only need to verify that the two conditions in
eq.

3.2
are satisfied. Since
AX=U_{A}\Sigma_{A}
, we have

\displaystyle A^{\top}(Ax-b)=X^{-\top}\Sigma_{A}^{\top}U_{A}^{\top}(U_{A}\Sigma_{A}\Sigma_{A}^{{\dagger}}U_{A}^{\top}b-b)=X^{-\top}(\Sigma_{A}^{\top}\Sigma_{A}\Sigma_{A}^{{\dagger}}-\Sigma_{A}^{\top})U_{A}^{\top}b=\mathbf{0},

since we can easily verify that
\Sigma_{A}^{\top}\Sigma_{A}\Sigma_{A}^{{\dagger}}=\Sigma_{A}^{\top}
.
For the second condition in
eq.

3.2
, if we partition
X
as

X=\vtop{\hbox{$\left(\vbox{\vbox{\halign{ \hfil$#$\hfil\kern 2\p@\kern\@leftbwd& \thinspace\hfil$#$\hfil&& \quad\hfil$#$\hfil\cr X_{1}$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&X_{2}&X_{3}&X_{4}&n\crcr\kern 2.0pt\cr q_{1}$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&q_{2}&q_{3}&n-r\cr}}}\kern 0.0pt\right)\kern-6.66669pt\kern 0.0pt$}\kern 32.83705pt}\ ,

(3.19)

we can verify that
\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}A)=\mathcal{N}(A)=\mathcal{R}((X_{3}\ X_{4}))
. On the other hand, it holds that
x\in\mathcal{R}(X\Sigma_{A}^{{\dagger}})
and

X\Sigma_{A}^{{\dagger}}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&X_{2}&X_{3}\end{pmatrix}C_{A}^{{\dagger}}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&X_{2}C_{q_{2}}^{-1}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},

(3.20)

which means that
x\in\mathcal{R}((X_{1}\ X_{2}))
. Notice that

\displaystyle G=X^{-\top}\left(\begin{pmatrix}C_{A}^{\top}C_{A}&\\
&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}S_{L}^{\top}S_{L}&\\
&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}\right)X^{-1}=X^{-\top}\begin{pmatrix}I_{r}&\\
&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}X^{-1},

leading to
X^{\top}GX=\begin{pmatrix}I_{r}&\\
&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}
. Thus,
\mathcal{R}(X_{4})=\mathcal{N}(G)
and the columns of
(X_{1}\ X_{2}\ X_{3})
are mutually
G
-orthonormal. It follows that
x^{\top}Gz=0
for any
z\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}A)=\mathcal{R}((X_{3}\ X_{4}))
.

Note that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}x=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}X\Sigma_{A}^{{\dagger}}U_{A}^{\top}b\in\mathcal{R}(G)
, which is the minimum 2-norm solution of
eq.

3.8
for an arbitrary

. We immediately obtain

3.18
.

This theorem provides a direct computational approach for
A_{IL}^{{\dagger}}
using the GSVD. However, if the matrices are very large, computing the GSVD is extremely expensive. In this case, we need an iterative approach to approximate
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}b
for any given
b
.

4
Iterative method for computing weighted pseudoinverse

By
Theorem

8
, computing
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}b
is equivalent to computing the minimum 2-norm solution of the GLS problem
eq.

3.1
. We aim to approximate the solution of the GLS problem through an iterative process. The starting point comes from
Theorem

7
. To solve the LS problem
eq.

3.6
, we apply the GKB process to the operator
\mathcal{A}
between the two Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
; see
[
4
]
for the GKB for LS problems in Hilbert spaces. Starting from the initial vector
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b
, the recursive relations of GKB can be expressed as follows:

\begin{cases}\beta_{1}u_{1}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b,\\
\alpha_{i}v_{i}=\mathcal{A}^{*}u_{i}-\beta_{i}v_{i-1},\\
\beta_{i+1}u_{i+1}=\mathcal{A}v_{i}-\alpha_{i}u_{i},\end{cases}

(4.1)

where
u_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
v_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
, and
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
are positive scalars such that
\|v_{i}\|_{\mathcal{X}}=\|u_{i}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}=1
. Note that
v_{0}:=\mathbf{0}
for the initial step. We remark that it is assumed that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b\neq\mathbf{0}
; otherwise, the case is trivial because
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}b=\mathbf{0}
. Using
Lemma

6
, we present the matrix form of the above recursive relations:

\begin{cases}\beta_{1}u_{1}=PP^{{\dagger}}b,\\
\alpha_{i}v_{i}=G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pu_{i}-\beta_{i}v_{i-1},\\
\beta_{i+1}u_{i+1}=PP^{{\dagger}}Av_{i}-\alpha_{i}u_{i}.\end{cases}

(4.2)

Note that if
G=I_{n}
and
P=I_{m}
, then the above recursive relations correspond to the standard GKB process of the matrix
A
. We name the iterative process corresponding to
eq.

4.2
the
generalized Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization
(
gGKB
). Before giving the practical computation procedure, let us explore how to further reduce the computational cost. In fact, computations involving
P^{{\dagger}}
can be avoided, as demonstrated by the following result.

Lemma 14
.

For the recursive relations

\begin{cases}\beta_{1}\tilde{u}_{1}=b,\\
\alpha_{i}\tilde{v}_{i}=G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P\tilde{u}_{i}-\beta_{i}\tilde{v}_{i-1},\\
\beta_{i+1}\tilde{u}_{i+1}=A\tilde{v}_{i}-\alpha_{i}\tilde{u}_{i},\end{cases}

(4.3)

where
\tilde{v}_{0}:=\mathbf{0}
, if
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
are the same as those in
eq.

4.2
, then
v_{i}=\tilde{v}_{i}
and
u_{i}=PP^{{\dagger}}\tilde{u}_{i}
.

Proof.

We prove it by mathematical induction. For
i=1
, we have
\beta_{1}PP^{{\dagger}}\tilde{u}_{1}=PP^{{\dagger}}b=\beta_{1}u_{1}
, implying
u_{1}=PP^{{\dagger}}\tilde{u}_{1}
. Note that
\tilde{v}_{0}=v_{0}
, and
Pu_{1}=PPP^{{\dagger}}\tilde{u}_{1}=P\tilde{u}_{1}
. It follows that
\alpha_{1}v_{1}=\alpha_{1}\tilde{v}_{1}
, meaning
v_{1}=\tilde{v}_{1}
. Now assume
v_{i}=\tilde{v}_{i}
and
u_{i}=PP^{{\dagger}}\tilde{u}_{i}
for
i\geq 1
. Then

PP^{{\dagger}}Av_{i}-\alpha_{i}u_{i}=PP^{{\dagger}}A\tilde{v}_{i}-\alpha_{i}PP^{{\dagger}}\tilde{u}_{i}=PP^{{\dagger}}(A\tilde{v}_{i}-\alpha_{i}\tilde{u}_{i})=\beta_{i+1}PP^{{\dagger}}\tilde{u}_{i+1}.

Thus, we have
\beta_{i+1}u_{i+1}=\beta_{i+1}PP^{{\dagger}}\tilde{u}_{i+1}
, meaning
u_{i+1}=PP^{{\dagger}}\tilde{u}_{i+1}
. Similar to the proof for
i=1
, using
Pu_{i+1}=PPP^{{\dagger}}\tilde{u}_{i+1}=P\tilde{u}_{i+1}
, we can also prove
v_{i+1}=\tilde{v}_{i+1}
.

Using the above result, we can simplify the computation if we only need to generate
v_{i}
but not
u_{i}
. At the initial step, we have

\beta_{1}=\|PP^{{\dagger}}b\|_{P}=[(PP^{{\dagger}}b)^{\top}PPP^{{\dagger}}b]^{1/2}=(b^{\top}Pb)^{1/2}.

At the
i
-th step, to compute
\beta_{i+1}
, let
r_{i}=Av_{i}-\alpha_{i}\tilde{u}_{i}
. Then we have
\beta_{i+1}u_{i+1}=PP^{{\dagger}}r_{i}
. Thus, it follows that

\beta_{i+1}=\|PP^{{\dagger}}r_{i}\|_{P}=[(PP^{{\dagger}}r_{i})^{\top}PPP^{{\dagger}}r_{i}]^{1/2}=(r_{i}^{\top}Pr_{i})^{1/2}.

Now we can give the whole iterative procedure of the
gGKB
process, as shown in
Algorithm

1
.

Note that at each iteration of
gGKB
we need to compute
G^{{\dagger}}\bar{s}
. For large-scale matrices, it is generally impractical to obtain
G^{{\dagger}}
directly. If
G
is sparse and positive definite, we can first apply the sparse Cholesky factorization to
G
and then compute
G^{{\dagger}}
. Otherwise, using the fact that
G^{{\dagger}}
is the minimum 2-norm solution of
\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|Gs-\bar{s}\|_{2}
, we can apply the iterative solver LSQR to
\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|Gs-\bar{s}\|_{2}
to approximate
G^{{\dagger}}\bar{s}

[
28
]
.

We remark that in

the author generalized the GKB process for computing nontrivial GSVD components of

The following result describes the subspaces generated by
gGKB
.

Proposition 15
.

The
gGKB
process generates vectors
v_{i}\in\mathcal{R}(G)
and
u_{i}\in\mathcal{R}(P)
, and
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a
G
-orthonormal basis of the Krylov subspace

\mathcal{K}_{k}(G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}PA,G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pb)=\mathrm{span}\{(G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}PA)^{i}G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pb\}_{i=0}^{k-1},

(4.4)

and
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is a
P
-orthonormal basis of the Krylov subspace

\mathcal{K}_{k}(PP^{{\dagger}}AG^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P,PP^{{\dagger}}b)=PP^{{\dagger}}\mathrm{span}\{(AG^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P)^{i}b\}_{i=0}^{k-1}.

(4.5)

Proof.

The proof is based on the property of GKB for linear compact operators. As demonstrated above, the
gGKB
of
A
is essentially the GKB of
\mathcal{A}
between the two Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
. Therefore, the generated vectors satisfy
v_{i}\in\mathcal{R}(G)
and
u_{i}\in\mathcal{R}(P)
, and
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
and
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
are
G
- and
P
-orthonormal bases of the Krylov subspaces
\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b)
and
\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{*},\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b)
, respectively. By
Lemma

6
, we have

(\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A})^{i}\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b=(G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}PPP^{{\dagger}}A)^{i}G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}PPP^{{\dagger}}b=(G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}PA)^{i}G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pb,

and

(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{*})^{i}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b=(PP^{{\dagger}}AG^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P)^{i}PP^{{\dagger}}b=PP^{{\dagger}}(AG^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P)^{i}b.

The desired result immediately follows.

Using
eq.

4.3
, one can also verify that
\mathrm{span}\{\tilde{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}=\mathcal{K}_{k}(AG^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P,b)
. Since the dimensions of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are
r_{G}=\mathrm{rank}(G)
and
r_{P}=\mathrm{rank}(P)
, respectively, by
Proposition

15
the
gGKB
process will eventually terminate in at most
\min\{r_{G},r_{P}\}
steps. Here “terminate” means that
\alpha_{i}
or
\beta_{i}
equals zero at the current step, thereby the Krylov subspaces can not expand any longer. The “terminate step” can be defined as

k_{t}=\min\{k:\alpha_{k+1}\beta_{k+1}=0\}.

(4.6)

Suppose
gGKB
does not terminate before the
k
-th iteration, i.e.
\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\neq 0
for
1\leq i\leq k
. Then the
k
-step
gGKB
process generates a
G
-orthonormal matrix
V_{k}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and a
P
-orthonormal matrix
U_{k}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
, which satisfy the relations

{}\begin{cases}\beta_{1}U_{k+1}e_{1}=PP^{{\dagger}}b,\\
PP^{{\dagger}}AV_{k}=U_{k+1}B_{k},\\
G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}PU_{k+1}=V_{k}B_{k}^{T}+\alpha_{k+1}v_{k+1}e_{k+1}^{\top},\end{cases}

(4.7)

where
e_{1}
and
e_{k+1}
are the first and
(k+1)
-th columns of
I_{k+1}
, and

B_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&&&\\
\beta_{2}&\alpha_{2}&&\\
&\beta_{3}&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\alpha_{k}\\
&&&\beta_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times k}

(4.8)

has full column rank. Note that it may happens that
\beta_{k+1}=0
, which means that
gGKB
terminates just at the
k
-th step and
u_{k+1}=\mathbf{0}
.

Based on
gGKB
, we can design an iterative approach for solving
eq.

3.6
, which will also solve
eq.

3.1
. Note that under the canonical bases, we can rewrite
eq.

3.6
as

\min_{x\in\mathcal{R}(G)}\|PP^{{\dagger}}A-PP^{{\dagger}}b\|_{P}

(4.9)

From
k=1
onwards, we seek an approximate solution to
eq.

4.9
in the subspace
\mathrm{span}\{V_{k}\}
. By letting
x=V_{k}y
with
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we obtain from
eq.

4.7
that

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathrm{span}\{V_{k}\}}\|PP^{{\dagger}}Ax-PP^{{\dagger}}b\|_{P}=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|PP^{{\dagger}}AV_{k}y-\beta_{1}U_{k+1}e_{1}\|_{P}

\displaystyle=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|U_{k+1}(B_{k}y-\beta_{1}e_{1})\|_{P}=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|B_{k}y-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2},

where we have used that
\{u_{i}\}
are
P
-orthonormal. Note that

\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|B_{k}y-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}=B_{k}^{{\dagger}}\beta_{1}e_{1}=:y_{k}

since
B_{k}
has full column rank.
Therefore, at the
k
-th iteration, the iterative approximation to
eq.

4.9
is given by

x_{k}=V_{k}y_{k}=V_{k}B_{k}^{{\dagger}}\beta_{1}e_{1}.

(4.10)

The above approach is very similar to the LSQR algorithm for the standard LS problem
[
28
]
. Moreover, the bidiagonal structure of
B_{k}
enables the design of a recursive procedure to update
x_{k}
step by step, without explicitly computing
B_{k}^{{\dagger}}\beta_{1}e_{1}
at each iteration. This procedure is based on the Givens QR factorization of
B_{k}
; see
[
28
, Section 4.1]
for details. Note that
u_{i}
is not required for computing
x_{k}
, so there is no need to compute
PP^{{\dagger}}
in
gGKB
. We summarize the iterative algorithm for solving
eq.

in

, which is named the

(

As the iteration proceeds, the
k
-th solution
x_{k}
gradually approximates the true solution of
eq.

4.9
, and consequently of
eq.

3.1
. We now state this property precisely.

Theorem 16
.

Suppose the
gGKB
process terminates at step
k_{t}
. Then
x_{k_{t}}
obtained by
gLSQR
is the exact minimum
2
-norm solution of
eq.

3.1
.

Proof.

Since
x_{k_{t}}\in\mathrm{span}\{V_{k}\}\subseteq\mathcal{R}(G)
, by
Theorem

4
, we only need to verify that
x_{k_{t}}
satisfies the two conditions in
eq.

3.2
.

Step 1: prove
A^{\top}P(Ax_{k_{t}}-b)=\mathbf{0}
. By writing
x_{k_{t}}
as
x_{k_{t}}=V_{k_{t}}y_{k_{t}}
, we obtain from
eq.

4.7
that

P(Ax_{k_{t}}-b)=P\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}(Ax_{k_{t}}-b)=PU_{k_{t}+1}(B_{k_{t}}y_{t}-\beta_{1}e_{1}).

Using
eq.

4.7
again, we get

\displaystyle G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P(Ax_{k_{t}}-b)

\displaystyle=G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}PU_{k_{t}+1}(B_{k_{t}}y_{k_{t}}-\beta_{1}e_{1})

\displaystyle=(V_{k_{t}}B_{k_{t}}^{\top}+\alpha_{k_{t}+1}v_{k_{t}+1}e_{k+1}^{\top})(B_{k_{t}}y_{k_{t}}-\beta_{1}e_{1})

\displaystyle=V_{k_{t}}(B_{k_{t}}^{\top}B_{k_{t}}y_{k_{t}}-B_{k_{t}}^{\top}\beta_{1}e_{1})+\alpha_{k_{t}+1}\beta_{k_{t}+1}v_{k_{t}+1}e_{k_{t}}^{\top}y_{k_{t}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k_{t}+1}\beta_{k_{t}+1}v_{k_{t}+1}e_{k_{t}}^{\top}y_{k_{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

since
\alpha_{k_{t}+1}\beta_{k_{t}+1}=0
and
B_{k_{t}}^{\top}B_{k_{t}}y_{k_{t}}=B_{k_{t}}^{\top}\beta_{1}e_{1}
due to
y_{k_{t}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{y}\|B_{k_{t}}y-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}
. Note that
x_{k_{t}}\in\mathcal{R}(G)
. Using the same approach as in the proof (Step 1) of
Theorem

7
, we obtain
A^{\top}P(Ax_{k_{t}}-b)=\mathbf{0}
.

Step 2: prove
x_{k_{t}}^{\top}Gz=0
for any
z\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
. By
Proposition

15
, we have
x_{k_{t}}\in\mathcal{R}(V_{k_{t}})\subseteq\mathcal{R}(G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P)
. Let
x_{k_{t}}=G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pw
. Then for any
z\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)
we have

\displaystyle x_{k_{t}}^{\top}Gz=(G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}Pw)^{\top}Gz=w^{\top}PAG^{{\dagger}}Gz.

Recall that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}(\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA))\subseteq\mathcal{N}(A^{\top}PA)=\mathcal{N}(L_{P}A)
, which has been proved in the proof of
Theorem

. Thus,
PAG^{{\dagger}}Gz=L_{p}^{\top}L_{P}A\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}z=\mathbf{0}
, leading to
x_{k_{t}}^{\top}Gz=\mathbf{0}
.

An iterative algorithm should include a stopping criterion to decide whether the current iteration can be stopped to obtain a solution with acceptable approximation accuracy. Notice from
Theorem

7
that
\mathcal{A}^{*}(\mathcal{A}x_{k}-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b)=:\mathcal{A}^{*}r_{k}
would be zero at the iteration where the accurate solution is computed. The scaling invariant quantity
\|\mathcal{A}^{*}r_{k}\|_{G}/(\|\mathcal{A}\|\|\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b\|_{P})
can be used to measure the accuracy of the iterative solution, where
\|\mathcal{A}\|
is the operator norm defined as
\|\mathcal{A}\|:=\max_{v\in\mathcal{R}(G)\atop v\neq\mathbf{0}}\frac{\|\mathcal{A}v\|_{P}}{\|v\|_{G}}
. Thus, we can use

\frac{\|\mathcal{A}^{*}r_{k}\|_{G}}{\|\mathcal{A}\|\|\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b\|_{P}}\leq\mathtt{tol}

(4.11)

as a stopping criterion for
gLSQR
. To ensure computational practicality, we discuss how to compute the quantities in
eq.

4.11
. It is obviously that
\|\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b\|_{P}=(b^{\top}Pb)^{1/2}
. Using
lemma

6
and the procedure in the proof of
Theorem

16
, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{*}r_{k}\|_{G}=\|G^{{\dagger}}A^{\top}P\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}(Ax_{k}-b)\|_{G}=\|\alpha_{k+1}\beta_{k+1}v_{k+1}e_{k}^{\top}y_{k}\|_{G}=\alpha_{k+1}\beta_{k+1}|e_{k}^{\top}y_{k}|,

where we have used
\|v_{i}\|_{G}=1
. Therefore,
\|\mathcal{A}^{*}r_{k}\|_{G}
can be computed quickly with very little additional cost.

To obtain an accurate estimate of
\|\mathcal{A}\|
, here we consider the GLS problem
eq.

3.8
with
M=I_{m}
for simplicity. Note that any GLS problem can be reduced to this form by substituting
A\leftarrow MA
and
b\leftarrow Mb
. We give a matrix expression for
\|\mathcal{A}\|
using the GSVD of
\{A,L\}
.

Proposition 17
.

Suppose the GSVD of
\{A,L\}
has the form
eq.

3.17
. Then we have

\|\mathcal{A}\|=\sigma_{\max}(C_{A}),

(4.12)

which is the largest singular value of
C_{A}
.

Proof.

Using the expression of
\mathcal{A}
under the canonical bases, we have

\|\mathcal{A}\|=\max_{v\in\mathcal{R}(G)\atop v\neq\mathbf{0}}\frac{\|\mathcal{A}v\|_{2}}{\|v\|_{G}}=\max_{v\in\mathcal{R}(G)\atop v\neq\mathbf{0}}\frac{v^{\top}A^{\top}Av}{v^{\top}Gv}.

In the GSVD of
\{A,L\}
, we use the partition of
X
as described in
eq.

3.19
and denote
\widetilde{X}_{1}=(X_{1}\ X_{2}\ X_{3})
. In the proof of
Theorem

13
we have shown that
\mathcal{N}(G)=\mathcal{R}(X_{4})
. Now we show
\mathcal{R}(G)=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{X}_{1})
. We only need to show
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{X}_{1}))=r=\mathrm{rank}(G)
, which is equivalent to that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}\widetilde{X}_{1}
has full column rank. Suppose
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}\widetilde{X}_{1}w=\mathbf{0}
for a
w\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Then
z:=\widetilde{X}_{1}w\in\mathcal{N}(G)
. Thus, we have
0=z^{\top}Gz=w^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{1}^{\top}G\widetilde{X}_{1}z=z^{\top}z
, leading to
z=0
. This proves that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}\widetilde{X}_{1}
has full column rank.

For any
v\in\mathcal{R}(G)
, write
v=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}\widetilde{X}_{1}y
with
y\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Then we have

v^{\top}Gv=y^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{1}^{\top}GG^{{\dagger}}GGG^{{\dagger}}\widetilde{X}_{1}y=y^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{1}^{\top}G\widetilde{X}_{1}y=\|y\|_{2},

and

Av=A\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(G)}\widetilde{X}_{1}y=A\widetilde{X}_{1}y-A\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}(G)}\widetilde{X}_{1}y=A\widetilde{X}_{1}y=U_{A}C_{A}y,

where we have used
\mathcal{N}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{N}(A)
. It follows that
v^{\top}A^{\top}Av=\|U_{A}C_{A}y\|_{2}=\|C_{A}y\|_{2}
. Therefore, we obtain

\|\mathcal{A}\|=\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\atop y\neq\mathbf{0}}\frac{\|C_{A}y\|_{2}}{\|y\|_{2}}=\|C_{A}\|_{2}=\sigma_{\max}(C_{A}).

The proof is completed.

Notice that
C_{A}
is a diagonal matrix (not necessarily square). Thus,
\sigma_{\max}(C_{A})
is the maximum value of the diagonals of
C_{A}
. For a regular matrix pair
\{A,L\}
, i.e.
G
is nonsingular, several iterative algorithms exist that can rapidly compute the largest generalized singular values of
\{A,L\}

[
36
,
18
]
, thereby providing an accurate estimate of
\|\mathcal{A}\|
. For a nonregular
\{A,L\}
, the method proposed in
[
20
]
can accomplish the same task.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we provide a new interpretation of the weighted pseudoinverse arising from the GLS problem
\min\|Lx\|_{2}\ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \|M(Ax-b)\|_{2}=\min
. By introducing the linear operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{X}=(\mathcal{R}(G),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G})\rightarrow(\mathcal{R}(P),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{P}),\ v\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}Av
with
P=M^{\top}M
and
G=A^{\top}PA+L^{\top}L
, we have shown that the minimum 2-norm solution of the GLS problem is the minimum
\mathcal{X}
-norm solution of the LS problem
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|\mathcal{A}x-\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}b\|_{P}
. Consequently, the weighted pseudoinverse
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
is shown to be equivalent to
\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}(P)}
under the canonical bases. With this new interpretation of
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}
, we have derived a set of generalized Moore-Penrose equations that completely characterize the weighted pseudoinverse, and provided a closed-form expression for
A_{IL}^{{\dagger}}
using the GSVD of
\{A,L\}
. We have generalized the GKB process and proposed the
gLSQR
algorithm for iteratively computing
A_{ML}^{{\dagger}}b
for any given
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which allows us to compute the minimum 2-norm solution of the GLS problem. Several numerical examples have been used to test the
gLSQR
algorithm, demonstrating that it can compute the solution of a GLS problem with satisfactory accuracy.

The results in this paper suggest that the closely related concepts—GLS, weighted pseudoinverse, GSVD, gGKB, and gLSQR—are appropriate generalizations of the classical concepts LS, pseudoinverse, SVD, GKB, and LSQR. This provides new tools for both analysis and computation of related applications.
