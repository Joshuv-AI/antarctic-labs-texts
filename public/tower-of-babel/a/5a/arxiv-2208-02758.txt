Jinchao Feng, Mauro Maggioni, Patrick Martin, Ming Zhong

Abstract
Dynamical systems across many disciplines are modeled as interacting particles or agents, with interaction rules that depend on a very small number of variables (e.g. pairwise distances, pairwise differences of phases, etc…), functions of the state of pairs of agents. Yet, these interaction rules can generate self-organized dynamics, with complex emergent behaviors (clustering, flocking, swarming, etc.). We propose a learning technique that, given observations of states and velocities along trajectories of the agents, yields both the variables upon which the interaction kernel depends and the interaction kernel itself, in a nonparametric fashion. This yields an effective dimension reduction which avoids the curse of dimensionality from the high-dimensional observation data (states and velocities of all the agents). We demonstrate the learning capability of our method to a variety of first-order interacting systems.

Introduction

Systems of interacting particles or agents are ubiquitous in science. They may be employed to model and interpret intriguing natural phenomena, such as superconductivity,
n
-body problems, and self-organization in biological systems. Collective dynamical systems demonstrate emergent behaviors such as clustering (
Krause 2000
;
Mostch and Tadmor 2014
), flocking (
Vicsek et al. 1995
;
Cucker and Smale 2007
), milling (
Carrillo et al. 2009
;
Albi et al. 2014
), synchronization (
Strogatz 2000
;
O’Keeffe et al. 2017
), and many more (see
Degond et al. 2013
).

We consider interacting-agent systems of
N
agents governed by the equations in the form

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{N}\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(t),\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}}(t))(\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}}(t)-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(t))\,,

(1)

for
i=1,\ldots,N
,
t\in[0,T]
;
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a state vector, for example describing position, speed, emotion, or opinions of the
i^{th}
agent, and
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the
interaction kernel
, governing how the state of agent
i^{\prime}
influences the state of agent
i
.

Note that while the state space of the system is
dN
-dimensional, the interaction kernel is a function of
2d
dimensions.
In fact, in many important models,
\Phi
depends on an even smaller number of natural variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, with
d^{\prime}\ll 2d
, which are functions of pairs
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In other words, the interaction kernel
\Phi
can be factorized (as composition of functions) as a
reduced interaction kernel

\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and
reduced variables

\boldsymbol{\xi}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, i.e.

\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}})=\phi(\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}})).

(2)

An important example is
\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}}):=||\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}}||
, i.e.
\Phi
is in fact just a function of pairwise distances, a one-dimensional variable.
Given a set of discrete observations of trajectory data

\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{(m)}(t_{l}),\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{(m)}(t_{l})\}_{i,l,m=1}^{N,L,M}\,,

with
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{L}=T
and initial conditions
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{(m)}(t_{1})
sampled i.i.d. from
\mu_{0}
(some unknown probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
) for
m=1,\ldots,M
, we are interested in estimating the interaction kernel
\Phi
.
Note that this is not a regression problem, but an inverse problem, since values of the function
\Phi
are not observed (only averages of such values, as per the right-hand side of (
1
), are).
In the works that considered this problem (
Bongini et al. 2017
;
Lu et al. 2019
;
Miller et al. 2020
),
the reduced variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}
were given
, and
\phi
was estimated as a function of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
One of the main takeaways of those works is that the estimation of
\phi
is not cursed by the dimension
dN
of the state space of the system, nor by the dimension
2d
of pairs of agent states, and, under suitable stable identifiability conditions, it is not harder than regression in
d^{\prime}
dimensions, with the min-max learning rate of
M^{-2/(2+d^{\prime})}
(up to log factors) being achievable, with efficient algorithms.

In this work, we consider the case where the reduced variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}
are
not
known: both
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\phi
need to be estimated from observations.
We wish to accomplish this while exploiting the factorization (
2
), in order to obtain rates of estimations that are independent of both the dimension
dN
of the state space and of the dimension of
2d
of pairs of states, and only dependent on
d^{\prime}
.
We relate this problem to the classical multi-index model: one considers functions
f(\boldsymbol{x})=g(A\boldsymbol{x})
, where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
A:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
is a linear map,
g:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and given i.i.d. samples
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))_{i=1}^{n}
, one constructs estimators of
g
and
A
. We defer to
Martin 2021
for discussions, references, and a novel technique for estimating
(g,A)
that avoids the curse of dimensionality and is computationally efficient.

In our setting, we cannot directly apply the techniques of multi-index regression, as we are facing an inverse problem.
We therefore consider a multi-task learning setting, where we are allowed to learn on systems with a varying number
N
of agents, driven by interaction kernels sharing the same variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, and then transfer to other systems with different
N
or
\phi
, but sharing the same
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
Crucially, when
N=2
, it is possible to re-cast the estimation problem for
\phi
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
as a multi-index regression problem, opening the way to efficient estimation.
Transferring to other systems with different
N
is straightforward, and to systems with different
\phi
’s but the same variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}
may be achieved by the techniques of
Lu et al. 2019
;
Miller et al. 2020
.

Related Methods
.
There are many related approaches in learning the governing structures from observation of dynamical trajectory data, i.e.
Ballerini et al. 2008
;
Herbert-Read et al. 2011
;
Brunton et al. 2016
; see also
Miller et al. 2020
for further references. However, most of them do not take into consideration the special structure of self-organized dynamics. Our learning method follows in the line of research of
Bongini et al. 2017
;
Lu et al. 2019
, which exploits both the structure of the equations, and the assumption that
\Phi
depends on a small number of reduced variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}
(e.g. pairwise distance). The learning framework developed in
Bongini et al. 2017
focused on the convergence case when
N
goes to infinity, while
Lu et al. 2019
consider the convergence when
M
goes to infinity; extensions include
Zhong et al. 2020
;
Miller et al. 2020
;
Maggioni et al. 2021
. In these works, the reduced variables are known and the focus was on learning the interaction kernels; here we generalize the framework to the case when the variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}
are unknown.

2
Learning Method

We assume the discrete observations of trajectory data,
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{m}(t_{l}),\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{m}(t_{l})\}_{i,m,l=1}^{N,M,L}
with
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{L}=T
, is equi-spaced in time:
t_{l+1}-t_{l}=T/(L-1)
for all
l
’s.
We seek to infer the variables
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and the reduced interaction kernel
\phi
in the factorization in (
2
).

We proceed by further assuming that
\boldsymbol{\xi}
can be factorized as
\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}}):=B\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}})
, where
\boldsymbol{y}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a
known
feature map to high-dimensional feature vectors, and the feature reduction map
B:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
is an
unknown
linear map to be estimated. What really matters is the range of
B
, and without loss of generality we assume
B
orthogonal. The factorization (
2
) becomes:
\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}})=\phi(B\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}}))
; we estimate
\phi
and
B
in two steps.

Step
1
. Estimating
B
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
.
The techniques of
Martin 2021
, for estimation of the multi-index model, are only applicable in the regression setting. However, we note that if we allow the learning procedure to conduct “experiments” and have access to observations of (
1
) for different values of
N
, and in particular for
N=2
, then from those observations it can extract equations that reveal the values taken by the interaction kernels, taking us back to a regression setting. We then apply the approach of
Martin 2021
to obtain
\hat{B}
, estimating
B
.
This technique is not cursed by the dimension
D
, making it possible to increase the range of the feature map
\boldsymbol{y}
with relatively small additional sampling requirements.
While the choice of the feature map
\boldsymbol{y}
is typically application-dependent, and can incorporate symmetries, or physical constraints on the system, a rather canonical choice is the map to polynomials in the states, and here we restrict ourselves to second-order polynomials, and therefore assume that:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}}):=\big[\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}},

\displaystyle((\boldsymbol{x}_{i})_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})_{j^{\prime}})_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq j^{\prime}\end{subarray}},(({\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}})_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime}})_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq j^{\prime}\end{subarray}},

(3)

\displaystyle((\boldsymbol{x}_{i})_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime}})_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,j^{\prime}=1,\dots,d\end{subarray}}\big]^{T}\!\!\!\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\,,

with
D=2d^{2}+3d
. Of course higher order terms, beyond the quadratic ones, could be added to the feature map
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Step
2
. Estimating
\phi
.
Once an estimate
\hat{B}
for the feature reduction map
B
has been constructed, we proceed to estimate
\phi
.
We project the pairs of states, using
\hat{B}
, to
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, and use the non-parametric estimation techniques of
Lu et al. 2019
: we minimize, over functions
\psi
in a suitable hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}
, the error functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\psi)\!\!:=\!\!\frac{1}{NLM}\!\!\sum_{i,l,m=1}^{N,L,M}\bigg\|{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i,l}^{m}\!-\!\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}=1\end{subarray}}^{N}\psi(\hat{B}\boldsymbol{y}_{i,i^{\prime},l}^{m})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime},l}^{m}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i,l}^{m})}\bigg\|^{2}

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{i,l}^{m}:=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{(m)}(t_{l})
for
i=1,2
, and
\boldsymbol{y}_{i,i^{\prime},l}^{m}:=\boldsymbol{y}_{i,i^{\prime}}^{(m)}(t_{l})
for
i,i^{\prime}=1,2
,
i\neq i^{\prime}
.
\mathcal{H}
is a convex and compact (in the
L^{\infty}
norm) set of functions of the estimated variables
\hat{B}\boldsymbol{y}
, e.g. spanned by splines with knots on a grid, or piecewise polynomials. As in classical nonparametric estimation, the dimension of
\mathcal{H}
will be chosen as a suitably increasing function of the number of training trajectories
M
.

2.1
Details on estimating the reduced variables and kernel

Step
1
described above requires two key ideas: first of all, mapping the inverse problem of estimating the interaction kernel to a regression problem, by considering the problem for
N=2
(from which the solution is immediately transferred to any
N\geq 2
). Secondly, given input-output pairs
((\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{m}(t_{l}),\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}}^{m}(t_{l})),\phi(B\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{m}(t_{l}),\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}}^{m}(t_{l}))))
, with
\phi
and
B
unknown and
\boldsymbol{y}
known, we estimate
B
while trying to avoid the curse of dimensionality – meaning both
2d
(the dimension of
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}})
, the domain of
\Phi
), and
D
(the dimension of the domain of
B
, which is the range of
\boldsymbol{y}
).

First-order system with
N=2
agents
.
We consider the controlled experiments where we observe a first-order system of only
N=2
agents (we drop the dependence on
t
for now to ease the notation), i.e.,

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\,,\,\,\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{1})(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}).

We can transform the equations to

\displaystyle\frac{2\langle{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}\rangle}{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\|^{2}}=\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\,,\,\frac{2\langle{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}\rangle}{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right\|^{2}}=\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}).

Let
\boldsymbol{y}_{1,2}:=\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
as in (
3
); we obtain a regression problem:

\phi(B\boldsymbol{y}_{1,2})=z_{1,2}\coloneqq 2\frac{\langle{\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}\rangle}{\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\|^{2}}=\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}).\\

Similar definitions are used for
\boldsymbol{y}_{2,1}
and
z_{2,1}
. We re-index the observations as
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}:=\{\boldsymbol{y}_{1,2}^{(m)}(t_{l}),\boldsymbol{y}_{2,1}^{(m)}(t_{l})\}_{l,m=1}^{L,M}
and the corresponding
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}:=\{z_{1,2}^{(m)}(t_{l}),z_{2,1}^{(m)}(t_{l})\}_{l,m=1}^{L,M}
, with
Q=2LM
. We are now ready to estimate
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times D}
.

Multiplicatively Perturbed Least Squares
(MPLS) is a computationally efficient algorithm introduced in
Martin 2021
for estimating multi-index models, which we apply here to the estimation of
B
. It decomposes the regression function into linear and nonlinear components:
\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}})=\phi(B\boldsymbol{y}_{i,i^{\prime}})=\langle{\beta,\boldsymbol{y}_{i,i^{\prime}}}\rangle+g(A\boldsymbol{y}_{i,i^{\prime}})
, where
(i,i^{\prime})=\{(1,2),(2,1)\}
and
g
is orthogonal to linear polynomials. The intrinsic domain of
\Phi
, the row-space of
B
, is spanned by
\beta
and the rows of
A
, estimated as follows:

Algorithm [MPLS]

Inputs: training data
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{q},z_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}
, partitioned into subsets
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with sizes
|\mathcal{S}|,|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|\geq\lfloor{Q}/{2}\rfloor
;
parameters
K
and
\lambda
, with
K\gtrsim d^{\prime}\log d^{\prime}
and
\lambda\approx{1}/{D}
(choosing
K
and
\lambda
is discussed in
Martin 2021
).

1.

Estimate
\beta
via ordinary least squares on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}\coloneqq\underset{\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}{\operatorname{arg}\operatorname{min}}\;\frac{2}{|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|}\sum_{(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\Big(z^{\prime}-\langle{\mathbf{\beta^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}\rangle\Big)^{2}.

2.

Let
\mathcal{R}\coloneqq\Big\{(\boldsymbol{y}-\langle\hat{\beta},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\|\hat{\beta}\|^{-2}\hat{\beta},z-\langle{\hat{\beta},\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle)\big|(\boldsymbol{y},z)\in\mathcal{S}\Big\}
be the residual data of this approximation on
\mathcal{S}
, paired with
\boldsymbol{y}
s projected on
\hat{\beta}^{\perp}
.

3.

Pick
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{u}_{K}
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
(e.g., a random subset of
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}
). For each
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
, center the residuals to their weighted mean: with
w(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\boldsymbol{u}_{i})=\exp(-\lambda\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}-\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\right\|^{2})
,

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\coloneqq\Bigg\{\bigg({\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},r-\frac{\sum_{(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},r)\in\mathcal{R}}w(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}};\boldsymbol{u}_{i})r}{\sum_{(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},r)\in\mathcal{R}}w(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}};\boldsymbol{u}_{i})}}\bigg)\bigg|(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},r)\in\mathcal{R}\Bigg\}\,,

​​​and compute the slope perturbations via least squares:

\mathbf{\hat{p}}_{i}\coloneqq\underset{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}{\operatorname{arg}\operatorname{min}}\;\frac{2}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\tilde{r})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}}(w_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}},\boldsymbol{u}_{i})\tilde{r}-\langle{\mathbf{p},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}}\rangle)^{2}\,.

4.

Let
\hat{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}
have rows
\mathbf{\hat{p}}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,K
, and compute the rank-
d^{\prime}
singular value decomposition of
\hat{P}\approx U_{d^{\prime}}\Sigma_{d^{\prime}}V_{d^{\prime}}^{T}
. Return
\hat{A}:=V_{d^{\prime}}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times D}
and
\hat{\beta}
.

The consistency of the estimators and convergence rates are discussed in
Martin 2021
; a key outcome of that analysis is that the sampling requirements for accurately estimating
\beta
and
A
are not exponential in
D
, but a low-degree polynomial in
D
, therefore avoiding the curse of dimensionality. Those results hold when the training data is sampled i.i.d. from some distribution over
\boldsymbol{y}
, while here the samples are clearly not independent along each trajectory, but they are on the
M
different trajectories, as the initial conditions are sampled independently.
It remains to convert the computed
\hat{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times D}
and
\hat{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}
(from linear components) into an estimate of the low-dimensional space
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times D}
. This is done by computing the regression error of a 1-dimensional constant spline approximation to
\{\langle\boldsymbol{y}_{q},\nu\rangle,z_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}
for
\nu\in\{\hat{\beta},\hat{A}_{d^{\prime}}\}
; comparing this error for
\hat{\beta}
and the
d^{\prime}
-th singular vector of
\hat{P}
. If the former’s error is less than the latter’s, which suggests that the linear component is a more significant feature, then we take
\hat{B}
to be the row vector
\hat{\beta}/\|\hat{\beta}\|
stacked on the first
d^{\prime}-1
rows of
\hat{A}
, otherwise we let
\hat{B}\coloneqq\hat{A}
. Note that because the
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
s used to compute
\hat{A}
are projected away from
\hat{\beta}
,
\hat{B}
will have orthonormal rows in either case. This procedure could be done at the beginning to identify useful vectors on the unit ball in
D
dimensions (as in
Juditsky et al. 2009
), however this would involve checking a number of vectors exponential in
D
, and is not computationally feasible here. MPLS allows us to the reduce the problem to one comparison, regardless of
D
.

2.2
Performance Measures

To measure the accuracy in approximating the feature reduction map
B
, we use

\text{Err}_{B}=||{B^{\top}B-\hat{B}^{\top}\hat{B}}||
.
To measure how well
(\hat{B},\hat{\phi})
approximates
(B,\phi)
we use

\displaystyle\text{Err}_{\phi}^{2}:=\int_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}}\big|\phi(B\boldsymbol{y})-\hat{\phi}(\hat{B}\boldsymbol{y})\big|^{2}W^{2}(\boldsymbol{y})\,d\rho_{T}(\boldsymbol{y})\,,

(4)

where
W(\boldsymbol{y}_{i,i^{\prime}})=\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i^{\prime}}\right\|
; its relative variant is

{\text{Err}}^{\text{rel}}_{\phi}:={\text{Err}_{\phi}}/({\int_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\big|\phi(B\boldsymbol{y})\big|^{2}W^{2}(\boldsymbol{y})\,d\rho_{T}(\boldsymbol{y})})^{1/2}
.
The dynamical system, together with its random initial conditions sampled from
\mu_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, induces the probability measure
\rho_{T}(\boldsymbol{y})
on
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, given by

\rho_{T}(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}(t_{1}),\boldsymbol{x}_{2}(t_{1}))\sim\mu_{0}}\int_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{{i,i^{\prime}=1,i\neq i^{\prime}}}^{N}\delta_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i,i^{\prime}}(t)}(\boldsymbol{y})\,.

We measure the trajectory accuracy between the observed trajectory,
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and the trajectory
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
obtained by the system driven by
(\hat{B},\hat{\phi})
(over matching initial conditions) as

\text{Err}_{\text{traj}}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}}\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{t=0}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(t)-\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}(t)\right\|^{2}\,dt\,,

(5)

with the expectation taken over initial conditions
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(t_{1}))_{i=1}^{N}
sampled i.i.d. from
\mu_{0}
.
A relative version
{\text{Err}}^{\text{rel}}_{\text{traj}}
, so that trajectory errors from different dynamics are more comparable, is obtained by dividing
\text{Err}_{\text{traj}}
by

(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(t_{1})}[\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{t=0}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}(t)\right\|^{2}\,dt])^{1/2}\,.

For a set of trajectories with
M
different initial conditions, we report the trajectory error as
\text{Err}_{\text{traj},\text{mean}}^{\text{rel}}
.

2.3
Convergence Characteristics and Limitations

We expect our approach to preserve the strong convergence properties of MPLS and of the estimators of interaction kernels in
Miller et al. 2020
, in particular avoiding the curse of dimensionality in
Nd
and
d^{2}
.
Limitations of our approach include the need of available training data for
N=2
, and the knowledge of the feature map
\boldsymbol{y}
, such that
\xi
is a linear function of
\boldsymbol{y}
.

4
Conclusion

We have shown that we can estimate factorizable high dimensional interaction kernel
\Phi
from high dimensional observation data by first learning the feature reduction map
B
via MPLS, then inferring the reduced interaction kernel
\phi
from a non-parametric regression with the reduction variable
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Our method can accurately estimate the feature reduction map, i.e. the unknown reduced variables in the interaction kernel, as well as the reduced interaction kernel, and provide accurate predictions for the dynamics of the system. Work on systems with different interaction structures, higher order systems, and transfer across multiple systems sharing reduced variables, is ongoing, as is the development of theoretical guarantees on the performance of the estimators, expecting to show that their consistency, under suitable identifiability assumptions, and convergence rate, not cursed by the dimension of the state space of the system nor by the rank of the feature map.
