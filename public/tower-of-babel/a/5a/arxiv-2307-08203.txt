A randomization-based theory for preliminary testing of covariate balance in controlled trials
Anqi Zhao, Peng Ding

Abstract
Randomized trials balance all covariates on average and provide the gold standard for estimating treatment effects. Chance imbalances nevertheless exist more or less in realized treatment allocations and intrigue an important question: what should we do in case the treatment groups differ with respect to some important baseline characteristics? A common strategy is to conduct a preliminary test of the balance of baseline covariates after randomization, and invoke covariate adjustment for subsequent inference if and only if the realized allocation fails some prespecified criterion. Although such practice is intuitive and popular among practitioners, the existing literature has so far only evaluated its properties under strong parametric model assumptions in theory and simulation, yielding results of limited generality. To fill this gap, we examine two strategies for conducting preliminary test-based covariate adjustment by regression, and evaluate the validity and efficiency of the resulting inferences from the randomization-based perspective. As it turns out, the preliminary-test estimator based on the analysis of covariance can be even less efficient than the unadjusted difference in means, and risks anticonservative confidence intervals based on normal approximation even with the robust standard error. The preliminary-test estimator based on the fully interacted specification is on the other hand less efficient than its counterpart under the always-adjust strategy, and yields overconservative confidence intervals based on normal approximation. In addition, although the Fisher randomization test is still finite-sample exact for testing the sharp null hypothesis of no treatment effect on any individual, it is no longer valid for testing the weak null hypothesis of zero average treatment effect in large samples even with properly studentized test statistics. These undesirable properties are due to the asymptotic non-normality of the preliminary-test estimators. Based on theory and simulation, we echo the existing literature and do not recommend the preliminary-test procedure for covariate adjustment in randomized trials.



Keywords:
Causal inference; design-based inference; efficiency; Fisher randomization test; regression adjustment; rerandomization

Introduction

1.1
Preliminary test of covariate balance

Randomized trials balance all observed and unobserved covariates on average, providing the gold standard for estimating treatment effects
(
Fisher 1935
;
Gerber and
Green 2012
;
Rosenberger and
Lachin 2015
)
.
Chance imbalances nevertheless exist more or less in realized treatment allocations
(
Altman 1985
;
Senn 1989
;
Senn 1994
;
Morgan and
Rubin 2012
)
, complicating the interpretation of experimental results; see
Miratrix
et al. 2013
for a canonical example from a clinical trial.
What should we do in case the treatment groups differ with respect to some important baseline characteristics?

There are in general three common responses to this question.
The
unadjusted
strategy trusts the stochastic balance ensured by the randomization mechanism, and uses the difference in average outcomes across treatment groups to estimate the average treatment effect without adjusting for covariates.
The resulting inference is unbiased without any assumptions on the outcome-generating process
(
Neyman 1923
;
Imbens and
Rubin 2015
;
Li and Ding 2017
)
.
The
always-adjust
strategy acknowledges the merits of covariate adjustment in improving the power and efficiency of inference, and always adjusts for all baseline covariates regardless of their balance across treatment groups
(
Fisher 1935
;
Freedman 2008b
;
Lin 2013
;
Li and Ding 2020
)
.
The
preliminary-test
strategy takes a middle ground between the unadjusted and always-adjust strategies, and invokes covariate adjustment for inference if and only if the realized allocation fails some prespecified balance test
(
Schelchter and
Forsythe 1985
;
Beach and
Meier 1989
;
Altman and
Doré 1990
;
Permutt 1990
;
Mutz
et al. 2019
)
.

Although the preliminary-test strategy is intuitive
and popular among practitioners, the theoretical literature holds a negative view on its implications on inference and discourages its use for experimental data in general.

Altman 1985
,
Begg 1990
, and
Senn 1994
questioned the logical foundation of preliminary tests for randomized trials, and argued that such practice is illogical by testing a null hypothesis that is known to be true.
In particular, with the randomization mechanism stochastically balancing all covariates between treatment groups on average, any significant result is by definition a false positive
(
Begg 1990
)
.
The
consort
guidelines
(
Schulz
et al. 2010
)
echoed
Altman 1985
and suggested that preliminary tests are superfluous and can be misleading for interpreting the experimental results.
They accordingly recommended reporting tables of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics by experimental condition but discouraged testing for baseline covariate balance; see also
Boer et al. 2015
.

Senn 1989
examined the effect of covariate imbalance on the type I error rates of a test of treatment efficacy in randomized trials under a joint Gaussian model of outcome and a single covariate, and concluded that preliminary tests of covariate balance are not guaranteed to control the type I error rates.

Permutt 1990
examined the operating characteristics of the preliminary-test procedure under a similar model, and concluded that the preliminary-test procedure has lower power than the always-adjust strategy.

Mutz
et al. 2019
provided an informative account of competing opinions in the medical and political science literature regarding the use of covariate balance test for experimental data,
and concluded that one should not perform such tests unless the experimental data are compromised by complications like faulty randomization or differential attrition.
Despite the strong objections to preliminary tests in the clinical trial literature, however, most theoretical investigations so far were conducted under restricted parametric models
(
Senn 1989
;
Permutt 1990
;
Mutz
et al. 2019
)
, limiting the generality of subsequent findings.
To fill this gap, we take a unified look at the role of covariate balance in controlled trials and clarify the implications of the preliminary-test procedure from the randomization-based perspective.

1.2
Our contributions

This article makes several contributions.
First, we extend the discussion of
Permutt 1990
and
Mutz
et al. 2019
to more general, non-parametric settings and formalize two schemes for preliminary test-based regression adjustment.
The proposed procedures use the Mahalanobis distance of the difference in covariate means to form the covariate balance criterion, and invoke the additive
(
Fisher 1935
)
and fully interacted
(
Lin 2013
)
specifications for regression adjustment, respectively, in case the realized allocation fails the balance test.

Second, we establish the sampling properties of the resulting point and interval estimators from the randomization-based perspective, and quantify their validity and efficiency relative to the unadjusted and always-adjust counterparts.
The main findings are twofold.
First, the preliminary-test estimator based on
Fisher 1935
’s analysis of covariance can be even less efficient than the unadjusted difference in means and, moreover, risks anticonservative confidence intervals based on normal approximation even with the robust standard error.
Second, the preliminary-test estimator based on
Lin 2013
’s fully interacted specification is less efficient than its counterpart under the always-adjust strategy and yields overconservative confidence intervals based on normal approximation.

We then extend the discussion to the Fisher randomization test, and examine the validity of the preliminary-test estimators for testing not only the sharp null hypothesis of no treatment effect on any individual
(
Fisher 1935
;
Rubin 1980
;
Imbens and
Rubin 2015
)
but also the weak null hypothesis of zero average treatment effect
(
Wu and Ding 2021
;
Zhao and Ding 2021a
;
Cohen and
Fogarty 2022
)
.
As it turns out, the asymptotic non-normality of the preliminary-test estimators renders the usual strategy of studentization by Eicker–Huber–White heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors no longer sufficient to ensure the asymptotic validity of the tests for the weak null hypothesis. This illustrates the additional complications incurred by the preliminary-test procedure.

Based on theory and simulation, we discourage the use of the preliminary-test procedure for covariate adjustment in randomized trials and recommend always adjusting for baseline covariates by
Lin 2013
’s specification for both estimation and hypothesis testing.

1.3
Notation and definitions

We use the notation from
Zhao and Ding 2021b
and
Zhao and Ding 2023
to facilitate discussion.
For a given set of tuples
\{(u_{i},v_{i}):u_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\ v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{J};\ i=1,\ldots,N\}
, denote by
\texttt{lm}(u_{i}\sim v_{i})
the least squares fit of the linear regression of
u_{i}
on
v_{i}
over
i=1,\ldots,N
.
We focus on the numeric outputs of least squares without any modeling assumption and evaluate their sampling properties from the randomization-based perspective.

Let
q_{1-\alpha/2}
denote the
(1-\alpha/2)
th quantile of the standard normal distribution.
Let
\mathcal{I}(\cdot)
denote the indicator function.
For two random variables
A
and
B
with cumulative distribution functions
F_{A}(t)={\mathbb{P}}(A\leq t)
and
F_{B}(t)={\mathbb{P}}(B\leq t)
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let

\displaystyle\textup{Mix}\begin{pmatrix}A&:&\pi\\
B&:&1-\pi\end{pmatrix}

denote the mixture of
A
and
B
with weights
(\pi,1-\pi)
for
\pi\in[0,1]
. The corresponding cumulative distribution function is
F(t)=\pi F_{A}(t)+(1-\pi)F_{B}(t)
.

Assume that
\hat{\theta}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\hat{\theta}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
are two consistent estimators for some parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
We say
\hat{\theta}_{1}
is
asymptotically more efficient
than
\hat{\theta}_{2}
if asymptotically,
\hat{\theta}_{1}-\theta
has smaller central quantile ranges than
\hat{\theta}_{2}-\theta
.
An asymptotically more efficient estimator has smaller asymptotic variance.
For
\hat{\theta}_{1}
and
\hat{\theta}_{2}
that are both asymptotically normal,
\hat{\theta}_{1}
is asymptotically more efficient than
\hat{\theta}_{2}
if and only if the asymptotic variance of
\hat{\theta}_{1}
is smaller than that of
\hat{\theta}_{2}
.

To avoid excessive notation, we state the theorems in general terms in the main paper and relegate the rigorous yet technical statements to the Supplementary Material.
In particular, all theoretical results in the main paper assume a completely randomized treatment-control trial with a given set of regularity conditions for asymptotic analysis.
We give the definition of completely randomized treatment-control trials in Section
2
and relegate the regularity conditions to the Supplementary Material.

Basic setting of the treatment-control trial

We review in this section the basic setting of completely randomized treatment-control trials under the potential outcomes framework
(
Neyman 1923
)
, and give an overview of the classical results on regression-based causal inference.
We then formalize the preliminary-test procedure as the focus of our discussion.

2.1
Potential outcomes and treatment effects

Consider an intervention of two levels,
z=0
(control),
1
(treatment), and a finite population of
N
units, indexed by
i=1,\ldots,N
.
We invoke the potential outcomes framework and denote by
Y_{i}(z)\in\mathbb{R}
the potential outcome of unit
i
under treatment level
z\in\{0,1\}
. This notation assumes the stable unit treatment value assumption
(
Rubin 1980
)
that the potential outcomes for any unit do not vary with the treatments assigned to other
units, and, for each unit, there are no different forms or versions of each treatment level,
which lead to different potential outcomes.
The individual treatment effect is
\tau_{i}=Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
, and the finite population average treatment effect is
\tau=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i}
.
Let
x_{i}=(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iJ})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
be the
J
-dimensional covariate vector for unit
i
.
We center the
x_{i}
’s with
\bar{x}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}=0_{J}
to simplify the presentation.

Let
Z_{i}\in\{0,1\}
denote the treatment level received by unit
i
.
For some prespecified, fixed treatment sizes
N_{1}>0
and
N_{0}=N-N_{1}>0
, complete randomization draws completely at random
N_{1}
units to receive treatment and assigns the remaining
N_{0}
units to control.
The observed outcome is
Y_{i}=Z_{i}Y_{i}(1)+(1-Z_{i})Y_{i}(0)
for unit
i
.

Let
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times J}
,
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
summarize the covariates, treatment assignments, and observed outcomes of all
N
units.
Let
e_{z}=N_{z}/N
for
z=0,1
.

2.2
Regression analysis and review of classical results

Let
\hat{Y}(z)=N_{z}^{-1}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}Y_{i}
denote the average observed outcome under treatment level
z
.
The difference in means
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}=\hat{Y}(1)-\hat{Y}(0)
is unbiased for
\tau
under complete randomization
(
Neyman 1923
)
, and can be computed as the coefficient of
Z_{i}
from the simple linear regression
\texttt{lm}(Y_{i}\sim 1+Z_{i})
.

The presence of covariates promises the opportunity to improve estimation efficiency.

Fisher 1935
suggested an estimator
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}}
for
\tau
, which equals the coefficient of
Z_{i}
from the additive linear regression
\texttt{lm}(Y_{i}\sim 1+Z_{i}+x_{i})
.

Lin 2013
recommended an alternative estimator,
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
, as the coefficient of
Z_{i}
from the fully interacted linear regression
\texttt{lm}(Y_{i}\sim 1+Z_{i}+x_{i}+Z_{i}x_{i})
.
We use the subscripts
n
,
f
, and
l
to signify
Neyman 1923
,
Fisher 1935
, and
Lin 2013
, respectively.

Standard theory ensures that

\hat{\tau}_{*}\ (*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
are all asymptotically normal with mean
\tau

and the asymptotic variance of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
is the smallest among the three
(
Neyman 1923
;
Li and Ding 2017
;
Freedman 2008a
;
Lin 2013
)
; we give the explicit forms of their asymptotic variances in the supplementary material.
This ensures the asymptotic efficiency of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
over
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
and
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}}
.
In addition, the corresponding Eicker–Huber–White heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors from least squares, denoted by
\hat{\textup{se}}_{*}
for
*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
, are asymptotically conservative for estimating the true sampling standard errors of the
\hat{\tau}_{*}
’s.
This justifies the large-sample Wald-type inference based on
(\hat{\tau}_{*},\hat{\textup{se}}_{*})
and normal approximation, with the resulting
1-\alpha
large-sample confidence intervals taking the form

\displaystyle\textup{CI}_{*}=\left[\hat{\tau}_{*}-q_{1-\alpha/2}\hat{\textup{se}}_{*},\ \hat{\tau}_{*}+q_{1-\alpha/2}\hat{\textup{se}}_{*}\right]\quad(*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}).

Asymptotically, the limit of
\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{l}}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{*}
is less than or equal to 1 for
*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}}

(
Zhao and Ding 2021a
, Corollary 3)
such that
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}}
is the shortest among
\{\textup{CI}_{*}:*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}\}
. Importantly, these theoretical guarantees are all randomization-based and hold regardless of how well the linear models represent the truth.

2.3
Preliminary-test procedure

In the context of regression analysis of randomized trials,
the
preliminary-test procedure
suggests that regression adjustment for covariates should only be conducted if the realized treatment allocation fails to meet some prespecified covariate balance criterion
(
Permutt 1990
)
.
Let
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}}
and
\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{adj}}
denote the covariate-adjusted estimator and its associated standard error based on some prespecified regression specification.
The resulting preliminary-test procedure proceeds with inference in the following two steps:

(i)

test for covariate balance based on
(X,Z)=(x_{i},Z_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
;

(ii)

conduct inference based on
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{adj}})
if the test is rejected, and based on
(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{n}})
if otherwise.

Let
\phi=\phi(X,Z)\in\{0,1\}
denote the corresponding covariate balance test, with
\phi=0
if the test is rejected and
\phi=1
if otherwise.
Intuitively,
\phi=1
indicates that the realized allocation is balanced by meeting the prespecified balance criterion, whereas
\phi=0
indicates otherwise.
The estimator used by the preliminary-test procedure, referred to as the
preliminary-test estimator
, is then

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}&\text{if $\phi=1$;}\\
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}}&\text{if $\phi=0$,}\end{array}\right.

with standard error

\displaystyle\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{n}}&\text{if $\phi=1$;}\\
\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{adj}}&\text{if $\phi=0$.}\end{array}\right.

We use the subscript
pt
to represent “preliminary test”. The definition of
\phi
ensures

\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt}}=\phi\cdot\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}+(1-\phi)\cdot\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}},\quad\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt}}=\phi\cdot\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{n}}+(1-\phi)\cdot\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{adj}}.

Recall
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}
as the
1-\alpha
large-sample confidence interval based on
(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{n}})
and normal approximation. Let
\textup{CI}_{\textup{adj}}=[\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}}-q_{1-\alpha/2}\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{adj}},\ \hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}}+q_{1-\alpha/2}\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{adj}}]
be the analog based on the covariate-adjusted estimator and its associated standard error.
Following the same spirit as the definitions of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt}}
and
\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt}}
, a common strategy is to
construct the large-sample preliminary-test confidence interval as

\displaystyle\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}&\text{if $\phi=1$;}\\
\textup{CI}_{\textup{adj}}&\text{if $\phi=0$.}\end{array}\right.

The definitions of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}}
and
\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{adj}}
above are general and compatible with all common covariate adjustment strategies in practice.
We categorize the commonly used preliminary-test procedures into two categories depending on whether
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{adj}})
adjusts for all or only a subset of the covariates.

2.3.1
Preliminary-test procedure without variable selection

The first category of procedures adjusts for all covariates if the realized allocation fails the balance test. Given the convenient regression-adjusted estimators
(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{f}})
and
(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{l}})
, a straightforward example is to define
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}},\textup{CI}_{\textup{adj}})=(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}},\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{f}}})
or
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}},\textup{CI}_{\textup{adj}})=(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}},\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}})
. The resulting preliminary-test estimator is then
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt}}=\phi\cdot\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}+(1-\phi)\cdot\hat{\tau}_{*}
for
*\in\{{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}\}
, equaling a mixture of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
and
\hat{\tau}_{*}
.

Let
\hat{\tau}_{x}=\hat{x}(1)-\hat{x}(0)

denote the difference in covariate means with
\hat{x}(z)=N_{z}^{-1}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=z}x_{i}
for
z=0,1
.
Its Mahalanobis distance from the origin, denoted by

\displaystyle M=\hat{\tau}_{x}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\{\text{cov}(\hat{\tau}_{x})\}^{-1}\hat{\tau}_{x},

defines a measure of covariate balance across all covariates
(
Morgan and
Rubin 2012
)
.
A possible test of covariate balance is to consider a treatment allocation as balanced if
M
is less than a prespecified threshold
a\geq 0
with

\displaystyle\phi=\phi_{\textsc{m}}=\mathcal{I}(M<a).

Zhao and Ding 2021b
showed that such a test is asymptotically equivalent to a multivariate two-sample
t
-test based on
(X,Z)=(x_{i},Z_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
.

2.3.2
Preliminary-test procedure with variable selection

The second category of procedures tests balance marginally for each covariate and adjusts for only the significantly imbalanced covariates in analysis. A common choice for the covariate-wise balance test is to conduct a two-sample
t
-test for each covariate and declare the covariate imbalanced if the associated
p
-value is less than a prespecified threshold such as 0.05.

We will focus on preliminary-test procedures based on
\phi_{\textsc{m}}
without variable selection for the rest of this article due to its conceptual straightforwardness, and derive in the following sections its sampling properties from the randomization-based perspective.
The preliminary-test procedures with variable selection, on the other hand, involve post-selection inference and are hence technically more challenging.
Despite the limited gain in efficiency from simulation evidence when the number of covariates is small
(
Schelchter and
Forsythe 1985
;
Permutt 1990
;
Raab
et al. 2000
)
,
we conjecture that such variable-selection procedures may have merits over the always-adjust strategy with high-dimensional covariates, where naive regression adjustment suffers from overfitting.
We relegate the formal theory to future research.

Preliminary test based on the Mahalanobis distance

The preliminary-test procedure based on
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=\mathcal{I}(M<a)
uses the Mahalanobis distance
M
to measure the covariate balance and conducts regression adjustment if and only if
M
is greater than or equal to some prespecified threshold
a\geq 0
.
We focus on the two special cases of
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{adj}})=(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{f}})
and
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{adj}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{adj}})=(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{l}})
for regression adjustment when
M\geq a
, and derive the sampling properties of the resulting
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt}}
and
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt}}
for inferring
\tau
.
To simplify the presentation,
let
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}},\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}})
and
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}},\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}})
denote the values of
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt}},\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt}})
when the analyzer uses the additive and fully interacted specifications for regression adjustment, respectively. We have

\displaystyle(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*},\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{n}},\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}})&\text{if $M<a$}\\
(\hat{\tau}_{*},\hat{\textup{se}}_{*},\textup{CI}_{*})&\text{if $M\geq a$}\end{array}\right.

with
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}=[\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}-q_{1-\alpha/2}\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*},\ \hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}+q_{1-\alpha/2}\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*}]
for
*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
.

3.1
Sampling distributions of the point estimators

Let

\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
be a standard normal random variable,

D=(D_{1},\ldots,D_{J})^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0_{J},I_{J})
be a
J
-dimensional standard normal random vector,
and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}\sim D_{1}\mid\{D^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}D<a\},\quad\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\sim D_{1}\mid\{D^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}D\geq a\}

be two truncated normal random variables independent of
\epsilon
.
Intuitively, the truncated normal distributions characterize the distributions of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
when
M<a
and
M\geq a
, respectively, with
{\mathcal{L}}
first introduced by
Li et al. 2018
in the theory of rerandomization.
Let
\pi_{a}={\mathbb{P}}(D^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}D<a)
denote the asymptotic probability of
M<a

(
Li et al. 2018
)
. For
*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
, let
v_{*}
denote the asymptotic variance of
\hat{\tau}_{*}
in the sense that
\sqrt{N}(\hat{\tau}_{*}-\tau)\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}(0,v_{*})
; we give the explicit forms of
v_{*}
in the supplementary material.
Proposition
1
below is our first new result in this article and establishes the asymptotic sampling distribution of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
as a mixture of two possibly non-normal distributions for
*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
.

Proposition 1
.

As
N\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}-\tau)

\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow

\displaystyle\textup{Mix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{\textsc{l}}^{1/2}\epsilon+(v_{\textsc{n}}-v_{\textsc{l}})^{1/2}\mathcal{L}&:&\pi_{a}\\
v_{\textsc{l}}^{1/2}\epsilon+(v_{*}-v_{\textsc{l}})^{1/2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}&:&1-\pi_{a}\end{pmatrix}

for
*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
.

Proposition
1
ensures that
\sqrt{N}(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}-\tau)
converges in distribution to a mixture of
v_{\textsc{l}}^{1/2}\epsilon+(v_{\textsc{n}}-v_{\textsc{l}})^{1/2}\mathcal{L}
and

v_{\textsc{l}}^{1/2}\epsilon+(v_{*}-v_{\textsc{l}})^{1/2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with weights
(\pi_{a},1-\pi_{a})
for
*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
. For
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
, the second component reduces to
v_{\textsc{l}}^{1/2}\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v_{\textsc{l}})
as the coefficient of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
equals
0
.

Theorem
1
below is a direct consequence of Proposition
1
and quantifies the asymptotic relative efficiency of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
.

Theorem 1
.

As
N\to\infty
,

(i)

\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
are less efficient than
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
;

(ii)

\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
is more efficient than
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
and
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
;

(iii)

\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
may be less efficient than
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
.

Theorem
1
(
i
), combined with the classical results in Section
2.2
, ensures that the
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
under the always-adjust strategy is asymptotically the most efficient among
\{\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}},\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}},\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}},\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}},\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}\}
.
Theorem
1
(
i
)–(
ii
) together ensure that the preliminary-test procedure, when using the fully interacted specification, improves efficiency over the unadjusted
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
asymptotically yet is always dominated by
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
.
This is intuitively because of its mixed use of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
and
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
depending on the covariate balance, where
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
ensures asymptotic efficiency over
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}

(
Lin 2013
)
.
Theorem
1
(
iii
), on the other hand, suggests that the preliminary-test procedure using the additive specification may be even worse than
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
.
This is intuitively the consequence of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}}
being not necessarily more efficient than
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}

(
Freedman 2008a
)
.
We illustrate these results by simulation in Section
7
.

3.2
Coverage rates of the large-sample confidence intervals

Recall that
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}

denotes the
1-\alpha
large-sample confidence interval based on
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*})
and normal approximation for
*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
.
We quantify in this subsection their asymptotic coverage rates for inferring
\tau
.

Recall that
\textup{CI}_{*}

denotes the
1-\alpha
large-sample confidence interval based on
(\hat{\tau}_{*},\hat{\textup{se}}_{*})
for
*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
.
They are
asymptotically valid
in the sense that each
\textup{CI}_{*}
covers
\tau
with probability at least
1-\alpha
as
N
tends to infinity and the coverage rates are asymptotically exact if
\tau_{i}
’s are constant over
i=1,\ldots,N
.
We call a confidence interval
overconservative
if its coverage rate exceeds the nominal level even under the constant treatment effects, and call a confidence interval
anticonservative
if its coverage rate can be less than the nominal level.
Theorem
2
below extends the classical results on
\textup{CI}_{*}\ (*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
to the preliminary-test intervals and states the asymptotic anti- and overconservativeness of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
and
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
, respectively.

Theorem 2
.

As
N\to\infty
,

(i)

\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
is overconservative;

(ii)

\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
is overconservative when either
e_{0}=e_{1}=1/2
or the individual effects
\tau_{i}
are constant across
i=1,\ldots,N
, but can be anticonservative in the presence of heterogeneous treatment effects and unequal treatment group sizes;

(iii)

\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}}
is the shortest among
\{\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}},\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{f}}},\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}},\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}},\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}\}
;
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
is no wider than
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}
, whereas
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
can be wider than
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}
.

Theorem
2
(
i
) justifies the Wald-type inference based on
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
but also points out the issue of overconservativeness.
Intuitively, it follows from the definition in (
3
) that the coverage rate of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
is a weighted average of the coverage rate of
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}
when
M<a
and the coverage rate of
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}}
when
M\geq a
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\in\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}})\ =\ {\mathbb{P}}(\tau\in\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}\mid M<a)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(M<a)+{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\in\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}}\mid M\geq a)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(M\geq a).

Standard theory ensures that
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}}
is asymptotically valid when
M\geq a
, whereas
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}
is asymptotically overconservative when
M<a
. This clarifies the source of overconservativeness in the coverage rate of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
. We relegate the details to Theorem
5
in Section
6
to avoid repetition.

Theorem
2
(
ii
) points out the possible anticonservativeness of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
in the presence of heterogeneous treatment effects such that normal approximation alone is not sufficient for interval estimation based on
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}})
.
Intuitively, the same reasoning as above ensures

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\in\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}})\ =\ {\mathbb{P}}(\tau\in\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}\mid M<a)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(M<a)+{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\in\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{f}}}\mid M\geq a)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(M\geq a),

with
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}
being asymptotically overconservative when
M<a
. Our theory in Theorem
5
later nevertheless suggests that

\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{f}}}
can be asymptotically anticonservative when
M\geq a
. This implies that the overall coverage rate of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
may be less than the nominal level asymptotically, especially when
a
is small such that
{\mathbb{P}}(M\geq a)
dominates the weighted average.
The issue becomes more severe when viewed from a conditional perspective, which we discuss in detail in Section
6
.
Two exceptions are when
e_{0}=e_{1}=1/2
with equal-sized treatment groups or when
\tau_{i}
’s are constant across all
i
.
They both ensure that
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{f}}}
is asymptotically valid when
M\geq a
such that
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
is overall overconservative as a result of the overcoverage of
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}
when
M<a
.
These results complement
Permutt 1990
, who studied
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
under a joint Gaussian model of outcome and a single covariate and derived its overconservativeness under constant treatment effects.
We illustrate the novel result on possible anticonservativeness by simulation in Section
7
.

Theorem
2
(
iii
) suggests that
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
is asymptotically narrower than
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}
despite the overconservativeness but is wider than
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}}
.
This, together with the classical results from Section
2.2
, ensures that
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}}
is the narrowest among
\{\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}},\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{f}}},\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}},\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}},\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}\}
, ensuring asymptotic validity without overconservativeness. It is our recommendation for interval estimation under complete randomization.

Corrections for the anti- and overconservativeness of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
can be made by using preliminary test-specific confidence intervals based on the asymptotic distributions in Proposition
1
.
The resulting intervals, while ensuring better calibration than
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}
, are computationally more complicated and asymptotically still wider than
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}}
.
We thus do not recommend them and relegate the details to the Supplementary Material.

Fisher randomization test

The discussion so far concerns the point and interval estimation of the average treatment effect
\tau
.
The Fisher randomization test (
frt
), on the other hand, provides a convenient way to test
not only the sharp null hypothesis of no treatment effect on any individual
(
Fisher 1935
;
Rubin 1980
;
Imbens and
Rubin 2015
)
but also the weak null hypothesis of zero average treatment effect
(
Wu and Ding 2021
;
Zhao and Ding 2021a
;
Cohen and
Fogarty 2022
)
.
We extend in this section the discussion to
frt
s with test statistics from the preliminary-test procedure and evaluate their validity for testing the sharp and weak null hypotheses, respectively.

A test statistic
T=T(Z,Y,X)
is a function of the treatment assignments, observed outcomes, and covariate vectors.
Let
\mathcal{Z}
denote the set of all
N!
permutations of the realized assignment vector
Z
.

Fisher 1935
considered testing the sharp null hypothesis

\displaystyle H_{0{\textsc{f}}}:\tau_{i}=0\quad\text{for all}\ \ i=1,\ldots,N

and proposed the
frt
to compute the
p
-value as

p_{\textsc{frt}}=|\mathcal{Z}|^{-1}\sum_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{I}\left\{\big|T(\bm{z},Y,X)\big|\geq\big|T(Z,Y,X)\big|\right\}

(7)

for a two-sided test.
Common choices of the test statistic include
\hat{\tau}_{*}
and
\hat{\tau}_{*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{*}
for
*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
.

When applied to test statistics based on
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*})
from the preliminary-test procedure, this means we compute the value of
M
and
(\hat{\tau}_{*},\hat{\textup{se}}_{*})\ (*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
for each
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
, denoted by
M(\bm{z})
and
(\hat{\tau}_{*}(\bm{z}),\hat{\textup{se}}_{*}(\bm{z}))
respectively, and form
T(\bm{z},Y,X)
using

\displaystyle\big(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}(\bm{z}),\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*}(\bm{z})\big)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\big(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}(\bm{z}),\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{n}}(\bm{z})\big)&\text{if $M(\bm{z})<a$}\\
\big(\hat{\tau}_{*}(\bm{z}),\hat{\textup{se}}_{*}(\bm{z})\big)&\text{if $M(\bm{z})\geq a$}\end{array}\right.

for
*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
.
The resulting inference is finite-sample exact for testing
H_{0{\textsc{f}}}
regardless of the choice of the test statistic
T
.
Recall that the confidence interval constructed based on
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}})
and normal approximation may be asymptotically invalid for inferring
\tau
. The
frt
for
H_{0{\textsc{f}}}
thus remains one way of drawing valid inference from
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}})
without additional modifications.
Edwards 1999
also made this point.

The nice properties of the
frt
motivate endeavors to generalize its use to other types of null hypothesis. That is, for an arbitrary null hypothesis
H_{0}
, we can pretend that we are testing the sharp null hypothesis, and compute
p_{\textsc{frt}}
based on imputation under
H_{0{\textsc{f}}}
. We then reject
H_{0}
if
p_{\textsc{frt}}
is less than the prespecified significance level
(
Wu and Ding 2021
)
.
Whereas the finite-sample exactness is in general lost for null hypotheses other than
H_{0{\textsc{f}}}
, a careful choice of the test statistic can nevertheless deliver asymptotically valid inference based on
p_{\textup{frt}}
.
Formally, for an arbitrary null hypothesis
H_{0}
, a test statistic
T
is
asymptotically valid
for testing
H_{0}
if under
H_{0}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(p_{\textsc{frt}}\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha\qquad\text{for all }\alpha\in(0,1).

Under this general definition of asymptotic validity,

Ding and
Dasgupta 2018
,
Wu and Ding 2021
, and
Zhao and Ding 2021a
applied the
frt
to testing the weak null hypothesis

H_{0{\textsc{n}}}:\tau=0

and showed that the studentized
\hat{\tau}_{*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{*}\ (*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
are asymptotically valid, whereas the unstudentized
\hat{\tau}_{*}\ (*=\textsc{n},{\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
are not.
This illustrates the utility of studentization by robust standard errors in delivering asymptotically valid
frt
s for testing
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
.
Theorem
3
below extends the theory to test statistics based on the preliminary-test procedure and gives a negative result on the validity of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
and
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
for testing
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
.

Theorem 3
.

None of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
and
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
are asymptotically valid for testing
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
.

Theorem
3
suggests that studentization by robust standard errors alone is no longer sufficient to ensure the asymptotic validity of test statistics from the preliminary-test procedure for testing
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
.
This is a consequence of the asymptotic non-normality of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
and illustrates the complications incurred by the preliminary-test procedure on subsequent analysis.
We illustrate the possibly liberal type I error rates by simulation in Section
7
.

In cases where inference based on the hypothesis testing of
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
is still desired for outputs from the preliminary-test procedure, we recommend using the prepivoting procedure proposed by
Cohen and
Fogarty 2022
to restore the asymptotic validity of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
and
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
.
The resulting
frt
s simultaneously deliver finite-sample exact inference for
H_{0{\textsc{f}}}
and asymptotically valid inference for
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
.
We relegate the details to the Supplementary Material.

The preliminary-test intervals
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
and
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
give another approach to testing
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
, recalling the duality between hypothesis testing and interval estimation. By Theorem
2
, hypothesis testing based on
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
preserves the type I error rates asymptotically, whereas that based on
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
does not.
This differs from Theorem
3
, illustrating the discrepancy between the two approaches in case of non-normal asymptotic distributions.
In addition, the prepivoted
frt
s are finite-sample exact for the sharp null hypothesis whereas the confidence interval-inverted tests are not.

A trio of covariate adjustment

Sections
3
and
4
clarify the sampling properties of the preliminary-test procedure from the randomization-based perspective.
In particular,
the confidence interval
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
based on
Fisher 1935
’s analysis of covariance and normal approximation may be anticonservative for interval estimation,
and studentization alone can no longer ensure the asymptotic validity of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
for testing the weak null hypothesis.
This illustrates the complications incurred by the preliminary-test procedure on subsequent inference.
We now put these results in perspective, and unify the preliminary-test procedure with regression adjustment and rerandomization as two prevalent approaches to handling covariate imbalance in randomized trials.

5.1
Regression adjustment for covariate imbalance

Regression adjustment by
Fisher 1935
and
Lin 2013
can be viewed as adjusting for covariate imbalance in the analysis stage.
Specifically, let
\hat{\gamma}_{\textsc{f}}
be the coefficient vector of
x_{i}
from
\texttt{lm}(Y_{i}\sim 1+Z_{i}+x_{i})
over
i=1,\ldots,N
, and let
\hat{\gamma}_{\textsc{l}}=e_{0}\hat{\gamma}_{{\textsc{l}},1}+e_{1}\hat{\gamma}_{{\textsc{l}},0}
, where
\hat{\gamma}_{{\textsc{l}},z}
denotes the coefficient vector of
x_{i}
from
\texttt{lm}(Y_{i}\sim 1+x_{i})
over
\{i:Z_{i}=z\}
.

Zhao and Ding 2021a
showed that

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{*}=\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}-\hat{\gamma}_{*}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\hat{\tau}_{x}\qquad(*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}).

The two covariate-adjusted estimators are thus effectively variants of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
with corrections for the difference in covariate means.

5.2
Rerandomization based on the Mahalanobis distance

Rerandomization, on the other hand, enforces covariate balance in the design stage
(
Morgan and
Rubin 2012
)
, and accepts an allocation if and only if it satisfies some prespecified covariate balance criterion.

Morgan and
Rubin 2012
and
Li et al. 2018
studied a special type of rerandomization, known as ReM, that uses the Mahalanobis distance
M
as the balance criterion and accepts a randomization if and only if
M<a
for some prespecified threshold
a\geq 0
.
It can be viewed as a more proactive alternative of the preliminary-test procedure, using results from the covariate balance test to guide not downstream analysis but upstream design, preventing covariate imbalance from the very beginning.

Li and Ding 2020
established the duality between ReM and regression adjustment for improving efficiency under the treatment-control trial, and showed that ReM does not affect the asymptotic efficiency of the efficiently adjusted
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
.
This justifies the large-sample Wald-type inference based on
\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{l}}}
under ReM, identical to that under complete randomization.

Zhao and Ding 2021a
further clarified the impact of ReM on the
frt
s for both
H_{0{\textsc{f}}}
and
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
. In particular, they showed that
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{n}}
is no longer asymptotically valid for testing
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
under ReM, whereas the covariate-adjusted
\hat{\tau}_{*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
still are. This highlights the value of covariate adjustment for valid
frt
s of
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
under ReM, and provides the intuition behind the asymptotic invalidity of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
in Theorem
3
as a result of the asymptotic invalidity of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{n}}
when
M<a
. See
Branson
et al. 2022
for a comparison of testing power under ReM and complete randomization.

A conditional perspective

Recall that
Begg 1990
and
Senn 1994
argued critically against the logical foundation of preliminary tests.
Both authors, nevertheless, acknowledged the value of covariate balance information in yielding more informed inference
conditionally
.
Intuitively, the conditional perspective evaluates the sampling properties of the procedure of interest over repeated samples that are
similar
to the one that is actually realized, promising more relevant quantification of the uncertainty with regard to the data at hand.
We extend in this section their discussion to conditional inference based on the preliminary-test procedure under the randomization-based framework,
and
consider the conditional properties of the preliminary-test procedure based on
\phi_{\textsc{m}}
.
The resulting theory, as it turns out, provides important building blocks for understanding the results under unconditional inference in Sections
3
–
5
.

6.1
Similarity of allocations based on covariate balance test

The discussion in Sections
3
–
5
takes an unconditional perspective, and evaluates the sampling properties of the preliminary-test procedure over all possible complete randomizations of treatment sizes
(N_{0},N_{1})
.
The conditional perspective instead focuses only on the subset of randomizations that are
similar
to the one that is actually realized.

Recall that
\mathcal{Z}
denotes the set of all possible permutations of the assignment vector
Z
.
Its nonrepetitive elements constitute all possible complete randomizations of treatment sizes
(N_{0},N_{1})
.
Motivated by the way the preliminary-test procedure determines the inference specification by the value of
\phi_{\textsc{m}}
, we define
\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})
as the value of
\phi_{\textsc{m}}
if
Z=\bm{z}
for each
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
, and view two allocations
\bm{z}
and
\bm{z}^{\prime}
as similar if
\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})=\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})
.
This divides all possible complete randomizations into two types: those that are balanced with
\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})=1
and those that are unbalanced with
\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})=0
.

The conditional inference then evaluates the sampling properties of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
and
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}
over all allocations that are similar to the realized
Z
in the sense of
\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})=\phi_{\textsc{m}}
.
Intuitively, this means that we evaluate the distribution of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
and the coverage rates of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}
over all balanced allocations with
\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})=1
if the realized allocation is balanced with
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
, and over all unbalanced allocations with
\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})=0
if the realized allocation is unbalanced with
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
.

6.2
Sampling distributions conditioning on
\phi_{\textup{M}}

Proposition
2
below states the asymptotic sampling distributions conditioning on
\phi_{\textsc{m}}
.

Proposition 2
.

(i)

Conditioning on
\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1\}
, we have
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}=\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
for
*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
, with

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}-\tau)\mid\left\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1\right\}

\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow

\displaystyle v_{\textsc{l}}^{1/2}\epsilon+(v_{\textsc{n}}-v_{\textsc{l}})^{1/2}\mathcal{L}\quad(*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}).

(ii)

Conditioning on
\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0\}
, we have
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}=\hat{\tau}_{*}
for
*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}
, with

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}-\tau)\mid\left\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0\right\}

\displaystyle\rightsquigarrow

\displaystyle v_{\textsc{l}}^{1/2}\epsilon+(v_{*}-v_{\textsc{l}})^{1/2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\quad(*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}}).

Proposition
2
establishes the asymptotic sampling distribution of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
as a convolution of independent normal and truncated normal random variables conditioning on
\phi_{\textsc{m}}
.
The forms of the asymptotic distributions in Proposition
2
(i) follow from the numerical equivalence
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}=\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
and the asymptotic conditional distribution of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
established by
Li et al. 2018
. Similarly, the forms of the asymptotic distributions in Proposition
2
(ii) follow from the numerical equivalence
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}=\hat{\tau}_{*}
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
and the asymptotic conditional distributions of
\hat{\tau}_{*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
as a novel result.
The truncation is intuitively the result of the restrictions imposed by
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
and
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
, respectively.
Juxtapose Proposition
2
with Proposition
1
.
The asymptotic unconditional distribution of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
in Proposition
1
is essentially a mixture of the asymptotic conditional distributions of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
and
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
, respectively, with
\pi_{a}
giving the asymptotic probability of
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
.
This gives the intuition behind Proposition
1
from a conditional perspective.

Further observe that Proposition
2
(
ii
) ensures that
\sqrt{N}(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}-\tau)
is asymptotically
\mathcal{N}(0,v_{\textsc{l}})
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
, identical to its asymptotic distribution without conditioning.
This, together with
\sqrt{N}(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}-\tau)\mid\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1\}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}(0,v_{\textsc{l}})
by
Li and Ding 2020
, ensures that the covariate balance status has no effect on the efficiently adjusted
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
asymptotically, echoing the duality between regression adjustment and ReM in improving efficiency
(
Li and Ding 2020
)
.

Theorem
4
below follows from Proposition
2
and quantifies the conditional relative efficiency of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
.

Theorem 4
.

(i)

Conditioning on
\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1\}
,
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}=\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
are asymptotically less efficient than
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
.

(ii)

Conditioning on
\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0\}
,
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}=\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
whereas
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}=\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}}
is asymptotically less efficient than
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
.

Recall the inferiority of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
to
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
for unconditional inference from Theorem
1
.
Theorem
4
ensures that such inferiority extends to the conditional inference given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}
, corroborating the advantage of the always-adjust strategy over the preliminary-test procedure.
Intuitively, the results follow from the numerical equivalence of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}
to
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
and
\hat{\tau}_{*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
and
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
, respectively, and the conditional inferiority of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
and
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}}
to
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
as established by
Li and Ding 2020
.

We focus on the conditional inference given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}
due to its connection with the preliminary-test procedure.
Alternatively, we can also consider the conditional inference given
\hat{\tau}_{x}
, which leaves
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
the only consistent estimator among
\{\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}},\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}},\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}},\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}},\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}\}
. This extends the discussion of
Senn 1989
, who formalized the notion of
conditional size
as the probability of a type I error given a particular value of
\hat{\tau}_{x}
and gave its explicit form under a joint Gaussian model of outcome and a single covariate with known dispersion parameters. See also
Begg 1990
,
Senn 1994
, and
Ding 2021
.

6.3
Coverage rates conditioning on
\phi_{\textup{M}}

Recall that
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
denote the
1-\alpha
large-sample confidence intervals based on
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*})
and normal approximation.
Theorem
2
evaluates their asymptotic coverage rates over all possible complete randomizations of treatment group sizes
(N_{0},N_{1})
.
Theorem
5
below instead clarifies their respective conditional coverage rates over all allocations with the same covariate balance status as the realized
Z
. The results, in addition to being informative in themselves, also provide the building blocks for understanding Theorem
2
.

Theorem 5
.

(i)

Conditioning on
\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1\}
, we have
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}=\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
are both asymptotically overconservative.

(ii)

Conditioning on
\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0\}
, we have
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}=\textup{CI}_{*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
with
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
being asymptotically valid. The
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
is asymptotically valid if either
e_{0}=e_{1}=1/2
or
\tau_{i}
’s are constant across
i=1,\ldots,N
, but can be asymptotically anticonservative if otherwise.

Theorem
5
follows from Proposition
2
and clarifies the possible over- and anticonservativeness of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
conditioning on
\phi_{\textsc{m}}
.
Recall that the unconditional coverage rate is essentially a weighted average of the conditional coverage rates given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
and
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
, respectively.
These conditional over- and anticonservativeness thereby elucidate the source of the unconditional over- and anticonservativeness in Theorem
2
.

In particular, Proposition
2
ensures that
\sqrt{N}(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}-\tau)\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
converge in distribution to
v_{\textsc{l}}^{1/2}\epsilon+(v_{\textsc{n}}-v_{\textsc{l}})^{1/2}\mathcal{L}
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
, which has narrower central quantile ranges than
\mathcal{N}(0,v_{\textsc{n}})
unless
v_{\textsc{n}}=v_{\textsc{l}}

(
Li et al. 2018
;
Li and Ding 2020
)
.
This underlies the overconservativeness of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},*}=\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{n}}}
based on normal approximation given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
.

Similarly, Proposition
2
ensures that
\sqrt{N}(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}-\tau)
converges in distribution to
v_{\textsc{l}}^{1/2}\epsilon+(v_{\textsc{f}}-v_{\textsc{l}})^{1/2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
, which has wider central quantile ranges than
\mathcal{N}(0,v_{\textsc{f}})
unless
v_{\textsc{f}}=v_{\textsc{l}}
.
This underlies the possible anticonservativeness of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}=\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{f}}}
based on normal approximation when
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
.
Two exceptions are when
e_{0}=e_{1}=1/2
or
\tau_{i}
’s are constant such that
v_{\textsc{f}}=v_{\textsc{l}}
. The resulting
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}=\textup{CI}_{{\textsc{f}}}
is asymptotically valid given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
.

Intuitively, balanced allocations with
\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})=1
narrow the asymptotic distributions of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}=\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
such that the confidence intervals based on normal approximation overcover.
In contrast, unbalanced allocations with
\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})=0
widen the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}=\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}}
such that the confidence interval based on normal approximation undercovers.
This provides the intuition behind Theorems
2
and
5
.

6.4
Conditional FRT

Recall the setting of the standard
frt
in Section
4
.
The original proposal by
Fisher 1935
computes
p_{\textup{frt}}
by (
7
) using all possible permutations of
Z
.
The conditional
frt
, in contrast, computes the
p
-value using only allocations that are
similar
to
Z
by some prespecified criterion
(
Hennessy et al. 2016
;
Branson and
Miratrix 2019
)
.
Under the definition of similarity by covariate balance test, this means
that we permute
Z
over all possible allocations
\bm{z}
with
\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})=\phi_{\textsc{m}}
, denoted by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\phi_{\textsc{m}})=\{\bm{z}:\phi_{\textsc{m}}(\bm{z})=\phi_{\textsc{m}}\},

and compute the resulting
p
-value as

p_{\textsc{frt}}(\phi_{\textsc{m}})=|\mathcal{Z}(\phi_{\textsc{m}})|^{-1}\sum_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}(\phi_{\textsc{m}})}\mathcal{I}\left\{\big|T(\bm{z},Y,X)\big|\geq\big|T(Z,Y,X)\big|\right\}

(9)

for a two-sided test given the observed value of
\phi_{\textsc{m}}
.
Theorem
6
below evaluates the validity of
p_{\textup{frt}}(\phi_{\textsc{m}})
for testing
H_{0{\textsc{f}}}
and
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
.

Theorem 6
.

(i)

Conditioning on
\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1\}
, we have
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*}=\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{n}}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
are both finite-sample exact for testing
H_{0{\textsc{f}}}
, but neither are asymptotically valid for testing
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
.

(ii)

Conditioning on
\{\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0\}
, we have
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},*}=\hat{\tau}_{*}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{*}\ (*={\textsc{f}},{\textsc{l}})
are both finite-sample exact for testing
H_{0{\textsc{f}}}
, but only
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
is asymptotically valid for testing
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
.

Juxtapose Theorem
6
with Theorem
3
.
The conditional
frt
restores the asymptotic validity of
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
for testing
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
when
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
.
This is intuitively the consequence of
(\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}})=(\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}},\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textsc{l}})
and the asymptotic normality of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
. The
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{f}}}
nevertheless remains invalid for
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
in both cases even asymptotically.
This is intuitively due to the asymptotic non-normality of
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{n}}
and
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}}
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=1
and
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
, respectively. We relegate the details to the Supplementary Material.

This concludes our discussion on the conditional inference based on the preliminary-test procedure. The conditional perspective restores the asymptotic validity of
\textup{CI}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
for interval estimation and
\hat{\tau}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}/\hat{\textup{se}}_{\textup{pt},{\textsc{l}}}
for testing
H_{0{\textsc{n}}}
given
\phi_{\textsc{m}}=0
, yet leaves all other problems with the preliminary-test procedure unsolved.

Discussion

We studied the role of covariate balance in randomized trials and evaluated the properties of the preliminary-test procedure from the randomization-based perspective.
The results discourage the use of preliminary tests for covariate adjustment and corroborate the advantages of regression adjustment by
Lin 2013
’s specification.
We list below some possible extensions for future research.

Importantly, our recommendation of the fully interacted adjustment by
Lin 2013
is based on large sample considerations.
In finite samples, the inclusion of the interactions almost doubles the model complexity compared with the additive adjustment, subjecting subsequent inference to large finite-sample variability.
We refer interested readers to
Zhao and Ding 2023
for a systematic discussion.

8.1
Covariate balance criteria based on statistical tests

We focused on the Mahalanobis distance-based criterion for measuring covariate balance in defining the preliminary-test procedure without variable selection.
We conjecture that the results extend to balance criteria based on significance tests
(
Cochran 1982
;
Permutt 1990
;
Hansen and
Bowers 2008
;
Zhao and Ding 2021b
)
with minimal modifications.
That is, we define the balance criterion based on results from one or more significance tests, and adjust for all covariates by
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{f}}
or
\hat{\tau}_{\textsc{l}}
if the realized allocation fails the criterion.

8.2
Variable selection by preliminary tests based on the outcome model

The discussion in Section
2.3.2
focuses on variable selection based on preliminary tests of covariate balance.
It is a special case of the broader class of statistical procedures that use a test of significance to guide the choice of specification for subsequent analysis
(
Bancroft 1944
)
.
An alternative, arguably more popular, strategy is to select the most prognostic covariates based on the outcome model.

Schelchter and
Forsythe 1985
and
Permutt 1990
conducted simulation studies to compare these outcome model-based selection criteria with those based on covariate balance, and concluded that the outcome model-based criteria often lead to superior performances than their covariate balance-based counterparts.

Raab
et al. 2000
, on the other hand, suggested that the outcome model-based criteria may lead to overestimation of the treatment effect.

Bloniarz
et al. 2016
studied regression adjustment by lasso in high-dimensional settings, which is essentially a variable selection procedure based on the outcome model.
We leave the theoretical comparison of such approaches with their covariate balance-based counterparts to future research.

8.3
Covariate adjustment in nonlinear regression models

We focused on covariate adjustment by linear regression.
Covariate adjustment in nonlinear regression models can be very different
(
Robinson and
Jewell 1991
)
, where even omission of a balanced prognostic covariate can lead to bias
(
Christensen
et al. 1985
;
Chastang
et al. 1988
;
Altman and
Doré 1991
;
Raab 1994
)
.

Beach and
Meier 1989
examined covariate selection with preliminary test in proportional hazards regression model using a simulation study, and concluded that adjustment can often decrease the precision under conditions typical in clinical trials.

Freedman 2008b
and
Guo and Basse 2023
laid down the theoretical basis for randomization-based inference from nonlinear regression.
We leave extensions to the preliminary-test procedure to future research.

8.4
Preliminary test for regression specification

We assumed that the analyzer predetermines to use either
Fisher 1935
’s additive or
Lin 2013
’s fully interacted specification for regression adjustment.
Alternatively, we can use preliminary tests to determine the regression specifications for covariate adjustment. An immediate example is to use data to determine whether a subset or all of the interaction terms can be omitted from the regression specification. Alternatively, we can use preliminary testing to decide whether to use linear or non-linear regression specification.
See also
Bloniarz
et al. 2016
for an example of using lasso to determine data-dependent specification of the mean model.
Such model selection parallels the variable selection discussed in Section
8.2
as a special case of the broader class of statistical procedures that use a test of significance to guide the choice of specification for subsequent analysis
(
Bancroft 1944
)
, and can be performed either independently or in combination with preliminary test of covariate balance.
We leave detailed theory to future research.

8.5
Preliminary tests in observational studies

Cochran 1982
studied the implications of covariate balance-based preliminary tests for covariate adjustment in observational studies, and suggested the practical value of preliminary tests when the number of covariates is large.

Raab 1994
considered two outcome model-based procedures for variable selection in observational studies, and suggested that they are inferior to the always-adjust counterpart in general.
This is coherent with our recommendation for the always-adjust strategy under randomized trials in low-dimensional settings.
In a similar spirit,
Branson 2021
used balance tests to assess if a matched data set should be analyzed as a completely randomized or
rerandomized experiment.
We leave the theory to future research.

In brief, prepivoting first transforms a test statistic

p

\displaystyle p_{\textup{frt}}^{\prime}=|\mathcal{Z}|^{-1}\sum_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{I}\big\{T^{\prime}(\bm{z},Y,X)\geq T^{\prime}(Z,Y,X)\big\}.
