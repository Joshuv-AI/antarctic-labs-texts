Navier-Stokes Equations for Low-Temperature One-Dimensional Fluids Preprint: APS/123-QED
Andrew Urichuk, Stefano Scopa, Jacopo De Nardis

Abstract
We consider one-dimensional interacting quantum fluids, such as the Lieb-Liniger gas. By computing the low-temperature limit of its (generalised) hydrodynamics we show how in this limit the gas is well described by a conventional viscous (Navier–Stokes) hydrodynamics for density, fluid velocity and the local temperature, and the other generalised temperatures in the case of integrable gases. The dynamic viscosity is proportional to temperature and can be expressed in a universal form only in terms of the emergent Luttinger Liquid parameter K K and its density. We show that the heating factor is finite even in the zero temperature limit, which implies that viscous contribution remains relevant also at zero temperatures. Moreover, we find that in the semi-classical limit of small couplings, kinematic viscosity diverges, reconciling with previous observations of Kardar-Parisi-Zhang fluctuations in mean-field quantum fluids.

Viscous CHD.—

We now wish to include diffusive or viscosity effects. In order to do so, we shall consider local thermodynamic states with finite but small temperature, as there are no viscosity effects at strictly zero temperature. We consider therefore local canonical equilibrium states with given density
\rho

\mu

The only way to clarify their role is to plug them within the hydrodynamic equation for the only two modes which are relevant here, i.e. density and momentum (fluid velocity). This already clarifies the essential difference of viscous CHD from Euler GHD: while the latter can be simply obtained as an equation for the evolution of the Fermi edges (

The argument is simple: at low temperatures, all quantum fluids become effectively integrable, as their description is in terms of Luttinger liquid freely propagating bosonic modes. Front dynamics in integrable gases.—

We now focus on the fate of an initial density bump, for example in a system at a given temperature
T_{0}\ll T_{d}
and density
\rho_{0}
. Such a setting is paradigmatic to understanding the response of a system to external perturbations, and we here use it to establish the main differences between viscous CHD and integrable diffusive GHD at low temperature, see Fig.

\gamma_{n}
associated to higher conserved quantities

, i.e. a Generalised Gibbs Ensembles (GGE), deviating this way from the behaviour of a generic non-integrable system. This amounts to replacing the bare energy

with a higher-order polynomial

(13)

in eq. (
5

By extending the result for the temperature field in (

for their expression. The main relevant fact is that while
\partial_{t}T\sim O(T)

Indeed, while GHD displays a growing shock region, viscous CHD converges to a given profile, with a finite front width, and it never develops a shock as temperature is lowered. Namely, even if temperature

On the other hand, observed deviations from viscous CHD are attributed to the absence of higher conservation laws, as discussed above.

The small coupling limit
c\to 0^{+}
and KPZ physics.—

As already discussed in the first Lieb-Liniger paper
Lieb and Liniger 1963
, the limit of small coupling of the Lieb-Liniger gas does not simply recover free bosons. Indeed, when
c\to 0^{+}
, its ground state becomes the one describing the so-called (semi-classical) condensate solution of the Nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS)
Ishikawa and Takayama 1980
. This is characterised by vanishing Fermi momentum
q\sim\sqrt{c}
but with diverging dressed functions
1^{\rm dr}\sim 1/\sqrt{c}
in order to keep the density
\rho
finite in the limit. The relevant excitations become then the Bogoliubov excitations with spectrum given by
\varepsilon_{k}\sim|k|
and therefore with a degenerate group velocity
v_{k}\sim{\rm sgn}(k)
. As
\partial_{\rho}v_{F}(\rho)\sim\lim_{q\to 0}\partial_{q}v_{q}
the latter diverge, giving a divergent dynamic viscosity at low coupling as

\mu(\rho,T)\sim\frac{T}{\sqrt{c}},

(14)

signalling the breakdown of the viscous CHD and the emergence of Kardar-Parisi-Zhang physics
Kardar et al. 1986
. The latter is well-known to emerge in the stochastic Burgers equation, i.e. given a white
\delta-
correlated noise
w
, a local perturbation of a hydrodynamic field
\phi
satisfying

\partial_{t}\phi+\partial_{x}\left(v\phi+\kappa\phi^{2}+w\right)=0,

moves with finite velocity
v
and
spreads super-diffusively
, as opposed to the diffusive case of eq. (
Navier-Stokes Equations for Low-Temperature One-Dimensional Fluids
) whenever
\mu
is finite. Such a phenomenon is known to appear in generic finite-components one-dimensional fluids, as described by the non-linear fluctuating hydrodynamic (NLFH) theory
Spohn 2014
, which can be successfully applied also to lattice (i.e. non-integrable) NLS
Mendl and Spohn 2015
. Again, the argument is simple: whenever the Euler currents contain non-linearities, one should expect that the introduction of a small noise (which for example can describe the interaction with other non-hydrodynamic modes) always leads to the KPZ universal fixed point. However, this is not the case at finite coupling since, although it is true that eq. (
Navier-Stokes Equations for Low-Temperature One-Dimensional Fluids
) contains the non-linearity
\eta^{2}
, there exists a continuous, thermally activated, spectrum of modes around the Fermi points with velocities
v_{F}\pm\delta v(\theta)
(with
\delta v(\theta)\ll v_{F}
peaked at
\theta=\theta^{\sigma}
) that are responsible for a finite diffusion constant in the system. It is only in the small coupling limit
c\to 0^{+}
that the velocities of all such excitations become degenerate, therefore diminishing the number of effective hydrodynamic modes to only the three macroscopic ones. In this limit, therefore, the theory of NLFH applies and KPZ physics emerges, as signalled by the divergent dynamic viscosity. We should stress that the existence of KPZ physics in the NLS at low temperature was first established in
Kulkarni and Lamacraft 2013
and we here give a first analytical proof of its divergent diffusion constant. Moreover, it is interesting to notice that the degeneracy of the hydrodynamic modes leads to super-diffusive KPZ physics similarly also in the Heisenberg spin chain (and any other integrable model with non-abelian symmetry)
Ljubotina et al. 2019
;
Bulchandani et al. 2021
at finite temperatures. There the relevant degenerate excitations are not the ones around the Fermi points but the so-called giant magnons
De Nardis et al. 2020c
;
Nardis et al. 2022b
, namely magnonic excitations with large spin and vanishing velocities.

Conclusion.—

We have here shown that the standard Navier-Stokes equations for the evolution of density, momentum and temperature can be derived from the low-temperature expansion of the GHD for the Lieb-Liniger gas. We have found universal expressions for the linear part in temperature of the dynamic viscosity, which we conjecture to apply to generic one-dimensional fluids. We have shown that the viscosity is zero in the free fermionic limit, as expected, and that it diverges in the semi-classical limit of weakly interacting bosons at small temperatures, which despite many numerical works, it was never established from first principles. The divergent viscosity signals the emergence of KPZ super-diffusive spreading
Kulkarni and Lamacraft 2013
, in analogy to the one observed in integrable spin chains
Wei et al. 2022
.

We have shown that the viscous terms regularise hydrodynamic instabilities in one-dimensional gases, although the inclusion of generalised temperatures is necessary in order to predict the full form of the shock front in the integrable gas.
Moreover, one should also expect that when the system is strongly out of equilibrium and thus gradients of
\eta
become large at the shocks points, the system strongly heats locally, invalidating, therefore, the zero-entropy approximation. Our findings therefore suggest that zero-entropy hydrodynamics becomes harder to physically realise whenever interactions are present.

Differently from previous attempts to derive viscosity in quantum fluids by perturbative corrections to Luttinger liquids, see for example
Sirker et al. 2009
;
Matveev and Pustilnik 2017
;
Maki and Zhang 2023
;
DeGottardi and Matveev 2020
, we here derive non-perturbatively using the generalised hydrodynamic of a specific model, the Lieb-Liniger gas, and we extend our result to generic systems, given the universality of its formulation. Clearly, a different derivation only involving Luttinger liquid modes would also be desirable in the future. Our result is ready to be checked by means of numerical simulations
Eberharter et al. 2023
;
Vanderstraeten et al. 2018
;
Van Damme et al. 2021
and to apply to different quantum fluids as such as chiral edge modes
Wen 1990
;
Wen 1992
;
Nardin and Carusotto 2023
, to open the way for a full fluctuating hydrodynamic theory
Delacretaz and Mishra 2023
of Luttinger liquid field theories, and to unveil Burgers-like turbulent phases
Gallone et al. 2022
in low-dimensional quantum fluids.

A.2

From the the definition of the thermal energy it is possible to identify the derivative of the thermal energy with the dressed energy derivative. For Lieb-Liniger with symmetric Fermi points this explicitly reads as

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}\epsilon=\theta+\int d\alpha\,\varphi(\theta-\alpha)n(\alpha)\partial_{\theta}\epsilon(\alpha)=\theta+\int d\alpha\,\varphi(\theta-\alpha)n(\alpha){\theta}^{\rm dr}(\alpha)=:{\theta}^{\rm dr}(\theta).

\displaystyle{f}^{\rm dr}(\theta)=f(\theta)+\int d\alpha\,\varphi(\theta-\alpha)n(\alpha){f}^{\rm dr}(\alpha).

\displaystyle\int d\theta{f}^{\rm dr}(\theta)n(\theta)g(\theta)=\int d\theta f(\theta)n(\theta){g}^{\rm dr}(\theta).

It is also possible to dress multivariate functions like the scattering kernel

(SA.9)

with a note that once again a left or right dressing are exactly equivalent.

(SA.10)

which are characterized by these dressed functions.

\displaystyle{f}^{\rm dr}(\theta-\eta)={f}^{\rm dr}_{s}(\theta)={(f(\theta-\eta))}^{\rm dr}.

\displaystyle{\theta}^{\rm dr}(\theta-\eta)

\displaystyle{(\theta-\eta)}^{\rm dr}={\theta}^{\rm dr}(\theta)-\eta{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta).

Furthermore, the Fermi velocity can be determined by evaluating the effective velocity at the symmetric Fermi point

\displaystyle v^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\pm})=\frac{{\theta}^{\rm dr}(\theta^{\pm})}{{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta^{\pm})}=\frac{{\theta}^{\rm dr}(\pm q)}{{1}^{\rm dr}(\pm q)}+\eta=\pm v_{F}+\eta.

Appendix B
Zero temperature Euler GHD and conventional hydrodynamics (CHD)

Generalized hydrodynamics (GHD) determines the time evolution of conserved charges
q_{i}
, which at the Euler scale involves evolution equations of the form

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle Q_{i}\rangle+\partial_{x}\langle J_{i}\rangle=\partial_{t}\int d\theta(q_{i}n{1}^{\rm dr})+\partial_{x}\int d\theta(q_{i}v^{\rm eff}n{1}^{\rm dr}).

(SB.14)

With the subscript ’
i
’ denoting the index of the conserved charge, the first few of which are density,
q_{0}=1
, momentum
q_{1}=\theta
, and energy
q_{2}=\theta^{2}/2
. What distinguishes GHD from conventional hydrodynamics (CHD) is the presence of an infinite number of conserved charges, our goal here is to demonstrate that at
T=0
the Euler scale GHD reduces to CHD. This is a well known result, however it should be noted that this result implies the presence of shocks at non-zero temperatures. A similar procedure for truncating the GHD equations at finite but low-temperature will then be necessary for introducing a diffusive correction to regularize these shocks.

B.1
Euler CHD equations

From GHD the CHD equations can be understood as demonstrated as emerging from a truncation of independent evolution equations, which occurs in the zero temperature limit of GHD. At the Euler scale the first three modes for GHD equations read

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int d\theta(n{1}^{\rm dr})+\partial_{x}\int d\theta\,v^{\rm eff}(n{1}^{\rm dr})=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p+\partial_{x}\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int d\theta\theta(n{1}^{\rm dr})+\partial_{x}\int d\theta\,\theta v^{\rm eff}(n{1}^{\rm dr})=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E+\partial_{x}J_{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int d\theta\theta^{2}(n{1}^{\rm dr})+\partial_{x}\int d\theta\,\theta^{2}v^{\rm eff}(n{1}^{\rm dr})=0.

(SB.15)

It should be noted that when the, generally, infinite number of evolution equations in GHD truncate to a finite number the result is some form of CHD.

A more conventional form of CHD can be written by noting

By use of their definition from TBA, the hydrodynamic fields for energy and energy current satisfy, where
i=t,x

\displaystyle\partial_{i}E

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\rho\partial_{i}\eta+\frac{\partial_{i}q}{v_{F}}(\mathcal{P}+E-\eta^{2}\rho),

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}+E)\partial_{i}\eta+\eta v_{F}^{2}\partial_{i}\rho-\eta^{2}\rho\partial_{i}\eta+\eta\partial_{i}E,

with the Fermi velocity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\partial_{t}E+\partial_{x}J_{E}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}e+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{s}}{\rho}\partial_{x}\eta+\eta\partial_{x}e+\frac{e}{\rho}\left(\partial_{t}\rho+\eta\partial_{x}\rho+\rho\partial_{x}\eta\right)+\eta(\partial_{t}\eta+\eta\partial_{x}\eta+v_{F}^{2}\partial_{x}\rho).

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}(\eta\rho)

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle 0.

(SB.20)

To prove the equivalence of GHD and CHD it must be demonstrated that GHD truncates so that these relations, or a subset thereof, are sufficient for determining the dynamics.

The second term picking up an overall ‘

By combining these derivative identities for the symmetric function and velocity the low-temperature diffusion can be evaluated to be

Appendix D
Equation for the energy density

At the Euler scale, the low-temperature energy evolution equation was found to be a restatement of the density and momentum relations. By adding diffusion this is no longer the case, due to the presence of the temperature field in the density and momentum equations. Since the diffusion is an
O(T)
effect, there is no avoiding the temperature field. A standard calculation akin to that done in the Euler case, but now retaining the diffusive effects, then implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\partial_{t}E+\partial_{x}J_{E}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}e+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{s}}{\rho}\partial_{x}\eta+\eta\partial_{x}e+0+\frac{\eta}{\rho}D(p)=\frac{1}{\rho}D(E).

(SD.46)

To close our diffusive equations at low-temperature there are several steps. First, the diffusive term is evaluated, as it was for the momentum. With the low-temperature energy diffusion characterized, the energy field can be recast in terms of a temperature field, which closes the set of equations. The density, momentum, and temperature evolution equations are then brought together and collectively understood as constituting low-temperature viscous GHD.

The diffusive correction to the energy expression is then computed here, where for convenience of notation we introduce
v_{E}=\frac{{(\theta_{s}^{2})}^{\rm dr}}{2({1}^{\rm dr})}
with the note that

\displaystyle D(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\int\frac{d\theta d\mu}{2}\frac{{(\theta^{2})}^{\rm dr}(\theta)}{2}({1}^{\rm dr}(\theta))\tilde{D}(\theta,\mu)\partial_{x}n(\mu)=\partial_{x}\int\frac{d\theta d\mu}{2}\left(v_{E}(\theta)+\eta v^{\rm eff}(\theta)+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\right)({1}^{\rm dr}(\theta))^{2}\tilde{D}(\theta,\mu)\partial_{x}n(\mu),

(SD.47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{v_{E}}+\mu(\rho,\beta)(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}+\eta D(p)+\eta^{2}D(\rho).

Only the first term
D_{v_{E}}
is new, the viscosity in the second term has been calculated already, see (
SC.45
), the third term is exactly the diffusive correction to momentum, and the third term the diffusive correction to density, which vanishes. Recall now the definition of the anti-symmetric function
h(\theta,\mu)

\displaystyle h(\theta,\mu)=2\pi\frac{({1}^{\rm dr}(\theta){1}^{\rm dr}(\mu))({\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\theta,\mu))^{2}}{\beta}\frac{(v^{\rm eff}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}(\mu))^{3}}{|v^{\rm eff}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}(\mu)|},

(SD.48)

which is helpful for simplifying the calculation.
With the antisymmetric function and noting that
v_{E}(q)=v_{E}(-q)
the diffusive contribution
D_{v_{E}}
, after applying the spatial derivative, is given by

\displaystyle D_{v_{E}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\theta d\mu}{4\beta}\left(\frac{\partial_{\theta}n}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\frac{\partial_{\mu}n}{\partial_{\mu}\epsilon}\partial_{x}(h(\theta,\mu)(v_{E}(\theta)-v_{E}(\mu))\partial_{x}\eta)+\right.

(SD.49)

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial_{\theta}n}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\frac{\partial_{\mu}n}{\partial_{\mu}\epsilon}(\partial_{x}q)(\partial_{x}\eta)\left(\partial_{\theta}\frac{h(\theta,\mu)(v_{E}(\theta)-v_{E}(\mu))}{v^{\rm eff}(\theta)}+\partial_{\mu}\frac{h(\theta,\mu)(v_{E}(\theta)-v_{E}(\mu))}{v^{\rm eff}(\mu)}\right),

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\partial_{\theta}n}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\frac{\partial_{\mu}n}{\partial_{\mu}\epsilon}(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}\left[\partial_{\theta}[h(\theta,\mu)(v_{E}(\theta)-v_{E}(\mu))]+\partial_{\mu}[h(\theta,\mu)(v_{E}(\theta)-v_{E}(\mu)]\right]\right).

Note that we have dropped the terms proportional to
\partial_{x}\beta
, since these terms contain
O((v_{E}(\theta)-v_{E}(\mu))^{2})
and so will always vanish when
\theta,\mu\to q,-q
.
The other terms can be determined as

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\theta d\mu}{4\beta}\frac{\partial_{\theta}n}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\frac{\partial_{\mu}n}{\partial_{\mu}\epsilon}\left(\partial_{\theta}\frac{h(\theta,\mu)(v_{E}(\theta)-v_{E}(\mu))}{v^{\rm eff}(\theta)}+\partial_{\mu}\frac{h(\theta,\mu)(v_{E}(\theta)-v_{E}(\mu))}{v^{\rm eff}(\mu)}\right)=0.

(SD.50)

Along with the second term contributing

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\theta d\mu}{4\beta}\frac{\partial_{\theta}n}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\frac{\partial_{\mu}n}{\partial_{\mu}\epsilon}(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}\left[(\partial_{\theta}+\partial_{\mu})[h(\theta,\mu)(v_{E}(\theta)-v_{E}(\mu)]\right]=\frac{16\pi v_{F}}{\beta}({\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q))^{2}(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}.

Likewise, the only surviving part of the first term is the spatial derivative of
v_{E}
. By use of dressed function identities it then follows that
the first term can be written as

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\theta d\mu}{4\beta}\frac{\partial_{\theta}n}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\frac{\partial_{\mu}n}{\partial_{\mu}\epsilon}\partial_{x}(h(\theta,\mu)(v_{E}(\theta)-v_{E}(\mu))\partial_{x}\eta)=-\frac{16\pi v_{F}}{\beta}({\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q))^{2}(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}.

(SD.52)

Taken together Eqs. (
D
) and (
SD.52
) imply the diffusive contribution from
D_{v_{E}}
vanishes

\displaystyle D_{v_{E}}=\partial_{x}\int d\theta d\mu\,v_{E}(\theta)({1}^{\rm dr}(\theta))^{2}\tilde{D}(\theta,\mu)\partial_{x}n(\mu)=0.

(SD.53)

Consequently, the total energy diffusion is given by

\displaystyle D(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{v_{E}}+(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}\left(\frac{8\pi}{\beta}({\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q))^{2}v^{\rm eff}(q)\right)+\eta D(p)+\eta^{2}D_{\rho},

(SD.54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}\left(\frac{8\pi}{\beta}({\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q))^{2}v^{\rm eff}(q)\right)+\eta D(p).

Finally, this is combined with the energy evolution equation, which after some simplification reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}e+\eta\partial_{x}e+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{s}}{\rho}\partial_{x}\eta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{\rho}(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}

(SD.55)

D.1
Thermal Conductivity

First consider the form of the GHD equation (restricted to two Fermi points). As we have already seen, the hydrodynamic equations can be written in the boosted frame.
So here Eq. (
SD.47
) is replicated for convenience

\displaystyle\partial_{t}e+\eta\partial_{x}e+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{s}}{\rho}\partial_{x}\eta=\frac{D_{v_{E}}+\mu(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}}{\rho},

(SD.56)

with the reminder that
e=E/\rho
. The thermal conductivity will be proportional to
\partial_{x}\beta
type terms, hence must come from a higher order temperature correction to the boosted diffusion
D_{v_{E}}
. The explicit form of this diffusion kernel is recalled as

\displaystyle D_{v_{E}}\sim\partial_{x}\left(\int d\theta d\alpha\,\frac{\pi{(\theta^{2}_{s})}^{\rm dr}(\theta)}{2\beta^{2}{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta)}\frac{\partial_{\theta}n}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\frac{\partial_{\alpha}n}{\partial_{\alpha}\epsilon}\frac{(v(\theta)-v(\alpha))^{2}}{|v(\theta)-v(\alpha)|}{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta){1}^{\rm dr}(\alpha)({\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\theta,\alpha))^{2}\left(\frac{\partial_{x}(\beta\epsilon(\alpha))}{{1}^{\rm dr}(\alpha)}-\frac{\partial_{x}(\beta\epsilon(\theta))}{{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta)}\right)\right).

To determine the low temperature thermal conductivity
\kappa(\rho,T)
we note that the term must appear as a higher order temperature correction to the energy diffusion, hence will be the first surviving term proportional to
\partial_{x}\beta
. With this in mind it is furthermore convenient to note that the low temperature thermal energy is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon(\theta)}{{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta)}\sim\frac{{(\theta^{2})}^{\rm dr}(\theta)}{2\,{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta)}-\mu.

(SD.58)

With the additional note that
\mu
is the local chemical potential, and hence constant so that it vanishes in the difference. This relation allows us to rewrite the part of the energy diffusion kernel containing the thermal conductivity as

\displaystyle D_{v_{E}}\overset{{O(\partial_{x}\beta)}}{\sim}\partial_{x}\left(\frac{\pi\partial_{x}\beta}{2\beta^{2}}\int d\theta d\alpha\,\frac{\partial_{\theta}n}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\frac{\partial_{\alpha}n}{\partial_{\alpha}\epsilon}\frac{(v(\theta)-v(\alpha))^{2}}{|v(\theta)-v(\alpha)|}{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta){1}^{\rm dr}(\alpha)({\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\theta,\alpha))^{2}\left(\frac{\epsilon(\alpha)}{{1}^{\rm dr}(\alpha)}-\frac{\epsilon(\theta)}{{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta)}\right)^{2}\right)

(SD.59)

Similarly to what we’ve previously encountered the relevant contributions are those at the Fermi points, which vanish at leading order as can be checked by noting. The corrections are implemented by
{f}^{\rm dr}(q_{T})={f}^{\rm dr}(q)+\frac{\delta{f}^{\rm dr}}{\delta q_{T}}\bigg|_{q}(q_{T}-q)
, along with both
\epsilon(q_{T})=0
and the small temperature Fermi point corrections found in Eq. (
SG.127
) and reproduced here as

\displaystyle 2(q_{T}-q)=-\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3{\theta}^{\rm dr}v_{F}}\left(2V(q)+\left[\frac{1}{v_{F}(q)}-2U(q)\right]\right),

(SD.60)

where
V(\theta)={\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\theta,q)-{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\theta,-q)
and
U(\theta)={\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\theta,q)+{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\theta,-q)
. The variation of
\epsilon/{1}^{\rm dr}
is readily determined from the zero temperature identity in Eq. (
SG.158
) as being

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{\delta q}\left(\frac{\epsilon(q)}{{1}^{\rm dr}(q)}\right)=\frac{\delta v_{E}}{\delta q}\bigg|_{q}(q_{T}-q)=v(q)(q_{T}-q).

(SD.61)

Combined with the Fermi point expression the

\displaystyle D_{v_{E}}\overset{O(\partial_{x}T)}{\sim}\partial_{x}\left[\frac{4v_{F}\pi^{5}T^{4}\partial_{x}T}{9({\theta}^{\rm dr}v_{F})^{2}}({\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q))^{2}\left(\frac{1-4v_{F}{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q)}{2v_{F}}\right)^{2}\right].

(SD.62)

To cast this in terms of the Luttinger liquid parameter
K=({1}^{\rm dr}(q))^{2}
, the identities
2K\partial_{\rho}v_{F}=(1-4{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q)v_{F})
and the expression for viscosity
\mu(\rho,T)
from Eq. (
SG.111
) are utilized. It follows that the thermal conductivity is given by

\displaystyle\kappa(\rho,\eta,T)\partial_{x}T=\frac{4\pi^{5}T^{4}\partial_{x}T}{9}v_{F}K^{3}\left(\frac{(\partial_{\rho}v_{F})(\partial_{\rho}K)}{\rho v_{F}}\right)^{2}

(SD.63)

In the notation with
\rho=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\theta\,{1}^{\rm dr}(\theta)n
this expression is equivalently given by

\displaystyle\kappa(\rho,\eta,T)\partial_{x}T=\frac{T^{4}\partial_{x}T}{32}\frac{K\mu(\rho,T)}{T}\left(\partial_{\rho}(\tilde{\chi}_{e}K)\right)^{2}.

(SD.64)

This can also be written in terms of
\gamma
finding that

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa(\gamma,\eta,T)}{c}\frac{\partial_{x}T}{c^{2}}=\frac{\tau^{3}\mu(\gamma,\tau)K\gamma^{4}}{32}[\partial_{\gamma}(\tilde{\chi}_{e}(\gamma)K)]^{2}\,\partial_{x}\tau.

(SD.65)

With additional simplifications in the final expression involving the observation that
\tilde{\chi}_{e}K=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3v_{F}^{2}}

This thermal conductivity is now combined with the rest of the hydrodynamic results to find

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}T+\eta\partial_{x}T\right)=-\frac{\mathcal{P}_{s}}{\rho T\chi_{e}}\partial_{x}\eta+(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}\frac{\mu+O(T^{3})}{\rho T\tilde{\chi}_{e}}+\frac{\partial_{x}(\kappa\partial_{x}T)}{\rho T\tilde{\chi}_{e}}.

(SD.66)

It should be noted that the thermal conductivity is subleading,
\kappa\sim O(T^{4})
, hence competes with other subleading corrections to the viscosity, pressure, and energy susceptibility. As a consequence, although physically significant, the thermal conductivity is not expected to be relevant in the low temperature limit.

Appendix E
Dynamics of the temperature and inclusion of other conserved quantities

Our goal here is to compute the evolution of the temperature and also to consider the impact of additional Lagrange parameters (temperatures) that are allowed to evolve at low temperatures in the integrable gas. This amounts to redefining the thermal energy and so obtain a more general local filling function. The simplest example is the case of two additional conserved quantities,
Q_{3}
and
Q_{4}

\displaystyle\beta\epsilon=\beta\left(\frac{(\theta-\eta)^{2}}{2}-\mu+\gamma_{3}\frac{(\theta-\eta)^{3}}{3!}+\gamma_{4}\frac{(\theta-\eta)^{4}}{4!}\right)+\int d\alpha\,\varphi(\theta-\alpha)\ln\left|1+\text{e}^{-\beta\epsilon}\right|.

(SE.67)

To begin only those temperatures that lead to an even Fermi weight will be considered, which will then be generalized to the anti-symmetric case.

Consider the general hydrodynamic charges corresponding to these generalized temperatures, which are explicitly given by

\displaystyle\frac{\langle Q_{2}\rangle}{\rho}=e-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\,,

\displaystyle\rho\langle e\rangle=\int d\theta\frac{(\theta^{2})^{\rm dr}}{2}n(\theta)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\langle Q_{3}\rangle}{\rho}=\langle q_{3}\rangle-\eta\langle q_{2}\rangle-\frac{\eta^{3}}{3!}\,,

\displaystyle\rho\langle q_{3}\rangle=\int d\theta\frac{(\theta^{3})^{\rm dr}}{3!}n(\theta)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\langle Q_{4}\rangle}{\rho}=\langle q_{4}\rangle-{\eta}\langle q_{3}\rangle-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\langle q_{2}\rangle-\frac{\eta^{4}}{4!}\,,

(SE.68)

Likewise, the corresponding diffusive terms are characterized by generalized viscosities

\displaystyle=

where

and noting that the only nontrivial viscosity terms are odd. The set of hydrodynamic equations for symmetric Fermi weights with an additional charge is given by

\displaystyle 0,

E.1
Derivation of the evolution of charges, eq. (
SE.80
)

To prove the general form of the evolution equations the idea is to apply the relevant shifts to the conserved charges, so that they are being considered in the symmetric basis. These shifts are expanded and so the evolution equation for each charge field can be written in terms of a sum over evolution equation of conserved charges in the symmetric basis. These sums are rearranged and noted to vanish term-wise, which yields the general form of the evolution equation whenever the Fermi weight is symmetric. The diffusive case is then considered, which requires the addition of an additional sum that is treated analogously to the Euler case. Finally, the symmetric condition for the Fermi weight is relaxed and the fully general expression is obtained.

In general the hydrodynamic evolution equations can be written with the notation
\rho\langle q_{n}\rangle=\int d\theta\frac{\theta^{n}}{n!}n(\theta)({1}^{\rm dr})
and
\rho\langle j_{n}\rangle=\mathcal{P}_{n}=\int d\theta\,\frac{\theta^{n}}{n!}({\theta}^{\rm dr})n(\theta)
. The density and
\eta
fields are identified as

\displaystyle\rho=\int d\theta\,{1}^{\rm dr}n,

\displaystyle\rho\eta=\int d\theta\,{\theta}^{\rm dr}n\theta,

(SE.84)

this choice of
\eta
fixes
\langle q_{1}\rangle=0
. These expression are used to rewrite the continuity equation as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\int d\theta(\theta+\eta)^{n}n{1}^{\rm dr}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left(\int d\theta(\theta+\eta)^{n}n{(\theta+\eta)}^{\rm dr}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n^{\underline{k}}}{n!}\left(\partial_{t}\left(\rho\eta^{n-k}\langle q_{k}\rangle\right)+\partial_{x}\left[\rho\eta^{n-k+1}\langle q_{k}\rangle+\rho\eta^{n-k}\langle j_{k}\rangle\right]\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n^{\underline{k}}}{n!}\left(\eta^{n-k}\partial_{t}(\rho\langle q_{k}\rangle)+(n-k)\langle q_{k}\rangle\eta^{n-k-1}\rho\partial_{t}\eta\right.+\eta^{n-k}\partial_{x}(\rho\eta\langle q_{k}\rangle)

\displaystyle+(n-k)\rho\langle q_{k}\rangle\eta^{n-k}\partial_{x}\eta+\left.\eta^{n-k}\partial_{x}(\rho\langle j_{k}\rangle)+(n-k)\rho\langle j_{k}\rangle\eta^{n-k-1}\partial_{x}\eta\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\left(\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}(\eta\rho)\right)+\frac{n^{\underline{n+1}}}{n!\eta}\left(\langle q_{n}\rangle\rho(\partial_{t}\eta+\eta\partial_{x}\eta)+\rho\langle j_{n}\rangle\partial_{x}\eta\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n^{\underline{k}}}{n!}\eta^{n-k}\left(\partial_{t}(\rho\langle q_{k}\rangle)+\partial_{x}(\rho\eta\langle q_{k}\rangle)+\partial_{x}(\rho\langle j_{k}\rangle)+\langle q_{k-1}\rangle\rho(\partial_{t}\eta+\eta\partial_{x}\eta)+\rho\langle j_{k-1}\rangle\partial_{x}\eta\right)

with the dropping power

, such that
n^{\underline{n+1}}=0
. These terms must vanish term by term and so yields the conservation equations for the general evolution equations, written with
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\rho\langle j_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}(\rho\eta),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\eta+\eta\partial_{x}\eta+\frac{\partial_{x}\mathcal{P}_{1}}{\rho},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle q_{k}\rangle+\eta\partial_{x}\langle q_{k}\rangle-\frac{\langle q_{k-1}\rangle}{\rho}\partial_{x}\mathcal{P}_{1}+\frac{1}{\rho}\partial_{x}\mathcal{P}_{k}+\frac{\mathcal{P}_{k-1}}{\rho}\partial_{x}\eta.

(SE.86)

When the Fermi weight is symmetric all odd,
n=2k-1
, charges
\langle q_{2k-1}\rangle=0
and all even,
n=2k
, currents
\langle j_{2k}\rangle=0
this reduces the general formula to the hydrodynamic equations for symmetric Fermi weights.

Now the diffusive corrections can be inserted and treated in a closely analogous fashion. Insert the diffusive correction

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\int d\theta(\theta+\eta)^{n}n{1}^{\rm dr}\right)+\partial_{x}\left(\int d\theta(\theta+\eta)^{n}n{(\theta+\eta)}^{\rm dr}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\int d\theta d\alpha\,\frac{(\theta+\eta)^{n}}{n!}D(\theta,\alpha)\partial_{x}(n{1}^{\rm dr})(\alpha)

(SE.87)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\int d\theta\,\frac{(\theta+\eta)^{n}}{n!}\hat{\mu}(\theta)\partial_{x}\eta,

where the

Our protocol is as follows: two Gaussian fields are introduced, the potential
\mu(x)=1+\mu_{0}\text{e}^{-\nu x^{2}}
and shifts in the rapidity given by
\eta(x)=\eta_{0}\text{e}^{-\sigma x^{2}}
. These two fields are used to determine the filling function by use of the thermal energy

\displaystyle\beta\epsilon(\theta,x)=\beta\frac{(\theta-\eta(x))^{2}}{2}-\mu(x)-\int d\alpha\,\varphi(\theta,\alpha)\ln\left|1+\text{e}^{-\beta\epsilon(\alpha,x)}\right|.

(SG.112)

Once the filling function is determined the full diffusion kernel is considered and applied to the bare charge to determine the general diffusion. The filling function is also used to determine the hydrodynamic density
\rho
and the Fermi points
\pm q
, which are then used to determine the Fermi velocity
v_{F}=v^{\rm eff}(q)
and dressed kernel
{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q)
thus determining our proposed viscosity. Comparing both methods leads to identical results, see fig.
S1
.

G.3
Proof of the low-temperature expansion of eq. (
SC.39
)

The goal of this section is to prove the identity

\displaystyle\beta(2n-1)\partial_{\theta}n\to-\sum_{\sigma}\frac{\sigma}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon(\theta^{\sigma})}\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma}).

(SG.115)

Consider the following integral with a smooth function
f(\theta)
. Expansion of the arguments will allow us to apply well known integral approximation methods to evaluate the result

\displaystyle\int d\theta f(\theta)\beta(2n(\theta)-1)\partial_{\theta}n=-\int d\theta\,f(\theta)\beta^{2}\frac{(1-\text{e}^{\beta\epsilon(\theta)})\text{e}^{\beta\epsilon}\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta)}{(1+\text{e}^{\beta\epsilon(\theta)})^{3}},

(SG.116)

It is key that there are Fermi points that can be identified, specifically
\epsilon(\theta^{\pm})=0
, such that expanding around these points the thermal energy may be rewritten as

\displaystyle\beta\epsilon(\theta)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\beta\epsilon(\theta^{\pm})+\beta\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{\pm})(\theta-\theta^{\pm)}+\frac{\beta}{2}\epsilon^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{\pm})(\theta-\theta^{\pm})^{2}+O((\theta-\theta^{\pm})^{2}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0+s+\frac{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{\pm})}{2\beta(\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{\pm}))^{2}}s^{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{4}}\right),

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{\pm})+\epsilon^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{\pm})(\theta-\theta^{\pm})+O((\theta-\theta^{\pm})^{2}).

(SG.117)

Transformation of the integration measure to our new measure
s
is then given by

\displaystyle s=\beta\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{\pm})(\theta-\theta^{\pm})

\displaystyle ds=\beta\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{\pm})d\theta.

(SG.118)

So that the integral under consideration is now given, in terms of these variables
s
. For concreteness consider the upper half of the integral, such that
\theta^{+}
is expanded around. Furthermore, introduce
\Lambda=\epsilon^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{+})/(2(\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{+}))^{2})
and note that
\theta^{+}>\theta^{-}
then the integral can be approximated as

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{(\theta^{+}+\theta^{-})/2}^{\infty}d\theta\,f(\theta)\beta^{2}\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta)\frac{(1-\text{e}^{\beta\epsilon(\theta)})\text{e}^{\beta\epsilon}}{(1+\text{e}^{\beta\epsilon(\theta)})^{3}}\sim-\int^{\infty}_{\beta\epsilon(\theta^{+})(\theta^{-}-\theta^{+})/2}ds\,f\left(\frac{s}{\beta\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{\pm})}+\theta^{+}\right)\partial_{s}\left(\frac{\text{e}^{s+s^{2}\frac{\Lambda}{\beta}}}{(1+\text{e}^{s+s^{2}\frac{\Lambda}{\beta}})^{2}}\right),

(SG.119)

\displaystyle\sim-\beta\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}ds\,\left(f\left(\theta^{+}\right)+\frac{s}{\beta\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{\pm})}f^{\prime}\left(\theta^{+}\right)\right)\partial_{s}\left(\frac{\text{e}^{s}}{(1+\text{e}^{s})^{2}}+\frac{\Lambda}{\beta}\frac{\text{e}^{s}(1-\text{e}^{s})s^{2}}{(1+\text{e}^{s})^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\right)\right).

Then to leading order as
\beta\to\infty
the integral yields

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{\frac{\theta^{+}+\theta^{-}}{2}}d\theta f(\theta)\beta(2n(\theta)-1)\partial_{\theta}n

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f(\theta^{+})\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}ds\,\beta\partial_{s}\frac{\text{e}^{s}}{(1+\text{e}^{s})^{2}}-\frac{f^{\prime}(\theta^{+})}{\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{+})}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}ds\,s\partial_{s}\frac{\text{e}^{s}}{(1+\text{e}^{s})^{2}}

(SG.120)

\displaystyle+f(\theta^{+})\Lambda\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{+})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{\text{e}^{s}(1-\text{e}^{s})s^{2}}{(1+\text{e}^{s})^{3}},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\theta^{+})\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{+})}.

The first term vanishes on integration, likewise the third term is also vanishing after integration by parts leaving only the second term. The procedure is identical with the left Fermi points, albeit with an additional sign flip and so it follows that

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\theta f(\theta)\beta(2n(\theta)-1)\partial_{\theta}n=\left(f^{\prime}(\theta^{+})\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{+})}-f^{\prime}(\theta^{-})\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\prime}(\theta^{-})}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\right).

(SG.121)

Thus identify the final result with the identity (
SC.39
)

\displaystyle\beta(2n-1)\partial_{\theta}n=-\sum_{\sigma}\frac{\sigma}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon(\theta^{\sigma})}\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma}).

(SG.122)

G.4
Specific heat at low temperatures

The low-temperature expansion for the specific heat
\tilde{\chi}_{e}
can be computed by standard methods

\tilde{\chi}_{e}=\frac{1}{\rho}\lim_{T\to 0}T^{-1}\left(\frac{dE(\rho,T)}{dT}\right)\bigg|_{\rho={\rm const}}.

(SG.123)

This will be obtained by determining the low-temperature energy at constant density. Such a quantity is obtained by expanding first around a constant chemical potential, then introducing a changing chemical potential correction that keeps
\rho
constant. By use of the general formula for a conserved charge (
SG.145
) at constant chemical potential the low-temperature energy is given in terms of
U(q)={\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q)+{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,q)
and

\displaystyle\int d\theta n(\theta)\left(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\right)^{\rm dr}

\displaystyle=\int_{-q(0)}^{q(0)}{\left[\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\right]}^{\rm dr}_{T=0}+\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3(\epsilon^{\prime}(q))^{2}}\left(\frac{({\theta}^{\rm dr})^{2}}{v_{F}}-2({1}^{\rm dr})V{\left[\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\right]}^{\rm dr}-2({1}^{\rm dr})\left(\frac{1}{v_{F}}-2U\right){\left[\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\right]}^{\rm dr}\right)(q).

(SG.124)

To keep density constant the zero temperature Fermi points
q(0)
must be changed, such that the chemical potential is allowed to vary. Consider a constant density
\rho_{\rm ref}=\rho(q_{B})
and a second low-temperature density at constant chemical potential
\rho(q(T))
. The low-temperature corrections to density are known, see App.
G.5
, to be

\displaystyle\rho(q(T))=\rho(q(0))-T^{2}f(q)=\rho(q(0))-\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3{\theta}^{\rm dr}v_{F}}\left(2V(q)({1}^{\rm dr}(q))+\left[\frac{1}{v_{F}}-2U(q)\right]({1}^{\rm dr}(q))\right).

(SG.125)

By imposing
\rho(q(T))=\rho(q_{B})
the condition of constant density requires that

\displaystyle\rho(q(T))=\rho(q(0))-T^{2}f(q)=\rho(q(0))+2({1}^{\rm dr}(q(0)))(q_{B}-q(0)).

(SG.126)

Consequently the Fermi points must satisfy

\displaystyle 2(q_{B}-q(0))=-\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3{\theta}^{\rm dr}v_{F}}\left(2V(q)+\left[\frac{1}{v_{F}}-2U(q)\right]\right),

(SG.127)

to keep the density constant. Now inserting
q(0)=q_{B}+T^{2}f/2
the low-temperature correction to energy with constant density is determined to be

\displaystyle\rho e(\rho,T)

\displaystyle=\rho e(\rho,0)+\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3{\theta}^{\rm dr}v_{F}}\left(2V(q)+\left[\frac{1}{v_{F}}-2U(q)\right]\right){\left[\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\right]}^{\rm dr},

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3(\epsilon^{\prime}(q))^{2}}\left(\frac{({\theta}^{\rm dr})^{2}}{v_{F}}-2({1}^{\rm dr})V{\left[\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\right]}^{\rm dr}-2({1}^{\rm dr})\left(\frac{1}{v_{F}}-2U\right){\left[\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\right]}^{\rm dr}\right)(q),

\displaystyle=\rho e(\rho,0)+\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3v_{F}}.

(SG.128)

An application of the temperature derivative on the above formula then implies the sought-after expression for the specific heat

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{e}=\frac{1}{T}\,(\partial_{T}e)_{\rho={\rm const}}=\frac{1}{T}\,\partial_{T}\left(\frac{E_{s}}{\rho}\right)_{\rho={\rm const}}=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3\rho v_{F}}.

(SG.129)

(SG.156)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3}\left(\frac{1}{v_{F}}+V(q)-U(q)\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3v_{F}}\left(1-2{v_{F}}{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q)\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3v_{F}}(1+\partial_{q}v_{F})=\frac{\pi^{2}T^{2}}{3v_{F}}(1+2K\partial_{\rho}v_{F}),

with the note that
\partial_{\rho}\mathcal{P}=v_{F}^{2}
.

G.6
Dressed functions identities at low temperature

For considering the spatial derivative of the thermal energy it is possible to construct a relation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{x}\epsilon}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}=-\partial_{x}\eta-\frac{v_{F}}{v_{s}(\theta)}\partial_{x}q,

(SG.157)

the utility of which is that the spatial derivative of a thermal sea with arbitrary Fermi points is put into correspondence with variables relying only on symmetric Fermi points.

At zero temperature the derivative of the dressed functions can be explicitly written out in terms of other dressed functions. These identities are collected here. These identities appear frequently at various intermediate steps.

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}{f}^{\rm dr}\bigg|_{\theta=\pm q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{(\partial_{\theta}f)}^{\rm dr}-{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\pm q,q){f}^{\rm dr}(q)+{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\pm q,-q){f}^{\rm dr}(-q),

\displaystyle(\partial_{q}{f}^{\rm dr})\bigg|_{\theta=\pm q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\pm q,q){f}^{\rm dr}(q)+{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\pm q,-q){f}^{\rm dr}(-q),

\displaystyle\partial_{q}({f}^{\rm dr}(q))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{(\partial f)}^{\rm dr}(q)+2{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q){f}^{\rm dr}(-q).

(SG.158)

With these relations many identities can be written out for other composite quantities like the effective velocity

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}v_{s}(\theta)\bigg|_{\theta=q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2v_{F}{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q),

\displaystyle\partial_{q}v_{s}(\theta)\bigg|_{\theta=q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2v_{F}{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q),

\displaystyle\partial_{q}(v_{s}(q))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-4v_{F}{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q),

(SG.159)

as well as
v_{E}={(\theta^{2})}^{\rm dr}/(2({1}^{\rm dr}))
, where

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(v_{E}(\theta))\bigg|_{\theta=q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-v_{F}\partial_{x}\eta,

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}v_{E}(\theta)\bigg|_{\theta=q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{F}.

(SG.160)

Finally, the dressed kernel derivative identities are given by

\displaystyle\partial_{x}{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(\partial_{x}q){\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,q){\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q),

\displaystyle\partial_{q}{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(\theta,\mu)\bigg|_{\theta,\mu=q,-q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2{\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,q){\varphi}^{\rm dr}(q,-q).

(SG.161)

Consistent with the more general identities previously noted.

G.7
Diffusion at large coupling

In the large
c
limit, keeping the leading term, the diffusion can be more directly computed. Many of the steps are similar to the interacting scenario, however due to explicit form of the thermal energy,

\beta\epsilon=\beta\frac{(\theta-\theta^{-})(\theta-\theta^{+})}{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{c}\right),

(SG.162)

it is possible to write out the relations in a more explicit form. This section is included for completeness, however the results are completely consistent with the
c\to\infty
limit keeping only the
O(1/c^{2})
terms. In the large
c
limit
{\varphi}^{\rm dr}\sim 1/(\pi c)
so that the leading contribution to the diffusion kernel yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}(\int d\alpha[\tilde{D}_{\infty}]\partial_{x}n)

\displaystyle\overset{c\gg 1}{\sim}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi c^{2}}\int d\alpha|\theta-\alpha|(\theta-\alpha)\partial_{x}\left[\frac{\partial_{\theta}n(\theta)}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\frac{\partial_{\alpha}n(\alpha)}{\beta\partial_{\alpha}\epsilon}(\partial_{x}\theta^{+}+\partial_{x}\theta^{-})\right].

(SG.163)

This may be more easily simplified by recalling that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{\theta}n}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}=-\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sigma\frac{\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})}{\theta-\frac{(\theta^{+}+\theta^{-})}{2}}.

(SG.164)

So find that the integral becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\int d\alpha[\tilde{D}_{\infty}]\partial_{x}n\right)

\displaystyle\overset{c\gg 1}{\sim}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi c^{2}}\partial_{x}\left(\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sigma|\theta-\theta^{\sigma}|\frac{(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})}{\theta^{\sigma}-\eta}\frac{\partial_{\theta}n(\theta)}{\beta\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}(\partial_{x}\theta^{+}+\partial_{x}\theta^{-})\right).

(SG.165)

Note that the presence of the
\delta
-function under the
\partial_{x}
complicates the picture, since now acting on the integral introduces a
\delta^{\prime}
-function that cannot be matched by other terms. This represents the fluctuations around the edge of the Fermi seas. Explicitly,
the low-temperature diffusion kernel in the large
c
limit is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\int d\alpha[\tilde{D}_{\infty}]\partial_{x}n\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\pi c^{2}\beta}\sum_{\sigma}\sigma\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\partial_{x}^{2}\eta+\frac{4}{\pi c^{2}}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\left(\frac{|\theta-\theta^{\sigma^{\prime}}|(\theta-\theta^{\sigma^{\prime}})}{\beta(\theta^{+}-\theta^{-})^{2}}\right)\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\frac{\partial_{x}(\beta\epsilon(\theta))}{\beta\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}(\partial_{x}\eta),

(SG.166)

\displaystyle-\frac{8}{\pi c^{2}}\sum_{\sigma}\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\frac{(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}}{\beta(\theta^{+}-\theta^{-})}-\frac{4q}{\pi c^{2}\beta^{2}}(\partial_{x}\eta)(\partial_{x}\beta)\sum_{\sigma}\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma}).

This an be combined with with Euler GHD in terms of a single evolution equation for the Fermi weights

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n+v\partial_{x}n=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\int d\alpha[\tilde{D}_{\infty}]\partial_{x}n\right),

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}\sigma\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}\theta^{\sigma}+v^{\rm eff}(\theta)\partial_{x}\theta^{\sigma}\right)=\sum_{\sigma}\sigma\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\left(\frac{4}{\pi c^{2}\beta}\partial_{x}^{2}\eta-\frac{8\sigma}{\pi c^{2}}\frac{(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}}{\beta(\theta^{+}-\theta^{-})}-\frac{4q\sigma}{\pi c^{2}\beta^{2}}(\partial_{x}\eta)(\partial_{x}\beta)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{4}{\pi c^{2}}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\left(\frac{|\theta-\theta^{\sigma^{\prime}}|(\theta-\theta^{\sigma^{\prime}})}{\beta(\theta^{+}-\theta^{-})^{2}}\right)\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\left(\frac{\partial_{x}\beta}{\beta}\frac{\epsilon(\theta)}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}-\partial_{x}\eta-\frac{q}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\partial_{x}q\right)(\partial_{x}\eta).

(SG.167)

In the second line the term containing
\partial_{x}\beta
should be noted as vanishing, since
\epsilon(\theta^{\sigma})=0
. Consequently, by inserting a test function and simplifying it follows that the
\partial_{x}\beta
term from the first and second lines are either identically zero or exactly cancel. Subsequent removal of the test function then implies

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}\sigma\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}\theta^{\sigma}+v^{\rm eff}(\theta)\partial_{x}\theta^{\sigma}\right)=\sum_{\sigma}\sigma\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\left(\frac{4}{\pi c^{2}\beta}\partial_{x}^{2}\eta-\frac{8\sigma}{\pi c^{2}}\frac{(\partial_{x}\eta)^{2}}{\beta(\theta^{+}-\theta^{-})}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{\pi c^{2}}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\left(\frac{|\theta-\theta^{\sigma^{\prime}}|(\theta-\theta^{\sigma^{\prime}})}{\beta(\theta^{+}-\theta^{-})^{2}}\right)\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\left(\partial_{x}\eta+\frac{q}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}\partial_{x}q\right)(\partial_{x}\eta).

(SG.168)

Now the
\delta^{\prime}
-function must be dealt with. By considering the diffusive corrections to conserved charges, which allows us to make sense of the
\delta^{\prime}
-function. When integrating over
\int d\theta\,1
the result is a vanishing correction. A less trivial result is obtained by considering the integration of
\int d\theta\,\theta
, and
\int d\theta\,\theta^{2}/2
, corresponding to the momentum and energy diffusion respectively

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int d\theta\,\theta\partial_{x}\left(\int d\alpha[\tilde{D}_{\infty}]\partial_{x}n\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8}{\pi c^{2}\beta}\left(q\partial_{x}^{2}\eta+(\partial_{x}q)(\partial_{x}\eta)-\frac{\partial_{x}\beta}{\beta}q\partial_{x}\eta\right)=\frac{8}{\pi c^{2}}\partial_{x}\left(\frac{q\partial_{x}\eta}{\beta}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int d\theta\,\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\partial_{x}\left(\int d\alpha[\tilde{D}_{\infty}]\partial_{x}n\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8\eta}{\pi c^{2}\beta}\left(q\partial_{x}^{2}\eta+(\partial_{x}q)(\partial_{x}\eta)-\frac{\partial_{x}\beta}{\beta}q\partial_{x}\eta\right)+\frac{8q}{\pi c^{2}\beta}\left(\partial_{x}\eta\right)^{2}.

(SG.169)

Note that this implies the viscosity in the large
c
limit, after identification of
\rho=2q
, as being given by

\displaystyle\mu(\rho,T)=\frac{4T}{\pi c^{2}}\rho,

(SG.170)

this result is consistent with the
c\gg 1
limit of the low-temperature diffusion with interaction.

G.8
Commutativity of low-temperature limit and spatial derivative

Along with the above, the
T=0
limit commutes with the spatial derivative. That this commutes is demonstrated below by considering the zero temperature Fermi weight in the large
c
limit and an LDA of the Fermi weight with

\displaystyle n(\theta)=\Theta(\theta-\theta^{-})-\Theta(\theta-\theta^{+})=-\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sigma\Theta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma}).

(SG.171)

The spatial derivatives are then computed as

\displaystyle\partial_{x}n(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sigma\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\partial_{x}\theta^{\sigma},

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}n(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\left(\sigma\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})(\partial_{x}^{2}\theta^{\sigma})-\sigma\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})(\partial_{x}\theta^{\sigma})^{2}\right).

(SG.172)

This result will now be contrasted with the finite temperature case. Application of the derivatives in this case makes use of the Eq. (
SC.39
),
\eta=\frac{\theta^{+}+\theta^{-}}{2}
, and
q=\frac{\theta^{+}-\theta^{-}}{2}
to write the finite temperature second spatial deriative as

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{x}n

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sigma\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\epsilon}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon}-\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\frac{\sigma}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon(\theta^{\sigma})}\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\frac{(\partial_{x}\epsilon)^{2}}{\partial_{\theta}\epsilon},

(SG.173)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sigma\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\frac{[(\theta^{-}-\theta)\partial_{x}^{2}\theta^{+}+(\theta^{+}-\theta)\partial_{x}^{2}\theta^{-}]+2\partial_{x}\theta^{+}\partial_{x}\theta^{-}}{2(\theta-\eta)}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\frac{1}{q}\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\frac{[(\theta-\theta^{-})\partial_{x}\theta^{+}+(\theta-\theta^{+})\partial_{x}\theta^{-}]^{2}}{4(\theta-\eta)}.

For clarity consider the
\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})
term on its own. Interpretation of the
\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})
motivates the application of an overall integral over
\theta
. So inserting the expressions for the derivatives of the energy
\epsilon
have

\displaystyle\int d\theta\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\frac{1}{q}\delta^{\prime}(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\frac{[(\theta-\theta^{-})\frac{\partial_{x}\theta^{+}}{2}+(\theta-\theta^{+})\frac{\partial_{x}\theta^{-}}{2}]^{2}}{4(\theta-\eta)}f(\theta)]

\displaystyle=-\left[(\partial_{x}\theta^{+}+\partial_{x}\theta^{-})\sum_{\sigma}\partial_{x}(\theta^{\sigma})\frac{f(\theta^{\sigma})}{q}-\sum_{\sigma}(\partial_{x}\theta^{\sigma})^{2}\frac{f(\theta^{\sigma})}{q}+[(\partial_{x}\theta^{+})^{2}-(\partial_{x}\theta^{-})^{2}]\partial_{\theta}f(\theta)\right],

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\sigma}\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})\left[\sigma(\partial_{x}\theta^{\sigma})^{2}(\partial_{\theta}f)(\theta)+\frac{(\partial_{x}\theta^{+})(\partial_{x}\theta^{-})}{q}f(\theta)\right].

(SG.174)

This is now recombined with the
\delta(\theta-\theta^{\sigma})
term and the equivalence between the two expressions follows.
