Exploiting cone approximations in an augmented Lagrangian method for conic optimization
Mituhiro Fukuda a,b,c,d , Walter Gómez e , Gabriel Haeser f , and Leonardo M. Mito f

Abstract
We propose an algorithm for general nonlinear conic programming which does not require the knowledge of the full cone, but rather a simpler, more tractable, approximation of it. We prove that the algorithm satisfies a strong global convergence property in the sense that it generates a strong sequential optimality condition. In particular, a KKT point is necessarily found when a limit point satisfies Robinson’s condition. We conduct numerical experiments minimizing nonlinear functions subject to a copositive cone constraint. In order to do this, we consider a well known polyhedral approximation of this cone by means of refining the polyhedral constraints after each augmented Lagrangian iteration. We show that our strategy outperforms the standard approach of considering a close polyhedral approximation of the full copositive cone in every iteration.

Nonlinear conic programming; sequential optimality condition; augmented Lagrangian method; nonlinear copositive programming

Introduction

We study a very general class of optimization problems, sometimes referred to as
nonlinear conic programming
(NCP), which can be stated in the form:

\displaystyle\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\text{Minimize}}

\displaystyle f(x),

(NCP)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle g(x)\in\mathcal{K},

where
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{E}
are continuously differentiable,
\mathbb{E}
is a finite-dimensional vector space equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, and
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{E}
is a nonempty closed convex cone. We will denote the feasible set of (NCP) by
\Omega
, which is assumed to be nonempty, and the Lagrangian function of (NCP) is defined as
L(x,\mu)=f(x)+\langle g(x),\mu\rangle,\textrm{ for }\mu\in\mathbb{E}
.

Despite its generality and simplicity of representation, studies on practical methods for solving (NCP) and even some of its particular cases are somewhat rare in the current literature, in comparison with the cases when
\mathcal{K}
is polyhedral, i.e.,
nonlinear programming
(NLP); or when
g
is affine, i.e.,
conic programming
(CP). This is no surprise, in view of the fact that evaluating feasibility in (N)CP may be computationally expensive or even NP-Hard, such as in the particular case of
copositive programming
(COP). For such intricate problems, some authors have found sequences of polyhedral approximations of
\mathcal{K}
and used them to compute bounds for the solutions of the original problems – see, for instance,
[
5
,
8
,
9
]
and references therein for additional information on COP. A particularly interesting algorithmic approach for solving COP problems was presented in Bundfuss and Dür
[
7
]
and also in Yıldırım
[
18
]
, which consists of computing increasingly better polyhedral approximations of
\mathcal{K}
and solving the approximate problems induced by them to generate a sequence of approximate solutions for the original problem. Both works
[
7
,
18
]
present impressive numerical results, using distinct approximations of
\mathcal{K}
.

Recently, Andreani et al.
[
1
]
extended the so-called “sequential optimality conditions” from NLP to the NCP world. In summary, sequential optimality conditions are parametric perturbed forms of the traditional Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions with three distinguishing properties:

1.

They are always fulfilled at local minimizers, even for degenerate problems;

2.

They are equivalent to the standard KKT condition in the presence of a constraint qualification;

3.

They are naturally satisfied at all the feasible accumulation points computed by a practical algorithm such as
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

The interested reader may check the earlier texts on sequential conditions in NLP
[
2
,
4
]
and an extension to
nonlinear semidefinite programming
(NSDP)
[
3
]
for more details. All these works use an augmented Lagrangian method to illustrate item 3 of the list above, but one of the most critical disadvantages of it in the conic context is the need of a projection onto
\mathcal{K}
that must be computed several times per iteration and that may be numerically very expensive. In this paper, we will study the effects of replacing
\mathcal{K}
with increasingly better polyhedral approximations of it, and the novelty in our proposal is the possibility of improving approximation mid-execution. As far as we are concerned, there is no previous work that allows this in the literature. Our first concern is to build a solid convergence theory for our method, which is done by means of a modified variant of a sequential optimality condition called
approximate gradient projection
(AGP), first introduced in
[
13
]
for NLP and then extended to the conic framework in
[
1
]
.

To illustrate the behavior of our method, we use it for solving a nonlinear COP problem via Yıldırım’s approximations
[
18
]
.

Since this is a first approach, there are some limitations. We can only prove that feasible
limit points will satisfy the necessary optimality condition under Robinson’s constraint qualification. Therefore,
the algorithm can generate points which might not get close to optimal if the Lagrange multipliers are not bounded.
Also a careful polyhedral approximation of the closed convex cone
\mathcal{K}
is necessary to guarantee cheap computation without turning
the subproblems too complex to solve.

There are many applications of the (NCP) formulation for tractable cones, for instance in case of nonlinear semidefinite programming
[
6
,
17
]
.
The copositive cone in contrast is an instance of an intractable cone.
As shown for example in
[
5
,
7
,
8
]
, NP-hard binary quadratic
programming problems can be equivalently reformulated as linear programs
over the copositive cone. The investigation of an equivalent reformulation
of nonlinear binary optimization problems as nonlinear copositve
programs would be an interesting extension. To our knowledge, this
has not been considered yet, possibly due to the lack of algorithms to deal with it. We believe that with our contribution it will be possible to model difficult problems with nonconvex (NCP) and to find approximate solutions using the proposed algorithm.

2
General framework

We study situations where the cone
\mathcal{K}
can be approximated by a sequence
\{\mathcal{K}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
that converges to
\mathcal{K}
in some sense, such that projecting onto each
\mathcal{K}^{k}
is relatively easy.

Definition 2.1

(Continuous approximation of
\mathcal{K}
)
.

A sequence
\{\mathcal{K}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of nonempty closed convex cones is a continuous approximation for
\mathcal{K}
when:

•

For every sequence
\{y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\to y
with
y^{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we must have
y\in\mathcal{K}
. In other words,
\limsup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{K}^{k}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
.

•

For every
y\in\mathcal{K}
, there exists a sequence
\{y^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\to y
with
y^{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{k}
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words,
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\liminf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{K}^{k}
.

When Definition
2.1
holds, we denote it by
\lim_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{K}^{k}=\mathcal{K}
. Let us begin with a technical lemma regarding continuous cone approximations, which is simply a generalization of some classical results on projections:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
and
\{\mathcal{K}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be nonempty closed convex cones in
\mathbb{E}
. Then:

1.

If
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\liminf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{K}^{k}
, then
\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z)\to z
for every
z\in\mathcal{K}
, where

\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z)
is the orthogonal projection of
z
onto
\mathcal{K}^{k}
;

2.

If
\lim_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{K}^{k}=\mathcal{K}
, then
\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z^{k})\to\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(z)
for every converging sequence
\{z^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\to z\in\mathbb{E}
.

Proof.

1.

Let
z\in\mathcal{K}
. By the hypothesis, there exists a sequence
\{z^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\to z
such that
z^{k}\in\mathcal{K}^{k}
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. From the definition of projection,
\|\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z)-z\|\leqslant\|z^{k}-z\|
, whence follows the result;

2.

Since the projection is nonexpansive and
0\in\mathcal{K}^{k}
,
\{\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded by
\|z\|
. Let
N\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be any infinite subset of
\mathbb{N}
such that
\{\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z)\}_{k\in N}
converges to, say,
w\in\mathbb{E}
. Since
\limsup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{K}^{k}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
we obtain
w\in\mathcal{K}
. Now, for every
y\in\mathcal{K}
, it follows from the previous item that

\|z-w\|=\lim_{k\in N}\|z-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z)\|\leqslant\lim_{k\in N}\|z-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(y)\|=\|z-y\|,

which means
w=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(z)
, hence
\lim_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z)=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(z)
. Now,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z^{k})-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(z)\|&\leqslant\|\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z^{k})-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z)\|+\|\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z)-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(z)\|\\
&\leqslant\|z^{k}-z\|+\|\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(z)-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(z)\|\to 0,\end{array}

which completes the proof.

∎

To study global convergence of algorithms that benefit from continuous approximations of
\mathcal{K}
, we propose an adapted version of the sequential optimality condition from
[
1
]
, called
approximate gradient projection
(AGP):

Definition 2.3

(R-AGP)
.

Let
\{\mathcal{K}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a continuous approximation of
\mathcal{K}
, in the sense of Definition
2.1
. We say a feasible point
\bar{x}
of (NCP) satisfies the
relaxed cone AGP
(R-AGP) condition if there exist sequences
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow\bar{x}
and
\{\mu^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mu^{k}\in(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

1.

\nabla L(x^{k},\mu^{k}):=\nabla f(x^{k})+Dg(x^{k})^{*}[\mu^{k}]\to 0,

2.

\langle\mu^{k},\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k}))\rangle\rightarrow 0
.

The reason of requiring
\mu^{k}
to be in the polar cone
(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{{}^{\textnormal{o}}}
instead of in the
dual cone of
\mathcal{K}^{k}
will be clear in the proof of Theorem
2.4
.
Recall that the KKT condition for (NCP) at
(\bar{x},\bar{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{K}^{\textnormal{o}}
is

1.

\nabla L(\bar{x},\bar{\mu})=\nabla f(\bar{x})+Dg(\bar{x})^{*}[\bar{\mu}]=0
;

2.

\langle\bar{\mu},\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(g(\bar{x}))\rangle=0
;

3.

\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(g(\bar{x}))=g(\bar{x})
.

Next, we prove that R-AGP is a necessary optimality condition. For this purpose, we concisely prove the convergence of a variation of the external penalty algorithm. This algorithm, even though it seems novel, is not highlighted here because R-AGP will be used later for building the convergence theory of an augmented Lagrangian algorithm, which is where we intend to focus. For doing so, besides continuity of the cone approximation, we assume also that locally the original feasible set
g^{-1}(\mathcal{K})
is asymptotically included in the approximate feasible set
g^{-1}(\mathcal{K}^{k})
. The proof employs an auxiliary problem which is an unconstrained version of the problem with two additional terms: a penalizing one to enforce feasibility and another regularizing one as in
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

If
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer of (NCP),
\{\mathcal{K}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a continuous approximation of
\mathcal{K}
, and there is a
\delta>0
such that
g^{-1}(\mathcal{K})\cap B[\bar{x},\delta]\subseteq\liminf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}g^{-1}(\mathcal{K}^{k})\cap B[\bar{x},\delta]
, then
\bar{x}
satisfies R-AGP.

Proof.

Let us assume that
\delta>0
is small enough such that
f(\bar{x})\leq f(x)
for all
x\in g^{-1}(\mathcal{K})\cap B[\bar{x},\delta]
and let
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\to\infty
. Consider the auxiliary problem of (NCP):

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\text{Minimize}}&P_{k}(x)\doteq f(x)+\frac{\rho_{k}}{2}\|\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(x))\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}\\
\text{subject to}&\|x-\bar{x}\|\leqslant\delta.\end{array}

(RegA-k)

Let
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of global minimizers of (
RegA-k
), which is bounded by the ball centered at
\bar{x}
with radius
\delta
, and let
w
be an arbitrary limit point of it. Then, by the optimality of
x^{k}
, we have
P_{k}(x^{k})\leqslant P_{k}(\bar{x})
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which implies

\frac{\rho_{k}}{2}\|\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(\bar{x}))\|^{2}\geqslant f(x^{k})-f(\bar{x})+\frac{\rho_{k}}{2}\|\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(x^{k}))\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\|^{2}.

(1)

Dividing everything by
\frac{\rho_{k}}{2}
, we obtain

\|\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(\bar{x}))\|^{2}\geqslant\frac{2(f(x^{k})-f(\bar{x}))}{\rho_{k}}+\|\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(x^{k}))\|^{2}+\frac{\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\|^{2}}{\rho_{k}}\geqslant\frac{2(f(x^{k})-f(\bar{x}))}{\rho_{k}},

(2)

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(\bar{x}))=g(\bar{x})-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(\bar{x}))\to 0
(Lemma
2.2
item 1) and
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, and
\rho_{k}\to\infty
, we obtain

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(x^{k}))\|=0,

(3)

which means
g(w)\in\mathcal{K}
because

\|\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(x^{k}))\|\leqslant\|g(x^{k})-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k}))\|+\|\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k}))-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(g(x^{k}))\|,

given that both terms vanish due to (
3
) and Lemma
2.2
item 2, respectively.

For every
z\in g^{-1}(\mathcal{K})\cap B[\bar{x},\delta]
, there is a sequence
\{z^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\to z
such that
z^{k}\in g^{-1}(\mathcal{K}^{k})\cap B[\bar{x},\delta]
, so

f(x^{k})+(1/2)\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\|^{2}\leqslant P_{k}(x^{k})\leqslant P_{k}(z^{k})=f(z^{k})+(1/2)\|z^{k}-\bar{x}\|^{2}.

Taking limits, we obtain
f(w)+(1/2)\|w-\bar{x}\|^{2}\leqslant f(z)+(1/2)\|z-\bar{x}\|^{2}
. Hence,
w
is a global minimizer of the following localized problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\text{Minimize}}&f(x)+\frac{1}{2}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}\\
\text{subject to}&g(x)\in\mathcal{K}\\
&\|x-\bar{x}\|\leqslant\delta,\end{array}

(Loc-NCP)

but the unique global minimizer of (
Loc-NCP
) is
\bar{x}
, which means
w=\bar{x}
. For
k
sufficiently large, we have
\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\|<\delta
and, by the first-order conditions for (
RegA-k
), we obtain

\nabla f(x^{k})+Dg(x^{k})^{*}[\rho_{k}\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(x^{k}))]=-(x^{k}-\bar{x}).

(4)

Defining
\mu^{k}\doteq\rho_{k}\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(x^{k}))
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
is enough to finish the proof taking into account the equality

\langle\mu^{k},\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k}))\rangle=\rho_{k}\langle\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(g(x^{k})),\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k}))\rangle=0,

and (
4
) with
x^{k}\to\bar{x}
.

∎

It is not true that the continuity of
g
together with the fact
\{\mathcal{K}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a continuous approximation of
\mathcal{K}
implies that
g^{-1}(\mathcal{K})\subseteq\liminf g^{-1}(\mathcal{K}^{k})
, even if all sets involved are closed convex cones. For example, take

g(x)\doteq\begin{pmatrix}x+1\\
x^{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\mathcal{K}\doteq\textnormal{cone}((1,0))\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\mathcal{K}^{k}\doteq\textnormal{cone}((1,-1/k)),\ \forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

In this case, we have
\mathcal{K}^{k}\to\mathcal{K}
, but
g^{-1}(\mathcal{K})=\{(1,0)\}
and
g^{-1}(\mathcal{K}^{k})=\emptyset
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In order to ensure the validity of this inclusion, we will consider from now on that
\{\mathcal{K}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an outer approximation of
\mathcal{K}
; that is, if
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then of course
g^{-1}(\mathcal{K})\subseteq g^{-1}(\mathcal{K}^{k})
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, whence follows that
g^{-1}(\mathcal{K})\subseteq\liminf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}g^{-1}(\mathcal{K}^{k})
.
In Section
3
, we will define an outer approximation
\mathcal{K}^{k}
for the cone of copositive matrices
\mathcal{K}
based on
[

]
to be used in the numerical experiments of Section

. Since
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{k}
, the hypothesis of local feasibility of Theorem

is automatically satisfied.

2.1
An augmented Lagrangian variant with projections onto
\mathcal{K}^{k}

Given a positive scalar sequence
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, a continuous approximation
\{\mathcal{K}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{K}
, a closed ball
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{E}
centered at the origin with radius
R
such that
\mathcal{B}\cap(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}\neq\emptyset
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
together with a sequence
\{\widehat{\mu}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, let
L_{\rho_{k},\widehat{\mu}^{k}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an
augmented Lagrangian function with a cone approximation
, defined as

L_{\rho_{k},\widehat{\mu}^{k}}(x)\doteq f(x)+\frac{\rho_{k}}{2}\left\|\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}\left(g(x)+\frac{\widehat{\mu}^{k}}{\rho_{k}}\right)\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\rho_{k}}\left\|\widehat{\mu}^{k}\right\|^{2}.

(5)

Let us now consider Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

General framework: Augmented Lagrangian

Inputs:

\{\varepsilon_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{++}
of scalars such that
\varepsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
; real parameters
\tau>1
,
\sigma\in(0,1)
,
\rho_{0}>0
, and

;

For every

:

1.

Compute
some point

such that

\|\nabla L_{\rho_{k},\widehat{\mu}^{k}}(x^{k})\|\leq\varepsilon_{k},

(6)

using an iterative method starting from

;

2.

Update the multiplier

\mu^{k}\doteq\rho_{k}\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}\left(g(x^{k})+\frac{\widehat{\mu}^{k}}{\rho_{k}}\right),

(7)

(8)

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\bar{x}
be a feasible limit point of a sequence

\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by Algorithm 1, for any given choice of parameters
\{\widehat{\mu}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

Then,
\bar{x}
satisfies R-AGP.

Proof.

First, let us assume for simplicity that
x^{k}\to\bar{x}
. Let
\{\mu^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be as in (
7
) and observe that
\nabla L_{\rho_{k},\widehat{\mu}^{k}}(x^{k})=\nabla_{x}L(x^{k},\mu^{k})\to 0
. If
\rho_{k}\to\infty
, then

\langle\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k})),\mu^{k}\rangle=\rho_{k}\left\langle\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k}))-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}\left(g(x^{k})+\frac{\widehat{\mu}^{k}}{\rho_{k}}\right),\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}\left(g(x^{k})+\frac{\widehat{\mu}^{k}}{\rho_{k}}\right)\right\rangle

which implies (using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the nonexpansiveness of the projection) that

\displaystyle|\langle\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k})),\mu^{k}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leqslant\rho_{k}\left\|\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k}))-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}\left(g(x^{k})+\frac{\widehat{\mu}^{k}}{\rho_{k}}\right)\right\|\left\|\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}\left(g(x^{k})+\frac{\widehat{\mu}^{k}}{\rho_{k}}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\rho_{k}\left\|\frac{\widehat{\mu}^{k}}{\rho_{k}}\right\|\left\|\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}\left(g(x^{k})+\frac{\widehat{\mu}^{k}}{\rho_{k}}\right)\right\|

but due to Lemma

\rho_{k}\to\infty

\langle\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k})),\mu^{k}\rangle\to 0
. If
\rho_{k}=\rho_{k_{0}}
for some
k_{0}
and every
k\geq k_{0}
, then using the fact

\mu^{k}=\widehat{\mu}^{k}-\rho_{k}v^{k}

we have that

\displaystyle\langle\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k})),\mu^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k})),\widehat{\mu}^{k}\rangle-\langle\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k})),\rho_{k}v^{k}\rangle,

but for any convergent subsequence of
\{\widehat{\mu}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, which we will assume to be itself, so
\{\widehat{\mu}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\to\bar{\mu}
,
we recall that
v^{k}\to 0
and
\rho_{k}\to\rho_{k_{0}}
to obtain that

\displaystyle\langle\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k})),\mu^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\to\langle\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(g(\bar{x})),\bar{\mu}\rangle=\langle g(\bar{x}),\bar{\mu}\rangle=0,

which follows from
v^{k}\to 0
, since it implies
\bar{\mu}=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{\textnormal{o}}}(\rho_{k_{0}}g(\bar{x})+\bar{\mu})
, which in turn holds if, and only if,
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}^{\textnormal{o}}
,
g(\bar{x})\in\mathcal{K}
, and
\langle g(\bar{x}),\bar{\mu}\rangle=0

In general, for any closed convex cone
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{E}
and any

implies that
\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(y+z)=y+z-\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{\textnormal{o}}}(y+z)=y

The proof above is an adaptation of the proof of

. Moreover, it is easy to show that if
\bar{x}
satisfies R-AGP and Robinson’s CQ:

0\in\textnormal{int}(\textnormal{Im}(Dg(\bar{x}))+\mathcal{K}-g(\bar{x})),

then
\bar{x}
must also satisfy the KKT conditions. This is a consequence of the boundedness of
\{\mu^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in the presence of Robinson’s CQ. Indeed, if
\{\mu^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is not bounded, then we may take a subsequence such that
\|\mu^{k}\|\to\infty
, so

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\nabla f(x^{k})}{\|\mu^{k}\|}+Dg(x^{k})^{*}\left[\frac{\mu^{k}}{\|\mu^{k}\|}\right]=\lim_{k\to\infty}Dg(x^{k})^{*}\left[\frac{\mu^{k}}{\|\mu^{k}\|}\right]=0,

but on the other hand we also have

\left\langle\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k})),\frac{\mu^{k}}{\|\mu^{k}\|}\right\rangle\to 0\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\Pi_{\mathcal{K}^{k}}(g(x^{k}))\to\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(g(\bar{x}))=g(\bar{x})

by Lemma
2.2
item 2. Define
H\doteq\textnormal{Im}(Dg(\bar{x}))+\mathcal{K}-g(\bar{x})
and the above reasoning tells us that any limit point
\tilde{\mu}\neq 0
of
\{\mu^{k}/\|\mu^{k}\|\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
must belong to
H^{\textnormal{o}}=\textnormal{Ker}(Dg(\bar{x}))\cap\mathcal{K}^{\textnormal{o}}\cap\{g(\bar{x})\}^{\perp}
. However, since
H^{\textnormal{o}}
is a cone, this also means that
\alpha\tilde{\mu}\in H^{\textnormal{o}}
for every
\alpha>0
. On the other hand, by Robinson’s CQ, we also have that
\alpha\tilde{\mu}\in H
for every
\alpha>0
small enough, implying that
\tilde{\mu}=0
, which is a contradiction.

The proposed algorithm has some theoretical and numerical limitations. On the one hand, in case
\rho_{k}\rightarrow\infty
, the condition numbers of auxiliary problems would be unbounded. As a consequence, the numerical solution of auxiliary problems turn numerically unstable and difficult to deal with. This case may be expected when Robinson’s CQ fails to hold.

On the other hand, for the interesting case of non polyhedral cones, a rapid local convergence can not be ensured, since the augmented Lagrangian does not include any information regarding the curvature of the cone. Nevertheless, the proposed algorithm provides a useful setting for the case of nonpolyhedral cones that are numericall difficult to represent and that lack of an efficient algorithm for calculating the projection.

3
Continuous (polyhedral) approximation of the cone of copositive matrices

Now, we illustrate a continuous outer approximation of
\mathcal{K}
(Definition
2.1
) by polyhedra through a
concrete example. Note that as discussed previously, this approximation satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
2.4
.

The cone of copositive matrices and its dual, the cone of
completely positive matrices,
are well studied closed convex cones. It is known that many NP-hard problems can be formulated as convex problems employing these cones
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
16
]
.
Among several inner and outer hierarchical and asymptotic approximations of
these cones, we consider the polyhedral approximation proposed by
Yıldırım
[
18
]
, due to its simplicity and cheap orthogonal projection onto.

For integers
m\geq 1
and
r\geq 0
, consider the discrete set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}

formed by regular grids of rational points on the
unit simplex:

\delta^{m}_{r}\doteq\bigcup_{k=0}^{r}\{z\in\Delta_{m}\ :\ (k+2)z\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\},

where
\Delta_{m}\doteq\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\ :\ e^{T}y=1\}
is the
(m-1)
-dimensional unit simplex.
Let
\ell_{r}^{m}:=|\delta^{m}_{r}|
be the number of elements in
\delta^{m}_{r}
, which can be roughly upper bounded
by
m^{2}(m^{r+1}-1)/(m-1)

[
18
]
.

Let
\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}
be the set of
m\times m
real symmetric matrices.
For each
r=0,1,\ldots
, we define the following closed convex cones, which in fact are
polyhedra:

\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{r}\doteq\{Y\in\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}\ :\ d^{T}Yd\geq 0,\forall d\in\delta^{m}_{r}\}

and its dual

\left(\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{r}\right)^{*}\doteq\left\{\sum_{d\in\delta^{m}_{r}}\lambda_{d}dd^{T}\ :\ \lambda_{d}\geq 0\right\},

(9)

which give an outer approximation of the closed convex cone of copositive matrices

\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m}\doteq\{Y\in\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}\ :\ u^{T}Yu\geq 0,\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\}

and an inner approximation of the closed convex cone of completely positive matrices

\left(\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m}\right)^{*}\doteq\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(v_{i})(v_{i})^{T}\ :\ k\geq 1,v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\right\},

respectively.

It can be shown
[
18
]
that

(\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{0})^{*}\subseteq(\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{1})^{*}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq(\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m})^{*}\subseteq\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}_{+}\cap\mbox{$\cal{N}$}^{m}\subseteq\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}_{+}\subseteq\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}_{+}+\mbox{$\cal{N}$}^{m}\subseteq\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{1}\subseteq\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{0},

\textrm{cl}\left(\displaystyle\bigcup_{r\in\mathbb{N}}(\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{r})^{*}\right)=(\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m})^{*}
, and

\displaystyle\bigcap_{r\in\mathbb{N}}\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{r}=\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m}
for
\mbox{$\cal{N}$}^{m}:=\{Y\in\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}\ :\ Y_{ij}\geq 0,\forall i,j=1,\dots,m\}
where
\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}_{+}
(
\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}_{-}
) is
the cone of
m\times m
positive (negative) semidefinite symmetric matrices. Moreover

(\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m})^{*}=\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}_{+}\cap\mbox{$\cal{N}$}^{m}
,
\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m}=\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}_{+}+\mbox{$\cal{N}$}^{m}
for
m\leq 4
, and
\{\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
is an outer continuous approximation of
\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m}
(Definition
2.1
).

An advantage of our implementation based on the polyhedral approximations is that the sets
\mathcal{K}^{k}
are defined by a moderate number of linear inequalities, such that the computational cost of projections is also moderate.
Namely, for
Y\in\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}
, let us compute its orthogonal projection onto the polar
cone of
\mathcal{K}^{k}:=\{Y\in\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}\ :\ d^{T}_{i}Yd_{i}\geq 0,\ \forall d_{i}\in J^{k}\}\supseteq\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{\bar{r}}

for some
\bar{r}\geq 0
and
\emptyset\not=J^{k}\subseteq\delta^{m}_{\bar{r}}
.
Since
\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{\textnormal{o}}}(Y)=-\Pi_{(\mathcal{K}^{k})^{*}(-Y)}
,
we need to solve the following optimization problem to
obtain the orthogonal projection
-Z
of
Y
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\textrm{Minimize}&\|-Y-Z\|_{F}^{2},\\
\textrm{subject to}&Z\in(\mbox{$\cal{K}$}^{k})^{*}.\end{array}

Using the definition of
\mbox{$\cal{K}$}^{k}
, the above problem is in fact
a convex quadratic program with
|J^{k}|
non-negative variables:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\textrm{Minimize}&\lambda^{T}R\lambda+2s^{T}\lambda,\\
\textrm{subject to}&\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{|J^{k}|}_{+}\end{array}

(10)

for
R_{ij}=(d_{i}^{T}d_{j})^{2}
and
s_{i}=d_{i}^{T}Yd_{i}
, where
d_{i}\in J^{k}

for
(i,j=1,2,\ldots,|J^{k}|)
. This is due to the fact that
if we
display all vectors of
J^{k}
as rows of a larger matrix

D\doteq\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d_{1}^{T}\\
d_{2}^{T}\\
\vdots\\
d^{T}_{|J^{k}|}\end{array}\right),

the matrix
R
will be the self Hadamard product of the Gram matrix
DD^{T}
, which
is positive semidefinite. Thus,
R=(DD^{T})\circ(DD^{T})
is also positive semidefinite.

As we can observe, other closed convex cones such as exponential, hyperbolicity
[
11
]
or any other cone can be treated in a similar manner as long as we have a continuous approximation of them by polyhedra.
This will permit us to minimize nonlinear functions over these cones by our proposed algorithm.

4.2
Details of the implementation

Algorithm
1
computes an approximate local optimal solution when
\|\nabla L_{\rho_{k},\widehat{\mu}^{k}}(x^{k})\|
and

\|v^{k}\|
are small enough since they correspond to the norms of derivatives of
the safeguarded augmented Lagrangian function
L_{\rho,\widehat{\mu}}(x)
in
relation to
x^{k}
and
\widehat{\mu}^{k}
, respectively.
Therefore,
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\mu^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
computed by Algorithm
1
are sequences which define a limit point

\bar{x}
satisfying R-AGP according to Theorem
2.5
.

Simple schemes were sought to define
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\mbox{$\cal{K}$}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in Algorithm
1
.
Among others, the empirical choice
\varepsilon_{k}\doteq\min\{\varepsilon_{0},\|v^{k}\|_{\max}\}
seemed to provide fast convergence,
where
\|v\|_{\max}=\max_{1\leq i,j\leq m}|v_{ij}|
.
This strategy revealed to be superior than a conventional choice
\varepsilon_{k}\doteq\eta^{k}\varepsilon_{0}
for
0<\eta<1

which guarantees
\varepsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
. Although our strategy may not guarantee
\varepsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
, it
appears to be reasonable when we expect that
\|v^{k}\|_{\max}\rightarrow 0
, whenever we succeed to compute
x^{k}
satisfying (
6
) at every iteration.

The strategy to choose
\{\mathcal{K}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
has more variations and after some
tests, we decided that the best strategy was to fix first the positive
integers
r_{\max}
, which defines
\mbox{$\cal{O}$}^{m}_{r_{\max}}\supseteq\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m}
(see Section
3
), and
\zeta<|\delta^{m}_{r_{\max}}|-|\delta^{m}_{0}|
.
Then, we order all vectors in
\delta^{m}_{r_{\max}}
such that
the first
\ell^{m}_{0}=|\delta^{m}_{0}|
elements are the vectors in
\delta^{m}_{0}
(in any order), followed by the vectors
in
\delta^{m}_{1}\backslash\delta^{m}_{0}
(in any order), until the vectors in
\delta^{m}_{r_{\max}}\backslash\delta^{m}_{r_{\max}-1}

(in any order).
Set
J^{0}\doteq\delta^{m}_{0}
in
\mbox{$\cal{K}$}^{k}=\{Y\in\mbox{$\cal{S}$}^{m}\ :\ d^{T}_{i}Yd_{i}\geq 0,\ \forall d_{i}\in J^{k}\}
, and
J^{k}\doteq J^{k-1}\cup\{\textrm{first $\zeta$ vectors (whenever possible) in $\delta^{m}_{r_{\max}}$ which are not in $J^{k-1}$ in the above order}\}
. That is, at every iteration of Algorithm
1
,
\mathcal{K}^{k}
is defined by
|J^{k}|
vectors and has exactly
\zeta
more vectors than
|J^{k-1}|
, unless

. In our numerical experiments (see Section
4.3
),
we considered test problems for
m=3
and
r_{\max}=15
which gives
\ell^{m}_{r_{\max}}=901
,
and
m=5
and
r_{\max}=7
which gives
\ell^{m}_{r_{\max}}=1816
. Notice that in order for our approximation of
\mbox{$\cal{K}$}=\mbox{$\cal{C}$}^{m}
to be continuous as in Definition
2.1
, we would need to keep approximating the sets
\delta^{m}_{r}
for

Another detail apparently hidden in the implementation of Algorithm
1
is the radius
R
of the
closed ball
{\mathcal{B}}
. At every iteration, we need to project
\mu^{k}
onto
{\mathcal{B}}
at step 2 and

In the numerical experiments, we set

Finally, a scaling of the safeguarded augmented Lagrangian function

is effective, specially because some objective functions

are badly scaled.
We divided the function

5
Concluding Remarks

The general optimization problem of minimizing a nonlinear function subject to nonlinear conic constraints has received increasing attention in the recent years. General algorithms for dealing with such generality are still under development. The linear case is of particular interest considering that in many applications the nonlinearities of the problem may be concentrated to lie in the cone itself. However, when it is not clear how to project onto the cone, a practical implementation is usually out of hand. In contrast, most algorithms require that one is able to deal with the full cone in every iteration of the method. In this paper we proposed an augmented Lagrangian algorithm that considers the possibility of iteratively approximating the cone in each iteration, without hindering well established global convergence results. Numerical experiments are conducted with the copositive cone and its polyhedral outer approximation where we demonstrate in a small collection of problems that our strategy is superior than the alternative one of considering the full cone in each iteration.
