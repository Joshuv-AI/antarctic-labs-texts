Tri-linear birational maps in dimension three
Laurent Busé, Pablo González-Mazón, Josef Schicho

Abstract
. A tri-linear rational map in dimension three is a rational map ϕ : ( ℙ ℂ 1 ) 3 ⇢ ℙ ℂ 3 \phi:(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{3}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3} defined by four tri-linear polynomials without a common factor. If ϕ \phi admits an inverse rational map ϕ − 1 \phi^{-1} , it is a tri-linear birational map. In this paper, we address computational and geometric aspects about these transformations. We give a characterization of birationality based on the first syzygies of the entries. More generally, we describe all the possible minimal graded free resolutions of the ideal generated by these entries. With respect to geometry, we show that the set 𝔅 ​ 𝔦 ​ 𝔯 ( 1 , 1 , 1 ) \mathfrak{Bir}_{(1,1,1)} of tri-linear birational maps, up to composition with an automorphism of ℙ ℂ 3 \mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3} , is a locally closed algebraic subset of the Grassmannian of 4 4 -dimensional subspaces in the vector space of tri-linear polynomials, and has eight irreducible components. Additionally, the group action on 𝔅 ​ 𝔦 ​ 𝔯 ( 1 , 1 , 1 ) \mathfrak{Bir}_{(1,1,1)} given by composition with automorphisms of ( ℙ ℂ 1 ) 3 (\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{3} defines 19 orbits, and each of these orbits determines an isomorphism class of the base loci of these transformations.



1.
Introduction

Motivation

Rational maps between multi-projective and projective spaces have been studied recently, with a special emphasis on the computation of the degrees and birationality (e.g.
[
6
,
8
,
5
,
21
,
13
]
). The introduction of the multi-projective setting is motivated by applications of these rational maps to Computer-Aided Geometric Design (CAGD), computer vision, shape optimization, animation, and other related fields (
[
21
]
and references therein). In particular, maps defined over products of projective lines are ubiquitous in geometric modeling. For instance, tensor-product rational Bézier surfaces and volumes are used intensively for the definition of geometric objects. More specifically,
dominant rational maps
(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and
(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{3}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
provide simple and meaningful control points for the deformation of 2D and 3D shapes, in a technique known as Free-Form Deformation (FFD) (e.g.
[
22
,
21
]
).
One of the requirements for these applications is the injectivity of the rational map. More interestingly, birational maps are globally injective, and have an inverse rational map. Moreover, the computation of preimages is necessary in some applications (
[
21
]
and references), and the inverse map allows the computation of these preimages avoiding the numerical resolution of equations.

Previous works and context

Approximately thirty years ago, modern techniques from commutative algebra were incorporated to the study of birational maps, primarily motivated by computational purposes. Syzygy-based birationality criteria date back to
[
20
]
, and have been further developed ever since (e.g.
[
19
,
23
]
). In this context, the Jacobian Dual Criterion (JDC) is a general method to decide if a rational map between projective varieties, or from a multi-projective variety to a projective variety, is birational
[
10
,
6
]
. The JDC relies on the computation of the defining equations of the Rees algebra associated to the rational map. Unfortunately, the derivation of these equations entails a high computational cost, and for some applications specialized methods are preferred.
In dimension two, there are efficient strategies for the manipulation of bi-linear birational maps
\phi:(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, whose entries are polynomials of bi-degree
(1,1)
without a common factor, where a syzygy-based characterization of birationality is known
[
22
,
5
]
. Larger bi-degrees have also been considered, but only partially. Namely, sufficient conditions under which birationality holds are known for maps with entries of bi-degree
(2,2)

[
5
]
and
(1,n)

[
21
]
.
In dimension three, no syzygy-based criteria for the birationality of a rational map
\phi:(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{3}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
are known yet, probably because the situation is much more involved.

On the other hand, the study of groups and spaces of birational maps has a long history in algebraic geometry, and there is an extensive literature about the topic (e.g.
[
17
,
1
,
7
,
3
,
4
]
). The
n
-dimensional Cremona group over
\mathbb{C}
, denoted by either
\text{Cr}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
or
\mathrm{Bir}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n})
, is the group of birational endomorphisms of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Given
d\geq 0
, the subset
\mathfrak{Bir}_{d}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n})\subset\text{Cr}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
consists of all the birational endomorphisms that are determined by
n+1
homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
, without a common factor.

Remarkably, we have a good understanding of
\mathfrak{Bir}_{d}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})

[
3
]
and a complete classification of the base loci is known for the degrees
d=2,3

[
7
]
. However, for
n\geq 3
the situation quickly becomes more complicated. For
n=3
, the algebraic structure of
\mathfrak{Bir}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
has been described together with a complete classification of the base loci of birational endomorphisms with quadratic entries
[
18
]
, but this is no longer the case if
d\geq 3
. For
d=3
, some of the irreducible components of
\mathfrak{Bir}_{3}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3})
have also been described
[
9
]
, but only a partial classification of the base loci is known
[
17
,
9
]
.
With respect to birational maps from a multi-projective space to a projective target, only the structure and classification of the base loci of bi-linear birational maps
(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{2}\dasharrow\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
have been addressed
[
22
,
5
]
. Namely,
the set of bi-linear birational maps is an irreducible hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{11}
, and the base locus is always a closed point.

In this paper, we study the algebraic set of tri-linear birational maps
\phi:(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{3}\dasharrow\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
and we give the complete classification of the base loci of such transformations, up to isomorphism of schemes. This geometric analysis targets the derivation of computational methods for the manipulation of these birational maps. Namely, we provide a syzygy-based characterization for birationality, whose proof strongly relies on the classification of the base loci.

2.
Preliminars

2.1.
Tri-linear birational maps in dimension three

A tri-linear rational map in dimension three
\phi
is a rational map

(2.1)

\displaystyle\phi:\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}

\displaystyle\dasharrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}

\displaystyle(x_{0}:x_{1})\times(y_{0}:y_{1})\times(z_{0}:z_{1})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle(f_{0}:f_{1}:f_{2}:f_{3})\ ,

where
f_{i}=f_{i}(x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1},z_{0},z_{1})
is a tri-linear polynomial, i.e
.
is linear and homogeneous with respect to the three pairs of variables
\{x_{0},x_{1}\}
,
\{y_{0},y_{1}\}
, and
\{z_{0},z_{1}\}
, and the
f_{i}
’s do not have a common factor. For simplicity in the notation, we will refer to them simply as tri-linear rational maps. If
\phi
admits an inverse rational map, then
\phi
is a tri-linear birational map.

Let
A_{1}=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},x_{1}]
,
A_{2}=\mathbb{C}[y_{0},y_{1}]
, and
A_{3}=\mathbb{C}[z_{0},z_{1}]
be the graded homogeneous coordinate rings of each
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
, with the standard grading in each ring given by the corresponding pair of variables. The tri-homogeneous coordinate ring of the product
X\coloneqq(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{3}
is the tensor product ring
R\coloneqq A_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}A_{2}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}A_{3}
, which inherits a tri-graded structure

R=\bigoplus_{(i,j,k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\,R_{(i,j,k)}=\bigoplus_{(i,j,k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\,(A_{1})_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}(A_{2})_{j}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}(A_{3})_{k}\ ,

where subindices stand for graded components. The irrelevant ideal of
R
is

\mathfrak{N}=\bigoplus_{i>0,\,j>0,\,k>0}\,(A_{1})_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}(A_{2})_{j}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}(A_{3})_{k}=(x_{0},x_{1})\cap(y_{0},y_{1})\cap(z_{0},z_{1})\ .

The tri-homogeneous ideal
B_{\phi}=(f_{0},f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})\subset R
is the
base ideal
of
\phi
. The subscheme
Z_{\phi}
of
X
defined by the base ideal
B_{\phi}
is the
base locus
or
base locus scheme
of
\phi
. The codimension of
Z_{\phi}
is always at least two, as otherwise we find a common factor to the
f_{i}
’s. On the other hand, a surface
S\subset X
is the vanishing locus of a tri-homogeneous polynomial in
R
. The
tri-degree of
S
, denoted by tri-deg
(S)
, is the tri-degree of its defining polynomial.

2.2.
Curves in
(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{3}

Let
Y
be a one-dimensional subscheme of
X
.
The
tri-degree of

Y
, denoted by
\text{tri-deg}(Y)
, is the triple
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
where
d_{i}
is the number of points, counted with multiplicity, in the intersection of
Y
with the subspace of
X
determined by a general linear form in
A_{i}
. Equivalently,
d_{i}
is the degree of the projection of
Y
onto the
i
-th
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
factor of
X
. For zero-dimensional subschemes of
X
, we define the tri-degree as
(0,0,0)
.

If all the irreducible components of
Y
have dimensione one, we say that
Y
is a curve. In this situation, we write
I_{Y}\subset R
for its defining ideal.
On the other hand, recall that
Y
is Cohen-Macaulay if for every point
P\in Y
the local ring of
Y
at
P
is Cohen-Macaulay. In particular,
Y
is a Cohen-Macaulay curve if and only if it does not have embedded nor isolated points
[
24
, Lemma 31.4.4]
.

We define
C_{Y}\subset X
as the subscheme determined by the intersection of all the associated primes to
I_{Y}
in
R
of codimension exactly two. Clearly,
C_{Y}\subset Y
is a Cohen-Macaulay curve. Additionally, when
Y=Z_{\phi}
we denote
C_{Y}
simply by
C_{\phi}
, and
I_{C_{Y}}
simply by
I_{\phi}
.

2.3.
The Jacobian Dual Criterion

Let
J
be the kernel of the ring homomorphism

\displaystyle R\,[t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}]

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle R\,[u]

\displaystyle t_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle f_{i}\,u\ \ \ \ \ \ \ .

The ideal

It is a prime ideal hence saturated with respect to

The Rees ideal

\mathbb{Z}^{4}
-graded structure, as it inherits the
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
-grading from

\mathbb{Z}
-grading from
\mathbb{C}[t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}]

for this multi-degree in
\mathbb{Z}^{4}
. More explicitly, we have

g=g(x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1},z_{0},z_{1},t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\in J_{(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3};d_{4})}

The elements in

’s with coefficients of tri-degree
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})

The multi-graded Jacobian Dual Criterion (JDC)

characterizes birationality in terms of the ideal

(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
consider the

J_{(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3};*)}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{d_{4}\in\,\mathbb{Z}}J_{(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3};d_{4})}\ .

Assuming that
\phi
is dominant, the JDC establishes that
\phi
is birational if and only if the modules

J_{(1,0,0;*)}\ ,\ J_{(0,1,0;*)}\ ,\ J_{(0,0,1;*)}

all have rank one when regarded as vector spaces over the field
\mathbb{C}(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})
.
In particular, we find minimal integers
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(2.2)

\begin{vmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}\\
a_{0}&a_{1}\end{vmatrix}\in J_{(1,0,0;\,r_{1})}\ ,\ \begin{vmatrix}y_{0}&y_{1}\\
b_{0}&b_{1}\end{vmatrix}\in J_{(0,1,0;\,r_{2})}\ ,\ \begin{vmatrix}z_{0}&z_{1}\\
c_{0}&c_{1}\end{vmatrix}\in J_{(0,0,1;\,r_{3})}\ ,

for some polynomials

.
Thus, we find

a_{i}\xmapsto{t_{l}\mapsto f_{l}}x_{i}\,s\ ,\ b_{j}\xmapsto{t_{l}\mapsto f_{l}}y_{j}\,t\ ,\ c_{k}\xmapsto{t_{l}\mapsto f_{l}}z_{k}\,u\ ,

and the rational map

(2.3)

\displaystyle\phi^{-1}:\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}

\displaystyle\dasharrow

\displaystyle X=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}

\displaystyle(t_{0}:t_{1}:t_{2}:t_{3})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle(a_{0}:a_{1})\times(b_{0}:b_{1})\times(c_{0}:c_{1})

must be the inverse of
\phi

yields the identity in any open subset of

where

, and

2.5.
Automorphisms of
(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{3}

Let

be the group of automorphisms of

. Consider the normal subgroup

, i.e

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle(x_{0}:x_{1})\times(y_{0}:y_{1})\times(z_{0}:z_{1})

3.
Factorization of tri-linear birational maps

6.
Syzygies of tri-linear birational maps

Finally, we study the syzygies of the entries of tri-linear birational maps. Interestingly, the following theorem yields a birationality criterion for tri-linear rational maps, based on the computation of the first syzygies in some tri-degrees. Furthermore, novel methods for the manipulation of birational volumes might be devised from this theorem, relying on the imposition of specific syzygies. In order to simplify the statement, we set

e_{1}=(1,0,0)\ ,\ e_{2}=(0,1,0)\ ,\ e_{3}=(0,0,1)\ .

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume that
\phi
is dominant, and let
i,j,k
be indices such that
\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}
. Then,
\phi
is birational if and only if one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

The
f_{i}
’s have syzygies of tri-degrees
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
, and
e_{3}
. In this case, the type of
\phi
is
(1,1,1)
.

(2)

The
f_{i}
’s have syzygies of tri-degree
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
, but not
e_{k}
. In this case, the type of
\phi
is
(1,1,1)+e_{k}
.

(3)

The
f_{i}
’s have a syzygy of tri-degree
e_{i}
, but neither
e_{j}
nor
e_{k}
. Moreover, the
f_{i}
’s also have a syzygy of tri-degree either
e_{i}+e_{j}
or
e_{i}+e_{k}
, independent from the first one. In this case, the type of
\phi
is
(1,1,1)+e_{j}+e_{k}
.

(4)

The
f_{i}
’s do not have syzygies of tri-degree
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
, or
e_{3}
. Moreover, the
f_{i}
’s have two independent syzygies in each of the tri-degrees
e_{1}+e_{2}
,
e_{1}+e_{3}
, and
e_{2}+e_{3}
satisfying the splitting property in Condition
6.7
(see §
6.4
). In this case, the type of
\phi
is
(2,2,2)
.

The proof of Theorem
6.1
follows immediately from Propositions
6.2
,
6.4
,
6.5
, and
6.8
, and it relies strongly on the geometric classification of §
5
. More explicitly, the composition of a rational map with automorphisms of
(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{3}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
preserves the structure of the module of first syzygies, with maybe a permutation in the tri-degrees (if this is not preserved by the automorphism of
(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1})^{3}
). Therefore, the syzygies of the entries can be computed for the representatives of the finitely many orbits of the right-action in
\mathfrak{Bir}_{(1,1,1)}
, listed in Theorems
5.1
,
5.4
,
5.6
, and
5.8
.

On the other hand,
although Theorem
6.1
is adequate for computational purposes, we provide further cohomological information about the base loci of tri-linear birational maps. Namely, we will prove that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the type of
\phi
and the shape of the minimal tri-graded free resolution of the base ideal
B_{\phi}
. For the sake of comparison, the minimal free resolution of a tri-linear rational map
\phi
with general entries is

0\xrightarrow{}R\xrightarrow{}R^{21}\xrightarrow{}R^{62}\xrightarrow{}R^{69}\xrightarrow{}R^{30}\xrightarrow{}R^{4}\xrightarrow{}B_{\phi}\xrightarrow{}0\ .

As we shall see, the resolution is remarkably simpler when
\phi
is birational.

6.1.
Syzygies of birational maps of type
(1,1,1)

For birational maps of type
(1,1,1)
, the minimal tri-graded free resolution of the base ideal
B_{\phi}
is Hilbert-Burch.

Proposition 6.2
.

Assume that
\phi
is dominant. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\phi
is birational of type
(1,1,1)
.

(2)

The minimal tri-graded free resolution of the base ideal
B_{\phi}
is Hilbert-Burch, i.e.

(6.1)

0\xrightarrow{}\begin{matrix}R(-2,-1,-1)\\
\oplus\\
R(-1,-2,-1)\\
\oplus\\
R(-1,-1,-2)\\
\end{matrix}\xrightarrow{}R(-1,-1,-1)^{4}\xrightarrow{(f_{0}\ f_{1}\ f_{2}\ f_{3})}B_{\phi}\xrightarrow{}0\ .

In particular, the base locus
Z_{\phi}
is a Cohen-Macaulay curve.

(3)

The
f_{i}
’s have syzygies of tri-degrees
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
, and
e_{3}
.

Proof.

We first prove that
(1)
implies
(2)
, so we assume that
\phi
is birational of type
(1,1,1)
. Maintaining the notation of (
2.3
) for
\phi^{-1}
, in the Rees ideal
J
we find the polynomials

(6.2)

g_{1}=\begin{vmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}\\
a_{0}&a_{1}\end{vmatrix}\ ,\ g_{2}=\begin{vmatrix}y_{0}&y_{1}\\
b_{0}&b_{1}\end{vmatrix}\ ,\ g_{3}=\begin{vmatrix}z_{0}&z_{1}\\
c_{0}&c_{1}\end{vmatrix}\ .

Consider the
4\times 3
matrix
M=M(x_{i},y_{j},z_{k})
, whose
(i,j)
-th entry is the coefficient of
g_{j}
in
t_{i}
. By definition, the tuple
(f_{0},f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})
lies in the cokernel of
M
. Now, write
\Delta_{i}
for the
3\times 3
signed minor obtained by deleting the
i
-th row of
M
. Then, the tuple
(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta_{3})
also lies in the cokernel of
M
.
Moreover, as
\phi
is birational the specialization of the
g_{i}
’s at a general point of
X
yields three independent linear forms, so the matrix
M
has generically rank
3
.
In particular
M
has no kernel in
R^{3}
, and its cokernel is a free
R
-module of rank one generated by
(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta_{3})
. As the tuples
(f_{0},f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})
and
(\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},\Delta_{3})
have tri-linear entries, they must differ by a non-zero constant. Therefore, as
\text{codim}\,B_{\phi}\geq 2
the complex

(6.3)

0\xrightarrow{}R^{3}\xrightarrow{M}R^{4}\xrightarrow{(\Delta_{0}\,\Delta_{1}\,\Delta_{2}\,\Delta_{3})}B_{\phi}\xrightarrow{}0

is a minimal free resolution of
B_{\phi}
. On the other hand, from the Auslander-Buchbaum formula the ring
R/B_{\phi}
is Cohen-Macaulay of codimension two (see e.g.
[
11
, §20.4]
), which implies that
Z_{\phi}
is a Cohen-Macaulay curve.

Clearly
(2)
implies
(3)
, so we now assume
(3)
and prove
(1)
. The syzygies of tri-degree
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
, and
e_{3}
can be written as

x_{0}\,a_{1}-x_{1}\,a_{0}=\begin{vmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}\\
a_{0}&a_{1}\end{vmatrix}\ ,\ y_{0}\,b_{1}-y_{1}\,b_{0}=\begin{vmatrix}y_{0}&y_{1}\\
b_{0}&b_{1}\end{vmatrix}\ ,\ z_{0}\,c_{1}-z_{1}\,c_{0}=\begin{vmatrix}z_{0}&z_{1}\\
c_{0}&c_{1}\end{vmatrix}\ ,

for some linear forms
a_{i},b_{j},c_{k}
in
\mathbb{C}[t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}]
. Then, the rational map from
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
to
X
given by

(t_{0}:t_{1}:t_{2}:t_{3})\mapsto(a_{0}:a_{1})\times(b_{0}:b_{1})\times(c_{0}:c_{1})

must be
\phi^{-1}
, so
\phi
is birational of type
(1,1,1)
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

Notice that the proof of Proposition
6.2
is independent from the classification of §
5
. In particular, it is valid to use Proposition
6.2
in the proof of Theorem

. On the other hand, it follows from the proof that the base locus

of a birational map

is an arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay curve, i.e
.
the tri-homogeneous coordinate ring

is Cohen-Macaulay, which is stronger than requiring that

is Cohen-Macaulay.

Finally, we study the syzygies of birational maps of type

. In this case, the entries of
\phi
do not have syzygies of tri-degree
e_{1},e_{2}
, or
e_{3}
, and the analysis is more complicated. Moreover, the tri-degree of the syzygies alone is not enough to decide birationality, as it is shown by Example
6.9
. However, from Proposition
6.8
it follows that birationality can still be decided from the first syzygies, by checking a splitting condition on three related polynomials.
Before stating this characterization, we first establish some hypotheses on the first syzygies.

Setup 6.6
.

Assume that the
f_{i}
’s do not have syzygies of tri-degree
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
, or
e_{3}
but have a pair of independent syzygies in each of the tri-degrees
e_{1}+e_{2}
,
e_{1}+e_{3}
, and
e_{2}+e_{3}
. In particular, the syzygies of tri-degree
e_{1}+e_{2}
can be written as

(6.10)

x_{0}y_{0}\,g_{00}+x_{1}y_{0}\,g_{10}-x_{0}y_{1}\,g_{01}-x_{1}y_{1}\,g_{11}\ ,\ x_{0}y_{0}\,h_{00}+x_{1}y_{0}\,h_{10}-x_{0}y_{1}\,h_{01}-x_{1}y_{1}\,h_{11}\ ,

for some linear forms
g_{ij},h_{ij}
in
\mathbb{C}[t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}]
. We define
u_{xy}=u_{xy}(x_{i},t_{j})
as

u_{xy}\coloneqq\begin{vmatrix}x_{0}\,g_{00}+x_{1}g_{10}&x_{0}\,g_{01}+x_{1}g_{11}\\
x_{0}\,h_{00}+x_{1}h_{10}&x_{0}\,h_{01}+x_{1}h_{11}\end{vmatrix}\ .

The polynomial
u_{xy}

is non-zero, as the syzygies in (
6.10
) are independent by assumption.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}\,g_{00}+x_{1}g_{10}&x_{0}\,g_{01}+x_{1}g_{11}\\
x_{0}\,h_{00}+x_{1}h_{10}&x_{0}\,h_{01}+x_{1}h_{11}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y_{0}\\
y_{1}\end{pmatrix}\xmapsto{t_{j}\mapsto f_{j}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}

implying that
u_{xy}

is a relation in the Rees ideal
J
of multi-degree
(2,0,0;2)
.
Similarly, we define the relations
u_{yz},u_{zx}
from the pair of syzygies of tri-degree
e_{2}+e_{3}
and
e_{1}+e_{3}
, respectively. In particular, the multi-degree in
J
of
u_{yz}
(resp
.

u_{zx}
) is
(0,2,0;2)
(resp
.

(0,0,2;2)
).

Condition 6.7
.

Assuming Setup
6.6
, at least two of the
u_{xy},u_{yz},
and
u_{zx}
have a linear factor in
\{x_{0},x_{1}\}
,
\{y_{0},y_{1}\}
, and
\{z_{0},z_{1}\}
, respectively.

Proposition 6.8
.

Assume that
\phi
is dominant. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\phi
is birational of type
(2,2,2)
.

(2)

The
f_{i}
’s satisfy Condition
6.7
. In this case, the minimal tri-graded free resolution of the base ideal
B_{\phi}
is

(6.11)

0\rightarrow R(-2,-2,-2)^{3}\xrightarrow{}\begin{matrix}R(-2,-2,-1)^{2}\\
\oplus\\
R(-2,-1,-2)^{2}\\
\oplus\\
R(-1,-2,-2)^{2}\end{matrix}\xrightarrow{}R(-1,-1,-1)^{4}\xrightarrow{(f_{0}\ f_{1}\ f_{2}\ f_{3})}B_{\phi}\xrightarrow{}0\ .

Proof.

The implication from
(1)
to
(2)
follows from the computation of the minimal tri-graded free resolutions of the base ideal of all the representatives listed in Theorem
5.8
, and the polynomials
u_{xy}
,
u_{yz}
, and
u_{zx}
always have a linear factor as in Condition
6.7
(these computations have been performed with the help of
Macaulay2
).

Now, we assume Condition
6.7
.
Without loss of generality, the polynomials
u_{xy}
and
u_{yz}
have linear factors
l_{1}=l_{1}(x_{0},x_{1})
and
l_{2}=l_{2}(y_{0},y_{1})
, i.e
.
we can write

(6.12)

u_{xy}=l_{1}(x_{0},x_{1})\,\begin{vmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}\\
a_{0}&a_{1}\end{vmatrix}\ ,\ u_{yz}=l_{2}(y_{0},y_{1})\,\begin{vmatrix}y_{0}&y_{1}\\
b_{0}&b_{1}\end{vmatrix}\ ,

for some quadratic forms
a_{i},b_{j}
in
\mathbb{C}[t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}]
. Let
P
given a general point in
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
. We now prove that
\phi^{-1}(P)
has a unique preimage in the open set
U\subset X
where
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
are both non-zero.
Given
Q\in U\cap\phi^{-1}(P)
, the point
Q\times P\in U\times\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
must vanish the two polynomials in (
6.12
). By assumption we have
l_{1}(Q)\not=0
and
l_{2}(Q)\not=0
, implying that
Q\times P
must vanish the second factor in each polynomial. In particular,
Q
must be of the form

Q=(\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{1})\times(\mu_{0}:\mu_{1})\times(z_{0}:z_{1})
for some
(z_{0}:z_{1})\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
, where
(\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{1}),(\mu_{0}:\mu_{1})\in\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
are the projective points satisfying

\begin{vmatrix}\lambda_{0}&\lambda_{1}\\
a_{0}(P)&a_{1}(P)\end{vmatrix}=0\ ,\ \begin{vmatrix}\mu_{0}&\mu_{1}\\
b_{0}(P)&b_{1}(P)\end{vmatrix}=0\ .

Repeating the argument in the proof of Proposition
6.4
with the restriction map
\phi^{\prime}
, it follows that the pullback
\phi^{-1}(P)
defines a unique point in
U
, so
\phi
is birational. On the other hand there are no syzygies of tri-degree
e_{1},e_{2}
or
e_{3}
, so the type of
\phi
must be
(2,2,2)
.
∎
