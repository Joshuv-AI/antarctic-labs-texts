Variational minimization scheme for the one-particle reduced density matrix functional theory in the ensemble N-representability domain
Matthieu Vladaj, Quentin Marécat, Bruno Senjean, Matthieu Saubanère

Abstract
The one-particle reduced density-matrix (1-RDM) functional theory is a promising alternative to density-functional theory (DFT) that uses the 1-RDM rather than the electronic density as a basic variable. However, long-standing challenges such as the lack of Kohn–Sham scheme and the complexity of the pure N N -representability conditions are still impeding its wild utilization. Fortunately, ensemble N N -representability conditions derived in the natural orbital basis are known and trivial, such that almost every functionals of the 1-RDM are actually natural orbital functionals which do not perform well for all the correlation regimes. In this work, we propose a variational minimization scheme in the ensemble N N -representable domain that is not restricted to the natural orbital representation of the 1-RDM. We show that splitting the minimization into the diagonal and off-diagonal part of the 1-RDM can open the way toward the development of functionals of the orbital occupations, which remains a challenge for the generalization of site-occupation functional theory in chemistry. Our approach is tested on the uniform Hubbard model using the Müller and the Töws–Pastor functionals, as well as on the dihydrogen molecule using the Müller functional.



I
Introduction

Over the last two decades, density functional theory (DFT) has played a major role in the
study of the physico-chemical properties of molecules and materials.
The key idea behind DFT, formulated by Hohenberg and Kohn
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
, is to replace the
N
-electron wavefunction by the electronic density as the fundamental variable of the many-electron problem.
Within the Kohn–Sham (KS) decomposition scheme
Kohn and Sham 1965
,
the electronic repulsion
effects are treated implicitly by a Hartree-exchange-correlation (Hxc) functional of the density, thus leading to a good ratio between
computational cost and accuracy.
However, despite the numerous Hxc functionals developed
in the past decades
Marques et al. 2012
;
Mardirossian and Head-Gordon 2017
,
KS-DFT still faces issues when dealing with systems that exhibit strong (or static) correlation effects
Cramer and Truhlar 2009
;
Cohen et al. 2008
.
Improvements have been developed
recently by considering fractional spin and charge corrections
Li et al. 2018
;
Su et al. 2018
,
or by using the many-electron expansion
Zhu et al. 2016
.
Another interesting approach is to construct the adiabatic connection from the interacting to the strong-interaction limit of KS-DFT
Seidl et al. 1999
;
Seidl 1999
;
Gori-Giorgi et al. 2009
;
Malet and Gori-Giorgi 2012
;
Friesecke et al. 2022
.

Alternatively,
replacing the density by the one-particle reduced density-matrix (1-RDM) as the fundamental
variable leads to the 1-RDM functional theory (1-RDMFT)
Löwdin 1955
;
Gilbert 1975
;
Coleman 1963
;
Haar 1961

and has several advantages.
First, kinetic and exchange energy contributions are expressed exactly and analytically with respect to the 1-RDM.
Second, fractional occupations of the orbitals are naturally obtained within 1-RDMFT, thus allowing for the proper description of bond dissociation where static correlation effects are significant.
Several works have used the advantages
of 1- and 2-RDMFT to describe
strong correlation in the framework of KS-DFT
Zhang and Su 2023
;
Gibney et al. 2023
.
Despite these advantages, 1-RDMFT does not yet compete with DFT, partially due to the lack of an efficient scheme as provided by Kohn and Sham.
Indeed, the non-idempotent 1-RDM of an interacting system cannot be mapped to its non-interacting equivalent that is idempotent by construction, as it results from a single-electron Schrödinger equation
Piris and Pernal 2017
.
Besides, the proper minimization of the energy functional of the 1-RDM is in principle highly non-trivial as it requires maintaining the
pure

N
-representability conditions of the 1-RDM, also known as the
generalized Pauli constraints (GPCs)
Altunbulak and Klyachko 2008
;
Schilling et al. 2018
;
Castillo et al. 2023
).
Indeed, the number of GPCs increases significantly with the number of electrons and orbitals
Theophilou et al. 2015a
,
although it can be drastically reduced by introducing
physically motivated ansatz, as suggested by Gritsenko and Pernal
Gritsenko and Pernal 2019
.
In practice, the 1-RDM is usually written in the natural orbital (NO) representation,
and the minimization is performed via a Langrangian-based system of differential equations where both the NOs and their occupation numbers are optimized while satisfying the more trivial
ensemble

N
-representability conditions
Coleman 1963
;
Coleman 1972
;
Valone 1980
,
which can coincide with the
pure
N
-representable set of 1RDMs in some
specific cases
Schilling and Schilling 2019
.
Because these conditions are known in the NO basis,
Hxc functionals
are almost all NO-functionals
(NOFs)
Müller 1984
;
Csányi and Arias 2000
;
Csányi et al. 2002
;
Buijse and Baerends 2002
;
Sharma et al. 2008
;
Lathiotakis et al. 2009
;
Goedecker and Umrigar 1998
;
Holas 1999
;
Cioslowski and Pernal 1999
;
Cioslowski et al. 2023
.
The most recent and efficient ones are
the
Piris natural orbitals functionals (PNOFs)
Piris and Otto 2003
;
Piris 2004
;
Leiva and Piris 2005
;
Piris 2006
;
Piris et al. 2007
;
Piris and Ugalde 2009
;
Piris et al. 2010
;
Piris et al. 2011
;
Piris 2013
;
Piris and Ugalde 2014
;
Lopez et al. 2015

based on the cumulants
Kutzelnigg and Mukherjee 1999
.
Note that despite promising results, NOFs do not perform well for all the correlation regimes
Ramos-Cordoba et al. 2015
;
Cioslowski et al. 2015
;
Mitxelena et al. 2017
;
Rodriguez-Mayorga et al. 2017
;
Mitxelena et al. 2018
, although recent progress are worth mentioning
Piris 2021
.

Solving the aforementioned non-linear differential equations is computationally demanding, so that alternative strategies have been developed to define efficient and robust algorithms to perform the energy functional minimization.
Gilbert proposed to determine the NOs using a non-local potential for functionals
that are explicitly dependent on the 1-RDM
Gilbert 1975
,
whose existence was later shown by Pernal in the case of NOFs
Pernal 2005
.
In this approach, partially occupied orbitals are all degenerate.
A faster scheme for the NO optimization is based on a hermitian matrix built from the Lagrange multiplier, as proposed by Piris and Ugalde
Piris and Ugalde 2009
.
One can also use the Hessian and
couple the optimization of the NOs and occupation numbers
Castillo et al. 2023
.
Another interesting scheme called the local 1-RDMFT was
developed by Lathiotakis and coworkers,
where the minimization of the NOs is restricted to a domain where the orbitals are eigenfunctions of a single-electron Hamiltonian with a local potential, thus
bridging the 1-RDMFT and KS-DFT approaches
Lathiotakis et al. 2014
;
Theophilou et al. 2015b
.
Written in the NO representation, these methods
as well as methods based on an analogy with the temperature
are particularly suited for NOFs
Goedecker and Colombo 1994
;
Baldsiefen and Gross 2013
;
Saubanère et al. 2016
;
Wang and Baerends 2022
.
Indeed, in that case the energy does only depend explicitly on the occupation numbers while
the dependence on the NOs is implicitly accounted to in the Hartree and exchange integrals.
Alternatively,
minimization schemes based on a different representation than natural orbitals were also proposed, for instance
using projected gradient-based algorithms constrained to preserve ensemble
N
-representability conditions
Cancès and Pernal 2008
.

In this work, we introduce a new variational minimization scheme so-called density-matrix interpolation variational ansatz (DIVA) which relies on remarkable topological properties of the ensemble
N
-representable set of 1-RDM.
In contrast to the usual minimization schemes adapted to
NOFs, DIVA aims to minimize the energy functional of the 1-RDM expressed in any orthonormal orbital basis, thus
motivating the development of new 1-RDM functionals beyond
NOFs.
By separating the minimization over
the diagonal and off-diagonal part of the 1-RDM,
one can also make a connection between
1-RDMFT and DFT in a lattice, also called site-occupation functional theory (SOFT)
Gunnarsson and Schönhammer 1986
;
Schönhammer et al. 1995
;
Lima et al. 2002
;
Lima et al. 2003
.
We show how this separation can be used to estimate Hartree Hxc potentials,
thus paving the way toward the development of functionals of
the orbital occupations which is the current limitation for the implementation of SOFT in chemistry
Fromager 2015
;
Coe 2019
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we give a brief introduction to 1-RDMFT. The separation of the minimization process into
that bridges 1-RDMFT and SOFT
is then presented in Sec.
III
.
Our variational minimization strategy and its various implementations are described in Sec.
IV
,
and tested on the Hubbard model and the hydrogen molecule
in Sec.
V
.
Finally, conclusions and perspectives are provided in Sec.
VI
.
Note that in the following, we will use ‘1-RDM’ and ‘density matrix’ to refer to the same object, i.e. the one-particle reduced density matrix.

II
Reduced density-matrix functional theory

Let us consider the second-quantized electronic Hamiltonian written in an orthonormal basis set, referred to as the computational basis set and composed of
N
spin-orbitals
\{\varphi_{i}({\bf{x}})\}
that are different from the set of natural spin-orbitals
\{\phi_{i}({\bf{x}})\}
,

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{T}+\hat{U}

(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}^{N}t_{ij}\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}+\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{ijkl}^{N}U_{ijkl}\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{k}\hat{c}_{l},

where
\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}
(
\hat{c}_{i}
) corresponds to the creation (annihilation) of an electron in
\varphi_{i}({\bf{x}})
.
The one-body integrals

\displaystyle t_{ij}=\int{\rm d}{\bf{x}}\varphi_{i}^{*}({\bf{x}})\left(-\dfrac{\nabla^{2}}{2}+\sum_{I}\dfrac{Z_{I}}{|\mathbf{r}-{\bf R}_{I}|}\right)\varphi_{j}({\bf{x}})

(2)

contain the kinetic and external potential contributions, while the two-body integrals

\displaystyle U_{ijkl}=\int{\rm d}{\bf{x}}_{1}{\rm d}{\bf{x}}_{2}\dfrac{\varphi_{i}^{*}({\bf{x}}_{1})\varphi_{j}^{*}({\bf{x}}_{2})\varphi_{k}({\bf{x}}_{2})\varphi_{l}({\bf{x}}_{1})}{|\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}|}

(3)

contain contributions from the electron-electron interactions.
The summation in Eq. (
2
)
runs over the number of atoms at positions
{\mathbf{R}_{I}}
and
{\bf{x}}_{i}=(\mathbf{r}_{i},\sigma_{i})
corresponds to the position
\mathbf{r}_{i}
and spin
\sigma_{i}
of the electron
i
.

In 1-RDMFT, the basic variable
{\bm{\gamma}}
is the 1-RDM given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}=\langle\Psi|\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}|\Psi\rangle,

(4)

with
|\Psi\rangle
the ground-state wavefunction of
\hat{H}
.
The energy can in principle be written as a functional of the 1-RDM,

\displaystyle E[{\bm{\gamma}}]=T[{\bm{\gamma}}]+W[{\bm{\gamma}}],

(5)

where

\displaystyle T[{\bm{\gamma}}]=\sum_{ij}t_{ij}\gamma_{ij}

(6)

is an explicit
functional of the 1-RDM
and

W[{\bm{\gamma}}]

is the energy functional describing the electronic repulsion.
The analytical form of this functional remains unknown and is usually approximated, though it
can be written using the Levy–Lieb formalism as follows,

\displaystyle W[{\bm{\gamma}}]=\min_{\Psi\rightarrow{\bm{\gamma}}}\bra{\Psi}\hat{U}\ket{\Psi},

(7)

where the notation
\Psi\rightarrow{\bm{\gamma}}
refers to any wavefunction giving the one-particle density matrix
{\bm{\gamma}}
.
Within the variational principle of 1-RDMFT, the ground-state energy
E_{0}
can be obtained as a saddle point,

\displaystyle E_{\rm 0}=\min_{{\bm{\gamma}}}E[{\bm{\gamma}}].

(8)

More precisely,
as a corollary of the Hohenberg–Kohn theorem (later extended by Gilbert
Gilbert 1975
to non-local potential),
there exists a one-to-one mapping between the ground-state wavefunction and the 1-RDM, thus allowing to determine the ground-state energy variationally.
The conditions for
{\bm{\gamma}}
to derive from a
N_{e}
-electron wavefunction – so-called
N
-representability conditions – are highly non-trivial
Altunbulak and Klyachko 2008
;
Schilling et al. 2018
;
Castillo et al. 2023
and cannot be used in practice to define the domain in which the minimization of Eq. (
8
) takes place.
However, the conditions for
{\bm{\gamma}}
to be ensemble
N
-representable are known and straightforward
Coleman 1963

and are given as follows,

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\eta_{k}=N_{e},\;\;0\leq\eta_{k}\leq 1,\end{split}

(9)

where
\eta_{k}
is the occupation number of the
k
-th natural orbital.
Hence, it is common to use
the natural orbital representation of the density
matrix, i.e.
{\bm{\gamma}}\equiv(\{\eta_{k}\},\{\ket{\phi_k}\})
.
As originally proposed by Gilbert
Gilbert 1975
, these conditions are fulfilled during the minimization by introducing a Lagrangian function in the ensemble
N
-representable domain,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E[\{\eta_{k}\},\{\ket{\phi_k}\}]-\mu\bigg(\sum_{k}\cos^{2}(\theta_{k})-N_{e}\bigg)

(10)

\displaystyle-\sum_{jk}\lambda_{jk}\bigg(\langle\phi_{j}|\phi_{k}\rangle-\delta_{jk}\bigg),

where
\mu
and
\{\lambda_{jk}\}
are Lagrange multipliers, the first term (
\eta_{k}=\cos^{2}(\theta_{k})
) fulfills Eq. (
9
) and the second term the orthonormality of the natural orbitals
\{|\phi_{k}\rangle\}
.
Minimizing Eq. (
10
) with respect to the whole set of occupation numbers and NOs leads to two non-linear differential equations,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial\theta_{k}}=\left(\mu-\dfrac{\partial E}{\partial\eta_{k}}\right)\sin(2 \theta_k)=0,

(11)

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial\langle\phi_{k}|}=\dfrac{\partial E}{\partial\langle\phi_{k}|}-\sum_{j}\lambda_{jk}|\phi_{j}\rangle=0,

(12)

that are solved successively but with slow convergence
Cartier and Giesbertz 2024
.
This situation contrasts with the variational equations
in KS-DFT and SOFT that are solved efficiently through the definition of a single-particle Schrödinger equation
Kohn and Sham 1965
;
Gunnarsson and Schönhammer 1986
.

III
Link between 1-RDMFT and SOFT

Rather than decomposing the 1-RDM into
natural orbitals and occupation numbers,
we decompose it in terms of its diagonal elements,
i.e. orbital (or site) occupations,

{\bf{n}}\equiv\{\gamma_{ii}\}

and the
N(N-1)
(not nil in general) off-diagonal elements denoted by
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}
such that
{\bm{\gamma}}={\bf{n}}\cdot\mathbb{1}+{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}
, where
\mathbb{1}
refers to the identity matrix.
Note that
Tr.({\bf{n}})=Tr.(\bm{\eta})=N_{e}
, the number of electrons, and
n_{i}=\sum_{k}\eta_{k}|u_{ik}|^{2}
where
{\bf{u}}
corresponds to the eigenvector matrix of
{\bm{\gamma}}
.
Hence,
{\bf{n}}
is a vector of size
N
that by definition differs from the occupation number vector
\bm{\eta}
, for instance each
n_{i}
can be fractional even if
{\bm{\gamma}}
is idempotent.
The energy functional in
Eq. (
5
) therefore
reads

E[{\bm{\gamma}}]=T[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]+W[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}].

(13)

Following the KS decomposition, Eq. (
13
) can be alternatively written as

\displaystyle E[{\bm{\gamma}}]=T_{s}[{\bf{n}}]+F[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}],

(14)

where
T_{s}[{\bf{n}}]
represents the one-body (kinetic and potential) energy contributions of the non-interacting system with orbital occupations
{\bf{n}}
, i.e.
T_{s}[{\bf{n}}]=\langle\Phi|\hat{T}|\Phi\rangle
differs from
T[{\bm{\gamma}}]
as
|\Phi\rangle
corresponds to a single Slater determinant such that
\langle\Phi|\hat{{\bf{n}}}|\Phi\rangle={\bf{n}}
.

F[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]
is a functional containing all non trivial terms defined by

\displaystyle F[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]=T[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]-T_{s}[{\bf{n}}]+W[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}].

(15)

In analogy with the natural orbital representation, the minimization of the energy leads to two saddle-point equations.
The first saddle-point equation reads

\displaystyle\delta{\bf{n}}\left\{\frac{\delta T_{s}[{\bf{n}}]}{\delta{\bf{n}}}+\left.\frac{\delta F[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]}{\delta{\bf{n}}}\right|_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}}\right\}=0,

(16)

and leads to a pseudo Kohn-Sham (pKS) equation:

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{\rm pKS}[{\bf{n}}]|\Phi^{\rm pKS}\rangle=\left(\hat{T}+\hat{V}_{{\rm Hxc}}[{\bf{n}}]\right)|\Phi^{\rm pKS}\rangle=\mathcal{E}^{\rm pKS}|\Phi^{\rm pKS}\rangle.

(17)

Eq. (
17
) is similar to the self-consistent equation in SOFT, where
\hat{h}_{\rm pKS}[{\bf{n}}]
corresponds to an effective non-interacting Hamiltonian.
However, in contrast to SOFT,
the Hxc potential
{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}[{\bf{n}}]
is defined through the full interacting 1-RDM, at the saddle point of
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}
,

\displaystyle{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}[{\bf{n}}]:=\left.{\bf{V}}_{\rm Hxc}[{\bf{n}}]\right|_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}}=\left.\frac{\delta F[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]}{\delta{\bf{n}}}\right|_{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}}.

(18)

{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}[{\bf{n}}]
is an on-site (diagonal) one-body potential that imposes
the equality between the effective chemical potential
\mu^{\rm pKS}
and the interacting chemical potential
\mu=\partial E/\partial{\bf{n}}

at convergence, or, in other words,
n_{i}^{\rm pKS}=\gamma_{ii}^{\rm pKS}=\langle\Phi|\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i}|\Phi\rangle=n_{i}
.
Note however that the off-diagonal elements of the idempotent 1RDM associated to the pKS Hamiltonian differs from the off-diagonal element of the interacting non-idempotent 1-RDM,
\gamma_{ij}^{\rm pKS}=\langle\Phi^{\rm pKS}|\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}|\Phi^{\rm pKS}\rangle\neq\gamma_{ij}
if
i\neq j
.
As in SOFT, only the chemical potential and site (or orbital) occupations of the interacting system should be recovered from the pKS one.
Therefore, 1RDMs and more specifically natural orbitals and occupation numbers are different in both systems.
Eq. (
17
) is determined concomitantly with the complementary saddle point equation:

\displaystyle\delta{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}\left\{\left.\frac{\delta F[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]}{\delta{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}}\right|_{{\bf{n}}={\bf{n}}^{\rm pKS}}\right\}=0,

(19)

where
{\bf{n}}^{\rm pKS}
are the orbital/site occupations resulting from Eq. (
17
).
In practice, solving Eq. (
17
) appears numerically easy when
{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}[{\bf{n}}]

is known,
however solving Eq. (
19
) remains difficult, in particular due to the non-trivial representability conditions that

{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}
has to fulfill. In Sec.
IV.2.3
, we propose an algorithm to determine
{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}[{\bf{n}}]
from Eq. (
19
) such that Eqs. (
17
) and (
19
) can be solved iteratively.

From a more conceptual perspective, one can also use a Levy–Lieb formalism to derive similar equations,

\displaystyle E_{\rm 0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf{n}}}\left\{\min_{{\bm{\gamma}}\rightarrow{\bf{n}}}\left\{E[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]\right\}\right\}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf{n}}}\left\{\min_{{\bm{\gamma}}\rightarrow{\bf{n}}}\left\{T_{s}[{\bf{n}}]+F[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf{n}}}\left\{T_{s}[{\bf{n}}]+\min_{{\bm{\gamma}}\rightarrow{\bf{n}}}\left\{F[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]\right\}\right\},

where
{\bm{\gamma}}\rightarrow{\bf{n}}
corresponds to density matrices with orbital occupations
{\bf{n}}
.
In that framework, the Hxc potential is defined as

\displaystyle{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}[{\bf{n}}]=\frac{\delta}{\delta{\bf{n}}}\min_{{\bm{\gamma}}\rightarrow{\bf{n}}}\left\{F[{\bf{n}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}]\right\},

(21)

thus bridging 1-RDMFT and SOFT, where
the Hxc potential functional of the orbital occupations can be obtained after solving Eq. (
19
) for a fixed orbital occupation
{\bf{n}}
.

IV
Density-matrix Interpolation Variational
ansatz

IV.1
Topological properties of the ensemble
N
-representable domain of the 1-RDM

Let us consider the space
\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
of
N\times N
symmetric and real matrices,

N
being the number of spin-orbitals in the system.
In this section we denote by
\Omega_{\bm{\gamma}}
the domain of ensemble
N
-representable density matrices
that is included in
\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
,
\Omega_{\bm{\gamma}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
.
A significant advantage of working with natural orbitals
is that the
ensemble
N
-representable conditions are straightforward, i.e. a matrix
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
is ensemble
N
-representable if all the occupation numbers

\eta_{k}
fulfill

0\leq\eta_{k}\leq 1
.
Similarly, it means that
{\bm{\gamma}}
is ensemble
N
-representable if both
{\bm{\gamma}}
and
\mathbb{1}-{\bm{\gamma}}
are positive semi-definite, i.e. both showing positive or null eigenvalues
\eta_{k}\geq 0
and
1-\eta_{k}\geq 0
for all
1\leq k\leq N
.
Hence, the domain of ensemble
N
-representable density matrices reads

\displaystyle\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}=\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\;|\;\eta_{k}\geq 0\;\&\;1-\eta_{k}\geq 0,\;\forall k\right\}.

(22)

Interesting topological properties for the ensemble
N
-representable 1-RDM domain
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
follow from these definitions.
The first remarkable property of
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
is the
convexity
, see proofs in Appendix
A
.
It follows that given a set of ensemble
N
-representable matrices
\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}\in\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}\right\}
, all matrices constructed as

{\bm{\gamma}}({\bf{z}})=\sum_{s}z_{s}{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}

(23)

also belong to
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
if all
z_{s}\geq 0
and
\sum_{s}z_{s}=1
.

Let us now define
\mathring{\Omega}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
the ensemble of matrices
{\bm{\gamma}}
such that both
{\bm{\gamma}}
and
\mathbb{1}-{\bm{\gamma}}
are positive definite (in contrast to Eq. (
22
) where they are positive semi-definite), i.e.

\displaystyle\mathring{\Omega}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}=\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{R})\;|\;\eta_{k}>0\;\&\;1-\eta_{k}>0,\;\forall k\right\}.

(24)

The convexity of
\mathring{\Omega}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
follows the same proof as in Appendix
A
for
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
.
Besides, we introduce

\displaystyle\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}=\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}\,\backslash\,\mathring{\Omega}_{{\bm{\gamma}}},

(25)

such that
\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
contains the density matrices
{\bm{\gamma}}
that exhibit at least one integer eigenvalue
\eta_{k}=\{0,1\}
.
Contrary to
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
and
\mathring{\Omega}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
,
\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
is not convex.
We show in Appendix
B
that
\mathring{\Omega}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
corresponds to the topological interior of
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
,
\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
is the topological boundary of
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
and
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}=\mathring{\Omega}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}\cup\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
is a topological closed set.

Finally, let us define
\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}\subset\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
the set of idempotent matrices, i.e.
{\bm{\gamma}}={{\bm{\gamma}}}^{2}
if
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}

and

\displaystyle\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}=\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}\,|\,\eta_{k}=\left\{0,1\right\},\,\forall k\right\},

(26)

which is also non convex.

\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}
corresponds to the set of extreme points of
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
, i.e. given
{\bm{\gamma}}_{*}\in\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}
there is no
{\bm{\gamma}}^{A}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}^{B}
in
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
such that
{\bm{\gamma}}_{*}=z{\bm{\gamma}}^{A}+(1-z){\bm{\gamma}}^{B}
with
0<z<1
. Following the Krein–Milman theorem,
there exists a decomposition in terms of idempotent matrices, i.e. for any
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Omega_{\bm{\gamma}}
,

{\bm{\gamma}}({\bf{z}})=\sum_{s}z_{s}{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}_{*},

(27)

with
{\bf{z}}=\{z_{s}\}
,
\sum_{s}z_{s}=1
and
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}_{*}\in\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{*}
.
Given Eqs. (
8
) and (
27
),
it follows the variational ansatz:

E_{\rm 0}=\min_{{\bf{z}}}E[{\bm{\gamma}}({\bf{z}})].

(28)

Hence, the energy is minimized with respect to the weights
{\bf{z}}
defining the interpolation of
{\bm{\gamma}}({\bf{z}})

in terms of idempotent 1-RDM.
We show in Appendix
C
that for each
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\mathring{\Omega}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
, the sum in Eq. (
27
) runs up to
2^{N}
idempotent matrices. It follows that the number of variational parameters rapidly becomes prohibitive when the size of the system increases.
To reduce the number of parameters, one could define the trial set as every Slater determinants corresponding to the singly- and doubly-excited determinants with respect to the Hartree–Fock state.
The efficiency and accuracy of such ansatz strongly depends on the size and relevance of the trial set of
\{{\bm{\gamma}}_{*}^{(s)}\}
.

Rather than interpolating
{\bm{\gamma}}
in terms of
idempotent 1-RDM, we can use the property that for each
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
there exists a decomposition in terms of
boundary
matrices
belonging to
\delta\Omega_{\bm{\gamma}}
[see Eq. (
25
)].
Then, Eq. (
27
) turns to

{\bm{\gamma}}({\bf{z}})=\sum_{s}z_{s}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}.

(29)

As
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}
can be seen as a linear combination of
{\bm{\gamma}}_{*}^{(s)}
, one can expect the amount of variational parameters
\{z_{s}\}
to be drastically reduced
when too much
\{{\bm{\gamma}}_{*}^{(s)}\}
would be needed to reproduce
{\bm{\gamma}}
.
Indeed, any non-idempotent
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\mathring{\Omega}_{\bm{\gamma}}
can in principle be obtained
by interpolating only two boundary matrices.
Hence,
while the set of idempotent matrices
\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}_{*}\right\}
in Eq. (
27
) is
very large but straightforward to construct,
only a small relevant set of boundary matrices
\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}\right\}
would suffice but is non-trivial to find.
An efficient algorithm would consist in constructing this non-trivial set of relevant
\left\{{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}\right\}
on which the minimization in Eq. (
28
) is performed.
Eqs. (
28
) and (
29
) constitute the density-matrix interpolation variational ansatz (DIVA).
Note that by construction, all density matrices of the form given by Eq. (
29
) are ensemble
N
-representable.

Interestingly, the topological properties presented in
\mathcal{M}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
also hold if we restrain the considered metric space to
\mathcal{M}^{N_{e}}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{M}^{{\bf{n}}}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, corresponding to the spaces of

N\times N
symmetric and real matrices with a fixed trace (fixed number of electron
N_{e}
) and fixed diagonal part
{\bf{n}}
, respectively.
Considering these restrictions should automatically reduce the number of trial 1-RDMs in Eq. (
29
).

IV.2
Iterative gradient-based implementations of DIVA

Let us now turn to the numerical implementations of DIVA.
Starting from a guess
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(0)}
that already belongs to
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
,
we want to construct iteratively a minimal trial set of boundary density matrices to minimize the 1-RDMFT energy [Eq. (
28
)]
according to Eq. (
29
),

{\bm{\gamma}}({\bf{z}})=z_{0}{\bm{\gamma}}^{(0)}+\sum_{s}z_{s}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}.

(30)

In the following, we highlight two ways of minimizing the energy functional within DIVA.
The first one consists in minimizing the energy functional
with respect to only one variational parameter
z_{i}
at each iteration
i
and is called the mono-parameter DIVA.
For the second one, the minimization is done over the whole set of parameters
\{z_{r}\}_{r\leq s}

at iteration
s
, and is called the multi-parameter DIVA. These approaches are pictured in Figs.
1
and
2
and detailed below.

\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}

\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
(blue circle) and the topological interior. Red arrows represent the direction of the energy gradient, used to find the
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}
belonging to the border of the representability domain (
\delta\Omega_{\bm{\gamma}}
). Each
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}
is obtained as an interpolation between
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s-1)}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s-1)}
.
The star represents the ground-state 1-RDM
{\bm{\gamma}}_{0}
(at the level of the functional approximation)
that is recovered after convergence.

Figure 2:
Illustration of the multi-parameter DIVA. The set
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
is illustrated by the union of
\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
(blue circle) and the topological interior. Red arrows represent the direction of the energy gradient, used to find the
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}
belonging to the border of the representability domain (
\delta\Omega_{\bm{\gamma}}
). Each
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s>2)}
is a linear combination of all
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(r)}
with
r<s
. The star represents the ground-state 1-RDM
{\bm{\gamma}}_{0}
(at the level of the functional approximation) that is recovered after convergence.

IV.2.1
Mono-parameter DIVA

The mono-parameter DIVA is implemented according to the following steps.

1.

Initialization (iteration
s=0
): set
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(0)}
to be the
(idempotent) density matrix coming from an Hartree–Fock or KS-DFT calculation,
thus ensuring that
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(0)}
belongs to
\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
,
and even to
\delta\Omega_{\bm{\gamma}}^{*}\subset\delta\Omega_{\bm{\gamma}}\subset\Omega_{\bm{\gamma}}
.
To avoid divergence of the energy gradient
with respect to
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(0)}
having occupation numbers equal to 0, which happens for many NOFs, the first gradient is set to
\nabla_{ij}[{\bm{\gamma}}]=(1-\delta_{ij})\gamma_{ij}^{(0)}
.

2.

Search the boundary density matrix
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}\in\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}

following the opposite direction of the energy gradient. This is done in two steps:

(a)

search for
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\rm out}={\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}-\theta{\bm{\nabla}}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}]
by incrementing
\theta
until
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\rm out}\notin\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}

(b)

Once
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\rm out}
is found, vary
\theta
using a bracketing method until
{\bm{\gamma}}_{\rm out}\in\delta\Omega_{{\bm{\gamma}}}
and set
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}={\bm{\gamma}}_{\rm out}

3.

start a new iteration,
s=s+1

4.

Interpolate between
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s-1)}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s-1)}
to find
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}=(1-z_{s}){\bm{\gamma}}^{(s-1)}+z_{s}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s-1)}
such that

\dfrac{\partial E[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}]}{\partial z_{s}}=0.

(31)

5.

Compute the energy gradient
{\bm{\nabla}}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}]
. Note that
the number of electrons is conserved by setting the diagonal part
\nabla_{ii}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}]=\nabla_{ii}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}]-\mu^{(s)}
with
\mu^{(s)}=(1/N)\sum_{i}\nabla_{ii}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}]
.
If the orbital occupations should be kept unchanged, set
\nabla_{ii}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}]=0
,
\forall i
.
Note that at convergence,
\mu^{(s)}
corresponds to the chemical potential and
\nabla_{ii}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}]-\mu^{(s)}\rightarrow 0
,
thus reflecting the equalization of the chemical potentials in the system.

6.

If
\delta E=|E^{(s-1)}-E^{(s)}|<\upsilon
and
\delta{\bm{\gamma}}=|{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s-1)}-{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}|<\varepsilon

where
\upsilon
and
\varepsilon
are the convergence criteria, set
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\rm opt}={\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}
, which should be equal
to the ground-state 1-RDM
{\bm{\gamma}}_{0}
corresponding
to the level of approximation of the chosen 1-RDM functional (the star in Fig.
1
).
Else, go to step 2.

At convergence, the density matrix fulfills Eq. (
29
),
{\bm{\gamma}}({\bf{z}})=\sum_{s}^{M}Z_{s}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}
, with
Z_{s}=z_{s+1}\prod_{s+2}^{M}(1-z_{s})
and
M
the number of iterations needed to achieve convergence.
Following Ref.
Cancès and Pernal 2008
,
step 2 of the algorithm
could be made more efficient by
projecting
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}
to the boundary domain
\delta\Omega_{\bm{\gamma}}
.
However, it would lead to a
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s)}
that deviates from the direction pointed by the gradient.
Also, the opposite energy gradient direction can be replaced by the conjugate gradient.
This is what we implemented in this work,
as we observed a much faster convergence using it.

IV.2.2
Multi-parameter DIVA

Apart from step 4, the multi-parameter approach remains the same as the mono-parameter one.
Rather than doing the interpolation between
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s-1)}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(s-1)}
only,
the interpolation is done over the whole set
of previously determined
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(r)}
with
r<s
:

{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}=z^{(0)}{\bm{\gamma}}^{(0)}+\sum_{r=1}^{s-1}z_{r}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}^{(r)}.

(32)

The minimization is done over the whole set of parameters
{\bf{z}}=(z_{0},\dots,z_{(s-1)})
,

\dfrac{\partial E[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}]}{\partial{\bf{z}}}=0.

(33)

In contrast to the previous implementation, we expect to achieve a much faster convergence at the expense of more expensive optimizations of the
{\bf{z}}
parameters.

IV.2.3
SOFT-DIVA

According to Sec.
III
the variational minimization of the energy can be split in two iterative sub-processes.
The first sub-process optimizes orbital/site occupations
{\bf{n}}
through a pseudo-KS eigenvalue problem [see Eq. (
17
)], while the second sub-process
optimizes the off-diagonal elements of the 1-RDM
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq}
[see Eq. (
19
)]
and can be performed within the DIVA framework.
Consequently the SOFT-DIVA algorithm can be implemented by performing the following steps.

1.

Initialize
{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}[{\bf{n}}]^{(s)}
at iteration
s=1
. In practice we set the diagonal potential
{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}[{\bf{n}}]^{(s)}
to be equal to the diagonal part of the XC potential obtained from standard DFT approximation.

2.

Plug
{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}[{\bf{n}}]^{(s)}
into the single-particle Hamiltonian (or Fock matrix) to generate a new idempotent 1-RDM
{\bm{\gamma}}^{{\rm pKS}(s)}
and thus a new set of orbital occupations
{\bf{n}}^{{\rm pKS}(s)}\equiv\{\gamma_{ii}^{{\rm pKS}(s)}\}
. The associated chemical potential
\mu^{{\rm pKS}(s)}
can be defined as the highest occupied molecular orbital energy of the pKS system.

3.

Optimize off diagonal element
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq(s)}
, see Eq. (
19
). To that aim, initialize with
\bm{\gamma}^{\rm pKS}
a DIVA process as described in Sec.
IV.2.1
or
IV.2.2

and set the diagonal part of the energy gradient to zero, such that the orbital occupations remain fixed and equal to

{\bf{n}}^{{\rm pKS}(s)}
(see step 5 in Sec.
IV.2.1
)
along the full DIVA process.
DIVA will converge to an optimal density matrix of the form
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}={\bf{n}}^{\rm pKS(s)}\cdot\mathbb{1}+{\bm{\gamma}}^{\neq(s)}
.

4.

Self-consistent condition. Compute the energy gradient vector
{\rm diag}({\bm{\nabla}}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}])=\{\nabla_{ii}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}]\}_{i}
obtained at the end of step 3:
This gradient is used to defined an updated
{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}^{(s+1)}[{\bf{n}}]
as

\displaystyle{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}^{(s+1)}[{\bf{n}}]\equiv{\rm diag}({\bm{\nabla}}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}])-\dfrac{\delta T_{s}^{(s)}[{\bf{n}}]}{\delta{\bf{n}}}.

(34)

\delta T_{s}^{(s)}[{\bf{n}}]/\delta{\bf{n}}

can be computed using the non-interacting pKS system as

\displaystyle\dfrac{\delta T_{s}^{(s)}[{\bf{n}}]}{\delta{\bf{n}}}=\mu^{{\rm pKS}(s)}\cdot\mathbb{1}_{1}-{\bf{V}}_{{{\rm Hxc}}}^{(s)}[{\bf{n}}]

(35)

where
\mathbb{1}_{1}
stands for the vector of size
N
filled with 1.
This finally leads to

\displaystyle{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}^{(s+1)}[{\bf{n}}]={\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}^{(s)}[{\bf{n}}]+{\rm diag}({\bm{\nabla}}[{\bm{\gamma}}^{(s)}])-\mu^{{\rm pKS}(s)}\cdot\mathbb{1}_{1}.

(36)

If
{\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}^{(s+1)}[{\bf{n}}]={\bf{V}}_{{\rm Hxc}}^{(s)}[{\bf{n}}]
convergence is reached since the interacting chemical potential
\mu
is equal to the pKS chemical potential
\mu=\mu^{\rm pKS}
.
Else,
s\rightarrow s+1
and go back to step 2.

We stress that the 1-RDM
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\rm pKS}
obtained at step 2 of the SOFT-DIVA algorithm is not diagonal but idempotent and shows equivalent diagonal element as the interacting non-idempotent 1-RDM constructed in step 3 of the SOFT-DIVA algorithm.

V
Results and discussions

We propose to study the performance of DIVA on the
one-dimensional (1D) Hubbard model and on a simple molecular system.
For the 1D Hubbard model, in the spirit of Mitxelena
et al.

Mitxelena et al. 2017
, we compare
a NOF (the Müller functional) with a functional derived in lattice representation such as proposed by Töws and Pastor to approximate the correlation energy
Töws and Pastor 2011
;
Töws and Pastor 2012
;
Töws et al. 2014
. For molecular systems, only NOFs are available and we also employ the Müller functional.
In order to be self-contained, we provide in Appendix
D
a brief description of the functionals used in the manuscript.

V.1
Hubbard model

The homogeneous Hubbard model can be defined by the following Hamiltonian,

\hat{H}=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j\sigma}+\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{i\sigma}

(37)

Focusing on the homogeneous model allows us to bypass

In practice, chains with 202 sites have been considered with periodic boundary conditions, and the bisection algorithm has been employed for bracketing and line minimization in steps 2b and 4, respectively (see Sec.
IV.2.1
).
We have considered the Müller functional, defined as a function of the natural orbital occupation number
(Blöch function for periodic systems),
and the Töws–Pastor functional that is defined in the lattice site representation.
Note that the Müller functional has been investigated in the context of finite Hubbard
chains
Kamil et al. 2016
;
Mitxelena et al. 2017

Töws and Pastor 2011

In terms of performance, we found that fast convergence is achieved with DIVA (5 iteration max to reach an energy difference of
\delta E/t<10^{-7}
) for both functionals.
However, computational time is smaller for the Töws–Pastor functional compared to the Müller one.
Indeed, for the Müller functional depending only on natural orbital occupation numbers, a standard minimization algorithm in the natural orbital representation appears well-suited
Piris and Ugalde 2009
, while a minimization scheme directly in the lattice representation such as DIVA appears more adapted for the Töws–Pastor functional.
For the latter, the computation of the energy and energy gradients (needed several time at each minimization step) do not require to switch between lattice and natural orbital representations.

It shows that the Töws–Pastor functional is in fair agreement with the exact Bethe–Ansatz (BA) solution

U/t

On the contrary, the Müller functional leads to inaccurate energies away from the half-band filling and especially for large
U/t

Note that this wrong behaviour goes together with
an unphysical negative value of
\partial E/\partial U

These unphysical results for the Müller functional arises from equivalent spin on-site exchange contributions to the energy, associated with the operator
\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i\sigma}\hat{c}_{i\sigma}

Turning to the three-quarter band filling, this behavior remains, however with large discrepancies between the Töws–Pastor and the Müller functional.

As expected, the multi-parameter implementation converges in much fewer iterations than the mono-parameter one, although it does not mean that it is
computationally faster than the mono-parameter DIVA.
Indeed, each iteration of mono-parameter DIVA has the same cost, i.e. the cost of the optimization of a single variational parameter
z
.
On the contrary, the number of parameters to optimize in multi-parameter DIVA increases at each additional iteration.
Hence, there is a trade-off between the number of iterations and the cost of optimizing many parameters at once.
Note that in this work, multi-parameter DIVA was always computationally much faster than mono-parameter DIVA.
Besides, the former always converges in around 20 iterations
while thousands of iterations are sometimes required using mono-parameter DIVA for some interatomic distances.
This very slow convergence has also been observed in other gradient-based algorithms and is attributed to the fact that NOFs are not proper functionals of the 1RDM, since these functionals are not invariant with respect to the rotation of two orbitals having degenerate occupation numbers
Cancès and Pernal 2008
.
We believe that the convergence of mono-parameter DIVA could be significantly improved by using analytical gradients.
Alternatively, considering a simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation
can reduce significantly the number of energy evaluations needed to compute the numerical gradient, compared to standard finite difference.
This is left for future work.

VI
Conclusions and perspectives

We have introduced a new variational minimization scheme for 1-RDMFT called density-matrix interpolation variational ansatz (DIVA) that works in any orbital basis representation while satisfying the ensemble
N
-representability conditions.
We have derived different implementations
and successfully tested them on the uniform Hubbard model with
the Müller and the Töws–Pastor functionals,
as well as the hydrogen molecule with the Müller functional.
In summary,
every implementations give the correct 1-RDM and energy.
The mono-parameter DIVA converges in a few iterations for the Hubbard model, but exhibits very slow convergence for the hydrogen molecule.
The multi-parameter DIVA led to a much faster convergence,
at the expense of more involved optimizations of the variational parameters.
Finally, by allowing the estimation of the
exchange and correlation potential for a given orbital occupation vector, the SOFT-DIVA implementation contributes to the development of orbital occupation functionals for chemistry.
Besides, we stress that DIVA is more adapted to the computational basis representation, as it would require less swapping between the computational and the NO basis for the calculation of energy and energy gradients.
As also mentioned by Cancès and Pernal
Cancès and Pernal 2008
such type of algorithm is expected to perform efficiently
for proper functionals of the 1RDM, that can be expressed in any orthonormal orbital
basis.
To date, only the Hartree–Fock functional fulfills this property.
Hence, this work motivates the future development of proper functionals of the 1RDM, beyond NOFs.
The implementation of analytical gradients will also be
