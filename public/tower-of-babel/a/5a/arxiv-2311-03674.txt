A midsurface elasticity model for a thin, nonlinear, gradient elastic plate
C. Rodriguez

Abstract
. In this paper, we derive a dynamic surface elasticity model for the two-dimensional midsurface of a thin, three-dimensional, homogeneous, isotropic, nonlinear gradient elastic plate of thickness h h . The resulting model is parameterized by five, conceivably measurable, physical properties of the plate, and the stored surface energy reduces to Koiter’s plate energy in a singular limiting case. The model corrects a theoretical issue found in wave propagation in thin sheets and, when combined with the author’s theory of Green elastic bodies possessing gradient elastic material boundary surfaces, removes the singularities present in fracture within traditional/classical models. Our approach diverges from previous research on thin shells and plates, which primarily concentrated on deriving elasticity theories for material surfaces from classical three-dimensional Green elasticity. This work is the first in rigorously developing a surface elasticity model based on a parent nonlinear gradient elasticity theory.

1.2.
Gradient elasticity

We now give a brief overview of (second-) gradient elasticity and our motivation for this work. In what follows,
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{\chi}:\mathcal{B}\times[t_{0},t_{1}]\rightarrow\mathbb{E}^{3}
is a smooth motion of a body with reference configuration
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{E}^{3}
, reference density
\rho_{R}:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow(0,\infty)

\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x},t)

V
and kinetic energy
T
of a part
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
at time
t
are of the form,

\displaystyle V(\mathcal{P})=\int_{\mathcal{P}}W(\boldsymbol{F})dV,\quad T(\mathcal{P})=\int_{\mathcal{P}}\frac{1}{2}\rho_{R}|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\chi}|^{2}dV,

(1.6)

where
\boldsymbol{F}=\partial_{X^{a}}\chi^{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}^{a}
is the deformation gradient. The equations of motion neglecting external body forces and boundary tractions can then be obtained via applying Hamilton’s variational principle to the action functional with Lagrangian density
L=T-V
.

Gradient elasticity
is a subtheory of gradient continuum mechanics in which the kinetic energy and stored energy densities can also depend on the spatial derivatives of
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\chi}
and
\boldsymbol{F}
respectively
[
28
]
. As before, the equations of motion are then obtained via Hamilton’s variational principle. Piola was the first to conceive of continua where the body’s internal work expenditures depend on spatial derivatives of higher order than classical Cauchy continua, dating back to 1846
[
32
,
9
]
. However, significant progress in this area didn’t emerge until the latter half of the 20th century. During this period, a surge of activity by prominent figures including Toupin
[

,
39
]
, Green and Rivlin
[
18
,
17
]
, Mindlin
[

,
30
]
, Mindlin and Eshel
[

]
, and Germain
[
15
,
16
]
resulted in the development of comprehensive theories of gradient continua, including gradient elasticity. A detailed review of the extensive work and applications of gradient continuum models since then is beyond the scope of this work. For further insights, we refer to the reviews
[
5
,
10
,
28
,
11
]
and the references cited therein.

In theories of gradient continua, an added layer of complexity emerges since it becomes necessary to prescribe additional boundary conditions. In particular, for the equilibrium theory derived from a stored energy density that depends on the spatial gradient of
\boldsymbol{F}
and satisfies strong ellipticity, the resulting equilibrium equations form an elliptic system of partial differential equations that include the fourth-order spatial derivatives of the configuration
\boldsymbol{\chi}

\boldsymbol{\chi}|_{\partial\mathcal{B}}

1.3.
Main results and outline

For simplicity, in this work we will only consider the case of plates

has small strain and normal components of the second gradient of the velocity (relative to fixed length and time scales) when evaluated on the midplane. We then prove that the associated stored surface energy per unit reference area,

Proof.

Via straightforward calculations, we have the relations

\begin{gathered}\frac{\partial\mathsf{E}_{\beta\nu}}{\partial\boldsymbol{y}_{,\alpha}}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}\boldsymbol{y}_{,\nu}+\delta^{\alpha}_{\nu}\boldsymbol{y}_{,\beta}\Bigr),\\

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}+w\boldsymbol{e}_{3},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}=\mathsf{u}^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha},\quad\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\alpha}\mathsf{u}_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}\mathsf{u}_{\alpha}).

(4.22)

Assuming that
|\boldsymbol{u}|
,
|\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|+|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|
, and
|\nabla\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}|+|\nabla\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|
, are bounded by
\delta\ll 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{\alpha\beta}=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}+O(\delta^{2}),\quad\mathsf{K}_{\alpha\beta}=\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}w+O(\delta^{2}),

(4.23)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{T}}^{\alpha}=a\Bigl(\nu(\textrm{tr}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\delta^{\alpha\beta}+(1-\nu)\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\Bigr)\boldsymbol{e}_{\beta}+O(\delta^{2}),

(4.24)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{M}}^{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\ell_{s}^{2}a\Bigl[\nu\Bigl((\textrm{tr}\,\partial^{\beta}\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\delta^{\alpha\rho}+(\textrm{tr}\,\partial^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\delta^{\beta\rho}\Bigr)+(1-\nu)\Bigl(\partial^{\alpha}\epsilon^{\beta\rho}+\partial^{\beta}\epsilon^{\alpha\rho}\Bigr)\Bigr]\boldsymbol{e}_{\rho}

(4.25)

\displaystyle+b\Bigl(\nu(\partial_{\nu}\partial^{\nu}w)\delta^{\alpha\beta}+(1-\nu)\partial^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}w\Bigr)\boldsymbol{e}_{3}+O(\delta^{2}),

(4.26)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\alpha}=\rho_{s}\Bigl[\frac{\nu^{2}}{(1-\nu)^{2}}c(\textrm{tr}\,\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\delta^{\alpha\beta}\boldsymbol{e}_{\beta}+c\delta^{\alpha\beta}(\partial_{\beta}\partial_{t}w)\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\Bigr]

(4.27)

\displaystyle+\rho_{s}\ell_{k}^{2}\delta^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+O(\delta^{2}).

(4.28)

The equations governing infinitesimal displacements correspond to the linearization of (
4.13
), i.e., we drop all terms that are
O(\delta^{2})
, leading to:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{s}\partial_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{u}=(1-\ell_{s}^{2}\partial_{\rho}\partial^{\rho})a\Bigl(\nu\partial^{\beta}\textrm{tr}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+(1-\nu)\partial_{\alpha}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\Bigr)\boldsymbol{e}_{\beta}-b(\partial_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha})^{2}w\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\\
+\rho_{s}\Bigl(\frac{\nu^{2}}{(1-\nu)^{2}}c\partial^{\beta}\partial_{t}^{2}\textrm{tr}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\ell_{k}^{2}\partial_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{2}\mathsf{u}^{\beta}\Bigr)\boldsymbol{e}_{\beta}+\rho_{s}(c+\ell_{k}^{2})\partial_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{2}w\boldsymbol{e}_{3}+\boldsymbol{f}.\end{split}

(4.29)

In terms of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}
,
w
, and standard notation wherein differential operators are with respect to the variables
\boldsymbol{x}=(x^{1},x^{2})=(Y^{1},Y^{2})
, we may express (
4.29
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{s}\Bigl((1-\ell_{k}^{2}\Delta)\partial_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}-\frac{\nu^{2}}{(1-\nu)^{2}}c\nabla\operatorname{div}\partial_{t}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}\Bigr)&=\operatorname{div}a(1-\ell_{s}^{2}\Delta)(\nu(\textrm{tr}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}+(1-\nu)\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\\
&\qquad+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}},\\
\rho_{s}\Bigl(\partial_{t}^{2}w-(c+\ell_{k}^{2})\Delta\partial_{t}^{2}w\Bigr)&=-b\Delta^{2}w+l,\end{split}

(4.30)

where we have written
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}+l\boldsymbol{e}_{3}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{f}}=f^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{P}}=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\boldsymbol{e}_{2}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{2}
. Now viewing
\mathcal{S}
as the midsurface of a gradient elastic plate with Young’s modulus
E
, Poisson’s ratio
\nu
, thickness
h
, and inter-particle spacing
d
, we have the identifications

\displaystyle a=\frac{hE}{1-\nu^{2}},\quad b=\frac{hE}{1-\nu^{2}}\Bigl(\frac{h^{2}}{24}+\ell_{s}^{2}\Bigr),\quad c=\frac{h^{2}}{12}+\ell_{k}^{2}.

(4.31)

We now consider a plane harmonic wave

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}=A\exp[i(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}-\omega t)]\boldsymbol{d},

(4.32)

where
\boldsymbol{x}=x^{\mu}\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}=k^{\mu}\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
is the wave vector,
\boldsymbol{d}=d^{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
is the unit length direction of motion,
\omega
is the angular frequency, and
c_{s}=\omega/|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|
is the phase velocity. We denote

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}}{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}^{\perp}=-k^{2}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+k^{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{2},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathsf{d}}=d^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}.

(4.33)

By inserting (
4.32
) into (
4.30
) we conclude that

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\rho_{s}\Bigl[(1+\ell_{k}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2})\boldsymbol{\mathsf{d}}

\displaystyle+c\frac{\nu^{2}}{(1-\nu)^{2}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{d})\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}\Bigr]

(4.34)

\displaystyle=a(1+\ell_{k}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2})\Bigl[\frac{1+\nu}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{d})\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}+\frac{1-\nu}{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{d}}\Bigr],

(4.35)

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\rho_{s}(1+(c+\ell_{k}^{2})|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2})(\boldsymbol{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{3})

\displaystyle={b}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{4}(\boldsymbol{d}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{3}).

(4.36)

The previous are equivalent to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}})\boldsymbol{d}=c_{s}^{2}\boldsymbol{d},

(4.37)

where
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}})
is the acoustical tensor defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}})&=\frac{a}{\rho_{s}}\frac{1+\ell_{s}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}{1+(c\nu^{2}(1-\nu)^{-2}+\ell_{k}^{2})|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}}\otimes\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}}+\frac{a(1-\nu)}{2\rho_{s}}\frac{1+\ell_{s}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}{1+\ell_{k}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}}^{\perp}\otimes\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}}^{\perp}\\
&\qquad+\frac{b}{\rho_{s}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}{1+(c+\ell_{k}^{2})|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{3}.\end{split}

(4.38)

From (
4.37
) and (
4.38
) we conclude that for a given nonzero wave vector
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}
, there are three types of plane harmonic waves corresponding to the three linearly independent directions of motion:

•

There is one longitudinal wave with direction of motion
\boldsymbol{d}=\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}
and phase velocity satisfying

\displaystyle c_{s,L}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)=\frac{a}{\rho_{s}}\frac{1+\ell_{s}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}{1+(c\nu^{2}(1-\nu)^{-2}+\ell_{k}^{2})|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}.

(4.39)

•

There is one tangentially transverse wave with direction of motion
\boldsymbol{d}=\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\perp}
and phase velocity satisfying

\displaystyle c_{s,T}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)=\frac{a(\nu-1)}{2\rho_{s}}\frac{1+\ell_{s}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}{1+\ell_{k}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}.

(4.40)

•

There is one normally transverse wave with direction of motion
\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{e}_{3}
and phase velocity satisfying

\displaystyle c_{s,N}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)=\frac{b}{\rho_{s}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}{1+(c+\ell_{k}^{2})|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}.

(4.41)

In our remaining discussion of the effects that the length parameters have on plane harmonic waves, we adopt the identifications from (
2.12
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\ell_{s}^{2}=\frac{d^{2}}{12},\quad\ell_{k}^{2}=\frac{d^{2}}{6}.

(4.42)

We denote the classical phase velocities where
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{s}=\boldsymbol{\ell}_{k}=0
by

\displaystyle c_{s,L,cl}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)=\frac{a}{\rho_{s}}\frac{1}{1+\frac{h^{2}}{12}\nu^{2}(1-\nu)^{-2}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}},

(4.43)

\displaystyle c_{s,T,cl}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)=\frac{a(\nu-1)}{2\rho_{s}},\quad c_{s,N,cl}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)=\frac{a}{\rho_{s}}\frac{\frac{h^{2}}{24}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}{1+\frac{h^{2}}{12}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}}

(4.44)

and we point out the degenerate property of classical longitudinal waves that the phase velocity vanishes in the short wavelength limit,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}c_{s,L,cl}^{2}(k)=0.

(4.45)

Now, when it comes to tangentially transverse waves, things change from the classical setting. The phase velocities of these waves show dispersion and are consistently
slower
than their classical counterparts: for all
|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|\neq 0
,

\displaystyle c_{s,T}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)<c_{s,T,cl}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|).

(4.46)

However, normally transverse waves behave differently. When
|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}<\frac{12}{h^{2}}
, their phase velocities are
faster
than the classical ones,
c_{s,N}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)>c_{s,N,cl}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)
, and when
|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}<\frac{12}{h^{2}}
their phase velocities are
slower
than the classical ones,
c_{s,N}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)<c_{s,N,cl}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)
.

Longitudinal waves also display a form of threshold behavior. When the wave number magnitude satisfies
|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}<\frac{12}{h^{2}}(2+(\frac{1}{\nu}-1)^{2})
, then the phase velocity is
slower
than the classical ones,
c_{s,N}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)<c_{s,N,cl}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)
. When the wave number magnitude is above this threshold,
|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|^{2}>\frac{12}{h^{2}}(2+(\frac{1}{\nu}-1)^{2})
, then the phase velocity is
faster
than the classical ones,
c_{s,N}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)>c_{s,N,cl}^{2}(|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}|)
.
Moreover, in stark contrast to the classical setting, the phase velocity is positive in the short wavelength limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}c_{s,L}^{2}(k)=\frac{a}{\rho_{s}}\frac{\ell_{s}^{2}}{c\nu^{2}(1-\nu)^{-2}+\ell_{k}^{2}}>0.

(4.47)

5.

The overall model can be physically understood as representing a body primarily made of a Green elastic material with an additional thin, gradient elastic region of thickness

(5.7)

The dimensionless parameters
\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma
are given by

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{h}{\ell}>0,\quad\beta=\frac{h\ell_{s}^{2}}{\ell^{3}}>0,\quad\gamma=\frac{\sigma}{\mu},

(5.8)

with
\beta=\frac{hd^{2}}{12\ell^{3}}
if we adopt (
2.12
). As discussed in
[
42
,
24
]
, the case
\ell_{s}=0

does not
lead to a model producing bounded stresses and strains up to the crack tips
x=\pm 1
, i.e., we have

\displaystyle\sup_{z>0}|\nabla_{x,z}\mathsf{v}(\pm 1,z)|=\infty.

(5.9)

By using the Hilbert transform (see Section 4 of
[
33
]
), the problem (
5.7
) can be completely reduced to an integro-differential equation on the boundary for
f:=\mathsf{v}|_{z=0}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\beta f^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(x)-\alpha f^{\prime\prime}(x)+\mathcal{H}f^{\prime}(x)=\gamma,\quad x\in(-1,1),\\
&f(\pm 1)=f^{\prime}(\pm 1)=0.\end{split}

(5.10)

Since
\beta>0
, the main result of Section 4 in
[
33
]
directly applies to (
5.10
), showing that our model for fracture rooted in
[
33
]
and utilizing
U
from this work generates stresses and strains that remain bounded up to the crack tips.

(Theorem 4.4,
[
33
]
)
.

There exists
C>0

\alpha

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{v}\|_{C^{1}(\{z\geq 0\})}\leq C|\gamma|.
