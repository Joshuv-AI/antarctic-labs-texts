An elementary approach to the group law on elliptic curves
Sander Zwegers

Abstract
. We revisit the group structure on elliptic curves and give a simple and elementary proof of the associativity of the addition. We do this by providing an explicit formula for the sum of three points, only using the explicit definition of the group structure. In the process we find a nice geometric interpretation of the sum of three points on the elliptic curve.



1.
Introduction and statement of results

We consider the elliptic curve

\{(x,y)\in K^{2}\mid y^{2}=x^{3}+ax+b\}\cup\{\mathcal{O}\}

in (reduced) Weierstraß form.
Here
K
is a field with characteristic
\neq 2,3
and
\mathcal{O}
is the point at infinity.
We assume
4a^{3}+27b^{2}\neq 0
, which ensures that the polynomial
x^{3}+ax+b
has no multiple zeroes.
We equip the elliptic curve with the usual group law.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
P_{1}=(x_{1},y_{1})
and
P_{2}=(x_{2},y_{2})
be in
E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
with
(x_{2},y_{2})\neq(x_{1},-y_{1})
.
We set

\begin{split}x_{3}:=-x_{1}-x_{2}+\alpha^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_{3}:=&-y_{1}-\alpha(x_{3}-x_{1})\\
=&-y_{2}-\alpha(x_{3}-x_{2}),\end{split}

where

\alpha=\alpha(P_{1},P_{2}):=\begin{cases}\frac{y_{2}-y_{1}}{x_{2}-x_{1}},&\text{if}\ x_{1}\neq x_{2},\\
\frac{3x_{1}^{2}+a}{2y_{1}},&\text{if}\ x_{1}=x_{2}.\end{cases}

Then we have
(x_{3},y_{3})\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
.

Definition 1.2
.

We define the addition
+:E\times E\longrightarrow E
by

(a)
P+\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}+P=P
for all
P\in E
;

(b)
(x,y)+(x,-y)=\mathcal{O}
for
(x,y)\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
;

(c)
(x_{1},y_{1})+(x_{2},y_{2})=(x_{3},y_{3})
for
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
with
(x_{2},y_{2})\neq(x_{1},-y_{1})
, where
x_{3}
and
y_{3}
are as in Lemma
1.1
.

This describes the usual addition on the elliptic curve
E
.
The next result is well known.

Theorem 1.3
.

(E,+)
is an abelian group with neutral element
\mathcal{O}
.

From the definition it is clear that the addition is commutative, that
\mathcal{O}
is the neutral element, that the inverse of
P=(x,y)\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
is given by
-P:=(x,-y)
and that
-\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}
.
The only non-trivial part is the associativity.
One approach to this is to concentrate on elliptic curves over
\mathbb{C}
and use the theory of elliptic functions, in particular the Weierstraß
\wp
-function.
See for example
[
5
]
or
[
6
]
for this approach.
Another possibility is to use somewhat advanced algebraic geometry, for example the Riemann–Roch theorem, the Cayley–Bacharach theorem and/or Bézout’s theorem.
We refer the reader to
[
3
,
4
,
7
,
9
]
for various geometric proofs.
In
[
2
]
a completely elementary proof of the group law is given only using the explicit formulas in the definition of the addition.
However, the proof of the associativity requires some advanced computer-aided arguments.
The author notes:
“…it turns out that the explicit computations in the proof are very hard.
The verification of some identities took several hours on a modern computer; this proof could not have been carried out before the 1980’s.”

Here we give an elementary proof of the associativity, not relying on the use of computer calculations.

Theorem 1.4
.

For all
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\in E
we have
(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{3})
.

For the proof we give an explicit formula for the coordinates of
(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}
in the generic case.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
with
P_{3}\neq\pm P_{1}
,
P_{2}\neq-P_{1}
,
P_{2}\neq-P_{3}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq-P_{3}
.
Then the coordinates of
P_{4}:=(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}
are given by

x_{4}=-x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{2}}-2\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}},\qquad y_{4}=-y_{2}-(x_{4}-x_{2})(c_{1}+x_{4}c_{2}),

where

c_{1}:=\frac{x_{1}\hskip 1.00006pt\alpha(P_{2},P_{3})-x_{3}\hskip 1.00006pt\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})}{x_{1}-x_{3}},\qquad c_{2}:=\frac{\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})-\alpha(P_{2},P_{3})}{x_{1}-x_{3}}.

The point is that these formulas are symmetric in
P_{1}
and
P_{3}
, which proves Theorem
1.4
in the generic case.
Note that a similar approach is used in
[
8
]
.
However, the formulas there involve polynomials of high degree in the coordinates of
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
with thousands of terms.
Checking the associativity therefor requires a computer.
Our proof relies mostly on simple and elementary algebraic transformations.

In
[
1
]
the author remarks the following about a potential proof with a brute force computation, using the explicit algebraic formulas for adding points:
“…, the proof does not reveal anything about the underlying algebraic and geometric structures and is not only extremely tedious, but also extremely uninstructive.
This seems to have deterred most authors, for, to my knowledge, this approach cannot be found in any publication.”
Although a bit tedious, our proof is fairly short and easy, and more interestingly, we actually find a nice geometric interpretation of the sum of three points:
we draw the parabola through the points
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
and
P_{3}
; then the parabola has a fourth point of intersection with the elliptic curve, which is
-(P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3})
(see Section
4
for details).

2.
Proof of Lemma
1.1
and Theorem
1.5

We start by proving Lemma
1.1
, which ensures that the addition on
E
is well-defined.
First we note that if
x_{1}=x_{2}
, then we have
y_{1}^{2}=y_{2}^{2}
, so
y_{1}=\pm y_{2}
.
Since we exclude
y_{2}=-y_{1}
in the lemma, we must have
y_{1}=y_{2}
(so
P_{1}=P_{2}
).
Therefor
y_{1}\neq 0
(otherwise
y_{2}=y_{1}=0=-y_{1}
), so we get
\alpha\in K
and
(x_{3},y_{3})\in K^{2}
.
We now consider the polynomial

f(x):=x^{3}+ax+b-\bigl(y_{1}+\alpha(x-x_{1})\bigr)^{2}\in K[x].

Since
(x_{1},y_{1})
is on the curve, we have
f(x_{1})=x_{1}^{3}+ax_{1}+b-y_{1}^{2}=0
.
Hence
x_{1}
is a zero of
f(x)
.
For the case
x_{2}\neq x_{1}
we have
y_{1}+\alpha(x_{2}-x_{1})=y_{2}
.
This gives
f(x_{2})=x_{2}^{3}+ax_{2}+b-y_{2}^{2}=0
, so
x_{2}
is another zero.
If we have
x_{2}=x_{1}
, then we consider the formal derivative

f^{\prime}(x)=3x^{2}+a-2\alpha\bigl(y_{1}+\alpha(x-x_{1})\bigr)\in K[x]

of
f(x)
.
We immediately see that
x_{1}
is a zero of
f^{\prime}(x)
, so
x_{1}
is a double zero of
f(x)
.
In both cases we can write
f(x)
as
f(x)=(x-x_{1})(x-x_{2})g(x)
, where
g(x)\in K[x]
.
Since
f(x)
has degree 3 and leading coefficient 1, we can write
g(x)
as
g(x)=x-x_{0}
with
x_{0}\in K
.
We thus have

x^{3}+ax+b-(y_{1}+\alpha(x-x_{1}))^{2}=(x-x_{1})(x-x_{2})(x-x_{0}).

Comparing the coefficients of
x^{2}
on the left and on the right we get
x_{0}+x_{1}+x_{2}=\alpha^{2}
, and so
x_{0}=x_{3}
and
-(y_{1}+\alpha(x_{0}-x_{1}))=y_{3}
.
Since
x_{0}
is a zero of
f(x)
, the point
(x_{0},-(y_{1}+\alpha(x_{0}-x_{1})))=(x_{3},y_{3})
lies on the curve.
Hence
(x_{3},y_{3})\in K^{2}
satisfies the equation
y^{2}=x^{3}+ax+b
.
Further, we notice that

x^{3}+ax+b-\bigl(y_{1}+\alpha(x-x_{1})\bigr)^{2}=(x-x_{1})(x-x_{2})(x-x_{3}).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
P_{1},P_{2}\in E
with
P_{1}+P_{2}=P_{1}
.
Then
P_{2}=\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

If
P_{1}=\mathcal{O}
, then we find
P_{2}=\mathcal{O}+P_{2}=P_{1}+P_{2}=P_{1}=\mathcal{O}
as desired.
If
P_{2}=-P_{1}
, then we get
P_{1}=P_{1}+P_{2}=\mathcal{O}
and again
P_{2}=-P_{1}=-\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}
.
Hence we must show that there exist no
P_{1},P_{2}\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
with
P_{2}\neq-P_{1}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}=P_{1}
.
We assume that
P_{2}\neq-P_{1}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}=P_{1}
hold and use Lemma
1.1
:
we have
(x_{1},y_{1})=(x_{3},y_{3})
and so we find
y_{1}=y_{3}=-y_{1}-\alpha(x_{3}-x_{1})=-y_{1}
,
y_{1}=0
and

x^{3}+ax+b-\alpha^{2}(x-x_{1})^{2}=(x-x_{1})^{2}(x-x_{2}).

This means that
x_{1}
is a double zero of
x^{3}+ax+b
, which contradicts our assumption
4a^{3}+27b^{2}\neq 0
.
∎

We now turn our attention to the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
We set
(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y}):=P_{1}+P_{2}=(x_{1},y_{1})+(x_{2},y_{2})
and
(x_{4},y_{4}):=(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})+(x_{3},y_{3})=(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}
.
Hence we have

\widetilde{x}=-x_{1}-x_{2}+\alpha^{2},\qquad\widetilde{y}=-y_{1}-\alpha(\widetilde{x}-x_{1}),

where
\alpha:=\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})
, and

x_{4}=-\widetilde{x}-x_{3}+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2},\qquad y_{4}=-y_{3}-\widetilde{\alpha}(x_{4}-x_{3}),

where
\widetilde{\alpha}:=\alpha(P_{1}+P_{2},P_{3})
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
with
P_{3}\neq\pm P_{1}
,
P_{2}\neq-P_{1}
,
P_{2}\neq-P_{3}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq-P_{3}
.
Further, let
c_{2}
be as in Theorem
1.5
.
Then
c_{2}(\alpha+\widetilde{\alpha})=1
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
1.1
we have

x^{3}+ax+b-\bigl(y_{1}+\alpha(x-x_{1})\bigr)^{2}=(x-x_{1})(x-x_{2})(x-\widetilde{x}).

(2.1)

If
P_{2}\neq P_{3}
, then we have

\begin{split}c_{2}&=\frac{\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})-\alpha(P_{2},P_{3})}{x_{1}-x_{3}}=\frac{\alpha-\frac{y_{2}-y_{3}}{x_{2}-x_{3}}}{x_{1}-x_{3}}\\
&=\frac{y_{3}-y_{2}+\alpha(x_{2}-x_{3})}{(x_{1}-x_{3})(x_{2}-x_{3})}=\frac{y_{3}-y_{1}-\alpha(x_{3}-x_{1})}{(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{2})}.\end{split}

If
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq P_{3}
, then we have

\alpha+\widetilde{\alpha}=\alpha+\frac{y_{3}-\widetilde{y}}{x_{3}-\widetilde{x}}=\alpha+\frac{y_{3}+y_{1}+\alpha(\widetilde{x}-x_{1})}{x_{3}-\widetilde{x}}=\frac{y_{3}+y_{1}+\alpha(x_{3}-x_{1})}{x_{3}-\widetilde{x}}.

We now consider 4 possible cases.
For the case
P_{2}\neq P_{3}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq P_{3}
we directly get

c_{2}(\alpha+\widetilde{\alpha})=\frac{y_{3}^{2}-\bigl(y_{1}+\alpha(x_{3}-x_{1})\bigr)^{2}}{(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{2})(x_{3}-\widetilde{x})}=1,

where for the last equality we used (
2.1
) with
x=x_{3}
and
y_{3}^{2}=x_{3}^{3}+ax_{3}+b
.

For the case
P_{2}\neq P_{3}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}=P_{3}
we have
(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{y})=(x_{3},y_{3})
, so
y_{3}=-y_{1}-\alpha(x_{3}-x_{1})
and

c_{2}=\frac{y_{3}-y_{1}-\alpha(x_{3}-x_{1})}{(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{2})}=\frac{2y_{3}}{(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{2})}.

In (
2.1
) we take the derivative with respect to
x
and set
x=\widetilde{x}=x_{3}
to get

3x_{3}^{2}+a+2\alpha y_{3}=(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{2}),\qquad\alpha+\widetilde{\alpha}=\frac{(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{2})}{2y_{3}}

(as remarked in the proof of Lemma
1.1
we have
y_{3}\neq 0
) and
c_{2}(\alpha+\widetilde{\alpha})=1
.

For the case
P_{2}=P_{3}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq P_{3}
we have
(x_{2},y_{2})=(x_{3},y_{3})
, so
y_{3}=y_{1}+\alpha(x_{3}-x_{1})
and

\alpha+\widetilde{\alpha}=\frac{y_{3}+y_{1}+\alpha(x_{3}-x_{1})}{x_{3}-\widetilde{x}}=\frac{2y_{3}}{x_{3}-\widetilde{x}}.

Again, we take the derivative in (
2.1
) and set
x=x_{2}=x_{3}
to get

3x_{3}^{2}+a-2\alpha y_{3}=(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-\widetilde{x}).

Hence we have

\alpha(P_{2},P_{3})-\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})=\frac{(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-\widetilde{x})}{2y_{2}},\qquad c_{2}=\frac{x_{3}-\widetilde{x}}{2y_{3}}

(
y_{2}=y_{3}\neq 0
) and
c_{2}(\alpha+\widetilde{\alpha})=1
.

Finally, according to Lemma
2.1
the case
P_{2}=P_{3}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}=P_{3}
can not occur.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

From the definition of
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
we immediately get

c_{1}+x_{1}\hskip 1.00006ptc_{2}=\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})=\alpha\qquad\text{and}\qquad c_{1}+x_{3}\hskip 1.00006ptc_{2}=\alpha(P_{2},P_{3}).

Further, Lemma
2.2
gives
\widetilde{\alpha}=1/c_{2}-\alpha
.
Hence we get

\begin{split}x_{4}&=-\widetilde{x}-x_{3}+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}=x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}-\alpha^{2}=x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{2}}-2\frac{\alpha}{c_{2}}\\
&=-x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{2}}-2\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\alpha-x_{1}\hskip 1.00006ptc_{2}}{c_{2}}=-x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{2}}-2\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}.\end{split}

Also we have

y_{3}-y_{2}=\alpha(P_{2},P_{3})(x_{3}-x_{2})=(x_{3}-x_{2})(c_{1}+x_{3}c_{2}),

so

\begin{split}y_{3}-y_{2}-(x_{4}-x_{2})(c_{1}+x_{4}c_{2})&=(x_{3}-x_{2})(c_{1}+x_{3}c_{2})-(x_{4}-x_{2})(c_{1}+x_{4}c_{2})\\
&=-(x_{4}-x_{3})\bigl(c_{1}+(-x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4})\hskip 1.00006ptc_{2}\bigr).\end{split}

With
y_{4}=-y_{3}-\widetilde{\alpha}(x_{4}-x_{3})
and
x_{4}=x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}-\alpha^{2}
we then obtain

\begin{split}y_{4}+y_{2}+(&x_{4}-x_{2})(c_{1}+x_{4}c_{2})=(x_{4}-x_{3})\bigl(-\widetilde{\alpha}+c_{1}+(-x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4})\hskip 1.00006ptc_{2}\bigr)\\
&=(x_{4}-x_{3})\bigl(-\widetilde{\alpha}+c_{1}+x_{1}c_{2}+(\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}-\alpha^{2})\hskip 1.00006ptc_{2}\bigr)\\
&=(x_{4}-x_{3})\bigl(-\widetilde{\alpha}+\alpha+(\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}-\alpha^{2})\hskip 1.00006ptc_{2}\bigr)=(x_{4}-x_{3})(\alpha-\widetilde{\alpha})\bigl(1-(\alpha+\widetilde{\alpha})\hskip 1.00006ptc_{2}\bigr)=0\end{split}

and
y_{4}=-y_{2}-(x_{4}-x_{2})(c_{1}+x_{4}c_{2})
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
P,P_{1},P_{2}\in E
we have:

(a)
-(-P)=P
;

(b)
-(P_{1}+P_{2})=(-P_{1})+(-P_{2})
;

(c)
(P_{1}+P_{2})+(-P_{1})=P_{1}+(P_{2}+(-P_{1}))=P_{2}
;

(d) if
P_{1}+P_{2}=\mathcal{O}
, then
P_{2}=-P_{1}
.

Proof.

(a) For
P=(x,y)\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
we have
-P=(x,-y)\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
, so
-(-P)=(x,y)=P
.
From
-\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}
we immediately get
-(-\mathcal{O})=\mathcal{O}
.

(b) If
P_{1}
or
P_{2}
equals
\mathcal{O}
, then the result follows directly:

\begin{split}P_{1}&=\mathcal{O}:\qquad-(P_{1}+P_{2})=-(\mathcal{O}+P_{2})=-P_{2}=\mathcal{O}+(-P_{2})=(-\mathcal{O})+(-P_{2})=(-P_{1})+(-P_{2})\\
P_{2}&=\mathcal{O}:\qquad-(P_{1}+P_{2})=-(P_{1}+\mathcal{O})=-P_{1}=(-P_{1})+\mathcal{O}=(-P_{1})+(-\mathcal{O})=(-P_{1})+(-P_{2})\end{split}

Now let
P_{1},P_{2}\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
.
If
P_{2}=-P_{1}
, then
-P_{2}=-(-P_{1})=P_{1}
, so

-(P_{1}+P_{2})=-(P_{1}+(-P_{1}))=-\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}=(-P_{1})+P_{1}=(-P_{1})+(-P_{2}).

Now assume
P_{2}\neq-P_{1}
.
By definition we have

P_{1}+P_{2}=(-x_{1}-x_{2}+\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})^{2},-y_{1}-\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})(x_{3}-x_{1})).

Further, we have
-P_{2}\neq-(-P_{1})
and
-P_{1}=(x_{1},-y_{1})
,
-P_{2}=(x_{2},-y_{2})
, so
\alpha(-P_{1},-P_{2})=-\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})
and

(-P_{1})+(-P_{2})=(-x_{1}-x_{2}+\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})^{2},y_{1}+\alpha(P_{1},P_{2})(x_{3}-x_{1}))=-(P_{1}+P_{2}).

(c) We first show
(P_{1}+P_{2})+(-P_{1})=P_{2}
.
For
P_{1}=\mathcal{O}
or
P_{2}=\mathcal{O}
the result follows immediately:

(\mathcal{O}+P_{2})+(-\mathcal{O})=P_{2}+\mathcal{O}=P_{2}
and
(P_{1}+\mathcal{O})+(-P_{1})=P_{1}+(-P_{1})=\mathcal{O}
.
Now let
P_{1},P_{2}\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
.
We first consider the case that (b) in Definition
1.2
applies for one of the additions.
If
P_{2}=-P_{1}
, then
P_{1}+P_{2}=\mathcal{O}
and so
(P_{1}+P_{2})+(-P_{1})=\mathcal{O}+P_{2}=P_{2}
.
On the other hand, the case
P_{1}+P_{2}=P_{1}
doesn’t occur, because it would contradict Lemma
2.1
.
Now assume that
P_{2}\neq-P_{1}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq P_{1}
hold.
With
P_{3}=-P_{1}
the notation as in Section
2
applies.
We then have
P_{2}\neq P_{3}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq-P_{3}
.
If
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq P_{3}
, then (as in the proof of Lemma
2.2
)

\alpha+\widetilde{\alpha}=\frac{y_{3}+y_{1}+\alpha(x_{3}-x_{1})}{x_{3}-\widetilde{x}},

which equals 0, since
x_{3}=x_{1}
and
y_{3}=-y_{1}
.
If
P_{1}+P_{2}=P_{3}
, then (again as in the proof of Lemma
2.2
)

\alpha+\widetilde{\alpha}=\frac{(x_{3}-x_{1})(x_{3}-x_{2})}{2y_{3}},

which again equals 0.
In both cases we have shown
\widetilde{\alpha}=-\alpha
, so we get

\begin{split}x_{4}&=-\widetilde{x}-x_{3}+\widetilde{\alpha}^{2}=-\widetilde{x}-x_{1}+\alpha^{2}=x_{2},\\
y_{4}&=-y_{3}-\widetilde{\alpha}(x_{4}-x_{3})=y_{1}+\alpha(x_{2}-x_{1})=y_{2}.\end{split}

This shows
P_{4}=P_{2}
and so
(P_{1}+P_{2})+(-P_{1})=(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}=P_{4}=P_{2}
, as desired.
Replacing
P_{1}
by
-P_{1}
and using (a) and the commutativity of the addition we then also get

P_{2}=((-P_{1})+P_{2})+(-(-P_{1}))=((-P_{1})+P_{2})+P_{1}=P_{1}+(P_{2}+(-P_{1})).

(d) If
P_{1}+P_{2}=\mathcal{O}
, then
P_{2}=(P_{1}+P_{2})+(-P_{1})=\mathcal{O}+(-P_{1})=-P_{1}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

If
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
or
P_{3}
equals
\mathcal{O}
, then the result follows directly:

\begin{split}P_{1}&=\mathcal{O}:\qquad(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}=(\mathcal{O}+P_{2})+P_{3}=P_{2}+P_{3}=\mathcal{O}+(P_{2}+P_{3})=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{3})\\
P_{2}&=\mathcal{O}:\qquad(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}=(P_{1}+\mathcal{O})+P_{3}=P_{1}+P_{3}=P_{1}+(\mathcal{O}+P_{3})=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{3})\\
P_{3}&=\mathcal{O}:\qquad(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}=(P_{1}+P_{2})+\mathcal{O}=P_{1}+P_{2}=P_{1}+(P_{2}+\mathcal{O})=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{3})\end{split}

If
P_{3}=P_{1}
, then the result follows from the commutativity of the addition:

(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}=(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{1}=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{1})=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{3})

For the case
P_{3}=-P_{1}
the result follows from Lemma
3.1
(c):

(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}=(P_{1}+P_{2})+(-P_{1})=P_{1}+(P_{2}+(-P_{1}))=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{3})

For
P_{2}=-P_{1}
or
P_{2}=-P_{3}
we also use Lemma
3.1
(c):

\begin{split}P_{2}=-P_{1}:\qquad(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}&=(P_{1}+(-P_{1}))+P_{3}=\mathcal{O}+P_{3}=P_{3}\\
&=P_{1}+(P_{3}+(-P_{1}))=P_{1}+((-P_{1})+P_{3})=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{3})\\
P_{2}=-P_{3}:\qquad(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}&=(P_{1}+(-P_{3}))+P_{3}=P_{3}+(P_{1}+(-P_{3}))=P_{1}\\
&=P_{1}+\mathcal{O}=P_{1}+((-P_{3})+P_{3})=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{3})\end{split}

Next we consider the case
P_{1}+P_{2}=-P_{3}
.
With Lemma
3.1
we find

P_{2}+P_{3}=P_{2}+(-(-P_{3}))=P_{2}+(-(P_{1}+P_{2}))=P_{2}+((-P_{1})+(-P_{2}))=-P_{1}

and so

(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}=(-P_{3})+P_{3}=\mathcal{O}=P_{1}+(-P_{1})=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{3}).

For the final case we assume
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
with
P_{3}\neq\pm P_{1}
,
P_{2}\neq-P_{1}
,
P_{2}\neq-P_{3}
and
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq-P_{3}
, which are exactly the conditions of Theorem
1.5
.
From the previous case we get that
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq-P_{3}
and
P_{2}+P_{3}\neq-P_{1}
are equivalent (we have shown that
P_{1}+P_{2}=-P_{3}
implies
P_{2}+P_{3}=-P_{1}
; the implication in the other direction follows by interchanging
P_{1}
and
P_{3}
).
Hence the conditions of the theorem are also satisfied if we permute
P_{1}
and
P_{3}
.
Since the formulas for
P_{4}
are directly seen to be invariant under this permutation, we thus get
(P_{1}+P_{2})+P_{3}=(P_{3}+P_{2})+P_{1}=P_{1}+(P_{2}+P_{3})
.
∎

4.
Geometric interpretation

For
K=\mathbb{R}
, we have a well known geometric interpretation of the addition of two points:
let
P_{1},P_{2}\in E\smallsetminus\{\mathcal{O}\}
with
P_{2}\neq-P_{1}
.
We consider the line
\ell:y=y_{1}+\alpha(x-x_{1})
through the points
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
.
If we have
P_{2}=P_{1}
, then this line is tangent to the elliptic curve in the point
P_{1}
.
This property uniquely determines
\ell
.
As we have seen in the proof of Lemma
1.1
the line
\ell
has a third point of intersection with the elliptic curve (counting multiplicities), which is exactly the point
(x_{3},-y_{3})=-(P_{1}+P_{2})
.
If
P_{2}=-P_{1}
, then the line through
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
is a vertical line and the third point of intersection is the point
\mathcal{O}
at infinity, which again equals
-(P_{1}+P_{2})
.

Here we give a similar geometric interpretation of the addition of three points
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
:
we start with the generic case
P_{i}\neq\pm P_{j}
for all
i,j
.
This implies that
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
are pairwise distinct.
If we further assume
P_{1}+P_{2}\neq-P_{3}
, then the three points are not collinear and hence there is a unique parabola through these points.
We claim that it is given by the quadratic polynomial
p(x)=y_{2}+(x-x_{2})(c_{1}+xc_{2})
(with
c_{1},c_{2}
as in Theorem
1.5
):
we have
p(x_{2})=y_{2}
and

\begin{split}p(x_{1})&=y_{2}+(x_{1}-x_{2})(c_{1}+x_{1}c_{2})=y_{2}+\alpha(x_{1}-x_{2})=y_{1},\\
p(x_{3})&=y_{2}+(x_{3}-x_{2})(c_{1}+x_{3}c_{2})=y_{2}+\alpha(P_{2},P_{3})(x_{3}-x_{2})=y_{3}.\end{split}

To find the points of intersection with the elliptic curve we have to solve
p(x)^{2}=x^{3}+ax+b
, which is a polynomial equation of degree 4 in
x
.
By construction
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
are solutions, so there exist
C,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
with

f(x):=(y_{2}+(x-x_{2})(c_{1}+xc_{2}))^{2}-(x^{3}+ax+b)=C(x-x_{1})(x-x_{2})(x-x_{3})(x-x_{0}).

Comparing the coefficients of
x^{4}
and
x^{3}
on the left and on the right we find
c_{2}^{2}=C
and
2c_{2}(c_{1}-c_{2}x_{2})-1=-C(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{0})
and so

x_{0}=-x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}-\frac{2}{c_{2}}(c_{1}-c_{2}x_{2})+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{2}}=-x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{2}}-2\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}=x_{4},

(by Theorem
1.5
).
Hence the fourth point of intersection is
(x_{4},p(x_{4}))=(x_{4},-y_{4})=-(P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3})
(again using Theorem
1.5
).
For the case
P_{1}=P_{2}\neq P_{3}
we can basically use the same argument, we just have to account for multiplicities:
we have
p^{\prime}(x_{1})=c_{1}+x_{1}c_{2}=\alpha
, so the parabola and the elliptic curve have the same tangent at
P_{1}=P_{2}
.
Again, this uniquely determines the polynomial
p(x)
.
Further,
f^{\prime}(x_{1})=2\alpha y_{1}-(3x_{1}^{2}+a)=0
, so
x_{1}
is a double zero of
f(x)
and we have

(y_{2}+(x-x_{2})(c_{1}+xc_{2}))^{2}-(x^{3}+ax+b)=C(x-x_{1})^{2}(x-x_{3})(x-x_{0}).

As before the fourth point of intersection is
(x_{4},-y_{4})=-(P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3})
.
With the associativity and a permutation of the indices this covers all cases where exactly two points are equal.

If for example
P_{2}=-P_{1}
, then
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are on a vertical line and we have
-(P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3})=-P_{3}
, which is again the fourth point of intersection, where the parabola through
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
and
P_{3}
is understood to consist of two vertical lines.

Finally, we briefly discuss the case
P_{1}=P_{2}=P_{3}
.
The polynomial
p(x)=y_{1}+(x-x_{1})(c_{1}+xc_{2})
with

c_{2}=\frac{3x_{1}-\alpha^{2}}{2y_{1}},\qquad c_{1}=\alpha-c_{2}x_{1}

fits the elliptic curve in the point
P_{1}
up to the second order derivative.
With the same computation as before we find that the fourth point of intersection is
(x_{4},p(x_{4}))
, with

x_{4}=-x_{1}+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{2}}-2\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}.

By a calculation similar to the one in Theorem
1.5
(or seeing this as limit of the case
P_{1}=P_{2}\neq P_{3}
), we can show that this equals
-3P_{1}
.
We leave the details to the reader.
