Estimation in Rotationally Invariant Generalized Linear Models via Approximate Message Passing
Ramji Venkataramanan Kevin Kögler and Marco Mondelli

Abstract
We consider the problem of signal estimation in generalized linear models defined via rotationally invariant design matrices. Since these matrices can have an arbitrary spectral distribution, this model is well suited for capturing complex correlation structures which often arise in applications. We propose a novel family of approximate message passing (AMP) algorithms for signal estimation, and rigorously characterize their performance in the high-dimensional limit via a state evolution recursion. Our rotationally invariant AMP has complexity of the same order as the existing AMP derived under the restrictive assumption of a Gaussian design; our algorithm also recovers this existing AMP as a special case. Numerical results showcase a performance close to Vector AMP (which is conjectured to be Bayes-optimal in some settings), but obtained with a much lower complexity, as the proposed algorithm does not require a computationally expensive singular value decomposition.

Introduction

We consider the problem of estimating a
d-
dimensional signal
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from an observation
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
obtained via a generalized linear model (GLM)
[
MN89
]
.
Specifically, given a design matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with rows
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the observation
{\boldsymbol{y}}\equiv(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is generated as

y_{i}=q(\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\rangle,\,\varepsilon_{i}),\quad\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n,

(1)

where
\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\rangle={\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{{\sf T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}
denotes the Euclidean inner product,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\equiv(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n})
is a noise vector and
q:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a known function. The model (
1
) covers many widely studied problems in statistical estimation and signal processing: examples include linear regression
[
Don06
,
EK12
]
(
y_{i}=\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\rangle+\varepsilon_{i}
), phase retrieval
[
SEC+15
,
FS20
]
(
y_{i}=\left\lvert{\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\rangle}\right\rvert^{2}+\varepsilon_{i}
), and 1-bit compressed sensing
[
BB08
]
(
y_{i}=\text{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\rangle+\varepsilon_{i})
).

A range of estimators based on convex relaxations, spectral methods, and non-convex methods have been proposed for specific instances of GLMs, such as sparse linear regression
[
Tib96
,
CT07
,
HTW19
]
, phase retrieval
[
NJS13
,
CSV13
,
CLS15
,
MM19
,
LAL19
,
LL20
]
and one-bit compressed sensing
[
PV12
,
PV13
,
JLBB13
]
. Most of these techniques are generic and can incorporate certain constraints like sparsity, but they are not well-equipped to exploit specific information about
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}
, e.g., a known signal prior.

Approximate message passing (AMP) is a family of iterative algorithms that can be tailored to take advantage of structural information known about the signal. AMP algorithms were first proposed for estimation in linear models
[
Kab03
,
BM12
,
BM11
,
DMM09
,
KMS+12
,
MAYB13
]
, but have since been applied to a range of statistical estimation problems, including generalized linear models
[
BKM+19
,
MXM19
,
MLKZ20
,
MV21a
,
Ran11
,
SR14
,
SC19
]
and low-rank matrix estimation
[
DM14
,
FR18
,
KKM+16
,
LKZ17
,
MV21c
,
BMR20
]
. An attractive feature of AMP is that under suitable model assumptions, its performance in the high-dimensional limit is precisely characterized by a succinct deterministic recursion called
state evolution

[
BM11
,
Bol14
,
JM13
]
. Using the state evolution analysis, it has been proved that AMP achieves Bayes-optimal performance for some models
[
DM14
,
DJM13
,
MV21c
,
BKM+19
]
, and a conjecture from statistical physics posits that AMP is optimal among polynomial-time algorithms. The above works, including the original GAMP algorithm
[
Ran11
]
for estimation in GLMs, all assume that the matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}
defining the model is i.i.d. Gaussian. While some of these results can be generalized to the broader class of i.i.d. sub-Gaussian matrices via universality arguments
[
BLM+15
,
CL21
]
, the i.i.d. assumption limits the applicability of AMP in practice.
In this paper, we present an AMP algorithm for generalized linear models defined via a rotationally invariant design matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}
. The class of rotational invariant matrices includes Gaussian matrices, but is much bigger. Rotational invariance only imposes that the orthogonal matrices in the singular value decomposition of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
are uniformly random, and allows for arbitrary singular values. Hence,
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is able to capture a complex correlation structure, which is typical in applications.

Main contributions.

We propose an AMP algorithm for GLMs with rotationally invariant design matrices. The algorithm, which we call RI-GAMP, uses a pair of multivariate ‘denoising’ functions to produce an updated signal estimate in each iteration. The iterates depend on the free cumulants of the spectral distribution of the design matrix. Assuming that these free cumulants are known (e.g., via the spectral distribution), the complexity of RI-GAMP is of the same order as that of the standard GAMP algorithm
[
Ran11
]
. Moreover, when the design matrix is i.i.d. Gaussian, RI-GAMP reduces to standard GAMP. Our main technical contribution is a state evolution result for RI-GAMP (Theorem
1
), which gives a rigorous characterization of its performance in the high-dimensional limit as
n,d\to\infty
with a fixed ratio
\delta=n/d
, for a constant
\delta>0
. We also present numerical simulation results for linear regression and 1-bit compressed sensing, showcasing the performance of RI-GAMP on both synthetic data and images. The performance of RI-GAMP closely matches that of Vector AMP
[
RSF19
,
PSAR+20
]
(which is conjectured to be Bayes-optimal in some settings), but obtained with much lower complexity, as RI-GAMP does not require computing the singular value decomposition of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
.

RI-GAMP offers a flexible framework to analyze other estimators for GLMs, e.g., spectral methods. Standard GAMP has been used as a proof technique to study the distributional properties of linear and spectral estimators under Gaussian model assumptions
[
MTV21
,
MV21a
]
. An exciting research direction is to use RI-GAMP to analyze spectral estimators for rotationally invariant GLMs in the high-dimensional limit.

Proof idea.

The key idea in establishing the state evolution result is to design an auxiliary AMP algorithm whose iterates are close to those of RI-GAMP. The auxiliary AMP is an instance of the abstract AMP iteration for rotationally invariant matrices analyzed in
[
Fan21
,
ZSF21
]
, hence a state evolution result can be obtained for it.
We then show that each iterate of RI-GAMP is close to one of the auxiliary AMP, and use this fact to translate the state evolution result for the latter to the RI-GAMP. We emphasize that the auxiliary AMP only serves as a proof technique. Indeed, it is initialized using the unknown signal
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}
, and therefore cannot be used for estimation.

RI-GAMP vs. Vector AMP.

Vector AMP (VAMP) is an iterative algorithm (based on Expectation Propagation) for estimation in rotationally invariant linear
[
RSF19
,
Tak20
,
Tak21b
]
and generalized linear models
[
SRF16
,
PSAR+20
]
. Like RI-GAMP, VAMP can be tailored to take advantage of prior information about the signal and its performance can be characterized by a state evolution recursion. It is also shown in
[
RSF19
,
PSAR+20
]
that the asymptotic estimation error of VAMP (with optimal denoising functions) coincides with the replica prediction for the Bayes-optimal error whenever the state evolution recursion has a unique fixed point.

The RI-GAMP algorithm proposed here is distinct from VAMP, and the associated state evolution recursions are also different. Let us highlight some attractive features of RI-GAMP. First, RI-GAMP does not require the computationally expensive (
O(d^{3})
) singular value decomposition used in VAMP. Instead, it uses the free cumulants of the design matrix which can be estimated in
O(d^{2})
time (details on p.
3
).
We confirm via numerical simulations that the complexity advantage of RI-GAMP over VAMP is significant and increases with the problem dimension (see Figure
4
).

The state evolution result (Theorem
1
) for RI-GAMP holds under mild assumptions on the denoising functions (see
(A1)
on p.
(A1)
), while the analysis for VAMP requires slightly stronger conditions, e.g., the denoising functions and their derivatives need to be uniformly Lipschitz continuous. The numerical results in Section
4
show that the performance of RI-GAMP matches that of VAMP, except near parameter values corresponding to a phase transition in the estimation error.

Other related work.

Orthogonal AMP
[
MP17
]
is an algorithm equivalent to VAMP for estimation in rotationally invariant linear models. A variant of Expectation Propagation (an algorithm closely related to VAMP) was proposed in
[
ÇOFW16
]
for rotationally invariant GLMs. Ma et al.
[
MXM21
]
recently studied the performance of Expectation Propagation for rotationally invariant GLMs, and analyzed the impact of the spectrum on the estimation performance. VAMP has also been used to obtain the asymptotic risk of convex penalized estimators for rotationally invariant GLMs
[
GAK20a
,
GAK20b
]
. A few lower complexity alternatives to VAMP have been proposed recently, including convolutional AMP
[
Tak21a
]
, Memory AMP for linear models
[
LHK20
]
, and Generalized Memory AMP for GLMs
[
TLC21
]
. The phase retrieval problem (which is a special case of a GLM) has been studied for design matrices with orthogonal columns, a model distinct from the rotationally invariant one considered here
[
DBMM20
,
DMM20
]
. Finally, we mention that AMP has also been applied to low-rank matrix estimation with rotationally invariant noise
[
OCW16
,
ÇO19
,
Fan21
,
ZSF21
,
MV21b
]
.

Preliminaries

Notation and definitions.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we use the shorthand
[n]
to denote the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Given a vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we write

. Similarly, the joint empirical distribution of the rows of a matrix

. Given a matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, we denote by
({\boldsymbol{A}})_{i,j}
its
(i,j)
-th element.
For a square matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}
, we follow the convention that
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{0}
is the identity matrix.

W_{2}
convergence.

We write

({\boldsymbol{x}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{W_{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})

for the convergence in Wasserstein-
2
distance of the joint empirical distribution of the rows of
({\boldsymbol{x}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
to the law of the random vector
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
. Equivalently
[
FVRS21
]
[Corollary 7.4], for any
L>0
and function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies

\left\lvert{\psi({\boldsymbol{u}})-\psi({\boldsymbol{v}})}\right\rvert\leq L\|{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{v}}\|\left(1+\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\|+\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|\right)

(2)

for all
{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have

\lim_{d\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi(x^{1}_{i},\ldots,x^{k}_{i})={\mathbb{E}}\{\psi(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})\}.

(3)

A function satisfying (
2
) for some fixed
L>0
is called a pseudo-Lipschitz function of order
2
.

Rotationally invariant generalized linear models.

The
n\times d
design matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is formed by stacking the sensing vectors
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{A}}=[{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}]^{{\sf T}}
. We assume that
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is bi-rotationally invariant in law, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{A}}={\boldsymbol{O}}^{\sf T}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}{\boldsymbol{Q}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\rm diag}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
is an
n\times d
diagonal matrix containing the singular values of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, and
{\boldsymbol{O}}
,
{\boldsymbol{Q}}
are Haar orthogonal matrices independent of one another and also of
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
. As
d\to\infty
, we assume that
n/d=\delta
, for some constant
\delta>0
.
The matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is independent of the signal
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the noise vector
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The observation
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is generated according to (
1
).
We assume that there exist random variables
X_{*},\varepsilon
with finite second moments such that
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{W_{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}X_{*}
, and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{W_{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\varepsilon
as
n\to\infty
. Furthermore, we assume that the empirical distribution of
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
converges weakly almost surely to a compactly supported random variable
\Lambda
.
We denote by
\{\kappa_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
the rectangular free cumulants associated with the moments
\{m_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, where
m_{2k}
is the
k
-th moment of the empirical eigenvalue distribution of
{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\sf T}
(for details, see (
34
)-(
35
) in Appendix
A
). For
\delta>1
, let
\tilde{\Lambda}
be a mixture of
\Lambda
(w.p.
1/\delta
) and a point mass at
0
(w.p.
1-1/\delta
); for
\delta\leq 1
, we set
\tilde{\Lambda}=\Lambda
. Then, the assumptions above imply that as
n,d\to\infty
,
m_{2k}\to\bar{m}_{2k}=\mathbb{E}\{\tilde{\Lambda}^{2k}\}
and
\kappa_{2k}\to\bar{\kappa}_{2k}
almost surely, where
\{\bar{m}_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\{\bar{\kappa}_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
are the even moments and rectangular free cumulants of
\tilde{\Lambda}
.

Rotationally Invariant Generalized AMP

Algorithm.

We propose the following rotationally invariant generalized AMP (RI-GAMP) to estimate
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from the observation
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the design matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. For
t\geq 1
, compute:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{{\sf T}}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\beta_{ti}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i},\qquad\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}=f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}),

(4)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{r}}^{t}={\boldsymbol{A}}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{ti}\,{\boldsymbol{s}}^{i},\qquad{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t+1}=h_{t+1}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{t},\,{\boldsymbol{y}}).

(5)

The iteration is initialized with
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{1}=h_{1}({\boldsymbol{y}})
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{1}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{{\sf T}}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{1}
.
For
t\geq 1
, the functions
f_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{t}\to\mathbb{R}
and
h_{t+1}:\mathbb{R}^{t+1}\to\mathbb{R}
are applied row-wise to vectors and matrices. The scalars
\{\alpha_{ti}\}_{i=1}^{t}
and
\{\beta_{ti}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
are obtained in terms of two lower-triangular matrices
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t+1)\times(t+1)}
. These matrices are defined as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&\langle\partial_{1}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{1}\rangle&0&\ldots&0\\
0&\langle\partial_{1}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}\rangle&\langle\partial_{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}\rangle&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\langle\partial_{1}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\rangle&\langle\partial_{2}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\rangle&\ldots&\langle\partial_{t}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\rangle\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\ldots&0&0\\
\langle\partial_{g}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{1}\rangle&0&0&\ldots&0\\
\langle\partial_{g}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{2}\rangle&\langle\partial_{1}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{2}\rangle&0&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\langle\partial_{g}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}\rangle&\langle\partial_{1}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}\rangle&\ldots&\langle\partial_{t-1}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}\rangle&0\end{pmatrix},

(6)

where for
k\in[t]
, the vector
\partial_{k}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the partial derivative
\partial_{x_{k}}f_{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
applied row-wise to
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}=f_{t}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
. Similarly, the vector
\partial_{k}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the partial derivative
\partial_{r_{k}}h_{t}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{t-1},y)
applied row-wise to
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}=h_{t}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{t-1},{\boldsymbol{y}})
. Recalling that
y=q(g,\varepsilon)
, we can view

h_{t}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{t-1},q(g,\varepsilon))
as a function of
(t+1)
variables, with
\partial_{g}h_{t}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{t-1},q(g,\varepsilon))
being the partial derivative with respect to
g
. For
t>1
, the vector
\partial_{g}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}
denotes this partial derivative applied row-wise to
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}=h_{t}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{t-1},q({\boldsymbol{g}},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}))
, and the vector
\partial_{g}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{1}
is defined via the partial derivative
\partial_{g}h_{1}(q(g,\varepsilon))
. Next, recalling that
\{\kappa_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
denote the rectangular free cumulants, define matrices
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\alpha}_{t+1},{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\beta}_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t+1)\times(t+1)}
as:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\alpha}_{t+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{t+1}\,\kappa_{2(j+1)}\,{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}\Big({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}\Big)^{j},\qquad

(7)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\beta}_{t+1}=\delta\sum_{j=0}^{t}\,\kappa_{2(j+1)}\,{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1}\Big({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1}\Big)^{j}.

(8)

Then, the coefficients
\{\alpha_{ti}\}_{i=1}^{t}
and
\{\beta_{ti}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
in (
4
)-(
5
) are obtained from the last row of
{\boldsymbol{M}}^{\alpha}_{t+1}
and

\displaystyle(\alpha_{t1},\ldots,\alpha_{tt})=(\,({\boldsymbol{M}}^{\alpha}_{t+1})_{t+1,2}\,,\,\ldots,\,({\boldsymbol{M}}^{\alpha}_{t+1})_{t+1,t+1}\,),

(9)

\displaystyle(\beta_{t1},\ldots,\beta_{t,t-1})=(\,({\boldsymbol{M}}^{\beta}_{t+1})_{t+1,2},\,\ldots,\,({\boldsymbol{M}}^{\beta}_{t+1})_{t+1,t}\,).

(10)

Estimating the free cumulants.

From the definitions of
\{\alpha_{ti}\}_{i=1}^{t}
and
\{\beta_{ti}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
above, it follows that we need the free cumulants

\{\kappa_{2(j+1)}\}_{j=0}^{t+1}

to compute the first
t
iterates of RI-GAMP in (

). These free cumulants can be recursively computed from the moments

\{m_{2(j+1)}\}_{j=0}^{t+1}
of the spectral distribution of

, using the formula (

) in Appendix

]
. Given

, pick an independent standard Gaussian vector
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},{\boldsymbol{I}}_{n})
, and for

, compute
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{k}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{{\sf T}}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{k-1}

State evolution.

The coefficients
\{\alpha_{ti}\}_{i=1}^{t}
and
\{\beta_{ti}\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
play a crucial role in debiasing the AMP iterates, ensuring that their limiting empirical distributions are accurately captured by state evolution. Indeed, Theorem

(12)

\displaystyle(X_{1},\ldots X_{t})=\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}X_{*}+(W_{1},\ldots,W_{t}),\ \text{where }(W_{1},\ldots,W_{t})\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t})\text{ is independent of }X_{*},

\displaystyle({\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}_{t+1})_{1,i}=({\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}_{t+1})_{i,1}=0,

\displaystyle({\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}_{t+1})_{i+1,j+1}={\mathbb{E}}\{S_{i}S_{j}\},\quad i,j\in[t],

\displaystyle({\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+1})_{1,1}={\mathbb{E}}\{X_{*}^{2}\},

\displaystyle({\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+1})_{1,i+1}=({\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+1})_{i+1,1}={\mathbb{E}}\{X_{*}\hat{X}_{i}\},

\displaystyle({\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+1})_{i+1,j+1}={\mathbb{E}}\{\hat{X}_{i}\hat{X}_{j}\},\quad i,j\in[t].

(15)

Furthermore, let
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1},\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1}
denote the deterministic versions of the matrices

(16)

We now describe how
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t+1},{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t+1},\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t+1}
are computed from
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t},{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t},\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}
. Given
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t},{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t},\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}
, we can evaluate the matrices
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}_{t+1},{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+1},\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1},\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1}
.
From these, we compute

{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t+1)\times(t+1)}
as

{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{2t+1}{\bar{\kappa}}_{2(j+1)}\,{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}_{t+1}^{(j)},

(17)

where
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}_{t+1}^{(0)}={\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+1}
, and for
j\geq 1
:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}_{t+1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1})^{i}\,{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+1}\Big((\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1})^{{\sf T}}\Big)^{j-i}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1})^{i}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}_{t+1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}^{{\sf T}}\Big(\hskip-1.99997pt(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+1})^{{\sf T}}\hskip-1.00006pt\Big)^{j-i-1}.

(18)

Recalling that
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t+1}
is the covariance of
(G,R_{1},\ldots,R_{t})
, we can now compute
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}_{t+2},\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+2}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t+2)\times(t+2)}
.
Using these, we define a symmetric

(t+2)\times(t+2)
matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\prime}_{t+2}
, whose first row and column equal zero and whose lower right
(t+1)\times(t+1)
submatrix equals
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t+1}
. Specifically,

{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\prime}_{t+2}=\delta\sum_{j=0}^{2(t+1)}{\bar{\kappa}}_{2(j+1)}{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t+2}^{(j)},

(19)

where
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t+2}^{(0)}={\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}_{t+2}
, and for
j\geq 1
:

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t+2}^{(j)}&=\sum_{i=0}^{j}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+2}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+2})^{i}\,{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}_{t+2}\Big((\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+2}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+2})^{{\sf T}}\Big)^{j-i}\\
&+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+2}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+2})^{i}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+2}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+2}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+2}^{{\sf T}}\Big(\hskip-1.00006pt(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+2}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+2})^{{\sf T}}\hskip-1.00006pt\Big)^{j-i-1}\hskip-1.99997pt.\end{split}

(20)

Then, the entries of the covariance matrix
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t+1)\times(t+1)}
are given by:

({\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t+1})_{ij}=({\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\prime}_{t+2})_{i+1,j+1},\quad i,j\in[t+1].

(21)

Finally, we compute the mean parameter

\bar{\mu}_{t+1}=\Big({\bar{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{\beta}_{t+2}\Big)_{t+2,1}
, where

{\bar{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{\beta}_{t+2}=\delta\sum_{j=0}^{t+1}\,\bar{\kappa}_{2(j+1)}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+2}\Big(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+2}\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+2}\Big)^{j}.

(22)

Though the formulas for
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{t+2}^{(j)}
in (
20
) and
{\bar{\boldsymbol{M}}}^{\beta}_{t+2}
in (
22
) contain
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+2}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+2}
, the last rows and columns of these matrices are zeroed out in the computation (due to the form of
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}_{t+2}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{t+2}
). Therefore the formulas depend only on the top left submatrices of
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+2}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+2}
, namely,
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{t+1}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{t+1}
. We also note that the matrices
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t}
and
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t}
are the top left submatrices of
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t+1}
and
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t+1}
, respectively. Similarly, the mean vector
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t+1}
is obtained by appending
\bar{\mu}_{t+1}
to
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}
.

Main result.

Having defined the state evolution recursion to compute
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t},{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t},{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t}
(which specify the joint distributions in (
12
)-(
13
)), we are ready to state our main result. We make the following assumption on the functions
f_{t},h_{t}
used in the AMP (
4
)-(
5
), for
t\geq 1
:

(A1)

The functions
f_{t}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t})
and
h_{t}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{t-1},\,q(G,\varepsilon))
are Lipschitz in each of their arguments. The partial derivatives
\partial_{X_{k}}f_{t}((X_{1},\ldots,X_{t}))
,

\partial_{G}h_{t}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{t-1},\,q(G,\varepsilon))
, and
\partial_{R_{\ell}}h_{t}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{t-1},\,q(G,\varepsilon))
are all continuous on sets of probability
1
, under the laws of
(X_{1}\ldots,X_{t})
and
(G,R_{1},\ldots,R_{t-1})
given in (
12
)-(
13
).

Theorem 1
.

Consider a rotationally invariant generalized linear model with the assumptions in Section
2
and the AMP (
4
)-(
5
) with the assumption
(A1)
above.
Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2t+1}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{2t+2}\to\mathbb{R}
be any pseudo-Lipschitz functions of order
2
. Then for each
t\geq 1
, we almost surely have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi(x^{1}_{i},\ldots,x^{t}_{i},\,\hat{x}^{1}_{i},\ldots,\hat{x}^{t}_{i},\,x^{*}_{i})={\mathbb{E}}\{\psi(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t},\hat{X}_{1},\ldots,\hat{X}_{t},\,X_{*})\},

(23)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(r^{1}_{i},\ldots,r^{t}_{i},\,s^{1}_{i},\ldots,s^{t+1}_{i},\,y_{i})={\mathbb{E}}\{\phi(R_{1},\ldots,R_{t},\,S_{1},\ldots,S_{t+1},\,Y)\},

(24)

where the random variables on the right are defined in (
12
)-(
13
).
Equivalently, as
n\to\infty
, the joint empirical distributions of
({\boldsymbol{x}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{1},\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})
and
({\boldsymbol{r}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{r}}^{t},{\boldsymbol{s}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t+1},{\boldsymbol{y}})
converge almost surely in Wasserstein-2 distance to
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t},\hat{X}_{1},\ldots,\hat{X}_{t},X_{*})
and
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{t},S_{1},\ldots,S_{t+1},Y)
, respectively.

The proof of the theorem is given in Appendix
D
, and we provide a proof sketch in Section
5
.
When the design matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}
has i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1/n)
entries, we have
{\bar{\kappa}}_{2}=\delta
and
{\bar{\kappa}}_{2k}=0
for
k\geq 2
. In this case, the RI-GAMP (

)-(

), with denoising functions of the form

and

, reduces to the existing GAMP algorithm
[

. The state evolution recursion also reduces to that of GAMP (see, e.g., Section 4 of
[
FVRS21
]
).
This opens up an exciting research direction on using RI-GAMP to generalize results where GAMP has been used as a proof technique under Gaussian model assumptions. One example is to determine the distributional properties of spectral estimators for rotationally invariant GLMs.

MSE and correlation.

The result (
23
) readily leads to the evaluation of the usual quantities of interest, such as the mean squared error (MSE) and the normalized squared correlation. Indeed, by taking
\psi(\hat{x}_{i}^{t},x_{i}^{*})=(\hat{x}_{i}^{t}-x_{i}^{*})^{2}
, we have that
\frac{1}{d}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\|^{2}_{2}

\to\mathbb{E}\{(\hat{X}_{t}-X_{*})^{2}\}
for each
t\geq 1
. Furthermore, by taking
\psi(\hat{x}_{i}^{t},x_{i}^{*})=\hat{x}_{i}^{t}\cdot x_{i}^{*}
,
\psi(\hat{x}_{i}^{t})=(\hat{x}_{i}^{t})^{2}
and
\psi(x_{i}^{*})=(x_{i}^{*})^{2}
, we have that
|\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\rangle|^{2}/(\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\|^{2})
tends to
(\mathbb{E}\{X_{t}\,X_{*}\})^{2}/(\mathbb{E}\{X_{t}^{2}\}\mathbb{E}\{X_{*}^{2}\})
.

Empirical state evolution parameters.

We can define empirical versions of the state evolution parameters, denoted by
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t+1},{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{t+1},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t+1}
, by replacing
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{k},\bar{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k},{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}}_{k},{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}}_{k}
(
k\in\{t+1,t+2\}
) with
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{k}
in (
18
), (

), and (

). The latter matrices are computed using empirical averages instead of expectations:
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{k}
are defined in (
3
) and for
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{k}
, we replace the expectations
{\mathbb{E}}\{S_{i}S_{j}\}
and
{\mathbb{E}}\{\hat{X}_{i}\hat{X}_{j}\}
in (
14
)-(
15
) by
\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}^{i},{\boldsymbol{s}}^{j}\rangle/n
and

\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{i},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{j}\rangle/d
. The expectations

in

can be estimated for the case of posterior mean denoisers using the identity in (

gives that the empirical versions of these matrices converge to the deterministic ones almost surely, and therefore,
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t+1}\to{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t+1}
,
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{t+1}\to{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t+1}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t+1}\to\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t+1}
. For the simulations in Section

, RI-GAMP is implemented with state evolution parameters empirically estimated, as this choice leads to more stable numerical results.

Initialization.

Note that the algorithm is initialized with
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{1}={\boldsymbol{A}}^{\sf T}h_{1}({\boldsymbol{y}})
. If this initialization is not effective, in the sense that
\frac{1}{d}\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\rangle\to 0
, then state evolution remains stuck at a trivial fixed point, i.e.,
\bar{\mu}_{t}=0
for all
t
, and all the iterates produced by RI-GAMP are not correlated with the signal.

For both linear regression and 1-bit compressed sensing, the standard initialization

Choice of denoisers.

The performance of RI-GAMP is determined by the functions
\{f_{t},h_{t+1}\}_{t\geq 1}
. A key question is how to choose these functions to optimize the estimation performance. Given any choice of
\{f_{t},h_{t+1}\}
satisfying Assumption
(A1)
, Theorem
1
implies

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{r}}^{t-1}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{W_{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\,R_{t-1}\equiv\frac{(\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t})_{t,1}}{(\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t})_{1,1}}G\,+\,W^{\prime}_{t-1},

\displaystyle W^{\prime}_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\,(\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t})_{t,t}-\frac{((\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t})_{t,1})^{2}}{(\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t})_{1,1}}\right),\quad t\geq 2,

(25)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{W_{2}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\,X_{t}\equiv\bar{\mu}_{t}X_{*}\,+\,W_{t},

\displaystyle W_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t})_{t,t})\text{ independent of }X_{*},\quad t\geq 1,

(26)

where the RHS of (
3
) and (
3
) follow from the joint distributions specified in (
12
)-(

). From (

\bar{\mu}_{t}^{2}/(\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t})_{t,t}

maximizes
\bar{\mu}_{t}^{2}/(\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}_{t})_{t,t}
. Similarly, given
\{f_{k}\}_{k\leq t-1}
and
\{h_{k}\}_{k\leq t}

f_{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
maximizes the normalized squared correlation of
R_{t}
and
G
, which is proportional to
\frac{(\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t+1})_{t+1,1}^{2}}{(\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t+1})_{t+1,t+1}}

X_{*}
) is known, finding these optimal denoisers is challenging due to the complicated nature of the state evolution recursion (
12
)-(

\displaystyle f_{t}(x_{t})=c_{1}\mathbb{E}\{X_{*}\mid X_{t}=x_{t}\},

(27)

\displaystyle h_{t+1}(r_{t},y)=\hskip-1.00006ptc_{2}(\mathbb{E}\{G|R_{t}=r_{t},Y=y\}\hskip-1.00006pt-\hskip-1.00006pt\mathbb{E}\{G|R_{t}=r_{t}\}),

\displaystyle f_{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})=\mathbb{E}\{X_{*}\mid X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{t}=x_{t}\},

(28)

\displaystyle h_{t+1}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{t},y)\hskip-1.00006pt=\hskip-1.00006pt\mathbb{E}\{G|R_{1}=r_{1},\ldots,R_{t}=r_{t},Y=y\}-\mathbb{E}\{G\mid R_{1}=r_{1},\ldots,R_{t}=r_{t}\}.

(29)

When
{\boldsymbol{A}}

Taking the results of Figures

[

Proof Sketch of Theorem
1

The proof is based on an auxiliary AMP algorithm whose iterates mimic the true AMP in (
4
)-(
5
). The iterates of the auxiliary AMP, denoted by
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{t},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}^{t},{\boldsymbol{u}}^{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, are computed as follows, for
t\geq 1
:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{z}}^{t}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{A}}^{{\sf T}}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}{\sf b}_{ti}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{i},\qquad{\boldsymbol{v}}^{t}=\tilde{f}_{t}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{t},\,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}),\hskip-10.00002pt

(30)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}

\displaystyle\hskip-1.99997pt=\hskip-1.99997pt{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{t}\hskip-1.99997pt-\hskip-1.99997pt\sum_{i=1}^{t}\hskip-1.99997pt{\sf a}_{ti}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{i},\qquad{\boldsymbol{u}}^{t+1}\hskip-1.99997pt=\hskip-1.99997pt\tilde{h}_{t+1}({\boldsymbol{m}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}\hskip-1.99997pt,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}).\hskip-5.0pt

(31)

The iteration is initialized with
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{1}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}=\boldsymbol{0}
. The function
\tilde{f}_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as
\tilde{f}_{1}(z_{1},x)=x
, which yields
{\boldsymbol{v}}^{1}={\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}
and

{\boldsymbol{m}}^{1}={\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{g}}
.
For
t\geq 1
, the functions
\tilde{f}_{t+1}:\mathbb{R}^{t+2}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\tilde{h}_{t+1}:\mathbb{R}^{t+1}\to\mathbb{R}
, which act row-wise on matrices, are defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{t+1}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{t+1},\,x)=f_{t}(z_{2}+\bar{\mu}_{1}x,\,z_{3}+\bar{\mu}_{2}x,\,\ldots,\,z_{t+1}+\bar{\mu}_{t}x),

(32)

Here,

are the functions from the original AMP, and

and

Idea of the proof.

The auxiliary AMP (
30
)-(
31
) is an instance of the abstract AMP recursion for non-symmetric rotationally invariant matrices, which was analyzed in
[
ZSF21
]
. The state evolution result in Theorem 2.6 of
[
ZSF21
]
implies that the joint empirical distribution of
({\boldsymbol{m}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t})
converges to a
t
-dimensional Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t})
. Similarly, the joint empirical distribution of
({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{t})
also converges to a a
t
-dimensional Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{t})
. The covariance matrices
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{t}
are recursively defined via the state evolution for the auxiliary AMP (for details, see Section
D.2
).

The proof of Theorem
1
consists of two steps. First, we show that the state evolution parameters of the auxiliary AMP match those of the true AMP. In particular, Lemma
D.2
proves that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{t}={\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{t}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\Omega}}^{\prime}_{t+1}
, where the matrices on the right are defined in (
17
) and (
19
). Next, we show in Lemma
D.3
that the true AMP iterates (
4
)-(
5
) are close to the auxiliary AMP iterates (
30
)-(
31
) in the following sense. For
t\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}-({\boldsymbol{z}}^{t+1}+\bar{\mu}_{t}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})\|^{2}}{d}\to 0,\ \ \frac{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}-{\boldsymbol{v}}^{t+1}\|^{2}}{d}\to 0,

\displaystyle\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{r}}^{t}-{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t+1}\|^{2}}{n}\to 0,\ \ \frac{\|{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}-{\boldsymbol{u}}^{t+1}\|^{2}}{n}\to 0.

(33)

Lemma
D.3
actually proves a more general convergence statement which implies (
33
). It shows that the empirical joint distribution of the iterates of the true AMP converges to that of the auxiliary AMP. The result of Theorem
1
then follows from Lemmas
D.2
and
D.3
.

Appendix A
Background on Rectangular Free Cumulants

Let
X
be a random variable of finite moments of all orders, and denote its even moments by
m_{2k}=\mathbb{E}\{X^{2k}\}
. In this paper,
X^{2}
represents either the empirical eigenvalue distribution of
{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\sf T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, or its limit law
\Lambda^{2}
(in the latter case, the moments and rectangular free cumulants are denoted by
\{\bar{m}_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\{\bar{\kappa}_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, respectively).
The rectangular free cumulants
\{\kappa_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of
X
are defined recursively by the moment-cumulant relations (cf. Section 3 of
[
BG09
]
)

m_{2k}=\delta\sum_{\pi\in{\rm NC}^{\prime}(2k)}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\pi\\
\min S\,\,\,\mbox{\scriptsize is odd}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|S|}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\pi\\
\min S\,\,\,\mbox{\scriptsize is even}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|S|},

(34)

where
{\rm NC}^{\prime}(2k)
is the set of non-crossing partitions
\pi
of
\{1,\ldots,2k\}
such that each set
S\in\pi
has even cardinality. Furthermore, by exploiting the connection between the formal power series with coefficients
\{m_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\{\kappa_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, each rectangular free cumulant
\kappa_{2k}
can be computed from
m_{2},\ldots,m_{2k}
and
\kappa_{2},\ldots,\kappa_{2(k-1)}
as (cf. Lemma 3.4 of
[
BG09
]
)

\kappa_{2k}=m_{2k}-[z^{k}]\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\kappa_{2j}\left(z(\delta M(z)+1)(M(z)+1)\right)^{j},

(35)

where
M(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}m_{2k}z^{k}
and
[z^{k}](q(z))
denotes the coefficient of
z^{k}
in the polynomial
q(z)
.

In the numerical simulations of Section
4
, the singular values of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
are i.i.d.
\sqrt{6}\cdot{\rm Beta}(1,2)
. Hence, for
\delta\in(0,1)
,
X
has distribution
\sqrt{6}\cdot{\rm Beta}(1,2)
and consequently
\bar{m}_{2k}=\frac{6^{k}}{(k+1)(2k+1)}
; for
\delta\geq 1
,
X
has distribution
\sqrt{6}\cdot{\rm Beta}(1,2)
w.p.
1/\delta
and it is equal to
0
w.p.
1-1/\delta
, and consequently
\bar{m}_{2k}=\frac{1}{\delta}\frac{6^{k}}{(k+1)(2k+1)}
. Then, given the moments
\{\bar{m}_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, the rectangular free cumulants
\{\bar{\kappa}_{2k}\}_{k\geq 1}
are computed recursively using (
35
).

Appendix E
An Auxiliary Lemma

Lemma E.1
.

Let

be a Lipschitz function, and let
\partial_{k}F
denote its derivative with respect to the
k
-th argument, for

is continuous almost everywhere in the
k
-th argument, for each

The result was proved for
t=2
in Lemma 6 of
[
BM11
]
. The proof for
t>2
is essentially the same; see also Lemma 7.14 in
[
FVRS21
]
.
