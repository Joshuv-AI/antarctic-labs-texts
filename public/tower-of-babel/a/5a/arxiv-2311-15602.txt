A nodally bound-preserving finite element method for reaction-convection-diffusion equations
Abdolreza Amiri, Gabriel R. Barrenechea, Tristan Pryer

Abstract
This paper introduces a novel approach to approximate a broad range of reaction-convection-diffusion equations using conforming finite element methods while providing a discrete solution respecting the physical bounds given by the underlying differential equation. The main result of this work demonstrates that the numerical solution achieves accuracy of O ⁡ ( h k ) O(h^{k}) in the energy norm, where k k represents the underlying polynomial degree. To validate the approach, a series of numerical experiments is conducted for various problem instances. Comparisons with the linear continuous interior penalty stabilised method, and the algebraic flux-correction scheme (for the piecewise linear finite element case) have been carried out, where we can observe the favourable performance of the current approach.



1
Introduction

In numerous fields of mathematical modelling, the partial differential
equations (PDEs) used to model the phenomenon in question respect the
basic laws that were used to derive them, and their solutions satisfy
these
first principles
. For example, in the modelling of
incompressible flows, the PDEs involved provide divergence-free
velocities, the PDEs modelling dissipative systems provide solutions
that are energy stable, and in phase-field modelling the solution
has strict global maxima and minima, just to give a few examples.
From a numerical approximation perspective, it is very desirable that
the finite element methods used to discretise these PDEs do respect
the respective laws. Unfortunately, in most instances this is not the
case. For example, the most popular finite element methods for
incompressible flows are not pointwise divergence-free (see, e.g.,

[
20
]
for a recent review on the topic), and proving that
schemes are energy stable is far from trivial (see, e.g.,

[
15
,
7
]
).

In the particular case of bound-preservation, it was understood very
early that the solution of a standard finite element method does not,
in general, respect the physical bounds. This was first formalised in

[
9
]
in the finite element context where it was shown that the
approximation using piecewise linear finite elements respects such
bounds only if the mesh satisfies certain assumptions about its
internal angles, and how refined it is. In addition to the mesh
restriction, for conservation laws finite element methods that respect
the physical bounds of the problem can either be first order accurate,
or nonlinear, due to the notorious Godunov order barrier theorem (se,
e.g.,
[
16
]
).

As a consequence of the above discussion, in the last few decades
numerous methods that respect global bounds have been proposed. A
special attention has been given, in fact, to a stronger
property. Namely, numerous methods respecting the
Discrete
Maximum Principle (DMP)
have been proposed, especially for
convection-dominated problems (see
[
27
,
30
,
5
,
24
,
3
]
,
just to name a few, and
[
4
]
for a recent review). These
methods are, for the most part, nonlinearly stabilised methods. That
is, methods that add a nonlinear stabilising term to the Galerkin
scheme in a way that diffusion is added locally, thus making the
problem locally diffusion-dominated, and avoiding the spurious
oscillations and local violations of the maximum principle.
Interestingly, despite the above-mentioned discretisations being
nonlinear, in most cases the finite element methods proposed are based
on piecewise linear elements. In fact, the extension to higher order
elements imposes even stronger mesh conditions (for example, in

[
18
]
it is proven that for the Poisson equation in two space
dimensions a monotone discretisation using quadratic elements can be
built if the mesh is either equilateral, or consists of squares in
which the squares are divided by arbitrary diagonals), and, on the
other hand, there is not much analysis available for nonlinear schemes
using higher order polynomials.

In many situations, the stability of the numerical method does not
require the discrete solution to be devoid of local spurious
oscillations, but only needs to satisfy the global bounds. In such a
case, the problem is actually simpler and there are several
methodologies available. The first option one might think of is simply
cutting off the values that lie outside the admissible range. This
approach is perhaps used in many simulations without being explicitly
mentioned, and it has been analysed for a linear reaction-diffusion
equation in
[
23
]
, and for parabolic problems in

[
25
]
. A more radical approach consists on
reformulating
the problem
in such a way that the discrete solution always
respects the corresponding bounds (see, e.g.
[
19
]
for
the application to Chemotaxis, or
[
14
]
for an extremely widely
used reformulation in non-Newtonian fluid mechanics). Alternatively,
global bounds can be enforced by introducing them as inequality
constraints and then approximate a control problem as it has been
done, e.g., in
[
12
]
. Similarly, inequality
constraints can be dealt with using Lagrange multipliers and solving
an extended system, as has been recently done in
[
8
]
,
or
[
29
]
,where a semi-smooth Newton method has also been
introduced to deal with the non-smoothness.

In the very recent work
[
2
]
a different strategy was
followed to impose global bounds in the solution. The first step is
to define the set, denoted
V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
, of
admissible
finite
element functions as those satisfying the global bounds
at their
degrees of freedom
(nodal values in the case of Lagrangian
elements); then, introduce an algebraic projection onto the admissible
set, denote by
u_{h}^{+}
the projection of
u_{h}
onto
V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
,
and write a finite element problem for the projected object.
Since this process introduces a kernel, as the
projection is not injective, a stabilising term is added to remove the
singularity. This last step allowed, in particular, to avoid the
introduction of Lagrange multipliers. Moreover, when applied to the
linear reaction-diffusion equation, it turns out that
u_{h}^{+}
is the
orthogonal projection onto
V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
, and thus is independent of the
stabilisation used (this last property is lost when applied to more
complicated problems, such as problems with nonlinear reaction, also
addressed in
[
2
]
). So, the purpose of the present work is
to extend the methodology presented in
[
2
]
to the
convection-diffusion equation. Although the driving principles are
similar, this work presents significant novelities with respect to

[
2
]
, namely

1.

the starting point in the construction of the method is not the
plain Galerkin scheme, but a stabilised finite element method
instead. The reason for this is twofold: first, in regions where the
constraint is not active, local oscillations might still appear (and
linear stabilisation helps with this), and second, our numerical
experiments show that the addition of linear stabilisation in

u_{h}^{+}
helps tremendously the good behaviour of the nonlinear
solver;

2.

the definition of the stabilisation form is different, since we
need to control the convective term;

3.

the analysis differs greatly from that of
[
2
]
. On the
one hand, the well-posedness analysis is very different, as the
discretisation is not driven by a monotone operator. On the other
hand, since the problem is non-symmetric the solution
u_{h}^{+}
is no
longer the orthogonal projection of
u
onto
V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
, and thus
the error analysis follows an alternative path.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows: In §
2
we introduce the notation, the model problem, and all
the preliminary material for the setup of the method (including the
choice of linearly stabilised method to be used). In
Section
3
we present the finite element method and show
its well-posedness. The error analysis is carried out in
Section
4
, and in §
5
we test the
performance of the method via numerical experiments, comparing also
with previously existing alterantives. Finally, some conclusions and
future directions are drawn in §
6
.

2
General Setting And The Model Problem

We will adopt standard notations for Sobolev spaces, in line with,
e.g.,
[
10
]
. For
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by

\|\cdot\|_{0,p,D}
the
L^{p}(D)
-norm; when
p=2
the subscript
p

will be omitted and we only write
\|\cdot\|_{0,D}
. In addition, for

s\geq 0
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, we denote by
\|\cdot\|_{s,p,D}
(
|\cdot|_{s,p,D}
) the norm (seminorm) in
W^{s,p}(D)
; when
p=2
, we
will again omit the subscript
p
and only write
\|\cdot\|_{s,D}

(
|\cdot|_{s,D}
). In addition, we denote by
H^{-1}(D)
the dual of

H_{0}^{1}(D)
while identifying
L^{2}(D)
with its dual. Thus, writing

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{D}
for the duality pairing, we have

\langle f,v\rangle_{D}=\int_{D}f(\boldsymbol{x})v(\boldsymbol{x})\textrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\qquad\forall\,v\in H^{1}_{0}(D)\,,

whenever
f\in H^{-1}(D)
is regular enough. We do not distinguish
between inner product and duality pairing for scalar or vector-valued
functions.

2.1
The Model Problem

Let
\Omega
be an open bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=2,3
) with polyhedral boundary
\partial\Omega
.
For a given
f\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
, we consider the following convection-diffusion problem:

\displaystyle-{\rm div}(\mathcal{D}\nabla u)+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla u+\mu u

\displaystyle=f\hskip 28.45274pt{\rm in}\;\Omega,

(1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0\hskip 28.45274pt{\rm on}\;\partial\Omega\,,

where
\mathcal{D}=(d_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d\times d}
,
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\beta_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d}
, and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, respectively, are the diffusion tensor, the convective field, and the reaction coefficient. We will assume that
{\rm div}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=0
, and the diffusion tensor
\mathcal{D}
is symmetric and uniformly strictly positive definite in
\Omega
; in other words, there exists a positive constant
d_{0}>0
such that for almost all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}y_{i}d_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x})y_{j}\geq d_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}^{2}\hskip 28.45274pt\forall(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2)

Remark 2.1
.

We have assumed
{\rm div}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=0
only to avoid technical diversions.
The results presented in this paper remind essentially unchanged under the milder condition

\mu-{\rm div}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}/2>0
. Moreover, all the results presented below also are applicable to
the case when
\mu\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is a function that is strictly positive in
\overline{\Omega}
.

The standard weak formulation of (
1
) reads as follows: find
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, such that

a(u,v)=\langle f,v\rangle_{\Omega}\hskip 28.45274pt\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

(3)

where
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is the bilinear form defined by

a(w,v):=\left(\mathcal{D}\nabla w,\nabla v\right)_{\Omega}+({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla w,v)_{\Omega}+(\mu w,v)_{\Omega}\hskip 28.45274pt\forall v,w\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(4)

The bilinear form
a(\cdot.\cdot)
induces the following “energy” norm in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

\|v\|_{a}=\sqrt{a(v,v)}\,,

and thus (
3
) is well-posed thanks to the Lax-Milgram Lemma
(see, e.g.
[
11
, Lemma 25.2]
).

As it was mentioned in the introduction, our aim is to look for
discrete solutions that respect the same bounds as the solution of
(
3
). Thus, we make the following assumption on
u
, solution
of (
3
).

Assumption (A1):
We will suppose that the weak solution of (
3
) satisfies

0\leq u(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\kappa,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for almost all}\ \ \boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,

(5)

where
\kappa
is a known positive constant.

Remark 2.2
.

Assumption (A1) is, in fact, a re-statement of one of the consequences of the maximum principle for elliptic partial differential equations, see e.g.
[
13
]
.
The lower bound in (
5
) is not required to be equal to zero, but we set it as zero for clarity of exposition. In addition, the same results
proven in this work hold if
\kappa
is replaced by a non-negative continuous function
\kappa(\boldsymbol{x})
. In general, sharp bounds for the constant
\kappa
are not available, but in some cases
they can be obtained. For example, as a consequence of maximum and comparison principles (see e.g,
[
28
, Corollary 4.4]
)
the following bounds can be proven: for almost all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega

the solution
u
of (
3
) satisfies

-\frac{\|f\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{\mu}\leq u(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\frac{\|f\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{\mu}\,.

(6)

Moreover, if
f\geq 0
in
\Omega
we can sharpen the above bound to:

0\leq u(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\frac{\|f\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{\mu}\,,

(7)

for almost all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
. Hence, a reasonable estimate for
\kappa
is
\frac{\|f\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{\mu}

(and this is, in fact, the estimate we have used in our numerical experiments).

2.2
Triangulations, finite element spaces, and preliminary results

Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a conforming, shape-regular, quasi-uniform partition of
\Omega
into closed
simplices (or affine quadrilateral/hexahedra).
Over
{\mathcal{P}}
, and for
k\geq 1
, we define the finite element spaces

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{\mathcal{P}}:=

\displaystyle\,\{v_{h}\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega}):v_{h}|_{K}\in\mathfrak{R}(K)\ \ \ \forall K\in{\mathcal{P}}\}\,,

(8)

\displaystyle V_{\mathcal{P}}:=

\displaystyle\,\tilde{V}_{\mathcal{P}}\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\,,

(9)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(K)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{P}_{k}(K),\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ if $K$ is a simplex},\\
\mathbb{Q}_{k}(K),\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ if $K$ is an affine quadrilateral/hexahedral},\end{array}\right.

with
\mathbb{P}_{k}(K)
denoting the polynomials of total degree
k
on
K
and
\mathbb{Q}_{k}(K)
denotes the mapped space of polynomial of degree of at most
k
in each variable.

Remark 2.3
.

The results presented below can, in
principle, be extended to more general quadrilateral meshes.
Nevertheless, that would require technical diversions due to the
need to prove norm-equivalences (that are classical for mapped
elements). To avoid these diversions and keep the presentation
focused on the bound-preservation aspects, we restrict the
presentation to affine simplices and quadrilateral/hexahedral
meshes.

For a mesh
\mathcal{P}
, the following notations are used:

•

let
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\}
denote the
set of internal nodes, and the usual Lagrangian basis functions
associated to these nodes, spanning the space
V_{{\mathcal{P}}}
, are
denoted by
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{N}
;

•

let
\mathcal{F}_{I}
denote the set of internal facets,

\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
denotes the set of boundary facets, and

\mathcal{F}_{h}=\mathcal{F}_{I}\cup\mathcal{F}_{\partial}
denotes the set
of all facets of
{\mathcal{P}}
; for an element
K\in{\mathcal{P}}
the set of its

\mathcal{F}_{K}
;

•

for
K\in{\mathcal{P}}
,
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
, and a node

\displaystyle\omega_{K}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\{K^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}:K\cap K^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\omega_{F}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\{K\in{\mathcal{P}}:F\subset K\},

\displaystyle\omega_{i}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\{K\in{\mathcal{P}}:\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in K\};

•

for a facet
F\in\mathcal{F}_{I}
,
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
denotes the jump of a function across

.

The diameter of a set
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
h_{G}
,
and

stands for the mesh size.
We also define the mesh function
\mathfrak{h}
as a continuous,
element-wise linear function defined as a local average of meshes
commonly used in finite element analysis
[
26
, c.f.]
.
For this, we introduce the set of
vertices
of the mesh,

\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{M}
, and
define
\mathfrak{h}
as
the piecewise linear function prescribed by the nodal
values

\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i})=\dfrac{\sum_{K:\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\in K}h_{K}}{\#\{K:\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\in K\}}\,.

(12)

In the construction of the method, and its analysis, the following
mass-lumped
L^{2}
-inner product will be of importance: for every
polynomial function defined on
K
, in the space
V_{\mathcal{P}}
, we
define

\displaystyle(v_{h},w_{h})_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{d}v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})w_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),

(13)

which induces the norm
\lvert v_{h}\lvert_{h}=(v_{h},v_{h})_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}
in
V_{\mathcal{P}}
. This
norm is, in fact, equivalent to the standard
L^{2}(\Omega)
-norm. More
precisely, the following result, whose proof can be found in

[

]
, will be used repeatedly in our analysis

C,c>0
, independent of
h
, such that

c\,\sum_{i:\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in K}h_{K}^{d}v_{h}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\leq\|v_{h}\|_{0,K}^{2}\leq C\,\sum_{i:\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in K}h_{K}^{d}v_{h}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\qquad\forall\,K\in{\mathcal{P}},

(14)

and thus, as a consequence of the mesh-regularity of the mesh, the following holds:

c\,|v_{h}|_{h}^{2}\leq\|v_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq C\,|v_{h}|_{h}^{2},

(15)

for all
v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Remark 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the mass matrix, whose its entries are
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{i,j}=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{j}\phi_{i}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\quad{\rm for}\hskip 2.84544pti,j=1,\ldots,N.

Then, setting
v_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\phi_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{v}:=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{N})
,
as a consequence of (
15
) and
[

, the following equivalence also holds:

c\,|v_{h}|_{h}^{2}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}v_{i}\mathcal{M}_{ij}v_{j}=\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\mathcal{M}\boldsymbol{v}\leq C\lvert v_{h}\lvert_{h}^{2}\,,

(16)

where
C,c
are positive constants independent of
h
and
v_{h}
.

Next, we recall that the Lagrange interpolation operator is defined by
(see, e.g.,
[
10

)

\displaystyle i_{h}

\displaystyle:\mathcal{C}^{0}(\overline{\Omega}){\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\longrightarrow V_{\mathcal{P}},

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\longmapsto i_{h}v=\sum_{j=1}^{N}v(x_{j})\phi_{j}.

(17)

In addition, the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-orthogonal projection operator (see

[
10
, Chapter 22]
)
\pi:L^{2}(\Omega)\longrightarrow V_{\mathcal{P}}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\pi:L^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow V_{\mathcal{P}},

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\longmapsto\pi(w)\;\textrm{where}\;\left(\pi(w),v_{h}\right)_{\Omega}=(w,v_{h})_{\Omega}\quad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,.

(18)

With the above ingredients, we now state some inequalities and

a) Inverse inequality:
(

) For

, such

\displaystyle|v_{h}|_{\ell,p,K}\leq Ch_{K}^{m-\ell+d\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\right)}\,|v_{h}|_{m,q,K}\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,.

(19)

b) Discrete Trace inequality:

(
[
10

and

,

-orthogonal projection

2.3
The algebraic projection onto the admissible set

With Assumption (A1) in mind, we define the following subset of finite
element functions that satisfy the bound (
5
) at the degrees
of freedom:

\displaystyle V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}:=\{v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}:v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\in[0,\kappa]\ \ \text{for all}\ \ i=1,\ldots,N\}.

(23)

Every element
v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
can be split as the sum

v_{h}=v_{h}^{+}+v_{h}^{-}
, where
v_{h}^{+}
and
v_{h}^{-}
are given by

\displaystyle v_{h}^{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max\Big\{0,\min\{v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),\kappa\}\Big\}\,\phi_{i}\,,

(24)

and

\displaystyle v_{h}^{-}=v_{h}-v_{h}^{+}.

(25)

We refer to
v_{h}^{+}
and
v_{h}^{-}
as the
constrained

and
complementary
parts of
v_{h}
, respectively. Using this
decomposition we define the following algebraic projection

(\cdot)^{+}:V_{\mathcal{P}}\rightarrow V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}\quad,\quad v_{h}\rightarrow v_{h}^{+}\,.

(26)

Remark 2.5
.

If
\kappa
is not a constant value, but a non-negative continuous
function, the only difference in the definition of the projection is that in such a case
the constrained part is given by

v_{h}^{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max\Big\{0,\min\{v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),\kappa(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\}\Big\}\,\phi_{i}\,.

To avoid technical diversions we will not detail such a case, but the results proven in this paper
remain unchanged.

The following results concerning this projection will be used repeatedly.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let the operator
(\cdot)^{+}
be defined in (
26
).
There exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
h
, such that

\displaystyle\|w^{+}_{h}-v^{+}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C\|w_{h}-v_{h}\|_{0,\Omega},

(27)

\displaystyle\|v_{h}^{+}\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C\kappa,

(28)

for all
w_{h},v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

During this proof we drop the subindex
h
to lighten the notation. Let
w,v\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
.
We start noticing that if
w(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\leq v(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
, then

v^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-w^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\leq v(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-w(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
, and when

v(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\leq w(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
, we have
v^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-w^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\leq-(v(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-w(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))
. So,

|v^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-w^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})|\leq|v(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-w(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})|
. Now, set
e_{i}:=|v(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-w(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})|
, and
\boldsymbol{e}=(e_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
. Then, using (
16
) we obtain

\displaystyle\|v^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-w^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(v^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-w^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))\phi_{i}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{i}\phi_{i}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}e_{j}\mathcal{M}_{ij}e_{i}\leq C\|v-w\|_{0,\Omega}^{2},

which proves (
27
).
To prove (
28
) we use (
15
) and the mesh regularity to obtain that, for all
v\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|v^{+}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{d}(v^{+}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\right)^{2}\leq C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{d}\right)\kappa^{2}\leq C\kappa^{2},

concluding the proof.
∎

2.4
A linear stabilised method

As it was mentioned in the introduction, in the method’s definition we
need to introduce a linear stabilising term aimed at dampening the
oscillations caused by the dominating convection. In this work we
have chosen to use the Continuous Interior Penalty (CIP) method
originally proposed in
[
6
]
. This method adds the following
stabilising term to the Galerkin scheme:

\displaystyle J(u_{h},v_{h})=\gamma\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{I}}\int_{F}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,F}h_{F}^{2}\,\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket\nabla v_{h}\rrbracket\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

(29)

Here,

\gamma\geq 0
is a non-dimensional constant. Using this stabilising
term, the CIP stabilised method proposed in
[
6
]
reads as
follows: find
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
such that

a_{J}(u_{h},v_{h}):=a(u_{h},v_{h})+J(u_{h},v_{h})=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle_{\Omega}\hskip 28.45274pt\forall v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,.

(30)

The bilinear form
a_{J}(\cdot,\cdot)
induces the following norm on
V_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle\|v_{h}\|_{h}:=a_{J}(v_{h},v_{h})^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla v_{h}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\lVert\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}v_{h}\lVert_{0,\Omega}^{2}+J(v_{h},v_{h})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(31)

The following result will be of use in the error analysis.

Lemma 2.7
.

There exists
C>0
such that for
v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
, the
penalty term (
29
) satisfies the following property

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}\left\|h^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla v_{h}-\pi({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla v_{h})\right)\right\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}\leq C\,J(v_{h},v_{h}).

(32)

Moreover, the stabilising term (
29
) satisfies the following bounds: there exists
C>0
, independent of
h
and any
physical constant, such that for all
w_{h},v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
the following holds

\displaystyle J(v_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle\leq C\gamma\,h\,\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}|v_{h}|_{1,\Omega}|w_{h}|_{1,\Omega},

(33)

\displaystyle J(v_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle\leq C\gamma\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}}h_{K}^{-1}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,K}\|v_{h}\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}}h_{K}^{-1}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,K}\|w_{h}\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(34)

Proof.

The inequality (
32
) is a direct consequence of the result proven in

[
6
, Lemma 5]
. To prove (
33
) we use the
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the local trace result (
20
) and
the inverse inequality (
19
) to obtain

\displaystyle J(v_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{I}}\int_{F}\gamma\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,F}h_{F}^{2}\llbracket\nabla v_{h}\rrbracket\cdot\llbracket\nabla w_{h}\rrbracket\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\left\{\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{I}}\gamma\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,F}h_{F}^{2}\|\llbracket\nabla v_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,F}^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\{\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{I}}\gamma\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,F}h_{F}^{2}\|\llbracket\nabla w_{h}\rrbracket\|_{0,F}^{2}\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\gamma\left(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{P}}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,K}h_{K}^{2}\|\nabla\big(v_{h}|_{K}\big)\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{P}}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,K}h_{K}^{2}\|\nabla\big(w_{h}|_{K}\big)\|_{0,\partial K}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\gamma\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}}h_{K}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,K}\|\nabla v_{h}\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}}h_{K}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,K}\|\nabla w_{h}\|_{0,K}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

which proves (
33
). The proof of (
34
) follows from
the last inequality above and one further the application of the
inverse inequality (
19
).
∎

Remark 2.8
.

An alternative definition of the CIP stabilising term, that we will
also use in some of our numerical results, is given by penalising
the upwind gradient jumps rather than the normal gradient, that is,

{J(u_{h},v_{h})=\gamma\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{I}}\int_{F}\frac{\gamma_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,F}}h_{F}^{2}\,\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla u_{h}\rrbracket\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\rrbracket\textrm{d}s\,,}

(35)

where
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\geq 0
is a non-dimensional constant. In all
our proofs we have used the term given by (
29
), but the
proofs remain valid if we use (
35
) instead.

Remark 2.9
.

The choice of CIP stabilisation has been made for convinience and
simplicity of the presentation. In the numerical experiments we will
show that the addition of the linear stabilising term has a positive
effect on the performance of the method, more specifically, it will
improve the performance of the nonlinear solver greatly. From a
stability/error estimates point of view, it is also worth mentioning
that the exact same results proven in this work are also valid for
other choices of linear stabilisation, e.g., local projection
stabilisation
[
22
]
, or subgrid viscosity

[
17
]
, for example.

3
The finite element method

The finite element method proposed in this work reads as follows: find

u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
such that

a_{h}(u_{h};v_{h})=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle_{\Omega}\hskip 28.45274pt\forall v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,,

(36)

where the nonlinear form
a_{h}(\cdot;\cdot)
is defined by

\displaystyle a_{h}(u_{h};v_{h}):=a_{J}(u_{h}^{+},v_{h})+s(u_{h}^{-},v_{h})\,.

(37)

Here,
a_{J}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the bilinear form defined in (
30
),

u_{h}^{+}
and
u_{h}^{-}
are defined in (
24
),(
25
). The
bilinear form
s(\cdot,\cdot)
is added in order to control the
complementary part
u_{h}^{-}
, and is defined as follows:

\displaystyle s(v_{h},w_{h})=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{i}}\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{d-2}+\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{i}}\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{d-1}+{\mu}\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{d}\right)v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})w_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\,,

(38)

where the parameter
\alpha>0
is a non-dimensional constant. The
stabilising form
s(\cdot,\cdot)
induces the following norm in

V_{\mathcal{P}}
:

\displaystyle\|v_{h}\|_{s}=\sqrt{s(v_{h},v_{h})}\,.

(39)

The following result, that appears as a consequence of (
15
),
shows that the stabilising bilinear form
s(\cdot,\cdot)
indeed
controls
u_{h}^{-}
, more specifically it controls the kernel of the
projection
(\cdot)^{+}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a constant
C_{\rm equiv}>0
, depending only on the shape regularity of
{\mathcal{P}}
, such that

\displaystyle\|v_{h}\|_{h}^{2}\leq\frac{C_{\rm equiv}}{\alpha}\|v_{h}\|_{s}^{2}\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,,

(40)

where
\|\cdot\|_{h}
is the norm defined in (
31
).

Proof.

Using the inverse inequality (
19
), (
14
), and the mesh regularity we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla v_{h}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\lVert\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}v_{h}\lVert_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{i}}\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{d-2}+{\mu}\,\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{d}\right)v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{2}.

(41)

Also, (
34
) and (
14
) yield

\displaystyle J(v_{h},v_{h})

\displaystyle\leq C\gamma\sum_{K\in\mathcal{P}}h_{K}^{-1}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,K}\|v_{h}\|_{0,K}^{2}\leq C\gamma\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{P}}}h_{K}^{-1}h_{K}^{d}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,K}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in K}v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{i}}\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{d-1}v_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{2}.

Gathering the last two bounds proves (
40
) with
C_{\rm equiv}=(1+\gamma)C
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The result of Lemma
3.1
explains the
scaling factors chosen for defining
s(\cdot,\cdot)
.
Additionally, our formula (
38
) differs from those used in
reaction diffusion equations (as seen in
[
2
]
) by
including a specific term,
\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\omega_{i}}\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{d-1}
. This
inclusion is crucial for proving (
40
), which is important
for both ensuring the problem is well-posed and for the error
analysis. From our practical experience, this term also enhances
the performance of the nonlinear solver. As for the structure of
the stabilisation, we opted for a mass-lumped approach in defining

s(\cdot,\cdot)
, largely because of the monotonicity we
established in Lemma
3.3
.

3.1
Well-posedness

In this section, we analyse the existence and uniqueness of solutions
for (
37
). The first step is given by the following
monotonicity result, whose proof is identical to that of

[
2
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The bilinear form
s(\cdot,\cdot)
defined in (
38
) satisfies
the following inequalities:

\displaystyle s(v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-},v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+})\geq 0\qquad\forall v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}},\hskip 22.76228pt

(42)

\displaystyle s(v_{h}^{-},w_{h}-v_{h}^{+})\leq 0\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}},w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}.

(43)

Despite the fact that
s(\cdot,\cdot)
is monotone, the discrete
problem (
36
) is not driven by a monotone nonlinear
mapping. So, the well-posedness of (
37
) needs to be proven
using different arguments to those used in
[
2
]
. We will
first prove existence that will appear as a consequence of Brouwer’s
fixed point theorem, and only after linking any solution
u_{h}^{+}
of
(
36
) to a variational inequality, we will be able to prove a
uniqueness result for
u_{h}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that
\alpha\geq C_{\rm equiv}
. Then,
there exists
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
that solves (
36
).

Proof.

We begin by defining the bilinear form

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{J}(v_{h},w_{h}):=\left(\mathcal{D}\nabla v_{h},\nabla w_{h}\right)_{\Omega}+\mu(v_{h},w_{h})_{\Omega}+J(v_{h},w_{h})\hskip 28.45274pt\forall v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}},

and the mapping

\displaystyle T:

\displaystyle V_{\mathcal{P}}\longrightarrow V_{\mathcal{P}},

\displaystyle\widehat{u}_{h}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow u_{h}=T(\widehat{u}_{h}),

where
u_{h}=T(\widehat{u}_{h})
solves the following equation

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{J}(u_{h}^{+},v_{h})+s(u_{h}^{-},v_{h})=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle_{\Omega}-({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla{\widehat{u}_{h}^{+}},v_{h})_{\Omega}\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}.

(44)

We observe that
u_{h}
solves (
36
) if and only if

T(u_{h})=u_{h}
. So, the proof will consist on proving that
T

satisfies the hypotheses of Brouwer’s fixed point Theorem

[
28
, Theorem 10.41]
.

i)
T
is well-defined:
To prove that
T
is
well-defined, we see that (
44
) is a particular example of
the method proposed in
[
2
]
. So, using

[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
, there exists a unique solution
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
of (
44
), and thus
T
is well-defined.

ii)

T
is continuous:

Since we have supposed that
\alpha
is large enough, we can use the
monotonicity result proven in
[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
and obtain that, for all

v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}

\tilde{a}_{J}(v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+},v_{h}-w_{h})+s(v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-},v_{h}-w_{h})\geq C\|v_{h}-w_{h}\|_{h}^{2}\,,

where
C>0
is independent of
h
.
Next, suppose that for
\widehat{v}_{h},\widehat{w}_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}

and let
v_{h}=T(\widehat{v}_{h})
and
w_{h}=T(\widehat{w}_{h})
. Integrating by
parts, using Hölder’s inequality, Lemma
2.6
, and (
40
)
we obtain

\displaystyle C\|v_{h}-w_{h}\|_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{a}_{J}(v_{h}^{+}-w_{h}^{+},v_{h}-w_{h})+s({v_{h}^{-}-w_{h}^{-}},v_{h}-w_{h})

\displaystyle=-({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla(\widehat{v}_{h}^{+}-\widehat{w}_{h}^{+}),v_{h}-w_{h})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{v}_{h}^{+}-\widehat{w}_{h}^{+},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla(v_{h}-w_{h}))_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\|\widehat{v}_{h}-\widehat{w}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}|v_{h}-w_{h}|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\|\widehat{v}_{h}-\widehat{w}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\mathcal{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(v_{h}-w_{h})\|_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{d_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\|\widehat{v}_{h}-\widehat{w}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|v_{h}-w_{h}\|_{h}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\|T(\widehat{v}_{h})-T(\widehat{w}_{h})\|_{h}\leq C\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{d_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\|\widehat{v}_{h}-\widehat{w}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega},

and
T
is Lipschitz continuous.

iii)
There exists
R>0
, such that

T(B(0,R))\subseteq B(0,R)
:
Let
\widehat{z}_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
be
arbitrary and
z_{h}=T(\widehat{z}_{h})
. By using
v_{h}=z_{h}^{+}
in
(
44
), Cauchy-Schwarz’s and Hölder’s inequalities, and (
28
) we get

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{J}(z_{h}^{+},z_{h}^{+})+\underbrace{s(z_{h}^{-},z_{h}^{+})}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle=\langle f,z_{h}^{+}\rangle_{\Omega}-({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla\widehat{z}_{h}^{+},z_{h}^{+})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{0,\Omega}\|z_{h}^{+}\|_{0,\Omega}+\,(\widehat{z}_{h}^{+},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla z_{h}^{+})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|f\|_{0,\Omega}\,\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|z_{h}^{+}\|_{h}+\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\|\widehat{z}_{h}^{+}\|_{0,\Omega}d_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|z_{h}^{+}\|_{h}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\left\{\frac{\|f\|_{0,\Omega}}{\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\,\kappa}{d_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right\}\,\|z_{h}^{+}\|_{h}\,,

and so
z_{h}^{+}
satisfies

\|z_{h}^{+}\|_{h}\leq C\,\left\{\frac{\|f\|_{0,\Omega}}{\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\,\kappa}{d_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right\}\,.

(45)

Next, we take
v_{h}=z_{h}^{-}
in (
44
). Integrating by
parts and using Hölder’s inequality we get

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{J}(z_{h}^{+},z_{h}^{-})+s(z_{h}^{-},z_{h}^{-})

\displaystyle=\langle f,z_{h}^{-}\rangle_{\Omega}-({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla{\widehat{z}_{h}^{+}},z_{h}^{-})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{0,\Omega}\|z_{h}^{-}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|{\widehat{z}_{h}^{+}}\|_{0,\Omega}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}|z_{h}^{-}|_{1,\Omega}.

Now using (
28
) we have

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{J}(z_{h}^{+},z_{h}^{-})+s(z_{h}^{-},z_{h}^{-})

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|f\|_{0,\Omega}\|z_{h}^{-}\|_{0,\Omega}+\kappa\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}|z_{h}^{-}|_{1,\Omega}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{\|f\|_{0,\Omega}}{\mu^{{1}/{2}}}+\kappa\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{d_{0}^{{1}/{2}}}\right)\|z_{h}^{-}\|_{h},

and applying (
40
) and Young’s inequality we get

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{J}(z_{h}^{+},z_{h}^{-})+s(z_{h}^{-},z_{h}^{-})

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{\|f\|_{0,\Omega}}{\mu^{{1}/{2}}}+\kappa\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}}{d_{0}^{{1}/{2}}}\right)^{2}+\frac{s(z_{h}^{-},z_{h}^{-})}{2}

\displaystyle=:\frac{M}{2}+\frac{s(z_{h}^{-},z_{h}^{-})}{2}.

Using Young’s and Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequalities for

\tilde{a}_{J}(z_{h}^{+},z_{h}^{-})
, and (
45
), yields

\displaystyle-\delta\tilde{a}_{J}(z_{h}^{-},z_{h}^{-})+s(z_{h}^{-},z_{h}^{-})\leq M+C\delta^{-1}\tilde{a}_{J}(z_{h}^{+},z_{h}^{+})\leq M+{C\,\,\delta^{-1}\left\{\frac{\|f\|_{0,\Omega}}{\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\,\kappa}{d_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right\}}\,,

for any
\delta>0
. Then, choosing
\delta
small enough, and
Lemma
3.1
we get

\displaystyle\|z_{h}^{-}\|_{h}\leq C\big(-\delta\tilde{a}_{J}(z_{h}^{-},z_{h}^{-})+s(z_{h}^{-},z_{h}^{-})\big)\leq C_{2}(f,\mu,\mathcal{D},{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\kappa),

where

{C_{2}(f,\mu,\mathcal{D},{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\kappa)=M+C\,\left\{\frac{\|f\|_{0,\Omega}}{\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\,\kappa}{d_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right\}\,.}

Hence,
z_{h}=T(\widehat{z}_{h})
satisfies the
following (uniform) bound

\displaystyle\|z_{h}\|_{h}

\displaystyle\leq\|z_{h}^{-}\|_{h}+\|z_{h}^{+}\|_{h}\leq C\,\left\{\frac{\|f\|_{0,\Omega}}{\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\,\kappa}{d_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right\}+C_{2}(f,\mu,\mathcal{D},{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\kappa)=:R.

Therefore,
z_{h}=T(\widehat{z}_{h})\in B(0,R)
, for every
\widehat{z}_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
, which shows that
T(B(0,R))\subseteq B(0,R)
.

Hence, using Brouwer’s fixed point theorem, there exists
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
such that
T(u_{h})=u_{h}
. In other words, problem
(
36
) has at least one solution.
∎

The proof of the last result does not imply uniqueness of
solutions. The next two results will close that gap, whilst at the
same time providing a very useful characterisation for
u_{h}^{+}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
solve (
36
). Then,
u_{h}^{+}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
satisfies

\displaystyle a_{J}(u_{h}^{+},v_{h}-u_{h}^{+})\geq\langle f,v_{h}-u_{h}^{+}\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+},

(46)

where
a_{J}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in (
30
). In addition,
u^{-}_{h}
is the unique solution of

\displaystyle s(u_{h}^{-},v_{h})=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle-a_{J}(u_{h}^{+},v_{h})\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}.

(47)

Proof.

Testing (
36
) with
v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
and
u_{h}^{+}
as test functions gives

\displaystyle a_{J}(u_{h}^{+},v_{h})+s(u_{h}^{-},v_{h})=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle_{\Omega},

\displaystyle a_{J}(u_{h}^{+},u_{h}^{+})+s(u_{h}^{-},u_{h}^{+})=\langle f,u_{h}^{+}\rangle_{\Omega}.

Subtracting the second equation from the first one we arrive at

\displaystyle a_{J}(u_{h}^{+},v_{h}-u_{h}^{+})+s(u_{h}^{-},v_{h}-u_{h}^{+})=\langle f,v_{h}-u_{h}^{+}\rangle_{\Omega}\hskip 14.22636pt\forall v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+},

and then, using (
43
) in Lemma
3.3
we get that

u_{h}^{+}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
satisfies (
46
).
Finally, since
s(\cdot,\cdot)
is an elliptic bilinear form in

V_{\mathcal{P}}
,
u_{h}^{-}
is the unique solution of (
47
), thus
concluding the proof.
∎

The last result provides a characterisation of any solution of
(
36
) as the solution of the two successive problems
(
46
), (
47
).

Corollary 3.6
.

Problem (
36
) has a unique solution.

Proof.

If
u_{1},u_{2}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
satisfy (
36
), then

u_{1}^{+}
and
u_{2}^{+}
satisfy (
46
). But the
solution of (
46
) is unique, thanks to Stampacchia’s
Theorem (see
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
). So,

u_{1}^{+}=u_{2}^{+}
. Therefore, (
47
) holds for

u_{1}^{-}
and
u_{2}^{-}
, but
u_{1}^{+}=u_{2}^{+}
shows that
the right hand side of both problems are the same, and since

s(\cdot,\cdot)
is an elliptic bilinear form, then

u_{1}^{-}=u_{2}^{-}
. Thus,
u_{1}=u_{1}^{+}+u_{1}^{-}=u_{2}^{+}+u_{2}^{-}=u_{2}
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

We finish this section by remarking that the complementary part

u_{h}^{-}
of
u_{h}
has a local support. In fact, we first remark that

\displaystyle(u_{h}^{+}+u_{h}^{-})^{+}=(u_{h}^{+}+u_{h}-u_{h}^{+})^{+}=u_{h}^{+}.

This implies that
u_{h}^{-}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\neq 0
if and only
if
u_{h}^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\kappa
or

u_{h}^{+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=0
. This shows that the support of

u_{h}^{-}
is contained in the region where
u_{h}^{+}=0
or

u_{h}^{+}=\kappa
. In other words,
u_{h}^{-}
has a localised support,
restricted to the regions where the constraint imposed in the
definition of
V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
is active.

4
Error analysis

This section is devoted to the error analysis of the method
(
36
). Since our interest in this work is to provide a
discrete solution that respects the bounds given by the continuous
problem, error estimates will be proven for the constrained part

u_{h}^{+}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
u\in H^{k+1}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be the solution of
(
1
) and
u_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
be the solution of
(
36
). Then, there exists
C>0
independent of
\mathcal{D}
,

\mu
,
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, and
h
, such that

\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{h}\leq Ch^{k}\left(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}+h\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)|u|_{k+1,\Omega}.

(48)

Proof.

As usual we decompose the error
u-u_{h}^{+}
as follows

\displaystyle u-u_{h}^{+}=(u-\pi(u))+(\pi(u)-u_{h}^{+})=:\eta_{h}+e_{h}\,,

(49)

where
\pi
is the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-orthogonal projection defined in
(
18
).

The bound for
\eta_{h}
is a direct consequence of (
22
) and
(
33
). In fact, using the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young
inequalities we get

\displaystyle\|\eta_{h}\|_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{J}(\eta_{h},\eta_{h})=(\mathcal{D}\nabla\eta_{h},\nabla\eta_{h})_{\Omega}+\mu(\eta_{h},\eta_{h})_{\Omega}+J(\eta_{h},\eta_{h})

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}|\eta_{h}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\mu\|\eta_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+h\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}|\eta_{h}|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2k}\left(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}+h\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}+h^{2}\mu\right)|u|_{k+1,\Omega}^{2}.

Next, to bound
\|e_{h}\|_{h}
we use the ellipticity of
a_{J}(\cdot,\cdot)
to get

\|e_{h}\|_{h}^{2}=a_{J}(\eta_{h},e_{h})-a_{J}(u-u_{h}^{+},\pi(u)-u_{h}^{+})=:{\rm I}+{\rm II}.

(50)

We start decomposing I as follows:

{\rm I}=(\mathcal{D}\nabla\eta_{h},\nabla e_{h})_{\Omega}+({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla\eta_{h},e_{h})_{\Omega}+\mu(\eta_{h},e_{h})_{\Omega}+J(\eta_{h},e_{h})=:{\rm(a)+(b)+(c)+(d)}\,,

(51)

and bound each one of the above terms separately. Firstly, by
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, then using (
22
) we have

{\rm(a)}\leq\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}|\eta_{h}|_{1,\Omega}\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla e_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq Ch^{k}\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\|e_{h}\|_{h}\,.

(52)

To bound (b) we first integrate by parts,
use the orthogonality of
\pi
, and Lemma
2.7
.

\begin{split}{\rm(b)}&=(\pi(u)-u,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla e_{h})_{\Omega}\\
&=\big(\pi(u)-u,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla e_{h}-\pi({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla e_{h})\big)_{\Omega}\\
&\leq\|\eta_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla e_{h}-\pi({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla e_{h})\|_{0,\Omega}\\
&\leq Ch^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\,\|e_{h}\|_{h}\,.\end{split}

(53)

The term (c) is handled analagously to (a)

{\rm(c)}\leq\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\eta_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\|\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}e_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq Ch^{k+1}\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\|e_{h}\|_{h}\,.

(54)

Finally, for (d), since
J(\cdot,\cdot)
is semi-positive definite
and symmetric we apply Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality followed by
Lemma
2.7
and (
22
)

\begin{split}{\rm(d)}&\leq J(\eta_{h},\eta_{h})^{\frac{1}{2}}J(e_{h},e_{h})^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq Ch^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\,\|e_{h}\|_{h}\,.\end{split}

(55)

Substituting (
52
), (
53
),
(
54
), and (
55
) into (
51
) we
obtain the following bound for I:

\textrm{I}\leq Ch^{k}\left(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}+h\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\|e_{h}\|_{h}\,.

(56)

To bound II we first recall that
(\pi(u))^{-}=\pi(u)-(\pi(u))^{+}
and then

\displaystyle{\rm II}=a_{J}(u-u_{h}^{+},e_{h})=a_{J}(u-u_{h}^{+},(\pi(u))^{+}-u_{h}^{+})+a_{J}(u-u_{h}^{+},(\pi(u))^{-}).

Now, thanks to the regularity of
u
, we note that

J(u,(\pi(u))^{+}-u_{h}^{+})=0
. Hence, using that
u_{h}^{+}
solves
(
46
), we have

\displaystyle a_{J}(u-u_{h}^{+},(\pi(u))^{+}-u_{h}^{+})

\displaystyle=a_{J}(u,(\pi(u))^{+}-u_{h}^{+})-a_{J}(u_{h}^{+},(\pi(u))^{+}-u_{h}^{+})

\displaystyle=\langle f,(\pi(u))^{+}-u_{h}^{+}\rangle_{\Omega}-a_{J}(u_{h}^{+},(\pi(u))^{+}-u_{h}^{+})\leq 0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\rm II}=a_{J}(u-u_{h}^{+},e_{h})\leq a_{J}(u-u_{h}^{+},(\pi(u))^{-}).

The term in the right-hand side of the above inequality is, in
essence, a consistency error, and will require special treatment.
Let
i_{h}
be the Lagrange interpolant defined in
(
17
). Since
u(x)\in[0,\kappa]
a.e. in
\Omega
, then

i_{h}(u)\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
. So,
i_{h}(u)=(i_{h}(u))^{+}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
, which implies
(i_{h}(u))^{-}=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle a_{J}(u-u^{+}_{h},(\pi(u))^{-})=a_{J}(u-u^{+}_{h},(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-}).

So, using the definition of
a_{J}(\cdot,\cdot)
we bound II as follows

\begin{split}{\rm II}&\leq a_{J}(u-{u_{h}^{+}},(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-})\\
&=(\mathcal{D}\nabla(u-u_{h}^{+}),\nabla((\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-})_{\Omega}+({\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla(u-u_{h}^{+}),(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-})_{\Omega}\\
&\quad+\mu(u-u_{h}^{+},(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-})_{\Omega}+J(u-u_{h}^{+},(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-})\\
&={\rm(e)+(f)+(g)+(h)}.\end{split}

(57)

We begin with (e), which can be bounded by Cauchy-Schwarz
inequality

\begin{split}{\rm(e)}&\leq\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(u-u_{h}^{+})\|_{0,\Omega}|(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-}|_{1,\Omega}\\
&\leq Ch^{-1}\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(u-u_{h}^{+})\|_{0,\Omega}\|(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-}\|_{0,\Omega},\end{split}

(58)

using an inverse inequality. Then, as
(\cdot)^{-}
is Lipschitz
continuous by Lemma
2.6

\begin{split}{\rm(e)}&\leq Ch^{-1}\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathcal{D}^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(u-u_{h}^{+})\|_{0,\Omega}\|\pi(u)-i_{h}(u)\|_{0,\Omega}\\
&\leq Ch^{k}\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{h}\,,\end{split}

by the approximation properties for
i_{h}
and
\pi
given in
(
21
) and (
22
). For (f) we begin by integrating
by parts

\begin{split}{\rm(f)}&=-\big(u-u_{h}^{+},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla((\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-})\big)_{\Omega}\\
&\leq\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{0,\Omega}|(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-}|_{1,\Omega}\,,\end{split}

using the Cauchy-Schwarz. inequality Then again, by an inverse estimate

\begin{split}{\rm(f)}&\leq Ch^{-1}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{0,\Omega}\|(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
&\leq Ch^{k}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}\,\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{h}\,,\end{split}

(59)

again using Lipschitz continuiuty of
(\cdot)^{-}
and the
approximation properties for
i_{h}
and
\pi
. Now (g) is controlled
in much the same way using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\begin{split}{\rm(g)}&\leq\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}(u-u_{h}^{+})\|_{0,\Omega}\|(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-}\|_{0,\Omega}\\
&\leq Ch^{k+1}\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{h}\,,\end{split}

(60)

and by Lipschitz continuiuty of
(\cdot)^{-}
and the approximation
properties for
i_{h}
and
\pi
. Finally for (h) Cauchy-Schwarz implies

\begin{split}{\rm(h)}&\leq J(u-u_{h}^{+},u-u_{h}^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}J((\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-},(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-})^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq C\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\,h^{\frac{1}{2}}|(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-}|_{1,\Omega}\,J(u-u_{h}^{+},u-u_{h}^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}},\end{split}

using Lemma
2.7
. Now by an inverse inequality

\begin{split}{\rm(h)}&\leq C\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}\,h^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|(\pi(u))^{-}-(i_{h}(u))^{-}\|_{0,\Omega}\,J(u-u_{h}^{+},u-u_{h}^{+})^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq Ch^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{h},\end{split}

(61)

through the approximability of
i_{h}
and
\pi
and by Lipschitz
continuiuty of
(\cdot)^{-}
.

Combining (
58
), (
59
), (
60
)
and (
61
) we arrive to the following bound for II:

{\rm II}\leq Ch^{k}\left(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}+h\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{h}|u|_{k+1,\Omega}.

(62)

Hence, inserting (
56
) and (
62
) into (
50
), and using Young’s inequality, we obtain the following bound for
\|e_{h}\|_{h}
:

\|e_{h}\|_{h}^{2}\leq Ch^{2k}\big(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}+h\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)^{2}\,|u|_{k+1,\Omega}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|e_{h}\|_{h}^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{h}^{2}.

Finally, collecting the bounds that have been obtained for
\|e_{h}\|_{h}
and
\|\eta_{h}\|_{h}
yields

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{h}

\displaystyle\leq\|e_{h}\|_{h}+\|\eta_{h}\|_{h}

\displaystyle\leq{Ch^{k}}\,\left(\|\mathcal{D}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0,\infty,\Omega}^{\frac{1}{2}}+h\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)|u|_{k+1,\Omega}+\frac{1}{2}\|u-u_{h}^{+}\|_{h}\,,

and (
48
) follows rearranging terms.
∎

4.1
The extension to problems with non-homogeneous boundary conditions

Although the presentation of the method and its analysis have been
done assuming that the Dirichlet boundary conditions are homogeneous,
many problems of practical interest involve non-homogeneous boundary
conditions. So, in this section we briefly describe the method for a
non-homogeneous Dirichlet problem. Let us assume that, instead of
(
1
) we are interested in solving

\displaystyle-{\rm div}(\mathcal{D}\nabla u)+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\cdot\nabla u+\mu u

\displaystyle=f\hskip 28.45274pt{\rm in}\;\Omega,

(63)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=g\hskip 28.45274pt{\rm on}\;\partial\Omega\,,

where
g\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
,
g\geq 0
on

\partial\Omega
. Thanks to the definition of
\kappa
and the
maximum and comparison principles for partial differential equations,

\kappa\geq\|g\|_{0,\infty,\partial\Omega}
. For simplicity, we
will assume that
g
is the trace of a function belonging to

\tilde{V}_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Let us consider the set of nodes of the triangulation
(including boundary nodes):
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{P}
, and, as
earlier in the manuscript, we denote the interior ones by

\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N},N<P
. Let us define the following
extension of
g
into
\Omega
:
u_{h,g}\in\tilde{V}_{\mathcal{P}}

defined as follows

u_{h,g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}g(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})&\textrm{if}\;i\in\{N+1,\ldots,P\}\,,\\
0&\textrm{else}\,.\end{array}\right.

(64)

With these ingredients the analogue of (
36
) for the
non-homogeneous boundary data is: Find
\tilde{u}_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}

such that

a_{J}\big((\tilde{u}_{h}+u_{h,g})^{+},v_{h}\big)+s\big((\tilde{u}_{h}+u_{h,g})^{-},v_{h}\big)=\langle f,v_{h}\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,.

(65)

The fundamental reason for choosing
u_{h,g}
as
extension of
g
, rather than any other, is that at each node of

{\mathcal{P}}
either
\tilde{u}_{h}
or
u_{h,g}
are zero, and then
the following important property holds:

(\tilde{u}_{h}+u_{h,g})^{+}=\tilde{u}_{h}^{+}+u_{h,g}\,,

(66)

and, as a consequence
(\tilde{u}_{h}+u_{h,g})^{-}=\tilde{u}_{h}^{-}
(as
u_{h,g}^{-}=0
since
\kappa\geq\|g\|_{0,\infty,\partial\Omega}
). Thus,
(
65
) can be rewritten as: Find
\tilde{u}_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}

such that

a_{J}\big(\tilde{u}_{h}^{+},v_{h}\big)+s\big(\tilde{u}_{h}^{-},v_{h}\big)=(f,v_{h})_{\Omega}-a_{J}\big(u_{h,g},v_{h}\big)\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}\,.

(67)

Now the proof of Theorem
3.4
remains unchanged, and thus
(
67
) has a solution. For the uniqueness result, the same
arguments used in the proof of Lemma
3.5
lead to the fact
that
\tilde{u}_{h}^{+}
solves the following variational inequality:

\tilde{u}_{h}^{+}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}
, and

a_{J}(\tilde{u}_{h}^{+},v_{h}-\tilde{u}_{h}^{+})\geq\langle f,v_{h}-\tilde{u}_{h}^{+}\rangle_{\Omega}-a_{J}\big(u_{h,g},v_{h}-\tilde{u}_{h}^{+}\big)\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}\,.

(68)

Since (
68
) has a unique solution thanks to Stampacchia’s Theorem,
the existence and uniqueness of solution for (
65
) follows using exactly the same arguments
as those for (
36
). Finally, to analyse the error we notice that, assuming enough regularity for the exact solution we get

a_{J}(u,v_{h}-\tilde{u}_{h}^{+})=\langle f,v_{h}-u_{h}^{+}\rangle_{\Omega}\,,

for all
v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}
. So, the following variational inequality holds

a_{J}((\tilde{u}_{h}+u_{h,g})^{+}-u,v_{h}-u_{h}^{+})\geq 0\qquad\forall\,v_{h}\in V_{\mathcal{P}}^{+}\,,

(69)

which is instrumental in the bound for the bound of II in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
Hence, the error analysis follows very similar arguments as those presented in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The goal of this numerical experiment is twofold. First, we aim at
testing the capabilities of the current bound preserving method (BPM)
(
36
) to suppress over- and under-shoots in regions where the
contraint is not needed. In fact, for this example
\kappa=1
in the
whole domain, while there are two inner layers inside the domain where
the solution varies rapidly from
0
to approximately
0.5
, and a
second one where it does from
0.5
to
1
. Now, around those layers
the BPM will control the undershoot at
u_{h}^{+}=0
, but there is no
explicit control on the region where the solution is approximately

0.5
. Our intent is to assess the capability of the current method to
suppress the possible overshoot at that region, even if the nonlinear
stabilisation is not active in it. The second goal of this numerical
experiment is to provide numerical evidence that the addition of the
(linear) CIP stabilisation on
u_{h}^{+}
has a positive impact on the
performance of the method.

6
Conclusions and outlook

The method proposed in this work constitutes an inexpensive and simple
way to impose global bounds in the numerical solution to
convection-diffusion equations (at least at the nodes of the
triangulation, and globally for the lowest order case
k=1
). Other
than its well-posedness, we proved optimal order error estimates for
the constrained part
u_{h}^{+}
. It is worth stressing that
optimal-order error estimates are not common for this type of method
(even in the piecewise linear case, see
[
4
]
for more
details), so we believe this result constitutes a highlight of this
paper. The numerical experiments presented show that it constitutes a
competitive alternative to previously existing alternatives.

Looking forward, the complementary part
u_{h}^{-}
has a localised
support. This has been checked numerically, but the important question
about whether this fact can be exploited in a posteriori error
analysis remains an open question. In addition, the generality of this
approach makes the applications of the method to unsteady nonlinear
fluids problems, for example of porous media and Allen-Cahn type,
quite natural. These, amongst other areas, are topics of ongoing
research that shall be reported in upcoming publications.
