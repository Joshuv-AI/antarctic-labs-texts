Topological games in Ramsey spaces
Julián C. Cano, Carlos A. Di Prisco

Abstract
Topological Ramsey theory studies a class of combinatorial topological spaces, called topological Ramsey spaces, unifying the essential features of those combinatorial frames where the Ramsey property is equivalent to Baire property. In this article, we expose a general overview of the combinatorial structure of topological Ramsey spaces and their main properties, and we propose an alternative proof of abstract Ellentuck theorem for a large family of topological Ramsey spaces. Additionally, we present the notion of selective topological Ramsey space, and generalize Kastanas games in order to characterize the Baire property for the family of selective topological Ramsey spaces. Key words and phrases: topological Ramsey space, Ellentuck topology, Ramsey and Baire properties, topological game. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 03E02, 03E05, 05D10.



1
Introduction

Infinite dimensional Ramsey theory has developed in different directions since its beginning in the 1960’s. For instance, topological Ramsey spaces, first introduced by Carlson and Simpson in
[
5
]
, and later reformulated and extended by Todorčević in
[
31
]
, provide a general framework to study a wide variety of combinatorial properties that have topological counterparts.

The prototype example of the topological characterization of an infinite dimensional combinatorial property is Ellentuck’s result in
[
13
]
, which establishes an infinite dimensional version of Ramsey’s theorem restricted to Baire measurable partitions of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
. On the Ramsey property of subsets of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, Erdős and Rado proved in
[
14
]
, using the axiom of choice, that there exist subsets of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
which are not Ramsey. Later, Galvin and Prikry showed in
[
16
]
that Borel subsets of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
in the metrizable topology are Ramsey, and Silver in
[
29
]
extended this fact to analytic subsets. Finally, Ellentuck in
[
13
]
characterized topologically Ramsey subsets of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
as those having the Baire property with respect to the exponential topology. Ellentuck’s proof of this result uses a powerful combinatorial technique, due to Nash-Williams in
[
27
]
, now known as
combinatorial forcing
(see
[
31
,
30
,
18
]
). However, Matet in
[
23
]
gives a shorter proof of Ellentuck’s result avoiding the use of combinatorial forcing.

Ellentuck’s theorem can be generalized using the combinatorial properties of two types of families of infinite subsets of integers, namely selective and semiselective coideals. This kind of coideals allow to construct local versions of Ellentuck’s space, where homogeneity of Borel partitions is delimited and located in a fixed coideal. The interaction between Ellenctuk’s theorem and coideals on
\mathbb{N}
has been studied by Mathias, Matet and Todorčević in
[
24
,
22
,
30
]
for the notion of selectivity, and extended by Farah and Todorčević in
[
15
,
31
]
for the notion of semiselectivity.

Kastanas in
[
20
]
gave a characterization of the Ramsey property of subsets of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
in terms of determined topological games. For a fixed partition of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
into finite pieces, he presents an infinite game such that either player has a winning strategy if and only if some homogeneous set for the partition can be constructed. Local versions of Kastanas’ game has been proposed by Matet in
[
22
]
for the class of selective coideals, and extended by Di Prisco, Mijares and Uzcátegui in
[
7
]
for the class of semiselective coideals.

Topological Ramsey spaces deal with the optimal conditions that must be imposed to a combinatorial structure in order to guarantee the existence of homogeneous substructures with respect to finite partitions whose parts are topologically definable. In this regard, topological Ramsey spaces form a general combinatorial framework in which the respective notions of Ramsey property and Baire property are equivalent.

The Ellentuck’s space
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
(
[
13
]
) is the quintessential example of a topological Ramsey space, although there are several combinatorial contexts that correspond to specific examples of topological Ramsey spaces, such as: the space of infinite block sequences of finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
(
[
25
]
); the space of strong subtrees of a fixed rooted pruned finitely branching tree of height
\omega
(
[
26
]
); the space of infinite partitions of
\mathbb{N}
(
[
4
]
); the space of reduced echelon infinite matrices over a fixed finite field (
[
2
]
); the space of rapidly increasing infinite sequences of variable words over a fixed alphabet (
[
3
]
); the space of infinite block sequences of vectors of finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
(
[
17
]
); the space of infinite block sequences of vectors of located variable words over a fixed alphabet (
[
31
]
); the hierarchy of Ramsey spaces
\{\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha<\omega_{1}}
corresponding to recursive projections of trees (
[
10
,
11
]
); the hierarchy of Ramsey spaces
\{\mathcal{E}_{k}\}_{k<\omega}
corresponding to high dimensional Ellentuck spaces (
[
8
]
); construction of topological Ramsey spaces through uniform barriers on
\mathbb{N}
(
[
9
]
); construction of topological Ramsey spaces from Fraïssé classes (
[
12
]
); spaces of alternating equivalence relations for finite partitions of
\mathbb{N}
(
[
21
]
); among others.

The main objective of this article is to propose an alternative proof of the abstract Ellentuck theorem about the Ramsey property for subsets of a topological Ramsey space given in
[
31
]
, and also to characterize this property in terms of infinite games through an abstract version of Kastanas’ theorem.

The article starts with a quick review of the Ramsey property of subsets of the Ellentuck space of infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
and the Kastanas game characterization (Section
2
). Then follows a presentation of Todorčević’s formulation of topological Ramsey spaces (Section
3
). After that, we define strong topological Ramsey spaces, and give an alternative proof of the abstract Ellentuck theorem for this class of Ramsey spaces that include a wide family of topological Ramsey spaces (Section
4
). Finally, we present an abstract notion of selectivity, and generalize the Kastanas game characterization of the Ramsey property to selective topological Ramsey spaces (Section
5
).

At this point, let us remember some important concepts related to any topological space
\mathcal{R}
. First, a set
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
is
nowhere dense
if for each basic open set
\mathcal{O}
there is a basic open subset
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
such that
\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{Q}=\emptyset
. Also, a set
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
is
meager
if
\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{N}_{n}
where each set
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is nowhere dense. In addition, a set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
has the
abstract Baire property
if for every basic open set
\mathcal{O}
there exists some basic open subset
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
such that either
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{Q}=\emptyset
. Finally, a set
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
has the
Baire property
if the set
\mathcal{B}\triangle\mathcal{O}
is meager for some open set
\mathcal{O}
.

2
Ramsey property on Ellentuck space

In this section, we briefly present the Ramsey property for families of infinite subsets of integers, and expose both
Ellentuck theorem
and
Kastanas theorem
. To do this, let us first fix some notation:

Given any countably infinite set
X
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
[X]^{n}
the set of subsets of
X
with exactly
n
elements; also, we denote by
[X]^{<\omega}
the set of finite subsets of
X
, hence
[X]^{<\omega}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[X]^{n}
; and analogously, we denote by
[X]^{\omega}
the set of infinite subsets of
X
.

Given
s\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
and
A\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, the notation
s\sqsubset A
means that
s
is initial segment of
A
. We denote by
A/s
the set
A/s=\{n\in A\,|\,s<n\}
, where we write
s<n
whenever
s=\emptyset
or
\max s<n
. Lastly, for each
s\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
and each
B\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
we define the set
[s,B]
as follows:

[s,B]=\{A\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\,|\,A\subseteq B\wedge s\sqsubset A\}
.

The collection
\left\{[s,B]\;|\;s\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}\wedge B\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\right\}
forms an uncountable basis of open-closed sets for a non-metrizable topology over
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, called the
Ellentuck topology
of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
. It should be noted that the Ellentuck topology is finer than the metrizable topology, which is obtained by identifying
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
as a subspace of the Cantor space
2^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Ramsey theorem
is a deep generalization of the combinatorial result known as the
pigeonhole principle
, which states that if any infinite set is partitioned into a finite number of pieces, then at least one of these pieces is infinite.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Ramsey,
[
28
]
). For all
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
n,k\neq 0
, if
[\mathbb{N}]^{n}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}G_{i}
is any finite partition of
[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
, then there is
H\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
such that
[H]^{n}\subseteq G_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

A set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
determines a partition of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
into two parts, namely
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\complement}
; so, one can ask when there exists some
H\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
such that
[H]^{\omega}
is contained in one of these parts. This question motivated the definition of the Ramsey property, which is a combinatorial quality about subsets of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
that captures the optimal conditions to guarantee an ideal infinite dimensional version of Ramsey theorem.

Definition 2.2
.

A set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
is
Ramsey
if for all
N\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
and
s\in[N]^{<\omega}
, there is some
M\in[N]^{\omega}
such that
s\in[M]^{<\omega}
and either
[s,M]\subseteq\mathcal{A}
or
[s,M]\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
. Moreover, if the second option always holds, then we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
Ramsey null
.

The Ramsey property has two important set-theoretic characterizations. The first one is given by the Ellentuck theorem, which states a topological characterization of the Ramsey property through the Baire property with respect to the Ellentuck topology on
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
. The second one is given by the Kastanas theorem, which states a game-theoretic characterization of the Ramsey property through topological games on
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

(Ellentuck,
[
13
]
). Consider the space
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
endowed with the Ellentuck topology.

1.

For any set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\mathcal{A}
is Ramsey null.

(b)

\mathcal{A}
is nowhere dense.

(c)

\mathcal{A}
is meager.

2.

For any set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\mathcal{A}
is Ramsey.

(b)

\mathcal{A}
has the abstract Baire property.

(c)

\mathcal{A}
has the Baire property.

3.

If
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{P}_{i}
is any finite partition of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, where each piece
\mathcal{P}_{i}
has the Baire property in the Ellentuck topology, then there is
H\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
such that
[H]^{\omega}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

Definition 2.4
.

(Kastanas,
[
20
]
). Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
,
N\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
and
s\in[N]^{<\omega}
be given. The
Kastanas game

\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{A},N,s)
is defined as follows:

\begin{matrix}\textrm{I}&&M_{0}&&M_{1}&&\cdots&&M_{k}&&\cdots&\\
\textrm{II}&&&(n_{0},N_{0})&&(n_{1},N_{1})&&\cdots&&(n_{k},N_{k})&&\cdots\end{matrix}

Two players I and II take turns in playing sequence of sets
\{M_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
and
\{N_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
respectively, but additionally player II is required to play a sequence of integers
\{n_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, such that the moves of both players respect the following conditions:

Rules of the game.
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

\textbf{(i) }M_{0}\subseteq N/s\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\textbf{(ii) }n_{k}\in M_{k}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\textbf{(iii) }N_{k}\subseteq M_{k}/\{n_{k}\}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\textbf{(iv) }M_{k+1}\subseteq N_{k}

Let
N_{\infty}\in[s,N]
be the set given by
N_{\infty}=s\cup\{n_{k}\,|\,k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Then, for the game
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{A},N,s)
the following is decided:

\bullet

Player I wins the game if
N_{\infty}\in\mathcal{A}
.

\bullet

Player II wins the game if
N_{\infty}\notin\mathcal{A}
.

Theorem 2.5
.

(Kastanas,
[
20
]
). For any set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\mathcal{A}
is Ramsey.

(b)

For all
N\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
and
s\in[N]^{<\omega}
, the Kastanas game
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{A},N,s)
is determined, this means that one of the players has a winning strategy in the game.

We notice that the Kastanas game
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{A},N,s)
on
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
is equivalent to a similar topological game in which the moves executed by the player II are nonempty basic open sets of the Ellentuck topology, satisfying the rules that we state in definition
5.1
for the abstract context. Thus, theorem
2.5
is just a particular case of our theorem
5.7
.

According to Todorčević in
[
31
]
, the
Ellentuck space
is the combinatorial structure
([\mathbb{N}]^{\omega},\subseteq,r)
, where
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
is ordered by the inclusion relation
\subseteq
, and
r
is the function
r:[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\times\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
given by
r(B,n)=r_{n}(B)=s
if
s\in[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
and
s\sqsubset B
. Notice that the basis for Ellentuck topology of
[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
is the collection of all sets
[s,B]=\{A\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}\,|\,A\subseteq B\wedge(\exists\,n\in\mathbb{N})(r_{n}(A)=s)\}
, with
s\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}
and
B\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\omega}
. This alternative presentation of the Ellentuck space allow to study the Ramsey property in an abstract sense through the combinatorial notion of topological Ramsey space, such as will be discussed in the next section.

3
Topological Ramsey spaces

In this section, we formally present the combinatorial structure of topological Ramsey spaces as well as the Ramsey property for this context, and we conclude with the exposition of the
abstract Ellentuck theorem
. The background on topological Ramsey spaces throughout this section is taken from
[
31
]
.

Consider a
combinatorial structure
of the form

(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)

where
\mathcal{R}
is a nonempty set of objects,
\leq
is a quasi-order relation on
\mathcal{R}
, and
r
is a mapping
r:\mathcal{R}\times\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{AR}
giving us the sequence of approximations mappings
r_{n}:\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{AR}_{n}
such that
r_{n}(A)=r(A,n)
for every
A\in\mathcal{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where the range of
r
is the set
\mathcal{AR}
of all the finite approximations of elements of
\mathcal{R}
, and the range of
r_{n}
is the set
\mathcal{AR}_{n}
of all the
n
-approximations, hence
\mathcal{AR}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{AR}_{n}
. Bearing this in mind, the idea is to identify each object
A\in\mathcal{R}
with the sequence of its respective approximations
\{r_{n}(A)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{AR}
.

For every object
B\in\mathcal{R}
, the restriction of the structure
\mathcal{R}
to
B
is the set
\mathcal{R}\!\restriction\!B
defined by all objects
A\in\mathcal{R}
such that
A
is below
B
with respect to quasi-order
\leq
, hence
\mathcal{R}\!\restriction\!B=\{A\in\mathcal{R}\,|\,A\leq B\}
.

Following Todorčević in
[
31
]
, will consider combinatorial structures
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
which satisfy the following list of combinatorial properties which will be called axioms of
metrization
,
finitization
,
amalgamation
and
homogeneity
(or
abstract pigeonhole principle
).

A.1. Axioms of Metrization.

1.

r_{0}(A)=\emptyset
for all
A\in\mathcal{R}
.

2.

For
A,B\in\mathcal{R}
, if
A\neq B
then
r_{n}(A)\neq r_{n}(B)
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.

For
A,B\in\mathcal{R}
, if
r_{n}(A)=r_{m}(B)
then
n=m
and
r_{k}(A)=r_{k}(B)
for each
k\leq n
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{R}
and
a,b\in\mathcal{AR}
be given. Then, we write
a\sqsubset A
, and say that
a

is an initial segment of

A
, if there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a=r_{n}(A)
. Likewise, we write
a\sqsubseteq b
, and say that
a

is an initial segment of

b
, if there are
B\in\mathcal{R}
and
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\leq m
such that
a=r_{n}(B)
and
b=r_{m}(B)
. Similarly, we write
a\sqsubset b
, and say that
a

is a proper initial segment of

b
, if
a\sqsubseteq b
and
a\neq b
. Lastly, the
length of
a
, denoted by
|a|
, is the unique
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a=r_{n}(B)
for some
B\in\mathcal{R}
.

A.2. Axioms of Finitization.
There is a quasi-order
\leq_{\text{fin}}
on
\mathcal{AR}
such that:

1.

For all
b\in\mathcal{AR}
, the set of approximations
\{a\in\mathcal{AR}\,|\,a\leq_{\text{fin}}b\}
is finite.

2.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{R}
, then
A\leq B
if and only if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r_{n}(A)\leq_{\text{fin}}r_{m}(B)
.

3.

For all
a,b,c\in\mathcal{AR}
, if
a\sqsubset b
and
b\leq_{\text{fin}}c
then there is
d\in\mathcal{AR}
such that
d\sqsubset c
and
a\leq_{\text{fin}}d
.

For every approximation
a\in\mathcal{AR}
, the set
[a]
is defined as the family of all objects
A\in\mathcal{R}
such that
a\sqsubset A
, that is,

[a]=\{A\in\mathcal{R}\,|\,r_{|a|}(A)=a\}
.

The collection
\{[a]\,|\,a\in\mathcal{AR}\}
forms a basis for a topology over
\mathcal{R}
, called the
metrizable topology
of
\mathcal{R}
, whose complete metric
\rho:\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is described by
\rho(A,B)=1/2^{k}
, whenever
A\neq B
and
k=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,r_{n}(A)\neq r_{n}(B)\}
.

For every approximation
a\in\mathcal{AR}
and every object
B\in\mathcal{R}
, the set
[a,B]
is defined as the family of all objects
A\in\mathcal{R}
such that
a\sqsubset A
and
A\leq B
, so that

[a,B]=\{A\in[a]\,|\,A\leq B\}=\{A\in\mathcal{R}\,|\,A\leq B\wedge r_{|a|}(A)=a\}
.

The collection
\{[a,B]\,|\,a\in\mathcal{AR}\wedge B\in\mathcal{R}\}
also forms a basis for a topology over
\mathcal{R}
, called the
Ellentuck topology
of
\mathcal{R}
, which extends and refines the metrizable topology. For simplicity, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
we denote:
[m,B]=\{A\in\mathcal{R}\,|\,A\leq B\wedge r_{m}(A)=r_{m}(B)\}=[r_{m}(B),B]
.

The
depth
of an approximation in an object of
\mathcal{R}
is defined as follows: given
a\in\mathcal{AR}
and
B\in\mathcal{R}
, the
depth of
a
in
B
, denoted by
\mathtt{depth}_{B}(a)
, is

\mathtt{depth}_{B}(a)=\begin{cases}\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,a\leq_{\text{fin}}r_{n}(B)\}&\text{if such minimum exists.}\\
\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

For each basic open set
[a,B]\neq\emptyset
in the Ellentuck topology of
\mathcal{R}
, the restriction of the collection of approximations
\mathcal{AR}
to
[a,B]
is the set
\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![a,B]=\{b\in\mathcal{AR}\,|\,a\sqsubseteq b\wedge\mathtt{depth}_{B}(b)<\infty\}
.

A.3. Axioms of Amalgamation.

1.

Let
a\in\mathcal{AR}
and
B\in\mathcal{R}
, if
\mathtt{depth}_{B}(a)<\infty
then
[a,A]\neq\emptyset
for all
A\in[\mathtt{depth}_{B}(a),B]
, in particular
[a,B]\neq\emptyset
.

2.

Let
a\in\mathcal{AR}
and
A,B\in\mathcal{R}
, if
A\leq B
and
\mathtt{depth}_{A}(a)\leq\mathtt{depth}_{B}(a)<\infty
then there is some
A^{\prime}\in[\mathtt{depth}_{B}(a),B]
such that
[a,A^{\prime}]\subseteq[a,A]
.

Let
[a,A]
be a basic open set in the Ellentuck topology of
\mathcal{R}
; then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq|a|
, we denote by
r_{n}\text{''}\,[a,A]
the direct image of the set
[a,A]
through the
n
-th approximation function
r_{n}
, hence
r_{n}\text{''}\,[a,A]=\{d\in\mathcal{AR}_{n}\,|\,(\exists D\in[a,A])(d=r_{n}(D))\}=\{d\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![a,A]\,|\,|d|=n\}
.

A.4. Abstract Pigeonhole Principle.
For all
a\in\mathcal{AR}
and
B\in\mathcal{R}
such that
\mathtt{depth}_{B}(a)<\infty
, if
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{AR}_{|a|+1}
then there exists some
A\in[\mathtt{depth}_{B}(a),B]
such that either
r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,A]\subseteq\mathcal{O}
or
\mathcal{O}\cap r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,A]=\emptyset
.

The combinatorial structure
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
is called
metrically closed
if
\mathcal{R}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{AR}^{\mathbb{N}}
with the product topology, where each object of
\mathcal{R}
has been identified with the sequence of its initial segments. Naturally, we consider the product topology on
\mathcal{AR}^{\mathbb{N}}
whenever
\mathcal{AR}
has the discrete topology, in which case the subspace topology on
\mathcal{R}
inherited from
\mathcal{AR}^{\mathbb{N}}
coincides exactly with the metrizable topology of
\mathcal{R}
.

Definition 3.1
.

(Todorčević,
[
31
]
). A metrically closed combinatorial structure
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
is a
topological Ramsey space
if
\mathcal{R}
is endowed with the Ellentuck topology and
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
satisfies the axioms
A.1
,
A.2
,
A.3
and
A.4
.

A topological Ramsey space
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
does not have isolated points if each of its nonempty basic open sets is infinite. Notice that the space
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
does not have isolated points whenever the set of approximations
r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,B]
is infinite for every basic open set
[a,B]\neq\emptyset
.

The Ellentuck space
([\mathbb{N}]^{\omega},\subseteq,r)
is the quintessential example of a topological Ramsey space without isolated points, and this fact naturally leads us to extend definition
2.2
to the abstract context. So, the Ramsey property related to topological Ramsey spaces is defined as follows:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a topological Ramsey space. A set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
is
Ramsey
if for every
[a,A]\neq\emptyset
there is some
B\in[a,A]
such that either
[a,B]\subseteq\mathcal{X}
or
[a,B]\cap\mathcal{X}=\emptyset
. Moreover, if the second option always holds, then we say that
\mathcal{X}
is
Ramsey null
.

Todorčević proved in
[
31
]
that for every topological Ramsey space
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
without isolated points there is a set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
which is not Ramsey. In the proof the use of the axiom of choice is indispensable.

An important tool in the study of topological Ramsey spaces is the concept of
fusion sequence
, formed by basic open sets of a space, since through it we can recursively construct new objects of the space satisfying specific and special properties.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a topological Ramsey space. A sequence
\{[n_{k},Y_{k}]\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of basic open sets of
\mathcal{R}
is a
fusion sequence
if:

\bullet

\{n_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a strictly increasing sequence.

\bullet

Y_{k+1}\in[n_{k},Y_{k}]
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

The limit of the fusion sequence
\{[n_{k},Y_{k}]\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
always exists and it is the unique object
Y_{\infty}\in\mathcal{R}
, denoted by
Y_{\infty}=\lim_{n_{k}}Y_{k}
, such that
Y_{\infty}\in[n_{k},Y_{k}]
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
; hence,
r_{n_{k}}(Y_{\infty})=r_{n_{k}}(Y_{k})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words,
Y_{\infty}=\lim_{n_{k}}Y_{k}
if and only if
\{Y_{\infty}\}=\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}[n_{k},Y_{k}]=\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}[r_{n_{k}}(Y_{k})]
.

At last, we present the main result of the topological Ramsey theory, which corresponds to the natural generalization of theorem
2.3
for the abstract context. In this regard, the abstract Ellentuck theorem states the equivalence between the Ramsey property and the Baire property on the combinatorial structure of topological Ramsey spaces.

Theorem 3.4
.

[
Abstract Ellentuck Theorem
]. (Todorčević,
[
31
]
). Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a topological Ramsey space. Then:

1.

For any
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\mathcal{X}
is Ramsey null.

(b)

\mathcal{X}
is nowhere dense.

(c)

\mathcal{X}
is meager.

2.

For any
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\mathcal{X}
is Ramsey.

(b)

\mathcal{X}
has the abstract Baire property.

(c)

\mathcal{X}
has the Baire property.

3.

If
\mathcal{R}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{P}_{i}
is any finite partition of the space
\mathcal{R}
, where each piece
\mathcal{P}_{i}
has the Baire property, then there is
H\in\mathcal{R}
such that
\mathcal{R}\!\restriction\!H\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

The original proof of the abstract Ellentuck theorem given by Todorčević in
[
31
]
mainly applies a combinatorial technique known as
abstract notion of combinatorial forcing
, whose essential idea is to force the recursive construction of an object in a topological Ramsey space such that it turns out to be homogeneous with respect to a fixed partition of the space that is finite and Baire measurable. In the next section, we present a new proof of abstract Ellentuck theorem for a large family of topological Ramsey spaces, in which we do not use combinatorial forcing.

4
On an alternative proof of the abstract Ellentuck theorem

In this section, we introduce a generalization of axioms of amalgamation to define the wide class of strong topological Ramsey spaces, with the aim of proposing an alternative proof of the abstract Ellentuck theorem for this new kind of Ramsey spaces.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a topological Ramsey space, and let
A,B\in\mathcal{R}
,
b\in\mathcal{AR}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
B\leq A
and
\mathtt{depth}_{A}(b)\leq m
. We define the set
\langle b,B\rangle_{m}^{A}
as follows:

\langle b,B\rangle_{m}^{A}=\{X\in[b,B]\;|\;\mathtt{depth}_{A}(r_{|b|+1}(X))>m\}.

Notice that
\langle b,B\rangle_{m}^{A}
is an open set of
\mathcal{R}
, since
\langle b,B\rangle_{m}^{A}=\bigcup\{[c,B]\,|\,c\in r_{|b|+1}\text{''}\,[b,B]\wedge\mathtt{depth}_{A}(c)>m\}
. Furthermore, notice that if
\mathtt{depth}_{A}(b)=m
then
\langle b,B\rangle_{m}^{A}=[b,B]
.

A.3*. Axioms of Strong Amalgamation.

1.

Let
b\in\mathcal{AR}
and
A\in\mathcal{R}
, if
\mathtt{depth}_{A}(b)\leq m<\infty
then
\langle b,B\rangle_{m}^{A}\neq\emptyset
for all
B\in[m,A]
, in particular
\langle b,A\rangle_{m}^{A}\neq\emptyset
.

2.

Let
b\in\mathcal{AR}
and
A,B\in\mathcal{R}
, if
B\leq A
and
\mathtt{depth}_{B}(b)\leq\mathtt{depth}_{A}(b)\leq m<\infty
then there is some
B^{\prime}\in[m,A]
such that
\langle b,B^{\prime}\rangle_{m}^{A}\subseteq\langle b,B\rangle_{m}^{A}
.

We point out that the axioms of strong amalgamation
A.3*
imply the axioms of amalgamation
A.3
, since taking
m=\mathtt{depth}_{A}(b)
, we can see that
A.3
is a particular case of
A.3*
.

Definition 4.1
.

A metrically closed combinatorial structure
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
is a
strong topological Ramsey space
if
\mathcal{R}
is endowed with the Ellentuck topology and
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
satisfies the axioms
A.1
,
A.2
,
A.3*
and
A.4
.

It should be noted that for each topological Ramsey space
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
, the axiom
A.3*.1
holds if and only if for every basic open set
[a,B]\neq\emptyset
the set of approximations
r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,B]
is infinite. Therefore, if the space
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
is strong, then it does not have isolated points.

In addition, clearly every strong topological Ramsey space is itself a topological Ramsey space, since
A.3*
imply
A.3
; however, we do not know the answer to the following question: is there some topological Ramsey space without isolated points that is not strong?

Next, we present an alternative proof of abstract Ellentuck theorem restricted to the collection of strong topological Ramsey spaces. The new proof of theorem
3.4
that we propose here is inspired on a short proof of theorem
2.3
given by Matet in
[
23
]
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be any topological Ramsey space. Given
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and
b\in\mathcal{AR}
, we define the set
\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
as follows:

\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}}=\{D\in\mathcal{R}\,|\,\emptyset\neq[b,D]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}\}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a strong topological Ramsey space. For each
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
A\in\mathcal{R}
, and
b,d\in\mathcal{AR}
, if
\mathtt{depth}_{A}(b)\leq\mathtt{depth}_{A}(d)\leq m
and
b\leq_{\text{fin}}d
, then there is some
B\in[m,A]
such that either
\langle d,B\rangle_{m}^{A}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}}
or
\langle d,B\rangle_{m}^{A}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

If
\langle d,A\rangle_{m}^{A}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}}=\emptyset
then we can simply take
B=A
. Otherwise, if
\langle d,A\rangle_{m}^{A}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}}\neq\emptyset
then we fix
C\in\langle d,A\rangle_{m}^{A}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}}
, hence
C\in[d,A]
and
\emptyset\neq[b,C]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
. Now, by
A.3*
there exists some
B\in[m,A]
such that
\emptyset\neq\langle d,B\rangle_{m}^{A}\subseteq\langle d,C\rangle_{m}^{A}
. Finally, notice that
\langle d,B\rangle_{m}^{A}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}}
, since every
D\in\langle d,B\rangle_{m}^{A}\subseteq\langle d,C\rangle_{m}^{A}\subseteq[d,C]
satisfies that
\emptyset\neq[b,D]\subseteq[b,C]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
, therefore
D\in\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}}
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a strong topological Ramsey space, and let
[a,A]\neq\emptyset
be a basic open set of
\mathcal{R}
. If
\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\wp(\mathcal{R})
is any sequence of subsets of
\mathcal{R}
for which
[a,B]\not\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}
for all
B\in[a,A]
, then there are
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
[c,C]\neq\emptyset
, with
C\in[a,A]
,
[c,C]\subseteq[a,A]
, and
|a|\leq|c|\leq|a|+i
, such that
[d,D]\not\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
for each
[d,D]\neq\emptyset
with
[d,D]\subseteq[c,C]
.

Proof.

Let
[a,A]\neq\emptyset
and
\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\wp(\mathcal{R})
be such that
[a,B]\not\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}
for all
B\in[a,A]
. We may suppose without loss of generality that
a\sqsubset A
.

First of all, we recursively construct a fusion sequence
\{[n_{k},A_{k}]\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have:

(i)

A_{0}=A
.

(ii)

n_{k}=\mathtt{depth}_{A}(a)+k=|a|+k
.

(iii)

For all
i\leq k
and for each
b,d\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![a,A_{k}]
with
\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(b)\leq\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(d)\leq n_{k}
and
b\leq_{\text{fin}}d
, the object
A_{k+1}\in[n_{k},A_{k}]
is such that either
\langle d,A_{k+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
or
\langle d,A_{k+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}=\emptyset
.

Suppose inductively that the basic open set
[n_{k},A_{k}]
has been constructed. Applying
A.2
, we consider the finite set
\{(b,d)\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![a,A_{k}]\times\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![a,A_{k}]\,|\,\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(b)\leq\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(d)\leq n_{k}\wedge b\leq_{\text{fin}}d\}
, for which we fix an arbitrary enumeration
\{(b_{0},d_{0}),\ldots,(b_{p},d_{p})\}
with
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, we construct a finite sequence
X_{0}\geq\cdots\geq X_{p+1}
of elements of
[n_{k},A_{k}]
such that for all
j\leq p
we have:

(iv)

X_{0}=A_{k}
.

(v)

X_{j+1}\in[n_{k},X_{j}]
.

(vi)

For all
i\leq k
, either
\langle d_{j},X_{j+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b_{j}}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
or
\langle d_{j},X_{j+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b_{j}}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}=\emptyset
.

Suppose that the object
X_{j}\in[n_{k},A_{k}]
has been defined, thus
r_{n_{k}}(X_{j})=r_{n_{k}}(A_{k})
, which implies that
\mathtt{depth}_{X_{j}}(b_{j})=\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(b_{j})
and
\mathtt{depth}_{X_{j}}(d_{j})=\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(d_{j})
. Then, applying iteratively lemma
4.2
, we construct a finite sequence
X_{j}^{0}\geq\cdots\geq X_{j}^{k+1}
of elements of
[n_{k},X_{j}]\subseteq[n_{k},A_{k}]
such that for all
i\leq k
we have:

(vii)

X_{j}^{0}=X_{j}
.

(viii)

X_{j}^{i+1}\in[n_{k},X_{j}^{i}]
.

(ix)

Either
\langle d_{j},X_{j}^{i+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b_{j}}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
or
\langle d_{j},X_{j}^{i+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b_{j}}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}=\emptyset
.

Suppose that the object
X_{j}^{i}\in[n_{k},X_{j}]
has been defined, in which case
\mathtt{depth}_{X_{j}^{i}}(b_{j})\leq\mathtt{depth}_{X_{j}^{i}}(d_{j})\leq n_{k}
, so by lemma
4.2
, we deduce that there is some
X_{j}^{i+1}\in[n_{k},X_{j}^{i}]\subseteq[n_{k},X_{j}]\subseteq[n_{k},A_{k}]
such that either
\langle d_{j},X_{j}^{i+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{X_{j}^{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b_{j}}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
or
\langle d_{j},X_{j}^{i+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{X_{j}^{i}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b_{j}}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}=\emptyset
; however, since
r_{n_{k}}(X_{j}^{i})=r_{n_{k}}(X_{j})=r_{n_{k}}(A_{k})
, then we infer that
\langle d_{j},X_{j}^{i+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{X_{j}^{i}}=\langle d_{j},X_{j}^{i+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}
, whereby we conclude that either
\langle d_{j},X_{j}^{i+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b_{j}}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
or
\langle d_{j},X_{j}^{i+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b_{j}}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}=\emptyset
.

Let
X_{j+1}\in[n_{k},X_{j}]
be the object
X_{j+1}=X_{j}^{k+1}
, and so we now define the object
A_{k+1}\in[n_{k},A_{k}]
by
A_{k+1}=X_{p+1}
. Thus, for each
i\leq k
and
j\leq p
we have that either
\langle d_{j},A_{k+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b_{j}}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
or
\langle d_{j},A_{k+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b_{j}}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}=\emptyset
, completing the induction on
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let
B\in[a,A]
be the limit
B=\lim_{n_{k}}A_{k}
of the fusion sequence
\{[n_{k},A_{k}]\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, so that
B\in[n_{k},A_{k}]
and
r_{n_{k}}(B)=r_{n_{k}}(A_{k})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
[a,B]\not\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, then there is some
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
[a,B]\not\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
; so, we choose
E\in[a,B]\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{i}
and we take
c=r_{\ell}(E)
with
\ell=\max\{m\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,\mathtt{depth}_{B}(r_{m}(E))\leq n_{i}\}
, obtaining
|a|\leq|c|\leq|a|+i
. Thus,
a\sqsubseteq c\sqsubset E
and
E\in[c,B]
, consequently we have that
[c,B]\not\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
; in fact, it is also true that
[c,E]\not\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
.

For each
b\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![c,E]
, we proceed to consider the family of approximations
T_{b}\subseteq r_{|b|+1}\text{''}\,[b,E]
as well as the collection of objects
\mathcal{K}_{b}\subseteq[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(b),E]
defined as follows:

\displaystyle T_{b}

\displaystyle=\{q\in r_{|b|+1}\text{''}\,[b,E]\;|\;[q,E]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}\},\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{b}

\displaystyle=\{D\in[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(b),E]\;|\;[b,D]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}\}=\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}\cap[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(b),E].

Claim 1
. If
b\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![c,E]
and
D_{1},D_{2}\in[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(b),E]
be such that
D_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{b}
and
D_{2}\leq D_{1}
then
D_{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{b}
. Indeed, notice that
[b,D_{2}]\subseteq[b,D_{1}]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
whenever
D_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{b}
and
D_{2}\leq D_{1}
, so that
D_{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{b}
.

Claim 2
. For all
b\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![c,E]
, either
\mathcal{K}_{b}=[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(b),E]
or
\mathcal{K}_{b}=\emptyset
. Indeed, let
b\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![c,E]
be fixed and put
d=r_{\mathtt{depth}_{E}(b)}(E)
, hence
b\leq_{\text{fin}}d
and
[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(b),E]=[d,E]
. Now, suppose that
\mathcal{K}_{b}\neq\emptyset
and let
D\in[d,E]
be such that
D\in\mathcal{K}_{b}
, which implies that
D\in\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
. We know that
B=\lim_{n_{k}}A_{k}
, so we can take
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n_{k}=\max\{\mathtt{depth}_{B}(d),n_{i}\}
. Thus
i\leq k
and
\mathtt{depth}_{B}(b)\leq\mathtt{depth}_{B}(d)\leq n_{k}
, and since
B\in[n_{k+1},A_{k+1}]\subseteq[n_{k},A_{k}]
, we also have that
\mathtt{depth}_{B}(b)=\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(b)
and
\mathtt{depth}_{B}(d)=\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(d)
. Therefore, we deduce that either
\langle d,A_{k+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
or
\langle d,A_{k+1}\rangle_{n_{k}}^{A_{k}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}=\emptyset
, and since
r_{n_{k}}(B)=r_{n_{k}}(A_{k+1})=r_{n_{k}}(A_{k})
and
B\leq A_{k+1}\leq A_{k}
, then we also obtain that either
\langle d,B\rangle_{n_{k}}^{B}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
or
\langle d,B\rangle_{n_{k}}^{B}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}=\emptyset
. However, notice that
D\in\langle d,B\rangle_{n_{k}}^{B}\cap\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
, so it must necessarily be true that
\langle d,B\rangle_{n_{k}}^{B}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
, and we infer that
E\in\mathcal{S}_{b}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
because
E\in\langle d,B\rangle_{n_{k}}^{B}
. We then conclude that
E\in\mathcal{K}_{b}
, and consequently
\mathcal{K}_{b}=[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(b),E]
.

Claim 3
. For all
b\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![c,E]
, it is true that
\mathcal{K}_{b}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
T_{b}=r_{|b|+1}\text{''}\,[b,E]
. Indeed, if
\mathcal{K}_{b}\neq\emptyset
then
\mathcal{K}_{b}=[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(b),E]
, in which case
E\in\mathcal{K}_{b}
and hence
[b,E]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, thus
[q,E]\subseteq[b,E]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
for each
q\in r_{|b|+1}\text{''}\,[b,E]
, as a result we obtain that
T_{b}=r_{|b|+1}\text{''}\,[b,E]
. Conversely, if
T_{b}=r_{|b|+1}\text{''}\,[b,E]
, then
[b,E]\subseteq\bigcup_{q\in T_{b}}[q,E]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, therefore
E\in\mathcal{K}_{b}
and in particular
\mathcal{K}_{b}\neq\emptyset
.

Claim 4
. For every
b\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![c,E]
, if
\mathcal{K}_{b}\neq\emptyset
then
b\neq c
and
b\in T_{b^{-}}
, where
c\sqsubseteq b^{-}\sqsubset b
with
|b^{-}|=|b|-1
. Indeed, since
[c,E]\not\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
then
E\notin\mathcal{K}_{c}
, in which case
\mathcal{K}_{c}\neq[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(c),E]=[c,E]
and hence
\mathcal{K}_{c}=\emptyset
. Consequently, if
\mathcal{K}_{b}\neq\emptyset
then
b\neq c
and also
\mathcal{K}_{b}=[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(b),E]
, so that
E\in\mathcal{K}_{b}
and hence
[b,E]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, therefore
b\in T_{b^{-}}
where
c\sqsubseteq b^{-}\sqsubset b
with
|b^{-}|=|b|-1
.

After that stage, we recursively construct a new fusion sequence
\{[m_{k},M_{k}]\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have:

(i)

M_{0}=E
.

(ii)

m_{k}=\mathtt{depth}_{E}(c)+k=|c|+k
.

(iii)

For all
b\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![c,E]
with
\mathtt{depth}_{M_{k}}(b)=m_{k}
, the object
M_{k+1}\in[m_{k},M_{k}]
is such that
T_{b}\cap r_{|b|+1}\text{''}\,[b,M_{k+1}]=\emptyset
.

By induction, suppose that the basic open set
[m_{k},M_{k}]
has been constructed. Applying
A.2
, we consider any enumeration of the finite set
\{b\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![c,E]\,|\,\mathtt{depth}_{M_{k}}(b)=m_{k}\}=\{b_{0},\ldots,b_{p}\}
with
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, we construct a finite sequence
Z_{0}\geq\cdots\geq Z_{p+1}
of elements of
[m_{k},M_{k}]
such that for each
j\leq p
we have:

(iv)

Z_{0}=M_{k}
.

(v)

Z_{j+1}\in[m_{k},Z_{j}]
.

(vi)

T_{b_{j}}\cap r_{|b_{j}|+1}\text{''}\,[b_{j},Z_{j+1}]=\emptyset
.

Suppose that the object
Z_{j}\in[m_{k},M_{j}]
has been defined, and notice that
\mathtt{depth}_{Z_{j}}(b_{j})=\mathtt{depth}_{M_{k}}(b_{j})=m_{k}
, then applying the abstract pigeonhole principle
A.4
to the family
T_{b_{j}}
, we deduce that there exists some
Z_{j+1}\in[m_{k},Z_{j}]
such that either
r_{|b_{j}|+1}\text{''}\,[b_{j},Z_{j+1}]\subseteq T_{b_{j}}
or
T_{b_{j}}\cap r_{|b_{j}|+1}\text{''}\,[b_{j},Z_{j+1}]=\emptyset
. However, the first alternative is impossible, since
r_{|b_{j}|+1}\text{''}\,[b_{j},Z_{j+1}]\subseteq T_{b_{j}}
implies that
[b_{j},Z_{j+1}]\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, hence
\mathcal{K}_{b_{j}}\neq\emptyset
because
Z_{j+1}\in\mathcal{K}_{b_{j}}
, so that
b_{j}\neq c
and
b_{j}\in T_{b_{j}^{-}}
; in fact, notice that
b_{j}\in T_{b_{j}^{-}}\cap r_{|b_{j}|}\text{''}\,[b_{j}^{-},Z_{j}]
, which is contradictory. Therefore, we must have
T_{b_{j}}\cap r_{|b_{j}|+1}\text{''}\,[b_{j},Z_{j+1}]=\emptyset
.

We now define the object
M_{k+1}\in[m_{k},M_{j}]
by
M_{k+1}=Z_{p+1}
; thus, for all
j\leq p
we have
T_{b_{j}}\cap r_{|b_{j}|+1}\text{''}\,[b_{j},M_{k+1}]=\emptyset
. In this way, we have completed the induction on
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Finally, let
C\in[c,E]\subseteq[c,B]\subseteq[a,A]
be the object given by the limit of the fusion sequence
\{[m_{k},M_{k}]\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, that is
C=\lim_{m_{k}}M_{k}
, so that
C\in[m_{k},M_{k}]
and
r_{m_{k}}(C)=r_{m_{k}}(M_{k})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, if
[d,D]\neq\emptyset
is any basic open set of
\mathcal{R}
such that
[d,D]\subseteq[c,C]\subseteq[c,E]
, then we have that
d\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![c,C]\subseteq\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![c,E]
. Furthermore, we can assume that
D\leq E
, in which case there is some
D^{\prime}\in[\mathtt{depth}_{E}(d),E]
such that
\emptyset\neq[d,D^{\prime}]\subseteq[d,D]
, in view of
A.3
. In closing, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
m_{k}=\mathtt{depth}_{C}(d)=\mathtt{depth}_{M_{k}}(d)
, thus we infer that
T_{d}\cap r_{|d|+1}\text{''}\,[d,M_{k+1}]=\emptyset
, and since
M_{k+1}\leq E
, then we deduce that
T_{d}\neq r_{|d|+1}\text{''}\,[d,E]
, for this reason
\mathcal{K}_{d}=\emptyset
, so that
D^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{K}_{d}
and hence
[d,D^{\prime}]\not\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, due to
D^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{S}_{d}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
. Therefore, we ultimately conclude that
[d,D]\not\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}
whenever
\emptyset\neq[d,D]\subseteq[c,C]
. ∎

Applying previous lemma, we finally conclude with our alternative proof of the abstract Ellentuck theorem for the class of strong topological Ramsey spaces. With reference to the statement of theorem
3.4
, its first item will be proved in proposition
4.4
, its second item will be shown in proposition
4.5
, and its third item will be verified in corollary
4.6
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a strong topological Ramsey space. Then, for any
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\mathcal{X}
is Ramsey null.

(b)

\mathcal{X}
is nowhere dense.

(c)

\mathcal{X}
is meager.

Proof.

Notice that it is immediate that every Ramsey null set is also a nowhere dense set and hence is itself a meager set. So, let us verify that every meager set is Ramsey null. Indeed, if
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
is a meager set, then there is a sequence
\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\wp(\mathcal{R})
of nowhere dense sets such that
\mathcal{X}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, so by considering the sequence
\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\complement}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, we have that
\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\complement}=(\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i})^{\complement}=\mathcal{X}^{\complement}
. Now, by reduction to absurd, suppose that
\mathcal{X}
is not a Ramsey null set, then there is
[a,A]\neq\emptyset
such that for every
B\in[a,A]
it is true that
[a,B]\cap\mathcal{X}\neq\emptyset
, so
[a,B]\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\complement}
and hence
[a,B]\not\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\complement}
. Thus, by virtue of lemma
4.3
, we deduce that there are
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
[c,C]\neq\emptyset
, with
C\in[a,A]
and
[c,C]\subseteq[a,A]
, such that for each
[d,D]\neq\emptyset
, if
[d,D]\subseteq[c,C]
then
[d,D]\not\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\complement}
and hence
[d,D]\cap\mathcal{Q}_{i}\neq\emptyset
; therefore, we conclude that
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
is not a nowhere dense set, which is contradictory. ∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a strong topological Ramsey space. Then, for any
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\mathcal{X}
is Ramsey.

(b)

\mathcal{X}
has the abstract Baire property.

(c)

\mathcal{X}
has the Baire property.

Proof.

As in the proof of proposition
4.4
it is immediate that every Ramsey set has the abstract Baire property and hence also has the Baire property.

Conversely, if
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
has the Baire property, then
\mathcal{X}\triangle\mathcal{O}
is meager for some open set
\mathcal{O}
, hence
\mathcal{X}\triangle\mathcal{O}
is nowhere dense by proposition
4.4
; thus, for each
[a,A]\neq\emptyset
there is
\emptyset\neq[b,B]\subseteq[a,A]
such that
[b,B]\cap(\mathcal{X}\triangle\mathcal{O})=\emptyset
. Now, since
\mathcal{O}
has the Baire property, there is
\emptyset\neq[c,C]\subseteq[b,B]\subseteq[a,A]
such that either
[c,C]\subseteq\mathcal{O}
or
[c,C]\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
. Also, since
[c,C]\subseteq[b,B]
, we have
[c,C]\cap(\mathcal{X}\triangle\mathcal{O})=\emptyset
. Therefore, when
[c,C]\subseteq\mathcal{O}
we deduce that
[c,C]\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, and when
[c,C]\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
we infer that
[c,C]\cap\mathcal{X}=\emptyset
; as a result, we conclude that
\mathcal{X}
has the abstract Baire property.

Finally, let us check that any set with the abstract Baire property is in turn a Ramsey set. Indeed, suppose that
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
is not a Ramsey set, then there exists
[a,A]\neq\emptyset
such that for every
B\in[a,A]
it is true that
[a,B]\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
[a,B]\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\complement}
. Now, we consider the eventually constant sequence
\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\wp(\mathcal{R})
defined by
\mathcal{Q}_{0}=\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\mathcal{R}
for each
i>0
, so that
\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\mathcal{Q}_{0}=\mathcal{X}
and hence
[a,B]\not\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}
for all
B\in[a,A]
. Then, applying lemma
4.3
to the sequence
\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, we necessarily conclude that there exists
C\in[a,A]
such that
[d,D]\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}
whenever
\emptyset\neq[d,D]\subseteq[a,C]
. Next, we consider the constant sequence
\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\wp(\mathcal{R})
defined by
\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{*}=\mathcal{X}^{\complement}
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, so that
\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{*}=\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{*}=\mathcal{X}^{\complement}
and hence
[a,B]\not\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{*}
for all
B\in[a,C]\subseteq[a,A]
. Then, applying lemma
4.3
to the sequence
\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, we deduce that there is some
[e,E]\neq\emptyset
, with
E\in[a,C]
and
[e,E]\subseteq[a,C]
, such that
[d,D]\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\complement}
whenever
\emptyset\neq[d,D]\subseteq[e,E]
. Therefore, the basic open set
[e,E]\neq\emptyset
satisfies both
[d,D]\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
[d,D]\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}^{\complement}
for every
[d,D]\neq\emptyset
such that
[d,D]\subseteq[e,E]
, and we can conclude that
\mathcal{X}
does not have the abstract Baire property.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a strong topological Ramsey space. If
\mathcal{R}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{P}_{i}
is any finite partition of the space
\mathcal{R}
, where each piece
\mathcal{P}_{i}
has the Baire property, then there is
H\in\mathcal{R}
such that
\mathcal{R}\!\restriction\!H\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

Proof.

For
k=2
, let
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\cup\mathcal{P}_{2}
be any partition of
\mathcal{R}
into two pieces such that
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
have the Baire property, being
\mathcal{P}_{2}=\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{1}
. By virtue of the proposition
4.5
we deduce that
\mathcal{P}_{1}
is a Ramsey set, then for any basic open set
[\emptyset,A]
there is
H\in[\emptyset,A]
such that either
[\emptyset,H]\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{1}
or
[\emptyset,H]\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}=\emptyset
, where
[\emptyset,H]=\mathcal{R}\!\restriction\!H
. Therefore, the object
H\in\mathcal{R}
satisfies that either
\mathcal{R}\!\restriction\!H\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{1}
or
\mathcal{R}\!\restriction\!H\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{2}
. Now, for
k>2
a completely analogous argument is used and the result is similarly proved by applying induction on
k
.
∎

5
On an abstract version of Kastanas theorem

In this section, we present an abstract version of Kastanas game in order to characterize the Ramsey property for the class of selective topological Ramsey spaces via topological games, so we conclude with the
abstract Kastanas theorem
, which is the main result of our article.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a topological Ramsey space, and let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and
[a_{0},B_{0}]\neq\emptyset
be fixed but arbitrary. Then, the
abstract Kastanas game

\mathcal{G}_{[a_{0},B_{0}]}(\mathcal{X})
is defined as follows:

\begin{matrix}\textrm{I}&&A_{1}&&A_{2}&&\cdots&&A_{k}&&\cdots&\\
\textrm{II}&&&[a_{1},B_{1}]&&[a_{2},B_{2}]&&\cdots&&[a_{k},B_{k}]&&\cdots\end{matrix}

Two players I and II take turns to play alternately both objects in
\mathcal{R}
and basic open sets of
\mathcal{R}
, such that the moves of both players respect the following conditions:

Rules of the game.
For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, both the sequence of objects
\{A_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and
\{B_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, as well as the sequence of approximations
\{a_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{AR}
, must satisfy the following rules:

\textbf{(i) }A_{k+1}\in[a_{k},B_{k}]\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\textbf{(ii) }a_{k}\sqsubset a_{k+1}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\textbf{(iii) }|a_{k+1}|=|a_{k}|+1\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\textbf{(iv) }B_{k}\in[a_{k},A_{k}]

Let
B_{\infty}\in[a_{0},B_{0}]
be the object given by
\{B_{\infty}\}=\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}[a_{k}]
. Then, for the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a_{0},B_{0}]}(\mathcal{X})
the following is decided:

\bullet

Player I wins the game if
B_{\infty}\in\mathcal{X}
.

\bullet

Player II wins the game if
B_{\infty}\notin\mathcal{X}
.

It should be noted that in the definition of the abstract Kastanas game
\mathcal{G}_{[a_{0},B_{0}]}(\mathcal{X})
, we can take
[a_{0},B_{0}]\neq\emptyset
with
a_{0}\sqsubset B_{0}
, in which case we have that the object
B_{\infty}\in[a_{0},B_{0}]
is such that
\{B_{\infty}\}=\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}[a_{k}]
if and only if
B_{\infty}=\lim_{n_{k}}B_{k}
where
n_{k}=\mathtt{depth}_{B_{k}}(a_{k})
.

A topological game, such as the abstract Kastanas game given in the above definition, is said to be
determined
if one of the players has a winning strategy for the game. The concept of strategy is well known (see for instance
[
19
, chapter 33]
), so we do not give a formal definition, though we can say that a strategy for one of the players is just a function that tells the player what to play at each step depending on the previous moves of both players. Furthermore, a strategy in the game is a winning strategy for one of the players if, using this strategy, the outcome of the game is a win for this player independently of the moves of the other player.

We start with the following result about topological Ramsey spaces, which characterizes when player I has a winning strategy in the abstract Kastanas game.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a topological Ramsey space, and let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and
[a,A]\neq\emptyset
be given. Then, the player I has a winning strategy in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,A]}(\mathcal{X})
if and only if there is
H\in[a,A]
such that
[a,H]\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

Given a topological Ramsey space
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
, we fix a set of objects
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and a basic open set
[a,M]\neq\emptyset
, for which we assume without loss of generality that
a\sqsubset M
. So, we proceed to consider the abstract Kastanas game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
.

First of all, suppose that there is an object
H\in[a,N]
such that
[a,H]\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, in which case it is natural to define a winning strategy for the player I in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, namely the player I must select the object
H
as the first move. Indeed, consider any complete run in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
of the form

\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})

\begin{matrix}\textrm{I}&&H&&A_{2}&&A_{3}&&\cdots&\\
\textrm{II}&&&[a_{1},B_{1}]&&[a_{2},B_{2}]&&[a_{3},B_{3}]&&\cdots\end{matrix}

So, notice that the object
B_{\infty}\in[a,M]
given by
\{B_{\infty}\}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}[a_{k}]
is such that
B_{\infty}\in[a_{k},B_{k}]
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}-\{0\}
, consequently
B_{\infty}\in[a,H]
and hence
B_{\infty}\in\mathcal{X}
; therefore, the player I wins the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
.

Conversely, suppose that the player I has a winning strategy
\sigma
in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, and we consider the strictly increasing sequence
\{n_{k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
described by
n_{k}=\mathtt{depth}_{M}(a)+k=|a|+k
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Now, our goal is to recursively construct a fusion sequence
\{[n_{k},A_{k}]\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that its limit
H=\lim_{n_{k}}A_{k}
will satisfy that
[a,H]\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.

For the first stage of the process, we put
A_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
and
a_{0}\in\mathcal{AR}
as
A_{0}=M
and
a_{0}=r_{n_{0}}(A_{0})=a
. Next, let
A_{1}\in[a_{0},A_{0}]
be the object determined by
A_{1}=\sigma\langle\emptyset\rangle
, so that
A_{1}
is the first move of the player I in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
using the winning strategy
\sigma
, in which case we take the approximation
a_{1}\in\mathcal{AR}
given by
a_{1}=r_{n_{1}}(A_{1})
, then
a_{0}\sqsubset a_{1}
and
|a_{1}|=|a_{0}|+1
.

A detailed description of the next stage of the construction will help the reader to go through the general inductive step.
In the next stage of the process, by
A.2.1
we can take the finite set
\{b\in\mathcal{AR}\,|\,a\sqsubset b\wedge\mathtt{depth}_{A_{1}}(b)=n_{1}\}\subseteq\{b\in\mathcal{AR}\,|\,b\leq_{\text{fin}}a_{1}\}
, for which we fix an enumeration
\{b_{1}^{0},b_{1}^{1},\ldots,b_{1}^{p_{1}}\}
with
p_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
, where
b_{1}^{0}=b_{1}^{p_{1}}=a_{1}
and
b_{1}^{i}\neq b_{1}^{j}
for every
1\leq i<j\leq p_{1}
. Next, we recursively construct a finite sequence of objects
C_{1}^{0},B_{1}^{0},A_{1}^{0},\ldots,C_{1}^{p_{1}},B_{1}^{p_{1}},A_{1}^{p_{1}}
in
[a,A_{1}]
, putting initially
C_{1}^{0}=B_{1}^{0}=A_{1}^{0}=A_{1}
. Afterwards, inductively suppose that
C_{1}^{m-1}\geq B_{1}^{m-1}\geq A_{1}^{m-1}
have been defined for some
1\leq m\leq p_{1}
, where
C_{1}^{m-1}\in[a_{1}]
and
B_{1}^{m-1},A_{1}^{m-1}\in[b_{1}^{m-1}]
. Notice that
r_{n_{1}}(C_{1}^{m-1})=a_{1}
, hence
\mathtt{depth}_{A_{1}^{m-1}}(b_{1}^{m-1})\leq\mathtt{depth}_{C_{1}^{m-1}}(b_{1}^{m-1})=n_{1}
, thus by
A.3.2
there is
C_{1}^{m}\in[a_{1},C_{1}^{m-1}]
such that
[b_{1}^{m-1},C_{1}^{m}]\subseteq[b_{1}^{m-1},A_{1}^{m-1}]
. Now, since also
r_{n_{1}}(C_{1}^{m})=a_{1}
then
\mathtt{depth}_{C_{1}^{m}}(b_{1}^{m})=n_{1}
, thus by
A.3.1
we have that
[b_{1}^{m},C_{1}^{m}]\neq\emptyset
, so we take
B_{1}^{m}\in[b_{1}^{m},C_{1}^{m}]
. Later, we consider
A_{1}^{m}\in[b_{1}^{m},B_{1}^{m}]
determined by
A_{1}^{m}=\sigma\langle A_{1};[b_{1}^{m},B_{1}^{m}]\rangle
, so that
A_{1}^{m}
is the second move of the player I in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
using the winning strategy
\sigma
, in response to the throw
[b_{1}^{m},B_{1}^{m}]
executed by the player II. As a result, we obtain the desired objects
C_{1}^{m}\geq B_{1}^{m}\geq A_{1}^{m}
for all
m\leq p_{1}
. Finally, for the last case
b_{1}^{p_{1}}=a_{1}
, we have that
A_{1}^{p_{1}}\in[a_{1}]
and hence
r_{n_{1}}(A_{1}^{p_{1}})=a_{1}
; consequently, we define
A_{2}\in[a_{1},A_{1}]
as the object
A_{2}=A_{1}^{p_{1}}
, and we take
a_{2}\in\mathcal{AR}
given by
a_{2}=r_{n_{2}}(A_{2})
, then
a_{1}\sqsubset a_{2}
and
|a_{2}|=|a_{1}|+1
.

In this order of ideas, by induction we fix
k\in\mathbb{N}-\{0\}
and suppose that the
j
-th stage of the process has been constructed for each
j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
, where we have defined a finite sequence of basic open sets
\{[n_{j},A_{j}]\}_{j=0}^{k}
such that
[n_{0},A_{0}]\supseteq\cdots\supseteq[n_{k},A_{k}]
, which it has associated a finite sequence of approximations
\{a_{j}\}_{j=0}^{k}
described by
a_{j}=r_{n_{j}}(A_{j})
, hence
a_{0}\sqsubset\cdots\sqsubset a_{k}
and
|a_{j+1}|=|a_{j}|+1
.

Now, for the
k
-th stage of the process, we consider the set of approximations
\{b\in\mathcal{AR}\,|\,a\sqsubset b\wedge\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(b)=n_{k}\}
, which corresponds to a finite set by virtue of
A.2.1
, since
\{b\in\mathcal{AR}\,|\,a\sqsubset b\wedge\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(b)=n_{k}\}\subseteq\{b\in\mathcal{AR}\,|\,b\leq_{\text{fin}}a_{k}\}
; thus, we fix an enumeration
\{b\in\mathcal{AR}\,|\,a\sqsubset b\wedge\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(b)=n_{k}\}=\{b_{k}^{0},b_{k}^{1},\ldots,b_{k}^{p_{k}}\}
with
p_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
b_{k}^{0}=b_{k}^{p_{k}}=a_{k}
and
b_{k}^{i}\neq b_{k}^{j}
for every
1\leq i<j\leq p_{k}
.

Next, we recursively define a finite sequence of objects
C_{k}^{0},B_{k}^{0},A_{k}^{0},C_{k}^{1},B_{k}^{1},A_{k}^{1},\ldots,C_{k}^{p_{k}},B_{k}^{p_{k}},A_{k}^{p_{k}}
in
[a,A_{k}]
, such that it satisfies the following properties:

(i)

C_{k}^{0}=B_{k}^{0}=A_{k}^{0}=A_{k}
.

(ii)

C_{k}^{m}\geq B_{k}^{m}\geq A_{k}^{m}
for every
m\in\{0,\ldots,p_{k}\}
.

(iii)

r_{n_{k}}(C_{k}^{m})=r_{n_{k}}(A_{k})=a_{k}
for every
m\in\{0,\ldots,p_{k}\}
.

(iv)

C_{k}^{m+1}\in[a_{k},C_{k}^{m}]
for every
m\in\{0,\ldots,p_{k}-1\}
.

(v)

[b_{k}^{m},C_{k}^{m+1}]\subseteq[b_{k}^{m},A_{k}^{m}]
for every
m\in\{0,\ldots,p_{k}-1\}
.

(vi)

B_{k}^{m}\in[b_{k}^{m},C_{k}^{m}]
for every
m\in\{0,\ldots,p_{k}\}
.

(vii)

A_{k}^{m}=\sigma\langle\overset{\longrightarrow}{\rho}[b_{j}^{n_{m}}];A_{j}^{n_{m}};[b_{k}^{m},B_{k}^{m}]\rangle
for every
m\in\{1,\ldots,p_{k}\}
, where
j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
and
n_{m}\in\{1,\ldots,p_{j}\}
are such that
\mathtt{depth}_{A_{j}}(b_{j}^{n_{m}})=n_{j}
and
a\sqsubseteq b_{j}^{n_{m}}\sqsubset b_{k}^{m}
with
|b_{j}^{n_{m}}|=|b_{k}^{m}|-1
, and being
\overset{\longrightarrow}{\rho}[b_{j}^{n_{m}}]
the partial run in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
coded by
b_{j}^{n_{m}}
in some previous stage of the construction, specifically in the
j
-th stage, for which
\sigma\langle\overset{\longrightarrow}{\rho}[b_{j}^{n_{m}}]\rangle=A_{j}^{n_{m}}
.

By induction, suppose that the objects
C_{k}^{m-1}\geq B_{k}^{m-1}\geq A_{k}^{m-1}
in
[a,A_{k}]
have been defined for some
1\leq m\leq p_{k}
, where
C_{k}^{m-1}\in[a_{k}]
and
B_{k}^{m-1},A_{k}^{m-1}\in[b_{k}^{m-1}]
. Since
r_{n_{k}}(C_{k}^{m-1})=r_{n_{k}}(A_{k})=a_{k}
, then
\mathtt{depth}_{C_{k}^{m-1}}(b_{k}^{m-1})=\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(b_{k}^{m-1})=n_{k}
, hence
\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}^{m-1}}(b_{k}^{m-1})\leq\mathtt{depth}_{C_{k}^{m-1}}(b_{k}^{m-1})<\infty
, so by applying
A.3.2
we infer that there is an object
C_{k}^{m}\in[a_{k},C_{k}^{m-1}]
such that
[b_{k}^{m-1},C_{k}^{m}]\subseteq[b_{k}^{m-1},A_{k}^{m-1}]
. Also, notice that
\mathtt{depth}_{C_{k}^{m}}(b_{k}^{m})=\mathtt{depth}_{A_{k}}(b_{k}^{m})=n_{k}
because
C_{k}^{m}\in[a_{k},A_{k}]
with
a_{k}=r_{n_{k}}(A_{k})
, thus by
A.3.1
we have that
\mathtt{depth}_{C_{k}^{m}}(b_{k}^{m})<\infty
implies
[b_{k}^{m},C_{k}^{m}]\neq\emptyset
, then we take any object
B_{k}^{m}\in[b_{k}^{m},C_{k}^{m}]
.

Moreover, we know that
b_{k}^{m}\leq_{\text{fin}}a_{k}
; thus, if
d\in\mathcal{AR}
is the approximation such that
a\sqsubseteq d\sqsubset b_{k}^{m}
and
|d|=|b_{k}^{m}|-1
, then by
A.2.3
we deduce that there is an approximation
a_{j}\sqsubset a_{k}
with
j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
such that
d\leq_{\text{fin}}a_{j}
; in fact, we can assume that
j
is the smallest satisfying this property, in which case
\mathtt{depth}_{A_{j}}(d)=n_{j}
because
a_{j}=r_{n_{j}}(A_{j})
, consequently there is some
n_{m}\in\{1,\ldots,p_{j}\}
such that
d=b_{j}^{n_{m}}
, whereby
\mathtt{depth}_{A_{j}}(b_{j}^{n_{m}})=n_{j}
and
a\sqsubseteq b_{j}^{n_{m}}\sqsubset b_{k}^{m}
with
|b_{j}^{n_{m}}|=|b_{k}^{m}|-1
.

Now, in the
j
-th stage previously constructed, we consider the partial run
\overset{\longrightarrow}{\rho}[b_{j}^{n_{m}}]
in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
coded by the approximation
b_{j}^{n_{m}}
, for which we have that
\sigma\langle\overset{\longrightarrow}{\rho}[b_{j}^{n_{m}}]\rangle=A_{j}^{n_{m}}
, so at some point
A_{j}^{n_{m}}
was the last move made by the player
I
through the winning strategy
\sigma
. To continue, notice that
B_{k}^{m}\in[b_{k}^{m},A_{j}^{n_{m}}]
, since
B_{k}^{m}\in[b_{k}^{m},C_{k}^{m}]\subseteq[b_{k}^{m},A_{k}]\subseteq[b_{j}^{n_{m}},C_{j}^{n_{m}+1}]\subseteq[b_{j}^{n_{m}},A_{j}^{n_{m}}]
whenever
1\leq n_{m}<p_{j}
, and also
B_{k}^{m}\in[b_{k}^{m},C_{k}^{m}]\subseteq[b_{k}^{m},A_{k}]\subseteq[a_{j},A_{j+1}]
if
n_{m}=p_{j}
. Therefore, we define the object
A_{k}^{m}\in[b_{k}^{m},B_{k}^{m}]
given by
A_{k}^{m}=\sigma\langle\overset{\longrightarrow}{\rho}[b_{j}^{n_{m}}];A_{j}^{n_{m}};[b_{k}^{m},B_{k}^{m}]\rangle
, so that
A_{k}^{m}
is the next move of the player I in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
using the winning strategy
\sigma
, in response to the throw
[b_{k}^{m},B_{k}^{m}]
executed by the player II. To conclude, in this way we have constructed the desired objects
C_{k}^{m}\geq B_{k}^{m}\geq A_{k}^{m}
for all
m\leq p_{k}
.

In the end, for the last case corresponding to
b_{k}^{p_{k}}=a_{k}
, we obtain that
A_{k}^{p_{k}}=\sigma\langle\overset{\longrightarrow}{\rho}[a_{k-1}];A_{k};[a_{k},B_{k}^{p_{k}}]\rangle
, then
A_{k}^{p_{k}}\in[a_{k},B_{k}^{p_{k}}]
and hence
r_{n_{k}}(A_{k}^{p_{k}})=a_{k}
. Therefore, we finally define
A_{k+1}\in[a_{k},A_{k}]
as the object
A_{k+1}=A_{k}^{p_{k}}
, and we take the approximation
a_{k+1}\in\mathcal{AR}
given by
a_{k+1}=r_{n_{k+1}}(A_{k+1})
, so that
a_{k}\sqsubset a_{k+1}
and
|a_{k+1}|=|a_{k}|+1
.

In this way, we have successfully constructed, among other considerations, the fusion sequence
\{[n_{k},A_{k}]\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\wp([a,M])
, where
n_{k}=\mathtt{depth}_{M}(a)+k=|a|+k
and
r_{n_{k}}(A_{k})=a_{k}
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, if the object
H\in[a,M]
is the limit
H=\lim_{n_{k}}A_{k}
of the fusion sequence
\{[n_{k},A_{k}]\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, then it is true that
H\in[a_{k},A_{k}]
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and consequently
\{H\}=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[a_{k}]
.

Next, we proceed to consider the following complete run in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
:

\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})

\begin{matrix}\textrm{I}&&A_{1}&&A_{2}&&A_{3}&&\cdots&\\
\textrm{II}&&&[a_{1},B_{1}^{p_{1}}]&&[a_{2},B_{2}^{p_{2}}]&&[a_{3},B_{3}^{p_{3}}]&&\cdots\end{matrix}

In view of the fact that this complete run is obtained by the player I applying the winning strategy
\sigma
in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, due to
A_{1}=\sigma\langle\emptyset\rangle
and
A_{k+1}=\sigma\langle\overset{\longrightarrow}{\rho}[a_{k-1}];A_{k};[a_{k},B_{k}^{p_{k}}]\rangle
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}-\{0\}
, then we deduce that the player I wins the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
; therefore, we infer that
H\in\mathcal{X}
because
\{H\}=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[a_{k}]
.

Finally, let us check that the object
H\in[a,M]\cap\mathcal{X}
satisfies that
[a,H]\subseteq\mathcal{X}
. Indeed, if
B\in[a,H]
then
B\leq H
with
r_{|a|}(B)=a=r_{|a|}(H)
; so, by virtue of
A.2.2
we infer that for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
there is some
j_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r_{|a|+m}(B)\leq_{\text{fin}}r_{|a|+j_{m}}(H)
, hence
r_{|a|+m}(B)\leq_{\text{fin}}a_{j_{m}}
, and without loss of generality we can suppose that
j_{m}
is the smallest satisfying this property, in which case
\mathtt{depth}_{A_{j_{m}}}(r_{|a|+j_{m}}(B))=n_{j_{m}}
, whereby
r_{|a|+j_{m}}(B)=b_{j_{m}}^{i_{m}}
for some
i_{m}\in\{1,\ldots,p_{j_{m}}\}
. Therefore, the object
B\in[a,H]
satisfies that
\{B\}=\bigcap_{m\in\mathbb{N}}[b_{j_{m}}^{i_{m}}]
; in view of the foregoing, we proceed to consider the following complete run in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
:

\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})

\begin{matrix}\textrm{I}&&A_{1}&&A_{j_{2}}^{i_{2}}&&A_{j_{3}}^{i_{3}}&&\cdots&\\
\textrm{II}&&&[b_{j_{1}}^{i_{1}},B_{j_{1}}^{i_{1}}]&&[b_{j_{2}}^{i_{2}},B_{j_{2}}^{i_{2}}]&&[b_{j_{3}}^{i_{3}},B_{j_{3}}^{i_{3}}]&&\cdots\end{matrix}

It should be noted that this complete run is obtained by applying the winning strategy
\sigma
of the player I in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, hence the player I wins the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, as a result we deduce that
B\in\mathcal{X}
because
\{B\}=\bigcap_{m\in\mathbb{N}}[b_{j_{m}}^{i_{m}}]
. Consequently, we finally conclude that
[a,H]\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.
∎

Motivated by the concept of selective coideal on the Ellentuck space, given by Mathias in
[
24
]
, as well as by the abstract approximation of local Ramsey theory in the context of topological Ramsey spaces, proposed by Di Prisco, Mijares and Nieto in
[
6
]
, we now introduce the wide class of selective topological Ramsey spaces.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a topological Ramsey space. Given any
[a,B]\neq\emptyset
and a sequence
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[a,B]
, we say that an object
D_{\infty}\in[a,B]
is a
diagonalization
of
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
whenever
\emptyset\neq[b,D_{\infty}]\subseteq[b,D_{n_{b}}]
for every
b\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![a,D_{\infty}]
, where
n_{b}=\mathtt{depth}_{B}(b)-\mathtt{depth}_{B}(a)
.

Definition 5.4
.

A topological Ramsey space
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
is
selective
if for any
[a,B]\neq\emptyset
and for all sequence
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[a,B]
such that
D_{n+1}\leq D_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists some
D_{\infty}\in[a,B]
such that
D_{\infty}
is a diagonalization of
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

We point out that many well known examples of topological Ramsey spaces are selective; however, there are exceptions, for instance, spaces of strong subtrees (see
[
26
,
31
]
).

We turn now to the case when player II has a winning strategy in the abstract Kastanas game. To do this, first we show a lemma about selectivity that we will need later, which is inspired by the development of local version of Kastanas game given by Matet in
[
22
]
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a selective topological Ramsey space. Let
[a,B]\neq\emptyset
with
a\sqsubset B
, and let
f:[a,B]\rightarrow\mathcal{AR}_{|a|+1}
and
g:[a,B]\rightarrow[a,B]
be functions such that
a\sqsubset f(A)
and
g(A)\in[f(A),A]
for each
A\in[a,B]
. Then, there exists some
E_{f,g}\in[a,B]
which satisfies that for every
q\in r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f,g}]
there is
A\in[a,B]
such that
f(A)=q
and
\emptyset\neq[q,E_{f,g}]\subseteq[q,g(A)]
.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a selective topological Ramsey space. Given a basic open set
[a,B]\neq\emptyset
with
a\sqsubset B
, we fix functions
f:[a,B]\rightarrow\mathcal{AR}_{|a|+1}
and
g:[a,B]\rightarrow[a,B]
such that
a\sqsubset f(A)
and
g(A)\in[f(A),A]
for each
A\in[a,B]
.

First of all, for every
p\in r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,B]
we consider the set
\mathcal{W}_{p}\subseteq[a,B]
defined by

\mathcal{W}_{p}=\{D\in[a,B]\,|\,(\exists\,A\in[a,B])(g(A)=D\wedge f(A)=p)\}
.

We proceed to recursively construct a sequence of objects
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[a,B]
, with
D_{0}=B
and
D_{n+1}\leq D_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, as follows:

By induction, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose that the objects
D_{0}\geq D_{1}\geq\cdots\geq D_{n-1}
in
[a,B]
has been constructed. Now, let
\Gamma_{n}=\{p\in r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,B]\,|\,\mathtt{depth}_{B}(p)=|a|+n\}
, then the set
\Gamma_{n}
is finite by virtue of
A.2.1
, since
\Gamma_{n}\subseteq\{p\in\mathcal{AR}\,|\,p\leq_{\text{fin}}r_{|a|+n}(B)\}
; so, we fix any enumeration
\Gamma_{n}=\{p_{n}^{1},\ldots,p_{n}^{k_{n}}\}
with
k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
. Next, we recursively define a finite sequence of objects
D_{n}^{0},D_{n}^{1},\ldots,D_{n}^{k_{n}}
in
[a,D_{n-1}]
. To do this, we put
D_{n}^{0}=D_{n-1}
, and assuming that the object
D_{n}^{i-1}
has been determined for
1\leq i\leq k_{n}
, we consider the set
\mathcal{W}_{p_{n}^{i}}\cap[a,D_{n}^{i-1}]
and we analyze the following cases: on the one hand, if
\mathcal{W}_{p_{n}^{i}}\cap[a,D_{n}^{i-1}]=\emptyset
, then we take
D_{n}^{i}=D_{n}^{i-1}
; on the other hand, if
\mathcal{W}_{p_{n}^{i}}\cap[a,D_{n}^{i-1}]\neq\emptyset
, then we take
D_{n}^{i}
as any fixed object
D_{n}^{i}\in\mathcal{W}_{p_{n}^{i}}\cap[a,D_{n}^{i-1}]
. In this way, we get finitely many objects
D_{n}^{0}\geq D_{n}^{1}\geq\cdots\geq D_{n}^{k_{n}}
in
[a,D_{n-1}]
; thus, we define
D_{n}\in[a,D_{n-1}]
as the object
D_{n}=D_{n}^{k_{n}}
.

As an additional remark to the construction just explained, it should be noted that the family of approximations
r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,B]
can be described in terms of the sequence of finite sets
\{\Gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as follows:

r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,B]=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Gamma_{n}=\{p_{n}^{i}\,|\,n\in\mathbb{N}\wedge 1\leq i\leq k_{n}\}
.

Now, we have that the sequence of objects
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[a,B]
is decreasing with respect to quasi-order
\leq
, then using the fact that our topological Ramsey space
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
is selective, we fix an object
D_{\infty}\in[a,B]
such that
D_{\infty}
corresponds to a diagonalization of sequence
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
; therefore, for every
b\in\mathcal{AR}\!\restriction\![a,D_{\infty}]
, if
\mathtt{depth}_{B}(b)=|a|+n
then
\emptyset\neq[b,D_{\infty}]\subseteq[b,D_{n}]
.

Next, let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{AR}_{|a|+1}
be the collection of approximations defined by

\mathcal{O}=\{p_{n}^{i}\in r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,B]\;|\;\mathcal{W}_{p_{n}^{i}}\cap[a,D_{n}^{i-1}]\neq\emptyset\}.

Applying the abstract pigeonhole principle
A.4
to the family
\mathcal{O}
, we deduce that there exists some object
E_{f,g}\in[a,D_{\infty}]\subseteq[a,B]
such that
r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f,g}]
is either contained in
\mathcal{O}
or disjoint from
\mathcal{O}
, so

r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f,g}]\subseteq\mathcal{O}\;\;\;\;\;\vee\;\;\;\;\;\mathcal{O}\cap r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f,g}]=\emptyset
.

However, the second option is impossible, this due to
f(E_{f,g})\in\mathcal{O}\cap r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f,g}]
. Indeed, on the one hand
a\sqsubset f(E_{f,g})
with
|f(E_{f,g})|=|a|+1
and also
g(E_{f,g})\in[f(E_{f,g}),E_{f,g}]
, so
r_{|a|+1}(g(E_{f,g}))=f(E_{f,g})
and hence
f(E_{f,g})\in r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f,g}]
. On the other hand,
f(E_{f,g})=p_{n}^{i}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq i\leq k_{n}
, so that
\mathtt{depth}_{B}(f(E_{f,g}))=|a|+n
and also
g(E_{f,g})\in\mathcal{W}_{p_{n}^{i}}
, then
[f(E_{f,g}),E_{f,g}]\subseteq[f(E_{f,g}),D_{\infty}]\subseteq[f(E_{f,g}),D_{n}]\subseteq[a,D_{n}]\subseteq[a,D_{n}^{i-1}]
, thus
g(E_{f,g})\in[a,D_{n}^{i-1}]
; therefore, we deduce that
\mathcal{W}_{f(E_{f,g})}\cap[a,D_{n}^{i-1}]=\mathcal{W}_{p_{n}^{i}}\cap[a,D_{n}^{i-1}]\neq\emptyset
, hence
f(E_{f,g})\in\mathcal{O}
. Consequently, it must necessarily be satisfied that
r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f,g}]\subseteq\mathcal{O}
.

Finally, let us verify that for every
q\in r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f,g}]
there is some
A\in[a,B]
such that
f(A)=q
and
\emptyset\neq[q,E_{f,g}]\subseteq[q,g(A)]
. Indeed, if
q\in r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f,g}]
then
q=p_{n}^{i}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq i\leq k_{n}
; additionally, since
r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f,g}]\subseteq\mathcal{O}
then
q\in\mathcal{O}
, so that
\mathcal{W}_{p_{n}^{i}}\cap[a,D_{n}^{i-1}]\neq\emptyset
and in particular
D_{n}^{i}\in\mathcal{W}_{p_{n}^{i}}
, that is
D_{n}^{i}\in\mathcal{W}_{q}
, which means that there exists some
A\in[a,B]
such that
f(A)=q
y
g(A)=D_{n}^{i}
, hence
[q,g(A)]=[q,D_{n}^{i}]
; moreover, since
\mathtt{depth}_{B}(q)=|a|+n
then we have that
[q,E_{f,g}]\subseteq[q,D_{\infty}]\subseteq[q,D_{n}]\subseteq[q,D_{n}^{i}]
, reason for which we conclude that
\emptyset\neq[q,E_{f,g}]\subseteq[q,g(A)]
. ∎

Applying the above lemma, we consider selective topological Ramsey spaces to obtain the following result that characterizes when player II has a winning strategy in the abstract Kastanas game.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a selective topological Ramsey space, and let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and
[a,M]\neq\emptyset
be given. Then, the player II has a winning strategy in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
if and only if for every
N\in[a,M]
there is
H\in[a,N]
such that
[a,H]\cap\mathcal{X}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Given a selective topological Ramsey space
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
, we fix a set of objects
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and a basic open set
[a,M]\neq\emptyset
, for which we assume without loss of generality that
a\sqsubset M
. So, we proceed to consider the abstract Kastanas game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
.

First of all, suppose that for all
N\in[a,M]
there exists an object
H\in[a,N]
such that
[a,H]\cap\mathcal{X}=\emptyset
, in this case a winning strategy for the player II in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, is given as follows: player II must be the basic open set
[r_{|a|+1}(H),H]
as the first move, in response to the first throw
N
executed by player I. Indeed, consider any complete run in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
of the form

\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})

\begin{matrix}\textrm{I}&&N&&A_{2}&&A_{3}&&\cdots&\\
\textrm{II}&&&[r_{|a|+1}(H),H]&&[a_{2},B_{2}]&&[a_{3},B_{3}]&&\cdots\end{matrix}

So, notice that the object
B_{\infty}\in[a,M]
given by
\{B_{\infty}\}=\bigcap_{k=2}^{\infty}[a_{k}]
is such that
B_{\infty}\in[a,H]
, thus
B_{\infty}\notin\mathcal{X}
and consequently the player II wins the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
.

Conversely, suppose that the player II has a winning strategy
\tau
in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, and let
N\in[a,M]
be a fixed but arbitrary object. Now, our goal is to construct a winning strategy
\sigma
for the player I in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
.

In order to successfully accomplish our goal, we must iteratively apply lemma
5.5
to recursively construct a complete run in the game
G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
with consecutive moves of the player II given by a sequence of basic open sets
\{[a_{n},B_{n}]\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, for which we define sequences of functions
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\{g_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
as well as sequences of objects
\{A_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\{E_{f_{n},g_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in the space
\mathcal{R}
, such that:

(1)

[f_{1}(A),g_{1}(A)]=\tau\langle A\rangle
for every
A\in[a,N]
.

(2)

[f_{n+1}(A),g_{n+1}(A)]=\tau\left\langle A_{1};[f_{1}(A_{1}),g_{1}(A_{1})];\ldots;A_{n};[f_{n}(A_{n}),g_{n}(A_{n})];A\right\rangle
for every
A\in[a_{n},B_{n}]
.

(3)

A_{1},E_{f_{1},g_{1}}\in[a,N]
and
A_{n+1},E_{f_{n+1},g_{n+1}}\in[a_{n},B_{n}]
.

(4)

f_{n}(A_{n})=a_{n}
and
B_{n}\in[a_{n},E_{f_{n},g_{n}}]\subseteq[a_{n},g_{n}(A_{n})]
.

For the first stage of the process, we take functions
f_{1}:[a,N]\rightarrow\mathcal{AR}_{|a|+1}
and
g_{1}:[a,N]\rightarrow[a,N]
defined by
[f_{1}(A),g_{1}(A)]=\tau\langle A\rangle
for each
A\in[a,N]
, so the basic open set
[f_{1}(A),g_{1}(A)]
is the first move of the player II using the winning strategy
\tau
in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, in response to any throw
A
in
[a,N]
given by the player I; then, we have
a\sqsubset f_{1}(A)
and
g_{1}(A)\in[f_{1}(A),A]
for each
A\in[a,N]
. Therefore, by lemma
5.5
, we infer that there exists an object
E_{f_{1},g_{1}}\in[a,N]
with the property that for every
q\in r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f_{1},g_{1}}]
there is
A\in[a,N]
such that
f_{1}(A)=q
and
\emptyset\neq[q,E_{f_{1},g_{1}}]\subseteq[q,g_{1}(A)]
. Thus, we define
\sigma\langle\emptyset\rangle=E_{f_{1},g_{1}}
.

Again, we give a detailed description of the next stage of the construction for the benefit of the reader. In the next stage of the process, suppose that the short partial run given by
E_{f_{1},g_{1}}
followed by
[a_{1},B_{1}]
, as the first moves of the players I and II, respectively, has been developed in the game
G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
, so that
B_{1}\in[a_{1},E_{f_{1},g_{1}}]
and
a\sqsubset a_{1}
with
|a_{1}|=|a|+1
. Since
a_{1}\in r_{|a|+1}\text{''}\,[a,E_{f_{1},g_{1}}]
, then there is
A_{1}\in[a,N]
such that
f_{1}(A_{1})=a_{1}
and
\emptyset\neq[a_{1},E_{f_{1},g_{1}}]\subseteq[a_{1},g_{1}(A_{1})]
, so
B_{1}\in[a_{1},g_{1}(A_{1})]
and hence
\emptyset\neq[a_{1},B_{1}]\subseteq[a_{1},g_{1}(A_{1})]
. Let
f_{2}:[a_{1},B_{1}]\rightarrow\mathcal{AR}_{|a_{1}|+1}
and
g_{2}:[a_{1},B_{1}]\rightarrow[a_{1},B_{1}]
be functions defined by
[f_{2}(A),g_{2}(A)]=\tau\langle A_{1};[a_{1},g_{1}(A_{1})];A\rangle
for each
A\in[a_{1},B_{1}]
, so the second move of the player II using the winning strategy
\tau
in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
is
[f_{2}(A),g_{2}(A)]
, in response to any throw
A
in
[a_{1},B_{1}]
given by the player I, who had selected
A_{1}
as previous throw; then,
a_{1}\sqsubset f_{2}(A)
and
g_{2}(A)\in[f_{2}(A),A]
for each
A\in[a_{1},B_{1}]
. Therefore, by the lemma
5.5
, there exists
E_{f_{2},g_{2}}\in[a_{1},B_{1}]
with the property that for every
q\in r_{|a_{1}|+1}\text{''}\,[a_{1},E_{f_{2},g_{2}}]
there is
A\in[a_{1},B_{1}]
such that
f_{2}(A)=q
and
\emptyset\neq[q,E_{f_{2},g_{2}}]\subseteq[q,g_{2}(A)]
. Thus, we define
\sigma\langle E_{f_{1},g_{1}};[a_{1},B_{1}]\rangle=E_{f_{2},g_{2}}
.

In this order of ideas, by induction we fix
n\in\mathbb{N}-\{0\}
and suppose that the
j
-th stage of the process has been constructed for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, that is, we are assuming defined the first
n
stages of the strategy
\sigma
for the player I in the game
G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
, where the finite sequence of basic open sets
\{[a_{j},B_{j}]\}_{j=1}^{n}
corresponds to the first
n
consecutive moves of the player II, which it has associated both functions
\{f_{n}\}_{j=1}^{n}
and
\{g_{n}\}_{j=1}^{n}
as well as objects
\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
and
\{E_{f_{j},g_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{n}
in the space
\mathcal{R}
, satisfying the conditions established in items (1), (2), (3), (4) above, such that
\sigma\langle\emptyset\rangle=E_{f_{1},g_{1}}
and
\sigma\langle E_{f_{1},g_{1}};[a_{1},B_{1}];\ldots;E_{f_{j-1},g_{j-1}};[a_{j-1},B_{j-1}]\rangle=E_{f_{j},g_{j}}
for every
1\leq j\leq n
.

Now, in the game
G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
we consider the partial run described by the consecutive moves
E_{f_{1},g_{1}},\ldots,E_{f_{n},g_{n}}
executed by the player I, which are alternated together with the successive throws
[a_{1},B_{1}],\ldots,[a_{n},B_{n}]
of the player II, so in particular we have
B_{n}\in[a_{n},E_{f_{n},g_{n}}]
and
a_{n-1}\sqsubset a_{n}
with
|a_{n}|=|a_{n-1}|+1
, hence
a_{n}\in r_{|a_{n-1}|+1}\text{''}\,[a_{n-1},E_{f_{n},g_{n}}]
; then, there exists some object
A_{n}\in[a_{n-1},B_{n-1}]
such that
f_{n}(A_{n})=a_{n}
and
\emptyset\neq[a_{n},E_{f_{n},g_{n}}]\subseteq[a_{n},g_{n}(A_{n})]
, this by virtue of the fact that the object
E_{f_{n},g_{n}}
was previously constructed using lemma
5.5
; therefore,
B_{n}\in[a_{n},g_{n}(A_{n})]
and hence
\emptyset\neq[a_{n},B_{n}]\subseteq[a_{n},g_{n}(A_{n})]
.

Next, we consider functions
f_{n+1}:[a_{n},B_{n}]\rightarrow\mathcal{AR}_{|a_{n}|+1}
and
g_{n+1}:[a_{n},B_{n}]\rightarrow[a_{n},B_{n}]
defined by
[f_{n+1}(A),g_{n+1}(A)]=\tau\langle A_{1};[a_{1},g_{1}(A_{1})];\ldots;A_{n};[a_{n},g_{n}(A_{n})];A\rangle
for each
A\in[a_{n},B_{n}]
, that is, the basic open set
[f_{n+1}(A),g_{n+1}(A)]
corresponds to the
(n+1)
-th move of the player II using the winning strategy
\tau
in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, in response to any throw
A
in
[a_{n},B_{n}]
given by the player I, who had successively selected
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
as previous throws; then, it is clear that
a_{n}\sqsubset f_{n+1}(A)
and
g_{n+1}(A)\in[f_{n+1}(A),A]
for each
A\in[a_{n},B_{n}]
. Therefore, by applying lemma
5.5
, we deduce the existence of some object
E_{f_{n+1},g_{n+1}}\in[a_{n},B_{n}]
with the special property that for every
q\in r_{|a_{n}|+1}\text{''}\,[a_{n},E_{f_{n+1},g_{n+1}}]
there is
A\in[a_{n},B_{n}]
such that
f_{n+1}(A)=q
and
\emptyset\neq[q,E_{f_{n+1},g_{n+1}}]\subseteq[q,g_{n+1}(A)]
. On merit of the above, we define
\sigma\langle E_{f_{1},g_{1}};[a_{1},B{1}];\ldots;E_{f_{n},g_{n}};[a_{n},B_{n}]\rangle=E_{f_{n+1},g_{n+1}}
.

In this way, we have successfully constructed, among other considerations, the strategy
\sigma
for the player I in the game
G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
, which consists in consecutively selecting the objects of the sequence
\{E_{f_{n},g_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in each of the moves, thus:

\sigma\langle\emptyset\rangle=E_{f_{1},g_{1}}\,\text{ and }\,\sigma\left\langle E_{f_{1},g_{1}};[a_{1},B_{1}];\ldots;E_{f_{n},g_{n}};[a_{n},B_{n}]\right\rangle=E_{f_{n+1},g_{n+1}}\,\text{ for every }n\in\mathbb{N}-\{0\}.

Now, let
B_{\infty}\in[a,N]
be the object determined by
\{B_{\infty}\}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}[a_{n}]
. To continue, we proceed to consider the following complete run in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
:

\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})

\begin{matrix}\textrm{I}&&A_{1}&&A_{2}&&A_{3}&&\cdots&\\
\textrm{II}&&&[a_{1},g_{1}(A_{1})]&&[a_{2},g_{2}(A_{2})]&&[a_{3},g_{3}(A_{3})]&&\cdots\end{matrix}

In view of the fact that this complete run is obtained by applying the winning strategy
\tau
for the player II in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, since
\tau\left\langle A_{1};[a_{1},g_{1}(A_{1})];\ldots;A_{n};[a_{n},g_{n}(A_{n})];A_{n+1}\right\rangle=[a_{n+1},g_{n+1}(A_{n+1})]
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}-\{0\}
, then we deduce that the player II wins the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,M]}(\mathcal{X})
, thus
B_{\infty}\notin\mathcal{X}
and hence
B_{\infty}\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X}
.

On the other hand, by applying the strategy
\sigma
that has been recursively constructed for the player I in the game
G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
, where
\{E_{f_{n},g_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
corresponds to the sequence of consecutive moves of the player I, with
\{[a_{n},B_{n}]\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
being the respective sequence of successive moves of the player II, then we obtain the following complete run in the game
G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
:

G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})

\begin{matrix}\textrm{I}&&E_{f_{1},g_{1}}&&E_{f_{2},g_{2}}&&E_{f_{3},g_{3}}&&\cdots&\\
\textrm{II}&&&[a_{1},B_{1}]&&[a_{2},B_{2}]&&[a_{3},B_{3}]&&\cdots\end{matrix}

Consequently, by virtue of the fact that
B_{\infty}\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X}
, then it follows that the player I wins the game
G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
; thus, we conclude that the strategy
\sigma
is actually a winning strategy for the player I in the game
G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
.

Finally, since the player I has a winning strategy in th game
G_{[a,N]}(\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X})
, then by proposition
5.2
we deduce that there exists some object
H\in[a,N]
such that
[a,H]\subseteq\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X}
, and thus
[a,H]\cap\mathcal{X}=\emptyset
.
∎

At last, we are ready to present the main result of our article, which corresponds to the generalization of theorem
2.5
for the abstract context. Thus, our abstract version of Kastanas theorem characterizes the Ramsey property through the existence of winning strategies in the abstract Kastanas game on the combinatorial structure of selective topological Ramsey spaces.

Theorem 5.7
.

[
Abstract Kastanas Theorem
]. Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a selective topological Ramsey space. Then, for every
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\mathcal{X}
is Ramsey.

(b)

For all
[a,A]\neq\emptyset
, the abstract Kastanas game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,A]}(\mathcal{X})
is determined.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{R},\leq,r)
be a selective topological Ramsey space, and fix both a set of objects
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and a basic open set
[a,A]\neq\emptyset
.

If the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,A]}(\mathcal{X})
is determined, then propositions
5.2
and
5.6
clearly imply that there exists some
H\in[a,A]
such that either
[a,H]\subseteq\mathcal{X}
or
[a,H]\cap\mathcal{X}=\emptyset
, so that
\mathcal{X}
is a Ramsey set.

Conversely, suppose that the set
\mathcal{X}
is Ramsey and that the player II does not have a winning strategy in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,A]}(\mathcal{X})
; thus, by proposition
5.6
, there exists some
B\in[a,A]
such that
[a,H]\cap\mathcal{X}\neq\emptyset
for all
H\in[a,B]
. Since
\mathcal{X}
is Ramsey, then for some
H\in[a,B]\subseteq[a,A]
we have
[a,H]\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, so that the player I has a winning strategy in the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,A]}(\mathcal{X})
, by virtue of proposition
5.2
. Therefore, we conclude that the game
\mathcal{G}_{[a,A]}(\mathcal{X})
is determined.
∎

As a final comment we mention that in view of these results it might be interesting to explore the Ramsey property on topological Ramsey spaces under determinacy axioms.
