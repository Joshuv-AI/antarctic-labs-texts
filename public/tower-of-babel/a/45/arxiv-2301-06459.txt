Sparse Bayesian factor analysis when the number of factors is unknown
Sylvia Frühwirth-Schnatter, Darjus Hosszejni, Hedibert Freitas Lopes

Abstract
There has been increased research interest in the subfield of sparse Bayesian factor analysis with shrinkage priors, which achieve additional sparsity beyond the natural parsimonity of factor models. In this spirit, we estimate the number of common factors in the highly implemented sparse latent factor model with spike-and-slab priors on the factor loadings matrix. Our framework leads to a natural, efficient and simultaneous coupling of model estimation and selection on one hand and model identification and rank estimation (number of factors) on the other hand. More precisely, by embedding the unordered generalised lower triangular loadings representation into overfitting sparse factor modelling, we obtain posterior summaries regarding factor loadings, common factors as well as the factor dimension via postprocessing draws from our efficient and customised Markov chain Monte Carlo scheme.

Introduction

Factor analysis aims at identifying common variation in multivariate observations and relating
it to hidden causes, the so-called common factors, see
Thurstone 1947
and, more recently,
Anderson 2003
. The common setup consists
of a sample
{\mathbf{y}}=\{{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{y}}_{T}\}
of
T
multivariate observations
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=(y_{1t},\ldots,y_{mt})^{{}^{\prime}}
of dimension
m
.
For a given factor dimension
r
, the basic factor model is defined as a latent
variable model, involving the common factors
{\mathbf{f}}_{t}=(f_{1t}\cdots f_{rt})^{{}^{\prime}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{f}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{r}\left({\mathbf{0}},{{\mathbf{I}}}_{r}\right),\quad{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{\mathbf{f}}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t},\quad\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{m}\left({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}\right),\quad{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}=\mbox{\rm Diag}\,(\sigma^{2}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{m}),

(1.1)

where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is the
m\times r
matrix of
factor loadings
\Lambda_{ij}
and
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}
is the covariance matrix of the idiosyncratic errors
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}
.
Model (
1.1
) implies that conditional on
{\mathbf{f}}_{t}
the
m
elements of
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
are independent and all dependence among these variables is explained through the common factors. Assuming independence of
{\mathbf{f}}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}

implies that, marginally,
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
arises from a multivariate normal distribution,
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{m}\left({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{\Omega}}\right)
, with zero mean and a covariance matrix
{\mathbf{\Omega}}

with the following constrained structure:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\Omega}}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}.

(1.2)

Since
r
typically is (much) smaller than
m
, factor models yield a parsimonious
representation of
{\mathbf{\Omega}}
with (at most)
m(r+1)
instead of the
m(m+1)/2
parameters of an unconstrained covariance matrix. Hence, factor models proved to be very useful for covariance estimation,
especially if
m
is large; see
Fan
et al. 2008
,
Forni
et al. 2009
,
Bhattacharya and
Dunson 2011
and
Kastner 2019
, among others.

From the very beginning, the goal
of factor analysis has been to extract the hidden factors
to understand the driving forces behind the observed covariance.
Using (
1.2
), this requires a decomposition of the covariance matrix
{\mathbf{\Omega}}
into the cross-covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}

and the covariance matrix
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}
of the uncorrelated idiosyncratic errors. With the only source of information
being the observed covariance of the data, this is more challenging than estimating
{\mathbf{\Omega}}
itself.
A huge literature, going back to
Koopmans and
Reiersøl 1950
,
Reiersøl 1950
and
Anderson and
Rubin 1956
has addressed this problem of identification
which can be resolved by imposing additional structure on the factor model, see

Neudecker 1990
,
Geweke and
Zhou 1996
,
Bai and Ng 2013
,
Chan
et al. 2018
, and
Williams 2020
, among many others.
Recently, a new identification strategy based on unordered generalized lower triangular (UGLT) structures

(
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2018
;
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
)
was introduced that allows to address not only the commonly known rotational invariance problem, but also the problem of variance identification
(
Anderson and
Rubin 1956
)
of which the literature is still less aware.

The main goal of the present paper is to foster mathematically rigorous identification in Bayesian factor analysis.
The recent years have seen many contributions in the field of sparse Bayesian factor analysis which
achieves additional sparsity beyond the natural parsimonity of factor models through the choice of shrinkage priors.
One strand of literature considers continuous shrinkage priors on the factor loadings, often in combination with a prior that allows infinitely many columns in the loading matrix
of which only a finite number is non-zero such as the Indian buffet process prior
(
Griffiths and
Ghahramani 2006
)
or the multiplicative Gamma process
(
Bhattacharya and
Dunson 2011
)
, see
Zhao
et al. 2016
,
Ročková
and George 2017
,
Kastner 2019
, and
Legramanti
et al. 2020
for recent contributions.
Alternatively, following the pioneering paper by
West 2003
, many authors considered point mass mixture
priors on the factor loadings in basic factor models
(
Carvalho et al. 2008
;
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2010
)
, in dedicated factor models with correlated
factors
(
Conti et al. 2014
)
and
dynamic factor models
(
Kaufmann and
Schuhmacher 2019
)
.

Sparse Bayesian factor analysis with point mass mixture (also called spike-and-slab)
priors allow factor loadings to be exactly zero
and treat the identification of these elements as a variable selection problem.
This allows to identify “simple structures” where in each row only a few nonzero loadings are present
(
Anderson and
Rubin 1956
)
, a long standing issue in factor analysis addressed in
Conti et al. 2014
.
It also allows to identify irrelevant variables
y_{it}
which are uncorrelated
with the remaining variables in
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
, since the entire row of
the factor loading matrix is zero for these variables.
Among other fields, this is of relevance in economics
(
Kaufmann and
Schuhmacher 2017
)
, where it is common practice to include as many variables as possible in factor analysis
(
Stock and
Watson 2002
;
Boivin and
Ng 2006
)
,
and in bioinformatics
(
Lucas et al. 2006
)
, where typically only a few out of potentially ten thousands of genes may be related to a certain physiological outcome.

A challenging problem in factor analysis is choosing the factor dimension
r

which is usually unknown, see
Owen and
Wang 2016
for an excellent review. Often information criteria

(
Bai and Ng 2002
)
are used also in a Bayesian context, see e.g.
Aßmann et al. 2016
and
Chan
et al. 2018
, other authors
employ marginal likelihoods
(
Lee and
Song 2002
;
Lopes and
West 2004
)
. Learning about the factor dimension
r
is intrinsic in Bayesian approaches that allow
infinitely many columns in the loading matrix
(
Griffiths and
Ghahramani 2006
;
Bhattacharya and
Dunson 2011
;
Ročková
and George 2017
;
Legramanti
et al. 2020
)
, even if this approach requires careful tuning of hyperparameters
(
Durante 2017
)
.
A number of authors exploit variable selection in a finite-dimensional overfitting factor model
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2010
;
Conti et al. 2014
;
Kaufmann and
Schuhmacher 2017
)
and we will follow their lead in the present paper.
Our approach relies on
the following sparse Bayesian
exploratory factor analysis (EFA) model:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}{\mathbf{f}}^{k}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t},\qquad\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{m}\left({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}\right),\quad{\mathbf{f}}^{k}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{k}\left({\mathbf{0}},{{\mathbf{I}}}_{k}\right),

(1.3)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
is an
m\times k
loading matrix
with elements
\beta_{ij}
and
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}
is a diagonal matrix with strictly positive diagonal elements. Model (
1.3
) is potentially overfitting, since
we assume a finite factor dimension
k

which is larger than true number of factors
r
.
We employ spike-and-slab priors, where the elements
\beta_{ij}
of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
are allowed to be exactly zero, with the corresponding
m\times k
sparsity matrix with elements
\delta_{ij}
being denoted by
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
.
To learn the factor dimension
r
, we
exploit a finite version of the
two-parameter Beta prior
(
Ghahramani
et al. 2007
;
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2022
)

to define a shrinkage process prior on
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
that induces increasing shrinkage
of the factor loadings toward zero as the column index increases, extending
recent work by
Legramanti
et al. 2020
.

To achieve identification, we
impose a UGLT structure
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
)

on the loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
and the corresponding sparsity matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
in the EFA model (
1.3
).
Commonly, in machine learning and statistics, no constraints are imposed on
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
; however, leaving
the
sparsity pattern unconstrained makes it
more difficult to recover the true number of factors and reconstruct
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
from
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
. In econometrics as well as in statistics,
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
is often constrained to a lower triangular matrix, however such a constraint is too restrictive
(
Jöreskog 1969
;
Carvalho et al. 2008
)
.
The UGLT structure that we impose is a much weaker
constraint which only requires the top non-zero elements in each column of the loading matrix to lie in different rows and still leaves the model unidentified.
As shown in
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
, on the one hand it is weak enough to ensure that
any loading matrix can be rotated into a GLT representation, on the other hand it strong enough to ensure “controlled unidentifiability” which can be easily resolved.

For practical Bayesian inference, we develop a new and efficient Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) procedure that delivers posterior draws from the EFA model (
1.3
) under point mass mixture priors, which is known to be particularly challenging
(
Pati et al. 2014
)
. As part of our algorithm, we design a (simple)
reversible jump MCMC sampler to navigate through the space of UGLT loading matrices of varying factor dimension. We achieve mathematically rigorous identification in the spirit of
Anderson and
Rubin 1956
through post-processing the posterior draws
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k})
from this unidentified EFA
model. By ensuring variance identification through the algorithm of

Hosszejni and
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2022

and resolving rotational invariance
during postprocessing, we are able recover the factor dimension

r
, the idiosyncratic variances
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}
and an ordered GLT representation
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
of the loading matrix from the posterior draws. In this way, we add
a mathematically rigorous approach to a growing literature where commonly more heuristic post-processing procedures are applied for this purpose; see
(
Aßmann et al. 2016
;
Kaufmann and
Schuhmacher 2019
;
Poworoznek
et al. 2021
;
Papastamoulis and
Ntzoufras 2022
)
.

Our sampling as well as our identification strategy work under arbitrary choices regarding the slab distribution, including fractional priors
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2010
)
, the horseshoe prior
(
Zhao
et al. 2016
)
and the Lasso prior
(
Ročková
and George 2017
)
. In high-dimensional models, we work with structured priors
with column-specific shrinkage
(
Legramanti
et al. 2020
)

and employ the triple gamma prior
(
Cadonna et al. 2020
)
to achieve good separation of signal and noise.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces sparse Bayesian EFA models with UGLT structures, while prior choices are discussed in Section
3
. Section
4
introduces our MCMC sampler for this model class and Section
5
discusses post-processing MCMC draws to achieve identification.
Section
6
considers applications to exchange rate data and NYSE100 returns, while Section
7
concludes.

2
Sparse Bayesian EFA models with UGLT structures

2.1
Model definition

Throughout the paper, we work with the exploratory factor analysis (EFA) model (
1.3
),
with
k
potential common factors.
Factor analysis based on this EFA model yields the extended variance decomposition

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\Omega}}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}^{{}^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k},

(2.1)

instead of the true variance decomposition (
1.2
) and the
question arises how to
recover
(r,\boldsymbol{\Lambda},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0})
from
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k})
.
Without imposing constraints on
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
, this question is not easily answered,
due to the many identifiability issues in factor analysis.
In recent work,
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
discuss econometric identification of
(r,\boldsymbol{\Lambda},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0})

from the sparse overfitting Bayesian factor model (
1.3
), if the loading matrices are restricted to unordered generalized lower triangular (UGLT) structures.

Definition 1
.

UGLT structures.

Let
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
be an binary matrix of
r
non-zero columns. Let
l_{j}
denote
the row index (also called pivot) of the top non-zero entry in the
j
th column of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
(i.e.
{\delta}_{ij}=0,\forall\,i<l_{j}
).

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
is said to be a UGLT structure,
if the pivot elements
{\mathbf{l}}_{r}=(l_{1},\ldots,l_{r})
lie in different rows. More generally, a binary matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
with zero columns has an unordered GLT structure,
if the submatrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
containing the
r
non-zero column of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
is a UGLT structure.

To facilitate identification in sparse Bayesian factor analysis, we assume in the present paper that the factor loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
and the corresponding sparsity matrix

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
in the EFA model (
1.3
) exhibit a UGLT structure.
Compared to the common literature, where all elements of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
are left unspecified,
this imposes the constraint on
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
that the top non-zero element in the various (non-zero) columns lie in different rows.
As discussed in
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
, the (weak) UGLT constraint on
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}

is sufficient for a mathematically rigourous
identification even in an overfitting factor model, see Section
2.2
for more details.
These insights will be exploited in Section
5
,
where the posterior draws from a sparse EFA model with UGLT structure
are screened in a post-processing manner to ensure identification
of the posterior draws and to learn
about the unknown factor dimension
r
, the loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
as well as
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}
from the data.

2.2
Econometric identification in factor models

Consider first an EFA model (
1.3
) that is not overfitting, i.e.
k=r
.
A rigorous approach toward identification of factor models was first offered by
Anderson and
Rubin 1956

who consider identification as a two-step procedure. The first step is
variance identification, i.e. identification of
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}
from the variance
decomposition (
1.2
). Variance identification is easily violated for sparse Bayesian factor models, regardless whether
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
is unconstrained or exhibits a UGLT structure.

Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
prove that for UGLT structures the so-called 3579 counting rule for the elements
in the
r
non-zero columns
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
is sufficient for variance identification.
Hosszejni and
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2022
provide an efficient algorithm to verify this rule.

Definition 2
.

3579 counting rule
.
A binary matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
satisfies the 3579 counting rule, if the following condition is satisfied: for each
q=1,\ldots,r
and for each submatrix consisting of
q
column of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
, the number of nonzero rows in this sub-matrix is at least equal to
2q+1
.

The 3579 counting rule states that every column of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
should have at
least 3, every pair of non-zero columns at least 5, every subset of 3 columns at least 7 elements and so forth. If an indicator matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
obeys the 3579 counting rule, this implies that
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}={\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}
and hence,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}^{{}^{\prime}}
is identified. A necessary condition for

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
to satisfy the 3579 counting rule is the following upper bound for
r
:

\displaystyle r\leq\frac{m-1}{2}.

(2.2)

The second step of identification is solving the rotational invariance problem provided that variance identification
holds for
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r})
.
Since variance identification implies that
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{{}^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}^{{}^{\prime}}
, it follows that

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{\mathbf{P}}
for some orthogonal matrix
{\mathbf{P}}

(
Anderson and
Rubin 1956
, Lemma 5.1)
.
The rotational identification problem is usually solved by imposing a structure on the loading matrices
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
that ensures identification of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
from

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}^{{}^{\prime}}
in the sense that
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{\mathbf{P}}
iff
{\mathbf{P}}
is equal to
the identity.
A weaker condition is rotational identification up to signed permutations

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{\mathbf{P}}_{\pm}{\mathbf{P}}_{\rho}
,
where the permutation matrix
{\mathbf{P}}_{\rho}
corresponds to one of the
r
! permutations and the reflection matrix

{\mathbf{P}}_{\pm}=\mbox{\rm Diag}\,(\pm 1,\ldots,\pm 1)
corresponds
to one of the
2^{r}
ways to switch the signs of the
r
columns of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.

Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
show that imposing a UGLT structure on
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
leads to
rotational identification up to signed permutations. Provided that

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
is variance identified,

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is recovered from
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
by reordering the columns of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}

such that the pivots
l_{1}<\ldots<l_{r}
are increasing.

In applied factor analysis, the EFA model (
1.3
) is typically overfitting with
k>r
and additional identifiability issues arise. In an overfitting EFA model,
identifiability of
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}^{{}^{\prime}}
from (
2.1
) is lost and infinitely many representations
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k})

with
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}\neq{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}
exist that imply the same covariance matrix
{\mathbf{\Omega}}
as
(\boldsymbol{\Lambda},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0})

(
Geweke and
Singleton 1980
;
Tumura and
Sato 1980
)
.

Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
show that imposing a UGLT structure on
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
allows to reveal
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
from
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
even in this case.

Consider, for illustration, an overfitting EFA model with
k=r+1
.
Factor analysis typically yields loading matrices
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r+1}
with
r+1
non-zero columns rather than matrices with
r
non-zero and a single zero column.

Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
prove that by imposing a UGLT structure on
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r+1}
, the additional column in the overfitting model
is equal to a so-called
spurious factor

\boldsymbol{\Xi}
, e.g.:

\displaystyle{\small\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r+1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}&\boldsymbol{\Xi}\end{array}\right),\hskip 9.24994pt\boldsymbol{\Xi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{0}}\\
\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}\\
{\mathbf{0}}\end{array}\right),\hskip 9.24994pt{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r+1}=\mbox{\rm Diag}\,(\sigma^{2}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}-\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}^{2},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{m}),}

with a single non-zero factor loading
\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}
satisfying
0<\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}^{2}<\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}

which lies in a pivot row
l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}
different from the pivot rows
l_{1},\ldots,l_{r}
in
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.

This result allows to identify spurious columns
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
in the loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r+1}
of the overfitting EFA model (
1.3
)
and to recover
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
from the remaining (active) columns up to a signed permutation,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{\mathbf{P}}_{\pm}{\mathbf{P}}_{\rho}
. The covariance matrix

{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{0}={\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r+1}+\boldsymbol{\Xi}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{{}^{\prime}}
is identified by moving the spurious column
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
to the idiosyncratic errors.
This results also holds for a higher degree of overfitting and is the cornerstone of our MCMC sampler which relates exploratory and confirmatory Bayesian factor analysis.

2.3
Relating exploratory to confirmatory Bayesian factor analysis

The sparsity matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
in the EFA model (
1.3
) allows to identify which factors are active (the corresponding column of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
has at least two non-zero loading), which factors are spurious (the corresponding column of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
has a single non-zero loading), and which ones are inactive (the corresponding column of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
is zero).
This allows to
split the sparsity matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}

into an

m\times r
submatrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
with
r
active columns,
an

m\times r_{\footnotesize sp}
submatrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\Xi}
with
r_{\footnotesize sp}
spurious columns, and a submatrix with
j_{\footnotesize 0}=k-r-r_{\footnotesize sp}
zero columns. The loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
is split accordingly into the
m\times r
submatrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
, the

m\times r_{\footnotesize sp}
submatrix
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
, and

j_{\footnotesize 0}
zero columns, while the factors
{\mathbf{f}}_{t}^{k}
are split
into
{\mathbf{f}}_{t}^{r}
,
{\mathbf{f}}_{t}^{\Xi}
and
{\mathbf{f}}^{0}_{t}
.

Exploiting representation (
2.2
), we
extract the following model of factor dimension
r

which is embedded in any EFA model with UGLT structure,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{r}\left({\mathbf{0}},{{\mathbf{I}}}_{r}\right),\quad{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t},\quad\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{m}\left({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}\right),\quad{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}={\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\Xi}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{{}^{\prime}},

(2.8)

by moving the
r_{\footnotesize sp}
spurious columns
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
to the idiosyncratic variances
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}
. We call (
2.8
) the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) model induced
by the active columns of the sparsity matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
in the EFA model. The likelihood function is invariant
to moving from
the EFA model to the CFA model (
2.8
),
since the implied covariance matrix

{\mathbf{\Omega}}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}^{{}^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}^{{}^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}
remains the same.
On the other hand, taking the CFA model (
2.8
) as a starting point,
we can move to the EFA model (
1.3
) without changing the likelihood function
by adding
r_{\footnotesize sp}
spurious columns
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\Xi}
to
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
.
The only relevant information needed for expanding the CFA model in this way
is that
r_{\footnotesize sp}
columns outside of

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
have column-size one, whereas all remaining columns are zero.

For
r_{\footnotesize sp}=1
, for instance,
a single
spurious column
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\Xi}

is added to
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
to define an EFA model with
k-r-1
zero columns.
The position of the only non-zero indicator in column
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\Xi}
, denoted by
\delta^{\Xi}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}
,
is not identified and can lie in any row
l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}

that is different from the pivots
{\mathbf{l}}_{r}
in
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
.
A spurious column
\boldsymbol{\Xi}

is added to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
to define
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
,
while the covariance matrix of the idiosyncratic errors in the EFA model is defined as
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}={\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}-\boldsymbol{\Xi}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{{}^{\prime}}
.
The only non-zero loading

\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}
in
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
can take any value such that
the
l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}
-th diagonal element of
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}
remains positive,
i.e.
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k,l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}=\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}-(\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}})^{2}>0
. This entire move only affects
the
l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}
-th row
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},\cdot}
of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
. More specifically, for
t=1,\ldots,T
:

\displaystyle y_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},\cdot}{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}+\epsilon_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},t},\,\epsilon_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}\right),

(2.9)

\displaystyle y_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},t}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},\cdot}{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}+\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}f_{t}^{\Xi}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},t},\,\tilde{\epsilon}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,(\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}})^{\mbox{\rm\tiny new}}\right),\,(\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}})^{\mbox{\rm\tiny new}}=\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}-(\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}})^{2}.

By integrating model (
2.9
) with respect to the spurious factor
f_{t}^{\Xi}
,
it can once more be verified that both models imply the same distribution
p(y_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},t}|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r,l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},\cdot},{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t},\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}})
,
independently of the specific value of
\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}
.
Also for arbitrary
r_{\footnotesize sp}\in\{1,\ldots,k-r\}
,
neither the position of the pivots
{\mathbf{l}}_{\Xi}

of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\Xi}
(which are the only non-zero indicator in each column) nor the
non-zero elements in the corresponding spurious loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
are
identified. However, the pivots in
{\mathbf{l}}_{\Xi}
must lie in different rows.

Moving forth and back between the EFA model (
1.3
) and the CFA model (
2.8
)
as described above is the cornerstone of an efficient MCMC algorithm developed in Section
4
that operates in the space of UGLT matrices with varying dimension without imposing further constraints.
In Section
3
,
suitable priors are defined for a sparse EFA model with UGLT structure that are (largely) invariant to these moves.

3
Prior specifications

3.1
Column sparsity through exchangeable shrinkage process priors

Bayesian inference is performed in the EFA model (
1.3
)
with a finite number
k
of potential factors. Dirac-spike-and-slab prior for the factor loadings are assumed,

\displaystyle\beta_{ij}|\tau_{j}\sim(1-\tau_{j})\Delta_{0}+\tau_{j}P_{\tiny\rm slab}(\beta_{ij}),

(3.1)

where the columns of the loading matrix are increasingly pulled toward 0 as the column index increases. This cumulative shrinkage is achieved indirectly by placing an exchangeable shrinkage process (ESP) prior on the slab probabilities
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{k}
:

\displaystyle\tau_{j}|k\sim\mathcal{B}\left(a_{k},b_{k}\right),\quad j=1,\ldots,k,

(3.2)

see
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2022
)
. The ESP prior turns model (
1.3
) into
a
sparse
EFA model, where the number
r
of active columns in
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
with at least two non-zero elements is a random variable which takes values smaller than
k
with positive probability.
As recently shown by
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2022
, prior (
3.2
)
has a representation as a finite cumulative shrinkage process
(CUSP) prior
(
Legramanti
et al. 2020
)
.
Prominent examples of such ESP priors are the finite
one-parameter-beta (1PB) prior:

\displaystyle\tau_{j}|k\sim\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{\alpha}{k},1\right),\quad j=1,\ldots,k,

(3.3)

and the finite two-parameter-beta (2PB) prior,

\displaystyle\tau_{j}|k\sim\mathcal{B}\left(\gamma\frac{\alpha}{k},\gamma\right),\quad j=1,\ldots,k.

(3.4)

Alternative choices are
a_{k}=\alpha/k
and
b_{k}=\gamma(k-1)/k

(
Paisley and
Carin 2009
)
.
For
k\rightarrow\infty
,
prior (
3.3
) converges to the Indian buffet process prior
(
Teh
et al. 2007
)

and has been
employed by
Ročková
and George 2017
in sparse Bayesian factor analysis. For
k\rightarrow\infty
, prior (
3.4
) converges to the two-parameter-beta prior introduced by
Ghahramani
et al. 2007

in Bayesian nonparametric latent feature models which can be regarded as a factor model with infinitely many columns of which only a finite number is non-zero.

As opposed to this literature, we stay within the framework of
factor models with finitely many columns.
While obeying the upper bound (
2.2
),
k
is selected large enough to encourage spurious columns and zero columns in
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
.
Spurious columns are essential for our strategy of recovering the factor dimension
r
through adding and deleting spurious columns
in the overfitting EFA model (
1.3
). The hyperparameters
\alpha
and
\gamma
are instrumental in controlling prior column sparsity. A prior with
\alpha<k
and
\gamma=1
, e.g., induces sparsity, since the
largest
slab probability
\tau_{(k)}\sim\mathcal{B}\left(\alpha,1\right)
, while the smallest slab probability

\tau_{(1)}\sim\mathcal{B}\left(\alpha/k,1\right)

(
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2022
)
. To adapt the hyperparameters to the data at hand,
\alpha
is assumed to be a random with prior
\alpha\sim\mathcal{G}\left(a^{\alpha},b^{\alpha}\right)
,while
\gamma
is random with prior

\gamma\sim\mathcal{G}\left(a^{\gamma},b^{\gamma}\right)
for the 2PB-prior (
3.4
).

Imposing a UGLT structure

For given numbers
r
and
r_{\footnotesize sp}
of, respectively, active and spurious columns,
we define a prior on the pivots
{\mathbf{l}}_{r}=(l_{1},\ldots,l_{r})
and

{\mathbf{l}}_{\Xi}=(l_{{\Xi},1},\ldots,l_{{\Xi},r_{\footnotesize sp}})

such that the non-zero columns of the sparsity matrix

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
exhibit a UGLT structure.
The prior
p({\mathbf{l}}_{r})
in the CFA model
is defined as follows. Let
{\cal L}({\mathbf{l}})=\{i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}:i\notin{\mathbf{l}}\}
.
Given the pivots
{\mathbf{l}}_{r,-j}
outside of any column
j
,
condition UGLT implies that
l_{j}
has to be different from
{\mathbf{l}}_{r,-j}
,
and we assume a uniform prior distribution over all admissible pivots
l_{j}\in{\cal L}({\mathbf{l}}_{r,-j})
:

\displaystyle p(l_{j}|{\mathbf{l}}_{r,-j})=\frac{1}{|{\cal L}({\mathbf{l}}_{r,-j})|}=\frac{1}{m-r+1}.

(3.5)

The pivots

{\mathbf{l}}_{\Xi}
of the spurious columns
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\Xi}
are assigned
a uniform prior over all admissible values, given the pivots
{\mathbf{l}}_{r}
, using the following order-independent construction. Given
{\mathbf{l}}_{r}
,

l_{{\Xi},1}
is uniform over
{\cal L}({\mathbf{l}}_{r})
; given
l_{{\Xi},1}
,
l_{{\Xi},2}
is uniform over
{\cal L}({\mathbf{l}}_{r}\cup\{l_{{\Xi},1}\})
, and so forth.

Given the pivots
l_{j}
in all non-zero columns of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}
, by definition
\delta_{l_{j},j}=1
and
\delta_{ij}=0
for
i<l_{j}
,
while the
m-l_{j}
indicators
\delta_{ij}
below
l_{j}
are subject to variable selection
with column-specific probabilities
\tau_{j}
following the ESP prior (
3.2
):

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Pr}(\delta_{ij}=1|l_{j},\tau_{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&i<l_{j},\\
1,&i=l_{j},\\
\tau_{j},&i=l_{j}+1,\ldots,m.\\
\end{array}\right.

Let
d_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\delta_{ij}}
be the number of non-zero indicators in columns
j
.
With
d_{j}-1
successes and
m-l_{j}-d_{j}+1
failures in the experiment defined in (
3.1
), we obtain the following prior for column
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{r}_{\cdot,j}
:

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Pr}(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{r}_{\cdot,j}|l_{j},\tau_{j})=\tau_{j}^{d_{j}-1}(1-\tau_{j})^{m-l_{j}-d_{j}+1}.

(3.10)

If we integrate over
\tau_{j}
, then we obtain:

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Pr}(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{r}_{\cdot,j}|l_{j})=\frac{B(a_{k}+d_{j}-1,b_{k}+m-l_{j}-d_{j}+1)}{B(a_{k},b_{k})}.

(3.11)

3.2
Choosing the slab distribution

To define a prior on the loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}
,
we split
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k}

as discussed in Section
2.3

and define a prior
p(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}|{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r})
on the factor loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
in the CFA model (
2.8
), conditional on

{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}=\mbox{\rm Diag}\,(\sigma^{2}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{m})
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
.
When expanding the CFA model to an EFA model with
r_{\footnotesize sp}
columns,
we define a prior
p(\boldsymbol{\Xi}|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r},{\mathbf{l}}_{\Xi})

on the spurious loadings conditional on
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
,
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}
, and
{\mathbf{l}}_{\Xi}
.
The spurious factor loadings
are assigned a uniform prior
over all values that lead to a positive definite matrix
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}={\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}-\boldsymbol{\Xi}\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{{}^{\prime}}
in the EFA model:

\displaystyle\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}^{2}|\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}\sim\mathcal{U}\left[0,\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}\right].

(3.12)

This ensures for all
l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}\in{\mathbf{l}}_{\Xi}
that

{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k,l_{\footnotesize\rm sp},l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}=\sigma^{2}_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}-\Xi_{l_{\footnotesize\rm sp}}^{2}>0
.
By this definition,
both the likelihood and the prior are invariant to moving between the EFA and the CFA model for a given number of spurious columns
r_{\footnotesize sp}
, regardless of the chosen slab distribution.

1

1

Note that this is a major improvement compared to
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2018
.

The spike-and-slab prior (
3.1
) is formulated for the factor loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
in the CFA model.
A broad range of slab distributions has been considered for sparse Bayesian factor analysis and can be combined
with the RJMCMC sampler we introduce in Section
4
.
Since the conditional likelihood function factors into a product over the rows of the loading matrix, prior independence across the rows is assumed. For each row
i
of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
with
q_{i}=\sum_{j}\delta_{ij}>0

nonzero elements, we consider two different prior families for the vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}

of unconstrained elements in this row. First, hierarchical Gaussian priors are considered, taking the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|\sigma^{2}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}_{q_{i}}\left({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{B}}_{i0}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\sigma^{2}_{i}\right),

(3.13)

where
{\mathbf{B}}_{i0}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
is a diagonal matrix.
The variance of prior (
3.13
)
is assumed to depend on
\sigma^{2}_{i}
, because this allows
joint drawing of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
and
\sigma^{2}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{m}
and, even more importantly, sampling the sparsity matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
without conditioning on the model parameters during MCMC estimation, see Algorithm
1
.
For models with a moderate number of features
m
, the choice
{\mathbf{B}}_{i0}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}=A_{0}{{\mathbf{I}}}_{q_{i}}
with a fixed
hyperparameter
A_{0}
is common
(
Lopes and
West 2004
;
Ghosh and
Dunson 2009
;
Conti et al. 2014
)
.
For high-dimensional models with larger
m
, often a structured hierarchical prior is assumed to achieve better shrinkage of the factor loadings. One example are spike-and-slab priors with a column specific shrinkage parameter
\theta_{j}
in the slab distribution of
\beta_{ij}
,
see e.g.
Kaufmann and
Schuhmacher 2019
,
Legramanti
et al. 2020
and
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2022
:

\displaystyle\beta_{ij}|\delta_{ij}=1,\theta_{j},\sigma^{2}_{i},\kappa\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\kappa\theta_{j}\sigma^{2}_{i}\right).

(3.14)

The column specific shrinkage parameters
follow an exchangeable prior, either
an inverse gamma prior,
\theta_{j}\sim\mathcal{G}^{-1}\left(c^{\theta},b^{\theta}\right)

(
Legramanti
et al. 2020
)
,
or a triple gamma prior,

\theta_{j}\sim\mbox{\rm F}\left(2a^{\theta},2c^{\theta}\right)

(
Cadonna et al. 2020
)
. The triple gamma prior
acts as a variance selection prior and puts considerable mass on small values of

\theta_{j}
which simultaneously pulls the factors
f_{jt}
toward 0 for all time points
t
.
For
a^{\theta}=c^{\theta}=0.5
, this yields a grouped version of the horseshoe prior
(
Zhao
et al. 2016
)
.
The global shrinkage parameter follows
\kappa\sim\mathcal{G}^{-1}\left(c^{\kappa},b^{\kappa}\right)
or

\kappa\sim\mbox{\rm F}\left(2a^{\kappa},2c^{\kappa}\right)
.
Prior (
3.14
) can be extended by assuming
local shrinkage parameters
\omega_{ij}
arising from an F-distribution
for each non-zero factor loading:

\displaystyle\beta_{ij}|\delta_{ij}=1,\omega_{ij},\theta_{j},\sigma^{2}_{i},\kappa\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\kappa\theta_{j}\sigma^{2}_{i}\omega_{ij}\right),\quad\omega_{ij}\sim\mbox{\rm F}\left(2a^{\omega},2c^{\omega}\right).

(3.15)

For
a^{\omega}=c^{\omega}=0.5
, reduces to the horseshoe prior employed by
Zhao
et al. 2016
.

As an alternative shrinkage prior,
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2010
introduced a conditionally conjugate fractional prior

p(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|\sigma^{2}_{i},b,{\mathbf{f}}_{r})\propto\displaystyle p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\sigma^{2}_{i})^{b}

in the spirit of
O’Hagan 1995
. It can be interpreted as the posterior of a non-informative prior and a small fraction
b>0
the conditional likelihood is derived from regression model

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}={\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i},

(3.16)

where
\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}=(y_{i1}\cdots y_{iT})^{{}^{\prime}}
,

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}=(\epsilon_{i1}\cdots\epsilon_{iT})^{{}^{\prime}}
and
{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
is a regressor matrix constructed from the latent factors
{\mathbf{f}}_{r}=\{{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{T}\}
(see Appendix
A.1
for details).
This yields:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|\sigma^{2}_{i},b,{\mathbf{f}}_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}_{q_{i}}\left({\mathbf{b}}_{iT}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}},{\mathbf{B}}_{iT}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\sigma^{2}_{i}/b_{N}\right),

(3.17)

where
{\mathbf{b}}_{iT}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}={\mathbf{B}}_{iT}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}({\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}})^{{}^{\prime}}\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}
and
{\mathbf{B}}_{iT}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}=(({\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}})^{{}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{X}}_{i}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}})^{-1}
.
Similar fractional priors have been applied for variable selection in other latent variable models
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Tüchler 2008
;
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Wagner 2010
)
.
In the spirit of
Foster and
George 1994
,
b_{N}=1/N
, since
N=mT
observations are available to estimate
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
.
The fractional prior often works better than hierarchical Gaussian priors for moderate
m
, however is not flexible enough to handle larger dimensions
m
.

3.3
The prior on the idiosyncratic variances

Finally, we define a prior
on the idiosyncratic variances
\sigma^{2}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{m}
,
independently of all other parameters.
When estimating factor models using classical statistical methods such as ML estimation,
it frequently happens that the optimal solution lies outside the admissible parameter space with one
or more of the idiosyncratic variances
\sigma^{2}_{i}
s being negative, see e.g.

(
Jöreskog 1967
;
Bartholomew 1987
)
.
This difficulty became
known as the Heywood problem. Using a prior on the idiosyncratic variances
\sigma^{2}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{m}

within a Bayesian framework, typically chosen from the inverted Gamma family,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{i}\sim\mathcal{G}^{-1}\left(c_{0},C_{i0}\right),

(3.18)

naturally avoids negative values for
\sigma^{2}_{i}
.
Nevertheless, there exists a Bayesian analogue of the Heywood problem which takes the form of
multi-modality of the posterior of
\sigma^{2}_{i}
with one mode lying at 0.
Heywood problems typically occur, if the constraint

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{i}}\geq({\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1})_{ii}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\sigma^{2}_{i}\leq\frac{1}{({\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1})_{ii}}

(3.19)

is violated, where
{\mathbf{\Omega}}
is the covariance matrix of
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
defined in (
1.2
),
see e.g.
Bartholomew 1987
. It is clear from inequality (
3.19
) that
1/\sigma^{2}_{i}
has to be bounded away
from 0. For this reason, improper priors on the idiosyncratic variances such as
p(\sigma^{2}_{i})\propto 1/\sigma^{2}_{i}

(
Martin and
McDonald 1975
;
Akaike 1987
)
are not able to prevent Heywood problems.
Similarly, proper inverted Gamma priors with small degrees of freedom such as
c_{0}=1.1

(
Lopes and
West 2004
)
allow values too close to 0.
Hence, the hyperparameters
c_{0}
and
C_{i0}
in (
3.18
) have to be selected in such a way that Heywood problems are avoided.
In particular,
c_{0}
should be chosen large enough to
bound the prior away from 0, typically
c_{0}=2.5
.
Regarding
C_{i0}
, the most common choice is using a fixed value
C_{i0}=C_{0}
.

An alternative choice has been suggested in
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2010
.
They reduce the occurrence probability of a Heywood problem which is equal to

\mbox{\rm Pr}(X\leq C_{i0}({\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1})_{ii})
where
X\sim\mathcal{G}\left(c_{0},1\right)
through
the choice of individual scalings
C_{i0}
. The smaller
C_{i0}
, the smaller is this probability. On the other hand, a downward bias is introduced, if
C_{i0}
is too small, since

\mbox{\rm E}(\sigma^{2}_{i})=C_{i0}/(c_{0}-1)
.
Choosing
C_{i0}=(c_{0}-1)/(\widehat{{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}})_{ii}

as the largest value for which inequality
(
3.19
) is fulfilled by the prior expectation
\mbox{\rm E}(\sigma^{2}_{i})
yields the following prior:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{i}\sim\mathcal{G}^{-1}\left(c_{0},(c_{0}-1)/(\widehat{{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}})_{ii}\right),

(3.20)

based on an estimator
\widehat{{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}}
of
{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}
.
In
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2018
,
a Bayesian estimator is proposed which combines the
sample information with an inverted Wishart prior
{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}\sim\mathcal{W}_{m}\left(\nu_{o},\nu_{o}{\mathbf{S}}_{o}\right)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}}=(\nu_{o}+T/2)(\nu_{o}{\mathbf{S}}_{o}+0.5\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\mathbf{y}}_{t}{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{{}^{\prime}})^{-1}.

(3.21)

\displaystyle O^{\mbox{\rm\tiny sp}}(r,r_{\footnotesize sp})=\frac{a_{k}(m-r-r_{\footnotesize sp})}{b_{k}+m-r-r_{\footnotesize sp}-1}.

At each sweep of the sampler, a split or a merge move is performed with,

The merge move (which requires

turns one of the spurious columns

A symmetric proposal is selected for all

\displaystyle A_{\mbox{\rm\footnotesize split}}(r,r_{\footnotesize sp})=\frac{q_{\mbox{\rm\footnotesize merge}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\mbox{\rm\tiny new}})}{q_{\mbox{\rm\footnotesize split}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\mbox{\rm\tiny new}}|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k})}O^{\mbox{\rm\tiny sp}}(r,r_{\footnotesize sp})=\frac{a_{k}(m-r-r_{\footnotesize sp})(k-r-r_{\footnotesize sp})}{(r_{\footnotesize sp}+1)(b_{k}+m-r-r_{\footnotesize sp}-1)},

\displaystyle A_{\mbox{\rm\footnotesize merge}}(r,r_{\footnotesize sp})=\frac{1}{A_{\mbox{\rm\footnotesize split}}(r,r_{\footnotesize sp}-1)}=\frac{r_{\footnotesize sp}(b_{k}+m-r-r_{\footnotesize sp})}{a_{k}(m-r-r_{\footnotesize sp}+1)(k-r-r_{\footnotesize sp}+1)}.

4.3
Boosting MCMC

Step (F) and Step (P) in Algorithm
1
perform full conditional Gibbs sampling for a confirmatory factor model with sparsity matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
, by sampling the factors
{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}
conditionally on the loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}

and the idiosyncratic covariance matrix
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}
and sampling
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
and
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}

conditionally on
{\mathbf{f}}_{r}=({\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{T})
. Depending on the signal-to-noise ratio of the latent variable representation, such full conditional Gibbs sampling tends to be poorly mixing.
In a CFA model, where
{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{r}\left({\mathbf{0}},{{\mathbf{I}}}_{r}\right)
,
the information in the data (the “signal”) can be
quantified by the matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}^{{}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
in comparison to the identity matrix
{{\mathbf{I}}}_{r}
(the “noise”) in the filter for
{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}|{\mathbf{y}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}
,
see (
4.1
).
In particular for large
factor models with many measurements, one would expect that the data contain ample information to estimate the factors
{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}
. However, this is true only, if the information matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}^{{}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
increases with
m
, hence if most of the factor loadings are nonzero. For sparse factor models many columns with quite a few zero loadings are present, leading to a low signal-to-noise ratio and ,consequently, to a poorly mixing Gibbs sampler, as illustrated in the left-hand panel in Figure
2
showing posterior draws of

\mbox{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}^{{}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r})
without boosting Step (A) for the exchange data to be discussed in Section
6.1
.

Figure 2
:

without boosting (left-hand side), boosting through ASIS with the largest loading (in absolute values) in each nonzero column
serving as
\sqrt{\Psi_{j}}
(middle) and boosting through MDA based on the inverted Gamma working prior
\Psi_{j}\sim\mathcal{G}^{-1}\left(1.5,1.5\right)
(right-hand side).

Hence, boosting steps are essential to obtain efficient MCMC schemes for sparse factor models.
Several papers
(
Ghosh and
Dunson 2009
;
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2010
;
Conti et al. 2014
;
Piatek and
Papaspiliopoulos 2018
)
apply
marginal data augmentation (MDA) in the spirit of
van Dyk and
Meng 2001
; others
(
Kastner et al. 2017
;
Frühwirth-Schnatter and Lopes 2018
)
exploit
the ancillarity-suffiency interweaving strategy (ASIS) introduced by
Yu and Meng 2011
.
Some boosting strategies enhances mixing at the cost of changing the prior of the factor loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}

(
Ghosh and
Dunson 2009
)
, however, this appears undesirable in a variable selection context and is avoided by the boosting strategies applied in the present paper.

Boosting is based on moving from the CFA model (
2.8
) where
{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{r}\left({\mathbf{0}},{{\mathbf{I}}}_{r}\right)
to an expanded model
with a more general prior:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r}\tilde{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t},\quad\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{m}\left({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}\right),\qquad\tilde{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}_{r}\left({\mathbf{0}},\boldsymbol{\Psi}\right),

(4.8)

where
\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\mbox{\rm Diag}\,(\Psi_{1},\ldots,\Psi_{r})
is a diagonal matrix. The relation between the two systems is given by the transformations

\tilde{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}=(\boldsymbol{\Psi})^{1/2}{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})^{-1/2}
, where the nonzero elements in
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{r}
have the same position as the nonzero elements in
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
and the sparsity matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
is not affected by the transformation.
The main difference between MDA and ASIS lies in the choice of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
.
While
\Psi_{j}
is sampled from a working prior for MDA,
\Psi_{j}
is chosen in a deterministic fashion for ASIS,
see Appendix
G
for details.
For illustration, Figure
2
shows considerable efficiency gain in the posterior draws of
\mbox{\rm tr}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}^{{}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r})
, when a boosting strategy such as ASIS (middle panel) or MDA (right-hand panel) is applied.

For hierarchical Gaussian priors with column specific shrinkage parameters,
such as (
3.14
) and (
3.15
), we found it more useful to apply

column boosting
and interweave
\theta_{j}

into the state equation by choosing
\Psi_{j}=\theta_{j}
. Column boosting and an additional boosting step which interweaves the global
shrinkage parameter
\kappa
into the prior of the shrinkage parameter

\theta_{j}
is discussed in detail in Appendix
G
.

5
Post-processing posterior draws

Algorithm
1
delivers posterior draws
(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r},{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r})
in a CFA model with a varying number
r
of active columns.
Instead of sampling these parameters without any constraints, our sampler imposes the (mild) constraint that the pivots (the first non-zero loading in each column) lie in different rows, ensuring that posterior draws of the loading matrix exhibit a UGLT structure.
While these draws are not
identified in the rigorous sense discussed in Section
2.2
,
the UGLT structure allows identification during post-processing.

We use
the 3579 counting rule and the algorithm of
Hosszejni and
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2022
to check for each draw
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
, if the variance decomposition is unique, and remove all posterior draws that are not variance identified.
In our experience, the fraction
M_{V}
of variance identified draws is relatively high for reasonably chosen priors. A low fraction of variance identified draws can be the result of poorly chosen shrinkage priors and
is a hint to consider another prior family. The fractional prior on the loadings
\beta_{ij}
, for instance, tends to deliver a low fraction of variance identified draws in high-dimensional factor models,
see Section
6
.

Many quantities can be inferred from the variance identified posteriors draws with varying factor dimension
r
, such as the marginal covariance matrix
{\mathbf{\Omega}}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}^{T}+{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}
and the idiosyncratic variances
\sigma^{2}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{m}
appearing in the diagonal of
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}
.
In addition, overall sparsity in terms of the number
d=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{ij}
of nonzero elements in
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
,
can be evaluated.
Posterior draws of
d
are particularly useful to check convergence and assessing efficiency of the MCMC sampler, as
d
captures the ability of the sampler to move across (variance identified) UGLT factor models of different dimensions.
Functionals of
{\mathbf{\Omega}}
and
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{r}
, such as the trace, the log determinant,
and
{\mathbf{1}}_{m}^{{}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{-1}{\mathbf{1}}_{m}
are further useful means to assess MCMC convergence.
Furthermore, for each variable
y_{it}
, inference with respect to the proportion of the variance
explained by the common factors (also known as communalities
R^{2}_{i}
) is possible:

\displaystyle R^{2}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}R_{ij}^{2},\qquad R_{ij}^{2}=\frac{\beta_{ij}^{2}}{\sum_{l=1}^{r}\beta_{il}^{2}+\sigma^{2}_{i}}.

(5.1)

Most importantly, variance identified draws
are instrumental for estimating the number of factors from an overfitting factor model with
cumulative shrinkage process priors.
As shown by
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
, the number
r
of nonzero columns in
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
is equal to the factor dimension, if the variance decomposition is unique for
r
. In this way, posterior draws of

r
can be used
for variance identified factor loading matrices
to draw posterior
inference on the factor dimension
r
.
The mode
\tilde{r}
of the posterior distribution
p(r|{\mathbf{y}})
, e.g., can be used to estimate the factor dimension.

Due to the UGLT structure imposed on
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
, rotational invariance reduces for variance identified draws to sign and column switching
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
, Theorem 1)
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
and
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\Lambda}
are easily recovered.
First, the columns of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
are ordered such that the pivots
{\mathbf{l}}_{r}=(l_{1},\ldots,l_{r})

obey
l_{1}<\ldots<l_{r}
; i.e.
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}{\mathbf{P}}_{\rho}
.
Then, the sign of all columns in
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}{\mathbf{P}}_{\rho}
is switched if the leading element
is negative; i.e.
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}{\mathbf{P}}_{\rho}{\mathbf{P}}_{\pm}
.
In addition, the factors
{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}
are reordered
through
{\mathbf{P}}_{\pm}^{{}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{P}}_{\rho}^{{}^{\prime}}{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{t}
for
t=1,\ldots,T
.
Finally,
{\mathbf{P}}_{\rho}
is used to reorder the draws of
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{r}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{r})
and any local and column-specific shrinkage parameters in a hierarchical Gaussian shrinkage prior.

The posterior draws of
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\Lambda}
are exploited in various ways.
The
highest probability model (HPM), i.e. the indicator matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\Lambda}_{H}
visited most often,
its frequency
p_{H}
(an estimator of the posterior probability of the HPM),
its factor dimension
r_{H}
,
its model size
d_{H}
, and its sequence of pivots
{\mathbf{l}}^{H}
are of interest.
For each draw of
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\Lambda}
, the ordered
pivots
l_{1}<\ldots<l_{r}
are draws from the marginal
posterior distribution
p(l_{1},\ldots,l_{r}|{\mathbf{y}})
, allowing
additional posterior inference w.r.t.
{\mathbf{l}}_{r}
.
First of all, the posterior probability that a specific feature (row) serves as a
pivot, i.e.
\mbox{\rm Pr}(i\in{\mathbf{l}}_{r}|{\mathbf{y}})
for
i=1,\ldots,m
is of interest.
Second, the sequence of pivots

{\mathbf{l}}^{\star}=(l_{1}^{\star},\ldots,l_{r^{\star}}^{\star})
visited most often is determined
together with its frequency
p_{L}
which reflects posterior uncertainty with respect to
choosing the pivots. The number
r^{\star}
of elements in
{\mathbf{l}}^{\star}
provides yet another estimator of the number of factors.
If the frequencies
p_{H}
and
p_{L}
are small,
the estimator
r^{\star}
and
r_{H}
might not coincide with the posterior mode
\tilde{r}
and

{\mathbf{l}}^{H}
will different from
{\mathbf{l}}^{\star}
.

To estimate the factor loading matrix for a chosen number of factors
r
,
Bayesian model averaging is performed conditionally on an estimator
\hat{{\mathbf{l}}}_{r}
of the pivots.
Averaging over all
posterior draws
with these specific pivots avoids column switching and provides an estimate of the factor loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}

and the marginal inclusion probabilities

\mbox{\rm Pr}(\delta^{\Lambda}_{ij}=1|{\mathbf{y}},\hat{{\mathbf{l}}}_{r})
for all elements of the corresponding sparsity matrix. The median probability model (MPM)
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\Lambda}_{M}

is obtained by setting each indicator to one
whenever
\mbox{\rm Pr}(\delta_{ij}^{\Lambda}=1|{\mathbf{y}},\hat{{\mathbf{l}}}_{r})\geq 0.5
. As for any Bayesian approach, the reliability of any of these estimators might depend on how informative the data are. In settings where the data are not very informative, the chosen degree of sparsity in the prior might exhibit considerable influence on the result.

7
Concluding remarks

We have estimated (from a Bayesian viewpoint) a fairly important and highly implemented class of sparse factor models when the number of common factors is unknown. Our framework leads to a natural, efficient and simultaneous coupling of model estimation and selection on one hand and model identification and rank estimation (number of factors) on the other hand. More precisely, by combining point-mass mixture priors with overfitting sparse factor modelling, in an unordered generalised lower triangular loadings representation
(
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2022
)
, we obtain posterior summaries regarding factor loadings, common factors as well as the factor dimension
via postprocessing draws from our highly efficient and customised MCMC scheme.

The new framework is readily available for some straightforward extensions. Relatively immediate extensions are
(i) idiosyncratic errors following Student’s
t
-distributions or more general Gaussian mixtures
and (ii) dynamic sparse factor models with stationary common factors; both extensions commonly found in econometrics applications, see e.g. the recent papers by
Piatek and
Papaspiliopoulos 2018
and
Kaufmann and
Schuhmacher 2019
.
A further interesting extension would be to design a prior on the sparsity matrix that a priori distinguishes between pervasive factors that loads on (nearly) all measurements,
group specific factors that load on selected measurements
and factors that mainly capture (weak) cross-sectional heterogeneity which is not built into the basic factor model. Such approximate factor models are very popular in non-Bayesian factor analysis, see e.g.
Chamberlain and
Rothschild 1983
and
Bai and Ng 2002
and would deserve more attention from the Bayesian community. However, we leave this interesting idea for future research.

B.1
Marginal likelihoods when the factors are known

Although we work throughout this paper with a factor model where the factors

are latent, several steps of Algorithm

perform model selection with respect to
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
conditional

Hence, to sample new indicators

If

is a zero row (i.e

\displaystyle p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r})=p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i})=\frac{\Gamma(c_{T}^{n})(C_{i0})^{c_{0}}}{(2\pi)^{T/2}\Gamma(c_{0})(C_{iT}^{n})^{c_{T}^{n}}},

under

If at least one element of

3.13

3.17

Marginal likelihoods for a hierarchical Gaussian prior.

For a hierarchical Gaussian prior, a well-known exercise in Bayesian regression analysis yields:

\displaystyle p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r},{\mathbf{f}}_{r})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{T/2}}\frac{|{\mathbf{B}}_{iT}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|^{1/2}}{|{\mathbf{B}}_{i0}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|^{1/2}}\frac{\Gamma(c_{T})(C_{i0})^{c_{0}}}{\Gamma(c_{0})(C_{iT}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}})^{c_{T}}},

(B.2)

where
{\mathbf{B}}_{iT}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
,

A.5
).

Marginal likelihoods for a fractional prior.

For a fractional prior, the derivation of the marginal likelihood if at least one element of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i\cdot}
is different from zero, is less standard and can be

A fraction
b

\displaystyle p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\sigma^{2}_{i})=p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\sigma^{2}_{i})^{1-b}p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\sigma^{2}_{i})^{b}\propto p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\sigma^{2}_{i})^{1-b}p(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|\sigma^{2}_{i},b,{\mathbf{f}}_{r}).

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|\sigma^{2}_{i},b,{\mathbf{f}}_{r})=\frac{p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\sigma^{2}_{i})^{b}}{c_{i}(\sigma^{2}_{i},{\mathbf{f}}_{r},b)}.

\displaystyle c_{i}(\sigma^{2}_{i},{\mathbf{f}}_{r},b)=\int p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\sigma^{2}_{i})^{b}\,d\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}=(2\pi\sigma^{2}_{i})^{\frac{q_{i}-Tb}{2}}b^{-\frac{q_{i}}{2}}|{\mathbf{B}}_{iT}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|^{1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{b}{2\sigma^{2}_{i}}{\mbox{\rm SSR}}_{i}\right),

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\sigma^{2}_{i},b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\sigma^{2}_{i})^{1-b}p(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\sigma^{2}_{i},b)\,d\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}=\frac{1}{c_{i}(\sigma^{2}_{i},{\mathbf{f}}_{r},b)}\int p(\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{i}|{\mathbf{f}}_{r},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}},\sigma^{2}_{i})\,d\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i\cdot}^{\boldsymbol{\delta}}

\displaystyle\beta_{ij}|\delta_{ij}=1,\omega_{ij},\tilde{\theta}_{j},\sigma^{2}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\tilde{\theta}_{j}\omega_{ij}\sigma^{2}_{i}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{j}|\kappa\sim\mathcal{G}^{-1}\left(c^{\theta},\kappa b^{\theta}_{j}\right),\quad\kappa\sim\mathcal{G}^{-1}\left(c^{\kappa},b^{\kappa}\right).

The global shrinkage parameter
\kappa
is then updated in Step (A) under this representation, combining the likelihood

\displaystyle p(\tilde{\theta}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\theta}_{r}|\kappa)\propto\kappa^{rc^{\theta}}\exp\left(-\kappa\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{b^{\theta}_{j}}{\tilde{\theta}_{j}}\right),

which is the kernel of a gamma density in
\kappa
with the inverse gamma prior
p(\kappa)
. This yields a generalized inverse Gaussian distribution
12
12

12

The generalized inverse Gaussian distribution,
Y\sim\mathcal{GIG}\left(p,a,b\right)
, is a three-parameter family of probability distribution with support
y\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The density is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(y)=\frac{(a/b)^{p/2}}{2K_{p}(\sqrt{ab})}y^{p-1}e^{-(a/2)y}e^{-b/(2y)},

where
K_{p}(z)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind,
a>0
,
b>0
and
p
is a real parameter.

to update
\kappa^{\mbox{\rm\tiny new}}
:

\displaystyle\kappa^{\mbox{\rm\tiny new}}|\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{r},\kappa\sim\mathcal{GIG}\left(rc^{\theta}-c^{\kappa},\frac{2}{\kappa}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{b^{\theta}_{j}}{\theta_{j}},2b^{\kappa}\right).

Given
\kappa^{\mbox{\rm\tiny new}}
, the column specific parameters are updated as

\displaystyle\theta_{j}^{\mbox{\rm\tiny new}}=\tilde{\theta}_{j}/\kappa^{\mbox{\rm\tiny new}}=\frac{\kappa}{\kappa^{\mbox{\rm\tiny new}}}\theta_{j},\qquad j=1,\ldots,r.

Appendix H
Initialising Algorithm
1

To check the mixing of the MCMC chain, two (or more) independent runs with different initial dimensions
r
of the CFA model and a positive number
r_{\footnotesize sp}
of spurious columns are performed.
On the one hand,
r
is chosen
to be smaller than the expected number of factors,
on the other hand a large value close to
k-r_{\footnotesize sp}
is chosen.

Initial values for the leading indices
{\mathbf{l}}_{r}=(l_{1},\ldots,l_{r})

of the sparsity matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}

are chosen by first sampling
l_{1}
from
\{1,\ldots,u_{1}\}
, where
u_{1}
is a small number, e.g. 5. Then for
j=2,\ldots,r
, we sample
l_{j}
uniformly from the set
{\cal L}({\mathbf{l}}_{-j})
with
{\mathbf{l}}_{-j}=\{l_{1},\ldots,l_{j-1}\}
.
Factor loadings below the leading elements are allowed to be zero with positive probability
p_{0}
, e.g.
p_{0}=0.5
.
We draw at most 100 initial values
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
(including the leading indices) in this way, until a matrix
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
is obtained which satisfies the 3579 counting rule. If no such indicator matrix is found, then we add enough nonzero elements in each column of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
(e.g., by setting
\delta_{l_{j}+1,j}=1,\ldots,\delta_{l_{j}+3,j}=1
) to ensure variance identification for the initial value.

To obtain starting values for the factors
{\mathbf{f}}_{r}
,
we perform a few (say 100) MCMC iterations in the confirmatory factor model corresponding to
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
, which is initialized by sampling the factors
{\mathbf{f}}_{r}=({\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{f}}^{r}_{T})
from the prior:
f_{jt}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
,
j=1,\ldots,r
,
t=1,\ldots,T
.
While holding
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{r}
fixed, we iterate between sampling the model parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{r}
and
\sigma^{2}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{2}_{m}

as in Step (P) and sampling the factors
{\mathbf{f}}_{r}
as in Step (F) of Algorithm
1
.
The resulting factors
{\mathbf{f}}_{r}
serve as starting values for the full-blown MCMC scheme described in
Algorithm
1
.
