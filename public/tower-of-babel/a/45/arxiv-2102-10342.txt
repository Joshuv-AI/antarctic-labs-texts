On a notion of independence proposed by Teddy Seidenfeld
Jasper De Bock and Gert de Cooman

Abstract
Teddy Seidenfeld has been arguing for quite a long time that binary preference models are not powerful enough to deal with a number of crucial aspects of imprecision and indeterminacy in uncertain inference and decision making. It is at his insistence that we initiated our study of so-called sets of desirable option sets, which we have argued elsewhere provides an elegant and powerful approach to dealing with general, binary as well as non-binary, decision-making under uncertainty. We use this approach here to explore an interesting notion of irrelevance (and independence), first suggested by Seidenfeld in an example intended as a criticism of a number of specific decision methodologies based on (convex) binary preferences. We show that the consequences of making such an irrelevance or independence assessment are very strong, and might be used to argue for the use of so-called mixing choice functions, and E-admissibility as the resulting decision scheme.

Our results so far have led us to agree with Seidenfeld’s criticism, and to follow him in moving from binary preference models to choice functions.
But they have not led us to abandon desirability.
On the contrary: on our account, desirability is also very well suited for describing and interpreting non-binary choice.
This interpretation—that (not) choosing an option from an option set with more than two elements can be brought back to an ‘OR’ of desirability statements—inspires a set of axioms which allows us to cover much—if not all—of the literature on the subject that we have come across.

Simply moving towards general choice functions doesn’t immunise us against all aspects of Seidenfelds’s criticism, however.
For it is not merely the systematic use of binary decision schemes that he has been arguing against, it is also—and perhaps foremost—some of their features, which may also be shared by some types of non-binary decision schemes.
In fact, he has a treasure trove of intricate little examples that he likes to pick apart other people’s pet theories about rational decision making with.
We have no doubt that some of them may still be brought to bear on specific decision schemes within our desirability-based theory of choice functions.

A crash course in desirability-based choice functions

A

C
is a set-valued operator on sets of options.
In particular, for any set of options
A

We will be considering throughout the special case where these options are gambles in
\mathscr{L}(\Omega)
: bounded real-valued maps on
\Omega

5
5

5

For a more general approach to desirability-based choice functions, where options can take values in an abstract vector space, we refer the interested reader to one of our earlier papers

The option sets

Again, if it is clear from the context what the possibility space is, we will use the simpler notations

Gambles can be ordered by the point-wise ordering, where

We will also use the notation

The purpose of a choice function then, is to represent a subject’s choice between the options in

Our interpretation for rejection—and hence also choice—functions now goes as follows.
Consider a subject whose uncertainty is represented by a rejection function

Then for a given option set

The connection with the language of desirability is now almost immediate, because it is eminently suited for dealing with binary preference statements such as “the gamble

In terms of desirability, this simply means that

By applying this to our interpretation for rejection in terms of preferences, we obtain an equivalent interpretation in terms of desirability:

A\ominus f\coloneqq\{g-f\colon g\in A\setminus\{f\}\}

So we find that under our interpretation, the study of choice and rejection functions reduces to the study of sets of gambles that contain at least one desirable gamble.

2.1
Choice functions based on linear previsions

Perhaps the best-known method for choosing between uncertain rewards, is to choose those options that have the highest expected utility with respect to some given probability measure.
This measure is often taken to be countably additive, but we will not impose this restriction here and work with finitely additive probability measures instead, defined on all events

The expectation operators that correspond to such measures are linear previsions.

2.2
Choice functions based on lower previsions

While intuitive and straightforward, E-admissibility is not the only possible generalisation of expectation maximisation.

By comparing this expression with Equation (

That maximality is also a special case of our desirability-based theory of choice functions can be seen by defining the following set of desirable option sets

(8)

It is easily verified to be coherent [this follows from

Since sets of desirable option sets of this kind are coherent, so are their intersections.
With any non-empty set

of coherent lower previsions, we can therefore associate a coherent set of desirable option sets

\displaystyle C_{\mathscr{P}}(A)\coloneqq

Definition 4

2.3
An axiomatic basis for working with linear and lower previsions

In the remainder of this contribution, we will focus on Archimedean sets of desirable option sets and their corresponding choice functions, either in their full generality or in particular cases.

11

Lemma 2

Consider any event
F\subseteq\Omega

\underline{P}

\smash{\underline{P}(F)=\overline{P}(F)}

To state this factorisation condition, we adopt

Theorem 3.3

In combination with Proposition

, in terms of their representing lower previsions.
In particular, we see that in the absence of triviality, S-independence implies precision and factorisation for (products of) gambles on

,
without the need for imposing mixingness
.
In the remainder of this section, we seek to exclude the trivial cases by considering a new potential property for events with respect to a coherent set of desirable option sets
K
.

We start by introducing the notion of credibility: we say that
E
is
credible
with respect to a coherent set of desirable option sets
K
whenever

(\exists\epsilon>0)\{\mathbb{I}_{E}-\epsilon\}\in K,

meaning that our subject is willing to bet on
E
at some positive—but possibly very small—betting rate
\epsilon
.
For an Archimedean
K
, this is equivalent to the lower probability of
E
being strictly bounded below by
\epsilon
for all representing lower previsions.

Proposition 10

If the set of desirable option sets
K
is Archimedean, an event
E\subseteq\Omega

such that
\underline{P}(E)>\epsilon
for all
\underline{P}\in\underline{\mathbf{P}}(K)
.

Proof

Consider any
\epsilon>0
.
Then for any
\underline{P}\in\underline{\mathbf{P}}(K)

\underline{P}(\mathbb{I}_{E}-\epsilon)>0

6

\underline{P}(\mathbb{I}_{E})>\epsilon

The result is therefore an immediate consequence of the fact that

We now say that an event
E
is

with respect to a coherent set of desirable option sets

if

are both credible with respect to

This condition of credible indeterminacy, when combined with S-independence, allows us to infer both precision and factorisation for every representing lower prevision of an Archimedean set of desirable option sets

Due to Proposition

For any
\underline{P}\in\underline{\mathbf{P}}(K)
, it then follows from Proposition
10
that there is some
\epsilon>0
such that
\underline{P}(\tilde{E})>\epsilon
.
Hence, since
\smash{\overline{P}(\tilde{E})\geq\underline{P}(\tilde{E})>\epsilon>0}

\underline{P}
.

4
S-Irrelevance for variables

Let us now extend the discussion from events to variables.
We still assume that our subject’s preferences are modelled by a set of desirable option sets
K
, where the possible options are the gambles on the possibility space
\Omega
.

We will follow the often used device of representing a variable
Z
as a map that is defined on the possibility space
\Omega
:
12
12

12

Although it is related to what we call a variable in spirit, we will refrain from using the term ‘random variable’, as that is typically associated with precise and countable additive probability models, and typically comes with a measurability requirement.

Z\colon\Omega\to\mathscr{Z}\colon\omega\mapsto Z(\omega),

where we denote by
\mathscr{Z}
the set of possible values of the variable
Z
.
The idea behind this device is that since our subject is uncertain about the value that
\omega
assumes in
\Omega
, she will typically also be uncertain about the value assumed by
Z(\omega)
in
\mathscr{Z}
.

If we want to talk about decisions involving the value of the variable
Z
, we need to consider uncertain rewards whose value depends only on the value of
Z
, or more specifically, gambles on
\Omega
of the type

h(Z)\coloneqq h\circ Z\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\colon\omega\mapsto h(Z(\omega)),

where
h
is any gamble on
\mathscr{Z}
.

In particular, with any
E\subseteq\mathscr{Z}
, we can associate the indicator (gamble)
\mathbb{I}_{E}
on
\mathscr{Z}
, which corresponds to a gamble
\mathbb{I}_{E}(Z)
on
\Omega
.
Since
\mathbb{I}_{E}(Z)=\mathbb{I}_{E}\circ Z=\mathbb{I}_{Z^{-1}(E)}
, we see that
\mathbb{I}_{E}(Z)
is an indicator on
\Omega
. The event
\smash{Z^{-1}(E)\subseteq\Omega}
that it indicates, corresponds to the proposition ‘
Z\in E
’.

4.1
Defining S-irrelevance for variables

To see what an assessment of S-irrelevance might mean for variables, we consider two variables
X
and
Y
, which we model as maps on the possibility space
\Omega
:

X\colon\Omega\to\mathscr{X}\text{ and }Y\colon\Omega\to\mathscr{Y},

where the respective non-empty sets
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{Y}
are the sets of possible values for
X
and
Y
.
Seidenfeld’s ‘independence’ requirement can then be extended straightforwardly from events to variables as follows:

When two variables,
X
and
Y
, are ‘independent’ then it is not reasonable to
spend resources
in order to use the observed value of one of them, say
X
, to choose between options that depend solely on the value of the other variable,
Y
.

As before for events, we recognise the essentially asymmetrical nature of this requirement, and will try to formulate a requirement of S-irrelevance of
X
to
Y
.
Because we care about the operational meaning of our criterion, we will allow the variables
X
and
Y
to assume infinitely many values, but want to keep our observations of the values of
X
and the choices between gambles on
Y
finitary.
Consequently, observing the value of
X
will be modelled by choosing a
finite
partition
\mathcal{P}
of the set
\mathscr{X}
, and finding out which event
E\in\mathcal{P}
in that partition obtains, meaning that
X\in E
.
For each such possible observation
E
, we consider a gamble
s_{E}\colon\mathscr{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
on the value of
Y
, which we will assume to be
finite-valued
---simple---in accordance with our finitary approach.
13
13

13

An engaged reader will be able to verify further on that, despite our insistence on a finitary approach, this doesn’t really matter from a mathematical point of view. In particular, none of our proofs—even the ones that establish
sufficient
conditions for S-irrelevance or S-independence for variables—will actually require the restriction that the gambles
s_{E}
—or
s_{G}
—should be simple.

Let us denote by
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{Y}}
the set of all simple gambles on
\mathscr{Y}
.

In summary, our subject needs to choose between the gambles
s_{E}
,
E\in\mathcal{P}
on the value of
Y
, or in other words, between the gambles
s_{E}(Y)\coloneqq s_{E}\circ Y
on the possibility space
\Omega
.
Observing the value of
X
to choose between these gambles then corresponds to the composite gamble
\sum_{G\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{I}_{G}(X)s_{G}(Y)
on the possibility space
\Omega
.
The purport of Seidenfeld’s requirement is that when our subject judges
X
to be irrelevant to
Y
, she should reject the composite option
\sum_{G\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{I}_{G}(X)s_{G}(Y)-\epsilon
from the set of options

\{s_{E}(Y)\colon E\in\mathcal{P}\}\cup\bigg\{\sum_{G\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{I}_{G}(X)s_{G}(Y)-\epsilon\bigg\},

for all real
\epsilon>0
.
Following the discussion in Sections
2
and
3
, and Equation (
1
) in particular, this means that the option set

\bigg\{s_{E}(Y)-\sum_{G\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{I}_{G}(X)s_{G}(Y)+\epsilon\colon E\in\mathcal{P}\bigg\}\\
\begin{aligned} &=\bigg\{\sum_{G\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{I}_{G}(X)s_{E}(Y)-\sum_{G\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{I}_{G}(X)s_{G}(Y)+\epsilon\colon E\in\mathcal{P}\bigg\}\\
&=\bigg\{\sum_{G\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\{E\}}\mathbb{I}_{G}(X)[s_{E}(Y)-s_{G}(Y)]+\epsilon\colon E\in\mathcal{P}\bigg\}\end{aligned}

must be desirable for our subject.
This leads to the following definitions.

Definition 9
(S-irrelevance and S-independence for variables)

Consider two variables
X
and
Y
and a coherent set of desirable option sets
K
.
We say that
X
is
S-irrelevant
to
Y
with respect to
K
if

\bigg\{\sum_{G\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\{E\}}\mathbb{I}_{G}(X)[s_{E}(Y)-s_{G}(Y)]+\epsilon\colon E\in\mathcal{P}\bigg\}\in K\\
\text{ for all finite partitions $\mathcal{P}$ of\penalty\ $\mathscr{X}$, all $s_{E}\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{Y}}$ and all $\epsilon>0$}.

(16)

We say that
X
and
Y
are
S-independent
with respect to
K
if
X
is S-irrelevant to
Y
and
Y
is S-irrelevant to
X
.

Interestingly, the assessments for variables imply similar assessments for events.

Proposition 11

Consider two variables
X
and
Y
and a coherent set of desirable option sets
K
.
If

is S-irrelevant to
Y
with respect to

E\subseteq\mathscr{X}
and
F\subseteq\mathscr{Y}

X^{-1}(E)
is S-irrelevant to the event
Y^{-1}(F)
with respect to

.

X
and
Y
are S-independent with respect to

and
F\subseteq\mathscr{Y}

X^{-1}(E)
and

.

Proof

It clearly suffices to give the proof for S-irrelevance.
So consider any
E\subseteq\mathscr{X}
and
F\subseteq\mathscr{Y}
and any gamble
\smash{f\in\mathscr{L}_{Y^{-1}(F)}(\Omega)}
.
Then
f
is completely characterised by the real values
f_{F}
and
f_{F^{\mathrm{c}}}
it assumes on
Y^{-1}(F)
and
(Y^{-1}(F))^{\mathrm{c}}=Y^{-1}(F^{\mathrm{c}})
, respectively:
f=f_{F}\mathbb{I}_{Y^{-1}(F)}+f_{F^{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbb{I}_{Y^{-1}(F^{\mathrm{c}})}

If we define the simple gamble
g\coloneqq f_{F}\mathbb{I}_{F}+f_{F^{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbb{I}_{F^{\mathrm{c}}}\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{Y}}

We now consider the partition

\mathscr{X}

and
s_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}\coloneqq 0
on
\mathscr{Y}
.
Then the S-irrelevance of

to
Y
implies in particular that for all

K\ni\big\{\mathbb{I}_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}(X)[s_{E}(Y)-s_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}(Y)]+\epsilon,\mathbb{I}_{E}(X)[s_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}(Y)-s_{E}(Y)]+\epsilon\big\}\\

is S-irrelevant to

with respect to
K
.

Consider two variables

4.2
S-irrelevance for variables with respect to lower prevision models

Because of Proposition

As a first step, we recall from Proposition

To get our feet wet, we look at Proposition

Definition 10
(Independent variables with respect to a linear prevision)

We call two variables

and

Its characterisation in terms of gambles on

Proposition 13

For any linear prevision

X
and
Y
are independent with respect to

P(f\/(X)g(Y))=P(f(X))P(g(Y))\text{ for all gambles $f$ on\penalty\ $\mathscr{X}$ and all gambles $g$ on\penalty\ $\mathscr{Y}$}.

Proof

The sufficiency part of the proof is immediate: it suffices to let
f\coloneqq\mathbb{I}_{E}
and
g\coloneqq\mathbb{I}_{F}
.
For the necessity part, we assume that
X
and
Y
are independent with respect to
P
and consider any gamble
f
on
\mathscr{X}
and
g
on
\mathscr{Y}

We need to prove that
P(f(X)g(Y))=P(f(X))P(g(Y))

It suffices to give the proof for simple—finite valued—gambles
f
and
g

LP
7

].
We may therefore assume that
f=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{X}}}f_{E}\mathbb{I}_{E}
for some partition
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{X}}
of
\mathscr{X}
and some choice of the real numbers
f_{E}
.
Similarly, we may assume that
g=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{Y}}}g_{F}\mathbb{I}_{F}
for some partition
\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{X}}
of
\mathscr{Y}
and some choice of the real numbers
g_{F}

But then

\displaystyle P(f(X)g(Y))

\displaystyle=P\bigg(\sum_{E\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{X}}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{Y}}}f_{E}g_{F}\mathbb{I}_{E}(X)\mathbb{I}_{F}(Y)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{X}}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{Y}}}f_{E}g_{F}P(\mathbb{I}_{E}(X)\mathbb{I}_{F}(Y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{X}}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{Y}}}f_{E}g_{F}P(\mathbb{I}_{E}(X))P(\mathbb{I}_{F}(Y))

\displaystyle=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{X}}}f_{E}P(\mathbb{I}_{E}(X))\sum_{F\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{Y}}}g_{F}P(\mathbb{I}_{F}(Y))

\displaystyle=P\bigg(\sum_{E\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{X}}}f_{E}\mathbb{I}_{E}(X)\bigg)P\bigg(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathscr{Y}}}g_{F}\mathbb{I}_{F}(Y)\bigg)=P(f(X))P(g(Y)).

where the second and fifth equality follow from coherence [use

P
2

and

P

], and the crucial third equality follows from the assumption that
X
and

are independent with respect to
P
.

To extend Proposition

We begin with precision.

Since a coherent lower prevision is uniformly continuous [see

s=\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}\mathbb{I}_{E_{{k}}}

\displaystyle\underline{P}(s\circ Z)

5
The far-reaching implications of S-irrelevance and S-independence

After the detailed mathematical analysis of the previous sections, let us now take a moment to consider what these mathematical results imply, and how far-reaching we believe these implications to be.
In doing so, we will also lay the foundations for talking about inferences and decisions involving variables and non-binary choice models.

If our subject has a choice model
K
for choosing between gambles on
\Omega

K_{Z}
for choosing between gambles on the value of a variable
Z\colon\Omega\to\mathscr{Z}

We will use the following (notational) device: for any option set

Z
, which are, of course, gambles whose domain is the possibility space
\Omega

Then clearly,

K_{Z}\coloneqq\{C\in\mathscr{Q}(\mathscr{Z})\colon C(Z)\in K\}

\mathscr{Z}
that represents the choices between gambles that depend on the variable

It is completely in the spirit of the existing terminology in standard probability theory to call this choice model

of the variable

assumes in
\mathscr{Z}

and
Y
are (both) credibly indeterminate and S-independent—then the same is true for the marginals of
X
.

What is perhaps the most striking about Theorem
4.4
, however, are its implications for the choice model
K_{Y}
.
Since
K
is Archimedean, we know from Proposition
18
that
K_{Y}
is Archimedean as well.
What is very surprising, though, is that our subject’s assessments—that
X
is credibly indeterminate and S-irrelevant to
Y
—imply that it must be
mixing
as well.

Corollary 1

Suppose that a variable
X
is credibly indeterminate and S-irrelevant to a variable
Y
with respect to an Archimedean set of desirable option sets
K
.
Then the distribution
K_{Y}
of
Y
is an Archimedean and mixing set of desirable option sets.

Proof

For any
B\in\mathscr{Q}(\mathscr{Y})
, we have that

\displaystyle B\in K_{Y}\Leftrightarrow(\forall\underline{P}\in\underline{\mathbf{P}}(K))B\in K_{\underline{P}_{Y}}\Leftrightarrow(\forall\underline{P}\in\underline{\mathbf{P}}(K))B\in K_{P_{Y}},

where the first equivalence follows from Proposition
18
, and the second from Theorem
4.4
.
Since we know from Theorem
2.1
that
\underline{\mathbf{P}}(K)
is non-empty, it therefore follows from Theorem
2.2
that
K_{Y}
is Archimedean and mixing.

If the Archimedean set of desirable option sets
K
is mixing, then the mixingness of
K_{Y}
follows easily from the fact that mixingness is preserved under marginalisation; see for example Proposition
18
.
The striking thing about Corollary
1
is that it doesn’t require
K
to be mixing: we obtain the mixingness of
K_{Y}
using only credible indeterminacy and S-irrelevance.

Since mixing sets of desirable options sets correspond to choice functions governed by E-admissibility, the implications of this result are far-reaching: we find that choices between gambles that depend only on
Y
will be governed by E-admissibility with respect to a set of linear previsions, for example
\mathbf{P}(K_{Y})
.
This is a very curious and amazingly strong result.
As soon as a subject assumes that there is
some
credibly indeterminate variable
X
that is S-irrelevant to a variable
Y
, which seems a very weak assumption to make, she is forced by coherence—and Archimedeanity—to use a
mixing
model for
Y
, and to use E-admissibility as her decision scheme for choosing between gambles on
Y
.
To give a simple example, we believe that our flipping a coin here in Ghent today will not affect in any way whether Teddy will have pickled herring for breakfast tomorrow morning.
As soon as we translate this belief into an assessment that the outcome of our coin flip today is credibly indeterminate—which seems uncontroversial for a coin flip—and
S-irrelevant
to Teddy’s choice of breakfast tomorrow, we are forced by coherence—and Archimedeanity—to use a mixing model for our uncertainty about Teddy’s breakfast choice.

It would seem, then, that our mathematical derivations in this paper lead to an argument in favour of using mixing models and decision schemes based on E-admissibility.
It is indeed very easy to imagine that there are experiments whose outcomes—variables
X
—are indeterminate and have nothing whatsoever to do with the outcome—variable
Y
—of the experiment that we are currently considering.
As soon as we translate this ‘being indeterminate and having nothing whatsoever to do with’ by an assessment of credible indeterminacy and S-irrelevance, we are led to using mixing models and E-admissibility only.

We don’t want to take this discussion too far, but still feel inclined to suggest that, perhaps, it is the translation that constitutes the Achilles’ heel of this argument.
Going back to binary variables, or events, in the interest of simplicity, isn’t requiring that the composite gamble
\mathbb{I}_{E}f+\mathbb{I}_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}g-\epsilon
must
always
be
rejected
from the set
\{f,g,\mathbb{I}_{E}f+\mathbb{I}_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}g-\epsilon\}
for
all

f,g\in\mathscr{L}_{F}
too strong if we want to express that ‘whether the event
E
obtains has nothing whatsoever to do with whether
F
obtains’?
At least one of us isn’t entirely convinced of the validity of this requirement.
Even if our subject believes that the event
E
has no effect on the event
F
, why should she then reject the gamble
\mathbb{I}_{E}f+\mathbb{I}_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}g-\epsilon
from the set
\{f,g,\mathbb{I}_{E}f+\mathbb{I}_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}g-\epsilon\}
?
Or equivalently, why should she then necessarily find
\mathbb{I}_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}(f-g)+\epsilon
or
\mathbb{I}_{E}(g-f)+\epsilon
desirable?
For example, if
f
and
g
are deemed incomparable by our subject, meaning that
f-g
nor
g-f
are desirable, what would then compel her to find
\mathbb{I}_{E}f+\mathbb{I}_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}g-\epsilon
comparable to—even dominated by—
f
or
g
. Or to rephrase it one more time: if
f-g
nor
g-f
are deemed desirable, why then should
\mathbb{I}_{E^{\mathrm{c}}}(f-g)+\epsilon
or
\mathbb{I}_{E}(g-f)+\epsilon
be desirable?
We definitely think that these and related questions merit further attention.
