Balanced Allocations in Batches: Simplified and Generalized
Dimitrios Los, Thomas Sauerwald

Abstract
We consider the allocation of m m balls (jobs) into n n bins (servers). In the Two-Choice process, for each of m m sequentially arriving balls, two randomly chosen bins are sampled and the ball is placed in the least loaded bin. It is well-known that the maximum load is m / n + log 2 ⁡ log ⁡ n + 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) m/n+\log_{2}\log n+\mathcal{O}(1) with high probability. Berenbrink, Czumaj, Englert, Friedetzky and Nagel [ 7 ] introduced a parallel version of this process, where m m balls arrive in consecutive batches of size b = n b=n each. Balls within the same batch are allocated in parallel, using the load information of the bins at the beginning of the batch. They proved that the gap of this process is 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(\log n) with high probability. In this work, we present a new analysis of this setting, which is based on exponential potential functions. This allows us to both simplify and generalize the analysis of [ 7 ] in different ways: 1. Our analysis covers a broad class of processes. This includes not only Two-Choice , but also processes with fewer bin samples like ( 1 + β ) (1+\beta) , processes which can only receive one bit of information from each bin sample and graphical allocation, where bins correspond to vertices in a graph. 2. Balls may be of different weights, as long as their weights are independent samples from a distribution satisfying a technical condition on its moment generating function. 3. For arbitrary batch sizes b ⩾ n b\geqslant n , we prove a gap of 𝒪 ⁡ ( b / n ⋅ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(b/n\cdot\log n) . For any b ∈ [ n , n 3 ] b\in[n,n^{3}] , we improve this to 𝒪 ⁡ ( b / n + log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(b/n+\log n) and show that it is tight for a family of processes. This implies the unexpected result that for e.g. ( 1 + β ) (1+\beta) with constant β ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \beta\in(0,1] , the gap is Θ ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Theta(\log n) for all b ∈ [ n , n ​ log ⁡ n ] b\in[n,n\log n] . We also conduct experiments which support our theoretical results, and even hint at a superiority of less powerful processes like ( 1 + β ) (1+\beta) for large batch sizes.

Introduction

Motivation.
We study the classical problem of allocating
m
balls (jobs) into
n
bins (servers). This framework also known as balls-into-bins or balanced allocations
[
4
]
is a popular abstraction for various resource allocation and storage problems such as load balancing, scheduling or hashing (see surveys
[
27
,
35
]
). Following a long line of previous works, we consider randomized allocation schemes where for each ball we take a certain number of bin samples and then allocate the ball into one of these samples.

For the simplest allocation scheme, called
One-Choice
, each
of the
m
balls is placed in a random bin chosen independently and uniformly. It is well-known that the maximum load is
\Theta(\log n/\log\log n)
w.h.p.

1-n^{-c}

c>0
.
for
m=n
, and
m/n+\Theta(\sqrt{(m/n)\log n})
w.h.p. for
m\gg n
. While this allocation scheme can be of course executed completely
in parallel
, it results in a significantly large gap between the maximum load and average load if
m
gets large.

Azar, Broder, Karlin and Upfal
[
4
]
(and implicitly Karp, Luby and Meyer auf der Heide
[
19
]
) proved that if the
m
balls are allocated
sequentially
, and each ball is placed in the lesser loaded of
d\geqslant 2
randomly chosen bins, then the maximum load drops to
\log_{d}\log n+\mathcal{O}(1)
w.h.p., if
m=n
. This dramatic improvement from
d=1
(
One-Choice
) to
d=2
(
Two-Choice
) is known as “power of two choices”, and similar effects have been observed in other problems including routing, hashing and randomized rounding
[
27
]
. Vöcking
[
33
]
proved that further improvements on the gap bound (which are more significant for larger
d
) are possible if one employs an asymmetric tie-breaking rule.

Berenbrink, Czumaj, Steger and Vöcking
[
8
]
extended the analysis of
[
4
]
(and
[
33
]
) to the so-called
heavily loaded case
, where
m\geqslant n
can be arbitrarily large. In particular, for
Two-Choice
an upper bound on the gap (the difference between the maximum and average load) of
\log_{2}\log n+\mathcal{O}(1)
w.h.p. was shown. A simpler but slightly weaker analysis was later given by Talwar and Wieder
[
32
]
.

The above studies for
d
-Choice
, as well as many other works in balanced allocations, usually make the following assumptions:

1.)

All
m
balls have to be allocated sequentially, with the load information being updated immediately.

2.)

All
m
balls are of the same weight.

3.)

All
m
balls need to take
d\geqslant 2
independent and uniform bin samples.

While these assumptions are crucial in many of the mathematical analyses, they may be difficult to satisfy in practical applications. For example, in a highly parallel environment, the load information of a bin may not include the most recent allocations. Further, processing times of jobs (size of data items) may not be identical but rather follow a heterogeneous distribution, which leads to the so-called
weighted balls-into-bins
setting. Finally,
Two-Choice
(and
d
-Choice
) assume that balls are able to sample two (or
d
) bins which are chosen independently and uniformly at random, in every round. It is natural to consider scenarios where the
d
samples are correlated (e.g., through a network structure), or for some balls only one sample is available.

Related Work.
In order to relax assumption
1.)
, Berenbrink, Czumaj, Englert, Friedetzky and Nagel
[
7
]
proposed a model where balls are allocated in consecutive batches of size
b
. Each ball in a batch is allocated using
Two-Choice
, but based on the load values of the bins prior to the batch. This means, that the decisions among the balls within the same batch do not depend on each other in any way and can therefore be executed in parallel. In
[
7
]
, it was shown that for
b=n
the gap is
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
w.h.p.

A related setting with ball removals was studied in Berenbrink, Friedetzky, Kling, Mallmann-Trenn, Nagel and Wastell
[
10
]
, where at each batch
\lambda\cdot n
arriving balls are allocated in parallel, and every non-empty bin removes one ball. The authors prove an upper bound of
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log n}{1-\lambda})
on the gap, when balls are allocated using
Two-Choice
. As mentioned in
[
10
, Lemma 3.5]
, their analysis can be modified to re-derive the main result in
[
7
]
for the batched setting, using a simpler proof.

In
[
26
]
, Mitzenmacher studied a similar model to the batched setting called
bulletin board model with periodic updates
. However, this model assumes stochastic arrivals and removals of balls, and the paper also does not provide any rigorous and quantitative bounds on the gap. On a high level, this work, as well as a study by Dahlin
[
12
]
raise the general question on how useful old load information is, and both works suggest that using a “less aggressive” strategy than
d
-Choice
may lead to better performance in practice.

Other parallel allocation schemes which are typically based on a small number of communication rounds between bins and balls were studied in
[
1
,
23
,
22
]
. For example, Lenzen and Wattenhofer
[
23
]
proved that, for
m=n
, a maximum load of
2
is possible using only
\log^{*}n+\mathcal{O}(1)
communication rounds. Recently,
[
22
]
also extended this direction to the heavily loaded case
m\geqslant n
, and proved that a maximum load of
m/n+\mathcal{O}(1)
is possible using
\mathcal{O}(\log\log(m/n)+\log^{*}n)
rounds. While the gap bounds in
[
23
,
22
]
are stronger than in our setting, they require more communication and coordination.

Assumption 2.) that balls are unweighted is made in the vast majority of theoretical works in balanced allocations. One exception is the work of Talwar and Wieder
[
31
]
, who analyzed a wide class of weight distributions satisfying some mild conditions on its second and fourth moment. They proved that the gap remains independent of
m
, even though heavier and heavier weights may be encountered if
m
gets large. For arbitrary weight distributions, results of
[
9
]
demonstrate that this setting is considerably harder than the unweighted setting, as many couplings and majorization results no longer hold.

Concerning assumption 3.) on how bins are sampled, many allocation schemes with fewer or correlated bin samples have been analyzed. One key example is the
(1+\beta)
-process introduced by Peres, Wieder and Talwar
[
30
]
, where each ball is allocated using
One-Choice
with probability
1-\beta
and otherwise is allocated using
Two-Choice
. The authors proved that for any
\beta\in(0,1]
, the gap is only
\mathcal{O}(\log n/\beta+\log(1/\beta)/\beta)
for any
m\geqslant n
. Hence, only a “small” fraction of
Two-Choice
rounds are enough to inherit the property of
Two-Choice
that the gap is independent of
m
. This result also applies to weighted balls into bins for a large class of weight distributions.

A nice application of the
(1+\beta)
-process is in the analysis of the so-called
graphical balanced allocations

[
30
]
. In this model, first studied in
[
20
]
for
m=n
, we are given a graph where each bin corresponds to a vertex. For each ball, we pick a uniform edge in
G
and place the ball in the lesser loaded endpoint of the edge. A reduction to the
(1+\beta)
-process implies that, if
G
is a regular expander graph, then for any
m\geqslant n
the gap is
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
w.h.p. Very recently, Bansal and Feldheim
[
5
]
presented a more elaborate protocol based on multi-commodity-flows that achieves a poly-logarithmic gap for any bounded-degree regular graph. A natural extension of the graphical process was also studied for hypergraphs, see, e.g.,
[
16
,
17
]
. Other applications of the
(1+\beta)
-process in parallel computing include population protocols
[
3
]
and distributed data structures
[
2
,
29
]
.

Related to
(1+\beta)
process is the two-
Thinning
process
[
15
,
18
,
13
]
with some load threshold
\ell
which works in a two-stage procedure: First, take a uniform bin sample
i
. Secondly, if the load of bin
i
is at most
\ell
then allocate a ball into
i
, otherwise place a ball into another bin sample
j
(without comparing its load with
i
). This process has received some attention recently, and several variations were studied in
[
15
]
for
m=n
and
[
14
,
24

25

.

investigated a variant of

called

, which uses

(1-\delta)\cdot n
lightest, for some quantile

, and otherwise the ball is allocated into a second bin sample. For both

, extensions exist which use more than two bin samples, and correspondingly, stronger gap bounds can be shown
[

24

.

An even stronger class of adaptive sampling schemes was analyzed by Czumaj and Stemann
[

]
, where unlike

Another model relaxing the

assumption was introduced in Wieder
[

, where the minimum and maximum probability for

. Wieder

proved some tight trade-off between

Our Results.

In this work we revisit the
batched model
from
[
7
]
, which allocates balls in batches of size
b
, but here we allow any value of
b\geqslant n
. Additionally, we consider a wider range of allocation processes, including not only
Two-Choice
, but also
(1+\beta)
or
Quantile
. This relaxes the requirement of
Two-Choice
of always taking two uniform bin samples at each round and allocating into the less loaded of the two (given the available load information).

Our results hold for any process satisfying two natural conditions:
(i)
there is a suitable bias away from allocating into the heavily loaded bins and
(ii)
no single bin experiences are a “too large” bias. The second condition may seem a bit counter-intuitive at first, but it is crucial in the batched setting to prevent a lightly loaded bin from receiving too many allocations within the same batch. The precise definition of these conditions is given in Section
2.2
.

Furthermore, our results are valid for the same class of weight distributions as considered in
[
30
]
, which includes, for example, the geometric and the exponential distributions.

Our first result is that for any batch size
b\geqslant n
, after allocating any number of balls
m\geqslant n
, a gap bound of
\mathcal{O}(b/n\cdot\log n)
holds w.h.p. For
b=n
, this matches the result of
[
7
]
for the
Two-Choice
process in the unweighted setting.
Unlike the analysis in
[
7
]
, which relies on some sophisticated Markov chain tools from
[
8
]
to prove a “short memory behaviour”, the derivation of this gap bound
\mathcal{O}(b/n\cdot\log n)
is based on a hyperbolic cosine potential function (a version of two exponential potential functions), and thus we believe it to be more elementary and self-contained. On a high level, this analysis shares some of the ideas from
[
10
,
30
]
which both uses similar versions of exponential functions, but it seems difficult to apply these existing approaches directly to the general setting with weighted balls and any
b\geqslant n
.

We then proceed to a tighter bound and prove that for any
n\log n\leqslant b\leqslant n^{3}
and any number of balls
m\geqslant n
, the gap is
\mathcal{O}(b/n+\log n)
w.h.p. This bound is derived through an interplay between different potential functions, in particular, we relate three hyperbolic cosine potential functions with different smoothing parameters.

Next we turn to proving asymptotically tight lower bounds. We prove that for any
b\geqslant n
, there are processes falling into our framework that produce for certain values of
m
a gap of
\Omega(b/n+\log n)
. These lower bounds are proven in the unweighted setting where all balls have weight one. For the
Two-Choice
process, the lower bounds are tight for
b\geqslant n\log n
.

Combining our upper with lower bounds reveals an interesting behavior: For any
b\in[n,\mathcal{O}(n\log n)]
, the gap is
\Theta(\log n)
w.h.p., whereas for
b\geqslant n\log n
, the gap is
\Theta(b/n)
w.h.p. In particular, the asymptotic gap bound does not change as
b
moves from
n
to
n\log n
.

We further demonstrate the flexibility of our techniques by deriving results for the
graphical allocation model
from
[
30
]
, where bins are arranged as a graph and at each round a pair of bins is sampled by picking a random edge from the graph. One open question in
[
30
, Section 4]
was to derive results for graphical allocation with weights. In this work, we make progress towards that question by proving gap bounds that hold not only for weighted balls but also in the batched setting. For example, if the graph is a bounded-degree expander, then we recover the gap bound of
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
from
[
30
]
even if balls are weighted and are allocated in batches up to a size of
n\log n
.
Finally, another consequence of our approach is a tight
\mathcal{O}(\log n/\beta)
upper bound for the
(1+\beta)
process for any
\beta\leqslant 1/2
.

2.1
Basic Notation and Specific Processes

We consider the allocation of
m
balls into
n
bins, which are labeled
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. For the moment, the
m
balls are unweighted (or equivalently, all balls have weight
1
). For any round
t\geqslant 0
,
x^{t}
is the
n
-dimensional
load vector
, where
x_{i}^{t}
is the number of balls allocated into bin
i
in the first
t
allocations. In particular,
x_{i}^{0}=0
for every
i\in[n]
. Finally, the
gap
is defined as

\Gap(t)=\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}-\frac{t}{n}.

It will be also convenient to keep the load vector
x
sorted. To this end, let
\widetilde{x}^{t}:=x^{t}-\frac{t}{n}
. Then, relabel the bins such that
y^{t}
is a permutation of
\widetilde{x}^{t}
and
y_{1}^{t}\geqslant y_{2}^{t}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant y_{n}^{t}
. Note that
\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{t}=0
and
\Gap(t)=y_{1}^{t}
. We will call a bin
i\in[n]

overloaded
, if
y_{i}\geqslant 0
and
underloaded
otherwise. Further, we say that a vector
v=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})

majorizes

u=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})
if for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, the prefix sums satisfy:
\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{i}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}
.

Following
[
30
]
, many allocation processes can be described by a time-invariant
probability vector

p_{i}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, such that at each step
t\geqslant 0
,
p_{i}
is the probability for allocating a ball into the
i
-th most heavily loaded bin (or equivalently, incrementing
y_{i}^{t}
by one).

By
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
we denote the filtration of the process until step
t
, which in particular reveals the load vector
x^{t}
.

We continue with a formal description of the
Two-Choice
process.

Two-Choice
Process:

Iteration:
For each
t\geqslant 0
, sample two bins
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
with replacement, independently and uniformly at random. Let
i
be one bin with
x_{i}^{t}=\min\{x_{i_{1}}^{t},x_{i_{2}}^{t}\}
, breaking ties randomly. Then update:

x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+1.

It is immediate that the probability vector of
Two-Choice
is

p_{i}=\frac{2(i-1)}{n^{2}},\qquad\mbox{ for all $i\in[n]$.}

Following
[
30
]
, we recall the definition of
(1+\beta)
which is a process interpolating between
One-Choice
and
Two-Choice
:

(
1+\beta
)-Process:

Parameter:
A mixing factor
\beta\in(0,1]
.

Iteration:
For each
t\geqslant 0
, sample two bins
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
with replacement, independently and uniformly at random. Let
i
be one bin with
x_{i}^{t}=\min\{x_{i_{1}}^{t},x_{i_{2}}^{t}\}
, breaking ties randomly. Then update:

\begin{cases}x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+1&\mbox{with probability $\beta$},\\
x_{i_{1}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{1}}^{t}+1&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In other words at each step,
(1+\beta)
-process allocates the ball following the
Two-Choice
rule with probability
\beta
, and otherwise allocates the ball following the
One-Choice
rule. Therefore, the probability vector is given by
[
30
]
:

p_{i}=(1-\beta)\cdot\frac{1}{n}+\beta\cdot\frac{2(i-1)}{n^{2}},\qquad\mbox{ for all $i\in[n]$.}

The next process is another relaxation of
Two-Choice
.

\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
Process:

Parameter:
A quantile
\delta\in\{1/n,2/n,\ldots,1\}
.

Iteration:
For each
t\geqslant 0
, sample two bins
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
with replacement, independently and uniformly at random, and update:

\begin{cases}x_{i_{2}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{2}}^{t}+1&\mbox{if $i_{1}$ is among the $\delta\cdot n$ most loaded bins},\\
x_{i_{1}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{1}}^{t}+1&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that the
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
processes can be also implemented as a two-phase procedure: First probe the bin
i_{1}
and place the ball there if
i_{1}
is not among the
\delta\cdot n
heaviest bins. Otherwise, take a second sample
i_{2}
and place the ball there. Since we only need to know whether a bin’s rank is above or below a value, the response by a bin can be encoded as a single bit. The probability vector of
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
is given by:

p_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{\delta}{n}&\mbox{ if $1\leqslant i\leqslant\delta\cdot n$},\\
\frac{1+\delta}{n}&\mbox{ if $\delta\cdot n<i\leqslant n$}.\end{cases}

Another, equivalent description of
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
is that we perform
Two-Choice
, but only get to know whether a sampled bin’s rank is below or above
\delta\cdot n
and breaking ties randomly.

2.2
Classes of Processes

We will now formulate general conditions based on the probability vector
p
to which our analysis will apply:

•

Condition
\mathcal{D}_{0}
:
(p_{i})_{i\in[n]}
is a non-decreasing probability vector in
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.

•

Condition
\mathcal{D}_{1}
: There exist constant
\delta\in(0,1)
and (not necessarily constant)
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

p_{\delta n}\leqslant\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}.

•

Condition
\mathcal{D}_{2}
: For some constant
C>1
,
\max_{i\in[n]}p_{i}\leqslant\frac{C}{n}
.

Note that condition
\mathcal{D}_{1}
only provides an upper bound for allocating into the heavier bins. However, due to
\mathcal{D}_{0}
, this also implies a lower bound on the probability for allocating into the lighter bins (see
2.1
). Finally, we remark any
d
-Choice
process satisfies
\mathcal{D}_{2}
for
C=d
.

For the application to graphical allocation, we will relax these conditions slightly and drop the assumption that
(p_{i})_{i\in[n]}
is non-increasing in exchange for a stronger version of condition
\mathcal{D}_{1}
that involves both prefix and suffix sums of
p
.

•

Condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
: There exist constant
\delta\in(0,1)
and (not necessarily constant)
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, such that for any
1\leqslant k\leqslant\delta\cdot n
,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{k}{n},

and similarly for any
\delta\cdot n+1\leqslant k\leqslant n
,

\sum_{i=k}^{n}p_{i}\geqslant\left(1+\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\right)\cdot\frac{n-k+1}{n}.

•

Condition
\mathcal{C}_{2}
: For some constant
C>1
,
\max_{i\in[n]}p_{i}\leqslant\frac{C}{n}
.

Observation 2.1
.

Conditions
\mathcal{D}_{0}
and
\mathcal{D}_{1}
with
\delta
and
\varepsilon
imply condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
with the same
\delta
and
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Since
p_{\delta n}\leqslant\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}
and
p_{i}
is non-decreasing, it follows that
p_{j}\leqslant\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant\delta n
, and thus the prefix sum condition of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
holds with equality. We can also conclude that

\sum_{i=1}^{\delta n}p_{i}\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\delta,

and hence

\sum_{i=\delta n+1}^{n}p_{i}\geqslant 1-(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\delta.

Since
p_{i}
is non-decreasing, it follows for any
\delta n+1\leqslant k\leqslant n
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k}^{n}p_{i}\geqslant\frac{n-k+1}{(1-\delta)n}\cdot\left(1-(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\delta\right)=\frac{n-k+1}{n}\cdot\left(1+\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\right).

∎

Using this observation, it is easy to verify that

(1+\beta)
and

\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
.

.

and

For any

proving that

2.3
Batched Model and Weights

We will now extend the definitions of
Section

2.1
and
Section

2.2
to
weighted balls
into bins. To this end, let

w^{t}\geqslant 0
be the weight of the
t
-th ball to be allocated (
t\geqslant 1
). By
W^{t}
we denote the the total weights of all balls allocated after the first
t\geqslant 0
allocations, so
W^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}w^{s}
. The normalized loads are
\widetilde{x}_{i}^{t}:=x_{i}^{t}-\frac{W^{t}}{n}
, and with
y_{i}^{t}
being again the decreasingly sorted, normalized load vector, we have
\Gap(t)=y_{1}^{t}
.

The weight of each ball will be drawn independently from a fixed distribution
W
over
[0,\infty)
. Following
[
30
]
, we assume that the distribution
W
satisfies:

•

\mathbf{E}[\,W\,]=1
.

•

\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\lambda W}\,]<\infty
for some constant
\lambda>0
.

It is clear that when
\mathbf{E}[\,W\,]=\Theta(1)
, by scaling
W
, we can always achieve
\mathbf{E}[\,W\,]=1
.
Specific examples of distributions satisfying above conditions (after scaling) are the geometric, exponential, binomial and Poisson distributions.

Similar to the arguments in
[
30
]
, the above two assumptions can be used to prove that:

Lemma 2.4
.

There exists
S:=S(\lambda)\geqslant\max(1,1/\lambda)
, such that for any
\alpha\in(0,\min(\lambda/2,1))
and any
\kappa\in[-1,1]
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\alpha\cdot\kappa\cdot W}\,\right]\leqslant 1+\alpha\cdot\kappa+S\alpha^{2}\cdot\kappa^{2}.

Proof.

This proof closely follows the argument in
[
30
, Lemma 2.1]
. Let
M(z)=\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{zW}\,\right]
, then using Taylor’s Theorem (mean value form remainder), for any
z\in[-\alpha,\alpha]
there exists
\xi\in[-\alpha,\alpha]
such that

M(z)=M(0)+M^{\prime}(0)\cdot z+M^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot z^{2}=1+z+M^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot z^{2}.

By the assumptions on
\alpha
and
\lambda
,

\displaystyle M^{\prime\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}[\,W^{2}e^{\xi W}\,]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[\,W^{4}\,]\cdot\mathbf{E}[\,e^{2\xi W}\,]}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\mathbf{E}[\,W^{4}\,]+\mathbf{E}[\,e^{2\xi W}\,]\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Big(\Big(\frac{8}{\lambda}\cdot\log\Big(\frac{8}{\lambda}\Big)\Big)^{4}+\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\lambda W}\,]+\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\lambda W}\,]\Big).

where
(a)
uses the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality
|\mathbf{E}\left[\,X\cdot Y\,\right]|\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{2}\,\right]\mathbf{E}\left[\,Y^{2}\,\right]}
for random variables
X
and
Y
,
(b)
uses a mean inequality, and
(c)
uses
A.5
. Now defining

S:=2\cdot\max\left\{\left(\frac{8}{\lambda}\cdot\log\Big(\frac{8}{\lambda}\Big)\right)^{4},2\cdot\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\lambda W}\,],1/2\right\},

and choosing
z:=\kappa\cdot\alpha
,
the lemma follows.
∎

We will now describe the allocation of weighted balls into bins using a batch size of
b\geqslant n
. For the sake of concreteness, let us first describe the batched model if the allocation is done using

. For a given batch size consisting of
b
consecutive balls, each ball of the batch performs the following. First, it samples two bins
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
and compares the load the two bins had at the beginning of the batch (let us denote the bin which has less load by
i

W
. Then a weighted ball is added to bin
i
. Recall that since the load information is only updated at the beginning of the batch, all allocations of the
b
balls within the same batch can be performed in parallel.

In the following, we will use a more general framework, where the process of sampling (one or more) bins and then deciding where to allocate the ball to is described by a probability vector
p
over the
n
bins (
Section

2.1
and
Section

2.2
). Also for the analysis, it will be convenient to focus on the normalized and sorted load vector
y
, which is why the definition below is based on

rather than the actual load vector

.

Lemma 2.5

(Smoothing argument)
.

Consider any allocation process in the weighted batched setting with
b\geqslant n
and a weight distribution satisfying
2.4
for some constants
\lambda>0
and
S:=S(\lambda)\geqslant 1
. If for some
m
being a multiple of
b
, some (not necessarily constant)
c>0
and constant
\kappa>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{1}^{m}\leqslant c\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\kappa},

then for any
t\in[m-b,m]
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{1}^{t}\leqslant c+\frac{2\ln(S)}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-\kappa}.

Similarly, if for some (not necessarily constant)
c>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,-y_{n}^{m}\leqslant c\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\kappa},

then there exists a constant
\kappa:=\kappa(S)>0
, such that for any
t\in[m,m+b]
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,-y_{n}^{t}\leqslant c+\frac{2\ln(S)}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-\kappa}.

Proof.

Applying
A.1
for the
k:=b\geqslant n
weights
(w^{m-b+j})_{j=1}^{b}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\sum_{j=1}^{b}w^{m-b+j}\leqslant\frac{2\ln(S)}{\lambda}\cdot b\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\Omega(b)}.

Hence, w.h.p. the mean load does not increase by more than
\frac{2\ln(S)}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{b}{n}
. Therefore by the union bound, for any
t\in[m-b,m]
, any bin load can increase by at most that amount,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{1}^{t}\leqslant c+\frac{2\ln(S)}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\Omega(b)}-n^{-\kappa}\geqslant 1-2n^{-\kappa},

Similarly, by the union bound, for any
t\in[m,m+b]
, any bin load can decrease by at most
\frac{2\ln(S)}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{b}{n}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,-y_{n}^{t}\leqslant c+\frac{2\ln(S)}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-e^{-\Omega(b)}-n^{-\kappa}\geqslant 1-2n^{-\kappa}.

which concludes the claim.
∎

3
Analysis of the Hyperbolic Cosine Potential

In this section we generalize
[
30
, Theorem 2.10]
. This generalization allows us to apply it to multi-step changes in
Section

4
, handle general quantile conditions (arbitrary constant
\delta>0
instead of
\delta=1/3
) and obtain tighter bounds of
\mathcal{O}(n)
on the expectation of the potential, which we make use of in
Section

5
. Further, using this generalization, we obtain bounds on graphical allocation with weights and batches and a tighter upper bound for the
(1+\beta)
process for very small
\beta
(
Section

6
). The
hyperbolic cosine potential
is defined as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{t}:=\Phi^{t}+\Psi^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\alpha y_{i}^{t}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-\alpha y_{i}^{t}},

(3.1)

for
\alpha>0
. We also decompose
\Gamma^{t}
across bins as follows, and define for any bin
i\in[n]
:

\Gamma_{i}^{t}:=\Phi_{i}^{t}+\Psi_{i}^{t}=e^{\alpha y_{i}^{t}}+e^{-\alpha y_{i}^{t}}.

Further, we use the following shorthands to denote the changes in the potentials
\Delta\Phi_{i}^{t}:=\Phi_{i}^{t+1}-\Phi_{i}^{t}
,
\Delta\Psi_{i}^{t}:=\Psi_{i}^{t+1}-\Psi_{i}^{t}
and
\Delta\Gamma_{i}^{t}:=\Gamma_{i}^{t+1}-\Gamma_{i}^{t}
.

The next result holds for any probability vector
p
satisfying condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and any load vector
x
. As, we show in
3.2
, this implies upper bounds on the expected change of the
\Gamma
potential, under certain conditions.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider any probability vector
p
satisfying condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for constant
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
, and any load vector
x
with
\Phi:=\Phi(x)
,
\Psi:=\Psi(x)
and
\Gamma:=\Gamma(x)
. Further for some
K>0
define,

\Delta\overline{\Phi}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}\cdot\Big(\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big),

and

\Delta\overline{\Psi}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big).

Then, there exists a constant
c:=c(\delta)>0
, such that for any
0<\alpha<\min(1,\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8K})
,

\Delta\overline{\Gamma}:=\Delta\overline{\Phi}+\Delta\overline{\Psi}\leqslant-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{n}\cdot\Gamma+c\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha.

Before presenting the proof, we begin with an outline of the key observations in the proof. Let
\Delta\overline{\Gamma}_{i}:=\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}+\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}
.

1.

It suffices to analyze the potential for a process with probability vector,

\displaystyle q_{i}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}&\text{if }i\leqslant\delta n,\\
\frac{1+\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(3.2)

where
\widetilde{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}
, as this maximizes the terms
\Delta\overline{\Phi}
and
\Delta\overline{\Psi}
.

2.

For any bin
i\in[n]
, there is one dominant term in
\Gamma_{i}
: for overloaded bins it is
\Phi_{i}
(and
\Psi_{i}\leqslant 1
) and for underloaded bins it is
\Psi_{i}
(and
\Phi_{i}\leqslant 1
). The change of the smaller term is absorbed by the change of the dominant and the additive term, i.e.,
c\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha
.

3.

It suffices to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\left(\Phi_{i}\cdot\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha+\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha\right)\leqslant-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{n}\cdot\Gamma+c\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha,

as half of the decrease term, i.e.,
-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{n}\cdot\Gamma
will counteract the increase term
K\cdot\alpha^{2}\cdot\Gamma
for sufficiently small
\alpha
. So, the main focus is on the coefficients of
\alpha
.

4.

Any overloaded bin
i\in[n]
with
i\leqslant\delta n
, satisfies
p_{i}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}
and so
\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}\leqslant-\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{n}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})
. We call these the set
\mathcal{G}_{+}
of
good overloaded bins
. The rest of the overloaded bins are the
bad overloaded bins

\mathcal{B}_{+}
and these still satisfy
\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}\leqslant+\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})
.

Similarly,
good underloaded bins

\mathcal{G}_{-}
with
i>\delta n
, satisfy
\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}\leqslant-\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})
and
bad underloaded bins

\mathcal{B}_{-}
satisfy
\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}\leqslant+\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{n}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})
.

5.

We can either have
\mathcal{B}_{+}\neq\emptyset
or
\mathcal{B}_{-}\neq\emptyset
(

).

p_{i}=q_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
.

Recall, that we partition overloaded bins
i
with
y_{i}\geqslant 0
into
good overloaded bins

\mathcal{G}_{+}
with
p_{i}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}
and into
bad overloaded bins

\mathcal{B}_{+}
with
p_{i}=\frac{1+\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}
. These are called good bins, because any bin
i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}
satisfies
\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}\leqslant\Phi_{i}\cdot\left(-\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right)
and since
\Psi_{i}\leqslant 1
for overloaded bins, this will imply the drop condition for
\Gamma_{i}
.

Case A [
1\leqslant|\mathcal{B}_{+}|\leqslant\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1-\delta)
]:
Intuitively, in this case the contribution of the bad bins is counteracted by the contribution of the good overloaded bins (
Fig.

3
).
To formalize this, let
z_{1}:=y_{\delta n}
(by assumption on
|\mathcal{B}_{+}|
, we know that
z_{1}\geqslant 0
). Then for any
i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}
,
y_{i}\geqslant z_{1}
and for any
i\in\mathcal{B}_{+}
,
y_{i}\leqslant z_{1}
. So,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{+}}\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\Big(\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{+}}e^{\alpha z_{1}}\cdot\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1-\delta)\cdot e^{\alpha z_{1}}\cdot\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha z_{1}}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n},

(3.4)

where we have used in the last inequality that
|\mathcal{B}_{+}|\geqslant 1
implies
|\mathcal{G}_{+}|=\delta\cdot n
. For bins in
\mathcal{G}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{4n}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{3\alpha\varepsilon}{4n}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{4n}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}e^{\alpha z_{1}}\cdot\frac{3\alpha\varepsilon}{4n}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{4n}-e^{\alpha z_{1}}\cdot\frac{3\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}.

(3.5)

Hence, combining
Eq.

3.4
and
Eq.

3.5
, the contribution for
\Phi
of overloaded bins is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{4n}-e^{\alpha z_{1}}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4}+\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{4n}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{+}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{2(1-\delta)n}+\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Phi_{i}\cdot\left(-\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\right).

So, using that
\Psi_{i}\leqslant 1
for overloaded bins,

\displaystyle\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Delta\overline{\Gamma}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}+\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Phi_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)+\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Gamma_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)+\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\frac{2\alpha}{n}\cdot\max\Big(\widetilde{\varepsilon},\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4}\Big).

(3.6)

Since in this case all underloaded bins are good, i.e., for any
i\in[n]
with
y_{i}<0
, we have
p_{i}=\frac{1+\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}\leqslant\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big).

(3.7)

Combining the contribution across all underloaded bins,

\displaystyle\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\Delta\overline{\Gamma}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}+\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\Phi_{i}\cdot\Big(\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)+\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\Gamma_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)+\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\frac{2\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n},

(3.8)

where in the first inequality we used
Eq.

3.7
and the precondition of the theorem, while in the last inequality we used that
\Phi_{i}\leqslant 1
for underloaded bins.

Combining
Eq.

3.6
and
Eq.

3.8
,

\displaystyle\Delta\overline{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Delta\overline{\Gamma}_{i}+\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\Delta\overline{\Gamma}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\Gamma_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)+\sum_{i:y_{i}\geqslant 0}\frac{2\alpha}{n}\cdot\max\Big(\widetilde{\varepsilon},\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\Gamma_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)+\sum_{i:y_{i}<0}\frac{2\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{2\alpha}{n}\cdot\max\Big(\widetilde{\varepsilon},\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant-\Gamma\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{8n}+2\alpha\cdot\max\Big(\widetilde{\varepsilon},\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4}\Big),

(3.9)

using in the last line that
\alpha\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8K}
.

Case B [
|\mathcal{B}_{+}|>\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1-\delta)
]:
We partition
\mathcal{B}_{+}
into
\mathcal{B}_{1}:=\mathcal{B}_{+}\cap\{i\in[n]:i\leqslant\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1+\delta)\}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}:=\mathcal{B}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{1}
. The positive contribution
\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}
for bins
i\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
will be counteracted by that of the bins in
\mathcal{G}_{+}
as in Case A. For that of bins in
\mathcal{B}_{2}
we consider two cases based on
z_{2}:=y_{\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1+\delta)}>0
, the load of the first bin in
\mathcal{B}_{2}
. Similarly to
Eq.

3.4
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{\alpha z_{2}}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}.

(3.10)

Case B.1 [
z_{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{1-\delta}{2\delta}\cdot\ln(8/3)
]:
In this case, we will show that the contribution of the bad bins can be absorbed by the additive term. In particular, the contribution of the bins in
\mathcal{B}_{2}
is

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Delta\overline{\Gamma}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}2\cdot e^{\alpha z_{2}}\cdot\Big(\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}4\cdot e^{\frac{1-\delta}{2\delta}\cdot\ln(8/3)}\cdot\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}

\displaystyle<4\cdot e^{\frac{1-\delta}{2\delta}\cdot\ln(8/3)}\cdot\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}.

Hence, counteracting the positive contribution of the bins in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
using that of the bins in
\mathcal{G}_{+}
as in Case A (since
|\mathcal{B}_{1}|\leqslant\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1-\delta)
) as in
Eq.

3.9
, we have

\Delta\overline{\Gamma}\leqslant-\Gamma\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{8n}+\max\Big(\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4},\widetilde{\varepsilon},2\cdot e^{\frac{1-\delta}{2\delta}\cdot\ln(8/3)}\cdot\widetilde{\varepsilon}\Big)\cdot 2\alpha.

Case B.2 [
z_{2}>\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{1-\delta}{2\delta}\cdot\ln(8/3)
]:
In this case, it means that there are substantially more holes in the underloaded bins than balls in the overloaded bins of
\mathcal{B}_{1}
. Hence, as we will prove below, the negative contribution
\Delta\overline{\Psi}
for bins in
\mathcal{G}_{-}
will counteract the positive contribution of
\Delta\overline{\Phi}
for
\mathcal{B}_{1}
(
Fig.

4
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{3\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4\cdot(1-\delta)\cdot n}.

(3.11)

The term
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}
is minimized when all underloaded bins are equal to the same load
-z_{3}<0
, i.e.
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}\geqslant|\mathcal{G}_{-}|\cdot e^{\alpha z_{3}}
. Note that
z_{3}\geqslant\frac{z_{2}\cdot(|\mathcal{B}_{1}|+|\mathcal{G}_{+}|)}{|\mathcal{G}_{-}|}\geqslant\frac{z_{2}\cdot\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1+\delta)}{|\mathcal{G}_{-}|}
and that the function
f(z)=z\cdot e^{k/z}
is decreasing for
0\leqslant z\leqslant k
(
A.8
). Hence, for
k=z_{2}\cdot\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1+\delta)
, the maximum size
|\mathcal{G}_{-}|=\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1-\delta)\leqslant k
, minimizes the term
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}
.
We lower bound
z_{3}
as follows,

z_{3}\geqslant\frac{z_{2}\cdot\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1+\delta)}{\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1-\delta)}=z_{2}+z_{2}\cdot\frac{2\delta}{1-\delta}\geqslant z_{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\ln(8/3),

using the lower bound on
z_{2}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{n}{2}\cdot(1-\delta)\cdot e^{\alpha\cdot(z_{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\ln(8/3))}\cdot\frac{3\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4\cdot(1-\delta)\cdot n}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha z_{2}}\cdot e^{\alpha\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\ln(8/3)}\cdot\frac{3\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{8}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha z_{2}}\cdot(\alpha\varepsilon\delta).

Applying this to
Eq.

3.11
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)-e^{\alpha z_{2}}\cdot(\alpha\varepsilon\delta).

(3.12)

Aggregating
Eq.

3.10
and
Eq.

3.12
, the contribution of underloaded bins to
\Delta\overline{\Phi}
is

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Delta\overline{\Psi}_{i}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Delta\overline{\Phi}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)-e^{\alpha z_{2}}\cdot(\alpha\varepsilon\delta)

\displaystyle\qquad+e^{\alpha z_{2}}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)-e^{\alpha z_{2}}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{(1-\delta)\cdot n}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Phi_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Delta\overline{\Gamma}_{i}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Delta\overline{\Gamma}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{4n}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{n}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Phi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{(1-\delta)\cdot n}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Psi_{i}\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{n}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Gamma_{i}\cdot K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}}\Gamma_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{-}\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}}\frac{2\alpha}{n}\cdot\max\Big(\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4},\widetilde{\varepsilon}\Big).

(3.13)

Aggregating similarly, to Case A, for
\mathcal{G}_{+}
and
\mathcal{B}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}}\Delta\overline{\Gamma}_{i}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\Delta\overline{\Gamma}_{i}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}\cup\mathcal{B}_{1}}\Gamma_{i}\cdot\Big(-\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{4n}+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{+}\cup\mathcal{B}_{1}}\frac{2\alpha}{n}\cdot\max\Big(\widetilde{\varepsilon},\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4}\Big).

(3.14)

Hence, combining
Eq.

3.13
and
Eq.

3.14
,

\displaystyle\Delta\overline{\Gamma}\leqslant-\Gamma\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{8n}+\max\Big(\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4},\widetilde{\varepsilon}\Big)\cdot 2\alpha.

Case C, D:
These are symmetric to Case A and Case B, but interchanging
\Phi
with
\Psi
,
\delta
with
1-\delta
and negating the normalized load vector and flipping the load vector.

Combining the four cases, we get that

\Delta\overline{\Gamma}\leqslant-\Gamma\cdot\frac{\alpha\varepsilon\delta}{8n}+c\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha,

where
c:=2\cdot\max\Big(\frac{\delta}{4},\frac{\delta}{1-\delta},2\cdot e^{\frac{1-\delta}{2\delta}\cdot\ln(8/3)}\cdot\frac{\delta}{1-\delta},2\cdot e^{\frac{\delta}{2(1-\delta)}\cdot\ln(8/3)}\Big)
, using that
\widetilde{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}
.
∎

By scaling the quantities
\Delta\overline{\Phi}
and
\Delta\overline{\Psi}
in
3.1
by some
\kappa>0
, we obtain:

Corollary 3.2
.

Consider any allocation process with probability vector
p
satisfying conditions
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for constant
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
. Further assume that it satisfies for some
K>0
and some
\kappa>0
, for any
t\geqslant 0
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Phi_{i}^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\kappa\cdot\alpha+K\cdot\kappa\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big),

and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Psi_{i}^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\kappa\cdot\alpha+K\cdot\kappa\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big).

Then, there exists a constant
c:=c(\delta)>0
, such that for
0<\alpha<\min(1,\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8K})

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Gamma^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8}\cdot\kappa\cdot\frac{\alpha}{n}\cdot\Gamma^{t}+c\cdot\kappa\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha,

and

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n.

Proof.

Applying
3.1
for the current load vector
x^{t}
and for

\Delta\overline{\Phi}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big)\quad\text{ and }\quad\Delta\overline{\Psi}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha+K\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big),

we get

\displaystyle\Delta\overline{\Phi}+\Delta\overline{\Psi}\leqslant-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{n}\cdot\Gamma^{t}+c\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha.

(3.15)

By the assumptions,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Gamma^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Phi^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]+\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Psi^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\kappa\cdot(\Delta\overline{\Phi}+\Delta\overline{\Psi}).

(3.16)

Hence, combining
Eq.

3.15
and
Eq.

3.16
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Gamma^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8}\cdot\kappa\cdot\frac{\alpha}{n}\cdot\Gamma^{t}+c\cdot\kappa\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha.

Now, we will show by induction that for any
t\geqslant 0
,
\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n
. Assume true for
t
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{t+1}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{t+1}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{t}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8}\cdot\kappa\cdot\frac{\alpha}{n}\Big)\,\right]+c\cdot\kappa\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n\cdot\Big(1-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8}\cdot\kappa\cdot\frac{\alpha}{n}\Big)+c\cdot\kappa\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n-c\cdot\kappa\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha+c\cdot\kappa\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\alpha=\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n.\qed

4
A Simple Upper Bound

In this section we derive an upper bound of
\mathcal{O}(b/n\cdot\log n)
for the weighted batched setting. This upper bound is tight for
b=\Theta(n)
, as shown in
Section

7
. We will make use of the hyperbolic cosine potential as defined in
Eq.

3.1
. This will also serve as the base case for the tighter analysis in
Section

5
.

The main goal is to derive the preconditions of
3.2
and apply it for
\kappa:=b
over the batches (not individual time steps).

Lemma 4.1
.

Consider the weighted batched setting with batch size
b\geqslant n
, for a process with probability vector
p
satisfying condition
\mathcal{C}_{2}
for some
C>1
and the weight distribution satisfying
2.4
for some
S\geqslant 1
. Then for any
0<\alpha\leqslant\frac{n}{2CSb}
, for any
t\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}[\,\Phi_{i}^{t+b}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,]\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+5\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot b\right),

(4.1)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}[\,\Psi_{i}^{t+b}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,]\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+5\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot b\right).

(4.2)

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary bin
i\in[n]
. Let
Z\in\{0,1\}^{b}
be the indicator vector, where
Z_{j}
indicates whether the
j
-th ball was allocated to bin
i
. The expected change for the overload potential
\Phi_{i}^{t}
, is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\,\Phi_{i}^{t+b}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,]=\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{b}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z=z\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.e^{\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{n}(z_{j}w^{t+j}-\frac{w^{t+j}}{n})}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},Z=z\,\right].

In the following, let us upper bound the factor of
\Phi_{i}^{t}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{b}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z=z\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.e^{\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{n}(z_{j}w^{t+j}-\frac{w^{t+j}}{n})}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},Z=z\,\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\!\!\!\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{b}}\prod_{j=1}^{b}(p_{i})^{z_{j}}(1-p_{i})^{1-z_{j}}(\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\alpha W(1-\frac{1}{n})}\,])^{z_{j}}(\mathbf{E}[\,e^{-\alpha\frac{W}{n}}\,])^{1-z_{j}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\!\!\!\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{b}}\prod_{j=1}^{b}\left(p_{i}\cdot\Big(1+\alpha\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)+S\alpha^{2}\Big)\Big)^{z_{j}}\cdot\Big((1-p_{i})\cdot\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{n}+S\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)\Big)^{1-z_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}\left(p_{i}\cdot\Big(1+\alpha\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)+S\alpha^{2}\Big)+(1-p_{i})\cdot\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{n}+S\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)\right)^{b}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha+p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}+(1-p_{i})\cdot\frac{S\alpha^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)^{b}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(1+\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\right)^{b},

(4.3)

using in
(a)
that the weights are independent given
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
, in
(b)
the
2.4
and in
(c)
the binomial theorem.
Let us define

y:=\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}.

We first claim that
y\cdot b\leqslant 1
, which holds indeed since

\displaystyle y\cdot b

\displaystyle=\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\cdot b

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{n}\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot\frac{C}{n}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\cdot b\leqslant 2CS\cdot\alpha\cdot\frac{b}{n}\leqslant 1,

where in the last inequality we used
\alpha\leqslant\frac{n}{2CSb}
.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\,\Phi_{i}^{t+b}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{y\cdot b}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+y\cdot b+y^{2}\cdot b^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\cdot b+\Big(\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\cdot b\Big)^{2}\right),

using in
(a)
that
1+y\leqslant e^{y}
for any
y
, and in
(b)
that
e^{y}\leqslant 1+y+y^{2}
for
y\leqslant 1.75
. Since
p_{i}\leqslant\frac{C}{n}
for all
i\in[n]
, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\,\Phi_{i}^{t+b}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot\frac{CS}{n}\cdot\alpha^{2}\cdot b+\left(\frac{2CS}{n}\cdot\alpha\cdot b\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+5\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot b\right).

Similarly, for the underloaded potential
\Psi^{t}
, for any bin
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\,\Psi_{i}^{t+b}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,]

\displaystyle=\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{b}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z=z\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.e^{-\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{n}(z_{j}w^{t+j}-\frac{w^{t+j}}{n})}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},Z=z\,\right].

As before, we will upper bound the factor of
\Psi_{i}^{t}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{b}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z=z\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.e^{-\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{n}(z_{j}w^{t+j}-\frac{w^{t+j}}{n})}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},Z=z\,\right]

(4.4)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\!\!\!\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{b}}\prod_{j=1}^{b}(p_{i})^{z_{j}}(1-p_{i})^{1-z_{j}}(\mathbf{E}[\,e^{-\alpha W\cdot(1-\frac{1}{n})}\,])^{z_{j}}(\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\alpha\frac{W}{n}}\,])^{1-z_{j}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\!\!\!\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{b}}\prod_{j=1}^{b}p_{i}\cdot\Big(1-\alpha\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)+S\alpha^{2}\Big)\Big)^{z_{j}}\cdot\Big((1-p_{i})\cdot\Big(1+\frac{\alpha}{n}+S\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)\Big)^{1-z_{j}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}\left(p_{i}\cdot\Big(1-\alpha\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)+S\alpha^{2}\Big)+(1-p_{i})\cdot\Big(1+\frac{\alpha}{n}+S\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n^{2}}\Big)\right)^{b}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha+p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}+(1-p_{i})\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}S}{n^{2}}\right)^{b}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(1+\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\right)^{b},

(4.5)

using in
(a)
that the weights
W
are independent given
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
, in
(b)

2.4
and in
(c)
the binomial theorem. So,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\,\Psi_{i}^{t+b}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i})\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\cdot b}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\cdot b+\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\cdot b\Big)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leqslant}}\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot\frac{CS}{n}\cdot\alpha^{2}\cdot b+\left(\frac{2CS}{n}\cdot\alpha\cdot b\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha\cdot b+5\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\cdot b\right),

using in
(a)
that
1+y\leqslant e^{y}
for any
y
, in
(b)
that
e^{y}\leqslant 1+y+y^{2}
for
y\leqslant 1.75
and that
(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i})\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\cdot b\leqslant\frac{C}{n}\cdot\alpha\cdot b+2\cdot\frac{C}{n}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\cdot b\leqslant 2CS\cdot\alpha\cdot\frac{b}{n}\leqslant 1
, since
\alpha\leqslant\frac{n}{2CSb}
.
∎

We are now ready to apply
3.2
for
\kappa=b
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider any process
p
satisfying conditions
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for constant
\delta\in(0,1)
and (not necessarily constant)
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
as well as condition
\mathcal{C}_{2}
for some constant
C>1
. Further, consider the batched setting with any
b\geqslant n
and a weight distribution satisfying
2.4
with constant
S\geqslant 1
.
Then there exists a constant
k:=k(\delta,C,S)>0
, such that for any
m\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{m}|\leqslant k\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Remark 4.3
.

The same upper bound as in
4.2
holds also for processes with a time-dependent probability vector
p^{t}
, as long as for all
t
being multiplies of
b
, the probability vector
p^{t}
satisfies
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
for the same
\varepsilon,\delta
and
C
.

Remark 4.4
.

The same upper bound as in
4.2
holds also for processes with random tie-breaking and a probability vector
p
satisfying the preconditions of
4.1
. The reason for this is that
(i)
averaging probabilities in
Eq.

2.1
can only reduce the maximum entry, i.e.
\max_{i\in[n]}\widetilde{p}_{i}(x^{t})\leqslant\max_{i\in[n]}p_{i}

and
(ii)
moving probability between bins
i,j

(and thus

Proof of
4.2
.

By
4.1
, the preconditions of
3.2
are satisfied for
K:=5\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}\cdot\frac{b}{n}
,
\kappa:=b

such that for any step

which is a multiple of

Hence, by Markov’s inequality

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gamma^{m}\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n^{3}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

To prove the claim, note that when
\{\Gamma^{m}\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n^{3}\}
holds, then also,

\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{m}|\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\left(\log\Big(\frac{8c}{\delta}\Big)+3\cdot\log n\right)\leqslant 4\cdot\frac{8\cdot 5\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}}{\varepsilon\delta}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\log n.\qed

5
An Improved Upper Bound for Batch Sizes
n\leqslant b\leqslant n^{3}

In this section, we will prove an improved (and tight) upper bound of
\mathcal{O}(b/n+\log n)
on the gap for the weighted batched setting with batch size
n\leqslant b\leqslant n^{3}
. We will be assuming processes satisfying conditions
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for constant
\delta>0
and constant
\varepsilon>0
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
with constant
C>1
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider any process
p
satisfying conditions
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for constant
\delta\in(0,1)
and constant
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
as well as
\mathcal{C}_{2}
for some constant
C>1
. Further, consider the batched setting with any
n\leqslant b\leqslant n^{3}
and a weight distribution satisfying
2.4
with constant
S\geqslant\max(1,1/\lambda)
.
Then, there is a constant
\kappa:=\kappa(\delta,\varepsilon,C,S)>0
, such that for any
m\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{1}^{m}\leqslant\kappa\cdot\left(\frac{b}{n}+\log n\right)\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Remark 5.2
.

The same gap bound holds also for processes with a time-dependent probability vector
p^{t}
, as long as for all
t
being a multiple of
b
, the probability vector
p^{t}
satisfies
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
for the same
\varepsilon,\delta
and
C
.

There are two key steps in the proof:

Step 1:
Similar to the analysis in
[
24
, Theorem 5.3]
, we will be using two instances of the
\Gamma
potential defined in
Section

4
for
\alpha:=\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{40\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}}\cdot\min\big(\frac{1}{\log n},\frac{n}{b}\big)
. The second instance
\widetilde{\Gamma}
has a smaller smoothing factor
\widetilde{\alpha}:=\frac{\alpha}{8\cdot 30}
,

\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(e^{\widetilde{\alpha}y_{i}^{t}}+e^{-\widetilde{\alpha}y_{i}^{t}}\right).

So, in particular
\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\leqslant\Gamma^{t}
holds. Note that by varying
b\in[n,n\log n]
, both smoothing factors do not change, but this will not affect the upper bound, as we shall see below.

We will show that w.h.p.
\widetilde{\Gamma}=\mathcal{O}(n)
for
\log^{3}n
batches.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\widetilde{c}:=2\cdot\frac{8c}{\delta}
where
c:=c(\delta)>0
is the constant from
3.2
. Then, for any
t\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{j\in[0,\log^{3}n]}\left\{\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+j\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.

We prove this by conditioning on
\Gamma^{t}=\operatorname{poly}(n)
which implies that
\Delta\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+1}=\mathcal{O}(\frac{n}{b}\cdot n^{1/4})
(
5.5

(ii)
). This in turn allows us to apply a bounded difference inequality (
A.4
) to prove concentration for
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. The complete proof is given in
Section

5.1
.

Step 2:
We start by exploiting that conditioning on
\{\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+j\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n\}
, the number of bins with load at least
k:=\frac{1}{\widetilde{\alpha}}\cdot\log(\widetilde{c}/\delta)=\Theta(\max(b/n,\log n))
is at most
\delta n
. We define the following potential function which only takes bins into account that are overloaded by at least
k
balls:

\Lambda^{t}:=\sum_{i:y_{i}^{t}\geqslant k}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{\gamma\cdot(y_{i}^{t}-k)},

where
\gamma:=\min\big(\frac{\varepsilon}{4CS},\frac{n\log n}{b}\big)
. This means that when
\{\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n\}
holds, the probability of allocating to one of these bins is
p_{i}\leqslant\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}
, because of condition
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Hence, the potential drops in expectation (
5.9
) and this means that w.h.p.
\Lambda^{m}=\operatorname{poly}(n)
, implying an
\mathcal{O}(k+\gamma^{-1}\cdot\log n)=\mathcal{O}(b/n+\log n)
gap.

5.1
Step 1:
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is linear w.h.p.

In this subsection, we will prove
5.3
. In
Section

5.1.1
, we prove some properties of the
\Gamma
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}
potential and in

we combine these to show that w.h.p.
\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}=\mathcal{O}(n)
for
\log^{3}n
batches.

5.1.1
Preliminaries

For constant
\lambda>0
as defined in
Section

2.3
, we define the following event, for any round
t\geqslant 0

\mathcal{H}^{t}:=\left\{w^{t}\leqslant\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\log n\right\},

which means that the weight of the ball sampled in round
t
is
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
b\leqslant n^{3}
and for any
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[t,t+2b\log^{3}n]}\mathcal{H}^{s}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-10}

Proof.

Since
w^{t}
is sampled according to
W
with
\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\lambda W}\,]<\infty
, by
A.1

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,w^{t}\geqslant\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\log n\,\right]\leqslant n^{-14}.

By taking the union bound over the interval
[t,t+2b\log^{3}n]
and since
b\leqslant n^{3}
we get the conclusion.
∎

We will now show that when
\Gamma^{t}=\operatorname{poly}(n)
and
\mathcal{H}^{t}
holds, then
\Delta\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+1}
is small.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\widetilde{c}:=\widetilde{c}(\delta)>0
be the constant defined in
5.3
. For any
t\geqslant 0
, where
\Gamma^{t}\leqslant 2\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{26}
and
\mathcal{H}^{t}
holds, then
(i)

\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\leqslant n^{5/4}
and
(ii)

|\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+1}-\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}|\leqslant\frac{n}{b}\cdot n^{1/4}
. Further, let
\widetilde{x}^{t}
be the load vector obtained by moving the
t
-th ball of the load vector
x^{t}
to some other bin, then
(iii)
when
\mathcal{H}^{t}
holds holds,
\Gamma^{t}(\widetilde{x}^{t})\leqslant 2\cdot\Gamma^{t}(x^{t})
.

Proof.

For any bin
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{t}\leqslant 2\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{26}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow e^{\alpha\cdot y_{i}^{t}}+e^{-\alpha\cdot y_{i}^{t}}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{26}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow y_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{27}{\alpha}\log n\,\wedge\,-y_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{27}{\alpha}\log n,

where in the second implication we used
\log(2\widetilde{c})+\frac{26}{\alpha}\log n\leqslant\frac{27}{\alpha}\log n
, for sufficiently large
n
.

This implies that

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}\leqslant e^{\widetilde{\alpha}y_{i}^{t}}+e^{-\widetilde{\alpha}y_{i}^{t}}\leqslant 2\cdot e^{\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{27}{\alpha}\log n}\leqslant 2\cdot n^{1/8},

(5.1)

using that
\widetilde{\alpha}:=\frac{\alpha}{8\cdot 30}
. Hence, by aggregating, we get the first claim
\Gamma^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{i}^{t}\leqslant 2\cdot n\cdot n^{1/8}\leqslant n^{5/4}
.

We now proceed to the second statement. Consider the change for the bin
j\in[n]
where the ball was allocated.
Since
\widetilde{\alpha}<\frac{1}{40\cdot S\cdot\log n}
and
S>\frac{1}{\lambda}
, we have
\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\log n\leqslant 1
and so by a Taylor estimate,
e^{\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\log n}\leqslant 1+2\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\log n
. If
j\in[n]
is an overloaded bin, then

\displaystyle|\Delta\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}\cdot e^{\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\log n}-\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}\cdot\Big(1+\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{30}{\lambda}\cdot\log n\Big)-\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{30}{\lambda}\cdot\log n\leqslant\frac{n}{b}\cdot n^{1/8}\cdot\log n,

using
Eq.

5.1
and
\widetilde{\alpha}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{40\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}}\cdot\frac{n}{b}
.
Similarly, if
j
is underloaded, then

\displaystyle|\Delta\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}-\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}\cdot e^{-\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\log n}\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}-\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}\cdot\Big(1-\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{30}{\lambda}\cdot\log n\Big)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}^{t}\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{30}{\lambda}\cdot\log n\leqslant\frac{n}{b}\cdot n^{1/8}\cdot\log n.

The rest of the bins’ contributions change due to the change in the average load. In particular, for any overloaded bin
i\in[n]\setminus\{j\}
,

\displaystyle|\Delta\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\log n}{n}}-\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(1+2\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)-\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{30}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\log n}{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{b}\cdot\log n\cdot n^{1/8}.

Similarly, for an underloaded bin
i\in[n]\setminus\{j\}
,

\displaystyle|\Delta\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}-\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{-\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\log n}{n}}\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}-\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(1-2\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{t}\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{30}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\log n}{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{b}\cdot\log n\cdot n^{1/8}.

Hence, aggregating over all bins

|\Delta\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+1}|\leqslant|\Delta\Gamma_{j}^{t+1}|+\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus\{j\}}|\Delta\Gamma_{i}^{t+1}|\leqslant 2\cdot\frac{n}{b}\cdot n^{1/8}\cdot\log n+n\cdot\frac{1}{b}\cdot\log n\cdot n^{1/8}\leqslant\frac{n}{b}\cdot n^{1/4},

for sufficiently large
n
.

For statement
(iii)
, let
i,j\in[n]
be the differing bins between
x^{t}
and
\widetilde{x}^{t}
. Then since
\mathcal{H}^{t}
holds,
w^{t}\leqslant\frac{15}{\lambda}\cdot\log n
, so

\Gamma_{i}(\widetilde{x}^{t})\leqslant e^{\alpha w^{t}}\cdot\Gamma_{i}^{t}(x^{t})\leqslant 2\cdot\Gamma_{i}^{t}(x^{t}),

since
\alpha<\frac{1}{40\cdot S\cdot\log n}
and
S>1/\lambda
. Similarly, for
j
,

\Gamma_{j}(\widetilde{x}^{t})\leqslant e^{\alpha w^{t}}\cdot\Gamma_{j}^{t}(x^{t})\leqslant 2\cdot\Gamma_{j}^{t}(x^{t}),

Hence,

\Gamma^{t}(\widetilde{x}^{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{k}^{t}(\widetilde{x}^{t})\leqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}2\cdot\Gamma_{k}^{t}(x^{t})=2\cdot\Gamma^{t}(x^{t}).

∎

Next, we will show that
\mathbf{E}[\,\widetilde{\Gamma}\,]=\mathcal{O}(n)
and that when
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is sufficiently large, it drops in expectation over the next batch.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\widetilde{c}:=2\cdot\frac{8c}{\delta}
where

. Then, for any step

(iii)\quad\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+b}\,\,\Big|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\geqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n\,\right]\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{\widetilde{c}_{1}}{\log n}\Big)\cdot\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t},

\widetilde{c}:=16c/\delta

.

Also, using

,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+b}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}\Big)+c\cdot b\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}.

(5.2)

Let
\widetilde{c}_{3}:=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}\geqslant\widetilde{c}_{1}/\log n

\widetilde{c}_{1}>0

and

\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\geqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n

5.2

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+b}\,\Big|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\geqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\cdot\Big(1-2\cdot\widetilde{c}_{3}\Big)+c\cdot b\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}-\widetilde{c}_{3}\cdot\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}+\Big(c\cdot b\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}-\widetilde{c}_{3}\cdot\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}-\widetilde{c}_{3}\cdot\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}+\Big(c\cdot b\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}\cdot\frac{16c}{\delta}\cdot n\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{\widetilde{c}_{1}}{\log n}\Big)\cdot\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}.

Similarly, when
\Gamma^{t}<\widetilde{c}\cdot n
,
Eq.

5.2
yields,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+b}\,\Big|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}<\widetilde{c}\cdot n\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n\cdot\Big(1-2\cdot\widetilde{c}_{3}\Big)+c\cdot b\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{c}\cdot n-\widetilde{c}\cdot\widetilde{c}_{3}\cdot n+\Big(c\cdot b\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\widetilde{\alpha}-\widetilde{c}\cdot\widetilde{c}_{3}\cdot n\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n-\frac{\widetilde{c}\cdot\widetilde{c}_{1}}{\log n}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n-\frac{n}{\log^{2}n}.\qed

Lemma 5.7

Let

b
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[t,t+2b\log^{3}n]}\left\{\Gamma^{s}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{26}\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-10}.

Proof.

Using
5.6

(i)
, Markov’s inequality and the union bound, we have for any
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[0,2\log^{3}n]}\left\{\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{12}\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-\frac{2\log^{3}n}{n^{11}}.

(5.3)

Given that
\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{12}
, we will upper bound
\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b+r}
for any

. To this end, we will upper bound for each bin

\Phi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b+r}

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Phi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b+r}\mid\Phi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\cdot\Big(1+\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\Big)^{r}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{C\alpha}{n}+2\cdot\frac{C}{n}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\Big)^{r}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\Phi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{2C\alpha}{n}\Big)^{r}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Phi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\cdot e^{2\alpha C\cdot\frac{r}{n}}\leqslant\Phi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\cdot e^{2\alpha C\cdot\frac{b}{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}2\cdot\Phi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b},

using in
(a)
that
\alpha\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{40\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}}\leqslant\frac{2}{S}
and in
(b)
that
\alpha\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{40\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}}\cdot\frac{n}{b}\leqslant\frac{1}{4C}\cdot\frac{n}{b}
. Similarly, using
Eq.

4.5
in
4.1
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Psi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b+r}\mid\Psi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Psi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\cdot\Big(1+\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha+2\cdot p_{i}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\Big)^{r}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Psi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{C\alpha}{n}+2\cdot\frac{C}{\alpha}\cdot S\alpha^{2}\Big)^{r}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\Psi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{2C\alpha}{n}\Big)^{r}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Psi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\cdot e^{2\alpha C\cdot\frac{r}{n}}\leqslant\Psi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b}\cdot e^{2\alpha C\cdot\frac{b}{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}2\cdot\Psi_{i}^{t+s\cdot b},

using in
(a)
that
\alpha\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{40\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}}\leqslant\frac{2}{S}
and in
(b)
that
\alpha\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{40\cdot C^{2}\cdot S^{2}}\cdot\frac{n}{b}\leqslant\frac{1}{4C}\cdot\frac{n}{b}
. Hence, aggregating over the bins,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b+r}\mid\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b}\,\right]\leqslant 2\cdot\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b}.

Applying Markov’s inequality, for any
r\in[0,b)
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b+r}\leqslant n^{14}\cdot\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b}\,\right]\geqslant 1-2\cdot n^{-14}.

Hence, by a union bound over the
2b\cdot\log^{3}n\leqslant 2\cdot n^{3}\cdot\log^{3}n
possible rounds for
s\in[0,2\log^{3}n]
and
r\in[0,b)
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{r\in[0,b]}\bigcap_{s\in[0,2\log^{3}n]}\left\{\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b+r}\leqslant n^{14}\cdot\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b}\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-2\cdot n^{-14}\cdot 2b\log^{3}n\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2}\cdot n^{-10}.

(5.4)

Finally, taking the union bound of
Eq.

5.3
and
Eq.

5.4
, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[t,t+2b\log^{3}n]}\left\{\Gamma^{s}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{26}\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{r\in[0,b]}\bigcap_{s\in[0,2\log^{3}n]}\left\{\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b+r}\leqslant n^{14}\cdot\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b}\right\}\cap\bigcap_{s\in[0,2\log^{3}n]}\left\{\Gamma^{t+s\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{12}\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2}\cdot n^{-10}-\frac{2\log^{3}n}{n^{11}}\geqslant 1-n^{-10}.\qed

\widetilde{\Gamma}
becomes
\mathcal{O}(n)

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\widetilde{c}:=2\cdot\frac{8c}{\delta}
be the constant defined in
5.6
. For any
t\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{s\in[0,b\log^{3}n]}\left\{\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+s\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-2\cdot n^{-8}.

Proof.

By
5.6

(ii)
, using Markov’s inequality at time

being a multiple of
b

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{9}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-8}.

(5.5)

Assuming
\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{9}
and by

(iii)
if at some step

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{r+1}\,\mid\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{r},\widetilde{\Gamma}^{r}>\widetilde{c}\cdot n\,\right]\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{\widetilde{c}_{1}}{\log n}\Big)\cdot\widetilde{\Gamma}^{r},

For any

5.6

\widehat{\Gamma}^{r}

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widehat{\Gamma}^{t+(r+1)\cdot b}\,\mid\,\widehat{\Gamma}^{t+r\cdot b}\,\right]\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{\widetilde{c}_{1}}{\log n}\Big)\cdot\widehat{\Gamma}^{t+r\cdot b}.

Inductively applying this for

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\widehat{\Gamma}^{t+(\log^{3}n)\cdot b}\,\,\right|\,\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{\widetilde{c}_{1}}{\log n}\Big)^{\log^{3}n}\cdot\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\leqslant e^{-\widetilde{c}_{1}\cdot\log^{2}n}\cdot\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{9}<n^{-7}.

So by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widehat{\Gamma}^{t+(\log^{3}n)\cdot b}\geqslant n\,\mid\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{9}\,\right]\leqslant n^{-{8}}

By union bound with

5.5
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widehat{\Gamma}^{t+(\log^{3}n)\cdot b}\geqslant n\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widehat{\Gamma}^{t+(\log^{3}n)\cdot b}\geqslant n\,\mid\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{9}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{9}\,\right]+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}>\widetilde{c}\cdot n^{9}\,\right]

\displaystyle<n^{-8}+n^{-8}=2\cdot n^{-8}.

Due to the definition of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, at any step
t\geqslant 0
, deterministically
\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t}\geqslant 2n
. So,
we conclude that w.p. at least

, there must be at least one time step

, with
\widehat{\Gamma}^{t+r\cdot b}=0
and so
\widetilde{\Gamma}^{t+s\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}\cdot n
for some

.
∎

See

Proof.

5.9
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}+(j+1)\cdot b}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\,\right]\leqslant\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\cdot e^{-\frac{\gamma\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot b}+n\cdot e^{\frac{C\gamma}{n}\cdot b}.

(5.12)

Assuming
\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}}
holds, we have

y_{1}^{t_{0}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\widetilde{\alpha}}\cdot(\log\widetilde{c}+\log n)\leqslant\frac{2}{\widetilde{\alpha}}\cdot\log n,

for sufficiently large
n
. Hence for some constant
\kappa_{1}>0
,

\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}}\leqslant n\cdot e^{\gamma\cdot y_{1}^{t_{0}}}\leqslant e^{\kappa\log^{2}n}.

Applying
A.6
to
Eq.

5.12
with
a:=e^{-\frac{\gamma\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot b}
and
b:=n\cdot e^{\frac{C\gamma}{n}\cdot b}
for
\log^{3}n
steps,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}^{m}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}}\leqslant e^{\kappa_{1}\log^{2}n}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{\kappa_{1}\log^{2}n}\cdot a^{\log^{3}n}+\frac{b}{1-a}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}1+1.5\cdot b\leqslant 2\cdot n\cdot e^{\frac{C\gamma}{n}\cdot b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}2\cdot n^{1+\kappa_{2}}.

(5.13)

using in
(a)
that
\frac{\gamma\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot b=\Omega(1)
and
a
a constant
<1
and in
(b)
that
\frac{C\gamma}{n}\cdot b\leqslant\kappa_{2}\cdot\log n
for some constant
\kappa_{2}>0
, since
\gamma=\min\big(\frac{\varepsilon}{4CS},\frac{n\log n}{b}\big)
.

By Markov’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}^{m}\leqslant 2\cdot n^{4+\kappa_{2}}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}}\leqslant e^{\kappa_{1}\log^{2}n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.

Hence, by
Eq.

5.11
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}^{m}\leqslant 2\cdot n^{4+\kappa_{2}}\,\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}^{m}\leqslant 2\cdot n^{4+\kappa_{2}}\mid\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant(1-n^{-3})\cdot(1-n^{-3}).

(5.14)

Combining
Eq.

5.11
and
Eq.

5.14
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Lambda^{m}\leqslant 2\cdot n^{4+\kappa_{2}}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{m}\leqslant 2\cdot n^{4+\kappa_{2}}\right\}\cap\bigcap_{j\in[0,\log^{3}n]}\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-n^{-3})\cdot(1-n^{-3})-n^{-3}\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Finally,
\{\Lambda^{m}\leqslant 2\cdot n^{4+\kappa_{2}}\}
implies

y_{1}^{m}\leqslant k+\frac{\log 2}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\cdot(4+\kappa_{2})\cdot\log n=\mathcal{O}(b/n+\log n),

since
\gamma=\min\big(\frac{\varepsilon}{4CS},\frac{n\log n}{b}\big)
and
\Theta(\max(b/n,\log n))
, so the claim follows.

For the case when
m<b\cdot\log^{3}n
, note that
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}}=2n
deterministically, which is a stronger starting point in
Eq.

5.13
to prove that
\mathbf{E}[\,\Lambda^{m}\,]\leqslant 2\cdot n^{1+\kappa_{2}}
, which in turn implies the gap bound.
∎

6
Application to Graphical Allocations and (
1+\beta
)-process

In
[
30
]
, the authors proved several bounds on the gap for the
(1+\beta)
process (in the setting without batches) where balls are sampled from a weight distribution with constant
\lambda>0
as defined in
Section

2.3
.
In the second part of
[
30
]
, the authors used a majorization argument to deduce gap bounds for graphical balanced allocation. However, due to the involved majorization argument not working for weights, all results for graphical allocation in
[
30
]
assume balls are unweighted. This lack of results for weighted graphical allocations is summarized as Open Question 1 in
[
30
]
. By leveraging the results in previous sections, we are able to fill this “gap”.

For a
d
-regular (and connected) graph, let us define the conductance as:

\Phi(G):=\min_{S\subseteq V\colon 1\leqslant|S|\leqslant n/2}\frac{|E(S,V\setminus S)|}{|S|\cdot d}.

We will call a family of graphs an
expander
, if
\Phi
is at least a constant bounded below from
0
(as
n\rightarrow\infty
).

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider
Graphical
on a
d
-regular graph with conductance
\Phi
with batch size
b=1
. Then for any
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
, the probability vector
p^{t}
in round
t\geqslant 0
satisfies for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant n/2
,

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{t}\leqslant(1-\Phi)\cdot\frac{k}{n},

and similarly, for any
n/2+1\leqslant k\leqslant n
,

\sum_{i=k}^{n}p_{i}^{t}\geqslant(1+\Phi)\cdot\frac{n-k+1}{n}.

Further,
\max_{i\in[n]}p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{d}{n}
. Thus, the vector
p^{t}
satisfies condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
with
\delta=1/2
,
\varepsilon=\Phi
and condition
\mathcal{C}_{2}
with
C=d
.

The proof of this lemma closely follows
[
30
, Proof of Theorem 3.2]
.

Proof.

Fix any load vector
x^{t}
in round
t
. Consider any
1\leqslant k\leqslant n/2
. Let
S_{k}
be the
k
“heaviest” bins with the largest load. Hence in order to allocate a ball into
S_{k}
, both endpoints of the sampled edge must be in
S_{k}
, and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\cdot|E(S_{k},S_{k})|}{2\cdot|E|}

\displaystyle=\frac{|E(S_{k},V)|-|E(S_{k},V\setminus S_{k})|}{2\cdot|E|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|S_{k}|\cdot d-|S_{k}|\cdot\Phi\cdot d}{nd}=(1-\Phi)\cdot\frac{k}{n},

where the inequality used the definition of conductance
\Phi
. Hence
p^{t}
satisfies condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
with
\varepsilon=\Phi
.
Now, we will consider the suffix sums for
n/2+1\leqslant k\leqslant n
. We start by upper bounding the prefix sum up to
k-1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_{i}^{t}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|S_{k}|\cdot d-|V\setminus S_{k}|\cdot\Phi\cdot d}{nd}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(k-1)\cdot d-(n-k+1)\cdot\Phi\cdot d}{nd}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-1)-(n-k+1)\cdot\Phi}{n},

where the inequality used our assumption that
G
has expansion
\Phi>0
.
Hence the suffix sum is

\sum_{i=k}^{n}p_{i}^{t}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}p_{i}^{t}\geqslant 1-\frac{(k-1)-(n-k+1)\cdot\Phi}{n}=(1+\Phi)\cdot\frac{n-k+1}{n}.

Finally, we also know that for any bin
i\in[n]
,

p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{d}{n},

since in the worst-case we allocate a ball into bin
i
whenever an edge incident to
i
is chosen.
∎

The next result is for
Graphical
in the unbatched setting.

Theorem 6.2
.

Consider
Graphical
on a
d
-regular graph with conductance
\Phi>0
. Further, consider the non-batched setting, i.e.,
b=1
and assume that balls are sampled from a weight distribution with constant
\lambda>0
. Then there is a constant
k:=k(\lambda)>0
such that for any
m\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{m}|\leqslant k\cdot\frac{d}{\Phi}\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Proof.

Using
[
30
, Lemma 2.1]
, we have for
\alpha=\frac{\Phi}{16\cdot d\cdot S}
and some constant
S:=S(\lambda)>1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Phi_{i}^{t+1}\mid x^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha+dS\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big),

and using
[
30
, Lemma 2.3]
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Psi_{i}^{t+1}\mid x^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha+dS\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big).

Hence, applying
3.2
for
\varepsilon:=\Phi
,
\delta:=1/2
, we get for any
m\geqslant 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{m}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n,

for some constant
c:=c(\delta)>0
. Hence, by Markov’s inequality

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gamma^{m}\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n^{3}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

The event
\{\Gamma^{m}\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n^{3}\}
implies that

\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{m}|\leqslant\log\Big(\frac{8c}{\delta}\Big)+3\cdot\frac{16\cdot d\cdot S}{\Phi}\cdot\log n\leqslant k\cdot\frac{d}{\Phi}\cdot\log n,

for some constant
k:=k(\lambda)>0
.
∎

The next result is the batched version of
6.2
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Consider
Graphical
on a
d
-regular graph with conductance
\Phi>0
. Further, consider the batched setting with
b\geqslant n
and assume that balls are sampled from a weight distribution with constant
\lambda>0
. Then there is a constant
k:=k(\lambda)>0
such that it holds for any
m\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{m}|\leqslant k\cdot\frac{d^{2}}{\Phi}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Further, if the conductance
\Phi
is lower bounded by a constant
>0
(i.e.,
G
is an expander),
d>0
is constant and
n\leqslant b\leqslant n^{3}
, then there is a constant
k:=k(\lambda,d)>0
such that for any
m\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{1}^{m}\leqslant k\cdot\left(\frac{b}{n}+\log n\right)\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Note that our first gap bound for constant
d>0
, generalizes
[
30
, Theorem 3.2]
, which is a gap bound of
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log n}{\Phi})
in the setting without batches and weights. Similarly, our second result extends the
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
bound from
[
30
]
for expanders, and proves that the same gap bound applies in the weighted batched setting with any
b=\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
.

Proof.

The first result follows directly from
6.1
and
4.2
.
For the second result,
\varepsilon=\Phi
is a constant
>0
, and we can apply the refined gap bound from
5.1
.
∎

Next we improve the upper bound on the gap for
(1+\beta)
for very small
\beta
. In
[
30
, Corollary 2.12]
, it was shown that this gap is
\mathcal{O}(\log n/\beta+\log(1/\beta)/\beta)
. For
1/\beta=n^{\omega(1)}
, the second term dominates. We improve this gap bound to
\mathcal{O}(\log n/\beta)
. This is tight up to multiplicative constants for
\beta\leqslant 1/2
, due to a lower bound of
\Omega(\log n/\beta)
as shown in
[
30
, Section 4]
.

Theorem 6.4
.

Consider the
(1+\beta)
process for any
\beta\in(0,1]
. Then there exists a constant
k>0
, such for any
m\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\leqslant k\cdot\frac{\log n}{\beta}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Proof.

Using
[
30
, Lemma 2.1]
, we have for
\alpha=\frac{\beta}{4}
and some constant
S>1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Phi_{i}^{t+1}\mid x^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Phi_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\Big(p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\alpha+2S\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big),

and using
[
30
, Lemma 2.3]
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Delta\Psi_{i}^{t+1}\mid x^{t}\,\right]\leqslant\Psi_{i}^{t}\cdot\Big(\Big(\frac{1}{n}-p_{i}\Big)\cdot\alpha+2S\cdot\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}\Big).

By
2.2
, the
1+\beta
process satisfies the
\mathcal{C}_{1}
condition for
\varepsilon=\frac{\beta}{4}
and
\delta=\frac{1}{4}
. By
3.2
, there exists
c:=c(\delta)>0
such that for any
m\geqslant 1

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma^{m}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{8c}{\delta}\cdot n.

Hence, using Markov’s inequality

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gamma^{m}\leqslant n^{4}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Note that when
\{\Gamma^{m}\leqslant n^{4}\}
holds, we have

\Gap(m)\leqslant\frac{4}{\alpha}\cdot\log n=4\cdot\frac{8\cdot(2S)}{\beta\delta}\cdot\log n=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\log n}{\beta}\Big).\qed

7
Lower Bounds

For the lower bounds we always assume that balls are unweighted (or equivalently, have unit weight). We recall the following result which assumes no batching, i.e., balls are allocated sequentially using perfect knowledge about the bin loads.

Lemma 7.1

(
[
25
, Theorem 10.4]
)
.

Consider any allocation process in the unweighted setting with probability vector
q
with
\min_{i\in[n]}q_{i}\geqslant C/n
for some constant
C>0
. Then there exists a constant
k>0
, such that for
m=\Theta(n\log n)
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant k\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

We use the following majorization result from
[
25
]
(see also
[
30
, Section 3]
).

Lemma 7.2

(Lemma 4.13 in
[
25
]
)
.

Consider two allocation processes
Q
and
P
. The allocation process
Q
uses at each round a fixed allocation distribution
q
. The allocation process
P
uses a time-dependent allocation distribution
p^{t}
, which may depend on
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
but majorizes
q
at each round
t\geqslant 0
.
Let
y^{t}(Q)
and
y^{t}(P)
be the two normalized load vectors, sorted decreasingly. Then there is a coupling such that for all rounds
t\geqslant 0
,
y^{t}(P)
majorizes
y^{t}(Q)
.

Combining the two lemmas above, we can now prove a lower bound which holds for any batch size:

Proposition 7.3
.

Consider any allocation process with probability vector
p
with
\min_{i\in[n]}p_{i}\geqslant C/n
for some constant
C>0
, in the unweighted batched setting for any
b\geqslant 1
. Then there exists a constant
k>0
such that for
m=\Theta(n\log n)
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(m)\geqslant k\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Note that this statement applies to the
(1+\beta)
-process for constant
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
and constant
\delta\in(0,1)
, but it does not apply to
Two-Choice
.

Proof.

For the purpose of this lower bound derivation, we assume that the batched setting allocates all
m
balls sequentially in rounds
t=1,2,\ldots,m
. As the load information does not get updated within each batch of size
b
, this means that the allocation made in each round is described by an allocation vector
p^{t}
, which depends on
t
but also on the history of the process, i.e.,
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
.

Let
q
be the vector
p
sorted in non-decreasing order. Then, at each round
p^{t}
majorizes
q
, since the outdated information implies that
p^{t}
is a permutation of
q
.

We apply Lemma
7.2
with
p^{t}
and
q
as defined above. Hence for
t=m
, there is a coupling such that the load vector
y^{m}(P)
majorizes
y^{m}(Q)
, in particular,

y_{1}^{m}(P)\geqslant y_{1}^{m}(Q),

which is equivalent to
\Gap(P,m)\geqslant\Gap(Q,m)
. Hence the statement of the lemma follows by
7.1
.
∎

Proposition 7.4
.

Consider any allocation process with probability vector
p
with
\max_{i\in[n]}p_{i}\geqslant\frac{C}{n}
for some
C>1
, in the unweighted batched setting with
b\geqslant n\log n
. Then, for
\gamma:=\min(C-1,0.5)
, any bin
j=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{i\in[n]}p_{i}
satisfies

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{j}^{b}\geqslant\frac{\gamma}{4}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\gamma^{2}/8}.

Proof.

For convenience, let us define
\gamma:=\min(C-1,0.5)
, so
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
.
Note that during the first batch consisting of
b\geqslant n\log n
balls, the load vector is never updated and all balls are allocated using the same probability vector
p
. Hence
each ball will be allocated into some bin
i
with probability
\frac{C}{n}
, independently. Let
X:=x_{i}^{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{b}X_{j}
, where the
X_{j}
’s are independent Bernoulli random variables with
\mathbf{E}\left[\,X_{j}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{1+\gamma}{n}
. Hence
\mathbf{E}\left[\,X\,\right]\geqslant b\cdot\frac{1+\gamma}{n}
. Using the following Chernoff bound, which states that for any
\lambda>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\leqslant(1-\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,X\,\right]\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\lambda^{2}/2\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\,X\,\right]\right).

Picking
\lambda=\gamma/2
implies

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{i}^{b}\leqslant(1-\gamma/2)\cdot(1+\gamma)\cdot\frac{b}{n}\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\right)\leqslant n^{-\gamma^{2}/8},

where the last inequality used our assumption that
b\geqslant n\log n
.
If
x_{i}^{b}\geqslant(1-\gamma/2)\cdot(1+\gamma)\cdot\frac{b}{n}
, then this implies for the normalised load,

y_{j}^{b}=x_{i}^{b}-\frac{b}{n}\geqslant\frac{\gamma}{2}\cdot\frac{b}{n}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\geqslant\frac{\gamma}{4}\cdot\frac{b}{n},

where the last inequality used
\gamma\leqslant 1/2
. ∎

The lemma above can be applied to any process satisfying condition
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, so unlike
7.3
, it applies to
Two-Choice
.

Note that for
d
-Choice
,
\max_{i\in[n]}p_{i}\approx\frac{d}{n}
. Hence this lower bound shows that, in sharp contrast to the classical setting without batches, that large values of
d
lead to a worse performance. This is explained by the higher bias towards underloaded bins, which when given no (or outdated) information about the bins, will lead to a larger gap.

Let us remark that in the proof above, we assumed that the allocation process uses the same probability vector and bin labeling in all rounds of the same batch. In particular, this analysis does not apply to
Two-Choice
with random tie-breaking. However,
Two-Choice
with random tie-breaking will allocate all balls in the first batch following
One-Choice
. Exploiting this, we can then prove that by the end of the batch, there is a unique bin which attains the minimum load if
b=\Omega(n\log n)
, which means for the second batch we can apply
7.4
, and conclude that a lower bound of
\Omega(\frac{b}{n})
holds with constant probability
>0
.

First, we will make use of the following property of
n
independent Poisson random variables:

Lemma 7.5
.

Consider any
n\geqslant 2
and
\lambda\geqslant 16\cdot\log n
. Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent Poisson random variables with
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)
, and denote by for
Y_{(n)},Y_{(n-1)}
the smallest and second smallest of the
X_{i}
’s. Then there exist constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0
such that,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Y_{(n-1)}-Y_{(n)}\geqslant\kappa_{1}\cdot\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}\,\right]\geqslant\kappa_{2}.

Proof.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)
, where
\lambda:=m/n\geqslant 16\cdot\log n
. Let
k\geqslant 0
be the minimal integer such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)\leqslant k\,\right]\geqslant n^{-1}.

By
A.2
for
\delta:=\sqrt{4\cdot\lambda^{-1}\cdot\log n}
, we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\leqslant\lambda-\sqrt{4\cdot\lambda\cdot\log n}\,\right]\leqslant e^{-\lambda\cdot\delta^{2}/2}=e^{-2\log n}=n^{-2}.

Hence it follows that
k\geqslant\lambda-2\cdot\sqrt{\lambda\cdot\log n}
.
Next note that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)=k+1\,\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)=k\,\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{k+1},

(7.1)

which, since
k\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\lambda
(as
\lambda\geqslant 16\log n
), also implies that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)\leqslant k\,\right]\leqslant 2\cdot n^{-1}.

Our next claim is that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)=k\,\right]\leqslant 2\cdot n^{-1}\cdot 1/\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}.

We will now derive this claim.
We have

\displaystyle 2n^{-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)\leqslant k\,\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)=j\,\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)=k\,\right]\cdot\prod_{i=j}^{k-1}\frac{i}{\lambda}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)=k\,\right]\cdot\left(\frac{k-\sqrt{\lambda}}{\lambda}\right)^{\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geqslant}}\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)=k\,\right]\cdot\left(\frac{\lambda-3\sqrt{\lambda\log n}}{\lambda}\right)^{\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)=k\,\right]\cdot\left(1-\frac{3}{\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}}\right)^{\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\geqslant}}\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)=k\,\right]\cdot c_{1},

for some constant
c_{1}>0
, where in
(a)
we used
Eq.

7.1
and in
(b)
we used that
k\geqslant\lambda-2\sqrt{\lambda\log n}
, and in
(c)
that
\lambda\geqslant 16\log n
.

Next we wish to upper bound

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)\leqslant k+c_{2}\cdot\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}\,\right],

for some constant
c_{2}>0
.
Note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)\leqslant k+c_{2}\cdot\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}\,\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)\leqslant k\,\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{c_{2}\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}}\frac{\lambda^{i}}{k\cdot(k+1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(k+i-1)}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)=k\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2n^{-1}+c_{2}\cdot\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{c_{2}\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}}}{k^{c_{2}\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}}}\cdot 2\cdot n^{-1}\cdot 1/\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}

\displaystyle=2n^{-1}+2c_{2}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{c\sqrt{\lambda\cdot\log n}}\right)^{-c_{2}\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}}\cdot n^{-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{3}\cdot n^{-1},

(7.2)

for another constant
c_{3}>0
, where
(a)
is due to
Eq.

7.1
.

We now use the principle of deferred decisions when exposing the
n
independent Poisson variables with mean
\lambda
denoted by
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
one by one. Let
\tau:=\min\{j\colon X_{j}\leqslant k\}
.
With probability
1-(1-1/n)^{n}\geqslant 1-1/e
, we have
\tau<n
. Conditional on that,
X_{\tau+1},\ldots,X_{n}
are still
n-\tau
independent Poisson variables with mean
\lambda
. Due to
Eq.

7.2
, the probability that all of the following Poisson random variables are larger than
k+c_{2}\cdot\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}
is at least

\left(1-c_{3}\cdot n^{-1}\right)^{\tau}\geqslant\left(1-c_{3}\cdot n^{-1}\right)^{n}\geqslant c_{4},

where
c_{4}>0
is another constant.

Hence with probability at least
(1-1/e)\cdot c_{4}
, we have a gap of at least
c_{2}\cdot\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}
between
Y_{(n-1)}
and
Y_{(n)}
.
∎

We can now derive the lower bound for allocation processes with random tie-breaking.

Lemma 7.6
.

Consider an allocation process with probability vector
p
and random tie-breaking, such that
p_{n}\geqslant\frac{C}{n}
for some constant
C\in(1,1.5]
in the unweighted batched setting with
b\geqslant\frac{384}{(C-1)^{2}}n\log n
. Then, there exist constants
\kappa_{1}:=\kappa_{1}(C),\kappa_{2}:=\kappa_{2}(C)>0
, such that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(2b)\geqslant\frac{C-1}{8}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\,\right]\geqslant\kappa_{2}.

Proof.

Initially, all bins have load
0
, so the first
b
balls will be allocated using
One-Choice
. In order to use the Poisson Approximation Method
[
28
, Theorem 5.6]
, let
\widetilde{X}_{1},\widetilde{X}_{2},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{n}
be
n
independent Poisson distributed random variables with rate
\lambda=(b-4\cdot\sqrt{b})/n
. By
A.1
, the sum
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{X}_{i}
is in the range
[b-8\sqrt{b},b]
, with probability at least
1-o(1)
. By
7.5
, we have that with at least constant
\kappa_{2}>0
probability, the difference between the smallest and second smallest bin is at least
\kappa_{1}\cdot\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}
, for some constant
\kappa_{1}>0
.

Consider now the allocation of the remaining
b-S_{n}\leqslant 8\sqrt{b}
balls. The average load of a bin through these balls is
8\sqrt{b}/n
. Using Markov’s inequality, the smallest bin does not receive more than
16\sqrt{b}/n
additional balls with probability at least
1/2
.

Since
\kappa_{1}\cdot\sqrt{\lambda/\log n}\geqslant\kappa_{1}\cdot\sqrt{0.5\cdot b/n\cdot 1/\log n}\geqslant 16\sqrt{b}/n
we can conclude that there is still a unique minimally loaded bin after the allocation of all
b
balls. Further, by using a Chernoff bound for
One-Choice
, it follows that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{n}^{b}\leqslant b/n-\sqrt{6\cdot b/n\log n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Taking the union bound, we conclude that at the end of the first batch, the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,y_{n}^{b}\in[-\sqrt{6\cdot b/n\log n},y_{n-1}^{b}-1]\,\right]\geqslant\kappa_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}-o(1)-n^{-2}.

(7.3)

Conditioning on
y_{n}^{b}\leqslant y_{n-1}^{b}-1
, we have
\widetilde{p}_{n}(x^{b})\geqslant p_{n}\geqslant\frac{C}{n}
. For simplicity, let us fix label
n
to be the index of the bin with smallest load at time
b
. Applying
7.4
to the allocations made in the second batch to bin
n
, we conclude that there is a constant
\gamma>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{n}^{2b}-x_{n}^{b}\geqslant\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)\cdot\frac{b}{n}~\Big|~y_{n}^{b}\in[-\sqrt{6\cdot b/n\log n},y_{n-1}^{b}-1]\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-\gamma^{2}/8}.

(7.4)

Both events in
Eq.

7.3
and
Eq.

7.4
hold with probability at least
\kappa_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{3}
, and in this case,

\displaystyle x_{n}^{2b}

\displaystyle=x_{n}^{b}+x_{n}^{2b}-x_{n}^{b}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{b}{n}-\sqrt{6\cdot b/n\log n}+\left(1+\frac{C-1}{4}\right)\cdot\frac{b}{n}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{2b}{n}-\sqrt{6\cdot b/n\log n}+\frac{C-1}{4}\cdot\frac{b}{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geqslant}}\frac{2b}{n}+\frac{C-1}{8}\cdot\frac{b}{n},

where we have used in
(a)
that if
b\geqslant\frac{384}{(C-1)^{2}}n\log n
then,

\frac{C-1}{4}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\geqslant 2\cdot\sqrt{6\cdot b/n\log n}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad b\geqslant\frac{384}{(C-1)^{2}}\cdot n\log n.

Hence
\Gap(2b)\geqslant\frac{C-1}{8}\cdot\frac{b}{n}
.
∎

9
Conclusions

In this work, we studied balanced allocations in a batched setting, following the model proposed in
[
7
]
. As our main result, we proved that for any batch size
n\leqslant b\leqslant n^{3}
and
m\geqslant n
, a gap bound of
\mathcal{O}(b/n+\log n)
holds with high probability. This analysis covered both weighted balls and a number of allocation processes satisfying two mild conditions on their probability vector, thereby demonstrating that many of the sequential allocation processes perform well in the batched setting, and can thus be “parallelized”. We also proved lower bounds which match our upper bound up to multiplicative constants for a family of processes.

Our results also imply a slight improvement on the gap for the
(1+\beta)
-process with very small
\beta
. Further, we proved the first gap bounds for graphical allocation with weights, thereby addressing Open Question 1 in
[
30
]
.

A natural open problem is to investigate other batch sizes, e.g.,
b<n
or
b=\Omega(n^{3})
, or consider a dynamic setting where the batch sizes may vary over time. Our new bounds for graphical allocation crucially depend on how much the maximum probability deviates from
1/n
, and thus on the maximum degree of the graph. Improving this dependence may lead to stronger bounds for dense graphs.

The experimental results exhibit an interesting trade-off between the probability vector and the achieved gap; having small probabilities for the heavily loaded bins is not significant for large
b
, but more important is to avoid large probabilities for the lightly loaded bins, which is achieved by processes like
\textsc{Quantile}((\log n)^{-1})
or
(1+\beta)
for
\beta=(\log n)^{-1}
.
In other words, following a more powerful process with more choices like
d
-Choice
leads to worse performance than a more “indifferent” allocation scheme like
(1+\beta)
with
\beta=(\log n)^{-1}
.

Recall that our lower bound in
7.4

sheds some light onto this phenomenon, but further bounds are needed (in particular, refined upper bounds based on
\max_{i\in[n]}p_{i}
) so that we can rigorously compare the performance of these processes.

A.1
Concentration inequalities

The first lemma is a standard Chernoff bound for sum of independent random variables whose moment generating function is bounded.

Lemma A.1
.

Assume
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{k}
are independent samples from a distribution
W
, for which there is a constant
\lambda>0
such that
\mathbf{E}\left[\,W\,\right]=1
and
\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\lambda W}\,]\leqslant S
. Then for
Z:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}Z_{i}
, it holds for that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z\geqslant 2\ln(S)/\lambda\cdot k\,\right]\leqslant\exp\left(-\ln(S)\cdot k\right).

Furthermore, for the special case
k=1
, we have for any
c>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z_{1}\geqslant 1/\lambda\cdot(c\cdot\ln(n)+\ln(S))\,\right]\leqslant n^{-c}.

Proof.

Let
t\in(0,\lambda]
to be specified later. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z\geqslant 2\ln(S)/\lambda\cdot k\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,e^{tZ}\geqslant e^{t\cdot 2\ln(S)/\lambda\cdot k}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{tZ}\,\right]\cdot\exp\left(-t\cdot 2\log(S)/\lambda\cdot k\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{tZ_{1}}\,\right]\right)^{k}\cdot\exp\left(-t\cdot 2\log(S)/\lambda\cdot k\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\lambda Z_{1}}\,]\right)^{k\cdot t/\lambda}\cdot\exp\left(-t\cdot 2\log(S)/\lambda\cdot k\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant S^{k\cdot t/\lambda}\cdot\exp\left(-t\cdot 2\ln(S)/\lambda\cdot k\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(k\cdot(\ln(S)\cdot t/\lambda-t\cdot 2\ln(S)/\lambda)\right),

where the second inequality is due to Jensen’s inequality. Choosing
t=\lambda
yields the claim.

For the second statement, for any
c>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,Z_{1}\geqslant 1/\lambda\cdot(c\cdot\ln(n)+\ln(S))\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,e^{\lambda Z_{1}}\geqslant e^{-c\cdot\ln(n)-\ln(S)}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\lambda W}\,\right]\cdot e^{-c\cdot\ln(n)-\ln(S)}

\displaystyle\leqslant S\cdot n^{-c}\cdot\frac{1}{S}=n^{-c}.\qed

Next we state a Chernoff bound for Poisson random variables.

Lemma A.2

(Theorem 5.4 from
[
28
]
)
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda)
, then for any
0<\delta<1
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\leqslant(1-\delta)\cdot\lambda\,\right]\leqslant e^{-\lambda\delta^{2}/2},

and

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\geqslant(1+\delta)\cdot\lambda\,\right]\leqslant e^{-\lambda\delta^{2}/3}.

Following
[
21
]
, we will now give the definition for
strongly difference-bounded
and then give the statement for a bounded differences inequality with bad events.

Definition A.3

(Strongly difference-bounded – Definition 1.6 in
[
21
]
)
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\ldots\Omega_{N}
be probability spaces. Let
\Omega=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Omega_{k}
and let
X
be a random variable on
\Omega
. We say that
X
is strongly difference-bounded by
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\xi)
if the following holds: there is a “bad” subset
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\Omega
, where
\xi=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\omega\in\mathcal{B}\,\right]
. If
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega
differ only in the
k
-th coordinate, and
\omega\notin B
, then

|X(\omega)-X(\omega^{\prime})|\leqslant\eta_{2}.

Furthermore, for any
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
differing only in the
k
-th coordinate,

|X(\omega)-X(\omega^{\prime})|\leqslant\eta_{1}.

Theorem A.4

(Theorem 3.3 in
[
21
]
)
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{N}
be probability spaces. Let
\Omega=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\Omega_{k}
, and let
X
be a random variable on
\Omega
which is strongly difference-bounded by
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\xi)
. Let
\mu=\mathbf{E}[\,X\,]
. Then for any
\lambda>0
and any

,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\geqslant\mu+\lambda\,\right]\leqslant\exp\Big(-\frac{-\lambda^{2}}{2\cdot\sum_{k\in[N]}(\eta_{2}+\eta_{1}\gamma_{k})^{2}}\Big)+\xi\cdot\sum_{k\in[N]}\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}.

A.2
Auxiliary Probabilistic Claims

We give a proof for the well-known fact that when
\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\lambda W}\,\right]<\infty
then
\mathbf{E}[\,W^{4}\,]
is also bounded.

Lemma A.5
.

Consider a random variable
W
with
\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\lambda W}\,\right]<\infty
for some
\lambda>0
. then

\mathbf{E}\left[\,W^{4}\,\right]<\left(\Big(\frac{8}{\lambda}\Big)\cdot\log\Big(\frac{8}{\lambda}\Big)\right)^{4}+\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\lambda W}\,\right].

Proof.

Let
\kappa:=(8/\lambda)\cdot\log(8/\lambda)
. Consider
x\geqslant\max(0,\kappa)=:\kappa^{*}
. Then

e^{\lambda x/4}=e^{\lambda x/8}\cdot e^{\lambda x/8}\geqslant e^{\log(8/\lambda)}\cdot e^{\lambda x/8}\geqslant\frac{8}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\lambda x}{8}=x,

using that
e^{z}\geqslant z
for any
z
. Hence,

e^{\lambda x}=(e^{\lambda x/4})^{4}\geqslant x^{4}.

Hence, if
p_{x}
is the pdf of
W
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,W^{4}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{x=0}^{\infty}x^{4}\cdot p_{x}dx=\int_{x=0}^{\kappa^{*}}x^{4}\cdot p_{x}dx+\int_{x=\kappa^{*}}^{\infty}x^{4}\cdot p_{x}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{x=0}^{\kappa^{*}}\kappa^{4}\cdot p_{x}dx+\int_{x=\kappa^{*}}^{\infty}e^{\lambda x}\cdot p_{x}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\kappa^{4}\cdot\int_{x=0}^{\infty}p_{x}dx+\int_{x=0}^{\infty}e^{\lambda x}\cdot p_{x}dx=\kappa^{4}+\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\lambda W}\,\right].\qed

Next we state an inequality for a sequence of random variables, related through a recurrence inequality.

Lemma A.6
.

Consider a sequence of random variables
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that there are
0<a<1
and
b>0
such that every
i\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\mid Z_{i-1}\,\right]\leqslant Z_{i-1}\cdot a+b.

Then for every
i\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\mid Z_{0}\,\right]\leqslant Z_{0}\cdot a^{i}+\frac{b}{1-a}.

Proof.

We will prove by induction that for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\mid Z_{0}\,\right]\leqslant Z_{0}\cdot a^{i}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a^{j}.

For
i=0
,
\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{0}\mid Z_{0}\,\right]\leqslant Z_{0}
. Assuming the induction hypothesis holds for some
i\geqslant 0
, then since
a>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i+1}\mid Z_{0}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i+1}\mid Z_{i}\,\right]\mid Z_{0}\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,Z_{i}\mid Z_{0}\,\right]\cdot a+b

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(Z_{0}\cdot a^{i}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a^{j}\Big)\cdot a+b

\displaystyle=Z_{0}\cdot a^{i+1}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i}a^{j}.

The claims follows using that for
a\in(0,1)
,
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a^{j}=\frac{1}{1-a}
.
∎

A.3
Auxiliary Non-Probabilistic Claims

For the next lemma, we define for two
n
-dimensional vectors
x,y
,
\langle x,y\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\cdot y_{i}
.

Lemma A.7
.

Let
(p_{k})_{k=1}^{n},(q_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
be two probability vectors and
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
be non-negative and non-increasing. Then if
p
majorizes
q
, i.e., for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
,
\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}
holds, then

\langle p,c\rangle\geqslant\langle q,c\rangle.

Proof.

We will consider a sequence of moves between
p
and
q
, which gradually moves probability mass from lower to higher coordinates. Specifically, we define the following sequence:

\displaystyle r^{1}

\displaystyle=(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4},\ldots,p_{n})=p

\displaystyle r^{2}

\displaystyle=(q_{1},p_{2}+(p_{1}-q_{1}),p_{3},p_{4},\ldots,p_{n})

\displaystyle r^{3}

\displaystyle=(q_{1},q_{2},p_{3}+(p_{1}+p_{2}-q_{1}-q_{2}),p_{4},\ldots,p_{n})

\displaystyle r^{4}

\displaystyle=(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},p_{4}+(p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}-q_{1}-q_{2}-q_{3}),\ldots,p_{n})

\displaystyle r^{n}

\displaystyle=\bigg(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},\ldots,q_{n-1},p_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(p_{i}-q_{i})\bigg)=q,

where in the last equation we used
p_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(p_{i}-q_{i})=p_{n}-p_{n}+q_{n}=q_{n}
.

For any
1\leqslant k<n
, since
r^{k}
and
r^{k+1}
differ only in the
k
-th and
(k+1)
-st coordinate, and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(p_{i}-q_{i})\geqslant 0
, we conclude
it follows that

\displaystyle\langle r^{k},c\rangle-\langle r^{k+1},c\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant r_{k}^{k}c_{k}+r_{k+1}^{k}c_{k+1}-r_{k}^{k+1}c_{k}+r_{k+1}^{k+1}c_{k+1}

\displaystyle=c_{k}\cdot\bigg(\Big(p_{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(p_{i}-q_{i})\Big)-q_{k}\bigg)+c_{k+1}\cdot\bigg(p_{k+1}-\Big(p_{k+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}(p_{i}-q_{i})\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=(c_{k}-c_{k+1})\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}(p_{i}-q_{i})

\displaystyle\geqslant 0.

Hence
\langle p,c\rangle=\langle r^{1},c\rangle\geqslant\langle r^{2},c\rangle\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\langle r^{n},c\rangle=\langle q,c\rangle
.

∎

Lemma A.8
.

The function
f(z)=z\cdot e^{k/z}
for
k>0
, is decreasing for
z\in(0,k]
.

Proof.

By differentiating,

f^{\prime}(z)=e^{k/z}-z\cdot e^{k/z}\cdot\frac{k}{z^{2}}=e^{k/z}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{k}{z}\Big).

For
z\in(0,k]
,
f^{\prime}(z)\leqslant 0
, so
f
is decreasing.
∎
