Pontryagin Duality for Modules over Compact Discrete Valuation Rings
by Milo Moses

Abstract
We establish an analogue of Pontryagin duality for modules over compact discrete valuation rings R R . Namely, we define the dual of a topological R R module to be its continuous R R -module homomorphisms into K / R K/R , the quotient module of the fraction field by its ring of integers. It is established that for locally compact R R -modules the double dual map is an isomorphism and homeomorphism. Additionally, given a non-topological R R -module a canonical topology is constructed, uniquely defined so that the double dual map will be injective and continuous. Finally, the functor assigning a module to itself equipped with canonical topology is shown to be fully faithful, allowing one to recontextualize the topological statements in purely algebraic forms.



1
Introduction

The modern field of Harmonic Analysis has its roots in the 1930s, with Lev Pontryagin’s generalization of the Fourier transform to the more general setting of locally compact abelian groups. Namely, he showed certain Haar integrals relate the space of functions on a locally compact abelian group
M
to the space of functions on a dual group
M^{\wedge}
, defined to be the space of continuous group morphisms into the circle group
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.

In the 1950s and 60s, the burgeoning field of
p
-adic analysis was gaining steam with the proof of Mahler’s theorem on Newton series
[
18
]
, and Kubota-Leopoldt’s introduction of
p
-adic L-functions
[
16
]
. This led to the very natural question of introducing a notion of Fourier transformation in the
p
-adic setting. The main problem was that
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
does not admit a
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-valued Haar measure. This was fixed in the 70s, when C. F. Woodcock introduced a best-possible
p
-adic valued invariant integral on
\mathbb{Z}_{p}

[
24
]
.

Later that year Woodcock introduced a
p
-adic Fourier theory on
\mathbb{Z}_{p}

[
23
]
. This was extended to a
p
-adic Fourier theory on
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
in G Borm’s thesis
[
6
]
. Early works of Amice
[
1
]
and modern generalizations such as
[
22
]
also treat the case of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
in much detail. Nobody has achieved Woodcock’s original dream, however, that these results “might be a special case of a ‘Harmonic Analysis’ for the category of locally compact
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-modules”.

In this paper we reinterpret classical Pontryagin duality in a strongly
p
-adic fashion, which allows us to prove strong results about the category of locally compact
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-modules. These are potentially useful not only for the development of a
p
-adic Harmonic Analysis but also in other fields where
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-modules are present. For example, the Iwasawa theory of Elliptic curves is an extremely important field in modern mathematics. The
p
-adic L functions of Elliptic curves are expressed as characteristic ideals of the duals of Selmer groups. This paper allows one to understand duals of Selmer groups more properly. In particular, one will know that the double dual will be an isomorphism even when the Selmer group is not cofinitely generated.

This paper performs all of these computations in the more general setting of modules over a compact Discrete Valuation Ring (DVR). Compact DVRs include not only rings of integers of finite extensions of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, but also completed polynomial rings over finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket
. It has long been understood that modules over these rings had the potential for better duality theories than most.

The first results in this direction came with Kaplansky’s 1952 treatment of discrete modules over compact DVRs
[
15
]
. He proved that discrete modules are dual to compact modules, and that applying duality twice gives the identity. More work was done in this area in
[
11
]
, and the paper
[
8
]
gave related results about discrete modules over some pseudocompact algebras. No paper, however, is able to deal with general modules over compact DVRs.

It is important to note that these is a divide in the literature between papers that define the dual of an
R
-module to be its continuous
\mathbb{Z}
-module homomorphisms into
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, and those which define the dual of a module to be its continuous
R
-module homomorphisms into
K/R
, where
K
is the field of fractions of
R
. This paper is the first to remark that these two spaces are functorially isomorphic when
R
is compact DVR.

2
Statement of Results

The primary objects of interest in this paper are Hausdorff topological modules over compact Discrete Valuation Rings (DVRs). A DVR is a local ring whose maximal ideal is principal. Such rings have a natural metric topology induced by their valuation, with a basis of neighborhoods of
0
given by powers of the maximal ideal. By compact DVR we refer to those rings which are compact spaces when considered with this topology. It is a standard result that a DVR is compact if and only if it is complete and has finite residue field: see e.g.,
[
20
]
Chapter 2 § 1 Proposition 1 for a reference.

A topological module over a topological ring
A
refers to an
A
-module
M
endowed with a Hausdorff topology such that the addition map
M\times M\xrightarrow{}M
, the inversion map
M\xrightarrow{}M
, and the scalar multiplication map
A\times M\xrightarrow{}M
are all continuous. We note that in general giving
M
the discrete topology will not necessarily produce a topological module, as the map
A\times M\xrightarrow{}M
need not be continuous.

Locally compact topological modules are of particular interest due to the storied history of locally compact topological abelian groups, i.e.,
\mathbb{Z}
-modules. They were first studied in
[
19
]
, where the following famous duality theorem was proved:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
M
be a locally compact abelian group. Define
\widehat{M}
to be the space of continuous group homomorphisms
M\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
endowed with the compact-open topology. Then,
\widehat{M}
is locally compact and the natural map

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}\widehat{\widehat{M}}

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\varphi\mapsto\varphi(m)\right)

is an isomorphism of groups and a homeomorphism of topological spaces.

One of the main purposes of this article is to prove an analogue of Theorem
2.1
in the case of compact DVRs. To do this, we fix the following notation:
R
is a compact DVR, with a distinguished uniformizing element
\pi
. We fix
K
to be the fraction field of
R
. The absolute value on
R
extends to
K
, endowing
K
with the structure of a (complete, compact) topological field. We fix
T
to be the
R
-module quotient
K/R
endowed with quotient topology, a local analogue of
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
M
be a locally compact
R
-module. Define
\widehat{M}
to be the space of continuous
R
-module homomorphisms
M\xrightarrow{}T
endowed with the compact-open topology. Then,
\widehat{M}
is locally compact and the natural map

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}\widehat{\widehat{M}}

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\varphi\mapsto\varphi(m)\right)

is an isomorphism of
R
-modules and a homeomorphism of topological spaces.

Other results obtained about locally compact
R
-modules reveal a distinctly different structure to that of abelian groups. In particular, we have the following:

Theorem 2.3
.

Modules over compact DVRs have at most one topology that make them locally compact topological modules.

This statement fails immediately for abelian groups, where any non-discrete locally compact module could also be given the discrete topology. An example of this theorem in action is as follows: Let
K/\mathbb{Q}_{p}
be a finite extension, and
R=\mathcal{O}_{K}
be its ring of integers. Then
R
is a compact DVR, and as such has a natural metric space topology. Additionally, the inclusion
\mathbb{Z}_{p}\hookrightarrow R
endows
R
with the structure of a topological
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module. Every finitely generated torsion-free module over a DVR is free, and hence we have a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module isomorphism
R\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{e}
for some
e\geq 1
. The question is whether or not the product topology on
r
copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and the metric space topology on
R
agree. Seeing as they are both locally compact, Theorem
2.3
immediately tells us they are the same. This could also be derived through a longer low-tech direct computation. This method of decomposing into components and applying the product topology is very powerful for all finitely generated modules. In particular, we have the following:

Theorem 2.4
.

For each finitely generated
R
-module
M
, there is a unique topology on
M
turning it into a topological module. If
M\cong R
this topology is obtained endowing
R
with its metric space topology. If
M\cong R/\mathfrak{m}^{n}
for some
n\geq 1
, where
\mathfrak{m}
is the maximal ideal of
R
, then this topology is discrete. If
M\times N
is the product of non-trivial modules, then this topology is equal to the product topology on
M
and
N
.

For more general modules, one cannot eliminate the possibility of there being multiple topological module structures on a given algebraic base. However, a canonical choice still distinguishes itself:

Theorem 2.5
.

Given an
R
-module
M
, the following topologies are all well defined and equivalent:

1.

The direct limit topology arising from the equality
M=\varinjlim_{S\leq M}S
, where
S
runs over finitely generated submodules of
M
, each given the unique topological module structure of Theorem
2.4
.

2.

The unique finest topological module structure on
M
, i.e., the unique structure such that every set open in any topological module structure on
M
is open in this one.

3.

The unique topological module structure on
M
for which the double dual map
M\xrightarrow{}M^{\wedge\wedge}
is injective and continuous.

We refer to this topology as the canonical topology on
M
, or refer to
M
as canonical.

With the above results in mind, it is clear that every locally compact topological module has the canonical topology: Their double dual maps are all injective and continuous by Theorem
2.2
, and hence canonical by point (3) of Theorem
2.5
. Thus, the statement that there is at most one locally compact topology in Theorem
2.3
is a simple corollary of the fact that the canonical topology is well defined and unique.

In general the double dual map on canonical modules will not be surjective, with failures as simple as
M=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}R
, the direct sum of infinitely many copies of
R
. The computation of this fact is not difficult, with the only apparent difficulty coming from the problem of determining which maps in the dual are continuous. This is no problem at all, however, because of the following theorem:

Theorem 2.6
.

Every homomorphism between canonical
R
-modules is continuous. In particular, the functor defined by sending an
R
-module to itself equipped with canonical topology is fully faithful.

This is useful, since it allows us to ignore topology in computations with canonical modules and use purely algebraic methods. An analogy can be drawn to the case of abelian groups, where one has a similar embedding sending any group to itself equipped with discrete topology.

While the results of Theorems
2.3
,
2.4
,
2.5
, and
2.6
appear to be new to literature, Theorem
2.2
is referenced implicitly by Flood in
[
9
]
. Namely, the following setup is considered. We let
A
be a locally compact topological ring, and define
\mathcal{T}_{A}
to be the space of continuous

-module homomorphisms from

to
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, equipped with the
A
-module structure
(a\cdot\varphi)(x)=\varphi(a\cdot x)
. We define the dual
M^{\wedge}
of a topological module

to be the space of continuous
A
-module homomorphisms
M\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{T}_{A}
endowed with the compact-open topology. We have the following:

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
M
be an
A
-module. Denote by
M^{\wedge}_{(\mathbb{Z})}
the
\mathbb{Z}
-module dual of morphisms into
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle M^{\wedge}_{(A)}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}M^{\wedge}_{(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\Phi

Thus, taking

-duals is the same, and hence Pontryagin duality as stated in Theorem

easily allows one to deduce that taking

-duals induces an isomorphism on double duals. While no special mention is made to the case that

is a compact DVR in either
[
9
]
or earlier works of this sort such as
[
17
]
, the duality stated in the above paragraph can be used to derive Theorem

via the following equivalence:

Theorem 2.8
.

There is a non-canonical

-module isomorphism and homeomorphism
\mathcal{T}_{R}\cong T
.

Thus,
T
and
\mathcal{T}_{R}
yield the same duality theory, and so

. To elaborate on the non-canonicalness of the isomorphism

, in general there is no clear way to write down any isomorphism between the two. To construct one, we first appeal to the classification theorem of complete DVRs given in

, which states that

is either isomorphic to the completed polynomial ring

over a finite field or isomorphic to the ring of integers

of a finite extension

Out of all the rings covered by
[
9
]
, some global rings also manifest particularly pleasant duality theories:

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
A
be a non-field domain, and
|\cdot|
an archimedean absolute value on
A
. Then
A
is complete and locally compact with respect to its topology if and only if it is isomorphic to either
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}\left[\delta\right]
,
\delta=\left(b+\sqrt{b^{2}+4a}\right)/2
where
a
is a negative integer and
b
is an integer satisfying
\left|b\right|<2\sqrt{-a}
. In the case that
A=\mathbb{Z}[\delta]
, there is a canonical isomorphism and homeomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{A}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{C}/A.

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\mapsto\varphi(1)+\varphi(\delta)\delta

Thus, in all locally compact complete cases the double dual map
M\xrightarrow{}M^{\wedge\wedge}
on
A
-modules

is an isomorphism and homeomorphism, where
\widehat{M}
is defined to be the space of continuous
A

\overline{F}/A
, with
\overline{F}
being the fraction field of

To demonstrate the above theory, we work the case of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-modules is detail. Suppose we are given a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module
M
. These often times appear in Iwasawa theory, where
M
will be some Galois group or Selmer group. In this context the dual
M^{\wedge}
consisting of all morphisms into
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is extremely natural, and is often refered to as the Pontryagin dual of

. We have shown that this name is warranted, since our results imply that when equipped with the canonical topology the natural map

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathrm{cont}}(M,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(M,\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p})

is an isomorphism and homeomorphism. The above map is defined by taking a morphism
\varphi:M\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, and noting that for any

implies that

and hence

is a rational number with

. Thus, the codomain of

can be taken to be
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and the above map is defined to be the identity.

A natural question one might ask is the follows: Given the
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module
M
, when is the algebraic double dual map

M\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\left(\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(M,\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}),\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\right)

an isomorphism? Our paper gives a very testable condition: It will be an isomorphism whenever
M
has any locally compact topological module structure. This is because as soon as
M
has some locally compact topological module structure, we know that this structure must be canonical and hence the topological dual
M^{\wedge}
consists of all
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module morphisms
M\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. The topological double dual map is an isomorphism for
M
by Theorem
2.2
, and using a bit more control (i.e., “dual of canonical is canonical”) we immediately get that the algebraic double dual map is an isomorphism.

This condition is simple to check in many scenerios. When
M
is a Galois group it naturally comes equipped with a profinite topology which will be compact. When
M
is torsion the canonical topology is clearly discrete. The condition can be stated in an if and only if manner: The algebraic double dual map on a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module will be an isomorphism if and only if the canonical topology (or any topological module structure for that matter) is “nuclear” in the sense of
[
4
]
.

The theory of compact DVRs is far simpler than that of
\mathbb{Z}
in the context of duality for a few reasons. Firstly
R
is compact, not just locally compact, which is very useful since compact metric spaces enjoy many nice properties. Secondly,
K
is already complete so there is no analogue of the discrepancy between
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{R}
. Lastly, the canonical topology on
T
is discrete (this is Lemma
3.1
), which makes the consideration of continuous morphisms into
T
far simpler.

Seeing as the canonical topology was defined as the simultaneous presence of three properties, none of which we have shown to be well defined and none of which we have shown to be equivalent, the notation currently has no meaning. For the sake of the rest of this introduction we use the term canonical to refer to the topology defined in point (1) of Theorem
2.5
. The proof that this topology is well defined is relatively straightforward, consisting of a large number of explicit computations; it is performed in the beginning of §
3
.

We now describe the proofs of Theorem
2.5
. The (1)-(2) equivalence is straightforward from Theorem
2.4
, by the universal property of the direct limit. Using explicit calculations in the finitely generated case and limiting arguments in the general case, it is also not too difficult to prove that the double dual map on canonical modules is injective and continuous. The real difficulty comes in showing that if the double dual map is injective and continuous, then the module is canonical.

Choose a topological module
M
, and let
M_{0}
denote that same module equipped with the canonical topology. The (1)-(2) equivalence implies that the identity map
M_{0}\xrightarrow{}M
is continuous. Taking duals, we get a continuous injection
M^{\wedge}\xhookrightarrow{\varphi}M_{0}^{\wedge}
. Restricting to the image, we get a continuous bijection
M^{\wedge}\xhookrightarrow{\varphi}\left.M_{0}^{\wedge}\right|_{\varphi\left(M^{\wedge}\right)}
. It is then proved that the dual of a canonical module is canonical, and that submodules of canonical modules are canonical. In particular, we have that
\left.M_{0}^{\wedge}\right|_{\varphi\left(M^{\wedge}\right)}
is canonical. Thus,
M^{\wedge}
has a continuous isomorphism into the module equal to it algebraically but with canonical topology, and hence the topology on
M^{\wedge}
is finer than the canonical topology. However, by point (2) of Theorem
2.5
the canonical topology on
M^{\wedge}
is the finest possible, and so we must have that the compact-open topology on
M^{\wedge}
is canonical

Since duals of canonical modules are canonical, we have now that
M^{\wedge\wedge}
is canonical. By assumption the double dual map
M\xrightarrow{}M^{\wedge\wedge}
is injective and continuous. Using the technique above of restricting to the image and squeezing with point (2) of Theorem
2.5
, we get that
M
must be canonical so we are done.

3
The Canonical Topology

This section seeks to show the well-definiteness of the canonical topology for finitely generated modules over a fixed compact DVR
R
, as well as some of its first properties. Currently the notion has no meaning, so in the interim we will be referring to product topologies on finitely generated modules: That is, those topologies that arise from first choosing an isomorphism
X\cong\prod R/(x)
between
X
and some product of principal quotients, and then putting the canonical topologies (discrete when
x\neq 0
, metric when
x=0
) on each component, and then putting the product topology on
X
. We will show that all product topologies on
X
are equal, and give rise to the canonical topology.

To begin, we prove a few simple lemmas that will be useful throughout the paper:

Lemma 3.1
.

The quotient topology on
T
is discrete.

Proof.

The preimage of the equivalence class
[t]\in T
under the projection
K\xrightarrow{}T
is
t+R
, which is open in
K
since it can be equivalently described as the open non-archimedean ball
\{x\in K\,\,\mathrm{s.t}\,\,|x-t|\leq 1\}
. Thus, by the definition of the quotient topology, each singleton in
T
must be open so the topology is discrete.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Given any two topological modules
M,N
over a topological ring
A
, the direct product with product topology
M\times N
is again a topological
A
-module. Moreover, it is a biproduct in the category of topological modules, i.e., it satisfies the universal properties of both the product and coproduct. Additionally, the product of complete modules is complete.

Proof.

The fact that
M\times N
is a topological module follows from an unenlightening exercise in computation. Seeing as
M\times N
is defined to have both the product module and product topological structure, it clearly satisfies the axioms of a categorical product. Hence, we are left with showing that it satisfies the coproduct axioms as well.

To do this, we take as input a topological module
X
and two continuous morphisms
f_{N}:N\xrightarrow{}X
,
f_{M}:M\xrightarrow{}X
. We wish to construct a map
f:N\times M\xrightarrow{}X
which acts like
f_{N},f_{M}
when restricted correctly. To do this, we simply consider both
f_{N}
and
f_{M}
as morphisms
N\times M\xrightarrow{}X
by first factoring through a restriction
N\times M\twoheadrightarrow{}M,N
. Taking the sum of
f_{N}
and
f_{M}
we get the desired continuous map.

We now prove that the product of complete modules is complete. Suppose
(z_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Cauchy sequence in
M\times N
. First, we write
z_{n}=(x_{n},y_{n})
. A Cauchy sequence in
M\times N
clearly must be Cauchy in each component, and hence
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
are both Cauchy. By hypothesis they have limit points
x_{\infty}
and
y_{\infty}
respectively, so we can set
z_{\infty}=(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})
. This is clearly a limit point for
(z_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, so we are done.
∎

We now can get to proving results about product topologies on finitely generated modules, when we remember that
\pi
is a distinguished uniformizing element for
R
:

Lemma 3.3
.

All product topologies on finitely generated modules induce compact complete topological structures.

Proof.

This is clearly true when the module under consideration is
R
, since the metric space topology on
R
makes it a topological ring. When the module is
R/(\pi^{n})
for some
n\geq 1
, addition and inversion are continuous since the space is discrete. To show scalar multiplication
R\times R/(\pi^{n})\xrightarrow{}R/(\pi^{n})
is continuous, we compute the preimage of
0
to be

\bigcup_{x\in R/(\pi^{n})}(\pi^{k_{x}})\times\{x\},

where
k_{x}
is the smallest integer such that
\pi^{k}x=0
. Since
(\pi^{k_{x}})
is open in
R
and
x
is open in
R/(\pi^{n})
, the union of these sets is open in
R\times R/(\pi^{n})
. Translating using addition we get that the preimage of any singleton in
R/(\pi^{n})
is open, and since singletons form a basis for the topology of
R/(\pi^{n})
we are done with the proof that scalar multiplication is continuous. The last condition needed for
R/(\pi^{n})
to be a topological module is for it to be Hausdorff, which is clear.

Both
R
and
R/(\pi^{n})
are compact complete topological modules, so by Lemma
3.2
all product topologies on fin gen modules give compact complete topological structures.
∎

We now work towards our proof that all topological structures on fin gen modules are equivalent, by showing that there is a unique topological structure on
R
. The proof strongly uses the fact that
R
is a compact metric space. In fact, it already fails for the case of
\mathbb{Z}
-modules. As an example, one can take the set of all arithmetic sequences as a basis of
\mathbb{Z}
: This topology is non-canonical (i.e., not discrete), metrizable, and is used in
[
12
]
to deduce the infinitude of primes.

Lemma 3.4
.

Every topological
R
-module
M
algebraically isomorphic to
R
must have the metric space topology.

Proof.

Let
M\in\mathrm{HM}_{R}
be algebraically isomorphic to
R
. We wish to show
M=R

We are now ready to prove Theorem

This is true for modules of the form

We thus can now refer to the canonical topology on fin gen modules. For general modules, it is clear that passing to the direct limit

Proposition 3.1
.

If

is in canonical form then it is Hausdorff regular, and all submodules of

are closed and canonical when given subspace topology.

Proof.

To prove that

is Hausdorff, we appeal to the general theory of sets given the direct limit topology on an ascending sequence of compact Hausdorff subspaces. They are known as

-spaces and enjoy many nice properties, one of which being that they are all Hausdorff regular. This was originally proved in the Russian language paper
[

, and subsequently exposited in a more modern fashion by
[

.

Now, let
N\leq M
be a submodule. To show

is closed it suffices to show that

is closed in

for all fin gen submodules

. This is clear, however, since

is complete by Lemma

and thus is closed since it contains all of its limit points.

We now show that

must be canonical, by showing that a set

is closed in the subspace topology if and only if it is closed in the canonical topology. If

is closed in the subspace topology then

for some closed

, and hence

for all

, and hence

is closed in the canonical topology. Conversely, if

is closed in the canonical topology then we must show that

for some closed set

. This is clear, however, since
W
is closed in

which is closed in
M
, and hence
W
is closed in
M
, so we can take

. Thus, we conclude the result.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

The canonical topology always induces an
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-structure on modules.

Proof.

Write

with

\displaystyle\left(f\,\,\mathrm{continuous}\right)

\displaystyle\iff\left(f^{-1}(U)\cap S\,\,\mathrm{open}\,\,\forall\,S\leq M,\,U\subseteq M\,\mathrm{open}\right)

\displaystyle\iff\left(\left.f\right|_{S}:S\xrightarrow{}M\,\,\mathrm{continuous}\,\,\forall S\leq M\right).

Now,

is exactly the inversion map on

and hence since

with subspace topology is canonical by Proposition

the inversion map on

must be continuous by the previously treated case.

We now show that the addition map

is open in

for all

to be the projection maps onto each component, we let

be the submodule of

and

. Since

and

are fin gen, so is

. Restricting the addition map on

to

, we obtain the addition map on

. By Proposition

,

is canonical when given subspace topology and hence the addition map is continuous by the previously treated case. Since

, the set

We now show that the scalar multiplication map

We now prove a technical lemma that may appear esoteric, but it plays a key role in our proof that the double dual map is injective and continuous. This is because in any suitably nice category the double dual map
\varinjlim A\xrightarrow{}[[\varinjlim A,B],B]
on a direct limit can be factored as

\varinjlim A\xrightarrow{i_{1}}\varinjlim[[A,B],B]\xrightarrow{i_{2}}[\varprojlim[A,B],B]\xrightarrow{i_{3}}[[\varinjlim A,B],B].

The map

comes from the compostum of all the double dual maps

. One can easily check that the double dual map is functorial, and makes the appropriate diagrams commute so that
i_{1}
is indeed a morphism of direct limits.

The map

comes from sending a morphism

to the composition

\varprojlim[A,B]\xrightarrow{}[A,B]\xrightarrow{f}B,

where the morphism
\varprojlim[A,B]\xrightarrow{}[A,B]
is projected onto the component.

The map

is the dual of the morphism
[\varinjlim A,B]\xrightarrow{}\varprojlim[A,B]
sending

to the system
(\left.f\right|_{A})
of restrictions.

The problem of analyzing the double-dual map in nice circumstances is thus reduced to analyzing these arrows one at a time. The next lemma serves to describe how the map

behaves in our case.

5
Topological Modules over Compact DVRs

In this section we prove relevant results pertaining to topological
R
-modules. We begin with a standard proposition about the compact-open topology, which is useful since it realizes duality as a contravariant functor
\mathrm{HM}_{R}\xrightarrow{}\mathrm{HM}_{R}
:

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-morphism
\varphi:M\xrightarrow{}N
, the precomposition map
\widehat{N}\xrightarrow{\widehat{\varphi}}\widehat{M}
is continuous. By precomposition, we mean that
\phi:N\xrightarrow{}T
maps to
\phi\circ\varphi:M\xrightarrow{}T
.

Proof.

Given any open set
V_{M}(Z;U)\subseteq M^{\wedge}
, we find that

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}^{-1}\left(V_{M}(Z,U)\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{\phi\in\widehat{N}\,\,\mathrm{s.t}\,\,(\phi\circ\varphi)(Z)\subseteq U\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\phi\in\widehat{N}\,\,\mathrm{s.t}\,\,\phi\left(\varphi(Z)\right)\subseteq U\right\}

\displaystyle=V_{N}(\varphi(Z),U).

Since the image of a compact set is compact, we are done.
∎

The following argument is taken from the proof of Proposition 1.1 in
[
2
]
. In this paper, Anh says that “It is well known that injectivity plays a very important role in duality theory”. Despite this statement being in reference to Morita duality, it is equally apt here. We repeatedly use the ability to lift continuous morphisms, making the below proposition a core part of the proof.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
R
be a compact DVR, let
M\in\mathrm{HM}_{R}
be an object, and let
N
be a submodule of
M
. The precomposition map
\widehat{M}\xrightarrow{}\widehat{N}
is surjective, i.e.,every
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-morphism
N\xrightarrow{}T
can be lifted to a continuous map
M\xrightarrow{}T
.

Proof.

Consider
\varphi\in N^{\wedge}
. We wish to show that there is a morphism in
M^{\wedge}
whose restriction to
N
is equal to
\varphi
. Since
\ker\varphi
is the preimage of
\{0\}
,
T
being discrete (Lemma
3.1
) implies that
\ker\varphi
is open. By the definition of the subspace topology this means that
\ker\varphi=U\cap N
where
U
is an open subset of
M
. Since
\ker\varphi
is a submodule we have that
U\cap N=\tilde{U}\cap N
where
\tilde{U}
is the submodule generated by
U
. Since the
\tilde{U}
contains an open neighborhood of one of its points it must contain an open neighborhood of all its points by shifting and using the continuity of addition, hence
\tilde{U}
is open.

We now define a morphism

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}:N+\tilde{U}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}T.

\displaystyle n+u

\displaystyle\mapsto\varphi(n)

The choice of decomposition of an element into the form
n+u
is unique up to moving across an element of
\tilde{U}\cap N
, and seeing as
\varphi
acts by zero on this set the map is indeed well defined. The preimage of
t\in T
is equal to
\varphi^{-1}(t)+\tilde{U}
, which is open since the sum of open sets is open, and hence
\tilde{\varphi}
is continuous.

It is clear that
\pi T=T
. Baer’s criterion (c.f,
[
3
]
) states that this is sufficient for
T
to be injective in the category of
R
-modules, meaning we can algebraically lift
\tilde{\varphi}
all the way to
M
. This lift is continuous on an open neighborhood of
0
, namely
\tilde{U}
. By continuity of addition, this means that
\tilde{\varphi}
is continuous on
x+\tilde{U}
for all
x\in M
, and hence it is continuous on a neighborhood of every point so we are done.
∎

Corollary 5.1
.

Over
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
,
\widehat{M}=0
if and only if
M=0
.

Proof.

Choose a nonzero
x\in M
. The submodule
R\cdot x
must be algebraically isomorphic either

or
R
, with
x
mapping to
1
, and by Theorem
2.4
the topology must be canonical. Either way, the map

distinguished by the property that

sends to

is well defined and continuous. Thus, we get that for any nonzero

there exists a continuous map

which does not act by

on

. Lifting with Proposition

we are done.
∎

It is an important part of Pontryagin duality theory that the dual of a locally compact abelian group is again locally compact, though that fact is very nontrivial to prove. It requires a proper development of the theory of measures and integration. The proof below is partially due to Eric Wofsey, through collaboration on the online Q
\&
A site Mathematics Stack Exchange, and is much more elementary in nature.

Proposition 5.3
.

The categories
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
and
\mathrm{LCM}_{R}
are closed under taking duals.

Proof.

To show that
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
is closed under taking duals, we choose
M\in\mathrm{HM}_{R}
and any unequal
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1}\in M^{\wedge}
. Clearly, we can find
m\in M
such that
\varphi_{0}(m)\neq\varphi_{1}(m)
. By Lemma
3.1
we have that
T
is discrete so
\{\varphi_{0}(m)\},\{\varphi_{1}(m)\}
are open. The sets
V(\{m\},\left\{\varphi_{0}(m)\right\})
and
V(\{m\},\left\{\varphi_{1}(m)\right\})
are thus disjoint open neighborhoods separating
\varphi_{0}
and
\varphi_{1}
, so
M^{\wedge}
is Hausdorff.

Now, we show that
\mathrm{LCM}_{R}
is closed as well. Choose
M\in\mathrm{LCM}_{R}
. Given any
\varphi\in M^{\wedge}
, the discreteness of
T
(Lemma
3.1
) tells us that
\ker\varphi
is clopen and hence by local compactness of
M
we have an inclusion of subsets
U\leq Z\leq\ker\varphi
with
U
nonzero open and
Z
compact. Dually, this gives an inclusion of subsets of
M^{\wedge}

V(\ker\varphi,\{0\})\leq V(Z,\{0\})\leq V(U,\{0\}).

Clearly
\varphi\in V(\ker\varphi,\{0\})
, and so
V(Z,\{0\})
is an open neighborhood of
f
. It remains to be shown that
V(U,\{0\})
is compact. To do this, we note that
V(U,\{0\})=V(\tilde{U},\{0\})
where
\tilde{U}
is the submodule of
M
generated by
U
.
\tilde{U}
is open, because addition is continuous and so the open neighborhood of
0
translates to open neighborhoods of every point of
\tilde{U}
.

Now,
V(\tilde{U},0)
can be naturally identified with
(M/\tilde{U})^{\wedge}
. The topology on
M/\tilde{U}
is discrete, and so the compact-open topology on
(M/\tilde{U})^{\wedge}
is equal to the product topology. Additionally,
M/\tilde{U}
is a torsion module since otherwise the sequence
(\pi^{n}x)_{n=1}^{\infty}
would be non-constant convergent. Hence,
(M/\tilde{U})^{\wedge}
is a subspace of the product
\prod_{x\in M/\tilde{U}}\pi^{-n_{x}}R/R
where
n_{x}
are chosen such that
\pi^{n_{x}}x=0
. Each
\pi^{-n_{x}}R/R\cong R/(\pi^{n_{x}})
is finite, so compact, and thus by Tychonoff’s theorem
\prod_{x\in M/\tilde{U}}\pi^{-n_{x}}R/R
is compact as well. The subset consisting of homomorphisms
M/\tilde{U}\xrightarrow{}T
is clearly closed, and hence
(M/\tilde{U})^{\wedge}
is compact so we are done.
∎

The following lemma condenses the “restrict to image and squeeze” procedure described in the introduction, which will be used keyly in the proofs of Theorem
2.5
.

Lemma 5.1
.

If

is an injection in
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
and

is canonical, then so is

.

Proof.

Restricting the map we get a continuous bijection from

to its image, which is canonical by Proposition

. Continuity means that

is finer than its image, and hence the topology on

is finer than the canonical topology. By Corollary

the canonical topology is the finest possible and hence

must be canonical as well.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

The dual of a module
M
in canonical form is also in canonical form, and is
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-isomorphic to
\varprojlim_{S\leq M}[S,T]
.

Proof.

We write
M=\varinjlim_{S\leq M}S
, where for all limits we will be running over fin gen submodules. Given any fin gen submodule

, we define
[S,T]_{L}
to be the submodule of
[S,T]
obtained by projecting
L
onto
[S,T]
. There is a canonical set injection
\varinjlim_{L\leq M^{\wedge}}[S,T]_{L}\xrightarrow{}[S,T]
. To show this map is surjective, we choose any
\varphi\in[S,T]
. Since

is surjective (Proposition
5.2
) we find
\tilde{\varphi}\in M^{\wedge}
which restricts to
\varphi
, and hence letting

be the submodule generated by
\tilde{\varphi}
we find that
\varphi\in[S,T]_{L}
so
\varphi
is in the image. Finitely generated modules are isomorphic to products of terms of the form
R
or
R/(x)
,

, and so their duals will be products of terms of the form
T
or
R/(x)
,

(by Lemma
3.2
and the computations of Propositions
4.1
and
4.3
) which both are discrete. Hence both

and
\varinjlim_{L\leq M^{\wedge}}[S,T]_{L}
are discrete so the natural bijection between them is an
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-isomorphism.

Next, the universal property of the inverse limit tells us the natural injection

is continuous. Moreover,
[S,T]_{L}
is defined to make the maps

surjective and hence the image of
L
is dense. Since
L
is fin gen it must be complete (Proposition
3.1
), meaning that so is its image and hence the map is surjective. Hence,

as modules and so

is also fin gen since the only topological structures on fin gen modules are canonical by Theorem

, and so we get that the equality is both algebraic and topological.

Finally, the universal property of the projective limit implies that

is continuous. Moreover, this map is a bijection because as a functor to the category of sets, the representable functor

turns colimits into limits (see e.g., Proposition 2.9.4 of
[
7
]
).

\displaystyle\widehat{M}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}\varprojlim_{S\leq M}[S,T]

\displaystyle=\varprojlim_{S\leq M}\varinjlim_{L\leq M^{\wedge}}[S,T]_{L}

\displaystyle=\varinjlim_{L\leq M^{\wedge}}\varprojlim_{S\leq M}[S,T]

\displaystyle=\varinjlim_{L\leq M^{\wedge}}L,

where interchange of limits is valid since limits can always be interchanged when they exist (see
[
7
]
Proposition 2.12.1). The module

is algebraically equal to

but given canonical topology, and so we get that

has a finer topology than the canonical topology. By Corollary

we know that the canonical topology is the finest possible, and hence the map must be a homeomorphism. Additionally, for the compositum to be a homeomorphism we must have that

is a homeomorphism so we get the second claim as well.
∎

, and we compute

\displaystyle d^{-1}\left(V_{M^{\wedge}}(Z;U))\right)

[\mathbb{F}_{q},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}]_{\mathbb{Z}}
is defined by choosing a basis and freely assigning each basis vector an element of
\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}
. Hence
[\mathbb{F}_{q},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}]_{\mathbb{Z}}
has
q
elements. Vector spaces (
\mathbb{F}_{q}
modules) are classified by their order, and hence we get that
[\mathbb{F}_{q},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}]_{\mathbb{Z}}\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}
as an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
module. The topology is Hausdorff, and since the only finite Hausdorff spaces are discrete this map is a homomorphism as well so we are done.
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

Choose a prime power
q=p^{e}
. Fix an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-module isomorphism
[\mathbb{F}_{q},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}]_{\mathbb{Z}}\xrightarrow{i}\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We now define a map

\displaystyle[\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}]_{\mathbb{Z}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\ell}\mathbb{F}_{q}((x))/\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket.

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}i\left(\left.\varphi\right|_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n}}\right)x^{-(n+1)}

All but finitely many of the terms in the summation are zero. In particular,
\ell
is well defined. Moreover,
\ell
is an
\mathrm{HM}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket}
-isomorphism.

Proof.

The elements in
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n}
uniformly approach zero in
\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket
as
n\to\infty
. Thus, since the image under
\varphi
of every element lies in the discrete subring
\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}
we must have
\varphi\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n}\right)=0
for all large
n
, and hence all but finitely many of the terms in the summation are zero. We now show that
\ell
satisfies the axioms of a
\mathrm{HM}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket}
-isomorphism:

•

\ell(\varphi_{0}+\varphi_{1})=\ell(\varphi_{0})+\ell(\varphi_{1})
: This is clear, since
\varphi
are
\mathbb{Z}
-module homomorphisms, and summation commutes with finite sums.

•

\ell(r\cdot\varphi)=r\cdot\ell(\varphi)
: We first prove this for the case that
r=h\cdot x^{k}
,
h\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Namely, we examine the following computation:

\displaystyle\ell\left((h\cdot x^{k})\cdot\varphi\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}i\left(\left.((h\cdot x^{k})\cdot\varphi)\right|_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n}}\right)x^{-(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\left(h\cdot x^{k}\right)\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}i\left(\left.\varphi\right|_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n+k}}\right)x^{-(n+k+1)}

\displaystyle=\left(h\cdot x^{k}\right)\cdot\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}i\left(\left.\varphi\right|_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n}}\right)x^{-(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\left(h\cdot x^{k}\right)\cdot\ell\left(\varphi\right).

The last equality is true in
\mathbb{F}_{q}((x))/\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket
since all of the terms
n<k
are
0
when multiplied by
x^{k}
, but is certainly false in
\mathbb{F}_{q}((x))
. Seeing as elements of the form
h\cdot x^{k}
generate
\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module we are done.

•

Injectivity: Every element of
\mathbb{F}((x))/\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket
can be uniquely written in the form
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\cdot x^{-(n+1)}
. Thus, two maps sending to the same element must satisfy
\left.\varphi_{0}\right|_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n}}=\left.\varphi_{1}\right|_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n}}
for all
n
since
i
is injective. Since the submodules
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n}
generate
\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket
this implies
\varphi_{0}=\varphi_{1}
and so we are done.

•

Surjectivity: Since the submodules
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n}
freely generate
\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module, we can make a well defined map
\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
by independently choosing the behavior for each submodule
\mathbb{F}_{q}\cdot x^{n}
. The condition that all but finitely many terms being zero is sufficient to guarantee continuity, and hence every element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}((x))/\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket
can be represented as the image of a continuous map, and hence
\ell
is surjective.

•

Homeomorphism: By Theorem
2.1
, the Pontryagin dual of any locally compact space is locally compact. Hence, by the above discussion,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket}
and
T
are algebraiclly equal. Since
T
is discrete by Lemma
3.1
and there is at most one locally compact topology on each algebraic module by Theorem
2.3
, we get that
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket}
is discrete as well so the map is a homeomorphism.

The above statements are enough to conclude that
\ell
is an
\mathrm{HM}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket}
-isomorphism, and hence we are done.
∎

Next we prove the mixed characteristic case in a non-constructive manner, remembering that
K
is fixed as the fraction field of
R
:

Lemma 6.2
.

If
K
has characteristic not equal to the characteristic of the residue field of
R
, then the module
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is
p^{\infty}
-torsion, in the sense that each element is annihilated by
p^{n}
for large enough
n
. Moreover, using
\#S
to denote the number of elements in a set
S
and using
M\left[p^{n}\right]
to denote the
p^{n}
-torsion of a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module
M
, we have that

\#\mathcal{T}_{R}[p^{n}]=\#T[p^{n}]

for all
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

There is a natural
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module structure on
R
. Since
R
is an integral domain whose fraction field has characteristic
0
this module structure is torsion free, and since every torsion free module over a DVR is free we obtain a (non-canonical)
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module isomorphism
R\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{e}
where
e
is the rank of
R
as a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module.
R
is locally compact, and hence the topology forcing theorem implies that the canonical topology on
R
is equal to the product topology on
e
copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. This computation can also be done explicitly by choosing a basis and looking at valuations, but we omit it for brevity. Finite topological products are both the product and coproduct in the category of topological modules (By Lemma
3.2
) and hence we have a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module isomorphism

\mathcal{T}_{R}\cong\left[\mathbb{Z}_{p},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\right]_{\mathbb{Z}}^{e}.

Since
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is topologically generated by
1
, the map

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{Z}_{p},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\right]_{\mathbb{Z}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\mapsto\varphi(1)

is an injection. Any element
\varphi(1)
must satisfy
\lim_{n\to\infty}p^{n}\varphi(1)=0
. This is true if and only if
\varphi(1)
is a rational number with a
p
-power denominator. Rationals in such form are naturally in bijection with elements of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and the module structure from the hom agrees, and hence we get a canonical
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module isomorphism

\left[\mathbb{Z}_{p},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\right]_{\mathbb{Z}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

Combining, we see that as a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-module
\mathcal{T}_{R}\cong\left(\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\right)^{r}
. It is clear from this that
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is
p^{\infty}
-torsion. Moreover, counting
p^{n}
torsion we get that

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{T}_{R}[p^{n}]

\displaystyle=\#\left(\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\right)^{r}[p^{n}]

\displaystyle=\left(\#\left(\mathbb{Q}_{p}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}\right)[p^{n}]\right)^{r}

\displaystyle=\left(\#\mathbb{Z}/(p^{n})\right)^{r}

\displaystyle=p^{rn}.

On the other hand, we have that

\#T[p^{n}]=\#R/(p^{n})=\left(\#R/(p)\right)^{n}.

Thus, all that is left to do for the proof is to show that
\#R/(p)=p^{r}
. Writing elements of
R
uniquely in a
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-basis and reducing mod
p
this is clear, so we are done.
∎

Proposition 6.3
.

If
K
has characteristic not equal to the characteristic of the residue field of
R
, then there exists an
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-isomorphism

\mathcal{T}_{R}\xrightarrow{\sim}T

Proof.

By Proposition
6.1
we have that

\left[T,\mathcal{T}_{R}\right]_{R}=\left[T,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\right]_{\mathbb{Z}}\neq 0,

and thus there exists a nonzero
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-morphism
f:T\xrightarrow{}\mathcal{T}_{R}
. Moreover, by factoring we get an injective map

f^{\prime}:T/\ker(f)\hookrightarrow{}\mathcal{T}_{R}.

The submodules of
T
are all of the form
\ker_{n}=\left\{x\,\,\mathrm{s.t}\,\,\pi^{n}x=0\right\}
. Multiplication by
\pi^{n}
gives an isomorphism

T\xrightarrow{\sim}T/\ker_{n},

and hence we can consider
f^{\prime}
as an
R
-module injection
T\hookrightarrow{}\mathcal{T}_{R}
. To start proving surjectivity, we note that the
p^{n}
-torsion of
T
must map into the
p^{n}
-torsion of
\mathcal{T}_{R}
. Since both sides have the same number of elements in their
p^{n}
torsions by Lemma
6.2
this means that the map is surjective onto
p^{n}
torsion for each
n
. Since
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is a
p^{\infty}
-torsion module this implies surjectivity.

By Theorem
2.1
, the Pontryagin dual of any locally compact space is locally compact. Hence, by the above discussion,
\mathcal{T}_{R}
and
T
are algebraiclly equal. Since
T
is discrete by Lemma
3.1
and there is at most one locally compact topology on each algebraic module by Theorem
2.3
, we get that
\mathcal{T}_{R}
is discrete as well so the map is a homeomorphism.
∎

Collecting, we can prove Theorem
2.8
:

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

If
R
is a compact DVR, then either the characteristic
K
is equal to the characteristic of the residue field or it is different. It it is equal, then by Chapter 2 §4 Theorem 2 of
[

]
we have that
R\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}\llbracket x\rrbracket
for some finite field

, and hence this case is treated by Proposition

. If the characteristic is different we can give a treatment with Proposition

∎

We now prove Theorem
2.2
:

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

As a special case of Proposition
6.1
setting
G=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, we get a canonical
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-isomorphism

[M,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}]_{\mathbb{Z}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\left[M,\mathcal{T}_{R}\right]_{R},

which yields a larger canonical
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-isomorphism

\left[\left[M,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\right]_{\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\right]_{\mathbb{Z}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\left[\left[M,\mathcal{T}_{R}\right]_{R},\mathcal{T}_{R}\right]_{R}.

Next, we choose any
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-isomorphism

\displaystyle\left[\left[M,\mathcal{T}_{R}\right]_{R},\mathcal{T}_{R}\right]_{R}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{}\left[\left[M,T\right]_{R},T\right]_{R}.

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\varphi\mapsto i^{-1}\left(\Phi\left(i\circ\varphi\right)\right)\right)

It is straightforward to check that this map is an
\mathrm{HM}_{R}
-isomorphism, with the inverse given by the same map in the opposite direction and

. Collecting these maps and including double-dual arrows we get a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left[\left[M,T\right]_{R},T\right]_{R}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}

Thus, moving up the diagram and using commutativity we get that the double dual map

7
Archimedean Results

In this section we prove results relating to the rings
A=\mathbb{Z}[\delta]
, where throughout
\delta
is fixed to be the quantity
\left(b+\sqrt{b^{2}+4a}\right)/2
, with
a
being a negative integer and
b
being an integer satisfying
\left|b\right|<2\sqrt{-a}
. Firstly, the classification part of Theorem
2.9
is proved:

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
R
be a non-field domain, and
|\cdot|
an archimedean absolute value on
R
. Then
R
is complete and locally compact with respect to its topology if and only if it is isomorphic to either
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}\left[\delta\right]
where
\delta
is as above.

Proof.

The closure
\overline{K}
of the fraction field of
R
is a complete field with respect to an archimedean absolute value. Since every absolute value on a field of characteristic
p
is non-archimedean, we may assume the characteristic of
\overline{K}
is
0
. In particular, we have
\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\overline{K}
. By Ostrowski’s classification of absolute values on
\mathbb{Q}
, we must have that
|\cdot|
restricts to the usual absolute value and hence we have
\mathbb{R}\subseteq\overline{K}
. Using this we conclude that
\overline{K}
can be viewed as a real Banach algebra and hence by the Gelfand-Mazur theorem
\overline{K}
is isomorphic to either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
.

We now have that
R
is a subring of
\mathbb{C}
. If the induced topology were not discrete, then one would have arbitrarily small elements in
R
. Using the total order of the reals this allows one to approximate every real number arbitrarily well, and hence by completeness we would have
\mathbb{R}\subseteq R
, and hence
R=\mathbb{R}
or
R=\mathbb{C}
which is not possible since
R
is assumed not a field. Hence, we have that
R
is a discrete subring of
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, every discrete subring is complete and hence we have that
R
satisfies the conditions of the theorem if and only if it is a discrete subring of
\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 12 of
[
21
]
gives a classification of discrete subrings of
\mathbb{C}
. Taking into account discrepancies in the definition of ring (i.e., we assume all rings are unital), this classification consists of exactly the rings listed in the statement of our lemma.
∎

Next, we prove the
\mathrm{HM}_{A}
-isomorphism part of Theorem
2.9
:

Lemma 7.2
.

There is a canonical
\mathrm{HM}_{A}
-isomorphism

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{A}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{C}/A

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\mapsto\varphi(1)+\varphi(\delta)\delta

Proof.

The quantity
\varphi(1)+\varphi(\delta)\delta
is well defined up to changing
\varphi(1)
and
\varphi(\delta)
by integer shifts, i.e., up to changing the full quantity by an element of
\mathbb{Z}[\delta]
, and hence the map’s image is a well defined element of
\mathbb{C}/A
. Moreover, every morphism in
\mathcal{T}_{A}
is determined by its action on
1
and
\delta
hence the above discussion implies that the map is injective.

Since
A
is discrete, any map
A\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is continuous and thus the image consists of all elements in
\mathbb{C}/A
. It remains to check that the map is a homeomorphism. A convergent sequence of functions in
[A,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}]_{\mathbb{Z}}
in the compact-open topology is pointwise convergent, and hence
\varphi(1)
and
\varphi(\delta)
converge. Moreover,
\varphi(1)
and
\varphi(\delta)
converging is sufficient for the a sequence of functions to be convergent since
\mathbb{Z}[\delta]
is generated by
\varphi(1)
and
\varphi(\delta)
uniformly on compact sets. Hence, the topology on
[A,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}]_{\mathbb{Z}}
is the product topology on its two copies of
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, and seeing as the topology on
\mathbb{C}
is the product topology on its two copies of
\mathbb{R}
we are done.
∎

Now, we state the final part needed for Theorem
2.9
:

The double dual map
M\xrightarrow{}\left[\left[M,\mathbb{C}/A\right]_{A},\mathbb{C}/A\right]_{A}

Proof.

This proof is identical to the alternate proof of Theorem
2.2
given in section
6
. The only change is that instead of using Theorem

one uses Lemma
7.2
.
∎
