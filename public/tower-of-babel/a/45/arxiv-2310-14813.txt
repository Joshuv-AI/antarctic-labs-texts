Catastrophe conditions for vector fields in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n}
Mike R. Jeffrey

Abstract
Practical conditions are given here for finding and classifying high codimension intersection points of n n hypersurfaces in n n dimensions. By interpreting those hypersurfaces as the nullclines of a vector field in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , we broaden the concept of Thom’s catastrophes to find bifurcation points of (non-gradient) vector fields of any dimension. We introduce a family of determinants ℬ j \mathcal{B}_{j} , such that a codimension r r bifurcation point is found by solving the system ℬ 1 = … = ℬ r = 0 \mathcal{B}_{1}=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}=0 , subject to certain non-degeneracy conditions. The determinants ℬ j \mathcal{B}_{j} generalize the derivatives ∂ j ∂ x j ​ F ​ ( x ) \mbox{$\frac{\partial^{j}\;}{\partial x^{j}}$}F(x) that vanish at a catastrophe of a scalar function F ⁡ ( x ) F(x) . We do not extend catastrophe theory or singularity theory themselves, but provide a means to apply them more readily to the multi-dimensional dynamical models that appear, for example, in the study of various engineered or living systems. For illustration we apply our conditions to locate butterfly and star catastrophes in a second order PDE.

Introduction

In this paper we give conditions that can be solved to locate degenerate critical points of a function
{\bf F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Letting
{\bf F}=(F_{1},F_{2},...,F_{n})
, a critical point in this context means a common zero of several functions

\displaystyle(F_{1},F_{2},...,F_{n})=(0,0,...,0)\;,

(1)

in some variable
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with parameters
{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
If the function
{\bf F}
is singular at a critical point
({\bf x},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})=({\bf x}_{*},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{*})
then, under perturbation to any nearby
\alpha
, it will typically bifurcate to produce multiple critical points of
{\bf F}
in the space of
{\bf x}
.

Quite simply, our aim here is to define a set of conditions

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}=\mathcal{B}_{2}=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}=0\neq\mathcal{G}_{r}

(2)

whose solution, along with the condition
{\bf F}=0
, provides the location of a codimension
r
local bifurcation point in the vector field
{\bf F}
. The functions
\mathcal{B}_{i}
are determinants of augmented Jacobian matrices that generalize the derivatives
\frac{d^{i}}{dx^{i}}
which characterise catastrophes in one dimension (see e.g.
[
20
,
26
,
28
]
), while
\mathcal{G}_{r}
represents a set of non-degeneracy conditions.

The main motivation behind this study comes from the problem of finding high codimension local bifurcation points of a vector field
{\bf F}
, specifically to study steady states of an ordinary differential equation such as
\mbox{$\frac{d\;}{dt}$}{\bf x}={\bf F}
, or of a partial differential equation such as
\mbox{$\frac{d\;}{dt}$}{\bf u}+k\mbox{$\frac{\partial^{2}\;}{\partial x^{2}}$}{\bf u}={\bf F}
. The results can also be used to characterise zeros of varieties
F_{i}
, or singular intersections of differentiable hypersurfaces defined by the equations
F_{i}=0
, for
i=1,...,n
.

Given a map
{\bf F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a particular point
({\bf x}_{*},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
, singularity theory provides the means to classify any singularity that may occur there with great generality, see e.g.
[
3
,
7
,
16
,
17
,
23
]
. Our aim here is essentially to turn this around, to provide readily solvable conditions that can be solved to find the point
({\bf x}_{*},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{*})
at which some suspected singularity or bifurcation occurs. To do so our subject matter must be significantly less general than singularity theory. We will look only for places where multiple critical points of a function
{\bf F}
coincide, and derive conditions that detect the
underlying catastrophe
, by which we will mean the coincidence of zeroes of the vector field, without reference to the vector field’s directionality (and therefore without reference to phase portraits or stability in a dynamical system generated by
{\bf F}
).

It is notable, for instance, that modern numerical continuation packages (e.g. COCO, MatCont, AUTO
[
1
,
9
,
10
]
) do not yet contain a built-in condition to locate and continue general high codimension bifurcation points. Solvable conditions to locate such points are limited to low codimension cases, in particular the fold (or saddle-node) and cusp bifurcations (see e.g.
[
15
]
), or involving folds or cusps accompanied by more than one vanishing eigenvalue of the vector field’s Jacobian (as in e.g. the ‘fold-Hopf’ or ‘zero-Hopf’ bifurcations
[
3
,
14
,
15
]
). The conditions
eq.

2
are intended to provide functions that can be defined for general codimension

, and solved by continuing successive zeroes of the functions
\mathcal{B}_{1}=0
,
\mathcal{B}_{2}=0
, … .

The classification of such points in either mappings or vector fields has particularly focused on showing equivalence to certain local forms. Arnold’s classification in
[
3

, for example, shows that the germ of a singularity in a system

reduces to a small number of known classes, such as class

, which has “one zero eigenvalue and

\displaystyle\dot{x}=\pm x^{\nu+1}+\varepsilon_{\nu}x^{\nu-1}+...+\varepsilon_{2}x+\varepsilon_{1}\;,

It is important to distinguish between the

Things are different if we consider points where

{\bf F}

In studying critical points of
{\bf F}

Let us now introduce these conditions and the geometry behind them.

3
The catastrophe conditions

Consider a set of functions

\displaystyle F_{i}=F_{i}(x_{1},...,x_{n};\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{p})\qquad{\rm for}\qquad i=1,...,n,\;

(5)

where
x_{i}
are variables and
\alpha_{i}
are parameters. In vector form we write
{\bf F}={\bf F}({\bf x},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})
, with
{\bf x}=(x_{1},...,x_{n})
,
{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{p})
, and
{\bf F}=(F_{1},...,F_{n})
. Let
{\bf F}:U\times A\to V
be a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
differentiable function in the variable
{\bf x}\in U
, for a parameter
{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\in A
, with
U,V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

. We assume
k>r
and
p\geq r
, where
r
will be the codimension of the underlying catastrophe introduced in
definition

3.2
below.

For convenience we will typically just write ‘
{\bf F}
’ for the vector field, rather than making the dependence on ‘(
{\bf x},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})
’ explicit.

We wish to characterise points where
{\bf F}=0
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
critical point
of
{\bf F}
is a point
({\bf x}_{*},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{*})\in U\times A

\displaystyle F_{1}=...=F_{n}=0\;.

(6)

A bifurcation occurs where a critical point is degenerate. This happens at an intersection of the hypersurfaces
F_{i}=0
where the Jacobian matrix
\frac{d{\bf F}}{d{\bf x}}
has rank less than
n
, so its determinant
\left|\mbox{$\frac{d{\bf F}}{d{\bf x}}$}\right|
vanishes.
This also means that the gradient vectors
\mbox{$\frac{d\;}{d{\bf x}}$}F_{1},...,\mbox{$\frac{d\;}{d{\bf x}}$}F_{n}
, are linearly dependent, but we shall assume in general that if we take any
n-1

are
linearly independent.
We then need a notion of
how
degenerate the point is, in some sense the order of contact
r
between the hypersurfaces, determining how many critical points may exist locally as we vary
\alpha

We will first define conditions for this purpose in

3.1

3.2
.

3.1
The conditions

Let us first define determinants
\mathcal{B}_{i}
, for
i=2,...,r,
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{i-1},F_{2},...,F_{n})}{\partial(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\right|\;,\qquad\mathcal{B}_{1}=\left|\frac{\partial(F_{1},...,F_{n})}{\partial(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\right|\;,

hence
\mathcal{B}_{1}
is just the determinant of the Jacobian of
{\bf F}
, while each

is a determinant in which the previous

These will be used for detecting singular intersections of the hypersurfaces

To establish non-degeneracy requires a larger family of functions, firstly the extended determinant

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1}=\left|\frac{\partial(F_{1},...,F_{n},\mathcal{B}_{1})}{\partial(x_{1},...,x_{n},\alpha_{1})}\right|\;,

(8)

and its extensions to higher codimensions. To define these we need a little notation. For any scalar
V
, let us denote

(F_{1},...,F_{n})\backslash^{k}V=(F_{1},...,F_{k-1},V,F_{k+1},...,F_{n})\;,

so for instance the numerator of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
in

7

\partial(F_{1},...,F_{n}\backslash^{1}\mathcal{B}_{i-1})
.
Let

denote an

of symbols
i_{j}\in\left\{1,...,n\right\}
, and define a generalization of the determinants

as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{s,I(s-1)}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\partial(F_{1},...,F_{n})\backslash^{i_{s-1}}\mathcal{B}_{s-1,I(s-2)}}{\partial(x_{1},...,x_{n})}\right|

(9)

for
s=2,...,r,
defining
\mathcal{B}_{1,I(0)}=\mathcal{B}_{1}
. Note that
\mathcal{B}_{i,1...1}\equiv\mathcal{B}_{i}
. Lastly then, we can define a set of extended determinants of these functions given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{r,I(r-1)}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\partial(F_{1},...,F_{n},\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,I(1)},...,\mathcal{B}_{r,I(r-1)})}{\partial(x_{1},...,x_{n},\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{r})}\right|\;,

(10)

.
For ease of reference these ‘

-
\mathcal{G}
functions’ are expanded in

appendix

If the vector field
{\bf F}
depends on more than

\mathcal{G}_{r,I(r-1)}^{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{r}}

Looking slightly ahead to the role of these

Notably, the family of functions

Using these we propose the following definitions.

Definition 3.2
.

A vector field
{\bf F}:U\times A\to V
exhibits an

of codimension

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}=0\neq\mathcal{B}_{r+1}\;.

We say the underlying catastrophe is

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{r,i_{1}...i_{r-1}}\neq 0\;,

The conditions

11

, in the sense that the augmented Jacobian determinants

Following Thom’s classification in

We propose this as a working definition. Its purpose is to identify the location of some catastrophe that underlies some singularity or bifurcation, whose proper equivalence class can then be found by standard local analysis.
By giving up the strict adherence to equivalence classes in this way, we obtain a practical method to locate bifurcation points via their underlying catastrophe only.

The name

highlights that we are extending the use of the term ‘catastrophe’ beyond functions that are reducible to Thom’s elementary catastrophes, but whose zeroes bifurcate in an analogous way. Moreover in
section

4

. The purpose of

One hopes that these definitions can be refined in the context of singularity theory, by relating the conditions

11

12

An important notion here is of being ‘full’ in

3.2
, which ensures that the system

In some circumstances a vector field that is not ‘full’ can be made so by the removal of redundant dimensions. For example, the dynamical system

More precisely, the conditions

, …

, hence in defining

3.2
Geometric interpretation

The idea behind
definition

3.2
is that catastrophes occur where the intersection of the hypersurfaces
F_{i}=0
has an
r^{th}
order contact at
({\bf x}_{*},{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{*})
, such that in a neighbourhood of
{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{*}
the system has up to and including
r+1
critical points.

In
n=1
dimension, the critical points of
F_{1}
can be identified with turning points of Thom’s potential functions
[
20
,
26
]
. In a codimension
r
catastrophe,
r+1
critical points of
F_{1}
coincide at a point
c
where
\mbox{$\frac{d\;}{dx_{1}}$}F_{1}=...=\mbox{$\frac{d^{r}\;}{dx_{1}^{r}}$}F_{1}=0
.
Each successive
\mbox{$\frac{d^{i}\;}{dx_{1}^{i}}$}F_{1}
that vanishes implies at least an
i^{th}
order degeneracy of the given root of
F_{i}=0
at
c
.

The purpose of each function
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is to generalize these derivatives, by thinking of a fold being a degeneracy of zeros of
F_{1}
due to coinciding with the set
\mbox{$\frac{d\;}{dx_{1}}$}F_{1}=0
, a cusp being a degeneracy between the fold and the zeros of
F_{1}
due to coinciding with the set
\mbox{$\frac{d^{2}\;}{dx_{1}^{2}}$}F_{1}=0
, and so on. We can then generalize those conditions to a vector field
{\bf F}
.
In two dimensions, for example, the zero sets of the two functions
F_{1}=x_{1}^{p}-x_{2}
and
F_{2}=x_{2}
clearly have order
p
contact at the origin, as the distance between them grows as
|x_{1}|^{p}
. For this we have

4
Primary forms

It is not in our interest here to define normal forms for the catastrophes or bifurcations of vector fields, since these are already provided in
[
3
]
. But it is instructive to embed the one-dimensional principal families
eq.

3
in an
n
-dimensional vector field, and we shall see how the functions
\mathcal{B}_{r}
then reduce in a natural way.

Let us define
primary forms
having the general expression

\displaystyle{\bf F}=\Big(\;\phi(x_{1})+\underline{k}\cdot\underline{x}\;,\;\lambda_{2}x_{2}\;,\;...\;,\;\lambda_{n}x_{n}\;\Big)

(16)

where
\underline{k}\cdot\underline{x}=k_{2}x_{2}+...+k_{n}x_{n}
(the underline denoting that these are
(n-1)
-dimensional vectors), and

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1})

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{r+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}x_{1}^{i-1}\;,

(17)

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{r+1}+\alpha_{r}x_{1}^{r-1}+...+\alpha_{2}x_{1}+\alpha_{1}\;.

These are consistent with Arnold’s
principal families

[
3
]
embedded along the
x_{1}
-axis of a system with dimension
n>1
.
The terms
\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{n}
, are just eigenvalues of the Jacobian of
{\bf F}
associated with eigenvectors normal to the
x_{1}
axis, and generically are nonzero. The terms
k_{2},...,k_{n},
are nonzero constants that ensure the system
eq.

16
is
full
as per
definition

3.2
, and that although the gradients
\mbox{$\frac{d\;}{d{\bf x}}$}F_{1},...,\mbox{$\frac{d\;}{d{\bf x}}$}F_{n}
, are linearly dependent at the catastrophe point, any
n-1
of these gradients are typically
are
linearly independent. The terms
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{r},
are bifurcation parameters.

For the system
eq.

16
the underlying catastrophe conditions take the simple form of derivatives in
x_{1}
.
To show this, define an
(n-1)
-dimensional square matrix
\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{n})
, such that
|\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}|=\lambda_{2}...\lambda_{n}
. Let
\underline{0}
represent the
(n-1)
-dimensional zero row or column vector as appropriate, and denote the derivatives of
\phi
with respect to
x_{1}
as
\phi^{\prime}
,
\phi^{\prime\prime}
, etc..
Then by straightforward calculation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(F_{1},...,F_{n})}{\partial(x_{1},...,x_{n})}$}\right|=\left|{\begin{array}[]{cccccc}\phi^{\prime}(x_{1})&\underline{k}\\
\underline{0}&\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}\end{array}}\right|=\lambda_{2}...\lambda_{n}\;\phi^{\prime}(x_{1})\;,

and for
r>1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{r}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{1},F_{2},...,F_{n})}{\partial(x_{1},...,x_{n})}$}\right|=\left|{\begin{array}[]{cccccc}|\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}|^{r-1}\phi^{(r)}(x_{1})&\underline{0}\\
\underline{0}&\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}\end{array}}\right|\qquad\quad

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{2}...\lambda_{n})^{r}\phi^{(r)}(x_{1})\;.

(22)

In addition, clearly
\phi^{\prime}(x_{1})
is also an eigenvalue of the Jacobian, some
\lambda_{1}=\phi^{\prime}(x_{1})
, hence
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}...\lambda_{r}
is just the determinant of the Jacobian of
{\bf F}
.

We can make a connection between these expressions and singularity theory in different ways. Perhaps the most simple is to note that the function
\phi(x_{1})
is the normal form of a singularity of the mapping

(x_{1},\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{r})\mapsto\left({\phi(x_{1}),\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{r}}\right)\;,

called a
Morin singularity
, see e.g.
[
12
,
16
,
18
]
).
The family of vector fields
{\bf F}
are unfoldings of

\displaystyle{\bf F}_{0}=\Big(\;x_{1}^{r+1}+\underline{k}\cdot\underline{x}\;,\;\lambda_{2}x_{2}\;,\;...\;,\;\lambda_{n}x_{n}\;\Big)\;.

(23)

An alternative unfolding of
eq.

23
can be written for
n=r+1
as

\displaystyle{\bf F}=\Big(\;\psi(x_{1})+\underline{k}\cdot\underline{x}\;,\;\lambda_{2}(x_{2}-\alpha_{1})\;,\;...\;,\;\lambda_{n}(x_{n}-\alpha_{r})\;\Big)

(24)

where

\displaystyle\psi(x_{1})=x_{1}^{r+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}x_{i+1}x_{1}^{i-1}\;,

(25)

in which
\psi(x_{1})
is then the normal form of a Morin singularity of the mapping

(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\mapsto\left({\psi(x_{1}),x_{2},,...,x_{n}}\right)\;.

We can generalize this to
n>r+1
by just adding to this
F_{i}=x_{i}
for
i>r+1
. Again the functions
\mathcal{B}_{r}
simplify to the same one dimensional derivatives
section

4
as the primary form
eq.

16
. Morin singularities are a special case of singularities within Thom’s much more general classification, namely those with Thom-Boardman symbols
\left\{i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{r}\right\}=\left\{1,1,...,1\right\}
. The Thom-Boardman symbols for mappings in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
are a non-increasing sequence of integers
\left\{i_{1},i_{2},...\right\}
, where each
i_{j}
is the rank of a Jacobian matrix consisting of
{\bf F}
and of its minors of higher orders, taken via an iterative procedure as set out in
[
4
,
7
,
25
,
24
]
. While this and other methods (see e.g.
[
13
,
21
]
) may permit one to classify the type of singularity in a mapping at a given point, as we have noted, our aim here is more pragmatically to first find
where
any given bifurcation event may occur, before such standard methods can then be used to determine its precise classification.

Below we use the conditions in

The index notation used in previous sections is convenient for general formulae, but for easier reading in the following examples we use variables

5.1
Swallowtails (
r=3
): a cubic-quadratic form

An obvious form of swallowtail catastrophe would be

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=\left({y+x^{4}+\alpha x^{2}+\beta x+\gamma,\;y}\right)\;,

and one can imagine numerous generalisations where the relation to Arnold’s principal families or Thom’s one-dimensional catastrophes is obvious.
Let us instead take a less trivial example, and show that it has a swallowtail catastrophe without performing any dimension reduction.

Consider the system

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=\left({y+x^{3}+\alpha x+\beta,\;y^{2}+x+\gamma}\right)\;,

(26)

which could have up to six critical points, which we label
(x_{*},y_{*})

\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha+3x^{2}&1\\
1&2y\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}=-1+2\alpha y_{*}+6x_{*}^{2}y_{*}\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{1},g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{ccc}12xy&2\alpha+6x^{2}\\
1&2y\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}=24x_{*}y_{*}^{2}-6x_{*}^{2}-2\alpha\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{3}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{2},g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cccc}24y_{*}^{2}-12x_{*}&48x_{*}y_{*}\\
1&2y\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}=24y_{*}(2y_{*}^{2}-3x_{*})\;,

which we can solve to find that these
\mathcal{B}_{i}
all vanish at

\displaystyle(x,y)=\left({\mbox{$\frac{1}{2^{3/5}3}$},\mbox{$\frac{1}{2^{4/5}}$}}\right)\;,\qquad(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\left({\mbox{$\frac{5}{2^{1/5}6}$},\mbox{$\frac{-35}{2^{4/5}27}$},\mbox{$\frac{-5}{2^{3/5}6}$}}\right)\;.

(33)

This is a swallowtail catastrophe, and is full because the degeneracy conditions are non-zero,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{3,11}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,1},\mathcal{B}_{3,11})}{\partial{(x,y,\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}}$}\right|_{*}=720\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{3,12}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,1},\mathcal{B}_{3,12})}{\partial{(x,y,\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}}$}\right|_{*}=-2^{4/5}360\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{3,21}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,2},\mathcal{B}_{3,21})}{\partial{(x,y,\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}}$}\right|_{*}=2^{3/5}360\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{3,22}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,2},\mathcal{B}_{3,22})}{\partial{(x,y,\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}}$}\right|_{*}=-2^{2/5}360\;.

(34)

Emanating from the swallowtail point in
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
space, there are curves of cusps where
\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}=\mathcal{B}_{2}{}_{*}=0
, and a surface of folds where
\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}=0
, each with suitable non-degeneracy conditions, only violated at the higher codimension catastrophes.

It is not easy to express the fold and cusp sets explicitly, but we can find them parametrically using the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions, resulting in the illustration in
fig.

5
.

shown from two directions. In

The fold set can be parameterised by setting

,

, as parameters, and seeking a solution of

in terms of these, giving

\displaystyle\alpha=(1-6s^{2}t)/2t\;,\quad\beta=2s^{3}-t-\mbox{$\frac{s}{2t}$}\;,\quad\gamma=-t^{2}-s\;.

(35)

Solving also
\mathcal{B}_{2}=0
we find the cusps appear at

\displaystyle\alpha=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2t}$}-\mbox{$\frac{1}{192t^{6}}$}\;,\quad\beta=\mbox{$\frac{1}{6912t^{9}}$}-\mbox{$\frac{1}{48t^{4}}$}-t\;,\quad\gamma=-\mbox{$\frac{1}{24t^{3}}$}-t^{2}\;.

5.2
Butterflies (
r=4
)

Similarly to the previous section, one can imagine numerous obvious ways of embedding the known normal forms of butterfly catastrophes in higher dimensions, for example

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=\left({y+x^{5}+\alpha x^{3}+\beta x^{2}+\gamma x,\;y-\delta}\right)\;.

However, as in the last section, let us look at a more novel form, followed by the application that inspired it.

Consider the vector field

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=\left({y+x^{3}+\alpha x+\beta,\;x+y^{3}+\gamma y+\delta}\right)\;,

(37)

which could, based on its order, have potentially up to 9 critical points
(x_{*},y_{*})
. The determinants
\mathcal{B}_{j}
evaluate as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=(\alpha+3x_{*}^{2})(\gamma+3y_{*}^{2})-1\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{1},g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=6x_{*}(\gamma+3y_{*}^{2})^{2}-6y_{*}(\alpha+3x_{*}^{2})\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{3}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{2},g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=6(\alpha+3x_{*}^{2})+6(\gamma+3y_{*}^{2})\left({(\gamma+3y_{*}^{2})^{2}-18x_{*}y_{*}}\right)\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{4}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{3},g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=72\left({15x_{*}y_{*}^{2}-3\gamma^{2}y_{*}-27y_{*}^{5}+2\gamma(x_{*}-9y_{*}^{3})}\right)\;.

(38)

These all vanish, indicating butterfly catastrophes, at

\displaystyle x=y=\pm\mbox{$\frac{1}{3}$}\;,\qquad\alpha=\gamma=\mbox{$\frac{2}{3}$}\;,\quad\beta=\delta=\mp\mbox{$\frac{16}{27}$}\;,

(39)

with non-degeneracy conditions that all evaluate as

\mathcal{G}_{4,i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}=\pm 103680
for any
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\in\left\{1,2\right\}
, hence they are full.
Emanating from the butterfly point in
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)
space, there are curves of swallowtails where
\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}=\mathcal{B}_{2}{}_{*}=\mathcal{B}_{3}{}_{*}=0
, surfaces of cusps where
\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}=\mathcal{B}_{2}{}_{*}=0
, and a volume of folds where
\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}=0
, each with suitable non-degeneracy conditions that are only violated at the higher codimension catastrophes.

As in the previous section, we can find a parameterisation of these geometries using the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions. To find the folds, solving
f=g=\mathcal{B}_{1}=0
for
(\alpha,\beta,\delta)
, and taking as parameters
x=s
,
y=t
, and
\gamma
, we find

\displaystyle\alpha=\mbox{$\frac{1}{\gamma+3t^{2}}$}-3s^{2}\;,\quad\beta=2s^{3}-t-\mbox{$\frac{s}{\gamma+3t^{2}}$}\;,\quad\delta=-s-\gamma t-t^{3}\;.

(40)

To find the cusps, solving also
\mathcal{B}_{2}=0
for
\gamma
in terms of the same parameters we find
\gamma=\mbox{$\frac{t^{1/3}}{s^{1/3}}$}-3t^{2}
, giving

\displaystyle\alpha=a(s,t)\;,\quad\beta=b(s,t)\;,\quad\gamma=a(t,s)\;,\quad\delta=b(t,s)\;,

(41)

where

\displaystyle a(s,t)=\mbox{$\frac{s^{1/3}}{t^{1/3}}$}-3s^{2}\;,\quad b(s,t)=2s^{3}-t-\mbox{$\frac{s^{4/3}}{t^{1/3}}$}\;.

(42)

The swallowtail is slightly more difficult to solve for. If we let
(s,t)=\big((uv)^{1/2},(v/u)^{1/2}\big)
, with inverse
(u,v)=(s/t,st)
, then
\mathcal{B}_{3}=0
has the solution
v=(u^{2/3}+u^{-2/3})/18
, hence we have swallowtail curves parameterised by

as

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=a\left({(uv)^{1/2},(v/u)^{1/2}}\right)\;,\quad\beta=b\left({(uv)^{1/2},(v/u)^{1/2}}\right)\;,

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=a\left({(v/u)^{1/2},(uv)^{1/2}}\right)\;,\quad\delta=b\left({(v/u)^{1/2},(uv)^{1/2}}\right)\;,

(43)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=(u^{2/3}+u^{-2/3})/18\;.

Lastly the butterfly is found by solving
\mathcal{B}_{4}=0
, which happens simply when
u=1
, giving

\displaystyle\alpha=\gamma=\mbox{$\frac{2}{3}$}\;,\quad\beta=\delta=\pm\mbox{$\frac{16}{27}$}\;.

(44)

We delay illustrations of this case to the more general scenario in the next section.

5.3
A motivating PDE example: stars and butterflies

The search for the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions in this paper was motivated by an application to a PDE system for modeling polarity in cell structures
[
2
]
(which includes bifurcation diagrams found by numerical continuation of the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions). Schematically this took the form

\displaystyle\mbox{$\frac{\partial\;}{\partial t}$}(A,B)

\displaystyle=\mbox{$\frac{\partial^{2}\;}{\partial u^{2}}$}(A,B)+(f,g)\;,

(45)

where

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=\left({\rho y+x^{3}+\alpha x+kxy+\beta,\;\sigma x+y^{3}+\gamma y+kxy+\delta}\right)\;.

(46)

The problem of interest in the application is to study both local and global spatiotemporal patterns, specifically heteroclinic connections between homogeneous steady states where
(f,g)=(0,0)
, which exist in regions bounded by catastrophes of the vector field
(f,g)
.

The system
eq.

46
is just a slight generalization of the double-cubic butterfly in
eq.

37
, but its analysis is made rather more difficult by the non-gradient terms
kxy
and
\rho\neq\sigma
. Nevertheless, like the system in
eq.

37
, this is invariant under the symmetry

(x,y,\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\rho,\sigma)\mapsto(y,x,\gamma,\delta,\alpha,\beta,\sigma,\rho)\;,

and this can be exploited to help find the critical points and catastrophes.

One can use the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions to find the full set of catastrophes numerically. We can also solve for them analytically if we restrict ourselves slightly, for example to looking for catastrophes that lie on the part of the invariant surface
x=y
,
\rho=\sigma
. Then solving
f=g=\mathcal{B}_{1}=0
, letting
x=s
,
y=t
, be parameters, we find the fold set is given by

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\mbox{$\frac{s\left\{\rho^{2}+3\rho s(k+s)+\delta(k+3s)+s^{2}(k^{2}-2ks-6s^{2})\right\}}{2s^{3}-\rho s-\delta}$}\;,

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\mbox{$\frac{\delta s(\rho-2s^{2})-s^{3}(k+2s)(2\rho+ks-2s^{2})}{2s^{3}-\rho s-\delta}$}\;,

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=-\rho-s(k+s)-\mbox{$\frac{\delta}{s}$}\;,

(47)

and this is full since
\mathcal{G}_{1}
(which we will not write here for brevity) does not vanish at typical
s,\rho,\delta,k
.

Solving also
\mathcal{B}_{2}=0
we find cusps at

\displaystyle\alpha=\gamma=\rho-3s^{2}\;,\quad\beta=\delta=s\left\{s(2s-k)-2\rho\right\}\;.

(48)

Here we find
\mathcal{G}_{2,i}=(-1)^{i}72(\rho+ks)^{2}(\rho+4ks-9s^{2})s
for

Closing Remarks

The
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions can be thought of as a geometric test. The functions
\mathcal{B}_{r}
characterise the order of contact between the hypersurfaces
F_{i}=0
as they intersect to form a critical point
{\bf F}=0
. They are therefore a means to
locate
bifurcation points, but as they contain no information about directionality of
{\bf F}
, they do not define bifurcation equivalence classes. Instead they define an underlying catastrophe, at which a bifurcation of the vector field occurs which may unfold via a number of non-equivalent routes. For example, if the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions reveal a swallowtail involving four equilibria in a vector field, then the bifurcation there could be a simple swallowtail, involving a bifurcation of four equilibria with no other local topological degeneracies, or it could involve homoclinic or heteroclinic connections and limit cycles, which require further local stability analysis beyond the underlying catastrophe. Once the underlying catastrophe is located, its stability and topology can be studied using standard local methods to fully define its bifurcation class.

The conditions of being
full
are necessary to guarantee that the catastrophe conditions have unique solutions. If the
\mathcal{G}
conditions are violated, then the problem
{\bf F}=\mathcal{B}_{1}=...=\mathcal{B}_{r}=0
may be unsolvable for some choices of the
\mathcal{B}_{i,j}
(recall in
definition

3.2
we are able to select
\mathcal{B}_{i,I(i-1)}=\mathcal{B}_{i,1...1}
without loss of generality), and then closer study is required to find the nature of the degeneracy. This is one reason why the relation between these conditions, and the classifications of singularity theory, are not yet fully understood; in seeking more practicality we have had to give up some generality.

Hence it remains to develop a theory relating the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions to the known classifications of singularity theory, to understand how they translate into certain germs, Thom-Boardman symbols, and versality conditions, and ultimately determine whether the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions at codimension
r
are sufficient to imply that a point breaks into
r+1
critical points under perturbation. The geometrical insight used here is rather different to the more powerful but less directly calculable classifications of singularity theory, namely that rather than characterize a singularity via its equivalence to a germ and the ranks of certain ideals, we interpret a critical point as an intersection of certain hypersurfaces and translate this into a readily calculable set of
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions. It is not immediately obvious how to relate these two approaches, but is no doubt possible.

In certain circumstances, a vanishing of some of the
\mathcal{G}
conditions may constitute a trivial violation of the property of being ‘full’, which may be resolved by eliminating one or more redundant dimensions (see
section

B.5
). It may be possible to derive a test for this trivial form of degeneracy.

Although the catastrophe conditions are derived from the geometry of the nullclines
F_{i}=0
, which are coordinate dependent, the functions
\mathcal{B}_{i}
are determinants expressing linear independency of the components of
{\bf F}
, and are therefore coordinate
in
dependent.

Appendix B
Further examples: folds and cusps

Although folds and cusps are relatively easy to study using conventional theory, we include a few examples here to further illustrate the calculation of the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions in
definition

3.2
.
For a fold in particular, the condition
\mathcal{B}_{1}=0
is just the well known condition that the system’s Jacobian has less than full rank at a singularity, so our interest lies mainly in interpreting the non-degeneracy conditions.

In some cases we apply the conditions to a dynamical system
(\dot{x},\dot{y},...)=(f,g,...)
to highlight the significance of ignoring the directionality of a vector field.

B.1
Simple fold

Consider the vector field

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=(x^{2}-y,\;y-\alpha)\;.

(54)

The critical points lie at
(x_{*},y_{*})=(\pm\sqrt{\alpha},\alpha)
, at which the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions evaluate as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cc}2x&-1\\
0&1\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}=\pm 2\sqrt{\alpha}\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,\mathcal{B}_{1})}{\partial{(x,y,\alpha)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{ccc}2x&-1&0\\
0&1&-1\\
2&0&0\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}=2\;.

We see that
\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}
vanishes at
\alpha=0
, and this is a full fold since
\mathcal{G}_{1}{}_{*}\neq 0
. The familiar bifurcation diagram is shown in
fig.

7
. In a dynamical system
(\dot{x},\dot{y})=(f,g)
this is a degenerate equilibrium that perturbs into a saddle and node.

Figure 7:
A simple fold where two branches of critical points coalesce.

If we swap
(f,g)\mapsto(g,f)
or
(f,g)\mapsto(f,-g)
, the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions are unchanged, and this remains a full fold. These vector fields have very different directional properties, though, and are only
topologically
equivalent up to a time reversal. This is because the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions detect only the underlying bifurcation and neglect directionality of the vector field. This is particularly well highlighted if we take a similar vector field

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=(y,\;x^{2}-\alpha)\;.

(56)

Here the fold that occurs is between a saddle and a center, as
(f,g)
is divergence-free, and therefore is not topologically equivalent to the cases above. Nevertheless, by classifying what happens at
x=y=\alpha=0
as a
fold
catastrophe underlying the topological and stability features of the vector field, we are able to locate the bifurcation point using the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions.

B.2
Generalised codimension 3 fold of
Dumortier, Roussarie, & Sotomayor

There are various non-trivial generalisations of the fold bifurcation in the singularity theory literature, for example the codimension 2 ‘fold-Hopf’ or ‘zero-Hopf’ bifurcation. Let us take an example from
[
11
]
, a codimension 3 fold-Hopf bifurcation with normal form

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=\left({y,\;x^{2}-\alpha+y(k_{0}+k_{1}x+x^{3})}\right)\;.

(57)

This has critical points
(x_{*},y_{*})=(\pm\sqrt{\alpha},0)
. Taking
\alpha
as the bifurcation parameter, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
2x+y(3x^{2}+k_{1})&k_{0}+k_{1}x+x^{3}\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}=\pm 2\sqrt{\alpha}\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,\mathcal{B}_{1})}{\partial{(x,y,\alpha)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1&0\\
2x+\alpha y(3x^{2}+k_{1})&k_{0}+k_{1}x+x^{3}&-1\\
-2-6xy&-k_{1}-3x^{2}&0\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}=2\;.

This therefore satisfies the conditions of a full fold at
\alpha=0
. The higher order bifurcation studied by
Dumortier et al.
in
[
11
]
is generated by the
y(k_{0}+k_{1}x+x^{3})
term in
eq.

57
, and is associated with a loss of stability that occurs where the trace of the Jacobian,
k_{0}\pm k_{1}\alpha^{1/2}\pm\alpha^{3/2}
, vanishes, around which can occur, in the language of a dynamical system
(\dot{x},\dot{y})=(f,g)
, Hopf bifurcations, homoclinic connections, and a saddle whose eigenvalues have equal magnitude.

Figure 8:
Bifurcation sets of the codimension 3 fold, showing the fold set where

and the set ‘stab’ where

. Right: sections of the ‘stab’ surface for

(red) and
k_{1}<0
(blue), with a cusp at
k_{1}=0
.

These sets are illustrated in parameter space in
fig.

. Two branches of the ‘stab’ surface intersect along the line
k_{0}=k_{1}+\alpha=0
. A codimension 3 catastrophe occurs at the origin. See
[

]
for in-depth analysis of this singularity, where it is referred to as a ‘cusp’ bifurcation due to the geometry of the stability curve, while here we classify it as a fold in keeping with it being a ‘fold’ of the critical points
x_{*}=\pm\sqrt{\alpha}
. The
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}

B.3
Bogdanov-Takens bifurcation

The vector field

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=(y,\;\alpha+\beta x+x^{2}-xy)\;

(59)

has critical points
(x_{*},y_{*})=(\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(-\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\alpha}),0)
. Let us consider taking either
\alpha
or
\beta
as a bifurcation parameter for a fold, and evaluate

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\

A fold occurs along the curve
\alpha=\beta^{2}/4
in parameter space. The function
\mathcal{G}_{1}^{\alpha}
indicates that the fold is full if we vary

\beta

indicates that the fold is full except at

9

As before, the

-

conditions reveal the underlying fold catastrophe. In this case a stability change also creates, taking a dynamical system
(\dot{x},\dot{y})=(f,g)
, an Andronov-Hopf bifurcation along the half-line
\alpha=0
,

, producing a limit cycle that disappears in a homoclinic bifurcation along a curve

B.4
Fold set as a Whitney umbrella

The vector field

\displaystyle(f,g,h)=(x^{2}+2zx+cy^{2},\;y-\beta,\;z-\alpha)

(61)

has critical points at
(x_{*},y_{*},z_{*})=(-\alpha\pm\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\gamma},\beta,\alpha)
. This is a contrived example but shows some novel geometry as seen in
fig.

10
.

The double conic set
f=0
and line

(left), and the fold set forming a Whitney umbrella (right).

The hypersurface
f=0
is a double conic surface, forming equilibria where it is pierced by the straight line given by
y-\beta=z-\alpha=0
, and as either of these move we will observe folds. We have 3 parameters and so could investigate bifurcations with respect to either of them, and we would obtain similar results, so let us take
\alpha

The
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,h)}{\partial{(x,y,z)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{ccc}2x+2z&2\gamma y&2x\\

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,h,\mathcal{B}_{1})}{\partial{(x,y,z,\alpha)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cccc}2x+2z&2\gamma y&2x&0\\

There is a fold where

B.5
Simple cusp, and the property of being ‘full’

Consider

\displaystyle(f,g,h)=(y+x^{3}+\alpha x+\beta,\;y+kz,z)\;,

(63)

which has critical points
(x_{*},y_{*},z_{*})=(x_{*},0,0)
, where
x_{*}
are solutions of a cubic polynomial with discriminant
(\alpha/3)^{3}+(\beta/2)^{2}
. The
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions evaluate as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,h)}{\partial{(x,y,z)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha+3x^{2}&1&0\\

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{1},g,h)}{\partial{(x,y,z)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{ccc}6x&0&0\\

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2,1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,h,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,1})}{\partial{(x,y,z,\alpha,\beta)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\alpha+3x^{2}&1&0&x&1\\

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2,2}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,h,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,2})}{\partial{(x,y,z,\alpha,\beta)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\alpha+3x^{2}&1&0&x&1\\

-6&0&0&0&0\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}=-6\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2,3}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,h,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,3})}{\partial{(x,y,z,\alpha,\beta)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\alpha+3x^{2}&1&0&x&1\\

0&0&1&0&0\\

We omit the intermediate calculations of the determinants
\mathcal{B}_{2,i}
. The familiar bifurcation diagram is shown in

.

As one would expect, the first condition
\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}
vanishes to create folds on the curve

(\alpha/3)^{3}+(\beta/2)^{2}=0

in parameter space, which are non-degenerate with
\mathcal{G}_{1}^{\alpha}{}_{*}=\alpha-2x_{*}^{2}
and
\mathcal{G}_{1}^{\beta}{}_{*}=-6x_{*}
(i.e. taken with respect to either
\alpha
or
\beta
), both non-zero provided
\alpha,\beta\neq 0
.

The second bifurcation condition
\mathcal{B}_{2}{}_{*}
vanishes at

, for which

(at which we see the non-degeneracy conditions for the fold would also be violated). Note that all

Note, however, that non-degeneracy requires

. This is an important illustration of the property of being ‘full’ in

, and stems from the gradient vectors, which evaluate at the cusp to
\mbox{$\frac{d\;}{d{\bf x}}$}f=(0,1,0)
,

,

), but to use

. Although the cusp obtained if

for

being unique. However, this also signals here that the variable

appears in a trivial fashion, and the resolution then is simply to isolate off the

B.6
A cusp with a degeneracy

Consider the planar vector field

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=\left({y^{2}+x^{3}-\alpha x^{2}+\gamma x-\beta,\;y-k}\right)\;,

with critical points
(x_{*},y_{*})=(x_{*},k)

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cc}\gamma-2\alpha x+3x^{2}&2y\\

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{1},g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cc}6x-2\alpha&0\\
0&1\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}=6x_{*}-2\alpha\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2,1}^{\alpha\beta}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,1})}{\partial{(x,y,\alpha,\beta)}}$}\right|_{*}=-4\alpha\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2,2}^{\alpha\beta}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,2})}{\partial{(x,y,\alpha,\beta)}}$}\right|_{*}=8\alpha k\;.

(66e)

There are folds where
\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}=0
when
(\beta+\mbox{$\frac{2}{27}$}a^{3}-k^{2}-\mbox{$\frac{1}{3}$}\alpha\gamma)^{2}=4(\alpha^{2}-3\gamma)^{3}
for which
x_{*}=\mbox{$\frac{1}{3}$}\alpha\pm\mbox{$\frac{1}{3}$}\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-3\gamma}
.

There are cusps when
\alpha=\pm\sqrt{3\gamma}
,
\beta=k^{2}\pm(\gamma/3)^{3/2}
, for which
x_{*}=\pm\sqrt{\gamma/3}
, hence we see these only occur for
\gamma>0
. Since the various
\mathcal{G}_{2,i}{}_{*}
are non-zero (and one finds similar results considering any pair of parameters from
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
), we see that the cusps are non-degenerate away from the origin of this parameter space. Note the importance of
k\neq 0
for non-degeneracy.

B.7
Cusp: double-quadratic form

A less obvious form for the cusp is

\displaystyle(f,g)

\displaystyle=\left({x^{2}-y-\alpha,\;y^{2}-\beta x-ky}\right)\;,

(67)

with critical points
(x_{*},y_{*})
that are roots of
x_{*}^{2}-y_{*}-\alpha=y_{*}^{2}-\beta x_{*}-ky_{*}=0
. For the
\mathcal{B}
-
\mathcal{G}
conditions we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cc}2x&-1\\
-\beta&2y-k\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}=4x_{*}y_{*}-2kx_{*}-\beta\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(\mathcal{B}_{1},g)}{\partial{(x,y)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cc}4y-2k&4x\\
-\beta&2y-k\end{array}$}}\right|=2(2y_{*}-k)^{2}+4\beta x_{*}\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2,1}{}_{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,1})}{\partial{(x,y,\alpha,\beta)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cccc}2x&-1&-1&0\\
-\beta*2y-k&0&-x\\

4\beta&16y-8k&0&4x\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}

\displaystyle=-12(k-2y_{*})(\beta-2kx_{*}+4x_{*}y_{*})\;,

\displaystyle=\left|\mbox{$\frac{\partial(f,g,\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2,2})}{\partial{(x,y,\alpha,\beta)}}$}\right|_{*}=\left|{\mbox{\scriptsize$\begin{array}[]{cccc}2x&-1&-1&0\\
-\beta*2y-k&0&-x\\
4y-2k&4x&0&-1\\
16x&4&0&0\end{array}$}}\right|_{*}

\displaystyle=4(\beta-2x_{*}(3k+8x_{*}^{2}-6y*))\;.

(68m)
