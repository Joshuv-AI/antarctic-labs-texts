Connecting Zeros in Pisano Periods to Prime Factors of K K -Fibonacci Numbers
Brennan Benfield, Oliver Lippard

Abstract
. The Fibonacci sequence is periodic modulo every positive integer m > 1 m>1 , and perhaps more surprisingly, each period has exactly 1, 2, or 4 zeros that are evenly spaced, which also holds true for more general K K -Fibonacci sequences. This paper proves several conjectures connecting the zeros in the Pisano period to the prime factors of K K -Fibonacci numbers. The congruence classes of indices for K K -Fibonacci numbers that are multiples of the prime factors of m m completely determine the number of zeros in the Pisano period modulo m m .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
11B39, 11B50

1.
Introduction

The Fibonacci sequence is defined by
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=1
, and
F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}
. A curious property was first recognized in 1877 by Lagrange
[
12
]
: the terms in the Fibonacci sequence modulo
10
(i.e. the one’s place digits) repeat every
60
terms. Inquiry regarding the periodicity of the sequence modulo a positive integer has continued and in 2004 it was proven by Everest & Shparlinski
[
6
]
that
every
binary recurrence sequence is periodic modulo a positive integer
m>1
.

Define the
Pisano period
as the length of one (shortest) period of the Fibonacci sequence modulo
m
, denoted
\pi(m)
. It is well established
[
8
,
13
,
20
]
that one period of the Fibonacci sequence modulo any
m>1
has exactly 1, 2, or 4 zeros, equally spaced in the cycle. The number of zeros in a Pisano period is the
order
of
m
, denoted
\omega(m)
. The
rank
of
m
is the index of the Fibonacci number of the first zero in a Pisano period. In other words, the rank, denoted
\alpha(m)
, is the index of the first Fibonacci number divisible by
m
. There are two conjectures in the OEIS: A053029
[
15
]
and A053031
[
17
]
regarding exactly which
m
has order 1, 2, or 4. Note that the Fibonacci sequence mod 1 is traditionally not considered and
\omega(1)
is defined to equal 1.

Conjecture 1.1

(A053029
[
15
]
)
.

An integer
m
has four zeros in its Pisano period if and only if
m
is an odd number, all of whose factors have four zeros in their Pisano period, or if
m
is twice such a number.

Conjecture 1.2

(A053031
[
17
]
)
.

An integer
m
has one zero in its Pisano period if and only if
m
is an odd number, all of whose factors have one zero in their Pisano period, or if
m
is twice or four times such a number.

It follows that all other positive integers have exactly two zeros in their Pisano period. This paper proves these conjectures and uses this curious property to show the relationship between the order of
m
and the prime factors of
F_{n}
. Namely, the order of a positive integer depends on exactly when it is a prime factor of
F_{n}
according to the congruence class of
n
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For a positive integer
m
,

(i)

For a positive integer
m
,
\omega(m)=4
for
m>3
if and only if m has prime factorization
m=2^{j}p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{e_{r}}
where
j\in\{0,1\}
and for each
1\leq i\leq r
there is an odd index
n_{i}
such that
p_{i}\mid F_{n_{i}}
.

(ii)

For a positive integer
m
,
\omega(m)=1
for
m>3
if and only if m has prime factorization
m=2^{j}p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{e_{r}}
where
j\in\{0,1,2\}
and for each
1\leq i\leq r
there is an index
n_{i}\equiv 2\pmod{4}
such that
p_{i}\mid F_{n_{i}}
, but
p_{i}\not\mid F_{\nu_{i}}
for all odd
\nu_{i}
.

(iii)

\omega(m)=2
for all other positive integers
m
.

This list is exhaustive, in the sense that every natural number falls into exactly one category of Theorem
1.3
. Note that every prime divides some Fibonacci number, and with the exceptions of
n=1,2,6,12
, every Fibonacci number
F_{n}
is divisible by a prime that is not a divisor of any smaller Fibonacci number.

Theorem 1.4

(Williams
[
21
]
)
.

For a prime
p
,
p\mid F_{p-\left(\frac{5}{p}\right)}
where
\left(\frac{5}{p}\right)
is the Legendre symbol.

Theorem 1.5

(Carmichael
[
3
]
)
.

For every index
n\neq 1,2,6,12
, there exists a prime
p
such that
p\mid F_{n}
and for all
m<n
,
p\not\mid F_{m}
.

There are theorems found in the work of Renault
[
13
]
that relate the order of
m
with the rank and the period of
m
; Theorem
2.3
collects these results in a single result.

Theorem 2.3

(Renault
[
13
]
)
.

•

For
m>3
,
\omega(m)=4
if and only if
\alpha(m)\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}
.

•

\omega(m)=2
if and only if
4\mid\pi(m)
and
2\mid\alpha(m)
.

•

\omega(m)=1
if and only if
4\not\mid\pi(m)
.

A result of Vinson
[
19
]
determines the order of powers of 2 in the Fibonacci sequence.

Theorem 2.4

(Vinson
[
19
]
)
.

\omega(2)=\omega(4)=1
, and for
x\geq 3
,
\omega(2^{x})=2
.

Renault
[
13
]
extended Vinson’s results, determining the order of any odd prime power.

Theorem 2.5

(Renault
[
13
]
)
.

For any odd prime
p
,
\omega(p^{e})=\omega(p)
.

Daykin & Dresel
[
5
]
discovered the connection between the rank of a prime that divides a Fibonacci index, and the divisibility of that Fibonacci number by the prime.

Theorem 2.6

(Daykin & Dresel
[
5
]
)
.

p\mid F_{n}
if and only if
\alpha(p)\mid n

Another useful result for the proof of Theorem
1.3
was found by Wyler, who showed the relationship between the rank of a prime and its Pisano period, according to its congruence class modulo 4.

Theorem 2.7

(Wyler
[
22
]
)
.

•

If
\alpha(p)\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, then
\pi(p)=1\cdot\alpha(p)
.

•

If
\alpha(p)\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then
\pi(p)=2\cdot\alpha(p)
.

•

If
\alpha(p)\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}
, then
\pi(p)=4\cdot\alpha(p)
.

Note that the converse of Theorem
2.7
is also true, allowing Wyler’s result to be stated as an if and only if statement.

Corollary 2.8
.

•

\omega(p)=4
if and only if
\alpha(p)\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}
.

•

\omega(p)=2
if and only if
\alpha(p)\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.

•

\omega(p)=1
if and only if
\alpha(p)\equiv 2\pmod{4}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Theorems
2.1
and
2.7
.
∎

While the second and third cases of Corollary
2.8
may seem different from Theorem
2.3
, a closer inspection reveals that Corollary
2.8
implies
2.3
. For the first case, when
\alpha(p)\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}
, the two theorems are identical. If
\alpha(p)\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, then
4\not\mid\pi(m)
, since
\pi(p)=\alpha(p)
. Likewise, if
\alpha(p)\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, then
2\mid\alpha(p)
and
4\mid\pi(p)
, since
\alpha(p)\mid\pi(p)
.

2.1.
Proof of Conjectures

With these tools in hand, proof of the conjectures found in the OEIS follow readily. As stated, these conjectures are circular (Conjecture
1.2
says that 19 has order 1 if and only if 19 has order 1, which is a tautology). Additionally, since 1 is a factor of every
m
, but
\omega(1)=1
, Conjecture
1.1
would suggest that
\omega(m)
never equals 4. Hence the idea of factors for these conjectures should exclude
1
and
m
. The case of primes cannot properly be included in these conjectures. Theorems
2.9
through
2.11
are reformulations of Conjectures
1.1
and
1.2
, respectively.

Theorem 2.9
.

If
m
is an odd number, all of whose factors (other than
1
and
m
) have order 4, or
m
is twice such a number, then
m
has order
4
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
.
∎

Theorem 2.10
.

If
m
has order
4
, then
m
is an odd number, all of whose factors (other than 1) have order
4
, or
m
is twice such a number.

Proof.

Suppose
m
has order
4
. If
m
is prime, then all of its factors have order
4
. If
m=2^{j}\cdot p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{t}^{e_{t}}
, then by Theorem
2.2
,
j\leq 1
and
\omega(p_{i}^{e_{i}})=4
for all odd prime factors of
m
.
∎

Theorem 2.11
.

If
m
has order
1
, then
m
is an odd number, all of whose factors have order 1, or
m
is twice such a number or four times such a number.

Proof.

Suppose
m=2^{j}\cdot p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{t}^{e_{t}}
and
\omega(m)=1
. By Theorem
2.4
,
j\leq 2
. By Theorem
2.2
,
\omega(ab)=1
if and only if
\omega(a)=\omega(b)=1
. Hence,
\omega(p_{i}^{e_{i}})=1
for all
1\leq i\leq t
.
∎

Theorem 2.12
.

If
m
is an odd number, all of whose factors (other than
1
and
m
) have order 1, or
m
is twice such a number or four times such a number, then
\omega(m)=1
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3

Proof. (i).

Suppose
p\mid F_{n}
for
n\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}
. By Lemma
2.6
this is true if and only if
\alpha(p)\mid n
. And for
n\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}
, this holds if and only if
\alpha(p)\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}
, which by Corollary
2.8
is true if and only if
\omega(p)=4
. From Table
2.2
,
\omega(m)=4
if and only if
m=2^{j}\cdot p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{e_{r}}
for
j\leq 1
and where
\omega(p_{i}^{e_{i}})=4
for
1\leq i\leq r
.
∎

Proof. (ii).

Suppose that
p\mid F_{n}
for
n\equiv 2\pmod{4}
and
p
is not also a divisor of some
F_{k}
for an odd index
k
. By Lemma
2.6
, this is true if and only if
\alpha(p)\mid n
and
\alpha(p)\not\mid k
for any odd
k
. Hence,
n\equiv 2\pmod{4}
if and only if
2\mid\alpha(p)
. Because
\alpha(p)\mid n
and
4\not\mid n
, it follows that
4\not\mid\alpha(p)
, so
\alpha(p)\equiv 2\pmod{4}
. By Corollary
2.8
,
\alpha(p)\equiv 2\pmod{4}
if and only if
\omega(p)=1
. From Table
2.2
,
\omega(m)=1
for
m=2^{j}\cdot p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{e_{r}}
whenever
j\geq 3
and every
p_{i}^{e_{i}}
has order 1.
∎

Proof. (iii).

This follows directly from the proof of (i) and (ii), but to illuminate the situation, consider
m=2^{j}p_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots p_{t}^{e_{t}}
. By Theorems
2.4
and
2.2
, if
j\geq 3
, then
\omega(m)=2
, and if even a single
p_{i}^{e_{i}}
has order
2
, then
\omega(m)=2
. This absorbs all divisors of
F_{n}
for indices
n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
as well as multiples of
2^{x}
times any divisor of
F_{n}
for
x\geq 3
, among other numbers.
∎

4.
K-Fibonacci and (a,b)-Fibonacci Sequences

Generalizations of the Fibonacci sequence are categorized according to which variables of a binary recurrence sequence are fixed. In particular, Renault
[
14
]
extends many properties of the Pisano period to
(a,b)
-Fibonacci sequences. These are sequences where
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=1
, and
F_{n}=aF_{n-1}+bF_{n-2}
for positive integers
a
and
b
. Unlike the Fibonacci sequence where
\omega(m)
can only equal 1, 2, or 4, such loose restrictions for
a
and
b
allow the number of zeros in a Pisano period to take on infinitely many values.

Theorem 4.1

(Renault
[
14
]
)
.

\omega(m)\mid 2\cdot\text{ord}_{m}(-b)

The reason
\omega(m)
only takes values 1, 2, and 4 in the Fibonacci sequence is because
b=1
and
\text{ord}_{m}(-1)=2
for all
m\geq 3
. Hence,
\omega(m)\mid 4
and 1, 2, and 4 are the only divisors. There is a well-studied category of binary recurrences where
b
is fixed at
1
while
a
varies. Traditionally in this case,
a
is swapped out for
K
, and these binary recurrences are known as
K
-Fibonacci sequences, where
F_{K,0}=0
,
F_{K,1}=1
, and
F_{K,n}=KF_{K,n-1}+F_{K,n-2}
. Because
b
is fixed at 1,
\omega(m)=1
, 2, or 4 for all
K
. Denote the
K
-Pisano period,
K
-order, and
K
-rank of a positive integer
m
by
\pi_{K}(m)
,
\omega_{K}(m)
, and
\alpha_{K}(m)
, respectively. Several important properties generalize to all
(a,b)
-Fibonacci sequences, which are stated here as they pertain to
K
-Fibonacci sequences:

Theorem 4.2
.

(Renault
[
14
]
)
For all
K
and
m
,
\pi_{K}(m)=\alpha_{K}(m)\omega_{K}(m)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

(Renault
[
14
]
)
For all
K
and
m
,

•

\pi_{K}(\text{lcm}[m,n])=\text{lcm}[\pi_{K}(m),\pi_{K}(n)]

•

\alpha_{K}(\text{lcm}[m,n])=\text{lcm}[\alpha_{K}(m),\alpha_{K}(n)]
.

There is a class of sequences that plays a role similar to that of the Lucas sequence for any generalized Fibonacci sequence. Fiebig, Mbirika, & Spilker
[
7
]
define a companion sequence for any
(a,b)
-Fibonacci sequence; in particular, the
K
-Lucas sequence is defined as the companion sequence for the
K
-Fibonacci sequence:

Definition 4.4
.

The
K
-Lucas sequence is the binary recurrence sequence
L_{K,n}
with
L_{K,0}=2
,
L_{K,1}=K
, and
L_{K,n}=KL_{K,n-1}+L_{K,n-2}
for
n\geq 2
.

The
K
-Lucas sequence has many remarkable relationships to the
K
-Fibonacci sequence. Presented here are two identities that will be useful later.

Lemma 4.5
.

(Fiebig, Mbirika, and Spilker
[
7
]
)
For any
K
and
n
, the following holds:

•

F_{K,2n}=F_{K,n}L_{K,n}

•

L_{K,n}=F_{K,n+1}+F_{K,n-1}
.

To extend these results to
K
-Fibonacci sequences, here some new notation is introduced.

Definition 4.6
.

Let the first number that appears after the first zero in the Pisano period modulo
m
(excluding the initial zero) be the
K
-Fibonacci residue of
m
, denoted
\beta_{K}(m)
.

For example, the residue of
5
in the classic Fibonacci sequence is
3
(in other notation,
\beta_{1}(5)=3
) because the sequence modulo 5 begins
0,1,1,2,3,0,\boxed{3},\ldots
. The residue has an interesting connection to the order, which follows from a preliminary lemma:

Lemma 4.7
.

For any
m
,
n
, and
K
, where
n\geq\alpha_{K}(m)
,
F_{K,n}\equiv\beta_{K}(m)F_{K,n-\alpha_{K}(m)}\pmod{m}
.

Proof.

It is useful to remark that
\alpha_{K}(m)
, or the rank, is the index of the first zero in the Pisano period modulo
m
. Note that
\beta_{K}(m)\equiv F_{K,\alpha_{K}(m)+1}\pmod{m}
. As a base case for an inductive proof, note that
F_{K,\alpha_{K}(m)}\equiv 0\equiv\beta_{K}(m)F_{K,0}\pmod{m}
and
F_{K,\alpha_{K}(m)+1}\equiv\beta_{K}(m)\equiv\beta_{K}(m)F_{K,1}\pmod{m}
, since
F_{K,1}=1
. Then, suppose
F_{K,n}\equiv\beta_{K}(m)F_{K,n-\alpha_{K}(m)}\pmod{m}
and
F_{K,n-1}\equiv\beta_{K}(m)F_{K,n-1-\alpha_{K}(m)}\pmod{m}
. By definition,

F_{K,n+1}\equiv K\beta_{K}(m)F_{K,n-\alpha_{K}(m)}+\beta_{K}(m)F_{K,n-1-\alpha_{K}(m)}\pmod{m}

=\beta_{K}(m)(KF_{K,n-\alpha_{K}(m)}+F_{K,n-1-\alpha_{K}(m)})

=\beta_{K}(m)F_{K,n+1-\alpha_{K}(m)},

which is the statement of the lemma with
n+1
substituted for
n
.
∎

Lemma
4.7
is a
K
-Fibonacci analog of Renault’s Identity 3.26
[
13
]
.

Proposition 4.8
.

For any
m
and
K
,
\omega_{K}(m)=\text{ord}_{m}(\beta_{K}(m))
.

Proof.

The Pisano period is complete when two consecutive entries in the
K
-Fibonacci sequence modulo
m
are
0
and
1
. By definition, the number appearing after the first zero,
F_{K,\alpha_{K}(m)+1}
is equivalent to
\beta_{K}(m)
modulo
m
. By repeated application of Lemma
4.7
,
F_{K,\alpha_{K}(m)r+1}=\left(\beta_{K}(m)\right)^{r}
. The Pisano period resets when
\left(\beta_{K}(m)\right)^{r}\equiv 1\pmod{m}
, so
r=\omega_{K}(m)=\text{ord}_{m}(\beta_{K}(m))
.
∎

The existence of the order establishes that
\gcd\left[\beta_{K}(m),m\right]=1
. There is also a matrix that parameterizes the
K
-Fibonacci sequence for any
K
, which allows a useful identity for the Fibonacci sequence to be proven for the
K
-Fibonacci sequence:

Theorem 4.9
.

(Cerda-Morales
[
4
]
).
Let
U(K)=\left(\begin{matrix}K&1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right)
, then for any integer
n\geq 1
,

U^{n}(K)=\left(\begin{matrix}F_{K,n+1}&F_{K,n}\\
F_{K,n}&F_{K,n-1}\end{matrix}\right).

The classic Fibonacci sequence is said to be
log-Fibonacci
, meaning it is log-convex at even indices and log-concave at odd indices. This property is likewise true for
K
-Fibonacci sequences.

Theorem 4.10
.

For all
n
and
K
,
F_{K,n}^{2}-F_{K,n+1}F_{K,n-1}=(-1)^{n+1}
.

Proof.

Note that det
U^{n}(K)=(\text{det }U(K))^{n}=(-1)^{n}
. But evaluating the determinant explicitly by Theorem
4.9
yields
F_{K,n+1}F_{K,n-1}-F_{K,n}^{2}
. Negating both expressions gives
F_{K,n}^{2}-F_{K,n+1}F_{K,n-1}=(-1)^{n+1}
.
∎

Renault uses a more generalized matrix to study
(a,b)
-Fibonacci sequences, but setting
b=1
provides the same result for
K
-Fibonacci sequences:

Theorem 4.11
.

(Renault
[
14
]
).
Let
K
be an integer and let
m>2
. Then
2\mid\pi_{K}(m)
.

As an immediate consequence, if the rank is odd, then the order must be even.

Corollary 4.12
.

If
\alpha_{K}(m)

Theorem 4.13

\displaystyle\equiv(-1)^{n}F_{K,n-1}-(-1)^{n+1}KF_{K,n}\pmod{m}

The following theorem is a direct generalization of the technique and result of Theorem

Theorem 4.22
.

Let

be an integer and let

•

For

\omega_{K}(m)=4
if and only if
\alpha_{K}(m)\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}

•

\omega_{K}(m)=2
if and only if

and
2\mid\alpha_{K}(m)

•

\omega_{K}(m)=1
if and only if

From Lemma

,
F^{2}_{\alpha_{K}(m)}-F_{\alpha_{K}(m)+1}F_{\alpha_{K}(m)-1}=(-1)^{\alpha_{K}(m)+1}
. This expression can be simplified since
F_{\alpha_{K}(m)}\equiv 0
and
F_{\alpha_{K}(m)+1}\equiv\beta_{K}(m)\pmod{m}
by definition. Furthermore,
F_{\alpha_{K}(m)+1}=KF_{\alpha_{K}(m)}+F_{\alpha_{K}(m)-1}\equiv F_{\alpha_{K}(m)-1}\pmod{m}
. Thus,
-\beta_{K}(m)^{2}\equiv(-1)^{\alpha_{K}(m)+1}
, and

(1)

\displaystyle\beta_{K}(m)^{2}\equiv(-1)^{\alpha_{K}(m)}.

Suppose
\alpha_{K}(m)\equiv\pm 1\pmod{4}
, so
\beta_{K}(m)^{2}\equiv-1\pmod{m}
by equation (1). It follows from Theorem
4.8
that
2\not\mid\omega_{K}(m)
. The only remaining option for
\omega_{K}(m)
is 4. For the converse, suppose
\omega_{K}(m)=4
. Then
\beta_{K}(m)^{2}\equiv-1\pmod{m}

. Because

, it follows that

Now suppose that

for an odd prime

; then it will be shown that
\omega_{K}(p)\neq 1
. Let

, which is odd since

. Then,

and hence cannot equal

. Therefore,
\pi_{K}(p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots p_{r-1}e^{r-1})=\alpha_{K}(p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots p_{r-1}e^{r-1})

\displaystyle\pi_{K}(m)=\text{lcm}[\pi_{K}(p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots p_{r-1}e^{r-1}),\pi_{K}(p_{r}^{e_{r}})]=\text{lcm}[\alpha_{K}(p_{1}^{e_{1}}p_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots p_{r-1}e^{r-1}),\alpha_{K}(p_{r}^{e_{r}})]=\alpha_{K}(m),

A general binary recurrence sequence

\mathcal{U}_{0}=c,\qquad\mathcal{U}_{1}=d,\qquad\mathcal{U}_{n}=a\times\mathcal{U}_{n-1}+b\times\mathcal{U}_{n-2}.

When

and

-Fibonacci sequences. Unfortunately, the techniques used in this paper will have to be adjusted when

In an
