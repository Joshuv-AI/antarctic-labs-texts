Stability Analysis for Electromagnetic Waveguides. Part 1: Acoustic and Homogeneous Electromagnetic Waveguides Jens M. Melenk a , Leszek Demkowicz b , Stefan Henneking b a Technische Universität Wien b Oden Institute, The University of Texas at Austin


Abstract
In a time-harmonic setting, we show for heterogeneous acoustic and homogeneous electromagnetic wavesguides stability estimates with the stability constant depending linearly on the length L L of the waveguide. These stability estimates are used for the analysis of the (ideal) ultraweak (UW) variant of the Discontinuous Petrov Galerkin (DPG) method. For this UW DPG, we show that the stability deterioration with L L can be countered by suitably scaling the test norm of the method. We present the “full envelope approximation”, a UW DPG method based on non-polynomial ansatz functions that allows for treating long waveguides.

Motivation.

Acoustic and electromagnetic (EM) waveguide problems have many important applications and are therefore discussed widely in the literature

,

It is therefore challenging to approximate the solution numerically; to obtain an accurate solution, one must sufficiently resolve the wavelength scale and additionally counter the effect of numerical pollution to overcome stability issues of the discretization
[

While the stability of Finite Element (FE) discretizations of Helmholtz and time-harmonic Maxwell problems has been analyzed in fixed domains for increasing wave frequency
\omega

[
33
,
6
,
34
]
, there is to the best of our knowledge no such corresponding analysis for the waveguide problem where
\omega
is fixed and the waveguide length
L
increases. In practical applications, this is of great relevance as the available computational tools become more powerful thereby enabling numerical solution of waveguide models of realistic length scales. We discuss the present work in the context of modeling optical fiber amplifiers but emphasize that the main results of this work are relevant to FE discretization of acoustic and EM waveguide problems with the Discontinuous Petrov–Galerkin (DPG) Method
[
5
]

in general.

Optical amplifiers.

Optical fiber amplifiers can produce highly coherent laser outputs with great efficiency, which has enabled advances in many engineering applications
[
24
]
. However, at high-power operation, these fiber laser systems are susceptible to the onset of various nonlinear effects that are adverse to the beam quality of the laser
[
1
,
27
,
38
]
. One particular challenge is mitigating the effects of heating of the silica-glass fiber. Under sufficient heat load, the fiber amplifier experiences a thermally-induced nonlinear effect called the transverse mode instability (TMI)
[
9
,
25
]
. TMI is characterized by a sudden reduction of the beam coherence above a certain power threshold.
This instability is a major limitation for the average power scaling of fiber laser systems
[
25
]
. While a scientific consensus on the thermal origins of TMI has developed over the past years, finding effective mitigation strategies that do not incite other power limiting nonlinearities remains an active field of research in fiber optics.

In the context of studying TMI and other nonlinear effects in fibers, numerical simulations play an important role. Typically, a model needs to account for two fields in the fiber amplifier: 1) the
signal laser
, a highly coherent light source that is seeded into the fiber core; and 2) the
pump field
, which provides the energy for amplification of the signal and is typically injected into the fiber cladding. A variety of different models are employed in fiber amplifier simulations (e.g.,
[
37
,
40
,
35
,
14
]
and references therein). These models are usually derived from the time-harmonic Maxwell equations, and by making additional modeling assumptions they become easier to discretize and compute than a vectorial Maxwell problem.

Vectorial Maxwell fiber amplifier model.

This work is part of a continued effort to build reliable, high-fidelity FE models for investigating TMI in optical amplifiers
[
36
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
18
]
. The model consists of a system of two nonlinear time-harmonic Maxwell equations (one for the signal field and one for the pump field) coupled with each other and with the transient heat equation to account for thermal effects
[
36
,
17
]
. Modeling of a 1–10 m long fiber segment involves the solution with
\mathcal{O}
(1–10 M) wavelengths. Solving such a problem with a direct FE discretization is infeasible, even on state-of-the-art supercomputers. Hence, our initial efforts focused on so-called

19
,
18

(using MPI+OpenMP parallelization and up to 512 manycore compute nodes).

Because the laser light in a meter-long optical fiber has millions of wavelengths, it is extremely difficult to resolve the wavelength scale of the propagating light for the full length. For this reason, even simplified models typically resolve a longer length scale. In the context of TMI studies, it is common to resolve only the length scale of the
mode beat
between the fundamental mode and higher-order modes since the mode instabilities occur at that scale. In a typical weakly-guiding, large-mode-area fiber amplifier, the mode beat length is on order of
\mathcal{O}
(1,000) wavelengths.

This brought forth the idea of the
full envelope approximation
, the solution to an alternative formulation of our vectorial Maxwell model in which the field is less oscillatory in the (longitudinal)
z
-direction. Consider the linear time-harmonic Maxwell equations in a non-magnetic and dielectric medium, in the absence of free charges:

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{E}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\mu_{0}\bm{H}\,,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{H}

\displaystyle=i\omega{\epsilon}\bm{E}\,,

(1.2)

where
i=\sqrt{-1}
,
\omega
is the angular frequency of the light,
\mu_{0}
denotes permeability in vacuum, and

denotes permittivity.
The idea of the full envelope approximation is very simple: Instead of solving for the original EM field
(\bm{E},\bm{H})
using (
1.1

\displaystyle\bm{E}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathsf{E}}(x,y,z)e^{-ikz}\,,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\bm{H}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathsf{H}}(x,y,z)e^{-ikz}\,,

(1.4)

corresponds to a dominant frequency in the waveguide

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\mathsf{E}}-ik\bm{e}_{z}\times\bm{\mathsf{E}}

\displaystyle=-i\omega\mu_{0}\bm{\mathsf{H}}\,,

(1.5a)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\mathsf{H}}-ik\bm{e}_{z}\times\bm{\mathsf{H}}

\displaystyle=i\omega{\epsilon}\bm{\mathsf{E}}\,,

(1.5b)

where
\bm{e}_{z}=(0,0,1)^{T}
is the unit vector in
z

[
20
]

2.3
Ultraweak DPG formulation and DPG essentials

Suppose we are given an injective closed operator representing a system of first-order linear Partial Differential Equations (PDEs),

A\,:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\supset D(A)\to L^{2}(\Omega)\,,

where
D(A)
is the domain of the operator incorporating (homogeneous) Boundary Conditions (BCs). We want to solve the problem,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}u\in D(A)\\[5.0pt]
Au=f\,.\end{array}\right.

The problem is trivially equivalent to the so-called
strong variational formulation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}u\in H_{A}(\Omega)\\[5.0pt]
(Au,v)=(f,v)\quad\forall\,v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,,\end{array}\right.

where
H_{A}(\Omega):=D(A)
is a Hilbert space equipped with the graph norm:

\|u\|^{2}_{H_{A}}:=\|u\|^{2}+\|Au\|^{2}\,.

The ultraweak (UW) variational formulation is obtained by integrating by parts and passing
all
derivatives to the test function. It reads as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\\[5.0pt]
(u,A^{\ast}v)=(f,v)\quad\forall\,v\in H_{A^{\ast}}(\Omega)\,,\end{array}\right.

(2.8)

where

A^{\ast}\,:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\supset D(A^{\ast})\to L^{2}(\Omega)

is the
L^{2}
-adjoint of the original operator in the sense of closed operator theory, assumed to be injective as well, and the test space
H_{A^{\ast}}(\Omega):=D(A^{\ast})
has been equipped with the scaled adjoint graph norm:

\|v\|^{2}_{H_{A^{\ast}}}:=\beta^{2}\|v\|^{2}+\|A^{\ast}v\|^{2}

with
\beta\geq 0
.
As the test functions are now more regular (and the solution less regular), the right-hand side may be upgraded to an arbitrary continuous and antilinear functional

l(\cdot)
on
H_{A^{\ast}}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 2.1

Assume that that the operator
A
is bounded below with a (boundedness below) constant
\alpha>0
:

\alpha\|u\|\leq\|Au\|\qquad\forall u\in D(A)\,.

The UW formulation (
2.8
) is then well-posed with the inf-sup constant

\gamma\geq\left[1+\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

Proof:

The Closed Range Theorem implies that adjoint
A^{\ast}
is bounded below with the same constant
\alpha
. Take an arbitrary
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and consider the corresponding solution
v_{u}
of the adjoint problem,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{u}\in D(A^{\ast})\\[5.0pt]
A^{\ast}v_{u}=u\,.\end{array}\right.

We have:

\|v_{u}\|\leq\alpha^{-1}\|u\|\quad\Rightarrow\quad\|v_{u}\|_{H_{A^{\ast}}}^{2}=\|A^{\ast}v\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\|v\|^{2}\leq\left[1+\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right]\|u\|^{2}\,,

and, in turn,

\sup_{v\in D(A^{\ast})}\frac{|(u,A^{\ast}v)|}{\|v\|_{A^{\ast}}}\geq\frac{|(u,u)|}{\|v_{u}\|_{A^{\ast}}}\geq[1+\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{2}]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|(u,u)|}{\|u\|}=\left[1+\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{2}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|\,.

Finally, the conjugate operator coincides with the strong operator
A
and its injectivity implies the existence of the solution for any right-hand side
l\in H_{A^{\ast}}(\Omega)^{\prime}
.

Note that, for
\beta=0
, the inf-sup constant is one.
The DPG Method with Optimal Test Functions is based on extending the UW formulation to a larger class
of
broken
test functions
H_{A^{\ast}}(\Omega_{h})

[
4
]
. Unfortunately, for
\beta=0
, the form
b(u,v)=(u,A^{\ast}v)
is not
localizable
, i.e. the adjoint test norm cannot be extended to the broken test space and
we must
use a positive scaling constant
\beta>0
. In principle though, by employing a sufficiently small constant
\beta
, we can make the inf-sup constant as close to one as we wish. It is not intuitive at all that the UW formulation allows for
improving
the stability of the original operator
A
in the strong setting.

Finally, the stability of the UW formulation
is inherited
by the
broken formulation

[
4
]
. In the
ideal DPG
method, the optimal test functions are assumed to be computed exactly; the discrete inf-sup constant is bounded from below by the continuous inf-sup constant which indicates the relevance of understanding the continuous variational problem. The ideal DPG method is merely a logical construct on the way to analyze the
practical DPG
method where the optimal test functions are computed approximately. The additional error resulting from the approximation of the test functions is analyzed with the help of appropriate Fortin operators
[
5
]
.

The full envelope approximation (

The analysis of the stability of the full envelope approximation method (

Lemma 2.2

Let

\widetilde{A}\widetilde{u}:=e^{ikz}A(e^{-ikz}\widetilde{u})\,,

3.2
The
\operatorname{DtN}
operator

We describe the
\operatorname{DtN}
-operator at the outflow boundary
\Gamma_{\text{out}}
. This is achieved in terms of a modal decomposition
obtained by a suitable eigenvalue problem in transverse direction.

Let
(\varphi_{n},\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset H^{1}(D)\setminus\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}

be the eigenpairs of the operator
p\mapsto-\div(a\nabla u)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle-\div(a\nabla\varphi_{n})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}\varphi_{n}\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{in $D$},

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\bm{n}\cdot a\nabla\varphi_{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad

on
\partial D

(3.5b)

with the normalization
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Here, the normal
vector
\bm{n}
is the outer normal on
\partial D
.
We have the orthogonalities

(\varphi_{n},\varphi_{m})_{L^{2}(D)}=\delta_{nm},\qquad(a\nabla\varphi_{n},\nabla\varphi_{m})_{L^{2}(D)}=\delta_{nm}\lambda_{n}.

(3.6)

The positive values
\lambda_{n}
are assumed sorted in ascending order and listed according to their multiplicity. We collect
them in the spectrum
\sigma=\{\lambda_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. It is convenient to derive the
operator
\operatorname{DtN}
in (
2.1d
)
using the second-order formulation for
(f,\bm{g})=(0,0)
, i.e., consider

\displaystyle-\div(\mathfrak{a}\nabla p)-{\omega}^{2}p

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{ on $D\times(L,\infty).$}

Making the ansatz
p(x,z)=\sum_{n}p_{n}(z)\varphi_{n}(x)
yields the 1D equations

\displaystyle-p_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)+(\lambda_{n}-{\omega}^{2})p_{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{ on $(L,\infty)$}

with the fundamental solutions
e^{\pm\kappa_{n}z}
, where

\kappa_{n}:=\sqrt{\lambda_{n}-{\omega}^{2}}

(3.7)

and the square root is taken to be the principal branch.
Writing
p|_{\Gamma_{\text{out}}}=\sum_{n}p_{n}(L)\varphi_{n}(x)
, the “outgoing” solution is defined
on
(L,\infty)
to be

p(x,z)=\sum_{n}p_{n}(L)\varphi_{n}(x)e^{-\kappa_{n}(z-L)}.

The operator
\operatorname{DtN}
is given by
p|_{\Gamma_{\text{out}}}\mapsto(\partial_{z}p(x,z))|_{z=L}
, viz.,

\displaystyle\operatorname{DtN}p

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{n}p_{n}\kappa_{n}\varphi_{n}(x),\qquad p_{n}:=(p,\varphi_{n})_{L^{2}(D)}.

(3.8)

The indices
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\kappa_{n}>0
are called
evanescent modes
, those with

\kappa_{n}\in i\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
are the
propagating modes
. We assume throughout that

\kappa_{n}\neq 0\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}

(3.9)

so that we can write
\mathbb{N}=I_{\text{prop}}\dot{\cup}I_{\text{eva}}
with the finite set
I_{\text{prop}}
of propagating modes and the infinite set
I_{\text{eva}}
of evanescent modes:

I_{\text{prop}}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,\kappa_{n}\in i\mathbb{R}\},\qquad I_{\text{eva}}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,\kappa_{n}>0\}.

(3.10)

Concerning the mapping properties of
\operatorname{DtN}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{DtN}:H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\text{out}})\rightarrow\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\text{out}}):=(H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\text{out}}))^{\prime}

is bounded, linear, which follows from the representation of
\operatorname{DtN}
:
Since
H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\text{out}})
is the interpolation space between
L^{2}(\Gamma_{\text{out}})
and
H^{1}(\Gamma_{\text{out}})
(see, e.g.,
[
29
]
), we
we have the norm equivalence
\|p\|^{2}_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\text{out}})}\sim\sum_{n}|p_{n}|^{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}}
, where

p=\sum_{n}p_{n}\varphi_{n}
with
p_{n}=(p,\varphi_{n})_{L^{2}(D)}
.
Hence, writing
p
,
q\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\text{out}})
in the form
p=\sum_{n}p_{n}\varphi_{n}
and
q=\sum_{n}q_{n}\varphi_{n}
, we estimate

\displaystyle\left|\langle\operatorname{DtN}p,q\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|-\sum_{n}\kappa_{n}p_{n}\overline{q}_{n}\right|\lesssim\|p\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\text{out}})}\|q\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\text{out}})}\,.

Lemma 3.3

Let

.
The solution

By orthogonality properties of the functions

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\|p_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)},

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\lambda^{2}_{n}\|p_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)},

\displaystyle\|\partial_{z}p\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle\|p_{n}\|_{1,|\kappa_{n}|}\leq C\begin{cases}L\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}&\mbox{ if $n\in I_{\text{prop}}$},\\

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{a}\nabla_{x}p\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle\|p\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\|p_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:lemma:L2-estimate-10})}}{{\lesssim}}L^{2}\sum_{n\in I_{\text{prop}}}|\kappa_{n}|^{-2}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}+\sum_{n\in I_{\text{prop}}}|\kappa_{n}|^{-4}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}

Lemma 3.4

Let
\bm{f}\in L^{2}({\Omega})

The solution

\displaystyle\|p\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

4.3
Structure of the single variable eigenproblems

Lemma 4.1
(Helmholtz decompositions)

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a simply connected domain.
For every
\bm{E}\in L^{2}(D)^{2}
there exist a unique
\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(D)
and a unique
\psi\in H^{1}(D)with\int_{D}\psi=0

such that

\bm{E}=\bm{\nabla}\phi+\bm{\nabla}\times\psi\,.

(4.10)

Similarly, for every
\bm{H}\in L^{2}(D)^{2}
there exist a unique
\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(D)
and a unique
\psi\in H^{1}(D)with\int_{D}\psi=0

such that

\bm{H}=\bm{\nabla}\times\phi+\bm{\nabla}\psi\,.

(4.11)

Proof:

The decomposition of
\bm{H}
can be found in
[
13
, Thm. 3.3/Rem. 3.3]
. The assertion about
\bm{E}
follows by similar arguments.

Consider now the eigenvalue problem (
4.7
) and the Helmholtz decomposition of
\bm{E}
. The boundary condition
\bm{E}_{t}=0

implies that
\frac{{\partial}\psi}{{\partial}n}=0
on
{\partial}D
.
Substituting (
4.10
) into (
4.7
), we obtain:

\bm{\nabla}\times(\underbrace{-\Delta\psi+(\beta^{2}-\omega^{2})\psi}_{=:\Psi})+\bm{\nabla}(\underbrace{-\Delta\phi+(\beta^{2}-\omega^{2})\phi}_{=:\Phi})=0\,.

(4.12)

The equation above represents the Helmholtz decomposition of the zero function. Indeed,
\div\bm{E}=\Delta\phi
is zero on the boundary and, therefore,
\Phi
is zero on the boundary as well. Multiplying (
4.12
) by
\bm{\nabla}\times\Psi
and
integrating the second term by parts, we learn that:

\Psi=-\Delta\psi+(\beta^{2}-\omega^{2})\psi=\mathrm{const}\,.

Consequently,

(\Psi,1)=(\bm{\nabla}\psi,\underbrace{\bm{\nabla}1}_{=0})-\langle\underbrace{\frac{{\partial}\psi}{{\partial}n}}_{=0},1\rangle+(\beta^{2}-\omega^{2})\underbrace{(\psi,1)}_{=0}=0\,.

Uniqueness of
\phi
and
\psi
in the Helmholtz decomposition implies that
\Phi=\Psi=0
.
Let
(\lambda_{i},\phi_{i})
and
(\mu_{j}\psi_{j})
be the Dirichlet and Neumann eigenpairs of the Laplacian in the domain

. Vanishing of

and

implies that there exist

\phi=\phi_{i},\,\omega^{2}-\beta^{2}=\lambda_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\psi=\psi_{j},\omega^{2}-\beta^{2}=\mu_{j}\,.

If the Dirichlet and Neumann eigenvalues are distinct, the eigenvector

must reduce to either a gradient or a curl.
This is the case, e.g., for a circular domain

. In the case of a common Dirichlet and Neumann eigenvalue,
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{j}

we obtain a multiple eigenvalue

, with the eigenspace consisting of vectors:

\bm{E}=A\bm{\nabla}\times\psi_{j}+B\bm{\nabla}\phi_{i}\,,\quad A,B\in\mathbb{C}\,.

Example 4.4
(Cylindrical waveguide)

Consider the Dirichlet or Neumann Laplace eigenvalue problem in a unit circle,

-\Delta u=\lambda u,\quad\lambda=\nu^{2}\,.

For the Dirichlet problem the operator is positive definite, so
\nu>0
, for the Neumann problem,
u=\mathrm{const}
corresponds to zero eigenvalue,
all other eigenvalues are positive as well.
Rewriting the operator in polar coordinates
r,\theta
,

-\frac{1}{r}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}r}(r\frac{{\partial}u}{{\partial}r})-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}u}{{\partial}\theta^{2}}=\nu^{2}u

and separating variables,
u=R(r)\Theta(\theta)
, we get

-\frac{1}{r}(rR^{\prime})^{\prime}\Theta-\frac{1}{r^{2}}R\Theta^{\prime\prime}=\nu^{2}R\Theta

or,

\frac{r(rR^{\prime})^{\prime}}{R}+\nu^{2}r^{2}=-\frac{\Theta^{\prime\prime}}{\Theta}=k^{2}

where
k^{2}
is a real and positive separation constant. We obtain,

\Theta=A\cos k\theta+B\sin k\theta

and periodic BCs on
u
and, therefore,
\Theta
, imply that
k=0,1,2,\ldots
.
This leads to the Bessel equation in
r
,

r(rR^{\prime})^{\prime}+(\nu^{2}r^{2}-k^{2})R=0

with solution:

R=CJ_{k}(\nu r)+DY_{k}(\nu r)\,.

The requirement of finite energy eliminates the second term,
D=0
.

Dirichlet BC:

R(1)=0
leads to
\nu
being a root of the Bessel function
J_{k}(\nu)=0
. We have a family of roots (and, therefore Dirichlet Laplace eigenvalues
\nu^{2}
) :
\nu=\nu_{k,m},\,k=0,1,2,\ldots,m=1,2,\ldots
. For
k=0
, the roots are simple, with corresponding
eigenvectors given by:

u=J_{0}(\nu_{0,m}r)\,.

For
k>0
, we have double eigenvectors with eigenspaces given by:

u=J_{0}(\nu_{k,m}r)(A\cos k\theta+B\sin k\theta)\,.

Neumann BC:

The situation is similar except that we are dealing now with the roots of the derivative of Bessel functions:
J_{k}^{\prime}(\lambda)=0
,

\lambda=\lambda_{k,m},\,k=0,1,2,\ldots,m=1,2,\ldots
.

5
Numerical results and conclusions

To test the dependence of the ultraweak DPG Maxwell discretization on the waveguide length
L
and the scaling constant
\beta
in the test norm, we solve the linear time-harmonic Maxwell equations in a homogeneous rectangular waveguide with transverse domain
D=(0.0,1.0)\times(0.0,0.5)
. At
\Gamma_{\text{in}}
, the waveguide is excited with the lowest-order transverse electric (TE) mode by prescribing the corresponding tangential electric field. At the waveguide exit
\Gamma_{\text{out}}
, the
\operatorname{DtN}
operator is approximated with an impedance boundary condition relating the tangential electric field
\bm{E}
with the rotated tangential magnetic field
\bm{n}\times\bm{H}
via the impedance constant for the propagating TE mode; for details on implementing impedance BCs in ultraweak DPG, we refer to
[
8
]
. The cross-section is modeled with two hexahedral elements, which is justified by the simple transverse mode profile. In the longitudinal direction, we use a “fixed discretization” of two elements per wavelength; that is, as the waveguide length
L
increases, the number of elements per wavelength remains the same. The DPG discretization uses uniform polynomial order
p=5
with enriched test functions of order
p+1
. All of the computations were done in
hp
3D
[
21
,
22
]
.

The analysis of Lemma

Conclusions. Non-homogeneous waveguide.

The convergence analysis for the DPG method for the modified Maxwell model resulting from a full envelope ansatz led us to the stability analysis for EM waveguides. We started with a simpler, acoustic waveguide, and then proceeded to the homogeneous Maxwell model. For both models, we have obtained the same result: the stability constant depends linearly on the length of the waveguide, i.e.,

\|\bm{E}\|+\|\bm{H}\|\leq CL\left(\|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{E}-i\omega\bm{H}\|+\|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{H}+i\omega\bm{E}\|\right)\,,\qquad C>0\,.

For the non-homogeneous optical waveguide,
\mu=1
,
\epsilon=1+\delta\epsilon
, the triangle inequality implies:

\displaystyle\|\bm{E}\|+\|\bm{H}\|

\displaystyle\leq CL\left(\|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{E}-i\omega\bm{H}\|+\|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{H}+i\omega(1+\delta\epsilon-\delta\epsilon)\bm{E}\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq CL\left(\|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{E}-i\omega\bm{H}\|+\|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{H}+i\omega(1+\delta\epsilon)\bm{E}\|+\omega\|\delta\epsilon\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\bm{E}\|\right)\,.

Consequently,

(1-CL\omega\|\delta\epsilon\|_{L^{\infty}}\omega)(\|\bm{E}\|+\|\bm{H}\|)\leq CL\left(\|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{E}-i\omega\bm{H}\|+\|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{H}+i\omega(1+\delta\epsilon)\bm{E}\|\right)\,,

which proves that, for a sufficiently small perturbation
\delta\epsilon
, the operator remains bounded below. However, the information about the linear dependence of the stability constant upon
L
is lost.

\|u\|_{1,|\kappa|}\leq C\left[\frac{1}{1+\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}}|g|\right].

Proof:

With the inf-sup constant
\gamma_{\kappa}
of Lemma
3.1
in hand, we get

\|u\|_{1,|\kappa|}\leq\frac{C}{\gamma_{\kappa}}\left[\frac{1}{|\kappa|}\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\kappa|}}|g|\right].

We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{\kappa}}\frac{1}{|\kappa|}\sim\frac{1}{1+C\frac{|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|}{1+\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}}\frac{1}{|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|+\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}

and now distinguish between the cases
|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|\leq 1/2
(which implies
\operatorname*{Re}\kappa\geq\sqrt{3/4}
by the requirement
|\kappa|\geq 1
)
and the case
|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|\geq 1/2
. In the first case, we have
|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|/\operatorname*{Re}\kappa\leq c
for some
c>0
and can simply

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+C\frac{|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|}{1+\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}}\frac{1}{|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|+\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}\sim\frac{1}{\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}\frac{1}{1+\frac{|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|}{\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}}\frac{1}{1+\frac{|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|}{\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}}\sim\frac{1}{\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}.

In the second case, we have
|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|/\operatorname*{Re}\kappa\geq c>0
and can estimate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+C\frac{|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|}{1+\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}}\frac{1}{|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|+\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}=\frac{1}{\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}\frac{1}{1+C\frac{|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|}{1+\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}}\frac{1}{\frac{|\operatorname*{Im}\kappa|}{\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}}\leq\frac{1}{\operatorname*{Re}\kappa}\frac{1}{c}.

We conclude that the prefactor of the term
\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}
has the desired form. The estimate for the prefactor of
|g|
is analogous.

\displaystyle(\nabla v,\mathfrak{a}\nabla q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-{\omega}^{2}(v,q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-\langle v,\operatorname{DtN}^{\ast}q\rangle=(v,f)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}

(D.7)

satisfies

\displaystyle\|q\|_{H^{1}({\Omega})}\leq CL\|f\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}.

Proof:

We make the ansatz

q(x,z)=\sum_{n}q_{n}(z)\phi_{n}(x)

and set

f_{n}(z):=(\phi_{n},f(\cdot,z))_{L^{2}(D)}.

By orthogonality properties of the functions
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n}
we have

\displaystyle\|q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\|q_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)},

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{a}\nabla_{x}q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\lambda^{2}_{n}\|q_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)},

\displaystyle\|\partial_{z}q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\|q^{\prime}_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)},

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}.

Testing (
D.7
) with
v(x,z)=v_{n}(z)\phi_{n}(x)
with arbitrary
v_{n}\in H^{1}_{(0}(0,L)

gives due to the orthogonalities satisfied by the functions
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n}

(v^{\prime}_{n},q^{\prime}_{n})_{L^{2}(0,L)}+\overline{{\kappa^{2}_{n}}}(v_{n},q_{n})_{L^{2}(0,L)}-v_{n}(L)\overline{\kappa_{n}}\overline{q}_{n}(L)=(v_{n},f_{n})_{L^{2}(0,L)}

From Lemma
3.2
, we conclude

\displaystyle\|q_{n}\|_{1,|\kappa_{n}|}\leq C\begin{cases}L\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}&\mbox{ if $n\in I_{\text{prop}}$},\\
\kappa_{n}^{-1}\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}&\mbox{ if $n\in I_{\text{eva}}$}.\end{cases}

We arrive at

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{a}\nabla_{x}q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\lambda^{2}_{n}\|q_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:lambda-tildelambda-adjoint})}}{{\lesssim}}L^{2}\sum_{n\in I_{\text{prop}}}\frac{\lambda^{2}_{n}}{|{\kappa}_{n}|^{2}}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}+\sum_{n\in I_{\text{eva}}}\frac{\lambda^{2}_{n}}{|{\kappa}_{n}|^{4}}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:lambda-tildelambda-adjoint})}}{{\lesssim}}L^{2}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})},

\displaystyle\|\partial_{z}q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\|q^{\prime}_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\lesssim L^{2}\sum_{n\in I_{\text{prop}}}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}+\sum_{n\in I_{\text{eva}}}|{\kappa}_{n}|^{-2}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\lesssim L^{2}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})},

\displaystyle\|q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\|q_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\lesssim L^{2}\sum_{n\in I_{\text{prop}}}|{\kappa_{n}}|^{-2}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}+\sum_{n\in I_{\text{eva}}}|{\kappa_{n}}|^{-4}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\lesssim L^{2}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}.

Lemma D.2

The solution
q\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}
of

\displaystyle(\nabla v,\mathfrak{a}\nabla q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-{\omega}^{2}(v,q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-\langle v,\operatorname{DtN}^{\ast}q\rangle=(\nabla v,\bm{f})_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}

(D.8)

satisfies

\displaystyle\|q\|_{H^{1}({\Omega})}\leq CL\|\bm{f}\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}.

Proof:

We write the vector
\bm{f}
as
\bm{f}=(\bm{f}_{x},f_{z})^{\top}
with a vector-valued function
\bm{f}_{x}
and a scalar function
f_{z}
. By linearity of the problem,
we may consider the cases
(\bm{f}_{x},0)^{\top}
and
(0,f_{z})^{\top}
as right-hand sides separately.
For
\bm{f}_{x}=0
, we proceed as in Lemma
D.1
by writing

f_{z}=\sum_{n}f_{n}(z)\phi_{n}(x)
and get with Lemma
3.2
for the corresponding functions
q_{n}

\displaystyle\|q_{n}\|_{1,|\kappa_{n}|}

\displaystyle\leq C\begin{cases}L|\kappa_{n}|\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}&\mbox{ if $n\in I_{\text{prop}}$},\\
\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}&\mbox{ if $n\in I_{\text{eva}}$}.\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq CL\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}

since
\max_{n\in I_{\text{prop}}}|\kappa_{n}|\leq C
.
We may repeat the calculations performed in Lemma
D.1
to establish

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{a}\nabla_{x}q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\|q_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq CL^{2}\sum_{n}\frac{\lambda_{n}}{|\kappa_{n}|^{2}}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq C\|f_{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})},

\displaystyle\|\partial_{z}q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\|q^{\prime}_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq CL^{2}\sum_{n}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq CL^{2}\|f_{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})},

\displaystyle\|q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\|q_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq CL^{2}\sum_{n}|\kappa_{n}|^{-2}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq CL^{2}\|f_{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}.

For the case of the right-hand side
\bm{f}=(\bm{f}_{x},0)^{\top}
, we define

f_{n}(z):=(\nabla\phi_{n},\bm{f}_{x}(\cdot,z))_{L^{2}(D)}=(a\nabla\phi_{n},a^{-1}\bm{f}_{x}(\cdot,z))_{L^{2}(D)}

and note by the fact that the functions
\{\|\sqrt{a}\nabla\phi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{-1}\nabla\phi_{n}\}_{n}
are an orthonormal (with respect to

(a\cdot,\cdot)_{L^{2}(D)}
) basis of its span that

\sum_{n}\lambda^{-1}_{n}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}=\int_{0}^{L}\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\|\sqrt{a}\nabla\phi_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}}|(a\nabla\phi_{n},a^{-1}\bm{f}_{x}(\cdot,z))_{L^{2}(D)}|^{2}\leq\|a^{-1}\bm{f}_{x}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}.

We expand the solution
q
as
q=\sum_{n}q_{n}(z)\phi_{n}(x)
. Testing the equation with functions of the form
v_{n}(z)\phi_{n}(x)
yields again
an equation for the coefficients
q_{n}
:

-\kappa^{2}_{n}(v_{n},q_{n})_{L^{2}(0,L)}+(v^{\prime}_{n},q^{\prime}_{n})_{L^{2}(0,L)}-\overline{\kappa}_{n}v_{n}(L)\overline{q}_{n}(L)=(v_{n},f_{n})_{L^{2}(0,L)}\qquad\forall v_{n}\in H^{1}_{(0}(0,L)

By Lemma
3.2
we get

\displaystyle\|q_{n}\|_{1,|{\kappa}_{n}|}

\displaystyle\leq C\begin{cases}L\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}&\mbox{ if $n\in I_{\text{prop}}$ },\\
|\kappa_{n}|^{-1}\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,L)}&\mbox{ if $n\in I_{\text{eva}}$ }.\end{cases}

Hence,

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{a}\nabla_{x}q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\|q_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq CL^{2}\sum_{n\in I_{\text{prop}}}\lambda_{n}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}+C\sum_{n\in I_{\text{eva}}}\frac{\lambda_{n}}{|\kappa_{n}|^{4}}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq CL\|\bm{f}_{x}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})},

\displaystyle\|\partial_{z}q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\|q^{\prime}_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq CL^{2}\sum_{n\in I_{\text{prop}}}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}+C\sum_{n\in I_{\text{eva}}}|\kappa_{n}|^{-2}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq C\|\bm{f}_{x}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle\|q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\|q_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq CL^{2}\sum_{n\in I_{\text{prop}}}|\kappa_{n}|^{-2}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}+\sum_{n\in I_{\text{eva}}}|\kappa_{n}|^{-4}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,L)}\leq C\|\bm{f}_{x}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}.

Putting together the above results proves the claim.

Theorem D.3

There is a constant
C>0
(depending on
\mathfrak{a}
and
{\omega}
) such that for all
(\bm{v},q)\in D(A^{\ast})

\|A^{\ast}(q,\bm{v})^{\top}\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\geq CL^{-1}\|(\bm{v},q)\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}.

Proof:

First, we note that
A^{\ast}:D(A^{\ast})\rightarrow L^{2}({\Omega})
is injective.
Indeed,
A^{\ast}(q,\bm{v})^{\top}=0
implies

\bm{v}=i{\omega}\nabla q
and therefore
q\in H^{1}({\Omega})
satisfies a homogeneous second-order equation. Together with the
boundary condition, one checks that
q=0
so that also
\bm{v}=0
.

Abbreviate for the two components of
A^{\ast}(q,\bm{v})^{\top}

\bm{f}:=-i{\omega}\bm{v}-\nabla q\in L^{2}({\Omega}),\qquad f:=-\div(\mathfrak{a}\bm{v})-i{\omega}q\in L^{2}({\Omega}).

Hence,
(q,\bm{v})
satisfy for smooth
(\widetilde{p},\widetilde{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\bm{u}},-i{\omega}\bm{v})_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-(\widetilde{\bm{u}},\nabla q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{\bm{u}},\bm{f})_{L^{2}({\Omega})},

\displaystyle(\widetilde{p},-\div(\mathfrak{a}\bm{v}))_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-(\widetilde{p},i{\omega}q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{p},f)_{L^{2}({\omega})}

Considering
\widetilde{p}
with
\widetilde{p}|_{\Gamma_{\text{in}}}=0
and using the boundary conditions satisfied by
(q,\bm{v})
(i.e.,
(q,\bm{u})\in D(A^{\ast})
) yields
after an integration by parts

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\bm{u}},-i{\omega}\bm{v})_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-(\widetilde{\bm{u}},\nabla q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{\bm{u}},\bm{f})_{L^{2}({\Omega})},

\displaystyle(\nabla\widetilde{p},\mathfrak{a}\bm{v})_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-(\widetilde{p},i{\omega}q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-\langle\widetilde{p},\frac{1}{i{\omega}}\operatorname{DtN}^{\ast}q\rangle_{\Gamma_{\text{out}}}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{p},f)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

Selecting
\widetilde{\bm{u}}=\frac{1}{i{\omega}}\mathfrak{a}\nabla\widetilde{p}
yields

\displaystyle(\nabla\widetilde{p},\mathfrak{a}\bm{v})_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-\frac{1}{i{\omega}}(\mathfrak{a}\nabla\widetilde{p},\nabla q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i{\omega}}(\nabla\widetilde{p},\bm{f})_{L^{2}({\Omega})},

\displaystyle(\nabla\widetilde{p},\mathfrak{a}\bm{v})_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-(\widetilde{p},i{\omega}q)-\langle\widetilde{p},\frac{1}{i{\omega}}\operatorname{DtN}^{\ast}q\rangle_{\Gamma_{\text{out}}}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{p},f)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

so that, by subtracting these two equations, we arrive at

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{i{\omega}}(\nabla\widetilde{p},\nabla q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}+(\widetilde{p},i{\omega}q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}+\langle\widetilde{p},\frac{1}{i{\omega}}\operatorname{DtN}^{\ast}q\rangle_{\Gamma_{\text{out}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i{\omega}}(\nabla\widetilde{p},\bm{f})_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-(\widetilde{p},f)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

Rearranging terms yields

\displaystyle(\nabla\widetilde{p},\nabla q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-{\omega}^{2}(\widetilde{p},q)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}-\langle\widetilde{p},\operatorname{DtN}^{\ast}q\rangle_{\Gamma_{\text{out}}}

\displaystyle=-(\nabla\widetilde{p},\bm{f})_{L^{2}({\Omega})}+i{\omega}(\widetilde{p},f)_{L^{2}({\Omega})}

From Lemmas
D.1
,
D.2
we infer

\displaystyle\|q\|_{H^{1}({\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leq CL\left[\|\bm{f}\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}+\|f\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\right],

which in turn yields

\|(q,\bm{v})\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\leq\|q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}+\|\bm{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\leq\|q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}+2{\omega}^{-2}\|\nabla q\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}+2{\omega}^{-2}\|\bm{f}\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\Omega})}.

In total, we arrive at
\|(q,\bm{v})\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\leq CL\left[\|\bm{f}\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}+\|f\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\right]
, i.e.,

\|(q,\bm{v})\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}\leq CL\|A^{\ast}(q,\bm{v})^{\top}\|_{L^{2}({\Omega})}
, which is the claim.
