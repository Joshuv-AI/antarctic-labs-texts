Embedding Probability Distributions into Low Dimensional ℓ 1 \ell_{1} : Tree Ising Models via Truncated Metrics
Moses Charikar, Spencer Compton, Chirag Pabbaraju

Abstract




Given an arbitrary set of high dimensional points in
\ell_{1}
, there are known negative results that preclude the possibility of always mapping them to a low dimensional
\ell_{1}
space while preserving distances with small multiplicative distortion. This is in stark contrast with dimension reduction in Euclidean space (
\ell_{2}
) where such mappings are always possible. While the first non-trivial lower bounds for
\ell_{1}
dimension reduction were established almost 20 years ago, there has been limited progress in understanding what sets of points in
\ell_{1}
are conducive to a low-dimensional mapping.

In this work, we study a new characterization of
\ell_{1}
metrics that are conducive to dimension reduction in
\ell_{1}
. Our characterization focuses on metrics that are defined by the disagreement of binary variables over a probability distribution – any
\ell_{1}
metric can be represented in this form. We show that, for configurations of
n
points in
\ell_{1}
obtained from tree Ising models, we can reduce dimension to
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
with constant distortion.
In doing so, we develop technical tools for embedding
truncated metrics
which have been studied because of their applications in computer vision, and are objects of independent interest in metric geometry.
Among other tools, we show how any
\ell_{1}
metric can be truncated with
O(1)
distortion and
O(\log(n))
blowup in dimension.

1
Introduction

Given an arbitrary set of
n
high-dimensional points in
\ell_{1}
, we know that it is impossible to always map them to a low dimensional
\ell_{1}
space while preserving distances with small multiplicative distortion.
Known lower bounds
[
BC05
,
LN04
,
ACNN11
,
Reg13
]
show that for any desired distortion
c
, at least
n^{\Omega(1/c^{2})}
dimensions are needed.
This is in stark contrast with dimension reduction in Euclidean space (
\ell_{2}
) where mappings to
O(\log(n))
dimensions are always possible with
1+\varepsilon
distortion.
While these results rule out dimension reduction for arbitrary sets of point in
\ell_{1}
, a natural question is:
Are there broad families of structured sets of points in
\ell_{1}
that admit low dimensional mappings with small distortion?

Despite the fact that strong lower bounds for
\ell_{1}
dimension reduction were established almost 20 years ago, there has been little progress in understanding this question.

In this work, we study a new characterization of
\ell_{1}
metrics that admit low-dimensional
\ell_{1}
representations.
Our starting point is that any
\ell_{1}
metric can be represented by the disagreement of binary variables over a probability distribution
D
– a simple consequence of the fact that any
\ell_{1}
metric can be embedded into the (possibly infinite-dimensional) Hamming cube.
This distribution viewpoint of
\ell_{1}
metrics leads to a natural reframing of the earlier question:
What classes of distributions
D
lead to
\ell_{1}
metrics that admit low-distortion, low-dimensional
\ell_{1}
embeddings?

We initiate a study of this question and show that, for configurations of
n
points in
\ell_{1}
obtained from
tree Ising models
, we can reduce dimension to
\mathrm{polylog}(n)
with constant distortion.
Within the family of graphical models, tree Ising models are a natural first candidate for distributions to study in this context. On the one hand, they have been studied extensively in the recent theoretical computer science literature on testing and learning

[
DP21
,
BGPV21
,
BABK22
,
KDDC23
]
.
On the other hand, it is well known that tree metrics embed isometrically into
\ell_{1}
, and the question of
\ell_{1}
dimension reduction for tree metrics has been studied in the literature

[
CS02
,
LdMM13
,
LM13
]
.
We should note, however, that the metrics that arise from tree Ising models are not tree metrics themselves – establishing that they admit low dimensional
\ell_{1}
embeddings is subtle and non-trivial.

In order to establish our results, we develop technical tools for embedding
truncated metrics
. Truncated metrics arise in image segmentation in computer vision

[
BVZ98
,
IG98
,
KVT11
,
Kum14
,
KD16
,
PPK17
]

and have been studied in theoretical computer science in the context of the metric labeling problem motivated by this application
[
GT00
,
CKNZ04
,
KKMR06
]
; they are also objects of independent interest in metric geometry, where they have been studied in the context of metric dimension

[
JO12
,
BRV16
,
TFL21
,
TFL23
]
, including very recent work on the metric dimension of truncated tree metrics
[
BKR23
,
GY23
]
.
Our techniques for truncated metrics relate closely with recent developments in truncated metrics for embedding shortest path decompositions
[
AFGN22
,
Fil20
]
. The work of
[
AFGN22
]
provides embeddings of
O(\log(n))
dimension and constant distortion for both “truncated
line
metrics” and “Lipschitz capped
line
metrics” (later defined in
Section

3.4.1
,
3.4.2
), whereas the work of
[
Fil20
]
provides
high-dimensional
and constant distortion embeddings for truncated tree metrics and Lipschitz capped tree metrics. Our work provides embeddings of
O(\log^{2}(n))
dimension
and
constant distortion for truncated tree metrics and Lipschitz capped tree metrics. Moreover, we provide a general result for truncating
any
arbitrary
\ell_{1}
metric that may be useful in future works dealing with truncation.

While our work shows that
\ell_{1}
metrics represented by tree Ising models allow for low dimensional embeddings, it would be interesting to see how far this program can be extended – namely, is there a more general class of graphical models (e.g., having bounded treewidth) or joint distributions that also allow for low dimensional embeddings in
\ell_{1}
? We discuss this in more detail in
Section

7
, where we show how we can express prior lower bounds for dimension reduction in
\ell_{1}
in this distributional formulation. In particular, we show that even Bayesian networks with treewidth-3 capture the lower bound of
[
BC05
]
, precluding the possibility of embedding them in
n^{o(1)}
dimensions.
This suggests that tree Ising models are near the boundary of families of graphical models representing
\ell_{1}
metrics that admit low dimensional embeddings.
We also discuss classes of graphical models for which this question remains open.

2
Overview of Results

2.1
Embedding Probability Distributions

In the context of understanding the classes of distributions that are amenable to low-dimensional
\ell_{1}
embeddings, our main result shows that it is possible to embed distance metrics over Tree Ising models:

Theorem 1

(Embedding Tree Ising Models)
.

Any distance metric over tree Ising models (i.e,
d_{D}(i,j)=c\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}_{X\sim D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]
, where
D
is a tree Ising model distribution on
n
random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
) can be embedded into
(\mathds{R}^{O(\log^{2}(n))},\ell_{1})
with
\Theta(1)
distortion.

We also show that this characterization of metrics with distributions is expressive enough to capture classical lower bounds of
[
BC05
]
. In particular, there exist Bayesian networks of treewidth 3 that are impossible to embed into low-dimensions with small distortion.

Claim 2.1

(Treewidth 3 lower bound)
.

There exists a Bayesian network
D
on
N
binary random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
whose underlying undirected graph has treewidth 3, such that embedding the metric
d_{D}(X_{i},X_{j})=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]
into
\ell_{1}
with
O(1)
distortion requires
N^{\Omega(1)}
dimensions.

The proof of our positive result
Theorem

1
follows two primary thrusts. First, in
Section

5.2
we characterize that distance metrics corresponding to tree Ising models can approximately have distances between
X_{i},X_{j}
decomposed into three sources:

•

The difference in the marginals between
X_{i},X_{j}
, or
|\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=0]-\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{j}=0]|
.

•

The amount of independence between
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
, which we formalize by a notion called “Bernoulli randomness” in
Section

5.1
. This simply-defined quantity behaves intuitively similar to Shannon entropy in how it measures randomness, yet has smoothness that makes it more amenable to the
\ell_{1}
setting.

•

The strength of any negative correlation between
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
.

Perhaps most interestingly, we sketch here how to embed distance originating from the “independence” between two variables
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
. For simplicity, suppose that the other two sources of distance are zero for
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
(meaning, their marginals are the same and they are not negatively correlated). Then, one could roughly view the joint distribution over the
X_{i},X_{j}
by some process where first
X_{i}
is realized, then with probability
p
we sample
X_{j}
independently, and with probability
1-p
we set
X_{j}=X_{i}
. The parameter
p
roughly corresponds to the independence between
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
: if
p=0
then they are completely dependent, and if
p=1
they are completely independent. In this setting, we find that the distance metric
\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]
is roughly equal to
\Theta(1)\cdot p\cdot M(x_{i})
, where
M(x_{i})=\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=0],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=1])
is a quantity that measures how far
X_{i}
is from being completely deterministic (i.e. biased towards one value).
We later non-trivially determine a weight
w_{e}
for each edge in the tree (in terms of “Bernoulli randomness”), such that, in this setting, if we were to think of the path connecting
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
,

\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]\approx\min(\sum_{e:i\rightarrow j}w_{e},M(x_{i}))
.
One interpretation of this is that independence can accumulate along a path in the tree, but at some point the effect of independence is capped by the amount of randomness in the marginals of
X_{i},X_{j}
.
We are able to generalize the usage of this idea, to reduce the task of embedding tree Ising models into
\ell_{1}
to the task of embedding a truncated tree metric, where the truncation parameter (we will call these “caps”) is a function of the vertices. In particular,
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{lipcap}}(x_{i},x_{j})\triangleq\min(\sum_{e:i\rightarrow j}w_{e},\max(M(x_{i}),M(x_{j}))
. Here,
M(x_{i}),M(x_{j})
are “caps” for each vertex, which, in the context of tree Ising models, ultimately end up being related to the biases of the variables. Crucially, our caps satisfy a Lipschitz property
|M(x_{i})-M(x_{j})|\leq d(x_{i},x_{j})
. We next discuss our second thrust that studies how to embed this metric.

2.2
Truncated Tree Metrics

We saw that one of our main challenges in proving
Theorem

1
ends up being able to embed truncated tree metrics. As a warm-up (used in our proof for symmetric tree Ising models), we first study truncated tree metrics with a fixed cap. This metric is of independent interest; Lemma 1 of
[
Fil20
]
provides a high-dimensional embedding using the results of
[
CJLV08
]
. We show a low-dimensional embedding:

Theorem 2

(Fixed cap tree into
\ell_{1}
)
.

Let
T_{n}
be an undirected edge-weighted tree on
n
vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, equipped with the standard graph metric, i.e.,
d(x_{i},x_{j})=\sum_{e:i\to j}e
, where
e:i\to j
indexes the (weights) of all edges
e
on the path from
x_{i}
to
x_{j}
. Let
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}
be the distance function defined as
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=\min(d(x_{i},x_{j}),M)
.
Then,
(T_{n},d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}})
can be embedded into
(\mathds{R}^{O(\log^{2}(n))},\ell_{1})
with
\Theta(1)
distortion.

Embedding this fixed cap tree metric will leverage three main ingredients: (i) truncated line metrics, (ii) the tree metric embedding of
[
CS02
]
, and (iii) a new truncated sparsity condition.

Truncated line metrics.
Truncated line metrics with a fixed cap are a special case of fixed cap tree metrics, and we will later leverage them as a subroutine. Lemma 2.1 of
[
AFGN22
]
designs an embedding for truncated line metrics with a probabilistic Sawtooth function. While this is sufficient for the required subroutine for fixed cap tree metrics, in
Section

4.2.1
we design an alternative embedding called
lazy snaking
that is more conducive to our later result on truncating general
\ell_{1}
metrics (
Theorem

4
).

Tree metric embedding of
[
CS02
]
.
The work of
[
CS02
]
designs an embedding for tree metrics with
O(1)
distortion and
O(\log^{2}(n))
dimension. Summarizing their techniques, they first provide a simple isometric embedding of dimension
O(n)
. They then leverage the
caterpillar decomposition
of trees to provide an isometric embedding of dimension
O(n)
that is
O(\log(n))
-sparse (meaning each vertex embedding has at most
O(\log(n))
nonzero entries). Moreover, they remark that any
d
-sparse embedding can be converted to a dimension
O(d\log(n))
embedding with
O(1)
distortion, by considering an embedding that hashes every coordinate into just
d
coordinates, constructing a
d
-dimensional embedding where the
i
-th coordinate is a line metric corresponding to the total value hashed to the
i
-th coordinate, and then boosting the success probability
O(\log(n))
times. Thus, as the caterpillar decomposition provided a
O(\log(n))
-sparse isometric embedding, they attain a
O(\log^{2}(n))
dimension embedding with
O(1)
distortion from their sparsity property.

Truncated sparsity condition.
Inspired by this approach, we introduce a new sparsity condition similar to the one leveraged for tree metrics that enables embedding a
truncated
tree metric. Namely, for a cap value
M
and target sparsity
d
, consider a modified sparsity condition such that: (i) each coordinate is bounded by
O(M/d)
and (ii) for any pair
x_{i},x_{j}
there exists a subset of
O(d)
coordinates such that the norm of the difference in embeddings restricted to just that subset of coordinates is within a constant factor of
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})
. Note how this condition is a generalization of the
[
CS02
]
sparsity condition when
M=\infty
. We show that, given this truncated sparsity condition, we can embed the truncated metric into
O(d\log(n))
dimensions with
O(1)
distortion. This is accomplished by a similar hashing method, where we replace the line metrics used in
[
CS02
]
with truncated line metrics of parameter
M/d
. Finally, we note that that the caterpillar decomposition embedding used in
[
CS02
]
does not naively satisfy our desired truncated sparsity condition, and we have to appropriately modify it to accomplish our requirements.

The above deals with fixed cap truncated tree metrics; however, to prove
Theorem

1
, we must embed a more nuanced truncated tree metric where the truncation is not fixed. We note that the techniques of Lemma 2 of
[
Fil20
]
can enable a
high-dimensional
embedding for this version of the problem. We show a low-dimensional embedding:

Theorem 3

(Embedding Lipschitz-Capped Trees)
.

Let
T_{n}
be an undirected edge-weighted tree on
n
vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, equipped with the standard graph metric, i.e.,
d(x_{i},x_{j})=\sum_{e:i\to j}e
, where
e:i\to j
indexes the (weights) of all edges
e
on the path from
x_{i}
to
x_{j}
. Consider a cap function
M(\cdot)
that assigns a nonnegative cap to every vertex
x_{i}
in the tree, such that cap values satisfy
|M(x_{i})-M(x_{j})|\leq d(x_{i},x_{j})
for all
i,j
. Let
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{lipcap}}
be the distance function defined as

\displaystyle d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{lipcap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=\min(d(x_{i},x_{j}),\max(M(x_{i}),M(x_{j}))).

(1)

Then,
(T_{n},d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{lipcap}})
can be embedded into
(\mathds{R}^{O(\log^{2}(n))},\ell_{1})
with
\Theta(1)
distortion.

Recall how our algorithm for the fixed cap tree metric involved taking a suitably modified version of the tree’s caterpillar decomposition and reducing (by hashing) the embedding problem to a collection of fixed cap line metric problems. However, we cannot reduce the Lipschitz cap tree metric to a collection of Lipschitz cap line metrics in an obvious manner. Crucially, a direct modification of the prior approach would not work for Lipschitz cap tree metrics, primarily because segments that are hashed to a line metric instance are not contiguous segments of the tree, and thus
the caps on these segments need no longer satisfy the necessary Lipschitz condition.
Consequently, we must design a new algorithm for this task.

First, we observe that the primary issue in extending our prior techniques was how tree edges hashed to the same line metrics potentially have very different caps. If we view the algorithm hierarchically (considering how the embedding evolves as we progress downwards in the tree), it appears to be desirable to “clean”/zero out the embedding somehow. If we clean the embedding frequently enough, we may expect that the only edges affecting some
embedding[x_{i}]
would be parts of the tree very close to node
x_{i}
, and thus by the Lipschitz property, all the relevant edges would have roughly the same cap. Ultimately, we obtain an algorithm that is less directly similar to the previous approaches, but is conducive towards this notion of periodic cleaning. This result is ultimately achieved by a novel algorithm that we call the
Build-Clean
approach (
Section

5.3.2
).

2.3
General Truncated
\ell_{1}
Metrics

Finally, we study how to truncate arbitrary
\ell_{1}
metrics. This is likely a result of independent interest, as truncation has been leveraged in the
\ell_{1}
embeddings literature for a variety of applications. For example,
[
GT00
]
provides a custom construction for embedding the truncated line metric that attains
O(1)
distortion
only in expectation
. Later,
[
AFGN22
]
proved a truncated line metric with
O(1)
distortion and leveraged this for embedding weighted paths of bounded pathwidth. Truncation has been further leveraged for embedding planar graphs
[
Fil20
]
. We show how to truncate any
\ell_{1}
metric with
O(1)
distortion and a
O(\log(n))
dimension blowup:

Theorem 4

(General Truncated
\ell_{1}
Metrics)
.

Let
S=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{d}
be
n
points. Let
d(x_{i},x_{j})=\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{1}
, and for any fixed cap
M>0
, consider the truncated
\ell_{1}
metric
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=\min(d(x_{i},x_{j}),M)
. Then
(S,d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}})
can be embedded into
(\mathds{R}^{O(d\log(n))},\ell_{1})
with
\Theta(1)
distortion.

We note that this
O(\log(n))
dimension blowup is optimal upto constant factors. For example, consider how a line metric (by definition) can be embedded in
\ell_{1}
using 1 coordinate. Observe how a uniform metric (where, say, every pair of points has distance 1) can be represented by a truncated line metric with a cap of
1
and all points sufficiently far apart on the line. Moreoever, this uniform metric requires
\Omega(\log(n))
dimension to obtain
O(1)
distortion by a simple folklore volume argument. Thus, truncating the line metric requires a
\Omega(\log(n))
dimension blowup and thus our general truncation result is optimal within constant factors.

Our result is attained by leveraging similar techniques to those used for embedding Lipschitz-Capped trees. Conceptually, we show that using these techniques enables one to map any metric in
\ell_{1}^{d}
to a metric in
\ell_{1}^{d^{2}}
that almost has similar distances to the truncated metric over the original points, yet satisfies the property that all coordinates have bounded magnitude of
O(\frac{M}{d})
, and that it almost resembles an embedding on the Hamming cube insofar as all but
O(d)
coordinates are one of two distinct values. This satisfies our truncated sparsity condition, enabling us to finally reduce the dimension from
O(d^{2})
to
O(d\log(n))
and obtain the desired truncated metric.

3
Preliminaries and Notation

3.1
Notation

The notation
[n]
denotes the integers
\{1,\dots,n\}
.
\mathds{R}
denotes the set of real numbers, and
\mathds{R}_{\geq 0},\mathds{R}_{>0}
denote the set of nonnegative and strictly positive reals respectively. Random variables are denoted by capital letters (e.g.,
X_{i}
), and points in metric spaces are denoted by lower case letters (e.g.,
x_{i}
). We use array-indexing notation to index into coordinates of vectors. For example, if
x\in\mathds{R}^{d}
,
x[k]
denotes the
k^{\text{th}}
coordinate of
x
. Points in a metric space will generally be denoted by
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. We will often seek to obtain embeddings of these points into a lower dimensional space. We will denote the embedding of a point
x_{i}
by
embedding[i]
, and sometimes by
embedding[x_{i}]
—both of these are supposed to stand for the same thing. Similarly, when talking about distances
d(x_{i},x_{j})
between two points
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
in the metric space, we will interchangeably use both
d(x_{i},x_{j})
and
d(i,j)
. We extensively make use of asymptotic notation
(\Omega(1),O(1))
in the place of absolute constants. These constants will not depend on the problem parameters, unless explicitly specified. For example, at multiple places, we lower bound the probability of an event of interest by an absolute constant larger than 0, and to avoid tracking this constant, we use the
\Omega(1)
notation. We emphasize that we do not require
n
to be sufficiently large when we use this notation, where
n
might be the number of joint random variables or points in the metric space.

3.2
Tree Ising Models

Given an underlying undirected tree on vertices
1,\dots,n
, let
i\sim j
denote the existence of an edge between
i
and
j
. Corresponding to this tree, a
Tree Ising Model
defines a joint probability distribution
D
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
as follows:

\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\propto\exp\left(\sum_{i\sim j}\beta_{ij}\cdot(-1)^{X_{i}\oplus X_{j}}+\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}\cdot(-1)^{X_{i}}\right).

(2)

Here,
\oplus
denotes the XOR operation. The parameters
\beta_{ij}
capture the
pairwise interactions
between adjacent variables in the tree, while the parameters
\gamma_{i}
specify the
external field
on the individual variables. The Tree Ising Model given by (
2
) for a given set of parameters
\{\beta_{ij}\},\{\gamma_{i}\}
can also be completely characterized by specifying the appropriate marginal probabilities for every variable
X_{i}
, and joint probabilities for every pair
(X_{i},X_{j})
corresponding to an edge
i\sim j
in the underlying tree. Under this specification, we can generate a sample from the model as follows: we arbitrarily root the tree at node 1, and direct all edges in the tree away from it. We realize a value for
X_{1}
by sampling from its marginal distribution. Thereafter, for every directed edge
i\to j
, if we have realized the value of
X_{i}
and haven’t yet realized the value of
X_{j}
, we do so by sampling from the
conditional
distribution
\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{j}|X_{i}]
. In other words, tree Ising models are equivalent to arbitrary tree-structured Bayesian networks (with all edges directed away from the root) over binary alphabets. This equivalent characterization has been described and used in other prior works, e.g.,
[
DP21
,
KDDC23
]
.

3.3
Symmetric Tree Ising Models

If all the
\gamma_{i}
’s are 0 in the expression for the joint probability distribution in
Equation

2
, we obtain a
symmetric
tree Ising model, or a tree Ising model with
no external field
. The joint distribution
D
for these models is simply given by

\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\propto\exp\left(\sum_{i\sim j}\beta_{ij}(-1)^{X_{i}\oplus X_{j}}\right).

(3)

In a symmetric tree Ising model,
\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}=0]=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}=1]=0.5
for all
i\in[n]
. These models are widely studied in the literature—the assumption of no external fields makes the analysis of these tree-structured models significantly easier, while also capturing the central aspects of a number of problems. For example,
[
BK20
]
, and more recently
[
BABK22
]

[

]
and its variants for

tree Ising models under the assumption of no external field—this assumption is necessary to make their analysis tractable. As we shall see, while we are able to obtain
\ell_{1}
dimension reduction results more generally for tree Ising models even with an external field, our analysis for the symmetric case ends up being much simpler.

We can identify a symmetric tree Ising model uniquely given the underlying tree, and a single parameter
\theta_{ij}
corresponding to every edge
i\sim j
that specifies “flip” probabilities, where

\theta_{ij}=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]=\frac{1}{1+\exp(2\beta_{ij})}.

(4)

Given this characterization, we can generate a sample from the symmetric tree Ising model as follows: we arbitrarily root the tree at node 1, and direct all edges away from the root. We first draw a uniformly random value in
\{0,1\}
for
X_{1}
. Thereafter, for every directed edge
i\to j
, if we have realized the value of
X_{i}
and haven’t yet realized the value of
X_{j}
, we do so as follows: independently, with probability
\theta_{ij}
, we set
X_{j}=1-X_{i}
, and with probability
1-\theta_{ij}
, we set
X_{j}=X_{i}
. A sample generated via this process has the same distribution as
Equation

3
, given the assignment to the
\theta_{ij}

Equation

4
. In other words, symmetric tree Ising models are equivalent to tree-structured Bayesian networks (with all edges directed away from the root) over binary alphabets, where the conditional distributions for every edge

\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{j}=0|X_{i}=0]=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{j}=1|X_{i}=1]=1-\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{j}=1|X_{i}=0]=1-\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{j}=0|X_{i}=1]
.

3.4
Metric Spaces of Interest

We define explicitly the metric spaces that we study in our paper here. Recall that a metric space
(S,d)
is defined by a set of points
S
, together with a distance function
d
that maps pairs of points to nonnegative numbers, and satisfies (i) symmetry, (ii) triangle inequality and (iii) the property that every point has zero distance only to itself.

1.

\ell_{1}
metric: This metric space comprises of a set of
n
points
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{d}
, where the distance function
d(x_{i},x_{j})=\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}|x_{i}[k]-x_{j}[k]|
.

2.

Tree metric: Consider an undirected edge-weighted tree
T_{n}
on
n
vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Let
d(x_{i},x_{j})
denote the standard graph distance on
T_{n}
, namely
d(x_{i},x_{j})=\sum_{e\in x_{i}\to x_{j}}e
, where the notation
e\in i\to j
indexes the (weights on the) edges on the shortest path from
x_{i}
to
x_{j}
.

3.

Line metric: This is simply a special case of the tree metric, where the tree is a line
L_{n}
of vertices
x_{1}\to x_{2}\to\dots\to x_{n}
. We can then instead think of the vertices as being points on the real line spaced according to the edge weights (with say
x_{1}
at the origin).

4.

Tree Ising model metric: Given a tree Ising model
D
on
n
random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, the distance function
d_{D}
is given by
d_{D}(X_{i},X_{j})=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]
.
1
1

1

This is technically a
pseudo-metric
space, because it may not satisfy property (iii) from above—two different variables
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
may both have
\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}=1]=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{j}=1]=1
, in which case they have zero distance. We will not care too much about this distinction.

We make a simple observation here: any
\ell_{1}
metric can be alternatively viewed as
d(x_{i},x_{j})=c\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]
where
D
is
some

\{0,1\}^{n}
(not necessarily a tree Ising model). To see this, let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be

. Now, consider shifting and scaling all the points, so that all coordinates of every point are in
[0,1]
. This preserves distances between points upto the constant scaling factor. Consider a joint distribution
D
on
n

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. A sample from this joint distribution is obtained as follows: first, we choose a coordinate

uniformly at random. Then, we sample a real number
p
uniformly at random from the interval
[0,1]
. For each

if

x_{i}
. Then, observe that for any

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left|x_{i}[k]-x_{j}[k]\right|=\frac{1}{d}\cdot\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{1}.

This observation motivates the main question in our work: is there a rich enough class of distributions

such that
\ell_{1}

Finally, when we say that a metric space

\displaystyle\beta\cdot d_{1}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq d_{2}(\sigma(x_{i}),\sigma(x_{j}))\leq\alpha\beta\cdot d_{1}(x_{i},x_{j}).

(5)

3.5
Caterpillar Tree Decomposition

Our low-dimension embedding for capped tree metrics crucially involves using a particular tree decomposition technique, known as the “caterpillar” decomposition, or also the “heavy-light” decomposition. This decomposition has been used in the past
[
LMS98
,
Gup99
,
Gup01
,
CS02
]
for embedding tree metrics into
\ell_{1}
. We briefly describe the caterpillar decomposition here. Given an undirected tree
T_{n}
on
n
vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, let us arbitrarily root the tree at
x_{1}
. The caterpillar decomposition then decomposes the edges of the tree into several disjoint vertical
3
3

3

Symmetric Tree Ising Models

We begin our analysis of embedding tree Ising models into

4.1
Symmetric Tree Ising Models Reduce to Fixed Cap Tree Metrics

Given a symmetric tree Ising model
D
, the metric of interest that we want to embed into
\ell_{1}
with few dimensions is

\displaystyle d_{D}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}].

First, let us consider the symmetric tree Ising model
D^{\prime}
with the same tree structure, but where the flip probabilities on the edges (
Equation

4
) are replaced from
\theta_{ij}
to
\min(\theta_{ij},1-\theta_{ij})
, so that all the flip probabilities are in
[0,0.5]
. We will show later how we are able to reduce to this case without loss of generality—for now, let us assume this is possible. We have the following lemma:

Lemma 4.1

(symmetric tree Ising model with no “bad” edges
\to
fixed cap tree)
.

Let
D^{\prime}
be a symmetric tree Ising model where all the edge-flip probabilities are at most
0.5
. Define the following capped embedding:

\displaystyle d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\min\left(\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e},\quad 0.5\right),

where the notation
e\in(i\to j)
indexes the edges on the (undirected) path from
i
to
j
in the tree, and
\theta_{e}
denotes the weight on the edge. Then, for all
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle 0.5\cdot d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)\leq d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j)\leq 8\cdot d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j).

Proof.

Fix
i,j
, and let us think of the generative process that realizes
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
. Observe that
X_{i}\neq X_{j}
if and only if we decide to flip realizations on an odd number of edges on the path from
i
to
j
. Then, by the union bound,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{at least one edge flip on $i\to j$}]\leq\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}

\displaystyle\implies\qquad

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{not a single edge flip on $i\to j$}]\geq 1-\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}.

Further, recall that we decide whether to flip on an edge independently of the others. We can then say that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{exactly one edge flip on $i\to j$}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}\left[e\text{ flips}\right]\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}_{p^{\prime}}\left[\text{edges on $i\to j$ other than $e$ don't flip}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}\left[e\text{ flips}\right]\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}\left[\text{all edges on $i\to j$ don't flip}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}\right)\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}.

We have two cases:

Case 1:
\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}\leq 0.5
.

In this case,
d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j)=\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}
. We have that

\displaystyle d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd number of flips on $i\to j$}]

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{exactly one flip on $i\to j$}]\geq\underbrace{\left(1-\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}\right)}_{\geq 0.5}\underbrace{\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}}_{=d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j)}

\displaystyle\geq 0.5\cdot d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j).

Furthermore, observe that

\displaystyle d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd number of flips on $i\to j$}]\leq\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{at least one flip on $i\to j$}]\leq\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}=d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j).

Together, we get that

\displaystyle d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)\leq d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j)\leq 2\cdot d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j).

Case 2:
\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}>0.5
.

In this case,
d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j)=0.5
. One direction of what we want to show is easy:

\displaystyle d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]\leq 1

\displaystyle\implies\qquad

\displaystyle 0.5\cdot d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)\leq 0.5=d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j).

For the other direction, we want to lower bound
d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)
by a constant. Observe that since
\sum_{e\in(i\to j)}\theta_{e}>0.5
and all
\theta_{e}\in[0,0.5]
, there must exist a prefix of edges on the path from
i
to
j
such that
0.25\leq\sum_{e\in\mathrm{prefix}}\theta_{e}<0.75
. For this prefix, using the same arguments as above, we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{even flips on prefix}]

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{no flips on prefix}]\geq 1-\sum_{e\in\mathrm{prefix}}\theta_{e}>1-0.75=0.25.

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd flips on prefix}]\geq\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{exactly one flip on prefix}]

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\sum_{e\in\mathrm{prefix}}\theta_{e}\right)\sum_{e\in\mathrm{prefix}}\theta_{e}

\displaystyle>(0.25)^{2}=2^{-4}.

Therefore, we get that

\displaystyle d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd number of flips on $i\to j$}]

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd flips on prefix}]\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{even flips on suffix}]+\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{even flips on prefix}]\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd flips on suffix}]

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\geq 2^{-4}\left(\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{even flips on suffix}]+\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd flips on suffix}]\right)=2^{-4}=2^{-3}\cdot d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j).

Putting the two bounds together, we have in all

\displaystyle 0.5\cdot d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)\leq d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j)\leq 8\cdot d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j).

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We will now see how we are able to reduce from an arbitrary symmetric tree Ising model
D
with edge-flip probabilities
\theta_{ij}\in[0,1]
to a symmetric tree Ising model
D^{\prime}
with edge-flip probabilities
\min(\theta_{ij},1-\theta_{ij})\in[0,0.5]
.
Let us call an edge
e
“bad” if
\theta_{e}>0.5
, and “good” if
\theta_{e}\leq 0.5
. Then, we have the following claim:

Claim 4.2
.

For any pair of nodes
i,j
,

\displaystyle\text{(1) If the number of bad edges between $i$ and $j$ is even, then }d_{D}(i,j)=d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j).

\displaystyle\text{(2) If the number of bad edges between $i$ and $j$ is odd, then }d_{D}(i,j)\geq 0.5.

Proof.

For the first part, observe that

\displaystyle d_{D}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{odd number of flips on $i\to j$}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{odd flips on bad edges}]\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{even flips on good edges}]

\displaystyle\qquad+\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{even flips on bad edges}]\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{odd flips on good edges}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{odd flips on bad edges}]\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{even flips on good edges}]

\displaystyle\qquad+\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{even flips on bad edges}]\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd flips on good edges}],

where the preceding equality follows because weights on good edges don’t change from
D
to
D^{\prime}
. For the first part, observe crucially that if the number of bad edges between
i
and
j
is even, the parity of flips and non-flips on the bad edges is always the same. Hence, we can replace non-flips to flips from
D
to
D^{\prime}
, to obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{odd flips on bad edges}]=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{odd non-flips on bad edges}]=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd flips on bad edges}]

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{even flips on bad edges}]=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[\text{even non-flips on bad edges}]=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{even flips on bad edges}].

This gives us that

\displaystyle d_{D}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd flips on bad edges}]\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{even flips on good edges}]

\displaystyle\qquad+\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{even flips on bad edges}]\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd flips on good edges}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D^{\prime}}[\text{odd number of flips on $i\to j$}]=d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j).

For the second part, let
Y_{e}
be a
\pm 1
random variable, which is
-1
if we flipped and
1
if we did not flip on edge
e
on the path from
i
to
j
. Let
Y=\prod_{e\in(i\to j)}Y_{e}
. Then, we have that
Y=-1
if and only if
X_{i}\neq X_{j}
. Observe that
d_{D}(i,j)=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]=\operatorname{{Pr}}[Y=-1]
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[Y]

\displaystyle=\prod_{e\in(i\to j)}\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[Y_{e}\right]=-d_{D}(i,j)+(1-d_{D}(i,j))=1-2d_{D}(i,j).

For each good edge
e
, since
\operatorname{{Pr}}[Y_{e}=-1]\leq 0.5
, we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[Y_{e}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Similarly, for each bad edge, we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[Y_{e}\right]

\displaystyle<0.

Since we are under the case that the number of bad edges from
i
to
j
is odd, we get that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[Y]

\displaystyle=\prod_{e\in(i\to j)}\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[Y_{e}\right]\leq 0\implies d_{D}(i,j)\geq 0.5.

∎

Thus, if we are able to embed
d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}
into
\ell_{1}
, with an additional coordinate which simply indicates if the number of bad edges on the root-to-node path is odd, we can achieve our goal of embedding
d_{D}
into
\ell_{1}
.

Claim 4.3
.

If
E
is a constant distortion embedding of
d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}
into
\ell_{1}
, then
E^{\prime}
, defined as the concatenation

E^{\prime}[i]=(E[i],\mathds{1}[\#\text{bad edges from root $X_{1}$ to $X_{i}$ is odd}])

is a constant distortion embedding of
d_{D}(i,j).

Proof.

Given
D
, we obtain
D^{\prime}
where the edge-flip probabilities are at most
0.5
. If the number of bad edges on the path between
i
and
j
is even, then

\displaystyle\mathds{1}[\#\text{bad edges from root $X_{1}$ to $X_{i}$ is odd}]-\mathds{1}[\#\text{bad edges from root $X_{1}$ to $X_{j}$ is odd}]=0

\displaystyle\implies\qquad

\displaystyle\|E^{\prime}[i]-E^{\prime}[j]\|_{1}=\|E[i]-E[j]\|_{1}.

Furthermore, from part (1) in
4.2
, we know that
d_{D}(i,j)=d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)
. Also, from
Lemma

4.1
, we know that
d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j)
is constant distortion embedding of
d_{D^{\prime}}(i,j)
. Thus, we are good if

is a constant distortion embedding of
d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}
.

If the number of bad edges on the path between
i
and

is odd, observe that

\displaystyle\left|\mathds{1}[\#\text{bad edges from root $X_{1}$ to $X_{i}$ is odd}]-\mathds{1}[\#\text{bad edges from root $X_{1}$ to $X_{j}$ is odd}]\right|=1

\displaystyle\implies\qquad

\displaystyle\|E^{\prime}[i]-E^{\prime}[j]\|_{1}=\|E[i]-E[j]\|_{1}+1\leq\Theta(1)\cdot d^{{0.5}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(i,j)+1\leq\Theta(1)\cdot 0.5+1\leq\Theta(1)\cdot 0.5

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\Theta(1)\cdot d_{D}(i,j),

where the last inequality follows from part (2) in
4.2
.
Also, we have that

\displaystyle d_{D}(i,j)=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{D}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]\leq 1\leq 1+\|E[i]-E[j]\|_{1}=\|E^{\prime}[i]-E^{\prime}[j]\|_{1},

giving us that

\displaystyle d_{D}(i,j)\leq\|E^{\prime}[i]-E^{\prime}[j]\|_{1}\leq\Theta(1)\cdot d_{D}(i,j).

This completes the proof.
∎

Thus, we have argued that the crux of embedding the metric

defined by the symmetric tree Ising model into

is obtaining a constant distortion embedding of the metric

into
\ell_{1}
.

Similar techniques have previously appeared in the literature: for example, in
[
AFGN22
]
, the “sawtooth” function is exactly a means of non-lazy snaking with a fixed period (i.e.,
\Delta=1
always in step 4 above), which similarly allows embedding fixed-cap metrics into
\ell_{1}
. Our version of
lazy
snaking is more conducive towards deriving our general result (
Theorem

4
) on truncated
\ell_{1}
metrics.

We will require the following claim, which says that for every pair of nodes, lazy snaking does not overestimate their distance, and also covers at least a constant fraction of their distance in expectation. As a point of comparison, this claim is qualitatively similar to
[
AFGN22
, Lemma 2]
, but our proof is a different case analysis.

Claim 4.4
.

Let
embedding=\textsc{LazySnake}(loc,M)
. For every fixed pair of nodes
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
,

\displaystyle\text{(1) }\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[|embedding[i]-embedding[j]|\leq d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})\right]=1.

\displaystyle\text{(2) }\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|embedding[i]-embedding[j]|\right]\geq\Omega(1)\cdot d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j}).

Proof.

The first part of the claim is straightforward, since snaking can only reduce distance between two nodes.

For the second part, let
i<j
without loss of generality. Let
E_{1}
be the event that
snake=0
for some
t\in[\max(0,loc[i]-2M),loc[i]]
. Since the width that we snake over (if at all we are not at rest) is
M
, observe that
\operatorname{{Pr}}[E_{1}]=1
.
We have three cases:

Case 1:
1\leq|loc[i]-loc[j]|\leq M.

In this case,
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=|loc[i]-loc[j]|
. Let
E_{2}
be the event that
snake=0
for some
t^{\prime}=[loc[i]-M/4,loc[i]]
. Then,
\operatorname{{Pr}}[E_{2}|E_{1}]\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{8}=\Omega(1)
. This is because, the time to reach
loc[i]
from
t
is at most
2M
, and in this duration, we flip the “rest” coin at most
\frac{2M}{M/4}=8=O(1)
times to arrive at such a
t^{\prime}
. Now, conditioned on
E_{2}
, we have that with probability
1/2
, we choose to move and snake for a width
M
, and if that happens, we have that that
0\leq embedding[i]\leq M/4
. Then, since
|loc[i]-loc[j]|\leq M
, we will have
embedding[j]-embedding[i]\geq\frac{1}{2}(loc[j]-loc[i])
. This is because, in the worst case,
embedding[i]=M/4
, and
loc[j]=loc[i]+M
, in which case
embedding[j]=3M/4
after snaking around once. Since we only had to condition on a constant number of coin flips to obtain this,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|embedding[i]-embedding[j]|\right]

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{{Pr}}[E_{1},E_{2}]\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|embedding[i]-embedding[j]|~|~E_{1},E_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(1)\cdot|loc[i]-loc[j]|.

Case 2:
M<|loc[i]-loc[j]|\leq 4M.

In this case,
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=M
. Let
E_{2}
be the event that
embedding[i]=0
and
snake=0
for some
t^{\prime}\in[loc[j]-M,loc[j]-3M/4]
. Then, we have that
\operatorname{{Pr}}[E_{2}|E_{1}]\geq\Omega(1)
. This is because, to arrive at such a
t^{\prime}
starting from
t
while also ensuring that
embedding[i]=0
, we may flip at most
\frac{2M+3M}{M/4}=O(1)
“rest” coins from time
t
— this happens with probability at least
\Omega(1)
. Thereafter, conditioned on
E_{2}
, the coin flips “snake” with probability at least
1/2
, and this ensures that
embedding[j]\geq 3M/4
, which in turn gives
|embedding[i]-embedding[j]|\geq 3M/4
. Again, since we only had conditioned on
O(1)
, we get that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|embedding[i]-embedding[j]|\right]

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{{Pr}}[E_{1},E_{2}]\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|embedding[i]-embedding[j]|~|~E_{1},E_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(1)\cdot M.

Case 3:
|loc[i]-loc[j]|>4M.

Here as well,
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=M
. First, just as in Case 1 above, let
E_{2}
be the event that
snake=0
for some
t^{\prime}=[loc[i]-M/4,loc[i]]
, for which we saw that
\operatorname{{Pr}}[E_{2}|E_{1}]\geq\Omega(1)
. Note that
E_{1},E_{2}
together ensure that
embedding[i]\leq M/4
. Now, let
E_{3}
be the event that
snake=0
for some
t^{\prime\prime}\in[loc[j]-4M,loc[j]-2M]
. Since
|loc[i]-loc[j]|>4M
, there exists such a
t^{\prime\prime}>t^{\prime}
with certainty, for which we have not yet assumed any conditioning, i.e.,
\operatorname{{Pr}}[E_{3}|E_{2},E_{1}]=1
. Now, following
E_{3}
, with
\frac{3M}{M/4}=O(1)
“rest” flips (happens with probability at least
\Omega(1)
), we ensure that
snake=0
up until some
t^{\prime\prime\prime}\in[loc[j]-M,loc[j]-3M/4]
. Thereafter, we may flip a “snake”, ensuring that
embedding[j]\geq 3M/4
. Putting together, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|embedding[i]-embedding[j]|\right]

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{{Pr}}[E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}]\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|embedding[i]-embedding[j]|~|~E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(1)\cdot M.

∎

Using
O(\log n)
dimensions, we can then boost the in-expectation guarantee of
4.4
to obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 5

(Fixed cap line into
\ell_{1}
)
.

(L_{n},d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}})
can be embedded into
(\mathds{R}^{d},\ell_{1})
where
d=O(\log n)
with
\Theta(1)
distortion.

Proof.

Let us invoke
LazySnake
(loc,M)

N
times to obtain
N
independent copies
embedding_{1},\dots,embedding_{N}
for all the nodes. Let
X^{(l)}_{ij}=|embedding_{l}[i]-embedding_{l}[j]|
. Then, observe that
0\leq X^{(l)}_{ij}\leq d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})
. Furthermore,
4.4
gives that
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}X^{(l)}_{ij}\right]\geq cNd^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})
for some constant
c>0
. Hoeffding’s inequality then gives us

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{N}X^{(l)}_{ij}\leq\frac{cNd^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})}{2}\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\Theta(N)\right),

or rather, choosing
N=C\log n
for an appropriately large constant
C
gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}X^{(l)}_{ij}\leq\frac{c}{2}\cdot d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})\right]\leq\frac{2}{n^{3}}.

Furthermore, since the embedding never overestimates distances, observe that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}X^{(l)}_{ij}>d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})\right]=0.

We can therefore rescale each
embedding_{k}
as
embedding_{k}\leftarrow embedding_{k}/N
, and union bound over all the
<n^{2}
pairs
i,j
to obtain that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\forall i,j:~\Omega(1)\cdot d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\sum_{l=1}^{N}|embedding_{l}[i]-embedding_{l}[j]|\leq d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})\right]>0,

which in particular, implies that there exists such an embedding that obtains
\Theta(1)
distortion with
N=O(\log n)
dimensions.
∎

4.2.2
Fixed Cap Tree Metrics

Consider now a tree
T_{n}
on
n
vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, for which recall that the distance is given by
d(x_{i},x_{j})=\sum_{e\in x_{i}\to x_{j}}e
. Again, we consider the (fixed) cap version of the tree metric distance, given by

\displaystyle d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=\min(d(x_{i},x_{j}),M).

(10)

We will show that
(T_{n},d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}})
also embeds into
\ell_{1}
using
O(\log^{2}n)
dimensions and constant distortion.

First, let us obtain the caterpillar decomposition (stated in
Section

3.5
) of
T_{n}
. Let
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{m}
denote the caterpillars in its caterpillar decomposition, where each
C_{i}=\{e_{i_{1}},\dots,e_{i_{l}}\}
is a simple path in the tree. The length of
C_{i}
is the sum of the weights of the edges that it is made up of. We will first quickly see how the caterpillar decomposition yields an isometric embedding of the tree metric into
\ell_{1}
, while possibly using a lot many dimensions. We will have a coordinate in the embedding dedicated for every caterpillar in the decomposition – thus, the number of dimensions can be as large as the number of edges in the tree, i.e.,
\Omega(n)
. For every node
x_{i}
, we walk up from
x_{i}
to the root, and for every caterpillar that we touch (at most
\log n
of them), we record the length traversed on that caterpillar at the coordinate dedicated for it. Thus, we will end up with a
\log n
-sparse embedding for every node. We can see that this is an isometric embedding, because for any pair of nodes, the lengths on the caterpillars from their least common ancestor up to root get canceled out when we take the difference of their embeddings, and only the lengths on caterpillars on the path joining them survive.

Now, we describe a slight modification to the above embedding, which possibly increases the dimensions even more, but still ensures that the embedding is isometric, and will be useful for constructing our final low-dimensional embedding for the capped tree metric with cap
M
. For any caterpillar
C_{i}
whose length is at most
M
, we do nothing. For caterpillars having length larger than
M
, we split it up into snippets of size exactly
M/\log n
, and possibly one left-over snippet of size smaller than
M/\log n
. Having snipped each caterpillar like so, let
S_{1},\dots,S_{s}
be all the resulting snippets — we will dedicate a single coordinate in the embedding to each
S_{i}
. As before, for every node, we walk up from the node to the root, and record the length of each snippet we traverse at the corresponding coordinate. Let this embedding be denoted by
\mathrm{SC}
(short for Snipped Caterpillar) i.e.,
\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})
is a vector of size
s
for each node
x_{i}
. This embedding is still isometric, however, the number of snippets
s
can now be huge, if a large number of the caterpillars had length
\gg M
.

We will now make a couple observations on the structure of the vector
\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})
for any pair of nodes
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
.

Claim 4.5
.

If
d(x_{i},x_{j})\leq M
, then
\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})
has at most
6\log n
non-zero coordinates, and each of these coordinates is at most
M/\log n
in magnitude.

Proof.

Let
z=\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})
. The only non-zero coordinates in
z
correspond to the caterpillar snippets on the path joining
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
. First, let us count the number of full
M/\log n
sized snippets on this path — since
\|z\|_{1}=d(x_{i},x_{j})\leq M
, we can have at most
\log n
many such snippets. Now, let us count the number of non-zero coordinates contributed by snippets of length
<M/\log n
. These could either be due to caterpillars that were smaller than
M/\log n
, and never got snipped — there could be at most
2\log n
many of these, since the (unsnipped) caterpillar embeddings of both
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are
\log n
sparse. Or, these could be due to partial snippets on the path joining
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
. Observe that we can have at most one partial snippet per whole caterpillar on this path (except possibly an extra at the least common ancestor), and thus, we can again only have
2\log n+1\leq 3\log n
many of these in total. Together, we get
\log n+2\log n+3\log n=6\log n
many non-zero coordinates, as claimed. Finally, by construction, each coordinate in both
\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})
and
\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})
is at most
M/\log n
, and hence the difference can be at most
M/\log n
in magnitude.
∎

Claim 4.6
.

If
d(x_{i},x_{j})>M
, then
\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})
contains a set of
\frac{3}{4}\log n
non-zero coordinates, each of which is at least
\frac{M}{20\log n}
and at most
\frac{M}{\log n}
in magnitude, and the sum of these coordinates is at least
3M/4
.

Proof.

Again, let
z=\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})
. First, let us count the number of snippets on the path joining
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
that are less than
\frac{M}{20\log n}
in absolute value. Either these arise due to unsnipped-caterpillars of length smaller than
M/\log n
in the embeddings of
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
— there can be at most
2\log n
many of these; or these arise due to partial snippets on the path joining them — there are again at most
2\log n+1\leq 3\log n
many of these, as argued above. Thus, there can only be at most
5\log n
many coordinates having magnitude smaller than
\frac{M}{20\log n}
in
z
. These coordinates account for length at most
M/4
out of
\|z\|_{1}
. All the other coordinates necessarily have magnitude at least
\frac{M}{20\log n}
, but also at most
M/\log n
by construction. Since
\|z\|=d(x_{i},x_{j})>M
, these other coordinates have to account for at least
3M/4
of the length. Thus, there needs to be a set of at least
\frac{3M}{4}\cdot\frac{\log n}{M}=\frac{3}{4}\log n
many coordinates, each of which is at least
\frac{M}{20\log n}
, and whose sum is at least
3M/4
as required.
∎

Now, for
k=6\log n
, consider choosing a uniformly random hash function
h:[s]\to[k]
, where recall that
s
is the total number of caterpillar snippets. For each node
x_{i}
, let
\mathrm{HSC}(x_{i})
(short for Hashed Snipped Caterpillar) be a vector of size
6\log n
, defined in the following. This will be the building block of our final
O(\log^{2}n)
sized embedding.

\displaystyle\mathrm{HSC}(x_{i})[p]=\sum_{q\in[s]:h(q)=p}\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})[q]\qquad\text{for }p\in\{1,\dots,k\}.

(11)

Now, we interpret each coordinate of
\mathrm{HSC}
as defining a line metric, over which we will do lazy snaking. Concretely, for each
p\in[k]
, let
loc_{p}[i]=\mathrm{HSC}(x_{i})[p]
— note that for the root
x_{1}
,
loc_{p}[1]=0
for all
p
, since
\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})=0^{s}
. Let
sloc_{p}=\textsc{LazySnake}(loc_{p},M/\log n)
, and consider the representation of each node
x_{i}
as

embedding[i]=(sloc_{1}[i],sloc_{2}[i],\dots,sloc_{k}[i]).

(12)

First, we make the following simple claim:

Claim 4.7

(No overestimation)
.

Fix any pair of nodes
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
. Then,
with probability 1,

\displaystyle\|embedding[i]-embedding[j]\|_{1}\leq 6d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j}).

Proof.

We have two cases:

Case 1:
d(x_{i},x_{j})\leq M.

In this case,
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=d(x_{i},x_{j})
. Observe that

\displaystyle\|embedding[i]-embedding[j]\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left|loc_{p}[i]-loc_{p}[j]\right|\quad\text{(snaking never overestimates distances)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left|\sum_{q\in[s]:h(q)=p}(\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})[q]-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})[q])\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=1}^{k}\sum_{q\in[s]:h(q)=p}\left|\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})[q]-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})[q]\right|

\displaystyle=d(x_{i},x_{j}).

Case 2:
d(x_{i},x_{j})>M.

In this case,
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=M
. We have

\displaystyle\|embedding[i]-embedding[j]\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=1}^{k}\frac{M}{\log n}\qquad\left(\text{snaking width is }\frac{M}{\log n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 6M.

∎

4.7
ensures that the embedding never overestimates distances beyond a constant factor. However, we also have the following nice property, which ensures that in expectation, the embedding captures at least a constant fraction of the distance between any fixed pair of nodes.

Lemma 4.8

(No underestimation)
.

Fix any pair of nodes
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
, and fix
p\in[k]
. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\left|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{h}\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{\mathrm{snake}}\left[\left|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]\right|\right]\geq\frac{\Omega(1)}{k}\cdot d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j}).

Thus, by linearity of expectation,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\|embedding[i]-embedding[j]\|_{1}\right]=\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\sum_{p=1}^{k}\left|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]\right|\right]\geq\Omega(1)\cdot d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j}).

Proof.

We have two cases:

Case 1:
d(x_{i},x_{j})\leq M.

In this case,
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=d(x_{i},x_{j})
. Recall from
4.5
that
\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})
has at most
6\log n
non-zero coordinates and each of these coordinates is at most
\frac{M}{\log n}
in magnitude. Let us possibly include some zero coordinates, so that we have exactly
k=6\log n
of these “special” coordinates
c_{1},\dots,c_{r},\dots,c_{k}
. Observe that
\sum_{r=1}^{k}|\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})[c_{r}]-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})[c_{r}]|=d(x_{i},x_{j})
, because the snipped-caterpillar embedding is isometric. Let us condition on the realization of the hash function
h
, which is independent of the randomness in the snaking. Conditioned on this realization, for
loc_{p}[i]=\mathrm{HSC}(x_{i})[p]
and
sloc_{p}=\textsc{LazySnake}(loc_{p},M/\log n)
we have from
4.4
that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{\mathrm{snake}}\left[|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]|\right]\geq\Omega(1)\cdot d^{{M/\log n}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(loc_{p}[i],loc_{p}[j]).

Now, taking an expectation with respect to the choice of the hash function, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{h}\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{\mathrm{snake}}\left[|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]|\right]\geq\Omega(1)\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{h}\left[d^{{M/\log n}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(loc_{p}[i],loc_{p}[j])\right].

Let
A
be the event that only one of the
k
special coordinates hashes to
p
. Then, we have that
\operatorname{{Pr}}[A]=\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{k-1}\geq\frac{1}{e}=\Omega(1)
, yielding

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{h}\left[d^{{M/\log n}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(loc_{p}[i],loc_{p}[j])\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(1)\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{h}\left[d^{{M/\log n}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(loc_{p}[i],loc_{p}[j])~|~A\right]

Furthermore, conditioned on
A
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{h}\left[d^{{M/\log n}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(loc_{p}[i],loc_{p}[j])~|~A\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\frac{1}{k}\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{h}\left[d^{{M/\log n}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(loc_{p}[i],loc_{p}[j])~|~\text{only $r^{\text{th}}$ special coordinate hashes to $p$}\right].

But now, observe that if only the
r^{\text{th}}
special coordinate hashes to
p
, we have
d^{{M/\log n}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(loc_{p}[i],loc_{p}[j])=|\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})[c_{r}]-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})[c_{r}]|
. This is because
|\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})[c_{r}]-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})[c_{r}]|
is at most
M/\log n
, which is smaller than the cap. Finally, recalling that
\sum_{r=1}^{k}|\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})[c_{r}]-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})[c_{r}]|=d(x_{i},x_{j})
, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{h}\left[d^{{M/\log n}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(loc_{p}[i],loc_{p}[j])~|~A\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\frac{1}{k}\left|\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})[c_{r}]-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})[c_{r}]\right|=\frac{1}{k}\cdot d(x_{i},x_{j}).

Putting everything together, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{h}\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{\mathrm{snake}}\left[|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]|\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\Omega(1)}{k}\cdot d(x_{i},x_{j})=\Omega(1)\cdot\frac{d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})}{k}.

Case 2:
d(x_{i},x_{j})>M.

In this case,
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=M
. Recall from
4.6
that
\mathrm{SC}(x_{i})-\mathrm{SC}(x_{j})
has a set of
k^{\prime}=\frac{3}{4}\log n
non-zero “special” coordinates
c_{1},\dots,c_{r},\dots,c_{k^{\prime}}
, each of which is at least
\frac{M}{20\log n}
and at most
\frac{M}{\log n}
in magnitude. Let
A
be the event that the non-special coordinates that get hashed to
p
amount for a distance of at least
\frac{M}{100\log n}
. Concretely, under
A
,
\left|\sum_{q\in[s]:q\text{ not special},h(q)=p}(SC(x_{i})[q]-SC(x_{j})[q])\right|\geq\frac{M}{100\log n}
.
We have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]|\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[A]\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]|~|~A\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\operatorname{{Pr}}[\neg A]\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]|~|~\neg A\right]

Conditioned on
A
, we are happy if none of the special coordinates hash to
p
, which happens with probability
\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{k^{\prime}}\geq\exp\left(-\frac{k^{\prime}}{\sqrt{k(k-1)}}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}\sqrt{\frac{6\log n}{6\log n-1}}\right)\geq 1/e=\Omega(1)
for
n\geq 2
. In this case, lazy snaking will capture at least a constant fraction of
\frac{M}{100\log n}
, yielding

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]|~|~A\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(1)\cdot\frac{M}{100\log n}.

If
A
does not occur, we have that
\left|\sum_{q\in[s]:q\text{ not special},h(q)=p}(SC(x_{i})[q]-SC(x_{j})[q])\right|<\frac{M}{100\log n}
. In this case, we are happy if exactly one of the special coordinates hashes to
p
, which happens with probability

5
General Tree Ising Models

We now turn our attention to the more general and challenging case of tree Ising models on
n
random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
where each variable can additionally have a nonzero external field (
Equation

2
). Recall that these tree Ising models can be uniquely described by specifying marginal probabilities for every
X_{i}
, and joint probabilities for every pair
(X_{i},X_{j})
that corresponds to an edge in the tree.

To exhaustively capture the probability of disagreement between two variables
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
, we end up requiring to crucially capture contribution towards this by the “independence” between the variables. In the typical notion of independence, this could be thought of in terms of being distance that grows with
H(X_{i}|X_{j})
or
H(X_{j}|X_{i})
, where
H
is the standard Shannon entropy. However, conditional Shannon entropy
H(X_{i}|X_{j})
seems at first glance an unwieldy quantity from the perspective of embedding into
\ell_{1}
(for reasons like, e.g., the behavior of
H(X_{i})
as
X_{i}\rightarrow 0
). We define an alternative seemingly simpler quantity, which we call ‘‘Bernoulli Randomness”
4
4

4

We expect this quantity (or equivalent versions of it) to have previously been defined and used in the literature.
, that essentially captures what we are hoping for.

5.1
Bernoulli Randomness

Definition 1

(Bernoulli Randomness)
.

Any binary random variable
X
can be written as the mixture of an unbiased random variable and a deterministic (i.e. single state of support) variable. Let
\operatorname{{Br}}(X)
be the weight of the mixture on the unbiased variable. We can see that
\operatorname{{Br}}(X)=2\cdot\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1])
.

A philosophical interpretation of Bernoulli randomness is that it corresponds to the probability that
X
is determined by a purely random coin flip, instead of being deterministically chosen. Like Shannon entropy, Bernoulli randomness increases as
X
becomes less biased.

We similarly define conditional Bernoulli randomness:

Definition 2

(Conditional Bernoulli Randomness)
.

\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y)=\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}\operatorname{{Pr}}(Y=y)\cdot\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y=y)=\sum_{y}2\cdot\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}(X=0,Y=y),\operatorname{{Pr}}(X=1,Y=y))

Figure 2
:
Bernoulli randomness and conditional Bernoulli randomness as “boxed” mass.

It helps a lot to keep
Figure

2
in mind when thinking of Bernoulli randomness. The probability mass of
X
has been divided by the red solid line into blue and green states corresponding to
\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0]
and
\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1]
respectively. Here,
\operatorname{{Br}}(X)
is exactly the mass “boxed” by the red dashed line—there is an equal amount of green and blue mass in the box. Similarly, on the right, the blue and green mass of
X
has been distributed in the states of
Y=0
and
Y=1
according to the joint distribution of
X
and
Y
. The total boxed mass across both the states is precisely
\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y)
.

We can lower bound the probability of disagreement between
X
and
Y
in terms of conditional Bernoulli randomness in a straightforward manner:

Lemma 5.1

(
\operatorname{{Br}}
lower-bounds distance)
.

\operatorname{{Pr}}[X\neq Y]\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{{Br}}(Y|X),\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y)
.

Proof.

This is true because, for example, because if
Y
is chosen via an unbiased coin from
X
with probability
\operatorname{{Br}}(Y|X)
, then for each unbiased coin flip there is at least
1/2
chance
X\neq Y
and thus
\operatorname{{Pr}}[X\neq Y]\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{{Br}}(Y|X)
. More formally,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[X\neq Y]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0,Y=1]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1,Y=0]

\displaystyle\geq\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0,Y=0],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0,Y=1])+\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1,Y=0],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1,Y=0])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{{Br}}(Y|X).

The argument for
\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y)
is identical.
∎

The following result also nicely matches the intuition from Shannon entropy:

Claim 5.2

(
\operatorname{{Br}}
maximized at independence)
.

Given two variables with fixed marginals
X,Y
, when optimizing over their joints it holds that
\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y)
and
\operatorname{{Br}}(Y|X)
are both maximized when
X,Y
are independent.

Proof.

We state the proof for
\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y)
—the proof for
\operatorname{{Br}}(Y|X)
is identical.

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y)

\displaystyle=2(\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0,Y=0],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1,Y=0])+\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0,Y=1],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1,Y=1]))

\displaystyle\leq 2(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0,Y=0]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0,Y=1])=2\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0],

and similarly,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y)

\displaystyle=2(\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0,Y=0],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1,Y=0])+\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0,Y=1],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1,Y=1]))

\displaystyle\leq 2(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1,Y=0]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1,Y=1])=2\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1].

This means
\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y)\leq 2\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=0],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X=1])=\operatorname{{Br}}(X)
. Note that equality is attained when
X
and
Y
are independent.
∎

The niceness of the aforementioned claim is that the same statement is classically known to hold when
\operatorname{{Br}}
terms are replaced with
H
(Shannon entropy) terms. A key difference, however, is that while the independent joint distribution is the
unique
maximizer for Shannon entropy, there are often many maximizers for Bernoulli randomness. For example, consider random variables
X,Y
with
p_{00}=0.1,p_{01}=0.2,p_{10}=0.2,p_{11}=0.5
. Here,
\operatorname{{Br}}(X|Y)=\operatorname{{Br}}(Y|X)=\operatorname{{Br}}(X)=\operatorname{{Br}}(Y)=0.6
, but
X
and
Y
are not independent. This indicates that although the notions share similar intuitions, there are key differences.

In our proof for tree Ising models, we will be studying the accumulation of conditional Bernoulli randomness. A simple lemma that may help see the niceness of this property (analogous to the data processing inequality for mutual information) is as follows:

Lemma 5.3

(Data Processing Inequality)
.

Consider a Bayesian network on a line specified by
X_{1}\to\dots\to X_{n}
. For any
i<j<k
, it must hold that
\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{i}|X_{k})\geq\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{i}|X_{j})
.

Proof.

Figure 3
:
The ratio of blue and green mass within
X_{j}=0
and
X_{j}=1
is preserved when it gets distributed between
X_{k}=0
and
X_{k}=1
. The picture above only shows this for the (purple) mass in state
X_{j}=1
. However, it is also true for the mass within state
X_{j}=0
that would occupy the leftover white regions in
X_{k}
.

The proof is aided by the pictoral definition of conditional Bernoulli randomness in terms of “boxed” mass that we stated above. In
Figure

3
, blue mass corresponds to
X_{i}=0
and green mass corresponds to
X_{i}=1
. The total boxed mass in
X_{j}
corresponds to
\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{i}|X_{j})
. Observe that because of the Markov property,
\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}|X_{j},X_{k}]=\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}|X_{j}]
. Namely, the mass in the bucket
X_{j}=1
must get distributed between the buckets
X_{k}=0
and
X_{k}=1
in a such a way that the blue-to-green ratio in both these buckets is equal to the ratio in
X_{j}=1
. Put another way, some fraction of the total mass in
X_{j}=1
goes to the bucket
X_{k}=0
and the remaining fraction goes to the bucket

X_{j}=0

X_{j}

X_{j}
remains boxed in

X_{k}
is at least as much as the boxed mass in

\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{i}|X_{k})\geq\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{i}|X_{j})
.
∎

5.2
General Tree Ising Models Reduce to Lipschitz Cap Tree Metrics

In this section, we will transform the task of embedding general tree Ising models into
\ell_{1}
to the task of embedding Lipschitz cap tree metrics into
\ell_{1}
. In what follows, we will reason about
\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]
for nodes
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
in the tree Ising model. For this, we will imagine the tree to be rooted at
X_{i}
, with all edges pointing away from the root. This ensures that all the edges on the path from
X_{i}
to
X_{j}
are in the same direction, which will allow us to use convenient independence properties to reason about the evolution of conditional Bernoulli randomness as we traverse the path. Furthermore, this rooting assumption is without loss of generality, by the equivalence of tree Ising models and tree-structured Bayesian networks, as mentioned in
Section

3.2
.

For a node
X_{i}
in the tree Ising model, let us define its bias
b(i)=\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=0],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=1])
. The following inequality upper-bounds the absolute difference in the biases of
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
in terms of Bernoulli randomness and the probability that they disagree.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
b(i)=\min(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=0],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=1])
.

(a)

b(i)-b(j)\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{i}|X_{j}).

(b)

|b(i)-b(j)|\leq\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}].

Proof.

Suppose
\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=0],\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{j}=0]\geq\frac{1}{2}
without loss of generality. Then,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{i}|X_{j})

\displaystyle\geq 2\cdot\min_{x}\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=x,X_{j}=0]=2\cdot\left(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{j}=0]-\max_{x}\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=x,X_{j}=0]\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2\cdot\left(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{j}=0]-\max_{x}\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=x]\right)=2\cdot\left((1-b(j))-(1-b(i))\right)=2\cdot\left(b(i)-b(j)\right)

Repeating the calculation above with
\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{j}|X_{i})
yields
\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{j}|X_{i})\geq 2\cdot\left(b(j)-b(i)\right)
. Thus, we get

\displaystyle|b(i)-b(j)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\max\left(\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{i},X_{j}),\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{j}|X_{i})\right)\leq\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}]

where we used
Lemma

5.1
in the last step.
∎

Now, let us define a strange but necessary quantity, that captures a “surprise” event when we traverse an edge.

Definition 3

(Cross)
.

We define an edge

as being a “cross” if
\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{u}=0,X_{v}=1]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{u}=1,X_{v}=0]>\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{u}=0,X_{v}=0]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{u}=1,X_{v}=1]

\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{u}=0,X_{v}=1]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{u}=1,X_{v}=0]>\frac{1}{2}
.

We will show how distance can be related to three sources and how we can embed these sources well.

Lemma 5.5
.

Each of the following quantities is upper bounded by

(a)

Difference in marginals:

(b)

Bernoulli randomness:

.

(c)

Negative correlation:
d_{\mathrm{negcor}}(i,j)=\begin{cases}\max(b(i),b(j))&\text{if number of crosses between $i$ and $j$ is odd.}\\
|b(i)-b(j)|&\text{if number of crosses between $i$ and $j$ is even.}\end{cases}

Proof.

For part (a), observe that

\displaystyle|\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=0]-\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{j}=0]|

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=0,X_{j}=1]-\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=1,X_{j}=0]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=0,X_{j}=1]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}=1,X_{j}=0]=\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}].

Part (b) follows from
Lemma

5.1
above.
Part (c) is relatively most involved. Note that the “even crosses” case follows from
Lemma

5.4
. For the “odd crosses” case, we have

Claim 5.6
.

If there are an odd number of crosses between

\min(b(i),b(j))=O(\operatorname{{Pr}}[X_{i}\neq X_{j}])

Proof.

Case 1:

Case 3:

\operatorname{{Br}}(X_{i}|X_{j})<2\cdot b(i),\frac{1}{2}
.

5.3.2
Lipschitz Cap Tree Metrics

We will now consider how to obtain the analogous result for Lipschitz cap tree metrics. It is tempting to try and combine our techniques from the fixed cap tree metric and our Lipschitz cap line metric. Recall how our main technique for the fixed cap tree metric was to obtain a modified “snipped” version of the tree’s caterpillar decomposition and to reduce (by hashing) the embedding problem to a collection of fixed cap line metric problems. However, we cannot reduce the Lipschitz cap tree metric to a collection of Lipschitz cap line metrics in an obvious manner. Crucially, a direct modification of the prior approach would not work for Lipschitz cap tree metrics, primarily because segments that are hashed to a line metric instance are not contiguous segments of the tree, and thus
the caps on these segments need no longer satisfy the necessary Lipschitz condition.
Consequently, we must design a new algorithm for this task.

Algorithm Intuition.
First, we observe that the primary issue in extending our prior techniques was how tree edges hashed to the same line metrics potentially have very different caps. If we view the algorithm hierarchically (considering how the embedding evolves as we progress downwards in the tree), it would be desirable to “clean”/zero out the embedding somehow. If we clean the embedding frequently enough, we may expect that the only edges affecting some
embedding[x_{i}]
would be parts of the tree very close to
x_{i}
, and thus by the Lipschitz property, all the relevant edges would have roughly the same cap. Ultimately, we obtain an algorithm that is less directly similar to the previous approaches, but is conducive towards this notion of periodic cleaning.

Build-Clean-Tree Algorithm.
We now define our “Build-Clean-Tree” algorithm.

Tree decomposition
. We will again consider a modified caterpillar decomposition. Abusing notation slightly, we will refer to the caterpillars/segments given by the caterpillar decomposition as edges themselves. For any edge
e
in the tree decomposition, we will denote
s(e)
to be the location of the “start” (or top) of the edge,
\ell(e)
the length of the edge, and
a(e)
will store an auxiliary value for the edge we will later define. After obtaining the original caterpillar decomposition, we will split up any overly long edges. More concretely, we chop off any edge
e
having length larger than
\frac{M(s(e))}{100\log(n)}
at
\frac{M(s(e))}{100\log(n)}
, and then recursively continue chopping the remaining part of the edge.
6
6

6

The most natural manner of doing this splitting faces a nuanced issue that if one of the caps at the endpoints is zero and the other is nonzero, then the preprocessing would not terminate as it splits the edge into infinitely many edges. This is remedied by separately handling the case where all caps are strictly positive in
Lemma

5.20
, and then reducing the general problem to this special case in
Lemma

5.24
.
This process ensures that the length of every (potentially chopped) edge
e
is at most
\frac{M(s(e))}{100\log(n)}
.
From this decomposition, we will create the embedding.
All embeddings will be of length
|H|=8\log(n)
. Consider building the embedding from the top to bottom, in a way such that we will only process an edge

Building
. One stage of our algorithm is

. If we process an edge

•

If

Note how this process is Lipschitz in how the embedding changes while moving along the tree, and is designed in a way such that we are trying to keep coordinates of the embedding to be at most

by leveraging properties of our tree decomposition that limits the sizes of edges.

Cleaning
. The other stage of our algorithm is

. We will design our stages such that build stages process

Case 2:

In this case,

. First, by

, we have that with constant probability over the build-clean process,

for some constant

that have magnitude smaller than

. Recall that by the properties of the build-clean algorithm (

must lie in this set of at most

, there is at least
\frac{49cM}{50}
distance left, and this must be contributed to by the set of “full” coordinates that is exactly
\frac{M}{100d}

\frac{cM}{100d}

.

Thus, under the conditioned event, we know that

has a set of

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[|sloc_{p}[i]-sloc_{p}[j]|\right]

Discussion and Open Problems

7.1
Embedding Probability Distributions

Our results show that
\ell_{1}
metrics that can be derived by Tree Ising Models allow for low-dimensional embeddings into
\ell_{1}
with constant distortion. Recall that Tree Ising Models are the class of undirected graphical models on trees, or equivalently, the class of Bayesian networks where the underlying graph is a directed tree with all edges pointing away from a designated root. It is natural to wonder if we can obtain low-dimensional
\ell_{1}
embeddings for more general classes of Bayesian networks. Tree-structured Bayesian networks are especially convenient because they allow for an efficient top-down sampling procedure. For general Bayesian networks that are not trees, the
treewidth
of the underlying graph is a relevant quantity that is known to characterize the difficulty of sampling from the model
[
PCdG+19
]
. The treewidth of a graph measures how “tree-like” a graph is, and given that sampling from tree-structured Bayesian networks is easy, it seems natural that the difficulty of sampling from general graphical models scales with its treewidth.

Because trees have treewidth 1, our result can be stated as the following—of the class of Bayesian networks on
N
variables whose underlying undirected graph has treewidth 1, the sub-class that has the special property that all the directed edges point away from the root allow for embedding into
\ell_{1}
with
\mathrm{polylog}(N)
dimensions and constant distortion. However, the question about general Bayesian networks with treewidth 1 still remains open. This class consists of directed trees where a single node can have multiple parents (also known as polytrees)
[
Das13
,
CV22
,
CYBC23
]
, and is known to be structurally very different from trees where all edges point away from a single root. In particular, our techniques don’t readily generalize to this class, because of the more complicated nature of dependencies in these models.

B.1
Coordinate-wise Embeddings are Good

In this section, we will essentially reuse the analysis of the build-clean procedure from

in order to prove

Proof.

The proof is constructive. Without loss of generality, consider say the first coordinate of each of the
n
points. We overload notation here and identify this coordinate of each point with
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. If we map these coordinates to the line, we obtain a line graph metric. Our goal is to embed this line metric with a fixed cap
M

Section

5.3.2
. We will root the line at the leftmost point on the line (say this is
r

at a distance
2M

\frac{M}{100d}

|H|

Section

5.3.2

Since the cap at every location is equal to

5.15
and
5.16

Next,
5.17

B.2
Concatenations of Coordinate-wise Embeddings are Good

First, we state a reverse application of Markov’s inequality:

Proposition B.1

(Reverse Markov)
.

Let
F
be a random variable such that
F\in[0,M]
with probability 1, and
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[F]\geq c_{1}M
, for
c_{1}\leq 1
. Then, for any
C_{2}\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[F\geq\frac{c_{1}}{C_{2}}\cdot M\right]\geq 1-\frac{1-c_{1}}{1-\frac{c_{1}}{C_{2}}}.

Proof.

Define
G=M-F
, so that
G\in[0,M]
. Then
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[G]=M-\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[F]\leq M-c_{1}M
. Applying Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[F<\frac{c_{1}}{C_{2}}\cdot M\right]=\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[G>\left(1-\frac{c_{1}}{C_{2}}\right)\cdot M\right]\leq\frac{1-c_{1}}{1-\frac{c_{1}}{C_{2}}}

\displaystyle\implies\qquad

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[F\geq\frac{c_{1}}{C_{2}}\cdot M\right]\geq 1-\frac{1-c_{1}}{1-\frac{c_{1}}{C_{2}}}.

∎

We can now use
Proposition

B.1
to prove the following general result which we can directly use to prove that the
z_{i}
vectors constructed in
Section

6
capture the capped distance well.

Lemma B.2
.

Suppose
R_{1},\dots,R_{d}
are independent random variables such that each
R_{i}\in[0,M]
with probability 1, and
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i}]\geq c\cdot M
for a constant
c\leq 1
. Then,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i}\geq\frac{c^{2}}{100}\cdot M\right]\geq\Omega(1).

Proof.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that

\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i}\geq\frac{c^{2}}{100}\cdot M\right]<\min\left(\frac{1}{100},1-\frac{1-\left(\frac{99c}{100}\right)^{2}}{1-\frac{c^{2}}{100}}\right).

In particular, since the
R_{i}
s are non-negative, this implies that

\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{j}R_{i}\geq\frac{c^{2}}{100}\cdot M\right]<\min\left(\frac{1}{100},1-\frac{1-\left(\frac{99c}{100}\right)^{2}}{1-\frac{c^{2}}{100}}\right)\quad\text{for all $j\in\{1,\dots,d\}$}.

(81)

We will reason about the random variable

\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i},cM\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\min\left[R_{i},\max\left(0,cM-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}R_{j}\right)\right].

Let
A_{i}
be the event that
\sum_{j=1}^{i}R_{j}<\frac{c}{100}\cdot M
and
B_{i}
be the event that
\sum_{j=1}^{i}R_{j}<\frac{c^{2}}{100}\cdot M
. Further, let
\mathds{1}[A_{i}]
and
\mathds{1}[B_{i}]
be their indicators. Note that
\operatorname{{Pr}}[B_{i}]\leq\operatorname{{Pr}}[A_{i}]
, since
c\leq 1
. Then, observe that

\displaystyle\min\left[R_{i},\max\left(0,cM-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}R_{j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{1}[A_{i-1}]\cdot\min\left(R_{i},\frac{99c}{100}\cdot M\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{1}[A_{i-1}]\cdot\frac{99c}{100}\cdot R_{i}.

Here, the last inequality follows because
\frac{99c}{100}\cdot R_{i}\leq\frac{99c}{100}\cdot M
as well as
\frac{99c}{100}\cdot R_{i}\leq R_{i}
, since
c\leq 1
. Thus, we obtain that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i},cM\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\min\left[R_{i},\max\left(0,cM-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}R_{j}\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathds{1}[A_{i-1}]\cdot\frac{99c}{100}\cdot R_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\mathds{1}[A_{i-1}]\cdot\frac{99c}{100}\cdot R_{i}\right]\qquad(\text{linearity of expectation})

\displaystyle=\frac{99c}{100}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{d}\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\mathds{1}[A_{i-1}]\right]\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[R_{i}\right]\qquad\text{(independence of $R_{i}$s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{99c}{100}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{d}\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[A_{i-1}\right]\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[R_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{99c}{100}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{d}\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[B_{i-1}\right]\cdot\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[R_{i}\right]\qquad\left(\text{since $\operatorname{{Pr}}[B_{i-1}]\leq\operatorname{{Pr}}[A_{i-1}]$}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{99c}{100}\cdot\frac{99}{100}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{d}\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[R_{i}\right]\qquad\text{(\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eqn:reverse-markovish-contradiction})}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{99c}{100}\right)^{2}M.

Now, we invoke
Proposition

B.1
with
F=\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i},cM\right)
. Note that
F\in[0,M]
with probability 1, and
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[F]\geq\left(\frac{99c}{100}\right)^{2}\cdot M
— thus, for
C_{2}=100\cdot\left(\frac{99}{100}\right)^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i},cM\right)\geq\frac{c^{2}}{100}\cdot M\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1-\left(\frac{99c}{100}\right)^{2}}{1-\frac{c^{2}}{100}}.

Notice that we have ended up showing

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i}\geq\frac{c^{2}}{100}\cdot M\right]\geq\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i},cM\right)\geq\frac{c^{2}}{100}\cdot M\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1-\left(\frac{99c}{100}\right)^{2}}{1-\frac{c^{2}}{100}},

which contradicts
Equation

81
. Hence, it must be true that

\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i}\geq\frac{c^{2}}{100}\cdot M\right]\geq\min\left(\frac{1}{100},1-\frac{1-\left(\frac{99c}{100}\right)^{2}}{1-\frac{c^{2}}{100}}\right)=\Omega(1).

∎

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|_{1}\geq\Omega(1)\cdot\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{1}\right]\geq\Omega(1).

Case 2:
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{1}>M.

In this case,
d^{{M}}_{\mathrm{cap}}(x_{i},x_{j})=M
. Observe first that for any
q\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, by part 1 of
Lemma

6.1
,
R_{q}\in[0,M]
with probability 1. Next, observe also that
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\sum_{q=1}^{d}R_{q}\right]\geq\Omega(1)\cdot M
. To see this, consider two cases. In one case, there exists a coordinate
q
such that
|x_{i}[q]-x_{j}[q]|>M
. In this case, part (2) of
Lemma

6.1
already promises us that for this
q
,
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}[R_{q}]\geq\Omega(1)\cdot M
, and since the
R_{q}
’s are non-negative, we have the same lower bound on
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\sum_{q=1}^{d}R_{q}\right]
. In the remaining case,
|x_{i}[q]-x_{j}[q]|\leq M
for all
q
. In this case, we can still apply part (2) of
Lemma

6.1
, to say that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\sum_{q=1}^{d}R_{q}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{d}\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[R_{q}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{q=1}^{d}\Omega(1)\cdot|x_{i}[q]-x_{j}[q]|

\displaystyle=\Omega(1)\cdot\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\Omega(1)\cdot M,

where the last inequality follows because
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{1}>M
in the present case. Thus, we have shown that every
R_{q}
is in
[0,M]
, and
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}\left[\sum_{q=1}^{d}R_{q}\right]\geq\Omega(1)\cdot M
. Instantiating
Lemma

B.2
gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\sum_{q=1}^{d}R_{q}\geq\Omega(1)\cdot M\right]\geq\Omega(1)

\displaystyle\implies\qquad

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|_{1}\geq\Omega(1)\cdot M\right]\geq\Omega(1).

∎
