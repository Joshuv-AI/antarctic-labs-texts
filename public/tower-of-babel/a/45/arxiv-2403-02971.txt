Space Complexity of Euclidean Clustering
Xiaoyi Zhu, Yuxiang Tian, Lingxiao Huang, Zengfeng Huang

Abstract
The ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering problem in Euclidean space ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} has been extensively studied. Given the scale of data involved, compression methods for the Euclidean ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering problem, such as data compression and dimension reduction, have received significant attention in the literature. However, the space complexity of the clustering problem, specifically, the number of bits required to compress the cost function within a multiplicative error ε \varepsilon , remains unclear in existing literature. This paper initiates the study of space complexity for Euclidean ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering and offers both upper and lower bounds. Our space bounds are nearly tight when k k is constant, indicating that storing a coreset, a well-known data compression approach, serves as the optimal compression scheme. Furthermore, our lower bound result for ( k , z ) (k,z) -Clustering establishes a tight space bound of Θ ⁡ ( n ​ d ) \Theta(nd) for terminal embedding, where n n represents the dataset size. Our technical approach leverages new geometric insights for principal angles and discrepancy methods, which may hold independent interest.

Recent research has focused on developing efficient coresets

,

On the other hand, dimension reduction methods have also proven to be effective for

-Clustering

Specifically, terminal embedding (

While the importance of compression for clustering has been widely acknowledged, the literature currently lacks clarity regarding the space complexity of the clustering problem itself.
Specifically, one may want to know how many bits are required to compress the cost function.
Space complexity, a fundamental factor in theoretical computer science, serves as a measure of the complexity of the cost function.
Previous research has investigated the space complexity for various other problems, including approximate nearest neighbor
[
34
]
, inner products
[
2
]

[
35
]

[

To investigate the space complexity of the
(k,z)
-Clustering

\tilde{O}(|\bm{S}|\cdot d)
using standard quantization methods (see Theorem
1.2

\bm{S}

One might wonder if it is possible to combine the benefits of coreset construction and dimension reduction to eliminate the dependence on the dimension

This leads to a natural question: “Is it possible to obtain an

(k,z)
-Clustering

Perhaps surprisingly, we show that

is necessary for interesting parameter regimes (see

1.3
in the paper).
This means a quantized coreset is optimal, and dimensionality reduction does not help with space complexity.
The proof of the lower bound for space complexity is our main contribution, which encounters more technical challenges.
Unlike upper bounds, existing lower bounds for coresets do not directly translate into lower bounds for space complexity since compression approaches can go beyond simply storing a subset of points as a coreset.
Overall, the study of space complexity is intricately connected to the optimality of coresets and poses technical difficulties.

1.2
Technical Overview

We now describe the high-level technical ideas behind our main contribution Theorem
Theorem

1.3
.
In general, our approach involves using a clever counting argument to establish lower bounds on space.
We do this by creating a large family of datasets
\mathcal{P}
where, for any pair
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
from this family, there exists a center set
\bm{C}
that separates their cost function by a significant margin, denoted as
\mathrm{cost}_{z}\left(\bm{P},\bm{C}\right)\notin\left(1\pm O(\varepsilon)\right)\mathrm{cost}_{z}\left(\bm{Q},\bm{C}\right)
.
This difference in cost implies that
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
can not share the same sketch, which leads to a lower bound on space of
\log\left(\left|\mathcal{P}\right|\right)
(Lemma
4.1
).
Hence, we focus on how to construct such a family
\mathcal{P}
.

We discuss the most technical bound, which is
\Omega\left(kd\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},\frac{n}{k}\right\}\right)
, when
n>k\geq 2
and
\Delta=\Omega\left(\frac{k^{\frac{1}{d}}\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right)
.
The proofs for other bounds are pretty standard.
For brevity, we will explain the technical idea for the case of
z=k=2
(
2-Means
).
The extension to general
z
and
k
is straightforward, by analyzing Taylor expansions for
(1+x)^{z}
(Section
4.4
) and make
\Omega(k)
copies of datasets in
\mathcal{P}
(Section
4.5
).
Our construction of
\mathcal{P}
relies on a fundamental geometric concept known as
principal angles
(Definition
2.3
).
The Cosine of these angles, when given the orthonormal bases
\bm{P}=\left\{\bm{p}_{i}:i\in[n]\right\}
and
\bm{Q}=\left\{\bm{q}_{i}:i\in[n]\right\}
of two distinct subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, uniquely correspond to the singular values of
\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}
(Lemma
2.4
).
This correspondence essentially measures how orthogonal the two subspaces are to each other.
With principal angles in mind, we outline the two main components of our proof.
Assuming that
d>n
, the first component (Lemma
4.2
) demonstrates that if the largest
O(n)
principal angles between two orthonormal bases
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
are sufficiently large, there exists a center set
\bm{C}=\left\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\right\}\in\mathcal{C}
with
\|\bm{c}\|_{2}=1
such that
\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})\geq\Omega\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
.
This induced error of
\Omega\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
from
C
achieves the desired scale of
\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\bm{C})=O(\varepsilon n)
when
n=O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
.
The second component (Lemma
4.3
) states that when
n=O\left(\frac{d}{\log d}\right)
, there exists a large family
\mathcal{P}
of orthonormal bases (for different
n
-dimensional subspaces) with size
\exp\left(nd\right)
such that most principal angles of any two different orthonormal bases in the family are sufficiently large.
The space lower bound
\Omega\left(d\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},n\right\}\right)
directly follows from these two lemmas.

Next, we delve into the technical insights behind Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
.

Lemma

4.2
: Reduction from principal angles to cost difference.

Recall that we aim to show the existence of a center set
\bm{C}=\left\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\right\}
that incurs a large cost difference between two orthonormal bases
\bm{P}=\left\{\bm{p}_{i}:i\in[n]\right\}
and
\bm{Q}=\left\{\bm{q}_{i}:i\in[n]\right\}
.
By the formulation of
\bm{C}
, we note that
\mathrm{cost}_{2}(\bm{P},\bm{C})-\mathrm{cost}_{2}(\bm{Q},\bm{C})=2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|-\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)
.
Hence, we focus on showing the existence of a unit vector
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|-\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\geq\Omega(\sqrt{n}).

(2)

Intuitively, our goal is to increase the magnitude of the first term
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|
while decreasing the magnitude of the second term
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|
.
One initial approach is to choose
\bm{c}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i\in[n]}\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{q}_{i}
, where
\bm{\zeta}\in\left\{-1,+1\right\}^{n}
.
By this selection, center
\bm{c}
lies on the subspace spanned by
\bm{Q}
and maximizes the first term
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|
to be
\sqrt{n}
.

Moreover, the second term becomes
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{n}\|\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}\|_{\infty}
and we want to minimize it.
This objective is very similar to the goal of coloring.
Informally speaking, the goal of coloring is to find a vector
\bm{\zeta}\in\left\{-1,+1\right\}^{n}
for a given matrix
\bm{U}
that minimizes
\|\bm{U}\bm{\zeta}\|_{\infty}
(See Definition
2.2
).

Ideally, if we can find a coloring
\bm{\zeta}\in\left\{-1,+1\right\}^{n}
such that
\|\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}\|_{\infty}\leq 0.5
, we can achieve the desired cost difference in Inequality (
2
).
However, the existence of such
\bm{\zeta}
appears to be non-trivial.
For instance, if we randomly select a coloring
\bm{\zeta}
from
\left\{-1,+1\right\}^{n}
, the expected value of
\|\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}\|_{\infty}
can be as large as
O(\log n)

[
51
]
.

On the other hand, directly applying proofs from discrepancy literature (e.g.,
[
51
,
19
]
) does not achieve the desired property
\|\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}\|_{\infty}\leq 0.5
.
This is because existing techniques work for arbitrary matrices
U
instead of the specific matrix
P^{\top}Q
that may have additional geometric properties, and hence, only yield unsatisfactory results.

To bypass this technical difficulty, we enhance the previous idea by allowing
\bm{\zeta}\in\left\{-1,0,+1\right\}^{n}
to be a partial coloring with
\|\bm{\zeta}\|_{1}\geq 0.75n
, which means
\bm{\zeta}
can now have at most 25% entries that are zero.
With this modification, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|=0.75\sqrt{n}
.
Thus, it still suffices to bound
\|\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}\|_{\infty}\leq 0.5
such that Inequality (
2
) holds.
Such a stricter bound calls for new ideas.

Our core objective is to find the conditions on
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
that allow us to identify such a partial coloring.
Let’s consider two simple examples to illustrate the idea.
When
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
are identical, we would have
\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}
is the identity matrix.
For any coloring vector
\bm{\zeta}
with
\|\bm{\zeta}\|_{0}>0
, we must have at least one entry of
|\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{Q}\zeta|
is 1 and thus
\|\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}\|_{\infty}=1
.
When
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
are orthogonal, we would have
\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}
is the zero matrix.
For any coloring vector
\bm{\zeta}
, we must have
\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{Q}\zeta=\bm{0}
and thus
\|\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}\|_{\infty}=0
.
This suggests that the greater the difference between P and Q, the easier it is to find a partial coloring that meets our requirements.
We will show that such differences can be characterized using principal angles.

After closely examining the value of the partial coloring, we find that this value is closely related to the norm of each row
\|(\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q})_{i}\|_{2}
.
Using random coloring as an example, applying the Chernoff bound, we would find that the magnitude of each corresponding value for a row is bounded by the norm of that row, i.e.
\|(\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta})_{i}\|_{2}\leq a\|(\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q})_{i}\|_{2}
for some constant
a
.
Therefore, to achieve a smaller partial coloring, we require the row norms of
\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}
to be relatively small.
Since
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
are two orthonormal bases, the row norms of
\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}
correspond to the length of the projection of
\bm{p}_{i}
to the subspace of
\bm{Q}
.
For example, when
\bm{p}_{i}
lies in the subspace of
\bm{Q}
, we have
\|(\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q})_{i}\|_{2}=1
.
On the other side, when
\bm{p}_{i}
is orthogonal to the subspace of
\bm{Q}
, we have
\|(\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q})_{i}\|_{2}=0
.
Our aim is to find
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
such that the length of the projection of each data point
\bm{p}_{i}
to the subspace of
\bm{Q}
is relatively small.

Note that in the simplified two-dimensional space cases where
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
are reduced to a single data point, a small projection length from
\bm{P}
to
\bm{Q}
is equivalent to having a large angle between
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
.
Based on this idea, we find a similar pattern for high-dimensional subspaces with the help of the notion “principal angles”.
The formal definition of principal angles can be found in Definition
2.3
.
Intuitively, principal angles are a set of minimized angles between the two subspaces.
Small principal angles indicate that the two subspaces are nearly parallel in many directions, and the length of projection to these directions would be high.
For example, when
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
are identical, the principal angles between them are all 0.

\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}
equals the identity matrix and the row norms of it are all 1.
On the other hand, large principal angles imply that
the two subspaces span many directions that are nearly orthogonal to each other.
For example, when
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
are orthogonal, the principal angles between them are all
\frac{\pi}{2}
.

\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}
equals the zero matrix and the row norms of it are all 0.

Therefore, large principal angles imply that the majority of the row norms
\|(\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q})_{i}\|_{2}
are small (Lemma
4.5
).
These small row sums enable us to find a partial coloring
\bm{\zeta}
that further reduces the bound for
\|\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}\|_{\infty}
to 0.5 (Lemma
4.6
), employing similar approaches as in
[
51
]
.
In summary, we have completed the proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Lemma

4.3
: Construction of
\mathcal{P}
.

Our construction is inspired by a geometric observation made in Absil et al.
[
1
]
, which states that the largest principal angle between the orthonormal bases
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
of two
n
-dimensional subspaces, independently drawn from the uniform distribution on the Grassmann manifold of
n
-planes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is at least
\Omega(1)
with high probability.
We extend this result and prove that even the largest
O(n)
principal angles between
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
are at least
\Omega(1)
(Lemma
4.8
).
This extension relies on a more careful integral calculation for the density function of principal angles.
Moreover, this extension leads to an enhanced geometric observation: on average, these two orthonormal bases
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
are distinct with respect to principal angles, which could be of independent research interest.
Then using standard probabilistic arguments, we can randomly select a family
\mathcal{P}
of
\exp(\Omega(nd))
orthonormal bases, ensuring that the largest
O(n)
principal angles between any pair
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
from
\mathcal{P}
are consistently large.

The proof of space lower bound in Section
4
relies on two key concepts.
The first one is partial coloring, which is commonly found in the discrepancy literature.

Definition 2.2

(Partial Coloring)
.

Let
\bm{U}
be a matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. The goal of a partial coloring is to find a vector
{\bm{\zeta}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n}
such that

1.

The number of zero entries
|i:{\bm{\zeta}}_{i}=0|\leq\frac{1}{4}n
;

2.

The discrepancy, i.e. maximum norm
\left\|\bm{U}{\bm{\zeta}}\right\|_{\infty}
is as small as possible.

The second concept is called the principal angles, which characterize the relative positions of two subspaces.

Definition 2.3

(Principal angles)
.

Suppose
n\leq d
.
Given two
n
-dimensional subspaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists then a sequence of angles called the principal angles (or canonical angles)
0\leq\theta_{1}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}),\cdots,\theta_{n}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
. The first one is defined as

\displaystyle\theta_{1}\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\right):=\min

\displaystyle\left\{\arccos\left(|\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{y}|\right)\mid\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y},\left\|\bm{x}\right\|_{2}=1,\left\|\bm{y}\right\|_{2}=1\right\}=\angle\left(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{y}_{1}\right),

where the vectors
\bm{x}_{1}
and
\bm{y}_{1}
are the corresponding principal vectors. The other principal angles and vectors are then defined recursively via

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):=\min

\displaystyle\left\{\arccos\left(\left.|\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{y}|\right)\right\rvert\,\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y},\left\|\bm{x}\right\|_{2}=1,\left\|\bm{y}\right\|_{2}=1,\bm{x}\perp\bm{x}_{j},\bm{y}\perp\bm{y}_{j},\right.

\displaystyle\left.\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,i-1\}\right\}.

Slightly abusing the notation, we use
\theta_{i}
for brevity when the corresponding two subspaces are clear from the context.
The notion of principal angles between subspaces was first introduced by Jordan
[
37
]
and has many important applications in statistics and numerical analysis
[
20
,
52
]
.
Intuitively, we can see that the principal vectors in each subspace form an orthonormal basis and the principal angles
(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{k})
are a set of minimized angles between the two subspaces.
Small principal angles indicate that the two subspaces are nearly parallel in many directions, while large principal angles imply that
the two subspaces are more distinct and span many directions that are nearly orthogonal to each other. For example, when
\mathcal{X}\perp\mathcal{Y}
, all principal angles
\theta_{i}=\frac{\pi}{2}
.

As another example, we consider two distinct planes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(i.e., two-dimensional subspaces) intersect along a line shown in Figure
1
. By the definition, we will choose
\bm{x}_{1}=\bm{y}_{1}
on the intersection line and thus
\theta_{1}=0
. We then have
\bm{x}_{2}
and
\bm{y}_{2}
as the orthogonal directions to the intersection line on each of the two planes respectively. The angle between them is the second principal angle
\theta_{2}=\theta
.

Given two

and

of

to be the singular values of the inner product matrix

.
We have

\sigma_{i}=\cos\left(\theta_{i}\right),\forall i\in[n].

Note that Lemma

holds for any orthonormal basis
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
of corresponding subspaces and the values of principal angles are independent of the choices of them.
For any orthogonal matrix

\sigma\left(\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{Y}\bm{B}\right)=\sigma\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{Y}\right)

Using Figure

\bm{X}=[\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}]

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}_{1}^{T}\\

\bm{x}_{2}^{T}\bm{y}_{1}&\bm{x}_{2}^{T}\bm{y}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(0)&0\\

\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{S}}\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})\in(1\pm 4\varepsilon)n
.

Proof.

Consider a center set
\bm{C}=\{\bm{c},\ldots,\bm{c}\}
such that for all
\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{2}\in\bm{P}
,

\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p}_{1},\bm{C})\in\left(\left(1\pm 0.2\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p}_{2},\bm{C})\right),

which could be obtained by choosing
\bm{C}
far away from
\bm{P}
. Then we have
\frac{\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{P}}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{C})}{\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{S}}\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{C})}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\left(1-0.2\varepsilon\right)n}{\left(1+0.2\varepsilon\right)\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{S}}\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})},\frac{\left(1+0.2\varepsilon\right)n}{\left(1-0.2\varepsilon\right)\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{S}}\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})}\right)\in\left(1\pm\varepsilon\right)\frac{n}{\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{S}}\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})}.

By the coreset definition, we have
\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\bm{C})}{\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{S}}\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{C})}=\frac{\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{P}}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{C})}{\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{S}}\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{C})}\in 1\pm\varepsilon
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{S}}\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle\in\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon},\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\right)n\in(1\pm 4\varepsilon)n.

∎

Now we are ready to prove the second part of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
(second part).

Correctness analysis.

We first show Algorithm
1
indeed outputs an
\varepsilon
-sketch of
\bm{P}
for
(k,z)
-Clustering
function.
We use
\mathcal{O}
to obtain
\widehat{\operatorname{w}}(\bm{p})=\operatorname{fraction}(\bm{p},\operatorname{w},\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{\operatorname{expo}(\bm{p},\operatorname{w})}
and
\widehat{\bm{p}}=\bm{p}_{0}+\bm{c}^{*}_{\bm{p}}
with
\bm{p}_{0}[i]=\operatorname{fraction}(\bm{p},i,\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{\operatorname{expo}(\bm{p},i)}
for
i\in[d]
.
Given a center set
\bm{C}\in\mathcal{C},
we approximate
(k,z)
-Clustering
function by the following value:

\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{S}}\widehat{\operatorname{w}}(\bm{p})\cdot\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\widehat{\bm{p}},\bm{C}).

We claim that for each
\bm{p}\in\bm{S}
,
\widehat{\operatorname{w}}(\bm{p})\in(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{4})\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})
when
\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})>\frac{\varepsilon}{4|\bm{S}|}.

This is because
\frac{\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})}{2}\leq 2^{\operatorname{expo}(\bm{p},\operatorname{w})}\leq\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})
and
|\operatorname{fraction}(\bm{p},\operatorname{w},\varepsilon)-\frac{\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})}{2^{\operatorname{expo}(\bm{p},\operatorname{w})}}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
, which implies that
\operatorname{fraction}(\bm{p},\operatorname{w},\varepsilon)\in(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{4})\frac{\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})}{2^{\operatorname{expo}(\bm{p},\operatorname{w})}}
and
\widehat{\operatorname{w}}(\bm{p})\in(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{4})\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})
.
When
\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4|\bm{S}|},
we have
\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{C})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4|\bm{S}|}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{C})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4|\bm{S}|}\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{P}}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{C})
, which means this quantity is too small to affect the
(k,z)
-Clustering
function and we could set all such
\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})
to zero.

Next, we analyze
\widehat{\bm{p}}
.
By Lemma
2.1
, for any
\bm{c}\in\bm{C},

|\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c})-\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\widehat{\bm{p}},\bm{c})|
is upper bounded by
\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c})+(1+\frac{4z}{\varepsilon})^{z-1}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\widehat{\bm{p}}).

By our construction,
\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p},\widehat{\bm{p}})=\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p}-\bm{c}^{*}_{\bm{p}},\bm{p}_{0})=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\bm{p}[i]-\bm{c}^{*}_{\bm{p}}[i]-\bm{p}_{0}[i])^{2}},
and as the same argument for weight,
\bm{p}[i]-\bm{c}^{*}_{\bm{p}}[i]-\bm{p}_{0}[i]\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4z}(\bm{p}[i]-\bm{c}^{*}_{\bm{p}}[i]),
thus
\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p},\widehat{\bm{p}})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4z}\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p},\bm{c}^{*}_{\bm{p}}).

Putting the above results together,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\widehat{\bm{p}},\bm{C})

\displaystyle\in\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c}_{\bm{p}})\pm\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c}_{\bm{p}})+\left(1+\frac{4z}{\varepsilon}\right)^{z-1}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\widehat{\bm{p}})\right)

\displaystyle\in\left(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c}_{\bm{p}})\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{4z}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4z}\right)^{z-1}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c}^{*}_{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle\in\left(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c}_{\bm{p}})\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{8}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c}^{*}_{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle\in\left(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c}_{\bm{p}}),

and thus we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{S}}\widehat{\operatorname{w}}(\bm{p})\cdot\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\widehat{\bm{p}},\bm{C})

\displaystyle\in\left(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\left(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\left(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{5}\right)\cdot\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{P}}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c}_{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\sum_{\bm{p}\in\bm{P}}\operatorname{dist}^{z}(\bm{p},\bm{c}_{\bm{p}}),

where the third line follows from
\ln(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{4z})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4z}
, the fourth line follows from
\bm{C}^{\star}
is a
2
-approximation of an optimal center set, and the penultimate line follows from the construction of coreset.
Therefore we construct an
\varepsilon
-sketch for
\bm{P}
.

Space complexity analysis.

We analyze its space complexity from now on.
The storage for
k
grid points
\bm{C}^{\star}
is
O(kd\log\Delta).

We store each weight by a set
(\operatorname{fraction}(\bm{p},\operatorname{w},\varepsilon),\operatorname{expo}(\bm{p},\operatorname{w}))
, where the first number is up to
O(\lceil\log 4/\varepsilon\rceil)
decimal places, and representing the integer number
\operatorname{expo}(\bm{p},\operatorname{w})=\lfloor\log\operatorname{w}(\bm{p})\rfloor
requires
O(\log\max\{\log\frac{4|\bm{S}|}{\varepsilon},\log n\})
bits by Lemma
3.1
.
Similarly, the storage for each
\bm{p}
is
O(d\log 4z/\varepsilon+d\log\log\Delta)
bits.
Combining them and notice that
|\bm{S}|\leq n,
we obtain the final bound

\displaystyle sc(P,\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq O\left(kd\log\Delta+|\bm{S}|\left(\log\frac{4}{\varepsilon}+\log\max\left\{\log\frac{4|\bm{S}|}{\varepsilon},\log n\right\}+d\log\frac{4z}{\varepsilon}+d\log\log\Delta\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(kd\log\Delta+\Psi(n)(d\log 1/\varepsilon d\log\log\Delta+\log\log n)\right),

where we ignore the dependence on
z.

∎

4
Proof of Theorem
1.3
: Space Lower Bounds

In this section, we prove the space lower bounds.
The high-level idea is to construct a large family of datasets such that any two of them can not use the same sketch; summarized by the following lemma.

The intuition behind this lemma is that: if two datasets
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
yield similar results under any clustering center set, our sketch does not need to allocate additional space to distinguish between them.
On the other hand, if they produce significantly different results under some clustering center set, our function must retain this information; otherwise, it would produce incorrect results on one of the datasets.

Lemma 4.1

(A family of datasets leads to space lower bounds)
.

Suppose there exists a family
\mathcal{P}
of datasets of size
n\geq 1
such that for any two datasets
\bm{P},\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
, there exists a center set
\bm{C}\in\mathcal{C}
with
\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\bm{C})\notin\left(1\pm 3\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\bm{C})
.
Then we have
\operatorname{sc}(n,\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon)\geq\Omega(\log\left|\mathcal{P}\right|)
.

Proof.

We prove this by contradiction.
Assume that
\operatorname{sc}(n,\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon)=o(\log\left|\mathcal{P}\right|)
, we must be able to find two datasets
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
such that they correspond to the same
\varepsilon
-sketch
\mathcal{O}
.
Since
\mathcal{O}
is an
\varepsilon
-sketch for both
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
, we have for every center set
\bm{C}\in\mathcal{C}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\bm{C})\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\bm{C}),~\mathcal{O}(\bm{C})\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\bm{C}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\bm{C})\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\mathcal{O}(\bm{C})\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\bm{C})\leq(1+3\varepsilon)\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\bm{C}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\bm{C})\geq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\mathcal{O}(\bm{C})\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\bm{C})\geq(1-3\varepsilon)\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\bm{C}).

This contradicts with our assumption that
\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\bm{C})\notin\left(1\pm 3\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\bm{C})
.
∎

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We first prove the lower bound
\Omega\left(nd\log\Delta\right)
when
n\leq k
.
In other words, we must store the entire dataset in this case.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
(first part).

We construct a family
\mathcal{P}
as follows: for each dataset
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, we
choose
n
different grid points in
[\Delta]^{d}
.
Note that for each single grid point, there are
\Delta^{d}
choices. Therefore, the size

\binom{\Delta^{d}}{n}
, which implies that
\log|\mathcal{P}|=\Omega(nd\log\Delta)

For any two datasets
\bm{P},\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{P}

\bm{q}
such that
\bm{q}\in\bm{Q}\setminus\bm{P}
.
Let

\bm{C}=\bm{P}\cup\{\underbrace{\bm{c},\cdots,\bm{c}}_{k-n\text{ points}}\}

, where
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an arbitrary point with
\bm{c}\neq\bm{q}
.
We have

\operatorname{cost}_{z}\left(\bm{Q},\bm{C}\right)\geq\operatorname{dist}\left(\bm{q},\bm{C}\right)^{z}>0,~\operatorname{cost}_{z}\left(\bm{P},\bm{C}\right)=0\notin\left(1\pm 3\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{cost}_{z}\left(\bm{Q},\bm{C}\right).

At the end of the proof, we will show how to round and scale these datasets

Applying

.

Combining Lemma

Proof of Theorem
1.3
(second part).

The lower bound of

Then by the first part of Theorem

Next, we prove the lower bound of
\Omega\left(kd\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},\frac{n}{k}\right\}\right)

For ease of analysis, we prove the case of

The extensions to general

We first ensure that our parameters meet the requirements of our previous lemmas.
Denote

\tilde{n}=\min\left\{\Theta\left(\frac{1}{484\varepsilon^{2}}\right),\Theta\left(\frac{d}{\log d}\right),n\right\}\geq 100,

where the first term
\Theta\left(\frac{1}{484\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

\Theta\left(\frac{d}{\log d}\right)

Since

Our proof proceeds as follows:
Lemma

shows that we can find a family
\mathcal{P}
of size
\exp\left(\frac{1}{256}10^{-6}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{32}10^{-6}}\right)\cdot\tilde{n}d\right)

\bm{P}
and

By our choice of

\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\tilde{n}}\geq 11\varepsilon\tilde{n}\geq 5\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{2}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})

4.1
. However, it is important to note that the family we have obtained so far is constructed in a continuous space. Therefore, we need to discretize this family and demonstrate that this process does not significantly affect the properties we need.

We then round and scale every dataset
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
to
[\Delta]^{d}

\Delta=\lceil\frac{10\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\rceil

The extra term

Without loss of generality, we may assume that

\Delta=\lceil\frac{10\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\rceil+1

Denote
\bm{1}=(1,\cdots,1).
For a dataset
\bm{P}=\left(\bm{p}_{1},\cdots,\bm{p}_{\tilde{n}}\right)\in\mathcal{P}

\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
to be our final family as follows: For each of dataset
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}

\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil\cdot\mathbf{1}

\frac{\Delta}{2}

\tilde{\bm{P}}=\left(\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\bm{p}_{1}\rceil,\cdots,\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\bm{p}_{\tilde{n}}\rceil\rceil\right)+\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil\cdot\mathbf{1}=\left(\tilde{\bm{p}}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\bm{p}}_{\tilde{n}}\right).
We will then show that this set fulfills the requirement of Lemma
4.1
. For ease of explanation, we also define
\hat{\bm{P}}
to be the dataset without rounding:
\hat{\bm{P}}=\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}\bm{p}_{1},\cdots,\frac{\Delta}{2}\bm{p}_{\tilde{n}}\right)+\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil\cdot\mathbf{1}=\left(\hat{\bm{p}}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\bm{p}}_{\tilde{n}}\right).
Moreover, let
\bar{\bm{c}}=\frac{\Delta}{2}\bm{c}+\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil\cdot\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\}\right)\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}\tilde{n}}{2},

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)-\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{Q}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}\left(\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})\right)\geq\frac{11\Delta^{2}\varepsilon\tilde{n}}{4}.

On the other hand, for the rounding dataset, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\hat{\bm{p}}_{i}-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\tilde{\bm{p}}_{i}-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\hat{\bm{p}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{p}}_{i}\right\|_{2}\left\|\hat{\bm{p}}_{i}-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\hat{\bm{p}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{p}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\Delta\sqrt{d}+d\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}\varepsilon}{4}.

The case for
-\bar{\bm{c}}
and other datasets is similar. Therefore, we have that for any dataset

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\hat{\bm{P}},\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\tilde{\bm{P}},\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\})\right|\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}\varepsilon\tilde{n}}{4},

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\tilde{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)\leq\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)+\frac{\Delta^{2}\varepsilon\tilde{n}}{4}\leq\frac{\Delta^{2}\tilde{n}}{2}+\frac{\Delta^{2}\varepsilon\tilde{n}}{4}\leq\frac{3\Delta^{2}\tilde{n}}{4}.

We now have a rounded family
\tilde{\mathcal{\bm{P}}}
such that all points are on the grid and we can find
\bar{\bm{c}}
that

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\tilde{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)-\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\tilde{\bm{Q}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)-\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{Q}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)-\frac{\Delta^{2}\varepsilon\tilde{n}}{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{9\Delta^{2}\varepsilon\tilde{n}}{4}.

Since the cost function value is upper bounded by
\frac{3\Delta^{2}\tilde{n}}{4}

\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\tilde{\bm{P}},\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\})\notin\left(1\pm 3\varepsilon\right)\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\tilde{\bm{Q}},\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\}).

Moreover, we can find origin being
\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil\cdot\mathbf{1}
such that all the center points and data points have distance to it less than
\frac{\Delta}{2}+\sqrt{d}\leq\Delta
.
By Lemma
4.1
, we have

\operatorname{sc}(n,\Delta,2,2,d,\varepsilon)\geq\Omega\left(\log\left|\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\right|\right)=\Omega\left(\log\left|\mathcal{P}\right|\right)\geq\Omega\left(d\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},n\right\}\right).

The extension to any constant

(1+x)^{z}
.
The extension to general

\Theta(k)
datasets from
\mathcal{P}
and set the positions of their center points in

“remote” from each other.

Finally, we prove the lower bound
\Omega\left(k\log\log\frac{n}{k}\right)
.
We again construct a large family
\mathcal{P}
of datasets.
For preparation, we find arbitrary
\frac{k}{2}
points, denoted as
\bm{p}_{1},\cdots,\bm{p}_{\frac{k}{2}}

This is available since

Every dataset
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
is constructed as follow: denote
\bm{e}_{1}=(1,0,\cdots,0)
for each

, we select

and put
2^{m_{i}}
points at
\bm{p}_{i}+\bm{e}_{1}
and
\frac{2n}{k}-2^{m_{i}}

\bm{p}_{i}
.
Therefore, the total number of possible datasets is
|\mathcal{P}|=\prod_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}=O\left(\left(\log\frac{n}{k}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}\right)
. We then consider for any two different datasets
\bm{P},\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{P}
, there must exist

\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
have different assignments for
\bm{p}_{l}
and
\bm{p}_{l}+\bm{e}_{1}
. Without loss of generality,assume that
\bm{P}
put
2^{i}
at
\bm{p}_{l}+\bm{e}_{1}
while
\bm{Q}
put
2^{j}
for
i<j
. Choosing center set

\bm{C}=\left\{\bm{p}_{1},\bm{p}_{1}+\bm{e}_{1},\cdots,\bm{p}_{l},\bm{p}_{l}+2\bm{e}_{1},\cdots,\bm{p}_{\frac{k}{2}},\bm{p}_{\frac{k}{2}}+\bm{e}_{1}\right\},

we must have that

\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\bm{C})=2^{i}\leq\frac{1}{2}2^{j}\notin\left(1\pm\frac{1}{2}\right)\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\bm{C}),

which satisfies our requirement of
\mathcal{P}
.
Lemma
4.1
provides us with a lower bound of
\Omega\left(\log\left|\mathcal{P}\right|\right)\geq\Omega\left(k\log\log\frac{n}{k}\right).

∎

4.2
Proof of Lemma
4.2
: Principal Angles to Cost Difference

In this section, we primarily prove Lemma
4.2
, restated as follows

Lemma 4.4

(Restatement of Lemma
4.2
)
.

Let
\bm{P}
,
\bm{Q}
be datasets of
n
orthonormal bases (
100\leq n\leq\frac{d}{2}
) satisfying that

\theta_{\frac{1}{32}10^{-6}\cdot n}\geq\arccos\left(\frac{10^{-3}}{4\sqrt{2}}\right).

There exists a unit vector
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n}.

Our proof strategy proceeds as follows: Let
\bm{P}=\left\{\bm{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
and
\bm{Q}=~\left\{\bm{q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
be two orthonormal bases and let their inner product matrix be

\bm{U}:=\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}=\left[\bm{U}_{1},\cdots,\bm{U}_{n}\right]^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{p}_{1}^{\top}\bm{q}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{p}_{1}^{\top}\bm{q}_{n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{p}_{n}^{\top}\bm{q}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{p}_{n}^{\top}\bm{q}_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\left(\bm{U}_{ij}\right)_{i,j\in[n]}.

Compute the cost function for any unit vector
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left\|\bm{p}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\bm{c}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)=2n-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\leq 2n.

The difference between the cost of the two datasets
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
is

\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})=2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|-\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right).

Our objective is to maximize this value as much as possible.
To maximize the first term, We choose
\bm{c}
to be a vector in the subspace spanned by
\bm{Q}
that
\bm{c}=\bm{Q}\bm{\zeta}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\zeta}_{i}\bm{q}_{i}
, thus our difference becomes:

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{P},\{{\bm{c}},-{\bm{c}}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{Q},\{{\bm{c}},-{\bm{c}}\})

\displaystyle=2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\bm{\zeta}_{i}\right|-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{\zeta}_{j}\bm{q}_{j}\rangle\right|\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\bm{\zeta}_{i}\right|-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{\zeta}_{j}\bm{U}_{ij}\right|\right).

The first term is maximized when most of the
|\bm{\zeta}_{i}|
are
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.
Additionally, we observe that minimizing the second term is very similar to the objective of partial coloring, which is formally defined in Definition
2.2
.
Partial coloring focuses on the infinity norm, whereas we are concerned with the 1-norm.
However, as long as this infinity norm is sufficiently small, our 1-norm can be controlled by it up to a factor of
n
.
Therefore, we consider employing techniques from this area to achieve this.
We note that if
\bm{U}
is an arbitrary matrix, it is challenging to minimize the second term effectively.
However, our
\bm{U}
is the inner product matrix of two subspaces with significantly different orientations (i.e., having many large principal angles), and its row norm is sufficiently small for us to bound the second term.

To this end, we first show that large principal angles imply that most rows of the inner product matrix
\bm{U}
have a small
\ell_{2}
-norm.

Lemma 4.5

(Principal angles to row norms)
.

Let
\bm{P}
,
\bm{Q}
be datasets of
n
orthonormal bases following the condition that

\theta_{\frac{1}{32}10^{-6}\cdot n}\geq\arccos\left(\frac{10^{-3}}{4\sqrt{2}}\right).

There exists a set
\mathcal{K}
of size larger than
\left(1-\frac{10^{-4}}{16}\right)n
and having property that the inner product matrix satisfies

\|\bm{U}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\bm{U}_{ij}\right)^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\leq 10^{-2},i\in\mathcal{K}\\
\leq 1,i\notin\mathcal{K}\end{array}\right..

Proof.

Since both of the datasets are composed of only orthonormal bases, we have

\forall i\in[n],\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\bm{U}_{ij}\right)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\bm{p}_{i}^{\top}\bm{q}_{j}\right)^{2}\leq\left\|\bm{p}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=1.

Therefore, we focus on the existence of set
\mathcal{K}
.
By the property of principal angles shown in Lemma
2.4
, we have that

\sigma_{i}=\cos\theta_{i},i=1,\cdots,n.

Moreover, we have the relation between singular values and Frobenius norm of the matrix being

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}=\left\|\bm{U}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\bm{U}_{ij}\right)^{2}.

On the other hand, the condition shows that

\displaystyle\theta_{n}\geq\cdots\geq\theta_{\frac{1}{32}10^{-6}\cdot n}\geq\arccos\left(\frac{10^{-3}}{4\sqrt{2}}\right),\sigma_{n}\leq\cdots\leq\sigma_{\frac{1}{32}10^{-6}\cdot n}\leq\cos\left(\arccos\left(\frac{10^{-3}}{4\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)=\frac{10^{-3}}{4\sqrt{2}}.

With the general upper bound that
\sigma_{i}=\cos\theta_{i}\leq 1
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{32}10^{-6}n+\left(1-\frac{1}{32}10^{-6}\right)n\cdot\left(\frac{10^{-3}}{4\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{16}10^{-6}n.

We would then have that the number of rows with sum of square larger than
10^{-2}
is less than
\frac{1}{16}10^{-6}n/10^{-2}=\frac{1}{16}10^{-4}n
, which completes the proof.
∎

Next, we show that a partial coloring can be found for the rows of
\bm{U}
using similar techniques as that of
[
51
]
.

Lemma 4.6

(Row norms to partial coloring)
.

Considering that we have a matrix
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
(with
n\geq 100
) such that we can find a set
\mathcal{K}
of size larger than
\left(1-\frac{10^{-4}}{16}\right)n
and having the property that

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{U}_{ij}^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\leq 10^{-2},i\in\mathcal{K}\\
\leq 1,i\notin\mathcal{K}\end{array}\right..

we can find a partial coloring
\{\bm{\zeta}_{i}\}_{[n]}\in\{-1,0,1\}_{[n]}
such that
\left|i:\bm{\zeta}_{i}=0\right|\leq\frac{1}{4}n
and
\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{\zeta}_{j}\bm{U}_{ij}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2},\forall i\in[n]
.

Proof.

Define
\operatorname{row}_{i}(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n}):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{\zeta}_{j}\bm{U}_{ij}
for all
i\in[n]
.
Define the rounding map

\operatorname{round}(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n})=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{n}),

where
b_{i}
is the nearest integer to
\operatorname{row}_{i}(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n})
.
That is,

b_{i}=0
if and only if
\left|\operatorname{row}_{i}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2},b_{i}=1
if and only if
\frac{1}{2}<\left|\operatorname{row}_{i}\right|\leq\frac{3}{2},b_{i}=-1
if and only if
-\frac{3}{2}\leq\left|\operatorname{row}_{i}\right|<-\frac{1}{2},
etc.
We then define a subset
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of the range that

\mathcal{B}=\{(b_{1},\cdots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:|\{i:|b_{i}|\geq s\}|<\alpha_{s}n,\text{for all positive integer }s\},
where

\alpha_{s}=\left[2\left(1-\frac{10^{-4}}{16}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{(2s-1)^{2}}{8\cdot 10^{-2}}\right)+2\cdot\frac{10^{-4}}{16}\exp\left(-\frac{(2s-1)^{2}}{8}\right)\right]2^{s+1}.

We firstly prove that
\left|\operatorname{round}^{-1}(\mathcal{B})\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}2^{n}.

To see this, let
\bm{\zeta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n}\in\{-1,+1\}
be independent and uniform and let
\operatorname{row}_{1},\cdots,\operatorname{row}_{n},b_{1},\cdots,b_{n}
be the values generated.
We shall note that the standard deviation of
\operatorname{row}_{i}
is the
l_{2}
-norm of
i
-th row.
Thus, classic Chernoff bound provides

\operatorname{Pr}\left[\left|b_{i}\right|\geq s\right]=\operatorname{Pr}\left[\left|\operatorname{row}_{i}\right|\geq\frac{2s-1}{2}\right]<\left\{\begin{matrix}2\exp\left(-\frac{(2s-1)^{2}}{8\cdot 10^{-2}}\right),i\in\mathcal{K}\\
2\exp\left(-\frac{(2s-1)^{2}}{8}\right),i\notin\mathcal{K}\end{matrix}\right..

As expectation is linear, we would have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\{i:\left|b_{i}\right|\geq s\right\}\right|\right]<n\left[2\left(1-\frac{10^{-4}}{16}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{(2s-1)^{2}}{8\cdot 10^{-2}}\right)+2\cdot\frac{10^{-4}}{16}\exp\left(-\frac{(2s-1)^{2}}{8}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\operatorname{Pr}\left[\left|\left\{i:\left|b_{i}\right|\geq s\right\}\right|\geq\alpha_{s}n\right]\leq\frac{1}{2^{s+1}},\operatorname{Pr}\left[\left(b_{1},\cdots,b_{n}\right)\notin\mathcal{B}\right]\leq\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{s+1}}=\frac{1}{2}.

That is, at least half of all
\left(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n}\right)\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
are in
\operatorname{round}^{-1}(\mathcal{B})
, yielding
\left|\operatorname{round}^{-1}(\mathcal{B})\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}2^{n}
.

We then consider the size of
\mathcal{B}
by crude counting arguments. We have

|\mathcal{B}|\leq\prod_{s=1}^{\infty}\left[\left[\sum_{l=0}^{\alpha_{s}n}{\binom{n}{l}}\right]2^{\alpha_{s}n}\right].

This is because
\left\{i:\left|b_{i}\right|=s\right\}
can be chosen in at most
\sum_{i=0}^{\alpha_{s}n}{\binom{n}{i}}
ways and, having been selected, can be split into
\left\{i:b_{i}=s\right\}
and
\left\{i:b_{i}=-s\right\}
in at most
2^{\alpha_{s}n}
ways.
We bound this value with

\sum_{l=0}^{\alpha n}{\binom{n}{l}}\leq 2^{n\cdot\operatorname{ent}(\alpha)},\operatorname{ent}(\alpha)=-\alpha\log_{2}\alpha-(1-\alpha)\log_{2}(1-\alpha),

where
\operatorname{ent}(\alpha)
is the entropy function. Therefore,
|\mathcal{B}|\leq 2^{hn},
where
h=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}[\operatorname{ent}(\alpha_{s})+\alpha_{s}].

In our case, we shall have that

\displaystyle h=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\left[\operatorname{ent}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)+\alpha_{s}\right]\leq\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\left[\exp(1)\cdot\alpha_{s}^{\frac{1}{\ln 4}}+\alpha_{s}\right]\leq(\exp(1)+1)\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{s}^{\frac{1}{\ln 4}},

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\alpha_{s+1}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{(2s+1)^{2}-(2s-1)^{2}}{8}\right)=\frac{\exp(s)}{2}\geq\frac{\exp(1)}{2},

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\left[2\left(1-\frac{10^{-4}}{16}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8\cdot 10^{-2}}\right)+2\cdot\frac{10^{-4}}{16}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}\right)\right]4\leq 10^{-4}.

Combining together, we have

h\leq\left(\exp(1)+1\right)\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{s}^{\frac{1}{\ln 4}}\leq(\exp(1)+1)\frac{\alpha_{1}^{\frac{1}{\ln 4}}}{1-\left(\frac{2}{\exp(1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\ln 4}}}\leq(\exp(1)+1)\frac{\left(10^{-4}\right)^{\frac{1}{\ln 4}}}{1-\left(\frac{2}{\exp(1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{\ln 4}}}\leq 0.03.

Applying the pigeonhole principle, there exists specific
(\tilde{b}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{b}_{n})\in\mathcal{B}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n})\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}:\operatorname{round}(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n})=(\tilde{b}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{b}_{n})\},

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}|\geq\frac{\operatorname{round}^{-1}(\mathcal{B})}{|\mathcal{B}|}|\geq 2^{n(1-h)-1}.

We use the following result.

Lemma 4.7

(
[
39
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{A}\subset\{-1,+1\}^{n}
and
|\mathcal{A}|\geq 2^{n\cdot\operatorname{ent}(1/2-p)}
with
p>0
, then
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{A})\geq(1-2p)n
.

In our case, we have for
n\geq 100
and hence

2^{n(1-h)-1}\geq 2^{n\left(1-0.03-0.01\right)}\geq 2^{n\cdot\operatorname{ent}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{8}\right)}.

Thus,
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{A})\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\right)n
. That is, there exist two vectors in
\mathcal{A}
which differ in at least
\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\right)n
coordinates. Let
\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}=\left(\bm{\zeta}_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n}^{\prime}\right),\bm{\zeta}^{\prime\prime}=\left(\bm{\zeta}_{1}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n}^{\prime\prime}\right)\in\mathcal{A}

. Set

\bm{\zeta}=\left(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n}\right)=\frac{\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}-\bm{\zeta}^{\prime\prime}}{2}.

All
\bm{\zeta}_{j}\in\{-1,0,+1\}
and
\bm{\zeta}_{j}=0
if and only if
\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{\prime}=\bm{\zeta}_{j}^{\prime\prime}
. Therefore,

\left|\left\{i:\bm{\zeta}_{i}=0\right\}\right|=n-\left\|\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}-\bm{\zeta}^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{1}=n-\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{A})\leq\frac{1}{4}n.

Moreover, for all
i
, since
\operatorname{round}
is identical on
\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}
and
\bm{\zeta}^{\prime\prime},\operatorname{row}_{i}\left(\bm{\zeta}^{\prime}\right)
and
\operatorname{row}_{i}\left(\bm{\zeta}^{\prime\prime}\right)
lie on a common interval of length

, we have

\left|\operatorname{row}_{i}(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n})\right|=\left|\frac{\operatorname{row}_{i}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n}^{\prime})-\operatorname{row}_{i}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,\bm{\zeta}_{n}^{\prime\prime})}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}.

∎

By Lemmas

and
4.6
, we are ready to prove Lemma
4.2

The cost difference w.r.t.

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{P},\{\tilde{\bm{c}},-\tilde{\bm{c}}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{Q},\{\tilde{\bm{c}},-\tilde{\bm{c}}\})

\left\|\tilde{\bm{c}}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{\zeta}_{i}^{2}}{n}}\leq 1.

Since
d>2n
, we can always find a vector

In this chapter, our goal is to prove the existence of a sufficiently large family that meets our conditions.
Our approach uses probabilistic methods to show that the probability of obtaining a sufficiently large family through random sampling of subspaces is greater than zero.

4.4

In this section, we generalize the lower bound to arbitrary powers

. We again focus on the cases where
k=2
as we can generalize the result to higher

in Section

For ease of analysis, we again do not require the construction of datasets

and actually ensure that every

consists of orthonormal bases of some subspaces.
At the end of the proof, we will show how to round and scale these datasets
\bm{P}

The cost function we have now without scaling is

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\left\{\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c})^{z},\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p}_{i},-\bm{c})^{z}\right\}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left\|\bm{p}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|\bm{c}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2-2\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}.

The value is upper bounded by
2^{\frac{z}{2}}n
and the difference between the cost of two datasets is

\left|\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})\right|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2-2\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2-2\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},c\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}\right|.

Similarly, to prove a lower bound on
\operatorname{sc}(n,\Delta,2,z,d,\varepsilon)
, we just need to find a large enough set

such that the cost function of any two elements is separated. The key tool is Lemma

Lemma 4.15

(Inequality of Taylor expansion)
.

For any
0<z\leq 2
and any

, we have

1-{\frac{z}{2}}x-z\left(1-{\frac{z}{2}}\right)x^{2}\leq(1-x)^{\frac{z}{2}}\leq 1-{\frac{z}{2}}x.

For any
z\geq 2
and any
x\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
, we have

1-{\frac{z}{2}}x\leq(1-x)^{\frac{z}{2}}\leq 1-{\frac{z}{2}}x+{\frac{z}{2}}\left({\frac{z}{2}}-1\right)x^{2}.

Proof.

The detailed proof is in Appendix
A
. The inequation is
essentially obtained by ignoring the higher-order terms in the Taylor expansion. The main idea is to calculate the first and second order of the function, and then use monotonicity to derive our final bound.
∎

In our previous conclusions, we have already identified a sufficiently large family such that for any two datasets, when

\left|\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})\right|=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},c\rangle\right|\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n}.

All we need to do is use our Lemma

Lemma 4.16
.

Assume
\bm{P}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}

z\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-{\frac{z}{2}}\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle\right|\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-{\frac{z}{2}}\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle\right|+{\frac{z}{2}}\left({\frac{z}{2}}-1\right)\langle\bm{p}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle={\frac{z}{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle\right|-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle\right|\right)-{\frac{z}{2}}\left({\frac{z}{2}}-1\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\bm{p}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{z}{8}\sqrt{n}-\frac{z\left({\frac{z}{2}}-1\right)}{8}.

Since
d>2n
, we can always find a vector
\bar{\bm{c}}
such that
\bar{\bm{c}}\perp\bm{p}_{i},\bm{q}_{i},i\in[n]
and
\left\|\bar{\bm{c}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=1-\left\|\tilde{\bm{c}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
.
Let
\bm{c}=\tilde{\bm{c}}+\bar{\bm{c}}
, we should have that
\bm{c}
is of unit norm and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\tilde{\bm{c}}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{z}{8}\sqrt{n}-\frac{z\left({\frac{z}{2}}-1\right)}{8}.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2-2\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2-2\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)^{\frac{z}{2}}\right|\geq\frac{2^{\frac{z}{2}}z}{8}\sqrt{n}-\frac{2^{\frac{z}{2}}z\left({\frac{z}{2}}-1\right)}{8}.

∎

With the results of Lemma
4.16
, we just need to perform scaling to finally generalize to arbitrary
z
.

Lemma 4.17
.

When
\Delta=\frac{3072\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}\sqrt{d}}{z^{2}\cdot\varepsilon}=\Theta\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right)
,
d
larger than a large enough constant and constant
z
, we have that
\operatorname{sc}(n,\Delta,2,z,d,\varepsilon)\geq\Omega\left(d\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},n\right\}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon=\frac{z}{96}\tilde{\varepsilon}
. Denote

\tilde{n}=\min\left\{\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}}\right),\Theta\left(\frac{d}{\log d}\right),n\right\}=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},n\right\}\right)\geq 100.

With the proof of second part of Theorem
1.3
and Lemma
4.16
, for constant
z
, we are able to find a set
\mathcal{P}
with all points being orthonormal bases and size being

\exp\left(\Theta\left(\tilde{n}d\right)\right)=\exp\left(\Theta\left(d\min\left\{\frac{1}{\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},n\right\}\right)\right)=\exp\left(\Theta\left(d\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},n\right\}\right)\right),

such that for any two dataset in this set
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
, we would be able to find a unit-norm vector
\bm{c}
satisfying

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2-2\left|\langle\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)^{z}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2-2\left|\langle\bm{q}_{i},\bm{c}\rangle\right|\right)^{z}\right|\geq\left\{\begin{matrix}\frac{2^{\frac{z}{2}}{z}}{8}\sqrt{\tilde{n}}-\frac{2^{\frac{z}{2}}{z}\left(1-{\frac{z}{2}}\right)}{4},0<z\leq 2\\
\frac{2^{\frac{z}{2}}{z}}{8}\sqrt{\tilde{n}}-\frac{2^{\frac{z}{2}}{z}\left({\frac{z}{2}}-1\right)}{8},z\geq 2\end{matrix}\right..

We now show how to round and scale every dataset
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
to
[\Delta]^{d}
, where
\Delta=\Theta\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right)
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\Delta
is an odd integer.
For a dataset
\bm{P}=\left(\bm{p}_{1},\cdots,\bm{p}_{\tilde{n}}\right)\in\mathcal{P}
, we will construct
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
to be our final family as follows:
For each of dataset
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, we shift the origin to
\left(\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil,\cdots,\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil\right)
, scale it by a factor of
\frac{\Delta}{2}
and finally perform an upward rounding on each dimension to put every point on the grid:

\tilde{\bm{P}}=\left(\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\bm{p}_{1}\rceil+\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil,\cdots,\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\bm{p}_{\tilde{n}}\rceil+\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil\right)=\left(\tilde{\bm{p}}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\bm{p}}_{\tilde{n}}\right).

We will then show that this set fulfills the requirement of Lemma
4.1
. For ease of explanation, we also define
\hat{P}
to be the dataset without rounding.

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{P}}=\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}\bm{p}_{1}+\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil,\cdots,\frac{\Delta}{2}\bm{p}_{\tilde{n}}+\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil\right)=\left(\hat{\bm{p}}_{1},\cdots,\hat{\bm{p}}_{\tilde{n}}\right).

Moreover, let
\bar{\bm{c}}=\frac{\Delta}{2}\bm{c}+\left(\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil,\cdots,\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil\right)
.
We must have that for the scaling dataset,

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{z}\left(\hat{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)=\frac{\Delta^{z}}{2^{z}}\operatorname{cost}_{z}\left(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\}\right)\leq\frac{\Delta^{z}\tilde{n}}{2^{\frac{z}{2}}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{z}\left(\hat{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)-\operatorname{cost}_{z}\left(\hat{\bm{Q}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{z}}{2^{z}}\left(\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})\right)\geq\left\{\begin{matrix}\frac{{z}\Delta^{z}}{8\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}\sqrt{\tilde{n}}-\frac{{z}\left(1-{\frac{z}{2}}\right)\Delta^{z}}{4\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}},0<z\leq 2\\
\frac{{z}\Delta^{z}}{8\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}\sqrt{\tilde{n}}-\frac{{z}\left({\frac{z}{2}}-1\right)\Delta^{z}}{8\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}},z\geq 2\end{matrix}\right..

On the other hand, for the rounding dataset, by our choice of
\Delta
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\hat{\bm{p}}_{i}-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\|_{2}^{z}-\left\|\tilde{\bm{p}}_{i}-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\|_{2}^{z}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta^{z}}{2^{z}}2^{z}\frac{2\sqrt{d}}{\Delta}\leq\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\Delta^{z}}{64\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}.

The case for
-\bar{\bm{c}}
and other datasets is similar. Therefore, we have that for any dataset

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\hat{\bm{P}},\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{2}(\tilde{\bm{P}},\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\})\right|\leq\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\Delta^{z}\tilde{n}}{64\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\tilde{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)\leq\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)+\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\Delta^{z}n}{64\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}\leq\frac{\Delta^{z}\tilde{n}}{2^{\frac{z}{2}}}+\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\Delta^{z}\tilde{n}}{64\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}\leq\frac{\Delta^{z}\cdot 2\tilde{n}}{2^{\frac{z}{2}}}.

We can then have a rounded family
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
such that all points are on the grid and we would be able to find
\bar{\bm{c}}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\tilde{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)-\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\tilde{\bm{Q}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{P}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)-\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{Q}},\left\{\bar{\bm{c}},-\bar{\bm{c}}\right\}\right)-\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\Delta^{z}\tilde{n}}{32\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{matrix}\frac{{z}\Delta^{z}}{8\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}\sqrt{\tilde{n}}-\frac{{z}\left(1-{\frac{z}{2}}\right)\Delta^{z}}{4\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}-\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\Delta^{z}\tilde{n}}{32\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}},0<z\leq 2\\
\frac{{z}\Delta^{z}}{8\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}\sqrt{\tilde{n}}-\frac{{z}\left({\frac{z}{2}}-1\right)\Delta^{z}}{8\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}-\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}\Delta^{z}\tilde{n}}{32\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}},z\geq 2\end{matrix}\right.

Note that for constant
z
and our choice of
\tilde{n}
that
\sqrt{\tilde{n}}\geq\tilde{\varepsilon}\tilde{n}
the right side is larger than
\frac{{z}\Delta^{z}}{16\cdot 2^{\frac{z}{2}}}\tilde{\varepsilon}\tilde{n}
.
On the other side, the cost function is upper bounded by
\frac{\Delta^{z}\cdot 2\tilde{n}}{2^{\frac{z}{2}}}
.
Therefore, as long as we make
\varepsilon
-approximation, we must have

\operatorname{cost}_{z}\left(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\}\right)\notin(1\pm 3\varepsilon)\operatorname{cost}_{z}\left(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\}.\right)

Moreover, we can find an origin being
\left(\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil,\cdots,\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\rceil\right)
such that all the center points and data points have distance to it less than
\frac{\Delta}{2}+\sqrt{d}\leq\Delta
.
By Lemma
4.1
, we have

\operatorname{sc}(n,\Delta,2,z,d,\varepsilon)\geq\Omega(\log\left|\mathcal{P}\right|)\geq\Omega\left(d\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},n\right\}\right).

∎

4.5
Extension to General
k\geq 2

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
k
is even.
(If
k
is odd, we can use
k-1
centers and put the rest center in a place not to affect the value of the function but still have a similar asymptotic lower bound on the size.)
In our previous proof, we have identified a sufficiently large family for
k=2
.
When
k>2
, our approach is to generate
\frac{k}{2}
copies of our previous family and ensure that the distance between each copy is sufficiently large so that they do not interfere with each other.
This way, as long as the two datasets we ultimately select have significant differences in a sufficient number of copies, their overall difference will also be substantial.
Let
\tilde{\Delta}=\Omega\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right)
be the large enough discretization parameter for
k=2
.
We have
\Delta\geq 4\lceil k^{\frac{1}{d}}\rceil\tilde{\Delta}=\Omega\left(\frac{k^{\frac{1}{d}}\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right)
.

By proof of second part of Theorem
1.3
and Lemma
4.17
, we can find a set
\mathcal{P}
with size being
\exp\left(\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)
with property that

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\}),\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})\leq\frac{\tilde{\Delta}^{z}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{2n}{k}}{2^{\frac{z}{2}}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c},-\bm{c}\})\geq\frac{3\varepsilon\tilde{\Delta}^{z}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{2n}{k}}{2^{\frac{z}{2}}}.

Moreover, we can find an origin such that all the center points and data points have the distance to it less than
\frac{\tilde{\Delta}}{2}+\sqrt{d}\leq\tilde{\Delta}=\Theta\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right)
.
The full instance is then made of
\frac{k}{2}
distinct copies of the
k=2
instance, denoted as
\mathcal{P}^{\frac{k}{2}}
.

We first prove that our entire space can accommodate those copies.
Note that each instance can be wrapped by a hypercube with side length
4\tilde{\Delta}
by putting its origin at the center of the hypercube.
In our model, The whole space is a hypercube with side length
\Delta
.
We can then put at most

\left(\frac{\Delta}{4\tilde{\Delta}}\right)^{d}\geq\left(\lceil k^{\frac{1}{d}}\rceil\right)^{d}\geq k.

in the large hypercube, which fulfills our requirements.

Moreover, the distance between the origins of any two different instances is at least
4\tilde{\Delta}
, which means that the points in the two instances will have a distance of at least
2\tilde{\Delta}
and will not interfere with the assignment of clustering.

We then force that any two datasets
\bm{P},\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{P}^{\frac{k}{2}}
is different on at least
\frac{k}{4}
copies.
It may be easier for us to think of each dataset as a "vector" of dimension
\frac{k}{2}
, where each entry
i
denotes the choice of the dataset on the
i
-th copy, and thus there are
\exp\left(\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)
choices.
Our additional requirement is equal to the condition that
\left\|\bm{P}-\bm{Q}\right\|_{0}\geq\frac{k}{4}.

We define
\bm{P}_{i},\bm{Q}_{i}
to be te dataset chosen in the
i
-th copy and we place two centers
\{\bm{c}_{i},-\bm{c}_{i}\}
.
The total cost of dataset
\bm{P}
is thus

\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c}_{i},-\bm{c}_{i}\}_{i\in\left[\frac{k}{2}\right]})=\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{k}{2}}\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P}_{i},\{\bm{c}_{i},-\bm{c}_{i}\})\leq\frac{1}{2}k\cdot\frac{\tilde{\Delta}^{z}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{2n}{k}}{2^{\frac{z}{2}}}.

On the other hand, when it comes to the cost difference of two datasets,
\bm{P}
and
\bm{Q}
are different on at least
\frac{k}{4}
copies.
Moreover, by the property of our chosen dataset, we can find
\{\bm{c}_{i},-\bm{c}_{i}\}
such that the cost on
i
-th copy is at least
3\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\tilde{\Delta}^{z}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{2n}{k}}{2^{\frac{z}{2}}}
.
We thus have the total cost difference be

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P},\{\bm{c}_{i},-\bm{c}_{i}\}_{i\in\left[\frac{k}{2}\right]})-\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c}_{i},-\bm{c}_{i}\}_{i\in\left[\frac{k}{2}\right]})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{k}{2}}\left[\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{P}_{i},\{\bm{c}_{i},-\bm{c}_{i}\})-\operatorname{cost}_{z}(\bm{Q},\{\bm{c}_{i},-\bm{c}_{i}\}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\bm{P}-\bm{Q}\right\|_{0}\cdot 3\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\tilde{\Delta}^{z}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{2n}{k}}{2^{\frac{z}{2}}}\geq\frac{3}{4}\varepsilon k\cdot\frac{\tilde{\Delta}^{z}\cdot 2\cdot\frac{2n}{k}}{2^{\frac{z}{2}}}.

This fulfills the requirement of family
\mathcal{P}
in Lemma
4.1
.

We then consider to compute the number of the dataset.
Note that without the additional requirement, the number of the dataset is

\left(\exp\left(\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}=\exp\left(\frac{k}{2}\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right).

On the other hand, we denote the neighbors of a dataset as those who have differences on less than
\frac{k}{4}
copies.
For a dataset, the number of neighbors is less than

\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{k}{4}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{k}{2}\\
i\end{pmatrix}\left(\exp\left(\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)\right)^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{k}{4}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{k}{2}\\
i\end{pmatrix}\exp\left(i\cdot\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right),

which means that we first choose
i
copies to be the different ones (the rest
\frac{k}{2}-i
copies are then fixed to be the same as the original dataset), and then choose the value on these positions arbitrarily. We can upper bound this value with the Stirling inequation that

\displaystyle\exp(1)\left(\frac{n^{n}}{\exp(n)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n^{n}}{\exp(n)}\right)\exp\left(\frac{1}{12n+1}\right)\leq n!

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n^{n}}{\exp(n)}\right)\exp\left(\frac{1}{12n}\right)\leq\exp(1)n\left(\frac{n^{n}}{\exp(n)}\right),

which gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{k}{4}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{k}{2}\\
i\end{pmatrix}\exp\left(i\cdot\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)\leq\frac{k}{4}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{k}{2}\\
\frac{k}{4}\end{pmatrix}\exp\left(\frac{k}{4}\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{4}\frac{\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)!}{\left(\left(\frac{k}{4}\right)!\right)^{2}}\exp\left(\frac{k}{4}\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)\leq\frac{k}{4}\frac{e\frac{k}{2}\left(\frac{k}{2e}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}}{e^{2}\left(\frac{k}{4e}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}}\exp\left(\frac{k}{4}\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{k}{4}\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)+\frac{k\ln 2}{2}+\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{8e}\right)\right).

The size of the final dataset is at least the total number divided by the number of neighbors and thus greater than

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\left(\frac{k}{2}\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)}{\exp\left(\frac{k}{4}\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)+\frac{k\ln 2}{2}+\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{8e}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{k}{4}\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)-\frac{k\ln 2}{2}-\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{8e}\right)\right)\geq\exp\left(\frac{k}{8}\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right),

where the last equation holds for
\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\geq 4\ln 2+\frac{8}{k}\ln\left(\frac{k^{2}}{8e}\right)
larger than a large enough constant.

Therefore, we can find a family
\mathcal{P}^{\frac{k}{2}}
with size being at least
\exp\left(\frac{k}{8}\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)
satisfying the requirement of Lemma
4.1
. We thus have,

\displaystyle\operatorname{sc}\left(n,\Delta,k,z,d,\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\log\left|\mathcal{P}^{\frac{k}{2}}\right|\right)=\Omega\left(k\cdot\operatorname{sc}\left(\frac{2n}{k},\tilde{\Delta},2,z,d,\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(kd\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},\frac{n}{k}\right\}\right).

5
Application to Space Lower Bound for Terminal Embedding

Recall that the definition of Terminal Embedding is given as

Definition 5.1

(Restatement of Definition
1.4
)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\bm{P}
be a dataset of
n
points.
A mapping
\tau:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called an
\varepsilon
-terminal embedding of
\bm{P}

if for any
\bm{p}\in\bm{P}
and
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p},\bm{q})\leq\operatorname{dist}(\tau(\bm{p}),\tau(\bm{q}))\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p},\bm{q}).

In the case that
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are both Euclidean metrics with
\mathcal{Y}
being lower-dimensional, work of
[
47
]
prove that the dimension of latter space can be as small as
O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\log n\right)
, which is optimal proven in
[
40
]
.

In the following part, we will show that our lower bound on the minimum number of bits of computing the cost function actually sheds light on the number of bits of terminal embedding.

Definition 5.2

(Space complexity for terminal embedding)
.

Let
\bm{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a dataset,
n\geq 1
and
\varepsilon>0
be an error parameter.
We define
\operatorname{embedsc}(\bm{P},d,\varepsilon)
to be the minimum possible number of bits of a
\varepsilon
-terminal embedding from
\bm{P}
into
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
m=O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\log n\right)
.
Moreover, we define
\operatorname{embedsc}(n,d,\varepsilon):=\sup_{\bm{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\bm{P}|=n}\operatorname{embedsc}(\bm{P},d,\varepsilon)
to be the space complexity function, i.e., the maximum cardinality
\operatorname{embedsc}(\bm{P},d,\varepsilon)
over all possible datasets
\bm{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of size
n
.

Given the definition, we have the space complexity lower bound for terminal embedding. Our proof idea is to use terminal embedding to construct an algorithm for computing
(k,z)
-Clustering
. Since the space complexity of
(k,z)
-Clustering
is very large, the terminal embedding will also have a significant space complexity.

Theorem 5.3

(Space lower bound for terminal embedding)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and assume
d=\Omega\left(\frac{\log n\log(n/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
.
The space complexity of terminal embedding
\operatorname{embedsc}(n,d,\varepsilon)=\Omega(nd)
.

Proof.

We will show that an
\varepsilon
-sketch can be constructed for the case of our lower bound by using terminal embedding.
We would consider the case where
z=2
and
k
is large enough such that
\frac{n}{k}\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d}\right\},k=O\left(n\right)
.
Note that
\Delta=\Theta\left(\frac{k^{\frac{1}{d}}\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right)=O\left(\frac{n^{\frac{1}{d}}\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right)
.
Moreover, in our proof of lower bound, since we are only considering the scaled orthonormal bases, we would have that for any considered datasets
\bm{P}
and center
\{\bm{c}_{j}\}_{j\in[2]}
.

\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\bm{P},\{\bm{c}_{j}\}_{j\in[2]}\right)\geq\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}\left(2n-\sqrt{n}\right)\geq\Omega\left(\Delta^{2}n\right).

To construct the
\varepsilon
-sketch, we first use the terminal embedding to lower the dimension to
\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}
.
For a dataset
\bm{P}=\left(\bm{p}_{1},\cdots,\bm{p}_{n}\right)
, Let
\hat{\bm{P}}=\left(\tau\left(\bm{p}_{1}\right),\cdots,\tau\left(\bm{p}_{n}\right)\right)
be the embedded dataset.
By the property of terminal embedding, we have for any center point
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c})\leq\operatorname{dist}(\tau(\bm{p}_{i}),\tau(\bm{c}))

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c}),\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c})^{2}\leq\operatorname{dist}(\tau(\bm{p}_{i}),\tau(\bm{c}))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+3\varepsilon)\cdot\operatorname{dist}(\bm{p}_{i},\bm{c})^{2}.

Therefore, for the cost function we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\bm{P},\{\bm{c}_{j}\}_{j\in[2]}\right)\leq\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{P}},\{\tau(\bm{c}_{j})\}_{j\in[2]}\right)\leq(1+3\varepsilon)\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\bm{P},\{\bm{c}_{j}\}_{j\in[2]}\right).

We then round the dataset to the grid points

\left[\Delta\right]^{\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}}
. Let the rounded dataset be

\tilde{\bm{P}}=\left(\lceil\tau\left(\bm{p}_{1}\right)\rceil,\cdots,\lceil\tau\left(\bm{p}_{n}\right)\rceil\right).

We would have that

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\hat{\bm{p}}_{i}-\tau(\bm{c})\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\tilde{\bm{p}}_{i}-\tau(\bm{c})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\hat{\bm{p}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{p}}_{i}\right\|_{2}\left\|\tilde{\bm{p}}_{i}-\tau(\bm{c})\right\|_{2}+\left\|\hat{\bm{p}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{p}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}}+\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\leq O\left(\Delta^{2}\varepsilon\right),

where the last inequation holds due to our choice of
\Delta
.
Therefore, we still have that

\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\hat{\bm{P}},\{\tau(\bm{c}_{j})\}_{j\in[2]}\right)-\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\tilde{\bm{P}},\{\tau(\bm{c}_{j})\}_{j\in[2]}\right)\right|\leq O\left(\Delta^{2}\varepsilon n\right)\leq\varepsilon\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\bm{P},\{\bm{c}_{j}\}_{j\in[2]}\right),

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\bm{P},\{\bm{c}_{j}\}_{j\in[2]}\right)\leq\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\tilde{\bm{P}},\{\tau(\bm{c}_{j})\}_{j\in[2]}\right)\leq(1+4\varepsilon)\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\bm{P},\{\bm{c}_{j}\}_{j\in[2]}\right).

We then only need to construct an
\varepsilon
-sketch for
\operatorname{cost}_{2}\left(\tilde{\bm{P}},\{\tau(\bm{c}_{j})\}_{j\in[2]}\right)
in the lower dimension of
\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}
.
With the result of Corollary
1.2
, the sketch only needs to use

\displaystyle\operatorname{sc}\left(n,\Delta,k,2,\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}},\varepsilon\right)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{k\log n\log\Delta}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\Psi(n)\left(\frac{\log n\left(\log\left(\log\Delta/\varepsilon\right)\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\log\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2n\frac{\log n\log\left(n/\varepsilon\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}},

where we bring in
\Delta=\Theta\left(\frac{k^{\frac{1}{d}}\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right),k,\Psi(n)\leq n
.
Combining together, our sketch exploits bits of only

\operatorname{sc}\left(n,\Delta,k,2,d,\varepsilon\right)\leq\operatorname{embedsc}(n,d,\varepsilon)+2n\frac{\log n\log\left(n/\varepsilon\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

On the other hand, with the result of the second part of Theorem
1.3
, we have that for
d={\Omega}\left(\frac{\log n\log\left(n/\varepsilon\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
and our choice of
n
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{sc}\left(n,\Delta,k,2,d,\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Omega\left(kd\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{d}{\log d},\frac{n}{k}\right\}\right)=\Omega\left(nd\right)\geq\Omega\left(n\frac{\log n\log\left(n/\varepsilon\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

Therefore, we must have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{embedsc}(n,d,\varepsilon)\geq\Omega\left(nd\right)-2n\frac{\log n\log\left(n/\varepsilon\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\geq\Omega\left(nd\right).

∎

The same technique can be applied to other dimensionality reduction methods.
For example,
[
25
]
initially applies dimensionality reduction to project the given set of points into an
m
-dimensional subspace
L
, with
m=O(k/\varepsilon^{2})
.
Subsequently, they construct an approximate coreset
S
within this subspace
L
.
Using a similar proof procedure, we can show that the storage of the projection to the reduced-dimensional space is
\Omega\left(md\right)=\Omega\left(kd/{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
.

6.1
Communication Cost for Distributed
(k,z)
-Clustering

In many practical applications, massive data is collected and stored
on a large number of nodes possibly deployed at different locations, while we want to learn properties of the union of the data.
For example, application data
from location based services
[
49
]
, images and videos over
networks
[
44
]
.
It has become increasingly important to develop effective clustering algorithms in distributed scenarios.
In such distributed systems and applications, communication cost is our major concern, since
communication is much slower than local computation.

Here we consider the distributed
(k,z)
-Clustering
model introduced in
[
4
]
.
In this model, there is a set of
l
sites

\mathcal{V}=\left\{\bm{v}_{i},1\leq i\leq l\right\}
,
each holding a local data set
\bm{P}_{i},

i=1,\ldots,l
.
These sites communicate through an undirected connected graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where an edge
(\bm{v}_{i},\bm{v}_{j})\in\mathcal{E}
indicates that sites
\bm{v}_{i}
and
\bm{v}_{j}
can communicate with each other.
Our goal is to construct an
\varepsilon
-sketch for
\cup_{i=1}^{l}\bm{P}_{i}
on a specified site, while keeping the communication efficient.
Previous research has primarily measured the communication cost in
number of points transmitted
[
4
]
.
Our approach, however, focuses on minimizing the worst-case communication costs, i.e., the total number of bits exchanged.

As done in
[
4
]
, we consider the coordinator model introduced in
[
22
]
. The formal definition of our problem is provided in Definition
6.1
. Similar results can be obtained for the general communication graphs using the Message-Passing algorithm proposed in
[
4
]
(See Algorithm 2 and Theorem 2 in
[
4
]
). The idea is to propagate messages on the graph in a breadth-first-search style so that all sites have a copy of the coreset at the end.

Definition 6.1

(Coreset for
(k,z)
-Clustering
in the coordinator model)
.

Given integers
n,k\geq 1
, constant
z\geq 1
and an error parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Let there be
l
sites each holding a private input data set
\bm{P}_{i}\subseteq[\Delta]^{d}
, and an additional site called coordinator.
Sites can only communicate with the coordinator.
The task of the coordinator is to collaborate with all sites to correctly output an
\varepsilon
-sketch for
\cup_{i=1}^{l}\bm{P}_{i}
.

Our objective is to minimize the communication cost defined in Definition
6.2
.

Definition 6.2

(Coreset for
(k,z)
-Clustering
in the distributed model and communication cost)
.

We define the communication cost
\operatorname{CC}(\cup_{i=1}^{l}\bm{P}_{i},\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon)
to be the minimum possible bits communicated by the sites to construct an
\varepsilon
-sketch.
Moreover, we define
\operatorname{CC}(l,n,\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon):=\sup_{\bm{P}_{i}\subseteq[\Delta]^{d}:\sum_{i=1}^{l}|\bm{P}_{i}|=n}\operatorname{CC}\left(\cup_{i=1}^{l}\bm{P}_{i},\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon\right)
to be the communication complexity function, i.e., the maximum cardinality
\operatorname{CC}\left(\cup_{i=1}^{l}\bm{P}_{i},\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon\right)
over all possible datasets
\cup_{i=1}^{l}\bm{P}_{i}\subseteq[\Delta]^{d}
of size at most
n
.

The idea for our algorithm is based on the mergeability of coresets, meaning that the union of coresets from multiple datasets forms a coreset for the combined datasets
[
4
]
. Consequently, we begin by constructing a sketch for each site and then transmit these to the coordinator. The final sketch is then assembled by merging the results from each local dataset.

Corollary 6.3

(Communication upper bounds for distributed Euclidean
(k,z)
-Clustering
)
.

In the distributed
(k,z)
-Clustering
problem, suppose for any dataset
\cup_{i=1}^{l}\bm{P}_{i}\subseteq[\Delta]^{d}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{l}|\bm{P}_{i}|=n
and
|\bm{P}_{i}|>k,

i=1,\ldots,l
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-coreset of
\bm{P}_{i}
for
(k,z)
-Clustering
of size at most
\Psi\left(|\bm{P}_{i}|\right)\geq 1
on each site.
Then the communication complexity to construct an
\varepsilon
-sketch for
\cup_{i=1}^{l}\bm{P}_{i}
is bounded by:

\operatorname{CC}(l,n,\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon)\leq O\left(lkd\log\Delta+\sum_{i=1}^{l}\Psi\left(|\bm{P}_{i}|\right)(d\log 1/\varepsilon+d\log\log\Delta+\log\log n)\right).

Proof.

We first let each site runs Algorithm
1
. Apply Theorem
1.2
, we have the bit complexity of the
\varepsilon
-sketch for each local dataset is

\operatorname{sc}(\bm{P}_{i},\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon)\leq O\left(kd\log\Delta+\Psi\left(|\bm{P}_{i}|\right)(d\log 1/\varepsilon+d\log\log\Delta+\log\log n)\right).

Each site will transmit its sketch to the coordinator, who will then combine these sketches to obtain the final result. The communication cost for transmitting these sketches is

\displaystyle\operatorname{CC}(l,n,\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l}\operatorname{sc}(\bm{P}_{i},\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq O\left(lkd\log\Delta+\sum_{i=1}^{l}\Psi\left(|\bm{P}_{i}|\right)(d\log 1/\varepsilon+d\log\log\Delta+\log\log n)\right).

∎

Combining with the recent breakthroughs that shows that for any
|\bm{P}_{i}|>k
,
\Psi(|\bm{P}_{i}|)=\tilde{O}\left(\min\left\{k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}\varepsilon^{-2},k\varepsilon^{-z-2}\right\}\right)

[
14
,
15
,
16
,
32
]
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\operatorname{CC}(l,n,\Delta,k,z,d,\varepsilon)\leq\tilde{O}\left(ld\cdot\min\left\{\frac{k^{\frac{2z+2}{z+2}}}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{k}{\varepsilon^{z+2}}\right\}\right).

6.2
Space Complexity for Streaming
(k,z)
-Clustering

Modern datasets have significantly increased in size, often consisting of hundreds of millions of points, which poses great challenges for analyzing
them. In typical applications, the total volume of data is very large and can not be stored in its entirety. Over the last decade, the streaming model has proven to be successful in dealing with big data
[
46
]
. In this model, the input data arrive sequentially and we usually require a data structure using limited working space compared with the huge volume of the data. Our major concern is the storage cost, which is the bit complexity of storing such a data structure. We consider the insertion-only streaming model formally defined in Definition
6.4
.

Definition 6.4

(Insertion-Only Streaming
(k,z)
-Clustering
)
.

Given integers
n,k\geq 1
, constant
z\geq 1
and an error parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Suppose a stream consists of
n
point
\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n}\in[\Delta]^{d}
that arrive sequentially.
The goal is to maintain an
\varepsilon
-sketch for the stream at every point while using limited bits.

There is a growing body of work studying Euclidean
(k,z)
-Clustering
problems over data streams. However, existing studies mainly focus on the size of the coreset
[
27
,
8
]
or assume a single word for storing the coordinate and weight
[
17
]
. In contrast, our approach focuses on minimizing the worst-case bit complexity. By using Algorithm
1
, we get Corollary
6.5
.

Corollary 6.5

(Space upper bounds for streaming Euclidean
(k,z)
-Clustering
)
.

In the streaming
(k,z)
-Clustering
problem, suppose for any stream consists of
n
point
\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{n}\in[\Delta]^{d}
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-coreset of the stream for
(k,z)
-Clustering
using at most
\Phi(n)
words.
When
n>k
, the bits needed for storage is upper bounded by:

O\left(kd\log\Delta+\Phi(n)\left(\log 1/\varepsilon+\log\log\Delta+\frac{1}{d}\log\log n\right)\right).

Combining with the result from
[
8
]
which constructed a streaming coreset using
\Phi(n)=O(\varepsilon^{-2}kd(\log k\log n+\log 1/\delta))
words with probability at least
1-\delta
, we obtain that the required number of bits is bounded by

O\left(kd\left(\log\Delta+\varepsilon^{-2}\left(\log k\log n+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\left(\log 1/\varepsilon+\log\log\Delta+\frac{1}{d}\log\log n\right)\right)\right).

7
Conclusions and Future Work

In this study, we initiate the exploration of space complexity for the Euclidean
(k,z)
-Clustering
problem, presenting both upper and lower bounds.
Our findings suggest that a coreset serves as the optimal compression scheme when
k
is constant.
Furthermore, the space lower bounds for
(k,z)
-Clustering
directly imply a tight space lower bound for terminal embedding when
d\geq\Omega(\frac{\log n\log(n/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}})
.
The techniques we employ for establishing these lower bounds contribute to a deeper geometric understanding of principal angles, which may be of independent research interest.

Our work opens up several interesting research directions.
One immediate challenge is to further narrow the gap between the upper and lower bounds of the space complexity for Euclidean
(k,z)
-Clustering
.
Additionally, it would be valuable to investigate whether a coreset remains optimal for compression when
k
is large.
