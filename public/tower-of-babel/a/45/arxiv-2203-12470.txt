1.
Introduction

Factor theory is one of the oldest and most active areas of graph theory
[
1
]
, that started in the 19th century when Petersen showed that every even regular graph is 2-factorable. In this note, we are primarily concerned with factors with prescribed degree conditions in bigraphs.

A bigraph
G
with bipartition
\{X,Y\}
will be denoted by
G[X,Y]
, and for
S\subseteq X
,
\overline{S}
means
X\backslash S
. For a real-valued function
f
on a domain
D
and
A\subseteq D
,
f(A):=\sum\nolimits_{a\in A}f(a)
. For a graph
G=(V,E)
,
u\in V
and
A\subseteq V
,
\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{G}(u)
, and
\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(uA)
denote the number of edges incident with
u
, and the number of edges between
u
and
A
, respectively. Let
f,g
be integer functions on the vertex set of a graph
G
such that
0\leq g(x)\leq f(x)
for all
x
. A
(g,f)
-factor
is a spanning subgraph
F
of
G
with the property that
g(x)\leq\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(x)\leq f(x)
for each
x
. An
f
-factor
is an
(f,f)
-factor. Ore
[
7
,
8
]
showed that
G[X,Y]
has an
f
-factor if and only if
f(X)=f(Y)
and

f(A)\leq\sum\nolimits_{y\in Y}\min\big\{f(y),\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(yA)\big\}\quad\forall A\subseteq X.

Folkman and Fulkerson proved a
(g,f)
-factor theorem for bigraphs
[
5
]
which was simplified by Heinrich et al. (Here,
x\dotdiv y
means
\max\{0,x-y\}
).

Theorem 1.1
.

[
6
, Theorem 1]

The bigraph
G[X,Y]
has a
(g,f)
-factor if and only if

\displaystyle f(A)

\displaystyle\geq\sum\nolimits_{u\notin A}\Big(g(u)\dotdiv\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{G-A}(u)\Big)\quad\forall A\subseteq X\cup Y.

Recently Cymer and Kano found another simple criteria.

Theorem 1.2
.

[
4
, Theorem 5]

The bigraph
G[X,Y]
has a
(g,f)
-factor if and only if the following conditions hold.

\displaystyle g(A)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\nolimits_{y\in Y}\min\Big\{f(y),\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(yA)\Big\}\quad

\displaystyle\forall A\subseteq X,

\displaystyle g(B)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\nolimits_{x\in X}\min\Big\{f(x),\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(xB)\Big\}\quad

\displaystyle\forall B\subseteq Y.

Theorem
1.2
has been particularly useful in solving various generalized Sudoku puzzles
[
2
,
3
]
; Solving some of these puzzles can be reduced to finding
(g,f)
-factors with the additional property that
g(y)=0
for
y\in Y
in a bigraph
G[X,Y]
. Motivated by solving such problems, we establish the following new criterion for a bigraph to have a factor with prescribed degrees.

Theorem 1.3
.

A bigraph
G[X,Y]
has a
(g,f)
-factor with
g(y)=0
for
y\in Y
if and only if

(1)

f(B)\geq\sum\nolimits_{x\in A}\Big(g(x)\dotdiv\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(x\overline{B})\Big)\quad\forall A\subseteq X,B\subseteq Y.

While Theorem
1.2
relies on Folkman-Fulkerson’s
(g,f)
-factor theorem,
our proof is self-contained and relies on alternating path technique
[
6
]
. Before we prove our main result, we provide the following corollary. Here,
N_{G}(S)
is the neighborhood of
S
in
G
.

Corollary 1.4
.

A bigraph
G[X,Y]
in which the mutiplicity of each edge is at least
m
, has a
(g,f)
-factor with
f(y)\leq m,g(y)=0
for
y\in Y
if and only if

(2)

f(N_{G}(S))\geq g(S)\quad\forall S\subseteq X.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.3
,
G[X,Y]
has a
(g,f)
-factor with
g(y)=0
for
y\in Y
if and only if

(3)

f(B)\geq\sum\nolimits_{x\in A}\Big(g(x)\dotdiv\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(x\overline{B})\Big)\quad\forall A\subseteq X,B\subseteq Y.

To complete the proof, we show that (
2
) and (
3
) are equivalent. First, let us assume that (
3
) holds, and let
A=S\subseteq X,B=N_{G}(S)\subseteq Y
. We have

(4)

f(N_{G}(S))\geq\sum\nolimits_{x\in S}\Big(g(x)\dotdiv\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(x\overline{N_{G}(S)})\Big)=\sum\nolimits_{x\in S}g(x)=g(S),

and so (
2
) is satisfied. Conversely, assume that (
2
) holds, and let
A\subseteq X,B\subseteq Y
. Let

S=\{x\in A\ |\ g(x)\geq\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(x\overline{B})\}.

If we show that
g(S)\leq f(B)+\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(S\overline{B})
, then we are done.
We have

g(S)\leq f(N_{G}(S))=f(N_{G}(S)\cap B)+f(N_{G}(S)\backslash\overline{B})\leq f(B)+m|N_{G}(S)\backslash B|\leq f(B)+\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(S\overline{B}).

∎

Remark 1.5
.

The case
m=1
of Corollary
1.4
was previously settled in
[
4
, Theorem 7]
. Observe that the case
m=1
,
f(x)=g(x)=1
for
x\in X
of Corollary
1.4
corresponds to the famous Hall’s marriage theorem.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

To prove the necessity, suppose that
G
has a
(g,f)
-factor
F
, and let
A\subseteq X,B\subseteq Y
. Define
C=\{x\in A\ |\ g(x)>\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(x\overline{B})\}
. If
C=\emptyset
, then (
1
) is trivial. Otherwise, let
x\in C
. There must be at least
g(x)-\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(x\overline{B})
edges in
F
joining
x
to vertices in
B
. Hence,

(5)

\sum\nolimits_{x\in C}\Big(g(x)-\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(x\overline{B})\Big)\leq\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{F}(CB)\leq\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{F}(AB)\leq\sum\nolimits_{y\in B}\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(y)\leq f(B).

To prove the sufficiency, suppose that (
1
) holds. Let
F
be a
(0,f)
-factor that minimizes
\delta:=\sum\nolimits_{x\in X}\Big(g(x)\dotdiv\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(x)\Big)
. If
\delta=0
, then
F
is a
(g,f)
-factor and we are done. So let us assume that
\delta>0
, and so

(6)

R:=\{x\in X\ |\ g(x)>\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(x)\}\neq\emptyset.

To complete the proof, we find sets
A\subseteq X,B\subseteq Y
such that (
1
) fails. A path (possibly of length zero) is
nice
if it starts with a vertex in
R
and an edge in
E(G)\backslash E(F)
and whose edges are alternately in
G-F
and
F
.
Let
W
be the set of terminal vertices of all nice paths. Let
A=R\cup S
where
S:=(X\backslash R)\cap W
, and let
B=Y\cap W
. We claim that

(a)

If
e\in E(F)
with
e=xy
and
y\in B
, then
x\in A
.

(b)

If
e\in E(G)\backslash E(F)
with
e=xy
and
x\in A
, then
y\in B
.

(c)

\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(x\overline{B})=\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(x)-e_{G}(xB)
for
x\in A
.

(d)

\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(y)=f(y)
for
y\in B
.

(e)

\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(x)=g(x)
for
x\in S
.

(f)

\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{G-B}(x)\leq g(x)
for
x\in A
.

Observe that (c) is an immediate consequence of (b), and (
6
) and (c) imply (f). To prove (a) and (b), let
e=xy
. If
y\in B
, there is a nice path
P
ending at
y
(whose last edge is not in
F
), and so if
e\in E(F)
and
x\notin R
, then
P+ex
is a nice path ending at
x
, and consequently,
x\in S
. Similarly, if
x\in A
, there is a nice path
P
ending at
x
(whose last edge is in
F
), and so if
e\in E(G)\backslash E(F)
, then
P+ey
is a nice path ending at
y
, and consequently,
y\in B
.
To prove (d), let
y\in B
. There is a nice path
P
ending at
y
. If
\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(y)<f(y)
, then since the last edge of
P
is in
E(G)\backslash E(F)
, the
(0,f)
-factor
F^{\prime}
with
E(F^{\prime})=E(F)\Delta E(P)
contradicts the minimality of
\delta
(We use
\Delta
for the symmetric difference). Similarly, to prove (e), let
x\in S
. There is a nice path
P
ending at
x
. If
\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(x)>g(x)
, then since the last edge of
P
is in
E(F)
, the
(0,f)
-factor
F^{\prime}
with
E(F^{\prime})=E(F)\Delta E(P)
contradicts the minimality of
\delta
. The following completes the proof.

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{x\in A}\Big(g(x)\dotdiv\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(x\overline{B})\Big)\mathop{\scalebox{3}[1]{=}}\limits^{\text{(f)}}

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{x\in A}\Big(g(x)-\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(x\overline{B})\Big)

\displaystyle\mathop{\scalebox{3}[1]{=}}\limits^{\text{(c)}}

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{x\in A}\Big(g(x)-\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(x)+\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(xB)\Big)

\displaystyle\mathop{\scalebox{3}[1]{=}}\limits^{\text{(e)}}

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{x\in R}\Big(g(x)-\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(x)\Big)+\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(AB)

\displaystyle\mathop{\quad>\quad}\limits^{\eqref{Rdef}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\textup{{e}}}_{G}(AB)

\displaystyle\mathop{\scalebox{3}[1]{=}}\limits^{\text{(a)}}

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{y\in B}\operatorname{\textup{{deg}}}_{F}(y)

\displaystyle\mathop{\scalebox{3}[1]{=}}\limits^{\text{(d)}}

\displaystyle f(B).

This completes the proof.
