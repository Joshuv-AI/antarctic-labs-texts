Convergence Analysis of Accelerated Stochastic Gradient Descent under the Growth Condition
You-Lin Chen ∗, Sen Na, Mladen Kolar

Abstract
We study the convergence of accelerated stochastic gradient descent for strongly convex objectives under the growth condition , which states that the variance of stochastic gradient is bounded by a multiplicative part that grows with the full gradient, and a constant additive part. Through the lens of the growth condition, we investigate four widely used accelerated methods: Nesterov’s accelerated method (NAM), robust momentum method (RMM), accelerated dual averaging method (DAM+), and implicit DAM+ (iDAM+). While these methods are known to improve the convergence rate of SGD under the condition that the stochastic gradient has bounded variance, it is not well understood how their convergence rates are affected by the multiplicative noise. In this paper, we show that these methods all converge to a neighborhood of the optimum with accelerated convergence rates (compared to SGD) even under the growth condition. In particular, NAM, RMM, iDAM+ enjoy acceleration only with a mild multiplicative noise, while DAM+ enjoys acceleration even with a large multiplicative noise. Furthermore, we propose a generic tail-averaged scheme that allows the accelerated rates of DAM+ and iDAM+ to nearly attain the theoretical lower bound (up to a logarithmic factor in the variance term). We conduct numerical experiments to support our theoretical conclusions.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider an unconstrained optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x),

(1)

where
f
is continuously differentiable,
\mathcal{L}
-smooth, and
\mathcal{m}
-strongly convex with respect to the
\ell_{2}
norm
\|\cdot\|
; that is, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\begin{split}f(y)\leq f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{\top}(y-x)+\frac{\mathcal{L}}{2}\|y-x\|^{2},\ \ \ f(y)\geq f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{\top}(y-x)+\frac{\mathcal{m}}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}.\end{split}

(2)

For solving Problem (
1
), the first-order methods utilize only the first-order information, that is the gradient
\nabla f(x)
. Due to their simplicity and light computational costs, the first-order methods are widely used in machine learning for solving large-scale optimization problems.

In deterministic setting, where the noiseless gradient
\nabla f(x)
can be accessed, it is known that the vanilla gradient descent has a sub-optimal convergence rate for optimizing a strongly convex objective, while accelerated methods, such as Nesterov’s accelerated method (NAM)
[
29
]
and Polyak’s heavy ball (HB)
[
31
]
, enjoy the optimal convergence rate
[
28
]
. However, in stochastic setting, the story is not so clear as the same type of analysis is inapplicable. The common belief is that stochastic accelerated methods mimic their deterministic counterparts, resulting in certain practical gains; strictly speaking, their theoretical underpinnings remain incomplete.

A stochastic method can only query a
stochastic oracle
(SO) to obtain a noisy gradient, i.e.,

g(x)=\nabla f(x)+\varepsilon(x),

where
\varepsilon(x)
is a mean-zero noise that depends on the iterate
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The assumption on the
stochastic oracle’s variance
,
\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon(x)\|^{2}
, is crucial for both the design and analysis of a practical stochastic method. Most of the literature is focused on a
SO with upper bounded variance
(OUBV), that is
\sup_{x}\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon(x)\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
for some constant
\sigma^{2}
. The OUBV assumption is reasonable when the domain of
f
is compact or the noise
\varepsilon(\cdot)
does not depend on the current iterate
x
(that is, the noise is additive). However, this assumption can also be restrictive as it excludes many important problem instances, including the least-squares regression (LSR). More importantly, existing analyses under OUBV are inconsistent with some empirical observations. For example,
[
1
,
3
]
proved under OUBV that both HB and NAM have a faster rate of convergence compared to stochastic gradient descent (SGD), while
[
23
,
25
]
constructed a least-squares problem for which HB and NAM
cannot
outperform SGD even with the best choice of tuning parameters. The reason for this discrepancy, which motivates our work, stems from the violation of OUBV in least-squares
problems
. For such problems,
\varepsilon(x)
depends on
x
so that the variance
\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon(x)\|^{2}
can be large and grow with the distance
\|x-x_{\star}\|^{2}
. Here,
x_{\star}=\arg\min f(x)
denotes the unique minimizer of
f
.

In this paper, we aim to answer the following fundamental question:

Do accelerated SGD methods enjoy accelerated (even optimal) convergence rates, as in deterministic case, without assuming OUBV?

We provide an affirmative answer to this question. We prove that under a
growth condition
on the variance
\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon(x)\|^{2}
, which is a weaker and more realistic condition, (some) accelerated SGD methods achieve near-optimal accelerated convergence rates. Specifically, our analysis unifies the error recursions of four accelerated methods into a single form in (
9
), which requires carrying out novel and sharper derivations upon the existing literature. By the unified recursion, we examine different bias-variance trade-offs of the four methods and compare their ability of robustness to the multiplicative noise. The comparison results are summarized in
Section

4.3
.

We first discuss LSR in detail in the next section to motivate our study. We will illustrate that OUBV is not a reasonable assumption for this type of problem, but a growth condition is satisfied.

1.1
Motivation

Consider the following LSR problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{a,b\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[a^{\top}x-b\right]^{2},

where
(a,b)
is a covariate-response pair sampled from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
. To simplify our discussion, we assume a linear model
b=a^{\top}x_{\text{LSR}}+\varepsilon_{\text{LSR}}
, where
\varepsilon_{\text{LSR}}
is a random variable independent from

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\text{LSR}}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\text{LSR}}^{2}]=\sigma_{\text{LSR}}^{2}
, and
\mathbb{E}[aa^{\top}]
is invertible. Note that
x_{\text{LSR}}(=x_{\star})
is the unique minimizer of
f
in this example. Given
n

\{a_{i},b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from
\mathcal{D}
,
[
26

showed that any estimator

of

has at least the following minimax risk

\mathbb{E}[f(\widehat{x})]-f(x_{\text{LSR}})=\Omega\left(\frac{d\sigma_{\text{LSR}}^{2}}{n}\right),

(3)

where
\alpha_{n}=\Omega(\beta_{n})
denotes that
\alpha_{n}\geq c\beta_{n}
for some constant
c
and large enough
n
. The above minimax risk can be achieved by the empirical risk minimizer. When applying the first-order stochastic approximation (SA) methods on
f
, where at each round only few samples are accessed to approximate

and
\nabla f
,
[
21
, Corollary 2]
showed that, with some moment conditions on
\mathcal{D}
, the tail-averaged SGD meets the risk bound (
\kappa=\mathcal{L}/\mathcal{m}
is the condition number)

\mathbb{E}[f(x_{n}^{\text{SGD}})]-f(x_{\text{LSR}})=\mathcal{O}\left(\exp\left(-\frac{n}{\kappa}\right)+\frac{d\sigma_{\text{LSR}}^{2}}{n}\right).

(4)

Here,
\alpha_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\beta_{n})
denotes that
\alpha_{n}\leq c\beta_{n}
for some constant
c
and large enough
n
. The above bound consists of two terms: the second term is the statistical rate or the variance term, which is optimal as seen from (
3
); the first term is the algorithmic rate or the bias term, which is
not
optimal. In fact, by standard complexity theory of convex programs, e.g.,
[
27
, Sections 5.3.1, 7.2.6]
,
[
28
, Theory 2.1.13]
,
[
13
, (1.3)]
,
[

, Corollary B.5]
,
[

, (5), (6)]
, and
[
8
]
, it is well known that the risk lower bound of first-order SA methods under OUBV is

\mathbb{E}[f(x_{n})]-f(x_{\text{LSR}})=\Omega\bigg(\underbrace{\exp\left(-\frac{n}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)}_{\text{bias term}}+\underbrace{\frac{d\sigma_{\text{LSR}}^{2}}{n}}_{\text{variance term}}\bigg).

(5)

Compared to (
4
), the dependence on the condition number
\kappa
in (
5
) improves from
1/\kappa
to
1/\sqrt{\kappa}
. The lower bound can be attained under OUBV by an accelerated method designed in
[
13
]
. We note that (
5
) is a valid lower bound even if a weaker assumption is imposed on the stochastic oracle than OUBV. This is simply because that the constructed problem instances for lower bound analysis are still applicable when weaker conditions are required. However, there exist only limited works that addressed the problem whether an algorithm can attain the bound (
5
) under weaker conditions on the oracle. A careful study of this problem helps us to explain the empirical observation that HB and NAM fail to accelerate SGD when applied to LSR in
[
23
,
25
]
, as OUBV does not hold for LSR in general. To see this clearly, the SO
g(x)
in LSR is

g(x)=a(a^{\top}x-b)=a(a^{\top}x-a^{\top}x_{\text{LSR}}-\varepsilon_{\text{LSR}})=aa^{\top}(x-x_{\text{LSR}})-a\varepsilon_{\text{LSR}},

where the second equality follows from the linear model setup. The noise is then

\varepsilon(x)=g(x)-\nabla f(x)=(aa^{\top}-\bm{\Sigma})(x-x_{\text{LSR}})-a\varepsilon_{\text{LSR}},

where
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}aa^{\top}
. Therefore, OUBV assumption is not satisfied unless
x
is in a compact domain. In particular, denoting
\mathbf{I}_{d}
as a
d\times d
identity matrix, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-x_{\text{LSR}})^{\top}\mathbb{E}[aa^{\top}-\bm{\Sigma}]^{2}(x-x_{\text{LSR}})+\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{\text{LSR}}^{2}\cdot a^{\top}a]

\displaystyle=\nabla f(x)^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(aa^{\top}-\bm{\Sigma})^{2}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\right]\nabla f(x)+\sigma_{\text{LSR}}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}a^{\top}a

\displaystyle=\nabla f(x)^{\top}\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbb{E}(aa^{\top})^{2}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}-\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)\nabla f(x)+\sigma_{\text{LSR}}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}a^{\top}a,

(6)

which motivates us to study a weaker condition on the variance, called the
growth condition
.

1.2
Growth condition

As shown in (
1.1
), a weaker condition on the noise
\varepsilon(x)
that is satisfied at least by LSR is the growth condition
[
35
,
37
,
33
,
38
,
30
,
36
]
. The formal definition is as follows.

Definition 1.1

(Growth Condition)

We say that the error
\varepsilon(x)
satisfies the growth condition with constants
\delta\geq 0
and
\sigma^{2}
if

\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon(x)\|^{2}\leq\delta\cdot\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}.

(7)

The growth condition states that the variance of the stochastic gradient is dominated by a multiplicative part
\delta\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}
and a constant additive part
\sigma^{2}
. The multiplicative part shrinks relative to the true gradient. We note that when
\delta=0
, the growth condition reduces to OUBV.

Our work provides comprehensive analyses of accelerated methods under the growth condition. The
accelerated rate
refers to a convergence rate, whose bias term is improved from
1-c(\delta)/\kappa
as (
4
) to
1-c(\delta)/\sqrt{\kappa}
as (
5
) with some constant
c(\delta)
depending on
\delta
. In this sense, we analyze four prominent accelerated stochastic methods: Nesterov’s accelerated method (NAM), robust momentum method (RMM), accelerated dual averaging method (DAM+), and implicit DAM+ (iDAM+). We show that all these methods attain accelerated rates under the growth condition, although they can tolerate different levels of
\delta
. In particular, we show the following results.

(a)

NAM, RMM, iDAM+ enjoy accelerated rates but only for
mild
multiplicative noise (e.g.,
\delta<1
).

(b)

DAM+ enjoys an accelerated rate for any
\delta\geq 0
. Thus, it uniformly improves upon SGD.

(c)

The rates of DAM+ and iDAM+, with a proper scheme for averaging iterates and diminishing algorithms’ parameters, nearly match (
5
) (up to a logarithmic factor in the variance term).

(d)

When
\delta=0
, the rates that we establish reduce to the known results under OUBV assumption; and all the four methods accelerate SGD.

Based on the above results, we now revisit the variance of the gradient’s noise of LSR in (
1.1
). It is straightforward to see that the multiplicative noise of LSR for some distribution
\mathcal{D}
is not “mild”. For example, if
a
follows multivariate normal distribution with mean zero and covariance
\bm{\Sigma}
, plugging the equality
\mathbb{E}(aa^{\top})^{2}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\bm{\Sigma})\bm{\Sigma}+2\bm{\Sigma}^{2}
into (
1.1
) shows that
\delta>1
. This calculation may reveal the reason why NAM, RMM (and iDAM+) do not outperform SGD on LSR, as observed in
[
23
,
25
]
. Although such a statement is not a rigorous validation on the failure of NAM, RMM, and iDAM+, which would require a lower bound analysis, we indeed identify an important characteristic that an accelerated method should enjoy to ensure acceleration on some problem instances.

In addition, although
[
25
]
proved that stochastic NAM fails to accelerate for LSR, and
[
10
]
argued that stochastic NAM is fragile for multiplicative noise by numerical studies, we rigorously prove, by a finer analysis, that it accelerates SGD for
mild
multiplicative noise, which is a novel and rather surprising result in stochastic analysis. We should mention that our analyses of NAM and RMM are only for constant algorithms’ parameters, confined by the dissipativity framework
[
24
]
that we rely on for proofs
1
1

1

We are aware that
[
17
]
analyzed the convergence properties of a stochastic method with varying parameters via the dissipativity framework, but that work only showed a sublinear convergence rate.
. We leave the analyses of NAM and RMM with diminishing parameters for future works. However, for DAM+ and iDAM+, we design a novel scheme to average iterates and diminish parameters, and show that their rates nearly attain the lower bound under the growth condition.

1.3
Contribution and related work

Our paper contributes to the convergence analysis of accelerated stochastic first-order methods under the growth condition (

[

]
, which we revisit for strongly convex objectives in

[

showed that (

[

]
designed an accelerated method and showed under the same setup that their method attains the accelerated rate for strongly convex objectives. Furthermore,
[

introduced a notion of

Under some extra assumptions, the authors also analyzed an accelerated SGD method called FISTA, and proved that the method attains the optimal iteration complexity for convex objectives.

As the important complement of SGD, the dual averaging method (DAM) updates the iterates by reusing the past gradients information via averaging
[
39
]
. We analyze its two accelerated variates—accelerated DAM (DAM+)

To sum up, the existing literature either analyzed SGD under different forms of growth conditions or analyzed accelerated SGD and DAMs under OUBV or over-parameterized regime. Throughout our analysis, we do not require

, i.e.
\mathbb{E}{V_{k+1}}\leq\rho^{2}\mathbb{E}{V_{k}}+c_{k}
. See Theorem 3 in
[
19
]
for more details.

2.1
SGD under the growth condition

We review the convergence of SGD for strongly convex objective
f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{m},\mathcal{L})
under the growth condition
[
5
]
. We first introduce some additional notation. Given a stochastic iteration sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
, we let
\mathcal{G}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{2}\subseteq\ldots
be a filtration of
\sigma
-algebras where
\mathcal{G}_{k}=\sigma(\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k})
contains all the randomness up to the iteration
k
. At the
k
-th iteration with the iterate
x_{k}
, we let
\varepsilon_{k}(x_{k})
be a realization that is drawn from
\varepsilon(x_{k})
. Furthermore, the gradient realization is
g_{k}(x_{k})=\nabla f(x_{k})+\varepsilon_{k}(x_{k})
.
Throughout the paper and for all algorithms, the (stochastic) gradient is always evaluated at
x_{k}
, even if algorithms involve other variables in the scheme
. To ease notation, we let
g_{k}=g_{k}(x_{k})
,
\nabla f_{k}=\nabla f(x_{k})
, and
\varepsilon_{k}=\varepsilon_{k}(x_{k})
. We further have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{k}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}]

\displaystyle=0,\hskip 54.06006pt\mathbb{E}[g_{k}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}]=\nabla f_{k},

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|g_{k}^{2}\|\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}]

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\|\varepsilon_{k}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:GC}}}{{\leq}}(1+\delta)\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}.

The (constant-stepsize) SGD scheme takes the form
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\eta g_{k}
. By Lemma
2.1
(b) and
2.2
(c), we get
\nabla f_{k}^{\top}(x_{k}-x_{\star})\geq\mathcal{m}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}
and
\nabla f_{k}^{\top}(x_{k}-x_{\star})\geq\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}/\mathcal{L}
, which implies

-2\nabla f_{k}^{\top}(x_{k}-x_{\star})\leq-\mathcal{m}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}.

(15)

Furthermore, using the above inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{pequ:1}}}{{=}}\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-2\eta\nabla f_{k}^{\top}(x_{k}-x_{\star})+\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|g_{k}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{pequ:1},\eqref{pequ:2}}}{{\leq}}(1-\mathcal{m}\eta)\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\frac{\eta}{\mathcal{L}}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\left((1+\delta)\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 4.0pt=(1-\mathcal{m}\eta)\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}-\left(\frac{\eta}{\mathcal{L}}-\eta^{2}-\delta\eta^{2}\right)\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}.

Letting
V_{k}=\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}
and taking full expectation, we further have

\mathbb{E}\left[V_{k+1}\right]-(1-\mathcal{m}\eta)\mathbb{E}\left[V_{k}\right]\leq-\left(\frac{\eta}{\mathcal{L}}-\eta^{2}-\delta\eta^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}.

(16)

From (
16
), we see that SGD has a term
-(\eta/\mathcal{L}-\eta^{2})\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}
to eliminate the multiplicative noise term
\delta\eta^{2}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}
, brought by the growth condition. In particular, since

\frac{\eta}{\mathcal{L}}-\eta^{2}\geq\delta\eta^{2}\Longleftrightarrow\eta\leq\frac{1}{(1+\delta)\mathcal{L}},

we can set
\eta=1/((1+\delta)\mathcal{L})
to achieve the fastest rate for SGD, and arrive at the error recursion

\mathbb{E}\left[V_{k+1}\right]\leq(1-\mathcal{m}\eta)\mathbb{E}\left[V_{k}\right]+\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}=\left(1-\frac{1}{(1+\delta)\kappa}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[V_{k}\right]+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(1+\delta)^{2}\mathcal{L}^{2}}.

(17)

Applying (
17
) iteratively, we obtain

\mathbb{E}V_{k}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{(1+\delta)\kappa}\right)^{k}\mathbb{E}V_{0}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(1+\delta)\mathcal{L}\mathcal{m}},\quad\forall k\geq 0.

(18)

The above derivation with
\delta=0
recovers the sharpest SGD analysis under OUBV
[
14
, Theorem 3.1]
[
5
, Theorem 4.6]
. Given the recursion (
18
), we rise the following question:

Is it possible to apply accelerated SGD methods (with constant parameters) to improve the dependence on the condition number in bias term from
1/\kappa
to
1/\sqrt{\kappa}
, under the growth condition?

We provide an affirmative answer for NAM, RMM in
Section

3
; and DAM+, iDAM+ in
Section

4
. In particular, we show that, for a certain (but different) range of
\delta
, the four methods all achieve the accelerated rates.

3
Nesterov’s Accelerated Method and Robust Momentum Method

We study NAM and RMM and show convergence properties under the growth condition. In particular, we establish the error recursions for NAM and RMM in terms of the multiplicative noise level
\delta

\displaystyle\leq 2\rho^{4k}\mathbb{E}\|y_{1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{4\eta}{1-\sqrt{\rho}}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\rho^{2.75(k-t)}\mathbb{E}\|s_{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eq:NAM-potential-ieq}}}}{{\leq}}\;\;2\rho^{4k}\mathbb{E}\|y_{1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{4\eta}{1-\sqrt{\rho}}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\rho^{2.75(k-t)}\left\{\rho^{2t}\mathbb{E}V_{1}+\frac{\eta(1+\mathcal{L}\eta)\sigma^{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\right\}

\displaystyle=2\rho^{4k}\mathbb{E}\|y_{1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{4\eta\rho^{2k}\mathbb{E}V_{1}}{1-\sqrt{\rho}}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\rho^{0.75(k-t)}+\frac{2\eta^{2}(1+\mathcal{L}\eta)\sigma^{2}}{(1-\sqrt{\rho})(1-\rho^{2})}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\rho^{2.75(k-t)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\rho^{4k}\mathbb{E}\|y_{1}-x_{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{4\eta\mathbb{E}V_{1}}{(1-\sqrt{\rho})(1-\rho^{0.75})}\rho^{2k}+\frac{2\eta^{2}(1+\mathcal{L}\eta)}{(1-\sqrt{\rho})(1-\rho^{2})(1-\rho^{2.75})}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\rho^{2(k+1)}+\sigma^{2}\right).

This completes the proof by noting that
\mathbb{E}\|y_{0}-x_{\star}\|^{2}
satisfies the above inequality trivially.

Corollary
3.3
states that NAM can accelerate constant-stepsize SGD under the growth condition when
\delta<1
, by improving the dependence on the condition number in the rate from
1/\kappa
to
1/\sqrt{\kappa}
. To our knowledge, the recursion (
21
) is the first result that shows NAM can tolerate a (mild) multiplicative noise. Compared to the existing analyses
[
17
,
2
,
3
]
that did not include the extra term
-\left\{\eta(1-\mathcal{L}\eta)/2\right\}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}
in the recursion to tolerate the multiplicative noise, our improvement is achieved by carefully designing the potential function and conducting a sharper analysis.

It is worth mentioning that Corollary
3.3
does not contradict observations in
[
25
]
, where the authors constructed a LSR problem on which NAM cannot outperform SGD. For most LSR problems with Gaussian designs, as in
[

, we have

, which contradicts assumptions of Corollary

3.2
RMM under the growth condition

We study the convergence of the robust momentum method (RMM) proposed in
[
10

, which has the following updating scheme

\displaystyle x_{k}

\displaystyle=y_{k}+\frac{\beta}{\eta}z_{k},

(32a)

\displaystyle z_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\beta z_{k}-\frac{\eta}{\mathcal{L}}g_{k},

(32b)

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=y_{k}+z_{k+1}.

(32c)

The work
[

proved that the deterministic RMM has linear convergence for strongly convex objectives, and empirically showed that RMM is robust to the noise satisfying
\|\varepsilon_{k}\|\leq\delta\|\nabla f_{k}\|
with small
\delta

7

3.1
and Corollary
3.3

Compared to deterministic analysis of RMM in

states that RMM can accelerate constant-stepsize SGD under the growth condition when

, by improving the dependence on the condition number in the rate from

to

. Our result strongly coincides with the empirical observation in

, where the authors observed that

and

An interesting observation is that we use two different sets of parameters for
\delta=0
and

to let

be as small as possible. In particular, when

, we obtain

[

. However, when

when

and

. The inconsistency comes from the extra parameter

We further illustrate the inconsistent setups of

and

as follows. Consider offsetting the multiplicative noise with

. By (

\nu g(\theta)\coloneqq\nu\left(1-2\theta+\frac{\theta^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\right)\geq(1-\nu+\eta+\beta)\delta.

(a)

When

•

If

3.3
Comparison of SGD, NAM, and RMM

We have shown that SGD, NAM, and RMM all converge under the growth condition, with the rates that are the same as theirs under the OUBV condition. However, the methods allow different levels of
\delta
. Table
3.3
summarizes the error recursions of (constant-parameters) SGD, NAM, and RMM. Note that the rates in Table
3.3
are consistent with the convergence rates of the iterates, but different methods have different potential functions
V_{k}
.

NAM and RMM are two accelerated methods that use different momentum designs. NAM uses the same parameter
\beta
of momentum
y_{k}-y_{k-1}
for
x_{k}
and
y_{k+1}
; while RMM employs distinct parameters
\beta/\eta
and
\beta
of momentum
y_{k}-y_{k-1}
for
x_{k}
and
y_{k+1}
, respectively. Both methods achieve the accelerated rate
1-\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\kappa})
for small
\delta
. The rate of SGD holds for any
\delta\geq 0
, but is not optimal in terms of
\kappa
. Since
\sqrt{(1-\delta)/(1+\delta)}<1/(1+\delta)
, the rate of NAM slows down faster than that of SGD as
\delta
increases. When
\delta=0
, RMM has a better rate than NAM: the former is
(1-1/\sqrt{\kappa})^{2}
, while the latter is
1-1/\sqrt{\kappa}
. However, when
\delta\in(0,1/4)
, NAM has a better rate than RMM since
1/\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{2\delta}<\sqrt{(1-\delta)/(1+\delta)}
.

When
\sigma^{2}=0
,
[
10
]
empirically showed that RMM is robust to small multiplicative noise while NAM is less robust. However, as shown in Table
3.3
, the variance of RMM is the worst among the three methods. Together with the observation in
[
10
]
, we reasonably believe that RMM is more suitable for over-parameterized models. In addition, the experiments of
[
10
]
did not choose algorithm’s parameters such as
\beta,\eta
based on the noise level
\delta
; while Corollaries
3.3
and
3.7
suggest to adjust parameters to mitigate multiplicative noise and obtain better convergence behavior. Thus, our two papers conduct the comparisons in different senses. They fixed parameters setup, and compared the performance of the setup on different
\delta
; while we fix
\delta
and set parameters accordingly, and compare the performance of different methods with optimal parameters.

We examine the dual averaging-based methods in the next section, which have received less attention in the literature on stochastic analysis. Our analysis of the dual averaging methods follows the same structure as the present section, where we first establish an error recursion in the form of (
9
), and then specify proper algorithm’s parameters to attain the accelerated rate. We note that the unified form of recursion enables us to examine different bias-variance trade-offs of all the methods and facilitates the comparison of their robustness ability to the multiplicative noise (see
Section

4.3
).

Lemma 4.1

We further present an analytical framework used in this section.

Analysis framework.

The accelerated DAMs are often studied based on the duality gap technique, developed in
[
12
]
. Different from the IQC technique used in
Section

3
, the duality gap technique focuses on the function value convergence by constructing upper and lower bounds of
f
, and allows varying parameters. There are three main steps:

Step 1
:
given an
output iterate

y_{k}
of the algorithm, we let the upper bound be
U_{k}=f(y_{k})
, while we construct a suitable lower bound
L_{k}
of
f(x_{\star})
, i.e.,
f(x_{\star})\geq L_{k}
. Thus, the gap
V_{k}=U_{k}-L_{k}
satisfies
f(y_{k})-f(x_{\star})\leq V_{k}
.

Step 2
: given the sequence
A_{k}
(recall the definition of
A_{k}
in (
39
)), we establish the upper bound of
A_{k}U_{k+1}-A_{k-1}U_{k}\leq\text{UpperBound}
and the lower bound of
A_{k}L_{k+1}-A_{k-1}L_{k}\geq\text{LowerBound}
.

Step 3
: Combining the above two steps, we get

A_{k}V_{k+1}-A_{k-1}V_{k}=(A_{k}U_{k+1}-A_{k-1}U_{k})-(A_{k}L_{k+1}-A_{k-1}L_{k})\leq\text{UpperBound}-\text{LowerBound},

which leads to a recursion
f(y_{k+1})-f(x_{\star})\leq V_{k+1}\leq V_{k}A_{k-1}/A_{k}+(\text{UpperBound}-\text{LowerBound})/A_{k}
; and the convergence rate is
A_{k-1}/A_{k}=1-a_{k}/A_{k}
.

We emphasize that we aim at bounding
f(y_{k})-f(x_{\star})
rather than
f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})
in the following presentation, where
y_{k}
is also the algorithm variable and usually regarded as the algorithm output. We take DAM+ as an example and show the details of three steps, while we only present the result of iDAM+ for conciseness and defer its proofs to Appendix
B
.

4.1

DAM+ combines the SGD and DAM

(43a)

\displaystyle z_{k+1}

\displaystyle=z_{k}-a_{k}g_{k},

(43b)

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}-\eta_{k}g_{k}.

(43c)

We see that

\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z)=\frac{z+\mathcal{m}\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}x_{i}}{A_{k}\mathcal{m}}=\frac{z+\mathcal{m}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}x_{i}}{m_{k}}+\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}x_{k},\quad\text{ where }\;m_{k}\coloneqq A_{k}\mathcal{m}.

Since

. The similar modification replacing

by
\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k})
is adopted in

to improve the factor in the convergence bound of DAM+.

We mention that DAM+ is a popular algorithm, and its formulation in different papers is slightly different. Our formulation is adapted from
[

, (ASC)]
and
[

, (AGD)]
, while similar formulations without involving
\phi_{k}^{\star}
can be found in
[

,

in (

[

Proposition 4.3

Consider the following two schemes for

:

\begin{split}x_{k}=\frac{A_{k-1}}{A_{k}}y_{k}+\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}\nabla\phi_{k-1}^{\star}(z_{k}),\ \ \ z_{k+1}=z_{k}-a_{k}g_{k},\ \ \ y_{k+1}=x_{k}-\eta_{k}g_{k},\end{split}

\begin{split}x_{k}^{\prime}=\frac{A_{k-1}}{A_{k}}y_{k}^{\prime}+\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}z^{\prime}_{k},\ \ \ z^{\prime}_{k+1}=\frac{\mathcal{m}a_{k}}{m_{k}}x_{k}^{\prime}+\frac{m_{k-1}}{m_{k}}z^{\prime}_{k}-\frac{a_{k}}{m_{k}}g_{k},\ \ \ y_{k+1}^{\prime}=x_{k}^{\prime}-\eta_{k}g_{k},\end{split}

where

. For consistency, we let

. If

where
\nabla\phi_{j}^{\star}(\cdot)
is defined in (

Proof 4.4

We prove the result by induction. Letting

in (

\displaystyle z^{\prime}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{m}a_{k}}{m_{k}}x_{k}^{\prime}-\frac{a_{k}}{m_{k}}g_{k}+\frac{m_{k-1}}{m_{k}}z^{\prime}_{k}=\frac{\mathcal{m}a_{k}}{m_{k}}x_{k}^{\prime}-\frac{a_{k}}{m_{k}}g_{k}+\frac{m_{k-1}}{m_{k}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{m}a_{k-1}}{m_{k-1}}x_{k-1}^{\prime}-\frac{a_{k-1}}{m_{k-1}}g_{k-1}+\frac{m_{k-2}}{m_{k-1}}z^{\prime}_{k-1}\right)

Theorem 4.7

with constants

,
\sigma^{2}

Proof 4.8

Thus,

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k+1})-\phi_{k-1}^{\star}(z_{k})

\displaystyle-(z_{k+1}-z_{k})^{\top}\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{npequ:9}}}{{\leq}}\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k+1})-\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k})-(z_{k+1}-z_{k})^{\top}\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eq:Bregman-divergence}}}}{{=}}\;\Delta_{\phi_{k}^{\star}}(z_{k+1},z_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma }~\ref{lemma:BD}(b)}}{{=}}\Delta_{\phi_{k}}(\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k+1}),\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m_{k}}{2}\|\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k+1})-\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k})\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:closed-form},\eqref{eq:ADAb}}}{{=}}\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2m_{k}}\|g_{k}\|^{2},

(51)

where
\Delta_{f}(x,y)
is defined in (
10

f(y_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:class}}}{{\leq}}\nabla f_{k}^{\top}(y_{k+1}-x_{k})+\frac{\mathcal{L}}{2}\|y_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\\[2.0pt]

(52)

Combining the results above, we have

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle V_{k+1}-A_{k-1}V_{k}

\displaystyle=(A_{k}U_{k+1}-A_{k-1}U_{k})-(A_{k}L_{k+1}-A_{k-1}L_{k})

\displaystyle\kern-14.81543pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\text{Lemma }\ref{lemma:ADAM}}}A_{k}(f(y_{k+1})-f(x_{k}))+a_{k}\nabla f_{k}^{\top}(\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k})-x_{k})+a_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}x_{\star}+a_{k}\nabla f_{k}^{\top}x_{k}+\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k+1})-\phi_{k-1}^{\star}(z_{k})

\displaystyle\kern-5.27228pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\eqref{eq:gd}}}A_{k}\left(-\eta_{k}\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}}{2}\right)\|g_{k}\|^{2}+\eta_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\right)+a_{k}\nabla f_{k}^{\top}\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k})+a_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}x_{\star}+\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k+1})-\phi_{k-1}^{\star}(z_{k})

\displaystyle\kern-13.90433pt\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{\eqref{eq:ADA-2}}}-A_{k}\eta_{k}\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}}{2}\right)\|g_{k}\|^{2}+A_{k}\eta_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}g_{k}+a_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}(x_{\star}-\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k}))-(z_{k+1}-z_{k})^{\top}\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k+1})-\phi_{k-1}^{\star}(z_{k})

\displaystyle\kern-9.33069pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\eqref{eq:BD2}}}-A_{k}\eta_{k}\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}}{2}\right)\|g_{k}\|^{2}+A_{k}\eta_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}g_{k}+a_{k}\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}(x_{\star}-\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k}))+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2m_{k}}\|g_{k}\|^{2}.

(53)

Recall that
\mathcal{G}_{k}
is the
\sigma

for (

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}x_{i}]=0

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}g_{i}]=0

\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}\nabla\phi_{k}^{\star}(z_{k})\middle|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:closed-form}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}g_{i}+\mathcal{m}\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}x_{i}\right)/m_{k}\middle|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{pequ:1}}}{{=}}0,\ \ \mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{k}^{\top}g_{k}|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{pequ:1}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\varepsilon_{k}\|^{2}|\mathcal{G}_{k}\right].

Multiplying (
4.8
) by

and taking full expectation on both sides, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V_{k+1}-\frac{A_{k-1}}{A_{k}}V_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq-\left(\eta_{k}\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}}{2}\right)-\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m}}\right)\mathbb{E}\|g_{k}\|^{2}+\eta_{k}\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\kern-7.48619pt\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{\mathclap{\eqref{pequ:1}}}}-\left(\eta_{k}\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}}{2}\right)-\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m}}\right)\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}+\left(\eta_{k}-\eta_{k}\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}}{2}\right)+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m}}\right)\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\left(\eta_{k}\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}}{2}\right)-\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m}}\right)\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}+\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{2}}{2}+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m}}\right)\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\kern-7.35217pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\mathclap{\eqref{eq:GC}}}}-\left(\eta_{k}-\frac{(1+\delta)\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{2}}{2}-\frac{(1+\delta)a_{k}^{2}}{2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m}}\right)\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}+\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{2}}{2}+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m}}\right)\sigma^{2}.

Theorem
4.7
suggests that, under the growth condition, the multiplicative noise affects the convergence of DAM+ through the term
\delta\{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{2}/2+a_{k}^{2}/(2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m})\}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}
, which can be negated by the term
-\{\eta_{k}-\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{2}/2-a_{k}^{2}/(2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m})\}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f_{k}\|^{2}

to let

(i.e., the convergence rate) be as large as possible, we maximize over
\eta_{k}\geq 0
on the right hand side and obtain
\eta_{k}=1/\{(1+\delta)\mathcal{L}\}
. Plugging into the above condition, we require

\frac{(1+\delta)a_{k}^{2}}{2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m}}\leq\frac{1}{2(1+\delta)\mathcal{L}}\Longleftrightarrow\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{(1+\delta)\sqrt{\kappa}}.

Note that any
\delta\geq 0
is allowed here. We summarize the convergence rate in the next corollary.

Corollary 4.9

Consider DAM+ in (
43
) under the growth condition with constants

and
\sigma^{2}
. Under the same definitions in Theorem
4.7

Proof 4.10

By Theorem
4.7
and noting that

\displaystyle\rho_{k}=\frac{A_{k-1}}{A_{k}}=1-\frac{a_{k}}{A_{k}}=1-\frac{1}{(1+\delta)\sqrt{\kappa}}\;\text{ and }\;\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{2}}{2}+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2A_{k}^{2}\mathcal{m}}=\frac{1}{2(1+\delta)^{2}\mathcal{L}}+\frac{1}{2(1+\delta)^{2}\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{(1+\delta)^{2}\mathcal{L}},

we immediately obtain the result.

Since
f(y_{k})-f(x_{\star})\leq V_{k}
, from (
54

\frac{\mathcal{m}}{2}\mathbb{E}\|y_{k}-x_{\star}\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:class}}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}[f(y_{k})-f(x_{\star})]=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{(1+\delta)\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)^{k}+\sigma^{2}\right).

When

. Furthermore, for any

, DAM+ accelerates SGD by improving the dependence on the condition number from

to
1/\sqrt{\kappa}
. This result is significantly better than the results of NAM and RMM, where a small

is required to attain the accelerated rate. Compared to
[

,

,
38
]

[

4.2

The implicit DAM+ is an alternative way to accelerate DAM. The iteration scheme of iDAM+ is

4.3
Comparison of DAM+ and iDAM+

We have studied the convergence of DAM+ and iDAM+ under the growth condition, and have shown that they converge with the rates that are the same as theirs under OUBV condition. Table
4.3
summarizes the results of DAM+ and iDAM+.

DAM+ and iDAM+ leverage different techniques to accelerate DAM and both achieve the accelerated rate
1-\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\kappa})
. In terms of the convergence rate, DAM+ is faster than iDAM+; while in terms of the variance, iDAM+ has smaller variance than DAM+. The rate of DAM+ holds for any
\delta\geq 0
, while the rate of iDAM+ only holds for
\delta\in[0,1)
. On the other hand, the tuning parameters of iDAM+ are the weights
a_{k}
, while the tuning parameters of DAM+ are the weights
a_{k}
and stepsizes
\eta_{k}
, as it is a hybrid of DAM and SGD.

We notice that, to enjoy linear convergence,
a_{k}/A_{k}
of both methods should be constant, that is,
a_{k}
should be proportional to
A_{k-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}
with different multipliers. Thus,
a_{k}
needs to increase exponentially (i.e.
a_{k}=\mathcal{O}(e^{k})
). However, if
a_{k}
increases too fast, it amplifies the noise even when the iterates are close to the minimizer
x_{\star}
, which counteracts the benefit of averaging the past gradients. Indeed, under bounded domain assumption,
[
9
]
also showed that a constant ratio
a_{k}/A_{k}
results in a
\mathcal{O}((1-1/\sqrt{\kappa})^{k})
bias term (i.e. linear rate) and a constant variance term; while if
a_{k}=\mathcal{O}(k^{p})
, then one has
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{p+1})
for bias (i.e. sublinear rate) and
\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{2}/k)
for variance. In other words, slowing down the increase of
a_{k}
decreases the variance term and even makes the variance converge to zero, but decelerates the convergence rate as well.

Combining Tables
3.3
and
4.3
together, we see clearly that all the studied accelerated methods, NAM, RMM, DAM+, and iDAM+, can accelerate SGD under the growth condition, as how they behave under OUBV condition. However, their ability to tolerate the multiplicative noise is different. The accelerated rate of DAM+ holds for any
\delta\geq 0
, while the accelerated rates of the other three methods hold for a moderate
\delta
(e.g.,
\delta<1
). When
\delta=0
(i.e., OUBV condition), RMM enjoys the best bias term with the worst variance term. Furthermore, iDAM+ and NAM enjoy similar bias and variance terms; DAM+ enjoys the same variance term as SGD but uniformly improves the bias term. Our findings are greatly aligned with the existing results on least-squares regressions: NAM and HB fail to accelerate SGD
[
23
,
25
]
while DAM+ can accelerate SGD
[
20
]
. Additionally, our results provably suggest that NAM can tolerate a
mild
multiplicative noise (
\delta<1
), which is somewhat surprising to the existing understanding where people tend to believe NAM is always fragile for the multiplicative noise, based on its performance on LSR
[
25
]
. Our results do not contradict the observations in
[
25
]
since LSR has a large multiplicative noise; see
Section

.

5
Unified Near-optimal Analysis Framework

In this section, we propose a generic scheme for averaging iterates and diminishing parameters for DAM+ and iDAM+. With this scheme, we show that their convergence rates
nearly
match the theoretical lower bound of first-order SA methods under OUBV (or compact domain, which implies OUBV). For convenience, we rewrite (
5
) as

f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})=\Omega\left(\exp\left(-\frac{k}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{k}\right).

(57)

Since an oracle that satisfies OUBV also satisfies the growth condition, this lower bound is also valid under the growth condition.

Let us consider the following recursion

e_{k+1}\leq(1-\gamma_{1}r_{k})e_{k}-\gamma_{2}r_{k}s_{k}+\gamma_{3}r_{k}^{2}

(58)

for a positive sequence
\{e_{k},s_{k},r_{k}\}_{k>0}
and non-negative constants
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
such that
r_{k}\leq 1/\gamma_{4}
and
\gamma_{1}/\gamma_{4}\leq 1
for some
\gamma_{4}
. The recursions established for DAM+ and iDAM+ in
Section

4
can be cast as (
58
). For example, we consider the recursion of DAM+ in Theorem
4.7
. Let
a_{k}^{2}/A_{k}^{2}=\mathcal{m}\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{2}
and restrict
\eta_{k}\leq 1/(2\mathcal{L}(1+\delta))
, then we obtain

\mathbb{E}V_{k+1}\leq(1-\sqrt{\mathcal{m}\mathcal{L}}\eta_{k})\mathbb{E}V_{k}-\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}+\mathcal{L}\sigma^{2}\eta_{k}^{2}.

Comparing the above display with (
58
), we have the correspondence

(e_{k},s_{k},r_{k})=(\mathbb{E}V_{k},\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2},\eta_{k}),\ \ (\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4})=\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{m}\mathcal{L}},1/2,\mathcal{L}\sigma^{2},2\mathcal{L}(1+\delta)\right).

If we choose
r_{k}
to be as large as possible, that is,
r_{k}=1/\gamma_{4}
, then solving (
58
) leads to

e_{k+1}\leq\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{4}}\right)^{k}e_{1}+\frac{\gamma_{3}}{\gamma_{4}\gamma_{1}},

(59)

which shows that the constant rate
r_{k}
implies a linear convergence to a neighborhood of the optimum. The term
\gamma_{3}/(\gamma_{4}\gamma_{1})
corresponds to the variance term of the algorithm that does not vanish when
k
increases. A scheme to diminish the algorithm’s parameters is required to eliminate the constant variance term and achieve the bound in (
57
).

We design the following scheme to diminish
r_{k}
and to average the first
k
iterates with weights
\{w_{k,t}\}_{t\leq k}
: given a tuning parameter
\alpha>0
,

r_{k}=\begin{cases}1/\gamma_{4}&\text{if }\alpha k<\gamma_{4}\\
1/(\gamma_{1}k)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad\quad\quad w_{k,t}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\alpha k<\gamma_{4}\text{ and }t<k\\
1&\text{if }\alpha k<\gamma_{4}\text{ and }t=k\\
0&\text{if }\alpha k\geq\gamma_{4}\text{ and }\alpha t<\gamma_{4}\\
1&\text{if }\alpha k\geq\gamma_{4}\text{ and }\alpha t\geq\gamma_{4}\end{cases}.

(60)

In what follows, we assume
\gamma_{4}/\alpha
is an integer for simplifying our presentation, but
\alpha=\gamma_{1}
is a more natural choice. If
\gamma_{3}=0
, that is
\sigma^{2}=0
, we can simply choose
\alpha=0
and (
60
) recovers the analysis under the over-parameterized regime. The setup (
60
) consists of two phases. In the first phase, we use a constant stepsize to reduce the bias term exponentially, and assign all weights on the current iterate, i.e.
w_{k,t}=0
for
t<k
and
w_{k,t}=1
for
t=k
. Thus, the iteration sequence approaches to a neighborhood of the optimum. In the second phase, we diminish the convergence rate
1-r_{k}
and average the last several iterates to reduce the variance term.

The scheme (
60
) is similar to the
\alpha
-suffix averaging
[
32
]
. Given the total number of iterations
K
and a constant
0<\alpha<1
, the
\alpha
-suffix averaging discards the first
\alpha K
iterates and averages the last
(1-\alpha)K
iterates. The major difference of (
60
) to the
\alpha
-suffix averaging is that (
60
) uses constant parameters for finite
k
iterations until
k=\gamma_{4}/\alpha
, which does not depend on the total number of iterations
K
. In contrast, the
\alpha
-suffix averaging employs constant parameters for
k
iterations until
k=\alpha K
. In other words, when
K
varies, the
\alpha
-suffix averaging results in different schemes to decrease parameters in different stages. Our scheme is consistent regardless of
K
.

The following proposition shows how to nearly achieve (
57
) with the scheme (
60
).

where the second inequality uses

. This completes the proof.

7
Conclusion

Momentum-based accelerated SGD has been successfully applied for training neural networks. However, the theoretical understanding of the acceleration in stochastic setting is less well understood. Under OUBV assumption, different accelerated methods have been proven to enjoy a faster rate than SGD. However, experiments have also shown that some accelerated methods cannot outperform SGD in a variety of problem instances when OUBV assumption fails. Thus, there exists an evident gap between the theory and empirical observations.

Our paper serves as a step towards understanding the behavior of different accelerated methods under a weaker and more realistic condition on the stochastic oracle—the growth condition. The growth condition assumes that the variance of stochastic noise is bounded by two parts: the additive part, which is bounded just like under OUBV assumption, and the multiplicative part, which is proportional to the square of the magnitude of the gradient. Under this weaker condition, we provide a comprehensive investigation of a variety of accelerated methods, including NAM, RMM, DAM+, and iDAM+. We show that all these methods converge under the growth condition with rates that are the same as their rates under OUBV assumption. However, their ability to tolerate noise is different. Among the methods that we studied, DAM+ achieves the accelerated rate for any
\delta\geq 0
, while the other three methods can only tolerate a small
\delta
(e.g.,
\delta<1
). We do not provide a lower bound analysis for NAM, RMM, iDAM+, and, therefore, cannot conclude that these methods do not improve the convergence over SGD when
\delta\geq 1
. However, our results indeed highly coincide with recent empirical observations that illustrate the lack of benefits of these methods over the simple SGD on LSR problems.

One of the limitations of our work is the sharpness of the analysis. Due to the lack of lower bound analysis on
\delta
, it is unclear whether the condition of having a small
\delta
for different methods is necessary for acceleration; and how sharp such a condition is. Considering our results are aligned with experiments in
[
10
]
, we believe that a small
\delta
is necessary for NAM and RMM to accelerate SGD. However, a rigorous lower bound analysis is required and is left for future work. In addition, we plan to extend the growth condition analysis to non-strongly convex and non-convex objectives.
