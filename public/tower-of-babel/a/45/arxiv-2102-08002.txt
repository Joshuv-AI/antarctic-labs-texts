Reversible Random Walks on Dynamic Graphs
Nobutaka Shimizu, Takeharu Shiraga

Abstract
Recently, random walks on dynamic graphs have been studied because of their adaptivity to the time-varying structure of real-world networks. In general, there is a tremendous gap between static and dynamic graph settings for the lazy simple random walk: Although O ⁡ ( n 3 ) O(n^{3}) cover time was shown for any static graphs of n n vertices, there is an edge-changing dynamic graph with an exponential hitting time. On the other hand, previous works indicate that the random walk on a dynamic graph with a time-homogeneous stationary distribution behaves almost identically to that on a static graph. For example, the lazy simple random walk on a dynamic regular graph has an O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) hitting time, which is the same order as that on a static regular graph. In this paper, we strengthen this insight by obtaining general and improved bounds. Specifically, we consider a random walk according to a sequence ( P t ) t ≥ 1 (P_{t})_{t\geq 1} of irreducible and reversible transition matrices such that all P t P_{t} have the same stationary distribution. We bound the mixing, hitting, and cover times in terms of the hitting and relaxation times of the random walk according to the worst fixed P t P_{t} . Moreover, we obtain the first bounds of the hitting and cover times of multiple random walks and the coalescing time on dynamic graphs. These bounds can be seen as an extension of the well-known bounds of random walks on static graphs. Our results generalize the previous upper bounds for specific random walks on dynamic graphs, e.g., lazy simple random walks and d max d_{\max} -lazy walks, and give improved and tight upper bounds in various cases. As an interesting consequence of our generalization, we obtain tight bounds for the lazy Metropolis walk [Nonaka, Ono, Sadakane, and Yamashita, TCS10] on any dynamic graph: O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) mixing time, O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) hitting time, and O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n^{2}\log n) cover time. Additionally, our coalescing time bound implies the consensus time bound of the pull voting on a dynamic graph. To obtain this bound, we establish a duality-like relation of the pull voting process and coalescing random walk on dynamic graphs, which is of independent interest. Keywords : Random walk, Markov chain, dynamic graph

Introduction

A random walk is a fundamental stochastic process on an undirected graph.
A walker starts from a specific vertex of a graph.
At each discrete time step,
a walker moves to a random neighbor.
The probability that the walker moves from
u
to
v
is given by
P(u,v)
,
where the matrix
P\in[0,1]^{V\times V}

is called

Because of their locality, simplicity, and low memory overhead, random walks have a wide range
of applications including network analysis, computational complexity, and distributed algorithms

[

The efficiency of a random walk can be
measured by the rate of
diffusion,
which has been formalized
by several notions including
mixing time
,

and

The mixing time is the
time for the distribution of the walker to converge to some limit distribution (called
stationary distribution
).
The hitting time is the maximum expected time of the walker to visit a target vertex where the maximum is taken over the starting and target vertex.
The cover time is the expected time of the walker to visit all vertices starting from the worst vertex.
The mixing, hitting, and cover times on static graphs have been extensively studied for several decades

Mixing time.

Our first result concerns the mixing time.
Specifically, for a sequence
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
of transition matrices, a positive probability vector
\pi\in(0,1]^{V}
, and a parameter
\epsilon>0
,
we define the
uniform mixing time

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty,\pi)}(\mathcal{P},\epsilon)
by

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty,\pi)}(\mathcal{P},\epsilon)=\min\left\{t\geq 0:\max_{s\geq 0,u,v\in V}\left|\frac{(P_{s+1}P_{s+2}\cdots P_{s+t})(u,v)}{\pi(v)}-1\right|\leq\epsilon\right\}

(1)

and let
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty,\pi)}(\mathcal{P})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty,\pi)}(\mathcal{P},1/2)
.
The uniform mixing time
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty)}(P,\epsilon)
for a static Markov chain
P
can be seen as the mixing time using the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm metric
and has been well studied (see, e.g., Section 4.7 in
[
34
]
).
The intuition behind our
definition of
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty,\pi)}(\mathcal{P},\epsilon)

is that the walk mixes well after
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty,\pi)}(\mathcal{P},\epsilon)
steps
even if the walker starts at any moment.

As a consequence of the previous work ((3.13) in
[
43
]
), we can easily obtain the following uniform mixing time bound.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible, aperiodic, and reversible transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
,

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty,\pi)}(\mathcal{P},\epsilon)=O\left(t_{\mathrm{REL}}(\mathcal{P})\log(\pi_{\min}^{-1}\epsilon^{-1})\right)
,
where
\pi_{\min}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\min_{v\in V}\pi(v)
.

Proposition

1.1
can be
seen as an extension of the well-known mixing time bound
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty)}(P,\epsilon)\leq\lceil t_{\mathrm{rel}}(P)\log(\pi_{\min}^{-1}\epsilon^{-1})\rceil

for static
P
(Theorem 12.4 in
[

) to the dynamic
\mathcal{P}

Our first result is the extension of the following well-known mixing time bound for static

(Theorem 10.22 in

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty)}(P,1/4)\leq 4t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)+1

(Main result 1)

Meeting time and coalescing time.

Our third result concerns the
meeting time

t_{\mathrm{meet}}(\mathcal{P})
and the
coalescing time

t_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})
of random walks on dynamic graphs.
Consider two independent
random walks according to
the same transition matrix sequence
\mathcal{P}
.
The meeting time
t_{\mathrm{meet}}(\mathcal{P})

is the expected time
for the two walkers
to meet starting from the worst initial positions.
In the coalescing random walk,
we consider
|V|
independent random walks
according to
\mathcal{P}

starting from
|V|
distinct initial positions.
Once two or more walkers gather at the same position,
the walkers are merged into one walker.
The coalescing time
t_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})

is the expected time
for the
|V|
walkers
to merge into one walker.
By definition, we have
t_{\mathrm{meet}}(\mathcal{P})\leq t_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})
in general.

For any irreducible, reversible, and lazy
P
, it is known that
t_{\mathrm{coal}}(P)=O(t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P))
(Theorem 1.4 in
[
40
]
).
Similarly to the hitting time,
we observe an exponential gap
between static and dynamic settings.

Proposition 1.5
.

There is a sequence of irreducible, reversible, and lazy transition matrices
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
satisfying
t_{\mathrm{meet}}(\mathcal{P})=2^{\Omega(n)}
.

Note that
Proposition

1.5
also gives an exponential lower bound of the coalescing time since
t_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})\geq t_{\mathrm{meet}}(\mathcal{P})

The random walk we consider in

is the lazy simple random walk
on the graph sequence presented by
Olshevsky and Tsitsiklis
[
41
]

which has an exponential meeting time.

t_{\mathrm{cov}}^{(k)}(P_{\mathrm{LS}}(G))=\Omega\bigl(\frac{n\log n}{k}\bigr)
holds for any (static)
G
and
k=O(n\log n)

[
42
]
.

d_{\max}
-lazy walk.

Let
d_{\max}=d_{\max}(G)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{v\in V(G)}\deg(v)
denote the maximum degree of
G
.
The transition matrix
P_{\mathrm{DM}}(G)
of the
d_{\max}
-lazy walk on a graph
G
is defined by

P_{\mathrm{DM}}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2d_{\max}}
if
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
,

P_{\mathrm{DM}}(u,u)=1-\frac{\deg(G,u)}{2d_{\max}}
,
and
P_{\mathrm{DM}}(G)(u,v)=0
otherwise.
It is known that
t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{\mathrm{DM}}(G))=O(n^{3})
holds for any
G
(
[
6
,
19
]
).

Note that
P_{\mathrm{DM}}(G)
has the uniform stationary distribution for any
G
since
P_{\mathrm{DM}}(G)
is symmetric.
Hence, for any sequence of graphs
\mathcal{G}=(G_{t})_{t\geq 1}
and
\mathcal{P}=(P_{\mathrm{DM}}(G_{t}))_{t\geq 1}
,
we can apply
Theorems

1.2
,
1.4
and
1.6
.
For example,
t_{\mathrm{hit}}(\mathcal{P})=O(t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P}))=O(n^{3})
holds from
Theorem

1.4
.
This improves the previous
O(n^{3}\log n)
bound in
[
19
]
.

Metropolis walk.

We saw in the previous paragraph
that the
d_{\max}
-lazy walk
has a polynomial cover time
for
any
dynamic graph.
However, as mentioned in
[
6
]
, the
d_{\max}
-lazy random walk requires knowledge of the maximum degree, which is a
global
information of
G_{t}
at each
t\geq 1
.
Regarding this issue,
we consider the lazy
Metropolis walk

of Nonaka, Ono, Sadakane, and Yamashita
[
38
]
.
The transition matrix
P_{\mathrm{LM}}(G)
of the lazy Metropolis walk on a graph
G
is defined by

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{LM}}(G)(u,v)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\max\{\deg(G,u),\deg(G,v)\}}&\text{if $\{u,v\}\in E(G)$},\\
1-\sum_{w\in N(G,u)}\frac{1}{2\max\{\deg(G,u),\deg(G,w)\}}&\text{if $u=v$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3)

Note that
a lazy Metropolis walk
uses
local
degree information around the walker.
For any
G
, Nonaka et al.
[
38
]
showed
t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{\mathrm{LM}}(G))=O(n^{2})
.

Since
the transition matrix of the lazy Metropolis walk
is symmetric, the stationary distribution is uniform
for any underlying graph.
Hence, we can apply
Theorems

1.2
,
1.4
and
1.6
.
Interestingly, our bounds for Metropolis walks in
Table

1

are tight up to a constant factor:
On the (static) cycle graph, the lazy Metropolis walk has
\Omega(n^{2})
mixing time and
\Omega(n^{2})
hitting time
[
2
]
.
On the glitter star graph of
[
38
]
, the lazy Metropolis walk has an
\Omega(n^{2}\log n)
cover time.

Metropolis walks on edge-Markovian graphs.

Our results
concern the sequence of
connected graphs.
Indeed, it is not difficult to see that
Theorems

1.2
,
1.4
and
1.6
also hold if

G_{t}
is connected at least once in every
C
steps for some positive constant
C
.
This setting was already studied in
[
19
]
.
With some additional arguments, we study random walks
on the
edge-Markovian graph

(G_{t})_{t\geq 1}
defined as follows:
Let
p,q\in[0,1]
be parameters and

G_{1}
be an arbitrary fixed graph.
The graph
G_{t+1}
is obtained by adding each
e\in\binom{V}{2}\setminus E(G_{t})
independently with probability
p
and removing each
e\in E(G_{t})
independently with probability
q
.

The model of edge-Markovian graph was introduced by Clementi, Macci, Monti, Pasquale, and Silvestri
[
13
]

as a wide generalization of time-independent dynamic random graphs.
Since then,
several properties
including the
flooding
[
13
,
7
]
,
rumor spreading
[
12
]
, and mixing time
[
11
]

have been investigated.
In this paper, we focus on the Metropolis walk on the edge-Markovian graph
(G_{t})_{t\geq 1}

and obtain the following result.
See
Section

7
for the proof.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
c\geq 1
be arbitrary.
Consider
\mathcal{G}(n,p,q)=(G_{t})_{\geq 0}
the edge-Markovian graph satisfying
\frac{p}{p+q}\geq 32(c+1)\frac{\log n}{n}
and
0<p+q\leq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{\mathrm{LM}}(G_{t}))_{t\geq 1}
.
Then, for any
k\geq 1
,
\mathcal{G}(n,p,q)
satisfies the following with probability
1-n^{-\Omega(1)}
:

t_{\mathrm{hit}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P})=O\left(\frac{\max\{1,q/p\}}{p+q}+\log n+\frac{n}{k}\right)
,

t_{\mathrm{cov}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P})=O\left(\frac{\max\{1,q/p\}}{p+q}+\log n+\frac{n\log n}{k}\right)
, and
t_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})=O\left(\frac{\max\{1,q/p\}}{p+q}+n\right)
.

1.3
Pull voting on dynamic graphs

In the
(weighted) pull voting
,
we consider an
n
-vertex graph
G=(V,E)

where each vertex
v\in V
holds
an opinion
\sigma_{v}\in\Sigma

for a finite set
\Sigma\subseteq\{0,\dots,n-1\}

of possible opinions.
Let
P\in[0,1]^{V\times V}
be a
transition matrix.
At every discrete time step,
each vertex
u
chooses a random neighbor
according to the distribution
P(u,\cdot)

and then
updates
its opinion with the neighbor’s opinion.
The aim of the protocol is to
reach consensus in which
every vertex supports the same opinion.
The
consensus time

\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}

is the time of the process to reach
consensus.

We consider the
pull voting on dynamic graphs
.
Specifically, let

\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a transition matrix sequence.
In the
pull voting according to
\mathcal{P}
,
at the
t
-th round,
vertices perform the one-round pull voting according to
P_{t}
.
We denote by
\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}(\mathcal{P})
the consensus time of the
pull voting according to
\mathcal{P}

and consider
t_{\mathrm{cons}}(\mathcal{P})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}(\mathcal{P})]
.
Combining
Theorem

1.6
and the idea of well-known
duality

between the coalescing random walk and pull voting
[
26
]

with some additional argument,
we prove the following.

Theorem 1.8

(Consensus time)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible, lazy, and reversible transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Then,
t_{\mathrm{cons}}(\mathcal{P})\leq O(t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P}))
.

On the other hand,
the consensus time
can be exponential in general.

Proposition 1.9
.

There is a sequence
(G_{t})_{t\geq 1}
of
n
-vertex connected graphs such that

t_{\mathrm{cons}}(\mathcal{P})=2^{\Omega(n)}

for
\mathcal{P}=(P_{\mathrm{LS}}(G_{t}))_{t\geq 1}
.

Another important question concerning pull voting is the probability that the process finally agrees with a specific opinion
\sigma\in\Sigma
.
Using the
voting martingale
argument (e.g.,
[
17
]
), we obtain the following result.

Proposition 1.10

(Winning probability)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible, lazy, and reversible transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Consider the pull voting over opinion set
\Sigma
according to
\mathcal{P}
.
Then, the process finally agrees with
\sigma\in\Sigma
with probability

\sum_{v\in V_{\sigma}}\pi(v)
,
where
V_{\sigma}
is the set
of vertices initially holding
opinion
\sigma
.

1.4
Proof overview

We overview the proof of the main theorems (
Theorems

1.2
,
1.4
and
1.6
).
Throughout this section, we assume that

\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is a sequence of irreducible and reversible transition matrices in which all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi\in(0,1]^{V}
.
For
f,g\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
, define the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\pi}
as
\langle f,g\rangle_{\pi}=\sum_{v\in V}f(v)g(v)\pi(v)
and the induced norm
\|f\|_{2,\pi}=\sqrt{\sum_{v\in V}f(v)^{2}\pi(v)}
.
Define
\frac{f}{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
by
\left(\frac{f}{\pi}\right)(v)=\frac{f(v)}{\pi(v)}
.
Let
\mathbbm{1}
denote the
|V|
-dimensional all-one vector.

For a vertex
w\in V
, let
D_{w}\in\{0,1\}^{V\times V}
be the diagonal matrix defined by
D_{w}(v,v)=1
if
v\neq w
and
D_{w}(w,w)=0
.
We are interested in
the substochastic matrix
D_{w}PD_{w}
.
Note that
D_{w}PD_{w}
is the matrix obtained by replacing elements of
P(w,\cdot)
and
P(\cdot,w)
with
0
.
It is known that
\rho(D_{w}PD_{w})\leq 1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)}
,
where
\rho(M)
is the spectral
radius of
M

(see
Lemma

A.1
or Section 3.6.5 in
[
2
]
).

Mixing time (
Section

3
).

Let
\mu_{0}\in[0,1]^{V}
be an initial distribution and
\mu_{T}=\mu_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{T}P_{i}
be the distribution of
X_{T}
.
Let
d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{T})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\|\frac{\mu_{T}}{\pi}-\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{2,\pi}
, i.e., the
\ell^{2}
-distance between
\mu_{T}/\pi
and
\mathbbm{1}
.
It is known that
\max_{v\in V}\left|\frac{\mu_{T}(v)}{\pi(v)}-1\right|
can be bounded
in terms of
d^{(2,\pi)}(\cdot)

(see, e.g.,
[
34
,
43
]
or
Eq.

8
).
Henceforth, we focus on bounding
d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{T})
.

The main part of the proof of
Theorem

1.2
is to prove the following inequality:
For any
t\geq 0
and
lazy

\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
,
d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{t+1})^{2}\leq d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{t})^{2}\left(1-\frac{d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{t})^{2}}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right)
holds.
Applying this inequality repeatedly, we obtain
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\infty,\pi)}(\mathcal{P})=O(t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P}))

(see
Lemma

C.4
or
[
46
]
for detail).

The aforementioned inequality comes from a variant of Mihail’s identity (
Lemma

A.4
): For any reversible and
lazy

P
and any probability vector
\mu
,
d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu P)^{2}\leq d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu)^{2}-\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(\mu/\pi)
holds.
Here,
\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v\in V}\pi(u)P(u,v)(f(u)-f(v))^{2}=\langle f,f\rangle_{\pi}-\langle f,Pf\rangle_{\pi}
is the Dirichlet form of
P
and
\pi
.
In
[
46
]
, authors consider a lazy simple random walk and give lower bounds of
\mathcal{E}_{P_{\mathrm{LS}},\pi}(\mu/\pi)
in terms of some graph parameters, e.g.,
\mathcal{E}_{P_{\mathrm{LS}},\pi}(\mu/\pi)\geq d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu)^{4}/n^{2}
for regular graphs.
This lower bound means
d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{t+1})^{2}\leq d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{t})^{2}\left(1-\frac{d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{t})^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)
holds.
Our technical contribution is to generalize the previous lower bound of
\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(\mu/\pi)
for any reversible and lazy
P
in terms of the hitting time (
Lemma

3.2
):
\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(\mu/\pi)\geq d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu)^{4}/t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)
holds.
This implies the desired inequality,
d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{t+1})^{2}\leq d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{t})^{2}\left(1-\frac{d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu_{t})^{2}}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right)
.

The proof of the key lemma (
Lemma

3.2
)
consists of four steps.
First, observe that
\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(f)=\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(-f)
and
\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(f)=\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(f+c\mathbbm{1})
for any
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence,

\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(f)=\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(f_{\max}\mathbbm{1}-f)
holds for
f_{\max}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{v\in V}f(v)
.
Second, let
g\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=f_{\max}\mathbbm{1}-f
.
Note that
g(w)=0
for
w\in V
satisfying
f(w)=f_{\max}
.
Therefore, for any
u,v\in V
, we have

g(v)P(v,u)g(u)=g(v)(D_{w}PD_{w})g(u)

and thus
\langle g,Pg\rangle_{\pi}=\langle g,D_{w}PD_{w}g\rangle_{\pi}
holds.
Hence, we have

\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(g)=\langle g,g\rangle_{\pi}-\langle g,Pg\rangle_{\pi}=\mathcal{E}_{D_{w}PD_{w},\pi}(g)
.
Third, from the known inequality

\langle g,D_{w}PD_{w}g\rangle_{\pi}\leq\rho(D_{w}PD_{w})\|g\|_{2,\pi}^{2}
for the spectral radius (
Lemma

A.2
)
and
\rho(D_{w}PD_{w})\leq 1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)}
(
Lemma

A.1
),
we have
\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(g)=\mathcal{E}_{D_{w}PD_{w},\pi}(g)\geq\frac{\|g\|_{2,\pi}^{2}}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)}
.
Finally, from a carefully calculation, it is not difficult to see that
\|g\|_{2,\pi}^{2}\geq d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu)^{4}
for
f=\mu/\pi
.

Hitting and cover times (
Section

4
).

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the random walk according to
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
.
To obtain an upper bound of the hitting time, it suffices to bound the probability
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\land_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w\}\right]
for any fixed vertex
w
.
Sauerwald and Zanetti
[
46
]

used the conditional expectation approach to bound this probability.
Instead of this strategy, we use the following key lemma.

Lemma 1.11
.

Suppose that
X_{0}
is sampled from
\pi
.
Then, for any vertex
w\in V
and
T\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w\}\right]\leq\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{t})}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{T}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right).

If the walker starts according to the stationary distribution,
Lemma

1.11
immediately gives the bounds of the hitting and cover times:
Since the event
\land_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w\}
means that the walk does not hit
w
until time step
T
, the expected hitting time is upper bounded by
\sum_{T=0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right)^{T}=t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})
.
From the union bound, the probability that the cover time is larger than
2t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})\log n
is upper bounded by
n\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right)^{2t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})\log n}\leq 1/n
.
This argument can be
easily extended to the case of

k
independent random walks starting from positions according to the stationary distribution; the
expected hitting time is bounded
by
\sum_{T=0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right)^{kT}=O(t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})/k)
.

Lemma

1.11
and the bounds of the separation distance (
Theorem

1.2
) enable us to obtain upper bounds of the hitting and cover times of
k
walkers from the
worst
initial positions.
Here is the proof sketch.
Let
(X_{t}(1),\dots,X_{t}(k))_{t\geq 0}
be
k
independent walks starting from the worst initial positions.
By definition of the separation distance
Eq.

2
, the probability that the walker
i\in[k]
is on
u\in V
at time
t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})
is
P_{[1,t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})]}(X_{0}(i),u)\geq\frac{1}{2}\pi(u)
.
Therefore, in expectation, half of the walkers are distributed according to
\pi
after
t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})
steps.
From
Lemma

1.11
, the probability that all of such walkers do not hit a specific vertex
w
within
T
steps is at most roughly

(1-1/t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P}))^{Tk/2}
.
Taking
T=O(t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})/k)
, this probability can be bounded by some constant probability.
This implies
t_{\mathrm{hit}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P})=O(t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})+t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})/k)
(see
Lemma

B.1
for detail).
The proof for the cover time proceeds in a similar way: From the union bound over
w\in V
, the probability that there is a vertex
w
such that all the
\pi
-distributed walkers do not hit
w
within
T
steps is at most roughly

n(1-1/t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P}))^{Tk/2}
.

The proof of
Lemma

1.11
goes as follows.
For a fixed
w\in V
,
consider the sequence of substochastic matrices

(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})_{t\geq 1}
.
By definition of
D_{w}
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w\}\right]=\sum_{x,y\in V}\pi(x)\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})\right)(x,y)\leq\left\|\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})\right)\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{2,\pi}
.
Furthermore, from the reversibility of
P_{t}
, all
D_{w}P_{t}D_{w}
are reversible. Thus we can apply a variant
of the Courant–Fischer theorem for the
\pi
-inner product (
Lemma

A.2
) repeatedly and obtain
\left\|\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})\right)\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{2,\pi}\leq\prod_{t=1}^{T}\rho(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})
.
Since
\rho(D_{w}PD_{w})\leq 1-1/t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)
(
Lemma

A.1
),
we obtain
Lemma

1.11
.

Meeting and coalescing times (
Section

5
).

To give an upper bound of the coalescing time, we recall the powerful
Meeting Time Lemma
given by Oliveira
[
39
]
(for continuous-time walks) and Oliveira and Peres
[
40
]
(for discrete-time lazy walks).
These are originally results for
time-homogeneous random walks.
Our key observation is that Meeting Time Lemma indeed holds for
time-inhomogeneous random walks with the same stationary distribution.
Suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is lazy (Note that
Lemma

1.11
does not need the laziness assumption).

Lemma 1.12
.

Suppose that
X_{0}
is sampled from
\pi
.
Then, for any sequence of vertices
(w_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w_{t}\}\right]\leq\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{t})}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{T}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right).

Suppose that the initial positions of two independent random walks
(X_{t}(1))_{t\geq 0}
and
(X_{t}(2))_{t\geq 0}
are according to the stationary distribution.

Lemma

1.12
gives an upper bound of the probability that two walkers do not meet in the dynamic setting:
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\land_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}(1)\neq X_{t}(2)\}\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{T}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right)
, i.e., the expected meeting time is upper bounded by
t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})
.
Note that
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\land_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}(1)\neq X_{t}(2)\}\right]\leq\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\land_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}(1)\neq w_{t})\}\right]
holds for some sequence of vertices
(w_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

The proof of our
O(t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P}))
coalescing time bound essentially consists of three parts.
In the first part, we bound
the probability that
coalescing time is larger than
Ct_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})
for a suitable constant
C>0

in terms of the probability of a suitable event of independent multiple random walks (
Lemma

5.3
).
This part proceeds similarly to the argument of static setting in
[
40
]
by coupling arguments.
In the second part, similar to the arguments of multiple random walks, we show that the positions of an appropriate group of walkers at
t=t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})
are according to the stationary distribution with constant probability.
In the third part,
we apply
Lemma

1.12
to such group of walkers (
Lemma

5.4
). Since
Lemma

1.12
works in the dynamic setting, we can complete this part in a similar way to the static setting
[
40
]
.

In the proof of
Lemma

1.12
, the laziness assumption of walkers is essential as well as that of
[
40
]
.
Since
P
is positive semidefinite by the laziness,
we rewrite

D_{x}PD_{y}=D_{x}\sqrt{P}\sqrt{P}D_{y}
, where
\sqrt{P}
is the square root of
P
.
Furthermore, the reversibility of
P
implies that
D_{x}\sqrt{P}
is the adjoint of
\sqrt{P}D_{x}
(that is,
\pi(u)(D_{x}\sqrt{P})(u,v)=\pi(v)(\sqrt{P}D_{x})(v,u)
for any
u,v\in V
).
Hence, we have
\|D_{x}PD_{y}f\|_{2,\pi}\leq\sqrt{\rho(D_{x}PD_{x})\rho(D_{y}PD_{y})}\|f\|_{2,\pi}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)}\right)\|f\|_{2,\pi}
for any vector
f
(
Lemmas

A.5
and
A.1
).
Combining this inequality and similar arguments in the proof of
Lemma

1.11
, we obtain
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w_{t}\}\right]\leq\left\|\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(D_{w_{t-1}}P_{t}D_{w_{t}})\right)\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{2,\pi}\leq\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{t})}\right)
.

1.5
Related work

Random walk on a static graph.

Consider a simple random walk on a connected graph
G
of
n
vertices and
m
edges.
Aleliunas, Karp, Lipton, Lovász, and Rackoff
[
3
]
showed that
the cover time is at most

2m(n-1)
. Kahn, Linial, Nisan, and Saks
[
30
]
showed that the cover time is at most
16mn/d_{\min}
,
while
the hitting time is at least
(1/2)m/d_{\min}
(see Corollary 3.3 of Lovász
[
35
]
).
Brightwell and Winkler
[
9
]
presented the lollipop graph on which the hitting time is approximately
(4/27)n^{3}
as
n
increases,
while
Feige
[
23
]
gave proved that the cover time is
(4/27)n^{3}+O(n^{5/2})

for any graph.
In addition to the trivial relation of

Notations and definitions

This section defines the mixing, hitting, cover, meeting, and coalescing times formally.
For
b\geq a\geq 1
and a sequence
(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
of transition matrices, let
P_{[a,b]}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=P_{a}P_{a+1}\cdots P_{b}
.
For
\pi\in[0,1]^{V}
, let
\pi_{\min}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\min_{v\in V}\pi(v)
.

For
p\geq 1
and probability vectors
\mu\in[0,1]^{V}
and
\pi\in(0,1]^{V}
, let

\displaystyle d^{(p,\pi)}(\mu)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{cases}\left\|\frac{\mu}{\pi}-\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{p,\pi}=\left(\sum_{v\in V}\pi(v)\left|\frac{\mu(v)}{\pi(v)}-1\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}&\text{if }1\leq p<\infty,\\
\max_{v\in V}\left|\frac{\mu(v)}{\pi(v)}-1\right|&\text{if }p=\infty\end{cases}

be the
\ell^{p}
-distance between
\mu/\pi
and
\mathbbm{1}
.
It is known that
d^{(p,\pi)}(\mu)\leq d^{(p+1,\pi)}(\mu)
holds for any
p\geq 1
(see, e.g., Section 4.7 in
[
34
]
).
For example,
\sum_{v\in V}|\mu(v)-\pi(v)|=d^{(1,\pi)}(\mu)\leq d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu)\leq d^{(\infty,\pi)}(\mu)
.
For
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
, a probability vector
\pi\in(0,1]^{V}
, and
\epsilon>0
, we define the
\ell^{p}
-mixing time as

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(p,\pi)}(\mathcal{P},\epsilon)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\min\left\{t\geq 0:\max_{s\geq 1,v\in V}d^{(p,\pi)}\left(P_{[s+1,s+t]}(v,\cdotp)\right)\leq\epsilon\right\}.

Write
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(p,\pi)}(\mathcal{P})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(p,\pi)}(\mathcal{P},1/2)
.

Consider
k
independent random walks
(X_{t}(1))_{t\geq 0},\ldots,(X_{t}(k))_{t\geq 0}
, where each walk is according to
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
.
Let
\tau_{\mathrm{hit}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P},w)
(for
w\in V
) and
\tau_{\mathrm{cov}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P})
be the
random variables denoting hitting and cover times of the
k
random walks, respectively.
Formally,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{hit}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P},w)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\bigcup_{i\in[k],0\leq s\leq t}\{X_{s}(i)\}\ni w\right\},

(4)

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{cov}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\bigcup_{i\in[k],0\leq s\leq t}\{X_{s}(i)\}=V\right\}.

(5)

Let
t_{\mathrm{hit}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{x\in V^{k},w\in V}\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{hit}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P},w)\middle|X_{0}=x\right]
for the expected hitting time of
k
random walks.
Here,
X_{t}=(X_{t}(1),\ldots,X_{t}(k))\in V^{k}
is a vector-valued random variable.
Similarly, the expected cover time of
k
random walks is defined by
t_{\mathrm{cov}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{x\in V^{k}}\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{cov}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P})\middle|X_{0}=x\right]
.
In particular, let
t_{\mathrm{hit}}(\mathcal{P})=t_{\mathrm{hit}}^{(1)}(\mathcal{P})
and
t_{\mathrm{cov}}(\mathcal{P})=t_{\mathrm{cov}}^{(1)}(\mathcal{P})
.

Let
(X_{t}(1))_{t\geq 0}
and
(X_{t}(2))_{t\geq 0}
be two independent random walks, where each walker is according to
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
.
Write
X_{t}=(X_{t}(1),X_{t}(2))\in V^{2}
.
Then, let
\tau_{\mathrm{meet}}(\mathcal{P})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\min\{t\geq 0:X_{t}(1)=X_{t}(2)\}
and define the meeting time of
\mathcal{P}
as
t_{\mathrm{meet}}(\mathcal{P})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{x\in V^{2}}\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{meet}}(\mathcal{P})\middle|X_{0}=x\right]
.

Let
(\mathrm{C}_{t}(1))_{t\geq 0},(\mathrm{C}_{t}(2))_{t\geq 0},\ldots,(\mathrm{C}_{t}(n))_{t\geq 0}
denote the coalescing random walks according to
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
.
In the coalescing random walks, once two or more walkers meet at the same vertex, they merge into one walker.
Formally, from a given initial state
\mathrm{C}_{0}=(\mathrm{C}_{0}(1),\ldots,\mathrm{C}_{0}(n))\in V^{n}
, we inductively determine
\mathrm{C}_{t}(a)
for each
t\geq 1
and
a\in[n]
, as follows.
Suppose that
\mathrm{C}_{t-1}=(\mathrm{C}_{t-1}(1),\ldots,\mathrm{C}_{t-1}(n))
and
\mathrm{C}_{t}(1),\ldots,\mathrm{C}_{t}(a-1)
are determined.
If there is some
b<a
such that
\mathrm{C}_{t-1}(a)=\mathrm{C}_{t-1}(b)
, let
\mathrm{C}_{t}(a)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathrm{C}_{t}(b)
.
Otherwise,
\mathrm{C}_{t}(a)
is determined by the random walk according to
P_{t}
, i.e.,
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathrm{C}_{t}(a)=v|\mathrm{C}_{t-1}(a)=u]=P_{t}(u,v)
for
u,v\in V
.
For
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in V^{n}
, let
S(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{x_{i}\}
(e.g.,
S(x)=\{a,b\}
for
x=(a,a,b)
).
Then, let
\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})=\min\{t\geq 0:|S(\mathrm{C}_{t})|=1\}

and define the coalescing time of
\mathcal{P}
as
t_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{x\in V^{n}}\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})|\mathrm{C}_{0}=x]
.

3
Mixing time

In this section, we show the following lemma, which immediately yields
Theorem

1.2
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible, reversible, and lazy transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Then, for any
u,v\in V
and any
0<\epsilon<1
,

\left|\frac{P_{[1,2T]}(u,v)}{\pi(v)}-1\right|\leq\epsilon^{2}
holds if

T\geq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}}{\mathrm{e}-1}t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})+2\log(4t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P}))+1+t_{\mathrm{REL}}(\mathcal{P})\log\epsilon^{-1}
.

To show
Lemma

3.1
, we introduce some terminology.
Let
\pi\in(0,1]^{V}
be a positive probability distribution and
f\in\mathbbm{R}^{V}
be a vector.
Then, let
\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{v\in V}\pi(v)f(v)=\langle f,\mathbbm{1}\rangle_{\pi}

and
\mathop{\mathbf{Var}}_{\pi}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{v\in V}\pi(v)(f(v)-\mathop{\mathbf{E}}_{\pi}(f))^{2}=\langle f,f\rangle_{\pi}-\langle f,\mathbbm{1}\rangle_{\pi}^{2}
.
Note that, for any probability vector
\mu\in[0,1]^{V}
, we have

\displaystyle d^{(2,\pi)}(\mu)^{2}=\sum_{v\in V}\pi(v)\left(\frac{\mu(v)}{\pi(v)}-1\right)^{2}=\sum_{v\in V}\pi(v)\left(\frac{\mu(v)}{\pi(v)}\right)^{2}-1=\mathop{\mathbf{Var}}_{\pi}\left(\frac{\mu}{\pi}\right).

For a transition matrix
P
such that
\pi(u)P(u,v)=\pi(v)P(v,u)
holds for all
u,v\in V
,
let

\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle f,f\rangle_{\pi}-\langle f,Pf\rangle_{\pi}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v\in V}\pi(u)P(u,v)(f(u)-f(v))^{2}

be the
Dirichlet form
.

3.1
Key lemma

The following key lemma connects the Dirichlet form,
\ell^{2}
-distance, and hitting time.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible, reversible, and lazy transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Then, for any probability vector
\mu\in[0,1]^{V}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}\left(\frac{\mu}{\pi}\right)\geq\frac{\mathop{\mathbf{Var}}_{\pi}\left(\frac{\mu}{\pi}\right)^{2}}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)}.

Proof.

Write
f=\frac{\mu}{\pi}
and
let
f_{\max}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max_{v\in V}f(v)
and
g\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=f_{\max}\mathbbm{1}-f
.
Since
f(u)-f(v)=g(v)-g(u)
for any
u,v\in V
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v\in V}\pi(u)P(u,v)(f(u)-f(v))^{2}=\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(g)=\langle g,g\rangle_{\pi}-\langle Pg,g\rangle_{\pi}.

Let
v_{\max}
denote a vertex satisfying
f(v_{\max})=f_{\max}
.
Recall that
D_{w}\in\{0,1\}^{V\times V}
is a diagonal matrix where
D_{w}(v,v)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbbm{1}_{v\neq w}
for all
v\in V
.
From
Lemmas

A.2
and
A.1
, we have

\displaystyle\langle Pg,g\rangle_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\langle D_{v_{\max}}PD_{v_{\max}}g,g\rangle_{\pi}\leq\rho(D_{v_{\max}}PD_{v_{\max}})\langle g,g\rangle_{\pi}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)}\right)\langle g,g\rangle_{\pi}.

Note that we have
g(u)P(u,v)g(v)=g(u)(D_{v_{\max}}PD_{v_{\max}})(u,v)g(v)
holds for any
u,v\in V
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\langle g,g\rangle_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\pi(v)(f_{\max}-f(v))^{2}=\sum_{v\in V}\pi(v)(f_{\max}-1+1-f(v))^{2}

\displaystyle=(f_{\max}-1)^{2}+\sum_{v\in V}\pi(v)(1-f(v))^{2}\geq\mathop{\mathbf{Var}}_{\pi}\left(f\right)^{2}+\mathop{\mathbf{Var}}_{\pi}\left(f\right).

The last inequality follows from
0\leq\mathop{\mathbf{Var}}_{\pi}\left(f\right)=\sum_{v\in V}\pi(v)\left(\frac{\mu(v)}{\pi(v)}\right)^{2}-1\leq f_{\max}-1
.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P,\pi}(f)

\displaystyle=\langle g,g\rangle_{\pi}-\langle Pg,g\rangle_{\pi}\geq\frac{\langle g,g\rangle_{\pi}}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)}\geq\frac{\mathop{\mathbf{Var}}_{\pi}\left(f\right)^{2}}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P)}.

∎

4.1
Key lemma

We prove the key result

.

Lemma 4.3

(Reminder of
Lemma

1.11
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible and reversible transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a random walk according to
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
.
Suppose that
X_{0}
is sampled from
\pi
.
Then, for any
w\in V
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w\}\right]\leq\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{t})}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{T}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right).

Proof.

Recall
D_{w}\in\{0,1\}^{V\times V}
is a diagonal matrix defined by
D_{w}(x,x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbbm{1}_{x\neq w}
.
From the definition of
D_{w}
, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w\},X_{T}=y\middle|X_{0}=x\right]=(D_{w}P_{1}D_{w}P_{2}D_{w}\cdots D_{w}P_{T}D_{w})(x,y)

holds for any
x,y\in V
.
Hence, from the assumption on
X_{0}
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V}\pi(x)\sum_{y\in V}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})\right)(x,y)\leq\left\|\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})\right)\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{2,\pi}.

(9)

From the reversibility of
P_{t}
, we have
\pi(u)(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})(u,v)=\pi(v)(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})(v,u)
for any
u,v\in V
and
t\geq 1
.
Hence, we can apply
Lemma

A.2
repeatedly to
Eq.

9
and obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})\right)\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{2,\pi}\leq\rho(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})\left\|\left(\prod_{t=2}^{T}(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})\right)\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{2,\pi}\leq\cdots\leq\prod_{t=1}^{T}\rho(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w}).

Then, using
Lemma

A.1
,
\rho(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})\leq 1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{t})}
holds for all
t
.
Thus, we obtain the claim.
Note that all
P_{t}
are irreducible by assumption.
∎

4.2
Upper bound of hitting time

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible and reversible transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Then, for any
k
,
x\in V^{k}
and
t\geq 0
,
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{hit}}^{(k)}(w)\geq t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})+\frac{20t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}{k}+t\middle|X_{t}=x\right]<0.9.

Proof.

Let
T\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})=t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P},1/2)
,
where
t_{\mathrm{sep}}
is the separation time defined by
Eq.

2
.
Then, the matrix
P_{[1,T]}=\prod_{t=1}^{T}P_{t}
can be written as

\displaystyle P_{[1,T]}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}\pi(u)+\frac{1}{2}Q(u,v)

for some transition matrix
Q\in[0,1]^{V\times V}
.
Thus, the position of a walker after a transition according to
P_{[1,T]}

has the same distribution as
the following transition:
The walker flips a fair coin.
If it is head, the walker moves according to the stationary distribution
\pi
.
Otherwise, the next position of the walker at vertex
u\in V
is determined by the distribution
Q(u,\cdot)
.

Suppose
k
independent walkers
flip their own coins
and then move to the position

X_{T}\in V^{k}
according to the transition probability
P_{[1,T]}
.
Let
S\subseteq[k]
be the random subset of indices of walkers
with a head coin.
Then, the distribution of
X_{T}(i)
conditioned on
i\in S
is
\pi
.
Let
w\in V
be a target vertex.
From
Lemma

4.3
and the independency of the walkers, for any
T^{\prime}\geq 0
and
U\subseteq[k]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in U}\bigwedge_{T\leq t\leq T+T^{\prime}}\{X_{t}(i)\neq w\}\middle|S=U\right]\leq\prod_{i\in U}\prod_{T\leq t\leq T+T^{\prime}}\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{t})}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{|U|T^{\prime}}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right).

(10)

From the Chernoff inequality (
Lemma

C.2
),
we have
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[|S|<k/4]\leq\exp(-k/16)
(note that
\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[|S|]=k/2
).
For any events
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
,

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}]=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}\land\mathcal{B}]+\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}\land\overline{\mathcal{B}}]\leq\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{B}]+\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\overline{\mathcal{B}}]
holds.
Therefore, setting
T^{\prime}=20t_{\mathrm{HIT}}/k
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau_{\mathrm{hit}}^{(k)}(\mathcal{P})\geq T+T^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in[k]}\bigwedge_{T\leq t\leq T+T^{\prime}}\{X_{t}(i)\neq y\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in S}\bigwedge_{T\leq t\leq T+T^{\prime}}\{X_{t}(i)\neq y\}\middle||S|\geq k/4\right]+\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[|S|<k/4]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{kT^{\prime}}{4t_{\mathrm{HIT}}}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{k}{16}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp(-5)+\exp\left(-\frac{1}{16}\right)<0.95

for any
k\geq 1
.
Since this inequality holds
regardless of the initial position
X_{0}\in V^{k}
,
we obtain the claim.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

Theorem

4.1
follows from
Corollaries

B.2
and
4.4
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible and reversible transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Then, for any
k
,
x\in V^{k}
,
t\geq 0
and every sufficiently large

,
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{cov}}^{(k)}(w)\geq t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})+\frac{20t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})\log n}{k}+t\middle|X_{t}=x\right]<0.95.

From
Eq.

10

and the union bound over the target vertex

,
we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigvee_{w\in V}\bigwedge_{i\in U}\bigwedge_{T\leq t\leq T+T^{\prime}}\{X_{t}(i)\neq w\}\middle|S=U\right]

\displaystyle=n\exp(-5\log n)+\exp\left(-\frac{k}{16}\right)

\displaystyle<0.94+O(n^{-4})<0.95

for any
k\geq 1
and every sufficiently large

.
Since this inequality holds
regardless of the initial position

,
we obtain the claim.
∎

4.2
.

B.2
and

.

5.1
Key lemma

Lemma 5.1

(Meeting Time Lemma on dynamic graphs; Reminder of
Lemma

1.12
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible, reversible, and lazy transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a random walk according to
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
.
Suppose that
X_{0}
is sampled from
\pi
.
Then, for any sequence
w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{T}
of vertices,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w_{t}\}\right]\leq\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{t})}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{T}{t_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}\right).

Proof.

Recall that
D_{w}\in\{0,1\}^{V\times V}
is a diagonal matrix defined by
D_{w}(x,x)=\mathbbm{1}_{x\neq w}
.
In the same
way as the proof of
Lemma

4.3
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w_{t}\},X_{T}=y\middle|X_{0}=x\right]

\displaystyle=(D_{w_{0}}P_{1}D_{w_{1}}P_{2}D_{w_{2}}\cdots D_{w_{T-1}}P_{T}D_{w_{T}})(x,y)

holds for any
x,y\in V
, and hence we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigwedge_{t=0}^{T}\{X_{t}\neq w_{t}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V}\pi(x)\sum_{y\in V}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(D_{w_{t-1}}P_{t}D_{w_{t}})\right)(x,y)\leq\left\|\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(D_{w_{t-1}}P_{t}D_{w_{t}})\right)\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{2,\pi}.

Here, we used the Caushy–Schwarz inequality.
Since
P_{t}
is reversible and lazy,
applying
Lemma

A.5
repeatedly yields

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}(D_{w_{t-1}}P_{t}D_{w_{t}})\right)\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{2,\pi}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\rho(D_{w_{0}}P_{1}D_{w_{0}})\rho(D_{w_{1}}P_{1}D_{w_{1}})}\left\|\left(\prod_{t=2}^{T}(D_{w_{t-1}}P_{t}D_{w_{t}})\right)\mathbbm{1}\right\|_{2,\pi}

\displaystyle\leq\cdots\leq\prod_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\rho(D_{w_{t-1}}P_{t}D_{w_{t-1}})\rho(D_{w_{t}}P_{t}D_{w_{t}})}.

Finally, from
Lemma

A.1
, we have
\rho(D_{w}P_{t}D_{w})\leq 1-\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{t})}
for any
t
.
Thus, we obtain the claim.
Note that
P_{t}
is irreducible for any
t
.
∎

5.2

Consider the coalescing random walks

(\mathrm{C}_{t}(1))_{t\geq 0},(\mathrm{C}_{t}(2))_{t\geq 0},\ldots,(\mathrm{C}_{t}(n))_{t\geq 0}
according to
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}

(see

2

This section is devoted to the proof of

Theorem

1.6
.

Theorem 5.2

(Precise statement of
Theorem

1.6
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible, reversible, and lazy transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Then,
t_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})\leq Ct_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})
holds for some positive constant
C
.

To prove
Theorem

5.2
, we introduce the following notation.
Let
\ell_{x}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\lceil\log_{2}(x)\rceil
and
\ell\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\ell_{n}
.
Let
K
be a suitable constant that will be determined later.
Define
L_{\ell},L_{\ell-1},\ldots,L_{0}
recursively
by
L_{\ell}=t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})
and
L_{i}=L_{i+1}+\left\lceil\frac{Kt_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}{2^{i}}\right\rceil
.
In other words,

L_{i}=t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})+\sum_{j=i}^{\ell-1}\left\lceil\frac{Kt_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}{2^{j}}\right\rceil
and

L_{0}=t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})+\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\left\lceil\frac{Kt_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})}{2^{j}}\right\rceil\leq t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})+\log_{2}(n)+2Kt_{\mathrm{HIT}}(\mathcal{P})
.

The following result states a relation between the coalescing random walk and the independent random walks.
The proof is
essentially based on the argument of
[
39
]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}=((X_{t}(1),\ldots,X_{t}(n)))_{t\geq 0}
be
n
independent random walks, where each walker is according to
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
.
Suppose that
\mathrm{C}_{0}=x
and
X_{0}=x
for an arbitrary initial position of walkers
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in V^{n}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})>L_{0}\right]\leq\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigvee_{a=2}^{n}\bigwedge_{b\in[2^{\ell_{a}}-1]}\bigwedge_{t\in[L_{\ell_{b}+1},L_{\ell_{b}}]}\{X_{t}(b)\neq X_{t}(a)\}\right].

Proof.

First, we define the
random walks with killings

(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}=((Y_{t}(1),\ldots,Y_{t}(n)))_{t\geq 0}
, where a walker is killed when it meets another walker with a smaller index.
Formally,
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
Markov chain on a state space
(V\cup\{\partial\})^{n}
,
where
\partial\not\in V
is a
coffin state
.
Set
Y_{t}(1)=X_{t}(1)
for all
t\geq 0
.
For
t\geq 0
and
a\geq 1
, define
Y_{t}(a)
inductively as follows.
Suppose that
(Y_{s})_{s=0}^{t-1}
and
Y_{t}(1),\ldots,Y_{t}(a-1)
are determined.
Then, define

\displaystyle\tau(a)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\min\{s\geq 0:\textrm{$X_{s}(a)=Y_{s}(b)$ for some $b<a$}\},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{and}

\displaystyle Y_{t}(a)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{cases}X_{t}(a)&\text{if $t<\tau(a)$},\\
\partial&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Next, we define the
random walks with a list of allowed killings

(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}=((Z_{t}(1),\ldots,Z_{t}(n)))_{t\geq 0}
, where
a walker
a\geq 2^{i+1}
is killed when it meets a “killer” walker
2^{i}\leq b<2^{i+1}
during the time period
L_{i+1}<t\leq L_{i}
.

Figure

2
is an overall picture.

Figure 2
:
Random walk with a list of allowed killings (
[
39
]
).

Formally, define the list of allowed killings
\mathcal{A}=(A_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as

A_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\emptyset
for
t\leq L_{\ell}
and

A_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\{(b,a):2^{i}\leq b<2^{i+1},a\geq 2^{i+1}\right\}
for
L_{i+1}<t\leq L_{i}
with
0\leq i<\ell
.
Let
Z_{t}(1)=X_{t}(1)
for all
t\geq 0
.
For
t\geq 0
and
a\geq 1
,
Z_{t}(a)
is inductively defined as follows.
Suppose that
(Z_{s})_{s=0}^{t-1}
and
Z_{t}(1),\ldots,Z_{t}(a-1)
are determined.
Then, let

\displaystyle\tau^{\mathcal{A}}(a)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\min\{s\geq 0:\textrm{$X_{s}(a)=Z_{s}(b)$ for some $(b,a)\in A_{s}$}\},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{and}

\displaystyle Z_{t}(a)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{cases}X_{t}(a)&\text{if $t<\tau^{\mathcal{A}}(a)$,}\\
\partial&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For a vector
x\in(V\cup\{\partial\})^{n}

\mathcal{S}(x)=\{i\in[n]:x(i)\neq\partial\}

Obviously,
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[|S(\mathrm{C}_{t})|\geq z|\mathrm{C}_{0}=x]=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[|\mathcal{S}(Y_{t})|\geq z|X_{0}=x]
holds for any
t\geq 0
,

and

.
Furthermore,
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[|\mathcal{S}(Y_{t})|\geq z|X_{0}=x]\leq\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[|\mathcal{S}(Z_{t})|\geq z|X_{0}=x]

.
To see this, consider using
(\mathrm{C}_{t})_{t\geq 0}

.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})>L_{0}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[|\mathcal{S}(Z_{L_{0}})|\geq 2\right]=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigvee_{a=2}^{n}\{\textrm{Walker $a$ is not killed}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigvee_{a=2}^{n}\bigwedge_{b=1}^{2^{\ell_{a}}-1}\bigwedge_{t=L_{\ell_{b}+1}}^{L_{\ell_{b}}}\{Z_{t}(a)\neq Z_{t}(b)\}\right]=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigvee_{a=2}^{n}\bigwedge_{b=1}^{2^{\ell_{a}}-1}\bigwedge_{t=L_{\ell_{b}+1}}^{L_{\ell_{b}}}\{X_{t}(b)\neq X_{t}(a)\}\right].

∎

Combining
Lemmas

5.3
and
5.1
, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of irreducible, reversible, and lazy transition matrices.
Suppose that all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution
\pi
.
Then, for any
x\in V^{n}
,

\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})>T\middle|\mathrm{C}_{0}=x\right]\leq 1-10^{-5}
holds if
T\geq t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})+80t_{\mathrm{hit}}(\mathcal{P})+\log_{2}(n)
.

Proof.

From the definition
Eq.

2
of the separation time
t_{\mathrm{sep}}^{(\pi)}(\mathcal{P})=L_{\ell}
, there is a transition matrix
Q\in[0,1]^{V\times V}
such that

\displaystyle P_{[1,L_{\ell}]}(x,u)=\frac{1}{2}\pi(u)+\frac{1}{2}Q(x,u)

holds for all
x,u\in V
. Hence, the distribution of
n
walkers
X_{L_{\ell}}(1),\ldots,X_{L_{\ell}}(n)
can be simulated as follows:
Each walker
i\in[n]
flips its own fair coin.
If it is head, the walker’s position
X_{L_{\ell}}(i)
is sampled according to
\pi
. Otherwise, it is sampled according to the distribution
Q(X_{0}(i),\cdotp)
.
Let
I\subseteq[n]
denote a random subset of indices with a head coin.
Let
\mathcal{W}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{W\subseteq[n]:\textrm{$|\{2^{i},\ldots,2^{i+1}-1\}\cap W|\geq 2^{i}/4$ holds for all $0\leq i<\ell$}\}
be a set of subsets of
[n]
.
Then, from
Lemma

5.3
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{coal}}(\mathcal{P})>L_{0}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{W\subseteq[n]}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigvee_{a=2}^{n}\bigwedge_{b\in[2^{\ell_{a}}-1]}\bigwedge_{t\in[L_{\ell_{b}+1},L_{\ell_{b}}]}\{X_{t}(b)\neq X_{t}(a)\}\middle|I=W\right]\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[I=W]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[I\notin\mathcal{W}]+\max_{W\in\mathcal{W}}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\bigvee_{a=2}^{n}\bigwedge_{b\in[2^{\ell_{a}}-1]\cap W}\bigwedge_{t\in[L_{\ell_{b}+1},L_{\ell_{b}}]}\{X_{t}(b)\neq X_{t}(a)\}\middle|\bigwedge_{i\in W}\{X_{L_{\ell}}(i)\sim\pi\}\right].

(11)

Let
I_{j}=\mathbbm{1}_{j\in I}\in\{0,1\}
denote the
binary indicator for the random subset
I
.
For the first term of
Eq.

11
, applying the Chernoff inequality (
Lemma

C.2

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[I\in\mathcal{W}]

Winning probability

We prove

1.10
.
Our proof is based on the
voting martingale argument
that was used to obtain
the winning probability result
for the static graph setting (cf.
[
26
,
17
]
).
We just verify that the argument
works for our dynamic graph setting.
For completeness, we write the proof
in this subsection.

Proof of
Proposition

1.10
.

We first consider the special case of
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
and then go on to the general case
\Sigma\subseteq\{0,\dots,n-1\}
.

The case of
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
.

Let
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}

be the pull voting according to

\mathcal{P}=(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}

with a time-homogeneous
stationary distribution
\pi
.
Note that
Y_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
.
In this proof, we promise that

Y_{t}
is an
n\times 1
vector
and
\pi\in[0,1]^{V}
is a
1\times n
vector.
Let
\pi(Y_{t})=\sum_{u\in V}\pi(u)Y_{t}(u)
.
We first claim that
(\pi(Y_{t}))_{t\geq 0}

is a martingale with respect to
Y_{t}
.
To see this, observe

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[\pi(Y_{t+1})|Y_{t}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in V}\pi(u)\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Y_{t+1}(u)=1|Y_{t}]=\sum_{u\in V}\pi(u)\sum_{w\in V}(P_{t})(u,w)Y_{t}(w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V}(\pi P_{t})(w)Y_{t}(w)=\sum_{w\in V}\pi(w)Y_{t}(w)=\pi(Y_{t}).

Since
\pi(Y_{t})
are bounded,
we can apply the Optimal Stopping Theorem
and obtain
\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[\pi(Y_{\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}})]=\pi(Y_{0})
.
Note that, since
Y_{\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}}
is either all-zero or all-one, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[\pi(Y_{\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}})]

\displaystyle=\pi(\mathbbm{1})\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Y_{\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}}=\mathbbm{1}]=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[Y_{\tau_{\mathrm{cons}}}=\mathbbm{1}].

In other words,
the probability that the opinion
1
wins is equal to
\pi(Y_{0})=\sum_{v\in V:Y_{0}(v)=1}\pi(v)
.

General case.

We reduce the general case to the binary opinion case by
regarding
\sigma=1
for a fixed opinion
\sigma\in\Sigma
and all other opinions are zero.
Then we obtain the claim from the argument for the binary opinion case.

8
Conclusion

We obtain new bounds of the mixing, hitting, and cover times of the random walk according to the sequence of irreducible, reversible, and lazy transition matrices that have the same stationary distribution.
These bounds generalize previous works for a lazy simple random walk or a
d_{\max}
-lazy walk and improve them in various cases.
Furthermore, we obtain the first bounds of the hitting and cover times of multiple random walks and the coalescing time on dynamic graphs.
Additionally, we bound the consensus time of the pull-voting on dynamic graphs.
Our results strengthen the observation that
time inhomogeneous Markov chains
with an invariant stationary distribution
behaves almost identically to
a time-homogeneous chain.
Specifically, we prove that if all
P_{t}
have the same stationary distribution,
then
t_{\mathrm{hit}}\left((P_{t})_{t\geq 1}\right)\leq O\left(\max_{t\geq 1}t_{\mathrm{hit}}(P_{t})\right)
holds (
Theorem

1.4
).
It is natural to ask for the same relation for other parameters.
For example, does
t_{\mathrm{cov}}(\mathcal{P})\leq O\left(\max_{t\geq 1}t_{\mathrm{cov}}(P_{t})\right)
hold?

Most previous works on
time-inhomogeneous random walks have
translated techniques from time-homogeneous chains into
time-inhomogeneous ones:
In particular, several known upper bounds (including ours) are based on
spectral
arguments,
which essentially requires the time-homogeneity of the stationary distribution.
On the other hand, known lower bounds such as the Sisyphus wheel
are based on
some
combinatorial
arguments.
To understand time-inhomogeneous random walks with time-varying stationary distributions,
it might be important to
interpolate the spectral and combinatorial arguments.
The simple random walk on
any static connected graph
has an
O(n^{3})
cover time.
This research question might be a possible future direction of the research of time-inhomogeneous chains.

holds.
Let
\sqrt{P}\in[0,1]^{V\times V}
be the positive semidefinite square root of
P
, i.e.,
\sqrt{P}(v,u)=\pi(u)\sum_{i=1}^{|V|}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}(P)}\psi_{i}(v)\psi_{i}(u)
.
Note that all eigenvalues are nonnegative since
P
is lazy.
It is easy to see that
(\sqrt{P})^{2}=P
and
\pi(v)\sqrt{P}(v,u)=\pi(u)\sqrt{P}(u,v)
holds for any
u,v\in V
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\pi(v)(D_{w}\sqrt{P})(v,u)=\pi(v)D_{w}(v,v)\sqrt{P}(v,u)=\pi(u)D_{w}(v,v)\sqrt{P}(u,v)=\pi(u)(\sqrt{P}D_{w})(u,v),

i.e.,
\langle D_{w}\sqrt{P}f,g\rangle_{\pi}=\langle f,\sqrt{P}D_{w}g\rangle_{\pi}
holds for any
f,g
.
This implies that both
\|D_{w}\sqrt{P}f\|_{2,\pi}

and
\|\sqrt{P}D_{w}f\|_{2,\pi}
are upper bounded by
\sqrt{\rho(D_{w}PD_{w})}\|f\|_{2,\pi}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\left\|D_{x}PD_{y}f\right\|_{2,\pi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|D_{x}\sqrt{P}\sqrt{P}D_{y}f\right\|_{2,\pi}^{2}\leq\rho\left(D_{x}PD_{x}\right)\left\|\sqrt{P}D_{y}f\right\|_{2,\pi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\rho\left(D_{x}PD_{x}\right)\rho\left(D_{y}PD_{y}\right)\left\|f\right\|_{2,\pi}^{2}

holds, and we obtain the claim.
∎

Appendix B
Tools for expected stopping time

Our upper bounds of the hitting, cover, and coalescing times rely on the following observation.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a sequence of
random variables where
Z_{t}\in\mathcal{S}

for a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
.
For an event
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
,
let
\tau(\mathcal{E})=\inf\{t\geq 0:Z_{t}\in\mathcal{E}\}
be the stopping time.
Suppose there exist
T>0
and
c>0
such that,
for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq T+t\mid\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq t]\leq 1-c

holds.
Then,
\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[\tau(\mathcal{E})]\leq\frac{T}{c}
.

Proof.

From the assumption, for any
k\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq kT+t\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq kT+t\mid\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq(k-1)T+t\right]\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq(k-1)T+t\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-c)\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}\left[\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq(k-1)T+t\right]

\displaystyle\dots

\displaystyle\leq(1-c)^{k}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[\tau(\mathcal{E})]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq kT+t]\leq T\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-c)^{k}=\frac{T}{c}.

∎

Corollary B.2
.

Let
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a sequence of
random variables where
Z_{t}\in\mathcal{S}

for a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
.
For an event
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
,
let
\tau(\mathcal{E})=\inf\{t\geq 0:Z_{t}\in\mathcal{E}\}
be the stopping time.
Suppose there exist
T>0
and
c>0
such that,
for any
t\geq 0
and
z\in\mathcal{S}
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq T+t\mid Z_{t}=z]\leq 1-c

holds.
Then,
\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[\tau(\mathcal{E})]\leq\frac{T}{c}
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq T+t\mid\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq t]\leq\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau(\mathcal{E})\geq T+t\mid Z_{t}]\leq 1-c.

∎

To obtain upper bounds for hitting, cover, and coalescing times, it suffices to
prove that the corresponding stopping time
satisfies the condition of
Corollary

B.2
.

Finally, we introduce the following lemma, which we use in the proof of the edge-Markovian graph (
Section

7
).

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\tau
be a stopping time of a sequence of random variables
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
Let
0=T_{0}\leq T_{1}\leq\cdots
be a non-decreasing sequence and

\epsilon
be a positive constant.
Suppose that
\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau>T_{\ell}|\tau>T_{\ell-1}]\leq 1-\epsilon
holds for all
\ell\geq 1
.
Then,
\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[\tau]\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(T_{\ell+1}-T_{\ell})(1-\epsilon)^{\ell}
.

Proof.

From the assumption,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau>T_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau>T_{\ell},\tau>T_{\ell-1}]=\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau>T_{\ell}|\tau>T_{\ell-1}]\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau>T_{\ell-1}]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\epsilon)\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau>T_{\ell-1}]\leq\cdots\leq(1-\epsilon)^{\ell}.

holds for any
\ell\geq 1
.
The first equality follows since
\tau
is a stopping time.
Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{E}}[\tau]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{t=T_{\ell}}^{T_{\ell+1}-1}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau>t]\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{t=T_{\ell}}^{T_{\ell+1}-1}\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}[\tau>T_{\ell}]\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(T_{\ell+1}-T_{\ell})(1-\epsilon)^{\ell}.

∎

(See, e.g., Theorem 13.10 in

34
]
)
.

Let

Lemma C.4

(
[
46
]
)
.

Let
x:\mathbbm{N}\to\mathbbm{R}_{>0}
be a positive non-increasing function.
Suppose that
x(t+1)\leq x(t)\left(1-\frac{x(t)}{K}\right)
holds for any
t\geq 0
.
Then,
x(t)\leq 1
holds for any
t\geq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}}{\mathrm{e}-1}K+\log x(0)+1
.

Proof.

First, we show that
x\left(a+\left\lceil\mathrm{e}K/x(a)\right\rceil\right)\leq x(a)/\mathrm{e}
holds for any
a\geq 0
by contradiction:
Suppose that
x(a+\ell)>x(a)/\mathrm{e}
holds for
\ell=\left\lceil\mathrm{e}K/x(a)\right\rceil
.
Since
x
is non-increasing and
x(t+1)\leq x(t)\left(1-\frac{x(t)}{K}\right)
holds, we have

\displaystyle x(a+\ell)

\displaystyle\leq x(a+\ell-1)\left(1-\frac{x(a+\ell-1)}{K}\right)\leq x(a+\ell-1)\left(1-\frac{x(a+\ell)}{K}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\cdots\leq x(a)\left(1-\frac{x(a+\ell)}{K}\right)^{\ell}\leq x(a)\exp\left(-\frac{\ell x(a+\ell)}{K}\right)

\displaystyle\leq x(a)\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{e}x(a+\ell)}{x(a)}\right)<x(a)/\mathrm{e}.

This contradicts the assumption and we obtain the claim:
x(a+\ell)\leq x(a)/\mathrm{e}
holds.
Now, let
\ell(1)=\left\lceil\frac{\mathrm{e}K}{x(0)}\right\rceil
and
\ell(i)=\left\lceil\frac{\mathrm{e}K}{x(L(i-1))}\right\rceil
for
i\geq 1
, where
L(i)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell(j)
for
i\geq 1
and
L(0)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=0
.
From the above argument, we have
x(L(i+1))\leq x(L(i))/\mathrm{e}
for any
i\geq 0
.
Let
H
be the first number with
x(L(H))\leq 1
.
Since

1<x(L(H-1))\leq x(L(H-2))/\mathrm{e}\leq\cdots\leq x(0)/\mathrm{e}^{H-1}
holds,
we have
H-1<\log x(0)
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle L(H)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{H}\ell(i)=\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}\left\lceil\frac{\mathrm{e}K}{x(L(i))}\right\rceil\leq\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}\left\lceil\frac{\mathrm{e}K}{\mathrm{e}^{H-1-i}x(L(H-1))}\right\rceil\leq\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}\left\lceil\frac{\mathrm{e}K}{\mathrm{e}^{i}}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq H+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}}{\mathrm{e}-1}K\leq\log x(0)+1+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2}}{\mathrm{e}-1}K.

∎
