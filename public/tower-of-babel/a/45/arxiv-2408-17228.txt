A Runge-type approximation theorem for the 3D unsteady Stokes system
Mitsuo Higaki, Franck Sueur

Abstract




Abstract.
We investigate Runge-type approximation theorems for solutions to the 3D unsteady Stokes system. More precisely, we establish that on any compact set with connected complement, local smooth solutions to the 3D unsteady Stokes system can be approximated with an arbitrary small positive error in
L^{\infty}
norm by a global solution of the 3D unsteady Stokes system, where the velocity grows at most exponentially at spatial infinity and the pressure grows polynomially. Additionally, by considering a parasitic solution to the Stokes system, we establish that some growths at infinity are indeed necessary.

1
Introduction and statement of the main results

1.1
Runge-type approximation theorems and the 3D unsteady Stokes system

A classical theorem by Runge
[
Run85
]
states, in particular, that any function which is holomorphic in a neighborhood of a compact set
K
of the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, such that the complementary set
\mathbb{C}\setminus K
is connected, can be uniformly approximated by some polynomials. Actually the full version of Runge’s theorem allows for any domain whose complement is disconnected up to extending the space of approximations to the space of rational functions with at least one pole in each connected component of the complementary.

This result has been the subject of many extensions. First the Mergelyan theorem
[
Mer52
]
in 1952 concerns functions which are only continuous on
K
and holomorphic in the interior of
K
. The result was also extended to the Laplace equations in any dimension by Walsh
[
Wal29
]
in 1929. Then Lax
[
Lax56
]
and Malgrange
[
Mal56
]
proved that for some classes of elliptic linear differential equations in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, solutions in a bounded set
\Omega
can be uniformly approximated by solutions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
provided that
\Omega
has a connected complement in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The classes of admissible elliptic operators, whose main example is the set of operators with analytic coefficients, were subsequently extended by Browder
[
Bro62
]
, including approximation in certain Hölder spaces. Later on some results were obtained for non-elliptic equations; see for example
[
Jon75
,
EGFPS19
,
EPS21
]
.

In this paper, we investigate Runge-type properties of the 3D unsteady Stokes system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}v-\Delta v+\nabla q=0,\\
\nabla\cdot v=0.\end{array}\right.

(S)

That is, the possibility to approximate local solutions of (
S
) by some global solutions. By a local solution, we mean a smooth solution
(v,q)
of (
S
) on some compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
denotes the full space for positive times
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times(0,\infty)
, in the sense that
(v,q)
satisfies (
S
) for some open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
and is smooth in
\mathcal{O}
. On the other hand, by a global solution, we mean a smooth solution
(u,p)
of the Stokes system in
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+\nabla p=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{4}_{+},\\[2.0pt]
\nabla\cdot u=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}.\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

In the sequel an important topological condition on
K
is that the time-sections
K(t)=K\cap(\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\{t\})
, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, have complementary sets
K^{\mathrm{c}}(t)
which are connected.

Our work is carried out in the class of functions without decay in space. The analysis of the unsteady Stokes system in such classes is known to present particular difficulties, with which we face in this paper can be summarized as: (I) Unboundedness of the Riesz operators in the
L^{\infty}
space; (II) Existence of certain exact solutions, called parasitic solutions.

Technical challenges may arise from Point (I). Recall that the Helmholtz projection (see
[
Soh01
]
) in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
involves the Riesz operators. In particular, when proving the global approximation theorems using the
L^{\infty}
norm, it is not clear that simply revisiting the approach in
[
EGFPS19
]
for the parabolic equations will be successful. Somewhat surprisingly, it is figured out in Sections
3.1
–
3.2
that at least in the “sweeping of poles and discretization” argument, which is the classical and first step towards Runge-type theorems, the issues related to Point (I) do not have any effect. This is thanks to off-diagonal nature of the argument. Consequently, we obtain a Runge-type approximation theorem as described in Theorem
A
in the next section, which is in the same vein as
[
EGFPS19
, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2]
.

Difficulties arising from Point (II) are more inherent in this paper. In fact, we point out that, unlike
[
EGFPS19
, Theorem 1.2]
, the approximations in Theorem
A
exhibit growth in space at spatial infinity rather than decay. This relates to the lack of uniqueness of solutions for the Cauchy problem of the Stokes system within the class of functions without decay at infinity. To see the breakdown of uniqueness, we refer to the following exact solutions

v(x,t)=c(t)\nabla h(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad q(x,t)=-c^{\prime}(t)h(x),

(1.2)

where
c(t)
is a function on
(0,\infty)
and
h(x)
is a harmonic function on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The solutions (
1.2
) are called parasitic solutions or Serrin’s examples
[
Ser62
]
. Note that parasitic solutions play a crucial role in the local regularity theory of the (Navier-)Stokes system
[
Ser15
]
since they indicate that the regularity of solutions in time is determined by the pressure, and hence, there is no smoothing in time in general. The presence of parasitic solutions stands in sharp contrast to the situation for the heat equation or, in particular, for
[
EGFPS19
, Theorem 1.2]
, where one can appeal to the uniqueness of solutions with exponential growth in space; see
[
Joh91
, Section 7.1 (b)]
. Furthermore, parasitic solutions provide counterexamples to
[
EGFPS19
, Theorem 1.2]
for the case of the Stokes system, as described in Theorem
B
in the next section. It states that there are parasitic solutions which cannot be approximated by bounded global solutions of the Stokes system. Therefore, some growths at infinity such as (
1.3
) in Theorem
A
are indeed necessary for the Runge-type theorems to hold.

1.2
A positive result with some growth at infinity

Our main result in this paper is the following.

Theorem A

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
be a compact set such that
K^{\mathrm{c}}(t)
is connected for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Given a smooth solution
(v,q)
of the Stokes system (
S
) in
K
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is a smooth solution
(u,p)
of (
1.1
) and positive real numbers
C,c,\rho
such that for all
(x,t)
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}

|u(x,t)|\leq Ce^{c|x|}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|p(x,t)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{\rho},

(1.3)

and

\|v-u\|_{C(K)}<\varepsilon.

(1.4)

The proof of Theorem
A
is given in Section
3
.

Remark 1.1

(i)

The approximation (
1.4
) can be improved in space to, for fixed
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

\displaystyle\|v-u\|_{C^{l}_{x}C_{t}(K)}<\varepsilon.

Indeed, one can apply to
v-u
the regularity theory of the steady Stokes system
[
Ser15
]
at each fixed
t
by regarding
-(\partial_{t}v-\partial_{t}u)
as a given forcing. Improvement in time cannot be expected due to the loss of smoothing implied by parasitic solutions (
1.2
).

(ii)

The statement of Theorem
A
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
for positive times is presented to underline the difference with
[
EGFPS19
, Theorem 1.2]
for the heat equations, although the global approximations
(u,p)
can be actually defined in the whole space-time
\mathbb{R}^{4}
as seen in the proof of Theorem
A
. In
[
EGFPS19
, Theorem 1.2]
, the approximations are expressed as
u=e^{t\Delta}u_{0}
using the heat semigroup
e^{t\Delta}
and a suitably chosen smooth, compactly supported initial data
u_{0}
. We emphasize that the difference stems from the uniqueness of solutions to the Cauchy problems in the class of functions with (exponential) growth in space. More details on this point can be found in Section
3.6
.

1.3
A negative result without any growth at infinity

A complementary result to Theorem
A
, which states that one cannot expect a similar result without some growth of the approximation at infinity, is the following.

Theorem B

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
be a flat compact space-time cylinder. There are a smooth parasitic solution
(v,q)
of the form (
1.2
) and
\varepsilon>0
such that the following holds: if
(u,p)
is a smooth solution of (
1.1
) which is bounded, that is which satisfies, for all
(x,t)
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
,

|u(x,t)|+|p(x,t)|\leq C

(1.5)

for some constant
C
, then

\displaystyle\|v-u\|_{C(K)}>\varepsilon.

The proof of Theorem
B
is given in Section
4
.

Remark 1.2

(i)

For the heat equation,
[
EGFPS19
, Theorem 1.2]
shows that any local solution
v
can be approximated by a global solution
u=e^{t\Delta}u_{0}
to the Cauchy problem with a suitably chosen smooth, compactly supported initial data
u_{0}
. Theorem
B
asserts that the analogue of
[
EGFPS19
, Theorem 1.2]
is not true for the Stokes system.

(ii)

Theorem
B
prevents us from investing the cost problem by a bounded smooth global approximation following what is done in
[
RS19
,
EPS21
]
for some other systems.

(iii)

Noticeably the situation is quite different from Theorem
B
in the case of fractional Fourier operators, in particular regarding the reachable states; see
[
DSV19
]
.

Notation

•

2.2
Spherical coordinates

Let
(r,\theta,\phi)
denote the spherical coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{3}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\begin{matrix}x^{1}\\
x^{2}\\
x^{3}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}r\sin\theta\cos\phi\\
r\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
r\cos\theta\end{matrix}\right),\quad(r,\theta,\phi)\in(0,\infty)\times[0,\pi]\times[0,2\pi)\end{split}

(2.1)

and
\bm{\hat{r}},\bm{\hat{\theta}},\bm{\hat{\phi}}
the unit vectors

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{r}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\sin\theta\cos\phi\\
\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
\cos\theta\end{matrix}\right),\quad\bm{\hat{\theta}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\cos\theta\cos\phi\\
\cos\theta\sin\phi\\
-\sin\theta\end{matrix}\right),\quad\bm{\hat{\phi}}=\left(\begin{matrix}-\sin\phi\\
\cos\phi\\
0\end{matrix}\right).

(2.2)

For a function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we slightly abuse the notation to denote

f(x)=f(r,\theta,\phi)=f(r\sin\theta\cos\phi,r\sin\theta\sin\phi,r\cos\theta).

Let
S_{r}
denote the sphere with radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

S_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}~|~|x|=r\},\quad r\in(0,\infty)

and
S=S_{1}
the unit sphere. For a function
f
on
S
, we denote

f(x)=f(\theta,\phi)=f(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta).

The integral over
S
is defined as

\int_{S}f=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\pi}f(\theta,\phi)\sin\theta\,{\rm d}\theta\bigg)\,{\rm d}\phi.

For

, we write

\langle f,g\rangle_{S}=\int_{S}f\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mug\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\qquad\|f\|_{L^{2}(S)}=\langle f,f\rangle_{S}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Following our notational convention, we define in the same manner the space

for vector fields on
S
as well as the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{S}
on

and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(S)}
.

The Laplace-Beltrami operator on
S
is defined by its action on a scalar field

by

\Delta_{S}f=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\phi^{2}}.

(2.3)

Then the Laplacian of a scalar field

in spherical coordinates is written as

\Delta f=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{S}f.

(2.4)

We also use the following: for a scalar field
f
,

\nabla f=\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\bm{\hat{r}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\bm{\hat{\theta}}+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}\bm{\hat{\phi}}.

(2.5)

For a vector field

, we will use the operators

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta w&=\Big(\Delta w^{r}-\frac{2w^{r}}{r^{2}}-\frac{2}{r^{2}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}(w^{\theta}\sin\theta)-\frac{2}{r^{2}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial w^{\phi}}{\partial\phi}\Big)\bm{\hat{r}}\\
&\quad+\Big(\Delta w^{\theta}-\frac{w^{\theta}}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}+\frac{2}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial w^{r}}{\partial\theta}-\frac{2\cos\theta}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial w^{\phi}}{\partial\phi}\Big)\bm{\hat{\theta}}\\
&\quad+\Big(\Delta w^{\phi}-\frac{w^{\phi}}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}+\frac{2}{r^{2}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial w^{r}}{\partial\phi}+\frac{2\cos\theta}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial w^{\theta}}{\partial\phi}\Big)\bm{\hat{\phi}}\end{split}

(2.6)

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nabla\cdot w&=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(w^{r}r^{2})+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}(w^{\theta}\sin\theta)+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial w^{\phi}}{\partial\phi},\\
\nabla\times w&=\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\Big\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}(w^{\phi}\sin\theta)-\frac{\partial w^{\theta}}{\partial\phi}\Big\}\bm{\hat{r}}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{r}\Big\{\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial w^{r}}{\partial\phi}-\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rw^{\phi})\Big\}\bm{\hat{\theta}}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{r}\Big\{\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rw^{\theta})-\frac{\partial w^{r}}{\partial\theta}\Big\}\bm{\hat{\phi}}.\end{split}

(2.7)

2.3
Scalar/Vector spherical harmonics

Let
Y_{lm}=Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)

denote the (scalar) spherical harmonics on
S
defined by

Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)=\sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}}P_{lm}(\cos\theta)e^{\mathrm{i}m\phi},\quad l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\mkern 9.0mum=0,\pm 1,\ldots,\pm l.

(2.8)

Here
P_{lm}=P_{lm}(y)
is the function defined by

P_{lm}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(-1)^{m}(1-y^{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}\dfrac{\,{\rm d}^{m}}{\,{\rm d}y^{m}}P_{l0}(y)&\mbox{if}\ m\geq 0,\\[10.0pt]
(-1)^{|m|}\dfrac{(l-|m|)!}{(l+|m|)!}P_{l|m|}(y)&\mbox{if}\ m<0\end{array}\right.

associated with the Legendre polynomial

P_{l0}(y)=\frac{1}{2^{l}l!}\dfrac{\,{\rm d}^{l}}{\,{\rm d}y^{l}}(y^{2}-1)^{l}.

(2.9)

The spherical harmonics satisfy the orthogonality relations

\langle Y_{lm},Y_{l^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\rangle_{S}=\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}},

(2.10)

where
\delta_{j,k}
denotes the Kronecker delta.

Lemma 2.1

The following hold.

(1)

For
f\in L^{2}(S)
, we have

\displaystyle f=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\langle f,Y_{lm}\rangle_{S}Y_{lm}.

(2.11)

The series on the right converges in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(S)}
.

(2)

For
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{S}Y_{lm}=-\mu_{l}Y_{lm},\qquad\mu_{l}:=l(l+1).

(2.12)

(3)

For
f\in C^{\infty}(S)
, we have

\displaystyle|\langle f,Y_{lm}\rangle_{S}|\lesssim_{\,f,k}(1+l)^{-2k},\quad l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

(2.13)

(4)

For
f\in C^{\infty}(S)
, the series in (
2.11
) converges absolutely and uniformly on
S
.

Proof.

(
1
)
This is well known and documented, for example, in
[
VMK88
, Section 5.6]
.

(
2
)
This follows from the direct calculation of the left of
\Delta(r^{l}Y_{lm})=0
using (
2.4
).

(
3
)
By integration by parts,

-\mu_{l}\langle f,Y_{lm}\rangle_{S}=\langle f,\Delta_{S}Y_{lm}\rangle_{S}=\langle\Delta_{S}f,Y_{lm}\rangle_{S}.

In the same manner, we have

(-\mu_{l})^{k}\langle f,Y_{lm}\rangle_{S}=\langle\Delta_{S}^{k}f,Y_{lm}\rangle_{S},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Thus the assertion follows from

|\langle f,Y_{lm}\rangle_{S}|\leq(\mu_{l}+1)^{-k}\|\Delta_{S}^{k}f\|_{L^{2}(S)}\|Y_{lm}\|_{L^{2}(S)}=(\mu_{l}+1)^{-k}\|\Delta_{S}^{k}f\|_{L^{2}(S)}.

(
4
)
By the Sobolev embedding theorem and by elliptic regularity of

, we have

\displaystyle\|Y_{lm}\|_{L^{\infty}(S)}\lesssim\|Y_{lm}\|_{W^{2,2}(S)}\lesssim\|\Delta_{S}Y_{lm}\|_{L^{2}(S)}+1\lesssim\mu_{l}+1.

(2.14)

which combined with (

We collect here some facts about the modified Bessel functions. References are

of order

(2.25)

and the Wronskian is

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}K_{\nu}(z)&I_{\nu}(z)\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle{\frac{\,{\rm d}K_{\nu}}{\,{\rm d}z}(z)}&\displaystyle{\frac{\,{\rm d}I_{\nu}}{\,{\rm d}z}(z)}\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{z}.

It is known that

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\,{\rm d}I_{\nu}}{\,{\rm d}z}(z)&=\frac{\nu}{z}I_{\nu}(z)+I_{\nu+1}(z)=-\frac{\nu}{z}I_{\nu}(z)+I_{\nu-1}(z),\\
\frac{\,{\rm d}K_{\nu}}{\,{\rm d}z}(z)&=\frac{\nu}{z}K_{\nu}(z)-K_{\nu+1}(z)=-\frac{\nu}{z}K_{\nu}(z)-K_{\nu-1}(z).\end{split}

(2.28)

The following lemma collects the properties of

We recall here the expression of the tensor Green’s function associated with the Stokes system in the full system; see

Define the tensor

by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{k}=-\Delta\gamma^{k}+\nabla(\nabla\cdot\gamma^{k}).

(3.4)

Here the vector

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\partial_{t}-\Delta)\Delta\gamma^{k}=\delta_{\{x=0\}}\delta_{\{t=0\}}{\bf e}_{k}&\mbox{if}\ t>0,\\

\displaystyle\gamma^{k}(x,t)=\bigg\{\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{\frac{5}{2}}t^{\frac{3}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{|x-z|}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4t}}\,{\rm d}z\bigg\}{\bf e}_{k},\quad t>0.

(3.7)

Moreover, define the vector
\Pi
by

\displaystyle\Pi(x)=\nabla\bigg(-\frac{1}{4\pi|x|}\bigg).

(3.8)

Then, for given

with suitable decay at infinity, the pair

\displaystyle\begin{split}v(x,t)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\Gamma(x-y,t-s)f(y,s)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}s,\\
q(x,t)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Pi(x-y)\cdot f(y,t)\,{\rm d}y\end{split}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}v-\Delta v+\nabla q=f&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{4},\\
\nabla\cdot v=0&\mbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{4}.\end{array}\right.

The solutions are unique in suitable classes thanks to the Liouville theorem
[
JSv12
]
.

uniformly in

Proof.

Let us first consider

\displaystyle\begin{split}|r^{-\frac{3}{2}}I_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau}r)|&\lesssim_{\,l}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|\tau|^{\frac{l}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}r^{l-1}&\mbox{if}\ r\leq(\Re\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau})^{-1},\\[5.0pt]

&\lesssim_{\,l}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|\tau|^{\frac{l}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}r^{l-1}&\mbox{if}\ r\leq(\Re\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau})^{-1},\\[5.0pt]

&\lesssim_{\,l}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tau_{2}^{\frac{l}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}r^{l-1}&\mbox{if}\ r\leq(\Re\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau})^{-1},\\[5.0pt]

which leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}|r^{-\frac{3}{2}}I_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau}r)|\lesssim_{\,\tau_{2},l}e^{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}r},\quad r\in(0,\infty).\end{split}

(3.54)

Next we estimate

in (
3.48
) defined by (

\displaystyle\big|r^{-\frac{3}{2}}K_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau}r)s^{l+\frac{3}{2}}I_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau}s)\big|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\,l}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r^{-l-2}s^{2l+2}&\mbox{if}\ s\leq r\leq(\Re\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau})^{-1},\\[5.0pt]

we see that

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{r}r^{-\frac{3}{2}}K_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau}r)s^{l+\frac{3}{2}}I_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau}s)\,{\rm d}s\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\,l}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r^{l+1}&\mbox{if}\ r\leq(\Re\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau})^{-1},\\[5.0pt]

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\,l}r^{l+1},\quad r\in(0,\infty).

In the similar manner, from

\displaystyle\big|r^{-\frac{3}{2}}I_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau}r)s^{l+\frac{3}{2}}K_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau}s)\big|

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{r}^{\infty}r^{-\frac{3}{2}}I_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau}r)s^{l+\frac{3}{2}}K_{l+\frac{1}{2}}(\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau}s)\,{\rm d}s\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\,l}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|\tau|^{-1}r^{l-1}&\mbox{if}\ r\leq(\Re\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\tau})^{-1},\\[5.0pt]

(3.55)

Consequently, from (

\displaystyle|c^{r}_{lm}(r)|\lesssim_{\,\tau_{1},\tau_{2},l}(|C^{r}_{lm}(\tau)|+1)e^{\sqrt{\tau_{2}}r},\quad r\in(0,\infty).

3.6
Discussions on asymptotics at infinity

Here we give some details of Theorem
A
and discuss the comparison with
[
EGFPS19
, Theorem 1.2]
for the heat equation where global approximations are decaying at infinity.

•

Since

and

are defined in (

) and (
3.61
), respectively, the estimate (

) and the Riemann-Lebesgue theorem imply that

(3.62)

for each fixed

In the case of the heat equation, the estimate

Proof of Theorem
B

In this section, we prove Theorem
B
. Without loss of generality, by translation and rescaling, we may consider that
K=[-2,2]^{3}\times[1,3]\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
. Set
\tilde{K}=[-1,1]^{3}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

4.1
A local parabolic Harnack inequality

We first recall the following local parabolic Harnack inequality on
\tilde{K}\times[2,3]
for nonnegative solutions of the heat equations in
K
; see
[
Eva10
, Section 7.1, Theorem 10]
.

Lemma 4.1

There is a constant
C_{0}>0
such that, for any nonnegative smooth solution
w
of
\partial_{t}w-\Delta w=0
in
K
, we have

\sup_{x\in\tilde{K}}w(x,2)\leq C_{0}\inf_{x\in\tilde{K}}w(x,3).

In the sequel we assume, without loss of generality, that
C_{0}\geq 1
.

4.2
Proof by contradiction

Set
\varepsilon_{0}=(2C_{0})^{-1}
and take a decreasing function
c\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that
c(2)=2
and
c(3)=\varepsilon_{0}
. Then, as the smooth parasitic solution
(v,q)
of the form (
1.2
), we define

v(x,t)=\left(\begin{matrix}c(t)&0&0\end{matrix}\right)^{\top}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q(x,t)=-c^{\prime}(t)x^{1}.

Moreover, we define
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}/2
. Notice that
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}\leq 1/2
.

Let us reason by contradiction. Assume that
(u,p)
in the statement satisfies

\|v-u\|_{C(K)}\leq\varepsilon.

(4.1)

Operating
\nabla\cdot
to the first line of (
1.1
), we see that
\Delta p=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
, namely, that
p=p(x,t)
is harmonic in
x
. Then, by the Liouville theorem,
p
is, for each time, constant in
x
. Using again (
1.1
), this implies that
u
actually satisfies the heat equation
\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
. Moreover, from (
4.1
) and from the fact that
c
is greater than
\varepsilon_{0}
on
[1,3]
, we deduce that the first component
u^{1}
of
u
is nonnegative in
K
. It is therefore a nonnegative smooth solution of the heat equation in
K
so that we can apply Lemma
4.1
and get

\sup_{x\in\tilde{K}}u^{1}(x,2)\leq C_{0}\inf_{x\in\tilde{K}}u^{1}(x,3).

(4.2)

But (
4.1
) implies that

\sup_{x\in\tilde{K}}u^{1}(x,2)\geq c(2)-\varepsilon>\frac{3}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\inf_{x\in\tilde{K}}u^{1}(x,3)\leq c(3)+\varepsilon<\frac{1}{C_{0}}.

(4.3)

Hence, by combining (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), we get
3/2<1
. This is the contradiction which completes the proof of Theorem
