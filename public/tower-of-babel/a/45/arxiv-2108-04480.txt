Predicting the Last Zero before an exponential time of a Spectrally Negative Lévy Process
Erik J. Baurdoux, &, José M. Pedraza

Abstract
Given a spectrally negative Lévy process, we predict, in a L 1 L_{1} sense, the last passage time of the process below zero before an independent exponential time. This optimal prediction problem generalises Baurdoux and Pedraza, 2020 where the infinite horizon problem is solved. Using a similar argument as that in Urusov, 2005 , we show that this optimal prediction problem is equivalent to solving an optimal prediction problem in a finite horizon setting. Surprisingly (unlike the infinite horizon problem) an optimal stopping time is based on a curve that is killed at the moment the mean of the exponential time is reached. That is, an optimal stopping time is the first time the process crosses above a non-negative, continuous and non-increasing curve depending on time. This curve and the value function are characterised as a solution of a system of non-linear integral equations which can be understood as a generalisation of the free boundary equations (see e.g. Peskir and Shiryaev, 2006 Chapter IV.14.1) in the presence of jumps. As an example, we calculate numerically such curve in the Brownian motion case and a compound Poisson process with exponential sized jumps perturbed by a Brownian motion.



Keywords: Lévy processes, optimal prediction, optimal stopping.

Mathematics Subject Classification (2000): 60G40, 62M20

1
Introduction

The study of last exit times has received much attention in several areas of applied probability, e.g. risk theory, finance and reliability in the past few years. Consider the Cramér–Lundberg process, a process consisting of a deterministic drift and a compound Poisson process with only negative jumps (see Figure
1
), which is typically used to model the capital of an insurance company. Of particular interest is the moment of ruin,
\tau_{0}
which is defined to refer to the first moment when the process becomes negative. Within the framework of the insurance company having sufficient funds to endure negative capital for a considerale amount of time, another quantity of interest is the last time,
g
that the process is below zero. In a more general setting, we can consider a spectrally negative Lévy process instead of the classical risk process. Several studies, for example
Baurdoux, 2009
and
Chiu and Yin, 2005
studied the Laplace transform of the last time before an exponential time that a spectrally negative Lévy process is below some given level.

Figure 1:
Cramér–Lundberg process with
\tau_{0}
, the moment of ruin and
g
, the last zero.

Last passage time is increasingly becoming a vital factor in financial modeling as shown in
Madan et al., 2008a
and
Madan et al., 2008b
where the authors concludes that the price of a European put and call options, modelled by non-negative and continuous martingales that vanish at infinity, can be expressed in terms of the probability distributions of some last passage times.

Another application of last passage times is in degradation models.
Paroissin and Rabehasaina, 2013
propose a spectrally positive Lévy process to model the ageing of a device in which they consider a subordinator perturbed by an independent Brownian motion. A motivation for considering this model is that the presence of a Brownian motion can model small repairs of the device and the jumps represent major deterioration. In the literature, the failure time of a device is defined as the first hitting time of a critical level
b
. An alternative approach is to consider instead, the last time that the process is under the level
b
since the paths of this process are not necessarily monotone and this allows the process to return below the level
b
after it goes above
b
.

The aim of this work is to predict the last time a spectrally negative Lévy process is below zero before an independent exponential time where the terms “to predict” are understood to mean to find a stopping time that is closest (in
L^{1}
sense) to this random time. This problem is an example of the optimal prediction problems which have been widely investigated by many.
Graversen et al., 2001
predicted the value of the ultimate maximum of a Brownian motion in a finite horizon setting whereas
Shiryaev, 2009
focused on the last time of the attainment of the ultimate maximum of a (driftless) Brownian motion and proceeded to show that it is equivalent to predicting the last zero of the process in this setting. The work of the latter was generalised by
du Toit et al., 2008
for a linear Brownian motion.
Bernyk et al., 2011
studied the time at which a stable spectrally negative Lévy process attains its ultimate supremum in a finite horizon of time and this was later generalised by
Baurdoux and van Schaik, 2014
for any Lévy process in infinite horizon of time. Investigations on the time of the ultimate minimum and the last zero of a transient diffusion process were carried out by
Glover et al., 2013
and
Glover and Hulley, 2014
respectively within a subclass of functions.

In
Baurdoux and Pedraza, 2020
the last zero of a spectrally negative Lévy process in a infinite horizon setting is predicted. It is shown that the an optimal stopping time that minimises the last zero of a spectrally negative Lévy process with drift is the first time the Lévy process crosses above a fixed level
a^{*}\geq 0
which is characterised in terms of the cumulative distribution of the overall infimum of the process. As is in the case in the Canadisation of American type options (see e.g.
Carr, 1998
), given the memoryless property of the exponential distribution, one would expect that the generalisation of the aforementioned problem to an exponential time horizon would result in an infinite horizon optimal prediction problem, and hence have a non time dependent solution. However, it turns out that this is not the case. Indeed, we show the existence of a continuous, non-increasing and non-negative boundary such that an optimal stopping time is given by the first passage time, before the median of the exponential time, above such curve. The proof relies on solving an equivalent (finite horizon) optimal stopping problem that depends on time and the process itself. Moreover, based on the ideas of
du Toit et al., 2008
we characterise the boundary and the value function as the unique solutions of a system of non-linear integral equations. Such system can be thought as a generalisation of the free boundary equation (see e.g.
Peskir and Shiryaev, 2006
, Section 14) allowing for the presence of jumps. We consider two examples where numerical calculations are implemented to find the optimal boundary.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we introduce some important notation regarding Lévy processes and we outline some known fluctuation identities that will be useful later. We then formulate the optimal prediction problem and we prove that it is equivalent to an optimal stopping problem. Section
3
is dedicated to the solution of the optimal stopping problem. The main result of this paper is stated in Theorem
3.9
and its proof is detailed in Section
3.1
. The last section makes use of Theorem
3.9
to find numerical solution of the optimal stopping problem for the Brownian motion with drift case and a compound Poisson process perturbed by a Brownian motion.

2
Prerequisites and Formulation of the Problem

We start this section by introducing some important notations and we give an overview of some fluctuation identities of spectrally negative Lévy processes. Readers can refer to
Bertoin, 1998
,
Sato, 1999
or
Kyprianou, 2014
for more details about Lévy processes.

A Lévy process
X=\{X_{t},t\geq 0\}
is an almost surely càdlàg process that has independent and stationary increments such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=0)=1
. Every Lévy process
X
is also a strong Markov
\mathbb{F}
-adapted process. For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, denote
\mathbb{P}_{x}
as the law of
X
when started at the point
x\in\mathbb{R}
, that is,
\mathbb{E}_{x}(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}(\cdot|X_{0}=x)
. Due to the spatial homogeneity of Lévy processes, the law of
X
under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
is the same as that of
X+x
under
\mathbb{P}
.

Let
X
be a spectrally negative Lévy process, that is, a Lévy process starting from
0
with only negative jumps and non-monotone paths, defined on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
where
\mathbb{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t},t\geq 0\}
is the filtration generated by
X
which is naturally enlarged (see Definition 1.3.38 in
Bichteler, 2002
). We suppose that
X
has Lévy triplet
(\mu,\sigma,\Pi)
where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma\geq 0
and
\Pi
is a measure (Lévy measure) concentrated on
(-\infty,0)
satisfying
\int_{(-\infty,0)}(1\wedge x^{2})\Pi(\textup{d}x)<\infty
.

Let
\psi
be the Laplace exponent of
X
defined as

\displaystyle\psi(\beta):=\log(\mathbb{E}(e^{\beta X_{1}})).

Then
\psi
exists in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, it is strictly convex and infinitely differentiable with
\psi(0)=0
and
\psi(\infty)=\infty
. From the Lévy–Khintchine formula,
we know that
\psi
takes the form

\displaystyle\psi(\beta)=-\mu\beta+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}+\int_{(-\infty,0)}(e^{\beta x}-1-\beta x\mathbb{I}_{\{x>-1\}})\Pi(\textup{d}x)

for all
\beta\geq 0
. Moreover, from Lévy–Itô decomposition, we know that there exists a Brownian Motion
B
and an independent Poisson random measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
with intensity
\textup{d}t\times\Pi(\textup{d}y)
such that for each
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle X_{t}=-\mu t+\sigma B_{t}+\int_{[0,t]}\int_{(-\infty,-1)}yN(\textup{d}s,\textup{d}y)+\int_{[0,t]}\int_{(-1,0)}y[N(\textup{d}s,\textup{d}y)-\Pi(\textup{d}y)\textup{d}s].

Denote
\tau_{a}^{+}
as the first time the process
X
is above the level
a\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tau_{a}^{+}=\inf\{t>0:X_{t}>a\}.

Then it can be shown that its Laplace transform is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{-q\tau_{a}^{+}}\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{a}^{+}<\infty\}})=e^{-\Phi(q)a},

(1)

where
\Phi
corresponds to the right inverse of
\psi
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\Phi(q)=\sup\{\theta\geq 0:\psi(\theta)=q\}

for any
q\geq 0
.

Now we introduce the scale functions. This family of functions is the key to the derivation of fluctuation identities for spectrally negative Lévy processes. The notation used is mainly based on
Kuznetsov et al., 2013
and
Kyprianou, 2014
(see Chapter 8). For
q\geq 0
, the function
W^{(q)}
is such that
W^{(q)}=0
for
x<0
and
W^{(q)}
is characterised on
[0,\infty)
as a strictly increasing and continuous function whose Laplace transform satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta x}W^{(q)}(x)\textup{d}x=\frac{1}{\psi(\beta)-q},\qquad\text{for }\beta>\Phi(q).

We further define the function
Z^{(q)}
by

\displaystyle Z^{(q)}(x)=1+q\int_{0}^{x}W^{(q)}(y)\textup{d}y.

Denote
\tau_{0}^{-}
as the first passage time of
X
of the set
(-\infty,0)
, that is,

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{-}=\inf\{t>0:X_{t}<0\}.

It turns out that the Laplace transform of
\tau_{0}^{-}
can be written in terms of the scale functions. Specifically,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}(e^{-q\tau_{0}^{-}}\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{0}^{-}<\infty\}})=Z^{(q)}(x)-\frac{q}{\Phi(q)}W^{(q)}(x)

(2)

for all
q\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It can be shown that the paths of
X
are of finite variation if only if

\displaystyle\sigma=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{(-1,0)}y\Pi(\textup{d}y)<\infty.

In such case, we may write

\displaystyle\psi(\lambda)=\delta\lambda-\int_{(-\infty,0)}(1-e^{\lambda y})\Pi(dy),

where

\displaystyle\delta:=-\mu-\int_{(-1,0)}x\Pi(\textup{d}x).

(3)

Note that monotone processes are excluded from the definition of spectrally negative Lévy processes so we assume that
\delta>0
when
X
is of finite variation.
The value of
W^{(q)}
at zero depends on the path variation of
X
. In the case where
X
is of infinite variation we have that
W^{(q)}(0)=0
, otherwise

\displaystyle W^{(q)}(0)=\frac{1}{\delta}.

(4)

For any
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
q\geq 0
, the
q
-potential measure of
X
killed upon entering the set
[a,\infty)
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. A density is given for all
x,y\leq a
by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-qt}\mathbb{P}_{x}\left(X_{t}\in\textup{d}y,t<\tau_{a}^{+}\right)\textup{d}t=[e^{-\Phi(q)(a-x)}W^{(q)}(a-y)-W^{(q)}(x-y)]\textup{d}y.

(5)

Let
g_{\theta}
be the last passage time below zero before an exponential time, i.e.

\displaystyle g_{\theta}=\sup\{0\leq t\leq\widetilde{e}_{\theta}:X_{t}\leq 0\},

(6)

where
\widetilde{e}
is an exponential random variable with parameter
\theta\geq 0
. Here, we use the convention that an exponential random variable with parameter
0
is taken to be infinite with probability
1
. In the case of
\theta=0
, we simply denote
g=g_{0}
.

Note that
g_{\theta}\leq\widetilde{e}_{\theta}<\infty

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for all
\theta>0
. However, in the case where
\theta=0
,
g
could be infinite. Therefore, we assume that
\theta>0
throughout this paper. Moreover, we have that
g_{\theta}
has finite moments for all
\theta>0
.

Remark 2.1
.

Since
X
is a spectrally negative Lévy process, we can exclude the case of a compound Poisson process and hence the only way of exiting the set
(-\infty,0]
is by creeping upwards. This tells us that
X_{g_{\theta}-}=X_{g_{\theta}}=0
in the event of
\{g_{\theta}<\widetilde{e}_{\theta}\}
and that
g_{\theta}=\sup\{0\leq t\leq\widetilde{e}_{\theta}:X_{t}<0\}
holds
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

Clearly, up to any time
t\geq 0
the value of
g_{\theta}
is unknown (unless
X
is trivial), and it is only with the realisation of the whole process that we know that the last passage time below
0
has occurred. However, this is often too late: typically, at any time
t\geq 0
, we would like to know how close we are to the time
g_{\theta}
so we can take some actions based on this information. We search for a stopping time
\tau_{*}
of
X
that is as “close” as possible to
g_{\theta}
. Consider the optimal prediction problem

\displaystyle V_{*}=\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}(|g_{\theta}-\tau|),

(7)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the set of all stopping times.

Note that the random time
g^{(\theta)}_{\theta}
is only
\mathbb{F}
measurable so it is not immediately obvious how to solve the optimal prediction problem by using the theory of optimal stopping. Hence, in order to find the solution (and hence prove Theorem
2.3
) we solved an equivalent optimal stopping problem. In the next Lemma we establish an equivalence between the optimal prediction problem (
7
) and an optimal stopping problem. This equivalence is mainly based on the work of
Urusov, 2005
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
\{X_{t},t\geq 0\}
is a spectrally negative Lévy process. Let
g_{\theta}
be the last time that
X
is below the level zero before an exponential time
\widetilde{e}_{\theta}
with
\theta>0
, as defined in (
6
). Consider the optimal stopping problem given by

\displaystyle V=\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}G^{(\theta)}(s,X_{s})\textup{d}s\right),

(8)

where the function
G^{(\theta)}
is given by
G^{(\theta)}(s,x)=1+2e^{-\theta s}\left[\frac{\theta}{\Phi(\theta)}W^{(\theta)}(x)-Z^{(\theta)}(x)\right]
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the stopping time which minimises (
7
) is the same which minimises (
8
). In particular,

\displaystyle V_{*}=V+\mathbb{E}(g_{\theta}).

(9)

Proof.

Fix any stopping time
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
. We have that

\displaystyle|g_{\theta}-\tau|=\int_{0}^{\tau}[2\mathbb{I}_{\{g_{\theta}\leq s\}}-1]\textup{d}s+g_{\theta}.

From Fubini’s theorem and the tower property of conditional expectations, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbb{I}_{\{g_{\theta}\leq s\}}\textup{d}s\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{I}_{\{s<\tau\}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}_{\{g_{\theta}\leq s\}}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]\textup{d}s\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbb{P}(g_{\theta}\leq s|\mathcal{F}_{s})\textup{d}s\right].

Note that in the event of
\{\widetilde{e}_{\theta}\leq s\}
, we have
g_{\theta}\leq s
so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(g_{\theta}\leq s|\mathcal{F}_{s})=1-e^{-\theta s}+\mathbb{P}(g_{\theta}\leq s,\widetilde{e}_{\theta}>s|\mathcal{F}_{s}).

On the other hand for
\{\widetilde{e}_{\theta}>s\}
, as a consequence of Remark
2.1
, the event
\{g_{\theta}\leq s\}
is equal to
\{X_{u}\geq 0\text{ for all }u\in[s,\widetilde{e}_{\theta}]\}
(up to a
\mathbb{P}
-null set). Hence, we get that for all
s\geq 0
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(g_{\theta}\leq s,\widetilde{e}_{\theta}>s|\mathcal{F}_{s})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{u}\geq 0\text{ for all }u\in[s,\widetilde{e}_{\theta}],\widetilde{e}_{\theta}>s|\mathcal{F}_{s})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\inf_{0\leq u\leq\widetilde{e}_{\theta}-s}X_{u+s}\geq 0,\widetilde{e}_{\theta}>s|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta s}\mathbb{P}_{X_{s}}\left(\underline{X}_{\widetilde{e}_{\theta}}\geq 0\right),

where the last equality follows from the lack of memory property of the exponential distribution and the Markov property for Lévy process. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(g_{\theta}\leq s,\widetilde{e}_{\theta}>s|\mathcal{F}_{s})

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta s}F^{(\theta)}(X_{s}),

where for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
F^{(\theta)}(x)=\mathbb{P}_{x}(\underline{X}_{\widetilde{e}_{\theta}}\geq 0)
. Then, since
\widetilde{e}_{\theta}
is independent of
X
, we have that for
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle F^{(\theta)}(x)=\mathbb{P}_{x}(\underline{X}_{\widetilde{e}_{\theta}}\geq 0)=\mathbb{P}_{x}(\widetilde{e}_{\theta}<\tau_{0}^{-})=1-\mathbb{E}_{x}(e^{-\theta\tau_{0}^{-}}\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{0}^{-}<\infty\}})=\frac{\theta}{\Phi(\theta)}W^{(\theta)}(x)-Z^{(\theta)}(x)+1,

where the last equality follows from equation (
2
). Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(g_{\theta}\leq s|\mathcal{F}_{s})

\displaystyle=1-e^{-\theta s}+e^{-\theta s}\left[\frac{\theta}{\Phi(\theta)}W^{(\theta)}(X_{s})-Z^{(\theta)}(X_{s})+1\right]

\displaystyle=1+e^{-\theta s}\left[\frac{\theta}{\Phi(\theta)}W^{(\theta)}(X_{s})-Z^{(\theta)}(X_{s})\right].

Therefore,

\displaystyle V_{*}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}(|g_{\theta}-\tau|)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(g_{\theta})+\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}[2\mathbb{P}(g_{\theta}\leq s|\mathcal{F}_{s})-1]\textup{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(g_{\theta})+\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(1+2e^{-\theta s}\left[\frac{\theta}{\Phi(\theta)}W^{(\theta)}(X_{s})-Z^{(\theta)}(X_{s})\right]\right)\textup{d}s\right).

The conclusion holds.
∎

Note that evaluating
\theta=0
, the function
G^{(0)}
coincides with the gain function found in
Baurdoux and Pedraza, 2020
(see Lemma 3.2 and Remark 3.3). In order to find the solution to the optimal stopping problem (
8
) (and hence (
7
)), we extend its definition to Lévy process (and hence strong Markov process)
\{(t,X_{t}),t\geq 0\}
in the following way. Define the function
V:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,x)=\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}G^{(\theta)}(s+t,X_{s+t})\textup{d}s\right)=\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}G^{(\theta)}(s+t,X_{s}+x)\textup{d}s\right).

(10)

So that

\displaystyle V_{*}=V^{(\theta)}(0,0)+\mathbb{E}(g_{\theta}).

The next theorem states the solution of the optimal stopping theorem (
10
) and hence the solution of (
7
).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\{X_{t},t\geq 0\}
be any spectrally negative Lévy process and
\widetilde{e}_{\theta}
an exponential random variable with parameter
\theta>0
independent of
\mathbb{F}
. There exists a non increasing and continuous curve
b^{(\theta)}:[0,m_{\theta}]\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
b^{(\theta)}\geq h^{(\theta)}
, where
h^{(\theta)}(t):=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:G^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq 0\}
and the infimum in (
10
) is attained by the stopping time

\displaystyle\tau_{D}=\inf\{t\in[0,m_{\theta}]:X_{t}\geq b^{(\theta)}(t)\},

where
m_{\theta}=\log(2)/\theta
. Moreover, the function
b^{(\theta)}
is uniquely characterised as in Theorem
3.9
.

Note that the proof of Theorem
2.3
is rather long and hence is split in a series of lemmas. We dedicate Section
3
for that purpose.

3
Solution to the optimal stopping problem

In this section we solve the optimal stopping problem (
10
). The proof relies on showing that
\tau_{D}
defined in Theorem
2.3
is indeed an optimal stopping time by using the general theory of optimal stopping and properties of the function
V^{(\theta)}
. Hence, some properties of
b^{(\theta)}
are derived. The main contribution of this section (Theorem
3.9
) characterises
V^{(\theta)}
and
b^{(\theta)}
as the unique solution of a non-linear system of integral equations within a certain family of functions.

Recall that the
V^{(\theta)}
is given by

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,x)=\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}G^{(\theta)}(s+t,X_{s+t})\textup{d}s\right).

From the proof of Lemma
2.2
we note that
G^{(\theta)}
can be written as,

\displaystyle G^{(\theta)}(s,x)=1+2e^{-\theta s}[F^{(\theta)}(x)-1],

where
F^{(\theta)}
is the distribution function of the positive random variable
-\underline{X}_{\widetilde{e}_{\theta}}
given by

\displaystyle F^{(\theta)}(x)=\frac{\theta}{\Phi(\theta)}W^{(\theta)}(x)-Z^{(\theta)}(x)+1

(11)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Now we give some intuitions about the function
G^{(\theta)}
. Recall that for all
\theta\geq 0
,
W^{\theta}
and
Z^{(\theta)}
are continuous and strictly increasing functions on
[0,\infty)
such that
W^{(\theta)}(x)=0
and
Z^{(\theta)}(x)=1
for
x\in(-\infty,0)
. From the above, equation (
11
) and from the fact that
F^{(\theta)}
is a distribution function, we have that for a fixed
t\geq 0
, the function
x\mapsto G^{(\theta)}(t,x)
is strictly increasing and continuous in
[0,\infty)
with a possible discontinuity at
0
depending on the path variation of
X
. Moreover, we have that
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}G^{(\theta)}(t,x)=1
for all
t\geq 0
. For
x<0
and
t\geq 0
, we have that the function
G^{(\theta)}
takes the form
G^{(\theta)}(t,x)=1-2e^{-\theta s}
. Similarly, from the fact that
F^{(\theta)}(x)-1\leq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that for a fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
the function
t\mapsto G^{(\theta)}(t,x)
is continuous and strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
. Furthermore, from the fact that
0\leq F^{(\theta)}(x)\leq 1
, we have that the function
G
is bounded by

\displaystyle 1-2e^{-\theta t}\leq G^{(\theta)}(x,t)\leq 1

(12)

which implies that
|G^{(\theta)}|\leq 1
. Recall that
m_{\theta}
is defined as the median of the random variable
\widetilde{e}_{\theta}
, that is,

\displaystyle m_{\theta}=\frac{\log(2)}{\theta}.

Hence from (
12
) we have that
G^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq m_{\theta}
. The above observations tell us that, to solve the optimal stopping problem (
10
), we are interested in a stopping time such that before stopping, the process
X
spends most of its time in the region where
G^{(\theta)}
is negative, taking into account that
(t,X)
can live in the set
\{(s,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}:G^{(\theta)}(s,x)>0\}
and then return back to the set
\{(s,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}:G^{(\theta)}(s,x)\leq 0\}
. The only restriction that applies is that if a considerable amount of time has passed, then
\{x\in\mathbb{R}:G^{(\theta)}(s,x)>0\}=\mathbb{R}
for all
s\geq m_{\theta}
.

Recall that the function
h^{(\theta)}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle h^{(\theta)}(t)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:G^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq 0\}=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F^{(\theta)}(x)\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}e^{\theta t}\}\qquad t\geq 0.

(13)

Hence, we can see that the function
h^{(\theta)}
is a non-increasing continuous function on
[0,m_{\theta})
such that
\lim_{t\uparrow m_{\theta}}h^{(\theta)}(t)=0
and
h^{(\theta)}(t)=-\infty
for
t\in[m_{\theta},\infty)
. Moreover, from the fact that
G^{(\theta)}(t,x)<0
for
(t,x)\in[0,m_{\theta})\times(-\infty,0)
, we have that
h^{(\theta)}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
.

In order to characterise the stopping time that minimises (
10
), we first derive some properties of the function
V^{(\theta)}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The function
V^{(\theta)}
is non-drecreasing in each argument. Moreover,
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)\in(-m_{\theta},0]
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq 0
. In particular,
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)<0
for any
t\geq 0
with
x<h^{(\theta)}(t)
and
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)=0
for all
(t,x)\in[m_{\theta},\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

First, note that
V^{(\theta)}\leq 0
follows by taking
\tau\equiv 0
in the definition of
V^{(\theta)}
. Moreover, since
G^{(\theta)}\geq 0
on
[m_{\theta},\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
we have that
V^{(\theta)}
vanishes on
[m_{\theta},\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
. The fact that
V^{(\theta)}
is non-decreasing in each argument follows from the non-decreasing property of the functions
t\mapsto G^{(\theta)}(t,x)
and
x\mapsto G^{(\theta)}(t,x)
as well as the monotonicity of the expectation. Moreover, using standard arguments we can see that
\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}:x<h^{(\theta)}(t)\}=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}:G^{(\theta)}(t,x)<0\}\subset\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}:V^{(\theta)}(t,x)<0\}
.

Next we will show that
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)>-m_{\theta}
for all
(t,x)\in[0,m_{\theta})\times\mathbb{R}
and for all
\theta>0
. Note that
t<m_{\theta}
if and only if
1-2e^{-\theta t}<0
. Then for all
(s,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
we have that

\displaystyle G^{(\theta)}(s,x)\geq 1-2e^{-\theta s}\geq(1-2e^{-\theta s})\mathbb{I}_{\{s<m_{\theta}\}}.

Hence, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t<m_{\theta}

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}(1-2e^{-\theta(s+t)})\mathbb{I}_{\{t+s<m_{\theta}\}}\textup{d}s\right)=-\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}(2e^{-\theta(s+t)}-1)\mathbb{I}_{\{t+s<m_{\theta}\}}\textup{d}s\right).

The term in the last integral is non-negative, so we obtain for all
t<m_{\theta}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
that

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq-\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}(2e^{-\theta(s+t)}-1)\mathbb{I}_{\{t+s<m_{\theta}\}}\textup{d}s\right)=-\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}(2e^{-\theta(s+t)}-1)\textup{d}s\right)>-m_{\theta}.

∎

By using a dynamic programming argument and the fact that
V^{(\theta)}
vanishes on the set
[0,m_{\theta})\times\mathbb{R}
we can see that

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge(m_{\theta}-t)}G^{(\theta)}(s+t,X_{s+t})\textup{d}s\right).

so that (since
|G^{(\theta)}|\leq 1
) we have that for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left(\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|\int_{0}^{s\wedge(m_{\theta}-t)}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r+t})\textup{d}r\right|\right)<\infty.

Moreover, as a consequence of the properties of
F^{(\theta)}
we have that the function
G^{(\theta)}
is upper semi-continuous we can see that
V^{(\theta)}
is upper semi-continuous (since
V^{(\theta)}
is the infimum of upper semi-continuous functions). Therefore, from the general theory of optimal stopping (see
Peskir and Shiryaev, 2006
Corollary 2.9) we have that an optimal stopping time for (
10
) is given by

\displaystyle\tau_{D}=\inf\{t\geq 0:(t,X_{t})\in D\},

(14)

where
D=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}:V^{(\theta)}(t,x)=0\}
is a closed set.

Hence, from Lemma
3.1
, we derive that
D=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}:x\geq b^{(\theta)}(t)\}
, where the function
b^{(\theta)}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle b^{(\theta)}(t)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:(t,x)\in D\},

for each
t\geq 0
. It follows from Lemma
3.1
that
b^{(\theta)}
is non-increasing and
b^{(\theta)}(t)\geq h^{(\theta)}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
. Moreover,
b^{(\theta)}(t)=-\infty
for
t\in[m_{\theta},\infty)
, since
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)=0
for all
t\geq m_{\theta}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, giving us
\tau_{D}\leq m_{\theta}
. In the case that
t<m_{\theta}
, we have that
b^{(\theta)}(t)
is finitely valued as we will prove in the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\theta>0
. The function
b^{(\theta)}
is finitely valued for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
.

Proof.

For any
\theta>0
and fix
t\geq 0
, consider the optimal stopping problem,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t}^{(\theta)}(x)=\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{m_{\theta}-t}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}[1+2e^{-\theta t}(F^{(\theta)}(X_{s})-1)]\textup{d}s\right),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

where
\mathcal{T}_{m_{\theta}-t}
is the set of all stopping times of
\mathbb{F}
bounded by
m_{\theta}-t
. From the fact that for all
s\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
G(s+t,x)\geq 1+2e^{-\theta t}(F^{(\theta)}(x)-1)
and that
\tau_{D}\in\mathcal{T}_{m_{\theta}-t}
(under
\mathbb{P}_{t,x}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
), we have that

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq\mathcal{V}_{t}^{(\theta)}(x)

(15)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence it suffices to show that there exists
\tilde{x}_{t}
(finite) sufficiently large such that
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{(\theta)}(x)=0
for all
x\geq\tilde{x}_{t}
.

It can be shown that an optimal stopping time for
\mathcal{V}_{t}^{(\theta)}
is
\tau_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}
, the first entry time before
m_{\theta}-t
to the set
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\mathcal{V}_{t}^{(\theta)}(x)=0\}
. We proceed by contradiction, assume that
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\emptyset
, then
\tau_{\mathcal{D}_{t}}=m_{\theta}-t
. Hence, by the dominated convergence theorem and the spatial homogeneity of Lévy processes we have that

\displaystyle 0\geq\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{V}_{t}^{(\theta)}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}[1+2e^{-\theta t}(F^{(\theta)}(X_{s}+x)-1)]\textup{d}s\right)=m_{\theta}-t>0

which is a contradiction. Therefore, we conclude that for each
t\geq 0
, there exists a finite value
\tilde{x}_{t}
such that
b^{(\theta)}(t)\leq\tilde{x}_{t}
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

From the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we find an upper bound of the boundary
b^{(\theta)}
. Define, for each
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
,
u^{(\theta)}(t)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\mathcal{V}^{(\theta)}_{t}(x)=0\}
. Then it follows that
u^{(\theta)}
is a non-increasing finite function such that

\displaystyle u^{(\theta)}(t)\geq b^{(\theta)}(t)

for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
.

Next we show that the function
V^{(\theta)}
is continuous.

Lemma 3.4
.

The function
V^{(\theta)}
is continuous. Moreover, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
t\mapsto V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
is Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and for every
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
x\mapsto V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
is Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

First, we are showing that, for a fixed
t\geq 0
, the function
x\mapsto V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
is Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}
. Recall that if
t\geq m_{\theta}
,
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
so the assertion is clear. Suppose that
t<m_{\theta}
. Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and define
\tau_{x}^{*}=\tau_{D(t,x)}=\inf\{s\geq 0:X_{s}+x\geq b^{(\theta)}(s+t)\}
. Since
\tau_{x}^{*}
is optimal in
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
(under
\mathbb{P}
) we have that

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,y)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{x}^{*}}G^{(\theta)}(s+t,X_{s}+y)\textup{d}s\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{x}^{*}}G^{(\theta)}(s+t,X_{s}+x)\textup{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{x}^{*}}2e^{-\theta(s+t)}[F^{(\theta)}(X_{s}+y)-F^{(\theta)}(X_{s}+x)]\textup{d}s\right).

Define the stopping time

\displaystyle\tau_{b(0)-x}^{+}=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}\geq b^{(\theta)}(0)-x\}.

Then we have that
\tau_{x}^{*}\leq\tau_{b^{(\theta)}(0)-x}^{+}
(since
b^{(\theta)}
is a non-increasing function). From the fact
that
F^{(\theta)}
is non-decreasing, we obtain that for
b^{(\theta)}(0)\geq y\geq x
,

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,y)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b(0)-x}^{+}}e^{-\theta s}[F^{(\theta)}(X_{s}+y)-F^{(\theta)}(X_{s}+x)]\textup{d}s\right).

Using Fubini’s theorem and a density of the potential measure of the process killed upon exiting
(-\infty,b^{(\theta)}(0)]
(see equation (
5
)) we get that

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,y)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{-\infty}^{b^{(\theta)}(0)}[F^{(\theta)}(z+y-x)-F^{(\theta)}(z)]\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\theta s}\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{s}\in\textup{d}z,\tau_{b(0)}^{+}>s)\textup{d}s

\displaystyle=2\int_{-\infty}^{b^{(\theta)}(0)}[F^{(\theta)}(z+y-x)-F^{(\theta)}(z)]\left[e^{-\Phi(\theta)(b^{(\theta)}(0)-x)}W^{(\theta)}(b^{(\theta)}(0)-z)-W^{(\theta)}(x-z)\right]\textup{d}z

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{-\Phi(\theta)(b^{(\theta)}(0)-x)}W^{(\theta)}(b^{(\theta)}(0)-x+y)\int_{x-y}^{b^{(\theta)}(0)}[F^{(\theta)}(z+y-x)-F^{(\theta)}(z)]\textup{d}z,

where in the last inequality, we used the fact that
W^{(\theta)}
is strictly increasing and non-negative and that
F^{(\theta)}
vanishes at
(-\infty,0)
. By an integration by parts argument, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{x-y}^{b^{(\theta)}(0)}[F^{(\theta)}(z+y-x)-F^{(\theta)}(z)]\textup{d}z=(y-x)F^{(\theta)}(b^{(\theta)}(0)+y-x).

Moreover, it can be checked that (see
Kuznetsov et al., 2013
lemma 3.3) the function
z\mapsto e^{-\Phi(\theta)(z)}W^{(\theta)}(z)
is a continuous function in the interval
[0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow\infty}e^{-\Phi(\theta)(z)}W^{(\theta)}(z)=\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}(\Phi(\theta))}<\infty.

This implies that there exist a constant
M>0
such that for every
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
0\leq e^{-\Phi(\theta)(z)}W^{(\theta)}(z)<M
. Then we obtain that for all
x\leq y\leq b^{(\theta)}(0)
,

\displaystyle 0\leq V^{(\theta)}(t,y)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x)\leq 2M(y-x)e^{\Phi(\theta)y}\leq 2M(y-x)e^{\Phi(\theta)b^{(\theta)}(0)}.

On the other hand, since
b^{(\theta)}(0)\geq b^{(\theta)}(t)
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
we have that for all
(t,x)\in[0,m_{\theta})\times[b^{(\theta)}(0),\infty)
,
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)=0
. Hence we obtain that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle|V^{(\theta)}(t,y)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x)|\leq 2M|y-x|e^{\Phi(\theta)b^{(\theta)}(0)}.

(16)

Therefore we conclude that for a fixed
t\geq 0
, the function
x\mapsto V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
is Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}
.

Using a similar argument and the fact that the function
t\mapsto e^{-\theta t}
is Lipschitz continuous on
[0,\infty)
we can show that for any
s,t<m_{\theta}
,

\displaystyle|V^{(\theta)}(s,x)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x)|\leq 2\theta m_{\theta}|s-t|

and therefore
t\mapsto V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
is Lipschitz continuous for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

In order to derive more properties of the boundary
b^{(\theta)}
, we first state some auxiliary results. Recall that if
f\in C_{b}^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})
, the set of real bounded
C^{1,2}
functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
with bounded derivatives, the infinitesimal generator of
(t,X)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{(t,X)}(f)(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(t,x)-\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}f(t,x)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{(-\infty,0)}[f(t,x+y)-f(t,x)-y\mathbb{I}_{\{y>-1\}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(t,x)]\Pi(\textup{d}y).

(17)

Let
C=\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\setminus D=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}:x<b^{(\theta)}(t)\}
be the continuation region. Then we have that the value function
V^{(\theta)}
satisfies a variational inequality in the sense of distributions. The proof is analogous to the one presented in
Lamberton and Mikou, 2008
(see Proposition 2.5) so the details are omitted.

Lemma 3.5
.

Fix
\theta>0
. The distribution
\mathcal{A}_{(t,X)}V^{(\theta)}+G^{(\theta)}
is non-negative on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, we have that
\mathcal{A}_{(t,X)}V^{(\theta)}+G^{(\theta)}=0
on
C
.

We define a special function which is useful to prove the left-continuity of the boundary
b^{(\theta)}
. For
\theta>0
, we define an auxiliary function in the set
D
. Let

\displaystyle\varphi^{(\theta)}(t,x)=\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(t,x+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,x),\qquad(t,x)\in D.

(18)

From the fact that
V^{(\theta)}
vanishes on
D
and that
\Pi
is finite on sets of the form
(-\infty,-\varepsilon)
for
\varepsilon>0
we can see that
|\varphi^{(\theta)}(t,x)|<\infty
for all
(t,x)\in D
. Moreover, by the Lemma above and the properties of
V^{(\theta)}
and
G^{(\theta)}
, it can be shown that
\varphi
is strictly positive, continuous and strictly increasing (in each argument) in the interior of
D
.

Now we are ready to give further properties of the curve
b^{(\theta)}
in the set
[0,m_{\theta})
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The function
b^{(\theta)}
is continuous on
[0,m_{\theta})
. Moreover we have that
\lim_{t\uparrow m_{\theta}}b^{(\theta)}(t)=0
.

Proof.

The method proof of the continuity of
b^{(\theta)}
in
[0,m_{\theta})
is heavyly based on the work of
Lamberton and Mikou, 2008
(see Theorem 4.2, where the continuity of the boundary is shown in the American option context) so is omitted.

We then show that the limit holds. Define
b^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta}-):=\lim_{t\uparrow m_{\theta}}b^{(\theta)}(t)
. We obtain
b^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta}-)\geq 0
since
b^{(\theta)}(t)\geq h^{(\theta)}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
. The proof is by contradiction so we assume that
b^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta}-)>0
. Note that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that
V^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta},0)=0
and
G^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta},x)=F^{(\theta)}(x)
. Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{X}(V^{(\theta)})+G^{(\theta)}=-\partial_{t}V^{(\theta)}\leq 0

in the sense of distributions on
(0,m_{\theta})\times(0,b^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta}-))
. Hence, by continuity, we can derive, for
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
, that
\mathcal{A}_{X}(V^{(\theta)})(t,\cdot)+G^{(\theta)}(t,\cdot)\leq 0
on the interval
(0,b^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta}-))
. Hence, by taking
t\uparrow m_{\theta}
we obtain that

\displaystyle 0\geq\lim_{t\uparrow m_{\theta}}\mathcal{A}_{X}(V^{(\theta)})(t,\cdot)+G^{(\theta)}(t,\cdot)=F^{(\theta)}>0

in the sense of distributions, where we used the continuity if
V^{(\theta)}
and
G^{(\theta)}
, the fact that
V^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta},x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and that
F^{(\theta)}(x)>0
for all
x>0
. Note that we have got a contradiction and we conclude that
b^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta})=0
.
∎

Define the value

\displaystyle t_{b}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:b^{(\theta)}(t)\leq 0\}.

(19)

Note that in the case where
X
is a process of infinite variation, we have that the distribution function of the random variable
-\underline{X}_{\widetilde{e}_{\theta}}
,
F^{(\theta)}
, is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, strictly increasing and strictly positive in the open set
(0,\infty)
with
F^{(\theta)}(0)=0
. This fact implies that the inverse function of
F^{(\theta)}
exists on
(0,\infty)
and then function
h^{(\theta)}
can be written for
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
as

\displaystyle h^{(\theta)}(t)=(F^{(\theta)})^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}e^{\theta t}\right).

Hence we conclude that
h^{(\theta)}(t)>0
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
. Therefore, when
X
is a process of infinite variation, we have
b^{(\theta)}(t)>0
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and hence
t_{b}=m_{\theta}
. For the case of finite variation, we have that
t_{b}\in[0,m_{\theta})
which implies that
b^{(\theta)}(t)=0
for all
t\in[t_{b},m_{\theta})
and
b^{(\theta)}(t)>0
for all
t\in[0,t_{b})
. In the next lemma, we characterise its value.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\theta>0
and
X
be a process of finite variation. We have that for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,0)}[V^{(\theta)}(t,x+y)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x)]\Pi(\textup{d}y)>-\infty.

Moreover, for any Lévy process,
t_{b}
is given by

\displaystyle t_{b}=\inf\left\{t\in[0,m_{\theta}]:\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}_{B}(t,y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,0)\geq 0\right\},

(20)

where
V^{(\theta)}_{B}
is given by

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}_{B}(t,y)=\mathbb{E}_{y}(\tau_{0}^{+}\wedge(m_{\theta}-t))-\frac{2}{\theta}e^{-\theta t}[1-\mathbb{E}_{y}(e^{-\theta(\tau_{0}^{+}\wedge(m_{\theta}-t))})]

for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Assume that
X
is a process of finite variation. We first show that

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,0)}[V^{(\theta)}(t,x+y)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x)]\Pi(\textup{d}y)>-\infty

for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The case
t\geq m_{\theta}
is straightforward since
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The case
t<m_{\theta}
follows from the Lipschitz continuity of the mapping
x\mapsto V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
, that
\Pi
is finite on intervals away from zero and since
\int_{(-1,0)}y\Pi(\textup{d}y)>-\infty
when
X
is of finite variation. Moreover, from Lemma
3.5
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,0)}[V^{(\theta)}(t,x+y)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x)]\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,x)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V^{(\theta)}(t,x)-\delta\frac{\partial}{\partial x}V^{(\theta)}(t,x)\leq 0

on
C
in the sense of distributions, where the last inequality follows since
V^{(\theta)}
is non-decreasing in each argument and
\delta>0
is defined in (
3
). Then by continuity of the functions
V^{(\theta)}
and
G^{(\theta)}
(recall that
G^{(\theta)}
is at least continuous on
(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)
and right-continuous at points of the form
(t,0)
for
t\geq 0
) we can derive

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,0)}[V^{(\theta)}(t,y)-V^{(\theta)}(t,0)]\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,0)\leq 0

(21)

for all
t\in[0,t_{b})
.

Next, we show that the set
\{t\in[0,m_{\theta}):b^{(\theta)}(t)=0\}
is non empty. We proceed by contradiction, assume that
b^{(\theta)}(t)>0
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
so that
t_{b}=m_{\theta}
. Taking
t\uparrow m_{\theta}
in (
21
) and applying dominated convergence theorem, we obtain that

\displaystyle 0\geq\lim_{t\uparrow m_{\theta}}\left\{\int_{(-\infty,0)}[V^{(\theta)}(t,y)-V^{(\theta)}(t,0)]\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,0)\right\}=G^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta},0)=F^{(\theta)}(0)>0,

where the strict inequality follows from
F^{(\theta)}(0)=\frac{\theta}{\Phi(\theta)}W^{(\theta)}(0)=\frac{\theta}{\delta\Phi(\theta)}>0
since
X
is of finite variation. Therefore, we observe a contradiction which shows that
\{t\in[0,m_{\theta}):b^{(\theta)}(t)=0\}\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, by the definition, we have that
t_{b}=\inf\{t\in[0,m_{\theta}):b^{(\theta)}(t)=0\}
.

Next we find an expression for
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
when
t\in(0,m_{\theta})
and
x<0
. Since
b^{(\theta)}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
, we have that

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{0}^{+}\wedge(m_{\theta}-t)}(1-2e^{-\theta(t+s)})\textup{d}s\right)+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{0}^{+}<m_{\theta}-t\}}V^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{0}^{+},0)\right)

\displaystyle=V^{(\theta)}_{B}(t,x)+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{0}^{+}<m_{\theta}-t\}}V^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{0}^{+},0)\right),

(22)

where the first equality follows since
X_{s}\leq 0
for all
s\leq\tau_{0}^{+}
and
G(t,x)=1-2e^{-\theta t}
for all
x<0
. Hence, in particular, we have that
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)=V^{(\theta)}_{B}(t,x)
for all
t\in[t_{b},m_{\theta})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

We show that (
20
) holds. From the discussion after Lemma
3.5
, we know that

\displaystyle\varphi^{(\theta)}(t,x)=\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(t,x+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,x)>0

for all
x>0
and
t\geq t_{b}
. Then by taking
x\downarrow 0
, making use of the right continuity of
x\mapsto G(t,x)
, continuity of
V^{(\theta)}
(see Lemma
3.4
) and applying dominated convergence theorem, we derive that

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(t_{b},y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t_{b},0)\geq 0.

In particular, if
t_{b}=0
, (
20
) holds since
t\mapsto V^{(\theta)}(t,y)
(for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
) and
G^{(\theta)}(t,0)
are non-decreasing functions. If
t_{b}>0
, taking
t\uparrow t_{b}
in (
21
) gives us

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(t_{b},y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,0)\leq 0.

Hence, we have that
\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}_{B}(t_{b},y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t_{b},0)=0
with (
20
) becoming clear due to the fact that
t\mapsto V^{(\theta)}_{B}(t,x)
is non-decreasing. If
X
is a process of infinite variation, we have that
h^{(\theta)}(t)>0
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and therefore
G^{(\theta)}(t,x)<0
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and
x\leq 0
which implies that

\displaystyle t_{b}=m_{\theta}=\inf\left\{t\in[0,m_{\theta}]:\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}_{B}(t,y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,0)\geq 0\right\}.

∎

Now we prove that the derivatives of
V^{(\theta)}
exist at the boundary
b^{(\theta)}
for those points in which
b^{(\theta)}
is strictly positive.

Lemma 3.8
.

For all
t\in[0,t_{b})
, the partial derivatives of
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
at the point
(t,b^{(\theta)}(t))
exist and are equal to zero, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t))=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial x}V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t))=0.

Proof.

First, we prove that the assertion in the first argument. Using a similar idea as in Lemma
3.4
, we have that for any
t<t_{b}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
and

,

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t))-V^{(\theta)}(t-h,b^{(\theta)}(t))}{h}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{b^{(\theta)}(t)}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}_{h}}\frac{[e^{-\theta(r+t-h)}-e^{-\theta(r+t)}]}{h}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{b^{(\theta)}(t)}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau^{+}_{b^{(\theta)}(t-h)}}\frac{[e^{-\theta(r+t-h)}-e^{-\theta(r+t)}]}{h}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{[e^{-\theta(t-h)}-e^{-\theta t}]}{h}\frac{1}{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{b^{(\theta)}(t)}\left(1-e^{-\theta\tau^{+}_{b^{(\theta)}(t-h)}}\right)

where
\tau^{*}_{h}=\inf\{r\in[0,m_{\theta}-t+h]:X_{r}\geq b^{(\theta)}(r+t-h)\}
is the optimal stopping time for
V^{(\theta)}(t-h,x)
and the second inequality follows since
b
is non increasing. Hence, by (
1
) and continuity of
b^{(\theta)}
we obtain that

\displaystyle\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t))-V^{(\theta)}(t-h,b^{(\theta)}(t))}{h}=0.

Now we show that the partial derivative of the second argument exists at
b^{(\theta)}(t)
and is equal to zero. Fix any time
t\in[0,t_{b})
,
\varepsilon>0
and
x\leq b^{(\theta)}(t)
(without loss of generality, we assume that
\varepsilon<x
). By a similar argument that as provided in Lemma
3.4
, we obtain that

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}

\displaystyle(t,x)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{-\infty}^{b^{(\theta)}(t)}[F^{(\theta)}(z+\varepsilon)-F^{(\theta)}(z)]\left[e^{-\Phi(\theta)(b^{(\theta)}(t)-x+\varepsilon)}W^{(\theta)}(b^{(\theta)}(t)-z)-W^{(\theta)}(x-\varepsilon-z)\right]\textup{d}z

\displaystyle=2e^{-\Phi(\theta)(b^{(\theta)}(t)-x+\varepsilon)}\int_{x-\varepsilon}^{b^{(\theta)}(t)}[F^{(\theta)}(z+\varepsilon)-F^{(\theta)}(z)]W^{(\theta)}(b^{(\theta)}(t)-z)\textup{d}z

\displaystyle\qquad+2\int_{0}^{x-\varepsilon}[F^{(\theta)}(z+\varepsilon)-F^{(\theta)}(z)]\left[e^{-\Phi(\theta)(b^{(\theta)}(t)-x+\varepsilon)}W^{(\theta)}(b^{(\theta)}(t)-z)-W^{(\theta)}(x-\varepsilon-z)\right]\textup{d}z

\displaystyle\qquad+2\int_{-\varepsilon}^{0}F^{(\theta)}(z+\varepsilon)\left[e^{-\Phi(\theta)(b^{(\theta)}(t)-x+\varepsilon)}W^{(\theta)}(b^{(\theta)}(t)-z)-W^{(\theta)}(x-\varepsilon-z)\right]\textup{d}z.

Dividing by
\varepsilon
, we have that for
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and
\varepsilon<x
that

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{V^{(\theta)}(t,x)-V^{(\theta)}(t,x-\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\leq R_{1}^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)+R_{2}^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)+R_{3}^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x),

where

\displaystyle R_{1}^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=2e^{-\Phi(\theta)(b^{(\theta)}(t)-x+\varepsilon)}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{x-\varepsilon}^{b^{(\theta)}(t)}[F^{(\theta)}(z+\varepsilon)-F^{(\theta)}(z)]W^{(\theta)}(b^{(\theta)}(t)-z)\textup{d}z,

\displaystyle R_{2}^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=2\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{x-\varepsilon}[F^{(\theta)}(z+\varepsilon)-F^{(\theta)}(z)]\left[e^{-\Phi(\theta)(b^{(\theta)}(t)-x+\varepsilon)}W^{(\theta)}(b^{(\theta)}(t)-z)-W^{(\theta)}(x-\varepsilon-z)\right]\textup{d}z,

\displaystyle R_{3}^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=2\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{-\varepsilon}^{0}F^{(\theta)}(z+\varepsilon)\left[e^{-\Phi(\theta)(b^{(\theta)}(t)-x+\varepsilon)}W^{(\theta)}(b^{(\theta)}(t)-z)-W^{(\theta)}(x-\varepsilon-z)\right]\textup{d}z.

By using that
W
and
F
are non-decreasing, that
W^{(\theta)}
(and hence
F^{(\theta)}
) has left and right derivatives and the dominated convergence theorem we can show that for
t\in[0,t_{b})
,
\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}R_{i}^{(\varepsilon)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t))=0
for each
i=1,2,3
. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t))-V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t)-\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}=0

proving that
x\mapsto V^{(\theta)}(x,t)
is differentiable at
b^{(\theta)}(t)
with
\partial/\partial xV^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t))=0
for
t\in[0,t_{b})
.

∎

Theorem 3.9
.

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}\int_{(-\infty,b^{(\theta)}(r+t)-X_{r})}V^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>b^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

3.1
Proof of Theorem

Since the proof of Theorem

is rather long, we split it into a series of Lemmas. This subsection is entirely dedicated for this purpose. With the help of Itô formula and following an analogous argument as in
Lamberton and Mikou, 2013
(in the infinite variation case), we prove that

and

are solutions to the integral equations listed above. The finite variation case is proved using an argument that considers the consecutive times in which
X
hits the curve
b^{(\theta)}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

The pair
(V^{(\theta)},b^{(\theta)})
are solutions to the equations (
3.9
)-(
25
).

Proof.

Recall from Lemma
3.7
that, when
t_{b}<m_{\theta}
, the value function
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
satisfies equation (
25
) for
t\in[t_{b},m_{\theta})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We also have that equation (
3.9
) follows from (
3.9
) by letting
x=b^{(\theta)}(t)
and using that
V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t))=0
.

We proceed to show that
(V^{(\theta)},b^{(\theta)})
solves equation (
3.9
). First, we assume that
X
is a process of infinite variation. We follow an analogous argument as
Lamberton and Mikou, 2013
(see Theorem 3.2). Consider a regularized sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of non-negative
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})
functions with support in
[-1/n,0]\times[-1/n,0]
such that
\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\rho_{n}(s,y)\textup{d}s\textup{d}y=1
. For every
n\geq 1
, define the function
V^{(\theta)}_{n}
by

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}_{n}(t,x)=(V^{(\theta)}\ast\rho_{n})(t,x)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{-\infty}^{0}V^{(\theta)}(t+s,x+y)\rho_{n}(s,y)\textup{d}s\textup{d}y.

for any
(t,x)\in[1/n,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
. Then for each
n\geq 1
, the function
V^{(\theta)}_{n}
is a
C^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})
bounded function (since
V^{(\theta)}
is bounded). Moreover, it can be shown that
V^{(\theta)}_{n}\uparrow V
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
when

and that (see
Lamberton and Mikou, 2008
, proof of Proposition 2.5),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V^{(\theta)}_{n}(t,x)+\mathcal{A}_{X}(V^{(\theta)}_{n})(t,x)=-(G^{(\theta)}*\rho_{n})(u,x)\qquad\text{for all }(t,x)\in[1/n,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\cap C,

(26)

where
\mathcal{A}_{X}
is the infinitesimal generator of

given in (
3
) and

. Let
t\in(0,t_{b}]
,

and

. Applying Itô formula to

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}_{n}(s+t,X_{s}+x)

\displaystyle=V^{(\theta)}_{n}(t,x)+M_{s}^{t,n}+\int_{0}^{s}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V^{(\theta)}_{n}(r+t,X_{r}+x)+\mathcal{A}_{X}(V^{(\theta)}_{n})(r+t,X_{r}+x)\right]\textup{d}r,

Hence, taking expectation and using (
26

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(V^{(\theta)}_{n}(s+t,X_{s}+x))

\displaystyle=V^{(\theta)}_{n}(t,x)+\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}V^{(\theta)}_{n}(r+t,X_{r}+x)+\mathcal{A}_{X}(V^{(\theta)}_{n})(r+t,X_{r}+x)\right]\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=V^{(\theta)}_{n}(t,x)-\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{s}(G^{(\theta)}*\rho_{n})(r+t,X_{r}+x)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<b^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}_{n}(r+t,X_{r}+x+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>b^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right),

where we used the fact that

and

For the finite variation case, we define the auxiliary function

\displaystyle R^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<b^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>b^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
. We then prove that

. First, note that from the discussion after Lemma

. Then we have that for all
(t,x)\in[0,m_{\theta}]\times\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle|R^{(\theta)}(t,x)|\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}|G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})|\textup{d}r\right)\leq m_{\theta}-t,

where we used that

in the last inequality. For each
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, we define the times at which the process

hits the curve
b^{(\theta)}
. Let
\tau_{b}^{(1)}=\inf\{s\in[0,m_{\theta}-t]:X_{s}\geq b^{(\theta)}(s+t)\}
and for

\displaystyle\sigma_{b}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\inf\{s\in[\tau_{b}^{k},m_{\theta}-t]:X_{s}<b^{(\theta)}(s+t)\}

\displaystyle\tau_{b}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\inf\{s\in[\sigma_{b}^{k},m_{\theta}-t]:X_{s}\geq b^{(\theta)}(s+t)\},

where in this context, we understand that
\inf\emptyset=m_{\theta}-t
. Taking
t\in[0,m_{\theta}]
and

and gives us

\displaystyle R^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}^{(1)}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\textup{d}r\right)+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{\sigma_{b}^{(1)}}^{\tau_{b}^{(2)}}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{b}^{(2)}<m_{\theta}-t\}}\int_{\tau_{b}^{(2)}}^{m_{\theta}-t}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<b^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{b}^{(2)}<m_{\theta}-t\}}\int_{\tau_{b}^{(2)}}^{m_{\theta}-t}\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>b^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{x}(R^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{b}^{(2)},X_{\tau_{b}^{(2)}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{b}^{(2)}<m_{\theta}-t\}}),

where the last equality follows from the strong Markov property applied at time

and

is optimal for

. Using the compensation formula for Poisson random measures (see

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}^{(1)}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\textup{d}r\right)=\mathbb{E}_{x}(V^{(\theta)}(t+\sigma_{b}^{(1)},X_{\sigma_{b}^{(1)}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\sigma_{b}^{(1)}<m_{\theta}-t\}}).

Hence, for all

, we have that

\displaystyle R^{(\theta)}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}(R^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{b}^{(2)},X_{\tau_{b}^{(2)}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{b}^{(2)}<m_{\theta}-t\}}).

Using an induction argument, it can be shown that for all
(t,x)\in D
and

\displaystyle R^{(\theta)}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}(R^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{b}^{(n)},X_{\tau_{b}^{(n)}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\tau_{b}^{(n)}<m_{\theta}-t\}})=\mathbb{E}_{x}(R^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{b}^{(n)},X_{\tau_{b}^{(n)}})),

(27)

where the last equality follows since

. Since

\mathbb{P}_{x}

\displaystyle|R^{(\theta)}(t,x)|\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(|R^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{b}^{(n)},X_{\tau_{b}^{(n)}})|\right)\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}(m_{\theta}-t-\tau_{b}^{(n)})=0,

where the last inequality follows from the dominated convergence theorem. On the other hand, if we take

∎

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}\int_{(-\infty,c^{(\theta)}(r+t)-X_{r})}U^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{c^{(\theta)}(t)}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,c^{(\theta)}(t)-x)}U^{(\theta)}(t,x+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,t_{b})\text{ and }x>c^{(\theta)}(t).

\displaystyle U^{(\theta)}(t,x)

The next lemma shows that

Lemma 3.12

U^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq V^{(\theta)}(t,x)

(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,m_{\theta}]

Proof.

, we have that

. Consider the stopping time

\displaystyle\tau_{c}=\inf\{s\in[0,m_{\theta}-t]:X_{s}\geq c^{(\theta)}(t+s)\}.

, using the fact that
X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(t+r)

\tau_{c}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle U^{(\theta)}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{c}}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})\textup{d}r\right)+\mathbb{E}_{x}(U^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{c},X_{\tau_{c}}))=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{c}}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})\textup{d}r\right),

(32)

where the second equality follows since
X
creeps upwards and therefore
X_{\tau_{c}}=c^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{c})
for
\{\tau_{c}<m_{\theta}-t\}
and
U^{(\theta)}(m_{\theta},x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then from the definition of
V^{(\theta)}
(see (
10
)), we have that

\displaystyle U^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq\inf_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}G^{(\theta)}(X_{t+r},t+r)dr\right)=V^{(\theta)}(t,x).

Therefore
U^{(\theta)}\geq V^{(\theta)}
on
[0,m_{\theta}]\times\mathbb{R}
.
∎

We proceed by showing that the function
c^{(\theta)}
is dominated by
b^{(\theta)}
. In the upcoming lemmas, we show that equality indeed holds.

Lemma 3.13
.

We have that
b^{(\theta)}(t)\geq c^{(\theta)}(t)
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
.

Proof.

The statement is clear for
t\in[t_{b},m_{\theta})
. We prove the statement by contradiction. Suppose that there exists a value
t_{0}\in[0,t_{b})
such that
b^{(\theta)}(t_{0})<c^{(\theta)}(t_{0})
and take
x\in(b^{(\theta)}(t_{0}),c^{(\theta)}(t_{0}))
. Consider the stopping time

\displaystyle\sigma_{b}=\inf\{s\in[0,m_{\theta}-t_{0}]:X_{s}<b^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+s)\}.

Applying the strong Markov property to the filtration at time
\sigma_{b}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle U^{(\theta)}(t_{0},x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}(U^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+\sigma_{b},X_{\sigma_{b}}))+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}}G^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r,X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(t+r,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r)\}}\textup{d}r\right),

where we used the fact that
U^{(\theta)}(t,x)=0
for all
(t,x)\in D_{c}
. From Lemma
3.12
and the fact that
U^{(\theta)}\leq 0
(by assumption), we have that for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and
x>b^{(\theta)}(t)
,
U^{(\theta)}(t,x)=0
. Hence, by the compensation formula for Poisson random measures, we obtain that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq U^{(\theta)}(t_{0},x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}(U^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+\sigma_{b},X_{\sigma_{b}})\mathbb{I}_{\{\sigma_{b}<m_{\theta}-t,X_{\sigma_{b}}<b^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+\sigma_{b})\}})+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}}G^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r,X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(t+r,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\textup{d}r\right)+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}}G^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r,X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(t+r,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}}\left[\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r,X_{r})\right]\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r)\}}\textup{d}r\right).

Recall from the discussion after Lemma
3.5
that the function
\varphi_{t}^{(\theta)}
is strictly positive on
D
. Hence, we obtain that for all
(t,x)\in D
,

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(t,x+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(t,x+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,x)=\varphi_{t}^{(\theta)}(t,x)>0.

The assumption that
b^{(\theta)}(t_{0})<c^{(\theta)}(t_{0})
together with the continuity of the functions
b^{(\theta)}
and
c^{(\theta)}
mean that there exists
s_{0}\in(t_{0},m_{\theta})
such that
b^{(\theta)}(r)<c^{(\theta)}(r)
for all
r\in[t_{0},s_{0}]
. Consequently, the
\mathbb{P}_{x}
probability of
X
spending a strictly positive amount of time (with respect to Lebesgue measure) in this region is strictly positive. We can then conclude that

\displaystyle 0\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma_{b}}\left[\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r,X_{r})\right]\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(t_{0}+r)\}}\textup{d}r\right)>0.

This is a contradiction and therefore we conclude that
b^{(\theta)}(t)\geq c^{(\theta)}(t)
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
.
∎

Note that the definition of
U^{(\theta)}
on
[t_{b},m_{\theta})\times\mathbb{R}
(see equation (
30
)) together with condition (
31
) imply that

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(t,x+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(t,x)\geq 0

for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and
x>c^{(\theta)}(t)
. The next Lemma shows that
U^{(\theta)}
and
V^{(\theta)}
coincide.

Lemma 3.14
.

We have that
b^{(\theta)}(t)=c^{(\theta)}(t)
for all
t\geq 0
and hence
V^{(\theta)}=U^{(\theta)}
.

Proof.

We prove that
b^{(\theta)}=c^{(\theta)}
by contradiction. Assume that there exists
s_{0}
such that
b^{(\theta)}(s_{0})>c^{(\theta)}(s_{0})
. Since
c^{(\theta)}(t)=b^{(\theta)}(t)=0
for all
t\in[t_{b},m_{\theta})
, we deduce that
s_{0}\in[0,t_{b})
. Let
\tau_{b}
be the stopping time

\displaystyle\tau_{b}=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{s}\geq b^{(\theta)}(s_{0}+t)\}.

With the Markov property applied to the filtration at time
\tau_{b}
, we obtain that for any
x\in(c^{(\theta)}(s_{0}),b^{(\theta)}(s_{0}))

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}(U^{(\theta)}(s_{0}+\tau_{b},X_{\tau_{b}}))

\displaystyle=U^{(\theta)}(s_{0},x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b}}G^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0},X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0})\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0},X_{r}+y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0})\}}\Pi(\textup{d}y)\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\geq V^{(\theta)}(s_{0},x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b}}G^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0},X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0})\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0},X_{r}+y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0})\}}\Pi(\textup{d}y)\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b}}G^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0},X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}\geq c^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0})\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0},X_{r}+y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0})\}}\Pi(\textup{d}y)\textup{d}r\right),

where the second inequality follows from the fact that
U^{(\theta)}\geq V^{(\theta)}
(see Lemma
3.12
) and the last equality follows as
\tau_{b}
is the optimal stopping time for
V^{(\theta)}(s_{0},x)
. Note that since
X
creeps upwards, we have that
U^{(\theta)}(s_{0}+\tau_{b},X_{\tau_{b}})=U^{(\theta)}(s_{0}+\tau_{b},b^{(\theta)}(s_{0}+\tau_{b}))=0
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b}}G^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0},X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}\geq c^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0})\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0},X_{r}+y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0})\}}\Pi(\textup{d}y)\textup{d}r\right)\leq 0.

However, the continuity of the functions
b^{(\theta)}
and
c^{(\theta)}
gives the existence of
s_{1}\in(s_{0},m_{\theta})
such that
c^{(\theta)}(r)<b^{(\theta)}(r)
for all
r\in[s_{0},s_{1}]
. Hence, together with the fact that
\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(x+y,t)\Pi(\textup{d}y)+G^{(\theta)}(x,t)>0
for all
(t,x)\in D_{c}
we can conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b}}G^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0},X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}\geq c^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0})\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{b}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0},X_{r}+y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(r+s_{0})\}}\Pi(\textup{d}y)\textup{d}r\right)>0,

which shows a contradiction.

∎

4
Examples

4.1
Brownian motion with drift

Suppose that
X=\{X_{t},t\geq 0\}
is a Brownian motion with drift. That is for any
t\geq 0
,
X_{t}=\mu t+\sigma B_{t}
, where
\sigma>0
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. In this case, we have that

\displaystyle\psi(\beta)=\mu\beta+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}

for all
\beta\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\Phi(q)=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left[\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2\sigma^{2}q}-\mu\right].

It is well known that
-\underline{X}_{\widetilde{e}_{\theta}}
has exponential distribution (see e.g.
Borodin and Salminen, 2002
pp251 or
Kyprianou, 2014
pp 233) with distribution function given by

\displaystyle F^{(\theta)}(x)=1-\exp\left(-\frac{x}{\sigma^{2}}\left[\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2\sigma^{2}\theta}+\mu\right]\right)\qquad\text{for }x>0.

Denote
\Phi(x;a,b^{2})
as the distribution function of a Normal random variable with mean
a\in\mathbb{R}
and variance
b^{2}
, i.e., for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\Phi(x;a,b^{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi b^{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2b^{2}}(y-a)^{2}}\textup{d}y.

For any
b,s,t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, define the function

\displaystyle K(t,x,s,b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(G^{(\theta)}(s+t,X_{s}+x)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{s}+x\leq b\}}\right).

Then it can be easily shown that

\displaystyle K(t,x,s,b)

\displaystyle=\Phi(b-x;\mu s,\sigma^{2}s)-2e^{-\theta(s+t)}\Phi(-x;\mu s,\sigma^{2}s)-2e^{-\theta t}\exp\left(-\frac{x}{\sigma^{2}}\left[\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2\sigma^{2}\theta}+\mu\right]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[\Phi(b-x,-s\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2\sigma^{2}\theta},s\sigma^{2})-\Phi(-x,-s\sqrt{\mu^{2}+2\sigma^{2}\theta},s\sigma^{2})\right].

Thus, we have that
b^{(\theta)}
satisfies the non-linear integral equation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}K(t,b^{(t)}(t),s,b^{(\theta)}(t+s))\textup{d}s=0

for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and the value function
V^{(\theta)}
is given by

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}K(t,x,s,b^{(\theta)}(t+s))\textup{d}s

for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
. Note that we can approximate the integrals above by Riemann sums so a numerical approximation can be implement. Indeed, take
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
sufficiently large and define
h=m_{\theta}/n
. For each
k\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,n\}
, we define
t_{k}=kh
. Then the sequence of times
\{t_{k},k=0,1,\ldots,n\}
is a partition of the interval
[0,m_{\theta}]
. Then, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
for
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
we approximate
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
by

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}_{h}(t_{k},x)=\sum_{i=k}^{n-1}K(t_{k},x,t_{i-k+1},b_{i})h,

where the sequence
\{b_{k},k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
is a solution to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k}^{n-1}K(t_{k},x,t_{i-k+1},b_{i})=0

for each
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Note that the sequence
\{b_{k},k=0,1,\ldots,n\}
is a numerical approximation to the sequence
\{b^{(\theta)}(t_{k}),k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
(for
n
sufficiently large) and can be calculated by using backwards induction. In the Figure
3
, we show a numerical calculation of the equations above. The parameters used are
\mu=2
and
\sigma=1
, whereas we chose
m_{\theta}=10
.

Figure 2:
Brownian motion with drift
\mu=2
and
\sigma=1
. Left hand side: Optimal boundary; Right hand side: Value function fixing
t=1
.

4.2
Brownian motion with exponential jumps

Let
X=\{X_{t},t\geq 0\}
be a compound Poisson process perturbed by a Brownian motion, that is

\displaystyle X_{t}=\sigma B_{t}+\mu t-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}Y_{i},

(33)

where
B=\{B_{t},t\geq 0\}
is a standard Brownian motion,
N=\{N_{t},t\geq 0\}
is Poisson process with rate
\lambda
independent of
B
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma>0
and the sequence
\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots\}
is a sequence of independent random variables exponentially distributed with mean
1/\rho>0
. Then in this case, the Laplace exponent is derived as

\displaystyle\varphi(\beta)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\beta^{2}+\mu\beta-\frac{\lambda\beta}{\rho+\beta}.

Its Lévy measure, given by
\Pi(\textup{d}y)=\lambda\rho e^{\rho y}\mathbb{I}_{\{y<0\}}\textup{d}y
is a finite measure and
X
is a process of infinite variation. According to
Kuznetsov et al., 2013
, the scale function in this case is given for
q\geq 0
and
x\geq 0
by,

\displaystyle W^{(q)}(x)=\frac{e^{\Phi(q)x}}{\psi^{\prime}(\Phi(q))}+\frac{e^{\zeta_{1}(q)x}}{\psi^{\prime}(\zeta_{1}(q))}+\frac{e^{\zeta_{2}(q)x}}{\psi^{\prime}(\zeta_{2}(q))},

where
\zeta_{2}(q),\zeta_{1}(q)
and
\Phi(q)
are the three real solutions to the equation
\psi(\beta)=q
, which satisfy
\zeta_{2}(q)<-\rho<\zeta_{1}(q)<0<\Phi(q)
. The second scale function,
Z^{(q)}
, takes the form

\displaystyle Z^{(q)}(x)

\displaystyle=1+q\left[\frac{e^{\Phi(q)x}-1}{\Phi(q)\psi^{\prime}(\Phi(q))}+\frac{e^{\zeta_{1}(q)x}-1}{\zeta_{1}(q)\psi^{\prime}(\zeta_{1}(q))}+\frac{e^{\zeta_{2}(q)x}-1}{\zeta_{2}(q)\psi^{\prime}(\zeta_{2}(q))}\right].

Note that since we have exponential jumps (and hence
\Pi(\textup{d}y)=\lambda\rho e^{\rho y}\mathbb{I}_{\{y<0\}}
), we have that for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and
x>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,-x)}V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t)+x+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)

\displaystyle=e^{-\rho x}\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t)+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y).

Then, for any
(t,x)\in[0,m_{\theta}]\times\mathbb{R}
, equation (
3.9
) reads as

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<b^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}e^{-\rho(X_{r}-b^{(\theta)}(r+t))}\mathcal{V}(r+t)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>b^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

where for any
r,s\in[0,m_{\theta})
,
b\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t)+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y).

Note that the equation above suggest that in order to find a numerical value of
b^{(\theta)}
using Theorem
3.9
we only need to know the values of the function
\mathcal{V}
and not the values of
\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(t,x+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta}]
and
x>b^{(\theta)}(t)
. The next Corollary confirms that notion.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
\theta>0
. Assume that
X=\{X_{t},t\geq 0\}
is of the form (
33
) with
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma,\lambda,\rho>0
. Suppose that
c^{(\theta)}
and
\mathcal{U}
are continuous functions on
[0,m_{\theta})
such that
c^{(\theta)}\geq h^{(\theta)}
and
0\geq\mathcal{U}(t)\geq-G^{(\theta)}(t,c^{(\theta)}(t))
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
. For any
(t,x)\in[0,m_{\theta}]\times\mathbb{R}
we define the function

\displaystyle U^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}e^{-\rho(X_{r}-c^{(\theta)}(r+t))}\mathcal{U}(r+t)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right).

Further assume that there exists a value
h>0
such that
U^{(\theta)}(t,x)=0
for any
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and
x\in[b^{(\theta)}(t),b^{(\theta)}(t)+h]
. If
U^{(\theta)}
is a non-positive function, we have that
c^{(\theta)}=b^{(\theta)}
and
U^{(\theta)}=V^{(\theta)}
.

Proof.

First note that, since
X
is of infinite variation,
\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{r}=c^{(\theta)}(r+t))=0
for all
r,t\in[0,m_{\theta})
such that
r+t<m_{\theta}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, by continuity of
G^{(\theta)}
and
\mathcal{U}
, and by dominated convergence theorem, we have that
U^{(\theta)}
is continuous. By means of Theorem
3.9
is enough to show that
U^{(\theta)}
satisfies the integral equation,

\displaystyle U^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}\int_{(-\infty,c^{(\theta)}(r+t)-X_{r})}U^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r}+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}G^{(\theta)}(r+t,X_{r})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}<c^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}e^{-\rho(X_{r}-c^{(\theta)}(r+t))}H(r+t)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}>c^{(\theta)}(r+t)\}}\textup{d}r\right),

where
H(r)=\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(r,c^{(\theta)}(r)+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)
for all
r\in[0,m_{\theta})
and in the last equality we used the explicit form of
\Pi(\textup{d}y)
. Then it suffices to show that
H(t)=\mathcal{U}(t)
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
.

Let
t\geq 0
. For any
\delta\in(0,m_{\theta}-t)
, consider the stopping time

\displaystyle\tau_{\delta}=\inf\{s\in[0,\delta]:X_{s}\notin[c^{(\theta)}(s+t),c^{(\theta)}(s+t)+h]\}.

Note that for any
s<\tau_{\delta}
we have that
X_{s}\in(c^{(\theta)}(s+t),c^{(\theta)}(s+t)+h)
and
X_{\delta}\in(c^{(\theta)}(s+t),c^{(\theta)}(s+t)+h)
in the event
\{\tau_{\delta}=\delta\}
. Then using the strong Markov property at time
\tau_{\delta}
, we have that for any
x\in[c^{(\theta)}(t),c^{(\theta)}(t)+h)
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=U^{(\theta)}(t,x)=-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{\delta}}e^{-\rho(X_{r}-c^{(\theta)}(r+t))}\mathcal{U}(r+t)\textup{d}r\right)+\mathbb{E}_{x}(U^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{\delta},X_{\tau_{\delta}})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{\tau_{\delta}}<c^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{\delta})\}}),

where in the last equality we used the fact that
U^{(\theta)}(t,x)=0
for all
x\in[c^{(\theta)}(t),c^{(\theta)}(t)+h]
, the continuity of
c^{(\theta)}
and that
X
can only cross above
c^{(\theta)}
by creeping. By using the compensation formula for Poisson Random measures, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}(U^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{\delta},X_{\tau_{\delta}})\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{\tau_{\delta}}<c^{(\theta)}(t+\tau_{\delta})\}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{\delta}}\int_{(-\infty,0)}U^{(\theta)}(t+r,X_{r}+y)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{r}+y<c^{(\theta)}(t+r)\}}\textup{d}r\Pi(\textup{d}y)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{\delta}}e^{-\rho(X_{r}-c^{(\theta)}(r+t))}H(r+t)\textup{d}r\right).

Hence we conclude that for any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{\delta}}e^{-\rho(X_{r}-c^{(\theta)}(r+t))}[H(r+t)-\mathcal{U}(r+t)]\textup{d}r\right)

(34)

and hence,

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{\delta}}[H(r+t)-\mathcal{U}(r+t)]\textup{d}r\right).

By continuity of
H
and
\mathcal{U}
we obtain that

\displaystyle 0\leq\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{x}(\tau_{\delta})}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{\delta}}[H(r+t)-\mathcal{U}(r+t)]\textup{d}r\right)=H(t)-\mathcal{U}(t).

Moreover, from equation (
34
) we conclude that
H(t)=\mathcal{U}(t)
for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta})
and the conclusion holds.
∎

Hence, for any
(t,x)\in[0,m_{\theta})\times\mathbb{R}
, we can write

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}K_{1}(t,x,r,b^{(\theta)}(r+t))\textup{d}r-\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}\mathcal{V}(r+t)K_{2}(t,x,r,b^{(\theta)}(r+t))\textup{d}r,

where for any
r,s\in[0,m_{\theta})
,
b\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{(-\infty,0)}V^{(\theta)}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t)+y)\Pi(\textup{d}y)

\displaystyle K_{1}(t,x,s,b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(G^{(\theta)}(s+t,X_{s}+x)\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{s}<b-x\}}\right)

\displaystyle K_{2}(t,x,s,b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-\rho(X_{s}+x-b)}\mathbb{I}_{\{X_{s}>b-x\}}\right).

Take a value
h_{0}>0
sufficiently small. Hence the functions
b^{(\theta)}
and
\mathcal{V}
satisfy the integral equations

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}

\displaystyle K_{1}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t),r,b^{(\theta)}(r+t))\textup{d}r-\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}\mathcal{V}(r+t,b^{(\theta)}(r+t))K_{2}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t),r,b^{(\theta)}(r+t))\textup{d}r=0

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}

\displaystyle K_{1}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t)+h_{0},r,b^{(\theta)}(r+t))\textup{d}r-\int_{0}^{m_{\theta}-t}\mathcal{V}(r+t,b^{(\theta)}(r+t))K_{2}(t,b^{(\theta)}(t)+h_{0},r,b^{(\theta)}(r+t))\textup{d}r=0

for all
t\in[0,m_{\theta}]
. We can approximate the integrals above by Riemann sums so a numerical approximation can be implement. Indeed, take
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
sufficiently large and define
h=m_{\theta}/n
. For each
k\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,n\}
, we define
t_{k}=kh
. Then the sequence of times
\{t_{k},k=0,1,\ldots,n\}
is a partition of the interval
[0,m_{\theta}]
. Then, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
, we approximate
V^{(\theta)}(t,x)
by

\displaystyle V^{(\theta)}_{h}(t_{k},x)=\sum_{i=k}^{n-1}[K_{1}(t_{k},x,t_{i-k+1},b_{i})-\mathcal{V}_{i}K_{2}(t_{k},x,t_{i-k+1},b_{i})]h,

where the sequence
\{(b_{k},\mathcal{V}_{k}),k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
is a solution to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k}^{n-1}[K_{1}(t_{k},b_{k},t_{i-k+1},b_{i})-\mathcal{V}_{i}K_{2}(t_{k},b_{k},t_{i-k+1},b_{i})]=0

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k}^{n-1}[K_{1}(t_{k},b_{k}+h_{0},t_{i-k+1},b_{i})-\mathcal{V}_{i}K_{2}(t_{k},b_{k}+h_{0},t_{i-k+1},b_{i})]=0

for each
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Note that, for
n
sufficiently large, the sequence
\{(b_{k},\mathcal{V}_{k}),k=0,1,\ldots,n\}
is a numerical approximation to the sequence
\{(b^{(\theta)}(t_{k}),\mathcal{V}(t_{k})),k=0,1,\ldots,n\}
(provided that
V^{(\theta)}_{h}\leq 0
) and can be calculated by using backwards induction. The functions
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
can be estimated using simulation methods. In the figures below we include a plot of the numerical calculation of
b^{(\theta)}
and
V^{(\theta)}(0,x)
using the parameters
\theta=\log(2)/10
,
\mu=3
,
\sigma=\lambda=\rho=1
.
