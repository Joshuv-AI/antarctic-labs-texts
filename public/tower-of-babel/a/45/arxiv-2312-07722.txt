Error Analysis for the Implicit Boundary Integral Method
Yimin Zhong, Kui Ren, Olof Runborg, Richard Tsai

Abstract
The implicit boundary integral method (IBIM) provides a framework to construct quadrature rules on regular lattices for integrals over irregular domain boundaries. This work provides a systematic error analysis for IBIMs on uniform Cartesian grids for boundaries with different degree of regularities. We first show that the quadrature error gains an addition order of d − 1 2 \frac{d-1}{2} from the curvature for a strongly convex smooth boundary due to the “randomness” in the signed distances. This gain is discounted for degenerated convex surfaces. We then extend the error estimate to general boundaries under some special circumstances, including how quadrature error depends on the boundary’s local geometry relative to the underlying grid. Bounds on the variance of the quadrature error under random shifts and rotations of the lattices are also derived.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a general simply connected domain with
C^{\infty}

\Gamma
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the origin
{\mathbf{0}}\in\Omega
and define the
\varepsilon

T_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in\Omega\mid 0\leq\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\Gamma)\leq\varepsilon\},

where
\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\Gamma)=\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\Gamma}|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}|
is the

\forall\mathbf{x}\in T_{\varepsilon}
, the projection
P_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}):T_{\varepsilon}\mapsto\Gamma
:

P_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\mathbf{y}\in\Gamma}|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}|

is well-defined and the

is defined by

d_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{n}(P_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}))\cdot(\mathbf{x}-P_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x})),

where
\mathbf{n}
denotes the outward unit normal vector on
\Gamma
.
Let
\sigma
be the boundary Lebesgue measure defined on
\Gamma
. We can then use the co-area formula to rewrite the following boundary integral for
f(\mathbf{x})\in C^{\infty}(\Gamma)

\mathcal{I}(f):=\int_{\Gamma}f(\mathbf{x})d\sigma(\mathbf{x})

into a volumetric integral in
T_{\varepsilon}
:

\mathcal{I}(f)=\int_{T_{\varepsilon}}f(P_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}))\theta_{\varepsilon}(d_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}))J_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},d_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}))d\mathbf{x}.

(1)

The weight function
\theta_{\varepsilon}(s):=\varepsilon^{-1}\theta(\varepsilon^{-1}s)
is a regularized 1D Dirac function such that

\operatorname{supp}\theta=[-1,1]\;\text{ and }\;\int_{-1}^{1}\theta(s)ds=1.

The function
J_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\eta)
is the Jacobian for the projection
P_{\Gamma}
on the level set surface
\{d_{\Gamma}=\eta\}
. For
d=2
,
J_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\eta)=1-\eta\kappa(\mathbf{x})
with
\kappa
the signed curvature of the level curve
\Gamma_{\eta}:=\{\mathbf{x}\mid d_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x})=\eta\}
. For
d=3
,
J_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\eta)=1-2\eta H(\mathbf{x})+\eta^{2}G(\mathbf{x})
with
H
and
G
denoting the mean curvature and Gaussian curvature of the level surface
\Gamma_{\eta}
, respectively.

For the convenience of analysis, we introduce the function class
\mathcal{W}_{q}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
that

\mathcal{W}_{q}:=\Set{f\in C^{q-1}(\mathbb{R})\ }{\begin{array}[]{l}\operatorname{supp}f=[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\;\text{ and }\;\displaystyle\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}f(s)ds=1,\\
\\
f^{(q)}\text{ is a piecewise $C^{1}$ function on $\mathbb{R}$.}\end{array}},\;\text{ if }q\geq 1

and

\mathcal{W}_{0}:=\Set{f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\ }{\begin{array}[]{l}\operatorname{supp}f=[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\;\text{ and }\;\displaystyle\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}f(s)ds=1,\\
\\
f\text{ is a piecewise $C^{1}$ function on $\mathbb{R}$.}\end{array}}.

Here

Usual choices such as

\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\cos}(s)=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left(1+\cos(\varepsilon^{-1}\pi s)\right)\in\mathcal{W}_{2},\quad\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\Delta}(s)=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}(1-\varepsilon^{-1}|s|)\in\mathcal{W}_{1}.

(2)

The implicit boundary integral method (IBIM)
[
chen2017implicit
,
kublik2013implicit
,
kublik2016integration
,
kublik2018extrapolative
]
uses the summation over the regular lattice
(h\mathbb{Z})^{d}
to approximate the integral (
1
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{h}(f)

\displaystyle=h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in(h\mathbb{Z})^{d}\cap T_{\varepsilon}}f(P_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{n}))\theta_{\varepsilon}(d_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{n}))J_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{n},d_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{n})).

(3)

We use
\Theta
and
\mathcal{O}
to denote the standard Landau notations for big-Theta and big-O, respectively. When the width parameter
\varepsilon=\Theta(h^{\alpha})
,
\alpha\in[0,1)
, the theoretical quadrature error previously derived is at the order of
\mathcal{O}(h^{(q+1)(1-\alpha)})
for a general domain with smooth boundary
[
engquist2005discretization
]
. However, the total number of lattice points is at the order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon h^{-d})
, and the width parameter is often chosen as
\varepsilon=\Theta(h)
. This turns the classical quadrature error into
\mathcal{O}(1)
in such a circumstance, a clearly undesired abrupt loss of accuracy.

The objective of this paper is to perform a more careful investigation of the above issue of vanishing accuracy. We perform a systematic error analysis using the Poisson Summation Formula for boundaries of different orders of regularity. For strongly convex smooth boundaries, we show that the quadrature error gains an addition order of
\frac{d-1}{2}
from the curvature of the boundaries due to the “randomness” in the signed distance functions for such boundaries. This additional gain avoids the accuracy catastrophe we see above.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We first discuss in Section
2
the error estimate of the implicit boundary integral method for strongly convex boundaries and the error statistics. We then investigate the issues and possible solutions for a general smooth convex boundary for 2D in Section
3
. Generalization of the estimate of implicit boundary integral to general open curves in 2D is then presented in Section
4
. Concluding remarks are offered in Section
.

2.1
Poisson Summation Formula

We use the standard notation
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for the Schwartz function space on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the Fourier transform of
f
is defined by

\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})e^{-2\pi i\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\zeta}}d\mathbf{x}.

The following Poisson Summation Formula is a standard tool whose proof can be found in
[
stein1993harmonic
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Poisson Summation Formula
[
stein1993harmonic
]
)
.

If
f
satisfies that
|f(\mathbf{u})|+|\widehat{f}(\mathbf{u})|=\mathcal{O}((1+|\mathbf{u}|)^{-d-\mu})
for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and certain
\mu>0
, then

\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{f}(\mathbf{n}).

(4)

In particular, the above formula holds for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Let
h\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
be the lattice resolution in the summation (
3
). If we apply the Poisson Summation Formula to
\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathcal{Q}(h{\mathbf{x}})\ast\psi_{\delta}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}(\mathbf{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}}(\mathbf{n})=h^{-d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}(h^{-1}{\mathbf{n}})\widehat{\psi}(\delta\mathbf{n})\,.

(5)

For the term
\mathbf{n}={\mathbf{0}}
in the last summation, it equals to

h^{-d}\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbf{0}})\widehat{\psi}({\mathbf{0}})=h^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=h^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}.

(6)

which produces the desired integral in (
1
). The objective of the rest of this section is to provide an estimate for the remaining terms that
\mathbf{n}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
.

2.2.1
Sub-critical regularity

We consider two separate cases.

Theorem 2.4
.

Under the assumption that
\Gamma\in C^{\infty}
is closed and strongly convex, if
\varepsilon=\Theta(h^{\alpha})
and
\min(2,\frac{d-1}{2})>q\geq 1
, then
|\mathcal{I}(f)-\mathcal{I}_{h}(f)|=\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{2d-\alpha(d+1)}{d+1-2q}})

Proof.

First, by Lemma
2.3
, we have,

\left|\mathcal{I}(f)-h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}(\mathbf{n})\right|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-q-1}h^{\frac{d+1}{2}+q}\delta^{-\frac{d-1}{2}+q})\,.

(8)

We also estimate the bound for

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})-\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}(\mathbf{n})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})-\mathcal{Q}(h(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{y}))\right)\psi_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}\right|\,.

(9)

\mathcal{Q}(h(\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{y}))-\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})=\mathcal{O}(Lh\delta)\,,

(10)

where

is the Lipschitz constant of

\left|\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})-\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}(\mathbf{n})\right|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}h^{-d+1}\delta)\,.

(11)

Combining the estimates (

\left|\mathcal{I}(f)-\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{-q-1}h^{\frac{d+1}{2}+q}\delta^{-\frac{d-1}{2}+q}+\varepsilon^{-1}h\delta\right)\,.

(12)

Let

(13)

This completes the proof.

2.2.2
Critical regularity

The same calculations can be performed in the case of critical regularity. We have the following result.

Corollary 2.6
.

Under the assumption that
\Gamma\in C^{\infty}
is closed and strongly convex, if
\varepsilon=\Theta(h^{\alpha})
and
\frac{d-1}{2}=q
, then
|\mathcal{I}(f)-\mathcal{I}_{h}(f)|=\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d-1}{2}+(q+1)(1-\alpha)}|\log h|)
.

Proof.

Using the Lemma
2.3
for the critical value
q=\frac{d-1}{2}
, we get

\left|\mathcal{I}(f)-h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}(\mathbf{n})\right|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-q-1}h^{\frac{d+1}{2}+q}|\log\delta|)\,.

If
q<2
, we reuse (
11
), and otherwise we reuse (
16
), to get

\left|\mathcal{I}(f)-h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})\right|=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{-q-1}h^{\frac{d+1}{2}+q}|\log\delta|+\varepsilon^{-1}h\delta\right)\quad&q<2\,,\\
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{-q-1}h^{\frac{d+1}{2}+q}|\log\delta|+\varepsilon^{-2}h^{2}\delta^{2}\right)\quad&q\geq 2\,.\end{cases}

(18)

Let
\varepsilon=\Theta(h^{\alpha})
and balance the orders between
h
and
\delta
in (
18
), we find:

(1)

If
0\leq q<2
, the balance is achieved when
h^{\frac{d-1}{2}+q-q\alpha}|\log\delta|\approx\delta
, which means the error is
\mathcal{O}(h^{1-\alpha+\frac{d-1}{2}+q-q\alpha}|\log\delta|)
. Since
\frac{d-1}{2}+q-q\alpha>0
,
\delta=\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d-1}{2}+q-q\alpha-\tau})
for any
\tau>0
. Hence
|\log\delta|=\mathcal{O}(|\log h|)
.

(2)

If
q\geq 2
, the balance is achieved when
h^{(-q+1)\alpha}h^{\frac{d-3}{2}+q}|\log\delta|\approx\delta^{2}
and the quadrature error is
\mathcal{O}(h^{-(q+1)\alpha}h^{\frac{d+1}{2}+q}|\log\delta|)
. Because
\frac{d-3}{2}+q+(1-q)\alpha>0
, we find that
\delta^{2}=\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d-3}{2}+q+(1-q)\alpha-\tau})
for certain
\tau>0
and it implies
|\log\delta|=\mathcal{O}(|\log h|)
.

The error bounds in both cases are the same; that is,

\left|\mathcal{I}(g)-h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})\right|=\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d-1}{2}+(q+1)(1-\alpha)}|\log h|)\,.

(19)

The finishes the proof.
∎

2.2.3
Super-critical regularity

The bound for the super-critical regularity case follows in the same way.

Corollary 2.7
.

Under the assumption that
\Gamma\in C^{\infty}
is closed and strongly convex, if
\varepsilon=\Theta(h^{\alpha})
and
\frac{d-1}{2}<q
, then
|\mathcal{I}(f)-\mathcal{I}_{h}(f)|=\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d-1}{2}+(q+1)(1-\alpha)})
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.3
, the estimate (
18
) now takes the form

\left|\mathcal{I}(f)-h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})\right|=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{-q-1}h^{\frac{d+1}{2}+q}+\varepsilon^{-1}h\delta\right)\quad&q<2\,,\\
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{-q-1}h^{\frac{d+1}{2}+q}+\varepsilon^{-2}h^{2}\delta^{2}\right)\quad&q\geq 2\,.\end{cases}

(20)

Therefore, the error estimate becomes

\left|\mathcal{I}(g)-h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})\right|=\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d-1}{2}+(q+1)(1-\alpha)})\,,

which is what we need to prove.

The tube width usually takes

as the characteristic function on the tube and

. In such case, a discontinuity of

exists across the boundary

Remark 2.8
.

One interesting case is when the weight function has a jump across the boundary. For instance, the case where
\theta^{C}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\chi_{T_{\varepsilon}}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
is the characteristic function on the tube. Due to the jump, we cannot directly reuse (
10
) for (
9
). We can estimate the summation in three categories

\left|\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})-\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}(\mathbf{n})\right|\leq\mathcal{V}_{in}+\mathcal{V}_{bd}+\mathcal{V}_{out},

where the summation
\mathcal{V}_{in}
,
\mathcal{V}_{bd}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{out}
are respectively

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{in}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{in}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})-\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}(\mathbf{n})\right|,\quad\text{ where }\mathcal{S}_{in}:=\{\mathbf{n}\mid h\mathbf{n}\in T_{\varepsilon},\quad B_{h\mathbf{n}}(h\delta)\subset T_{\varepsilon}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{bd}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{bd}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})-\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}(\mathbf{n})\right|,\quad\text{ where }\mathcal{S}_{bd}:=\{\mathbf{n}\mid h\mathbf{n}\in T_{\varepsilon},\quad B_{h\mathbf{n}}(h\delta)\not\subset T_{\varepsilon}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{out}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\mathcal{S}_{out}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})-\mathcal{Q}_{h,\delta}(\mathbf{n})\right|,\quad\text{ where }\mathcal{S}_{out}:=\{\mathbf{n}\mid h\mathbf{n}\not\in T_{\varepsilon},\quad B_{h\mathbf{n}}(h\delta)\cap T_{\varepsilon}\neq\emptyset\}

We can estimate
\mathcal{V}_{in}
by
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon h^{-d+2}\delta^{2})
by the second order Taylor expansion at
h\mathbf{n}
. For both
\mathcal{V}_{bd}
and
\mathcal{V}_{out}
, there are
\mathcal{O}(\delta h^{-d+1})
lattice points, and each summand is at order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
. Therefore, we obtain the estimate

\left|\mathcal{I}(f)-h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h\mathbf{n})\right|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}h^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\delta^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}+h^{2}\delta^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}\delta h)\,.

(21)

The balance is attained at
\delta\sim\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d-1}{d+1}})
and error is
\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{2d-\alpha(d+1)}{d+1}})
for
\varepsilon=\Theta(h^{\alpha})
. In particular, when
\alpha=1
, we find that the error is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d-1}{d+1}})
. As a comparison, in this case, a standard Monte Carlo integration for
\alpha=1
gives a smaller error, bounded by
\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d-1}{2}})
.

For fixed tube width of
\mathcal{O}(1)
(
\alpha=0
), the error estimates are
\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{2d}{d-1}})
for
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\Delta}
(
q=1
and
d>3
), which is almost second order convergence in high dimensions. It exceeds the theoretical estimate
\mathcal{O}(h)
. For
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\cos}
(
q=2
and
d>5
), the error becomes
\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{4d}{d-1}})
, which is almost 4th order when the dimension is high, exceeding the theoretical estimate
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
. We gain these additional convergence rates from the strong convexity in high dimensions. However, for low dimensional surfaces of
d=2
or
d=3
, once the regularity
q\geq 1\geq\frac{d-1}{2}
, we have a generic error estimate
\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d+1}{2}+q}|\log h|)
or
\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{d+1}{2}+q})
.

2.3
Variance of error under random translations

We now characterize the variance of the quadrature error caused by random shifts of the lattice, an important issue for practical applications
[
izzo2022corrected
,
izzo2023high
,
engquist2005discretization
]
. We denote by
\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the random shift, and denote by
\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\boldsymbol{\zeta})
the implicit-boundary-integral quadrature with the random shift; that is,

I
_h(f;
ξ
) := h^d ∑_
n
∈
Z
^d
Q
(h(
n
+
ξ
)) .
It is clear that the quadrature error is
h
-periodic, hence we only have to consider a random offset
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in[0,1]^{d}
. First, we note that
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathcal{I}(f)
, since

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\boldsymbol{\xi})=h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h(\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\xi}))d\boldsymbol{\xi}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=\mathcal{I}(f).

We therefore define the variance of
\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\boldsymbol{\zeta})
as

\textrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}[\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\boldsymbol{\xi})]:=\int_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in[0,1]^{d}}\left|\mathcal{I}(f)-h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h(\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\xi}))\right|^{2}d\boldsymbol{\xi}\,.

(22)

We are interested in providing an estimate for this variance.

The following theorem follows the idea of the classical result by Kendall
[
kendall1948number
]
. General
L^{p}
estimates can be derived similarly; see, for instance,
[
brandolini2015lp
,
huxley2014fourth
]
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
\Gamma\in C^{\infty}
be closed and strongly convex. If
\varepsilon=\Theta(h^{\alpha})
, then

\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left[\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\boldsymbol{\xi})\right]=\mathcal{O}(h^{d-1+2(q+1)(1-\alpha)})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}(h,\boldsymbol{\zeta}):=h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h(\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}))
. It is then clear that
\mathcal{A}(h,\boldsymbol{\zeta})
has a Fourier series expansion that converges in
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
:

\mathcal{A}(h,\boldsymbol{\zeta})\sim\sum_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}a_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(h)e^{2\pi i(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})}

where the Fourier coefficients are given as

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathcal{A}(h,\boldsymbol{\zeta})e^{-2\pi i(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})}d\boldsymbol{\zeta}

\displaystyle=h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h(\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}))e^{-2\pi i(\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\zeta})}d\boldsymbol{\zeta}

\displaystyle=h^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(h(\mathbf{n}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}))e^{-2\pi i(\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\zeta})}d\boldsymbol{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{x})e^{-2\pi i(h^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\mathbf{x})}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}(h^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})\,.

Notice that
a_{{\mathbf{0}}}=\mathcal{I}(f)
. Therefore, we have

\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left[\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\boldsymbol{\xi})\right]=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\neq 0}|a_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(h)|^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\neq 0}|\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}(h^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})|^{2}\,.

Using Lemma
2.2
, with
q\geq 0
, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\left[\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\boldsymbol{\xi})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2(q+1)}h^{d+1+2q})\sum_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|\boldsymbol{\xi}|\neq 0}|\boldsymbol{\xi}|^{-(d+1)-2q}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(h^{d-1+2(q+1)(1-\alpha)})\,.

The proof is complete.
∎

A probabilistic consequence of the above variance characterization is the following. If one randomly chooses a shift
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the implicit boundary integration, then by the Chebyshev inequality, there exists a constant
C
, depending on both
f
and the surface
\Gamma
, that

\mathbb{P}\Big(|\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\boldsymbol{\xi})-\mathcal{I}(f)|\geq kh^{\frac{d-1}{2}+(q+1)(1-\alpha)}\Big)<Ck^{-2},\ \ k>0\,.

Hence, for weight functions with sub-critical regularity
q<\frac{d-1}{2}
, this implies that it is rare to achieve the possible worst bounds shown in Theorem
2.4
and Theorem
2.5
.

It turns out that the results in this section can be modified slightly for closed, convex but not strongly convex, surface

Let

and Section

As an extreme example, in the following Figure

Remark 2.13
.

Such a difference between smooth convex sets and polytopes has already been spotted in the famous lattice point problem. The early works trace back to Hardy and Littlewood
[
hardy1922some
,
hardy1922some2
]
over a century ago. The lattice point problem studies the discrepancy

The discrepancy could be regarded as random fluctuations if

has a smooth convex boundary. In contrast, if
B
is a polytope, the leading term in the discrepancy may still behave polynomially
[
macdonald1971polynomials
]
in the variable

, which conceals the randomness behind the successive order. In addition, the fluctuations of randomness are closely related to the Diophantine approximation problem; see
[
beck1994probabilistic
,
matousek1999geometric

and references therein.

3.2
Variance of error under random rigid transformations

The discussions in Section

In this section, we show that under random rigid transformations, even with a finite number of points on

alter the “average” quadrature error

Theorem 3.2

Let

Corollary 3.3
.

The same estimate in Theorem
3.2
holds for any closed boundary
\Gamma\in C^{\infty}
if it only has finitely many points with vanishing curvature, whose maximal order is
(\kappa-2)
for
\kappa\in(2,\infty)
.

4
Further discussions on open curves

In this section, we consider the quadrature error on open curves instead of closed ones. For technical reasons in the derivation of the theory, mainly the fact that in the case of open curves, we cannot construct a partition of unity with
C_{0}^{\infty}
functions, we have only weaker results compared to the ones in Section
2
and Section
3
.

We consider two groups of curves: (i) curves with finitely many inflection points (curvature zero) and (ii) line segments.

4.1
General curves

Let
\Gamma
be a collection of curves that
\Gamma=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\Gamma_{k}
,
where each curve
\Gamma_{k}\in C^{\infty}
has only a finite number of points with vanishing curvature, whose maximal order is
(\kappa-2)
,
\kappa\in(2,\infty)
. Let
\mathcal{Q}_{k}(\mathbf{x}):=f(P_{\Gamma_{k}}\mathbf{x})\theta_{\varepsilon}(d_{\Gamma_{k}}(\mathbf{x}))J_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z},d_{\Gamma_{k}}(\mathbf{x}))
, we define generalized implicit boundary integral on the collection
\Gamma
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{h}(f):=h^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in(h\mathbb{Z})^{2}\cap T_{k,\varepsilon}}\mathcal{Q}_{k}(\mathbf{z}),

(29)

where
T_{k,\varepsilon}=\{\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{n}_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mid(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Gamma_{k}\times[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\}
is the segmented tube associated with
\Gamma_{k}
and
\mathbf{n}_{k}(\mathbf{x})
is the unit normal vector at
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma_{k}
. Clearly, the smooth closed boundary in Corollary
3.3
is a special case for
m=1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any rotation
\eta\in\mathrm{SO}(2)
and translation
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in[0,1]^{2}
, we denote the generalized implicit boundary integral with tube width
\varepsilon=\Theta(h^{\alpha})
on the transformed curve collection
\eta\Gamma+h\boldsymbol{\xi}
as
\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\eta,\boldsymbol{\xi})
, then if there are no points with vanishing curvature or there exist finitely many points with vanishing curvature to the maximal order
\kappa-2
for
\kappa\in[2,\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2})
, then

\int_{\mathrm{SO(2)}}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}|\mathcal{I}(f)-\mathcal{I}_{h}(f;\eta,\boldsymbol{\xi})|^{2}d\boldsymbol{\xi}d\eta=\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{2}{\kappa}+{\frac{2}{\kappa-1}(1-\alpha)}}).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
\Gamma_{k}
has at most one point with vanishing curvature at its endpoint, otherwise one can split the curve into shorter pieces. Let
Γ_k ≔{(x, g_k(x)) ∣x∈[0, r]}
such that
g_{k}(x)=|x|^{\kappa}h(x)
,
h\in C^{\infty}[0,r]
and
h(x)>0
for all
x\in[0,r]
.
When
\kappa=2
, then there are no points with vanishing curvature. Using the derivation in Appendix
, the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{Q}_{k}\chi_{T_{k,\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Q}_{k}\chi_{T_{k,\varepsilon}}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\int_{T_{k,\varepsilon}}\mathcal{Q}_{k}(\mathbf{x})e^{-2\pi i\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\zeta}}d\mathbf{x}

(30)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\theta_{\varepsilon}(s)\int_{\Gamma_{k}}f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})e^{-2\pi i(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+s\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\cdot\boldsymbol{\zeta}}d\sigma ds

(30)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\theta_{\varepsilon}(s)\int_{0}^{r}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\phi(x,\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},s)}\sqrt{1+|g^{\prime}_{k}(x)|^{2}}dxds

(30)

where

is the Lebesgue measure on
\Gamma_{k}
,
\chi_{T_{k,\varepsilon}}
denotes the characteristic function on
T_{k,\varepsilon}

\phi(x,\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},s)=x\zeta_{1}+g_{k}(x)\zeta_{2}+s\frac{-g^{\prime}_{k}(x)\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}}{\sqrt{1+|g^{\prime}_{k}(x)|^{2}}}\,.

f(x)

f(r)=0

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Q}_{k}\chi_{T_{k,\varepsilon}}}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\theta_{\varepsilon}(s)\int_{0}^{r}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\phi(x,\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},s)}\sqrt{1+|g^{\prime}_{k}(x)|^{2}}dxds
