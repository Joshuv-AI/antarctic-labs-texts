Survey of Natural Language Processing for Education: Taxonomy, Systematic Review, and Future Trends
Yunshi Lan*, Xinyuan Li, Hanyue Du, Xuesong Lu, Ming Gao, Weining Qian and Aoying Zhou

Abstract
Natural Language Processing (NLP) aims to analyze text or speech via techniques in the computer science field. It serves the applications in domains of healthcare, commerce, education and so on. Particularly, NLP has been widely applied to the education domain and its applications have enormous potential to help teaching and learning. In this survey, we review recent advances in NLP with the focus on solving problems relevant to the education domain. In detail, we begin with introducing the related background and the real-world scenarios in education where NLP techniques could contribute. Then, we present a taxonomy of NLP in the education domain and highlight typical NLP applications including question answering, question construction, automated assessment, and error correction. Next, we illustrate the task definition, challenges, and corresponding cutting-edge techniques based on the above taxonomy. In particular, LLM-involved methods are included for discussion due to the wide usage of LLMs in diverse NLP applications. After that, we showcase some off-the-shelf demonstrations in this domain. At last, we conclude with six promising directions for future research, including more datasets in education domain, controllable usage of LLMs, intervention of difficulty-level control, interpretable educational NLP, methods with adaptive learning, and integrated systems for education. We organize all relevant datasets and papers in the open-available Github Link for better review https://github.com/LiXinyuan1015/NLP-for-Education .

In the education domain, NLP techniques can help education in the following ways:

•

NLP technologies provide automatic key information extraction which facilitates to highlight the key points.
In the cases where teachers and students read passages, tutorials, and textbooks.
The key points and knowledge should be annotated or noticed from unstructured text.
Automatic key information extraction can assistant to identify the key notes which help them understand and comprehend during teaching and learning.

•

NLP technologies offer intelligent tutoring and recommendation.
For example, when students are learning a concept, a system can help recommend relevant concepts, practices, and reading materials such that the students can find proper reading materials as references, which enables active learning and self-inspection for students.

•

NLP technologies help automatic problem solving.
For example, in math or science courses, students may have extensive questions towards the exercises after the classes.
To avoid repeat and overloading questions for teachers, NLP technologies can conduct reasoning and answer the questions automatically.
Moreover, they can give explanations to the complex questions and intermediate solutions for the problems.

•

NLP technologies facilitate automatic smart content generation.
For example, teachers have trouble in preparing the teaching materials from a rush of information on the Internet or textbooks.
With the help of retrieval technologies, we can create a wide variety of digital learning materials, such as retrieving reading passages for students in a reading course.
Similarly, given a passage in the textbook as the context and a concept as the correct answer, NLP techniques can quickly construct a multiple-choice question associated with the test question and the distractors.
This could facilitate the teachers to make quick quizzes for classes.

•

NLP techniques enables personalized instruction.
Students may come from different levels, learn different subjects and have difficulties in different concepts.
In such a case, it is vital to track the learning trajectory and provide personalized instruction to the students.
Various NLP models trained on different annotated data collected from different scenarios could contribute to the personalized education.

•

NLP technologies achieve AI-supported assessment and correction.
For example, it is time-consuming for teachers to grade the essays and codes from students in linguistics or coding classes, by analyzing the textual contents of the assignments, NLP applications can figure out the errors in the assignment and return the corresponding correction to the students.

Textbook Question Answering

Textbook Question Answering (TQA) is a task that requires a system to comprehensively understand the multi-modal information from the textbook curriculum, spreading across text documents, images, and diagrams.
The major challenge of textbook question answering is to comprehend the multi-modal domain-specific contexts as well as the questions, and then identify the key information to the questions.

Datasets.

et al.

Math Word Problem solving

Math Word Problem (MWP) solving is a specific type of question answering, aiming to convert a narrative description to an abstract expression.
This task is challenging because there remains a wide semantic gap to parse human-readable words into machine-understandable logic.

As we can see, there is a set of MWP datasets that examine various mathematics capabilities.

Methods.
The investigation of MWP solving can be traced back to
1960

[
125
]

Early work leverages manually crafted rules and schemas for pattern matching, where human intervention is heavily relied on.
Subsequently, various strategies of feature engineering and statistical learning are proposed to boost the performance.
With the development of techniques and the emergence of large-scale datasets, deep learning-based methods have been widely applied to MWP solving.
The majority of the methods follow an encoder-decoder framework
[
36
]
, which first encodes the narratives into dense vector space, then generates the mathematical expression token as the output sequence.
To improve the generation of complex solution expressions, some methods are proposed to formulate a mathematical expression as an abstract tree
[

,

,

Due to the weak supervision caused by the lack of annotated expressions, multiple studies
[

,
42

Also, some researchers
[

As the MWP solving task is the desirable testbed for evaluating the reasoning capabilities, a series of studies have been developed to discuss the abilities of LLMs for solving MWPs.
Researchers find that prompting the LLMs with effective planning strategies could activate the potential of LLMs, which results in impressive results on MWP solving under the zero/few-shot scenarios.
Chain-of-thought is a typical promoting strategy that is meant to decompose a complicated MWP to sub-questions such that LLMs could conduct reasoning step by step

In this case, the reasoning step can be verified in an iteration manner.

Question Generation

The goal of QG is to automatically generate a question from a given sentence or paragraph, with the assistance of answer information when available.
The challenge lies in identifying the key statement based on the context with or without the utilization of the answer (namely answer-aware QG or answer-agnostic QG

The prompt, which indicates the background knowledge of students or presupposition of the questions, provides hints on the information that should be brought into focus during question generation.
EduQG is a high-quality MCQ dataset generated by educational experts and questions are linked to their cognitive complexity, which makes this dataset move closer to reality.

Distractor Generation

As we mentioned above, DG is another component for question construction, which aims to generate distractors with the given context, question, and correct answer.
A good distractor should be relevant to the context, grammatically coherent to the interrogative of the question, and deceptive as a false answer
[
132
]
.

Datasets.

The aforementioned MCQs for QG tasks such as SciQ and RACE can also be leveraged for DG tasks.
Specifically, the distractors in SciQ are designed by crowd workers with reference
[
132
]
.
Instead of providing entities and concepts as distractors, the distractors of RACE are at sentence-level
[
148
]
, which are collected from the secondary school English exams.
Alongside the datasets mentioned before, there are other MCQ datasets especially designed for DG.
The data of MCQL
[
68
]
is crawled from the web and provides distractors that are either phrases or sentences, covering multi-subjects.
Besides, Televic
[
149
]
is collected through Televic Education’s platform with distractors manually crafted by experts, featuring distractors across multiple languages and domains.

Methods.
Early studies for DG are rule-based and feature-based, which rely on linguistic rules
[
64
]
and specific features like word frequency
[
65
]
or diverse similarity measurement between distractors and correct answers
[
68
]
. With the rapid development of deep learning, the construction of distractors can be divided into two lines of mainstream: generation-based and ranking-based categories
[
70
]
. Generation-based methods automatically generate distractors token by token and ranking-based methods regard DG as a ranking task for a pre-defined distractor candidate set.
Amongst the generation-based methods,
Gao
et al.

[
66
]
proposed a hierarchical model with dual attention for sentence and word relevance and irrelevant information filtering to generate distractors that are semantically consistent with and traceable in the context. Besides,
Bitew
et al.

[
67
]
employed LLMs for DG. They feed relevant questions as in-context examples to generate more plausible distractors.
As for the ranking-based method, Liang
et al.

[
68
]
proposed a two-stage framework: the first stage trains a model as a filter to reduce the candidate size, and the second stage trains another model for ranking the candidate. However, the two-stage framework encounters error accumulation, and some studies have discarded the first stage to address this issue, but led to reduced efficiency
[
69
]
. As follow-up studies, Wang
et al.

[
70
]
proposed an end-to-end model for retrieval in the distractor candidates with a balance of efficiency and high performance.

Due to the availability of the annotation and wide applicability of the commercial value, the majority of the studies usually predict a single score for an essay.

Automated Code Scoring

Similar to AES, ACS grades the score of a code snippet on various dimensions.
Comparing with essay grading, code grading is more complicated in understanding the long-dependency inside a code snippet and the relationship between identifiers.

Datasets.

We notice that there are few publicly-available ACS datasets due to the privacy issue.
The source data contain submissions in programming courses that can be collected on the academy platforms such as JetBrains Academy, MOOC, tutoring sessions and so on

Methods.

Code correctness is a vital factor in judging the quality of a code snippet, which can be quickly evaluated using compilers and interpreters.
Therefore, dynamic analyses evaluate a code snippet by its performance of execution.
By executing on a set of test cases, the execution results are supposed to be the same as the excepted results.
In this way, many dynamic analysis tools for detecting code quality issues are developed, such as

The static features include complexity, comments, number of lines, number of operators, number of operands and so on. The static analysis approaches leverage these features to identify the quality of code
[
86
]
.
Machine learning algorithms also have been applied to ACS.
Clustering, breadth-first search, and neural network approaches are utilized to track the logic errors in code
[

,

]
.
Besides, Queiros
et al.

[

Later, Feng

III-D
1
Grammatical Error Correction

GEC tasks are designed to identify and correct grammatical errors in text, including spelling errors caused by phonological confusion and visual confusion, as well as grammatical errors caused by incorrect use of grammatical rules.
For the GEC task, how to correctly identify and correct grammar errors is difficult, and how to avoid over-correction is challenging.

Datasets.

Lang-8 is one of the most widely used datasets for language error correction, covering 80 languages, but its distribution of languages is very skewed, with Japanese and English being its most prevalent languages
[
168
]
.
CLANG-8
[
168
]
is a clean dataset based on Lang-8, fixing some mismatches between data sources and targets in Lang-8.
CoNLL-2014
[
169
]
and BEA-2019
[
170
]
are both commonly used English language error correction datasets that refine different types of syntax errors.
In addition, with the popularity of GEC tasks, more and more non-English datasets have been proposed and widely used by researchers.
SIGHAN
[
171
]
is an early proposed data set for Chinese spelling check, which only contains grammatical errors such as pronunciation and shape errors of characters.
The CTC
[
172
]
dataset not only contains spelling errors, but also introduces grammatical error and semantic error, which further improves the error types of the Chinese grammatical error dataset.
The FCGEC
[
173
]
and FlaCGEC
[
174
]
datasets have added more linguistic annotations, introduced more granular grammatical rules, and covered 28 and 210 error types, respectively, presenting new challenges to the task of language error correction.
At the same time, there are many GEC datasets for low-resource languages.
There are dataset GECCC
[
175
]
developed in Czech, dataset RULEC-GEC
[
176
]
developed in Russian, dataset Falko-Merlin
[
177
]
developed in German, dataset COWS-L2H
[
178
]
developed in Spanish, dataset UA-GEC
[

]
developed in Ukrainian and dataset RONACC
[

]
developed in Romanian.

Methods.

Early work to handle language error correction tasks is mostly achieved by manually defining rules or building classification-based models, with modest performance
[
91
,
92
]
.
With the widespread use of neural networks, Seq2seq
[
181
]
model has become the mainstream method to solve the task.
It treats grammatical error correction as a monolingual machine translation task, in which source sentences containing grammatical errors are tagged one by one to be translated into the correct target sentences.
The Seq2seq methods are mainly built based on RNN, CNN, or Transformer framework.
BART
[
93
]
, T5
[
94
]
, EBGEC
[
95
]
, SynGEC
[
96
]
and CSynGEC
[
97
]
are good models in practice.
After that, Sequence-to-editing (Seq2edit)
[
182
]
methods have gradually emerged.
It treats the GEC task as a sequential labeling problem, marking the text spans with appropriate error tags, leaving the rest of the text unchanged, and generating one edit operation per prediction.
The commonly used efficient Seq2edit models include PIE
[
98
]
, EditNTS
[
99
]
, LaserTagger
[
100
]
, GECToR
[
101
]
and so on.
Recently, the TemplateGEC
[
183
]
model proposed by Li
et al.
integrates the two frameworks of Seq2seq and Seq2edit, making use of their ability in error detection and correction, alleviating the problem of over-correction of seq2seq model to a certain extent, enhancing the effectiveness and robustness of the model.
As a result, it gains impressive results in GEC tasks.
Even though LLMs are able to solve GEC tasks in nature, researchers found that they encounter severe issues of over-correction
[
184
]
.

Code Error Correction

Code Error Correction (CEC) fixes a buggy code snippet to make it error-free and more coherent.
It is a challenging task as it requires the model to have a sufficient understanding of the code in long-distance dependency and make proper corrections to the erroneous code spans.

Datasets.

Defects4J
[
187
]
, ManyBugs
[
188
]
and IntroClass
[
188
]
are some of the earlier datasets proposed for CEC tasks, focusing on Java and C languages respectively, but with small amounts of data.
Lin
et al.

[
189
]
proposed the first multi-lingual parallel corpus of CEC benchmarks, namely QuixBugs
[
189
]
, which contains
40
buggy code snippets translated into both Java and Python.
Xia
et al.

[
190
]
combined the aforementioned Defects4J, ManyBugs, and QuixBugs datasets into a single CEC evaluation dataset to test the effect of PLM on the CEC task.
Nowadays, the widespread use of collaborative software development platforms such as Github
9
9

9

https://github.com/
makes it possible to collect and analyze code review data from open source projects.
GitHub’s Forge provides a large amount of history-changing and bug-fixing commits from software projects, which can be the resource of CEC datasets in nature.
Bugs2Fix
[
191
]
is one such dataset mined from GitHub bug-fixing commits, containing 2.3M bug-fix pairs (BFPS).
CodeReview
[
192
]
is also a large-scale dataset of code changes and code reviews collected from open source projects, involving nine of the most popular programming languages.
Because CodeReview has some low-quality code review data, and the data distribution in its test set is very similar to the data distribution in the pre-training and fine-tuning datasets, there is a potential risk when training with a model such as ChatGPT, which can affect the evaluation of the model’s generalization ability.
Therefore, Guo
et al.

[
193
]
create a new code review dataset CodeReview-New
[
193
]
based on it.
By designing strict filtering rules to screen out low-quality data in CodeReview, they ended up retaining data from 467 repositories out of an initial 2,029.

Methods.

The CEC and GEC tasks share similar principles and we can formulate both of them as sequence modeling, neural machine translation, and language modeling
[
102
,
103
]
.
Nevertheless, there are still some fundamental differences in their technical implementations.
GEC tasks are typically solved by representing text as word embeddings or other forms of semantic vectors.
While CEC tasks often require models to have a better understanding of long contexts, existing techniques capture the structural and semantic information of code by representing it as a sequence of symbols or an Abstract Syntax Tree (AST)
[
194
]
.
Therefore, several pre-trained language models are trained using a large corpus of programming languages.
CuBERT
[
167
]
is a pre-trained model for CEC tasks, which models only the Python language and is mainly applied to discriminative tasks of code understanding.
CodeBERT
[
195
]
proposed in the same period is also modified on the basis of the BERT model, which is a multilingual programming language model that can handle dual-modal data (i.e. programming language (PL) and natural language (NL)). It supports modeling in 6 programming languages (i.e. Python, Java, JavaScript, PHP, Ruby, and Go) and combines pre-training tasks for replacement tag detection with hybrid objective functions, enabling the model to handle training using NL-PL data pairs.
Both CuBERT and CodeBERT ignore the structural information of code.
In response to this issue, Guo
et al.

[
104
]
proposed the GraphCodeBert model to represent source code information based on AST data flow, so as to reflect deeper code information.
As AST has a tree structure and cannot be directly used as the input of Transformer models, GraphCodeBERT allows the model to learn the AST data flow at the cost of losing a considerable part of information.
In order to make more effective and comprehensive use of the code structure information provided by AST, UniXcoder
[
105
]
improves on the basis of GraphCodeBERT.
They construct a one-to-one mapping function to convert an AST into a sequence structure, and then encode it together with the code text.
In addition, some models use other ways rather than ASTs to better understand and adapt to the structural characteristics of a code snippet, and perform well.
PLBART
[
106
]
adds additional denoising strategies to the BART model to correlate PL and NL text. It generates grammatically and logically valid code via large-scale sequence-to-sequence pre-training that incorporates the denoising strategies to better understand program grammars and logical flows.
CodeT5
[
107
]
uses the T5 model architecture and makes use of the identifier tagging information and comment information to better model the structural characteristics of code.
CodePAD
[
108
]
designs an approach based on pushdown automaton (PDA), which takes into account the grammar constraints of programming languages. It ensures the availability of generated code and the correctness of grammar, and avoids the time-consuming and labor-intensive disadvantages of AST-based approaches.

V
Future Trends

According to the taxonomy and advanced techniques of NLP in the education domain, we discuss several promising future directions for different tasks.

More datasets in education domain
.
We have summarized the commonly used datasets for educational NLP, which range from question answering to error correction, we also observe there is still a demand for datasets fitting in more diverse education scenarios.
Regarding QG tasks, instead of simply considering the single-turn question generation based on the context and answer, we may assume that multi-turn question generation will frequently happen in a tutoring scenario.
Specifically, a leading question around a certain knowledge topic is important in guiding students to learn an ultimate subject goal.
The distinction between such leading QG and traditional QG challenges lies in the connection between the leading generated questions and the final goal
[
11
]
.
However, we find there is no existing challenge that has been defined in this way.
Regarding AES and GEC tasks, event though we have collected a set of AES and GEC datasets for supporting the researches of natural language learning, there is a lack of AES and GEC datasets in non-English languages, which is fundamental in developing systems for low-resource languages.
Furthermore, more interpretable datasets should be proposed to explicit more on the instructional feedback.
For example, diverse dimensional explanation could be given in AES datasets and richer error types aligned to the specific grammatical knowledge schema could be provided in GEC datasets.
Regarding ACS tasks, public ACS datasets are in high demand as the testbed for evaluating the ACS systems.

The controllable usage of LLMs
.
Due to the advancement of LLMs, recent studies have started to incorporate LLMs with various educational NLP tasks.
Researchers have explored to prompt LLMs for solving zero/few-shot QA, generating questions over knowledge bases
[
203
]
, and correcting grammatical errors
[
204
]
but there remain some drawbacks to be resolved.
For example, in QA tasks, even though an LLM is able to act as a “decision center” to understand the questions as well as context and conduct parsing and reasoning, it may perform poorly in a specific knowledge domain.
In this case, techniques like Retrieval-Augmented-Generation (RAG) and Multi-Agents are leveraged to enhance the performance of the LLM
[
33
,
48
]
.
But here comes some new questions.
For example, the presence of noise or contradictory information retrieving from textbooks can detrimentally affect the quality of LLMs’ outputs.
In QG tasks, strongly aligning the outputs of LLMs to the given textbook contexts and generating more controllable questions is an open question.
Similarly, in GEC tasks, multiple studies
[
184
]
point out that applying LLMs on GEC tasks has a high risk of over-correction, that is failing to preserve the semantics of the original sentences.
Therefore, we expect more studies could be proposed to investigate the controllable leverage of LLMs on GEC tasks.

Intervention of difficulty-level control
.
Most existing NLP techniques for education applications neglect difficulty-level control, which is an important factor in the procedure of learning, comprehension, and assessment at different levels.
For example, in QG tasks, generating questions that could be aligned to the level of students is significant in teaching.
Even though there are multiple studies
[
57
,
58
,
59
]
trying to model the difficulty level in the QG task, they simply define with the semantic or reasoning complexity of the questions and fail to align the level to the difficulty level of syllabus.
For generating distractors, we rarely observe existing studies considering the difficulty level.
But this is critical to identify a false candidate answer that is not only relevant to the question but also in accord with the difficulty level of students.
In EC tasks, researchers focus more on the accuracy of the correction but neglect the level of the corrected sentences, including the level of substitute words, the syntactic and semantic level of the corrected sentences.
Further researches should fuse the difficulty level with various tasks to make it serve educational NLP applications better.

Interpretable educational NLP
.
Existing studies are able to obtain good results on various tasks like question answering, question construction, automated assessment, and error correction.
However, there are limited techniques developed with the perspective of interpretability.
As shown in the technical review of QA tasks, recent studies try to explicit the process of thinking in solving Textbook QA and MWPs tasks
[
24
,
127
]
.
In other tasks, interpretability should also be taken into account.
It would be promising to show a process of thinking path to make the construction of the questions more rational.
For AS and EC tasks, by showing the fine-grained explanation to the scoring and correction, it would not only improve tutoring experience but also benefit the methods as well as systems to diagnose their functions.

, interpretablity should be further studied and explored with more attention.

Methods with adaptive learning
.
Even though NLP applications can be applied to education domain, there are a few studies involving adaptive learning to different tasks.
For QG task, we notice some researchers
[

This benefits to build a more customized education system.
Such adaptive learning is in high demand to collaborate with other tasks.
For example, in EC tasks, when we correct the sentences and codes, we can consider the level of the students based on their input to decide the way to correct.
If it is a simple sentence with a mis-used word, we should not substitute it with an obscure words.
Similarly, for a piece of erroneous code, we should not correct its errors using the knowledge beyond the knowledge of the students.
In this case, developing methods with adaptive learning for various NLP tasks is important.

Generating responses from the system based on the question from the users may be not practical.

Conclusions

This survey attempted to provide a comprehensive overview of NLP in the education domain, as depicted in Figure
2
.
We highlight that NLP techniques can be applied to various procedure of education.
After process and analysis the raw data from diverse data source, NLP applications can be directly serve to tutoring and learning.
Our survey highlights four representative tasks (i.e., question answering, question construction, automated assessment, and error correction) with their fine-grained sub-tasks as the focus of this survey due to their significant impact in teaching and learning.
According to the taxonomy, we go deep into these tasks and illustrate the evolution of their techniques.
For each task, we describe its task definition, unique challenges in education domain, and its corresponding solutions.
Early studies developed based on rule-based, deep learning-based methods to solve these tasks.
Thank to the advancement of LLMs, a number of methods start collaborating LLMs with traditional methods.
This innovation has led to a easier paradigm that improve the performance and decrease the demand of the annotation data.
However, LLMs also encounter some new issues like domain knowledge incorporation and over-correction when we apply them to specific tasks.
Therefore, future studies need to fuse LLMs to the diverse tasks with the consideration of solving the their drawbacks.
Then, to help readers apply applications on education scenarios, we present the off-the-shelf toolkits and demonstrations.
Despite the strides made in NLP applications on education domain, there still remains improvement room for diverse tasks.
