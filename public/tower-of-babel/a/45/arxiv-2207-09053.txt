On k k -layered numbers
Farid Jokar

Abstract
. A positive integer n n is said to be k k -layered if its divisors can be partitioned into k k sets with equal sum. In this paper, we start the systematic study of these class of numbers. In particular, we state some algorithms to find some even k k -layered numbers n n such that 2 α ​ n 2^{\alpha}n is a k k -layered number for every positive integer α \alpha . We also find the smallest k k -layered number for 1 ≤ k ≤ 8 1\leq k\leq 8 . Furthermore, we study when n ! n! is a 3 3 -layered and when is a 4 4 -layered number. Moreover, we classify all 4 4 -layered numbers of the form n = p α ​ q β ​ r ​ t n=p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}rt , where α \alpha , 1 ≤ β ≤ 3 1\leq\beta\leq 3 , p p , q q , r r , and t t are two positive integers and four primes, respectively. In addition, in this paper, some other results concerning these numbers and their relationship with k k -multiperfect numbers, near-perfect numbers, and superabundant numbers are discussed. Also, we find an upper bound for the differences of two consecutive k k -layered numbers for every positive integer 1 ≤ k ≤ 5 1\leq k\leq 5 . Finally, by assuming the smallest k k -layered number, we find an upper bound for the difference of two consecutive k k -layered numbers.



1.
Introduction

A perfect number is a positive integer that is equal to the sum of its proper positive divisors. In 2003, the idea of Zumkeller numbers, which is a generalization of perfect numbers, was first introduced by Zumkeller in the Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS)
[
14
]
(
A083207
).

Definition 1.1
.

A positive integer
n
is said to be Zumkeller if the set of all positive divisors of
n
can be partitioned into two subsets with the same sum. A Zumkeller partition for a Zumkeller number
n
is
a partition
\{A_{1},A_{2}\}
of the set of all positive divisors of
n
such that
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
sums to the same value.

Shortly thereafter, Clark et al.
[
12
]
announced several results and conjectures related to Zumkeller numbers. Peng and Bhaskara Rao
[
18
]
found some other results about Zumkeller numbers; they also studied the relationship between practical numbers (to be defined later in the paper) and Zumkeller numbers. They also settled a conjecture from
[
12
]
. Moreover, they made substantial contributions towards the second conjecture of Clark et al.
[
12
]
. Recently, the author studied a generalization of Zumkeller numbers called
k
-layered numbers
[
5
]
which is the principal object of study in this paper.

Definition 1.2
.

A positive integer
n
is said to be
k
-layered if the set of all positive divisors of
n
can be partitioned into
k
subsets with the same sum. A
k
-layered partition for a
k
-layered number

is
a partition
\{A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}\}
of the set of all positive divisors of
n
such that for every
1\leq i\leq k
, each of
A_{i}
sums to the same value.

In
[
9
]
, the authors found some new results related to Zumkeller numbers and
k
-layered numbers. It is clear from the definition of
k
-layered numbers that all Zumkeller numbers are
2
-layered numbers. Another generalization of perfect numbers which we will consider are multiperfect numbers or
k
-multiperfect number and near-perfect numbers. A positive integer
n
is called a multiperfect number or a

-multiperfect number if
n
satisfies the equation
\sigma(n)=kn
, for some positive integer

. It is easy to check that every perfect number is Zumkeller; this immediately gives us a further motivation to define
k
-layered numbers as all multiperfect numbers will be
k
-layered numbers.

This paper is arranged as follows: in Sections
2
and

, we recall and generalize some results related to

-layered numbers, taking as basis the results already proved for Zumkeller numbers. In Section

Lastly, by assuming the smallest

3.
Almost practical numbers and
k
-layered numbers

In this section we discuss the connection between
k
-layered numbers and almost practical numbers, which are a generalization of practical numbers. The results discussed here will be a generalization of the results connecting Zumkeller numbers and practical numbers that were found by Peng and Bhaskara Rao
[
18
]
.

First, we recall the definition of practical numbers.

Definition 3.1
.

A positive integer
n
is said to be a practical number if every positive integer less than
n
can be represented as a sum of distinct positive divisors of
n
.

Now we recall two results about practical numbers.

Proposition 3.2

(Corollary 1,
[
3
]
)
.

A positive integer
n
with the prime factorization
p_{1}^{t_{1}}p_{2}^{t_{2}}\cdots p_{m}^{t_{m}}
and
p_{1}<p_{2}<\dots<p_{m}
is a practical number if and only if
p_{1}=2
and
p_{i+1}\leq\sigma(p_{1}^{t_{1}}\cdots p_{i}^{t_{i}})+1
for
1\leq i\leq m-1
.

Proposition 3.3

(Proposition 8,
[
18
]
)
.

A positive integer
n
is a practical number if and only if every integer less than or equal to
\sigma(n)
can be written as a sum of distinct divisors of
n
.

We now define almost practical numbers, which are a generalization of practical numbers. These numbers were first studied by Stewart
[
3
]
.

Definition 3.4
.

A positive integer
n
is called an almost practical number if all of the numbers
j
such that

2<j<\sigma(n)-2
can be written as a sum of distinct divisors of
n
.

Remark 3.5
.

It is clear that every practical number is an almost practical number.

Now we recall some results related to almost practical numbers from
[
3
]
.

Proposition 3.6

(Theorem 3,
[
3
]
)
.

Let
n\neq 3
be an odd positive integer and
1=d_{1}<d_{2}<\dots<d_{k}=n
are the divisors of
n
. We also define
\sigma_{i}=d_{1}+d_{2}+\cdots+d_{i}
. Then,
n
is an almost practical number if and only if
d_{2}=3,d_{3}=5
and for
i\geq 3
, at least one of the following is true:

(1)

d_{i+1}\leq\sigma_{i}-2
and
d_{i+1}\neq\sigma_{i}-4
,

(2)

d_{i+1}=\sigma_{i}-4
and
d_{i+2}=\sigma_{i}-2
.

Remark 3.7
.

If
n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\dots p_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}
is an odd almost practical number where
p_{1}<p_{2}<\dots<p_{m}
are all prime factors of
n
, then by Proposition
3.6
, it is clear that
p_{3}=7
.

Theorem 3.8

(Theorem 4,
[
3
]
)
.

Let
n\neq 3
be an odd almost practical number and
p
be an odd prime, then
pn
is an almost practical number if and only if
2p\leq\sigma(n)-2
and
2p\neq\sigma(n)-4
.

Remark 3.9
.

By Propositions
3.6
and
3.2
, if
n
is an odd almost practical number, then
2n
is a practical number.

Proposition 3.10

(Corollary 4,
[
3
]
)
.

Let
n\neq 3
be an odd almost practical number and
p
be a prime dividing
n
, then
pn
is an almost practical number.

Example 3.11
.

By Proposition
3.6
, one can check directly that
3^{3}\times 5\times 7
is an almost practical number. Therefore, by Proposition
3.10
, if
n=3^{\alpha_{1}}\times 5^{\alpha_{2}}\times 7^{\alpha_{3}}
such that
\alpha_{1}\geq 3
,
\alpha_{2}
, and
\alpha_{3}
are positive integers, then the number
n
is an almost practical number.

Example 3.12
.

Let
k\geq 4
be a positive integer. Also, let
p_{i}
denotes
i
th prime number. By Bertrand postulate, for every integer
i\geq 4
, we have

2p_{i}<4p_{i-1}\leq\sigma(p_{2}p_{3}\cdots p_{i-1})-4.

Thus, by Example
3.11
and Theorem
3.8
, if
m=p_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{3}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{\alpha_{k-1}}
such that
\alpha_{1}\geq 3
,
\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1}
are positive integers, then
m
is an almost practical number.

Now we are going to investigate the relation between almost practical numbers and Zumkeller numbers.
The following proposition is a generalization of Proposition 10 of
[
18
]
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
n\neq 3
be an almost practical number. Then,
n
is Zumkeller if and only if
\sigma(n)
is even.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Proposition 10 of
[
18
]
.
∎

Example 3.14
.

Let
k\geq 4
be a positive integer, and also let
p_{i}
denotes
i
th prime number. Now let

\alpha_{1}\geq 3

\alpha_{2},\ldots

is an odd number. In other words, the number

Now we state a proposition which is a generalization of Proposition

[

]

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
\alpha
be a positive integer. Also, let

be non-

\gcd(n,p)=1

-layered number, then

. Moreover, if

Proof.

The proof is identical to the proof of Proposition

of
[

In Proposition 20 of
[
18
]
, the authors by a simple method proved that every odd Zumkeller number possesses at least three distinct prime factors. On the other hand, we know that for every positive integer

is a deficient number. Therefore, by Propositions

and

Corollary 3.16

Let

and

be prime numbers. Also, let
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
be positive integers. The number
p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}
is Zumkeller if and only if
p_{1}=2
,
p_{2}\leq\sigma(2^{\alpha_{1}})
and
\alpha_{2}
is odd.

Remark 3.17
.

Let

be an odd prime number. Also. let
\alpha
and
\beta
be positive integers. One can check that the number

is abundant if and only if

(for more details, see the proof of Theorem 2.6 of
[

]
). Thus, if

is an abundant number that possesses two factors, then

is Zumkeller if and only if

satisfies the conditions
\mathrm{(i)}\
and
\mathrm{(iii)}\
of Proposition
2.3
.

We start this section with two definitions.

Definition 4.1

A positive integer

.

Definition 4.2

A positive integer

Remark 4.3

It is clear that a positive integer

Let

, and

Proposition 4.4
.

For positive integers

Proof.

By Proposition

∎

We recall that a positive integer
n

I(n)>I(m)
for all positive integers

. Now we recall a well-know theorem about superabundant numbers of
[

Theorem 4.5

If

, where
p_{i}
is

. Also,

except when

or

Thus, by previous theorem, every superabundant is almost practical (see Example

Corollary 4.6
.

A positive integer
n
is superabundant if and only if one of the following holds:

\mathrm{(i)}\

n
is Zumkeller;

\mathrm{(ii)}\

n=4
or
n=36
.

A list of all superabundant numbers less than
10^{1200}
can be found in
[
13
]
. The following proposition states one of the special properties of superabundant numbers.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
m_{1}<m_{2}
be two consecutive superabundant numbers. For every positive integer
t<m_{2}
,
I(t)\leq I(m_{1})
, and the equality holds when
t=m_{1}
.

Proof.

If
t\leq m_{1},
then
I(t)\leq I(m_{1})
. So, we assume
m_{1}<t_{1}<m_{2},
such that
I(t_{1})>I(m_{1}).
Since
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are consecutive superabundant numbers, therefore
t_{1}
is not a superabundant number. So, there exists
t_{2}<t_{1}
such that
I(t_{2})>I(t_{1})
. Hence
I(t_{2})>I(t_{1})>I(m_{1}),
which implies
m_{1}<t_{2}<t_{1}
. We once again conclude that there exists an integer
t_{3}
where
m_{1}<t_{3}<t_{2}<t_{1}
such that
I(m_{1})<I(t_{1})<I(t_{2})<I(t_{3})
. Thus, for every positive integer

, by this algorithm, inductively, we can find distinct positive integers

and

such that

and

; this contradicts the finiteness of the set

∎

p_{m+1}
). Thus,
\ell^{\prime}=(b+t)^{s+1}_{t=0}
is a
(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m},p_{m+1})
-
\gcd
chain. This completes the proof.
∎

In remainder of this note, we define
\phi
to be the Euler’s totient function. Let
n
be a positive integer with prime factorization
p^{\alpha_{1}}_{1}p^{\alpha_{2}}_{2}\cdots p^{\alpha_{m}}_{m}
. We recall that
\phi(n)=n\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-\dfrac{1}{p_{i}})
. In Proposition
8.4
, we found a lower bound for
L_{max}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m})
. Now we find an upper bound for
L_{max}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m})
.

Proposition 8.6
.

L_{max}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m})\leq p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}-(p_{1}-1)(p_{2}-1)\cdots(p_{m}-1).

Proof.

Let
t=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}
. Let
r
be a non-negative integer such that
0\leq r<t
. We know that for every positive integer
j
,
\gcd(jt+r,t)=1
if and only if
\gcd(r,t)=1
. Therefore, between
0
and
t
there exist exactly
\phi(t)
distinct integers
r_{1},r_{2},\dots,
and
r_{\phi(t)}
such that
1=r_{1}<r_{2}<\dots<r_{\phi(t)}<t
and
\gcd(jt+r_{i},t)=1
for every integers
1\leq i\leq\phi(t)
and
j\geq 0
. This concludes that between
t
and
2t
there exist exactly
\phi(t)
positive integers
n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots<n_{\phi(t)}
such that
\gcd(n_{i},t)=1
for every integer
1\leq i\leq\phi(t)
. Thus,
L_{max}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m})\leq t-\phi(t).
This completes the proof.
∎

The following proposition calculates the function
L_{max}
at some value.

Proposition 8.7
.

\mathrm{(i)}\ L_{max}(3,5,7,11)=6
.

\mathrm{(ii)}\ L_{max}(3,5,7,11,13,17)=12
.

Proof.

\mathrm{(i)}\

(mod

(mod

a\equiv-3
(mod

a\equiv-4
(mod
3
) and
a\equiv-5
(mod
5

\ell=(a+t)^{5}_{t=0}
is a
(3,5,7,11)
-
\gcd
chain. Now it is sufficient to prove that for every positive integer
a^{\prime}
, there exists an integer

\gcd(a^{\prime}+i,3\times 5\times 7\times 11)=1

\ell^{\prime}=(a^{\prime}+t)^{6}_{t=0}

|A_{5}|\leq 2

|A_{3}\cup A_{5}\cup A_{7}\cup A_{11}|\leq 6

|A_{3}\cup A_{5}\cup A_{7}\cup A_{11}|\leq 6

\mathrm{(ii)}\

We can find some more results about the function
L_{max}
. But we do not pursue this here. Now we state a proposition that finds a lower density for the set of
k
-layered numbers by having the smallest

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
k
be a positive integer, and
n
be the smallest
k
-layered number with prime factorization
p^{\alpha_{1}}_{1}p^{\alpha_{2}}_{2}\cdots p^{\alpha_{m}}_{m}
, where
p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{m}
. Then
(L_{max}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m})+1)n
is an upper bound for the difference of two consecutive
k
-layered numbers.

Proof.

Let
a
and
b
be two consecutive
k
-layered numbers. By the division algorithm, there exist non-negative integers
s\neq 0
and
r
such that
a=sn+r
and
0\leq r<s
. By the definition of
L_{max}
and Proposition
2.6
, we know that there exists an integer
\ell
with
s+1\leq\ell\leq s+1+L_{max}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m})
such that
\ell n
is
k
-layered. Then
b-a\leq\ell n-sn\leq(L_{max}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m})+1)n
.
∎
