A Pythagorean theorem for partitioned matrices
Jean-Christophe Bourin and Eun-Young Lee

Abstract




Abstract.
We establish a Pythagorean theorem for the absolute values of the blocks of a partitioned matrix. This leads to a series of remarkable operator inequalities. For instance, if the matrix
\mathbb{A}
is partitioned into three blocks
A,B,C
, then

|\mathbb{A}|^{3}\geq U|A|^{3}U^{*}+V|B|^{3}V^{*}+W|C|^{3}W^{*},

\sqrt{3}|\mathbb{A}|\geq U|A|U^{*}+V|B|V^{*}+W|C|W^{*},

for some isometries
U,V,W
, and

\mu_{4}^{2}(\mathbb{A})\leq\mu_{3}^{2}(A)+\mu_{2}^{2}(B)+\mu_{1}^{2}(C)

where
\mu_{j}
stands for the
j
-th singular value. Our theorem may be used to extend a result by Bhatia and Kittaneh for the Schatten
p
-noms and to give a singular value version of Cauchy’s Interlacing Theorem.

Keywords.
Partitioned matrices, functional calculus, matrix inequalities.

2010 mathematics subject classification.
15A18, 15A60, 47A30.

1
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{M}_{d}
denote the space of
d
-by-
d
matrices. If
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
, the polar decomposition holds,

\mathbb{A}=U|\mathbb{A}|

(1.1)

where
|\mathbb{A}|\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
is positive semi-definite and
U\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
is a unitary matrix. The matrix
|\mathbb{A}|
is called the absolute value of
\mathbb{A}
, and its
eigenvalues are the singular values of
\mathbb{A}
. The absolute value can be defined for
d\times d^{\prime}
matrices
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
as a positive matrix
|\mathbb{A}|\in\mathbb{M}_{d^{\prime}}
, and the factor
U
in (
1.1
) is an isometry (
d\geq d^{\prime}
) or a coisometry (
d<d^{\prime}
).

If
\mathbb{A}
is partitioned in some number of rectangular blocks, say four blocks
A,B,C,D
, it is of interest to have a relation between the absolute value
|\mathbb{A}|
and the absolute values of the blocks. By using the standard inner product of
\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
, we immediately have the trace relation

{\mathrm{Tr\,}}|\mathbb{A}|^{2}={\mathrm{Tr\,}}|A|^{2}+{\mathrm{Tr\,}}|B|^{2}+{\mathrm{Tr\,}}|C|^{2}+{\mathrm{Tr\,}}|D|^{2}.

This note aims to point out a much stronger Pythagorean theorem, Theorem
2.1
, and several consequences. This result holds for many partitionings of
\mathbb{A}
, not only when
A
is a block matrix partitioned into
p\times q
blocks. For instance, one may consider the matrix

\mathbb{A}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}&b_{4}&b_{5}&b_{6}\\
a_{5}&a_{6}&c_{1}&c_{2}&d_{1}\\
a_{7}&a_{8}&c_{3}&c_{4}&d_{2}\\
a_{9}&a_{10}&c_{5}&c_{6}&d_{3}\end{pmatrix}

partitioned into four obvious blocks
A,B,C,D
.

If
\mathbb{A}
is partitioned into
r
blocks
A_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{k},m_{k}}
, we write

\mathbb{A}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}A_{k}=A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{r}

(1.2)

where we can use the
=
sign if one considers
A_{k}
not only as an element of
\mathbb{M}_{n_{k},m_{k}}
but also as a submatrix of

\mathbb{A}
with its position in
\mathbb{A}
.

We say that the partitioning (
1.2
) is colum compatible, or that
\mathbb{A}
is partitioned into colum compatible blocks if for all pairs of indexes
k,l
, either
A_{k}
and
A_{l}
lie on the same set of columns of
\mathbb{A}
, or
A_{k}
and
A_{l}
lie on two disjoint sets of columns of
\mathbb{A}
. Similarly, (
1.2
) is row compatible, if for all pairs of indexes
k,l
, either
A_{k}
and
A_{l}
lie on the same set of rows of
\mathbb{A}
, or
A_{k}
and
A_{l}
lie on two disjoint sets of rows of
\mathbb{A}
.

Our Pythagorean Theorem
2.1
will be stated for row or column compatible blocks. An application is a Theorem of Bhatia and Kittaneh for the Schatten
p
-norms (Corollary
2.5
). Another application is an inequality for the singular values of compression onto hyperplanes. A matrix
A\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
is an operator on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Given a hyperplane
{\mathcal{S}}
of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, we have a unit vector
h
such that
{\mathcal{S}}=h^{\perp}
, that is
x\in{\mathcal{S}}\iff\langle h,x\rangle=h^{*}x=0
. The compression
A_{\mathcal{S}}
of
A
onto
{\mathcal{S}}
is the operator acting on
{\mathcal{S}}
defined as the restriction of
EA
to
{\mathcal{S}}
where
E
stands for the (orthogonal) projection onto
{\mathcal{S}}
. Theorem
2.1
entails a bound for the singular values of
A_{\mathcal{S}}
in terms of those of
A
. These results are given in Section 3; we state a special case in the following corollary. Let
\mu_{j}
denote the
j
-th singular value arranged in nonincreasing order.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be a normal matrix and let
{\mathcal{S}}
be a hyperplane of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
orthogonal to the unit vector
h
. Set
\beta=\|Ah\|^{2}-|\langle h,Ah\rangle|^{2}
. Then,
for
j=1,\ldots,d-1
,

\mu_{j}^{2}(A)\geq\mu_{j}^{2}(A_{\mathcal{S}})\geq\mu^{2}_{j+1}(A)-\beta.

We discuss the case of four and five blocks in Section 4. For four blocks, our Pythagorean theorem entails an interesting inequality stated in the next corollary.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
be partitioned into four blocks
A,B,C,D
. Then, there exist some isometries
U,V,W,X
of suitable sizes such that

2|\mathbb{A}|\geq U|A|U^{*}+V|B|V^{*}+W|C|W^{*}+X|D|X^{*}.

The last section is devoted to several other operator inequalities such as the first inequality in the abstract.

2
A Pythagorean theorem

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
be partitioned into
r
row or column compatible blocks
A_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{k},m_{k}}
. Then, there exist some isometries
U_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{d^{\prime},m_{k}}
such that

|\mathbb{A}|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}U_{k}|A_{k}|^{2}U_{k}^{*}.

Recall that
U\in\mathbb{M}_{d^{\prime},m}
,
m\leq d^{\prime}
, is an isometry if
U^{*}U=\mathbb{1}_{m}
, the identity on
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. If
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,1}
, then the theorem reads as Pythagoras Theorem.

Proof.

Consider a positive matrix in
\mathbb{M}_{n+m}
partitioned as

\begin{bmatrix}A&X\\
X^{*}&B\end{bmatrix}

with diagonal blocks
A\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
and
B\in\mathbb{M}_{m}
. By
[
5
, Lemma 3.4]
we have two unitary matrices
U,V\in\mathbb{M}_{n+m}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}A&X\\
X^{*}&B\end{bmatrix}=U\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}U^{*}+V\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&B\end{bmatrix}V^{*},

(2.1)

equivalently,

\begin{bmatrix}A&X\\
X^{*}&B\end{bmatrix}=U_{1}AU_{1}^{*}+U_{2}BU_{2}^{*}

for two isometry matrices
U_{1}\in\mathbb{M}_{n+m,n}
and
U_{2}\in\mathbb{M}_{n+m,m}
. An obvious iteration of (
2.1
) shows that, given a positive block matrix in
\mathbb{M}_{m}
partitioned into
p\times p
blocks,

\mathbb{B}=\left(B_{i,j}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq p},

with square diagonal blocks
B_{i,i}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{i}}
and
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{p}=m
, we have the decomposition

\mathbb{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}U_{i}B_{i,i}U_{i}^{*}

(2.2)

for some isometries
U_{i}\in\mathbb{M}_{m,n_{i}}
.

We use (
2.2
) to prove the theorem. Consider first the column compatible case. Thus we have a partitioning into
p
block columns,

\mathbb{A}={\mathbf{C}}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup{\mathbf{C}}_{p},

(2.3)

and each block
A_{k}
belongs to one block column
{\mathbf{C}}_{q}
. By relabelling the
A_{k}
’s if necessary, we may assume that we have
p
integers
1=\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{p}<r
such that

{\mathbf{C}}_{q}=A_{\alpha_{q}}\cup\cdots\cup A_{\alpha_{q+1}-1},\quad 1\leq q<p,\quad{\text{and}}\quad{\mathbf{C}}_{p}=A_{\alpha_{p}}\cup\cdots\cup A_{\alpha_{r}}.

We also have a partitioning into
p
block rows,

\mathbb{A}^{*}={\mathbf{C}}^{*}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup{\mathbf{C}}^{*}_{p},

(2.4)

and multiplying (
2.4
) and (
2.3
) we then obtain a block matrix for
\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}=|\mathbb{A}|^{2}\in\mathbb{M}_{d^{\prime}}
,

|\mathbb{A}|^{2}=\left({\mathbf{C}}^{*}_{i}{\mathbf{C}}_{j}\right)_{1\leq,i,j\leq p}.

By the decomposition (
2.2
) we have

|\mathbb{A}|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}U_{i}{\mathbf{C}}^{*}_{i}{\mathbf{C}}_{i}U_{i}^{*}

for some isometries
U_{i}\in\mathbb{M}_{d^{\prime},n_{i}}
, where
n_{i}
is the number of columns of
{\mathbf{C}}_{i}
. Hence, with the convention
\alpha_{p+1}:=r+1
,

|\mathbb{A}|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{k=\alpha_{i}}^{\alpha_{i+1}-1}U_{i}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}U_{i}^{*}

establishing the theorem for a column compatible partitioning.

Now, we turn to the row compatible case. Thus we have a partitioning into
p
block rows,

\mathbb{A}={\mathbf{R}}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup{\mathbf{R}}_{p},

(2.5)

and each block
A_{k}
belongs to one block row
{\mathbf{R}}_{q}
and, as in the colum compatible case, we may assume that we have
p
integers
1=\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{p}<r
such that

{\mathbf{R}}_{q}=A_{\alpha_{q}}\cup\cdots\cup A_{\alpha_{q+1}-1},\quad 1\leq q<p,\quad{\text{and}}\quad{\mathbf{R}}_{p}=A_{\alpha_{p}}\cup\cdots\cup A_{\alpha_{r}}.

We also have a partitioning into
p
block columns,

\mathbb{A}^{*}={\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup{\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{p}

(2.6)

Mutiply (
2.6
) and (
2.5
) and note that

|\mathbb{A}|^{2}=\sum_{l=1}^{p}{\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{l}{\mathbf{R}}_{l}.

(2.7)

with
p
block matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{d^{\prime}}
, (
l=1,\ldots,p
),

{\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{l}{\mathbf{R}}_{l}=\left(A^{*}_{i}A_{j}\right)_{\alpha_{l}\leq i,j<\alpha_{l+1}}

(2.8)

where we still use
\alpha_{p+1}:=r+1
. Applying the decomposition (
2.2
) to the block matrices
(
2.8
) yields

{\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{l}{\mathbf{R}}_{l}=\sum_{i=\alpha_{l}}^{\alpha_{l+1}-1}U_{i}|A_{i}|^{2}U_{i}^{*}

for some isometries
U_{i}
of suitable sizes,
and combining with (
2.7
) completes the proof. ∎

Denote by
\mu_{1}(S)\geq\mu_{2}(S)\geq\cdots
the singular values of a matrix
S\in\mathbb{M}_{n,m}
. This list is often limited to
\min\{n,m\}
elements, however we can naturally define
\mu_{k}(S)=0
for any index
k
larger than
\min\{n,m\}
. Given two matrices of same size, a classical inequality of Weyl asserts that

\mu_{j+k+1}(S+T)\leq\mu_{j+1}(S)+\mu_{k+1}(T)

for all nonnegative integers
j
and
k
. This inequality and Theorem
2.1
entail the next corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
be partitioned into
r
row or column compatible blocks
A_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{k},m_{k}}
. Then, for all nonnegative integers
j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{r}
,

\mu^{2}_{j_{1}+j_{2}+\cdots+j_{r}+1}(\mathbb{A})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mu^{2}_{j_{k}+1}(A_{k}).

A special case of this inequality is given in the abstract for three blocks and
j_{1}=2
,
j_{2}=1
,
j_{3}=0
.

Since any partitinioning into three blocks is row or column compatible we have the next corollary.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
be partitioned into three blocks
A,B,C
. Then, there exist some isometries
U,V,W
of suitable sizes such that

|\mathbb{A}|^{2}=U|A|^{2}U^{*}+V|B|^{2}V^{*}+W|C|^{2}W^{*}.

By using the triangle inequality for the Schatten
p
-norms we have the trace inequality

\left\{{\mathrm{Tr}}\,|\mathbb{A}|^{2p}\right\}^{1/p}\leq\left\{{\mathrm{Tr}}\,|A|^{2p}\right\}^{1/p}+\left\{{\mathrm{Tr}}\,|B|^{2p}\right\}^{1/p}+\left\{{\mathrm{Tr}}\,|C|^{2p}\right\}^{1/p},\quad p\geq 1,

(2.9)

equivalently

\|\mathbb{A}\|^{2}_{q}\leq\|A\|^{2}_{q}+\|B\|^{2}_{q}+\|C\|^{2}_{q}

(2.10)

for all Schatten
q
-norms,
q\geq 2
.

Theorem
2.1
entails another interesting relation between the blocks of a partitioned matrix and the full matrix.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
be partitioned into
r
row or column compatible blocks
A_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{k},m_{k}}
.
Then, for some isometries
V_{j}\in\mathbb{M}_{m,d^{\prime}}
, with
m=\sum_{k=1}^{r}m_{k}
,

\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}|A_{k}|^{2}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}V_{j}|\mathbb{A}|^{2}V_{j}^{*}.

Proof.

From Theorem
2.1
and the main result of
[
6
]
we have

\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}U_{k}|A_{k}|^{2}U_{k}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}W_{j}|\mathbb{A}|^{2}W_{j}^{*}

for some isometries
U_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{d^{\prime},m_{k}}
and some isometries
W_{j}\in\mathbb{M}_{rd^{\prime},d^{\prime}}
. Since

\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}|A_{k}|^{2}=C\left\{\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}U_{k}|A_{k}|^{2}U_{k}\right\}C^{*}

for some contraction
C\in\mathbb{M}_{m,rd^{\prime}}
, we infer

\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}|A_{k}|^{2}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}CW_{j}|\mathbb{A}|^{2}W_{j}^{*}C^{*}.

If
|\mathbb{A}|
is invertible, then, taking trace, the above equality ensures that contractions
CW_{j}
satisfy
W_{j}^{*}C^{*}CW_{j}={\mathbf{1}}_{d^{\prime}}
for all
j
. Hence the result is proved with
V_{j}=CW_{j}
. The general case follows by a limit argument.
∎

We are in a position to estimate the Schatten norms of the blocks with the full matrix.
The following corollary was first obtained by Bhatia and Kittaneh
[
4
]
in case of a matrix partitioned into
n\times n
blocks.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
be partitioned into
r
row or column compatible blocks
A_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{k},m_{k}}
.
Then, for all Schatten
q
-norms,
q\geq 2
,

r^{{\frac{2}{q}}-1}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\|A_{k}\|_{q}^{2}\leq\|\mathbb{A}\|_{q}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r}\|A_{k}\|_{q}^{2}

These two inequalities are reversed for
2>q>0
.

Proof.

For
p:=q/2\geq 1
, the second inequality contains (
2.10
) and immediately follows from Theorem
2.1
and the triangle inequality for the Schatten
p
-norms. Corollary
2.4
gives

\||\mathbb{A}|^{2}\|_{p}\geq\left\||A_{1}|^{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus|A_{r}|^{2}\right\|_{p}

and since the concavity of
t\mapsto t^{1/p}
entails

\left\||A_{1}|^{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus|A_{r}|^{2}\right\|_{p}=\left(\||A_{1}|^{2}\|_{p}^{p}+\cdots\||A_{r}|^{2}\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{1/p}\geq r^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\left(\||A_{1}|^{2}\|_{p}+\cdots+\||A_{r}|^{2}\|_{p}\right)

we get the first inequality. These inequalities are reversed for
0<p<1
.
∎

Corollary
2.4
is relevant to Majorisation Theory. We take this opportunity to point out an interesting
fact about majorisation in the next proposition. Though this result might be well-known of some experts, it does not seem to be in the literature. Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{n}^{+}
, the positive semi-definite cone of
\mathbb{M}_{n}
. The majorisation
A\prec B
means that

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{j}(A)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{j}(B)

for all
k=1,2,\ldots n
, with equality for
k=n
.
The majorisation
A\prec B
is equivalent to

A=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}U_{i}BU_{i}^{*}

for some unitary matrices
U_{i}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
and weights
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}=1
. This can be easily derived from Caratheodory’s theorem
[
8
]
. A more accurate statement holds.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{n}^{+}
,
A\prec B
.Then, for some unitary matrices
U_{i}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
,

A=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}BU_{i}^{*}.

Proof.

By the Schur-Horn Theorem, we may assume that
A
is the diagonal part of
B
. Then we use the simple idea of Equation (2) in the nice paper of Bhatia
[
3
]
.
∎

Note that Corollary
2.4
may be restated as

\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}|A_{k}|^{2}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}U_{j}(|\mathbb{A}|^{2}\oplus O)U_{j}^{*}.

for some unitary matrices
U_{j}
and some fixed zero matrix
O
. Hence we have an average of
r
matrices in the unitary orbit of
|\mathbb{A}|^{2}\oplus O
, this number
r
being (much) smaller than the one given by Proposition
2.6
,
d=m_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}
.

3
Compression onto a hyperplane

By a hyperplane of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
we mean a vector subspace of dimension
d-1
. The next corollary is a singular value version of Cauchy’s Interlacing Theorem
[
2
, p. 59]
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
and let
{\mathcal{S}}
be a hyperplane of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
orthogonal to the unit vector
h
.
Set
\beta=\min\{\|Ah\|^{2},\|A^{*}h\|^{2}\}-|\langle h,Ah\rangle|^{2}
. Then, for all
j=1,\ldots,d-1
,

\mu_{j}^{2}(A)\geq\mu_{j}^{2}(A_{\mathcal{S}})\geq\mu_{j+1}^{2}(A)-\beta.

This double inequality is stronger than
\mu_{j}(A)\geq\mu_{j}(A_{\mathcal{S}})\geq\mu_{j+1}(A)-\sqrt{\beta}
. If
A
is a normal matrix, then
\|Ah\|=\|A^{*}h\|
and we have Corollary
1.1
. If
A=V
is a unitary matrix,
\mu_{j}(V)=1
for all
j
and
\|Vh\|=\|V^{*}h\|=1
for all unit vectors, so we deduce from Corollary
3.1
that

\mu_{j}(V_{\mathcal{S}})\geq|\langle h,Vh\rangle|
. In fact one can easily check that
\mu_{j}(V_{\mathcal{S}})=1
for
j\leq d-2
and
\mu_{d-1}(V_{\mathcal{S}})=|\langle h,Vh\rangle|
. Hence Corollary
3.1
is sharp.

Proof.

(Corollary
3.1
) The inequality
\mu_{j}(A)\geq\mu_{j}(A_{\mathcal{S}})
is trivial. To deal with the other inequality
we may assume that
h
is the last vector of the canonical basis and that
A_{\mathcal{S}}
is the submatrix of
A
obtained by deleting the last column and the last line. We partition
A
as

A=A_{\mathcal{S}}\cup B\cup C

where
B
contains the
d-1
entries below
A_{\mathcal{S}}
and
C
is the last colum of
A
. We then apply to this partitioning
Corollary
2.2
with
j_{1}=j-1
,
j_{2}=0
, and
j_{3}=1
to get

\mu_{j+1}^{2}(A)\leq\mu_{j}^{2}(A_{\mathcal{S}})+\mu_{1}^{2}(B)+\mu_{2}^{2}(C).

Since
\mu_{2}(C)=0
, we have

\ \mu_{j+1}^{2}(A)-\mu_{1}^{2}(B)\leq\mu_{j}^{2}(A_{\mathcal{S}})

Observe that
\mu_{1}^{2}(B)=\|A^{*}h\|^{2}-|\langle hAh\rangle|^{2}
, hence

\mu_{j}^{2}(A_{\mathcal{S}})\geq\mu_{j+1}^{2}(A)-\|A^{*}h\|^{2}+|\langle h,Ah\rangle|^{2}.

(3.1)

We may also partition
A
as

A=A_{\mathcal{S}}\cup R\cup L

where
R
contains the
d-1
entries on the right of
A_{\mathcal{S}}
and
L
stands for the last line of
A
.
Arguing as above with
R
and
L
in place of
B
and
C
yields

\mu_{j+1}^{2}(A)-\mu_{j}^{2}(A_{\mathcal{S}})\leq\mu_{1}^{2}(R)=\|Ah\|^{2}-|\langle h,Ah\rangle|^{2}

(3.2)

Combining (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
be partitioned into
r
row or column compatible blocks
A_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{k},m_{k}}
. Then, for each block
A_{k}
and all
j\geq 1
,

\mu^{2}_{j}(\mathbb{A})-\mu^{2}_{j}(A_{k})\leq\sum_{l\neq k}\mu^{2}_{1}(A_{l}).

Proof.

Apply Corollary
2.2
with
j_{k}=j-1
and
j_{l}=0
for all
l\neq k
. ∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
and let
{\mathcal{S}}
be a hyperplane of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
orthogonal to the unit vector
h
. Then for all
j=1,\ldots,d-1
,

\mu^{2}_{j}(A)-\mu^{2}_{j}(A_{\mathcal{S}})\leq\|Ah\|^{2}+\|A^{*}h\|^{2}-|\langle h,Ah\rangle|^{2}.

Proof.

We may suppose that
h
is the last vector of the canonical basis and we partition
A
into three blocks :
A_{\mathcal{S}}
, the last column of
A
, and the
d-1
entries below
A_{\mathcal{S}}
. We then apply the previous corollary.
∎

4
Four and five blocks

Partitionings into four blocks are not necessarily row or column compatible. However, for such partitionings, Theorem
2.1
still holds.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
be partitioned into four blocks
A,B,C,D
. Then, there exist some isometries
U,V,W,X
of suitable sizes such that

|\mathbb{A}|^{2}=U|A|^{2}U^{*}+V|B|^{2}V^{*}+W|C|^{2}W^{*}+X|D|^{2}X^{*}.

Proof.

We assume that
A
is the block in the upper left corner and we distinguish three cases.

(1)

A
has the same number
d
of lines as
\mathbb{A}
.
In such a case, letting
A^{\prime}=B\cup C\cup D
, the partitioning
\mathbb{A}=A\cup A^{\prime}
is column compatible, and we have two isometry matrices
U,U^{\prime}
such that

|\mathbb{A}|^{2}=U|A|^{2}U^{*}+U^{\prime}|A^{\prime}|^{2}U^{\prime*}.

(4.1)

Since
A^{\prime}
is partitioned into three blocks, necessarily a row or column partitioning, we can apply the theorem to obtain the decomposition

|A^{\prime}|^{2}=V^{\prime}|B|^{2}V^{\prime*}+W^{\prime}|B|^{2}W^{\prime*}+X^{\prime}|B|^{2}X^{\prime*}

(4.2)

for some isometry matrices
V^{\prime},W^{\prime},X^{\prime}
of suitable sizes. Combining (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) we get the conclusion of the corollary with the isometry matrices
V=U^{\prime}V^{\prime}
,
W=U^{\prime}W^{\prime}
, and
X=U^{\prime}X^{\prime}
.

(2)

A
has the same number
d^{\prime}
of columns as
\mathbb{A}
.
Letting again
A^{\prime}=B\cup C\cup D
, the partitioning
\mathbb{A}=A\cup A^{\prime}
is row compatible, and we may argue as in case (1).

(3)

A
has
l<d
lines and
c<d^{\prime}
columns. There exist then a block, say
B
, on the top position, and just on the right of
A
, and another block, say
C
just below
A
and on the left side. We consider three subcases (a), (b), (c).

(a)
B
has less than
l
lines. Then, the last block
D
is necessarily below
B
with the same number of columns as
B
, and so
C
has the same number of columns as
A
, hence
\mathbb{A}=A\cup C\cup B\cup D
is a column compatible partitioning and we can apply the theorem.

(b)
B
has exactly
l
lines, like
A
. We denote by
\gamma
the number of columns of
B
and we consider three situations.

(i)
C
has more than
c+\gamma
columns. Then necessarily
C
has
d^{\prime}
columns and
D
is the upper right block with
l
lines, hence
\mathbb{A}=A\cup B\cup D\cup C
is a line compatible partitioning and we may apply the theorem.

(ii)
C
has exactly
c+\gamma
columns. Then, letting
\mathbb{A}^{\prime\prime}=A\cup B\cup C
with have a partitioning into three blocks, and
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}^{\prime\prime}\cup D
. Thus applying the theorem twice as in case (1) yields the conclusion.

(iii)
C
has fewer than
c+\gamma
columns. Then
D
is the lower right block, with the same number of lines as
B
, and
\mathbb{A}
is partitioned into line compatible blocks. Thus the theorem can be applied.

(c)
B
has more lines than
A
. Let
\lambda
be the number of line of
B
. Hence
\lambda>l
. There exist two situations

(iv)
\lambda<d
. Then
D
is the lower right block, with the same number of columns as
B
, and
\mathbb{A}
is partitioned into line compatible blocks. Thus we may apply the theorem.

(v)
\lambda=d
. Then
A^{\prime\prime\prime}=A\cup C\cup D
is a partitioning into three blocks and
\mathbb{A}=A^{\prime\prime\prime}\cup B
, thus applying twice the theorem completes the proof.
∎

We do not know wether Corollary
4.1
can be extended or not to any partitioning in five blocks. For instance we are not able to prove or disprove a version of Corollary
4.1
for the matrix

\mathbb{A}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&b_{1}&b_{2}\\
a_{4}&a_{5}&a_{6}&b_{3}&b_{4}\\
d_{1}&d_{2}&x&b_{5}&b_{6}\\
d_{3}&d_{4}&c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}\\
d_{5}&d_{6}&c_{4}&c_{5}&c_{6}\end{pmatrix}

partitioned into five obvious blocks
A,B,C,D,X
. Hence, that Theorem
2.1
holds or not for any partitioning into five blocks is an open problem. More generally, we may consider the following two questions.

Question 4.2
.

For which partitionings does Theorem
2.1
hold ? For which partitionings does Corollary
2.2
hold ?

Matrices partitioned into four blocks (usually of same size) are comon examples of partitionings. A nontrivial inequality follows from the previous corollary.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
be partitioned into four blocks
A,B,C,D
, and let
p>2
. Then, there exist some isometries
U,V,W,X
of suitable sizes such that

2^{2-p}|\mathbb{A}|^{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}p}\leq U|A|^{p}U^{*}+V|B|^{p}V^{*}+W|C|^{p}W^{*}+X|D|^{p}X^{*}.

The inequality reverses for
2>p>0
.

Letting
p=1
we have Corollary
1.2
with the constant 2 which is sharp, even for a positive block matrix, as shown by the simple example

\mathbb{A}=\begin{bmatrix}A&A\\
A&A\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

For any monotone convex function
f(t)
on the nonnegative axis, we have thanks to
[
5
, Corollary 2.4]
and Corollary
4.1
,

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{|\mathbb{A}|^{2}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(\frac{U|A|^{2}U^{*}+V|B|^{2}V^{*}+W|C|^{2}W^{*}+X|D|^{2}X^{*}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda\frac{f(U|A|^{2}U^{*})+V(f|B|^{2}V^{*})+f(W|C|^{2}W^{*})+f(X|D|^{2}X^{*})}{4}\Lambda^{*}

for some unitary matrix
\Lambda\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{\prime}
. Picking
f(t)=t^{p/2}
with
p>2
yields the result. The reverse inequalities hold for monotone concave functions
f(t)
and
0<p<2
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The version of Corollary
4.3
for three blocks
A,B,C
, and
p=1
reads as the inequality of the abstract,

\sqrt{3}|\mathbb{A}|\geq U|A|U^{*}+V|B|V^{*}+W|C|W^{*}.

The constant
\sqrt{3}
is the best one: we cannot take a smaller constant for

\mathbb{A}=\begin{bmatrix}x&y&z\\
x&y&z\\
x&y&z\end{bmatrix}

partitioned into its three lines. For two blocks, a similar sharp inequality holds with the constant
\sqrt{2}
.

5
Concave or convex functions

For sake of simplicity we state our results for a square matrix
\mathbb{A}
partitioned into blocks. By adding some zero rows or zero columns to a rectangular matrix, we could obtain statements for rectangular matrices (Remark
5.9
).

Suppose that
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
is partitioned into blocks
A_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{k},m_{k}}
,
k=1,\ldots,r
. From Thompson’s triangle inequality
(
[
7
]
or
[
2
, p. 74]
we have

|\mathbb{A}|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r}U_{k}|A_{k}|U_{k}^{*}

(5.1)

for some isometry matrices
U_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,m_{k}}
. The equality of Theorem
2.1
and (
5.1
) suggest several other inequalities, in particular, if
\mathbb{A}
is partioned in row or column compatible bloks,

|\mathbb{A}|^{3}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{r}V_{k}|A_{k}|^{3}V_{k}^{*}

(5.2)

for some isometries
V_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,m_{k}}
. This is indeed true as shown in the following theorem. We do not know if (
5.2
) can be extended to any partitioning. Corollary
4.1
and the proof of Theorem
5.1
show that (
5.2
) holds for four blocks. The case of five blocks is open.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be partitioned into
r
row or column compatible blocks
A_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{k},m_{k}}
, and let
\psi(t)
be a monotone function on
[0,\infty)
such that
\psi(\sqrt{t})
is convex and
\psi(0)=0
. Then there exist some isometries
V_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,m_{k}}
such that

\psi(|\mathbb{A}|)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{r}V_{k}\psi(|A_{k}|)V_{k}^{*}.

Theorem
5.1
considerably improves (
2.9
). A special case with
\psi(t)=t^{3}
is given in the abstract.

Proof.

Let
g(t)
be a monotone convex function on
[0,\infty)
such that
g(0)\leq 0
, and let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
be positive (semidefinite). By
[
1
]
or
[
5
, Corollary 3.2]
we have

g(A+B)\geq Ug(A)U^{*}+Vg(B)V^{*}

for some unitary matrices
U,V\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
. Using this inequality and Theorem
2.1
we infer

g(|\mathbb{A}|^{2})\geq\sum_{k=1}^{r}W_{k}g(U_{k}|A_{k}|^{2}U_{k}^{*})W_{k}^{*}

for some unitary matrices
W_{k}
and some isometry matrices
U_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,m_{k}}
. If
g(0)=0
, we have

g(U_{k}|A_{k}|^{2})U_{k}=U_{k}g(|A|^{2})U_{k}^{*}
. Hence

g(|\mathbb{A}|^{2})\geq\sum_{k=1}^{r}V_{k}g(|A_{k}|^{2})V_{k}^{*}

with the isometry matrices
V_{k}=W_{k}U_{k}
. Applying this to
g(t)=\psi(\sqrt{t})
completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be partitioned into
r
row or column compatible blocks
A_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n_{k},m_{k}}
, and let
\varphi(t)
be a nonnegative function on
[0,\infty)
such that
\varphi(\sqrt{t})
is concave. Then there exist some isometries
U_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,n_{k}}
such that

\varphi(|\mathbb{A}|)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r}U_{k}\varphi(|A_{k}|)U_{k}^{*}.

Proof.

Since
\varphi(\sqrt{t})
is nonnegative and concave, it is necessarily a monotone function (nondecreasing), hence continuous on
(0,\infty)
. Since we are dealing with matrices we may further suppose that
\varphi(t)
is also continuous at
t=0
.

(1) Assume that
\varphi(0)=0
. Theorem
5.1
applied to
\psi(t)=-\varphi(t)
proves the corollary.

(2) Assume that
\varphi(0)>0
. Since the continuous functional calculus is continuous on the positive semidefinite cone of any
\mathbb{M}_{m}
, by a limit argument, we may assume that
|\mathbb{A}|
is invertible. So, suppose that the spectrum of
|\mathbb{A}|^{2}
lies in an interval
[r^{2},s^{2}]
with
r>0
.
Define a convex function
\phi(\sqrt{t})
by
\phi(\sqrt{t})=\varphi(\sqrt{t})
for
t\geq r^{2}
,
\phi(0)=0
, and the graph of
\phi(\sqrt{t})
on
[0,r^{2}]
is a line segment. Hence
\phi(t)\leq\varphi(t)
and
\phi(|\mathbb{A}|)=\varphi(|\mathbb{A}|)
. Applying case (1) to
\phi
yields

\varphi(|\mathbb{A}|)=\phi(|\mathbb{A}|)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r}U_{k}\phi(|A_{k}|)U_{k}^{*}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r}U_{k}\varphi(|A_{k}|)U_{k}^{*}

for some isometry matrices
U_{k}
.
∎

The next three corollaries follow from Corollary
5.2
.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{mn}
be partitioned into a family of
m\times m
blocks
A_{i,j}\in\mathbb{M}_{n,n}
, and let
0<q\leq 2
. Then there exist some isometries
U_{i,j}\in\mathbb{M}_{mn,n}
such that

|\mathbb{A}|^{q}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}U_{i,j}|A_{i,j}|^{q}U_{i,j}^{*}.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{mn}
be partitioned into a family of
m\times m
blocks
A_{i,j}\in\mathbb{M}_{n,n}
, let
s\geq 1
and
0<q\leq 2
. Then,

\left\{{\mathrm{Tr}\,}|\mathbb{A}|^{qs}\right\}^{1/s}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\left\{{\mathrm{Tr}\,}|A_{i,j}|^{qs}\right\}^{1/s}.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
, let
c_{k}
be the norm of the
k
-th column of
A
and let
0<q\leq 2
. Then there exist some rank one projection
E_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
such that

|\mathbb{A}|^{q}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{q}E_{k}

The last corollaries follow from Theorem
5.1
.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{mn}
be partitioned into a family of
m\times m
blocks
A_{i,j}\in\mathbb{M}_{n,n}
, and let
p\geq 2
. Then there exist some isometries
U_{i,j}\in\mathbb{M}_{mn,n}
such that

|\mathbb{A}|^{p}\geq\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}U_{i,j}|A_{i,j}|^{p}U_{i,j}^{*}.

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{mn}
be partitioned into a family of
m\times m
blocks
A_{i,j}\in\mathbb{M}_{n,n}
, let
0\leq s\leq 1
and
p\geq 2
. Then,

\left\{{\mathrm{Tr}\,}|\mathbb{A}|^{ps}\right\}^{1/s}\geq\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\left\{{\mathrm{Tr}\,}|A_{i,j}|^{ps}\right\}^{1/s}.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
, let
r_{k}
be the norm of the
k
-th row of
A
and let

. Then there exist some rank one projections
E_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}
such that

|\mathbb{A}|^{p}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{p}E_{k}.

Remark 5.9
.

The proof of Theorem
5.1
is the same for a
d\times d^{\prime}
matrix
\mathbb{A}
. So Corollary
5.2
also holds for
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{M}_{d,d^{\prime}}
if
\varphi(0)=0
. In case of
d\geq d^{\prime}
, we may again use a limit argument and assume that
|\mathbb{A}|
is invertible. In case of
d^{\prime}>d
we may argue as follows. Add some zero lines to
\mathbb{A}
in order to obtain
a square matrix
\mathbb{A}_{0}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{\prime}
. Let
B_{1}\ldots,B_{p}
be the blocks at the bottom of
\mathbb{A}
, and
R_{1},\ldots,R_{q}
be the remaining blocks of
\mathbb{A}
. Add some zeros to the blocks
B_{i}
in order to obtain blocks
B_{i}^{0}
of
\mathbb{A}_{0}
in such a way that

\mathbb{A}_{0}=\left(\bigcup_{i}B_{i}^{0}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{j}R_{j}\right)

is a row or colum compatible partitioning of
\mathbb{A}_{0}
. Since it is a square matrix, we may apply Corolloray
5.2
and since

|\mathbb{A}_{0}|=|\mathbb{A}|
and
|B_{i}^{0}|=|B^{i}|
, we see that Corollary
5.2
holds for
d\times d^{\prime}
matrices.
