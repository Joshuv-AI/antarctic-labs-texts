Butson Hadamard matrices, bent sequences, and spherical codes
Minjia Shi, Danni Lu, Andrés Armario, Ronan Egan, Ferruh Ozbudak, Patrick Solé

Abstract
We explore a notion of bent sequence attached to the data consisting of an Hadamard matrix of order n n defined over the complex q t ​ h q^{th} roots of unity, an eigenvalue of that matrix, and a Galois automorphism from the cyclotomic field of order q . q. In particular we construct self-dual bent sequences for various q ≤ 60 q\leq 60 and lengths n ≤ 21 . n\leq 21. Computational construction methods comprise the resolution of polynomial systems by Groebner bases and eigenspace computations. Infinite families can be constructed from regular Hadamard matrices, Bush-type Hadamard matrices, and generalized Boolean bent functions. As an application, we estimate the covering radius of the code attached to that matrix over ℤ q . \mathbb{Z}_{q}. We derive a lower bound on that quantity for the Chinese Euclidean metric when bent sequences exist. We give the Euclidean distance spectrum, and bound above the covering radius of an attached spherical code, depending on its strength as a spherical design.



Keywords:
Hadamard matrices, Butson matrices, covering radius, bent sequences, spherical codes

1
Introduction

Bent sequences are important combinatorial objects that are relevant to difference sets, Boolean functions, strongly regular graphs, and symmetric cryptography
[
21
]
. They are classically defined in relation to the Sylvester matrix, the matrix of the Walsh-Hadamard transform.
Recently a new notion of bent sequences, motivated by a cryptographic problem (PUFs=Physically Unclonable Functions), appeared, that is defined for any Hadamard matrix
[
28
]
. Constructions and properties of these sequences in the self-dual case were investigated in
[
27
]
.
The advantage of self-dual bent sequences over unrestricted bent sequences
is that they allow linear algebra to bear on the problem. Note that every bent sequence is self-dual for some matrix
[
26
]
.
The extension of this notion to
complex Hadamard matrix in the sense of Turyn, that is to say with entries in
\Omega_{4}=\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
, was done in
[
26
]
. A self-dual bent sequence
X
attached to a matrix
H
is then an eigenvector of
H
for an eigenvalue
\lambda
of
H
(some Gaussian integer) that have its entries in
\Omega_{4}.
Thus
HX=\lambda X.
A new twist in the definition
of self-dual bent sequences was given in
[
29
]
were it is suggested to replace the above equation by
HX=\lambda\overline{X},
where the bar denotes complex conjugation. The motivation for that change was to obtain more bent sequences, in particular in relation with generalized bent functions.

In the present paper, we extend the notion of self-dual bent sequences in two directions. First, we consider Butson Hadamard matrices, that is to say Hadamard matrices with values in the complex roots of unity of a given order. Next, we replace the notion of conjugate by that of multiplier, that is a bijective map of the form
z\mapsto z^{k}
with
(k,q)=1
which preserves globally the complex
q^{th}
roots of unity. When
k=1
this new definition reduces to that of
[
26
,
27
]
. When
k=q-1
, we recover the definition of
[
29
]
. This change of definition affects the notion of a strong group, a matrix group that preserves the set of self-dual bent sequences.
We extend the computational techniques (linear algebra and Groebner bases) from
[
26
,
27
]
to this new setting. This allows us to obtain many examples of self-dual bent sequences from the matrices of the database
[
5
]
. General constructions of Butson Hadamard matrices with designed self-dual bent sequences are then derived from, respectively, Kronecker products of Butson matrices, regular Butson matrices, Bush type Hadamard matrices, and generalized Walsh Hadamard
matrices. In the last case we use group invariant Butson matrices in the sense of
[
8
,
22
]
. Strong necessary existence conditions for Butson matrices admitting self-dual bent sequences with trivial multiplier are given. In a second part of the article, we turn our attention towards Butson-Hadamard codes, and their metric properties with respect to covering and packing either a finite set of strings or the Euclidean sphere. While the packing properties of the code with respect to the homogeneous and other metrics have been investigated by various
authors
[
1
,
13
]
, we focus on the Chinese Euclidean metric and the covering properties in connection with bent sequences. The Chinese Euclidean metric, which is first mentioned in
[
6
]
, should not be confused with that in
[
18
]
.
We also associate with every such code of length
n
a spherical code in dimension
2n.
This code is shown to be optimal for the minimum distance in the case of
q=4,
as meeting the Levenshtein bound. Pending on some properties of the Hadamard matrix, we can obtain spherical designs of strength one or two. Spherical designs are finite sets of points on the unit Euclidean sphere which occur naturally in relation to the “cubature formulae” of numerical analysis,
and can be thought of as analogues of combinatorial designs
[
7
]
. In
[
25
]
, the existence of spherical designs is established, providing a solid foundation for their theoretical exploration. Moreover, the utilization of spherical designs in motivating the definition of conformal
t
-designs is demonstrated in
[
14
]
. Building spherical designs from Butson Hadamard matrices seems to be a new approach. It enters here as a way to use upper bounds on the covering radius of spherical designs
[
11
,
30
]
to control the covering radius of the said spherical code.

The material is arranged as follows. Section 2 collects the definitions needed for the rest of the paper. Section 3 defines and constructs the strong automorphism group of a Butson matrix with a given multiplier. Section 4 studies computational methods and results. Section 5 gives four general constructions of Butson Hadamard matrices with designed self-dual bent sequences. Section 6 derives a necessary condition for the existence of Butson matrices admitting a self-dual bent sequence. Section 7 derives the Euclidean distance spectrum of Butson Hadamard codes. Section 8 contains the lower and upper bounds on the covering radius of Butson Hadamard codes and attending spherical codes. Section 9 concludes the article.

Preliminaries

Definition 2.1
.

A complex
Hadamard
matrix of order
n
is an
n\times n
matrix
H
with entries in the complex unit satisfying the equation
HH^{*}=nI,
where the * denotes the transpose conjugate. If all of its elements are in
\Omega_{q}=\left\{z\in C\mid z^{q}=1\right\},
for some integer
q,
then
H
is said to be
Butson
type, and we write
H\in BH(n,q).
A matrix
H\in BH(n,q)
is in
dephased
form when both first row and first columns only contain ones.

Definition 2.2
.

Two Hadamard matrices
H
and
K
of
BH(n,q)
are called
equivalent
if there are two permutation matrices
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
and two diagonal matrices
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
with entries in
\Omega_{q}
such that

H=P_{1}D_{1}KP_{2}D_{2}.

Every matrix of
BH(n,q)
is equivalent to its dephased form.

Definition 2.3
.

Given
H\in BH(n,q)
the sequence
X\in\Omega_{q}^{n}
is a
bent sequence
if there is
Y\in\Omega_{q}^{n}
such that
HX=\lambda Y
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{q}].

Definition 2.4
.

For any
k
coprime with
q
we define the
multiplier

\mu_{k}:\Omega_{q}\longrightarrow\Omega_{q}
by the rule
\mu_{k}(z)=z^{k}.

Now a self-dual bent sequence attached to
H\in BH(n,q)
is defined as
X\in\Omega_{q}^{n}
such that

HX=\lambda\mu_{k}(X),

where
\lambda
is an element of
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{q}].
(When
k=1
it is also an eigenvalue of
H
).
When
k=1,
this is the definition of self-dual bent sequence in
[
26
]
.
When
k=q-1,
this is the definition of self-dual bent sequence in
[
29
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

(1)

Any multiplier
\mu_{k}
can be extended into a Galois automorphism of the cyclotomic field of index
q.
Writing an element of that field as

a=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{q-1}a_{i}\zeta^{i}
for rationals
a_{i}
’s, we define
\mu_{k}(a)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{q-1}a_{i}\zeta^{ki}.
In fact all Galois automorphisms are of that form
[
16
]
.
They thus satisfy the following three properties.

(i)

If
x\in\mathbb{Q},
then
\mu_{k}(x)=x.

(ii)

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{q}),\,\mu_{k}(x+y)=\mu_{k}(x)+\mu_{k}(y).

(iii)

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{q}),\,\mu_{k}(xy)=\mu_{k}(x)\mu_{k}(y).

(2)

If
X
is a bent sequence for
H\in BH(n,q)
with
HX=\lambda Y,
then
Y
is a self-dual bent sequence with trivial multiplier for the matrix
HD,
where
D
is a diagonal matrix such that
X=DY.
Note that the diagonal of
D
lies in
\Omega_{q},
which implies that
HD\in BH(n,q).

Definition 2.5
.

The
Chinese Euclidean weight

w_{CE}(x)
of a vector
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
is

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[2-2\cos(\frac{2\pi x_{i}}{q})\right],

and the
Chinese Euclidean distance
between the codewords
u
and
v\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
is defined as

d_{CE}(u,v)=w_{CE}(u-v).

Definition 2.6
.

A Butson matrix
H\in BH(n,q)
is conveniently represented in logarithmic form, that is, the matrix
H=\left[\zeta_{q}^{\varphi_{i,j}}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}
is represented by the matrix
L(H)=\left[\varphi_{i,j}\bmod q\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}
with the convention that
L_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
for all
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}.
Given
H\in BH(n,q),
we denote by
F_{H}
the
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
-code of length
n
consisting of the rows of
L(H),
and by
C_{H}=\cup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}(F_{H}+\alpha\textbf{1})
where
1
denotes the all-one vector. The code
C_{H}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
is called a
Butson Hadamard
code. Any subset of
\Omega_{q}^{n}
for some integer
n
is called a
polyphase
code, following
[
10
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
C_{H}
be a Butson Hadamard code of length
n
over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
coming from
H\in BH(n,q)
. The
covering radius
of
C_{H}
with Chinese Euclidean distance
d_{CE}
is defined as:

r_{CE}(C_{H})=\max\{d_{CE}(x,C_{H})]\mid x\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}\}=\max\limits_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}}\min\limits_{y\in C_{H}}d_{CE}(x,y).

3
Automorphism group

Denote the set of
n\times n
monomial matrices with nonzero entries in
\Omega_{q}
by
M(n,q)
. The set
M(n,q)
is a group generated by the set
P_{n}
of all permutation matrices of order
n
, and
\Delta(n,q)
, the group of all diagonal matrices of order
n
with entries in
\Omega_{q}
. The group of all pairs of matrices from
M(n,q)
acts on the set
\mathrm{BH}(n,q)
. The action is given by

(P,Q)H=PHQ^{\ast}

for all
H\in\mathrm{BH}(n,q)
. The orbit of
H
under this action is the equivalence class of
H
. The stabilizer of
H
under this action, i.e., the set of all
(P,Q)\in M(n,q)^{2}
such that

PHQ^{\ast}=H

is called the
automorphism group
of
H
, and is denoted
Aut(H)
. The subgroup of
Aut(H)
comprised of pairs of matrices in
P_{n}
is called the
permutation automorphism group
of
H
, denoted
PAut(H)
.

We define the
strong automorphism group
with trivial multiplier of
H
to be the subgroup
SAut(H,1)\leq Aut(H)
comprised of pairs of the form
(P,P)
. It follows from the definition of an automorphism that
(P,P)\in SAut(H,1)
if and only if
PH=HP
. Hence
SAut(H,1)
is in bijection with the centralizer of
H
in
M(n,q)
.

Calculating the automorphism groups of
H\in\mathrm{BH}(n,q)
is an onerous task in general. The most efficient approaches all involve a similar idea - constructing an appropriate larger
(0,1)
-matrix and using graph theoretic tools such as
nauty

[
20
]
which are highly efficient. In the following section we condense the relevant details on computing the automorphism group of a pairwise combinatorial design given in
[
17
, Chapter 9]
down for just the purpose of handling a Butson Hadamard matrix.

Computational techniques

In this section we extend the computational construction methods of self-dual bent sequences of
[
26
,
27
]
to Butson matrices.

4.1

Groebner bases

The system
HX=\lambda\mu_{k}(X)
with
X\in\Omega_{q}^{n}
can be solved using Groebner bases, as it is equivalent to a polynomial system, which,
instead of being quadratic as in
[
26
]
is now of degree
q.
More concretely, we can consider the following steps.

(1)

Construct the ring
P
of polynomial functions in
n
variables
X_{i},i=1,\dots,n.

(2)

Construct the linear constraints
HX=\lambda\mu_{k}(X).

(3)

Construct the constraints
\forall i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\},X_{i}^{q}=1.

(4)

Compute a Groebner basis for the ideal
I
of
P
determined by constraints (ii) and (iii).

(5)

Compute the solutions as the zeros determined by
I
.

Complexity:
As is well-known, the complexity of computing Groebner bases can be doubly exponential in the number of variables
[
2
]
. However, for the system at hand, it might at most be simply exponential
[
12
]
.

4.2

Linear algebra

Consider the system
HX=\lambda\mu_{k}(X).
Denote by
t
the multiplicative order of
k
modulo
q,
implying
\mu_{k}^{t}=1.
We can then obtain, by repeated application of
\mu_{k}
the equations

\mu_{k}^{i}(H)\mu_{k}^{i}(X)=\mu_{k}^{i}(\lambda)\mu_{k}^{i+1}(X),i=1,2,\dots,t-1.

By successive substitution, we get

\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mu_{k}^{t-1-i}(H)X=\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mu_{k}^{t-1-i}(\lambda)X.

From this equation, we can give an algorithm for the construction of self-dual bent sequences.

(1)

Construct
H\in BH(n,q).

(2)

Compute
\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mu_{k}^{t-1-i}(H)
and
\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mu_{k}^{t-1-i}(\lambda).

(3)

Compute a basis of the eigenspace associated to the eigenvalue
\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mu_{k}^{t-1-i}(\lambda)
of
\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mu_{k}^{t-1-i}(H).

(4)

Let
B
denote a matrix with rows such a basis of size
l\leq n.
Pick
B_{l}
a
l
-by-
l
submatrix of
B
that is invertible, by the algorithm given below.

(5)

For all
Z\in\Omega_{q}^{l}
solve the system in
C
given by
Z=CB_{l}.

(6)

Compute the remaining
n-l
entries of
CB.

(7)

If these entries are in
\Omega_{q}
declare
CB
a self-dual bent sequence attached to
\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mu_{k}^{t-1-i}(H).

(8)

Sieve out the self-dual bent sequence that satisfies
HX=\lambda\mu_{k}(X).

To construct
B_{l}
we apply a greedy algorithm. We construct the list
J
of the indices of the columns of
B_{l}
as follows.

(i)

Initialize
J
at
J=[1].

(ii)

Given a column of index
{l}
we compute the ranks
r
and
r^{\prime}
of the submatrices of
B
with
l
rows and columns defined by the respective lists
J
and
J^{\prime}=Append(J,{l}).

(iii)

If
r<r^{\prime},
then update
J:=J^{\prime}.

(iv)

Repeat until
\left|J\right|=rank(B).

Remark 4.1
.

If the first column of
B
is zero, step (i) does not make sense, but then there is no self-dual bent sequence in that situation, as all eigenvectors have first coordinate zero.

Complexity:
If we estimate at
O(n^{3})
the complexity of computation of an eigenspace basis, then the total complexity is
O(n^{3}+nkq^{k})
where
k
is the dimension of the relevant eigenspace.

General constructions

5.1
Kronecker product

If we keep the field of definition and the multiplier then we can take the Kronecker product of Butson matrices with self-dual bent sequences and
obtain another Butson matrix with a self-dual bent sequence, as the next result shows.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
H
(resp.
K
) be a Butson matrix in
BH(n,q)
(resp.
BH(m,q)
) affording a self dual bent sequence
X
(resp.
Y
) for the multiplier
\mu_{k}.

Then
X\otimes Y
is a self-dual bent sequence with multiplier
\mu_{k},
for
H\otimes K\in BH(mn,q).

Proof.

From
HX=\lambda\mu_{k}(X)
and
KY=\nu\mu_{k}(Y)
we obtain, taking Kronecker products

H\otimes K(X\otimes Y)=\lambda\nu\mu_{k}(X)\otimes\mu_{k}(Y).

The result follows by
\mu_{k}(X)\otimes\mu_{k}(Y)=\mu_{k}(X\otimes Y).

∎

Example:
Taking
q=3,
we write
j
for a complex cubic root of unity. We find an example of application of this proposition for
n=3,\,m=9,\ k=2
when

X=(1,j,j),
and
Y=(1,1,1,1,j^{2},j,1,j,j^{2}).

5.2

Regular matrices

A Butson Hadamard matrix
H
of order
n
is
regular
if it has constant row and column sum. A direct connection between self-dual bent sequences and regular Butson Hadamard matrices is as follows. Denote by
{\bf 1}_{n}
the all-one vector of length
n.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
H
is a regular Butson Hadamard matrix of order
n,
then for all
u\in\Omega_{q},
we have that
u{\bf 1}_{n}
is a self-dual bent sequence for
H
with trivial multiplier.

Proof.

Denote by
\sigma
the sum of elements of any row. By definition of regular Butson Hadamard matrices
H{\bf 1}_{n}=\sigma{\bf 1}_{n}.

The result follows by linearity.
∎

A strong existence condition for regular Butson matrices is Lemma 5.2 in
[
9
]
.

5.3

Bush type

A Bush-type Hadamard matrix, say
H,
is a Butson Hadamard matrix of order
n^{2}
over the
q^{th}
roots of unity which is subdivided into
n^{2}
blocks
H_{11},H_{12},\dots,H_{nn}
of order
n
such that
JH_{ij}=H_{ij}J=\delta_{ij}nJ,
where
J
denotes the all ones matrices.
Bush type Butson matrices were constructed recently in
[
15
]
. They have many self-dual bent sequences attached to
H
as the next result shows.

Proposition 5.3
.

If
H
is a Bush-type Hadamard matrix of order
n^{2}
over the
q^{th}
roots of unity, then there are at least
q^{n}
self-dual bent sequences with trivial multiplier attached to
H.

Proof.

From the definition, we see that the sequence
X
defined by
X^{T}=(u_{1}{\bf 1}_{n},\dots,u_{n}{\bf 1}_{n}),
where the
u_{k}
’s are arbitrary in
\Omega_{q},
is a self-dual bent sequence.
∎

5.4

Fourier transforms

Let
q\geq 2
be an integer, not necessarily a prime power. Let
r\geq 1
be an integer. We define the inner product
\cdot
on
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}
as

\displaystyle(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})\cdot(y_{1},\ldots,y_{r})=x_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+x_{r}y_{r},

(2)

where we use the ring structure of
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
on the right-hand side. Note that
x\cdot y=y\cdot x
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}
.

Let
n=q^{r}
and
H
be the
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{C}
defined as follows: For
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}
,
the entry
H(x,y)
of
H
corresponding to row
x
and column
y
is given by

\displaystyle H(x,y)=\zeta_{q}^{x\cdot y}.

(3)

It follows from the orthogonality of group characters that
H
is a Butson type Hadamard matrix of order
n
belonging to
BH(n,q).

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{q}
be a function. Let
X=X(f)
be the
r\times 1
matrix over
\mathbb{C}
such that the
x
-th entry of
X
is
\zeta_{q}^{f(x)}
. We assume that
f
is not the zero function so that
X
is not the trivial column matrix, i.e. not the full one column.
Recall that if there exists a multiplier
\mu_{k}:\Omega_{q}\rightarrow\Omega_{q}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{q}]
such that
HX=\lambda\mu_{k}(X)
, then
X
is called a self-dual bent sequence attached to
H
with multiplier
\mu_{k}
and the coefficient
\lambda
(see Definition
2.4
above).

Let us point out that
when
X
is a self-dual bent sequence attached to the conjugate matrix of (
3
), then
f:\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{q}
is a generalized bent function (in sense of
[
22
]
).

In the following theorem, when
r
is even and
f
is a generalized Maiorana-McFarland (MM) function, we characterize all
f
such that
X(f)
is self-dual.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
q\geq 2
be an integer, not necessarily a prime power. Let
m\geq 1
be an integer. Let
r=2m
. Let
\phi:\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
be a permutation. Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{q}
be the map defined as

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\cdot\phi(x_{2}),{}

where
\cdot
is the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
defined as in (
2
).

Let
1\leq k\leq q-1
and
\mu_{k}(z)=z^{k}
be as in Definition
2.4
. Put
n=q^{r}

and let
H
be the matrix of order
n
given in (
3
).
Let
X
be the
n\times 1
column such that
(x_{1},x_{2})
-th entry of
X
is
\zeta_{q}^{f(x_{1},x_{2})}
. Then
X
is a self-dual bent sequence attached to
H
with

\displaystyle HX=\lambda\mu_{k}(X){}

if and only if all of the followings hold:

(1)

x_{1}\cdot x_{2}+k\phi(x_{1})\cdot\phi(x_{2})=0
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
.

(2)

\lambda=q^{m}
.

Proof.

Let
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}
. The
(x_{1},x_{2})
-th entry of
HX
is

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\sum_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\sum_{y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{(x_{1},x_{2})\cdot(y_{1},y_{2})}\zeta_{q}^{f(y_{1},y_{2})}=\sum_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\sum_{y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{x_{1}\cdot y_{1}+x_{2}\cdot y_{2}+y_{1}\cdot\phi(y_{2})}\\
\\
\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{x_{2}\cdot y_{2}}\sum_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{y_{1}\cdot\left(x_{1}+\phi(y_{2})\right)}=q^{m}\zeta_{q}^{\phi^{-1}(-x_{1})\cdot x_{2}}.\end{array}{}

Hence
X
is a self-dual bent sequence if and only if
\lambda=q^{m}
and

\displaystyle\phi^{-1}(-x_{1})\cdot x_{2}=kx_{1}\cdot\phi(x_{2})\;\;\mbox{for all $x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}$}.{}

Consider the change of variable
x_{1}^{\prime}=\phi^{-1}(-x_{1})\iff\phi(x_{1}^{\prime})=-x_{1}
. Using this change of variable, the last condition is equivalent to the condition that
x_{1}\cdot x_{2}+k\phi(x_{1})\cdot\phi(x_{2})=0\;\;\mbox{for all $x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}$}.

∎

The following corollary is immediate.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
q\geq 2
be an integer, not necessarily a prime power. Let
m\geq 1
be an integer. Let
r=2m
. Let
H
be the matrix of order
n
given in (
3
). Consider the set

\displaystyle S=\left\{\frac{-1}{d^{2}}:1\leq d\leq q-1,\;\;\gcd(d,q)=1\right\}.{}

If
k\in S
, then there exists a self-dual bent sequence
X
attached to
H
with
HX=q^{m}\mu_{k}(X)
.

Proof.

Let
1\leq d\leq q-1
be an integer with
\gcd(d,q)=1
. Note that the map
\phi(x)=dx
is a permutation on
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
. Moreover we have

\displaystyle x_{1}\cdot x_{2}+k\phi(x_{1})\cdot\phi(x_{2})=(1+kd^{2})x_{1}\cdot x_{2}{}

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
. If
k\in S
, then
1+kd^{2}=0
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

Taking
d=1
in Corollary
5.1
, then for
k=q-1
and
\phi(x)=x
, we have that

g(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\cdot x_{2}
is a (self-dual) generalized bent function with
HX=q^{m}\;\overline{X}
.

Next, we consider some group invariant Butson type Hadamard matrices having self-dual bent sequences.

Let
q\geq 2
be an integer, not necessarily a prime power. Let
m\geq 1
be an integer and
r=2m
. Let
n=q^{r}
and
H
be the
n\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{C}
defined as follows: For
(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}
,
the entry
H((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2}))
of
H
corresponding to the row
(x_{1},x_{2})
and the column
(y_{1},y_{2})
is given by

\displaystyle H((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2}))=\zeta_{q}^{(x_{1}-y_{1})\cdot(x_{2}-y_{2})}{=\zeta_{q}^{g((x_{1},x_{2})-(y_{1},y_{2}))}\quad\mbox{for $g$ from Remark \ref{rm1}}}.

(8)

This implies that

\displaystyle H((x_{1}+a_{1},x_{2}+a_{2}),(y_{1}+a_{1},y_{2}+a_{2}))=H((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})){}

for all
(a_{1},a_{2})\in Z_{q}^{r}
and hence the matrix
H

defined in (
8
) is
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}
-group invariant in the sense of
[
8
]
. Note that the matrix defined in (
3
) is not
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}
-group invariant. Let us point out that
H(x,y)=\zeta_{q}^{g(x-y)}
is a group invariant Butson type Hadamard matrix when
g\colon\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{q}
is a generalized bent function (see
[
22
, Proposition 2.3]
).

Now we are ready to present the analogous result of Theorem
5.1
for the group invariant
matrix
H
defined in (
8
). We also show that
H
is a Butson type Hadamard matrix in the next theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
q\geq 2
be an integer, not necessarily a prime power. Let
m\geq 1
be an integer. Let
r=2m
. Let
\phi:\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
be a permutation. Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{q}
be the map, different from Theorem
5.1
, defined as

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\cdot\phi(x_{2})-x_{1}\cdot x_{2},{}

where
\cdot
is the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
defined as in (
2
).
Assume that
f
is not the zero map.

Let
1\leq k\leq q-1
and
\mu_{k}(z)=z^{k}
be as in Definition
2.4
. Put
n=q^{r}

and let
H
be the matrix of order
n
given in (
8
).
We have that
H
is a Butson type Hadamard matrix.

Moreover, let
X
be the
n\times 1
column such that
(x_{1},x_{2})
-th entry of
X
is
\zeta_{q}^{f(x_{1},x_{2})}
. Then
X
is a self-dual bent sequence attached to
H
with

\displaystyle HX=\lambda\mu_{k}(X){}

if and only if all of the followings hold:

(1)

kx_{1}\cdot\phi^{2}(x_{2})-(k+1)x_{1}\cdot\phi(x_{2})+x_{1}\cdot x_{2}=0
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
.

(2)

\lambda=q^{m}
.

Proof.

We first prove that
H
defined in (
8
) is a Butson type Hadamard matrix. Let
(x_{1},x_{2}),(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}
. The entry of
HH^{*}
corresponding to the row
(x_{1},x_{2})
and the column
(z_{1},z_{2})
is

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\sum_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\sum_{y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{(x_{1}-y_{1})\cdot(x_{2}-y_{2})}\zeta_{q}^{-(y_{1}-z_{1})\cdot(y_{2}-z_{2})}=\sum_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\sum_{y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{x_{1}\cdot x_{2}-z_{1}\cdot z_{2}+y_{1}\cdot(z_{2}-x_{2})+y_{2}\cdot(z_{1}-x_{1})}\\
\\
\displaystyle=\zeta_{q}^{x_{1}\cdot x_{2}-z_{1}\cdot z_{2}}\sum_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{y_{1}\cdot(z_{2}-x_{2})}\sum_{y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{y_{2}\cdot(z_{1}-x_{1})}.\end{array}{}

These arguments and the usual orthogonality relations imply that
HH^{*}=q^{r}I_{n}
. This completes the proof of the statement that
H
defined in (
8
) is a Butson type Hadamard matrix.

Let
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{r}
. The
(x_{1},x_{2})
-th entry of
HX
is

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\sum_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\sum_{y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{(x_{1}-y_{1})\cdot(x_{2}-y_{2})}\zeta_{q}^{f(y_{1},y_{2})}=\sum_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\sum_{y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{x_{1}\cdot x_{2}+y_{1}\cdot y_{2}-x_{1}\cdot y_{2}-x_{2}\cdot y_{1}-y_{1}\cdot y_{2}+y_{1}\cdot\phi(y_{2})}\\
\\
\displaystyle=\zeta_{q}^{x_{1}\cdot x_{2}}\sum_{y_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{-x_{1}\cdot y_{2}}\sum_{y_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}}\zeta_{q}^{y_{1}\cdot\left(\phi(y_{2})-x_{2}\right)}=q^{m}\zeta_{q}^{x_{1}\cdot x_{2}-x_{1}\cdot\phi^{-1}(x_{2})}.\end{array}{}

Hence
X
is a self-dual bent sequence if and only if
\lambda=q^{m}
and

\displaystyle x_{1}\cdot x_{2}-x_{1}\cdot\phi^{-1}(x_{2})=-kx_{1}\cdot x_{2}+kx_{1}\cdot\phi(x_{2})\;\;\mbox{for all $x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}$}.{}

By a suitable change of variables as in the proof of Theorem
5.1
, the last condition is equivalent to the condition that
kx_{1}\cdot\phi^{2}(x_{2})-(k+1)x_{1}\cdot\phi(x_{2})+x_{1}\cdot x_{2}=0\;\;\mbox{for all $x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}$}.

∎

The following corollary is immediate.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
q\geq 3
be an integer, not necessarily a prime power. Let
m\geq 1
be an integer. Let
r=2m
. Let
H
be the matrix of order
n
given in (
8
). Consider the set

\displaystyle S=\left\{\frac{1}{d}:1\leq d\leq q-1,\;\;\gcd(d,q)=1,\;\;d\neq 1\right\}{}

If
k\in S
, then there exists a self-dual bent sequence
X
attached to
H
with
HX=q^{m}\mu_{k}(X)
.

Proof.

Let
1\leq d\leq q-1
be an integer with
d\neq 1
. Note that the map
\phi(x)=dx
is a permutation on
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
. Also
f
is not the zero map as
d\neq 1
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle kx_{1}\cdot\phi^{2}(x_{2})-(k+1)x_{1}\cdot\phi(x_{2})+x_{1}\cdot x_{2}=\left(kd^{2}-(k+1)d+1\right)x_{1}\cdot x_{2}=(kd-1)(d-1)x_{1}\cdot x_{2}{}

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
. If
k\in S
, then
kd-1=0
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Corollaries
5.1
and
5.2
only present very simple examples satisfying conditions of Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
, respectively. For example, if
q=2
and
m\geq 1
is any integer, there are also such maps
\phi
proving self-dual bent sequences satisfying the conditions of Theorem
5.2
.

Existence conditions

The following result is inspired by
[
9
, Lemma 5.2]
. The hypothesis holds true when
H
admits a self-dual bent sequence with
k=1.

Theorem 6.1
.

If
H
in
BH(n,q)
admits an eigenvalue
\lambda
in
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{q}],
with an eigenvector in
\Omega_{q}^{n},
then there are
q
nonnegative integers
y_{r}\leq n
summing up to
n,
and such that

n=\left(\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}y_{r}c_{r}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}y_{r}s_{r}\right)^{2}

where
\zeta_{q}^{r}=c_{r}+is_{r}
and
i=\zeta_{4}.

Proof.

Projecting the eigenvalue equation
Hx=\lambda x
on the first coordinate yields after dividing out by
x_{1}
the relation

\sum_{j=1}^{n}H_{1j}\frac{x_{j}}{x_{1}}=\lambda.

Let
y_{r}
denote the number of occurrences of
\zeta_{q}^{r}
among the summands in the above sum. By definition

\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}y_{r}=n,\;\mbox{where $0\leq y_{r}\leq n$}.

A well-known consequence of the Hadamard equation is that

n=|\lambda|^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2},

where
a=\Re(\lambda),\,b=\Im(\lambda).
The result follows upon computing
a
and
b
from the above expression for
\lambda.

∎

Remark 6.1
.

In the literature of combinatorics, the sequence

is called a

of

into

.

,

\displaystyle=0+4+2=2+4+0=2+0+4=0+2+4=4+1+1=1+4+1=1+1+4

\displaystyle=3+3+0=3+0+3=0+3+3=3+2+1=3+1+2=1+3+2=2+3+1

when

when

7
Distance spectrum of Butson Hadamard codes

Definition 7.1
.

The
unit sphere

\Upsilon_{d}
in Euclidean
d
-space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the set of all unit norm vectors:

\Upsilon_{d}\triangleq\left\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|x\right\|=1\right\}

Definition 7.2
.

The
hermitian inner product
of
x
and
y
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is defined as

\left\langle x,y\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\overline{y_{i}},

with
\overline{z}
denoting the complex conjugate of
z.
The
(squared) Euclidean distance
of
x
and
y
is then

d_{E}(x,y)=\left\langle x-y,x-y\right\rangle=\left\langle x,x\right\rangle+\left\langle y,y\right\rangle-2\Re(\left\langle x,y\right\rangle).

Definition 7.3
.

A
spherical code
in dimension
d
is a finite set
X\subseteq\Upsilon_{d}.
Its minimum distance for the squared Euclidean distance is denoted by
\rho.

Its parameters are denoted compactly by
(d,\rho,|X|).
The function
A_{d}(\rho)
can then be defined as

A_{d}(\rho)=\max\{|X|\mid X\text{ spherical code of parameters }(d,\rho,|X|)\}.

In the next proposition we compute the possible Chinese Euclidean distances of
{C_{H}}
, which are also the squared Euclidean distances of the spherical code
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
to be defined below.

Proposition 7.1
.

The Chinese Euclidean distances of
{C_{H}}
are

\left\{d_{E}(x,y)\mid x\neq y,x,y\in w^{C_{H}}\right\}=\left\{2n\right\}\cup\left\{2n(1-\cos\frac{2\pi t}{q})\mid t=1,2,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{q}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}.

Proof.

For simplicity put
w=\zeta_{q}
and write
w^{F_{H}+i}
for
w^{i}w^{F_{H}}.
Note that, by definition of
C_{H}
we have
w^{C_{H}}=w^{F_{H}}\cup w^{F_{H}+1}\cup\cdots\cup w^{F_{H}+q-1}.

The Chinese Euclidean distances of
{C_{H}}
can then be computed as follows.
Let
x=w^{i}h,y=w^{j}h^{\prime},
where
h,h^{{}^{\prime}}\in w^{F_{H}}
and
i,j\in\left\{0,1,\dots,q-1\right\}.
We have

\left\langle x,y\right\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&h\neq h^{{}^{\prime}},\\
nw^{t},&h=h^{{}^{\prime}},\end{array}\right.

where
t\in\left\{1,2,\dots,q-1\right\}.
From the equation
d_{E}(x,y)=2n-2\Re(\left\langle x,y\right\rangle),
the result follows.
∎

The map
\psi:\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},x+iy\longmapsto(x,y)
is an isometry from
\left(\mathbb{C}^{n},d_{E}\right)
to
\left(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\delta\right),
where
\delta(U,V)=\left\|U-V\right\|^{2},
for all
U,V\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.
Note that

(\psi(a),\psi(b))=\Re\big(\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\big),

where
a
and
b\in\mathbb{C}^{n},
and
(,)
denotes the standard
inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

For normalization purposes,
we will let
\phi(z)=\frac{\psi(z)}{\sqrt{n}},
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Corollary 7.1
.

The spherical code
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})\subseteq\Upsilon_{2n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
has a size of
nq
and a distance of

\rho=\frac{d_{CE}}{n}=2(1-\cos\frac{2\pi}{q}).

We note an unexpected consequence for the parameters of a Butson Hadamard matrix.

Proposition 7.2
.

If
H\in BH(n,q)
exits, then the following inequality holds.

nq\leq A_{2n}(2(1-\cos{\frac{2\pi}{q}})).

We will require a special case of Levenshtein bound
[
10
, Theorem 2.5.1]
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
X
be a spherical code with parameters
(d,\rho,|X|)
and let
s=1-\frac{\rho}{2}.
Then the size
|X|
is necessarily bounded according to
|X|\leq L_{3}(s)=\frac{d(2+(d+1)s)(1-s)}{1-ds^{2}},
for
s\in[0,\frac{1}{(\sqrt{d+3}+1)}).

We deduce from Corollary
7.1
the following optimality result in relation to complex Hadamard matrices in the Turyn sense.

Corollary 7.2
.

If
H\in BH(n,4),
then the spherical code
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
is optimal in dimension
2n.

Proof.

By Corollary
7.1
we know that, in the notation of Theorem
7.1
, we have
s=\cos(\frac{2\pi}{4})=0,
and
d=2n.
The result follows by
Theorem
7.1
since the size of the code is
4n=2d.

∎

8

Covering radius of Butson Hadamard codes

8.1

Lower bound

Definition 8.1
.

The
deviation
of an arbitrary vector
x\in\Omega_{q}^{n}
from a polyphase code
C
is defined as

\theta(C,x)=\max_{y\in C}\{\left|\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\right|\},

where
\left\langle x,y\right\rangle
denotes the hermitian inner product of
x
and
y.

Proposition 8.1
.

If
X
is a bent sequence for
H\in BH(n,q),
then its corresponding polyphase code
\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}}
has deviation
\theta(C,X)=\sqrt{n},
where
X\in\Omega_{q}^{n}.

Proof.

HX=\lambda\mu_{k}(X),
where
k
and
q
are coprime.
Let
h_{i}
be the
i
-th row of
H,
then

\left|\left\langle h_{i},X\right\rangle\right|=\left|\lambda\right|\left|\mu_{k}(X_{i})\right|=\sqrt{n}.

Since
\left|\left\langle\zeta_{q}^{t}h_{i},X\right\rangle\right|=\left|\left\langle\ h_{i},X\right\rangle\right|
for all
i=1,\cdots,n
and all
t=1,\cdots,q
the result follows.
∎

We consider the polyphase codes attached to some
H\in BH(n,q)
when
q
equals
4,6
and
8.
By induction on
n,
we can get
d_{CE}(u,v)=d_{E}(x,y),
where
x=\zeta_{q}^{u}
and
y=\zeta_{q}^{v}
with
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}.

It can be seen by expanding
\left\langle x-y,x-y\right\rangle
that

Re(\left\langle x,y\right\rangle)=\frac{2n-d_{E}(x,y)}{2}=\frac{2n-d_{CE}(u,v)}{2},

for all
x,y\in\Omega_{q}^{n}.

The simple inequality
Re(\left\langle x,y\right\rangle)\leq\left|\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\right|
in the complex field shows that

r_{CE}(C_{H})\geq 2n-2\theta(C,x).

Combining this fact with the above proposition
8.1
yields the following bound.

Corollary 8.1
.

If there is a bent sequence
X
for
H\in BH(n,q),
then the covering radius of its attached
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
-code
C_{H}
is bounded below as

r_{CE}(C_{H})\geq 2n-2\sqrt{n}.

Upper bounds

In this subsection, we derive upper bounds on the covering radius of the spherical code
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
(after normalization to belong to the unit sphere) defined in the preceding section, as a function of its strength as a spherical design.
This bound is then directly an upper bound on the covering radius of the code
C_{H}
for the Chinese Euclidean distance. However, the two quantities may or may not coincide.

Definition 8.2
.

A spherical code
X
is a
1-design
if its center of mass is the origin or, more concretely, for all coordinate indices
i
satisfy
\sum_{x\in X}x_{i}=0.
It is
antipodal
if
X=-X.

Proposition 8.2
.

If
H\in BH(n,q)
with
q
is even, then the covering radius of
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
is at most
\sqrt{2}.

Proof.

The spherical code
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
is antipodal, because
-1
is a power of
\zeta_{q}.
The result follows then by
[
30
, Theorem
1]
.
∎

The same bound can be obtained from different hypotheses on
H.

Proposition 8.3
.

If
H
is dephased, then
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
is a
1-design
and its covering radius is at most
\sqrt{2}.

Proof.

Note that
H^{t}
is also a Hadamard matrix.
By taking scalar products between columns of
H
and its first column, which is all-one, we see that
\sum_{x\in X}x_{i}=0
for
i>1.

That
\sum_{x\in X}x_{1}=0,
follows by the well-known property of the roots of unity that
\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\zeta_{q}^{k}=0.
Hence the said spherical code is a
1
-design.

The result follows by Table 1 of
[
11
]
.
∎

Definition 8.3
.

A spherical code
X
is a
2-design
if it is a
1-design
, and if, furthermore, for all pairs
i\neq j
of coordinate indices the following two relations hold.

\sum_{x\in X}(x_{i}^{2}-x_{j}^{2})=0,\;\sum_{x\in X}x_{i}x_{j}=0.

Theorem 8.1
.

If
H\in BH(n,q)
is dephased, then
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
is a 2-design and its covering radius is at most
\sqrt{2(1-\frac{1}{2n})}.

Proof.

We have seen in Proposition 8.3 that
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
is a 1-design.
Denote by
h_{j}
the column number
j
of
H.
Write
w=\zeta_{q}.

That

\sum_{x\in X}x_{i}^{2}=\sum_{x\in X}x_{j}^{2}=q

is immediate by the fact that the columns of
H
have the same norm, by the Hadamard property of
H^{t}.
Further, still using that property we get

\sum_{x\in X}x_{i}x_{j}=\sum_{t=0}^{q-1}(\psi(w^{t}h_{i}),\psi(w^{t}h_{j}))=\frac{1}{{n}}\sum_{t=0}^{q-1}\Re(\langle h_{i},h_{j}\rangle)=0.

Thus
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
is a 2-design. The upper bound follows then by
[
11
, Table 1]
.
∎

This bound can be sharpened under some extra conditions.

Theorem 8.2
.

If
H\in BH(n,q),
with
q
even, is dephased, then
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
is an antipodal 2-design and its covering radius is at most
\sqrt{2(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}})}.

Proof.

We see by Theorem 8.1 that
\phi(\zeta_{q}^{C_{H}})
is a 2-design. It is antipodal by the evenness of
q.
The result follows then by Theorem 3 of
[
30
]
.
∎

To compare with the results on the previous subsection we can give the following corollary.

Corollary 8.2
.

If
H\in BH(n,q),
with
q
even, is dephased, then the covering radius of
C_{H}
for the Chinese Euclidean distance is bounded above as follows.

r_{CE}(C_{H})\leq 2n-\sqrt{2n}

Proof.

Immediate upon considering the normalization of the spherical code. ∎

9

Conclusion and open problems

In this paper we have explored a new definition of self-dual bent sequences in relation with Butson Hadamard matrices. The strong group of automorphisms which preserves the set of bent sequences for a given multiplier has been introduced, and given a generation algorithm. We have given computational methods to construct, for a given matrix, such a sequence. We have also given infinite families of Butson Hadamard matrices with attending self-dual bent sequences. We have also given an arithmetic criterion that allows to rule out all the matrices of
BH(n,q)
from admitting eigenvalues allowing to define such sequences. In a second part, we have explored the code attached to the Butson Hadamard matrix over a finite ring and its concomitant spherical code from the viewpoint of the Chinese Euclidean distance, and of the standard Euclidean distance. The existence of bent sequences implies then a lower bound on the covering radius of these codes.
Upper bounds on the covering radius of the spherical code can be provided by showing its strength as a spherical design. The main open problem left is then to know for
n\to\infty

which bound is closer to the true value of the covering radius of
C_{H},
the lower bound of Corollary
8.1
or the upper bound of Corollary
8.2
? A similar question was solved
for the Sylvester matrix and the generalized Sylvester matrix in
[
23
,
24
]
, using difficult techniques from number theory, probability, and discrepancy theory. In our case, Tables 4, 5, 6 seem to indicate that the true value equals the lower bound.
