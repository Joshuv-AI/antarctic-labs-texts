A Cross Validation Framework for Signal Denoising with Applications to Trend Filtering, Dyadic CART and Beyond
Anamitra Chaudhuri, Sabyasachi Chatterjee, Department of Statistics, University of Illinois, Urbana Champaign

Abstract
This paper formulates a general cross validation framework for signal denoising. The general framework is then applied to nonparametric regression methods such as Trend Filtering and Dyadic CART. The resulting cross validated versions are then shown to attain nearly the same rates of convergence as are known for the optimally tuned analogues. There did not exist any previous theoretical analyses of cross validated versions of Trend Filtering or Dyadic CART. To illustrate the generality of the framework we also propose and study cross validated versions of two fundamental estimators; lasso for high dimensional linear regression and singular value thresholding for matrix estimation. Our general framework is inspired by the ideas in Chatterjee and Jafarov 2015 and is potentially applicable to a wide range of estimation methods which use tuning parameters. Keywords: Cross Validation, Trend Filtering, Dyadic CART, Singular Value Threshholding, Lasso, Adaptive Risk Bounds.

Introduction

Cross Validation (CV) is a general statistical technique for choosing tuning parameters in a data driven way and is heavily used in practice for a wide variety of statistical methods. In spite of this, there is very little theoretical understanding of most CV algorithms used in practice. Within the nonparametric regression literature, rigorous theoretical guarantees for cross validated methods are limited to kernel smoothers, local linear regression methods or ridge regression (see
Wong 1983
,
Shao 1993
,
Li and
Racine 2004
,
Golub
et al. 1979
) which are all linear functions of the data
y
. There appears to be a need for theoretically backed general framework for building cross validation procedures for modern nonlinear regression methods. In this paper we attempt to start filling this gap in the literature by providing a general recipe to build provably adaptive and rate optimal CV estimators for some nonparametric estimation methods of current interest.

As an illustrative modern nonparametric regression method, we consider Trend Filtering (TF), proposed by
Kim
et al. 2009
; see
Tibshirani 2020
for a comprehensive overview. TF estimators, of order
r\geq 1
, fit
r
th degree (discrete) splines (piecewise polynomials with certain regularity). In contrast to classical nonparametric regression methods such as local polynomials, splines, kernels etc., TF is a
spatially adaptive
method as the knots of the piecewise polynomials are chosen in a data driven fashion. The last few years have seen a flurry of research (e.g.,
Tibshirani 2014
,
Guntuboyina et al. 2020
,
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
) in trying to understand the theoretical properties of TF. However, all the existing guarantees hold when the tuning parameter is chosen in an optimal way depending on problem parameters which are typically unknown. On the other hand, the practical applications of TF almost always involves cross validating the tuning parameter. This motivates the following natural question.
Is it possible to define a cross validated version of Trend Filtering which provably maintains all the risk guarantees known for optimally tuned Trend Filtering?
This is an important open question which motivates the study in this paper.

Our main focus is on developing theoretically tractable CV versions for modern fixed design nonparametric regression/signal denoising methods such as Trend Filtering, Dyadic CART, other image/matrix denoising methods, etc. Inspired by the idea underlying the cross validation method for Lasso, proposed by
Chatterjee and
Jafarov 2015
, we formalize a general cross validation framework for estimators in the so called
sequence model
. This framework, a variant of
K
fold CV, provides a unified, theoretically principled and computationally efficient way to design CV versions for a variety of estimation methods. In particular, we establish a general result about any CV estimator (which fits in our framework) in Theorem
2.1
which can then be used to obtain rate optimal guarantees for different estimators of interest.

We use this framework to propose and study a cross validated version of Trend Filtering with nearly matching theoretical guarantees known for the corresponding optimally tuned version; thereby answering our main question (in bold) posed above in the affirmative. To the best of our knowledge, before our work there has been no study done on the theoretical properties of a cross validated version of Trend Filtering. In practice, a particular CV version, implemented in the Rpackage Genlasso
Arnold et al. 2022
, is commonly used. However, no theoretical guarantees are available for this particular version. We outline the differences and similarities of our CV version with this one and present simulations which suggest that our CV version exhibits competitive finite sample performance as compared to this version.

We then use this framework to propose and study a cross validated version of Dyadic CART (DC), a classical regression tree method originally proposed in
Donoho 1997
. In a sense, DC can be thought of as an
\ell_{0}
penalized version of Trend Filtering which is an (generalized)
\ell_{1}
penalized least squares estimator. In
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
, DC has been shown to be a computationally faster and statistically competitive alternative to Trend Filtering and its multivariate versions such as the Total Variation Denoising estimator (proposed by
Rudin
et al. 1992
and used heavily for image processing). This makes it natural for us to consider Dyadic CART alongside Trend Filtering in this paper. In spite of Dyadic CART being a classical nonparametric regression method and having been applied in various settings over the years; all the available theoretical results depend on a theoretical choice of the tuning parameter
\lambda
which depends on unknown problem parameters. We again show our cross validated version is able to attain nearly the same risk bound as is known for the optimally tuned one.

Trend Filtering and Dyadic CART are the two prime examples considered in this paper where we apply our general CV framework. However, our CV framework is quite general and is potentially applicable to any other method which uses tuning parameters. To illustrate the generality and flexibility of our CV framework, we further consider two fundamental estimation methods, Singular Value Thresholding for Matrix Estimation and Lasso for high dimensional regression. We propose and study new cross validated versions of these fundamental methods. In the case of matrix estimation, we consider Singular Value Thresholding which is a canonical matrix estimation method; see
Cai
et al. 2010
,
Donoho and
Gavish 2014
,
Chatterjee 2015
. We use our cross validation framework to derive a cross validated version of Singular Value Thresholding and provide rigorous adaptivity guarantees for it. Finally, for the case of the Lasso, our cross validated version can be thought of as the penalized counterpart of the estimator proposed in
Chatterjee and
Jafarov 2015
which cross validates constrained Lasso. We show our cross validated Lasso estimator enjoys both types of standard rates of convergence known for optimally tuned Lasso.

To summarize, this paper gives a general framework for cross validation and presents one general risk bound (Theorem
2.1
) for any CV version of an estimation method which is built within our framework. Then we consider four different estimation methods, namely a) Trend Filtering, b) Dyadic CART, c) Singular Value Thresholding and d) Lasso. For each of these methods, we show how to construct a CV version within our framework. Next, we show how to apply Theorem
2.1
to our CV versions and establish rate optimality and adaptivity which is only known for the optimally tuned analogues of these methods. Essentially, our results for these estimators look like the one below (stated informally),

Theorem 1.1

(Informal)
.

Let
\widehat{\theta}
be an optimally tuned estimation method (any one of the four stated above). Let
\widehat{\theta}_{CV}
be our CV version. Then, with high probability,

MSE(\widehat{\theta}_{CV},\theta^{*})\leq MSE(\widehat{\theta},\theta^{*})\>p(\log n)

where
\theta^{*}
denotes the true signal,
MSE
denotes the usual mean squared error and
p(\log n)
is a (low degree) polynomial factor of
\log n
where
n
is the sample size.

2
Cross Validation Framework

The precise setting we consider is that of signal denoising or fixed design regression where we observe
y=\theta^{*}+\epsilon
, where all these are
n\times 1
vectors or vectorized matrices/tensors.
\theta^{*}
is the true signal and
\epsilon
is the noise vector consisting of i.i.d mean
0
subgaussian noise with subgaussian norm
\sigma
. This model is sometimes called the
subgaussian sequence model
and we will use the notation
y\sim Subg(\theta^{*},\sigma^{2})
to mean that
y
arises from this probabilistic model. The precise distribution of the
errors

\epsilon
could be anything as long as subgaussianity is satisfied. The problem is to denoise or estimate the signal
\theta^{*}
after observing
y.
Many well known and popular methods to estimate
\theta^{*}
in this model involve the use of tuning parameters. For such methods, we now lay out our general
K
fold cross validation framework.

2.1
A General Framework of Cross Validation

The following
6
general steps constitute our CV framework. This is a variant of
K\geq 2
fold CV and is different from the traditional
K
fold CV in some respects. Let
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
be a given family of estimators (with tuning parameter
\lambda
) for which a CV version is desired.

1.

Choose the number of folds
K.

2.

Partition
[n]
into
K
disjoint index sets or folds
I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{K}
. We allow this division to be done in a deterministic way or by using additional randomization. For any
j\in[K]
, denote
I^{c}_{j}
to be the index set which excludes the indices in
I_{j}
, that is
I_{j}^{c}=[n]\setminus I_{j}.

3.

For each
j\in[K]
and any choice of a tuning parameter
\lambda_{j}>0
, construct a version of
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j})}
which only depends on the data
y
through the coordinates in
I_{j}^{c}
or in other words is a function of
y_{I_{j}^{c}}.
We denote this estimator by
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

4.

For each
j\in[K]
, choose a finite set of possible candidate values of the tuning parameter
\lambda_{j}
, namely
\Lambda_{j}
. The set
\Lambda_{j}
can be chosen deterministically or even in a data driven way as a function of
y_{I_{j}^{c}}
.

5.

For any
j\in[K]
, denote the total squared prediction error (as a function of
\lambda
) on the
j
th fold by

CVERR_{j}(\lambda)=\left|\left|y_{I_{j}}-\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}\right|\right|^{2}.

Define
\widehat{\lambda}_{j}
to be the candidate in
\Lambda_{j}
for which the prediction error on the
j
th fold is the minimum, that is,

\widehat{\lambda}_{j}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}CVERR_{j}(\lambda).

Now define an intermediate estimator
\widetilde{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\widetilde{\theta}_{I_{j}}=\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda}_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\quad j\in[K].

(2.1)

6.

Define
\widehat{\lambda}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}-\widetilde{\theta}\|^{2}
where
\Lambda
is a deterministic set of candidate tuning parameter values to be chosen by the user.
Now define the final estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{CV}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be

\widehat{\theta}_{CV}=\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda})}.

(2.2)

We now discuss about various aspects of our
K
fold CV scheme and how it differs from the typical
K
fold CV scheme.

•

Dividing the dataset into folds is typically done randomly which is natural when the design is random. Since our focus is on fixed design methods or signal denoising methods our framework is a bit more general and allows deterministic folds as well. In our application to Trend Filtering we prefer using a simple deterministic strategy to create the folds. This avoids the use of extra randomization and makes our estimator conceptually simpler. In other applications such as Low Rank Matrix Estimation and Lasso it is not clear if there is a sensible way to create folds deterministically and thus we propose to create the folds randomly.

•

In any cross validation procedure, one needs to predict on a part of the data based on observations from the rest of the data. In our framework, the way one does this is by constructing estimators which are a function of a strict subset of the data. In particular, for each fold
j\in[K]
, the user needs to define estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
\theta^{*}
which are functions only of
y_{I_{j}^{c}}
. Estimating the true signal
\theta^{*}
based on only a subset of the data
y
can be thought of as a
completion
problem. Thus, we refer to the estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}
as
completion
estimators. One can use these completion estimators to define the predictions on the
j
th fold as
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}.
As an example, if the estimation method under consideration is Trend Filtering, then the user needs to design completion versions of Trend Filtering which are based only on a strict subset of the data. How exactly can one define these completion versions is problem specific and is described later.

•

In our framework, for each fold
j\in[K]
, the user needs to build a finite set of candidate tuning values
\Lambda_{j}
which is allowed to depend on
y_{I_{j}^{c}}.
This gives quite a bit of flexibility to the user. For example, for Trend Filtering we can use a particular data driven choice of
\Lambda_{j}
(see the discussion in Section
6.3.1
). However, it should be said here that for all of our estimators, we find that setting
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
with
N^{*}=O(\log n)
, a simple deterministic exponentially growing grid, is sufficient for our purposes.

•

In traditional/typical
K
fold CV, a single optimized tuning parameter is commonly chosen by taking
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda
and by minimizing the sum of prediction errors over all folds, that is,

\widehat{\lambda}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{K}CVERR_{j}(\lambda).

(2.3)

Subsequently, we refer to this as the
typical
or
traditional
CV framework. In Section
7
, we give simulations comparing this typical CV framework to our framework where in contrast, we first construct
K
optimized tuning parameters
\widehat{\lambda}_{j}
(one for each fold) which minimize the prediction error on each fold. We then construct an intermediate interleaved estimator
\widetilde{\theta}
by gluing together the optimized fits on each of the folds as in (
2.1
). Finally, we then come up with a single optimized tuning parameter by minimizing the squared distance of
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
(over a set
\Lambda
) to the intermediate fit
\widetilde{\theta}
as in (
2.2
). This seemingly roundabout way of choosing
\widehat{\lambda}
makes our cross validation scheme theoretically tractable; see our explanation in Section
2.3
.

•

The main advantage of our variant of
K
fold CV versus the traditional or typical version of
K
fold CV is mathematical tractability (see Theorem
2.1
below). We believe that ingredients of the theoretical analysis of our variant could be a stepping stone towards a theoretical analysis of other CV versions used in practice. Furthermore, our simulations suggest that our CV versions not only enjoy rigorous theoretical guarantees but are also practically useful, providing good finite sample performance. For example, we found that in our simulations for Trend Filtering (see Section
7
), the practical performance of our CV variant is very similar with the state of the art CV version implemented by the R package
Arnold et al. 2022
.

2.2
A General Result

We now describe the main theoretical result underlying our cross validation framework. This result is our main tool and is used throughout the paper. This result bounds the squared error loss of the cross validated estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{CV}
defined in (
2.2
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
be a given family of estimators in the subgaussian sequence model for a tuning parameter
\lambda
ranging in the set
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then the
K
fold cross validated estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{CV}
defined in (
2.2
) satisfies for all
x\geq 0
, with probability not less than
1-2K\exp(-x^{2}/2)
, the following inequality:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\theta}_{CV}-\theta^{*}\|\leq\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}-\theta^{*}\|+4\sum_{j\in[K]}\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\|\>\>+\>\>8\sqrt{2}\sigma

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[K]}\sqrt{\log|\Lambda_{j}|}

\displaystyle+8\sigma Kx.

We now explain the above theorem in more detail.

•

The above theorem holds for all subgaussian error distributions. In the above theorem, the stated high probability event holds under the joint distribution of the errors
\epsilon
and the (possibly) independently randomized assignment
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
.

•

Theorem
2.1
bounds the root sum of squared error (RSSE) of the cross-validated estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{CV}
as a sum of four terms. The first term is
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\sqrt{SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})}.
This is basically the RSSE of the optimally tuned version of
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
as long as
\Lambda
is chosen to contain the theoretically optimal value of
\lambda.
Clearly, this term is necessary as the CV version has to incur RSSE atleast as much as what is incurred by the optimally tuned version. For instance, considering the example of Trend Filtering, state of the art bounds for the RSSE are known under appropriate choices of the tuning parameter (see
Wang
et al. 2016
,
van de Geer and
Ortelli 2019
,
Guntuboyina et al. 2020
). As long as
\Lambda
is chosen containing these ideal choices of the tuning parameter; this term will scale exactly like the known bounds for Trend Filtering. The third term says that the dependence on the cardinality of
\Lambda_{j}
in the bound in Theorem
2.1
is logarithmic so as long as the cardinalities of
\Lambda_{j}
are bounded above by a polynomial in
n
our bound would only incur an additional
\log n
term. It is not hard to ensure that the cardinality of
\Lambda_{j}
is at most a polynomial in
n
as will be shown in our applications. The fourth term gives a parametric
O(1/\sqrt{n})
rate which is always going to be a lower order term.

•

The second term appearing in the bound in Theorem
2.1
is really the key term which arises due to cross validation. Bounding this term becomes the central task in our applications. The second term behooves us, for each
j\in[K]
, to bound
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}})
for
some good choice
of the tuning parameter
\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}
.

•

As per the earlier point, the main mathematical problem then facing us is to design completion estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}
and bound the prediction errors
\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}).
For instance, a first trivial step could be to write

\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}})\leq\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})},\theta^{*})

where in the inequality we have just dropped the subscript
I_{j}.
Now, the problem of bounding the R.H.S
\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})},\theta^{*})
looks similar to the problem of bounding the SSE of the original estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
with one major difference. The estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}
is a completion estimator, meaning that it is a function only of
y_{I_{j}}^{c}
in contrast with the original estimator (optimally tuned)
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
which is based on the full data. Nevertheless, we will show that for several estimation methods, there exists a way to divide the data into folds
I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{K}
, design completion estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j})}
for
j\in[K]
so that the estimation errors
\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})},\theta^{*})
scale like the usual SSE (possibly with an extra multiplicative log factor) for the original estimation method with optimal tuning. In Sections
4
,
3
we will propose some specific ways to do this for Trend Filtering and Dyadic CART respectively. A high level intuition why we can expect
\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})},\theta^{*})
to have same rates of convergence as
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})
is the following. Observe that
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}
is based on
y_{I_{j}}^{c}
which has on the order of
\left(n-\frac{n}{K}\right)
data points if
I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{K}
is chosen to have roughly equal size. On the other hand,
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
is based on the full dataset with
n
points. A good estimator based on
\left(n-\frac{n}{K}\right)
representative samples should have the same rate of convergence as a good estimator based on all the
n
samples with at most worse constants (since
K
is a small constant).

Remark 2.1
.

Theorem
2.1
is useful only when
K
is constant and not growing with
n
. So it is not useful for leave one out cross validation for instance where
K=n.
A different theory would be needed for that and we leave it as a topic for future research.

2.3
Why is our CV Estimator theoretically tractable?

In this section we explain what makes our CV Estimator theoretically tractable. In particular, we give a proof sketch of Theorem
2.1
.

Proof Sketch:

•

Step
1
:

A simple argument (see Lemma
8.1
) shows

\|\widehat{\theta}_{CV}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2\|\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|+\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}-\theta^{*}\|.

Therefore, this step reduces our problem to bounding the squared error of the intermediate estimator
\widetilde{\theta}.

•

Step
2
:

We note that

\|\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda}_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}).

Therefore, this step reduces our problem to bounding
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda}_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}})
for
j=1
say, as the same argument can be used for all
j\in[K]
.

•

Step
3
:

At this point, we make the crucial observation that conditionally on the (possibly random) assignment of folds
I_{1},\dots,I_{K}
and the noise variables
\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}
(on all folds except the
j
th fold), the estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda}_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}
can be seen as a least squares estimator (see Step
5
in Section
2.1
) over the finite set
\{\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}:\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}\}.
Note that this finite set becomes non random, once we condition on
I_{1},\dots,I_{K}
and
\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}.
This is because in our framework both the estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}
and the set
\Lambda_{j}
can only be functions of
y_{I_{j}^{c}}
. This allows us to use an oracle risk bound for a least squares estimator over a finite set (see Lemma
8.3
) to conclude a conditional high probability statement for all
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle P\Bigg(SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda}_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}})\leq 2\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\|+4\sqrt{2}\sigma

\displaystyle\sqrt{\log|\Lambda_{j}|}+4x\Bigg|I_{1},,\dots,I_{K},\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq 1-2\exp(-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}).

•

Step
4
:

Note that the probability on the R.H.S in the above conditional high probability statement does not depend on the conditioned variables. Hence, we realize that we can actually drop the conditioning in the above statement which, along with the display in Step
1
, then furnishes the statement in Theorem
2.1
.

Let’s compare with the typical/traditional
K
fold CV framework where
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda
and the final CV estimator is
\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda})}
where
\widehat{\lambda}
is chosen according to (
2.3
).
To the best our knowledge, there is yet no general analysis available for this typical CV framework. There does not seem to be a way to invoke a conditional least squares estimator intepretation here as we are minimizing over the sum of prediction errors over all folds at once. In general, the above method seems harder to analyze and we leave this for future research.

3
Dyadic CART

3.1
Background and Related Literature

The Dyadic CART estimator is a computationally feasible decision tree method proposed first in
Donoho 1997
in the context of regression on a two-dimensional grid design. This estimator optimizes a penalized least squares criterion over the class of
dyadic decision trees
. Subsequently, several papers have used ideas related to dyadic partitioning for regression, classification and density estimation, e.g., see
Nowak
et al. 2004
,
Scott and
Nowak 2006
,
Blanchard et al. 2007
,
Willett and
Nowak 2007
.

The two main facts about Dyadic CART are

•

The Dyadic CART estimator attains an oracle risk bound; e.g see Theorem
2.1
in
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
. This oracle risk bound can then be used to show that the Dyadic CART estimator is minimax rate optimal (up to small log factors) for several function classes of interest.

•

The Dyadic CART estimator can be computed very fast by a bottom up dynamic program with computational complexity linear in the sample size, see Lemma
1.1
in
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
.

These two properties of the Dyadic CART make it a very attractive signal denoising method.
However, this oracle risk bound is satisfied only when a tuning parameter is chosen to be larger than a threshhold which depends on the unknown noise variance of the error distribution. In practice, an user is naturally led to cross validate this tuning parameter. To the best of our knowledge, there has been no rigorous study done so far on Dyadic CART when the tuning parameter is chosen by cross validation. Our goal here is to propose a cross validated version of Dyadic CART in general dimensions which retain the two properties stated above. We now set up notations, define the Dyadic CART estimator more precisely and state the existing oracle risk bound.

3.2
Notations and Definitions

Let us denote the
d
dimensional lattice with
N
points by
L_{d,n}\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}^{d}
where

N=n^{d}.
The lattice design is quite
commonly used for theoretical studies in multidimensional nonparametric function estimation
(see, e.g.
Nemirovski 2000
) and is also the natural setting for
certain applications such as image denoising, matrix/tensor estimation.
Letting
\theta^{*}
denote the true signal, our observation
model becomes

y=\theta^{*}+\epsilon,

where

y,\theta^{*},\epsilon
are real valued functions on
L_{d,n}
and
hence are
d
dimensional arrays. Furthermore,
\epsilon
is a noise array consisting of i.i.d subgaussian errors with an unknown subgaussian norm
\sigma>0.

For any
a<b\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, let us define the interval of positive integers
[a,b]:=\{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:a\leq i\leq b\}
where
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
denotes
the set of all positive integers. For a positive integer
n
we
also denote the set
[1,n]
by just
[n].
A subset
R\subset L_{d,n}
is called an
axis aligned
rectangle
if
R
is a product of
intervals, i.e.
R=\prod_{i=1}^{d}[a_{i},b_{i}].

Henceforth, we will just use the word rectangle to denote an
axis aligned rectangle. Let us define a
rectangular
partition
of
L_{d,n}
to be a set of rectangles

\mathcal{R}
such that (a) the rectangles in
\mathcal{R}

are pairwise disjoint and (b)
\cup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}R=L_{d,n}.

For a given rectangle
R\subset L_{d,n}
and any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}
let us denote the array obtained by restricting
\theta
to
R
by
\theta_{R}.
For a given array
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}
, let
k(\theta)
denote the smallest positive integer
k
such that a set of
k
rectangles
R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
form a rectangular partition of
L_{d,n}
and the restricted array
\theta_{R_{i}}
is a constant array.
In other words,
k(\theta)
is the cardinality of the minimal rectangular partition of
L_{d,n}
such that
\theta
is piecewise constant on the partition.

3.2.1
Description of Dyadic CART

Let us consider a generic discrete interval
[a,b].
We define a
dyadic split
of the interval to be a split of the interval
[a,b]
into two equal intervals. To be concrete, the interval
[a,b]
is split into the intervals
[a,a-1+\lceil(b-a+1)/2\rceil]
and
[a+\lceil(b-a+1)/2\rceil,b].
Now consider a generic rectangle
R=\prod_{i=1}^{d}[a_{i},b_{i}].
A
dyadic split
of the rectangle
R
involves the choice of a coordinate
1\leq j\leq d
to be split and then the
j
th interval in the product defining the rectangle
R
undergoes a dyadic split. Thus, a dyadic split of
R
produces two sub rectangles
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
where
R_{2}=R\cap R_{1}^{c}
and
R_{1}
is of the following form for some
j\in[d]
,

R_{1}=\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}[a_{i},b_{i}]\times[a_{j},a_{j}-1+\lceil(b_{j}-a_{j}+1)/2\rceil]\times\prod_{i=j+1}^{d}[a_{i},b_{i}].

Starting from the trivial partition which is just
L_{d,n}
itself, we can create a refined partition by dyadically splitting
L_{d,n}.
This will result in a partition of
L_{d,n}
into two rectangles. We can now keep on dividing recursively, generating new partitions. In general, if at some stage we have the partition
\Pi=(R_{1},\dots,R_{k})
, we can choose any of the rectangles
R_{i}
and dyadically split it to get a refinement of
\Pi
with
k+1
nonempty rectangles.
A recursive dyadic partition
(RDP) is any partition reachable by such successive dyadic splitting. Let us denote the set of all recursive dyadic partitions of
L_{d,n}
as
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n}.
Indeed, a natural way of encoding any RDP of
L_{d,n}
is by a binary tree where each nonleaf node is labeled by an integer in
[d].
This labeling corresponds to the choice of the coordinate that was used for the split.

For a given array
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}
, let
k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\theta)
denote the smallest positive integer
k
such that a set of
k
rectangles
R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
form a recursive dyadic partition of
L_{d,n}
and the restricted array
\theta_{R_{i}}
is a constant array for all
1\leq i\leq k.
In other words,
k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\theta)
is the cardinality of the minimal recursive dyadic partition of
L_{d,n}
such that
\theta
is constant on every rectangular partition.

By definition, we have for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}
,

k(\theta)\leq k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\theta).

We can now define the Dyadic CART estimator for a tuning parameter
\lambda>0
,

\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}\coloneqq\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}}\big(\|y-\theta\|^{2}+\lambda k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\theta)\big).

(3.1)

Equivalently, we can also define
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}=P_{S_{\widehat{\pi}^{(\lambda)}}}y
, where
\widehat{\pi}^{(\lambda)}
is a data dependent partition defined as

\widehat{\pi}^{(\lambda)}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n}}\big(\|y-P_{S_{\pi}}y\|^{2}+\lambda|\pi|\big).

In the above, for any
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n}
,
|\pi|
denotes the number of rectangles constituting
\pi
,
S_{\pi}
denotes the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}
which consists of all arrays which are constant on every rectangle of
\pi
and
P_{S_{\pi}}
denotes the orthogonal projection matrix on that subspace. The discrete optimization problem in the last display can be solved by a dynamic programming algorithm in
O_{d}(N)
time which makes fast computation of Dyadic CART possible.

3.2.2
Existing Oracle Risk Bound and its Implications

We now state the oracle risk bound satisfied by the Dyadic CART estimator.

Theorem 3.1
.

[Theorem
2.1
in
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
]

Suppose the error vector
\epsilon
is gaussian with mean
0
and covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}I
. Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that if we set
\lambda\geq C\sigma^{2}\log N
, then we have the following risk bound

\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\leq\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left[3\>\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|^{2}+2\lambda\>k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\theta)\right]+C\sigma^{2}.

In the case when
d=2
the above oracle risk bound had already appeared in the original paper
Donoho 1997
, albeit up to an extra log factor. This oracle risk bound actually can be used to show that the Dyadic CART estimator adaptively attains near optimal rates of convergence for several function classes of interest. For instance, the above oracle risk bound was used in
Donoho 1997
to show that Dyadic CART is minimax rate optimal over several bivariate anistropic smoothness classes of functions. In
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
, the above oracle risk bound was used to show that the Dyadic CART estimator is minimax rate optimal over the class of bounded variation signals in general dimensions and thus matches the known rates of convergence attained by the Total Variation Denoising estimator; see
Hütter and
Rigollet 2016
,
Sadhanala
et al. 2016
. It has been explained in detail in
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
how the above oracle risk bound (along with its fast computation) puts forward Dyadic CART as a computationally faster alternative to Trend Filtering and its multidimensional versions while essentially retaining (and even improving in some aspects) its statistical benefits.

The main question we consider here is the following.

Can a cross validated version of Dyadic CART still attain the oracle risk bound in Theorem
3.1
?

To the best of our knowledge, the above question is unanswered as of now. We answer this question in the affirmative in this paper. Since our cross validated version of Dyadic CART will also satisfy a result very similar to the oracle risk bound as in Theorem
3.1
it will essentially inherit all the known results for the usual Dyadic CART mentioned above.

3.3
Description of the CVDCART estimator

We will follow our general scheme of defining cross validated estimators as laid out in Section
2.1
. In what follows and in the descriptions of all our CV estimators in the later sections as well, we will only describe the first three steps to avoid repetition. The first three steps describe the number of folds, the construction of the folds and the construction of the completion estimators respectively. The last three steps are common to all of them and are the same as the last three steps laid out in the general framework in Section
2.1
. In all of our CV estimators, we take our grid of candidate tuning values

\Lambda=\Lambda_{j}=\{1,2,2^{2},2^{3},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}

for an appropriate
N^{*}
.

Let
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
be the family of Dyadic CART estimators with tuning parameter
\lambda\geq 0
as defined in (
3.1
). We denote the final resulting cross validated Dyadic CART estimator by
\widehat{\theta}_{CVDC}
.

1.

Set
K=2
.

2.

We divide
L_{d,n}
randomly into two folds/subsets
I_{1},I_{2}
as follows:
Let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}
be a random array consisting of i.i.d
Bernoulli(1/2)
entries. Now define

I_{1}=\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\in L_{d,n}:W(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})=1\}.

The set
I_{2}
is just the complement of
I_{1}
in
L_{d,n}.

3.

Next, we define the estimators for
j\in\{1,2\}
,

\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}}||y_{I_{j}}-\theta_{I_{j}}||^{2}+\lambda k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\theta).

(3.2)

Note that
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}
is a completion version of Dyadic CART because it only depends on
y_{I_{j}}.

3.4
Computation of the CVDCART estimator

The major step in computing
\widehat{\theta}_{CVDC}
is to compute
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}
for
j=\{1,2\}.
We present a lemma below stating the computational complexity of
\widehat{\theta}_{CVDC}.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
I
denote the set
I_{1}
or
I_{2}.
The computational complexity of the completion estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
, i.e the number of elementary operations involved in computing
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
is bounded by
C2^{d}dN
for some absolute constant
C>0
. Therefore, the overall computational complexity of
\widehat{\theta}_{CVDC}
is bounded by
CN^{*}2^{d}dN.
Since
N^{*}
can be taken to be
O(\log N)
(as explained later), the overall computational complexity becomes
O_{d}(N\log N)
in this case.

Remark 3.1
.

The above lemma ensures that our CVDCART estimator can also be computed in near linear time in the sample size.

In Section
9
we describe a bottom up dynamic programming based algorithm to compute
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
. The underlying idea behind this algorithm is similar to the original algorithm given in
Donoho 1997
to compute the original version of Dyadic CART based on the full data. The description of the algorithm in Section
9
also clarifies what is the computational complexity of the algorithm, thereby proving Lemma
3.2
.

3.5
Specification of
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}

There may be multiple solutions to the optimization problem defined in (
3.2
). For our main result (which is Theorem
3.3
) to hold, we need to add one more specification which will complete the definition of
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
for any given subset
I\subset L_{d,n}.
Below, we use the notation
\overline{y}_{I}
to denote the mean of all entries of
y
in
I
.

In Section
9
it is shown that the optimization problem in (
3.2
) can be solved by first solving the following discrete optimization problem over the space of all recursive dyadic partitions

\widehat{\pi}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n}}\big[\sum_{R\in\pi}\mathrm{1}(R\cap I\neq\emptyset)\sum_{u\in R\cap I}(y_{u}-\overline{y}_{R\cap I})^{2}+\lambda|\pi|\big]

(3.3)

where the notation
\sum_{R\in\pi}
means summing over all the constituent rectangles
R
of
\pi
and
|\pi|
denotes the number of constituent rectangles of the partition
\pi.

It is then shown that a solution
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
to the optimization problem (
3.2
) is a piecewise constant array over the optimal partition
\widehat{\pi}
. For all
u\in R
, for each constituent rectangle
R
of
\widehat{\pi}
such that the set
R\cap I
is non empty,

\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{u}=\overline{y}_{R\cap I}.

(3.4)

It is possible for a constituent rectangle
R
of the optimal partition
\widehat{\pi}
to not contain any data point from
I
, i.e, the set
R\cap I
is empty. In that case,
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
can take any constant value within
R
and still be an optimal solution to the optimization problem (
3.2
). In such a case, for all
u\in R
, we set

\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{u}=\overline{y}_{I}

(3.5)

This fully specifies the estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
which is a valid completion version of the Dyadic CART estimator being a function of
y_{I}
only.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that the above specification still does not mean that
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
is uniquely defined. This is because
\widehat{\pi}
in (
3.3
) is not necessarily uniquely defined. However, as long as we take a solution
\widehat{\pi}
to the optimization problem in (
3.3
) and then construct
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
satisfying both (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), Theorem
3.3
holds.

3.6
Main Result for the CVDCART estimator

Now we state an oracle risk bound for our proposed CVDCART estimator in general dimensions. Before that, let us define the following quantities

\displaystyle R(\theta^{*},\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}}\big(3\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|^{2}+2\lambda k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\theta)\big),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle V(\theta^{*})

\displaystyle:=\max_{u,v\in L_{d,n}}|\theta^{*}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{v}|.

Theorem 3.3
.

Fix any
\alpha\geq 1
and any
\delta>0
. There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that if we set our grid
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
for
j=\{1,2\}
, satisfying

2^{N^{*}}>C\sigma^{2}\log N,

then we have the following bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\|\widehat{\theta}_{CVDC}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\leq\frac{C_{3}}{N}\Bigg\{\big(R(\theta^{*},C\sigma^{2}\log n)+\alpha\sigma^{2}\log N\big)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{V(\theta^{*})}{\sigma}+\sqrt{\log N}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sigma^{2}\log(4N^{*}/\delta)\Bigg\}

with probability at least
1-\delta-C_{1}\log(V(\theta^{*})\sqrt{N})N^{-C_{2}\alpha}
, where
C,C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
are absolute positive constants which may only depend on the underlying dimension
d.

It is now worthwhile discussing some aspects of Theorem
3.3
.

1.

The above theorem basically ensures that the mean squared error (MSE) of our cross validated estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{CVDC}
can also essentially be bounded (up to additive and multiplicative log factors) by the desired factor
R(\theta^{*},\sigma^{2}\log N)
. The same bound holds for the optimally tuned version of Dyadic CART as is stated in Theorem
3.1
. The only essential difference is that our bound contains an extra multiplicative factor
(\frac{V(\theta^{*})}{\sigma}+\sqrt{\log N})^{2}.
The term
V(\theta^{*})
captures the range of the underlying signal. For realistic signals, the range should stay bounded. In these cases, this extra multiplicative factor would then be a logarithmic factor.

2.

Theorem
3.3
ensures that our cross validated estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{CVDC}
(up to log factors) enjoys a similar oracle risk bound as the optimally tuned version of Dyadic CART. Therefore, the CVDCART estimator essentially inherits all the known statistical risk bounds for Dyadic CART. In particular, CVDCART estimator would be minimax rate optimal (up to log factors) for several function/signal classes of interest such as anisotropically smooth functions (see
Donoho 1997
), piecewise constant signals on arbitrary rectangular partitions when
d\leq 2
and signals with finite bounded variation (see
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
).

3.

The existing risk bound Theorem
3.1
says that the optimal tuning parameter choice is
C\sigma^{2}\log N
for some absolute constant
C>0
. Theorem
3.3
holds as long as such a choice of
\lambda
is included in
\Lambda.
This suggests that we would like to have the grid of choices
\Lambda
to be dense enough so that we do not miss the optimal tuning value range. On the other hand, we would like to have a sparse grid
\Lambda
because the computational complexity scales like the cardinality of
\Lambda
times the complexity of computing one Dyadic CART estimator for a given
\lambda.

Observe that our risk bound (in particular
R(\theta^{*},\lambda)
) scales proportionally with
\lambda
. This implies that missing the optimal tuning value by a factor of
2
means that we pay at most
2
times the MSE of the ideally tuned version. This fact allows us to take a geometrically growing grid
\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}.
Selecting such a sparse grid
\Lambda
then has obvious computational benefits. The only disadvantage here is that
N^{*}
then becomes like a tuning parameter to be set by the user. However, in practice and in theory, this seems to be a minor issue. Theorem
3.3
holds if
2^{N^{*}}
is larger than the theoretically recommended choice of
\lambda=C\sigma^{2}\log N
. Hence, plugging in even a gross overestimate
\sigma_{over}
of
\sigma
and choosing
N^{*}=C^{\prime}\left(\log\log N+\log\sigma_{over}\right)
for a large enough constant
C^{\prime}
would suffice for any realistic value of
\sigma.
In our simulations we simply take
N^{*}=\log_{2}N.

4
Trend Filtering

4.1
Background and Related Work

Trend Filtering, proposed by
Kim
et al. 2009
, is a univariate nonparametric regression method that has become popular recently; see
Tibshirani 2020
for a comprehensive overview. For a given integer
r\geq 1
and any tuning parameter
\lambda\geq 0
, the
r^{th}
order trend filtering estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{\lambda}
is defined as the minimizer of the sum of squared errors when we penalize the sum of the absolute
r^{th}
order discrete derivatives of the signal. Formally, given a data vector
y
,

\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{\lambda}:=\left(\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}||y-\theta||^{2}+\lambda n^{r-1}||D^{(r)}\theta||_{1}\right)

(4.1)

where
D^{(1)}\theta:=\left(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1},\theta_{3}-\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{n}-\theta_{n-1}\right)
and
D^{(r)}\theta
, for
r\geq 2
, is recursively defined as
D^{(r)}\theta:=D^{(1)}D^{(r-1)}\theta
. For any positive integer
r\geq 1
, let us now define the
r^{th}
order total variation of a vector
\theta
as follows:

\mathrm{TV}^{(r)}(\theta)=n^{r-1}\|D^{(r)}(\theta)\|_{1}

(4.2)

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes the usual
\ell_{1}
norm of a vector.

Remark 4.1
.

The
n^{r-1}
term in the above definition is a normalizing factor and is written following the convention adopted in the trend filtering literature; see for instance the terminology of canonical scaling introduced in
Sadhanala
et al. 2016
. If we think of
\theta
as evaluations of a
r
times differentiable function
f:[0,1]\rightarrow R
on the grid
(1/n,2/n\dots,n/n)
then the Riemann approximation to the integral
\int_{[0,1]}|f^{(r)}(t)|dt
is precisely equal to
\mathrm{TV}^{(r)}(\theta).
Here
f^{(r)}
denotes the
r
th derivative of
f.
Thus, for natural instances of
\theta
, the reader can imagine that
\mathrm{TV}^{(r)}(\theta)=O(1).

A continuous version of these trend filtering estimators, where discrete derivatives are replaced by continuous derivatives, was proposed much earlier in the statistics literature
by
Mammen and van de
Geer 1997
under the name
locally adaptive regression splines
.
By now, there exists a body of literature studying the risk properties of trend filtering under squared error loss. There exists two strands of risk bounds for trend filtering in the literature focussing on two different aspects.

Firstly, it is known that for any
r\geq 1,
a well tuned trend filtering estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{\lambda}
attains a MSE bound
O\left(\frac{(TV^{(r)}(\theta^{*}))^{1/r}}{n}\right)^{2r/2r+1}.
This bound is minimax rate optimal over the space
\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathrm{TV}^{(r)}(\theta)\leq V\}
for a given
V>0
and has been shown in
Tibshirani 2014
and
Wang
et al. 2014
building on earlier results by
Mammen and van de
Geer 1997
. A standard terminology in this field terms this
O(n^{-2r/2r+1})
rate as the
slow rate
.

Secondly, it is also known that an ideally tuned Trend Filtering (of order
r
) estimator can adapt to
\|D^{r}(\theta)\|_{0}
, the number of non zero elements in the
r^{th}
order differences,
under some assumptions on
\theta^{*}
. Such a result has been shown in
Guntuboyina et al. 2020
(for the constrained version of Trend Filtering of all orders) and
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
(for the penalized version of Trend Filtering with
r\leq 4
). In this case, the Trend Filtering estimator of order
r
attains the near parametric
\widetilde{O}(\|D^{(r)}(\theta)\|_{0}/n)
rate which can be much faster than the
O(n^{-2r/2r+1})
rate. Standard terminology in this field terms this as the
fast rate
.

The big problem is that the results described above are shown to hold only under theoretical choices of the tuning parameter. These choices depend on unknown problem parameters and hence cannot be directly implemented in practice. Moreover different tuning is needed to achieve slow or fast rates. A square root version of Trend Filtering was proposed by
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2021
to mitigate this issue. It has been shown that the ideal choice of the tuning parameter for the square root version does not depend on the noise variance. However, the tuning parameter still needs to be set to a particular unspecified constant (differently depending on whether slow or fast rates are desired) and thus does not really solve this problem.

Therefore, a version of Trend Filtering which chooses the tuning parameter in a data driven way and attains both slow and fast rates is highly desirable. This naturally leads us to consider cross validation. Practical usage of Trend Filtering almost always involves cross validation to choose
\lambda
; e.g, see
Politsch et al. 2020
. However, no theoretical properties are known for a cross validated version of Trend Filtering. We attempt to fill this gap in the literature by proposing a cross validated version of Trend Filtering based on our general framework. Our goal here is to show that our cross validated version nearly (atmost up to log factors) attains the risk (both the slow rate and the fast rate) of the ideally tuned versions.

4.2
Description of the CVTF Estimator

We will again follow our general scheme of defining cross validated estimators as laid out in Section
2.1
. Fix any
r\geq 1
and let
\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{\lambda}
be the family of
r^{th}
order Trend Filtering estimators with tuning parameter
\lambda\geq 0
as defined in (
4.1
). We denote the final resulting cross validated trend filtering estimator of order
r\geq 1
by
\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{CVTF}.

1.

Set
K=r+1
.

2.

Divide
[n]
deterministically into
K
disjoint index sets (ordered)
I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{K}
as follows. Let
n_{0}=\lfloor\frac{n}{K}\rfloor
. Then for any
j\in[K]
, define

I_{j}=\{Kt+j\leq n:t=0,1,\dots,n_{0}\}.

In words, data points
K
positions apart are placed into the same fold.

3.

For all
j\in[K]
, define
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by interpolating
y_{I_{j}^{c}}
as follows:

\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i}\ \quad&\text{if}\ i\in I_{j}^{c}\\
\sum_{l=1}^{r}(-1)^{l+1}{r\choose l}y_{i+l}&\text{if}\ i\in I_{j}\ \text{and}\ i+r\leq n\\
\sum_{l=1}^{r}(-1)^{l+1}{r\choose l}y_{i-l}&\text{if}\ i\in I_{j}\ \text{and}\ i+r>n\end{cases}.

In words,
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})
is defined in such a way that within the index set
I_{j}^{c}
it is same as
y
but for any index in
I_{j}
it is linearly interpolated from the neighbouring indices of
y
in
I_{j}^{c}
by a
r^{th}
order polynomial interpolation scheme.

Next, we define the completion estimators

\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c},r)}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})-\theta\right\|^{2}+\lambda n^{r-1}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta\right\|_{1},\quad j\in[K].

Note that
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c},r)}
is a valid completion version as it is a function of
y_{I_{j}^{c}}
only.

Remark 4.2
.

A different yet valid choice of
\Lambda_{j}
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{j}
,
j\in[K]
,
\Lambda
and
\widehat{\lambda}
is described in Section
6.3.1
.

4.3
Main Results for the CVTF Estimator

Below we state both the slow rate and the fast rate results for the proposed CVTF estimator.

Theorem 4.1
.

[Slow Rate]

Fix any
r\geq 1
and any
\delta>0.
There exists a constant
C_{r}
only depending on
r
such that if we take our grid
\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},2^{3},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
satisfying

2^{N^{*}}\geq C_{r}\sigma\big(n\log n\big)^{1/(2r+1)}

then we have the following bound with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{CVTF}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\leq\frac{2C_{r}V^{*}}{n^{r}}\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}+C_{r}\sigma^{2}\left(n^{-\frac{2r}{2r+1}}(V^{*}\log(n/\delta))^{\frac{1}{2r+1}}+\frac{N^{*}+\log(1/\delta)}{n}\right),

where
V^{*}=n^{r-1}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}=\mathrm{TV}^{(r)}(\theta^{*})
.

Theorem 4.2
.

[Fast Rate]

Fix any
1\leq r\leq 4
and any
\delta>0
. Let
s=\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\|_{0}
and
\mathcal{S}=\{j\,:\,(D^{(r)}\theta^{*})_{j}\neq 0\}
. Then
\mathcal{S}
can be represented as
\mathcal{S}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{s}\}\subseteq[n-r]
, where
1\leq t_{1}<\dots<t_{s}\leq n-r
. Also, let
t_{0}:=0
and
t_{s+1}:=n-r+1
. Next, we define
n_{i}:=t_{i}-t_{i-1}
,
i\in[s+1]
and
n_{\rm\max}:=\max_{i\in[s+1]}n_{i}
.

Define the sign vector
q^{*}\in\{-1,+1\}^{s}
containing the signs of the elements in
(D^{(r)}\theta^{*})_{\mathcal{S}}
, that is, for every
i\in[s]
,

q^{*}_{i}:=sign(D^{(r)}\theta^{*})_{t_{i}},

and the index set

\mathcal{S}^{\pm}:=\{2\leq i\leq s:q^{*}_{i}q^{*}_{i-1}=-1\}\cup\{1,s+1\}.

Suppose
\theta^{*}
satisfies the following minimum length assumption, for a constant
c>1
,

n_{\rm\max}\leq cn_{i}\ \text{and}\ n_{i}\geq r(r+2)\ \text{for all}\ i\in\mathcal{S}^{\pm}.

Then there exists a constant
C_{r}
only depending on
r
such that if we take our grid
\Lambda=\{1,2^{1},2^{2},2^{3},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
satisfying

2^{N^{*}}\geq C_{r}\sigma s^{-(2r-1)/2}\sqrt{n\log n}

then we have the following bound with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{CVTF}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{r}s}{n}\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}^{2}+C_{r}\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{s}{n}\log n\log(n/\delta)+\frac{N^{*}+\log(1/\delta)}{n}\right).

We now make some remarks to explain certain aspects of the above theorems.

1.

We have presented both our slow rate and the fast rate theorem following the notations and presentation style adopted by
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
in Theorem
1.1
in their paper. We have done this mainly because our proofs rely on the results developed by
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
and also to remain consistent with the existing literature. The two theorems above ensure that the
\widehat{\theta}_{CVTF}
estimator essentially attains the slow rate and the fast rate (both implied by Theorem
1.1
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
) known for an ideally tuned penalized Trend Filtering estimator. The main difference in both our bounds are the extra additive terms involving
|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}|_{\infty}.
However, as we explain below, this is typically a lower order term.

2.

Both the bounds above involve the term
|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}|_{\infty}.
Note that under the canonical scaling where
V^{*}=O(1)
, we have
|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}|_{\infty}\leq\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\|_{1}=O(n^{1-r}).
This means that the terms involving
|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}|_{\infty}
in our bounds can again be considered to be of a lower order for all
r\geq 1
under realistic regimes of
V^{*}.

3.

In light of the above two remarks, under the canonical scaling, the bound in Theorem
4.1
can be read as scaling like the near minimax rate
\widetilde{O}(n^{-\frac{2r}{2r+1}})
and the bound in Theorem
4.2
scales like the near parametric rate
\widetilde{O}(|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}|_{0}\>\>n^{-1})
up to additive lower order terms. Thus, our bounds show that the CVTF estimator attains the slow rate and the fast rate, up to log factors, and hence does not suffer too much in comparison to ideally tuned trend filtering estimators, atleast in the context of rates of convergence.

4.

We only state Theorem
4.2
for
r\in\{1,2,3,4\}
and the assumptions on
\theta^{*}
in Theorem
4.2
are identical to the assumptions made in Theorem
1.1
of
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
. This is because our proof is based on the proof technique employed by
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
, as explained in Section
11.1
. The fast rate result in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
also is shown to hold for
r\in\{1,2,3,4\}
. To the best of our knowledge, a complete proof of the fast rate for penalized trend filtering of order
r>4
is not yet available in the literature. In contrast, fast rates have been established for an ideally tuned constrained trend filtering of all orders; see
Guntuboyina et al. 2020
. It is possible to develop a cross validated version of the constrained trend filtering using our framework and show that it will then enjoy fast rates for all orders
r\geq 1.
However, in this paper we prefer considering the penalized version due to its popularity and computational benefits.

5.

The assumption
n_{max}\leq cn_{i}
for a constant
c>1
means that the length of each of the blocks in
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}
are within a constant factor of each other. This kind of minimum length assumption is standard and is also known to be necessary for fast rates to hold; see Remark
2.4
in
Guntuboyina et al. 2020
. Note that such a minimum length assumption is needed only for the blocks in
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}
and not for all blocks.
For example, when
r=1
, the blocks in
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}
are either the first and last constant pieces of
\theta^{*}
or those constant pieces of
\theta^{*}
which constitute a local maxima stretch or a local minima stretch.

6.

The ideal choice of the tuning parameter (as shown in Theorem
1.1
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
) depends on whether we desire the slow rate or the fast rate. However, both these choices scale (with
n
) like
n^{\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha<1.
Therefore, as long as
2^{N^{*}}
is chosen to be larger than these idealized choices, both the theorems presented above will hold. By construction,
\Lambda
contains an exponentially growing grid which means that
N^{*}
can be chosen so that it grows logarithmically in
n,\sigma
. Therefore, in the regime where
\sigma
stays bounded away from
\infty
, the term
\sigma^{2}\frac{N^{*}+\log(1/\delta)}{n}=O(\log n/n)
appearing in both of the above theorems is a lower order term. In practice, one can choose
2^{N^{*}}=n
, for instance, which will satisfy the required condition for realistic sample sizes
n
and
\sigma.

5
Singular Value Thresholding for Matrix Estimation

Singular Value Thresholding (SVT) is a fundamental matrix estimation and completion method; see
Cai
et al. 2010
,
Donoho and
Gavish 2014
,
Chatterjee 2015
. It is known that Singular Value thresholding is an
all purpose
matrix estimation method and performs well in a wide variety of structured matrix estimation problems; see
Chatterjee 2015
. However, the existing guarantees for this estimator depend on a thresholding parameter being chosen to be larger than a cutoff value which depends on the noise variance. In practice, the choice of the threshold matters in regards to the finite sample performance of the SVT estimator; see Section
5
(simulations) in
Chatterjee and
Mukherjee 2019
where the authors were investigating the SVT estimator in the context of estimating Nonparametric Bradley Terry Matrices. Thus, it is of both theoretical and practical interest in using a cross validated version of the SVT estimator. To the best of our knowledge, a theoretical analysis of a CV version of SVT is not available in the literature. Our goal here is to demonstrate that our CV framework is well suited to develop a cross validated version of this fundamental estimator.

5.1
Background and Related Literature

The literature on SVT is vast. For our purposes here, we will just consider one particular result known for an optimally tuned SVT. We will then develop a CV version of SVT and show that this particular result continues to hold for our CV version of SVT as well. We consider the basic denoising setting where we observe

y=\theta^{*}+\epsilon

where
\theta^{*}
is an underlying
n\times n
signal matrix and
\epsilon
is a
n\times n
noise matrix consisting of i.i.d subgaussian errors with unknown subgaussian norm
\sigma.
Consider the data matrix
y
and consider its singular value decomposition

y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}u_{i}v_{i}^{t}.

Let
S_{\lambda}=\{i:|s_{i}|>\lambda\}
be the set of thresholded singular values of
y
with threshold level
\lambda>0.
Define the estimator

\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}=\sum_{i\in S_{\lambda}}s_{i}u_{i}v_{i}^{t}.

(5.1)

This is how a standard version of the SVT estimator is defined. Under this setting, the following lemma can be traced back at least to Lemma
3
in
Shah et al. 2016
. It is probable that this result is even older. We use the notation
\|M\|_{op}
to denote the operator norm of a
n\times n
matrix
M.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any fixed
\eta>0
, if the threshold
\lambda
is chosen such that
\lambda=(1+\eta)\tau
with
\tau>\|\epsilon\|_{op}
then we have the following inequality:

\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\leq 8(1+\eta)^{2}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\min\{\tau^{2},\sigma_{j}^{2}(\theta^{*})\}\right],

where
\sigma_{j}(\theta^{*})
is the
j
th largest singular value (in absolute value) of
\theta^{*}
.

Remark 5.1
.

The above lemma is purely a deterministic inequality; there is no notion of randomness here.

The following is a standard bound on the maximum singular value of a random subgaussian matrix quoted from
Vershynin 2018
.

Theorem 5.2

(Theorem
4.4.5
from
Vershynin 2018
)
.

Let
\epsilon
be an
n\times n
matrix whose entries are independent mean
0
subgaussian random variables with subgaussian norm at most
\sigma.
Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for any
t>0
, we have

P\left(\|\epsilon\|_{op}\leq C\sigma(\sqrt{n}+t)\right)\geq 1-2\exp(-t^{2}).

Combining Lemma
5.1
and Theorem
5.2
(after plugging in
t=\sqrt{n})
immediately yields the following theorem.

Theorem 5.3
.

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that if the threshold
\lambda
is chosen satisfying
\lambda=C\>\sigma\sqrt{n}
, then the following inequality holds with probability at least
1-2\exp(-n)
,

\frac{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{n^{2}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\min\{n\sigma^{2},\sigma_{j}^{2}(\theta^{*})\}\right]

This theorem reveals the adaptive nature of the SVT estimator. This is because the right hand side is a deterministic quantity which only depends on the true signal
\theta^{*}.
Intuitively, this term can be thought of as describing the spectral complexity of
\theta^{*}.
The above risk bound can be used to derive the rates of convergence of the SVT estimator for several different types of classes of matrices of interest. We mention two standard classes below. For more interesting matrix classes where SVT can be applied; see
Chatterjee 2015
.

1.

Low Rank Matrices
: If
\theta^{*}
has rank
k
, then
\sigma_{j}(\theta^{*})=0
for
j>k
and hence we obtain a bound on the MSE which is
C\frac{k\sigma^{2}}{n}.
It is well known that this is the minimax rate of estimation for the class of
n\times n
matrices of rank
k.

2.

Nonparametric Bradley Terry Matrices
: For a general structured class of matrices, one can typically show by an approximation theoretic argument that the singular values decay at a certain rate, even if they do not become exactly
0
as in the exact low rank case. For example,
Shah et al. 2016
showed that the right hand side in the above theorem scales like
\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}
for the class of
n\times n
Nonparametric Bradley Terry Matrices. These matrices are monotone in both row and column, up to an unknown permutation and arise in modeling of pairwise comparison data; see
Shah et al. 2016
,
Chatterjee and
Mukherjee 2019
.

The important point to note here is that
\lambda
needs to be set proportional to
\sigma\sqrt{n}
for the above theorem to hold. Since
\sigma
is typically unknown and the constant
C
is unspecified it is natural to cross validate over
\lambda.
Therefore, it would be highly desirable for a cross validated version (where the tuning parameter is chosen in a data driven way) of the SVT estimator to also satisfy a risk bound of the form given in Theorem
5.3
. We propose such an estimator in the next section.

5.2
Description of the CVSVT estimator

We will follow our general scheme of defining cross validated estimators as laid out in Section
2.1
. Let
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
be the family of Singular Value thresholding estimators with threshold parameter
\lambda\geq 0
as defined in (
5.1
). We denote the final resulting cross validated SVT estimator as
\widehat{\theta}_{CVSVT}.

1.

Set
K=2
.

2.

Divide
[n]\times[n]
into
I_{1},I_{2}
randomly as follows. Each entry
(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]
belongs to
I_{1}
or
I_{2}
with probability
1/2
independently of other entries.

3.

Let us denote
I_{1}
by
I
and
I_{2}
by
I^{c}.
Define the
n\times n
binary matrix
W
which takes the value
1
on the entries in
I
and
0
elsewhere. Now define
\widetilde{y}(I)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as follows:

\widetilde{y}(I)=2y\circ W

where
\circ
denotes the operation of entrywise multiplication of two matrices of the same size. Similarly, define

\widetilde{y}(I^{c})=2y\circ(1-W).

Thus,
\widetilde{y}(I),\widetilde{y}(I^{c})
are matrices obtained by zeroing out entries of
y
(corresponding to entries in
I
or
I^{c}
) and then doubling it.

Next, we define the completion estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
to be the
SVT estimator applied to the matrix
\widetilde{y}(I)
with threshold
\lambda.
Define
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I^{c})}
similarly using the matrix
\widetilde{y}(I^{c}).
Note that by definition,
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
is a function only of
y_{I}
and hence is a valid completion estimator.

Remark 5.2
.

The main difference with Dyadic CART and Trend Filtering here is in the way we construct the completion estimator. We essentially construct
\widetilde{y}(I)
as an unbiased estimator of
\theta^{*}
by randomly doubling or zeroing out each entry of
y
and then perform SVT on
\widetilde{y}(I)
to create our completion version of the SVT estimator. This idea of randomly zeroing out and inflating other entries to preserve unbiasedness is not new and appears in several matrix completion papers. We call this particular method of creating completion estimators as the zero doubling method. This method is quite generic and can be used for several other signal denoising methods, see Section
6.2
for more on this.

5.3
Main Result

Theorem 5.4
.

Fix any
\delta>0
. There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that if we set our grid
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
for
j=\{1,2\}
, satisfying

2^{N^{*}}>C\left(|\theta^{*}|_{\infty}+\sigma\right)\sqrt{n},

then the following inequality holds with probability atleast
1-2\exp(-n)-\delta
,

\frac{\|\widehat{\theta}_{CVSVT}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{n^{2}}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\min\{n\left(|\theta^{*}|_{\infty}+\sigma\right)^{2},\sigma_{j}^{2}(\theta^{*})\}\right]+\frac{C}{n^{2}}\sigma^{2}\log(N^{*}/\delta).

We now make some remarks about this theorem.

Remark 5.3
.

The above theorem ensures that our CVSVT estimator also enjoys the adaptive risk bound given in Theorem
5.3
with the only difference being that
\sigma
is replaced by the term
|\theta^{*}|_{\infty}+\sigma.
In realistic scenarios, the term
|\theta^{*}|_{\infty}
should remain bounded even if
n
grows. Therefore, our CVSVT estimator essentially inherits all the implications of Theorem
5.3
for various structured subclasses of matrices.

Remark 5.4
.

As mentioned before, setting
N^{*}
so that
2^{N^{*}}>C\left(|\theta^{*}|_{\infty}+\sigma\right)\sqrt{n}
is a mild requirement as it is not usually difficult to set upper bounds on the values of
\sigma
and
|\theta^{*}|_{\infty}
. Note that,
N^{*}
would scale logarithmically in
n
,
\sigma
and
|\theta^{*}|_{\infty}
.

6
Discussion

In this section we discuss some naturally related matters.

6.1
Other Signal Denoising Methods

In this paper, we applied our CV framework to produce CV versions of Trend Filtering, Dyadic CART, Singular Value Thresholding, and Lasso (see Section
13
) with the main focus being on the first two estimators whereas the latter two are considered to further illustrate the generality of the framework. Our CV framework is based on a general principle and should be looked upon as providing a general recipe to develop
theoretically tractable
CV versions of potentially any other estimator that uses a tuning parameter, for example, the Total Variation Denoising estimator proposed by
Rudin
et al. 1992
(also see
Hütter and
Rigollet 2016
,
Sadhanala
et al. 2016
,
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019b
), the Hardy Krauss estimator (see
Fang
et al. 2021
,
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2020
), the Optimal Regression Tree estimator proposed in
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
, a higher dimensional version of Trend Filtering of order
2
proposed in
Ki
et al. 2021
and many more. As a starting point, considering the Zero Doubling method for constructing the completion estimators along with a geometrically doubling grid of candidate tuning values should be useable in these problems.

6.2
Three Different Methods for Creating Completion Estimators

One of the main ingredients of the proposed CV framework is the construction of the completion estimators, where the user needs to build a version of the estimator of interest depending only on a subset of the data, namely
y_{I}.
In this paper, we have considered three different strategies for constructing the completion estimators. In Dyadic CART and Lasso, we restrict the squared error term in the optimization objective to only be summed over the subset
I
, and let us call this method
Restricted Optimization
(RO). In Trend Filtering, we first construct an interpolated data vector
\widetilde{y}(I)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by interpolating on the subset of indices
I^{c}
based on
y_{I}
, and then feed
\widetilde{y}(I)
into the Trend Filtering optimization objective; let us call this method
Interpolate then Optimize
(IO). In Singular Value Thresholding, we zero out entries in
I^{c}
and double the entries in
I
to create a new matrix
\widetilde{y}(I)
, and then use the singular value thresholding operator on
\widetilde{y}(I)
; let us call this method
Zero Doubling
(ZD). To summarize, RO, IO and ZD are three different ways to construct completion estimators, among which the user can try any one or even come up with a different method in some given problem. For example, the ZD method is extremely generic and could have been applied in Trend Filtering or Dyadic CART as well, however, we did not use it because in our numerical experiments we observed that RO (resp. IO) was performing better (in MSE) than ZD for Dyadic CART (resp. Trend Filtering) by a factor of
2
or
3
.

6.3
Comparison with the R Package
Arnold et al. 2022
Cross Validation Version of Trend Filtering

It is instructive to compare the proposed CVTF estimator with the CV version of Trend Filtering implemented in the R package
Arnold et al. 2022
(see the R command
cv.trendfilter
). In particular, it is worth noting the similarities and the differences in the two CV algorithms. Simulations comparing the finite sample performance of both these algorithms is given in Section
7
.

1.

We construct the folds in the same way as in the R package: after deciding the number of folds
K
, the folds are created by placing every
K
th index into the same fold. However, unlike the R package, for
r
th order Trend Filtering we specifically set
K=r+1
to enable our interpolation scheme to create
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})
,
j\in[K]
in such a way that further allows us to obtain the two inequalities stated in Step
3
of the proof sketch in Section
11.1
.

2.

In the R package, the predictions for a given fold
I_{j}
is made by first performing trend filtering on the shortened data vector
y_{I_{j}^{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I_{j}^{c}|}
and then at any point in
I_{j}
, the prediction is given by averaging the fits at its two neighbors (guaranteed to be in a different fold). However, we follow the reverse order by first applying a specific polynomial interpolation scheme on
y_{I_{j}^{c}}
, and then performing trend filtering to this interpolated data vector
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and finally obtain the completion estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}
, based on which our prediction at any point in
I_{j}
is given by the fit
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}
at that point.

3.

The main point of difference of the two methods is how they choose the final data driven value of the tuning parameter
\widehat{\lambda}.
The R package implementation uses (
2.3
) to choose
\widehat{\lambda}
whereas our method is different as has been explained before.

4.

The grid of candidate tuning values
\Lambda_{j}
and
\Lambda
are chosen in a fully data driven way in the R package implementation as explained in Section
6.3.1
below. However, we prefer to simply set
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
for a large enough
N^{*}
such that
2^{N^{*}}=O(n).
We could also mimick the R package implementation in choosing
\Lambda_{j}
and
\Lambda
and this will be perfectly in accordance with our CV framework as also explained in Section
6.3.1
.

6.3.1
An Alternative Way to Construct
\widehat{\theta}_{CVTF}^{(r)}

Recall that the last few steps to construct the CVTF estimator is identical to the corresponding steps to construct the CVDCART estimator. However, unlike Dyadic CART, Trend Filtering is based on convex optimization which brings with it its inherent advantages. For the discussion below let us fix
r=1.
Infact, (see
Hoefling 2010
) for Trend Filtering of order
1
, also known as Fused Lasso, the solution (as a function of
\lambda
) is piecewise constant with a finite number of pieces. Moreover, the entire path of solutions (for all
\lambda>0
) can be computed in
O(n\log n)
time and the number of distinct solutions is always bounded by
O(n).

In the Rpackage, CV for Fused Lasso is implemented by using these facts, and the grid of candidate tuning parameters is simply chosen to be a (random and finite) set of tuning values
\lambda
’s that correspond to the set of all possible solutions.

We can mimick the Rpackage implementation in the last few steps and still stay within our CV framework which gives us a different CV version of Fused Lasso. This is because we can define
\Lambda_{j}
,
j\in[K]=[r+1]=[2]
, to be a finite set of tuning values, one for each of the distinct solutions of the following optimization problem

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})-\theta\right\|^{2}+\lambda n^{r-1}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta\right\|_{1}.

This is allowed in our framework because this set only depends on
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})
by definition. Similarly, we can define
\Lambda
to be a set of tuning values, one for each of the distinct solutions of the full optimization problem

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-\theta\right\|^{2}+\lambda n^{r-1}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta\right\|_{1}.

Under these choices of
\Lambda_{j},\Lambda
, our slow rate and fast rate theorems are still valid because of the following.
In view of Theorem
2.1
, we need to bound
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{\lambda},\theta^{*})
and
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I,r)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
, where
I=I_{j}
for
j\in[K]
. Note that,
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{\lambda},\theta^{*})=\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{\lambda},\theta^{*})
and thus, we can use known bounds for the ideally tuned versions. Bounding
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I,r)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
can be similarly accomplished for this data driven choice of
\lambda_{j}
by again noting that,
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I,r)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})=\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I,r)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
, and then simply following our existing proof. The only point to further consider would be the term involving
\log|\Lambda_{j}|
because now it is random unlike previous. Here, we can invoke the result of
Hoefling 2010
and use a deterministic bound (scaling like
O(n))
on
|\Lambda_{j}|.
Thus, the term involving
\log|\Lambda_{j}|
can be bounded by
O({\log n}/{n})
, a lower order term.

The advantage of this particular variant is that the entire procedure is fully data driven and one does not even need to set the value of
N^{*}
as before. This furnishes a truly completely data driven cross validated Fused Lasso estimator which attains both the slow rate and the fast rate. To the best of our knowledge, such a version of Fused Lasso did not exist in the literature before our work here.

One can also use this approach and choose
\Lambda_{j}
and
\Lambda
similarly, for Trend Filtering of any general order
r\geq 1
. This is because it is known (see Section
6.2
in
Tibshirani and
Taylor 2011
) that the entire path of solutions (for all
\lambda>0
) can again be computed for Trend Filtering of any order
r\geq 1.
Moreover, the solution (as a function of
\lambda
) is piecewise linear and convex with a finite number of knots. Therefore, one can simply take
\Lambda_{j},j\in[K]
and
\Lambda
to be the finite set of knots of the appropriate optimization problems. This would produce fully data driven CV (within our framework) versions of Trend Filtering of general order
r\geq 1
, and to obtain a theoretical guarantee one can then use Theorem
2.1
. The only missing part is that a deterministic bound on
\log|\Lambda_{j}|
is not known for
r>1
, however, from numerical experiments we are led to conjecture that the number of distinct Trend Filtering solutions (of any order, w.r.t
\lambda
) grows at most polynomially with
n
. If this conjecture were true, we can then again conclude that the term involving
\log|\Lambda_{j}|
is of a lower order term. We prefer to write our theorem for the current version because a) we have a complete proof of a risk bound for all orders
r\geq 1
, b) practically, the parameter
N^{*}
is not difficult to set and in our simulations both these versions perform similarly when we set
N^{*}=\log_{2}n.

6.4
Heavy Tailed Errors

The proof of our main result in Theorem
3.1
relies heavily on the errors being subgaussian. It would be interesting to explore the robustness of our CV framework to heavy tailed errors. In particular, can one develop CV algorithms for corresponding quantile versions of Dyadic CART and Trend Filtering (see
Hernan and
Chatterjee 2021
and
Padilla and
Chatterjee 2021
) using our framework? We leave this question for future research.

8
Proof of General Risk Bound for CV Estimator

In this section we will prove Theorem
2.1
.
Recall the definition of the intermediate estimator
\widetilde{\theta}
in (
2.1
). We start by stating the following lemma.

Lemma 8.1
.

The following inequality is true:

\|\widehat{\theta}_{CV}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2\|\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|+\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}-\theta^{*}\|.

Proof.

Recall the definition
\widehat{\lambda}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}-\widetilde{\theta}\|^{2}
and the notation
\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda})}=\widehat{\theta}_{CV}.
We have by triangle inequality,

\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda})}-\theta^{*}\|\leq\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda})}-\widetilde{\theta}\|+\|\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|.

Further, for any fixed
\lambda\in\Lambda
we have

\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda})}-\widetilde{\theta}\|\leq\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}-\widetilde{\theta}\|\leq\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}-\theta^{*}\|+\|\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|

where the first inequality follows from the definition of
\widehat{\lambda}
and the second inequality again follows from the triangle inequality.
∎

We next state a proposition bounding the root squared error of
\widetilde{\theta}.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
be a given family of estimators in the subgaussian sequence model for a tuning parameter
\lambda
ranging in the set
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then the intermediate estimator
\widetilde{\theta}
defined in (
2.1
) satisfies for all
x\geq 0
, with probability not less than
1-2K\exp(-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2})
, the following inequality:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2\sum_{j\in[K]}\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\|\>\>+\>\>4\sqrt{2}\sigma\sum_{j\in[K]}\sqrt{\log|\Lambda_{j}|}+4Kx.

Note that combining Proposition
8.2
with Lemma
8.1
finishes the proof of Theorem
2.1
. Therefore the remaining task is to prove Proposition
8.2
. Towards this end, the first thing to do is to state an oracle inequality for a least squares estimator over a finite parameter space in the subgaussian sequence model. The topic of oracle inequalities for least squares estimators is classical and its origins date back atleast to
Li and Barron 2000
and
Barron
et al. 1999
. The fact that such oracle inequalities hold for least squares is well known; also see Chapter
2
of
Rigollet and
Hütter 2015
. For us, the following version of oracle inequality is the main tool in the proof of Proposition
8.2
.

Lemma 8.3

(Oracle Inequality for Finite Least Squares)
.

Fix any positive integer
m.
Let
y\sim Subg(\theta^{*},\sigma^{2})
where
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.
Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a finite parameter space not necessarily containing
\theta^{*}.
Let
\widehat{\theta}
be the least squares estimator over the finite parameter space
\Theta
, that is,

\widehat{\theta}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\|y-\theta\|^{2}.

Then we have for all
x\geq 0
,

P\big(\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|+4\sqrt{2}\sigma(\sqrt{\log|\Theta|})+4x\big)\geq 1-2\exp(-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}).

Proof.

For any arbitrary
\theta\in\Theta
, we have

\|y-\widehat{\theta}\|^{2}\leq\|y-\theta\|^{2}.

From here, writing
y=\theta^{*}+\epsilon
and expanding the squares one obtains the following pointwise inequality

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\leq\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|^{2}+2\langle\epsilon,\widehat{\theta}-\theta\rangle=\underbrace{\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|^{2}}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{2\langle\epsilon,\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\rangle}_{T_{21}}+\underbrace{2\langle\epsilon,\theta^{*}-\theta\rangle}_{T_{22}}

This leads to the fact that for any
t\geq 0
,

\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|\leq t+\mathbbm{1}\left\{\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|>t\right\}\frac{T_{1}+T_{21}+T_{22}}{\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|}\\
=t+A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3},

(8.1)

where

A_{1}:=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|>t\right\}\frac{T_{1}}{\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|},

A_{2}:=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|>t\right\}\frac{T_{21}}{\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|},

A_{3}:=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|>t\right\}\frac{T_{22}}{\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|}.

Now we bound each of these three terms in the RHS. Note that, since
T_{1}
is non-negative we have

A_{1}\leq\frac{T_{1}}{t}.

The second term

\displaystyle A_{2}\leq\frac{|T_{21}|}{\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|}=2\frac{\left|\left<\epsilon,\>\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\right>\right|}{\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|}\leq 2\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{\left|\left<\epsilon,\>\theta-\theta^{*}\right>\right|}{\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|}.

Now note that the RHS in the above expression is the maximum of a finite number of sub-gaussian random variables (with absolute value) each of subgaussian norm atmost
\sigma.
Therefore, by Lemma
10.11

we have

P\left(\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{\left|\left<\epsilon,\>\theta-\theta^{*}\right>\right|}{\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|}>\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}\log|\Theta|}+x\right)\leq 2\exp(-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2})\>\>\>\forall x\geq 0.

The third term

\displaystyle A_{3}\leq\frac{|T_{22}|}{t}\leq\frac{2}{t}\frac{|\langle\epsilon,\theta^{*}-\theta\rangle|}{\|\theta^{*}-\theta\|}\|\theta^{*}-\theta\|\leq\frac{2}{t}\sqrt{T_{1}}\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{|\langle\epsilon,\theta^{*}-\theta\rangle|}{\|\theta^{*}-\theta\|}.

Plugging these bounds for
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
in (
8.1
) we have the following inequality:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|\leq\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\inf_{t\geq 0}\big[t+\frac{T_{1}}{t}+2(1+\frac{\sqrt{T_{1}}}{t})\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{|\langle\epsilon,\theta^{*}-\theta\rangle|}{\|\theta^{*}-\theta\|}\big].

Now setting
t=\sqrt{T_{1}}
in the R.H.S gives us

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|\leq\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\big[2\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|+4\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{|\langle\epsilon,\theta^{*}-\theta\rangle|}{\|\theta^{*}-\theta\|}\big].

Now plugging in the tail bound for
\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{|\langle\epsilon,\theta^{*}-\theta\rangle|}{\|\theta^{*}-\theta\|}
we obtain for any
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle P\big(\|\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|+4\sqrt{2}\sigma\sqrt{\log|\Theta|}+4x)\geq 1-2\exp(-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}).

∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
8.2
.

Proof of Proposition
8.2
.

By definition of the intermediate estimator
\widetilde{\theta}
as in (
2.1
), we have

\|\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{*}\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\widetilde{\theta}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\|=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda}_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\|.

Therefore, it is enough to bound
\|\widetilde{\theta}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\|
for any fixed
j\in[K].

Fix a
j\in[K].
We will now argue conditionally on the assignment of folds
\{I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{K}\}
and
\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}.
Since the (finite) set of candidate tuning values
\Lambda_{j}
itself can only depend on
\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}
and each of the estimators in the set
\Theta_{j}=\{\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}:\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}\}
are measurable functions of
\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}
we can treat this set
\Theta_{j}
as a fixed finite set of vectors after conditioning on
\{I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{K}\}
and
\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}.
Now recall that

\widehat{\lambda}_{j}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}\left|\left|y_{I_{j}}-\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}\right|\right|^{2}.

Therefore, we can view
\widetilde{\theta}_{I_{j}}=\widehat{\theta}^{(\widehat{\lambda}_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}
as a least squares estimator for
\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}
over this finite set
\Theta_{j}.
Since the coordinates of
\epsilon
are i.i.d and the assignment
\{I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{K}\}
is either deterministic or done using randomization independent of
\epsilon
therefore the conditional distribution of
\epsilon_{I_{j}}
given the assignment
\{I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{K}\}
and
\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}
is same as the unconditional distribution of the random vector
(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{|I_{j}|})
. Therefore, we are exactly in the setting of Lemma
8.3
and we can apply the oracle inequality for finite least squares to obtain a conditional probability statement for all
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle P\big(\|\widetilde{\theta}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\|\leq 2\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\|+4\sqrt{2}\sigma\sqrt{\log|\Lambda_{j}|}+4x\Bigg|I_{1},,\dots,I_{K},\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}\big)\geq

\displaystyle 1-2\exp(-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}).

Since the upper bound on the probability in the R.H.S above does not depend on
\{I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{K}\},\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}
we can drop the conditioning to deduce the unconditional probability statement for all
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle P\big(\|\widetilde{\theta}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\|\leq 2\min_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda_{j},I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\|+4\sqrt{2}\sigma\sqrt{\log|\Lambda_{j}|}+4x\big)\geq 1-2\exp(-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}).

We can now consider a union bound over
j\in[K]
to finish the proof.
∎

9
Computation of CVDCART

In this section, we describe an algorithm to compute a completion version of the Dyadic CART estimator defined as

\widehat{\theta}\coloneqq\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}}||y_{I}-\theta_{I}||^{2}+\lambda k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\theta)

where
I
is any given arbitrary subset of
L_{d,n}
and
\lambda>0.

The first observation to make is the following fact.

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}}\big(||y_{I}-\theta_{I}||^{2}+\lambda k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\theta)\big)=\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n}}\big(\min_{\theta\in S_{\pi}}||y_{I}-\theta_{I}||^{2}+\lambda|\pi|\big)

(9.1)

Take
\widehat{\theta}
, a minimizer of the L.H.S in (
9.1
). Let
\widehat{\pi}
be the minimal recursive dyadic partition correspoding to
\widehat{\theta}.
Then
\widehat{\theta}\in S_{\widehat{\pi}}.
Clearly, L.H.S is at most the R.H.S in (
9.1
) because the R.H.S takes minimum over all recursive dyadic partitions
\pi
and and all
\theta\in S_{\pi}.

Now, consider
\widehat{\pi}
, a minimizer of the R.H.S in (
9.1
). Also, let
\widehat{\theta}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in S_{\widehat{\pi}}}||y_{I}-\theta_{I}||^{2}.
It is clear that
\widehat{\theta}
is a piecewise constant array such that, within every rectangle
R
constituting
\widehat{\pi}
, it takes the mean value of the entries of
y_{I}
within
R
, if
R\cap I\neq\emptyset
; otherwise, it can take any arbitrary value. In particular,

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in S_{\pi}}||y_{I}-\theta_{I}||^{2}+\lambda|\pi|=\sum_{R\in\pi}\mathrm{1}(R\cap I\neq\emptyset)\sum_{u\in R\cap I}(y_{u}-\overline{y}_{R\cap I})^{2}+\lambda|\pi|=Cost(\pi).

Now, note that
k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\widehat{\theta})=|\widehat{\pi}|.
This is because if there exists a recursive dyadic partition
\pi^{\prime}
such that
\widehat{\theta}\in S_{\pi^{\prime}}
and
|\pi^{\prime}|<|\widehat{\pi}|
then this violates the fact that
\widehat{\pi}
minimizes the R.H.S in (
9.1
). Therefore, the R.H.S in (
9.1
) equals
||y_{I}-\widehat{\theta}_{I}||^{2}+\lambda k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\widehat{\theta})
and hence is not smaller than the L.H.S in (
9.1
). This shows the correctness of (
9.1
).

Next, we describe the algorithm to compute
\widehat{\theta}
. It is clear that, we need to compute the minimum:

OPT(L_{d,n}):=\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n}}\min_{\theta\in S_{\pi}}||y_{I}-\theta_{I}||^{2}+\lambda|\pi|=\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n}}Cost(\pi),

and find the optimal partition
\widehat{\pi}
.

Now, for any given rectangle
R\subset L_{d,n}
we can define the minimum of the corresponding subproblem restricted to
R
,

OPT(R):=\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},R}}\min_{\theta\in S_{\pi}(R)}||y_{R\cap I}-\theta_{R\cap I}||^{2}+\lambda|\pi|,

(9.2)

where we are now optimizing only over the class of recursive dyadic partitions of the rectangle
R
denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},R}
and for any such partition
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},R}
, let
S_{\pi}(R)
denote the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{R}
which consists of all arrays which are constant on every rectangle of
\pi
.
Furthermore, we can again write

\min_{\theta\in S_{\pi}(R)}||y_{R\cap I}-\theta_{R\cap I}||^{2}+\lambda|\pi|=\sum_{R^{\prime}\in\pi}\mathrm{1}(R^{\prime}\cap I\neq\emptyset)\sum_{u\in R^{\prime}\cap I}(y_{u}-\overline{y}_{R^{\prime}\cap I})^{2}+\lambda|\pi|,

and let the quantity in the right hand side be denoted as
Cost(\pi;R)
.
Therefore, from (
9.2
), we can write

OPT(R)=\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},R}}Cost(\pi;R).

A key point to note here is that the objective function
Cost(\pi;R)
enjoys an additively separable property. By this, we mean that if we know the first split of the optimal partition
\widehat{\pi}
which minimizes the above problem; then we can separately solve the problem in the resulting two disjoint sub rectangles of
R.

This property implies the following
dynamic programming principle
for computing
OPT(R)
:

OPT(R)=\min_{(R_{1},R_{2})}\left\{OPT(R_{1})+OPT(R_{2}),\quad\mathrm{1}(R\cap I\neq\emptyset)\sum_{u\in R\cap I}(y_{u}-\overline{y}_{R\cap I})^{2}+\lambda\right\}.

In the above,
(R_{1},R_{2})
ranges over all possible
nontrivial
dyadic splits of
R
into two disjoint rectangles. The minimizer of the above problem lets us obtain the optimal first split of
R
, which we denote by
SPLIT(R)
.

Now it is important to make some observations. For any rectangle
R
, the number of non trivial dyadic splits possible is at most
d
, one for each dimension. Any split of
R
creates two disjoint sub rectangles
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
. Suppose we know
OPT(R_{1})
and
OPT(R_{2})
for
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
arising out of each possible split. Then, to compute
SPLIT(R)
we have to compute the minimum of the sum of
OPT(R_{1})
and
OPT(R_{2})
for each possible split as well as the number
\mathrm{1}(R\cap I\neq\emptyset)\sum_{u\in R\cap I}(y_{u}-\overline{y}_{R\cap I})^{2}
, which corresponds to not splitting
R
at all. Thus, we need to compute the minimum of at most
d+1
numbers.

From Lemma
10.10
, the number of distinct dyadic rectangles of
L_{d,n}
is at most
2^{d}N
. Any rectangle
R
has dimension
n_{1}\times n_{2}\times\cdots\times n_{d}
. Let us denote the number
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d}
by
Size(R)
. Now we are ready to describe the main scheme of computing the optimal partition.

For each rectangle
R
, the goal is to store
SPLIT(R)
and
OPT(R)
. We do this inductively on
Size(R)
. We will make a single pass/visit through all distinct rectangles
R\subset L_{d,n}
, in increasing order of
Size(R)
. Thus, we will first start with all
1\times 1\times\cdots\times 1
rectangles of size equals
d
. Then we visit rectangles of size
d+1
,
d+2
all the way to
nd
. Fixing the size, we can choose some arbitrary order in which we visit the rectangles.

For
1\times 1\times\cdots\times 1
rectangles, computing
SPLIT(R)
and
OPT(R)
is trivial. Consider a generic step where we are visiting some rectangle
R
. Note that we have already computed
OPT(R^{\prime})
for all rectangles
R^{\prime}
with
Size(R^{\prime})<Size(R)
. Since a possible split of
R
generates two rectangles
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
of strictly smaller size, to compute
OPT(R_{1})+OPT(R_{2})
we just need to sum two previously computed numbers and store it. We do this for each possible split to get a list of at most
d
numbers. Moreover, we also compute
\mathrm{1}(R\cap I\neq\emptyset)\sum_{u\in R\cap I}(y_{u}-\overline{y}_{R\cap I})^{2}
(described later) and add this number to the list. Finally, we take the minimum of these
d+1
numbers. In this way, we obtain
OPT(R)
and
SPLIT(R)
.

The number of basic operations needed per rectangle here is
O(d)
. Since there are at most
2^{d}N
many rectangles in all, the total computational complexity of the overall inductive scheme scales like
O(2^{d}dN)
.

To compute
\mathrm{1}(R\cap I\neq\emptyset)\sum_{u\in R\cap I}(y_{u}-\overline{y}_{R\cap I})^{2}
for every rectangle
R
, we can again induct on size in increasing order. Define
SUM(R)
to be the sum of entries of
y_{R\cap I}
and
SUMSQ(R)
to be the sum of squares of entries of
y_{R\cap I}
. One can easily keep storing
SUM(R)
and
SUMSQ(R)
in a similar bottom up fashion, visiting rectangles in increasing order of
Size(R)
. This requires constant number of basic operations per rectangle
R
. Once we have computed
SUM(R)
and
SUMSQ(R)
, we can then calculate
\mathrm{1}(R\cap I\neq\emptyset)\sum_{u\in R\cap I}(y_{u}-\overline{y}_{R\cap I})^{2}
. Thus, this inductive scheme requires lower order computation.

Once we finish the above inductive scheme, we have stored
SPLIT(R)
for every rectangle
R
. We can now start going topdown, starting from the biggest rectangle which is
L_{d,n}
itself. We can recreate the full optimal partition by using
SPLIT(R)
to split the rectangles at every step. Once the full optimal partition
\widehat{\pi}
is obtained, computing
\widehat{\theta}
just involves computing the mean value of the entries of
y_{I}
within the rectangles. If a rectangle does not contain any entry of
y_{I}
, we simply compute the mean value of the entries of
y_{I}
, i.e.,
\overline{y}_{I}
. It can be checked that this step requires lower order computation as well.

10
Proofs for Dyadic CART

The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem
3.3
.

10.1
Sketch of Proof of Theorem
3.3

For the convenience of the reader, we first present a sketch of proof of Theorem
3.3
. This sketch is divided into several steps and is meant to convey the essential aspects of our proof strategy.

Let
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
denote the usual Dyadic CART estimator based on the full data array
y
as defined in (
3.1
).
In view of Theorem
2.1
, it is enough to bound
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})
and
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
, where
I=I_{1}
or
I_{2}
.

The existing bound in Theorem
3.1
gives the desired upper bound on
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})
as long as
\lambda
is chosen to be not smaller than
C\sigma^{2}\log N
where
C
is some absolute constant. Since
2^{N^{*}}
is assumed to be strictly larger than
C\sigma^{2}\log N
, therefore
\Lambda
contains this good choice
C\sigma^{2}\log N
of the tuning parameter. In particular, by construction of
\Lambda
, there exists a
\lambda^{*}\in\Lambda
satisfying
C\sigma^{2}\log n<\lambda^{*}\leq 2C\sigma^{2}\log n.
For our purpose, this
\lambda^{*}
can be thought of as a sufficiently good choice of the tuning parameter.

Clearly,
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})\leq SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*})},\theta^{*}).
We can now use the existing bound in Theorem
3.1
to bound
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*})},\theta^{*})
for the usual Dyadic CART estimator by generalizing the proof of Theorem
3.1
given in
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
to general subgaussian errors. Therefore, the desired bound for
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})
follow more or less directly from existing results.

The main new task for us here is bounding
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
where
I=I_{1}
or
I=I_{2}.
Again, since
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})\leq SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
it is sufficient for us to bound
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
which in turn is trivially upper bounded by
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)},\theta^{*})
.

We now outline the main steps in our proof which bounds
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)},\theta^{*})
.
Before that we say a few words about notation at this point. In this proof sketch, we abuse notation and write
A\lesssim B
, which basically means
A\leq B
after ignoring multiplicative and additive logarithmic factors. This is done to increase readability and interpretability of our bounds within this proof sketch. Also, we write
A(\leq^{+})B
to mean that
A\leq\max\{B,0\}.

1.

Preliminary Localizations

We first establish a few preliminary localization properties of the completion estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}.
Let us introduce a few notations at this point. Let us denote
L^{*}=V(\theta^{*})+\sqrt{\log N}
where we recall that
V(\theta^{*})=\max_{u,v\in L_{d,n}}|\theta^{*}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{v}|.
For realistic signals we would expect
V(\theta^{*})=O(1)
and not grow with
n
to
\infty.
Therefore, the reader can think of
L^{*}
as a
\widetilde{O}(1)
term, growing at most logarithmically.

Now define the events

\begin{split}A_{1}&=\left\{|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}|_{\infty}\lesssim L^{*}\right\},\\
A_{2}&=\left\{||\theta^{*}_{I}-\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I}||^{2}+\lambda^{*}k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)})\lesssim R(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})\right\}.\end{split}

(10.1)

We first show that both the events
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
hold with high probability. This is formalized in Proposition
10.1
. In the rest of the steps, we will assume that the events
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
hold.

2.

Total Error = In Sample Error + Out of Sample Error

We need to bound
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)},\theta^{*})
; let’s term this as the total error. We now decompose the total error as twice the in sample error plus the difference of out of sample and in sample errors where by
out of sample error
we mean
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
and by
in sample error
we mean
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I},\theta^{*}_{I})
.

\displaystyle SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)},\theta^{*})=SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I},\theta^{*}_{I})+SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})=

\displaystyle 2SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I},\theta^{*}_{I})+SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})-SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I},\theta^{*}_{I}).

It would be convenient if we further rewrite the earlier display after division by the square root of
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)},\theta^{*})
to obtain

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|

\displaystyle=2\frac{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}\|^{2}}{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|}+\frac{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I^{c})}_{I^{c}}-\theta^{*}_{I^{c}}\|^{2}-\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}\|^{2}}{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{2\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}\|}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{\frac{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I^{c})}_{I^{c}}-\theta^{*}_{I^{c}}\|^{2}-\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}\|^{2}}{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|}}_{T_{2}}.

(10.2)

Since the in sample error term
T_{1}
has the desired oracle risk bound (under the event
A_{2}
) from Step
1
itself, our focus henceforth is on bounding the normalized difference of out of sample and in sample errors which is the term
T_{2}.

3.

Reduction to Bounding Rademacher Averages

To bound
T_{2}
, we express the numerator in
T_{2}
as a Rademacher average.

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I^{c}}-\theta^{*}_{I^{c}}\|^{2}-\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}\|^{2}=\sum_{u\in L_{d,n}}\eta_{u}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})^{2},

where
\eta_{u}=\mathrm{1}(u\in I^{c})-\mathrm{1}(u\in I)
are i.i.d
\pm 1
rademacher random variables (same as the entries of
W
or
-W
depending on whether
I=I_{1}
or
I=I_{2}.
). This is the step where we really use the fact that our folds are random and chosen in a uniformly i.i.d manner. The above display gives

T_{2}=\sum_{u\in L_{n,d}}\frac{\eta_{u}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})^{2}}{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|}.

4.

Peeling Step

At this point we are still not quite ready to bound the R.H.S in the last display in Step
3
, primarily because of the presence of the square in the exponent of
(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u}).
If there were no square,
T_{2}
would have been in ”standard” form and could have been directly bounded by a Rademacher complexity term which in turn could have been bounded by standard techniques as laid out in the proof of Theorem
2.1
in
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
. Henceforth, our effort will be to ”drop the square”. This will require us to use standard techniques from the theory of bounding maxima of stochastic processes, carefully and in appropriate order.

We first implement the so called peeling step where we peel on the value of
\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|.

\displaystyle T_{2}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{u\in L_{n,d}}\frac{\eta_{u}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})^{2}}{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|}\mathrm{1}\left(2^{l}<\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1}\right)(\leq^{+})

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}\underbrace{\frac{1}{2^{l}}\sum_{u\in L_{d,n}}\eta_{u}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})^{2}\mathrm{1}\left(2^{l}<\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1}\right)}_{T_{2,l}}.

Since the event
A_{1}
holds,
|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}|_{\infty}\lesssim L^{*}
and hence
\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\lesssim N(L^{*})^{2}.
Therefore, in the above sum,
l
only needs to go up to
L\lesssim\log\left(N(L^{*})^{2}\right)=\widetilde{O}(1).

5.

Reduction to Bounding Suprema

In view of the last display in the last step, we need to bound
T_{2,l}
for each
l
from
1
to
L=\widetilde{O}(1)
. At this point, we ”sup out” the random variable
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}
using the high probability localization properties established in Step
1.
Specifically, for any fixed
l\in[L]
we can write

\displaystyle T_{2,l}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{l}}\sum_{u\in L_{d,n}}\eta_{u}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})^{2}\mathrm{1}\Bigg(2^{l}<\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1},k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)})\lesssim\frac{R(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})}{\lambda^{*}},

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}-\theta^{*}|_{\infty}\lesssim L^{*}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq 2L^{*}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n},\\
|\pi|\lesssim\frac{R(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})}{\lambda^{*}}\end{subarray}}\>\frac{1}{2^{l}}\>\underbrace{\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in S_{\pi}:2^{l}<\|v-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1},\\
|v-\theta^{*}|_{\infty}\lesssim L^{*}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2L^{*}}\sum_{u\in L_{d,n}}\eta_{u}(v_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})^{2}}_{X_{\pi,l}}.

where the first equality follows because the events
A_{1},A_{2}
hold. Here
S_{\pi}
refers to the subspace of arrays in
\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}
which are piecewise constant on each of the rectangles of
\pi.

6.

Contraction Principle for Rademacher Averages

At this point we can indeed ”drop the square” by using the so-called contraction principle for Rademacher averages, see Theorem
2.2
in
Koltchinskii 2011
. This is allowed because the square function is Lipschitz in
[-L^{*},L^{*}]
with lipschitz constant
2L^{*}.
Note that we can localize to
[-L^{*},L^{*}]
precisely because of the
\ell_{\infty}
localization property we established in Step
1.
In particular, an in probability version of the contraction principle allows us to conclude that the random variable
X_{\pi,l}
(defined in the last display in Step
5
) is stochastically upper bounded by the random variable
Y_{\pi,l}
which is defined as

Y_{\pi,l}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in S_{\pi}:\|v-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1},\\
|v-\theta^{*}|_{\infty}\leq L^{*}\end{subarray}}\sum_{u\in{L_{d,n}}}\eta_{u}(v_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u}).

Note that there is no square in the exponent of
v-\theta^{*}
in
Y_{\pi,l}
anymore.

This in turn essentially implies that we can write

T_{2,l}\leq 2L^{*}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n},\\
|\pi|\leq\frac{R(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})}{\lambda^{*}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2^{l}}Y_{\pi,l}.

Note that there is no square in the exponent of
v-\theta^{*}
in
Y_{\pi,l}
anymore. So we have succesfully dropped the square while going from
X_{\pi,l}
to
Y_{\pi,l}.
This entire step is carried out within the proof of Proposition
10.2
.

7.

Bounding Suprema

The final step is to observe that the right hand side of the last display is a Rademacher complexity term in the ”standard form”. Moreover, the
\frac{1}{2^{l}}
factor effectively cancels out with the
2^{l+1}
radius of the
\ell_{2}
ball over which we are taking the supremum in the term
Y_{\pi,l}.
Hence, we can give a bound (for any
l\in[L]
) by essentially similar proof techniques as laid out in the proof of Theorem
2.1
in
Chatterjee and
Goswami 2019a
. This is carried out by using the so called basic inequality and applying standard bounds on suprema of maxima of subgaussian random variables along with basic estimates of the cardinality of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n}.
This yields a bound

T_{2,l}\lesssim L^{*}\sqrt{\frac{R(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})}{\lambda^{*}}}

which holds with high probability. Since there are only
\widetilde{O}(1)
many
l
’s which need to accounted for, a simple union bound applied to the above display then furnishes with high probability

T_{2}\lesssim L^{*}\sqrt{\frac{R(\theta^{*},\lambda^{*})}{\lambda^{*}}}.

This display gives us the desired bound for
T_{2}
and thus finishes the proof.

10.2
Detailed Proof of Theorem
3.3

We now give the detailed proof.
Throughout the proofs in this section, we will use
C,C_{1},C_{2}
to denote constants which may only depend on the dimension
d
but not the sample size
N
, the true signal
\theta^{*}
or the distribution of the errors. Also, the precise values of these constants may change from line to line. Generically, we will denote any partition in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n}
by
\pi.
We will then denote by
S_{\pi}
the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}
consisting of all arrays which are constant on every subrectangle of
\pi.

The proof of Theorem
3.3
is organized in the following way. First, we state two propositions which are crucial ingredients in this proof. After stating these two propositions, we finish the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Proposition 10.1
.

Let
I
denote any fixed subset of
L_{d,n}.
Recall that the estimator
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
is defined as

\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}\coloneqq\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}}||y_{I}-\theta_{I}||^{2}+\lambda k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\theta).

There exists an absolute constant
C
such that if
\lambda\geq C\sigma^{2}\log N
then for any
\alpha>1
,

P(A_{1}\cap A_{2})\geq 1-CN^{-C\alpha},

where the events
A_{1},A_{2}
are defined as follows:

\begin{split}A_{1}&=\left\{|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}|_{\infty}\leq V(\theta^{*})+(2+\sqrt{\alpha})\sigma\sqrt{\log N}\right\},\\
A_{2}&=\left\{||\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}||^{2}+\lambda k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)})\leq R(\theta^{*},\lambda)+C\alpha\sigma^{2}\log N\right\}\end{split}

(10.3)

Since, the probability lower bound
1-CN^{-C\alpha}
does not depend on the subset
I
, the same conclusion is true even if
I
is now a random subset (chosen independently of
\epsilon
), such as the random folds
I_{1}
or
I_{2}.

Proposition 10.2
.

Suppose
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d,n}}
is a random array consisting of i.i.d Rademacher random variables. For any integer
l\geq 0
and
L,M>0
, define the random variable

\displaystyle T_{l}:=\sum_{u\in L_{d,n}}\frac{\eta_{u}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})^{2}}{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|}\mathrm{1}\Bigg(2^{l}<\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1},

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)})\leq M,

\displaystyle|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}|_{\infty}\leq L\Bigg).

Then there exists absolute positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for any
\alpha\geq 1
,

P\left(T_{l}\geq(C_{1}\sqrt{\alpha}+C_{2})L\sqrt{M\log N}\right)\leq N^{-\alpha}.

Now we will finish the proof of Theorem
3.3
assuming these propositions hold.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Let
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)}
denote the usual Dyadic CART estimator based on the full data array
y
as defined in (
3.1
).
In view of Theorem
2.1
, it is enough to bound
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})
and
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)},\theta^{*})
, where
I=I_{1}
or
I_{2}
.

We can use the existing bound in Theorem
3.1
to bound
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})
for the usual Dyadic CART estimator (under a Gaussian assumption on the errors). In fact, Proposition
10.1
generalizes Theorem
3.1
by giving bounds for a completion version of Dyadic CART (and for general subgaussian errors). By setting
I
equal to the entire set
L_{d,n}
, it is seen that Theorem
3.1
is a special case of Proposition
10.1
. Thus, Proposition
10.1
itself also implies that if
\lambda\geq C\sigma^{2}\log N
then for any
\alpha>1
, there exists an absolute constant
C
such that with probability at least
1-CN^{-C\alpha}
, we have

SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})\leq R(\theta^{*},\lambda)+C\alpha\sigma^{2}\log N.

Our main goal now is to bound
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)},\theta^{*})
, for
I=I_{1}
or
I_{2}
, under the choice of
\lambda\geq C\sigma^{2}\log N.
Consider the events
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
as defined in (
10.3
). Furthermore, define the event

A_{3}:=\{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|>1\}.
In the calculation below we will assume that the event
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap A_{3}
holds because
A_{1}\cap A_{2}
hold with high probability and on
A_{3}^{c}
we anyway have
\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 1
.

We first write

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}\|^{2}+\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I^{c}}-\theta^{*}_{I^{c}}\|^{2}-\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}\|^{2}+\sum_{u\in L_{d,n}}\eta_{u}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})^{2}

where the array
\eta\coloneqq\eta_{u}=\mathrm{1}(u\in I^{c})-\mathrm{1}(u\in I)
consists of i.i.d
\pm 1
rademacher random variables (same as
W
or
-W
depending on whether
I=I_{1}
or
I=I_{2}.
).

Dividing both sides by
\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|
(nonzero on
A_{3}
) we can write

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|

\displaystyle=2\frac{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}\|^{2}}{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|}+\sum_{u\in L_{n,d}}\frac{\eta_{u}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})^{2}}{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{2\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I}-\theta^{*}_{I}\|}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{\sum_{u\in L_{n,d}}\frac{\eta_{u}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})^{2}}{\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|}}_{T_{2}}.

(10.4)

Now on the event
A_{2}
we have

T_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{R(\theta^{*},\lambda)+C\alpha\sigma^{2}\log N}.

(10.5)

Next, we proceed to bound
T_{2}.

The event
A_{1}
in particular implies that
\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}\leq N(L^{*})^{2}
where we denote

L^{*}=V(\theta^{*})+(2+\sqrt{\alpha})\sigma\sqrt{\log N}.

Therefore, on the event
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap A_{3}
, we can write
T_{2}=\sum_{l=0}^{\log_{2}(L^{*}\sqrt{N})\>-\>1}T_{2,l}
, where

\displaystyle T_{2,l}:=T_{2}\;\mathrm{1}\Bigg(2^{l}<\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1},k_{\mathrm{rdp}}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}(R(\theta^{*},\lambda)+C\alpha\sigma^{2}\log N),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}|_{\infty}\leq L^{*}\Bigg).

To bound
T_{2}

Lemma 10.4
.

Let
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random vector with independent sub-Gaussian entries with mean
0
and sub-Gaussian norm
\sigma^{2}
,
S
be a subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any fixed vector. Then there exist absolute constants
c
and
C
such that for any
u>0
, with probability at least
1-2e^{-Cu}
we have

\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in S\\
v\neq\theta\end{subarray}}\left<\frac{v-\theta}{||v-\theta||},Z\right>\right)^{2}\leq c\sigma^{2}\big(Dim(S)+1\big)+4\sigma^{2}u.

Proof.

Let
P_{S}
be the orthogonal projection matrix on to the subspace
S
and
P_{S^{\prime}}
be the orthogonal projection matrix on to the subspace
S^{\prime}
, where
S^{\prime}
is the one dimensional subspace spanned by the vector
(I-P_{S})\theta

Then we can write,

\displaystyle\left|\sup_{v\in S,v\neq\theta}\left<\frac{v-\theta}{||v-\theta||},Z\right>\right|=\left|\sup_{v\in S,v\neq\theta}\left<\frac{v-P_{S}\theta-(I-P_{S})\theta}{\sqrt{||v-P_{S}\theta||^{2}+||(I-P_{S})\theta||^{2}}},Z\right>\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sup_{v\in S,v\neq\theta}\left<\frac{v-P_{S}\theta}{\sqrt{||v-P_{S}\theta||^{2}+||(I-P_{S})\theta||^{2}}},Z\right>\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left|\sup_{v\in S,v\neq\theta}\left<\frac{(I-P_{S})\theta}{\sqrt{||v-P_{S}\theta||^{2}+||(I-P_{S})\theta||^{2}}},Z\right>\right|

(10.10)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sup_{v\in S,||v||\leq 1}\left<Z,v\right>\right|+\left|\sup_{v\in S^{\prime},||v||\leq 1}\left<Z,v\right>\right|,

Note that, for any subspace
S
,

\sup_{v\in S,||v||\leq 1}\left<Z,v\right>=||P_{S}Z||.

Therefore, from (
10.10
),

\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in S\\
v\neq\theta\end{subarray}}\left<\frac{v-\theta}{||v-\theta||},Z\right>\right)^{2}\leq 2||P_{S}Z||^{2}+2||P_{S^{\prime}}Z||^{2}=2Z^{T}P_{S}Z+2Z^{T}P_{S^{\prime}}Z.

Now we will give a high probability bound on both of the above terms separately.
From the Hanson-Wright concentration inequality, see Theorem 6.2.1 in
Vershynin 2018
, we obtain that, for any matrix
A_{m\times n}
and

P\left(|Z^{T}AZ-\mathbb{E}(Z^{T}AZ)|>u\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-C\min\left\{\frac{u}{\sigma^{2}||A||_{OP}},\frac{u^{2}}{\sigma^{4}\|A\|_{F}^{2}}\right\}\right),

(10.12)

for some

It can be checked that if

the first term inside the brackets in the R.H.S above dominates.

Now letting

, we have
\mathbb{E}(Z^{T}AZ)=trace(P_{S}\Sigma)
, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z^{T}AZ)=trace(P_{S}\Sigma)

\displaystyle\leq trace(P_{S})\;\max_{i\in[n]}\Sigma_{ii}\leq c\sigma^{2}Rank(P_{S})=c\sigma^{2}Dim(S)

for some absolute constant

. Similarly by letting

since

. Therefore, by (

) and the arguments outlined thereafter, we can actually assert that for any

\displaystyle\leq c\sigma^{2}Dim(S)+u

\displaystyle Z^{T}P_{S^{\prime}}Z

\displaystyle\leq c\sigma^{2}+u

with probability at least

. Finally, by using (

Handel 2014
, which is useful to provide a probability tail bound for
X_{\pi,l}
.

Lemma 10.7

(Panchenko’s Lemma)
.

Suppose
X,Y
are real valued random variables such that
\mathbb{E}F(X)\leq\mathbb{E}F(Y)
for every non decreasing convex function
F:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.
Suppose also the following inequality is true for some
a>0,c>0
and every
u>0
,

P(Y\geq a+u)\leq\exp(-cu^{2}).

Then the following inequality is also true for all
u>1
,

P(X\geq a+u)\leq\exp(-cu^{2}/4).

Proof.

Take the non decreasing convex function
F(x)=(x-t)_{+}
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}.
Note that
\mathbb{E}F(X)=\mathbb{E}(X-t)_{+}=\int_{t}^{\infty}P(X\geq s)\>ds.
We now obtain for any
t\geq a
,

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}P(X\geq s)\>ds\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}P(Y\geq s)\>ds\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}\exp(-c(s-a)^{2})ds=

\displaystyle\int_{t-a}^{\infty}\exp(-cu^{2})du=P(N(0,\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{2c})\geq t-a)\leq\exp(-c(t-a)^{2}).

where the first two inequalities follow due to our assumptions and the last inequality follows by the standard Mills ratio upper bound to Gaussian tails.

Theefore, this implies that

\displaystyle P(X\geq a+u)\leq\frac{1}{u-u^{\prime}}\int_{a+u^{\prime}}^{a+u}P(X\geq s)ds\leq\frac{1}{u-u^{\prime}}\int_{a+u^{\prime}}^{\infty}P(X\geq s)ds\leq\frac{1}{u-u^{\prime}}\exp(-cu^{\prime 2}).

Setting
u^{\prime}=u/2
we get

\displaystyle P(X\geq a+u)\leq\frac{2}{u}\exp(-c\frac{u^{2}}{4})\leq 2\exp(-c\frac{u^{2}}{4})

where the last inequality is true because
u>1.

∎

Finally, we have the following result that provides a probability tail bound for
X_{\pi,l}
.

Lemma 10.8
.

For all
u\geq 1
, we have the following probability tail bound

P\left(\frac{1}{2}X_{\pi,l}\geq\mathbb{E}Y_{\pi,l}+u\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{32\cdot 2^{2l}}\right).

Proof.

First, by Lemma
10.5
, we obtain the following concentration inequality for
Y_{\pi,l}
, that for every
u>0
,

P\left(Y_{\pi,l}\geq\mathbb{E}Y_{\pi,l}+u\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{8\cdot 2^{2l}}\right).

Next, by Lemma
10.6
, we obtain the fact that
X_{\pi,l}
is stochastically dominated by
Y_{\pi,l}
.
Therefore, we can use Panchenko’s lemma; see Lemma
10.7
, to conclude that for all
u\geq 1
,

P\left(\frac{1}{2}X_{\pi,l}\geq\mathbb{E}Y_{\pi,l}+u\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{32\cdot 2^{2l}}\right).

(10.15)

∎

Now, in view of Lemma
10.8
, we need to provide an upper bound of
\mathbb{E}Y_{\pi,l}
, which is done in the following lemma.

Lemma 10.9
.

For any
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{rdp},d,n}
with
Dim(S_{\pi})\leq M
we have

\mathbb{E}Y_{\pi,l}\leq C2^{l}\sqrt{M},

for some
C>0
.

Proof.

Recall that

Y_{\pi,l}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in S_{\pi}:\|v-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1},\\
|v-\theta^{*}|_{\infty}\leq L\end{subarray}}\sum_{u\in{L_{d,n}}}\eta_{u}(v_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u}).

We can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Y_{\pi,l}\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S_{\pi}:\|v-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1}}\sum_{u\in{L_{d,n}}}\eta_{u}(v_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})\leq\sqrt{\pi/2}\>\>\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S_{\pi}:\|v-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1}}\sum_{u\in{L_{d,n}}}Z_{u}(v_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})

where
Z
is an array consisting of i.i.d
N(0,1)
entries. In the last display we bounded the rademacher complexity by gaussian complexity which is a well known result; see Page
132
in
Wainwright 2019
. Now note that we can write

\displaystyle\quad\;\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S_{\pi}:\|v-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1}}\sum_{u\in{L_{d,n}}}Z_{u}(v_{u}-\theta^{*}_{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S_{\pi}:\|v-\theta^{*}\|\leq 2^{l+1}}\sum_{u\in{L_{d,n}}}Z_{u}(v_{u}-(O_{\pi}\theta^{*})_{u})+\mathbb{E}\sum_{u\in{L_{d,n}}}Z_{u}(\theta^{*}_{u}-(O_{\pi}\theta^{*})_{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S_{\pi}:\|v\|\leq 2^{l+1}}\sum_{u\in{L_{d,n}}}Z_{u}v_{u}=2^{l+1}\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in S_{\pi}:\|v\|\leq 1}\sum_{u\in{L_{d,n}}}Z_{u}v_{u}

\displaystyle=2^{l+1}\sqrt{Dim(S_{\pi})}\leq 2^{l+1}\sqrt{M}.

In the display above,
O_{\pi}
refers to the orthogonal projection matrix for the subspace
S_{\pi}
and the last equality follows from standard facts about projection of a standard gaussian vector onto a subspace.
∎

10.5
Auxiliary Lemmas

Lemma 10.10
.

The number of dyadic rectangles of
L_{d,n}
is at most
2^{d}N
.

Proof.

Any dyadic rectangle must have each side length dyadic or equivalently, it is a product of dyadic rectangles. In any dimension, the number of dyadic intervals is at most
2n
which then furnishes the lemma as
(2n)^{d}=2^{d}N.

In one dimension, we can count the number of dyadic intervals as follows. Start bottom up and there are at most
n
singletons which are dyadic intervals of length
1.
Then there are at most
n/2
dyadic intervals of length
2
and so on. Hence the total number of dyadic intervals is at most
n+n/2+n/4+\dots\leq 2n.

∎

The following result is a standard tail inequality for a maximum of finitely many subgaussian random variables; also see Lemma
5.2
in
van
Handel 2014
.

Lemma 10.11
.

Let
Z_{i}
be a sub-Gaussian random variable with sub-Gaussian norm
\sigma_{i}^{2}
, where
i\in[n]
. Then for all
x\geq 0
,

P\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|Z_{i}|>\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}\log n}+x\right)\leq 2\exp(-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}),

where
\sigma^{2}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\sigma_{i}^{2}
.

Proof.

We have, for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle P\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|Z_{i}|>t\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{|Z_{i}|>t\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(|Z_{i}|>t)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\exp(-t^{2}/2\sigma_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\exp(-t^{2}/2\sigma^{2})=2n\exp(-t^{2}/2\sigma^{2}),

and the proof follows.
∎

11
Proofs for Trend Filtering

11.1
Sketch of Trend Filtering Proofs

For the convenience of the reader, we first present a sketch of proof of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
.

Fix
r\geq 1.
Let
\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{\lambda}
be the usual
r
th order Trend Filtering estimators based on the full data as defined in (
4.1
). Also, to reduce notational clutter let us denote the completion estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I,r)}
by simply
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
where
I=I_{j}
for
j\in[K]=[r+1].

In view of Theorem
2.1
, it suffices to bound two main quantities:
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{\lambda},\theta^{*})
and
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
.
State of the art existing bounds for penalized Trend Filtering are available in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
. In particular, Theorem
1.1
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
gives the desired upper bounds (both slow and fast rates) on
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}_{\lambda},\theta^{*})
as long as
\lambda
is chosen appropriately depending on the unknown
\sigma
and some properties of
\theta^{*}.
We choose
\Lambda_{j}=\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
for all
j\in[K]
and assume that
2^{N^{*}}
exceeds these theoretically optimal choices of
\lambda.
Therefore, by construction of
\Lambda
, there exists a
\lambda^{*}\in\Lambda
which (up to a factor of
2
) scales like the theoretically recommended choice in Theorem
1.1
.

Clearly,
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})\leq SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*})},\theta^{*}).
We can now use the existing bound given by Theorem
1.1
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
to bound
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*})},\theta^{*})
for the usual Trend Filtering estimator by generalizing its proof to hold for general subgaussian errors. Therefore, the desired bound for
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})
again follows pretty much directly from the existing result Theorem
1.1
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
.

As in the Dyadic CART proof, the main new task for us here is to bound the quantity:
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
, where
I=I_{j}
for
j\in[K].
For each
j\in[r+1]
our eventual bound will be the same and hence our final bound on the MSE of the CVTF estimator will merely be
(r+1)
times the bound for
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j}}SSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I^{c})}_{I},\theta^{*}_{I}\right)
when
I=I_{1}.
Therefore, we can just consider
I=I_{1}
below.

Again, since
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})\leq SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
it is sufficient for us to bound
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)}_{I^{c}},\theta^{*}_{I^{c}})
which in turn is trivially upper bounded by
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)},\theta^{*})
.

We now outline the main steps in our proof which bounds
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda^{*},I)},\theta^{*})
.

1.

KKT Condition:

Recall the definition of
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})
in the description of the CVTF estimator (see Step
3
in Section
4.2
).
Define

\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c}):=E\left[\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})\right],\ \text{and}\ \ \widetilde{\epsilon}(I_{j}^{c}):=\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})-\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c}).

Since
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})=\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c})+\widetilde{\epsilon}(I_{j}^{c})
, we can think of
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})
as a noisy version of
\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c}).
Therefore,
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}
, being the trend filtering estimator applied to
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})
should estimate the mean of
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})
, which is
\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c})
, very well. This suggests decomposing
SSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})},\theta^{*}\right)
into a sum of two errors; one is
SSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})},\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c})\right)
and the other is
SSE\left(\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c}),\theta^{*}\right)
. The first term is simply the usual mean squared error of the usual Trend Filtering estimator and the second is a non-stochastic approximation error term. This decomposition is enabled for us once we write down the KKT conditions for the Trend Filtering convex opptimization objective (see Lemma
11.3
). This yields

\displaystyle SSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})},\theta^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{2\left<\widetilde{\epsilon}(I_{j}^{c}),\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta^{*}\right>+2\lambda n^{r-1}\left(\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\|_{1}-\|D^{(r)}\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}\|_{1}\right)}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{\left\|\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c})\right\|^{2}}_{T_{2}}.

Here the term
T_{1}
can be thought of as a further upper bound to
SSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})},\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c})\right).

We now bound the two terms
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
separately.

2.

Bounding
T_{1}
by suitably modifying arguments in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
:

Note that
\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is linearly extrapolated from the vector
y_{I_{j}^{c}}.
Therefore, there exist matrices
H^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n-|I_{j}|}
such that

\widetilde{y}(I_{j}^{c})=H^{j}y_{I_{j}^{c}}\>\>\>\>j\in[K].

The exact form of these matrices
H^{j}
is explicitly described later in the detailed proof.
Now, a similar term to
T_{1}
appears in the analysis of SSE of the trend filtering estimator
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
. So we could apply the entire proof machinery developed
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
to bound
T_{1}.
The only potential problem is that while
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
considered the case where the error vector is i.i.d gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}
, the error vector here is
\widetilde{\epsilon}(I_{j}^{c})=H^{j}\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}.
Thus, in our setting
\widetilde{\epsilon}(I_{j}^{c})
is a linear transformation of a vector of i.i.d subgaussian
\sigma
random variables for a general subgaussian distribution. In particular, in our setting the error variables are no longer even independent.

By using appropriate concentration inequalities which hold for general subgaussian errors and the fact that the operator norm of
H^{j}
stays bounded away from
\infty
; (see Lemma
11.11
), we show that the entire proof machinery of
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
to bound
T_{1}
can be adapted to our setting as well. We state and prove Propositions
11.1
and
11.2
to bound
T_{1}
which can be thought of as extensions (to general subgaussian error distribution and dependent errors) of the two bounds in Theorem
2.2
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
proved for the standard i.i.d Gaussian errors setting. This means that up to constant factors, our slow rate and fast rate bounds on
T_{1}
match the corresponding bounds given in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
under their recommended choice of the tuning parameter, which
\lambda^{*}
is taken to be.

3.

Bounding the Approximation Error Term
T_{2}
:

To bound the deterministic quantity
T_{2}
, we show that

(a)

\left\|\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c})\right\|^{2}\leq\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}.

(b)

\left\|\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}(I_{j}^{c})\right\|^{2}\leq\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{0}.

The first bound is used for the slow rate and the second bound is used for the fast rate. These bounds work well for our purposes because they turn out to be of lower order than the bounds for
T_{1}
under realistic choices of the true signal
\theta^{*}.
Therefore, obtaining this particular form of the upper bounds is important for us. This is ensured by our
r^{th}
order polynomial interpolation scheme (step
3
of our description of the CVTF estimator). Other simpler interpolation schemes (like simple two neighbor averaging for all orders
r\geq 1
as used in the Rpackage
Arnold et al. 2022
; see Section
6.3
for more on this) may potentially be used but with our particular interpolation scheme, it becomes possible to prove inequalities (see Lemma
11.5
) like in the above display in a fairly simple fashion.

11.2
Detailed Proofs for Trend Filtering

First, we will state two propositions which go a long way towards proving Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
. After stating these two propositions, we finish the proofs of the two theorems assuming these propositions. In these proofs, we will again drop the superscript
r
and denote the completion estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I,r)}
by simply
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
where
I=I_{j}
for
j\in[K]=[r+1].

11.3
Proposition
11.1
and Proposition
11.2

For
r\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{D}=[n-r]
. The following two propositions hold for a fixed but arbitrary index set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
. We fix
\mathcal{S}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{s}\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
, where
1\leq t_{1}<\dots<t_{s}\leq n-r
. Hence,
s
denotes the cardinality of the subset
\mathcal{S}.
Also, let
t_{0}:=0
and
t_{s+1}:=n-r+1
. Next, we define
n_{i}:=t_{i}-t_{i-1}
,
i\in[s+1]
and
n_{\rm\max}:=\max_{i\in[s+1]}n_{i}
. One could think of
\mathcal{S}
as forming a partition of
[n-r]
into
s+1
blocks where the
i
th block has right end point
t_{i}
and left end point
t_{i-1}.
Then
n_{i}
is the length of the
i
th block and
n_{\rm\max}
is the length of the longest block. Also as in the previous section, since
r\geq 1
is fixed, we reduce notational clutter by denoting the completion estimators
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I,r)}
as simply
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
where
I=I_{j}
for
j\in[K]=[r+1].

Proposition 11.1
.

For any
u,v>0
, if
\lambda
is chosen such that

\lambda\geq C_{r}\frac{\sigma}{n^{r-1}}\left(\frac{n_{\rm max}}{2}\right)^{\frac{2r-1}{2}}\sqrt{2\log(2(n-s-r))+2u},

(11.1)

then there exist positive constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
such that with probability at least
1-e^{-C_{1}u}-e^{-C_{2}v}
, for any
j\in[K]
,

\displaystyle SSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}+8\lambda n^{r-1}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad+4\left(\sqrt{C_{r}(s+r)}+\sigma\sqrt{v}\right)^{2}

for some positive constant
C_{r}
which depends only on
r
.

Proposition 11.2
.

Fix
r\in\{1,2,3,4\}.
Define the sign vector
q^{*}\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{s}
containing the signs of the elements in
(D^{(r)}\theta^{*})_{\mathcal{S}}
, that is, for every
i\in[s]
,

q^{*}_{i}:=sign(D^{(r)}\theta^{*})_{t_{i}},

and the index set

\mathcal{S}^{\pm}:=\{2\leq i\leq s:q^{*}_{i}q^{*}_{i-1}=-1\}\cup\{1,s+1\}.

Suppose
n_{i}\geq r(r+2)
for all
i\in\mathcal{S}^{\pm}
.
For any
u,v>0
, if
\lambda
is chosen such that

\lambda\geq C_{r}\frac{\sigma}{n^{r-1}}\left(\frac{n_{\rm max}}{2}\right)^{\frac{2r-1}{2}}\sqrt{2\log(2(n-s-r))+2u},

then there exist positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}
such that with probability at least
1-e^{-C_{1}u}-e^{-C_{2}v}
, for any
j\in[K]
,

\displaystyle SSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{0}+8\lambda n^{r-1}\left\|(D^{(r)}\theta^{*})_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad+4\left(\sqrt{C_{r}(s+r)}+\sigma\sqrt{v}+\lambda n^{r-1}\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}\right)^{2},

where

\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}:=\widetilde{C}_{r}\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\pm}}\frac{1+\log n_{i}}{n_{i}^{2r-1}}+\sum_{i\notin\mathcal{S}^{\pm}}\frac{1+\log n_{i}}{n_{\rm max}^{2r-1}}\right\},

for some positive constants
C_{r}
and
\widetilde{C}_{r}
which depend only on
r
.

We now explain the role of these propositions in our proof. Both these propositions hold for any fixed subset
\mathcal{S}\subset[n-r].
Proposition
11.1
will be used to prove the slow rate theorem and Proposition
11.2
will be used to prove the fast rate theorem. These two propositions can be thought of as extensions (to general subgaussian error distribution and dependent errors of the kind we are interested in) of the two bounds in Theorem
2.2
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
proved for the standard i.i.d Gaussian errors setting.

Note that the bounds in these two propositions involve cardinalities of the blocks of
\mathcal{S}
and
s=|\mathcal{S}|
itself. When we apply these propositions we just need to choose the set
\mathcal{S}
appropriately. For the fast rate theorem, the choice of
\mathcal{S}
is clear, it is just the set of indices where
D^{(r)}\theta^{*}
is non zero. For the slow rate theorem, we choose
\mathcal{S}
to consist of blocks of equal size where the number of blocks is obtained by optimizing a trade off of terms. We will now finish the proofs of Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
assuming these two propositions.

11.4
Proof of Theorem
4.1

In view of Theorem
2.1
, it is enough to bound the quantities:
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})
and
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)},\theta^{*})
, where
I=I_{j}
for
j\in[K].
The desired bounds (both slow rate and fast rate) for
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})
follow from Theorem
1.1
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
. The final bounds are going to be exactly similar to the bounds we obtain here for
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda)},\theta^{*})
. Infact, Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
generalize Theorem
1.1
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
by giving bounds for a completion version of Trend Filtering (and for general subgaussian errors). By setting
I_{j}^{c}
equal to the entire set
[n]
and following our proof, it can be checked that Theorem
1.1
in
Ortelli and van de
Geer 2019
is a special case of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
combined.

Our main goal therefore is to bound
SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)},\theta^{*})
, where
I=I_{j}
for
j\in[K]
, under the optimal choices of
\lambda
, which are different for slow rate and fast rate. However, since we assume that
2^{N^{*}}
contains both these optimal choices, this will essentially give us bounds on
\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}SSE(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)},\theta^{*})
.

In particular, for the slow rate, it suffices to prove that,
for any
u,v>0
, if
\lambda
is chosen such that

\lambda\geq C_{r}\sigma\left(n^{1/(2r+1)}(\log n+u)^{1/(2r+1)}\right),

(11.2)

and
n^{r-1}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}=V^{*}
,
then there exists positive constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
such that with probability at least
1-e^{-C_{1}u}-e^{-C_{2}v}
, for any
j\in[K]
,

\displaystyle MSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\right)\leq\frac{2C_{r}}{n}\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}+C_{r}\sigma^{2}\left(n^{-\frac{2r}{2r+1}}(V^{*}(\log n+u))^{\frac{1}{2r+1}}+\frac{v}{n}\right),

where
C_{r}
is a constant only depending on
r
.
In order to prove the above result, we will use Proposition
11.1
, which we are going to prove later. Consider
\mathcal{S}
in Proposition
11.1
such that each block is of equal size, which implies
n_{\rm max}\approx\frac{n}{s}
. Then, by choosing

\lambda\geq C_{r}\sigma\left(\frac{1}{n^{r-1}}\left(\frac{n}{s}\right)^{\frac{2r-1}{2}}\sqrt{\log n+u}\right),

there exists positive constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
such that with probability at least
1-e^{-C_{1}u}-e^{-C_{2}v}
, for any
j\in[K]
,

\displaystyle SSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}+8\lambda V^{*}+4\left(\sqrt{C_{r}(s+r)}+\sigma\sqrt{v}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}+8\lambda V^{*}+8C_{r}(s+r)+8\sigma^{2}v,

where the right hand side is a function of
s
. In order to optimize this with respect to
s
, we equate

\frac{1}{n^{r-1}}\left(\frac{n}{s}\right)^{\frac{2r-1}{2}}(\sqrt{\log n+u})V^{*}=s,

which leads to
s=n^{\frac{1}{2r+1}}(V^{*}(\log n+u))^{\frac{1}{2r+1}}
. This completes the proof.

11.5
Proof of Theorem
4.2

For the fast rate, it suffices to prove that,
for any
u,v>0
, if
\lambda
is chosen such that

\lambda\geq C_{r}\sigma s^{-(2r-1)/2}\sqrt{n(\log n+u)},

(11.3)

then there exists positive constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
such that with probability at least
1-e^{-C_{1}u}-e^{-C_{2}v}
, for any
j\in[K]
,

\displaystyle MSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{r}}{n}\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{0}+8\lambda n^{r-2}\left\|(D^{(r)}\theta^{*})_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+C_{r}\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{s}{n}\log n(\log n+u)+\frac{v}{n}\right),

where
C_{r}
is a constant only depending on
r
.
In order to prove the above result, we will use Proposition
11.2
, which we are going to prove later. Apply Proposition
11.2
for the particular
\mathcal{S}=\{j\,:\,(D^{(r)}\theta^{*})_{j}\neq 0\}
which satisfies the length assumption. Then, by choosing

\lambda\geq C_{r}\sigma\left(\frac{1}{n^{r-1}}\left(\frac{n}{s}\right)^{\frac{2r-1}{2}}\sqrt{\log n+u}\right)=C_{r}\sigma\left(s^{-(2r-1)/2}\sqrt{n(\log n+u)}\right),

there exists positive constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
such that with probability at least
1-e^{-C_{1}u}-e^{-C_{2}v}
, for any
j\in[K]
,

\displaystyle SSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{0}+8\lambda n^{r-1}\left\|(D^{(r)}\theta^{*})_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\right\|_{1}+C_{r}\sigma^{2}\left(v+\lambda^{2}n^{2r-2}\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\right).

In the third term above, we use the fact that
\frac{n}{2s}\leq n_{\rm max}\leq cn_{i}
for all
i\in\mathcal{S}^{\pm}
to bound
\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}
as follows

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{C}_{r}\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\pm}}\frac{1+\log n_{i}}{n_{i}^{2r-1}}+\sum_{i\notin\mathcal{S}^{\pm}}\frac{1+\log n_{i}}{n_{\rm max}^{2r-1}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq C_{r}\left\{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\pm}}\frac{\log n}{(n/s)^{2r-1}}+\sum_{i\notin\mathcal{S}^{\pm}}\frac{\log n}{(n/s)^{2r-1}}\right\}=C_{r}\frac{s^{2r}}{n^{2r-1}}\log n,

and this completes the proof.

It now remains to give the proofs of Proposition
11.1
and Proposition
11.2
which we provide in the next section.

11.6.2
Upper Bound on
T_{11}

Next, we state and prove Lemma
11.4
which will help in providing an upper bound of the random variable
T_{11}=\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta\right>
.

At this point, we introduce some additional notations which we are going to use in the rest of the results. Let
\mathcal{N}_{-\mathcal{S}}
denote the following subspace

\mathcal{N}_{-\mathcal{S}}:=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(D^{(r)}\theta)_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}}=0\}

and
\mathcal{N}_{-\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
is its orthogonal complement. It is not difficult to observe that
dim(\mathcal{N}_{-\mathcal{S}})=r+s
. For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\theta_{\mathcal{N}_{-\mathcal{S}}}
and
\theta_{\mathcal{N}_{-\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}}
be its projection onto the subspaces
\mathcal{N}_{-\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathcal{N}_{-\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}
respectively. Furthermore, we denote
\Psi^{-\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-r-s)}
to be the matrix for which the following relation holds:

\theta_{\mathcal{N}_{-\mathcal{S}}^{\perp}}=\Psi^{-\mathcal{S}}(D^{(r)}\theta)_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}}.

For any
k\in[n-r-s]
, the
k^{th}
column of
\Psi^{-\mathcal{S}}
is denoted by
\Psi_{k}^{-\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Now we are ready to prove Lemma
11.4
.

Lemma 11.4
.

For any
u,v>0
, suppose
\lambda
is chosen such that

\lambda\geq\frac{\eta(u)}{n^{r-1}}\max_{k\in[n-r-s]}\left\|\Psi_{k}^{-\mathcal{S}}\right\|,

(11.4)

where
\eta(u):=\sigma\left\|{H^{j}}^{T}\right\|_{\rm OP}\sqrt{2\log(2(n-r-s))+2u}
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n-r-s}
is defined as

w_{k}:=\frac{\eta(u)}{\lambda n^{r-1}}\left\|\Psi_{k}^{-\mathcal{S}}\right\|,\quad k\in[n-r-s],

(11.5)

then with probability at least
1-e^{-C_{1}u}-e^{-C_{2}v}
,

\displaystyle\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\theta\right>\leq\left(\sqrt{||H^{j}||_{\rm OP}(r+s)}+\sigma\sqrt{v}\right)||\theta||+\lambda n^{r-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-r-s}\left|w_{k}((D^{(r)}\theta)_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}})_{k}\right|\>\>\>\forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

for some positive constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
.

Proof.

We can write

\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\theta\right>=T_{1}+T_{2},

where
T_{1}=\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\theta_{\mathcal{N}_{-S}}\right>
and
T_{2}=\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\theta_{\mathcal{N}_{-S}^{\perp}}\right>
. Let

\mathcal{V}:=\left\{\|\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\mathcal{N}_{-S}}\|\leq\sqrt{||H^{j}||_{\rm OP}(r+s)}+\sigma\sqrt{v}\right\}

Now, on
\mathcal{V}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{1}=\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\theta_{\mathcal{N}_{-S}}\right>=\left<\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\mathcal{N}_{-S}},\theta_{\mathcal{N}_{-S}}\right>

\displaystyle\leq\|\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\mathcal{N}_{-S}}\|\|\theta_{\mathcal{N}_{-S}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\mathcal{N}_{-S}}\|\|\theta\|\leq\left(\sqrt{||H^{j}||_{\rm OP}(r+s)}+\sigma\sqrt{v}\right)||\theta||.

Also, by Lemma
11.8
(a result about norms of projection of a matrix times a subgaussian random vector),
P(\mathcal{V})\geq 1-e^{-C_{2}v}
for some positive constant
C_{2}
. Next, let

\mathcal{U}:=\left\{\max_{k\in[n-r-s]}\left|\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\frac{\Psi^{-S}_{k}}{\|\Psi^{-S}_{k}\|}\right>\right|\leq\eta(u)\right\}.

Then, on
\mathcal{U}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\theta_{\mathcal{N}_{-S}^{\perp}}\right>

\displaystyle=\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\Psi^{-\mathcal{S}}\left(D^{(r)}\theta\right)_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\right>

\displaystyle=\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\sum_{k=1}^{n-r-s}\Psi^{-\mathcal{S}}_{k}((D^{(r)}\theta)_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}})_{k}\right>

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-r-s}\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\Psi^{-\mathcal{S}}_{k}\right>((D^{(r)}\theta)_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}})_{k}

\displaystyle=\lambda n^{r-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-r-s}\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\frac{\Psi^{-\mathcal{S}}_{k}}{\|\Psi^{-S}_{k}\|\eta(u)}\right>\frac{\eta(u)}{\lambda n^{r-1}}\|\Psi^{-S}_{k}\|((D^{(r)}\theta)_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}})_{k}

\displaystyle=\lambda n^{r-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-r-s}\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\frac{\Psi^{-\mathcal{S}}_{k}}{\|\Psi^{-S}_{k}\|\eta(u)}\right>w_{k}((D^{(r)}\theta)_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}})_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda n^{r-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-r-s}\left|\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\frac{\Psi^{-\mathcal{S}}_{k}}{\|\Psi^{-S}_{k}\|\eta(u)}\right>\right|\left|w_{k}((D^{(r)}\theta)_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}})_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda n^{r-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-r-s}\left|w_{k}((D^{(r)}\theta)_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}})_{k}\right|.

Also, by Lemma
11.9
(a standard result about maxima of subgaussians),
P(\mathcal{U})\geq 1-e^{-C_{1}u}
for some positive constant
C_{1}
. Combining the above results, the proof is complete. Lemmas
11.8
and
11.9
which have been used in this proof are stated and proved in Section
11.7
.
∎

11.6.3
Upper Bound on
T_{2}
when
\theta=\theta^{*}

Next, we prove a lemma that will provide an upper bound of the quantity
T_{2}=\left\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\right\|^{2}
, when
\theta=\theta^{*}
.

Lemma 11.5
.

We have

1.

\left\|\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\|^{2}\leq\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}.

2.

\left\|\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\|^{2}\leq\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{0}.

Proof.

Note that, by the construction of
\widetilde{y}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\left(\theta^{*}_{i}-\widetilde{\theta}_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I_{j}:i+r\leq n}\left(\theta^{*}_{i}-\sum_{l=1}^{r}(-1)^{l+1}{r\choose l}\theta^{*}_{i+l}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i\in I_{j}:i+r>n}\left(\theta^{*}_{i}-\sum_{l=1}^{r}(-1)^{l+1}{r\choose l}\theta^{*}_{i-l}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}.

Now note that one can further upper bound
\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}
in either way:

\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}\leq\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1},\quad\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}\leq\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{0}.

∎

Proof of Proposition
11.1
.

Using Lemmas
11.3
and
11.4
, we have for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
\lambda
is chosen according to (
11.4
) and
w
is defined according to (
11.5
) then with probability at least
1-e^{-C_{1}u}-e^{-C_{2}v}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\right\|^{2}+2\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta\right>+2\lambda n^{r-1}\left(\|D^{(r)}\theta\|_{1}-\|D^{(r)}\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}\|_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\theta-\widetilde{\theta}\right\|^{2}+2\left(\sqrt{||H^{j}||_{\rm OP}(r+s)}+\sigma\sqrt{v}\right)\left\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta\right\|

\displaystyle+\underbrace{2\lambda n^{r-1}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n-r-s}\left|w_{k}((D^{(r)}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta))_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}})_{k}\right|+\left(\|D^{(r)}\theta\|_{1}-\|D^{(r)}\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}\|_{1}\right)\right\}}_{T_{3}}.

(11.6)

By definition,
|w|_{\infty}\leq 1
. Thus, replacing
w_{k}
by
1
for all
k\in[n-r-s]
, the third term of (

), namely

can be further bounded by

\displaystyle 2\lambda n^{r-1}\left\{\|(D^{(r)}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta))_{\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{S}}\|_{1}+\left(\|D^{(r)}\theta\|_{1}-\|D^{(r)}\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}\|_{1}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda n^{r-1}\left\{\|D^{(r)}(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta)\|_{1}+\left(\|D^{(r)}\theta\|_{1}-\|D^{(r)}\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}\|_{1}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda n^{r-1}\left\{\|D^{(r)}\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}\|_{1}+\|D^{(r)}\theta\|_{1}+\|D^{(r)}\theta\|_{1}-\|D^{(r)}\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}\|_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=4\lambda n^{r-1}\|D^{(r)}\theta\|_{1}.

Imposing the above bound in (

), and plugging in
\theta=\theta^{*}
we have

\displaystyle SSE\left(\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}},\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\right)=\left\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}_{I_{j}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{j}}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\theta^{*}-\widetilde{\theta}\right\|^{2}+2\left(\sqrt{||H^{j}||_{\rm OP}(r+s)}+\sigma\sqrt{v}\right)\left\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta^{*}\right\|+4\lambda n^{r-1}\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right|_{\infty}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}+4\lambda n^{r-1}\left\|D^{(r)}\theta^{*}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\left(\sqrt{C_{r}(r+s)}+\sigma\sqrt{v}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}.

The last inequality is obtained by bounding the first term by using the inequality (1) in Lemma
11.5
, and the second term by using the inequality
2ab\leq 2a^{2}+\frac{b^{2}}{2}
and Lemma
11.11
together for some positive constant
C_{r}
that depends only on
r

All that remains is to check whether our choice of

\max_{k\in[n-r-s]}\left\|\Psi_{k}^{-\mathcal{S}}\right\|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{n_{\rm max}}{2}\right)^{2r-1}.

Plugging the above we obtain an upper bound on the right hand side of (

\displaystyle\frac{\eta(u)}{n^{r-1}}\max_{k\in[n-r-s]}\left\|\Psi_{k}^{-\mathcal{S}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma}{n^{r-1}}\left\|{H^{j}}^{T}\right\|_{\rm OP}\sqrt{2\log(2(n-r-s))+2u}\max_{k\in[n-r-s]}\left\|\Psi_{k}^{-\mathcal{S}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{r}\frac{\sigma}{n^{r-1}}\left(\frac{n_{\rm max}}{2}\right)^{\frac{2r-1}{2}}\sqrt{2\log(2(n-s-r))+2u},

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
11.11
for some positive constant
C_{r}
that depends only on

.
Thus, the requirement on the tuning parameter is met when

is chosen according to (
11.1
).
Hence, the proof is complete.

Lemma 11.6

We have

11.7
Auxiliary Lemmas

Lemma 11.8
.

For any subspace
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\widetilde{\epsilon}_{S}
be the projection of
\widetilde{\epsilon}
on
S
. For any
u>0
, with probability at least
1-2e^{-Cu/2}
,

\left\|\widetilde{\epsilon}_{S}\right\|\leq\sqrt{||H^{j}||_{\rm OP}}\sqrt{Dim(S)}+\sigma\sqrt{u},

where
||\cdot||_{\rm OP}
denotes the operator norm of a matrix and
C
is some positive constant.

Proof.

Let
P_{S}
denote the projection matrix of subspace
S
. Then

\widetilde{\epsilon}_{S}=P_{S}\widetilde{\epsilon}=P_{S}H^{j}\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}.

From the Hanson-Wright concentration inequality, see Theorem 6.2.1 in
Vershynin 2018
, we obtain that, for any matrix
A_{m\times n}
and
u>0
, if
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})\overset{iid}{\sim}Subg(0,\sigma^{2})
then

P\left(\left|||AZ||-||A||_{\rm HS}\right|>u\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-C\frac{u^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

for some
C>0
. Here, the notation
\|A\|_{\rm HS}
refers to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the matrix
A
.
Letting
A=P_{S}H^{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|A\right\|_{\rm HS}

\displaystyle=\left\|P_{S}H^{j}\right\|_{\rm HS}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{trace((P_{S}H^{j})^{T}(P_{S}H^{j}))}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{trace({H^{j}}^{T}P_{S}H^{j})}

Let
r_{0}=rank({H^{j}}^{T}P_{S}H^{j})
and the non-zero eigenvalues are
\{\lambda_{i},i\in[r_{0}]\}
. Also, for any matrix
A
, let
\lambda_{\rm max}(A)
denote its largest eigenvalue. Therefore,

\displaystyle trace({H^{j}}^{T}P_{S}H^{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{0}}\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\rm max}({H^{j}}^{T}P_{S}H^{j})rank({H^{j}}^{T}P_{S}H^{j})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x}\frac{x^{T}{H^{j}}^{T}P_{S}H^{j}x}{||x||}rank(P_{S})

\displaystyle=\sup_{x}\frac{x^{T}{H^{j}}^{T}P_{S}H^{j}x}{||H^{j}x||}\frac{||H^{j}x||}{||x||}\times rank(P_{S})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\rm max}(P_{S})||H^{j}||_{\rm OP}\times rank(P_{S})

\displaystyle=||H^{j}||_{\rm OP}\times Dim(S).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 11.9
.

Consider a set of
m
nonzero vectors
\{v_{i}:i\in[m]\}
. Then for any
u>0
, with probability at least
1-e^{-Cu}
,

\max_{k\in[m]}\left|\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\frac{v_{k}}{||v_{k}||}\right>\right|\leq\sigma\left\|{H^{j}}^{T}\right\|_{\rm OP}\sqrt{2\log(2m)+2u},

where
C
is some positive constant.

Proof.

Fix any
k\in[m]
. Since
\widetilde{\epsilon}=H^{j}\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}
, we can write

\displaystyle\left|\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\frac{v_{k}}{||v_{k}||}\right>\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left<H^{j}\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}},\frac{v_{k}}{||v_{k}||}\right>\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}^{T}{H^{j}}^{T}\frac{v_{k}}{||v_{k}||}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}}^{T}\frac{{H^{j}}^{T}v_{k}}{\left\|{H^{j}}^{T}v_{k}\right\|}\right|\frac{\left\|{H^{j}}^{T}v_{k}\right\|}{\|v_{k}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left<\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}},\frac{{H^{j}}^{T}v_{k}}{\left\|{H^{j}}^{T}v_{k}\right\|}\right>\right|\left\|{H^{j}}^{T}\right\|_{\rm OP}

Therefore,

\displaystyle P\left(\max_{k\in[m]}\left|\left<\widetilde{\epsilon},\frac{v_{k}}{||v_{k}||}\right>\right|\leq\sigma\left\|{H^{j}}^{T}\right\|_{\rm OP}\sqrt{2\log(2m)+2u}\right)

\displaystyle\geq P\left(\max_{k\in[m]}\left|\left<\epsilon_{I_{j}^{c}},\frac{{H^{j}}^{T}v_{k}}{\left\|{H^{j}}^{T}v_{k}\right\|}\right>\right|\leq\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2m)+2u}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-e^{-Cu}.

The last result follows from the standard maximal inequality of
m
subgaussian random variables.
∎

0&0&1\dots&0\\
&\vdots\\
0&0&0\dots&1\\
(-1)^{r+1}\dots&+{r\choose 3}&-{r\choose 2}&{r\choose 1}\end{bmatrix},

In general, it is not difficult to observe that for any

Next, in Lemma

Lemma 11.10
.

If a block matrix
H\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
is of the form

H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{1}&0&\dots&0\\
0&H_{2}&\dots&0\\
&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&H_{m}\end{bmatrix},

where
H_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}\times q_{i}},i\in[m]
,
\sum_{i}p_{i}=p
and
\sum_{i}q_{i}=q
, then

\|H\|_{\rm OP}\leq\max_{i\in[m]}\|H_{i}\|_{\rm OP}.

Proof.

For any
x=(x_{1}^{T},x_{2}^{T}\dots x_{m}^{T})
such that
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}}
for all
i\in[m]
, we have

Hx=\begin{bmatrix}H_{1}x_{1}\\
H_{2}x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
H_{m}x_{m}\end{bmatrix},

implying

\displaystyle\|Hx\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|H_{i}x_{i}\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|H_{i}\|_{\rm OP}^{2}\|x_{i}\|^{2}\leq\max_{i\in[m]}\|H_{i}\|_{\rm OP}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|x_{i}\|^{2}=\max_{i\in[m]}\|H_{i}\|_{\rm OP}^{2}\|x\|^{2}.

∎

Recall that,
for any
j\in[K]
, the matrix
H^{j}
is a block matrix of the form of
H
in Lemma
11.10
for some
m\geq 1
with
H_{i}\in\{A,B,I_{d}:1\leq d\leq K\}
,
i\in[m]
.
Thus, in order to have an upper bound of
\|H^{j}\|_{\rm OP}
and
\|{H^{j}}^{T}\|_{\rm OP}

,
\|A^{T}\|_{\rm OP}
,
\|B\|_{\rm OP}
and

.

Lemma 11.11
.

The following results hold.

1.

\|A\|_{\rm OP}\leq\left\{1+{r\choose 1}^{2}+{r\choose 2}^{2}+\dots+{r\choose r}^{2}\right\}=:c_{r}
,

2.

\|A^{T}\|_{\rm OP}\leq\left\{r+{r\choose 1}^{2}+{r\choose 2}^{2}+\dots+{r\choose r}^{2}\right\}=:c^{\prime}_{r}
,

3.

\|B\|_{\rm OP}\leq 2c_{r}
,

4.

\|B^{T}\|_{\rm OP}\leq 2c^{\prime}_{r}
.

Thus,
\|H^{j}\|_{\rm OP},\|{H^{j}}^{T}\|_{\rm OP}\leq 2c^{\prime}_{r}
.

Proof.

We will only prove the first result since the rest follow in a similar way. Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that
||x||=1
. Then using Cauchy Schwarz inequality we have,

\displaystyle||Ax||^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{{r\choose 1}x_{1}-{r\choose 2}x_{2}+\dots+(-1)^{r+1}x_{r}\right\}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\dots+x_{r}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\{{r\choose 1}^{2}+{r\choose 2}^{2}+\dots+1\right\}(x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{r}^{2})+(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\dots+x_{r}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\left\{1+{r\choose 1}^{2}+{r\choose 2}^{2}+\dots+{r\choose r}^{2}\right\}||x||^{2}=c_{r}.

Using Lemma
11.10
, we have

\|H^{j}\|_{\rm OP}\leq\max\{\|A\|_{\rm OP},\|B\|_{\rm OP},1\}\leq 2c_{r},\;\text{and}\;\|{H^{j}}^{T}\|_{\rm OP}\leq\max\{\|A^{T}\|_{\rm OP},\|B^{T}\|_{\rm OP},1\}\leq 2c^{\prime}_{r}.

Since
c_{r}<c^{\prime}_{r}
, one can bound both
\|H^{j}\|_{\rm OP}
and
\|{H^{j}}^{T}\|_{\rm OP}
by
2c^{\prime}_{r}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.4
.

As in all of our applications of Theorem
2.1
, the main task for us is to bound the prediction errors for
I=I_{1},I_{2}
,

\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}_{I^{c}}-\theta^{*}_{I_{c}}\|^{2}\leq\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}.

Therefore, our task is to bound
\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}
for an appropriate choice of
\lambda\in\Lambda.

Recall that
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
is the SVT estimator applied to the matrix
\widetilde{y}(I).

Now we make a few observations.

1.

\widetilde{y}(I)
is unbiased for
\theta^{*}.
This is because

\mathbb{E}\widetilde{y}(I)_{ij}=2\mathbb{E}[y_{ij}W_{ij}]=2\mathbb{E}y_{ij}\mathbb{E}W_{ij}=\mathbb{E}y_{ij}=\theta^{*}.

2.

Define
\widetilde{\epsilon}(I)=\widetilde{y}(I)-\theta^{*}
. Observe that we can write

\widetilde{\epsilon}(I)_{ij}=\begin{cases}\theta^{*}_{ij}+2\epsilon_{ij}\>\>&\text{with probability}\>\>1/2\\
-\theta^{*}_{ij}\>\>&\text{with probability}\>\>1/2.\end{cases}

This implies that the matrix
\widetilde{\epsilon}
has independent subgaussian entries with subgaussian norm at most
c\left(|\theta^{*}|_{\infty}+\sigma\right)
for an absolute constant
c>0.
This conclusion follows from Lemma
12.1
(stated and proved after this proof.)

Based on the above two observations, we can think of
\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}
as a usual SVT estimator under the model
\widetilde{y}(I)=\theta^{*}+\widetilde{\epsilon}(I).
The matrix
\widetilde{\epsilon}(I)
plays the role of the error matrix here which has mean zero and has independent subgaussian entries with maximum subgaussian norm
c\left(|\theta^{*}|_{\infty}+\sigma\right).
We can now apply Theorem
5.3
to conclude that as long as
\lambda\geq C\left(|\theta^{*}|_{\infty}+\sigma\right)\sqrt{n}
for an appropriate absolute constant
C>0
, the same bound as in the right hand side of Theorem
5.3
also holds for
\|\widehat{\theta}^{(\lambda,I)}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 12.1
.

Suppose
\epsilon
is a mean
0
subgaussian random variable with subgaussian norm at most
\sigma.
For a given
K>0
, consider the random variable

X=\begin{cases}K+2\epsilon\>\>&\text{with probability}\>\>1/2\\
-K\>\>&\text{with probability}\>\>1/2.\end{cases}

Then
X
is also a mean
0
subgaussian random variable with subgaussian norm at most
CK\sigma
where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

We will use the following characterization of a subgaussian random variable, see Proposition
2.5.2
in
Vershynin 2018
.

A random variable
U
is subgaussian
(\sigma)
if there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all integers
p\geq 1
, we have

\left(\mathbb{E}|U|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq C\sigma\sqrt{p}.

We will now show that
X
satisfies the above characterization. Fix any integer
p\geq 1.
We can write

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}|X|^{p}\right)^{1/p}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}|K+2\epsilon|^{p}+\frac{1}{2}K^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}|K+2\epsilon|^{p}\right)^{1/p}+\left(\frac{1}{2}K^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}|K+2\epsilon|^{p}\right)^{1/p}+K\leq K+\left(\mathbb{E}|2\epsilon|^{p}\right)^{1/p}+K\leq 2K+C\sigma\sqrt{p}\leq\max\{2,C\}\left(K+\sigma\right)\sqrt{p}.

In the above display, the first inequality follows by using the elementary inequality
(a+b)^{1/p}\leq a^{1/p}+b^{1/p}
for any
a,b>0
, second inequality uses the triangle inequality for the
\ell_{p}
norm of a random variable, the fourth inequality uses the subgaussian characterization for
\epsilon
and the last inequality uses the fact that
p\geq 1.

∎

13
Lasso

13.1
Background and Related Literature

The lasso, proposed by
Tibshirani 1996
is one of the most popular tools for high dimensional regression. By now, there is a vast literature on analyzing the mean squared error of lasso. The typical statement of the results say that if the tuning parameter
\lambda
is chosen appropriately depending on some problem parameters (which are typically unknown); then a certain MSE bound holds. However, in practice, the tuning parameter is often chosen using cross validation. The literature giving rigorous theoretical analysis of cross validated lasso is far thinner. As far as we are aware, the first few papers undertaking theoretical analysis of cross validated lasso are
Lecué
et al. 2012
,
Homrighausen and
McDonald 2014
,
Homrighausen and
McDonald 2013
,
Homrighausen and
McDonald 2017
,
Miolane and
Montanari 2018
. Two papers which contain the state of the art theoretical results on cross validated lasso are the papers
Chatterjee and
Jafarov 2015
,
Chetverikov et al. 2020
. The paper
Chatterjee and
Jafarov 2015
is the object of inspiration for the current article. They analyzed a two fold cross validated version of the constrained or primal lasso proposed in
Tibshirani 1996
. Their result gives the analogue of the so-called slow rate for Lasso (e.g, see Theorem
2.15
in
Rigollet and
Hütter 2015
) in the fixed design setup. On the other hand, the paper
Chetverikov et al. 2020
analyzes a related but different cross validated Lasso estimator and their main result gives an analogue of the fast rate for Lasso (e.g, see Theorem
2.18
in
Rigollet and
Hütter 2015
) under random design with certain assumptions on the distribution of the covariates and the noise variables.

To the best of our knowledge, a single cross validated lasso estimator which attains both the slow rate and the fast rate in the fixed design setup has not yet been proposed in the literature. Our goal here is to demonstrate that designing such a cross validated lasso is possible. We consider a two fold cross validated version
\widehat{\beta}_{cvlasso}
of the penalized lasso and prove two results. The first result, Theorem
13.1
gives the so-called slow rate under essentially no assumptions on the design matrix. This extends the result of
Chatterjee and
Jafarov 2015
to cross validated penalized lasso. Our second result gives the fast rate for the same estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{cvlasso}
under a standard incoherence condition on the design matrix
X.
Thus, we are able to ensure that qualitatively both the results of
Chatterjee and
Jafarov 2015
and
Chetverikov et al. 2020
hold for our
\widehat{\beta}_{cvlasso}
estimator. We now describe the
\widehat{\beta}_{cvlasso}
estimator precisely. We consider a well specified linear model
y=X\beta^{*}+\epsilon
where
X
is a fixed
n\times p
design matrix and
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an error vector consisting of i.i.d mean
0
subgaussian entries. We denote the standard lasso estimator with tuning parameter
\lambda
by
\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda)}
, defined as follows:

\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda)}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-x_{i}^{t}\beta)^{2}+\lambda\|\beta\|_{1}\right],

where
x_{i}^{t}
is the
i
th row of the design matrix
X.

13.2
Description of the CVLASSO estimator

1.

Set
K=2
.

2.

Divide
[n]
into
I_{1},I_{2}
randomly as follows. Each entry
i\in[n]
belongs to
I_{1}
or
I_{2}
with probability
1/2
independently of other entries.

3.

For
j\in\{1,2\}
, define

\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left[\sum_{i\in I_{j}}(y_{i}-x_{i}^{t}\beta)^{2}+\lambda\|\beta\|_{1}\right].

4.

Consider a finite grid of possible values of the tuning parameter
\lambda
, namely
\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},2^{3},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
where
N^{*}
is chosen by the user. For any
j\in\{1,2\}
, define
\widehat{\lambda}_{j}
to be the candidate in
\Lambda
for which the prediction error is the minimum, that is,

\widehat{\lambda}_{j}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\sum_{i\in I_{j}}(y_{i}-x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j}^{c})})^{2}.

Note that
I_{1}^{c}=I_{2}
and vice-versa.

5.

Now define an intermediate estimator
\widetilde{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for any
j\in\{1,2\}
, if
i\in I_{j}
then

\widetilde{\theta}_{i}=x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\widehat{\lambda}_{j},I_{j}^{c})}.

6.

Define

\widehat{\lambda}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\|X\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda)}-\widetilde{\theta}\|^{2}

Finally, our estimator (CVLASSO) is defined to be

\widehat{\beta}_{cvlasso}=\widehat{\beta}^{(\widehat{\lambda})}.

13.3
Main Results

Theorem 13.1
.

[Slow Rate]
Suppose
M>0
is a number such that the design matrix
X
satisfies

\max_{j\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}^{4}\leq M.

Fix any
0<\delta<1.
Suppose we take our grid
\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
such that

2^{N^{*}}\geq 4\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}M^{1/2}n\log p}+\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}M^{1/2}n\log 1/\delta}.

Then we have the following bound with probability atleast
1-7\delta
for an appropriate absolute constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{n}\|X\widehat{\beta}_{cvlasso}-X\beta^{*}\|^{2}\leq\frac{C}{n}\Big[\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}^{2}\sqrt{Mn\log p}+\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}\sigma

\displaystyle\sqrt{Mn\log p/\delta}+\sigma^{2}N^{*}

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\log 1/\delta\Big].

Remark 13.1
.

The above result is qualitatively similar to the result in Theorem
2.1
in
Chatterjee and
Jafarov 2015
. The main difference is that while that result is about a cross validated version of the constrained lasso, our result is about the corresponding cross validated version of the penalized lasso. There are certain advantages of using the penalized form of Lasso instead of the constrained form as mentioned in Remark
13.6
.

Remark 13.2
.

The bound in Theorem
13.1
basically says that the MSE of
\widehat{\theta}_{cvlasso}
scales like
O(\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}})
with high probability if
M
and
\sigma
are bounded away from
\infty.
Note that, this result holds essentially without any assumptions on the design matrix. As mentioned in
Chatterjee and
Jafarov 2015
, this MSE scaling agrees with the persistency condition for lasso (under random design) defined in
Greenshtein
et al. 2004
which says that if
\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}=o\big((\frac{n}{\log p})^{1/4}\big)
then persistency holds.

Theorem 13.2
.

[Fast Rate]
Suppose that the design matrix
X
satisfies an incoherence condition

\left|\frac{X^{t}X}{n}-I_{p\times p}\right|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{64k}

where
k=\|\beta^{*}\|_{0}.

Suppose
M>0
is a number such that the design matrix
X
satisfies

\max_{j\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ij}^{4}\leq M.

Assume that the sample size
n
is large enough so that

2\log p<\frac{n}{2^{14}k^{2}M^{2}}.

Fix any
0<\delta<1.
Suppose we take our grid
\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
such that

2^{N^{*}}\geq 4\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}M^{1/2}n\log p}+\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}M^{1/2}n\log 1/\delta}.

Then we have the following bound with probability atleast
1-6\delta-\exp\left(-\frac{n}{2^{14}k^{2}M^{2}}\right)
for an appropriate absolute constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{n}\|X\widehat{\beta}_{cvlasso}-X\beta^{*}\|^{2}\leq\frac{C}{n}\Bigg[\frac{k^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}M(\log(p/\delta))^{3/2}+\sigma^{2}k\sqrt{M}\log(p/\delta)

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}N^{*}

\displaystyle+\sigma^{2}\log 1/\delta\Bigg].

Remark 13.3
.

Assuming that
M
and
\sigma
are terms bounded away from
\infty
, the first term (inside the brackets) in the bound given in Theorem
13.2
which scales like
O\left(\frac{k^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}(\log p)^{3/2}\right)
is dominated by the second term
O(k\log p)
as long as
k<O(\sqrt{n/\log p})
which we can readily check is the interesting regime where we can expect fast rates. This is because as soon as
k=O(\sqrt{n/\log p})
, the fast SSE rate
O(k\log p)=O(\sqrt{n\log p})
which matches the slow rate. Therefore, when
k\geq O(\sqrt{n/\log p})
, one should use the slow rate result in Theorem
13.1
. To summarize, the above result in Theorem
13.2
is useful in the sparse regime when
k<O(\sqrt{n/\log p})
in which case the MSE of
\widehat{\theta}_{cvlasso}
scales like the fast rate
O(\frac{k\log p}{n})
with high probability.

Remark 13.4
.

In Theorem
13.2
, we need a slightly stronger (by a factor of
2
) incoherence condition than the standard one assumed for penalized Lasso in the literature. For example, Theorem
2.18
in
Rigollet and
Hütter 2015
assumes that
\left|\frac{X^{t}X}{n}-I_{p\times p}\right|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{32k}.
It is well known that weaker (than incoherence) assumptions on the design matrix
X
such as the restricted isometry property are also sufficient to ensure fast rates for the lasso. Such results are likely to be true for our cross validated estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{cvlasso}
as well. However, we leave this for future research and just consider the incoherence condition because of two reasons. Firstly, it seems to be the simplest sufficient condition for fast rates available in the literature and is actually checkable in practice in contrast to the restricted isometry type properties which are computationally intractable to check. Secondly, our goal here is to simply demonstrate that both the slow rate and the fast rate are attainable for a single cross validated lasso estimator. Thus, we prefer to sacrifice some generality in exchange to demonstrate a phenomenon under a simpler sufficent condition.

Remark 13.5
.

For both of our theorems, the grid
\Lambda=\{1,2,2^{2},\dots,2^{N^{*}}\}
needs to satisfy that
2^{N^{*}}\geq C\sigma\sqrt{M^{1/2}n\log p}
for a specified constant
C.
This is a very mild condition to ensure in practice. The parameter
\sigma
is the only unknown term and as explained before, even a gross over estimate can be plugged in without any serious consequences since our grid grows exponentially. Thus, the number of grid points would be
O(\log n\log\log p)
which means we would need to solve the lasso optimization problem
O(\log n\log\log p)
times to compute
\widehat{\theta}_{cvlasso}.
In other words, the computational complexity of
\widehat{\theta}_{cvlasso}
would be
O(\log n\log\log p)
times the computational complexity of computing a single instance of the penalized lasso estimator.

Remark 13.6
.

A potential advantage of the cross validated penalized lasso over constrained lasso is as follows. It is known that under certain nonsingularity conditions on the design matrix
X
, to attain fast rates for the usual constrained lasso, it is sufficient that the tuning parameter is chosen to be exactly equal to
\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}
; see Theorem
2.1
in
Chatterjee 2014
. Clearly, this is hard to achieve in practice. It is not known to what extent is this result robust to the choice of this tuning parameter. For example, Theorem
2.1
in
Chatterjee 2014
further indicates that if the tuning parameter is chosen to be
\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}\pm 1
then it is not possible for the constrained lasso to attain fast rates. In contrast, the penalized lasso seems to be more robust with respect to the choice of its tuning parameter. For example, if one sets
\lambda
to be twice the ideal choice of
\lambda
known to achieve fast rates for penalized lasso (e.g, see Theorem
2.18
in
Rigollet and
Hütter 2015
), then the risk at most doubles and hence the rate of convergence remains the same.

The upshot of this is that we can afford to have a grid of
\lambda
growing exponentially and still attain fast rates for our estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{cvlasso}
. This has significant computational advantages as this means the cardinality of our grid
\Lambda
is only growing like
O(\log n\log\log p)
which means we have to solve the lasso optimization problem at most
O(\log n\log\log p)
times to compute
\widehat{\theta}_{cvlasso}
. In contrast, it is likely that the
\Lambda
grid needs to be much finer in resolution (with cardinality growing like
n^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
) for the cross validated constrained Lasso proposed in
Chatterjee and
Jafarov 2015
to attain fast rates.

Remark 13.7
.

Like in
Chatterjee and
Jafarov 2015
we have proposed a
2
fold cross validated version of Lasso. However, if it is so desired, one can easily construct a similar
K
fold version as should be clear from our general framework and the description of
\widehat{\theta}_{cvlasso}
. Similar risk bounds as in Theorem
13.1
and Theorem
13.2
would hold for the
K
fold version as well.

14.1
Auxiliary Lemmas

Lemma 14.3
.

Let
X
be a
n\times p
matrix. Let
M=\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}^{4}\right].
Also for
i\in[n]
, let
\eta_{i}
be i.i.d rademacher random variables. Then we have the following inequality:

P\left(\sup_{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\Delta\|_{1}\leq L}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}(x_{i}^{t}\Delta)^{2}\leq L^{2}(2\sqrt{\log p}\>n^{1/2}M^{1/2}+x)\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right).

Proof.

We can expand the squares and write

\displaystyle\sup_{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\Delta\|_{1}\leq L}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}(x_{i}^{t}\Delta)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\Delta\|_{1}\leq L}\sum_{j,k=1}^{p}\Delta_{j}\Delta_{k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}x_{ij}x_{ik}\right)

\displaystyle\leq L^{2}\max_{1\leq j,k\leq p}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}x_{ij}x_{ik}\right|.

By Lemma
10.11
, we have for all
x\geq 0
,

P\left(\max_{1\leq j,k\leq p}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}x_{ij}x_{ik}\right|\leq 2\sqrt{\log p}\left[\max_{1\leq j,k\leq p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}^{2}x_{ik}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\>+x\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right).

Now note that by the Cauchy Schwarz inequality

\max_{1\leq j,k\leq p}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}^{2}x_{ik}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq n^{1/2}M^{1/2}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 14.4
.

Let
X
be a
n\times p
matrix with
M=\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}^{4}\right].
Let
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a random vector of i.i.d mean
0
subgaussian random variables with subgaussian norm
\sigma
. Then we have for any
\delta>0
,

P\left(|X^{t}\epsilon|_{\infty}\geq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}M^{1/2}n\log p}+\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}M^{1/2}n\log 1/\delta}\right)\leq\delta.

Proof.

Observe that for each
i\in[p]
we have
X[,i]^{t}\epsilon
is subgaussian with subgaussian norm atmost
\sigma\|X[,i]\|\leq\sigma M^{1/4}n^{1/2}
by Cauchy Schwarz inequality. Therefore, applying lemma
10.11
we have for all
x\geq 0
,

P\left(|X^{t}\epsilon|_{\infty}\geq\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}M^{1/2}n\log p}+x\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}M^{1/2}n}\right).

Setting
\delta
to be the right hand side in the above display finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 14.5
.

If
\lambda
is chosen such that
\lambda\geq 4|X^{t}\epsilon|_{\infty}
then for any
j\in\{1,2\}
we have two pointwise inequalities. Firstly,

\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-\beta^{*}\|_{1}\leq 4\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}.

Secondly, we also have

\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\left(x_{i}^{t}\beta^{*}-x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}\lambda\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}.

Proof.

By definition, we have the basic inequality:

\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\left(y_{i}-x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\right)^{2}+\lambda\left\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\right\|_{1}\leq\sum_{i\in I_{j}}(y_{i}-x_{i}^{t}\beta^{*})^{2}+\lambda\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}

Writing
y=X\beta^{*}+\epsilon
and expanding the squares in above we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\left(x_{i}^{t}\beta^{*}-x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\right)^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\right\|_{1}\leq 2\sum_{i\in I_{j}}(x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-x_{i}^{t}\beta^{*})\epsilon_{i}\>\>-\;

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\lambda\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}.

(14.3)

Now using Holder’s inequality and the given choice of
\lambda
we further obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\left(x_{i}^{t}\beta^{*}-x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\right)^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2|X_{I_{j}}^{t}\epsilon|_{\infty}\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\|_{1}+2|X_{I_{j}}^{t}\epsilon|_{\infty}\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\|_{1}+\lambda\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}\leq\frac{3}{2}\lambda\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}.

This further implies

\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\|_{1}\leq 3\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}.

We can now apply triangle inequality above to get
\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-\beta^{*}\|_{1}\leq 4\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}.

We can also conclude from the second last display that

\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\left(x_{i}^{t}\beta^{*}-x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}\lambda\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}.

∎

Lemma 14.6
.

Fix any
j\in\{1,2\}
. Suppose
X_{I_{j}}
satisfies

\left|\frac{X_{I_{j}}^{t}X_{I_{j}}}{n}-\frac{1}{2}I_{p\times p}\right|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{64k}.

Also suppose that
\lambda
is chosen such that
\lambda\geq 4|X^{t}\epsilon|_{\infty}.
Then we have two pointwise inequalities. Firstly,

\|X_{I_{j}}(\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-\beta^{*})\|\leq 8\lambda\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}.

Secondly,

\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-\beta^{*}\|_{1}\leq 128\lambda\frac{k}{n}.

Proof.

Let
S=\{j\in[p]:\beta_{j}\neq 0\}.
An equivalent way to write (
14.3
) is as follows:

\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\left(x_{i}^{t}\beta^{*}-x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\right)^{2}\leq 2\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\left(x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-x_{i}^{t}\beta^{*}\right)\epsilon_{i}\>\>-\lambda\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\|_{1}+\lambda\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}.

Now we can write

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\left(x_{i}^{t}\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-x_{i}^{t}\beta^{*}\right)\epsilon_{i}\leq 2|X_{I_{j}}^{t}\epsilon|_{\infty}\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-\beta^{*}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-\beta^{*}\|_{1},

where we have used Holder’s inequality and the fact that
\lambda\geq 4|X^{t}\epsilon|_{\infty}
.

The last two displays imply that

\displaystyle\|X_{I_{j}}(\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-\beta^{*})\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-\beta^{*}\|_{1}\leq\lambda\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-\beta^{*}\|_{1}+\lambda\|\beta^{*}\|_{1}-\lambda\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}\|_{1}=

\displaystyle\lambda\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}_{S}-\beta^{*}_{S}\|_{1}+\lambda\|\beta^{*}_{S}\|_{1}-\lambda\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}_{S}\|_{1}\leq 2\lambda\|\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}_{S}-\beta^{*}_{S}\|_{1}.

In particular, the above display implies that the vector
\widehat{\Delta}=\widehat{\beta}^{(\lambda,I_{j})}-\beta^{*}
lies in the set

C=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|v\|_{1}\leq 4\|v_{S}\|_{1}\}.

This is commonly referred to as the
cone condition
in the Lasso literature.

We can further write

\displaystyle\|X_{I_{j}}\widehat{\Delta}\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\widehat{\Delta}\|_{1}\leq 2\lambda\|\widehat{\Delta}_{S}\|_{1}\leq 2\lambda\sqrt{k}\|\widehat{\Delta}_{S}\|\leq 2\lambda\sqrt{k}\|\widehat{\Delta}\|\leq 2\lambda\sqrt{k}\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}\|X_{I_{j}}\widehat{\Delta}\|,

where in the second inequality we have used Cauchy Schwarz inequality and in the final inequality we have used Lemma
14.7
. The above display implies the conclusions of this lemma.

∎

The following lemma is a version of a standard result (e.g, see Lemma
2.17
in
Rigollet and
Hütter 2015
) in the lasso literature which says that if the design matrix
X
satisfies an incoherence condition and a vector satisfies a certain cone condition then the matrix
X
satisfies a restricted eigenvalue condition.

Lemma 14.7
.

Fix any
j\in\{1,2\}
. Suppose
X_{I_{j}}
satisfies

\left|\frac{X_{I_{j}}^{t}X_{I_{j}}}{n}-\frac{1}{2}I_{p\times p}\right|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{64k}.

Let
S\subset[p]
such that
|S|=k.

Then for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfying
\|v\|_{1}\leq 4\|v_{S}\|_{1}
we have

\|v\|\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}\|X_{I_{j}}v\|

Proof.

Let us denote
W=X_{I}
within this proof. We can write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|Wv\|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}v_{i}^{2}\frac{(W^{t}W)_{ii}}{n}+\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq p}v_{i}v_{j}\frac{(W^{t}W)_{ij}}{n}\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{64k}\right)\|v\|^{2}-\frac{1}{64k}\|v\|_{1}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{64k}\right)\|v\|^{2}-\frac{1}{64k}16\|v_{S}\|_{1}^{2}\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{64k}\right)\|v\|^{2}-\frac{1}{64k}16k\|v_{S}\|^{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{64k}\right)\|v\|^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\|v\|^{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{64k}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\|v\|^{2}.

In the second inequality we used the fact that
\|v\|_{1}\leq 4\|v_{S}\|_{1}
, in the next inequality we used the Cauchy Schwarz inequality and in the last inequality we used the fact that
k\geq 1.

∎

Lemma 14.8
.

Let
I=I_{j}
for
j\in\{1,2\}.
If
\;2\log p<\frac{n}{2^{14}k^{2}M^{2}}
then

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\frac{X_{I}^{t}X_{I}}{n}-\frac{X^{t}X}{2n}\right|_{\infty}\geq 1/128k\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n}{2^{14}k^{2}M^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Note that the
(i,j)
th element of
\frac{X_{I}^{t}X_{I}}{n}
can be written as follows:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l\in I}X_{li}X_{lj}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\mathrm{1}(l\in I)X_{li}X_{lj}.

Therefore, by the standard tail bound for a subgaussian random variable we can write

\displaystyle P\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}[\mathrm{1}(l\in I)-1/2]X_{li}X_{lj}\geq t\right)\leq\exp\left(-2nt^{2}/M^{2}\right).

Now by a union bound argument,

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\frac{X_{I}^{t}X_{I}}{n}-\frac{X^{t}X}{2n}\right|_{\infty}\geq t\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\max_{i,j\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}[\mathrm{1}(l\in I)-1/2]X_{li}X_{lj}\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq p^{2}\exp(-2nt^{2}/M^{2}).

By setting
t=\frac{1}{128k}
we finish the proof.

∎
