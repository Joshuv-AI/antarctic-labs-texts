k k -Factorizations of the full cycle and generalized Mahonian statistics on k k -forests
John Irving, Amarpreet Rattan

Abstract
. We develop direct bijections between the set ℱ n k \mathcal{F}_{n}^{k} of minimal factorizations of the long cycle ( 0 1 ⋯ k n ) (0\,1\,\cdots\,kn) into ( k + 1 ) (k+1) -cycle factors and the set ℛ n k \mathcal{R}_{n}^{k} of rooted labelled forests on vertices { 1 , … , n } \{1,\ldots,n\} with edges coloured with { 0 , 1 , … , k − 1 } \{0,1,\ldots,k-1\} that map natural statistics on the former to generalized Mahonian statistics on the latter. In particular, we examine the generalized major index on forests ℛ n k \mathcal{R}_{n}^{k} and show that it has a simple and natural interpretation in the context of factorizations. Our results extend those in [ IR21 ] , which treated the case k = 1 k=1 through a different approach, and provide a bijective proof of the equidistribution observed by Yan [ Yan97 ] between displacement of k k -parking functions and generalized inversions of k k -forests.

The aim of this article is to recast and generalize our earlier work
[

The following notational conventions are used throughout.
For nonnegative integers
m\leq n

\mathfrak{S}_{X}

1.1.
Mahonian Statistics on Rooted Forests

A
rooted forest
is graph whose components are rooted trees,
i.e.
trees with a distinguished vertex.
Let
\mathcal{R}_{n}
be the set of rooted forests on vertices
[1,n]
.

For convenience we regard the edges of every forest
F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
as being directed away from the roots of their components. We identify an edge directed from
u
to
v
by the pair
(u,v)
. If
F
contains such an edge then we say
u
is the
parent
of
v
and
v
is a
child
of
u
. More generally,
u
is an
ancestor
of
v
— and
v
is a
descendant
of
u
— if there is a nonempty directed path from
u
to
v
. The subtree of
F
induced by
u
and all its descendants is called the
hook
at
u
. We write
H(u)
for this hook and
h(u)
for the number of vertices contained therein, commonly known as the
hook length
at
u
. The
total depth
of
F
is the sum of all non-root hook lengths,

0pt(F):=\sum_{(u,v)\in E(F)}h(v).

Equivalently, this is the sum of the distances from all verties to the roots of their components, sometimes known as the
path length
of
F
.

The
major
and
comajor indices
of
F
are defined by

\mathrm{maj}(F):=\sum_{u\in V(F)}h_{L}(u)\qquad\textnormal{ and
}\qquad\mathrm{comaj}(F):=\sum_{u\in V(F)}h_{R}(u),

where

h_{L}(u):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,v)\in E(F)\\
v<u\end{subarray}}h(v)\qquad\textnormal{ and }\qquad h_{R}(u):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,v)\in E(F)\\
u<v\end{subarray}}h(v).

We refer to the quantities
h_{L}(u)
and
h_{R}(u)
as the
left
and
right hook lengths
at
u
. The rationale for this terminology will be apparent later.
Note that
h(u)=h_{L}(u)+h_{R}(u)+1
and thus

\mathrm{maj}(F)+\mathrm{comaj}(F)=\sum_{u\in V(F)}(h(u)-1)=0pt(F).

If
v
is a descendant of
u
, then the pair
(u,v)
is said to be an
inversion
of
F
when
u>v
and a
coinversion
when
u<v
. Let
\mathrm{inv}(u):=\#\{v\in H(u)\,:\,v<u\}
and
\mathrm{coinv}(u):=\#\{v\in H(u)\,:\,v>u\}
denote the number of inversions and coinversions in
F
of the form
(u,v)
for some
v
. Clearly
h(u)=\mathrm{inv}(u)+\mathrm{coinv}(u)+1
.
The
inversion
and
coinversion indices
of
F
are defined by

\mathrm{inv}(F):=\sum_{u\in V(F)}\mathrm{inv}(u)\qquad\textnormal{ and
}\qquad\mathrm{coinv}(F):=\sum_{u\in V(F)}\mathrm{coinv}(u).

These are simply the total number of inversions and coinversions in
F
. Observe that
\mathrm{inv}(F)+\mathrm{coinv}(F)=0pt(F)
, because every pair of vertices

(u,v)
with
v\in H(u)
is either an inversion or a
coinversion, but not both.

and
\mathrm{comaj}(F)

. While we have cast our work in terms of rooted forests, all statements regarding

mutatis mutandis
to the language of trees.

1.2.
Factorizations of Full Cycles

It is well known that every
r
-cycle
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{[n]}
can be expressed as a product of
r-1
transpositions but no fewer. Accordingly,
a sequence
(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{r-1})
of transpositions satisfying
\sigma=\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\cdots\tau_{r-1}
is called a
minimal factorization
of
\sigma
. For example, the canonical decompositions

(1)

\displaystyle(a_{0}\,a_{1})(a_{0}\,a_{2})\cdots(a_{0}\,a_{r})

and

(2)

\displaystyle(a_{0}\,a_{r})(a_{1}\,a_{r})\cdots(a_{r-1}\,a_{r})

are minimal factorizations of
\sigma=(a_{0}\,a_{1}\,\cdots\,a_{r})
. These will play a central role in our analysis and we refer to them as the
lower
and
upper decompositions
of
\sigma
, respectively.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
be the set of minimal factorizations of the full cycle
\sigma_{n}=(0\,1\,2\,\cdots\,n)
. For example

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(0\;1)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(0\;1)(0\;2),(0\;2)(1\;2),(1\;2)(0\;1)\}.

Minimal factorizations of a fixed full cycle have long been known to be related to labelled trees (equivalently, rooted forests). The identity
|\mathcal{F}_{n}|=(n+1)^{n-1}=|\mathcal{R}_{n}|
dates back at least to Hurwitz but is often credited to Dénes
[
Dé59
]
, who offered an elegant proof via indirect counting. Direct bijections between
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}
came later. The simplest of these, due to Moszkowski
[
Mos89
]
, has been rediscovered in different guises by a number of authors. Its essence is the fact that trees are a special class of planar maps, and minimal factorizations (broadly speaking) serve as combinatorial encodings of planar embeddings. A version of this bijection is central to our construction and described in Section
2.1
.

The connection between
\mathcal{R}_{n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}
can be refined to account for forest inversions/coinversions. The corresponding statistics for factorizations, which we call
area
and
coarea
, are defined for
f=(a_{1}\,b_{1})(a_{2}\,b_{2})\cdots(a_{n}\,b_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
by

\mathrm{area}(f)=\binom{n}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\qquad\textnormal{ and }\qquad\mathrm{coarea}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-1)-\binom{n}{2}.

With this terminology the main result of our previous paper
[
IR21
]
can be stated as follows.

[

]
this result is phrased in terms of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and area and coarea are called

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
n\geq 0
, the bi-statistics
(\mathrm{inv},\mathrm{coinv})
on
\mathcal{R}_{n}
and
(\mathrm{area},\mathrm{coarea})
on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
share the same joint distribution.

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
relied on generating series techniques but was effectively based on a recursive bijection. In Section
2
we shall reestablish this result by describing a natural and
direct
bijection between
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}
that maps
(\mathrm{area},\mathrm{coarea})
to
(\mathrm{maj},\mathrm{comaj})
. This will serve as a base case toward extending Theorem
1.1
to treat factorizations into
(k+1)
-cycles for arbitrary
k\geq 1
.

1.3.
Minimal
k
-Factorizations

For
k,n\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
be the set of all sequences
(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n})
of
(k+1)
-cycles
\tau_{i}\in\mathfrak{S}_{[kn]}
such that
\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\cdots\tau_{n}=\sigma_{kn}
. In particular, we have
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{1}=\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

Certainly
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
is nonempty, as taking
\tau_{i}=(0,in+1,in+2,\ldots,in)
for
1\leq k\leq n
defines one canonical element. Moreover, the cycle
\sigma_{kn}
cannot be factored into fewer
(k+1)
-cycles, since replacing each factor with its lower expansion would then yield a factorization into fewer than
kn
transpositions. As such we call the elements of
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}

minimal
k
-factorizations
of
\sigma_{kn}
, or simply
k
-factorizations
for short.

Minimal
k
-factorizations of full cycles are well-studied and, unsurprisingly, they correspond with a class of decorated forests. These will be defined shortly,
but in preparation for stating our main result, we first describe how the area/coarea statistics on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
can be extended to
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
.

Let

(3)

f=(a_{1}^{0}\,\cdots\,a_{1}^{k})\cdots(a_{n}^{0}\,\cdots\,a_{n}^{k})

be a generic element of
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
, keeping in mind our convention that
a_{i}^{0}
is the least element of the
i
-th factor. Then we define

\begin{split}\mathrm{area}_{k}(f)&:=\binom{kn}{2}-n\binom{k}{2}-k\sum_{j=1}^{n}a^{0}_{j},\\
\mathrm{coarea}_{k}(f)&:=k\sum_{j=1}^{n}(a^{k}_{j}-1)-\binom{kn}{2}-n\binom{k}{2}.\end{split}

The careful reader may observe that these expressions share a common factor of
k
,
e.g.

\mathrm{area}_{k}(f)=k\cdot(k\binom{n}{2}-\sum_{j}a_{j}^{0})
. This apparent redundancy will arise naturally in our analysis so we have chosen not to “normalize” it out of our definitions.

We further introduce two additional statistics on
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
that we call
semiarea
and

cosemiarea
, given by

\begin{split}\mathrm{semiarea}_{k}(f)&:=\binom{kn}{2}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}^{i},\\
\mathrm{cosemiarea}_{k}(f)&:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j}^{i}-1)-\binom{kn}{2}.\end{split}

Although not obvious from these definitions, it transpires that
\mathrm{semiarea}_{k}(f)
and
\mathrm{cosemiarea}_{k}(f)
are also always divisible by
k
.
Note that both
\mathrm{area}_{k}(f)
and
\mathrm{semiarea}_{k}(f)
revert to
\mathrm{area}(f)
at
k=1
, but are otherwise distinct. The same is true for coareas.

1.4.
Rooted
k
-Forests

A
k
-forest
is a rooted labelled forest
F
equipped with a function
\kappa:E(F)\rightarrow[k-1]
that assigns one of
k

colours

\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
to each edge of
F
. Two
k
-forests
(F,\kappa)
and
(F^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
are isomorphic if there is a graph isomorphism
\zeta\,:\,V(F)\rightarrow V(F^{\prime})
that preserves roots and edge colours.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
be the set of
k
-forests on vertices
[1,n]
. Note that there are no restrictions on the colouring function, so there are
k^{|E(F)|}=k^{n-c}
elements of
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
arising from any forest
F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
with
c
components. For brevity we will suppress explicit mention of the colouring function when working with elements of
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
.

Our definition of
k
-forests follows Yan
[
Yan97
]
, who attributes them to Stanley. These objects appear elsewhere in the literature in the different but equivalent form of
k
-cacti
, which are tree-like structures with edges replaced by
(k+1)
-gons.
In particular, there is a simple correspondence between
k
-forests on
n
labelled vertices and
k
-cacti with
n
labelled polygons. See Figure
2
.

3
-forest
F
and its associated
3

. Vertices of of

correspond with polygons of

. The colouring of
F
is indicated in red. The colour of edge
(i,j)
describes where
P_{j}
(polygon

) is attached to

in

. For example, edge
(9,8)
with colour 1 indicates

is attached to
P_{9}
at the second vertex clockwise from where
P_{9}
is attached to its parent,

.

A straightforward extension of the Prüfer encoding yields
|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}|=(kn+1)^{n-1}
, and the Moszkowski correspondence
\mathcal{F}_{n}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n}
likewise extends to a bijection between
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
. In particular, we have
|\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}|=(kn+1)^{n-1}
. We direct the reader to
[
Irv09
]
and references therein for further information, however all details relevant to our discussion will be outlined when required.

The Mahonian statistics
\mathrm{inv}/\mathrm{coinv}
and
\mathrm{maj}/\mathrm{comaj}
on
\mathcal{R}_{n}
can be applied to
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
simply by ignoring edge colourings. However, we also wish to introduce extended versions of these statistics specifically for
k
-forests.

To this end we first define the

chromatic depth
and
chromatic codepth
of
F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
by

(4)

\mathrm{chr}_{k}(F)=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E(F)}\kappa(e)\cdot h(v)\;\;\;\textnormal{and}\;\;\;{\mathrm{cochr}}_{k}(F)=\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E(F)}(k-1-\kappa(e))\cdot h(v),

respectively. Note that

\mathrm{chr}_{k}(F)=0pt(F)
when
F
has all its edges coloured 1, so
\mathrm{chr}_{k}(F)

can be regarded as a colour-weighted depth of
F
. We then let

\begin{split}\mathrm{inv}_{k}(F)&:=\mathrm{inv}(F)+\mathrm{chr}_{k}(F),\\
\mathrm{coinv}_{k}(F)&:=\mathrm{coinv}(F)+{\mathrm{cochr}}_{k}(F),\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\mathrm{maj}_{k}(F)&:=\mathrm{maj}(F)+\mathrm{chr}_{k}(F),\\
\mathrm{comaj}_{k}(F)&:=\mathrm{comaj}(F)+{\mathrm{cochr}}_{k}(F).\end{split}

These extended statistics revert to
\mathrm{inv}/\mathrm{maj}
at
k=1
since
\mathrm{chr}_{1}(F)={\mathrm{cochr}}_{1}(F)=0
for all
F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
.

This definition of
\mathrm{inv}_{k}(F)
is equivalent to that appearing in
[
Yan97
]
,
where it arose in an effort to generalize Kreweras’ identity between the
inversion enumerator for trees and the discrepancy enumerator for parking
functions (see
[
Kre80
]
). This connection will be discussed in more
detail in Section
1.6
. Consideration of
\mathrm{maj}_{k}(F)
appears to be
novel. It is straightforward (using Figure
2
as a guide) to
adapt the definitions of
\mathrm{inv}_{k},\mathrm{coinv}_{k},\mathrm{maj}_{k},
and
\mathrm{comaj}_{k}
so they apply to
k
-cacti.

1.5.
Main Result

We can now state our main theorem, which relates area statistics on
k
-factorizations to major
indices on
k
-forests.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
n,k\geq 1
, there is an explicit bijection

\phi:\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
such that for
F=\phi(f)
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{area}_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=k\cdot\mathrm{maj}_{k}(F),

\displaystyle\mathrm{coarea}_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=k\cdot\mathrm{comaj}_{k}(F)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{semiarea}_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=k\cdot\mathrm{maj}(F),

\displaystyle\mathrm{cosemiarea}_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=k\cdot\mathrm{comaj}(F).

Moreover, if
f=(a_{1}^{0}\,\cdots\,a_{1}^{k})\cdots(a_{n}^{0}\,\cdots\,a_{n}^{k})
then
a_{i}^{k}-a_{i}^{0}=k\cdot h(i)
for
i\in[1,n]
, where
h(i)
is the hook length at vertex
i
in
F
.

Theorem
1.2
will be proved first for
k=1
in Section
2
and then in general in Section
3
. In each case the proof is through construction of the promised bijection
\phi
.
Note that the theorem implies
\mathrm{semiarea}_{k}(f)
and
\mathrm{cosemiarea}_{k}(f)
are independent of the edge colouring of
F=\phi(f)
. The edge colours of
F
are only relevant to the evaluation of
\mathrm{area}_{k}(f)
and
\mathrm{coarea}_{k}(f)
.

We will now describe precisely how Theorem
1.2
can be viewed
as a generalization of Theorem
1.1
. The key is that
\mathrm{maj}(F)

and
\mathrm{inv}(F)
are equidistributed not only over
\mathcal{R}_{n}
, but over every
isomorphism class thereof. This was first proved inductively by Björner and
Wachs
[
BW89
]
, and later bijectively by Liang and Wachs
[
LW92
]
.
More recently, Grady and Poznanović
[
GP16
]
established this
result by mapping both
\mathrm{inv}(F)
and
\mathrm{maj}(F)
to a common code called a

subexcedant sequence
on
F
. We will not go into further detail here.
The salient point is that there are known bijections
\xi\,:\,\mathcal{R}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n}
satisfying
\mathrm{maj}(F)=\mathrm{inv}(\xi(F))
with
F
isomorphic to
\xi(F)

for all
F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
. For definiteness, let
gp
be the Grady-Poznanović
bijection of this type.

Note that
gp
extends to a bijection on
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
by effectively ignoring edge colours.

Let
F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
and
F^{\prime}={\textsc{gp}}(F)
, so that
F
and
F^{\prime}
are isomorphic as
k
-forests and
\mathrm{maj}(F)=\mathrm{inv}(F^{\prime})
. Then

\displaystyle 0pt(F)=0pt(F^{\prime})

\displaystyle\implies\mathrm{maj}(F)+\mathrm{comaj}(F)=\mathrm{inv}(F^{\prime})+\mathrm{coinv}(F^{\prime})

\displaystyle\implies\mathrm{comaj}(F)=\mathrm{coinv}(F^{\prime}).

It is clear from (
4
) that chromatic depth and codepth are invariant on isomorphism classes of
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
, so there follows
\mathrm{maj}_{k}(F)=\mathrm{inv}_{k}(F^{\prime})
and
\mathrm{comaj}_{k}(F)=\mathrm{coinv}_{k}(F^{\prime})
.

Let
\phi:\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
be the bijection guaranteed by Theorem
1.2
. Then taking
\hat{\phi}={\textsc{gp}}\circ\phi
proves the following generalization of Theorem
1.1
. We shall see in the next section how this sheds light on an open question concerning the relationship between
k
-forests and generalized parking functions.

Corollary 1.3
.

For any
n,k\geq 1
, there is an explicit bijection
\hat{\phi}:\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
such that for
F=\hat{\phi}(f)
we have
(\mathrm{area}_{k}(f),\mathrm{coarea}_{k}(f))=k\cdot(\mathrm{inv}_{k}(F),\mathrm{coinv}_{k}(F))
and
(\mathrm{semiarea}_{k}(f),\mathrm{cosemiarea}_{k}(f))=k\cdot(\mathrm{inv}(F),\mathrm{coinv}(F))
.

Let us now consider the latter claim of Theorem
1.2
, regarding hook lengths. In case
k=1
the theorem stipulates that the distribution of the hook length vector
(h(1),\ldots,h(n))
over
F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
matches that of the
difference index

(b_{1}-a_{1},\cdots,b_{n}-a_{n})
over
f=(a_{1}\,b_{1})\cdots(a_{n}\,b_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
. A similar result appears in
[
GY02
]
, although there the authors compute transposition differences circularly, replacing
b-a
with
\min\{b-a,n+1+a-b\}
. This has the effect of disguising the connection with hook lengths, despite the use of a dual construction equivalent to that used here (see Section
2.2
).

Over the past couple decades, a considerable amount of effort has been put toward the development of
hook length formulae
for trees and forests. These formulae generally provide simple multiplicative expressions for sums of the form
\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{v\in V(T)}\alpha(v)
, where
\mathcal{T}
is a class of rooted trees and
\alpha(v)
can be expressed in terms of the hook length of
T
at
v
.

For the class of rooted labelled forests, one of the simplest such formulae is

(5)

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}z^{c(F)}\prod_{v\in V(F)}\frac{1}{h(v)}=z(z+1)(z+2)\cdots(z+n-1),

where
c(F)
is the number of components in
F
.
This reflects the fact that permutations on
[1,n]
with
m
cycles — which are well known to be counted by the coefficient of
z^{m}
on the right-hand side,
i.e.
the signless Stirling number
s(n,m)
— are in correspondence with increasing rooted forests on
[1,n]
with
m
components. See
[
GS06
]
for a more general approach, in particular Corollary 6.3, of which (
5
) is a special case.

Using (
5
) at
z=1
together with Theorem
1.2
at
k=1
yields the curious identity

\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{b_{i}-a_{i}}=n!,

where the sum extends over all factorizations
f=(a_{1}\,b_{1})\cdots(a_{n}\,b_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
. More generally, since there are
k^{n-c(F)}
ways of colouring a forest
F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
to create a
k
-forest
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
, we can apply (
5
) at
z=1/k
to get:

Corollary 1.4
.

For any
n,k\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{a_{i}^{k}-a_{i}^{0}}=\frac{(k+1)(2k+1)\cdots((n-1)k+1)}{k^{n}},

where the sum extends over all
f=(a_{1}^{0}\,\cdots\,a_{1}^{k})\cdots(a_{n}^{0}\,\cdots\,a_{n}^{k})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
.

1.6.
k
-Parking Functions

A sequence
p=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
of
nonnegative integers is called a
k
-parking function

if its nondecreasing rearrangement
(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
satisfies
b_{i}\leq k(i-1)
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
be the set of
k
-parking functions of length
n
.
Elements of
\mathcal{P}_{n}:=\mathcal{P}_{n}^{1}
are simply called
parking
functions
. There is an extensive body of literature on these object and we will only skim the surface here. We refer the reader to the comprehensive surveys by Yan
[
Yan15
]
and Haglund
[
Hag08
]
for further information.

It is well known, and easy to prove via cycle lemma or direct bijection, that
|\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}|=|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}|=(kn+1)^{n-1}
. This can be refined to account for inversions in
k
-forests. The companion statistic on
k
-parking functions is called
displacement
, defined for
p=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
by

(6)

\mathrm{disp}_{k}(p):=k\binom{n}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}.

Then we have the following result, which was first proved for
k=1
by Kreweras

[
Kre80
]
and then for general
k\geq 1
by Yan.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
Yan97
]
)
.

For
n,k\geq 1

\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}}q^{\mathrm{disp}_{k}(p)}=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}}q^{\mathrm{inv}_{k}(F)}.

Yan’s proof of Theorem
1.5
is inductive, and it has been an open problem to find a bijective proof.
Such a proof is afforded by Corollary
1.3
in tandem with the simple correspondence between
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
described below.

Proposition 1.6
.

For fixed
n,k\geq 1
, define
L\,:\,\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}\rightarrow[kn]^{n}
by

L:(a_{1}^{0}\,\cdots\,a_{1}^{k})\cdots(a_{n}^{0}\,\cdots\,a_{n}^{k})\mapsto(a_{1}^{0},\dots,a_{n}^{0}).

Then
L
is a bijection from
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
to
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
, and
\mathrm{area}_{k}(f)=k\cdot\mathrm{disp}_{k}(L(f))
for all
f
.

Assuming the truth of the proposition, and letting
\hat{\phi}\,:\,\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
be the bijection from Corollary
1.3
, observe that
L\circ\hat{\phi}^{-1}
maps
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
bijectively to
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
while satisfying
\mathrm{inv}_{k}(F)=\frac{1}{k}\cdot\mathrm{area}_{k}(\hat{\phi}^{-1}(f))=\mathrm{disp}_{k}(L(\hat{\phi}^{-1}(F)))
. This is the promised bijective proof of Theorem
1.5
.

The latter claim of Proposition
1.6
is an immediate
consequence of the definitions.
The bijectivity of
{L}:{\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}}
when
k=1
was proved
explicitly by Biane
[
Bia02
]
and is equivalent to an earlier result of Stanley
[
Sta97
, Theorem 3.1]
. We call this special case of
L
the
Stanley-Biane bijection
, denoted
{\textsc{sb}}:\mathcal{F}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{n}
. A proof of bijectivity for general
k
recently appeared in
[
MNW20
]
, where it was established through a straightforward generalization of Biane’s argument. We will now describe a different (independent) proof that relies on a reduction to
sb
.

Consider the function
{\textsc{expand}}:\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{kn}
that replaces each entry of a
k
-parking function with
k
copies of itself. For instance,
p=(0,5,1)\in\mathcal{P}_{3}^{3}
has
{\textsc{expand}}(p)=(0,0,0,5,5,5,1,1,1)\in\mathcal{P}_{9}
.

Similarly define the function
{\textsc{lower}}:\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{kn}
that replaces each cyclic factor of a
k
-factorization with its lower decomposition; see (
1
). For example,
f=(1\,2\,5)(0\,1\,6)(3\,4\,5)\in\mathcal{F}_{3}^{2}
has
{\textsc{lower}}(f)=(1\,2)(1\,5)(0\,1)(0\,6)(3\,4)(3\,5)\in\mathcal{F}_{6}
.

Certainly both
expand
and
lower
are injective for all
k
(although not bijective for
k>1
). It is also clear that
{\textsc{sb}}\circ{\textsc{lower}}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k})\subseteq{\textsc{expand}}(\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k})
. Thus
{\textsc{expand}}^{-1}\circ{\textsc{sb}}\circ{\textsc{lower}}
is a well-defined injective function from
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
to
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
, and by definition it agrees with
L
on its domain. Since
|\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}|=|\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}|
, we conclude that
L
is bijective.

Finally, a comment on nomenclature. It is common to view a
k
-parking function
p=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
as a specially labelled lattice path from
(0,0)
to
(n,kn)
, with unit steps to the north and east, that remains weakly below the line
y=kx
. For each
h\in[kn-1]
let
I_{h}=\{i:a_{i}=h\}
. Then the path
P
corresponding to
p
has
|I_{h}|
horizontal steps at height
h
and these are labelled in increasing left-right order with the set
I_{h}
. The displacement
\mathrm{disp}_{k}(p)
is then the number of whole squares between
P

and the line
y=kx
. For this reason,
\mathrm{disp}_{k}(p)
is also known as the
area
of
p
. In light of Proposition
1.6
, this explains our naming of the statistics
\mathrm{area}_{k}/\mathrm{coarea}_{k}
on
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
.

2.
The construction for
k=1

In this section we focus on the case
k=1
of Theorem

1.2
. The bijection
{\phi}:{\mathcal{F}_{n}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}_{n}}
that we construct in this case has a particularly simple description and will be central to our analysis for general
k
.
Throughout we shall simplify our notation by omitting the value of
k
, using

\mathcal{R}_{n}
in place of
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{1}
,
\mathrm{area}(f)
for
\mathrm{area}_{1}(f)
,
etc.

2.1.
Arch Diagrams

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}
be the set of vertex-rooted, edge-labelled trees on
n
edges, by which we mean trees with a distinguished vertex whose edges are distinctly labelled with
[1,n]
. Vertices are not labelled.

We typically will not distinguish between an edge and its label;
i.e.
we view
[1,n]
as the edge-set of any tree
T\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
. As with rooted forests, we regard the edges of these trees as being directed away from the root. It will be convenient to let
d(i)
denote the child (‘down’) endpoint of edge
i
.

There is a simple correspondence between
\mathcal{E}_{n}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}
, defined as
follows: Given
T\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
, we first ‘push’ the label of each edge
i

away from the root onto vertex
d(i)
, and then remove the root to obtain a
rooted forest
F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
. We call this the
Cayley bijection
, written

{\textsc{cay}}\,:\,\mathcal{E}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n}
. See Figure
3
.

Figure 3.
A vertex-rooted, edge-labelled tree

T\in\mathcal{E}_{10}
(left) and its image
{\textsc{cay}}(T)\in\mathcal{R}_{10}

(right). The boxed vertices indicate roots. The major index of both
T
and
{\textsc{cay}}(T)
is 7 and their comajor index is 5.

Our interest in edge-labelled trees stems from the fact that they are in natural correspondence with factorizations of full cycles.

Consider a planar embedding of
T\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
described as follows:

(1)

the root is at
(0,0)
, and all other vertices at
(i,0)
for
1\leq i\leq n
;

(2)

edges are labelled
[1,n]
and are drawn above the
x
-axis without crossings;
and

(3)

the sequence
of edge labels around each vertex, taken in counterclockwise order
beginning on the
x
-axis, is increasing.

Following
[
IR21
]
, we call an embedding of a member of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
satisfying (1)–(3) an
arch diagram
of size
n
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{n}
be set of all such diagrams up to topological equivalence. The embedding process described above provides a one-one correspondence
\mathcal{E}_{n}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

We canonically label the vertices of each diagram
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
by assigning label
i
to the vertex at
(i,0)
, for
0\leq i\leq n
. We emphasize that
these labels are completely determined by
A
itself, or equally by its skeletal tree
T\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
. We then obtain from
A
a factorization
f=(a_{1}\,b_{1})(a_{2}\,b_{2})\cdots(a_{n}\,b_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
by letting
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
be the endpoints of arch
i
.
See Figure
4
.

Figure 4.
The arch diagram
A\in\mathcal{A}_{10}
corresponding to the factorization
f=(6\,7)(0\,2)(3\,6)(3\,10)(8\,9)(0\,3)(5\,6)(4\,5)(8\,10)(1\,2)\in\mathcal{F}_{10}
. Vertices are canonically labelled.

The transformation
A\mapsto f
turns out to be a bijection from
\mathcal{A}_{n}
to
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. We denote this function by
{{\textsc{fact}}}:{\mathcal{A}_{n}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}_{n}}

and its inverse by
{{\textsc{arch}}}:{\mathcal{F}_{n}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{n}}
. The direct definition of
arch
is obvious: Given
f=(a_{1}\,b_{1})\cdots(a_{n}\,b_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, construct
{\textsc{arch}}(f)
by drawing an arch labelled
i
from
(a_{i},0)
to
(b_{i},0)
for each
1\leq i\leq n
. See
[
IR21
]
for a more detailed description of these transformations based on the ‘circle-chord’ construction from
[
GY02
]
. The composite mapping
{\textsc{fact}}\circ{\textsc{cay}}^{-1}
is essentially a repackaging of Moszkowski’s bijection
\mathcal{R}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{n}
referenced in Section
1.2
.

Let
A
be a fixed arch diagram. We write
l(i)
and
r(i)
, respectively, for the left and right endpoints of edge
i
in
A
, and we define its
span
to be the half open interval
\mathrm{span}(i):=\{(x,0):x\in[l(i),r(i))\}
. Since distinct edges cannot cross, their spans are either disjoint or one is contained in the other. Thus the edges of
A
are partially ordered by inclusion of their spans. We say
j

covers

i
if
\mathrm{span}(i)\subset\mathrm{span}(j)
and there is no arch
\ell
with
\mathrm{span}(i)\subsetneq\mathrm{span}(\ell)\subsetneq\mathrm{span}(j)
.

2.2.
Dual Diagrams

An arch diagram
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
divides the upper half-plane
\{(x,y)\,:\,y\geq 0\}
into
n+1
regions, each of which contains exactly one point from the set

H_{n}=\{(i+\frac{1}{2},0):0\leq i\leq n\}
. Each arch separates two
regions and hence two points of
H_{n}
. We construct a planar dual
D
of
A

by first placing a vertex at each point of
H_{n}
and then, for each
i\in[1,n]
, drawing an arch labelled
\bar{i}
between the two points of
H_{n}
that
are separated by arch
i
of
A
. See Figure
5
. Note our
use of overlined symbols
\bar{1},\bar{2},\ldots,\bar{n}
for edge labels in the
dual. This notational convention will be used systematically to distinguish
labels in
D
from those in
A
.

So constructed, the dual of any arch diagram
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
is an embedding of a tree
T\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
satisfying the following:

(1’)

vertices lie at the points
H_{n}
, with the root at
(n+\frac{1}{2},0)
;

(2’)

edges are labelled
[\bar{1},\bar{n}]
and are drawn above the
x
-axis without crossings;
and

(3’)

the sequence
of edge labels around each vertex, taken in counterclockwise order
beginning on the
x
-axis, is
decreasing
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{n}
be set of topologically inequivalent embeddings satisfying

(1^{\prime})-(3^{\prime})
. Clearly the map

{{\textsc{dual}}}:{\mathcal{A}_{n}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{D}_{n}}
described above is a
bijection. We call elements of
\mathcal{D}_{n}

dual diagrams
of
size
n
and canonically label their vertices by assigning label
i
to the vertex at
(i+\tfrac{1}{2},0)
for
0\leq i\leq n
.

The transparent bijection between dual diagrams and their skeletal trees allows
us to identify
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. In particular, we shall view the
Cayley bijection as a correspondence between
\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Note
that
{\textsc{cay}}({\textsc{dual}}(A))
is the Hasse diagram of the poset of edges of
A
(ordered
by inclusion). Compare, for instance, Figures
3
and

4
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f=(a_{1},\,b_{1})\cdots(a_{n}\,b_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
,
A={\textsc{arch}}(f)
,
D={\textsc{dual}}(A)
and
F={\textsc{cay}}(D)
. For each
i\in[1,n-1]
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

a_{i}=a_{i+1}

(2)

edge
i+1
covers edge
i
in
A

(3)

edge
\overline{i}
is incident with vertex
d(\overline{i+1})
in
D

(4)

vertex
i
is a child of vertex
i+1
in
F

Proof.

Condition (1) is equivalent to
l(i)=l(i+1)
in
A
(by definition of

arch
), and the requirement that edge labels increase counterclockwise
around vertex
l(i)
makes this equivalent to (2). The equivalences
(2)\iff(3)\iff(4)
follow by definition of
dual
and
cay
.
∎

2.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
(Base Case)

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n},D={\textsc{dual}}(A)
and
F={\textsc{cay}}(D)
. Then for
i\in[1,n]

we have

h(i)=r(i)-l(i)
, where
i
specifies a vertex of
F
on the left-hand side
and an edge of
A
on the right. More precisely, we have

h_{L}(i)=d(\bar{i})-l(i)\qquad\text{and}\qquad h_{R}(i)=r(i)-d(\bar{i})-1.

Proof.

We refer the reader to Figure

6
, which illustrates a portion of
A
in solid black and
D
in dashed red.

Figure 6.
A portion of an arch diagram
A
(black) and its dual
D
(red).

Consider edge
i
of
A
.
By construction, only one dual edge may cross this arch, namely
\bar{i}
. Thus
the vertices of
D
lying within
\mathrm{span}(i)
are precisely those in the dual
hook
H(d(\bar{i}))
. Clearly there are
r(i)-l(i)
dual vertices in
\mathrm{span}(i)
,
so we have
h(i)=r(i)-l(i)
.

Let
\bar{j}_{1}>\cdots>\bar{j}_{q}>i>\bar{i}_{1}>\cdots>\bar{i}_{p}
be the
edges of
D
incident with
d(\bar{i})
. Since edge labels must decrease
counterclockwise around
d(\bar{i})
, and since edges may not cross, all vertices
of
H(d(\bar{i}_{1})),\ldots,H(d(\bar{i}_{p}))
must lie to the left of
d(\bar{i})
,
while those of
H(d(\bar{j}_{1})),\ldots,H(d(\bar{j}_{q}))
lie to the right. There
are
d(\bar{i})-l(i)
dual vertices in
\mathrm{span}(i)
to the left of
d(\bar{i})
, and

r(i)-d(\bar{i})-1
to the right. Thus
d(\bar{i})-l(i)=\sum_{s=1}^{p}h(d(\bar{i}_{s}))
, and this is precisely the left hook length
h_{L}(i)
in
F
.
The expression for
h_{R}(i)
follows since
h(i)=h_{L}(i)+h_{R}(i)+1
.
∎

Example 2.3
.

, we have
d(\bar{4})=7
and hook

of

contains all dual (red) vertices lying within

For fixed
n\geq 1
, let
cda
denote the composite map
{\textsc{cay}}\circ{\textsc{dual}}\circ{\textsc{arch}}:\mathcal{F}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n}
, where again

is interpreted to act on the skeletal trees of dual diagrams. We now show that this bijection satisfies the conditions described by Theorem

, thus proving the theorem in the case

. (Recall that
\mathrm{(co)semiarea}_{k}
coincides with
\mathrm{(co)area}
in this case.)

Theorem 2.4
.

The map
{\textsc{cda}}:\mathcal{F}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n}
is a bijection, and for
F={\textsc{cda}}(f)
we have

\mathrm{area}(f)=\mathrm{maj}(F)
and
\mathrm{coarea}(f)=\mathrm{comaj}(F)
. Moreover, if

f=(a_{1}\,b_{1})\cdots(a_{n}\,b_{n})
then
h(i)=a_{i}-b_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, where
h(i)
is the hook length at vertex
i
in
F
.

Proof.

We have already seen that
{\textsc{cda}}\,:\,\mathcal{F}_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n}
is a bijection.

Let
f=(a_{1}\,b_{1})\cdots(a_{n}\,b_{n})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
,
A={\textsc{arch}}(f)
and
D={\textsc{dual}}(A)
. By construction we have
l(i)=a_{i}
and
r(i)=b_{i}
for each arch
i
of
A
, so Lemma
2.2
gives
h(i)=b_{i}-a_{i}
for all
i
.
Note that every non-root vertex of
D
is the endpoint

of a unique edge
\bar{i}
. Thus
\{d(\bar{1}),\ldots,d(\bar{n})\}
is a rearrangement of
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
. With Lemma

we find that

\mathrm{maj}(F)=\sum_{i\in[1,n]}h_{L}(i)=\sum_{i\in[1,n]}(d(\bar{i})-l(i))=\binom{n}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=\mathrm{area}(f).

The proof that
\mathrm{coarea}(f)=\mathrm{comaj}(D)
is similar.
∎

3.
The main bijection for general

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
in the general case, building on the constructions used to prove Theorem

.

Geometrically, our bijection from
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}
to
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
is a natural extension
of the map
cda
described in Section
2
. Whereas

correspond with planar embeddings of edge-labelled

n
edges (

k
-factorizations

correspond with embeddings of
k
-cacti having
n
labelled
polygons. We call such configurations

.
Duals can be constructed analogously to the case
k=1
, and the skeletal

k
-cacti of the duals then correspond with a
k
-forests
F\in\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
as
described in Section
1.4
. This generalized mapping
f\mapsto F

is illustrated in Figure
7
.

having the bijection

at its core. This allows direct use of Theorem

.

3.1.
Defining the Bijection

Recall from Section

the function
{\textsc{lower}}:\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{kn}
that replaces each factor of

with its lower decomposition. As noted there,

is injective for all

but not surjective for

.
Let

be its image and let

.

Explicitly, a
k
-factorization

(7)

f=(a_{1}^{0}\,\cdots\,a_{1}^{k})\cdots(a_{n}^{0}\,\cdots\,a_{n}^{k})\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{k},

decomposes into the 1-factorization

For notational convenience we shall label the factors of
{f^{*}}
with a linearly ordered set different from
[1,kn]
. We instead use

(8)

S=\{1_{1},1_{2},\ldots,1_{k},2_{1},2_{2},\ldots,2_{k},\ldots,n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k}\},

ordered as they are presented above; that is,

if and only if

or

and

. These labels are applied in left-to-right order to the factors of

, so

gets label

.

Example 3.1
.

A factorization
f\in\mathcal{F}_{10}^{2}
and its decomposition

Proposition 3.3
.

Let

\mathrm{cosemiarea}_{k}(f)=k\cdot\mathrm{comaj}(F)

a_{j}^{k}-a_{j}^{0}=k\cdot h(j)
for

h(j)
is the hook length at
j
in
F

Proof.

Let
{f^{*}}={\textsc{lower}}(f)
. Then by definition of area/coarea we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{area}({f^{*}})=\binom{kn}{2}-k\sum_{j=1}^{n}a^{0}_{j},

\displaystyle\mathrm{coarea}({f^{*}})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j}^{i}-1)-\binom{kn}{2},

and from the definition of
\mathrm{area}_{k}
and
\mathrm{coarea}_{k}

(11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{area}_{k}(f)=\mathrm{area}({f^{*}})-n\binom{k}{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{cosemiarea}_{k}(f)=\mathrm{coarea}({f^{*}}).

Now let
{F^{*}}={\textsc{cda}}({f^{*}})
and
F={\textsc{join}}({F^{*}})
. Theorem
2.4
gives

(12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{area}({f^{*}})=\mathrm{maj}({F^{*}}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{coarea}({f^{*}})=\mathrm{comaj}({F^{*}}),

which together with (

) reduces the first claims of the proposition to the identities

(13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{maj}({F^{*}})=n\binom{k}{2}+k\cdot\mathrm{maj}_{k}(F),

\displaystyle\mathrm{comaj}({F^{*}})=k\cdot\mathrm{comaj}(F).

These will be verified below in Lemma
3.5
, following an example. For the final claim of the proposition, observe that the
factor
(a_{j}^{0}\,a_{j}^{k})
of
{f^{*}}
has label
j_{k}
, so Theorem
2.4

implies
a_{j}^{k}-a_{j}^{0}
is the size of the hook at vertex
j_{k}
in
{F^{*}}
. The
definition of
join
makes clear that this is
k
times the size of the hook
at vertex
j
of
F
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Identities (
13
) are valid for any

{F^{*}}\in\mathcal{R}^{*}_{kn}
and
F={\textsc{join}}({F^{*}})\in\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}
.

Proof.

We begin with a general observation regarding weighted
k
-forests.
Let
W
be such a forest with edge colouring
\kappa:E(W)\rightarrow[k-1]
, and let edge
(x,y)\in E(W)
be such that

(A)

x
and
y
have consecutive labels, with
x>y
, and

(B)

\kappa(x,y)=0
.

Let
W^{\prime}
be the weighted
k
-forest obtained by
collapsing
edge
(x,y)
as follows:

(1)

replace each edge
(y,v)
with an edge
(x,v)
coloured
\kappa(y,v)+1
,

(2)

increase
\mathrm{wt}(x)
by
\mathrm{wt}(y)
, and

(3)

remove edge
(x,y)
and vertex
y
.

This is illustrated in Figure
9
. Our interest in this process arises from the fact that the
join
map can be viewed as iteratively collapsing edges of a weighted
k
-forest.

Figure 9.
Collapsing edge
(x,y)
of a weighted
k
-forest. Each edge
(y,v)
with colour
\kappa
is replaced by an edge
(x,v)
with colour
\kappa+1
. The weight of vertex
x
on the right is the sum of the weights of vertices
x
and
y
on the left.

Observe that collapsing
(x,y)
leaves
\bar{h}_{L}(u)
and
\bar{h}_{R}(u)
unchanged
for all vertices
u\neq x,y
, whereas
\bar{h}_{L}(x)
and
\bar{h}_{R}(x)

increase by
\bar{h}_{L}(y)-\bar{h}(y)
and
\bar{h}_{R}(y)
, respectively, as a result
of Condition (A).
The net changes to major and comajor indices are therefore

(\bar{h}_{L}(y)-\bar{h}(y))-\bar{h}_{L}(y)=-\bar{h}(y)
and
\bar{h}_{R}(y)-\bar{h}_{R}(y)=0
,
respectively, where the terms
-\bar{h}_{L}(y)
and
-\bar{h}_{R}(y)

account for removal of
y
. That is,

(14)

\overline{\mathrm{maj}}(W^{\prime})=\overline{\mathrm{maj}}(W)-\bar{h}(y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\mathrm{comaj}}(W^{\prime})=\overline{\mathrm{comaj}}(W).

Using Condition (B) we also have

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{chr}}_{k}(W^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathrm{chr}}_{k}(W)-\underbrace{\kappa(x,y)}_{=0}\bar{h}(y)-\sum_{e=(y,v)}\kappa(e)\bar{h}(v)+\sum_{e=(y,v)}(\kappa(e)+1)\bar{h}(v)

(15)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathrm{chr}}_{k}(W)+\sum_{e=(y,v)}\bar{h}(v)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathrm{chr}}_{k}(W)+\bar{h}(y)-\mathrm{wt}(y).

Together, (
14
) and (
3.2
) give

(16)

\overline{\mathrm{maj}}_{k}(W^{\prime})=\overline{\mathrm{maj}}_{k}(W)-\mathrm{wt}(y).

We are now prepared to prove the lemma. Let the vertices of
{F^{*}}
be labelled by
S
as defined
in (
8
).
Assign colour 0 to each edge of
{F^{*}}
and weight 1 to each of its vertices to transform it into a weighted
k
-forest

. Clearly
\mathrm{maj}({F^{*}})=\overline{\mathrm{maj}}_{k}(W^{*})
and
\mathrm{comaj}({F^{*}})=\overline{\mathrm{comaj}}(W^{*})
.

Transform
W^{*}
by iteratively collapsing edges
(1_{2},1_{1}),(1_{3},1_{2}),\ldots,(1_{k},1_{k-1})
in that order. This yields a sequence of forests
W^{*}=W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{k}
such that vertex
1_{i}
has weight
i
in
W_{i}
. Moreover, (
14
) and (
16
) give

\overline{\mathrm{comaj}}(W_{k})=\overline{\mathrm{comaj}}(W^{*})=\mathrm{comaj}(F^{*})

and

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{maj}}_{k}(W_{k})=\overline{\mathrm{maj}}_{k}(W^{*})-(1+2+\cdots+(k-1))=\mathrm{maj}({F^{*}})-\binom{k}{2}

Continue to iterate by collapsing edges

.
When complete, this results in a weighted

{W}
on vertices

such that
