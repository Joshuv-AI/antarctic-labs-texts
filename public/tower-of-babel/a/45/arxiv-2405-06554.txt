Tradeoffs among Action Taking Policies Matter in Active Sequential Multi-Hypothesis Testing: the Optimal Error Exponent Region
Chia-Yu Hsu, I-Hsiang Wang

Abstract
Reliability of sequential hypothesis testing can be greatly improved when the decision maker is given the freedom to adaptively take an action that determines the distribution of the current collected sample. Such advantage of sampling adaptivity has been realized since Chernoff’s seminal paper in 1959 [ 1 ] . While a large body of works have explored and investigated the gain of adaptivity, in the general multiple-hypothesis setting, the fundamental limits of individual error probabilities have not been fully understood. In particular, in the asymptotic regime as the expected stopping time tends to infinity, the error exponents are only characterized in specific cases, such as that of the total error probability. In this paper, we consider a general setup of active sequential multiple-hypothesis testing where at each time slot, a temporally varying subset of data sources (out of a known set) emerges from which the decision maker can select to collect samples, subject to a family of expected selection budget constraints. The selection of sources, understood as the “action” at each time slot, is constrained in a predefined action space. At the end of each time slot, the decision maker either decides to make the inference on the M M hypotheses, or continues to observe the data sources for the next time slot. The optimal tradeoffs among M ⁡ ( M − 1 ) M(M-1) types of error exponents are characterized. A companion asymptotically optimal test that strikes the balance between exploration and exploitation is proposed to achieve any target error exponents within the region. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time in the literature to identify such tradeoffs among error exponents in active sequential hypothesis testing, and it uncovers the tension among different action taking policies even in the basic setting of Chernoff [ 1 ] .

Introduction

Adaptivity in taking samples from different distributions is known to greatly enhance the performance of sequential statistical inference. The intuition is that different distributions have different levels of informativeness of the inference target, and the most informative one may vary with the ground truth.
The decision maker builds up more confidence towards the ground truth as it collects more samples, so it should choose the most informative distribution at the moment accordingly.
As for hypothesis testing, the paradigm is called active sequential hypothesis testing

It can be understood as the setting where the decision maker selects a particular data source out of multiple ones to collect a sample, each of which corresponds to a possible action.
This paradigm finds extensive application in various fields such as spectrum sensing, crowdsourced data analytics, etc., and motivated from these applications, there have been extensions that incorporate a variety of expected selection budget constraints

Our primary contribution lies in the characterization of the optimal individual error exponent region, revealing the
existence of tradeoffs among certain groups of individual error exponents

We show that the achievable error exponents can be represented as linear combinations of Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergences, each of which corresponds to a particular set of data sources’ joint level of informativeness for distinguishing one hypothesis from another. The coefficients in the linear combinations correspond to the expected selection frequencies of data sources under the given policy and turn out to be the key design choices. In our characterization, these selection frequencies are indexed naturally by the taken action and the available subset of data sources.
The optimal error exponent region is the union of these
M(M-1)

Leveraging the fact that these constraints are all affine, we further note that the optimal error exponent region is a convex set.
The characterized region turns out to be a direct product of
M

(M-1)

M-1

To the best of our knowledge, such tradeoffs among
M(M-1)

1

The characterization of the optimal region also helps elucidate the gain of adaptivity in terms of error exponents, which has not been fully understood in the literature. To do so, we analyze the optimal achievable region of error exponents for
non-adaptive

To characterize the optimal error exponent region, we divide the proof of our main theorem into two parts: the converse part and the achievability part.
The converse part of our main theorem is proved by standard arguments that involves data processing inequality and Doob’s Optional Stopping Theorem, similar to that in

The stopping and inference rules of our test mainly follow a family of multi-hypothesis sequential probability ratio tests (MSPRT) proposed in

For the action taking policy, since the constraints are all in expectation, it is natural to use a randomized one, and hence the design boils down to choosing the action taking

To achieve a given tuple of error exponents in the region, the coefficients in the linear combinations of the KL divergences serve as the guidance for setting these action taking probabilities in the randomized policy. As suggested in the literature

[

Literature review

The study of asymptotic fundamental limits of hypothesis testing problems could be traced back to Hoeffding
[
10
]
and Blahut
[
11
]
, where the optimal tradeoff between the type-I and the type-II error exponents was characterized for fixed-length binary hypothesis testing.
For sequential binary hypothesis testing, based on Wald and Wolfowitz’s seminal result
[
12
]
, it can be shown (see e.g.
[
13
, Chapter 16.3]
) that under the expected stopping time constraints, the type-I and type-II error exponents can be
maximized simultaneously
, thereby eliminating the tradeoff observed in the fixed-length setting and demonstrating the gain of sequentiality (variable-length sample size) in error exponents.
For multiple hypothesis testing, the optimal individual error exponent region in the fixed-length setting was characterized by Tuncel
[
14
]
, while for the the sequential setting, one can follow a similar recipe in
[
13
, Chapter 16.3]
and derive the optimal error exponent region based on Dragalin
et al.
’s work
[
9
]
. It can then be shown that sequentiality also eliminates the tradeoffs among the individual error exponents.

The focus of the works above is on a
passive
setting for decision making, where the decision maker collects data from a single source throughout the entire process. An
active
setting with multiple sources was introduced by Chernoff
[
1
]
, where the decision maker can dynamically select data sources, either adaptively or non-adaptively, thereby influencing the distribution of the collected data samples. Following Chernoff’s seminal work
[
1
]
, various extensions have been made
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, and they all take a similar problem formulation as Chernoff
[
1
]
: minimize a cost related to the expected stopping time subject to constant constraints on certain kinds of error probabilities, such as a constant total error probability
[
2
]
, a constant maximal error probability
[
1
,
7
]
, constant type-I and type-II error probabilities
[
5
,
6
,
4
]
, and constant decision risks
[
3
]
.
Meanwhile, a comprehensive study by Naghshvar and Javidi
[
15
]
compared various settings, including fixed-length versus sequential (variable-length), passive versus active, and non-adaptive versus adaptive decision making. The problem formulation is also to minimize a cost related to the expected stopping time under a constant total error probability constraint
[
15
]
, and it is shown that combining sequentiality and adaptivity yields the most favorable performance.

The findings of these existing works indicate that sequentiality eliminates tradeoffs among error exponents when reformulated as an error probability minimization problem subject to expected stopping time constraints, and that adaptivity further improves error exponents while still avoiding tradeoffs.
Interestingly when it comes to individual error exponents, our result reveals that tradeoffs among certain groups of
individual
error exponents
may exist
even for sequential adaptive tests, a phenomenon that has not been observed in the literature of active sequential multi-hypothesis testing.

3
]
, where all data sources are available at each time instance, but only one data source can be selected at each time slot, and no budget constraints are imposed. In our notations, it corresponds to the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left[1:n\right]\right\},

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[1:n\right]\ \text{with probability $1$},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\left\{\emptyset,\{1\},\{2\},...\{n\}\right\}.

Notably, since there is no budget constraint (
\mathcal{I}=\emptyset
) and the only constraint is on the expected stopping time, the optimal action taking policy will choose
A_{t}=\emptyset
with probability
0
.

Under this setup, since there is no need to describe random availability and the selection is constrained to a single source, the notations in the statistical model can be simplified. Let us specialize the formulation to this setup and introduce corresponding simplified notations to better present the result. Note that they are used only in this section. In particular, for any hypothesis
m\in\left[0:M-1\right]
and data source
j\in\left[1:n\right]
, if the true hypothesis is
m
, the data source
j
(if it is selected) generates samples according to the distribution

P_{m,j}\equiv P_{X_{j};m},

the marginal distribution of data source
j
under hypothesis
m
.

Before presenting the optimal exponent region, let us first provide an intuitive argument on a simple example to illustrate why the tradeoff among individual error exponents exists and how it looks like.
Suppose there are just two data sources (
n=2
). First note that the error exponents depend largely on the
selection frequency
(normalized by the expected stopping time constraint
T
) of the sources get selected. Let
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
denote the selection frequencies of source
1
and
2
respectively (
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\geq 0,\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}=1
). The achievable error exponents should be those satisfying

\displaystyle e_{m|\theta}\leq\beta_{1}{D}\left(P_{m,1}\,\middle\|P_{\theta,1}\right)+\beta_{2}{D}\left(P_{m,2}\,\middle\|P_{\theta,2}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\,m,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right],\ m\neq\theta,

and the right-hand-side could be interpreted as the average level of distinguishability of hypothesis
m
from
\theta
over the two data sources.
The decision maker may choose the best selection frequencies to maximize the individual error exponent. However, the maximizer may be different across different
m,\theta
.
For example, suppose data source
1
is the most informative for distinguishing hypothesis
0
from hypothesis
1
, that is, the KL divergence of
P_{0,1}
from
P_{1,1}
is larger than that of
P_{0,2}
from
P_{1,2}
, that is,
{D}\left(P_{0,1}\,\middle\|P_{1,1}\right)>{D}\left(P_{0,2}\,\middle\|P_{1,2}\right)
.
Meanwhile, data source
2
is the most informative for distinguishing hypothesis
1
from hypothesis
0
, that is,
{D}\left(P_{1,1}\,\middle\|P_{0,1}\right)<{D}\left(P_{1,2}\,\middle\|P_{0,2}\right)
. Then, to maximize
e_{0|1}
, one would choose data source
1
over source
2
, that is,
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(1,0)
, and to maximize
e_{1|0}
, one would do the other way around, that is,
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0,1)
.
To this end, it seems impossible to achieve the best individual error exponents
simultaneously
. Interestingly, contrary to the intuition, in the binary hypothesis setting, both
e_{0|1}={D}\left(P_{0,1}\,\middle\|P_{1,1}\right)
and
e_{1|0}={D}\left(P_{1,2}\,\middle\|P_{0,2}\right)
can be achieved simultaneously by the following
adaptive
approach proposed by Chernoff
[
1
]
: at each time instance, the decision maker first compute the likelihood functions of the two hypotheses based on the samples collected so far, and it chooses source
1
if hypothesis
0
is more likely, and source
2
is hypothesis
2
is more likely.

Chernoff’s approach for the general
M
-ary hypothesis setting can be described as follows
[
1
]
: the decision maker can design
M
selection frequency pairs
\big\{(\beta^{m}_{1},\beta^{m}_{2})\big\}_{m=0}^{M-1}
, understood as
M
different selection policies. If the likelihood of hypothesis
m\in\left[0:M-1\right]
is the largest, that is, the maximum likelihood estimate
\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}=m
, it selects the data sources according to the corresponding frequency pair
\bm{\beta}^{m}\equiv(\beta^{m}_{1},\beta^{m}_{2})
, indexed by the hypothesis
m
. However, unlike in the binary hypothesis testing setting where there is a single optimal
\bm{\beta}^{m}
for each
m\in\{0,1\}
(in the above example, the optimal
\bm{\beta}^{0}=(1,0)
and the optimal
\bm{\beta}^{1}=(0,1)
), there may not exist such an optimal
\bm{\beta}^{m}
. The reason is that there are multiple individual error exponents correspond to the same
m
. For example, consider hypothesis
m=0
. Again, suppose data source
1
is the most informative for distinguishing hypothesis
0
from hypothesis
1
, that is,
{D}\left(P_{0,1}\,\middle\|P_{1,1}\right)>{D}\left(P_{0,2}\,\middle\|P_{1,2}\right)
. Meanwhile, data source
2
is the most informative for distinguishing hypothesis
0
from hypothesis
2
, that is,
{D}\left(P_{0,1}\,\middle\|P_{2,1}\right)<{D}\left(P_{0,2}\,\middle\|P_{2,2}\right)
. Then, to maximize
e_{0|1}
, one would choose data source
1
over source
2
, that is,
\bm{\beta}^{0}=(1,0)
, and to maximize
e_{0|2}
, one would do the other way around, that is,
\bm{\beta}^{0}=(0,1)
. This time, the tension in choosing
\bm{\beta}^{0}
immediately shows up, and it is hence impossible to achieve the best individual error exponents
e_{0|1}
amd
e_{0|2}

simultaneously
. Note that this tension cannot be mitigated by Chernoff’s approach, as the two individual error exponents corresponds to the same
m
.

The above discussion illustrates the structure of tradeoffs among the individual error exponents: there is no tradeoff among
M
error exponent
(M-1)
-tuples

\displaystyle\big(e_{0|1},e_{0|2},\ldots,e_{0|M-1}\big),\ \big(e_{1|0},e_{1|2},\ldots,e_{1|M-1}\big),

(3)

\displaystyle\ldots,\ \big(e_{M-1|0},e_{M-1|2},\ldots,e_{M-1|M-2}\big),

while tradeoff exists among
M-1
individual error exponents
\big\{e_{m|\theta}\,\big|\,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}\big\}
for each
m\in\left[0:M-1\right]
.
The following theorem summarizes the optimal error exponent region for Chernoff’s setup with general number of sources.

Theorem 1
(The optimal error exponent region in Chernoff’s setup)

For any
n\geq 1
and
M\geq 2
, the optimal achievable error exponent region for individual error probabilities in Chernoff’s setup
[
1
]
is given by

\zeta=\bigtimes\limits_{m=0}^{M-1}\,\left\{\bigcup\limits_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right\},

(4)

where
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the
n
-dimensional probability simplex,

\bm{\beta}^{m}\triangleq\big(\beta^{m}_{1},\beta^{m}_{2},\ldots,\beta^{m}_{n}\big)\in\mathcal{P}_{n}

(5)

denotes a tuple of selection frequencies of
n
data sources, and
\forall\,m\in\left[0:M-1\right]
, the region

\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
is defined on the top of the next page.

\displaystyle\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\triangleq\left\{\big\{e_{m|\theta}\,\big|\,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}\big\}\,\middle|\,e_{m|\theta}\leq e^{*}_{m|\theta}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\quad\forall\,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}\right\},

\displaystyle e^{*}_{m|\theta}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta^{m}_{j}{D}\left(P_{m,j}\,\middle\|P_{\theta,j}\right)\quad\forall\,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}.

(6)

Theorem
1
is a special case of the general version (Theorem
2
in the next section). Note that the direct product in (
4
) arises because the tradeoff only appears among the
M-1
individual error exponents in
\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
for each
m\in\left[0:M-1\right]
. In words, for each declared hypothesis
m
, one may not find an optimal
\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
to maximize all
M-1
bounds
\big\{e^{*}_{m|\theta}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\,\big|\,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}\big\}
simultaneously, leading to the tradeoff among these
M-1
individual error exponents.

Theorem
1
also recovers the asymptotic limit of many different performance metrics in the literature.
For example,
[
1
,
3
,
2
]
focused on a coarser performance metric, namely, the decision risk of declaring the decision as hypothesis
m
:

\mathrm{R}_{m}\triangleq\sum_{\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}}\rho_{\theta}\pi_{m|\theta}\quad\forall\,m\in\left[0:M-1\right],

(7)

where
\rho_{\theta}
is the prior probability for hypothesis
\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]
.
The optimal exponentially decaying rates of the decision risks, denoted as
\{\gamma_{m}\mid m\in\left[0:M-1\right]\}
, can then be characterized from our main result as a simple corollary given below.

Corollary 1
(Exponents of decision risks)

The optimal exponent region of the decision risks consists of non-negative
M
-tuples
(\gamma_{0},...,\gamma_{M-1})
satisfying

\gamma_{m}\leq\max_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{P}^{n}}\min_{\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}}e^{*}_{m|\theta}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\quad\forall\,m\in\left[0:M-1\right],

where
\{e^{*}_{m|\theta}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\}
are defined in (
6
).

Note that Corollary
1
can also be derived from the respective results in
[
1
,
3
,
2
]
. Clearly there are no tradeoffs among these
\gamma_{m}
’s due to the product form shown in (
4
).

Meanwhile, suppose we take the error probabilities of individual hypotheses

\pi_{\theta}\triangleq\sum_{m\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{\theta\}}\pi_{m|\theta}\quad\forall\,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]

as performance metrics. We then denote the achievable exponentially decaying rates of the error probability
\pi_{\theta}
as
e_{\theta}
for any
\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]
. The following corollary of Theorem
1
is also immediate.

Corollary 2
(Exponents of error probabilities of individual hypotheses)

The optimal exponent region of the error probabilities of individual hypotheses consists of non-negative
M
-tuples
(e_{0},...,e_{M-1})
satisfying

e_{\theta}=\min_{m\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{\theta\}}\{e_{m|\theta}\}\quad\forall\,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]

for some
M(M-1)
-tuples
\big\{e_{m|\theta}\,\big|\,m\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{\theta\}\big\}\in\zeta
, where
\zeta
is given in (
4
).

In contrast to the decision risks, there exist tradeoffs among the
M
error exponents for error probabilities of individual hypotheses, and this has not been studied in the literature
[
1
,
3
,
2
]
.

III-B

Gain of sequentiality and adaptivity

In the following, we illustrate the gain of sequentiality and adaptivity by comparing the error exponent regions in the non-sequential non-adaptive
[
14
]
, sequential non-adaptive
[
9
]
, and sequential adaptive settings.
For fair comparison, we extend the results in the first two settings to the case where non-adaptive sampling from multiple data sources is allowed.
First, we demonstrate that the optimal error exponent region of the sequential adaptive setting contains that of the sequential non-adaptive setting, which in turn contains that of the non-sequential non-adaptive setting. Then, we provide a numerical example to illustrate the gain of sequentiality and adaptivity for the case
M=3,n=2
. For the sake of illustration, as in Corollary
2
, we take the error probabilities of individual hypotheses as performance metrics.

In the non-sequential non-adaptive setting
[
14
]
, we allow the decision maker to collect
T
data samples independently from multiple data sources. The sampling proportions are specified by a fixed
\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, the
n
-dimensional probability simplex, chosen prior to data collection. For instance, if there are two data sources and
\bm{\beta}=[0.3,0.7]
, the decision maker will draw
0.3T
samples from the first source and
0.7T
samples from the second source.
By the independence among samples and the non-adaptivity of sampling, it is straightforward to derive the optimal error exponent region based on Tuncel’s result
[
14
]
, as shown below: let
\zeta^{\text{Tuncel}}
denote the optimal region, then

\zeta^{\text{Tuncel}}=\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}:\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\zeta^{\text{Tuncel}}(\bm{\beta})

where
\zeta^{\text{Tuncel}}(\bm{\beta})
is defined on the top of the next page.

\zeta^{\text{Tuncel}}(\bm{\beta})\triangleq\left\{\big\{e_{m|\theta}\,\big|\,m,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right],m\neq\theta\big\}\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}\forall\,P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}^{(j)},\ j\in[1:n],\ \exists\,\theta\in[0:M-1]\ \text{such that}\\
e_{m|\theta}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}{D}\left(P_{j}\,\middle\|P_{\theta,j}\right)\quad\forall\,m\in[0:M-1]\setminus\{\theta\}\end{array}\right\},

(8)

where
\mathcal{P}^{(j)}
denotes the set of all possible distributions of the alphabet of the
j
-th source,
j=1,2,\ldots,n
.

In the sequential non-adaptive setting, the decision maker selects an action
A_{t}
at each time slot
t\geq 1
independently of the collected information
F_{t-1}
, where
A_{t}
and
F_{t-1}
are defined in Definition
1
. Due to the lack of adaptivity, the decision maker can no longer leverage Chernoff’s approach to eliminate the tradeoffs among the
M
error exponent
(M-1)
-tuples in (
3
). Hence, the direct product in the achievable individual error exponent region (
4
) disappears, and it becomes

\zeta^{\text{NA}}=\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}:\,\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\zeta^{\text{NA}}(\bm{\beta}),

(9)

where
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the
n
-dimensional probability simplex and
\zeta^{\text{NA}}(\bm{\beta})
is defined on the top of the next page.

\displaystyle\zeta^{\text{NA}}(\bm{\beta})\triangleq\big\{\{e_{m|\theta}\}\,\big|\,e_{m|\theta}\leq e^{*}_{m|\theta}(\bm{\beta})\quad\forall\,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right],\ \forall\,m\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{\theta\}\big\},

\displaystyle e^{*}_{m|\theta}(\bm{\beta})\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{j}{D}\left(P_{m,j}\,\middle\|P_{\theta,j}\right)\quad\forall\,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right],\ \forall\,m\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{\theta\}\quad\text{(the same as
\eqref{eq:exponents_bd})}.

(10)

Here the superscript
(\cdot)^{\text{NA}}
in the notation means “non-adaptive.”

Comparing
\zeta^{\text{Tuncel}}
and
\zeta^{\text{NA}}
, it can be immediately noted that
\zeta^{\text{Tuncel}}\subseteq\zeta^{\text{NA}}
,
since for any given
\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, the largest possible
e_{m|\theta}
in (
10
) can be shown to be in
e^{*}_{m|\theta}(\bm{\beta})
in (
10
) using a similar argument as in
[
14
]
.
Meanwhile, comparing
\zeta^{\text{NA}}
and the region
\zeta
characterized in Theorem
1
(the sequential adaptive setting),
it can be observed that

\zeta^{\text{NA}}(\bm{\beta})=\bigtimes\limits_{m=0}^{M-1}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}),

and hence
\zeta^{\text{NA}}\subseteq\zeta
.
As a result, we have
\zeta^{\text{Tuncel}}\subseteq\zeta^{\text{NA}}\subseteq\zeta
, demonstrating the gain of sequentiality and adaptivity.

IV
Main results in the General Setup

In this section, we first present the optimal error exponent region for the general setup described in Section
II
.
Compared to the vanilla setup in Chernoff’s work
[
1
]
, the generalized setup further incorporates temporal variation of available data sources, the possibility to access multiple sources at each time slot, and general linear budget constraints. Nevertheless, the structure of the main theorem (Theorem
2
) is similar to that of Theorem
1
. Accordingly, we interpret Theorem
2
using the same insights as those obtained in Section
III
for Theorem
1
. We then discuss about the novelty and implications of the main theorem, emphasizing its contributions compared with existing literatures. Finally, we close this section with an overview of our proposed asymptotically optimal scheme.

IV-A

Tradeoffs among individual error exponents

For the characterization of the optimal error exponents, the following two technical assumptions are imposed.

Assumption 1

There exists
L<\infty
such that

\sup_{m\in\left[0:M-1\right]}\sup_{\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\theta}}\left\lvert\log\frac{P_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n};\theta}(\mathbf{x})}{P_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n};m}(\mathbf{x})}\right\rvert\leq L,

where
\mathcal{X}_{\theta}
denotes the support of the joint distribution
P_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n};\theta}
,
\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]
.

Assumption 2

For any two hypotheses
m,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]
,
\theta\neq m
,

\exists\,z\in\mathcal{Z},\ \exists\,a\in\mathcal{A}\quad\text{such that}\quad{D}\left(P^{a,z}_{m}\,\middle\|P^{a,z}_{\theta}\right)>0.

Assumption
1
is used in our achievability and converse proof and commonly made in the literature
[
2
,
1
,
3
,
7
,
8
]
. Assumption
2
is needed for proving the achievability, and further discussion will be made in Section
V
. Roughly speaking, it implies that for any two different hypotheses, there exists a feasible set of data sources that can distinguish one from the other and vice versa. Note that these two assumptions are also needed for Theorem
1
.
However, to better serve the purpose of Section
III
, we do not state them explicitly there.

Before formally stating the theorem, let us first note that when generalizing the result from Chernoff’s setup to the setup in Section
II
, the definition of the selection frequency tuple and its constraint given in (
5
) inevitably require modification and extension. First of all, since the decision maker is faced with a temporally varying set of available data sources
Z_{t}
(governed by
P_{Z}
) and it can select a subset of sources
A_{t}
(recall
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
), one should focus on the selection frequency of each “action”
a\in\mathcal{A}
when the available set of sources is
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Collecting all of them, with slight abuse of notation, we shall now use

\bm{\beta}\triangleq\Big\{\beta_{a,z}\,\Big|\,a\in\mathcal{A},z\in\mathcal{Z}\Big\}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{Z}|}_{+}

to denote a selection frequency tuple, where each component
\beta_{a,z}
stands for the frequency that the selected set of data sources is
a
and the available set of sources is
z
.

Since we consider budget constraints and temporarily available data sources, unlike Chernoff’s setup where the selection frequency tuple
\bm{\beta}
is only required to lie in the probability simplex
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, additional constraints on
\bm{\beta}
that correspond to budget and temporal availability emerge naturally.
For each data source
j\in\left[1:n\right]
, define

\omega_{j}(\bm{\beta})\triangleq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\beta_{a,z}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{j\in a\cap z\right\}},

(11)

standing for the frequency with which source
j
is selected during the decision process. Hence, multiplying (
11
) by
T
gives the
expected
number of times that data source
j
is selected in the decision process. Consequently, the budget constraints (
1
) becomes

b_{i}\left(\omega_{1}(\bm{\beta}),\ldots,\omega_{n}(\bm{\beta})\right)\leq r_{i}\quad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{I}.

(12)

Also note that
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\beta_{a,z}
stands for the frequency that the decision maker takes an action when the available set of sources is
z
. Hence, it is obvious that

\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\beta_{a,z}=\alpha_{z}\quad\forall\,z\in\mathcal{Z},

(13)

where the shorthand notation
\alpha_{z}\triangleq P_{Z}(z)
is introduced for convenience.

We are ready to state the main result.

Theorem 2
(The optimal error exponent region in the general setup)

Suppose both Assumption
1
and
2
hold. Define

\textstyle\zeta\triangleq\bigtimes\limits_{m=0}^{M-1}\,\left\{\bigcup\limits_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{C}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right\}

(14)

where

\mathcal{C}\triangleq\left\{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}||\mathcal{Z}|}_{+}\,\middle|\,\text{$\bm{\beta}$ satisfies \eqref{eq:constraints_budget} and \eqref{eq:constraints_availability}}\right\},

(15)

and for each
m\in\left[0:M-1\right]
,
\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
is defined on the top of the next page.

Remark 4

All the intuitions and discussions in Section

Remark 5

Note that the tradeoffs among error exponents largely depend on the constraint set

From the expression, it can be seen that besides the heterogeneity among data sources, parameters of the problem formulation (the action space, the temporal availability, and the budget constraints) also influence the tradeoffs. For instance, if the parameters of the problem formulation allow the decision maker to select all data sources simultaneously at each time slot, there will be no tradeoffs among error exponents.

Remark 6
(Convexity of the optimal error exponent region

The convexity of

is a convex set for any index set

, and the fact that each
\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
is a hyper-rectangle with boundaries being linear functions of
\bm{\beta}^{m}
, that is,
\big\{e^{*}_{m|\theta}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\,\big|\,m\in\left[0:M-1\right],\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}\big\}
.

Remark 7
(Computation of the optimal error exponent region
\zeta
)

The complexity of computing the optimal error exponent region
\zeta
largely depends on the constraint set
\mathcal{C}
as well.
If
\mathcal{C}
is complex, determining
\zeta
might be computationally intractable.
To illustrate, let us first consider the case where
|\mathcal{I}|<\infty
. In this setting,
\mathcal{C}
becomes a convex polytope.
Since each
\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
is a hyper-rectangle with boundaries being linear functions of
\bm{\beta}^{m}
,
the set
\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{C}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
contains only a finite number of extreme points and is a convex polytope. Recall the definition of an extreme point: for any set
A
, a point

is an extreme point of the set
A
if for any
b,c\in A
,
b\neq c
and for any
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the point

satisfies
a\neq\lambda b+(1-\lambda)c
.
Consequently, due to the direct product form of
\zeta
, it is also a convex polytope. This leads to the following equivalent characterization of
\zeta
.

Proposition 1
(
\zeta
is a convex polytope)

\textstyle\zeta=\bigtimes\limits_{m=0}^{M-1}\,\text{Conv}\left\{\text{extr}\left(\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{\mathcal{C}}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right)\right\}

where
V_{\mathcal{C}}
denotes the set of all extreme points of the constraint set
\mathcal{C}
when
\lvert\mathcal{I}\rvert<\infty
.

The proof of the proposition is given in Appendix
-B
. One key implication of Proposition

is that if
\mathcal{C}
has a simple structure (for example, when
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{P}_{n}
, the probability simplex), finding

could be straightforward. In such cases, one can efficiently compute

\textstyle\text{extr}\left(\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{\mathcal{C}}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right)

and apply a off-the-shelf convex hull algorithm to compute
\zeta
. However, when
\mathcal{C}
is more complex, constructing
V_{\mathcal{C}}
may become computationally intractable. As a result, computing

Overview of the proposed test

For the achievability part of Theorem

, from the expression in (

\bm{\beta}^{0},\bm{\beta}^{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}^{M-1}\in\mathcal{C}

Recall that
\mathcal{C}
is the constraint set defined in (

and each tuple

As for the strategy of taking actions, note that
\bm{\beta}^{\theta}
(here we change the indexing convention from

Chernoff’s strategy

that

\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}\in\left[0:M-1\right]
and employ the policy corresponding to
\bm{\beta}^{\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}}
. In our design, in addition to exploitation, we further mix it with a small (vanishing-in-

) probability of

inspired by
[
7
]
, which is a common idea in the literature of sequential decision making problems such as bandits. This probability of exploration and the rate of
uniformly
exploring all sources are carefully chosen to meet the constraints and achieve the target error exponents.

As for the stopping rule and the inference strategy, it follows those in the multiple-hypothesis sequential probability ratio tests (MSPRT)
[
9
]
with thresholds designed judiciously to achieve the target error exponents and meet the constraints. A key statistic here is the cumulative log-likelihood ratio

S_{t,\theta,m}\triangleq\log\frac{\mathsf{P}_{\theta}^{(t)}(F_{t})}{\mathsf{P}_{m}^{(t)}(F_{t})},

where
\mathsf{P}_{\theta}^{(t)}(\cdot)
represents the probability distribution of
F_{t}
, the accumulated information up to the end of time instance
t
(see Definition
1
) under hypothesis
\theta
.
The expression of

will be further expanded in Appendix
-A
.

To this end, let us sketch the proposed test, with more details left in Section
V-A
.
For a given
(\bm{\beta}^{0},\bm{\beta}^{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}^{M-1})
, at each time instance
t\geq 1
, upon
Z_{t}
being revealed, the decision maker carries out the following:

1.

Action taking policy and sample collection:

At the end of time slot
t-1
, find the MLE
\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}
, that is,
\theta
such that
\min_{m\neq\theta}S_{t-1,\theta,m}\geq 0
.
Based on this
\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}
and the corresponding
\bm{\beta}^{\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}}
together with a judiciously designed exploration rate, we then set up a conditional PMF
P_{A_{t}|Z_{t},F_{t-1}}(\cdot|z,f_{t-1})
for randomized action taking.
How to design this conditional PMF is elaborated later.
Subsequently, a random action
a
is drawn from the conditional PMF, and the decision maker collects a tuple of samples
\mathbf{X}_{t}
accordingly.

2.

Stopping and inference rule:

If there exists a hypothesis
\theta
such that
S_{t,\theta,m}
exceeds a set of designed thresholds
\forall\,m\neq\theta
, then stop and declare such a
\theta
as the inferred result.

As for the conditional PMF used for action taking, if hypothesis
\theta
has the largest cumulative log-likelihood ratio
(\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}=\theta)
at the end of time slot
t-1
, then we set

\displaystyle P_{A_{t}|Z_{t},F_{t-1}}(a|z,f_{t-1})=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(1-\epsilon(T))\frac{\beta^{\theta}_{a,z}}{\alpha_{z}}+\epsilon(T)\frac{1}{h\alpha_{z}},&\text{if }a\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\emptyset\}\\[4.30554pt]
1-\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\emptyset\}}P_{A_{t}|Z_{t},F_{t-1}}(a^{\prime}|z,f_{t-1}),&\text{if }a=\emptyset\end{cases}

(16)

where for notational convenience,
\alpha_{z}\triangleq P_{Z}(z)
, the probability of set
z
being available,
\forall\,z\in\mathcal{Z}
. The probability of conducting exploration
\epsilon(T)
is judiciously designed to attain the asymptotic optimality of the test, and it vanishes as

. How to choose

will be elaborated in Section

The
exploration rate

is chosen to be bounded as

\frac{1}{h}\leq\min\left(\ \frac{\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\alpha_{z}}{\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert}\ ,\ \inf_{i\in\mathcal{I},r_{i}>0}\frac{r_{i}}{b_{i}\left(\omega_{1}(\mathbf{1}),\ldots,\omega_{n}(\mathbf{1})\right)}\ \right),

The upper bound of

indicates that the exploration rate cannot exceed the probability of the minimum availability of the data sources and must satisfy the expected budget constraints. Note that in the upper bound of

in (

such that

1/h
times the cardinality of the effective action space to be no more than

for all

, it is still a valid choice to take the denominator of the first term in the “
\min

Before we move on to further technical details of the proposed scheme and the full proof of the achievability, let us close this section with some discussions about our proposed scheme.
Note that our proposed scheme achieves optimality under Assumption
2
, which is less stringent compared to the following assumption that was imposed in Chernoff’s original work
[
1
]
.

Assumption 3

For any two hypotheses
m,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]
,
\theta\neq m
,

\forall\,z\in\mathcal{Z},\ \forall\,a\in\mathcal{A}\quad{D}\left(P^{a,z}_{m}\,\middle\|P^{a,z}_{\theta}\right)>0.

It is interesting to note that under Assumption
3
, it suffices to employ “pure exploitation,” that is,
\epsilon(T)=0
, to achieve optimality. With that being said, technically speaking, exploration is introduced so that achievability can be proved under Assumption
2
.

Note that under Assumption
2
, for any two distinct hypotheses, it is essential to develop techniques that enable the decision maker to select data sources capable of distinguishing between them, thereby ensuring vanishing individual error probabilities.
In the literature, alternative methods have been proposed towards this goal. In
[
2
]
, a scheme with a warm-up phase is proposed, where actions are taken according to a predefined selection frequency before a specified time slot and then is adjusted to follow the maximum likelihood estimate selection afterwards.
In
[
3
]
, an alternative way for exploration is employed, where at preset time instances the decision maker uniformly select a source.
We fail to extend these methods to prove the achievability in our problem setup.
Our proposed method is similar to that in
[
7
]
. The major difference is that the probability of exploration in
[
7
]
is fixed, while our
\epsilon(T)
vanishes with
T
and the decaying rate is judiciously designed. As a result, the method in
[
7
]
is asymptotically sub-optimal, while our proposed method not only effectively controls budget constraints using an intuitive exploration probability but also provides a rigorous proof for achieving the asymptotic optimal error probabilities.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=\kappa_{\theta,T,l}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\tau_{\theta}>k\right)

\displaystyle\leq MB(l)\frac{e^{-\kappa_{\theta,T,l}b(l)}}{1-e^{-b(l)}}

VIII
Conclusion

In this work, we characterize the optimal individual error exponent region in active sequential multiple-hypothesis testing problems with temporally available data sources under linear expected budget constraints (including the expected stopping time constraint). From the characterization of the region, it is found that the tradeoffs among the individual error exponents only appear when there are more than two hypotheses to be distinguished. Such tradeoffs are mainly due to the heterogeneous level of informativeness across data sources in distinguishing a declared hypothesis against other ground truth hypotheses, and they appear even in the most basic setting in
[
1
]
.
Our asymptotically optimal test strikes a balance between exploitation and exploration and enjoys a less strong assumption than Assumption
3
while achieving the optimal error exponents.
The main result is general and encompasses various scenarios in the existing literature, leading to the characterization of performance gains in terms of different metrics due to adaptivity. For future work, one fruitful direction is to extend our results on individual error exponents to settings with unknown distributions of each data sources, namely, the composite hypothesis testing problem, where the state of the art for asymptotic optimality only considers the single performance metric (the maximal error probability, that is, the probability of false detection) versus a total cost
[
18
]
. While the general composite hypothesis setting might be intractable, it may worth the effort to focus on special cases such as the outlier hypothesis testing problem
[
19
,
20
]
.

Z_{s}=z_{s}\end{subarray}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1},Z_{1}=z_{1},A_{1}=a_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{s=2}^{t}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{l}P_{Z}\left(z_{s}\right)P_{A_{s}|Z_{s},F_{s-1}}\left(a_{s}\,\middle|\,z_{s},f_{s-1}\right)\\
\cdot\mathbb{P}_{m}\left(\mathbf{X}_{s}=\mathbf{x}_{s}\,\middle|\,\begin{subarray}{l}F_{s-1}=f_{s-1},Z_{s}=z_{s},\\
A_{s}=a_{s}\end{subarray}\right)\end{array}\!\!\right)

\displaystyle=p_{\text{init}}\prod_{s=2}^{t}P_{Z}\left(z_{s}\right)P_{A_{s}|Z_{s},F_{s-1}}\left(a_{s}\,\middle|\,z_{s},f_{s-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\prod_{s=1}^{t}P^{a_{s},z_{s}}_{m}\left(\mathbf{x}_{s}\right)

(58)

where
p_{\text{init}}\triangleq P_{Z}(z_{1})P_{A_{1}|Z_{1}}(a_{1}\,|\,z_{1})
,
\mathbf{x}_{t}\triangleq\{x_{t,j}\,|\,j\in a_{t}\cap z_{t}\}
, and by convention,
\mathsf{P}_{m}^{(0)}\left(f_{0}\right)\triangleq 0
. Note that (
55
) holds true since
Z_{t}
and
F_{t-1}
are independent
\forall\,t\geq 1
, and (
58
) is due to the conditional independence of samples given the current actions across time slots and the distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{s}
is
P^{a_{s},z_{s}}_{m}
given
A_{s}=a_{s},Z_{s}=z_{s}
under hypothesis
m
,
\forall\,s\geq 1
.

Hence, we have the explicit expression of
S_{t,m,\theta}
:
\forall\,m,\theta\in\left[0:M-1\right]
,
m\neq\theta
,
\forall\,t\geq 1
,

S_{t,m,\theta}=\log{\frac{\mathsf{P}_{m}^{(t)}(F_{t})}{\mathsf{P}_{\theta}^{(t)}(F_{t})}}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\log{\frac{P^{A_{s},Z_{s}}_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{s})}{P^{A_{s},Z_{s}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{s})}}

where
S_{0,m,\theta}\triangleq 0
by convention.

Then, we are ready to show the martingale property. To begin with,
\forall\,t\geq 0
, by the expression of
S_{t,m,\theta}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[e^{S_{t+1,m,\theta}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=e^{S_{t,m,\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[e^{\log{\frac{P^{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}}_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}{P^{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

where
\left\{e^{S_{t,m,\theta}}\,\middle|\,t\geq 0\right\}
is adapted to the natural filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
.
The goal is turned to show for any
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[e^{\log{\frac{P^{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}}_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}{P^{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=1.

Since the distribution of action taking at time slot
t+1
is a function of
F_{t}
and
Z_{t+1}
(see Definition
1
),
we simply switch from conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
to merely conditioning on
F_{t}
without loss of generality in the following steps. So, we have,
\forall\,t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[e^{\log{\frac{P^{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}}_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}{P^{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{l}P_{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}|F_{t}}\left(a,z\,\middle|\,f_{t}\right)\\
\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[e^{\log{\frac{P^{a,z}_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}{P^{a,z}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}}}\,\middle|\,\begin{subarray}{l}A_{t+1}=a,Z_{t+1}=z,\\
F_{t}=f_{t}\end{subarray}\right]\end{array}\!\!\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{l}P_{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}|F_{t}}\left(a,z\,\middle|\,f_{t}\right)\\
\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{P^{a,z}_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}{P^{a,z}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}\,\middle|\,\begin{subarray}{l}A_{t+1}=a,Z_{t+1}=z,\\
F_{t}=f_{t}\end{subarray}\right]\end{array}\!\!\right).

Subsequently, since the random sample set
\mathbf{X}_{t+1}
follows the marginal distribution
P^{a,z}_{\theta}
under a given
A_{t+1}=a
and
Z_{z+1}=z
, and the samples are conditionally independent given the current action across time slots, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{P^{a,z}_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}{P^{a,z}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}\,\middle|\,A_{t+1}=a,Z_{t+1}=z,F_{t}=f_{t}\right]=1

by Assumption
1
.

To complete the proof, we can conclude,
\forall\,t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[e^{\log{\frac{P^{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}}_{m}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}{P^{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}_{t+1})}}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}P_{A_{t+1},Z_{t+1}|F_{t}}\left(a,z\,\middle|\,f_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\alpha_{z}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}P_{A_{t+1}|Z_{t+1},F_{t}}\left(a\,\middle|\,z,f_{t}\right)=1

where the last equality holds by Definition
1
and the stochastic model of temporally available data sources in Section
II-A
.
∎

-B

Proof of Proposition
1

To prove Proposition
1
, due to the direct product form of the region
\zeta
, it is sufficient to show that,
\forall\,m\in\left[0:M-1\right]
,

\textstyle\bigcup\nolimits_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{C}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})=\text{Conv}\left\{\text{extr}\left(\bigcup\nolimits_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right)\right\}

Note that for notational simplicity, we let
\underline{x}\triangleq\left\{x_{i}\,\middle|\,i\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}\right\}
and in the following proof, we just consider a fixed index
m\in\left[0:M-1\right]
without loss of generality. Additionally, the definition of an extreme point is that, for any set
A
, a point
a\in A
is an extreme point of the set
A
if for any
b,c\in A
,
b\neq c
and for any
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the point
a
satisfies
a\neq\lambda b+(1-\lambda)c
.

Direction “LHS
\subseteq
RHS"

For any
\underline{x}\in\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{C}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
, there exists a
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{m}\in\mathcal{C}
and some
\eta_{1},...,\eta_{M-1}\in\left[0,1\right]
such that

x_{i}=\eta_{i}e^{*}_{m|i}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{m})\quad\forall\,i\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}

where
e^{*}_{m|i}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{m})
is defined in Theorem
2
.

Moreover, since the constraint set
\mathcal{C}
is clearly a convex polytope as
|\mathcal{I}|<\infty
, we assume that
V_{c}=\{\tilde{\underline{v}}_{1},...,\tilde{\underline{v}}_{p}\}
where
p<\infty
. Therefore, there exists a sequence of
\lambda_{1},..,\lambda_{p}\in\left[0,1\right]
and
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}=1
such that
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}\tilde{\underline{v}}_{j}
.

As a result, by the definition of
e^{*}_{m|i}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{m})
, we have,
\forall\,i\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}
,

\textstyle x_{i}=\eta_{i}e^{*}_{m|i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}\tilde{\underline{v}}_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}\eta_{i}e^{*}_{m|i}\left(\tilde{\underline{v}}_{j}\right)

Then, due to
\left\{\eta_{i}e^{*}_{m|i}\left(\tilde{v}_{j}\right)\,\middle|\,i\in\left[0:M-1\right]\setminus\{m\}\right\}\in\zeta_{m}\left(\tilde{\underline{v}}_{j}\right)
for any
j\in\{1,...,p\}
, it is straightforward that

\textstyle\underline{x}\in\text{Conv}\left\{\bigcup\nolimits_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right\}

Note that the index
m
in
\bm{\beta}^{m}
here is just for the notational consistency with respect to our main theorem.

Furthermore, because the region
\text{Conv}\left\{\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right\}
is a compact convex set, by Krein–Milman Theorem (a well-known result in Topology), we have

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\text{Conv}\left\{\bigcup\nolimits_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right\}

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle=\text{Conv}\left\{\text{extr}\left(\text{Conv}\left\{\bigcup\nolimits_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right\}\right)\right\}.

Consequently, by the fact that the convex hull operation on the set
\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
does not add or eliminate any extreme points of
\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
, namely,

\textstyle\text{extr}\left(\text{Conv}\left\{\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right\}\right)=\text{extr}\left(\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right),

we can conclude that

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\text{Conv}\left\{\text{extr}\left(\text{Conv}\left\{\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right\}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle=\text{Conv}\left\{\text{extr}\left(\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right)\right\},

thereby

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{C}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\subseteq\text{Conv}\left\{\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right\}

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle=\text{Conv}\left\{\text{extr}\left(\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right)\right\}.

Direction “LHS
\supseteq
RHS"

Since
\mathcal{C}
is a convex polytope,
V_{c}
is a finite set when
|\mathcal{I}|<\infty
. Hence, we assume there are
k<\infty
extreme points of the region
\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
, i.e.,
\text{extr}\left(\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right)=\{\underline{v}_{1},\underline{v}_{2},...,\underline{v}_{k}\}
.
Then, we have
\forall\,\underline{x}\in\text{Conv}\left\{\text{extr}\left(\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right)\right\}
,

\exists\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{k}\in[0,1]
,
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1
, such that

\underline{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\underline{v}_{i}
.
Furthermore, we know
\forall\,i\in\{1,...,k\}
,

\textstyle\underline{v}_{i}\in\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\subseteq\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{C}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m}),

and
\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{C}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})
is also a convex set.
Therefore, we have

\textstyle\underline{x}\in\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{C}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m}).

As a result,

\textstyle\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in\mathcal{C}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\supseteq\text{Conv}\left\{\text{extr}\left(\bigcup_{\bm{\beta}^{m}\in V_{c}}\zeta_{m}(\bm{\beta}^{m})\right)\right\}.

∎
