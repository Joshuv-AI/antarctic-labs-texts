Abstract
. In this work, we investigate a mathematical model of nonlinear ultrasonic heating based on a coupled system of the Westervelt equation and the hyperbolic Pennes bioheat equation (Westervelt–Pennes–Cattaneo model). Using the energy method together with a fixed point argument, we prove that our model is locally well-posed and does not degenerate under a smallness assumption on the pressure data in the Westervelt equation. In addition, we perform a singular limit analysis and show that the Westervelt–Pennes–Fourier model can be seen as an approximation of the Westervelt–Pennes–Cattaneo model as the relaxation parameter tends to zero. This is done by deriving uniform bounds of the solution with respect to the relaxation parameter.

1.
Introduction

We are interested in the analysis of a nonlinear thermo-acoustic system modeling the propagation of ultrasonic waves, such as the high–intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU), through thermoviscous fluids. With various applications including medical and industrial use in lithotripsy, thermotherapy, ultrasound cleaning, and sonochemistry
[
8
,
10
,
11
,
19
,
32
]
, the behavior of HIFU and the mathematical models describing it are receiving a great deal of attention from researchers. For instance, in medical procedures, focused ultrasound is used to generate localized heating that can destroy the targeted region. Indeed, this technique is proving its success in the treatment of both benign and malignant tumors
[
8
,
19
]
. Due to the high frequency of the sound waves, the nonlinear effect of their propagation cannot be eluded and it is well-established in nonlinear acoustics that Westervelt equation
[
28
]
takes this effect into consideration.

In this paper we consider a coupled system of the Westervelt equation for the pressure and a hyperbolic Pennes equation for the temperature. More precisely, we consider the system

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &p_{tt}-c^{2}(\bar{\Theta})\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=K(\bar{\Theta})\left(p^{2}\right)_{tt},\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\rho_{\textup{a}}C_{\textup{a}}\bar{\Theta}_{t}+\nabla\cdot q+\rho_{\textup{b}}C_{\textup{b}}W(\bar{\Theta}-\Theta_{\textup{a}})=\mathcal{Q}(p_{t}),\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\tau q_{t}+q+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\nabla\bar{\Theta}=0,\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T).\end{aligned}\right.

Here the acoustic pressure and the temperature fluctuations are denoted respectively by
p
and
\bar{\Theta}
;
c
is the speed of sound and
b>0
is the sound diffusivity. The function
K(\bar{\Theta})
is allowed to depend on
\bar{\Theta}
and is given by

K(\bar{\Theta})=\frac{\beta_{\text{acous}}}{\rho c^{2}(\bar{\Theta})},

where
\rho
is the mass density and
\beta_{\text{acous}}
is the parameter of nonlinearity.
The source term in the second equation
\mathcal{Q}(p_{t})
represents the acoustic energy absorbed by the tissue. The medium parameters
\rho_{\textup{a}},C_{\textup{a}}
and
\kappa_{\textup{a}}
stand, respectively, for the ambient density, the ambient heat capacity and the thermal conductivity of the tissue. The additional term
\rho_{\textup{b}}C_{\textup{b}}W(\bar{\Theta}-\Theta_{\textup{a}})
accounts for the heat loss due to blood circulation, with
\rho_{\textup{b}},C_{\textup{b}}
being the density and the specific heat capacity of blood, and
W
expressing the tissue’s volumetric perfusion rate measured in milliliters of blood per milliliter of tissue per second.

The second and the third equations in (
1.1
) constitute the hyperbolic version of the Pennes equation
(
1.5
), where the heat conduction is described by the Cattaneo law (or the Cattaneo–Maxwell law).
We supplement (
1.1
) with the initial conditions

(1.2)

p|_{t=0}=p_{0},\quad p_{t}|_{t=0}=p_{1},\quad\bar{\Theta}|_{t=0}=\bar{\Theta}_{0},\quad q|_{t=0}=q_{0}

and Dirichlet boundary conditions

(1.3)

p|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\qquad\bar{\Theta}|_{\partial\Omega}=\Theta_{\textup{a}},

where
\Theta_{\textup{a}}
denotes the ambient temperature, that is typically taken in the human body to be
37^{\circ}C
; see
[
5
]
.

When
c
and
K
are constants,
the first equation in (
1.1
) reduces to the the Westervelt equation for the pressure
p(x,t)
:

(1.4)

p_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=K\left(p^{2}\right)_{tt}.

Equation (
1.4
) is widely used in acoustics and describes the propagation of sound waves in a fluid medium. It can be derived from the Navier–Stokes–Fourier model by assuming the Fourier law of heat conduction and by taking into account the thermoviscous effects. See
[
28
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
17
, Chapter 5]
for more details. Significant progress has been made recently toward the understanding of the solutions to the Westervelt equation and their behavior; see
[
14
,
4
,
13
,
20
,
16
,
27
]
and the references therein. The results in
[
14
,
20
,
27
]
investigated local well-posedness, global well-posedness, and asymptotic behavior of solutions, subject to different types of boundary conditions and in various functional settings. Particularly in
[
20
,
27
]
, the authors relied on maximal regularity in
L^{p}
-spaces to obtain the existence of a unique solution with low regularity assumptions on the initial data.
This is feasible thanks to the strong damping represented by the term
-b\Delta p_{t}
when
b>0
, which lends to the Westervelt equation its parabolic character.

Combining the second and the third equation equations in (
1.1
), we obtain the hyperbolic Pennes equation (see
[
12
, Eq. 3]
and
[
30
, Eq. 7]
)

(1.5)

\displaystyle\tau\rho_{\textup{a}}C_{\textup{a}}\bar{\Theta}_{tt}+(\rho_{\textup{a}}C_{\textup{a}}+\tau\rho_{\textup{b}}C_{\textup{b}}W)\bar{\Theta}_{t}+\rho_{\textup{b}}C_{\textup{b}}W(\bar{\Theta}-\Theta_{\textup{a}})-\kappa_{\textup{a}}\Delta\bar{\Theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(p_{t})+\tau\partial_{t}\mathcal{Q}(p_{t}).

The terms in (
1.5
) that appear with the
\tau
prefactor arise from the use of the Cattaneo law of heat conduction
[
3
]
:

(1.6)

\tau q_{t}+q+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\nabla\bar{\Theta}=0

which is a modified version of the classical Fourier law:

(1.7)

q+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\nabla\bar{\Theta}=0.

Equation (
1.7
) implies instantaneous thermal energy deposition in the medium. That is, any temperature disturbance causes instantaneous perturbation in the temperature at each point in the medium.
The Cattaneo law was introduced to overcome this drawback of the infinite speed of thermal signals in the Fourier law. The idea is to introduce a time lag into the relationship between the heat flux and the temperature gradient, which results in the term
\tau q_{t}
in (
1.6
), where
\tau
is the relaxation time parameter.

The Cattaneo model (
1.6
) has the effect of changing the heat equation into a damped wave equation, which describes a physical phenomenon of heat waves.
This phenomenon is known as the
second sound
effect, and it is experimentally observed in materials at a very low temperature, where the heat seems to propagate as a thermal wave, which is the reason for this name (see the review paper
[
25
]
).

For
\tau=0
, equation (
1.5
) becomes

(1.8)

\rho_{\textup{a}}C_{\textup{a}}\bar{\Theta}_{t}-\kappa_{\textup{a}}\Delta\bar{\Theta}+\rho_{\textup{b}}C_{\textup{b}}W(\bar{\Theta}-\Theta_{\textup{a}})=\mathcal{Q}(p_{t}).

Equation (
1.8
) is the parabolic Pennes equation, which is a bioheat transfer equation that is widely used for studying heat transfer in biological systems. It takes into account the heat transfer by conduction in the tissues and the convective heat transfer due to blood perfusion. See
[
24
]
for the derivation of (
1.8
).

In
[
22
]
Nikolić and Said-Houari considered the Westervelt–Pennes–Fourier system, which is the coupling between the first equation in (
1.1
)
and (
1.8
). That is, they investigated the system

(1.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &p_{tt}-c^{2}(\bar{\Theta})\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=k(\bar{\Theta})\left(p^{2}\right)_{tt},\\
&\rho_{\textup{a}}C_{\textup{a}}\bar{\Theta}_{t}-\kappa_{\textup{a}}\Delta\bar{\Theta}+\rho_{\textup{b}}C_{\textup{b}}W(\bar{\Theta}-\Theta_{\textup{a}})=\mathcal{Q}(p_{t})\end{aligned}\right.

in
\Omega\times(0,T)
with Dirichlet–Dirichlet boundary conditions. They proved a local well-posedness of (
1.9
) using the energy method together with a fixed point argument. The work in
[
22
]
was followed by
[
23
]
, where the authors proved the global existence of the solution of (
1.9
) under a smallness assumption on the initial data, and they studied its asymptotic behavior as
t
goes to infinity. Using the maximal regularity estimate for parabolic systems, Wilke in
[
29
]
improves slightly the regularity assumptions in
[
22
]
and also considered the case
b=b(\bar{\Theta})
.

In this paper, we consider the Westervelt–Pennes–Cattaneo system (
1.1
) and investigate the local well-posedness and the singular limit as the time relaxation parameter
\tau
tends to zero. To state and prove our result and to lighten the notation, we put

(1.10)

m=\rho_{\textup{a}}C_{\textup{a}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ell=\rho_{\textup{b}}C_{\textup{b}}W,

and make the change of variables

\Theta=\bar{\Theta}-\Theta_{a}

in the temperature. Denoting by

(1.11)

k(\Theta)=K(\Theta+\Theta_{\textup{a}})=\frac{1}{\rho c^{2}(\Theta+\Theta_{\textup{a}})}\beta_{\text{acou}}\quad\text{and}\quad h(\Theta)=c^{2}(\Theta+\Theta_{\textup{a}}),

we get the following system

(1.12a)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &p_{tt}-h(\Theta)\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=k(\Theta)\left(p^{2}\right)_{tt},\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T)\\
&m\Theta_{t}+\nabla\cdot q+\ell\Theta=\mathcal{Q}(p_{t}),\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T)\\
&\tau q_{t}+q+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\nabla\Theta=0,\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T)\end{aligned}\right.

complemented with homogeneous boundary conditions

(1.12b)

p|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\qquad\Theta|_{\partial\Omega}=0

and the initial conditions

(1.12c)

p|_{t=0}=p_{0},\quad p_{t}|_{t=0}=p_{1},\quad\Theta|_{t=0}=\Theta_{0}:=\bar{\Theta}_{0}-\Theta_{\textup{a}},\quad q|_{t=0}=q_{0}.

As in
[
22
]
, here the medium parameters
c
and
K
in the Westervelt equation are not constant. They are taken explicitly dependent on the temperature in order to account for the fact that the heating generated by the ultrasound waves affects their speed of propagation and the position of the focal region, a phenomenon known as thermal lensing
[
5
,
9
]
. Precisely, we assume this dependence to be polynomial, in agreement with the experimentally observed behavior documented in
[
1
]
. Also for simplicity, we assume that the function
\mathcal{Q}
has the form

(1.13)

\mathcal{Q}(p_{t})=\frac{2b}{\rho_{\textup{a}}C_{\textup{a}}^{4}}(p_{t})^{2},

although our proof works for quite general
\mathcal{Q}(p_{t})
satisfying Assumption 2 in
[
22
]
.

To establish well-posedness, we carry out an energy analysis for the linearization of the underlying system, which will allow applying a fixed-point argument to work out the existence of a unique solution to the nonlinear problem (
1.12
). In doing so, we encounter two main challenges. On the one hand, we have to take into account the interplay between the pressure and the temperature owing to the coupling of their respective equations. In the pressure equation, the dependence of the coefficients
h(\Theta)
and
k(\Theta)
on the temperature suggests that they should be kept bounded for all time. That is to say, the function
\Theta
needs to be in
L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)
. Moreover, note that the first equation in (
1.12
) is quasilinear. To see this, it suffices to write the right-hand side as
k(\Theta)(p^{2})_{tt}=2k(\Theta)pp_{tt}+2k(\Theta)(p_{t})^{2}
. This brings about the risk of degeneracy of the term
(1-2k(\Theta)p)p_{tt}
, which is usually avoided by imposing a smallness constraint on the acoustic pressure
[
14
]
. Therefore, we are led to work with higher-order energies for both the pressure and the temperature. One key ingredient in obtaining the
a priori
estimates for these energies is the Sobolev embedding theorem, especially the continuous embedding
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. On the other hand, obtaining uniform energy bounds with respect to
\tau
for the heat conduction system requires careful attention.
Further, considering that in practical situations,
\tau
takes very small values, it is interesting to investigate the behavior of the system when
\tau
goes to zero. In order to do this, we adopt the approach in
[
15
,
21
]
. See also the important results in
[
2
]
. Even though the models under consideration in these works involve single equations, the method is quite constructive and could be applied to the coupled problem at hand. The key ideas are to first derive estimates that are uniform with respect to
\tau
, which will justify taking the limit as
\tau
tends to zero; then we make use of the compactness of Sobolev embeddings to show that the solution of (
1.12
) converges to the solution of the system corresponding to
\tau=0
.

Our paper is organized as follows. The main results are stated in Section
2
. In Section
3
, we collect some theoretical results that will prove useful in the sequel, and we state the general assumptions on the coefficients in system (
1.12
). Sections
4
is devoted to the energy analysis of the hyperbolic Pennes equation (the Cattaneo system), while Section
5
treats the linearized Westervelt equation. In Section
6
, we prove the local well-posedness of the nonlinear problem (
1.12
). Finally, in Section
7
, we perform the singular limit analysis and show that the solution of the Westervelt–Pennes–Cattaneo model converges to the solution of the Westervelt–Pennes–Fourier system when the time relaxation vanishes.

2.
Main results

In this section, we state the main results of this paper and give the strategy of the proof.
In order to give context to the main results, we begin by specifying the functional setup adopted in this work. We define

(2.1)

\mathcal{X}:=X_{p}\times X_{\Theta}\times X_{q},

where the spaces
X_{p},X_{\Theta}
and
X_{q}
are given, respectively as

(2.2)

\displaystyle X_{p}=

\displaystyle\Big\{p\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle p_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle p_{tt}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle p_{ttt}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\Big\};

\displaystyle X_{\Theta}=

\displaystyle\{\Theta\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)),\Theta_{t}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\Theta_{tt}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\};

\displaystyle X_{q}=

\displaystyle\{q\in L^{\infty}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{d});q_{t},q_{tt}\in L^{2}(0,T;(L^{2}(\Omega))^{d})\}.

Our first result ensures local in time well-posedness, which is uniform with respect to the relaxation parameter
\tau
.

Assumption 1
.

Let the initial data satisfy

(2.3)

\displaystyle(p_{0},p_{1})

\displaystyle\in\big[H^{3}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\big]\times\big[H^{3}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\big],

\displaystyle(\Theta_{0},q_{0})

\displaystyle\in\big[H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\big]\times(H^{2}(\Omega))^{d},

such that
1-2k(\Theta_{0})p_{0}
does not degenerate. We also assume the compatibility conditions:

(2.4)

p_{2},\Theta_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\quad\Theta_{2},q_{1},q_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega)

where
p_{2}:=\partial_{t}^{2}p(0,x),\Theta_{k}:=\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta(0,x),q_{k}:=\partial_{t}^{k}q(0,x),x\in\Omega,k=1,2
are defined formally and recursively in terms of
p_{0},p_{1},\Theta_{0},q_{0}
from the equations (
1.12a
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
T>0
and
\bar{\tau}>0
be a fixed small constant. Let
\tau\in(0,\bar{\tau}]
and let Assumption
1
hold.
There exists
\delta=\delta(T)>0
such that if

(2.5)

\|p_{0}\|_{H^{3}}+\|p_{1}\|_{H^{2}}+\|p_{2}\|_{H^{1}}\leq\delta

then system (
1.12
) has a unique solution
(p,\Theta,q)\in\mathcal{X}
.

Let us now outline the main steps in the proof of Theorem
2.1
and list some comments about the above result.

1.

We consider a linearization of the underlying model (
1.12
) that will see the Westervelt equation decoupled from the Cattaneo system for heat transfer. This allows us to treat the first equation in (
1.12
) separately from the second and third equations. However, the decoupling of the linearized system does not allow us to transfer the damping induced by the damped wave equation for the temperature to the linearized Westervelt equation.

We use Galerkin approximations to prove the existence of a unique solution for the linearized Cattaneo system (
4.1
), together with uniform energy estimates with respect to
\tau
.
Next, motivated by
[
22
]
, we often rely on the Sobolev embedding theorem to conduct an energy analysis yielding the well-posedness of the linearized Westervelt equation in a finite time horizon
T>0
. In addition, we derive some energy bounds that will be useful in the analysis of the nonlinear problem.

2.

Having all the necessary ingredients, we can tackle the nonlinear coupled problem (
1.12
) by defining the solution of the system (
1.12
) as the fixed point of a carefully defined mapping. Thus, bringing together the already established energy estimates and using the
Banach fixed-point theorem, we prove well-posedness for the nonlinear problem and show that the solution does not degenerate under a smallness assumption on the initial data of the acoustic pressure.

3.

Note that the smallness condition in Theorem
2.1
is imposed only on the pressure data and not on the temperature data. This seems necessary to avoid the degeneracy of the Westervelt equation.

4.

Using the same method, we can also treat the case
b=b(\Theta)
. Our assumption on
b
to be constant is just to avoid technicalities in the proof. Also, the condition
b>0
is crucial in our analysis. It is an important open problem to study the case
b=0
.

In the following theorem, we state a result on the convergence of the solution of the Westervelt–Pennes–Cattaneo model to the solution of the Westervelt–Pennes–Fourier model as the relaxation time
\tau
tends to zero, and as a consequence, we recover at the limit, the well-posedness result in
[
22
]
. To facilitate relating the limit to the solution of the Westervelt–Pennes–Fourier model, in this part we use the wave equation (
1.5
) instead of Cattaneo’s system. We denote by
\Theta_{1}^{\tau}:=\Theta^{\tau}_{t}(0,x)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Given
T>0
and
\tau\in(0,\bar{\tau}]
. Let the initial data
(p_{0}^{\tau},p_{1}^{\tau}),(\Theta_{0}^{\tau},\Theta_{1}^{\tau})

satisfy Assumption
1
and the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
. Then, the familly of solutions
(p^{\tau},\Theta^{\tau})_{\tau\in(0,\bar{\tau}]}
converges weakly (see (
7.7
), (
7.5
)) to the solution
(p,\Theta)\in X_{p}\times X_{\Theta}
of the Westervelt–Pennes–Fourier system:

(2.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(1-2k(\Theta)p)p_{tt}-h(\Theta)\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=2k(\Theta)(p_{t})^{2},\qquad&\text{in}&\ \Omega&\times(0,T),\\
&m\Theta_{t}+\ell\Theta-\kappa_{\textup{a}}\Delta\Theta=\mathcal{Q}(p_{t}),\qquad&\text{in}&\ \Omega&\times(0,T),\\
&(p,p_{t})|_{t=0}=(p_{0},p_{1}),\quad\Theta|_{t=0}=\Theta_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions (
1.12b
).

It is essential to note that the main difficulty in proving Theorem
2.2
lies in obtaining energy estimates that are uniform with respect to
\tau
. In other words, our goal is to prevent the constants from becoming infinitely large as
\tau
approaches zero in the estimates. This justifies the process of passing to the limit when the parameter
\tau
tends to zero.
The uniformity in
\tau
is not a requirement for the well-posedness result in Theorem
2.1
, but it is essential in the proof of Theorem
2.2
. Lastly, we remark that as the estimates are dependent on the time
T>0
, the existence of solutions is local in nature. However, we can expect to reach global well-posedness by taking the approach in
[
23
]
.

3.
Preliminaries and assumptions

In this section, we introduce a few notations and collect some helpful embedding results and inequalities that we will repeatedly use in the proofs.

3.1.
Notation

Throughout the paper, we assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d\in\{1,2,3\}
, is a bounded,
C^{2}
smooth domain. We denote by
T>0
the final propagation time. The letter
C
denotes a generic positive constant
that does not depend on time, and can have different values on different occasions.
We often write
f\lesssim g
where there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of parameters of interest such that
f\leq Cg
.
We often omit the spatial and temporal domain when writing norms; for example,
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}L^{q}}
denotes the norm in
L^{p}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))
.

3.2.
Inequalities and embedding results

In the upcoming analysis, we shall employ the continuous embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Omega)
,
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
and
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. In particular, using Poincaré’s inequality we obtain for
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
(see
[
26
, Theorem 7.18]
)

(3.1)

\displaystyle\text{if}\ d>2,\quad\|v\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}\quad\text{for}\quad 2\leq p\leq\frac{2d}{d-2},

\displaystyle\text{if}\ d=2,\quad\|v\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}}\quad\text{for}\quad 2\leq p<\infty.

Moreover, taking into account the boundedness of the operator
(-\Delta)^{-1}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we find the inequality

\qquad\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{1}\|v\|_{H^{2}}\leq C_{2}\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}}.

We will also call on the 1D-embedding
H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow C(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. That is, we have the following inequality

(3.2)

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[0,T]}\|v(t)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|v\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}+\|v_{t}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}})

where the constant
C>0
depends only on
T
(see, e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 2, p. 286]
). Additionally, if
v\in H^{1}(0,T,H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
, then the above embedding combined with Poincaré’s inequality yields

(3.3)

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[0,T]}\|v(t)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}+\|v_{t}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}).

We recall Young’s
\varepsilon
-inequality

(3.4)

xy\leq\varepsilon x^{n}+C(\varepsilon)y^{m},\quad\text{where}\quad\ x,y>0,\quad 1<m,n<\infty,\quad\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{n}=1,

and
C(\varepsilon)=(\varepsilon n)^{-m/n}m^{-1}
.

Further, we will make use of Ladyzhenskaya’s inequality for
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)

(3.5)

\|u\|_{L^{4}}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{1-d/4}\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{d/4},\qquad 1\leq d\leq 4.

We state a version of Gronwall’s inequality which will be utilized in the proofs and has been provided in
[
22
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
I=[0,t]
and let
\alpha,\beta:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be locally integrable functions. Given
u,v:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
v
is non-negative and integrable and
u
is in
C^{1}(I)
. We assume that

(3.6)

u^{\prime}(t)+v(t)\leq\alpha(t)u(t)+\beta(t),\ \text{for}\ t\in I,\quad u(0)=u_{0}.

Then, it holds that

(3.7)

u(t)+\int_{0}^{t}v(s)\,\textup{d}s\leq u_{0}e^{A(t)}+\int_{0}^{t}\beta(s)e^{A(t)-A(s)}\,\textup{d}s,

where

(3.8)

A(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)\,\textup{d}s.

3.3.
Assumptions

In accordance with the perceived polynomial growth of the speed of sound
c=c(\Theta)
, we make the following assumptions on the functions
h
and
k
.

We assume that
h\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and there exists
h_{1}>0
such that

(H1)

h(s)\geq h_{1},\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, we assume that there exist
\gamma_{1}>0
and
C>0
, such that

(H2)

|h^{\prime\prime}(s)|\leq C(1+|s|^{\gamma_{1}}),\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}.

So that by using Taylor’s formula, we also have

(H3)

|h^{\prime}(s)|\leq C(1+|s|^{1+\gamma_{1}}),\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}.

Since the function
k
is related to the speed of sound by the formula (
1.11
), it follows that

(K1)

|k(s)|\leq k_{1}:=\frac{\beta_{\text{acous}}}{\rho h_{1}},\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}.

Further, we have

(3.10a)

\displaystyle|k^{\prime\prime}(s)|

\displaystyle\lesssim k_{1}^{2}|h^{\prime\prime}(s)|+k_{1}^{3}|h^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\lesssim k_{1}^{2}(1+|s|^{\gamma_{1}})+k_{1}^{3}(1+|s|^{1+\gamma_{1}})^{2},

which by using Taylor’s formula, implies that there exists
\gamma_{2}>0
, such that

(K2)

|k^{\prime}(s)|\lesssim(1+|s|^{1+\gamma_{2}}),\qquad|k^{\prime\prime}(s)|\lesssim(1+|s|^{\gamma_{2}}),\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}..

4.
The hyperbolic bioheat equation

In this section, we consider the hyperbolic heat equation

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &m\Theta_{t}+\nabla\cdot q+\ell\Theta=f,\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\tau q_{t}+q+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\nabla\Theta=0,\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),\end{aligned}\right.

together with the relevant initial conditions in (
1.12c
) and the boundary condition in (
1.12b
).
Our main goal is to prove a priori estimates under minimal assumptions on the initial data
\Theta_{0}
and
q_{0}
and on the the source term
f
.

In order to state and prove our main result, we define the total energy associated to (
4.1
) as

(4.2)

E^{\tau}[\Theta,q](t):=\sum_{k=0}^{2}E_{k}[\Theta,q](t),\quad t\geq 0

where the energies
E_{k},k=0,1,2
are given by

(4.3)

E_{k}[\Theta,q](t):=\frac{1}{2}\Big(m\kappa_{\textup{a}}\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\tau\|\partial_{t}^{k}q(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\Big).

We also define the associated dissipation

(4.4)

D[\Theta,q](t):=\sum_{k=0}^{2}D_{k}[p,\Theta](t),\quad t\geq 0

with

(4.5)

D_{k}[\Theta,q](t):=\ell\kappa_{\textup{a}}\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{t}^{k}q(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}},\quad k=0,1,2.

Since the coefficients in the first equation in (
1.12a
) depend on
\Theta
, we should establish some higher–order estimates on
\Theta
, which will allow us to control the
L^{\infty}
-norm of
\Theta
through the Sobolev embedding theorem. To this end, we introduce the following energy in terms of
\Theta
only

(4.6)

\mathcal{E}[\Theta](t):=\mathcal{E}_{0}[\Theta](t)+\mathcal{E}_{1}[\Theta](t),\quad t\geq 0

where
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
are defined as follows

(4.7)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{E}_{0}[\Theta](t):=\frac{m\kappa_{\textup{a}}}{2}(\|\Theta(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\Theta_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\Theta_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}),\\
&\mathcal{E}_{1}[\Theta](t):=\frac{m+\tau\ell}{2}\|\nabla\Theta(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\|\nabla\Theta_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\|\Delta\Theta(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.\end{aligned}\right.

The associated dissipation rate to
\mathcal{E}[\Theta](t)
is

(4.8)

\mathcal{D}[\Theta](t):=\mathcal{D}_{0}[\Theta](t)+\mathcal{D}_{1}[\Theta](t),

with

(4.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{D}_{0}[\Theta](t):=\ell\kappa_{\textup{a}}(\|\Theta(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\Theta_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\Theta_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}),\\
&\mathcal{D}_{1}[\Theta](t):=\ell\|\nabla\Theta(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\|\nabla\Theta_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\|\Delta\Theta(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.\end{aligned}\right.

The definitions of
\mathcal{E}[\Theta](t)
and
\mathcal{D}[\Theta](t)
are inspired from the damped wave equation in
\Theta
(
1.5
) that can be obtained by combining the two equations in system (
4.1
). See also equation (
4.20
) below.

The following lemma will allow us to estimate
\mathcal{E}[\Theta]
in terms of
E^{\tau}[\Theta,q]
uniformly with respect to
\tau
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\bar{\tau}>0
be a fixed small number. Then for all
t\geq 0
, the estimate

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[\Theta](t)\lesssim(1+\bar{\tau}+

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}^{2})\big(E^{\bar{\tau}}[\Theta,q](t)+\|f(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|f_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\big)

holds uniformly in
\tau\in(0,\bar{\tau}]
.
The hidden constant in (
4.10
) does not depend on
\tau
.

Proof.

It is clear that we have for all
t\geq 0

(4.11)

\mathcal{E}_{0}[\Theta](t)\leq E^{\tau}[\Theta,q](t).

Now, if we want to show that
\mathcal{E}_{1}[\Theta](t)
is also bounded by
E^{\tau}[\Theta,q](t)
, we can simply make use of the second equation in (
4.1
) to get

(4.12)

\kappa_{\textup{a}}^{2}\|\nabla\Theta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 2\tau^{2}\|q_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\|q\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C(\tau)E^{\tau}[\Theta,q].

However the constant
C(\tau)\rightarrow\infty
as
\tau\rightarrow 0
. To avoid this, we take the time derivative
\partial_{t}^{k},k=0,1
of the system (
4.1
) to obtain

(4.13)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &m\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\partial_{t}^{k}q)+\ell\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta=\partial_{t}^{k}f,\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\tau\partial_{t}^{k}q_{t}+\partial_{t}^{k}q+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\nabla(\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta)=0,\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T).\end{aligned}\right.

We multiply the second equation in (
4.13
) by
\nabla(\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta),k=0,1
and integrate over
\Omega
. Using the divergence theorem and the fact that
\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta|_{\partial\Omega}=0
, we obtain

(4.14)

\kappa_{\textup{a}}\|\nabla(\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=-\tau\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}q_{t}\cdot\nabla(\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta)\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot(\partial_{t}^{k}q)\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x,\quad k=0,1.

Note that we can recover the last term on the right from the first equation in (
4.13
) by testing by
\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta
and integrating over
\Omega
. Hence, we get

(4.15)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot(\partial_{t}^{k}q)\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x=-m\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta_{t}\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x-\ell\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}f\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x.

Combining (
4.14
) and (
4.15
), we have

(4.16)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\textup{a}}\|\nabla(\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=-\tau

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}q_{t}\cdot\nabla(\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta)\,\textup{d}x-m\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta_{t}\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle-\ell\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}f\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x.

Thus using Cauchy-Schwarz and Young inequalities, it results that

(4.17)

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{\textup{a}}}{2}\|\nabla\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\ell}{2}\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau^{2}}{2\kappa_{\textup{a}}}\|\partial_{t}^{k}q_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\frac{m^{2}}{\ell}\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\ell}\|\partial_{t}^{k}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{2m}{\ell\kappa_{\textup{a}}}+\frac{\tau}{\kappa_{\textup{a}}}\Big)E^{\tau}[\Theta,q]+\frac{1}{\ell}\|\partial_{t}^{k}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}},\quad k=0,1.

The above estimate for
k=0
also implies

(4.18)

\frac{\tau\ell}{2}\|\nabla\Theta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim(\tau+\tau^{2})E^{\tau}[\Theta,q]+\tau\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Next, we focus on estimating the term
\|\Delta\Theta\|_{L^{2}}
in
\mathcal{E}_{1}[\Theta](t)
. First, we take the time derivative of the first equation in (
4.1
) and apply the divergence operator to the second equation, so that we have

(4.19)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &m\Theta_{tt}+\nabla\cdot q_{t}+\ell\Theta_{t}=f_{t},\\
&\tau\nabla\cdot q_{t}+\nabla\cdot q+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\Delta\Theta=0.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

We note that the above process of obtaining system (
4.19
) is formal. This can be made rigorous by first considering smooth approximations for the initial data so that the corresponding solutions will have the required smoothness that will allow us to pass to the limit to infer that (
4.19
) still holds.

Combining these two equations,
and using the first equation in (
4.1
), we obtain

(4.20)

\tau m\Theta_{tt}+(m+\tau\ell)\Theta_{t}+\ell\Theta-\kappa_{\textup{a}}\Delta\Theta=f+\tau f_{t}.

From here, we infer that

(4.21)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\textup{a}}^{2}\|\Delta\Theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\ell^{2}\|\Theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+(m+\tau\ell)^{2}\|\Theta_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\tau^{2}m^{2}\|\Theta_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\tau^{2}\|f_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\tau^{2})\big(E^{\tau}[\Theta,q]+\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|f_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\big).

Putting together the estimates (
4.11
), (
4.17
), (
4.18
), (
4.21
) and noting that
\tau\in(0,\bar{\tau}]
, we get the estimate (
4.10
).
This completes the proof of Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Proposition 4.1
.

Given
T>0
, and
f\in H^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Assume that the initial data
(\Theta_{0},q_{0})
satisfy Assumption
1
.
Then, the system (
4.1
) has a unique solution
(\Theta,q)\in X_{\Theta}\times X_{q}
. Furthermore, the solution satisfies

(4.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[\Theta](t)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{D}[\Theta](s)\,\textup{d}s\lesssim(1+\bar{\tau}+\bar{\tau}^{2})\big(E^{\bar{\tau}}[\Theta,q](0)+\|f\|^{2}_{H^{2}L^{2}}\big)

and

(4.23)

\displaystyle\|q(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{2}\int^{t}_{0}\|\partial_{t}^{k}q(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|q_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+(1+\bar{\tau}+\bar{\tau}^{2})\big(E^{\bar{\tau}}[\Theta,q](0)+\|f\|^{2}_{H^{2}L^{2}}\big)

for all
0\leq t\leq T
, where the hidden constants in (
4.22
) and (
4.23
) are uniform with respect to
\tau\in(0,\bar{\tau}]
.

We rely on the Faedo–Galerkin approach to prove the result above. In the first step, we show some uniform
a priori
estimates to hold for the approximations of the solution
(\Theta,q)
. Precisely, we take the eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet–Laplacian as approximations for the temperature
\Theta
. As for the heat flux
q
, we exploit the fact that
(H^{2}(\Omega))^{d}
is separable, yielding the existence of a dense sequence that can be used to approximate
q
(see
[
18
]
). In the second step, due to the fact that the estimates are uniform, we can pass to the limit as in
[
7
, chapter 7]
(see also
[
18
, Chapter 1]
) to obtain the existence of solutions. Further, uniqueness follows by noting that the only solution to the homogeneous problem (
f=0,\Theta_{0}=0
and
q_{0}=0
) is
(\Theta,q)=(0,0)
. Lastly, from the weak and weak-
\star
semi-continuity of norms, we get that the estimates (
4.22
), (
4.23
) also hold for the solution
(\Theta,q)
. Since the approach is quite classical, our emphasis in the following is on the energy analysis, and
we refer to
[
7
,
18
]
for the procedure of passing to the limit. To start with, we prove the following intermediate estimates.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\tau\in(0,\bar{\tau}]
. Assume that
f\in H^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Then for all
0\leq t\leq T
, we have the following estimate

(4.24)

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}E_{k}[\Theta,q](t)+D_{k}[\Theta,q](t)\lesssim\|\partial_{t}^{k}f(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}},\quad k=0,1,2.

where the hidden constant is independent of
\tau
.

Proof.

Applying
\partial_{t}^{k},\ k=0,1,2
to system (
4.1
), we get

(4.25)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &m\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta_{t}+\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{k}q+\ell\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta=\partial_{t}^{k}f,\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\tau\partial_{t}^{k}q_{t}+\partial_{t}^{k}q+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\nabla\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta=0,\qquad&\text{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T).\end{aligned}\right.

Multiplying the first equation in (
4.25
) by
\kappa_{\textup{a}}\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta
and integrating over
\Omega
, it follows

(4.26)

\frac{m\kappa_{\textup{a}}}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\nabla\cdot\partial_{t}^{k}q\,\textup{d}x+\ell\kappa_{\textup{a}}\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\kappa_{\textup{a}}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}f\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x.

Using the divergence theorem on the second term on the left and the fact that

(due to (

)), we obtain

(4.27)

\frac{m\kappa_{\textup{a}}}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\kappa_{\textup{a}}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}q\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x+\ell\kappa_{\textup{a}}\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\kappa_{\textup{a}}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}f\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x.

Next, multiplying the second equation in (
4.25
) by
\partial_{t}^{k}q
and integrating over
\Omega
, we get

(4.28)

\frac{\tau}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\partial_{t}^{k}q\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{t}^{k}q\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}q\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x=0.

Collecting (
4.27
) and (
4.28

(4.29)

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}E_{k}[\Theta,q]+D_{k}[\Theta,q]=\kappa_{\textup{a}}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}^{k}f\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta\,\textup{d}x,

where

and

are given by (

4.24
).

\int_{0}^{t}(\|q(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{t}(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{tt}(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2})\,\textup{d}s\lesssim E^{\tau}[\Theta,q](0)+\|f\|_{H^{2}L^{2}}^{2}.

Thus, it remains to prove that
q
is in
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{d}
. From the second equation in (
4.1
), we obtain

(4.52)

q(x,t)=q_{0}(x)-\frac{\kappa_{\textup{a}}}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\Theta e^{-(t-s)/\tau}\,\textup{d}s,

which implies that for all
t\geq 0

(4.53)

\|q(t)\|_{H^{1}}\leq\|q_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+\frac{\kappa_{\textup{a}}}{\tau}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)/\tau}\|\nabla\Theta(s)\|_{H^{1}}\,\textup{d}s.

Using elliptic regularity, one finds

(4.54)

\displaystyle\|q(t)\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|q_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+\kappa_{\textup{a}}(1-e^{-t/\tau})\|\Delta\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|q_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+\|\Delta\Theta\|_{L^{\infty}L^{2}}.

This last estimate along with (
4.50
) gives

(4.55)

\displaystyle\|q(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\lesssim\|q_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+(1+\bar{\tau}+\bar{\tau}^{2})\big(E^{\bar{\tau}}[\Theta,q](0)+\|f\|^{2}_{H^{2}L^{2}}\big).

Summing up the estimates (
4.51
) and (
4.55
), we obtain (
4.23
). Further, from (
4.22
), (
4.23
), we deduce that
(\Theta,q)\in X_{\Theta}\times X_{q}
. This concludes the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

5.
The linearized Westervelt equation

In this section, we consider the linearization of the Westervelt equation in (
1.12
):

(5.1)

\alpha(x,t)p_{tt}-r(x,t)\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=g(x,t),\qquad x\in\Omega,\quad t\geq 0,

supplemented by the relevant initial conditions in (
1.12c
) and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition (
1.12b
).

We define the energies associated to (
5.1
) as follows

(5.2)

\displaystyle E_{1}[p](t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\big(\|\sqrt{\alpha(t)}p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{r(t)}\nabla p(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\big),

\displaystyle E_{2}[p](t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\big(\|\sqrt{\alpha(t)}p_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{r(t)}\nabla p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{b}\Delta p(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\big),

\displaystyle E_{3}[p](t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\big(\|\sqrt{b}\nabla p_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{b}\nabla\Delta p(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\big).

The total acoustic energy is given by

(5.3)

\mathfrak{E}[p](t):=\sum_{k=1}^{3}E_{k}[p](t),\qquad t\geq 0.

We denote by
\mathfrak{D}[p]
its associated dissipation rate given by

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}[p](t):=\mathfrak{D}_{0}[p](t)+b\|\nabla\Delta p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+b\|\Delta p_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}},\qquad t\geq 0

where
\mathfrak{D}_{0}[p]
is given by

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{0}[p](t):=b\|\nabla p_{t}(t)

\displaystyle\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+b\|\nabla p_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{r(t)}\Delta p(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+b\|\Delta p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|\sqrt{r(t)}\nabla\Delta p(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{\alpha(t)}p_{ttt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

We make the following regularity and non-degeneracy assumptions on the coefficients
\alpha
and
r
.

Assumption 2
.

Assume that

(A)

•

\alpha\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,3}(\Omega))
,

•

\alpha_{t}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{3}(\Omega))\cap L^{\frac{4}{4-d}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
,

•

There exist
0<\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{1}
such that

(5.6)

\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha(x,t)\leq\alpha_{1}\quad\text{a.e. in}\quad\Omega\times(0,T).

(R)

•

r\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,3}(\Omega))
,

•

r_{t}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{3}(\Omega))\cap L^{\frac{4}{4-d}}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
,

•

There exist
0<r_{0}\leq r_{1}

(5.7)

r_{0}\leq r(x,t)\leq r_{1}\quad\text{a.e. in}\quad\Omega\times(0,T).

The main result of this section reads as follows.

Proposition 5.1
.

Given
T>0
and
g\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
. Let
(p_{0},p_{1})
satisfy Assumption
1
.
Then under Assumption
2
, the linearized pressure equation (
5.1
) has a unique solution
p\in X_{p}
, which satisfies for all
0\leq t\leq T

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}[p](t)+b\|\Delta p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{D}[p](s)\,\textup{d}s\lesssim

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}[p](0)\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{t}(1+\Lambda(s))\,\textup{d}s\Big)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{F}(s)\exp\Big(\int_{s}^{t}(1+\Lambda(\sigma))\textup{d}\sigma\Big)\,\textup{d}s

where

(5.9)

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)=\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|\nabla r(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|\nabla r(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|\nabla\alpha(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2},

and

(5.10)

\mathfrak{F}(t)=\|\nabla g(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|g_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

We carry out the proof of Proposition
5.1
by means of the Faedo-Galerkin method using the smooth eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet-Laplacian as approximations of the solution in space, see
[
7
]
. Below, we present the energy analysis for the mentioned approximations, which leads to
uniform
a priori
estimates, and we direct the reader interested in the process of taking the limit towards
[
7
, chapter 7]
.

Before embarking on the proof of Proposition
5.1
, we first prove two lemmas.

Lemma 5.1
.

Given
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that
g_{t}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Let Assumption
2
hold. Then for all
t\geq 0
, we have the following energy estimate

(5.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\Big(E_{1}[p](t)+E_{2}[p](t)\Big)+b\|\nabla p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+b\|\nabla p_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{r(t)}\Delta p(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+b\|\Delta p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(1+\Lambda_{1}(t))(E_{1}[p](t)+E_{2}[p](t))+\|g(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|g_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

where

(5.12)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(t)=

\displaystyle\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|\nabla r(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|\nabla r(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Proof.

The proof is based on ideas that are drawn from
[
22
]
.
Multiplying the equation (
5.1
) by
p_{t}
, integrating over
\Omega
and using integration by parts, we find

(5.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}E_{1}[p](t)+b\int_{\Omega}|\nabla p_{t}|^{2}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}gp_{t}\,\textup{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{t}p_{t}^{2}\,\textup{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\nabla r\cdot\nabla pp_{t}\,\textup{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}r_{t}|\nabla p|^{2}\,\textup{d}x.

Our goal now is to estimate the terms on the right-hand side of (
5.13
). Applying Young and Poincaré inequalities, we obtain

(5.14)

\int_{\Omega}gp_{t}\,\textup{d}x\leq C(\varepsilon)\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|\nabla p_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

The next two terms on the right-hand side of (
5.13
) can be estimated using Hölder, Young and Poincaré’s inequalities as well as the embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Omega)
. Hence, we have

(5.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{t}p_{t}^{2}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\|\alpha_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\|p_{t}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon)\|\alpha_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\|\sqrt{r}\nabla p_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\nabla p_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Considering
\varepsilon
suitably small and using Assumption
2
, we can absorb the
\varepsilon
–terms on the above estimates by the dissipation terms on the left-hand side of
(
5.13
)
.

Likewise, we obtain

(5.16)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla r\cdot\nabla pp_{t}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla r\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla p\|_{L^{4}}\|p_{t}\|_{L^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon)\|\nabla r\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\|\sqrt{r}\nabla p_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\Big\|\frac{1}{r}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\sqrt{r}\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

where we have also taken into account the elliptic estimate

(5.17)

\|\nabla p\|_{H^{1}}\leq\|p\|_{H^{2}}\leq C\|\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}.

To estimate the last term on the right-hand side of (
5.13
), we employ the Ladyzhenskaya inequality (
3.5
) together with (
5.17
) to find

(5.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}r_{t}|\nabla p|^{2}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\|r_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla p\|^{2}_{L^{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|r_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\Big\|\frac{1}{r}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\sqrt{r}\nabla p\|_{L^{2}}^{2(1-\frac{d}{4})}\|\sqrt{r}\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon)\|r_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}\|\sqrt{r}\nabla p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\Big\|\frac{1}{r}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{4}{d}}\|\sqrt{r}\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Altogether, the estimates above yield for all
t\geq 0

(5.19)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}E_{1}[p](t)+b\|\nabla p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}E_{1}[p](t)

\displaystyle+(\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla r(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}})E_{2}[p](t)

\displaystyle+\|g(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\sqrt{r(t)}\Delta p(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

In order to get an estimate for the energy
E_{2}[p]
, we differentiate (
5.1
) with respect to
t
to obtain

(5.20)

\alpha p_{ttt}-r\Delta p_{t}-b\Delta p_{tt}=-\alpha_{t}p_{tt}+r_{t}\Delta p+g_{t}.

Next, we multiply (
5.20
) by
p_{tt}
and integrate over
\Omega
. Using integration by parts, we find

(5.21)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\Big(\|\sqrt{\alpha}p_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{r}\nabla p_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\Big)+b\|\nabla p_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{t}p_{tt}^{2}\,\textup{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}r_{t}|\nabla p_{t}|^{2}\,\textup{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\nabla r\cdot\nabla p_{t}p_{tt}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}r_{t}\Delta pp_{tt}\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}g_{t}p_{tt}\,\textup{d}x.

First, Ladyzhenskaya and Young inequalities along with the embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Omega)
allow us to get the following upper bound for the first term on the right-hand side of (
5.21
). That is,

(5.22)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{t}p_{tt}^{2}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\alpha_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\|p_{tt}\|_{L^{4}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big\|\frac{1}{\alpha}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1-\frac{d}{4}}\|\alpha_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\|\sqrt{\alpha}p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2(1-\frac{d}{4})}\|\nabla p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon)\|\alpha_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}\|\sqrt{\alpha}p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\Big\|\frac{1}{\alpha}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{4}{d}-1}\|\nabla p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We can take
\varepsilon
as small as needed in order to absorb the last term in the estimate above into the dissipation term on the left-hand side of (
5.21
).

Similarly, we can derive the following estimate for the second term on the right-hand side of (
5.21
)

(5.23)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}r_{t}|\nabla p_{t}|^{2}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\|r_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla p_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big\|\frac{1}{r}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1-\frac{d}{4}}\|r_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\|\sqrt{r}\nabla p_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2(1-\frac{d}{4})}\|\Delta p_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon)\|r_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}\|\sqrt{r}\nabla p_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\Big\|\frac{1}{r}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{4}{d}-1}\|\Delta p_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

where we have also used elliptic regularity as in (
5.17
). Moreover, making use of the embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
and Poincaré’s inequality, it follows

(5.24)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla r\cdot\nabla p_{t}p_{tt}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla r\|_{L^{3}}\|\nabla p_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\|p_{tt}\|_{L^{6}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon)\|\nabla r\|^{2}_{L^{3}}\|\sqrt{r}\nabla p_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\nabla p_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Again, we call on the same tools to estimate the two remaining terms on the right of (
5.21
). So we can show that

(5.25)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}r_{t}\Delta pp_{tt}\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\frac{r_{t}}{b}\Big\|_{L^{3}}\|p_{tt}\|_{L^{6}}\|\sqrt{b}\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon)\|r_{t}\|_{L^{3}}^{2}\|\sqrt{b}\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

In addition, we have

(5.26)

\int_{\Omega}g_{t}p_{tt}\,\textup{d}x\leq C(\varepsilon)\|g_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|\nabla p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Collecting the above estimates and selecting
\varepsilon
small enough we obtain for all
t\geq 0

(5.27)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\Big(\|\sqrt{\alpha(t)}p_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{r(t)}\nabla p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\Big)+\frac{b}{2}\|\nabla p_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\Big(\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|\nabla r(t)\|^{2}_{L^{3}}\Big)E_{2}[p](t)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\Big\|\frac{1}{r}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{4}{d}-1}\|\Delta p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|g_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Now, we focus on establishing estimates for
\|\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|\Delta p_{t}\|_{L^{2}}
. Testing the equation (
5.1
) by
-\Delta p
and integrating over
\Omega
gives

(5.28)

\frac{b}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\int_{\Omega}r|\Delta p|^{2}\,\textup{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}g(\Delta p)\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\alpha p_{tt}\Delta p\,\textup{d}x.

Applying Hölder, Young and Poincaré inequalities, we get the following bound

(5.29)

\displaystyle\frac{b}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{r}\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\frac{2}{b}\|\sqrt{b}\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C(\varepsilon)\|\sqrt{b}\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\nabla p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Adding up (
5.29
) and (
5.27
), now the last term on the right of (
5.29
) can be absorbed by the left-hand side of (
5.27
). Hence, we get

(5.30)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}E_{2}[p](t)+\frac{b}{3}\|\nabla p_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{r(t)}\Delta p(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(1+\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|\nabla r(t)\|^{2}_{L^{3}})E_{2}[p](t)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\Big\|\frac{1}{r}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{4}{d}-1}\|\Delta p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|g(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|g_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Next, we multiply the equation (
5.1
) by
-\Delta p_{t}
and we integrate over
\Omega
to get

(5.31)

b\|\Delta p_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\int_{\Omega}\alpha p_{tt}\Delta p_{t}\,\textup{d}x-\int_{\Omega}r\Delta p\Delta p_{t}\,\textup{d}x+\int_{\Omega}g(-\Delta p_{t})\,\textup{d}x.

Using Hölder and Young inequalities and recalling Assumption
2
, we obtain

(5.32)

\displaystyle b\|\Delta p_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle C(\varepsilon)\Big(\|\sqrt{\alpha}p_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|r\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\sqrt{b}\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\Big)+\varepsilon\|\Delta p_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Taking
\varepsilon
small enough, we find

(5.33)

\frac{b}{2}\|\Delta p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\lesssim E_{2}[p](t)+\|g(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}},\quad t\geq 0.

Thus, putting (
5.30
) and (
5.33
) together, we have

(5.34)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}E_{2}[p](t)+\frac{b}{3}\|\nabla p_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{r(t)}\Delta p(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{b}{3}\|\Delta p_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\Big(1+\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|\nabla r(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}\Big)E_{2}[p](t)

\displaystyle+\|g(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|g_{t}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Finally, summing up the estimates (
5.19
), (
5.34
) and selecting
\varepsilon
small enough in the last term on the right-hand side of (
5.19
), we obtain (
5.11
). This completes the proof of Lemma
5.1
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
g\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
g_{t}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then, under Assumption
2
, the energy
E_{3}
satisfies for
t\geq 0
the estimate

(5.35)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}E_{3}[p](t)+\|\sqrt{r}\nabla\Delta p(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{\alpha}p_{ttt}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(1+\Lambda_{2}(t))E_{3}[p](t)+\|\sqrt{b}\Delta p_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla p_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla g(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|g_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

where

(5.36)

Proof.

We test the linearized Westervelt equation (
5.1
) by
\Delta^{2}p
and we integrate over
\Omega
. Using integration by parts, we obtain

(5.37)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(r|\nabla\Delta p|^{2}+b\nabla\Delta p_{t}\cdot\nabla\Delta p)\,\textup{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(-\alpha\nabla p_{tt}-p_{tt}\nabla\alpha-\Delta p\nabla r-\nabla g)\cdot\nabla\Delta p\,\textup{d}x.

(5.38)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\sqrt{b}\nabla\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{r}\nabla\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\alpha

\displaystyle\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla\alpha\|_{L^{3}}\|p_{tt}\|_{L^{6}}\|\nabla\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla r\|_{L^{3}}\|\Delta p\|_{L^{6}}\|\nabla\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}.

Moreover, taking advantage of Assumption
2
, the embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
and applying Young’s inequality, we find

(5.39)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\sqrt{b}\nabla\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{r}\nabla\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\varepsilon)\Big(1+\|\nabla\alpha\|_{L^{3}}^{2}\Big)\|\sqrt{b}\nabla\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|\nabla p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+C(\varepsilon)\Big(\|\nabla r\|_{L^{3}}^{2}\|\sqrt{b}\nabla\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla g\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\Big)+\varepsilon\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\|\sqrt{r}\nabla\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We can fix
\varepsilon
as small as needed to absorb the last term on the right-hand side into the dissipative term on the left-hand side. Hence, we have

(5.40)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\sqrt{b}\nabla\Delta p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{r}\nabla\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(1+\|\nabla\alpha\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|\nabla r\|_{L^{3}}^{2})\|\sqrt{b}\nabla\Delta p\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla g\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\|\nabla p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Next, we multiply the time-differentiated equation (
5.20
) by
p_{ttt}
and we integrate over
\Omega
to find

(5.41)

\frac{b}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\nabla p_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{\alpha}p_{ttt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\int_{\Omega}(-\alpha_{t}p_{tt}+r_{t}\Delta p+r\Delta p_{t}+g_{t})p_{ttt}\,\textup{d}x.

Our goal now is to estimate the terms of the right-hand side of (
5.41
).

First, we have by using Hölder’s inequality

(5.42)

\displaystyle\frac{b}{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\nabla p_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\sqrt{\alpha}p_{ttt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{t}\|_{L^{3}}\|p_{tt}\|_{L^{6}}\|p_{ttt}\|_{L^{2}}+\|r_{t}\|_{L^{3}}\|\Delta p\|_{L^{6}}\|p_{ttt}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|r\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\Delta p_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\|p_{ttt}\|_{L^{2}}+\|g_{t}\|_{L^{2}}\|p_{ttt}\|_{L^{2}}.

Furthermore, making use of Young’s inequality together with the embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)

\displaystyle+\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4}{4-d}}+\|\nabla r(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|r_{t}(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\alpha_{t}(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|\nabla r(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2}+\|\nabla\alpha(t)\|_{L^{3}}^{2},

so altogether the above estimates imply that

(6.24)

\|\Lambda\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}\leq C_{1}(T,R_{1},R_{2}).

Now, we turn our attention to the source term
g
. Using Hölder’s inequality, the embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Omega)
,
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
and the fact that
\|(p^{\ast}_{t})^{2}\|_{L^{3}}=\|p^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{6}}^{2}
, we have

(6.25)

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{2}H^{1}}+\|g_{t}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}=

\displaystyle\|4k(\Theta^{\ast})\nabla p^{\ast}_{t}p^{\ast}_{t}+2k^{\prime}(\Theta^{\ast})\nabla\Theta^{\ast}(p^{\ast}_{t})^{2}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|4k(\Theta^{\ast})p^{\ast}_{t}p^{\ast}_{tt}+2k^{\prime}(\Theta^{\ast})\Theta^{\ast}_{t}(p^{\ast}_{t})^{2}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|k(\Theta^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|\nabla p^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}\|p^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}

\displaystyle+\|k^{\prime}(\Theta^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|\nabla\Theta^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{6}}\|(p^{\ast}_{t})^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{3}}

\displaystyle+\|k(\Theta^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|p^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\|p^{\ast}_{tt}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}

\displaystyle+\|k^{\prime}(\Theta^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|\Theta^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{2}L^{6}}\|(p^{\ast}_{t})^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{3}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|p^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{2}H^{2}}\|p^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}H^{1}}+(1+\|\Theta^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{\gamma_{2}+1})\|\Theta^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}H^{2}}\|p^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}H^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|p^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}H^{1}}\|p^{\ast}_{tt}\|_{L^{2}H^{1}}+(1+\|\Theta^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{\gamma_{2}+1})\|\Theta^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{2}H^{1}}\|p^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}H^{1}}^{2},

which gives

(6.26)

\|g\|_{L^{2}H^{1}}+\|g_{t}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\leq C_{T}R_{1}^{2}(1+R_{2}+R_{2}^{2+\gamma_{2}}).

Thus, we can bound from above the function
\mathfrak{F}
defined in (
5.10
) as

(6.27)

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{F}\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}=\int_{0}^{t}(\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|g_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2})\,\textup{d}s\leq

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{2}H^{1}}^{2}+\|g_{t}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{T}R_{1}^{4}(1+R_{2}^{2}(1+R_{2}^{2+2\gamma_{2}})).

Hence, it results that

(6.28)

\|\mathfrak{F}\|_{L^{1}(0,t)}\leq R_{1}^{4}C_{2}(T,R_{2}).

Consequently, from Proposition
5.1
we have the existence of a unique solution
p\in X_{p}
to the first equation in (
6.8
). Moreover, since
(p^{\ast},\Theta^{\ast},q^{\ast})\in B
, we have
f=\mathcal{Q}(p^{\ast})\in H^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Then, according to Proposition
4.1
, there exists a unique solution
(\Theta,q)\in X_{\Theta}\times X_{q}
of the second and third equations in (
6.8
). That is to say, the mapping
\mathcal{T}
is well-defined.

On account of (
5.8
) and the fact that
\mathfrak{E}[p](0)\leq\delta
, we obtain

(6.29)

\displaystyle\|p\|_{X_{p}}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\mathfrak{E}[p](t)+\sup_{t\in(0,T)}b\|\Delta p_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\mathfrak{D}[p](s)\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\delta\exp(T(1+C_{1}(T,R_{1},R_{2})))+TR_{1}^{4}\exp(T(1+C_{1}(T,R_{1},R_{2})))C_{2}(T,R_{2}).

Thus, by choosing
\delta
and
R_{1}
small enough, we get

(6.30)

\|p\|_{X_{p}}\leq R_{1}.

Also, observing that

(6.31)

\|p\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\Delta p\|_{L^{\infty}L^{2}}\lesssim\|p\|_{X_{p}},

we obtain an upper bound
\gamma<\dfrac{1}{2k_{1}}
for
\|p\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}
by possibly reducing
R_{1}
.

Therefore, it remains to verify that
\|(\Theta,q)\|_{X_{\Theta}\times X_{q}}\leq R_{2}
. First, we have by employing Hölder’s inequality

(6.32)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{2}L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|(p_{t}^{\ast})^{2}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}+\|2p_{t}^{\ast}p_{tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}+\|2(p_{tt}^{\ast})^{2}+2p_{t}^{\ast}p_{ttt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|p_{t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}^{2}\|p_{t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}^{2}+\|p_{t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}^{2}\|p_{tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|p_{tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}^{2}\|p_{tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}^{2}+\|p_{t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{2}\|p_{ttt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}^{2}.

Thanks to the embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Omega),L^{\infty}(0,T)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(0,T)
, we find

(6.33)

\|f\|_{H^{2}L^{2}}^{2}\leq C_{T}\|p\|_{X_{p}}^{4}\leq C_{T}R_{1}^{4}.

Then, according to Proposition
4.1
and particularly the estimates (
4.22
) and (
4.23
), we get

(6.34)

\displaystyle\|(\Theta,q)\|_{X_{\Theta}\times X_{q}}^{2}\lesssim C_{T}\Big(\|q_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}}+(1+\bar{\tau}+\bar{\tau}^{2})(E^{\bar{\tau}}[\Theta,q](0)+\|f\|_{H^{2}L^{2}}^{2})\Big).

We emphasize that the constant
C_{T}>0
in this inequality does not depend on the parameter
\tau
. So it suffices to take
R_{2}
large enough such that

(6.35)

C_{T}\Big(\|q_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}}+(1+\bar{\tau}+\bar{\tau}^{2})(E^{\bar{\tau}}[\Theta,q](0)+R_{1}^{4})\Big)\leq R_{2}^{2},

in order to conclude that the solution
(p,\Theta,q)
of the system (
6.8
) remains in
B
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\tau\in(0,\bar{\tau}]
. If
R_{1}
and
\delta
are sufficiently small, then the mapping
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction on
B
.

Proof.

Let
(p^{\ast}_{1},\Theta^{\ast}_{1},q_{1}^{\ast}),(p^{\ast}_{2},\Theta^{\ast}_{2},q_{2}^{\ast})\in B
and let
(p_{1},\Theta_{1},q_{1}),(p_{2},\Theta_{2},q_{2})
stand for their corresponding images by the operator
\mathcal{T}
; that is,

(6.36)

\mathcal{T}(p^{\ast}_{1},\Theta^{\ast}_{1},q_{1}^{\ast})=(p_{1},\Theta_{1},q_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{T}(p^{\ast}_{2},\Theta^{\ast}_{2},q_{2}^{\ast})=(p_{2},\Theta_{2},q_{2}).

Since
(p_{1},\Theta_{1},q_{1}),(p_{2},\Theta_{2},q_{2})
are both solutions to system (
6.8
), clearly the differences

(6.37)

\displaystyle\hat{p}=p_{1}-p_{2},\quad\hat{\Theta}=\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2},\quad\hat{q}=q_{1}-q_{2},

\displaystyle\hat{p}^{\ast}=p_{1}^{\ast}-p_{2}^{\ast},\quad\hat{\Theta}^{\ast}=\Theta_{1}^{\ast}-\Theta_{2}^{\ast},\quad\hat{q}^{\ast}=q_{1}^{\ast}-q_{2}^{\ast}

solve the following system

(6.38)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(1-2k(\Theta^{\ast}_{1})p_{1}^{\ast})\hat{p}_{tt}-h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\Delta\hat{p}-b\Delta\hat{p}_{t}=g_{1},&\qquad&\\
&m\hat{\Theta}_{t}+\nabla\cdot\hat{q}+\ell\hat{\Theta}=f_{1},\\
&\tau\hat{q}_{t}+\hat{q}+\kappa_{\textup{a}}\nabla\hat{\Theta}=0,\\
\end{aligned}\text{in}\quad\Omega\times(0,T),\right.

with the initial and boundary conditions

(6.39)

\displaystyle\hat{p}(x,0)=\hat{p}_{t}(x,0)=\hat{\Theta}(x,0)=0,\quad

\displaystyle\hat{q}(x,0)=0,

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\ \Omega.

\displaystyle\hat{p}=\hat{\Theta}=0,

\displaystyle\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega\times(0,T).

The forcing terms
f_{1}
and
g_{1}
are given by

(6.40)

\displaystyle f_{1}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(p_{1t}^{\ast})-\mathcal{Q}(p_{2t}^{\ast}),

\displaystyle g_{1}=

\displaystyle 2(k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})p_{1}^{\ast}-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})p_{2}^{\ast})p_{2tt}+(h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\Delta p_{2}+2k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})(p^{\ast}_{1t})^{2}-2k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})(p^{\ast}_{2t})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))(p_{2}^{\ast}p_{2tt}+(p^{\ast}_{2t})^{2})+(h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\Delta p_{2}

\displaystyle+2k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\big((p^{\ast}_{1t}+p^{\ast}_{2t})\hat{p}^{\ast}_{t}+\hat{p}^{\ast}p_{2tt}\big)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle g_{11}+g_{12}+g_{13}.

We start by recalling some estimates derived in
[
22
]
on the functions
k,h
and their derivatives. We have

(6.41)

k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})=(\Theta_{1}^{\ast}-\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\int_{0}^{1}k^{\prime}(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}+\sigma(\Theta_{1}^{\ast}-\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\textup{d}\sigma;

hence using (
K2
), together with the Sobolev embedding
H^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
, we get

(6.42a)

\displaystyle\|k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\Big\|(\Theta_{1}^{\ast}-\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\int_{0}^{1}k^{\prime}(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}+\sigma(\Theta_{1}^{\ast}-\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\textup{d}\sigma\Big\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\Theta_{1}^{\ast}-\Theta_{2}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\Big(1+\|\Theta_{2}^{\ast}+\sigma(\Theta_{1}^{\ast}-\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{1+\gamma_{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(1+\|\Theta_{1}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{1+\gamma_{2}}+\|\Theta_{2}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{1+\gamma_{2}}\Big).

In a similar fashion, on account of (
H2
), (
H3
) and (
K2
), we can show that

(6.42b)

\|k^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k^{\prime}(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(1+\|\Theta_{1}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{\gamma_{2}}+\|\Theta_{2}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{\gamma_{2}}\Big);

(6.43a)

\displaystyle\|h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(1+\|\Theta_{1}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{1+\gamma_{1}}+\|\Theta_{2}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{1+\gamma_{1}}\Big);

(6.43b)

\displaystyle\|h^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h^{\prime}(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(1+\|\Theta_{1}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{\gamma_{1}}+\|\Theta_{2}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}^{\gamma_{1}}\Big).

These estimates will be repeatedly used in the course of this proof.

First, we focus on estimating the
L^{2}
-norm of the gradient of
g_{1}
. Recall that

(6.44)

g_{11}=2(k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))(p_{2}^{\ast}p_{2tt}+(p^{\ast}_{2t})^{2}).

This entails that

(6.45)

\displaystyle\nabla g_{11}=

\displaystyle 2\nabla(k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))(p_{2}^{\ast}p_{2tt}+(p^{\ast}_{2t})^{2})

\displaystyle+2(k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\nabla(p_{2}^{\ast}p_{2tt}+(p^{\ast}_{2t})^{2}).

Since the heat flux
q
is not present in the expression of the source term
g_{1}
; we can proceed as in
[
22
, Section 4]
to estimate
\|\nabla g_{11}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}
. The fact that the operator
\mathcal{T}
is self-mapping along with the estimate

(6.46)

\|\Theta_{1}^{\ast}-\Theta_{2}^{\ast}\|_{X_{\Theta}}^{2}+\|p_{1}^{\ast}-p_{2}^{\ast}\|_{X_{p}}^{2}\leq\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2},

allows us to obtain the following bound

(6.47)

\displaystyle\|\nabla g_{11}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\leq C_{T}R_{1}^{2}(1+R_{2}+R_{2}^{1+\gamma_{2}})\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Analogously, we can follow the arguments in
[
22
]
in order to deal with the gradient of the second contribution

(6.48)

\nabla g_{12}=\nabla(h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\Delta p_{2}+h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\nabla\Delta p_{2}.

In fact, using the assumption (
H3
) on the function
h
together with (
6.43a
) and (
6.43b
), we find

(6.49)

\|\nabla g_{12}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\leq C_{T}R_{1}(1+R_{2}+R_{2}^{1+\gamma_{1}})\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Thus, it remains to bound the gradient of the last component of
g_{1}

(6.50)

\nabla g_{13}=2\big(\nabla(k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast}))\big((p^{\ast}_{1t}+p^{\ast}_{2t})\hat{p}^{\ast}_{t}+\hat{p}^{\ast}p_{2tt}\big)+k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\nabla\big((p^{\ast}_{1t}+p^{\ast}_{2t})\hat{p}^{\ast}_{t}+\hat{p}^{\ast}p_{2tt}\big)\big).

Again, we can do as in
[
22
]
to get

(6.51)

\|\nabla g_{13}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\leq C_{T}R_{1}(1+R_{2}+R_{2}^{\gamma_{2}+2})\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Collecting the estimates of the components of
g_{1}
, it follows that

(6.52)

\|\nabla g_{1}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\leq R_{1}C(T,R_{1},R_{2})\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Next, we seek a similar estimate for the derivative in time of
g_{1}
. Differentiating
g_{11}
with respect to
t
, we have

(6.53)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{11}=

\displaystyle 2\Big(\partial_{t}(k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))(p_{2}^{\ast}p_{2tt}+(p^{\ast}_{2t})^{2})+(k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\partial_{t}(p_{2}^{\ast}p_{2tt}+(p^{\ast}_{2t})^{2})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Big(\big(k^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\hat{\Theta}^{\ast}_{t}+(k^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k^{\prime}(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\Theta_{2t}^{\ast}\big)(p_{2}^{\ast}p_{2tt}+(p^{\ast}_{2t})^{2})

\displaystyle+(k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))(p_{2t}^{\ast}p_{2tt}+p_{2}^{\ast}p_{2ttt}+2p^{\ast}_{2t}p^{\ast}_{2tt})\Big).

Using Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

(6.54)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}g_{11}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\big(\|k^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|\hat{\Theta}^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}+\|k^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k^{\prime}(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|\Theta_{2t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}\big)

\displaystyle\times\big(\|p_{2}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|p_{2tt}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}+\|p_{2t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|p_{2t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\big)

\displaystyle+\|k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-k(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\big(\|p_{2t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\|p_{2tt}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}+\|p_{2}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|p_{2ttt}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|p^{\ast}_{2t}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\|p^{\ast}_{2tt}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}\big)

Then taking into account (
K2
), (
6.42a
), (
6.42b
) and using the embedding
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Omega)
, it follows

(6.55)

\|\partial_{t}g_{11}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\leq C_{T}R_{1}^{2}(1+R_{2}+R_{2}^{1+\gamma_{2}})\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

The time derivative of
g_{12}
is given by

(6.56)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{12}=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\Delta p_{2}+(h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\Delta p_{2t},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(h^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\hat{\Theta}^{\ast}_{t}+(h^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h^{\prime}(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\Theta_{2t}^{\ast}\big)\Delta p_{2}+(h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h(\Theta_{2}^{\ast}))\Delta p_{2t}.

Hence, it holds that

(6.57)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}g_{11}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\big(\|h^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|\hat{\Theta}^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}+\|h^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h^{\prime}(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|\Theta_{2t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}\big)\|\Delta p_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}

\displaystyle+\|h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})-h(\Theta_{2}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|\Delta p_{2t}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}.

Thanks to (
H3
), (
6.43a
) and (
6.43b
), the above estimate implies that

(6.58)

\|\partial_{t}g_{12}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\leq C_{T}R_{1}(1+R_{2}+R_{2}^{1+\gamma_{1}})\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Differentiating in time the last contribution
g_{13}
, we find

(6.59)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{13}=

\displaystyle 2\big(k^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\Theta_{1t}^{\ast}\big((p^{\ast}_{1t}+p^{\ast}_{2t})\hat{p}^{\ast}_{t}+\hat{p}^{\ast}p_{2tt}\big)

\displaystyle+k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\big((p^{\ast}_{1tt}+p^{\ast}_{2tt})\hat{p}^{\ast}_{t}+(p^{\ast}_{1t}+p^{\ast}_{2t})\hat{p}^{\ast}_{tt}+\hat{p}^{\ast}_{t}p_{2tt}+\hat{p}^{\ast}p_{2ttt}\big)\big).

Applying Hölder’s inequality yields

(6.60)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}g_{13}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|k^{\prime}(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|\Theta_{1t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}\big(\|p^{\ast}_{1t}+p^{\ast}_{2t}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\|\hat{p}^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}+\|\hat{p}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|p_{2tt}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\big)

\displaystyle+\|k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\big(\|p^{\ast}_{1tt}+p^{\ast}_{2tt}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}\|\hat{p}^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}+\|p^{\ast}_{1t}+p^{\ast}_{2t}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\|\hat{p}^{\ast}_{tt}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}

\displaystyle+\|\hat{p}^{\ast}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\|p_{2tt}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}+\|\hat{p}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|p_{2ttt}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\big).

Then, from the assumption (
K2
) and the fact that the mapping
\mathcal{T}
is self-mapping, we arrive at

(6.61)

\|\partial_{t}g_{13}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\leq C_{T}R_{1}(1+R_{2}+R_{2}^{2+\gamma_{2}})\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Putting together the above estimates results in

(6.62)

\|\partial_{t}g_{1}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\leq R_{1}C(T,R_{1},R_{2})\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

As for the source term
f_{1}
in the temperature equation, we can handle it as follows

(6.63)

\displaystyle\|f_{1}\|_{H^{2}L^{2}}=

\displaystyle\|f_{1}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}+\|f_{1t}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}+\|f_{1tt}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|(p_{1t}^{\ast})^{2}-(p_{2t}^{\ast})^{2}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}+\|(p_{1tt}^{\ast}-p_{2tt}^{\ast})(p_{1t}^{\ast})\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}+\|(p_{1t}^{\ast}-p_{2t}^{\ast})(p_{2tt}^{\ast})\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|(p_{1ttt}^{\ast}-p_{2ttt}^{\ast})p_{1t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}+\|(p_{1tt}^{\ast}-p_{2tt}^{\ast})p_{1tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}

\displaystyle+\|(p_{1tt}^{\ast}-p_{2tt}^{\ast})p_{2tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}+\|(p_{1t}^{\ast}-p_{2t}^{\ast})p_{2ttt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}.

Thus, we get

(6.64)

\displaystyle\|f_{1}\|_{H^{2}L^{2}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|p_{1t}^{\ast}-p_{2t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|p_{1t}^{\ast}+p_{2t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}+\|p_{1tt}^{\ast}-p_{2tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}\|p_{1t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}

\displaystyle+\|p_{1t}^{\ast}-p_{2t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\|p_{2tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}+\|p_{1ttt}^{\ast}-p_{2ttt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}\|p_{1t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle+\|p_{1tt}^{\ast}-p_{2tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\|p_{1tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}+\|p_{1tt}^{\ast}-p_{2tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{4}}\|p_{2tt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{4}}

\displaystyle+\|p_{1t}^{\ast}-p_{2t}^{\ast}\|_{L^{\infty}L^{\infty}}\|p_{2ttt}^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}L^{2}}.

Using the embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{4}(\Omega)
,
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and elliptic regularity, we deduce that

(6.65)

\|f_{1}\|_{H^{2}L^{2}}\leq R_{1}C_{T}\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

From the previous Lemma
6.1
, we know that the coefficients of the first equation in the system (
6.38
)

\alpha_{1}=1-2k(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})p_{1}^{\ast},\qquad r_{1}=h(\Theta_{1}^{\ast})

satisfy the requirements of Proposition
5.1
. Further, setting
g=g_{1}
and
f=f_{1}
and taking into account the above estimates, we deduce that the results of the Propositions
4.1
,
5.1
hold. In particular, the energy estimates (
4.22
), (
4.23
) and (
5.8
) hold for the solution
(\hat{p},\hat{\Theta},\hat{q})
; that is

(6.66)

\displaystyle\|(\hat{p},\hat{\Theta},\hat{q})\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}(p^{\ast}_{1},\Theta^{\ast}_{1},q^{\ast}_{1})-\mathcal{T}(p^{\ast}_{2},\Theta^{\ast}_{2},q^{\ast}_{2})\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\mathfrak{E}[\hat{p}](t)+\sup_{t\in(0,T)}b\|\Delta\hat{p}_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\mathfrak{D}[\hat{p}](s)\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle+\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\mathcal{E}[\hat{\Theta}](t)+\|\hat{q}\|_{L^{\infty}H^{1}}^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{t}^{k}\hat{q}(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\exp\big(\int_{0}^{T}(1+\Lambda(s))\,\textup{d}s\big)\int_{0}^{T}(\|\nabla g_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|g_{1t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2})\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle+(1+\bar{\tau}+\bar{\tau}^{2})\|f_{1}\|_{H^{2}L^{2}}^{2}.

Hence, due to estimates (
6.24
), (
6.52
), (
6.62
) and (
6.65
), we find

(6.67)

\displaystyle\|(\hat{p},\hat{\Theta},\hat{q})\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle R_{1}^{2}C(T,R_{1},R_{2})\exp(T+TC_{1}(T,R_{1},R_{2}))\big\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\big\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}

\displaystyle+(1+\bar{\tau}+\bar{\tau}^{2})R_{1}^{2}C_{T}^{2}\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2},

where
C_{1}(T,R_{1},R_{2})
is the constant in (
6.24
). From here, we infer that

(6.68)

\|\mathcal{T}(p^{\ast}_{1},\Theta^{\ast}_{1},q^{\ast}_{1})-\mathcal{T}(p^{\ast}_{2},\Theta^{\ast}_{2},q^{\ast}_{2})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\lesssim R_{1}C(T,R_{1},R_{2},\bar{\tau})\|(\hat{p}^{\ast},\hat{\Theta}^{\ast},\hat{q}^{\ast})\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Consequently, it suffices to select
R_{1}
as small as needed to ensure that the mapping
\mathcal{T}
is a strict contraction.
∎

7.

: Proof of Theorem

We aim in this section to prove Theorem

. More precisely, we prove that the solution of the Westervelt–Pennes–Cattaneo system (

) converges as

goes to zero, to the solution of the limiting system, namely the Westervelt–Pennes–Fourier system corresponding to

\displaystyle\text{in}

Next, we claim that for all

(7.17)
