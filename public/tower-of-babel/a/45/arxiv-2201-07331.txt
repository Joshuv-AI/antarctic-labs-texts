Bubble Nucleation to All Orders
Andreas Ekstedt , a, b c

Abstract
This paper extends classical results by Langer and Kramers [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] and combines them with modern methods from high-temperature field theory [ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] . Assuming Langevin dynamics, the end-product is an all-orders description of bubble-nucleation at high temperatures. Specifically, it is shown that equilibrium and non-equilibrium effects factorize to all orders—the nucleation rate splits into a statistical and a dynamical prefactor. The derivation clarifies, and incorporates, higher-order corrections from zero-modes [ 9 , 10 , 11 ] . The rate is also shown to be real to all orders in perturbation theory. The methods are applied to several models. As such, Feynman rules are given; the relevant power-counting is introduced; RG invariance is shown; the connection with the effective action is discussed, and an explicit construction of propagators in an inhomogeneous background is given. The formalism applies to both phase and Sphaleron transitions. While mainly focused on field theory, the methods are applicable to finite-dimensional systems. Finally, as this paper assumes an effective Langevin description [ 12 , 6 , 13 , 14 , 7 , 4 , 5 ] , all results only hold within this framework.

2.1 High-temperature corrections

Perturbative calculations converge slower at high-temperatures. To see this, consider a scalar field with mass
m^{2}
. At high temperatures this mass is changed by thermal corrections: to one loop
m^{2}\rightarrow m^{2}_{\text{eff}}=m^{2}+aT^{2}
, where
a

T^{2}\gg m^{2}\gg m^{2}_{\text{eff}}
. This means that two-loop thermal masses are of similar size as

, and in addition, logarithms of the form
\log T^{2}/m^{2}_{\text{eff}}
enhance higher-order corrections.

These problems can be removed by integrating out high-energy modes with momenta
k\sim T

[
48
,
49
,
46
]
. The resulting effective theory lives in three spatial dimensions; effective masses and couplings in this theory automatically incorporate thermal resummations. This effective theory can be used to both calculate equilibrium quantities and the nucleation rate
[

Yet thermal escape is a classical process.

To illustrate the physics it is useful to consider a classical particle moving in three dimensions. We assume that the potential of this particle has one metastable minimum at
\vec{x}=\vec{x}_{S}

\vec{x}=\vec{x}_{\text{TV}}

If this was a tunneling process, the particle would start at the metastable minimum and go through the barrier with some probability. The most likely path of escape, the bounce, results in a negative eigenvalue, which gives an imaginary energy. The rate is identified with
\Gamma=-2\text{Im}\mathcal{E}

But the picture is different for thermal escape, where the particle travels over the barrier.
That is, the probability for the particle to have an energy

. This means that the particle is most likely to escape where the barrier is lowest (
V_{B}=V(x)|_{x=\vec{x}_{B}}

\Gamma\sim e^{-V_{B}/T}
. Although, the particle can also escape close-by

by moving slightly in the

A better approximation is

What about non-equilibrium physics? When the particle jumps over the barrier, it first needs to get sufficient energy to escape, and then move to the other side. If the
negative

\kappa

Intuitively the Boltzmann factor describes the probability to jump up to, and the dynamical factor describes the flow from, the barrier. To leading order this flow is in the concave direction, while higher-order corrections make the flow veer down the barrier from orthogonal directions.

Note that it does not matter that

is concave in the

3.2 Corrections to the statistical prefactor

Consider the statistical prefactor. As mentioned, the negative eigenvalue does not cause any issues because everything is calculated at
u=0
.

To proceed, consider the integral in Equation
3.12
:

\displaystyle Z[\overline{U}]=\int d^{n}xe^{iku-1/T\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{i}\omega^{ij}x_{j}\right)}.

(3.17)

Integrating over
k
enforces
u=0
, and we leave this integration for last.

Barring some subtleties with the negative eigenvalue, the integrals give

\displaystyle Z[\overline{U}]=\left[\text{det}^{\prime}\omega/(2\pi)\right]^{-1/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}T\overline{U}^{i}\omega^{-1}_{ij}\overline{U}^{j}}.

(3.18)

Technically
\overline{U}^{i}\omega^{-1}_{ij}\overline{U}^{j}<0
, so the integral over
k
formally diverges. Being more careful one can let
k\rightarrow-ik
and do a Wick-rotation. This gives

\displaystyle Z[\overline{U}]=\left[|\text{det}^{\prime}\omega/(2\pi)|\right]^{-1/2}e^{\frac{1}{2}k^{2}T\overline{U}^{i}\omega^{-1}_{ij}\overline{U}^{j}}.

(3.19)

We identify
Z[\overline{U}]
as a generating function: correlators can be calculated via

\displaystyle<x^{i}x^{j}>\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\int d^{n}xx^{i}x^{j}e^{iku-1/T\left(x_{i}\omega^{ij}x_{j}\right)}\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk(k)^{-2}\frac{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt^{2}}{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt\overline{U}^{i}\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt\overline{U}^{j}}Z.

(3.20)

We see that
Z[\overline{U}]
is a generating function with a non-vanishing current. In terms of Feynman diagrams this means that in addition to usual diagrams, there are now diagrams with external-current insertions.

To see the form of these external-current insertions, consider the potential

\displaystyle V(x+x_{B})=V_{B}+\frac{1}{2}x_{i}\omega^{ij}x_{j}+\frac{1}{4!}\lambda_{4}^{ijkl}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}x_{l}.

(3.21)

3.3 Corrections to the dynamical prefactor

Consider now the dynamical prefactor. Again, we assume a potential

\displaystyle V(x+x_{B})=V_{B}+\frac{1}{2}x_{i}\omega^{ij}x_{j}+\frac{1}{3!}\lambda^{ijk}_{3}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}+\frac{1}{4!}\lambda_{4}^{ijkl}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}x_{l}.

(3.24)

Including higher-order terms in
V
does two things. First, the Boltzmann factor changes. This is encoded in the statistical prefactor. Second, the rate of flow across the saddle-point changes. This flow is controlled by deviations from thermal equilibrium, which is encoded in the dynamical prefactor.

Because Equation
3.6
describes the deviation from equilibrium, we need to find
\zeta
to higher orders. Explicitly, we need to solve
T\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{i}^{2}\zeta-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{i}V\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt^{i}\zeta=0
with the inclusion of
\lambda_{3}
and
\lambda_{4}
terms. The leading-order solution, denoted henceforth as
\zeta_{0}
, is given in Equation
3.10
.

The boundary conditions force
\zeta(\vec{x})
to vanish as
u\rightarrow\infty
, which is enforced directly by the ansatz

\displaystyle\zeta(\vec{x})=\zeta_{0}(u)+\zeta^{\prime}_{0}(u)\zeta_{1}(\vec{x})+\ldots

(3.25)

It is assumed that
\zeta_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left(\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}\right)
.

The equation for
\zeta_{1}
can be found by combing Equations
3.25
and
3.6
:

\displaystyle T\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{i}^{2}\zeta_{1}+\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{i}\zeta_{1}\left[2\kappa u\overline{U}^{i}-\omega^{ij}x_{j}\right]+\kappa\zeta_{1}=\left\langle\overline{U}x^{2}\right\rangle+\left\langle\overline{U}x^{3}\right\rangle.

(3.26)

We use the notation
\left\langle x^{3}\right\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{2!}\lambda_{3}^{ijk}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}
and
\left\langle x^{4}\right\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{3!}\lambda_{4}^{ijkl}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}x_{l}
.

All homogenous solutions of Equation
3.26
grow exponentially with
u
, and so do not satisfy the boundary conditions. This leaves the particular solution.

To find the particular solution, note that if we make a polynomial ansatz [in
x^{i}
], neither of the terms on the left-hand side of Equation
3.26
increase the polynomial’s degree. Whence the ansatz go through the equations. It is also useful to work in the eigenbasis of
\omega:\omega^{ij}\overline{V}^{j}_{a}=\lambda_{a}\overline{V}^{i}_{a}
. That is, we expand
x^{i}=u\overline{U}^{i}+\sum_{a}v_{a}\overline{V}_{a}^{i}
where
v_{a}=\overline{V}_{a}^{i}x_{i}
. This basis is useful because
\overline{V}_{a}^{i}
is orthogonal to
\overline{U}^{i}
and since
\omega^{ij}x_{j}
reduces to a sum of eigenvalues.

The idea is to make a polynomial ansatz in
u
and
v_{a}
, where
a
denote all eigenvalues except for the negative one.

For simplicity, consider first the two-dimensional case
(x^{i})=(x^{1},x^{2})
and ignore the
\left\langle x^{4}\right\rangle
term for the moment. That is, we assume the potential

\displaystyle V(x+x_{B})=V_{B}+\frac{1}{2}x_{i}\omega^{ij}x_{j}+\frac{1}{3!}\lambda^{ijk}_{3}x_{i}x_{j}x_{k}.

(3.27)

There are two eigenvectors:
\omega^{ij}\overline{U}^{j}=\kappa\overline{U}^{i}
and
\omega^{ij}\overline{V}^{j}=\lambda_{v}\overline{V}^{i}
. These eigenvectors define
u\equiv\overline{U}^{i}x_{i}
and
v=\overline{V}^{i}x_{i}
.

Expanding
x^{i}
in
u
and
v
gives

\displaystyle\left\langle\overline{U}x^{2}\right\rangle=u^{2}\left\langle\overline{U}^{3}\right\rangle+2uv\left\langle\overline{U}^{2}\overline{V}\right\rangle+v^{2}\left\langle\overline{U}\overline{V}^{2}\right\rangle.

(3.28)

Since
\left\langle\overline{U}x^{2}\right\rangle
is a degree
2
polynomial, the relevant ansatz is

\zeta_{1}=A+B_{1}u+B_{2}v+C_{1}u^{2}+C_{2}v^{2}+C_{3}uv.

After using this ansatz in Equation
3.26
, and collecting terms, one finds

\displaystyle B_{1}=B_{2}=0,\quad

\displaystyle A=-2T(C_{1}+C_{2})/\kappa,

\displaystyle C_{1}=\left(3\kappa\right)^{-1}\left\langle\overline{U}^{3}\right\rangle,\quad

\displaystyle C_{2}=\left(\kappa-2\lambda_{v}\right)^{-1}\left\langle\overline{U}\overline{V}^{2}\right\rangle,

(3.29)

\displaystyle C_{3}=2\left(2\kappa-\lambda_{v}\right)^{-1}\left\langle\overline{U}^{2}\overline{V}\right\rangle.

Note that most of the terms in
\zeta_{1}
are irrelevant. This is because the rate is given by
\int_{u=0}d^{n}x\overline{U}^{i}J^{i}\sim\int\overline{U}^{i}\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{i}\zeta_{1}|_{u=0}
.
For the above example only the
C_{3}
term contributes since if the derivative (
\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt^{i}
) hits anything else, that term is proportional to
u
or
\overline{U}^{i}\overline{V}^{i}
. Both which vanish. This holds in general: we only have to care about terms containing
one
factor of
u
.

The n-dimensional case is similar.
Including the quartic term in Equation
3.26
, the ansatz is a polynomial of degree
3
:

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}=A+B_{1}u+\sum_{a}B_{a}v_{a}+\ldots+u\sum C_{a}v_{a}+\ldots+u\sum_{a,b}D_{ab}v_{a}v_{b}+\ldots

(3.30)

Following the discussion above, only the
B_{1}
,
C_{a}
, and
D_{ab}
terms contribute to the rate. One finds

\displaystyle C_{a}=2\left(2\kappa-\lambda_{a}\right)^{-1}\left\langle\overline{U}^{2}\overline{V}_{a}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle D_{ab}=3\left(2\kappa-\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{b}\right)^{-1}\left\langle\overline{U}^{2}\overline{V}_{a}\overline{V}_{b}\right\rangle,

(3.31)

\displaystyle B_{1}=-6T(2\kappa^{-1})\left((4\kappa)^{-1}\left\langle\overline{U}^{4}\right\rangle+\sum_{a}(2\kappa-2\lambda_{a})^{-1}\left\langle\overline{U}^{2}\overline{V}^{2}_{a}\right\rangle\right).

The complete solution for
\zeta_{1}
is given in Appendix
B
, and the result for potentials with
x^{5}
and
x^{6}
terms is also given in Appendix
B
.

Note from Equation
3.3
that
\zeta_{1}
can be written in terms of Green’s functions of the form

\displaystyle\left(\omega_{ij}-c\delta_{ij}\right)G^{jk}_{c}=\delta_{ik}\implies G^{ij}_{c}=\sum_{a}\frac{\overline{V}^{i}_{a}\overline{V}^{j}_{a}}{\lambda_{a}-c}.

(3.32)

As before
a
excludes the negative eigenvalue.

Before showing how
\zeta_{1}
affects the rate, note that
\zeta_{1}
can considered as a set of operator insertions. To see this, pick one term from Equation
3.30
, for example
D_{ab}uv_{a}v_{b}
. When calculating the probability current, the factor of
u
disappears, and the term is proportional to
v_{a}v_{b}=\overline{V}_{a}^{i}\overline{V}_{b}^{j}x_{i}x_{j}
. Adding this term to Equation
3.12
, one could equally well have inserted an operator
\mathcal{O}\propto D_{ab}\overline{V}_{a}^{i}\overline{V}_{b}^{j}x_{i}x_{j}
. This operator can also receive loop corrections like in normal field theory.

Additional corrections to
\zeta
can be found by using
\zeta=\zeta_{0}+\zeta_{1}\zeta^{\prime}_{0}+\zeta_{2}\zeta^{\prime}_{0}+\ldots
. Where it is assumed that
\zeta_{n}=\mathcal{O}\left(\lambda^{n}\right)
. In general,
\zeta_{n}
is a polynomial of degree
4n-1
, and is completely determined by
\zeta_{n-1}
. Namely, for
n>0

\displaystyle T\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{i}^{2}\zeta_{n}+\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{i}\zeta_{n}\left[2\kappa u\overline{U}^{i}-\omega^{ij}x_{j}\right]+\kappa\zeta_{n}=

\displaystyle\left(\kappa\frac{u}{T}\zeta_{n-1}+\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{u}\zeta_{n-1}\right)\left(\left\langle\overline{U}x^{2}\right\rangle+\left\langle\overline{U}x^{3}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{a}\frac{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt}{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75ptv_{a}}\zeta_{n-1}\left(\left\langle\overline{V}^{a}x^{2}\right\rangle+\left\langle\overline{V}^{a}x^{3}\right\rangle\right)

(3.33)

Naively one would think that
\zeta_{2}
is sub-leading compared to
\zeta_{1}
. However, from Equation
3.30
we see that the
c_{a}
term does not contribute directly to the rate. Instead this term contributes via the one-loop tadpole shown in Figure
3
.

Figure 3
:

This tadpole insertion scales as
\lambda_{3}^{2}
, and similar terms exist in
\zeta_{2}
. As such, all
\lambda_{3}^{2}
terms in
\zeta_{2}
are relevant.

We here only note that these terms are of the form

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}=uB^{\prime}+uD^{\prime}_{ab}v_{a}v_{b}+uF_{abcd}v_{a}v_{b}v_{c}v_{d}+\ldots

(3.34)

The explicit expressions are given in Equation

In summary, the dynamical prefactor consists of operator insertions, which multiply the full statistical prefactor.

4  Generic damping coefficients

Consider now the generic-damping case. There are a few new features as compared to the overdamped system. First, the velocity, or rather the conjugate momenta, contribute to the Boltzmann factor. Second, the damping is not solely a multiplicative factor as in Equation
3.15
. Third, the negative-eigenvalue direction contributes to the Boltzmann integral.

As before, the starting point is the Fokker-Planck equation
[
70
,
1
,
68
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}P=-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt^{i}_{x}J_{x}^{i}-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt^{i}_{\pi}J_{\pi}^{i}=\left[-\pi_{i}\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{x}^{i}+\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{\pi}^{i}\left[\eta\pi^{i}+\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{x}^{i}V\right]+\eta T\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{\pi}^{i}\right]P=0,

(4.1)

here
\pi^{i}
are conjugate momenta associated with
x^{i}
, and
\eta
is the damping.

To derive the rate, we start by assuming

\displaystyle V(x+x_{B})\approx V_{B}+\frac{1}{2}x_{i}\omega^{ij}x_{j}.

(4.2)

Next, we make the ansatz
P=\zeta(\vec{x},\vec{v})P_{0}
where
P_{0}=e^{-E/T}
and
E=\frac{1}{2}\pi_{i}^{2}+V(x)
. Hence

\displaystyle\left[\pi^{i}\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{x}^{i}+\left[\eta\pi^{i}-\omega^{ij}x_{j}\right]\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{\pi}^{i}-\eta T\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{\pi}^{2}\right]\zeta=0.

(4.3)

Using the ansatz
\zeta(\vec{x},\vec{v})=\zeta(u)
, where
u=\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}\pi^{i}+\overline{U}_{x}^{i}x^{i}
, the equation becomes

\displaystyle\pi^{i}\overline{U}_{x}^{i}\zeta^{\prime}(u)+\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}\left[\eta\pi^{i}-\omega^{ij}x_{j}\right]\zeta^{\prime}(u)-\eta T\overline{U}_{\pi}^{2}\zeta^{\prime\prime}(u)=0.

(4.4)

For consistency

\displaystyle\pi^{i}\overline{U}_{x}^{i}+\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}\left[\eta\pi^{i}-\omega^{ij}x_{j}\right]=-\lambda u=-\lambda\left[\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}\pi^{i}+\overline{U}_{x}^{i}x^{i}\right].

(4.5)

This implies that
\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}
is an eigenvector of
\omega^{ij}
:
\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}\omega^{ij}x_{j}=\kappa\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}x_{i}
.

Thus

\displaystyle-\lambda\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}=\left(\overline{U}_{x}^{i}+\eta\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}\right)\hskip 5.69046pt\&\hskip 5.69046pt-\kappa\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}=-\lambda\overline{U}_{x}^{i},

(4.6)

so
\overline{U}_{x}^{i}
is also an eigenvector of
\omega^{ij}
.

Putting everything together one finds
[
1
,
66
]

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\left(\pm\sqrt{\eta^{2}-4\kappa}-\eta\right).

(4.7)

It will be shown below that
\lambda
needs to be positive, so perforce
\kappa<0
and we must choose
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{\eta^{2}-4\kappa}-\eta\right)
. Next, using Equations
4.4
and
4.6
gives
[
1
,
66
]

\displaystyle\gamma\zeta^{\prime\prime}(u)+u\zeta^{\prime}(u)=0,\quad\gamma=\eta T\frac{\lambda}{\kappa^{2}}>0.

(4.8)

The solution satisfying the boundary conditions is

\displaystyle\zeta(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}}\int_{u}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{u^{\prime 2}}{2\gamma}}du^{\prime}

(4.9)

Given
\zeta(u)
, it is straightforward to calculate the rate.
Explicitly
[
1
,
66
]

\displaystyle\Gamma=\frac{\overline{U}_{\pi}^{2}\eta}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}}\int_{u=0}d^{n}xd^{n}\pi e^{-E/T},

(4.10)

where
E=\frac{1}{2}\pi_{i}^{2}+V(x)
.

Doing the Gaussian integrals one finds up to a normalization

\displaystyle\Gamma=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\left|\text{det}^{\prime}\omega\right|^{-1/2}e^{-V_{B}/T}.

(4.11)

As before there are two types of contributions from higher orders. First, corrections to
\zeta
, and second, higher-order corrections to
E
in the integral
\int_{u=0}d^{n}xd^{n}ve^{-E/T}
.

Before calculating these corrections, note that the rate is still real. To see this, denote the negative-eigenvalue direction by
x_{u}\equiv\overline{U}_{x}^{i}x_{i}
. The problematic term in the energy is

\displaystyle E=-\frac{1}{2}|\kappa|x_{u}^{2}+\ldots

(4.12)

If the rate is calculated at
u=0
, we have
\pi_{u}=\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}\pi^{i}=\frac{|\kappa|}{\lambda}x_{u}
. Hence

\displaystyle-|\kappa|\rightarrow\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}-|\kappa|=\frac{1}{2}\eta\left(\sqrt{\eta^{2}+4|\kappa|^{2}}+\eta\right)>0.

(4.13)

We see that the Gaussian integral in Equation
4.10
converges, and that the rate is real.

4.1 Physical interpretation and applicability

The rate is calculated at
u=0
. Let us now see what this means. For clarity we use
x_{u}\equiv\overline{U}_{x}^{i}x_{i}
and
\pi_{u}\equiv\overline{U}_{\pi}^{i}\pi^{i}
. That is,
x_{u}
and
\pi_{u}
are the position and conjugate momentum in the negative-eigenvalue direction. Denote the energy in this direction by
E_{u}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\pi_{u}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa x_{u}^{2}
. Consider now the motion of a particle starting at the saddle point. To do so, recall the static Fokker-Planck equation:

\displaystyle\left[\pi_{i}\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{x}^{i}-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{\pi}^{i}\left[\eta\pi^{i}+\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{x}^{i}V\right]-\eta T\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{\pi}^{i}\right]P=0.

(4.14)

In addition, if
P=\zeta(\vec{x},\vec{\pi})e^{-E/T}
, the equation for
\zeta(\vec{x},\vec{\pi})
is

\displaystyle\left[\pi^{i}\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{x}^{i}+\left[\eta\pi^{i}-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt^{i}_{x}V\right]\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{\pi}^{i}-\eta T\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{\pi}^{2}\right]\zeta=0.

(4.15)

Namely, deviations from thermal equilibrium follow Equation
4.15
, which is known as the adjoint Fokker-Planck equation.

Now, the Fokker-Planck equation corresponds to a particle obeying

\displaystyle\dot{\pi}^{i}=-\eta\pi^{i}-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{x}^{i}V+f(t),

(4.16)

the last term represents thermal noise and vanishes on average.

Likewise, the backward Fokker-Planck equation follows from

\displaystyle\dot{\pi}^{i}=\eta\pi^{i}-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{x}^{i}V+f(t),

(4.17)

Imagine now releasing a particle close to
x_{u}=\pi_{u}=0
. If the particle evolves according to Equation
4.16
, an unstable solution is
[
1
]

\displaystyle x^{i}=x_{0}e^{\lambda t}\quad\pi^{i}=x_{0}\lambda e^{\lambda t}.

(4.18)

On this solution the energy is

\displaystyle E_{u}=-\frac{1}{2}\eta\lambda x_{0}^{2}e^{2t\lambda}.

(4.19)

As expected
\frac{d}{dt}E=-\eta v^{2}<0
.

Next, consider instead a particle following Equation
4.17
. The unstable solution is now

\displaystyle x^{i}=x_{0}e^{\frac{|\kappa|}{\lambda}t}\quad\pi^{i}=x_{0}\frac{|\kappa|}{\lambda}e^{\frac{|\kappa|}{\lambda}t},

(4.20)

with associated energy

\displaystyle E_{u}=\frac{1}{2}\eta\frac{|\kappa|}{\lambda}x_{0}^{2}e^{2\frac{|\kappa|}{\lambda}t}.

(4.21)

This is precisely the solution implied by
u=0
:
\pi_{u}=\frac{|\kappa|}{\lambda}x_{u}
. Note that the energy in Equation
4.21
suppresses fluctuations away from the saddle point via the Boltzmann factor. This is why the Boltzmann-integral is localized at
x_{u}=0
for
\eta\rightarrow\infty

Equation
4.21
also highlights a problem for small damping. Namely, for
\eta=0
the energy in Equation
4.21
identically vanishes. Effectively the negative-eigenmode turns into a zero-mode. While the rate is finite in the
\eta\rightarrow 0
limit to leading order, this does not generalize to higher orders.

There are other indications that the formalism breaks down for small damping
[
66
]
, and one should be cautious when using existing formulas
[

,
62
]
for an underdamped system.

\displaystyle S_{\text{eff}}[\phi_{b}]=S_{\text{LO}}[\phi_{b}]+S_{\text{NLO}}[\phi_{b}]+\ldots

\displaystyle\implies S_{\text{LO}}[\phi_{\text{LO}}]+\left[S_{\text{NLO}}[\phi_{\text{LO}}]-\frac{1}{2}\phi_{\text{NLO}}^{2}\delta^{2}S_{\text{LO}}[\phi_{\text{LO}}]\right]

(5.26)

Diagrammatically

The actual expression for

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{NLO}}(x)=-\int d^{3}y\Delta(x,y)\frac{\delta S_{\text{NLO}}[\phi_{\text{LO}}]}{\delta\phi(y)}.

(5.27)

This follows from the definition

\displaystyle-\left[-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}[\phi_{\text{LO}}(x)]\right]\phi_{\text{NLO}}(x)=\frac{\delta S_{\text{NLO}}[\phi_{\text{LO}}]}{\delta\phi(x)}.

(5.28)

Note that both the propagator, and implicitly the effective action, are defined with zero-modes projected out.

5.4 Renormalization-scale invariance of the rate

Calculations should be independent of the renormalization scale. To show that the rate is scale-independent is however non-trivial.

Note that renormalization-scale dependence first shows up first at two loops in three dimensions. Moreover, the two-loop beta function is the complete beta function, and only mass parameters depend on the scale in three dimensions. For the real-scalar model considered in Equation

Before considering the rate, it is instructive to first calculate the effective potential.

5.6 Factorization of the rate

In the previous sections we have seen that the rate factorizes as

\displaystyle\Gamma=A_{\text{dyn}}\times A_{\text{stat}}.

(5.50)

The statistical prefactor is

\displaystyle A_{\text{stat}}=\int_{u=0}\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S[\phi+\phi_{b}]}\times J_{ZM}\equiv e^{-\overline{S}_{\text{eff}}[\phi_{b}]}.

(5.51)

Here
\overline{S}_{\text{eff}}[\phi_{b}]
omits all contributions from negative and zero eigenmodes. The zero modes also generate operator insertions; these are included in
J_{ZM}
as discussed in Section
5.2
. For concrete calculations, the Feynman rules given in Section
5.1
can be used.

The rate should be normalized with
e^{-\int d^{3}xV_{\text{eff}}(\phi_{S})}
, where
V_{\text{eff}}(\phi_{S})
denotes the effective potential in the metastable state.
12
12

12

This last point is important since
A_{\text{stat}}
is not finite without the normalization. In practice
V_{\text{eff}}(\phi_{S})
should be included diagram-by-diagram as discussed in Section
6.2
.

The dynamical prefactor is defined as

\displaystyle A_{\text{dyn}}=\frac{1}{\eta\times A_{\text{stat}}}\int_{u=0}\mathcal{D}\phi\left[\int d^{3}x\overline{U}(x)\delta_{\phi(x)}\zeta(\phi)\right]e^{-S[\phi+\phi_{b}]}\times J_{ZM}.

(5.52)

The virtue of these definitions is that the renormalization-scale dependence for
A_{\text{dyn}}
and
A_{\text{stat}}
are simple; both
A_{\text{stat}}
and
A_{\text{dyn}}
are manifestly real to all orders;
A_{\text{dyn}}
and
A_{\text{stat}}
can be calculated independently; there is a clear physical interpretation with
A_{\text{stat}}
as the Boltzmann suppression, and
A_{\text{dyn}}
as the rate of probability-flow across the saddle-point.

To leading order
A_{\text{dyn}}
can be interpreted as the growth-rate of the bubble.

6  Considerations for concrete calculations

This section shows how to calculate propagators in an inhomogeneous bounce background; what diagrams appear at two loops; and how to isolate divergences in dimensional regularization.

6.1 Propagators

The propagator satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\left[-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}[\phi_{b}(x)]\right]\Delta(x,y)=\delta^{3}(x-y).

(6.1)

Because the bounce is spherically symmetric, it is useful to expand the propagator in spherical harmonics
[
80
]

\displaystyle\Delta(x,y)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)P_{l}(\cos\alpha)\Delta_{l}(r,r^{\prime}),

(6.2)

with the short-hand
r\equiv|\vec{x}|,\penalty\ r^{\prime}\equiv|\vec{y}|
and
\cos\alpha=\frac{\vec{x}\cdot\vec{y}}{|\vec{x}||\vec{y}|}
.

Here
\Delta_{l}(r,r^{\prime})
satisfies

\displaystyle\left[-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{r}^{2}-\frac{2}{r}\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt_{r}+\frac{l(l+1)}{r^{2}}+V^{\prime\prime}[\phi_{b}(r)]\right]\Delta_{l}(r,r^{\prime})=-\delta(r-r^{\prime})/r^{2}.

(6.3)

Given the bounce, Equation
6.3
can be solved numerically for each
l
.

There are two caveats. First, for practical reasons one can not solve Equation
6.3
for infinitely many
l
. Also, the propagator diverges in the
\vec{x}\rightarrow\vec{y}
limit.

Both of these problems have a common solution
[
81
,
75
]
. The idea is to solve Equation
6.3
for
l\in(1,L)
where
L
is typically between 50 and 100. The remaining part of the sum can then be done analytically by using the WKB approximation.

There are two cases. First, assume that
\cos\alpha<1
. Solving Equation
6.3
(see Appendix
D
) for large
l
one finds to leading order

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{l=L+1}^{\infty}(2l+1)P_{l}(\cos\alpha)\Delta_{l}(r,r^{\prime})=\delta_{L}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{1}{|\vec{x}-\vec{y}|},

(6.4)

\displaystyle\delta_{L}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{l=0}^{L}(2l+1)\Delta_{l}(r,r^{\prime})P_{l}(\cos\alpha).

(6.5)

The
\delta_{l}
term can be evaluated numerically for given
L
.

For the second case we assume
\cos\alpha=1
, and find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{l=L+1}^{\infty}(2l+1)P_{l}(1)\Delta_{l}(r,r^{\prime})=\delta_{L}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{\Gamma(1-2\epsilon)}{\Gamma(1-\epsilon)}\left(\mu^{2}e\right)^{\epsilon}(||x|-|y||)^{2\epsilon-1}

(6.6)

\displaystyle\delta_{L}=\begin{cases}-\frac{1-\left(|y|/|x|\right)^{L+1}}{4\pi(|x|-|y|)}&\quad\text{if }|x|>|y|\\
-\frac{1-\left(|x|/|y|\right)^{L+1}}{4\pi(|y|-|x|)}&\quad\text{if }|x|<|y|\end{cases}

(6.7)

Note that the leading
\delta_{L}
term is finite in the
|x|\rightarrow|y|
limit. Higher-order terms are given in Appendix
D
.

Zero modes only contribute when
l=1
. To remove the zero-modes, start with Equation

\displaystyle\left[-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\textstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptstyle\partial$}}{\scalebox{0.9}{$\scriptscriptstyle\partial$}}\kern-0.75pt^{2}+V^{\prime\prime}[\phi_{b}(x)]\right]\Delta(x,y)=\delta^{3}(x-y).

(6.8)

where

6.2 The statistical prefactor

Consider now possible diagrams appearing at the two-loop level. As before we use the real-scalar model defined in Section
5
with a large damping.

Diagrams contributing to the statistical prefactor can be divided into three classes: vacuum diagrams, external-current insertions, and zero-mode insertions. Note that at NLO—equivalent to two loops for equilibrium observables—there are contributions from lower-loop diagrams with operator-insertions.

The Feynman rules are the same as in Section
5.1
, and
\kappa
denotes the negative eigenvalue, while
\overline{U}(x)
is the corresponding eigenvector normalized as
\int d^{3}x\overline{U}(x)^{2}=1
. All propagators are defined with zero-modes removed.

6.2.1  Vacuum diagrams

There are three vacuum diagrams at two loops, which are given in Figure
9
. The bubble and sunset diagrams are only finite after normalizing with the effective potential evaluated in the metastable state. As such, for actual calculations one should always add the corresponding diagram for the effective potential with a relative minus sign. For example, the double-bubble diagram in Figure
9
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\lambda\int d^{3}x\Delta(x,x)^{2}.

(6.15)

The divergence comes from the
|x|\rightarrow\infty
region. For numerical stability one should include the effective-potential term directly:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\lambda\int d^{3}x\Delta(x,x)^{2}\rightarrow\frac{1}{8}\lambda\int d^{3}x\left[\Delta(x,x)^{2}-\Delta_{S}(x,x)^{2}\right],

(6.16)

where
\Delta_{S}(x,x)
is the propagator for a constant
\phi=\phi_{S}
.

Since all diagrams exponentiate we can directly include them in the effective action. This gives an extra minus sign for each diagram. To estimate the size of each diagram we use the power-counting

\displaystyle\Delta(x,y)\sim m,\quad\phi_{b}^{2}\sim m^{2}\lambda^{-1},\quad d^{3}x\sim m^{-3},\quad m^{2}\sim\lambda\sim g^{2},\quad\frac{m}{\lambda}\gg 1.

(6.17)

The leading-order action then scales as
S_{\text{LO}}\sim\frac{m}{\lambda}
. At NLO the relevant contributions scale as
\frac{\lambda}{m}
.

The double-bubble diagram in Figure
9
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\lambda\int d^{3}x\Delta(x,x)^{2}\sim\frac{\lambda}{m},

(6.18)

and the dumbbell diagram gives

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8}\int d^{3}xd^{3}y(\lambda\phi_{b}(x)-g)(\lambda\phi_{b}(y)-g)\Delta(x,x)\Delta(x,y)\Delta(y,y)\sim\frac{\lambda}{m}.

(6.19)

Finally, the sunset diagram scales as

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3!\times 2!}\int d^{3}xd^{3}y(\lambda\phi_{b}(x)-g)(\lambda\phi_{b}(y)-g)\Delta(x,y)^{3}\sim\frac{\lambda}{m}.

(6.20)

7  Discussion

7.1 Summary

In this paper we have shown that the nucleation rate factorizes into an equilibrium and a non-equilibrium contribution—a statistical and a dynamical prefactor. This factorization holds to all orders assuming an effective Langevin description. The formula derived in this paper lays the groundwork for calculating the nucleation rate beyond leading order. This is critical for getting a hold on uncertainties, and in particular, for estimating when the formalism breaks down.

To illustrate the calculations, this paper derived special Feynman rules. In addition, explicit calculations confirm that both the dynamical and the statistical prefactors are renormalization-scale invariant to two-loops.

7.2 Future work

This paper does not show that the dynamical prefactor is gauge invariant. Even though the statistical prefactor is gauge invariant
[
57
,
64
,
60
,
59
]
, it is still important to verify that this holds for the full rate.

Another avenue is to apply the formalism of this paper to Sphaleron transitions. This is of great importance for Baryogenesis scenarios
[
43
]
. In particular, the literature lacks a robust calculation of the Sphaleron rate with a radiative barrier. Moreover, current calculations of the functional determinant are limited to the Standard Model
[
82
,
83
]
.

Lastly, this paper is based on classical field theory. This is motivated at high temperatures: integrating out high-energy modes results in a classical field theory with effective damping, couplings, and thermal noise. Explicit calculations of the damping are however sparse
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
14
]
. There is also the question whether a Langevin description is applicable at higher orders. Answering these questions is essential for robust predictions.

7.3 Conclusion

It is the hope that the results of this paper will aid the theoretical understanding of phase transitions, and thereby reduce uncertainties for gravitational-wave predictions. Yet there are several open questions, and many problems remain. Solving these problems requires applying existing methods to new models; pushing high-temperature field theory to higher orders; and doing vital cross-checks with lattice computations.
