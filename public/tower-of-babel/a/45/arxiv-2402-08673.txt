On reduced expressions for core double cosets
Ben Elias, Hankyung Ko, Nicolas Libedinsky, Leonardo Patimo

Abstract
. The notion of a reduced expression for a double coset in a Coxeter group was introduced by Williamson, and recent work of Elias and Ko has made this theory more accessible and combinatorial. One result of Elias–Ko is that any coset admits a reduced expression which factors through a reduced expression for a related coset called its core. In this paper we define a class of cosets called atomic cosets, and prove that every core coset admits a reduced expression as a composition of atomic cosets. This leads to an algorithmic construction of a reduced expression for any coset. In types A A and B B we prove that the combinatorics of compositions of atomic cosets matches the combinatorics of ordinary expressions in a smaller group. In other types the combinatorics is new, as explored in a sequel by Ko.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Double cosets and reduced expressions

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system, with simple reflections
\{s_{i}\}_{i\in S}
. For any subset
I\subset S
, one can consider the parabolic subgroup
W_{I}
generated by
\{s_{i}\}_{i\in I}
. For
I,J\subset S
, double cosets in
W_{I}\backslash W/W_{J}
are a subject of common study in algebra and combinatorics. We call them
(I,J)
-cosets
. In this paper we only treat the case when
W_{I}
and
W_{J}
are finite groups, in which case
I
and
J
are called
finitary
. In this case, any double coset
p
has a unique maximal element
\overline{p}
and minimal element
\underline{p}
with respect to the Bruhat order (see
[
Rea06
, Prop. 31]
). Let
w_{I}
denote the longest element of
W_{I}
, and let
\ell(I)
denote its length.

The concept of a reduced expression for a double coset was introduced in
[
Wil08
]
. Many equivalent and more user-friendly definitions were given in
[
EK23
]
. A
(multistep)
(I,J)
-expression
is a sequence of finitary subsets of the form

(1)

M_{\bullet}=[[I=I_{0}\subset K_{1}\supset I_{1}\subset K_{2}\supset\ldots\subset K_{m}\supset I_{m}=J]].

(Note that
I_{0}=K_{1}
and other equalities are permitted.) This expression
M_{\bullet}
is a reduced expression for
p
if and only if

(2)

\overline{p}=w_{K_{1}}w_{I_{1}}^{-1}w_{K_{2}}\cdots w_{K_{m}}

and

(3)

\ell(\overline{p})=\ell(K_{1})-\ell(I_{1})+\ell(K_{2})-\ldots+\ell(K_{m}).

If
M_{\bullet}
is an
(I,J)
-expression and
N_{\bullet}
is a
(J,K)
-expression, then the concatenation
M_{\bullet}\circ N_{\bullet}
(defined in the intuitive way) is an
(I,K)
-expression.

Many properties of reduced expressions and tools for studying them were developed in
[
EK23
]
. Of particular note are the
(double coset) braid relations
, which are certain transformations between expressions, and the double coset Matsumoto theorem, which states that any two reduced expressions are related by braid relations.

What is absent from
[
EK23
]
is a practical matter: guidance on how to construct reduced expressions for double cosets. This is what we aim to rectify in this paper, with the introduction of atomic expressions. We feel that atomic expressions will be an interesting new gadget for combinatorial study, a study which is begun here and continued in
[
Ko23
]
.

1.3.
Atomic cosets and atomic expressions

We now introduce the main new concepts in this paper.

Let
M\subset S
be finitary and
s\in M
, and let
t=w_{M}sw_{M}
. Let
I=\widehat{s}:=M\setminus s
and
J=\widehat{t}:=M\setminus t
. Let
p
be the
(I,J)
-coset containing
w_{M}
. Then
p
is a core coset. Any coset of this form will be called an
atomic coset
.

Example 1.7
.

Here are two prototypical examples of atomic cosets in type
A
.

(9)

In each example there are two blocks which cross each other. If
I=M\setminus s
and
J=M\setminus t
as above, then
s
is the crossing between these two blocks on top, and
t
the crossing between them on bottom, so that in
M
the two blocks are merged. In the first example,
s=s_{4}
and
t=s_{2}
.

It is proven in
[
EK23
, Proposition 5.14]
that an atomic coset has a unique reduced expression, namely
[[I\subset M\supset J]]
, which we shorten to
[I,M,J]
or
[I+s-t]
, using the different notations introduced in
[
EK23
]
. A
reduced atomic expression
is a reduced expression which is a composition of reduced expressions for atomic cosets.

Theorem 1.8
.

Any core coset has a reduced atomic expression.

Example 1.9
.

A reduced atomic expression for
p^{\core}
in our running example is

(10)

[I+s_{8}-s_{8}+s_{9}-s_{10}+s_{7}-s_{6}+s_{8}-s_{8}+s_{5}-s_{5}+s_{6}-s_{7}+s_{4}-s_{2}].

In this picture, we drew the minimal elements in each atomic coset, and composed them to obtain the minimal element of
p^{\core}
. This is not how typical cosets compose, but it is how core cosets compose, see Lemma

.

In this paper we study atomic expressions, we develop some of the basic properties thereof, and we develop an algorithm to find them.

Example 1.10
.

Let
I=J=\emptyset
. For any
w\in W
, the
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
-coset
\{w\}
is a core coset. The atomic
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
-cosets are
\{s\}
, where
s\in S
is a simple reflection. The unique reduced expression for
\{s\}
is
[[\emptyset\subset\{s\}\supset\emptyset]]
, shortened to
[\emptyset,s,\emptyset]
. There is a bijection between ordinary reduced expressions
s_{1}s_{2}\ldots s_{d}
for
w
, and atomic reduced expressions
[\emptyset,s_{1},\emptyset,s_{2},\emptyset,\cdots,s_{d},\emptyset]
for
\{w\}
.

Example 1.11
.

An important class of atomic expressions has already appeared in
[
EK23
]
, though we did not think of them as atomic expressions at the time. They appear within the
switchback relation
, which is one of the aforementioned braid relations. Suppose
I
is finitary and
s,t\in I
(possibly equal), but
t\neq w_{I}sw_{I}
. Then
[[\widehat{s}\subset I\supset\widehat{t}]]
is reduced, but not atomic or core. The switchback relation gives another reduced expression for the same double coset, having the form

(12)

[[\widehat{s}\supset K]]\circ A_{\bullet}\circ[[L\subset\widehat{t}]].

Here
A_{\bullet}
is an atomic expression for the core coset. For an explicit description of
A_{\bullet}
in each case, see
[
EK23
, Section 6]
.

Remark 1.12
.

A core coset can have many reduced expressions which are not atomic expressions. Again, these are of interest, but we do not focus on them in this paper.

Remark 1.13
.

Theorem
1.8
will be used to enable a polynomial forcing result for singular Soergel bimodules in future work.

1.4.
Atomic reduced expressions in type
A

From the example above, the reader may already see how to construct an atomic expression for a core coset in type
A
. For a core coset
p
, the strands within each irreducible component of the redundancy are bundled together and never leave each others’ side (they have the same color in the picture above). Consequently, one can squash them into a single strand, to obtain a permutation
w
in a smaller symmetric group.

Theorem 1.14
.

Let
(W,S)
have type
A_{n-1}
so that
W\cong\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Let
J\subset S
have size
m
. Then there is a bijection
\mathbb{S}q^{J}
between core
(I,J)
-cosets (as
I
varies) and permutations in
\mathcal{S}_{n-m}
. For a core
(I,J)
-coset
p
,
\mathbb{S}q^{J}
induces a bijection between atomic reduced expressions for
p
and ordinary reduced expressions for
\mathbb{S}q^{J}(p)
.

Example 1.15
.

Here is the result of squashing in our running example.

(13)

\mathbb{S}q\colon\quad\vbox{\lx@xy@svg{\hbox{\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\kern 24.21045pt\hbox{{\hbox{\kern-24.21045pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise-42.6393pt\hbox{$\textstyle{\includegraphics[scale]{./EPS/examplecoreatomicrex}}$}}}}}}}}}}\quad\mapsto\quad\vbox{\lx@xy@svg{\hbox{\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\kern 17.70615pt\hbox{{\hbox{\kern-17.70615pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise-30.35341pt\hbox{$\textstyle{\includegraphics[scale]{./EPS/examplesquashed}}$}}}}}}}}}}

We hope the construction of
\mathbb{S}q
is clear from the example above. For the proof and the details see Theorem
3.8
. A similar squashing result holds in type
B
, see Section
4
. We explicitly describe how every ordinary braid relation corresponds under
\mathbb{S}q^{-1}
to a composition of several double coset braid relations between atomic expressions (which pass through non-atomic expressions en route). See Theorem
3.14
and the preceding discussion for details.

This result is positive in the sense that it gives a complete understanding of atomic reduced expressions in types
A
and
B
, in terms of existing combinatorics.
This is extremely useful for practical purposes, such as finding reduced expressions of double cosets.
Once the right perspective is in place, the result is relatively straightforward, but before this paper it was not known how to efficiently find reduced expressions for double cosets, nor was there any expectation that it would be easy.

The result is negative for combinatorial thrill-seekers, in that no new combinatorics arises in types
A
and
B
. However, the case of a general Coxeter group is quite different. That is, the set of atomic expressions for a core coset is not related to reduced expressions for some other Coxeter group, but gives rise to a new combinatorial structure. This is studied in the follow-up paper
[
Ko23
]
.

1.5.
Non-reduced atomic expressions

In ordinary Coxeter theory there are many variants of the group algebra of
W
, which go under the name of
generalized Hecke algebras
. All of them have a basis parametrized by
W
. These include among others the Iwahori-Hecke algebra, the nilHecke algebra, and the
0
-Hecke algebra which is the linearization of the
*
-monoid. These agree in how they treat reduced expressions, but disagree on how to interpret non-reduced expressions. Similarly, there are multiple categories one can build whose objects are
I\subset S
finitary, and where
\Hom(J,I)
has a basis parametrized by
(I,J)
-cosets. For the discussion below we focus on
\mathcal{SC}
(Section
2.1
),
which corresponds to the
*
-monoid, and on
\mathcal{D}
, which corresponds to the nilHecke algebra, see
[
EKLP23
]
for more details.

It is interesting to consider the non-full subcategory
\mathcal{AC}\subset\mathcal{SC}
or
\mathcal{AD}\subset\mathcal{D}
generated by atomic cosets, and ask for a presentation thereof. One way of phrasing our results from the previous section is that, in types
A
and
B
, there are braid relations in
\mathcal{AC}
and
\mathcal{AD}
, which become the ordinary braid relations after squashing.

A nice feature of the nilHecke algebra, or its double coset analogue
\mathcal{D}
, is that any non-reduced expression is equal to zero. This property is inherited by
\mathcal{AD}
. Therefore, in type
A
, squashing induces an equivalence of categories between
\mathcal{AD}
and a nilHecke algebroid. See
3.19
for a precise statement. A similar theorem holds in type
B
.

In the follow-up paper
[
Ko23
]
, the second author gives a presentation for
\mathcal{AD}
for any Coxeter type. The same problem for
\mathcal{AC}
seems more difficult and is open. See
[
Ko23
, Section 6]
for some atomic non-braid relations in
\mathcal{AC}
and more discussion.

2.
Atomic cosets and expressions

2.1.
Coxeter monoids

We recall the regular and singular Coxeter monoids. See
[
EK23
, Section 1.3, Section 2]
for details.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system. The
Coxeter monoid

(W,S,*)
(also known as the
0-Hecke monoid
or the
star monoid
) is the monoid generated by
S
with relations

(14a)

s*s=s\text{ for each }s\in S,

(14b)

\underbrace{s*t*\cdots}_{m}=\underbrace{t*s*\cdots}_{m},\text{ for each }s,t\in S\text{ with }m=m_{st}<\infty.

The relation (
14a
) is called the
*
-quadratic relation
, and (
14b
) is the
braid relation
.

If
s*t*\cdots*u
is a reduced expression in
(W,*)
, then
st\cdots u
is a reduced expression in the Coxeter group
W
, and we identify the element
w=s*t*\cdots*u\in(W,*)
with the element
w=st\cdots u\in W
. More generally, if
\ell(xy)=\ell(x)+\ell(y)
, where
\ell
denotes the Coxeter length, i.e., the length of a reduced expression, then we both write
x*y=x.y
in
(W,*)
and
xy=x.y
in
W
, and we call this a
reduced composition
.

The
singular Coxeter monoid
is the category
\mathcal{SC}=\mathcal{SC}(W,S)
defined as follows. Its objects are the finitary subsets
I\subset S
. Morphisms from
J
to
I
are identified with the set of
(I,J)
-cosets, i.e.

\Hom_{\mathcal{SC}}(J,I)=W_{I}\backslash W/W_{J}.

Composition
*
of cosets is defined so that
\overline{p*q}=\overline{p}*\overline{q}
. That is, for
p\in W_{I}\backslash W/W_{J}
and
q\in W_{J}\backslash W/W_{K}
we have

p*q=W_{I}(\overline{p}*\overline{q})W_{K}.

We say that
p*q
is reduced if

(\overline{p}w_{J}^{-1}).\overline{q}=\overline{p}*\overline{q}=\overline{p}.(w_{J}^{-1}\overline{q}).

In this case we write
p.q=p*q
. Even though
w_{J}
is an involution, we write
w_{J}^{-1}
to emphasize that this product lowers the length accordingly.

Throughout this paper we write
Is
to denote
I\cup\{s\}
, when
s\in S\setminus I
and
Is
is finitary.

2.2.
Reduced expressions, descent sets, and redundancy

For a double coset
p
, let
\leftdes(p)
and
\rightdes(p)
, the left and right descent sets of
p
,
denote the (ordinary) left and right descent sets of
\overline{p}
. Because we use it often, let us recall and rephrase a result from
[
EK23
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
p
be an
(I,J)
coset, and
s\in S
. There is a reduced expression for
p
…

•

…of the form
[I,Is,\ldots,J]\phantom{J\setminus s}
if and only if
s\in\leftdes(p)\setminus I
.

•

…of the form
[I,I\setminus s,\ldots,J]\phantom{Js}
if and only if
s\in I\setminus\leftred(p)
.

•

…of the form
[I,\ldots,Js,J]\phantom{I\setminus s}
if and only if
s\in\rightdes(p)\setminus J
.

•

…of the form
[I,\ldots,J\setminus s,J]\phantom{Is}
if and only if
s\in J\setminus\rightred(p)
.

Proof.

This is a conglomeration of the results from
[
EK23
, Sections 4.8 and 4.9]
, see also
[
EKLP23
, Theorems 2.20 and 2.22]
. ∎

2.3.
Core cosets

Definition 2.2
.

Let
p
be an
(I,J)
-coset and set
K=\leftred(p)
and
L=\rightred(p)
. The
core
of
p
is the
(K,L)
-coset
p^{\core}
with minimal element
\underline{p^{\core}}=\underline{p}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
p
be an
(I,J)
-coset. We call
p
a
core coset
if
\leftred(p)=I
and
\rightred(p)=J
, or equivalently if
I=\underline{p}J\underline{p}^{-1}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
p
is any double coset, then
p^{\core}
is a core coset. If
p
is a core coset, then
p=p^{\core}
.

Proof.

The first statement is a consequence of
[
EK23
, Lemma 4.27]
. The second statement follows from the definitions. ∎

Example 2.5
.

Any
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
-coset is a core coset.

Example 2.6
.

The
(I,I)
-coset containing the identity is a core coset, for any
I
.

Example 2.7
.

Let
W=S_{3}
and
S=\{s,t\}
. Aside from the core cosets guaranteed by the previous examples, the only core cosets are the
(s,t)
-coset and the
(t,s)
-coset containing the longest element.

As in earlier works, we write
p\leftrightharpoons I_{\bullet}
whenever
I_{\bullet}
is an expression for
p
, or
I_{\bullet}\leftrightharpoons I^{\prime}_{\bullet}
if they both express the same coset.

Example 2.8
.

Let
W=S_{4}
and
S=\{s,t,u\}
with
m_{su}=2
. Consider the
(s,u)
-coset
p
with

(15)

\overline{p}=tstut,\quad\underline{p}=tsut,\qquad p\leftrightharpoons[s,st,t,tu,u].

Consider the
(t,s)
-coset
q
with

(16)

\overline{q}=stsu,\quad\underline{q}=stu,\qquad q\leftrightharpoons[t,st,s,su,s].

These are both core cosets.

In the next few lemmas we give several basic properties of core cosets.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
p
be an
(I,J)
-coset. Then
p
is a core coset if and only if

(17)

\overline{p}=w_{I}.\underline{p}=\underline{p}.w_{J}.

Proof.

That equation (
17
) holds if and only if
\leftred(p)=I
and
\rightred(p)=J
is immediate from
Howlett’s lemma
[
EK23
, Lemma 2.12 (3)]
. ∎

The following lemma is a crucial technical tool, and fails when
p
and
q
are not core cosets.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
p
be a core
(I,J)
-coset and
q
a core
(J,K)
-coset. Then
p*q
is reduced if and only if
\underline{p}*\underline{q}
is reduced. If so, then

(18)

\underline{p*q}=\underline{p}.\underline{q}

holds, and
p*q
is core.

Proof.

Recall that by definition
p*q
is reduced if
(\overline{p}w_{J}^{-1})*\overline{q}
is a reduced composition. By Lemma
2.9
,
\overline{p}w_{J}^{-1}=\underline{p}
and
\overline{q}=\underline{q}.w_{K}
. Thus
p*q
is reduced if and only if
\underline{p}*\underline{q}*w_{K}
is a reduced composition. Certainly this requires the subword
\underline{p}*\underline{q}
to be a reduced composition.

Now suppose
\underline{p}*\underline{q}
is reduced, but
\underline{p}*\underline{q}*w_{K}
is not. Then
\rightdes(\underline{p}*\underline{q})\cap K
is nonempty.
Let
s\in\rightdes(\underline{p}*\underline{q})\cap K
. By the exchange property (see e.g.,
[
BB05
, Theorem 1.4.3]
), either the product
\underline{q}*s
or
\underline{p}*t
is not reduced, where
t=\underline{q}s\underline{q}^{-1}\in\underline{q}K\underline{q}^{-1}=J
. This contradicts the minimality of
\underline{p}
in
p
or
\underline{q}
in
q
. Thus
\underline{p}*\underline{q}
being reduced implies that
\underline{p}*\underline{q}*w_{K}
and hence
p*q
is reduced.

Suppose
p*q
is reduced. Since

2.4.
Atomic cosets and reduced expressions for core cosets

Now we address the decomposition of reduced expressions for core cosets.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let

(I,K)
-coset, and suppose that

Proof.

By

Suppose that

Example 2.15
.

We continue with the notation given in Example

Let

2.5.
Algorithm

To conclude, here is an algorithm which produces a reduced atomic expression for any core
(I,J)
-coset
p
. That this algorithm works is effectively the proof of Theorem
2.16
.

Choose an element
s\in\leftdes(\overline{p})\setminus I
, and add it. Now subtract
t=w_{Is}sw_{Is}
. Let
I^{\prime}=Is\setminus t
. Let
q
be such that
p=[I+s-t].q
. Explicitly, we let
q
be the
(I^{\prime},J)
-coset containing
w_{I^{\prime}}w_{Is}\overline{p}
, justified since the definition of a reduced expression forces

(23)

\overline{p}=(w_{Is}w_{I^{\prime}}^{-1}).\overline{q}.

Now repeat with the core
(I^{\prime},J)
-coset
q
replacing
p
.

This process terminates when
\leftdes(\overline{q})=I^{\prime}
, in which case
q
is the identity coset.
The process is guaranteed to terminate by induction on the length of
\overline{p}
.

2.6.
Composition of atomic cosets

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
be atomic cosets such that the composition
p_{1}*\ldots*p_{k}
is well-defined and reduced. Then

p_{1}*\ldots*p_{k}
is a core coset.

Proof.

The case
k=2
is a special case of
2.10
. The general case easily follows by induction.
∎

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
p
be an atomic
(I,J)
-coset, and
q
be an atomic
(J,K)
-coset.

(1)

If
p*q
is not a reduced composition, then
p*q
is a core coset if and only if
I=J=K
and
p=q
, in which case we have
p*p=p
.

(2)

We have
p*(p^{-1})*p=p
.

Proof.

Let
p\leftrightharpoons[I+s-s^{\prime}]
and
q\leftrightharpoons[J+t-t^{\prime}]
, so that
Js^{\prime}=Is
and
Kt^{\prime}=Jt
, and suppose
p*q\leftrightharpoons[I+s-s^{\prime}+t-t^{\prime}]
is not reduced. We know that
[I+s-s^{\prime}]
is reduced. Let
r
be the coset expressed by
[I+s-s^{\prime}+t]
. If
[I+s-s^{\prime}+t]
were reduced, then
[I+s-s^{\prime}+t-t^{\prime}]
would also be reduced, since subtracting a simple reflection always preserves reduced expressions. Thus reducedness must fail at the step
+t
, see
[
EK23
, Definition 2.29]
. This can happen in one of two ways: either
\rightred(p)\subsetneq\rightred(r)
, or
\underline{p}>\underline{r}
. The former is impossible, since
\rightred(p)=J
while the size of
\rightred(r)
is at most the size of
I
, which is the size of
J
. Thus
\underline{p}\neq\underline{r}
.

As
\underline{p}
is a minimal coset representative,
\rightdes(\underline{p})
is disjoint from
J
. Since
\underline{p}
is not the identity element,
\rightdes(\underline{p})
is nonempty. Since
\underline{p}\in W_{Js^{\prime}}
we must have
\rightdes(\underline{p})=\{s^{\prime}\}
. If
s^{\prime}\neq t
then
\rightdes(\underline{p})\cap Jt
would be empty, implying that
\underline{p}=\underline{r}
is minimal in its
(I,Jt)
coset, a contradiction. Thus
s^{\prime}=t
.

Then

[I+s-s^{\prime}+t-t^{\prime}]=[I+s-t+t-t^{\prime}]\leftrightharpoons[I+s-t^{\prime}]

by the
*
-quadratic relation. This expresses a core coset if and only if
t^{\prime}=s^{\prime}
, which is if and only if
p=q
.

The last statement is straightforward. If
p\leftrightharpoons[I,M,J]
then
\overline{p}=\overline{p^{-1}}=w_{M}
and the result follows from
w_{M}*w_{M}=w_{M}
.
∎

2.7.
Atomic subcategories

In §
2.1
we recalled from
[
EK23
]
the definition of the category
\mathcal{SC}=\mathcal{SC}(W,S)
, also called the singular Coxeter monoid. The objects are the finitary subsets of
S
, and the morphisms from
J
to
I
are
(I,J)
-cosets
p
, with composition operation
*
.

Definition 2.21
.

Let
\mathcal{AC}=\mathcal{AC}(W,S)
be the (non-full) subcategory of
\mathcal{SC}(W,S)
generated by the atomic cosets.

Every core coset
p
appears in the subcategory
\mathcal{AC}
by Theorem
1.8
. Moreover, every reduced composition of atomic cosets is core by Lemma
2.19
. However, there are non-reduced compositions of atomic cosets which are not core, so that
\mathcal{AC}
contains more than just the core cosets. Indeed, it is unclear which cosets have expressions as products of atomic cosets, and this makes
\mathcal{AC}
fairly mysterious.

Example 2.22
.

Let
W=S_{3}
and
I=\{s_{1}\}\subset S=\{s_{1},s_{2}\}
. There are two
(I,I)
-cosets, namely the identity coset

(which is core) and the complement

. The coset
p
has
\leftred(p)=\emptyset
, so it is not a core coset, while its non-reduced expression

[I,S,\{s_{2}\},S,I]=[I,S,\{s_{2}\}]\circ[\{s_{2}\},S,I]

shows that

belongs to
\mathcal{AC}
.

Example 2.23
.

Let
W=S_{4}
and
I=\{s_{1},s_{3}\}\subset S=\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}
. There are three
(I,I)
-cosets
p,q,r
with
\underline{p}=e,\underline{q}=s_{2},\underline{r}=s_{2}s_{3}s_{1}s_{2}
. The cosets

and

are core and have atomic reduced expressions

p\leftrightharpoons[I],\quad r\leftrightharpoons[I,S,I].

The coset

has a reduced expression

q\leftrightharpoons[[I\supset\emptyset\subset\{s_{2}\}\supset\emptyset\subset I]]

and is not core.
Since

is the unique atomic
(I,J)
-coset in

(for any

), and since

, the coset

is not a composition of atomic cosets.

To isolate core cosets, we can shift our attention to a setting which does away with non-reduced compositions. In the traditional setting, the Coxeter monoid has generators
s\in S
with quadratic relation
s*s=s
, while the nilCoxeter algebra has generators
\partial_{s}
with quadratic relation
\partial_{s}\partial_{s}=0
. The nilCoxeter algebra has the advantage that compositions of the generators associated to non-reduced compositions are zero. Returning to the world of double cosets, in
[
EKLP23
]
we introduced a variant on
\mathcal{SC}
called the nilCoxeter algebroid and denoted
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}(W,S)
. The objects are finitary subsets of
S
, and the morphism space from
J
to
I
is the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module spanned by morphisms
\partial_{p}
for
(I,J)
-cosets

. We have

(24)

\partial_{p}\circ\partial_{q}=\begin{cases}\partial_{p*q}&\text{ if $p*q=p.q$ is reduced,}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Remark 2.24
.

In
[
EKLP23
]
we provide several equivalent constructions of
\mathcal{D}
. It can be viewed as the associated graded of the linearization of
\mathcal{SC}
with respect to a length filtration. It has a presentation by generators and relations, with the same generators and braid relations as
\mathcal{SC}
but with a vanishing quadratic relation. Finally, it also appears as a non-full subcategory of vector spaces, encoding the action of push and pull operators on the cohomology rings of partial flag varieties.

Remark 2.25
.

In
[
EKLP23
]
we defined
\mathcal{D}
over a field, rather than over
\mathbb{Z}
. The equation (
24
) gives a well-defined presentation over
\mathbb{Z}
. After base change to any field, this recovers the construction in
[
EKLP23
]
, where it is proven that
\{\partial_{p}\}
forms a basis. Therefore,
\{\partial_{p}\}
forms a basis over
\mathbb{Z}
as well.

Definition 2.26
.

Let
\mathcal{AD}=\mathcal{AD}(W,S)
be the (non-full) subcategory of
\mathcal{D}
generated by
\partial_{p}
for atomic cosets
p
.

Unlike
\mathcal{AC}
, we have a precise handle on the size of
\mathcal{AD}
.

Theorem 2.27
.

The subcategory
\mathcal{AD}
has a basis
\{\partial_{p}\}_{p\;\mathrm{is}\;\mathrm{core}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{AD}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{D}
be the span of
\partial_{p}
as

ranges over all core cosets. Note that such
\partial_{p}
then form a basis of
\mathcal{AD}^{\prime}
since
\partial_{p}
, for all double cosets

, form a basis for
\mathcal{D}
(see
[
EKLP23
, Corollary 3.20]
). We have
\mathcal{AD}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{AD}
by Theorem

, since

Remark 2.28
.

While
\mathcal{D}
is the associated graded of the linearization of
\mathcal{SC}
, it is not true that
\mathcal{AD}
is the associated graded of the linearization of

. As noted earlier,
\mathcal{AC}
may contain non-core cosets, making it bigger than

.

Remark 2.29
.

In the categories
\mathcal{SC}
and
\mathcal{D}
, there are morphisms from

to

for any finitary subsets

. In

and

and

3.
Atomic expressions in type A

In this section, we explain how to handle atomic expressions in type
A
. Through an operation called ”squashing,” we establish a bijection between core cosets and permutations in smaller permutation groups. This is well-illustrated in the picture in
1.9
, which the reader is encouraged to keep in mind. Moreover, in §
3.4
, we describe how ordinary braid relations are transformed into compositions of singular braid relations between atomic expressions.

3.1.
Special notation in type
A

Let
(W,S)=(\mathcal{S}_{n},\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\})
be the Coxeter group of type
A_{n-1}
.
We view
W
as the permutation group
\mathcal{S}(X)
on the ordered set
X=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
The length of an element
w\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
is then interpreted as the number of inversions,

(25)

\ell(w)=\#\{(i,j)\ |\ 1\leq i<j\leq n,w(i)>w(j)\}.

We use the common shorthand of referring to subsets of
S
by their indices rather than their simple reflections. Thus
J=\{2,3,6\}
or for short
J=236
represents the subset
\{s_{2},s_{3},s_{6}\}
of
S
. An intentional side effect of this shorthand is that
J
can also be viewed as a subset of
X
, via the mapping
s_{j}\mapsto j
. For example, if
J\subset S
and
w\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we can consider both
w(J)
, a subset of
X
, and
wJw^{-1}
, a set of reflections in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. If
J
appears within parentheses as in
w(J)
, then it is being viewed as a subset of
X
.

3.2.
Redundancy in type A

The redundancy of a double coset in type A has an alternative description. This is best explained in terms of the string diagram, but let us put it in words first.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
p
be an
(I,J)
-coset, and let
j\in J
. Then

(1)

\leftred(p)=\underline{p}(\rightred(p))
;

(2)

j\in\rightred(p)
if and only if
\underline{p}(j+1)=\underline{p}(j)+1
and
\underline{p}(j)\in I
;

(3)

Assume further that
p
is a core coset. Then
j\in\rightred(p)
if and only if
\underline{p}(j+1)=\underline{p}(j)+1
.

Example 3.2
.

Returning to Example
1.3
, we have
3\in\rightred(p)
and
2\in\leftred(p)
. We also have
\underline{p}(3)=2
and
\underline{p}(4)=3
. Now considering the coset
p^{-1}
, note that
\underline{p}^{-1}(7)=10
and
\underline{p}^{-1}(8)=11
and
7\in I
, but
7\notin\leftred(p)=\rightred(p^{-1})
. This is not a violation of the last statement, since
p^{-1}
is not a core coset.

Proof.

Let
j\in J
. Then by definition, we have
s_{j}\in\rightred(p)
if and only if
\underline{p}s_{j}=s_{i}\underline{p}
for some

. Notice that in this case we have
i\in\leftred(p)
.
We have

(26)

\underline{p}(j+1)=\underline{p}s_{j}(j)=s_{i}\underline{p}(j)=s_{i}(\underline{p}(j)).

If
i\not\in\{\underline{p}(j),\underline{p}(j)-1\}
then (
26
) becomes
\underline{p}(j+1)=\underline{p}(j)
, a contradiction since
\underline{p}
is a permutation.
If
i=\underline{p}(j)-1
then we have
\underline{p}(j+1)<\underline{p}(j)
, and thus
\underline{p}s_{j}<\underline{p}
by (

). The latter contradicts that
\underline{p}
is minimal in
\underline{p}W_{J}
. This proves

, which implies
\underline{p}(j+1)=s_{i}(\underline{p}(j))=\underline{p}(j)+1
. This justifies both statement (
1
) and the ‘only if’ part of statement (
2
).

If
\underline{p}(j)=i
and
\underline{p}(j+1)=i+1
then
\underline{p}s_{j}=s_{i}\underline{p}
. From this, the ‘if’ part of statement (
2
) follows.

If
p
is core, then
I=\leftred(p)
and

, so
\underline{p}(j)\in I
directly follows from (
1
). Thus statement (
2
) can be rewritten as statement (
3
).
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
p
be an
(I,J)
-coset. Then the connected components in
\leftred(p)
and
\rightred(p)
are the maximal subsets

\{s_{i},s_{i+1}\ldots,s_{i+k}\}\subset I,\qquad\{s_{j},s_{j+1},\ldots,s_{j+k}\}\subset J

where
\underline{p}
maps
\{j,j+1,\ldots,j+k,j+k+1\}
to

in order-preserving fashion.

Proof.

This follows easily from the characterization in Proposition

(

). For example, if

3.3.
Squashing

As is well-known, parabolic subgroups of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
have the form
\mathcal{S}_{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{S}_{n_{k}}
with

\sum_{c=1}^{k}n_{c}=n.

Thus there is a bijection between subsets of
S
and contiguous set partitions of
X=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

More pedantically, given
J\subset S
with parabolic subgroup as above, let
\sim_{J}
be the equivalence relation on
X
generated by the relation
j\sim_{J}(j+1)
whenever
s_{j}\in J
. The number of equivalence classes (denoted
k
above) is
n-\#J
. The equivalence classes will be denoted
C_{c}
or
C_{c}^{(J)}
for
1\leq c\leq k
. Thus
C_{1}=\{1,\ldots,n_{1}\}
. Let
X/\sim_{J}
denote the set of equivalence classes under
\sim_{J}
. Each
C_{c}
inherits an order from
X
, and the set
X/\sim_{J}
itself inherits an order from
X
.

Example 3.4
.

Let
n=7
and
J=\{s_{2},s_{3},s_{6}\}
. Then
X/\sim_{J}=\{C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}\}
where

C_{1}=\{1\}\;\;<\;\;C_{2}=\{2<3<4\}\;\;<\;\;C_{3}=\{5\}\;\;<\;\;C_{4}=\{6<7\}.

Fixing a number
m
and only considering subsets
J\subset S
of size
m
, we identify
X/\sim_{J}
as an ordered set with
Y_{n-m}:=\{1<2<\ldots<n-m\}
.

Definition 3.5
.

Fix
I
and
J
of size
m
. Suppose
y
is a permutation of
X
which sends each component
C_{c}^{(J)}
via an order-preserving bijection onto a component
C_{c^{\prime}}^{(I)}
. We say that
y
is a
block permutation
relative to
I
and
J
. Clearly
y
induces a bijection
X/\sim_{J}\to X/\sim_{I}
, which we can view as a permutation of
Y_{n-m}
. This permutation we denote
\mathbb{S}q(y)\in\mathcal{S}(Y_{n-m})\cong\mathcal{S}_{n-m}
, and call it the
squashed permutation
of
y
.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
y
is a block permutation relative to
I
and
J
, and
z
is a block permutation relative to
J
and
K
, then
yz
is a block permutation relative to
I
and
K
. Moreover, if
m
is the size of
I
(and
J
and
K
), then
\mathbb{S}q(y)\mathbb{S}q(z)=\mathbb{S}q(yz)
within
\mathcal{S}(Y_{n-m})
.

Proof.

This is obvious. ∎

Definition 3.7
.

Let
p
be a core
(I,J)
coset. By Proposition
3.3
,
\underline{p}
is a block permutation relative to
I
and
J
. We call
\mathbb{S}q(p):=\mathbb{S}q(\underline{p})
the
squashed permutation
of
p
.

We refer to
1.15
for an illustration of Definition
3.7
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Fix
J\subset S
of size
m
. Then
\mathbb{S}q
induces a bijection

(27)

\mathbb{S}q^{J}\colon\{(I,p)\mid p\text{ is a core $(I,J)$-coset}\}\to\mathcal{S}(Y_{n-m}).

Proof.

By Proposition
3.3
, the minimal element
\underline{p}
of a core coset is determined by its induced permutation of
X/\sim_{J}
, i.e. by
\mathbb{S}q(\underline{p})
. Moreover,
I
and
p
are determined by
\underline{p}
, since
I=\underline{p}J\underline{p}^{-1}
. Thus
\mathbb{S}q^{J}
is injective.

Surjectivity of
\mathbb{S}q^{J}
follows because any permutation in
\mathcal{S}(Y_{n-m})
lifts to a block permutation
y
of
\mathcal{S}(X)
relative to
J
. Setting
I=y(J)
,
y
is the minimal element of a core
(I,J)
-coset.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

If
p=p^{\prime}.p^{\prime\prime}
and
p,p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime}
are core cosets in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, then we have
\mathbb{S}q(p)=\mathbb{S}q(p^{\prime}).\mathbb{S}q(p^{\prime\prime})
.

Proof.

First we note that the assumption implies that the subsets

\rightred(p),\leftred(p),\rightred(p^{\prime}),\leftred(p^{\prime}),\rightred(p^{\prime\prime}),\leftred(p^{\prime\prime})

all have the same size, say
m
. The equality
\mathbb{S}q(p)=\mathbb{S}q(p^{\prime}).\mathbb{S}q(p^{\prime\prime})
makes sense in
\mathcal{S}(Y_{n-m})
. Now, if
p,p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime}
are core cosets, then
p=p^{\prime}.p^{\prime\prime}
implies

\underline{p}=\underline{p^{\prime}}.\underline{p^{\prime\prime}}

by
2.10
, and thus the claim follows from Lemma
3.6
.
∎

Let us continue to fix
m
, and view
\mathcal{S}(Y_{n-m})
as a Coxeter group, with simple reflections
t_{j}
which transpose
j
and
j+1
.

Proposition 3.10
.

If
p
is an atomic coset in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, then
\mathbb{S}q(p)
is a simple reflection. If
J
is fixed, then
\mathbb{S}q^{J}
induces a bijection between atomic
(I,J)
-cosets (as
I
varies) and simple reflections in
\mathcal{S}(Y_{n-\#J})
.

Proof.

Let
p
be an atomic
(I,J)
coset. Then there are simple reflections
s
and
t
such that
Is=Jt=M
and
p\leftrightharpoons[I,M,J]
. Moreover,
s
and
t
are in the same component of
M
.

The difference between
\sim_{J}
and
\sim_{M}
is that two adjacent components are merged into one. More precisely, if
t
is the
r
th simple reflection in
S\setminus J
, then

C_{r},C_{r+1}\in X/\sim_{J}
are merged into
C_{r}\cup C_{r+1}\in X/\sim_{M}
. We know that
\overline{p}=w_{M}
. It is straightforward to deduce that
\underline{p}
is the block permutation which swaps
C_{r}
and
C_{r+1}
, so that
\mathbb{S}q(p)=t_{r}
. See Example
1.7
for some illustrations.

For each simple reflection
t\in S\setminus J
, letting
M=Jt
and
s=w_{M}tw_{M}
, there is exactly one atomic coset with reduced expression
[M\setminus s,M,J]
. The injectivity and surjectivity of
\mathbb{S}q^{J}
(as a map between atomic cosets and simple reflections) is obvious.∎

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
p
be a core
(I,J)
-coset. Then
\mathbb{S}q
induces a bijection

\{\text{atomic}\text{ reduced expressions for $p$}\}\to\{\text{ordinary reduced expressions for }\mathbb{S}q(p)\}.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.9
, reduced compositions for core cosets are sent to reduced compositions, and by Proposition
3.10
, atomic cosets are sent to simple reflections. Thus atomic reduced expressions for
p
are sent to ordinary reduced expressions for
\mathbb{S}q(p)
. Injectivity of this map on reduced expressions follows from injectivity of squashing (
3.8
). That there is an atomic expression lifting any ordinary expression follows from the surjectivity in Proposition
3.10
, and that it is a reduced atomic expression follows from Lemma
2.10
.
∎

3.4.
Atomic braid relations in type
A

Let
J\subset S
and
\ell=\#(S\setminus J)
, and label
S\setminus J=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{\ell}\}
in increasing order. For
s=s_{d_{i}}\in S\setminus J
we write
{\mathtt{a}}^{J}_{i}
for the atomic coset expressed by
[Js\setminus t,Js,J]
, where
t=w_{Js}sw_{Js}
. Then the atomic cosets ending in
J
are precisely
\{{\mathtt{a}}^{J}_{1},\ldots,{\mathtt{a}}^{J}_{\ell}\}
.

When
J
is understood we omit the superscript and write
{\mathtt{a}}_{i}
. We denote by
{\mathtt{a}}_{i_{d}}*\cdots*{\mathtt{a}}^{J_{0}}_{i_{0}}

the composition
{\mathtt{a}}^{J_{d}}_{i_{d}}*\cdots*{\mathtt{a}}^{J_{0}}_{i_{0}}
where
J_{d},\cdots,J_{0}
is the unique sequence of superscripts such that the composition is well-defined.
We also suppress the superscript
J_{0}
when any choice will do, as we do in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.12
.

The composition
{\mathtt{a}}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}_{i\pm 1}*{\mathtt{a}}_{i}
is reduced and thus core. Moreover, we have

(28)

\displaystyle{\mathtt{a}}_{i}.{\mathtt{a}}_{i+1}.{\mathtt{a}}_{i}

\displaystyle\leftrightharpoons

\displaystyle[I+s_{a}-s_{b}+s_{c}-s_{d}+s_{b}-s_{e}]

\displaystyle\underset{\text{switchback}}{\leftrightharpoons}

\displaystyle[I+s_{a}+s_{c}-s_{d}-s_{e}]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{downdown}}{\underset{\text{upup}}{\leftrightharpoons}}

\displaystyle[I+s_{c}+s_{a}-s_{e}-s_{d}]

\displaystyle\underset{\text{switchback}}{\leftrightharpoons}

\displaystyle[I+s_{c}-s_{f}+s_{a}-s_{e}+s_{f}-s_{d}]\leftrightharpoons{\mathtt{a}}_{i+1}.{\mathtt{a}}_{i}.{\mathtt{a}}_{i+1}.

Here the middle
`\leftrightharpoons
’ is the composition of two commuting braid relations, and
I,a,b,c,d,e,f
are determined by
i
and the hidden superscript
J_{0}
.

Proof.

To ease the notation we assume
i=1
and let
x=|C_{1}^{(I)}|=|C_{3}^{(J_{0})}|
,
y=|C_{2}^{(I)}|=|C_{2}^{(J_{0})}|
,
z=|C_{3}^{(I)}|=|C_{1}^{(J_{0})}|
.
The general case is obtained from this by shifting the indices.

We have

{\mathtt{a}}_{1}*{\mathtt{a}}_{2}*{\mathtt{a}}_{1}\leftrightharpoons[I+s_{x}-s_{y}+s_{x+y}-s_{y+z}+s_{y}-s_{z}],

where the switchback relation
[-s_{y}+s_{x+y}-s_{y+z}+s_{y}]\leftrightharpoons[s_{x+y}-s_{y+z}]
applies (see
[
EK23
, Subsection 6.1]
).
Similarly, we have

{\mathtt{a}}_{2}*{\mathtt{a}}_{1}*{\mathtt{a}}_{2}=[I+s_{x+y}-s_{x+z}+s_{x}-s_{z}+s_{x+z}-s_{y+z}],

where the switchback relation
[-s_{x+z}+s_{x}-s_{z}+s_{x+z}]\leftrightharpoons[s_{z}-s_{x}]
applies.
Composing with the upup and downdown relations, we complete the relation (
28
).

Since the middle two expressions are reduced (any multistep expression of the form
[[I\subset K\supset J]]
is reduced) and the relations are braid relations, all expressions in (
28
) are reduced, confirming the first claim.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

For
|i-j|>1
, the composition
{\mathtt{a}}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}_{j}
is reduced and thus core. We have

(29)

\displaystyle{\mathtt{a}}_{i}.{\mathtt{a}}_{j}

\displaystyle\leftrightharpoons

\displaystyle[I+s_{a}-s_{b}+s_{c}-s_{d}]

\displaystyle\underset{\text{switchback}}{\leftrightharpoons}

\displaystyle[I+s_{a}+s_{c}-s_{b}-s_{d}]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{downdown}}{\underset{\text{upup}}{\leftrightharpoons}}

\displaystyle[I+s_{c}+s_{a}-s_{d}-s_{b}]

\displaystyle\underset{\text{switchback}}{\leftrightharpoons}

\displaystyle[I+s_{c}-s_{d}+s_{a}-s_{b}]\leftrightharpoons{\mathtt{a}}_{j}.{\mathtt{a}}_{i},

where the middle
`\leftrightharpoons
’ is the composition of two commuting braid relations and
I,a,b,c,d
are determined by
i
and the hidden superscript
J_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
c,d,J
be such that
{\mathtt{a}}_{j}^{J_{0}}\leftrightharpoons[J+s_{c}-s_{d}]
and let
a,b,J^{\prime}
be such that
{\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{J_{0}}\leftrightharpoons[J^{\prime}+s_{a}-s_{b}]
. It is easy to verify that
{\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{J}=[I+s_{a}-s_{b}]
and
{\mathtt{a}}_{j}^{J^{\prime}}=[I+s_{c}-s_{d}]
for the same parabolic subset
I
. If
|i-j|>1
, then
s_{b}
and
s_{c}
(resp.,
s_{a}
and
s_{d}
) belong to different connected components of
J\sqcup s_{c}
(resp.,
J^{\prime}\sqcup s_{a}
) and thus the claimed switchback relations hold, see
[
EK23
, §5.6]
.
The last equality is straightforward.

Since the middle two expressions are reduced and the involved relations are braid relations, all expressions in (
28
) are reduced, confirming the first claim.
∎

We call
atomic braid relations
in type
A
the two relations

{\mathtt{a}}_{i}.{\mathtt{a}}_{i+1}.{\mathtt{a}}_{i}\leftrightharpoons{\mathtt{a}}_{i+1}.{\mathtt{a}}_{i}.{\mathtt{a}}_{i}\qquad{\mathtt{a}}_{i}.{\mathtt{a}}_{j}\leftrightharpoons{\mathtt{a}}_{j}.{\mathtt{a}}_{i}\text{ if }|i-j|>1

between atomic reduced expression
that we have just proved in
3.13
and
3.12
. We obtain an atomic version of the Matsumoto theorem.

Theorem 3.14

(Matsumoto’s theorem for atomic reduced expressions in type
A
)
.

Let
p
be a core
(I,J)
-coset. Then any two atomic reduced expressions of
p
are related by atomic braid relations.

Proof.

3.11
tells us that atomic reduced expressions for
p
correspond to ordinary reduced expressions for
\mathbb{S}q(p)
. By Matsumoto’s theorem on ordinary expressions, all reduced expression for
\mathbb{S}q(p)
are related by braid moves, so also atomic reduced expressions for
p
are related by atomic braid relations.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

Theorem
3.14
does not say that every relation between atomic reduced expressions of a core
(I,J)
-coset
p
is a composition of the relations of the form (
29
) and (
28
). Such a composition factors only through the expressions involving the subsets of
S
whose size is between
\#J
and
\#J+2
. For example, when
S\setminus J
has size at least
3
, and
\mathbb{S}q(p)
is the longest element, one can find a reduced expression
I_{\bullet}
for
p
which factors through
[[I\subset S]]
, and a sequence of singular braid moves between atomic reduced expressions which factors through
I_{\bullet}
.

3.5.
Non-reduced atomic expressions

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
{\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{J}\leftrightharpoons[I,Js,J]
be an atomic coset.

(1)

The composition
{\mathtt{a}}^{I}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}^{J}_{i}
is not reduced and
{\mathtt{a}}^{I}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}^{J}_{i}\cong[J,Js,J]

(2)

The composition
{\mathtt{a}}^{I}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}^{J}_{i}
is core if and only if

, in which case we have
{\mathtt{a}}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}_{i}={\mathtt{a}}_{i}
.

(3)

{\mathtt{a}}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{J}={\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{J}
.

We have
{\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{J}\leftrightharpoons[I+s^{\prime}-s]
where
s^{\prime}
is the
i
-th element in
S\setminus I
.
By definition, we can write
{\mathtt{a}}^{I}_{i}\leftrightharpoons[K,It,I]
, where

is the

-th element in

. Thus, we have
s^{\prime}=t
.

Since
{\mathtt{a}}^{I}_{i}
is atomic, we can also write
{\mathtt{a}}^{I}_{i}\leftrightharpoons[K+t^{\prime}-t]
with

{\mathtt{a}}^{I}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}^{J}_{i}\leftrightharpoons[K+t^{\prime}-t]*[I+s^{\prime}-s]=[K+t^{\prime}-t+s^{\prime}-s]

contains the reducible expression

.

Moreover, we also have have

and

The second statement follows from
2.20
(

) and
the third follows from

(

{\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{I}\leftrightharpoons[J,Js,I]\leftrightharpoons({\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{J})^{-1}

.

If

3.6.
Atomic categories in type
A

Recall from §
2.7
the definitions of the categories
\mathcal{AC}
and
\mathcal{AD}
. We now discuss these categories in type
A
.

The braid relations of §
3.4
and the non-braid relations from Lemma

can be interpreted as relations in
\mathcal{AC}
. They induce relations in
\mathcal{AD}
. More precisely, the braid relations can be copied to
\mathcal{AD}
verbatim (after a change in notation), while Lemma

implies that
\partial_{{\mathtt{a}}_{i}}\circ\partial_{{\mathtt{a}}_{i}}=0
.

We will discuss
\mathcal{AD}
first, as it is easier to understand, and will resume the discussion of
\mathcal{AC}
afterwards.
Our first goal is to present
\mathcal{AD}
by generators and relations, akin to typical descriptions of the KLR algebra
[
KL09
]
. Indeed,
\mathcal{AD}
is simply an algebroid version of the usual nilCoxeter algebra.

Let
y
be a block permutation relative to
I
and
J
. We write
J\leftrightarrow(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
when
W_{J}\cong\mathcal{S}_{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{S}_{n_{k}}
. After squashing

to a permutation on

through
n_{k}
as in the following example.

(30)

\mathbb{S}q\colon\quad\vbox{\lx@xy@svg{\hbox{\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\kern 24.21045pt\hbox{{\hbox{\kern-24.21045pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise-42.6393pt\hbox{$\textstyle{\includegraphics[scale]{./EPS/examplecoreatomicrex}}$}}}}}}}}}}\quad\mapsto\quad{\labellist\small\hair 2pt\pinlabel{1}[]at0-5\pinlabel{1}[]at8-5\pinlabel{3}[]at16-5\pinlabel{2}[]at24-5\pinlabel{1}[]at32-5\pinlabel{2}[]at40-5\pinlabel{1}[]at48-5\pinlabel{1}[]at090\pinlabel{3}[]at890\pinlabel{1}[]at1690\pinlabel{2}[]at2490\pinlabel{1}[]at3290\pinlabel{1}[]at4090\pinlabel{2}[]at4890\endlabellist\centering\vbox{\lx@xy@svg{\hbox{\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\kern 17.70615pt\hbox{{\hbox{\kern-17.70615pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise-30.35341pt\hbox{$\textstyle{\includegraphics[scale]{./EPS/examplesquashed}}$}}}}}}}}}}\@add@centering}

In this way we can view the image of
\mathbb{S}q^{J}
as permutations of labeled strands, with the bottom labels determined by

.

Definition 3.18
.

Let
n\geq 0
. The
labeled nilCoxeter algebroid

\nilcox(n)
is the
\mathbb{Z}
-linear category presented as follows. The objects are compositions of
n
, i.e. sequences
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
with
\sum n_{c}=n
, identified with parabolic subsets
J
as above. We draw the identity map

of a composition as a sequence of labeled strands.
The generating morphisms are crossings
\partial_{i}^{J}=\partial_{i}1_{J}

to
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{i+1},n_{i},\ldots,n_{k})

(31)

\partial_{i}\partial_{i+1}\partial_{i}^{J}=\partial_{i+1}\partial_{i}\partial_{i+1}^{J}\quad\text{for $1\leq i\leq k-2$}

(32)

\partial_{i}\partial_{j}^{J}=\partial_{j}\partial_{i}^{J}\quad\text{ if $|i-j|>1$}

(33)

\partial_{i}\partial_{i}^{J}=0\text{ for $1\leq i\leq k-1$}.

We have omitted superscripts when they are uniquely determined.

The RHS of (

) now represents the morphism in
\nilcox(11)

\partial_{5}\partial_{6}\partial_{4}\partial_{5}\partial_{3}\partial_{4}\partial_{2}\colon(1,1,3,2,1,2,1)\to(1,3,1,2,1,1,2).

Proposition 3.19
.

We have isomorphisms of
\Bbbk
-linear algebroids

\displaystyle\psi\colon\nilcox(n)

\displaystyle\partial_{i}^{J}

\displaystyle\mapsto\partial_{{\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{J}}.

Proof.

The relations (

) and (

) hold in
\mathcal{AD}
by
3.12
and
3.13
respectively. The relation (

) also follows by
3.16
, since non-reduced compositions are zero in

. Thus the functor

is well-defined.

Fix

, corresponding to a composition with

(I,J)
-coset. We know that

is equal to a composition of

over an atomic reduced expression for

. By Theorem

,
\psi

, interpreted as a product of the generators

, to
\partial_{p}
in
\mathcal{AD}
. Thus

,

has a

, in bijection with permutations of

.

Let
\mathcal{N}(k)
denote the ordinary nilCoxeter algebra on

\mathcal{N}(k)
and
\nilcox(n)\cdot 1_{J}
. Indeed, these spaces have exactly the same presentation by generators and relations. This proves that the size of
\nilcox(n)\cdot 1_{J}
is exactly as desired.
∎

Finding a presentation for
\mathcal{AC}
is more difficult. The relation

(34)

{\mathtt{a}}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}_{i}*{\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{I}={\mathtt{a}}_{i}^{I}

holds in general, replaced by

when

is an

-coset. We are unsure whether these relations, combined with the braid relations, are sufficient to describe

.

In the special case where

of

. The monoid

agrees with the

4.

Let
