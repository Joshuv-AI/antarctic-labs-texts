Balanced Allocations in Batches: The Tower of Two Choices Full version of a paper appearing in SPAA 2023.
Dimitrios Los, Thomas Sauerwald

Abstract
In the balanced allocation framework, the goal is to allocate m m balls into n n bins, so as to minimize the gap (difference of maximum to average load). The One-Choice process allocates each ball to a bin sampled independently and uniformly at random. The Two-Choice process allocates balls sequentially, and each ball is placed in the least loaded of two sampled bins. Finally, the ( 1 + β ) (1+\beta) -process mixes these processes, meaning each ball is allocated using Two-Choice with probability β ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \beta\in(0,1) , and using One-Choice otherwise. Despite Two-Choice being optimal in the sequential setting, it has been observed in practice that it does not perform well in a parallel environment, where load information may be outdated. Following [ 6 ] , we study such a parallel setting where balls are allocated in batches of size b b , and balls within the same batch are allocated with the same strategy and based on the same load information. For small batch sizes b ∈ [ n , n ​ log ⁡ n ] b\in[n,n\log n] , it was shown in [ 23 ] that Two-Choice achieves an asymptotically optimal gap among all allocation processes with two (or any constant number of) samples. In this work, we focus on larger batch sizes b ∈ [ n ​ log ⁡ n , n 3 ] b\in[n\log n,n^{3}] . It was proved in [ 21 ] that Two-Choice leads to a gap of Θ ⁡ ( b / n ) \Theta(b/n) . As our main result, we prove that the gap reduces to 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( b / n ) ⋅ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}) , if one runs the ( 1 + β ) (1+\beta) -process with an appropriately chosen β \beta (in fact this result holds for a larger class of processes). This not only proves the phenomenon that Two-Choice is not the best (leading to the formation of “towers” over previously light bins), but also that mixing two processes ( One-Choice and Two-Choice ) leads to a process which achieves a gap that is asymptotically smaller than both. We also derive a matching lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( ( b / n ) ⋅ log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}) for any allocation process, which demonstrates that the above ( 1 + β ) (1+\beta) -process is asymptotically optimal. Our analysis also works in the presence of randomly weighted balls, and also implies exponential tails for the number of bins above a certain load value.

Our techniques.

Our techniques build on and refine those in
[
21
]
, making use of the hyperbolic cosine potential function
[
34
]
and variants. More specifically, a slightly weaker version of our tight upper bound is based on
[
21
, Theorem 3.1]
and a refinement of
[
21
, Lemma 4.1]
. For our tight gap bound, our approach uses an interplay between two hyperbolic cosine potential functions to prove concentration and then an exponential potential with a larger smoothing parameter to deduce the refined gap.
A similar method was used in
[
21
, Section 5]
, but one crucial novelty here is that we consider allocation processes whose probability allocation vector have a small
\ell_{\infty}
distance from the uniform distribution. We believe that relating and comparing different allocation processes based on their
\ell_{\infty}
distance (or other metrics) could be a promising avenue for future work. This can be also seen as a natural relaxation of the
majorization technique
, which has been the dominant tool to relate different allocation processes
[
34
,
22
]
.

Notation, Processes and Settings

In this section, we introduce notation, processes and settings used throughout this work.

2.1
Basic Notation

We consider the allocation of
m
balls into
n
bins, which are labeled
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. For the moment, the
m
balls are unweighted (or equivalently, all balls have weight
1
). For any step
t\geqslant 0
,
x^{t}
is the
n
-dimensional
load vector
, where
x_{i}^{t}
is the number of balls allocated to bin
i
in the first
t
allocations. In particular,
x_{i}^{0}=0
for every
i\in[n]
. Finally, the
gap
is defined as

\Gap(t)=\max_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{t}-\frac{t}{n}.

It will also be convenient to sort the load vector
x
. To this end, let
\widetilde{x}^{t}:=x^{t}-\frac{t}{n}
. Then, relabel the bins such that
y^{t}
is a permutation of
\widetilde{x}^{t}
and
y_{1}^{t}\geqslant y_{2}^{t}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant y_{n}^{t}
. Note that
\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{t}=0
and
\Gap(t)=y_{1}^{t}
. We call a bin
i\in[n]

overloaded
, if
y_{i}^{t}\geqslant 0
and
underloaded
otherwise.

A
probability vector

p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is any vector satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1
and
p_{i}\in[0,1]
for
i\in[n]
. Following
[
34
]
, many allocation processes can be described by a time-invariant
probability allocation vector

p^{t}
, which is the probability vector with
p_{i}^{t}
being probability of allocating a ball to the
i
-th heaviest bin.

By
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
we denote the filtration of the process until step
t
, which in particular reveals the load vector
x^{t}
.

2.2
Processes

We start with a formal description of the
One-Choice
process.

One-Choice
Process:

Iteration:
For each
t\geqslant 0
, sample one bin
i
, independently and uniformly at random. Then update:

x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+1.

We continue with a formal description of the
Two-Choice
process.

Two-Choice
Process:

Iteration:
For each
t\geqslant 0
, sample two bins
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
, independently and uniformly at random. Let
i\in\{i_{1},i_{2}\}
be such that
x_{i}^{t}=\min\{x_{i_{1}}^{t},x_{i_{2}}^{t}\}
, breaking ties randomly. Then update:

x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+1.

It is immediate that the probability allocation vector of
Two-Choice
is

p_{i}=\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}},\qquad\mbox{ for all $i\in[n]$.}

Following
[
34
]
, we recall the definition of the
(1+\beta)
-process which interpolates between
One-Choice
and
Two-Choice
:

(
1+\beta
) Process:

Parameter:
A
mixing factor

\beta\in(0,1]
.

Iteration:
For each
t\geqslant 0
, sample two bins
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
, independently and uniformly at random. Let
i\in\{i_{1},i_{2}\}
be such that
x_{i}^{t}=\min\big\{x_{i_{1}}^{t},x_{i_{2}}^{t}\big\}
, breaking ties randomly. Then update:

\begin{cases}x_{i}^{t+1}=x_{i}^{t}+1&\mbox{with probability $\beta$},\\
x_{i_{1}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{1}}^{t}+1&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In other words at each step, the
(1+\beta)
-process allocates the ball following the
Two-Choice
rule with probability
\beta
, and otherwise allocates the ball following the
One-Choice
rule. Therefore, its probability allocation vector is given by

p_{i}=(1-\beta)\cdot\frac{1}{n}+\beta\cdot\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}},\qquad\mbox{ for all $i\in[n]$.}

Recall that in
[
34
]
(and
[
21
]
), it was shown that
\Gap(m)=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\log n}{\beta}\big)
for any
m\geqslant n
and
\beta\in(0,1]
; so in particular, this gap (bound) does not grow with
m
.

The next process is another relaxation of
Two-Choice
.

\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
Process:

Parameter:
A
quantile

\delta\in\{1/n,2/n,\ldots,1\}
.

Iteration:
For each
t\geqslant 0
, sample two bins
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
, independently and uniformly at random. Then update:

\begin{cases}x_{i_{2}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{2}}^{t}+1&\mbox{if $i_{1}$ is among the $\delta n$ heaviest bins},\\
x_{i_{1}}^{t+1}=x_{i_{1}}^{t}+1&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that the
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
processes can be implemented as a two-phase procedure: First probe the bin
i_{1}
and place the ball there if
i_{1}
is not among the
\delta n
heaviest bins. Otherwise, take a second sample
i_{2}
and place the ball there. Since we only need to know whether a bin’s rank is above or below a value, the response by a bin can be encoded as a single bit (at the cost of knowing the rank of each bin). The probability allocation vector of
\textsc{Quantile}(\delta)
is given by:

p_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{\delta}{n}&\mbox{ if $1\leqslant i\leqslant\delta n$},\\
\frac{1+\delta}{n}&\mbox{ if $\delta n+1\leqslant i\leqslant n$}.\end{cases}

Conditions on Probability Vectors

In

21
]

b
-Batched

\varepsilon/n
bias to place away from overloaded and towards underloaded bins; and the second condition says that no bin has too high probability of being allocated.

•

Condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
: There exist constant quantile

3

: There exists a

\max_{i\in[n]}\left|p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\right|\leqslant\frac{C-1}{n}.

.

For any

Proof.

Recall that for the
(1+\beta)
-process, the probability allocation vector satisfies

p_{i}=(1-\beta)\cdot\frac{1}{n}+\beta\cdot\frac{2i-1}{n^{2}},\quad\text{for all }i\in[n].

We will first show that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
holds with
\delta=1/4
and
\varepsilon=\beta/2
. For any
1\leqslant k\leqslant\delta n
, since
p
is non-decreasing the prefix sums satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\leqslant p_{k}\cdot k\leqslant p_{\delta n}\cdot k\leqslant\left((1-\beta)+\beta\cdot(2\delta)\right)\cdot\frac{k}{n}=\left(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{k}{n},

Similarly, for any
\delta n+1\leqslant k\leqslant n
, the suffix sums satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k}^{n}p_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\frac{n-k+1}{n}\cdot(1-\beta)+\frac{\beta}{n^{2}}\cdot(n^{2}-(k-1)^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{n-k+1}{n}\cdot(1-\beta)+\frac{\beta}{n^{2}}\cdot(n-k+1)\cdot(n+k-1)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{\beta}{n}\cdot(k-1)\right)\cdot\frac{n-k+1}{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geqslant}}(1+\beta\cdot\delta)\cdot\frac{n-k+1}{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\geqslant}}\left(1+\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\right)\cdot\frac{n-k+1}{n},

using in
(a)
that
\sum_{i=1}^{u}(2i-1)=u^{2}
, in
(b)
that
k\geqslant\delta n+1
and in
(c)
that
\delta=1/4
and
\varepsilon=\beta/2
.

Condition
\mathcal{C}_{3}
for
C=1+\beta
(and hence
\mathcal{C}_{2}
as well) is verified as follows. As
p_{i}
is increasing in
i\in[n]
,

\max_{i\in[n]}\left|p_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\right|=\max\left\{\frac{1}{n}-p_{1},p_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\right\}=\frac{\beta}{n}-\frac{\beta}{n^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\beta}{n}.\qed

Note that in contrast to
Two-Choice
which satisfies
\mathcal{C}_{3}
for
C=2-\frac{1}{n}
, by choosing
\beta
small enough we can make the probability allocation vector arbitrarily close to uniform.

We also note that for any process
\mathcal{P}
satisfying condition
\mathcal{C}_{3}
for some
C>1
, we can define a process
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
satisfying condition
\mathcal{C}_{3}
for
C^{\prime}\in(1,C)
by mixing the probability allocation vector of
\mathcal{P}
with that of
One-Choice
with probability
\eta=\frac{C^{\prime}-1}{C-1}
.

For instance, the
\textsc{Quantile}(1/2)
process satisfies condition
\mathcal{C}_{3}
for any
C=1+1/2
(since
\min_{i\in[n]}p_{i}=\frac{1}{2n}
and
\max_{i\in[n]}p_{i}=\frac{3}{2n}
). Therefore, mixing
\textsc{Quantile}(1/2)
with
One-Choice
with probability
\eta\in[0,1]
, gives the following probability allocation vector satisfying condition
\mathcal{C}_{3}
for
C=1+\eta/2
,

p_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{n}\cdot(1-\eta)+\frac{1}{2n}\cdot\eta=\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\eta}{2n}&\text{if }i\leqslant\frac{1}{2}n,\\
\frac{1}{n}\cdot(1-\eta)+\frac{3}{2n}\cdot\eta=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{\eta}{2n}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Observation 2.2
.

The process obtained by mixing
\textsc{Quantile}(1/2)
with
One-Choice
satisfies condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
with

and

\mathcal{C}_{2}

\mathcal{C}_{3}

2.4
Weighted and Batched Settings

As in
[
21
]
, we now extend the definitions of

and
Section

2.2
to

and later to the
batched setting
. To this end, let

be the weight of the
t
-th ball to be allocated for
t\geqslant 1
. By
W^{t}
we denote the total weights of all balls allocated after the first
t\geqslant 0
allocations, so
W^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}w^{s}
. The normalized loads are
\widetilde{x}_{i}^{t}:=x_{i}^{t}-\frac{W^{t}}{n}
, and with
y_{i}^{t}
being again the decreasingly sorted, normalized load vector, we have
\Gap(t)=y_{1}^{t}
.

The weight of each ball will be drawn independently from a fixed distribution
\mathcal{W}
over
[0,\infty)
. Following
[
34
]
, we assume that the distribution
\mathcal{W}
satisfies:

•

\mathbf{E}[\,\mathcal{W}\,]=1
.

•

\mathbf{E}[\,e^{\zeta\mathcal{W}}\,]<\infty
for some
\zeta>0
.

Specific examples of distributions satisfying above conditions (after scaling) are the geometric, exponential, binomial and Poisson distributions.

In the analysis we will be using the following property (see also
[
34
]
) and refer to these distributions as
\textsc{Finite-MGF}(\zeta)
(or
\textsc{Finite-MGF}(S)
):

Lemma 2.3

(
[
21
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

There exists
S:=S(\zeta)\geqslant\max\{1,1/\zeta\}
, such that for any
\gamma\in(0,\min\{\zeta/2,1\})
and any
\kappa\in[-1,1]
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,e^{\gamma\cdot\kappa\cdot\mathcal{W}}\,\right]\leqslant 1+\gamma\cdot\kappa+S\gamma^{2}\cdot\kappa^{2}.

We will now describe the allocation of weighted balls into bins using a batch size of
b\geqslant n
. For the sake of concreteness, let us first describe the
b
-Batched
setting if the allocation is done using
Two-Choice
. For a given batch size consisting of
b
consecutive balls, each ball of the batch performs the following. First, it samples two bins
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
independently and uniformly at random, and compares the load the two bins had at the beginning of the batch (let us denote the bin which has less load by
i_{\min}
). Secondly, a weight is sampled from the distribution
\mathcal{W}
. Then a weighted ball is added to bin
i_{\min}
. Recall that since the load information is only updated at the beginning of the batch, all allocations of the
b
balls within the same batch can be performed in parallel.

In the following, we will use a more general framework, where the process of sampling (one or more) bins and then deciding where to allocate the ball to is described by a probability allocation vector
p
over the
n
bins (
Section

2.1
). Also for the analysis, it will be convenient to focus on the normalized and sorted load vector

rather than the actual load vector

3
Warm-up:
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{b/n}\cdot\log n)

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider any allocation process with probability allocation vector
p^{t}
satisfying conditions
\mathcal{C}_{1}
for constant
\delta\in(0,1)
and (not necessarily constant)
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
as well as condition
\mathcal{C}_{3}
for some

. Further, consider the weighted
b
-Batched
setting with weights from a
\textsc{Finite-MGF}(S)
distribution with
S\geqslant 1
and a batch size
b\geqslant\frac{2CS}{(C-1)^{2}}\cdot n
.
Then, there exists a constant
k:=k(\delta)>0
, such that for any step
m\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{i\in[n]}|y_{i}^{m}|\leqslant k\cdot\frac{(C-1)^{2}}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\cdot\log n\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Recall that by
2.1
, the
(1+\beta)
-process satisfies condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
with
\varepsilon=\frac{\beta}{2}
and
\delta=\frac{1}{4}
, and conditions
\mathcal{C}_{2}
and
\mathcal{C}_{3}

.

In particular, by choosing

and which is within just a

Let

The analysis is based on the

\displaystyle\Gamma^{t}:=\Gamma^{t}(\gamma):=\Phi^{t}+\Psi^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\gamma y_{i}^{t}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-\gamma y_{i}^{t}}.

Step 1:

In the following lemma, proven in

Step 2:

\Gamma_{2}:=\Gamma_{2}(\gamma_{2})

Third statement.
Furthermore, by

(4.3)

We define the constant

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\cdot b\cdot\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8n}\cdot\gamma_{2}=b\cdot\frac{\delta^{2}}{16\cdot 8\cdot 30\cdot 40S\cdot n}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n}{b\log n}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n\log n}{b}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}}{16\cdot 8\cdot 30\cdot 40\cdot S}.

When

4.3

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Gamma_{2}^{t+b}\,\Big|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\Gamma_{2}^{t}\geqslant\widetilde{c}n\,\right]

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{2}^{t}-\widetilde{c}_{1}\cdot\Gamma_{2}^{t}+\Big(b\cdot c\gamma_{2}\varepsilon-\widetilde{c}_{1}\cdot\Gamma_{2}^{t}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Gamma_{2}^{t}-\widetilde{c}_{1}\cdot\Gamma_{2}^{t}+\Big(b\cdot c\gamma_{2}\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}\cdot b\cdot\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{8n}\cdot\gamma_{2}\cdot\frac{16c}{\delta}\cdot n\Big)

Fourth statement.
Similarly, when

Lemma 4.8

(4.4)

Finally, taking the union bound of

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[t,t+2b\log^{3}n]}\left\{\Gamma_{1}^{s}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n^{26}\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\Pr\Bigg[\bigcap_{r\in[0,b]}\bigcap_{s\in[0,2\log^{3}n]}\left\{\Gamma_{1}^{t+s\cdot b+r}\leqslant n^{14}\cdot\Gamma_{1}^{t+s\cdot b}\right\}

Lemma 4.9

(Recovery)
.

Consider any process satisfying the conditions in
4.2
. Let
\widetilde{c}:=2\cdot\frac{8c}{\delta}
be the constant defined in
4.7
. For any step
t\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{s\in[0,\log^{3}n]}\left\{\Gamma_{2}^{t+s\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\right\}\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-8}.

Proof.

By
4.7

(ii)
, using Markov’s inequality at step
t
being a multiple of
b
, we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n^{9}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-8}.

(4.6)

We will be assuming
\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n^{9}
. By
4.7

(iii)
, for any step

, then

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Gamma_{2}^{r+1}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\Gamma_{2}^{r}>\widetilde{c}n\,\right]\leqslant\Gamma_{2}^{r}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{\log n}\Big).

In order to prove that
\Gamma_{2}^{t+s\cdot b}
is small for some

, we define the “killed” potential function for any

,

\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t+r\cdot b}:=\Gamma_{2}^{t+r\cdot b}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\bigcap_{s\in[0,r]}\{\Gamma_{2}^{t+s\cdot b}>\widetilde{c}n\}}.

Note that
\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t+r\cdot b}\leqslant\Gamma_{2}^{t+r\cdot b}
and that

implies that
\big\{\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t+r\cdot b+1}=0\big\}
. Hence, the
\widehat{\Gamma}
potential satisfies unconditionally the drop inequality of

(iii)
, that is,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t+(r+1)\cdot b}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t+r\cdot b},\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t+r\cdot b}\,\right]\leqslant\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t+r\cdot b}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{\log n}\Big).

Inductively applying this for

batches and using that
\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t}\leqslant\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n^{9}
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t+(\log^{3}n)\cdot b}\,\,\right|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n^{9}\,\right]\leqslant\Gamma_{2}^{t}\cdot\Big(1-\frac{1}{\log n}\Big)^{\log^{3}n}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n^{9}\cdot e^{-\log^{2}n}<n^{-7}.

So by Markov’s inequality,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t+(\log^{3}n)\cdot b}<n\,\,\right|\,\,\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n^{9}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-{8}}

By combining with

4.6
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t+(\log^{3}n)\cdot b}<n\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-n^{-8}\right)\cdot\left(1-n^{-8}\right)\geqslant 1-2n^{-8}.

Due to the definition of
\Gamma_{2}
, at any step
t\geqslant 0
, deterministically
\Gamma_{2}^{t}\geqslant 2n
. So,
we conclude that w.p. at least
1-2n^{-8}
, we have that
\widehat{\Gamma}_{2}^{t+(\log^{3}n)\cdot b}=0
or equivalently the event

\neg\bigcap_{s\in[0,\log^{3}n]}\left\{\Gamma_{2}^{t+s\cdot b}>\widetilde{c}n\right\},

holds, which implies the conclusion.
∎

4.1.2
Completing the Proof of Lemma
4.2

We are now ready to prove
4.2
, using a method of bounded differences with a bad event
A.6
(
[
20
, Theorem 3.3]
).

Proof of
4.2
.

Our starting point is to apply

4.9
, which proves that there is at least one step
t+\rho\cdot b\in[t-b\log^{3}n,t]
with
\rho\in[-\log^{3}n,0]

\Gamma_{2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{\rho\in[-\log^{3}n,0]}\left\{\Gamma_{2}^{t+\rho\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-2n^{-8}.

(4.7)

Note that if
t<b\log^{3}n
, then deterministically
\Gamma_{2}^{0}=2n\leqslant\widetilde{c}n
(which corresponds to
\rho=-t/b
).

We are now going to apply the concentration inequality
A.6
to each of the batches starting at

at the last step of each batch. More specifically, we will show that for any
\widetilde{r}\in[\rho,\log^{3}n]

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\Gamma_{2}^{r+b}>\widetilde{c}n\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\Gamma_{2}^{r}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\,\right]\leqslant 3n^{-4}.

We will show this by applying
A.6
for all steps of the batch
[r,r+b]
. We define the good event

\mathcal{G}_{r}:=\mathcal{G}_{r}^{r+b}:=\bigcap_{s\in[r,r+b]}\left(\left\{\Gamma_{1}^{s}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n^{26}\right\}\cap\mathcal{H}^{s}\right),

and the bad event
\mathcal{B}_{r}:=(\mathcal{G}_{r})^{c}
. Using a union bound over
4.5
and
4.8
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{s\in[t-b\log^{3}n,t+b\log^{3}n]}\left(\left\{\Gamma_{1}^{s}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n^{26}\right\}\cap\mathcal{H}^{s}\right)\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-10}.

(4.8)

Consider any

. Further, we define the slightly weaker good event,
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{u}:=\bigcap_{s\in[r,u]}\left(\left\{\Gamma_{1}^{s}\leqslant 2\widetilde{c}n^{26}\right\}\cap\mathcal{H}^{s}\right)

\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{u}:=\Gamma_{2}^{u}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{u}}.

We will show that the sequence
\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r},\ldots,\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}
is strongly difference-bounded by
(n^{5/4},n^{1/4}\cdot\sqrt{(n/b)\cdot\log n},2n^{-10})
(
A.5
).

Let
\omega\in[n]^{b}
be an allocation vector encoding the allocations made in
[r,r+b]

\omega^{\prime}
be an allocating vector resulting from
\omega
by changing one arbitrary allocation. It follows that,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}(\omega)-\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}(\omega^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{\widetilde{\omega}}\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}(\widetilde{\omega})-\min_{\widetilde{\omega}}\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}(\widetilde{\omega})

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{\widetilde{\omega}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{r+b}}\Gamma_{2}^{r+b}(\widetilde{\omega})-0

\displaystyle\leqslant n^{5/4},

where in the last inequality we used
4.6

(i)
that for any
\widetilde{\omega}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{r+b}
, we have
\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}(\widetilde{\omega})\leqslant\Gamma_{2}^{r+b}(\widetilde{\omega})\leqslant n^{5/4}
.

We will now derive a refined bound by additionally assuming that
\omega\in\mathcal{G}_{r}
. Then, for any
u\in[r,r+b]
,

\Gamma_{1}^{u}(\omega^{\prime})\leqslant 2\cdot\Gamma_{1}^{u}(\omega)\leqslant 2\widetilde{c}n^{26},

where the first inequality is by
4.6

(iii)
.
Hence
\omega^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{r+b}
, so
\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{r+b}(\omega^{\prime})}=1

\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}(\omega^{\prime})=\Gamma_{2}^{r+b}(\omega^{\prime})

, we have
\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}(\omega)=\Gamma_{2}^{r+b}(\omega)
and by
4.6

(ii)

\left|\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}(\omega)-\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}(\omega^{\prime})\right|=\left|\Gamma_{2}^{r+b}(\omega)-\Gamma_{2}^{r+b}(\omega^{\prime})\right|\leqslant n^{1/4}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n}{b}\cdot\log n}.

Within a single batch all allocations are independent, so we apply
A.6
, choosing
\gamma_{k}:=\frac{1}{b}
and
N:=b
, which states that for any
T>0
and
\mu:=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}>\mu+T\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\Gamma_{2}^{r}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\,\right]

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}>\mu+T\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\Gamma_{2}^{r}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\,\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{T^{2}}{2\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{b}(n^{1/4}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n}{b}\cdot\log n}+n^{5/4}\cdot\frac{1}{b})^{2}}\right)+2n^{-10}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{b}b.

4.7

(iv)

T:=n/\log^{2}n

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{r+b}>\widetilde{c}n\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\Gamma_{2}^{r}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}/\log^{4}n}{2\cdot b\cdot(2\cdot n^{1/4}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{n}{b}\cdot\log n})^{2}}\right)+2n^{-10}\cdot b^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{n^{1/2}}{8\cdot\log^{5}n}\right)+2n^{-10}\cdot n^{6}\leqslant 3n^{-4}.

\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\widetilde{r}}:=\mathcal{G}_{\rho}^{t+\widetilde{r}\cdot b}\cap\{\Gamma_{2}^{t+\rho\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\}
for
\widetilde{r}\in[\rho,\log^{3}n]
. For any
\widetilde{r}\geqslant\rho
, since
\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\widetilde{r}+1}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\widetilde{r}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{t+(\widetilde{r}+1)\cdot b}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\widetilde{r}+1}}>\widetilde{c}n\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{r},\widehat{\Gamma}_{r}^{t+\widetilde{r}\cdot b}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\widetilde{r}}}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\,\right]\leqslant 3n^{-4}.

By union bound of
Eq.

4.7
and
Eq.

4.8
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{\rho\in[-\log^{3}n]}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\log^{3}n}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{G}_{-\log^{3}n}^{\log^{3}n}\cap\bigcup_{\rho\in[-\log^{3}n,0]}\left\{\Gamma_{2}^{t+\rho\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-2n^{-8}-2n^{-10}\geqslant 1-3n^{-8}.

4.9

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{A}_{\rho}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{A}_{\rho}\,\left|\,\,\Gamma_{2}^{t+\rho\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\right.\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gamma_{2}^{t+\rho\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\,\right]+1\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\neg\left\{\Gamma_{2}^{t+\rho\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\right\}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-6n^{-4}\cdot\log^{3}n.

Note that for any

. Hence we conclude by the union bound of

4.10
and

, that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{A}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcup_{\rho\in[-\log^{3}n,0]}\mathcal{K}_{\rho}^{\log^{3}n}\cap\bigcap_{\rho\in[-\log^{3}n,0]}\mathcal{A}_{\rho}\,\right]\geqslant 1-3n^{-8}-6n^{-4}\cdot\log^{6}n\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.\qed

Lemma 4.10
.

Consider any process satisfying the conditions in

\widetilde{c}:=2\cdot\frac{8c}{\delta}
where

is the constant from

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary step
t\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
and consider a labeling of the bins so that they are sorted by load. Assuming that
\{\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\}
holds, the number of bins with load
y_{i}^{t}\geqslant z
is at most

\widetilde{c}n\cdot e^{-\gamma_{2}\cdot z}=\widetilde{c}n\cdot e^{-\log(\widetilde{c}/\delta)}=\delta n.

For any bin
i\in[n]
with
y_{i}^{t}\geqslant z
, we get as in
Eq.

3.5
(using that
\lambda\leqslant 1
and that
p
satisfies
\mathcal{C}_{3}
for
C\in(1,1.9)
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda_{i}^{t+b}\,\,\right|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\Big(p_{i}^{t}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdot\lambda+2\cdot p_{i}^{t}\cdot S\lambda^{2}\right)^{b}.

Since there are at most
\delta n
such bins (i.e.,
i\leqslant\delta n
),
p
satisfies condition
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and the normalized vector
y^{t}
is sorted, by
A.2
the upper bound on
\mathbf{E}\left[\,\Lambda^{t+b}\mid\mathfrak{F}^{t},\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\,\right]
is maximized when
p_{i}^{t}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{n}
, so

\displaystyle\sum_{i:y_{i}^{t}\geqslant z}\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda_{i}^{t+b}\,\,\right|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\,\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\sum_{i:y_{i}^{t}\geqslant z}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{n}+2CS\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}\right)^{b}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\sum_{i:y_{i}^{t}\geqslant z}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{2n}\right)^{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leqslant}}\sum_{i:y_{i}^{t}\geqslant z}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot b},

using in
(a)
that
p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{C}{n}
, in
(b)
that
\lambda=\frac{\varepsilon}{4CS}
and in
(c)
that
1+v\leqslant e^{v}
for any
v
. For the rest of the bins with
i>\delta n
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda_{i}^{t+b}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\left(p_{i}^{t}-\frac{1}{n}\right)\cdot\lambda+2\cdot p_{i}^{t}\cdot S\lambda^{2}\right)^{b}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+\frac{C-1}{n}\cdot\lambda+2CS\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}\right)^{b}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leqslant}}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot\left(1+2\cdot\frac{C-1}{n}\cdot\lambda\right)^{b}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leqslant}}\left(1+2\cdot\frac{C-1}{n}\cdot\lambda\right)^{b}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\leqslant}}e^{2\cdot\frac{C-1}{n}\cdot\lambda b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{\leqslant}}n,

using in
(a)
that
p_{i}^{t}\leqslant\frac{C}{n}
, in
(b)
that
2CS\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{n}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot\lambda\leqslant\frac{C-1}{2n}\cdot\lambda
since
\lambda=\frac{\varepsilon}{4CS}
and
\varepsilon=C-1
, in
(c)
that
\Lambda_{i}^{t}\leqslant 1
, in
(d)
that
1+v\leqslant e^{v}
for any
v
and in
(e)
that
2\cdot\frac{C-1}{n}\cdot\lambda b\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2CS}\cdot\frac{b}{n}\leqslant\log n
(since
C>1
and
S\geqslant 1
).

Aggregating the contributions over all bins,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\left.\Lambda^{t+b}\,\right|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t},\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i:y_{i}^{t}\geqslant z}\Lambda_{i}^{t}\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot b}+\sum_{i:y_{i}^{t}<z}n\leqslant\Lambda^{t}\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot b}+n^{2}.\qed

Now we are ready to complete the proof of
4.1
.

Proof of
4.1
.

First consider the case when
m\geqslant b\cdot\log^{3}n
. Let
t_{0}=m-b\cdot\log^{3}n
. Let
\mathcal{E}^{t}:=\big\{\Gamma_{2}^{t}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\big\}
. Then using
4.2
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\bigcap_{j\in[0,\log^{3}n]}\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.

(4.12)

We define the killed potential
\widetilde{\Lambda}
, with
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}}:=\Lambda^{t_{0}}
and for
j>0
,

\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}:=\Lambda^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{s\in[0,j]}\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}+s\cdot b}}.

Since
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t}\leqslant\Lambda^{t}
, we have that by
4.10
for
t=t_{0}+j\cdot b
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}+(j+1)\cdot b}\,\,\left|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b},\Gamma_{2}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\leqslant\widetilde{c}n\right.\,\right]\leqslant\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot b}+n^{2}.

When
\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}
does not hold, then deterministically
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}+(j+1)\cdot b}=\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}=0
. Hence, we have the following unconditional drop inequality

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}+(j+1)\cdot b}\,\,\left|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\right.\,\right]\leqslant\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot b}+n^{2}.

(4.13)

Assuming
\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}}
holds, we have

\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{t_{0}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\cdot\left(\log\widetilde{c}+\log n\right)\leqslant\frac{2}{\gamma_{2}}\cdot\log n,

for sufficiently large
n
. Recalling that
\gamma_{2}=\Theta(\lambda\cdot\log n)
, there exists a constant
\kappa_{1}>0
such that

\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}}\leqslant n\cdot e^{\lambda\cdot y_{1}^{t_{0}}}\leqslant e^{\kappa_{1}\log^{2}n}.

Applying
A.1
to
Eq.

4.13
with
a:=e^{-\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot b}
and
b:=n^{2}
for
\log^{3}n
steps,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}^{m}\,\,\left|\,\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}}\leqslant e^{\kappa_{1}\log^{2}n}\right.\,\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{\kappa_{1}\log^{2}n}\cdot a^{\log^{3}n}+\frac{b}{1-a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leqslant}}1+1.5\cdot b\leqslant 2n^{2}.

(4.14)

using in
(a)
that
\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{2n}\cdot b=\Omega(\log n)
, since
\lambda=\frac{\varepsilon}{4CS}
and
\varepsilon=\sqrt{(n/b)\cdot\log n}
.

By Markov’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}^{m}\leqslant 2n^{5}\,\left|\,\mathfrak{F}^{t_{0}},\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}}\leqslant e^{\kappa_{1}\log^{2}n}\right.\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-3}.

Hence, by
Eq.

4.12
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{\Lambda}^{m}\leqslant 2n^{5}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left.\widetilde{\Lambda}^{m}\leqslant 2n^{5}\,\right|\,\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}}\,\right]\geqslant\left(1-n^{-3}\right)\cdot\left(1-n^{-3}\right)\geqslant 1-2n^{-3}.

Combining with
Eq.

4.12
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Lambda^{m}\leqslant 2n^{5}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\left\{\widetilde{\Lambda}^{m}\leqslant 2n^{5}\right\}\cap\bigcap_{j\in[0,\log^{3}n]}\mathcal{E}^{t_{0}+j\cdot b}\,\right]\geqslant 1-2n^{-3}-n^{-3}\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Finally,
\{\Lambda^{m}\leqslant 2n^{5}\}
implies that

\max_{i\in[n]}y_{i}^{m}\leqslant z+\frac{\log 2}{\lambda}+\frac{5\log n}{\lambda}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}\right),

since
\lambda=\frac{\varepsilon}{4CS}=\Theta(\sqrt{(n\log n)/b})
, so the claim follows.

For the case when
m<b\cdot\log^{3}n
, it deterministically holds that
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{t_{0}}\leqslant n
, which is a stronger starting point in
Eq.

4.14
to prove that
\mathbf{E}[\,\Lambda^{m}\,]\leqslant 2n^{5}
, which in turn implies the gap bound.
∎

5
Lower Bounds on the Gap

In this section, we prove two lower bounds of
\Omega(\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n})
on the gap. Both lower bounds hold even in the unit weights case.

Observation 5.1
.

Consider the
b
-Batched
setting with any
b\geqslant n\log n
, and assume all balls have unit weights. Then, for any process which uses the same probability allocation vector within each batch with random tie breaking,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\Gap(b)\geqslant\frac{1}{10}\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}\,\right]\geqslant 1-n^{-2}.

Proof.

Any such process behaves exactly like
One-Choice
in the first batch and so the lower bound follows from that of
One-Choice
for
b
balls into
n
bins (cf.
[
35
]
and
[
24
, Lemma A.2]
).
∎

The next lower bound is more involved. This bound also applies to processes which are allowed to adjust the probability allocation vector from one batch to another arbitrarily; e.g., the probability for a heavily underloaded bin might be set close to (or even equal to)
1
, and similarly, the probability for a heavily overloaded bin might be set close to (or equal to)
0
. Additionally, the lower bound below applies to any two consecutive batches, and not only to the end of the first batch as in
5.1
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Consider the
b
-Batched
setting with any
b=\Omega(n\log n)
in the unit weights case. Furthermore, consider an allocation process which may adaptively change the probability allocation vector for each batch. Then there is a constant
\kappa>0
such that for any allocation process (which may adaptively change the probability for each batch) it holds that for every
t\geqslant 0
being a multiple of
b
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max\left\{\Gap(t),\Gap(t+b)\right\}\geqslant\kappa\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}\,\right]\geqslant 1/2.

Proof.

In the proof, we shall prove a slightly stronger statement:

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max\left\{\Gap(t),\Gap(t+b)\right\}\geqslant\kappa\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}~~\Bigg|~~\mathfrak{F}^{t}\,\right]\geqslant 1/2.

That is, there is no load configuration and no probability allocation vector (depending on
\mathfrak{F}^{t}
) such that the gap is small, both before and at the end of an arbitrary batch.

For notational convenience, we will prove this statement by assuming that
t=0
, and
x^{0}
is an arbitrary load vector satisfying
\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}^{0}=0
(in other words, we shift time backwards by
t
steps) and
p=p^{0}
is the probability allocation vector used by the process.
Consider one arbitrary bin
j\in[n]
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,x_{j}^{b}-\frac{b}{n}+z_{j}\,\right]=b\cdot p_{j}-\frac{b}{n}+z_{j}=:\varphi_{j}

For a sufficiently large constant
C>0
, let us now assume
\max_{j\in[n]}z_{j}\leqslant C/2\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}
; clearly, if this is not the case, we already have a large gap already before the next batch.

Next consider a bin
j\in[n]
with

p_{j}\leqslant\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{b}\cdot\left(-10C\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}\right).

We will now apply a Chernoff bound (
A.4
) for
x_{j}^{b}\sim\mathsf{Bin}(b,p_{j})
,
with
\delta:=C\cdot\sqrt{(n/b)\cdot\log n}
,
\mu:=b\cdot p_{j}
and
\mu_{H}:=\frac{b}{n}-10C\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}\geqslant\mu
to get that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{j}^{b}\geqslant\frac{b}{n}-C\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{j}^{b}\geqslant\mu_{H}\cdot(1+\delta)\,\right]\leqslant e^{-\delta^{2}\mu_{H}/3}=e^{-\frac{C^{2}}{3}\cdot\log n}\leqslant n^{-4},

using that
C\geqslant 4
.
If
\left\{x_{j}^{b}\leqslant\frac{b}{n}-C\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}\right\}
occurs, then

x_{j}^{b}-\frac{b}{n}+z_{j}\leqslant-C\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}+z_{j}\leqslant-C/2\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}\leqslant 0,

and thus bin
j
will not contribute to the gap at step
b
.

Hence in the remainder of the proof, we would like to assume that for all bins
j\in[n]
,

p_{j}\geqslant\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{b}\cdot\left(-10C\cdot\sqrt{(b/n)\cdot\log n}\right)=:p_{\operatorname{low}}

Note
p_{\operatorname{low}}\leqslant 1/n
.
Consider now a transformation of the probability vector
(p_{i})_{i\in[n]}
into
(\widetilde{p}_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, where
\widetilde{p}
satisfies for all
j\in[n]
,
p_{\operatorname{low}}\leqslant\widetilde{p}_{j}\leqslant\max\left\{p_{\operatorname{low}},p_{j}\right\}.

In other words, in
\widetilde{p}
we only increase probabilities of bins
j\in[n]
, for which
p_{j}<p_{\operatorname{low}}
. Let us define
\mathcal{J}:=\left\{j\in[n]\colon p_{j}<p_{\operatorname{low}}\right\}
. For
b\geqslant(20C^{2})n\log n
, this implies
\widetilde{p}_{j}\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}
for all
j\in[n]
.

Further,
let
(x_{i}^{b})_{i\in[n]}
be a load vector where the locations of the next
b
balls are sampled according to
p
, and
(\widetilde{x}_{i}^{b})_{i\in[n]}
be a load vector where these locations are sampled according to
\widetilde{p}
. Clearly, there is a coupling so that for every
j\in[n]\setminus\mathcal{J}
,
x_{j}^{b}\geqslant\widetilde{x}_{j}^{b}
(since
p_{j}^{b}\geqslant\widetilde{p}_{j}^{b}
). Further, for any
j\in\mathcal{J}
, by a union bound,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{b}>0\,\right]\leqslant|\mathcal{J}|\cdot n^{-4}\leqslant n^{-3}.

Hence it follows that, for any threshold
T>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{j\in[n]}x_{j}^{b}\geqslant T\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{j\in[n]}\widetilde{x}_{j}^{b}\geqslant T\,\right]-\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}}x_{j}^{b}\geqslant T\,\right]\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{j\in[n]}\widetilde{x}_{j}^{b}\geqslant T\,\right]-n^{-3}.

Therefore, in the remainder of the proof, we will lower bound
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\max_{j\in[n]\setminus\mathcal{J}}\widetilde{x}_{j}^{b}\geqslant T\,\right]
for a suitable value of
T=\Omega(\sqrt{b/n\cdot\log n})
. We will also use the definition

\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}:=b\cdot\widetilde{p}_{j}-\frac{b}{n}+z_{j}.

Finally, we define
\xi=0.1
as a (sufficiently) small constant.

Case 1:
We have at least
n-n^{\xi}
bins for which
\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\leqslant-C\sqrt{b/n}
. Since
\sum_{i\in[n]}\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}=0
, this implies that there must be at least one bin with
j\in[n]
with

\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\geqslant\frac{n-n^{\xi}}{n^{\xi}}\cdot C\cdot\sqrt{b/n}\geqslant 1/2\cdot n^{1-\xi}\cdot\sqrt{b/n}.

Further, using that the median of a
\mathsf{Bin}(N,q)
r.v. is either
\lfloor Nq\rfloor
or
\lceil Nq\rceil
, then

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{j}^{b}\geqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,x_{j}^{b}\,\right]-\frac{1}{4}n^{1-\xi}\,\right]\geqslant\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,x_{j}^{b}\geqslant\left\lfloor\mathbf{E}\left[\,x_{j}^{b}\,\right]\right\rfloor\,\right]\geqslant 1/2,

it follows that with probability at least
1/2
we will have a large gap.

Case 2:
We have at least
n^{\xi}
bins with
\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}\geqslant-C\sqrt{b/n}
; call this set
\mathcal{B}
. We further know that, due to the definition of
\widetilde{p}
, we have for all bins
j\in[n]
that
\widetilde{p}_{j}\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}
. Hence, we set
T:=b\cdot\widetilde{p}_{j}+\kappa\cdot\sqrt{b\cdot\widetilde{p}_{j}\cdot\log n}
, and applying
A.3
yields for any bin
j\in\mathcal{B}
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{x}_{j}^{b}\geqslant T\,\right]\geqslant n^{-\xi/2}.

Since
\widetilde{p}_{j}\geqslant\frac{1}{2n}
,
|\mathcal{B}|\geqslant n^{\xi}
, the claim follows.∎

7
Conclusions

In this work, we revisited the outdated information setting of
[
6
]
, where balls are allocated to bins in batches of size
b
, using the load information available at the beginning of the batch. We established that by defining the mixing factor
\beta
carefully as a function of the batch size
b
,
(1+\beta)
achieves the asymptotically optimal gap for any
b\geqslant n\log n
. That is, by having
\beta
chosen appropriately small,
(1+\beta)
circumvents the “herd behavior” (as called in
[
28
]
), where some of the previously underloaded bins are chosen too frequently, turning them into heavily overloaded bins in the next batch. Similarly,
\beta
should also not be too small, as otherwise the process would be too close to
One-Choice
.

There are several directions for future work. First, recall that our lower bounds apply to a large class of processes which allocate all balls within the
same batch
independently. However, there are processes which allocate multiple balls in a coordinated way. For example, the process of Park
[
33
]
draws
d
samples, and then places into each of the
k
least loaded bins one ball. It would be interesting to explore the gap of this type of processes in the
b
-Batched
setting. A second avenue is to analyze
Two-Thinning
processes (and in particular processes that use a fixed load threshold relative to the average) in outdated information settings. An experimental study of threshold processes with outdated information was already conducted in 1989
[
26
, Figure 8]
, but no rigorous bounds were proven. A third possibility is to investigate whether the
(1+\beta)
and related processes are superior to
Two-Choice
in other settings, like the
\tau
-Delay
or random noise settings studied in
[
23
]
. Finally, one could study settings where the load information of bins is updated at different rates, depending on the specific bin. In such a setting, when deciding between sampled bins, both their reported load estimates and update rates should be taken into account.

Appendix A
Tools

A.1
Auxiliary Probabilistic Claims

For convenience, we add the following well-known inequality for a sequence of random variables, whose expectations are related through a recurrence inequality.

Lemma A.1
.

Consider a sequence of random variables
(X^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that there exist
a\in(0,1)
and
b>0
such that every
i\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{i-1}\,\right]\leqslant X^{i-1}\cdot a+b.

Then, for every
i\geqslant 1
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant X^{0}\cdot a^{i}+\frac{b}{1-a}.

Proof.

We will prove by induction that for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant X^{0}\cdot a^{i}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a^{j}.

For
i=0
, it trivially holds that
\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{0}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant X^{0}
. Assuming the induction hypothesis holds for some
i\geqslant 0
, then since
a>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i+1}\mid X^{0}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\left[\,\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i+1}\mid X^{i}\,\right]\mid X^{0}\,\right]\leqslant\mathbf{E}\left[\,X^{i}\mid X^{0}\,\right]\cdot a+b

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(X^{0}\cdot a^{i}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}a^{j}\Big)\cdot a+b

\displaystyle=X^{0}\cdot a^{i+1}+b\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{i}a^{j}.

The claims follows using that for
a\in(0,1)
,
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a^{j}=\frac{1}{1-a}
.
∎

For the next lemma, we define for two
n
-dimensional vectors
x,y
,
\langle x,y\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\cdot y_{i}
.

Lemma A.2

(
[
21
, Lemma A.7]
)
.

Let
(p_{k})_{k=1}^{n},(q_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
be two probability vectors and
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
be non-negative and non-increasing. Then if
p
majorizes
q
, i.e., for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant n

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}
holds, then

\langle p,c\rangle\geqslant\langle q,c\rangle.

We continue with an “anti-concentration” result, i.e., a lower bound on the probability that a binomial random variable is significantly larger than its expectation.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
m,n
be integers such that
m\geqslant n\log n
. Further, let
p
be a probability satisfying
p\in[1/(2n),1/2]
and let
X\sim\mathsf{Bin}(m,p)
. Then for any constant
\xi\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
\kappa\geqslant 0
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X\geqslant m\cdot p+\kappa\cdot\sqrt{mp\cdot\log n}\,\right]\geqslant n^{-\xi/2}.

Proof.

Since
X\sim\mathsf{Bin}(m,p)
, we know that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X=m\cdot p\,\right]=\binom{m}{m\cdot p}\cdot p^{m\cdot p}\cdot(1-p)^{m-m\cdot p}.

Let
\mu:=m\cdot p
and
f(z):=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,X=z\,\right]
for any
z\in[0,m]
. Then, for any integer
k\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{f(\mu+k)}{f(\mu+(k-1))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{m!}{(\mu+k)!(m-\mu-k)!}}{\frac{m!}{(\mu+k-1)!(m-\mu-k+1)!}}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}

\displaystyle=\frac{m-\mu-k+1}{\mu+k}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}

\displaystyle=\frac{m\cdot(1-\frac{\mu+k-1}{m})}{(1+\frac{k}{\mu})m\cdot p}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\frac{\mu+k-1}{m}}{1+\frac{k}{\mu}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p\cdot\frac{\mu+k-1}{\mu}}{1+\frac{k}{\mu}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-p}.

The first factor is decreasing in
k
. Hence,
for any
K\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle f(\mu+K)

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mu)\cdot\left(\frac{\mu\cdot(1-p)-(K-1)p}{\mu+K}\right)^{K}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1-p}\right)^{K}

\displaystyle=f(\mu)\cdot\left(\frac{(\mu+K)\cdot(1-p)-K(1+p)}{(\mu+K)\cdot(1-p)}\right)^{K}

\displaystyle=f(\mu)\cdot\left(1-\frac{K(1+p)}{(\mu+K)\cdot(1-p)}\right)^{(\frac{(\mu+K)\cdot(1-p)}{K(1+p)}-1)\cdot\frac{K}{\frac{(\mu+K)\cdot(1-p)}{K(1+p)}-1}}

We will now make use of the fact that
(1-\frac{1}{N})^{N-1}\geqslant e^{-1}
, where

N:=\frac{(\mu+K)\cdot(1-p)}{K\cdot(1+p)}
.
With this we have,

\displaystyle f(\mu+K)

\displaystyle=f(\mu)\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)^{(N-1)\cdot\frac{K}{N-1}}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mu)\cdot e^{-\frac{K}{\frac{(\mu+K)\cdot(1-p)}{K(1+p)}-1}}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mu)\cdot e^{-\frac{2K}{\frac{(\mu+K)\cdot(1-p)}{K(1+p)}}}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mu)\cdot e^{-\frac{8K^{2}}{(\mu+K)}}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mu)\cdot e^{-\frac{16K^{2}}{\mu}},

having used that
p\leqslant 1/2
and
K\leqslant 4\cdot\mu
.
Further, by a concentration bound, e.g., by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,|X-\mu|\geqslant 2\sqrt{\mathbf{Var}\left[\,X\,\right]}\,\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{4}.

This implies that at least
\frac{3}{4}
of the probability of
X
is on the interval
[\mu-2\sqrt{\mathbf{Var}\left[\,X\,\right]},\mu+2\sqrt{\mathbf{Var}\left[\,X\,\right]}]
. Since the mode, i.e., largest probability is
\lfloor(m+1)p\rfloor
or
\lceil(m+1)p\rceil-1
, it follows that

f(\mu)\geqslant\frac{3}{8\cdot\mathbf{Var}\left[\,X\,\right]}=\frac{3}{8\cdot\sqrt{m\cdot p(1-p)}}\geqslant\frac{3}{8\sqrt{\mu}}.

Then,
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\,\widetilde{x}_{j}\geqslant\mu+\xi/2\cdot\sqrt{\mu\log n}\,\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{K=\xi/100\cdot\sqrt{\mu\log n}}^{m}f(\mu+K)

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{K=\xi/100\cdot\sqrt{\mu\log n}}^{\xi/50\cdot\sqrt{\mu\log n}}f(\mu+K)

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mu)\cdot\sum_{K=\xi/100\cdot\sqrt{\mu\log n}}^{\xi/50\cdot\sqrt{\mu\log n}}e^{-\frac{16K^{2}}{\mu}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{3}{8\sqrt{\mu}}\cdot\xi/100\cdot\sqrt{\mu\log n}\cdot e^{-16\xi^{2}/2500\cdot\log n}

\displaystyle\geqslant n^{-32/2500\xi^{2}}\geqslant n^{-\xi/2}.

∎
