Spectral transform for the Ising model
Terrence George

Abstract
. We prove a correspondence between Ising models in a torus and the algebro-geometric data of a Harnack curve with a certain symmetry and a point in the real part of its Prym variety, extending the correspondence between dimer models and Harnack curves and their Jacobians due to Kenyon and Okounkov.

2.1.
Zig-zag paths and the Newton polygon

A
zig-zag path
in
G
is an oriented path in
G
that alternately turns maximally left or right at each vertex. Zig-zag paths in
G
come in pairs with opposite orientations; we denote by
\overline{\alpha}
the zig-zag path opposite to the zig-zag path
\alpha
. Let
Z_{G}
denote the set of zig-zag paths in
G
.

Let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}
denote the universal cover of
\mathbb{T}
. We say that
G
is
minimal
if the lift of any zig-zag path to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
does not have a self-intersection and the lifts of two zig-zag paths to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
share at most one edge. Hereafter, we assume that our graphs are minimal.

Choose a fundamental rectangle
R
for
\mathbb{T}
and let
\gamma_{z},\gamma_{w}
be loops in
\mathbb{T}
along the sides of
R
as shown in Figure
3
(a). Then
\{[\gamma_{z}],[\gamma_{w}]\}
is a basis for
H_{1}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{Z})
identifying
H_{1}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{Z})
with
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
A nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is called
primitive
if
v
is not a multiple of another vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, i.e., if
v=\lambda w
for
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
then
v=w
and
\lambda=1
. A convex polygon
N\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is said to be
integral
if all of its vertices are contained in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Let
\sigma
denote the involution
v\mapsto-v
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A convex integral polygon
N
is called
centrally symmetric
if
N
is invariant under
\sigma
.

Associated to a minimal
G
is a centrally-symmetric convex integral polygon
N_{G}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
as follows: each zig-zag path
\alpha\in Z_{G}
defines a primitive vector given by the homology class
[\alpha]\in H_{1}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. There is a unique (modulo translation) convex integral polygon in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose counterclockwise-oriented boundary consists of the vectors
\{[\alpha]\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\alpha\in Z_{G}\}
. The translation is fixed by centering at
(0,0)
.

2.2.
Kramers–Wannier duality

Let
(G,J)
be an Ising model in
\mathbb{T}
. Let
G^{*}
denote the dual graph of
G
in
\mathbb{T}
. For
e\in E(G)
, let
e^{*}\in E(G^{*})
denote the dual edge. Define
J^{*}:E(G^{*})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
by the condition

\operatorname{sinh}(2J_{e^{*}}^{*})=\frac{1}{\operatorname{sinh}(2J_{e})}.

Then
(G^{*},J^{*})
is called the
dual Ising model
. Duality defines a bijection

\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{G^{*}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}).

It is convenient to introduce the following coordinates. Let
x:E(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be defined as
x_{e}:=\exp(2J_{e})
. Then
x^{*}_{e^{*}}:=\exp(2J^{*}_{e^{*}})
is given by the unique positive solution to

x_{e}+x^{*}_{e^{*}}+x_{e}x^{*}_{e^{*}}=1.

(

For every centrally symmetric convex integral polygon

Background on the dimer model

In this section, we give a brief background on the dimer model and its spectral transform, mostly following
[
Ken09
,
GK13
]
.

3.1.
Dimer models in
\mathbb{T}

A
dimer model
in
\mathbb{T}
is a pair
(\Gamma,[\mathrm{wt}])
where

(1)

\Gamma=(B\sqcup W,E,F)
is a bipartite graph embedded in
\mathbb{T}
such that the faces of
\Gamma
are topological disks,

(2)

[\mathrm{wt}]
is the gauge-equivalence class of
\mathrm{wt}:E\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
which is a function assigning to each edge its
edge weight
, and two edge weights
\mathrm{wt}_{1}
and
\mathrm{wt}_{2}
are
gauge equivalent
if there is a function
f:B\sqcup W\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
such that for every edge
e=\{\mathrm{b},\mathrm{w}\}\in E
(where
\mathrm{b}\in B,\mathrm{w}\in W
),

\mathrm{wt}^{\prime}(e)=f(\mathrm{b})^{-1}\mathrm{wt}(e)f(\mathrm{w}).

Let
\mathcal{X}_{\Gamma}
denote the space of edge weights modulo gauge equivalence. It will be convenient to rephrase the above in the language of algebraic topology. In particular, doing so will help us identify coordinates on
\mathcal{X}_{\Gamma}
that are invariant under gauge equivalence.

We consider
\Gamma
as a cell complex where the vertices are the
0
-cells and the edges are the
1
-cells, where we orient each edge
e=\{\mathrm{b},\mathrm{w}\}
from
\mathrm{b}
to
\mathrm{w}
. The cellular chain complex is

0\rightarrow C_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\xrightarrow{\partial}C_{0}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow 0,

where
C_{0}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}B\oplus\mathbb{Z}W
,
C_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}E
and
\partial e=\mathrm{w}-\mathrm{b}
. Therefore,

H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})=\ker(C_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\xrightarrow{\partial}C_{0}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})),

so
1
-homology classes in
\Gamma
are the same thing as
1
-cycles in
\Gamma
.

The cellular cochain complex is

1\rightarrow C^{0}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{\times})\xrightarrow{d}C^{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{\times})\rightarrow 1,

where

(1)

C^{0}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{\times})=\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(C_{0}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{C}^{\times})\cong\{f:B\sqcup W\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}\}
,

(2)

C^{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{\times})=\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(C_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{C}^{\times})\cong\{\mathrm{wt}:E\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}\}
,

(3)

(df)(e)={f(\mathrm{b})}^{-1}{f(\mathrm{w})}
,

where
\text{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\cdot,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
denotes the space of abelian group homomorphisms from
\cdot
to
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. Therefore, a
1
-cocycle is the same thing as an edge weight and two
1
-cocycles differ by a
1
-coboundary if they are gauge equivalent, so

\mathcal{X}_{\Gamma}=H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{\times}).

With this identification, we can describe coordinates on
\mathcal{X}_{\Gamma}
. We describe this abstractly first and then explain what it means concretely.

Since
H^{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{\times})=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{C}^{\times})
is the algebraic torus with lattice of characters
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
, its coordinate ring is

\mathbb{C}[H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})]=\mathbb{C}\left[\sum_{\gamma\in H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})}a_{\gamma}X_{\gamma}:a_{\gamma}=0\text{ for all but finitely many }\gamma\right]

with multiplication given by
X_{\gamma_{1}}\cdot X_{\gamma_{2}}=X_{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}
, where
X_{\gamma}:\mathcal{X}_{\Gamma}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
is the character given by
X_{\gamma}([\mathrm{wt}])=[\mathrm{wt}](\gamma)
, i.e., by evaluating the cohomology class
[\mathrm{wt}]
on the cycle
\gamma
. Explicitly, if
\gamma
is the cycle
{\rm b}_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}{\rm w}_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}{\rm b}_{2}\xrightarrow{e_{3}}{\rm w}_{2}\xrightarrow{e_{4}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{2n-2}}{\rm b}_{n}\xrightarrow{e_{2n-1}}{\rm w}_{n}\xrightarrow{e_{2n}}{\rm b}_{1}
, then

[\mathrm{wt}](\gamma)=\frac{\mathrm{wt}({e_{1}})\cdots\mathrm{wt}{(e_{2n-1}})}{\mathrm{wt}{(e_{2})}\cdots\mathrm{wt}{(e_{2n})}}.

In down to earth terms, this means that the
X_{\gamma}
’s are a set coordinates on
\mathcal{X}_{\Gamma}
but they are not independent and have to satisfy some relations. If we choose a basis for
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
, then we obtain a basis for the set of coordinates. There is no canonical choice for a basis but the following choice is convenient for computations. We identify faces of
\Gamma
with the cycles given by their counterclockwise-oriented boundaries. Then each face
f
determines a regular function
X_{f}
on
\mathcal{X}_{\Gamma}
. Since
\sum_{f\in F}f=0
in
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
, we have
\prod_{f\in F}X_{f}=1
. Additionally if we choose two cycles
a,b
in
\Gamma
generating
H_{1}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{Z})
, then
\{\text{all faces except one},a,b\}
is a basis for
H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
and so

(3.1)

\{X_{f}\}_{\text{all faces $f$ except one}}\sqcup\{X_{a},X_{b}\}

is a basis for the coordinate ring.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
denote the positive real valued points of
\mathcal{X}_{\Gamma}
, i.e., the set of points
[\mathrm{wt}]
where
X_{\gamma}([\mathrm{wt}])\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for all
\gamma\in H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
.

3.2.

A

in
\Gamma
is a cycle that turns maximally right at black vertices and maximally left at white vertices. We denote the set of zig-zag paths of
\Gamma
by

. The unique (modulo translation) convex integral polygon

whose counterclockwise-oriented boundary consists of

of

. For a side

3.5.
The spectral transform

Let
(\Gamma,[\mathrm{wt}])
be a dimer model in
\mathbb{T}
. We assign to each edge
e=\{\mathrm{b},\mathrm{w}\}
of
\Gamma
a monomial
\phi(e):=z^{i}w^{j}
where
i
(resp.,
j
) records the number of signed intersections of
e
with
\gamma_{w}
(resp.,
\gamma_{z}
) when we orient
e
from
\mathrm{b}
to
\mathrm{w}
.

A
Kasteleyn sign
on
\Gamma
is a
1
-cohomology class
[\kappa]\in H^{1}(\Gamma,\{\pm 1\})
such that for any face
f\in F
,

[\kappa](f)=(-1)^{\frac{\#\partial f}{2}+1},

where
e\in f
means
e
is incident to
f
. Here
\{\pm 1\}
is the multiplicative group of square roots of unity. There are four choices of
[\kappa]
corresponding to the four elements of
H^{1}(\mathbb{T},\{\pm 1\})
.
If we use the basis (
3.1
), a choice of
[\kappa]
is equivalent to a choice of
(X_{a},X_{b})\in\{\pm 1\}^{2}
.

Let
\mathrm{wt}
be a
1
-cocycle representing the cohomology class
[\mathrm{wt}]
and let
\kappa:E\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
be a
1
-cocycle representing
[\kappa]
. The
Kasteleyn matrix

K_{(\Gamma,\mathrm{wt},\kappa)}(z,w)
is the Laurent-polynomial-valued matrix with rows and columns indexed by white and black vertices respectively defined by

K_{(\Gamma,\mathrm{wt},\kappa)}(z,w)_{\mathrm{w}\mathrm{b}}:=\sum_{e=\{\mathrm{b},\mathrm{w}\}}\mathrm{wt}(e)\kappa(e)\phi(e),

where the sum is over all edges with endpoints
\mathrm{b}
and
\mathrm{w}
. The determinant

P_{(\Gamma,\mathrm{wt},\kappa)}(z,w):=\det K_{(\Gamma,\mathrm{wt},\kappa)}(z,w)

is a Laurent polynomial called the
characteristic polynomial
. The
Newton polygon
of
P_{(\Gamma,\mathrm{wt},\kappa)}(z,w)
is defined as

\text{convex-hull}\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\text{coefficient of $z^{i}w^{j}$ is nonzero in $P_{(\Gamma,\mathrm{wt},\kappa)}(z,w)$}\}.

For minimal
\Gamma
, the Newton polygon of
P_{(\Gamma,\mathrm{wt},\kappa)}(z,w)
coincides with the Newton polygon of
\Gamma
, whence the name. The vanishing locus
C^{\circ}:=\{(z,w)\in(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2}:P_{(\Gamma,\mathrm{wt},\kappa)}(z,w)=0\}
is called the
open spectral curve
.

The
amoeba

\mathbb{A}(C^{\circ})
is defined to be the image of
C^{\circ}
under the map
\operatorname{Log}:(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by
(z,w)\mapsto(\log|z|,\log|w|)
. A curve
C^{\circ}
defined by a real Laurent polynomial
P(z,w)
is said to be a
Harnack curve
if the restriction

\operatorname*{Log}:C^{\circ}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}(C^{\circ})

is
2:1
over the interior of
\mathbb{A}(C^{\circ})
. For Harnack curves, the boundary of
\mathbb{A}(C^{\circ})
is the image of the real points
C^{\circ}(\mathbb{R})
.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
KS04
,
KOS06
]
)
.

The open spectral curve
C^{\circ}
of a dimer model is a Harnack curve.

The Newton polygon
N
defines a compactification

\mathcal{N}:=\overline{\{[z^{i}w^{j}]_{(i,j)\in N\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}}:(z,w)\in(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2}\}}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{\#N\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}-1}

of
(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2}
called a
toric surface
. The boundary

\mathcal{N}-(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2}=\bigcup_{S\text{ side of }N}D_{S}

where each
D_{S}\cong\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
is called a

. Taking closure, we get a compactification

5.
A characterization of

If we apply square moves at all the square faces of
G^{\square}
, then the resulting graph is
\overline{G^{\square}}
. Let
s_{*}:H_{1}(G^{\square},\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow H_{1}(\overline{G^{\square}},\mathbb{Z})
denote the induced map of cycles and
\mu:\mathcal{X}_{G^{\square}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{\overline{G^{\square}}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})

(G^{\square},[\mathrm{wt}])
, there are two ways to get weights on
\overline{G^{\square}}
:
[\overline{\mathrm{wt}}]
and
\mu([\mathrm{wt}])
. The main result of this section is:

Theorem 5.1
.

The subset
\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\subset\mathcal{X}_{G^{\square}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
is the set of weights such that
\mu([\mathrm{wt}])=[\overline{\mathrm{wt}}]
.

The proof of Theorem
5.1
relies on the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose

\mu([\mathrm{wt}])=[\overline{\mathrm{wt}}].

Then
[\mathrm{wt}]

(X_{f}([\mathrm{wt}]))_{\text{square faces }f}

Proof.

Let
\gamma

6.
Ising spectral data

The goal of this section is to describe the subset of spectral data that corresponds to Ising models, i.e., the image of

\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{X}_{G^{\square}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{G^{\square}}.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
G
be a minimal graph in
\mathbb{T}
,
G^{\square}
the corresponding bipartite graph,
{\mathrm{w}}
a white vertex of
G^{\square}
and
[\kappa]
a Kasteleyn sign on
G^{\square}
. Suppose
{\mathrm{b}}
denotes the black vertex incident to
\mathrm{w}
that is not a part of the square containing
\mathrm{w}

denote the zig-zag paths that contain the edge

. Let
[\mathrm{wt}]\in\mathcal{X}_{G^{\square}}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
and let
(C,D_{{\mathrm{w}}},\nu_{G^{\square}})=\lambda_{(G^{\square},{\mathrm{w}})}([\mathrm{wt}],[\kappa])
. Then
[\mathrm{wt}]\in\mathcal{I}_{G}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
if and only if

(1)

C
is invariant under the involution
\sigma:(z,w)\rightarrow(z^{-1},w^{-1})
.

(2)

D_{{\mathrm{w}}}
is a standard divisor satisfying

D_{{\mathrm{w}}}+\sigma(D_{{\mathrm{w}}})\sim K_{C}+\nu_{G^{\square}}(\alpha)+\nu_{G^{\square}}(\overline{\alpha}).

Such divisors are in the Prym variety of

[

). If

as in Remark
3.3
.

(3)

For every zig-zag path
\alpha
of

we have

\nu_{G^{\square}}(\overline{\alpha})=\sigma(\nu_{G^{\square}}(\alpha)).

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{d}_{G^{\square}}(\mathrm{b})-\mathbf{d}_{G^{\square}}(\mathrm{w})=\nu_{G^{\square}}(\alpha)+\nu_{G^{\square}}(\overline{\alpha})
, by Theorem
