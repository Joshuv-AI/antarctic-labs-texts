From Parseval to Grothendieck and a little beyond, part I
Ron Blei

Abstract




1.
Foreward

Le théorème fondamental de la théorie metrique des produits tensoriels

had first appeared, so dubbed, in Alexander Grothendieck’s landmark work
(
Grothendieck, 1953
)
that at the time went largely unnoticed. Fifteen years later in another landmark work
(
Lindenstrauss and Pelczynski, 1968
)
,
the fundamental theorem
, its essence distilled, was reformulated
as
the Grothendieck inequality
, and, since then, has been applied, extended, and reinterpreted in diverse contexts and settings
(
Pisier, 2012
)
.

Our work on and around the Grothendieck inequality – a fundamental statement about Euclidean spaces – began four or so decades ago with the observation that Littlewood’s
mixed-norm inequality and the Khintchin
(L^{1}_{R}\hookrightarrow L^{2})
-inequality are, in a precise sense, equivalent. Noting that Littlewood’s inequality is a special case of Grothendieck’s, and therefore so is Khintchin’s, we considered a question that seemed natural to us: can the Grothendieck inequality be derived from the Khintchin inequality? The answer was
yes
, and the key an ’upgrade’ of the Khintchin inequality
(
Blei, 1977
)
.

An ’upgrade’ here means this. Each of the aforementioned inequalities is equivalent to existence of
dual maps
:

(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})
-
interpolants
(the Khintchin inequality),
(l^{\infty,2}\hookrightarrow M)
-
interpolants
(Littlewood’s inequality), and
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})
-
representations
(the Grothendieck inequality), each in effect expressing a property of Euclidean space. An ’upgrade’ in our context refers to existence of dual maps with an additional property, not
a priori
guaranteed by the corresponding inequality.
The task at hand – a derivation of the Grothendieck inequality from Khintchin’s – required control over
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})
-interpolants that was not provided by the Khintchin inequality
proper
. The derivation generalized, and indeed opened paths to other ’Grothendieck-type’ descriptions of Euclidean as well as non-Euclidean spaces.

Our starting point is a ’dual’ view of the Grothendieck inequality: the existence of representations of Euclidean vectors by bounded functions (via
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})
-
representations
), analogous to the classical representations by square-integrable functions (via
unitary maps
), wherein Parseval’s identity looms large and central. Indeed, the Grothendieck inequality in its dual form manifests a Parseval-like formula, synthesized – in our proof of it – from the usual Parseval formula in a harmonic analysis setting. In the first part of the monograph (Ch.2 - Ch.6), we explain the underlying motivation, review classical and neo-classical results, and describe upgrades of the Grothendieck inequality, as well as extensions of it to
l^{p}
-sequence spaces. In the second part (Ch.7 - Ch.11), building on ideas detailed in the first, we derive Grothendieck-type representations of mixed-norm spaces, including spaces of operators.

The monograph is intended for readers with interest in analysis, and specifically with interest in the
Grothendieck inequality
and topics around it. Since its appearance, the inequality has indeed gained in stature over the years, impacting en route functional analysis, harmonic analysis, probability theory, theoretical physics, and recently also theoretical computer science; see
Pisier, 2012
. This book, starting from first principles, tells a tale about a subject that – we believe – is still in its ’mid-life,’ still open-ended with a range of unresolved issues.

We assume familiarity with rudiments of functional and harmonic analysis usually covered in introductory courses. And otherwise we will review and explain, as we move along, relevant concepts and results that are either studied in advanced courses, or found in the research literature at large.

2.
Classical and neoclassical notions

2.1.
Unitary maps

Let
A
be a set, and let
l^{2}(A)
denote the Euclidean space whose ’coordinate axes’ are indexed by
A
. That is, consider the space of complex-valued, square-summable functions on
A
,

(2.1)

l^{2}(A)\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \big\{{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{C}^{A}:\sum_{\alpha\in A}|{\bf{x}}(\alpha)|^{2}\ <\infty\big\},

equipped with the usual inner product,

(2.2)

\langle{\bf{x}},{\bf{y}}\rangle\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \sum_{\alpha\in A}{\bf{x}}(\alpha)\overline{{\bf{y}}(\alpha)},\ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A),\ {\bf{y}}\in l^{2}(A),

and Euclidean norm,

(2.3)

\|{\bf{x}}\|_{2}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \sqrt{\langle{\bf{x}},{\bf{x}}\rangle}\ =\ \bigg(\sum_{\alpha\in A}|{\bf{x}}(\alpha)|^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},\ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A).

We view
A
merely as an indexing set, with no assumptions about its cardinality, or structures therein. Summing nonnegative scalars
y_{\alpha}
over
\alpha\in A
has the usual meaning,

(2.4)

\sum_{\alpha\in A}y_{\alpha}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\sup_{\text{finite }F\subset A}\ \sum_{\alpha\in F}y_{\alpha}.

If
(y_{\alpha}:\alpha\in A)\in\mathbb{C}^{A}
is absolutely summable over
A
(i.e.,
\sum_{\alpha\in A}|y_{\alpha}|<\infty
), then its support
\big\{\alpha:y_{\alpha}\neq 0\big\}=A^{\prime}
is at most countably infinite, and the sum
\sum_{\alpha\in A}y_{\alpha}
is well-defined: for any sequence of finite sets
E_{j}
increasing to
A^{\prime}
,

(2.5)

E_{j}\subset E_{j+1}\subset A^{\prime},\ \ \ j\in\mathbb{N},\ \ \ \ \ \ \bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}E_{j}=A^{\prime},

the limit of the partial sums

(2.6)

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in E_{j}}y_{\alpha}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \sum_{\alpha\in A}y_{\alpha}

exists, and does not depend on the choice of
(E_{j})_{j}
.

Let
(\Omega,\mu)
be a general measure space, and consider

(2.7)

L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)=\big\{\mathbb{C}\text{-valued measurable functions $f$ on}\ \Omega:\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}d\mu<\infty\big\},

wherein elements are listed modulo the equivalence

f\equiv g\ \Leftrightarrow\ f=g\ \ \text{a.e.}(\mu),\ \ \ f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu),\ g\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu).

The resulting space of equivalence class representatives – denoted also by
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
– is a Hilbert space, with
inner product

(2.8)

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \int_{\Omega}f\ \overline{g}\ d\mu,\ \ \ \ \ f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu),\ g\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu),

and
L^{2}
-norm

(2.9)

\|f\|_{L^{2}}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle_{L^{2}}}=\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}d\mu\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},\ \ \ \ \ f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu).

Assuming the dimension of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
is at least the cardinality of
A
, we take an orthonormal family

\{f_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}\subset L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
, and consider the linear injection

(2.10)

U\ (=U_{\{f_{\alpha}\}}):l^{2}(A)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)

defined by

(2.11)

U{\bf{x}}=\sum_{\alpha\in A}{\bf{x}}(\alpha)f_{\alpha},\ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A).

For
{\bf{x}}
and
{\bf{y}}
in
l^{2}(A),

(2.12)

\langle{\bf{x}},{\bf{y}}\rangle\ =\ \int_{\Omega}{U\bf{x}}\ \overline{U{\bf{y}}}\ d\mu\ =\ \langle{U\bf{x}},U{\bf{y}}\rangle_{L^{2}},\\
\\

and (therefore)

(2.13)

\|{\bf{x}}\|_{2}=\|U{\bf{x}}\|_{L^{2}}.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be Hilbert spaces with respective inner products
\langle\bm{\cdot},\bm{\cdot}\rangle_{H_{1}}
and
\langle\bm{\cdot},\bm{\cdot}\rangle_{H_{2}}.
A map
V:H_{1}\rightarrow H_{2}
is said to be
unitary
, and
H_{1}

unitarily equivalent
to
V[H_{1}]
, if

(2.14)

\langle{\bf{x}},{\bf{y}}\rangle_{H_{1}}=\langle{V(\bf{x}}),V({\bf{y}})\rangle_{H_{2}},\ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in H_{1},\ {\bf{y}}\in H_{2}.

Every unitary map is necessarily
injective, linear
, and
bounded
, and in particular norm-continuous. In our case,
U
in (
2.11
) is unitary, and
l^{2}(A)
unitarily equivalent to

L^{2}_{\{f_{\alpha}\}}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ L^{2}\text{-closure of the linear span of}\ \{f_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}.

Remark 2.2

(a classical example; e.g.,
Katznelson, 1976
)
.

Let

A=\mathbb{Z}\ (\text{the integer group}),\ \ \ \ \Omega=[0,2\pi)\ {=}\ \mathbb{T}\ (\text{the circle group}),

\mu=dt/2\pi\ \ (\text{normalized Lebesgue measure}),

and
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{T},dt/2\pi)
,

(2.15)

e_{j}(t)\ =\ \exp({\mathfrak{i}jt}),\ \ \ \ j\in\mathbb{Z},\ \ t\in\mathbb{T},\ \ \mathfrak{i}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sqrt{-1}.

Then
U_{\{e_{j}\}}:l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\ \rightarrow\ L^{2}(\mathbb{T},dt)
,

(2.16)

U_{\{e_{j}\}}{\bf{x}}\ =\ \sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}{\bf{x}}(j)\ e_{j},\ \ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}),

is the inverse of the Fourier transform map

(2.17)

\mathcal{F}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T},dt/2\pi)\ \rightarrow\ l^{2}(\mathbb{Z}),

(2.18)

\big(\mathcal{F}g\big)(j)\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \widehat{g}(j)\ =\ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{t\in\mathbb{T}}g(t)\ e_{j}(-t)\ dt,\ \ \ \ \ g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},dt/2\pi),\ \ j\in\mathbb{Z},

whereby

(2.19)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{t\in\mathbb{T}}f(t)\ \overline{g(t)}\ dt\ =\ \sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{f}(j)\ \overline{\widehat{g}(j)},\ \ \ \ f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},dt/2\pi),\ \ g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},dt/2\pi).

Remark 2.3

(Parseval)
.

In a framework of harmonic analysis, if
\Omega
is a compact abelian group with normalized Haar measure
\mu
, and
\{f_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
is a set of characters of
\Omega
, then the assertion in (
2.12
) is generally referred to as
Parseval’s formula
(e.g., (
2.19
) above). As such, Parseval’s formula is a special case of the more general Plancherel theorem
(
Rudin, 1967
, §1.6)
: if
\Omega
is a locally compact abelian group with dual group
\Gamma
, then there exist Haar measures
\mu
on
\Omega
and
\nu
on
\Gamma
, with the property that the transform

(2.20)

\widehat{f}(\gamma)\ \overset{def}{=}\ \int_{G}f\ \overline{\gamma}\ d\mu,\ \ \ \ \ f\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mu),\ \ \gamma\in\Gamma,

is uniquely extendible to a unitary map from
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
onto
L^{2}(\Gamma,\nu)
, i.e.,

(2.21)

\int_{\omega\in\Omega}f(\omega)\ \overline{g}(\omega)\ \mu(d\omega)\ =\ \int_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\widehat{f}(\gamma)\ \overline{\widehat{g}}(\gamma)\ \nu(d\gamma),\ \ \ \ \ f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu),\ \ g\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu).\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \

(E.g., see
Katznelson, 1976
for the classical
(\Omega,\mu)=(\mathbb{R},dt)
and
(\Gamma,\nu)=(\mathbb{R},dt/2\pi)
.) The statement in (
2.21
) – a non-trivial generalization of the analogous assertion for compact
\Omega
– is sometime referred to also as a
Parseval formula
. In the parlance of harmonic analysis, a
Parseval formula
could also refer to relations between maps and their transforms in ’non-Hilbertian’ settings (e.g.,
Katznelson, 1976
. In the case of a general measure space
(\Omega,\mu)
, we refer to (
2.12
) as
Parseval’s identity
.

Remark 2.4

(notation)
.

Given an orthonormal system
\{f_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}\subset L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
, we let
U_{\{f_{\alpha}\}}
denote the map from
\mathbb{C}^{A}
to the class of series spanned by
\{f_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
, formally defined by

(2.22)

{\bf{x}}\ \mapsto\ U_{\{f_{\alpha}\}}{\bf{x}}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \sum_{\alpha\in A}{\bf{x}}(\alpha)f_{\alpha},\ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{C}^{A}.

When restricted to
{\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A)
, the series
\sum_{\alpha\in A}{\bf{x}}(\alpha)f_{\alpha}
converges in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
, and Parseval’s identity holds.

Henceforth,
(\Omega,\mu)
will be a probability space, i.e., a measure space with
\mu(\Omega)=1
.

2.2.
(
l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{p}
)-representations

The effect of an orthonormal
\{f_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}\subset L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
on the corresponding
U_{\{f_{\alpha}\}}
can be calibrated by tail-probabilities

(2.23)

\mu\big(|U_{\{f_{\alpha}\}}{\bf{x}}|\geq t\big),\ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A),\ \ t>0

(e.g.,
Blei, 2011
). In the case of
complete
systems
\{f_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
, the ’square power’ estimates

(2.24)

\mu\big(|U_{\{f_{\alpha}\}}{\bf{x}}|\geq t\big)\ \leq\frac{\|{\bf{x}}\|_{2}^{2}}{t^{2}},\ \ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A),

are optimal: for infinite
A
, if
\{f_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
, then

(2.25)

\sup\bigg\{\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}{t^{q}}\mu\big(|U_{\{f_{\alpha}\}}{\bf{x}}|>t\big):{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{l^{2}(A)}\bigg\}=\infty,\ \ \ \ \ \ q>2.

(Throughout,
\mathcal{B}_{Y}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\big\{y\in Y:\|y\|_{Y}\leq 1\big\}
will denote the closed unit ball in a normed linear space
Y.
) Moving away from ’completeness,’ as systems become ’sparser,’ tail-probabilities get ’smaller’ and become sub-Gaussian in the limit.
To illustrate the limiting case in a generic setting, we take

(2.26)

\Omega\ =\ \Omega_{A}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \{-1,1\}^{A}\ \ \ (\ =\ \text{$\{-1,1\}$-valued functions on $A$})

endowed with the product topology, i.e., weakest topology with respect to which the coordinate functions
r_{\alpha}:\Omega_{A}\rightarrow\{-1,1\},

(2.27)

r_{\alpha}(\omega)=\omega(\alpha),\ \ \ \omega\in\Omega_{A},\ \ \ \alpha\in A,

are continuous, and then let
\mu
be the uniform probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{A}
on the resulting Borel field (the product probability measure heuristically associated with independent tosses of a fair coin), i.e.,

(2.28)

\mathbb{P}_{A}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \bigtimes_{\alpha\in A}\mathbb{P}_{0}\ \ \ \ (\text{product measure}),

where
\mathbb{P}_{0}
is the uniform probability measure on
\{-1,1\}
,

(2.29)

\mathbb{P}_{0}(\{-1\})=\mathbb{P}_{0}(\{1\})=1/2.

Equipped with coordinate-wise multiplication (point-wise product of
\{-1,1\}
-valued functions on
A
),
\Omega_{A}
becomes a compact abelian group with Haar measure
\mathbb{P}_{A}
.
The dual group
\widehat{\Omega}_{A}
is the
Walsh system

(2.30)

W_{A}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{A,k},

where
W_{A,0}=\{r_{0}\},

r_{0}\equiv 1
on
\Omega_{A}
, and

(2.31)

W_{A,k}=\bigg\{\prod_{\alpha\in F}r_{\alpha}:F\subset A,\ \#F=k\bigg\}\ \ \ \text{(\emph{Walsh characters} of order $k$),}\ \ \ k\in\mathbb{N}.

We distinguish (for reasons that will later become apparent) between
characters of odd and even order,

(2.32)

W_{A,odd}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{A,2k+1},\ \ \ \ W_{A,even}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{A,2k}.

At one end, we have the
Walsh system

W_{A}
, an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\Omega_{A},\mathbb{P}_{A})
, and at the other end, we have the
Rademacher system

(2.33)

R_{A}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \{r_{\alpha}:\alpha\in A\}=W_{A,1},

an ’optimally’ sparse set of independent characters of
\Omega_{A}
(e.g.,
Blei, 2001
). Taking

(2.34)

{\bf{x}}\rightarrow U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}\ =\ \sum_{\alpha\in A}{\bf{x}}(\alpha)r_{\alpha},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A),

we obtain from the
(L^{2}_{R}\hookrightarrow L^{p}
)-
Khintchin inequalities
(Remark
2.9
below) sub-Gaussian tail estimates

(2.35)

\mathbb{P}_{A}\big(|U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}|>t\big)\leq e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}},\ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{l^{2}(A)},\ \ \ t>0,

which are sharp:
for infinite
A
,

(2.36)

\sup\bigg\{\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}e^{t^{q}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\big(|U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}|>t\big):{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{l^{2}(A)}\bigg\}=\infty,\ \ \ \ \ \ q>2.

Generally, under ’unitarity,’ sub-Gaussian tails are always optimal: for infinite
A,
if
V:l^{2}(A)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
is unitary, then

(2.37)

\sup\bigg\{\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}e^{t^{q}}\mu\big(|V{\bf{x}}|>t\big):{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{B}_{l^{2}(A)}\bigg\}=\infty,\ \ \ \ \ \ q>2.

(This was pointed out to me by Gilles Pisier, citing results in
Gordon and Lewis, 1975
.)

Question 2.5
.

Could ’smaller’ tails be achieved with maps ’slightly less’ than unitary?

Definition 2.6
.

Let
A
be a set, and
(\Omega,\mu)
a probability space. An injection

(2.38)

\Phi:\ l^{2}(A)\ \rightarrow\ L^{p}(\Omega,\mu),\ \ \ \ \ 2\ \leq\ p\ \leq\ \infty,

is a
(l^{2}(A)\hookrightarrow L^{p})
-
representation
– an
L^{p}
-
representation of

– if

(2.39)

\begin{split}\Phi({\bf{x}})\ &=\ \|{\bf{x}}\|_{2}\ \Phi(\bm{\sigma}{\bf{x}})\ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\neq{\bf{0}},\\

(2.40)

(Remark

),

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\Phi({\bf{x}})\ \Phi({\bf{y}})\ d\mu\ &=\ \sum_{\alpha\in A}{\bf{x}}(\alpha){\bf{y}}(\alpha)\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ {\bf{x}}\bm{\cdot}{\bf{y}}\ \ \text{(dot product)},\ \ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A),\ {\bf{y}}\in l^{2}(A),\end{split}

,

).

(notation and simple observations)
.

. The unit sphere in a normed linear space

When

, and
\bm{\sigma}
when

.

ii
. If

, then

\|F\|_{Y\rightarrow Z}\ \overset{def}{=}\ \sup\big\{\|F({\bf{y}})\|_{Z}:\ {\bf{y}}\in\mathcal{S}_{Y}\big\},

Remark 2.9

(Khintchin)
.

The inequalities

(2.47)

\|U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}\|_{L^{p}}\ \leq\ \sqrt{p},\ \ \ \ \ \text{sets}\ A,\ \ \ p>2,\ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}(A)},

stated and proved first in
Khintchin, 1923
for

, are arguably among the important mathematical discoveries in the 20th century. We refer to (
2.47
) as the
Khintchin

(L^{2}_{R}\hookrightarrow L^{p})
-inequalities, which can be equivalently expressed as the sub-Gaussian tail estimates in (
2.35
), or as the exponential-square integrability property

(2.48)

\sup\bigg\{\int_{\Omega_{A}}e^{|U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}|^{2}}\ d\mathbb{P}_{A}:\ \text{sets}\ A,\ \ {\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}(A)}\bigg\}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \mathfrak{K}\ <\infty.

(For a proof that (
2.47
), (
2.35
), and (
2.48
) are equivalent, see
Stein, 2016
.)

By (
2.47
), the unitary map
U_{R_{A}}
is a
\big(l^{2}(A)\hookrightarrow L^{p}\big)
-representation for all
p\in(2,\infty)
. Whereas
U_{R_{A}}
is a
\big(l^{2}(A)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}\big)
-representation
only if

A
is finite – by (
2.36
), but more simply, because
2\|U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\geq\|{\bf{x}}\|_{1}
for

with finite support. In due course, by using (
2.47
), we will ’slightly’ perturb

to construct
\big(l^{2}(A)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}\big)
-representations of

for arbitrary
A.

Remark 2.10

(representations
vs.
unitary maps)
.

Unlike unitary maps,
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{2})
-representations need not be linear, homogeneous, or continuous. (E.g., see proof of Proposition
3.3
in the next chapter.) If a
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{2})
-representation
\Phi
commutes with complex conjugation, i.e., if

(2.49)

\Phi(\overline{{\bf{x}}})=\overline{\Phi({\bf{x}})},\ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A),

then
\Phi
is unitary, and (therefore) linear. Conversely, if an
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{2})
-representation
\Phi
is linear, then
\Phi
is continuous, and (therefore) commutes with conjugation. That is,

Proposition 2.11
.

A
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{2})
-representation is unitary if and only if it is linear.

If
V:l^{2}(A)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
is unitary, and
V({\bf{e}}_{\alpha})
is real-valued (
\alpha\in A
), where
\{{\bf{e}}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
is the canonical basis of
l^{2}(A)
,

(2.50)

\begin{split}{\bf{e}}_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\prime})\ &=\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}1,&\quad\ \alpha=\alpha^{\prime}\\
\\
0,&\quad\ \alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime},\end{array}\right.\end{split}

then
V
is a
L^{2}
-representation of
l^{2}(A)
. Otherwise, not every unitary map is a
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{2})
-representation; e.g., in Remark
2.2
, if
A=\mathbb{Z}
,
\Omega=[0,2\pi)
, and
\mu=\text{Lebesgue measure}
, then
\Phi=U_{\{e_{j}\}}
fails (
2.41
).

If an
L^{\infty}
-representation of
l^{2}(A)
is unitary, then the underlying
A
must be finite: e.g., by (
2.37
); or, via the precise quantitative results in
Alon et al., 2006
(cf. Remark
3.8
). In Chapter 4, we construct
(l^{2}(A)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})
-representations for arbitrary
A
, uniformly bounded in the
(l^{2}\rightarrow L^{\infty})
-norm, and arbitrarily close to unitary maps in the
(l^{2}\rightarrow L^{2})
-norm. Our focus here on uniformly bounded
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})
-representations is motivated mainly by Proposition
3.3
(in the next chapter), asserting that existence of such representations is equivalent to a phenomenon known as
the Grothendieck inequality
.

3.
The Grothendieck inequality

3.1.
Statement

The Grothendieck constant

\mathcal{K}_{G}
is the ’smallest’
K>1
such that for all sets
A
, finite sets
B
, and scalar-valued arrays
(a_{uv})_{(u,v)\in B\times B}\in\mathbb{C}^{B\times B}
,

(3.1)

\begin{split}\sup\big\{\big|&\sum_{(u,v)\in B\times B}a_{uv}\ {\bf{x}}_{u}\bm{\cdot}{\bf{y}}_{v}\big|:{\bf{x}}_{u}\in\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}(A)},\ {\bf{y}}_{v}\in\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}(A)},\ (u,v)\in B\times B\big\}\\
\\
&\ \leq\ K\ \sup\big\{\big|\sum_{(u,v)\in B\times B}a_{uv}\ s_{u}t_{v}\big|:|s_{u}|=1,\ |t_{v}|=1,\ (u,v)\in B\times B\big\}.\\
\ \end{split}

The Grothendieck inequality
is the assertion

(3.2)

\mathcal{K}_{G}\ <\ \infty.

\mathcal{K}_{G}
has two values, depending on the underlying scalar field: if the supremum on the left of (
3.1
) is over
\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)}
(
\mathbb{R}
-valued vectors) and on the right over
\{s_{u}=\pm 1,\ t_{v}=\pm 1\}
, then

\mathcal{K}_{G}\ =\ \mathcal{K}_{G}^{\mathbb{R}}\ \ \ \ \text{(the 'real' Grothendieck constant)};

if supremum is over
\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}(A)}
(
\mathbb{C}
-valued vectors) on the left and
\{|s_{u}|=1,\ |t_{v}|=1\}
on the right, then

\mathcal{K}_{G}\ =\ \mathcal{K}_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}\ \ \ \ \text{(the 'complex' Grothendieck constant)}.

The numerical values of
\mathcal{K}_{G}^{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}
are open problems of long standing, and indeed of ongoing interest; e.g., see
Braverman et al., 2011
.

Distinguishing between scalar fields on the right of (
3.1
),
we have for finite
B
and
(a_{u})_{u\in B}\in\mathbb{C}^{B}
,

(3.3)

\sum_{u\in B}|a_{u}|\ \leq\ \frac{\pi}{2}\sup\big\{\big|\sum_{u\in B}a_{u}s_{u}\big|:\ s_{u}=\pm 1,\ u\in B\big\}\ =\ \frac{\pi}{2}\big\|\sum_{u\in B}a_{u}r_{u}\big\|_{\infty},

where
\pi/2
is ’best’ possible
(
Seigner, 1997
)
, and then for
(a_{uv})_{(u,v)\in B\times B}\in\mathbb{C}^{B\times B}
,

(3.4)

\begin{split}\|{\bf{a}}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon}}\ \overset{def}{=}\ \sup\big\{\big|\sum_{(u,v)\in B\times B}a_{uv}&\ s_{u}t_{v}\big|:|s_{u}|=1,\ |t_{v}|=1,\ (u,v)\in B\times B\big\}\\
\ &\leq\ \frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\sup\big\{\big|\sum_{(u,v)\in B\times B}a_{uv}\ s_{u}t_{v}\big|:s_{u}=\pm 1,\ t_{v}=\pm 1,\ (u,v)\in B\times B\big\}\\
\ &=\ \frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\big\|\sum_{(u,v)\in B\times B}a_{uv}\ r_{u}\otimes r_{v}\big\|_{\infty}\ \overset{def}{=}\ \frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\ \|{\bf{a}}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon}^{\mathbb{R}}}.\end{split}

Remark 3.1

(about the norms)
.

A finite-dimensional scalar matrix

{\bf{a}}=(a_{uv})_{(u,v)\in B\times B}\in\mathbb{C}^{B\times B}\ \ \ (\text{finite}\ B)

can be viewed as a representing matrix of a
bilinear
functional acting on
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions defined on
B
,

(3.5)

({\bf{s}},{\bf{t}})\ \mapsto\ \sum_{(u,v)\in B\times B}a_{uv}\ s_{u}t_{v},\ \ \ \ {\bf{s}}=(s_{u})_{u\in B}\ \in\mathbb{C}^{B},\ \ {\bf{t}}=(t_{u})_{u\in B}\ \in\mathbb{C}^{B}.

With the supremum norm on
\mathbb{C}^{B}
,

(3.6)

\|{\bf{s}}\|_{\infty}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \max\{|s_{u}|:u\in B\}\ \ \ \ \ {\bf{s}}=(s_{u})_{u\in B}\ \in\mathbb{C}^{B},

the norm of the bilinear functional in (
3.5
) is

(3.7)

\|{\bf{a}}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon}}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \sup\big\{\big|\sum_{(u,v)\in B\times B}a_{uv}\ s_{u}t_{v}\big|:|s_{u}|=1,\ |t_{v}|=1\big\}.

We can similarly view
{\bf{a}}=(a_{uv})_{(u,v)\in B\times B}\in\mathbb{C}^{B\times B}
as a representing matrix of the bilinear functional on
\big(l^{2}(A)\big)^{B}
defined by

(3.8)

\begin{split}({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\ \mapsto\ \sum_{(u,v)\in B\times B}a_{uv}\ &{\bf{x}}_{u}\bm{\cdot}{\bf{y}}_{v},\\
\ &{\bf{x}}=({\bf{x}}_{u})_{u\in B}\ \in\ \big(l^{2}(A)\big)^{B},\ \ {\bf{y}}=({\bf{y}}_{v})_{v\in B}\ \in\ \big(l^{2}(A)\big)^{B},\end{split}

whence, with the supremum norm on
\big(l^{2}(A)\big)^{B}

(3.9)

\|{\bf{x}}\|_{l^{\infty}(B;l^{2})}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \max\{\|{\bf{x}}_{u}\|_{l^{2}(A)}:u\in B\},\ \ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}=({\bf{x}}_{u})_{u\in B}\ \in\ \big(l^{2}(A)\big)^{B},

the norm of the functional in (
3.8
) becomes

(3.10)

\|{\bf{a}}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon,l^{2}(A)}}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \sup\big\{\big|\sum_{(u,v)\in B\times B}a_{uv}\ {\bf{x}}_{u}\bm{\cdot}{\bf{y}}_{v}\big|:{\bf{x}}_{u}\in\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}(A)},\ {\bf{y}}_{v}\in\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}(A)}\big\}.

The Grothendieck inequality asserts that
\|\bm{\cdot}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon,l^{2}}}
and
\|\bm{\cdot}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon}}
are equivalent norms;
namely, that there exist
K\in(1,\infty)
with the property that for all sets
A
and finite sets
B
,

(3.11)

\|{\bf{a}}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon}}\ \leq\ \|{\bf{a}}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon,l^{2}(A)}}\ \leq\ K\|{\bf{a}}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon}},\ \ \ \ \ \ {\bf{a}}\in\mathbb{C}^{B\times B}.

In particular,

(3.12)

\sup\big\{\|{\bf{a}}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon,l^{2}(A)}}/\|{\bf{a}}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon}}:A,\ \text{finite}\ B,\ \text{non-zero}\ {\bf{a}}\in\mathbb{C}^{B\times B}\big\}\ {=}\ \mathcal{K}_{G}\ <\ \infty.

In a setting of topological tensor products (e.g.,
Ryan, 2002
), the norm
\|\bm{\cdot}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon}}
in (
3.7
) is an instance of the
injective tensor norm
, sometimes denoted by
\|\bm{\cdot}\|_{{\vee\atop\otimes}}
and referred to as the
smallest crossnorm
(e.g.,
Schatten, 1943
). In a framework of harmonic analysis,
\|\bm{\cdot}\|_{\otimes_{\epsilon}}
is the supremum norm of linear combinations of two-fold products of Rademacher or Steinhaus characters, a view that will facilitate here duality arguments and, in particular, applications of the Riesz-Kakutani representation theorem:

Theorem 3.2

(F. Riesz
(
Riesz, 1909
)
, S. Kakutani
(
Kakutani, 1941
)
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a compact Hausdorff space,
C(\mathcal{X})
the space of scalar-valued continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}
, and
M(\mathcal{X})
the space of scalar-valued regular Borel measures on
\mathcal{X}
. If
\theta
is a continuous linear functional on
C(\mathcal{X})
, then there exists a unique
\mu\in M(\mathcal{X})
, such that

(3.13)

\theta(f)\ =\ \int_{\mathcal{X}}f\ d\mu,\ \ \ \ \ f\in C(\mathcal{X}),

and

(3.14)

\|\theta\|\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \sup_{\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1}|\theta(f)|\ =|\mu|(\mathcal{X})\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \|\mu\|_{M},

where
|\mu|
is the total variation measure of
\mu
.

3.2.
Dual statement

For a set
A
, let

(3.15)

\mathcal{K}^{\star}(A)\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \inf\bigg\{\|\Phi\|_{l^{2}(A)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}}:\big(l^{2}(A)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}\big)\text{-representations}\ \Phi\bigg\},

and

(3.16)

\mathcal{K}^{\star}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \sup_{A}\mathcal{K}^{\star}(A),

where supremum ranges over all sets
A
.
Like the Grothendieck constant,
\mathcal{K}^{\star}
is two-valued:
\mathcal{K}^{\star}=\mathcal{K}^{*,\mathbb{R}}
if infimum in (
3.15
) ranges over
L^{\infty}
-representations of
l^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
(
\mathbb{R}
-valued vectors),
and
\mathcal{K}^{\star}=\mathcal{K}^{*,\mathbb{C}}
if infimum ranges over
L^{\infty}
-representations of
l^{2}(A)
(
\mathbb{C}
-valued vectors). Representations in both cases are generally complex-valued: for, if
\Phi
is an
L^{\infty}
-representation of
l^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(A),
and
\Phi({\bf{x}})
is real-valued for every
{\bf{x}}\in l^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
, then
\Phi
is necessarily unitary, and

must be finite.

The proposition below – a statement about duality – asserts that the Grothendieck inequality and the existence of uniformly bounded
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})
-representations are equivalent in the sense that

(3.17)

\mathcal{K}^{\star}<\infty\ \Leftrightarrow\ \mathcal{K}_{G}<\infty.

Proposition 3.3

(cf.
Blei, 2014
)
.

(3.18)

\big(\mathcal{K}^{*,\mathbb{R}}\big)^{2}/4\ \leq\ \mathcal{K}_{G}^{\mathbb{R}}\ \leq\ \frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\big(\mathcal{K}^{*,\mathbb{R}}\big)^{2},

and

(3.19)

\big(\mathcal{K}^{*,\mathbb{C}}\big)^{2}/4\ \leq\ \mathcal{K}_{G}^{\mathbb{C}}\ \leq\ \big(\mathcal{K}^{*,\mathbb{C}}\big)^{2}.

3.4

Remark 3.5

(proposition deconstructed, and a preview)
.

The proof of Proposition
3.3
rests on the equivalence (via Riesz-Kakutani and Hahn-Banach) between ’
\mathcal{K}_{G}<\infty
’ and the assertion (cf.
Blei, 1976

for every set

\nu\in M(\Omega_{\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}(A)}}\times\Omega_{\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}(A)}})
such that

4.
The Khintchin, Littlewood, and Orlicz inequalities

4.1.
Preliminaries

In this chapter we consider three classical precursors to the Grothendieck inequality, with emphasis on their dual statements.

We assume some familiarity with harmonic analysis on groups, and will use terminology and notation that is mostly standard. (E.g., Chapters 1 and 2 of
Rudin, 1967
.) For a discrete abelian group
\Gamma,
its compact dual
\widehat{\Gamma}
and normalized Haar measure
\mathfrak{m}
on it, and
E\subset\Gamma
, the following spaces recur throughout:

(4.1)

\begin{split}L^{p}_{E}\ &{=}\ \big\{f\in L^{p}(\widehat{\Gamma},\mathfrak{m}):\widehat{f}(\gamma)=0,\ \gamma\in\Gamma\setminus E\big\},\ \ \ \ \ 1\leq p\leq\infty,\\
\\
C_{E}\ &{=}\ \big\{f\in C(\widehat{\Gamma}):\widehat{f}(\gamma)=0,\ \gamma\in\Gamma\setminus E\big\},\\
\\
M_{E}\ &{=}\ \big\{\mu\in M(\widehat{\Gamma}):\widehat{\mu}(\gamma)=0,\ \gamma\in\Gamma\setminus E\big\}.\end{split}

The space of
\mathbb{C}
-valued
\Gamma
-series spanned by
E
is denoted by

(4.2)

\mathfrak{S}_{E}\ =\ \bigg\{\sum_{\gamma\in E}\phi(\gamma)\ \gamma:\ \phi\in\mathbb{C}^{E}\bigg\},

and if
\phi
in (
4.2
) is restricted to a subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{E}
, then the corresponding resulting subspace of
\mathfrak{S}_{E}
will be formally so designated, e.g.,

(4.3)

\mathfrak{S}_{E}^{s}\ \overset{def}{=}\ \bigg\{\sum_{\gamma\in E}\phi(\gamma)\ \gamma:\ \phi\in l^{s}(E)\bigg\},\ \ \ \ \ s\in[1,\infty].

The
\Gamma
-
transform of

\mathfrak{s}=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\phi(\gamma)\gamma\ \in\mathfrak{S}_{\Gamma}
is denoted by

(4.4)

\widehat{\mathfrak{s}}(\gamma)\ =\ \phi(\gamma),\ \ \ \ \gamma\in\Gamma.

The
spectrum
of
\mathfrak{s}
is the support of
\widehat{\mathfrak{s}}
. If
E\subset\Gamma
, and the spectrum of
\mathfrak{s}\in\mathfrak{S}_{E}
is finite, then
\mathfrak{s}
is an
E
-

.

With each
\mu\in M(\widehat{\Gamma})
(a regular Borel measure on
\widehat{\Gamma}
) we associate the
\Gamma
-series

(4.5)

\begin{split}\mu\ &=\ \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\widehat{\mu}(\gamma)\gamma,\\
\ \widehat{\mu}(\gamma)\ =\ \int_{\widehat{\Gamma}}\overline{\gamma}\ d\mu,\ \ \ \ &\gamma\in\Gamma\ \ \ (\text{\emph{Fourier-Stieltjes transform})},\end{split}

which uniquely determines
\mu
. We then formally identify, via (
4.5
), each space in (
4.1
) as a subspace of
\mathfrak{S}_{\Gamma}.

In this monograph we work mostly in the ’dyadic’ setting

\Gamma\ =\ W_{A},\ \ \ \widehat{\Gamma}\ =\ \Omega_{A},\ \ \ \mathfrak{m}\ =\ \mathbb{P}_{A},

a basic and convenient setting for our purposes here.

We will be dealing in this chapter, and also in later chapters, with mixed-norm spaces. Specifically, for
s\in[1,\infty]
,
t\in[1,\infty]
, sets
A
and
B
, and
\phi\in\mathbb{C}^{A\times B}

(4.6)

\begin{split}\|\phi\|_{s,t}\ &\overset{def}{=}\ \bigg(\sum_{\alpha\in A}\big(\sum_{\beta\in B}|\phi(\alpha,\beta)|^{t}\big)^{s/t}\bigg)^{1/s},\ \ \ \ \ s\in(1,\infty),\ \ t\in(1,\infty)\\
\\
\|\phi\|_{\infty,t}\ &\overset{def}{=}\ \sup_{\alpha\in A}\big(\sum_{\beta\in B}|\phi(\alpha,\beta)|^{t}\big)^{1/t},\ \ \ \ \ s=\infty,\ \ t\in(1,\infty),\\
\\
\|\phi\|_{s,\infty}\ &\overset{def}{=}\ \bigg(\sum_{\alpha\in A}\ \sup_{\beta\in B}|\phi(\alpha,\beta)|^{s}\bigg)^{1/s},\ \ \ \ \ s\in(1,\infty),\ \ t=\infty,\end{split}

and let

(4.7)

l^{s}\big(A;l^{t}(B)\big)\overset{def}{=}\ l^{s,t}(A,B)\ =\ \big\{\phi\in\mathbb{C}^{A\times B}:\ \|\phi\|_{s,t}<\infty\big\}.

4.4.
Notion of an upgrade

The inequalities

\mathcal{K}_{G}<\infty,\ \ \kappa<\infty,\ \ \kappa_{L}<\infty,\ \ \kappa_{O}<\infty,

in their equivalent dual forms

(4.94)

\begin{split}&(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})\text{-representations \ \ (Grothendieck),}\\
\ &(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})\text{-interpolants \ \ (Khintchin),}\\
\ &(l^{\infty,2}\hookrightarrow M)\text{-interpolants \ \ (Littlewood),}\\
\ \text{and}\ \ \ &\\
&(l^{2,\infty}\hookrightarrow M)\text{-interpolants \ \ (Orlicz).}\end{split}

4.93
),
we obtain a dual map by selecting an element from each equivalence class in a quotient space, whereby the inequality guarantees that elements can be
chosen to be uniformly bounded, and guarantees no more. An
upgrade
is the feasibility of selecting an element in each equivalence class so that the resulting dual map is bounded by an absolute constant, and possesses also an additional property not a priori guaranteed by the inequality proper.

, there exist
g\in\mathcal{G}_{A,\delta}({\bf{x}})
, such that

(4.105)

\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\ \leq\ \mathfrak{e}^{-1}(\delta)+\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2}}.

Proof.

Given
\delta\in(0,1)
, let

(4.106)

\xi\ =\ \mathfrak{e}^{-1}(\delta).

For
{\bf{x}}\in\mathcal{S}_{l^{2}(A)}
, let

(4.107)

h_{{\bf{x}},\xi}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}},&\ \ \ |U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}|\ \leq\ \xi\\
\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

and

(4.108)

\phi_{{\bf{x}},\xi}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}-h_{{\bf{x}},\xi}.

Using (
4.102
), we estimate

(4.109)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{A}}|\phi_{{\bf{x}},\xi}|^{2}\ d\mathbb{P}_{A}\ &=\ \int_{\big\{|U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}|\ >\ \xi\big\}}e^{|U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}|^{2}}\ \big(|U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}|^{2}\ e^{-|U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}|^{2}}\big)\ d\mathbb{P}_{A}\\
\\
&\leq\ \xi^{2}e^{-\xi^{2}}\ \mathfrak{K}.\end{split}

Splitting
\phi_{{\bf{x}},\xi}
into two Walsh series spanned by
R_{A}
and
W_{A}\setminus R_{A}
,
we obtain from (
4.109
)

(4.110)

\big\|\sum_{\alpha\in A}\widehat{\phi_{{\bf{x}},\xi}}(r_{\alpha})r_{\alpha}\big\|_{L^{2}}\ \leq\ \xi e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}\ \sqrt{\mathfrak{K}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \big\|\sum_{w\in W_{A}\setminus R_{A}}\widehat{\phi_{{\bf{x}},\xi}}(w)w\big\|_{L^{2}}\ \leq\ \xi e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}\ \sqrt{\mathfrak{K}}.

Taking
G_{A}=U_{R_{A}}+\mathfrak{p}_{A}
in (
4.96
), and applying it to
{\bf{v}}_{{\bf{x}}}\in l^{2}(A)
, where

{\bf{v}}_{{\bf{x}}}(\alpha)\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \widehat{\phi_{{\bf{x}},\xi}}(r_{\alpha}),\ \ \alpha\in A,

we obtain

(4.111)

G_{A}({\bf{v}}_{{\bf{x}}})\ =\ \sum_{\alpha\in A}\widehat{\phi_{{\bf{x}},\xi}}(r_{\alpha})r_{\alpha}\ +\ \mathfrak{p}_{A}({\bf{v}}_{{\bf{x}}}),

(4.112)

\|G_{A}({\bf{v}}_{{\bf{x}}})\|_{L^{\infty}}\ \leq\ \xi e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}\ \sqrt{2\mathfrak{K}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \|\mathfrak{p}_{A}({\bf{v}}_{{\bf{x}}}\|_{L^{2}}\ \leq\ \xi e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}\ \sqrt{\mathfrak{K}}.

Putting it all together, we have

(4.113)

\begin{split}g\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ h_{{\bf{x}},\xi}+G_{A}({\bf{v}}_{{\bf{x}}})\ &=\ U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}\ +\ \bigg(\mathfrak{p}_{A}({\bf{v}}_{{\bf{x}}})\ -\ \sum_{w\in W_{A}\setminus R_{A}}\widehat{\phi_{{\bf{x}},\xi}}(w)w\bigg)\\
\ &\overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \ U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}\ +\ \widetilde{\mathfrak{p}_{A}}({\bf{x}}),\end{split}

whence, from (
4.112
), (
4.110
), and (
4.107
),

(4.114)

\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\ \leq\ \xi+\xi e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}\ \sqrt{2\mathfrak{K}}\ =\ \mathfrak{e}^{-1}(\delta)+\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2}},

and

(4.115)

\|g-U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}\|_{L^{2}}\ \leq\ 2\xi e^{-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}\ \sqrt{\mathfrak{K}}\ =\delta.

∎

Corollary 4.17

(cf.

)
.

(4.116)

\sqrt{2}\ =\ \kappa\ \leq\ \beta_{R}(\delta)\ \leq\ \mathfrak{e}^{-1}(\delta)+\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2}},\ \ \ \ \delta\in(0,1).

Remark 4.18

, there exist

, such that for each set

is defined in (
4.99
). We refer to the property expressed by (

) as
\Lambda(2)
-
uniformizability

(

)
, and to

-

of Rademacher systems. Little is known about

). (Is the estimate sharp? Is

Remark 4.21

(ultra-interpolants)
.

In the next section we produce

, and
\big(l^{2}(A)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega_{A^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}})\big)
-interpolants
\Phi_{A}
(on

) that satisfy

(4.134)

{\bf{x}}\bm{\cdot}{\bf{y}}\ =\ \int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\Phi_{A}({\bf{x}})\ \Phi_{A}({\bf{y}})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}},\ \ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A),\ \ {\bf{y}}\in l^{2}(A),

and

(4.135)

\|\Phi_{A}({\bf{x}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\ \leq\ K\|{\bf{x}}\|_{2},\ \ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A),

where
K>1
does not depend on
A
. (See Theorem
5.4
and Corollary
5.5
.) Existence of
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})
-interpolants that are also
(l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty})
-representations upgrades both the Khintchin inequality and the Grothendieck inequality in the sense stated at the start of the section. We refer to such maps as
ultra-interpolants.

5.
The Grothendieck inequality: upgrades and extensions

5.1.
From Parseval to Grothendieck (via Khintchin)

An interpolant

(5.1)

G_{A}=U_{R_{A}}+\mathfrak{p}_{A^{\prime}}:\ l^{2}(A)\rightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega_{A^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}),\ \ \ \ A^{\prime}\supset A,

is an ultra-interpolant if and only if for all
{\bf{x}}
and
{\bf{y}}
in
l^{2}(A)
,

(5.2)

\widehat{\mathfrak{p}_{A^{\prime}}({\bf{x}})}\bm{\cdot}\widehat{\mathfrak{p}_{A^{\prime}}({\bf{y}})}\ =\ \int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{p}_{A^{\prime}}({\bf{x}})\ \mathfrak{p}_{A^{\prime}}({\bf{y}})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\ =\ 0.

Namely, the Parseval formula

(5.3)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}G_{A}({\bf{x}})\ G_{A}({\bf{y}})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\ &=\ \int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}U_{R_{A}}{\bf{x}}\ U_{R_{A}}{\bf{y}}\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\ +\ \int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{p}_{A^{\prime}}({\bf{x}})\ \mathfrak{p}_{A^{\prime}}({\bf{y}})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}},\\
\\
=\ {\bf{x}}\bm{\cdot}{\bf{y}}\ &+\ \sum_{w\in W_{A^{\prime}}\setminus R_{A}}\widehat{\mathfrak{p}_{A^{\prime}}({\bf{x}})}(w)\ \widehat{\mathfrak{p}_{A^{\prime}}({\bf{y}})}(w),\ \ \ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A),\ \ {\bf{y}}\in l^{2}(A),\end{split}

morphs into the Parseval-like formula in (
2.41
) precisely when (
5.2
) holds for all
{\bf{x}}
and
{\bf{y}}
in
l^{2}(A)
.
This simple observation is the motivation behind the recursive construction of the ultra-interpolants below.

To start, given an arbitrary set
A
, we initialize
A_{1}=A
, and generate from it a sequence of disjoint sets

(5.4)

A_{j+1}\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ W_{A_{j}}\setminus(R_{A_{j}}\cup\{r_{0}\}),\ \ \ \ j=1,\ldots.\

(We refer to
\big(A_{j}\big)_{j}
as a
set-cascade generated by
A
.) Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
, and let

\beta=\beta_{R}(\delta)<\infty

(Corollary
4.17
). For
j=1,\ldots,
select
\big(l^{2}(A_{j})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega_{A_{j}},\mathbb{P}_{A_{j}})\big)
-interpolants

(5.5)

G_{A_{j},\delta}=U_{R_{A_{j}}}+\mathfrak{p}_{A_{j},\delta},

such that for
{\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A_{j})
,

(5.6)

\|G_{A_{j},\delta}({\bf{x}})\|_{L^{\infty}}\ \leq\ \beta\|{\bf{x}}\|_{2}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \|\mathfrak{p}_{A_{j},\delta}({\bf{x}})\|_{L^{2}}\ \leq\ \delta\|{\bf{x}}\|_{2}\ .

We can assume

(5.7)

\int_{\Omega_{A_{j}}}G_{A_{j},\delta}({\bf{x}})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A_{j}}\ =\ 0,\ \ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A),\ \ j=1,\ldots\ .

Let

(5.8)

A^{\prime}\ =\ \bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}A_{j},

and consider the product space (a compact abelian group)

(5.9)

\Omega_{A^{\prime}}\ =\ \bigtimes_{j=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{A_{j}},

with the uniform probability measure on it (normalized Haar measure)

(5.10)

\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\ =\ \bigtimes_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{A_{j}}.

We take the independent projections

(5.11)

\pi_{A_{j}}:\ \Omega_{A^{\prime}}\ \rightarrow\ \Omega_{A_{j}},\ \ \ j=1,\ldots,

given by

(5.12)

\pi_{A_{j}}(\bm{\omega})\ =\ \big(\bm{\omega}(\alpha)\big)_{\alpha\in A_{j}},\ \ \ \ \ \bm{\omega}\in\{-1,+1\}^{A^{\prime}},

and view the
G_{A_{j},\delta}
as independent
\big(l^{2}(A_{j})\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega_{A^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}})\big)
-interpolants
G_{j,\delta}
, where

(5.13)

G_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}})\ =\ G_{A_{j},\delta}({\bf{x}})\circ\pi_{A_{j}}\ =\ U_{R_{A_{j}}}{\bf{x}}\ +\ \mathfrak{p}_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}}),\ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A_{j}),\ \ j=1,\ldots\ ,

and

(5.14)

\mathfrak{p}_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}})\ =\ \mathfrak{p}_{A_{j},\delta}({\bf{x}})\circ\pi_{A_{j}}.

For
j=1,\ldots,
and
{\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A_{j})
,

(5.15)

\widehat{G_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}})}(r_{\alpha})\ =\ {\bf{x}}(\alpha),\ \ \ \ \alpha\in A_{j},

and

(5.16)

\big\{w\in W_{A^{\prime}}:\widehat{G_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}})}(w)\ \neq\ 0\big\}\ \subset W_{A_{j}}.

In particular, for
j_{1}\neq j_{2},\ \ {\bf{x}}_{1}\in l^{2}(A_{j_{1}}),\ {\bf{x}}_{2}\in l^{2}(A_{j_{2}})
,

(5.17)

\int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}G_{j_{1},\delta}({\bf{x}}_{1})\ G_{j_{2},\delta}({\bf{x}}_{2})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\ =\ 0.

Next, for arbitrary
{\bf{x}}\in{l^{2}(A)}
, we initialize
{\bf{x}}^{(1)}={\bf{x}}
, and generate a sequence of vectors
{\bf{x}}^{(j)}\in l^{2}(A_{j}),
defined recursively by

(5.18)

{\bf{x}}^{(j+1)}(\alpha)\ =\ \mathfrak{p}_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(j)})^{\wedge}(\alpha),\ \ \ \ \alpha\in A_{j+1},\ \ j=1,\ldots\ .

(We refer to
({\bf{x}}^{(j)})_{j}
as a
vector-cascade
generated by
{\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A)
.)

Lemma 5.1
.

For
{\bf{x}}\in{l^{2}(A)}
,

(5.19)

\|{\bf{x}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\ \leq\ \delta^{j-1}\ \|{\bf{x}}\|_{2},\ \ \ \ j=1,\ldots\ .

Proof (by induction on
j
).

The case
j=1
is the initial assignment
{\bf{x}}^{(1)}={\bf{x}}
. For
j\geq 1
,

(5.20)

\|{\bf{x}}^{(j+1)}\|_{2}\ =\ \|\mathfrak{p}_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(j)})\|_{L^{2}}\ \leq\ \delta\|{\bf{x}}^{(j)}\|_{2}\leq\ \delta\cdot\delta^{j-1}\ \|{\bf{x}}\|_{2},\\
\\

where the equality on the left follows from (
5.18
), the first inequality from (
5.6
), and the second from the induction hypothesis.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

For
{\bf{x}}
and
{\bf{y}}
in
{l^{2}(A)}
,

(5.21)

\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{n}&(-1)^{j-1}\ \int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}G_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(j)})\ G_{j,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(j)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\\
\\
&=\ {\bf{x}}\bm{\cdot}{\bf{y}}\ +(-1)^{n-1}\ \int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{p}_{n,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(n)})\ \mathfrak{p}_{n,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(n)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}},\ \ \ \ \ \ n=1,\ldots\ .\end{split}

Proof (by induction on
n
).

The case
n=1
is (
5.3
). For
n>1,

(5.22)

\begin{split}&\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j-1}\int_{\Omega_{A}^{\prime}}G_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(j)})\ G_{j,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(j)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\ =\ {\bf{x}}\bm{\cdot}{\bf{y}}\\
\\
&+(-1)^{n-2}\int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{p}_{n-1,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(n-1)})\ \mathfrak{p}_{n-1,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(n-1)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A}\ +\ (-1)^{n-1}\int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}G_{n,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(n)})\ G_{n,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(n)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\\
\\
&\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(induction hypothesis}).\ \end{split}

By (
5.3
), (
5.4
), and (
5.18
) (via Parseval),

(5.23)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}&G_{n,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(n)})\ G_{n,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(n)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\ =\ {\bf{x}}^{(n)}\bm{\cdot}{\bf{y}}^{(n)}\ +\ \int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{p}_{n,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(n)})\ \mathfrak{p}_{n,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(n)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A}\\
\\
&=\ \int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{p}_{n-1,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(n-1)})\ \mathfrak{p}_{n-1,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(n-1)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\ +\ \int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{p}_{n,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(n)})\ \mathfrak{p}_{n,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(n)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}},\end{split}

which, put in (
5.22
), implies (
5.21
).

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

For
{\bf{x}}\in{l^{2}(A)}
and
{\bf{y}}\in{l^{2}(A)}
,

(5.24)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\big|G_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(j)})\ G_{j,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(j)})\big|\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\ \leq\ \frac{\beta^{2}}{1-\delta^{2}}\ \|{\bf{x}}\|_{2}\|{\bf{y}}\|_{2},

and

(5.25)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{j-1}\ \int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}G_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(j)})\ G_{j,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(j)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\ =\ {\bf{x}}\bm{\cdot}{\bf{y}}.

Proof.

By Hölder, Cauchy-Schwarz, (
5.6
), and (
5.19
),

(5.26)

\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\big|G_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(j)})\ G_{j,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(j)})\big|\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\big|\ &\leq\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\ \|G_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(j)})\|_{L^{\infty}}\ \|G_{j,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(j)})\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
\\
&\leq\ \beta^{2}\ \|{\bf{x}}\|_{2}\ \|{\bf{y}}\|_{2}\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\ \delta^{2(j-1)},\end{split}

and

(5.27)

\begin{split}\bigg|\int_{\Omega_{A^{\prime}}}\mathfrak{p}_{n,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(n)})\ \mathfrak{p}_{n,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(n)})\ d\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}}\bigg|\ &\leq\ \|\mathfrak{p}_{n,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(n)})\|_{L^{2}}\ \|\mathfrak{p}_{n,\delta}({\bf{y}}^{(n)})\|_{L^{2}}\\
\\
&\leq\ \delta^{2}\ \|{\bf{x}}^{(n)}\|_{2}\ \|{\bf{y}}^{(n)}\|_{2}\\
\\
&\leq\ \delta^{2n}\ \|{\bf{x}}\|_{2}\ \|{\bf{y}}\|_{2}\ .\\
\end{split}

The lemma follows by letting

in (

) and (
5.21
).

Theorem 5.4
.

For

and set
A
,

(5.28)

\Phi_{A,\delta}({\bf{x}})\ \overset{\text{def}}{=}\ \ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\ \mathfrak{i}^{j-1}\ G_{j,\delta}({\bf{x}}^{(j)}),\ \ \ \ \ {\bf{x}}\in l^{2}(A)\ \ \ \ (\mathfrak{i}=\sqrt{-1}),

converges absolutely in
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{A^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}})
and uniformly on bounded subsets of
l^{2}(A)
. Moreover,
\Phi_{A,\delta}
is a
\big(l^{2}(A)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega_{A^{\prime}},\mathbb{P}_{A^{\prime}})\big)
-ultra-interpolant on
R_{A}
,
and

(5.29)

\|\Phi_{A,\delta}\|_{l^{2}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}}\ \leq\ \frac{\beta_{R}(\delta)}{1-\delta}.

Proof.

By (
5.6
) and (
5.19

5.2.

For a set
A

-series formally given by (

&\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ w\in W_{A,k},\ \ w=r_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots r_{\alpha_{k}},\ \ k=1,2,\ldots\ .\\
\end{split}

3
. For

\begin{split}\big\|(\mathfrak{R}_{A}({\bf{x}})\big)^{\wedge}\big\|_{p}^{p}\ &=\ 1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\ \sum_{\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\}\subset A}|{\bf{x}}(\alpha_{1})\cdots{\bf{x}}(\alpha_{k})|^{p},\\

i

whence

&\leq\ \epsilon^{2}\|{\bf{x}}\|_{2},\ \ \ \ \ 0<\epsilon<1.\end{split}

\|Q_{A,\epsilon}({\bf{x}})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\big(\frac{e^{\epsilon^{2}/2}}{\epsilon}\big)\|{\bf{x}}\|_{2}\ .
