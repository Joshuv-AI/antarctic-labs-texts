Information Leakage Detection through Approximate Bayes-optimal Prediction Journal: Information Sciences
Pritha Gupta, Marcel Wever, Eyke Hüllermeier

Abstract
In today’s data-driven world, the proliferation of publicly available information raises security concerns due to the IL ( IL ) problem. IL involves unintentionally exposing sensitive information to unauthorized parties via observable system information. Conventional statistical approaches rely on estimating MI ( MI ) between observable and secret information for detecting IL , face challenges of the curse of dimensionality, convergence, computational complexity, and MI misestimation. Though effective, emerging supervised ML based approaches to detect IL are limited to binary system sensitive information and lack a comprehensive framework. To address these limitations, we establish a theoretical framework using statistical learning theory and information theory to quantify and detect IL accurately. Using AutoML , we demonstrate that MI can be accurately estimated by approximating the typically unknown Bayes predictor’s Log-Loss and accuracy. Based on this, we show how MI can effectively be estimated to detect IL . Our method performs superior to state-of-the-art baselines in an empirical study considering synthetic and real-world OpenSSL TLS server datasets.

2.2.1
Mutual Information

MI
(
MI
) measures the extent to which knowledge of one random variable informs about another, quantifying their dependence degree
[
12
]

Consider a pair of random variables

and
Y
with joint distribution
p_{(X,Y)}(\cdot)
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.
We assume that
X
is a continuous
d

\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
), and
Y
is a discrete random variable with
C
possible values—as in the
IL
scenario relevant to us
[
10
]
.
Let the measure
P
induces a marginal
PDF
(
PDF
) on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
denoted by
p_{X}(\cdot)
and
p_{Y}(\cdot)
of the joint distribution
p_{(X,Y)}(\cdot)
, which induces the conditional distributions
p_{{Y}|{X}}(\cdot)
and
p_{{X}|{Y}}(\cdot)
.

The entropy of a discrete random variable
Y
is defined as

H(Y)=-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p_{Y}(y)\log(p_{Y}(y))\,,

(1)

where
0\log(0)=0
by definition.
It reaches the maximum value of
\log(C)
when outcomes are equally likely (
p_{Y}(y)=1/C,\forall y\in[C]
), indicating complete uncertainty of the outcome.
The minimum value of
0
occurs in the case of a Dirac measure when only one outcome is certain, i.e.,
p_{Y}(y)=0,\forall y\in[C]\setminus{c},\,p_{Y}(c)=1
.

The conditional entropy of
Y
given
X
is defined as

H({Y}\,|\,{X})=-\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p_{{Y}|{X}}({y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})\log(p_{{Y}|{X}}({y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}}))\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,.

Conditional entropy measures the residual uncertainty in one random variable
Y
given knowledge of the other
X
— more specifically, it measures the
expected
residual uncertainty, with the expectation taken with respect to the marginal distribution of
Y
.
It reaches its maximum value of
H(Y)
, when
X
does not inform about
Y
, i.e.,
H({Y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})=p_{Y}(\cdot),\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, and the minimum entropy of
0
occurs when
X
completely determines
Y
and again all conditionals
p_{{Y}|{X}}({\cdot}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})
are Dirac distributions.

MI
measures the reduction of uncertainty about variable
Y
by observing variable
X
:

I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H({Y}\,|\,{X})\,.

(2)

Plugging in the expressions for (conditional) entropy and rearranging terms shows that
MI
equals the
KL
(
KL
) divergence of the joint distribution
p_{(X,Y)}(\cdot)
from the product of the marginals (i.e., the joint distribution under the assumption of independence):

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=H(Y)-H({Y}\,|\,{X})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}{p_{(X,Y)}(\boldsymbol{x},y)\log{\left({\frac{p_{(X,Y)}(\boldsymbol{x},y)}{p_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot p_{Y}(y)}}\right)}}\,d\boldsymbol{x}\,.

(3)

MI
is a symmetric measure ranging from
0
to
\min(\{H(X),H(Y)\})
, where
0
indicates independence and the maximum value signifies full dependence
[
12
]
.
In this work,
MI
is measured in
bits
using base-2 logarithms (
\log(\cdot)
).

2.2.2
Classification Problem

In the realm of classification, the learning algorithm (learner) is provided with a training dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
of size
N\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the input (instance) space and
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,2,\ldots,C\}=[C],\,C\in\mathbb{N}
the output (categorical classes) space
[
60
]
, and the
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})
are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) according to
p_{(X,Y)}(\cdot)
.
The primary goal of the learner in standard classification is to induce a hypothesis
h\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y},\,h\in\mathcal{H}
, with low generalization error (risk)

R(h)=\mathbb{E}[\ell(y,h(\boldsymbol{x}))]=\int\limits_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\ell(y,h(\boldsymbol{x}))\,d\,p_{(X,Y)}(\boldsymbol{x},y)\,,

(4)

where
\mathcal{H}
is the underlying hypothesis space,
\ell\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a loss function, and
p_{(X,Y)}(\cdot)
is the joint probability measure modeling the underlying data-generating process.
The risk minimizer defined as
h^{*}=\operatorname*{\arg\,\min}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R(h)
, achieves the minimum expected loss
\mathbb{E}[\ell(\cdot)]
in terms of the loss function
\ell
across the entire joint distribution
p_{(X,Y)}(\cdot)
.
The
0\text{-}1
loss defined as
\ell\_{01}(y,\hat{y})\coloneq\llbracket\hat{y}\neq y\rrbracket
, is commonly used in standard classification.
In practice, the
p_{(X,Y)}(\boldsymbol{x},y)
is not directly observed by the learner, so minimizing the risk (
4
) is not feasible.
Instead, learning in a standard classification setting is commonly accomplished by minimizing (a regularized version of)
empirical risk
for
h
:

R_{\text{emp}}(h)=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(y_{i},h(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))\,.

(5)

In the subsequent discussions, we denote by
g=\operatorname*{\arg\,\min}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R_{\text{emp}}(h)
the (learned) hypothesis that minimizes (
5
), i.e., the empirical risk minimizer, which in principle is the best possible approximation (empirical estimation)
h^{*}
and is an approximation thereof
[
60
]
.

Probabilistic Classification

Distinct from standard classifiers, probabilistic classifiers focus on estimating the (conditional) class probabilities
p_{{Y}|{X}}({y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})
for each class

in
\mathcal{Y}
, for a given input instance

We denote predictions of that kind by
\hat{p}_{{Y}|{X}}({y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})

\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}

Now, however, instead of comparing a predicted class

with a true class

, the loss

compares a predicted probability distribution with

The most commonly used loss function

[
6

The

[
25
]

Since
\mathcal{Y}
is finite and consists of

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Y})
can be represented by the

-simplex, i.e., predictions can be represented as probability vectors

, where
\hat{p}_{c}=\boldsymbol{\hat{p}}[c]=\hat{p}_{{Y}|{X}}({c}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})
is the probability assigned to class

.
Typically, learning predictors of that kind involve minimizing the
empirical risk

R_{{\text{emp}}|{p}}(h_{p})=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{p}(y_{i},h_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))\,.

(6)

In the subsequent discussions, we denote the empirical risk minimizer by
g_{p}=\operatorname*{\arg\,\min}_{h_{p}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}}R_{{\text{emp}}|{p}}(h_{p})

[
60
,
6
, chap. 4]
.
In the case where

) is again the best constant probability predictor, i.e., the one with the lowest risk (

) among all constant predictors.
Obviously, a probabilistic prediction can be used deterministically by selecting the class with the highest predicted probability:
\hat{y}=\operatorname*{\arg\,\max}_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\hat{p}_{{Y}|{X}}({y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})
to induce a deterministic classifier

This approach minimizes the standard

[

]

2.2.3
Bayes-optimal Predictor

In statistical learning theory, the
Bayes predictor
is the optimal classification function
g^{\textnormal{b}}\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, which minimizes expected risk (
4
) for a given loss function
\ell(\cdot)\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle g^{\textnormal{b}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{\arg\,\min}_{\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\ell(y,\hat{y})\,\cdot p_{{Y}|{X}}({y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})=\operatorname*{\arg\,\min}_{\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{y}[{\ell(y,\hat{y})}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}}]\,,

where
\mathbb{E}_{y}[\ell]
is the expected loss of prediction
\hat{y}
for
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and
p_{{Y}|{X}}({y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})
is the (conditional) class
y
probability for input
\boldsymbol{x}

[
15
]
.
For

loss, it simplifies to:

g^{\textnormal{bc}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\operatorname*{\arg\,\max}_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p_{{Y}|{X}}({y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})\,.

(7)

It produces the minimum expected loss, known as

, denoted by
\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})}

When

and

g^{\textnormal{bc}}(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv g^{\textnormal{mc}}(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv\operatorname*{\arg\,\max}_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p_{Y}(y)\,.

(8)

It assigns to each input
\boldsymbol{x}
a class label from the set of labels with the highest marginal probability, as input features are completely uninformative.
Strictly speaking, as the maximum in (
8
) is not necessarily unique, the marginal Bayes predictor may pick any label with the highest probability —  in that case, we nevertheless assume that it picks the same label for every
\boldsymbol{x}
, so that it is a constant function.

3.1.1
Mid-point Estimation

The

approach estimates

by leveraging the relationship between Bayes error rate

and the conditional entropy

for a classification task

]

The

The conditional entropy

is bounded as

\displaystyle H_{l}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})},C)\leq H({Y}\,|\,{X})\leq H_{u}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})},C)\,

\displaystyle H_{l}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})},C)=\log(c)+c(c+1)\left(\log\left(\frac{c+1}{c}\right)\right)\left(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})}-\frac{c-1}{c}\right)

\displaystyle H_{u}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})},C)=H_{2}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})})+\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})}\cdot\log(C-1)\,,

where

derived by
Hellman and Raviv 1970
is valid for
\frac{1}{c+1}\leq 1-\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})}\leq\frac{1}{c}\,,c=1,\ldots,C-1
and

H_{2}(a)=-a\log(a)-(1-a)\log(1-a)
.

Plugging in

and

in (
2

the lower bound

and the upper bound

\displaystyle H(Y)-H_{u}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})},C)\leq I(X;Y)\leq H(Y)-H_{l}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})},C)

\displaystyle F_{l}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})},C)\leq I(X;Y)\leq F_{u}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(,)}{g^{\textnormal{bc}}},C).

MI
is estimated as

\widehat{I}(X;Y)\approxeq\frac{F_{u}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g)},C)+F_{l}(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g)},C)}{2}\,,

(9)

where
g
is the
empirical risk minimizer
of (
5
) and serves as a proxy of the Bayes predictor, as
\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g^{\textnormal{bc}})}\approxeq\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}{(g)}
.

3.1.2
Log-Loss
Estimation

We propose the
Log-Loss
approach for MI estimation to address the overestimation and false positives of the
Mid-Point
approach and the false negatives of baseline
ILD
approaches.
This approach uses the empirical risk minimizer
g_{p}
minimizing (
6
) to approximate the log-loss of the Bayes predictor.

The
MI
between
X
and
Y
in (
2
) is defined as

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=-H({Y}\,|\,{X})+H(Y)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}p_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p_{{Y}|{X}}({y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})\log\left(p_{{Y}|{X}}({y}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)d\boldsymbol{x}-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p_{Y}(y)\log{\left(p_{Y}(y)\right)}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}[\ell_{ll}(g^{\textnormal{bc}})]+\mathbb{E}[\ell_{ll}(g^{\textnormal{mc}})]\approxeq-\mathbb{E}[\ell_{ll}(g_{p})]+\mathbb{E}[\ell_{ll}(g_{p}^{\textnormal{mc}})]\,,

where
\mathbb{E}[\ell_{ll}(\cdot)]
represents the expected log-loss.
The conditional entropy
H({Y}\,|\,{X})
equals the expected log-loss of the Bayes predictor
g^{\textnormal{bc}}
, while the entropy of
Y
corresponds to the expected log-loss of the marginal Bayes predictor
g^{\textnormal{mc}}
, reaffirming
MI
as a special case of
LAS
(c.f.
Section

2.3
).

Log-loss

For probabilistic classifiers
g_{p}
,
CCE
is used to obtain estimated conditional class probabilities, forming a vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{p}}=(\hat{p}_{1},\ldots,\hat{p}_{C})
for each input
\boldsymbol{x}
.
The log-loss for
g_{p}
is defined in
Bishop 2006
as

\ell_{ll}(y,\boldsymbol{\hat{p}})=-\sum_{c=1}^{C}\hat{p}_{c}\log(\hat{p}_{c})=-\sum_{c=1}^{C}g_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})[c]\log(g_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})[c])\,.

It reaches its minimum value of
0
when one class dominates, i.e.,
\hat{p}_{c}\approxeq 1
and
\hat{p}_{j}\approxeq 0
for all
j\in[C]\setminus\{c\}
, and its maximum
\log(C)
when class probabilities are uniform, i.e.,
\hat{p}_{c}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{C}}
for all
c\in[C]
, implying that high classification uncertainty increases the log-loss, making it suitable for estimating the conditional entropy
H({Y}\,|\,{X})
.

Marginal Bayes predictor

The marginal Bayes predictor, denoted by
g_{p}^{\textnormal{mc}}
, is estimated from the class distribution in the dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
using (
6
).
The class probabilities for each input
\boldsymbol{x}
are
g_{p}^{\textnormal{mc}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{\hat{p}}_{mc}=(\hat{p}_{1},\ldots,\hat{p}_{C})
, where
\hat{p}_{c}=\tfrac{\lvert\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}\;\lvert\;y_{i}=c\}\rvert}{\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert}
is the fraction of instances for class
c
.
The log-loss of the marginal Bayes predictor suitably estimates the entropy of
Y
, i.e.,
H(Y)\approxeq\ell_{ll}(y,\boldsymbol{\hat{p}}_{mc})
, ranging from
0
to
\log(C)
, with the maximum acquired for a balanced dataset.

MI
estimation

We approximate the expected log-loss of the Bayes predictor and marginal Bayes predictor by evaluating the log-loss of
g_{p}
and
g_{p}^{\textnormal{mc}}
for the dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell_{ll}(g_{p})]=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{ll}(y_{i},g_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))=-\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C}g_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})[c]\log(g_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})[c])

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell_{ll}(g_{p}^{\textnormal{mc}})]=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{ll}(y_{i},g_{p}^{\textnormal{mc}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}))=-\sum_{c=1}^{C}\boldsymbol{\hat{p}}_{mc}[c]\log(\boldsymbol{\hat{p}}_{mc}[c]).

The
MI
is then estimated as

\widehat{I}(X;Y)\approxeq\sum_{c=1}^{C}\left(\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})[c]\log(g_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})[c])-\boldsymbol{\hat{p}}_{mc}[c]\log(\boldsymbol{\hat{p}}_{mc}[c])\right).

(10)

Classifier Calibration

Despite the sound theoretical grounding of the proper scoring rule loss function,
CCE
often yields poorly calibrated probabilities in practice, with neural networks tending to be overconfident, especially on imbalanced datasets
[
55
]
.
Post-hoc
calibration
techniques address this by learning mappings from predicted to improved probabilities using validation data
[
54
]
.
To enhance
Log-Loss
estimation accuracy, we employ calibration techniques like Isotonic Regression (
IR Cal Log-Loss
)
[
2
]
, Platt’s Scaling (
PS Cal Log-Loss
)
[
49
]
, Beta Calibration (
Beta Cal Log-Loss
)
[
36
]
, Temperature Scaling (
TS Cal Log-Loss
)
[
26
]
, and Histogram Binning (
HB Cal Log-Loss
)
[
61
]
, referred collectively as
Cal Log-Loss
.
These calibration techniques are pivotal for enhancing
Log-Loss
, and enabling accurate
MI
estimation—thereby avoiding the false positives often observed with the
Mid-Point
approach.

-based and classification-based methods.
We propose to use the
AutoML
tools, AutoGluon and TabPFN, described in
A.2.2
, for an accurate estimation of empirical risk minimizers (
g
or
g_{p}
) to serve as an appropriate proxy for Bayes predictor.
We propose to apply statistical tests on the
LAS
, calculated using
Log-Loss
, accuracy, and
CM
(
CM
) of the top-performing
AutoML
models or pipelines (
g
or
g_{p}
), to obtain a
p
-value.
All statistical tests in our setup follow a common framework, where the null hypothesis
H_{0}(\cdot)
assumes no
IL
, and the alternative
H_{1}(\cdot)
indicates its presence.
A
p
-value below the significance level
\alpha
leads to rejecting
H_{0}
, thereby inferring the presence of
IL
.
The threshold
\alpha=0.01
is particularly appropriate for avoiding false positives using the Holm-Bonferroni correction when testing multiple hypotheses to ensure high-confidence detection in security-sensitive settings
[
27
,
31
]
.
We propose to use
\alpha=0.01
for the Holm-Bonferroni procedure, tuned with the cutoff parameter
\tau=5
from our prior work
[
27
]
.

MI
-based Approach

The
MI
-based
ILD
approach is based on the condition that
IL
occurs in the system if
MI
or
LAS
with log-loss must be significantly greater than
0
, i.e.,
\delta(\ell_{ll})\gg 0
, as per
Section

2.3
.
To archive this, we propose to apply the
OTT
(
OTT
) on
10

MI
estimates obtained using
KFCV
(
KFCV
) on the
Log-Loss
and
Mid-Point
techniques on top-
j
th performing pipeline, as well as baseline methods, denoted by vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{I}}_{j}
.
The
OTT
provides a
p
-value representing the probability of observing the sample mean to be around the actual mean (
0
in our case)
[
14
]
.
The null hypothesis
H_{0}(\boldsymbol{\hat{I}}_{j}\sim 0)
implies absence of
IL
in a system.

Classification-based Approaches

Our prior work
Gupta et al. 2022
proposes classification-based
ILD
approaches based on the condition that
IL
occurs if the Bayes predictor accuracy significantly surpasses that of a marginal Bayes predictor, i.e.,
LAS
is significantly greater than
0
, as per
Section

2.3
.

PTT
-based Approach

PTT
is used to compare two samples (generated from an underlying population) where the observations in one sample can be paired with observations in the other sample
[
14
]
.
The approach tests the condition that
LAS
is significantly greater than
0
for
IL
to exist in a system, i.e.,
\delta(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{ACC}}})\gg 0
.
The
PTT-Majority
approach achieves this by applying
PTT
between the
10
accuracy estimates from
KFCV
for the
j
-th best performing
AutoML
pipeline (
g
minimizing (
5
), proxy of Bayes predictor) and of
g_{p}^{\textnormal{mc}}
(proxy of marginal Bayes predictor c.f.
Section

3.1.2
), denoted by
\boldsymbol{a}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{mc}
, respectively.
We propose to use the corrected version of
PTT
, which considers the dependency in estimates due to
KFCV
, to provide an unbiased estimate of the
p
-value
[
45
]
.
The
p
-value represents the probability of obtaining our observed mean accuracy difference, assuming the null hypothesis
H_{0}(\cdot)
holds, implying that accuracies are drawn from the same distribution or have nearly zero average difference
[
14
]
.
The null hypothesis is
H_{0}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j}=\boldsymbol{a}_{mc})
, which implies no
IL
and the alternate hypothesis
H_{1}(\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\neq\boldsymbol{a}_{mc})
implies presence of
IL
in the system.
However,
PTT
assumes asymptotic behavior and normal distribution of accuracy differences, which can lead to optimistic
p
-values, and using accuracy can misestimate results on imbalanced datasets
[
50
,
47
]
.

FET
-based Approach

FET
is a non-parametric test used to determine the probability of independence (or non-dependence) between two classification methods, in this case, classifying instances based on ground truths
\boldsymbol{y}
and classifying them based on
AutoML
predictions
\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}

[
21
]
.
The
FET
-based approach addresses the class imbalance issue and improves
p
-value estimation by applying the
FET
on
K

CM

\mathcal{M}_{j}=\{\boldsymbol{M}_{j}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
obtained using
KFCV
to detect
IL
.
If a strong correlation exists between inputs
\boldsymbol{x}
and outputs
y
in
\mathcal{D}
, the
AutoML
pipeline’s prediction
\hat{y}=g(\boldsymbol{x})
encapsulates input information, and
FET
can asses
IL
using the
CM
(analyzing
LAS

\delta(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{CM}}})
), which contains relevant information for predicting correct outputs (
\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{tp}}},\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{tn}}}
).
The
p
-value represents the probability of independence between the ground truth
\boldsymbol{y}
and
AutoML
predictions
\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}
defined by the null hypothesis (
H_{0}(\cdot)
).
The null hypothesis
H_{0}(p({\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}}|{\boldsymbol{M}})=p({\boldsymbol{y}}|{\boldsymbol{M}})p({\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}}|{\boldsymbol{M}}))
posits that
\boldsymbol{y}
and
\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}
are independent, indicating no
IL
, while the alternative hypothesis

H_{1}(p({\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{M}})\neq p({\boldsymbol{y}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{M}})p({\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{M}}))
suggests significant dependence, implying
IL
.

The

implicitly accounts for dataset imbalance by testing

using
MCC
(

\delta(\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{MCC}}})\gg 0
).
The

\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{fp}}}
and
\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{fn}}}

[
11
]

statistic, i.e.,
\lvert\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{MCC}}}\rvert=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{\chi^{2}}}{{N}}}

[
9
]
.
Since the
\chi^{2}
test is asymptotically equivalent to
FET
, this supports using
FET
to evaluate the learnability of
g
(a proxy for the Bayes predictor) via the
CM
, while accounting for class imbalance and yielding accurate
p
-values for detecting
IL
.
We aggregate the
10

p
-values obtained using
FET
on
K

CM
through
median
and
mean
, referred to as the
FET-Median
and
FET-Mean
approaches
[
4
]
.

5
Conclusion

This paper presents a comprehensive framework for
ILD
, leveraging information theory and statistical learning theory concepts to enhance cybersecurity by identifying vulnerabilities.
We introduced two techniques for quantifying and detecting
IL
by estimating
MI
between a system’s observable and secret information, using the Bayes predictor’s
Log-Loss
and accuracy.
Our methods employ two powerful
AutoML
tools, TabPFN and AutoGluon, for efficient
MI
estimation, offering an automated alternative to traditional statistical techniques or deep
MLP
.
We propose applying the
OTT
on the estimated
MI
, enhancing robustness with Holm-Bonferroni correction and boosting confidence in
IL
decisions, as established in our prior work
[
27
]
.

Our empirical results show that our approach, mainly using TabPFN for calibrated
Log-Loss
approximation, effectively estimates
MI
and detects timing side-channel leaks in OpenSSL TLS servers, outperforming state-of-the-art methods.
The key contributions of our work include a comprehensive
ILD
framework, addressing imbalance with minimal false positives, robustness to noise in generated system datasets, and an adaptable, scalable
IL
detection solution for real-world scenarios.
Furthermore, our work concludes that the choice and requirement of calibration techniques for
Log-Loss
estimation depend on the characteristics of the system datasets.

In future work, we aim to use attention-based transformer embeddings to reduce high-dimensional network traces into compact, semantically meaningful representations, enabling the effective quantification and detection of
IL
while addressing TabPFN’s input limitations
[
37
]
.
The new version of AutoGluon supports advanced search algorithms such as Hyperband, which can be explored to accelerate the overall process of finding an optimal model using evaluation-efficient successive halving
[
17
]
.
As TabPFN is now included in the model pool of the AutoGluon lists in
Table

5
, a promising future direction is to integrate it with suitable feature selection and reduction techniques to develop an end-to-end
AutoML
pipeline capable of handling high-dimensional and multi-class datasets with more than
10
classes (
C>10
)
[
30
,
17
]
.
This tool will incorporate dimensionality reduction and calibration methods to accurately estimate
MI
and detect
IL
, thereby addressing the current scalability constraints of TabPFN using the new AutoGluon
[
30
,
52
]
.
Improving the
Mid-Point
approach to account for dataset imbalance and extending the
BER
and
MI
relationship by
Zhao et al. 2013
for multiple classes is an essential direction for future work as well.
We also plan to extend our experiments to finer-grained imbalance levels and time-delay variations to evaluate robustness and scalability further.
Another future direction could be to integrate the fast Westfall–Young permutation procedure to improve statistical power and empirically adjust the Holm-Bonferroni cut-off thresholds to a widely accepted significance level of
\alpha=0.05

[
57
,
14
,

A.1
Simulating Synthetic Systems

We use the
MVN
distribution, which is ideal for simulating real-world vulnerable and non-vulnerable systems generating classification datasets, providing a straightforward means of obtaining ground truth
MI
, as described in
A.1.3
.
The
MVN
distribution is widely used for benchmarking classifiers due to its simplicity, ability to model inter-variable correlations, and well-established statistical properties
[
6
, chap. 2]
.
Its use is further supported by the central limit theorem, which justifies normality in aggregated data
[
6
, chap. 4]
. We simulate both balanced and imbalanced datasets with varying
LAS
or
MI
levels using
MVN
perturbation and proximity techniques.

A.1.1
Generation Method

Synthetic datasets are generated using
MVN
to define the joint
PDF
(
p_{(X,Y)}
) between
X
and
Y
, inducing their marginals on
X
and
Y
.
The dataset
\mathcal{D}
is created by sampling instances from
p_{{X}|{Y}}(\cdot)
with class distribution
p_{Y}(y)
, as illustrated in
Figure

2
.

Formal Definition of
PDF

We define the joint distribution
p_{(X,Y)}(\cdot)
and marginal on
Y

p_{X}(\cdot)
, required to generate the dataset
\mathcal{D}
by sampling
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\sim p_{{X}|{Y}}({\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}\,|\,{y_{i}}),\forall i\in[N]
with class distribution defined by
p_{Y}(y)
.
These
PDF
are used to define the conditionals
p_{{Y}|{X}}(\cdot)
and
p_{{X}|{Y}}(\cdot)
and induce the marginal on
\mathcal{X}
, denoted by
p_{X}(\cdot)
.
The conditional
PDF
on
X
given
Y
is defined for class
c
as

\displaystyle p_{{X}|{Y}}({\boldsymbol{x}}\,|\,{c})=\textsc{MVN}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m},\Sigma)\,.

(12)

where
\Sigma
is the covariance matrix and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{m}
is the mean vector for class
c
.
The covariance matrix should be positive semi-definite and is typically sampled using eigenvalue decomposition
[
6
]
.

Generating Imbalanced Datasets: Marginal on
Y

The parameter
r
, referred to as
class imbalance
, is the minimum proportion of instances for any class
c\in[C]
, defined as
r=\min_{c\in[C]}\frac{\lvert\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}\;\lvert\;y_{i}=c\}\rvert}{\lvert\mathcal{D}\rvert}
.
For balanced datasets,
r=\nicefrac{{1}}{{C}}
, and for imbalanced ones,
r<\nicefrac{{1}}{{C}}
, i.e.,
r\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{C}}]
.
To generate imbalanced multi-class datasets (
C>2
), we introduce two methods:
Minority
and
Majority
, with an example class frequency in each case shown in
Figure

3
.
In the
Minority
method, the
minority class
is assigned
r
fraction of total data points, and remaining samples are uniformly distributed among other classes (
>\frac{N}{C}
).
While in the
Majority
method, all classes apart from the selected
majority class
is assigned
r
fraction of total data points (
<\frac{N}{C}
), and the
majority class
gets the remaining data points.
The marginal on
Y
for a balanced dataset with
r=\nicefrac{{1}}{{C}}
is defined as
p_{Y}(c)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{C}}
and for imbalanced datasets using
Minority
or
Majority
generation methods is defined as

\mathsf{g}_{r}=\begin{cases}\text{Majority}&\begin{cases}p_{Y}(c)=r,&\text{if }c\in[C]\setminus C\\
p_{Y}(c)=1-r\cdot(C-1),&\text{if }c=C\end{cases}\\
\hline\cr\text{Minority}&\begin{cases}p_{Y}(c)=\frac{1-r}{C-1},&\text{if }c\in[C]\setminus C\\
p_{Y}(c)=r,&\text{if }c=C\end{cases}\end{cases}

(13)

Using these, the joint distribution
p_{(X,Y)}(\boldsymbol{x},c)
for class
c
is defined as
p_{(X,Y)}(\boldsymbol{x},c)=p_{Y}(c)\cdot p_{{X}|{Y}}({\boldsymbol{x}}\,|\,{c})
, which induces a marginal on
X
as

p_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{c=1}^{C}p_{Y}(c)\cdot p_{{X}|{Y}}({\boldsymbol{x}}\,|\,{c})=\sum_{c=1}^{C}p_{Y}(c)\cdot\textsc{MVN}(\mu_{m},\Sigma)

(14)

Conditional on
Y
given
X

The conditional
p_{{Y}|{X}}(\cdot)
on
Y
given
X
for instance
(\boldsymbol{x},c)
is defined as

\displaystyle p_{{Y}|{X}}({c}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{Y}(c)\cdot p_{{X}|{Y}}({\boldsymbol{x}}\,|\,{c})}{\sum_{c=1}^{C}p_{Y}(c)\cdot p_{{X}|{Y}}({\boldsymbol{x}}\,|\,{c})}=\frac{p_{(X,Y)}(\boldsymbol{x},c)}{p_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,.

(15)

A.1.2
Introducing Noise (
\epsilon
)

To simulate real-world
IL
scenarios in cryptographic systems, we introduce noise, which decreases the certainty about output
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
with more observed inputs
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
, resulting in
H({Y}\,|\,{X})>0
and
I(X;Y)<H(Y)
.
We propose two methods: the
MVN
perturbation and proximity techniques.
The perturbation technique introduces noise by flipping a percentage of outputs or classes in the dataset.
In contrast, the proximity technique reduces the distance between mean vectors (
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}
) of each class, leading to overlap between the Gaussians
\textsc{MVN}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c},\Sigma)
.
These methods simulate scenarios where cryptographic systems leak no information (non-vulnerable) with
I(X;Y)=0
.

MVN
Perturbation Technique

This approach introduces noise by flipping a percentage, denoted by
\epsilon
, of class labels (
y\sim\mathcal{Y}
) in the dataset, simulating perturbed systems to generate classification datasets.
This technique modifies the conditional
PDF

p_{{Y}|{X}}(\cdot)
in (
15
) and
p_{{X}|{Y}}(\cdot)
in (
12
).

Output Variable
Y_{\epsilon}

Let random variables
B\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p=\epsilon,q=1-\epsilon)
and
\tilde{Y}\sim\text{Categorical}(p_{Y}(1),\ldots,p_{Y}(C))
are independent of
\{X,Y\}
, for a fixed
\epsilon
.
The modified output random variable
Y_{\epsilon}
is defined as
Y_{\epsilon}=Y\cdot\llbracket B=0\rrbracket+\llbracket B=1\rrbracket\cdot\tilde{Y}

The marginal distributions on
X
remain unchanged because only the class labels are flipped.
Accordingly, the modified marginal on
Y_{\epsilon}
is derived as
p_{Y_{\epsilon}}(Y_{\epsilon}=y)=\epsilon\cdot p_{Y}(Y=y)+(1-\epsilon)\cdot p_{Y}(Y=y)=p_{Y}(Y=y)
.
So, the marginal on
Y_{\epsilon}
is the same as that of
Y
.

Conditional on
Y_{\epsilon}
given
X

The conditional on the modified output variable
Y_{\epsilon}
given
X
is

\displaystyle p_{{Y_{\epsilon}}|{X}}({Y_{\epsilon}=c}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=p_{B}(B=0)\cdot p_{{Y}|{X}}({c}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})+p_{B}(B=1)\cdot p_{\tilde{Y}}(\tilde{Y}=c)

(16)

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon)\cdot p_{{Y}|{X}}({c}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})+\epsilon\cdot p_{Y}(c)\,.

(17)

Conditional on
X
Given
Y_{\epsilon}

The conditional
p_{{X}|{Y_{\epsilon}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}\,|\,{Y_{\epsilon}=c})
combines multiple
MVN
components is defined as

p_{{X}|{Y_{\epsilon}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}\,|\,{Y_{\epsilon}=c})=\frac{p_{{Y_{\epsilon}}|{X}}({Y_{\epsilon}=c}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})p_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})}{p_{Y}(c)},.

This results in a more complex distribution, which is more difficult to approximate for performing
MI
estimation.

MVN
Proximity Technique

Our second approach introduces noise in the simulated systems by reducing the distance between the Gaussians generated by
MVN
distributions.
This is achieved by moving the mean vectors
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{c},\forall c\in[C]_{0}
corresponding to
MVN
distribution representing each class closer to each other.
The updated
MVN
for each class
c
is defined by
\textsc{MVN}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{c},\Sigma)
, which in turn represents the conditional
PDF

p_{{X}|{Y}}(\cdot)
for the underlying generated system dataset.
Notably, when introducing proximity, only the original
MVN
is modified, which only modifies the conditional on
X
given
Y
, i.e.,
p_{{X}|{Y}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{x}}\,|\,{c})=\textsc{MVN}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}_{c},\Sigma)
.
Consequently, the conditional
PDF

p_{{Y}|{X}}(\cdot)
and the marginal on
X
can be computed using (
15
) and (
) as

\displaystyle p_{{Y}|{X}}^{\prime}({c}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{Y}(c)\cdot p_{{X}|{Y}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{x}}\,|\,{c})}{\sum_{c=1}^{C}p_{Y}(c)\cdot p_{{X}|{Y}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{x}}\,|\,{c})}=\frac{p_{(X,Y)}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},c)}{p_{X}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,.

(18)

Therefore, the underlying distribution for synthetic datasets generated through the introduction of noise using the proximity approach remains the same as for the datasets using the
MVN
distribution with updated means.

A.1.3
Ground truth
MI

By plugging in the conditional
p_{{Y}|{X}}(\cdot)
and the marginal
p_{Y}(\cdot)
defined above in (
2
), the ground truth
MI
for the generated system dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i}\}
is approximated as

GI(\mathcal{D})\approxeq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{{Y}|{X}}({y_{i}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}})\log\left(p_{{Y}|{X}}({y_{i}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}})\right)-\sum_{c=1}^{C}p_{Y}(c)\log\left(p_{Y}(c)\right)\,.

(19)

For systems simulated using perturbation and proximity techniques, the modified conditional
p_{{Y_{\epsilon}}|{X}}(\cdot)
in (
17
) and
p_{{Y}|{X}}^{\prime}({c}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{x}})
in (
18
) and unchanged marginal on
Y
is used to calculate the ground truth
MI
.

Performance Evaluation Metrics

Koyejo et al. 2015
define evaluation measures used for evaluating the performance of classifiers, using the ground truth labels denoted by
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})
for a given
\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
and the predictions denoted by the vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}=(\hat{y}_{1},\ldots,\hat{y}_{N})
, acquired from the
empirical risk minimizers

g
and
g_{p}
(c.f.
Section

2.2.2
).

Accuracy and Error-rate

The accuracy is defined as the proportion of correct predictions, while error-rate is the proportion of incorrect predictions

\displaystyle\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Acc}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})\coloneq\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\llbracket y_{i}=\hat{y}_{i}\rrbracket\,,\quad\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{Err}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})\coloneq\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\llbracket y_{i}\neq\hat{y}_{i}\rrbracket\,.

CM
(
CM
)

The
CM
is defined using the
true positive
(
\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{tp}}}
),
true negative
(
\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{tn}}}
),
false positive
(
\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{fp}}}
), and
false negative
(
\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{fn}}}
) as

\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{CM}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{tn}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\llbracket y_{i}=0,\hat{y}_{i}=0\rrbracket&\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{fp}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\llbracket y_{i}=0,\hat{y}_{i}=1\rrbracket\\
\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{fn}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\llbracket y_{i}=1,\hat{y}_{i}=0\rrbracket&\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{tp}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\llbracket y_{i}=1,\hat{y}_{i}=1\rrbracket\end{array}\right]

FNR
(
FNR
) and
FPR

The
FNR
(type 2 error) is defined as the ratio of

to the total number of positive instances, while the

(type 1 error) is the ratio of

to the number of negative instances, defined as

\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{FNR}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})=\tfrac{\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{fn}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})}{\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{fn}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})+\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{tp}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})}\,,\quad\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{FPR}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})=\tfrac{\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{fp}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})}{\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{fp}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})+\operatorname{m}_{\textsc{{tn}}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}})}\,.
