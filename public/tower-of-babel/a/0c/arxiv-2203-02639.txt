Theoretical results and modeling under the discrete Birnbaum-Saunders distribution
Filidor Vilca, Roberto Vila, Helton Saulo, Luis Sánchez, Jeremias Leão, Department of Statistics, Universidade Estadual de Campinas, Campinas, Brazil, Department of Statistics, Universidade de Brasília, Brasília Brazil, Institute of Statistics, Universidad Austral de Chile, Valdivia, Chile, Department of Statistics, Universidade Federal do Amazonas, Manaus, Brazil

Abstract
In this paper, we discuss some theoretical results and properties of a discrete version of the Birnbaum-Saunders distribution. We present a proof of the unimodality of this model. Moreover, results on moments, quantile function, reliability and order statistics are also presented. In addition, we propose a regression model based on the discrete Birnbaum-Saunders distribution. The model parameters are estimated by the maximum likelihood method and a Monte Carlo study is performed to evaluate the performance of the estimators. Finally, we illustrate the proposed methodology with the use of real data sets. Keywords Birnbaum-Saunders distribution; Regression model; Maximum likelihood; Monte Carlo simulation; R software.

Introduction

Despite the increasing number of works on discrete distributions in reliability, one can note that in many practical cases there is the need of more flexible distributions to model lifetime data. One way to develop new discrete distributions is by generating the discrete analogous of usual distributions for continuous lifetimes; see
Alzaatreh et al., 2012
. Some interesting discrete distributions are for example the discrete gamma distribution
(
Abouammoh and Alhazzani, 2015
)
and the discrete Weibull distribution
(
Vila et al., 2019
)
. It is well known that in reliability, the continuous Birnbaum-Saunders (BS) distribution, proposed by
Birnbaum and Saunders, 1969
, takes advantage than most continuous probability distributions, including the continuous gamma and Weibull distributions; see
Leiva, 2016
. The continuous BS distribution is a positively skewed model that is closely related to the normal distribution. Despite its origin in material fatigue, it has been considered in business, industry, insurance, inventory, quality control, among others; see, for example,

Lio and Park, 2008
,

Balakrishnan et al., 2009
,

Ahmed et al., 2010
,

Vilca et al., 2010
,

Paula et al., 2012
,

Marchant et al., 2013
,

Rojas et al., 2015
,

Wanke and Leiva, 2015
,

Leiva et al., 2011
;
Leiva et al., 2014a
;
Leiva et al., 2014b
;
Leiva et al., 2017
,

Saulo et al., 2019
,

Desousa et al., 2018
,

Leão et al., 2018
, and

Ventura et al., 2019
. Good recent references on the Birnbaum-Saunders distribution are
Leiva, 2016
and
Balakrishnan and Kundu, 2019
. In particular, a positive random variable
T
is said to follow a continuous BS distribution if its cumulative distribution function (CDF) is given by

\displaystyle F_{T}(t;\bm{\theta})=\Phi\left[a(t;\bm{\theta})\right],\,t>0,

(1)

where
\bm{\theta}=(\alpha,\beta)^{\top}
, with
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
denoting the shape and scale parameters, respectively,
a(t;\bm{\theta})=\big(\sqrt{{t}/{\beta}}-\sqrt{{\beta}/{t}}\big)/\alpha
, and
\Phi[\cdot]
is the standard normal CDF. This distribution is usually denoted by
T\sim{\rm BS}(\bm{\theta})
. Even though the number of applications of the usual continuous BS distribution has been growing, there is a big number of applications where a discrete version of this distribution could be more appropriate. For example, to model the number of cycles or runs that a material or equipment supports before failing or breaking, the number of sessions of a treatment until the cure of a patient, or even the shelf life (in days) of a food product; see
Vila et al., 2019
.

In this paper, we study in more depth a discrete version of the continuous BS distribution, which was initially introduced by
Sen et al., 2010
. The primary objectives of this paper are: (i) to discuss novel theoretical results and properties of this discrete BS (
{\rm BS_{d}}
) distribution; and (ii) to introduce the corresponding regression model. The secondary objectives are: (i) to obtain the maximum likelihood estimates of the model parameters; (ii) to carry out Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate the performance of
the maximum likelihood estimators; and (iv) to discuss real data applications of the proposed methodology.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. In Section
2
, we present the
{\rm BS_{d}}
model and discuss some of its mathematical properties. Also, it is considered estimation of the model parameters based on maximum likelihood method. In Section
3
, a
{\rm BS_{d}}
regression model is proposed, and the model parameter estimation is approached by using the maximum likelihood method. In Section
4
, we carry out Monte Carlo simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the estimators and we illustrate the proposed methodology with two real data sets. Finally, in Section
5
, we make some concluding remarks.

2
Discrete Birnbaum-Saunders distribution

Before defining the proposed discrete distribution, we present the probability density function (PDF) and quantile function of the continuous BS distribution. If
T\sim{\rm BS}(\bm{\theta})
, then its PDF is given by

\displaystyle f_{T}(t;\bm{\theta})=\phi\big[a(t;\bm{\theta})\big]a^{\prime}(t;\bm{\theta}),\,t>0,

where
\phi[\,\cdot\,]
is the PDF of the standard normal distribution,
a(t;\bm{\theta})
is as in (
1
)
and
a^{\prime}(t;\bm{\theta})=(t+\beta)/(2t^{3/2}\alpha\beta^{1/2})

is the derivative of
a(t;\bm{\theta})
with respect to

t
. Moreover, the
p
-th quantile of
T\sim{\rm BS}(\bm{\theta})
is given by

Q_{p}={\beta\over 4}\,\left\{\alpha\Phi^{-1}(p)+\sqrt{[\alpha\Phi^{-1}(p)]^{2}+4}\right\}^{2},

(2)

where
p\in(0,1)
.

Now, we are ready to present a discrete random variable associated to the positive
T
as follows

S=\lfloor T\rfloor,

where
\lfloor t\rfloor
denotes the largest integer contained in
t
.
As the set of all possible values of
T
is the set
(0,\infty)
, then

S=s
iff
s<T\leq s+1,\,s=0,1,\ldots.

Consequently, the probability mass function (PMF) of
S
can be expressed by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S=s)=\mathbb{P}(s<T\leq s+1)=\begin{cases}\Phi\big[a(1;\bm{\theta})\big],&\text{if}\ s=0,\\[5.69046pt]
\Phi\big[a(s+1;\bm{\theta})\big]-\Phi\big[a(s;\bm{\theta})\big],&\text{if}\ s=1,2,\ldots,\end{cases}

(3)

where
a(\cdot;\bm{\theta})
is as in (
1
). We can show that
\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(S=s)=\mathbb{P}(T>0)=1
, so
\mathbb{P}(S=s)
is a PMF. On the other hand, the CDF of
S
is given by

\displaystyle{F}(s;\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{P}(S\leq s)=\Phi\big[a(\lfloor s\rfloor+1;\bm{\theta})\big]\mathds{1}_{\{s\geq 0\}}.

The distribution of the discrete random variable
S
will be denoted by
S\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta})
and will be called

{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution.

The reliability function (RF) and hazard rate function (HR) of
S\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta})
are, respectively, given by

\displaystyle R(s;\bm{\theta})=1-F(s;\bm{\theta})=1-\Phi\big[a({\lfloor s\rfloor}+1;\bm{\theta})\big]\mathds{1}_{\{s\geq 0\}},

(4)

\displaystyle{H}(s;\bm{\theta})={\mathbb{P}(S=s)\over\mathbb{P}(S=s)+R(s;\bm{\theta})}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Phi\big[a({1};\bm{\theta})\big],&\text{if}\ s=0,\\[5.69046pt]
{\Phi[a({s+1};\bm{\theta})]-\Phi[a({s};\bm{\theta})]\over 1-\Phi[a({s};\bm{\theta})]}&\text{if}\ s=1,2,\ldots,\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1-R(0;\bm{\theta}),&\text{if}\ s=0,\\[5.69046pt]
1-{R(s;\bm{\theta})\over R(s-1;\bm{\theta})},&\text{if}\ s=1,2,\ldots.\end{cases}

From the above identity, we have

\displaystyle R(s;\bm{\theta})=\prod_{y=0}^{s-1}{R(y;\bm{\theta})\over R(y-1;\bm{\theta})}=\prod_{y=0}^{s-1}\big[1-H(y;\bm{\theta})\big],\quad s=0,1,2,\ldots,

with the convention that
\prod_{y=0}^{-1}b_{y}=1
and that
R(-1;\bm{\theta})=1
.

Figures
1
and
2
displays different shapes of the
{\rm BS_{d}}
PMF and HR for different choices of parameters. From these figures, we observe that the
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution possesses unimodal shapes for the PMF and HR.

2.1
Properties

We present some properties of the
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution, many of the results can be easily derived from the definition of the
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution.

Proposition
1
.

If
S\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta})
and
t>0
, the following holds:

(a)

\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\Phi\big[a({s+1};\bm{\theta})\big]=1+\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\Phi\big[a({s};\bm{\theta})\big];

(b)

\mathbb{P}(S\leq t)=\mathbb{P}(S\leq\lfloor t\rfloor)=\Phi\big[a({\lfloor t\rfloor+1};\bm{\theta})\big]=\mathbb{P}(T\leq\lfloor t\rfloor+1);

(c)

\mathbb{P}(S<t)=\mathbb{P}(T\leq\lfloor t\rfloor);

(d)

\mathbb{P}(S\geq t)=\mathbb{P}(T\geq\lfloor t\rfloor+1);

(e)

\mathbb{P}(S\leq\lfloor t\rfloor)=\mathbb{P}(T\leq\lfloor t\rfloor+1);

(f)

\mathbb{P}(S\geq\lfloor t\rfloor)=\mathbb{P}(T>\lfloor t\rfloor)
.

2.1.1
\boldsymbol{p}
-th quantile

Proposition
2
.

Let
S\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta})
and
Q_{p}
the quantile function in (
2
),
p\in(0,1)
.
Then,

(a)

If
Q_{p}>0
is a natural number, then
Q_{p}-1
is the
p
-th quantile of the distribution of
S
;

(b)

If
Q_{p}>0
is not a natural number, then
p
-th quantile of the distribution of
S
can be represented by any value in the interval
\big[\lfloor Q_{p}\rfloor,\lfloor Q_{p}\rfloor+1\big)
.

Proof.

Since
Q_{p}
is the
p
-th quantile for the continuous random variable
T\sim{\rm BS}(\bm{\theta})
,
from Proposition
1
, Item (e), we have

\mathbb{P}(S<Q_{p}-1)\leq\mathbb{P}(S\leq Q_{p}-1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm(e)}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}(T\leq Q_{p})=p,\quad S\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta}),

whenever
Q_{p}>0
is a natural number.
So, we have that
Q_{p}-1
is the
p
-th quantile of the distribution of
S
. This proves the first item.

Now, let
t=Q_{p}>0
be not a natural number. From Items (d) and (c) of Proposition
1
and from inequalities
\lfloor Q_{p}\rfloor\leq Q_{p}\leq\lfloor Q_{p}\rfloor+1
,
we have the following

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S<Q_{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm(d)}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}(T\leq\lfloor Q_{p}\rfloor)\leq\mathbb{P}(T\leq Q_{p})=p,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S\leq Q_{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm(c)}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}(T\leq\lfloor Q_{p}\rfloor+1)\geq\mathbb{P}(T\leq Q_{p})=p,

and consequently

\mathbb{P}(S<Q_{p})\leq p\leq\mathbb{P}(S\leq Q_{p})
. This will be true for any
\lfloor Q_{p}\rfloor\leq t<\lfloor Q_{p}\rfloor+1
. So, we have that,
at the percentage point
p
, the quantile for
S
can be represented by any value in

\big[\lfloor Q_{p}\rfloor,\lfloor Q_{p}\rfloor+1\big)
.
Thus we complete the proof.
∎

2.1.2
Shape properties

The next two results are related to the unimodality of the
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution.

Proposition
3
.

The
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution is unimodal.

Proof.

Let
T\sim{\rm BS}(\bm{\theta})
be a random variable with continuous
{\rm BS}
distribution. Let
f_{T}(t;\bm{\theta})
,
t>0
be their respective PDF.
It is well-known that this distribution is unimodal (see Proposition 7 in
Vila et al., 2020
), then there exists a unique point
t_{0}>0

such that its PDF satisfies the following inequalities:

f_{T}(t;\bm{\theta})\geq f_{T}(t-1;\bm{\theta}),\quad\text{for all}\ t\leq t_{0},

and

f_{T}(t;\bm{\theta})\geq f_{T}(t+1;\bm{\theta}),\quad\text{for all}\ t\geq t_{0}.

If
s
is a natural number such that
s\leq\lfloor t_{0}\rfloor-1
, then

\mathbb{P}(S=s)=\int_{s}^{s+1}f_{T}(t;\bm{\theta})\,{\rm d}t\geq\int_{s}^{s+1}f_{T}(t-1;\bm{\theta})\,{\rm d}t=\mathbb{P}(S=s-1),

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S=s)-\mathbb{P}(S=s-1)\geq 0\quad\text{for all}\ s\leq\lfloor t_{0}\rfloor-1.

Similarly, for
s\geq\lfloor t_{0}\rfloor+1
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(S=s+1)-\mathbb{P}(S=s)\leq 0.

It follows that
\big\{\mathbb{P}(S=s):s=0,1,2,\ldots\big\}
is unimodal, whatever sign
\mathbb{P}(S=\lfloor t_{0}\rfloor)-\mathbb{P}(S=\lfloor t_{0}\rfloor-1)
may have.
∎

Remark
2
.

As a sub-product of the proof of Proposition
3
,
the mode of the
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution is
\lfloor t_{0}\rfloor
,
where

t_{0}
is the mode of the corresponding continuous
{\rm BS}
distribution.

Proposition
4
.

The
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution has a unique mode in the set
\big\{s=0,1,\ldots:0\leq s\leq\lfloor\beta\rfloor\big\}
.

Proof.

Proposition
3
guarantees the uniqueness of mode. It remains to prove that the
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution
\mathbb{P}(S=s)
is decreasing for all
s\geq\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1
. We prove this by comparing the continuous BS distribution with the corresponding
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution. Indeed, in Lemma 2.1. of
Vila et al., 2020
is proved that the PDF
f_{T}(t;\bm{\theta})
of the continuous BS distribution is a decreasing function when
t>\beta
. However this extends to every
t\geq\beta
because

\big\{{{\rm d}\over{\rm d}t}\log[f_{T}(t;\bm{\theta})]\big\}|_{t=\beta}=a^{\prime\prime}(\beta;\bm{\theta})/a^{\prime}(\beta;\bm{\theta})<0
.
Hence, as a sub-product of the proof of Proposition
3
, it follows that the
{\rm BS_{d}}
PMF (
3
) is decreasing for all
s\geq\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1
. Thus, we have completed the proof.
∎

2.1.3
Order statistics

Proposition
5
.

If
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random
variables such that
S_{1}\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta})
,
then, the
i
th;
i=1,2,\ldots,n
; order statistic of the
{\rm BS_{d}}

distribution, denoted
S_{(i)}
, can be written as

\mathbb{P}(S_{(i)}\leq s)=\sum_{k=i}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n-k}{j}(-1)^{j}{F}\big[a({\lfloor s\rfloor};\bm{\theta});k+j\big],\quad s\geq 1,

where

{F}[\cdot;k+j]

denotes the CDF of the power normal distribution (PND).
Different properties of the PND have been discussed by
Gupta and Gupta, 2008
.

Proof.

It is well-known that

\mathbb{P}(S_{(i)}\leq s)=\sum_{k=i}^{n}\binom{n}{k}[F(s;\bm{\theta})]^{k}[R(s;\bm{\theta})]^{n-k}
,
s\geq 1

(see Item (2.7) of
Shahbaz et al., 2016
).
Using the Newton binomial formula and the definition of a PND, the proof follows.
∎

Remark
3
.

By using the identity

\sum_{k=i}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}=i\binom{n}{i}\int_{0}^{p}t^{i-1}(1-t)^{n-i}\,{\rm d}t,

the distribution function of
S_{(i)}
can also be written as

{P}(S_{(i)}\leq s)={\Gamma(n+1)\over\Gamma(i)\Gamma(n-i+1)}\,\int_{0}^{\Phi[a(\lfloor s\rfloor;\bm{\theta})]}t^{i-1}(1-t)^{n-i}\,{\rm d}t,\quad s\geq 1,

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function.

2.1.4
Mean residual life function and variance residual life function

Let
S\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta})
, the mean residual life function (MRLF) and
variance residual life function (VRLF) are defined by

\mu_{S}(k)={\mathbb{E}(S-k|S\geq k)}={\sum_{s=k}^{\infty}R(s;\bm{\theta})\over R(k-1;\bm{\theta})}

and

\sigma^{2}_{S}(k)={\rm Var}(S-k|S\geq k)=2{\sum_{s=k}^{\infty}s\,R(s;\bm{\theta})\over R(k-1;\bm{\theta})}-(2k-1)\mu_{S}(k)-\mu^{2}_{S}(k),

respectively, where
R(s;\bm{\theta})
is given in (
4
) and
k=0,1,\ldots,
.

Proposition
6
.

Let
S\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta})
with
\bm{\theta}
belongs to the set

\Theta=\big\{\bm{\theta}_{*}\in(0,\infty)^{2}:\ C(t;\bm{\theta}_{*})=a^{\prime\prime}(t;\bm{\theta}_{*})-a(t;\bm{\theta}_{*})[a^{\prime}(t;\bm{\theta}_{*})]^{2}>0,\ \ \forall\ t>0\big\}.

Then,

(a)

S
has decreasing MRLF;

(b)

S
has increasing HR;

(c)

S
has decreasing VRLF,

whenever
\mathbb{P}(S>0)=1
.

Proof.

For
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
, we have

\displaystyle{{\rm d}^{2}\over{\rm d}t^{2}}\log\big[R(t;\bm{\theta})\big]

\displaystyle=-{\phi\big[a(t;\bm{\theta})\big]\over 1-\Phi\big[a(t;\bm{\theta})\big]}\,\left\{{\phi\big[a(t;\bm{\theta})\big]\big[a^{\prime}(t;\bm{\theta})\big]^{2}\over 1-\Phi\big[a(t;\bm{\theta})\big]}+C(t;\bm{\theta})\right\}<0\quad\text{for all}\ t>0.

In other words, the function
\log[R(t;\bm{\theta})]
is concave. This condition
implies that

\log\big[R({t_{1}+t_{2}\over 2};\bm{\theta})\big]\geq{1\over 2}\log\big[R(t_{1};\bm{\theta})\big]+{1\over 2}\log\big[R(t_{2};\bm{\theta})\big]

or equivalently that

\big[R({t_{1}+t_{2}\over 2};\bm{\theta})\big]^{2}\geq R(t_{1};\bm{\theta})R(t_{2};\bm{\theta})

for all

t_{1},t_{2}>0.

Hence, taking

t_{1}=s+2
and
t_{2}=s
for
s=1,2,3,\ldots
, we have

\displaystyle\big[R(s+1;\bm{\theta})\big]^{2}\geq R(s+2;\bm{\theta})R(s;\bm{\theta})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H(s+1;\bm{\theta})\leqslant H(s+2;\bm{\theta}).

That is,

S
has increasing hazard rate
H(\cdot;\bm{\theta})
.
Then, by Theorem 2.1 of

Gupta, 2015
, it follows that
S
has decreasing mean residual life function. This proves the statement in Items (a) and (b).
Finally, the proof of Item (c) follows directly by combining Item (a) with Theorem 2.2 in

Gupta, 2015
.
∎

2.1.5
Moments properties

Proposition
7
.

The distribution of a random variable
S
with
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution has all moments.

Proof.

In Proposition
3
is proved that
S
has a
strongly unimodal distribution (see
Keilson and Gerber, 1971
, for a formal definition).
Since that all strongly unimodal distribution have all moments (see Theorem 7 in
Keilson and Gerber, 1971
), the proof of the proposition follows.
∎

Proposition
8
.

If
S\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta})
is a random variable, for each natural number
r
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lllll}\textrm{\rm(a)}&\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(S^{r})=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\big[(s+1)^{r}-s^{r}\big]\left\{1-\Phi\big[a(s+1;\bm{\theta})\big]\right\};\\[14.22636pt]
\textrm{\rm(b)}&\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(S^{r})=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{r}\sum_{i=0}^{r-k}\binom{r-k}{i}s^{k+i}\,\left\{1-\Phi\big[a(s+1;\bm{\theta})\big]\right\};\\[18.49411pt]
\textrm{\rm(c)}&\displaystyle{\rm Var}(S)=2\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}s\left\{1-\Phi\big[a(s+1;\bm{\theta})\big]\right\}\\[5.69046pt]
&\qquad\quad\,+\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\left\{1-\Phi\big[a(s+1;\bm{\theta})\big]\right\}\bigg[1-\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\left\{1-\Phi\big[a(s+1;\bm{\theta})\big]\right\}\bigg].\end{array}

Proof.

The whole proof follows closely Proposition 2 of
Saulo et al., 2021
and we present it for the sake of completeness.
We emphasize that the statements of Items (a), (b) and (c) are valid for any
discrete random variable
S
with support
\{0,1,\ldots\}
.

By using the telescopic series
\sum_{x=0}^{i-1}[(x+1)^{r}-x^{r}]=i^{r}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(S^{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\{s<i\}}[(s+1)^{r}-s^{r}]\,\mathbb{P}(S=i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}[(s+1)^{r}-s^{r}]\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\{i>s\}}\,\mathbb{P}(S=i),

where in the second equality we exchange the orders of the summations because

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\{s<i\}}\big|(s+1)^{r}-s^{r}\big|\,\mathbb{P}(S=i)=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\{s<i\}}\cdot[(s+1)^{r}-s^{r}]\,\mathbb{P}(S=i)=i^{r}\mathbb{P}(S=i),

is finite for each
i=0,1,\ldots
; and because

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}i^{r}\mathbb{P}(S=i)=\mathbb{E}(S^{r})
always exists (see Proposition
7
).
This proves Item (a).
The second item follows by combining Item (a) with
the polynomial identity
a^{n}-b^{n}=(a-b)\sum_{k=0}^{r}a^{r-k}b^{k}
and the binomial expansion.
Already, the proof of Item (c) is obtained by using Item (a) and simple algebraic manipulations.
∎

2.2
Maximum likelihood estimation

In this section, we discuss the maximum likelihood estimation for the unknown model parameters based on a random sample
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{n}
from
S\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta})
, with
\bm{\theta}=(\alpha,\beta)
.
Thus, the log-likelihood function for
\bm{\theta}
is given by

l(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\Phi\big[a({s_{i}+1};\bm{\theta})\big]-\Phi\big[a({s_{i}};\bm{\theta})\big]\right\}.

(6)

In order to obtain the maximum likelihood estimate of
\bm{\theta}
, we have the score function given by

\dot{\bm{l}}(\bm{\theta})=[\dot{l}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}),\dot{l}_{\beta}(\bm{\theta})]^{\top}
, whose elements are given by

\displaystyle\dot{l}_{z}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{1}(-1)^{j+1}\,{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial z}\,\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta}),\quad z\in\{\alpha,\beta\},

(7)

where

\displaystyle\frac{\partial a(s_{i};\bm{\theta})}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\alpha}a(s_{i};\bm{\theta}),\,\frac{\partial a(s;\bm{\theta})}{\partial\beta}=-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\frac{1}{s^{1/2}\beta^{3/2}}(s+\beta)\,\,{\rm and}\,\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta})={\phi\big[a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\big]\over\mathbb{P}(S=s_{i})}.

The maximum likelihood estimate of
\alpha
and
\beta
can be
obtained solving the equations
\dot{l}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})=0
and
\dot{l}_{\beta}(\bm{\theta})=0
by an iterative procedure for non-linear optimization. The Hessian matrix of
{l}(\bm{\theta})
is given by

\ddot{\bm{l}}(\bm{\theta})=\big[\frac{\partial^{2}l(\bm{\theta})}{\partial z\partial w}\big]_{2\times 2},

for each
w,z\in\{\alpha,\beta\}
, where

\displaystyle\ddot{l}_{wz}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{1}(-1)^{j+1}{\partial^{2}a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial w\partial z}\,{\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta})}

(8)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{1}(-1)^{j+1}a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\,{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial w}\,{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial z}\,{\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta})}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{1}(-1)^{j+1}{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial z}\,{\phi_{j}\big[s_{i},\bm{\theta}\big]}\sum_{k=0}^{1}(-1)^{k+1}\,{\partial a({s_{i}+k};\bm{\theta})\over\partial w}\,{\phi_{k}(s_{i},\bm{\theta})},

where
\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta})
is as in (
7
) and the second-order partial derivatives of
a(\cdot;\bm{\theta})
, with respect to the parameters, are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}a(s_{i};\bm{\theta})}{\partial\alpha\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}}a(s_{i}),\,\,\,\frac{\partial^{2}a(s_{i};\bm{\theta})}{\partial\beta\partial\alpha}=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\partial a(s_{i};\bm{\theta})}{\partial\beta},\,\,\,\frac{\partial^{2}a(s_{i};\bm{\theta})}{\partial\beta\partial\beta}=\frac{1}{4\alpha s_{i}^{1/2}\beta^{5/2}}(3s_{i}+\beta).

3
Discrete Birnbaum-Saunders regression model

In the context of count data, the
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution may be an interesting alternative distribution to usual discrete distributions or to those discrete distributions have been derived from continuous distributions. Then, for the
{\rm BS_{d}}
distribution we are also going to consider its associated regression model, which will be the goal of this part of the study. The associated
{\rm BS_{d}}
regression model that we are going to introduce is inspired by continuous BS regression model developed by
Balakrishnan and Zhu, 2015
, where they considered the scale parameter depending on covariates.

Suppose that we observe independent failure times
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
, such as

\displaystyle S_{i}\sim{\rm BS_{d}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}),

(9)

where
\bm{\theta}_{i}=(\alpha,\beta_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. The distribution depends on covariates
\bm{x}_{i}=(x_{1i},\ldots,x_{pi})^{\top}
associated with
\beta_{i}
thought
\beta_{i}=\exp(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\eta})
, with
\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{p})^{\top}
being a vector of unknown parameters. The corresponding PMF associated with (
9
) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=s_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi\big[a(s_{i};\bm{\theta}_{i})\big]\mathds{1}_{\{s_{i}=0\}}+\left\{\Phi\left(a(s_{i}+1;\bm{\theta}_{i})\right)-\Phi\left(a(s_{i};\bm{\theta}_{i})\right)\right\}\mathds{1}_{\{s_{i}\geqslant 1\}},

i=1,\ldots,n
.

3.1
Maximum likelihood estimation

The log-likelihood function for
{\bm{\theta}}=(\alpha,\bm{\eta}^{\top})^{\top}
is given by

\displaystyle l(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\Phi\big[a(s_{i};\bm{\theta}_{i})\big]\mathds{1}_{\{s_{i}=0\}}+\left\{\Phi\left(a(s_{i}+1;\bm{\theta}_{i})\right)-\Phi\left(a(s_{i};\bm{\theta}_{i})\right)\right\}\mathds{1}_{\{s_{i}\geqslant 1\}}\right\}.

(10)

Then, the first derivatives of the log-likelihood function (
10
),
with
{\bm{\theta}}=(\alpha,\bm{\eta}^{\top})^{\top}
, can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{l}_{u}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{1}(-1)^{j+1}\,{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial z_{i}}\,{\partial z_{i}\over\partial u}\,\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta})\quad u\in\{\alpha,\bm{\eta}\},z_{i}\in\{\alpha,\beta_{i}\},

where

\dot{l}_{u}(\bm{\theta})={\partial l(\bm{\theta})}/{\partial u}
, and

{\partial a({s}_{i};\bm{\theta})/\partial z_{i}}
is as in (
7
). Specifically

\displaystyle\dot{l}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{1}(-1)^{j+1}\,{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial\alpha}\,\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta}),

\displaystyle\dot{l}_{\eta}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{1}(-1)^{j+1}\,{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial\beta_{i}}\,\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta}){\partial\beta_{i}\over\partial\bm{\eta}},

where
{\partial\beta_{i}/\partial\bm{\eta}}=\beta_{i}\bm{x}_{i};i=1,\ldots,n.
From the likelihood equations

\dot{l}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})=0
and
\dot{l}_{\eta}(\bm{\theta})=0
, we can see that there is no closed-form solution to the maximization problem, so we implement two algorithms in software
R
to find the maximum likelihood estimates of
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\eta_{i}
,
i=0,\ldots,p
, by using the function
optim()
; see
R Core Team, 2020
. These procedures are evaluated and used in the next section.

Furthermore, the Hessian matrix of
l(\bm{\theta})
is given by

\big[\ddot{l}_{vu}(\bm{\theta})\big]_{p\times p}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial^{2}l(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\alpha^{2}}&\frac{\partial^{2}l(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\alpha\partial\bm{\eta}^{\top}}\\[5.69046pt]
\frac{\partial^{2}l(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\bm{\eta}\partial\alpha}&\frac{\partial^{2}l(\bm{\theta})}{\partial\bm{\eta}\partial\bm{\eta}^{\top}}\end{bmatrix},

where, for each
v,u\in\{\alpha,\bm{\eta}\}
and
w_{i},z_{i}\in\{\alpha,\beta_{i}\}
, the elements of the Hessian matrix are given by

\displaystyle\ddot{l}_{vu}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{1}(-1)^{j+1}\left[{\partial^{2}a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial w_{i}\partial z_{i}}\,{\partial z_{i}\over\partial u}\,{\partial w_{i}\over\partial v}+{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial z_{i}}\,{\partial^{2}z_{i}\over\partial v\partial u}\right]\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{1}(-1)^{j+1}a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\,{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial w_{i}}\,{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial z_{i}}\,{\partial z_{i}\over\partial u}\,{\partial w_{i}\over\partial v}\,\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{1}(-1)^{j+1}{\partial a({s_{i}+j};\bm{\theta})\over\partial z_{i}}\,\phi_{j}(s_{i},\bm{\theta})\sum_{k=0}^{1}(-1)^{k+1}\,{\partial a({s_{i}+k};\bm{\theta})\over\partial w_{i}}\,{\partial z_{i}\over\partial u}\,{\partial w_{i}\over\partial v}\,\phi_{k}(s_{i},\bm{\theta}),

where
{\partial^{2}a({s}_{i};\bm{\theta})/\partial w_{i}\partial z_{i}}
is as in (
8
)
and

\frac{\partial\beta_{i}}{\partial\bm{\eta}}=\beta_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\,\,\,{\rm and}\,\,\,\frac{\partial^{2}\beta_{i}}{\partial\bm{\eta}\partial\bm{\eta}^{\top}}=\beta_{i}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}

Again, note that the equation
\dot{l}_{u}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{0}
does not provides analytic solutions for
\widehat{\alpha}
and
\widehat{\eta_{j}}
,
j=0,\ldots,p
. Therefore, we have implemented two algorithms in software
R
to find the maximum likelihood estimates of
\alpha
and
\eta_{i}
,
i=0,\ldots,p
, by using the function
optim()
; see
R Core Team, 2020
. These procedures are evaluated and used in the next section.

5
Concluding remarks

The continuous Birnbaum-Saunders distribution has been widely used in several areas, besides being an alternative to the Weibull and gamma distributions. However, in many practical problems, the use of discrete distributions is more appropriate. In this sense, we have studied a discrete version of the Birnbaum-Saunders distribution. Some important properties have been presented, such as moments, quantile function and reliability. We have presented a formal proof concerning the unimodality property of discrete Birnbaum-Saunders distribution. In addition, we have proposed a new discrete Birnbaum-Saunders regression model. Monte Carlo simulations have been carried out to evaluate the behaviour of
the maximum likelihood estimators. Two examples with real data have illustrated the proposed methodology. The results are seen to be quite favorable to the discrete Birnbaum-Saunders distribution as well as its regression model in terms of model fitting.
