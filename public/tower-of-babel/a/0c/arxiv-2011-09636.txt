Robust shadow estimation
Senrui Chen, Wenjun Yu, Pei Zeng, Steven T. Flammia

Abstract
Efficiently estimating properties of large and strongly coupled quantum systems is a central focus in many-body physics and quantum information theory. While quantum computers promise speedups for many of these tasks, near-term devices are prone to noise that will generally reduce the accuracy of such estimates. Here, we propose a sample-efficient and noise-resilient protocol for learning properties of quantum states building on the shadow estimation scheme [Huang et. al., Nature Physics 16, 1050–1057 (2020)]. By introducing an experimentally-friendly calibration procedure, our protocol can efficiently characterize and mitigate noises in the shadow estimation scheme, given only minimal assumptions on the experimental conditions. When the strength of noises can be bounded, our protocol approximately retains the same order of sample-efficiency as the standard shadow estimation scheme, while also possesses a provable noise resilience. We give rigorous bounds on the sample complexity of our protocol and demonstrate its performance with several numerical experiments, including estimations of quantum fidelity, correlation functions and energy expectations, etc., which highlight a wide spectrum of potential applications of our protocol on near-term devices.



I
Introduction

We are in the process of building large-scale and controllable quantum systems.
This not only provides new insights and tool kits for fundamental research in quantum many-body systems
Amico et al. 2008
and the quantum nature of spacetime
Qi 2018
, but also yields fruitful applications in computing
Shor 1994
;
Grover 1997
;
Lloyd 1996
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2011
, communication
Bennett and Brassard 1984
;
Ekert 1991
;
Bennett et al. 1993
, and sensing
Wineland et al. 1992
;
Giovannetti et al. 2006
.
Learning the properties, e.g., fidelity
da Silva et al. 2011
;
Flammia and Liu 2011
, entanglement
Brandão 2005
;
Abanin and Demler 2012
, and energy
Peruzzo et al. 2014
of generated quantum states is usually a major step in many quantum benchmarking protocols and quantum algorithms.
Among various figure of merits, robustness and efficiency are two key factors to assess the practicality of any property learning protocol.

In the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era
Preskill 2018
, quantum circuits inevitably suffer from noise.
The robustness of a property learning protocol then refers to the ability to tolerate such noise.
In a typical property estimation process, we generate several identical copies of the target quantum states, and then measure them using some devices which might be noisy and uncharacterized.
To verify the property estimates, one has to introduce new benchmarking devices, which (in the NISQ era) will also be noisy.
Consequently, we will be trapped into a loop of benchmarking.
To get rid of this, at least two approaches have been proposed: One is to introduce extra assumptions on the noise model, in which case we might be able to mitigate the error
Temme et al. 2017
;
Endo et al. 2018
;
Maciejewski et al. 2020
;
Bravyi et al. 2021
, but such assumptions may not be verifiable.
The other is to use device-independent protocols
McKague et al. 2012
;
Brunner et al. 2014
;
Gühne and Tóth 2009
which do not have any assumptions on the devices, but such protocols are mostly designed for some specific property learning tasks (
e.g.
entanglement detection), and their requirements on devices and computational/sample complexity can be too strict to produce anything informative in practice.

Thus, while property learning and testing leads to large efficiency gains in sample and computational complexity, one must in general have a well-characterized device for these methods to be applicable.
Quantum tomography
D’Ariano and Perinotti 2007
;
Guta et al. 2020
is a standard method to extract complete characterization information, but it requires exponentially many samples with respect to the number of qubits.
Several efficient tomographic schemes were proposed based on some properties of the prepared states, such as the low rank property
Gross et al. 2010
;
Flammia et al. 2012
, permutation symmetry
Tóth et al. 2010
;
Moroder et al. 2012
, and the locality of Schmidt decomposition
Cramer et al. 2010
;
Baumgratz et al. 2013
.
Nevertheless, such assumptions are restrictive and not applicable in many cases.
Another line of research focuses on efficiently extracting partial information of a quantum state without any prior knowledge.
An example is the quantum overlapping tomography
Cotler and Wilczek 2020
;
Bonet-Monroig et al. 2020
which can simultaneously estimate all
k
-qubit reduced density matrix of an arbitrary quantum state in a sample-efficient manner for small
k
.
The simplest version of this idea is to measure uniformly random Pauli strings
Evans et al. 2019
, which leads to a sample complexity of
O(k3^{k}\log n)
for estimating all
k
-body Pauli observables to fixed precision.
Machine-learning based approaches are also proposed
Carrasquilla et al. 2019
in this direction.

Recently, a new paradigm for efficient and universal quantum property estimation has been proposed named quantum shadow estimation.
Shadow estimation was first put forward in Ref.
Aaronson 2018
.
Roughly speaking, this scheme can simultaneously estimate the expectation values with respect to
N
observables of an unknown
d
-dimensional quantum state with order
\log d\log N
number of samples, which is usually more efficient than either conducting full tomography or measuring the
N
observables one by one.
Later on, a more experiment-friendly shadow estimation scheme was proposed
Huang et al. 2020
, which is able to estimate many useful properties of a quantum system with a small number of samples (see also
Paini et al. 2021
).
This protocol is also proven to be worst-case sample-optimal in the sense that any other protocol that is able to accurately estimate any collection of arbitrary observables must consume a number of samples at least comparable to this one.
Although promising for a broad spectrum of applications, the shadow estimation scheme in Ref.
Huang et al. 2020
(as well as the random Pauli scheme from
Evans et al. 2019
) assume perfect implementation of a group of unitary gates as well as ideal projective measurement on the computational basis.
It remains unclear how experimental noise can affect the performance of this scheme.

In this work, we reexamine the shadow estimation scheme and regard it as a twirling and retrieval procedure of the measurement channel.
In this way, we extend shadow estimation to the case when the unitaries and measurements are noisy.
With similar techniques used in the study of randomized benchmarking
Emerson et al. 2005
;
Knill et al. 2008
;
Chow et al. 2009
;
Magesan et al. 2011
;
Helsen et al. 2019
, we propose a modified shadow estimation strategy which is noise-resilient.
When the noise in the unitary operations and measurements is small, the robust shadow tomography scheme is able to faithfully estimate the required properties with a small additional cost, subject only to the assumption that one can prepare the initial ground state
\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
with high fidelity.
The proposed scheme is both robust and efficient, and hence highly practical for property estimation of a quantum system.

II
Preliminaries

We first introduce the Pauli-transfer-matrix (PTM) representation (or Liouville representation) to simplify the notation.
Note that all the linear operators
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
on the underlying
n
-qubit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
with
d=2^{n}
can be vectorized using the
n
-qubit (normalized) Pauli operator basis
\{\sigma_{a}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=P_{a}/\sqrt{d}\}_{a}
, where
P_{a}
are the usual Pauli matrices.
For a linear operator
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
, we define a column vector
|A\rangle\!\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{d^{2}}
with the
a
-th entry to be
|A\rangle\!\rangle_{a}=\tr(P_{a}A)/\sqrt{d}
.
The inner product on the vector space
\mathcal{H}_{d^{2}}
is defined by the
Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
as
\langle\!\langle A|B\rangle\!\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\tr(A^{\dagger}B)
.
The normalized Pauli basis
\{\sigma_{a}\}_{a}
is then an orthonormal basis in
\mathcal{H}_{d^{2}}
.
Superoperators on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
are linear maps taking operators to operators
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
.
In the vector space
\mathcal{H}_{d^{2}}
, a superoperator
\mathcal{E}
can be represented by a matrix in the Pauli basis, with the entries given by
\mathcal{E}_{ab}=\langle\!\langle\sigma_{a}|\mathcal{E}(\sigma_{b})\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle\sigma_{a}|\mathcal{E}|\sigma_{b}\rangle\!\rangle
.
Choosing the Pauli basis for the superoperator is sometimes called the Pauli transfer matrix.
With a slight abuse of notation, we sometimes denote a superoperator and its PTM using the same notation.
A detailed introduction to the PTM is given in Appendix
A.3
.

In this work, we focus on the task of estimating the expectation values
\{\tr(O_{i}\rho)\}_{i}
of a set of observables
\{O_{i}\}_{i}
on an underlying unknown quantum state
\rho
,

\tr(O_{i}\rho)=\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle,\quad 1\leq i\leq N.

(1)

When the number of observables
N
is large, a direct exhaustive measurement of the (generally incompatible) observables
\{O_{i}\}
on
\rho
is expensive.
Besides, in many cases we may want to perform tomographic experiments on
\rho
before deciding which observables
\{O_{i}\}
should be estimated.
To realize this, a natural idea is to insert an extra prepare-and-measure superoperator between
\langle\!\langle O_{i}|
and
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
,

\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\to\sum_{x}\langle\!\langle O_{i}|A_{x}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle E_{x}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle.

(2)

In an experiment, we first apply a POVM measurement
\{E_{x}\}_{x}
at
\rho
.
Then, conditioned on the outcome
x
, we calculate
\langle\!\langle O_{i}|A_{x}\rangle\!\rangle
via classical post-processing.
If we repeat this procedure, then the sample average over these experiments gives an estimator for
\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
.
As long as the inserted superoperator
\sum_{x}|A_{x}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle E_{x}|
equals to
\mathcal{I}
, this estimator will be unbiased.

To construct a realization of such a superoperator, we consider the dephasing channel in the computational basis (
Z
-basis)
\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\sum_{z}|z\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle z|
, where
|z\rangle\!\rangle
is the vectorization of the
Z
-basis eigenstate
\ket{z}\bra{z}
, with
z\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}
.
Expanding
\mathcal{M}_{Z}
in the Pauli operator basis
\{|\sigma_{0}\rangle\!\rangle,|\sigma_{x}\rangle\!\rangle,|\sigma_{y}\rangle\!\rangle,|\sigma_{z}\rangle\!\rangle\}^{\otimes n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{Z}

\displaystyle=\left(|\sigma_{0}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{0}|+|\sigma_{z}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{z}|\right)^{\otimes n}

(3)

\displaystyle=\left[\text{diag}(1,0,0,1)\right]^{\otimes n},

where
\text{diag}(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots)
is a diagonal matrix with the diagonal elements
a_{1},a_{2},\cdots
.
If
\mathcal{M}_{Z}
were invertible, we could insert the superoperator
\mathcal{M}_{Z}^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{Z}=\sum_{z}|\mathcal{M}_{Z}^{-1}(z)\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle z|=\mathcal{I}
.
However,
\mathcal{M}_{Z}
is not invertible due to the lack of
X,~Y
-basis information in a
Z
-basis measurement.
To make
\mathcal{M}_{Z}
invertible, we can introduce an extra unitary twirling
Huang et al. 2020
,

\mathcal{M}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\in\mathbb{G}}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathcal{U}.

(4)

Here,
\mathbb{G}
is a subset of the unitaries
\{U\}
in
U(d)
to be specified later, and
\mathcal{U}
is the PTM representation of
U
.

When
\mathbb{G}
forms a group, the PTMs
\{\mathcal{U}\}
forms a representation of
\mathbb{G}
.
A direct application of Schur’s Lemma
Fulton and Harris 2013
(see Appendix
A.1
) allows us to calculate the explicit form of
\mathcal{M}
,

\mathcal{M}=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{\mathbb{G}}}\frac{\tr[\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda}]}{\tr[\Pi_{\lambda}]}\Pi_{\lambda},

(5)

where
R_{\mathbb{G}}
is the set of irreducible sub-representations of the group
\mathbb{G}
, and
\Pi_{\lambda}
is the corresponding projector onto the invariant subspace.
Since the projectors are complete and orthogonal to each other,
\mathcal{M}
is invertible if and only if all the coefficients are non-zero.
Therefore the twirling group
\mathbb{G}
needs to satisfy

\tr[\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda}]\neq 0,\ \forall\,\lambda\in R_{\mathbb{G}}.

(6)

Once Eq. (
6
) is satisfied, we can construct a shadow estimation protocol based on the equation

\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle=\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{U\in\mathbb{G}}\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}|z\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle z|\mathcal{U}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle.

(7)

To implement shadow estimation, one can repeat the following experiment: generate a single copy of
\rho
, act via a randomly sampled unitary
U
, and then perform a
Z
-basis measurement to return an output bit string
b
.
Then
\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle
is calculated on a classical computer.
Thanks to this decoupled processing of
\rho
with respect to
O_{i}
, the estimation of different observables can be done in parallel with a relatively small increase in sample complexity.

III
Robust Shadow Estimation

In practice, the unitary operations
and measurements used in the standard shadow estimation algorithm will be noisy.
We want to mitigate the effect of this noise on the output estimate of the shadow.
Our strategy to do this is simple: we first learn the noise as a simple stochastic model and then compensate for these errors using robust classical post-processing.

In general, noise in quantum devices is not stochastic, and coherent errors must be addressed.
However, thanks to the unitary twirling in shadow estimation, the stochastic nature of the noise is inherent to the protocol itself.
For example, any noise map that is twirled over a Clifford group that contains the Pauli group as a subgroup will reduce the noise to a purely stochastic Pauli channel
Knill 2005
.
The complete characterization of such noise channels can be efficiently and accurately performed
Harper et al. 2020
;
Flammia and Wallman 2020
;
Harper et al. 2021
.
It is then straightforward to compensate for such errors by modifying the classical post-processing, although a lengthy analysis is required to show the efficacy of this strategy.

In order to pursue a rigorous analysis of this strategy, we make the following two assumptions on the noise in the experimental device implementing the shadow estimation.

Assumptions 1
.

(Simplifying noise assumptions)

\bf A1

The noise in the circuit is gate-independent, time-stationary, Markovian noise.

\bf A2

The experimental device can generate the computational basis state
\ket{\bm{0}}\equiv\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
with sufficiently high fidelity.

Our first assumption is used throughout to ensure that there exists a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map such that the noisy gate
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
can be decomposed into
\Lambda\mathcal{U}
, where
\mathcal{U}
is the ideal gate while
\Lambda
is the noise channel.
The noise map
\Lambda
is independent of the unitary
\mathcal{U}
and the time
t
.
It also implies that the noise map occurring in the measurement is fixed independent of time and hence can be absorbed into
\Lambda
.
We remark that assumption
\bf A1
is widely used in the analysis of randomized benchmarking protocols.
The gate-independent part of the assumption is especially appropriate when the experimental unitaries are single-qubit gates, but it has been shown that the effect of weak gate dependence (a form of non-Markovianity) generally leads to weak perturbations
Wallman 2018
;
Merkel et al. 2018
. We also provide numerical evidences in Sec.
VIII
showing that our scheme is still quite robust against realistic gate-dependent noise models in experiment.

For our second assumption
A2
, from Sec.
III
to Sec.
V
we initially make the stronger assumption that the experimental device can prepare the
\ket{\bm{0}}
state
exactly
.
In Sec.
VI
we relax this to show that when
\ket{\bm{0}}
is not precisely prepared, but is prepared with sufficiently high fidelity, our protocol still gives a good estimation.
Fortunately, the computational basis state
\ket{\bm{0}}
is relatively easy to generate faithfully in many experimental platforms.

To see how unitary twirling helps to reduce the number of noise parameters, we calculate the noisy version of the random measurement channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\in\mathbb{G}}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Lambda\mathcal{U}

(9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{\mathbb{G}}}\frac{\tr[\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Lambda\Pi_{\lambda}]}{\tr[\Pi_{\lambda}]}\Pi_{\lambda}=\sum_{\lambda}f_{\lambda}\Pi_{\lambda},

where the
\{f_{\lambda}\}
are expansion coefficients of the twirled channel.
Note that the channel
\Lambda
describes both the noise in the gate
\mathcal{U}
and in the measurement
\mathcal{M}_{Z}
, which is always possible under our assumption
\bf{A1}
.
The number of
\{f_{\lambda}\}
is related to the number of irreducible representations in the PTM representation of the twirling group
\mathbb{G}
.
Later we will show that the coefficients
\{f_{\lambda}\}
can be estimated in parallel, similar to the normal shadow estimation procedure (referred to as the
calibration procedure
).

IV
Robust shadow estimation using global Clifford group

We first present a robust shadow estimation protocol using the
n
-qubit global Clifford group,
{\sf Cl}(2^{n})
.
The
n
-qubit Clifford group has many useful properties such as being a unitary 3-design
Webb 2016
;
Zhu 2017
;
Kueng and Gross 2015
, which is widely used in many tasks of quantum information and quantum computation.
It is a standard result that the
n
-qubit Clifford group has two irreducible representations in the Liouville representation whose projectors are given by
|\sigma_{\bm{0}}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{\bm{0}}|
and
I-|\sigma_{\bm{0}}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{\bm{0}}|
.
Assuming the
\widetilde{}\mathcal{M}
channel defined in Eq. (
9
) is trace preserving, it can be written as

\widetilde{}\mathcal{M}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}(2^{n})}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Lambda\mathcal{U}=|\sigma_{\bm{0}}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{\bm{0}}|+f(I-|\sigma_{\bm{0}}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{\bm{0}}|)

(10)

for some
f\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e. as a depolarizing channel.
It is easy to obtain
f=1/(2^{n}+1)
for the noiseless case using Eq. (
9
).
The noise characterization subroutine with
{\sf Cl}(2^{n})
is defined as follows,

\displaystyle\text{{NoiseEst}}_{{\sf Cl}(2^{n})}(U,b)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\frac{2^{n}\langle\!\langle b|\mathcal{U}|\bm{0}\rangle\!\rangle-1}{2^{n}-1},

(11)

where
|b\rangle\!\rangle
is the Liouville representation of the computational basis state
\ket{b}\bra{b}
and similar for
|\bm{0}\rangle\!\rangle
.

Next, define the Z-basis average fidelity of a noise channel
\Lambda
as
F_{Z}(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\langle\!\langle b|\Lambda|b\rangle\!\rangle
.
The following theorem demonstrates the correctness and sample efficiency of our protocol. We remark that the validity of this theorem relies on Assumptions
1
.

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

For
RShadow
with
\mathbb{G}={\sf Cl}(2^{n})
, if the number of samples for the calibration procedure satisfies

R=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-2}F_{Z}^{-2}),

(12)

where
F_{Z}\equiv F_{Z}(\Lambda)

and we assume
F_{Z}\gg 2^{-n}
,
then the subsequent estimation procedure with high probability satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}^{(r)})-\Tr(O\rho)\right|\leq\varepsilon\|O\|_{\infty},

(13)

for any observable
O
and quantum state
\rho
, where
\hat{o}^{(r)}
is the single-round estimator defined as in Algorithm
1
.

Here and throughout the paper, we use
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
to represent the Big-O notation with poly-logarithmic factors suppressed.
The more rigorous version of Theorem

is Theorem
7
in Appendix

.
We see that our protocol indeed eliminates the
systematic error
of shadow estimation in a sample-efficient manner, since without the calibration step the empirical expectation value would converge to a value that conflated the noise map

into the estimate, whereas

does not appear in Eq. (

More specifically, if the
Z
-basis average fidelity of the noise channel
\Lambda
is lower bounded by some constant (
e.g.
constant-strength depolarizing noise), then the sample complexity of our calibration stage is approximately independent of the system size
n
.

A more realistic noise model to consider is that of local noise with fixed strength, where
\Lambda\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\Lambda_{i}
and each single-qubit noise channel
\Lambda_{i}
satisfies
F_{Z}(\Lambda_{i})\geq 1-\xi
.
In that case, we have
F_{Z}(\Lambda)^{-2}\approx\exp(2n\xi)
for small
\xi
, so we can efficiently deal with a system size
n
that is comparable to
\xi^{-1}
.

Next, we consider the sample complexity of the estimation procedure.
Following a similar methodology of bounding the sample complexity in the noise-free standard shadow estimation scheme
Huang et al. 2020
, we bound the sample complexity of our
RShadow
estimation procedure as follows.

Theorem 2

(Informal)
.

For
RShadow
with
{\sf Cl}(2^{n})
, if the number of calibration samples
R_{C}
and the number of estimation samples
R_{E}
satisfies

\displaystyle R_{C}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon_{1}^{-2}F_{Z}^{-2}),

(14)

\displaystyle R_{E}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon_{2}^{-2}F_{Z}^{-2}\log M),

respectively, then the protocol can estimate
M
arbitrary linear functions
\Tr(O_{1}\rho),...\Tr(O_{M}\rho)
such that
\max_{i}\Tr(O_{i}^{2})\leq 1
, up to accuracy
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
with high success probability.

The rigorous version of Theorem
2
is Theorem
8
in Appendix
B
.
Compared with results in Ref.
Huang et al. 2020
, one can see that the
RShadow
scheme has nearly the same sample complexity order as the noise-free standard shadow estimation methods in a low-noise regime.

Finally, we comment on the computational complexity of
RShadow
.
The computational complexity of our calibration procedure is favorable since the single-round fidelity estimator can be calculated efficiently with the Gottesman-Knill theorem
Gottesman 1997
;
Aaronson and Gottesman 2004
.
However, a efficient computation using the Gottesman-Knill theorem for the estimation procedure would require the observable
O
to have additional structure, such as being a stabilizer state or being a Pauli operator.
The standard shadow estimation scheme of Ref.
Huang et al. 2020
or the fast Pauli expectation estimation method of Ref.
Evans et al. 2019
also have such a requirement.

V
Robust shadow estimation using local Clifford group

Despite the useful properties the global Clifford group possesses, it is often challenging to implement the full
n
-qubit Clifford group under current experimental conditions.
The local Clifford group
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
, which is the
n
-fold tensor product of the single-qubit Clifford group, is an experimentally friendly alternative.
We now present an robust shadow estimation protocol based on the local Clifford group which can efficiently calibrate and mitigate the error in estimating any
local property
.

It is known that the
n
-qubit local Clifford group has
2^{n}
irreducible representations
Gambetta et al. 2012
.
Being twirled by the local Clifford group, the channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
becomes a Pauli channel that is symmetric among the
x,y,z
indices, and the Pauli-Liouville representation is

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Lambda\mathcal{U}=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}}f_{z}\Pi_{z},

(15)

where
\Pi_{z}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\Pi_{z_{i}}
,

\Pi_{z_{i}}=\begin{cases}\ |\sigma_{0}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{0}|,&z_{i}=0,\\
\ I-|\sigma_{0}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{0}|,&z_{i}=1,\end{cases}

for
f_{z}\in\mathbb{R}
which is called the Pauli fidelity.
Here, for any string
m\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we define
|m\rangle\!\rangle
to be the Liouville representation of the computational basis state
\ket{m}\bra{m}
, and define
P_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}P_{Z}^{m_{i}}
and
\sigma_{m}
to be the corresponding normalized Pauli operators.
In the noiseless case, one can obtain
f_{z}=3^{-|z|}
using Eq. (
9
), where
|z|
is the number of
1
s in
z
.

The noise characterization subroutine with
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
is defined as follows

\text{{NoiseEst}}_{{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}}(z,U,b)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\langle\!\langle b|\mathcal{U}|P_{z}\rangle\!\rangle,~\forall z\in\{0,1\}^{n}.

(16)

In the standard shadow estimation using
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}

Huang et al. 2020
(and in the earlier work
Evans et al. 2019
), one can only efficiently estimate observables with small Pauli weight.
An
n
-qubit observable
O
is called
k
-local if it can be written as
O=\tilde{O}_{S}\otimes I_{[n]\backslash S}
for some
k
-element index set
S\subset[n]
and a
k
-qubit observable
\tilde{O}
.
Similarly, our
RShadow
protocol with
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
is also designed for predicting
k
-local observables.
The correctness and efficiency is given by the following theorem.

Theorem 3

(Informal)
.

For
RShadow
with
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
, if the number of samples for the calibration procedure satisfies

R=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left({3^{k}}{\varepsilon^{-2}F_{Z}^{-2}}\right),

(17)

then the subsequent estimation procedure with high probability satisfies

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}^{(r)})-\Tr(O\rho)\right|\leq\varepsilon 2^{k}\|O\|_{\infty},

(18)

for any
k
-local observable
O
and quantum state
\rho
, where
\hat{o}^{(r)}
is the single-round estimator defined as in Algorithm
1
.

The rigorous version of Theorem
3
is Theorem
9
in Appendix
C
.
Indeed, this protocol can calibrate the shadow estimation process for all
k
-local observables using a small number of samples that only depends on
k
(but basically not on the system size
n
).
Note that, Theorem
3
holds for any gate-independent noise model, even for
global unitary noise
.

Now we investigate the sample complexity of the estimation procedure.
We are currently unable to bound the sample complexity against the most general noise channel, but we do have a bound for a local noise model, as shown in the following theorem:

Theorem 4

(Informal)
.

For
RShadow
with
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
,
suppose the noise is local, i.e.
\Lambda\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\Lambda_{i}
, and satisfies
F_{Z}(\Lambda_{i})\geq 1-\xi
for all
i
and some
\xi\ll\frac{1}{2}
.
If the number of calibration samples
R_{C}
and the number of estimation samples
R_{E}
satisfy

\displaystyle R_{C}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(12^{k}e^{4k\xi}\varepsilon_{1}^{-2}),

(19)

\displaystyle R_{E}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(4^{k}e^{4k\xi}\varepsilon_{2}^{-2}\log M),

respectively,
then the protocol can estimate
M
arbitrary linear functions
\Tr(O_{1}\rho),...\Tr(O_{M}\rho)
such that every
O_{i}
is
k
-local and
\|O_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, up to accuracy
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
with high success probability.

The rigorous version of Theorem
4
is Theorem
10
in Appendix
C
.
Again, we see
RShadow
using
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
has a sample complexity similar to the noiseless standard shadow estimation protocol when the noise is local and not too strong.
We also remark that, although we do not have a sample complexity bound against a more general noise model, our numerical results show that
RShadow
can still perform well in that case (see Appendix
E
).
Furthermore, in realistic experiments, one can monitor the standard deviation of estimators in real time, which means they can still suppress statistical fluctuations to an acceptable level even without a theoretical sample complexity bound.

Regarding the computational complexity, it is obviously impractical to calibrate all
2^{n}
parameters
f_{z}
.
However, since we only care about
k
-local observables, only
\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)}
such that
|z|\leq k
needs to be computed, the number of which is no greater than
n^{k}
.
Further note that
\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)}
can be decomposed as
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\langle\!\langle b_{i}|U_{i}|P_{Z}^{z_{i}}\rangle\!\rangle
, so all these
\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)}
can be computed within
O(n^{k})
time using dynamic programming.
If there is extra structure of the observables to be predicted (
e.g.
spatially local), the number of necessary
\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)}
can be further reduced.
In practice, one may store the raw data of the calibration procedure and see what observables are to be predicted, before deciding which set of
f_{z}
need to be calculated.
An example is given below in our numerical experiments.
The computational complexity for the estimation procedure is therefore low when the observables are
k
-local for reasonably small
k
.

VI
Robustness against state preparation noise

In the last two sections, we prove the performance of the
RShadow
protocol based on the assumption of perfect
\ket{\bm{0}}
preparation.
Although
\ket{\bm{0}}
is relatively easy to prepare on most current quantum computing platforms, state preparation (SP) noise is still inevitable.
In this section, we show that the
RShadow
protocol is also robust against small SP noise in the following sense: when
\ket{\bm{0}}
can be prepared with high fidelity during the calibration procedure, the estimators for the estimation procedure will not be too biased, and the sample complexity will not increase drastically.

Formally, in a realistic calibration procedure, one prepares some
\rho_{\bm{0}}
instead of
\ket{\bm{0}}\bra{\bm{0}}
for each round.
We assume
\rho_{\bm{0}}
is time-independent, which is reasonable if the experimental conditions do not change much during this process.
We have the following theorems:

Theorem 5
.

For
RShadow
using
\sf{Cl}(2^{n})
, if the state-preparation fidelity satisfies

F(\ket{\bm{0}}\bra{\bm{0}},\rho_{\bm{0}})\geq 1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{SP}},

(20)

then with the same number of calibration samples as in Theorem
1
, the subsequent estimation procedure with high probability satisfies

\left|\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}^{(r)})-\Tr(O\rho)\right|\leq(\varepsilon+2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{SP}})\|O\|_{\infty}.

(21)

up to the first order of
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{SP}}
.

Theorem 6
.

For
RShadow
using
\sf{Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
, if the state is prepared as a product state
\rho_{\bm{0}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{0,i}
and the single-qubit state-preparation fidelity satisfies

F(\ket{0}\bra{0},\rho_{0,i})\geq 1-\xi_{\mathrm{SP}},\quad\forall i\in[n],

(22)

then with the same number of calibration samples as in Theorem
3
, the subsequent estimation procedure with high probability satisfies

\left|\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}^{(r)})-\Tr(O\rho)\right|\leq(\varepsilon+2k\xi_{SP})2^{k}\|O\|_{\infty}.

(23)

up to the first order of
\varepsilon
and
k\xi_{SP}
.
k
is the locality of observable
O
.

The proof is given in Appendix
D
.
The above two theorems show that the effect of state-preparation noise can indeed be bounded for
RShadow
. They also enable an experimentalist to decide a practical sample number according to how well his device can prepare
|\bm{0}\rangle\langle\bm{0}|
.

VIII
Gate-dependent noise

Perhaps the strongest assumption we made is the gate-independence of the noise channel
\Lambda
with respect to the unitary gate
U
being sampled.
In this section, we present numerical evidence showing that even with an experimentally realistic gate-dependent noise model,
RShadow
can still greatly reduce noise bias.
Throughout this section, we focus on
RShadow
with the local Clifford group, which is experimentally implementable on most near-term platforms.
The task we consider here is the
electronic structure problem
: decide the ground state energy of a molecule with an unknown electronic structure.
This is a important problem in quantum chemistry, and is viewed as one of the most promising applications of near-term quantum algorithms, see
e.g.

McArdle et al. 2020
.
Several recent works have already applied shadow estimation related methods to study this problem
Hadfield et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2021
.

Specifically, we choose a benchmark molecule and use a certain encoding scheme to map the molecular Hamiltonian into a qubit Hamiltonian.
Then, given the ground state of this Hamiltonian, we numerically run the (standard and robust) shadow estimation protocols to estimate its energy, and compare the estimation with the classically computed true value, in the presence of noise.
In our setting, we choose
\mathrm{H}_{2}
and apply the Bravyi-Kitaev encoding
Bravyi and Kitaev 2002
to map it to a
4
-qubit Hamiltonian.

To come up with a realistic gate-dependent noise model, we first need to decide how the local Clifford group is implemented on real experimental platforms.
One common approach is to decompose all unitary gates into a small set of generators.
Here, we consider the generating set consisting of the following three single-qubit generators

\left\{R_{P}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=\exp(-i\frac{\pi}{4}P),~P=X,Y,Z\right\}

(24)

which can be understood as
\pi/2
rotations along the X,Y,Z axes respectively.
Every single-qubit Clifford gate can be decomposed into two subsequent rotations along two out of these three axes.
For example, the Hadamard gate can be implemented by first applying a
\pi/2
rotation pulse along the Y axis, and then a
\pi
rotation pulse along the X axis, which is in turn implemented by concatenating two
\pi/2
X pulses.
(See App.
E.2
for more details.)
This generating set is wildly used in real experiments.

Our numerical simulations will deal with the following two kinds of errors that naturally appear in experiments:

1.

Pulse mis-calibration
:
The
\pi/2
-pulses have some fixed error due to
e.g.
an uncharacterized constant magnetic field.
These noisy generators would look like

\widetilde{R}_{P}=\exp\left(-i\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}P+\Delta_{0}\right)\right).

for
P=X,Y,Z
and some traceless Hermitian operator
\Delta_{0}
representing the uncalibrated Hamiltonian.
Although
\Delta_{0}
is the same for all three generators, the commutator
[P,\Delta_{0}]
is in general different for different
P
.
Thus, one can verify that this is a gate-dependent noise model by expanding
\widetilde{R}_{P}
using the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula.

2.

Random over-rotation
: Due to imperfect pulse control, the actual rotation angle for each generator could be modeled as
\pi/2+\delta
for some zero-mean Gaussian random variable
\delta
.
The noisy generators look like

\widetilde{R}_{P}=\exp\left(-i\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta\right)P\right).

We assume that the value of
\delta
is re-sampled every time a generator is applied.
One can verify that this noise model is equivalent to a dephasing noise on the eigenbasis of Pauli P following the noiseless generator
R_{P}
, thus it is a gate-dependent noise model.
(See App.
E.2
.)

These numerical results provide evidence for the advantage of
RShadow
over standard shadow estimation even with realistic gate-dependent noise.
Specifically, for
Pulse mis-calibration
noises, it seems that
RShadow
cannot eliminate all biases when the noise strength becomes very large.
(Note that, noise intensity
\|\Delta_{0}\|=0.1\pi

So why does
RShadow
work for these gate-dependent noise models?
One possible explanation is as follows.
The key subroutine of
RShadow
is to learn a Pauli channel.
Even though the noise has strong gate-dependence, if the
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
channel defined in Eq. (
15

|\bf 0\rangle\!\rangle
into an approximate complex projective 2-designs, then we expect
RShadow
to still perform well.
A more rigorous and comprehensive analysis of
RShadow
’s noise-resilience against general gate-dependent noises is left for future work.

IX
Concluding remarks

We have analyzed the shadow estimation protocol proposed in Ref.
Huang et al. 2020
by considering the gate and measurement errors occurring during the process, and have proposed a modified protocol that is robust against such noise.
We have proven that, in both the global and the local random Clifford group version of the robust shadow protocol, we can efficiently benchmark and suppress the effects caused by the noise.
On account of the broad application prospects of the shadow estimation protocol in predicting various important properties of quantum states, e.g., entanglement witness, fidelity estimation, correlation functions,
etc.
, we expect that our robust protocol is practical and feasible for current experiments.

While we only focus on estimating linear properties in this work,
RShadow
can also be used to calibrate the estimation of higher-order properties such as the subsystem R
\mathrm{\acute{e}}
nyi-2 entropy with similar methods shown in
Huang et al. 2020
.
An exploration into the corresponding sample complexity bound is left for future studies.
It is also interesting to explore how
RShadow
can be incorporated with other variants of shadow estimation such as the locally-biased classical shadow
Hadfield et al. 2020
and derandomized classical shadow
Huang et al. 2021
. These two methods can greatly improve the sample efficiency of shadow estimation when one has prior knowledge about which properties are to be predicted, like in the
electronic structure problem
. We believe these techniques can also be applied to
RShadow
and have been actively developing these methods.

The idea of using additional calibration processes and classical post-processing to eliminate noise effects also appears in the field of error mitigation
Temme et al. 2017
;
Endo et al. 2018
;
Maciejewski et al. 2020
;
Bravyi et al. 2021
.
Among them, it is particularly interesting to compare our work with Ref.
Maciejewski et al. 2020
and Ref.
Bravyi et al. 2021
, which mitigate the measurement readout error on multi-qubit devices using a measurement calibration (or detector tomography) process.
The spirit of these works is quite similar to ours, but their assumptions on the noise model are much stronger, and their calibration algorithm is more like a heuristic one without an explicit bound on the sample complexity.
One reason why we are able to obtain a useful sample complexity bound against a more general noise model is that the random twirling in
RShadow
greatly simplifies our analysis of the noise estimation.
For future research, it is interesting to explore the relationship between
RShadow
and other error mitigation schemes, and see if any general results for error mitigation
Takagi 2020
can be applied to our scenario.
Very recently, error mitigation has been shown to be helpful even for fault-tolerant quantum computing
Suzuki et al. 2020
.
We expect
RShadow
to be a useful protocol in the fault-tolerant regime as well.

For our performance guarantee of the robust shadow estimation protocol, the noise in the random gates is allowed to be coherent and highly correlated, but cannot depend on the unitary gate to be implemented.
This assumption is reasonable in many cases, especially in the protocol with local Clifford gates, where the noise is mainly caused by amplitude damping and decoherence of the system to the environment
Blais et al. 2021
.
Nevertheless, it is important to analyze how gate-dependency and non-Markovianity of the noise can affect the performance of
RShadow
.
We have provide some numerical evidences for
RShadow
’s resilience against gate-dependent noise in Sec.
VIII
, and left more rigorous analysis for future research.

In the PTM representation, the picture of quantum state shadow estimation can be easily extended to the shadow estimation of quantum measurements and quantum channels.
For example, in order to estimate
\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\mathcal{E}|\rho_{j}\rangle\!\rangle
for some unknown
n
-qubit quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
and a set of given observables
\{O_{i}\}
and states
\{\rho_{j}\}
, one may insert two random measurement channels into the expression,

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\mathcal{E}|\rho_{j}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{M}^{-1}|\rho_{j}\rangle\!\rangle

(25)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U,V\in\mathbb{G}}\sum_{x,y}\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}|x\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle x|\mathcal{U}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{V}|y\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle y|\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}^{-1}|\rho_{j}\rangle\!\rangle.

In the experiment, one can randomly prepare a computational basis state
\ket{y}
, apply a random unitary
V
and send to the channel
\mathcal{E}
, then apply another random unitary
U
and measure in the computational basis, getting outcome
\ket{x}
.
Then
2^{-n}\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}|x\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle y|\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}^{-1}|\rho_{j}\rangle\!\rangle
is an unbiased estimator of
\langle\!\langle O_{i}|\mathcal{E}|\rho_{j}\rangle\!\rangle
.
This is only the most straightforward way to extend robust shadow estimation to quantum channels; there may exist other schemes that have even better performance.
We believe a complete analysis of the channel version of shadow estimation will be an interesting direction for further study.

Finally, one can also consider applying (standard or robust) shadow estimation to qudit systems, Boson/Fermion systems and other continuous-variable systems using the techniques developed in this work.

A.1
Groups and representations

The group representation theory plays an important role in the shadow estimation protocol.
Denote a generic group as
\mathbb{G}=\{g_{i}\}_{i}
, where
g_{i}
is one of the group elements.
Denote a unitary representation of
\mathbb{G}
to be a map

\phi:\mathbb{G}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d}):\mathbb{G}\mapsto\phi(\mathbb{G}),

(26)

with the homomorphism

\phi(g)\phi(h)=\phi(gh),\forall g,h\in\mathbb{G}.

(27)

Moreover, we denote all the irreducible representations (irreps.) of the group
\mathbb{G}
as
R_{\mathbb{G}}=\{\phi_{\lambda}(\mathbb{G})\}_{\lambda}
.
The Maschke’s Lemma ensures that, every representation of a group can be written as a direct sum of irreps,

\phi(g)\backsimeq\bigoplus_{\lambda\in R_{\mathbb{G}}}\phi_{\lambda}(g)^{\otimes m_{\lambda}},\forall g\in\mathbb{G},

(28)

where
m_{\lambda}
is an integer implying the multiplicity of the irrep
\phi_{\lambda}
.

In the later discussion, we will frequently come across the
twirling
of an linear operator
O
on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with respect to a group representation
\phi(\mathbb{G})
,

\mathcal{T}_{\phi}(O)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{G}|}\sum_{g\in\mathbb{G}}\phi(g)O\phi(g)^{\dagger}.

(29)

As a result of the group structure
\mathbb{G}
, the twirling result
\mathcal{T}_{\phi}(O)
owns a simple structure, which is related to the irreps in
\phi(\mathbb{G})
.
The following lemma is a corollary of Schur’s lemma.

Lemma 1
.

(Lemma 1.7 and Prop 1.8 in
Fulton and Harris 2013
, rephrased by
Helsen et al. 2019
) For a finite group
\mathbb{G}
and a representation
\phi
of
\mathbb{G}
on a complex vector space
\mathcal{H}
with decomposition

\phi(g)\backsimeq\bigoplus_{\lambda\in R_{\mathbb{G}}}\phi_{\lambda}(g)^{\otimes m_{\lambda}},\forall g\in\mathbb{G},

(30)

where
\{\phi_{\lambda}\}
are the irreps of
\phi(\mathbb{G})
,
m_{\lambda}
is the multiplicity of
\phi_{\lambda}
.
Then for any linear map
O\in GL(\mathcal{H})
, the twirling of
O
with respect to
\phi
can be written as

\mathcal{T}_{\phi}(O)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{G}|}\sum_{g\in\mathbb{G}}\phi(g)O\phi(g)^{\dagger}=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{\mathbb{G}}}\sum_{j_{\lambda},j^{\prime}_{\lambda}=1}^{m_{\lambda}}\frac{\tr\left(O\Pi^{j^{\prime}_{\lambda}}_{j_{\lambda}}\right)}{\tr\left(\Pi^{j^{\prime}_{\lambda}}_{j_{\lambda}}\right)}\Pi^{j^{\prime}_{\lambda}}_{j_{\lambda}},

(31)

where
\Pi^{j^{\prime}_{\lambda}}_{j_{\lambda}}
is a linear map from the support of the
j^{\prime}_{\lambda}
-th copy of
\phi_{\lambda}
to the support of the
j_{\lambda}
-th copy of
\phi_{\lambda}
.

In this work, we focus on the group representation
\phi
with no multiplicities,

\phi(g)\backsimeq\bigoplus_{\lambda\in R_{\mathbb{G}}}\phi_{\lambda}(g),\quad\forall g\in\mathbb{G}.

(32)

In this case, Eq. (
31
) can be simplified as

\mathcal{T}_{\phi}(O)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{G}|}\sum_{g\in\mathbb{G}}\phi(g)O\phi(g)^{\dagger}=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{\mathbb{G}}}\frac{\tr(O\Pi_{\lambda})}{\tr(\Pi_{\lambda})}\Pi_{\lambda},

(33)

where
\Pi_{\lambda}
is the projector onto the support of
\phi_{\lambda}
.

Here, we introduce some common groups that will be frequently used.
Note that all the linear operators in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
form a Lie group
GL(d,\mathbb{C})
.
The unitaries in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
also form a Lie group
U(d)
.

Denote
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{0,1\}
to be the 2-element cyclic group.

\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=(\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{\otimes n}
is the
n
-copy tensor of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
group.
Denote
\mathbb{A}=\langle\{a_{i}\}\rangle
with
\{a_{i}\}
the generators of the group.
In the later discussion, we will also slightly abuse
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
to denote the set of
n
-bit binary string.

For
n
-qubit quantum system, the Pauli group is

\mathbb{P}^{n}=\{\langle i\rangle\otimes\{I,X,Y,Z\}\}^{\otimes n},

(34)

with
I,X,Y,Z
the qubit Pauli matrices.
Denote the quotient of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
to be
\mathsf{P}^{n}=\mathbb{P}^{n}/\langle i\rangle
, which is an Abelian group and isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.
Therefore, we will use a

-bit string to denote the elements in
\mathsf{P}^{n}
and choose the elements to be

P_{a}=P_{(a_{x},a_{z})}=i^{a_{x}\cdot a_{z}}X^{\otimes a_{x}}Z^{\otimes a_{z}}.

(35)

The multiplication and commutation of elements in
\mathsf{P}^{n}
follows,

\displaystyle P_{a}P_{b}

\displaystyle=(-i)^{\braket{a,b}}P_{a+b},

(36)

\displaystyle P_{a}P_{b}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\braket{a,b}}P_{b}P_{a},

with

\braket{a,b}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=a_{x}\cdot b_{z}-a_{z}\cdot b_{x}\quad\text{mod }4,

(37)

a binary symplectic product.
This symplectic product owns the following properties

\displaystyle\braket{a,b}

\displaystyle=-\braket{b,a},

(38)

\displaystyle(-i)^{\braket{a,b}}

\displaystyle(-1)^{\braket{a,b}}

A.2
Random unitaries and t-designs

The shadow estimation is a direct application of twirling in random unitaries. The ideal “uniformly distributed” randomized unitaries over the Lie group
GL(d,\mathbb{C})
is characterized by
Haar measure

\mu(\mathcal{H}_{d})

Collins and Nechita 2016
. The Haar measure is defined to be the unique countably additive, nontrivial measure of the group
U
such that,

\int_{\mu(\mathcal{H}_{d})}dU=1,\quad\int_{\mu(\mathcal{H}_{d})}dUf(U)=\int_{\mu(\mathcal{H}_{d})}dUf(UV)=\int_{\mu(\mathcal{H}_{d})}dUf(VU),

(40)

where
f(U)
is any matrix function of
U
.

In practice, to sample unitaries with respect to Haar measure is challenging due to its continuity. Alternatively, one may choose to sample from a finite subset
\mathcal{K}=\{U_{k}\}_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{K}|}
over the unitaries in
GL(d,\mathbb{C})
.

Definition 1
.

A finite subset
\mathcal{K}=\{U_{k}\}_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{K}|}\subset\mathcal{U}(d)
is a unitary
t
-design if

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}|}\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{K}|}f_{(t,t)}(U_{k})=\int_{\mu(\mathcal{H}_{d})}dUf_{(t,t)}(U),

(41)

for all the polynomial
f_{(t,t)}(U)
of degree at most
t
in the matrix elements of
U
and at most
t
in the matrix elements of
U^{*}
.

It has been proven that, the Clifford gate set
{\sf Cl}(d)\subset\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})
is a unitary
3
-design
Webb 2016
;
Zhu 2017
, while fails to be a unitary
4
-design
Zhu et al. 2016
.

A.3
Quantum channel and the representations

Quantum channels are the linear maps
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
which are completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP).

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
be a linear map. We say that

1.

\mathcal{E}
is positive if
\mathcal{E}(\rho)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
for any
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
.

2.

\mathcal{E}
is completely positive (CP) if
\mathcal{I}_{d^{\prime}}\otimes\mathcal{E}
is positive, for all the dimension
d^{\prime}
.

3.

\mathcal{E}
is trace preserving (TP) if
\tr[\mathcal{E}(\rho)]=1
for any
\tr[\rho]=1
.

4.

\mathcal{E}
is a quantum channel if it is both CP and TP.

In this work, we will come across two representations of the quantum channels : Kraus representation and Liouville representation. For a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
, its action on a linear operator
O\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
can be expressed as

\mathcal{E}(O)=\sum_{t=1}^{k}K_{t}OK_{t}^{\dagger},

(42)

where
\{K_{t}\}_{t=1}^{k}
are the Kraus operators satisfying
\sum_{t=1}^{k}K_{t}^{\dagger}K_{t}=I
.

To represent the effect of quantum channels in a convenient way, we first introduce the Pauli basis
P^{n}
on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
to vectorize the linear operators in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
. Define the inner product between two operators to be the Hilbert-Schmidt product

\braket{Q,W}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\tr(QW^{\dagger}),\quad\forall Q,W\in GL(\mathcal{H}_{d}).

(43)

In this case, the operators in
P^{n}
form an orthogonal basis. We introduce the operators

\sigma_{a}=P_{a}/\sqrt{d},

(44)

as the orthonormal basis. To vectorize the linear space spanned by
\{\sigma_{a}\}
, we introduce the notation
\{|\sigma_{a}\rangle\!\rangle\}
. For the single-qubit case, we will also use the following notations,

\displaystyle\sigma_{I}=\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{(0,0)},\quad\sigma_{X}=\sigma_{(1,0)},

(45)

\displaystyle\sigma_{Z}=\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{(0,1)},\quad\sigma_{Y}=\sigma_{(1,1)}.

Then the operators on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
can be vectorized as

|Q\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{a\in Z^{2n}_{2}}\langle\!\langle Q|\sigma_{a}\rangle\!\rangle|\sigma_{a}\rangle\!\rangle.

(46)

The quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
can then be represented as

\mathcal{E}=\sum_{a,b\in Z^{2n}_{2}}\langle\!\langle\sigma_{a}|\mathcal{E}|\sigma_{b}\rangle\!\rangle|\sigma_{a}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{b}|,

(47)

with

\langle\!\langle\sigma_{a}|\mathcal{E}|\sigma_{b}\rangle\!\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\braket{\sigma_{a},\mathcal{E}(\sigma_{b})}.

(48)

The matrix
\mathcal{E}
is the Pauli-transfer matrix (PTM) or Pauli-Liouville representation. In this work, we slightly abuse the notation of a superoperator
\mathcal{E}
to represent its PTM. For a unitary matrix
U
, we use the calligraphic
\mathcal{U}
to represent its PTM.

For a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
with state
\rho
input, and POVM measurement
M=\{M_{b}\}
with
\sum_{b}M_{b}=I
, the probability to get the measurement result
b
is

p_{b}=\langle\!\langle M_{b}|\mathcal{E}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle.

(49)

Under the PTM representation, the composition and tensor product of channels
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
can be naturally expressed as

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{1}\circ\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho)\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{1}\mathcal{E}_{2}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle,

(50)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho^{\otimes 2})\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2}|\rho^{\otimes 2}\rangle\!\rangle.

The PTM of the unitaries in
U(d)
forms a natural group representation of
U(d)
. Denote the PTM of a given unitary
U
as
\phi^{P}(U)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\mathcal{U}
, we have

\phi^{P}(U)\phi^{P}(V)=\phi^{P}(UV).

(51)

The PTM representation
\phi^{P}(U(d))
can be decomposed to two irreps,

\phi^{P}(U)\backsimeq\phi^{P}_{I}(U)\oplus\phi^{P}_{\sigma}(U),\quad\forall U\in U(d).

(52)

Here,

\displaystyle\phi^{P}_{I}(U)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{I}\,\phi^{P}(U)\,\Pi_{I},

(53)

\displaystyle\phi^{P}_{I}(U)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\sigma}\,\phi^{P}(U)\,\Pi_{\sigma},

the projectors
\Pi_{I}
and
\Pi_{\sigma}
are

\displaystyle\Pi_{I}

\displaystyle=|\sigma_{0}^{\otimes n}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{0}^{\otimes n}|,

(54)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=I-\Pi_{I}=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n},\;a\neq(0,0)^{\otimes n}}|\sigma_{a}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{a}|.

The
n
-qubit Clifford group
{\sf Cl}(2^{n})
, as the subset of
n
-qubit unitary group, can also be represented by the PTM matrices. The PTM representation
\phi^{P}({\sf Cl}(2^{n}))
can be decomposed similarly,

\phi^{P}(U)\backsimeq\phi^{P}_{I}(U)\oplus\phi^{P}_{\sigma}(U),\quad\forall U\in{\sf Cl}(2^{n}),

(55)

where
\phi^{P}_{I}
and
\phi^{P}_{\sigma}
are two irreps on the support
\Pi_{I}
and
\Pi_{\sigma}
, respectively.

In following sections, we are interested in the case

\vec{W}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\begin{bmatrix}W_{()},&W_{(1,2)},&W_{(1,3)},&W_{(2,3)},&W_{(1,2,3)},&W_{(1,3,2)}\end{bmatrix}.

\mathcal{M}^{-1}
replaced by

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\left[\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\hat{f}^{-1}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\hat{f}^{-1}\\
\end{matrix}\right]

in the Liouville representation.

In Protocol
1
, the unitary operations and the measurement are assumed to contain gate-independent noise, and the preparation of
\ket{\bm{0}}
is assumed to be perfect. The next theorem shows that
\hat{f}^{(r)}
is an unbiased estimator of
f
and its variance can be bounded.

Proposition 1
.

The single-round fidelity estimator
\hat{F}^{(r)}
given in Protocol
1
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(\hat{F}^{(r)})=F_{\text{avg}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})=\cfrac{F_{Z}(\Lambda)+1}{d+1},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\hat{F}^{(r)})\leq\cfrac{2}{d^{2}},

(65)

where
F_{\text{avg}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})=\int_{\psi\in\text{Haar}}d\psi\langle\!\langle\psi|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\psi\rangle\!\rangle
is the average fidelity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
, and
F_{Z}(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\langle\!\langle b|\Lambda|b\rangle\!\rangle
is the Z-basis average fidelity of
\Lambda
.

Moreover, the single-round estimator
\hat{f}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}^{(r)})=f=\cfrac{dF_{Z}(\Lambda)-1}{d^{2}-1},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}^{(r)})\leq\cfrac{2}{(d-1)^{2}}.

(66)

Before we provide the proof of Proposition
1
, we first introduce two lemmas.

Lemma 2
.

(see e.g.
(
Zhu et al. 2016
, Proposition 4)
)
If a group
\mathbb{G}\subseteq U(d)
forms a unitary
t
-design, then

\mathop{\mathbb{}}E_{U\sim\mathbb{G}}(U|\bm{0}\rangle\langle\bm{0}|U^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}=\cfrac{P_{\text{sym}^{t}}}{\binom{d+t-1}{t}},

(67)

where
P_{\text{sym}^{t}}
is the projector onto the
t
-fold symmetric space, or equivalently,
P_{\text{sym}^{t}}=\frac{1}{|S_{t}|}\sum_{\pi\in S_{t}}{W_{\pi}}
where
W_{\pi}
is the permutation operator defined in Eq. (
58
).

Lemma 3
.

For two operators
A
,
B
acting on
\mathcal{H}(d)
,

\displaystyle\tr(P_{\text{sym}^{2}}A\otimes B)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\tr A\tr B+\tr(AB))

(68)

\displaystyle\tr(P_{\text{sym}^{3}}A\otimes B\otimes B)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}(\tr A(\tr B)^{2}+\tr A\tr(B^{2})+2\tr(AB)\tr B+2\tr(AB^{2})).

(69)

Proof of Lemma
3
.

For the first equation,

\Tr(P_{\text{sym}^{2}}A\otimes B)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\Tr(I(A\otimes B))+\Tr(S(A\otimes B))\right]=\frac{1}{2}(\tr A\tr B+\tr(AB)),

(70)

where
S
is the swap operator.

For the second equation, using the language of tensor network (see
e.g.

(
Gross et al. 2015
, Sec. 3.1)
), we can derive,

\Tr(\vec{W}(A\otimes B\otimes B))=\begin{bmatrix}\Tr A(\Tr B)^{2},&\Tr(AB)\Tr B,&\Tr(AB)\Tr B,&\Tr A\Tr(B^{2}),&\Tr(AB^{2}),&\Tr(AB^{2})\end{bmatrix},

(71)

where
\vec{W}
is a vectorization of
S_{3}
defined in Eq. (
60
). Averaging this up gives the second equation.
∎

Now we present the proof of Proposition
1
.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Firstly, from Eq. (
64
) we immediately have

f=\cfrac{\tr(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})-1}{d^{2}-1}.

(72)

We also have the following relation between the average fidelity of a channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
and the trace of its Pauli transformer matrix (see
e.g.

Helsen et al. 2019
),

F_{\text{avg}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})=\cfrac{d^{-1}\tr(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})+1}{d+1}.

(73)

Combining the above two equations, we get

f=\cfrac{dF_{\text{avg}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})-1}{d-1},

(74)

hence Eq. (
66
) follows directly from Eq. (
65
). We only need to calculate the expectation and variance of
\hat{F}^{(r)}
.

Denote the Kraus operators of the noise channel
\Lambda
as
\{K_{t}\}
. The average fidelity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
can be explicitly written as follows

\displaystyle F_{\text{avg}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})

\displaystyle=\int_{d\psi}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim Cl}\bra{\psi}U^{\dagger}\circ M_{z}\circ\Lambda\circ U\left(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\right)\ket{\psi}

(75)

\displaystyle=\int_{d\psi}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim Cl}\sum_{b,t}\bra{\psi}U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|K_{t}U|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|U^{\dagger}K_{t}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|U\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\int_{d\psi}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim Cl}\sum_{b,t}\left|\bra{b}K_{t}U\ket{\psi}\right|^{2}\left|\bra{b}U\ket{\psi}\right|^{2}.

On the other hand, the expectation of
\hat{F}^{(r)}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{F}^{(r)})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim Cl}\sum_{b,t}\left|\bra{b}K_{t}U\ket{\bm{0}}\right|^{2}\left|\bra{b}U\ket{\bm{0}}\right|^{2}

(76)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{V\sim Cl}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{W\sim Cl}\sum_{b,t}\left|\bra{b}K_{t}VW\ket{\bm{0}}\right|^{2}\left|\bra{b}VW\ket{\bm{0}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{d\psi}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{V\sim Cl}\sum_{b,t}\left|\bra{b}K_{t}V\ket{\psi}\right|^{2}\left|\bra{b}V\ket{\psi}\right|^{2},

where the first equality is by definition of expectation, the second equality is by the fact that sampling an element
U
from a group is equivalent to independently sampling two elements
V
,
W
from the group and taking
U=V\circ W
, and the last equality uses the fact that
{\sf Cl}(2^{n})
is a unitary 2-design. As a result, we’ve shown that

\mathbb{E}(\hat{F}^{(r)})=F_{\text{avg}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}).

(77)

Next, in order to get
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{F}^{(r)})
, we calculate the value of
\mathbb{E}(\hat{F}^{(r)})
and
\mathbb{E}(\hat{F}^{(r)^{2}})
explicitly. Based on Lemma
2
and
3
, and recalling the fact that
{\sf Cl}(2^{n})
is a unitary 3-design
Zhu 2017
;
Webb 2016
;
Kueng and Gross 2015
, we are able to do the following calculations.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{F}^{(r)})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim Cl}\sum_{b,t}\left|\bra{b}K_{t}U\ket{\bm{0}}\right|^{2}\left|\bra{b}U\ket{\bm{0}}\right|^{2}

(78)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b,t}\tr\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim Cl}\left(U|\bm{0}\rangle\langle\bm{0}|U^{\dagger}\right)^{\otimes 2}~\left(K_{t}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|K_{t}\otimes|b\rangle\langle b|\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\cfrac{2}{(d+1)d}~\sum_{b,t}\tr\left[P_{\text{sym}^{2}}~\left(K_{t}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|K_{t}\otimes|b\rangle\langle b|\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\cfrac{2}{(d+1)d}~\sum_{b,t}\cfrac{1}{2}\left(\bra{b}K_{t}K_{t}^{\dagger}\ket{b}+\left|\bra{b}K_{t}\ket{b}\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{(d+1)d}~\left(d+\sum_{b,t}\left|\bra{b}K_{t}\ket{b}\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1+F_{Z}}{d+1}~,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{F}^{(r)^{2}})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim Cl}\sum_{b,t}\left|\bra{b}K_{t}U\ket{\bm{0}}\right|^{2}\left|\bra{b}U\ket{\bm{0}}\right|^{4}

(79)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b,t}\tr\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim Cl}\left(U|\bm{0}\rangle\langle\bm{0}|U^{\dagger}\right)^{\otimes 3}~\left(K_{t}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|K_{t}\otimes|b\rangle\langle b|\otimes|b\rangle\langle b|\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{(d+2)(d+1)d}\sum_{b,t}\tr\left[P_{\text{sym}^{3}}~\left(K_{t}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|K_{t}\otimes|b\rangle\langle b|\otimes|b\rangle\langle b|\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{(d+2)(d+1)d}\sum_{b,t}\frac{1}{3}\left(\bra{b}K_{t}K_{t}^{\dagger}\ket{b}+2|\bra{b}K_{t}\ket{b}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(1+2F_{Z})}{(d+2)(d+1)},

where we write
F_{Z}\equiv F_{Z}(\Lambda)
as the Z-basis average fidelity of
\Lambda
.

Now we can bound the variance of
\hat{F}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{F}^{(r)})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\hat{F}^{(r)^{2}})-(\mathbb{E}(\hat{F}^{(r)}))^{2}

(80)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{-(d+2)F_{Z}^{2}+2dF_{Z}+d}{(d+2)(d+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{d^{2}}.

where we use the fact that
F_{Z}\leq 1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now we analyse the sample complexity of Protocol
1
in order to guarantee the protocol to succeed within a given level of precision. Specifically, we consider using the protocol to estimate a linear function of
\rho
,
i.e.

\langle\!\langle O|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
. Given that one makes sufficiently many samples in the estimation procedure, the estimation of this function will be close to
\langle\!\langle O|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
. Hence, we are concerned about the following error

\displaystyle\left|\langle\!\langle O|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle O|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\right|

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\langle\!\langle O|\left[\begin{matrix}0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\hat{f}^{-1}f-1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\hat{f}^{-1}f-1\\
\end{matrix}\right]|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\right|.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\langle\!\langle O_{0}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\right|\cdot\left|\hat{f}^{-1}f-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|O_{0}\|_{\infty}\cdot\left|\hat{f}^{-1}f-1\right|,

where
O_{0}=O-\cfrac{\tr(O)}{d}I
is the traceless part of
O
.
Now we want to upper bound
|\hat{f}^{-1}f-1|
by some
\varepsilon>0
.
Suppose with high probability the estimator in Protocol
1
satisfies
|\hat{f}-f|\leq\gamma
for some
0\leq\gamma\leq|f|
. Then we have,

|\hat{f}^{-1}f-1|=|\hat{f}^{-1}|\cdot|\hat{f}-f|\leq\cfrac{\gamma}{|\hat{f}|}\leq\cfrac{\gamma}{|f|-\gamma}

(82)

where the last inequality is by the triangular inequality. Now if we have

\cfrac{\gamma}{|f|-\gamma}\leq\varepsilon\Longleftrightarrow\gamma\leq\cfrac{\varepsilon|f|}{1+\varepsilon},

(83)

then we obtain the bound
|\hat{f}^{-1}f-1|\leq\varepsilon
with high success probability.
Now is the time to calculate the number of rounds
R
in order to bound
|\hat{f}-f|
as we want with high confidence. As noted before, we will uses the median of means estimator
Jerrum et al. 1986
;
Nemirovsky and Yudin 1983
in order to get a preferable scaling with respect to the failing probability. Similar techniques are also applied in
Huang et al. 2020
. Specifically, we conduct
R=KN
rounds of the procedure in Protocol
1
, calculate
K
estimators each of which is the average of
N
single-round estimators
\hat{f}
, and take the median of these
K
estimators as our final estimator
\hat{f}
. In formula,

\displaystyle\bar{f}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\cfrac{1}{N}\sum_{r=(k-1)N+1}^{kN}\hat{f}^{(r)},\quad k=1,2,...,K.

(84)

\displaystyle\hat{f}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\text{median}\left\{\bar{f}^{(1)},~\bar{f}^{(2)},~...,~\bar{f}^{(K)}\right\}.

The performance of this estimator is given in the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

(
Jerrum et al. 1986
;
Nemirovsky and Yudin 1983
, rephrased by
Huang et al. 2020
)
For the estimator described by Eq. (
84
) where
\hat{f}^{(r)}
is identical and independent sample of
f
, if
N=34\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f})/\gamma^{2}
for any given
\gamma>0
, then

\Pr\left(\left|\hat{f}-\mathbb{E}\hat{f}\right|\geq\gamma\right)\leq 2\exp(-K/2).

(85)

Further, by taking
K=2\ln(2\delta^{-1})
for any
\delta>0
, one have

\Pr\left(\left|\hat{f}-\mathbb{E}\hat{f}\right|\geq\gamma\right)\leq\delta.

(86)

Thanks to this Lemma and the above discussion, we reach the following theorem which summarize the trade-off between precision and the sample complexity of our main protocol. This theorem is the rigorous version of Theorem
1
in the main text.

Theorem 7
.

Given
\varepsilon,~\delta>0
, the following number of rounds of calibration in Protocol
1

R=136\ln(2\delta^{-1})\cfrac{(1+{\varepsilon}^{2})(1+\frac{1}{d})^{2}}{{\varepsilon}^{2}(F_{Z}-\frac{1}{d})^{2}}

(87)

is enough for the asymptotic error of the subsequent estimation procedure to satisfy

\left|\langle\!\langle O|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle O|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\right|\leq\varepsilon\|O_{0}\|_{\infty},\quad\forall O\in\mathrm{Herm}(2^{n}),~\forall\rho\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n}).

(88)

with a success probability at least
1-\delta
, where

F_{Z}\equiv F_{Z}(\Lambda)

is the Z-basis average fidelity of the noise channel
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Construct the median of means estimator
\hat{f}
with
K=2\ln(2\delta^{-1})
and
N=34\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f})/\gamma^{2}
, where
\gamma=\cfrac{\varepsilon|f|}{1+\varepsilon}
as Eq. (
82
) suggests. Use Proposition
1
to get

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f})\leq\cfrac{2}{(d-1)^{2}},\qquad|f|=\cfrac{dF_{Z}-1}{d^{2}-1}.

(89)

Then Lemma
4
guarantees

R=KN=136\ln(2\delta^{-1})\cfrac{(1+{\varepsilon}^{2})(d+1)^{2}}{{\varepsilon}^{2}(dF_{Z}-1)^{2}}.

(90)

∎

Theorem
7
provides an upper bound on the necessary number of rounds that scales as

R=O\left(\cfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}(F_{Z}-1/d)^{2}}\right).

(91)

B.2
Estimation Procedure: Global

Till now, we have proved the efficiency of the calibration procedure, but have not addressed the efficiency of the estimation procedure. In the noiseless case, the performance of the standard quantum shadow estimation protocol has been characterized in
Huang et al. 2020
. Here, we extend their methods to show the performance of the
RShadow
estimation procedure.

For any set of observables
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and an unknown state
\rho
, the single-round estimation and the final estimation of
o_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\Tr(O_{i}\rho)
is denoted by
\hat{o}_{i}^{(r)}
and
\hat{o}_{i}
respectively, given by Algorithm
2
. The deviation of
\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}_{i}^{(r)})
from
o_{i}
has been bounded by Theorem
7
. Now we want to bound
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{o}_{i}^{(r)})
. We first introduce the following lemma,

Lemma 5
.

For any
O\in\mathrm{Herm}(2^{n})
and an unknown state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n})
, the single-round estimator
\hat{o}^{(r)}
given by the
RShadow
protocol using either
{\sf Cl}(2^{n})
or
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
satisfies

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{o}^{(r)})\leq\|O_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}

(92)

where
O_{0}\equiv O-\frac{\Tr(O)}{2^{n}}I
. The function
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}
depends on the noise channel and the unitary group being used:

\|O\|_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n})}\left(\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathbb{G}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\bra{b}\Lambda(U\sigma U^{\dagger})\ket{b}\bra{b}U\widetilde{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)U^{\dagger}\ket{b}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(93)

When
\Lambda=\mathrm{id}
, the function
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}
degrades to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}
defined in
Huang et al. 2020
.

Proof.

First observe that the variance of
\hat{o}^{(r)}
from Algorithm
2
only depends on the traceless part of
O
:

\displaystyle\hat{o}^{(r)}-\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}^{(r)})

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle O|\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle O|\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle

(94)

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle O_{0}|\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle O_{0}|\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{o}_{0}^{(r)}-\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}_{0}^{(r)})

which is because
\widehat{}\mathcal{M}
is diagonal in Pauli transfer matrix representation, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is a trace-preserving map. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{o}^{(r)})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{o}^{(r)}-\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}^{(r)})\right)^{2}\right]

(95)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\langle\!\langle O_{0}|\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle O_{0}|\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}~\langle\!\langle O_{0}|\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathbb{G}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\langle\!\langle b|\Lambda{\mathcal{U}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle b|{\mathcal{U}}\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}|O_{0}\rangle\!\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n})}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathbb{G}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\langle\!\langle b|\Lambda{\mathcal{U}}|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle b|{\mathcal{U}}\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}|O_{0}\rangle\!\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\|O_{0}\|_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}^{2}.

∎

In the special case that
\mathbb{G}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu={\sf Cl}(2^{n})
, we can obtain the following bound on the shadow norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}
.

Lemma 6
.

For
RShadow
using
{\sf Cl}(2^{n})
, if the calibration procedure guarantees
\hat{f}\geq\delta f
for some
\delta>0
, and we assume
F_{Z}(\Lambda)\geq\frac{1}{d}
, then we have

\|O_{0}\|_{\mathrm{shaodw},\Lambda}^{2}\leq\delta^{-2}\left(F_{Z}-\frac{1}{d}\right)^{-2}~3\Tr(O_{0}^{2}),

(96)

for any observable
O
.

Proof.

From the definition of the noisy shadow norm and using the Weingarten functions from Eq. (
57
) we have

\displaystyle\|O_{0}\|_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n})}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}(2^{n})}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\hat{f}^{-2}\Tr\left[(U\sigma U^{\dagger}\otimes UO_{0}U^{\dagger}\otimes UO_{0}U^{\dagger})(\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b})\right]

(97)

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n})}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\hat{f}^{-2}\Tr\left[\Phi^{(3)}_{\mathrm{Haar}}(\sigma\otimes O_{0}\otimes O_{0})(\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b})\right]

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n})}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\hat{f}^{-2}\sum_{\pi,\xi\in S_{3}}c_{\pi,\xi}\Tr\left[W_{\pi}(\sigma\otimes O_{0}\otimes O_{0})\right]\Tr\left[W_{\xi}(\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b})\right]\,,

where in the last equation we use the Weingarten function to expand the Haar intergral (see Eq. (
57
)). Now using Eq. (
71
) to compute the traces appearing above, we have

\Tr\left(\vec{W}(\sigma\otimes O_{0}\otimes O_{0})\right)=\begin{bmatrix}0,&0,&0,&\Tr(O_{0}^{2}),&\Tr(\sigma O_{0}^{2}),&\Tr(\sigma O_{0}^{2})\end{bmatrix}.

(98)

Recall that
F_{Z}(\Lambda)
is the Z-basis average fidelity of
\Lambda
as defined in Prop.
1
, and we denote it simply as
F_{Z}
in the following.
Then we also have

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\Tr\left(\vec{W}(\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b})\right)

(99)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\left[\begin{matrix}\Tr(\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})),&\bra{b}\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\ket{b},&\bra{b}\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\ket{b},&\Tr(\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})),&\bra{b}\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\ket{b},&\bra{b}\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\ket{b}\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d*\begin{bmatrix}1,&F_{Z}(\Lambda),&F_{Z}(\Lambda),&1,&F_{Z}(\Lambda),&F_{Z}(\Lambda)\end{bmatrix}.

Again,
\vec{W}
is a vectorization of
S_{3}
defined in Eq. (
60
), just for the simplicity of notation.
Inserting the above two equations and the value of the Weingarten matrix from Eq. (
62
),

\displaystyle\|O_{0}\|_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n})}\hat{f}^{-2}\frac{\Tr(O_{0}^{2})(d-2F_{Z}+1)+2\Tr(\sigma O_{0}^{2})(dF_{Z}-1)}{(d+2)(d^{2}-1)}

(100)

\displaystyle\leq\hat{f}^{-2}~\frac{2dF_{Z}+d-2F_{Z}-1}{(d+2)(d^{2}-1)}\Tr(O_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{2}}{\hat{f}^{2}}\left(\frac{d^{2}-1}{dF_{Z}-1}\right)^{2}\frac{2dF_{Z}+d-2F_{Z}-1}{(d+2)(d^{2}-1)}\Tr(O_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f^{2}}{\hat{f}^{2}}~\left(F_{Z}-\frac{1}{d}\right)^{-2}~3\Tr(O_{0}^{2})

where the first inequality is by the fact that
\Tr(\sigma O^{2}_{0})\leq\|O^{2}_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\Tr(O_{0}^{2})
and the assumption
F_{Z}\geq\frac{1}{d}
, and in the second equality we use the expression of
f
from Proposition
1
.
∎

Compared to Proposition 1 from
Huang et al. 2020
which states that
\|O_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{shadow}}\leq 3\Tr(O_{0}^{2})
, we conclude the following.
As long as the noise channel
\Lambda
has a Z-basis fidelity that is not too low and the noise calibration procedure is conducted with sufficiently many rounds, then the estimation procedure of our
RShadow
protocol using
{\sf Cl}(2^{n})
is as efficient as the noiseless standard quantum shadow estimation protocol
Huang et al. 2020
up to a small multiplicative factor.
That is to say, the expectation value of any observable
O
that has small Hilbert-Schmidt norm can be efficiently estimated by
RShadow
.

To complete the discussion, we give the following theorem as a rigorous version of Theorem
2
in the main text.

Theorem 8
.

For
RShadow
with
{\sf Cl}(2^{n})
, given the noise channel satisfies
F_{Z}(\Lambda)\geq\frac{1}{d}
, if the number of calibration samples
R_{C}
and the number of estimation samples
R_{E}
satisfies

\displaystyle R_{C}

\displaystyle=136\ln(2\delta_{1}^{-1})\cfrac{(1+{\varepsilon_{1}}^{2})(1+\frac{1}{d})^{2}}{{\varepsilon_{1}}^{2}(F_{Z}-\frac{1}{d})^{2}},

(101)

\displaystyle R_{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{204}{\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}\ln(2M/\delta_{2})(1+\varepsilon_{1})^{2}(F_{Z}-\frac{1}{d})^{-2},

respectively, then the protocol can estimate
M
arbitrary linear functions
\Tr(O_{1}\rho),...\Tr(O_{M}\rho)
such that
\Tr(O_{i}^{2})\leq 1
, up to accuracy
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
with success probability at least
1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}
.

Proof.

First, according to Theorem
7
, for the given number of samples
R_{C}
one have

\left|\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}_{i}^{(r)})-\Tr(O_{i}\rho)\right|\leq\varepsilon_{1}.

(102)

Meanwhile, from the proof of Theorem
7
(see Eq. (
82
)), one also have

|\hat{f}^{-1}f-1|\leq\varepsilon_{1}\Rightarrow\hat{f}\geq(1+\varepsilon_{1})^{-1}f.

(103)

Both of the above equations hold simultaneously with probability at least
1-\delta_{1}
.

Now, by Lemma
5
and Lemma
6
, the single-round estimators in the estimation procedure satisfy:

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{o}_{i}^{(r)})\leq 3(1+\varepsilon_{1})^{2}(F_{Z}-\frac{1}{d})^{-2}.

(104)

So we set the median of mean estimators
\hat{o}_{i}
of the estimation procedure with the following parameters:

N=\frac{34}{\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}\cdot 3(1+\varepsilon_{1})^{2}(F_{Z}-\frac{1}{d})^{-2},\quad K=2\ln(2M/\delta_{2}).

(105)

Then Lemma
4
combined with the union bound gives that the following holds for all
i
with probability at least
1-\delta_{2}
:

\left|\hat{o}_{i}-\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}_{i}^{(r)})\right|\leq\varepsilon_{2},

(106)

Combining Eq. (
102
) and Eq. (
106
) using the triangular inequality gives

|\hat{o}_{i}-\Tr(O_{i}\rho)|\leq\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2},

(107)

which holds with probability at least
1-\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Sample Complexity of
RShadow
with Local Clifford Group

The result in App.
B
is based on the
n
-qubit Clifford group, which is challenging to implement in experiment. In this section, we analyze the protocol using
n
-qubit local Clifford group, denoted as
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
, which is the
n
-fold direct product of the single-qubit Clifford group. Such unitaries are all single-qubit operations, thus much easier to implement in the experiment.

C.1
Calibration Procedure: Local

Being twirled by the local Clifford group, the channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
becomes a Pauli channel that is symmetric among the
X,Y,Z
index, whose Pauli-Liouville representation is
Gambetta et al. 2012

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}M_{z}\Lambda\mathcal{U}=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}}f_{z}\Pi_{z},

(108)

where
\Pi_{z}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\Pi_{z_{i}}
,

\Pi_{z_{i}}=\begin{cases}&|\sigma_{0}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{0}|,\quad z_{i}=0,\\
&I-|\sigma_{0}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{0}|,\quad z_{i}=1,\end{cases}

(109)

and
f_{z}
is the Pauli fidelity. In the noiseless case, one can obtain
f_{z}=3^{-|z|}
where
|z|
is the number of
1
in
z
.

Notation:
For any string
m\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we define
|m\rangle\!\rangle
to be the Liouville representation of the computational basis state
\ket{m}
, while
|\sigma_{m}\rangle\!\rangle
stands for the normalized Pauli operator corresponding to
P_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}P_{Z}^{m_{i}}
. On the other hand, the notation of
z
in this section consistently stands for an
n
-bit string and should not be confused with the Pauli-Z index.

The
RShadow
protocol using local Clifford group can be written as follows.

Protocol 2
.

[
RShadow
with
Cl_{2}^{\otimes n}
]

1.

Prepare
\ket{\bm{0}}\equiv\ket{0}^{\otimes n}
. Sample
U
uniformly form
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
and apply it to
\ket{\bm{0}}
.

2.

Measure the above state in the computational basis. Denote the outcome state vector as
\ket{b}
.

3.

Calculate the single-round Pauli fidelity estimator

\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z}=\langle\!\langle b|{\mathcal{U}}|P_{z}\rangle\!\rangle

for all
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

4.

Repeat step 1–3 for
R=NK
rounds. Then the final estimation of
f_{z}
is given by a median of means estimator
\hat{f}_{z}
constructed from the single round estimators
\{\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{R}
with parameter
N,~K
(see Eq. (
84
)).

5.

After the above steps, apply the standard shadow estimation protocol of
Huang et al. 2020
on
\rho
, with the inverse channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}
replaced by

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}=\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\hat{f}^{-1}_{z}\Pi_{z},

(110)

Of course, it is unaffordable in classical computational resource to compute all
\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)}
in a single round. In practice, we only need to compute those
f_{z}
of interest. For example, if we only want to predict
k
-local properties, then only
\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)}
such that
|z|\leq k
need to be computed. If we are only interested in nearby qubits, then the number of necessary
\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)}
can be further reduced.

Now we show that the single-round estimators
\{\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z}\}
are unbiased and the variance of them are bounded.

Proposition 2
.

The single-round Pauli fidelity estimator
\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z})=f_{z}=3^{-|z|}~\Gamma_{\Lambda}(z),\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z})\leq 3^{-|z|}.

(111)

where
\Gamma_{\Lambda}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\cfrac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x,b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{z\cdot(x\oplus b)}\langle\!\langle b|\Lambda|x\rangle\!\rangle
.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

To begin with, we show that
\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z}
is an unbiased estimator of
f_{z}
.
From the definition of
\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z}
in Protocol
2
above, we have that the expectation value over the experiments is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}}\sum_{b}\langle\!\langle P_{z}|{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle b|\Lambda{\mathcal{U}}|\bm{0}\rangle\!\rangle

(112)

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle P_{z}|\tilde{M}|\bm{0}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=f_{z}\langle\!\langle P_{z}|\bm{0}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=f_{z}.

To derive the expression for
\hat{f}_{z}
that depends on the noise channel
\Lambda
, we can alternatively expand the expectation as follows,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}}\sum_{b}\bra{b}\Lambda\left(U|\bm{0}\rangle\langle\bm{0}|U^{\dagger}\right)\ket{b}\tr\left[U^{\dagger}\ket{b}\bra{b}UP_{z}\right]

(113)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\tr\left[\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}}\left(U\ket{\bm{0}}\bra{\bm{0}}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{z}U^{\dagger}\right)\left(\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\right)\right].

To evaluate this expression, we first consider the single-qubit case.
By direct calculation we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}}\left(U\ket{0}\bra{0}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{I}U^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{2}I,

(114)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}}\left(U\ket{0}\bra{0}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{Z}U^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{2}{3}P_{\text{sym}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}I.

Hence, for any
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(2)
and
b\in\{0,1\}
, by Lemma
3
,

\displaystyle\tr\left[\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}}\left(U\ket{0}\bra{0}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{I}U^{\dagger}\right)\left(X\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\bra{b}X\ket{b}+\bra{b\oplus 1}X\ket{b\oplus 1})

(115)

\displaystyle\tr\left[\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}}\left(U\ket{0}\bra{0}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{Z}U^{\dagger}\right)\left(X\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}(\bra{b}X\ket{b}-\bra{b\oplus 1}X\ket{b\oplus 1}).

Applying this to the
n
-qubit case, one can then verify that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z})=\frac{1}{3^{|z|}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x,b}(-1)^{z\cdot(x\oplus b)}\bra{x}\Lambda^{\dagger}(|b\rangle\langle b|)\ket{x}=\frac{1}{3^{|z|}}\Gamma_{\Lambda}(z).

(116)

To compute the variance, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}^{(r)^{2}}_{z})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}}\sum_{b}\bra{b}\Lambda\left(U|\bm{0}\rangle\langle\bm{0}|U^{\dagger}\right)\ket{b}\tr\left[U^{\dagger}\ket{b}\bra{b}UP_{z}\right]^{2}

(117)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\tr\left[\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}}\left(U\ket{\bm{0}}\bra{\bm{0}}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{z}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{z}U^{\dagger}\right)\left(\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\right)\right].

Again, first consider the single-qubit case. One can verify that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}}\left(U\ket{0}\bra{0}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{I}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{I}U^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}I,

(118)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}}\left(U\ket{0}\bra{0}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{Z}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{Z}U^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}P_{\text{sym}^{3}}+\frac{1}{3}\left(P_{\text{sym}^{2}}^{(2,3)}-P_{\text{sym}^{2}}^{(1,2)}-P_{\text{sym}^{2}}^{(1,3)}\right).

Hence, for any
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(2)
and
b\in\{0,1\}
, by Lemma
3
,

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}}\left(U\ket{0}\bra{0}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{I}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{I}U^{\dagger}\right)(X\otimes|b\rangle\langle b|\otimes|b\rangle\langle b|)\right]=\frac{1}{2}\tr(X),

(119)

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}}\left(U\ket{0}\bra{0}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{Z}U^{\dagger}\otimes UP_{Z}U^{\dagger}\right)(X\otimes|b\rangle\langle b|\otimes|b\rangle\langle b|)\right]=\frac{1}{6}\tr(X).

One can also verify these equations using the Weingarten matrix. Applying to the
n
-qubit case, one can verify that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}^{(r)^{2}}_{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\frac{1}{3^{|z|}}\sum_{b}\tr(\Lambda^{\dagger}(|b\rangle\langle b|))

(120)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\frac{1}{3^{|z|}}\sum_{x,b}\bra{b}\Lambda(|x\rangle\langle x|)\ket{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3^{|z|}}.

Since
\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)^{2}})
serves as an upper bound of
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)})
, this completes the proof of Proposition
2
.
∎

Based on Proposition
2
, we can now bound the sample complexity of Protocol
2
. Firstly, we set the median of mean estimator
\hat{f}_{z}
according to Lemma
4
as

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{z}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\cfrac{1}{N}\sum_{r=(t-1)N+1}^{tN}\hat{f}_{z}^{(r)},\quad t=1,2,...,K,

(121)

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{z}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=\textrm{median}\left\{\bar{f}_{z}^{(1)},\bar{f}_{z}^{(2)},...,\bar{f}_{z}^{(K)}\right\},

(122)

with
N
and
K
to be specified. The following theorem gives the performance of Protocol
2
.

Theorem 9
.

Given
\varepsilon,~\delta>0
, the number of qubits
n\geq 2
, and an integer
k\leq n
,
the following number of samples for the calibration procedure

R=\mathcal{O}\left(\cfrac{3^{k}(k\ln n+\ln\delta^{-1})}{\varepsilon^{2}\min_{|z|\leq k}\Gamma_{\Lambda}^{2}(z)}\right)

(123)

is enough for the subsequent shadow estimation procedure to estimate any
k
-local observable for any state to the following precision

\left|\langle\!\langle O|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle O|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\right|\leq\varepsilon 2^{k}\|O\|_{\infty},\quad\forall~\text{k-local}~O\in\mathrm{Herm}(2^{n}),~\forall\rho\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n}).

(124)

with a success probability at least
1-\delta
.

Here, An operator
O
is called
k
-local if it only non-trivially acts on a
k
-qubit subspace,
i.e.

O=\tilde{O}_{S}\otimes I_{[n]\backslash S}
for some index set
S\subset[n]
and
|S|=k
.

Proof.

We first notice that

\displaystyle\left|\langle\!\langle O|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle O|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}^{2n}_{2}}(\hat{f}_{z(a)}^{-1}f_{z(a)}-1)\langle\!\langle O|\sigma_{a}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{a}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\right|

(125)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{|z|\leq k}\left|\hat{f}^{-1}_{z}f_{z}-1\right|\cdot\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}}|\langle\!\langle O|\sigma_{a}\rangle\!\rangle|\cdot|\langle\!\langle\sigma_{a}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{|z|\leq k}\left|\hat{f}^{-1}_{z}f_{z}-1\right|\cdot\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}|\langle\!\langle O|P_{a}\rangle\!\rangle|

where the first equality is by expanding the Pauli transfer basis and we define the mapping
z
as

z:\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}\to\{0,1\}^{n},~z(p)_{i}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,\quad(P_{p})_{i}=I,\\
&1,\quad(P_{p})_{i}\neq I,\end{aligned}\right.

(126)

and the first inequality uses the fact that
O
is
k
-local. Now we bound the second factor of the above equation. Without loss of generality, suppose
O
acts non-trivially on the first
k
qubits:

O=\tilde{O}\otimes I_{2^{n-k}}
, and that
\tilde{O}
can be decomposed as

\tilde{O}=\sum_{\tilde{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2k}}\alpha_{\tilde{a}}P_{\tilde{a}}.

(127)

Then we naturally have

O=\tilde{O}\otimes I_{2^{n-k}}=\sum_{\tilde{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2k}}\alpha_{\tilde{a}}P_{\tilde{a}}\otimes P_{I}^{\otimes(n-k)}.

(128)

So,

\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}}\frac{1}{2^{n}}|\langle\!\langle O|P_{a}\rangle\!\rangle|=\sum_{\tilde{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2k}}|\alpha_{\tilde{a}}|\leq\sqrt{4^{k}}\sqrt{\sum_{\tilde{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2k}}{\alpha}^{2}_{\tilde{a}}}=2^{k}\sqrt{\frac{\Tr(\tilde{O}^{2})}{2^{k}}}\leq 2^{k}\|\tilde{O}\|_{\infty}=2^{k}\|O\|_{\infty},

(129)

where the first inequality is by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Combining the above results, we have

\left|\langle\!\langle O|\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle-\langle\!\langle O|\rho\rangle\!\rangle\right|\leq\max_{|z|\leq k}\left|\hat{f}^{-1}_{z}f_{z}-1\right|\cdot 2^{k}\|O\|_{\infty}

(130)

For any
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, suppose
|\tilde{f_{z}}-f_{z}|\leq\gamma_{z}
, and then we have

\left|1-\hat{f}_{z}^{-1}f_{z}\right|\leq\cfrac{|\hat{f}_{z}-f_{z}|}{|\hat{f}_{z}|}\leq\cfrac{\gamma_{z}}{|f_{z}|-\gamma_{z}}.

(131)

By setting
\gamma_{z}=\cfrac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}|f_{z}|
, the above equation is upper bounded by
\varepsilon
.
Therefore, if we set

N=34\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}_{z})/\gamma_{z}^{2},\quad K=2\ln(2\delta^{-1})

for the median of mean estimator in Eq. (
121
) and Eq. (
122
), by Lemma
4
we have
|1-\hat{f}_{z}^{-1}f_{z}|\leq\varepsilon
with a success probability at least
1-\delta
. Now we want all
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
|z|\leq k
to statisfy this inequality. The number of such strings is no larger than
n^{k}
, so we set

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\max_{|z|\leq k}34\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}_{z})/\gamma_{z}^{2}\leq 34\cdot 3^{k}\cfrac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\max_{|z|\leq k}\Gamma_{\Lambda}^{-2}(z),

(132)

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=2\ln(2(\delta/n^{k})^{-1}),

(133)

and apply the union bound. Now we have
|1-\hat{f}_{z}^{-1}f_{z}|\leq\varepsilon
for all
|z|\leq k
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Our final upper bound of the sample complexity is

R=NK\leq 68\cdot 3^{k}\cfrac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(k\ln n+\ln 2\delta^{-1}\right)\max_{|z|\leq k}\Gamma_{\Lambda}^{-2}(z),

(134)

which completes the proof.
∎

The quantity
\Gamma_{\Lambda}(z)
can be lower bounded when
\Lambda
is close to an identity channel, as shown by the following lemma.

Lemma 7
.

if the Z-basis average fidelity of
\Lambda
satisfies
F_{Z}(\Lambda)\geq 1-c
for some
0\leq c\leq 1
, then
\Gamma_{\Lambda}(z)\geq 1-2c
for all
z\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\Lambda}(z)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x,\delta\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{z\cdot\delta}\langle\!\langle x\oplus\delta|\Lambda|x\rangle\!\rangle

(135)

\displaystyle\geq\cfrac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\left(\langle\!\langle x|\Lambda|x\rangle\!\rangle-\sum_{\delta\in\{0,1\}^{n},|\delta|\neq 0}\langle\!\langle x\oplus\delta|\Lambda|x\rangle\!\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(2\langle\!\langle x|\Lambda|x\rangle\!\rangle-1)

\displaystyle=2F_{Z}(\Lambda)-1

\displaystyle\geq 1-2c.

where the second equality is by the fact that
\Lambda
is trace-preserving, and hence
\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\langle\!\langle b|\Lambda|x\rangle\!\rangle=1
.
∎

Specifically, if we substitute the bound for
\Gamma_{\Lambda}(z)
from Lemma
7
into the above theorem, we get Theorem
3
in the main text. We conclude that our Protocol
2
can mitigate the noise in the computation of the expectation of any
k
-local observable efficiently, given that
k
is small and the noise is weak.

C.2
Estimation Procedure: Local

Now we consider the
RShadow
estimation procedure using
{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}
.
Thanks to Lemma
5
, we only need to characterize
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}^{2}
.
Due to technical difficulties, we are currently not able to bound
\|\cdot\|^{2}_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}
for the most general noise channel
\Lambda
, but we do have results for local noise channel
\Lambda
(hence also for any separable
\Lambda
by linearity). Suppose

\Lambda\equiv\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\Lambda_{i}
,
and denote the Z-basis fidelity of the qubit channels
\Lambda_{i}
as
F_{{Z},i}
. Further assume
O
is
k
-local, which means it is non-trivially supported on only
k
qubits.
We have

\displaystyle\|O\|^{2}_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n})}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}^{\otimes n}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\Tr\left[\left(\sigma\otimes\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)\otimes\widehat{}\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)\right)U^{\dagger\otimes 3}\left(\Lambda^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\right)U^{\otimes 3}\right]

(136)

Consider the single-qubit case, one have

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\sf Cl}_{2}}\sum_{b={0,1}}U^{\dagger\otimes 3}\left(\Lambda_{i}^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\right)U^{\otimes 3}

(137)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b=0,1}\Phi^{(3)}_{\mathrm{Haar}}\left(\Lambda_{i}^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b=0,1}\sum_{\pi,\xi\in S_{3}}c_{\pi,\xi}W_{\pi}\Tr\left(W_{\xi}(\Lambda_{i}^{\dagger}(\ket{b}\bra{b})\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b}\otimes\ket{b}\bra{b})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\left[(3-2F_{{Z},i})(W_{()}+W_{(2,3)})+(2F_{{Z},i}-1)(W_{(1,2)}+W_{(1,3)}+W_{(1,2,3)}+W_{(1,3,2)})\right]

where we use the Weingarten function to expand the Haar integral, see Eq. (
57
), and the value of the Weingarten matrix is from Eq. (
63
).

For any
X\in\mathrm{Herm}(2^{n})
and single-qubit Pauli operators
P_{p},P_{q}
, we want to calculate the following quantity
\Tr\left[(X\otimes P_{p}\otimes P_{q})\Phi_{i}\right]
. By direct calculation using Eq. (
137
), one can verify that there are following four different cases

\Tr\left[(X\otimes P_{p}\otimes P_{q})\Phi_{i}\right]=\Tr(XP_{p}P_{q})\cdot\left\{\begin{aligned} 1,&\quad P_{p}=P_{q}=I,\\
\frac{1}{3},&\quad P_{p}=P_{q}\neq I,\\
\frac{2F_{{Z},i}-1}{3},&\quad(P_{p}=I,P_{q}\neq I)\text{ or }(P_{p}\neq I,P_{q}=I),\\
0,&\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

(138)

This indicates that, the value
\Tr\left[(X\otimes P_{p}\otimes P_{q})\Phi_{i}\right]
is non-zero if and only if the two single-qubit Pauli operators
P_{p}
and
P_{q}
commute.

Now we return to the evaluation of Eq. (
136
). Our strategy is similar to
Huang et al. 2020
. We first decompose
O
into the Pauli operator basis (Note that, we use un-normalized Pauli operators here)

O\equiv\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}}\alpha_{p}P_{p},\quad\text{for~}\alpha_{p}\in\mathbb{R}.

(139)

Since
O
is
k
-local, one have
\alpha_{p}=0
for all
|p|>k
, where for any
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
we denote the Pauli weight of
P_{p}
as
|p|
. Also recall from Eq. (
110
) that

\widehat{}\mathcal{M}=\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}}\hat{f}_{z(p)}|\sigma_{p}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\sigma_{p}|,

(140)

where we define
z
as the following mapping

z:\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}\to\{0,1\}^{n},~z(p)_{i}=0\text{~iff~}(P_{p})_{i}=I,\quad\forall i\in[n].

(141)

The intuition is that after twirling over the local Clifford group the Pauli X, Y, Z indexes are symmetrized.

Now we can calculate Eq. (
136
) as follows

\displaystyle\|O\|^{2}_{\mathrm{shadow},\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n})}\sum_{p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}}\hat{f}_{z(p)}^{-1}\hat{f}_{z(q)}^{-1}\alpha_{p}\alpha_{q}\Tr\left[(\sigma\otimes P_{p}\otimes P_{q})(\otimes_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i})\right]

(142)

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(2^{n})}\sum_{p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}}\hat{f}_{z(p)}^{-1}\hat{f}_{z(q)}^{-1}\alpha_{p}\alpha_{q}\delta(p,q)\Tr(\sigma P_{p}P_{q})\frac{\prod_{i\in[n]:(P_{p,i}=I,P_{q,i}\neq I)\lor(P_{p,i}\neq I,P_{q,i}=I)}(2F_{{Z},i}-1)}{3^{|p\lor q|}}

\displaystyle|p\land q|\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=

The third equality is by the dual characterization of the operator norm. The first inequality is by the fact that the operator norm of a Pauli operator is

\displaystyle\sum_{p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}}\delta(p,q)3^{|p\land q|}|\alpha_{p}||\alpha_{q}|

Lemma 8

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\hat{F}_{\mathrm{SP}}^{(r)})

(161)

\left|\hat{f}_{z,\mathrm{SP}}-\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z,\mathrm{SP}})\right|\leq\varepsilon f_{z},\quad\forall~z\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|z|\leq k.

(180)

We also have, from Lemma
11
, that

\left|\mathbb{E}(\hat{f}_{z,\mathrm{SP}}^{(r)})-f_{z}\right|\leq 2\xi_{\mathrm{SP}}|z|f_{z},\quad\forall~z\in\{0,1\}^{n}.

(181)

Therefore, our final bound is as claimed

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}(\hat{o}^{(r)})-\Tr(O\rho)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}\|O\|_{\infty}\cdot\max_{|z|\leq k}\frac{|f_{z}-\hat{f}_{\mathrm{z,SP}}|}{|\hat{f}_{\mathrm{z,SP}}|}

(182)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}\|O\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon+2k\xi_{\mathrm{SP}}}{1-\varepsilon-2k\xi_{\mathrm{SP}}}

\displaystyle=2^{k}\|O\|_{\infty}\cdot(\varepsilon+2k\xi_{\mathrm{SP}}+o(\varepsilon+2k\xi_{\mathrm{SP}})).

The sample complexity is

\displaystyle R=NK

\displaystyle\leq 2\ln(2\delta^{-1}n^{k})\cdot 34\cdot 3^{k}\varepsilon^{-2}\Gamma_{z}(\Lambda)^{-2}

(183)

\displaystyle\leq 2\ln(2\delta^{-1}n^{k})\cdot 34\cdot 3^{k}\varepsilon^{-2}F_{Z}(\Lambda)^{-2}

\displaystyle=\tilde{}\mathcal{O}(3^{k}\varepsilon^{-2}F_{Z}^{-2}),

where we have used Lemma
11
and Proposition
2
to bound
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{f}^{(r)}_{z,\mathrm{SP}})
and
f_{z}
, respectively. The second inequality is by Lemma
7
. We remark that one can alternatively use a stronger bound given in Lemma
8
when the noise model is assumed to be local.

E.2
Gate-dependent noise: more details

Here, we present more details about the gate-dependent noise simulations in Sec.
VIII
of the main text.

Gate decomposition
: In our simulations, we decompose each single-qubit Clifford gate using the following three generators

\left\{R_{P}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=\exp(-i\frac{\pi}{4}P),~P=X,Y,Z\right\}.

(185)

To see how this works, note that each single-qubit Clifford gate can be uniquely specified by its conjugation on Pauli Z and Pauli X. In notations, any single-qubit Clifford gate
C
can be equivalently described by

\{st[C]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=CZC^{\dagger},\quad de[C]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\penalty\mkern-1.2mu=CXC^{\dagger}\},

(186)

where
st[C]
and
de[C]
are both one of
\{\pm X,\pm Y,\pm Z\}
. They are usually called the stabilizer and de-stabilizer of
C
, respectively.
Another way to say this is that
C
rotates the 3d Cartesian coordinates so that the
+X
(
+Z
) direction is now at the
st[C]
(
de[C]
) direction.

Now, we can decompose
C
into two subsequent rotations: First rotate the
+Z
direction into the direction specified by
st[C]
along either the
X
or
Y
axis. Then rotate the
new

+X
direction into
de[C]
along the
st[C]
axis. Each rotation is of angle
\{0,\frac{\pi}{2},\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2}\}
, so it can be implemented by concatenating up to three generators.

As an example mentioned in the main text, the Hadamard gate has the following stabilizer and de-stabilizer

\{st[H]=+X,\quad de[H]=+Z\},

so it can be decomposed as
H=R_{X}^{2}(\frac{\pi}{2})R_{Y}(\frac{\pi}{2})
, as shown in Fig.
9
.

Gate-dependence of the noise model
:
We claim in the main text that both
pulse mis-calibration
and
random over-rotation
are gate-dependent noise model. Here we explain in more details. The noisy generators for
pulse mis-calibration
error are

\{\widetilde{R}_{P}(\frac{\pi}{2})=\exp(-i\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\pi}{2}P+\Delta_{0})),~P=X.Y.Z\}.

By the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula, this can be expanded as

\widetilde{R}_{P}(\frac{\pi}{2})=\exp(-i\frac{\pi}{4}P)\exp(-i\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{0})\exp(\frac{\pi}{16}[{P},{\Delta_{0}}])\cdots.

As long as
[P,\Delta_{0}]
is different for different
P
, the noise channel is different for different generators. We also notice that the gate-dependent effect only appears in higher-order terms.

For
random over-rotation
error, the noisy generators are

\widetilde{R}_{P}=\exp(-i\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta)P).

For a generator
R_{P}
, the noise channel for this noise model can be written as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{P}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int d\delta~p(\delta;\sigma)R_{P}(\delta)\rho R_{P}^{\dagger}(\delta)

(187)

\displaystyle=\int d\delta~p(\delta;\sigma)e^{-\frac{i}{2}\delta P}\rho e^{\frac{i}{2}\delta P}

where
p(\delta;\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}})
is the Gaussian distribution. Let
\ket{P\pm}
denote the
\pm 1
eigen-vectors of
P
and carry out the Gaussian integral, we get

\displaystyle\Lambda_{P}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\ket{P+}\bra{P+}\bra{P+}\rho\ket{P+}+\ket{P-}\bra{P-}\bra{P-}\rho\ket{P-}

(188)

\displaystyle+e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}}\ket{P+}\bra{P-}\bra{P+}\rho\ket{P-}+e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}}\ket{P-}\bra{P+}\bra{P-}\rho\ket{P+}

This is a dephasing channel on the
\ket{P\pm}
basis. Since this basis is different for different Pauli
P
, this noise model is gate-dependent by definition.
